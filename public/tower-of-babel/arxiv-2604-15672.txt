Faster LLM Inference via Sequential Monte Carlo
Yahya Emara, Mauricio Barba da Costa, Chi-Chih Chang, Cameron Freer, Tim Vieira, Ryan Cotterell, Mohamed S. Abdelfattah

Abstract
Speculative decoding (SD) accelerates language model inference by drafting tokens from a cheap proposal model and verifying them against an expensive target model via rejection sampling. Because rejection truncates the draft block at the first error, throughput degrades when draft and target diverge. Rather than rejecting draft tokens outright, we propose to reweight them. To this end, we introduce sequential Monte Carlo speculative decoding (SMC-SD), which replaces token-level rejection with importance-weighted resampling over a population of draft particles. SMC-SD is a principled approximate inference scheme that trades exactness for additional speed, while preserving theoretical bounds on its per-step approximation error. Because LLM inference is memory bandwidth-bound, the arithmetic needed to draft particles and to score them in parallel comes nearly for free—SMC-SD uses idle compute to turn verification into a vectorized, fixed-size operation with no rollback. Empirically, SMC-SD achieves 2.36 × 2.36\times speed-up over speculative decoding and a 5.2 × 5.2\times speed-up over autoregressive decoding, while remaining within 3% of the target model’s accuracy on reasoning, instruction-following, and coding benchmarks. https://github.com/abdelfattah-lab/smcsd

Both the drafting and verifying stages of SMC-SD use a vectorizable execution structure that map naturally onto GPU hardware and enable greater parallelism than standard speculative decoding. Given a language model

.
We call a language model that is autoregressively factorized an

.
Finally, we say that a language model

{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}})=0\implies p({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}})=0,\forall{\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}}

2.2
Speculative Decoding

Speculative decoding
(
18
;
5
)
accelerates autoregressive inference by applying
rejection sampling
at the token level.
We consider a
target model

p
and a smaller, faster-to-evaluate
draft model

{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}
over the same alphabet
{\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\mathcal{X}}
.
Given a draft length
K
and the draft–target pair
({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q},p)
, each round proceeds in two phases.
In the
draft
phase, the draft model generates a continuation
{\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{d}}
of up to
K
tokens autoregressively from
\overrightarrow{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}
.
In the
verification
phase, the target model scores all
K
draft tokens in a single forward pass.
Each token
{\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}d}_{j}
is accepted with probability
\alpha_{j}=\min\bigl(1,\,{\overrightarrow{p}}({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}d}_{j}\mid{\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}}\,{\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{d}}_{<j})/\overrightarrow{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}d}_{j}\mid{\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}}\,{\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{d}}_{<j})\bigr)
.
On the first rejection, the token is resampled from the residual distribution and all subsequent draft tokens are discarded;
if every token is accepted, a bonus token is drawn from
{\overrightarrow{p}}(\cdot\mid{\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}}\,{\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{d}})
.
In either case, every round produces at least one token whose distribution involves the target model.
This procedure guarantees that the output is distributed
exactly
according to
p

(
18
)
; the full pseudocode is given in
Appendix

A
.
The effective speed-up depends on the
acceptance rate
: the closer
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}
is to
p
, the more tokens survive per round.

The SMC-SD Algorithm.

We instantiate the SMC framework for speculative decoding in

{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}

At the start of each round, the particle population represents a set of partially generated strings
{\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}}^{(N)}

For a weighted particle population
\{({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}}^{(n)},{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}w}^{(n)})\}_{n=1}^{N}
, draft length
K
, and ESS threshold

Algorithm

K

Figure

2

Each particle independently drafts
K

{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}
, then the target model

N

The resulting block weights update the particle population, and a low-ESS resampling step duplicates high-weight trajectories while pruning low-weight ones.
The round updates the internal particle population block by block; after the final round, one complete sequence is sampled from the terminal normalized weights and returned as the model output.
That is, SMC-SD maintains latent particle trajectories during generation and samples the returned sequence only at termination.

3.1
Speed-up Analysis

We analyze SMC-SD TPS under the roofline model
(
32
)
, which classifies a workload according to its
arithmetic intensity
, or the floating-point operations (FLOPs) used per byte of memory loaded for the workload. Whether a workload is memory bandwidth-bound or compute-bound depends on whether its arithmetic intensity is greater than or less than the
ridge point
of the hardware, or its peak FLOPs per memory bandwidth.
Let
B
denote the number of independent user requests served concurrently (the
batch size
), and let the
effective batch size
denote
BN
, the total number of particles across all requests.
The target model’s forward pass processes
BN(K{+}1)
tokens per round:
N
particles per request, each extended by
K
draft tokens plus one bonus token.
Let
c=T_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}/T_{p}\ll 1
be the draft-to-target decoding-time ratio; under the roofline model,
c\approx\rho\mathrel{\overset{\raisebox{-0.75346pt}{{def}}}{=}}\mathcal{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}/\mathcal{P}_{p}
(assuming the draft and target have the same quantization), since memory-bound latency is proportional to parameter count.
Let
R
denote the effective batch size (in tokens) at which a forward pass reaches the ridge point; this depends only on the hardware and quantization format.
Below, we characterize the speed-up factor depending on whether the verification pass is memory-bound or compute-bound (proofs in
Lemmas

D.3
and
D.4
).

Memory-bound regime.

When
BN(K{+}1)\leq R
, the target scores all
BN(K{+}1)
tokens in a single weight-load and the speed-up over autoregressive decoding is

S^{(\text{mem})}=\frac{K+1}{\rho K+1},

(3)

independent of
BN
—particles are “free.”

Compute-bound regime.

Once
BN(K{+}1)
exceeds
R
, the target pass becomes compute-bound.
Writing
\hat{\rho}(BN)=\max(\rho,\rho BN/R)
for the effective draft cost ratio, the speed-up falls to

S^{(\text{comp})}=\frac{K+1}{\hat{\rho}(BN)\,K+BN(K{+}1)/R},

(4)

which degrades linearly in the effective batch size after the ridge point has been reached.

Figure

N
and
K

K

in the memory-bandwidth bound regime, but degrades linearly once the workload becomes compute-bound.

3.2
Characterizing SMC-SD Approximation Error

Because SMC-SD is an approximate sampler, it is important to characterize its approximation quality. Fortunately, since SMC-SD is based on importance sampling, bounds on its divergence from a target distribution can be obtained using known techniques
(
1
;
8
)
.
If
f
is any evaluation metric bounded in

3.3
High-Throughput SMC-SD Engine Design

SMC-SD is not merely an algorithm that happens to run on GPUs—its vectorized structure maps onto the batch-parallel execution model of modern accelerators and makes use of modern optimizations on inference engines including dynamic batching, PagedAttention, and RadixAttention
(
35
;
16
;
38
)
. As such, we build our SMC-SD engine as a fork of SGLang
(
38
)
. We describe our high-throughput inference engine for SMC-SD, organized around a series of observations at the intersection of algorithm and hardware.

Observation 1: SMC-SD converts under-utilized compute into speed.

Autoregressive decoding is heavily memory bandwidth-bound: each token requires
loading the full model weights yet performs only a single vector–matrix
multiply. The
arithmetic intensity
, the FLOPs performed per byte of memory load, for a forward pass of a language model quantized to
b
bytes per parameter remains at just
\mathrm{AI}_{\mathrm{AR}}=2/b
. Standard speculative decoding increases the arithmetic intensity by a factor of
K

but both methods remain far below the roofline ridge points of modern
GPUs—312 for A100, 412 for H200, 584 for B300 in
FP16
(
27
;
29
;
30
;
28
)
—leaving the vast majority
of available compute idle.

SMC-SD closes this gap by leveraging parallelism along the
batch-wise
and
sequence-wise
axes. The draft model uses dynamic batching to concurrently generate
N
tokens at a time in lockstep, followed by the target model scoring a block of
N(K{+}1)
tokens using a single re-prefill step.
Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the target model’s parameter count,
b
the bytes per parameter (
b{=}2
for FP16,
b{=}1
for INT8), and
\rho=\mathcal{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}/\mathcal{P}_{p}
the draft-to-target parameter ratio.
Over a full SMC-SD cycle, the arithmetic intensity is (proofs in
Appendix

D
)

\text{AI}_{\text{cycle}}=\frac{2N(\rho K{+}K{+}1)}{b(\rho K+1)}\;\approx\;\frac{2N(K{+}1)}{b}\quad\text{when }\rho\ll 1,

(9)

an
\approx N\times
increase over standard SD.
For Llama-3.1-70B in FP16 with
N{=}8
and
K{=}16
,
\text{AI}_{\text{cycle}}\approx 136
compared to just
17
for standard SD with the same
K
.

Observation 2: Pointer operations turn prefix-sharing into compute and memory gains.

The resampling step in SMC-SD introduces a novel challenge in inference engine design. When resampling duplicates a high-weight particle, a naïve implementation would require KV-cache data movement across particles and
re-prefill
for the duplicated sequences. In SMC-SD, we avoid both of these costs by exploiting the prefix-sharing structure together with page-based KV-cache management
(
16
)
and RadixAttention
(
38
)
. Specifically, duplicated particles share exactly the same prefix as their ancestors up to the resampling point, so the engine can perform resampling by copying page metadata and incrementing the reference counts rather than copying the underlying KV tensors directly. As a result, resampling is implemented as an
O(\ell_{\text{seq}})
page-metadata operation, rather than an
O(\ell_{\text{seq}}\times d_{\text{model}})
KV-tensor copy.
Exploiting prefix sharing not only avoids KV-cache data movement and reduces FLOPs, but also lowers memory usage. Empirically, for the Llama-1B
\,\to\,
8B,
N{=}8
,
K{=}16
configuration, our optimization yields a
72.3%
reduction in KV-cache usage.

Related Work

SMC for Language Models.

Recent work has applied SMC to large language models for
constrained generation
: steering outputs toward syntactic constraints
(
25
;
33
)
, semantic specifications
(
19
)
, or reward-defined posteriors
(
37
)
.
In these settings, the target is a posterior
p(x\mid\text{constraint})
and the proposal is the unconstrained language model.
SMC-SD repurposes the same machinery for
inference acceleration
: the target is
p
itself and the proposal is a faster draft model
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}
.

Exact speculative decoding.

Tree decoding algorithms
(
4
;
22
)
extend standard SD by proposing a tree of tokens at each position, increasing the expected number of accepted tokens while preserving exact sampling.
SMC-SD trades exactness for speed via a tunable approximation.

Approximate speculative decoding.

Several methods relax exactness through heuristic acceptance criteria.
FSD
(
11
)
accepts tokens based on draft–target divergence;
FLy
(
20
)
uses an entropy gate with a deferred acceptance window;
Judge Decoding
(
3
)
trains a judge network to replace rejection sampling.
All retain a per-token accept/reject gate: a single rejection truncates the block, each token’s contribution is binary, and outputs are variable-length.
SMC-SD instead assigns each
block
a continuous importance weight, produces a fixed number of tokens per round, and provides asymptotic convergence guarantees—all without additional training.

Complementary strategies.

Several orthogonal techniques can be combined with SMC-SD.
On the
drafting
side, EAGLE
(
21
;
22
;
23
)
conditions the draft model on target-model embeddings, Medusa
(
4
)
attaches parallel decoding heads, and DFlash
(
6
)
uses a diffusion model—all producing higher-quality or faster proposals that would directly benefit SMC-SD.
On the
scheduling
side, Specdec++
(
12
)
adaptively modulates speculation length and SSD
(
15
)
asynchronously overlaps drafting and verifying; analogous adaptive strategies over
N
and
K
are a natural extension of SMC-SD. All results use the optimal temperature parameters for each task for both SMC-SD and SD.

6
Conclusion

We presented SMC-SD, an algorithm that accelerates LLM inference by replacing speculative decoding’s token-level rejection with importance-weighted resampling over a population of draft particles.
We established non-asymptotic error bounds showing that the bias and MSE of the importance sampling component of SMC-SD decays as
O(1/N)
, and we demonstrated empirically that SMC-SD achieves
2.36\times
the throughput of optimized speculative decoding and
5.2\times
over autoregressive decoding in a multi-GPU setting, while remaining within 3% of the target model’s accuracy across reasoning, instruction-following, and coding benchmarks.

Beyond acceleration, a key property of SMC-SD is that the same importance-weighting machinery can target distributions beyond
p
itself.
Given a factor
\Psi
, SMC-SD can sample approximately from distributions of the form
\pi(x_{1:T})\propto p(x_{1:T})\cdot\Psi(x_{1:T})
, provided one can define a sequence of potential functions
\{\psi_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
from which properly weighted pairs
({\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}w}_{t},x_{t})\sim\psi_{t}
can be drawn
(
8
)
. The usual SMC convergence guarantees carry over. Three natural instantiations are:

•

Power sampling
(
\Psi=p^{\alpha-1}
, targeting
\pi\propto p^{\alpha}
). Unlike per-token temperature scaling, this tempers the distribution over
entire sequences
.
Appendix

F
shows a 10% Pass@1 improvement on MATH500 with Llama-1B
\,\to\,
8B and a dominating Pass@
k
for
k>1
.

•

Constrained generation
(
\Psi=\mathbf{1}[x\in\mathcal{A}]
). Hard constraints—syntactic validity, format compliance, length bounds—can be enforced while preserving the target’s distribution conditioned on the constraint. Prior work
(
19
;
25
;
33
)
has shown that such constraints can improve accuracy on coding benchmarks; incorporating them into SMC-SD could simultaneously increase downstream performance while accelerating inference.

•

Reward-weighted decoding
(
\Psi=\exp(\beta\cdot R(x))
, targeting
\pi\propto p\cdot e^{\beta R}
). This targets the KL-regularized optimal policy arising in RLHF without fine-tuning, which is relevant for agentic settings where the same base model must be steered toward different objectives.

Standard speculative decoding cannot express any of these: it requires knowledge of the normalizing constant of the target, which is unavailable for this class of distributions.

On the systems side, much work remains to achieve the theoretical roofline model speedup of SMC-SD demonstrated in
Figure

3
.
First, there are opportunities to overlap resampling with drafting to remove its performance overhead altogether.
This can be done by preemptively speculating the result of the ESS threshold, and beginning the next round of drafting accordingly.
Furthermore, the draft and target models can be disaggregated across hardware, and asynchronously dispatch drafting and scoring calls.
Second, by allowing
N
and
K
to vary dynamically, SMC-SD can adapt to underlying hardware and workload characteristics, enabling runtime exploration of its tunable Pareto frontier.
Finally, SMC-SD reflects a trend in co-designing inference algorithms with asymmetric hardware scaling—compute grows faster than memory bandwidth
(
36
)
. For instance, the Blackwell GPU architecture significantly increases FLOPS over the previous generation of Hopper GPU and keeps memory bandwidth approximately equal; this increased compute budget makes it an ideal target for SMC-SD.

Proof.

We first prove the general event-level bound (a), then derive the singleton refinement (b).

Step 1: Setup.

Let
S\mathrel{\overset{\raisebox{-0.75346pt}{{def}}}{=}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\widetilde{p}_{N}}({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\mathcal{X}}^{*})
and note
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[S]=1
. For any
A\subseteq{\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\mathcal{X}}^{*}
, define the importance-weighted estimator
{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\widetilde{p}_{N}}(A)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}W^{(n)}\mathbbm{1}\{{\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}}^{(n)}\in A\}
where
W^{(n)}\mathrel{\overset{\raisebox{-0.75346pt}{{def}}}{=}}p({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}}^{(n)})/{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}}^{(n)})
, and set

U_{A}\mathrel{\overset{\raisebox{-0.75346pt}{{def}}}{=}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\widetilde{p}_{N}}(A)-p(A),\qquad V\mathrel{\overset{\raisebox{-0.75346pt}{{def}}}{=}}S-1.

Both
U_{A}
and
V
have mean zero, and
{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\widehat{p}_{N}}(A)-p(A)=(U_{A}-p(A)V)/(1+V)

Step 2: Bound the Numerator.

The numerator simplifies to a single sum of i.i.d. centered terms:

U_{A}-p(A)V=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}W^{(n)}\bigl(\mathbbm{1}\{A\}({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}}^{(n)})-p(A)\bigr).

(18)

Each summand has mean zero under
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}
because
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}[W(\mathbbm{1}\{A\}-p(A))]=p(A)-p(A)=0
. Therefore

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\bigl[(U_{A}-p(A)V)^{2}\bigr]=\frac{1}{N}\,\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}\left[\frac{p({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{X}})^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{X}})^{2}}\bigl(\mathbbm{1}\{A\}({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{X}})-p(A)\bigr)^{2}\right].

(19)

Since
(\mathbbm{1}\{A\}({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{X}})-p(A))^{2}\leq 1
, this yields

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\bigl[(U_{A}-p(A)V)^{2}\bigr]\leq\frac{1}{N}\,\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}\left[\frac{p({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{X}})^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{X}})^{2}}\right]=\frac{1+\chi^{2}(p\|{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q})}{N},

(20)

using
\chi^{2}(p\|{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}[(W-1)^{2}]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}[W^{2}]-1
.

Step 3: The Denominator Contributes a Higher-order Correction.

Write
Z_{A}\mathrel{\overset{\raisebox{-0.75346pt}{{def}}}{=}}U_{A}-p(A)V=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}W^{(n)}(\mathbbm{1}\{A\}({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}}^{(n)})-p(A))
as in (
18
),
and let
Y\mathrel{\overset{\raisebox{-0.75346pt}{{def}}}{=}}W(\mathbbm{1}\{A\}({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{X}})-p(A))
so that
Z_{A}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}Y^{(n)}
with
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[Y]=0
.
Because
V
is unbounded, the power-series expansion
1/(1+V)^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}(k+1)V^{k}
need not converge pointwise.
We handle this by splitting into a
good
event
\mathcal{G}\mathrel{\overset{\raisebox{-0.75346pt}{{def}}}{=}}\{|V|<\tfrac{1}{2}\}
, on which the series converges absolutely, and its complement
\mathcal{B}\mathrel{\overset{\raisebox{-0.75346pt}{{def}}}{=}}\{|V|\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\}
:

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\frac{Z_{A}^{2}}{(1+V)^{2}}\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\frac{Z_{A}^{2}}{(1+V)^{2}}\,\mathbbm{1}\{\mathcal{G}\}\right]+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\frac{Z_{A}^{2}}{(1+V)^{2}}\,\mathbbm{1}\{\mathcal{B}\}\right].

(21)

Bounding the bad event.

Note that
S=1+V=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}W^{(n)}>0
a.s. (since
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}})>0
whenever
p({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}})>0
).
Since
W^{(n)}\geq 0
and
|\mathbbm{1}\{A\}({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}}^{(n)})-p(A)|\leq 1
, the triangle inequality gives

|Z_{A}|\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}W^{(n)}=S,

(22)

and therefore
Z_{A}^{2}/(1+V)^{2}=Z_{A}^{2}/S^{2}\leq 1
almost surely.
By Markov’s inequality applied to
V^{4}

\Pr(\mathcal{B})=\Pr(|V|\geq\tfrac{1}{2})\leq 16\,\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[V^{4}]=16\!\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}[(W-1)^{4}]}{N^{3}}+\frac{3(N-1)(\chi^{2})^{2}}{N^{3}}\right)=O(N^{-2}),

(23)

where
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}[(W-1)^{4}]<\infty
since expanding
(W-1)^{4}
yields a polynomial in
W
of degree four and
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}[W^{4}]<\infty
.
Combining (
22

23

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\frac{Z_{A}^{2}}{(1+V)^{2}}\,\mathbbm{1}\{\mathcal{B}\}\right]\leq\Pr(\mathcal{B})=O(N^{-2}).

(24)

Series expansion on the good event.

On
\mathcal{G}
,
|V|<\frac{1}{2}
, so the series
1/(1+V)^{2}=1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}(k+1)V^{k}
converges absolutely.
We bound the correction by factoring out one power of
|V|

\mathcal{G}

\left|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}(k+1)V^{k}\right|\leq|V|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(k+1)|V|^{k-1}\leq|V|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(k+1)\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{k-1}\eqqcolon C_{0}\,|V|,

where

is a numerical constant.
Therefore

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\frac{Z_{A}^{2}}{(1+V)^{2}}\,\mathbbm{1}\{\mathcal{G}\}\right]\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{A}^{2}\mathbbm{1}\{\mathcal{G}\}]+C_{0}\,\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{A}^{2}\,|V|\,\mathbbm{1}\{\mathcal{G}\}]\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{A}^{2}]+C_{0}\,\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{A}^{2}\,|V|].

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{A}^{2}\,|V|]\leq\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{A}^{4}]\,\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[V^{2}]}.

(26)

For the
Z_{A}
factor, since

and

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[Y^{2}]\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}[W^{2}]=1+\chi^{2}

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[Y^{4}]\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}[W^{4}]

The fourth moment of the sample mean is

(27)

For the
V

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[V^{2}]=\chi^{2}/N

\mathbb{V}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}(W)=\chi^{2}(p\|{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q})

Substituting into (

(28)

Combining the good-event bound with the bad-event bound (

20

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\frac{Z_{A}^{2}}{(1+V)^{2}}\right]\leq\frac{1+\chi^{2}(p\|{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q})}{N}+O(N^{-3/2}).

Combining with (

B.2
ESS Rate Results

Because the importance weight of a bonus particle depends only on the
K
draft tokens (the bonus token cancels between the target and proposal densities), the ESS is governed by the
\chi^{2}
-divergence between the
K
-token
block
distributions
p_{K}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}_{K}
—not the
(K\!+\!1)
-token distributions.

Theorem B.3

(ESS Rate)
.

Under the assumption
\chi^{2}(p_{K}\|{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}_{K})<\infty
, the ESS fraction converges almost surely:

\frac{\mathrm{ESS}_{N}}{N}\;\xrightarrow{\;\mathrm{a.s.}\;}\;\frac{1}{1+\chi^{2}(p_{K}\|{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}_{K})}

(36)

Crucially, the divergences are between the
K
-token block distributions, even though each particle produces
K+1
tokens.

Proof of
Theorem

B.3
.

By the strong law of large numbers,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}w}_{i}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}_{K}}[{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}w}]=1

and

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}w}_{i}^{2}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}_{K}}[{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}w}^{2}]=1+\chi^{2}(p_{K}\|{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}_{K})
.
Therefore

\frac{\mathrm{ESS}_{N}}{N}=\frac{\bigl(\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i}{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}w}_{i}\bigr)^{2}}{\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i}{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}w}_{i}^{2}}\;\xrightarrow{\;\mathrm{a.s.}\;}\;\frac{(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}_{K}}[{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}w}])^{2}}{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}_{K}}[{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}w}^{2}]}=\frac{1}{1+\chi^{2}(p_{K}\|{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}_{K})}.

(37)

∎

Appendix D
Hardware Utilization and Speedup Proofs

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the target model’s parameter count,
b
the bytes per parameter,
\rho=\mathcal{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}/\mathcal{P}_{p}
the draft-to-target parameter ratio,
c=T_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}/T_{p}\ll 1
the draft-to-target decoding-time ratio.
Let
B
denote the number of independent user requests served concurrently (the
batch size
), and let
BN
denote the
effective batch size
—the total number of particles across all requests.
In the memory-bound regime
c\approx\rho
.
In one SMC-SD step, the draft model runs
K
sequential forward passes (one per token position, with all
BN
particles batched), taking
cT_{p}K
seconds.
The target model scores all
BN(K{+}1)
tokens (including bonus tokens) in a single forward pass.
The resampling step is negligible.

D.1
Arithmetic Intensity

Lemma D.1

(Target-pass arithmetic intensity)
.

The arithmetic intensity of the target model’s forward pass in one SMC-SD cycle is

\text{AI}_{\text{target}}=\frac{2BN(K{+}1)}{b}\;\;\text{FLOPs/byte}.

(42)

Proof.

Using the standard approximation that a forward pass through a model with
\mathcal{P}
parameters requires
2\mathcal{P}
FLOPs (one multiply-add per parameter), the target pass processes
BN(K{+}1)
tokens in one invocation—
BNK
draft tokens plus
BN
bonus tokens carried over from the previous round—for a total of
2\mathcal{P}\cdot BN(K{+}1)
FLOPs.
The bytes transferred are
b\mathcal{P}
(loading the weights once), giving
\text{AI}_{\text{target}}=2\mathcal{P}\cdot BN(K{+}1)/(b\mathcal{P})=2BN(K{+}1)/b
.
For FP16 (
b{=}2
) this reduces to
BN(K{+}1)
.
For comparison, autoregressive decoding achieves
\text{AI}=2B/b
and standard SD achieves
\text{AI}=2B(K{+}1)/b
when all
K
draft tokens are accepted.
When
\text{AI}_{\text{target}}
exceeds the hardware’s ridge point (peak FLOPs / peak bandwidth), the target pass becomes compute-bound and further increasing the effective batch size
BN
no longer comes for free.
∎

Lemma D.2

(Full-cycle arithmetic intensity)
.

Over a full SMC-SD cycle (including
K
draft forward passes), the arithmetic intensity is

\text{AI}_{\text{cycle}}=\frac{2BN(\rho K{+}K{+}1)}{b(\rho K+1)}\;\approx\;\frac{2BN(K{+}1)}{b}\quad\text{when }\rho\ll 1.

(43)

Proof.

The total FLOPs over one cycle are
2\mathcal{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}\cdot BNK+2\mathcal{P}\cdot BN(K{+}1)=2\mathcal{P}\,BN(\rho K+K+1)
: the draft model performs
K
forward passes each processing
BN
tokens, and the target model performs one pass processing
BN(K{+}1)
tokens.
The total bytes transferred are
b\mathcal{P}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}q}}\cdot K+b\mathcal{P}=b\mathcal{P}(\rho K+1)
: the draft weights are loaded
K
times and the target weights once.
Dividing gives
\text{AI}_{\text{cycle}}=2BN(\rho K+K+1)/(b(\rho K+1))
.
When
\rho\ll 1
, the draft-model terms become negligible and this simplifies to
2BN(K{+}1)/b
.
∎

D.2
Speedup Proofs

Lemma D.3

(Memory-bound speed-up)
.

If the target forward pass is memory bandwidth-bound (i.e.
BN(K{+}1)\leq R
, where
R
is the hardware ridge-point batch size), the SMC-SD speed-up over autoregressive decoding is

S^{(\text{mem})}=\frac{K+1}{cK+1},

(44)

independent of the effective batch size
BN
.

Proof.

When the target forward pass is memory bandwidth-bound, loading the model weights dominates and the verification cost is approximately constant regardless of
BN
.
The cycle time is

t_{\text{SMC-SD step}}^{(\text{mem})}=T_{p}\bigl(cK+1\bigr).

(45)

Since autoregressive decoding takes
T_{p}K
seconds for
K
tokens and each SMC-SD cycle produces
K{+}1
tokens per request, the speed-up is
S^{(\text{mem})}=(K{+}1)/(cK+1)
.
Adding more particles or requests is “free” because the target weights are loaded only once regardless of
BN
.
∎

Lemma D.4

(Compute-bound speed-up)
.

If the target forward pass is compute-bound (i.e.
BN(K{+}1)>R
), the SMC-SD speed-up over autoregressive decoding is

S^{(\text{comp})}=\frac{K+1}{\hat{c}(BN)\,K+BN(K{+}1)/R},

(46)

where
\hat{c}(BN)=\max(c,\,c\cdot BN/R)
is the effective draft cost ratio and
R
is the hardware ridge-point batch size in tokens.

Proof.

As
BN(K{+}1)
grows, the arithmetic intensity of the target pass eventually exceeds the hardware ridge point, and the forward pass becomes compute-bound.
In this regime, the target verification time scales linearly with the number of tokens processed.
Let
T_{p}^{(1)}
denote the (memory-bound) time to decode a single token with the target model.
The compute-bound verification of
BN(K{+}1)
tokens takes approximately
T_{p}^{(1)}\cdot BN(K{+}1)/R
seconds, where
R=\text{peak FLOPs}\,/\,\text{peak bandwidth}\cdot b/2
tokens (with
b
bytes per parameter).
The draft model, which processes
BN
tokens in parallel at each of its
K
steps, may itself become compute-bound if
BN>R
.
In the compute-bound regime, the draft model’s per-step cost scales as
c\cdot BN/R
relative to
T_{p}^{(1)}
, since the draft model has
c\approx\rho
times the cost of the target per token.
Writing
\hat{c}(BN)=\max(c,\,c\cdot BN/R)
for the effective draft cost ratio, the cycle time becomes

t_{\text{SMC-SD step}}^{(\text{comp})}=T_{p}^{(1)}\!\left(\hat{c}(BN)\,K+\frac{BN(K{+}1)}{R}\right).

(47)

Since autoregressive decoding takes
T_{p}^{(1)}
per token, the speed-up is
S^{(\text{comp})}=(K{+}1)/(\hat{c}(BN)K+BN(K{+}1)/R)
.
∎

Appendix F
Power Sampling

One advantage of SMC is that it allows us to sample approximately from un-normalized target distributions whose normalizing constants are intractable. One such target distribution is the
power distribution

(
14
)
, given by

p_{\textrm{pow}}({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}})\propto p({\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}})^{\alpha}

where
{\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\bm{x}}\in{\color[rgb]{0.5508,0.0391,0.3477}\mathcal{X}}^{*}
. In contrast to temperature sampling, which tempers the distribution of each
token
, power sampling tempers the distribution over entire
strings
. Applying SMC-SD to sample approximately from this distribution is simply a matter of weighting the draft model

14B — Assess credibility

Instruction:
How can you assess the credibility of a source of information, such as a news article or blog post, without relying solely on the reputation of the author or publisher?

SMC-SD:
Assessing the credibility of a source of information, including a news article or blog post, is a matter of taking a few key steps to understand the reliability and quality of the information presented. Here”s a checklist of steps that can be followed to evaluate the credibility of the source:

•

Check the Author and Publisher’s Background:
Look for the author’s credentials, expertise, and the publication’s reputation. A reputable author or publisher is less likely to have a history of making false claims or spreading misinformation.

•

Review the Bias and Methodology:
Assess the sources of information used in the article or blog post. Check if the data or other sources are clearly stated and verified. Look for indications of bias—bias can come from multiple sources, leading to an incomplete or potentially misleading narrative.

•

Check for Reproducibility of the Data:
Determine whether the data used in the article or blog post can be reproduced. This is crucial to ensure that the information presented is verifiable. If the data could be easily modified, then the information is less credible.

•

Evaluate the Relevance to the Topic:
The article should be based on issues of relevance and current importance. The information should address a specific problem or discuss an issue of public interest. The author must also support their claims with facts and evidence.

•

Check for Peer Reactions and Expert Consensus:
The credibility of the source also depends on the extent to which other experts in the field agree with the information. Support from respected figures can significantly bolster the credibility of a source.

•

Use External References and Other Sources:
Look for credible references that support the claims made in the article or blog post. Cross-referencing can help verify the accuracy of the information presented.

•

Look for a Balanced View:
Ensure that the article or blog post provides a fair and balanced view of the topic. Avoid articles that are overly negative or sensationalistic.

•

Check for Up to Date Information:
Keep up with the latest developments in the field or topic being discussed. This can often provide the most reliable and up-to-date information.

By following these steps, you can effectively assess the credibility of a source of information, ensuring that you obtain accurate and trustworthy information.
