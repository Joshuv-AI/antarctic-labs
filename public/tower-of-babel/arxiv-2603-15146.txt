Infinite families of APN permutations in constrained trivariate classes over 𝔽 2 m \mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
Daniele Bartoli, Pantelimon Stănică

Abstract
The construction of permutation polynomials over finite fields with simple algebraic structure in multiple variables is a challenging problem with significant applications in cryptography and coding theory. Recently, Li and Kaleyski (IEEE, Trans. Inf. Th., 2024) generalized two sporadic quadratic APN permutations from dimension 9 into infinite families of trivariate functions with coefficients in 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} . In this paper we extend this work by investigating generalizations of both families where the scalar coefficient is allowed to range freely over 𝔽 2 m ∗ \mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*} . For the family G a ​ ( x , y , z ) = ( x q + 1 + a ​ x q ​ z + y ​ z q , x q ​ z + y q + 1 , x ​ y q + a ​ y q ​ z + z q + 1 ) , G_{a}(x,y,z)=(x^{q+1}+ax^{q}z+yz^{q},\;x^{q}z+y^{q+1},\;xy^{q}+ay^{q}z+z^{q+1}), with a ∈ 𝔽 2 m ∗ a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*} , q = 2 i q=2^{i} , gcd ⁡ ( i , m ) = 1 \gcd(i,m)=1 , and m m odd, we prove that the permutation property is characterized by the absence of roots in 𝔽 2 m ∗ \mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*} of an associated one-variable polynomial, and that this root condition is also equivalent to the APN property. This yields a quantitative lower bound on the number of good parameters: if d = q 2 + q + 1 d=q^{2}+q+1 , then at least 2 m + 1 − ( d − 1 ) ​ ( d − 2 ) ​ 2 m / 2 − d d \frac{2^{m}+1-(d-1)(d-2)2^{m/2}-d}{d} values of a ∈ 𝔽 2 m ∗ a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*} give APN permutations G a G_{a} . In the binary case q = 2 q=2 , we prove that a = 1 a=1 is good whenever 7 ∤ m 7\nmid m , recovering the Li–Kaleyski family and yielding APN permutations in that range. For the generalized second family H a ​ ( x , y , z ) = ( x q + 1 + a ​ x ​ y q + y ​ z q , x ​ y q + z q + 1 , x q ​ z + y q + 1 + a ​ y q ​ z ) , H_{a}(x,y,z)=(x^{q+1}+axy^{q}+yz^{q},\;xy^{q}+z^{q+1},\;x^{q}z+y^{q+1}+ay^{q}z), we obtain the analogous root criterion and show that its defining one-variable polynomial is root-equivalent to that of G a G_{a} . Consequently, the same parameters a a produce APN permutations in both families. We also establish strong non-equivalence results. First, G a G_{a} (resp. H a H_{a} ) is diagonally equivalent to the Li–Kaleyski representative G 1 G_{1} (resp. H 1 H_{1} ) of Li–Kaleyski (IEEE Trans. Inform. Introduction and tools from algebraic geometry

Let

2
Characterization of the first generalized family
G_{a}

2.1
The first class and equivalent polynomials

Throughout,
m\geq 3
is an odd integer,
i
is a positive integer with
\gcd(i,m)=1
, and
q=2^{i}
. Since
m
is odd and all characteristics are 2, every element of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
has a unique
q
-th root and the Frobenius
x\mapsto x^{q}
is a bijection on
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
.

Definition 2.1
.

For
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
, define
G_{a}:\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{3}\to\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{3}
by

G_{a}(x,y,z)=\bigl(x^{q+1}+ax^{q}z+yz^{q},\;x^{q}z+y^{q+1},\;xy^{q}+ay^{q}z+z^{q+1}\bigr).

When
a=1
this is the first Li–Kaleyski family
F_{1}
; see
[
10
]
. The function
G_{a}
is a degree-2 polynomial map over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{3}

with the following symmetry: if
\sigma
denotes the coordinate swap
(x,y,z)\mapsto(z,y,x)
, then

\sigma\circ G_{a}\circ\sigma=G_{a}
,
as one verifies directly by checking that the first and third components of
G_{a}(z,y,x)
are the third and first components of
G_{a}(x,y,z)
respectively, while the second component is unchanged. This symmetry will play a role in the proofs below.

The permutation and APN properties of
G_{a}
are both controlled by a single polynomial in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}[T]
. Several polynomial forms arise naturally, and the next proposition shows they are all root-equivalent.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
. Consider the following polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}[T]
:

\displaystyle P_{a}(T)

\displaystyle=T^{q^{2}+q+1}+(aT^{q}+1)^{q+1},

\displaystyle P_{a}^{\prime}(T)

\displaystyle=T^{q^{2}+q+1}+aT^{q^{2}+q}+1,

\displaystyle Q_{a}(T)

\displaystyle=T^{q^{2}+q+1}+aT+1,

\displaystyle Q_{a^{q}}(T)

\displaystyle=T^{q^{2}+q+1}+a^{q}T+1,

\displaystyle R_{a}(T)

\displaystyle=T^{q^{2}+q+1}+(aT+1)^{q+1},

\displaystyle S_{a}(T)

\displaystyle=T^{q^{2}+q+1}+a^{q}T^{q+1}+1.

Each polynomial evaluates to
1
at
T=0
, so none has
0
as a root.
Moreover, any one of them has a root in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
if and only if all of them do (we call such, root-equivalent).

Proof.

We establish the equivalences through a chain of explicit substitutions, each preserving the root locus in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
.

Step 1:
P_{a}\leftrightarrow P_{a}^{\prime}
.

Expanding
(aT^{q}+1)^{q+1}
gives

P_{a}(T)=T^{q^{2}+q+1}+a^{q+1}T^{q^{2}+q}+a^{q}T^{q^{2}}+aT^{q}+1.

Let

P_{a}^{*}(T):=T^{q^{2}+q+1}P_{a}(1/T)

be the reciprocal polynomial. A direct computation yields

P_{a}^{*}(T)=1+a^{q+1}T+a^{q}T^{q+1}+aT^{q^{2}+1}+T^{q^{2}+q+1}=T(T^{q}+a)^{q+1}+1.

Since the map
T\mapsto T^{-1}
is a bijection of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
,
P_{a}
has a root in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
if and only if
P_{a}^{*}
does.
We now show that
P_{a}^{*}
and
P_{a}^{\prime}
are root-equivalent on
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
.
Suppose
U\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
satisfies
P_{a}^{*}(U)=0
. Set

W:=U^{q}+a
.
Then
W\neq 0
(otherwise
P_{a}^{*}(U)=U\cdot 0^{q+1}+1=1\neq 0
). From

P_{a}^{*}(U)=U(U^{q}+a)^{q+1}+1=0

we get

U\,W^{q+1}=1,\quad\text{hence}\quad U=W^{-(q+1)}.

Substituting into
W=U^{q}+a
gives
W=W^{-q(q+1)}+a
.
Multiplying by
W^{q^{2}+q}
(which is valid since
W\neq 0
) yields

W^{q^{2}+q+1}+aW^{q^{2}+q}+1=0,\text{ i.e. }P_{a}^{\prime}(W)=0.

Thus every nonzero root of
P_{a}^{*}
produces a nonzero root of
P_{a}^{\prime}
.
Conversely, suppose
W\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
satisfies
P_{a}^{\prime}(W)=0
, i.e.

W^{q^{2}+q+1}+aW^{q^{2}+q}+1=0.

Define

U:=W^{-(q+1)}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
.
Dividing the equation by
W^{q^{2}+q}
gives

W+a+W^{-q(q+1)}=0
.
Since
U^{q}=W^{-q(q+1)}
, this becomes
W=U^{q}+a
.
Therefore

P_{a}^{*}(U)=U(U^{q}+a)^{q+1}+1=UW^{q+1}+1=W^{-(q+1)}W^{q+1}+1=1+1=0.

So
P_{a}^{\prime}
has a nonzero root if and only if
P_{a}^{*}
does, and hence (via reciprocity) if and only if
P_{a}
does.

Step 2:
P_{a}^{\prime}\leftrightarrow Q_{a}
.

Observe that
Q_{a}
is the reciprocal of
P_{a}^{\prime}
:

T^{q^{2}+q+1}P_{a}^{\prime}(1/T)=1+aT+T^{q^{2}+q+1}=Q_{a}(T).

Hence
P_{a}^{\prime}(T_{0})=0
iff
Q_{a}(1/T_{0})=0
, and the map
T_{0}\mapsto 1/T_{0}
is a bijection on
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
. Therefore
P_{a}^{\prime}
and
Q_{a}
have roots in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
simultaneously.

Step 3:
Q_{a}\leftrightarrow Q_{a^{q}}
.

The Frobenius automorphism
x\mapsto x^{q}
is a bijection on
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
. If
T_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
satisfies
Q_{a}(T_{0})=0
, i.e.,
T_{0}^{q^{2}+q+1}+aT_{0}+1=0
, then raising both sides to the
q
-th power gives
(T_{0}^{q})^{q^{2}+q+1}+a^{q}T_{0}^{q}+1=0
, i.e.,
Q_{a^{q}}(T_{0}^{q})=0
. Since
T_{0}^{q}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
and the map
T_{0}\mapsto T_{0}^{q}
is a bijection,
Q_{a}
has a root in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
iff
Q_{a^{q}}
does.

Step 4:
Q_{a}\leftrightarrow R_{a}
.

Suppose
R_{a}(T_{0})=0
for some
T_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
, i.e.,

T_{0}^{q^{2}+q+1}+(aT_{0}+1)^{q+1}=0
. Dividing by
T_{0}^{q+1}
(which is nonzero) and setting
U_{0}=T_{0}^{-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
:

U_{0}^{-q^{2}}+(a+U_{0})^{q+1}=0.

Multiplying by
U_{0}^{q^{2}}
and expanding

(a+U_{0})^{q+1}=(a^{q}+U_{0}^{q})(a+U_{0})
gives

1+a^{q+1}U_{0}^{q^{2}}+aU_{0}^{q^{2}+q}+a^{q}U_{0}^{q^{2}+1}+U_{0}^{q^{2}+q+1}=0,

which is precisely
P_{a}(U_{0})=0
. By Steps 1–2,
P_{a}(U_{0})=0
implies
Q_{a}
has a root in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
. The argument reverses (replacing
T_{0}
by
U_{0}^{-1}
), completing the equivalence.

Step 5:
S_{a}\leftrightarrow Q_{a}
.

Since
S_{a}(0)=1
, all roots of
S_{a}
in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
are nonzero. Set

F_{a}(T):=T^{q^{2}+q+1}+a^{q}T^{q^{2}}+1
.
Then
T^{q^{2}+q+1}S_{a}(1/T)=F_{a}(T)
,
so
S_{a}
and
F_{a}
are root-equivalent over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
.
Now let
u\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
. Then

F_{a}(u)=0
if and only if
u^{q^{2}+q+1}+a^{q}u^{q^{2}}+1=0
.
Raising both sides to the
q
-th power and using
u^{q^{3}}=u
gives

u^{q^{2}+q+1}+a^{q^{2}}u+1=0
, i.e.,
Q_{a^{q^{2}}}(u)=0
.
Conversely, if
Q_{a^{q^{2}}}(u)=0
, then raising to the
q^{2}
-th power yields
u^{q^{2}+q+1}+a^{q}u^{q^{2}}+1=0
, that is,
F_{a}(u)=0
.
Hence
F_{a}
and
Q_{a^{q^{2}}}
are root-equivalent over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
. By Step 3,

Q_{a^{q^{2}}}
and
Q_{a}
are root-equivalent. Therefore
S_{a}
and
Q_{a}
are
root-equivalent over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
.

Combining all five steps yields the claimed equivalence, and the proof of the proposition is shown.
∎

Theorem 2.3

(
[
2
, Theorem 4.2]
)
.

Let
m\geq 3
be odd,
\gcd(i,m)=1
, and
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
. The function
G_{a}
is a permutation on
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{3}
if and only if the polynomial
Q_{a}(T)=T^{q^{2}+q+1}+aT+1
has no root in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
.

The proof in
[
2
]
proceeds by analyzing the collision system
G_{a}(x+\alpha,y+\beta,z+\gamma)=G_{a}(x,y,z)
for
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\neq(0,0,0)
. After algebraic elimination one reduces to a homogeneous polynomial
L(x,y,\alpha,\beta)
of degree
q^{3}+2q^{2}+2q+1
in the variables
(x,y,\alpha,\beta)
(with
\gamma
eliminated via one of the equations), which factors as

L(x,y,\alpha,\beta)=\prod_{\theta\in\Theta}F_{\theta}(x,y,\alpha,\beta),

where
\Theta=\{\theta\in\overline{\mathbb{F}_{2}}:\theta^{q^{2}+q+1}+a^{q}\theta^{q^{2}+q}+1=0\}
is the root set of
P_{a^{q}}^{\prime}
(root-equivalent to
Q_{a}
by Proposition
2.2
), and each factor
F_{\theta}
is shown to be absolutely irreducible by the Jacobian criterion. The three cases
\Theta\cap\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}\neq\emptyset
,
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2^{2m}}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
, and
\Theta\cap(\mathbb{F}_{2^{2m}}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}})\neq\emptyset
are handled separately using the Lang–Weil bound (Theorem
1.1
) and the Aubry–McGuire–Rodier lemma (Lemma
1.2
) to guarantee the existence of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
-rational points on
F_{\theta}
in the first and third cases, yielding a non-trivial collision and hence non-permutation.
In particular, in the rational-root case
\Theta\cap\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}\neq\emptyset
, the same geometric argument applied to the corresponding differential slice yields, for sufficiently large
m
, more than the two trivial solutions in one direction, and hence failure of the APN property.
We refer to
[
2
]
for a related argument; the proof there is self-contained and complete.

The following classical fact will be needed several times.

Lemma 2.4

(Trace Criterion)
.

Let
\gcd(i,m)=1
. The Artin–Schreier equation
t^{q}+t=1
has no solution in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
if and only if
m
is odd.

Proof.

The equation
t^{q}+t=c
is solvable in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
if and only if
{\textrm{Tr}}_{\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{2^{k}}}(c)=0
, where
k=\gcd(i,m)=1
, so the relevant trace is
{\textrm{Tr}}_{\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{2}}(c)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}c^{2^{j}}
. For
c=1\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
, this equals
\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}1=m\pmod{2}
. Hence
{\textrm{Tr}}(1)=1\neq 0
when
m
is odd, meaning no solution exists; when
m
is even,
{\textrm{Tr}}(1)=0
and solutions do exist.
Hence the claim follows.
∎

We now show that, for the family
G_{a}
, the APN property is equivalent to the
permutation property, and both are governed by the same univariate root condition.
Recall that

G_{a}(x,y,z)=\bigl(x^{q+1}+ax^{q}z+yz^{q},\;x^{q}z+y^{q+1},\;xy^{q}+ay^{q}z+z^{q+1}\bigr),

where
q=2^{i}
,
\gcd(i,m)=1
,
m
is odd, and
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
, and that

Q_{a}(T)=T^{q^{2}+q+1}+aT+1,

and denote

D_{\mathbf{u}}G_{a}(\mathbf{x}):=G_{a}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u})+G_{a}(\mathbf{x})+G_{a}(\mathbf{u}).

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
m\geq 3
be odd,
q=2^{i}
,
\gcd(i,m)=1
, and
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
.
For every nonzero direction
\mathbf{d}=(A,B,C)\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{3}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
,
the kernel size
|\ker D_{\mathbf{d}}G_{a}|
satisfies the following.

(a)

Axis directions.

If exactly one of
A,B,C
is nonzero, then
|\ker D_{\mathbf{d}}G_{a}|=2
.

(b)

Type
1
(
C=0
,
AB\neq 0
).

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
m\geq 3
be odd,
q=2^{i}
,
\gcd(i,m)=1
,

, and

on

.
The following are equivalent:

(i)

has no root in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
.

(ii)

G_{a}
is a permutation on
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{3}
.

(iii)

G_{a}
is APN on
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{3}
.

Proof.

By
[
2
, Theorem 4.2]
(equivalently, Theorem
2.3
),
(i) and (ii) are equivalent. It remains to prove the equivalence
between (i) and (iii).

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i).

We prove the contrapositive. If
Q_{a}
has a root in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
, then

G_{a}
is not APN since
|\ker D_{\mathbf{d}}G_{a}|>2
.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(iii).

Assume that
Q_{a}
has no root in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
. By Corollary
2.6
,
|\ker D_{\mathbf{d}}G_{a}|=2
for any
{\mathbf{d}}
. Thus the number of solutions of

is at most

for each
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
and

is APN.

∎

2.3

We now show that good values of

(those for which

has no roots) exist for every odd

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
m\geq 3
be odd,
q=2^{i}
with
\gcd(i,m)=1
, and
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
.
Define the linearized polynomial

Then the following hold:

1.

If

has a nonzero root in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}

Q_{a}(T)=T^{q^{2}+q+1}+aT+1
has a root in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
.

2.

If
\gcd(i,m)=1
(equivalently,
\gcd(q-1,2^{m}-1)=1

Hence in this case,

Q_{a}\text{ has a root in }\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}\iff L_{a}\text{ has a nonzero root in }\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}.

Proof.

(1) Assume
S\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
satisfies
L_{a}(S)=0
, i.e.,

S^{q^{3}}+aS^{q}+S=0
.
Since
\gcd(i,m)=1
, the map
S\mapsto S^{q-1}
is a bijection of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
.
Set

T:=S^{q-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
.
Dividing
L_{a}(S)=0
by
S
gives
S^{q^{3}-1}+aS^{q-1}+1=0
.
Note that
S^{q^{3}-1}=(S^{q-1})^{q^{2}+q+1}=T^{q^{2}+q+1}
.
Thus

T^{q^{2}+q+1}+aT+1=0
,
i.e.,
Q_{a}(T)=0
. Hence
Q_{a}
has a root in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
.

(2) Conversely, assume
\gcd(i,m)=1
and let
T\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
satisfy
Q_{a}(T)=0
, i.e.,

T^{q^{2}+q+1}+aT+1=0
.
Since
\gcd(q-1,2^{m}-1)=\gcd(2^{i}-1,2^{m}-1)=2^{\gcd(i,m)}-1=1
,
the map
S\mapsto S^{q-1}
is a bijection of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
.
Therefore there exists
S\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
such that

S^{q-1}=T
.
Then
T^{q^{2}+q+1}=(S^{q-1})^{q^{2}+q+1}=S^{q^{3}-1}
.
Substituting into
Q_{a}(T)=0
gives

S^{q^{3}-1}+aS^{q-1}+1=0
.
Multiplying by
S
yields

S^{q^{3}}+aS^{q}+S=0
,
i.e.,
L_{a}(S)=0
. Thus
L_{a}
has a nonzero root in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
.
This proves the claims.
∎

Proposition 2.9
.

Let

be odd with
\gcd(i,m)=1

Q_{1}(T)=T^{q^{2}+q+1}+T+1

\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
if and only if

Proof.

By Proposition
2.8

has a root in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}

\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}

are equal (since

where
C_{0}=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0&1\\
1&0&1\\
0&1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)

C_{0}
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
is
T^{3}+T+1
, which is irreducible of order 7 (the multiplicative order of its roots in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{3}}

iff

. Therefore
\det(A_{L}(m)-I_{3})=0
iff
7\mid m
, i.e.,
Q_{1}
has a root in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
iff
7\mid m
. The stated condition follows.
This recovers
[

, Prop. 4]
, and our claim follows.
∎

\gcd(m,7)=1
. It is natural to ask whether the same method extends to

L_{a}(S)=S^{q^{3}}+aS^{q}+S,\qquad a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}.

For a general
\sigma
-linearized polynomial, the kernel is controlled by the product

A_{L}=C_{L}C_{L}^{\sigma}\cdots C_{L}^{\sigma^{m-1}},\qquad\sigma(x)=x^{q},

where
C_{L}
is the companion matrix; see
[
11
]
. In our case,

C_{L}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\
1&0&a\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad C_{L}^{\sigma^{k}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\
1&0&a^{q^{k}}\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix}.

When
a=1
, all factors are equal, so
A_{L}=C_{L}^{m}
, which is exactly the situation treated in
[
10
]
. For general
a
, however, the Frobenius conjugates
a,a^{q},\dots,a^{q^{m-1}}
appear in different factors, so the product no longer reduces to a matrix power. Thus the argument for
a=1
does not extend directly.

This already explains the main difficulty: the matrix approach isolates the bad parameters through the condition
\det(A_{L}-I_{3})=0
, but it does not by itself yield a convenient closed description in terms of the single parameter
a
. It is therefore preferable to return to the equivalent one-variable criterion from Proposition
2.8
, namely that
L_{a}
has a nonzero root if and only if

Q_{a}(T)=T^{q^{2}+q+1}+aT+1

has a root in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
.

The advantage of
Q_{a}
is that the bad-parameter set can then be described exactly as the image of a rational function, as follows.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let

d:=q^{2}+q+1\qquad\text{and}\qquad g:\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}},\qquad g(u):=\frac{u^{d}+1}{u}.

Then, for
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

Q_{a}(T)=T^{d}+aT+1
has a root in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
;

(ii)

a\in g(\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*})
.

Consequently, the set of good parameters is

\mathcal{B}_{m,q}:=\{a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}:Q_{a}\text{ has no root in }\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}\}=\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}\setminus g(\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}).

Proof.

Let
u\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
. Then

Q_{a}(u)=u^{d}+au+1.

Hence

Q_{a}(u)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a=\frac{u^{d}+1}{u}=g(u).

Therefore
Q_{a}
has a root in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
if and only if
a
lies in the image
of
g
.
∎

To study the size of
g(\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*})
, it is natural to consider the collision
curve
g(x)=g(y)
. Clearing denominators gives

x^{d}y+xy^{d}+x+y=0.

Since

x^{d}y+xy^{d}+x+y=(x+y)\left(xy\sum_{j=0}^{d-2}x^{d-2-j}y^{j}+1\right),

the diagonal component
x+y=0
splits off, and the residual curve is

\Gamma:\quad xy\sum_{j=0}^{d-2}x^{d-2-j}y^{j}+1=0.

Lemma 2.11
.

The curve
\Gamma
is absolutely irreducible.

Proof.

Consider the birational morphism
(x,y)\mapsto(xy,y)
applied to
g(x)+g(y)=0
. Recall that a birational morphism provides a bijection between absolutely irreducible components of curves. This gives

x^{d}y^{d+1}+xy^{d+1}+xy+y=y(x^{d}y^{d}+xy^{d}+x+1).

The factor
y
corresponds to the factor
(x+y)
in
g(x)+g(y)
. The second factor is absolutely irreducible, since

\varphi(u):=u^{d}+\frac{v+1}{v^{d}+v}

satisfies Eisenstein’s criterion for function fields (see
[
14
, Proposition 3.1.15]
), considering as place
P
in
\overline{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(v)
the unique zero of
v
. In fact,

v_{P}(1)=0,\quad v_{P}\left(\frac{v+1}{v^{d}+v}\right)=-1,\quad\gcd(d,-1)=1,

and, applying
[
14
, Proposition 3.1.15(2)]
,
\Gamma
is absolutely irreducible.
∎

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
m\geq 3
be odd,
q=2^{i}
with
\gcd(i,m)=1
, and
d=q^{2}+q+1
. Define

g:\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}\to\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
by
g(u)=\frac{u^{d}+1}{u}
, and let

\mathcal{B}_{m,q}:=\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}\setminus g(\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*})
be the set of good parameters.
Then

|\mathcal{B}_{m,q}|\geq\frac{2^{m}+1-(d-1)(d-2)2^{m/2}-d}{d},

and for every
a\in\mathcal{B}_{m,q}
, the function
G_{a}
is an APN permutation on
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{3}
.

Proof.

Let
C_{i}:=\{v\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}:\#g^{-1}(v)=i\}
for
i\geq 0
. Clearly, the sets
C_{i}
partition
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
, so
\sum_{i}\#C_{i}=2^{m}
. For a fixed
v\in C_{i}
with
i>0
, there exist
u_{1},\ldots,u_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
such that
g(u_{1})=\dots=g(u_{i})=v
. These correspond to
i^{2}
pairs
(u_{j_{1}},u_{j_{2}})
on the curve
\mathcal{C}_{g}:g(x)+g(y)=0
.

Let
\Gamma
be the curve defined by the polynomial
\phi(x,y)=\frac{g(x)+g(y)}{x+y}=0
. The
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
-rational affine points of
\Gamma
correspond to the
i^{2}-i
non-diagonal pairs for each
v\in C_{i}
. Thus, the number of affine rational points is:

\#\Gamma(\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}})_{\text{aff}}=\sum_{i}(i^{2}-i)\#C_{i}.

Note that

\frac{\#\Gamma(\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}})_{\text{aff}}}{d}\leq\sum_{i\geq 2}(i-1)\#C_{i}\leq\frac{\#\Gamma(\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}})_{\text{aff}}}{2}

and

C_{1}+\sum_{i\geq 2}iC_{i}=2^{m}.

Thus

\displaystyle C_{1}+\sum_{i\geq 2}C_{i}

\displaystyle=2^{m}-\sum_{i\geq 2}iC_{i}+C_{2}+\cdots C_{d}

\displaystyle=2^{m}-\sum_{i\geq 2}(i-1)C_{i}\leq 2^{m}-\frac{\#\Gamma(\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}})_{\text{aff}}}{d},

which shows that
C_{0}=2^{m}-\sum_{i\geq 1}C_{i}\geq\frac{\#\Gamma(\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}})_{\text{aff}}}{d}
. Now, to get a lower bound on
\#\Gamma(\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}})_{\text{aff}}
, it is enough to observe that
\Gamma
has degree
d
and then its genus
g
satisfies
2g\leq(d-1)(d-2)
. Applying the Hasse-Weil bound and accounting for the points at infinity (at most
d
), we have:

\#\Gamma(\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}})_{\text{aff}}\geq 2^{m}+1-(d-1)(d-2)2^{m/2}-d,

and thus

C_{0}\geq\frac{2^{m}+1-(d-1)(d-2)2^{m/2}-d}{d},

which completes the proof.
∎

4
Diagonal equivalence to the Li–Kaleyski representatives

We continue by asking when the generalized families
G_{a}
and
H_{a}
are
equivalent to the Li–Kaleyski representatives

F_{1}=G_{1}
and
F_{2}=H_{1}
.
Recall that two functions
F,G\colon\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{3}\to\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{3}
are

affinely equivalent
if there exist affine bijections

A_{1}(\mathbf{u})=L_{1}(\mathbf{u})+c_{1}
and

A_{2}(\mathbf{x})=L_{2}(\mathbf{x})+c_{2}
such that

G=A_{1}\circ F\circ A_{2}
. If
c_{1}=c_{2}=0
, this reduces to

linear equivalence
. In the present section we do not attempt to solve
the full affine-equivalence problem for the families
G_{a}
and
H_{a}
.
Instead, we restrict to
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
-linear maps and, within that class, to the
diagonal subclass. This is natural because both families are built from
coordinatewise monomials of the form
x_{i}^{q}x_{j}
, so diagonal scalings are the
first symmetries to test; moreover, this already suffices to show that many
good parameters yield functions inequivalent to the corresponding
Li–Kaleyski representatives.

Accordingly, throughout this section we consider diagonal bijections

A_{1}(u,v,w)=(\mu u,\nu v,\rho w),\qquad A_{2}(x,y,z)=(\lambda_{1}x,\lambda_{2}y,\lambda_{3}z),

with
\mu,\nu,\rho,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
.

Remark 4.1

(Affine versus linear equivalence)
.

Two functions
F,G\colon\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{3}\to\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{3}
are
affinely equivalent
if

G=A_{1}\circ F\circ A_{2}
for affine bijections
A_{j}(\mathbf{x})=L_{j}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{c}_{j}
.
Since
G_{a}(\mathbf{0})=H_{a}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}
for all
a
, any affine equivalence

G_{1}=A_{1}\circ G_{a}\circ A_{2}
imposes constraints on the translation parts
\mathbf{c}_{1},\mathbf{c}_{2}
.
We show here that these translations must vanish, reducing affine equivalence to linear equivalence.

Indeed,
G_{1}
is homogeneous of degree
q+1
(algebraic degree 2 over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
),
so it contains no linear or degree-
q
terms.
Consider the expansion of
G_{a}(N\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}_{2})
.
Terms of the form
(x_{i}+c_{2i})^{q+1}
expand to
x_{i}^{q+1}+x_{i}^{q}c_{2i}+x_{i}c_{2i}^{q}+c_{2i}^{q+1}
.
The presence of
x_{i}^{q}c_{2i}
(degree
q
) and
x_{i}c_{2i}^{q}
(degree 1) implies that
if
\mathbf{c}_{2}\neq\mathbf{0}
, the composition
A_{1}(G_{a}(A_{2}(\mathbf{x})))
would contain
linear and degree-
q
terms that cannot be cancelled by
G_{1}(\mathbf{x})
.

Explicitly, collecting the coefficients of the linear terms in
\mathbf{y}=N\mathbf{x}

from the expansion of
G_{a}(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{c}_{2})
yields the system

\begin{pmatrix}c_{21}^{q}&c_{23}^{q}&ac_{21}^{q}\\
0&c_{22}^{q}&c_{21}^{q}\\
c_{22}^{q}&0&ac_{22}^{q}+c_{23}^{q}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{y}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

For this to hold for all
\mathbf{y}
, we must have
c_{21}=c_{22}=c_{23}=0
, i.e.,
\mathbf{c}_{2}=\mathbf{0}
.
Substituting
\mathbf{c}_{2}=\mathbf{0}
into the constant terms gives

L_{1}(G_{a}(\mathbf{0}))+\mathbf{c}_{1}=\mathbf{0}
. Since
G_{a}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}
, this forces
\mathbf{c}_{1}=\mathbf{0}
.
An analogous argument holds for the family
H_{a}
.
Thus, we restrict our attention to
linear
equivalences (
A_{1},A_{2}
linear) and,
within that class, to the diagonal subclass.

The main point is that both families lead to the same numerical condition on
the parameter
a
.

Remark 4.4

(CCZ, EA, and EL Equivalence for Quadratic APN Functions)
.

For general
(n,n)
-functions, the equivalence notions satisfy the strict hierarchy

\text{EL}\;\Rightarrow\;\text{EA}\;\Rightarrow\;\text{CCZ},

where EL (extended linear,
\sim_{\mathrm{EL}}
), EA (extended affine,
\sim_{\mathrm{EA}}
), and CCZ (Carlet–Charpin–Zinoviev,
\sim_{\mathrm{CCZ}}
) equivalence are defined as in
[
12
]
. However, for
quadratic
APN functions with
F(\mathbf{0})=G(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}
, these notions
coincide
. Specifically:

(i)

By
[
12
, Proposition 1]
(attributed to Yoshiara
[
15
]
), for quadratic APN functions on
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
with
n\geq 2
, CCZ-equivalence implies EA-equivalence. Since EA always implies CCZ, we have

F\sim_{\mathrm{CCZ}}G\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F\sim_{\mathrm{EA}}G.

(ii)

By
[
12
, Proposition 2]
(attributed to Kaspers–Zhou
[
16
]
), if
F
and
G
are EA-equivalent quadratic functions with
F(\mathbf{0})=G(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}
, then they are EL-equivalent. Thus

F\sim_{\mathrm{EA}}G\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F\sim_{\mathrm{EL}}G.

Combining (i) and (ii), for the APN members of our families
G_{a}
and
H_{a}
(which are quadratic and satisfy
G_{a}(\mathbf{0})=H_{a}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}
), we obtain

F\sim_{\mathrm{CCZ}}G\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F\sim_{\mathrm{EA}}G\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F\sim_{\mathrm{EL}}G.

Therefore, any CCZ-invariant that separates two maps also proves they are not EL-equivalent, and conversely, proving EL-inequivalence suffices to establish CCZ-inequivalence.

Furthermore, by
[
12
, Theorem 8]
, for
(q,q,q)
-triprojective polynomial triples with
m>2
,
m\neq 4,6
, and
7\nmid m
, any EL-equivalence mapping must be
monomial
(a composition of coordinate permutations, diagonal scalings, and Frobenius twists). This dramatically restricts the search space for equivalence maps. Our Theorem
4.2
establishes that
G_{a}
is
diagonally
equivalent to
G_{1}
if and only if
a^{q^{2}+q+1}=1
. For
m=5
(
q=2
), this condition holds only for
a=1
(since
\gcd(7,31)=1
), yet Table
1
shows 11 good parameters yield APN permutations. Computational verification confirms that no
non-diagonal
monomial maps exist between
G_{a}
and
G_{1}
for the remaining 10 good parameters with
a^{7}\neq 1
. Consequently, these 10 functions are EL-inequivalent (hence CCZ-inequivalent) to the Li–Kaleyski representative
G_{1}
. An analogous statement holds for the family
H_{a}
.

We now address the natural question of whether any member of the
G_{a}
-family can be EL-equivalent to any member of the
H_{b}
-family
for the same Frobenius parameter
q
.
(Note that Theorem 11 of
[
12
]
shows

G_{1}(q)\sim_{\mathrm{EL}}H_{1}(q^{-1})
with
different
parameters; the same-
q
question is entirely distinct.)

5
Conclusion and open problems

We established a root-theoretic characterization of the generalized families
G_{a}
and
H_{a}
on
\mathbb{F}_{2^{3m}}
, with
q=2^{i}
,
\gcd(i,m)=1
, and
m
odd. For
G_{a}
, the permutation property is equivalent to the absence of roots in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
of the associated polynomial
Q_{a}
, and this root condition is also equivalent to the APN property (Theorem
2.7
). We also obtained a quantitative lower bound on the number of good parameters
a
(Theorem
2.12
), and in the binary case
q=2
with
7\nmid m
we showed that
a=1
is good. For
H_{a}
, we obtained the analogous root criterion and proved that its one-variable condition is root-equivalent to that of
G_{a}
; hence the same good parameters yield APN permutations in both families.

First,
G_{a}
(resp.
H_{a}
) is diagonally equivalent to the Li–Kaleyski representative
G_{1}
(resp.
H_{1}
) of
[
10
]
if and only if
a^{q^{2}+q+1}=1
(Theorem
4.2
); for
m>4
,
m\neq 6
,
7\nmid m
, diagonal non-equivalence implies CCZ non-equivalence via
[
12
]
, and since
\gcd(7,2^{m}-1)=1
when
q=2
and
7\nmid m
, every good parameter
a\neq 1
yields APN permutations CCZ-inequivalent to those of
[
10
]
. Second, for
m>4
,
m\neq 6
, and
7\nmid m
, no member of the
G_{a}
-family is CCZ-equivalent to any member of the
H_{b}
-family for the same
q
(Theorem
4.5
). The two families therefore constitute genuinely new, mutually inequivalent infinite sources of APN permutations on
\mathbb{F}_{2^{3m}}
.

We close with a list of open problems:

1.

For
q=2
and odd
m
with
7\mid m
: find an explicit good
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
(i.e.,
a\neq 1
with
Q_{a}
root-free). Computational data shows they exist in abundance; an algebraic characterization of the good
a
set in terms of the norm
N_{\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{2}}(a)
is desirable.

2.

The Type I permutations (
a_{1}=a_{4}=0
) found computationally for
m=3
: do they extend to an infinite family for larger
m
? Are any of them APN?

3.

The trace criterion (Lemma
2.4
) fails for even
m
. Does an analogue of Theorem
2.7
hold in even dimension, possibly with a different characterizing polynomial?

4.

Do analogous
n
-variate constructions (
n>3
) yield APN permutations, and can the polynomial root criterion be generalized to that setting?

\theta^{q^{3}+q^{2}+q}=\theta^{q^{2}+q}
. Then
\theta^{q^{2}+q}=a^{q}\theta^{q}+1
. Combined with
\theta^{q^{2}+q+1}=a\theta+1
we get
\theta\cdot(a^{q}\theta^{q}+1)=a\theta+1
, i.e.,
a^{q}\theta^{q+1}+\theta=a\theta+1
, i.e.,
a^{q}\theta^{q+1}+\theta+a\theta+1=0
. This is a polynomial equation of degree
q+1=3
in
\theta
, which may or may not have solutions. A direct check over
\mathbb{F}_{8}
(which has characteristic 2 and
|\mathbb{F}_{8}^{*}|=7
) confirms that
Q_{a}(T)
has no roots in
\mathbb{F}_{8}
for any
a\in\mathbb{F}_{8}^{*}
, so all 7 values are good.

Case
m=5
:
Exactly 11 of 31 elements
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{5}}^{*}
are good; the remaining 20 have
Q_{a}
with 1 or 3 roots in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{5}}
. The value
a=1
is good (confirming the
\gcd(5,7)=1
condition).
