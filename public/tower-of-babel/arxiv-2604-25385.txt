On the Relation Between Field-Level Posteriors, Correlators, and their Likelihoods
Fabian Schmidt

Abstract
We develop a field-level posterior for cosmological data by marginalizing over initial conditions and noise in a general forward model. While our focus is on large-scale structure data, the results generalize to any weakly non-Gaussian observable. Moreover, the construction is non-perturbative with respect to the forward model and applies equally well to perturbative calculations, simulation-based predictions, and more general effective descriptions. Expanding the FLP around its Gaussian limit, we derive a general expression for the Fisher matrix and reorganize the field-level information into contributions associated with the connected correlators of the evolved field. This makes explicit which terms are captured by likelihood analyses based on the power spectrum, the bispectrum, or finite sets of summary statistics, and which are lost under compression. We recover the standard Gaussian-covariance result for the power spectrum, show that the Gaussian bispectrum likelihood reproduces the corresponding field-level contribution, and show how cross-covariances among summaries progressively reconstruct more of the full field-level information. As an application to the BAO scale, we show how the field contains all the information required for its optimal reconstruction in the presence of noise, and identify the contributions in the FLP needed to attain this limit. We also show that the reconstruction of the initial field arises naturally as a byproduct of our approach, yielding the optimal estimate of the initial conditions given the data and the noise. Our results provide a unified framework to compare field-level and correlator-based inference, to quantify the information loss induced by compression, and to explore the role of stochasticity.

Introduction

The statistical analysis of large-scale structure is often formulated in terms of summary statistics, most prominently the power spectrum and the bispectrum. This strategy is attractive both conceptually and computationally: it organizes the information into a small set of observables and allows one to build likelihoods directly for the corresponding estimators. At the same time, any such compression raises a basic question: which part of the information contained in the full field is retained, and which part is discarded? Answering this question systematically is becoming increasingly important as galaxy surveys move deeper into the mildly nonlinear regime and as forward models become more accurate and versatile.

Field-level inference offers a natural framework in which to pose this question. Instead of compressing the data at the outset, one writes a probabilistic description directly for the observed field, given a forward model and the relevant noise properties. In principle, this gives access to the full information content of the data. In practice, however, the field-level approach is often viewed as tied either to specific perturbative descriptions, explicit reconstruction algorithms, or high-dimensional numerical implementations whose relation to more standard correlator-based analyses is not always transparent.

In this paper we develop a field-level posterior (FLP) for large-scale structure that makes this connection explicit, by directly expanding the FLP in terms of expectation values of correlators (see

Marginalizing over these latent variables yields a posterior directly for the evolved field. The construction is non-perturbative with respect to the forward model itself and therefore applies equally well when the correlators entering the description are obtained from perturbation theory, effective models, simulations, or more general data-driven approaches.

The main conceptual step is to expand the FLP around its Gaussian limit. Here,
“Gaussian” refers to the statistics of the field, and does not necessarily imply linear-order perturbation theory as usually assumed in cosmology. That is,
the Gaussian part in principle already contains the fully nonlinear two-point information, while departures from Gaussianity are organized in terms of connected higher-order correlators. An example where such a reordering is beneficial is thermal field theory, where the thermal correction to the mass can be absorbed at all orders in a redefined free theory (see the discussion in Sect.
2.2
).

Our expansion allows us to derive a general expression for the field-level Fisher matrix and to rewrite it as a series of contributions associated with the connected correlators of the evolved field.
This organization makes it possible to compare field-level and correlator-based inference on the same footing. We show explicitly which terms in the field-level Fisher information are reproduced by likelihoods based on the power spectrum or the bispectrum, and how including cross-covariances among summary statistics
is important for consistency, i.e. to recover the correct match of information with the FLP.
In this sense, the FLP provides a unified language for quantifying the information loss induced by compression and for identifying which ingredients are needed for a given set of summaries to reproduce the field-level result.

A further motivation for this work is that the FLP also clarifies the status of “reconstruction,” or inference of the initial matter density field given the data. As an application, we discuss the baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) scale and show how the full field contains the information needed for its optimal reconstruction in the presence of noise. Within the same formalism, the reconstruction of the initial field appears naturally as a byproduct. It is simply the optimal estimate of the initial conditions given the data and the noise model, thereby providing a direct bridge between field-level inference, correlator likelihoods, and reconstruction-based intuition.

2
The field-level posterior

Our goal is to compute the field-level posterior,

\displaystyle{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]=

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}\delta_{\rm in}\,{\cal D}\epsilon\;\tilde{\delta}_{D}\left[\delta_{d}-\bar{\delta}[\delta_{\rm in},\epsilon]\right]\,{\cal P}[\delta_{\rm in},\,\epsilon]\,,

(2.1)

where

{\cal P}[\delta_{\rm in},\epsilon]
denotes the joint probability distribution for the field initial conditions and the noise (see
[
32
]
for a derivation of why a single noise field is sufficient to describe a single tracer in the EFTofLSS). We consider a finite cubic grid of size
L
and
N_{\rm g}^{3}
grid-node positions
{\bf{x}}_{i}
.
Our field variables are
dimensionless
fields in Fourier space,

\delta_{\rm in}({\bf{m}})\equiv\frac{1}{N_{\rm g}^{3}}\sum_{i}e^{-i{\bf{k}}_{\bf{m}}\cdot{\bf{x}}_{i}}\delta_{\rm in}({\bf{x}}_{i})\,,

(2.2)

with
\delta_{\rm in}({\bf{x}}_{i})
the configuration-space field, and analogous definition for the noise field in Fourier space,
\epsilon({\bf{m}})
. The wavevector
{\bf{k}}_{\bf{m}}
is expressed in terms of the fundamental grid mode
k_{f}\equiv 2\pi/L
as

{\bf{k}}_{\bf{m}}\equiv k_{f}\,{\bf{m}}\,,

(2.3)

where

{\bf{m}}=(m_{x},m_{y},m_{z}),\qquad{\rm and}\qquad m_{x,y,z}=-\frac{N_{\rm g}}{2}+1,\cdots,\frac{N_{\rm g}}{2}\,,

(2.4)

excluding the zero mode
m_{x}=m_{y}=m_{z}=0
. Note that the grid size
N_{\rm g}
provides a UV cutoff for all fields,
k_{\rm uv}\sim(N_{\rm g}/2)k_{f}
.
We will assume that the model has been properly renormalized, so that all physical observables, more specifically, all correlators of the field
\bar{\delta}[\delta_{\rm in},\epsilon]
, become independent of
N_{\rm g}
on the scales of interest
(see below), or alternatively, that the model considered has a physical UV cutoff.
1

1

Note that the forward model might internally use a larger grid than
N_{\rm g}
, for example to avoid aliasing. Here, we set the initial conditions grid equal to the UV cutoff to avoid unnecessary notational complications.

The corresponding integration measures are therefore defined as

{\cal D}\delta_{\rm in}\equiv\Pi_{{\bf{m}}}^{N_{\rm g}}\,d\delta_{\rm in}({\bf{m}})\,,\qquad{\cal D}\epsilon\equiv\Pi_{{\bf{m}}}^{N_{\rm g}}\,d\epsilon({\bf{m}})\,,

(2.5)

where the product is taken over the range (
2.4
).

\bar{\delta}[\delta_{\rm in},\epsilon]({\bf{m}})
represents the field generated by a forward model at redshift
z
, evolved from the initial conditions
\delta_{\rm in}({\bf{m}})
defined at an early time
z_{\rm in}
and incorporating the stochastic noise field
\epsilon({\bf{m}})
, while
\delta_{d}({\bf{m}})
is the data field, to be compared to the model. We restrict this comparison to modes with

|{\bf{k}}_{\bf{m}}|\leq k_{\mathrm{max}}\,.

(2.6)

While
k_{\mathrm{max}}\leq k_{\rm uv}
is a physical requirement, in our derivation we need to make no further assumptions about a hierarchy between these two scales.
Finally,
\tilde{\delta}_{D}\left[\varphi\right]
indicates a functional delta function which enforces the
k_{\mathrm{max}}
cut, defined as

\tilde{\delta}_{D}\left[\varphi\right]\equiv\Pi_{m}^{m_{\rm max}}\,\delta_{D}(\varphi({\bf{n}}))\,,

(2.7)

where the product of ordinary delta functions is taken over the wavenumbers satisfying Eq. (
2.6
).

To disentangle the part of the FLP that originates from the model, we first recast (
2.1
) in the form

\displaystyle{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]=

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}\delta_{\rm M}\,{\cal D}\delta_{\rm in}\,{\cal D}\epsilon\;\tilde{\delta}_{D}\left[\delta_{d}-\delta_{\rm M}\right]\,\tilde{\delta}_{D}\left[\delta_{\rm M}-\bar{\delta}[\delta_{\rm in},\epsilon]\right]\,\,{\cal P}[\delta_{\rm in},\,\epsilon]\,,

(2.8)

where the measure of the auxiliary field
\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}})
is

{\cal D}\delta_{\rm M}\equiv\Pi_{m}^{m_{\rm max}}\,d\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}})\,,

(2.9)

with the product taken over the range (
2.6
), again excluding the zero mode (equivalently, this field could also be defined with the full measure up to
N_{\rm g}
). At several points it is useful to distinguish clearly among the three fields appearing in the construction:
\delta_{d}
denotes the observed data field,
\delta_{\rm M}
the auxiliary nonlinear model field, and
\delta_{\rm in}
the initial field. We then express the first functional delta as

\tilde{\delta}_{D}\left[\delta_{d}-\delta_{\rm M}\right]=\int{\cal D}J_{f}\;e^{iJ_{f}\cdot\left(\delta_{d}-\delta_{\rm M}\right)},

(2.10)

with

{\cal D}J_{f}\equiv\Pi_{m}^{m_{\rm max}}\;\frac{dJ({\bf{m}})}{2\pi}\,,\qquad J_{f}\cdot\delta\equiv\sum_{m}^{m_{\rm max}}J_{f}({\bf{m}})\delta({\bf{m}})\,.

(2.11)

Note that the support of
J_{f}
is limited to those modes for which data and model are compared, i.e. Eq. (
2.6
).
The FLP is then the functional Fourier transform of the generating functional,

{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]=\int{\cal D}J_{f}\;e^{iJ_{f}\cdot\delta_{d}}Z\left[J_{f}\right]\,,

(2.12)

where

Z\left[J_{f}\right]\equiv\int{\cal D}\delta_{\rm M}\,e^{-iJ_{f}\cdot\delta_{\rm M}}{\cal P}_{M}[\delta_{\rm M}]\,,

(2.13)

with

{\cal P}_{M}[\delta_{\rm M}]\equiv\int{\cal D}\delta_{\rm in}\,{\cal D}\epsilon\;\,\tilde{\delta}_{D}\left[\delta_{\rm M}-\bar{\delta}[\delta_{\rm in},\epsilon]\right]\,{\cal P}[\delta_{\rm in},\epsilon]\,.

(2.14)

It is straightforward to verify directly from Eq. (
2.1
) that

{\cal P}_{M}[\delta]={\cal L}[\delta]\,.

(2.15)

Nonetheless, we find it conceptually clearer to work first with the theoretical object
Z\left[J_{f}\right]
, evaluate it for a chosen forward model, and only at the end relate it to the data likelihood through Eq. (
2.12
).

2.1
Expansion in connected functions

We define the generating functional,

W[J_{f}]\equiv\ln Z[J_{f}]\,,

(2.16)

from which we obtain the connected correlators of the evolved model field
\delta_{\rm M}
, including noise contributions,

i^{j}\left.\frac{\delta^{j}W[J_{f}]}{\delta J_{f}({\bf{m}}_{1})\cdots\delta J_{f}({\bf{m}}_{j})}\right|_{J_{f}=0}=\langle\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}}_{1})\cdots\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}}_{j})\rangle_{c}\equiv W_{j}({\bf{m}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{m}}_{j})\,.

(2.17)

In this definition, we absorb into the dimensionless correlator
W_{j}
the momentum-conserving factor
L^{3}\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{1,\cdots,j}}
, which equals
L^{3}
for
{\bf{m}}_{1,\cdots,j}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{j}{\bf{m}}_{i}=0
and vanishes otherwise, as a consequence of translational invariance. This implies
W_{1}({\bf{m}})=\langle\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}})\rangle=0
. For
j=2
, we use the notation

W_{2}({\bf{m}})\equiv W_{2}({\bf{m}},-{\bf{m}})\,.

(2.18)

The relation between our dimensionless correlators and the dimensional ones is given in Appendix
A
(see Eqs. (
A.5
) and (
A.6
)).

The normalization of
{\cal P}_{M}[\delta_{\rm M}]
ensures
Z[0]=1
and therefore
W[0]=0
. We can then express
Z[J_{f}]
as

Z[J_{f}]=e^{W[J_{f}]}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}J_{f}\cdot W_{2}\cdot J_{f}}\;e^{V_{I}[J_{f}]}

(2.19)

where we isolate the term quadratic in the source,

J_{f}\cdot W_{2}\cdot J_{f}\equiv\sum_{m}^{m_{\rm max}}J_{f}({\bf{m}})W_{2}({\bf{m}})J_{f}(-{\bf{m}})\,,

(2.20)

and expand the rest as

\displaystyle V_{I}[J_{f}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=3}^{\infty}\frac{(-i)^{j}}{j!}\sum_{m_{1},\cdots,m_{j}}^{m_{\rm max}}\,W_{j}({\bf{m}}_{1},\cdots{\bf{m}}_{j})J_{f}({\bf{m}}_{1})\cdots J_{f}({\bf{m}}_{j})\,,

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{j=3}^{\infty}\frac{(-i)^{j}}{j!}W_{j}\cdot J_{f}^{j}\,,

(2.21)

where the second line defines a short-hand notation we will use in the following, where contraction over
j
implies sum over
\{{\bf{m}}_{1},\cdots{\bf{m}}_{j}\}
.
Inserting Eq. (
2.19
) into Eq. (
2.12
) and taking the interaction term outside the functional integral, we obtain

\displaystyle{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]

\displaystyle=\int{\cal D}J_{f}\,e^{iJ_{f}\cdot\delta_{d}}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}J_{f}\cdot W_{2}\cdot J_{f}}\;e^{V_{I}[J_{f}]}

\displaystyle=e^{V_{I}\left[-i\frac{\delta}{\delta\delta_{d}}\right]}{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]\,,

(2.22)

where the Gaussian likelihood is

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\rm g}[\delta_{d}]

\displaystyle\equiv\int{\cal D}J_{f}\,e^{iJ_{f}\cdot\delta_{d}}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}J_{f}\cdot W_{2}\cdot J_{f}}

\displaystyle=\det\left[2\pi W_{2}\right]^{-1/2}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{d}\cdot W_{2}^{-1}\cdot\delta_{d}}\,,

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}^{m_{\rm max}}\left[\frac{\left|\delta_{d}({\bf{m}})\right|^{2}}{W_{2}({\bf{m}})}+\log\left(2\pi W_{2}({\bf{m}})\right)\right]\right\}\,,

(2.23)

and the non-Gaussian part is encoded in the operator

\displaystyle V_{I}\left[-i\frac{\delta}{\delta\delta_{d}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=3}^{\infty}\frac{\left(-1\right)^{j}}{j!}\sum_{m_{1},\cdots,m_{j}}W_{j}({\bf{m}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{m}}_{j})\frac{\delta^{j}}{\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{m}}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{m}}_{j})}\,\,,

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{j=3}^{\infty}\frac{\left(-1\right)^{j}}{j!}W_{j}\cdot\frac{\delta^{j}}{\delta\delta_{d}^{j}}\,.

(2.24)

Eqs. (
2.22
) and (
2.23
)
display how the information about the model, encoded in its connected correlators
W_{j}
, is propagated to the posterior probability of the field after marginalizing over both the initial conditions and the noise realizations.
Note that
W_{2}
represents the fully nonlinear power spectrum of the forward model, including the contribution from noise, and similarly
W_{3}
,
W_{4}
, etc. correspond to the fully nonlinear bispectrum, trispectrum, and higher-order spectra of the model. Up to this stage, no specific assumption about the forward model has been imposed, apart from translational invariance of the field
\delta_{d}
.
Although Eq. (
2.22
) is exact and non-perturbative, it naturally motivates an expansion in powers of the non-Gaussian contribution
V_{I}
, and, within that, an expansion in connected correlators starting from
j=3
. In what follows, we derive this double expansion.

2.2
Expansion in
V_{I}

We expand Eq. (
2.22
)
as

\displaystyle{\cal L}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(V_{I})^{k}}{k!}{\cal L}_{g}={\cal L}_{g}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\cal L}_{g}^{-1}\frac{(V_{I})^{k}}{k!}{\cal L}_{g}\,.

(2.25)

The
k
th term in the expansion is

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{g}^{-1}\frac{(V_{I})^{k}}{k!}{\cal L}_{g}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{k}=3}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}}}{j_{1}!\cdots j_{k}!}{\cal L}_{g}^{-1}\left(W_{j_{1}}\cdot\frac{\delta^{j_{1}}}{\delta\delta_{d}^{j_{1}}}\right)\cdots\left(W_{j_{k}}\cdot\frac{\delta^{j_{k}}}{\delta\delta_{d}^{j_{k}}}\right){\cal L}_{g}\,,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{k}=3}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j_{1}!\cdots j_{k}!}\left[W_{j_{1}}\cdots W_{j_{k}}\right]\cdot{\cal H}_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}}\,,

(2.26)

where we have introduced the notation

\displaystyle\left[W_{j_{1}}\cdots W_{j_{k}}\right]

\displaystyle\cdot{\cal H}_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{n_{1}^{1},\cdots,n^{1}_{j_{1}}}^{m_{\rm max}}W_{j_{1}}({\bf{n}}^{1}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{1}_{j_{1}})\cdots\sum_{n_{1}^{k},\cdots,n_{j_{k}}^{k}}^{m_{\rm max}}W_{j_{k}}({\bf{n}}^{k}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{j_{k}}^{k})

\displaystyle\qquad\times{\cal H}_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}}({\bf{n}}^{1}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{1}_{j_{1}},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{k}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{k}_{j_{k}})\,,

(2.27)

and the
{\cal H}_{j}[\delta_{d}]
’s are the functional generalizations of the probabilists’ Hermite polynomials,

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{j}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{j})

\displaystyle\equiv(-1)^{j}\,{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]^{-1}\frac{\delta^{j}}{\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{n}}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{n}}_{j})}{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]\,.

(2.28)

{\cal H}_{j}[\delta_{d}]
is a
j
-th order polynomial in the field
\delta_{d}
, whose coefficients are completely determined by
W_{2}({\bf{m}})
.
The first polynomials are

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{0}[\delta_{d}]=1\,

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{1}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{m}})=\frac{\delta_{d}(-{\bf{m}})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}})}\,,

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{2}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{m}}_{1},{\bf{m}}_{2})=\frac{\delta_{d}(-{\bf{m}}_{1})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{1})}\frac{\delta_{d}(-{\bf{m}}_{2})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{2})}-\frac{\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{1}+{\bf{m}}_{2}}}{W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{1})}\,,

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{3}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{m}}_{1},{\bf{m}}_{2},{\bf{m}}_{3})=\frac{\delta_{d}(-{\bf{m}}_{1})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{1})}\frac{\delta_{d}(-{\bf{m}}_{2})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{2})}\frac{\delta_{d}(-{\bf{m}}_{3})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{3})}-\frac{\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{1}+{\bf{m}}_{2}}}{W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{1})}\frac{\delta_{d}(-{\bf{m}}_{3})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{3})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\frac{\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{2}+{\bf{m}}_{3}}}{W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{2})}\frac{\delta_{d}(-{\bf{m}}_{1})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{1})}-\frac{\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{3}+{\bf{m}}_{1}}}{W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{3})}\frac{\delta_{d}(-{\bf{m}}_{2})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{2})}\,.

(2.29)

Some useful properties of the
{\cal H}_{j}[\delta_{d}]
’s are collected in Appendix
B
.

In conclusion, the FLP (
2.22
), expanded as in Eqs. (
2.25
) and (
2.26
), takes the form

\displaystyle{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]=

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{k}=3}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j_{1}!\cdots j_{k}!}\left[W_{j_{1}}\cdots W_{j_{k}}\right]\cdot{\cal H}[\delta_{d}]_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}}\right)\,,

(2.30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{N=3}^{\infty}\frac{1}{N!}\left[\tilde{W}_{N}^{k}\right]\cdot\mathcal{H}_{N}[\delta_{d}]\right)\,,

(2.31)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{W}_{N}^{k}({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{N})\equiv\sum_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{k}=3}^{\infty}\frac{N!}{j_{1}!\cdots j_{k}!}\,\delta_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}-N}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times W_{j_{1}}({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{j_{1}})\cdots W_{j_{k}}({\bf{n}}_{N-j_{k}+1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{N})\,.

(2.32)

Equation (
2.31
) expresses the likelihood as a series expansion in Hermite functionals of increasing order, which is formally analogous to a functional Edgeworth expansion

. Such expansions are known to fail to preserve the positive definiteness of the PDF at extreme field values. In our setting, however, this approximation to the FLP remains well motivated because our primary aim is to compare field-level and correlator-based analyses in a regime where both are trustworthy, namely the regime where the higher cumulants of the FLP are small.

The expansion introduced here differs from the purely perturbative one because, at zeroth order, the likelihood is Gaussian but
W_{2}
is identified with the fully nonlinear power spectrum, rather than with its linear counterpart. Within our framework, this choice emerges quite naturally and effectively corresponds to absorbing the complete non-Gaussian correction to the quadratic piece of the log-likelihood—i.e., the nonlinear power spectrum—into
{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]
. To begin with, as demonstrated in Appendix
F
, employing the fully nonlinear
W_{2}
in
{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]
yields a Gaussian likelihood for the power spectrum that is centered on the nonlinear (instead of the linear) power spectrum, with a ‘Gaussian’ covariance of
2W_{2}^{2}
, where
W_{2}
is the nonlinear kernel. Secondly, this procedure is entirely standard in Quantum Field Theory (QFT) whenever the physical degrees of freedom of interest differ from those of the non-interacting theory. As an illustration, take a scalar QFT in a thermal environment. A scalar field with bare mass
\mu
and self-coupling
\lambda
at temperature
T
acquires a thermal mass correction,
\mu^{2}\to\mu^{2}+\lambda T^{2}/24
, due to forward scattering on the particles in the heat bath. It is then advantageous to rewrite the potential as

\displaystyle V(\phi)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}\phi^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{4}\phi^{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}\phi^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{24}T^{2}\right)\phi^{2}

\displaystyle\;-\frac{\lambda}{24}T^{2}\phi^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{4}\phi^{4}\,,

(2.33)

where the first term in the last expression defines the new free (Gaussian) theory, and the remaining terms now play the role of the interaction, to be handled perturbatively.
Although, to all orders, this reorganization is equivalent to the standard perturbative expansion in powers of
\lambda
, it is significantly more efficient because it better captures the actual physical degrees of freedom.

Coming back to our setting, a key question will be precisely to compare the expansion around the Gaussian FLP with a nonlinear power spectrum to the corresponding SPT expansion.

Our goal is to confront the FLP with likelihoods constructed from various summary statistics (see Sect.
4
), all developed within the same theoretical framework and truncated at the same order in the correlators
W_{j}
, in order to assess how efficiently each method can extract information from the data. The Fisher matrix formalism offers a natural and self-consistent framework for carrying out this comparison.

3
Field-level Fisher matrix

We now derive the Fisher matrix associated with the FLP and organize it into contributions labeled by the connected correlators entering the expansion of Sect.
2
.

Consider the first and second derivatives of the likelihood with respect to the model parameters
\alpha_{a}
(
a=1,\cdots,N_{\rm par}
),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{a}}\left(-2\log{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{a}}{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\,,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha_{a}\,\partial\alpha_{b}}\left(-2\log{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{a}}{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{b}}{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]-\frac{1}{{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha_{a}\,\partial\alpha_{b}}{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\,.

(3.2)

We take the average of the above quantities assuming that the data field
\delta_{d}
is distributed according to the fiducial model, using

\langle{\cal F}[\delta_{d}]\rangle_{\rm fid}\equiv\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,{\cal P}_{\rm M,\,fid}[\delta_{d}]\,{\cal F}[\delta_{d}]=\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,{\cal L}_{\rm fid}[\delta_{d}]\,{\cal F}[\delta_{d}]\,,

(3.3)

where
{\cal F}[\delta_{d}]
is a generic functional of
\delta_{d}
,
{\cal P}_{\rm M,\,fid}[\delta_{d}]
is the probability distribution of the field of the fiducial model, and in the last equality we have used Eq. (
2.15
).

The average of (
3.1
) gives

\left\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{a}}\left(-2\log{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\right)\right\rangle_{\rm fid}=-2\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,\frac{{\cal L}_{\rm fid}[\delta_{d}]}{{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{a}}{\cal L}[\delta_{d}].

(3.4)

When the model of the likelihood coincides with the fiducial one, that is,
\alpha_{a}=\alpha_{a}^{\rm fid}
for all the parameters, and therefore
{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]={\cal L}_{\rm fid}[\delta_{d}]
, we get

-2\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{a}}{\cal L}_{\rm fid}[\delta_{d}]=-2\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{a}}\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,{\cal L}_{\rm fid}[\delta_{d}]=0\,,

(3.5)

where the last equality comes from the normalization condition
\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,{\cal L}_{\rm fid}[\delta_{d}]=1
. Similarly, from (
3.2
) we get the Fisher matrix element,

F_{ab}=\left\langle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha_{a}\,\partial\alpha_{b}}\left(-2\log{\cal L}_{\rm fid}[\delta_{d}]\right)\right\rangle_{\rm fid}=\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,\frac{1}{{\cal L}_{\rm fid}[\delta_{d}]}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{a}}{\cal L}_{\rm fid}[\delta_{d}]\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{b}}{\cal L}_{\rm fid}[\delta_{d}]\,,

(3.6)

where the contribution from the second term in (
3.2
) vanishes again by normalization. Equation (
3.6
) thus expresses the Fisher matrix as a functional integral of the FLP and its first derivatives, evaluated on the fiducial values of the model parameters.

Using the first line of Eq. (
2.22
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{a}}{\cal L}_{\rm fid}[\delta_{d}]=

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}J_{f}\,e^{iJ_{f}\cdot\delta_{d}-\frac{1}{2}J_{f}\cdot W^{\rm fid}_{2}\cdot J_{f}+V^{\rm fid}_{I}[J_{f}]}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{(-i)^{j}}{j!}\sum_{{\bf{m}}_{1}\,\cdots,{\bf{m}}_{j}}^{m_{\rm max}}\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{1,\cdots,j}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times J_{f}({\bf{m}}_{1})\cdots J_{f}({\bf{m}}_{j})\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{a}}W^{\rm fid}_{j}({\bf{m}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{m}}_{j})\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\sum_{m_{1}\,\cdots,m_{j}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{\delta^{j}{\cal L}_{\rm fid}[\delta_{d}]}{\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{m}}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{m}}_{j})}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{a}}W^{\rm fid}_{j}({\bf{m}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{m}}_{j})\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{a}}W^{\rm fid}_{j}\cdot\frac{\delta^{j}{\cal L}_{\rm fid}[\delta_{d}]}{\delta\delta_{d}^{j}}\,,

(3.7)

where in the last line we have introduced a more compact notation.

The Fisher matrix (
3.6
) can then be expressed as the sum of contributions from different correlators of the model,

\displaystyle F_{ab}=\sum_{i,j=2}^{\infty}F_{ab}^{(i,j)}\,,

(3.8)

where, noting that the contribution
\propto(\partial^{2}/\partial\alpha_{a}\partial\alpha_{b})W_{j}^{\rm fid}
vanishes under the ensemble average and
using compact notation, we have,

\displaystyle F_{ab}^{(i,j)}\equiv\frac{(-1)^{i+j}}{i!\,j!}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{a}}W^{\rm fid}_{i}\right)\cdot\left(\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,\frac{1}{{\cal L}_{\rm fid}[\delta_{d}]}\frac{\delta^{i}{\cal L}_{\rm fid}[\delta_{d}]}{\delta\delta_{d}^{i}}\frac{\delta^{j}{\cal L}_{\rm fid}[\delta_{d}]}{\delta\delta_{d}^{j}}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{b}}W^{\rm fid}_{j}\right)\,.

(3.9)

Notice that although the full field-level Fisher matrix, Eq. (
3.6
), is explicitly positive definite, this property does not generally hold for its individual components, Eq. (
3.9
). This fact provides a useful diagnostic for assessing the reliability of the correlator expansion of the full FLP. From now on, to avoid clutter, we denote the fiducial FLP simply by
{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]
whenever no confusion can arise.

Power spectrum contribution

W_{2}

i=j=2
in Eq. (

Specifically, this is the Fisher matrix for parameters that only affect the power spectrum

At second order in

is the Gaussian contribution, and

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{W_{4}({\bf{m}}_{1},-{\bf{m}}_{1},{\bf{n}},-{\bf{n}})W_{4}(-{\bf{n}},{\bf{n}},{\bf{m}}_{2},-{\bf{m}}_{2})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{1})^{2}W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{2})^{2}W_{2}({\bf{n}})^{2}}\Bigg]\,,

\displaystyle F_{ab,\text{FLP}}^{(2,2)}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\sum^{m_{\rm max}}_{m_{1},m_{2}}\frac{\partial W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{1})}{\partial\alpha_{a}}\frac{\partial W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{2})}{\partial\alpha_{b}}\frac{1}{W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{1})W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{2})}\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\sum_{j=5}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(j-2)!}\sum_{n_{1}\cdots n_{j-2}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{W_{j}({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{j-2},{\bf{m}}_{1},-{\bf{m}}_{1})W_{j}(-{\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,-{\bf{n}}_{j-2},{\bf{m}}_{2},-{\bf{m}}_{2})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{1})W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{2})W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{1})\cdots W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{j-2})}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=3}^{\infty}\Bigg[\frac{4\,\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{1}+{\bf{m}}_{2}}}{(j-1)!}\sum_{n_{1}\cdots n_{j-1}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{W_{j}({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{j-1},-{\bf{m}}_{1})W_{j}(-{\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,-{\bf{n}}_{j-1},{\bf{m}}_{1})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{1})W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{1})\cdots W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{j-1})}

\displaystyle+\frac{4}{(j-2)!}\sum_{n_{1}\cdots n_{j-2}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{W_{j}({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{j-2},-{\bf{m}}_{1},-{\bf{m}}_{2})W_{j}(-{\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,-{\bf{n}}_{j-2},{\bf{m}}_{1},{\bf{m}}_{2})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{1})W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{2})W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{1})\cdots W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{j-2})}

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{(j-1)!}\sum_{n_{1}\cdots n_{j-1}}^{m_{\rm max}}\Bigg(\frac{W_{j}({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{j-1},-{\bf{m}}_{1})W_{j+2}(-{\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,-{\bf{n}}_{j-1},{\bf{m}}_{1},{\bf{m}}_{2},-{\bf{m}}_{2})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{1})W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{2})W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{1})\cdots W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{j-1})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+({\bf{m}}_{1}\leftrightarrow{\bf{m}}_{2})\Bigg)\Bigg]\Bigg\}\,,

(3.13)

is a genuine new contribution present in the field-level Fisher matrix but not in the one for the power spectrum.

4
The likelihood and Fisher matrix for summary statistics

We now compare the field-level Fisher matrix with the Fisher matrices obtained after compressing the data to finite sets of summary statistics. The aim is to identify which terms in the FLP expansion are retained by a given compression and which are lost.

We denote by
\hat{S}[\delta]
the estimator of a given summary statistic
S
built from the field
\delta({\bf{n}})
. In general,
\hat{S}[\delta]
is a data vector. For correlators, for example, this vector is composed of the different power-spectrum bins, the bispectrum triangles, and so on, if higher-order correlators are included. The associated likelihood can be written compactly as a functional integral over the FLP,

{\cal L}_{S}[\hat{S}_{d}]=\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,\delta_{D}\!\left(\hat{S}_{d}-\hat{S}[\delta_{d}]\right)\,{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\,.

(4.1)

Note that, unlike in (
2.1
), where a functional delta function appears, the delta function in the expression above acts solely on the components of the data vector, rather than on the far more numerous modes or pixels on which the field is defined. Consequently, the mapping imposed by the delta function in Eq. (
4.1
) is highly non-invertible and generally results in a significant compression of the data.

From this likelihood, we introduce the Fisher matrix associated with the summary statistics
S
as

\displaystyle F^{S}_{ab}=

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha_{a}\,\partial\alpha_{b}}\left(-2\log{\cal L}_{S}[\hat{S}_{d}]\right)\right\rangle_{\rm fid}\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}\hat{S}_{d}\,\frac{1}{{\cal L}^{\text{fid}}_{S}[\hat{S}_{d}]}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{a}}{\cal L}^{\text{fid}}_{S}[\hat{S}_{d}]\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{b}}{\cal L}^{\text{fid}}_{S}[\hat{S}_{d}]\,,

(4.2)

where, as in the previous discussion, we omit the superscript “fid” from now on whenever doing so does not lead to confusion.

Using Eq. (
4.1
) we can relate this Fisher matrix to the one at field level. Indeed, the derivative with respect to the model parameter
\alpha_{a}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\cal L}_{S}[\hat{S}_{d}]}{\partial\alpha_{a}}

\displaystyle=\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,\delta_{D}\left(\hat{S}_{d}-\hat{S}[\delta_{d}]\right)\,\frac{\partial{{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]}}{\partial\alpha_{a}}\,,

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\frac{\partial W_{j}}{\partial\alpha_{a}}\cdot\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,\delta_{D}\left(\hat{S}_{d}-\hat{S}[\delta_{d}]\right)\,\frac{\delta^{j}{{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]}}{\delta\delta_{d}^{j}}\,,

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\frac{\partial W_{j}}{\partial\alpha_{a}}\cdot{{\cal L}_{S}^{(j)}[\hat{S}_{d}}]\,,

(4.3)

where

{\cal L}_{S}^{(j)}[\hat{S}_{d}]({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{j})\equiv\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,\delta_{D}\left(\hat{S}_{d}-\hat{S}[\delta_{d}]\right)\,\frac{\delta^{j}{{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]}}{\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{n}}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{n}}_{j})}\,.

(4.4)

We can then express the Fisher matrix (
4.2
) as

\displaystyle F^{S}_{ab}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=2}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{i+j}}{i!j!}\frac{\partial W_{i}}{\partial\alpha_{a}}\cdot\left(\int{\cal D}\hat{S}_{d}\,{\cal L}_{S}^{(i)}[\hat{S}_{d}]\frac{1}{{\cal L}_{S}[\hat{S}_{d}]}{\cal L}_{S}^{(j)}[\hat{S}_{d}]\right)\cdot\frac{\partial W_{j}}{\partial\alpha_{b}}\,.

(4.5)

The structural similarity to Eq. (
3.9
) enables a clear comparison between the information carried by the full field and that encoded in a given summary statistic, represented by the data vector
\hat{S}_{d}
.

4.1
Likelihood for the power spectrum

To proceed, we consider the power spectrum. The estimator for the
i
-th spherical bin in momentum space is

\hat{P}^{i}_{d}=\hat{P}^{i}[\delta_{d}]=\frac{L^{3}}{N_{i}}\sum_{{\bf{n}}\in V_{i}}|\delta_{d}({\bf{n}})|^{2}\,,

(4.6)

where the number of modes contained in the volume
V_{i}
of the bin is given by
3
3

3

Note that only
1/2
of the modes in the Fourier-space volume are actually independent, due to the reality constraint on
\delta_{d}
. In the literature, the Fourier-space volume is also often restricted to the half-space for this reason.

N_{i}=\frac{L^{3}k_{i}^{2}\Delta k}{2\pi^{2}}=4\pi n_{i}^{2}\Delta n\,,

(4.7)

which is typically much larger than unity (it is
O(10^{5})
on the scales of interest for modern galaxy surveys).
Its expectation value can be computed using the FLP, as

\displaystyle\langle\hat{P}^{i}_{d}\rangle=\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\hat{P}^{i}[\delta_{d}]

\displaystyle=\frac{L^{3}}{N_{i}}\sum_{{\bf{n}}\in V_{i}}\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\,|\delta_{d}({\bf{n}})|^{2}\,,

\displaystyle=\frac{L^{3}}{N_{i}}\sum_{{\bf{n}}\in V_{i}}W_{2}(n)\equiv L^{3}W_{2}^{i}\,,

(4.8)

and is given by the average power spectrum in the bin considered. Notice that, in the correlator expansion of the FLP, Eq. (
2.31
), only the Gaussian
\mathcal{L}_{g}
contributes, since

\langle{\cal H}_{N}[\delta_{d}]|\delta_{d}|^{2}\rangle_{g}=0

(4.9)

for any
N\geq 3
, where
\langle\cdots\rangle_{g}
indicates the average with respect to the Gaussian FLP.
The same result is obtained directly from the power-spectrum likelihood, using Eq. (
4.1
),

\displaystyle\langle\hat{P}^{i}_{d}\rangle=\int d\hat{P}^{i}\,{\cal L}_{P^{i}}[\hat{P}^{i}]\,\hat{P}^{i}

\displaystyle=\int d\hat{P}^{i}\int\,{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,\delta_{D}\left(\hat{P}^{i}-\hat{P}^{i}[\delta_{d}]\right)\,{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\hat{P}^{i}[\delta_{d}],

\displaystyle=\int\,{\cal D}\,\delta_{d}{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\hat{P}^{i}[\delta_{d}]\,,

(4.10)

which is identical to (
4.8
).

Considering
N_{\rm bin}
bins, the power-spectrum estimator becomes a vector of
N_{\rm bin}
elements
\{\hat{P}_{d}^{i}\}
, and its likelihood becomes

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{P}[\hat{P}_{d}]

\displaystyle=\Pi_{i=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}\,{\cal L}_{P^{i}}[\hat{P}_{d}^{i}]\,,

\displaystyle=\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,\Pi_{i=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}\delta_{D}(\hat{P}_{d}^{i}-\hat{P}^{i}[\delta_{d}])\,{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\,,

\displaystyle=\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,\int\left(\Pi_{i=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}\,\frac{dJ_{i}}{2\pi L^{3}}\right)e^{\frac{i}{L^{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}J_{i}\left(\hat{P}_{d}^{i}-\hat{P}^{i}[\delta_{d}]\right)}{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\,,

\displaystyle=\int{\cal D}J\,e^{iJ\cdot\hat{P}_{d}}\,Z_{P}[J]\,,

(4.11)

where we have introduced a vector of sources,
\{J_{i}\}
, and the compact notation

{\cal D}J\equiv\left(\Pi_{i=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}\,\frac{dJ_{i}}{2\pi L^{3}}\right)\,,\quad J\cdot\hat{P}_{d}\equiv\frac{1}{L^{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}J_{i}P_{d}^{i}\,.

(4.12)

We also define the generating functional for the power spectrum,

Z_{P}[J]\equiv e^{W_{P}[J]}\equiv\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,\,e^{-iJ\cdot\hat{P}[\delta_{d}]}\,{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\,.

(4.13)

The first functional derivative gives the expectation value of the power spectrum,

\displaystyle i\left.\frac{\delta W_{P}[J]}{\delta J_{i}}\right|_{J=0}

\displaystyle=L^{-3}\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\hat{P}^{i}[\delta_{d}]=W_{2}^{i},

(4.14)

and the second derivative gives the power spectrum covariance,

\displaystyle i^{2}\left.\frac{\delta^{2}W_{P}[J]}{\delta J_{i}\,\delta J_{j}}\right|_{J=0}

\displaystyle\equiv\Sigma^{ij}\,,

\displaystyle=L^{-6}\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\hat{P}^{i}[\delta_{d}]\,\hat{P}^{j}[\delta_{d}]-W_{2}^{i}\,W_{2}^{j}\,,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{i}N_{j}}\sum_{{\bf{n}}\in V_{i},\,{\bf{m}}\in V_{j}}\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]|\delta_{d}({\bf{n}})|^{2}|\delta_{d}({\bf{m}})|^{2}-W_{2}^{i}\,W_{2}^{j}\,.

(4.15)

To compute the first term in the last line we employ again the expansion (
2.31
). The Gaussian approximation to the FLP gives

\frac{1}{N_{i}N_{j}}\sum_{{\bf{n}}\in V_{i},\,{\bf{m}}\in V_{j}}\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\,{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]|\delta_{d}({\bf{n}})|^{2}|\delta_{d}({\bf{m}})|^{2}-\,W_{2}^{i}W_{2}^{j}=\frac{2\delta_{ij}}{N_{i}^{2}}\sum_{{\bf{n}}\in V_{i}}(W_{2}({\bf{n}}))^{2}=\frac{2\delta_{ij}}{N_{i}}(W_{2}^{i})^{2}\,.

(4.16)

Among the non-Gaussian contributions to Eq. (
2.31
), only the term linear in
W_{4}
contributes,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{i}N_{j}}\sum_{{\bf{n}}\in V_{i},\,{\bf{m}}\in V_{j}}\frac{1}{4!}\langle[W_{4}]\cdot{\cal H}_{4}[\delta_{d}]|\delta_{d}({\bf{n}})|^{2}|\delta_{d}({\bf{m}})|^{2}\rangle_{g}\,,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{i}N_{j}}\sum_{{\bf{n}}\in V_{i},\,{\bf{m}}\in V_{j}}W_{4}({\bf{n}},-{\bf{n}},{\bf{m}},-{\bf{m}})=\,W_{4}^{ij}\,,

(4.17)

where we have defined the bin-averaged trispectrum as

W_{4}^{ij}\equiv\frac{1}{N_{i}N_{j}}\sum_{{\bf{n}}\in V_{i},\,{\bf{m}}\in V_{j}}W_{4}({\bf{n}},-{\bf{n}},{\bf{m}},-{\bf{m}})\,.

(4.18)

Therefore, the full power spectrum covariance is

\Sigma^{ij}=\frac{2}{N_{i}}(W_{2}^{i})^{2}\,\delta_{ij}+\,W_{4}^{ij}\,.

(4.19)

We now expand the generating functional
W_{P}[J]
as (noticing that
W_{P}[0]=0
due to the normalization of
{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]
)

\displaystyle W_{P}[J]=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}\left.\frac{\delta W_{P}[J]}{\delta J_{i}}\right|_{J=0}J_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}\left.\frac{\delta^{2}W_{P}[J]}{\delta J_{i}\delta J_{j}}\right|_{J=0}J_{i}J_{j}+\cdots\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\,J\cdot W_{2}-\frac{1}{2}\,J\cdot\Sigma\cdot J+\cdots\,.

(4.20)

Since we are interested in comparing the Fisher matrix from
{\cal L}_{P}[\hat{P}_{d}]
with the one obtained from the FLP, we truncate the expansion at quadratic order in the sources
J_{i}
. See Appendix
F
for a discussion of higher orders and for an assessment of the accuracy of the Gaussian approximation to
{\cal L}_{P}[\hat{P}_{d}]
in the large
N_{i}
limit.

By inserting Eq. (
4.20
) into Eq. (
4.11
) and performing the integration over the sources, we obtain the Gaussian approximation to the power spectrum likelihood,

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{P}[\hat{P}_{d}]\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{N_{\rm bin}}\det(L^{6}\Sigma)}}\,\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{P}_{d}L^{-3}-W_{2}\right)\cdot\Sigma^{-1}\cdot\left(\hat{P}_{d}L^{-3}-W_{2}\right)\right]\,,

(4.21)

from which the Fisher matrix follows as

F_{P;ab}=\sum^{N_{\rm bin}}_{i,j=1}\frac{\partial W_{2}^{i}}{\partial\alpha_{a}}\frac{\partial W_{2}^{j}}{\partial\alpha_{b}}\,\Sigma_{ij}^{-1}\,.

(4.22)

Inverting the covariance in Eq. (
4.19
) and expanding in powers of
W_{4}
, we find

\displaystyle\Sigma_{ij}^{-1}=

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{ij}}{2(W_{2}^{i})^{2}}N_{i}-\frac{1}{4}N_{i}N_{j}\frac{W_{4}^{ij}}{(W_{2}^{i})^{2}(W_{2}^{j})^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{8}N_{i}N_{j}\sum_{l=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}N_{l}\frac{W_{4}^{il}W_{4}^{lj}}{(W_{2}^{i})^{2}(W_{2}^{l})^{2}(W_{2}^{j})^{2}}+O(W_{4}^{3})\,.

(4.23)

The leading term, when substituted into Eq. (
4.22
), reproduces the Gaussian part of the “2-2” block of the Fisher matrix obtained from the FLP, Eq. (
3.11
),

\displaystyle F_{ab,\text{g}}^{(2,2)}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum^{m_{\rm max}}_{m,n}\frac{\partial W_{2}({\bf{m}})}{\partial\alpha_{a}}\frac{\partial W_{2}(n)}{\partial\alpha_{b}}\frac{\delta_{{\bf{m}}+{\bf{n}}}}{W_{2}({\bf{m}})^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum^{N_{\rm bin}}_{i,j=1}\;\;\sum_{{\bf{m}}\in V_{i},\,{\bf{n}}\in V_{j}}\frac{\partial W_{2}({\bf{m}})}{\partial\alpha_{a}}\frac{\partial W_{2}(n)}{\partial\alpha_{b}}\frac{\delta_{{\bf{m}}+{\bf{n}}}}{W_{2}({\bf{m}})^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum^{N_{\rm bin}}_{i,j=1}\delta_{ij}\;\;\sum_{{\bf{m}}\in V_{i}}\frac{\partial W_{2}({\bf{m}})}{\partial\alpha_{a}}\frac{\partial W_{2}({\bf{m}})}{\partial\alpha_{b}}\frac{1}{W_{2}({\bf{m}})^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\sum^{N_{\rm bin}}_{i,j=1}\delta_{ij}\frac{\partial W_{2}^{i}}{\partial\alpha_{a}}\frac{\partial W_{2}^{i}}{\partial\alpha_{b}}\frac{N_{i}}{2(W_{2}^{i})^{2}}\,,

(4.24)

where we have replaced the power spectrum (and its derivatives) within each bin with its bin-averaged value,

W_{2}({\bf{m}})\simeq W_{2}^{i}\qquad\text{for}\;\;{\bf{m}}\in V_{i}\,.

Similarly, the
O(W_{4})
and
O(W_{4}^{2})
contributions in Eq. (
4.23
) can be derived from Eq. (
3.12
) by approximating the mode-level power spectrum and
W_{4}
with their bin-averaged counterparts. These three contributions are represented by the first two diagrams in Fig.
1
, and by the third diagram for
j=4
. Continuing the series in Eq. (
4.23
) generates chain-type diagrams of all orders in
W_{4}
, as depicted in Fig.
4
.

In summary, the Fisher matrix for the power spectrum corresponds to the sum of Eqs. (
3.11
), (
3.12
), and the chain diagrams of Fig.
4
,

F_{P;ab}\simeq F_{ab,\text{g}}^{(2,2)}+F_{ab,\text{Pcov}}^{(2,2)}+\text{``$W_{4}$ chain diagrams''}\,.

(4.25)

Figure 4
:
Higher-order contributions to the power spectrum Fisher matrix, obtained by the expansion in Eq. (
4.23
).

In contrast, the remaining contributions to the “2-2” field-level Fisher matrix, indicated as
F_{ab,\text{FLP}}^{(2,2)}
in Eq. (
3.13
) (and corresponding to diagram c) with
j>4
and diagrams d)–f) in Fig.
1
), have no counterpart in the power-spectrum Fisher matrix. To capture them, the correlator data vector must be enlarged.

It might be surprising at first that higher-order summary statistics are important, given that, by assumption, the parameter vector considered only modifies
W_{2}({\bf{m}})
. This is due to the cross-covariance of
W_{2}
with higher-order correlation functions, which are implicitly kept fixed when considering the Fisher information. As demonstrated in App.
D
, for a parameter
p_{\alpha}
that affects only the power spectrum, the information obtained about it from the power spectrum and from higher-order statistics becomes equivalent once we marginalize over the parameters governing the latter. Importantly, this no longer holds if parameters modify both
W_{2}
and higher-order statistics, in which case the latter will add genuine new information.

4.2
Likelihood for P+B

We now enlarge the data vector by adding the bispectrum estimator to the power-spectrum estimator (
4.6
):

\hat{B}^{t}_{d}=\hat{B}^{t}[\delta_{d}]=\frac{L^{6}}{N_{T}^{t}}\sum_{{\bf{m}}_{1}\in V_{i}}\sum_{{\bf{m}}_{2}\in V_{j}}\sum_{{\bf{m}}_{3}\in V_{k}}\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{123}}\,\delta_{d}({\bf{m}}_{1})\delta_{d}({\bf{m}}_{2})\delta_{d}({\bf{m}}_{3})\,,

(4.26)

where
t
identifies the set of closed triangles with side lengths in the momentum bins
V_{i,j,k}
, and
N_{T}^{t}
denotes the total number of triangles in the set
t
.

Following the same steps as for the power spectrum alone, we obtain the Gaussian likelihood for the joint “P+B” data vector,

V_{PB}^{\alpha}\equiv\{P_{d}^{i}L^{-3},\,B_{d}^{t}L^{-6}\}\,,

(4.27)

where
\alpha=i,\,j,\,k,\cdots
labels the power spectrum bins, and
\alpha=t,\,u,\,v,\cdots
labels the binned triangles.
The covariance matrix now has the block form

\displaystyle\Sigma_{P+B}^{\alpha\beta}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Sigma^{ij}&\Sigma_{PB}^{iu}\\
\Sigma_{BP}^{tj}&\Sigma_{B}^{tu}\end{array}\right)\,,

with

\displaystyle\Sigma_{B}^{tu}=\delta_{tu}\Sigma_{g}^{t}+\Sigma_{W_{3}^{2}}^{tu}+\Sigma_{W_{2}W_{4}}^{tu}+\Sigma_{W_{6}}^{tu}\,.

(4.30)

Here
\Sigma_{g}^{t}
is the Gaussian bispectrum covariance, cubic in the power spectrum, while the remaining terms arise respectively from
W_{3}^{2}
,
W_{2}W_{4}
, and
W_{6}
.

The new ingredient relative to the power-spectrum-only case is the mixed covariance between the power spectrum and the bispectrum. At leading order it is generated by the disconnected contraction of five fields and scales as

\Sigma_{PB}^{it}\propto W_{2}^{i}W_{3}^{t}\,,

(4.31)

where
t
labels the triangle configuration. Upon inverting the block covariance matrix, the power spectrum block of the inverse covariance is given by the Schur complement,

\displaystyle\left(\Sigma_{P+B}^{-1}\right)_{PP}=

\displaystyle\,\Sigma^{-1}+\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma_{PB}\left(\Sigma_{B}-\Sigma_{BP}\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma_{PB}\right)^{-1}\Sigma_{BP}\Sigma^{-1}\,.

(4.32)

Expanding Eq. (
4.32
) to leading order in the mixed covariance gives

\displaystyle\left(\Sigma_{P+B}^{-1}\right)_{PP}\simeq\Sigma^{-1}+\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma_{PB}\Sigma_{B,g}^{-1}\Sigma_{BP}\Sigma^{-1}+\cdots\,.

(4.33)

Therefore the power-spectrum entry of the Fisher matrix receives the extra contribution

\displaystyle F_{P+B;ab}=F_{P;ab}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}\frac{\partial W_{2}^{i}}{\partial\alpha_{a}}\frac{\partial W_{2}^{j}}{\partial\alpha_{b}}\frac{N_{i}N_{j}}{(W_{2}^{i})^{2}(W_{2}^{j})^{2}}\sum_{t}\frac{\Sigma_{PB}^{it}\Sigma_{BP}^{tj}}{\Sigma_{g}^{t}}\,,

(4.34)

where
\Sigma_{g}^{t}
is the Gaussian bispectrum covariance for triangle bin
t
. This reproduces the
j=3
contribution in the third and fourth lines of Eq. (
3.13
), namely the first non-trivial FLP correction to the power-spectrum block that cannot be captured by a power-spectrum-only likelihood.

Analogously, the BB block of
\Sigma_{P+B}^{-1}
gives the bispectrum contribution to the Fisher matrix. Its leading term,
\Sigma_{B,g}^{-1}
, reproduces the Gaussian BB entries, namely Eq. (
3.17
) and thus Eq. (
3.16
), while the non-Gaussian terms in
\Sigma_{B}^{tu}
, starting with
\Sigma_{W_{3}^{2}}^{tu}
, generate the corresponding non-Gaussian corrections. In this sense, Eq. (
3.16
) can also be viewed as arising from the Gaussian part of a likelihood for the bispectrum, and more generally from the BB sector of a likelihood for an infinite hierarchy of correlators.

The same logic extends to higher correlators. Enlarging the data vector to include the trispectrum, the five-point function, and so on, progressively reproduces the remaining terms in the FLP expansion through the corresponding covariance blocks and cross-covariances. The key point is therefore that the Fisher information encoded in the FLP can be reorganized as the information contained in a Gaussian likelihood for an infinite hierarchy of correlators, provided their full covariance matrix—including all cross-correlations—is retained.

Our calculation thus shows that incorporating the cross-covariance between power spectrum and bispectrum is necessary to obtain a consistent posterior. In general, neglecting the cross-covariance could lead to either over- or underestimation of inferred parameter errors, depending on the detailed structure of the joint covariance as well as the parameter dependence of power spectrum and bispectrum.

4.3
Other summary statistics

When dealing with summary statistics other than correlators, a natural question is how to interpret their information content in the language of correlators.
Eq. (
2.30
) offers a natural quantitative framework to address this. Specifically, for a chosen fiducial cosmology, the expectation value of a given summary statistic can be written as

\displaystyle\langle S[\delta]\rangle^{\rm fid}_{\cal L}=

\displaystyle\langle S[\delta]\rangle^{\rm fid}_{{\cal L}_{g}}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{L^{3k}}{k!}\sum_{N=3}^{\infty}\left[W_{N}^{k}\right]\cdot\langle{\cal H}_{N}[\delta]\,S[\delta]\rangle^{\rm fid}_{{\cal L}_{g}}\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle S[\delta]\rangle^{\rm fid}_{{\cal L}_{g}}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{L^{3k}}{k!}\sum_{N=3}^{\infty}\left[W_{N}^{k}\right]\cdot{\cal C}_{N}\,,

(4.35)

where

{\cal C}_{N}({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{N})\equiv\langle{\cal H}_{N}[\delta]({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{N})\,S[\delta]\rangle^{\rm fid}_{{\cal L}_{g}}\,,

(4.36)

denotes the
N
-th order non-Gaussian weight associated with the summary statistic

S[\delta]
.

Here the average is taken with respect to the Gaussian FLP. Thus these weights can be computed from Gaussian realizations of the field
\delta
, followed by an evaluation of the corresponding correlators.

5
Recovering the BAO scale

In this section we illustrate how the FLP framework captures the recovery of the BAO scale once information carried by higher-order correlators is included.
Precisely, we show how the joint dependence of
W_{2}
and
W_{3}
on the BAO scale effectively captures the leading additional information that is obtained with BAO reconstruction techniques, where “leading” refers to the impact of modes much larger than the BAO scale.

When an additional physical scale is present, the standard perturbation theory (PT) power counting can break down: higher-order PT terms may become comparable in size to lower-order ones and must therefore be retained in the calculation. A classic and phenomenologically important case is the BAO scale, denoted by
r_{d}
, which shows up in the oscillatory, or “wiggly,” component of the nonlinear power spectrum,

W_{2}(k)=W_{2,\text{nw}}(k)+W_{2,\text{w}}(k),

(5.1)

with

W_{2,\text{w}}(k)=A_{\text{w}}(k)\sin(kr_{d})\,,

(5.2)

where
W_{2,\text{nw}}(k)
and
A_{\text{w}}(k)
are smooth on the characteristic scale
r_{d}^{-1}
, in the sense that

\frac{d\log W_{2,\text{nw}}}{d\log k}\ll kr_{d}\,,\qquad\frac{d\log A_{\text{w}}}{d\log k}\ll kr_{d}\,.

(5.3)

In this section we work in the continuum limit for the correlators, i.e. we assume
qL/(2\pi)\gg 1
for all relevant modes
q
, and therefore sums over discrete wavevectors are replaced by integrals.

The couplings between long-wavelength modes
q\ll k
and modes with
k>2\pi/r_{d}
lead to a damping of the oscillatory part of the power spectrum,

A_{\text{w}}(k)=A^{0}_{\text{w}}(k)e^{-k^{2}\Sigma_{\Lambda}(r_{d})}\,,

(5.4)

where, assuming that the long modes are linear, the damping exponent is

-k^{2}\Sigma_{\Lambda}(r_{d})=-k^{2}\int^{\Lambda}\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{P(q)}{q^{2}}\mu^{2}\left(1-\cos(q\mu r_{d})\right),

(5.5)

with
P(q)
the (dimensional) linear matter power spectrum,
\mu\equiv({\bf{k}}\cdot{\bf{q}})/(kq)
, and the integral restricted to
q\leq\Lambda\ll k
. By splitting the integration domain, this integral can be approximated as

k^{2}\Sigma_{\Lambda}(r_{d})\simeq k^{2}\sigma_{\Lambda,v}^{2}+\frac{k^{2}r_{d}^{2}}{10}\Delta^{2}_{r_{d}}\,,

(5.6)

where the contribution from large-scale bulk flows is

\sigma_{\Lambda,v}^{2}\equiv\frac{1}{3}\int_{2\pi r_{d}^{-1}}^{\Lambda}\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{P(q)}{q^{2}}\,,

(5.7)

and the contribution from long-wavelength tidal fields is

\Delta^{2}_{r_{d}}\equiv\int^{2\pi r_{d}^{-1}}\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}P(q)\,.

(5.8)

We now define the parameter

\beta\equiv\log\left(\frac{r_{d}}{r_{d}^{\text{fid}}}\right)\,,

(5.9)

where
r_{d}^{\text{fid}}
is the fiducial BAO drag scale. We then compute the Fisher matrix for
\beta
. The Gaussian power-spectrum contribution (diagram a in Fig.
1
) yields

\displaystyle F_{\beta,g}^{(2,2)}=

\displaystyle L^{3}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left(\frac{\partial W_{2}(k)}{\partial\beta}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{2W_{2}(k)^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle L^{3}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left(\frac{\partial W_{2,\text{w}}(k)}{\partial\beta}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{2W_{2}(k)^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle L^{3}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,k^{2}r_{d}^{2}\frac{A^{0}_{\text{w}}(k)^{2}\cos^{2}(kr_{d})}{2W_{2}(k)^{2}}e^{-2k^{2}\Sigma_{\Lambda}(r_{d})}\,,

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle L^{3}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,k^{2}r_{d}^{2}\frac{A^{0}_{\text{w}}(k)^{2}\cos^{2}(kr_{d})}{2W_{2}(k)^{2}}\left(1-2k^{2}\Sigma_{\Lambda}(r_{d})\right)\,,

(5.10)

where, in the derivative of
W_{2}
, we have kept only terms enhanced by
k^{2}r_{d}^{2}
.
The second, negative term in parentheses represents the loss of information on the characteristic scale
r_{d}
that is caused by modes with
q<\Lambda
.

This information is restored once we include the bispectrum–bispectrum block of the Fisher matrix, in particular its Gaussian piece (diagram a in Fig.
2
), where we focus on contributions with one soft internal momentum:

\displaystyle F_{\beta,g}^{(3,3)}=

\displaystyle\frac{L^{6}}{2}\int_{\Lambda}\frac{d^{3}p_{1}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{d^{3}p_{2}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int^{\Lambda}\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{D}({\bf{q}}+{\bf{p}}_{1}+{\bf{p}}_{2})}{W_{2}(q)W_{2}(p_{1})W_{2}(p_{2})}\left(\frac{\partial W_{3}(q,p_{1},p_{2})}{\partial\beta}\right)^{2}\,.

(5.11)

The soft limit of the bispectrum is fixed by the consistency relation
[
26
,
17
]
,

L^{3}W_{3}(k,|{\bf{k}}+{\bf{q}}|,q)\to\frac{k}{q}\mu\,b_{1}P(q)\left(W_{2}(k)-W_{2}(|{\bf{k}}+{\bf{q}}|)\right)+O\big((q/k)^{0}\big)\,,

(5.12)

valid when
q/k\ll 1
, with
b_{1}
the linear bias.

Restricting to the oscillatory (wiggly) part of the power spectrum, we obtain

W_{2,\text{w}}(k)-W_{2,\text{w}}(|{\bf{k}}+{\bf{q}}|)=A_{\text{w}}(k)\left[\sin(kr_{d})\left(1-\cos(q\mu r_{d})\right)-\cos(kr_{d})\sin(q\mu r_{d})\right]\,.

(5.13)

In the range
q\ll 2\pi/r_{d}\ll k
, the right-hand side becomes

-A_{\text{w}}(k)\cos(kr_{d})q\mu r_{d}+O(q^{2}r_{d}^{2})\simeq q\mu\;dW_{2,\text{w}}(k)/dk
, whereas for
2\pi/r_{d}\ll q\ll k
the oscillatory factors in
q\mu r_{d}
average out and we can approximate this difference by
W_{2,\text{w}}(k)
(see, for instance,
[
6
,
25
]
). In summary, the soft limit of the bispectrum acquires a wiggly contribution sourced by short modes of the form

L^{3}W_{3,\text{w}}(k,|{\bf{k}}+{\bf{q}}|,q)\to\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}-\mu^{2}b_{1}P(q)\,k\frac{dW_{2\text{w}}(k)}{dk}&(q\ll 2\pi/r_{d}\ll k)\\
&\\
\mu\frac{k}{q}b_{1}P(q)\,W_{2\text{w}}(k)&(2\pi/r_{d}\ll q\ll k)\end{array}\right.\,.

(5.14)

Using these relations, we can evaluate the contribution of soft modes
q
to the bispectrum Fisher matrix. Modes in the interval
2\pi r_{d}^{-1}\ll q\ll k
contribute to Eq. (
5.11
) as

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Lambda}\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,k^{2}r_{d}^{2}\frac{A^{0}_{\text{w}}(k)^{2}\cos^{2}(kr_{d})}{W_{2}(k)^{2}}\frac{2}{3}\int_{2\pi r_{d}^{-1}}^{\Lambda}\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{k^{2}}{q^{2}}\frac{b_{1}^{2}P(q)^{2}}{W_{2}(q)}\,,

(5.15)

where we have accounted for the two configurations
{\bf{p}}_{1}={\bf{k}}
,
{\bf{p}}_{2}=-{\bf{k}}-{\bf{q}}
and
{\bf{p}}_{2}={\bf{k}}
,
{\bf{p}}_{1}=-{\bf{k}}-{\bf{q}}
, and we have approximated
W_{2}(|{\bf{k}}+{\bf{q}}|)\simeq W_{2}(k)
in the denominator. When we add this term to the negative contribution in Eq. (
5.10
) for the same momentum configurations, we obtain

-\frac{L^{3}}{3}\int_{\Lambda}\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}k^{2}r_{d}^{2}\frac{A^{0}_{\text{w}}(k)^{2}\cos^{2}(kr_{d})}{W_{2}(k)^{2}}\int_{2\pi r_{d}^{-1}}^{\Lambda}\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{k^{2}}{q^{2}}P(q)\,\frac{P_{\epsilon}}{b_{1}^{2}P(q)+P_{\epsilon}}\,,

(5.16)

where for the soft
q
modes we have assumed the model

L^{3}W_{2}(q)=b_{1}^{2}P(q)+P_{\epsilon},

(5.17)

with
P_{\epsilon}
denoting the noise power spectrum.
Equation (
5.16
) describes the residual information loss due to very long modes, which is controlled by the noise amplitude.

The contribution of modes with wavenumbers
q\ll 2\pi r_{d}^{-1}\ll k
to Eqs. (
5.10
) and (
5.11
) yields the following residual information loss:

\displaystyle-\frac{L^{3}}{10}\int_{\Lambda}\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{k^{4}r_{d}^{4}A^{0}_{\text{w}}(k)^{2}}{W_{2}(k)^{2}}\int^{2\pi r_{d}^{-1}}\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}P(q)\left(\cos^{2}(kr_{d})-\frac{b_{1}^{2}P(q)}{L^{3}W_{2}(q)}\sin^{2}(kr_{d})\right)\,,

\displaystyle\simeq-\frac{L^{3}}{20}\int_{\Lambda}\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{k^{4}r_{d}^{4}A^{0}_{\text{w}}(k)^{2}}{W_{2}(k)^{2}}\int^{2\pi r_{d}^{-1}}\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}P(q)\,\frac{P_{\epsilon}}{b_{1}^{2}P(q)+P_{\epsilon}}\,,

(5.18)

where, in the second line, we have replaced
\sin^{2}(kr_{d})
and
\cos^{2}(kr_{d})
by their averages. This is justified because
kr_{d}\gg 2\pi
. After performing this averaging, the information loss in this regime is again controlled by the noise amplitude.

Higher powers of the damping factor
k^{2}\Sigma_{\Lambda}(r_{d})
are obtained by evaluating the Fisher matrix of higher-order correlators that contain only two hard modes, while the remaining modes are soft. In this case, Eq. (
5.14
) generalizes to

\displaystyle L^{3j}\,W_{2+j,\text{w}}

\displaystyle({\bf{k}},-{\bf{k}}-{\bf{q}}_{1\cdots j},{\bf{q}}_{1},\cdots,q_{j_{\text{t}}},q_{j_{\text{t}}+1},\cdots,{\bf{q}}_{j})

\displaystyle\to(-1)^{j_{\text{t}}}\,\left(\Pi_{i=1}^{j_{\text{t}}}\mu_{i}^{2}b_{1}P(q_{i})\right)\left(\Pi_{l={j_{\text{t}}+1}}^{j}\mu_{l}\frac{k}{q_{l}}b_{1}P(q_{l})\right)\;k^{j_{\text{t}}}\frac{d^{j_{\text{t}}}W_{2,\text{w}}(k)}{dk^{j_{\text{t}}}}\,,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad(q_{i}\ll 2\pi/r_{d}\ll k,\quad 2\pi/r_{d}\ll q_{l}\ll k)\,.

(5.19)

We have thus shown that the FLP includes the information from the soft (squeezed) limits of all correlators, combining them to optimally recover the information on the scale
r_{d}
, given the noise. In the evaluation of the FLP, correlators are summed over all configurations however. Hence, this information recovery is only under control when the full prediction for the higher-order correlators considered is under control over all configurations.

Further, we should highlight that the sole consideration of
F_{\beta\beta}
implies that all other parameters are kept fixed. In practice, in analysis using the FLP, one would also vary the other parameters that are relevant for predicting the correlators
W_{n}
. This includes in particular the bias parameters. Ref.
[
4
]
argues that the broad-band contribution to the correlators could further enhance the information in the FLP over low-order summary statistics. It would be interesting to confirm this by extending the single-parameter Fisher calculation to the multi-parameter case. However, it might be difficult to obtain closed analytical results in this case.

6
Reconstruction of the initial field

We now show how the formalism developed so far naturally yields, as a byproduct, the optimal reconstruction of the initial conditions of the field.
To achieve this, we adjust Eq. (
2.8
) by adding a source term for the initial conditions,

\displaystyle\hat{\cal L}[\delta_{d};J_{\rm in}]=

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}\delta_{\rm M}\,{\cal D}\delta_{\rm in}\,{\cal D}\epsilon\;\tilde{\delta}_{D}\left[\delta_{d}-\delta_{\rm M}\right]\,\tilde{\delta}_{D}\left[\delta_{\rm M}-\bar{\delta}[\delta_{\rm in},\epsilon]\right]\,\,{\cal P}[\delta_{\rm in},\,\epsilon]\,e^{-iJ_{\rm in}\cdot\delta_{\rm in}}\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}J_{f}\;e^{iJ_{f}\cdot\delta_{d}}\,e^{\hat{W}[J_{f},J_{\rm in}]}\,,

(6.1)

where

e^{\hat{W}[J_{f},J_{\rm in}]}\equiv\int{\cal D}\delta_{\rm M}\,{\cal D}\delta_{\rm in}\hat{P}_{M}[\delta_{\rm M},\delta_{\rm in}]e^{-\left(iJ_{\rm in}\cdot\delta_{\rm in}+J_{f}\cdot\delta_{\rm M}\right)}\,,

(6.2)

with

\hat{P}_{M}[\delta_{\rm M},\delta_{\rm in}]\equiv\int{\cal D}\epsilon\,{\cal P}[\delta_{\rm in},\,\epsilon]\,\tilde{\delta}_{D}\left[\delta_{\rm M}-\bar{\delta}[\delta_{\rm in},\epsilon]\right]\,.

(6.3)

\hat{W}[J_{f},J_{\rm in}]
is the generating functional of the connected mixed correlation functions, namely,

\displaystyle\left.i^{n+m}\left(\frac{\delta\;}{\delta\,J_{f}({\bf{n}}_{1})}\cdots\frac{\delta\;}{\delta\,J_{f}({\bf{n}}_{n})}\frac{\delta\;}{\delta\,J_{\rm in}({\bf{m}}_{1})}\cdots\frac{\delta\;}{\delta\,J_{\rm in}({\bf{m}}_{m})}\right)\hat{W}[J_{f},J_{\rm in}]\right|_{J_{\rm in}=J_{f}=0}

\displaystyle\qquad\equiv W_{n,m}({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{n},{\bf{m}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{m}}_{m})\,,

(6.4)

where
W_{n,m}({\bf{p}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{p}}_{n},{\bf{q}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{q}}_{m})
is the connected correlator of
n
final (that is, nonlinear) fields and
m
initial (linear) ones; as before, we absorb a total momentum delta function into the definition. It then follows that, when
m=0
, we recover the correlation functions of the final field, namely,

W_{n,0}=W_{n}\,.

(6.5)

Notice that

\hat{\cal L}[\delta_{d};J_{\rm in}=0]={\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\,,

(6.6)

and
\hat{W}[J_{f},J_{\rm in}=0]=W[J_{f}]
.
We define the
average
initial field for given data
\delta_{d}
and source
J_{\rm in}
as

\tilde{\delta}_{\rm in}[\delta_{d},J_{\rm in}]({\bf{n}})\equiv i\frac{\delta\log\hat{\cal L}[\delta_{d};J_{\rm in}]}{\delta J_{\rm in}(-{\bf{n}})}\,,

(6.7)

which is a functional of the data field
\delta_{d}
and of the source term
J_{\rm in}
, and,
by a Legendre transform, we define the likelihood

L[\delta_{d},\tilde{\delta}_{\rm in}]\equiv\hat{\cal L}[\delta_{d};J_{\rm in}]\,e^{-iJ_{\rm in}\cdot\tilde{\delta}_{\rm in}}\,.

(6.8)

The reconstructed “classical” field
\delta^{c}_{\rm in}[\delta_{d}]
is the field configuration that extremizes
\log L
for given
\delta_{d}
,

\frac{\delta\log L[\delta_{d},\delta^{c}_{\rm in}]}{\delta\delta^{c}_{\rm in}[{\bf{m}}]}=0\,.

(6.9)

Since, from (
6.8
) we have,

\frac{\delta\log L[\delta_{d},\tilde{\delta}_{\rm in}]}{\delta\tilde{\delta}_{\rm in}({\bf{m}})}=-iJ_{\rm in}(-{\bf{m}}),

(6.10)

the classical field is given by

\delta^{c}_{\rm in}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{n}})\equiv\tilde{\delta}_{\rm in}[\delta_{d},J_{\rm in}=0]({\bf{n}})\,,

(6.11)

and, moreover,

L[\delta_{d},\delta^{c}_{\rm in}]={\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\,.

(6.12)

From Eq. (
6.1
) we have

\delta^{c}_{\rm in}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{n}})={\cal L}[\delta_{d}]^{-1}\int{\cal D}J_{f}\,e^{iJ_{f}\cdot\delta_{d}}e^{W[J_{f}]}\,\left.\frac{i\,\delta\hat{W}[J_{f},J_{\rm in}]}{\delta J_{\rm in}(-{\bf{n}})}\right|_{J_{\rm in}=0}\,.

(6.13)

We now expand the functional inside the integral as

\displaystyle\left.\frac{i\,\delta\hat{W}[J_{f},J_{\rm in}]}{\delta J_{\rm in}(-{\bf{n}})}\right|_{J_{\rm in}=0}=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-i)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{m_{1},\cdots,m_{n}}^{m_{\rm max}}W_{n,1}({\bf{m}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{m}}_{n},{\bf{n}})J_{f}({\bf{m}}_{1})\cdots J_{f}({\bf{m}}_{n})\,,

(6.14)

Inserting this expression into Eq. (
6.13
), and converting the factors of
J_{f}
into functional derivatives with respect to
\delta_{d}
, we finally obtain the expression of the classical reconstructed field in terms of derivatives of the FLP,

\displaystyle\delta^{c}_{\rm in}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{n}})=

\displaystyle{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{m_{1},\cdots,m_{n}}^{m_{\rm max}}W_{n,1}({\bf{m}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{m}}_{n},{\bf{n}})\frac{\delta}{\delta\delta_{d}(-{\bf{m}}_{1})}\cdots\frac{\delta}{\delta\delta_{d}(-{\bf{m}}_{n})}{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\,.

(6.15)

At lowest order in derivatives (
n=1
) and using the Gaussian approximation of the FLP,
{\cal L}[\delta_{d}]\simeq{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]

\displaystyle\delta^{c,(0)}_{in}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{n}})=

\displaystyle\frac{W_{1,1}(n)}{W_{2}(n)}\delta_{d}({\bf{n}})\,.

(6.16)

To build some intuition for this result, we assume a simple linear model with noise, with the following two-point correlators,

L^{3}W_{2}^{(0)}(n)=(A^{2}b_{1}^{2}P^{0}(n)+P_{\epsilon}(n))D_{f}^{2},\qquad L^{3}W_{1,1}^{(0)}(n)=A^{2}b_{1}^{2}P^{0}(n)D_{in}D_{f}\,,

where

and

are the linear and final growth factors, respectively.
Inserted into (

\displaystyle\delta^{c,(0)}_{in}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{n}})

\displaystyle=\frac{D_{in}}{D_{f}}\left(1+\frac{P_{\epsilon}(n)}{A^{2}b_{1}^{2}P^{0}(n)}\right)^{-1}\delta_{d}({\bf{n}})=\frac{D_{in}}{D_{f}}\delta_{d,\epsilon}({\bf{n}})\,,

(6.17)

where we have defined the noise-filtered field

\delta_{d,\epsilon}({\bf{n}})\equiv\left(1+\frac{P_{\epsilon}(n)}{A^{2}b_{1}^{2}P^{0}(n)}\right)^{-1}\delta_{d}({\bf{n}})\,.

(6.18)

This is the expected Wiener-filter result (e.g.,
[

The prefactor tends to unity in the noiseless regime (that is, for large scales such that
P_{\epsilon}(n)/P^{0}(n)\ll 1
), and is damped on small scales.

At next order, we include the contribution of the three-point correlators

and
W_{2,1}

\displaystyle\delta^{c,(1)}_{in}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{n}})=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{m_{\rm max}}\bigg[\frac{W^{(0)}_{1,1}(n)}{W^{(0)}_{2}(n)}W_{3}^{(1)}(m_{1},m_{2},n)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-W_{2,1}^{(1)}(m_{1},m_{2},n)\bigg]\frac{\delta_{d}({\bf{m}}_{1})}{W_{2}^{(0)}(m_{1})}\frac{\delta_{d}({\bf{m}}_{2})}{W_{2}^{(0)}(m_{2})}\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{D_{in}}{D_{f}}\Bigg\{\left(1+\frac{P_{\epsilon}(n)}{A^{2}b_{1}^{2}P^{0}(n)}\right)^{-1}\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{m_{\rm max}}\Bigg[K_{2}({\bf{m}}_{1},{\bf{m}}_{2})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-P_{\epsilon}(n)\left(\frac{K_{2}(-{\bf{n}},{\bf{m}}_{1})}{A^{2}b_{1}^{2}P^{0}(m_{2})}+\frac{K_{2}(-{\bf{n}},{\bf{m}}_{2})}{A^{2}b_{1}^{2}P^{0}(m_{1})}\right)\Bigg]\delta_{d,\epsilon}({\bf{m}}_{1})\delta_{d,\epsilon}({\bf{m}}_{2})\Bigg\}\,.

(6.19)

We have used the tree-level expressions,

\displaystyle L^{6}W_{3}^{(1)}[{\bf{m}}_{1},{\bf{m}}_{2},{\bf{m}}_{3}]=

\displaystyle\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{1}+{\bf{m}}_{2}+{\bf{m}}_{3}}\,2\,D_{f}^{4}A^{4}b_{1}^{2}\,\bigg[K_{2}({\bf{m}}_{1},{\bf{m}}_{2})P^{0}(m_{1})P^{0}(m_{2})

\displaystyle\qquad\;+K_{2}({\bf{m}}_{2},{\bf{m}}_{3})P^{0}(m_{2})P^{0}(m_{3})+K_{2}({\bf{m}}_{3},{\bf{m}}_{1})P^{0}(m_{3})P^{0}(m_{1})\bigg]\,,

(6.20)

\displaystyle L^{6}W_{2,1}^{(1)}[{\bf{m}}_{1},{\bf{m}}_{2},{\bf{m}}_{3}]=

\displaystyle\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{1}+{\bf{m}}_{2}+{\bf{m}}_{3}}\,2\,D_{f}^{3}D_{in}A^{4}b_{1}^{2}\bigg[K_{2}({\bf{m}}_{2},{\bf{m}}_{3})P^{0}(m_{2})P^{0}(m_{3})

\displaystyle\qquad+K_{2}({\bf{m}}_{3},{\bf{m}}_{1})P^{0}(m_{3})P^{0}(m_{1})\bigg]\,,

(6.21)

where the difference between the two expressions is due to the fact that in
W_{2,1}[{\bf{p}}_{1},{\bf{p}}_{2},{\bf{p}}_{3}]
the field carrying momentum
{\bf{p}}_{3}
is evaluated at the initial time. The contribution coming from its second-order correction is therefore suppressed by a factor
D_{in}/D_{f}
with respect to the other two and is neglected.
It is straightforward to check that, in the noiseless limit, this result coincides with that of the inverse PT of
[
9
]
. However, besides holding also in the noisy regime, this approach provides a more direct derivation of the connection to the correlators.

In PT, the next order will include contributions involving two nonlinearities, that is
F_{3}
and

7
Summary and outlook

In this paper we have developed a field-level posterior (FLP) for large-scale structure and other cosmological observables in a form that is fully non-perturbative with respect to the forward model, and that can therefore be applied equally well to perturbative calculations, simulation-based predictions, or more general effective descriptions. The only basic assumptions entering the construction are translational invariance, the existence of a probability distribution for the initial conditions and the noise, and the possibility of organizing the result in terms of connected correlators of the evolved field. Analytical progress can then be made in case of a Gaussian PDF of the initial conditions, the relevant case for cosmology.

Expanding the FLP around its Gaussian limit, we derive a general decomposition of the Fisher matrix into contributions labeled by the connected correlators
W_{j}
. This makes it possible to identify, term by term, which pieces of the full field-level information are retained by likelihoods for compressed observables and which are instead lost when the data are reduced to a finite set of summary statistics. In particular, we recovered the standard power spectrum Fisher matrix from the Gaussian part of the likelihoods for the power spectrum and the bispectrum, and clarified how cross-covariances among different summaries reconstruct part of the information that is absent in single-summary analyses.

A central message of our analysis is that the FLP provides a unified language to compare field-level inference with correlator-based likelihoods. From this viewpoint, the information contained in the FLP can be reorganized as the information carried by an infinite hierarchy of correlators, provided one keeps not only their individual likelihoods but also their full covariance and cross-covariance structure. Truncating this hierarchy at the level of the power spectrum, of the bispectrum, or of a finite joint data vector such as
P+B
, leads to a precise and quantifiable information loss, which we expressed in terms of the missing higher-order connected correlators.
An advantage of this expansion is that it allows one to apply the same truncation scheme as in standard correlator analyses (e.g.,
P^{\rm 1-loop}+B^{\rm tree}
). In contrast, a field-level forward model is always truncated at a fixed perturbative order
[
9
,
35
]
; for instance, at second order it would contain the
P^{(22)}
contribution to the 1-loop power spectrum but omit the
P^{(13)}
one.
Our treatment also makes explicit the regime of validity of the expansion around the Gaussian limit. The relevant control parameters are set by combinations of higher-point correlators and powers of
W_{2}
, and are closely related to the quantities that govern the signal-to-noise of non-Gaussian observables. This provides a useful criterion for assessing when Gaussian likelihoods for summary statistics are adequate, when non-Gaussian covariance corrections are required, and when one should instead resort to the full field-level description.

We have illustrated these ideas in several concrete settings. For the BAO scale, the main lesson is not only that the FLP clarifies the relation between field-level and correlator-based extractions of the information, but also that it makes explicit how the field contains all the information needed for an optimal reconstruction of the BAO scale in the presence of noise. In this sense, the FLP identifies which terms in the expansion are required to saturate that optimal limit, and therefore provides a systematic way to understand what is retained or lost in approximate reconstruction schemes based on compressed observables. For reconstruction of the initial conditions, we showed that the classical initial field emerges naturally as a byproduct of the formalism itself. The resulting estimator is the optimal reconstruction of the initial field given the observed data and the noise, and it connects in the appropriate limit to the Wiener filter (when truncating at the power spectrum) as well as previous perturbative inverse methods (at higher order). These examples underline that the formalism is not restricted to abstract information-theory questions, but also provides a practical framework to analyze commonly used inference strategies within a single language.

There are several natural directions for future work. A first one is to extend the present formalism to redshift-space distortions, survey windows, and multi-tracer observables; we note that the formalism developed here directly applies to redshift-space distortions, by inserting predictions for redshift-space correlators for the
W_{j}
. A second is to investigate approximation schemes or resummations that remain under control beyond the regime where the expansion in connected correlators holds. A third is to exploit the flexibility of the FLP in situations where the forward model is provided by simulations, emulators, or machine-learning surrogates, so that the correlators entering the expansion can be measured or inferred without relying on a strict perturbative treatment.

Another important direction concerns the treatment of noise. Recent work has emphasized that non-Gaussian noise must be handled carefully in order to recover unbiased field-level inference
[
1
,
32
]
. In our approach, noise properties enter directly at the correlator level, through the
W_{j}
’s, rather than via explicit noise realizations drawn from an assumed probability distribution. This suggests that incorporating realistic noise and exploring its impact within the present framework may benefit from established EFTofLSS results for correlators. Finally, it would be interesting to explore in more detail the relation between the expansion parameters of the FLP and the signal-to-noise of higher-order observables, in order to sharpen the practical criterion for when field-level inference yields a substantial gain over compressed-likelihood approaches.

In this sense, the FLP is not only a formal generalization of correlator likelihoods, but also a tool to organize systematically the comparison between different inference strategies in large-scale structure, identify their domains of validity, and quantify the information that is gained or lost by compression. Future research should further specify how to carry out an efficient numerical implementation of the method introduced in this paper.

Appendix A
Conventions

In this appendix we collect the conventions for Fourier transforms and connected correlators used throughout the paper.

We work in a cubic box of side
L
, with periodic boundary conditions, and label Fourier modes by integer triplets

{\bf{m}}=(m_{x},m_{y},m_{z})

(A.1)

with associated wavevector

{\bf{k}}_{\bf{m}}=k_{f}\,{\bf{m}}\,,\qquad k_{f}\equiv\frac{2\pi}{L}\,.

(A.2)

The fundamental field variable used in the paper is the
dimensionless
Fourier-space density field,

\delta({\bf{m}})\equiv\frac{1}{L^{3}}\int_{L^{3}}d^{3}x\;e^{-i{\bf{k}}_{\bf{m}}\cdot{\bf{x}}}\,\delta({\bf{x}})\,.

(A.3)

On the discrete grid this is equivalent to the convention used in Eq. (
2.2
), namely dividing the Fourier transform by the box volume
L^{3}
(or, equivalently on the lattice, by the total number of grid points). With this choice, both
\delta({\bf{x}})
and
\delta({\bf{m}})
are dimensionless. Moreover, since
\delta({\bf{x}})
is real, Fourier space modes satisfy the constraint,

\delta(-{\bf{m}})=\delta({\bf{m}})^{\ast}\,.

Translational invariance implies momentum conservation. Throughout the paper we absorb the corresponding Kronecker delta and the
L^{3}
factor into the definition of the connected correlators and write

\langle\delta({\bf{m}}_{1})\cdots\delta({\bf{m}}_{j})\rangle_{c}=\,W_{j}({\bf{m}}_{1},\ldots,{\bf{m}}_{j})\,,

(A.4)

where
{\bf{m}}_{1\cdots j}\equiv{\bf{m}}_{1}+\cdots+{\bf{m}}_{j}
. The quantities
W_{j}
are therefore the reduced, dimensionless connected correlators that appear in the FLP expansion, in agreement with the convention adopted in the main text.

For the lowest orders, the relation with the standard connected correlators is

\displaystyle\langle\delta({\bf{m}}_{1})\delta({\bf{m}}_{2})\rangle_{c}

\displaystyle=W_{2}({\bf{m}}_{1},{\bf{m}}_{2})=\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{1}+{\bf{m}}_{2}}\,P({\bf{k}}_{1})L^{-3}\,,

\displaystyle\langle\delta({\bf{m}}_{1})\delta({\bf{m}}_{2})\delta({\bf{m}}_{3})\rangle_{c}

\displaystyle=W_{3}({\bf{m}}_{1},{\bf{m}}_{2},{\bf{m}}_{3})=\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{123}}\,B({\bf{k}}_{1},{\bf{k}}_{2},{\bf{k}}_{3})\,L^{-6}\,,

\displaystyle\langle\delta({\bf{m}}_{1})\cdots\delta({\bf{m}}_{4})\rangle_{c}

\displaystyle=W_{4}({\bf{m}}_{1},{\bf{m}}_{2},{\bf{m}}_{3},{\bf{m}}_{4})=\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{1234}}\,T({\bf{k}}_{1},{\bf{k}}_{2},{\bf{k}}_{3},{\bf{k}}_{4})\,L^{-9}\,,

(A.5)

and similarly for higher connected correlators. In other words, with our Fourier convention, the objects
W_{2}
,
W_{3}
,
W_{4}
, …are the dimensionless counterparts of the usual power spectrum, bispectrum, trispectrum, ….

More generally, if one indicates dimensional connected correlators of order
j
as
C_{j}

then our convention gives

W_{j}({\bf{m}}_{1},\ldots,{\bf{m}}_{j})=\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{1\cdots j}}\,C_{j}({\bf{k}}_{1},\ldots,{\bf{k}}_{j})\,L^{3(1-j)}\,.

(A.6)

Finally, for the two point function we often abbreviate

W_{2}({\bf{m}})\equiv W_{2}({\bf{m}},-{\bf{m}})\,.

(A.7)

Analogous shorthand notations are used for bin-averaged bispectra, trispectra, and higher correlators when discussing compressed likelihoods.

Appendix B
Properties of the
{\cal H}_{j}[\delta_{d}]
functionals

We define the probabilist’s Hermite functional polynomials as

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{j}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{j})

\displaystyle\equiv(-1)^{j}\,{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]^{-1}\frac{\delta^{j}}{\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{n}}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{n}}_{j})}{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]\,,

(B.1)

where
\mathcal{L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]
is defined in Eq. (
2.23
).
Introducing an auxiliary field
\chi[{\bf{n}}]
and the generating functional

\displaystyle{\cal G}[\delta_{d},\chi]

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\;\sum_{{\bf{m}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{m}}_{j}}\frac{1}{j!}\,{\cal H}_{j}({\bf{m}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{m}}_{j})\chi({\bf{m}}_{1})\cdots\chi({\bf{m}}_{j})\,,

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\bf{m}}}\frac{|\chi({\bf{m}})|^{2}-2\,\chi({\bf{m}})\delta_{d}(-{\bf{m}})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}})}\right]\,,

(B.2)

the generic
{\cal H}_{j}[\delta_{d}]
functional can be computed as

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{j}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{j})

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\delta^{j}}{\delta\chi({\bf{n}}_{1})\cdots\delta\chi({\bf{n}}_{j})}{\cal G}[\delta_{d},\chi]\right|_{\chi=0}\,.

(B.3)

The averages with respect to the Gaussian FLP, have the following properties

\displaystyle\langle{\cal H}_{j}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{j})\rangle_{g}=\delta_{j0}\,,

(B.4)

\displaystyle\langle{\cal H}_{j}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{j}){\cal H}_{j^{\prime}}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}})\rangle_{g}=\delta_{jj^{\prime}}\,\frac{\sum_{\sigma\in S_{j}}\delta_{{\bf{n}}_{1}+{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{\sigma(1)}}\cdots\delta_{{\bf{n}}_{1}+{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{\sigma(j)}}}{W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{1})\cdots W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{j})}\,,

(B.5)

where
S_{j}
is the permutation group of
\{1,2,\cdots,j\}
.
Eq. (
B.4
) can be shown directly from the definition (
B.1
) and the vanishing of the (functional) integral over derivatives of a functional vanishing at infinity,

\displaystyle\langle{\cal H}_{j}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{j})\rangle_{g}=

\displaystyle(-1)^{j}\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]^{-1}\frac{\delta^{j}}{\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{n}}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{n}}_{j})}{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{j}\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\frac{\delta^{j}}{\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{n}}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{n}}_{j})}{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]=\delta_{j0}\,.

(B.6)

As for the orthogonality condition, Eq. (
B.5
), we can write

\displaystyle\langle{\cal H}_{j}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{j}){\cal H}_{j^{\prime}}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}})\rangle_{g}

\displaystyle=\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\;\mathcal{L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]\,{\cal H}_{j}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{j}){\cal H}_{j^{\prime}}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}})\,,

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j}\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\left(\frac{\delta^{j}}{\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{n}}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{n}}_{j})}{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]\right){\cal H}_{j^{\prime}}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}})\,,

\displaystyle=\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]\frac{\delta^{j}}{\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{n}}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{n}}_{j})}{\cal H}_{j^{\prime}}[\delta_{d}]({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}})\,,

\displaystyle=\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]\left.\frac{\delta^{j^{\prime}+j}}{\delta\chi({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1})\cdots\delta\chi({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}})\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{n}}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{d}({\bf{n}}_{j})}{\cal G}_{j^{\prime}}[\delta_{d},\chi]\right|_{\chi=0}\,,

\displaystyle=\delta_{jj^{\prime}}\,\frac{\sum_{\sigma\in S_{j}}\delta_{{\bf{n}}_{1}+{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{\sigma(1)}}\cdots\delta_{{\bf{n}}_{j}+{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{\sigma(j)}}}{W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{1})\cdots W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{j})}\int{\cal D}\delta_{d}\;{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{d}]\,,

(B.7)

which equals the RHS of Eq. (
B.5
), thanks to the normalization of
{\cal L}_{g}
.

Another important feature is the composition rule for two polynomials, which allows to express the product
{\cal H}_{j}{\cal H}_{j^{\prime}}
as a sum of terms that are linear in the polynomials, involving only
{\cal H}_{j^{\prime\prime}}
with
j^{\prime\prime}
ranging from
|j-j^{\prime}|
to
j+j^{\prime}
in increments of two. The explicit form of the expansion can be derived by considering the product of generating functionals

{\cal G}[\delta_{d},\chi_{1}]{\cal G}[\delta_{d},\chi_{2}]=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left[|\chi_{1}|^{2}+|\chi_{2}|^{2}-2(\chi_{1}+\chi_{2})\delta_{d}\right]}={\cal G}[\delta_{d},\chi_{1}+\chi_{2}]e^{\chi_{1}\chi_{2}}\,,

(B.8)

where we have used a compact notation.
Therefore,

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{j}{\cal H}_{j^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\delta^{j}}{\delta\chi_{1}^{j}}{\cal G}[\delta_{d},\chi_{1}]\right|_{\chi_{1}=0}\left.\frac{\delta^{j^{\prime}}}{\delta\chi_{2}^{j^{\prime}}}{\cal G}[\delta_{d},\chi_{2}]\right|_{\chi_{2}=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\delta^{j+j^{\prime}}}{\delta\chi_{1}^{j}\delta\chi_{2}^{j^{\prime}}}\left({\cal G}[\delta_{d},\chi_{1}+\chi_{2}]e^{\chi_{1}\chi_{2}}\right)\right|_{\chi_{1}=\chi_{2}=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{j+j^{\prime}}+\cdots\,.

(B.9)

As an example, in Eq. (

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{j}\frac{1}{W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{i})}\left(\delta_{{\bf{n}}_{i}+{\bf{m}}_{1}}{\cal H}_{j}({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{i-1},{\bf{n}}_{i+1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{j},{\bf{m}}_{2})+({\bf{m}}_{1}\leftrightarrow{\bf{m}}_{2})\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=2}^{j}\sum_{l=1}^{i-1}\frac{1}{W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{i})W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{l})}\left(\delta_{{\bf{n}}_{l}+{\bf{m}}_{1}}\delta_{{\bf{n}}_{i}+{\bf{m}}_{2}}+\delta_{{\bf{n}}_{l}+{\bf{m}}_{2}}\delta_{{\bf{n}}_{i}+{\bf{m}}_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times{\cal H}_{j-2}({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{i-1},{\bf{n}}_{i+1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{l-1},{\bf{n}}_{l+1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{j})\,.

Appendix D
Fisher information for multiple summary statistics

We have found that
(F_{P+B})_{\alpha\alpha}
is different from
(F_{P})_{\alpha\alpha}
even for a parameter
p_{\alpha}
that only enters
P
, i.e.
W_{2}
. This initially surprising finding can be understood as follows.

Consider a data vector
\mathbf{x}
with
n_{x}
elements whose expectation value
\bar{\mathbf{x}}(\vec{p})
depends on a
k_{x}
-dimensional parameter vector
\vec{p}
. The Fisher information on
\vec{p}
for observable
\mathbf{x}
is

\displaystyle F_{p_{\alpha}p_{\beta}}(x)=\frac{\partial\bar{\mathbf{x}}}{\partial p_{\alpha}}\cdot C_{(x)}^{-1}\cdot\frac{\partial\bar{\mathbf{x}}}{\partial p_{\beta}}\,,

(D.1)

where
C_{(x)}
is the covariance of the data vector
\mathbf{x}
. The error on
p_{\alpha}
marginalized over the
p_{\beta\neq\alpha}
is given by
[F(\mathbf{x})]^{-1}_{\alpha\alpha}
.

Now consider adding an observable vector
\mathbf{y}
with length
n_{y}
whose mean
\bar{\mathbf{y}}(\vec{q})
depends on another,
k_{y}
-dimensional parameter set
q
, but not
p
. This is the case considered in the derivation in Sect.
3
.
Then, the joint derivative matrix is
k\times n
, where
k\equiv k_{x}+k_{y}
,

n\equiv n_{x}+n_{y}
, and block-diagonal:

D\equiv\frac{\partial(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\partial(\vec{p},\vec{q})}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\partial\bar{\mathbf{x}}}{\partial\vec{p}}&0\\
0&\frac{\partial\bar{\mathbf{y}}}{\partial\vec{q}}\end{array}\right)\,.

(D.2)

We will further assume that the parameters are non-degenerate, which implies that
D
has full row-rank
k
. We can then write the full Fisher matrix as

F(x\oplus y)=DC_{(x\oplus y)}^{-1}D^{\top}\,.

(D.3)

It is then easy to see that the
\vec{p}
-block of is not equal to
F(x)
,
since the
xx
-block of
C_{(x\oplus y)}^{-1}
is not equal to
C_{(x)}^{-1}
if
x

and
y
are covariant, i.e. if the
xy
-block of
C_{(x\oplus y)}
is nonzero This is what was found in Eq. (
3.13
).

The difference arises because
F(x\oplus y)_{\alpha\alpha}
corresponds to
the information on
p_{\alpha}
while keeping all parameters in
\vec{p}
and
\vec{q}

apart from
p_{\alpha}
fixed. Even though
\mathbf{y}
by itself does not contain
information on
p_{\alpha}
, the Fisher information is effectively modified due
to the covariance of
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
.

The proper comparison between the
x
-only and
x\oplus y
cases is to
marginalize over the parameters
\vec{q}
, thus removing the information
that comes from
\mathbf{y}
. This is accomplished by inverting the Fisher matrix.
We can do this by using
D^{\top}(DD^{\top})^{-1}
as the right pseudo-inverse of
D
. This yields

\displaystyle F^{-1}(x\oplus y)

\displaystyle=(DD^{\top})^{-1}DC_{(x\oplus y)}D^{\top}(DD^{\top})^{-1}

(D.4)

where

(DD^{\top})_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{\partial(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\partial(\vec{p},\vec{q})_{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{\partial(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\partial(\vec{p},\vec{q})_{\beta}}

(D.5)

and the contraction is done over the
n
-dimensional combined observable vector. Our assumption that
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
depend on disjoint parameter sets
implies that
DD^{\top}
is block-diagonal. In this case, one can easily verify
that the
\vec{p}
-block of
F^{-1}(x\oplus y)
is the same as
F^{-1}(x)
.

That is, once marginalizing over the parameters controlling the additional
data vector
\mathbf{y}
(e.g., the bispectrum), the information on the parameters

\vec{p}
is the same as when considering only the data vector
\mathbf{x}
(e.g., the power spectrum). It is worth highlighting that this is independent of the
precise model
\bar{\mathbf{y}}(\vec{q})
assumed. One canonical choice for this
would be to set
\bar{\mathbf{y}}=\vec{q}
, i.e. simply allow every element of the
bispectrum to be a free parameter.

This equivalence no longer holds as soon as one or more parameters
control both
\bar{\mathbf{x}}
and
\bar{\mathbf{y}}
, such that the matrix
D
is
no longer block-diagonal. In cosmology, this is usually the case.

\displaystyle+\sum_{n^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,n^{\prime}_{j-2}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{3}{(j-2)!}\frac{W_{j}({\bf{n}}_{2},{\bf{n}}_{3},{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j-2})W_{j+1}({\bf{n}}_{1},{\bf{m}},-{\bf{m}},{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j-2})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}})^{2}W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{1})W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{2})W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{3})W_{2}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1})\cdots W_{2}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j-2})}

\displaystyle+\sum_{n^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,n^{\prime}_{j-1}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{2}{(j-1)!}\frac{W_{j}({\bf{m}},{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j-1})W_{j+3}({\bf{n}}_{1},{\bf{n}}_{2},{\bf{n}}_{3},-{\bf{m}},{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j-1})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}})^{2}W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{1})W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{2})W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{3})W_{2}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1})\cdots W_{2}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j-1})}

\displaystyle+\sum_{n^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,n^{\prime}_{j-1}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{3}{(j-1)!}\frac{W_{j}({\bf{n}}_{1},{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j-1})W_{j+3}({\bf{n}}_{2},{\bf{n}}_{3},{\bf{m}},-{\bf{m}},{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j-1})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}})^{2}W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{1})W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{2})W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{3})W_{2}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1})\cdots W_{2}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j-1})}

\displaystyle+\sum_{n^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,n^{\prime}_{j-2}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{6}{(j-2)!}\frac{W_{j}({\bf{n}}_{1},{\bf{m}},{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j-2})W_{j+1}({\bf{n}}_{2},{\bf{n}}_{3},-{\bf{m}},{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j-2})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}})^{2}W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{1})W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{2})W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{3})W_{2}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1})\cdots W_{2}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j-2})}

\displaystyle+\sum_{n^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,n^{\prime}_{j-1}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{12\,\delta_{{\bf{n}}_{1}+{\bf{m}}}}{(j-1)!}\frac{W_{j}({\bf{n}}_{2},{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j-1})W_{j+1}({\bf{n}}_{3},{\bf{m}},{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j-1})}{W_{2}({\bf{m}})^{2}W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{2})W_{2}({\bf{n}}_{3})W_{2}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1})\cdots W_{2}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{j-1})}\Bigg]\Bigg\}\,.

(E.7)

Appendix F
On the Gaussian approximation to the power spectrum likelihood

In this appendix, we present an alternative derivation of the power spectrum likelihood that relies on the FLP expansion of correlators, and we emphasize the corrections to the standard Gaussian approximation given in Eq. (
4.21
).

F.1
Gaussian contribution

We begin with the Gaussian likelihood, Eq. (
2.23
), which, inserted in (
4.13
), gives

\displaystyle Z_{P,g}[J]=e^{W_{P,g}[J]}

\displaystyle=\int{\cal D}\delta_{\rm M}\,e^{-iJ\cdot\hat{P}[\delta_{\rm M}]}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{d}W_{2}^{-1}\delta_{d}}\det[2\pi W_{2}]^{-1/2}\,,

\displaystyle=\int\left(\Pi_{\bf{m}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{d\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}})}{\sqrt{2\pi\,W_{2}({\bf{m}})}}\right)e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bf{m}}^{m_{\rm max}}\left(\frac{1}{W_{2}({\bf{m}})}+2i\frac{J({\bf{m}})}{N({\bf{m}})}\right)|\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}})|^{2}}\,,

(F.1)

where the product and the sum run over all modes
{\bf{m}}
, and we have introduced the functions
J({\bf{m}})
and
N({\bf{m}})
, which evaluate to
J_{l}
and
N_{l}
, respectively, for
{\bf{m}}\in V_{l}
.
Redefining the field variable,

\tilde{\delta}_{\rm M}({\bf{m}})\equiv D({\bf{m}})^{1/2}\,\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}})=\left(1+2i\frac{J({\bf{m}})W_{2}({\bf{m}})}{N({\bf{m}})}\right)^{1/2}\,\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}})\,,

(F.2)

the Gaussian integrals in (
F.1
) give

\displaystyle e^{W_{P,g}[J]}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\bf{m}}^{m_{\rm max}}\left(1+2i\frac{J({\bf{m}})W_{2}({\bf{m}})}{N({\bf{m}})}\right)^{-1/2}\,,

\displaystyle=\Pi_{l=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}\,\Pi_{{\bf{n}}\in V_{l}}\left(1+2i\frac{J_{l}W_{2}(n)}{N_{l}}\right)^{-1/2}\,

\displaystyle\simeq\Pi_{l=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}\,\Pi_{{\bf{n}}\in V_{l}}\left(1+2i\frac{J_{l}W_{2}^{l}}{N_{l}}\right)^{-1/2}\,

\displaystyle=\exp\left[{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}N_{l}\log\left(1+2i\frac{J_{l}W_{2}^{l}}{N_{l}}\right)}\right]\,,

(F.3)

where, for
{\bf{n}}\in V_{l}
we have approximated each
W_{2}({\bf{n}})
with its bin averaged value. In the large
N_{l}
regime, the logarithm can be expanded, to give,

\displaystyle W_{P,g}[J]=\sum_{l=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-i)^{n}}{n}\left(\frac{2}{N_{l}}\right)^{n-1}\left(J_{l}W_{2}^{l}\right)^{n}\,,

(F.4)

which is also an expansion in the sources
J_{l}
. Truncating at second order, and inserting in Eq. (
4.11
), we get the Gaussian likelihood for the power spectrum,

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{P,g}[\hat{P}_{d}]

\displaystyle=\Pi_{l=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}\int\frac{dJ_{l}}{2\pi}\;e^{iJ_{l}(\hat{P}_{d}^{l}L^{-3}-W_{2}^{l})-\frac{(J_{l}W_{2}^{l})^{2}}{N_{l}}}\,,

\displaystyle=\Pi_{l=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}\;\frac{e^{-N_{l}\frac{(\hat{P}_{d}^{l}L^{-3}-W_{2}^{l})^{2}}{4(W_{2}^{l})^{2}}}}{\sqrt{4\pi L^{6}\frac{(W_{2}^{l})^{2}}{N_{l}}}}\,,

(F.5)

where the covariance is given at leading order by

\Sigma^{lm}\simeq\Sigma^{lm}_{g}=\frac{2}{N_{l}}(W_{2}^{l})^{2}\,\delta_{lm}\,.

(F.6)

From this likelihood we can compute the Fisher matrix. Allowing all the
W_{2}^{l}
to depend on the parameters, we can check that the maximum of the likelihood corresponds to
\hat{P}_{d}^{l}=L^{3}W_{2}^{l}+O(1/N_{l})
, and the second derivatives computed on these values give

F^{(2,2)}_{PS;ab}=\left.\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha_{a}\partial\alpha_{b}}\left(-\log{\cal L}_{P,g}\right)\right|_{\hat{P}_{d}=L^{3}W_{2}}=\sum_{l=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}\frac{\partial W_{2}^{l}}{\partial\alpha_{a}}\frac{\partial W_{2}^{l}}{\partial\alpha_{b}}\left(2\frac{(W_{2}^{l})^{2}}{N_{l}}\right)^{-1}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{N_{l}}\right)\right)\,,

(F.7)

where
O(1/N_{l})
corrections
include terms coming from cubic and higher orders in (
F.4
), like

e^{W_{P,g}[J]}=e^{-iJ_{l}W_{2}^{l}-\frac{(J_{l}W_{2}^{l})^{2}}{N_{l}}}\left(1+i\frac{4}{3}\frac{(J_{l}W_{2}^{l})^{3}}{N_{l}^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{N_{l}^{3}}\right)\right)\,.

(F.8)

F.2
First-order non-Gaussian contribution

The first non-Gaussian correction to the field-level likelihood, from Eq. (
2.22
), has the form

V_{I}\left[-i\frac{\delta}{\delta\delta_{\rm M}}\right]{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{\rm M}]\,,

(F.9)

which, inserted in (
4.13
), gives the non-Gaussian correction
\Delta Z_{P}[J]
.
The first contribution, which is linear in the three point function
W_{3}
, is zero because it involves three derivatives of the Gaussian likelihood and thus leads to a Gaussian integral over an odd number of fields. We are therefore lead to compute the contribution linear in the four point function,

\displaystyle\Delta Z_{P,W_{4}}[J]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4!}\sum_{{\bf{m}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{m}}_{4}}^{m_{\rm max}}W_{4}({\bf{m}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{m}}_{4})\int{\cal D}\delta_{\rm M}\;e^{-iJ\cdot\hat{P}[\delta_{\rm M}]}\frac{\delta^{4}}{\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}}_{4})}{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{\rm M}]\,.

(F.10)

The integral to be performed can be written as (see Eq. (
F.1
))

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}\delta_{\rm M}\;e^{-iJ\cdot\hat{P}[\delta_{\rm M}]}{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{\rm M}]\;{\cal H}_{4}[\delta_{\rm M}]\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}\delta_{\rm M}\;e^{-\frac{1}{2L^{3}}\sum_{\bf{m}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{|\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}})|^{2}}{W_{2}({\bf{m}})}D(m)}\;\;{\cal H}_{4}[\delta_{\rm M}]\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}\left(\tilde{\delta}_{\rm M}/D^{1/2}\right)\;e^{-\frac{1}{2L^{3}}\sum_{\bf{m}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{|\tilde{\delta}_{\rm M}({\bf{m}})|^{2}}{W_{2}({\bf{m}})}}\;\;{\cal H}_{4}[\tilde{\delta}_{\rm M}/D^{1/2}]\,,

(F.11)

which is a Gaussian integral on the variable
\tilde{\delta}_{\rm M}
.
We get,

\displaystyle\Delta Z_{P,W_{4}}[J]=

\displaystyle\;\;=e^{W_{P},g[J]}\frac{1}{4!}\sum_{{\bf{m}},{\bf{n}}}^{m_{\rm max}}W_{4}({\bf{m}},-{\bf{m}},{\bf{n}},-{\bf{n}})\frac{3}{W_{2}({\bf{m}})W_{2}({\bf{n}})}\left(\frac{1}{D({\bf{m}})D({\bf{n}})}-\frac{1}{D({\bf{m}})}-\frac{1}{D({\bf{n}})}+1\right)\,,

\displaystyle\;\;\simeq e^{W_{O},g[J]}\frac{1}{8}\sum_{l,m}^{N_{\rm bin}}\frac{W_{4}^{l,m}}{W_{2}^{l}W_{2}^{m}}N_{l}N_{m}\left(\frac{1}{D_{l}D_{m}}-\frac{1}{D_{l}}-\frac{1}{D_{m}}+1\right)\,,

\displaystyle\;\;=e^{W_{O},g[J]}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l,m}^{N_{\rm bin}}W_{4}^{l,m}J_{l}J_{m}+O\left(\frac{1}{N_{l,m}}\right)\right]\,,

(F.12)

where we have defined the bin averaged trispectrum as

W_{4}^{l,m}\equiv\frac{1}{N_{l}N_{m}}\sum_{{\bf{m}}\in V_{l}}\sum_{{\bf{n}}\in V_{m}}W_{4}({\bf{m}},-{\bf{m}},{\bf{n}},-{\bf{n}})\,,

(F.13)

and, again, approximated the
W_{2}({\bf{n}})
with their bin-averaged values.

Summing Eq. (
F.12
) and
Z_{P,g}[J]
, we obtain, from (
4.11
) after performing the integral in
J_{i}
,

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{P}[\hat{P}_{d}]

\displaystyle\simeq{\cal L}_{P,g}[\hat{P}_{d}]\Bigg[1-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{l,m=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}\frac{W_{4}^{l,m}}{W_{2}^{l}W_{2}^{m}}\left(\delta_{lm}N_{l}-N_{l}N_{m}\frac{(\hat{P}_{d}^{l}L^{-3}-W_{2}^{l})(\hat{P}_{d}^{m}L^{-3}-W_{2}^{m})}{2W_{2}^{l}W_{2}^{m}}\right)\Bigg]\,,

(F.14)

and the Fisher matrix elements are then

\displaystyle F^{(2,2)}_{PS;ab}=\left.\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha_{a}\partial\alpha_{b}}\left(-\log{\cal L}_{P}\right)\right|_{O_{d}=W_{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\simeq\sum_{l,m=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}\frac{\partial W_{2}^{l}}{\partial\alpha_{a}}\frac{\partial W_{2}^{m}}{\partial\alpha_{b}}\Bigg[\frac{N_{l}}{2(W_{2}^{l})^{2}}\delta_{lm}-\frac{1}{4}N_{l}N_{m}\frac{W_{4}^{l,m}}{(W_{2}^{l})^{2}(W_{2}^{m})^{2}}\Bigg]\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{N_{l,m}}\right)\right)\,,

(F.15)

where, the
O(W_{4})
term is the binned version of the one obtained at the field-level, given by the first line in Eq. (
3.12
). Taking the derivative of the trispectrum with respect to the parameters
\alpha_{a,b}
gives contributions suppressed by
O(1/N_{l,m})
.

The next contribution, still at linear order in
V_{I}
, comes from the connected six-point function
W_{6}
,

\displaystyle\Delta Z_{P,W_{6}}[J]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6!}\sum_{{\bf{m}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{m}}_{6}}^{m_{\rm max}}W_{6}({\bf{m}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{m}}_{6})\int{\cal D}\delta_{\rm M}\;e^{-iJ\cdot\hat{P}[\delta_{\rm M}]}\frac{\delta^{6}}{\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}}_{6})}{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{\rm M}]\,.

(F.16)

Proceeding as for
\Delta Z_{P,W_{4}}[J]
, we get

\Delta Z_{P,W_{6}}[J]=e^{W_{P},g[J]}\left[\frac{i}{3!}\sum_{lmn}^{N_{\rm bin}}W_{6}^{l,m,n}J_{l}J_{m}J_{n}+O\left(\frac{1}{N_{l,m,n}}\right)\right]\,,

(F.17)

where

W_{6}^{l,m,n}\equiv\frac{1}{N_{l}N_{m}N_{n}}\sum_{{\bf{m}}\in V_{l}}\sum_{{\bf{n}}\in V_{m}}\sum_{{\bf{l}}\in V_{n}}W_{6}({\bf{m}},-{\bf{m}},{\bf{n}},-{\bf{n}},{\bf{l}},-{\bf{l}})\,,

(F.18)

and, for a generic
2n
-point connected correlator,

\Delta Z_{P,W_{2n}}[J]=e^{W_{P},g[J]}\left[\frac{(-i)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{n}}^{N_{\rm bin}}W_{2n}^{i_{1},\cdots,i_{n}}J_{i_{1}}\cdots J_{i_{n}}+O\left(\frac{1}{N_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{n}}}\right)\right]\,,

(F.19)

where the bin-averaged correlators have been defined analogously to
W_{6}^{i,j,k}
in (
F.18
).
Including all the non-Gaussian contribution at linear order in
V_{I}
, and at leading order in
1/N_{i}
, then gives the power spectrum likelihood, from
(
4.11
),

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{P}

\displaystyle[\hat{P}_{d}]

\displaystyle\simeq{\cal L}_{P,g}[\hat{P}_{d}]\Bigg[1+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{n}=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}\frac{W_{2n}^{i_{1},\cdots,i_{n}}}{W_{2}^{i_{1}}\cdots W_{2}^{i_{n}}}\left(\frac{(\hat{P}_{d}^{i_{1}}L^{-3}-W_{2}^{i_{1}})}{2W_{2}^{i_{1}}/N_{i_{1}}}\cdots\frac{(\hat{P}_{d}^{i_{n}}L^{-3}-W_{2}^{i_{n}})}{2W_{2}^{i_{n}}/N_{i_{n}}}\right)\Bigg]\,.

(F.20)

Notice, however, that the contributions for
n>2
do not affect the Fisher matrix, which is still given by (
F.15
).

F.3
Higher-order non-Gaussian contributions

Inserting into (
4.13
) the second order non-Gaussian likelihood,

\frac{1}{2}\left(V_{I}\left[-i\frac{\delta}{\delta\delta_{\rm M}}\right]\right)^{2}{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{\rm M}]\,,

(F.21)

we get contributions from bispectrum squared,

\displaystyle\Delta Z_{P,W_{3}^{2}}[J]=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{-1}{3!}\right)^{2}\sum_{{\bf{n}}_{1}\,\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{3}}\sum_{{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1}\,\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{3}}\delta_{{\bf{n}}_{1,\cdots,3}}\delta_{{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1,\cdots,3}}W_{3}({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{3})W_{3}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{3})

\displaystyle\quad\times\int{\cal D}\delta_{\rm M}e^{-iJ\cdot\hat{P}[\delta_{\rm M}]}\frac{\delta^{3}}{\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{n}}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{n}}_{3})}\frac{\delta^{3}}{\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{3})}{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{\rm M}]\,,

(F.22)

and trispectrum squared,

\displaystyle\Delta Z_{P,W_{4}^{2}}[J]=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{4!}\right)^{2}\sum_{{\bf{n}}_{1}\,\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{4}}\sum_{{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1}\,\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{4}}\delta_{{\bf{n}}_{1,\cdots,4}}\delta_{{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1,\cdots,4}}W_{4}({\bf{n}}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}_{4})W_{4}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,{\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{4})

\displaystyle\quad\times\int{\cal D}\delta_{\rm M}e^{-iJ\cdot\hat{P}[\delta_{\rm M}]}\frac{\delta^{4}}{\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{n}}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{n}}_{4})}\frac{\delta^{4}}{\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{4})}{\cal L}_{g}[\delta_{\rm M}]\,.

(F.23)

We are interested in extracting the leading contributions in the large
N_{i}
limit.
To do so, we bring the functional derivatives on the source term in the integrals, leading to, in (
F.22
),

\displaystyle\int\left(\Pi_{\bf{m}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{d\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}})}{\sqrt{2\pi\,W_{2}({\bf{m}})}}\right)e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bf{m}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{|\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}})|^{2}}{W_{2}({\bf{m}})}}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\delta^{3}}{\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{n}}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{n}}_{3})}\frac{\delta^{3}}{\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{3})}\left(e^{-i\sum_{\bf{m}}\frac{J({\bf{m}})}{N({\bf{m}})}|\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}})|^{2}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle=\int\left(\Pi_{\bf{m}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{d\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}})}{\sqrt{2\pi\,W_{2}({\bf{m}})}}\right)e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bf{m}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{|\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}})|^{2}}{W_{2}({\bf{m}})}}e^{-i\sum_{\bf{m}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{J({\bf{m}})}{N({\bf{m}})}|\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}})|^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\delta^{3}}{\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{n}}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{n}}_{3})}\frac{\delta^{3}}{\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{1})\cdots\delta\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{n}}^{\prime}_{3})}\left[\frac{1}{3!}\left(-i\sum_{\bf{m}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{J({\bf{m}})}{N({\bf{m}})}|\delta_{\rm M}({\bf{m}})|^{2}\right)^{3}\right]+\cdots\,,

\displaystyle=i3!\,e^{W_{P,g}[J]}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sum_{{\bf{m}},{\bf{m}}^{\prime},{\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{J({\bf{m}})J({\bf{m}}^{\prime})J({\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime})}{N({\bf{m}})N({\bf{m}}^{\prime})N({\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime})}\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{1}+{\bf{m}}}\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{2}+{\bf{m}}^{\prime}}\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{3}+{\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime}}\delta_{{\bf{m}}^{\prime}_{1}-{\bf{m}}}\delta_{{\bf{m}}^{\prime}_{2}-{\bf{m}}^{\prime}}\delta_{{\bf{m}}^{\prime}_{3}-{\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime}}+\cdots\,,

(F.24)

where the dots indicate terms giving contributions subleading in
1/N({\bf{m}})
.
Inserting in (
F.22
) we get

\displaystyle\Delta Z_{P,W_{3}^{2}}[J]=\frac{i\,e^{W_{P,g}[J]}}{12}\sum_{{\bf{m}},{\bf{m}}^{\prime}}^{m_{\rm max}}\frac{J({\bf{m}})J({\bf{m}}^{\prime})J(-{\bf{m}}-{\bf{m}}^{\prime})}{N({\bf{m}})N({\bf{m}}^{\prime})N(-{\bf{m}}-{\bf{m}}^{\prime})}\,\left(W_{3}({\bf{m}},{\bf{m}}^{\prime},-{\bf{m}}-{\bf{m}}^{\prime})\right)^{2}+\cdots\,,

(F.25)

which is
O(1/N({\bf{m}}))
, since the sum runs only over two independent wavevectors,
{\bf{m}}
,
{\bf{m}}^{\prime}
.

Proceeding in a similar way, we obtain the contribution quadratic in the trispectrum as,

\displaystyle\Delta Z_{P,W_{4}^{2}}[J]=e^{W_{P,g}[J]}\Bigg\{\frac{1}{8}\left(\sum_{{\bf{m}},{\bf{m}}^{\prime}}^{m_{\rm max}}W_{4}({\bf{m}},-{\bf{m}},{\bf{m}}^{\prime},-{\bf{m}}^{\prime})\frac{J({\bf{m}})J({\bf{m}}^{\prime})}{N({\bf{m}})N({\bf{m}}^{\prime})}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{{\bf{m}},{\bf{m}}^{\prime}{\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime}}^{m_{\rm max}}\Bigg[W_{4}({\bf{m}},-{\bf{m}},{\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime},-{\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime})W_{4}(-{\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime},{\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime},{\bf{m}}^{\prime},-{\bf{m}}^{\prime})\frac{J({\bf{m}})J({\bf{m}}^{\prime})J({\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime})J(-{\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime})}{N({\bf{m}})N({\bf{m}}^{\prime})N({\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime})N(-{\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime})}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{3}\left(W_{4}({\bf{m}},{\bf{m}}^{\prime},{\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime},-{\bf{m}}-{\bf{m}}^{\prime}-{\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime})\right)^{2}\frac{J({\bf{m}})J({\bf{m}}^{\prime})J({\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime})J(-{\bf{m}}-{\bf{m}}^{\prime}-{\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime})}{N({\bf{m}})N({\bf{m}}^{\prime})N({\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime})N(-{\bf{m}}-{\bf{m}}^{\prime}-{\bf{m}}^{\prime\prime})}\Bigg]\Bigg\}\,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad=e^{W_{P,g}[J]}\frac{1}{8}\left(\sum_{j,k}^{N_{\rm bin}}W_{4}^{j,k}J_{j}J_{k}\right)^{2}+O\left(\frac{1}{N_{i}}\right)\,,

(F.26)

which, unlike
\Delta Z_{P,W_{3}^{2}}[J]
, contains an unsuppressed term. Summing it to (
F.19
) we get,

\displaystyle Z_{P}[J]\simeq

\displaystyle e^{W_{P},g[J]}\left[1-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k}^{N_{\rm bin}}W_{4}^{j,k}J_{j}J_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k}^{N_{\rm bin}}W_{4}^{j,k}J_{j}J_{k}\right)^{2}\right.\,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left.\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}\frac{(-i)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{n}}^{N_{\rm bin}}W_{2n}^{i_{1},\cdots,i_{n}}J_{i_{1}}\cdots J_{i_{n}}+O\left(\frac{1}{N_{i}}\right)\right]\,.

(F.27)

Inspecting the structure of unsuppressed terms at higher orders, one verifies that the
W_{4}
contributions indeed exponentiate,

Z_{P}[J]\simeq e^{W_{P},g[J]}\left[e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k}^{N_{\rm bin}}W_{4}^{j,k}J_{j}J_{k}}+\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}\frac{(-i)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{n}}^{N_{\rm bin}}W_{2n}^{i_{1},\cdots,i_{n}}J_{i_{1}}\cdots J_{i_{n}}\right]\,.

(F.28)

Taking the Fourier transform, Eq. (
4.11
),
the terms that involve powers of
W_{4}
sum up to the full inverse covariance of the power spectrum, i.e. the inverse of Eq. (
4.19
), while terms involving
W_{\geq 6}
lead to non-Gaussian corrections to the power spectrum likelihood:

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{P}[\hat{P}_{d}]\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{N_{\rm bin}}\det(L^{6}\Sigma)}}\,\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{P}_{d}L^{-3}-W_{2}\right)\cdot\Sigma^{-1}\cdot\left(\hat{P}_{d}L^{-3}-W_{2}\right)\right]\,

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{P,g}[\hat{P}_{d}]\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{n}=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}\frac{W_{2n}^{i_{1},\cdots,i_{n}}}{W_{2}^{i_{1}}\cdots W_{2}^{i_{n}}}\left(\frac{(P_{d}^{i_{1}}L^{-3}-W_{2}^{i_{1}})}{2W_{2}^{i_{1}}/N_{i_{1}}}\cdots\frac{(P_{d}^{i_{n}}L^{-3}-W_{2}^{i_{n}})}{2W_{2}^{i_{n}}/N_{i_{n}}}\right)\,,

(F.29)

with
\Sigma^{jk}
the power spectrum covariance. The first line matches the Gaussian covariance form, Eq. (
4.21
), derived in Sect.
4.1
, and thus reproduces the same Fisher matrix, namely Eq. (
4.22
).

If, however, one is interested in the full likelihood for the power spectrum and not only in the Fisher matrix, the second line of Eq. (
F.29
), which does not affect the Fisher matrix, must also be taken into account. Notice that, for example, the term quadratic in
W_{4}
appearing in the first line is of higher order than the term linear in
W_{6}
in the second line, when adopting a perturbation-theory power counting in the power spectrum.

F.4
Likelihood for the bispectrum

The estimator for the bispectrum is

B^{t}[\delta]=\frac{1}{L^{3}N_{T}^{t}}\sum_{{\bf{m}}_{1}\in V_{i}}\sum_{{\bf{m}}_{2}\in V_{j}}\sum_{{\bf{m}}_{3}\in V_{k}}\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{123}}\,\delta({\bf{m}}_{1})\delta({\bf{m}}_{2})\delta({\bf{m}}_{3})\,,

(F.30)

where
t
labels the set of closed triangles with side lengths in the momentum bins
V_{i}
,
V_{j}
, and
V_{k}
, and
N_{T}^{t}
is the number of triangles in the bin
t
.
Following Eq. (
4.1
), we can define a likelihood for the bispectrum,

{\cal L}_{B}[B_{d}]=\int{\cal D}J\,e^{iJ\cdot B_{d}}\,Z_{B}[J]\,,

(F.31)

with

Z_{B}[J]=e^{W_{B}[J]}=\int{\cal D}\delta\,e^{-iJ\cdot B[\delta]}\,{\cal L}[\delta]\,,

(F.32)

and

\displaystyle J\cdot B

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{L^{6}}\sum_{t}^{\text{b.t.}}\,J_{t}\,B^{t}\,,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L^{9}}\sum_{{\bf{m}}_{1}\in V_{i}}\sum_{{\bf{m}}_{2}\in V_{j}}\sum_{{\bf{m}}_{3}\in V_{k}}\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{123}}\frac{J({\bf{m}}_{1},{\bf{m}}_{2},{\bf{m}}_{3})}{N({\bf{m}}_{1},{\bf{m}}_{2},{\bf{m}}_{3})}\,\delta({\bf{m}}_{1})\delta({\bf{m}}_{2})\delta({\bf{m}}_{3})\,,

(F.33)

where the first sum is taken over all binned triangles.
We define

J({\bf{m}}_{1},{\bf{m}}_{2},{\bf{m}}_{3})=J_{t}\,,\quad N({\bf{m}}_{1},{\bf{m}}_{2},{\bf{m}}_{3})=N_{T}^{t}\,,\qquad\text{for}\;{\bf{m}}_{1}\in V_{i}\,,{\bf{m}}_{2}\in V_{j}\,,{\bf{m}}_{3}\in V_{k}\,,

(F.34)

and

{\cal D}J\equiv\Pi_{t}^{\text{b}.t.}\,\frac{dJ_{t}}{2\pi}\,.

(F.35)

We then expand
W_{B}[J]
around
J=0
,

\displaystyle W_{B}[J]=

\displaystyle W_{B}[0]+\sum_{t}^{\text{b.t.}}\left.\frac{\partial W_{B}[J]}{\partial J_{t}}\right|_{J=0}J_{t}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t,u}^{\text{b.t.}}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}W_{B}[J]}{\partial J_{t}\partial J_{u}}\right|_{J=0}J_{t}\,J_{u}+O(J^{3})\,.

(F.36)

From Eq. (
F.32
) we get,

W_{B}[0]=0\,,\qquad\left.\frac{\partial W_{B}[J]}{\partial J_{t}}\right|_{J=0}=-\frac{i}{L^{6}}W_{3}^{t}\,,

(F.37)

where
W_{3}^{t}
is the binned bispectrum of the model,

W_{3}^{t}\equiv\frac{1}{N_{T}^{t}}\sum_{{\bf{m}}_{1}\in V_{i}}\sum_{{\bf{m}}_{2}\in V_{j}}\sum_{{\bf{m}}_{3}\in V_{k}}\delta_{{\bf{m}}_{123}}\,W_{3}({\bf{m}}_{1},{\bf{m}}_{2},{\bf{m}}_{3})\,.

(F.38)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial^{2}W_{B}[J]}{\partial J_{t}\partial J_{u}}\right|_{J=0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{L^{12}}\left[\langle B^{t}B^{u}\rangle-\langle B^{t}\rangle\langle B^{u}\rangle\right]\,,

\displaystyle\equiv-\Sigma_{B}^{t,u}\,,

(F.39)

is the bispectrum covariance. It can be decomposed into four contributions,

\Sigma_{B}^{t,u}=\delta_{tu}\Sigma_{g}^{t}+\Sigma_{W_{3}^{2}}^{t,u}+\Sigma_{W_{2}W_{4}}^{t,u}+\Sigma_{W_{6}}^{t,u}\,,

(F.40)

where the first ‘Gaussian’ term includes contributions that are cubic in the power spectrum, the second involves terms quadratic in the bispectrum, and the third consists of products of the trispectrum with the power spectrum.
We therefore obtain

\displaystyle e^{W_{B}[J]}=

\displaystyle\,e^{-iJ\cdot W_{3}-\frac{1}{2}J\cdot\Sigma_{W_{2}^{3}}\cdot J}\left[1-\frac{1}{2}J\cdot\left(\Sigma_{W_{3}^{2}}+\Sigma_{W_{2}W_{4}}+\Sigma_{W_{6}}\right)\cdot J+O(J^{3})\right]\,,

(F.41)

where, in analogy with the treatment of the power spectrum, only the first of the three covariance contributions has been exponentiated.
This gives the bispectrum likelihood via Eq. (
F.31
),

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{B}[B_{d}]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{N_{\rm bt}}\det(\Sigma_{g})}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t}^{N_{\rm bt}}\frac{(B_{d}^{t}-W_{3}^{t})^{2}}{\Sigma_{g}^{t}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\times\Bigg(1-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t,u}^{N_{\rm bt}}\frac{\Sigma_{W_{3}^{2}}^{t,u}+\Sigma_{W_{2}W_{4}}^{t,u}+\Sigma_{W_{6}}^{t,u}}{\Sigma_{g}^{t}\Sigma_{g}^{u}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times(B_{d}^{t}-W_{3}^{t})(B_{d}^{u}-W_{3}^{u})+O\left((B_{d}-W_{3})\right)^{3}\Bigg)\,.

(F.42)

Computing the Fisher matrix from the Gaussian part, we recover the field-level result, Eq. (
3.16
).
This is the likelihood-level counterpart of the comparison between Eqs. (
3.17
) and (
3.16
). In the present notation, the Gaussian bispectrum likelihood leads to the same Fisher information once the sum over triangle bins
t
and their covariance
\Sigma_{g}^{t}
are identified with the standard bispectrum data vector and covariance.
