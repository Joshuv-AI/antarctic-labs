ABSTRACT
BackgroundNoncommunicable diseases (NCDs), particularly cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), hypertension and diabetes, are major contributors to morbidity and mortality globally and in Rwanda, where the dual burden of HIV and NCDs is increasing. Evidence remains limited on how biological, behavioural, social, environmental and health-system determinants influence cardiovascular and metabolic outcomes among people living with HIV (PLHIV) in Rwanda. The NCOHIRWA cohort was established to prospectively investigate determinants associated with CVDs, hypertension, diabetes and related NCD outcomes among PLHIV, newly diagnosed PLHIV and people without HIV (PWoH). This manuscript presents the protocol for the NCOHIRWA cohort study.MethodsThe NCOHIRWA study is a prospective cohort with a mixed-methods implemented across 12 health facilities providing HIV and NCD services in Rwanda. Adults aged ≥ 18 years, including PLHIV, newly diagnosed PLHIV and PWoH were enrolled beginning in March 2021 and will be followed longitudinally for 10 years. Baseline and follow-up data capture sociodemographic characteristics, behavioural, psychosocial, family history of NCDs, environmental and health-system factors, and clinical, anthropometric and laboratory measures relevant to cardiovascular and metabolic health.ResultsA total of 1,546 participants with complete baseline data were included, comprising 1,234 (79.8%) PLHIV and 312 (20.2%) PWoH. The median age was 44 years (range: 18–81) among PLHIV and 42 years (range: 18–75) among PWoH. Women represented 785 (63.6%) PLHIV and 197 (63.1%) PWoH. Baseline determinants relevant to future NCD outcome analyses included overweight [256 (21.0%) PLHIV versus 75 (24.4%) PWoH], obesity [118 (9.7%) versus 39 (12.6%)], ever smoking [260 (21.1%) versus 55 (17.7%)], family history of hypertension [219 (17.8%) versus 84 (26.9%)] and elevated fasting blood glucose [37 (3.0%) versus 28 (9.1%)]. These baseline characteristics define the cohort profile and provide the foundation for longitudinal analyses of incident cardiovascular and metabolic outcomes.DiscussionThis protocol describes the design and baseline profile of the NCOHIRWA cohort study. The cohort is positioned to generate longitudinal evidence on determinants of cardiovascular and metabolic NCDs among PLHIV in Rwanda. As follow-up accrues,the study will support estimation of incident outcomes, identification of risk pathways and development of context-appropriate strategies for integrated HIV and NCD prevention and care.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

Noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) are the foremost cause of death worldwide, accounting for 41 million deaths each year. Every two seconds, an individual dies from an NCD before the age of 70 years, resulting in premature mortality, with 77% of these deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the predominant cause of mortality within the spectrum of NCDs and are driven by modifiable risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, tobacco use, dyslipidaemia, excess weight, unhealthy diets and physical inactivity. In Rwanda, CVD accounted for 17% of all deaths in 2019, representing 51% of deaths attributed to NCDs [1–3].

At the end of 2023, approximately 39.9 million people were living with HIV globally, 65% of whom were in Africa, with sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) home to approximately 20.8 million people living with HIV (PLHIV) [4, 5].

The widespread availability of antiretroviral therapy (ART) has transformed HIV into a manageable chronic condition. In Rwanda, 218,314 (92.3%) PLHIV receive ART, and globally, 29.8 million people are on treatment. Despite improved access to care, PLHIV, particularly those in SSA, are undergoing rapid epidemiological and sociodemographic transitions and are at increased risk of developing NCDs because of ageing, urbanisation, chronic immune activation, systemic inflammation and long-term ART exposure [5–8].

Understanding NCD risk requires a comprehensive view of determinants of health, which encompass multiple interacting domains. These include biological determinants (age, sex, genetic predisposition and comorbidities), behavioural determinants (diet, physical activity, tobacco and alcohol use), social determinants (education, income, employment and social support), environmental determinants (urbanisation, living conditions and food environment) and health-system or structural determinants (access to care, continuity of ART and integration of HIV and NCD services) [9]. Together, these determinants may shape vulnerability to CVD, hypertension, diabetes and other NCDs among PLHIV.

Assessment and management of CVDs, hypertension, diabetes and related NCDs are increasingly important components of care in SSA. The need to understand the epidemiology, pathophysiology, prevention and treatment of complications in the context of HIV, and to translate this evidence into policy and clinical practice, is widely recognised. Despite this recognition of the dual burden of HIV and NCDs, evidence from Rwanda remains limited. Since June 2016, Rwanda has implemented immediate ART initiation upon HIV diagnosis [10–12]. However, gaps persist in the capacity to provide comprehensive long-term chronic care services, including integrated screening and management of common NCDs.

Without strengthened systems, the progress achieved through HIV care and treatment programs may be undermined by rising NCD-related morbidity and mortality among PLHIV.

We hypothesised that validated and affordable screening methods, including lipid profiling, fasting blood glucose testing, coagulation and inflammation marker assessments, and standardised blood pressure monitoring, could support development of a risk-stratification framework for CVD and related metabolic outcomes among PLHIV in resource-constrained settings.

The NCOHIRWA cohort was therefore established to prospectively examine biological, behavioural, social, environmental and structural determinants associated with CVD, hypertension, diabetes and related outcomes among PLHIV and PWoH in Rwanda. The study aims to generate evidence to inform integrated, person-centred models of care and to support the development of context-appropriate CVD risk prediction tools for Rwanda and similar settings. Here, we present the protocol for the NCOHIRWA prospective cohort study and the baseline profile of enrolled participants.

Methods

Design

This is a prospective cohort study with a mixed-methods component. The quantitative arm follows adults with HIV, newly diagnosed PLHIV and PWoH longitudinally to assess incident CVD, hypertension, diabetes and related NCD outcomes. The qualitative component captures perspectives from PLHIV serving as peer educators, healthcare providers and stakeholders involved in the implementation of HIV and NCD initiatives. The comparison group of PWoH allows assessment of differences in risk pathways by HIV status while preserving the prospective cohort design.

Setting

Participants were recruited from health facilities in five provinces across Rwanda. The preselection process identified health facilities with HIV and NCD services, suitable infrastructure, trained healthcare professionals capable of conducting examinations, a functional electronic medical record system and capacity to follow up patients who develop NCDs. Participants

Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older who were engaged in clinical care at a participating health facility, were able to provide written informed consent and were willing to commit to long-term follow-up. These criteria were selected to include adults representative of routine HIV and primary-care populations while minimising collider bias by avoiding selection based on existing NCD outcomes. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy or being less than 3 months postpartum; a pre-existing diagnosis of CVD, hypertension, diabetes or cancer; evident mental health disorders that impaired consent or participation; or blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg at screening, for which participants were referred for clinical management. Excluding individuals with established NCDs reduces baseline confounding when assessing incident outcomes, while excluding pregnancy and acute mental health conditions minimises physiological and behavioural confounding that could distort cardiometabolic measurements.

Eligible PLHIV participants already on ART were identified from programme databases at the selected health facilities. Proportional random selection was used to obtain the required number of participants per site. Those who agreed to participate received an appointment for screening. On the day of the appointment, all participants were screened for hypertension; those with blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg were excluded from enrolment for incident hypertension analyses and were advised to seek care in the NCD clinic for confirmation and further management.

Newly diagnosed PLHIV were defined as individuals first diagnosed with HIV during recruitment or within six months of starting ART after diagnosis.

Upon confirmation of their HIV status, the voluntary counselling and testing services team facilitated the connection of these patients with trained mobilisers, comprising nurses and social workers from HIV clinics located at selected sites in proximity to the university teaching hospital of Kigali (CHUK), who assessed the participants’ eligibility criteria. If participants agreed to take part in the study, the mobiliser contacted the research personnel at CHUK to arrange enrollment and examinations within a one-week timeframe.

PWoH were identified and mobilised from voluntary counselling and testing services and neighbouring communities among individuals who opted to test for HIV, thereby ensuring that the comparison group came from the same source population as the PLHIV enrolled in the study. Inclusion criteria for PWoH were identical to those for PLHIV, with the additional criterion that they were HIV negative and lived in the catchment area of the participating health facility. At each visit, all participants were screened for HIV to confirm their status.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes are incident CVD risk factors and events during follow-up, including newly detected high blood pressure, metabolic syndrome features, lipid disorders, elevated blood glucose, type 2 diabetes and CVD events such as ischaemic heart disease, myocardial infarction, hospitalisation for unstable angina, coronary revascularisation by percutaneous intervention or surgery, hospitalisation for heart failure, stroke and cardiovascular mortality.

Secondary outcomes include incident non-cardiovascular NCDs and all-cause mortality during follow-up.

Key covariates include age, sex, marital status, education level, employment status, socioeconomic status, smoking status, alcohol use, physical activity, diet, body mass index (BMI), family history of NCDs and clinical/laboratory measures. For PLHIV, HIV-related covariates include ART regimen, CD4 count where available and viral load.

Study procedures

Upon enrolment, which began in March 2021, participants undergo scheduled study visits every 12 months for follow-up data collection during the first three years and every three years thereafter over a 10-year period. A ± 12-month visit window is used to accommodate missed appointments. To reduce attrition bias, the study employs facility-based tracking, follow-up phone calls, peer educator outreach for missed visits and integration with routine clinical appointments to encourage retention. At each visit, participant characteristics are collected to track prespecified outcomes. HIV screening with counselling is conducted for PWoH to ascertain HIV status. Participants diagnosed with HIV at any point during the study are promptly referred to an HIV care and treatment programme for confirmation and management. When an NCD is identified at any visit, the observation is recorded and the participant is referred to the NCD service for continued care in line with established management protocols.

Key definitions

Definitions of the eligibility criteria

Hypertension is characterised as the presence of two or three consecutive measurements of systolic (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP), where the mean SBP is equal to or exceeds 140 mmHg and/or the mean DBP is equal to or exceeds 90 mmHg, and includes individuals who are using antihypertensive medication [13]

CVDs are defined as a group of disorders of the heart and blood vessels and include coronary heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, rheumatic heart disease, peripheral arterial disease, congenital heart diseases, deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism [14]

Diabetes is a chronic, metabolic disease characterised by elevated levels of blood glucose (or blood sugar) [15]

Cancer is defined as a disease of uncontrolled proliferation by transformed cells subjected to evolution by natural selection [16]

Mental disorders are characterised by a clinically significant disturbance in an individual’s cognition, emotional regulation or behavior [17]

Weight was measured in kilograms (kg), and height was measured in centimeters (cm). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight (kg) by height (m) squared and expressed as kg/m2. The following BMI classifications were used: underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥30 kg/m2) [19]. Waist circumference was measured at the midpoint between the lower margin of the last palpable rib and the top of the iliac crest (hip bone). The hip circumference was measured around the widest portion of the buttocks, with the tape parallel to the floor [20]. The waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) is determined by dividing the waist circumference (cm) by the hip circumference (cm). A WHR greater than 0.90 in men and 0.85 in women is considered indicative of an elevated metabolic disease risk. For both men and women, a waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) greater than 0.49 is considered indicative of abdominal obesity. WHtR is calculated by dividing the waist measurement (cm) by the height (cm) [21].

Blood pressure (BP) readings were taken three times following a 10-minute rest period, spaced 3–5 minutes apart, via a digital automatic blood pressure monitor (Omron M2 Eco (HEM-7120-AF)). For analytical purposes, the mean of these three readings was used. BP classifications were as follows: normal-optimal (less than 130/85 mmHg), high-normal (130–139/85–89 mmHg), grade 1 hypertension (140–159/90–99 mmHg), and grade 2 hypertension (greater than 160/100 mmHg) [13]

Glycemia was assessed after 8 to 12 hours of fasting. The results were categorised as follows: low fasting blood glucose (<3.9 mmol/l); normal fasting blood glucose (<5.6 mmol/l); high fasting blood glucose (5.6 - 6.9 mmol/l); and ≥7 mmol/l) on two separate occasions is indicative of diabetes [22].

The lipid profile measurements included total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), and triglycerides. An abnormal lipid profile was classified as follows: total cholesterol ≥ 200 mg/dL, HDL-c < 40 mg/dL, LDL-c ≥ 130 mg/dL, non-HDL cholesterol ≥ 160 mg/dL, TG ≥ 150 mg/dL, and total cholesterol/HDL-c ratio ≥ 5 [23].

LDL-c was calculated via the Martin–Hopkins formula [23], chosen because it provides a comparable estimate for participants with low and normal triglyceride concentrations (<70 mg/dL and 70 - 400 mg/dL, respectively) and a more accurate estimate at high triglyceride concentrations (>400 mg/dL) [23, 24]. Abnormal levels of any of the aforementioned lipid parameters, one or more, are considered dyslipidemic.

Metabolic syndrome was defined as the presence of any three or more of the following conditions: high blood glucose levels, low HDL-c levels, high triglyceride levels, a large waist circumference (>102 cm in men and >88 cm in women), and hypertension.

Blood samples for laboratory tests were drawn at healthcare facilities by certified laboratory technicians via a uniform protocol. After centrifugation, the samples were allocated into two cryotubes, one for immediate analysis and the other for biobanking, and stored at −20 °C. These samples were later conveyed to the Rwanda National Reference Laboratory, a facility with ISO15189 accreditation. The samples were analysed using the roche Cobas c311, c411, and c4800 systems.

Sample size

For sample-size estimation, PLHIV in Rwanda were assumed to be three times more likely to experience CVD events than PWoH. Data on CVD events are collected at each study visit and will be analysed with models appropriate for prospective cohort data [25]. A two-sided significance level of 5 and 80% power to detect a true difference in CVD events between PLHIV and PWoH were assumed. The total sample size was estimated at 1,516 participants. After accounting for an anticipated 10% dropout or loss to follow-up, the target enrolment was 1,668 participants. Participants were allocated at an approximate 4:1 ratio because adults with HIV on treatment are recommended to undergo NCD testing every 3 months, whereas PWoH are advised to test once a year. This allocation corresponds to 1,334 PLHIV and 334 PWoH participants. In addition, we planned to enrol a convenience sample of 120 newly diagnosed PLHIV.

Statistical considerations

Categorical variables will be summarised using frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables will be summarised using means and standard deviations for approximately normally distributed data and medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for non-normally distributed data. Baseline results are reported as descriptive characteristics of the cohort and not as prevalence estimates intended to define disease burden.

For longitudinal follow-up, incident CVD, hypertension, diabetes and related NCD outcomes will be assessed from baseline to the date of first diagnosis, death, last completed study visit or censoring, whichever occurs first. Incidence rates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) will be calculated as the number of new events divided by person-time at risk.

Kaplan–Meier time-to-event curves will be used to visualise cumulative incidence of major outcomes by HIV status and other key determinants, and groups will be compared using log-rank tests where appropriate.

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models will be used to estimate associations between determinants of health and incident outcomes. Results will be reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs. The proportional hazards assumption will be assessed using log-log plots and other diagnostic procedures as appropriate.

For binary outcomes assessed over fixed follow-up intervals, modified Poisson regression with robust error variance will be used to estimate adjusted relative risks [26]. Multivariable models will include potential confounders selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature, including age, sex, family history of CVD, systolic blood pressure, smoking status, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, HIV status and diabetes, where appropriate.

HIV status is a main exposure of interest, and additional HIV-related predictors among PLHIV will include ART regimen, viral load and CD4 count where available. The analytic plan therefore reflects the prospective cohort design by distinguishing baseline descriptive data from longitudinal outcome analyses.

Established cardiovascular risk models, including D:A:D 2010 and 2016, Framingham CVD and CHD models, PREVENT, ASCVD and WHO cardiovascular disease risk charts, will be evaluated for applicability in the local context [27–33]. This benchmarking will inform whether existing models require recalibration or adaptation for Rwanda and similar settings [26, 31]. Where model performance is insufficient, the cohort data will support development of a context-appropriate risk prediction approach based on observed longitudinal outcomes and locally relevant determinants.

Baseline participant characteristics and physical measurement data were collected using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) application preloaded on tablets. Laboratory results were extracted from the laboratory information system. Microsoft Excel (MS Excel 16.89.1) and the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 28.0.1) were used for data processing and data cleaning, and R (version 4.2.1) was used for data analysis.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Rwanda National Ethics Committee (RNEC) under approval No. 210/RNEC/2020 dated 13 March 2020 and No. 84/RNEC/2021 dated 15 March 2021, and aligns with good clinical practice guidelines. All participants provided written informed consent before taking part in the study. Participant information is kept confidential through coded identifiers and a password-protected database. Each participant received a unique research identification number.

Selected baseline characteristics of study participants by HIV status

The median age of PLHIV was 44 years (range: 18–81), compared with 42 years (range: 18–75) among PWoH. Women comprised 785 (63.6%) PLHIV and 197 (63.1%) PWoH. More than half of PLHIV [684 (55.5%)] and two-thirds of PWoH [209 (67.0%)] were married or cohabiting. Educational attainment was similar across groups, with 603 (48.9%) PLHIV and 152 (48.7%) PWoH having completed primary or partial secondary education. Ever smoking was reported by 260 (21.1%) PLHIV and 55 (17.7%) PWoH; among those who had ever smoked, most were current smokers. Alcohol consumption was reported by 710 (57.6%) PLHIV and 163 (52.2%) PWoH. Mean BMI was 23.48 ± 4.51 kg/m2 among PLHIV and 24.28 ± 4.93 kg/m2 among PWoH, with overweight of 256 (21.0%) and 75 (24.4%), respectively. High-normal blood pressure was observed in 262 (21.2%) PLHIV and 63 (20.2%) PWoH. Liver enzyme levels were higher among PLHIV (ALAT: 17.6 U/L vs 14.1 U/L; ASAT: 29.1 U/L vs 24.0 U/L), as were median creatinine levels (77.0 µmol/L, IQR: 64.0–91.0 vs 69.0 µmol/L, IQR: 60.0–80.0). Total protein levels were similar between groups.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, NCOHIRWA is the first prospective cohort study designed to follow CVD, hypertension, diabetes and related NCD outcomes among PLHIV and PWoH in Rwanda. HIV infection, ART exposure and interactions with underlying and often unrecognised cardiometabolic risk factors may contribute to premature NCD risk, underscoring the need for early detection and prevention. This protocol describes the design and baseline profile of the NCOHIRWA cohort, which aims to evaluate determinants of major cardiovascular and metabolic NCDs and support development of context-appropriate risk prediction and integrated care approaches in Rwanda and similar settings.

The baseline characteristics highlight sociodemographic, behavioural, clinical and laboratory patterns that are relevant to future longitudinal analyses. PLHIV were more likely to be widowed, have lower educational attainment, and present with elevated blood pressure and higher smoking prevalence than PWoH [34, 35].

The age and sex distribution of PLHIV in cohort aligns with national ART program data, with more than 20% aged above 50 years and a predominance of women [36], consistent with regional and global trends [37–43]. Overweight and obesity were common, with 21.0% of PLHIV overweight and 9.7% obese at baseline, comparable to findings from other African cohorts [44, 45]. Similar prevalence patterns have been reported in Kenya [46], and in general population studies across the region [47], reflecting shared demographic and lifestyle transitions.

The proportion of participants with high-normal blood pressure in the cohort falls between estimates reported from Tanzania and Uganda. Smoking was reported less frequently among PLHIV than in many African studies, consistent with Rwanda’s generally low national smoking rates, yet remained higher in PLHIV than in PWoH. Alcohol consumption was also more common among PLHIV and is concerning given its association with increased CVD risk, reduced ART efficacy and poorer adherence [37, 38, 48–54].

Baseline lipid profiles revealed modest differences between PLHIV and PWoH. TC level were lower among PLHIV (157.3 ± 39.7 mg/dL) compared with PWoH (165.8 ± 47.6 mg/dL),while TG level were similar in both groups (94.8 mg/dL vs 96.1 mg/dL). HDLc levels were also comparable (47.1 mg/dL vs 46.4 mg/dL). These patterns are consistent with several recent studies reporting lower TC and broadly similar TC and HDL-C levels among PLHIV [55], although lipid profiles vary across settings.

With respect to glycemic measures, the baseline findings differ from those of a cross-sectional study in central Kenya, which reported a substantially higher prevalence of prediabetes (14.2%) among PLHIV [56]. Multiple studies have similarly documented elevated fasting blood glucose levels in PLHIV compared with those in PWoH [57]. A systematic review reported a high prevalence of prediabetes (15%) among PLHIV in Africa [58], while a study from a high-income country reported 8.1% prevalence in the general population [59]. These variations likely reflect differences in ART exposure and metabolic risk profiles, underscoring the importance of longitudinal follow-up in our cohort to better understand glycemic trajectories over time.

Liver enzyme (ALAT and ASAT) and creatinine were higher among PLHIV than PWoH, consistent with previous studies linking ART exposure to hepatotoxicity and early renal impairment [60, 61]. Total protein level were similar between PLHIV and PWoH, aligning with findings from other cohorts [62].

These baseline findings are consistent with other African studies documenting a substantial burden of NCD risk factors among PLHIV and related populations, reinforcing the need to strengthen health systems to prevent and manage chronic diseases. Across the region, integration of HIV and NCD care services remains limited. Studies from South Africa and other countries highlight persistent barriers, including under-resourced facilities, fragmented care pathways and limited access to NCD medicines, which may contribute to suboptimal management of comorbid conditions [7, 46, 63, 64]. Our findings support the need for integrated, patient-centred models of care.

A major strength of the NCOHIRWA cohort is its large sample size and prospective design, which surpass many comparable studies in the region. Whereas previous investigations often relied on cross-sectional designs with small samples and limited geographic coverage, this cohort follows a large number of participants over time, enabling robust longitudinal analyses.

Rwanda’s rapid socioeconomic transitions, mirroring broader patterns across SSA, have contributed to rising exposure to NCD risk factors among vulnerable populations, as highlighted in recent analyses of the region’s shifting disease burden [65].

Longitudinal evidence from Zimbabwe and Uganda has demonstrated increasing cumulative incidence of NCDs among PLHIV with longer ART exposure. In Zimbabwe, NCDs accounted for 56% of morbidity and 40% of mortality among PLHIV in a decade-long cohort, underscoring the importance of integrated NCD prevention and management into HIV care [66, 67].

Compared with well established cohorts in high-income countries, studies in LMICs often face challenges in accessing complete medical histories, limiting the ability to identify long-term risk trajectories. The prospective design of the NCOHIRWA cohort therefore provides a more rigorous framework for establishing risk factors and causal pathways. By enabling long-term follow-up, the cohort will support analyses that move beyond prediction to generate evidence based on observed outcomes.

The absence of adequate research facilities in sub-Saharan Africa poses significant challenges for conducting CV examinations and implementing measures across healthcare facilities. Furthermore, the scarcity of experts inclined toward research impedes the development of evidence-based interventions and policies to address the CVD burden in the region. To address these issues, a substudy focused on CV examinations is underway at Kigali (CHUK), where the necessary facilities and expertise are readily accessible. By harnessing the existing infrastructure and the expertise available at CHUK, this substudy holds promise for generating valuable insights into cardiovascular health and disease management within the SSA context.

Limited research funding for NCDs in SSA remains a major barrier to evidence generation. Despite the disproportionate burden of NCDs in LMICs, including SSA, research funding continues to focus primarily on communicable diseases, neglecting the needs of populations experiencing excess morbidity and mortality from NCDs, as well as low research output compared with other regions [68–70]. Additionally, the absence of quality health information due to the funding gap is a major obstacle in developing national strategies for an effective response to NCDs, perpetuating the dependence of African research on Western nations for support and perpetuating global health disparities in research output and funding allocation [71, 72]. By generating high-quality longitudinal data, the NCOHIRWA cohort contributes to addressing these gaps and supports the development of evidence-based policies.

The study also benefits from rigorous data collection procedures, including the use of validated questionnaires and standardised measurement protocols. The inclusion of both rural and urban participants enhances the representativeness of the cohort, whereas many previous studies have focused exclusively on either urban or rural settings. As the cohort matures, longitudinal follow-up will allow for more detailed assessment of incident NCD outcomes and evolving risk profiles among PLHIV, including contrasts with older cohorts such as the REPRIEVE trial [73].

Overall, the baseline profile of the NCOHIRWA cohort highlights the importance of integrating NCD screening, prevention and management into routine HIV services. As follow-up data accrue, the cohort will enable estimation of incident outcomes and investigation of determinants and risk pathways to inform person-centred models of care addressing both infectious and chronic disease burdens.

Limitations

The study encountered several operational challenges during implementation. The COVID-19 pandemic created substantial logistical constraints, particularly in the procurement of laboratory reagents and consumables sourced from international suppliers. Global transportation disruptions and stock-outs at manufacturing sites resulted in delays in laboratory processing. These challenges required protocol adjustments, which were communicated to the ethics committee during the annual renewal process.

Another challenge was limited awareness among participants regarding their blood pressure status. Approximately 10% of screened participants had blood pressure measurements above 140/90 mmHg and were therefore excluded on the day of their appointment and referred for clinical care. This required an extension of the enrolment period to achieve the desired sample size. Although the cohort is broadly representative of PLHIV attending health facilities in Rwanda, differences in healthcare access, urbanisation and demographic characteristics may limit comparability with non-facility-based populations in other settings.

The cohort includes relatively young adults, which is important because PLHIV may have a higher relative risk of CVD than the general population, including among those who acquired HIV early in life. Although the date of enrolment in HIV treatment is usually available, the exact duration of HIV infection may be uncertain and was not fully captured for this analysis. This may limit assessment of cumulative HIV exposure as a determinant of future NCD outcomes.

Furthermore, baseline data reflect a relatively young cohort, which may partly explain the higher proportion of participants meeting ideal CVD health criteria compared with older cohorts [73]. As with all prospective cohort studies, loss to follow-up remains a potential source of bias over time. In addition, despite standardised measurement protocols and planned multivariable analyses, residual confounding may persist. Finally, longitudinal outcome accrual requires time, and early reports from the cohort should therefore be interpreted primarily as protocol and baseline descriptive evidence rather than definitive outcome evidence.
