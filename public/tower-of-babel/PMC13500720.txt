Friction and wear behavior of TiN/Ti6Al4V composite material synthesized in-situ using laser directed energy deposition
Authors: Zhiheng Tai, Yang Wei, Vyacheslav Trofimov, Di Wang, Guijun Bi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLaser directed energy deposition (LDED) was employed to fabricate Ti6Al4V titanium alloy and TiN/Ti6Al4V composites through nitrogen-assisted in-situ synthesis. Two fabrication routes were investigated, including direct deposition under a mixed nitrogen-argon atmosphere (TS sample) and nitrogen-assisted remelting under a pure nitrogen atmosphere (TR sample). Dry sliding tribological tests were conducted at room temperature and 300 °C. The results showed that nitrogen-assisted processing promoted the in-situ formation of TiN within the Ti6Al4V matrix. Compared with the Ti6Al4V sample, the TR sample exhibited a higher friction coefficient but significantly improved wear resistance, with the wear volume reduced by approximately 44.8% at room temperature and 78.8% at 300 °C. In contrast, the TS sample containing relatively sparse and smaller TiN particles showed limited improvement in wear resistance. At elevated temperature, all samples exhibited reduced wear loss, which was likely associated with oxidation-assisted surface protection during sliding. Microstructural observations indicated that the remelting-assisted process promoted the formation of larger TiN particles with a denser distribution in the TR sample, significantly influencing the wear evolution behavior. However, the tribological response was likely affected by the combined effects of TiN reinforcement and processing-induced microstructural evolution associated with different thermal histories. This study demonstrates that nitrogen-assisted remelting is an effective approach for improving the tribological performance of Ti6Al4V alloys and provides insight into the influence of TiN distribution on wear behavior in LDED-fabricated titanium matrix composites.Keywords: Laser directed energy deposition, In-situ synthesis, Titanium matrix composites, Friction and wear behaviorSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Ti6Al4V titanium alloy, a typical α + β type alloy, has been widely utilized in aerospace, chemical processing, medical devices, and high-end equipment manufacturing due to its low density, high specific strength, excellent corrosion resistance, favorable high-temperature properties, and outstanding biocompatibility1–3. However, its relatively poor wear resistance under frictional environments remains a critical limitation for its long-term service applications4. In particular, under severe working conditions such as dry friction, high loads, or boundary lubrication, the surface of Ti6Al4V is prone to severe adhesive wear and plastic deformation, which leads to increased friction coefficient, accelerated material loss, and ultimately reduced service life or even premature failure5,6. Therefore, enhancing the friction and wear performance of Ti6Al4V alloy while retaining its excellent mechanical properties has become a key research focus in the field of titanium alloys.

To improve the tribological performance of Ti6Al4V titanium alloy, various strengthening strategies have been proposed, with surface modification and composite reinforcement being the most widely adopted. Common surface treatments include gas nitriding7,8, shot peening9,10, and anodic oxidation11,12, which typically enhance the hardness and wear resistance of the alloy by forming hard phases or dense oxide layers on the surface. Xiao et al.13 developed a combined approach involving shot peening (SP), low-temperature plasma nitriding (PN), and arc deposition (AD) to improve the wear resistance of Ti6Al4V alloy. SP pretreatment enabled rapid low-temperature nitriding of the Ti6Al4V substrate, forming an interlayer suitable for subsequent coatings. Compared to single AD or PN-AD treatments, the SP-PN-AD coating exhibited superior load-bearing capacity and adhesion strength, which ensured excellent wear resistance, especially under high-load wear conditions.

However, these surface treatments often suffer from several limitations, including shallow modified layers, poor treatment uniformity, microcrack formation or delamination in the heat-affected zone, and insufficient bonding strength with the substrate. Such drawbacks are particularly pronounced when applied to components with complex geometries, significantly limiting their practical engineering applications.

In recent years, researchers have attempted to fabricate reinforced titanium matrix composites via techniques such as spark plasma sintering14–16, powder metallurgy17–19, and friction stir processing20,21. By introducing ceramic phases with high hardness, high melting point, and excellent chemical stability, the wear resistance of the titanium matrix can be significantly improved. For instance, Lal and Dey22 fabricated Ti6Al4V alloys with varying TiC contents using the spark plasma sintering technique and investigated the effects of TiC nanoparticles on the microstructure and tribological performance of Ti6Al4V composites. Their results demonstrated that increasing TiC content notably reduced both wear volume and friction coefficient, primarily due to the enhanced hardness and oxidation resistance imparted by TiC. Muniyappa et al.23 employed laser surface texturing to modify the titanium alloy surface prior to coating deposition. A biocompatible TiN-based protective coating with a thickness of 4 μm was deposited using physical vapor deposition. The experimental results revealed that the newly developed material exhibited superior frictional behavior and wear resistance under various sliding speeds and loading conditions compared to conventional materials.

Nevertheless, traditional composite fabrication methods still face several challenges: (1) complex processes and long preparation cycles, which are unsuitable for rapid manufacturing of intricate components; (2) reinforcement phases are typically externally added pre-synthesized particles, leading to agglomeration, inhomogeneous distribution, and weak interfacial bonding with the matrix; and (3) the resulting composites often have poor densification and dimensional accuracy, making them difficult to apply directly in functional components.

The development of additive manufacturing technologies has provided new solutions for enhancing the tribological performance of titanium alloys24,25. Among them, laser directed energy deposition (LDED) is a promising additive manufacturing technique that integrates material feeding, melting, and shaping into a single process. It offers notable advantages such as in-situ phase synthesis, precise control of composition and microstructure, and the ability to fabricate geometrically complex components26,27. By introducing reactive materials during the deposition of titanium alloy powders, reinforcing phases such as TiN can be synthesized in-situ during the build process28–30. This approach enables tight interfacial bonding between the reinforcement and the matrix, uniform phase distribution, and tunable mechanical properties. Compared with conventional sintering or surface treatment methods, LDED-produced in-situ TiN/Ti6Al4V composites exhibit higher fabrication efficiency, improved structural continuity, and better suitability for complex-shaped parts, making them a recent focus in the field of titanium matrix composites.

Sahasrabudhe et al.31 reported the in-situ fabrication of Ti6Al4V-CaP-nitride composite coatings using laser engineered net shaping (LENS™), where calcium phosphate addition and nitrogen-assisted processing significantly improved the bio-tribological performance of the coatings in aqueous environments. However, their study mainly focused on biomedical composite coatings containing CaP phases, while the effects of different in-situ synthesis strategies on the tribological behavior and wear mechanisms of TiN/Ti6Al4V composites under dry sliding conditions remain insufficiently understood.

In this context, the present study proposes a novel approach to in-situ synthesize TiN reinforcing phases within a Ti6Al4V matrix via the LDED process. TiN/Ti6Al4V composites were successfully fabricated, and their friction and wear behavior under dry sliding conditions was systematically investigated. By comparing the friction coefficient, wear rate, and microscopic wear mechanisms between the in-situ composite and the Ti6Al4V matrix alloy, the mechanisms and pathways by which in-situ formed TiN particles enhance wear resistance were elucidated. This study not only provides a new strategy for optimizing the tribological performance of Ti6Al4V titanium alloys but also offers theoretical and experimental support for the broader application of laser additive manufacturing in the development of high-performance titanium-based functional structural materials.

Materials and methods

Experimental methods

In this study, three types of samples were prepared for comparative investigation. The first sample was fabricated by depositing Ti6Al4V alloy under a pure argon atmosphere and served as the reference group; it is designated as Ti6Al4V (Fig. 1a). The second sample, denoted as TS (Fig. 1b), was produced by first depositing Ti6Al4V under a pure argon atmosphere, followed by additional deposition in a mixed gas environment containing 10% nitrogen and 90% argon. The third sample, labeled as TR (Fig. 1c), was prepared by initially depositing Ti6Al4V in a pure argon atmosphere, followed by laser surface remelting under a pure nitrogen atmosphere. The TR sample was remelted once using a laser power of 1200 W.

Schematic diagram of the deposition process for different samples.

During direct deposition, stable fabrication could not be achieved under a pure nitrogen atmosphere due to process instability. Therefore, a mixed nitrogen-argon atmosphere was employed for the TS sample to ensure stable deposition while enabling in-situ TiN formation. In contrast, pure nitrogen could be successfully used during the remelting treatment of the TR sample because no additional powder feeding or layer buildup was involved.

The experimental powder employed in this study was spherical Ti6Al4V powder procured from Avimetal AM Tech Co., Ltd., with a purity of 99.96%. The chemical composition is detailed in Table 1. The particle size of the powder falls within the range of 53–150 μm (with specific values: D10 = 48.1 μm, D50 = 78.1 μm, and D90 = 125 μm). The deposition parameters and the detailed microstructural characteristics of the nitrogen-remelted TiN layer, including its thickness, were consistent with those reported in our previous work32,33.

Friction experiments

After deposition, all samples were subjected to wire electrical discharge machining, followed by grinding and polishing. Tribological performance was then evaluated through a series of tests, including both room-temperature and high-temperature friction and wear experiments, to comprehensively assess the material behavior under different service conditions.

Room-temperature friction and wear tests were conducted using a multifunctional tribometer (RTEC MFT-5000, USA). During the test, a φ6 mm silicon nitride (Si3N4) ball was used to slide against the polished surface of the samples in a ball-on-flat reciprocating configuration. The applied normal load was 20 N, with a reciprocating speed of 10 mm/s, a stroke length of 5 mm, and a total sliding duration of 30 min.

High-temperature friction and wear tests were carried out using a micro-oscillation tribometer (OPTIMOL SRV-IV, Germany), with the testing temperature set at 300 °C. The same type of φ6 mm Si3N4 ball was used as the counterpart, and the normal load was also maintained at 20 N. The reciprocating speed was set to 8 mm/s with a stroke length of 4 mm, and the friction duration remained 30 min.

The wear duration of 30 min was selected based on commonly adopted conditions in previous tribological studies on titanium alloys and titanium matrix composites. The primary objective of this work was to comparatively evaluate the friction and wear behaviors of different samples under identical testing conditions. Although certain samples may not have reached a fully stabilized long-term steady state within the selected duration, the adopted testing time was sufficient to reveal the differences in friction evolution, wear morphology, and dominant wear mechanisms among the investigated materials.

After each test, a high-precision electronic balance was used to measure the sample mass before and after wear in order to determine the wear loss. By systematically analyzing both room-temperature and high-temperature test results, key tribological parameters such as the coefficient of friction (COF) and wear rate under different operating conditions were obtained.

Characterization

After the friction and wear tests, a KEYENCE VR-6000 3D optical profilometer was employed to measure the three-dimensional wear profile, two-dimensional cross-sectional profile, and wear cross-sectional area of the worn surfaces. The surface morphology was obtained by scanning the worn track of the samples, with a scanning resolution of 0.1 μm.

To further investigate the wear mechanisms, the worn surfaces were examined and subjected to elemental analysis using a Quanta 200 environmental scanning electron microscope (SEM). In addition, selected samples were cross-sectioned, ground, and polished, followed by chemical etching using Kroll’s reagent for approximately 30 s. The etched samples were then rinsed with absolute ethanol and dried with compressed air. The microstructure of the samples was observed using a Leica ICC50W optical metallographic microscope.

Results

Microstructure

The optical microstructures of the TS and TR samples are shown in Fig. 2. As observed, both samples exhibit distinct interfacial boundaries. In the remelted region of the TR sample, noticeable white granular features are present. In contrast, the nitrogen–argon mixed gas-deposited region of the TS sample contains fine needle-like structures.

Optical microstructures of samples: (a) TR sample, (b) TS sample.

According to previous studies32,33, the white granular features are identified as TiN particles, while the needle-like structures correspond to martensitic α’ phase. Due to the significantly higher nitrogen concentration in the remelting process of the TR sample compared to the deposition process of the TS sample, the amount of TiN formed in the TR sample is considerably greater than that in the TS sample.

Friction behavior at room temperature

Figure 3a presents the variation in friction coefficient over time for the three samples under room-temperature conditions. It can be observed that all samples exhibit a similar trend. At the initial stage of the wear process, the friction coefficient increases rapidly—this period is commonly referred to as the “running-in” stage. During this phase, the contact area between the counterface ball and the sample surface is relatively small, resulting in stress concentration and severe wear, which leads to a sharp rise in the friction coefficient.

(a) Variation of friction coefficient with time at room temperature for different samples; (b) Microhardness of various samples under room-temperature conditions.

As the wear time increases, the contact area gradually enlarges, surface contact stress decreases, and the friction coefficient becomes more stable. For the TS and TR samples, the friction coefficient stabilizes after approximately 2 min of sliding. In contrast, the Ti6Al4V sample exhibits a slight increase in friction coefficient beyond the 2-min mark, reaching a steady state after around 10 min.

Once stabilized, the friction coefficients of all three samples fluctuate around their respective average values. The average steady-state friction coefficient was approximately 0.42 for the Ti6Al4V sample, 0.38 for the TS sample, and 0.66 for the TR sample. The difference between the Ti6Al4V and TS samples was relatively small, while the TR sample exhibited a 57.1% higher friction coefficient compared to the Ti6Al4V reference.

Figure 4 presents the three-dimensional wear track morphologies of the different samples. The maximum wear depths measured for the Ti6Al4V, TS, and TR samples were − 51.46 μm, − 48.73 μm, and − 30.43 μm, respectively. It is evident that the wear tracks of the Ti6Al4V and TS samples are deeper and relatively similar in depth, whereas the TR sample exhibits a significantly shallower and smoother wear track profile.

Three-dimensional wear scar morphologies of different samples at room temperature: (a) Ti6Al4V sample, (b) TS sample, (c) TR sample and (d) two-dimensional wear scar profiles of different samples at room temperature.

As shown in the 2D wear scar profile curves in Fig. 4d, a similar trend is observed: the depth of the wear tracks for the Ti6Al4V and TS samples are comparable, while the TR sample exhibits a significantly shallower wear track. Specifically, the maximum wear depths for the Ti6Al4V, TS, and TR samples are measured to be 50.7 μm, 52.6 μm, and 27.8 μm, respectively. Correspondingly, the calculated wear rates are 3.62 × 10−4 mm3/(N m) for Ti6Al4V, 4.38 × 10−4 mm3/(N m) for TS, and 2.00 × 10−4 mm3/(N m) for TR. The variations in both wear depth and wear rate follow the same trend, suggesting that the TR sample exhibits markedly enhanced wear resistance compared to Ti6Al4V and TS, which show relatively similar performance. These findings are further corroborated by the results in Figs. 3 and 4.

Figure 5 presents the SEM images of the wear scars for the three samples. From Fig. 5a–c, the widths of the wear tracks are measured to be 1.12 mm for Ti6Al4V, 1.07 mm for TS, and 0.87 mm for TR under identical test conditions, which is consistent with the trends observed in Figs. 4 and 5. The narrower wear track of the TR sample confirms its superior wear resistance.

SEM morphologies of wear scars for different samples at room temperature: (a), (d) Ti6Al4V sample; (b), (e) TS sample; (c), (f) TR sample.

As shown in Fig. 5d,e, the wear tracks exhibit distinct band-like scratches together with slight flake-like spallation, which are indicative of adhesive wear. In contrast, Fig. 5f reveals extensive spallation on the worn surface. Given that the hardness of both Ti6Al4V and TiN is lower than that of the counterpart ball, severe plastic deformation occurred during sliding, accompanied by strain hardening. This process promoted the formation of subsurface fatigue cracks parallel to the surface. The observed flake-like debris and surface spallation features may be associated with crack initiation and propagation during repeated sliding contact, eventually leading to delamination-like material removal. Therefore, the wear behavior of the TR sample is considered to be consistent with delamination-related wear characteristics.

Friction behavior at 300 °C temperature

Figure 6 illustrates the variation of the COF of the three samples as a function of time at an elevated temperature of 300 °C. The Ti6Al4V and TS samples exhibit similar trends: the COF increases rapidly at the onset of sliding and stabilizes after approximately 7 min. The average steady-state COF for the Ti6Al4V sample is 0.57, whereas the TS sample shows a higher value of 0.77. Additionally, the TS sample exhibits more pronounced fluctuations in the COF during the steady-state period. After 25 min of testing, the COF of the TS sample slightly increases from 0.77 to 0.82, indicating potential instability in friction behavior during prolonged operation.

Variation of friction coefficient with time at high temperature for different samples.

In contrast, the TR sample shows a more complex COF evolution. Initially, the COF rises rapidly during the running-in stage, and after approximately 1 min, the rate of increase slows. The COF reaches a peak value of 1.15 at around 4 min. Subsequently, the COF gradually decreases and stabilizes within 2 min, with an average steady-state value of 0.84. Notably, a slight decline in the COF is observed at 25 min, reaching 0.80, which is marginally lower than the average value recorded for the TS sample.

By comparing the friction behaviors of the three materials, it can be observed that although the TR sample exhibits a relatively high friction coefficient during the initial stage, its stabilized friction coefficient gradually approaches that of the TS sample and is slightly lower than the latter. This suggests that the friction characteristics at elevated temperatures are influenced not only by the intrinsic material properties but also by the duration of the running-in period and total friction time.

The variation trends of the high-temperature friction coefficients for the Ti6Al4V and TS samples are similar to those observed at room temperature. The initial increase and subsequent stabilization of the friction coefficient are consistent across these two samples. The TR sample, however, exhibits an initial rise followed by a decline in the friction coefficient, which may be attributed to its relatively higher surface roughness. During the friction test, the micro-asperities on the sample surface deform and gradually flatten during sliding, increasing the actual contact area between the counterface ball and the sample and leading to stabilization of the friction coefficient at a relatively lower level.

Overall, the TR sample demonstrates superior performance over the TS sample at elevated temperature, with enhanced microhardness directly contributing to reduced wear and improved friction behavior. These results underscore the complexity of material behavior at high temperatures, where performance depends not only on hardness but also on factors such as surface friction characteristics and microstructural changes.

Figure 7 displays the three-dimensional wear scar morphologies of the samples. The maximum wear depths for the Ti6Al4V, TS, and TR samples are − 52.70 μm, − 88.44 μm, and − 17.71 μm, respectively. The TS sample exhibits the deepest wear scar, followed by Ti6Al4V, while the TR sample shows a relatively smooth and shallow wear track.

Three-dimensional wear scar morphologies of different samples at 300 °C: (a) Ti6Al4V sample, (b) TS sample, (c) TR sample and (d) two-dimensional wear scar profiles of different samples at 300 °C.

The 2D wear scar profiles in Fig. 7d reveal a consistent trend, with the TS sample showing the greatest wear depth, followed by Ti6Al4V, and the TR sample exhibiting the shallowest depth. Specifically, the wear depths are 50.60 μm for Ti6Al4V, 85.38 μm for TS, and 17.67 μm for TR. The calculated wear rates are 1.70 × 10−4 mm3/(N m) for Ti6Al4V, 3.26 × 10−4 mm3/(N m) for TS, and 0.36 × 10−4 mm3/(N m) for TR. The trends in wear depth and wear rate are consistent, indicating that compared to the Ti6Al4V sample, the wear resistance of the TS sample decreases, whereas the TR sample shows significant improvement.

Figure 8 presents SEM images of the wear scars for the different samples. The wear track widths measured from Fig. 8a–c are 1.26 mm, 1.59 mm, and 0.72 mm for the Ti6Al4V, TS, and TR samples, respectively, consistent with the findings from Figs. 6 and 7. The narrowest wear track of the TR sample further confirms its superior wear resistance.

SEM morphologies of wear scars for different samples at 300 °C: (a), (d) Ti6Al4V sample; (b), (e) TS sample; (c), (f) TR sample.

Figure 8e shows that the wear track surface is covered with numerous scratches parallel to the sliding direction. In certain regions, similar to those in Fig. 8d, detached debris adhered to the surface, indicating a mixed mechanism of abrasive and adhesive wear. As illustrated in Fig. 8f, the high-temperature wear morphology of the TR sample exhibits flake-like spallation features, suggesting the possible involvement of fatigue-related damage during sliding.

Figure 9 illustrates the surface element distribution of the TR sample after high-temperature friction testing at 300 °C. The left side of the figure shows the worn region, while the right side depicts the unworn region. Noticeable oxygen enrichment was observed within the wear track, suggesting that oxidation-related tribological behavior may have occurred during high-temperature sliding. However, the present results do not provide direct evidence for a fully oxidation-dominated wear mechanism. Therefore, the high-temperature wear behavior of the TR sample is more reasonably interpreted as the combined effect of mechanical wear and oxidation-assisted surface interactions, potentially involving fatigue-related damage and oxide-containing wear debris.

Discussion

Relationship between friction coefficient and wear resistance

The COF is only one indicator of tribological performance and does not fully represent the overall wear behavior34,35. As shown in Figs. 3 and 6, although the TR sample exhibits a significantly higher COF compared to the Ti6Al4V sample, its wear resistance is markedly improved. This indicates a close but not strictly equivalent relationship between COF and wear resistance. TiN, as a ceramic phase with a high hardness (~ 2000 HV), effectively resists plastic deformation and micro-cutting during friction35. Thus, even with an increased COF, the material surface was less prone to wear, resulting in a reduced wear volume.

The in-situ precipitation of TiN may increase surface roughness, enlarging the real contact area and friction force, but these hard particles are difficult to detach or spall, thereby preventing severe wear. At the microscopic contact scale, the densely distributed TiN particles may assist in load sharing and suppression of plastic deformation during sliding. This microstructural arrangement can increase the friction coefficient due to greater resistance, while actually protecting the Ti6Al4V matrix from severe damage.

Effect of elevated temperature on tribological behavior

Comparison of the room-temperature and high-temperature results demonstrates that all three samples exhibit improved wear resistance at elevated temperaturess, consistent with earlier reports37. This behavior can be ascribed to the formation of a dense oxide film on the Ti6Al4V surface under high-temperature conditions. The oxide film serves as a protective barrier against direct asperity contact and simultaneously provides solid lubrication, which together markedly reduced material loss during sliding.

Influence of TiN distribution and processing strategy on wear mechanisms

Although both direct in-situ synthesis and remelting in-situ synthesis methods generate TiN in the titanium matrix, the wear resistance of the resulting composites differs substantially. The high-temperature wear resistance of the directly synthesized Ti6Al4V/TiN composite (TS sample) even decreases compared to the Ti6Al4V substrate. Figure 10 schematically illustrates the tribological behaviors of the three samples: the green spheres represent the Si3N4 counterface ball, the blue cubes represent the Ti6Al4V matrix, and the yellow irregular shapes indicate in-situ synthesized TiN particles. Figure 10a depicts the friction and wear of the Ti6Al4V sample deposited under pure argon, where no TiN particles are present; Fig. 10b shows the TS sample with sparse, small TiN particles; Fig. 10c illustrates the TR sample featuring abundant, larger TiN particles produced by remelting in a nitrogen atmosphere.

Schematic diagrams of friction and wear for different samples: (a) Ti6Al4V sample; (b) TS sample; (c) TR sample.

According to our previous study32, the thickness of the TiN-enriched remelted layer generated under similar nitrogen-remelting conditions reached approximately 1 mm. Under the present tribological testing conditions, the wear-track depth remained substantially smaller than the thickness of the modified layer. Therefore, the friction and wear behavior observed in the TR sample was still primarily governed by the TiN-enriched remelted region rather than by the underlying Ti6Al4V substrate.

In the TS sample, the low quantity and small size of TiN particles result in wide spacing between them. During the initial friction stage, the counterface ball primarily contacts the relatively soft Ti6Al4V matrix. As wear progresses, the matrix gradually detaches, exposing the hard TiN particles, which bear increasing load individually. These particles tend to loosen and detach from the matrix, becoming hard abrasive debris that causes severe grooves on the wear surface (Fig. 8e), thereby accelerating wear. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies38. Conversely, in the TR sample, the larger TiN particles with a denser distribution collectively participate in localized load sharing during the early stage of friction. Even with partial detachment of the Ti6Al4V matrix at the surface, the TiN particles can still participate in localized load sharing, thereby reducing severe material removal from the counterface and improving wear resistance.

Compared with the Ti6Al4V-CaP-nitride composite coatings reported by Sahasrabudhe et al.31., the present study focuses on TiN/Ti6Al4V composites fabricated via different in-situ synthesis strategies without CaP addition. Although both studies demonstrated that nitrogen-assisted LDED processing can effectively enhance the tribological performance of titanium alloys through in-situ TiN formation, the present work further reveals the influence of remelting-assisted processing on TiN distribution, friction evolution behavior, and wear mechanisms under dry sliding conditions.

Potential engineering implications

Considering that the TR sample demonstrates superior wear resistance, while the TS sample possesses higher tensile strength, a practical approach may be to combine their advantages. Layered composite parts could be fabricated by alternating deposition under pure argon and nitrogen-argon mixed atmospheres, followed by nitrogen remelting of the surface. This strategy can simultaneously improve the strength and substantially enhance the wear resistance of the component.

Limitations and future perspectives

The present tribological tests were conducted over a relatively limited sliding duration of 30 min, primarily for the comparative evaluation of different fabrication strategies. Although distinct differences in friction and wear behavior were observed among the samples, longer-duration wear tests are still required to further evaluate the long-term steady-state tribological behavior and service stability of the TiN/Ti6Al4V composites, particularly under elevated-temperature conditions.

It should also be noted that the TS and TR samples differ not only in nitrogen-assisted TiN formation, but also in thermal processing history due to the differences between direct deposition and remelting operations. Therefore, the observed tribological behavior may originate from the combined effects of TiN reinforcement, thermal cycling, microstructural evolution, and residual stress state. Additional control experiments under identical processing conditions are still required to further decouple these influencing factors.

Furthermore, the proposed mechanisms regarding localized load sharing and suppression of severe plastic deformation by TiN particles have not yet been directly validated in the present work. Further investigations involving subsurface microstructural characterization, quantitative spatial distribution analysis of TiN reinforcements, local mechanical property mapping, finite element modeling, and post-wear interface characterization would be valuable for clarifying the stress-transfer behavior and interfacial stability of the TiN-reinforced regions.

Conclusion

This study investigated the room-temperature and elevated-temperature tribological behaviors of Ti6Al4V titanium alloy fabricated under pure argon atmosphere, TS samples produced by direct in-situ synthesis in a nitrogen-argon mixed atmosphere, and TR samples prepared through nitrogen-assisted remelting in a pure nitrogen atmosphere. The main conclusions are summarized as follows:

Nitrogen-assisted processing promoted the in-situ formation of TiN within the Ti6Al4V matrix, while different processing strategies significantly influenced the size and distribution characteristics of the TiN particles. Compared with the TS sample, the TR sample contained relatively larger TiN particles with a denser distribution generated during the remelting process.

Nitrogen-assisted processing promoted the in-situ formation of TiN within the Ti6Al4V matrix, while different processing strategies significantly influenced the size and distribution characteristics of the TiN particles. Compared with the TS sample, the TR sample contained relatively larger TiN particles with a denser distribution generated during the remelting process.

Under room-temperature conditions, the TS sample exhibited tribological behavior similar to that of the Ti6Al4V sample, whereas the TR sample showed significantly improved wear resistance with markedly reduced wear loss and wear rate, despite exhibiting a relatively higher friction coefficient.

Under room-temperature conditions, the TS sample exhibited tribological behavior similar to that of the Ti6Al4V sample, whereas the TR sample showed significantly improved wear resistance with markedly reduced wear loss and wear rate, despite exhibiting a relatively higher friction coefficient.

At 300 °C, the wear resistance of the TS sample decreased compared with the Ti6Al4V sample, whereas the TR sample still maintained substantially enhanced wear resistance. The improved tribological performance of the TR sample was likely associated with the larger and denser distribution of TiN particles formed during the remelting process.

At 300 °C, the wear resistance of the TS sample decreased compared with the Ti6Al4V sample, whereas the TR sample still maintained substantially enhanced wear resistance. The improved tribological performance of the TR sample was likely associated with the larger and denser distribution of TiN particles formed during the remelting process.

Wear morphology observations suggested that different TiN distributions significantly influenced the wear evolution behavior and material removal characteristics of the composites. The present results demonstrate that nitrogen-assisted remelting is an effective approach for improving the tribological performance of Ti6Al4V alloys. However, the tribological behavior may originate from the combined effects of TiN reinforcement and processing-induced microstructural evolution associated with different thermal histories.
