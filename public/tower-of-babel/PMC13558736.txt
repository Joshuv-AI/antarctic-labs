A dual track predictive model for assessing crow’s feet aging across different clinical severity grades
Authors: Qitian Fu, Yuqing Han, Jiaqi Zhang, Xinyuan Li, Miao Yu, Qi Liu, Yao Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate assessment of crow’s feet is limited by subjective scales and single-dimensional objective tools, impeding standardized evaluation. We recruited 350 healthy Chinese women and acquired macroscopic and microstructural parameters using VISIA-CR, DermaTOP®, and two-photon microscopy. Following expert clinical grading, we developed a dual-track modeling strategy that combines interpretable Ridge Regression and TPOT-optimized Support Vector Regression (SVR) to translate macroscopic parameters into clinical scores. SVR model interpretability was evaluated by SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis. Crow’s feet severity increased non-linearly with age. Two-photon microscopy qualitatively supported the hypothesis that grade 4 corresponds to a phase of notable epidermal barrier disruption and grade 5 to pronounced dermal matrix disorganization. The Ridge Regression model achieved a robust overall fit (R2 = 0.519). Macroscopically, the Stacked SVR model achieved superior predictive precision in the Early Stage (Grades 0–2) by capturing subtle non-linear textural shifts, whereas the Ridge Regression model maintained robustness from the Transition Stage (Grades 3–4) onwards. SHAP analysis supported the physiological plausibility of the non-linear model. This study clarifies the micro‑physiological basis of crow’s feet aging and establishes a stage-specific dual-track predictive framework, providing an objective, standardized approach for skin aging assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-59266-4.Keywords: Crow’s feet, Ridge regression, Two-photon microscopy, Multimodal imaging, Skin aging, Skin microstructureSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Skin aging is characterized by the degradation of facial morphological structures, with crow’s feet being one of the most prominent features1,2. Currently, the assessment of crow’s feet primarily relies on two approaches: subjective clinical grading and objective instrumental evaluation. Subjective grading remains the “gold standard” in dermatology. Widely utilized subjective assessment scales include the Atlas of Asian Skin Aging3, the Investigator’s General Assessment of the Lateral Line of the Orbicularis Oculi at Rest (IGA-LCL) Severity Scale4, and the Mertz 5-point scale5. However, it inherently relies on the evaluator’s clinical experience, which can introduce high intra- and inter-observer variability6. Conversely, objective instrumental tools (such as VISIA-CR and DermaTOP®) offer non-invasive, high-resolution evaluations7,8. Yet, the multidimensional parameters they generate—such as area, volume, and depth—are often highly fragmented and lack a direct correspondence with clinical severity grades. This disconnect between quantitative metrics and subjective assessment poses a major barrier to standardized, biologically grounded evaluation of crow’s feet.

To bridge this gap, quantitative predictive models have been widely explored. Traditional linear models, such as Ridge Regression, effectively handle the high multicollinearity among skin parameters and provide highly interpretable mathematical formulas to link objective data with subjective scores9,10. However, skin aging is a highly complex biological process involving multi-cellular interactions, the emergence of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), and extracellular matrix remodeling, which frequently exhibit non-linear acceleration11,12. Purely linear models often oversimplify these physiological transition points. Recently, Automated Machine Learning (AutoML) frameworks, such as the Tree-based Pipeline Optimization Tool (TPOT), have demonstrated immense potential in complex biomedical modeling13,14. This data-driven, model-agnostic approach can automatically search and construct optimal predictive architectures within a vast algorithm space, providing an ideal tool for decoding the high-dimensional mapping rules between objective parameters and subjective scores.

Therefore, this study aims to achieve two primary objectives. First, we use two-photon microscopy to uncover the microstructural features across different subjective grades, establishing a solid biological basis for evaluation. Second, we construct a “dual-track” quantitative prediction framework using macroscopic parameters from VISIA-CR and DermaTOP®. This framework integrates an interpretable Ridge Regression model with an innovative TPOT AutoML model to balance clinical transparency with high-precision assessment. Ultimately, this synergistic approach explores the complementary value of linear and data-driven models, establishing a biologically grounded objective evaluation method for crow’s feet.

Materials and methods

Instrument

Skin images and biological parameters of the crow’s feet were acquired using a VISIA® CR system (Canfield Scientific, United States), a DermaTOP® system (Eotech, Marcoussis, France), and a portable two-photon microscope (SV780, Transcend Vivoscope, China). DermaTOP acquisition parameters were configured with an object area threshold of 0.5 mm², an object count of 100, and a histogram threshold of 20%. The bilateral crow’s feet regions were captured at a 60° angle, utilizing a positioning system to ensure stability. For the SV780 two-photon microscope, imaging utilized a ~ 100 fs pulsed laser at an excitation wavelength of 780 ± 10 nm. Depth-compensated laser power was configured with an initial 300–500 mW and 20–40 mW steps. A customized clinical micro-objective yielded a field of view of ≥ 125 × 125 µm2. Detection filters were set to 420–580 nm for the autofluorescence (AF) channel and 375–405 nm for the second harmonic generation (SHG) channel. For each subject, the probe was positioned 1–1.5 cm lateral to the outer canthus and centered over the primary wrinkle; the operator was blinded to the clinical grade. To mitigate inherent motion artifacts associated with in vivo two-photon imaging, three consecutive technical replicate scans were acquired at this single standardized anatomical location. From these three replicates, one representative image stack was selected strictly on the basis of optical clarity, consistent focus across all skin layers, and absence of macroscopic motion artifacts, and was used as the standard reference for morphological assessment. No digital enhancement or post-processing modification was applied to the raw image data. These two-photon images were only used for qualitative morphological assessment.

Subjects

A cohort of 350 healthy Chinese females (aged 18–60 years; Fitzpatrick skin types III–IV) was recruited in Beijing, China. The study was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Beijing Technology and Business University (Approval No. 2023-73) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines, with all participants providing written informed consent. Inclusion criteria required participants to be healthy and compliant. Key exclusion criteria included pregnancy, recent facial medical procedures, or concurrent clinical study participation. Participants abstained from applying facial products prior to evaluation.

Study design

Following facial cleansing, participants acclimatized for 30 min in a controlled environment (22 ± 1 °C, 50 ± 5% relative humidity). Three clinical experts independently graded crow’s feet severity using the Asian Skin Aging Atlas3. Specifically, each expert performed three independent assessments per subject, and the arithmetic mean of these nine ratings was calculated as the final continuous subjective grade. This continuous approximation was deliberately adopted to preserve the subtle clinical granularity captured by the independent expert evaluators. Bilateral oblique images were captured via VISIA-CR under S1 natural light and analyzed using Image-Pro Plus (Media Cybernetics, United States) to extract wrinkle parameters. The DermaTOP system was utilized to capture the topographical parameters of the crow’s feet. In vivo microstructural imaging was performed using the SV780 two-photon microscope. Scanning initiated from the stratum corneum (0 μm) down to the dermis, capturing 65 consecutive layers at 2 μm increments (total depth: 130 μm) to reconstruct 3D image stacks.

Linear model building and validation

Data were randomly partitioned into a training set (n = 245) and a validation set (n = 105) at a 70:30 ratio15. To strictly prevent data leakage, all subsequent preprocessing and feature selection steps were performed exclusively on the training set. Following standard data preprocessing and outlier removal, multicollinearity among selected variables was diagnosed. A Ridge Regression model was then constructed on the training set to establish a linear predictive baseline, effectively mitigating multicollinearity. A 5-fold cross-validation procedure was employed to objectively determine the optimal regularization parameter (K) for the Ridge Regression model. Continuous model predictions were subjected to a pre-specified clinical clipping step to constrain outputs within the valid 0–6 grading range. Model performance was evaluated on the independent validation set using root mean squared error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE), with both unclipped and clipped metrics reported to ensure transparency in sensitivity.

Automated machine learning model development and validation

To capture complex, non-linear relationships between objective parameters and clinical grades, we employed the Python-based TPOT framework for automated machine learning (AutoML). Features were standardized, and TPOT was configured to optimize across five algorithms suitable for our dataset characteristics: Random Forest16, Gradient Boosting17, ElasticNet18, Support Vector Regression (SVR)19, and K-Neighbors Regressor (KNN)20. Following the TPOT framework13, optimization utilized a genetic algorithm configured for 10 generations and a population size of 30. The optimal pipeline, minimizing mean squared error (MSE) via 10-fold cross-validation on the training cohort, was subsequently tested on the validation cohort. Finally, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was applied to visualize feature importance and elucidate the decision-making logic of the optimal model21.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 22.0 and GraphPad Prism 7.0. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A continuous piecewise linear regression model was used to explore the relationship between age and subjective grading. Pearson correlation analysis evaluated parameter relationships, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. Multicollinearity was diagnosed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) to guide feature selection. To formally compare the predictive performance on the validation cohort, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were applied to paired absolute errors, and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the difference in RMSE (ΔRMSE) were estimated using bootstrapping with 1,000 iterations. Additionally, to quantify the sampling uncertainty of model performance comparisons across clinical stages, a 1000-iteration bootstrap resampling procedure was performed. All bootstrap analyses in this study were conducted exclusively on the locked independent validation cohort, with all preprocessing steps, feature selection results, hyperparameter tuning, and final model fittings held constant across iterations; only subjects in the validation set were resampled with replacement for each iteration. Bland-Altman analysis was conducted to assess agreement between predicted and actual subjective grades. The mean bias and 95% limits of agreement (LoA) were calculated, proportional bias was tested by linear regression of the difference scores against the mean scores, and 95% confidence intervals for bias and LoA were likewise estimated via 1000 iterations of bootstrap resampling on the fixed validation set.

Linear model establishment

Guided by the microstructural transitions identified via two-photon microscopy, we next developed macroscopic predictive models to translate surface parameters into clinical scores. Given that the averaged subjective gradings form a continuous variable, Pearson correlation was utilized to strictly evaluate linear relationships, aligning with the foundational assumptions of standard linear regression. This analysis identified significant linear correlations (p < 0.05) for 15 parameters, excluding Textures (Table S4). Consequently, only these 15 parameters were selected to establish an initial linear baseline model. A VIF threshold exceeding 10 was applied to diagnose multicollinearity, revealing that 12 parameters exhibited collinearity issues (Table S4). To mitigate this, Ridge Regression was employed. Analysis of the ridge trace plot (Fig. 4) demonstrated that the standardized regression coefficients broadly stabilized when the penalty parameter (K) approached 0.1. However, to objectively determine the exact optimal penalty and avoid subjective visual bias, a 5-fold cross-validation procedure was subsequently performed. This algorithmic search identified K = 0.04 as the precise parameter that minimizes the cross-validation error. Utilizing this optimized penalty, a two-stage Ridge Regression procedure was executed. Initially, the full model evaluated all 15 candidate parameters to establish stabilized feature contributions. Based on these penalized estimates, 8 variables were excluded due to high informational overlap (penalized p ≥ 0.05). To prevent over-parameterization and maximize clinical interpretability, a final refined Ridge Regression model was subsequently re-estimated exclusively on the remaining 7 robust variables (F = 28.073, p < 0.05, Table S5). Non-linear prediction models establishment based on TPOT

While the Ridge Regression model established a reliable linear baseline, our microstructural evaluations highlighted pronounced non-linear transitions in skin aging, particularly the structural collapse observed at grades 4 and 5. To formally assess these non-linear relationships, we performed Spearman rank correlation analysis, which revealed that all 16 parameters exhibited highly significant monotonic correlations with subjective grades (p < 0.001, Table S4). Consequently, all 16 parameters were retained as candidate features for the TPOT framework to address the limitations of strict linear assumptions. After optimizing five candidate machine learning algorithms, their predictive performances were systematically compared (Table S6). The Stacked SVR model emerged as the most effective pipeline among those evaluated.The base layers of this model utilized a linear kernel to extract the overarching linear aging trend, while the top layer applied a radial basis function (RBF) kernel to more closely model the non-linear inflection points.

Compared to the Ridge Regression baseline, the optimized SVR model delivered superior overall precision and required no clipping intervention (unclipped and clipped metrics were identical). The SVR model achieved a lower RMSE of 0.7698, an MAE of 0.6131, and an improved R2 of 0.5987. This enhanced accuracy is visually confirmed in the SVR scatter plot (Fig. 6b), where predictions are more tightly clustered along the identity line, particularly in the early grading range, validating its robust ability to capture non-linear aging transitions.

Ultimately, these two models offer complementary strengths: the Ridge Regression model provides a transparent mathematical formula with stable performance at extreme grades, while the SVR model minimizes absolute prediction errors by accurately accommodating complex, data-driven patterns.

To further investigate predictive consistency, a stratified formal comparison was conducted across mutually exclusive stages (Table 6), supplemented by raw error metrics for each individual grade (Table S7). While no statistically significant difference in absolute errors was observed across the entire validation cohort (p = 0.1873), distinct performance patterns emerged upon stratification. In the Early Stage (Grades 0–2, n = 43), the Stacked SVR model demonstrated significantly higher precision (p < 0.001). Conversely, from the Transition Stage onwards (Grades 3–4, n = 49), the Ridge Regression model exhibited a significant advantage in minimizing prediction errors (p < 0.05). Crucially, for the extreme Advanced Stage (Grades 5–6), noting the very small sample size (N = 13) which inherently limits statistical power, the non-parametric Wilcoxon test showed no significance (p = 0.0625). Our 1,000-iteration bootstrap resampling analysis provided preliminary evidence suggesting potential superiority of the Ridge Regression model in this subgroup (Table S8), but this finding should be interpreted with caution. In this stage, Ridge Regression maintained a substantially lower RMSE, yielding a 95% Confidence Interval entirely below zero (95% CI: [-1.1909, -0.4287]).

Furthermore, an ordinal sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of this continuous modeling framework. When continuous predictions were mapped back to the original 0–6 discrete scale, both the Ridge Regression and SVR models achieved a clinically acceptable accuracy of 93.33% (error ≤ 1 grade) and substantial ordinal agreement (Quadratic Weighted Kappa > 0.66, Table S10), demonstrating that the continuous assumption did not materially alter the predictive validity.

Discussion

Crow’s feet, a hallmark of skin aging, are predominantly assessed using subjective scales with weak physiological basis, while objective grading methods are scarce, hindering standardized quantitative and reproducible evaluation. To address this gap, this study systematically mapped the microstructural aging spectrum to subjective grades using two-photon microscopy, and developed “dual-track” predictive models to convert macroscopic objective data into subjective scores.

Based on our findings, the non-linear progression of crow’s feet is consistently reflected across chronological age, microstructural degeneration, and objective parameter quantification. During the initial stages (grades 0–1), subjects are typically under 30 years old and exhibit no visible signs of aging. Qualitative two‑photon imaging demonstrates a smooth and continuous stratum corneum with intact dermal fibers, consistent with a state of physiological homeostasis. Consistently, objective parameters such as Textures show minimal variation at this stage (Fig. 3b). During the third to fourth decade of life (corresponding predominantly to clinical grade 2), a notable subclinical alteration emerges. While macroscopic wrinkles remain unformed, early cellular swelling and declining collagen density initiate at the microscopic level23. From a clinical perspective, the unique ability of the “Textures” parameter to capture these subtle surface variations identifies it as a highly sensitive biomarker for early anti-aging interventions (Table 4)12,24. These accumulating subclinical micro-lesions eventually contribute to the non‑linear age‑related acceleration observed in our piecewise regression (Fig. 1). Propelled by this accelerated velocity, the tissue undergoes comprehensive failure over the subsequent decade. Consequently, a clear qualitative shift manifests predominantly in the 40s (grades 3–4). Two-photon imaging captures this transition as an epidermal barrier disruption featuring a fragmented stratum corneum and disordered granular cells. This microscopic collapse is accompanied by significant increases in critical‑point identification indicators such as Ra, Rz (Fig. 3b, d,e, h,j). Mechanistically, this transition is likely driven by the accumulation of senescent cells and the SASP, which promotes chronic inflammation and impairs barrier function25,26. Consequently, our qualitative two-photon microscopy observations support the hypothesis that grade 4 corresponds to a phase of accelerated structural deterioration, where cumulative subclinical damage progresses to more pronounced epidermal barrier disruption and dermal matrix disorganization.27. This interpretation remains preliminary and requires quantitative validation in future studies. In advanced stages (grades 5–6), typically after age 50, the damage shifts to comprehensive dermal mechanical failure. Consistent with previous multiphoton microscopy studies characterizing the aging dermal matrix28,29, our large-cohort imaging qualitatively revealed a progressive decline in SHG signals with increasing severity grades. In two-photon images, this optical decline physically manifests as extensive collagen fragmentation and volume loss (Fig. 2e–g), which are, in turn, accurately captured by late-stage dominant macroscopic indicators such as Volume Sum and Mean Depth Average (Fig. 3n, p)30,31. This matrix degradation directly underlies the formation of deep wrinkles and loss of skin elasticity, which may be attributed to cytokine-induced enzymatic degradation and dysregulated assembly of the extracellular matrix (ECM) network32,33. Recent regenerative studies show that exosome-based therapies, polydeoxyribonucleotide, and hyaluronic acid complexes can remodel ECM, enhance collagen reorganization, and promote angiogenesis34–39, suggesting that the dermal alterations observed here may be reversible targets for future anti-aging interventions. Together, these converging lines of evidence, including our qualitative two-photon observations, provide a supportive biological context for our subsequent predictive modeling.

To address the high multicollinearity among the 16 parameters (Table S4, we developed a Ridge Regression model with a regularization penalty (K = 0.04), yielding a clinically interpretable linear formula40,41. In scenarios involving severe multicollinearity (12 variables with VIF > 10), traditional ordinary least squares standard errors become excessively inflated, rendering conventional p-values completely unreliable. We therefore utilized the Ridge-adjusted pseudo p-values derived from the asymptotic covariance matrix of the penalized estimator (as implemented in IBM SPSS), an approach well-supported by statistical literature demonstrating superior statistical power and stability for highly correlated predictors42,43. Based on these stabilized estimates, we excluded variables with high informational overlap and retained 7 core parameters; the final model was re-fitted with the ridge regularization penalty retained to mitigate residual multicollinearity. This approach preserves the stabilizing effect of L2 penalization while producing a more parsimonious formula for clinical use, and avoids the unstable variable selection behavior of LASSO among highly correlated predictors44. As emphasized by Chicco et al.45, the coefficient of determination (R2) is a robust metric for assessing the proportion of explained variance. While our Ridge Regression model demonstrated strong explanatory power (R2 = 0.519), its absolute prediction error (RMSE = 0.8428) underscores the inherent limitations of linear modeling when applied to complex biological systems46. Consequently, we introduced a TPOT-optimized SVR model to capture the pronounced non-linear dermal transitions observed at grades 4–5. This non-linear approach significantly reduced the RMSE to 0.7698, offering superior precision in aligning with the skin’s microstructural collapse. To ensure the interpretability of this “black-box” model, SHAP analysis confirmed that its directional impacts (e.g., SA and Rz exerted positive driving forces) strongly align with the Ridge Regression coefficients (Table S5), suggesting physiological plausibility21. More importantly, SHAP demonstrated the advantage of machine learning in uncovering non-linear relationships47. Specifically, “Textures” was excluded from the linear model due to a non‑significant Pearson correlation, yet emerged as the SVR’s fifth most important feature. LOESS spline analysis (Figure S2) revealed a phase‑dependent non‑linear trajectory. Specifically, Textures showed high sensitivity with a steeper slope at early stages (Grades 1–3) but plateaued at advanced stages (Grades 4–6). This aligns with evidence that Textures is a highly sensitive indicator of early subclinical aging12,24, and its successful inclusion in the SVR model represents a advantage for evaluating complex aging trajectories.

To objectively assess the agreement and systematic bias beyond overall error metrics, Bland-Altman analysis was conducted48. Figure 7 and Table S9 visually confirmed that the SVR model reduced the absolute mean bias from 0.38 to -0.19, while both models exhibited significant proportional bias (p < 0.001) due to the bounded clinical scale. This bias reduction, rather than a change in LoA width, accounts for the SVR’s improved overall agreement. However, stratified analysis revealed a clear stage-dependent divergence in algorithmic superiority (Table 6). In the Early Stage (Grades 0–2), Stacked SVR achieved significantly higher accuracy, as non-linear architectures are better equipped to capture subtle textural shifts before macroscopic furrows dominate12,24,47. Conversely, this advantage reversed from the transition stage onwards (Grades 3–4), where the parsimonious Ridge Regression model demonstrated superior performance. For the extreme Advanced Stage (Grades 5–6), we explicitly acknowledge the very small sample size (N = 13) which limits the strength of stage-specific conclusions. While our bootstrap validation (Table S8) and consistently lower absolute errors with strictly negative confidence intervals (Table 6) suggest a potential advantage for the Ridge Regression model, this finding requires confirmation in larger cohorts enriched for severe aging phenotypes. This reversal reflects the vulnerability of complex algorithms to conservative estimation when trained on unequal distributions of extreme phenotypes41. Linear models, by contrast, maintain superior predictive stability and reliable extrapolation when available data for extreme clinical grades are sparse40,46. Ultimately, this highlights a trade-off between capturing non-linear biological changes and ensuring clinical predictive robustness.

Based on these complementary characteristics, we propose a targeted “dual-track” application framework, which stems from the inherent trade-off between transparency and precision in clinical dermatology. Since no single algorithm can satisfy both demands, we provide the following stage‑dependent guidelines. For Early Stage (Grades 0–2) screening, the Stacked SVR track should be prioritized due to its high sensitivity to subtle non‑linear textural shifts, making it ideal for digital diagnostic devices and early anti‑aging monitoring. For transitional grades (3–4), the Ridge Regression model demonstrated statistically significant superior performance (p = 0.0377, Table 6) and is therefore recommended for this stage. For Advanced Stage evaluation (Grades 5–6), although the Wilcoxon signed-rank test did not reach statistical significance due to the small sample size (N = 13), our 1000-iteration bootstrap analysis consistently supported the Ridge track as the more robust option. Its stability against sparse extreme samples and transparent macroscopic volume markers make it reliable for assessing severe structural collapse, while its interpretable formula further aids clinical consultation. This dual strategy provides an essential tool for standardized clinical trials and advanced dermatological research, where precisely tracking the subtle, non-linear efficacy of anti-aging interventions is paramount49.

Despite offering a novel perspective on crow’s feet assessment, this study has several limitations. First, regarding sample generalizability, the cohort was restricted to healthy Chinese women aged 18–60 years with Fitzpatrick skin types III–IV, limiting broader generalizability to other ethnic groups, skin types, or male populations. Second, in terms of methodological assumptions for outcome definition and post-processing, averaging ordinal expert scores to construct a continuous clinical endpoint inherently assumes equal perceptual intervals between clinical grades; although computationally practical and supported by our sensitivity analysis, this approach may oversimplify the discrete, non-linear nature of human clinical assessments. Similarly, imposing hard clinical boundaries on model outputs via post-processing clipping (e.g., constraining predictions to the 0–6 range) artificially truncates the natural variance of the predictive distribution at the extremes, a methodological nuance that must be acknowledged when interpreting overall model performance. Third, regarding model validation and algorithmic constraints, the bootstrap resampling analyses in this study were performed within a single stratified train-test split and did not repeat the full modeling workflow across multiple random data partitions. These analyses quantify only the sampling uncertainty of performance estimates for the finalized models, rather than the overall variability of the entire model development pipeline. The generalizability of the observed stage-dependent performance differences across alternative data splits requires further confirmation in larger cohorts with adequate representation of severe crow’s feet grades. Additionally, the relatively small sample size of the Advanced Stage (Grades 5–6) subgroup limits the robustness of stage-specific conclusions. Finally, to prevent severe overfitting on our relatively small training cohort (n = 245) and to maintain clinical interpretability, the TPOT AutoML search space was deliberately constrained to five established algorithm families with restricted population sizes (30) and generations (10). While this operational configuration ensured computational feasibility and model robustness, it is possible that an unconstrained search with larger populations (e.g., 50 or 100) might yield alternative, albeit more complex, pipeline architectures. Fourth, with respect to the two-photon microscopy component, while two‑photon microscopy qualitatively revealed notable microscopic degeneration, quantitative structural analysis was not pursued as the primary focus of this study was the macroscopic predictive model. Specifically, quantitative measures of collagen fiber organization, normalized SHG signal intensity, epidermal layer thickness, and elastin fiber density were not performed. The two‑photon data thus serve solely as qualitative physiological context, and all associated biological interpretations should be treated with appropriate caution. Owing to motion artifact concerns inherent to in vivo imaging, only three technical replicate scans were acquired at the standardized anatomical site for each subject, which may not fully capture the regional spatial variation of crow’s feet. Multiphoton devices are also not widely available in routine clinical settings. Dedicated quantitative analyses of two‑photon features are warranted in future studies. Consequently, we used microstructural observations from two-photon microscopy as an objective physiological benchmark to validate the biological relevance of macroscopic parameters and the tipping points captured by the model. This ensures that the final predictive model is clinically practical while being firmly supported by robust physiological mechanisms. Fifth, in terms of study design, the cross-sectional design also precludes the evaluation of dynamic aging trajectories and causal relationships between structural degeneration and wrinkle progression. Looking forward, future research should encompass multi-ethnic, multi-gender, and longitudinal cohorts with adequate representation of all clinical grades to validate the robustness of the observed stage-dependent performance pattern. Integrating multi-omics data (e.g., skin microbiome or metabolome) and real-time AI algorithms will further promote the clinical translation of objective grading and personalized anti-aging interventions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that crow’s feet severity increases non‑linearly with age. Using two‑photon microscopy, we qualitatively map microstructural changes across clinical grades, supporting the hypothesis that grade 4 corresponds to a phase of notable epidermal barrier disruption and grade 5 to substantial dermal matrix disorganization. Moreover, we develop a dual‑track predictive framework combining interpretable Ridge Regression and non‑linear SVR models, which effectively translates macroscopic skin parameters into clinical scores. The Stacked SVR model outperforms in the Early Stage (Grades 0–2), whereas the Ridge Regression model remains robust in the Transition Stage (Grades 3–4) and Advanced Stage (Grades 5–6). SHAP analysis supports the physiological plausibility of the non‑linear model. Ultimately, our findings clarify the microstructural basis of crow’s feet aging and provide an objective, standardized approach for skin aging assessment and anti‑aging intervention monitoring.
