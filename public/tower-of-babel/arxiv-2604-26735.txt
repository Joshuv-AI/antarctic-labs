Quasar-Convex Optimization: Fundamental Properties and High-Order Proximal-Point Methods
Masoud Ahookhosh, José M. M. de Brito, Alireza Kabgani, Felipe Lara, Jinyun Yuan

Abstract
We study the optimization of (strongly) quasar-convex functions, a class that arises naturally in many machine learning and data science applications due to its favorable properties. The fundamental properties of this class are first developed, including its stability under standard calculus operations, growth conditions, and the absence of spurious critical points, which together imply a benign global geometry with no saddle points. Motivated by these properties, a class of proximal-point algorithms (HiPPA) with high-order regularization of order p > 1 p>1 is introduced. Conditions are identified under which the iterates converge globally to minimizers, and a unified convergence analysis is provided with explicit rates and iteration complexity bounds under appropriate regularity assumptions. The results reveal a sharp transition in behavior with respect to the order p p : for p ∈ ( 1 , 2 ) p\in(1,2) , the method achieves local linear convergence with complexity 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( ε − 1 ) ) \mathcal{O}(\log(\varepsilon^{-1})) when initialized sufficiently close to a minimizer; for p = 2 p=2 , it attains global linear convergence with the same complexity; and for p > 2 p>2 , it exhibits superlinear convergence with complexity 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ ( ε − 1 ) ) \mathcal{O}(\log\log(\varepsilon^{-1})) , where ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 denotes the target accuracy. The theory is complemented with preliminary numerical experiments on selected machine learning problems, which illustrate the effectiveness of the proposed methods and are consistent with the theoretical findings. Keywords : Nonsmooth and nonconvex optimization; Quasar-convex functions; High-order proximal-point method; (Super)linear convergence rate; Complexity; Generalized linear model; Robust multi-task regression Mathematics Subject Classification: 49J52; 65K05; 90C26.

1.1
Contribution

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

(i)

(
Fundamental properties of (strongly) quasar-convex functions
) We characterize the class of strongly quasar-convex functions and establish its associated calculus, encompassing addition, scalar multiplication, and composition. We also examine coercivity properties within this class. In particular, we study the landscape of this class of functions and especially show that every local minimizer is global, thus excluding the presence of spurious local minimizers and saddle points. Furthermore, we derive first-order characterizations and corresponding Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) inequality, growth conditions, and error bounds for optimization problems in this framework.

(ii)

(
Convergence and complexity analysis of HiPPA
) We begin by presenting a convergence analysis of the sequence generated by HiPPA toward global minimizers of (
1
). Specifically, if
\gamma=0
(i.e., quasar-convex case), then, for
p\in(1,2)
, HiPPA attains the convergence rate
\mathcal{O}(k^{1-p})
and the complexity
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1/(p-1)})
, for
p=2
, it gets the convergence rate
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})
and the complexity
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})
, and for
p>2
, it achieves the convergence rate
\mathcal{O}(k^{-p/2})
and the complexity
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2/p})
. More specifically, if
\gamma>0
(i.e., strongly quasar-convex case), then, for
p\in(1,2)
, HiPPA exhibits linear convergence with complexity
\mathcal{O}(\log(\varepsilon^{-1}))
if it starts within a ball around the minimizer, for
p=2
, it attains linear convergence with the complexity
\mathcal{O}(\log(\varepsilon^{-1}))
, and for
p>2
, it achieves superlinear convergence with the complexity
\mathcal{O}(\log(\log(\varepsilon^{-1})))
. To the best of our knowledge, this constitutes the first comprehensive analysis of convergence rates and complexity of HiPPA in the context of strongly quasar-convex optimization.

(iii)

(
Applications
) We apply the HiPPA algorithm to two practical problems: (a) generalized linear models (GLMs) and (b) robust multi-task regression (RMTR). We show, in particular, that RMTR is nonsmooth and strongly quasar-convex, yet not strongly star-convex. To the best of our knowledge, HiPPA is the first method equipped with a rigorous convergence theory for RMTR.

Definition 3

(
Strong quasar-convexity
)
.

Let
\kappa\in(0,1]
,
\gamma\geq 0
, and let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper function with convex domain such that
\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h\neq\emptyset
. The function
h
is said to be
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
if, for all
x\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
and all
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle h(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x)\leq\kappa\lambda h(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa\lambda)h(x)-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa}\right)\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\lVert x-\overline{x}\rVert^{2},

(9)

If
\gamma=0
, we simply say that
h
is
\kappa
-quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
.

Strong convexity controls the function along every segment joining two points in its domain through a quadratic correction term. In contrast,
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strong quasar-convexity imposes such control only along segments joining an arbitrary point to a minimizer
\overline{x}
, while also weakening the right-hand side through the parameter
\kappa
. In this sense, strong quasar-convexity can be viewed as a one-sided relaxation of strong convexity, in which curvature is imposed only along directions pointing toward a minimizer.

In Definition
3
, we omit the phrase “with respect to
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
” whenever the reference point
\overline{x}
is clear from the context.

Remark 5

(
Non-comparability of (strong) quasiconvexity and (strong) quasar-convexity

There is, in general, no inclusion relationship between (strong) quasiconvexity and (strong) quasar-convexity.
Indeed, the function in

Example 6

(
Strong quasar-convexity beyond star-convexity
)
.

Define
h:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
by

h(x):=\begin{cases}\|x\|^{1/2}+\left(2+\sin(3\boldsymbol{\arg}(x))\right)\|x\|^{2},&x\neq 0,\\
0,&x=0.\end{cases}

where
\boldsymbol{\arg}(x)\in(-\pi,\pi]
denotes the principal argument of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
. Then
h
is proper, nonsmooth, nonconvex, and

(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}=0
for
\kappa=\frac{1}{2}
and
\gamma=1
.
Indeed, for
x\neq 0
, write
x=ru
with
r=\|x\|>0
and
\|u\|=1
, and set
q(u):=2+\sin(3\boldsymbol{\arg}(u))\in[1,3]
. Then,

h(x)=r^{1/2}+q(u)r^{2}.

Let
\lambda\in[0,1]
and put
s:=1-\lambda
. We have

h(sx)=s^{1/2}r^{1/2}+s^{2}q(u)r^{2}.

Since
s=1-\lambda
and
q(u)\geq 1
, we have

\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)q(u)-s^{2}q(u)=\lambda\left(\frac{3}{2}-\lambda\right)q(u)\geq\lambda\left(\frac{3}{2}-\lambda\right)\geq\frac{\lambda}{4}\left(1-\frac{2\lambda}{3}\right),

which yields

s^{2}q(u)\leq\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)q(u)-\frac{\lambda}{4}\left(1-\frac{2\lambda}{3}\right).

This, together with
s^{1/2}\leq 1-\frac{\lambda}{2}
, implies

\displaystyle h((1-\lambda)x)

\displaystyle=s^{1/2}r^{1/2}+s^{2}q(u)r^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)r^{1/2}+\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)q(u)r^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{4}\left(1-\frac{2\lambda}{3}\right)r^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)h(x)-\frac{\lambda}{4}\left(1-\frac{2\lambda}{3}\right)\|x\|^{2}.

Equivalently, since
h(0)=0
, we get

h(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x)\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}h(\overline{x})+\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)h(x)-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa}\right)\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2},

with
\kappa=\frac{1}{2}
and
\gamma=1
. Hence
h
is
\bigl(\frac{1}{2},1\bigr)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}=0
.

Moreover,
h
is not star-convex with respect to
\overline{x}=0
. Indeed, if it were, then

h(tx)\leq t\,h(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
t\in[0,1]
.
Taking
t=\frac{1}{2}
, we get

h\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2}h(x)=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\|x\|^{1/2}-\frac{1}{4}q(u)\|x\|^{2}.

Since

, the right-hand side is positive for all sufficiently small

, which is a contradiction. Thus

is not star-convex, and hence it is not convex.

\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
if and only if

h(\overline{x})\geq h(y)+\frac{1}{\kappa}\langle\nabla h(y),\overline{x}-y\rangle+\frac{\gamma}{2}\lVert y-\overline{x}\rVert^{2},\qquad\forall~y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(11)

By Fact
7
, quasi-strongly convex functions
[
51
]
are closely connected to strongly quasar-convex functions. Additional properties for differentiable (strongly) quasar-convex functions can be found in
[
21
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
65
]
.

The next result provides a sufficient condition for strongly quasar-convex functions.

Fact 8

(
Sufficient condition).

[
25
, Proposition 8]

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex function with
f(0)=0=h^{*}
and
g:S^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable function with
g(x)\geq 1
for all
x\in S^{d-1}
. Define

h(x)=f(\|x\|)g\left(\frac{x}{\|x\|}\right),\quad h(0)=0.

Then,
h
is
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar convex.

Fact 9

(
Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) inequality).

[
25
, Lemma 2]

A PL function with modulus
\mu>0
satisfies an error bound with modulus
\mu>0
. A function which is
L
-smooth and satisfies error bound with modulus
\theta>0
satisfies PL with modulus
\mu:=\frac{\theta}{L}
.

Let us recall the restricted secant inequality property.

Definition 10

(
Restricted secant inequality).

It is said that a function
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
satisfies a restricted secant inequality with modulus
\nu>0
if

\langle\nabla h(x),x-\overline{x}\rangle\geq\nu\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2},\quad\forall~x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Fact 11

(
Restricted secant inequality).

[
25
, Lemma 3]

Suppose
h
satisfies the uniform acute angle condition (UAAC)

\forall~x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad 1\geq\frac{\langle\nabla h(x),x-\overline{x}\rangle}{\|\nabla h(x)\|\|x-\overline{x}\|}\geq a>0.

(12)

Then, if
h
satisfies the error bound with modulus
\theta>0
, then
h
satisfies the restricted secant inequality with modulus
\theta a>0
.

Fact 12

(
Restricted secant inequality).

[
25
, Lemma 4]

Let
h
be a
L
-smooth function. Then the following assertions hold:

(a)

If
h
is
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex, then
h
satisfies the restricted secant inequality with modulus
\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2-\kappa}>0
.

(b)

If
h
satisfies the restricted secant inequality with modulus
\nu>0
and
0<\kappa<\frac{2\nu}{L}
, then
h
is
\left(\kappa,\frac{\nu}{\kappa}-\frac{L}{2}\right)
-strongly quasar-convex.

The subsequent result provides a relationship between strongly quasar-convex functions and functions that satisfy the PL property.

Fact 13

(
PL inequality).

[
25
, Theorem 7]

Let
h
be a
L
-smooth and
\mu
-PL function with a unique minimizer. Then there exists
\kappa,\gamma^{\prime}>0
such that
h
is
(\kappa,\gamma^{\prime})
-strongly quasar-convex if and only if for some
a\in(0,1]
,
h
satisfies relation (

).
In particular, if (UAAC) holds, then as long as
\kappa<\frac{2\mu a}{L}
,
h
is
(\kappa,\frac{\mu a}{\kappa}-\frac{L}{2})
-strongly quasar-convex.

Fact 14

(
PL inequality).

[
25
, Proposition 10]

Let
h
be
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex function. Then
h
satisfies the PL property with modulus
\gamma\kappa^{2}>0
.

In the case of a twice continuously differentiable function, we have the following results.

Fact 15

(
Twice continuously differentiability).

(see
[

Let
h
be a twice continuously differentiable and

-strongly quasar-convex function. Let
x\neq\overline{x}

\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\langle\nabla^{2}h(\overline{x}+s(x-\overline{x}))(\overline{x}-x),\overline{x}-x\rangle}{\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2}}ds\geq\kappa\frac{\gamma}{2}.

Finally, observe that in the following sufficient condition, no assumption on (strong) quasar-convexity is needed.

Fact 16

3
Nonsmooth quasar-convex functions: Fundamental properties

In this section, we investigate the characterization and calculus of the class of quasar-convex functions without differentiability assumptions. For the differentiable case, we refer to
[
21
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
59
,
65
]
and references therein.

Let us begin with an example showing that, in the nonsmooth setting,
\kappa
-quasar-convexity may hold either with respect to every minimizer or only with respect to some of them, depending on the geometry of the minimizer set.

Example 17
.

Let
C
be a nonempty closed proper subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with nonempty kernel

\ker(C):=\{\overline{x}\in C:[\overline{x},z]\subset C\text{ for every }z\in C\},

let
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and define

h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by
h(x)=\boldsymbol{{\rm dist}}(x,C)^{\alpha}.

Since
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the function
h
, in general, is nonsmooth and nonconvex. Moreover,

\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h=C.

If
\overline{x}\in\ker(C)
, then for every
0<\kappa\leq\alpha
,
h
is
\kappa
-quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
. Indeed, if
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
, choose
p\in C
such that

\|x-p\|=\boldsymbol{{\rm dist}}(x,C).

Since
\overline{x}\in\ker(C)
, we have
c_{\lambda}:=\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)p\in C
and

\boldsymbol{{\rm dist}}\bigl(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x,C\bigr)\leq\|\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x-c_{\lambda}\|=(1-\lambda)\boldsymbol{{\rm dist}}(x,C).

Therefore,

h\bigl(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x\bigr)\leq(1-\lambda)^{\alpha}h(x).

Since
t\mapsto t^{\alpha}
is concave on
[0,1]
, we have

(1-\lambda)^{\alpha}\leq 1-\alpha\lambda\leq 1-\kappa\lambda.

Hence

h\bigl(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x\bigr)\leq(1-\kappa\lambda)h(x)=\kappa\lambda h(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa\lambda)h(x),

and thus
h
is
\kappa
-quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
.

On the other hand, if
\overline{x}\in C\setminus\ker(C)
, then
h
fails to
be
\kappa
-quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
for every

\kappa\in(0,1]
. Indeed, there exist
z\in C
and
\lambda_{0}\in(0,1)
such
that

\lambda_{0}\overline{x}+(1-\lambda_{0})z\notin C.

Since
h(\overline{x})=h(z)=0
but

h\bigl(\lambda_{0}\overline{x}+(1-\lambda_{0})z\bigr)>0,

the quasar-convexity inequality cannot hold. Thus,
h
is

\kappa
-quasar-convex exactly with respect to the minimizers in
\ker(C)
.

In particular, if
C
is convex, then
\ker(C)=C
, and
h
is

\kappa
-quasar-convex with respect to every minimizer. Figures
2

illustrate these two situations for
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
, by considering the sets

C_{1}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 1\}\quad\text{and}\quad C_{2}=\bigl([-2,2]\times[-1,1]\bigr)\cup\bigl([-1,1]\times[-2,2]\bigr).

Here,
C_{1}
is the convex unit disk, while
C_{2}
is a nonconvex cross-shaped set
with

\ker(C_{2})=[-1,1]^{2}
.

Figure 2
:
A nonsmooth, nonconvex function that is
\kappa
-quasar-convex with respect to some minimizers; see Example
17
.

3.1
Basic properties and calculus rules

We begin with a result showing that strong quasar-convexity is preserved under positive scaling, translation, and a reduction of the quasar parameter
\kappa
, with a corresponding adjustment of the parameter
\gamma
.

Proposition 18

(
Scaling, translation, and parameter transformation
)
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper function and let
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
. Assume that
h
is
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
, where
\gamma\geq 0
. Then the following assertions hold:

(a)

For every
\alpha>0
, the function
g(x):=\alpha h(x)
is
(\kappa,\alpha\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
.

(b)

Define
g(z):=h(\overline{x}+z)
. Then
g
is
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{z}=0\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g
.

(c)

For every
\theta\in\,(0,1]
, the function
h
is
(\theta\kappa,\frac{\gamma}{\theta})
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
.

Proof.

(a)
: It is straightforward.

(b)

We have
\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(g)=\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)-\overline{x}
. Hence,
\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(g)
is convex and
0\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(g)
.
Set
\overline{z}=0
. Since
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
, for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it holds that

g(\overline{z})=h(\overline{x})\leq h(\overline{x}+z)=g(z)
.
Thus
\overline{z}=0\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g
. Now, let
z\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(g)
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
, i.e.,

g(\lambda\overline{z}+(1-\lambda)z)=g((1-\lambda)z)=h(\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)z).

(13)

Applying the
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strong quasar-convexity of
h
at the point
y:=\overline{x}+z
ensures

\displaystyle h(\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)z)

\displaystyle=h\bigl(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)(\overline{x}+z)\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa\lambda h(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa\lambda)h(\overline{x}+z)-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa}\right)\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\|(\overline{x}+z)-\overline{x}\|^{2}.

This, together with (
13
), implies that
g
is
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{z}
.

(c)
: Let
x\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
. In virtue of the
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strong quasar-convexity of
h
with respect to
\overline{x}
, we define:

\displaystyle R_{\kappa,\gamma}(x,\lambda):=\kappa\lambda h(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa\lambda)h(x)-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa}\right)\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2},

\displaystyle R_{\kappa,\gamma}^{\theta}(x,\lambda):=\theta\kappa\lambda h(\overline{x})+(1-\theta\kappa\lambda)h(x)-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\theta\kappa}\right)\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2}.

Thus, it is enough to prove

R_{\kappa,\gamma}(x,\lambda)\leq R_{\kappa,\gamma}^{\theta}(x,\lambda),\qquad\forall~x\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h).

(14)

A direct computation gives

R_{\kappa,\gamma}^{\theta}(x,\lambda)-R_{\kappa,\gamma}(x,\lambda)=\lambda\kappa(1-\theta)\big(h(x)-h(\overline{x})\big)-\frac{\lambda^{2}\kappa^{2}\gamma(1-\theta)}{2(2-\kappa)(2-\theta\kappa)}\,\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2},

i.e.,

R_{\kappa,\gamma}^{\theta}(x,\lambda)-R_{\kappa,\gamma}(x,\lambda)=\lambda\kappa(1-\theta)\left(h(x)-h(\overline{x})-\frac{\lambda\kappa\gamma}{2(2-\kappa)(2-\theta\kappa)}\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2}\right).

(15)

Since
\lambda\in[0,1]
and
2-\theta\kappa\geq 1
, we get

\frac{\lambda}{2-\theta\kappa}\leq 1
, i.e.,

\frac{\lambda\kappa\gamma}{2(2-\kappa)(2-\theta\kappa)}\,\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2}\leq\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2(2-\kappa)}\,\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2}.

(16)

Using (
16
) and relation (
10
), the right-hand side in (
15
) is nonnegative, and (
14
) holds. Consequently, we have
h(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x)\leq R_{\kappa,\gamma}^{\theta}(x,\lambda)
for all
x\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
, i.e.,
h
is
(\theta\kappa,\gamma/\theta)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
.
∎

We next establish that the class of strongly quasar-convex functions is closed under finite positive weighted sums, provided that the functions share a common minimizer.

Proposition 19

(
Closure under finite positive weighted sums
)
.

Let
h_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be proper functions for
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, and assume that

\bigcap^{m}_{i=1}\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h_{i})\neq\emptyset
and
\overline{x}\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h_{i}
. Let
\alpha_{i}>0
for all
i
, and define

h(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}h_{i}(x).

(17)

Assume that each
h_{i}
is
(\kappa_{i},\gamma_{i})
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
, where
\gamma_{i}\geq 0
. Then
h
is
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
, where
\kappa:=\boldsymbol{\min}_{1\leq i\leq m}\kappa_{i}
and
\gamma:=\frac{1}{\kappa}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\kappa_{i}\gamma_{i}
.

Proof.

Since each
h_{i}
is
(\kappa_{i},\gamma_{i})
-strongly quasar-convex, the set
\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h_{i})
is convex for every
i
. Hence,

\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)=\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h_{i})
is convex. Moreover, because
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h_{i}
for every
i
and
\alpha_{i}>0
, we have
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
.
For each
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
,
since
\kappa\leq\kappa_{i}
, define
\theta_{i}:=\frac{\kappa}{\kappa_{i}}\in(0,1]
.
By Proposition
18

(c)
,
the
(\kappa_{i},\gamma_{i})
-strong quasar-convexity of
h_{i}
implies that
h_{i}
is also

\left(\theta_{i}\kappa_{i},\frac{\gamma_{i}}{\theta_{i}}\right)=\left(\kappa,\frac{\kappa_{i}}{\kappa}\gamma_{i}\right)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
. Hence,

h_{i}(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x)\leq\kappa\lambda h_{i}(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa\lambda)h_{i}(x)-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa}\right)\frac{\kappa_{i}\gamma_{i}}{2}\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2},\qquad\forall x\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h),\lambda\in[0,1],

for
i=1,\dots,m
. Multiplying by
\alpha_{i}>0
and summing over
i=1,\dots,m
, for every
x\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
and every
\lambda\in[0,1]
, we obtain

h(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x)\leq\kappa\lambda h(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa\lambda)h(x)-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa}\right)\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\kappa_{i}\gamma_{i}\right)\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2}.

Since
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\kappa_{i}\gamma_{i}=\kappa\gamma
, it follows that

h(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x)\leq\kappa\lambda h(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa\lambda)h(x)-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa}\right)\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2}.

Therefore,
h
is
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
.
∎

The following proposition shows that
\kappa
-quasar-convex functions admit no spurious local minima and that local maxima, if they exist, must occur at global minimizers.

Proposition 20

(
Local extrema of quasar-convex functions
)
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper function and let
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
. Assume that
h
is
\kappa
-quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
. Then, the following assertions hold:

(a)

If
x
is a local minimizer of
h
, then
x\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
.

(b)

Every local maximizer of
h
belongs to
\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
. In particular,
h
has no strict local maximizers.

Proof.

(a)
: Suppose, by contradiction, that
x
is a local minimizer of
h

and
x\notin\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
. Then
h(x)>h(\overline{x})
. For
\lambda\in(0,1]
, set
x_{\lambda}:=\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x
. By
\kappa
-quasar-convexity, it is evident that

h(x_{\lambda})\leq\kappa\lambda h(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa\lambda)h(x)=h(x)-\kappa\lambda(h(x)-h(\overline{x}))<h(x).

Since
x_{\lambda}\to x
as
\lambda\downarrow 0
, this contradicts the local minimality of
x
. Therefore,

x\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
.

(b)
: We prove the contrapositive. Let
x\notin\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
. Then
h(x)>h(\overline{x})
. For
\varepsilon>0
, define
y_{\varepsilon}:=\overline{x}+(1+\varepsilon)(x-\overline{x})
and
\lambda_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\in(0,1)
. Then
x=\lambda_{\varepsilon}\overline{x}+(1-\lambda_{\varepsilon})y_{\varepsilon}
and
y_{\varepsilon}\to x
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
. Since
h
is
\kappa
-quasar-convex at
\overline{x}
, we get

h(x)\leq\kappa\lambda_{\varepsilon}h(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa\lambda_{\varepsilon})h(y_{\varepsilon})
, and hence

h(y_{\varepsilon})\geq\frac{h(x)-\kappa\lambda_{\varepsilon}h(\overline{x})}{1-\kappa\lambda_{\varepsilon}}=h(x)+\frac{\kappa\lambda_{\varepsilon}}{1-\kappa\lambda_{\varepsilon}}\big(h(x)-h(\overline{x})\big)>h(x).

Thus, every neighborhood of
x
contains a point
y_{\varepsilon}
such that

h(y_{\varepsilon})>h(x)
, so
x
is not a local maximizer.
Therefore, every local maximizer of
h
belongs to
\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
.
Finally, if
x
were a strict local maximizer, then all nearby points would satisfy

h(y)<h(x)
, which contradicts the fact that
x\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}h
, i.e.,
h
accepts no strict local maximizers.
∎

Proposition
20
shows that
\kappa
-quasar-convexity with respect to a minimizer
\overline{x}
imposes a strong global structure on the function. In particular, the function admits no spurious
local minima: every local minimizer must be global. Moreover, for every point
x\notin\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
, there exist points arbitrarily close to
x
on the line through
\overline{x}
and
x
with strictly smaller and strictly larger function values. Consequently,
h
has no strict local maximizers.

Next, we study the preservation of strong quasar-convexity under linear composition.

Proposition 21

(
Preservation under linear composition
)
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
be
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{y}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}h
, where
\gamma\geq 0
, and let
A:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a linear operator. Define
g:=h\circ A
. Then the following assertions hold:

(a)

If
\overline{y}\in\mathcal{R}(A)
, then there exists
\overline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
A\overline{x}=\overline{y}
, and for every
x\in\overline{x}+\mathcal{R}(A^{\top})
and every
\lambda\in[0,1]
, we have

g(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x)\leq\kappa\lambda g(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa\lambda)g(x)-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa}\right)\frac{\kappa\gamma\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\min}}^{2}(A)}{2}\lVert x-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}.

(18)

(b)

If, in addition,
A
has full column rank and
\overline{y}\in\mathcal{R}(A)
, then
g
is
(\kappa,\gamma\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\min}}^{2}(A))
-strongly quasar-convex on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with respect to
\overline{x}
.

Proof.

(a)
: Since
\overline{y}\in\mathcal{R}(A)
, choose
\overline{x}
such that
A\overline{x}=\overline{y}
.
Let
x\in\overline{x}+\mathcal{R}(A^{\top})
and

\lambda\in[0,1]
. Since
h
is
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex
with respect to
\overline{y}
, we have

\displaystyle g(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa\lambda h(\overline{y})+(1-\kappa\lambda)h(Ax)-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa}\right)\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\lVert Ax-\overline{y}\rVert^{2}

(19)

\displaystyle=\kappa\lambda g(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa\lambda)g(x)-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa}\right)\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\lVert A(x-\overline{x})\rVert^{2}.

Since
x-\overline{x}\in\mathcal{R}(A^{\top})
, we have
\lVert A(x-\overline{x})\rVert^{2}\geq\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\min}}^{2}(A)\lVert x-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}
, i.e., (
18
) follows.

(b)
:
Assume that
A
has full column rank. Then

\mathcal{N}(A)=\{0\}
, and therefore
\mathcal{R}(A^{\top})=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, leading to (
18
) for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Since
\overline{y}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}h
and
A\overline{x}=\overline{y}
, it follows that

g(\overline{x})=h(\overline{y})\leq h(Ax)=g(x)\qquad\forall~x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

i.e.,
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g
. Therefore
g
is

(\kappa,\gamma\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\min}}^{2}(A))
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to

\overline{x}
.

∎

Remark 22

(
Necessity of full column rank
)
.

Even when
A
does not have full column rank, the proof of Proposition
21

(a)
shows that, whenever
\overline{y}\in\mathcal{R}(A)
and
A\overline{x}=\overline{y}
, the composition
g=h\circ A
is
\kappa
-quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
; that is, the corresponding inequality holds without the quadratic term.
On the other hand, if
A
does not have full column rank, then
\mathcal{N}(A)\neq\{0\}
. Hence there exists
d\neq 0
such that
Ad=0
,
and therefore

g(x+td)=h(A(x+td))=h(Ax)=g(x),\quad\forall~x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad\forall~t\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus,
g
is constant along a nontrivial affine line and cannot be

(\kappa,\tilde{\gamma})
-strongly quasar-convex on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with respect to
\overline{x}
for any
\tilde{\gamma}>0
.

The following example shows that the assumption
\overline{y}\in\mathcal{R}(A)
in Proposition
21
is essential.

Example 23

(
Necessity of the range condition in linear composition
)
.

The condition
\overline{y}\in\mathcal{R}(A)
in Proposition
21

is essential. For example, define
h:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
by

h(y):=\begin{cases}\|y-\overline{y}\|^{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\sin^{2}\big(3~\boldsymbol{\arg}(y-\overline{y})\big)\right),&y\neq\overline{y},\\
0,&y=\overline{y}.\end{cases}

where
\boldsymbol{\arg}(z)\in(-\pi,\pi]
denotes the principal argument of
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
. The function
h
is nonconvex, but it is
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{y}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\,h
; see
[
25
, Proposition 8]
.

Consider the linear operator
A:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
defined by
Ax:=(x,0)
.
Then
\mathcal{R}(A)=\{(t,0):t\in\mathbb{R}\}
, and hence, for
\overline{y}:=(0,1)
, we have
\overline{y}\notin\mathcal{R}(A)
.
Define
g:=h\circ A
. Then for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
Ax-\overline{y}=(x,-1)
. Let
\theta:=\boldsymbol{\arg}(Ax-\overline{y})
. Then

\sin(\theta)=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}},

and hence

\sin(3\theta)=3\sin(\theta)-4\sin^{3}(\theta)=\frac{1-3x^{2}}{(1+x^{2})^{3/2}}.

Consequently,

g(x)=h(Ax)=1+x^{2}+\frac{(3x^{2}-1)^{2}}{2(1+x^{2})^{2}}.

Hence,
g(0)=\frac{3}{2}
and
g(\frac{1}{{\sqrt{3}}})=\frac{4}{3}<g(0)
, i.e.,
0\notin\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}}\,g
. Moreover,

g(x)-g(0)=\frac{x^{2}(x^{4}+6x^{2}-3)}{(1+x^{2})^{2}},

and thus
g(x)<g(0)
for all
0<|x|<\sqrt{-3+2\sqrt{3}}
. Then
x=0
is a strict local maximizer of
g
.

By Proposition
20

(b)
,
g
cannot be

\kappa
-quasar-convex with respect to any global minimizer. Thus,
when
\overline{y}\notin\mathcal{R}(A)
, the composition
h\circ A
may fail to
be even
\kappa
-quasar-convex. This shows that the assumption

\overline{y}\in\mathcal{R}(A)
in Proposition
21
cannot be removed.

The following auxiliary lemma will be used in the next proposition. Since its proof is elementary, we omit it.

Lemma 24

(
Auxiliary constant
)
.

Let
\kappa_{1}\in\,(0,1)
and
\kappa_{2}\in\,(0,1]
. Then, it follows that

C_{\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}}:=\mathop{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\inf}}}\limits_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\frac{1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa_{1}}}{1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}}=\frac{(1-\kappa_{1})(2-\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})}{(2-\kappa_{1})(1-\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})}>0.

(20)

As a consequence, we obtain a closure criterion for compositions with a nondecreasing quasar-convex function.

Proposition 25

(
Composition with a quasar-convex function
)
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper function,
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
,
\kappa_{1}\in\,(0,1)
, and
\gamma\geq 0
. Assume that
h
is
(\kappa_{1},\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
. Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an interval such that
h(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subseteq I
, and let
\varphi:I\to\mathbb{R}
be
\kappa_{2}
-quasar-convex function with respect to
h(\overline{x})
, where
\kappa_{2}\in\,(0,1]
. Suppose in addition that there exists
m\geq 0
such that

\varphi(t)-\varphi(s)\geq m(t-s),\qquad\forall~t\geq s,~t,s\in I.

(21)

Then, the function
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
given by
g:=\varphi\circ h

is
(\kappa,\gamma_{g})
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
, where
\kappa:=\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}
,
\gamma_{g}:=\frac{C_{\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}}\,m\,\gamma}{\kappa_{2}}
and
C_{\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}}>0
is the constant given in Lemma
24
.

Proof.

Since
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
and
\varphi
is nondecreasing,
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,g
. Fix
x\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Since
h
is
(\kappa_{1},\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
, we have

h(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x)\leq\kappa_{1}\lambda h(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa_{1}\lambda)h(x)-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa_{1}}\right)\frac{\kappa_{1}\gamma}{2}\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2}.

Set
t:=\kappa_{1}\lambda h(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa_{1}\lambda)h(x)
and
s:=h(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x)
. Then,
t\geq s
. Applying (
21
) implies
\varphi(s)\leq\varphi(t)-m(t-s)
, i.e.,

g(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x)\leq\varphi\big(\kappa_{1}\lambda h(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa_{1}\lambda)h(x)\big)-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa_{1}}\right)\frac{m\kappa_{1}\gamma}{2}\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2}.

(22)

Invoking the
\kappa_{2}
-quasar-convexity of
\varphi
with respect to
h(\overline{x})
ensures

\varphi\big(\kappa_{1}\lambda h(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa_{1}\lambda)h(x)\big)\leq\kappa_{2}\kappa_{1}\lambda g(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa_{2}\kappa_{1}\lambda)g(x).

(23)

Together with (
22
), (
23
) implies

g(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x)\leq\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}\lambda g(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}\lambda)g(x)-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa_{1}}\right)\frac{m\kappa_{1}\gamma}{2}\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2}.

(24)

By the definition of
C_{\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}}
, and applying Lemma
24
in (
24
), it can be concluded that

g(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x)\leq\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}\lambda g(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}\lambda)g(x)-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}\right)\frac{C_{\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}}\,m\kappa_{1}\gamma}{2}\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2}.

Since
\kappa=\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}
, this shows that
g
is
(\kappa,\gamma_{g})
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
.
∎

Remark 26

3.2
First-order characterizations and further properties

In this subsection, we derive first-order characterizations of strongly quasar-convex functions and establish several structural properties that will be used in the subsequent analysis. In the nonsmooth setting, strong quasar-convexity admits a characterization in terms of the Clarke subdifferential, extending the classical gradient-based formulation available in the differentiable case. This viewpoint clarifies the underlying geometry and provides a convenient tool for studying optimality conditions, error bounds, and convergence properties of high-order proximal-point methods.

We begin with the following auxiliary lemma, which will be instrumental in deriving the first-order characterization of strong quasar-convexity.

Lemma 30

(
Chain rule along a segment
)
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper function with convex domain, and assume that
h
is locally Lipschitz on
\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
. Let
x,y\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
, and for
\lambda\in[0,1]
define

x_{\lambda}:=(1-\lambda)x+\lambda y
, and
g(\lambda):=h(x_{\lambda})
.
Then
g
is absolutely continuous on
[0,1]
and, for almost everywhere
\lambda\in(0,1)
, there exists
\xi_{\lambda}\in\partial^{C}h(x_{\lambda})
such that

g^{\prime}(\lambda)=\langle\xi_{\lambda},y-x\rangle.

Proof.

Since
[x,y]
is compact and
h
is locally Lipschitz around each point of this segment, it follows that
h
is Lipschitz on
[x,y]
. Hence,
g
is Lipschitz on
[0,1]
, and therefore absolutely continuous and differentiable almost everywhere on
(0,1)

[
62
, Theorem 7.20]
. Since
x_{\lambda}
is affine with derivative
x_{\lambda}^{\prime}=y-x
, Clarke’s chain rule
[
10
, Theorem 2.3.10]
yields

\partial^{C}g(\lambda)\subseteq\{\langle\eta,y-x\rangle:\eta\in\partial^{C}h(x_{\lambda})\}.

At every point
\lambda
where
g
is differentiable, we have
\partial^{C}g(\lambda)=\{g^{\prime}(\lambda)\}
. Therefore, for almost every
\lambda\in(0,1)
, there exists
\xi_{\lambda}\in\partial^{C}h(x_{\lambda})
such that

g^{\prime}(\lambda)=\langle\xi_{\lambda},y-x\rangle
.
∎

When
h
is locally Lipschitz, strong quasar-convexity admits the following characterization in terms of the Clarke subdifferential. This result extends
[
26
, Lemma 10]
from the differentiable setting to locally Lipschitz functions.

Theorem 31

(
Clarke first-order characterization).

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper function with convex domain, and let
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
. Assume that
h
is locally Lipschitz on
\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
. Then
h
is
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
, where
\gamma\geq 0
, if and only if

h(\overline{x})\geq h(x)+\frac{1}{\kappa}\langle v,\overline{x}-x\rangle+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2},\qquad\forall~x\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h),~\forall~v\in\partial^{C}h(x).

(25)

Proof.

See Appendix
A
.
∎

As an immediate consequence of Theorem
31
(see relation (
95
) in Appendix
A
), we obtain the following quadratic growth property; see also
[
26
, Corollary 1]
.

Corollary 32

(
Quadratic growth from the Clarke condition
)
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper function with convex domain, let
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
,
\kappa\in(0,1]
and
\gamma\geq 0
. Assume that
h
is locally Lipschitz on
\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
. If relation (
25
) holds, then

h(x)\geq h(\overline{x})+\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2(2-\kappa)}\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2},\quad\forall~x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(26)

That is,
h
satisfies a quadratic growth condition with modulus
\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2(2-\kappa)}\geq 0
.

The following result shows that strongly quasar-convex functions do not admit spurious Clarke critical points. In particular, every Clarke critical point is a global minimizer.

Proposition 33

(
Clarke critical points are global minimizers
)
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper function with convex domain, and let
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
. Assume that
h
is
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
, where
\gamma\geq 0
, and that
h
is locally Lipschitz on
\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
. If
0\in\partial^{C}h(x)
, then
x\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
.

Proof.

Invoking Theorem
31
, condition (
25
) holds. Taking
v=0\in\partial^{C}h(x)
in (
25
), we obtain

h(\overline{x})\geq h(x)+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2}.

In particular,
h(\overline{x})\geq h(x)
. Since

\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
, it is clear that

h(\overline{x})\leq h(x)
, i.e.,
h(x)=h(\overline{x})
, and therefore
x\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
.
∎

For the next result, recall that a one-dimensional function is called unimodal if it decreases monotonically to its minimizer and then increases monotonically thereafter.
The following result extends the necessary condition in
[
26
, Observation 1]
from the differentiable to the nonsmooth setting.

Proposition 34

(
Unimodality on an interval
)
.

Let
f:[a,b]\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper and locally Lipschitz function, and let
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{[a,b]}f
. If
f
is
\kappa
-quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
, with
\kappa\in(0,1]
, then
f
is unimodal and every Clarke critical point is a global minimizer.

Proof.

Let
a\leq x_{1}<x_{2}\leq\overline{x}
and define

\lambda:=\frac{x_{2}-x_{1}}{\overline{x}-x_{1}}\in[0,1]
.
Then
x_{2}=\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x_{1}
. Using the
\kappa
-quasar-convexity of
f
and
f(\overline{x})\leq f(x_{1})
, it follow that

f(x_{2})\leq\kappa\lambda f(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa\lambda)f(x_{1})\leq f(x_{1}),

i.e.,
f
is nonincreasing on
[a,\overline{x}]
.

Similarly, let
\overline{x}\leq x_{1}<x_{2}\leq b
and define
\lambda=\frac{x_{2}-x_{1}}{x_{2}-\overline{x}}\in[0,1]
.
Then
x_{1}=\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x_{2}
. Together with the
\kappa
-quasar-convexity of
f
,
f(\overline{x})\leq f(x_{2})
ensures

f(x_{1})\leq\kappa\lambda f(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa\lambda)f(x_{2})\leq f(x_{2}),

which shows that
f
is nondecreasing on
[\overline{x},b]
. Therefore,
f
is unimodal.
The statement regarding the Clarke critical points follows from Proposition
33
applied with
\gamma=0
.
∎

The converse of Proposition
34
fails in general, even for
C^{k}
functions with
k\geq 1
, as shown in the following example. This also shows that the converse statement suggested by
[
26
, Observation 1]
does not hold in general.

Example 35

(
A smooth unimodal function that is not quasar-convex
)
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and define the function
f:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
by

f(x):=\int_{0}^{x}\phi(t)\,dt,

where

\phi(t):=\begin{cases}t^{2k-1}\sin^{2}(1/t)+t^{2k+1},&t\neq 0,\\
0,&t=0.\end{cases}

Clearly,
\phi\in C^{k-1}([-1,1])
, and therefore
f\in C^{k}([-1,1])
. Moreover,
f^{\prime}(0)=\phi(0)=0
, and for
x\neq 0
,

f^{\prime}(x)=x^{2k-1}\sin^{2}(1/x)+x^{2k+1}.

Therefore,
f^{\prime}(x)<0
on
(-1,0)
and
f^{\prime}(x)>0
on
(0,1)
,
i.e.,
f
is unimodal. In particular,
x=0
is its unique critical point and global minimizer.

We claim that
f
is not
\kappa
-quasar-convex at
x=0
for any
\kappa\in(0,1]
. Suppose, by contradiction, that
f
is
\kappa
-quasar-convex with respect to
x=0
for some
\kappa\in(0,1]
. Since
f
is differentiable, applying (
11
) for
\gamma=0
implies

f(0)\geq f(x)+\frac{1}{\kappa}f^{\prime}(x)(0-x),\qquad~\forall~x\in[-1,1].

Since
f(0)=0
and
f(x)>0
for every
x\in(0,1]
, we obtain

0<\kappa\leq\frac{xf^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)},\qquad~\forall~x\in(0,1].

(27)

Setting
x_{n}:=1/(n\pi)
and
\sin(1/x_{n})=0
, we come to
x_{n}f^{\prime}(x_{n})=x_{n}^{2k+2}
.
On the other hand,

\displaystyle f(x_{n})\geq\int_{x_{n}/2}^{x_{n}}t^{2k-1}\sin^{2}(1/t)\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{n\pi}^{2n\pi}\frac{\sin^{2}(u)}{u^{2k+1}}\,du\geq\frac{1}{(2n\pi)^{2k+1}}\int_{n\pi}^{2n\pi}\sin^{2}(u)\,du=\frac{x_{n}^{2k}}{2^{2k+2}}.

Substituting this into (
27
), it can be concluded that

\frac{x_{n}f^{\prime}(x_{n})}{f(x_{n})}\leq 2^{2k+2}x_{n}^{2}\to 0,

which contradicts
\kappa>0
.
Therefore,
f
is not
\kappa
-quasar-convex with respect to
x=0
for any
\kappa\in(0,1]
.

We recall the (nonsmooth) Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) property (see, e.g.,
[
8
]
), which plays a central role in first-order convergence analysis. A proper function
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
with
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
is said to satisfy the PL property with modulus
\mu>0
if

\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{{\rm dist}}^{2}(0,\partial^{C}h(x))\;\geq\mu(h(x)-h(\overline{x})),\qquad\forall~x\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h).

(28)

In the next two results, we assume that the curvature parameter
\gamma
is strictly positive.

Proposition 36

(
PL inequality).

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper function with convex domain, and let
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
. Assume that
h
is
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
, where
\kappa\in(0,1]
and
\gamma>0
, and that
h
is locally Lipschitz on
\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
. Then
h
satisfies the PL property with modulus
\mu:=\gamma\kappa^{2}>0
.

Proof.

Fix
x\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
and let
v\in\partial^{C}h(x)
. It follows from Theorem
31
that

h(x)-h(\overline{x})\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\langle v,x-\overline{x}\rangle-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2}.

Applying Young’s inequality,

\frac{1}{\kappa}\langle v,x-\overline{x}\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma\kappa^{2}}\|v\|^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2}
, leads to

h(x)-h(\overline{x})\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma\kappa^{2}}\|v\|^{2}.

Since this inequality holds for every
v\in\partial^{C}h(x)
, taking infimum over
v\in\partial^{C}h(x)
yields

h(x)-h(\overline{x})\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma\kappa^{2}}\boldsymbol{{\rm dist}}^{2}(0,\partial^{C}h(x)),

giving our desired result.
∎

Next, we show that strongly quasar-convex functions satisfy both value-based and subgradient-based error bounds. In particular, the distance to the solution set can be controlled either by function values or by the magnitude of the Clarke subdifferential.

Proposition 37

(
Quadratic growth and error bound).

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper function and let
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
. Assume that
h
is
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}
, where
\kappa\in(0,1]
and
\gamma>0
, and that
h
is locally Lipschitz on
\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
. Then, for every
x\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
, it holds that

\|x-\overline{x}\|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2(2-\kappa)}{\kappa\gamma}}\,\sqrt{h(x)-h(\overline{x})},

(29)

and

\|x-\overline{x}\|\leq\frac{2}{\kappa\gamma}\,\boldsymbol{{\rm dist}}\big(0,\partial^{C}h(x)\big).

(30)

Proof.

It follows from the quadratic growth estimate (
26
) that

h(x)-h(\overline{x})\geq\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2(2-\kappa)}\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2},\qquad\forall~x\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h),

which is equivalent to (
29
).
Now, fix
x\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
and let
v\in\partial^{C}h(x)
. Invoking Theorem
31
ensures

h(\overline{x})\geq h(x)+\frac{1}{\kappa}\langle v,\overline{x}-x\rangle+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2}.

Since
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}\,h
, we have
h(\overline{x})\leq h(x)
, hence using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{2}\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2}\leq-\frac{1}{\kappa}\langle v,\overline{x}-x\rangle\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\|v\|\,\|x-\overline{x}\|.

If
x\neq\overline{x}
, dividing by
\|x-\overline{x}\|
yields

\|x-\overline{x}\|\leq\frac{2}{\kappa\gamma}\|v\|.

The inequality is trivial if
x=\overline{x}
. Taking the infimum for
v\in\partial^{C}h(x)
leads to (
30
).
∎

3.3
Landscape of (strongly) quasar-convex functions

We next refine the geometric picture of quasar-convex functions by focusing on their stationary structure and the shape of their minimizer sets. This viewpoint is useful for two reasons. First, it does not require local Lipschitz continuity and therefore applies beyond the Clarke-subdifferential framework (see Remark
40
below). Second, it further clarifies the benign landscape of quasar-convex functions: Proposition
20
already shows that every local minimizer is global and that strict local maximizers do not exist, while the result below excludes saddle points defined through Dini-stationarity.

Another important distinction concerns the geometry of the minimizer set itself. If
h
is
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with
\gamma>0
, then
\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
is a singleton by Remark
4

(b)
.
Hence, this class does not admit nonisolated global minimizers. In contrast, in the quasar-convex case
(\gamma=0)
, the set of global minimizers may be nonisolated; if it is not singleton, then it is star-convex by Remark
4

(a)
. Thus, even in the absence of strong quasar-convexity, quasar-convex functions still
enjoy a highly structured and benign landscape.

We first recall the lower Dini directional derivative. For a proper function

h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, a point
x\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
, and a direction

d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define

h^{D-}(x;d):=\mathop{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\liminf}}}\limits_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{h(x+td)-h(x)}{t}.

Definition 38

(
Stationary point in the Dini sense
)
.

A point
x\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
is called a stationary point of
h
if

h^{D-}(x;d)\geq 0\qquad\forall d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

The set of all such points is denoted by
\boldsymbol{\rm Dcrit}(h)
.

The next result shows that quasar-convexity rules out such saddle points.

Theorem 39

(
No Dini saddle points
)
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be proper, and let
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
.

(a)

If
h
is
\kappa
-quasar-convex
with respect to
\overline{x}
, where
\kappa\in(0,1]
. Then every Dini-stationary point of
h
is a global minimizer, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\rm Dcrit}(h)\subseteq\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
. Consequently,
h
has no saddle points in the Dini sense.

(b)

If
h
is
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with
\gamma>0
, then

\boldsymbol{\rm Dcrit}(h)=\{\overline{x}\}
, that is, the unique stationary point is the unique global minimizer.

Proof.

(a)

Let
x\in\boldsymbol{\rm Dcrit}(h)
and assume, by contradiction, that
x\notin\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
. Then

h(x)>h(\overline{x})
. Set
d:=\overline{x}-x
. For every
t\in(0,1]
, by
\kappa
-quasar-convexity of
h
with respect to
\overline{x}
,
we have

h(x+td)=h((1-t)x+t\overline{x})\leq\kappa t\,h(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa t)\,h(x)=h(x)-\kappa t\bigl(h(x)-h(\overline{x})\bigr).

Therefore,

\frac{h(x+td)-h(x)}{t}\leq-\kappa\bigl(h(x)-h(\overline{x})\bigr)<0.

Passing to the lower limit as
t\downarrow 0
, we obtain
h^{D-}(x;d)<0
, which contradicts the stationarity of
x
. Hence

x\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
, and thus
\boldsymbol{\rm Dcrit}(h)\subseteq\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
.
Since every stationary point is a global minimizer, it cannot be a saddle point.

(b)
If
h
is
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with
\gamma>0
, then
\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
is a singleton by Remark
4

(b)
. Hence Assertion
(a)
yields

\boldsymbol{\rm Dcrit}(h)\subseteq\{\overline{x}\}
, while
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\rm Dcrit}(h)
is immediate from the definition of the minimizer. Therefore,
\boldsymbol{\rm Dcrit}(h)=\{\overline{x}\}
.
∎

Remark 40

(
Dini and Clarke stationary points
)
.

If
h
is locally Lipschitz on
\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
, then by Theorem
31
and Proposition
33
, every Clarke critical point of a
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex function is a global minimizer. In particular, there are no spurious stationary points in the Clarke sense. In the strongly quasar-convex case, Remark
4

(b)
implies that this Clarke critical point is unique. Therefore, for locally Lipschitz quasar-convex functions, both the Dini-stationary and the Clarke-critical viewpoints lead to the same conclusion: the only stationary
objects are global minimizers, and in the strongly quasar-convex case the unique stationary point is the unique global minimizer.

In the following example, we exhibit a nonconvex quasar-convex function whose set of global minimizers is nonisolated. More precisely, the minimizer set is star-convex.

Example 41

(
Nonisolated star-convex minimizers
)
.

Let
R(\theta):=1+0.35\cos(4\theta)
and, for
x\neq 0
, written in polar form
x=r(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)
, define

\rho(x):=\frac{r}{R(\theta)}
with
\rho(0):=0
, and
h(x):=\boldsymbol{\max}\{0,\rho(x)-1\}
. Then, for every

t\in[0,1]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have
\rho(tx)=t\rho(x)
, and therefore

h(tx)=\boldsymbol{\max}\{0,t\rho(x)-1\}\leq t\boldsymbol{\max}\{0,\rho(x)-1\}=t\,h(x).

Hence
h
is
1
-quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}=0
. Moreover,

\mathop{\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}}\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}h(x)=\left\{r(\cos\theta,\sin\theta):0\leq r\leq R(\theta),\ \theta\in[0,2\pi)\right\},

which is star-convex with respect to the origin and not convex. In particular, this example
shows that a quasar-convex function may be genuinely nonconvex and may possess a
nonisolated minimizer set that is star-convex but not convex. A visualization of this example is provided in Figure
3
, which
highlights both the nonconvexity of the landscape and the star-convex, nonconvex geometry
of the minimizer set.

:

41
.

4
High-order proximal-point methods

In this section, we study the high-order proximal-point algorithm (HiPPA) (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
2
,
1
,
33
,
34
,
31
,
54
,
55
]
), in which the regularization term is of the form
\|\cdot\|^{p}
with
p>1
. As noted in
[
1
]
, HiPPA exhibits superlinear convergence for uniformly quasiconvex functions, a class that includes uniformly convex and strongly quasiconvex functions. We show here that HiPPA with
p>2
also attains superlinear convergence for strongly quasar-convex functions.

Let us begin by introducing the high-order proximal operator, which serves as the fundamental building block of the algorithmic framework developed in this section. It can be viewed as a natural generalization of the classical proximal mapping (corresponding to
p=2
), where the quadratic regularization is replaced by a higher-order power term

Definition 42

(
High-order proximal operator).

Let
p>1
,
\beta>0
, and let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper lsc function. The high-order proximal operator (HOPE) of
h
with parameter
\beta
, denoted

\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta h}^{p}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
, is defined as

\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta h}^{p}(x):=\mathop{\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}}\limits_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{h(y)+\frac{1}{p\beta}\|x-y\|^{p}\right\}.

(31)

In general, the mapping
\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta h}^{p}
is set-valued. However, if

h
is convex, then the function

y\mapsto h(y)+\frac{1}{p\beta}\|x-y\|^{p}

is strictly convex for
p>1
, and therefore admits a unique minimizer.
In this case,
\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta h}^{p}
is single-valued (see, for instance,

[
5
, Proposition 12.15]
).

We next introduce the notion of a fixed point of the high-order proximal mapping, which will play a central role in the analysis of the iterative scheme. Under suitable assumptions, fixed points of
\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta h}^{p}
characterize minimizers of the original problem.

Definition 43

(
Proximal fixed point).

Let
p>1
,
\beta>0
, and let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper lsc function.
A point
x^{*}\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
is called a proximal fixed point if
x^{*}\in\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta h}^{p}(x^{*})
. The set of all such points is denoted by

{\boldsymbol{\rm Fix}}(\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta h}^{p})
.

The following theorem collects basic properties of HOPE. Note that the result holds for

(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex functions with modulus
\gamma\geq 0
, that is, including the merely quasar-convex case.

Theorem 44

(
Properties of HOPE).

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper lsc function that is

(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with
\kappa\in(0,1]
and
\gamma\geq 0
.
The following statements hold:

(a)

For each
\widehat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\beta>0
, and
p>1
, the set
\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta h}^{p}(\widehat{x})
is nonempty and compact;

(b)

For each
\beta>0
and
p>1
,

\mathop{\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}}\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h(x)={\boldsymbol{\rm Fix}}(\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta h}^{p}).

(c)

Let
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\{\beta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\subseteq(0,+\infty)
satisfy

x^{k}\to\widehat{x}
and
\beta_{k}\to\beta>0
.
If
y^{k}\in\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta_{k}h}^{p}(x^{k})
for all
k
, then the sequence
\{y^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is bounded, and all its cluster points belongs to
\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta h}^{p}(\widehat{x})
.

Proof.

(a)

Fix
\widehat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\beta>0
, and define

F(y):=h(y)+\frac{1}{p\beta}\|y-\widehat{x}\|^{p}.

If
\gamma>0
, then
h
is
2
-supercoercive by
[
13
, Proposition 12]
, and therefore coercive. Therefore
F
is coercive and lsc.
If
\gamma=0
, then
h
is quasar-convex and admits a global minimizer,
hence it is bounded from below. Since
p>1
, the function

\|y-\widehat{x}\|^{p}
is coercive, and thus the sum
h(y)+\frac{1}{p\beta}\|y-\widehat{x}\|^{p}

is coercive.
In both cases,
F
is coercive and lsc.
By
[
60
, Corollary 1.10]
, the compactness of sublevel sets of theses functions implies that

\mathop{\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}}\limits_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{h(y)+\frac{1}{p\beta}\|\widehat{x}-y\|^{p}\right\}\neq\emptyset,

and
\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta h}^{p}(\widehat{x})
is compact.

(b)
Let
\widehat{x}\in{\boldsymbol{\rm Fix}}(\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta h}^{p})
and
\beta>0
be arbitrary. Then,

h(\widehat{x})\leq h(x)+\frac{1}{p\beta}\lVert\widehat{x}-x\rVert^{p},\qquad\forall~x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Take
x_{\lambda}:=\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)\widehat{x}
with
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
. From
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strong quasar-convexity of
h
, for any
\lambda\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle h(\widehat{x})

\displaystyle\leq h(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)\widehat{x})+\frac{1}{p\beta}\lVert\lambda(\overline{x}-\widehat{x})\rVert^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\kappa h(\overline{x})+(1-\lambda\kappa)h(\widehat{x})-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa}\right)\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\lVert\widehat{x}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{p}}{p\beta}\lVert\widehat{x}-\overline{x}\rVert^{p}.

This leads to,

\lambda\kappa(h(\widehat{x})-h(\overline{x}))\leq-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa}\right)\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\lVert\widehat{x}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{p}}{p\beta}\lVert\widehat{x}-\overline{x}\rVert^{p},

which in turn implies,

h(\widehat{x})-h(\overline{x})\leq-\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa}\right)\frac{\gamma}{2}\lVert\widehat{x}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{p-1}}{p\kappa\beta}\lVert\widehat{x}-\overline{x}\rVert^{p}.

By letting
\lambda\downarrow 0
, it comes to

h(\widehat{x})-h(\overline{x})\leq-\frac{\gamma}{2}\lVert\widehat{x}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2},

i.e.,
h(\widehat{x})\leq h(\overline{x})
, thus
\widehat{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
.
Now, let
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
. For every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

h(\overline{x})+\frac{1}{p\beta}\lVert\overline{x}-\overline{x}\rVert^{p}=h(\overline{x})\leq h(y)\leq h(y)+\frac{1}{p\beta}\lVert y-\overline{x}\rVert^{p},

and the result follows.

(c)
Since
h
is quasar-convex, it admits a global minimizer, hence it is bounded from below. Since it is also lsc, it satisfies the assumptions of
[
31
, Theorem 3.4]
, and the result follows from
[
60
, Theorem 1.17 (b)]
.
∎

We are now in a position to introduce the high-order proximal-point algorithm (HiPPA) associated with the mapping
\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta h}^{p}
. Starting from an initial point
x^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the method generates a sequence
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
by successively solving high-order proximal subproblems with parameters
\{\beta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. In view of Theorem
44

(b)
, if the stopping criterion
x^{k+1}=x^{k}
is satisfied, then
x^{k}
is a proximal fixed point and therefore a global minimizer of
h
.

Algorithm 1

HiPPA (High-Order Proximal-Point Algorithm)

Step 0.

Choose an initial point
x^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a parameter
p>1
, and a sequence

\{\beta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\subseteq(0,+\infty)
bounded away from zero. Set
k=0
.

Step 1.

Choose

x^{k+1}\in\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta_{k}h}^{p}(x^{k}).

(32)

Step 2.

If
x^{k+1}=x^{k}
, then STOP; in this case,
x^{k}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
. Otherwise, set
k=k+1
and return to
Step 1
.

A natural stopping criterion for Algorithm
1
is
x^{k+1}=x^{k}
. In practice, however, it is typically replaced by the condition
\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|\leq\varepsilon
, where
\varepsilon>0
is a prescribed tolerance. The following result provides an upper bound on the number of iterations required to satisfy this practical stopping rule.

Proposition 45

(
Iteration complexity).

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper, lsc, and
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly
quasar-convex function with respect to some
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
, where
\kappa\in(0,1]
and

\gamma\geq 0
. Assume that

0<\beta^{\prime}\leq\beta_{k}\leq\beta^{\prime\prime},\qquad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(33)

Let
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, the stopping criterion
\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|\leq\varepsilon
is satisfied after at most

\left\lceil\frac{p\beta^{\prime\prime}(h(x^{0})-\boldsymbol{\min}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(x))}{\varepsilon^{p}}\right\rceil,

(34)

iterations.

Proof.

If
x^{k+1}=x^{k}
for some
k
, then the conclusion is immediate. Thus, assume that
x^{k+1}\neq x^{k}
for all
k
. Since

x^{k+1}\in\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta_{k}h}^{p}(x^{k})
, we have

h(x^{k+1})<h(x^{k+1})+\frac{1}{p\beta_{k}}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{p}\leq h(x^{k}),

which implies

\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{p}\leq p\beta_{k}(h(x^{k})-h(x^{k+1})),\qquad~\forall~k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(35)

Summing (
35
) for
k=0,\dots,N-1
and using
\beta_{k}\leq\beta^{\prime\prime}
, we obtain

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{p}\leq p\beta^{\prime\prime}(h(x^{0})-\mathop{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\min}}}\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(x)),

i.e.,

N\mathop{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\min}}}\limits_{0\leq k\leq N-1}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{p}\leq p\beta^{\prime\prime}(h(x^{0})-\mathop{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\min}}}\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(x)).

Hence, if

N\geq\frac{p\beta^{\prime\prime}\bigl(h(x^{0})-\mathop{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\min}}}\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(x)\bigr)}{\varepsilon^{p}},

then

\mathop{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\min}}}\limits_{0\leq k\leq N-1}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|\leq\varepsilon.

In other words, there exists some
k\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
such that
\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|\leq\varepsilon
, adjusting our desired result.
∎

Note that Proposition
45
provides an iteration bound for the
practical stopping criterion based on the successive iterations. It does not,
by itself, quantify objective accuracy or distance to the minimizer.

In the subsequent subsections, we analyze the behavior of the sequence
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
generated by Algorithm
1
. We first establish basic properties of the iterations, including well-posedness and descent
properties of the proximal steps. We then prove global convergence of the method under quasar-convexity assumptions. Finally, we derive convergence rates and complexity estimates,
highlighting the influence of the parameter
p
on the asymptotic behavior
of the algorithm.

4.1
Global convergence

As the first step, we establish fundamental properties of the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
, including descent, the summability of the steps, boundedness, and convergence to global minimizers.

Theorem 46

(
Convergence analysis).

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper, lsc, and
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly
quasar-convex function with respect to some

\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
, for
\kappa\in(0,1]
and

\gamma\geq 0
. Assume that relation (
33
) holds.
Let
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be generated by Algorithm
1
, with
p>1
. Then the following assertions hold:

(a)

The sequence
\{h(x^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is decreasing.

(b)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{p}<\infty
.

(c)

The sequence
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is bounded and the sequence

\{\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is nonincreasing.

(d)

The sequence
\{h(x^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
converges to
\boldsymbol{\min}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(x)
.

(e)

Every cluster point of
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
belongs to
\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
.

(f)

If
\gamma=0
and
h
is
\kappa
-quasar-convex with respect to every

\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
, then
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
converges to a point in

\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
.

(g)

If
\gamma>0
, then
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
converges to
\{\overline{x}\}=\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
.

Proof.

(a)
Suppose that
x^{k+1}\neq x^{k}
for all
k
. Since

x^{k+1}\in\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta_{k}h}^{p}(x^{k})
, we have

h(x^{k+1})<h(x^{k+1})+\frac{1}{p\beta_{k}}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{p}\leq h(x^{k}),

(36)

and the result follows.

(b)
: It follows immediately by telescoping (
36
) and using
\beta_{k}\geq\beta^{\prime}>0
.

(c)
:
Since every strongly quasar-convex function is quasar-convex, we only need to prove the case
\gamma=0
. Indeed,
we will show that
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is Fejér monotone with respect to
\{\overline{x}\}
(see
[
5
, Definition 5.1]
). Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Since
x^{k+1}\in\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta_{k}h}^{p}(x^{k})
, taking

x=\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x^{k+1},

we obtain

\displaystyle h(x^{k+1})+\frac{1}{p\beta_{k}}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq h(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x^{k+1})+\frac{1}{p\beta_{k}}\|\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa\lambda h(\overline{x})+(1-\kappa\lambda)h(x^{k+1})+\frac{1}{p\beta_{k}}\|(x^{k+1}-x^{k})+\lambda(\overline{x}-x^{k+1})\|^{p},

(37)

where the second inequality follows from the
\kappa
-quasar-convexity of
h
with respect to
\overline{x}
.
Rearranging (
4.1
), we get

\beta_{k}\kappa\big(h(x^{k+1})-h(\overline{x})\big)\leq\frac{\|(x^{k+1}-x^{k})+\lambda(\overline{x}-x^{k+1})\|^{p}-\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{p}}{p\lambda}.

(38)

Since the mapping
z\mapsto\|z\|^{p}
is Fréchet differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for every
p>1
,
letting
\lambda\downarrow 0
in (
38
) yields

\beta_{k}\kappa\left(h(x^{k+1})-h(\overline{x})\right)\leq\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{p-2}\langle x^{k+1}-x^{k},\overline{x}-x^{k+1}\rangle.

(39)

Applying the identity (
2
) yields

2\langle x^{k+1}-x^{k},\overline{x}-x^{k+1}\rangle=\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|^{2}-\|x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\|^{2}-\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}.

Together with (
39
) and
h(x^{k+1})\geq h(\overline{x})
, this ensures

0\leq 2\beta_{k}\kappa\big(h(x^{k+1})-h(\overline{x})\big)\leq\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{p-2}\left(\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|^{2}-\|x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\|^{2}-\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}\right),

(40)

i.e.,

\|x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\|\leq\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|,\qquad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

i.e.,
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is Fejér monotone with respect to
\{\overline{x}\}
.
In particular, it is bounded.

(d)
: Applying (
39
) and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, it follows that

0\leq\beta_{k}\kappa\big(h(x^{k+1})-h(\overline{x})\big)\leq\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{p-1}\|\overline{x}-x^{k+1}\|.

By Assertions
(b)
and
(c)
,

\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|\to 0
and
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is bounded. Since
p>1
and

\beta_{k}\geq\beta^{\prime}>0
, we obtain

h(x^{k+1})\to h(\overline{x})=\boldsymbol{\min}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(x).

(e)
: We define
y^{k}:=x^{k+1}
. Let
\widehat{x}
be a cluster point of the sequence
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. Then there exists a subsequence
\{x^{k_{j}}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
such that
x^{k_{j}}\to\widehat{x}
as
j\to+\infty
. By considering the corresponding subsequence
\{y^{k_{j}}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, Assertion
(b)
implies that
\|y^{k_{j}}-x^{k_{j}}\|\to 0.
Consequently, we also have
y^{k_{j}}\to\widehat{x}
as
j\to+\infty
. Since the sequence
\{\beta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is bounded, we may assume, by passing to a further subsequence if necessary, that
\beta_{k_{j}}\to\beta
as
j\to+\infty
for some
\beta>0
.
By the definition of iterations,
y^{k_{j}}\in\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta_{k_{j}}h}^{p}(x^{k_{j}})
. Applying Theorem
44

(c)
, we conclude that
\widehat{x}\in\boldsymbol{{\rm Prox}}_{\beta h}^{p}(\widehat{x})
, i.e.,
\widehat{x}
is a fixed point. Finally, by Theorem
44

(b)
, we have
\widehat{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
.

(f)
: Assume that
\gamma=0
. By Assertion
(c)
, the sequence

\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is bounded, i.e., it admits a cluster point. By Assertion
(e)
, every cluster point belongs to
\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
. Since, in addition,

\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is Fejér monotone with respect to
\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
, it follows from

[
5
, Theorem 5.11]
that
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
converges to a point in

\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
.

(g)
: It follows from Assertion
(c)
and
(e)
and because the set of minimizers of strongly quasar-convex functions is a singleton.
∎

Remark 47
.

(i)

Theorem
46
shows that HiPPA inherits the main structural properties of the classical proximal-point method, while accommodating the broader class of quasar-convex functions. In particular, the method produces a descent sequence with vanishing steps, and all accumulation points are global minimizers.

(ii)

Note that in Theorem
46
, part
(f)
, function
h
needs to be quasar-convex with respect to every
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
. This assumption can not be weakened, as there are functions which are quasar-convex with respect to some minimizer and that are not quasar-convex with respect to any other minimizer (see Example
17
).

4.2
Convergence rate analysis

We now analyze the asymptotic behavior of HiPPA in the strongly quasar-convex regime, namely when
\gamma>0
. In this setting, the minimizer is unique, and the geometry induced by the power
p
leads to different local estimates depending on whether
p<2
,
p=2
, or
p>2
.

Theorem 48

(
Convergence rates of the sequence of iterations:
\gamma>0
).

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper, lsc, and
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex function with respect to its unique minimizer
\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
, for
\kappa\in(0,1]
and
\gamma>0
. Let
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be generated by Algorithm
1
, and assume that relation (
33
) holds. Then the following assertions hold:

(a)

for
p\in(1,2)
, the convergence rate is locally linear, i.e., there exist
\overline{k}_{p}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\eta_{p}\in(0,1)
such that

\frac{\|x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\|}{\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|}\leq\eta_{p},\qquad\forall~k\geq\overline{k}_{p}.

(41)

(b)

for
p=2
, the convergence rate is linear, i.e.,

\frac{\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert}{\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\kappa\beta^{\prime}\gamma+(\frac{\kappa^{2}\beta^{\prime}\gamma}{2-\kappa})}},\qquad\forall~k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(42)

(c)

for
p>2
, the convergence rate is superlinear, i.e.,

\frac{\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert}{\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|^{p-1}}\leq c,\qquad\forall~k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

(43)

with
c:=\frac{2-\kappa}{\beta^{\prime}(\kappa\gamma)}
.

Proof.

From the definition, for each
p>1
, we obtain

h(x^{k+1})+\frac{1}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{p}\leq h(x)+\frac{1}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert x-x^{k}\rVert^{p},\qquad\forall~x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(44)

For each
\lambda\in[0,1]
, we have
x_{\lambda}:=\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x^{k+1}\in\boldsymbol{{\rm dom}}(h)
. Now, we prove the assertions.

(a)
:
Let
p\in(1,2)
be arbitrary. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\overline{x}=0
. Indeed, defining
g(z):=h(\overline{x}+z)
, the function
g
is
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
0
(see Proposition
18

(b)
), and the translated iterations satisfy the same recursion. Thus, it is enough to prove the result when
\overline{x}=0
.

Fix
r>0
such that
r\leq\left(\frac{\kappa_{p}}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{2-p}}
, where
\kappa_{p}
is introduced in (
7
). Since
x^{k}\to\overline{x}=0
by Theorem
46

(g)
and
\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|\to 0
by Theorem
46

(b)
, there exists

\overline{k}_{p}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
x^{k}\in\mathbf{B}(0,r)
for all
k\geq\overline{k}_{p}
.
Set
\sigma_{p}:=\frac{\kappa_{p}r^{p-2}}{2}>0
. Then, by Fact
2

(b)
, for every
k\geq\overline{k}_{p}
and every
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle h(x^{k+1})+\frac{1}{p\beta_{k}}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq h(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x^{k+1})+\frac{1}{p\beta_{k}}\|\lambda(\overline{x}-x^{k})+(1-\lambda)(x^{k+1}-x^{k})\|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\kappa h(\overline{x})+(1-\lambda\kappa)h(x^{k+1})-\lambda\Bigl(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa}\Bigr)\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\|x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\lambda}{p\beta_{k}}\|\overline{x}-x^{k}\|^{p}+\frac{1-\lambda}{p\beta_{k}}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{p}-\frac{\lambda(1-\lambda)\sigma_{p}}{p\beta_{k}}\|\overline{x}-x^{k+1}\|^{p}.

Since
\lambda\kappa\,h(\overline{x})+(1-\lambda\kappa)h(x^{k+1})\leq h(x^{k+1})
, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{p}\leq-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa}\right)\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert\overline{x}-x^{k}\rVert^{p}-\frac{\lambda(1-\lambda)\sigma_{p}}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert\overline{x}-x^{k+1}\rVert^{p}.

By dividing both sides by
\lambda
and letting
\lambda\downarrow 0
, we get

\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}+\frac{\sigma_{p}}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert\overline{x}-x^{k+1}\rVert^{p}\leq\frac{1}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert\overline{x}-x^{k}\rVert^{p}-\frac{1}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{p}.

(45)

In light of (
6
), we get

\|x+y\|^{p}\leq\|x\|^{p}+p\|x+y\|^{p-2}\langle x+y,y\rangle.

by setting
x=x^{k+1}-x^{k}
and
y=\overline{x}-x^{k+1}
, we get

\|\overline{x}-x^{k}\|^{p}\leq\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{p}+p\|\overline{x}-x^{k}\|^{p-1}\lVert\overline{x}-x^{k+1}\rVert.

Combining this and (
45
) imply

\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}+\frac{\sigma_{p}}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert\overline{x}-x^{k+1}\rVert^{p}\leq\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}\|\overline{x}-x^{k}\|^{p-1}\lVert\overline{x}-x^{k+1}\rVert.

Dropping the nonnegative first term on the left, we obtain

\frac{\sigma_{p}}{p\beta_{k}}\|\overline{x}-x^{k+1}\|^{p}\leq\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}\|\overline{x}-x^{k}\|^{p-1}\|\overline{x}-x^{k+1}\|,

i.e.,

\|\overline{x}-x^{k+1}\|\leq\left(\frac{p}{\sigma_{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\|\overline{x}-x^{k}\|.

Since
r\leq\left(\frac{\kappa_{p}}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{2-p}}
, we have
\sigma_{p}=\frac{\kappa_{p}r^{p-2}}{2}\geq 2>p
. Therefore,
\eta_{p}:=\left(\frac{p}{\sigma_{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\in(0,1)
, and

\|x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\|\leq\eta_{p}\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|,\qquad\forall~k\geq\overline{k}_{p}.

This proves that the convergence is eventually locally linear.

(b)
: See
[
13
, Theorem 18]
.

(c)
: Invoking Fact
2

(c)
and (
44
), for every
p>2
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
, leads to

\displaystyle h(x^{k+1})+\frac{1}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{p}

\displaystyle\leq h(\lambda\overline{x}+(1-\lambda)x^{k+1})+\frac{1}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert\lambda(\overline{x}-x^{k})+(1-\lambda)(x^{k+1}-x^{k})\rVert^{p},

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\kappa\,h(\overline{x})+(1-\lambda\kappa)h(x^{k+1})-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa}\right)\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert x^{k}-\overline{x}\rVert^{p}

\displaystyle~~~~+\frac{(1-\lambda)}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{p}-\frac{\lambda(1-\lambda)\hat{\sigma}_{p}}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert^{p},

for
\hat{\sigma}_{p}:=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{3p-2}{2}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lambda\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2-\kappa}\right)\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}+\frac{\lambda(1-\lambda)\hat{\sigma}_{p}}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\kappa(h(\overline{x})-h(x^{k+1}))+\frac{\lambda}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert x^{k}-\overline{x}\rVert^{p}-\frac{\lambda}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{p}.

By dividing both sides by
\lambda
and letting
\lambda\downarrow 0
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}+\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{p}}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert^{p}\leq

\displaystyle\,\kappa(h(\overline{x})-h(x^{k+1}))+\frac{1}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert x^{k}-\overline{x}\rVert^{p}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{p}.

(46)

Combining this with (
6
) results in

\displaystyle\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|^{p}

\displaystyle=\|(x^{k}-x^{k+1})-(\overline{x}-x^{k+1})\|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{p}-p\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|^{p-2}\langle x^{k}-\overline{x},\overline{x}-x^{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{p}+p\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|^{p-2}\langle x^{k}-\overline{x},x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{p}+p\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|^{p-1}\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert.

It follows from this and (
46
) that

\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}+\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{p}}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert^{p}\leq\kappa(h(\overline{x})-h(x^{k+1}))+\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|^{p-1}\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert.

Using (
10
) with
y=x^{k+1}
, we get
h(\overline{x})-h(x^{k+1})\leq-\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2(2-\kappa)}\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}
, i.e.,

\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}\gamma}{2(2-\kappa)}+\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\right)\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}+\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{p}}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert^{p}\leq\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|^{p-1}\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert,

leading to

\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}\gamma}{2(2-\kappa)}+\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\right)\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert+\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{p}}{p\beta_{k}}\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert^{p-1}\leq\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|^{p-1},

i.e.,

\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}\gamma}{2(2-\kappa)}+\frac{\kappa\gamma}{2}\right)\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert\leq\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|^{p-1}.

This consequently ensures

\frac{\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert}{\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|^{p-1}}\leq\frac{2-\kappa}{\beta^{\prime}(\kappa\gamma)}.

Since
p>2
and
x^{k}\rightarrow\overline{x}
by Theorem
46

(g)
, it follows that
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
converges superlinearly to the unique solution
\overline{x}
, which completes
the proof.
∎

Note that the convergence rate provided in Theorem
48

(c)
is superlinear, which is

\frac{\|x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\|}{\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|}\to 0\quad\text{as }k\to\infty.

Moreover, if

\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|\leq c^{-\frac{1}{p-2}},

with
c:=\frac{2-\kappa}{\beta^{\prime}(\kappa\gamma)}
, one obtains the monotonic decay

\|x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\|\leq\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|,\qquad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Theorem 49

(
Convergence rates for the sequence of function values:
\gamma>0
).

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper, lsc and
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex function
with respect to
\{\overline{x}\}=\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
, where
\kappa\in(0,1]
and
\gamma>0
.
Let
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
and suppose that relation (
33
) holds. Then the following assertions hold:

(a)

If

, then there exist
\overline{k}_{p}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\eta_{p}\in(0,1)
such that
for every

,

h(x^{k+1})-h(\overline{x})\leq\frac{1}{p\beta^{\prime}}\,\eta_{p}^{\,p(k-\overline{k}_{p})}\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|^{p}.

(47)

(b)

If
p=2
, then for every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

h(x^{k+1})-h(\overline{x})\leq\frac{1}{2\beta^{\prime}}\,r^{2k}\,\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|^{2},

for

(c)

, define

.

,

(49)

(a)

48

(a)

41

).

(b)

(b)
,
for every

Theorem 50

(
Convergence rates for the sequence of function values:
\gamma=0
).

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper, lsc and
\kappa
-quasar-convex function (i.e.,
\gamma=0
) with respect to every element of
\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
. Suppose that relation (
33
) holds.
Let
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be generated by Algorithm
1
, and assume that
x^{k}\to\overline{x}\in\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
. Then the following assertions hold:

(a)

If
p\in(1,2)
, then for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

h(x^{k})-h(\overline{x})\leq\frac{\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|^{p}}{2^{p-1}\beta^{\prime}\kappa}\frac{1}{k^{p-1}}.

(51)

(b)

If
p=2
, then for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

h(x^{k})-h(\overline{x})\leq\frac{\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|^{2}}{2\kappa\beta^{\prime}}\frac{1}{k}.

(52)

(c)

If
p>2
, then for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

h(x^{k})-h(\overline{x})\leq\frac{1}{\beta^{\prime}\kappa p}\left(\frac{p-2}{p}\right)^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\frac{\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|^{p}}{k^{\frac{p}{2}}},

(53)

Proof.

We recall first from the proof of Theorem
46

(c)
(see (
40
)) that

\displaystyle 2\beta^{\prime}\kappa(h(x^{k+1})-h(\overline{x}))\leq\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{p-2}\left(\lVert x^{k}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}-\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}-\lVert x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rVert^{2}\right).

Let us set

\Delta_{k}:=\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|^{2}-\|x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\|^{2}.

Note that from Theorem
46

(c)
,
\Delta_{k}\geq 0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

(a)
: Since
p\in(1,2)
, we have
p-2<0
.
By the triangle inequality,

\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|\geq\big|\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|-\|x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\|\big|.

Since the function
t\mapsto t^{p-2}
is decreasing on
(0,+\infty)
, it follows from
(
40
) and (

) that

2\beta^{\prime}\kappa\big(h(x^{k+1})-h(\overline{x})\big)\leq\big(\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|-\|x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\|\big)^{p-2}\,\Delta_{k}.

Applying the identity

\Delta_{k}=\big(\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|-\|x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\|\big)\big(\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|+\|x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\|\big),

it can be concluded that

2\beta^{\prime}\kappa\big(h(x^{k+1})-h(\overline{x})\big)\leq\big(\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|+\|x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\|\big)^{2-p}\Delta_{k}^{\,p-1}.

Since
\{\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|\}

\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|+\|x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\|\leq 2\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|.

It follows from
2-p>0
and the inequality (
56
) that

h(x^{k+1})-h(\overline{x})\leq\frac{(2\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|)^{2-p}}{2\beta^{\prime}\kappa}\,\Delta_{k}^{\,p-1}.

(57)

Together with (
57
) and the fact that
\{h(x^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}

k\big(h(x^{k})-h(\overline{x})\big)\leq\frac{(2\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|)^{2-p}}{2\beta^{\prime}\kappa}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\Delta_{j}^{\,p-1}.

Since the function
t\mapsto t^{p-1}
is concave on
[0,+\infty)
, Jensen’s inequality ensures

\dfrac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\Delta_{j}^{\,p-1}\leq\left(\dfrac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\Delta_{j}\right)^{p-1}.

Using the telescoping sum,

\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\Delta_{j}=\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|^{2}-\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|^{2}\leq\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|^{2},

it can be concluded that

h(x^{k})-h(\overline{x})\leq\frac{(2\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|)^{2-p}}{2\beta^{\prime}\kappa}\,\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|^{2(p-1)}\frac{1}{k^{p-1}}.

The inequalities (
51
) and (
72
) follow directly from the last inequality.

(b)
: Follows from
[
13
, Proposition 25]
.

(c)
: Let
d_{k}=x^{k+1}-x^{k}
.
From (
54
), we obtain

2\beta^{\prime}\kappa\big(h(x^{k+1})-h(\overline{x})\big)\leq\|d_{k}\|^{p-2}\Delta_{k}-\|d_{k}\|^{p}.

(58)

We note that the function
\psi_{k}(t):=\Delta_{k}t^{p-2}-t^{p}
on
t\geq 0
.
For
p>2
, a simple calculation shows that

t_{k}=\left(\dfrac{p-2}{p}\Delta_{k}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

is the global maximizer of
\psi_{k}(t)
, and

\mathop{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\max}}}\limits_{t\geq 0}\psi_{k}(t)=\frac{2}{p}\Big(\frac{p-2}{p}\Big)^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\Delta_{k}^{p/2}.

Applying this bound in (
58
) results in

2\beta^{\prime}\kappa\big(h(x^{k+1})-h(\overline{x})\big)\leq\psi_{k}(\lVert d_{k}\rVert)\leq\frac{2}{p}\Big(\frac{p-2}{p}\Big)^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\Delta_{k}^{p/2},

i.e.,

\big(h(x^{k+1})-h(\overline{x})\big)^{\frac{2}{p}}\leq\left(\frac{1}{\beta^{\prime}\kappa p}\right)^{\frac{2}{p}}\Big(\frac{p-2}{p}\Big)^{\frac{p-2}{p}}\Delta_{k}.

Similar to Assertion
(a)

4.3
Complexity analysis

In this section, we study the non-asymptotic behavior of the sequence generated by HiPPA by deriving iteration-complexity bounds for quasar-convex functions in both regimes
\gamma>0
and
\gamma=0
.

Our first result establishes iteration complexity bounds in terms of the distance to the minimizer, distinguishing the regimes
p\in(1,2)
,
p=2
, and
p>2
.

Theorem 51

(
Complexity for the sequence of iterations:
\gamma>0
).

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper, lsc and

(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex function
with respect to
\{\overline{x}\}=\boldsymbol{\arg\boldsymbol{\min}}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
, where
\kappa\in(0,1]
and
\gamma>0
.
Let
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
and suppose that relation (
33
) holds. Then the following assertions hold:

(a)

If
p\in(1,2)
and
x^{0}\in\mathbf{B}(\overline{x},r)
with
r\leq\left(\frac{\kappa_{p}}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{2-p}}
, where
\kappa_{p}
is introduced in (
7
), then for a given
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, the number of iterations to reach

\|\overline{x}-x^{k}\|<\varepsilon
, denoted by
\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon)
, satisfies

\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon)\leq\left\lceil 1+\frac{\log(\varepsilon^{-1})+\log\left(\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|\right)}{\log(1/\eta_{p})}\right\rceil,

(59)

for
\eta_{p}=(\frac{2p}{\kappa_{p}r^{p-2}})^{\frac{1}{p-1}}<1
.
In particular,

\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{O}(\log(\varepsilon^{-1}))
.

(b)

If
p=2
, then, for a given
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, the number of iterations to reach

\|\overline{x}-x^{k}\|<\varepsilon
, denoted by
\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon)
, satisfies

\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon)\leq\left\lceil 1+\frac{\log(\varepsilon^{-1})+\log(\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|)}{\log(1/r)}\right\rceil,

(60)

for
r:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\kappa\beta^{\prime}\gamma+\frac{\kappa^{2}\beta^{\prime}\gamma}{2-\kappa}}}\in(0,1)
. In particular,

\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{O}(\log(\varepsilon^{-1}))
.

(c)

If
p>2
, define
c:=\frac{2-\kappa}{\beta^{\prime}\kappa\gamma}
and assume that

\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|\leq c^{-1/(p-2)}.

(61)

Then, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|\leq c^{-1/(p-2)}\Big(c^{1/(p-2)}\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|\Big)^{(p-1)^{k}}.

(62)

Consequently, for a given
\varepsilon\in(0,c^{-1/(p-2)})
, the number of iterations to reach
\|\overline{x}-x^{k}\|<\varepsilon
, denoted by
\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon)
, satisfies

\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon)\leq\left\lceil 1+\frac{\log\!\left(\frac{\log\!\big((c^{1/(p-2)}\varepsilon)^{-1}\big)}{\log\!\big((c^{1/(p-2)}\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|)^{-1}\big)}\right)}{\log(p-1)}\right\rceil.

(63)

In particular,

\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{O}\big(\log\log(\varepsilon^{-1})\big)
.

Proof.

(a)
If
\|\overline{x}-x^{0}\|<\varepsilon
, the stopping criterion is already satisfied and the algorithm terminates at the initial iterate. Let us assume that

\|\overline{x}-x^{0}\|\geq\varepsilon
, which ensures that

\log(\varepsilon^{-1})+\log\left(\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|\right)\geq 0.

(64)

Since the sequence starts in
\mathbf{B}(\overline{x},r)
, from Theorem
46
(c)
,

x^{k}\in\mathbf{B}(\overline{x},r)
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, i.e., for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the proof of Theorem
48

(a)
yields

\|x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\|\leq\eta_{p}\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|,

for
\eta_{p}=(\frac{2p}{\kappa_{p}r^{p-2}})^{\frac{1}{p-1}}<1
.
Using the induction, this consequently implies

\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|\leq\eta_{p}^{k}\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|,\qquad\forall\,k\geq 0.

(65)

Let
\mathcal{K}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
be the smallest integer such that

\eta_{p}^{\mathcal{K}}\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|<\varepsilon
, which in turn implies
\|x^{\mathcal{K}}-\overline{x}\|<\varepsilon
, i.e.,
\eta_{p}^{\mathcal{K}-1}\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|\geq\varepsilon
. Taking logarithms,

(\mathcal{K}-1)\log(\eta_{p})\geq\log(\varepsilon)-\log\left(\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|\right),

i.e.,

\mathcal{K}\leq 1+\frac{\log(\varepsilon^{-1})+\log\left(\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|\right)}{\log(1/\eta_{p})}.

Thus, the bound (
59
) follows.

(b)
:
Similar to the proof of Assertion
(a)
, if the algorithm does not terminate at the initial iterate, (
64
) holds.
From Theorem
48

(b)
,

\lVert x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\rVert\leq r\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|
.
Consequently, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|\leq r^{k}\|x^{0}-\overline{x}\|.

(66)

Proceeding as above gives (
60
).

(c)
: In light of Theorem
48

(c)
, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, it follows that

\|x^{k+1}-\overline{x}\|\leq c\,\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|^{p-1}.

(67)

Let
e_{k}:=\|x^{k}-\overline{x}\|
and define
u_{k}:=c^{1/(p-2)}e_{k}
. Then, the inequality (
67
) becomes

u_{k+1}\leq u_{k}^{\,p-1},\qquad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

By (
61
), we have
u_{0}\leq 1
, i.e., by the induction, it follows that

u_{k}\leq u_{0}^{(p-1)^{k}},\qquad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Returning to
e_{k}
, we obtain (
62
).
Now, let
\varepsilon\in(0,c^{-1/(p-2)})
. In order to guarantee
e_{k}<\varepsilon
, it is enough that

c^{-1/(p-2)}\Big(c^{1/(p-2)}e_{0}\Big)^{(p-1)^{k}}<\varepsilon.

Let
\mathcal{K}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
be the smallest integer such that the above inequality holds, i.e.,

c^{-1/(p-2)}\Big(c^{1/(p-2)}e_{0}\Big)^{(p-1)^{\mathcal{K}-1}}\geq\varepsilon.

Since
0<c^{1/(p-2)}e_{0}\leq 1
and
0<c^{1/(p-2)}\varepsilon<1
, this yields

(p-1)^{\mathcal{K}-1}\leq\frac{\log\left(\left(c^{1/(p-2)}\varepsilon\right)^{-1}\right)}{\log\left(\left(c^{1/(p-2)}e_{0}\right)^{-1}\right)}.

Since
p-1>1
, taking logarithms again gives (
63
).
∎

Remark 54
.

Even in the absence of strong quasar-convexity, i.e., when
\gamma=0
, Theorem
53
shows that different values of power
p
lead to different iteration complexity bounds. The complexity bounds obtained in Theorem
53
can be compared with the classical case with
p=2
. In this case, even for convex functions, PPA attains the iteration complexity
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})
, a rate that has been extended to quasar-convex functions (see
[
13
, Proposition 25
(d)
]
).

When
p\in(1,2)
, the resulting complexity
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1/(p-1)})
is slower than the classical
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})
rate. When
p>2
, the HiPPA method attains the complexity
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2/p})
, which is faster than
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})
.

Numerical experiments

In this section, we implement HiPPA and report our numerical results on two prominent applications: (i) Generalized linear model; (ii) Nonconvex robust multi-task regression.
All experiments are conducted in Python on a laptop equipped with a 12th Gen Intel
\circledR
Core
{}^{\text{TM}}
i7-12800H CPU (1.80 GHz) and 16 GB of RAM.

5.1
Application to generalized linear models

We first recall the ReLU generalized linear model (GLM) setting and the strong quasar-convexity result that motivates our benchmark. We then present numerical experiments comparing HiPPA with projected first-order methods on the corresponding population loss.

Let
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
be data points, where
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are sampled i.i.d. from a distribution
D_{x}
, and the responses are generated according to the generalized linear model

y_{i}=\sigma(\langle w_{t}^{\ast},x_{i}\rangle)+\varepsilon_{i},

(75)

where
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a known monotone activation function,
\varepsilon_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
are i.i.d. mean-zero noises independent of
x_{i}
, and
w_{t}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the unknown ground-truth vector. Denote by
D
the joint distribution of
(x,y)
.
The empirical squared risk is

\widehat{f}(w)=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(y_{i}-\sigma(\langle w,x_{i}\rangle)\bigr)^{2},

(76)

and the corresponding population risk is

f(w)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim D}\Bigl[\bigl(y-\sigma(\langle w,x\rangle)\bigr)^{2}\Bigr].

(77)

We consider the ReLU activation
\sigma(z)=\boldsymbol{\max}\{0,z\}
.
We recall the structural assumption used in
[
59
]
.

Assumption 55
.

For every
t=1,\dots,T
and every
w\neq w_{t}^{\ast}
, let
\mathcal{P}_{w,w_{t}^{\ast}}
denote the marginal distribution of
x
on the subspace spanned by
w
and
w_{t}^{\ast}
, viewed as a distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Assume that
\mathcal{P}_{w,w_{t}^{\ast}}
admits a density
p_{w,w_{t}^{\ast}}
such that

\boldsymbol{\inf}_{\|x\|\leq\varepsilon}p_{w,w_{t}^{\ast}}(x)\geq\beta
,
for some
\varepsilon>0
and
\beta>0
.

The following result is the main justification for the benchmark used below.

Proposition 56

(
[
59
, Corollary 3]
)
.

Assume Assumption
55
. Suppose
\|x\|\leq c
almost surely for
x\sim\mathcal{P}
, with
c\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
. For
t=1,\dots,T
, define

\mathcal{R}_{t}=\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|w-w_{t}^{\ast}\|^{2}\leq\|w_{t}^{\ast}\|^{2},\ \|w\|\leq 2\|w_{t}^{\ast}\|\right\}.

(78)

Then
f_{t}
is
(\rho,\mu)
-strongly quasar-convex with respect to
w_{t}^{\ast}
over
\mathcal{R}_{t}
, for

\rho=\frac{\varepsilon^{4}\boldsymbol{\sin}^{3}(\pi/8)}{8\sqrt{2}\,c}
and
\mu=c
.

We optimize the population objective

f(w)=E_{x\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\operatorname{ReLU}(\langle w,x\rangle)-\operatorname{ReLU}(\langle w_{t}^{\ast},x\rangle)\bigr)^{2}\right],

(79)

where model is noiseless, namely
y(x)=\operatorname{ReLU}(\langle w^{\ast},x\rangle)
.
The distribution
\mathcal{P}
is chosen as the uniform distribution on the Euclidean ball

\mathbf{B}(0,\sqrt{c})
,
i.e.,
\|x\|^{2}\leq c
almost surely, and
\mathcal{P}
is absolutely continuous. This choice is compatible with the assumptions underlying Proposition
56
. Moreover, after every algorithmic update, we project the iterate onto the region
\mathcal{R}
in (
78
), so that all iterations remain in the theorem-relevant set.

In our implementation, we use the default parameters
n=100
and
c=4
. The ground-truth vector is generated by drawing
\widetilde{w}^{\ast}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})
and setting
w^{\ast}=2\,\frac{\widetilde{w}^{\ast}}{\|\widetilde{w}^{\ast}\|}
.
Thus
\|w^{\ast}\|=2
.
The initial point is chosen as
w^{0}=w^{\ast}+\eta\|w^{\ast}\|d

with
\eta=0.3
, where
d
is a random unit vector. The point
w^{0}
is then projected onto
\mathcal{R}
.

Since the objective in (
79
) is an expectation, all function values and gradients are computed by Monte Carlo approximation.
For reporting
f(w)
, we use an independent batch of size
M_{\mathrm{eval}}=50{,}000
and compute

f(w)\approx\frac{1}{2M_{\mathrm{eval}}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\mathrm{eval}}}\bigl(\operatorname{ReLU}(\langle w,x_{j}\rangle)-\operatorname{ReLU}(\langle w^{\ast},x_{j}\rangle)\bigr)^{2}.

(80)

For a sampled batch
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
, we use the approximation

\nabla f(w)\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\bigl(\operatorname{ReLU}(\langle w,x_{j}\rangle)-\operatorname{ReLU}(\langle w^{\ast},x_{j}\rangle)\bigr)\,g_{\operatorname{ReLU}}(\langle w,x_{j}\rangle)\,x_{j},

(81)

where
g_{\operatorname{ReLU}}(z)=1
for
z>0
and
g_{\operatorname{ReLU}}(0)=0
.
In the implementation, we use
M_{\mathrm{full}}=12{,}000
for high-accuracy gradient evaluations and
M_{\mathrm{sgd}}=256
for stochastic gradient steps.

In our comparison, we used the following methods:

•

PGD
: Projected gradient descent

w^{k+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{R}}\bigl(w^{k}-\alpha\,\widehat{\nabla}f(w^{k})\bigr),

(82)

where
\Pi_{\mathcal{R}}
denotes projection onto
\mathcal{R}
, and
\widehat{\nabla}f(w^{k})
is the Monte Carlo gradient estimator from (
81
) using batch size
M_{\mathrm{full}}
. We use the constant step-size
\alpha_{\mathrm{PGD}}=0.12
.

•

PSGD
: Projected stochastic gradient descent

w^{k+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{R}}\bigl(w^{k}-\alpha_{k}\,\widehat{\nabla}f_{B_{k}}(w^{k})\bigr),

(83)

where
B_{k}
is a mini-batch of size
M_{\mathrm{sgd}}=256
. We use a constant step-size
\alpha_{\mathrm{PSGD}}=0.07
.

•

HiPPA
: We test two HiPPA variants, corresponding to
p=2
and
p=3
. We use
\beta=0.8
.
The subproblem is solved numerically using
L-BFGS-B
.

All methods are allowed to run for at most
2000
iterations. They stop earlier whenever the relative error satisfies

\operatorname{RelErr}(w^{k}):=\frac{\|w^{k}-w^{\ast}\|}{\|w^{\ast}\|}<10^{-2}.

(84)

The results are displayed in Figure

.
Subfigure
4
(a) reports the objective value against elapsed time. This comparison is especially informative because each HiPPA iteration requires the solution of an inner optimization problem, while PGD and PSGD only need a gradient step. Even under this more demanding metric, HiPPA-
p=3
remains the best method. HiPPA-

is also clearly competitive and outperforms both first-order baselines. PGD is substantially faster than PSGD in terms of practical decrease per unit time.

(b) shows the relative error against elapsed time. The same ranking is observed. HiPPA-

is the most efficient method in practice, followed by PSGD, then HiPPA-

5.2
Application to robust multi-task regression

In this section, we consider a nonconvex and nonsmooth robust multi-task regression (RMTR) model and use it as a benchmark for HiPPA.
We show that, although the model is neither convex nor strongly star-convex, it is strongly quasar-convex on bounded balls around the ground-truth matrix. Then, we describe the numerical implementation used in our experiments.

Let
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
for
i=1,\dots,N
, and let
W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
denote the matrix of regression coefficients. We study the objective

\Phi(W):=\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|Wx_{i}-y_{i}\|_{2}\right)^{q},

(85)

with
q\in(0,1)
. We assume the realizable model

y_{i}=W^{\ast}x_{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,N,

(86)

for some unknown ground-truth matrix
W^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
. The inner loss

W\mapsto\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|Wx_{i}-y_{i}\|_{2}

is an
\ell_{2,1}
-type multi-task residual model. It couples the tasks through Euclidean norms of vector residuals and is therefore naturally suited to joint learning settings where several related prediction tasks are learned simultaneously. Such
\ell_{2,1}
-based formulations are widely used in multi-task feature learning and shared-sparsity modeling
[
45
]
. Moreover, robust multi-task regression models are especially relevant when responses may be contaminated by large corruptions or outliers
[
66
]
. The additional exponent
q\in(0,1)
in (
85
) yields a genuinely nonconvex and nonsmooth extension of the classical convex baseline, in the same spirit as nonconvex multi-task feature learning models designed to sharpen convex surrogates
[
15
]
.

Accordingly, (
85
) should be interpreted as a nonconvex robust multi-task regression model.
Throughout this section, we identify
\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
with the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{md}
endowed with the Frobenius norm

\|W\|_{F}:=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}W_{j\ell}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

We also write
X:=[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}

for the sample matrix and assume that
X
has full row rank. For
R>0
, define

K_{R}:=\{W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}:\|W-W^{\ast}\|_{F}\leq R\}.

(87)

The next proposition shows that the model (
85
) is strongly quasar-convex on every bounded ball centered at
W^{\ast}
.

Proposition 57
.

Let
q\in(0,1)
,
W^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
, and let
X=[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
have full row rank.
Assume the model (
86
) and define the function
\Phi
as (
85
).
Fix
R>0
and let
K_{R}
be given by (
87
). Then, for every
\kappa\in(0,q)
, the function
\Phi
is
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex on
K_{R}
with respect to
W^{\ast}
, where

\gamma=\frac{2(q-\kappa)}{\kappa}\,c_{X}^{q}\,R^{q-2},

(88)

with

c_{X}:=\mathop{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\min}}}\limits_{\|U\|_{F}=1}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|Ux_{i}\|_{2}>0.

Moreover,
\Phi
is neither convex nor strongly star-convex.

Proof.

See Appendix
B
.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we considered the optimization of (strongly) quasar-convex functions. As a first step, we studied the fundamental properties of this class of functions, which pave the way for its applicability across many domains, including machine learning and data science. Next, we developed and analyzed high-order proximal-point methods (HiPPA with
p>1
) for this class of nonconvex optimization and established conditions ensuring convergence to global minimizers. We further derived convergence rates and complexity bounds, showing local linear convergence for
p\in(1,2)
, linear convergence for
p=2
. and superlinear convergence for
p>2
. Preliminary numerical results on a generalized linear model support the efficiency of our approach.
