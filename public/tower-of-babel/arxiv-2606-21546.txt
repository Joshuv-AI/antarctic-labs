1.
Introduction

Stable solutions of semilinear elliptic equations of the form

-\Delta u=f(u)

occupy a central place in nonlinear elliptic theory and arise naturally in a variety of problems. For specific nonlinearities, such as
e^{u}
,
(1+u)^{p}
, or
(1-u)^{-p}
, a well-developed theory identifies sharp regularity thresholds and, in several cases, provides quantitative information on the singular set.

The aim of this paper is to develop a sharp partial regularity theory for general positive, increasing, and convex nonlinearities, with estimates that are as uniform as possible with respect to
f
. This requires overcoming two structural difficulties. First, for an arbitrary nonlinearity
f
, there is no known monotonicity formula suitable for dimension reduction. Second, natural compactness arguments force one to consider limits in which both the solution and the nonlinearity vary, and the limiting nonlinearity may blow up at a finite level.

The main ingredient is a uniform
\varepsilon
-regularity theorem for stable solutions, which answers the intrinsic scale-invariant formulation (
1.6
) of Brezis’ question (
1.3
); see
Theorem

2.7
below. Before stating our results, we recall the classical Gelfand problem

1.1.
Motivation: the Gelfand problem and a question of Brezis

We begin with the classical Gelfand-type Dirichlet problem: for a parameter
\lambda>0
, consider the PDE

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\Delta u=\lambda f(u)&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u>0&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded smooth domain and the nonlinearity
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies

f>0,\qquad f^{\prime}\geq 0,\qquad f^{\prime\prime}\geq 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{f(t)}{t}\to+\infty\ \text{ as }t\to+\infty.

Let us establish some terminology for solutions of (
1.1
):

•

u\in C^{2}(\Omega)\cap C(\overline{\Omega})
is a
classical solution
if it solves (
1.1
).

•

u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is a
weak solution
if
f(u)\cdot\dist(\cdot,\partial\Omega)\in L^{1}(\Omega)
and

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\zeta\,dx=\lambda\int_{\Omega}f(u)\,\zeta\,dx\qquad\text{for all }\zeta\in C^{1}_{c}(\Omega).

•

u\in L^{1}(\Omega)
is an
L^{1}
-weak solution
if
f(u)\cdot\dist(\cdot,\partial\Omega)\in L^{1}(\Omega)
and

-\int_{\Omega}u\,\Delta\xi\,dx=\lambda\int_{\Omega}f(u)\,\xi\,dx\qquad\text{for all }\xi\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})\ \text{with }\xi|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

A solution (of any of the above types) is said to be
stable
if the linearized operator is nonnegative in the Dirichlet sense, i.e.

\lambda_{1}(-\Delta-\lambda f^{\prime}(u),\Omega)\geq 0.

Equivalently (and this is the form we will use throughout),
u
is stable if
f^{\prime}(u)\in L^{1}_{\text{{loc}}}(\Omega)
and

(1.2)

\int_{\Omega}\lambda\,f^{\prime}(u)\,\xi^{2}\,dx\leq\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\xi|^{2}\,dx\qquad\text{for all }\xi\in C^{1}_{c}(\Omega).

The main qualitative results for (
1.1
) can be summarized as follows:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
1
,
3
,
11
]
)
.

There exists a constant
\lambda^{\star}\in(0,+\infty)
such that:

(i)

For every
\lambda\in(0,\lambda^{\star})
there is a unique weak stable solution
u_{\lambda}
, which is also classical. Furthermore,
u_{\lambda}>u_{\lambda^{\prime}}
for
\lambda>\lambda^{\prime}
.

(ii)

For every
\lambda>\lambda^{\star}
there is no
L^{1}
-weak solution.

(iii)

For
\lambda=\lambda^{\star}
there exists a unique
L^{1}
-weak solution
u^{\star}
, which is also a weak stable solution and
u_{\lambda}\uparrow u^{\star}
as
\lambda\uparrow\lambda^{\star}
. Additionally, if
n\leq 9
, then
u^{\star}
is necessarily classical.

Remark 1.2
.

A convenient way to construct
u_{\lambda}
is via monotone iteration: set
u_{0,\lambda}=0
and solve

-\Delta u_{k+1,\lambda}=\lambda f(u_{k,\lambda})\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u_{k+1,\lambda}=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.

Then for
\lambda
small one proves that the solutions remain bounded in
L^{1}(\Omega)
and
u_{k,\lambda}\uparrow u_{\lambda}
, while for
\lambda
large there is complete blow-up, in the sense that
\{u_{k,\lambda}(x)\}_{k}
diverges for each
x\in\Omega
.

In the influential work
[
1
]
, Brezis asked several questions concerning (
1.1
), especially regarding the properties of the extremal solution
u^{\star}
.
The starting point of the present work is a resolution of a natural scale-invariant formulation of

[
1
, Open problem 4]
:

“Suppose
u^{\star}
has an isolated singularity at
x_{0}\in\Omega
. Is it true that

(1.3)

f^{\prime}(u^{\star}(x))\simeq\frac{1}{|x-x_{0}|^{2}}\quad\text{as }x\to x_{0}\text{?''}

This conjecture is motivated by two considerations. First, for the model nonlinearities

f(u)=e^{u}\quad\text{or}\quad f(u)=(1+u)^{p},

one can construct explicit radial extremal solutions
u^{\star}
for which the conjecture holds. Second, the scaling
1/|x|^{2}
is the right one when comparing the stability condition (
1.2
) with Hardy’s inequality, which states that for
n\geq 3
,

(1.4)

\frac{(n-2)^{2}}{4}\int_{B_{1}}\frac{\xi(x)^{2}}{|x|^{2}}\,dx\leq\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla\xi(x)|^{2}\,dx\qquad\forall\,\xi\in C^{1}_{c}(B_{1}).

When
n=2
, Hardy’s inequality includes a logarithmic correction:

(1.5)

\frac{1}{4}\int_{B_{1}}\frac{\xi(x)^{2}}{|x|^{2}(1-\log|x|)^{2}}\,dx\leq\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla\xi(x)|^{2}\,dx\qquad\forall\,\xi\in C^{1}_{c}(B_{1}).

If one interprets (
1.3
) in a pointwise sense, then Brezis’ question has been answered in the negative by Villegas
[
18
]
: the quantity
f^{\prime}(u^{\star})
can oscillate.
More precisely, Villegas managed to construct a convex, increasing nonlinearity
f_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and a corresponding radial weak stable solution
u^{\star}_{0}
such that

0=\liminf_{r\to 0}r^{2}f^{\prime}_{0}(u^{\star}_{0}(r))<\limsup_{r\to 0}r^{2}f^{\prime}_{0}(u^{\star}_{0}(r))=\frac{(n-2)^{2}}{4}.

This suggests that Brezis’ question may only be true in a limsup sense; indeed, still in
[
18
]
, Villegas proved the validity of the corresponding limsup statement in the radial case. Our
Theorem

1.4
will, in particular, extend his result to the general case.

One should note that the pointwise form (
1.3
) is meaningful only for isolated singularities and, if interpreted with universal constants at definite scales, requires quantitative separation from the rest of the singular set. An intrinsic formulation, which remains meaningful for arbitrary singular sets and is not merely a technical weakening, is instead in terms of the scale-invariant mass of the stability potential at the singular point:

(1.6)

Let
u^{\star}
be singular at
x_{0}
. Is
\limsup_{r\to 0}r^{2}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\lambda^{\star}f^{\prime}(u^{\star})
bounded below by a positive dimensional constant?

Remark 1.3
.

The scale-invariant quantity

r^{2}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\lambda^{\star}f^{\prime}(u^{\star})

is always bounded above by a dimensional constant (at least away from
\partial\Omega
).
Indeed, fix
r>0
such that
B_{2r}(x_{0})\subset\Omega
. Testing the stability inequality (
1.2
) with a cutoff function
\xi
such that

\xi=1
on
B_{r}(x_{0})
,
\xi=0
on
\Omega\setminus B_{2r}(x_{0})
, and
|\nabla\xi|\leq C(n)r^{-1}
, yields

\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\lambda^{\star}f^{\prime}(u^{\star})\,dx\leq\int_{\Omega}\lambda^{\star}f^{\prime}(u^{\star})\,\xi^{2}\,dx\leq\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\xi|^{2}\,dx\leq C(n)r^{n-2}.

Equivalently,
r^{2}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\lambda^{\star}f^{\prime}(u^{\star})\leq C(n)
.
Thus (
1.6
) can be viewed as asking whether the
smallness
of this scale-invariant quantity forces regularity near
x_{0}
.

1.2.
First main result:
\varepsilon
-regularity and the Brezis question

Our first main result gives a complete answer to the scale-invariant question (
1.6
), proving that at any singular point the scale-invariant mass of
f^{\prime}(u^{\star})
cannot be arbitrarily small along all scales. As discussed in
Remark
1.6
, this result is optimal.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
u^{\star}
be the extremal solution of (
1.1
) at
\lambda=\lambda^{\star}
, and let
x_{0}\in\Omega
. There is a dimensional constant
C=C(n)>0
such that either
u^{\star}
is classical in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
, or

\frac{1}{C}\leq\limsup_{r\to 0}r^{2-n}\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\lambda^{\star}f^{\prime}(u^{\star})\,dx\leq C.

Theorem

1.4
is a consequence of the more general
Theorem

2.7
and
Corollary

2.9
below.

Remark 1.5
.

In the case of an isolated singularity
x_{0}\in\Omega
, using the universal upper bound to pass from balls to dyadic annuli,
Theorem

1.4
gives in particular a sequence
x_{k}\to x_{0}
such that
\lambda^{\star}f^{\prime}(u^{\star}(x_{k}))\geq C(n)|x_{k}-x_{0}|^{-2}
. This provides the limsup lower-bound conclusion in the original pointwise formulation of Brezis’ question (
1.3
).

Remark 1.6
.

A modification of the examples of Villegas (see Appendix
A.3
) shows that the “limsup” in
Theorem

1.4
cannot be improved to a “liminf”.
Indeed, we can construct an explicit nonlinearity
f_{1}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
(convex, increasing, positive) and a corresponding radial
singular
weak stable solution
u_{1}^{\star}
such that

\liminf_{r\to 0}r^{2-n}\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\lambda^{\star}f^{\prime}_{1}(u^{\star}_{1})=0,

so
Theorem

1.4
is optimal.

Theorem

1.4
is only the starting point of the present paper, which contains a number of new results.

-regularity is concerned:

•

The lower bound in

This was previously known only for specific model nonlinearities, while here

•

We work in the closed class of PDEs obtained as limits of stable solutions of

with

positive, increasing, and convex (possibly taking the value

This class, introduced in

and denoted here by

We prove that the regular set is

•

In

1.3.
Second main result: a dimensional bound on the singular set

With
\varepsilon
-regularity at hand, one can hope to estimate the size of the singular set for general nonlinearities.
Note that, in this context, no “dimension reduction principle” is available due to the lack of a suitable monotonicity formula.
Hence, knowing that no singular solutions exist below a certain dimension does
not
directly imply a corresponding codimension bound on
\Sigma(u^{\star})
.

Our strategy is the following: we show that higher integrability of
f^{\prime}(u^{\star})
implies quantitative bounds on the size of the singular set.
More precisely, as a direct application of our
\varepsilon
-regularity theorems, we prove that
L^{q}
bounds on
f^{\prime}(u^{\star})
yield
(n-2q)
-dimensional bounds on
\Sigma(u^{\star})
.
Thus, in order to bound
\Sigma(u^{\star})
, it suffices to establish higher integrability estimates on
f^{\prime}(u^{\star})
.
2
2

2

The idea of proving higher integrability estimates on
f^{\prime}(u^{\star})
in the stable regime is classical; see for instance
[
7
]
and references therein. However, most available results only concern specific classes of nonlinearities.

Let
T_{f}\in(-\infty,+\infty]
denote the blow-up level of
f
(see (
2.2
) below).
In
Theorem

2.18
, under mild asymptotic assumptions on
f
, we prove that

f^{\prime}(u^{\star})\in L^{q}_{{\text{{loc}}}}
for every
q<q_{f}
, where
q_{f}
is defined by

(1.7)

q_{f}:=1+2\liminf_{t\uparrow T_{f}}\frac{\log f(t)+\int^{t}\sqrt{\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(s)}{f(s)}}\,ds}{\log f^{\prime}(t)}

(since
\log f^{\prime}(t)\to+\infty
as
t\uparrow T_{f}
, the choice of the lower limit in the integral does not affect the value of
q_{f}
).
As a consequence, we obtain

(1.8)

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Sigma(u^{\star})\leq n-2q_{f}.

Although the expression (
1.7
) may appear somewhat unusual at first glance,
the quantity
q_{f}
arises naturally from the structure of the stability inequality underlying our estimates.
Remarkably, it also recovers the optimal exponent in all standard model nonlinearities, for instance for

f(t)=(1+t)^{p}\ (p>1),\qquad f(t)=e^{t},\qquad f(t)=(1-t)_{+}^{-p}\ (p>0),\quad\ldots

(see
Example

2.24
, Section
2.7
, and also
Remark

2.21
).
This agreement with the known sharp examples indicates that
q_{f}
is the natural quantity governing the singular set in general,
and leads to the following optimality conjecture.

Conjecture
.

Let
q_{f}
be defined by (
1.7
). Within the smooth asymptotic class of
Theorem

2.18
, the bound (
1.8
) cannot in general be improved.

We note that, at this level of generality, quantitative bounds on the singular set were not previously available:
earlier results either proved full regularity in low dimensions or obtained bounds on the singular set only for very specific model nonlinearities.

1.4.
Third main result: the two-dimensional case

In dimension two,
Corollary

2.14
below shows that the singular set is empty whenever our
\varepsilon
-regularity theorem applies. In particular, if
T_{f}<+\infty
, this follows under a MEMS-type growth assumption, which is sharp in dimensions
n\geq 3
(see
Example

2.12
). We can actually prove a much stronger conclusion: in the full local class
\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
introduced in (
2.5
), every local weak stable solution satisfies a universal interior Hessian estimate (previously known, by a different argument, in the radial case).

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
(u,f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
, with
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then

\|D^{2}u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1/2})}\leq C\,\|u\|_{L^{1}(B_{1})},

where
C
is universal.
In particular, if
\Sigma(u)\neq\emptyset
, then necessarily

T_{f}<+\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(T_{f})<+\infty.

Note that this theorem does not imply that the singular set is empty: indeed,
T_{f}
may be finite with
f(T_{f})<+\infty
, while
f^{\prime}(T_{f})=+\infty
. As discussed in
Remark

2.27
and
Remark

5.8
, this
C^{1,1}_{{\text{{loc}}}}
regularity is expected to be optimal.

The key new ingredient is a universal estimate for
f(u)
, proved by reducing suitable averages of
f(u)
to a delayed ODE.
Once
f(u)
is universally bounded, an intrinsic rescaling argument of obstacle-problem type near high-level contact points yields an
L^{\infty}
-based Hessian estimate, and a final interpolation step gives the
L^{1}
bound above.

2.1.
Local weak stable solutions

In order to study blow-up limits of stable solutions (where both the solution and the nonlinearity may vary),
we work in the closed class introduced in
[
3
]
.
Throughout, we allow nonlinearities that may take the value
+\infty

Consider the class of nonlinearities

(2.1)

\mathcal{N}:=\big\{f\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty]:f\text{ is lower semicontinuous, nondecreasing, and convex}\big\}.

For
f\in\mathcal{N}
we denote by
f^{\prime}
the
left derivative
, namely

f^{\prime}(t):=\sup_{\delta>0}\frac{f(t)-f(t-\delta)}{\delta}.

By convexity,
f^{\prime}
is defined everywhere on
\mathbb{R}
, is nondecreasing, and is left-continuous.

We also introduce the (possibly infinite) blow-up level of

(2.2)

T_{f}:=\inf\{s\in\mathbb{R}:f^{\prime}(s)=+\infty\}\in(-\infty,+\infty].

When
T_{f}<+\infty
, we will refer to

as a
singular
nonlinearity; otherwise (
T_{f}=+\infty
) we call it

.
We use the convention

f(T_{f}):=\lim_{t\uparrow T_{f}}f(t)\in[0,+\infty],

including the case
T_{f}=+\infty
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let

and

We say that

is a

and, for all
\xi\in C^{1}_{c}(\Omega)
, it holds

(2.4)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\xi\,dx=\int_{\Omega}f(u)\,\xi\,dx\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\xi|^{2}\,dx\geq\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u)\,\xi^{2}\,dx.

We denote the class of such pairs by

(2.5)

\mathcal{WS}(\Omega):=\big\{(u,f):\text{$u$ is a local weak stable solution of \eqref{eq:PDE} in }\Omega\big\}\subset H^{1}_{\text{{loc}}}(\Omega)\times\mathcal{N}.

possible: a modification of Villegas’ counterexamples shows that in fact
f^{\prime}(u)
may oscillate strongly near a singularity, both in the pointwise and in the integral sense. See
Remark

1.6
and Appendix
A.3
.

Combining
Theorem

2.7
with the universal upper bound obtained by testing stability with a cutoff
(the analogue of
Remark

1.3
in the local setting), we obtain:

Corollary 2.9
.

Let
(u,f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
,
T_{f}=+\infty
, and
x_{0}\in B_{1/2}\cap\Sigma(u)
.
Then

\varepsilon(n)\leq\limsup_{r\downarrow 0}r^{2-n}\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}f^{\prime}(u)\,dx\leq C(n).

Corollary

2.9
and
Remark

2.8
give a complete answer to the natural limsup versions of Brezis’ question (
1.6
), together with counterexamples to the corresponding liminf strengthening.

2.3.
\varepsilon
-regularity à la Brezis for singular nonlinearities

When
T_{f}<+\infty
, the smallness of the scale-invariant quantity
r^{2-n}\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}f^{\prime}(u)
is not, by itself, enough to force
u(x_{0})<T_{f}
.
This is because, in this case, at singular points the function
u
may remain bounded (and even be
C^{1}
),
so detecting singular behavior requires finer information.

Theorem 2.10
.

Given any
\sigma>0
, there exists
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(n,\sigma)>0
such that the following holds.
Assume
(u,f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
,
T_{f}<+\infty
, and
x_{0}\in B_{1/2}
.
If

\limsup_{r\downarrow 0}r^{2-n}\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}f^{\prime}(u)\,dx<\varepsilon,

then either
x_{0}\in\reg(u)
or

(2.8)

\limsup_{x\to x_{0}}\frac{T_{f}-u(x)}{|x-x_{0}|^{2-\sigma}}<+\infty.

Alternative (
2.8
) means that the graph of
u
in
B_{1}\times\mathbb{R}
touches the hyperplane
\{x_{n+1}=T_{f}\}
in a
C^{2-\sigma}
fashion at
x_{0}
.
When
f
blows up at least like some inverse power (MEMES-type behavior), this is incompatible with stability,
and we recover an analogue of
Theorem

2.7
:

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
T_{f}<+\infty
and assume

(2.9)

\limsup_{t\uparrow T_{f}}\,(T_{f}-t)^{p}f(t)>0\quad\text{ for some }p>0.

There exists
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(n,p)>0
such that, if
(u,f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
and
x_{0}\in B_{1/2}
satisfy

\limsup_{r\downarrow 0}r^{2-n}\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}f^{\prime}(u)\,dx<\varepsilon,

then
x_{0}\in\reg(u)
.

The following explicit example shows that assumption (
2.9
) is necessary.

Example 2.12
.

For
n\geq 3
and
r_{0}>0
small, consider the decreasing radial function

u(r):=1-\log(1/r)\,r^{2},\qquad r\in(0,r_{0}].

A direct computation gives

\Delta u(r)=u^{\prime\prime}(r)+\frac{n-1}{r}u^{\prime}(r)=n+2-2n\log(1/r).

Since
u\colon(0,r_{0}]\to(u(r_{0}),1]
is a
C^{1}
diffeomorphism for
r_{0}
small,
we can define implicitly a nonlinearity
f:(u(r_{0}),1]\to[0,+\infty)
via the relation
f(u)=-\Delta u
, that in this case takes the form

(2.10)

f\bigl(1-r^{2}\log(1/r)\bigr)=2n\log(1/r)-n-2.

One checks that
f
is increasing, convex, and blows up at
1
.
Moreover, differentiating (
2.10
) yields

f^{\prime}(u(r))=\frac{2n}{(2\log(1/r)-1)\,r^{2}},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad r^{2}f^{\prime}(u(r))=\frac{2n}{2\log(1/r)-1}\xrightarrow[r\downarrow 0]{}0.

In particular,

(1-t)\,f^{\prime}(t)=n+o(1)\qquad\text{as }t\uparrow 1,

and
f
blows up logarithmically as
t\uparrow 1
.
Consequently, for
r_{0}
small enough the stability inequality holds (since
f^{\prime}(u(r))=o(r^{-2})
is dominated by the Hardy weight),
and
0
is a singular point (since
u(0)=1=T_{f}
).
However, in this case,

\lim_{r\downarrow 0}r^{2-n}\int_{B_{r}}f^{\prime}(u)\,dx=0,

so the conclusion of
Corollary

2.11
does not hold.
We can note that indeed (
2.8
) holds for every
\sigma>0
(since
r^{\sigma}\log(1/r)\to 0
).

It is not a coincidence that this example requires
n\geq 3
: in
Section

2.6
below we show that
C^{1,1}
regularity holds in two dimensions, meaning that one can take
\sigma=0
. In particular we prove that, without any further assumption, if
f(T_{f})=+\infty
then
\Sigma(u)=\emptyset
.

2.4.
Higher integrability of
{f^{\prime}(u)}
and the size of the singular set

Since solutions of
-\Delta u=f(u)
lack a monotonicity formula suitable for dimension reduction,
our approach to bounding
\Sigma(u)
uses the following standard principle:

Higher integrability of
f^{\prime}(u)
forces quantitative smallness of the set of points

where the scale-invariant mass of
f^{\prime}(u)

stays bounded from below.

Concretely,

(2.11)

f^{\prime}(u)\in L^{q}_{\text{{loc}}},\quad q\geq 1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathcal{H}^{n-2q}(\Sigma(u))=0.

This implication follows by combining our
\varepsilon
-regularity statements (namely
Theorem

2.7
and
Corollary

2.11
)
with the following classical covering lemma (for the reader’s convenience, we provide a proof in Appendix
A.2
).

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
\varphi\in L^{q}_{\text{{loc}}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be nonnegative,
\alpha>0
,
q\geq 1
, and
E\subset B_{1}
be closed.
Assume

E\subset\bigg\{x\in B_{1}:\limsup_{r\downarrow 0}r^{\alpha-n}\int_{B_{r}(x)}\varphi\,dx\geq 1\bigg\}.

Then

\mathcal{H}^{n-\alpha q}(E)=0.

Here, if
n-\alpha q\leq 0
, the conclusion is understood as
E=\emptyset
.

Since stability provides an a priori
L^{1}
bound on
f^{\prime}(u)
in interior balls (simply test (
2.4
) with a cutoff),
we immediately deduce the following very general result.

Corollary 2.14
.

Let
(u,f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
and assume either

T_{f}=+\infty\qquad\text{or}\qquad T_{f}<+\infty\ \text{ and \eqref{eq:assMEMS} holds.}

Then

\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(\Sigma(u))=0.

Remark 2.15
.

The dimensional bound
n-2
is optimal as a uniform bound over the class
\mathcal{WS}
, as the example
f(u)=(1-u)^{-p}
for
p>0
small shows, see Example
2.24
(iii) below.

If we fix a certain
f\in\mathcal{N}
, we can improve
Corollary

2.14
by proving higher integrability estimates

f^{\prime}(u)\in L^{q}
for some
q>1
depending on
f
.
For this, we need to impose two assumptions:

\bullet

There exists
\gamma_{\circ}>0
such that

(2.12)

\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\frac{f^{\prime}(t)}{(\log t)^{2+\gamma_{\circ}}}>0.

(For
T_{f}<+\infty
this condition is automatically satisfied.)

\bullet

There exist
m_{f}>0
and a continuous increasing function
\omega\colon[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
, with
\omega(0)=0
,
such that
f(m_{f})>0
,
f^{\prime}(m_{f})>0
, and

(2.13)

\frac{f^{\prime}(s)}{f^{\prime}(t)}\leq\omega\bigg(\frac{f(s)}{f(t)}\bigg)\qquad\text{for all }m_{f}\leq s<t<T_{f}.

Remark 2.16
.

Assumption (
2.12
) is a very mild growth hypothesis, satisfied in all model cases of interest.

Concerning (
2.13
), since
f^{\prime}(s)\leq f^{\prime}(t)
for
s<t
,
this condition is only relevant in the regime where
f(s)/f(t)\ll 1
.
Based on this observation, we can show that (
2.13
) is robust under perturbations that preserve the growth of
f
. More precisely:

If
f
satisfies (
2.13
) and
g\sim f
, then also
g
satisfies (
2.13
) (possibly with a different
\omega
).

The notation
g\sim f
by definition means that for some constant
C=C(f,g)
it holds

(2.14)

\frac{1}{C}\,f(t)-C\leq g(t)\leq C\,f(t)+C\qquad\forall\,t\in\mathbb{R},

so in particular
T_{g}=T_{f}
. In order to check the claim we consider two cases:

(i) If
\lim_{t\uparrow T_{f}}f(t)<+\infty
, then
f(s)/f(t)\sim 1
for all
m_{f}\leq s<t<T_{f}
,
so (
2.13
) holds trivially.

(ii)
If instead
\lim_{t\uparrow T_{f}}f(t)=+\infty
, then (
2.14
) implies that

\frac{f(s)}{f(t)}\leq C^{\prime}\,\frac{g(s)}{g(t)}\qquad\text{whenever }\frac{g(s)}{g(t)}\ll 1,

and hence also
g
satisfies a condition of the form (
2.13
) (with a different
\omega
).

Remark 2.17
.

Assumption (
2.13
) is implied by the Crandall–Rabinowitz-type condition

(2.15)

\liminf_{t\uparrow T_{f}}\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(t)f(t)}{f^{\prime}(t)^{2}}>0,

whenever
f\in C^{2}
.
Indeed, if (
2.15
) holds, then there exist
\delta,m_{\delta}>0
such that

f^{\prime\prime}(t)f(t)\geq\delta f^{\prime}(t)^{2}
for all
t\geq m_{\delta}
. This implies that
f^{\prime}(t)\,f(t)^{-\delta}
is increasing on
[m_{\delta},T_{f})
, therefore (
2.13
) holds with
\omega(s)=s^{\delta}
.

Because of this observation, one can immediately check that (
2.13
)
is satisfied by all classical examples of nonlinearities.

We now define the exponent
q_{f}\geq 1
controlling the integrability of
f^{\prime}(u)
in the smooth asymptotic regime.

(2.16)

q_{f}:=1+2\liminf_{t\uparrow T_{f}}\frac{\log f(t)+\int^{t}_{m_{f}}\sqrt{{f^{\prime\prime}(s)}/{f(s)}}\,ds}{\log f^{\prime}(t)}.

Since
f^{\prime}(t)\to+\infty
as
t\uparrow T_{f}
, the precise choice of
m_{f}
does not affect
q_{f}
.
Although this quantity may look unusual at first glance, Section
2.7
shows that our formula is sharp in all model cases, providing strong evidence that it is optimal in general.

Theorem 2.18
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{N}
satisfy (
2.12
) and (
2.13
). Assume in addition that
f\in C^{2}((m_{f},T_{f}))
and that
f(t)\to+\infty
as
t\uparrow T_{f}
.
Then for all
(u,f)\in\mathcal{WS}(\Omega)
and all
q<q_{f}
,

f^{\prime}(u)\in L^{q}_{\text{{loc}}}(\Omega).

In particular,

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Sigma(u)\leq n-2q_{f},

provided

T_{f}=+\infty\qquad\text{or}\qquad T_{f}<+\infty\ \text{and \eqref{eq:assMEMS} holds.}

Combining
Theorem

2.18
with Lemma
A.2
, we get a general codimension bound.

Corollary 2.19
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and let
(u,f)\in\mathcal{WS}(\Omega)
, where
f\in\mathcal{N}
satisfies the hypotheses of
Theorem

2.18
. Assume in addition that

\lim_{t\uparrow T_{f}}\frac{\log f(t)}{\log f^{\prime}(t)}\quad\text{exists.}

(1)

If
T_{f}=+\infty
, then
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Sigma(u)\leq n-6
.

(2)

If
T_{f}<+\infty
and both
\int_{0}^{T_{f}}f(s)\,ds=+\infty
and (
2.9
) hold, then
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Sigma(u)\leq n-4
.

Here, if the right-hand side is negative, the conclusion means
\Sigma(u)=\emptyset
.

Finally, under a Crandall–Rabinowitz-type regime, one can obtain an explicit lower bound on
q_{f}
.

Corollary 2.20
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{N}
satisfy the hypotheses of
Theorem

2.18
, and assume in addition that
f\in C^{2}((-\infty,T_{f}))
. Define
\gamma_{\pm}\in[0,+\infty]
as

\gamma_{-}:=\liminf_{t\uparrow T_{f}}\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(t)f(t)}{f^{\prime}(t)^{2}},\qquad\gamma_{+}:=\limsup_{t\uparrow T_{f}}\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(t)f(t)}{f^{\prime}(t)^{2}}.

If
0<\gamma_{+}<+\infty
, then

(2.17)

q_{f}\geq 1+\frac{2}{\gamma_{+}}+\frac{2\sqrt{\gamma_{-}}}{\gamma_{+}}.

Remark 2.21
.

The exponent
q_{f}
in (
2.16
) is defined via a
\liminf
, and we do not expect that one can
replace it by a
\limsup
in the generality of
Theorem

2.18
.
The reason is the following: Proposition
4.3
provides a priori
L^{1}
bound on the function
f^{\prime}(u)\,f(u)^{2}\,H(u)
where
H
is a function depending on
f
(see Definition
4.2
), and then the
L^{q}
estimate ultimately relies on a
uniform
pointwise comparison for large
t
,
schematically of the form (cf. (
4.7
))

f^{\prime}(t)^{q}\;\lesssim\;f^{\prime}(t)\,f(t)^{2}\,H(t)\qquad\text{for all $t$ sufficiently close to $T_{f}$},

which is exactly the information captured by a
\liminf
in (
2.16
).
A
\limsup
would only control the right-hand side along a subsequence of
t
’s and would allow arbitrarily deep
downward oscillations at intermediate values, where the above comparison fails.

This “worst–case” nature is consistent with the oscillatory phenomena discovered by Villegas (see
[
18
]
and Appendix
A.3
below):
in dimensions where singular stable solutions exist (e.g.,
n\geq 10
), Villegas constructed convex increasing nonlinearities and
singular stable radial solutions for which the stability potential oscillates sharply across scales, in the sense that

r^{2}f^{\prime}(u(r))
can have
\liminf_{r\downarrow 0}r^{2}f^{\prime}(u(r))=0
while
\limsup_{r\downarrow 0}r^{2}f^{\prime}(u(r))
remains at the
Hardy threshold
(n-2)^{2}/4
.
Such examples show that, near a singularity, quantities governed by stability may be arbitrarily small along infinitely many
scales even if they are large along other scales. For general
f
we therefore expect that the admissible integrability range
for
f^{\prime}(u)
is dictated by the least favorable asymptotic regime of
f
, and hence that the
\liminf
in (
2.16
) is the
natural (and in this sense optimal) quantity.

Remark 2.22
.

If
f
grows very slowly (e.g.
f(t)\sim t\log t
as
t\to+\infty
), then (
2.12
) fails and our result does not apply.
On the other hand, in standard slowly growing regimes the problem is subcritical in all dimensions and
\Sigma(u)=\emptyset
follows from standard elliptic regularity and Sobolev embeddings.

Remark 2.23
.

We record that, in
Proposition

5.1
below, we additionally obtain for all
(u,f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
the universal local bound:

\int_{B_{1/2}}f^{\prime}(u)f(u)\,dx\leq C(n)\,\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})},

which was known only qualitatively (see for example
[
24
, Theorem 1]
).

2.5.
Application of our results to model cases

A useful quantity to discuss explicit examples with
f\in C^{2}
is the function

(2.18)

\tau_{f}(t):=\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(t)\,f(t)}{f^{\prime}(t)^{2}}.

If
T_{f}=+\infty
and
\tau_{\infty}:=\lim_{t\to+\infty}\tau_{f}(t)
exists, then necessarily
\tau_{\infty}\leq 1

(otherwise
f
would blow up in finite time).
Applying (
2.17
) with
\gamma_{-}=\gamma_{+}=\tau_{\infty}
yields
q_{f}\geq 5
and hence
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Sigma(u)\leq n-10
, the expected bound in view of
[
3
]
.

Let us discuss some models examples.

Example 2.24
.

Let
(u,f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
with
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(i)

If
f(t)=(1+t)^{p}
with
p>1
, then
\tau_{f}(t)\equiv(p-1)/p
, so

q_{f}=1+2\sqrt{\frac{p}{p-1}}+2\frac{p}{p-1},\qquad\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Sigma(u)\leq n-2-4\frac{p}{p-1}-4\sqrt{\frac{p}{p-1}}<n-10.

(ii)

If
f(t)=e^{t},\,e^{t^{2}},\,e^{e^{t}}
, etc., then
\tau_{f}(t)\to 1
as
t\to+\infty
, hence
q_{f}=5
and

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Sigma(u)\leq n-10.

(iii)

If
f(t)=(1-t)^{-p}
with
p>0
, then
T_{f}=1
and
\tau_{f}(t)\to(p+1)/p
as
t\uparrow 1
, so

q_{f}=1+2\sqrt{\frac{p}{p+1}}+2\frac{p}{p+1},

and therefore

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Sigma(u)\leq n-2-4\frac{p}{p+1}-4\sqrt{\frac{p}{p+1}}<n-2.

(iv)

If
f(t)=\exp((1-t)^{-p})
(
p>0
),
\exp\exp\!\big(\frac{1}{1-t}\big)
, etc., then
T_{f}=1
and
\tau_{f}(t)\to 1
as
t\uparrow 1
, hence
q_{f}=5
and

\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Sigma(u)\leq n-10.

Notice that the exponent
q_{f}
is

q_{f}=1+2\liminf_{t\uparrow T_{f}}\big(\alpha_{f}(t)+\beta_{f}(t)\big)\geq 1+2\liminf_{t\uparrow T_{f}}\alpha_{f}(t).

where we defined the two functions

\alpha_{f}(t):=\frac{\log f(t)}{\log f^{\prime}(t)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\beta_{f}(t):=\frac{\int^{t}_{m_{f}}\sqrt{{f^{\prime\prime}(s)}/{f(s)}}\,ds}{\log f^{\prime}(t)}.

Also,
if
g^{\prime}\sim f^{\prime}
and both
f
and
f^{\prime}
blow up as
t\to T_{f}
, then
\liminf_{t\uparrow T_{f}}\alpha_{f}(t)=\liminf_{t\uparrow T_{f}}\alpha_{g}(t)
. Based on this observation, the estimates above yield the following:

•

If
g^{\prime}\sim f^{\prime}
with
f
as in model cases (i) or (iii), then

q_{g}\geq 1+2\frac{p}{p-1},\qquad\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Sigma(u)\leq n-2-4\frac{p}{p-1}.

•

If
g^{\prime}\sim f^{\prime}
with
f
as in model cases (ii) or (iv), then

q_{g}\geq 3,\qquad\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Sigma(u)\leq n-6.

2.6.
The two-dimensional case

As anticipated, in dimension two we are able to get a very precise result.
The key is the following universal interior bound for
f(u)
, whose proof relies on a localized second order delayed-ODE argument for averages of
f(u)
.

Proposition 2.25
.

Assume
(u,f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
with
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then

(2.19)

f(u)\leq C\,\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\qquad\text{a.e. in }B_{3/4},

where
C
is a purely dimensional constant.

Previously, this result was known only in the radial case, see
[
10
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Theorem 2.26
.

Let
(u,f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
with
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then

\|D^{2}u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1/2})}\leq C\,\|u\|_{L^{1}(B_{1})},

where
C
is universal. Moreover,

(2.20)

either
\Sigma(u)=\emptyset
,

or
T_{f}<+\infty
and
f(T_{f})<+\infty
.

The following example shows that the
C^{1,1}
regularity is optimal in dimension two: indeed one can build examples with
f(T_{f})<+\infty
and
f^{\prime}(T_{f})=+\infty
. In particular,
\Sigma(u)\neq\emptyset
and
u\notin C^{2,\varepsilon}
for any
\varepsilon>0
. We explain in
Remark

5.8
below why we do not expect
C^{2}
regularity to hold in such generality, drawing a connection with the regularity theory of singular points in the obstacle problem.

Remark 2.27
.

For
n\geq 2
, consider the radial solution of

u^{\prime\prime}+(n-1)\tfrac{1}{r}u^{\prime}=-f(u),\qquad u(0)=1,\qquad u^{\prime}(0)=0,

with, for some
a>0
, and
t_{0}
close to 1,

f(t):=1-a\big(\log(\tfrac{1}{1-t})\big)^{-1}\quad\text{for all }t\in[t_{0},1).

One can check that, for
t_{0}
sufficiently close to
1
, it holds

f\in\mathcal{N},\quad f(1-)=1,\quad f^{\prime}(t)=\tfrac{a}{1-t}\big(\log(\tfrac{1}{1-t})\big)^{-2}\to+\infty\text{ as }t\uparrow 1,

so
T_{f}=1
. One checks that

u(r)=1-\big(\tfrac{1}{2n}-O(\log(1/r)^{-1})\big)r^{2},

hence
1-u(r)\sim r^{2}
, and therefore

f^{\prime}(u(r))\sim 4a\,r^{-2}\big(\log(r^{-2})\big)^{-2}\qquad\text{ as }r\downarrow 0.

By comparison with Hardy’s inequality,
(u,f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{r_{0}})
for
r_{0}
small enough and
a<1/4
(cf. (
1.5
) above).

2.7.
Comparison with the existing literature

The literature for (
2.3
) is abundant; we recall here only previous partial regularity results.
Most works are restricted to particular nonlinearities and determine a critical dimension
n_{f}
below which all stable solutions are smooth,
but
do not
provide a bound of the form
\dim_{\mathcal{H}}\Sigma(u)\leq n-n_{f}
(with the exception of a few specific model nonlinearities).

•

For
f(t)=e^{t}
(Liouville equation), Wang
[
19
,
21
]
proved an
\varepsilon
-regularity theorem and
L^{5-}
bounds for
e^{u}
, obtaining that the singular set has dimension at most
n-10
(this corresponds to our Example
2.24
(ii)).
Earlier partial regularity results were obtained by Da Lio for stationary solutions in dimension
3

[
5
]
.

•

For
f(t)=t^{p}
(Lane–Emden equation), partial regularity results (covering finite Morse index)
were proved by Pacard
[
15
]
, Dávila–Dupaigne–Farina
[
6
]
, and Wang
[
20
]
.

•

For
f(t)=(1-t)^{-p}
(MEMS-type), Mignot–Puel
[
12
]
identified the critical dimension in terms of
p
(the critical dimension coincides with the formula obtained in Example
2.24
(iii)).

•

For
f
satisfying a Crandall–Rabinowitz-type condition

f^{\prime\prime}(t)f(t)\geq\gamma_{-}\,f^{\prime}(t)^{2}\quad\text{for all $t$ close to $T_{f}$}

the best results in the regular case are due to Ye–Zhou
[
23
]
, which we recover and extend to the singular case
(see
Remark

4.4
).
For singular nonlinearities, we also recover the recent results of Bruera–Cabré
[
2
]

and the related results of Luo–Ye–Zhou
[
10
]
.

3.
\varepsilon
-regularity results

3.1.
Notation

Throughout this section, unless otherwise specified, balls are centered at the origin:

B_{r}:=B_{r}(0)
. (All statements are translation invariant, so centering at
0
causes no loss
of generality.)

We will use the averaged quantities

\avg_{r}(u):=\fint_{B_{r}}u\,dx,\qquad\osc_{r}(u):=\fint_{B_{r}}\bigl|u-\avg_{r}(u)\bigr|\,dx.

Note that
\osc_{r}(u)
is the
mean
oscillation, rather than an
L^{\infty}

oscillation. This is important since
u
may be unbounded.

For
M
,
r>0
, we also consider the auxiliary functional

(3.1)

Q^{M}_{r}(u):=\avg_{r}(u)+M\cdot\osc_{r}(u).

Heuristically,
Q_{r}^{M}(u)
is an “upper Campanato envelope” of
u
at scale
r
.

Given a function
w
and a scale
r>0
, we denote by

w_{r}(x):=w(rx)

its rescaling. Then, by a change of variables, one has

\avg_{r}(u)=\avg_{1}(u_{r}),\qquad\osc_{r}(u)=\osc_{1}(u_{r}).

Also, if
-\Delta u=f(u)
in
B_{1}
, then
-\Delta u_{r}=r^{2}f(u_{r})
in
B_{1/r}
, so the natural
rescaling of the nonlinearity is
f\mapsto r^{2}f
.

3.2.
Oscillation decay lemma

The next lemma is the key compactness input: it says that if the set where
f^{\prime}(u)
is large
has very small measure at a certain scale, then the mean oscillation of
u
decays going to a smaller scale. This is
a De Giorgi/Campanato-type mechanism adapted to the stability inequality.

Lemma 3.1
.

Given
\lambda>0
and
\theta\in(0,\tfrac{1}{3})
there is
\eta=\eta(n,\theta,\lambda)>0
with the following property.
If
(u,f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
and

(3.2)

|\{f^{\prime}(u)\geq\lambda\}\cap B_{1/2}|\leq\eta,

then

\osc_{\theta}(u)\leq C(n)\,(1+\lambda)^{n+1}\,\theta\cdot\osc_{1}(u).

Remark 3.2
.

A convenient sufficient condition for (
3.2
) is given by Markov/Chebyshev inequality:
if

\int_{B_{1/2}}f^{\prime}(u)\,dx\leq\eta\,\lambda,

then (
3.2
) holds.

Proof of
Lemma

3.1
.

We argue by contradiction. Then there exist
(u_{k},f_{k})\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
and
\eta_{k}\downarrow 0
such that

|\{f_{k}^{\prime}(u_{k})\geq\lambda\}\cap B_{1/2}|\leq\eta_{k}\quad\text{but}\quad\osc_{\theta}(u_{k})>C(1+\lambda)^{n+1}\theta\,\osc_{1}(u_{k}),

where
C
will be chosen (depending only on
n
) at the end.

Step 1: Normalization.

Set

a_{k}:=\avg_{1}(u_{k}),\qquad\omega_{k}:=\osc_{1}(u_{k}),\qquad v_{k}(x):=\frac{u_{k}(x)-a_{k}}{\omega_{k}}.

Then
\avg_{1}(v_{k})=0
and
\osc_{1}(v_{k})=1
, i.e.

(3.3)

\fint_{B_{1}}|v_{k}|=1,\qquad\osc_{\theta}(v_{k})>C(1+\lambda)^{n+1}\theta.

Define the rescaled nonlinearities

g_{k}(s):=\frac{f_{k}(a_{k}+\omega_{k}s)}{\omega_{k}},\qquad s\in\mathbb{R}.

This rescaling ensures
(v_{k},g_{k})\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
and a direct computation shows that the stability potential is unchanged:

g_{k}^{\prime}(v_{k})=f_{k}^{\prime}(u_{k})\quad\text{in }B_{1}.

Step 2: Limit problem.

Since
g_{k}(v_{k})\geq 0
, each
v_{k}
is superharmonic. Using compactness of superharmonic functions
(e.g.,
[
3
, Lemma A.1]
), after extracting a subsequence we have

v_{k}\to v_{*}\quad\text{in }L^{1}_{{\text{{loc}}}}(B_{1}),

and by
Theorem

2.6
there exists
g_{*}\in\mathcal{N}
such that
(v_{*},g_{*})\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
and

g_{k}\to g_{*}
locally uniformly below
\sup v_{*}
.

Because
\avg_{1}(v_{k})=0
and
\fint_{B_{1}}|v_{k}|=1
, passing to the limit gives

\avg_{1}(v_{*})=0,

\fint_{B_{1}}|v_{*}|\leq 1
and
\osc_{\theta}(v_{*})\geq C(1+\lambda)^{n+1}\theta
, hence
v_{*}
is not constant. Therefore we can use
(
2.6
) in
Theorem

2.6
to get that,
for a.e.
x\in B_{1}
and every fixed
\delta>0
,

(3.4)

g_{k}(v_{k}(x))\to g_{*}(v_{*}(x))\quad\text{ and }\quad g_{k}(v_{k}(x)-\delta)\to g_{*}(v_{*}(x)-\delta)\quad\text{ as }k\to+\infty.

Step 3:
g_{*}
is
\lambda
-Lipschitz.

Fix
\delta>0
. Since
g_{k}
is convex, for every
t
one has

\frac{g_{k}(t)-g_{k}(t-\delta)}{\delta}\leq g_{k}^{\prime}(t),

hence

\{g_{k}(v_{k})-g_{k}(v_{k}-\delta)\geq\lambda\delta\}\cap B_{1/2}\subset\{g_{k}^{\prime}(v_{k})\geq\lambda\}\cap B_{1/2}=\{f_{k}^{\prime}(u_{k})\geq\lambda\}\cap B_{1/2}.

Recalling (
3.2
), this implies

|\{g_{k}(v_{k})-g_{k}(v_{k}-\delta)\geq\lambda\delta\}\cap B_{1/2}|\leq\eta_{k}\to 0,

that together with (
3.4
) gives

g_{*}(v_{*}(x))-g_{*}(v_{*}(x)-\delta)\leq\lambda\delta\quad\text{for a.e.\ }x\in B_{1/2}.

Letting
\delta\downarrow 0
and recalling that
g_{*}^{\prime}
denotes the left derivative, we obtain

g_{*}^{\prime}(v_{*}(x))\leq\lambda\quad\text{for a.e.\ }x\in B_{1/2}.

Step 4: Elliptic bootstrap.

By the mean value inequality for superharmonic functions, there exists
C_{0}=C_{0}(n)
such that

v_{*}\geq-C_{0}\quad\text{in }B_{1/2}.

Since
g_{*}
is nondecreasing, this gives
g_{*}(v_{*})\geq g_{*}(-C_{0})
in
B_{1/2}
.
Choose
\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{3/4})
with
\xi\equiv 1
on
B_{1/2}
and
0\leq\xi\leq 1
.
Testing
-\Delta v_{*}=g_{*}(v_{*})
against
\xi
yields

g_{*}(-C_{0})\,|B_{1/2}|\leq\int_{B_{1/2}}g_{*}(v_{*})\,dx\leq\int g_{*}(v_{*})\,\xi\,dx=-\int(\Delta v_{*})\,\xi\,dx=-\int v_{*}\,\Delta\xi\,dx\leq C_{1}(n),

where we used
\fint_{B_{1}}|v_{*}|=1
and the boundedness of
\Delta\xi
.
Therefore,

0\leq g_{*}(-C_{0})\leq C_{1}(n).

On the other hand, since
g_{*}^{\prime}
is bounded by
\lambda
a.e. on the range of
v_{*}
in
B_{1/2}
(recall Step 3),
it follows that
g_{*}
is
\lambda
-Lipschitz there, hence for
x\in B_{1/2}
,

0\leq g_{*}(v_{*}(x))\leq g_{*}(-C_{0})+\lambda\,(v_{*}(x)+C_{0})\leq C(n)(1+\lambda)\,(1+|v_{*}(x)|).

As a consequence,

(3.5)

\|\Delta v_{*}\|_{L^{p}(B_{r})}=\|g_{*}(v_{*})\|_{L^{p}(B_{r})}\leq C(n,p)\,(1+\lambda)\,\bigl(1+\|v_{*}\|_{L^{p}(B_{r})}\bigr),\qquad\forall\,r\in[\tfrac{1}{3},\tfrac{1}{2}],\ \forall p\geq 1.

We now bootstrap integrability. Fix equally spaced radii

\tfrac{1}{2}=r_{0}>r_{1}>\cdots>r_{n+1}=\tfrac{1}{3}.

Start from
v_{*}\in L^{1}(B_{1/2})
.
By the
L^{1}
Poisson estimate, for any
q\in\bigl(1,\frac{n}{n-2}\bigr)
, with the usual convention that
\frac{n}{n-2}=+\infty
when
n=2
,

\|v_{*}\|_{L^{q}(B_{r_{1}})}\leq C(n,q)\Big(\|\Delta v_{*}\|_{L^{1}(B_{1/2})}+\|v_{*}\|_{L^{1}(B_{1/2})}\Big)\leq C(n,q)(1+\lambda)\Big(1+\|v_{*}\|_{L^{1}(B_{1/2})}\Big),

where we used (
3.5
) with
p=1
.

Now set
p_{1}:=q
and, as long as
2p_{i}<n
, define for
i\geq 1
the Sobolev exponents

p_{i+1}:=\frac{np_{i}}{n-2p_{i}}\qquad\implies\qquad p_{i+1}=\frac{np_{1}}{n-2ip_{1}}.

Interior elliptic regularity and Sobolev embedding give, for
i\geq 1
,

\|v_{*}\|_{L^{p_{i+1}}(B_{r_{i+1}})}\leq C(n,p_{i})\|v_{*}\|_{W^{2,p_{i}}(B_{r_{i+1}})}\leq C(n,p_{i})\Big(\|\Delta v_{*}\|_{L^{p_{i}}(B_{r_{i}})}+\|v_{*}\|_{L^{p_{i}}(B_{r_{i}})}\Big).

Using (
3.5
) to bound
\|\Delta v_{*}\|_{L^{p_{i}}}
in terms of
\|v_{*}\|_{L^{p_{i}}}
,
we obtain

\|v_{*}\|_{L^{p_{i+1}}(B_{r_{i+1}})}\leq C(n,p_{i})\|v_{*}\|_{W^{2,p_{i}}(B_{r_{i+1}})}\leq C(n,p_{i})\,(1+\lambda)\,\bigl(1+\|v_{*}\|_{L^{p_{i}}(B_{r_{i}})}\bigr).

Iterating this bound until
i=m
with
p_{m}<n/2<p_{m+1}
, it follows that

\|v_{*}\|_{W^{2,p_{m+1}}(B_{r_{m+2}})}\leq C(n)(1+\lambda)^{m+2}\,\bigl(1+\|v_{*}\|_{L^{1}(B_{1/2})}\bigr)\leq C(n)\,(1+\lambda)^{m+2},

hence
v_{*}
becomes bounded in
B_{r_{m+2}}
.
Plugging boundedness back into (
3.5
) yields
\Delta v_{*}\in L^{\infty}(B_{r_{m+2}})
.
Therefore elliptic estimates imply
v_{*}\in W^{2,p}(B_{r_{m+3}})
for every
p<+\infty
, and choosing
p>n

gives, by Sobolev–Morrey,

\|\nabla v_{*}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r_{m+3}})}\leq C(n)\,(1+\lambda)^{m+3}.

Noticing that, by choosing
p_{1}
very close to
\frac{n}{n-2}
(or arbitrarily large if
n=2
), the exponent
m+2
is bounded by
n
, we conclude that

\|\nabla v_{*}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1/3})}\leq C(n)\,(1+\lambda)^{n+1}.

Finally, since

\osc_{\theta}(v_{*})\leq\fint_{B_{\theta}}|v_{*}-\avg_{\theta}(v_{*})|\leq\sup_{B_{\theta}}|v_{*}-\avg_{\theta}(v_{*})|\leq\theta\,\|\nabla v_{*}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1/3})},

we conclude

\osc_{\theta}(v_{*})\leq\bar{C}(n)(1+\lambda)^{n+1}\,\theta.

This contradicts (
3.3
) provided
C
is chosen larger than
\bar{C}(n)
,
and completes the proof.
∎

3.3.
A Campanato-type criterion in terms of
Q_{r}^{M}

The next corollary packages the previous decay lemma into a convenient “self-propagating ” criterion:
if
f^{\prime}
is small at the level
Q_{r}^{M}(u)
, then
u
is regular at the center and enjoys a
quantitative Hölder estimate.

Corollary 3.3
.

For any
\lambda>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, there is
M=M(n,\alpha,\lambda)>0
with the following property.
If
(u,f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
and

(3.6)

r^{2}f^{\prime}\big(\avg_{r}(u)+M\osc_{r}(u)\big)<\lambda\quad\text{ for some }r\in(0,1),

then
0\in\reg(u)
. More precisely,
f^{\prime}(u(0))<\lambda/r^{2}
and

(3.7)

\fint_{B_{\rho}}|u-u(0)|\leq C\left(\frac{\rho}{r}\right)^{\alpha}\osc_{r}(u)\quad\text{ for all }\rho\in(0,r),

for some constant
C=C(n,\alpha,\lambda)
.

Remark 3.4
.

Condition (
3.6
) is open with respect to small translations: it remains true for

u(\cdot+z)
provided
|z|\ll r
. This is crucial to guarantee that the regular set will be open.

Proof of
Corollary

3.3
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Choose
\theta\in(0,1/3)
(depending on
n,\lambda,\alpha
) so small that

C(n)(1+\lambda)^{n+1}\,\theta\leq\theta^{\alpha}.

Let
\eta
be the constant given by
Lemma

3.1
with these choices of
n,\theta,\lambda
.

Step 1: Normalization.

Consider
(v,g)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
defined by

v(x):=\frac{u(rx)-\avg_{r}(u)}{\osc_{r}(u)},\qquad g(s):=r^{2}\frac{f(\avg_{r}(u)+s\cdot\osc_{r}(u))}{\osc_{r}(u)}.

Then
g^{\prime}(v)=r^{2}f^{\prime}(\avg_{r}(u)+v\,\osc_{r}(u))
.

Recalling notation (
3.1
), assumption (
3.6
) says
r^{2}f^{\prime}(Q_{r}^{M}(u))<\lambda
. Since
f^{\prime}
is nondecreasing,
if
v(x)<M
one has
\avg_{r}(u)+v(x)\,\osc_{r}(u)<Q_{r}^{M}(u)
and therefore
g^{\prime}(v(x))<\lambda
.
Hence,

\{g^{\prime}(v)\geq\lambda\}\subset\{v\geq M\}.

By Markov’s inequality and the normalization
\osc_{1}(v)=1
,

|\{g^{\prime}(v)\geq\lambda\}|\leq|\{v\geq M\}|\leq\frac{1}{M}\int_{B_{1}}|v|=\frac{|B_{1}|}{M}.

Thus
Lemma

3.1
applies provided
M\geq|B_{1}|/\eta
, and yields

\osc_{\theta}(v)\leq\theta^{\alpha}\osc_{1}(v)=\theta^{\alpha}.

Rescaling back, this becomes

\osc_{\theta r}(u)\leq\theta^{\alpha}\osc_{r}(u).

Step 2: Decay and control
Q_{r}^{M}
.

Repeating the same argument at the smaller scales (using the fact that
Q^{M}
is designed to
propagate across scales), we obtain for all
j\geq 0
,

\osc_{\theta^{j+1}r}(u)\leq\theta^{\alpha}\osc_{\theta^{j}r}(u),\qquad\text{hence}\qquad\osc_{\theta^{j}r}(u)\leq\theta^{\alpha j}\osc_{r}(u).

Moreover, for the averages we have

|\avg_{\theta r}(u)-\avg_{r}(u)|\leq\fint_{B_{\theta r}}|u-\avg_{r}(u)|\leq\frac{|B_{r}|}{|B_{\theta r}|}\fint_{B_{r}}|u-\avg_{r}(u)|=\theta^{-n}\osc_{r}(u).

Therefore,

\displaystyle Q^{M}_{\theta r}(u)

\displaystyle=\avg_{\theta r}(u)+M\osc_{\theta r}(u)

\displaystyle\leq\avg_{r}(u)+\theta^{-n}\osc_{r}(u)+M\theta^{\alpha}\osc_{r}(u)\leq Q^{M}_{r}(u),

provided
M
was chosen so big that
\theta^{-n}+M\theta^{\alpha}\leq M
.

Since
u(0)=\lim_{\rho\downarrow 0}\avg_{\rho}(u)
and
\avg_{\theta^{j}r}(u)\leq Q^{M}_{\theta^{j}r}(u)\leq Q^{M}_{r}(u)
,
we get
u(0)\leq Q^{M}_{r}(u)
, hence by monotonicity of
f^{\prime}
,

f^{\prime}(u(0))\leq f^{\prime}(Q^{M}_{r}(u))<\frac{\lambda}{r^{2}}.

Step 3: Campanato estimate.

The geometric decay of
\osc_{\theta^{j}r}(u)
implies the Campanato estimate (
3.7
):
for
\rho\in(0,r)
choose
j
such that
\theta^{j+1}r<\rho\leq\theta^{j}r
. Then

\fint_{B_{\rho}}|u-u(0)|\leq C\fint_{B_{\theta^{j}r}}|u-u(0)|\leq C\osc_{\theta^{j}r}(u)\leq C\theta^{\alpha j}\osc_{r}(u)\leq C\left(\frac{\rho}{r}\right)^{\alpha}\osc_{r}(u).

This proves (
3.7
) and completes the proof.
∎

3.4.
Proofs of
Theorem

2.4
and
Theorem

2.7

We first prove that the regular set is always open in the class
\mathcal{WS}
.

Proof of
Theorem

2.4
.

Fix
(u,f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
and assume
f^{\prime}(u(0))<+\infty
. Our aim is to show that
f^{\prime}(u)<+\infty
in a neighbourhood of the origin.

By monotonicity of
f^{\prime}
,
we can choose some
\delta>0
so that

\lambda:=1+f^{\prime}(u(0)+2\delta)<+\infty.

Let
M=M(n,1/2,\lambda)
be the constant from
Corollary

3.3
with
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}
and this choice of
\lambda
.

By the mean value property for superharmonic functions,
\avg_{r}(u)\uparrow u(0)
as
r\downarrow 0
.
Thus, to guarantee (
3.6
) for small
r
, it is enough to know that

(3.8)

\osc_{r}(u)\to 0\quad\text{as }r\downarrow 0.

Indeed, if (
3.8
) holds, then for
r
small enough we have

Q^{M}_{r}(u)=\avg_{r}(u)+M\osc_{r}(u)<u(0)+\delta,

so
f^{\prime}(Q^{M}_{r}(u))\leq f^{\prime}(u(0)+\delta)<\lambda
, hence (since
r<1
)

r^{2}f^{\prime}(Q^{M}_{r}(u))<\lambda,

and
Corollary

3.3
implies
0\in\reg(u)
and provides a Hölder estimate at the origin.
This also gives openness of
\reg(u)
by translation (see
Remark

3.4
),
and
C^{2,\alpha}
regularity for
\alpha<1
inside
\reg(u)
then follows by standard elliptic regularity.

It now remains to prove (
3.8
).
To this aim, we show that blow-ups of
u
at
x=0
converge to a constant, which is equivalent to the vanishing
of the mean oscillation.

Let
r_{j}\downarrow 0
and set
u_{j}:=u_{r_{j}}=u(r_{j}\,\cdot)
. Given
R>0
,
if
j
is large enough so that

Rr_{j}\leq 1/2
we have

u_{j}\geq\inf_{B_{1/2}}u\quad\text{in }B_{R}.

Therefore, since
u
is superharmonic (and therefore
u_{-}
is subharmonic),
for
j
large (depending on
R
) we have

\displaystyle\fint_{B_{R}}|u_{j}|

\displaystyle\leq\fint_{B_{R}}u_{j}-\inf_{B_{1/2}}u+\big|\inf_{B_{1/2}}u\big|

\displaystyle=\fint_{B_{R}}u_{j}+2\big(\inf_{B_{1/2}}u\big)_{-}

(3.9)

\displaystyle\leq u(0)+2\sup_{B_{1/2}}u_{-}\leq u(0)+C(n)\|u\|_{L^{1}(B_{1})}.

By compactness of superharmonic functions, passing to a diagonal subsequence we may assume

u_{j}\to v
in
L^{1}_{{\text{{loc}}}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and a.e.
By
Theorem

2.6
,
(u_{j},r_{j}^{2}f)\to(v,g)\in\mathcal{WS}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
But for every fixed
t<\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v
, we have
r_{j}^{2}f(t)\to 0
, hence necessarily
g\equiv 0

on
(-\infty,\sup v)
, and therefore
v
is harmonic in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Finally, (
3.4
) implies that
\fint_{B_{R}}|v|\leq C
for every
R>0
, therefore
v
must be constant by Liouville’s theorem.
This proves that every blow-up limit is constant, which is equivalent to (
3.8
).
∎

We turn to the proof of our
\varepsilon
-regularity result for regular nonlinearities, i.e.,
T_{f}=+\infty
.

Proof of
Theorem

2.7
.

Assume (
2.7
) holds at
x_{0}=0
and let
\varepsilon>0
be chosen (depending only on
n
) so small
that
Lemma

3.1
applies with
\lambda=1
and yields a contraction factor
\theta^{\alpha}
.

Since

\limsup_{r\to 0}r^{2-n}\int_{B_{r}}f^{\prime}(u)<\varepsilon,

there exists
r_{0}>0
such that for all
r\in(0,r_{0})
,

(3.10)

r^{2-n}\int_{B_{r}}f^{\prime}(u)\,dx<\varepsilon.

Fix
r\in(0,r_{0})
. For the rescaled solution
u_{r}=u(r\,\cdot)
we have
-\Delta u_{r}=r^{2}f(u_{r})
, and we see that

\int_{B_{1/2}}(r^{2}f^{\prime})\bigl(u_{r}\bigr)\,dx=r^{2-n}\int_{B_{r/2}}f^{\prime}(u)\,dx\leq r^{2-n}\int_{B_{r}}f^{\prime}(u)\,dx<\varepsilon.

By
Remark

3.2
, choosing
\varepsilon
small enough guarantees that
(u_{r},r^{2}f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
satisfies (
3.2
) with
\lambda=1
. Hence
Lemma

3.1
yields, for a fixed
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

\osc_{\theta}(u_{r})\leq\theta^{\alpha}\osc_{1}(u_{r}),

which rescales to

\osc_{\theta r}(u)\leq\theta^{\alpha}\osc_{r}(u).

Because the smallness assumption (
3.10
) holds for
all
radii in
(0,r_{0})
, we can iterate
and obtain

\osc_{\theta^{j+1}r}(u)\leq\theta^{\alpha}\osc_{\theta^{j}r}(u)\qquad\forall\,j\geq 0.

Now take
M
as in
Corollary

3.3
(with
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}
and
\lambda=1
). As in the proof of

Corollary

3.3
, the oscillation decay implies

Q^{M}_{\theta s}(u)\leq Q^{M}_{s}(u)\qquad\text{for }s\in\{r,\theta r,\theta^{2}r,\dots\}.

Therefore, for all
N\geq 0
,

(\theta^{N}r)^{2}\,f^{\prime}\bigl(Q^{M}_{\theta^{N}r}(u)\bigr)\leq\theta^{2N}r^{2}\,f^{\prime}\bigl(Q^{M}_{r}(u)\bigr).

Since
T_{f}=+\infty
, the number
f^{\prime}(Q^{M}_{r}(u))
is finite. Choose
N
so large that

\theta^{2N}r^{2}f^{\prime}(Q^{M}_{r}(u))<1
. Then

(\theta^{N}r)^{2}f^{\prime}\bigl(Q^{M}_{\theta^{N}r}(u)\bigr)<1,

so
Corollary

3.3
applies at scale
\theta^{N}r
and yields
0\in\reg(u)
.
∎

3.5.
Proofs of
Theorem

2.10
and
Corollary

2.11

We now discuss the “singular” case
T_{f}<+\infty
. The key challenge now is that at a singular point

u
may still remain bounded (even
C^{1,\alpha}
), so smallness of the scale-invariant mass of
f^{\prime}(u)

does not force regularity; instead, we first obtain a quantitative decay for
T_{f}-u
.

Lemma 3.5
.

For every
\sigma>0
there are
\varepsilon,\rho\in(0,1)
with the following property.
If
(u,f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
,
0\in\Sigma(u)
, and

(3.11)

r^{2-n}\int_{B_{r}}f^{\prime}(u)\,dx<\varepsilon\quad\text{ for all }r\in(0,1),

then

\sup_{B_{\rho}}(T_{f}-u)\leq\rho^{2-\sigma}\,\sup_{B_{1}}(T_{f}-u).

Proof.

Assume by contradiction that there exist
(u_{k},f_{k})\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
and
\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0
such that

r^{2-n}\int_{B_{r}}f_{k}^{\prime}(u_{k})\,dx<\varepsilon_{k}\quad\forall\,r\in(0,1),

but for some
\rho\in(0,1)
(to be chosen depending only on
n,\sigma
) one has

(3.12)

\sup_{B_{\rho}}(T_{f_{k}}-u_{k})>\rho^{2-\sigma}\,\sup_{B_{1}}(T_{f_{k}}-u_{k}).

Set
A_{k}:=\sup_{B_{1}}(T_{f_{k}}-u_{k})>0
and define the normalized functions

v_{k}(x):=\frac{u_{k}(x)-T_{f_{k}}}{A_{k}}\in[-1,0],\qquad w_{k}:=-v_{k}=\frac{T_{f_{k}}-u_{k}}{A_{k}}\in[0,1].

Then
w_{k}
is nonnegative and subharmonic (since
\Delta(T_{f_{k}}-u_{k})=f_{k}(u_{k})\geq 0
), hence it satisfies the
mean value inequality.

Also
(v_{k},g_{k})\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
with

g_{k}(s):=\frac{f_{k}(T_{f_{k}}+A_{k}s)}{A_{k}}\in\mathcal{N}.

Indeed
-\Delta v_{k}=g_{k}(v_{k})
and
g_{k}^{\prime}(v_{k})=f_{k}^{\prime}(u_{k})
a.e. Rescaling, for each
r\in(0,1)
,

((v_{k})_{r},r^{2}g_{k})\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
and

Lemma 4.1

) and then combining (
4.10
) with (
4.14
) yields

c\int_{\{u\geq\kappa\}}f^{\prime}(u)H_{2\nu}(u)\zeta^{2}\,dx-C\int f(u)\zeta^{2}\leq C\varepsilon\int_{\{u\geq\kappa\}}f^{\prime}(u)H_{2\nu}(u)\zeta^{2}\,dx+C_{\gamma,\varepsilon}.

Choosing
\varepsilon
so that
C\varepsilon\leq c/2
, we reabsorb the main term and obtain (
4.7
) (recall that, up to the harmless constant
f(\kappa)^{-2}
, one has
H_{2\nu}=f^{2}H_{2\mu\sqrt{\tau}}
by the
H
-identities).

Step 5: Proof of claim (
4.13
).

We use the identity

2\Gamma_{f}(t)=\int_{\kappa}^{t}\big[(f^{\prime})^{2}\big]^{\prime}f^{2}=f^{\prime}(t)^{2}f(t)^{2}-f^{\prime}(\kappa)^{2}f(\kappa)^{2}-\int_{\kappa}^{t}(f^{\prime})^{2}(f^{2})^{\prime}.

Fix
t\in(\kappa,T_{f})
and let
s\in(\kappa,t)
to be specified. Since
f^{\prime}
is nondecreasing,

\displaystyle\int_{\kappa}^{t}(f^{\prime})^{2}(f^{2})^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\kappa}^{s}(f^{\prime})^{2}(f^{2})^{\prime}+\int_{s}^{t}(f^{\prime})^{2}(f^{2})^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq f^{\prime}(s)^{2}\int_{\kappa}^{s}(f^{2})^{\prime}+f^{\prime}(t)^{2}\int_{s}^{t}(f^{2})^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq f^{\prime}(s)^{2}f(s)^{2}+f^{\prime}(t)^{2}\big(f(t)^{2}-f(s)^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(t)^{2}f(t)^{2}\bigg(1-\frac{f(s)^{2}}{f(t)^{2}}+\frac{f^{\prime}(s)^{2}f(s)^{2}}{f^{\prime}(t)^{2}f(t)^{2}}\bigg).

Choose
\varepsilon_{\circ}\in(0,\varepsilon_{\omega})
such that
\omega(\varepsilon_{\circ})\leq 1/2
.
If
\varepsilon_{\circ}f(t)\leq f(\kappa)
, then

\Gamma_{f}(t)\geq f(\kappa)^{2}\int_{\kappa}^{t}f^{\prime\prime}f^{\prime}\,ds=\frac{f(\kappa)^{2}}{2}\big(f^{\prime}(t)^{2}-f^{\prime}(\kappa)^{2}\big),

and (
4.13
) follows, with a smaller
c_{\circ}
, from
f(t)\leq f(\kappa)/\varepsilon_{\circ}
.
We may therefore assume that
\varepsilon_{\circ}f(t)>f(\kappa)
. By continuity and monotonicity of
f
on
(\kappa,T_{f})
, we can choose
s=s(t)\in(\kappa,t)
such that

\frac{f(s)}{f(t)}=\varepsilon_{\circ}.

Then, by (
2.13
),

\frac{f^{\prime}(s)}{f^{\prime}(t)}\leq\omega\Big(\frac{f(s)}{f(t)}\Big)=\omega(\varepsilon_{\circ})\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Therefore

1-\frac{f(s)^{2}}{f(t)^{2}}+\frac{f^{\prime}(s)^{2}f(s)^{2}}{f^{\prime}(t)^{2}f(t)^{2}}\leq 1-\varepsilon_{\circ}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon_{\circ}^{2}=1-\frac{3}{4}\,\varepsilon_{\circ}^{2}.

Hence

\int_{\kappa}^{t}(f^{\prime})^{2}(f^{2})^{\prime}\leq\Big(1-\tfrac{3}{4}\varepsilon_{\circ}^{2}\Big)f^{\prime}(t)^{2}f(t)^{2}.

Plugging back gives

2\Gamma_{f}(t)\geq\frac{3}{4}\varepsilon_{\circ}^{2}\,f^{\prime}(t)^{2}f(t)^{2}-f^{\prime}(\kappa)^{2}f(\kappa)^{2},

so (
4.13
) holds with
c_{\circ}=\frac{3}{8}\,\varepsilon_{\circ}^{2}
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{N}\cap C^{2}
and assume that

\tau(t):=\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(t)f(t)}{f^{\prime}(t)^{2}}\geq\gamma_{-}\quad\text{ for all }\kappa<t<T_{f},

for some constant
\gamma_{-}\geq 0,

so that

H_{2\mu\sqrt{\tau}}(u)\geq H_{2\mu\sqrt{\gamma_{-}}}(u)=\bigg(\frac{f(u)}{f(\kappa)}\bigg)^{2\mu\sqrt{\gamma_{-}}}.

Since
\mu
can be chosen arbitrarily close to
1
, (

) yields

\int_{B_{1/2}}f^{\prime}(u)\,f(u)^{p}\leq C+C\int_{B_{1}}|u|<+\infty\qquad\text{for all }1\leq p<2+2\sqrt{\gamma_{-}}.

Since
-\Delta u=f(u)
and
p\geq 1
, a direct computation gives

\displaystyle-\Delta\big(f(u)^{p}\big)

\displaystyle=p\,f^{\prime}(u)\,f(u)^{p}-p(p-1)\,f(u)^{p-2}\,f^{\prime}(u)^{2}\,|\nabla u|^{2}-p\,f(u)^{p-1}\,f^{\prime\prime}(u)\,|\nabla u|^{2}\leq p\,f^{\prime}(u)\,f(u)^{p},

Since
f^{\prime}(u)\,f(u)^{p}\in L^{1}(B_{1/2})
,
this implies that
\Delta\big(f(u)^{p}\big)
is a measure with finite mass in
B_{2/5}
, and by
interior regularity estimates we deduce that
f(u)\in L^{r}(B_{1/3})
for all

r<\frac{2n}{n-2}(1+\sqrt{\gamma_{-}}).

Thus, recalling that
f(u)=-\Delta u
, elliptic regularity provides an Hölder bound on

inside
B_{1/4}
as soon as

(4.17)

n<6+4\sqrt{\gamma_{-}}.

In the singular case
T_{f}<+\infty
, Hölder continuity is not enough to exclude the presence of singular points (cf
Remark

2.5
), so one argues as in
[
2
]
: the function
F(u):=\int^{u}_{0}f(s)\,ds
solves

. If we additionally assume the non-integrability condition

, this gives that no singular solution can exist if

, that is

(4.18)

n<4+2\sqrt{\gamma_{-}}.

4.4.
Approximation by classical solutions

We next recall a known sub/supersolution procedure that will be used to approximate possibly singular stable solutions for a certain
f\in\mathcal{N}
by bounded stable solutions for the slightly smaller nonlinearity
f_{\varepsilon}:=(1-\varepsilon)f
. While more direct approximation schemes exist (cf.
[
3
, Proposition 4.2]
), the procedure presented here is necessary in order not to disrupt our asymptotic quantity, namely keeping
q_{f_{\varepsilon}}=q_{f}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{N}
and
V\in H^{1}(B_{1})
with
f(V)\in L^{1}(B_{1})
.
Assume that
V
is a weak supersolution:

\int_{B_{1}}\nabla V\cdot\nabla\xi\,dx\geq\int_{B_{1}}f(V)\,\xi\,dx\qquad\forall\,\xi\in C^{1}_{c}(B_{1}),\ \xi\geq 0.

Then there exists
w\in H^{1}(B_{1})
such that
w=V
on
\partial B_{1}
,
w\leq V
a.e. in
B_{1}
, and

\int_{B_{1}}\nabla w\cdot\nabla\xi\,dx=\int_{B_{1}}f(w)\,\xi\,dx\qquad\forall\,\xi\in C^{1}_{c}(B_{1}).

Moreover,

\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}\leq 4\,\|\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}.

Proof.

Let
w_{0}
be the harmonic replacement of
V
in
B_{1}
(i.e.
-\Delta w_{0}=0
in
B_{1}
and
w_{0}=V
on
\partial B_{1}
).
Define iteratively
w_{k+1}
as the weak solution of

-\Delta w_{k+1}=f(w_{k})\quad\text{in }B_{1},\qquad w_{k+1}=V\ \text{on }\partial B_{1}.

By comparison and monotonicity of
f
we have

w_{0}\leq w_{k}\leq w_{k+1}\leq V\qquad\text{a.e. in }B_{1}.

Testing the equation for
w_{k}
with
w_{k}-w_{0}\geq 0
and using
w_{k}\leq V
yields an energy bound

\|\nabla w_{k}\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}\leq 2\|\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}+2\|\nabla w_{0}\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}\leq 4\|\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}.

Since the sequence
\{w_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is monotone increasing and bounded above by
V
, it converges a.e. to a limit
w\leq V
; the uniform
H^{1}
bound yields weak
H^{1}
convergence along subsequences, and passing to the limit gives the desired weak solution with the stated energy bound.
∎

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
(u,f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
with
\Sigma(u)\cap B_{1/2}\neq\emptyset
and let
\varepsilon>0
.
Then there exists
u_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}(B_{1/2})
such that

(u_{\varepsilon},(1-\varepsilon)f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1/2})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Sigma(u_{\varepsilon})=\emptyset.

Moreover, as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
,
u_{\varepsilon}\uparrow u
pointwise in
B_{1/2}
and
u_{\varepsilon}\to u
strongly in
H^{1}(B_{1/2})
.

Proof.

Since
\Sigma(u)\cap B_{1/2}\neq\emptyset
, elliptic regularity forces
\lim_{t\uparrow T_{f}}f^{\prime}(t)=+\infty
.
Fix
t_{0}<T_{f}
such that
f(t_{0})>0
and
f^{\prime}(t_{0})>1
, and define the strictly increasing
C^{1}
function

h(t):=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{ds}{f(s)},\qquad t<T_{f}.

For
\varepsilon>0
define
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
by

\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)=\begin{cases}t,&t\leq t_{0},\\[5.69054pt]
h^{-1}\big((1-\varepsilon)h(t)\big),&t_{0}<t<T_{f}.\end{cases}

For
t>t_{0}
the identity
h(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t))=(1-\varepsilon)h(t)
implies

\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t)\,f(t)=(1-\varepsilon)\,f(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)).

Since
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)\leq t
and
f^{\prime}
is increasing, we have
f^{\prime}(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t))\leq f^{\prime}(t)
and thus

\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(t)=(1-\varepsilon)\frac{f(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t))}{f(t)^{2}}\Big((1-\varepsilon)f^{\prime}(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t))-f^{\prime}(t)\Big)\leq 0,

so
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
is concave on
(t_{0},T_{f})
.
Consequently,

-\Delta(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(u))=\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(u)\,f(u)-\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(u)|\nabla u|^{2}\geq(1-\varepsilon)f(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(u)),

meaning that
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(u)
is a weak supersolution for the equation
-\Delta w=(1-\varepsilon)f(w)
.

Applying
Lemma

4.5
in
B_{1/2}
with
V=\Phi_{\varepsilon}(u)
, and taking the monotone-iteration solution constructed in its proof, we obtain
u_{\varepsilon}\leq\Phi_{\varepsilon}(u)
in
B_{1/2}
with

-\Delta u_{\varepsilon}=(1-\varepsilon)f(u_{\varepsilon})\quad\text{in }B_{1/2}.

By the minimality of this solution we also get stability of
u_{\varepsilon}
, hence
(u_{\varepsilon},(1-\varepsilon)f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1/2})
.
If
h(T_{f})=\int_{t_{0}}^{T_{f}}\frac{ds}{f(s)}<+\infty
, then
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)\to\Phi_{\varepsilon}(T_{f}):=h^{-1}((1-\varepsilon)h(T_{f}))<T_{f}
as
t\uparrow T_{f}
, hence
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(u)\leq\Phi_{\varepsilon}(T_{f})<T_{f}
and thus
\Sigma(u_{\varepsilon})=\emptyset
.

If instead
h(T_{f})=+\infty
, one can iterate this concave truncation construction a finite number of times (exactly as in
[
7
, Theorem 3.2.1]
) to obtain a bounded supersolution, and then repeat the argument to conclude again that
\Sigma(u_{\varepsilon})=\emptyset
.

Finally, since
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)\uparrow t
pointwise as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, comparison and the minimal choice of
u_{\varepsilon}
give that
u_{\varepsilon}
converges monotonically a.e. to a function
\tilde{u}\leq u
.
If by contradiction
\tilde{u}\neq u
, the argument of
[
3
, Proposition 4.2, Step 3]
implies that
f
must be affine on the family of intervals
[\tilde{u}(x),u(x)]
as
x
varies in
B_{1}
, forcing
f(u)
to be affine in
u
on a nontrivial interval of the form
[t_{0},T_{f})
; however, this would imply that
u
is regular, contradicting
\Sigma(u)\cap B_{1/2}\neq\emptyset
. This proves that
\tilde{u}=u
.

Finally, the strong convergence of
u_{\varepsilon}
to
u
in
H^{1}(B_{1/2})
follows from
Theorem

2.6
.
∎

Proof of

.

Given

\gamma_{-}:=\liminf_{t\uparrow T_{f}}\tau(t),\qquad\gamma_{+}:=\limsup_{t\uparrow T_{f}}\tau(t).

\gamma_{-}>0
.

Then, for every
\delta\in(0,\gamma_{-})

\gamma_{-}-\delta\leq\tau(t)\leq\gamma_{+}+\delta\qquad\forall\,t\in(t_{\delta},T_{f}).

5.
The two-dimensional case

The goal of this section is to prove
Theorem

2.26
. We recall that, in dimension
n\leq 9
, stable solutions are Hölder continuous
[
3
]
, so in particular
u
and
f(u)
are pointwise well-defined.

5.1.
A priori estimates for singular nonlinearities in all dimensions

Our first estimate is a local
L^{1}
estimate on
f^{\prime}(u)f(u).
This is based on
Lemma

4.1
and reads as follows:

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
(u,f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
with
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then

(5.1)

\int_{B_{1/2}}f^{\prime}(u)f(u)\,dx\leq C(n)\,\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}.

Remark 5.2
.

This bound implies that, if
T_{f}<+\infty
or
n\leq 9
(see
[
3
]
), then
f^{\prime}(u)f(u)\in L^{1}_{\text{{loc}}}
.

To prove the result, we first
show a preliminary bound in
B_{1}
that we will iterate at smaller scales.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
(u,f)\in\mathcal{WS}(B_{1})
with
\Sigma(u)=\emptyset
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

(5.2)

\int_{B_{1/2}}f(u)f^{\prime}(u)\,dx\leq\varepsilon\int_{B_{1}}f(u)f^{\prime}(u)\,dx+\frac{C}{\varepsilon}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})},

where
C=C(n)
is dimensional.

Proof.

We first record two elementary bounds. By stability, applied with a cutoff equal to one in
B_{1/2}
,

(5.3)

\int_{B_{1/2}}f^{\prime}(u)\,dx\leq C.

Also, if
\eta\in C_{c}^{2}(B_{1})
satisfies
\eta\geq\chi_{B_{2/3}}
, then testing the equation against
\eta
gives

(5.4)

\int_{B_{2/3}}f(u)\,dx=-\int_{B_{1}}u\,\Delta\eta\,dx\leq C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}.

Let
K>0
be a parameter to be chosen. On the set
\{f(u)\leq K\}
, (
5.3
) gives

(5.5)

\int_{\{f(u)\leq K\}\cap B_{1/2}}f(u)f^{\prime}(u)\,dx\leq K\int_{B_{1/2}}f^{\prime}(u)\,dx\leq CK.

We now estimate the contribution of
\{f(u)>K\}
. If this set is empty, then (
5.2
) follows from (
5.5
), after the choice of
K
below. Otherwise, choose
\kappa<T_{f}
such that

f(\kappa)=K,

and apply
Lemma

4.1
with

h(t):=\max\{f(t),K\},\qquad g(t):=\int_{\kappa}^{\max\{t,\kappa\}}f^{\prime}(s)^{2}\,ds.

Then
g^{\prime}\geq(h^{\prime})^{2}
. Moreover, for
t\geq\kappa
, convexity gives

g(t)\leq f^{\prime}(t)\int_{\kappa}^{t}f^{\prime}(s)\,ds=f^{\prime}(t)(f(t)-K),

hence

\bigl(h^{2}f^{\prime}-gf\bigr)(u)\geq Kf(u)f^{\prime}(u)\qquad\text{inside }\{f(u)\geq K\}.

Let
\zeta\in C_{c}^{2}(B_{2/3})
satisfy
\zeta\equiv 1
in
B_{1/2}
, and set

G(t):=\int_{\kappa}^{\max\{t,\kappa\}}g(s)\,ds.

Then

0\leq G(t)\leq\frac{1}{2}(f(t)-K)_{+}^{2},\qquad h(t)^{2}\leq C\bigl((f(t)-K)_{+}^{2}+K^{2}\bigr),

and the right-hand side in
Lemma

4.1
is bounded by

C\int_{B_{2/3}}(f(u)-K)_{+}^{2}\,dx+CK^{2}.

Combining this with the lower bound on the left-hand side yields

K\int_{\{f(u)\geq K\}\cap B_{1/2}}f(u)f^{\prime}(u)\,dx\leq C\int_{B_{2/3}}(f(u)-K)_{+}^{2}\,dx+CK^{2}.

After dividing by
K
and adding (
5.5
), we obtain

(5.6)

\int_{B_{1/2}}f(u)f^{\prime}(u)\,dx\leq\frac{C}{K}\int_{B_{2/3}}(f(u)-K)_{+}^{2}\,dx+CK.

We next bound the square term. On
\{f(u)>K\}=\{u>\kappa\}
, convexity gives

f(u)-K\leq f^{\prime}(u)(u-\kappa),

thus

(f(u)-K)_{+}^{2}\leq(f(u)-K)_{+}f^{\prime}(u)(u-\kappa)_{+}\leq f(u)f^{\prime}(u)\,\|u-\kappa\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}.

Plugging this into (
5.6
) gives

(5.7)

\int_{B_{1/2}}f(u)f^{\prime}(u)\,dx\leq\frac{C\|u-\kappa\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}}{K}\int_{B_{1}}f(u)f^{\prime}(u)\,dx+CK.

It remains to choose
K
. Let

m:=\inf_{B_{1}}u

and choose

K:=\frac{A}{\varepsilon}\bigl(f(m)+\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\bigr),

where
A
is a dimensional constant large enough. By (
5.4
) and monotonicity of
f
,

f(m)\leq\fint_{B_{2/3}}f(u)\,dx\leq C\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})},\qquad\text{so}\quad K\leq\frac{CA}{\varepsilon}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}.

Since
\{f(u)>K\}
is nonempty, then
f(\kappa)=K>f(m)
. Hence
\kappa\geq m\geq-\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}
, while also
\kappa\leq\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}
, therefore

\|u-\kappa\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\leq 2\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}.

Thus, choosing
A
sufficiently large,

\frac{C\|u-\kappa\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}}{K}\leq\varepsilon.

Using this in (
5.7
), and recalling the bound on
K
, gives

\int_{B_{1/2}}f(u)f^{\prime}(u)\,dx\leq\varepsilon\int_{B_{1}}f(u)f^{\prime}(u)\,dx+\frac{C}{\varepsilon}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})},

as desired.
∎

Lemma 5.4

Let

Lemma 5.5
.

If

, and

Proof.

The function

Let

is subharmonic and

Thus, since

by the restriction on

\displaystyle\|h\|_{L^{p}(B_{1/2})}

\displaystyle\leq\|H\|_{L^{p}(B_{1/2})}+\|(h-H)_{+}\|_{L^{p}(B_{1/2})}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{G}\|_{L^{p}(B_{1})}\|f^{\prime}(u)f(u)\|_{L^{1}(B_{3/4})}+C_{p}\|(h-H)_{+}\|_{L^{1}(B_{3/4})}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\,\|f^{\prime}(u)f(u)\|_{L^{1}(B_{3/4})}+C_{p}\,\|h\|_{L^{1}(B_{3/4})},

and

8

Next, in the two-dimensional case, we prove a quasi-monotonicity statement for averages of

Lemma 5.7

Let

Fix

Let
t=\log(1/r)
, and write
\mathcal{W}(t):=W(e^{-t})
. Define

(5.10)

\lambda(t):=\frac{\mathcal{W}_{t}(t)}{\mathcal{W}(t)},\qquad\text{so that}\quad\mathcal{W}(t-1)=\mathcal{W}(t)\exp\!\left\{-\int_{t-1}^{t}\lambda(s)\,ds\right\}.

t

Since

\lambda_{t}+\lambda(\lambda-2)+A(t)\exp\!\left\{-\int_{t-1}^{t}\lambda(s)\,ds\right\}=0.

\lambda(t)\leq 2.

Second, we estimate

Note now that the maximum principle applied to

w\in C^{2}
.
In the present setting, however, the error terms in these monotonicity formulas are governed by

g(0)-g(w).

The
C^{1,1}
estimate above gives a local quadratic bound
w\leq Ar^{2}
around
z
, for some finite constant
A
. Hence one expects Weiss-type almost-monotonicity, and then uniqueness of quadratic blow-ups via a Monneau-type formula, provided a Dini condition of the form

\int_{0}^{1}\omega(r)\,\frac{dr}{r}<+\infty,\qquad\omega(r):=g(0)-g(Ar^{2}),

holds. In particular, without such an additional assumption, only
C^{1,1}
regularity is expected to hold.

A.4.
A useful logarithmic comparison lemma

Lemma A.2
.

Let
f:(t_{0},T_{f})\to(0,+\infty)
be convex.

(i)

Assume
T_{f}<+\infty
,
f(t)\to+\infty
as
t\uparrow T_{f}
, and

\int_{t_{0}}^{T_{f}}f(s)\,ds=+\infty.

Then

\limsup_{t\uparrow T_{f}}\frac{\log f(t)}{\log f^{\prime}(t)}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{2}.

(ii)

Assume
T_{f}=+\infty
,
f(t)\to+\infty
as
t\to+\infty
, and
f^{\prime}(t)>1
for all sufficiently large
t
. Then

\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\log f(t)}{\log f^{\prime}(t)}\;\geq\;1.

Proof.

(i)

Assume by contradiction that

L:=\limsup_{t\uparrow T_{f}}\frac{\log f(t)}{\log f^{\prime}(t)}<\frac{1}{2}.

Choose
\varepsilon\in\bigl(0,\tfrac{1}{2}-L\bigr)
so that
L\leq\tfrac{1}{2}-2\varepsilon
.
For
t
close enough to
T_{f}
, we have
\log f(t)>0
,
\log f^{\prime}(t)>0
, and

\frac{\log f(t)}{\log f^{\prime}(t)}\leq\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon,

i.e.

f^{\prime}(t)\geq f(t)^{\,q},\qquad q=\frac{2}{1-2\varepsilon}>2.

Since
f
is increasing, set
y=f(t)
. From this inequality we obtain
dt/dy\leq y^{-q}
, hence

\int_{t_{1}}^{T_{f}}f(s)\,ds=\int_{f(t_{1})}^{\infty}y\,\frac{dt}{dy}\,dy\leq\int_{f(t_{1})}^{\infty}y^{1-q}\,dy<+\infty,

contradicting
\int_{t_{0}}^{T_{f}}f=+\infty
.

(ii)

Assume by contradiction that

L:=\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\log f(t)}{\log f^{\prime}(t)}<1.

Then for some
\varepsilon>0
and all
t
large enough,

\frac{\log f(t)}{\log f^{\prime}(t)}\leq 1-\varepsilon,\qquad\text{so}\qquad f^{\prime}(t)\geq f(t)^{\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}}=f(t)^{1+\delta},\quad\delta=\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}>0.

This differential inequality forces
f
to blow up in finite time, contradicting
T_{f}=+\infty
.
∎

A.5.
Construction of suitable cut-off functions

We construct the cutoff lemma used in the proof of
Proposition

4.3
.

Lemma A.3
.

Given
p>1
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\zeta\in C^{2}_{c}(B_{1})
such that
\zeta\geq\chi_{B_{1/2}}
and

\int_{B_{1}}w\Big(|\nabla\zeta|^{2}+|\zeta\Delta\zeta|\Big)\,dx\leq\varepsilon\int_{B_{1}}w\,|\log w|^{2p}\,\zeta^{2}\,dx+C,

for all nonnegative measurable
w:B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
C=C(n,p,\varepsilon)
is a large constant.

Proof.

Let
q:=\frac{p+1}{2}\in(1,p)
and choose a radially decreasing cutoff
0\leq\zeta\leq 1
with

\chi_{B_{1/2}}\leq\zeta\leq\chi_{B_{1}},\qquad\zeta>0\ \text{on }B_{1},

and such that near
\partial B_{1}
(i.e. for
\zeta
small) it satisfies

\zeta(|x|)=e^{-(1-|x|)_{+}^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}},\qquad\text{thus}\quad\partial_{r}\zeta=-\frac{1}{q-1}\zeta\,|\log\zeta|^{q}.

Note that, with this choice, for
x
close to
\partial B_{1}
we have

\zeta\Delta\zeta=\zeta\left(\partial_{rr}\zeta+(n-1)\frac{\partial_{r}\zeta}{r}\right)=\frac{1}{(q-1)^{2}}\zeta^{2}|\log\zeta|^{2q}\Big(1-\frac{q}{|\log\zeta|}-\frac{(q-1)(n-1)}{|x|\,|\log\zeta|^{q}}\Big)\sim\zeta^{2}|\log\zeta|^{2q}.

Since away from
\partial B_{1}
we can bound
|\nabla\zeta|^{2}+|\zeta\Delta\zeta|\leq C\zeta^{2}
, we conclude that

|\nabla\zeta|^{2}+|\zeta\Delta\zeta|\leq\bar{C}\,\zeta^{2}\left(1+|\log\zeta|^{2q}\right)\qquad\text{in }B_{1},

and therefore

\int w\Big(|\nabla\zeta|^{2}+|\zeta\Delta\zeta|\Big)\leq\bar{C}\int w\,\zeta^{2}\left(1+|\log\zeta|^{2q}\right).

Now, recalling that
w\geq 0
and
0\leq\zeta\leq 1
, a standard Young-type splitting yields that, for any
\varepsilon>0
,
9
9

9

Indeed, set

t:=\sqrt{1+|\log\zeta|^{2q}}\geq 1
, so that
1+|\log\zeta|^{2q}=t^{2}
.
If
t\leq\varepsilon|\log w|^{p}
, then
wt^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}w|\log w|^{2p}
, giving the desired inequality.
Otherwise,
|\log w|\leq(t/\varepsilon)^{1/p}
, hence
w\leq\exp((t/\varepsilon)^{1/p})
, and therefore

w\,t^{2}\leq t^{2}\exp\!\bigl((t/\varepsilon)^{1/p}\bigr).

Since
\zeta^{-2}=e^{2|\log\zeta|}=\exp\!\bigl(2(t^{2}-1)^{1/(2q)}\bigr)
and
q<p
, the function

t\mapsto t^{2}\exp\!\Bigl((t/\varepsilon)^{1/p}-2(t^{2}-1)^{1/(2q)}\Bigr)

is bounded on
[1,+\infty)
. This implies that
w\,t^{2}\leq C_{\varepsilon,p,q}\,\zeta^{-2}
, and therefore the desired inequality holds also in this case.

w\,\bigl(1+|\log\zeta|^{2q}\bigr)\leq\varepsilon^{2}\,w\,|\log w|^{2p}+C_{\varepsilon,p,q}\,\zeta^{-2}.

so multiplying by
\zeta^{2}
and integrating yields

\int w\Big(|\nabla\zeta|^{2}+|\zeta\Delta\zeta|\Big)\leq\bar{C}\varepsilon^{2}\int w|\log w|^{2p}\zeta^{2}+C_{\varepsilon,p,q}.

Assuming without loss of generality that
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small so that
\bar{C}\varepsilon\leq 1
, the result follows.
