Periods of N N -Body Systems Determined through Dimensional Analysis
Dan Jonsson

Abstract
. A generalization of classical dimensional analysis, presented in a separate article, makes it possible to derive Kepler’s third law without solving the equations of motion. Here we show how to derive generalizations of Kepler’s third law to n n -body systems by the same technique. Our results agree with conjectures by Sun on the period of a Newtonian n n -body system and by Semay and Sun on the quantum-theoretical counterpart of an n n -body system.



1.
Introduction

The classical theory of
n
-body systems concerns
n
point masses,
“bodies” or “particles”
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}}}\right\}

that move in space under influence of their mutual gravitational attraction
in accordance with the laws of Newtonian mechanics. The equations
of motion for a collisionless
n
-body system can be derived directly
from the force law
\mathbf{F}=m\mathbf{a}
and Newton’s law of gravitation

\mathbf{F}=G\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{r^{2}}\mathbf{r}
, where
\mathbf{r}

is a unit vector pointing in the same direction as
\mathbf{F}
.
In vector form, the equations are

(1.1)

\begin{cases}m_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{q}_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}=G\sum_{j\in\mathbf{n}\setminus\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{i}}\right\}}m_{i}m_{j}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{q}_{j}-\mathbf{q}_{i}}}\right)}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathbf{q}_{j}-\mathbf{q}_{i}}}\right\|^{3}}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i=1,\ldots,n}}\right)\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{n}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,\ldots,n}}\right\}
,
t
is time,
G

the gravitational constant,
m_{i}
the mass of
p_{i}
and
\mathbf{q}_{i}

its radius vector as a function of
t
.

For a spatially bounded
n
-body system there may exist some time

T_{0}
such that at a later time
T_{0}+T
each body has the same
position and velocity as at
T_{0}
. Then it has the same position
and velocity at any time
T_{0}+nT
for any positive integer
n

since the equations of motion determine the future trajectory of each
body given only its position and velocity at an arbitrary time. We
call a trajectory followed from
T_{0}
to
T_{0}+T
an
orbit
,
an
n
-body system with trajectories made up of orbits an
orbital
(periodic)

n
-body system, and
T
the
period
of the orbital
system.

In the two-body case, we obtain, under suitable initial conditions,
the familiar elliptical orbits, with the two bodies in addition obeying
Kepler’s second and third laws. Kepler’s third law concerns the period
of an orbital two-body system. It can be shown from the equations
of motion that

(1.2)

T^{2}=4\pi^{2}a^{3}G^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1}+m_{2}}}\right)^{-1},

where
T
is the period and
a
the semi-major axis of the elliptical
orbit
[
1
, eq. 2.37]
.

Let us regard two bodies in an
n
-body system from the point of
view of kinematics as a two-body system where one body
p_{i}
orbits
a central body
p_{j}
, the radius vector of which is set to
\mathbf{0}
.
The
mean extremal distance
between
p_{i}
and
p_{j}
is
defined as

(1.3)

\mu_{ij}=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathbf{r}_{a}-\mathbf{0}}}\right\|+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathbf{r}_{p}-\mathbf{0}}}\right\|}{2},

where
\mathbf{r}_{a}
and
\mathbf{r}_{p}
are the radius vectors
of the apoapsis and periapsis, respectively, of
p_{i}
. In the proper
two-body case, with elliptical orbits, we have
\mu_{12}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathbf{r}_{a}-\mathbf{r}_{p}}}\right\|/2

and
a=\mu_{12}
. Also,
a
is a
characteristic distance
,
that is,
T
depends separately on the mean extremal distance between
the bodies (up to a multiplicative constant). It turns out that
T

depends on any
d\not\propto a
(for example, the semi-minor axis)
only in conjunction with a dimensionless shape parameter
s
(such
as the eccentricity of the orbital ellipse), so that
T^{\mathfrak{K}}=\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1},m_{2},a,G}}\right)

becomes
T^{\mathfrak{K^{\prime}}}=\varphi^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1},m_{2},d,s,G}}\right)
.

Let
E
be the total mechanical energy of an orbital
n
-body system,
normalized by letting its potential energy tend to zero as all distances
between bodies approach infinity. Then it can be shown that in the
two-body case we have

(1.4)

E=-\frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{2a}.

E
needs to be negative since otherwise we would not have spatially
bounded trajectories, so that
T
would not be defined. (
1.4
)
implies that
a^{3}=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-E}}\right)^{3}G^{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1}m_{2}}}\right)^{3}}{8}
,
and substituting this in (

) gives

(1.5)

T^{2}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-E}}\right)^{-3}G^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1}m_{2}}}\right)^{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1}+m_{2}}}\right)^{-1}.

Unfortunately, the equations of motion (

) do not admit

. Even the Newtonian three-body problem
is much more difficult than the two-body problem. While the latter
was solved completely by Newton
[
2
]
and Johann Bernoulli

[
3
]
, the former is a famous problem that has remained recalcitrant

]

[
5
]
,

]

Recently, BoHua Sun

7

has formulated a bold conjecture

).
He suggests that the orbital period of a three-body system is given

T^{2}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-E}}\right)^{-3}G^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1}m_{2}}}\right)^{3}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1}m_{3}}}\right)^{3}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{2}m_{3}}}\right)^{3}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3}}}\right)^{-1},

and, more generally, that the period of an orbital

-body system
is given by

There is an inaccuracy in

T^{2}=\phi_{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n},E,G}}\right)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-E}}\right)^{-3}G^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{i}m_{j}}}\right)^{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}}}\right)^{-1}.

In addition to the analogy between (

Let
\sigma

a corresponding relabeling of the bodies in the

Each

of the

-tuples of scalars
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}}}\right)

It is shown in Section

that

must satisfy

\displaystyle\phi_{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n},E,G}}\right)=\phi_{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)},\ldots,m_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)},E,G}}\right)

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Augmented dimensional analysis

Dimensional analysis can be described as a method of transforming
”physically meaningful” equations of the form

t=\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}}}\right),

describing relationships among quantities, into equivalent but more
informative equations, using data about the dimensions of
t,t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
.
This can be done by representing the unknown function
\phi
as a
product of the form
\prod\nolimits_{j=1}^{r}\!x{}_{j}^{\mathfrak{K}_{j}}\psi
,
where
\psi
, while also unspecified, is a function of fewer arguments
than
\phi
(possibly none, in which case
\psi
is an unknown constant).

Specifically, for a suitable partition
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-r}}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}}\right\}}}\right\}

of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}}}\right\}
,

(2.1)

t^{\mathfrak{K}}=\prod\nolimits_{j=1}^{r}\!x{}_{j}^{\mathfrak{K}_{j}}\,\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n-r}}}\right),

where
\mathfrak{K}
is a positive integer,
\mathfrak{K}_{j}
are
integers,
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n-r}
and
\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n-r}}}\right)

are dimensionless quantities and
\pi_{i}
is a product of the form

(2.2)

\pi_{i}=y_{i}^{k_{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\prod\nolimits_{j=1}^{r}\!x{}_{j}^{k_{ij}}}}\right)^{-1}\quad(i=1,\ldots,n-r),

where
k_{i}
is a positive integer and
k_{ij}
are integers.

Integers
\mathfrak{K}
,
\mathfrak{K}_{j}
,
k_{i}
and
k_{ij}

satisfying (
2.1
) and (
2.2
)
exist if and only if
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}}\right\}
, commonly
called a set of ”repeating variables”, is a set of dimensionally
independent variables which is maximal in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t,t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}}}\right\}
,
so that

(2.3)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[t}}\right]^{\mathfrak{K}}=\prod\nolimits_{j=1}^{r}\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{j}}}\right]^{\mathfrak{K}_{j}},\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[y_{i}}}\right]^{k_{i}}=\prod\nolimits_{j=1}^{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{j}}}\right]^{k_{ij}}\quad(i=1,\ldots,n-r),

where
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x}}\right]
is the dimension of
x
, has solutions
\mathfrak{K}
,

\mathfrak{K}_{j}
,
k_{i}
and
k_{ij}
,
which are unique if we also require that
\mathrm{lcd}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathfrak{K},\mathfrak{K}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{K}_{r}}}\right)=\mathrm{lcd}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{i},k_{i1},\ldots,k_{ir}}}\right)=1
.

The expansions of the form (
2.3
) can be specified by
dimensional matrices, where each quantity variable corresponds to
a column vector giving the dimensional formula of that quantity relative
to given base dimensions, in mechanics usually
\mathsf{L},\mathsf{T},\mathsf{M}
.
For example, if the dimensional matrix is

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[t}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[y_{1}}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{1}}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{2}}}\right]\\
\mathsf{L}&1&0&1&0\\
\mathsf{T}&-2&0&0&1\\
\mathsf{M}&1&0&1&0\end{array},

then the dimensional formulas are
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[t}}\right]=\mathsf{L}\mathsf{T}^{-2}\mathsf{M}
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[y_{1}}}\right]=\mathsf{L}^{0}\mathsf{T}^{0}\mathsf{M}^{0}
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{1}}}\right]=\mathsf{LM}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{2}}}\right]=\mathsf{T}
.
Thus,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x_{1},x_{2}}}\right\}
is the maximal subset of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y_{1},x_{1},x_{2}}}\right\}

with independent variables, and we have
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[t}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{1}}}\right]\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{2}}}\right]^{-2}

and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[y_{1}}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{1}}}\right]^{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{2}}}\right]^{0}
,
so
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathfrak{K},\mathfrak{K}_{1},\mathfrak{K}_{2}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,1,-2}}\right)

and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{1},k_{11},k_{12}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,0,0}}\right)
, so (
2.1
)
becomes
t=x_{1}x_{2}^{-2}\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1}}}\right)
.

It is important to note that there may be more than one set of repeating
variables. In augmented dimensional analysis
[
11
]
the result
obtained is not one equation of the form (
2.1
) but a system
of
p\geq 1
such equations,

(2.4)

\begin{cases}t^{\mathfrak{K}_{1}}=\prod\nolimits_{j=1}^{r}x{}_{1j}^{k_{1j}}\,\psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{11},\ldots,\pi_{1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n-r}}\right)}}}\right),\\
\cdots\\
t^{\mathfrak{K}_{p}}=\prod\nolimits_{j=1}^{r}x{}_{pj}^{k_{pj}}\,\psi_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{p1},\ldots,\pi_{p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n-r}}\right)}}}\right),\end{cases}

each equation corresponding to a distinct maximal set of dimensionally
independent variables
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x_{i1},\ldots,x_{ir}}}\right\}
. (All

\mathfrak{K}_{i}
will be equal in the applications below.) For
example, if we had
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[y_{1}}}\right]=\mathsf{L}^{0}\mathsf{T^{-1}}\mathsf{M}^{0}

in the example above then
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y_{1},x_{1}}}\right\}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x_{1},x_{2}}}\right\}

would both be sets of repeating variables, and we would obtain

\begin{cases}t=y_{1}^{2}x_{1}\,\psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{2}y_{1}}}\right),\\
t=x_{1}x_{2}^{-2}\,\psi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1}x_{2}}}\right).\end{cases}

By combining a system of two or more equations with additional information,
we can derive results that could not have been obtained from these
equations individually, even in combination with the same additional
information. More specifically, while classical dimensional analysis
produces one equation of the form (
2.1
), where
\psi

is unknown, augmented dimensional analysis not only produces a system
of equations (
2.4
), but may also make it possible, introducing
a symmetry assumption, to find
\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{p}
, and hence
also
\phi
, up to a multiplicative constant. This principle, first
formulated and demonstrated in
[
10
]
, will be used repeatedly
in this article.

2.2.
Symmetries

An
n
-body system has important symmetries which are encoded in
its equations of motion and their initial conditions. Consider the
system of equations

(2.5)

\begin{cases}m_{1}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}_{1}=G\sum_{j=2}^{n}m_{1}m_{j}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{q}_{j}-\mathbf{q}_{1}}}\right)}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathbf{q}_{j}-\mathbf{q}_{1}}}\right\|^{3}},\quad\mathbf{q}_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)=\mathrm{q}_{1},\quad\dot{\mathbf{q}}{}_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)=\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{1}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right),\\
\cdots\\
m_{n}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}_{n}=G\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}m_{n}m_{j}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{q}_{j}-\mathbf{q}_{n}}}\right)}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathbf{q}_{j}-\mathbf{q}_{n}}}\right\|^{3}},\quad\mathbf{q}_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)=\mathrm{q}_{1},\quad\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)=\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{n}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right),\end{cases}

which contains all equations in (
1.1
) together with their
initial conditions. Let again
\overline{\sigma}:p_{i}\mapsto p_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}

be a relabeling of the bodies in the
n
-body system
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}}}\right)
.
As
m_{i}
is the mass of
p_{i}
and
\mathbf{q}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

the radius vector of
p_{i}
at
t
,
\overline{\sigma}
corresponds
to permutations
m_{i}\mapsto m_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}
and
\mathbf{q}_{i}\mapsto\mathbf{q}_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}

of the n-tuples of scalars
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}}}\right)
and
vector-valued functions
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{q}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{q}_{n}}}\right)
,
respectively, so
\overline{\sigma}
sends the row

m_{i}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}_{i}=G\sum_{j\in\mathbf{n}\setminus\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{i}}\right\}}m_{i}m_{j}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{q}_{j}-\mathbf{q}_{i}}}\right)}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathbf{q}_{j}-\mathbf{q}_{i}}}\right\|^{3}},\quad\mathbf{q}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)=\mathrm{q}_{i},\quad\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)=\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)

in (
2.5
) to the row

\displaystyle m_{i^{\prime}}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}_{i^{\prime}}=G\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\mathbf{n}\setminus\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{i^{\prime}}}\right\}}m_{i^{\prime}}m_{j^{\prime}}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{q}_{j^{\prime}}-\mathbf{q}_{i^{\prime}}}}\right)}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathbf{q}_{j^{\prime}}-\mathbf{q}_{i^{\prime}}}}\right\|^{3}},\quad\mathbf{q}_{i^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)=\mathrm{q}_{i^{\prime}},\quad\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{i^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)=\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i^{\prime}}}\right),

where
i^{\prime}=\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)
and
j^{\prime}=\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right)
. Thus,

\overline{\sigma}
just permutes the rows in (
2.5
),
so it is a mapping of (
2.5
) to itself, since two equation
systems with the same set of differential equations and corresponding
initial conditions are equal regardless of the order in which the
equations and conditions are listed. Hence, for every variable the
value determined by (
2.5
) is invariant under a relabeling

\overline{\sigma}
of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}}}\right)
.

Consider the three functions

\displaystyle\phi_{T}:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n},E,G}}\right)\mapsto T^{\mathfrak{J}},\qquad\varphi{}_{T}:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n},d,G}}\right)\mapsto T^{\mathfrak{K}},

\displaystyle\phi_{d}:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n},E,G}}\right)\mapsto d^{\mathfrak{L}}.

The distance
d
is assumed to be given by a function
f
such that

d=f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{q}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{q}_{n}}}\right)=f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{q}_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{q}_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)}}}\right)

for every permutation
\sigma
of
1,\ldots,n
; we say that
d

is a
symmetric distance
. Also, the total mechanical energy

E
is given by

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{m_{i}^{2}}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{\dot{q}}_{i}}}\right)^{2}-G\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\frac{m_{i}m_{j}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathrm{\mathbf{q}}_{j}-\mathrm{\mathbf{q}}_{i}}}\right\|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\frac{m_{i^{\prime}}^{2}}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{\dot{q}}_{i^{\prime}}}}\right)^{2}-G\sum_{i=1^{\prime}}^{n-1}\sum_{j^{\prime}=i+1}^{n}\frac{m_{i^{\prime}}m_{j^{\prime}}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathrm{\mathbf{q}}_{j^{\prime}}-\mathrm{\mathbf{q}}_{i^{\prime}}}}\right\|},

where
i^{\prime}=\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)
and
j^{\prime}=\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right)
. Furthermore,

T
is determined by (
2.5
) regardless of
\overline{\sigma}
,
and
d
and
E
by definition do not depend on
\overline{\sigma}
,
so we have

(2.6)

\displaystyle T^{\mathfrak{J}}=\phi_{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n},E,G}}\right)=\phi_{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)},\ldots,m_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)},E,G}}\right),

(2.7)

\displaystyle T^{\mathfrak{K}}=\varphi_{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n},d,G}}\right)=\varphi_{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)},\ldots,m_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)},d,G}}\right)

(2.8)

\displaystyle d^{\mathfrak{L}}=\phi_{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n},E,G}}\right)=\phi_{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)},\ldots,m_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)},E,G}}\right)

for any
\phi_{T},\varphi{}_{T},\phi_{d}
and any permutation
\sigma

of
1,\ldots,n
.

3.
Two-body systems

In this section, we give examples of augmented dimensional analysis
related to equations (
1.2
) and (
1.5
)
above.

Example 3.1
.

T^{\mathfrak{K}}=\varphi{}_{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1},m_{2},d,G}}\right)

Let two bodies with masses
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
revolve around each
other in empty space under influence of their mutual gravitational
attraction. Assume that the orbital period
T
depends on the two
masses, a symmetric characteristic distance
d
and the gravitational
constant
G
, that is,
T^{\mathfrak{K}}=\phi_{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1},m_{2},d,G}}\right)
.

The dimensional matrix expressing the dimensionality
of the variables in this equation relative to the base dimensions

\mathsf{L},\mathsf{T},\mathsf{M}
is

\begin{array}[]{cccccc}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[T}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m_{1}}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m_{2}}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[G}}\right]\\
\mathsf{L}&0&0&0&1&3\\
\mathsf{T}&1&0&0&0&-2\\
\mathsf{M}&0&1&1&0&-1\end{array}.

The sets of repeating variables are

If

Example 3.2

\mathsf{M}&0&1&1&1&-1\end{array}.

The sets of repeating variables are
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{m_{1},E,G}}\right\}

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{m_{2},E,G}}\right\}

\begin{cases}d=m_{1}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-E}}\right)^{-1}G\,\psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{2}/m_{1}}}\right),&(B1)\\

By (

4.
Three-body systems

When going from

to

, equations change form, and the three-body

n
-body

Example 4.1
.

T^{\mathfrak{K}}=\varphi{}_{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3},d,G}}\right)

Assume that the orbital period of a three-body system
T
is determined
by the masses
m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}
of the three bodies, a symmetric
characteristic distance
d
, and the gravitational constant
G
;
that is,
T^{\mathfrak{K}}=\phi_{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3},d,G}}\right)

The dimensional matrix corresponding to this equation is

\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[T}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m_{1}}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m_{2}}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m_{3}}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[G}}\right]\\
\mathsf{L}&0&0&0&0&1&3\\
\mathsf{T}&1&0&0&0&0&-2\\
\mathsf{M}&0&1&1&1&0&-1\end{array},

and the sets of repeating variables are
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{m_{1},d,G}}\right\}
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{m_{2},d,G}}\right\}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{m_{3},d,G}}\right\}
. Corresponding
to the six equations of the form

T^{\mathfrak{K}}=\varphi_{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{\text{$\sigma$$\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)$}},m_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2}}\right)},m_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(3}}\right)},d,G}}\right),

where
\sigma
is a permutation of
1,2,3
, we obtain the equation
system

(4.1)

\begin{cases}T^{2}=m_{1}^{-1}d^{3}G^{-1}\,\psi_{11}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{2}/m_{1},m_{3}/m_{1}}}\right),\\
T^{2}=m_{1}^{-1}d^{3}G^{-1}\,\psi_{12}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{3}/m_{1},m_{2}/m_{1}}}\right),\\
T^{2}=m_{2}^{-1}d^{3}G^{-1}\,\psi_{21}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1}/m_{2},m_{3}/m_{2}}}\right),\\
T^{2}=m_{2}^{-1}d^{3}G^{-1}\,\psi_{22}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{3}/m_{2},m_{1}/m_{2}}}\right),\\
T^{2}=m_{3}^{-1}d^{3}G^{-1}\,\psi_{31}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1}/m_{3},m_{2}/m_{3}}}\right),\\
T^{2}=m_{3}^{-1}d^{3}G^{-1}\,\psi_{32}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{2}/m_{3},m_{1}/m_{3}}}\right).\end{cases}

We have
\psi_{11}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)=\psi_{12}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y,x}}\right)

can reduce (
4.1
) without loss of information to

(4.2)

\begin{cases}T^{2}=m_{1}^{-1}d^{3}G^{-1}\,\psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{2}/m_{1},m_{3}/m_{1}}}\right),&(A1)\\
T^{2}=m_{2}^{-1}d^{3}G^{-1}\,\psi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1}/m_{2},m_{3}/m_{2}}}\right),&(A2)\\
T^{2}=m_{3}^{-1}d^{3}G^{-1}\,\psi_{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1}/m_{3},m_{2}/m_{3}}}\right),&(A3)\end{cases}

where
\psi_{1}=\psi_{11}
,
\psi_{2}=\psi_{21}
and
\psi_{3}=\psi_{31}
.

In view of (
2.7
), exchanging
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
in (
A1
)
gives

T^{2}=m_{2}^{-1}d^{3}G^{-1}\,\psi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1}/m_{2},m_{3}/m_{2}}}\right)\quad(A1^{\prime}),

and it follows from
(A1^{\prime})
and (A2) that
\psi_{1}=\psi_{2}=\psi
.

Combining (
A1
) and (
A2
) we obtain

m_{1}^{-1}d^{3}G^{-1}\,\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{2}/m_{1},m_{3}/m_{1}}}\right)=m_{2}^{-1}d^{3}G^{-1}\,\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1}/m_{2},m_{3}/m_{2}}}\right),

so setting
x=m_{2}/m_{1}
and
y=m_{3}/m_{1},
so that

,
we obtain the functional equation

(4.3)

\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)=x^{-1}\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{-1},x^{-1}y}}\right).

(
4.3
)
has solutions

(4.4)

\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)=c_{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+x+y}}\right)^{-1}

since

Thus, substituting (

(4.5)

Example 4.2
.

T^{2}=c_{T}d^{3}G^{-1}(m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3})^{-1}

\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m_{1}}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m_{2}}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m_{3}}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[E}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[G}}\right]\\

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{m_{1},E,G}}\right\}
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{m_{2},E,G}}\right\}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{m_{3},E,G}}\right\}
, and

\begin{cases}d=m_{1}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-E}}\right)^{-1}G\,\psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{2}/m_{1},m_{3}/m_{1}}}\right),&(B1)\\

and
m_{2}

where

Case
1.

Case

9.
Conclusions about uniqueness

Let us return to the question of the uniqueness of the generalizations
of (
1.5
)

T^{2}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-E}}\right)^{-3}G^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1}m_{2}}}\right)^{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1}+m_{2}}}\right)^{-1},

derived from the equations of motion, to a equation of the form

(9.1)

T^{\mathfrak{J}}=\phi_{T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n},E,G}}\right)

in the
n
-body case. There are two basic requirements on such generalizations.

First, (
9.1
) must be dimensionally homogeneous, since otherwise
the scalar representation of the underlying quantity function would
depend on an arbitrary choice of units of measurement. Second,
\phi_{T}

must be symmetric in
m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}
. In Section
2.2
,
we proved this by a formal approach, using the fact that the permutations

\begin{cases}m_{i}\mapsto m_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)},\quad\mathbf{q}_{i}\mapsto\mathbf{q}_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i=1,\ldots,n}}\right)\end{cases}

that correspond to a relabeling of
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
maps the
related system of equations of motion and initial conditions to itself.
Informally, the labeling of the bodies in an
n
-body system is just
a reference frame, and as always the physical reality remains unchanged
when the reference frame is changed, implying the required symmetries.

Unfortunately, these two criteria do not sufficiently restrict the
range of possible equations. For example,

\displaystyle T^{2}=k_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-E}}\right)^{-3}G^{2}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1}m_{2}}}\right)^{3}}{\sqrt{m_{1}m_{2}}},\qquad T^{2}=k_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-E}}\right)^{-3}G^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m_{1}^{5}+m_{2}^{5}}}\right),
