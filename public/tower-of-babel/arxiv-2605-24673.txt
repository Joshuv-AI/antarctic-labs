Affinity Graph Connectivity in Convex Clustering
Sam Rosen, Jason Xu

Abstract
We generalize finite-sample bounds for convex clustering to the setting where affinity weights appearing in the objective correspond to a general connected graph. These bounds and their analysis lead to a better understanding of clustering behavior under various implied connectivity structures behind the data and to new rates of convergence for centroid recovery. The new theoretical framework is based on random walks, which allow application of concentration inequalities related to random graph models, and formalizes the relationship between the clustering performance and the connectivity of the graph structures. Through the form of the bound and empirical results, we argue proper tuning of hyperparameters to convex clustering problems should also include tuning of input affinity weights.

Introduction

Convex clustering, also referred to as clusterpath
[
18
]
, relaxes single linkage hierarchical clustering into a convex optimization problem

\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}}\gamma\sum_{i<j}^{n}\sqrt{\Phi_{ij}}\|\boldsymbol{U}_{i\cdot}-\boldsymbol{U}_{j\cdot}\|_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{U}-\boldsymbol{X}\|^{2}_{F},

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is a data matrix of
n
samples and
p
features and
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm. The effect of the regularization term are determined by affinities
\Phi_{ij}>0
, which encourage
\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i\cdot}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j\cdot}
to be close. Hence, the regularization term in
Eq.

1
causes solutions to have a small number of unique rows where
\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i\cdot}
is interpreted as the cluster centroid of
\boldsymbol{X}_{i\cdot}
. If
\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i\cdot}=\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j\cdot}
, then samples
i
and
j
are fit to the same cluster. As
\gamma
increases, the regularization term encourages
\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}
to coalesce into fewer unique centroids, and eventually leads to a single unique row in
\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}
equal to the grand mean of
\boldsymbol{X}

[
28
]
.

Convex clustering has many appealing properties as well as practical merits. The solution to
Eq.

1

\boldsymbol{X}
,
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
, and

. Furthermore, the convexity of the objective function gives a single global optima that is not sensitive to initialization and can be optimized in a variety of manners, including MM algorithms

,

and other scalable variants
[

,
26
,
38
,
39
]
. Under any method, a sparse choice of

,
36
]
, graph-structured data
[

]
, supervised learning
[

]
, and robust loss functions
[

.

In all of these cases, the user needs to specify some choice of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
, and the quality of solutions to

1

\Phi_{ij}\geq 0

a priori

and

. Intuitively, since every

encourages samples

,

,
7
,

0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

These studies have noted that this heuristic performs well in practice, but rigorous support for this observation is lacking in the literature. It is not clear how densely
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
should be chosen in order to recover clusters well. For instance, existing work has noted that under uniform weights
\Phi_{ij}=1
for
i\neq j
, convex clustering fails in simple cases such as data supported on two disjoint balls
[
15
]
, or with a single corrupted data point

]
. When allowing

to be non-uniform, convex clustering is able to maintain practical guarantees.

for any

]
, effecting a disconnect between theory and practice. A recent exception is a theoretical study by

that establishes under quite technical conditions on the support of

that if

is set to zero when

is a disjoint union of “effectively star-shaped” sets.

In this article, we propose a simple new perspective that leads to bounds on the mean squared error of convex clustering solutions under general choices of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
. Under weaker assumptions than prior work,
we extend the finite-sample bounds in
[
28
]
and identify the key properties of

]

6

20

35
]

[
28

lack flexibility in the affinities

\Phi_{ij}=1
for

. In turn, the bound only implies consistency of convex clustering with specific conditions on the data-generating mechanism: cluster centers differing by a fixed set of features, yet

and
\sqrt{\frac{n\log(n^{2}p)}{p}}=o(1)

, some finite-sample bounds in the specific case of convex clustering on low-rank data matrices exist

20
]
, and we standardize these results for objective functions of the form
Eq.

1
. Finally, our results contribute to the broader statistical theory behind convex clustering in establishing new prediction consistency bounds. The proof technique is generalizable and leverages a novel connection to commute times of a random walk on graphs formed by the affinities. These findings are supported by our empirical study, which demonstrates that the properties of this random walk are related to solution quality in practice.

2
Convex Clustering Setup and Basic Bounds

To facilitate our analysis, here we define the data-generating mechanism and framework for affinity weights. We begin by imposing a minimal set of assumptions for a notion of consistency to be well-defined: there exists a fixed number of unique centers from which the observations (rows) are sampled under any sub-Gaussian distribution. This can be interpreted as a generic mixture model without further parametric assumptions on its components.

Assumption 1
.

The data
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is generated from
n
independent observations of a center added to sub-Gaussian noise. Specifically,
\boldsymbol{X}_{i\cdot}=\boldsymbol{U}_{i\cdot}+\boldsymbol{E}_{i\cdot}
where
\boldsymbol{U}_{i\cdot}
is selected from
\mathcal{C}=(\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{c}_{m})
with membership probability
\boldsymbol{p}
, and
\boldsymbol{E}_{i\cdot}
is mean-zero with sub-Gaussian norm
\sigma_{p}
, i.e.

\sigma_{p}=\|\boldsymbol{E}_{i\cdot}\|_{\psi_{2}}=\sup_{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}=1}\inf\{K>0\colon\mathbb{E}[\exp((\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{E}_{i\cdot})^{2}/K^{2})]\leq 2\}.\quad(\text{Definition 3.4.1 of \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{vershyninHighDimensionalProbabilityIntroduction2026}{}{}]}})

This assumption will allow us to discuss clustering consistency in the sense of the mean squared error,
\frac{1}{np}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}-\boldsymbol{U}\|_{F}^{2}
, converging in probability to zero.

Instead of viewing each affinity
\Phi_{ij}
individually, we consider them holistically as a graph
G
. For additional clarity, we define the affinity graph and some derived quantities used throughout this work.

Definition 1

(Affinity Graph)
.

For a data matrix
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, a valid affinity graph,
G=(V,\mathcal{E})
, is undirected, connected, and has no negative edge weights or self-loops. Each valid affinity graph corresponds to a symmetric adjacency matrix,
\boldsymbol{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, with nonnegative entries and a zero diagonal. Each node
i\in V
corresponds to sample
\boldsymbol{X}_{i\cdot}
and
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}\iff\Phi_{ij}>0
.

We further define the following quantities for affinity graphs:

•

the degree matrix
\boldsymbol{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, a diagonal matrix where
D_{ii}
is the degree of node
i
;

•

an oriented edge-incidence matrix
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times|\mathcal{E}|}
where each column has two nonzero values corresponding to the two nodes connected by that edge, e.g.
\boldsymbol{F}_{j,\mathcal{E}(j,k)}=-\boldsymbol{F}_{k,\mathcal{E}(j,k)}=\sqrt{\Phi_{jk}}
, where
\mathcal{E}(j,k)
is the index of the edge between nodes
j
and
k
(without loss of generality, assume
j<k
);

•

and the Laplacian,
\boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{F}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{D}-\boldsymbol{\Phi}
.

We note that imposing connectivity is a mild condition in practice, as multiple disconnected components suggests an immediate partition of the samples. The user can then simply consider further clustering on each component individually, where our contributions apply. Connectivity is immediately satisfied under a variety of graph constructions. For example, a complete graph is trivially connected almost surely, and under commonly used embeddings such as
\epsilon
-radius or
k
-nearest neighbor graphs, conditions which guarantee that
G
will be connected for large enough datasets are well-studied
[
21
]
.

Using the definition of
\boldsymbol{F}
from
Definition

1
, we can write

\operatorname{col}^{\top}_{\mathcal{E}(i,j)}(\boldsymbol{F})\boldsymbol{U}=\sqrt{\Phi_{ij}}(\boldsymbol{U}_{i\cdot}-\boldsymbol{U}_{j\cdot}),

allowing us to
rewrite
Eq.

1
as

\min_{\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}}\gamma\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}}\|\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{E}(i,j)\cdot}^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}\|_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{U}-\boldsymbol{X}\|^{2}_{F}.

This reformulation is key to analyzing statistical properties of
Eq.

1
. As an example of convex clustering and an affinity graph,

Theorem 1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{U}+\boldsymbol{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}

3
Further Bounds via Commute Times

The discussion above motivates us to examine properties of
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}
more closely in order to better understand and bound the oracle term, leading to tighter bounds from
Theorem

1
. This section provides a conceptual interpretation of the oracle term, linking convex clustering error to graph bottlenecks concretely. We begin by relating
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}
to the commute times of a random walk over the corresponding affinity graph.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
be the symmetric adjacency matrix of an undirected graph
G=(V,\mathcal{E})
with degree matrix
\boldsymbol{D}
and Laplacian
\boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{D}-\boldsymbol{\Phi}
. Define the natural random walk over
G
as the Markov chain with transition matrix
\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}
. Then, there exists an oriented edge-incidence matrix
\boldsymbol{F}
such that

\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)\cdot}=\sqrt{\Phi_{jk}}(\boldsymbol{L}^{\dagger}_{\cdot j}-\boldsymbol{L}^{\dagger}_{\cdot k})=\frac{\sqrt{\Phi_{jk}}}{2\vol(G)}\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\boldsymbol{I}_{n}-\frac{\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}}{n}}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}(\boldsymbol{C}_{\cdot k}-\boldsymbol{C}_{\cdot j}),

where
\vol(G)=\operatorname{trace}(\boldsymbol{D})
is the volume of
G
and
\boldsymbol{C}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n\times n}
is the matrix of commute times, i.e.
C_{ij}=H_{ij}+H_{ji}
, with
H_{ij}
the expected time to travel from node
i
to node
j
in the Markov chain.

Lemma

2
follows directly from the definition of
\boldsymbol{F}
, and the ability to write terms of the Laplacian pseudoinverse as commute times; this interpretation is described in detail in
[
32
]
. The characterization in
Lemma

2
explicitly reveals how
\|\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)\cdot}\|_{2}
is a bottleneck measure. For an edge in
G
, the quantity describes the average difference in commute times across the entire graph for the nodes sharing the edge. Note the Laplacian
\boldsymbol{L}
is invariant to the choice of orientation for
\boldsymbol{F}
, so this norm is invariant as well. The representation of
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}
in terms of commute times will provide a conduit for finite-sample bounds on the mean squared error of solutions to
Eq.

1
via the oracle term. By bounding the row norms of
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}
, concentration inequalities can be used to bound the oracle term with high probability. This can occur by either explicitly calculating the commute times for an affinity graph model, or appealing to various concentration inequalities for commute times.

Lemma

2
has utility toward strengthening our error bounds for a variety of graph construction methods.
Lemma

2
allows us to write
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}
in
Eq.

4
as a function of the hitting/commute times, which behave in a more transparent way.
[
34
]

Proposition 1

(Proposition 5 from
[
34
]
)
.

Let
G
be a fixed, connected, undirected, possibly weighted graph that is not bipartite, with adjacency matrix
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
and degree matrix
\boldsymbol{D}
. Then for all
i\neq j

\bigg|\frac{H_{ij}}{\vol(G)}-\frac{1}{D_{jj}}\bigg|\leq 2\mathopen{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}{\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{2}}+1}\mathclose{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\displaystyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\textstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}}}}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}\|_{\max}}{\min_{k}D_{kk}^{2}},

(5)

where

is the second largest eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}

is the expected hitting time from node

-nearest neighbor,
\epsilon
-radius, complete weighted, and several other random graph models relevant to affinities in convex clustering

Lemma 3
.

Let
G=(V,\mathcal{E})
be a fixed, connected, undirected, possibly weighted graph that is not bipartite, with adjacency matrix
\boldsymbol{\Phi}

5
Discussion and Future Directions

This article has contributed a framework for understanding the theoretical performance of convex clustering as it relates to the affinity weights appearing in the objective (
1
). In particular, we find that the connectivity of the underlying graph is a key relevant quantity, which is formally related to clustering performance by bridging analytical techniques from the convex clustering literature to results for random graphs and commute times of random walks. There is extensive literature on both these topics
[
12
,
16
,
17
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
and further application may lead to tighter bounds or extensions of these results to related settings.

These contributions lead to a generalization of finite-sample error bounds to settings that closely align with how convex clustering and its variants are used in practice. At a high level, our theoretical results now match the intuition of convex clustering, while previous results under uniform affinity weights effected a disconnect with the empirical trends observed and well-documented by practitioners. The core quantities identified in our analysis
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{E}
and
\sum\sqrt{\Phi_{ij}}\|\boldsymbol{U}_{i\cdot}-\boldsymbol{U}_{j\cdot}\|_{2}
quantify the intuition that high within-cluster connectivity and sparse connectivity between-clusters leads to better performance. For example,
Theorem

2
implies that convex clustering can remain consistent even in the presence of many between-cluster edges, provided that within-cluster connectivity is sufficiently high. The existing analyses in the convex clustering literature assume fully-connected, uniform affinities
[
5
,
6
,
35
]
and require
p
to grow with
n
or specific qualities of the data-generating mechanism to establish consistency. The theoretical framework here could be applied to these works, generalizing their theorems while giving insight into the effect of affinities in these different settings.

In practice, the choice of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
is not independent of
\boldsymbol{E}
, and it may be possible to strengthen our results to apply in this case by leveraging dependence structure. Our current work exploits concentration inequalities for
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}
and
\boldsymbol{E}
separately, and it is not clear how to bound the entries of
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{E}
when
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}
is correlated with
\boldsymbol{E}
through
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
being a function of the data. By using results from
[
34
]
for common constructions of data-dependent
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
and applying the two sets of concentration inequalities with a union bound, the oracle term becomes bounded by a constant with high probability, similar to the case for fully uniform affinities. Some additional theoretical works that may be helpful to bridge this gap include convergence of the spectrum of the Laplacian matrix for
k
-nearest neighbor and
\epsilon
-radius graphs
[
4
]
and a variety of results related to the number of between-cluster edges, or cuts, for random graphs constructed from data
[
23
,
24
,
31
]
.

We find that to acquire the best possible clustering from a fixed dataset, one should tune over a range of affinity graphs along with tuning over
\gamma
. Compared to tuning over
\gamma
with a fixed
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
, it may be equally as valid to tune over
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
with fixed
\gamma
. Existing consistency results for
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
depending on the data from
[
14
]
suggest this, which is now further supported by the trade-off implied by
Eq.

4
: additional between-cluster edges could be offset by gains in within-cluster connectivity. In
Section

4
, we find empirically that the solution quality is highly dependent on the structure of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
for any given
\boldsymbol{X}
. Existing algorithms for calculating the solution path over
\gamma
for
Eq.

1
with fixed
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
benefit from using the previous solution as a warm start to efficiently calculate the solution path. A similar idea could be used to calculate the optimal solution for increasingly dense values of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
with
\gamma
fixed. Better yet, a state-of-the-art convex clustering package would calculate the solution path over
\gamma
and
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
jointly, giving a wide range of high quality solutions to choose from. Approaching
Eq.

1
as a parametric optimization problem of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
could lead to fast optimization over this grid of hyperparameters
[
3
]
.

\displaystyle\sup_{\|\boldsymbol{V}\|_{F}=1}\bigg\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{p}E_{ij}V_{ij}\bigg\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}

Lemma 6
.

Let
\boldsymbol{F}
be the oriented edge-incidence matrix for any unweighted connected graph

. Then

\|\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}\|_{\max}\leq 1.

Proof.

Via
Lemma

2
we have that
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)\cdot}=\boldsymbol{L}^{\dagger}_{\cdot j}-\boldsymbol{L}^{\dagger}_{\cdot k}=\boldsymbol{L}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{e}_{j}-\boldsymbol{e}_{k})
. Hence,
\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)\cdot}=\boldsymbol{e}_{j}-\boldsymbol{e}_{k}
. This gives for

,

(15)

Now assume the maximum of
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)\cdot}
occurs at index
i\notin\{j,k\}
. Then, from

15

\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)i}=\frac{1}{D_{ii}}\sum_{\ell\colon\Phi_{\ell i}\neq 0}\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)\ell}\leq\frac{1}{D_{ii}}\sum_{\ell\colon\Phi_{\ell i}\neq 0}\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)i}=\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)i}.

The assumption implies
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)\ell}=\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)i}
if
\ell
is a neighbor of vertex

. Because
G
is connected, this would imply
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)\cdot}
is a vector of constants. However,

2
implies
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)\cdot}
sums to zero, which would then require all entries of
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}
to be zero, a contradiction. Hence, the maximum entry of
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)\cdot}
must be at index

or

j
or
k
.

The effective resistance (see
[
16
]
and
[
32
]
for details) between nodes

and

is defined as

[

]
has that

is less than or equal to the size of the path between the two nodes. However, vertices
j
and

have an edge between them, so

. These are the maximum and minimum elements of
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)\cdot}
so
|\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)i}-\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)\ell}|\leq 1
for all pairs of nodes. Now assume
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)i}>1
for some index
i
. Since,
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)\cdot}
sums to 0, then
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)i}=-\sum_{\ell\neq i}\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)i}
. However, this implies negative numbers in
\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)\cdot}
and some
\ell
where
|\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)i}-\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)\ell}|>1
. This contradiction can be repeated in the other direction, showing
|\boldsymbol{F}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{E}(j,k)i}|\leq 1
for all
i
and all
j,k
sharing an edge.
∎
