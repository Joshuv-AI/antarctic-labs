Knowledge, Attitudes and Dispensing Practices Regarding Antimicrobial Resistance and Stewardship: A comparative national assessment among primary healthcare pharmacists in Oman
Authors: Mohammed Al Ghafari, Meher Rizvi, Hana Al Ghusaini, Zaima Al Rawahi, Wafa Al Balushi, Manal Al Ghafri, Hamed Al-Qanubi, Zuhair Al Salti, Idris Al Jumaa, Muhammed Rafiq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectives:Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is driven by inappropriate antibiotic use. This study aimed to assess the knowledge, attitudes and dispensing practices (KAP) regarding AMR and antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) among primary healthcare (PHC) pharmacists and assistant pharmacists in Oman. Furthermore, the study sought to compare these parameters across facilities with historically high versus standard antibiotic consumption to identify structural determinants of AMS.Methods:This national cross-sectional study was conducted among PHC pharmacy staff in February 2025. KAP were assessed via a structured electronic questionnaire. Data were analysed using generalized estimating equation models to identify KAP predictors while accounting for facility-level clustering.Results:A total of 333 PHC pharmacy staff were included in this study. Overall, 72.1% of participants had adequate knowledge and 78.7% showed favourable attitudes; however, only 56.2% demonstrated good practice. Knowledge was adequate in only 20.8% of staff at high-consumption centres compared to 76.1% at standard centres (P <0.001). Multivariable analysis confirmed that working in a high-consumption centre was strongly associated with reduced odds of adequate knowledge (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 0.08, 95% confidence interval: 0.05–0.14). While prior AMR training improved the odds of good practice (aOR = 2.04), this benefit was significantly diminished in high-consumption settings (interaction P = 0.030).Conclusion:Although Omani PHC pharmacy staff exhibit positive attitudes, significant knowledge gaps and practical barriers exist, particularly in high-consumption facilities. Interventions must move beyond generic training to include system-level strategies, such as integrating AMS tools within electronic health records, to address structural challenges in high-risk settings.

1. Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a critical global health threat, directly causing 1.14 million deaths in 2021 and posing a substantial economic burden with projected global gross domestic product losses of up to US$3.4 trillion by 2030.[12] A primary driver of AMR is the inappropriate use of antibiotics, particularly in primary healthcare (PHC), where 30–50% of outpatient prescriptions are often unnecessary.[34] While the World Health Organization's (WHO) AWaRe classification provides a framework for appropriate use, many nations struggle to meet the target of ≥70% “access” antibiotic consumption.[56]

The Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries faces unique challenges, including high rates of multi-drug resistant pathogens and frequent inappropriate prescribing in outpatient settings.[7] Oman has launched its National Action Plan (NAP) in 2015 and co-hosting the 3rd Global High-Level Ministerial Conference on AMR in 2022.[89] Despite these initiatives, suboptimal antibiotic use persists. Recent Omani data indicate that over 92% of PHC antibiotic prescriptions for respiratory infections may be inappropriate, while tertiary care studies show non-adherence to guidelines in up to 63% of adult cases.[1011]

PHC serves as a critical battleground for antimicrobial stewardship (AMS). Pharmacists, as frontline providers and the final checkpoint in the medication chain, are pivotal to these efforts.[12] In Oman, the Ministry of Health (MoH) defines the pharmacist as a “medication expert” with a legal mandate to ensure the safety and quality of medicines.[13] This gatekeeping function is codified in the national pharmacy practice guideline, which mandates prospective review of all antibiotic prescriptions for appropriateness before dispensing.

Evidence suggests that pharmacist-led interventions—including prescription review and patient counselling—significantly improve prescribing appropriateness.[14] Under national guidelines, pharmacists are required to monitor drug interactions, contraindications and allergies, and are formally tasked with documenting pharmaceutical interventions and participating in clinical rounds to affect prescribing outcomes. Assistant pharmacists support these processes under direct supervision, focusing on safe dispensing and patient guidance.[13] However, the success of these interventions depends on the pharmacists’ knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) regarding AMR.

While physician prescribing behaviours in Oman have been investigated, a striking gap exists concerning the role of the pharmacy workforce. International studies suggest that even when pharmacists recognise AMR as a threat, knowledge-practice gaps remain common in Middle Eastern settings.[15] Understanding these gaps within Oman is essential to operationalise national AMS strategies and meet global targets.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess the KAP concerning AMR and AMS of pharmacists and assistant pharmacists in Oman's PHC system. Uniquely, this study compared these factors across centres with historically high versus standard antibiotic consumption rates to identify structural and individual determinants of AMS. These findings would aid in targeted interventions to strengthen Oman's regional and global AMR containment efforts.

2. Methods

This national cross-sectional study was carried out within MoH PHC institutions in February 2025. To ensure a comprehensive analysis of different prescribing environments, facilities were stratified based on the MoH's national benchmark; 9 centres were purposively selected due to historically high antibiotic prescribing rates, defined as ≥27% of total prescriptions as per the 2022 clinical audit issued by the Directorate of Rational Use of Medicine.[16] These centres included Al Musanaah (South Al-Batinah), Al Uwafiya and Wadi Mahram (Al-Dakhliyah), Wadi Dimaa (North Al-Sharqiya) and Sur, Al Ayja, Jaalan Bani Bu Hassan, Al Ruwais and Al Wafi (South Al-Sharqiya). Representing a diverse range of urban and rural PHC settings, these facilities were compared against the remaining centres represented standard prescribing norms, maintaining the national target of <27%. This stratification allowed for a robust comparative assessment of how facility-level consumption patterns influence individual knowledge, attitudes and dispensing practices.

The target population comprised all pharmacists and assistant pharmacists actively practicing in MoH PHC facilities (N = 1,171). The minimum required sample size was calculated at 290 using a standard formula for estimating a population proportion (95% CI, 5% margin of error and P = 0.5). A multi-stage sampling approach was adopted to ensure national representation across all 11 governorates. All eligible professionals in the purposively selected high-prescribing centres were invited to participate. Simultaneously, staff from standard-prescribing centres were recruited to ensure adequate power for comparative analysis. Inclusion required current employment and at least 6 months of experience in the current role to ensure familiarity with institutional dispensing protocols.

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered electronic questionnaire distributed via secure online platforms from February 1 to February 28, 2025 [Supplementary Appendix 1]. The instrument was organised into 4 distinct domains: (1) respondent demographics, including age, sex, nationality and years of professional experience; (2) assessment of knowledge through items focused on awareness of AMR, WHO AWaRe classifications and familiarity with national guidelines, scored on a 100% scale; (3) evaluation of attitudes using a 5-point Likert scale to gauge perceptions of patient pressure, confidence in guideline adherence and the perceived threat of AMR; and (4) dispensing practices were measured via a weighted index (0–1). This index was constructed as a normalized composite of items related to patient counseling, adherence to national guidelines, and the use of institutional dispensing protocols. Specifically, each positive practice behavior was scored and the total was divided by the maximum possible score to achieve a value between 0 (no adherence) and 1 (full adherence). The questionnaire underwent a rigorous validation process to ensure internal and construct validity. Content validity was established by a panel of 8 domain specialists, comprising clinical pharmacists and senior family physicians. Each item was evaluated on a 4-point relevance scale and rated as ‘essential’ or ‘not essential’ for computing the content validity ratio. Item-level CVI (I-CVI) values ranged from 0.75 to 1.00 across all 39 items, with 33 items achieving perfect expert agreement (I-CVI = 1.00). Items with I-CVI below 1.00 (Q8, Q19, Q22, Q30, Q33 and Q37) were reviewed and retained or revised based on panel consensus, with all values meeting or exceeding the minimum acceptable threshold of 0.75 for an 8-member panel. At the scale level, the S-CVI/Ave was 0.978 and the S-CVI/UA was 0.846, both surpassing the recommended thresholds of ≥0.90 and ≥0.80, respectively, confirming strong overall content validity of the instrument. Because the KAP scales utilises categorical and Likert-type items, reliability was assessed using both classical and ordinal metrics to provide a robust evaluation of internal consistency. The Knowledge domain (21 items) demonstrated high reliability with an ordinal alpha of 0.795 and a total omega of 0.901. The Attitudes scale (overall) showed strong internal consistency (ordinal alpha = 0.749; total omega = 0.831). For the Practices index, which is a broad, formative-leaning construct, the total omega was high at 0.865, confirming the reliability of the composite score used for categorisation. These psychometric properties validate the thresholds used to define ‘adequate’ knowledge (≥70%) and ‘good’ practice (≥0.75). To ensure clarity, a pilot study (n = 15) was conducted; however, these data were excluded from the final analysis.

Data were analysed using R software, Version 4.1.1 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). Adequate thresholds were predefined as knowledge ≥70% (aligning with academic benchmarks), favourable attitude ≥3.5 (identifying positive leanings) and good practice ≥0.75 (representing the upper quartile of adherence). Univariate comparisons between facility types used Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous scores and Fisher's exact or chi-square tests for categorical outcomes. Multivariable generalized estimating equation (GEE) models (logit link, exchangeable correlation structure) were employed to identify predictors of adequate KAP while accounting for facility-level clustering. Covariates included facility type, prior AMS training and demographics. A facility type × training interaction was retained where significant (P <0.05). Sensitivity analyses utilised inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to ensure balance across facility types. All analyses were two-sided with alpha = 0.05.

3. Results

A total of 333 pharmacists and assistant pharmacists participated in this study. Participants had a mean age of 37.5 ± 7.1 years and an average of 13.0 ± 7.5 years of healthcare experience. The majority were female (71.8%) and Omani nationals (83.2%). In terms of roles, 55.6% were pharmacists and 44.4% were assistant pharmacists. Overall, 37.5% (n = 125) reported prior formal training on AMS or appropriate prescribing practices. On comparing participants from high-consumption centres (n = 24) and standard-consumption facilities (n = 309), no significant differences were observed in terms of age, years of experience, gender, professional roles or prior AMS training. Nationality differed significantly as all participants at high-consumption centres were Omani nationals (100%) compared with 81.9% at standard-consumption facilities (P = 0.02) [Table 1].

The mean knowledge score was 73.6% ± 11.9% and 72.1% of participants (n = 240) achieved adequate knowledge (≥70%). Attitudes were generally favorable, with a mean of 3.74 ± 0.40 on a 5-point scale; 78.7% (n = 262) scored ≥3.5. The mean practice index was 0.73 ± 0.20 on a 0–1 scale and 56.2% (n = 187) met the threshold for good practice (≥0.75). Comparisons by facility type indicated significant differences in knowledge. Pharmacists at high-consumption centres had lower knowledge scores than those at standard-consumption facilities (61.0% ± 9.6% versus 74.5% ± 11.5%; P <0.001) and fewer reached adequate knowledge (20.8% versus 76.1%; P <0.001). Attitudes were uniformly favourable at high-consumption centres compared with standard-consumption facilities (100% versus 77.0%; P = 0.017); mean attitude scores were slightly higher at high-consumption centres (3.86 ± 0.22 versus 3.73 ± 0.41; P = 0.097). Practice scores were similar across groups (0.76 ± 0.17 versus 0.72 ± 0.20; P = 0.483), as was the prevalence of good practice (62.5% versus 55.7%; P = 0.662) [Table 1].

Kruskal–Wallis tests assessed governorate-level variation in KAP. Knowledge scores ranged from 62% to 90%, with borderline significance (χ2[10] = 16.8; P = 0.079). No significant differences were observed for attitudes (χ2 = 13.4; P = 0.202) or practices (χ2 = 10.7; P = 0.378). The marginal knowledge heterogeneity reflects national homogeneity, as the sample (N = 333) exceeded the required 290, ensuring adequate power. Multivariable GEE and IPTW models identified facility-level consumption status—not geographic governorate—as the primary predictor of knowledge gaps (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 0.08; P <0.001).

Working at a high-consumption centre was associated with lower odds of adequate knowledge (aOR = 0.08, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.05–0.14; P <0.001). Prior AMS training increased the odds of adequate knowledge (aOR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.09–3.52; P = 0.024), while male pharmacists had lower odds than females (aOR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.21–0.80; P = 0.009). An interaction between facility type and training indicated that the benefit of training was diminished at high-consumption centres, with the predicted probability of adequate knowledge dropping from 82.5% at standard-consumption facilities to 9.5% [Table 2].

Continuous KAP outcomes analysed using linear mixed models aligned with these findings. High-consumption centre status was associated with a 14.9-point lower knowledge score (β =-$14.9, 95% CI: -19.9 to -10.0; P <0.001). 4. Discussion

This study provides the first system-wide assessment of AMS KAP among pharmacists and assistant pharmacists in Oman's PHC. Overall performance was encouraging but uneven, with profound facility-level disparities in knowledge that paradoxically did not extend to practice behaviours, weak inter-domain correlations and uniformly positive attitudes even in the poorest-performing facilities—patterns that collectively signal structural implementation barriers rather than individual knowledge or motivation deficits.

AMS research in the Middle East has traditionally focused on either tertiary hospital settings or private community pharmacies. In hospitals, success is often driven by multidisciplinary Infectious Diseases (ID) teams and resource-intensive interventions. Conversely, studies in community pharmacies—such as those in Saudi Arabia, Jordan and Palestine—frequently identify commercial interests and legislative gaps as the primary hurdles to good practice.[171819] The current study fills a critical void by evaluating the public PHC sector, where Omani pharmacy staff operate within a non-profit, highly regulated environment. Unlike community settings where profit might influence dispensing, the practice gaps identified here (56.2% good practice) are likely driven by structural bottlenecks, such as high patient volume and limited guideline accessibility (28.2%). This suggests that while regional community pharmacists require better regulation, Omani PHC staff—who already exhibit positive attitudes—require ‘active’ system-level enablers such as electronic health record (EHR)-integrated decision support tools to translate their knowledge into clinical action.

In the current study, most respondents achieved adequate knowledge (72.1%) and the mean knowledge score was 73.6 ± 11.9%, indicating solid baseline understanding of core AMS concepts. This aligns with regional studies reporting strong theoretical knowledge among healthcare professionals in the Middle East.[2021] Yet, facility-level disparities were pronounced; pharmacists in high-consumption centres had substantially lower knowledge (mean of 61.0 ± 9.6% versus 74.5 ± 11.5% at standard-consumption facilities) and were far less likely to meet the ≥70% adequacy threshold (20.8% versus 76.1%). These gaps persisted after adjustment, with high-consumption centre status associated with markedly lower odds of adequate knowledge (aOR = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.05–0.14). Concrete system-level barriers identified in this study include low availability of local prescribing guidelines (reported by only 28.2% of all respondents) and limited accessibility among those who have them (41.5%), challenges that may be amplified in high-pressure, high-volume settings. Additionally, knowledge gaps related to environmental consequences of antimicrobial misuse remain concerning, given Oman's commitment to a One Health approach under its NAP.[722] Furthermore, these gaps are particularly salient as AMR is not confined to clinical settings; for instance, the potential for antibiotic residues in the food chain—such as in poultry or livestock—may further exacerbate selection pressure for resistant pathogens in the community. This concern is particularly relevant in the Gulf region, where studies have detected antibiotic residues exceeding maximum residue limits in poultry products across GCC markets.[23] In Oman specifically, the marine and aquatic environment has been identified as a potential reservoir for AMR genes, with the Gulf's warm waters facilitating horizontal gene transfer.[7] The Omani NAP 2024–2030 explicitly recognises the need to address AMR across human, animal and environmental sectors, making pharmacist awareness of these pathways a national priority. This underscores the necessity for pharmacists to understand their role within the broader One Health framework, as clinical over-prescription combined with environmental exposure creates a multi-faceted threat to public health.[24] Linear models confirmed this facility-level disparity, with high-consumption centre status associated with a 14.9-point deficit in mean knowledge scores (β =-$14.9, 95% CI: -19.9 to -10.0; P <0.001), equivalent to approximately 1.3 standard deviations.

4.1. Attitudes, practices and the ‘know–do’ gap

Attitudes were generally favourable in the sample (mean of 3.74 ± 0.40; 78.7% ≥3.5). Paradoxically, attitudes were universally favourable at high-consumption centres (100% ≥3.5) despite these facilities having the lowest knowledge scores—a dissociation suggesting that positive attitudes alone are insufficient to drive knowledge acquisition or practice improvement in challenging work environments. Practice levels were comparable between facility groups (0.76 ± 0.17 versus 0.72 ± 0.20; good practice 62.5% versus 55.7%) and inter-domain correlations were modest (knowledge with practice ρ = 0.15; attitudes with practice ρ = 0.32), underscoring a persistent ‘know–do’ gap. The particularly weak correlation between knowledge and practice (ρ = 0.15) indicates that theoretical understanding accounts for less than 3% of the variance in practice behaviours, reinforcing that knowledge deficits are not the primary bottleneck. Strikingly, facility type showed no significant association with good practice in adjusted models (aOR = 1.43, 95% CI: 0.70–2.92; P = 0.32), despite a 3.6-fold difference in knowledge adequacy between high-consumption and other facilities. This decoupling demonstrates that practice behaviours are largely determined by structural factors—workflow constraints, dispensing protocols and institutional norms—rather than individual knowledge levels. This disconnect mirrors findings from other GCC studies and points to the need for interventions that link attitudes to actionable behaviours.[252627]

4.2. Determinants and equity considerations

In multivariable models, prior AMS training emerged as the only significant predictor of good practice (aOR = 2.04, 95% CI: 1.36–3.07; P <0.001), with a modest but significant effect on continuous practice scores (β = 0.06; P = 0.008). Notably, facility type, gender, professional role and years of experience showed no independent association with practice after adjustment. This pattern suggests that while training can shift practice behaviours, its effectiveness depends heavily on the context in which pharmacists work, as evidenced by the dramatic attenuation of training benefits in high-consumption centres (interaction P = 0.030).

Training emerged as a consistent lever, prior AMS training nearly doubled the odds of adequate knowledge (aOR = 1.96) and good practice (aOR = 2.04), and was associated with a small but significant increase in the practice index (β = 0.06). However, the benefit of training was sharply attenuated in high-consumption centres (predicted probability of adequate knowledge dropping from 82.5% in standard-consumption facilities to 9.5% in high-consumption centres), indicating that educational inputs must be coupled with implementation supports in these settings. This near-complete erosion of training benefits in high-consumption settings (probability dropping from 82.5% to 9.5%) suggests that passive knowledge transfer is overwhelmed by competing demands and inadequate implementation infrastructure in these facilities, underscoring the futility of education-only approaches where system supports are absent. Male pharmacists had significantly lower odds of achieving adequate knowledge compared to females (aOR = 0.41). Notably, training showed selective effectiveness; it significantly improved knowledge and practice but had no significant effect on attitudes (aOR = 1.16, 95% CI: 0.73–1.84; P = 0.526), suggesting that attitudinal factors may be more influenced by workplace culture and peer norms than by formal education. While GCC studies rarely report sex-disaggregated knowledge outcomes, international evidence suggests similar patterns, with male pharmacy staff showing less favourable attitudes toward AMS in multi-country surveys.[28] This highlights the need for gender-sensitive training and mentorship strategies, in line with WHO recommendations for equity in AMR programs.[29]

4.3. Systemic barriers and professional dynamics

System-level barriers emerged as critical challenges. Only 28.2% of respondents (n = 94) reported the presence of local upper respiratory tract infection prescribing guidelines, and among those, just 41.5% (n = 39) found these guidelines easily accessible. This gap between guideline existence and accessibility likely contributes to persistent inappropriate prescribing in primary healthcare settings, despite generally high knowledge levels.[1030] The absence of practical, easily accessible guidance makes it difficult for pharmacists to consistently apply AMS principles in high-volume environments, particularly when facing patient pressure.[31] The confluence of low guideline availability and high patient volume likely creates a vicious cycle in high-consumption settings, where time-pressured pharmacists lack easy access to evidence-based protocols and may default to broader-spectrum or more familiar agents.

Professional role showed no significant association with practice outcomes in adjusted models (aOR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.55–1.48; P = 0.676), suggesting that pharmacists and assistant pharmacists face similar implementation challenges despite differences in formal training. This finding highlights that practice behaviours in primary care settings are shaped more by institutional protocols and daily workflows than by differences in professional credentials. Interventions should therefore target the shared practice environment rather than focusing exclusively on role-specific training.

4.4. Implications for Omani policy and practice

Oman's strong commitment to AMR control through its NAP 2024–2030 and national AMS guidelines provides an opportunity to operationalise stewardship at the primary care level. These national efforts are supported by the transformative blueprint of Oman Vision 2040, which aims to build a leading healthcare system following international standards.[32] Bridging the gap between guideline availability and accessibility should be a priority. Practical measures include embedding AWaRe-based quick-reference tools and mandatory counselling checklists within the EHR system (Al Shifa 3+), ensuring completion before dispensing. Educational strategies should move beyond passive dissemination toward interactive, case-based modules that link individual dispensing decisions to population-level AMR outcomes using real-time EHR data. Given the near-complete attenuation of training effectiveness in high-consumption centres, these facilities should be prioritised for system-level interventions—including automated clinical decision support within the EHR, real-time prescription auditing and pharmacist-led stewardship teams—before investing in additional educational programmes. Finally, quarterly AMS dashboards aligned with Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System indicators can provide feedback on adherence and support continuous quality improvement.

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. The cross-sectional design, while providing a snapshot of current KAP levels, precludes the assessment of causality. The reliance on self-reported data is susceptible to social desirability bias, which may have led to an overestimation of positive practices. Furthermore, the study was conducted within the MoH's PHC system and the findings may not be generalisable to the private healthcare sector. While the KAP scales demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (knowledge ordinal α = 0.795; practice α = 0.726), they capture self-reported perceptions rather than observed behaviours or clinical outcomes. Additionally, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference; therefore, it was not possible to determine whether high antibiotic consumption drives lower knowledge or whether pre-existing knowledge deficits contribute to inappropriate prescribing patterns. Furthermore, a notable limitation is the sample size imbalance between high-consumption (n = 24) and standard-consumption (n = 309) facilities. This imbalance reflects the epidemiological reality that only 9 of Oman's 274 PHC centres exceeded the national antibiotic consumption threshold; the current sample represents a near-census of all pharmacy staff in these facilities. Nonetheless, small subgroup sizes may reduce the precision of interaction estimates and limit the generalisability of subgroup-specific findings. This was mitigated through GEE models (which accommodate unbalanced clusters), IPTW with stabilised weights trimmed at the 1st–99th percentile and by reporting confidence intervals alongside point estimates to convey estimation uncertainty [Supplementary Table 1]. Sensitivity analyses across 3 analytical approaches (GEE, IPTW-GEE, IPTW-generalised linear model with CR2 cluster-robust standard errors) yielded concordant results, confirming robustness [Supplementary Table 2].

5. Conclusion

This study establishes a critical baseline for AMS implementation in Oman's primary healthcare system. While overall knowledge and attitudes are promising, substantial gaps in practice and facility-level disparities demand urgent attention. Closing these gaps requires a dual approach; (1) strengthening system-level enablers such as integrated EHR tools and (2) tailoring educational interventions to address attitudinal and behavioural barriers. Moreover, clinical stewardship must be integrated into a broader One Health strategy that addresses antibiotic use across the human, animal and environmental sectors to comprehensively mitigate the AMR burden in Oman. Specifically, future AMS training for PHC pharmacists should include modules on the One Health dimension of AMR—covering antibiotic residues in food production, environmental dissemination of resistant genes and the pharmacist's role in patient counselling regarding non-prescription antibiotic exposure through diet and the environment. Future research should include longitudinal studies, qualitative exploration of behavioural drivers and implementation trials of targeted interventions. These efforts will accelerate Oman's progress toward its national AMR goals and offer a scalable model for AMS advancement across the GCC region.
