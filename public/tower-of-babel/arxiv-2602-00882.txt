On interpolation in Carathéodory hyperbolic domains
Anindya Biswas

Abstract
. We study the relation between Pick bodies on Carathéodory hyperbolic domains and contractions on finite dimensional Hilbert spaces. We give a condition sufficient to realize Pick bodies on Carathéodory hyperbolic domains as a Pick body on the open unit disc.

1.
Introduction and statements of the results

Suppose that
\Omega
is a Carathéodory hyperbolic domain in the complex space
\mathbb{C}^{m}
and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
are mutually distinct points in
\Omega
. The object of our study is the
Pick body
(also known as the
interpolation body
) which is given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=\{(f(z_{1}),\ldots,f(z_{n})):f\in\mathcal{O}(\Omega,\overline{\mathbb{D}})\},

where
\mathcal{O}(\Omega,\overline{\mathbb{D}})
is the space of all holomorphic functions from
\Omega
to the closure of the open unit disc
\mathbb{D}
. We will often abbreviate
n
-tuples
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
as
\underline{x}
when there is no scope of confusion. The study of Pick bodies, was initiated by Cole, Lewis, and Wermer (
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
) to understand the Pick interpolation problem which asks for a necessary and sufficient condition for a given interpolation problem
\Omega\ni z_{j}\mapsto w_{j}\in\overline{\mathbb{D}},j=1,\ldots,n,
to have a solution in
\mathcal{O}(\Omega,\overline{\mathbb{D}})
. When
\Omega=\mathbb{D}
, the problem has a well understood solution: An interpolation problem
\mathbb{D}\ni\lambda_{j}\mapsto w_{j}\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}
has a solution in
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{D},\overline{\mathbb{D}})
if and only if the matrix

(1.1)

\displaystyle\Bigg(\frac{1-w_{i}\overline{w_{j}}}{1-\lambda_{i}\overline{\lambda_{j}}}\Bigg)_{i,j}

is positive semidefinite. Moreover, the solution is unique if and only if the rank of the matrix (
1.1
) is less than
n
, and in that case the solution a finite Blaschke product whose degree equals the rank (
[
3
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
17
]
, for a simpler proof see
[
15
]
). Thus

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}}(\underline{\lambda})=\Big\{(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:\Big(\frac{1-w_{i}\overline{w_{j}}}{1-\lambda_{i}\overline{\lambda_{j}}}\Big)_{i,j}\geq\mathbf{0}\Big\}.

Since
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
are mutually distinct, the matrix
\Big(\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{i}\overline{\lambda_{j}}}\Big)_{i,j}
is positive definite and we can find a map
k:\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
\langle k(\lambda_{j}),k(\lambda_{i})\rangle=\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{i}\overline{\lambda_{j}}}
. This gives rise to an inner product
\langle.,.\rangle_{\underline{\lambda}}
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
which is given by

\displaystyle\langle(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}),(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})\rangle_{\underline{\lambda}}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}t_{i}\overline{s_{j}}\langle k(\lambda_{i}),k(\lambda_{j})\rangle.

For
\underline{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, if we define
T_{\underline{w}}(k(\lambda_{j}))=\overline{w_{j}}k(\lambda_{j})
, then we find that
\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}}(\underline{\lambda})
can be identified with
\{\underline{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:||T_{\underline{w}}||\leq 1\}
, that is,
\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}}(\underline{\lambda})
is the closed unit ball of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, equipped with some norm (in our case an operator norm, see
[
18
]
). A generalization of this set up is the following: Consider a positive definite
n\times n
matrix
K=(K(i,j))_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
and define

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{K}=\big\{\underline{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:\big((1-w_{i}\overline{w_{j}})K(i,j)\big)\geq\mathbf{0}\big\}.

For the case of
\mathbb{D}
, we see that there is a single positive definite matrix
K
satisfying
\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}}(\underline{\lambda})=\mathscr{D}_{K}
. Sometimes, we express
K
as a
\mathbb{C}
-valued function defined on
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}\times\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}
to emphasize the involvement of the points
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}
and in that case we will write
\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z})
instead of
\mathscr{D}_{K}
.

When
\Omega
is the bidisc
\mathbb{D}^{2}
, the article
[
11
]
explores the question of finding a single matrix
K
so that
\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}^{2}}(\underline{z})
can be expressed as
\mathscr{D}_{K}
. In this article, we extend some of those results for any Carathéodory hyperbolic domain. Our first result in this direction is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any Carathéodory hyperbolic domain
\Omega
and
n
distinct points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
in
\Omega
, there is a collection
\mathcal{K}_{\Omega}
of positive definite
n\times n
matrices such that

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})=\bigcap_{K\in\mathcal{K}_{\Omega}}\mathscr{D}_{K}.

Given
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\Omega
and a map
\phi\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{D},\Omega)
sending
\lambda_{j}
to
z_{j}
, it is known that
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})=\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}}(\underline{\lambda})
if and only if
\phi
is a Carathéodory geodesic (
[
6
]
). Therefore, having
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})=\mathscr{D}_{K}
for a single matrix
K
is a necessary condition for the existence of a Carathéodory geodesic containing all
z_{j}
. In the next result, we give a condition under which
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})
is expressible as
\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})
(cf. Theorem 3. and Lemma 4.1. in
[
11
]
).

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}
are three distinct points in a Carathéodory hyperbolic domain
\Omega
and
\underline{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})\in\partial\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})\cap\mathbb{D}^{3}
is a point such that the following hold:

(1)

There is
3\times 3
positiive definite matrix
K
such that
\mathscr{D}_{K}=\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})
.

(2)

For at least two pairs
(i,j),1\leq i<j\leq 3
,
(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})\in\partial\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(z_{i},z_{j})
.

Then
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
and
\alpha_{3}
are distinct and
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})=\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}}(\underline{\alpha})
.

For
\mathbb{D}^{2}
, the coordinate functions play a fundamental role in the study of
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{D}^{2},\overline{\mathbb{D}})
. Apart from the fact that these functions are essential for the Agler decomposition (
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
), a building block for the study of
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{D}^{2},\overline{\mathbb{D}})
, they are the unique Carathéodory extremals and contains sufficient data to identify
\mathbb{D}^{2}
(
[
1
]
). The significance of this extremality of certain functions with respect to the points
z_{j}
is implicit in Cole and Wermer’s work on
\mathbb{D}^{2}
(
[
11
]
). Also the fact that for
\mathbb{D}^{2}
there are only two such functions has an important role in that development (cf. the proof of Theorem 3. in
[
11
]
). But, for general domains, neither such convenient Agler decomposition exists, nor the Carathéodory extremals are well understood yet. However, it is possible to find meaningful results in the context of Pick bodies using the Carathéodory pseudodistance, the definition of which and a generalization of it are the following.

Definition
.

Suppose that
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
are points in a domain
\Omega
.

(1)

The Carathéodory pseudodistance between
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
is defined by

\displaystyle c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{1},z_{2})=sup\{m(f(z_{1}),f(z_{2}))=\Big|\frac{f(z_{1})-f(z_{2})}{1-\overline{f(z_{1})}f(z_{2})}\Big|:f\in\mathcal{O}(\Omega,\mathbb{D})\}.

(2)

The generalized Carathéodory function (also called the generalized Möbius function, see
[
14
]
) is defined by

\displaystyle c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{1};z_{2},\ldots,z_{n})=sup\{|f(z_{1})|:f\in\mathcal{O}(\Omega,\mathbb{D}),f(z_{2})=\ldots=f(z_{n})=0\}.

Before stating the next results, we need a few more definitions.

Definition
.

Suppose that
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
are mutually distinct points in a domain
\Omega
and
K:\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}\times\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a function such that
\big(K(z_{i},z_{j})\big)_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
is a positive definite matrix. Let
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})
be a
k
-tuple of integers such that
1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n
.

(1)

By
\pi_{I}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{k}
we denote the mapping
\underline{z}\mapsto\underline{z}_{I}=(z_{i_{1}},\ldots,z_{i_{k}})
.

(2)

For
\underline{z}\in\Omega^{n}
, we define
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z}_{I})=\{(f(z_{i_{1}}),\ldots,f(z_{i_{k}})):f\in\mathcal{O}(\Omega,\overline{\mathbb{D}})\}
.

(3)

The set
\{(w_{1},\ldots,w_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{k}:\Big((1-w_{l}\overline{w_{m}})K(z_{i_{l}},z_{i_{m}})\Big)\geq\mathbf{0}\}
will be denoted by
\mathscr{D}_{K}(z_{i_{1}},\ldots,z_{i_{k}})=\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z}_{I})
.

It is easy to verify that when
\Omega
is Carathéodory hyperbolic,
\pi_{I}(\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z}))=\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z}_{I})
for any
I
as above. One way to see this is to observe that a point of
\partial\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z}_{I})
(the boundary of a set
X
will be denoted by
\partial X
) can not come from the components of an interior point of
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})
. Note that if
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})=\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}}(\underline{\lambda})
, then for any
k
-tuple
I
,
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z}_{I})=\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}}(\underline{\lambda}_{I})
. Observing this property, we now define the following.

Definition
.

A function
K:\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}\times\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is said to be an
n
-extremal kernel (or, an extremal kernel if
n
is clear from the context) if the matrix
(K(z_{i},z_{j}))_{1\leq i,j\leq n}

is positive definite and for each
k
-tuple
I
and each
\underline{w}^{\prime}\in\partial\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z}_{I})
, there is a
\underline{w}\in\partial\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z})
such that
\pi_{I}(\underline{w})=\underline{w}^{\prime}
.

Example
.

(1)

Suppose that
(K(i,j))_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
is a diagonal
n\times n
matrix with positive diagonal entries. It is easy to see that
\mathscr{D}_{K}
is the closed unit polydisc in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Since a point lies on the boundary of a polydisc if and only if at least one of its component has modulus one, we see that
K
is an extremal kernel.

(2)

Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
be
n
distinct points in the open unit disc
\mathbb{D}
. Then the function
K
defined on
\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}\times\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
by
K(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})=\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{i}\overline{\lambda_{j}}}
gives an extremal kernel. If we are given a
k
-tuple
I
, then for any
\underline{w}^{\prime}\in\partial\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z}_{I})
, there is a unique Blaschke product of degree at most
k-1
that interpolates
\underline{\lambda}_{I}\mapsto\underline{w}^{\prime}
(see
[
6
]
). The values of this Blaschke product at other
\lambda_{j}
give us the corresponding point which is projected by
\pi_{I}
onto
\underline{w}^{\prime}

We later show that if
\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z})
is a Pick body
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})
, then the entries of
K
are determined up to unimodular constants. Now, with the notion of extremal kernels, we have two theorems. The first one gives an estimate for the generalized Carathéodory function.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}
be three distinct points in a Carathéodory hyperbolic domain
\Omega
, and
K
a
3
-extremal kernel defined on
\{z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}\}
satisfying
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})=\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z})
. If
\underline{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})\in\partial\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})\cap\mathbb{D}^{3}
satisfies
m(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})=c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{1},z_{2})
, then

\displaystyle c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{3};z_{1},z_{2})\geq max\Biggl\{\sqrt{\frac{c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{j},z_{3})^{2}-m(\alpha_{j},\alpha_{3})^{2}}{1-m(\alpha_{j},\alpha_{3})^{2}}}:j=1,2\Biggr\}.

The following theorem gives a sufficient condition for a
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})
to be expressible as
\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}}(\underline{\lambda})
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose that
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
are mutually distinct points in a Carathéodory hyperbolic domain
\Omega
and
K
is an extremal kernel on
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}
with
K(z_{i},z_{i})=1
for all
i
. If
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})=\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z})
and there is an
\underline{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})
such that
m(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{j})=c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{1},z_{j})
for
j=2,\ldots,n
, then
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})=\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}}(\underline{\alpha})
.

Proofs of the results

First we prove Theorem
1.1
. Before going into the proof we recall the notion of Schur-Agler class generated by a collection of test function (
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
13
]
). Suppose we are given a collection
\Psi
of functions in
\mathcal{O}(\Omega,\mathbb{D})
, and we consider kernels
\Gamma:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that

\big((1-\psi(z),\overline{\psi(w)})\Gamma(z,w)\big)_{z,w\in\Omega}\geq\mathbf{0}\,\,\text{for all}\,\,\psi\in\Psi.

The set of all such
\Gamma
is called the set of admissible kernels and is denoted by
\mathcal{K}_{\Psi}
.
The
\Psi
-Schur-Agler class is the collection of all functions
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that

\big((1-f(z),\overline{f(w)})\Gamma(z,w)\big)_{z,w\in\Omega}\geq\mathbf{0}\,\,\text{for all}\,\,\Gamma\in\mathcal{K}_{\Psi}.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We fix an arbitrary point
z_{0}\in\Omega
different from
z_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq n
, and consider
\Psi=\{f\in\mathcal{O}(\Omega,\mathbb{D}):f(z_{0})=0\}
. By Proposition
2.5
in
[
7
]
, the Schur-Agler class generated by
\Psi
is precisely the space
\mathcal{O}(\Omega,\overline{\mathbb{D}})
. Let the set of all
\Psi
-admissible kernels be denoted by
\mathcal{K}_{\Psi}
. For
\Gamma\in\mathcal{K}_{\Psi}
, if
K_{\Gamma}=\Gamma|_{\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}\times\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}}
is positive semidefinite, we consider the collection
\{K_{\Gamma}+\frac{1}{m}I_{n}:m\geq 1\}
. Clearly each of these matrices is positive definite. If
K_{\Gamma}
is positive definite, we consider the singleton set
\{K_{\Gamma}\}
. Let
\mathcal{K}_{\Omega}
be the union of the collections we just constructed.

Now we show that
\mathcal{K}_{\Omega}
satisfies (
1.3
). Let
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in\bigcap_{K\in\mathcal{K}_{\Omega}}\mathscr{D}_{K}
and
\Gamma\in\mathcal{K}_{\Psi}
. If
K_{\Gamma}
is positive definite, then it is in
K_{\Omega}
and we have

\displaystyle\big((1-w_{i}\overline{w_{j}})K_{\Gamma}(z_{i},z_{j})\big)=\big((1-w_{i}\overline{w_{j}})\Gamma(z_{i},z_{j})\big)\geq\mathbf{0}.

If
K_{\Gamma}
is positive semidefinite, then
K_{\Gamma}+\frac{1}{m}I_{n}\in\mathcal{K}_{\Omega}
for all
m\geq 1
. Hence, for all
m
we have

\displaystyle\big((1-w_{i}\overline{w_{j}})\Gamma(z_{i},z_{j})\big)+\frac{1}{m}diag(1-|w_{1}|^{2},\ldots,1-|w_{n}|^{2})\geq\mathbf{0}.

So we conclude
\big((1-w_{i}\overline{w_{j}})\Gamma(z_{i},z_{j})\big)\geq\mathbf{0}
for all
\Gamma\in\mathcal{K}_{\Psi}
and therefore, there is an element
f
in the
\Psi
-Schur-Agler class, that is, in
\mathcal{O}(\Omega,\overline{\mathbb{D}})
such that
f(z_{j})=w_{j},1\leq j\leq n
. This shows that
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})
.

Conversely, if
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})
, then we find an
f\in\mathcal{O}(\Omega,\overline{\mathbb{D}})
sending
z_{j}
to
w_{j}
. For any
\Gamma\in\mathcal{K}_{\Psi}
,
\big((1-f(z^{\prime})\overline{f(z^{\prime\prime})})\Gamma(z^{\prime},z^{\prime\prime})\big)_{z^{\prime},z^{\prime\prime}\in\Omega}
is a positive kernel. In particular,
\big((1-w_{i}\overline{w_{j}})\Gamma(z_{i},z_{j})\big)+\frac{1}{m}diag(1-|w_{1}|^{2},\ldots,1-|w_{n}|^{2})\geq\mathbf{0}
irrespective of whether
\Gamma|_{\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}\times\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}}
is positive definite or not. By our construction of
\mathcal{K}_{\Omega}
,
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in\mathscr{D}_{K}
for all
K\in\mathcal{K}_{\Omega}
.

This completes the proof.

Suppose that we are given a compact convex subset
D
of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with nonempty interior and
\underline{w}\in\partial D
. If the supporting hyperplane at
\underline{w}
is not unique, we call
\underline{w}
a singular point. When there is a unique supporting hyperplane, we call call
\underline{w}
a smooth point (
[
11
]
). The following proposition and Theorem
1.2
are similar to the corresponding results in
[
11
]
. Since our definitions slightly differ from theirs, we have included the proofs here. The proofs follow almost the same arguments that are in
[
11
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose that
K
is a
3\times 3
positive definite matrix,
\mathscr{D}_{K}=\{\underline{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}:\big((1-w_{i}\overline{w_{i}})K_{i,j}\big)\geq\mathbf{0}\}
, and
\underline{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})\in\partial\mathscr{D}_{K}\cap\mathbb{D}^{3}
is a singular point on
\partial\mathscr{D}_{K}
. Then
\mathscr{D}_{K}=\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}}(\underline{\alpha})
.

Proof.

Since
K
is a positive definite matrix there exist three linearly independent elements
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
such that
K_{i,j}=\langle k_{j},k_{i}\rangle
. For
\underline{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
, we define
T_{\underline{w}}:\mathbb{C}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{3}
by
T_{\underline{w}}(k_{j})=\overline{w_{j}}k_{j}
. Then
\mathscr{D}_{K}=\{\underline{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}:I_{3}-T_{\underline{w}}^{*}T_{\underline{w}}\geq\mathbf{0}\}=\{\underline{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}:||T_{\underline{w}}||\leq 1\}
. Since
\underline{\alpha}\in\partial\mathscr{D}_{K}
,
||T_{\underline{\alpha}}||=1
, and hence
rank(I_{3}-T_{\underline{w}}^{*}T_{\underline{w}})
is one of
0,1
and
2
.

Since
\underline{\alpha}\in\mathbb{D}^{3}
, the rank is not
0
. Suppose that the rank is
2
. If the eigenvalues of
T_{\underline{\alpha}}^{*}T_{\underline{\alpha}}
are
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}
, we may arrange them as
0\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}<\lambda_{3}=1
(using the spectral theorem and the fact that our operator is a positive one). Write

\displaystyle F(\lambda,\underline{w})

\displaystyle=det\big(\lambda I_{3}-T_{\underline{w}}^{*}T_{\underline{w}}\big)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{3}-a(\underline{w})\lambda^{2}-b(\underline{w})\lambda-c(\underline{w})

where
a,b
and
c
are polynomials in
\underline{w}
and its conjugate. Note that
F(\lambda,\underline{\alpha})
has a simple zero at
\lambda=1
and this implies
\frac{\partial F}{\partial\lambda}(1,\underline{\alpha})\neq 0
. By implicit function theorem, we can find open neighborhoods
U_{1}
of
1
in
\mathbb{R}
and
U_{\underline{\alpha}}
of
\underline{\alpha}
in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
, and a smooth function
g:U_{\underline{\alpha}}\rightarrow U_{1}
such that

\displaystyle\{(\lambda,\underline{w})\in U_{1}\times U_{\underline{\alpha}}:F(\lambda,\underline{w})=0\}=\{(g(\underline{w}),\underline{w}):\underline{w}\in U_{\underline{\alpha}}\}.

We can take
U_{1}\times U_{\underline{\alpha}}
small enough so that
\frac{\partial F}{\partial\lambda}
does not vanish in this neighborhood of
(1,\underline{\alpha})
. Thus for a fixed
\underline{w}\in U_{\underline{\alpha}}
,
F(.,\underline{w})
has exactly one zero in
U_{1}
, namely
g(\underline{w})
, and this zero depends smoothly on
\underline{w}
.

Claim.
We can choose
U_{1}\times U_{\underline{\alpha}}
so that given
\underline{w}\in U_{\underline{\alpha}}
,
g(\underline{w})
is the largest zero of
F(.,\underline{w})
.

Proof of the claim.
If not, then with a choice of
U_{1}\times U_{\underline{\alpha}}
such that
\frac{\partial F}{\partial\lambda}
does not vanish in this neighborhood, we can find a sequence
\{\underline{w}_{m}\}
in
U_{\underline{\alpha}}
converging to
\underline{\alpha}
and
\{\lambda_{m}\}
in
\mathbb{R}
such that
g(\underline{w}_{m})<\lambda_{m}
and
F(\lambda_{m},\underline{w}_{m})=0
. Since
|\lambda_{m}|
are dominated by
||T_{\underline{w}_{m}}||
and the sequence
\{\underline{w}_{m}\}
converges, we may assume that
\{\lambda_{m}\}
converges to some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus we get
F(\lambda,\underline{\alpha})=0
and
\lambda\geq 1
. But this implies
\lambda=1
and hence the sequence
\{\lambda_{m}\}
is eventually in
U_{1}
. This contradicts the fact that
F(.,\underline{w}_{m})
has exactly one zero in
U_{1}
for all large enough
m
.

Thus for all
\underline{w}
in a neighborhood
U_{\underline{\alpha}}
of
\underline{\alpha}
,
g(\underline{w})=||T_{\underline{w}}^{*}||=||T_{\underline{w}}||
, and hence we have

(2.1)

\displaystyle g(t\underline{w})=tg(\underline{w})

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
close to
1
. We differentiate (
2.1
) with with respect to
t
and obtain

\displaystyle 1=g(\underline{\alpha})=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\Big\{\frac{\partial g}{\partial w_{j}}(\underline{\alpha})\alpha_{j}+\frac{\partial g}{\partial\overline{w_{j}}}(\underline{\alpha})\overline{\alpha_{j}}\Big\}.

So the gradient of
g
does not vanish near
\underline{\alpha}
and hence
g
gives a local defining function for
\partial\mathscr{D}_{K}
at
\underline{\alpha}
. This implies that
\underline{\alpha}
is not a singular point and we arrive at a contradiction.

Thus we have
rank\big(1-T_{\underline{\alpha}}^{*}T_{\underline{\alpha}}\big)=1
. By Lemma
3.1.
in
[
11
]
, we can find
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
K_{i,j}=\frac{\overline{c_{i}}c_{j}}{1-\alpha_{i}\overline{\alpha_{j}}}
. Since
K
is positive definite, we conclude that
\alpha_{i}
are mutually distinct and
\mathscr{D}_{K}
coincides with
\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}}(\underline{\alpha})
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})\in\partial\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(z_{i},z_{j})
for
(i,j)=(1,2)
and
(1,3)
. Therefore we have
m(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{j})=c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{1},z_{j}),j=2,3
.

We define

K_{2\times 2}^{(1,2)}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{i}|^{2}}\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{j}|^{2}}}{1-\alpha_{i}\overline{\alpha_{j}}}\end{pmatrix}_{1\leq i,j\leq 2}\,\,\text{and}\,\,K^{(1,2)}=\begin{pmatrix}K_{2\times 2}^{(1,2)}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Then
\mathscr{D}_{K_{2\times 2}^{(1,2)}}=\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})=\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{2})
(
[
6
]
) and
\mathscr{D}_{K^{(1,2)}}=\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{2})\times\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. Let
L^{(1,2)}
be the supporting hyperplane to
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{2})
at
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. We define
\Sigma^{(1,2)}=\{(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3})\in\mathbb{C}^{3}:(w_{1},w_{2})\in L^{(1,2)},w_{3}\in\mathbb{C}\}
which is a supporting hyperplane to
\mathscr{D}_{K^{(1,2)}}
at
\underline{\alpha}
in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
.

Similarly we define
K_{2\times 2}^{(1,3)},K^{(1,3)},L^{(1,3)}
and the supporting hyperplane
\Sigma^{(1,3)}
to
\mathscr{D}_{K^{(1,3)}}
at
\underline{\alpha}
in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
.

Note that
\underline{\alpha}\in\partial\mathscr{D}_{K^{(1,2)}}\cap\partial\mathscr{D}_{K^{(1,3)}}
. It is easy to see that the hyperplanes
\Sigma^{(1,2)}
and
\Sigma^{(1,3)}
are distinct (see page
210
in
[
11
]
). Thus we have
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})\subseteq\mathscr{D}_{K^{(1,2)}}\cap\mathscr{D}_{K^{(1,3)}}
and
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})
lies in one of the four quadrants that
\Sigma^{(1,2)}
and
\Sigma^{(1,3)}
divides
\mathbb{C}^{3}
into. This implies that
\underline{\alpha}
is a singular point on
\partial\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})
, that is, on
\partial\mathscr{D}_{K}
. By Proposition
2.1
, we conclude that
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
and
\alpha_{3}
are distinct and
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})=\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}}(\underline{\alpha})
.
∎

Now we describe a few properties of extremal kernels.

Lemma 2.2
.

A function
K:\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}\times\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is an extremal kernel if and only if
\pi_{I}(\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z}))=\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z}_{I})
for every
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and every
k
-tuple
I
.

Proof.

Let
K
be an extremal kernel and consider any
k
-tuple
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})
. If
\underline{w}\in\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z})
, then there is a
\underline{w}_{I}\in\pi_{I}(\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z}))
such that
\pi_{I}(\underline{w})=\underline{w}_{I}
. Since
\underline{w}\in\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z})
, we have
\big((1-w_{i}\overline{w_{j}})K(z_{i},z_{j})\big)\geq\mathbf{0}
and this implies
\big((1-w_{i_{l}}\overline{w_{i_{m}}})K(z_{i_{l}},z_{i_{m}})\big)\geq\mathbf{0}
. Thus
\underline{w}_{I}\in\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z}_{I})
and hence
\pi_{I}(\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z}))\subseteq\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z}_{I})
. Now for
\underline{x}\in\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z}_{I})
we find an
r\geq 1
such that
r\underline{x}\in\partial\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z}_{I})
. This gives us a
\underline{y}\in\partial\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z})
such that
\pi_{I}(y)=r\underline{x}
. Now using the facts that
\pi_{I}
is linear and
\frac{1}{r}\underline{y}\in\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z})
, we conclude that
x\in\pi_{I}(\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z}))
and hence
\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z}_{I})\subseteq\pi_{I}(\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z}))
.

Conversely, let
\pi_{I}(\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z}))=\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z}_{I})
for every
k
-tuple
I
. Let
\underline{w}_{I}\in\partial\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z}_{I})
. We find a
\underline{w}\in\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z})
such that
\underline{w}_{I}=\pi_{I}(\underline{w})
. If
\underline{w}
lies in the interior of
\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z})
, there is an
r>1
such that
r\underline{w}\in\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z})
. This implies
\big((1-r^{2}w_{l}\overline{w_{m}})K(z_{l},z_{m})\big)_{l,m\in I}\geq\mathbf{0}
which contradicts the assumption that
\underline{w}_{I}
is a boundary point. Thus
\underline{w}\in\partial\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z})
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose that
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
are mutually distinct points in the Carathéodory hyperbolic domain
\Omega
and
K
is an
n
-extremal kernel on
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}
with
K(z_{i},z_{i})=1
for all
i
. If
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})=\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z})
, then
|K(z_{i},z_{j})|=\sqrt{1-c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{i},z_{j})^{2}}
for all
i
and
j
. Also,
(0,\ldots,0,\mu)\in\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z})
if and only if
|\mu|\leq c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{n};z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})
.

Proof.

For any pair
(i,j)
, we have, by Lemma
2.2
,

\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(z_{i},z_{j})=\pi_{(i,j)}(\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z}))=\pi_{(i,j)}(\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z}))=\mathscr{D}_{K}(z_{i},z_{j}).

By Theorem
2.3
in
[
6
]
,
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(z_{i},z_{j})=\{(w_{i},w_{2})\in\mathbb{D}^{2}:m(w_{1},z_{2})\leq c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{i},z_{j})\}\cup\{(e^{i\theta},e^{i\theta}):\theta\in\mathbb{R}\}
. On the other hand it is easy to see that
(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathscr{D}_{K}(z_{i},z_{j})
if and only if either
w_{1}=w_{2}\in\partial\mathbb{D}
or
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{D}
and
|K(z_{i},z_{j})|\leq\sqrt{1-m(w_{1},w_{2})^{2}}
. Thus we obtain
|K(z_{i},z_{j})|\leq\sqrt{1-c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{i},z_{j})^{2}}
. Since
c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{i},z_{j})
is attained, if we assume
|K(z_{i},z_{j})|<\sqrt{1-c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{i},z_{j})^{2}}
, then there is an
r>1
and
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{D}
such that
rw_{1},rw_{2}\in\mathbb{D}
and

c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{i},z_{j})=m(w_{1},w_{2})<m(rw_{1},rw_{2})<\sqrt{1-|K(z_{i},z_{j})|^{2}}.

This implies
(rw_{1},rw_{2})\in\mathscr{D}_{K}(z_{i},z_{j})-\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(z_{i},z_{j})
which is a contradiction.

The statement involving
\mu
follows easily from the definition of
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
K(z_{i},z_{i})=1
for all
i
. By Lemma
2.3
, there is a
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
K(z_{1},z_{2})=e^{i\theta}\frac{\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{1}|^{2}}\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{2}|^{2}}}{1-\alpha_{1}\overline{\alpha_{2}}}
. We find a nonzero
(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
such that

(2.2)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}(1-|\alpha_{1}|^{2})&(1-\alpha_{1}\overline{\alpha_{2}})K(z_{1},z_{2})\\
(1-\alpha_{2}\overline{\alpha_{1}})K(z_{2},z_{1})&(1-|\alpha_{2}|^{2})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{0}.

Note that,
v_{1}v_{2}\neq 0
. Now, for any
t,v_{3}\in\mathbb{C}
, we now have that the quantity

\displaystyle\Bigg\langle\begin{pmatrix}(1-|\alpha_{1}|^{2})&(1-\alpha_{1}\overline{\alpha_{2}})K(z_{1},z_{2})&(1-\alpha_{1}\overline{\alpha_{3}})K(z_{1},z_{3})\\
(1-\alpha_{2}\overline{\alpha_{1}})K(z_{2},z_{1})&(1-|\alpha_{2}|^{2})&(1-\alpha_{2}\overline{\alpha_{3}})K(z_{2},z_{3})\\
(1-\alpha_{3}\overline{\alpha_{1}})K(z_{3},z_{1})&(1-\alpha_{3}\overline{\alpha_{2}})K(z_{3},z_{2})&(1-|\alpha_{3}|^{2})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}tv_{1}\\
tv_{2}\\
v_{3}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}tv_{1}\\
tv_{2}\\
v_{3}\end{pmatrix}\Bigg\rangle

is non-negative. Simplifying it and using (
2.2
) we find

(1-|\alpha_{3}|^{2})|v_{3}|^{2}+2Re\bigg(t\overline{v_{3}}\big((1-\alpha_{3}\overline{\alpha_{1}})K(z_{3},z_{1})v_{1}+(1-\alpha_{3}\overline{\alpha_{2}})K(z_{3},z_{2})v_{2}\big)\bigg)\geq 0

for all
t,v_{3}\in\mathbb{C}
. Hence we conclude
(1-\alpha_{3}\overline{\alpha_{1}})K(z_{3},z_{1})v_{1}+(1-\alpha_{3}\overline{\alpha_{2}})K(z_{3},z_{2})v_{2}=0
. Again using (
2.2
), we see that there is a nonzero
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\displaystyle K(z_{1},z_{2})=e^{i\theta}M_{1,2},K(z_{3},z_{1})=\mu M_{3,1},\,\,\text{and}\,\,K(z_{3},z_{2})=\mu e^{i\theta}M_{3,2}

where
M_{i,j}=\frac{\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{i}|^{2}}\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{j}|^{2}}}{1-\alpha_{i}\overline{\alpha_{j}}}
. Therefore we have

\displaystyle|\mu|=\frac{\sqrt{1-c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{j},z_{3})^{2}}}{\sqrt{1-m(\alpha_{j},\alpha_{3})^{2}}}\,\,\,\text{for}\,\,\,\,j=1,2.

Now it is easy to see that
\Bigg(0,0,\sqrt{\frac{c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{j},z_{3})^{2}-m(\alpha_{j},\alpha_{3})^{2}}{1-m(\alpha_{j},\alpha_{3})^{2}}}\Bigg)\in\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z})
and the result follows from Lemma
2.3
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
K
be an
n
-extremal kernel defined on
\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
K(i,i)=1
for all
i
and let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
be points in
\mathbb{D}
. If
\mathscr{D}_{K}=\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}}(\underline{\alpha})
, then
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
are mutually distinct and there exist
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle K(l,m)=e^{i(\theta_{l}-\theta_{m})}\frac{\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{l}|^{2}}\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{m}|^{2}}}{1-\alpha_{l}\overline{\alpha_{m}}}

for all
l
and
m
.

Proof.

Since
K
is positive definite, we can find a
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in\mathscr{D}_{K}(1,\ldots,n)
such that
w_{i}\neq w_{j}
for all
i\neq j
. So

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1-w_{i}\overline{w_{j}}}{1-\alpha_{i}\overline{\alpha_{j}}}\end{pmatrix}\geq\mathbf{0},

and using Lemma
3.2
in
[
11
]
we find that
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
are mutually distinct.

By Lemma
2.3
,
|K(l,m)|=\sqrt{1-m(\alpha_{l},\alpha_{m})^{2}}
for all
l
and
m
, and hence there exist
\theta_{l,m}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
K(l,m)=e^{i\theta_{l,m}}\frac{\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{l}|^{2}}\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{m}|^{2}}}{1-\alpha_{l}\overline{\alpha_{m}}}
with
\theta_{l,l}=0
for all
l
. Now, because
\underline{\alpha}\in\mathscr{D}_{K}(1,\ldots,n)
, we have

\Big(\frac{1-\alpha_{l}\overline{\alpha_{m}}}{\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{l}|^{2}}\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{m}|^{2}}}K(l,m)\Big)=\big(e^{i\theta_{l,m}}\big)\geq\mathbf{0}
. Since
\theta_{l,l}=0
for all
l
, the result follows from this.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

We apply induction on
n
. For
n=2
, we have
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{2})=\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
by Theorem 2.3 in
[
6
]
. Let the statement be true for
n-1
.

Suppose now we are given an
n
-extremal kernel
K
on
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}
satisfying
\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z})=\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})
and a point
\underline{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})
such that
m(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{j})=c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{1},z_{j})
for
j=2,\ldots,n
. Since
K
is extremal, we have
\mathscr{D}_{K}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})=\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})
, and the point
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1})\in\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})
satisfies
m(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{j})=c_{\Omega}^{*}(z_{1},z_{j})
for
j=2,\ldots,n-1
. By induction hypothesis and Lemma
2.4
, there are
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle K(z_{l},z_{m})=e^{i(\theta_{l}-\theta_{m})}\frac{\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{l}|^{2}}\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{m}|^{2}}}{1-\alpha_{l}\overline{\alpha_{m}}}

for all
l,m=1\ldots,n-1
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1-\alpha_{i}\overline{\alpha_{j}}}{\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{i}|^{2}}\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{j}|^{2}}}K(z_{i},z_{j})\end{pmatrix}_{n\times n}=\begin{pmatrix}\big(e^{i(\theta_{l}-\theta_{m})}\big)_{n-1\times n-1}&\big(a_{j,n}\big)_{n-1\times 1}\\
\big(a_{n,j}\big)_{1\times n-1}&1\end{pmatrix}\geq\mathbf{0},

where
a_{j,n}=\frac{1-\alpha_{j}\overline{\alpha_{n}}}{\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{j}|^{2}}\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{n}|^{2}}}K(z_{j},z_{n})
. Note that
|a_{1,n}|=1
by Lemma
2.3
. We now consider any
l,m\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
with
l<m
and the
\{l,m,n\}\times\{l,m,n\}
block, that is,

\displaystyle A_{l,m}=\begin{pmatrix}1&e^{i(\theta_{l}-\theta_{m})}&a_{l,n}\\
e^{i(\theta_{m}-\theta_{l})}&1&a_{m,n}\\
a_{n,l}&a_{n,m}&1\end{pmatrix}.

Since
A_{l,m}
is positive semidefinite, for any
t,v\in\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle\Bigg\langle A_{l,m}\begin{pmatrix}-t\\
te^{i(\theta_{m}-\theta_{l})}\\
v\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}-t\\
te^{i(\theta_{m}-\theta_{l})}\\
v\end{pmatrix}\Bigg\rangle\geq 0.

Hence
|v|^{2}+2Re\big(v\overline{t}(-a_{n,l}+e^{i(\theta_{m}-\theta_{l})}a_{n.m})\big)\geq 0
for all
t,v\in\mathbb{C}
. Thus we find
e^{i\theta_{l}}a_{n,l}=e^{i\theta_{m}}a_{n,m}
. Since
l
and
m
is arbitrary and
|a_{1,n}|=1
, there is a
\theta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
a_{l,n}=e^{i(\theta_{l}-\theta_{n})}
for all
l
. This is sufficient to conclude
\mathscr{D}_{K}(\underline{z})=\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}}(\underline{\alpha})
, that is,
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}(\underline{z})=\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{D}}(\underline{\alpha})
.
∎
