Test-Time Compute and Budget (“Reasoning Effort”): An Underrecognized Methodologic Variable in Medical Large Language Model Research
Authors: Dong Kyun Kim, Joon-Yong Jung
Published: 2026

THINK DEEPLY: WHAT IS TEST-TIME COMPUTE?

Test-time compute (TTC) is the allocation of additional computation during inference to enable a large language model (LLM) to perform internal and deliberate reasoning to solve complex tasks (“think deeply before answering”). Driven by this mechanism, current LLMs have shifted from immediate heuristic pattern completion to slower multi-step reasoning.

Nowadays, these new LLMs are often called “reasoning models.” Unlike traditional language models constrained to single-pass autoregressive next-token predictions based on statistical regularities, they can explore, evaluate, and refine candidate solution paths before answering [1]. In the nascent stage, Snell et al. [2] demonstrated that TTC can outperform simply scaling training-time resources, such as model parameters, pretraining data, or total training compute. The TTC has since emerged as a key paradigm, ushering in the post-training era.

However, the TTC landscape is enormously broad, and the methods are too diverse and multidimensional to be captured by a single hierarchical taxonomy. Representative strategies include 1) repeated parallel sampling with aggregation/re-ranking (e.g., majority voting [self-consistency] or best-of-N), 2) structured search using explicit search algorithms and value functions (Tree of Thoughts, Monte Carlo Tree Search), 3) discriminative verifiers (outcome/process reward models) or generative critics (LLM-as-a-Judge), and 4) self-correction (Self-Refine) [3]. “REASONING EFFORT”: HOW IS TTC REALIZED IN PRACTICE AND BUDGETED?

TTC has moved beyond experimental concepts to practical deployment. OpenAI’s o1 series was one of the pioneers in commercially deploying TTC and offering users a “reasoning effort” configuration to control the compute budget. In terms of clinical translation, a recent study showed that the o1-preview reasoning model can match or outperform physicians in clinical diagnostic and management tasks by leveraging its advanced internal reasoning capabilities [4]. In parallel, a milestone among the open-weight models was DeepSeek-R1-Zero, which demonstrated that pure reinforcement learning can elicit emergent behaviors such as self-reflection and alternative-solution exploration. Together, these advancements have established reasoning models as a new paradigm that offers promising medical applications [5].

Fundamentally, the internalization of TTC through training [123] and adaptive compute allocation, which dynamically scales the inference budget according to problem complexity [6], have catalyzed progress in this field, overcoming substantial drawbacks in the external orchestration of TTC concerning efficiency, latency, and reliability.

Following these breakthroughs, most recent LLMs have incorporated internal reasoning processes such as decomposing prompts, considering intermediate paths, and refining responses. Certain vendors provide user-adjustable levels of this internal computation (e.g., GPT’s reasoning effort) and/or dynamically adjust the budget according to the task complexity (e.g., Gemini’s dynamic thinking and Claude’s adaptive thinking). However, such internal deliberation processes are often not fully observable in the final visible response. In other words, their exact internal mechanisms and compute-allocation policies remain largely proprietary black boxes.

UNCOVERING METHODOLOGICAL PITFALLS: ARE “REASONING EFFORTS” STANDARDIZED ACROSS PLATFORMS?

Conceptually, “reasoning effort” is not a classical decoding hyperparameter but a vendor-defined request-level option controlling the TTC budget. A higher level of reasoning effort encourages more thorough reasoning but increases reasoning token usage, latency (especially time to first visible token), and cost when using application programming interface (API)-based access [7]. While parameters such as temperature (randomness) or top-p (nucleus sampling) govern visible token-level sampling during decoding, reasoning effort determines how much the model thinks before producing visible response tokens, using “reasoning tokens” in that process. In addition, the maximum output-token parameter limits the total number of generated tokens, including both reasoning and visible output tokens.

However, in practice, frontier vendors differ substantially in how they allow users to control reasoning effort in both API and chatbot environments (Table 1) [891011]. Consequently, this heterogeneity leads to significant methodological pitfalls. First, when multiple LLMs are benchmarked, differences in reasoning configurations can confound the true inter-model performance gap. Second, inconsistent reasoning settings can undermine the comparability of the same model’s performance across studies, and even across researchers within the same study. Third, recent GPT models allow temperature adjustment only when the reasoning effort is set to “none” (test code available at https://github.com/Dongguri-RAD/GPT-reason-effort-temp). One plausible rationale for this incompatibility is that externally restricted stochasticity could narrow the model’s internal search over the solution paths in complex multi-step reasoning tasks [12].

Therein lies the problem. Implementing a deterministic temperature of zero is often adopted in clinical research to ensure reproducibility, whereas the enforced lack of reasoning effort may lead to suboptimal model performance, so-called “underthinking” [13]. For sufficiently challenging clinical tasks, avoiding the enforcement of artificial deterministic model behavior may yield optimal performance in reasoning models [4].

Currently, there is no established consensus on how to balance the tradeoff between reasoning depth and scientific reproducibility. One previously proposed way to circumvent this dilemma was to elicit deliberate reasoning through prompt directives (e.g., instructing the model to “think”), rather than relying on built-in reasoning knobs. However, current guidance increasingly favors model-native reasoning effort and shorter, outcome-focused prompts over manual, process-heavy prompting [14]. Nevertheless, when internal reasoning is disabled, deliberate prompting by domain experts may still provide an external reasoning scaffold, thereby remaining methodologically justifiable.

Furthermore, there is no definitive guidance on determining the appropriate inference budget for medical tasks of varying difficulty. For easier tasks, performance differs minimally across effort levels. For moderate-difficulty tasks, increasing reasoning effort might yield meaningful gains, whereas for hard tasks, performance might reach an early plateau beyond which additional effort confers little benefit. To our knowledge, the current radiological evidence seems to be limited to a single empirical study on chest radiograph reporting [15].

THE WAY FORWARD: TRANSPARENT REPORTING

As it stands, individual researchers must make study-specific choices and report them transparently to ensure appraisal and reproducibility. Nevertheless, current LLM-specialized reporting guidelines have yet to clearly identify “reasoning effort” as a distinct reporting item, despite the well-established reporting axis for stochasticity [16171819].

Therefore, future updates to these reporting guidelines should incorporate a dedicated category for vendor-specific reasoning settings. At a minimum, researchers should be required to report 1) the selected reasoning effort level, 2) the rationale for that choice, 3) whether the setting was explicitly adjusted or left at the vendor default, 4) the exact default level for that model at the time of the study, since defaults vary across models and may change over time even within the same model family (e.g., the default changed from “none” in GPT-5.2 and GPT-5.4 to “medium” in GPT-5.5) [8]; and, most importantly, 5) the prompting strategy used when reasoning effort is disabled to preserve temperature control, especially in GPT models.
