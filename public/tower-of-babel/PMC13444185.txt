Comment on: “Use of combined spinal epidural technique with milrinone nebulization in a patient with pulmonary stenosis posted for elective cesarean section - A case report -”
Authors: Rohan Magoon
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR: In the recent issue of Anesthesia and Pain Medicine, an interesting case report by Sandeep et al. [1] outlines the use of combined spinal-epidural accompanied by milrinone nebulization for the successful conduct of cesarean section (CS) in a 27-year-old primigravida with pulmonary stenosis. The parturient, over the preceding 10 weeks prior to elective CS, demonstrated significant clinical improvement following pulmonary valve balloon dilatation at 22 weeks of gestation [1]. Nonetheless, there exist other details regarding echocardiography and maternal-fetal characteristics that can assist the readers in the comprehensive interpretation of this high-risk case [1-5]. The authors discuss the potential favorable effects of nebulized milrinone in their setting involving a dilated and a hypertrophied right ventricle (RV), owing to the former's inodilator properties. Herein, the presentation of either the RV dimensions and/or objective estimates of the RV systolic-diastolic performance (as recommended in the 2025 American Society of Echocardiography guidelines), would have substantiated the index case and the allied management [1,2]. Moreover, as for the combined spinal-epidural technique, the narration of physical attributes of the parturient, especially the patient height, could have aided an improved comprehension of the context [1,3]. At the same time, the importance of the associated Apgar score cannot be overemphasized in an elective CS performed under steroid cover in backdrop of fetal growth retardation, especially when pertaining to the neonatal outcome in a high-risk parturient with pulmonary stenosis or heart disease, in general [1,3,4]. Finally, given that an intra-arterial catheter was already in situ, arterial blood gas analysis could have provided useful information, particularly in a spontaneously breathing patient with a vulnerable RV. Hypercapnia should be avoided in such patients, while careful hemodynamic management is required to prevent systemic hypotension [1,5].
