Engineered Salmonella delivering the NMDAR antagonist conantokin G synergizes with PD-L1 blockade to enhance colorectal cancer regression
Authors: Solbi Kim, Sora Kang, Eunji Kim, Minju Han, Minchan Jeong, Jin-Man Kim, Heung Jin Jeon, Hyo-Jin Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Engineered attenuated Salmonella typhimurium has been explored as a tumor-targeting platform for localized therapeutic delivery. Here, we developed a strain capable of secreting the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) antagonist conantokin G (Con G) via the flagellar type III secretion system (T3SS). The engineered bacteria preferentially accumulated in tumor tissues and induced robust colorectal cancer cell death characterized by membrane disruption, leading to significant tumor suppression in vitro and in vivo with minimal systemic toxicity. Bulk RNA-seq analysis confirmed induction of immunogenic cell death pathways and concurrent suppression of Treg-associated gene expression. Salmonella colonization was associated with increased tumor PD-L1 expression, likely driven by infection-induced inflammatory signaling, which provided a rationale for combination with α–PD-L1 therapy. Combination treatment enhanced tumor regression, prolonged survival, and achieved complete tumor clearance in a subset of mice. Analysis of 106 clinical colorectal cancer specimens further demonstrated that high expression of NR2B, a subunit of NMDAR, is associated with advanced disease stage and poor prognosis. Together, these findings establish NR2B as a clinically relevant therapeutic target and highlight engineered bacterial delivery of Con G as a strategy to sensitize colorectal cancer to immune checkpoint blockade.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40164-026-00794-0.

To the editor

Microsatellite-stable (MSS) colorectal cancer (CRC) remains largely unresponsive to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), highlighting the need for therapeutic strategies that integrate tumor-intrinsic vulnerabilities with immune modulation [1–4]. Here, we identify the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor subunit NR2B (GRIN2B) as a clinically relevant therapeutic target and demonstrate that tumor-targeted delivery of the NR2B antagonist conantokin G (Con G) via engineered Salmonella synergizes with α–PD-L1 therapy to overcome immunotherapy resistance.

To strengthen the robustness of our findings despite the relatively small NR2B-high subgroup (18%), we performed independent validation using publicly available datasets, including GEPIA and Kaplan–Meier Plotter. Kaplan–Meier Plotter analysis demonstrated significantly worse relapse-free and overall survival in patients with high GRIN2B expression (HR ~ 1.5, p < 0.001; Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. 1 A). These findings were independently validated in a cohort of 106 CRC specimens, in which NR2B-high expression (IHC score 3+) was observed in 18% of cases and significantly correlated with advanced T and N stages as well as reduced disease-free survival (p = 0.0033; Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. 1B, C, Supplementary Table 3). Multivariate Cox regression analysis confirmed NR2B-high status as an independent predictor of poor prognosis (HR 2.06; p = 0.043; Fig. 1C, Supplementary Table 4). Together, these data establish NR2B as a clinically meaningful therapeutic target in CRC.

Based on this rationale, we engineered an attenuated Salmonella strain to secrete Con G directly into tumor tissues using the flagellar type III secretion system (T3SS) [5, 6]. A flgM–Con G fusion construct was introduced into an inducible pBAD vector and enabled efficient secretion upon L-arabinose induction without evidence of bacterial lysis, as confirmed by Western blotting and ELISA (Fig. 1D–H, Supplementary Fig. 2) [7]. Reduced bacterial motility further supported effective engagement of the secretion system (Fig. 1I). This observation suggests that the engineered strain operates in a secretion-preferred state rather than exhibiting enhanced flagellar-dependent invasiveness, thereby minimizing the likelihood of hyper-invasiveness contributing to cytotoxicity. Importantly, secreted flgM-Con G induced robust tumor cell death across multiple CRC cell lines. This effect was characterized by extracellular calcium release, membrane disruption, and the presence of intracellular proteins in culture supernatants, reflecting membrane integrity loss rather than NMDAR-mediated calcium influx or classical caspase-dependent apoptosis (Fig. 1J–M). Consistently, detection of intracellular proteins such as GAPDH and pro-caspase in the extracellular fraction indicates passive leakage following cell lysis rather than apoptotic activation. In line with these observations, Z-score-normalized heatmap analysis of bulk RNA-seq data revealed upregulation of genes associated with immunogenic cell death, DAMP signaling, and interferon responses (Supplementary Fig. 3). Notably, the cytotoxic activity of secreted flgM-Con G was comparable to or greater than that of commercially available Con G at similar concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 4), supporting the therapeutic relevance of the secreted dose. All in vitro cytotoxicity and organoid experiments were performed using bacterial culture supernatants rather than live bacteria to specifically assess the activity of the secreted peptide. These effects were preserved in 3D tumor spheroids and patient-derived organoids established from two independent CRC patients, including one MSS and one MSI-high tumor, demonstrating the translational relevance of this approach (Fig. 1N–O). Together with the immune-competent CT26 syngeneic CRC model, these findings further support the relevance of NR2B targeting in colorectal cancer, particularly in the MSS/ICI-refractory context, with CT26 confirmed as an NR2B-high model at both the mRNA and protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 5).

In vivo, systemic administration of engineered Salmonella was well tolerated, causing only transient body weight loss and no detectable toxicity in major organs. Histopathological analysis at Day 14 and 28 confirmed the absence of tissue damage or chronic inflammation (Fig. 2A–B, Supplementary Fig. 6). IVIS imaging demonstrated preferential accumulation of Salmonella within tumor tissues followed by gradual clearance, and serum TNF-α and IL-6 levels showed a transient early increase with rapid normalization, indicating a self-limiting inflammatory response (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Fig. 6D). Treated mice exhibited significantly reduced tumor burden (~ 45% reduction), prolonged survival, increased markers of tumor cell death and immunogenic cell death, and reduced tumor cell proliferation (Fig. 2D–F, Supplementary Fig. 7 A, B). Despite these benefits, complete tumor eradication was not consistently achieved, coinciding with therapy-induced PD-L1 upregulation within the tumor microenvironment (Supplementary Fig. 7 C). PD-L1 upregulation is likely driven by infection-associated inflammatory responses induced by Salmonella colonization, including TLR4-NF-κB signaling and IFN-γ. However, the relative contributions of bacterial infection versus flgM-Con G expression cannot be fully distinguished from the present data. Flow cytometric analysis further revealed that combination therapy markedly remodeled the tumor immune microenvironment, as evidenced by a clearly defined gating strategy and representative flow plots (Supplementary Fig. 8, 9). Notably, PD-1 expression was increased in CD8+ T cells without a concomitant increase in TIM-3 or LAG-3, suggesting an activated or early dysfunctional state rather than terminal exhaustion. In parallel, CRT exposure following flgM-Con G treatment suggests induction of immunogenic cell death that may promote secondary T-cell activation via DAMP release. This effect likely reflects a combination of DAMP-mediated immune priming and direct bacterial immunomodulation, although the relative contribution of each mechanism cannot be fully distinguished in the present study. Consistent with these findings, combination therapy with α–PD-L1 antibodies reduced PD-L1 levels and synergistically enhanced antitumor efficacy, resulting in complete tumor regression in 3 of 8 mice (37.5%) and significantly improved survival outcomes (Fig. 2G–J, Supplementary Fig. 10). The uninduced group served as a vector-only control, enabling assessment of the specific contribution of flgM-Con G secretion. Although subcutaneous models allow controlled assessment of tumor colonization and therapeutic efficacy, orthotopic CRC models may better capture mucosal immunity and microbiome-related factors and warrant future evaluation.

H-J Tumor growth, body weight change, and survival curves of mice were analyzed. In summary, we identify NR2B as a prognostic and therapeutic target in CRC and demonstrate that tumor-targeted delivery of Con G via engineered Salmonella effectively suppresses tumor growth and sensitizes MSS CRC to immune checkpoint blockade. This strategy represents a novel convergence of microbial oncology and immunotherapy and offers a promising approach to overcome immunotherapy resistance in colorectal cancer (Fig. 2K). Although comprehensive MSI/MSS stratification was not available, functional data from MSS-derived organoids and in vivo models support the relevance of NR2B targeting in MSS colorectal cancer. While genetic validation of NR2B function and long-term immune memory studies remain to be addressed, our findings provide strong evidence for the therapeutic potential of this approach.
