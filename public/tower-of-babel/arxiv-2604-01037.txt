Stabilizing the Rayleigh–Ritz procedure by randomization
Nian Shao

Abstract
Extracting approximate eigenpairs from a prescribed subspace is of fundamental importance in eigenvalue computation. While projecting the target eigenvector onto the subspace yields satisfactory accuracy, extracting an approximate eigenpair that attains a comparable convergence rate has remained a long-standing open problem. Although the standard Rayleigh–Ritz procedure is widely used for this purpose, it may suffer from deteriorated convergence of Ritz values and may even fail to produce convergent Ritz vectors. In this paper, we address this long-standing open problem by introducing a randomized Rayleigh–Ritz procedure whose output converges at a rate similar to the ideal projection. Our analysis requires only the simplicity of the target eigenvalue and extends naturally to nonlinear eigenvalue problems.

Rayleigh–Ritz procedure, randomized algorithms, eigenvalue problems

Before describing the RRR procedure, we briefly recall the standard Rayleigh–Ritz procedure. For the eigenvalue problem associated with

\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A_{0}W_{\epsilon}y_{\epsilon}=\mu_{\epsilon}\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}W_{\epsilon}y_{\epsilon}.

2
Stability issues of standard Rayleigh–Ritz procedure

In this section, we present several examples illustrating the stability issues of the standard Rayleigh–Ritz procedure. Throughout this section, as well as in all results of this paper, we assume that
(\lambda,v)
is a simple eigenpair.

We begin with a Hermitian matrix
A_{0}
. When
\lambda
is the largest (or smallest) eigenvalue, the minimax principle ensures that the second eigenvalue of the compressed matrix
W_{\epsilon}^{\mathsf{H}}A_{0}W_{\epsilon}
is bounded by the second eigenvalue of
A_{0}
. If
\mu_{\epsilon}
converges to
\lambda
, then it remains well separated from the remaining eigenvalues of
W_{\epsilon}^{\mathsf{H}}A_{0}W_{\epsilon}
. Consequently, Ritz values converge quadratically, while Ritz vectors converge linearly; see, for instance,
[
19
, Sec. 4.3.2]
.

In contrast, for an interior eigenvalue of a Hermitian matrix, the convergence of Ritz values may deteriorate to linear, and Ritz vectors may even fail to converge. This occurs because the target Ritz value can become arbitrarily close to other eigenvalues of
W_{\epsilon}^{\mathsf{H}}A_{0}W_{\epsilon}
.

Example 1

(Interior eigenvalue of a Hermitian matrix: linear convergence of Ritz values and failure of convergence of Ritz vectors)
.

Consider the eigenvalue
0
of the matrix

A_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}-1&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad W_{\epsilon}=\begin{bmatrix}\epsilon/\sqrt{2}&1/\sqrt{2}\\
\sqrt{1-\epsilon^{2}}&0\\
\epsilon/\sqrt{2}&-1/\sqrt{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Then the compressed matrix is

W_{\epsilon}^{\mathsf{H}}A_{0}W_{\epsilon}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\epsilon\\
-\epsilon&0\end{bmatrix}.

Ritz values are
\pm\epsilon
, yielding only linear convergence.
Corresponding Ritz vectors are
W_{\epsilon}[1,-1]^{\mathsf{T}}
and
W_{\epsilon}[1,1]^{\mathsf{T}}
, which provide no accuracy for target eigenvectors.

For a non-Hermitian matrix
A_{0}
, as shown in
[
11
]
, applying Elsner’s theorem
to the compressed matrix ensures the convergence of Ritz values. However, the convergence rate may deteriorate substantially to
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1/m})
, since the compressed matrix can become (nearly) defective.

Example 2

(Non-Hermitian matrix:
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1/m})
convergence on Ritz values)
.

Consider the eigenvalue
0
of the matrix

A_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\
0&1&3\\
0&0&2\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad W_{\epsilon}=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{1-\epsilon^{2}}&0\\
\epsilon/\sqrt{2}&1/\sqrt{2}\\
\epsilon/\sqrt{2}&-1/\sqrt{2}\end{bmatrix}.

The compressed matrix is

W_{\epsilon}^{\mathsf{H}}A_{0}W_{\epsilon}=\begin{bmatrix}\epsilon\left(3\epsilon+\sqrt{(1-\epsilon^{2})/2}\right)&\sqrt{(1-\epsilon^{2})/2}-2\epsilon\\
\epsilon&0\end{bmatrix}\approx\begin{bmatrix}\epsilon/\sqrt{2}&1/\sqrt{2}\\
\epsilon&0\end{bmatrix},

whose eigenvalues are
\pm 2^{-1/4}\sqrt{\epsilon}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
as
\epsilon\to 0
.

An even more severe situation arises for generalized eigenvalue problems, where Ritz values may fail to converge. This occurs because the compressed matrix pencil can be
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
-close to singular even if the original pencil is regular. Consequently, the convergence result for standard eigenvalue problems cannot be extended to the generalized case.

For a non-Hermitian problem with left and right eigenvectors

At the end of this section, we comment on the instabilities of the standard Rayleigh–Ritz procedure and give an intuitive explanation of how randomization can stabilize it.
Consider a matrix
A_{0}
with a simple eigenpair
(\lambda,v)
. Let
W
be an orthonormal matrix such that
v\in\mathsf{range}(W)
.
The simplicity of
\lambda
implies that zero is a simple singular value of
(A_{0}-\lambda I)W
.
However, the compressed matrix
W^{\mathsf{H}}(A_{0}-\lambda I)W
does
not
generally inherit this property unless
A_{0}
is Hermitian and
\lambda
is an exterior eigenvalue.
In fact, the compression may degenerate to a zero matrix in the Hermitian case (see
Example

1
) or form a Jordan block in the non-Hermitian case (see
Example

2
).
As a result, the convergence of Ritz values may deteriorate and Ritz vectors may fail to converge.
In contrast, compressing
(A_{0}-\lambda I)W
with an oblivious (complex) Gaussian random matrix preserves the simplicity of the zero singular value almost surely.
Specifically, in
Lemma

3
, we show that, with high probability, the eigenvalue and eigenvector condition numbers of the compressed problem (at
\lambda
) are bounded by moderate multiples of those of the original problem.
These properties enable us to establish a probabilistic convergence result for the RRR procedure.

RRR procedure for nonlinear eigenvalue problems

In this section, we extend the RRR procedure to nonlinear eigenvalue problems (
Algorithm

).
Readers interested solely in standard eigenvalue problems may skip this section, with the exception of the perturbation result in

Assumption 1
.

Throughout this paper, we assume that
A(\cdot)
is a regular holomorphic matrix-valued function defined on a nonempty bounded domain
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{C}
that admits an analytic extension to
\partial\mathcal{D}
.
Here, regular means that
\det A(\xi_{0})\neq 0
for some
\xi_{0}\in\mathcal{D}
.

The nonlinear eigenvalue problem
[
7
]
consists of finding eigenpairs
(\lambda,v)
such that

A(\lambda)v=0,\quad\lambda\in\mathcal{D}\quad\text{and}\quad v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}.

In particular, if
0
is a simple eigenvalue of the matrix
A(\lambda)
, and
u^{\mathsf{H}}A^{\prime}(\lambda)v\neq 0
for a left eigenvector
u
, then the eigenvalue
\lambda
of
A(\cdot)
is called simple
[
16
, Prop. 1]
.

Similar to standard eigenvalue problems, we impose a Petrov–Galerkin condition on
A(\cdot)W_{\epsilon}
using a complex Gaussian random matrix
\Omega\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
and solve the following compressed nonlinear eigenvalue problem:

B_{\epsilon}(\mu_{\epsilon})y_{\epsilon}=0,\quad\mu_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{D},\quad y_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\setminus\{0\},\quad\text{where}\quad B_{\epsilon}(\xi):=\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A(\xi)W_{\epsilon}.

(1)

To refine randomized Ritz values, we may use the
Rayleigh functional

[
20
]
.

Definition 1
.

Let
\lambda
be a simple eigenvalue of
A(\cdot)
associated with an eigenvector
v
. Given two open sets
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}\subset\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}_{v}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
\lambda\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
and
v\in\mathcal{D}_{v}
, a functional
\mathsf{Rf}\colon\mathcal{D}_{v}\mapsto\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
is called a Rayleigh functional of
A(\cdot)
if

\mathsf{Rf}(\alpha w)=\mathsf{Rf}(w),\quad w^{\mathsf{H}}A\bigl(\mathsf{Rf}(w)\bigr)w=0,\quad w^{\mathsf{H}}A^{\prime}\bigl(\mathsf{Rf}(w)\bigr)w\neq 0

holds for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and
w\in\mathcal{D}_{v}
.

For a simple eigenpair
(\lambda,v)
, if
v^{\mathsf{H}}A^{\prime}(\lambda)v\neq 0
, then the Rayleigh functional exists uniquely
[
20
, Thm. 21]
. In particular, if
A(\lambda)
is Hermitian, the simplicity of
\lambda
implies the existence of
\mathsf{Rf}
. For a linear eigenvalue problem
A(\xi)=A_{0}-\xi A_{1}
, if
v^{\mathsf{H}}A_{1}v\neq 0
, then the Rayleigh functional is the usual Rayleigh quotient

\mathsf{Rf}(w)=\frac{w^{\mathsf{H}}A_{0}w}{w^{\mathsf{H}}A_{1}w}.

If a Rayleigh functional is available, randomized Ritz values can be refined by
\rho_{\epsilon}=\mathsf{Rf}(W_{\epsilon}y_{\epsilon})
, where
W_{\epsilon}y_{\epsilon}
is a randomized Ritz vector.
However, for non-Hermitian problems, a Rayleigh functional may not exist because
v^{\mathsf{H}}A^{\prime}(\lambda)v
could be zero, such as the matrix pencil in
Example

3
. In this situation, we can still perform the refinement via finding a stationary point

\rho_{\epsilon}\in\Bigl\{\rho\in\mathbb{C}\Bigm|\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\rho}}\lVert A(\rho)W_{\epsilon}y_{\epsilon}\rVert^{2}=0\Bigr\},

(2)

where
\bar{\rho}
is the complex conjugate of
\rho
and

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\rho}}\lVert A(\rho)W_{\epsilon}y_{\epsilon}\rVert^{2}=\bigl(A^{\prime}(\rho)W_{\epsilon}y_{\epsilon}\bigr)^{\mathsf{H}}\bigl(A(\rho)W_{\epsilon}y_{\epsilon}\bigr)

is the Wirtinger derivative.
When
A(\xi)=A_{0}-\xi A_{1}
, this reduces to

\rho_{\epsilon}=\frac{(W_{\epsilon}y_{\epsilon})^{\mathsf{H}}A_{1}^{\mathsf{H}}A_{0}(W_{\epsilon}y_{\epsilon})}{(W_{\epsilon}y_{\epsilon})^{\mathsf{H}}A_{1}^{\mathsf{H}}A_{1}(W_{\epsilon}y_{\epsilon})}.

For a general
A(\cdot)
, finding a stationary point of
Equation

2
is closely related to a nonlinear least squares problem, which can be solved by the Gauss–Newton method or the Levenberg–Marquardt method
[
17
, Chap. 10]
.
If the set in
Equation

2
admits multiple stationary points, additional selection criteria are required to identify an appropriate
\rho_{\epsilon}
, for instance, choosing the one closest to a prescribed shift.

We remark that those refinements described above, including both Rayleigh functional and stationary point approaches, typically do
not
require additional matrix-vector multiplications with
A(\cdot)
, since
A(\cdot)W_{\epsilon}
has already been computed when forming the compressed eigenvalue problem.

We conclude this section with the following first-order perturbation results.

Theorem 1
.

Given a matrix-valued function
B(\cdot)\colon\mathcal{D}\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
satisfying
1
. Let
\lambda
be a simple eigenvalue of
B(\cdot)
associated with left and right eigenvectors
x
and
y
.
Let
Z_{\perp}
and
Y_{\perp}
be orthonormal bases of
\mathsf{span}\{B^{\prime}(\lambda)y\}^{\perp}
and
\mathsf{span}\{y\}^{\perp}
, respectively. Then
Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}B(\lambda)Y_{\perp}
is nonsingular.

Consider a perturbation
B+\Delta B
, where
\Delta B(\cdot)\colon\mathcal{D}\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
is a holomorphic matrix-valued function that admits an analytic extension to
\partial\mathcal{D}
.
Let

M=\max\bigl\{\lVert B\rVert_{\mathcal{D}},\lVert B^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}},\lVert B^{\prime\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}}\bigr\}\quad\text{and}\quad\epsilon=\max\bigl\{\lVert\Delta B\rVert_{\mathcal{D}},\lVert\Delta B^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}},\lVert\Delta B^{\prime\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}}\bigr\}.

When
\epsilon
is small enough, there exists an eigenpair
(\lambda+\Delta\lambda,y+\Delta y)
of
B+\Delta B
such that

\lvert\Delta\lambda\rvert\leq\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(B,\lambda)\cdot\lVert\Delta B(\lambda)\rVert+C\epsilon^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\lVert\Delta y\rVert}{\lVert y\rVert}\leq\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(B,\lambda)\cdot\lVert\Delta B(\lambda)\rVert+C\epsilon^{2},

where

\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(B,\lambda):=\frac{\lVert x\rVert\lVert y\rVert}{\lvert x^{\mathsf{H}}B^{\prime}(\lambda)y\rvert}\quad\text{and}\quad\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(B,\lambda):=\bigl\lVert(Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}B(\lambda)Y_{\perp})^{-1}\bigr\rVert.

Here, we use
y^{\mathsf{H}}\Delta y=0
as the normalization condition for
y+\Delta y
, and
C
satisfies

C\leq\widetilde{C}M\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(B,\lambda)\cdot\bigl(\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(B,\lambda)+\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(B,\lambda)\bigr)^{2}

for some universal constant
\widetilde{C}
.

The proof of
Theorem

1
is provided in
Appendix

A
.
We refer to the quantities
\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(B,\lambda)
and
\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(B,\lambda)
as the eigenvalue and eigenvector condition numbers, respectively.
These names are consistent with the standard eigenvalue problem setting
[
21
, pp. 48–50]
.
Compared with existing first-order perturbation results, such as asymptotic and non-asymptotic bounds for linear eigenvalue problems in
[
22
]
, and asymptotic results for (homogeneous) polynomial eigenvalue problems in
[
5
]
, our result is slightly different.
Strictly speaking,
Theorem

1
is a non-asymptotic perturbation bound.
However, unlike
[
22
]
, where the non-asymptotic control applies to the first-order term, our explicit bound controls the second-order remainder.
This formulation preserves the sharpness of the first-order expansion while simultaneously providing a non-asymptotic guarantee on higher-order terms, whose importance will be further explained in

4.1
Exact recovery

Let

A(\cdot)

B(\xi):=\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A(\xi)W,\quad\text{where}\quad W=[v,W_{\perp}]

(3)

\mathcal{W}

Lemma 1
.

Let
(\lambda,v)

W\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}

We_{1}=v

The matrix-valued function
B(\xi)=\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A(\xi)W

(\lambda,e_{1})

Proof.

Since

\xi_{0}\in\mathcal{D}
. Then
A(\xi_{0})W
is full rank, hence
\det B(\xi_{0})=\det\bigl(\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A(\xi_{0})W\bigr)\neq 0
generically because this is a polynomial of the entries of
\Omega
.

Note that
B(\lambda)e_{1}=\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A(\lambda)v=0
, hence
(\lambda,e_{1})
is an eigenpair of
B(\cdot)
.
We now show its simplicity. Since
\lambda
is a simple eigenvalue of
A(\cdot)
, the null space dimension of the matrix
A(\lambda)
is one. It follows that, generically, the null space dimension of the matrix
B(\lambda)=\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A(\lambda)W
is also one, implying that
0
is a simple eigenvalue of the matrix
B(\lambda)
.

Write
W=[v,W_{\perp}]
. Then
\mathrm{rank}(A(\lambda)W_{\perp})=m-1
. Let
u
be a left eigenvector of
A(\cdot)
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda
. We have
u^{\mathsf{H}}A(\lambda)W_{\perp}=0
and
u^{\mathsf{H}}A^{\prime}(\lambda)v\neq 0
. Hence,
A^{\prime}(\lambda)v\notin\mathsf{range}(A(\lambda)W_{\perp})
, which implies that
\mathrm{rank}[A^{\prime}(\lambda)v,A(\lambda)W_{\perp}]=m
. Consequently, for a generic
\Omega
, the matrix
\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}[A^{\prime}(\lambda)v,A(\lambda)W_{\perp}]\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
is nonsingular.
Let
x
be a left eigenvector of
B(\cdot)
corresponding to
\lambda
. Since
x^{\mathsf{H}}B(\lambda)=x^{\mathsf{H}}\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A(\lambda)W=0
, we have

x^{\mathsf{H}}\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}[A^{\prime}(\lambda)v,A(\lambda)W_{\perp}]=[x^{\mathsf{H}}B^{\prime}(\lambda)e_{1},0_{1\times(m-1)}].

(4)

Since
\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}[A^{\prime}(\lambda)v,A(\lambda)W_{\perp}]
is nonsingular and
x\neq 0
, we conclude that
x^{\mathsf{H}}B^{\prime}(\lambda)e_{1}\neq 0
.
∎

The proof of
Lemma

1
provides useful intuition for the subsequent analysis of robust recovery.
Actually, we can directly derive an upper bound for the eigenvalue condition number
\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(B,\lambda)
from
Equation

4
:

\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(B,\lambda)=\frac{\lVert x\rVert\lVert e_{1}\rVert}{\lvert x^{\mathsf{H}}B^{\prime}(\lambda)e_{1}\rvert}=\frac{\lVert x\rVert}{\lVert x^{\mathsf{H}}\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}[A^{\prime}(\lambda)v,A(\lambda)W_{\perp}]\rVert}\leq\bigl\lVert\bigl(\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}[A^{\prime}(\lambda)v,A(\lambda)W_{\perp}]\bigr)^{-1}\bigr\rVert.

Although this bound is not as sharp as what we will establish in
Lemma

3
, it suggests studying the matrix
[A^{\prime}(\lambda)v,A(\lambda)W_{\perp}]
and exploiting the orthogonality between
\Omega x
and
A(\lambda)W_{\perp}
.

4.2
Probabilistic analysis on eigenvalue and eigenvector condition numbers

Consider the ideal proxy
B(\cdot)
defined in
Equation

3
.
In this subsection, we derive probabilistic bounds for its eigenvalue condition number
\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(B,\lambda)
and eigenvector condition number
\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(B,\lambda)
, as introduced in
Theorem

1
. For this aim, we first recall some standard tail bounds for extreme singular values of complex Gaussian random matrices. These results can be found, for example, in
[
18
,
6
]
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\Omega\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
be a complex Gaussian random matrix. Then

\Pr\bigl(\lVert\Omega\rVert\geq\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{\ln(2/\delta)}\bigr)\leq\delta\quad\text{for all}\quad\delta\geq 0.

When
m=n
, that is,
\Omega\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
is a square matrix (or a scalar), it holds that:

\Pr\bigl(\lVert\Omega^{-1}\rVert\geq\sqrt{m/\delta}\bigr)\leq\delta\quad\text{for all}\quad\delta\geq 0.

The following lemma shows that compressing the matrix
A(\lambda)W
with a complex Gaussian random matrix
\Omega
preserves both eigenvalue and eigenvector condition numbers with high probability.
We remark that replacing
\Omega
with
W
, as in the standard Rayleigh–Ritz procedure, may completely destroy a well-conditioned eigenpair of
A(\cdot)
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
u
and
v
be unit left and right eigenvectors of
A(\cdot)
corresponding to a simple eigenvalue
\lambda
.
Given an arbitrary fixed orthonormal matrix
W
admitting
W=[v,W_{\perp}]\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
, we define
B(\xi)=\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A(\xi)W
, where
\Omega\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
is a complex Gaussian random matrix. Then

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(B,\lambda)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}\cdot\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(A,\lambda)

\displaystyle\text{holds with probability at least $1-\delta$,}

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(B,\lambda)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{m-1}}{\sqrt{\delta}}\cdot\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(A,\lambda)

\displaystyle\text{holds with probability at least $1-\delta$.}

Here,
\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(A,\lambda)
and
\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(A,\lambda)
are eigenvalue and eigenvector condition numbers of
A(\cdot)
:

\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(A,\lambda):=\frac{\lVert u\rVert\lVert v\rVert}{\lvert u^{\mathsf{H}}A^{\prime}(\lambda)v\rvert}\quad\text{and}\quad\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(A,\lambda):=\bigl\lVert(Q_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}A(\lambda)V_{\perp})^{-1}\bigr\rVert,

(5)

where
Q_{\perp}
and
V_{\perp}
are orthonormal bases of
\mathsf{span}\{A^{\prime}(\lambda)v\}^{\perp}
and
\mathsf{span}\{v\}^{\perp}
, respectively.

Proof.

By
Lemma

1
, we know that
(\lambda,e_{1})
is a simple eigenpair of
B(\cdot)
almost surely. We further denote its corresponding unit left eigenvector by
x
.

Eigenvalue condition number:

Since
(0,v)
is a simple eigenpair of the matrix
A(\lambda)
and
[v,W_{\perp}]
is orthonormal, we can let
P\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times(m-1)}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathsf{range}(A(\lambda)W_{\perp})
and
p_{\perp}=(I-PP^{\mathsf{H}})A^{\prime}(\lambda)v
. Since
u^{\mathsf{H}}A(\lambda)=0
, we know that
u^{\mathsf{H}}P=0
and

\lVert p_{\perp}\rVert\geq\lVert uu^{\mathsf{H}}(I-PP^{\mathsf{H}})A^{\prime}(\lambda)v\rVert=\lVert uu^{\mathsf{H}}A^{\prime}(\lambda)v\rVert=\lvert u^{\mathsf{H}}A^{\prime}(\lambda)v\rvert=\frac{1}{\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(A,\lambda)}.

Since the eigenvalue
0
of the matrix
B(\lambda)
is also simple, we know that

\mathsf{span}\{x\}=\mathsf{range}\bigl(B(\lambda)\bigr)^{\perp}=\mathsf{range}\bigl(\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A(\lambda)W_{\perp}\bigr)^{\perp}=\mathsf{range}(\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}P)^{\perp},

and in turn,
x^{\mathsf{H}}\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}p_{\perp}=x^{\mathsf{H}}\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A^{\prime}(\lambda)v-x^{\mathsf{H}}\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}PP^{\mathsf{H}}A^{\prime}(\lambda)v=x^{\mathsf{H}}\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A^{\prime}(\lambda)v
.
Note that
\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}P
and
\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}p_{\perp}
are independent; consequently,
x
and
\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}p_{\perp}
are also independent. Thus,

\frac{1}{\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(B,\lambda)\cdot\lVert p_{\perp}\rVert}=\frac{\lvert x^{\mathsf{H}}B^{\prime}(\lambda)e_{1}\rvert}{\lVert p_{\perp}\rVert}=\frac{\lvert x^{\mathsf{H}}\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A^{\prime}(\lambda)v\rvert}{\lVert p_{\perp}\rVert}=\frac{\lvert x^{\mathsf{H}}\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}p_{\perp}\rvert}{\lVert p_{\perp}\rVert}=\Bigl\lvert x^{\mathsf{H}}\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}\bigl(p_{\perp}/\lVert p_{\perp}\rVert\bigr)\Bigr\rvert

is the magnitude of a complex Gaussian random variable. Using the tail bound in
Lemma

2
, we know that, with probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds that

\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(B,\lambda)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}\cdot\lVert p_{\perp}\rVert}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}\cdot\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(A,\lambda).

Eigenvector condition number:
Let

Q_{\perp}Q_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}A(\lambda)W_{\perp}=QR

be the thin QR decomposition. Since the matrix
[A^{\prime}(\lambda)v,A(\lambda)V_{\perp}]
is nonsingular and
\mathsf{range}(W_{\perp})\subset\mathsf{range}(V_{\perp})
. We know that
Q\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times(m-1)}
and

\lVert R^{-1}\rVert\leq\bigl\lVert\bigl(Q_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}A(\lambda)V_{\perp}\bigr)^{-1}\bigr\rVert=\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(A,\lambda),

where we use the interlacing property on the smallest singular values of
Q_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}A(\lambda)W_{\perp}
and
Q_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}A(\lambda)V_{\perp}
.
Recall that

\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(B,\lambda)=\bigl\lVert\bigl((\Omega Z_{\perp})^{\mathsf{H}}A(\lambda)W_{\perp}\bigr)^{-1}\bigr\rVert,

where
Z_{\perp}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathsf{span}\{\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A^{\prime}(\lambda)v\}^{\perp}
. We know that
(\Omega Z_{\perp})^{\mathsf{H}}A^{\prime}(\lambda)v=0
, hence
\Omega Z_{\perp}=Q_{\perp}Q_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}\Omega Z_{\perp}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(B,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\bigl\lVert\bigl((\Omega Z_{\perp})^{\mathsf{H}}Q_{\perp}Q_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}A(\lambda)W_{\perp}\bigr)^{-1}\bigr\rVert=\bigl\lVert\bigl((\Omega Z_{\perp})^{\mathsf{H}}QR\bigr)^{-1}\bigr\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\bigl\lVert\bigl(Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}(\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}Q)\bigr)^{-1}\bigr\rVert\cdot\lVert R^{-1}\rVert\leq\bigl\lVert\bigl(Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}(\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}Q)\bigr)^{-1}\bigr\rVert\cdot\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(A,\lambda).

Note that
A^{\prime}(\lambda)v
and
Q
are orthogonal. We know that
\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A^{\prime}(\lambda)v
and
\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}Q
are independent; consequently,
Z_{\perp}
and
\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}Q
are also independent. Then
Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}(\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}Q)\in\mathbb{C}^{(m-1)\times(m-1)}
is a complex Gaussian random matrix. By
Lemma

2
,

\bigl\lVert\bigl(Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}(\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}Q)\bigr)^{-1}\bigr\rVert\leq\sqrt{(m-1)/\delta}

holds with probability at least
1-\delta
.
∎

4.3
Main result: convergence of randomized Ritz pairs

Theorem 2
.

Let
(\lambda,v)
be a simple eigenpair of
A(\cdot)
.
For
0<\delta<1/4
, define

C_{\mathsf{val}}:=\frac{C_{0}}{\sqrt{\delta}}\cdot\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(A,\lambda),\quad\text{and}\quad C_{\mathsf{vec}}:=1+\frac{C_{0}\sqrt{m-1}}{\sqrt{\delta}}\cdot\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(A,\lambda),

(6)

where
C_{0}=\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{\ln(2/\delta)}
, and
\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(A,\lambda)
and
\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(A,\lambda)
are defined in
Equation

5
.
Then there exist constants
\epsilon_{0}>0
and
C>0
, depending only on
m
,
n
,
\delta
,
\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(A,\lambda)
,
\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(A,\lambda)
,
\lVert A\rVert_{\mathcal{D}}
,
\lVert A^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}}
,
\lVert A^{\prime\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}}
,
such that the following statement holds.

Consider an arbitrary fixed orthonormal matrix
W_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
with
\epsilon=\angle(v,W_{\epsilon})\leq\epsilon_{0}
.
Let
B_{\epsilon}(\xi)=\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A(\xi)W_{\epsilon}
, where
\Omega\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
is a complex Gaussian random matrix. Then, with probability at least
1-4\delta
, there exists an eigenpair
(\mu_{\epsilon},y_{\epsilon})
of

such that

\lvert\mu_{\epsilon}-\lambda\rvert\leq C_{\mathsf{val}}\epsilon+C\epsilon^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\angle(v,W_{\epsilon}y_{\epsilon})\leq C_{\mathsf{vec}}\epsilon+C\epsilon^{2}.

(7)

Remark 2
.

To achieve linear convergence, the coefficients
\{C_{\mathsf{val}},C_{\mathsf{vec}},C\}
must be independent of the choice of
W_{\epsilon}
. Moreover, the failure event depends on
W_{\epsilon}
. Consequently,
Theorem

1
, on which

2
relies, cannot be replaced by asymptotic results, since we
cannot
take a union bound over a
family
of bases

.

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

Since the choice of orthonormal bases of
\mathsf{range}(W_{\epsilon})
does not affect randomized Ritz pairs, without loss of generality, we assume

W_{\epsilon}=\begin{bmatrix}v\cos\epsilon+w\sin\epsilon&W_{\perp}\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{where}\quad\begin{bmatrix}v,w,W_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{is orthonormal.}

Let
B(\xi)=\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A(\xi)W
, where

.
Taking a union bound over
Lemmas

2
,
1
and
3
, we know that, with probability at least

, the following statements hold:

\displaystyle\text{$B(\cdot)$ is regular and $(\lambda,e_{1})$ is its simple eigenpair},

(8)

\displaystyle\lVert\Omega\rVert\leq\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{\ln(2/\delta)},

\displaystyle\bigl\lVert\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A(\lambda)[v,w]\bigr\rVert\leq C_{0}\lVert A(\lambda)\rVert,

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(B,\lambda)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}\cdot\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(A,\lambda),

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(B,\lambda)\leq\frac{\sqrt{m-1}}{\sqrt{\delta}}\cdot\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(A,\lambda).

In what follows, we assume that
Equation

8
holds and apply
Theorem

1
to

and

.
By submultiplicativity, we know that, for any integer

and

\Bigl\lvert\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{k}}{\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{k}}e_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}B(\xi)e_{j}\Bigr\rvert=\Bigl\lvert(\Omega e_{i})^{\mathsf{H}}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{k}}{\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{k}}A(\xi)(We_{j})\Bigr\rvert\leq\lVert\Omega\rVert\bigl\lVert\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{k}}{\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{k}}A(\xi)\bigr\rVert,

implying that

satisfies
1

\max\bigl\{\lVert B\rVert_{\mathcal{D}},\lVert B^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}},\lVert B^{\prime\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}}\bigr\}\leq\bigl(\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{\ln(2/\delta)}\bigr)\cdot\max\bigl\{\lVert A\rVert_{\mathcal{D}},\lVert A^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}},\lVert A^{\prime\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}}\bigr\}.

Let
\Delta B(\xi):=B_{\epsilon}(\xi)-B(\xi)=\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A(\xi)(W_{\epsilon}-W)

W_{\epsilon}-W=\begin{bmatrix}v(\cos\epsilon-1)+w\sin\epsilon&0_{n\times(m-1)}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}v&w\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\cos\epsilon-1\\
\sin\epsilon\end{bmatrix}&0_{n\times(m-1)}\end{bmatrix}.

(9)

Since

\max\bigl\{\lVert\Delta B\rVert_{\mathcal{D}},\lVert\Delta B^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}},\lVert\Delta B^{\prime\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}}\bigr\}\leq\bigl(\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{\ln(2/\delta)}\bigr)\cdot\max\bigl\{\lVert A\rVert_{\mathcal{D}},\lVert A^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}},\lVert A^{\prime\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}}\bigr\}\cdot\epsilon.

When

is sufficiently small,

yields the existence of an eigenpair

of

\displaystyle\lvert\mu_{\epsilon}-\lambda\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(B,\lambda)\cdot\lVert\Delta B(\lambda)\rVert+C\epsilon^{2}\leq\frac{C_{\mathsf{val}}}{C_{0}}\lVert\Delta B(\lambda)\rVert+C\epsilon^{2},

(10)

\displaystyle\tan\angle(e_{1},y_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(B,\lambda)\cdot\lVert\Delta B(\lambda)\rVert+C\epsilon^{2}\leq\frac{C_{\mathsf{vec}}-1}{C_{0}}\lVert\Delta B(\lambda)\rVert+C\epsilon^{2},

where

is a constant depending only on
m
,

,
\delta
,
\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(A,\lambda)
,
\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(A,\lambda)

,
\lVert A^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{D}}

According

\lVert\Delta B(\lambda)\rVert=\Bigl\lVert\Omega^{\mathsf{H}}A(\lambda)\begin{bmatrix}v&w\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\cos\epsilon-1\\

Combining it with

4.4
Refining randomized Ritz values

This subsection investigates the accuracy of refined randomized Ritz values obtained from strategies described in
Section

3
.
With the convergence of randomized Ritz vectors in
Theorem

2
, the convergence of refined randomized Ritz values is actually straightforward.
Before presenting results for general situations, we first state the result for standard eigenvalue problems.

Theorem 3
.

Let
(\lambda,v)
be a simple eigenpair of a matrix
A_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
. For
0<\delta<1/4
, we define
C_{\mathsf{vec}}
,
C
and
\epsilon_{0}
as in
Theorem

2
.
Consider an arbitrary fixed orthonormal matrix
W_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}

(\mu_{\epsilon},y_{\epsilon})

\lvert\rho_{\epsilon}-\lambda\rvert\leq\lVert A_{0}-\lambda I\rVert\,\tan^{\gamma}\bigl(C_{\mathsf{vec}}\epsilon+C\epsilon^{2}\bigr),\quad\text{where}\quad\rho_{\epsilon}=\frac{(W_{\epsilon}y_{\epsilon})^{\mathsf{H}}A_{0}(W_{\epsilon}y_{\epsilon})}{(W_{\epsilon}y_{\epsilon})^{\mathsf{H}}(W_{\epsilon}y_{\epsilon})},

if

is Hermitian and

otherwise.

The proof follows by substituting the convergence result for randomized Ritz vectors in
Theorem

2
into the standard analysis of compression methods; see, for example,
[
19

.
Compared with randomized Ritz values

leverages the Hermiticity of

\mu_{\epsilon}

Refinement via Rayleigh functional.

​We adopt the following lemma from
[

Lemma 4

Let
(\lambda,v)
be a simple eigenpair of

and

and

(\mu,w)
is measured by

\lvert\lambda-\mu\rvert\quad\text{and}\quad\angle(v,w),

(12)

respectively.
The experiments are designed to examine different aspects of the RRR procedure. First, we consider neutral modes arising in Hamiltonian systems, where the standard Rayleigh–Ritz procedure fails while the RRR procedure remains robust. Second, we solve the
butterfly
nonlinear eigenvalue problem from NLEVP
[
3
]
and illustrate that when
A(\lambda)
is Hermitian, refined randomized Ritz values exhibit quadratic convergence comparable to that of (standard) Ritz values. Finally, we investigate the empirical failure probability of the RRR procedure, illustrating the sharpness of the factor
1/\sqrt{\delta}
in
Theorem

2
.
This experiment also shows that this factor cannot be improved by oversampling.

5.1
Neutral modes in Hamiltonian systems

Consider a matrix pencil
A_{0}-\xi A_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n}
, where

A_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}&-A_{21}^{\mathsf{H}}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad A_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&I\\
I&0\end{bmatrix}

with
A_{11}
and
A_{22}
being Hermitian. Such generalized eigenvalue problems are often referred to as Hamiltonian eigenvalue problems
[
2
]
. We are interested in the neutral modes
[
4
]
, that is, an eigenvector
v
of the pencil
A_{0}-\xi A_{1}
satisfying
v^{\mathsf{H}}A_{1}v=0
.
Computing these neutral modes is important because they capture the directions in which the quadratic form associated with
A_{1}
vanishes, highlighting marginally stable behavior in the system. Identifying these modes allows for a more accurate analysis of the system’s critical dynamics and helps preserve the Hamiltonian structure in numerical computation, preventing spurious growth or decay of nearly neutral modes.

In this experiment, we consider a smoothly parameterized
A_{0}(\tau)
. Let
\lambda(\tau)
be a branch of eigenvalues of
A_{0}(\tau)-\lambda A_{1}
associated with neutral modes
v(\tau)
. Suppose that we have computed
v(\tau_{k})
for some integers
k
, and our goal is to approximate
v(\tau_{0})
and
\lambda(\tau_{0})
. In the following, we omit the parameter when it equals
\tau_{0}
. Let
\mathcal{W}_{k}=\mathsf{span}\{v(\tau_{1}),\dotsc,v(\tau_{k})\}
. Then
\angle(v,\mathcal{W}_{k})
is usually very small for a smooth
A_{0}(\cdot)
. However, since
v(\tau_{k})
are all neutral modes, the standard Rayleigh–Ritz procedure fails in exact arithmetic as the compressed matrix pencil vanishes. In the following, we show that RRR works well for this task.

We construct an example as follows: Let

Q=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}Q_{1}+Q_{2}&Q_{1}-Q_{2}\\
Q_{1}-Q_{2}&Q_{1}+Q_{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n},

where
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
are Gaussian unitary ensembles.
Here, a Gaussian unitary ensemble is constructed from the
Q
-factor of the QR decomposition of a (square) complex Gaussian random matrix.
Then
Q
is also a unitary matrix and
Q^{\mathsf{H}}A_{1}Q=A_{1}
.
Given a unit vector
v_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we assume the neutral modes admit

v(\tau)=\eta(\tau)Q\begin{bmatrix}\exp\bigl((\tau-\tau_{0})G\bigr)v_{1}\\
0\end{bmatrix}

where
G\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is a complex Gaussian random matrix.
Here
\eta(\tau)
is a normalization factor such that
\lVert v(\tau)\rVert=1
. Let

A_{0}=Q\begin{bmatrix}(I-v_{1}v_{1}^{\mathsf{H}})(G_{11}+G_{11}^{\mathsf{H}})(I-v_{1}v_{1}^{\mathsf{H}})&-\overline{\lambda}v_{1}v_{1}^{\mathsf{H}}-(I-v_{1}v_{1}^{\mathsf{H}})G_{21}^{\mathsf{H}}\\
\lambda v_{1}v_{1}^{\mathsf{H}}+G_{21}(I-v_{1}v_{1}^{\mathsf{H}})&G_{22}+G_{22}^{\mathsf{H}}\end{bmatrix}Q^{\mathsf{H}},

(13)

where
G_{11},G_{22}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
are also complex Gaussian random matrices,
G_{21}
will be specified later, and
\lambda
is the target eigenvalue.
Then by construction, we know that
A_{0}v=\lambda A_{1}v
.

Setting
n=2000
and
\lambda=1
, we choose
v_{1}
as a complex Gaussian random vector. We consider two choices of
G_{21}
, a zero matrix or a complex Gaussian random matrix. When
G_{21}=0
, then
v
is both a left and a right eigenvector, while it is only a right eigenvector if
G_{21}
is complex Gaussian. Let
\tau_{k}=10^{-3}\cdot k
. We report numerical results for
k=1,\dotsc,10
in
Figure

1
. It is clear that (standard) Ritz values cannot provide meaningful approximation of the target eigenvalue. In exact arithmetic, (standard) Ritz vectors are not well-defined. Due to roundoff errors, the standard Rayleigh–Ritz procedure returns Ritz vectors, but they lose three digits of accuracy.
The RRR procedure works well in this situation: the accuracy of randomized Ritz vectors is nearly optimal, that is, they closely follow the subspace angles
\angle(v,\mathcal{W}_{k})
; and refined randomized Ritz values achieve quadratic convergence when
G_{21}=0
and linear convergence for a complex Gaussian
G_{21}
.

5.2
The
butterfly
nonlinear eigenvalue problem

Let

A(\xi)=\sum_{i=0}^{4}c_{i}\xi^{i}A_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},

where
A_{0}
,
A_{2}
and
A_{4}
are symmetric and
A_{1}
and
A_{3}
are skew-symmetric.
Using the default parameters
c_{i}
from
[
3
]
and
n=4096
, we compute the eigenvalue closest to
\sigma\in\{2\!\cdot\!\mathsf{i},1+1\!\cdot\!\mathsf{i}\}
with a nonlinear Arnoldi-type method
[
13
]
.
Specifically, we take
w_{0}
as a complex Gaussian random starting vector, and then perform the residual inverse iteration
[
16
]
:

w_{k+1}=\eta_{k+1}\Bigl(w_{k}-\bigl(A(\sigma)\bigr)^{-1}A(\rho_{k})w_{k}\Bigr),

where
\eta_{k+1}
is a normalization factor such that
\lVert w_{k+1}\rVert=1
, and

\rho_{k}=\mathsf{Rf}(w_{k})=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\rho\in\mathbb{C}}\bigl\{\lvert\rho-\sigma\rvert\mid w_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}A(\rho)w_{k}=0\bigr\}.

We construct a trial subspace as
\mathcal{W}_{k}=\mathsf{span}\{w_{1},\dotsc,w_{k}\}
, and perform the standard and randomized Rayleigh–Ritz procedures to extract eigenpair approximations. The reference solution is computed by performing the residual inverse iteration until the residual norm is below
10^{-12}
.

\sigma=2\!\cdot\!\mathsf{i}

\lambda

A(\lambda)
Hermitian, both (standard) Ritz values and refined randomized Ritz values converge quadratically. In this situation, the behavior of the standard and randomized Rayleigh–Ritz procedures are similar. For the other situation, since
A(\lambda)
is non-Hermitian, both methods exhibit linear convergence, and (refined) randomized Ritz values and vectors achieve approximately two additional digits of accuracy compared to (standard) Ritz values and vectors.

5.3
Empirical probability and oversampling

The prefactors
C_{\mathsf{val}}
and
C_{\mathsf{vec}}
in
Theorem

2
both depend on
1/\sqrt{\delta}
. In certain randomized algorithms, such as the randomized SVD
[
8
]
, this issue can be mitigated by oversampling; that is, by replacing the
n\times m
complex Gaussian matrix
\Omega
with an
n\times(m+s)
complex Gaussian matrix
\Omega_{\mathrm{os}}
for some integer
s\geq 1
.
However, such a strategy is not effective in our setting.

We illustrate that with a numerical experiment. Consider a matrix pencil
A_{0}-\xi A_{1}
, where
A_{0},A_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{1000\times 1000}
are both complex Gaussian random matrices. We are interested in the eigenvalue closest to
0.01
. Let
W_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{C}^{1000\times 10}
be an orthonormal basis computed by running
10
steps of subspace shift-and-invert iterations from a complex Gaussian random starting block, where
\epsilon\approx 6.4\times 10^{-8}
. Then we extract approximate eigenpairs by the RRR procedure with and without oversampling. For oversampling, we use complex Gaussian matrices
\Omega_{\mathrm{os}}\in\mathbb{C}^{1000\times 20}
, that is
m=s=10
, and solve the rectangular eigenvalue problem of the pencil
\Omega_{\mathrm{os}}^{\mathsf{H}}A_{0}W_{\epsilon}-\xi\Omega_{\mathrm{os}}^{\mathsf{H}}W_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{C}^{20\times 10}
by the minimal perturbation approach proposed by
[
9
]
, which essentially solves a total least squares problem via SVD. With
2^{17}
independent trials on
\Omega
and
\Omega_{\mathrm{os}}
, we collect the empirical result in
Figure

3
, which indicates that the factor
1/\sqrt{\delta}
is sharp and cannot be improved by oversampling.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have introduced a randomized Rayleigh–Ritz (RRR) procedure for nonlinear eigenvalue problems.
Unlike the standard Rayleigh–Ritz approach, the RRR procedure reliably extracts approximate eigenpairs while achieving a convergence rate comparable to the ideal projection.
This addresses the long-standing eigenpair extraction problem posed in
1
.
Future work includes exploring ways to preserve Hermiticity for Hermitian problems and investigating how randomization can stabilize subspace methods in other contexts, such as evaluating matrix functions applied to vectors.

Appendix A
Proof of
Theorem

1

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\lVert y\rVert=1
.
We first show that
Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}B(\lambda)Y_{\perp}
is nonsingular via an argument similar to
Lemma

1
.
Since
\lambda
is a simple eigenvalue associated with left and right eigenvectors
x
and
y
, we know that

\mathrm{rank}(B(\lambda)Y_{\perp})=m-1,\quad x^{\mathsf{H}}B(\lambda)Y_{\perp}=0\quad\text{and}\quad x^{\mathsf{H}}B^{\prime}(\lambda)y\neq 0.

Thus,
[B^{\prime}(\lambda)y,B(\lambda)Y_{\perp}]\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
is nonsingular. In turn,
Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}B(\lambda)Y_{\perp}
is nonsingular, where we recall that
Z_{\perp}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathsf{span}\{B^{\prime}(\lambda)y\}^{\perp}
.

Then we consider the analytic expansion.
Since
\lambda
is simple,
[
1
, Thm. 2.1]
ensures that the eigenvalue
\lambda
and its corresponding eigenvector
y
are (locally) analytic with respect to the perturbation.
Thus, when
\epsilon
is sufficiently small, we can assume
\lambda+t\Delta\lambda\in\mathcal{D}
for all
0\leq t\leq 1
.
Consider the vector-valued function

f(t)=B(\lambda+t\Delta\lambda)\cdot(y+t\Delta y)+t\Delta B(\lambda+t\Delta\lambda)\cdot(y+t\Delta y).

Since
f(0)=f(1)=0
, we know that
\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=0
. Hence,

\lVert f^{\prime}(0)\rVert=\Bigl\lVert\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(0)-f^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigr\rVert\leq\int_{0}^{1}\lVert f^{\prime}(0)-f^{\prime}(t)\rVert\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\lVert f^{\prime\prime}(t)\rVert.

(14)

By the definition of
f
, we have

f^{\prime}(0)=\Delta\lambda\cdot B^{\prime}(\lambda)y+B(\lambda)\Delta y+\Delta B(\lambda)\cdot y.

(15)

For the second order derivative, direct computation yields that

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(t)=

\displaystyle(\Delta\lambda)^{2}\cdot\bigl(B^{\prime\prime}(\lambda+t\Delta\lambda)+\Delta B^{\prime\prime}(\lambda+t\Delta\lambda)\bigr)\cdot(y+\Delta y)

\displaystyle+2(\Delta\lambda)\cdot\bigl(B^{\prime}(\lambda+t\Delta\lambda)+\Delta B^{\prime}(\lambda+t\Delta\lambda)\bigr)\cdot\Delta y+2\Delta B(\lambda+t\Delta\lambda)\cdot\Delta y.

Applying the triangle inequality and
Equation

14
, we have

\lVert f^{\prime}(0)\rVert\leq\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\lVert f^{\prime\prime}(t)\rVert\leq(M+\epsilon)\lvert\Delta\lambda\rvert^{2}(1+\lVert\Delta y\rVert)+2(M+\epsilon)\lvert\Delta\lambda\rvert\lVert\Delta y\rVert+2\epsilon\lVert\Delta y\rVert.

(16)

Multiplying
x^{\mathsf{H}}
from the left-hand side of
Equation

15
yields that

\lvert\Delta\lambda\rvert=\frac{\lvert-x^{\mathsf{H}}\Delta B(\lambda)y+x^{\mathsf{H}}f^{\prime}(0)\rvert}{\lvert x^{\mathsf{H}}B^{\prime}(\lambda)y\rvert}\leq\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(B,\lambda)\cdot\bigl(\lVert\Delta B(\lambda)\rVert+\lVert f^{\prime}(0)\rVert\bigr),

(17)

where
x^{\mathsf{H}}B(\lambda)=0
is used. Multiplying
Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}
from the left-hand side of
Equation

15
yields

Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}f^{\prime}(0)=\Delta\lambda\cdot Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}B^{\prime}(\lambda)y+Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}B(\lambda)\begin{bmatrix}y&Y_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}y^{\mathsf{H}}\\
Y_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}\end{bmatrix}\Delta y+Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}\Delta B(\lambda)y.

Using
Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}B^{\prime}(\lambda)y=0
and
B(\lambda)y=0
, we have

Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}B(\lambda)Y_{\perp}\cdot Y_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}\Delta y=Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}f^{\prime}(0)-Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}\Delta B(\lambda)y.

Multiplying
(Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}B(\lambda)Y_{\perp})^{-1}
from the left-hand side and using
\Delta y=Y_{\perp}Y_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}\Delta y
, we have

\lVert\Delta y\rVert=\bigl\lVert(Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}B(\lambda)Y_{\perp})^{-1}Z_{\perp}^{\mathsf{H}}\bigl(\Delta B(\lambda)y-f^{\prime}(0)\bigr)\bigr\rVert\leq\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(B,\lambda)\cdot\bigl(\lVert\Delta B(\lambda)\rVert+\lVert f^{\prime}(0)\rVert\bigr).

(18)

We are now ready to complete the proof via a bootstrap argument. In the following, we assume
\epsilon\leq 1
and use
c
to denote generic constants independent of
\epsilon
, whose values may vary from line to line.
First, by
[
1
, Thm. 2.1]
, we have
\lvert\Delta\lambda\rvert\leq c\epsilon
and
\lVert\Delta y\rVert\leq c\epsilon
. Hence,
Equation

16
implies that
\lVert f^{\prime}(0)\rVert\leq c\epsilon^{2}
. Substituting this bound into
Equations

17
and
18
, we obtain

\lvert\Delta\lambda\rvert\leq\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(B,\lambda)\cdot(\lVert B(\lambda)\rVert+c\epsilon^{2})\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert\Delta y\rVert\leq\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(B,\lambda)\cdot(\lVert B(\lambda)\rVert+c\epsilon^{2}).

(19)

Substituting
Equation

19
into
Equation

16
yields

\lVert f^{\prime}(0)\rVert\leq M\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(B,\lambda)\cdot\bigl(\kappa_{\mathsf{val}}(B,\lambda)+4\cdot\kappa_{\mathsf{vec}}(B,\lambda)\bigr)\epsilon^{2}+c\epsilon^{3}.

(20)

Substituting
Equation

20
back into
Equations

17
and
18
completes the proof.

Appendix B
Proof of
Lemma

5

To avoid the proliferation of constants, we use
C_{A}
to denote generic constants depending only on
A(\cdot)
, whose values may change from line to line.

Let us first show that, as
\epsilon\to 0
, there exists a stationary point

\rho_{\epsilon}\in\Bigl\{\rho\in\mathbb{C}\Bigm|\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\rho}}\lVert A(\rho)w_{\epsilon}\rVert^{2}=0\Bigr\},\quad\text{such that}\quad\lvert\rho_{\epsilon}-\lambda\rvert\leq C_{A}\epsilon.

(21)

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\lVert v\rVert=\lVert w_{\epsilon}\rVert=1
and
\lVert v-w_{\epsilon}\rVert=2\sin\epsilon/2\leq\epsilon
. Denote

\lambda=\alpha_{0}+\beta_{0}\!\cdot\!\mathsf{i}\quad\text{and}\quad v=x_{0}+y_{0}\!\cdot\!\mathsf{i},\quad\text{where}\quad\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\quad\text{and}\quad x_{0},y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Consider the
real
analytic function

f(\alpha,\beta,x,y)=\lVert A(\rho)w\rVert^{2},\quad\text{where}\quad\rho=\alpha+\beta\!\cdot\!\mathsf{i}\quad\text{and}\quad w=x+y\!\cdot\!\mathsf{i}.

Since
A(\lambda)v=0
, we have
f(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0},x_{0},y_{0})=0
. Define the function
G\colon\mathbb{R}^{2n+2}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{2}
as

G(\alpha,\beta,x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{\alpha}f(\alpha,\beta,x,y)\\
\nabla_{\beta}f(\alpha,\beta,x,y)\end{bmatrix}.

We know
G(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0},x_{0},y_{0})=0
. Since
A(\cdot)
is holomorphic, Taylor expansion yields that

A(\lambda+\Delta\lambda)v=A(\lambda)v+\Delta\lambda\cdot A^{\prime}(\lambda)v+\mathcal{O}(\lvert\Delta\lambda\rvert^{2})=\Delta\lambda\cdot A^{\prime}(\lambda)v+\mathcal{O}(\lvert\Delta\lambda\rvert^{2}).

Since
\lambda
is a simple eigenvalue, we know
u^{\mathsf{H}}A^{\prime}(\lambda)v\neq 0
, implying that
A^{\prime}(\lambda)v\neq 0
. Thus,

\lVert A(\lambda+\Delta\lambda)v\rVert^{2}=\lvert\Delta\lambda\rvert^{2}\lVert A^{\prime}(\lambda)v\rVert^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\lvert\Delta\lambda\rvert^{3}),

implying that the Jacobian matrix of
G
is

\nabla_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}f(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0},x_{0},y_{0})=2\lVert A^{\prime}(\lambda)v\rVert^{2}I_{2}\succ 0.

The implicit function theorem asserts that there exist real analytical functions
f_{\alpha}(x,y)
and
f_{\beta}(x,y)
defined in a neighborhood of
(x_{0},y_{0})
such that

\alpha_{0}=f_{\alpha}(x_{0},y_{0}),\quad\beta_{0}=f_{\beta}(x_{0},y_{0}),\quad G\bigl(f_{\alpha}(x,y),f_{\beta}(x,y),x,y\bigr)=0.

Here both
f_{\alpha}
and
f_{\beta}
are independent of
w_{\epsilon}
.
Since
\lVert w_{\epsilon}-v\rVert\leq\epsilon
, we know that
\lVert\Re w_{\epsilon}-x_{0}\rVert\leq\epsilon
and
\lVert\Im w_{\epsilon}-y_{0}\rVert\leq\epsilon
. When
\epsilon
is sufficiently small, we can take

\rho_{\epsilon}=f_{\alpha}(\Re w_{\epsilon},\Im w_{\epsilon})+f_{\beta}(\Re w_{\epsilon},\Im w_{\epsilon})\!\cdot\!\mathsf{i}\in\Bigl\{\rho\in\mathbb{C}\Bigm|\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\rho}}\lVert A(\rho)w_{\epsilon}\rVert^{2}=0\Bigr\},

such that
\lvert\rho_{\epsilon}-\lambda\rvert\leq C_{A}\epsilon
. Thus,
Equation

21
is justified.

Then we further investigate
\lvert\rho_{\epsilon}-\lambda\rvert
. By the stationary condition of
Equation

21
, we have

\bigl(A^{\prime}(\rho_{\epsilon})w_{\epsilon}\bigr)^{\mathsf{H}}\bigl(A(\rho_{\epsilon})w_{\epsilon}\bigr)=0.

(22)

Taylor expansions of
A(\rho_{\epsilon})w_{\epsilon}
and
A^{\prime}(\rho_{\epsilon})w_{\epsilon}
give that

A(\rho_{\epsilon})w_{\epsilon}=(\rho_{\epsilon}-\lambda)A^{\prime}(\lambda)v+A(\lambda)(w_{\epsilon}-v)+r_{2},\quad\text{and}\quad A^{\prime}(\rho_{\epsilon})w_{\epsilon}=A^{\prime}(\lambda)v+r_{1},

where
\lVert r_{2}\rVert\leq C_{A}\epsilon^{2}
and
\lVert r_{1}\rVert\leq C_{A}\epsilon
.
Here, we use
\lVert v-w_{\epsilon}\rVert\leq\epsilon
,
\lvert\lambda-\rho_{\epsilon}\rvert\leq C_{A}\epsilon
and
A(\lambda)v=0
.
Substituting these two expansions into
Equation

22
, we have

\Bigl\lvert(\rho_{\epsilon}-\lambda)\lVert A^{\prime}(\lambda)v\rVert^{2}+\bigl(A^{\prime}(\lambda)v\bigr)^{\mathsf{H}}\bigl(A(\lambda)(w_{\epsilon}-v)\bigr)\Bigr\rvert\leq C_{A}\epsilon^{2}.

Rearranging this equation and using the submultiplicativity, we complete the proof:

\lvert\rho_{\epsilon}-\lambda\rvert\leq\frac{\lVert A(\lambda)\rVert}{\lVert A^{\prime}(\lambda)v\rVert}\epsilon+C_{A}\epsilon^{2}.
