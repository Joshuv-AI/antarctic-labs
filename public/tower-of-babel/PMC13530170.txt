First report of tenacibaculosis in wild-caught Pacific salmon
Authors: Emiliano Di Cicco, Sean C. Godwin, Kaitlyn R. Zinn, Stephen D. Johnston, Karia H. Kaukinen, Angela D. Schulze, Shaorong Li, Jonathan F. Archambault, Kaleb N. R. Mantha-Rensi, Kayla A. J. Zielke, William S. Bugg, Gideon J. Mordecai, Arthur L. Bass, Christoph M. Deeg, Andrew W. Bateman, Scott G. Hinch, Kristina M. Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding disease dynamics in wild fish populations is essential for effective conservation and fisheries management, yet diagnosing marine diseases in wild organisms is challenging. Tenacibaculosis, a bacterial disease caused by Tenacibaculum spp., is emblematic of this challenge, having been widely reported in farmed Atlantic (Salmo salar) and Chinook (Oncorhynchus tshawytscha) salmon, but never confirmed in any species of wild Pacific salmon (Oncorhynchus spp.). Here, we present the first report of tenacibaculosis in wild-caught Pacific salmon. During holding experiments conducted with Chinook salmon captured in Barkley Sound, Canada in 2022, we observed external lesions in 16 of 99 fish and associated high mortality. Through molecular screening, analysis of genetic sequences, and microscopic localisation of the infectious agents present, we identified Tenacibaculum dicentrarchi as the likely causative agent of tenacibaculosis in the fish with the most severe lesions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-55640-4.

Introduction

Identifying, detecting, and evaluating the effects of marine pathogens is of growing importance to scientists and managers focused on at-risk marine organisms1. Pathogens can regulate populations in any habitat, but the diseases causes by marine pathogens tend to be particularly challenging to study because: (1) pathogen detection and host collection are difficult2, (2) marine hosts often die before disease can be observed3,4, and (3) pathogens spread much faster in the ocean than on land5. These challenges are exacerbated for migratory species like Pacific salmon (Oncorhynchus spp.), which can travel past multiple exposure hotspots of interacting infectious agents6.

Of the many marine diseases affecting salmonids globally, a recent focus of concern has been tenacibaculosis, a cosmopolitan bacterial disease of marine fishes caused by Tenacibaculum species7. Tenacibaculum species are considered opportunistic pathogens, causing disease in otherwise compromised hosts8. Tenacibaculosis has been documented globally in salmon aquaculture facilities (e.g., Norway9:, Chile10:, New Zealand11:) and is characterized by ulcerative skin lesions, mouth erosion, and frayed fins12. Tenacibaculosis has been observed in both farmed Atlantic (Salmo salar)13 and Pacific salmon (Oncorhynchus spp.), including in net-reared coho (O. kisutch)14,15 and Chinook (O. tshawytscha)11,16. The disease has also been reproduced in experimental challenge studies of Chinook with two species of Tenacibaculum17.

While the progression of infection by some Tenacibaculum species into clinical disease (i.e., tenacibaculosis) is relatively well resolved in farmed salmonids, evidence of tenacibaculosis in wild Pacific salmon is considered equivocal despite regular detections of Tenacibaculum spp. in these fish18,19. In British Columbia (BC), Canada, outbreaks of tenacibaculosis comprise > 60% of the fish health events reported on Atlantic salmon farms20 and can lead to cumulative mortality upwards of 40%, if left untreated21. These disease manifestations in farmed Atlantic salmon in BC are usually referred to as “mouthrot” or “yellow mouth” due to the presence of oral plaques and ulcers, but these symptoms are a subset of clinical signs for tenacibaculosis in Atlantic salmon22and other salmonids worldwide (e.g10,11.,). Tenacibaculum species are regularly detected in wild juvenile Pacific salmonids via molecular methods18 (K. Miller unpubl. data), and one well-studied species (T. maritimum) is associated with reduced body condition and cohort survival in these fish23. Despite these frequent detections of the causative pathogen for tenacibaculosis, to our knowledge no report exists of the disease in wild-caught Pacific salmonids. Given that tenacibaculosis occurs in Pacific salmon protected from predation10,16,24, it seems likely that diseased wild individuals simply die from disease or predation before they can be collected—a classic conundrum in fish disease ecology3.

Here, we present the first report of tenacibaculosis in wild-caught Pacific salmon, and identify the likely causative agent of the disease in the severely lesioned fish. We observed development of the disease in wild-caught Chinook held in tanks for a broader study25. We describe the clinical manifestation of the disease in the fish with the most severe lesions and through molecular screening, analysis of genetic sequences, and microscopic localisation of the infectious agents present, we identify Tenacibaculum dicentrarchi as the likely causative agent of tenacibaculosis in these fish.

Materials and methods

Fish collection and holding

As part of a broader study to assess the survival of salmon captured and released in recreational fisheries25, we caught wild Chinook salmon using recreational fishing gear in Barkley Sound, British Columbia, Canada (48.845° N, 125.215° W) from June to September 2022 for four holding experiments. In the first experiment, ulcerative lesions began appearing on the fish within a few days and we observed high mortality of the affected fish. We collected lesion samples from these fish, but lacked samples taken upon capture to determine baseline infection status. After consulting a veterinarian, we amended the fish collection protocol for the subsequent experiments to include non-lethal skin swab and gill sampling at the time of capture to determine infection status, in case similar outbreaks of disease occurred upon holding.

The fish analysed in this study were from the third and fourth holding experiments, for which fish were caught over four days and held for a subsequent 10 days. The third experiment involved 47 fish and was carried out from August 9 to 22, 2022; the fourth experiment contained 52 fish and was conducted from August 23 to September 5. Hereafter, we refer to these as the ‘Aug9’ and ‘Aug23’ holds. We captured fish with recreational trolling gear, held them in a 660 L insulated container with seawater pumped from below the thermocline to ensure continuity in environmental conditions between capture and holding, and collected non-lethal tissue samples (see section below). Within two hours, we transferred the fish to four 12,800 L flow-through tanks at the Bamfield Marine Sciences Centre (0.0012–0.0026 kg/L). Tank water was pumped from Bamfield Inlet (at ~ 30 m depth, below the thermocline) and filtered for coarse materials. We disinfected tanks with Virkon S (1% solution for 10 min; Dynamic Aqua Supply Ltd., Surrey, Canada) before, between, and after the experiments to avoid contamination or cross-infection. Details on fish lengths and environmental conditions for capture and holding are provided in Table S1.

All of this study’s procedures were conducted in accordance with the Canadian Council of Animal Care guidelines using methods approved by the University of British Columbia (permit #A19-0193) and Bamfield Marine Sciences Centre (RS-22-04). All fish that didn’t die due to illness while being held in the tanks after capture (i.e., the moribund fish and the live, apparently healthy fish at the end of each experiment) were euthanized with a blow to the head followed by cervical dislocation, in compliance with these protocols, and disposed of in accordance with institutional biosecurity protocols. Fish were collected under Fisheries and Oceans Canada (DFO) scientific collection permit (XR 143-2022) and moved to the onshore holding tanks with DFO transport permits (129330-2022). All methods are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Tissue sampling

To assess infectious disease progression in the Aug9 and Aug23 holds, we collected skin swab samples and portions of gills for molecular analysis, both at the time of capture in the field and again at the end of each experiment or when fish became moribund or died (see Table S1 for sample sizes). Skin swabs were taken on the flanks at the lateral line, and non-lethal gill samples were taken from the tips of 3–4 primary lamellae using sterile cuticle cutters26. From seven individuals showing the most severe lesions—hereafter called ‘featured’ fish (Table S2)—we also sampled gills, liver, and kidney (for molecular analysis and histology), as well as spleen, heart, and muscle (for histology). We preserved molecular samples in RNAlater (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and histology samples in 10% neutral buffered formalin.

Molecular screening

Using high-throughput quantitative polymerase chain reaction (HT-qPCR), we screened a subset of samples (Table S1), in roughly equal numbers of lesioned and non-lesioned fish, for nine infectious agents that could potentially be associated with ulcerative lesions in salmonids in BC (Table S3), including three species of Tenacibaculum (T. maritimum, T. dicentrarchi, and T. finnmarkense).

Genetic sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

To confirm Tenacibaculum species identification, we performed multilocus sequence analysis (MLSA) targeting seven informative loci (Table S4) on ten samples from eight qPCR-positive fish (five featured and three non-featured fish; Table S5) selected from those with ulcerative lesions and high Tenacibaculum loads across a range of dates and tanks. We assessed the PCR amplicons for the seven loci for size and quantity and pooled for each sample prior to purification with DNA Ampure Beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, USA), after which we re-quantified the purified, pooled samples using the Quant-IT dsDNA BR kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and performed PCR barcoding using the 1D PCR Barcoding 96 amplicons kit for the Nanopore (Oxford Nanopore Technologies Ltd., Oxford, UK).

Samples were sequenced at the Canada’s Michael Smith Genome Sciences Centre (GSC; Vancouver, Canada). Sequencing by the GSC involved ONT tethering and loading onto one flow cell of the promethION (Oxford Nanopore Technologies Ltd., Oxford, UK). Amplicon sequences were obtained as FASTQ files and processed using NanoPlot27 for quality assessment. FASTQ files were analyzed using Medaka28 for consensus sequence generation using a custom Bash script, aligning them to a reference database to generate polished consensus sequences. A secondary filtering step using Samtools depth ensured sequences with a coverage depth of less than 20 were masked.

To confirm the identity of these sequences, we performed a nucleotide Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) query for each one29 against the National Center for Biotechnology (NCBI) nucleotide database30. We retained the top three hits for phylogenetic analysis, downloading them from Genbank31 alongside reference sequences of Tenacibaculum species known to occur on salmon, using the rentrez package32 in R version 4.4.033. The majority of top BLAST hits were from Tenacibaculum spp. in Atlantic salmon, there were also hits to Tenacibaculum spp. in other teleost species from around the world.

To provide further support for the molecular-detection and sequencing results, we generated a consensus phylogenetic tree using a maximum likelihood approach. We first concatenated and aligned the sequences of the seven loci using Multiple Alignment using Fast Fourier Transform (MAFFT34;). Not all sequence isolates on genbank contain all seven loci. We generated maximum likelihood phylogenetic trees using IQ-TREE 235 and its automatic model selection feature to select a substitution model based on best-fit criteria. This method was first recommended by36 and widely used in the literature, for example by37. We used ultrafast bootstrap approximation with 1000 replications to calculate branch support values38. For visualisation purposes, we midpoint-rooted the phylogenetic trees using the R package phytools39 and annotated them using the ggtree package40.

Histology and in-situ hybridisation

We conducted standard histological analyses for the seven featured fish—i.e., those with the most severe lesions, for which we retained tissue samples for histology. We embedded the histology tissue samples in paraffin and prepared the histology slides from these, staining with routine hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) solutions for standard pathological evaluation. From consecutive tissue sections, we performed in-situ hybridisation (ISH) with T. dicentrarchi and T. maritimum specific probes to detect and localise each bacterial agent within the lesions. Given the results of the molecular tests, T. dicentrarchi was the primary ISH target, but we also performed ISH against T. maritimum to assess whether a mixed infection may have contributed to the observed disease manifestation. We conducted ISH on all seven featured fish as per the manufacturer’s instructions (ACD Bio Amplification kit RNAscope 2.5HD detection reagent RED, ref. #322360) using probes against T. maritimum (ACD Bio RNAscope, ref. #1200621/C1) and T. dicentrarchi (ACD Bio RNAscope, ref. #1200611/C1). These probes incorporated the regions used in the species-specific qPCR assays for the respective species, as well as additional sequences unique to the respective species, to ensure specificity. We also used positive and negative control probes to determine the accuracy and specificity of the test (ACD Bio RNAscope Positive CTRL Om/ppib ref. #540651; Negative CTRL DapB ref. #310043).

Results

Clinical observations

Within four days of transportation to the holding tanks, 13% and 17% of the fish in the Aug9 and Aug23 holds, respectively, displayed classic clinical signs of tenacibaculosis (Nowlan et al. 2020): lethargy, loss of balance, abnormal swimming behavior, and external lesions involving the caudal peduncle, fins, belly, trunk and mouth (n = 7 featured fish, n = 8 non-featured fish; Table S1). The erosions were mainly located on the fins and tails, while the ulcers typically involved the deeper muscle tissue (Fig. 1). Genetic sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

For the eight sequenced post-hold samples (Table S5), from six fish qPCR-positive for T. dicentrarchi, the top BLAST hits matched most closely with T. dicentrarchi across the majority of loci (Fig. S1A). On our constructed phylogenetic tree, all the post-hold sequences grouped together adjacent to the T. dicentrarchi clade, with swabs and tissues tending to cluster within their respective categories (Fig. S1B). The two sequenced pre-hold samples, which were qPCR-positive for T. maritimum and -negative for T. dicentrarchi (Table S5), showed considerable variability across loci (Fig. S1A), but overall phylogenetic analysis placed these closest to either T. ovolyticum or T. maritimum (Fig. S1B).

Histology and in-situ hybridisation

Histological analyses of the seven featured fish revealed lesions primarily affecting the skin and muscle, and consisting of ulcerative dermatitis and myositis, with all featured fish presenting mild to severe scale loss, epidermal and dermal spongiosis, and deep ulcerative lesions. The ulcers often extended past the dermis and involved the underlying muscle bundles, which showed myodegeneration and necrosis. The lesions were associated with mats of filamentous, rod-shaped bacteria (Figs. 3A, S2), which were stained by a T. dicentrarchi-specific probe via ISH (Figs. 3B, S2; Table S6). The gills also showed necrosis and lysis of the lamellar epithelium, filamentous bacteria infiltrating into the epithelium, and mats of bacteria proliferating in the overlying mucus (Figs. 3C, S3). Discussion

In this report, we document an outbreak of tenacibaculosis in wild-caught Chinook salmon held in seawater-fed, experimental tanks. To our knowledge, these findings constitute the first report of tenacibaculosis in wild-caught Pacific salmon. We also identify T. dicentrarchi as the likely causative agent of the disease in the most severely lesioned (i.e., featured) fish.

Multiple lines of evidence converge to support our final diagnosis of T. dicentrarchi as the causative agent of tenacibaculosis and mortality. First, qPCR testing detected high levels of T. dicentrarchi (but not T. maritimum, except in one fish, at a low load) in skin swabs and tissues of the most severely lesioned (i.e., featured) fish, with no consistent detection of other infectious agents known to cause ulcerative lesions in Pacific salmon (Table S7). Second, genetic sequencing confirmed detections of T. dicentrarchi in qPCR-positive swabs and tissue samples collected after holding (Table S5). Third, sequences from these samples were all placed adjacent to the T. dicentrarchi clade in our consensus phylogenetic tree (Fig. S1). Fourth, histological analysis of the featured fish revealed the typical signs of tenacibaculosis, including deep ulceration of the dermis and muscle, resulting in necrotic lesions associated with mats of filamentous rod-shaped bacteria (Avendaño‐Herrera et al. 2016; Figs. 3, S2, S3). Finally, in-situ hybridisation demonstrated localisation of T. dicentrarchi (but not T. maritimum) nucleic acids in these bacterial mats in the gills and skin lesions of the featured fish, as well as evidence of systemic infection in one individual. We cannot, however, exclude the possibility that T. maritimum may have caused less severe tenacibaculosis in some non-featured fish, particularly in the Aug23 hold where T. maritimum (but not T. dicentrarchi) was detected in two of the post-hold samples from non-featured lesioned fish (Table S7).

While our findings represent the first report of tenacibaculosis resulting from T. dicentrarchi infection in wild-caught Pacific salmon, additional work is needed to verify the mechanisms underlying this outbreak. Confirmatory research should include culture of the bacterium from wild-caught fish and controlled challenge experiments. Unfortunately, our attempts at culturing bacteria from the featured fish in this study failed due to unsuitable media and storage conditions. Challenge experiments would isolate the chain of transmission, in contrast to observational studies like this one that allow for multiple possibilities. Transmission of T. dicentrarchi to the featured fish in this study, for instance, could have arisen from: (1) T. dicentrarchi in the wild and subsequent co-habitation, (2) contamination of the holding tanks (unlikely, given Virkon S disinfection protocols), or (3) colonisation via raw seawater in the flow-through tanks. Further, the wild-caught fish from this study were handled multiple times during capture and transport, which could have damaged mucous surfaces, made the fish more vulnerable to infection as well as the development of ulcers, and increased the risk of an outbreak in holding tanks. Such experimental work could also allow for the disentangling of co-infection effects, given that we observed T. maritimum in numerous individuals prior to and during the study (Fig. 2), though not at high loads in any of the most severely lesioned (i.e., featured) fish. Our results suggest that T. dicentrarchi was likely the etiological agent of morbidity and mortality, but we cannot exclude the involvement or synergistic effects of T. maritimum in disease onset or progression. Challenge experiments could also help clarify any role that mucus removal during capture and handling may have played in the development of the mouth and caudal peduncle lesions we observed.

Together, these results represent the first report of tenacibaculosis in wild-caught Pacific salmon, with T. dicentrarchi as the likely causative agent. These findings add to the global body of evidence linking Tenacibaculum spp. to tenacibaculosis in Pacific salmon, aligning with previous cases in cultured settings, including hatchery Chinook in California16, farmed Chinook in New Zealand11, farmed coho in Chile14, and hatchery coho in Alaska15.
