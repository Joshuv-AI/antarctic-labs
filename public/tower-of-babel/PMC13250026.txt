European caring in later life: taking a life course perspective
Authors: Anne McMunn, Jeroen Spijker
Published: 2026

Increased longevity has produced dramatic shifts in the population structure of European countries (Eurostat 2019). In some European countries, but not all, these demographic changes have not been accompanied by gains in later-life health, leading to morbidity expansion and a greater need for care for older people (Straka et al. 2024; Permanyer and Spijker, 2020). While European countries vary dramatically in how they have responded to rising care needs, all rely to a certain extent on unpaid care provided by family members and friends (Eurocarers 2020; Social Protection Committee, the European Commission 2014). Throughout this special collection, we use the terms ‘care’ and ‘caregiving’ to refer to help or assistance that is provided, usually unpaid, to family members or friends with mental and physical health conditions, disabilities, and addictions.

While there is consistent evidence on inequalities in who provides care, as well as on the potential consequences of caring, important gaps remain in our understanding of care in Europe from a life course perspective. Gender and socio-economic inequalities in both the likelihood and intensity of caregiving are well documented. Women are generally more likely than men to provide care, do so for longer periods, and more intensively, particularly in midlife (McMunn et al. 2019; Rodriques et al. 2013). Among older carers, however, gender differences tend to be smaller (Dahlberg et al. 2007; The Eurocare Team 2024a, b; Glauber 2017), in part because retirement may reduce the employment constraints that limit men’s involvement in caregiving (Glauber 2017), and because spousal care becomes more prominent; indeed, among married older people, gender differences in caregiving may disappear altogether (Del Bono et al. 2009). Carers have also consistently been shown to be socio-economically disadvantaged (Petrillo et al. 2025; Quashie et al. 2022; von Saenger et al. 2023). However, few studies have investigated the life course predictors of who becomes a carer. One previous study investigating the role of work and partnership life courses as predictors of midlife care for parents/in-laws in the UK showed gender differences for employment, but not partnership life courses. Long periods of part-time employment predicted a greater likelihood of taking up parental care for women, while life courses characterised by strong ties to full-time employment predicted parental care for men, and long-term marriage was linked with parental care for both men and women (McMunn et al. 2020).

Regarding the impact of caring, much of the research has been based on cross-sectional samples or has focused on care for specific groups, such as those with dementia. One systematic review of the impact of care on health outcomes identified 15 studies which used some form of causal analysis, of which only five employed a longitudinal design and included both men and women (Bom et al. 2019). Of these, two studies focusing on care for parents found an increase in depressive symptoms following take up of care provision, while the remaining three reported mixed results depending on the subgroups or time frames investigated. At the same time, a growing comparative literature using longitudinal data has investigated the impact of care provision on employment outcomes (Carr et al. 2018; Gomez-Leon et al. 2017; Moussa 2018), in some cases using robust causal inference techniques (Ciccarelli and Van Soest 2018). Studies of the impact of care on both health and employment consistently show that it is intensive care that impacts people’s lives most (Bom and Stöckel 2021; Carr et al. 2018; Ciccarelli and Van Soest 2018; Gomez-Leon et al. 2017; Kaschowitz and Brandt 2017; Lacey et al. 2024; Zueras and Grundy 2024). However, very few studies have investigated the dynamic nature of care and its characteristics over the adult life course. In addition, while the impact of care on health and employment has been investigated, there is no large-scale population evidence on the impact of caregiving on wider social participation beyond employment.

The impact of care on health, employment, and social participation outcomes is likely to vary by country context due to differing cultural norms and expectations, as well as the availability of support resources. Studies from previous decades have shown that both gender inequality in care provision and the impact of caregiving on employment are greater in countries that rely on family-based care models (Colombo et al. 2011; Rodriques et al. 2013). More recently, a comparison of associations between partner care and well-being across European regions found the impact on women’s well-being was greater in Southern European countries. Another recent study found increased spending on health and social care to be associated with a slightly lower likelihood—but increased country-level income inequality with a slightly higher likelihood—of providing care (Floridi et al. 2022; Quashie et al. 2022). Current evidence on how country-level ‘care regimes’—the nexus of formal mechanisms for supporting carers—have influenced gender inequality in caregiving, as well as the impact of caregiving on health, employment, and wider participation in society among the current generations of older people in Europe is lacking.

A life course perspective is useful for helping us to think about the dynamic nature of care, such as characterising durations of care intensity; investigating life course predictors of who provides care, including the impact of life course transitions on the uptake of care; and disentangling directions of association between care, health, and other life course domains. Taking a life course approach centres our attention on time, on predictors of development and decline with age, differences by geographic and historic contexts, and the influential role of ‘linked lives’, and the connections and interdependencies between individuals (Elder et al. 2003). Bernardi and colleagues have characterised this life course complexity as a cube, capturing the three interdependent axes of time, life domain, and levels of action from inner-individual to macrolevel contexts (Bernardi et al. 2019). More closely focused on causal questions linking risk factors and later health, life course epidemiology brings a focus on developmentally sensitive periods, life course accumulation of (dis)advantage, and mechanistic pathways (Kuh et al. 2025). These perspectives invite us to think about the potential importance of when care provision occurs in the life course in relation to other transitions, its duration and intensity, the relationships from which the dyadic care activity derives, the distal and proximal mechanisms linking care with health, well-being, and social outcomes, and differences by policy and cultural context, and individual-level resources.

Eurocare was a three-year (2021–2024) consortium project of researchers in the UK, Germany, Spain, and Norway, funded by the Joint Programming Initiative, More Years Better Lives Programme, to investigate inequalities in the impact of providing care at different stages of the life course across European countries. Work packages focused on providing care in early adulthood, midlife, and later life and on a range of outcomes, as well as changes over time and comparisons across European countries. The aim of this Special Collection on ‘European Caring in Later life: Taking a Life Course Perspective’ is to draw together six articles from our work package focused on caring in later life. In line with the previous evidence (Verbakel 2018), we found that the prevalence of providing care varies dramatically across European countries, with older people more likely to provide care in Nordic countries but for fewer hours. Carers are more likely to care for long hours in Southern and Eastern European countries (The Eurocare Team 2024b)), suggesting that state support for care allows family members to participate more fully in care, but at levels that are compatible with maintaining employment and good health. Articles in this collection adopt a life course perspective to ask who becomes a carer in later life, what the longer-term consequences of caring in later life are, and whether there are inequalities in these associations, using two large-scale European data sets: the Survey of Health and Retirement in Europe (SHARE) and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA). Many readers of this journal will be familiar with SHARE and ELSA, longitudinal sister studies that are nationally representative panel studies of ageing and designed to be comparable. Both include participants aged 50 + and collect information on physical and mental health, well-being, finances, employment and retirement, and social and civic participation, as well as information on caring, relationship to care recipient, weekly hours spent caring, and type of care provided. ELSA started in 2002/03 and respondents are interviewed every two years with a nurse visit every other wave. More than 18,000 people have taken part in ELSA, and nine waves of data are currently available. SHARE began in 2004, and currently, eight waves of data are available from 29 countries with more than 100.000 respondents.

Three articles in this collection examine how country context shapes care provision and its consequences in late life and how it shapes gender inequity in caregiving (Almeida-Meza et al. 2025; Deindl et al. 2026; Spijker et al. 2025). At a contextual level, the division of care responsibilities between state and family depends on the country’s care regime, which varies by the extent to which policies seek to unburden families from care responsibilities (de-familialism) or instead foster dependencies among family members (familialism, which can be supported or go unsupported) (Zigante 2018). In A care regime typology of elder, long-term care institutions, Damme et al. (2025) build on the seminal work of Saraceno and Keck (2010), using latent profile analysis to derive a European typology of later-life care regimes based on a range of indicators of institutional support. This regime typology is then used in the aforementioned papers to investigate country context differences in how employment and family histories shape men’s and women’s likelihood of caregiving, as well as the consequences of caregiving on employment exits and wider social participation.

The impact of care on labour market participation has been a focus of academic research, but its impact on wider forms of social participation, which may become more salient post-retirement, is less well understood. In Care provision and social participation among older adults in Europe: longitudinal evidence from the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing, Almeida-Meza et al. (2025) investigate associations between becoming a carer in later life and subsequent changes in participation in volunteering activities and membership of community organisations. Contrary to hypotheses, they find that carers who care more frequently (weekly or more) are more likely than non-carers or those caring infrequently to volunteer or belong to a community group. Importantly, this is more likely to be the case in countries with the most supportive care regimes. In The impact of providing intensive care and practical help in midlife on employment transitions in Europe, Spijker and colleagues (2025) use causal methods in SHARE to investigate intersectional (gender x income x country context) differences in the impact of providing intensive, daily care on employment exits or reduced working hours. They find that women are less likely to exit employment in response to daily caring in countries with strong support regimes. Thus, both of these articles add to the evidence that state support for care reduces gender inequalities and allows caregivers to combine care with alternative activities.

All of the articles in this issue consider differences by care characteristics, such as type of care, care intensity, and the relationship to the care recipient. The next set of three articles describes the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of care characteristics, as well as gender and socio-economic inequality in caregiving and its impact. The first investigates the impact of care on depression. While recent investigations of carers’ mental health trajectories in SHARE suggest that depressive symptoms decrease over time (Agapitos et al. 2024), this is likely to vary by care characteristics. In How are location and type of caring associated with the carer’s mental health? Cross-sectional and longitudinal findings from SHARE, Schaps et al. (2025) examine associations between three types of care (personal care, practical help, and paperwork) and number of depressive symptoms. They show that in-home personal care in particular is associated with an increase in depressive symptoms. This is true for men and women, but it is women and those from socio-economically disadvantaged households who are more likely to provide in-home personal care. Most studies of care provision provide snapshots or repeated snapshots over time. In Determinants of trajectories of informal caregiving in later life: evidence from England, Di Gessa and Deindl (2024) use latent trajectory analysis in ELSA to derive longitudinal caregiving trajectories, which include both care transitions and changes in intensity. They find that women, those in good health, those living in socio-economically disadvantaged households, and those living with someone in poor health are most likely to provide long-term intensive care. And, finally, The association between employment and family histories and caregiving in later life: European findings based on SHARE and ELSA (Deindl et al. 2026) also characterises life course trajectories, but here sequence analysis is used to identify patterns in employment and family histories as predictors of later caregiving. As care can be time demanding, those with time restraints due to employment and family responsibilities might be less inclined to take up caregiving, although gender norms suggest this may not be equally the case for men and women. As was seen among midlife carers of parents in the UK (McMunn et al. 2020), Deindl and colleagues find gender differences in the ways in which employment predicts uptake of caregiving. Women who spent long periods out of employment to provide home and childcare, and those in self-employment, were more likely to take up later caregiving, but the same was not true for men. Unlike in the UK, in SHARE, partnership life courses only predicted uptake of caregiving for men (separated men more likely to provide care outside the household and less likely to provide in-home care), but parental life courses predicted caregiving uptake for both men and women (childless more likely to care).

The articles in this special collection add life course detail on the topic of caregiving in Europe. Consistent findings across the collection highlight that it is intensive forms of caregiving—long hours, in-home, personal care, and care of long durations—that are most likely to lead to increased depression and employment exits. They also demonstrate the persistence of gender and socio-economic inequity in providing these types of care that are shown to be detrimental. However, findings here also suggest, in line with the previous evidence, that state investment in carer support can reduce the intensity of care provision, which is likely to pay dividends in improved mental health and employment participation, while also mitigating inequities in care provision. Given increasing care need across European countries, alongside the potentially diminishing pool of family carers due to smaller family sizes, partnership dissolution, and women’s strengthening ties to paid work, these lessons are vital for informing long-term care policy in Europe.
