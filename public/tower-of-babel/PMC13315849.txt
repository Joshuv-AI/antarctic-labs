Protocol for mitochondrial DNA extraction, purity evaluation, and global methylation assessment via LC-MS/MS
Authors: Argyri Chroni, Theodora Alexiou, John Zafeiropoulos, Vasiliki Chondrou, Argiris Symeonidis, Argyro Sgourou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEstablished epigenetic mechanisms, including nuclear (n)DNA methylation, are also known to regulate the mitochondrial (mt) genome despite its lack of histones. However, studies referring to evaluations of mtDNA methylation are often influenced by technical and biological confounders. We present an optimized protocol for extracting mtDNA from peripheral blood and bone marrow mononuclear cells, followed by steps for qualitative assessment to exclude nDNA contaminations and a downstream quantitative analysis of the mtDNA global methylation profile using liquid chromatography combined with mass spectrometry.Subject areas: Cancer, Cell Biology, Cell separation/fractionation, Genetics, Genomics, Mass Spectrometry, Metabolomics, Molecular Biology

Before you begin

A workflow is outlined for the isolation of peripheral blood and bone marrow mononuclear cells (PBMCs and BMMCs respectively) from human samples, with a further stepwise process of mitochondria concentration by a sucrose gradient method, followed by mtDNA extraction, qualitative evaluation of the purity of mitochondrial enrichment via multiplex PCR and assessment of mtDNA methylation levels quantitatively using the Liquid Chromatography tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) methodology.

Peripheral blood and bone marrow samples should be collected in anticoagulant-treated tubes, with EDTA, and should be processed immediately after collection or else, they should be maintained at 4°C until processing within less than 12 hours.

Before proceeding to the LC-MS/MS analytical method for the determination of mtDNA methylation levels, mitochondria enrichment must be verified. For an accurate mtDNA methylation assessment, rigorous exclusion of nDNA contamination is required.

To verify the purity of mitochondrial fraction a multiplex PCR was designed by targeting both nuclear gene glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and mitochondrial gene encoding cytochrome B (MT-CYB). Only mtDNA samples that exhibited amplification of MT-CYB with simultaneous absence of GAPDH amplification were proceeded to LC-MS/MS methylation analysis.

In general, methods of evaluation of mtDNA methylation are considered highly tricky processes, arising first from purity and integrity of mtDNA isolated fractions. Secondly, classical bisulfite conversion and next generation sequencing (NGS) workflows suffer from technical limitations: a) harsh treatment during bisulfite conversion of mtDNA often leads to degradation of the small circular mtDNA fragments, b) heavy (H) and light (L) strands of mtDNA have asymmetric base composition, which biases sequencing and conversion efficiency and also, c) the high copy number heterogeneity of mtDNA relying on the mixed populations (heteroplasmy), further complicate interpretation of NGS results. On the other hand, assessment of mtDNA methylation by LC-MS/MS, offers a quick solution for global rather than targeted estimation1 albeit very low methylation levels (<1-2%) can be effectively measured. Finally, the 5-methyl-2′-deoxycytidine (5-mdC) and 5-hydroxymethyl-2′-deoxycytidine (5-hmdC) epigenetic marks can be distinguished in contrast to NGS methods, providing a more accurate detection of the true methylation profile.

Innovation

This method has primarily been designed for human bone marrow and peripheral blood mononuclear cells and integrates isolation of mitochondria, qualitative evaluation of mtDNA fraction purity and detection of methylation levels of mtDNA into a consistent one-day protocol. The method has also been applied to cultured human erythroleukemic cells K562. Innovation angles of the present method are the reproducibility and the accuracy of identifying genomic DNA contamination in the mtDNA fraction through a multiplex PCR design, concurrently targeting nuclear and mitochondrial loci in the same reaction. Finally, this method allows the quick, downstream assessment of mtDNA epigenetic modifications of 5-mdC and 5-hmdC nucleosides, providing accurate quantification of mtDNA methylation down to 0,5ppb and simultaneous 5-mdC/5-hmdC detection with the requirement of ≥200 ng mtDNA. The whole procedure from blood/marrow collection to mtDNA methylation assessment requires no longer than 10-11 laboratory working hours for application in a small number of samples and provides high reproducibility of results and low cost per analysis compared to NGS technology.

Expected outcomes

Purified mtDNA is extracted from human PBMCs and BMMCs with undetectable nDNA contamination using the presented method, which is suitable for further epigenetic research. 5x106 cells should be at least obtained as a starting material for downstream isolation of mtDNA. Enrichment of mitochondria followed by organic solvent-based DNA extraction method usually generates sufficient mtDNA (>300 ng) for qualitative PCR-based purity assessment as well as quantitative methylation analysis. Commercial kits with columns for the mtDNA isolation largely decimate its biochemical composition of cytosine modifications 5-mdC and 5-hmdC, therefore the organic solvent-based DNA extraction was the method of choice in the present protocol.

Different amplification patterns emerged by performing the designed multiplex conventional PCR using two primer sets, depending on the DNA template. Total DNA samples from PBMCs and BMMCs produced two distinct PCR products that corresponded to MT-CYB and GAPDH sets of primers, respectively. However, a single PCR product, matching only to the MT-CYB, was anticipated to be amplified by mtDNA and successfully confirmed the absence of nuclear DNA contamination (Figure 8).

Amplification of MT-CYB is detected in all mtDNA and total DNA samples.

Amplification of GAPDH-Intron is detected only in total DNA samples, confirming the purity of mtDNA samples.

Band sizes correspond to the expected amplicons.

The present protocol provides an efficient and reproducible workflow for pure mtDNA extraction and quantification of mtDNA global methylation levels using LC-MS/MS. Well-resolved chromatographic peaks are extracted for each monitored nucleoside (5-mdC and 5-hmdC) (Figure 7). Each nucleoside elutes at a consistent and reproducible retention time (6.4 min for 5-hmdC and 7.6 min for 5-mdC), with clearly distinguishable peak shapes and minimal baseline interference, enabling reliable peak integration. dG with retention time 9.8 min is included as an internal standard which ensures normalization across samples and compensates for variability introduced at any step of the process. It also enhances the quantitative accuracy and comparability of measurements across experimental runs. Calibration curves generated from pure nucleoside standards demonstrated strong linearity (R2 > 0.99), supporting absolute quantification. Standard curves for 5mdC/102dG and 5hmdC/105dG ranged between 0.02-1 for 5mdC/102dG and 20-100 for 5hmdC/105dG (Figure 9).

Standard curves for 5mdC/102dG and 5hmdC/105dG

Application of the protocol to mtDNA samples derived from human PBMCS and BMMCs yielded reproducible and quantifiable levels of 5mdC. MtDNA inputs of 200 ng, exhibited a range from 0.15 to 0.30 for 5-mdC. However, 5-hmdC mark was present at levels near the limit of detection (LOD), therefore quantitative values cannot be reported confidently.

Together, these measurements provide both DNA relative methylation levels (normalized to dG) and absolute analyte concentrations, confirming the sensitivity and quantitative consistency of the LC–MS/MS workflow. Replicate analyses showed consistent retention times and peak area ratios, indicating high analytical reproducibility.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantification of mtDNA modifications is performed using calibration curves generated from pure nucleoside standards. For each modification, calibration curves are constructed by plotting the measured LC–MS/MS peak area ratio relative to dG (5-mdC/dG and 5-hmdC/dG) against the corresponding molar ratio of each modification normalized to dG (5-mdC/102 dG and 5-hmdC/105 dG). Linear regression is applied to generate the equation of the calibration curve for each modified nucleoside (y = a+bx), where y is the peak area ratio and x is the molar ratio. Peak area ratios obtained from sample chromatograms after integration are then used to determine the corresponding concentration of each modification by inserting into the appropriate calibration curve equation. Sample measurements are accepted for quantification only when peak areas fall within the linear dynamic range of the calibration curve and show consistent retention times compared with standards. Samples are measured in duplicates. For statistical analysis, the mean value of the duplicate measurements is used as the representative value per sample. Statistical analysis is performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 20).

Limitations

The present protocol is designed for the extraction of mtDNA from PBMCs and BMMCs as well as for evaluation of its’ purity through multiplex PCR and downstream assessment of mtDNA methylation profile.

The designed multiplex conventional PCR assay provides a qualitative assessment of mtDNA but cannot represent an absolute quantitative assay. Even though GAPDH (nuclear gene) amplification was not detected in any of the mtDNA samples when tested by the multiplex PCR, a more sensitive method (for example a digital PCR assay) would probably be capable of detecting small traces of nDNA, indicating minimal contaminations. The implementation of dPCR however, would substantially increase the cost per sample without delivering proportional benefits to the workflow.

Balanced amplification of both targets (nuclear and mitochondrial genes) is essential for multiplex PCR. Optimizing primer concentration and PCR conditions is crucial when adapting the protocol to different primer sets or sample types. Differences occurred in primer efficiency or template concentration may affect band intensity in gel electrophoresis and should not be quantitatively interpreted. The efficiency of mtDNA isolation may vary depending on numerous factors, such as sample quality, cell number and handling conditions. Downstream applications may be affected by low mtDNA yield, when higher DNA concentrations are required.

Conclusively, it can be considered safe to assess mtDNA methylation levels internally compared across different mitochondrial samples with extremely low-level genomic DNA carryover homogeneously present, if all mtDNA samples are isolated by the same method and all precautions mentioned above have been taken into account. Even though nuclear sequences cannot be totally eliminated, suitable primer design and mitochondrial enrichment process limit their impact on downstream methylation analysis.

The protocol for LC-MS/MS requires a minimum of 200 ng of purified mtDNA for reliable quantification. Very low mtDNA yields from human PBMCs or BMMCs samples cannot be subjected to methylation analysis. While LC-MS/MS enables accurate discrimination between 5-mdC and 5-hmdC, very low-abundance of cytosine modifications below the detection limits of the instrument are not captured (mainly for 5-hmdC), potentially leading to minor underestimation of total cytosine modification content.

Another limitation is that this method measures global, not locus-specific, mtDNA methylation levels.

Overall, the presented protocol gives highly accurate and cost/time effective global estimations of mtDNA content for 5-mdC and 5-hmdC modifications providing a novel and reliable method for epigenetic studies related to mtDNA.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Small pellet of mononuclear cells from peripheral blood and bone marrow samples. (Related to step Isolation of mononuclear cells from peripheral blood and bone marrow samples).Cell loss during washing steps or insufficient separation during density gradient centrifugation.

Potential solution

When layering diluted samples over the Lymphosep medium, verify that a clear interphase develops. Also, careful handling will not disturb the interphase when collecting. During the washing phases, carefully aspirate the supernatants without disturbing the pelleted cells.

Problem 2

Low concentration yields of mtDNA from peripheral blood and bone marrow mononuclear cells. (Related to Step: Extraction of mtDNA) Lower mitochondrial content per cell, or mitochondrial loss during enrichment steps.

Potential solution

Process samples promptly after collection. Maintain all buffers and samples on ice to preserve organelle integrity during mitochondrial isolation and minimize mitochondrial loss.

Problem 3

Amplification of MT-CYB is weak or absent in mtDNA samples (Related to Step: Detection of mtDNA purity by multiplex conventional PCR).

Low mtDNA concentration due to inadequate starting material, loss of mitochondria during isolation or during organic solvent-based mtDNA extraction.

Potential solution

Verify that during the extraction and precipitation procedures that mitochondria pellet is apparent. Before setting up the PCR, confirm concentrations of DNA samples and increase the amount of mtDNA template used for amplification if needed.

Problem 4

MT-CYB and GAPDH exhibit distinct amplification efficiencies in multiplex PCR (Related to Step: Detection of mtDNA purity by multiplex conventional PCR).

Differences in amplification efficiency or primer binding efficiency or competition between the two sets of primers or differences in template concentration.

Potential solution

Perform optimization of the concentration for each of the primer sets separately to accomplish balanced amplification or lower the concentration of the more effective primer set. The annealing temperatures of the two primer sets should be compatible and the two PCR products should have sufficiently different sizes for agarose gel resolution.

Problem 5

GAPDH amplification is detected in mtDNA samples. (Related to Step: Agarose gel electrophoresis) During subcellular compartmentalization (fractionation) there was incomplete separation of mitochondria from nuclei. Nuclear membranes may be ruptured through excessive mechanical force during homogenization, leading to genomic DNA carryover. Furthermore, nuclear pellets may be disrupted by improper supernatant aspiration.

Potential solution

Avoid excessive force and perform gently and consistently homogenization and cell lysis. Carefully aspirate supernatants without disrupting pellets. If contamination persists, include an additional low-speed centrifugation step to improve nuclear removal.
