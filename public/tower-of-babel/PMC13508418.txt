Morpho‑physiological and biochemical response of coriander to silicon with bacterial inoculants in silicon-deficient soil
Authors: Dibyajyoti Nath, Duraisamy Selvi, Subramanium Thiyageshwari, Rangasamy Anandham, Kandasamy Venkatesan, Sumit Sow, Shivani Ranjan, Majed Alotaibi, Nawab Ali, Mahmoud F Seleiman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSilicon (Si) deficiency limits plant growth, physiological efficiency, and yield in high-value crops such as Coriandrum sativum L. A field experiment was conducted on Si-deficient soil at TNAU Coconut Farm, Coimbatore, India, using coriander variety CO (CR) 4. Seven treatments in a randomized block design with three replications evaluated calcium silicate (CaSiO3), and rice husk ash (RHA) at 225, and 275 kg Si ha− 1, alone, and combined with Bacillus altitudinis SSB4, across growth, physiological, biochemical, antioxidant, and yield parameters.ResultsSi + SSB4 integration significantly improved plant height (up to 60.9% over control), leaf area index, SPAD index, and shoot and root dry matter production. Leaf Si content increased by 84.1–88.4% over absolute control, and 21.7–24.5% over RDF alone. Antioxidant enzymes (CAT, POD, and SOD), and biochemical attributes (total soluble sugars, total soluble protein, total phenols, and ascorbic acid) were markedly enhanced, with total soluble protein recording the highest increase (194.8% over absolute control). RHA-based treatments marginally but non-significantly outperformed CaSiO3-based treatments. The highest leaf yield (5.46 t ha− 1) was recorded with RDF + RHA at 275 kg Si ha− 1 + SSB4 (53.8% over RDF), followed by RDF + CaSiO3 at the same level (52.1%). Strong positive correlations (r = 0.863–0.990) among Si content, antioxidant enzymes, biochemical parameters, and yield confirmed a coordinated Si-mediated improvement.ConclusionsIntegrated application of CaSiO3 or RHA with B. altitudinis SSB4 synergistically enhanced growth, antioxidant defence, biochemical quality, and leaf yield of coriander in Si-deficient soils, supporting the adoption of combined chemical-biological Si management strategies.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09257-4.Keywords: Antioxidants, Coriander, Leaf yield, Rice husk ash, Silicate-solubilizing bacteria, Silicon

Introduction

India is recognized globally as the “Home of Spices,” with coriander (Coriandrum sativum L.) being one of the most widely cultivated, and consumed spice crops in the country [1]. Native to Southern Europe, and the Mediterranean region, coriander is grown for both its aromatic leaves, and seeds, and is commonly known as dhania, cilantro, or Chinese parsley [2]. The fresh leaves are rich in essential oils, polyphenols, vitamin C, vitamin A, and B vitamins, making coriander an important nutritional, and medicinal herb [3]. Although coriander can be cultivated in diverse soil types, its productivity is often constrained by poor soil fertility, and micronutrient limitations.

Silicon (Si), the second most abundant element in the Earth’s crust, has gained attention as a functional nutrient that enhances plant growth, physiological resilience, and stress tolerance [4]. In soils, Si occurs primarily as quartz or silica (SiO2) [5] and its solubility depends on pH and weathering intensity, forming silicic acid (H4SiO4)—the form plants can absorb [6, 7]. Although soil Si concentrations typically range from 0.1 to 0.6 mM [8], plant uptake varies widely among species, with Si accumulation ranging from 0.1 to 10% of plant dry weight depending on genotype [9]. Si deficiency is common in coarse-textured, and highly weathered soils such as those found in parts of India [10].

Coriander, being a relatively shallow-rooted herb cultivated predominantly in light-textured, Si-deficient soils, is particularly vulnerable to Si limitation. Si plays a critical role in strengthening cell walls, improving stomatal regulation, and enhancing the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase, and catalase, all of which are essential for maintaining leaf quality, essential oil content, and overall biomass in aromatic herbs like coriander [11, 12]. Despite its agronomic importance, coriander receives far less attention than cereal crops regarding Si nutrition, and the extent to which supplemental Si can optimize its growth, biochemical quality, and yield in deficient soils remains largely unexplored.

The application of Si fertilizers has been shown to improve crop performance, particularly in Si-deficient soils. Calcium silicate (CaSiO3), a commonly used Si source, increases Si availability, improves nutrient uptake, and enhances crop yield in rice, and other cereals [13]. Similarly, foliar, and soil application of CaSiO3 enhances biomass production, chlorophyll content, and postharvest quality in leafy vegetables such as lettuce [14]. Rice husk ash (RHA), an agricultural byproduct rich in amorphous silica (60–70%), has emerged as an affordable, and sustainable Si source, especially in rice-growing regions of India where large quantities of rice husk are generated annually [15]. When incorporated into soil, RHA improves aeration, drainage, and nutrient release, making it useful in vegetable production systems [16].

Beyond chemical Si sources, plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) play a crucial role in improving nutrient availability, plant vigor, and soil health [17]. Several PGPR genera—including Pseudomonas, Bacillus, and Azospirillum—enhance plant growth through solubilization of nutrients, production of phytohormones, and modulation of antioxidant pathways [18]. Silicate-solubilizing bacteria (SSB) are particularly important because they release plant-available Si from insoluble silicate minerals, and agricultural residues, thereby increasing Si uptake, and improving crop stress tolerance [19, 20].

Despite the established benefits of Si fertilizers, and SSB individually, their combined effects on coriander growth, physiology, and biochemical composition under field conditions in Si-deficient sandy clay loam soils of South India remain poorly understood. Critically, no field-scale study has directly compared the efficacy of two structurally distinct Si sources — crystalline CaSiO3, and amorphous RHA in combination with Bacillus altitudinis SSB4 for improving coriander performance in such soils. Furthermore, the specific capacity of SSB4 to mobilize Si from both mineral (CaSiO3), and agricultural by-product (RHA) sources, and whether such mobilization can compensate for differences in source solubility to produce comparable improvements in growth, physiological, biochemical, and leaf yield of coriander, has not been investigated. Given that coriander is predominantly grown in light-textured soils in India, and receives far less attention than cereal or cash crops in Si nutrition, this knowledge gap represents a critical barrier to developing region-specific Si management strategies for this high-value spice crop. We hypothesized that the integrated application of Si fertilizers (CaSiO3, and RHA) at varying levels combined with SSB4 would synergistically enhance Si availability, thereby improving the growth, quality, and yield of coriander beyond what either input achieves alone. Accordingly, the objective of this study was to evaluate the influence of varying level of Si from different sources in combination with SSB4, on the morpho-physiological, biochemical, and productivity of coriander grown in sandy clay loam soil of India.

Materials and methods

Experimental site

The field experiment was conducted at Field No. 49 of the Tamil Nadu Agricultural University (TNAU) Coconut Farm, Coimbatore, Tamil Nadu, India, on low-Si soil of the Kilpudupatti series. The site is located at 11°00’41.3” N latitude, and 76°55’40.8” E longitude, with an elevation of 428.15 ± 8 m. The soil was alkaline (pH 8.05) with an electrical conductivity (EC) of 0.23 dS m− 1, low organic carbon (0.35%), and organic matter (0.60%). Available nitrogen was low (212.6 kg ha− 1), phosphorus was medium (17.3 kg ha− 1), and potassium was high (525.0 kg ha− 1). Plant available Si, determined by the 0.5 M acetic acid method, was low (15.9 mg kg− 1 soil) according to the classification proposed by Narayanaswamy and Prakash [21]. Coriander variety CO (CR) 4 was used as the test crop.

Meteorological data

Meteorological conditions recorded during the field experiment (June–July 2022) are presented in Fig. 1. The cropping season was characterized by average maximum and minimum temperatures of 31.23 °C and 23.33 °C, respectively, with a total seasonal rainfall of 41.44 mm distributed over four rainy days. Daily sunshine duration averaged 4.77 h, and pan evaporation was recorded at 6.14 mm day− 1. Relative humidity ranged from 70 to 84% in the morning, and 48–66% in the afternoon, while average daily solar radiation during the cropping period was 273.92 cal cm− 2.

Experimental design and treatments

The treatment framework comprised two Si sources—CaSiO3, and RHA—and two Si application levels (225, and 275 kg Si ha− 1) combined with the silicate-solubilizing bacterium Bacillus altitudinis SSB4 (JX042464). These Si rates were selected based on a prior pot experiment evaluating multiple Si levels, where they represented the optimum range for improving coriander growth, and physiological performance without causing nutrient imbalance or diminishing returns. Accordingly, these doses were adopted in the present field experiment to validate their effectiveness under open-field conditions, and to maintain methodological continuity with earlier findings. The details of the treatments are: (1) T1–Absolute Control; (2) T2–RDF (recommended dose of fertilizers; 20:40:20 kg N:P:K ha− 1); (3) T3–RDF + CaSiO3 at 225 kg Si ha− 1 + SSB4; (4) T4–RDF + CaSiO3 at 275 kg Si ha− 1 + SSB4; (5) T5–RDF + RHA at 225 kg Si ha− 1 + SSB4; (6) T6–RDF + RHA at 275 kg Si ha− 1 + SSB4; and (7) T7–Farmers’ Practice (application of farmyard manure (FYM) at the quantity of 4 t ha− 1, without any specific recommended rate, and without the addition of inorganic fertilizers or Si supplements). Each plot measured 4 m × 3 m in size. The experiment was conducted using a randomized block design (RBD), with three replications of each treatment under field conditions (Fig. 2).

The Si content of the sources used was analytically determined prior to application, and found to be 24.01% in CaSiO3, and 40.50% in RHA. RHA was obtained from a local rice mill, where rice husk is combusted under controlled aerobic conditions to produce ash predominantly composed of amorphous silica, as widely reported in the literature [15]. The collected RHA was sieved through a 0.5 mm mesh, and homogenized before application to ensure uniformity. Detailed characteristics of RHA has been given in supplementary Table S1. Application rates for both Si sources were calculated on the basis of elemental Si content to supply the targeted levels of 225, and 275 kg Si ha− 1, such that although the total mass of CaSiO3, and RHA applied differed between sources, the amount of elemental Si supplied remained equivalent across corresponding treatments. Seeds of coriander CO (CR) 4 were procured from the Department of Spices, and Plantation Crops, TNAU, Coimbatore, India. Before sowing, seeds were split, soaked overnight in water, and treated with Trichoderma viride (10 g kg− 1 seed), and Azospirillum (150 g kg− 1 seed). Sowing was done at a spacing of 20 × 15 cm, and adequate irrigation was maintained throughout the crop growth period. A lignite-based bio-inoculant of SSB4 (1 × 106 CFU g− 1), obtained from the Department of Agricultural Microbiology, TNAU, was soil-applied at 2 kg ha− 1. All agronomic practices were followed as per the TNAU Crop Production Guide [22]. The crop was harvested for leaf yield at 45 days after sowing (DAS), before the flowering stage.

Phenotypic and physiological traits

Plant growth parameters

Growth parameters, including plant height, number of leaves per plant, shoot, and root dry matter production (DMP), and root volume, were measured by standard agronomic procedures.

Physiological parameters

Leaf SPAD (Soil, and Plant Analysis Development) values, indicating leaf greenness, were measured using a portable SPAD-502 chlorophyll meter (Minolta Camera Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) on the second fully expanded leaf from the top at 25, and 45 DAS. Five leaf readings per plot were recorded, and their mean was used as the SPAD value [23].

After recording the fresh weight of the plants, three representative plants from each plot were randomly selected at each sampling stage for leaf area determination. The leaf area of all leaves from the selected plants was measured using a leaf area meter (LA-3100, LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, Nebraska, USA). Following leaf area measurement, the same plant samples were oven-dried to a constant weight, and used for dry matter estimation. Relative water content (RWC) of coriander leaves was determined following the method of Mahmood et al. [25]. Leaves were randomly sampled at both growth stages, and fresh weight was recorded. The samples were then placed in closed Petri dishes, and immersed in distilled water for 24 h at 4 °C to obtain turgid weight after blotting on tissue paper. Subsequently, the leaves were oven-dried at 72 °C for 24 h to record dry weight. RWC was calculated using the equation described by Teulat et al. [26].

Coriander leaf yield

For each treatment, plants were randomly sampled from the respective plots, and the fresh leaf weight was recorded using an electronic balance after gently removing any adhering soil. The leaf yield was subsequently extrapolated to tonnes per hectare (t ha− 1).

Leaf Si content

Leaf Si content was determined following the method of Narayanaswamy and Prakash [21] at 45 DAS. Powdered dry leaf samples (0.1 g) were subjected to microwave-assisted acid digestion using a mixture of 7 mL HNO3 (70%), 2 mL H2O2 (30%), and 1 mL HF (40%) in a microwave digestion system (Anton Paar-Multiwave PRO, Austria). Digestion was carried out in two stages: an initial ramp phase at 1200 W for 20 min at a rate of 7 °C min− 1, followed by a hold phase at 170 °C, and 1200 W for 30 min, with subsequent venting for 20 min. The digests were then neutralized, and made up to a final volume of 50 mL using 4% boric acid solution. For colorimetric determination of Si, a 0.25 mL aliquot of the digest was pipetted into polypropylene tubes, and the volume was adjusted to 12.5 mL by the sequential addition of 3.75 mL HCl (0.2 N), 0.5 mL ammonium molybdate (10%), 0.5 mL tartaric acid (20%), and 0.5 mL of the reducing agent 1-amino-2-naphthol-4-sulfonic acid. The mixture was allowed to stand for 1 h for complete colour development, after which the absorbance of the resulting blue colour was measured at 600 nm using a UV-Visible spectrophotometer (UV-3200 series, LABINDIA Analytical, India). A series of Si standards ranging from 0.2 to 1.6 ppm were prepared, and processed under identical conditions to construct the calibration curve.

Plant enzymatic activity

Antioxidant enzyme activities were analysed at 25, and 45 DAS using fresh leaf samples collected from each treatment. Catalase (CAT) activity was determined following Pereira et al. [27] using a chilled reaction mixture containing 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.5), and 25 mM H2O2. The reaction was initiated by adding 0.1 mL enzyme extract, and CAT activity was measured as the decrease in H2O2 absorbance at 240 nm for 1 min at 25 °C, with activity expressed as units µg− 1 protein. Peroxidase (POD) activity was assayed according to Polle et al. [28] in a 3 mL reaction mixture comprising 50 µL enzyme extract, 20 mM guaiacol, 10 mM H2O2, and 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). The increase in absorbance due to guaiacol oxidation was recorded at 470 nm for 3 min, and activity was calculated using an extinction coefficient of 2.8 mM− 1 cm− 1, and expressed as µmol g− 1 FW min− 1. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was estimated following Beauchamp and Fridovich [29] by homogenizing 500 mg fresh leaf tissue in 10 mL HEPES–KOH buffer, and centrifuging at 15,000 rpm for 15 min. A 3 mL reaction mixture containing 1 mL enzyme extract was prepared, and absorbance was measured at 560 nm. One unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to inhibit 50% of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) reduction, and results were expressed as units g-1 FW.

Quality parameters

The quality parameters were analysed at 45 DAS using representative plant samples collected from each treatment. Total soluble sugars (TSS) was estimated from dry leaf samples using the anthrone method described by Yemm and Willis [30]. Glucose was used as the standard, and absorbance was recorded at 620 nm using a UV/VIS spectrophotometer (UV-3200 series, LABINDIA Analytical, India). The results were expressed as mg g− 1 DW. Total soluble protein (TSP) was determined by the Lowry method using the Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, with absorbance recorded at 660 nm on the same spectrophotometer, and expressed as mg g− 1 FW [31]. Total phenols (TP) was estimated using the Folin–Ciocalteu assay [32], measured at 550 nm, and expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE) g− 1 FW. Ascorbic acid content was determined by the 2,6-dichlorophenol-indophenol dye titration method, in which 5 mL aliquots of the supernatant were titrated against the indophenol dye to estimate ascorbic acid concentration, and expressed as mg 100 g− 1 FW [33].

Statistical analysis

All datasets were initially tested for normality using standard diagnostic procedures, and homogeneity of error variances across treatments was verified using Levene’s test prior to statistical analysis. Data conforming to these assumptions were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) following the procedure of Panse and Sukhatme [34]. Mean separation was performed using Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (DMRT) at p ≤ 0.05, and non-significant differences were denoted as NS. Additionally, a pairwise correlation matrix was generated using the GGally, ggplot2, psych, and RColorBrewer packages in R (version 4.5.0) to visualize interrelationships among measured variables.

Results

Plant growth and physiological parameters

The results showed that coriander plant height was significantly influenced by different Si sources, and SSB4 inoculation at both 25, and 45 DAS (Fig. 3A). Plant height ranged from 6.88 to 14.91 cm at 25 DAS, and from 21.26 to 34.22 cm at 45 DAS, with a clear increasing trend under higher Si application levels combined with SSB4 inoculation. Among the treatments, T6 (RDF + RHA at 275 kg Si ha− 1 + SSB4) recorded the highest plant height at both growth stages (14.91, and 34.22 cm at 25, and 45 DAS, respectively), and was statistically on par with T4 (RDF + CaSiO3 at 275 kg Si ha− 1 + SSB4). At 45 DAS, T6 increased plant height by 38.8% over RDF (T2), 46.8% over farmers’ practice (T7), and 60.9% over the absolute control (T1), whereas T4 recorded increases of 30.4, 38.0, and 51.3%, respectively. Moreover, the lowest plant height was observed in T1, indicating the positive influence of integrated Si application, and SSB4 inoculation on coriander growth.

The number of leaves per plant, and SPAD index values were significantly influenced by different Si sources, and SSB4 inoculation at both 25, and 45 DAS. The number of leaves per plant ranged from 4.12 to 8.89 at 25 DAS, and from 22.56 to 40.78 at 45 DAS, with a clear increasing trend under higher Si application levels combined with SSB4 inoculation (Fig. 3B). Among the treatments, T6 recorded the highest number of leaves at 45 DAS (40.78), while T4 recorded the highest at 25 DAS (8.89); both treatments were statistically on par with each other at both growth stages. At 45 DAS, T6 increased the number of leaves per plant by 55.7% over T2, 66.0% over T7, and 80.8% over T1, whereas T4 recorded increases of 53.2, 63.3, and 77.8%, respectively. Similarly, SPAD index values ranged from 25.69 to 37.15 at 25 DAS, and from 22.36 to 36.84 at 45 DAS, consistently following the same increasing trend as leaf number across treatment levels (Table 1). T6 recorded the highest SPAD values at both growth stages (37.15, and 36.84 at 25, and 45 DAS, respectively), and was statistically on par with T4 (36.16, and 36.10 at 25, and 45 DAS, respectively). However, the lowest values for both parameters at both growth stages were recorded in the absolute control.

Leaf Si content

Leaf Si content was significantly influenced by Si sources, and SSB4 inoculation, with a clear increasing trend observed in the order T1 ≈ T7 < T2 < T3 ≈ T5 ≈ T4 ≈ T6 (Fig. 6). Si content ranged from 1.89% of dry weight in T1 to 3.56% of dry weight in T4, with all Si + SSB4 treatments (T3–T6) falling within a narrow range of 3.48–3.56% of dry weight. T4 recorded the numerically highest Si content (3.56%), closely followed by T6 (3.52%), T3 (3.49%), and T5 (3.48%), all of which were statistically on par. All Si + SSB4 treatments increased leaf Si content by 21.7–24.5% as compared to T2. Treatment T7 (1.93%), and T1 (1.89%) were statistically on par, and recorded the lowest leaf Si content.

Plant enzymatic activity

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that integrating Si fertilization with SSB4 inoculation substantially improved coriander growth, physiology, antioxidant activity, biochemical quality, and leaf yield in Si-deficient sandy clay loam soil, with T6 (RDF + RHA at 275 kg Si ha− 1 + SSB4), and T4 (RDF + CaSiO3 at 275 kg Si ha− 1 + SSB4) consistently outperforming all other treatments. While the beneficial effects of Si on crop performance are well established [35, 36], the novel contribution of this study lies in demonstrating how SSB4 differentially enhances Si bioavailability from two structurally distinct Si sources — crystalline CaSiO3, and amorphous RHA — and how this translates into measurable agronomic gains across multiple parameters in coriander.

CaSiO3, and RHA differ fundamentally in their Si release dynamics. CaSiO3 is a crystalline mineral that releases Si slowly through weathering, and dissolution, while RHA contains predominantly amorphous silica (60–70%) that is structurally disordered, and therefore more rapidly solubilized in soil solution [15, 37]. Despite this difference, both sources produced statistically comparable outcomes when combined with SSB4, suggesting that SSB4 effectively compensated for the lower intrinsic solubility of CaSiO3 by accelerating Si release through organic acid production, and siderophore secretion [38, 39]. Recent evidence further indicates that soil amendments can alter bacterial community structure and nutrient transformation processes, thereby enhancing nutrient bioavailability and mineral weathering in soils [40]. This equalization effect is agronomically significant: in Si-deficient soils where plant-available H4SiO4 is inherently limited, SSB4 inoculation transforms relatively recalcitrant Si minerals into plant-accessible forms, narrowing the performance gap between low- and high-solubility Si sources. The marginally superior performance of RHA-based treatments — T6 over T4, and T5 over T3 across most parameters — likely reflects the residual advantage of amorphous silica’s faster baseline solubilization, even after accounting for SSB4-mediated enhancement. Leaf Si content across all Si + SSB4 treatments fell within a narrow range of 3.48–3.56% of dry weight, compared to 2.86% of dry weight under T2, and 1.89% of dry weight under T1, confirming that SSB4 inoculation with either source effectively elevated leaf Si status by 21.7–24.5% over T2, and 84.1–88.4% over T1.

The improvement in leaf Si content directly underpinned the observed gains in growth, and physiological parameters. Plant height, leaf number, LAI, SPAD values, and shoot, and root DMP all followed a consistent treatment-wise increasing trend (T1 < T7 < T2 < T3 < T5 < T4 < T6) at both 25, and 45 DAS, reflecting a clear dose-dependent response to Si application combined with SSB4. At 45 DAS, T6 increased plant height by 60.9%, and shoot DMP by 72.9% over T1, while root DMP increased by 150.1%, a magnitude that highlights the particular sensitivity of root architecture to Si availability in deficient soils. Si deposition in cell walls strengthens structural integrity, and promotes cell elongation, further supports chlorophyll synthesis [41, 42]. The progressive increases in LAI (+ 14.7 to + 32.4% over T2 at 45 DAS), and root volume (+ 22.6 to + 39.9%) with increasing Si levels confirm that SSB4-facilitated Si availability gradually enhanced both canopy development, and below-ground resource acquisition. The absence of significant treatment differences in RWC was consistent with reports that Si-induced water relations responses occur primarily under stress, not well-watered conditions [43, 44], suggests that the growth benefits observed here operated through structural, and nutritional rather than osmotic mechanisms.

Antioxidant enzyme activities — CAT, POD, and SOD — increased progressively from 25 to 45 DAS across all treatments, with the most pronounced enhancement under Si + SSB4 combinations. At 45 DAS, T6 increased CAT, POD, and SOD by 88.1, 51.4, and 85.7% over T2, respectively, while T4 recorded increases of 80.4, 45.9, and 71.4%. The progressive enhancement from 225 to 275 kg Si ha− 1 within each Si source confirms a dose-dependent upregulation of antioxidant capacity. In Si-deficient soils, compromised membrane stability, and suboptimal metabolic conditions reduce enzymatic efficiency [17]; exogenous Si restores these conditions by stabilizing membrane conformation, and improving the metabolic environment for enzyme activity [45]. The amplifying role of SSB4 in this context is particularly relevant: by increasing plant-available Si, SSB4 facilitates greater Si deposition in leaf tissues, which in turn supports more robust antioxidant responses [46, 47]. Similar increases in SOD, POD, and CAT with Si application have been reported in coriander [39, 48], corroborating the present findings. Importantly, these mechanistic interpretations are based on the enzyme activity data directly measured in this study rather than on inferred molecular pathways.

The biochemical quality parameters of coriander leaves responded strongly to Si + SSB4 treatments, with TSS, TSP, TP, and ascorbic acid all following the same treatment-wise increasing trend. TSP showed the most dramatic response, with T6, and T4 exceeding T1 by 194.8, and 182.4%, respectively, and surpassing T2 by 82.6, and 74.9%. These gains reflect Si-mediated improvements in nitrogen assimilation, and photosynthate partitioning, supported by the concurrent increases in SPAD values, and LAI that indicate enhanced photosynthetic capacity [49]. TP content increased by 52.8% in T6 over T2, indicating activation of antioxidant metabolism under improved Si nutrition, consistent with reports of Si-stimulated phenylpropanoid pathway activity in vegetable crops [50]. The 75.5% increase in ascorbic acid content in T6 over T2, and 130.2% over T1, reflects strengthened antioxidant recycling supported by improved enzyme activities documented in the same treatments [51]. Similar enhancement of antioxidant-related quality attributes has been reported in plant-derived food products with improved phytochemical status [52]. The convergence of all quality parameters under the same treatment ranking reinforces the interpretation that SSB4-mediated Si availability drives a coordinated improvement in coriander leaf metabolism rather than isolated biochemical responses.

These cumulative physiological, and biochemical improvements translated directly into leaf yield gains, with T6, and T4 recording 5.46, and 5.40 t ha− 1, representing increases of 53.8, and 52.1% over T2, and 122.0, and 119.5% over T1, respectively. The fact that lower Si levels (225 kg Si ha− 1) combined with SSB4 also produced yields of 5.18–5.23 t ha− 1 — increases of 45.9–47.3% over T2 — suggests that SSB4 inoculation partially compensates for reduced Si application rates by improving Si use efficiency, offering flexibility for resource-limited farmers. The similar yield improvements reported in coriander, and leafy vegetables with Si, and biological amendments [53, 54] support the generalizability of this approach. However, the findings of this study establish that the integration of SSB4 with CaSiO3 or RHA is not merely additive but functionally synergistic, enabling efficient Si mobilization from both crystalline, and amorphous sources, and delivering consistent, data-supported improvements across growth, physiology, antioxidant status, nutritional quality, and yield in coriander cultivated on Si-deficient soils.

It should be noted that the present field experiment did not include a standalone RDF + SSB4 treatment without Si, which limits the precise partitioning of the independent contribution of SSB4 from the combined Si + SSB4 effect. However, a prior pot experiment conducted as part of this research programme included RDF + SSB4 without Si as a treatment, and confirmed that SSB4 alone produced significantly lower improvements in coriander growth, and yield compared to Si + SSB4 combinations. The present field experiment was therefore designed to validate the optimum Si levels identified in the pot experiment under open-field conditions, maintaining methodological continuity. Additionally, SSB4 viability, and rhizosphere persistence at 45 DAS were not directly confirmed in this study; however, the consistent improvements in leaf Si content, and biochemical parameters across all Si + SSB4 treatments at both growth stages indirectly support sustained SSB4 activity throughout the crop growth period.

Conclusions

The present study clearly demonstrates the pivotal role of Si in improving the morpho-physiological, biochemical, and yield of coriander under Si-deficient soil conditions. Si supplementation significantly enhanced growth traits, physiological efficiency, antioxidant enzyme activity, and leaf biochemical quality compared with the non-Si control, confirming the importance of correcting Si deficiency for optimal coriander performance. Both CaSiO3, and RHA were effective Si sources when combined with B. altitudinis SSB4, with RHA exhibiting a marginal advantage due to faster amorphous silica solubilization, though overall performance remained statistically comparable across application rates. SSB4 effectively compensated for the lower intrinsic solubility of CaSiO3, narrowing the performance gap between the two sources, and demonstrating the functional advantage of integrating chemical, and biological Si inputs. Even at 225 kg Si ha− 1, SSB4 combinations delivered yield gains of 45.9–47.3% over RDF, offering a resource-efficient option for smallholder farmers. Moreover, integrated application of CaSiO3 or RHA with SSB4 can be recommended as an effective nutrient management strategy for coriander in Si-deficient soils of South India. Future research should include an RDF + SSB4 treatment without Si, rhizosphere sampling for SSB4 persistence, XRD characterization of Si sources, and multi-environment evaluation of abiotic stress tolerance across diverse soil types, and agro-climatic conditions.
