EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Lighting significantly contributes to electricity consumption in buildings. The most widely used
measure of lighting energy efficiency, luminous efficacy, quantifies only the intensity of light
generated by light sources, weighted by the spectral sensitivity of the human visual system (i.e.,
the energy efficiency of individual lighting devices). However, luminous efficacy does not fully
address the use of lighting in architectural spaces. To address these limitations, we developed a
framework to framework to calculate lighting application efficacy (LAE), the relationship between
the electrical power consumed by lighting hardware, and the amount of light that contributes to
the visual perception of building occupants.

The proposed LAE framework is based on the primary pathway of light in architectural settings:
the generation and emission of light from a luminaire, the travel of the light throughout the space
and into the eyes of occupants, and the process of visual perception. The project’s primary outputs
are a method for measuring lighting application efficacy and a framework for facilitating future
research to improve the metric. In the first year of the project, computational methods were
developed for estimating the proportion of emitted light directed to task areas and areas
corresponding to occupants’ visual fields using ray-tracing lighting design software. This work
yielded a functional, albeit a rudimentary, version of the LAE metric for immediate use. A
spreadsheet tool was developed for lighting professionals to calculate lighting application efficacy.

In the second year of the project, the visual sensitivity within the LAE framework was evaluated.
A series of visual experiments were conducted to validate the effect of visual field size of the
stimuli and the field of view (FOV) of the participant on spatial peripheral brightness perception.
Experiments were carried out utilizing different FOV, background luminance, stimuli size, and
contrast (positive vs negative). Results suggest that there were small differences in peripheral
spatial brightness perception under different luminance surrounding conditions, but increased
FOV reduces the importance of background luminance in negative contrast. However, this effect
was not consistent in positive contrast conditions. Results do not provide a necessity to alter the
calculation of visual efficiency in the LAE framework.

Finally, reductions in radiant efficiency with operating time has been integrated into the proposed
LAE framework. The changes in radiant and luminous efficacy with time were calculated, using

 2

the existing approaches outlined by the American National Standards Institute (ANSI) and
Illuminating Engineering Society (IES). The temporal dimension of light loss in a lighting system
was implemented into the LAE framework as weighing factors to address the overall targeted
performance of lighting design in installation over time. By extending the model to include user-
defined operational times and enabling the calculation of weighting factors or average efficiencies,
this methodology provides significant value for both academic research and practical applications.

1. INTRODUCTION
1.1 Background
Lighting consumes approximately 18% of electricity in buildings [1], and much investment and
effort are dedicated to improving the energy efficiency of lighting hardware. This approach has
demonstrated success – the widespread commercialization and adoption of light-emitting diodes
(LEDs) has notably reduced the energy consumed by lighting in the United States [2]. However,
there are physical limits to the efficiency with which light can be generated by electricity, so the
strategy of improving lighting hardware alone cannot produce energy conservation gains
indefinitely.

The most widely used measure of lighting energy efficiency, luminous efficacy (unit: lm/W),
quantifies the amount of light generated by light sources, weighted by the spectral sensitivity of
the human visual system, from one watt of electrical power. While luminous efficacy provides
lighting manufacturers and designers with an accurate and useful metric for quantifying the energy
efficiency of individual lighting devices, it does not fully address the use of lighting in architectural
spaces. In buildings, the primary function of light is to facilitate the visibility of surfaces: walls,
people, food, books, artwork, furniture, etc. Light strikes these surfaces, reflects off of them and
enters the eyes of building occupants, resulting in visual perception. Light that does not enter an
eye does not contribute to visual perception– for illumination purposes, it is wasted. Even when
high luminous efficacy products are used, significant electrical power is wasted in real
architectural lighting applications because only a fraction of the light generated is seen by building
occupants at any moment.

Several modern lighting design practices perpetuate this problem. For instance, lighting
application performance standards have been used for decades to guide lighting designers. These
recommendations usually encourage rather uniform lighting (i.e., high illuminance uniformity) so
that occupants can successfully engage in visual tasks throughout an architectural space [e.g.,[3],
[4]]. However, this often results in the unnecessary illumination of surfaces when occupants are
not viewing the entirety of a space. Nonetheless, research has generated a range of potential
approaches to maximizing the usefulness of light that is generated. For instance, “smart” dimming
systems that automatically reduce the amount of illumination in a space by an amount that is
visually negligible to occupants could be implemented relatively easily to reduce energy

 5

consumption by a small amount [5]. More radical ideas, such as absorption-minimizing lighting
systems that tailor the spectrum of light illuminating individual surfaces to reduce the amount of
light lost to absorption [6], [7] and gaze-dependent lighting systems that project light only to the
portions of the visual environment being viewed by occupants at any given time [8], could save
substantially more energy but would require more drastic changes to both lighting product design
and architectural lighting design practices.

Researchers can quantify changes in energy consumption when they investigate different
architectural lighting design strategies, but there is currently no practicable way for lighting
practitioners to understand the effects of the wide range of design decisions (e.g., luminaire
selection, surface reflectances, occupancy of the space, tasks undertaken in the space) undertaken
when designing for specific environments. Similarly, lighting building standards,
recommendations, and incentive programs encourage efficient use of lighting such as by
incentivizing the use of energy-saving lighting controls, but they do not offer methods for
quantifying the impacts of different design approaches. In this regard, the existing measure of
lighting efficiency (i.e., luminous efficacy) is insufficient. Instead, application efficacy, the
relationship between the electrical power consumed by lighting hardware and the amount of light
that contributes to the visual perception of building occupants, should be measured [9], [10], [11].
The ability to measure lighting application efficacy (LAE) can drive the adoption of more energy-
efficient design practices, foster product innovation, and, ultimately, reduce the energy consumed
by lighting.

Though relatively little research has been conducted on the measurement of lighting application
efficacy, a few approaches to quantifying it have been previously proposed. For instance, Rea and
Bullough developed an application efficacy calculation that characterizes luminous efficacy for a
particular solid angle (a three-dimensional angular volume that is analogous to a plane angle in
two dimensions) [10]. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) has proposed a lighting application
efficiency framework that combines light source efficiency, optical delivery efficiency (“how
efficiently light is delivered for all of the various ‘jobs’ of the lighting”), spectral efficiency (“the
overlap of the ultimate spectrum that reaches the task or eye with an optimum spectrum for the
activity or intent of the lighting”), and intensity effectiveness (“the difference between the intensity
of the provided light and the optimum intensity for the specific intent of the light”) [11]. While
both approaches have merits, neither were designed to accommodate the full range of variables

 6

that influence the “illumination lifecycle,” do not have the capacity to model changes in lighting
conditions over time, and are not designed to explicitly invite additional ideas about the factors
influencing the usefulness of light in real applications.

Here, we propose a framework to not only provide a functional measurement method and outline
for future research in the area, but also facilitate advancement in lighting design practices. Many
approaches to lighting design have evolved in response to the limitations of the older technologies,
many of which do not apply to modern solid-state lighting (SSL) technologies. Furthermore,
lighting design practices have been driven by what can be reasonably calculated, simulated, or
measured. One aim for the proposed work is to serve as an impetus for the lighting design
community to critically examine standard practices and re-think the customs and traditions that are
no longer advancing the field.

A singular focus on application efficacy – in which the usefulness of every photon of light that is
generated is prioritized above all else – is unlikely to yield optimal illuminated environments. That
is not the aim of this work. Instead, application efficacy should be one consideration among many,
such as color quality, glare control, and aesthetics [12], [13]. The lighting community needs to
develop additional strategies for balancing the conflicting lighting goals and requirements for any
particular architectural space, and information about application efficacy can stimulate that. It is
envisioned that lighting professional associations may consider developing relevant guidelines for
typical applications, such as methods for estimating occupancy, consensus about the most
important task areas for specific types of spaces, etc.

The commercial availability of lighting hardware is another limitation that the LAE metric can
influence. Lighting manufacturers optimize their offerings to the metrics that their customers care
about. With regard to energy efficiency, that is currently limited to luminous efficacy. When LAE
can be measured, manufacturers will likely be incentivized to develop and commercialize lighting
systems that support innovative, application efficacy-focused design approaches.

1.2 Lighting Application Efficacy Framework
The LAE framework can enable the holistic evaluation of building lighting systems rather than
individual luminaires. The primary objectives of the LAE framework are:

 • To enable lighting industry stakeholders to evaluate the efficiency of lighting design
 solutions in a holistic manner.

 7

 • To characterize the energy performance of lighting systems, including adaptive lighting
 systems and technologies.

 • To cultivate new areas of research and approaches to lighting design and product
 development.

The model that guides the LAE framework is based on the primary pathway of light in architectural
settings: the generation and emission of light from a luminaire, the travel of the light throughout
the space and into the eyes of occupants, and the process of visual perception {Citation}. This
definition of LAE can be mathematically expressed as

 LAE = " 𝜂luminaire × 𝜂spatial × 𝑆sensory (1)

where 𝜂luminaire is the efficiency of the luminaire, accounting for the conversion of electricity into
light, as well as losses from optics and other absorption of light within the luminaire, 𝜂spatial is
spatial efficiency, characterizing the proportion of electromagnetic energy emitted by the
luminaire(s) that reflects off visually meaningful surfaces and ultimately reaches the eyes of
occupants, 𝑆vensory is the sensory sensitivity of the occupant (in its current form, this is the current
standard photopic luminous efficiency function [14], but can also account for factors beyond
spectral luminous efficiency, such as adaptation and contrast effects, melanopic contribution to
visual and non-visual pathways), and t representing the temporal dimension to account for energy
use over time.

This final report details the technical activities conducted throughout the project. The proposed
LAE framework was established in three phases, starting with developing the first version of LAE,
as shown in Table 1. In the first phase, spatial efficiency was calculated and studied based on the

framework in offering a method to review and compare the efficiency of light reaching the
occupants. In the second phase, the performance of sensory sensitivity in relation to the visual
angle and contrast of stimuli was evaluated in a series of psychophysical experiments. In the final

Spatial efficiency is an independent measure that can be used to compare the efficiency of

Instead of calculating a single case scenario, simulations with various factors were generated to
find the significant contributing parameters to spatial efficiency from a set of hypothesized
variables. It is reasonable to hypothesize that a set of room and lighting system variables will have
a significant effect on spatial efficiency. For example, room size influences the number of
luminaires and the lighting zones. When an indoor environment is occupied even by a single
occupant, the lighting systems must serve the entire zone [16]. While such operational features of
lighting systems contribute to energy inefficiency [17], gaining knowledge on spatial efficiency
offers an opportunity to save energy through design optimization [16]. For example, Makaremi et
al. have studied the effect of surface reflectance, luminaire type, luminaire mounting height, and
the number of luminaires on energy consumption based on the average electric power required to
maintain 500 lux on a measurement plane [18]. Accordingly, a range of various parameters that
could potentially impact spatial efficiency was chosen to be investigated. The parameters are
shown in Table 2.

The simulation settings were designed to be basic yet as representative as possible. All rooms were
modeled in a square shape with a height of 3 m. The materials representing the three reflectance
levels were all in grayscale to maintain simplicity. Although weighing in the diversity of LED
luminaires is not practically possible, in stages I and III, three main distribution types (direct,
direct-indirect, and indirect) were considered with a mounting height of 2.5 m. All luminaires were
placed in a symmetric proportional pattern, once in a high-density arrangement (smaller area per
luminaire) and once in a low-density arrangement (larger area per luminaire). The simulation

number of 72 room settings that, based on the number of the work planes in different room sizes,

The only difference in the calculation process of spatial efficiency on the work plane level and
based on the visual field angle is that in the latter, the Radiance rsensor program is used instead of
rtrace. Rsensor can directly output radiant flux in RGB format. Additionally, the simulation
process for this part was primarily performed manually.

Three visual field angles of 30, 90, and 160 degrees were applied at a seated eye height of 1.2 m
(representing viewing directions and heights in indoor spaces [19]) to investigate significant
differences between different visual field angles. These visual field angles were spread identically
for vertical and horizontal visual fields for clarity and were homogenously sensitive to the radiant
flux at all angles. The visual fields matched hypothetical cones (i.e., solid angles), with the cone
vertex representing the location of the eye and the polar angle q representing half of the designated
visual field angle.

The number of occupants in each room size was defined based on the NFPA regulations
recommending a minimum of 14 m2 for general office spaces [20]. Accordingly, the maximum
number of occupants in each room was specified by dividing the area of each room by 14 m2.
Respectively 1, 2, and 6 occupants were assigned to room areas of 12.25 m2, 25 m2, and 90.25 m2.
The location of the occupants in each room is shown in Figure 4. The number of occupants in the
modeled architectural space was increased gradually from one to the maximum occupancy load of
the given space.

In each simulation, the radiant flux values were simulated in four main directions at each occupant

It should also be noted that spatial efficiency calculations for the occupant field of view in
Radiance can address negative impacts of direct light, such as glare. To account for the negative
effects of glare in the proposed LAE method, users can calculate the amount of light reaching the
sensor with normal surface reflectances and subtract it from the amount of light reaching the sensor
when surface reflectances are completely black (0% reflectance). This method assumes that all
direct light reaching the observer causes glare. There might be exceptions to direct light causing
glare, such as diffuse light sources with larger luminous surfaces. A detailed investigation of the
glare in LAE framework is planned for the following year of the project. Although the proposed
glare calculation process was not accounted for in the iterative simulations due to computational
time limitations, users will be able to perform these calculations using Radiance.

In this section, the spatial efficiency values computed using Radiance were compared against the

Assessing alternative methods to calculate spatial efficiency

Spatial efficiency is calculated by dividing the radiant flux falling on a work plane by the optical
radiant flux emitted from all the luminaires in a room. However, it can be argued that an alternative,

luminous flux emitted by the luminaire(s). To evaluate the accuracy of this alternative illuminance-

3. EVALUATING VISUAL SENSITIVITY
3.1 Methods
The International Commission of Illumination (CIE) describes brightness as the “attribute of a
visual perception according to which an area appears to emit, transmit or reflect, more or less light
[24]”. Since brightness is observers’ subjective and relatively inconsistent response to physical
stimuli (optical radiation), a reliable and repeatable measurement system to quantify the visibility
effects of optical radiation requires an additive system. In 1924, the CIE adopted a photopic
luminous efficiency function (also called the CIE 2-degree standard observer) based on mean
values from several studies of flicker heterochromatic photometry and step-by-step brightness
matching that used large groups of people [25]. The photopic luminous efficiency function curve
is sometimes called relative visibility or luminosity curve and aims to represent an “average human
observer.” The term “brightness” is not often used in conjunction with the standard observer since
not all participants in the original psychophysical experiments completed a brightness adjustment
task [26]. Although the authors of the original studies and the CIE considered the photopic
luminous efficiency function provisional at the time [25], [27], it became the basis for an additive,
repeatable, and relatively reliable measurement system for photometric quantities that is still used
today.

The current photometric system provides a quantitative and repeatable measurement of visible
optical radiation, but its limitations may nod to the need for more comprehensive metrics for
architectural spaces. Since photometric intensity (i.e., luminous intensity) and efficacy measures
(i.e., luminous efficacy) are based on the CIE standard observer, they may not capture the
effectiveness of complex light stimuli in realistic spaces. The proposed LAE framework [28] can
address the inherent limitations of photometric measures, such as the FOV and rod or ipRGC
contribution to brightness [21]. However, the LAE framework requires the validation of spatial
peripheral brightness perception that can be affected by the FOV of the stimuli and the effective
FOV of the participant(s).

3.2 Results
This second year of the project focuses on the adjustment of the LAE framework, based on data
from two psychophysical experiment. Findings of the studies are detailed below.

Experiment I: Effect of experimental protocols and field of view on brightness
The available data collected using either the magnitude estimation method or the 2AFC method to
assess the peripheral brightness perception were examined independently. The results were
compared between the two methods to examine the compatibility and differences between the two
data collection methods. Data visualization and statistical analysis were performed in R Studio
environment version 2022.07.1.

In analyzing the effect of low versus medium luminance surround condition on participants’
perception of brightness in experiment parts I and II with the 2AFC method, a McNemar’s chi-
squared analysis showed no significant difference at 95% significance level (X2 = 0.94, p = 0.33).
Figures 18 and 19 illustrate the participants’ choice of brighter condition over the reference

luminance conditions. In both medium and low luminance surround settings, in approximately
55% of choice cases, the participants chose the test conditions with the same luminance as the
reference over the reference condition. While the observed preference indicates the effectiveness
of the control scenario, it is noteworthy that the ideal scenario would have been at 50% selection
rate. This slightly skewed response could be caused by the small sample size as well as the
individual variations in participants' responses.

4 INCORPORATING RADIANT EFFICIENCY

4.1 Methods

Efficient lighting installations are essential for optimizing energy consumption and ensuring long-
term sustainability. The concept of luminaire efficiency in the LAE framework, encompasses the
change of radiant efficiency of lighting products over time. Understanding and predicting the
changes in efficiency of luminaries is essential for a holistic approach to overall application
efficiency, optimizing energy usage, determining maintenance schedules, and forecasting lifecycle
energy costs.

In the field of illumination engineering, the energy efficiency of a luminaire is primarily assessed
using luminous efficacy, as the ratio of luminous flux to electrical power input (unit: lm/W) [37],
[38]. Another metric, radiant efficiency (or external quantum efficiency) focuses on the ratio of
optical output power (unit: W) to electrical input power (unit: W), regardless of wavelength, hance
it is unitless [37]. SSL devices, particularly LEDs, has demonstrated significant advancements in
both radiant and luminous efficiency, outperforming traditional lighting technologies in most
categories [39], [40]. Modern LEDs have luminous efficacies reaching 330 lm/W [39], with
projections suggesting even greater potential in the future [38], [40].

Over time, however, the efficiency of luminaires diminishes due to multiple factors, including
luminous flux (colloquially lumen) depreciation [39], [41], phosphor degradation [37], [39], and
color shift [42]. Lumen depreciation refers to the gradual reduction in luminous flux output over
time, a phenomenon observed in all lighting technologies but significantly mitigated in LEDs
compared to traditional sources [39]. Spectral changes can also affect the perceived luminous
efficiency by shifting emitted light away from the peak sensitivity of the human visual system.
These factors underline the importance of modeling efficiency changes to inform evidence-based
decision-making.

While significant research has focused on improving the initial efficiency of luminaires, less
attention has been given to the dynamic nature of efficiency over the operational life of a lighting
installation. Predicting efficiency degradation and scaling these predictions to estimate efficiency
at specific points of time in future or over defined periods is essential for accurate efficiency

 54

evaluations [28]. Additionally, accurate predictions enable improved decision-making regarding
lighting installations in applications ranging from urban lighting to industrial environments [41].

To ensure the consistency and reliability in evaluating the performance of LEDs over time, two
leading organizations that set benchmarks for quality and performance (ANSI and IES) have
developed the standard procedures of ANSI/IES TM-28 Technical Memorandum: Projecting
Long-Term Luminous Flux Maintenance of LED Lamps and Luminaires [43] and ANSI/IES TM-
21 Technical Memorandum: Projecting Long-Term Luminous, Photon, and Radiant Flux
Maintenance of LED Light Sources [44]. These standardized methodologies enable manufacturers,
designers, and professionals to predict and communicate the long-term behavior of LED products
effectively, which allows scaling efficiency estimates for specific dates or determine averages over
defined periods.

ANSI/IES TM-21 focuses on a standardized methodology for predicting LED lumen depreciation,
including key metrics such as L70 (the time when light source luminous flux reduces to 70%
compared to its initial output), L80, and L90. ANSI/IES TM-28 provides broader guidance on
projecting long-term light output using test data and extrapolation methods, complementing
ANSI/IES TM-21 by addressing additional modeling scenarios. Using test data collected through
protocols such as ANSI/IES LM-80 [45], ANSI/IES TM-21 ensures an accurate and consistent
calculation method for long-term flux projection and maintenance of LED light sources.

While standardized protocols such as ANSI/IES TM-21, reflect on reductions in radiant efficiency
with operating time, the evaluation of lighting product efficiency has yet to be integrated with
design and spatial factors (i.e., spatial efficiency) and end user (i.e., visual sensitivity) into the
comprehensive framework of LAE, that accounts for temporal changes in lighting design
efficiency in application. Here the changes in efficiency are calculated, using the existing
approaches outlines in ANSI/IES TM-21 were implemented into the LAE framework as weighing
factors to address the overall targeted performance of lighting design in installation over time.

Sensitivity Analysis of Efficiency Over Time

While the baseline LAE formulation incorporates radiant flux maintenance through an exponential
decay model, this deterministic approach assumes a single set of degradation parameters (α, β). In
reality, these parameters are subject to uncertainty due to variations in material properties, thermal
environments, and drive current stress, as reflected in LM-80 test data. Without accounting for
such variability, the projected efficiency values may appear overly precise and fail to capture the
real-world range of performance outcomes. To address this variation, we extended the flux
maintenance analysis by incorporating a sensitivity study on the exponential decay parameters
used in ANSI/IES TM-21–style lifetime projections. This sensitivity analysis investigates how
variations in the exponential decay parameters—α (decay rate) and β (initial flux constant)—affect
the projected efficiency of a luminaire over its operational lifetime. The exponential model used
is derived from ANSI/IES TM-21-21, which extrapolates long-term flux maintenance from LM-
80 test data. IES suggests that all data from the sample set at a given case temperature and drive
current from the ANSI/IES LM-80 test report for a specific product model should be used for flux
maintenance life projection. The recommended number for the sample set is a minimum of 20
units in order to be able to use a multiplication factor of 6 times the test duration for flux
maintenance life projection.

Additional measurements after the initial 1,000 hr at intervals less than 1,000 hr are encouraged.
Additional measurements beyond 6,000 hours are encouraged and will provide the basis for more
accurate flux maintenance predictions. Data collected within 48 hr (a 96-hr window) of each
“1,000 hr measurement point” are acceptable for use in product flux maintenance projections. This
48-hr leeway is also applicable to intervals smaller than 1,000 hr. The exact time designation shall
be used in the projection calculation. The same time interval requirements apply to luminous flux
maintenance, photon flux maintenance and radiant flux maintenance projections [44].

To perform the sensitivity analysis, α and β were varied by ±10% and ±20% to simulate
manufacturing tolerances, environmental conditions, and operational variability [49], [50].
Efficiency curves were plotted for each scenario to visualize the impact over time. This method
helps identify which parameter has a greater influence on long-term performance and informs
design tolerances and maintenance planning.

Real-World Luminous Flux Data Analysis

For projects such as the development of LAE, LM-80 data provide more than just a measure of
product durability. Flux degradation directly impacts the radiant efficiency component of LAE by
determining how effectively a luminaire maintains its luminous output over time relative to input
power. While initial luminous efficacy values are important, they fail to capture long-term
performance trends that influence both energy use and user experience in built environments [51].
Integrating LM-80-derived degradation profiles allows LAE metrics to account for temporal
efficiency decay, producing more realistic application-level efficiency values across the
operational life of a system.

Moreover, variability in flux degradation across manufacturers—as illustrated in real-world LM-
80 reports—highlights the role of material science, thermal management, and phosphor stability
in determining long-term outcomes [52]. In addition, considering luminous flux degradation in
LAE aligns the framework with sustainability principles, as it connects product reliability to
embodied carbon and life cycle assessments of lighting systems. To evaluate whether luminous
flux degradation patterns differed significantly across three LED chip manufacturers (anonymized
for privacy), we conducted a one-way ANOVA. The dataset comprised normalized flux retention
values (𝛷$,0123 ) for Company 1, Company 2, and Company 3, measured under comparable LM-
80 test conditions (55 °C, 700 mA drive current). Because LM-80 datasets often contain
incomplete overlap in reporting intervals, only operation times with data from all three companies
were retained for the analysis. This ensured that comparisons were based on matched time points,
reducing bias from unequal sample sizes.

The ANOVA tested the null hypothesis that the mean flux values across the three companies were
equal at corresponding operational times. The alternative hypothesis was that at least one company
exhibited a statistically different mean flux level. Given the relatively small variation within each
company’s SEM and the consistent time-series structure, ANOVA was chosen as an appropriate
parametric method. The analysis was supplemented with descriptive statistics and graphical
inspection to verify general trends and data consistency.

Calculated efficiencies allow for more nuanced applications, such as the development of weighting
factors for particular time points or the determination of average efficiencies over an operational
period. These applications support a range of decision-making processes, including efficiency
assessment, lifecycle cost analysis, energy performance optimization, and maintenance
scheduling.

The primary limitation for such calculations is the difficulty in accessing radiant flux maintenance
data. Testing at the module or luminaire level significantly delays product launches, whereas the
ANSI/IES TM21/LM80 approach utilizes LED chip manufacturer data. During luminaire
development, the only required data is LED temperature, which is typically gathered during safety
compliance testing. While the ANSI/IES TM21/LM80 approach does not account for system

 62

degradation factors such as plastic discoloration, driver changes, or thermal variations over time,
manufacturers generally do not find it necessary or face customer demand for this additional, more
complex step, given their component choices.

In addition, we combined the radiant flux calculations into the LAE calculations in a spreadsheet
calculator form using Microsoft Visual Basic Application (VBA) programming language, as
shown in Fig. 30. The latest form of the LAE calculator combines spatial efficiency calculations
completed in year 1 with radiant flux maintenance calculations from year 3. This final version of
the LAE calculator takes inputs related to the space (dimensions, reflectance), target (field of view
or task plane), occupants (field of view, number of users), task plane (number of task planes, area),
lighting system (density, spatial distribution), and combine them with luminaire flux efficiency
and visual sensitivity (both accounted as percentages), and provide an output of application
efficacy. Spectral considerations can be integrated into the analysis to account for shifts in the
distribution of emitted SPDs in the future, which could have potential implications based on
application or aesthetic qualities. By combining the extrapolated lumen maintenance data with
spectral degradation factors, this approach can further refine the projection of efficiency changes.

Sensitivity Analysis of Efficiency Over Time

The sensitivity analysis revealed that small variations in the decay rate (α) and initial flux constant
(β) significantly influence the projected efficiency of luminaires over time. Increasing α by 10%
resulted in a noticeably faster decline in efficiency, while decreasing it extended the luminaire’s
effective lifespan, as shown in Fig. 31. The sensitivity analysis revealed clear trends in how
parameter changes influence lifetime projections. Variations in β primarily shifted the starting
point of the decay curve, while changes in α significantly altered the slope and long-term
performance trajectory. The comparison across scenarios highlighted that uncertainty in α, even at
±10%, can lead to substantial differences in predicted flux maintenance, whereas β variations exert
a more modest effect. These findings underscore the importance of accurate characterization of

 64

LED degradation parameters in long-term performance modeling. The exponential model used,
based on ANSI/IES TM-21-21, proved effective in capturing these trends and can be used to
inform design tolerances and maintenance schedules.

The incorporation of sensitivity analysis of efficiency over time into the LAE framework addresses
a critical limitation of relying solely on deterministic models of radiant efficiency reduction. While
the exponential decay function provides a tractable means of representing luminous flux
degradation (with parameters α and β derived from LM-80 data), it assumes fixed values that may
not reflect the variability inherent in real-world systems. In practice, these parameters are
influenced by numerous factors, including material composition, thermal stress, drive current, and
manufacturer-specific design differences. Consequently, deterministic efficiency projections may
overstate the precision of long-term predictions and obscure the range of possible outcomes.

Real-World Luminous Flux Data Analysis

In addition to the theoretical and modeled calculations of flux degradation, this study incorporated
real-world LM-80 test data from three major LED chip manufacturers. The selected products were
comparable in type, with luminous outputs of approximately 200 lm with driver current at 700 mA
under a case temperature of 55 °C. By analyzing luminous flux maintenance across these datasets,
we established a practical benchmark that complements the projections derived from the ANSI/IES
TM-21 methodology. This real-world perspective not only validates the modeled decay trends but
also highlights inter-manufacturer variability under standardized test conditions.

The one-way ANOVA revealed a highly significant difference among from three companies’
luminous flux performance over time (F = 217.86, p < 0.0001). Company 1 exhibited relatively
stable flux maintenance close to 98–99%, with very gradual declines, as shown in Fig. 32.
Company 2, in contrast, consistently reported values above 100%, ranging from approximately
99.3% to 102.9%, suggesting potential calibration differences or conservative initial baselines.
Company 3 demonstrated a middle trajectory, maintaining near 99% flux initially but slowly
decreasing toward 98.5% at 10,000 hours. The real world LM-80 data from manufacturers lied
within ±10% variations in the initial flux constant (β), but over the ±10% decay rate (α) variations.
These findings indicate that, while all three manufacturers maintain flux levels above the LM-70
threshold, the statistical differences highlight non-negligible variability in reported performance.
Such differences could stem from variations in chip design, phosphor stability, or measurement

manufacturer variability when projecting long-term efficiency or developing weighting factors for

5 EXTENDING LIGHTING APPLICATION EFFICACY

Since the inception of our LAE framework, it has become increasingly evident that the concept
provides fertile ground for methodological innovation across both research and practice. Originally
designed to integrate photometric performance, spectral weighting, and application-specific
human and environmental outcomes into a single evaluative metric, LAE has now inspired
multiple strands of scholarly work that seek to refine, expand, and operationalize this approach in
diverse contexts. A central motivation behind these extensions has been the recognition that light
must be understood not only in terms of luminaire efficiency but also in how it interacts with
human observers, architectural environments, cultural settings, and sustainability imperatives.

Recent contributions illustrate how LAE’s foundational logic is being reinterpreted and applied.
He and colleagues [54], [55], [56] have pioneered methods to capture and quantify luminous flux
entering human eyes using camera-based techniques, both in theoretical formulation and field
validation. Their work pushes the LAE framework toward a personalized, occupant-centered
metric by measuring actual environmental light exposure rather than assuming uniform field
conditions. In parallel, Bhattacharya et al. [57] developed a framework for evaluating indoor
lighting in higher education spaces, balancing energy efficiency with human-centric design, which
reflects the LAE principle of multi-dimensional optimization. Applications have also expanded
into cultural and sustainability domains: Cerpentier et al. [58] leveraged adaptive museum lighting
with CNN-based segmentation to align conservation and visual experience goals, while Cárdenas
Magaña et al. [59] integrated sustainable lighting methodologies with the UN Sustainable
Development Goals, further embedding LAE-like reasoning into institutional decision-making.
Collectively, these studies demonstrate that LAE has provided not just a metric but a conceptual
paradigm for uniting efficiency, human well-being, and environmental stewardship.

Looking forward, the LAE framework can be extended in several important directions. First,
quantitative methods such as those introduced by He et al. enable dynamic, exposure-based metrics
that account for gaze, position, and temporal variations in light reception [60]. A parallel direction
is the application of LAE to circadian and non-visual human outcomes. Traditional lighting metrics
fail to capture the influence of spectral content on the human circadian system, which is primarily
sensitive to short-wavelength light as quantified by melanopic weighting functions. By
incorporating these alternative spectral sensitivities, LAE can evolve into a framework for

 68

evaluating circadian lighting efficacy, bridging the gap between engineering design and human
health. This extension aligns with the increasing interest in circadian lighting in workplaces,
schools, and healthcare environments, where appropriate light exposure is linked to sleep quality,
alertness, and long-term well-being. Just as V(λ) served as the historical anchor for visually
effective lighting, the adoption of circadian-relevant functions into LAE paves the way for a new
generation of biologically effective lighting standards.

One of the most significant opportunities for extending the LAE framework lies in replacing the
conventional V(λ) photopic weighting with alternative spectral sensitivity functions tailored to
specific biological or ecological endpoints. For example, in horticultural lighting, photosynthetic
photon flux density (PPFD) and action spectra for photosynthesis or photomorphogenesis provide
more appropriate bases for quantifying useful radiant energy [61]. By substituting these biological
weighting functions for V(λ), LAE can be adapted to evaluate lighting efficacy in terms of plant
productivity and growth quality rather than human visual performance. This shift is already evident
in controlled environment agriculture, where optimizing spectral power distributions is critical not
only for energy efficiency but also for crop yield and nutritional content. Extending LAE into
horticultural contexts therefore enables a more integrated assessment that balances sustainability,
food security, and operational costs.

Another promising frontier is the application of LAE principles to outdoor lighting design and the
mitigation of anthropogenic light at night (ALAN). Light pollution is not simply a matter of wasted
energy; it affects ecological systems, disrupts circadian rhythms in humans and wildlife, and
obscures the visibility of the night sky [62], [63]. By redefining “application efficacy” in outdoor
contexts, LAE can help establish metrics that reward lighting solutions which balance task
visibility with minimized spectral and spatial overreach. For instance, replacing V(λ) with
ecological sensitivity functions—such as those describing insect attraction or melatonin
suppression—allows the evaluation of outdoor lighting systems in terms of their environmental
impact. In practice, this could mean using LAE to compare conventional white LED streetlights
against narrowband or spectrally tuned alternatives (e.g., red or amber LED lighting) that reduce
skyglow and ecological disruption. Such an approach provides a quantitative framework for
municipalities and agencies to select lighting technologies not solely on the basis of luminous
efficacy, but on their ability to deliver sufficient visibility while minimizing adverse biological

 69

and environmental effects. In this way, LAE becomes an actionable tool for combating ALAN,
aligning urban lighting policy with ecological stewardship and human health. These extensions
underscore the broad relevance of LAE and point toward a research trajectory in which lighting
performance is measured not only in light source efficacy but also in meaningful application
efficacy across human, cultural, and ecological domains.

6 CONCLUSIONS

Lighting is necessary for visibility in buildings, but it also contributes to energy efficiency. Current
energy efficiency metrics are limited to measuring the efficacy of individual luminaires and cannot
capture the energy savings gained by adopting next-generation intelligent solid-state lighting
devices. Instead, lighting application efficiency, characterizing the efficient delivery of light from
the light source to the target tasks, should be measured [14]. A novel LAE framework has been
developed to quantify the effectiveness of lighting in indoor spaces and the ability to actively
control the source to minimize energy consumption when the light is not being used. The proposed
LAE framework is based on three distinct parameters: luminaire efficiency, spatial efficiency, and
visual sensitivity of the occupant. In the first year of the project, the spatial efficiency calculation
method was developed as described in this report. Spatial efficiency can be calculated in user-
defined task locations in a modeled architectural space using Radiance lighting simulation
software. Similarly, the relationship between spatial efficiency and occupants’ field of view, which
can be described in solid angles, can also be calculated.

Consequently, psychophysical experiments were conducted to test the effect of contrast and field
of view on brightness perception to decide if an adjustment is needed to LAE. The results revealed
no statistically significant differences in peripheral brightness perception between FOVs under
various background luminance conditions. Further investigation into low versus medium
luminance surround within a 60-degree FOV showed no significant difference between
participants’ choice of higher perceived brightness. In the second part of the analysis, the results
revealed no statistically significant differences in participants’ peripheral brightness perception
various background luminance conditions in negative contrast settings except in 30° FOV and 60°
FOV. This could be due to having a high luminance exposure in the central visual field, which
made the changes to the peripheral luminance values imperceptible, especially in the larger FOV
sizes.

Finally, the luminaire efficiency component of the LAE framework was strengthened by
incorporating efficiency reduction over operational lifetime, with explicit consideration of flux
depreciation. The projection methods and their adaptation of the ANSI/IES TM-21 framework
demonstrated a versatile and practical approach to evaluating long-term lighting performance. The
sensitivity analysis of efficiency over time further expanded this foundation by examining how

 71

variations in degradation trajectories influence calculated outcomes, thereby highlighting the
importance of operational assumptions in efficiency assessments. Complementing these modeled
approaches, the real-world luminous flux data analysis, based on LM-80 test results from multiple
LED manufacturers, grounded the framework in empirical evidence of lumen depreciation under
realistic conditions. Collectively, these efforts not only enhance the rigor of the LAE methodology
but also provide a more robust and adaptable foundation for both academic inquiry and practical
decision-making in sustainable lighting design.
