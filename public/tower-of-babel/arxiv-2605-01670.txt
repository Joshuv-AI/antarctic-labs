Maxwell à la Helmholtz: Direct boundary integral equations for 3D scattering by perfect electric conductors via Helmholtz operators
Carlos Pérez-Arancibia, Catalin Turc

Abstract
This paper is the direct-formulation companion to [ 3 ] , which developed indirect combined-field-only boundary integral equations (BIEs) for time-harmonic electromagnetic scattering by smooth perfectly electrically conducting (PEC) obstacles, relying entirely on Helmholtz boundary integral operators. Here we exploit the same equivalence between the Maxwell PEC scattering problem and a pair of vector Helmholtz boundary value problems—one for the electric field and one for the magnetic field—to derive direct BIE formulations whose unknowns are the Dirichlet and Neumann traces of the total fields, decomposed into their normal and tangential surface components. These unknowns carry direct physical meaning: in particular, the magnetic-field formulation yields the surface electric currents as part of its solution. The mixed regularity of the two field-trace components requires introducing a tailored product Hölder space, a distinctive feature absent from the indirect approach. We prove that the resulting Direct Electric and Magnetic Combined-Field-Only Integral Equations (D-ECFOIE and D-MCFOIE) are uniquely solvable at all frequencies, and introduce Calderón-type regularizations (RD-ECFOIE and RD-MCFOIE) that render them of the Fredholm second kind. We further examine the low-frequency breakdown affecting the electric-field formulation and introduce a modified equation that enforces the physical charge-conservation constraints, which restores numerical accuracy and well-conditioned linear systems for frequencies arbitrarily close to zero. Numerical experiments, performed using a high-order Nyström solver based on the Density Interpolation Method and implemented in the Julia package Inti.jl , validate the accuracy and robustness of the proposed formulations across a range of geometries and frequencies.

Introduction

The numerical solution of frequency-domain electromagnetic scattering problems via boundary integral equations (BIEs) is widely regarded as significantly more challenging than the analogous acoustic problem governed by the Helmholtz equation. Traditional Maxwell BIE formulations—such as the electric field integral equation (EFIE), the magnetic field integral equation (MFIE), and their combined-field counterpart (CFIE)
[
32
]
—involve surface-tangential vector unknowns, complex hypersingular kernels, and notorious difficulties including low-frequency breakdown and the need for specialized Calderón preconditioning strategies. While important contributions have addressed these issues—including high-order Nyström methods
[
2
,
11
,
12
,
17
]
and current-and-charge reformulations incorporating Helmholtz operators
[
7
,
8
,
31
,
1
]
—the gap between the practical accessibility of Helmholtz and Maxwell BIE solvers remains significant.

The companion paper
[
3
]
introduced a framework—referred to as
Maxwell à la Helmholtz
—that bridges this gap by reformulating 3D electromagnetic scattering by smooth perfectly electrically conducting (PEC) obstacles entirely in terms of Helmholtz boundary integral operators. The central idea rests on two equivalence theorems establishing that the Maxwell PEC scattering problem is equivalent to a pair of vector Helmholtz boundary value problems (BVPs) for the electric and magnetic fields individually, with boundary conditions expressed through the Dirichlet and Neumann traces of the respective field components. Building on these theorems, in
[
3
]
we develop
indirect
combined-field-only BIE formulations—the Electric and Magnetic Field-Only Integral Equations (ECFOIE and MCFOIE)—in which the unknowns are auxiliary surface densities appearing in a combined-field layer potential ansatz. Crucially, these densities carry no direct physical meaning. The companion paper also introduces Calderón-regularized variants (R-ECFOIE and R-MCFOIE) that render the formulations of the second kind, and addresses the low-frequency breakdown inherent in the electric-field formulation via a modified combined-field potential.

The present paper develops the
direct
counterpart of this framework. Rather than representing the scattered field through an ansatz in terms of auxiliary densities, we apply Green’s representation formula directly to the scattered field in the exterior domain and to the incident field in the interior, combining them to obtain integral representations in terms of the Dirichlet and Neumann traces of the
total
fields. These traces carry clear physical meaning: for the electric formulation, the unknown decomposes into the normal component of the total electric field on the surface and the tangential component of its normal derivative; for the magnetic formulation, the roles are interchanged. In particular, the solution of the direct magnetic formulation yields the surface electric currents directly, which are central to many applications and can be used to reconstruct the full scattered field via the classical Stratton–Chu formulas
[
27
]
.

A related class of methods—known as
field-only surface integral equations

[
20
,
28
,
30
,
29
]
—has previously explored direct reformulations of Maxwell problems in terms of Helmholtz equations. In particular,
[
30
]
uses the same non-standard boundary condition on the normal derivative of the electric field (appearing in the equivalence theorems of
[
3
]
) to construct a direct BIE. However, that formulation suffers from spurious resonances, is not of Fredholm second kind, and its low-frequency behavior is not analyzed. Spurious resonances for the case of a sphere were partially addressed in
[
9
]
, but neither work treats the magnetic formulation or provides a thorough well-posedness analysis. The present paper provides a complete theoretical treatment of the direct approach that was missing in the prior literature.

Concretely, the contributions of this paper are the following:

•

We derive direct combined-field-only integral equations for both the electric and magnetic fields—the D-ECFOIE and D-MCFOIE—whose unknowns are projections of the Dirichlet and Neumann traces of the respective total fields onto the tangential and normal components at the surface. These unknowns live in a product function space
\mathcal{C}^{p,q}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)
, defined in (
17
), with mixed Hölder regularity reflecting the different smoothness of the two components, which is a distinctive feature of the direct approach not present in the indirect formulation.

•

We prove uniqueness of solutions to the D-ECFOIE and D-MCFOIE for all wavenumbers
k>0
(Theorems
4.2
and
5.1
). The argument proceeds by reducing the homogeneous BIEs to the uniqueness question for the underlying PEC scattering problem, formulated as a pair of vector Helmholtz boundary value problems derived in
[
3
]
.

•

We introduce Calderón-type (single-layer) regularizers for both formulations (the RD-ECFOIE and RD-MCFOIE), and establish that the resulting regularized equations are of the Fredholm second kind on
C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
, from which existence of solutions follows by the Fredholm alternative.

•

We address the potential low-frequency breakdown of the electric-field formulation as
k\downarrow 0
by introducing a modified formulation that enforces the underlying charge-conservation constraints. We prove the unique solvability of the modified formulation for all
k>0
under mild conditions on the stabilization parameter (
\xi
), and demonstrate numerically that it restores accuracy and well-conditioned GMRES iteration counts for frequencies arbitrarily close to
k=0
in the cases severely affected by the phenomenon. The magnetic-field formulation, by contrast, is unaffected by this breakdown on simply-connected surfaces.

•

We validate the proposed formulations through numerical experiments using a high-order Nyström method based on the General-Purpose Density Interpolation Method (GP-DIM)
[
10
]
, as implemented in the open-source Julia package
Inti.jl

[
18
]
. The examples cover smooth surfaces of varying topology and geometry under planewave incidence, and span a wide range of frequencies including the near-zero regime.

Preliminaries

The essential definitions and notation used throughout the paper are collected in this section.

Function spaces

First, we introduce the notation for the function spaces that will be used throughout the paper. Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
Y\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Given
p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the Hölder space
C^{p,\alpha}(X,Y)
is defined as

C^{p,\alpha}(X,Y)=\left\{F=(f_{1},\cdots,f_{n}):X\to Y\ \middle|\ f_{i}\in C^{p}(X),\ \forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n\},\ \text{and}\ \forall|\beta|=p,\ {\rm D}^{\beta}f_{i}\in C^{0,\alpha}(X)\right\},

with the norm

\|F\|_{C^{p,\alpha}(X,Y)}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{|\beta|\leq p}\|{\rm D}^{\beta}f_{i}\|_{C(X)}+\sum_{|\beta|=p}\left[{\rm D}^{\beta}f_{i}\right]_{C^{0,\alpha}(X)}\right).

Throughout, we use standard multi-index notation, and the symbol
{\rm D}
denotes the total derivative. For the definition and properties of the standard Hölder space
C^{0,\alpha}(X)
, as well as the norm
\|\cdot\|_{C(X)}
and the seminorm
[~\cdot~]_{C^{0,\alpha}(X)}
, we refer the reader to
[
14
, Sec. 4.1]
or
[
4
, Sec. 2.2]
.

Domain geometry and boundary regularity

We let
\Gamma
denote a smooth surface that encloses an open, bounded region
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and assume that the exterior domain
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
is connected. The boundary
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
admits the decomposition
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\Gamma_{J}
into
J
pairwise disjoint, closed, bounded, and connected components
\Gamma_{j}
, for
j\in\{1,\ldots,J\}
, each assumed to be at least
C^{2,\alpha}
-smooth for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
. The outward unit normal vector field is denoted by
\nu
and belongs to
C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{R}^{3})
. This regularity guarantees that the shape operator, defined as
\mathscr{R}={\rm D}_{\Gamma}\nu
, where
{\rm D}_{\Gamma}
denotes the surface derivative, belongs to
C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Similarly, the mean curvature, given by
\mathscr{H}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{div}_{\Gamma}\nu
in terms of the surface divergence, lies in
C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma)
.

Specifically, we let
{\rm x}:\mathcal{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\Gamma
be a
C^{2,\alpha}
-smooth local regular parametrization mapping an open set
\mathcal{N}
onto a neighborhood of a point on
\Gamma
. The coefficients of the first and second fundamental forms are then given by

E={\rm x}_{u}\cdot{\rm x}_{u},\quad F={\rm x}_{u}\cdot{\rm x}_{v},\quad G={\rm x}_{v}\cdot{\rm x}_{v},

and

L=-{\rm x}_{u}\cdot\nu_{u},\quad M=-{\rm x}_{u}\cdot\nu_{v}=-{\rm x}_{v}\cdot\nu_{u},\quad N=-{\rm x}_{v}\cdot\nu_{v},

respectively, where subscripts denote partial derivatives with respect to the local coordinates
(u,v)\in\mathcal{N}
. Letting
{\rm x}_{u}
and
{\rm x}_{v}
be represented as column vectors, the shape operator
\mathscr{R}
can then be expressed in terms of these coefficients as

\mathscr{R}=-[{\rm x}_{u}\ {\rm x}_{v}]\begin{bmatrix}E&F\\
F&G\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}L&M\\
M&N\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}E&F\\
F&G\end{bmatrix}^{-1}[{\rm x}_{u}\ {\rm x}_{v}]^{\top},

and the mean curvature is recovered via
\mathscr{H}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\mathscr{R}
. As is well known
[
6
]
, both
\mathscr{R}
and
\mathscr{H}
are intrinsic geometric quantities of the surface, independent of the particular parametrization, and can be defined globally on all of
\Gamma
. In fact they define the following multiplication operators, which will arise naturally in our boundary integral equation formulations:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{R}}:C^{p,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3}),

\displaystyle\quad({\mathsf{R}}\varphi)(x)=\mathscr{R}(x)\varphi(x),\quad\text{ and}

(1)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{H}}:C^{p,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3}),

\displaystyle\quad({\mathsf{H}}\varphi)(x)=\mathscr{H}(x)\varphi(x),\quad x\in\Gamma,\ p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(2)

Traces and projectors

Next, we introduce the notation for the exterior and interior traces of sufficiently regular
\mathbb{C}^{n}
-valued vector fields, with
n\in\{1,3\}
, which will be used throughout the paper to impose boundary conditions and to define the decomposition of surface unknowns.
Let
U^{+}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
(resp.
U^{-}\subset\Omega
) be an open set such that its closure contains
\Gamma
.
The exterior and interior Dirichlet traces are then defined as

\displaystyle\gamma^{+}:C^{p,\beta}(\overline{U^{+}},\mathbb{C}^{n})\to C^{p,\beta}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{n}),

\displaystyle\quad\gamma^{+}F(x)=\lim_{\delta\to 0+}F(x+\delta{\nu}(x)),

(3a)

\displaystyle\gamma^{-}:C^{p,\beta}(\overline{U^{-}},\mathbb{C}^{n})\to C^{p,\beta}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{n}),

\displaystyle\quad\gamma^{-}F(x)=\lim_{\delta\to 0+}F(x-\delta{\nu}(x)),\quad x\in\Gamma,

(3b)

where
(p,\beta)\in(\{0,1\}\times[0,1])\cup(\{2\}\times[0,\alpha])
, respectively.

Similarly, we introduce the following notation for the exterior and interior Neumann traces:

\displaystyle\partial^{+}_{\nu}:C^{p+1,\beta}(\overline{U^{+}},\mathbb{C}^{n})\to C^{p,\beta}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{n}),

\displaystyle\quad\partial^{+}_{\nu}F(x):=\lim_{\delta\to 0+}{\rm D}F(x+\delta\nu(x)){\nu}(x),

(4a)

\displaystyle\partial^{-}_{\nu}:C^{p+1,\beta}(\overline{U^{-}},\mathbb{C}^{n})\to C^{p,\beta}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{n}),

\displaystyle\quad\partial^{-}_{\nu}F(x):=\lim_{\delta\to 0+}{\rm D}F(x-\delta\nu(x)){\nu}(x),\quad x\in\Gamma,

(4b)

for
(p,\beta)\in(\{0,1\}\times[0,1])\cup(\{1\}\times[0,\alpha])
, respectively,
where

{\rm D}:F\mapsto({\rm D}F)_{i,j}=\frac{\partial F_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
j\in\{1,2,3\},

denotes the total derivative (which is represented by the Jacobian matrix
(\partial F_{i}/\partial x_{j})_{i,j}
). The trace operators
\gamma^{\pm}
and
\partial^{\pm}_{\nu}
are well-defined and bounded due to the assumed
C^{2,\alpha}
-regularity of the surface
\Gamma
, with
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Throughout this paper, unless the interior trace is explicitly required, we adopt the convention

\gamma:=\gamma^{+}
and
\partial_{\nu}:=\partial^{+}_{\nu}
, i.e.,
\gamma
and
\partial_{\nu}
without a superscript always denote the exterior traces. We also use the alternative notation
\frac{\partial F}{\partial\nu}:=\partial^{+}_{\nu}F
for the exterior Neumann trace of a field
F
.

It is important to note that, when
n=3
, the above definition (
4
) implies that the Neumann traces
\partial^{\pm}_{\nu}F
are a vector field whose coordinate components correspond to the scalar Neumann traces of each component
(F)_{i}=F_{i}
(
i\in\{1,2,3\}
) of the vector field
F
, i.e.,
(\partial^{\pm}_{\nu}F)_{i}=\partial^{\pm}_{\nu}F_{i}
.

We make extensive use of the projectors on the tangent plane of
\Gamma
and along its normal. Specifically, we define the operators:

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{\nu}:C^{p,\beta}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{p,\beta}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3}),

\displaystyle\quad{\mathsf{P}_{\nu}}\varphi(x)=(\nu(x)\cdot\varphi(x))\nu(x)\quad\text{and}

(5a)

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{t}:C^{p,\beta}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{p,\beta}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3}),

\displaystyle\quad{\mathsf{P}_{t}}\varphi(x)=\varphi(x)-(\nu(x)\cdot\varphi(x))\nu(x),\quad x\in\Gamma,

(5b)

for
(p,\beta)\in(\{0\}\times[0,1])\cup(\{1\}\times[0,\alpha])
, which map the vector
\varphi(x)
onto the normal and the tangent plane at
x\in\Gamma
, respectively. Clearly, since
\nu\in C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{R}^{3})
, both projectors are bounded.

Helmholtz integral operators

Our boundary integral equation formulations are based on Helmholtz layer potentials and boundary integral operators, which act component-wise on the three Cartesian components of a vector density, introduced below. We begin by defining the vector-valued Helmholtz layer potentials and their associated boundary integral operators. Let
\mathcal{S}:C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
and
\mathcal{D}:C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})

\alpha\in(0,1)
, denote the Helmholtz single- and double-layer potentials, respectively, defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}[\varphi](x):=

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}G(x,y)\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}s(y)\quad\text{and}

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}[\varphi](x):=

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\partial G(x,y)}{\partial\nu(y)}\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}s(y),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Gamma,

(7)

for vector density functions
\varphi\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
, where

G(x,y):=\frac{\operatorname{e}^{\mathrm{i}k|x-y|}}{4\pi|x-y|},\quad x\neq y,\quad k\geq 0,

is the free-space Green’s function for the Helmholtz equation. Here and throughout this work, the (vectorial) layer potentials (
6
) and (
7
), along with the associated (vectorial) boundary integral operators introduced below, are to be interpreted component-wise.

It follows from the standard jump relations for scalar Helmholtz layer potentials
[
4

that analogous jump conditions hold for the vector-valued Helmholtz layer potentials. Specifically, consider a sufficiently regular vector density function
\varphi:\Gamma\to\mathbb{C}^{3}
. The vectorial layer potentials (
6
) and (
7
) admit extensions to the closure of both the exterior domain
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Omega
and the interior domain
\overline{\Omega}
, and satisfy the following limiting relations as the target point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Gamma
approaches the boundary
\Gamma
from either side:

\displaystyle\gamma^{\pm}(\mathcal{S}\varphi)=

\displaystyle~{\mathsf{S}}\varphi,

\displaystyle\gamma^{\pm}(\mathcal{D}\varphi)=

\displaystyle\pm\frac{1}{2}\varphi+{\mathsf{K}}\varphi,

(8a)

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}^{\pm}(\mathcal{S}\varphi)=

\displaystyle\mp\frac{1}{2}\varphi+{\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}\varphi,

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}^{\pm}(\mathcal{D}\varphi)=

\displaystyle~{\mathsf{T}}\varphi,

(8b)

where
\gamma^{\pm}
and
\partial_{\nu}^{\pm}
denote the exterior/interior Dirichlet and Neumann traces defined in (
3
) and (
4
), respectively, and
{\mathsf{S}}
,
{\mathsf{K}}
,
{\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}
and
{\mathsf{T}}
are the (vector) boundary integral operators:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{S}}:C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3}),

\displaystyle\quad{\mathsf{S}}[\varphi](x):=\int_{\Gamma}G(x,y)\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}s(y),

(9a)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{K}}:C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3}),

\displaystyle\quad{\mathsf{K}}[\varphi](x):=\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\partial G(x,y)}{\partial\nu(y)}\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}s(y),

(9b)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}:C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3}),

\displaystyle\quad{\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}[\varphi](x):=\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\partial G(x,y)}{\partial\nu(x)}\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}s(y),

(9c)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{T}}:C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3}),

\displaystyle\quad{\mathsf{T}}[\varphi](x):={\rm f.p.}\!\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\partial^{2}G(x,y)}{\partial\nu(x)\partial\nu(y)}\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}s(y),

(9d)

for
x\in\Gamma
, which are well defined component-wise and bounded under the assumption that
\Gamma
is
C^{2,\alpha}
-smooth. This result directly follows from the properties of the corresponding scalar operators
[
5

. The hypersingular integral defining

Problem setup

This paper addresses the problem of time-harmonic electromagnetic scattering arising when an incident electromagnetic field
(E^{i},H^{i})
impinges on the surface
\Gamma
of a PEC obstacle, embedded in an unbounded, homogeneous, isotropic medium characterized by dielectric and magnetic constants
\epsilon>0
and
\mu>0
, respectively.

The incident electromagnetic field
(E^{i},H^{i})
, where each field belongs to
C^{1,\alpha}(U,\mathbb{C}^{3})
, is assumed to be defined in an open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
containing
\Omega
, where it satisfies the time-harmonic Maxwell equations:

\operatorname{curl}E^{i}-\mathrm{i}\omega\mu H^{i}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{curl}H^{i}+\mathrm{i}\omega\epsilon E^{i}=0\quad\text{in}\quad U,

(10)

with
\omega>0
denoting the angular frequency.

The scattered electric and magnetic fields, denoted by
E^{s}
and
H^{s}
, respectively, are generated by the interaction of the incident field with the PEC boundary. These scattered fields satisfy the time-harmonic Maxwell equations:

\operatorname{curl}E^{s}-\mathrm{i}\omega\mu H^{s}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{curl}H^{s}+\mathrm{i}\omega\epsilon E^{s}=0\quad\text{in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega},

(11a)

with the electric field subject to the PEC boundary condition
[
19
]
:

\mathsf{P}_{t}\gamma E^{s}=-\mathsf{P}_{t}\gamma E^{i}\quad\text{on}\quad\Gamma,

(11b)

which is written using the tangential projector (
5b
). The scattered field is further required to satisfy the Silver–Müller radiation condition at infinity, given by either

\begin{split}\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}|x|\left(\operatorname{curl}E^{s}(x)\times\frac{x}{|x|}-\mathrm{i}kE^{s}(x)\right)=0\quad\text{or}\quad\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}|x|\left(\operatorname{curl}H^{s}(x)\times\frac{x}{|x|}-\mathrm{i}kH^{s}(x)\right)=0,\end{split}

(11c)

where the limit holds uniformly with respect to all directions
x/|x|
and where
k:=\omega\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}>0
is the wavenumber. The time dependence
\operatorname{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}
is assumed throughout the paper.

The following theorem, whose proof can be found in
[
3
, Corollary 5.14]
, establishes the well-posedness of the scattering problem (
11c
) in Hölder spaces suitable for our analysis:

Theorem 3.1
.

Under the stated assumptions on the surface
\Gamma
and the incident fields
E^{i}
and
H^{i}
, the PEC scattering problem (
11c
) admits a unique solution

E^{s},H^{s}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{C}^{3})\cap C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Omega,\mathbb{C}^{3})
for all
\omega>0
.

[
3
]
that are central to our formulations. They establish that the electromagnetic scattering problem (
11c
) is equivalent to a pair of vector Helmholtz boundary value problems—one for the electric field and one for the magnetic field—in which the boundary conditions are expressed solely in terms of the Dirichlet and Neumann traces of the respective fields. and

H^{s}=(\mathrm{i}\omega\mu)^{-1}\operatorname{curl}E^{s}
and

satisfies:

\displaystyle\Delta E^{s}+k^{2}E^{s}=

\displaystyle~0\quad\text{ in }\ \mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega},

(12a)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}_{t}}(\gamma E^{s})=

\displaystyle-{\mathsf{P}_{t}}(\gamma E^{i}),

(12b)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}_{\nu}}(\partial_{\nu}E^{s}+2\mathscr{H}\gamma E^{s})=

\displaystyle-{\mathsf{P}_{\nu}}(\partial_{\nu}E^{i}+2\mathscr{H}\gamma E^{i}),

\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}|x|\left\{({\rm D}E^{s})\frac{x}{|x|}-\mathrm{i}kE^{s}\right\}=

4
Direct electric combined-field-only formulation

In this section, we derive a direct boundary integral equation formulation for the electric field scattering problem (
12
), and study its existence and uniqueness.

Under the assumption that the incident fields
E^{i}
and
H^{i}
satisfy (
10
), we have

\operatorname{curl}\operatorname{curl}E^{i}-k^{2}E^{i}=0
in
\Omega\subset U.

Using the vector identity
\operatorname{curl}\operatorname{curl}E^{i}=\nabla\operatorname{div}E^{i}-\Delta E^{i}
and the fact that
\operatorname{div}E^{i}=0
in
\Omega
, it follows that

\Delta E^{i}+k^{2}E^{i}=0
in
\Omega
. Applying Green’s representation theorem (component-wise) to
E^{i}
in
\Omega
then yields

0=\mathcal{D}[\gamma E^{i}](x)-\mathcal{S}[\partial_{\nu}E^{i}](x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega}

Similarly, leveraging the fact that
E^{s}
satisfies the vector Helmholtz equation (
12a
) and the component-wise Sommerfeld radiation condition (
12d
), we apply Green’s representation formula for the Helmholtz equation to the scattered field
E^{s}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
, and obtain:

E^{s}(x)=\mathcal{D}[\gamma E^{s}](x)-\mathcal{S}[\partial_{\nu}E^{s}](x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega}.

By adding these two integral representations, we arrive at the following expression:

E^{s}(x)=\mathcal{D}[\gamma E](x)-\mathcal{S}[\partial_{\nu}E](x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega},

(14)

where we introduce the following notation for the traces of the total field
E:=E^{s}+E^{i}
defined in
(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega})\cap U
:

\gamma E:=\gamma E^{s}+\gamma E^{i}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{\nu}E:=\partial_{\nu}E^{s}+\partial_{\nu}E^{i}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3}).

Expressing the boundary conditions (
12c
) and (
12b
) in terms of the traces of the total field, we obtain:

\gamma E=\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\gamma E\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{\nu}E=\mathsf{P}_{t}\partial_{\nu}E-2{\mathsf{H}}\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\gamma E,

where
\mathsf{P}_{\nu}
and
\mathsf{P}_{t}
denote the normal and tangential projections, respectively, as defined in (
5
), and
{\mathsf{H}}
denotes the mean curvature multiplication operator (
2
).

Upon substituting these identities into Green’s representation formula (
14
), we obtain:

E^{s}(x)=\mathcal{D}\left[\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\gamma E\right](x)-\mathcal{S}\left[\mathsf{P}_{t}\partial_{\nu}E-2{\mathsf{H}}\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\gamma E\right](x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega}.

(15)

Taking the exterior Dirichlet and Neumann traces on both sides of (
15
) yields:

\displaystyle-\gamma E^{i}=

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\gamma E+({\mathsf{K}}+2{\mathsf{S}}{\mathsf{H}})\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\gamma E-{\mathsf{S}}\mathsf{P}_{t}\partial_{\nu}E,

(16a)

\displaystyle-\partial_{\nu}E^{i}=

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathsf{P}_{t}\partial_{\nu}E-2{\mathsf{H}}\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\gamma E)+({\mathsf{T}}+2{\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}{\mathsf{H}})\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\gamma E-{\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}\mathsf{P}_{t}\partial_{\nu}E.

(16b)

By taking the linear combination (
16b
)
+\mathrm{i}\eta
(
16a
) of the Neumann and Dirichlet trace equations with
\eta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, we arrive at

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}\{\mathsf{P}_{t}\partial_{\nu}E+(\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{I}}-2{\mathsf{H}})\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\gamma E\}+\{{\mathsf{T}}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{K}}+2({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}}){\mathsf{H}}\}\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\gamma E-({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}})\mathsf{P}_{t}\partial_{\nu}E=

\displaystyle-\partial_{\nu}E^{i}-\mathrm{i}\eta\gamma E^{i}.

At first glance, the relation above may appear to define an underdetermined BIE, since both the Dirichlet and Neumann traces,
\gamma E
and
\partial_{\nu}E
, are involved. However, this is not the case, as we now show. Before proceeding, we introduce the function space in which we will seek our direct BIE solutions. For
p,q\in\{0,1\}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
—where
\alpha
corresponds to the
C^{2,\alpha}
-regularity of the surface
\Gamma
—we define the space

\mathcal{C}^{p,q}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma):=\left\{\varphi:\Gamma\to\mathbb{C}^{3}\mid\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\varphi\in C^{p,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3}),\ \mathsf{P}_{t}\varphi\in C^{q,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\right\},

(17)

equipped with the norm

\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{C}^{p,q}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)}:=\|\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\varphi\|_{C^{p,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})}+\|\mathsf{P}_{t}\varphi\|_{C^{q,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})}.

(18)

Lemma 4.1
.

\mathcal{C}^{p,q}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)
is a Banach space under the norm (
18
).

Proof.

That (
18
) defines a norm follows readily: if
\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{C}^{p,q}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)}=0
then
\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\varphi=0
and
\mathsf{P}_{t}\varphi=0
, so
\varphi=\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\varphi+\mathsf{P}_{t}\varphi=0
; homogeneity and the triangle inequality are inherited from those of the Hölder norms.

For completeness, let
\{\varphi_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{C}^{p,q}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)
be a Cauchy sequence. By definition (
18
),
\{\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\varphi_{n}\}
is Cauchy in
C^{p,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
and
\{\mathsf{P}_{t}\varphi_{n}\}
is Cauchy in
C^{q,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
. Since both Hölder spaces are Banach, there exist limits
f\in C^{p,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
and
g\in C^{q,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
such that
\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\varphi_{n}\to f
and
\mathsf{P}_{t}\varphi_{n}\to g
. Define
\varphi:=f+g
. Since
\mathsf{P}_{\nu}+\mathsf{P}_{t}={\mathsf{I}}
, we have
\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\varphi=\mathsf{P}_{\nu}f+\mathsf{P}_{\nu}g=f\in C^{p,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
and
\mathsf{P}_{t}\varphi=\mathsf{P}_{t}f+\mathsf{P}_{t}g=g\in C^{q,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
, where we used that
f=\lim_{n}\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\varphi_{n}
lies in the range of
\mathsf{P}_{\nu}
and
g=\lim_{n}\mathsf{P}_{t}\varphi_{n}
lies in the range of
\mathsf{P}_{t}
, which are closed subspaces of
C^{p,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
and
C^{q,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
, respectively. Hence
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{p,q}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)
, and
\|\varphi_{n}-\varphi\|_{\mathcal{C}^{p,q}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)}=\|\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\varphi_{n}-f\|_{C^{p,\alpha}}+\|\mathsf{P}_{t}\varphi_{n}-g\|_{C^{q,\alpha}}\to 0
.
∎

Our direct electric combined-field-only integral equation (D-ECFOIE) is then given by:

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}\{\varphi_{t}+(\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{I}}-2{\mathsf{H}})\varphi_{\nu}\}+\{{\mathsf{T}}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{K}}+2({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}}){\mathsf{H}}\}\varphi_{\nu}-({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}})\varphi_{t}=f,

(19a)

where the unknown density is

\varphi:=\varphi_{t}+\varphi_{\nu}\in\mathcal{C}_{\nu,t}^{1,0}(\Gamma),\quad\varphi_{t}:=\mathsf{P}_{t}(\partial_{\nu}E),\quad\varphi_{\nu}:=\mathsf{P}_{\nu}(\gamma E)

(19b)

and the right-hand-side is given by

f:=-\partial_{\nu}E^{i}-\mathrm{i}\eta\gamma E^{i}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3}).

(19c)

The scattered electric field can then be retrieved from the BIE solution via the representation formula (
15
).

Clearly, by leveraging the surface projection operators, the equation above can be recast in the form

{\mathsf{L}}_{e}\varphi=f,

(20)

where
{\mathsf{L}}_{e}:\mathcal{C}^{1,0}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)\to C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})

is the operator defined by

{\mathsf{L}}_{e}:=-\tfrac{1}{2}\{\mathsf{P}_{t}+(\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{I}}-2{\mathsf{H}})\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\}+\{{\mathsf{T}}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{K}}+2({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}}){\mathsf{H}}\}\mathsf{P}_{\nu}-({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}})\mathsf{P}_{t}.

(21)

We are now ready to establish the uniqueness of solutions to the above BIE:

Theorem 4.2
.

The D-ECFOIE given by (
19
) admits at most one solution
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{\nu,t}^{1,0}(\Gamma)
for all wavenumbers
k>0
and all coupling parameters
\eta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

Suppose there exists a non-trivial
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{1,0}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)
satisfying the homogeneous BIE:

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}\{\varphi_{t}+(\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{I}}-2{\mathsf{H}})\varphi_{\nu}\}+\{{\mathsf{T}}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{K}}+2({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}}){\mathsf{H}}\}\varphi_{\nu}-({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}})\varphi_{t}=0.

(22)

Let
\varphi_{\nu}:=\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\varphi
and
\varphi_{t}:=\mathsf{P}_{t}\varphi
, and define the layer potential:

U(x):=\mathcal{D}[\varphi_{\nu}](x)-\mathcal{S}[\varphi_{t}-2{\mathsf{H}}\varphi_{\nu}](x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Gamma.

(23)

By taking the traces of the layer potential (
23
) and applying the jump relations (
8
), it follows that

\gamma^{\pm}U=\pm\tfrac{1}{2}\varphi_{\nu}+({\mathsf{K}}+2{\mathsf{S}}{\mathsf{H}})\varphi_{\nu}-{\mathsf{S}}\varphi_{t}\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{\nu}^{\pm}U=\pm\tfrac{1}{2}(\varphi_{t}-2{\mathsf{H}}\varphi_{\nu})+({\mathsf{T}}+2{\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}{\mathsf{H}})\varphi_{\nu}-{\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}\varphi_{t}.

(24)

Therefore, it is clear from this expression that (
22
) corresponds to the Robin boundary condition:

\partial^{-}_{\nu}U+\mathrm{i}\eta\gamma^{-}U=0.

Since the restriction
U|_{\Omega}\in C^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}^{3})\cap C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{C}^{3})
of the layer potential
U
to the interior domain
\Omega
satisfies the Helmholtz equation
\Delta U|_{\Omega}+k^{2}U|_{\Omega}=0
in
\Omega
,
it follows from the uniqueness of the interior Robin boundary value problem that
U|_{\Omega}=0
and hence
U=0
in
\overline{\Omega}
. Consequently, both interior traces
\gamma^{-}U
and
\partial_{\nu}^{-}U
vanish. Using the jump relations from (
24
), we then obtain

\gamma^{+}U-\gamma^{-}U=\gamma^{+}U=\varphi_{\nu}\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{\nu}^{+}U-\partial_{\nu}^{-}U=\partial_{\nu}^{+}U=\varphi_{t}-2{\mathsf{H}}\varphi_{\nu}.

(25)

From the expressions for the exterior traces obtained above, we observe that both
\varphi_{\nu}
and
\varphi_{t}
vanish if and only if the exterior Dirichlet and Neumann traces of the potential
U
vanish. To show that the exterior traces vanish, we note that from (
25
) it follows that

\mathsf{P}_{t}\gamma^{+}U=0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsf{P}_{\nu}(\partial^{+}_{\nu}U+2{\mathsf{H}}\gamma^{+}U)=0.

Then, given that in addition,
U
in (
23
) satisfies the Helmholtz equation (
12a
) as well as the radiation condition (
12d
), by the equivalence between the Maxwell and vector Helmholtz formulations established in Theorem
3.2
, we conclude that the restriction
U|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega}}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{C}^{3})\cap C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Omega,\mathbb{C}^{3})
of the layer potential
U
to the exterior domain solves the PEC scattering problem (
11c
) with homogeneous boundary data, i.e.,
\mathsf{P}_{t}\gamma E^{i}=0
. Since this problem admits a unique solution by Theorem
3.1
, it follows that
U=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Omega
.

This leads to a contradiction, because the boundary data
\varphi=\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\gamma^{+}U+\mathsf{P}_{t}\partial_{\nu}^{+}U
must then vanish identically, implying that the original density
\varphi
is zero. This completes the proof of uniqueness.
∎

We now present the following lemma, which is used to establish the existence of solutions to the integral equation (
4
) by means of the Fredholm alternative.

Lemma 4.3
.

The operators

\displaystyle{\mathsf{C}}_{\nu}:C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3}),

\displaystyle{\mathsf{C}}_{\nu}\varphi:=

\displaystyle~\nu S_{0}(\nu\cdot\varphi)-{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\varphi,

(26a)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{C}}_{t}:C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3}),

\displaystyle{\mathsf{C}}_{t}\varphi:=

\displaystyle~\mathsf{P}_{t}{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\varphi-{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\mathsf{P}_{t}\varphi,

(26b)

where
S_{0}:C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma)\to C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma)
denotes the scalar Laplace single-layer operator, admit the integral representations:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{C}}_{\nu}[\varphi](x)=

\displaystyle~\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\nu(y)\cdot\varphi(y)}{4\pi|x-y|}\left\{\nu(x)-\nu(y)\right\}\,\mathrm{d}s(y),

\displaystyle{\mathsf{C}}_{t}[\varphi](x)=

\displaystyle~-\int_{\Gamma}\frac{1}{4\pi|x-y|}\cdot\{\varphi(y)\cdot\nu(x)\nu(x)-\varphi(y)\cdot\nu(y)\nu(y)\}\,\mathrm{d}s(y),\quad x\in\Gamma,

and are compact.

Proof.

The well-definedness of the operators (
26
) and their integral representations follow directly by substituting the integral kernel of the Laplace single-layer operator (
9a
) (with
k=0
) into the definitions (
26a
) and (
26b
).

In order to prove that
{\mathsf{C}}_{\nu}
in (
26a
) is compact, we examine its Cartesian components, namely
{\mathsf{C}}_{\nu,j}
for
j\in\{1,2,3\}
. It hence suffices to show that the operators
\delta{\mathsf{C}}_{\nu,j}:C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
, defined by
\delta{\mathsf{C}}_{\nu,j}\varphi:=\nabla_{\Gamma}({\mathsf{C}}_{\nu,j}\varphi),
are compact.

Let
\varphi\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
. Applying the surface gradient to the integral representation of
{\mathsf{C}}_{\nu,j}
and using the well-known property that the tangential derivative of the single-layer operator
S_{0}
can be computed by differentiation under the integral sign (see, e.g.
[
4
, Thm. 2.17]
), we obtain

\begin{split}\delta{\mathsf{C}}_{\nu,j}[\varphi](x)=\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\varphi(y)\cdot\nu(y)}{4\pi|x-y|^{3}}\left\{(x-y)-\nu(x)\cdot(x-y)\nu(x)\right\}\left\{\nu_{j}(x)-\nu_{j}(y)\right\}\,\mathrm{d}s(y)+\hskip 56.9055pt\\
\nabla_{\Gamma}\nu_{j}(x)\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\varphi(y)\cdot\nu(y)}{4\pi|x-y|}\,\mathrm{d}s(y),\quad x\in\Gamma,\end{split}

(27)

where all the surface integrals are to be understood in the improper sense. Note that they are well defined by virtue of the fact that each of the integrands is weakly singular.

In view of the mapping properties of the Laplace single-layer operator
S_{0}
and the fact that
\nabla_{\Gamma}\nu_{j}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
, we readily conclude that the last term in (
27
) defines a compact operator on
C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
. On the other hand, the mapping properties of the operator defined by the first integral in (
27
) are governed by the smoothness of the kernels defining each of the operator’s Cartesian components, which are given by

\kappa_{i,j}(x,y):=\frac{\left\{(x_{i}-y_{i})-\nu(x)\cdot(x-y)\nu_{i}(x)\right\}\left\{\nu_{j}(x)-\nu_{j}(y)\right\}}{|x-y|^{3}},\quad\Gamma\ni x\neq y\in\Gamma,\quad i,j\in\{1,2,3\}.

where
\nu_{j}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma)
denote the components of the unit normal vector.
Clearly, since the Cartesian components of the unit normal
\nu_{j}
are Lipschitz continuous, there exists a constant
L>0
such that

|\kappa_{i,j}(x,y)|\leq L|x-y|^{-1}

(28)

for all
x,y\in\Gamma
,
x\neq y
.
Moreover, for all
x,y,z\in\Gamma
, we have

\begin{split}\kappa_{i,j}(x,y)-\kappa_{i,j}(z,y)=&~\frac{(x_{i}-z_{i})\left\{\nu_{j}(x)-\nu_{j}(y)\right\}}{|x-y|^{3}}-\frac{\left\{\nu(x)\cdot(x-z)\nu_{i}(x)\right\}\left\{\nu_{j}(x)-\nu_{j}(y)\right\}}{|x-y|^{3}}\\
&-\frac{\left\{(\nu(x)-\nu(z))\cdot(z-y)\nu_{i}(x)\right\}\left\{\nu_{j}(x)-\nu_{j}(y)\right\}}{|x-y|^{3}}\\
&-\frac{\left\{\nu(z)\cdot(z-y)(\nu_{i}(x)-\nu_{i}(z)\right\}\left\{\nu_{j}(x)-\nu_{j}(y)\right\}}{|x-y|^{3}}\\
&+\frac{\left\{(z_{i}-y_{i})-\nu(z)\cdot(z-y)\nu_{i}(z)\right\}\left\{\nu_{j}(x)-\nu_{j}(z)\right\}}{|x-y|^{3}}\\
&+\left\{(z_{i}-y_{i})-\nu(z)\cdot(z-y)\nu_{i}(z)\right\}\left\{\nu_{j}(z)-\nu_{j}(y)\right\}\left\{\frac{1}{|x-y|^{3}}-\frac{1}{|z-y|^{3}}\right\}.\end{split}

Taking absolute value and applying the triangle inequality, we arrive at

\begin{split}|\kappa_{i,j}(x,y)-\kappa_{i,j}(z,y)|\leq&~2\frac{|x-z|}{|x-y|^{2}}+2\frac{|x-z||z-y|}{|x-y|^{2}}+2\frac{|z-y||x-z|}{|x-y|^{3}}\\
&+2|z-y|^{2}\left|\frac{1}{|x-y|^{3}}-\frac{1}{|z-y|^{3}}\right|\end{split}

(29)

Then, under the assumption that
2|x-z|\leq|x-y|
, we have (see
[
3
, Eq. (72)]
with
m=3
):

|z-y|\leq\frac{3}{2}|x-y|\quad\text{and}\quad\left|\frac{1}{|x-y|^{3}}-\frac{1}{|z-y|^{3}}\right|\leq 48\frac{|x-z|}{|x-y|^{4}}.

Using these inequalities in (
29
) we obtain

\begin{split}|\kappa_{i,j}(x,y)-\kappa_{i,j}(z,y)|\leq&~2\frac{|x-z|}{|x-y|^{2}}+3\frac{|x-z|}{|x-y|}+3\frac{|x-z|}{|x-y|^{2}}+216\frac{|x-z|}{|x-y|^{2}}.\end{split}

(30)

It then follows that the kernels
\kappa_{i,j}
satisfy all the conditions in
[
4
, Thm.2.7]
, so we can conclude that the first integral in (
27
) defines a bounded operator from
C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
to
C^{0,\beta}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
for all
\beta\in(\alpha,1]
. Then, using the compact embedding of
C^{0,\beta}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
into
C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
for any
\beta>\alpha

[
4
, Thm. 2.5]
, we conclude that each operator
\delta{\mathsf{C}}_{\nu,j}
for
j\in\{1,2,3\}
is compact on
C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
. Hence,
{\mathsf{C}}_{\nu}
is compact as an operator from
C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
to
C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
.

The compactness of the operator
{\mathsf{C}}_{t}:C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
can be established by noting that it can be expressed as

{\mathsf{C}}_{t}[\varphi](x)=-\nu(x)\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\varphi(y)\cdot\{\nu(x)-\nu(y)\}}{4\pi|x-y|}\,\mathrm{d}s(y)-\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\varphi(y)\cdot\nu(y)}{4\pi|x-y|}\{\nu(x)-\nu(y)\}\,\mathrm{d}s(y)

where the second term is exactly
{\mathsf{C}}_{\nu}\varphi
, and the first term can be analyzed analogously by exploiting the smoothness of the unit normal vector.

This completes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to state and prove the well-posedness of (
19
).

Theorem 4.4
.

The D-ECFOIE (
19
) is uniquely solvable in
\mathcal{C}^{1,0}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)
for all wavenumbers
k>0
and coupling parameters
\eta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

We begin the proof by introducing the following regularizing operator:

{\mathsf{R}}_{\nu}:\mathcal{C}^{1,0}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)\to C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3}),\qquad{\mathsf{R}}_{\nu}\widetilde{\varphi}:=-2\mathsf{P}_{t}\widetilde{\varphi}-4\nu S_{0}(\nu\cdot\widetilde{\varphi}),

(31)

which, in view of the invertibility of the Laplace single-layer operator
S_{0}:C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma)\to C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma)
, is invertible
[
21
, Thm. 7.40]
. Indeed, its inverse

{\mathsf{R}}^{-1}_{\nu}:C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to\mathcal{C}^{1,0}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)

is well defined, bounded and given by the formula

{\mathsf{R}}^{-1}_{\nu}\varphi=-\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{P}_{t}\varphi-\frac{1}{4}\nu S_{0}^{-1}(\nu\cdot\varphi).

We then perform the substitution
\varphi={\mathsf{R}}_{\nu}\widetilde{\varphi}
into (
19
), where
\widetilde{\varphi}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
. This substitution yields

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{t}\widetilde{\varphi}+2(\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{I}}-2{\mathsf{H}})\nu S_{0}(\nu\cdot\widetilde{\varphi})-4\{{\mathsf{T}}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{K}}+2({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}}){\mathsf{H}}\}[\nu S_{0}(\nu\cdot\widetilde{\varphi})]+2({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}})\mathsf{P}_{t}\widetilde{\varphi}=f.

Next, using the identity

\nu S_{0}(\nu\cdot\widetilde{\varphi})={\mathsf{C}}_{\nu}\widetilde{\varphi}+{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\widetilde{\varphi},

which follows from the definition of the operator
{\mathsf{C}}_{\nu}
in (
26a
), we can rewrite the equation above as

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{t}\widetilde{\varphi}+2(\mathrm{i}\eta-2{\mathsf{H}})\nu{\mathsf{S}}_{0}(\nu\cdot\widetilde{\varphi})-4\{{\mathsf{T}}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{K}}+2({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}}){\mathsf{H}}\}\{{\mathsf{C}}_{\nu}\widetilde{\varphi}+{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\widetilde{\varphi}\}+2({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}})\mathsf{P}_{t}\widetilde{\varphi}=f.

(32)

From Calderón’s identity

{\mathsf{T}}_{0}{\mathsf{S}}_{0}={{\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}_{0}}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}{\mathsf{I}},

(33)

for the operators defined with
k=0
, we obtain

{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{S}}_{0}={\mathsf{T}}_{0}{\mathsf{S}}_{0}+({\mathsf{T}}-{\mathsf{T}}_{0}){\mathsf{S}}_{0}=-\tfrac{1}{4}{\mathsf{I}}+{{\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}_{0}}^{2}+({\mathsf{T}}-{\mathsf{T}}_{0}){\mathsf{S}}_{0}.

(34)

Therefore, substituting this expression into (
32
), the equation can be recast as

\widetilde{\varphi}+{\mathsf{A}}\widetilde{\varphi}=f

(35a)

where
{\mathsf{A}}:=\sum_{j=1}^{4}{\mathsf{A}}_{j}
and the operators
{\mathsf{A}}_{j}:C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
for
j\in\{1,2,3,4\}
are defined by:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{A}}_{1}\varphi:=

\displaystyle~2(\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{I}}-2{\mathsf{H}})\nu S_{0}(\nu\cdot\varphi),

(35b)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{A}}_{2}\varphi:=

\displaystyle-4\{{{\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}_{0}}^{2}+({\mathsf{T}}-{\mathsf{T}}_{0}){\mathsf{S}}_{0}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{K}}{\mathsf{S}}_{0}+2({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}}){\mathsf{H}}{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\}\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\varphi,

(35c)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{A}}_{3}\varphi:=

\displaystyle-4\{{\mathsf{T}}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{K}}+2({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}}){\mathsf{H}}\}{\mathsf{C}}_{\nu}\varphi,

(35d)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{A}}_{4}\varphi:=

\displaystyle~2({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}})\mathsf{P}_{t}\varphi.

(35e)

As it turns out, each of these operators, and therefore the full operator
{\mathsf{A}}
, is compact on
C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
. In particular,
{\mathsf{A}}_{1}
is compact, as it is the composition of the bounded operator
2(\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{I}}-2{\mathsf{H}})
with the compact operator
{\mathsf{C}}_{\nu}+{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\mathsf{P}_{\nu}
. Regarding
{\mathsf{A}}_{2}
, the operator
{\mathsf{T}}-{\mathsf{T}}_{0}
, along with all the other operators appearing within the curly brackets in its definition, are compact on
C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
. Since
\mathsf{P}_{\nu}
is bounded in that space, their composition with
\mathsf{P}_{\nu}
remains compact. Similarly,
{\mathsf{A}}_{3}
is compact, as it is the composition of the compact operator
{\mathsf{C}}_{\nu}:C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
(see Lemma
4.3
) with the bounded operator
4\{{\mathsf{T}}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{K}}+2({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}}){\mathsf{H}}\}:C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
.
Finally,
{\mathsf{A}}_{4}
is compact on
C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
, as it is the composition of the compact operator
2({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}})
with the bounded operator
\mathsf{P}_{t}
.

Now, to establish the uniqueness of solutions to the regularized BIE (
35
), suppose that it admits two distinct solutions
\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
for
j\in\{1,2\}
. Then, by the injectivity of the regularizer, the corresponding densities
\varphi_{j}:={\mathsf{R}}_{\nu}\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\in\mathcal{C}^{1,0}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)
are both solutions to the homogeneous boundary integral equation (
22
), and satisfy
\varphi_{1}\neq\varphi_{2}
. This, however, contradicts Theorem
4.2
. We therefore conclude that (
35
) admits at most one solution
\widetilde{\varphi}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
. Finally, by the Fredholm alternative, since (
35
) is of the form “invertible plus compact”, the existence of such a solution follows.

Finally, from the unique solution
\widetilde{\varphi}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
of the regularized BIE (
35
), we recover the unique solution of the original BIE (
19
) by applying the regularizer, that is,
\varphi={\mathsf{R}}_{\nu}\widetilde{\varphi}\in\mathcal{C}^{1,0}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)
. This completes the proof.

∎

Low-frequency breakdown

As shown in
[
3
]
, the low-frequency breakdown of the indirect (R-)ECFOIE formulations is rooted in the lack of uniqueness of the
k=0
limit of problem (
12
): in the zero-frequency limit, the homogeneous exterior boundary value problem for the vector Laplacian admits non-trivial solutions unless the surface charge integrals are prescribed for each boundary component
[
33
]
(see also
[
3
, Remark 4.2]
). The same issue affects the direct formulations of the present paper: both the D-ECFOIE (
19
) and the RD-ECFOIE (
35
) suffer from low-frequency breakdown as
k\downarrow 0
, meaning that the accuracy of the numerical solutions and the conditioning of the linear systems arising from the discretization of the BIEs degrade as
k\downarrow 0
. The remedy, as in the indirect case, is to enforce the charge conditions satisfied by any physical solution of (
12
), namely

\int_{\Gamma_{j}}\nu\cdot\gamma E^{s}\,\,\mathrm{d}s=0,\qquad j\in\{1,\ldots,J\},

which are precisely the constraints that restore uniqueness in the zero-frequency limit
[
33
]
. Well-conditioning can then be enforced by augmenting
{\mathsf{L}}_{e}
with a low-rank perturbation that penalizes violations of these charge conditions.

To this end, we introduce the following bounded linear functionals:

\ell_{j}:C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to\mathbb{C},\qquad\ell_{j}(\varphi):=\int_{\Gamma_{j}}\nu\cdot\varphi,\mathrm{d}s,\quad j\in\{1,\ldots,J\},

(36)

along with the vector fields

\phi_{j}:={\mathsf{L}}_{e}\nu_{j}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3}),\qquad\text{where}\qquad\nu_{j}(x):=\begin{cases}\nu(x),&x\in\Gamma_{j},\\
0,&x\in\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{j},\end{cases}\qquad j\in\{1,\ldots,J\}.

(37)

We then consider the following modification of the D-ECFOIE:

\Big({\mathsf{L}}_{e}+\xi\sum_{j=1}^{J}\phi_{j}\ell_{j}\Big)\varphi=f,

(38)

where
{\mathsf{L}}_{e}
is the operator defined in (
21
), and the unknown density
\varphi=\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\gamma E+\mathsf{P}_{t}\partial_{\nu}E\in\mathcal{C}^{1,0}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)
.

Similarly, we propose a corresponding modification of the regularized formulation (
35
), given by:

\Big({\mathsf{I}}+{\mathsf{A}}+\xi\sum_{j=1}^{J}\phi_{j}\ell_{j}{\mathsf{R}}_{\nu}\Big)\widetilde{\varphi}=f,

(39)

for
\widetilde{\varphi}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
, where the regularizing operator
{\mathsf{R}}_{\nu}
is defined in (
31
).

Note that we have used the identity
{\mathsf{L}}_{e}{\mathsf{R}}_{\nu}={\mathsf{I}}+{\mathsf{A}}
, as established in the proof of Theorem
4.4
. Also observe that the modified formulation (
39
) remains a second-kind BIE, as the added term corresponds to a finite-rank operator, which is compact.

We now present the following lemma, which establishes the existence of solutions to the modified equations:

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{1,0}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)
be the unique solution to the D-ECFOIE (
19
). Then, for any
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
,
\varphi
also satisfies the modified equation (
38
). Similarly, if
\widetilde{\varphi}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
is the unique solution to the RD-ECFOIE (
35

39
) for all
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi=\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\gamma E+\mathsf{P}_{t}\partial_{\nu}E\in\mathcal{C}^{1,0}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)
be the unique solution to the D-ECFOIE (
19
) with datum defined in (
19c
). Then, for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,J\}
,

\ell_{j}(\varphi)=\int_{\Gamma_{j}}\nu\cdot\gamma E\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{\Gamma_{j}}\nu\cdot\gamma E^{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{\Gamma_{j}}\nu\cdot\gamma E^{i}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Since the incident field
E^{i}
is assumed to satisfy Maxwell’s equations in
U
and therefore also in
\Omega
, we have
\operatorname{div}E^{i}=0
in
\Omega
. Applying the divergence theorem over the subdomain
\Omega_{j}\subset\Omega
yields

\ell_{j}(\gamma E^{i})=\int_{\Gamma_{j}}\nu\cdot\gamma E^{i}\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{\Omega_{j}}\operatorname{div}E^{i}\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

To analyze the contribution of the scattered field, we first observe that the traces

\gamma E^{s}=\mathsf{P}_{\nu}(\varphi)-\gamma E^{i}
and
\partial_{\nu}E^{s}=\mathsf{P}_{t}\varphi-2\mathscr{H}\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\varphi-\partial_{\nu}E^{i}

allow us to recover the scattered field
E^{s}
throughout the exterior domain
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
via Green’s representation formula. We can then make use of the series expansion of the scalar field
E^{s}(x)\cdot x
in the region
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{B_{R}(0)}
, where
B_{R}(0)
is a ball large enough to contain the domain
\Omega
. As shown in
[
10
]
, this field admits the expansion

E^{s}(x)\cdot x=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}c_{l}^{m}\,h_{l}^{(1)}(k|x|)Y_{l}^{m}\left(\frac{x}{|x|}\right),\qquad|x|\geq R,

where
\{Y_{l}^{m}\}_{m=-l}^{l}
are spherical harmonics and
\{h_{l}^{(1)}\}_{l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
are spherical Hankel functions of the first kind. Since the spherical harmonics satisfy

\int_{\partial B_{1}(0)}Y_{l}^{m}(\hat{x})\,\mathrm{d}s(\hat{x})=0\quad\text{for all }l\in\mathbb{N},\;-l\leq m\leq l,

it follows that

\frac{1}{R}\int_{\partial B_{R}(0)}E^{s}(x)\cdot x\,\mathrm{d}s(x)=\int_{\partial B_{R}(0)}E^{s}(x)\cdot\frac{x}{|x|}\,\mathrm{d}s(x)=0.

Now, define the scalar field
u_{j}:=\operatorname{div}E^{s}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{j}}
, which vanishes in the exterior domain
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
, and is extended by zero to all of
\Omega
except
\Omega_{j}
, i.e.,
u_{j}=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{j}
. Let
B_{R}(0)
be as before. Applying the divergence theorem to
u_{j}
over
B_{R}(0)\setminus\Omega_{j}
, we obtain

0=-\int_{B_{R}(0)\setminus\Omega_{j}}u_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x=\ell_{j}(\gamma E^{s})-\frac{1}{R}\int_{\partial B_{R}(0)}E^{s}(x)\cdot x\,\mathrm{d}s(x).

Since the surface integral over
\partial B_{R}(0)
above vanishes, it follows that
\ell_{j}(\gamma E^{s})=0.

Combining this with the result for
\ell_{j}(\gamma E^{i})
, we conclude that

\ell_{j}(\varphi)=\ell_{j}(\gamma E^{s})+\ell_{j}(\gamma E^{i})=0
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,J\}.
This shows that
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{1,0}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)
also satisfies the modified equation (
38
). Finally, the fact that the unique solution
\widetilde{\varphi}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
of (
35
) satisfies (
39
) follows immediately from the identity
\varphi={\mathsf{R}}_{\nu}\widetilde{\varphi}
, since

\ell_{j}(\varphi)=\ell_{j}({\mathsf{R}}_{\nu}\widetilde{\varphi})=0,\quad j\in\{1,\ldots,J\}.

This completes the proof.
∎

It remains to determine under which conditions on the stabilization parameter
\xi
the modified boundary integral equations, (

Theorem 4.6
.

Suppose
\xi\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{J}\{-|\Gamma_{j}|^{-1}\}
, where
|\Gamma_{j}|
denotes the surface area of the connected component
\Gamma_{j}
of
\Gamma
. Then, for all
k>0
and
\eta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, the modified BIEs (
38
) and (
39
) each admit a unique solution,
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{1,0}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)
and
\widetilde{\varphi}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
, respectively. Moreover, these solutions coincide with the unique solutions of the D-ECFOIE (
19
) and the RD-ECFOIE (
35
), respectively.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the statement for (
39
). It follows from Theorem
4.4
that the operator
{\mathsf{I}}+{\mathsf{A}}
admits a bounded inverse on
C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
. Let us define the operator

{\mathsf{Q}}:C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to\mathbb{C}^{J},\qquad{\mathsf{Q}}\varphi:=[\ell_{1}\varphi,\ldots,\ell_{J}\varphi]^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{J},

in terms of the linear functionals
\ell_{j}
introduced in (
36

{\mathsf{V}}:\mathbb{C}^{J}\to C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3}),\qquad{\mathsf{V}}\mathbf{c}:=\sum_{j=1}^{J}c_{j}\phi_{j},\quad\mathbf{c}=[c_{1},\ldots,c_{J}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{J},

in terms of the vector fields
\phi_{j}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
defined in (
37
). It is clear that both
{\mathsf{Q}}
and
{\mathsf{V}}
are bounded linear operators on their respective domains.

With these definitions, the modified regularized equation (
39

\left({\mathsf{I}}+{\mathsf{A}}+\xi{\mathsf{V}}{\mathsf{Q}}{\mathsf{R}}_{\nu}\right)\widetilde{\varphi}=f.

Applying the Woodbury identity, one finds that the inverse of this operator is given by

\left({\mathsf{I}}+{\mathsf{A}}+\xi{\mathsf{V}}{\mathsf{Q}}{\mathsf{R}}_{\nu}\right)^{-1}=({\mathsf{I}}+{\mathsf{A}})^{-1}-\xi({\mathsf{I}}+{\mathsf{A}})^{-1}{\mathsf{V}}\left({\mathsf{I}}_{J}+\xi\Xi\right)^{-1}{\mathsf{Q}}{\mathsf{R}}_{\nu}({\mathsf{I}}+{\mathsf{A}})^{-1},

\Xi:={\mathsf{Q}}{\mathsf{R}}_{\nu}({\mathsf{I}}+{\mathsf{A}})^{-1}{\mathsf{V}}={\mathsf{Q}}{\mathsf{L}}_{e}^{-1}{\mathsf{V}}\in\mathbb{C}^{J\times J}.

Clearly, the inverse operator (

so the matrix
{\mathsf{I}}_{J}+\xi\Xi
is invertible if and only if
\xi\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{J}\{-|\Gamma_{j}|^{-1}\}
, which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

To explain the rationale behind the modified BIEs (
38
) and (
39
), note that the modified D-ECFOIE (
38
) can be rewritten, using
\phi_{j}={\mathsf{L}}_{e}\nu_{j}
, as

{\mathsf{L}}_{e}\!\left({\mathsf{I}}+\xi\sum_{j=1}^{J}\nu_{j}\ell_{j}\right)\varphi=f,

or equivalently
\left({\mathsf{I}}+\xi\sum_{j=1}^{J}\nu_{j}\ell_{j}\right)\varphi={\mathsf{L}}_{e}^{-1}f
. Applying the functional
\ell_{i}
to both sides and using the fact that
\ell_{i}({\mathsf{L}}_{e}^{-1}f)=0
(which follows from the preceding lemma applied to the datum
f
), we obtain

\ell_{i}\varphi+\xi\sum_{j=1}^{J}(\ell_{i}\nu_{j})(\ell_{j}\varphi)=\left(1+\xi|\Gamma_{i}|\right)\ell_{i}\varphi=0,

where we used
\ell_{i}\nu_{j}=\delta_{ij}|\Gamma_{i}|
. Therefore, since
1+\xi|\Gamma_{i}|\neq 0
for
\xi\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{J}\{-|\Gamma_{j}|^{-1}\}
, the charge conditions
\ell_{i}\varphi=0
are automatically enforced for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,J\}
. In practice, however, the condition
\ell_{i}({\mathsf{L}}_{e}^{-1}f)=0
is only approximately satisfied due to discretization errors and potential matrix ill-conditioning, so the right-hand side above is a small but nonzero residual
\varepsilon_{i}\approx 0
. In this case the identity becomes
(1+\xi|\Gamma_{i}|)\ell_{i}\varphi=\varepsilon_{i}
, so that
\ell_{i}\varphi=\varepsilon_{i}/(1+\xi|\Gamma_{i}|)
. Choosing
\xi\gg 1
drives
\ell_{i}\varphi
toward zero, thereby penalizing deviations from the charge condition even in the presence of discretization errors.

Theorem 5.1
.

The D-MCFOIE given by (

We proceed as in the proof of Theorem

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\{\psi_{\nu}+(\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{I}}-{\mathsf{R}})\psi_{t}\}+\{{\mathsf{T}}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{K}}+({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}}){\mathsf{R}}\}\psi_{t}-({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}})\psi_{\nu}=0.

\psi_{\nu}=\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\psi

\gamma^{\pm}V=\pm\frac{1}{2}\psi_{t}+({\mathsf{K}}+{\mathsf{S}}{\mathsf{R}})\psi_{t}-{\mathsf{S}}\psi_{\nu},\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{\nu}^{\pm}V=\pm\frac{1}{2}(\psi_{\nu}-{\mathsf{R}}\psi_{t})+({\mathsf{T}}+{\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}{\mathsf{R}})\psi_{t}-{\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}\psi_{\nu},

As in the case of the D-ECFOIE, we rely on a Calderón-type regularization of the magnetic boundary integral equation (

Lemma 5.2
.

The operator

{\mathsf{R}}_{t}:\mathcal{C}^{0,1}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)\to C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3}),\quad{\mathsf{R}}_{t}\psi=-2(\mathsf{P}_{\nu}+2\mathsf{P}_{t}{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\mathsf{P}_{t})\psi,

(51)

Proof.

Clearly,
{\mathsf{R}}_{t}
is linear and bounded. We then proceed to show that
{\mathsf{R}}_{t}
is injective. Suppose there exists a nontrivial function
\psi\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
such that

-\frac{1}{2}{\mathsf{R}}_{t}\psi=\psi_{\nu}+2\mathsf{P}_{t}{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\psi_{t}=0,

(52)

where we let
\psi_{\nu}:=\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\psi
and
\psi_{t}:=\mathsf{P}_{t}\psi
.

Let us denote by

(\varphi,\psi)_{L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})}:=\int_{\Gamma}\varphi\cdot\overline{\psi}\,\mathrm{d}s

the inner product on the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
, equipped with the norm

\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})}:=\sqrt{(\psi,\psi)_{L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})}}.

Taking the inner product with
\psi
on both sides of (

\displaystyle(\psi,\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\psi)_{L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})}+2(\psi,\mathsf{P}_{t}{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\mathsf{P}_{t}\psi)_{L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})}=(\psi_{\nu},\psi_{\nu})_{L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})}+2(\psi_{t},{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\psi_{t})_{L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})}\

\displaystyle=0.

Extracting the real part of the identity above yields

\|\psi_{\nu}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})}^{2}+2\mathrm{Re}\,(\psi_{t},{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\psi_{t})_{L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})}=0.

(53)

Then, in view of the facts that
\mathrm{Re}\,(\psi_{t},{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\psi_{t})_{L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})}\geq 0
, and that equality holds if and only if
\psi_{t}=0
, since
\psi_{t}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\subset L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\subset H^{-1/2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})

Therefore,

\psi_{\nu}=0
and
\psi_{t}=0
, which implies that
\psi=0
, contradicting the assumption that
\psi
is nontrivial. We then conclude that
{\mathsf{R}}_{t}
is injective.

To finish the proof, it suffices to show that for all
\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{0,1}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)
there exists a unique
\widetilde{\psi}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
such that

-\frac{1}{2}{\mathsf{R}}_{t}\widetilde{\psi}=(\mathsf{P}_{\nu}+2\mathsf{P}_{t}{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\mathsf{P}_{t})\widetilde{\psi}=\psi.

(54)

It follows directly from (
54
) and the mapping properties of
\mathsf{P}_{\nu}
that the normal component
\widetilde{\psi}_{\nu}:=\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\widetilde{\psi}=\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\psi\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
. To prove the existence of the tangential component
\widetilde{\psi}_{t}:=\mathsf{P}_{t}\widetilde{\psi}
, we rewrite (
54
) as

\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\psi+2({\mathsf{S}}_{0}+{\mathsf{C}}_{t})\widetilde{\psi}_{t}=\psi,

in terms of the commutator
{\mathsf{C}}_{t}
defined in (
26b
). Subtracting
\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\psi
from both sides and using that
\mathsf{P}_{t}\psi=\psi-\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\psi\in C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
, we obtain

({\mathsf{S}}_{0}+{\mathsf{C}}_{t})\widetilde{\psi}_{t}=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathsf{P}_{t}\psi.

Applying
{\mathsf{S}}_{0}^{-1}:C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
and using that this operator is bounded
[
21
, Thm. 7.40]
, we get

\widetilde{\psi}_{t}+{\mathsf{S}}_{0}^{-1}{\mathsf{C}}_{t}\widetilde{\psi}_{t}=\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathsf{S}}_{0}^{-1}\mathsf{P}_{t}\psi.

(55)

Since
{\mathsf{C}}_{t}:C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
is compact by Lemma
4.3
, the composition
{\mathsf{S}}_{0}^{-1}{\mathsf{C}}_{t}
is compact on
C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
. Hence, in view of the injectivity of
{\mathsf{R}}_{t}
and the fact that
\frac{1}{2}{\mathsf{S}}_{0}^{-1}\mathsf{P}_{t}\psi\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
, the Fredholm alternative guarantees the existence and uniqueness of a solution
\widetilde{\psi}_{t}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
to (
55
) which corresponds to the tangential component of
\widetilde{\psi}
. This completes the proof.

∎

We are now in a position to establish the well-posedness of the magnetic BIE (
45
).

Theorem 5.3
.

The D-MCFOIE (
45
) is uniquely solvable in
\mathcal{C}^{0,1}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)
for all wavenumbers
k>0
and coupling parameters
\eta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

Consider the regularized BIE resulting from substituting
\psi={\mathsf{R}}_{t}\widetilde{\psi}
into (
45
), where
{\mathsf{R}}_{t}
is the regularizing operator defined in (
51
). This substitution leads to the equation

\widetilde{\psi}_{\nu}+2(\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{I}}-{\mathsf{R}})\mathsf{P}_{t}{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\widetilde{\psi}_{t}-4\{{\mathsf{T}}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{K}}+({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}}){\mathsf{R}}\}\mathsf{P}_{t}{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\widetilde{\psi}_{t}+2({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}})\widetilde{\psi}_{\nu}=g,

(56)

where we seek a solution
\widetilde{\psi}=\widetilde{\psi}_{\nu}+\widetilde{\psi}_{t}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
with
\widetilde{\psi}_{\nu}:=\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\widetilde{\psi}
and
\widetilde{\psi}_{t}:=\mathsf{P}_{t}\widetilde{\psi}
.

It then follows from the invertibility of
{\mathsf{R}}_{t}
(Lemma
5.2
) and the uniqueness of solutions to (
45
) (Theorem
5.1
) that, to complete the proof, it suffices to show that the regularized equation (
56
) is of the form identity plus a compact perturbation in
C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
. This is so because, by the Fredholm alternative, uniqueness implies existence when the operator is of Fredholm type with index zero.

To this end, we substitute the identities (
34
) and
\mathsf{P}_{t}{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\mathsf{P}_{t}\widetilde{\psi}={\mathsf{S}}_{0}\mathsf{P}_{t}\widetilde{\psi}+{\mathsf{C}}_{t}\mathsf{P}_{t}\widetilde{\psi}
into (
56
), which allows us to rewrite the equation in the form

\widetilde{\psi}+{\mathsf{B}}\widetilde{\psi}=g,

(57)

where
{\mathsf{B}}:=\sum_{j=1}^{4}{\mathsf{B}}_{j}
and the operators
{\mathsf{B}}_{j}:C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
for
j\in\{1,2,3,4\}
are given by

\displaystyle{\mathsf{B}}_{1}\psi:=

\displaystyle~2(\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{I}}-{\mathsf{R}})\mathsf{P}_{t}{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\psi,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{B}}_{2}\psi:=

\displaystyle-4\{{{\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}_{0}}^{2}+({\mathsf{T}}-{\mathsf{T}}_{0}){\mathsf{S}}_{0}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{K}}{\mathsf{S}}_{0}+({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}}){\mathsf{R}}{\mathsf{S}}_{0}\}\mathsf{P}_{t}\psi,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{B}}_{3}\psi:=

\displaystyle-4\{{\mathsf{T}}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{K}}+({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}}){\mathsf{R}}\}{\mathsf{C}}_{t}\mathsf{P}_{t}\psi,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{B}}_{4}\psi:=

\displaystyle~2({\mathsf{K}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta{\mathsf{S}})\mathsf{P}_{\nu}\psi.

Following the arguments in the proof of Theorem
4.4
, it is straightforward to show that each of the operators
{\mathsf{B}}_{j}
is compact on
C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
. In particular,
{\mathsf{B}}_{3}
is compact because it involves the composition
{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{C}}_{t}
, where
{\mathsf{C}}_{t}:C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
is compact, as established in Lemma
4.3
, and
{\mathsf{T}}:C^{1,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})\to C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
is bounded. It follows that
{\mathsf{B}}
is compact on
C^{0,\alpha}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{3})
, which completes the proof.∎

Remark 5.4
.

Theorem
5.3
can be extended to the zero-frequency limit
k=0
for a simply connected surface
\Gamma
. In that case, the magnetostatic exterior problem—the
k=0
limit of (
13
)—is uniquely solvable without additional constraints
[
33
]
, in contrast to the electrostatic case which requires prescribing the charge integrals. This unique solvability can be leveraged, following the same arguments as in
[
3
]
for the indirect (R-)MCFOIE, to show that the D-MCFOIE remains well-posed at
k=0
for any
\eta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. We do not pursue this extension here, as the proof is analogous to that in
[
3
]
.

We conclude this section by noting that the D-MCFOIE (
45
) solution
\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{0,1}_{\nu,t}(\Gamma)
provides a direct representation of the surface electric currents:

J:=\nu\times\gamma H=\nu\times\mathsf{P}_{t}\psi.

These surface currents are of practical importance in their own right, playing a central role in various applications. Moreover, they can be directly used to reconstruct both the magnetic and electric components of the scattered electromagnetic field via the Stratton–Chu integral representation formulae
[
19
, Thm. 3.30]
:

\displaystyle E^{s}(x)=-\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\omega\epsilon}\operatorname{curl}\operatorname{curl}\int_{\Gamma}J(y)G(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}s(y)\quad\text{and}\quad H^{s}(x)=\operatorname{curl}\int_{\Gamma}J(y)G(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}s(y).

(58)

We next examine the low-frequency regime on the same surface
\Gamma=\mathbb{S}^{2}
, now holding the mesh fixed at
h\approx 0.16

More surprisingly, the (R)D-ECFOIE formulations also remain well-behaved throughout: field errors, divergence errors, and surface charge integrals all stay at the level of the discretization error, and iteration counts remain stable. This is in stark contrast to their indirect counterparts
[
3
]
on the same problem. The stabilization parameter
\xi=\pi\cdot 10^{4}
has negligible effect on accuracy or conditioning here, consistent with the absence of breakdown. We attribute this robustness to the simple connectedness of the sphere, which, for these formulations, appears to suppress the near-kernel components responsible for low-frequency breakdown in more general, non-simply-connected geometries, as confirmed by the results on the torus and flower surface below.

Still on the sphere, Table

reports results at higher frequencies, with meshes refined to maintain

so that the discretization error stays approximately constant across all

7
Conclusions

We have developed direct combined-field-only boundary integral formulations—D-ECFOIE and D-MCFOIE—for time-harmonic electromagnetic scattering by smooth PEC obstacles, building on the
Maxwell à la Helmholtz
framework of
[
3
]
. Unlike their indirect counterparts, the proposed formulations take as unknowns the physical field traces on the scattering surface; in particular, the D-MCFOIE yields the surface electric currents directly. We proved unique solvability at all frequencies
k>0
, introduced Calderón regularizations that render the equations of Fredholm second kind, and resolved the low-frequency breakdown of the electric-field formulation via a charge-conservation-enforcing modification. Numerical experiments with a GP-DIM-based Nyström solver implemented in
Inti.jl
confirmed the accuracy and robustness of all formulations across a range of geometries and frequencies. Future work includes extensions to Sobolev-space settings and transmission problems, all within the same Helmholtz-operator framework.
