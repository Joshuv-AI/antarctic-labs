Proximal Causal Inference For Hidden Outcomes
Helen Guo, Ilya Shpitser, Elizabeth L. Ogburn

Abstract
Methods that rely on proxies, without imposing strong parametric structure, are increasingly used to deal with unobserved variables in causal inference. One influential line of this work reconstructs latent distributions used to identify the target functional by exploiting eigenvalue-eigenvector structure. Within this framework, we first establish identification of the full-data law in the presence of hidden outcomes, and then develop influence function–based estimators for causal effects. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to develop influence function–based estimators in this setting without relying on unbiased proxy measurements or partial observation, while achieving multiple robustness and desirable efficiency properties. We demonstrate the performance of our approach through simulation studies.

Introduction

Hidden variables remain a central challenge in causal inference, often preventing identification and estimation of causal targets of interest. Proxy-based approaches have recently emerged as a way to circumvent such hidden structure without relying on strong parametric assumptions
[
17
,
1
,
18
,
9
,
28
]
. A prominent line of this work achieves identification by reconstructing latent distributions from observed data under relatively mild conditions, exploiting eigenvalue–eigenvector structure
[
17
,
1
,
28
]
.

These methods are often described as “nonparametric,” in the sense that their assumptions are sufficiently weak; under relative support size/dimensionality conditions on the variables, distributions that violate them can be approximated arbitrarily closely by distributions that satisfy them
[
4
,
2
]
. Conceptually, this line of work builds on ideas dating back to Kruskal’s uniqueness theorem for the Candecomp/Parafac (CP) decomposition
[
16
]
, which characterizes when a three-way array admits a unique representation as a sum of rank-one components. This uniqueness result allows recovery of the joint distribution of hidden and observed variables by ensuring that the observed joint distribution (represented as a three-way array) admits a unique factorization.

Using this framework, we establish identification of the full-data law of hidden and observed variables in the presence of proxies for hidden outcomes and develop an influence function-based approach for estimating causal effects. Our estimators do not require unbiased proxy measurements, are
\sqrt{n}
-consistent under standard regularity conditions, and exhibit
multiple robustness
, in the sense that consistency of estimation is preserved when certain nuisance functions are correctly specified, even if others are misspecified.

Substantial literature studies causal identification under missing outcomes or bounded measurement error
[
6
,
3
,
21
,
12
,
27
,
7
]
, and recent work has considered proxy-based methods for hidden outcomes under the presence of at least one unbiased proxy
[
8
]
. To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to establish semiparametric estimation results for hidden outcomes that do not rely on unbiased proxies or partial observations of the true outcome. We assess the performance of our proposed estimators through simulation studies on discrete and mixed data generating processes.

2.3
Related Work

Our work builds on classic results such as Kruskal’s uniqueness theorem for the Candecomp/Parafac (CP) decomposition
[
16
]
and subsequent developments
[
17
,
1
]

Under conditional independence of the observed variables given latent variable(s), together with appropriate rank conditions, this array admits a unique factorization that recovers the full-data law. The underlying argument hinges on unique eigenspaces
[
22
]
; obtaining the full-data law from the observed-data law can often be achieved via a sequence of eigendecomposition steps
[
17
,
1
]
, or alternatively through iterative search procedures (e.g., alternating least squares, gradient descent)
[
26
,
15
]

Continuous analogues replace these finite-dimensional decompositions with operator-based formulations, relying on
completeness
conditions characterizing the variability of conditional distributions. This perspective was developed by
11

5

28
. Obtaining the full-data law from the observed-data law can be achieved through sieve-based or expectation-maximization approaches
[
11
,
28
]
.

We build on the ideas from
11
,
5
, and

28
by adapting such identifying assumptions to settings with analogous proxies for hidden outcomes. In addition, we develop influence function–based estimators for the resulting causal effects, yielding estimators that are multiply robust and
\sqrt{n}
-consistent under standard regularity conditions.

4
Estimation of Causal Effects

Influence functions characterize the local sensitivity of a parameter to perturbations of the data-generating distribution, and yield estimators that are multiply robust—i.e., consistent under partial model misspecification—and
\sqrt{n}
-consistent under standard regularity conditions
[
14
]

6
Conclusion

In this work, we study causal inference in the presence of hidden variables through a proxy-based lens. Leveraging mild structural assumptions, we show that it is possible to recover the full joint law over latent and observed variables from observed data alone. Building on this identification strategy, we develop an influence function–based approach for estimating causal effects, yielding estimators that are
\sqrt{n}
-consistent and exhibit multiple robustness under standard regularity conditions. Notably, our framework avoids reliance on unbiased proxies or strong parametric restrictions.

A number of key direction remain to be explored. While our framework assumes three proxies, many applications feature multiple candidate proxies. Understanding how to optimally combine information across these proxies for efficient estimation remains an open problem. Second, our estimation strategy relies on achieving product bias convergence rates for nuisance functions; developing principled modeling strategies and learning procedures that reliably attain these rates in practice is an important challenge, particularly in high-dimensional or nonparametric settings. Finally, our results hint at a more general theory for influence functions under proxy-based full law identification, including systematic construction of influence function-based estimators across a broad class of target functionals and characterization of their efficiency properties. Addressing these questions would further strengthen the connection between identification theory and practical, scalable estimation.

G
Binary Data-Generating Process

Confounder
C
and treatment
A
are generated as follows:

C\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.3),\quad A\mid C\sim\begin{cases}\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.4),&C=0,\\
\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.6),&C=1.\end{cases}

Additionally, outcomes are generated according to a logistic (expit) model:

Y\mid A,C\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}\!\big(\mathrm{expit}(-1+3A-1.5C)\big).

Finally, proxies
Z,W,V
are independently generated conditional on
Y
as follows:

p(Z=1\mid Y=1)=p(W=1\mid Y=1)=p(V=1\mid Y=1)=0.9,

p(Z=1\mid Y=0)=p(W=1\mid Y=0)=p(V=1\mid Y=0)=0.1.

By construction,

W\perp Z\perp V\mid Y.

We form the empirical three-way array/tensor

\widehat{\mathcal{T}}(w,z,v)=\widehat{p}(W=w,Z=z,V=v).

Under Assumption
5
,

p(W,Z,V)=\sum_{y=0}^{1}p(Y=y)p(W\mid Y=y)p(Z\mid Y=y)p(V\mid Y=y).

Eigendecomposition tasks discussed in Section
2.3
yield estimates of components

\widehat{p}(Y),\qquad\widehat{p}(W\mid Y),\qquad\widehat{p}(Z\mid Y),\qquad\widehat{p}(V\mid Y),

up to labeling of
Y
. Labels are aligned by ordering the recovered latent components according to
\widehat{p}(W=1\mid Y)
, since this conditional probability is strictly increasing in
Y
under the data-generating process (option 2 in Assumption
8
).

This procedure can also be applied within strata of
(A,C)
, yielding
\widehat{p}(Y=1\mid A,C)
.

H
Mixed Data-Generating Process

Confounder
C
, treatment
A
, outcome
Y
, and proxies
W,Z,V
are generated according to

C\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1);

A\mid C\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}\!\left(\operatorname{expit}(-0.3+0.8C)\right),

Y\mid A,C\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}\!\left(\operatorname{expit}(\beta_{0}+\beta_{A}A+\beta_{C}C)\right),

with
(\beta_{0},\beta_{A},\beta_{C})=(-3,3,0.5)
;

W=-0.3+1.5Y+\varepsilon_{W};\qquad Z=0.5+1.2Y+\varepsilon_{W};\qquad V=1.0+0.8Y+\varepsilon_{V},

where

\varepsilon_{W},\varepsilon_{Z},\varepsilon_{V}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

By construction,

W\perp Z\perp V\mid Y.

We directly model the continuous measurement distribution using a two-component latent Gaussian mixture model. Under Assumption
5
,

p(W,Z,V)=\sum_{y=0}^{1}p(Y=y)\,p(W\mid Y=y)\,p(Z\mid Y=y)\,p(V\mid Y=y),

where conditional independence implies

p(W,Z,V\mid Y=y)=p(W\mid Y=y)\,p(Z\mid Y=y)\,p(V\mid Y=y).

We assume Gaussian measurement models

W\mid Y=y\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{W,y},\sigma_{W,y}^{2}),

with analogous models for
Z
and
V
. Parameters

p(Y=y),\qquad(\mu_{W,y},\sigma_{W,y}),\qquad(\mu_{Z,y},\sigma_{Z,y}),\qquad(\mu_{V,y},\sigma_{V,y})

are estimated via an expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm maximizing the observed-data log-likelihood

\ell=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left[\sum_{y=0}^{1}p(Y_{i}=y)\,p(W_{i}\mid Y_{i}=y)\,p(Z_{i}\mid Y_{i}=y)\,p(V_{i}\mid Y_{i}=y)\right].

Since the latent classes are only identified up to label switching, latent outcome labels are determined using the recovered Gaussian measurement distributions
\{\widehat{p}(W\mid Y=y):y\in\{0,1\}\}
via their estimated means
\widehat{\mu}_{W,y}
. Specifically, because the conditional mean of
W
is strictly increasing in
Y
under the data-generating process, satisfying option 2 in Assumption
8
, the recovered distribution with larger estimated mean is identified with
Y=1
.

Linear Gaussian coefficients are then recovered from the labeled class-specific means for nuisance function modeling. For example,

\widehat{\alpha}_{W,0}=\widehat{\mu}_{W,0},\qquad\widehat{\alpha}_{W,Y}=\widehat{\mu}_{W,1}-\widehat{\mu}_{W,0}.

Next, we estimate the outcome regression model

p(Y=1\mid A,C)=\operatorname{expit}(\beta_{0}+\beta_{A}A+\beta_{C}C).

The previously recovered parameters determine the estimated measurement distributions
\widehat{p}(W\mid y)
,
\widehat{p}(Z\mid y)
, and
\widehat{p}(V\mid y)
. We then estimate the outcome regression model
p(Y=y\mid A,C)
by maximizing the observed-data log-likelihood

\ell(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left[\sum_{y=0}^{1}\Pr_{\beta}(Y_{i}=y\mid A_{i},C_{i})\widehat{p}(W_{i}\mid y)\,\widehat{p}(Z_{i}\mid y)\,\widehat{p}(V_{i}\mid y)\right].

Thus, we first recover the latent Gaussian measurement model
p(W,Z,V\mid Y)
, which is then used to estimate the hidden outcome regression
p(Y\mid A,C)
through the induced observed-data likelihood.
