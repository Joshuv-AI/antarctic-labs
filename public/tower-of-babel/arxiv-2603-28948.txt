Scaling Limits for Exponential Hedging in Trinomial Models Thanks: Y. Dolinsky is partially supported by the ISF grant 305/25. X. Zhang is partially supported by the NSF grant DMS-2508556.
Yan Dolinsky, Xin Zhang

Abstract
. We study scaled trinomial models converging to the Black–Scholes model, and analyze exponential certainty-equivalent prices for path-dependent European options. As the number of trading dates n n tends to infinity and the risk aversion is scaled as n ​ l nl for a fixed constant l > 0 l>0 , we derive a nontrivial scaling limit. Our analysis is purely probabilistic. Using a duality argument for the certainty equivalent, together with martingale and weak-convergence techniques, we show that the limiting problem takes the form of a volatility control problem with a specific penalty. For European options with Markovian payoffs, we analyze the optimal control problem and show that the corresponding delta-hedging strategy is asymptotically optimal for the primal problem.



1.
Introduction and the Main Result

Understanding hedging and pricing in incomplete markets is a central problem in mathematical finance. In complete models, such as the Cox–Ross–Rubinstein binomial model
[
11
]
, a European contingent claim can be perfectly replicated, and the absence of residual risk leads to a unique price. In incomplete models, by contrast, perfect replication typically fails, and one must replace replication-based pricing by criteria that account for risk preferences. Exponential utility and the associated certainty equivalent provide a natural and tractable framework for this purpose
[
9
]
.

This paper studies exponential hedging in trinomial models that converge to the Black–Scholes model. The trinomial setting is a natural extension of the binomial model: in addition to upward and downward movements, the stock may also remain unchanged. This additional flexibility destroys market completeness, but it also makes the model rich enough to capture nontrivial hedging effects in the large-
n
limit. Our main result identifies this limit and shows that, after a suitable scaling of risk aversion, the discrete-time exponential hedging problem converges to a continuous-time volatility control problem with an explicit entropy-type penalty. In this way, the paper provides a connection between discrete-time utility-based hedging and continuous-time stochastic control.

The problem is also motivated by recent developments in divergence-based approaches to model uncertainty and calibration. In particular, a recent paper
[
8
]
introduced the reciprocal specific relative entropy between continuous martingale measures through trinomial approximations. That divergence has potential applications to model calibration
[
1
]
, and is closely related to specific relative entropy
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
and to specific Wasserstein-type divergences
[
7
]
. Our scaling limit therefore gives a concrete financial interpretation of these quantities through exponential hedging in discrete incomplete markets.

More precisely, fix a constant
\bar{\sigma}>0
and
p\in(0,1)
, and consider the probability space

\Omega:=\{-1,0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}},\qquad\mathbb{P}:=\{p/2,\,1-p,\,p/2\}^{\mathbb{N}}.

Let
\{\xi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be the canonical process and let
\mathcal{F}_{k}=\sigma\{\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k}\}
, with
\mathcal{F}_{0}
trivial. For each
n
, we consider the
n
-step trinomial model on the time grid

0,\frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n},\dots,1,

with zero interest rate and stock price process

S_{k}^{n}=S_{0}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1+\frac{\bar{\sigma}}{\sqrt{n}}\xi_{j}\right),\qquad k=0,1,\dots,n.

We assume
n
is sufficiently large so that
S_{k}^{n}>0
for all
k
.

A trading strategy
\gamma=(\gamma_{0},\dots,\gamma_{n-1})
is an adapted process, where
\gamma_{k}
denotes the number of shares held at time
\frac{k}{n}
. Its terminal gain is

V^{\gamma}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\gamma_{i}\left(S_{i+1}^{n}-S_{i}^{n}\right).

Let
\Gamma_{n}
denote the set of all such strategies.

Denote by
C[0,1]
the space of continuous functions on
[0,1]
,
equipped with the uniform norm.
Denote by
C_{++}[0,1]\subset C[0,1]
the space of all strictly positive continuous functions.
Consider a continuous map
F:C_{++}[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
and assume there exist constants
C,r>0
such that

|F(y)|\leq C\left(1+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left(y_{t}^{r}+\frac{1}{y^{r}_{t}}\right)\right),\qquad\forall\,y\in C_{++}[0,1].

(1.1)

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
H^{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to C[0,1]
be the linear interpolation operator

\displaystyle H^{n}(z)_{t}:=\bigl([nt]+1-nt\bigr)z_{[nt]}+\bigl(nt-[nt]\bigr)z_{[nt]+1},\qquad t\in[0,1],

(1.2)

where
z=(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
[t]
denotes the integer part of
t
.

We consider a path-dependent European payoff of the form

F_{n}:=F(H_{n}(S^{n})),

where
S^{n}=(S_{0}^{n},\dots,S_{n}^{n})
. For a fixed
\ell>0
, we impose exponential utility with absolute risk aversion
n\ell
, and define the corresponding
certainty equivalent
by (see Chapter 2 in
[
9
]
)

\mathcal{C}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n\ell}\log\left(\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\big[\exp(n\ell(F_{n}-V^{\gamma}))\big]\right).

Economically,
\mathcal{C}_{n}
is the amount of certain wealth that makes the investor indifferent between selling and hedging the claim, and not entering the position. Equivalently,
\mathcal{C}_{n}
is the unique solution of

\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exp\left(nl\left(F_{n}-V^{\gamma}-\mathcal{C}_{n}\right)\right)\right]=1.

Our main theorem shows that
\mathcal{C}_{n}
admits a nontrivial limit. Consider a complete probability space

(\Omega_{W},\mathcal{F}_{W},\mathbb{P}_{W})
supporting
a standard Brownian motion
W
with right-continuous filtration

\mathcal{F}^{W}_{t}
,
t\in[0,1]
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{\bar{\sigma}}
denote the set of all bounded, progressively measurable, nonnegative processes
(\alpha_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfying
\alpha_{t}\leq\bar{\sigma}
for all
t\geq 0
. Define

S_{t}^{\alpha}=S_{0}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\alpha_{u}\,dW_{u}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\alpha_{u}^{2}\,du\right),\qquad t\in[0,1].

Theorem 1.1
.

We have that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{C}_{n}=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{\bar{\sigma}}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\mathbb{W}}}\left[F(S^{\alpha})-\frac{1}{\ell}\int_{0}^{1}G_{p}\!\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\right)\,dt\right],

(1.3)

where

G_{p}(x):=x\log\left(\frac{x}{p}\right)+(1-x)\log\left(\frac{1-x}{1-p}\right),\qquad 0\leq x\leq 1.

This limit identifies the effective continuous-time object behind exponential hedging in scaled trinomial models. The control variable is the volatility process
\alpha
, while the running penalty
G_{p}(x)
is given by the relative entropy between the Bernoulli laws
\mathrm{Ber}(x)
and
\mathrm{Ber}(p)
. Thus the limit has a clear interpretation: the investor optimizes over effective volatilities, but deviations from the reference trinomial structure are penalized entropically.

The theorem also clarifies how the limiting problem interpolates between two familiar regimes. When
\ell\to\infty
, the penalty disappears and the limit reduces to

\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{\bar{\sigma}}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\mathbb{W}}}[F(S^{\alpha})],

which can be viewed as a
G
-expectation
[
24
]
over volatility scenarios in
[0,\bar{\sigma}]
. This corresponds to the case where the investor aims to super-replicate the payoff
F(S)
under any market model whose volatility lies within this uncertainty interval (see
[
25
]
).

In the case where
\ell\to 0
, it is straightforward to check that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{C}_{n}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{W}}\left[F\left(S^{\sigma}\right)\right]

where
\sigma\equiv\sqrt{p}\bar{\sigma}
.
We recover the Black–Scholes price with volatility
\sqrt{p}\bar{\sigma}
.

For fixed
\ell
, the certainty equivalent therefore lies between classical Black–Scholes pricing
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{W}}\left[F\left(S^{\sigma}\right)\right]
and super-replication
\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{\bar{\sigma}}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{W}}\left[F\left(S^{\alpha}\right)\right]
. This makes the limit particularly appealing: it quantifies how utility-based hedging in an incomplete discrete model interpolates between risk-neutral valuation and worst-case pricing. Moreover, if the payoff function
F
is convex, then the above
G
-expectation coincides with the Black–Scholes price with volatility
\bar{\sigma}
(see the argument in Section 6 of
[
21
]
).

Our limit theorem can be viewed as a natural extension of the limit theorems in
[
10
,
16
]
,
where similar scaling limits were considered for the discretized Bachelier model.
While
[
10
]
studied European contingent claims with path-dependent payoffs,
the proof of the upper bound was incomplete. In particular, to ensure compactness of the pre-limit,
the authors introduced an artificial term in the discrete-time hedging strategy due to technical difficulties.
This issue was later resolved in
[
16
]
by applying tools from stochastic control,
but only in the case of Markovian payoffs.
The present trinomial structure is more amenable to a probabilistic approach,
as martingale measures in this setting are tight.

Finally, for European options with Markovian payoffs, we analyze the limiting control problem further. We derive the associated HJB equation and show that the corresponding delta-hedging strategy is asymptotically optimal for the original discrete-time problem; see Theorem
4.6
. Thus the paper not only identifies the scaling limit, but also extracts an effective hedging rule from it.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we establish sensitivity properties of the certainty equivalent in trinomial models. In Section
3
, we prove Theorem
1.1
. Section
4
provides, for Markovian payoffs, an HJB formulation for the right-hand side of (
1.3
) and establishes an asymptotically optimal hedging strategy for this case.

2.
Properties of the Certainty Equivalent

This section provides a sensitivity analysis of the certainty equivalent. In particular, for convex payoffs it establishes monotonicity with respect to the model parameters
p
and
\bar{\sigma}
, which helps explain how the pricing/hedging problem varies across trinomial specifications. We begin with the following technical lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function.
Fix
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
x>0
, and define
\Upsilon:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\Upsilon(\hat{p},\hat{\sigma}):=\log\left(\frac{\hat{p}}{2}\left(e^{h(x(1+\hat{\sigma}))-a\hat{\sigma}x}+e^{h(x(1-\hat{\sigma}))+a\hat{\sigma}x}\right)+(1-\hat{p})e^{h(x)}\right).

Then
\Upsilon
is non-decreasing with respect to both variables.

Proof.

By applying standard density arguments, we may assume that
h
is differentiable.
Since
h
is convex, the function
\Delta\mapsto e^{h(x+\Delta)-a\Delta}
,
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}
, is also convex. Hence,

\frac{\partial\Upsilon}{\partial\hat{p}}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{h(x(1+\hat{\sigma}))-a\hat{\sigma}x}+e^{h(x(1-\hat{\sigma}))+a\hat{\sigma}x}\right)-e^{h(x)}}{\frac{\hat{p}}{2}\left(e^{h(x(1+\hat{\sigma}))-a\hat{\sigma}x}+e^{h(x(1-\hat{\sigma}))+a\hat{\sigma}x}\right)+(1-\hat{p})e^{h(x)}}\geq 0

and

\frac{\partial\Upsilon}{\partial\hat{\sigma}}=\frac{\frac{\hat{p}x}{2}\left(\left(e^{h(x+\Delta)-a\Delta}\right)^{\prime}_{\Delta=\hat{\sigma}x}-\left(e^{h(x+\Delta)-a\Delta}\right)^{\prime}_{\Delta=-\hat{\sigma}x}\right)}{\frac{\hat{p}}{2}\left(e^{h(x(1+\hat{\sigma}))-a\hat{\sigma}x}+e^{h(x(1-\hat{\sigma}))+a\hat{\sigma}x}\right)+(1-\hat{p})e^{h(x)}}\geq 0.

This completes the proof.
∎

Next, fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\hat{F}:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a given function. Consider the
n
-step trinomial model with payoff (of a European contingent claim) given by

\hat{F}_{n}:=\hat{F}(S^{n}_{0},S^{n}_{1},\ldots,S^{n}_{n}).

Without loss of generality, we assume exponential utility with risk aversion normalized to one. The corresponding certainty equivalent is given by

\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{n}:=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}}\log\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exp\left(\hat{F}_{n}-V^{\gamma}\right)\right]\right).

Define the functions
U^{p,\bar{\sigma}}_{k}
,
k=0,1,...,n
by backward recursion:

\displaystyle U^{p,\bar{\sigma}}_{n}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}):=\hat{F}_{n}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}),

\displaystyle U^{p,\bar{\sigma}}_{k-1}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{k-1})

(2.1)

\displaystyle=\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}}\log\Bigg(\frac{p}{2}\Big[\exp\Big(U^{p,\bar{\sigma}}_{k}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{k-1},z_{k-1}(1+\bar{\sigma}/\sqrt{n}))-a\bar{\sigma}z_{k-1}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\exp\Big(U^{p,\bar{\sigma}}_{k}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{k-1},z_{k-1}(1-\bar{\sigma}/\sqrt{n}))+a\bar{\sigma}z_{k-1}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad+(1-p)\,\exp\Big(U^{p,\bar{\sigma}}_{k}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{k-1},z_{k-1})\Big)\Bigg).

From standard dynamic programming in discrete time and space we get

\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{n}=U^{p,\bar{\sigma}}_{0}(S_{0}).

(2.2)

For each
k
, the function
U^{p,\bar{\sigma}}_{k-1}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{k-1})
is the certainty equivalent
at a given node for a one-period trinomial model with random payoff

U_{k}^{p,\bar{\sigma}}\big(z_{0},\ldots,z_{k-1},z_{k-1}(1+\bar{\sigma}\xi_{k}/\sqrt{n})\big).

Clearly, a probability measure is a martingale measure for our trinomial model
if and only if it assigns equal probabilities to the up and down moves at each node.
Thus, from the dual representation of the certainty equivalent in a one-period model
(see Chapter 3 in
[
18
]
), we obtain

\displaystyle U^{p,\bar{\sigma}}_{k-1}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{k-1})=\sup_{0<q<1}\Bigg(\frac{q}{2}\Big[U^{p,\bar{\sigma}}_{k}\big(z_{0},\ldots,z_{k-1},z_{k-1}(1+\tfrac{\bar{\sigma}}{\sqrt{n}})\big)

\displaystyle+U^{p,\bar{\sigma}}_{k}\big(z_{0},\ldots,z_{k-1},z_{k-1}(1-\tfrac{\bar{\sigma}}{\sqrt{n}})\big)\Big]+(1-q)\,U^{p,\bar{\sigma}}_{k}\big(z_{0},\ldots,z_{k-1},z_{k-1}\big)

\displaystyle-q\log\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)-(1-q)\log\left(\frac{1-q}{1-p}\right)\Bigg).

(2.3)

We arrive at the following corollary.

Corollary 2.2
.

Assume that
\hat{F}_{n}
is convex. Then, for all
k
, the function
U^{p,\bar{\sigma}}_{k}
is convex.

Proof.

The statement follows by backward induction using the representation (
2
).
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Assume that
\hat{F}_{n}
is convex. Let
1>p_{2}\geq p_{1}>0
and
\bar{\sigma}_{2}\geq\bar{\sigma}_{1}>0

U^{p_{2},\bar{\sigma}_{2}}_{k}\geq U^{p_{1},\bar{\sigma}_{1}}_{k}

for all
k=0,1,\dots,n
.

Proof.

The proof proceeds by backward induction. For
k=n
, the statement is immediate. Assume that the statement holds for some
k
, and prove it for

From the induction assumption and the dynamic programming (

)
we obtain

\displaystyle U^{p_{2},\bar{\sigma}_{2}}_{k-1}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{k-1})

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}}\log\Bigg(\frac{p_{2}}{2}\Big[\exp\Big(U^{p_{1},\bar{\sigma}_{1}}_{k}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{k-1},z_{k-1}(1+\bar{\sigma}_{2}/\sqrt{n}))-a\bar{\sigma}_{2}z_{k-1}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\exp\Big(U^{p_{1},\bar{\sigma}_{1}}_{k}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{k-1},z_{k-1}(1-\bar{\sigma}_{2}/\sqrt{n}))+a\bar{\sigma}_{2}z_{k-1}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad+(1-p_{2})\,\exp\Big(U^{p_{1},\bar{\sigma}_{1}}_{k}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{k-1},z_{k-1})\Big)\Bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}}\log\Bigg(\frac{p_{1}}{2}\Big[\exp\Big(U^{p_{1},\bar{\sigma}_{1}}_{k}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{k-1},z_{k-1}(1+\bar{\sigma}_{1}/\sqrt{n}))-a\bar{\sigma}_{1}z_{k-1}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\exp\Big(U^{p_{1},\bar{\sigma}_{1}}_{k}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{k-1},z_{k-1}(1-\bar{\sigma}_{1}/\sqrt{n}))+a\bar{\sigma}_{1}z_{k-1}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad+(1-p_{1})\,\exp\Big(U^{p_{1},\bar{\sigma}_{1}}_{k}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{k-1},z_{k-1})\Big)\Bigg)

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
2.1

U^{p_{1},\bar{\sigma}_{1}}_{k}
.
∎

We conclude this section with the following corollary.

Corollary 2.4

Assume that
\hat{F}_{n}
is convex, and fix

The result follows by combining (

is a martingale, together with the uniform integrability of marginal distributions, the weak limit is a non-negative martingale.

Let us prove that

is strictly positive. Define a function

. It can be shown that

is continuous under the Skorokhod topology. Hence, from the Fatou lemma and (

) it follows that

is strictly positive.
Therefore

(L^{n}(S^{n}),L^{n}(1/S^{n});\mathbb{Q}_{n})\Rightarrow(M,1/M;\mathbb{P}^{M}).

Take

,
t\in[0,1]
, and take

\displaystyle N^{n}_{k}:=\int_{0}^{k/n}\frac{dL^{n}(S^{n})_{t}}{L^{n}(S^{n})_{t}}=\frac{\bar{\sigma}}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\xi_{i},\quad k=1,\dotso,n.

Invoking
[

, we obtain the weak convergence

\left(L^{n}(S^{n}),L^{n}(N^{n});\,\mathbb{Q}_{n}\right)\Rightarrow\left(M,N;\mathbb{P}^{M}\right).

Clearly,
\{N^{n}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
is a
\mathbb{Q}_{n}
-martingale with uniformly bounded jumps. Define its quadratic variation process

\Lambda^{n}_{k}=\frac{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\xi^{2}_{i}
,
k=0,1,...,n
. Hence, from Theorem 4.3 in
[

]
and the equalities

\displaystyle\Lambda^{n}_{k}=|N^{n}_{k}|^{2}-2\sum_{i=1}^{k}N^{n}_{i-1}(N^{n}_{i}-N^{n}_{i-1}),\,k=1,...,n,

\displaystyle\langle\log M\rangle_{t}=\langle N\rangle_{t}=N^{2}_{t}-2\int_{0}^{t}N_{u}dN_{u},\ t\in[0,1]

we obtain

\left(L^{n}(S^{n}),L^{n}(\Lambda^{n});\mathbb{Q}_{n}\right)\Rightarrow(M,\langle\log M\rangle;\mathbb{P}^{M}).

Thus, in order to complete the proof it remains to show that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\max_{0\leq k\leq n}|\Lambda^{n}_{k}-\Psi^{n,\mathbb{Q}_{n}}_{k}|=0\ \mbox{in}\ \mbox{probability}.

(3.4)

To this end we observe that for any

the process
\Lambda^{n}_{k}-\Psi^{n,\mathbb{Q}_{n}}_{k}
,
0\leq k\leq n
is a martingale and the corresponding quadratic variation satisfies

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\Lambda^{n}_{k}-\Psi^{n,\mathbb{Q}_{n}}_{k}-(\Lambda^{n}_{k-1}-\Psi^{n,\mathbb{Q}_{n}}_{k-1})\right)^{2}\leq 4\bar{\sigma}^{2}/n.

This together with the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality gives (
3.4
).
∎

Next, let
(W_{t})_{[0,1]}
be the canonical process on

, and let
\mathcal{A}^{0}_{\bar{\sigma}}\subset\mathcal{A}_{\bar{\sigma}}

(3.5)

where

is a finite deterministic partition of

,
\phi_{0}>0
is a constant and for each

,

, (for some

) is continuous. Without loss of generality

.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{0}_{\bar{\sigma}}
. There exists a sequence of probability measures

\mathbb{Q}_{n}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}
,

(L^{n}(S^{n}),L^{n}(\Phi^{n,\mathbb{Q}_{n}});\mathbb{Q}_{n})\Rightarrow(\{S^{\alpha}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{1},\{\alpha^{2}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{1};\mathbb{P}_{W})\ \mbox{on}\ D^{2}[0,1]

(3.6)

Proof.

Let

.
For

, let

.
Define

,
\Phi^{n}_{0}:=\phi_{0}
and by recursion for

\displaystyle\Phi^{n}_{k}:=\phi^{2}_{j(k)-1}\left(W^{n}_{[nt_{0}]},\dots,W^{n}_{[nt_{j(k)-1}]}\right),

\displaystyle W^{n}_{k}:=W^{n}_{k-1}+\frac{\bar{\sigma}\xi_{k}}{\sqrt{n\Phi^{n}_{k}}}.

Let us notice that there exists a unique probability measure

for all

.

Next, observe that

- martingale and for any

Thus, by the Martingale Central Limit Theorem (Theorem 7.4.1 in

From the continuity of the functions

we obtain the joint convergence

\big(\{W^{n}_{[nt]}\}_{t=0}^{1},\{\Phi^{n,\mathbb{Q}_{n}}_{[nt]}\}_{t=0}^{1};\mathbb{Q}_{n}\big)\ \Rightarrow\ \big(\{W_{t}\}_{t=0}^{1},\{\alpha^{2}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{1};\mathbb{P}_{W}\big)\ \mbox{on}\ D^{2}[0,1].

(3.7)

By combining Theorems 4.3-4.4 in
[

]

) and the relations

∎

Next, we apply the dual representation for the certainty equivalent in discrete time (see Chapter 11 in

\mathcal{C}_{n}:=\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[F_{n}-\frac{1}{nl}\log\left(\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}\right)\right].

(3.8)

Fix

.
Observe that for any

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{n}(\xi_{k}=1|\mathcal{F}_{k-1})=\mathbb{Q}_{n}(\xi_{k}=-1|\mathcal{F}_{k-1})=\frac{\Phi^{n,\mathbb{Q}_{n}}_{k}}{2\bar{\sigma}^{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{n}}\left[\log\left(\frac{d\mathbb{Q}_{n}}{d\mathbb{P}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{n}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\mathbb{Q}_{n}(\xi_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1})}{\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1})}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{n}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{n}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\mathbb{Q}_{n}(\xi_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1})}{\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1})}\right)\left|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right.\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{n}}\left[\frac{\Phi^{n,\mathbb{Q}_{n}}_{k}}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\log\left(\frac{\Phi^{n,\mathbb{Q}_{n}}_{k}}{p\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\right)+\left(1-\frac{\Phi^{n,\mathbb{Q}_{n}}_{k}}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\right)\log\left(\frac{1-\frac{\Phi^{n,\mathbb{Q}_{n}}_{k}}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}}{1-p}\right)\right].

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{n}}\left[\log\left(\frac{d\mathbb{Q}_{n}}{d\mathbb{P}}\right)\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{n}}\left[G_{p}\left(\frac{\Phi^{n,\mathbb{Q}_{n}}_{k}}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\right)\right].

(3.9)

Now we have all the ingredients to prove Theorem

Proof.

Lower bound:

First, we show that

\lim\inf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{C}_{n}\geq\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{\bar{\sigma}}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{W}}\left[F\left(S^{\alpha}\right)-\frac{1}{l}\int_{0}^{1}G_{p}\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\right)\,dt\right].

By applying standard density arguments as in Lemma 7.3 in
[

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{\bar{\sigma}}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{W}}\left[F\left(S^{\alpha}\right)-\frac{1}{l}\int_{0}^{1}G_{p}\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\right)\,dt\right]

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{0}_{\bar{\sigma}}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{W}}\left[F\left(S^{\alpha}\right)-\frac{1}{l}\int_{0}^{1}G_{p}\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\right)\,dt\right].

Thus, in order to establish the lower bound we need to prove that for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{0}_{\bar{\sigma}}

\lim\inf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{C}_{n}\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{W}}\left[F\left(S^{\alpha}\right)-\frac{1}{l}\int_{0}^{1}G_{p}\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\right)\,dt\right].

(3.10)

Indeed, let
\alpha
be as in (
3.5
). From Lemma

there exists a sequence

,

such that (

(3.11)

Next, since the function

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{n}}\left[\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\frac{d\mathbb{Q}_{n}}{d\mathbb{P}}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{W}}\left[\int_{0}^{1}G_{p}\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}_{t}}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\right)dt.\right]

Upper bound:

By passing to a subsequence we assume without loss of generality

In view of (

) there exists

\mathbb{Q}_{n}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}

(3.12)

From the Skorohod representation theorem (Theorem 3 of
[

]
), Lemma
3.2
, and passing to a subsequence
it follows that there exists a probability space

Again, Lemma

Next, (observe that

)
by Lemma A1.1 in

\{\eta^{n}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{1}\in\mathrm{conv}\left(\{\Phi^{n,\mathbb{Q}_{n}}_{[nt]}\}_{t=0}^{1},\{\Phi^{n+1,\mathbb{Q}_{n+1}}_{[(n+1)t]}\}_{t=0}^{1},\dots\right),\ n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}\left[F(M)-\frac{1}{l}\int_{0}^{1}G_{p}\left(\frac{\frac{d}{dt}\langle\log M\rangle_{t}}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\right)\,dt\right]

and complete the proof.
∎

4.
Markovian Case

In this section, we assume Markovian payoffs, i.e.

F_{n}=F(S^{n}_{n})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, for some
F\in C(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathbb{R})
.
As in (
2
), iteratively, we can define for
i=n,n-1,\dotso,0
,

\displaystyle U^{n}_{n}(x)=

\displaystyle F(x),

\displaystyle nlU^{n}_{i}(x)=

\displaystyle\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}}\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exp\left(nlU^{n}_{i+1}(S_{i+1}^{n})-nla(S_{i+1}^{n}-S_{i}^{n})\right)\,|\,S_{i}^{n}=x\right].

Assuming that
U^{n}
converges to a function
v(t,x)
, then from the equality above we deduce that for large enough
n

\displaystyle 1=\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\exp\left(lv_{t}(t,x)+nlv_{x}(t,x)(S_{i+1}^{n}-x)\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\ \ +nlv_{xx}(t,x)(S_{i+1}^{n}-x)^{2}/2-nla(S_{i+1}^{n}-x)\right)\big|\,S_{i}^{n}=x\right].

Heuristically we set
a=v_{x}(t,x)
, and get the PDE

\begin{cases}v_{t}(t,x)+K\bigl(x^{2}v_{xx}(t,x)\bigr)=0,&(t,x)\in[0,1)\times(0,\infty),\\[3.00003pt]
v(1,x)=F(x),&x>0,\end{cases}

(4.1)

where

\Lambda:=\bar{\sigma}^{2}>0,\qquad K(w):=\frac{1}{l}\log\left((1-p)+p\exp\left(\frac{l}{2}\Lambda w\right)\right),\qquad w\in\mathbb{R}.

Equation (
4.1
) is exactly the HJB corresponding to the right hand side of (
1.3
).

In the calculation above, the choice of
a=v_{x}(t,x)
gives a trading strategy in the primal problem. In Theorem
4.6
, we will prove that such a strategy asymptotically optimal.

As
\Lambda>0
, the function
K
is smooth, strictly increasing, and strictly convex on
\mathbb{R}
, with

\displaystyle K^{\prime}(w)\in(0,\Lambda/2),\quad K^{\prime\prime}(w)\in(0,l\Lambda^{2}/16),\quad w\in\mathbb{R}.

(4.2)

Therefore, (
4.1
) is locally uniformly elliptic and convex.

We transform (
4.1
) to forward parabolic equations. Setting
u(t,x):=v(1-t,x)
,

\begin{cases}u_{t}(t,x)=K\bigl(x^{2}u_{xx}(t,x)\bigr),&(t,x)\in(0,1]\times(0,\infty),\\[3.00003pt]
u(0,x)=F(x),&x\in(0,\infty).\end{cases}

(4.3)

Take
f(y):=F(e^{y})
,
y\in\mathbb{R}
. By a log-space transformation,

\displaystyle\bar{u}(t,y):=u(t,e^{y}),\quad(t,y)\in[0,1]\times\mathbb{R},

satisfies the forward Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}\bar{u}_{t}(t,y)=K\bigl(\bar{u}_{yy}(t,y)-\bar{u}_{y}(t,y)\bigr),&(t,y)\in(0,1]\times\mathbb{R},\\[3.00003pt]
\bar{u}(0,y)=f(y),&y\in\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

(4.4)

where we use equalities

\bar{u}_{y}(t,y)=x\,u_{x}(t,e^{y}),\qquad\bar{u}_{yy}(t,y)-\bar{u}_{y}(t,y)=x^{2}u_{xx}(t,e^{y}).

Clearly, (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) are equivalent. Throughout the remainder of this paper, we shall pass from one formulation to the other whenever convenient for the analysis. Under some assumptions on
f(\cdot)=F(e^{\cdot})
, we will prove that there exists a unique classical solution to (
4.3
).

Assumption 4.1
.

Take
f(y):=F(e^{y})
,
y\in\mathbb{R}
.

(i)

f
is continuous with at most linear growth.

(ii)

f
is second-order differentiable and locally
\alpha
-Hölder continuous for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, i.e.
f\in C^{2,\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
.

(iii)

With
w_{0}:=f^{\prime\prime}-f^{\prime}
, it holds that

\displaystyle-\infty<m_{0}:=

\displaystyle\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}}w_{0}(y)\leq\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}w_{0}(y)=:M_{0}<\infty.

(iv)

w_{0}\in C^{2+\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Under Assumption
4.1
(i), a comparison principle holds for (
4.4
) among the functions with at most linear growth. In particular, there exists a unique viscosity solution with at most linear growth to (
4.4
).

Proof.

For the existence, a viscosity solution to (
4.4
) is provided by the stochastic control problem corresponding to the right hand side of (
1.3
).

As
r\mapsto K(r)
is strictly increasing, equation (
4.4
) is parabolic. Noting that
K
is Lipschitz, comparison principle holds for functions with at most linear growth by a standard doubling variable argument.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Under Assumption
4.1
(i) (ii), there exists a unique classical solution to (
4.3
). Moreover, we have the regularity

u\in C^{1+\alpha/2,\,2+\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,1]\times(0,\infty))\cap C^{\infty}((0,1]\times(0,\infty)).

Proof.

Step 1: Hölder regularity.

According to
[
19
, Theorem 2.3]
, for any fixed
x_{0}>0
, the solution to

\displaystyle\phi_{t}(t,x)=K(x_{0}^{2}\phi_{xx}(t,x))

belongs to
C^{1+\beta/2,\,2+\beta}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,1]\times(0,\infty))
for a universal small

.
Applying
[
26
, Theorem 1.1]
, the solution to (
4.3
) belongs to
C^{1+\alpha/2,\,2+\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,1]\times(0,\infty))
with some positive
\alpha<\beta
.

Note that
f\in C^{2,\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
is equivalent to
F\in C^{2,\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,\infty)
. Applying
[
26
, Theorem 2.14]
, we get the regularity at

, and conclude that
u\in C^{1+\alpha/2,\,2+\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,1]\times(0,\infty))
.

Step 2: Schauder bootstrapping.
Recall that (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) are equivalent. Differentiate (
4.4
)
w.r.t time, and setting

, we get that

\displaystyle\phi_{t}(t,y)=K^{\prime}(\bar{u}_{yy}(t,y)-\bar{u}_{y}(t,y))\left(\phi_{yy}(t,y)-\phi_{y}(t,y)\right).

Restricted to compact domains
[0,1]\times[-R,R]
,

is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous, and

with some

is also Hölder continuous on the parabolic boundary of

, using the regularity results for linear parabolic equations, e.g.
[

,

\displaystyle\phi\in C^{1+\alpha/2,\,2+\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,1]\times\mathbb{R}).

Repeating this step, we get that
\bar{u}\in C^{\infty}((0,1]\times\mathbb{R})
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Under Assumption
4.1
(i)-(iii), the solution

to (

\displaystyle\bar{u}_{t}\in[K(m_{0}),K(M_{0})],\quad\bar{u}_{yy}-\bar{u}_{y}\in[m_{0},M_{0}],\quad(t,y)\in[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}.

For any continuous functions

with at most linear growth, define

to be the solution to (

) with initial condition

. It can be easily verified that

(i)

\mathcal{S}_{t}(\mathcal{S}_{h}(g))=\mathcal{S}_{t+h}(g)
.

(ii)

\mathcal{S}_{t}(g+c)=\mathcal{S}_{t}(g)+c
, where

is a constant.

(iii)

\mathcal{S}_{t}(g)\leq\mathcal{S}_{t}(l)
if

and with at most linear growth.

Let us define

\displaystyle\phi(t,y):=f(y)+tK(m_{0}),

\displaystyle\psi(t,y):=f(y)+tK(M_{0}).

As

is increasing,
\phi
and

Therefore by comparison principle in Lemma

\phi(t,y)\leq\bar{u}(t,y)\leq\psi(t,y).

Applying

,

, to the inequality above, we obtain

\displaystyle\bar{u}(t,y)+hK(m_{0})\leq\bar{u}(t+h,y)\leq\bar{u}(t,y)+hK(M_{0}),

and hence

\displaystyle K(m_{0})\leq\frac{\bar{u}(t+h,y)-\bar{u}(t,y)}{h}\leq K(M_{0}).

Letting
h\downarrow 0
, we conclude the result.

∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Under Assumption
4.1
,
\bar{u}_{t}
and
\bar{u}_{yy}-\bar{u}_{y}
are Lipschitz, where
\bar{u}
is the solution to (
4.4
).

Proof.

Let us take a smooth modification
\tilde{K}
of
K
such that

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(y)=K(y),\qquad\ \ \ \text{ for }y\in[m_{0},M_{0}],

\displaystyle 0<h<\tilde{K}^{\prime}(y)<H\ \,\,\,\text{ for }y\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\tilde{K}^{\prime\prime}\geq 0,\qquad\qquad\quad\ \ \text{ for }y\in\mathbb{R}.

According to Lemma
4.4
, the unique solution to the forward PDE

\begin{cases}\bar{u}_{t}(t,y)=\tilde{K}\bigl(\bar{u}_{yy}(t,y)-\bar{u}_{y}(t,y)\bigr),&(t,y)\in(0,1]\times\mathbb{R},\\[3.00003pt]
\bar{u}(0,y)=f(y),&y\in\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

is exactly given by (
4.4
). Setting
\phi=\bar{u}_{t}
, it satisfies the quasi linear PDE

\begin{cases}\phi_{t}(t,y)=\tilde{K}^{\prime}(\bar{u}_{yy}(t,y)-\bar{u}_{y}(t,y))\left(\phi_{yy}(t,y)-\phi_{y}(t,y)\right)&(t,y)\in(0,1]\times\mathbb{R}\\[3.00003pt]
\phi(0,y)=\tilde{K}(f^{\prime\prime}(y)-f^{\prime}(y))&y\in\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

which is uniform elliptic. Invoking
[
20
, Section 5.5, Theorem 2]
, we get that its solution
\phi=\bar{u}_{t}
is Lipschitz. Restricted to
[m_{0},M_{0}]
, function
\tilde{K}
is bi-Lipschitz, and
\bar{u}_{yy}-\bar{u}_{y}=K^{-1}(\bar{u}_{t})
is also Lipschitz.
∎

With the above results at hand, we show that the delta hedging strategy

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}^{n}:=\left(v_{x}\left(\frac{i+1}{n},S^{n}_{i}\right)\right)_{0\leq i\leq n-1}

(4.5)

is asymptotically optimal for the exponential hedging in (
1.3
). Define its corresponding value

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{n}:=\frac{1}{nl}\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exp\left(nl\left(F(S^{n}_{n})-\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{n}_{i}(S^{n}_{i+1}-S^{n}_{i})\right)\right)\right].

(4.6)

Theorem 4.6
.

Under Assumption
4.1
, it holds that,

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{C}_{n}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{n},

that is, the delta strategies defined in (
4.5
) is asymptotically optimal.

Proof.

By duality (
1.3
) and the HJB (
4.1
), it suffices to prove

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{n}\leq v(0,1).

Let us define for
i=0,\dotso,n-1

\displaystyle t^{n}_{i}:=\frac{i}{n},\qquad\Delta S_{i}^{n}:=S_{i+1}^{n}-S_{i}^{n},\qquad\tilde{\gamma}_{i}^{n}:=v_{x}(t^{n}_{i+1},S_{i}^{n}),

\displaystyle I_{i}^{n}:=v(t^{n}_{i+1},S_{i+1}^{n})-v(t^{n}_{i},S_{i}^{n})-v_{x}(t^{n}_{i+1},S_{i}^{n})\Delta S_{i}^{n},

Using
v(1,x)=F(x)
and the equality

\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}I_{i}^{n}=v(1,S_{n}^{n})-v(0,S_{0}^{n})-\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\tilde{\gamma}_{i}^{n}(S_{i+1}^{n}-S_{i}^{n}),

it suffices to prove

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{nl}\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exp\left(nl\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}I_{i}^{n}\right)\right]\leq 0.

By the fundamental theorem of calculus in time,

\displaystyle v(t^{n}_{i+1},S_{i+1}^{n})-v(t^{n}_{i},S_{i}^{n})-v_{x}(t^{n}_{i+1},S_{i}^{n})\Delta S_{i}^{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}v_{t}(s,S_{i}^{n})\,ds+\int_{S_{i}^{n}}^{S_{i+1}^{n}}(S_{i+1}^{n}-y)\,v_{xx}(t^{n}_{i+1},y)\,dy.

Denote by
A^{n}_{i}
and
B^{n}_{i}
the first and the second term on the right hand side of the equation above.

Since
v_{t}
is Lipschitz in time by Lemma
4.5
, there exists
L>0
such that

\displaystyle\left|nA_{i}^{n}-v_{t}(t^{n}_{i},S_{i}^{n})\right|

\displaystyle\leq n\int_{t^{n}_{i}}^{t^{n}_{i+1}}|v_{t}(s,S_{i}^{n})-v_{t}(t^{n}_{i},S_{i}^{n})|\,ds

\displaystyle\leq n\int_{t^{n}_{i}}^{t^{n}_{i+1}}L(s-t^{n}_{i})\,ds=\frac{L}{2n}.

Next, using the identity

\int_{x}^{x+\delta}(x+\delta-y)f(y)\,dy=\delta^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\theta)f(x+\theta\delta)\,d\theta,

valid for any
x,\delta\in\mathbb{R}
, we obtain

\displaystyle nB_{i}^{n}

\displaystyle=n(\Delta S_{i}^{n})^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\theta)\,v_{xx}(t^{n}_{i+1},S_{i}^{n}+\theta\Delta S_{i}^{n})\,d\theta

\displaystyle=n(S_{i}^{n})^{2}(h_{i}^{n})^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\theta)\,v_{xx}(t^{n}_{i+1},S_{i}^{n}(1+\theta h_{i}^{n}))\,d\theta,

where
h^{n}_{i}:=\frac{\bar{\sigma}}{\sqrt{n}}\xi_{i+1}
.
Denoting

\Gamma(t,x)=x^{2}v_{xx}(t,x),

it follows that

nB_{i}^{n}=\bar{\sigma}^{2}\xi_{i+1}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\theta)\,\frac{\Gamma(t^{n}_{i+1},S_{i}^{n}(1+\theta h_{i}^{n}))}{(1+\theta h_{i}^{n})^{2}}\,d\theta.

Noting that
\Gamma(t,x)=\bar{u}_{yy}(1-t,\log x)-\bar{u}_{y}(1-t,\log x)
is Lipschitz in
(t,\log x)
by Lemma
4.5
, we have the inequality

\left|\frac{\Gamma(t^{n}_{i+1},S_{i}^{n}(1+\theta h_{i}^{n}))}{(1+\theta h_{i}^{n})^{2}}-\Gamma(t^{n}_{i},S_{i}^{n})\right|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}},

uniformly in
i
and
\theta\in[0,1]
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\left|nB_{i}^{n}-\frac{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\Gamma(t^{n}_{i},S_{i}^{n})\xi_{i+1}^{2}\right|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}.

Combining the bounds for
A_{i}^{n}
and
B_{i}^{n}
, we obtain

nI_{i}^{n}=v_{t}(t^{n}_{i},S_{i}^{n})+\frac{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\Gamma(t^{n}_{i},S_{i}^{n})\xi_{i+1}^{2}+R_{i}^{n},

with

|R_{i}^{n}|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}.
Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exp(nlI_{i}^{n})\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(lv_{t}(t^{n}_{i},S_{i}^{n})+\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\bar{\sigma}^{2}l}{2}\Gamma(t^{n}_{i},S_{i}^{n})\xi_{i+1}^{2}\right)\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(lv_{t}(t^{n}_{i},S_{i}^{n})+lK(\Gamma(t^{n}_{i},S^{n}_{i}))+\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

where we used the condition law of
\xi_{i+1}
under
\mathbb{P}
.

Since
v
solves the HJB equation (
4.1
)

v_{t}(t,x)+K(\Gamma(t,x))=0,

it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exp\left(nl\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}I_{i}^{n}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exp\left(nl\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}I_{i}^{n}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[e^{nlI_{n-1}^{n}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{C/\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exp\left(nl\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}I_{i}^{n}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\cdots\leq e^{Cn/\sqrt{n}}=e^{C\sqrt{n}}.

Therefore we conclude that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{nl}\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exp\left(nl\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}I_{i}^{n}\right)\right]\leq 0.

∎

Example 4.7
.

If
F(x)=\alpha+\beta\log(x)
with some constant
\alpha,\beta
, then we have an explicit solution to (
4.1
),

v(t,x)=\alpha+\beta\log(x)+\frac{1-t}{l}\log\left((1-p)+p\exp\left(-l\bar{\sigma}^{2}\beta/2\right)\right).

The delta-hedging strategy
v_{x}(t,x)=\frac{\beta}{x}
is time-independent.
