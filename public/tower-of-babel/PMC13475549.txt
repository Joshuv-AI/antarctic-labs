The global health workforce crisis toward 2030: A scoping review and systems-based policy framework
Authors: María José Ferreira Díaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo integrate global workforce indicators and narrative evidence in order to develop a systems-based policy framework informing coordinated workforce sustainability strategies toward 2030.Study designScoping review with mixed-methods synthesis.MethodsA structured scoping review was conducted of peer-reviewed literature, global datasets, and institutional policy documents published between 2014 and 2024. A mixed-methods synthesis combined quantitative analysis of workforce indicators with qualitative thematic analysis of structural drivers, governance gaps, and health system impacts.ResultsGlobal projections indicate a shortfall of approximately 11.1 million health workers by 2030. The global nursing shortage was estimated at 5.9 million in 2018, while up to 13 million additional nurses may be required over the following decade when existing shortages, projected retirements, and future demand are considered together. An aging workforce, intensified migration flows, insufficient domestic training capacity, and deteriorating working conditions contribute to persistent shortages. Sustainable solutions require coordinated long-term planning, strengthened retention and wellbeing strategies, ethical migration governance, and expansion of domestic training capacity. The proposed framework supports multi-level policy coordination toward workforce sustainability by 2030.Keywords: Health workforce, Nurse shortage, Migration, Workforce aging, Global health systems, Retention, Health policy, 2030 agenda

Introduction

Current WHO projections indicate a global shortfall of approximately 11.1 million health workers by 2030. [1] Within nursing, the global shortage was estimated at 5.9 million in 2018, with the greatest deficits concentrated in low- and lower-middle-income countries. [2] Workforce scarcity therefore represents a major long-term threat to health-system resilience and progress toward universal health coverage. These pressures extend across nursing, midwifery, and the wider health workforce and require coordinated investment in education, employment, workforce data, and cross-sector workforce planning. [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]

This crisis is driven by the convergence of three major forces. First, workforce ageing is rapidly reducing the supply of experienced professionals at a pace that training systems cannot match; in several high-income regions, up to one-third of nurses may retire within the decade. [8] Second, international migration has intensified, creating global asymmetries in which high-income countries compensate domestic shortages through reliance on internationally educated personnel, exacerbating workforce depletion in low- and middle-income countries. [[9], [10], [11]] Third, deteriorating working conditions, chronic understaffing, burnout and weak retention structures accelerate attrition, particularly among early-career nurses, primary care clinicians and mental health professionals. [[12], [13], [14]]

The consequences extend beyond staffing metrics. Insufficient staffing has been associated with increased mortality, treatment delays, adverse events and weakened continuity of chronic care. [[15], [16], [17]] At system level, persistent instability undermines progress toward universal health coverage (UHC) and weakens preparedness for emergencies and demographic pressures. [18]

Despite abundant reports and datasets, evidence remains fragmented—profession-specific, region-specific or limited to single drivers. A comprehensive synthesis is therefore needed to integrate global workforce indicators with qualitative evidence and structural determinants. The objective of this study is to develop an integrated systems perspective of the global workforce crisis and identify policy-relevant mechanisms shaping workforce sustainability toward 2030.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a scoping review following the methodological principles of Arksey and O'Malley [18] and the PRISMA-ScR extension. [19] This design was chosen because the global health workforce crisis spans multiple disciplines (public health, health policy, economics, nursing, demography), involves heterogeneous data sources, and includes both peer-reviewed studies and international policy documents. The review was complemented by a mixed-methods synthesis, integrating quantitative workforce indicators with qualitative thematic analysis of causal factors and policy responses.

The aim of this analysis was not to replicate or update existing WHO or OECD reports, but to integrate heterogeneous data sources within a unified systems framework to identify reinforcing policy-relevant dynamics.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was performed across major scientific databases and institutional repositories to identify relevant literature published between January 2014 and December 2024. The 10-year timeframe was selected to capture post-2014 evidence, coinciding with major WHO global workforce initiatives and the adoption of the Sustainable Development Goals. All search strategies were executed on the same date to ensure consistency.

Study selection

Two reviewers independently screened all titles and abstracts. Full texts were retrieved for articles meeting inclusion criteria or where eligibility was uncertain. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer. No automation tools were used during the screening or selection process.

A total of 5482 records were identified. After removing 2268 duplicates, 3214 unique records were screened. Of these, 412 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 126 studies and policy reports were included in the final synthesis.

Quality assessment

Given the scoping nature of the review, no formal risk-of-bias tool was applied to exclude studies. However, we conducted a descriptive appraisal of methodological robustness, data transparency, and policy relevance. Institutional reports from WHO, OECD, ICN and the World Bank were automatically classified as high-credibility sources based on established global standards.

Synthesis of evidence

We used a mixed-methods analytical strategy:

Quantitative synthesis.

Workforce indicators (supply, density, shortages, migration rates, retirement projections) were aggregated and compared across regions and professional categories. Where feasible, values were harmonised using WHO/ILO standard definitions.

Qualitative thematic analysis.

Textual data were coded inductively using a three-stage approach:

1.Open coding of textual data describing causal factors and system drivers.

Open coding of textual data describing causal factors and system drivers.

2.Axial coding to generate thematic clusters (e.g., working conditions, retention, migration incentives, demographic transitions).

3.Selective coding to identify cross-cutting determinants shaping workforce sustainability.

The resulting themes informed the development of the Systems-Based Workforce Sustainability Framework presented in the Discussion.

Ethical considerations

This study involved secondary analysis of publicly available data and published literature. Ethical approval was therefore not required, in accordance with international standards for scoping reviews and the use of publicly accessible data.

Synthesis of findings

Overall, findings indicate a structurally interconnected crisis characterized by escalating quantitative shortages, systemic qualitative drivers—including burnout, migration, and governance gaps—and profound health-system impacts affecting patient safety, continuity of care, geographic workforce distribution, and system resilience. This evidence underscores the need for coordinated, multi-level, systems-based responses, as represented in the Systems-Based Workforce Sustainability Framework presented in the Discussion.

Discussion

This scoping review provides a comprehensive synthesis of global evidence on the health workforce crisis, revealing a system-wide pattern of escalating shortages, intensifying migration flows, and an aging workforce. Quantitative indicators show that the global shortfall of health workers is projected to reach approximately 11.1 million by 2030, with the largest deficits concentrated in low- and lower-middle-income countries. [1] At the same time, qualitative findings demonstrate that these shortages are reinforced by structural drivers such as deteriorating working conditions, weak retention strategies, demographic pressures, and governance gaps, which together create a self-reinforcing cycle of workforce instability. [[26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31],41]

Comparison with previous literature

Our findings align strongly with previous global analyses from WHO, ICN, and OECD, all of which emphasize the structural and multi-level nature of the workforce crisis. [[32], [33], [34]] Earlier reports have highlighted the uneven geographic distribution of health workers, the increasing dependence of high-income countries on internationally educated professionals, and the chronic underinvestment in domestic training pipelines. [20,23,25] This review extends those conclusions by integrating both quantitative indicators and qualitative determinants, demonstrating how systemic pressures interact across macro-, meso-, and micro-level domains.

An aging workforce represents an important demographic challenge. OECD and WHO analyses indicate that population aging is increasing demand for health and long-term care while retirement pressures are reducing workforce supply across many high-income settings. [21,22,39] Our synthesis confirms that retirement-driven losses may outpace the capacity of education systems to replenish the workforce, especially in nursing, primary care, mental health, and geriatrics.

Migration patterns identified in this review are also highly consistent with earlier evidence documenting strong push–pull dynamics. [23,24,36] High-income countries continue to recruit aggressively from LMICs, deepening structural imbalances and limiting progress toward universal health coverage in resource-constrained settings. These findings reinforce longstanding concerns regarding limited adherence to the WHO Global Code of Practice on the International Recruitment of Health Personnel. [37]

Interpretation through a systems perspective

The Systems-Based Workforce Sustainability Framework (SWSF) developed in this review conceptualizes the global workforce crisis as the product of interdependent determinants operating across three interlinked levels:

•Macro-level: national health policy, migration governance, demographic change, and financing

•Meso-level: organizational conditions, staffing ratios, management practices, leadership, career structures

•Micro-level: individual motivations, burnout, career trajectories, and intent to migrate or leave the profession

Three reinforcing feedback loops help explain why workforce instability persists despite targeted interventions:

1.Shortage → Burnout → Attrition → More Shortage

Shortage → Burnout → Attrition → More Shortage

Chronic understaffing increases burnout and turnover, which in turn worsens shortages. [26,27]

2.Migration → Dependency → Underinvestment → More Migration

Migration → Dependency → Underinvestment → More Migration

Reliance on foreign-trained workers discourages investment in domestic education and retention, perpetuating long-term dependency. [23,24,36]

3.Aging → Increased Demand → Shrinking Supply

Aging → Increased Demand → Shrinking Supply

Population aging simultaneously increases healthcare demand while shrinking the active workforce through retirements. [21,22]

Sustainable workforce strengthening requires coordinated action targeting structural, organizational, and individual determinants simultaneously. While individual components of the workforce crisis—such as shortages, migration or aging—have been described extensively in prior reports, their interaction through reinforcing feedback loops has not been systematically articulated in the policy literature. The Systems-Based Workforce Sustainability Framework addresses this gap by making these dynamics explicit and policy-actionable.

Policy implications

Addressing global workforce shortages requires coordinated long-term planning that strengthens domestic training pipelines, improves retention and reduces structural dependence on internationally educated nurses. Policy action should prioritise investment in primary care and community health systems, alongside the expansion of advanced nursing roles. Furthermore, workforce sustainability must incorporate routine monitoring of burnout, wellbeing and retention as core indicators to guide system performance and inform strategic planning.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. Heterogeneity in reporting standards and data availability across countries limited comparability of workforce indicators and prevented robust quantitative synthesis. Migration data remain particularly incomplete in many regions, constraining interpretation of trends. In addition, reliance on secondary sources introduces potential bias and restricts causal inference. Nonetheless, the breadth of evidence included allows identification of convergent structural drivers and provides a coherent systems perspective on global workforce sustainability.

Future research directions

Future research should focus on cross-country evaluation of workforce interventions, improved modelling of retirement patterns and migration flows, and longitudinal analyses of workforce resilience. Advancing standardized global indicators for workforce wellbeing and integrating artificial intelligence and predictive analytics into workforce forecasting will also be essential to support more accurate and policy-relevant projections.

Conclusion

This scoping review provides the most up-to-date synthesis of global evidence on the structural, organizational, and professional determinants driving the health workforce crisis. Quantitative indicators reveal a substantial and geographically uneven workforce shortfall, projected to reach approximately 11.1 million health workers by 2030 [1]. Qualitative findings demonstrate how shortages are perpetuated by burnout, inadequate retention strategies, migration dependencies, demographic pressures, and longstanding governance gaps.

By integrating these insights within the Systems-Based Workforce Sustainability Framework, this study advances a comprehensive conceptual model that explains how macro-level policies, meso-level organizational dynamics, and micro-level professional decisions interact to shape workforce stability.

The implications are clear: incremental or isolated policies will not reverse current trajectories. Sustainable workforce strengthening demands long-term planning, investment in retention and wellbeing, ethical migration governance, and expansion of domestic training pipelines.

Without decisive, system-wide reforms, health systems will face increasing difficulty meeting population needs, protecting patient safety, and achieving universal health coverage.
