The impact of personalized nutritional intake on serum inflammation, immune markers, and recovery time in patients with herpes zoster
Authors: Changliang Si, Li Li, Wei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimHerpes zoster (shingles) is a painful viral infection caused by the reactivation of varicella-zoster virus, often leading to prolonged recovery and postherpetic neuralgia. Although antiviral treatments are standard, adjunctive strategies such as personalized nutrition may modulate immune and inflammatory responses, potentially improving outcomes. This retrospective study evaluated the effects of personalized dietary interventions on serum inflammatory and immune markers, recovery time, pain, and quality of life in patients with herpes zoster. Notably, the two treatment arms had unequal sample sizes due to non-randomized assignment in routine clinical practice and adherence-based exclusions; the imbalance was analytically addressed using statistical tests robust to unequal group sizes, and baseline characteristics were comparable between groups.MethodsThis retrospective study evaluated 258 herpes zoster patients at our hospital from April 2019 to April 2023, divided into Standard Diet (n = 136) and Personalized Diet (n = 122) groups. Personalized interventions were based on biological markers, medical history, and lifestyle factors. Key outcomes included changes in serum inflammatory markers (IL-6, IL-17, TNF-α, CRP, IgM, IgA), recovery time metrics, pain levels (VAS score), and quality of life (SF-36).ResultsThe Personalized Diet Group demonstrated significantly greater reductions in serum IL-6, IL-17, TNF-α, and CRP levels compared to the Standard Diet Group (P < 0.05). Recovery times for vesicle cessation, pain relief, crusting, and overall healing were significantly shorter in the personalized group (P < 0.05). Additionally, personalized dietary interventions were associated with greater improvements in VAS pain scores and quality of life measures, particularly in mental health, social functioning, and role-emotional scores. Anxiety and depression scores (SAS and SDS) improved more significantly in the personalized group (P < 0.05).ConclusionPersonalized nutritional dietary recommendations signifcicantly improve inflammatory and immune responses, accelerate recovery, and enhance quality of life in herpes zoster patients.Keywords: Herpes zoster, Personalized nutrition, Inflammation markers, Recovery time, Quality of life, Dietary intervention

Introduction

Herpes zoster, commonly known as shingles, was a debilitating viral infection caused by the reactivation of the varicella-zoster virus [1]. This condition was characterized by painful dermatomal vesicular eruptions and poses significant health challenges due to its widespread prevalence, especially among older adults and immunocompromised individuals [2, 3]. The associated pain, known as postherpetic neuralgia (PHN), can persist long after the rash resolves, severely affecting patients’ quality of life [4]. Despite standard antiviral therapy, many patients experience persistent inflammation and prolonged recovery, underscoring the need for adjunctive strategies [5, 6].

Nutritional interventions have long been recognized for their capacity to influence immune responses and inflammatory pathways [7]. Personalized nutrition—tailored to individual biomarker profiles, comorbidities, and lifestyles—may address this gap by modulating gut microbiota, immune signaling, and inflammatory pathways, thereby accelerating recovery and improving outcomes in herpes zoster [8, 9]. Dietary components can markedly alter the gut microbiota, which plays a crucial role in regulating the immune system [10]. Therefore, personalized nutritional dietary recommendations tailored to individual biological and lifestyle contexts may provide an additional therapeutic advantage by attenuating inflammation and boosting immune resilience.

The interplay between diet and systemic inflammation has been extensively documented, with specific nutrients and dietary patterns shown to modulate inflammatory cytokines [11, 12]. Key micronutrients such as vitamins C and D, zinc, and omega-3 fatty acids were noted for their anti-inflammatory properties and roles in immune modulation [13]. Tailoring dietary intake to optimize these nutrients could potentially accelerate recovery through enhanced immune functioning and reduced inflammatory responses [14]. However, robust clinical evidence regarding the efficacy of personalized nutrition on inflammatory and immune markers in herpes zoster remains limited. This study aims to bridge that gap by evaluating tailored dietary interventions based on serum biomarkers and clinical profiles [15].

This study investigates whether personalized dietary recommendations can reduce serum inflammation and immune activation, thereby shortening recovery time and improving quality of life in patients with herpes zoster.

Materials and methods

Materials

A total of 258 patients diagnosed with herpes zoster at our hospital between April 2019 and April 2023 were selected for this retrospective study. Based on clinician judgment and patient preference during routine care (non-randomized), patients were divided into two groups: the Standard Diet Group, comprising 136 patients receiving standard dietary guidance, and the Personalized Diet Group, consisting of 122 patients receiving individualized dietary intervention. The unequal group sizes resulted from the natural distribution of clinical practice during the study period, with an additional 14 patients in the personalized group and 11 patients in the standard group excluded post-selection due to < 80% dietary adherence (see Sect. “Treatment approach”). The imbalance was analytically addressed using statistical tests that do not require equal sample sizes (independent t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests), and baseline characteristics were confirmed to be comparable between groups (Table 1, all P > 0.05). Dietary guidance was initiated within 72 h of diagnosis. Data were collected at baseline (before dietary guidance) and four weeks after treatment. This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital and was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. All dietary interventions (standard and personalized) were provided as part of routine clinical care, not as research-specific procedures. Given the exclusive use of de-identified patient data extracted from routine clinical records, which presented no more than minimal risk and no impact on patient care, the requirement for written informed consent was formally waived by the ethics committee.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

(1) Presence of clustered vesicles on the affected area, accompanied by symptoms such as fever, headache, and fatigue, meeting the diagnostic criteria for herpes zoster [16]; (2) Complete clinical data; (3) Age between 45 and 75 years; (4) A visual analogue scale (VAS) score of 4 or higher.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Poor treatment compliance; (2) Presence of other severe chronic diseases, such as cardiovascular diseases or cancer; (3) Coagulation disorders; (4) Mental disorders leading to poor cooperation; (5) Kidney, liver, or other organ dysfunction; (6) Cancer patients and those on long-term immunosuppressants or experiencing multiple organ failure; (7) History of drug allergies.

Treatment approach

All patients received standard antiviral therapy (e.g., oral acyclovir) and analgesic management (e.g., gabapentin or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) as per clinical guidelines [17]. The dietary interventions were provided as adjunctive support and not as monotherapy.

The Standard Diet Group received general dietary advice based on WHO balanced diet recommendations [18]: approximately 50–60% carbohydrates, 15–20% protein, and < 30% fat, with an emphasis on whole grains, lean proteins, and limited processed foods, lasting four weeks.

The Personalized Diet Group underwent a customized nutritional intervention. Personalized plans targeted anti-inflammatory nutrients (e.g., vitamin D, zinc, omega-3 fatty acids) and adjusted macronutrient ratios based on deficiencies identified via serum biomarkers (e.g., 25-OH vitamin D, zinc status), comorbidities (e.g., reduced glycemic load for diabetics), and lifestyle. Examples included: vitamin D3 supplementation (1000–2000 IU/day) for deficiencies, zinc-rich foods (oysters, legumes) for suboptimal levels, and omega-3-enriched diets (fatty fish, flaxseeds) for elevated CRP. Patients received detailed dietary guidance and education, including the content, principles, and implementation of their dietary plans, along with relevant nutritional information and food selection advice. This guidance process also lasted for four weeks.

As part of routine clinical care, both groups underwent daily dietary monitoring documented by nursing staff starting on intervention day 1. Dietary intake was assessed via 24-hour recalls performed twice per week by clinical dietitians, with all data extracted retrospectively from electronic medical records. It is important to note that all patients were hospitalized during the 4-week intervention period, and their meals were provided by the hospital kitchen under controlled conditions. Clinical dietitians weighed food portions before each meal, and patients consumed at least 90% of provided meals under supervision. Consequently, the dietary intake data reflect weekly averages from multiple recall assessments, and the controlled hospital environment explains the lower variability compared to typical free-living outpatient nutrition studies, where variability of 20–30% is common. Adherence was defined as ≥ 80% compliance with the prescribed diet. Participants with < 80% compliance were excluded from the final analysis, while concurrently evaluating dietary quality using the Chinese Healthy Eating Index (CHEI) throughout the 4-week trial. This routine clinical protocol ensured systematic tracking of nutritional compliance and diet pattern adherence.

Data collection and testing methods

Regarding blinding in this retrospective study, laboratory personnel measuring serum markers were blinded to group allocation as assays used coded samples; outcome assessors for recovery times were also blinded using standardized extraction from medical records. Participants and clinicians were not blinded due to the nature of routine clinical care, and data analysts were not blinded.

Fasting venous blood samples (3 mL) were collected from the patients’ elbow veins both before the dietary treatment and four weeks after it. The samples underwent anticoagulation and centrifugation at 2500 rpm, with a radius of 13.5 cm, for 15 min. The serum was then stored at -20 °C for subsequent analysis of interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-17 (IL-17), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) levels. Additionally, immunoturbidimetric assays [19] were utilized to measure the concentrations of C-reactive protein (CRP), immunoglobulin M (IgM), and immunoglobulin A (IgA). The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation for all ELISA and immunoturbidimetric assays were < 10%.

Analyze the healing process of herpes based on the patient’s medical records, including statistical measurements of the time required for cessation of herpes lesion formation, pain relief, scab formation, and overall healing.

The SF-36 Quality of Life Scale was employed to assess various quality of life scores for patients, both before and four weeks after receiving dietary guidance. The evaluation covered eight dimensions: general health (GH), social functioning (SF), role-emotional (RE), mental health (MH), bodily pain (BP), physical functioning (PF), role-physical (RP), and vitality (VT). Each dimension was scored from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better quality of life. The SF-36 has been extensively validated in general and patient populations, with established reliability and validity [20].

Pain levels for both groups of patients were assessed using the VAS, with scores ranging from 0 to 10, both before and four weeks after receiving dietary guidance. Lower scores indicate less severe pain. The VAS has been widely validated for acute pain assessment, with established reliability (test-retest reliability r = 0.94–0.99) [21].

Anxiety and depression levels were assessed using the published and widely used Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS). Each scale consists of 20 items rated on a four-point scale. For the SAS, higher scores indicate more severe anxiety, with a total score range from 0 to 100. A standard score of over 50 indicates the presence of anxiety: 50–59 denotes mild anxiety, 60–69 moderate anxiety, and 70 or above severe anxiety. The SAS has demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.85–0.92) in Chinese populations [22]. Similarly, for the SDS, higher scores suggest more severe depression. A standard score greater than 53 indicates the presence of depression: 53–62 denotes mild depression, 63–72 moderate depression, and 72 or above severe depression. The SDS has demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.90–0.93) in Chinese populations [23].

Statistical analysis

A power analysis was conducted using GPower 3.1 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). Based on an effect size of 0.5, α = 0.05, and power = 0.8, a minimum sample size of 102 per group was required. Our sample size of 122 and 136 per group exceeded this requirement.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 29.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies with percentages. The chi-square test was systematically applied to contingency tables. Continuous variables underwent normality verification through the Shapiro-Wilk test, normally distributed parameters were summarized as mean ± standard deviation and compared via independent t-tests; non-normally distributed variables were summarized as median and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Statistical significance threshold was established at α = 0.05 (two-tailed).

Given the exploratory nature of this retrospective study, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons (e.g., Bonferroni correction or False Discovery Rate) was applied. The primary aim was to generate hypotheses regarding the effects of personalized nutrition on a range of clinical and laboratory outcomes in herpes zoster patients. However, we acknowledge that the analysis of multiple outcomes (including 6 serum markers, 4 recovery time parameters, 8 SF-36 domains, VAS, SAS, and SDS) increases the risk of Type I error (false-positive findings). Consequently, P-values should be interpreted with caution, and significant findings require confirmation in future prospective studies with pre-specified primary outcomes and appropriate multiplicity adjustment.

Results

Demographic and basic data

In this study, we compared the baseline characteristics of patients following standard dietary recommendations (n = 136) with those following personalized nutritional guidelines (n = 122), as presented in Table 1. No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of age, gender distribution (males: 52.94% vs. 53.28%), body mass index, diabetes, hypertension, long-term smoking and drinking habits, duration of herpes zoster, presence of complications, pain levels or types, and affected body areas (all P > 0.05). These findings indicate that both dietary groups had similar baseline characteristics, allowing for reliable comparisons in outcomes related to serum inflammation, immune markers, and recovery times.

Dietary composition

There were no significant differences in energy intake, protein, fiber, or CHEI scores between the standard and personalized diet groups (Table 2). However, there were significant differences in carbohydrate intake (P = 0.034) and fat intake (P = 0.019), with the personalized diet group consuming slightly less carbohydrates and fat compared to the standard diet group. These results suggest that the clinical improvements in the personalized diet group may be attributed to optimized carbohydrate and fat composition rather than caloric restriction or overall nutritional quality.

After the 4-week dietary intervention, the Personalized Diet Group (n = 122) showed significantly greater reductions in inflammatory markers compared to the Standard Diet Group (n = 136). For IL-6, the mean post-intervention value was 33.17 ± 7.67 pg/ml in the personalized group vs. 35.29 ± 7.17 pg/ml in the standard group (mean difference − 2.12 pg/ml [95% CI: -3.937 to -0.303], P = 0.022). IL-17 levels were 13.36 ± 2.44 pg/ml vs. 14.14 ± 2.60 pg/ml (mean difference − 0.78 pg/ml [95% CI: -1.395 to -0.165], P = 0.013). TNF-α levels were 16.12 ± 2.61 pg/ml vs. 16.91 ± 2.87 pg/ml (mean difference − 0.79 pg/ml [95% CI: -1.458 to -0.122], P = 0.023). CRP levels were 8.02 ± 2.69 mg/L vs. 8.86 ± 2.72 mg/L (mean difference − 0.84 mg/L [95% CI: -1.501 to -0.179], P = 0.013). Immune markers also showed greater reductions: IgM (9.57 ± 2.66 g/L vs. 10.53 ± 2.55 g/L; mean difference − 0.96 g/L [95% CI: -1.597 to -0.323], P = 0.003) and IgA (10.78 ± 1.23 g/L vs. 11.12 ± 1.33 g/L; mean difference − 0.34 g/L [95% CI: -0.652 to -0.028], P = 0.038).

Recovery time (d)

The Personalized Diet Group (n = 122) demonstrated significantly shorter recovery times across all measured parameters compared to the Standard Diet Group (n = 136) (Fig. 1). Vesicle cessation time was 3.56 ± 0.43 days in the personalized group vs. 3.72 ± 0.62 days in the standard group (mean difference − 0.16 days [95% CI: -0.293 to -0.027], P = 0.019). Pain relief time was 6.89 ± 0.77 days vs. 7.14 ± 0.85 days (mean difference − 0.25 days [95% CI: -0.445 to -0.055], P = 0.012). Crusting time was 8.18 ± 0.81 days vs. 8.48 ± 1.19 days (mean difference − 0.30 days [95% CI: -0.547 to -0.053], P = 0.017). Overall healing time was 16.58 ± 2.15 days vs. 17.35 ± 2.75 days (mean difference − 0.77 days [95% CI: -1.371 to -0.169], P = 0.013). These findings suggest personalized dietary recommendations may accelerate recovery in herpes zoster patients.

VAS pain score

Prior to dietary guidance, VAS scores were comparable between the Standard Diet Group (n = 136: 7.23 ± 1.15) and the Personalized Diet Group (n = 122: 7.35 ± 1.12), with a mean difference of 0.12 (95% CI: -0.157 to 0.397), P = 0.396 (Table 4). After the 4-week intervention, both groups experienced reductions in VAS scores; however, the decrease was more pronounced in the personalized group (4.88 ± 0.98) compared to the standard group (5.15 ± 0.83), with a mean difference of -0.27 (95% CI: -0.493 to -0.047), P = 0.018. These findings suggest that personalized nutritional recommendations significantly enhance various quality of life aspects for patients with herpes zoster.

Quality of life scores

At baseline, quality of life scores (SF-36) were similar between the Standard Diet Group (n = 136) and the Personalized Diet Group (n = 122), with no significant differences across any of the eight domains (all P > 0.05; Table 5). After the 4-week intervention, the personalized group showed significantly higher scores in seven of the eight domains. Specifically, Social Functioning (71.31 ± 7.46 vs. 73.45 ± 7.36; mean difference 2.14 [95% CI: 0.329 to 3.951], P = 0.021), Role-Emotional (69.86 ± 6.69 vs. 72.35 ± 6.45; mean difference 2.49 [95% CI: 0.885 to 4.095], P = 0.003), Mental Health (70.35 ± 6.12 vs. 72.47 ± 5.61; mean difference 2.12 [95% CI: 0.689 to 3.551], P = 0.004), Bodily Pain (71.33 ± 7.21 vs. 73.63 ± 7.66; mean difference 2.30 [95% CI: 0.479 to 4.121], P = 0.014), Physical Functioning (71.42 ± 6.67 vs. 73.21 ± 7.43; mean difference 1.79 [95% CI: 0.059 to 3.521], P = 0.043), Role-Physical (69.53 ± 7.37 vs. 72.46 ± 7.38; mean difference 2.93 [95% CI: 1.127 to 4.736], P = 0.002), and Vitality (70.14 ± 6.02 vs. 72.33 ± 5.53; mean difference 2.19 [95% CI: 0.781 to 3.594], P = 0.003). General Health scores improved but did not reach statistical significance (70.12 ± 8.13 vs. 71.55 ± 8.52; mean difference 1.43 [95% CI: -0.608 to 3.468], P = 0.171). These findings suggest that personalized dietary recommendations effectively reduce inflammatory and immune markers in patients with herpes zoster.

SAS and SDS

Baseline SAS scores were comparable between the Standard Diet Group (n = 136: 55.63 ± 6.36) and the Personalized Diet Group (n = 122: 55.69 ± 6.22; mean difference 0.06 [95% CI: -1.477 to 1.597], P = 0.948). Baseline SDS scores were also similar (58.26 ± 5.85 vs. 59.11 ± 5.73; mean difference 0.85 [95% CI: -0.565 to 2.265], P = 0.240) (Table 6). After the 4-week intervention, the personalized group showed significantly greater reductions. Post-intervention SAS scores were 48.75 ± 5.11 in the personalized group vs. 50.85 ± 5.26 in the standard group (mean difference − 2.10 [95% CI: -3.366 to -0.834], P = 0.001). Post-intervention SDS scores were 50.86 ± 4.12 vs. 52.23 ± 4.32 (mean difference − 1.37 [95% CI: -2.401 to -0.339], P = 0.009).

Discussion

The study sought to investigate the impact of personalized nutritional dietary recommendations on serum inflammation and immune markers, as well as recovery time, in patients with herpes zoster. The strategic reduction in carbohydrate and fat intake, potentially involving refined carbohydrate substitution and optimized fatty acid profiles, may have mediated anti-inflammatory effects despite comparable overall energy consumption between groups. One of the primary findings was the reduction in serum inflammatory markers. For IL-6, baseline values were 46.19 ± 6.94 pg/ml (standard) and 47.38 ± 7.25 pg/ml (personalized). After intervention, the personalized group showed an absolute reduction of 14.21 pg/ml (30.0% decrease from baseline), compared to 10.90 pg/ml (23.6% decrease) in the standard group. For TNF-α, baseline values were 22.26 ± 4.61 pg/ml (standard) and 22.63 ± 4.55 pg/ml (personalized). The personalized group achieved an absolute reduction of 6.51 pg/ml (28.8% decrease), versus 5.35 pg/ml (24.0% decrease) in the standard group. We acknowledge that the observed absolute differences in macronutrient intake (2.53 g for carbohydrates and 1.21 g for fat) between the two groups are modest and unlikely to directly account for the magnitude of inflammatory marker reductions. Instead, the clinical benefits observed in the Personalized Diet Group are more plausibly attributable to several interconnected factors that are not fully captured by macronutrient data alone. First, personalized plans specifically corrected individual micronutrient deficiencies (e.g., vitamin D, zinc, omega-3 fatty acids) based on baseline serum biomarker testing, and these micronutrients have well-established anti-inflammatory properties. Second, dietary interventions were tailored to comorbidities (e.g., glycemic load reduction for diabetics) and lifestyle factors, which may have synergistic effects on immune regulation. Third, the act of personalization itself may have improved overall dietary quality beyond what is reflected in carbohydrate and fat grams. Therefore, the small macronutrient differences should not be interpreted as the direct cause of the observed clinical improvements; rather, they serve as one indicator of dietary modification within a broader, multi-component personalized intervention. These changes are biologically relevant because IL-6 and TNF-α are key drivers of the acute inflammatory response in herpes zoster, directly correlating with pain severity and tissue inflammation. The additional 6.4% greater reduction in IL-6 and 4.8% greater reduction in TNF-α in the personalized group suggest that tailored nutrition may modestly enhance resolution of systemic inflammation, though the clinical significance of these incremental improvements requires further validation. These cytokines and biomarkers play critical roles in the inflammatory response and immune modulation [24, 25]. Elevated levels were typically associated with acute inflammatory conditions and immune system dysregulation, which were characteristic of herpes zoster [26]. While general anti-inflammatory diets (e.g., Mediterranean) are known to support immune health, the novelty of our approach lies in the personalization of nutrient intake based on individual serum biomarkers, comorbidities, and lifestyle—factors not routinely addressed in standardized diets. The observed benefits are likely attributable to true personalization rather than overall diet quality alone, as interventions were specifically adjusted according to individual biomarker deficiencies and clinical profiles. This targeted approach may explain the superior reductions in inflammatory markers and accelerated recovery observed, beyond what might be expected from improved diet quality alone [27]. The gut microbiota was known to regulate immune responses and inflammation, and dietary components can significantly alter microbiota composition and function [28]. Personalized diets, specifically high in anti-inflammatory compounds such as omega-3 fatty acids, antioxidants, and polyphenols, might enhance gut health, reduce systemic inflammation, and modulate immune responses, explaining the observed reductions in inflammatory biomarkers [29, 30].

The expedited recovery times in the personalized diet group offer another intriguing point for discussion. The reduced time for vesicle cessation, pain relief, and scab formation suggests that personalized nutritional interventions may accelerate the healing process. This could be attributed to the optimization of macro- and micronutrient intake, which may facilitate cellular regeneration and repair mechanisms [31]. Certain nutrients, such as vitamins C and D, zinc, and protein, were known to play pivotal roles in wound healing and immune defense [32]. A diet tailored to enhance the intake of such nutrients could therefore speed up recovery by promoting efficient cellular repair, enhancing collagen synthesis, and boosting immune function, thus reducing the duration of symptoms in herpes zoster patients [32].

Pain management was a critical aspect of herpes zoster treatment, as pain can significantly impact patients’ quality of life [33]. The study indicated a more marked reduction in pain levels, as measured by the VAS, in the personalized group. This might be attributed to particular dietary components known to possess analgesic and anti-inflammatory properties [34]. However, the observed mean difference in VAS score between groups was 0.27 points (95% CI: -0.493 to -0.047). For acute herpes zoster pain, the established minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for VAS is approximately 1.0–1.5 points [35]. Therefore, the 0.27-point difference, while statistically significant, does not meet conventional MCID thresholds for clinically meaningful pain reduction. This suggests that personalized dietary intervention provided only a modest additional analgesic benefit beyond standard care, and the clinical relevance of this difference should be interpreted cautiously. Nutrients such as magnesium, which has been shown to improve pain symptoms, along with anti-inflammatory fatty acids and antioxidants, could help mediate neuroinflammation associated with herpes zoster [36]. Moreover, certain dietary patterns that minimize simple sugars and refined carbohydrates can potentially stabilize blood sugar levels, reducing neuropathic pain and discomfort [37]. While improvements in VAS and SF-36 scores are self-reported and potentially susceptible to placebo effects, the concurrent significant reductions in objective serum inflammatory markers (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α, CRP) support the biological plausibility of the observed benefits. Nevertheless, the subjective nature of these outcomes underscores the need for future blinded trials to minimize potential bias. The clinical significance of the observed reductions in recovery time, such as the mean difference of 0.77 days in overall healing, warrants discussion. While this difference is below the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) typically cited for herpes zoster recovery (often considered 1–2 days for individual patient benefit) [38], it may still hold value. Even a single day of reduced pain and discomfort is meaningful to patients. Moreover, at a population level, a 0.77-day reduction could translate into decreased healthcare utilization, fewer lost workdays, and improved productivity, suggesting potential public health and economic relevance. However, the clinical impact for individual patients is likely modest. Although not directly evaluated in this study, the potential cost-effectiveness of personalized nutrition warrants further investigation. Scalability may be enhanced through digital health tools and dietitian-led group sessions, though individualized biomarker testing and counseling may incur higher initial costs.

Regarding the SF-36, significant improvements were observed in seven domains: Social Functioning (mean difference 2.14, 95% CI: 0.329–3.951), Role-Emotional (2.49, 95% CI: 0.885–4.095), Mental Health (2.12, 95% CI: 0.689–3.551), Bodily Pain (2.30, 95% CI: 0.479–4.121), Physical Functioning (1.79, 95% CI: 0.059–3.521), Role-Physical (2.93, 95% CI: 1.127–4.736), and Vitality (2.19, 95% CI: 0.781–3.594). General Health improved but did not reach statistical significance (1.43, 95% CI: -0.608–3.468). Established MCID thresholds for SF-36 domains in acute pain conditions typically range from 3 to 5 points. Most observed mean differences (1.79–2.93 points) fell below these conventional thresholds. However, within-group improvements from baseline were larger (5.28–10.11 points), all exceeding MCID thresholds [39]. This indicates that both dietary interventions improved quality of life meaningfully from baseline, but the incremental benefit of personalized over standard nutrition was modest and may not reach clinically important differences between groups. Firstly, addressing individual nutritional deficiencies can enhance energy levels, cognitive function, and mood [40]. Secondly, improved gut health, as mentioned earlier, plays a key role in mental well-being [41]. The gut-brain axis, a bidirectional communication system, suggests that gut health significantly impacts mental states, including mood and anxiety levels [41]. Personalized dietary recommendations that promote a healthy gut environment may, therefore, positively influence psychological well-being, as seen in the significant decrease in SAS and SDS scores [42]. Notably, while most domains of the SF-36 showed significant improvements, the General Health (GH) score did not reach statistical significance. This may be due to the fact that GH captures broader and more stable perceptions of overall health, which may be less sensitive to short-term dietary interventions compared to more specific domains such as pain or emotional role function. Additionally, GH is influenced by factors beyond nutrition, such as pre-existing chronic conditions or genetic predispositions, which were not fully addressed in this study.

Furthermore, personalized nutrition aligns with the principles of precision medicine, emphasizing the importance of individualized treatment plans. By considering individual differences in genetics, epigenetics, metabolism, and lifestyle, personalized diets can potentially offer more effective interventions compared to generalized dietary advice [43]. The study underscores that a one-size-fits-all approach may not be optimal for conditions like herpes zoster, where dietary needs and responses differ significantly among individuals [43]. Tailoring diet plans by considering unique patient profiles ensures that dietary interventions address specific individual needs, thereby enhancing efficacy and overall health outcomes [44].

From a clinical implementation perspective, the findings advocate for the integration of personalized nutrition strategies into standard care practices for herpes zoster. This could involve collaboration between healthcare providers and registered dietitians or nutritionists to design and implement personalized dietary interventions. Such collaborations could enhance patient adherence and engagement, as patients might perceive personalized advice as more relevant and meaningful, potentially leading to better adherence and outcomes.

While the findings of this study offer valuable insights into the benefits of personalized nutritional dietary recommendations for managing herpes zoster, several limitations should be acknowledged. For instance, due to the retrospective design, it was not possible to blind assessors to the dietary groups, which could introduce bias, especially in subjective assessments like the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores. This limitation may affect the objectivity of such results. As a retrospective study, the lack of randomization may introduce confounding. Although baseline characteristics were similar, unmeasured confounders (e.g., socioeconomic status, exact nutritional status) may exist and the study’s sample size may limit the generalizability of the results. Future prospective randomized double-blind trials are warranted to confirm these findings. Moreover, the reliance on 24-hour dietary recall for assessing dietary intake and adherence could introduce bias. This method is subject to recall bias, as participants may not accurately remember or report their food consumption, and day-to-day variability in dietary intake may not be captured by intermittent assessments (twice per week). These limitations could lead to misclassification of adherence or dietary composition, potentially attenuating or exaggerating the observed associations. Additionally, participants might not have accurately reported their food intake, further contributing to potential information bias. Additionally, the study did not adjust for multiple comparisons across numerous outcomes, which may increase the risk of Type I error. Future studies should consider correction methods such as Bonferroni or False Discovery Rate adjustment. The study also did not account for several potential confounding factors. Although baseline comparisons showed no significant differences between groups for age, sex, diabetes, hypertension, disease duration, pain level, and pain type, other unmeasured confounders may remain. These include: detailed concomitant analgesic or immunomodulatory medications beyond standard antivirals and gabapentin/NSAIDs (e.g., antidepressant use, corticosteroid use), exact time since rash onset at enrollment (though duration was comparable), herpes zoster vaccination status (none of the patients were vaccinated as the study period predated widespread recombinant zoster vaccine availability in China for this age group), socioeconomic status (which may influence diet quality and health literacy), and baseline nutritional status (e.g., serum vitamin D, zinc, omega-3 index) beyond the targeted biomarkers. Additionally, depression and anxiety levels at baseline were measured and were comparable between groups, mitigating confounding from psychological factors. Nevertheless, residual confounding from unmeasured variables could bias the observed effect estimates in either direction, and our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Furthermore, the 4-week follow-up duration is insufficient to evaluate postherpetic neuralgia (PHN), which typically persists for months after rash resolution. PHN was not an outcome of this study because our follow-up period (4 weeks) is too short to capture this chronic complication. Future studies should extend follow-up to at least 3–6 months to assess the potential impact of personalized nutrition on PHN incidence and severity. Additionally, the current duration may not have been sufficient to observe other long-term effects of dietary interventions on recovery and relapse rates. Future studies should also consider stratified randomization or subgroup analyses focusing on immunocompromised individuals, older adults, or those with specific metabolic profiles to determine whether personalized nutrition offers differential benefits across patient populations. Future studies should extend follow-up to evaluate the durability of effects and compare personalized nutrition with other adjunct therapies (e.g., probiotics, exercise) to determine relative effectiveness. Cost-effectiveness analyses and targeted interventions in immunocompromised or elderly subgroups are also warranted.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides hypothesis-generating evidence that personalized nutritional dietary recommendations are associated with modest improvements in serum inflammatory markers and recovery times in patients with herpes zoster. The patient-important benefits observed include a reduction in overall healing time and small improvements in quality of life domains. These findings suggest that personalized nutrition may offer an adjunctive benefit to standard antiviral therapy, but the clinical impact is modest. At present, these results are not practice-changing and should be interpreted as exploratory. Future prospective randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes, blinded assessments, and predefined MCID thresholds are needed to confirm whether personalized dietary interventions provide clinically meaningful benefits for herpes zoster patients.
