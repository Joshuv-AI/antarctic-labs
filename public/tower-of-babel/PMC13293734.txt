Reproducible bicarbonate thresholds predict critically ill patient mortality in an international personalized survival study
Authors: Can Xie, Jing Wang, Ruyan Lv, Qixiu Li, Zhifan Li, Wei Xu, Xiaobing Zhai, Xiaofei Li, Ping Xu, Gang Luo, Xinqi Cheng, Xicheng Song, Kefeng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBicarbonate abnormalities are common in intensive care unit (ICU) patients, but prior mortality studies neglected critical illness pathophysiology. This study aimed to identify optimal admission bicarbonate thresholds for mortality prediction and develop a deep learning survival model. Using data from 208,072 ICU patients across three independent cohorts (MIMIC-IV, eICU-CRD, YHD-HOSP) in the US and China, causal inference and an integrated framework quantified in-hospital mortality, with thresholds estimated and validated across cohorts. Thresholds of 24 mEq/L and 33 mEq/L were critical; patients with levels ≤24 or >33 mEq/L had significantly higher mortality risk (adjusted HR: 1.22; HR: 1.17). Causal SurvivalNet, a deep neural network, generated personalized survival curves (integrated Brier scores: 0.15, 0.076, 0.16) accessible via a web tool. The study identified reproducible, causal bicarbonate thresholds, with Causal SurvivalNet outperforming conventional approaches, underscoring admission bicarbonate’s utility for early critical care risk stratification.

Introduction

Bicarbonate is a pivotal component of the body’s acid-based buffering system, essential for regulating acid-base balance and supporting critical metabolic, respiratory, and cellular functions.1,2 Abnormal serum bicarbonate levels are commonly observed in critically ill patients and are associated with adverse outcomes such as insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, hypotension, and muscle weakness, making it a critical biomarker of disease severity in ICU patients.

Previous studies have explored the relationship between admission serum bicarbonate levels and mortality in non-ICU settings, such as in patients with renal disease,3,4 demonstrating that abnormal levels are associated with a higher risk of in-hospital mortality.5 More recent research has extended these investigations to specific conditions such as stroke, myocardial infarction, and COVID-19.6,7,8,9 However, these analyses have limited applicability to critically ill populations, who experience more complex metabolic disturbances from conditions such as multi-organ failure or sepsis. Therefore, the bicarbonate-mortality association requires rigorous investigation within ICU populations, which may reveal distinct relationships due to the unique pathophysiology of critical illness. Furthermore, the generalizability of prior findings has been constrained by small sample sizes, single-center designs, and reliance on conventional reference ranges.

Prognostic models have traditionally relied on correlations and often fail to account for causal relationships explicitly. Causal inference offers a powerful approach to uncover potential causal mechanisms from large-scale data. For example, computational methods such as the fast causal inference (FCI) algorithm have successfully identified factors influencing chronic lung disease prognosis10 and cardiac surgery length-of-stay.11 However, the causal drivers of in-hospital mortality in ICU patients remain largely underexplored.

Traditional survival analysis methods, such as the Cox Proportional Hazards model, assume proportional hazards—an assumption often violated in ICU populations with complex, nonlinear risk relationships.12 These techniques also fail to produce individualized risk assessments. While deep learning-based survival models such as Cox-Time have shown promise in accommodating non-proportional hazards and capturing nonlinearities in fields such as oncology,13,14 their performance and utility have not been rigorously validated in the ICU setting. Moreover, the interpretability of such models alone remains a challenge.

Given the prognostic value of serum bicarbonate, identifying clinically actionable thresholds is critical for decision-making. This large-scale, multinational retrospective cohort study first aimed to identify optimal admission serum bicarbonate thresholds and quantify their association with in-hospital mortality. Our second aim was to develop and validate a novel survival prediction model that integrates causal inference with deep learning to provide personalized risk stratification for ICU patients. To enhance clinical utility, we developed a web-based tool for this model, Causal SurvivalNet, available at https://bicarbonate.deepsurvival.cc/.

Results

Causal discovery identifies clinical variables with potential causal association with in-hospital mortality

We first performed causal graphical analysis on the MIMIC-IV dataset to identify the clinical variables collected upon ICU admission with potential causal associations with the patient's survival during hospitalization. Interestingly, we found that serum bicarbonate levels had significant causal relationships with mortality outcome. Cox regression and frailty modeling confirm in-hospital mortality risk with serum bicarbonate ranges

To further explore the bicarbonate-mortality association, patients with MIMIC-IV were stratified by the RCS-derived thresholds. The cohort included 27,790 ICU patients with admission serum bicarbonate between 24 and 33 mEq/L. A total of 42,844 patients had levels ≤24 mEq/L, while only 1,632 patients had levels >33 mEq/L.

We constructed unadjusted and four adjusted Cox regression and Cox frailty models, accounting for confounders and ICU heterogeneity. With the 24–33 mEq/L bicarbonate range as reference, patients above the upper threshold (>33 mEq/L) or below the lower threshold (≤24 mEq/L) demonstrated significantly increased in-hospital risk across both the unadjusted model and the four adjusted models (Figure 3B). The heightened risk of hospital mortality for patients with serum bicarbonate ≤24 mEq/L persisted in Cox frailty models, indicating robustness despite ICU variation (Figure 3C). However, for patients with bicarbonate >33 mEq/L, this association reached borderline statistical significance in the frailty model, likely due to the smaller sample size in this segment.

The Kaplan-Meier survival curves were then generated to visualize the differences in survival probabilities among the three stratified bicarbonate ranges at the population level. Overall, patients in MIMIC-IV with bicarbonate levels ≤24 mEq/L or >33 mEq/L demonstrated lower survival probabilities compared to those with bicarbonate levels ranging between 24 mEq/L and 33 mEq/L (Figure 3D).

Tables S2, S3, S4, and S5 present the multicollinearity assessment results of the adjusted model variables across the three cohorts, quantified using the VIF. For the MIMIC-IV cohort, Tables S2 and S3 consistently show that all covariates have VIF values ranging from 1.001 to 4.921, respectively; notably, no variable exceeds a VIF of 5, and the vast majority of variables have VIF <3, confirming the absence of significant multicollinearity. For the eICU-CRD cohort, Table S4 demonstrates that the VIF values of all included covariates are confined to 1.001–2.475, with the highest value in Chloride (2.475), which is substantially below the critical threshold of 5, indicating minimal multicollinearity. For the YHD-HOSP cohort, Table S5 reveals that VIF values of all variables range from 1.021 to 1.321, maintaining a consistently low level and confirming the absence of multicollinearity.

Cross-cohort and cross-country validation

We further validated the association between ICU admission serum bicarbonate and mortality risk using the eICU-CRD and YHD-HOSP cohorts. Patients in these two cohorts were stratified using the same mortality-associated bicarbonate thresholds identified in the MIMIC-IV cohort. In eICU-CRD, the number of ICU patients with serum bicarbonate levels ≤24 mEq/L, 24–33 mEq/L, and >33 mEq/L was 70,483, 55,233, and 4,981, respectively. In the YHD-HOSP cohort, 863 patients had serum bicarbonate between 24 and 33 mEq/L upon ICU admission, 4,200 patients had levels ≤24 mEq/L, and only 46 patients had levels >33 mEq/L.

Consistent with MIMIC-IV results, Cox regression analysis in the eICU-CRD showed that patients with the bicarbonate levels>33 mEq/L have a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.20 (95% CI: 1.01–1.43) for in-hospital mortality after adjusting demographics, critical illness, underlying diseases, ions, biochemical indicators, and vital signs (Figure 4A). The adjusted HR was 1.07 (95% CI: 1.00–1.14) for patients with the serum bicarbonate levels ≤24 mEq/L compared to the reference range (24–33 mEq/L). Similar to the two US cohorts, in the YHD-HOSP cohort, with the 24–33 mEq/L bicarbonate range as reference, patients with bicarbonate levels ≤24 mEq/L demonstrated significantly increased in-hospital risk across both unadjusted (HR: 1.17, 95% CI: 1.03–1.33), and four adjusted models (e.g., Model 3: HR: 1.17, 95% CI: 1.02–1.34; see Figure 4B for all models).Figure 4Cross-cohort and cross-country validation of the association between ICU admission serum bicarbonate levels and in-hospital mortality risk(A and B) Cox regression analysis results for in-hospital mortality risk in the eICU-CRD (A) and YHD-HOSP (B) cohorts. While the analysis showed that higher severity scores were appropriately associated with increased mortality in the eICU-CRD (Figure 6B) and YHD-HOSP (Figure 6C) cohorts, it yielded clinically paradoxical results in the MIMIC-IV cohort. Counterintuitively, in the MIMIC-IV dataset, higher SOFA/APACHE IV scores appeared to be protective in patients with bicarbonate levels ≤24 mEq/L (HR: 0.61, 95% CI: 0.53–0.69) and >33 mEq/L (HR: 0.79, 95% CI: 0.69–0.90) (Figure 6A).

This biologically implausible finding—that higher illness severity predicts better survival—strongly suggests that the relationships between bicarbonate, baseline severity scores, and mortality are characterized by complex interactions that are not captured by conventional stratification. This highlights the limitations of traditional analytical approaches in handling such multifaceted data and reinforces the necessity for an integrative model, which is designed to learn these complex patterns and provide more reliable, personalized risk predictions.

Discussion

This study conducted a cross-national integrative analysis of data from three large, multicenter ICU cohorts in the United States and China, and for the first time established a causal association between serum bicarbonate concentration at ICU admission and in-hospital mortality within a causal inference framework. Using RCS modeling, we identified two critical thresholds with significant prognostic implications: 24 mEq/L and 33 mEq/L. Both Cox proportional hazards models and stratified Cox frailty models consistently demonstrated that patients with admission bicarbonate levels ≤24 mEq/L or >33 mEq/L had significantly increased risks of in-hospital mortality, and these findings were robustly validated across multiple independent cohorts. These findings informed the development of a deep survival model integrating bicarbonate thresholds and other causal drivers, enabling high-precision prediction of individualized survival trajectories and mortality risk in ICU patients.

To ensure the identified thresholds possess clinical reproducibility and biological plausibility, this study employed a robust, multi-stage research design. First, we leveraged the MIMIC-IV database, a large-scale repository, combined with the FCI, to systematically elucidate the underlying causal graph structure among bicarbonate levels, confounding variables, and in-hospital mortality. As a standard constraint-based causal discovery algorithm for observational data, FCI effectively controls for the influence of unmeasured confounders through conditional independence tests,16 making it particularly suitable for the highly heterogeneous and multiply biased real-world data encountered in ICU settings.17 Building on this, RCS analysis revealed a U-shaped nonlinear relationship between bicarbonate levels and mortality, suggesting that traditional linear assumptions may obscure critical pathophysiological turning points. Notably, standard Cox regression models typically assume population homogeneity and may inadequately adjust for structural differences across ICU types (e.g., cardiac ICU, surgical ICU, general ICU), potentially leading to biased effect estimates.18 To address this, we introduced Cox frailty models with ICU type as a random effect, effectively adjusting for clustering effects due to institutional characteristics and enhancing the external validity of the model.19,20 Furthermore, cross-cohort external validation and pH-adjusted sensitivity analyses further confirmed the stability and clinical robustness of the identified thresholds.

Notably, the risk thresholds identified in this study (24 mEq/L and 33 mEq/L) partially overlap with yet are distinct from the current reference range for adult serum bicarbonate (22–29 mEq/L) recommended by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute.21 The overlapping segment (24–29 mEq/L) reflects acid-base homeostasis in healthy individuals and corresponds to the most favorable prognosis in critically ill patients, while key differences lie in the two ranges’ inherent purposes: The conventional normal range is derived from healthy populations and does not account for ICU patients’ impaired compensatory capacity or complex metabolic disturbances, whereas the 24–33 mEq/L interval is a prognosis-driven “clinically actionable threshold” tailored to critical care. For ICU patients, bicarbonate levels of 22–24 mEq/L—though within the traditional normal range—may indicate early organ dysfunction, inadequate tissue perfusion, or subclinical renal impairment, serving as a “subclinical high-risk interval” associated with elevated mortality. Conversely, levels of 30–33 mEq/L, while exceeding the traditional upper limit, reflect the body’s adaptive compensatory alkaline reserve in response to acute stress without severe electrolyte disorders or tissue hypoxia, thus remaining within the favorable prognosis range. This finding challenges the clinical paradigm of “intervening whenever values deviate from conventional reference ranges” and provides a more tailored standard for acid-base balance assessment in critical care. In ICU patients, whose compensatory capacity is often impaired, bicarbonate levels within the conventional “normal” range may still indicate underlying conditions or poor prognosis; we found that even mild metabolic acidosis (e.g., <24 mEq/L) was associated with significantly elevated in-hospital mortality risk, while patients with bicarbonate levels between 24 and 33 mEq/L exhibited relatively favorable survival curves, indicating that moderate compensatory alkaline reserve may reflect the body’s favorable adaptive capacity in response to acute stress.

From a pathophysiological perspective, serum bicarbonate is a central indicator reflecting acid-base balance, renal function, and internal homeostasis.22 Its concentration is primarily regulated by the kidneys through filtration, reabsorption, and de novo generation to neutralize endogenous acid load. Low bicarbonate levels often indicate undiagnosed renal impairment,23 progression of acute kidney injury (AKI), lactate accumulation due to tissue hypoperfusion (e.g., from shock or severe infection leading to anaerobic metabolism), or microcirculatory dysfunction triggering inflammatory responses and multi-organ failure—mechanisms that also underpin the elevated mortality risk in patients with levels of 22–24 mEq/L. For this specific subgroup (22–24 mEq/L), clinical decision-making should primarily regard bicarbonate as a sensitive marker of disease severity rather than a direct trigger for active metabolic acidosis correction. Hasty Alkali Agent Administration may mask underlying disease progression and interfere with intrinsic compensatory mechanisms; instead, the indicator’s core value lies in prompting clinicians to promptly investigate and address root causes, such as optimizing tissue perfusion through fluid resuscitation, actively controlling infections, and dynamically monitoring renal function and lactate levels. Targeted acid-base correction should only be considered when patients present with strong indications, such as severe acidemia (arterial pH < 7.2), life-threatening electrolyte disturbances (e.g., hypokalemia compromising cardiac function), or definitive evidence of tissue hypoxia—aligning with the logic of “guiding etiological intervention through biomarkers rather than merely intervening on biomarkers themselves.” In contrast, elevated bicarbonate levels (>33 mEq/L) are commonly observed in conditions such as the compensated phase of chronic respiratory acidosis,24 diuretic-induced metabolic alkalosis, gastric fluid loss, or adrenal insufficiency. These patients frequently experience complications such as electrolyte disturbances (e.g., hypokalemia, hypochloremia), leftward shift of the oxygen dissociation curve causing tissue hypoxia, and prolonged mechanical ventilation dependence, thereby increasing mortality risk.

Widely used clinical scoring systems such as SOFA25 are well-recognized for comprehensively reflecting disease severity in critically ill patients, and their clinical utility in risk stratification is well-established. However, the conventional unadjusted stratified approach we tested in a supplementary analysis—where we only examined the association between disease severity scores and in-hospital mortality within each predefined serum bicarbonate subgroup (≤24 mEq/L, 24–33 mEq/L, and >33 mEq/L) without incorporating other covariates included in this study—may have limitations in capturing certain complex relationships. As shown in Figure 6, this approach yielded inconsistent and clinically counterintuitive findings in the MIMIC-IV cohort, with higher severity scores appearing to be paradoxically associated with lower mortality. This highlights a critical limitation of the conventional stratified method rather than the scoring systems themselves: While SOFA often relies on linear relationship assumptions,26 they may not fully resolve the complex, nonlinear interactions between metabolic status (bicarbonate), organ dysfunction scores, and mortality when applied through an unadjusted stratified strategy. This limitation of the conventional approach reinforces the need for an integrative model like ours to more accurately map these intricate relationships, complementing the well-established value of SOFA in clinical practice.

In contrast, the hierarchical stepwise modeling strategy employed in this study enabled fine-grained characterization of the bicarbonate - mortality relationship. Low range (≤24 mEq/L): Significantly increased mortality risk may stem from microcirculatory dysfunction, myocardial depression, and immune dysregulation induced by severe metabolic acidosis; intermediate range (24–33 mEq/L): The body maintains relative internal stability through respiratory and renal compensation, with a gradual increase in mortality risk, suggesting this range represents a “tolerance homeostasis” window; High range (>33 mEq/L): Excessive alkalinization leads to impaired oxygen unloading from hemoglobin, hypokalemia, and increased risk of arrhythmias, constituting independent mortality risk factors.

This modeling framework provides clinicians with a dynamic risk stratification tool based on acid-based homeostasis, offering the following potential applications: (1) improved sensitivity in early identification of high-risk patients; (2) guidance for individualized fluid management and acid-base correction strategies; and (3) optimization of biomarker-driven patient stratification design in clinical trials.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the total number of patients in the YHD-HOSP cohort, as well as the count of those with serum bicarbonate >33 mEq/L, was lower than in the MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD cohorts. This limited sample size in the subgroup may have affected the statistical power of the identified thresholds (e.g., mortality rates in the ≤24 and >33 mEq/L ranges were slightly higher in YHD-HOSP compared to the corresponding intervals in the other two cohorts); however, this limitation was partially mitigated by the integration of multicenter data. However, this limitation has been partially mitigated through multicenter data integration. Second, as an observational analysis, although we have employed causal inference methods to control potential confounders as rigorously as possible, we cannot entirely rule out the influence of unmeasured variables. Future incorporation of emerging clinical data, such as genomic markers, may help deepen mechanistic understanding and further refine the predictive model. Furthermore, while the inclusion of multicenter ICU populations from both the United States and China provides preliminary support for the generalizability of our findings, the external validity of the YHD-HOSP cohort—particularly in healthcare systems or populations with distinct genetic backgrounds—requires further validation. The inclusion of larger, more geographically and demographically diverse cohorts in future studies will help consolidate and extend the present findings. This large-scale multinational cohort study provides strong evidence for a causal association between serum bicarbonate levels at intensive care unit (ICU) admission and in-hospital mortality. Using rigorous statistical and computational approaches, we identified clinically relevant thresholds of 24 mEq/L and 33 mEq/L that define distinct prognostic groups. Patients with admission bicarbonate levels outside the 24–33 mEq/L range exhibited significantly higher mortality, a finding consistently validated across over 200,000 ICU patients in the United States and China. To translate these findings into practice, we developed Causal SurvivalNet, an interpretable deep learning tool that integrates these bicarbonate thresholds with other key clinical variables to generate individualized survival predictions.

These results underscore the clinical value of admission serum bicarbonate for risk stratification in critically ill patients. The proposed thresholds and predictive framework offer a biomarker-informed foundation for clinical decision support systems, advancing critical care toward more precise and dynamic patient management. Future efforts should focus on validating the model’s adaptability in broader healthcare settings and enhancing its performance by integrating multimodal clinical data.

Method detailsdetails

Patient cohort

This multinational study analyzed data from three independent ICU cohorts: the MIMIC-IV (2008–2019)27 and eICU-CRD (2014–2015)28 in the United States, and the YHD-HOSP cohort (2021–2023) in China.

Data pre-processing

The detailed inclusion criteria and selection strategies are described in Method S1 and Figure 1. Briefly, for MIMIC-IV, we first excluded non-ICU patients, then ICU patients with missing discharge status or serum bicarbonate measurements. Patients lacking SOFA or APACHE IV scores were also excluded. For analysis, we used the first serum bicarbonate measurement recorded upon admission. We extracted 42 demographic and clinical variables shared across all three cohorts (Table S1). Duplicate records were removed based on patient identifiers, retaining one valid observation per patient. Continuous variables with >20% missing values were excluded from the analysis. Missing values < 20% were imputed using probabilistic principal component analysis (PPCA). Identical inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to all three cohorts (MIMIC-IV, eICU-CRD, and YHD-HOSP).

Exposure and outcome definition

The primary exposure was the serum bicarbonate level at admission, and the primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. To minimize potential bias from reverse causality, all variables were sourced from the first available measurements upon ICU admission, ensuring they reflect the patient’s baseline status rather than evolving critical illness.

Covariates definition

The covariates were categorized into six groups: demographic information, critical illness (Sepsis, Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome [ARDS], and organ failure), underlying chronic diseases, ions and electrolytes, biochemical indicators, and vital signs.

Risk and survival analysis

An overview of the data analysis flow is provided in Figure S1, and a brief description of the methods is detailed below:

Causal graphical analysis

We employed the Fast Causal Inference (FCI) algorithm29,30,31 within the TETRAD visualization software (V7.5.0) to explore the causal links among exposure, covariates, and the outcome. This approach analyzes the conditional independence in patient data, deduces causal directions between variables, and produces Partial Ancestral Graphs (PAGs), thereby enhancing our understanding of potential causal relationships.32 For detailed FCI procedures, please refer to Method S3.

Restricted cubic spline analysis

RCS was utilized to analyze the nonlinear relationship between serum bicarbonate levels and in-hospital mortality, and to determine the critical ranges of bicarbonate among ICU patients. Critical interval values were identified at the intersection of the reference line (Hazard ratio, HR = 1) and the RCS curve.

Cox regression and cox frailty analysis based on the identified crucial ranges of serum bicarbonate level

The Cox regression analysis was used to investigate the survival risk within critical serum bicarbonate ranges at the population level (Method S2). Additionally, we applied the Cox frailty model33 to confirm the results from the traditional Cox regression analysis by accounting for the heterogeneity across ICUs. The underlying principle of the Cox frailty model can be summarized as follows:(Equation 1)hij(t|X)=μih0(t|X)exp(Xβ)Where μi represents the frailty term for each ICU, assumed to follow a distribution with mean 1 and independent of X. When μi = 1, the model simplifies to Cox regression.

We conducted an unadjusted model (bicarbonate, model 0), and several adjusted models as follows: model 1: model 0 + SOFA/APACHE IV + demographics (age, sex, smoke, marriage, and race) + critical illness (sepsis, acute pancreatitis, ARDS, kidney failure, and respiratory failure) + underlying chronic diseases (Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease [COPD], cirrhosis, hepatic failure, fatty liver, malignancy, cerebral infarction, Coronary Heart Disease [CHD], solid tumors, cerebral hemorrhage, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, and obesity); model 2: model 1 + ions and electrolytes (potassium, phosphate, magnesium, and sodium); model 3: model 2 + biochemical indicators (platelet count, White Blood Cell [WBC] count, creatinine, International Normalized Ratio [INR], hemoglobin, glucose, and urea nitrogen), and vital signs (Peripheral Oxygen Saturation [SPO2], respiratory rate, Mean Blood Pressure [MBP], heart rate, temperature); model 4: model 0 + all causal covariates identified by causal graphical analysis (Figure 2).

To rigorously evaluate potential multicollinearity among conceptually overlapping covariates in the adjusted models (Models 1–4) - specifically SOFA score vs. sepsis diagnosis, renal failure vs. serum creatinine levels, and ARDS vs. respiratory failure - this study employed Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) analysis as a quantitative diagnostic tool. The multicollinearity assessment was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0, incorporating all covariates from the MIMIC-IV, eICU-CRD, and YHD-HOSP cohorts. In-hospital mortality was specified as the dependent variable in the regression framework. The VIF values for each covariate were automatically calculated through SPSS’s “Collinearity Diagnostics” module. Multicollinearity interpretation followed established thresholds: VIF <5 indicates absence of severe multicollinearity, VIF 5–10 represents moderate multicollinearity (generally acceptable), and VIF >10 signifies severe multicollinearity requiring intervention.

The conventional Cox regression was executed using IBM SPSS statistics 27 and the Cox frailty model was constructed using the survival package (V3.8–6) in R (version 4.4.0).

Population-based survival curves based on the Kaplan-Meier method

We generated prognostic survival curves for populations stratified by serum bicarbonate intervals using the Kaplan-Meier method. This analysis was implemented in R (version 4.4.0) using the survival package (V3.8–6) and survminer package (V0.5.2).

Cross-cohort validation and sensitivity analysis

The MIMIC-IV dataset served as the discovery set, while the eICU-CRD dataset from the US and the YHD-HOSP dataset from China provided the external validation. We first segmented the eICU-CRD and YHD-HOSP populations using the three bicarbonate critical intervals identified in MIMIC-IV. Cox regression and Cox frailty modeling were then used to compute the HRs within these bicarbonate intervals for the validation cohorts. These HRs were compared to those of the discovery cohort to evaluate the reproducibility of the identified serum bicarbonate thresholds associated with mortality. Furthermore, we generated Kaplan-Meier survival curves stratified by bicarbonate intervals for the validation cohorts and compared them to the discovery cohort curves to determine the consistency in survival probability patterns.

In the MIMIC-IV database, 29,102 patients (40.3%) had missing pH records. Therefore, the pH values were initially excluded from model adjustment to avoid the impact of a significant amount of missing data on the results. However, considering the significance of acid-base factors in this study, we conducted a sensitivity analysis for pH. In this analysis, 29,102 patients with missing pH data were excluded, and the remaining 43,164 patients with recorded pH values were included in the multivariate Cox regression analysis to assess the impact of pH on the outcome.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Deep learning-based characterization of individual patient survival curves

We developed a deep learning survival model, Causal SurvivalNet, based on Cox-Time architecture. This approach replaces the linear predictor term (βTXT) of the traditional Cox model with a multi-layer neural network, allowing it to capture complex, nonlinear relationships between patient features and mortality risk. Our analysis utilized the Anaconda platform (V3), with source code design accomplished through the Pycox (V0.2.3) software development kit (SDK), the PyTorch framework (V1.12.1), and Python programming language (V3.9.0). Unlike a neural network, the traditional cox model assumes a predetermined linear relationship between covariates and the log-hazard, and the model’s task is to estimate the coefficient vector βT. However, for linear functions, we can replace βTXT with θTXT mathematically, where the neural network weights θ are related to the CPH model. To expedite the convergence of the model, we sequentially incorporated the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU)34,35 activation function and batch normalization layer downstream of the FC layer. The ReLU activation function is defined as y = max⁡(0,x), where x is the input value and y are the output value. The ReLU function offers advantages such as computational simplicity, ease of gradient calculation, and effective mitigation of the vanishing gradient problem. The batch normalization layer normalizes input data within each batch, transforming it into a standard normal distribution with a mean of 0 and variance of 1, which helps alleviate gradient vanishing issues.

Model performance was evaluated using the Concordance index (CI) and Integrated Brier score (IBS). The Concordance index (CI) evaluates the model’s discriminative ability. It measures the proportion of patient pairs for which the model correctly predicts that the patient with the higher risk score experienced the event earlier. assesses model calibration and overall accuracy. It calculates the average squared difference between the predicted survival probabilities and the actual outcomes (0 for survival, 1 for event) over a range of time points. We utilized the EvalSurv method in the Pycox 0.2.3 library to calculate the CI and IBS.

To ensure the robustness and generalizability of the deep learning survival model, this study implemented a two-stage validation strategy adhering to established prognostic modeling standards in critical care. The MIMIC-IV dataset served as the primary development cohort, wherein a 5-fold cross-validation scheme was employed to optimize hyperparameters and mitigate overfitting. To assess cross-population adaptability, independent external validation was conducted across two heterogeneous cohorts (eICU-CRD and YHD-HOSP). Model performance was rigorously quantified using the CI and IBS, thereby capturing both discriminative power and calibration accuracy.
