The Herbicide Crisis: Addressing the High Fatality of Paraquat Poisoning
Authors: Gunchan Paul
Published: 2026

In the agricultural belts of South India, a silently toxic, emerald-colored, non-selective contact herbicide remains readily available on the shelves of local shops. Paraquat (PQ), a highly effective killer, has become a “poison potion” for many. According to the latest data released by the National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB), Telangana recorded the highest number of suicides due to insecticide consumption in India in 2024.1 The figure accounts for nearly 16% of the country's total insecticide-related suicides, accounting to 6,921 out of 24,252 deaths. Telangana is closely followed by Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Madhya Pradesh. One out of every four such nationwide deaths come from the two Telugu States, Telangana and Andhra Pradesh and PQ has emerged as one of the most common substances involved in such fatal poisoning cases.2

In many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including India, it is a prevalent weapon for purposeful self-harm with a high fatality rate ranging from 50 to 90%.3 The recent study from a tertiary ICU in South India published in this issue of ISCCM offers a dual-pronged message, of hope and urgent call to action. While the medical protocol may help in improving survival rates, the ultimate solution lies in public awareness and government restriction on this lethal chemical.

Biology of Suffocation

To understand why intensivists fear PQ, it is important to know its toxokinetics. Once ingested—even in quantities as small as 20–40 mg/kg—the poison triggers the process of redox cycling, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS).4 While these free radicals cause significant injury to the liver and kidneys, their primary target is the lung. The result is gradually progressive, irreversible pulmonary fibrosis, leading to a slow and agonizing death from respiratory failure. It is incredibly difficult and emotionally disturbing for any physician to witness the conscious patient exhibiting such a progressive decline, characterized by escalating oxygen requirements as they approach end-of-life.

“Golden Period”: Why Every Minute Counts

The concentration of PQ is many folds in the lungs than in the plasma, due to the energy-dependent uptake of PQ by the alveoli. There are two main reasons related to the kinetics of PQ that suggest the benefits of extracorporeal treatment of PQ poisoning will be very limited beyond the Golden period of 2–4 hours.5 Firstly, the endogenous renal clearance of PQ is quite high in the initial hours, before renal failure sets in, and thus most PQ is eliminated rapidly and any additional method of elimination have relatively modest effect on plasma concentration. Secondly, the timeframe in which the increased elimination would have an impact on the distribution into the lungs is very short. Data from previous studies and the one published in this volume also show that commencing hemodialysis/hemoperfusion (HD/HP) after 2–4 hours does remove PQ from the plasma compartment but reduction of PQ taken up by the lungs is negligible beyond this time window and hence, unlikely to change overall patient outcome. Retrospective data from our own center of one hundred of cases of PQ demonstrated a high mortality rate as only few reached the hospital within this golden time frame.6

The choice of method for enhanced elimination has also been studied, with literature favoring HP as being moderately superior in clearance to HD.7 In an experimental model, while, the plasma PQ clearance remains static with HD, it was reduced substantially by 4 hours of HP in both survivors (80%) and non-survivors (76%).7 However, the primary factor to consider is initiating the therapy early, and the choice of method is secondary.

The most critical finding of the recent retrospective study of 27 patients is the absolute necessity of early intervention. Among those who survived, 71.4% reached the hospital within 4 hours of ingestion.8 In contrast, only 20% of those who presented after that window managed to survive. The results of this study echo with the author's own experience from retrospective data collection, which showed that 36% of the patients who received HP survived (p = 0.03) and time interval from ingestion of PQ to presentation to the hospital had significant relation with mortality (p = 0.001).6

Breakthrough in Resource-Limited Settings

The study published in this issue introduces a low-cost bedside decision-making framework to guide the clinician managing PQ poisoning patients. The urine sodium dithionite test (UDT) that is used to confirm PQ poisoning, is a rapid, inexpensive colorimetric assay that changes the color of urine from light yellow–green–blue–black according to the concentration of PQ present.9 This semiquantitative nature of the test has been utilized to formulate a protocol to guide the need for hemoperfusion.

The quantitative methods that measure PQ levels in plasma, urine, or gastric aspirate using different techniques help to confirm the diagnosis and are used to indicate severity or outcome. These levels are used to construct the Proudfoot curve or calculate severity index of PQ poisoning scores (SIPP score), which are best predictors of survival after self-poisoning by PQ.10 However, these quantitative analyses are available only in specialized laboratories that have limited availability, at least not within the time frame to guide critical interventions, apart from being prohibitively expensive.

Hence, in the Indian ICU context, the UDT-guided protocol would help to guide clinicians in secondary and tertiary hospitals to make evidence-based decisions at the bedside without waiting for laboratory results. The results of this study show that by serially repeating the UDT at regular intervals, clinicians were able to guide the initiation, continuation, and cessation of hemoperfusion. With a median of 3.3 sessions of charcoal hemoperfusion for survivors, compared to 2.6 for non-survivors, a 51.9% survival rate was achieved. This is a significant improvement, as in our experience, many poor patients do not afford to continue treatment beyond a few sessions of hemoperfusion.

Call for Government Action

While medical science continues research and brings hope to the dismal outcome of PQ ingestion, there are limitations to medicine. 80% of non-survivors initially presented with acute kidney injury, multi-organ dysfunction syndrome occurred exclusively in those who did not make it and about 50% succumbed despite best medical treatment.

Is it not high time that we ask ourselves why we should settle for 50% survival, if deaths from pesticide poisoning are totally preventable? It is not time for India to follow other LMICS and remove PQ from its soil once and for all? With the demographics reporting the mean age of the patients as 30.5 years, the study shows that PQ remains a major public health concern because of absence of strict regulatory efforts.11 No amount of medical innovation can substitute for the primary prevention of access to such a potent toxin.

Recently in March 2026, a coordinated public health movement driven by the coalition of medical professionals and legal advocates in Telangana aimed at eliminating the use of PQ culminated in a landmark 60-day ban on PQ in Telangana.12 The central argument for this initiative was that preventing access to hazardous pesticides is the primary strategy for reducing suicide rates and agricultural accidents. These efforts transitioned from hospital-level to formal legal action and high-level political engagement. Although the ban was temporary, it sets an example for national reform, demonstrating how state-level leadership can prioritize human life over hazardous chemical use.

Conclusion: The Triple Mandate (Fig. 1)

The triple mandate - public awareness, quick medical protocolized action, and government regulatory actions are essential steps to bring down mortality by paraquat poisoning

The path forward requires a triple mandate involving the public, the medical community, and the stakeholders at national level:

For the Public: Awareness that PQ is not just another pesticide; it is a very lethal poison as compared to other herbicides. It is fatal following ingestion of a very small amount, as the saying goes, One Sip Can Kill.13 It has no known antidote. Public awareness must focus on this singular truth: PQ ingestion is a medical emergency where delay of even a few hours can be the difference between life and death.
