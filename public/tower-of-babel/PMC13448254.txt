Near-infrared stress memory emitters enable delayed impact visualization in illuminated environments
Authors: Xin Zhang, Shuohan Li, Hao Suo, Yu Zhang, Yu Wang, Li Li, Feng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rapid and accurate mechanical detection is essential across numerous fields. However, conventional camera-based methods, which depend on computationally intensive analysis of high-frame-rate footage and predictive algorithms, are often time-consuming and can introduce interpretive errors that compromise reliability. Herein, we present a class of near-infrared stress memory emitters, allowing fast and visualized mechanical detection in ambient environments. We develop Ca(Sr)ZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+ crystals and achieve persistent mechanoluminescence at 981 nm by combinatorial engineering of sub-bandgap states. Deliberate modulation of the persistent mechanoluminescence intensity and duration is achieved through isostructural host blending coupled with prescribed ultraviolet charging, enabling direct capture and visualization of ball impacts with short processing time (0.39 s) and high accuracy. The bright (up to 11 × 107 photons per event) and durable (up to 100 s) persistent mechanoluminescence broadens the scope of optical materials in applications such as stress sensors, human-machine interfaces, and mechano-opto-electronics.

Introduction

Accurate mechanical detection is vital in sports, engineering, and smart interactive devices. One of the most widely adopted technologies for tracking dynamic events is the Hawk-Eye system, a computer vision platform that reconstructs the motion of balls or athletes by triangulating and combining videos from multiple high-speed cameras1. This technology has been implemented in various ball sports, such as tennis, badminton, and football, to assist referees in making accurate decisions and minimizing disputes. A typical Hawk-Eye system comprises several high-speed video cameras strategically positioned around the playing area. These cameras capture visual image sequences, which are fed into computer algorithms to build a comprehensive view of ball movement, predicting its future path and landing point on the court. These data are invaluable for officiating to speed up decisions and clarify evidence, as well as for athletes and coaches to gain detailed performance analysis. Mechanoluminescence (ML) materials can produce photon emissions in response to various types of mechanical stress or deformation, such as friction, compression, tension, and impact, providing a visualization solution for mechanical sensing2–4. ML has been observed in a variety of natural and synthetic materials, including organic and inorganic compounds5, with emission colors ranging from ultraviolet to near-infrared6. The physical principles governing ML are diverse, including discharge due to material fracture7, triboelectric interaction8–11 and piezoelectric response during elastic deformation12. Among these, piezoelectric-induced ML is recognized as an inherent property of the material and has been extensively investigated for its robust and repeatable behavior as seen in materials like ZnS:Mn2+ (Ref. 12), SrAl2O4:Eu2+/Dy3+ (Ref. 13), CaZnOS:Mn2+ (Ref. 14), LiNbO3:Pr3+ (Ref. 15) and Sr3Sn2O7:Nd3+ (Ref. 16). The direct mechano-optical transduction property has been utilized in applications like self-sustaining lighting and displays17, stress sensing, memory and visualization18–20, human-machine interfaces21–23 and biomedical engineering24–27.

ML is a dynamic phenomenon, whose intensity is proportional to the stress/strain level and its changing rates28,29. This transient nature of ML necessitates that the equipment used to capture ML signals must be fast-responding, and physically present during mechanical stimulation30. This limitation consequently makes it elusive to analyze the complete pressure event, which also restricts the broader application of ML in practical scenarios (Fig. 1b). Persistent mechanoluminescence (Pers-ML) offers a solution to overcome the drawbacks of instantaneous ML by providing a delayed acquisition and prolonged sensing approach31, where the mechanically induced luminescence can last for an extended period in a Pers-ML material. However, Pers-ML is currently only available in limited ML materials, such as SrAl2O4:Eu2+/Dy3+ (Ref. 32) and BaSi2O2N2:Eu2+ (Refs. 33,34), which exclusively emit in the visible spectrum35,36. The unfavorable emission property represents a significant obstacle to real-world applications due to strong ambient light interference in the visible spectrum. While near-infrared (NIR) emissions also experience ambient interference, particularly from sunlight, the relatively weaker NIR background compared to the intense visible component can be advantageous for signal discrimination.

In this study, we report a design principle for achieving intense and long-lasting near-infrared (NIR) Pers-ML in CaZnOS:Yb3+, a prototype instantaneous ML material37. In complementary to the conventional approach of screening ML among persistent luminescence (PersL) materials, our strategy involves precise control over charge carrier storage in the well-known ML host. We show that the Pers-ML performance can be optimized by tuning the trap distribution and filling, achieved through isostructural blending of CaZnOS and SrZnOS. Benefiting from the superior NIR Pers-ML in the Ca(Sr)ZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+ crystals with a long duration of 100 seconds, we develop an integrated monitoring system that directly visualizes impact events using a delayed optical signal in ambient environments (Fig. 1c).

Results

Experimental setup and measurement protocol

CaZnOS is a well-established host material for instantaneous ML. By selectively doping it with lanthanide (i.e., Pr3+, Tb3+, Ho3+, Dy3+, Eu3+, Sm3+, Er3+, Tm3+, Nd3+, and Yb3+), transition-metal (e.g., Mn2+ and Cu+) or main group (e.g., Bi3+ and Sb3+) ions, tunable ML across the visible and near-infrared is achieved with high brightness and reproducibility6,38. However, the “persistent” component of ML has never been investigated in this material system or its derivatives (e.g., SrZnOS and BaZnOS). We choose CaZnOS:Yb3+ as the base material because of its outstanding instantaneous ML property in the NIR. Our central hypothesis was that the intrinsic trap structure governing carrier storage could be deliberately engineered to convert the instantaneous ML into a long-lasting Pers-ML signal. Consequently, Pb2+ was explored as a co-dopant for its remarkable effect on promoting trap filling in CaZnOS39. This design represents a targeted defect engineering, moving beyond simple doping towards the controlled creation of a specific trap population optimized for Pers-ML.

For comparative investigation, CaZnOS:Yb3+ (1%) and CaZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+ (1/0.6%) were synthesized using a solid-state reaction method. The microcrystals were examined using X-ray diffraction (XRD), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and scanning electron microscope (SEM), confirming their single-phase hexagonal structure as CaZnOS (ICSD #245309) (Supplementary Fig. 1–3). Notably, the Li+-based flux agent (Li2CO3) was added together with the reaction reagents at a mole ratio of 10%, which largely promotes the incorporation of Yb3+ into the CaZnOS lattice and reduces Yb2O3 impurities. Luminescence properties were also assessed in both samples. We detected the characteristic Pb2+ band in the photoluminescence excitation (PLE) spectrum by monitoring Yb3+ emission (981 nm), indicating intense energy exchange between Pb2+ and Yb3+ (Supplementary Fig. 4). Significantly, we noted an appreciable enhancement of PersL intensity and duration in CaZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+ than the CaZnOS:Yb3+ counterpart, implying a substantial amplification of charge carrier storage that ensures Pers-ML (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Our investigation aimed to quantitatively analyze the Pers-ML delivered by multiple samples. To this end, we developed a specialized measurement system to accurately detect and measure the light emitted from these samples under mechanical action. The system includes a customized mechanical control unit, a quartz sample holder, a NIR camera, and a fiber-coupled charge-coupled device (CCD) spectrometer (Fig. 2a). Specifically, the powder samples were accurately weighed (0.001 mol) and sealed between two ethylene-vinyl acetate (EVA) coated polyethylene terephthalate (PET) sheets to form a square film (2.5 cm × 2.5 cm). During the measurement, the Pers-ML film was first exposed to UV light for charging, followed by a period of delay time to diminish the interference from PersL (Fig. 2b). The NIR camera was then switched on concurrently with the initiation of mechanical stimulation by the movement of a metal indenter attached to a force gauge (lasting ~0.5 s). The NIR camera was set to successively capture images at a fixed time interval of 0.5 seconds. Pers-ML behaviors in CaZnOS crystals

We first conducted a comparative study of the Pers-ML between CaZnOS:Yb3+ and CaZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+. The results, as shown in Fig. 2d, highlight the occurrence of instantaneous ML in both samples (0 s). This phenomenon was noted to be independent of whether the testing film had been subjected to prior UV charging, suggesting an inherent ML property in response to mechanical stimuli. The pronounced increase in ML intensity after UV charging points to the presence of metastable trap states within the material. These states are also responsive to mechanical energy, thus leading to brighter ML (Supplementary Fig. 6). However, a marked contrast was observed in the Pers-ML between the two samples. CaZnOS:Yb3+ exhibits minimal Pers-ML after 30 seconds of charging, whereas CaZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+ demonstrated a pronounced Pers-ML under the same experimental conditions. The Pers-ML decay curves derived from a sequence of Pers-ML images signify the robust and durable PersL-ML characteristic of the CaZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+ sample (Fig. 2e). The comparison substantiates the close correlation of Pers-ML with charge carrier storage, which is improved after Pb2+ co-doping. Furthermore, spectral analyses reveal the sole Yb3+ emission for both instantaneous ML and Pers-ML in CaZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+, indicative of a consistent recombination process (Fig. 2f).

Subsequent control experiments were carried out to validate the trap-controlled nature of Pers-ML in CaZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+. As depicted in Fig. 2g, the charging duration was found to significantly influence Pers-ML, with a tendency to reach saturation at prolonged charging times (Supplementary Fig. 7). Thermoluminescence (TL) intensity followed a pattern analogous to Pers-ML when varying the charging time, indicating a close relationship between the stored charge carriers and Pers-ML (Supplementary Fig. 8). With a fixed charging time, the interval before commencing measurements also influences Pers-ML behavior, with both Pers-ML and TL exhibiting a comparable decline as the delay time increases (Supplementary Fig. 9, 10). Notably, the apparent discrepancy where the initial instantaneous ML signal seems less sensitive to delay time than the subsequent Pers-ML, stems from a measurement artifact. In the 8‑bit NIR images used to derive Pers-ML decay, the bright impact flash often saturates the sensor, forcing the first data point (t = 0 s) to a maximum digital count (255). This saturation obscures the true decay of the instantaneous ML and the associated trap depletion. Direct spectroscopy confirms that the instantaneous ML peak does decrease with longer delays (Supplementary Fig. 11), aligning with trap-emptying kinetics. These results reinforce that Pers‑ML is a trap‑controlled process in this system.

We next captured the PersL excitation spectra to investigate how Pb2+ modifies the trapping pathway of the CaZnOS crystals (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 12). The CaZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+ displays a marked increase in PersL intensity compared to the CaZnOS:Yb3+ throughout the entire excitation wavelength range. Moreover, a distinct broad band peaking at 350 nm was observed in the CaZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+, which was assigned to the Pb2+ excitation according to the corresponding PLE spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 4). These findings suggest that Pb2+ plays an essential role in enhancing the trap-filling process. The PL decay serves as a reliable measure of the energy exchange between Pb2+ and Yb3+, showing a significant reduction of Pb2+ lifetime from 92.8 ns to 54.0 ns upon the inclusion of Yb3+ (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 13). The obviously shortened lifetime of Pb2+ can be attributed to two aspects: resonant energy transfer to Yb3+ (as a sensitizer) and direct charge transfer to Yb3+-associated trap states (as a trap filler; vide infra). The latter interaction contributes to a marked increase in energy storage capacity. This enhancement is strongly dependent on the excitation pathway. Under excitation at 365 nm, which corresponds to the Pb2+ absorption band, the integrated TL intensity shows a nearly 20‑fold amplification compared to the Yb3+ singly doped sample (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Figs. 14, 15). This dramatic increase highlights the exceptional trap-filling efficiency of Pb2+. A more modest (approximately threefold) enhancement occurs under host‑band excitation (Fig. 3a). Further investigation into the role of Yb3+ ions in the Pers-ML process was conducted by examining a set of CaZnOS:Ln3+/Pb2+ (Ln = Er, Nd, or Tm) crystals (Supplementary Fig. 16, 17). These samples showed no detectable Pers-ML signal (Fig. 3d), despite the presence of strong instantaneous ML (Supplementary Fig. 18). These observations suggest much lower carrier storage capacity in these samples compared to the CaZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+ counterpart, which is confirmed by TL analysis (Fig. 3e, f). Notably, we recorded a distinct TL glow curve for CaZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+ compared to CaZnOS:Ln3+/Pb2+ (Ln = Er, Nd, or Tm) (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 19), indicating different trap state characteristics across these samples. Therefore, the large carrier storage capacity in CaZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+ could be attributed to the particular role of Yb3+ ions in establishing electron traps by virtue of their high electron-accepting tendency40–43. The presence of a photochromic absorption band, revealed by comparing diffuse reflectance spectra of CaZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+ before and after a 5-minute charge under 365 nm light (Supplementary Fig. 20), confirms photo-induced charge carrier trapping by Yb3+ ions44. In this connection, the inclusion of Li2CO3 flux was essential to achieve bright Pers-ML by facilitating Yb3+ doping into the CaZnOS lattice. In the absence of Li+-based flux, the Pers-ML performance was substantially degraded due to a simultaneous decrease in the number of trap states and emission centers (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 21).

The experimental observation strongly supports a mechanistic model where: (1) Yb3+ is uniquely essential as the electron trapping center, as evidenced by the negligible Pers-ML observed when Pb2+ is co-doped with other lanthanides (Er3+, Tm3+, Nd3+) despite confirmed optical interactions (Supplementary Fig. 17); and (2) Pb2+ acts primarily as an efficient trap filler, facilitating transfer of excited electrons to pre-existing Yb3+-associated trap states rather than creating new traps (Supplementary Fig. 22). This behavior is consistent with the isovalent substitution of Pb2+ for Ca2+, which precludes charge-compensating defects. Based on the fact that Yb3+ acts both as an electron trapping center and as an emitter, we adopt a hole detrapping PersL/Pers-ML model, in which holes are thermally or mechanically released from the charge compensation or intrinsic host defect sites (e.g., zinc vacancies or oxygen‑related defects) and subsequently recombine with trapped electrons (at Yb2+ sites) to produce characteristic NIR emission at 981 nm.

In-situ TL measurement was employed to provide more insight into the behavior of trapped charge carriers in response to mechanical stimuli. As shown in Fig. 3h, the notable decrease in TL intensity in the 360–420 K range suggests that mechanical action releases charge carriers from medium traps, resulting in the observed instantaneous ML. Simultaneously, the TL signal in the shallow trap region (300–325 K) increases by up to 35% relative to the unstimulated control, demonstrating that a portion of the released carriers is re‑trapped into shallow traps rather than recombining immediately. This net increase represents an unbalanced rate between de-trapping by mechanical load and re-trapping from medium traps; similar trap redistribution effects have been quantified in other persistent phosphors (e.g., a 20% increase in deep trap occupation upon pressure application in BaSi2O2N2:Eu2+)18. The effect becomes even more pronounced when mechanical stimulation is applied after a 30‑minute delay following UV pre‑irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 23), where the shallow trap TL intensity increases by ~350% compared to the unstimulated control. This striking enhancement directly confirms that mechanical action refills shallow traps from medium‑depth reservoirs. The shallow traps then gradually release the captured charge carriers under ambient conditions, sustaining the Pers‑ML phenomenon. The intricate interplay between these metastable traps and their role in the luminescence properties of the material is further illustrated in Fig. 3i, which encapsulates the experimental observations and summarizes the proposed Pers-ML mechanism.

Storage capacity manipulation for brighter Pers-ML

The identification of trap filling and trap state allows us to regulate the storage capacity through host compound engineering. Accordingly, we explored extended control of Pers-ML intensity by manipulating the storage capacity through isostructural host blending45. This modification was enabled by the similar crystal structures of CaZnOS and SrZnOS, both of which crystallize in the hexagonal wurtzite structure. We prepared a series of intermediate phases of Ca(Sr)ZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+ with Sr mole fractions ranging from 0 to 0.85, which were verified to be single hexagonal structures (Supplementary Fig. 24). According to the initial evaluation following UV charging, we observed a significant increase in PersL intensity as the Sr alloying content reached 0.70 (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Figs. 25, 26). The TL glow peaks in Ca(Sr)ZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+ exhibit a non-monotonic shift along with Sr alloying, characterized by an initial movement to higher temperatures followed by a shift to lower temperatures (Fig. 4b). This phenomenon is consistently reflected in Ca(Sr)ZnOS:Yb3+, indicating that Pb2+ only influences the trap-filling process without introducing new hole traps. As supporting evidence, the TL glow curves recorded for Yb3+ singly and Yb3+/Pb2+ co‑doped Ca(Sr)ZnOS alloys are essentially identical (Supplementary Figs. 27, 29c-f). Note that the Pb2+-induced alteration of the TL profile from two peaks to a single peak in Ca‑rich compositions arises from selective filling of the existing hole trap manifold via direct electron transfer from Pb2+ to nearby Yb3+, rather than from modification of the underlying hole trap distribution (Supplementary Figs. 27, 29). Therefore, the non-monotonic shift observed in the co-doped system could be rationalized by considering the Yb3+ singly doped Ca(Sr)ZnOS as a model system. We propose that this shift arises from the antagonistic effects of Sr incorporation on two different recombination pathways, namely local model and global model (Supplementary Fig. 30). For the localized charge transfer associated with the ~375 K TL peak in CaZnOS:Yb3+, lattice expansion induced by Sr substitution systematically increase the trap depth; subsequently, at higher Sr concentrations, increased physical separation between the trap center and the activator ion reduces the probability of localized recombination46, effectively diminishing the observable de-trapping signal for this peak (Supplementary Fig. 27). Conversely, the high‑temperature TL peak (~460 K in CaZnOS:Yb3+) is dominated by delocalized recombination via the valence band; Sr‑induced bandgap narrowing lowers the thermal detrapping barrier, shifting this peak to lower temperatures. The net observed shift in the overall TL peak results from the competing effects of these two mechanisms: initial dominance of local transition, followed by the increasing influence of bandgap reduction on the global transition. This variation in the de-trapping mechanism explains the non-monotonic peak evolution. While further studies are needed to fully validate this model, it consistently explains all experimental observations.

The Pers-ML properties of the Ca(Sr)ZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+ crystals were subsequently assessed using the experimental protocol depicted in Fig. 2b, setting the charging and waiting times at 30 and 60 seconds, respectively. In line with our expectation, we observed a steady enhancement of Pers-ML as the Sr ratio increased to 0.55. However, a decline in Pers-ML performance was detected with further Sr addition (Fig. 4d, e). We attribute the noticeably incompatible trends between Pers-ML and PersL to their dissimilar dependence on charge carriers at specific trap levels. As evidenced in Fig. 3h, Pers-ML primarily stems from charge carriers escaped from medium traps by mechanical stimulation, whereas PersL is dominated by charge carriers released from shallow traps in the ambient thermal field. To validate our hypothesis, the TL glow curves were further analyzed by integrating the intensity within specified temperature ranges, corresponding to shallow (300–360 K) and medium (360–420 K) trap depths (Fig. 4c). As anticipated, the results respectively reflect the trends of Pers-ML and PersL with varying Sr contents in the host materials.

The non-monotonic variation of the TL intensity, and thus the Pers-ML performance, in Ca(Sr)ZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+ stems from multiple effects of Sr2+ alloying. Primarily, Sr2+ incorporation non-monotonically modifies trap distribution, as revealed by the erratic evolution of TL glow peaks (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 27–30). For Sr contents exceeding the optimal value (x > 0.55), further trap shallowing leads to rapid thermal leakage of charge carriers upon mechanical action, which attenuates the subsequent persistent ML. In addition, Sr-induced structural changes elevate the luminescence efficiency of Yb3+ (Supplementary Fig. 31). Furthermore, Sr substitution also influences the promoting effect of Pb2+ on the trap-filling process during optical charging (Supplementary Fig. 32). The synergy of these effects (i.e., non-monotonically modified trap distribution, boosted luminescence efficiency of Yb3+, and altered trap-filling efficiency) explains the non-monotonic Pers-ML trend. Accordingly, the Pers-ML lifetime and the total Pers-ML photon output for the optimal composition were assessed, revealing a long Pers-ML duration of over 100 seconds and a maximum photon yield of ~11×107 photons per Pers-ML event (Supplementary Tables 1, 2).

Uninterrupted operation for interference-free mechanical visualization

Before adopting these stress memory emitters into practical scenarios, the charging protocol needs to be reassessed. Given that Pers-ML is a trap-controlled luminescence process, typical measurement involves pre-charging with high-intensity UV light to generate a detectable signal47, which inevitably demands an extended charging duration to ensure adequate trap filling. Additionally, to avoid interference from PersL, a subsequent delay period is required to remove the interference of PersL, leaving behind pure Pers-ML signals. The charging and delay processes result in a period during which the Pers-ML emitter is unusable, referred to as dead time (Fig. 5a). Moreover, Pers-ML continues to decay due to the dissipation of trapped charge carriers (Fig. 2g), necessitating frequent recharging to maintain sufficient optical output. To tackle these issues, we implemented an unsaturated charging scheme using a pulsed light source. This method involves constant charging of the Pers-ML materials to a steady state, corresponding to a dynamic equilibrium between trapping and releasing of charge carriers (Fig. 5b). Notably, the dynamic equilibrium, which dictates the steady-state luminescence intensity, can be precisely tailored by modifying the charging pulse width or frequency (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 33). Each equilibrium state can be reached within seconds upon switching on the pulsed excitation, implying negligible dead time for Pers-ML operation. The charging technique is further analyzed by examining luminescence photographs of the Pers-ML film under pulse charging at 100 Hz with varying pulse widths (Fig. 5d). The results show increased brightness and mean grayscale value per pixel with larger pulse widths, approaching the digital count limit (Fig. 5e). Further increasing the pulse width will overexpose the NIR camera, blocking ML/Pers-ML detection. Accordingly, a specific pulse width could be selected to ensure sufficient Pers-ML intensity while minimizing the interference of background signals (Fig. 5b). To achieve this, a free-falling ball impact test was integrated into the pulsed light charging experiment (varying pulse widths at a fixed frequency of 100 Hz). The results, as depicted in Fig. 5f, show that Pers-ML intensity is positively related to pulse width, that is, the time-averaged charging intensity. Meanwhile, the background signal also steadily ascends and becomes overwhelming at pulse widths above 0.5 ms. Thus, a critical pulse width of 0.5 ms was selected to maximize charging efficiency (trap filling) while maintaining sufficient SNR before it approaches zero at higher pulse widths (Fig. 5f, g).

The low-intensity short-pulse charging approach bypasses the dead time encountered in saturated charging, enabling continuous and uninterrupted Pers-ML detection. This method streamlines the Pers-ML operation process, which inspired us to develop an integrated system for ball impact monitoring (Supplementary Fig. 34). Benefiting from the NIR emission characteristic, the system can function in ambient environments without interference from surrounding light (a household LED source with 1500 lux illuminance, Supplementary Figs. 35, 36). In a proof-of-concept illustration, stainless steel balls (Ø 10 mm; drop height: 5–30 cm) were used to simulate ball impacts to the playing area, in conjunction with a NIR camera, a 365 nm LED source, an arbitrary waveform generator, and a Pers-ML film to probe the impact event (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Movie 1). As shown in Fig. 6b, the Ca(Sr)ZnOS:Yb/Pb recorder efficiently visualizes ball impact point sequences via the NIR channel in real time, regardless of ambient lighting. In comparison, using Ca(Sr)ZnOS:Yb as the recorder failed to capture ball impact points due to the absence of Pers-ML signal (Fig. 6b, middle panel). The instantaneous ML signal is obscured by the ball at the instant of impact (Supplementary Fig. 37), preventing camera recording. Furthermore, an algorithm was developed to automatically pinpoint the contact location. A sharpening and Gaussian blurring kernel were successively convolved with the original NIR image (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Figs. 39, 40), which enabled precise coordinate detection (Fig. 6d). Accordingly, 60 consecutively recorded images were processed offline using our location identification algorithm on a consumer laptop (Apple M1, 8 GB RAM), achieving near-complete reconstruction of impact scenarios (Supplementary Figs. 41, 42). The average per-frame processing time, including convolution, detection, and coordinate transformation, was 0.39 seconds, significantly faster than the typical Hawk-Eye system with tens of seconds of review time. On a side note, the long lifetime (up to 100 sec) Pers-ML raises concerns about signal overlap in rapid multi-impact scenarios. Our solution leverages spatial discrimination to isolate individual impacts and intensity-based prioritization (brightest spot corresponds to freshest impact) to ensure correct temporal sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 43). This approach effectively resolves ambiguity, enabling reliable detection even during consecutive impacts. The key advancement of our prototype impact monitoring system lies in its ability to accurately track ball impact points in a delayed manner under challenging lighting (indoor or indirect sunlight with ~1800 lux illuminance), enabled by the NIR Pers-ML signal from Ca(Sr)ZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+, which eliminates visible light interference and offers a solution for delayed force detection (Supplementary Figs. 44–46).

Critically, supplementary measurement confirms that the Pers-ML emission intensity of optimized films enables reliable NIR signal capture at distances of 2–5 meters (Supplementary Fig. 47). Despite this appreciable brightness for long‑distance NIR signal capture, impact tests using a standard tennis ball produced no detectable Pers‑ML signal under the tested conditions (impact speed up to ~100 km h-1). This result indicates that, while the material is highly sensitive to sharp, concentrated impacts (e.g., a steel ball) and suitable for distance‑based NIR detection, its current performance is insufficient for real-time sports line-calling applications such as Hawk-Eye. Nevertheless, this study provides a practical benchmark and offers experimental guidance for future sensitivity improvements.

Discussion

Although the principle of detecting a mechanically induced afterglow has been demonstrated18,32–34,48,49, existing Pers-ML materials emit exclusively in the visible spectrum and often lack the combination of brightness and duration required for robust, delayed signal acquisition under ambient lighting. Our work directly addresses this bottleneck by creating a Pers-ML material that operates in the near-infrared. This strategic shift to the NIR window is crucial, as it renders the signal inherently resistant to interference from ambient visible light, a decisive advantage for applications in illuminated environments. Furthermore, the Pers-ML in our Ca(Sr)ZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+ system is characterized by an intense, trap-mediated afterglow lasting from seconds to over one hundred seconds, with a photon yield rivaling that of bright visible benchmarks (~24 × 107 photons recorded for SrAl2O4:Eu2+/Dy3+ under identical evaluation conditions). This performance combination, i.e., NIR emission, high brightness, and long duration, transforms Pers-ML from a laboratory observation into a practical tool for post-event mechanical sensing. It enables the optical retrieval of impact information well after the stress event has ceased and even in the presence of strong ambient light (1800 lux), thereby expanding the potential for ML-based technologies in scenarios where real-time detection is impractical or where visible light would overwhelm the signal.

In conclusion, the investigation of stress memory in Ca(Sr)ZnOS:Yb/Pb crystals enhances our understanding of the underlying mechanisms that govern mechanically induced luminescence in condensed matter. Our research thus provides a framework for converting conventional instantaneous ML to long-lasting Pers-ML through storage capacity manipulation, which may inspire the exploration of novel Pers-ML emitters in alternative material systems, such as organic dye, polymer, or glass. The Ca(Sr)ZnOS: Yb/Pb with bright Pers-ML, long lifetime of 100 seconds, and NIR emission characteristics, outperforming visible Pers‑ML phosphors by minimizing interference from common indoor lighting sources (e.g., LED and fluorescent lamps). The ball impact monitoring system, featuring fast response and easy operation, demonstrates the high applicability of the NIR Pers-ML emitter in real-world scenarios. Consequently, our work opens new possibilities for delayed mechanical detection across various applications, such as stress sensors, human-machine interfaces, and battery-free mechano-opto-electronics.

Methods

Reagents

CaCO3 (≥99.0%, Sigma-Aldrich), SrCO3 (≥99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), ZnS (99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich), PbCl2 (≥99.99%, Aladdin), YbF3 (99.9%, Alfa Aesar), ErF3 (99.9%, Alfa Aesar), NdF3 (99.9%, Alfa Aesar), TmF3 (99.9%, Alfa Aesar), Li2CO3 (≥99%, Aladdin), Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, SYLGARDTM 184 Silicone Elastomer) and its curing agent, were used as received without further processing.

Preparation of Pers-ML phosphors

Doped Ca(Sr)ZnOS crystals were synthesized using a high-temperature solid-state reaction method. For the synthesis of Ca1-xSrxZnOS:1%Yb3+/0.6%Pb2+ (x = 0–0.85) crystals, stoichiometric amounts of raw materials (CaCO3, SrCO3, ZnS, YbF3 and PbCl2) and Li2CO3 flux (10% Li+ unless otherwise stated) were weighed, thoroughly mixed, and heat-treated (1000 °C for 3 h) in a tube furnace under a constant flow of N2 gas (100 mL min−1). After naturally cooling to room temperature, the samples were ground into fine powders and stored in a desiccator for further characterization. The synthesis methods for CaZnOS:Yb3+ and CaZnOS:Ln3+/Pb2+ (Ln = Er, Nd or Tm) were similar to that of Ca(Sr)ZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+, except for the substitution of the corresponding raw materials.

General characterization of Pers-ML phosphors

X-ray diffraction patterns were recorded on a Bruker D2 phaser X-ray diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy spectra were measured on a Thermo Scientific ESCALAB Xi+ X-ray photoelectron spectrometer microprobe. Scanning electron microscopy analysis was performed on a JEOL SEM (JSM-IT500). Photoluminescence excitation, photoluminescence emission spectra, decay curves, and persistent luminescence decay curves were recorded by an Edinburgh Instruments spectrometer (FLS1000). Mechanoluminescence, persistent mechanoluminescence, and persistent luminescence spectra were recorded by an Ocean Optics Maya2000 Pro spectrometer in the range of 200–1100 nm or a Hamamatsu PMA-12 Photonic multichannel analyzer in the range of 900–1700 nm. Persistent luminescence excitation spectra were recorded by measuring the emission intensity 10 s after ceasing a 30-second monochromatic excitation. The excitation wavelength was varied in 5 nm increments using a Zolix GLORIA-X150A source. The resulting spectra plot the integrated persistent luminescence intensity against the corresponding excitation wavelength. Thermoluminescence spectra were recorded by a TL/OSL spectrometer (LTTL-3DS). Prior to measurement, the samples were irradiated by an internal UV source (365 nm) for 30 seconds. After switching off the light source with a short delay of 1 min, thermoluminescence signals were measured at a constant heating rate (5 K s−1). The TL/OSL spectrometer captured the thermoluminescence spectra at each temperature point, and the two-dimensional thermoluminescence glow curves were calculated by integrating across the wavelength dimension.

In-situ TL measurement

The measurement was conducted using the TL/OSL spectrometer (LTTL-3DS). First, the Pers-ML phosphors were mixed with high-temperature resistive epoxy resin. The mixture was then blade-coated onto a copper substrate (0.5 cm × 0.5 cm) to form a thin film (~50 μm). After curing, the Pers-ML film was exposed to UV light for 30 seconds, then mechanically stimulated by moving a glass rod back and forth over its surface for 15 seconds within the 80-second delay window prior to TL measurement. Additionally, a reference glow curve was also recorded without mechanical stimulation under otherwise identical conditions. Consequently, the pointwise intensity ratio of the two curves is obtained, which clarifies the impact of mechanical energy on the carrier traps.

Fabrication of PET film for Pers-ML evaluation

The prepared Pers-ML particles (0.001 mol) were thoroughly mixed with PDMS paste ( ~ 0.015 g), then transferred onto a 5 cm × 5 cm EVA-covered PET film to form a 2.5 cm × 2.5 cm square. Subsequently, the film was covered with another piece of EVA-covered PET film, placed face-to-face, and sent into a thermal laminator for packaging. For the ball impact demonstration, larger Pers-ML films were fabricated. The Pers-ML particles were arranged to form a 5 cm × 5 cm square, corresponding to 0.004 mol of Ca0.45Sr0.55ZnOS:Yb3+/Pb2+ (1/0.6%) crystals, which has the optimum Pers-ML performance.

Quantitative analysis of Pers-ML performance

The specialized measurement system includes a customized mechanical control unit, a sample holder, an NIR camera, and a fiber-coupled CCD spectrometer. The home-made mechanical control unit, comprising a force gauge, a linear translation stage, a linear track, and a stepper motor, was utilized to apply quantitative mechanical stimulation. The force gauge was mounted on the linear translation stage, the movement axis of which was perpendicular to the linear track. The magnitude of force was adjusted by controlling the relative position between the force gauge and sample holder, accomplished by screwing the micrometer on the translation stage. Mechanical stimulation is achieved by the displacement of the force gauge along the linear track (~10 mm), resulting in the sliding of its metal indenter over the sample film. The metal indenter attached to the force gauge had a hemispherical tip with a diameter of 2 mm. For the typical applied force (10 N) and the contact area (approximated as the cross-section of the hemisphere), the applied pressure is estimated to be ≈3.2 MPa. The Allied Vision NIR camera (Goldeye G-130 TEC1) was used to monitor the Pers-ML signal during measurement, mounted on a stage with its focal plane perpendicular to the force gauge.

Our quantification setup uses a lateral configuration for load application and sliding tests. To perform drop-weight impact tests while protecting the optical setup from debris, a front-surface mirror placed at 45° below the sample can be used to redirect the Pers-ML signal to a laterally positioned camera. This configuration effectively prevents damage to the imaging equipment and is therefore recommended for future implementations involving high-energy impacts.

With the system set up, time series Pers-ML signals were captured by the NIR camera in continuous shooting mode with a 0.5-second exposure time. Since the maximum frame rate of the NIR camera is 94 fps, the time interval between adjacent images is negligible. The output NIR image is monochromatic with an 8-bit depth, and each pixel has a grayscale value ranging from 0 to 255. The Pers-ML intensity at each time point was calculated by summing the grayscale values within the ROI (20 × 200) from the corresponding NIR image using a Python script. The zero point time (t = 0 s) was designated as the first image, representing the instantaneous ML. Consequently, Pers-ML decay curves were obtained. For better visualization, the original grayscale images generated by the NIR camera are displayed as pseudocolor images using Matplotlib’s built-in “viridis” colormap throughout the article.

Measurement of Pers-ML lifetime

For visible PersL materials, the PersL lifetime is defined as the time between the end of charging and the moment at which the PersL brightness reaches 0.32 mcd m−2. However, since photometric measurement is not applicable to the NIR region, the Pers-ML lifetime is determined by the time at which the net Pers-ML signal reaches zero after mechanical action. The net Pers-ML signal is calculated by subtracting the intensity of the reference region (of equal size to the ROI) where no mechanical stimulation is applied.

An arbitrary waveform generator (RIGOL DG811) is used to modulate the light output of the UV LED source (Thorlabs M365FP1) set in “Modulation” mode. The waveform generator controls the excitation light pulse, adjusting the frequency and pulse width according to experimental requirements.

Assembly of the Pers-ML powered ball impact monitoring system

The prototype Pers-ML powered impact monitoring system was built by integrating the Pers-ML film (Ca0.45Sr0.55ZnOS:1%Yb3+/0.6%Pb2+), a fiber-coupled UV LED source (365 nm; Thorlabs M365FP1), an arbitrary waveform generator (RIGOL DG811), a NIR camera (Allied Vision Goldeye G-130 TEC1), as well as a laptop to control the camera. The waveform generator produces pulsed electric signals (frequency of 100 Hz and pulse width of 0.5 ms) to power the LED source, continuously charging the Pers-ML film. Under this condition, the pulsed LED has an average power of 0.27 μW cm−2. The NIR camera is positioned vertically above the Pers-ML film at a distance of 32 cm. It is equipped with a bandpass optical filter (950–1050 nm for NIR Pers-ML sample and 500–550 nm for SrAl2O4:Eu2+/Dy3+) and captures successive images with an exposure time of 0.5 s. An array of steel balls was dropped from various heights (5–30 cm) to simulate ball impact on the Pers-ML. The consecutively captured NIR images are collected for further analysis. The setup was tested under both dark and ambient environments.
