Sacral Neuromodulation for Refractory Overactive Bladder: Closing the Gaps in Anatomy, Mechanisms, and Parameter Selection
Authors: Daniel P. Marshall, Isha Chugh, Cindy L. Amundsen, Nazema Y. Siddiqui, Warren M. Grill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sacral neuromodulation is an effective therapy to control overactive bladder symptoms, yet the underlying mechanisms and optimal stimulation parameters remain unclear. Analysis of 45 clinical studies from 1994 to 2023 demonstrated no significant change in rates of long-term symptom control despite technological advancements, and analysis of recent studies (2015 onward) showed that more than 40% of patients receiving test stimulation do not benefit from sacral neuromodulation. Critical knowledge gaps persist. A lack of evidence guiding stimulation parameter selection has resulted in largely unchanged programming protocols since initial US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval in 1997. There is little knowledge of sacral nerve anatomy or nerve fiber organization, and the specific neural pathways mediating therapeutic effects remain unclear. Additionally, there is a limited understanding of how anatomical variations affect stimulation outcomes and few validated predictors of long-term success. We propose that developing detailed computational models incorporating realistic nerve morphology and tissue properties, combined with systematic documentation of programming parameters and outcomes will enable development of more effective stimulation protocols and electrode designs. Bridging these knowledge gaps is essential for improving the consistency and efficacy of sacral neuromodulation for refractory overactive bladder.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03658-5.

Introduction

Overactive bladder (OAB) is a debilitating condition characterized by frequent and uncontrollable urges to urinate and may be accompanied by incontinence and/or nocturia. In the US, OAB affects 16% of men and 17% of women [1]. However, actual OAB rates may be higher; one study [2] found 27% of men and 43% of women “sometimes” experienced OAB symptoms, and incidence in older populations is 40% in men and 47% in women [3]. Therapies for OAB include bladder retraining (i.e., timed voiding), fluid management, pelvic floor muscle therapy, optimized treatment of medical comorbidities, medications such as antimuscarinic or β3-adrenoceptor agonists, intradetrusor onabotulinum toxin A injections (BTX), tibial neuromodulation (TNM), and sacral neuromodulation (SNM). The recent American Urologic Association Guideline emphasized the importance of shared decision-making between the provider and the patient when selecting the best therapy, taking into consideration patient needs, desires, and side effect tolerance [4]. SNM is a commonly used therapy in patients with refractory OAB, particularly when other treatments fail to provide symptom relief.

SNM delivers electrical stimulation to sacral nerve roots—typically unilaterally to the third sacral nerve (S3)—to reduce the symptoms of OAB. The first SNM device, the Medtronic InterStim™, was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 1997; Axonics® later developed a competing device that was FDA approved in 2019. Decades of clinical data have demonstrated the efficacy of SNM to treat OAB [5–10], and several device refinements have reduced lead migration, increased battery longevity, and improved ease of use. However, outcomes remain inconsistent—some patients achieve dramatic and sustained relief, while others fail to benefit or experience declining efficacy over time [11]. A lack of effective treatment can be driven by inadequate delivery of SNM, or because a patient’s specific etiology is not treatable with SNM. Although SNM was introduced decades ago [12], its mechanism of action remains unclear. There is a limited understanding of the underlying neuroanatomy and the neural pathways mediating the therapeutic effects of SNM. While it is well established that stimulation influences control of bladder storage and voiding, the specific nerve fiber populations responsible for improvement and the precise physiological mechanisms of action remain speculative [13]. Likewise, evidence guiding the choice of stimulation parameters—such as contact configuration, waveform, amplitude, pulse width, frequency, and duty cycle—is sparse [11, 14]. Standard settings, originally established almost 30 years ago, persist largely due to a lack of robust data supporting alternatives. Clinicians often rely on trial-and-error reprogramming to improve outcomes [15, 16], and there are few established predictors of long-term success [17, 18]. This persistent gap in knowledge has implications for SNM lead design. While the implantable pulse generator (IPG) and lead anchoring/placement have evolved, the design of the stimulating contacts—arguably the most critical component for selective, effective neuromodulation—has remained unchanged. A more detailed understanding of sacral nerve anatomy, nerve fiber organization, and the relationship between the stimulation field and nerve activation thresholds would support the rational development of electrodes and parameter settings to enable greater control over the effects of SNM.

In this review, we summarize the current state of SNM for OAB, including benefits and shortcomings, and highlight impediments to further improvement of the therapy. We explore the current device designs, the anatomy and neurophysiology underlying SNM, proposed mechanisms of action, and the evidence that guides implantation and programming decisions. We conduct a literature analysis of clinical studies to assess the effectiveness of treatment and improvements over time. We highlight critical knowledge gaps, including limited understanding of sacral nerve anatomy and fiber organization, uncertainty about the precise mechanisms mediating therapeutic effects, and sparse evidence guiding stimulation parameter selection. We emphasize the opportunity to advance the therapy using computational modeling incorporating detailed anatomical data, systematic documentation of programming parameters and outcomes, and high-quality clinical trials.

Current State of SNM for Overactive Bladder

Contact configuration determines which electrodes deliver stimulation, as well as the stimulation polarity (Fig. 1d). Monopolar (a.k.a. unipolar) stimulation uses one contact as a cathode (negative polarity during the leading phase of the biphasic pulse) and the IPG as the return electrode (an anode with positive polarity during leading phase of biphasic pulse). For multipolar stimulation, multiple contacts deliver stimulation and are each assigned a polarity. Most commonly, SNM is delivered in a bipolar fashion, with one contact as a cathode and one as an anode. Bipolar stimulation is distinct from biphasic stimulation, i.e., a waveform which has two phases of opposite polarity. SNM waveforms are technically biphasic but are often called “pseudo-monophasic” since the first leading phase is much higher in amplitude than the second phase (Fig. 1c). Therefore, contact polarity is described by the leading, “active” phase of the waveform. An interphase delay (no current flow) separates the active phase from the oppositely signed “recharge” phase, allowing the procession of neural activation before reversing polarity. The recharge phase recovers charge at the electrode-tissue interface, reducing the risk of damage to the electrode and tissues. Stimulation amplitude determines the strength of the active phase. Pulse width controls the duration of the active phase. Higher stimulation amplitude and longer pulse widths both deliver more charge to the tissue, increasing the activation of nerve fibers. Typically, stimulation is titrated to just above the threshold of sensory perception. Pulse repetition rate, measured in hertz, defines the frequency with which stimulation pulses are delivered. Finally, cycling (a.k.a. duty cycle) refers to periods where stimulation is turned on and off.

Efficacy and Side Effects of Sacral Neuromodulation

SNM is a proven treatment for OAB, demonstrating robust efficacy in both the short and long term [9, 21]. SNM is effective across diverse patient populations—for instance, older patients benefit from SNM with only slightly reduced efficacy and similar adverse event rates compared to younger patients [22–24]. Although not FDA-approved for children under 16 years of age, preliminary research in pediatric patients with bladder and bowel dysfunction yielded encouraging outcomes, despite a 17% incidence of lead breakage due to trauma [25].

In direct comparisons with other advanced therapies for OAB, SNM exhibited comparable or superior efficacy. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 17 randomized controlled trials [7] found that between SNM, TNM, and BTX, SNM had the most symptom improvement, although the differences between treatments were not statistically significant. BTX was associated with higher rates of adverse events, including urinary tract infections and urinary retention. BTX is often used because of its greater cost-effectiveness over the short term (≤ 5 years) [26], but SNM becomes more cost-effective over a longer duration (≥ 10 years) [27]. Both percutaneous [28] and newly emerging implantable TNM therapies [5] have comparable efficacy and adverse event profiles to SNM.

Most adverse events associated with SNM are in the immediate postoperative period (e.g., infection and delayed healing), declining efficacy, and pain at the IPG site. In an analysis of 2680 patient records, the primary SNM-related complications were device malfunction/decrease in efficacy (8.6%) and pain (4.3%) [29]. Lack of consistent efficacy can lead to explantation, and may prompt reoperation to reposition the lead, which restores effectiveness for some patients [30]. SNM can also elicit unwanted acute responses, including activation of motor fibers in S3 which manifests as leg or pelvic floor twitches; SNM may also elicit uncomfortable or painful sensory responses [31] possibly from activating sensory fibers in S3 or near the IPG. These effects are usually mitigated by setting the stimulation amplitude at or just below the sensory threshold or changing other stimulation parameters. Importantly, the specific nerve fibers that mediate the therapeutic benefit of SNM remain unclear, making it challenging to fine-tune parameters for selective activation of therapeutic targets while avoiding side-effect-producing fibers. A better understanding of the relevant neural pathways could enable higher stimulation amplitudes without evoking unwanted responses, potentially enhancing the efficacy of SNM.

Review and Analysis of Efficacy of SNM in OAB

We conducted a secondary analysis of published studies of SNM to treat OAB to evaluate whether reported treatment efficacy changed over time. The typical implementation reported in studies of SNM is shown in Fig. 2 (noting that retrospective studies, by definition, do not implement a screening phase). Eligible subjects initially undergo test stimulation: baseline symptoms are measured, a lead is implanted in the sacral foramen, and symptoms are evaluated over the following week(s) with external stimulation. Patients with a > 50% improvement relative to baseline are classified as “responders” and are implanted with a permanent SNM device. Symptoms are evaluated at follow-up and compared to baseline. Studies typically reported the percentage of patients who maintain a benefit relative to baseline as the “treatment efficacy”. Treatment efficacy therefore represents the percentage of patients that receive a permanent device implantation and continue to have effective therapy after a certain period of extended follow-up. Importantly, these individuals have already demonstrated > 50% improvement in symptoms during the test phase. For our secondary analysis of published data, we sourced studies from systematic reviews of sacral neuromodulation; we included studies from Table 1 of Brazzelli et al. [10], Tables 1 and 2 of Siddiqui et al. [8], and Table 1 of Amundsen et al. [5]. We included only peer-reviewed, full-text clinical studies, therefore conference abstracts were excluded. We identified studies with overlapping cohorts as described in “Methods for elimination of overlapping cohorts” in the Supplementary Material. We eliminated three studies with duplicate cohorts [32–34], in each case keeping the latest study by publication date. This left 42 studies for further analysis. For each included study, we examined the original publication to determine the number of patients tested, the number of patients implanted, responder rate, treatment efficacy, and overall success rate. We did not evaluate the number of patients screened, as this is only reported for prospective clinical trials, and most of the studies in our analysis were retrospective analyses. If a value could not be determined from the text, it was labeled as not reported (NR). Additionally, if the data reporting was such that a value could not be differentiated specifically for OAB (e.g., was reported as mixed with other symptom groups such as urinary retention) we also labeled it as NR. Because efficacy measures varied across studies, we defined treatment efficacy as a > 50% improvement in one or more OAB symptoms defined by frequency of voids, urgency incontinence episodes, or volume voided. We excluded efficacy reports based on subjective measures, such as patient reported quality of life. We excluded any data where the relevant sample size was not reported or was fewer than 10; for treatment efficacy this is the number of implanted patients, for overall success rate and responder rate, this is the number of tested patients. Where multiple timepoints for follow-up were reported, we used the value closest to 1-year follow-up. Dropouts were considered treatment failures unless the number of dropouts was not provided. We did not distinguish between OAB subtypes (e.g., wet and dry), nor between patients receiving percutaneous nerve evaluation (PNE) vs. staged test stimulation. In two studies [35, 36], the device was implanted without a test phase, and responders were identified 1 month after implantation. To maintain consistency with the other studies, we treated the number of responders at this 1-month assessment as the “number implanted” group and evaluated treatment efficacy within only this group. Responder rate has increased substantially over time, likely reflecting improvements in patient selection, lead placement, lead anchoring, and the adoption of implanted leads during the testing phase. As a result, the overall success rate has also increased. In contrast, treatment efficacy has remained essentially unchanged, leaving about one-quarter of implanted patients with suboptimal long-term therapy. Notably, symptom improvement during the test phase has been shown to be a poor predictor of treatment success long term [76]. Because OAB symptoms are variable over time, assessment at a single time point may misclassify outcomes: some patients who lose efficacy after implantation may not have been true responders, while others who could benefit from SNM may be incorrectly classified as non-responders during the test phase. Ultimately, 41% of patients who undergo test stimulation do not achieve effective therapy (overall success rate). This figure likely includes patients for whom SNM could be effective, but due to limitations of current methods fail to respond during the test phase or initially respond but later lose efficacy. This figure also likely includes patients with an underlying etiology that renders them unlikely to respond to any form of SNM. Therefore, these findings underscore the need for innovation aimed at treating patients for whom current SNM techniques are suboptimal, and to identify the patients who are unlikely to benefit from SNM and should be directed to alternatives.

The numerical analysis presented here is intended to characterize trends in reported SNM outcomes over the past three decades, but several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. Many studies did not report complete data on the number of patients tested, implanted, or followed, requiring exclusion of those studies from analysis. Most studies were retrospective and used heterogeneous test-phase protocols, implantation methods, and follow-up intervals. As a result of heterogeneous and aggregate reporting, our analysis did not account for per-patient data or patient-specific factors such as age, comorbidities, or OAB subtype, nor for differences in surgical technique, lead placement, or programming strategies. For the same reason, we could not disaggregate the data to analyze contributing reasons for treatment failure. Much of the available evidence and therefore our included studies of SNM for OAB derives from retrospective studies as relatively few randomized controlled trials have been conducted to date. Only the most recent meta-analysis [5] from which we sourced studies considered study bias and excluded high-bias studies. We excluded subjective outcome measures as a further effort to reduce bias in our analysis. Identifying these limitations highlights where future clinical studies must improve to enable more rigorous comparisons and to support the development of evidence-based device designs and programming strategies.

Challenges to the Improvement of SNM

Despite demonstrated efficacy, SNM fails to provide adequate symptom relief for several subpopulations of patients (Figs. 2 and 3b, c). These include patients who show no improvement during test stimulation, those who initially respond but subsequently lose therapeutic effectiveness, as well as those who experience suboptimal symptom improvement (e.g., 50–75% reduction). The reasons for inadequate treatment outcomes are multifaceted and likely include variability in patient-specific factors, device limitations, and gaps in anatomical and mechanistic understanding. Although some patients may inherently be non-responders, advancements in both SNM technology and our understanding of underlying therapeutic mechanisms hold potential to enhance outcomes.

There is a profound lack of knowledge regarding the relevant anatomy including S3 nerve morphology and fiber organization, and how variability in patient anatomy affects stimulation outcomes. The anatomical target—which specific fibers mediate the therapeutic effects of SNM—has not been conclusively determined, impeding the development of targeted stimulation and advancements in lead design. Further, there has been little high-quality research on predictive factors for responders [18] and more research is needed to identify responder criteria.

Compounding this issue, SNM programming remains largely a trial-and-error process [15, 16]. In a retrospective study of 9501 patients with SNM, nearly a third required office-based reprogramming in the first 5 years of treatment, with a mean of 2.5 sessions per patient [77]. There is a paucity of information on the direct effects of stimulation settings used in SNM, such as pulse width or pulse rate, which hampers the ability to standardize effective therapeutic protocols [11, 16]. Likely because of this dearth of knowledge, the design of the stimulating contacts and recommended stimulation parameters have not undergone substantial changes since FDA approval nearly 30 years ago.

Relevant Anatomy and Mechanisms of Action

Anatomy of S3

S3 originates from the spinal cord and divides into ventral and dorsal rami, which exit through their respective sacral foramina. SNM electrodes are typically placed along the ventral ramus, with the most rostral electrode positioned inside the foramen (Fig. 1a). The S3 ventral root has a diameter of 0.55 ± 0.07 mm (mean ± SD), with an average fiber count of 2233 ± 299 (mean ± SD) [78]. Approximately 20–30% of these fibers are afferents greater than 5 µm in diameter, with both afferent and efferent fibers reaching diameters of 13 μm [79]. A detailed analysis [80] found that the organization of S3 fibers was “somewhat somatotopic,” i.e., fibers were collected in distinct functional groups; this suggests that selective stimulation strategies based on targeting specific fiber populations may be feasible.

The ventral S3 ramus contributes to three major nerves of interest (Table 3): (1) the pelvic nerves, which innervate the bladder; (2) the tibial portion of the sciatic nerve, which innervates the foot and portions of the leg; and (3) the pudendal nerve, which innervates the external urethral sphincter, perineum, genitalia, and other organs of the pelvic cavity [81]. These nerves arise from combinations of branches from S3 as well as branches from the other sacral and lumbar spinal nerves [82]. The remaining nerve branches from S3 contribute to pelvic floor musculature and cutaneous sensation of parts of the leg and perineum (Table 3) [81, 82]. Many fibers projecting from S3 are involved in micturition (Table 3). Pelvic nerve afferents carry information about bladder fullness and pelvic efferents excite the detrusor, promoting micturition [88]. Pudendal nerve afferents carry information about fluid in the urethra, as well as other somatic information from many nearby organs, and pudendal efferents contract the external urethral sphincter [88]. S3 carries the primary innervation of the bladder among the sacral nerve roots [89]. S3 also contributes heavily to urethral closure; 70% of maximal closure pressure of the external urethral sphincter can be generated by stimulation of S3 alone (the remaining 30% arise from S2 and S4) [90].

Physiological Mechanisms of SNM for OAB

The effect of SNM is thought to involve the modulation of spinal reflexes and brain areas mediated by the activation of large-diameter afferent fibers [92–94]. Early work focused on the possible direct effects of SNM on spinal reflexes involving micturition. In a rat model of OAB, SNM acutely inhibited micturition, an effect that was eliminated if glutamate antagonists were applied to the spinal cord [95]. It was postulated that afferent activation of S3 could inhibit parasympathetic efferents innervating the bladder directly, or by inhibiting interneuron transmission in the spinal cord [94]. Such inhibition could be mediated by activation of the large number of sensory pudendal afferents within S3 [96, 97]. However, research in rats demonstrated that clinically relevant stimulation amplitudes led to a lack of direct reflex effects on bladder storage, and much higher stimulation amplitudes were required to activate inhibitory reflexes [98]. Another possible direct effect of SNM is the activation of large-diameter efferents in S3 leading to the contraction of pelvic floor muscles and the external urethral sphincter [92, 99]. Such contractions could possibly cause direct increases in urethral outlet resistance or engage reflex pathways, including the guarding reflex, to subsequently inhibit the bladder.

SNM can treat urinary retention using the same stimulation parameters used to treat OAB. This challenges the notion that the effects of SNM on micturition are simply mediated by direct inhibition. Recent work has focused on the broader effects of SNM, particularly in brain areas involved with micturition. In healthy individuals, the pontine micturition center (PMC) is responsible for inhibiting the positive feedback loop that would otherwise lead to micturition [100]. When the PMC ceases its inhibitory signaling, spinal reflexes respond to visceral pelvic afferents encoding bladder fullness, causing the bladder to contract and the urethral sphincter to relax (i.e., the micturition reflex). It has been suggested that SNM reinstates PMC control over micturition, potentially explaining why SNM can treat diseases with opposite pathologies [92, 101]. Indeed, the evidence for the effects of SNM on the brain is growing. Early work showed that SNM could evoke cortical potentials in sensory areas, even if stimulation was not perceptible to the patient [102]. Recent studies using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), and positron emission tomography (PET) demonstrated differences in brain activation with vs. without SNM [103–106]. While the specific effects of SNM on the brain and thereby descending input remain unclear, a growing body of evidence suggests that brain-level effects of stimulation may contribute to its mechanism of action.

Influences of Stimulation Programming on Outcomes

Lead Design and Placement

Lead placement in SNM focuses on maximizing the number of electrode contacts that reliably evoke sensory or motor responses by following the curve of S3, and thereby minimizing electrode–fiber distance [16, 107]. Such placement improves battery efficiency (since fiber activation thresholds increase with the square of distance) and the number of contacts available for programming. Indeed, fewer total electrode responses (especially fewer great toe responses) were associated with an increased need for lead revision in a large retrospective study [108]. Perineal sensation is often used to confirm S3 targeting [109], but intraoperative motor responses also predict treatment success [108], and motor and sensory thresholds are highly correlated (r = 0.90) [110]. Moreover, SNM implantation guided only by motor responses (due to anesthesia type) does not reduce SNM efficacy compared to having both sensory and motor responses [111]. A recent study of 64 patients showed that treatment success was correlated with lower motor thresholds and higher pelvic floor muscle activation [112]. Overall, this suggests that motor responses alone can guide lead placement during surgery; however, intravenous sedation can often be preferred by patients over general anesthesia and therefore sensory response is used to confirm stimulation distribution and tolerance.

Several studies considered the effect of lead placement on sensory/motor responses and treatment success. A retrospective review of 189 patients found that none of lead angle, foramen depth, or the number of electrodes evoking sensory/motor responses significantly influenced success, although lateral lead deflection did increase motor responses [113]; however, most placement categories had few non-responders and this may have influenced sensitivity to any particular factor. A large randomized multicenter trial found that a bellows response on contact 3 (the most caudal contact) predicted poorer stimulation outcomes, but was otherwise unable to identify predictive factors for success [17]. A study in eight patients found that more medial and rostral electrode placements had lower thresholds to evoke pelvic floor EMG [114]. A recent study also revealed that pelvic floor motor thresholds are lower on average than sensory thresholds, and tend to decrease the more rostral the contact placement [115]. However, lower thresholds on the more rostral contacts could be due to suboptimal placement of the more caudal electrodes, as the presence of the foramen limits the possible electrode–fiber distance of these contacts [17].

Design improvements in SNM have focused on implantation strategies and the IPG, with little change to the stimulating electrodes since initial FDA approval. While earlier practice was to use a peripheral nerve evaluation (PNE) to evaluate patient response to SNM, testing with the permanent lead has become more common. Lead migration was a serious issue, leading to high rates of surgical revision [34], and the introduction of a tined lead resulted in more stable lead positioning [8]. The implementation of a curved stylet allows lead insertion that follows the path of S3, increasing the potential proximity of contacts between lead and nerve and thereby increasing the number of electrodes available for programming [17]. While cuff electrodes or intrafascicular designs have been proposed to increase selective activation [116], such designs would require more invasive surgery for placement. However, approaches from other neuromodulation therapies, such as spinal cord stimulation or deep brain stimulation, that allow current steering may enable more selective control of the population of activated fibers.

IPG Design and Stimulation Parameters

There have been several design improvements to the IPGs used for SNM. Newer devices with current-controlled stimulation (Table 1) may reduce the need for reprogramming by delivering a constant current regardless of changes in impedance, as observed in deep brain stimulation [117]. More recent innovations in IPG design include rechargeable devices and MRI-conditional devices. The operational life of the battery is estimated to be longer for rechargeable devices, although newer recharge-free options may last up to 15 years (Table 1). There are many considerations in choosing between these options, including the patient burden of recharging [118].

Contact configuration is a typical focus of reprogramming stimulation settings, particularly after loss of efficacy might otherwise necessitate surgical revision. In one study of 50 patients, all were started on continuous, bipolar stimulation [119]. After 20 months, 17% had switched to intermittent stimulation, and 34% to a monopolar configuration, although most remained on bipolar stimulation. The most common configuration is directly adjacent bipolar contacts [15]. This may be suboptimal, as monopolar configurations have lower motor [120] and sensory [114] thresholds than bipolar configurations, and a study in sheep found more widely spaced configurations reduced EMG thresholds [121]. Lower thresholds are related to greater treatment success [112], extend battery life, and are typically indicative of an electrode close to the nerve, which may reduce the possibility of side effects from the activation of nearby nerve branches.

Typical (default) parameters are continuous stimulation at a pulse repetition rate of 14 Hz and pulse width of 210 μs. Some of the default parameters have a basis in experimental evidence, such as pulse rates of approximately 10 Hz showed the best efficacy in animal models [16]. However, most stimulation parameters have little to no evidence to support specific settings [14]. A study of 50 patients with blinded, randomly assigned stimulation frequencies found that no pulse rate was significantly better than other, but in some cases changes in pulse rate improved SNM outcomes [122]. A single-blinded study of 18 patients who were satisfied with SNM treatment randomly assigned participants to a non-standard pulse width; overall, there was no significant difference across treatment groups using non-standard pulse widths, but some patients did see significant improvements using non-standard pulse widths [123]. There has been little research on duty cycle; one prospective randomized feasibility study found that SNM efficacy was retained even with a 30-min-on, 23.5-h-off cycle [66]. More research on the effects on efficacy and side effects of specific stimulation parameters is needed to inform best practices on IPG programming.

Where Do We Go from Here?

SNM substantially improves symptoms for many patients with refractory OAB, yet clinical outcomes remain highly variable and reliable predictors of therapeutic success have yet to be established. Previous research examining predictors such as lead-placement characteristics, intraoperative sensory and motor responses, and patient demographics has yielded inconclusive results. One challenge is inconsistent and incomplete documentation of post-implantation settings. An analysis of 127 records from 40 patients with SNM revealed critical omissions. For example, no records indicated which active contacts were used [124]. Symptom improvement fluctuates over time after permanent SNM implantation [125], which also complicates assessment of the effects of parameter changes. More systematic tracking of contact configurations, amplitude, frequency, and clinical results would facilitate retrospective analyses to identify effective settings, contributing to informed programming and potentially better SNM outcomes [11]. Machine learning could then be applied to uncover predictors of success—spanning patient characteristics (e.g., age, comorbidities, baseline OAB severity), lead-placement metrics (e.g., angle, deflection, foramen depth, intraoperative sensory and motor thresholds), and stimulation parameters. A greater understanding of stimulation programming is essential to increase therapeutic efficacy over time, using individualized programming to optimize long-term outcomes. Further, such analyses are critical to better identify patients for whom SNM will not be effective so they can be directed to alternative treatments. Analysis of Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction Research Network (LURN) data using semi-supervised clustering identified five clinically and biochemically distinct lower urinary tract symptom clusters, laying a foundation for integrating technical data and developing subtype-specific treatments [126]. Since the majority of patients with OAB are female, such studies are often performed on datasets comprising outcomes of mostly or solely female patients, and the conclusions may not extend to male patients. By clarifying which parameters and patient characteristics forecast a positive response, clinicians could better tailor implantation and programming decisions, minimize trial-and-error reprogramming, and improve SNM’s therapeutic reliability.

While no research on the effects of anatomical variability on stimulation outcomes has been conducted, variability in local tissues and the anatomy of the nerve between patients are likely to influence the activation of S3 fibers. The internal organization of S3 remains understudied. Although a histological study [80] noted “somewhat somatotopic” organization in sacral nerves, detailed mapping of fiber populations is lacking. While activation of sensory afferents is hypothesized to be related to the therapeutic effects of SNM, these fibers have not been localized within S3; further, it is unknown if the mechanism is limited solely to the activation of S3 and whether other nearby nerve branches might also contribute to efficacy and/or side effects. The sacral plexus is highly variable across patients, and it is unknown what effect these interpatient variations may have on the efficacy of SNM. Indeed, we could not identify publications with data on the intersubject variability of fiber distributions, fiber counts, and fascicular organization within the sacral nerves. These factors greatly affect stimulation outcomes in models of other peripheral nerves [127, 128], and warrant future study.

Multimodality research is needed to advance our understanding of the impact of anatomical factors on SNM outcomes. High-resolution MRI studies can map the sacrum and sacral plexus, providing 3D reconstructions of nerve paths and surrounding tissues. Complementary cadaveric dissections with histological analysis could yield data on fiber types, fiber diameter distributions, and fascicular arrangement. Integrating these datasets would enable anatomically faithful computational models that can predict how specific anatomical variations influence nerve activation. Investigating how anatomical variability affects SNM has the potential to inform the mechanism of action of SNM, i.e., identify target nerve fibers, and reveal factors that contribute to therapeutic success.

Computational modeling is a powerful approach to guide the design of stimulation therapies. Preclinical and clinical studies are constrained by the very large parameter space—lead placement, stimulation parameters, patient anatomy—and by ethical considerations. Computational modeling can fill critical knowledge gaps by simulating nerve fiber activation, novel electrode designs, and the impact of anatomical variability. Modeling approaches have been successfully applied in other stimulation modalities. For example, computational modeling of deep brain stimulation developed optimized stimulation that reduced energy usage of patients’ implants while maintaining efficacy [129], and in spinal cord stimulation, patient-specific models enabled the investigation of mechanisms of action and accurate prediction of sensory thresholds [130, 131]. Such models require integrating detailed anatomy of both the tissues surrounding the nerve and lead as well as nerve anatomy derived from histology. Histology can provide distributions of fiber types and fiber diameters, allowing realistic fiber populations to be simulated in the model. Modeling results can be compared to thresholds for motor and sensory responses to provide further confidence in the model simulations [128].

Computational models of nerve stimulation typically involve two stages: first, a volume conductor model of the lead, nerve, and surrounding tissues is used to compute the electrical voltages generated in the tissue during stimulation; second, those voltages are sampled along the paths of the nerve(s) and applied to biophysical cable models of the nerve fibers. Past computational models of SNM were limited in their biophysical accuracy and thus their relevance to clinical neuromodulation. For example, Hirata et al. [132] found that transcutaneous stimulation fields are highly sensitive to tissue composition, and Yousif et al. [133] found that inhomogeneous tissues substantially affect current flow in a finite element model of the InterStim lead, sacrum, and colon. However, neither study considered nerve tissue or its response to stimulation. Representations of the nerve tissues and their associated electrical properties are critical for accurate computational modeling of fiber responses [128, 134, 135]. Ciotti et al. [116] developed an SNM model with a realistic sacrum and found that intrafascicular electrodes enabled greater selectivity than the InterStim electrode but used a synthetic nerve cross section. The anatomical detail required for robust SNM simulations remains unclear, but it is likely that high-fidelity 3D segmentations derived from volumetric imaging will be necessary for accurate models [136]. Such 3D segmentations, combined with cadaveric sacral histology, promise more biologically accurate and thus clinically relevant models. Merging realistic pelvic and nerve morphologies with biophysical axon models will enable the patterns of fiber activation to be quantified across varied stimulation parameters and lead placements, ultimately guiding the development of improved SNM devices and programming.

Conclusions

SNM has matured into an important therapy for patients with OAB who are refractory to initial conservative therapy options and/or medications. Multiple clinical studies report durable, if longitudinally variable, symptom improvements, and device refinements have helped reduce surgical revisions and increase responder rate. Despite these improvements, outcomes remain variable, and more than 40% of patients in recent clinical studies do not benefit from SNM therapy. A lack of fundamental knowledge about the specific neural targets and mechanisms underlying SNM can impede gains in efficacy. Existing devices and programs rely heavily on legacy settings, in part because the anatomical structure of sacral nerves and the functional basis of therapy remain only partially understood. Moving forward, the field must expand high-fidelity anatomical and histological studies of the sacral plexus, employ realistic computational models to test new electrode designs and stimulation waveforms, and systematically document programming parameters and clinical outcomes. By clarifying which nerve fibers—and which neural pathways—mediate therapeutic benefit, rational refinements in lead design and stimulation protocols can reduce variability, broaden the responder pool, and improve the durability of SNM for OAB. The future of SNM thus lies in bridging these fundamental knowledge gaps through robust clinical studies and advanced engineering approaches that will mutually and synergistically enhance the science and clinical practice of sacral neuromodulation.
