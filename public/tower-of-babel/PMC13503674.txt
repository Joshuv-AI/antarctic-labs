GMMA as delivery system for Klebsiella pneumoniae O-antigens and K-antigens
Authors: Maria Michelina Raso, Rebecca Nappini, Gloria Gnoni, Maria Grazia Aruta, Mariagrazia Molfetta, Vanessa Arato, Salvatore Durante, Paola Cescutti, Martina Carducci, Renzo Alfini, Carlo Giannelli, Omar Rossi, Mariagrazia Pizza, Francesca Micoli, Gianmarco Gasperini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKlebsiella pneumoniae (Kp) is a major cause of healthcare-associated infections in developed countries and neonatal sepsis in low- and middle-income countries, posing a global public health threat due to its antimicrobial resistance. Although potential vaccine targets have been identified, no vaccine is currently licensed for Kp. Two classes of polysaccharides displayed on the Kp surface—capsular polysaccharides (KAg) and lipopolysaccharide O-antigens (OAg)—determine serotypes and represent promising candidates for vaccine development. We purified GMMA (Generalized Modules for Membrane Antigens), from hypervesiculating genetically engineered Kp strains which effectively display both OAg and KAg polysaccharides, and we used them to investigate OAg-mediated cross-reactivity and immuno-interference between OAg-KAg combinations. In mice, GMMA elicited strong antibody responses against both polysaccharides, with no negative impact on OAg immunogenicity in presence of KAg. These findings support the potential of combining multiple GMMA to achieve broad serotype coverage in Kp vaccines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57020-4.Subject terms: Diseases, Immunology, Microbiology

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae (Kp) is a Gram-negative bacterium belonging to the Enterobacteriaceae family. Kp, first identified as a causative agent of pneumonia, is one of the most important causes of opportunistic healthcare associated infections, categorized by the World Health Organization (WHO) into the critical priority group of multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens for which development of new interventions is recommended1,2. Moreover, results from the Child Health and Mortality Prevention Surveillance (CHAMPS) network, the Barnards study, as well as studies currently collecting more field data, clearly highlighted Kp as leading cause of neonatal sepsis in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)3,4. No vaccine is currently licensed against Kp, but potential vaccine targets have been described in the past few decades5,6. In particular, Kp surface polysaccharides were identified to be key virulence factors and potential vaccine antigens.

Kp produces two types of polysaccharides on the cell surface: capsular polysaccharides, also known as K-antigens (KAg), and lipopolysaccharide O-antigens (OAg)7. To date, > 180 distinct KAg have been identified based on sequence analysis of the genes of the KAg locus (KL)8. On the contrary, a relatively lower number of OAg (> 10) has been reported9. A recent analysis on the distribution of KAg and OAg serotypes in neonatal sepsis isolates from 13 surveillance studies across 35 sites in 13 countries reported the 5 most prevalent KAg serotypes (KL2, KL102, KL25, KL15, and KL62) accounting for ~ 50% and O1 and O2 galactan-based OAg serotypes for ~ 70% of the dataset10. All galactan-based OAg polysaccharides share the [→ 3)-α-DGalp-(1 → 3)-β-D-Galf(1 →] repeating unit, also known as Galactan-I (Gal-I), defining serotype O2a or O2v1. This backbone can be further modified by the addition of an α(1 → 4)-linked galactose side chain, which modifies Gal-I to Galactan-III (Gal-III) and converts serotype O2a into O2afg or O2v2. Moreover, the addition of a capping polysaccharide composed by the [→ 3)-α-D-Galp-(1 → 3)-β-D-Galp-(1 →] repeating unit, also known as Galactan-II (Gal-II), converts serotype O2v1 and O2v2 into serotype O1v1 and serotype O1v2, respectively11.

In Kp, as for several other pathogens, the KAg promotes immune evasion12. Past work suggests that anti-OAg antibodies developed against several common serotypes can bind to encapsulated bacteria to a certain degree13. Nevertheless, different subsequent studies demonstrated that OAg antibody binding is reduced in encapsulated isolates, and the protection provided by those antibodies is diminished in mouse models of infection12–16. However, it is important to note that not all invasive Kp strains express high levels of capsule, and in some infections, KAg production may be downregulated17,18. Interestingly, our recent work further confirmed the ability of anti-OAg sera to kill encapsulated neonatal sepsis isolates in the presence of rabbit complement in vitro19.

The aim of this work was to investigate the immunogenicity and cross-reactivity of Kp surface polysaccharides using GMMA (Generalized Modules for Membrane Antigens) as delivery systems20. Indeed, since GMMA faithfully resemble the bacterial surface, they represent a useful tool to present polysaccharides in their native environment. Furthermore, genetic engineering of GMMA-producing strains to display different OAg variants in an isogenic Kp background can facilitate investigation of potential cross-reactivity21.

Here, for the first time, we showed that both OAg and KAg can be co-displayed on Kp GMMA, opening the possibility to present multiple polysaccharide antigens to the immune system with potential to achieve broad coverage with relatively low-component formulations.

Results

GMMA generation and characterization

A panel of different Kp wild type strains, differing for O- and K-serotype, was engineered by removing the tolR gene to obtain a hypervesiculating phenotype22,23. GMMA were purified from each resulting strain and characterized. Polysaccharides were extracted through acetic acid hydrolysis and analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography–size exclusion chromatography24 (HPLC–SEC) (Fig. 1A). Polysaccharides obtained from NCTC11228 ∆tolR GMMA were characterized by one major O1v1 OAg population at medium molecular mass, together with one low molecular mass peak corresponding to OAg core. Interestingly, an additional population at high molecular mass was present in GMMA obtained from NCTC9138, NCTC8172 and NCTC9182 ∆tolR strains, corresponding to K18, K64 and K62 KAg, respectively. Interestingly, lack of K2 KAg in GMMA from strain NCTC11228 ∆tolR was verified to be due to the selection of a spontaneous capsule-null clone during genetic engineering (Supplementary Fig. 1). Polysaccharide structures were confirmed by high performance anion exchange chromatography-pulsed amperometric detection (HPAEC-PAD) and 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR).

(A) HPLC–SEC profiles detected by refractive index (dRI) of polysaccharide extracted from the different GMMA. (B) OAg biosynthetic genes deleted (wzm-wzt, wbbY) or complemented (glmABC).

Subsequently, further mutations were introduced in selected strains to specifically modify the OAg structure (Fig. 1B). NCTC11228, NCTC9138 and NCTC8172 strains were genetically engineered to delete the wbbY gene in order to abolish the Gal-II capping, thus converting O1v1 to O2v1. Moreover, a plasmid carrying the genes responsible for Gal-III biosynthesis was introduced in strains NCT11228 ∆tolR and NCT11228 ∆tolR ∆wbbY, to convert O1v1 to O1v2 and O2v1 to O2v2 respectively. Finally, both NCTC11228 ∆tolR and NCTC9138 ∆tolR mutant strains were further engineered to abolish OAg synthesis, by removing the wzm-wzt genes in the rfb locus. HPLC-SEC profiles, with differential refractive index detection (dRI), confirmed the expected phenotypes resulting from these mutations, particularly the reduced molecular mass of O2v1/O2v2 compared to O1v1/O1v2, and the lack of OAg peaks in the OAg-negative mutants (Fig. 1A). 1H NMR analysis was performed on polysaccharides extracted from NCTC11228 and NCT9138 derived GMMA to further confirm OAg identity after each genetic engineering step (Supplementary Fig. 2). NMR analysis also demonstrated 100% of Gal-III in O2v2 and O1v2 GMMA and a molar ratio close to 1 for Gal-I:Gal-II repeating units in O1v1 and Gal-III:Gal-II repeating units in O1v2 GMMA (Supplementary Fig. 3).

GMMA were characterized by different OAg/protein ratios (Table 1), with O2v1/O2v2-displaying GMMA showing lower galactose content than O1v1/O1v2-displaying GMMA, consistently with reduced OAg size. Interestingly, GMMA size appeared to be influenced by the length of the displayed polysaccharides, with GMMA carrying KAg showing diameters up to ~ 250 nm (Table 1).

Immunogenicity and cross-reactivity of galactan-based O-antigens displayed on GMMA in mice

An in vivo study in mice was designed to investigate the cross-reactivity of the different galactan-based OAg displayed on GMMA. All GMMA, produced from the same parental strain NCTC11228 and modified to display the different OAg serotypes, were compared in mice at the same OAg dose (1 µg) by two intraperitoneal immunizations at 4-week intervals. Sera were tested by ELISA against all four different purified OAg structures used as coating antigens. To test the serum bactericidal activity (SBA) of resulting immune sera, a panel of isogenic strains displaying the four different OAg structures was generated starting from wild-type NCTC11228 bacteria, applying the same strategy used for GMMA-producing strains without introducing the hypervesiculating mutation. Interestingly, HPLC-SEC dRI profiles of the extracted polysaccharides revealed the presence of K2 KAg in the SBA strains, different from what observed in GMMA, while still confirming the display of the modified OAg structures (Supplementary Fig. 4).

All galactan-based GMMA induced a significant antigen specific IgG response after one injection, and this response was boosted following the second dose (Supplementary Fig. 5). As shown in Fig. 2, GMMA displaying O1v1 elicited an anti-O1v1-specific IgG response that was not significantly different from that induced by GMMA displaying O1v2. Similarly, GMMA displaying O1v2 elicited an anti-O1v2-specific IgG response that was not significantly different from that induced by GMMA displaying O1v1. SBA data confirmed cross-reactivity between the O1v1 and O1v2 OAg, and validated the functionality of these sera against capsulated Kp strains. Conversely, O2v1 and O2v2 GMMA elicited significantly higher IgG and SBA titers against the strain carrying the homologous OAg than against all other galactan-based structures. Interestingly, GMMA displaying O2v1 induced a low anti-O1v1 OAg-specific IgG response, while GMMA displaying O2v2 induced a low anti-O1v2 OAg-specific IgG response, although this cross-reactivity was not confirmed to be functional in SBA against O1v1 nor O1v2 strains.

Immune response elicited in mice by Kp OAg and KAg on GMMA

GMMA obtained from strain NTCT9138 and its mutants, characterized by the presence of both OAg (O1v1 or O2v1) and KAg (K18) were compared to GMMA obtained from strain NCTC11228 and corresponding mutants, characterized by the presence of OAg (O1v1 or O2v1) only. GMMA were compared in mice at the same OAg dose (0.5 µg of O2v1, shared backbone in both O1v1 and O2v1 OAg) to investigate the relative influence of the two displayed polysaccharides on the overall immunogenicity. GMMA displaying K18 only or no polysaccharides at all, were included in the study as additional controls and normalized based on the protein antigen content to the previous groups.

The resulting sera were analyzed in ELISA to measure IgG responses specific for the different polysaccharides. All GMMA were able to induce a significant IgG response to the displayed OAg and, if present, to the displayed KAg. Specific antibodies were generated against both O1v1 (Fig. 3A) or O2v1 OAg (Fig. 3B), as well as against K18 (Fig. 3C). Importantly, the presence of K18 did not negatively impact the immune response elicited against the co-displayed OAg (Fig. 3A, B), and vice versa (Fig. 3C). This was verified both for longer O1v1 OAg by comparing KAg-neg:O1v1 and K18:O1v1 GMMA and shorter O2v1 OAg by comparing KAg-neg:O2v1 and K18:O2v1 GMMA (Fig. 3A, B). Functional anti-OAg immune responses and lack of immune-interference for the presence of KAg were verified, using SBA strains displaying homologous OAg and mismatched KAg (Fig. 3D).

Moreover, another animal study was performed to test the immunogenicity of GMMA from strains NCTC9182 (K62:O1v1) and NCTC8172 (K64:O2v1), displaying both OAg and KAg, either formulated singularly or co-formulated, to verify the possibility of combining more antigens in one formulation with no immune interference. GMMA were tested at the same OAg dose of 0.3 µg and mixed in a 1:1 ratio. The humoral response induced by K62:O1v1 GMMA and K64:O2v1 GMMA was investigated by ELISA, in comparison to corresponding mono-component formulations. The bicomponent formulation was able to induce similar anti-OAg (both anti-O1v1 and anti-O2v1, Fig. 4A) and anti-KAg (both anti-K62 and anti-K64, Fig. 4B) IgG response respect to the corresponding mono-component formulations. Importantly, lack of immuno-interference for functional anti-OAg immune responses was verified via SBA, using test strains displaying homologous OAg and mismatched KAg (Fig. 4C).

Discussion

Both OAg and KAg polysaccharides have been proposed as potential target antigens for vaccine development against Kp. Recent studies have indicated the possibility to combine more prevalent KAg and OAg for a broadly protective vaccine against Kp19. GMMA have been proposed as a delivery system for OAg supporting the development of multi-component vaccines against Shigella25 and non-typhoidal Salmonellae (NTS)26,27. GMMA can be produced at high yields using a simple and robust manufacturing process22 and are a promising technology for the development of multivalent vaccines, as it is needed for Kp.

Here, the GMMA technology was applied to Kp, first verifying the suitability of the genetic engineering strategy (i.e. tolR gene knock-out) that has widely been applied to Enterobacteriaceae. Interestingly, Kp GMMA were characterized by great amounts of two distinct polysaccharides, not only the OAg but also the KAg. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of a GMMA displaying a Group 1 capsule (differently from group 4 capsules observed in certain Shigella GMMA28 or Group 2 capsules like the Vi antigen heterologous displayed in Salmonella Paratyphi A GMMA29). Indeed, while Group 1 capsules are thought to be anchored to the outer membrane through non-covalent interactions via the protein Wzi30, they have also been described to be covalently attached to lipid A to form a composite structure known as K-LPS31. Both forms could be present on GMMA, and further research is needed to fully investigate the nature of the interaction between KAg and the bacterial outer membrane. Moreover, as previously shown for Shigella and Salmonella GMMA, OAg variants were easily generated in Kp through genetic manipulation of the OAg biosynthetic genes, allowing direct comparison of GMMA derived from the same parental strain differing only in OAg structural features in a mouse model21,26,32.

High cross-reactivity was observed between O1v1 and O1v2 serotypes, most likely due to the shared Gal-II epitope. The immunodominance of Gal-II with respect to the underlying Gal-I or Gal-III epitopes was further supported by the low reactivity of sera elicited against O1v1 toward O2v1 and sera elicited against O1v2 toward O2v2. Cross-reactivity between the O1 and O2 serotypes was observed by David Goldblatt et al. during assay qualification using rabbit sera generated against an OAg bioconjugate vaccine33. Moreover, notable cross-reactivity between the O1v1/O1v2 and O2v1/O2v2 serotypes was observed testing plasma antibody responses to OAg in a cohort of patients with Kp34, underscoring the potential for streamlined vaccine formulations. In the current study, and differently from our previous study on a set of neonatal sepsis clinical isolates19, no functional cross-reactivity was found between O2v1 and O2v2, highlighting how results could be specific for a given Kp strain and its inherent characteristics like presence of capsule, capsule type, complement sensitivity, etc.

High levels of capsule expression have been reported18 to obscure sub-capsular surface targets, potentially reducing the effectiveness of OAg-based vaccines. In this study, anti-OAg antibodies generated through GMMA vaccination resulted functional in SBA against capsulated strains. Furthermore, GMMA co-displaying OAg and KAg were able to induce anti-OAg antibodies with similar levels of bactericidal activity to those induced by corresponding GMMA without KAg, against strains with homologous OAg and mismatched KAg. This lack of negative immune interference was verified both for O1 and shorter O2 OAg. Whether anti-OAg and anti-KAg antibodies could synergize to improve the overall bacterial killing remains an area of future research.

To advance toward clinical applications, bacteria used to generate GMMA should be further mutated to reduce possible reactogenicity due to the presence of wild-type lipid A, while ensuring that the desired OAg/protein and KAg/protein ratio in the final GMMA are maintained35. This is particularly important for KAg as spontaneous mutants can arise and be selected in the genetic engineering phases, resulting in loss of capsule production, as observed in our study after the introduction of the tolR mutation in strain NCTC11228 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Interestingly, the lipid A detoxification strategy for Kp GMMA has been recently described, involving the deletion of pagP and lpxM genes36. In the same study, researchers enhanced the expression of OAg biosynthesis, successfully co-expressing both O1 and O2 OAg on the same GMMA. This finding supports the feasibility of co-expressing more than two polysaccharides on the same particle. Additional data have been generated showing the ability of GMMA to display more than one polysaccharide on their surface with no negative immune interference37. While other preclinical reports of Kp outer membrane vesicle-based vaccines have demonstrated efficacy in mouse peritoneal challenge models36, we acknowledge that the absence of such data in our current study represents a temporary limitation. These crucial in vivo protection experiments remain a key step for future research to fully validate the protective potential of GMMA candidates and select optimal multi-component formulation.

Our results show the possibility of presenting both KAg and OAg on the same Kp GMMA. This means that co-formulation of more GMMA could result in combination of multiple polysaccharides in a relatively simpler formulation. Here preliminary data have been generated showing no immune interference when two different GMMA are combined to present four different polysaccharides. Indeed, IgG response elicited against each polysaccharide and bactericidal anti-OAg titers were similar in mono-component and in bicomponent formulations. Overall, these results are encouraging and pave the way for testing a more complex combination of GMMA that could potentially cover a higher number of different K:O serotypes, with broad protection against the most prevalent circulating strains. Testing high valency formulation would support the feasibility of using the low-cost GMMA technology to develop a multivalent candidate vaccine against Kp.

Materials and methods

Bacteria strains, mutants generation and growth conditions

Klebsiella pneumoniae wild type strains were obtained from Public Health England (replaced by UK Health Security Agency and Office for Health Improvement and Disparities) NCTC collection (Table 1).

To generate the mutants, the kanamycin resistance gene aph was used to replace the tolR, wbbY and wzm-wzt genes. The resistance cassette replacement constructs were amplified from the pKD4 vector using forward and reverse primers composed of 50 bp homologous to the flanking regions of the gene to be deleted and approximately 20 bp at the 3’end matching the flanking region of the resistance gene. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1. PCR products were purified and were used to transform recombination-prone Kp recipient cells carrying pAJD434 by following methods described previously38. After each gene deletion, the kanamycin resistance gene was removed through FLP-mediated recombination using the pCP20 plasmid to yield marker less mutant strains. To complement the glmABC genes, a plasmid was synthesised at Twist Bioscience, using a high-copy number vector with a pMB1 origin of replication and kanamycin resistance marker, and cloning the coding DNA sequences (CDS) after Shigella ompC promoter (Supplementary Table 2). This plasmid was used to transform electro-competent Kp recipient cells.

All bacterial strains were grown at 30 °C in liquid Luria–Bertani (LB) medium in a rotary shaker for 16 h. For GMMA production, overnight cultures were diluted in HTMC medium (15 g/L glycerol, 30 g/L yeast extract, 0.5 g/L MgSO4, 5 g/L KH2PO4, 20 g/L K2HPO4) to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.3 and grown at 30 °C in a rotary shaker for 8 h using baffled flasks with a liquid to air volume ratio of 1:5. After each growth, bacteria were pelleted through centrifugation at 5000 × g for 45 min. Cell-free supernatants were recovered and filtered through 0.22 μm Stericup filters (Millipore). After ultracentrifugation of filtered supernatants at 175,000 × g for 2 h at 4 °C, the resulting pellet, containing GMMA, was washed with PBS, further ultra-centrifuged at 175,000 × g for 2 h and finally resuspended in PBS39.

Characterization of GMMA and polysaccharides on GMMA

GMMA purity was assessed by HPLC-SEC analysis40; total protein content was estimated by micro BCA using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as a reference following the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Scientific); GMMA size was determined by DLS40.

Total polysaccharide content, KAg and OAg, were quantified by direct HPAEC-PAD analysis on all GMMA, because KAg and OAg were composed of peculiar and distinct sugar monomers24,41. In case of NCTC 9182 K62:O1v1 GMMA, KAg and OAg are undistinguishable by HPAEC-PAD. At first, GMMA were hydrolyzed with AcOH 1% for 2 h at 0 °C, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 rcf at 4 °C for 30 min. KAg and OAg, in the supernatant, were isolated and fractionated via HPLC-SEC. TSK G3000 PWXL column with a TSK PWXL guard column was used for fractionation, with 0.1 M NaCl, 0.1 M NaH2PO4, pH 7.2 buffer as mobile phase at the flow rate 0.5 mL/min. HPAEC-PAD was performed on the collected KAg and OAg separated fractions24.

Polysaccharide extraction from GMMA was performed by boiling with AcOH as previously described42,43. KAg and OAg populations were characterized by HPLC–SEC with differential refractive index (dRI) detection to estimate the molecular size distribution. The samples were run on a TSK gel G3000 PWXL column (30 cm × 7.8 mm; particle size 7 µm; cod. 808,021) with TSK gel PWXL guard column (4.0 cm × 6.0 mm; particle size 12 µm; cod.808033; Tosoh Bioscience Tokyo, Japan). The mobile phase was 0.1 M NaCl, 0.1 MNaH2PO4, 5% CH3CN, pH 7.2 at the flow rate of 0.5 mL/min (isocratic method for 35 min)39.

1H-NMR spectroscopy was used to confirm polysaccharide identity and structure. All NMR experiments were performed with a Bruker AEON AVANCE III 600 MHz spectrometer equipped with a high-precision temperature controller using a 5 mm QCI CryoProbe. NMR spectra were recorded at 50.0 ± 0.1 °C. The transmitter was set at the water frequency (4.70 ppm). Proton spectra were acquired using a 90° pulse duration automatically calculated and collected with 32 K data points over a 12 ppm spectral width, accumulating 128 scans. Spectra were processed by applying an exponential function to the FID with a line broadening of 0.80 Hz to increase the signal-to-noise ratio and then Fourier transformed. Data acquisition and processing were performed with TopSpin 3.5 software package (Bruker BioSpin)32.

Immunogenicity studies in mice

Kp GMMA were tested in mice. GSK is committed to the Replacement, Reduction and Refinement of animal studies (3Rs). Non-animal models and alternative technologies are part of our strategy and employed where possible. When animals are required, the application of robust study design principles and peer review minimizes animal use, reduces harm, and improves benefit in studies. All methods are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. Animal studies were performed at GSK Animal Resources Centre in Siena under the animal project 643/2021-PR approved by the Italian Ministry of Health. That was ethically reviewed and carried out in accordance with European Directive 2010/63/EEC and the GSK policy on the Care, Welfare and Treatment of Animals. Five weeks old female CD1 mice were used. Based on historical data with GMMA vaccines tested in CD1 mice, and considering SDLog10 of 0.3, a number of 10 mice per group has 80% power to detect a fold difference of geomeans equal to 2.5 times between two groups (by using Student’s t-test and a = 0.05). Randomization was used at animal arrival to group them in cages (5 mice/cage) and at sera collection timepoints. Mice were immunized intraperitoneally with vaccine at day 0 and 28 and observed following immunizations, no sign of ill health or death was recorded. Single sera were collected at days 27 and 42. Individual mouse sera were tested for anti-O1v1, anti-O2v1, anti-O1v2, anti-O2v2, anti-K18, anti-K64 and anti-K62 total IgG by ELISA19. Results are reported as anti-antigen specific IgG EU/mL. One ELISA unit is defined as the reciprocal of the standard serum dilution that gives an absorbance value equal to 1. Single sera collected 2 weeks after second injection were tested for SBA against a panel of Kp strains using a high throughput assay based on luminescent readout19,44. In particular, strain NCTC11228 from PHE (and isogenic mutants) and 2 clinical isolates collected in the BARNARDS study4 were used. The % of Baby Rabbit Complement (BRC) was identified for each strain as the highest % not causing any aspecific killing. NCTC11228 was tested with 10% BRC, clinical isolates K2:O1v2 and K102:O2v1 were tested with 50% and 30% BRC, respectively19. Results of the assay were expressed as the IC50, the reciprocal serum dilution that resulted in a 50% reduction of luminescence and thus corresponding to 50% growth inhibition of the bacteria present in the assay. Titers below lowest dilution tested (100) were assigned a value of half of it.

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10. The non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis analysis with post-hoc Dunn’s test or the Mann–Whitney analysis were used respectively to compare multiple groups or two groups. Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank two-tailed test was used to compare results from the same group at different time points. For each experimental group, there were no exclusions of animals.
