Conservation and management implications of genetic and geometric scale variation in the ancient fish giant featherback (Chitala lopis)
Authors: Boby Muslimin, Gunawan Muhammad, Arif Wibowo, Kurniawan Kurniawan, Vitas Atmadi Prakoso, Rendy Ginanjar, Mochammad Zamroni, Bayu Kreshna Adhitya Sumarto, Septiana Sri Astuti, Deni Irawan, Agus Priyadi, Mirna Dwirastina, Siti Rachmi Indahsari, Haryono Haryono, Hadi Dahruddin, Indah Lestari, Flandrianto Sih Palimirmo, Nunak Nafiqoh, Mochamad Chandra Wirawan Arief, Asep Sahidin, Oriza Savitri Ariantie, Indah Anggraini Yusanti, Sujaka Nugraha, Ivor G Stuart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiant featherback (Chitala lopis) is an ancient species of freshwater fish considered globally extinct until extant populations were re‐discovered in the Sundaland region of Indonesia in 2022. To improve management of the remaining populations, this study used genomic DNA and landmark‐based geometric morphometric analyses of fish scales to evaluate whether these approaches can discriminate among phylogenetic clades, sexes and broad habitat ecotypes inhabited by extant C. lopis populations. DNA analyses of 39 specimens from eight rivers provided the first evidence of two phylogenetic clades: (i) those from Central and South Borneo and (ii) those from West Borneo, Riau, South Sumatra and Jambi. Scale morphology was characterised across eight body areas using scale dimensions (relative height and width to standard length and height‐to‐width ratio), centroid size and Procrustes shape coordinates, with repeated‐measures structure accounted for using linear mixed models and Procrustes ANOVA with a randomised residual permutation procedure (RRPP). Scale dimensions and centroid size varied significantly among body areas (all p < 0.001), with lateral and dorsal regions bearing the largest scales and the pectoral region the smallest. Clade membership had a small but significant effect on relative scale height (H/SL; β = −0.44, p = 0.024), with Clade 2 individuals consistently showing lower values than Clade 1, though scale geometry overall showed limited promise as a rapid non‐destructive stock discriminator. Both clade and sex had small but significant effects on Procrustes scale shape (R 2 = 0.015 and 0.013, respectively; both p = 0.001), while phenotypic trajectory analysis revealed that males exhibited a significantly greater amplitude of scale shape change across body positions than females (trajectory magnitude: M = 0.289, F = 0.207; p = 0.034), despite both sexes following parallel shape trajectories along the body axis. These results provide a novel morphometric baseline for C. lopis and highlight the importance of body‐position‐specific and multivariate approaches when characterising scale morphology in endangered freshwater fishes.Keywords: Chitala lopis, featherback fish, morphometrics, scales, Sundaland

INTRODUCTION

Freshwater fish are one of the most diverse groups of vertebrates globally but are also under immense threat from human activities, such as dams fragmenting migration pathways, pollution, over‐fishing and climate change (Ahmed et al., 2022; Su et al., 2021). Tropical Southern Asia, including Indochina, Malaysia, Thailand and Indonesia, are a biodiversity hotspot exemplified by the Sunda Region of Indonesia, or the Sundaland, which is known for its freshwater fish diversity with approximately 900 species, of which 50% are endemic (Chua et al., 2019; Collen et al., 2014; Giam et al., 2012). The main islands of Java, Borneo (Kalimantan) and Sumatra are characterised by a diverse array of aquatic habitats that support approximately 10 species of primitive fish that evolved in freshwater known as the featherback fishes from the family Osteoglossiformes (bony‐tongued), including the giant featherback (Chitala lopis), which grow up to 1.5 m long.

Chitala lopis populations have declined throughout their southern Asian distribution exemplified by Indonesian populations have undergone a precipitous decline due to over‐fishing, along with river pollution, urban land development and agriculture (Chong et al., 2010; Siddik et al., 2015). The IUCN has eventually categorised C. lopis as extinct in 2019 (Ng, 2020) and in Indonesia they had not been seen for 160 years. This status, however, was contested by local testimonies (Nugroho et al., 2019; Nugroho et al., 2020), and the presence of extant specimens was officially confirmed in 2022 (Wibowo et al., 2023). Re‐discovery of several island populations of giant featherback was encouraging, but they remain threatened with few data to support their conservation and management, including the extant geographic distribution, population dynamics and local adaptation to the diversity of habitats found on the Indonesian archipelago.

With the return of a once ‘lost’ species comes a need to determine whether isolated extant populations have undergone local adaptation or remain genetically and phenotypically similar (Wibowo et al., 2023). Our study used both DNA and geometric morphometric scale analyses to determine whether extant C. lopis populations have formed genetically and morphologically distinct populations among the Indonesian islands. Where DNA could confirm divergence then we predicted that there could also be differences in scale morphology (e.g., shape, size, meristics), which typically occur after geographic isolation and where fish inhabit different habitat ecotypes (Garduño‐Paz et al., 2010; Viertler et al., 2021). Landmark‐based geometric morphometry analyses, as applied to giant featherback scales, was evaluated as a simple non‐destructive and cost‐effective tool to discriminate among different phylogenetic clades, sexual dimorphism and environmental preferences, (Ibáñez et al., 2017); this technique has been previously proven a useful population discriminant for many other fish species (Bánó et al., 2024; Poulet et al., 2005; Staszny et al., 2012; Ujjania et al., 2023).

To assist in managing the few remaining giant featherback populations, our first objective was to use DNA analysis to identify if different phylogenetic clades of C. lopis were present on the Indonesian archipelago. Our second objective was to evaluate landmark‐based scale geometric morphometrics—encompassing scale dimensions, centroid size (CS) and Procrustes shape analysis—to determine whether these approaches can discriminate among phylogenetic clades, sexes or broad habitat ecotypes. This research is important to provide a novel baseline for whether scale shape analyses can be used to rapidly distinguish among the remaining isolated populations of C. lopis in tropical Southern Asia and to help formulate appropriate management and conservation programmes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Fish samples

From April through September 2022, C. lopis samples were collected from across various regions in Indonesia's Sundaland area, that is, Sumatra, Java and Kalimantan Island, with a total of 33 sites and totalling 39 individuals. These regions encompassed the Kalimantan Island (covering South, Central and West Kalimantan Provinces), Sumatra Island (spanning South Sumatra, Riau and Jambi Provinces) and Banten Province representing Java Island (see Figure 1). The habitat ecotypes observed at the sampling sites consist of slow‐flowing lowland areas. Our in‐field sampling methods sought to maximise catch of a protected and uncommon species. Hence, we employed a range of fishing gears, including fishing rods, nets, traps, gill nets and cast nets. Each giant featherback recorded in the dataset of Wibowo et al. (2023) was photographed, individually labelled, and its capture location documented, including geographic coordinates (Table 1). Following this, we preserved the specimen in 96% C2H6O (ethanol) for DNA barcoding and phylogenetic analyses.

Recognising that C. lopis is protected under the regulations of the Ministry of Marine Affairs and Fisheries of the Republic of Indonesia (MMAF‐RI), we ensured compliance with all necessary permissions prior to beginning our sample collection. The Agency for Research and Human Resources Development, MMAF‐RI, provided the Research Permit (No: 1584/BRSDM‐BRPBATPP/TU.420/X/2021), and the Directorate General of Marine Space Management, MMAF‐RI, granted Collection Permit Quotas for protected fish species for research (No. 36 tahun 2023). While we acknowledge the benefits of a larger sample size for in‐depth statistical analysis, we adhered to the limitations set by the Indonesian authorities regarding the number of specimens permissible for our research.

Sequencing and phylogenetic inferences

Genomic DNA was isolated from pinna dorsalis using the Qiagen DNeasy 96 tissue extraction kit, adhering to the provided protocol. Amplification of a 652‐base pair segment of the cytochrome oxidase I gene's 5′ region (COI) was achieved with the FishF1/FishR1 primer pair (Ward et al., 2005) or the C_FishF1t1/C_FishR1t1 primer cocktail (Ivanova et al., 2007). PCR reactions were conducted in a Veriti 96‐well Fast thermocycler (ABI‐Applied Biosystems). The reaction mix for the primer cocktail included 5.0 μL of 2× Buffer, 3.3 μL of ultrapure water, 1.0 μL of each primer (10 μM), 0.2 μL of Phire Hot Start II DNA polymerase (5 U) and 0.5 μL of template DNA (~50 ng) in a total volume of 10.0 μL. For the FishF1/FishR1 primers, the total volume was 25.0 μL, comprising 12.5 μL of Taq ready mix, 9.5 μL of water, 1.0 μL of each primer and 1.0 μL of DNA template.

Thermocycling conditions for the primer cocktail involved an initial denaturation at 98°C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of 98°C for 5 s, 56°C for 20 s and 72°C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. For the FishF1/FishR1 primers, the program began with a denaturation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 35 cycles at 94°C for 60 s, 48°C for 60 s and 72°C for 60 s, ending with a final extension at 72°C for 7 min. PCR products were purified using ExoSap‐IT (USB Corporation, Cleveland, OH, USA) and sequenced bidirectionally. Sequences and related data were uploaded to the BOLD database (Ratnasingham & Hebert, 2013) and GenBank, which are detailed in Table 1.

Phylogenetic tree reconstruction was performed using IQ‐TREE 3 (v3.1.0; Wong et al., 2025) on a sequence alignment of 41 COI sequences comprising 616 nucleotide sites, of which 51 (8.3%) were parsimony‐informative. The optimal substitution model was selected using ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017) based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), identifying HKY + F + G4 (Hasegawa–Kishino–Yano model with empirical base frequencies and a discrete gamma distribution, four rate categories) as the best‐fit model. Maximum‐likelihood (ML) tree searches were conducted under this model using Chitala chitala (GenBank accession MK577977) as the outgroup. Branch support was evaluated using both the ultrafast bootstrap approximation (UFBoot2; 5000 replicates; Hoang et al., 2018) and the SH‐aLRT branch test; nodes were considered well‐supported when SH‐aLRT ≥80% and ultrafast bootstrap ≥95% (Guindon et al., 2010). The present phylogenetic analysis expands upon Wibowo et al. (2023) by including additional specimens collected in 2022 from South and Central Borneo (Kalimantan), with all sequences deposited in GenBank (Table 1).

Scale samples

Scale samples were collected from eight body areas in each preserved fish specimen (Zhu et al., 2019) as depicted in Figure 2. These scales were then stored dry in 2 mL microcentrifuge tubes. Each tube was labelled corresponding to its respective fish sample and body area.

Illustrative diagram of the great featherback.

Microscopic identification and morphometric analysis

Dried scale samples were examined using an Olympus Stereomicroscope System (Olympus Stereomicroscope System SZX16, Evident Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Images were captured with a digital camera connected to a microscope (Olympus DP28, Evident Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), with magnifications ranging from 8× to 32×. Subsequently, for more detailed observations, we further examined the scales under a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Tabletop Microscope TM3030, Hitachi High‐Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) at a higher magnification of 100×.

Twenty‐one landmark points determined using MakeFan software (Figure 3) were then marked using ImageJ software (the National Institute of Health, USA) (Traverso et al., 2024). Subsequently, the height and width of the scales were measured using ImageJ also (Panda et al., 2024). Generalised Procrustes analysis (GPA) was performed on the 21‐landmark configurations using a custom Python pipeline (scipy, numpy and pandas packages) to superimpose all specimens by iteratively removing differences in position, scale and orientation. Procrustes shape coordinates and CSs were extracted for each specimen and body area. Scale height and width were expressed as proportions of individual standard length (SL) to account for body size variation (Bánó et al., 2024).

Three landmark points (red dots) and 18 pseudo‐landmark points (orange dots) for geometric analysis (a) and the measuring dimensions for morphometric analysis (b).

Habitat ecotype

The aquatic environment of C. lopis was characterised using water quality measurements across three main islands (Java, Sumatra and Borneo). Ecotype classification is important for understanding environmental variability among habitats and for establishing ecotype‐specific reference conditions for water quality assessment. Water quality parameters were measured using a multiparameter water quality meter (Lutron DO‐5510) for dissolved oxygen, temperature, pH and conductivity.

Statistical analyses

Because scale samples from the eight body areas were collected from the same individual specimens, body area constitutes a repeated‐measures factor and observations within individuals are not independent. Accordingly, the effects of body area, phylogenetic clade and sex on scale dimensions—relative scale height to width ratio (H/W), relative scale height (H/SL) and relative scale width (W/SL)—were tested using linear mixed models (LMM) with individual specimen as a random effect, implemented via the lmer() function in the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015) in R, with p‐values obtained using the lmerTest package (Kuznetsova et al., 2017). Fixed effects included body area, clade and sex. Estimated coefficients (β) and standard errors are reported for clade and sex effects. Pairwise comparisons among body areas were conducted using Dunn's test with Bonferroni correction via the emmeans package (Lenth, 2023). Results were visualised in RStudio using the ggplot2, lme4, lmerTest and emmeans packages.

CS, a scale‐invariant size measure calculated as the square root of the summed squared distances of each landmark from the configuration centroid (Dryden & Mardia, 1998), was extracted for each specimen and body area following GPA. Differences in CS among the eight body areas were assessed using a Kruskal–Wallis H‐test with Dunn's post‐hoc comparisons (Bonferroni correction). Comparisons of CS between clades and between sexes within each body region were conducted using Mann–Whitney U tests (α = 0.05), with Cohen's d reported as a measure of effect size.

To account for this repeated‐measures structure, effects of clade, sex, body area and their two‐way interactions on Procrustes shape coordinates were tested using Procrustes ANOVA with a randomised residual permutation procedure (RRPP; 999 permutations), implemented via the lm.rrpp() function in the RRPP package (Collyer & Adams, 2018) in R. Partial R 2 values are reported as effect size estimates. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was also used to test for differences among habitat ecotypes based on water quality parameters.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed independently for each body area on Procrustes shape coordinates using the prcomp() function in R. Scatter plots of PC1 versus PC2 were generated with 95% confidence ellipses per group (clade, sex and clade × sex). Canonical Variate Analysis (CVA) was performed per body area using linear discriminant analysis (MASS::lda() in R) to maximise group separation for clade, sex and clade × sex comparisons. For analyses with only two groups (e.g., sex), canonical scores along the single discriminant axis (CV1) are presented as strip plots with density overlays. All PCA and CVA plots were created in RStudio using the ggplot2, MASS, ggforce and scales packages. PCA was also applied to water quality parameters following standardisation of variables to examine patterns of environmental variation among populations. Dimension reduction and rotated component structures were evaluated using the psych package, and correlations among water quality variables were analysed using the tidyverse, dplyr and ggplot2 packages in RStudio.

Phenotypic trajectory analysis (PTA) Collyer and Adams (2007) was used to characterise progressive changes in scale shape across the eight body areas, treated as an ordered positional sequence from dorsal anterior (Area 1) to pectoral (Area 8), within each clade and sex. PTA was implemented using the trajectory.analysis() function in the geomorph package (Baken et al., 2021) in R. Trajectory magnitude (path length in shape space), direction and shape were compared between clades and between sexes using 999 permutations, with pairwise p‐values reported.

Thin‐plate spline (TPS) deformation grids were used to visualise shape differences between the grand mean configuration (reference) and each group mean (target: Clade 1, Clade 2, Female, Male) within each body area, implemented using plotRefToTarget() in the geomorph package with a magnification factor of 2×. Direct deformation grids, showing shape change from one group mean directly to another (Clade 1 → Clade 2; Female → Male), were additionally produced to facilitate direct visual comparison between groups. A wireframe connecting adjacent landmarks along the scale outline was superimposed on each grid to facilitate interpretation of local shape changes.

RESULTS

Phylogenetic reconstruction and clade assignment

The maximum‐likelihood phylogenetic tree constructed with 10,000 bootstrap replicates is shown in Figure 4. DNA sequencing grouped C. lopis samples into two geographically structured mitochondrial lineages. Clade 1 comprises populations from Central and South Borneo provinces, whereas Clade 2 comprises populations from West Borneo, Riau, South Sumatera and Jambi provinces. The C. lopis samples formed a distinct lineage separate from both C. borneensis and C. chitala. Within Clade 2, samples from Sumatra and West Borneo showed close genetic association, whereas populations from Central and South Borneo formed a separate grouping within Clade 1. These phylogenetic relationships provided a clear basis for defining the two clades and supported subsequent analyses of morphological variation in scale shape among populations.

Maximum‐likelihood phylogenetic tree of Great featherback (Chitala lopis) populations included in this study, constructed with 10,000 bootstrap replicates.

The phylogenetic structure observed in this study differs from that reported by Wibowo et al. (2023). Whereas the present analysis focuses on intraspecific clade structure within C. lopis across Borneo and Sumatra, Wibowo et al. (2023) included comparisons with C. hypselonotus to address broader taxonomic resolution and species delimitation.

Scale structure of giant featherback

Upon close examination, the scales of C. lopis presented remarkable structural attributes, with a cycloid morphology. Characterised by their oval form, these scales displayed a smooth, subtle curvature on their posterior, or cranial portion (Figure 5), which remains exposed to external environmental factors. The anterior part of each scale is protected beneath the posterior margin of the scale that comes before it, with the cycloid scales' imbricate arrangement being more shielded and less visible, and exhibiting a pronounced and more defined tapering curvature. A unique observation was made in certain samples that showed atypical scale shapes, exemplified in Figure 5j. Notably, such aberrations in shape were infrequent and could likely be attributed predominantly to the aftermath of healing processes on the scales. The posterior segment of these scales presented another intriguing feature: a deeper hue with dispersed pigment spots. These spots appeared to radiate outwards, forming a noticeable angle of approximately 60° relative to the scale's central focus.

The diversity in scale shapes among the body areas was particularly evident upon further observation. Specifically, certain scales stood out with their posterior portion being substantially more expansive than both their lateral fields and anterior, manifesting in a conspicuous tapering shape, a feature vividly illustrated in Figure 5e. Conversely, some scales delineated a dominant lateral field, rendering the scale with a more circular or rounded appearance as demonstrated in Figure 5b. Nevertheless, a significant number of scales maintained a harmonious proportion between their posterior, lateral field and anterior parts, ensuring a more aligned and straightened form, as captured in Figure 5a,c,d,f,g–i.

Distinctive to the species, the focal point on the scales was a prominent feature. Its vertical position displayed a consistent trend, leaning more towards the posterior section than its anterior counterpart. However, the focal points had variable horizontal positioning, sometimes occupying a central position and sometimes veering distinctly towards the left or right. Another intriguing observation was the occasional presence of organic structures, resembling fine hair, concentrated around the focal area in some scales, as visualised in Figure 6. Proceeding from this focal point and extending towards the anterior were long, linear structures, termed ‘radii’ as depicted in Figure 5 Ra. While these structures varied in their number and length on different scales, their overarching layout and pattern remained relatively constant. Additionally, the entirety of the scales displayed circular configurations known as ‘circuli’, with each circulus maintaining a variable but discernible distance from its neighbours (Figure 5 Ci).

Variation among body areas

All three scale dimensions varied significantly among the eight body areas (LMM, all p < 0.001; Figure 7a,d,g). For H/SL and W/SL, the lateral anterior region (Area 3) bore the largest scales (H/SL: 5.70 ± 0.75; W/SL: 3.78 ± 0.58), followed closely by the dorsal posterior region (Area 2; H/SL: 5.55 ± 0.72; W/SL: 3.78 ± 0.52), while the pectoral (Area 8) and caudal (Area 7) regions consistently bore the smallest scales (H/SL: 2.84 ± 0.66 and 3.10 ± 0.70, respectively; W/SL: 1.68 ± 0.43 and 2.09 ± 0.48). Post‐hoc comparisons confirmed that Areas 2 and 3 were each significantly larger than Areas 7 and 8 for both H/SL and W/SL (Bonferroni‐corrected p < 0.001; Table 2; Figure 7a,d).

The H/W ratio showed a distinct pattern from H/SL and W/SL. Rather than peaking at the lateral regions, H/W increased progressively from the dorsal anterior area (Area 1: 1.40 ± 0.14, group a) towards the posterior, reaching its highest values at the ventral posterior (Area 6: 1.65 ± 0.13) and pectoral regions (Area 8: 1.71 ± 0.21, group c). This indicates that pectoral scales are disproportionately taller relative to their width compared with scales from other body regions, while dorsal anterior scales are the most elongated in the anterior–posterior direction.

Differences between phylogenetic clades

A significant effect of phylogenetic clade was detected for H/SL (LMM: β = −0.44, SE = 0.19, p = 0.024; Figure 7b), indicating that Clade 2 individuals (West Borneo, Riau, South Sumatra and Jambi) had consistently lower relative scale height than Clade 1 individuals (Central and South Borneo) across all body areas. The difference was most pronounced at the ventral anterior (Area 5; Clade 1: 4.91, Clade 2: 4.27) and pectoral regions (Area 8; Clade 1: 3.20, Clade 2: 2.59), and smallest at the lateral anterior area (Area 3; Clade 1: 5.82, Clade 2: 5.62). Notably, the body‐area trajectory was parallel between the two clades—both groups followed the same rank ordering across positions—indicating a consistent but uniform offset in scale height rather than a region‐specific divergence.

Neither W/SL (β = −0.25, SE = 0.13, p = 0.068) nor H/W (β = −0.02, SE = 0.03, p = 0.378) differed significantly between clades, suggesting that clade‐level differentiation in scale morphology is specific to the relative height dimension (Figure 7b,h; Table 2).

Sexual dimorphism

No significant sexual dimorphism was detected in any of the three scale dimensions (LMM: H/SL β = −0.14, p = 0.461; W/SL β = −0.10, p = 0.447; H/W β = +0.01, p = 0.629; Figure 7c,f,i; Table 2). Although female specimens tended to show marginally higher H/SL and W/SL values than males across most body areas, these differences were small and statistically non‐significant, indicating that scale dimensions do not reliably distinguish between sexes in C. lopis.

Centroid size

CS, the square root of the summed squared distances of each landmark from the configuration centroid (Dryden & Mardia, 1998), differed significantly among the eight body areas (Kruskal–Wallis H = 112.6, df = 7, p < 0.001; Figure 8). The lateral anterior (Area 3: 4715 ± 1650 pixel units) and dorsal posterior regions (Area 2: 4643 ± 1611) harboured the largest scales and were not significantly different from each other or from the lateral posterior region (Area 4: 4382 ± 1652; all Bonferroni‐corrected p ≥ 0.385). The pectoral (Area 8: 2134 ± 791) and caudal (Area 7: 2580 ± 1027) regions bore the smallest scales and were not significantly different from one another (p = 0.035 uncorrected, ns after Bonferroni), but were each significantly smaller than all mid‐body regions (Areas 2–4; all p < 0.001). The dorsal anterior region (Area 1: 3530 ± 1186) occupied an intermediate position, differing significantly from the two largest regions (Areas 2 and 3; p < 0.001) but not from the ventral or lateral posterior areas (Areas 4–6; all p ≥ 0.132). Overall, scales in the lateral anterior region were approximately 2.2 times larger than those in the pectoral region.

Scale shape variation (geometric morphometrics)

Procrustes ANOVA—Sources of shape variation

Because scale samples from the eight body areas were collected from the same individual specimens, body area was treated as a repeated‐measures factor and observations were therefore non‐independent. Accordingly, the effects of clade, sex, body area and their two‐way interactions on Procrustes shape coordinates were tested using Procrustes ANOVA with a randomised residual permutation procedure (RRPP; 999 permutations; Collyer & Adams, 2018).

In the clade model (Table 3A), body area was by far the dominant source of shape variation (R 2 = 0.157, F 7,296 = 8.19, p = 0.001), accounting for more than 15% of total shape variance. The dominant effect of body position relative to clade or sex demonstrates that sampling location on the body is a primary determinant of scale geometry and must be standardised in future morphometric stock‐identification studies. Clade had a small but statistically significant effect (R 2 = 0.015, F 1,296 = 5.50, p = 0.001), whereas the Clade × Body area interaction was not significant (R 2 = 0.015, p = 0.808), indicating that the shape trajectories of the two clades across body areas were parallel rather than divergent.

In the combined PC1–PC2 scatter plot (Figure 9a), specimens from different body areas formed partially overlapping clusters with distinct centroids, reflecting a continuum of shape variation rather than discrete groupings. Sexual dimorphism in scale shape

Per‐area Procrustes ANOVA revealed a significant effect of sex on scale shape in the lateral anterior region (Area 3: F 1,36 = 2.31, R 2 = 0.060, p = 0.046), and borderline effects in the caudal region (Area 7: p = 0.085). In the remaining areas, neither sex nor the Clade × Sex interaction was significant (all p > 0.10).

CVA performed independently within each body area showed the greatest sex‐based shape separation in the ventral posterior (Area 6: |ΔCV1| = 405.9) and caudal regions (Area 7: |ΔCV1| = 405.4), followed by the dorsal posterior (Area 2: |ΔCV1| = 222.0) and lateral anterior areas (Area 3: |ΔCV1| = 209.3; Figure 12b,e). DISCUSSION

In the wake of the re‐discovery of C. lopis on the Indonesian archipelago it is imperative that the remaining populations be managed to promote long‐term persistence (Wibowo et al., 2023). DNA sequencing in this study revealed two geographically structured mitochondrial lineages: (i) those from Central and South Borneo provinces and (ii) those from West Borneo, Riau, South Sumatra and Jambi provinces. The identification of two geographically structured clades suggests that remaining populations should be managed as separate conservation units until connectivity among river basins is clarified. With limited resources to manage endangered species, it is important to conserve genetic diversity and determine the phenotypic characteristics which may be useful in rapidly distinguishing among populations.

Geometric landmark‐based measurements of scale morphology have proven to be a useful method for distinguishing among local populations within a fish species (Bánó et al., 2024; Bräger et al., 2017). For giant featherback, scale geometry revealed significant differentiation among phylogenetic clades and between sexes, indicating some potential as a rapid, non‐destructive tool for stock identification. The observed morphological variation in scales likely reflects both underlying genetic divergence among clades and sexual dimorphism.

Nonetheless, some overlap in scale geometry among populations from Indonesian islands may result from their close phylogenetic relationships and similar aquatic ecotypes (Grady et al., 2022; Khosravi et al., 2017), which could constrain the discriminatory power of scale‐based methods in certain contexts. Therefore, while scale geometry shows some promise for distinguishing stocks, it should be employed in conjunction with complementary approaches to more fully resolve population structure and account for ecological and phylogenetic similarities. The limited discriminatory power of scale morphology indicates that genetic tools remain essential for monitoring population structure in this rediscovered species. Future improvements to allometric scale differentiation will likely require broader sampling across the Indonesian archipelago or comparison to even more distant C. lopis populations in central Thailand and Malaysia where additional extant populations were also recently discovered (Musikasinthorn & Ngamtampong, 2022; Ruzman et al., 2024).

While scale geometry could not differentiate among giant featherback phylogenetic clades, this technique was useful for distinguishing fish sex, which is also supported for other species in the literature (Requieron et al., 2012). For C. lopis, there was significant sexual dimorphism for scales taken from the anterior and mid‐dorsal body area, where the ocular side from males and females was rough, and all the scales (the anterior and mid‐dorsal body area) were cycloid. For other fish species, different scale morphologies can reflect different adaptations between genders, reflecting differences in reproductive roles and behaviours (Bılıcı et al., 2016; Tomiyama, 2013). These aspects require further investigation for C. lopis; landmark‐based geometric scale morphology shows promise for characterising some population parameters.

Geometric scale shape

SEM revealed that the scales of C. lopis possess cycloid type scales with an elongated oval shape, characterised by a prominent circuli grooves centred on the focus area (Videler, 1994). Even though the scales morphology of giant featherback generally had similar characteristics across the whole body, this study discovered that there were differences in scales dimensions in each body area indicating that scales from different body areas have distinct morphometric properties. Similar results were reported for Coho salmon, sea mullet and sardines possibly reflecting adaptations to their external environment, such as local environmental preferences and hydrodynamic conditions (Ibañez et al., 2009; Phillips, 1948; Scarnecchia, 1979).

Scales are not only specialised in protecting a fish body against predators (Bereiter‐Hahn & Zylberberg, 1993; Bruet et al., 2008; Louise et al., 1992), but are also crucial for their swimming ability (Videler, 1993). The variation in C. lopis scale dimensions, with wider scales on the anterior and central body relative to the posterior, may have specific functional implications. As for some other fish, we postulate that the wider scales in the anterior and central regions are adapted to facilitate the side‐to‐side undulations required for subcarangiform swimming by being relatively wide in the dorsoventral direction and short in the anterior–posterior direction. This design reduces drag and enhances propulsion efficiency by minimising resistance to lateral movements (Ibañez et al., 2009; Videler, 1993). Conversely, scales from the posterior region are longer anteroposteriorly, with a central focus and a larger area of insertion into the epidermis. These characteristics help resist the vortex, thrust and water pressure produced by caudal fin propulsion, providing structural support and enhancing thrust and stability during swimming (Ibañez et al., 2009; Scarnecchia, 1979).

Water temperature is well documented as an important driver of fish physiology and health (Corrêa et al., 2025; Topal et al., 2021). Temperatures outside optimal ranges increase metabolic stress, potentially impacting scales as part of overall physiological condition. Likewise, elevated temperatures coupled with reduced dissolved oxygen have been associated with respiratory distress in fish, which may indirectly affect scale condition (Fadel et al., 2024). Differences in habitat ecotypes between the two clades may therefore contribute to observed variation in scale morphology; for example, the RI population experienced higher temperatures than the KS population. However, recorded temperatures across sites (27–30°C) remained within the typical range reported for C. lopis habitats (Wibowo et al., 2025).

Conductivity and pH also varied between sites in Sumatra and Borneo and may contribute to scale differentiation. Lower pH conditions can interfere with the ability of fish to sequester calcium and phosphorus from the surrounding water, potentially resulting in softer scales (Metz et al., 2014). In addition, elevated or fluctuating conductivity reflects changes in ionic strength that may influence scale density and thickness through effects on collagen‐mineral interactions (Li et al., 2025).

CONCLUSION

Giant featherback are an endangered species emblematic of the challenges facing conservation of freshwater biodiversity in the Sundaland and globally. With the species only recently re‐discovered, there are very few ecological data concerning their population status, habitat preferences and population dynamics. We provide the first DNA evidence of two geographically structured mitochondrial lineages: (i) those from Central and South Borneo provinces and (ii) those from West Borneo, Riau, South Sumatera and Jambi provinces. By contrast, scale geometry showed no immediate promise as a rapid non‐destructive stock differentiator among phylogenetic clades or sampling locations. The reasons underlying this lack of distinction remain unclear and in need of further work including further sampling of extant populations on the Indonesian archipelago and Indochinese peninsula. This study did, however, reveal significant variations in scale morphology across different body areas and distinct sexual dimorphism. Scale morphology in C. lopis is structured primarily by body position rather than population identity, highlighting the importance of standardised sampling locations in future morphometric studies. Differences in scale morphology may reflect functional adaptations to habitat ecotypes, but further work is needed to clarify this element. For endangered fishes, it is imperative to collect the relevant taxonomic and biogeographic data to improve conservation and management of the remaining populations, promote long‐term persistence and as a baseline to provide a standardised sampling protocol.
