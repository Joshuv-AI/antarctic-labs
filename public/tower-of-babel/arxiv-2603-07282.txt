Fundamental Groups of Disjointly Tree-Graded Spaces
Jeremy Brazas, Curtis Kent

Abstract
. Tree-graded spaces are a generalization of ℝ \mathbb{R} -trees and play an important role in describing the large-scale geometry of relatively hyperbolic groups. We consider a subclass of tree-graded spaces that we call disjointly tree-graded spaces , determined by maps to ℝ \mathbb{R} -trees. We characterize the fundamental group of a disjointly tree-graded space ( X , 𝒫 ) (X,\mathscr{P}) in terms of the fundamental groups of its pieces. Our results apply even in cases where neither X X nor its pieces are locally simply connected. In particular, we show that if the pieces are uniformly 1 1 - U ​ V 0 UV_{0} , then the fundamental group of a disjointly tree-graded space embeds into the inverse limit of the free products of the fundamental groups of finitely many pieces.

1.
Introduction

The concept of a “tree-graded space” was introduced by Dru

t

ˇ

u and Sapir in
[
13
]
as a generalization of
\mathbb{R}
-trees that allows one to characterize the large scale geometry of relatively hyperbolic groups. A complete geodesic metric space
X
is said to be
tree-graded
with respect to a collection
\mathscr{P}
of closed geodesic subsets of
X
(called
pieces
) if (1) any two distinct pieces meet in at most one point and (2) every simple closed curve (equivalently, any geodesic triangle) in
X
is contained in one piece. In a tree-graded space, pieces are arranged in a tree-like fashion so that an injective path starting and ending in distinct pieces must pass through a unique linearly ordered family of pieces and each connected subspace of
X
that is disjoint from
\bigcup\mathscr{P}
is an
\mathbb{R}
-tree. The use of tree-graded spaces in geometric group theory stems from the fact that, for a group, the property of all asymptotic cones having non-trivial tree-gradings is a weak form of hyperbolicity. In
[
20
]
, it is shown that a
CAT(0)
group has isolated flats if and only if all its asymptotic cones are tree-graded with pieces isometric to euclidean space. This is a special case of the general results that a finitely generated group is hyperbolic relative to a collection of subgroups if and only if all of its asymptotic cones are tree-graded relative to limits of the peripheral subgroups
[
13
, Theorem 8.5]
.

Fundamental groups of tree-graded spaces play an important role in their applications within geometric group theory. In
[
13
, Proposition 2.2]
it is proved that if the pieces
\mathscr{P}
of

are locally uniformly contractible, then
\pi_{1}(X)
is isomorphic to the free product of the fundamental groups of the pieces. The hypothesis “locally uniformly contractible” restricts one to considering tree-graded spaces where all of the pieces are all large and tame, excluding situations where null-sequences of essential loops may appear. In this paper, we develop tools for understanding fundamental groups of tree-graded spaces where pieces are allowed to have arbitrarily small diameters. Note this situation can occur when considering asymptotic cones of relatively hyperbolic groups. For example, any finitely generated group that is hyperbolic relative to a Baumslag-Solitar group (with
|p|\neq|q|
, where the relation is

[
7

showed that the asymptotic cones of Baumslag-Solitar groups have
\pi_{1}
-essentially embedded infinite earrings. In fact, Theorem B and Corollary C of
[
8
]
give a condition for a group to have asymptotic cones that are not locally simply connected and some natural examples satisfying this condition. Additionally, many lacunary hyperbolic groups can by tree-graded by pieces which are not locally simply connected by applying techniques from
[
24

25

In the current paper, we loosen some of the geometric restrictions typically imposed on the underlying space

of a tree-graded space
(X,\mathscr{P})

be a path-connected, locally path-connected, metrizable space. On the other hand, we impose more restrictions on what we permit to be called a “piece.” Making this restriction is important to achieving our goal of characterizing the fundamental group of tree-graded space in terms of the fundamental groups of it’s pieces. For instance, an
\mathbb{R}

,

(1)

Pieces are the path-components of the union

The set

Our definition is also motivated by an apparent relevance to fundamental groups of Peano continua. If

When a space

-shape injective

,

[

]

[

[

However, our approach is quite a bit more general since we do not require pieces of our disjointly tree-graded spaces to be polyhedra, have tame fundamental groups, or even be

If

is a disjoint tree-grading on

Theorem 1.1
.

Let

-
UV_{0}

Example

can fail if

is not uniformly
1
-
UV_{0}
. The proof of Theorem

\Gamma_{\mathscr{F}^{\prime},\mathscr{F}\#}:\pi_{1}(X_{\mathscr{F}^{\prime}},x_{\mathscr{F}^{\prime}})\to\pi_{1}(X_{\mathscr{F}},x_{\mathscr{F}})

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
(X,\mathscr{P})
be a disjointly tree-graded space for which
\mathbf{Pc}(X)
is uniformly
1
-
UV_{0}

For each

If

2.
Preliminaries

All spaces considered in this paper are assumed to be Hausdorff. We take
[0,1]
,
S^{1}
, and
\mathbb{D}
to denote the closed unit interval, unit circle, and closed unit disk respectively. A space homeomorphic to
[0,1]
is called an
arc
and a space homeomorphic to
S^{1}
is called a
simple closed curve
. Generally, the term “map” will refer to a continuous function. If
f:(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
is a based map of spaces
f_{\#}:\pi_{1}(X,x_{0})\to\pi_{1}(Y,y_{0})
will denote the homomorphism induced on fundamental groups. If
f:(X,d)\to(Y,\rho)
is a function of metric spaces, we say
f
is
non-expansive
if
f
does not increase distance, i.e.
\rho(f(x_{1}),f(x))\leq d(x_{1},x_{2})
for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
.

A
path
in a space
X
is a continuous function
\alpha:[a,b]\to X
. When
\alpha(a)=\alpha(b)
, we call
\alpha
a
loop
and we may regard
\alpha
as a map
S^{1}\to X
. A loop
\alpha:S^{1}\to X
is
inessential (in
X
)
if there exists a map
g:\mathbb{D}\to X
such that
g|_{S^{1}}=\alpha
(equivalently, if
\alpha
is null-homotopic) and is
essential
otherwise. We let
\alpha\cdot\beta
denote the concatenation of paths and
\alpha^{-1}
denote the reverse path of
\alpha
.

A space
X
is
(uniquely) arcwise-connected
if for all distinct points
x,y\in X
there is a (unique) arc
A\subseteq X
with endpoints
x
and
y
. Every path-connected Hausdorff space is arcwise-connected and it is well-known that a Hausdorff space is uniquely arcwise-connected if and only if
X
does not contain a simple closed curve as a subspace. This characterization is implied by the following lemma, which is more specific to our purposes.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
X
is a Hausdorff space,
A_{1},A_{2}\subseteq X
are arcs sharing the same endpoints
x
and
y
, and
z\in A_{1}\backslash A_{2}
, then there exists a simple closed curve
C\subseteq A_{1}\cup A_{2}
containing (1) the point
z
, (2) a subarc of
A_{1}
, and (3) a subarc of
A_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}:[0,1]\to X
be injective paths parameterizing
A_{1},A_{2}
respectively. Let
s
be the unique element of
\alpha_{1}^{-1}(z)
. The set
C=\alpha_{1}^{-1}(\operatorname{Im}(\alpha_{2}))
is closed in
[0,1]
and we have
\{0,1\}\subseteq C\subseteq[0,1]\backslash\{s\}
by assumption. Hence, there exists a connected component
(a,b)
of
[0,1]\backslash C
with
s\in(a,b)
. Since
a,b\in C
and
\alpha_{2}
is injective, we have
\alpha_{1}(a)=\alpha_{2}(c)
and
\alpha_{1}(b)=\alpha_{2}(d)
for unique
c,d\in[0,1]
. Taking the reverse path of
\alpha_{2}
if necessary, we may assume that
c<d
. Now
C=\alpha_{1}([a,b])\cup\alpha_{2}([c,d])
is a simple closed curve satisfying the desired properties.
∎

Definition 2.2
.

A topological space
T
is

(1)

a
topological tree
if
T
is Hausdorff, uniquely arcwise-connected, and locally arcwise-connected,

(2)

a
topological
\mathbb{R}
-tree
if
T
is a metrizable topological tree,

(3)

a
dendrite
if
T
is a compact topological
\mathbb{R}
-tree.

It follows from Lemma
2.1
that a Hausdorff space
X
is a topological tree if and only
X
is connected, locally path-connected and contains no simple closed curve. Note also that a subspace of a topological tree is connected (resp. totally disconnected) if and only if it is path-connected (resp. totally path-disconnected). A metric space
(X,d)
is an
\mathbb{R}
-tree
if
X
is uniquely arcwise connected and if each arc in
X
is the image of an isometric embedding
[a,b]\to X
of a real closed interval
[
26
]
. If
(X,d)
is an
\mathbb{R}
-tree, then
X
is certainly a topological
\mathbb{R}
-tree. Far less trivial is the fact that every topological
\mathbb{R}
-tree
X
admits a compatible metric
d
for which
(X,d)
is an
\mathbb{R}
-tree
[
21
]
. It is well-known that
\mathbb{R}
-trees are contractible. Clearly every topological tree is weakly equivalent to a point. However, it is apparently an open question if all topological trees are contractible (in ZFC).

A
Peano continuum
is a connected, locally connected compact metrizable space. The Hahn-Mazurkiewicz states that a Hausdorff space
X
is a Peano continuum if and only if there exists a surjective path
\alpha:[0,1]\to X
. A topological tree is a Peano continuum if and only if it is a dendrite.

3.
Disjointly tree-graded spaces and their parameterizations

3.1.
Basic definitions

Definition 3.1
.

Let
X
be a path-connected, locally path-connected, metrizable space and
\mathscr{P}
be a collection of non-empty closed subsets of
X
(called
pieces
) such that the following properties are satisfied:

(1)

\bigcup\mathscr{P}
is closed in
X
,

(2)

The elements of
\mathscr{P}
are the path-components of
\bigcup\mathscr{P}
,

(3)

Every simple closed curve in
X
is contained in one piece.

We call
\mathscr{P}
a
disjoint tree-grading
on
X
and we refer to the pair
(X,\mathscr{P})
as a
disjointly tree-graded space
.

We refer to
\mathbf{Pc}(X)=\bigcup\mathscr{P}
as the
piece-portion
of
X
and
\mathbf{Tr}(X)=X\backslash\mathbf{Pc}(X)
as the
tree-portion
of
X
. We say a piece is
degenerate
if it is a one-point space and
non-degenerate
otherwise.

Remark 3.2
.

In Lemma
3.12
below, we will show that a disjointly tree-graded space retracts onto every one of it’s pieces. Since disjointly tree-graded spaces are assumed to be locally path-connected, it follows that every piece is locally path-connected.

Remark 3.3
.

When
X
can be equipped with a complete geodesic metric, our notion of “disjoint tree-grading” on a space
X
can be compared with the notion of “tree-grading” in the sense of Dru

t

ˇ

u-Sapir
[
13
]
. In particular, every disjoint tree-grading
\mathscr{P}
on
X
is a tree-grading. On the other hand, if
\mathscr{P}^{\prime}
is a tree-grading on
X
, then
\bigcup\mathscr{P}^{\prime}
need not be closed, the pieces need not be the path-components of
\bigcup\mathscr{P}^{\prime}
, and it may be that two distinct pieces meet at a point. In general, given a tree-grading
\mathscr{P}^{\prime}
on
X
, one can construct a disjoint tree-grading
\mathscr{P}
on
X
by taking the new pieces to be the smallest closed, geodesic subsets
C
such that
P\subseteq C
whenever
P\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}
and
P\cap C\neq\emptyset
. This process will result in a
non-trivial
disjoint tree-grading
\mathscr{P}
if and only if
X\backslash\bigcup\mathscr{P}^{\prime}
contains a non-empty open subset, which is an
\mathbb{R}
-tree. For example, if
X=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}D_{n}
where
D_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the disk of radius
1/2
centered at
(n,0)
, then
\mathscr{P}^{\prime}=\{D_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is a tree-grading on
X
. However, no non-trivial disjoint tree-grading on
X
exists. This particular example also highlights the fact that one cannot collapse pieces in an ordinary tree-graded space without affecting other pieces (if each disk
D_{n}\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}
is identified to a point, the resulting quotient of
X
will be a one-point space).

Definition 3.4
.

A
path-diameter metric
on a set
X
is a metric
\rho
on
X
that satisfies

\rho(a,b)=\inf\{\diam(\operatorname{Im}(\alpha))\mid\alpha\text{ is a path from }a\text{ to }b\}.

If
\rho
is a path-diameter metric on
X
, the open balls
B_{\epsilon}(x)=\{y\in X\mid\rho(x,y)<\epsilon\}
are path-connected. Throughout the remainder of the paper, we will assume that the metric inducing the topology of a disjointly tree-graded space is a path-diameter metric. This is justified by the following remark.

Remark 3.5
.

If
d
is an arbitrary metric on a set
X
such that the induced topology gives
X
the structure of a locally path-connected space, then we can define

\rho(a,b)=\inf\{\diam_{d}(\operatorname{Im}(\alpha))\mid\alpha:[0,1]\to X\text{ is a path from }a\text{ to }b\}

where the diameter in this definition is taken with respect to
d
. It is straightforward to check that
\rho
is a metric on
X
, which is compatible with the topology of
X
and such that the identity function
(X,\rho)\mapsto(X,d)
is non-expansive. Moreover, since
\diam_{\rho}(\operatorname{Im}(\alpha))=\diam_{d}(\operatorname{Im}(\alpha))
for all paths
\alpha:[0,1]\to X
,
\rho
is a path-diameter metric on
X
. Hence, any locally path-connected metrizable space may be equipped with a path-diameter metric.

3.2.
The topological structure of
\mathbf{Tr}(X)

In the following two subsections, we fix a disjointly tree-graded space
(X,\mathscr{P})
and a path-diameter metric

on

Proposition 3.6
.

\mathbf{Tr}(X)
is a disjoint union of open topological
\mathbb{R}
-trees. Moreover, if

, then
\overline{U}\backslash U\subseteq\mathbf{Pc}(X)

Proof.

If

is an element of some other component

. But since

, violating the fact that

are distinct connected components of

.
∎

If

Since

y
. If
A_{1}\nsubseteq U
, then there exists a point
z\in A_{1}\backslash U
. By Proposition
3.6
,
U
is an
\mathbb{R}
-tree and so there exists a unique arc
A_{2}
in
U
with endpoints
x
and
y
. By Lemma
2.1
, there exists a simple closed curve
C\subseteq A_{1}\cup A_{2}
that contains a subarc of
A_{2}
. However, this contradicts (3) of Definition
3.1
.

Case II: Suppose
x\in U
and
y\in\overline{U}\backslash U
. By Proposition
3.6
, we have
y\in P
for some
P\in\mathscr{P}
. First we verify the existence of the desired arc. Find a properly nested neighborhood basis
X=W_{1}\supsetneq W_{2}\supsetneq W_{3}\supsetneq\cdots
at
y
consisting of path-connected open sets and finds points
w_{i}\in(W_{i}\cap U)\backslash W_{i+1}
. Specifically, choose
w_{1}=x
. Let
\beta_{i}:[0,1]\to W_{i}
be an injective path from
w_{i}
to
w_{i+1}
. By Case I, we have
\operatorname{Im}(\beta_{i})\subseteq U
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Define path
\beta:[0,1]\to X
by defining
\beta
to be
\beta_{i}
on
\left[\frac{i-1}{i},\frac{i}{i+1}\right]
and
\beta(1)=y
. By construction,
\beta
is continuous and satisfies
\beta(0)=x
,
\beta([0,1))\subseteq U
, and
\beta(1)=y
. Any choice of injective path
\alpha:[0,1]\to\operatorname{Im}(\beta)
from
x
to
y
parameterizes an arc
A
with the desired properties. For uniqueness, let
A_{1}=A
and suppose that
A_{2}
is an arc in
X
with the same endpoints but for which
A_{1}\neq A_{2}
. If there is a point
z\in A_{1}\backslash A_{2}
, then Lemma
2.1
gives a simple closed curve
C
containing
z
. However
z\in A_{1}\backslash\{x,y\}\subseteq U
, contradicting (3) in Definition
3.1
. If there is a point
z\in A_{2}\backslash A_{1}
, then Lemma
2.1
gives a simple closed curve
C
containing a subarc of
A_{1}
. However, every subarc of
A_{1}
must meet
U
, again giving a contradiction.

Case III: Suppose
x,y\in\overline{U}\backslash U
. Fix
z\in U
. By Case II, find arcs
A_{1},A_{2}\subseteq\overline{U}
with
\overline{U}\backslash A_{1}=\{x\}
,
\overline{U}\backslash A_{2}=\{y\}
, and
z\in A_{1}\cap A_{2}
. An arc
A\subseteq A_{1}\cup A_{2}
with endpoints
x
and
y
satisfies the desired conditions. Uniqueness follows by the same type of argument used in Case II.
∎

Proposition 3.9
.

If
U
is a connected component of
\mathbf{Tr}(X)
, then
\overline{U}
is a topological
\mathbb{R}
-tree that meets each piece of
X
in at most one point.

Proof.

It follows from Proposition
3.8
that
\overline{U}
is uniquely arcwise connected. If
x\in\overline{U}\backslash U
and
V
is a path-connected open neighborhood of
x
in
X
, then Proposition
3.8
also implies that
V\cap\overline{U}
is path-connected. Hence,
\overline{U}
is locally path-connected and it follows that
\overline{U}
is a topological
\mathbb{R}
-tree.

Suppose
P
is a piece of
X
and
x,y
are distinct points of
\overline{U}\cap P
. By Proposition
3.8
, there exists an arc
A_{1}\subseteq\overline{U}
with endpoints
x
and
y
and such that
A_{1}\backslash\{x,y\}\subseteq U
. Since pieces are path-connected, there exists an arc
A_{2}\subseteq P
with endpoints
x
and
y
. Then
A_{1}\cup A_{2}
is a simple closed curve that meets
U
, contradicting (3) in Definition
3.1
.
∎

The above characterization of
\overline{U}
for a connected component
U
of
\mathbf{Tr}(X)
allows us to identify convenient representatives of path-homotopy classes.

Definition 3.10
.

A path
\beta:[0,1]\to X
is
tree-efficient
if for every connected component
J
of
\beta^{-1}(\mathbf{Tr}(X))
, the restriction
\beta|_{\overline{J}}:\overline{J}\to X
is an injective path satisfying
\beta|_{\overline{J}}^{-1}(\mathbf{Tr}(X))=J
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Every non-constant path
\alpha:[0,1]\to X
is path-homotopic to a tree-efficient path
\beta:[0,1]\to X
. Moreover, we may choose the homotopy
H
from
\alpha
to
\beta
to be the constant homotopy on
\alpha^{-1}(\mathbf{Pc}(X))
and so that
\operatorname{Im}(H)\subseteq\operatorname{Im}(\alpha)
.

Proof.

If
\alpha
has image in
\mathbf{Pc}(X)
, then we take
\alpha=\beta
and let
H
be the constant homotopy. Otherwise, for each connected component
J
of
\alpha^{-1}(\mathbf{Tr}(X))
(which is an open or half-open interval), the path
\alpha|_{\overline{J}}
maps
\operatorname{int}(J)
into some connected component
U_{J}
of
\mathbf{Tr}(X)
and at least one endpoint of
J
into
\mathbf{Pc}(X)
. We have
\alpha(\overline{J})\subseteq\overline{U_{J}}
where
\overline{U_{J}}
is a topological
\mathbb{R}
-tree by Proposition
3.9
.

If
\alpha|_{\overline{J}}
is a loop based at a point
x\in\mathbf{Pc}(X)
, then
\alpha|_{\overline{J}}
is a loop in the topological
\mathbb{R}
-tree
\overline{U_{J}}
and is therefore null-homotopic in it’s own image. Hence, there exists a map
H_{J}:\overline{J}\times[0,1]\to\alpha(\overline{J})
with
H_{J}(s,t)=\alpha(s)
for
s\in\overline{J}
and
H_{J}(\partial J\times[0,1]\cup\overline{J}\times\{1\})=x
. If
\alpha|_{\overline{J}}
is path with distinct endpoints, we write
\overline{J}=[a,b]
and define
\beta_{J}:[0,1]\to\alpha(\overline{J})
to be an injective path parameterizing the unique arc in the dendrite
\alpha(\overline{J})
from
\alpha(a)
to
\alpha(b)
. Since
\alpha(\overline{J})
is simply connected, there exists a map
H_{J}:\overline{J}\times[0,1]\to\alpha(\overline{J})
with
H_{J}(s,0)=\alpha(s)
,
H_{J}(s,1)=\beta(s)
for
s\in\overline{J}
and such that
H_{J}(\{a\}\times[0,1])=\alpha(a)
and
H_{J}(\{b\}\times[0,1])=\alpha(b)
.

With all of the maps
H_{J}
defined, we define a map
H:[0,1]^{2}\to X
so that
H(s,t)=\alpha(s)
if
s\in\alpha^{-1}(\mathbf{Pc}(X))
. If
s\in J
for some component
J
of
\alpha^{-1}(\mathbf{Tr}(X))
, we define
H(s,t)=H_{J}(s,t)
. Since
\operatorname{Im}(H_{J})\subseteq\alpha(\overline{J})
for each
J
, the continuity of
H
follows easily from the continuity of
\alpha
. The path
\beta:[0,1]\to X
defined by
\beta(s)=H(s,1)
is tree-efficient and the homotopy constructed has the desired properties.
∎

3.3.
Metric retractions

Here, we consider special subspaces
Y
of
X
that admit a canonical retraction
r:X\to Y
with several convenient properties.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
Y\subseteq X
be a closed, path-connected subspace with the property that if a piece
P
of
X
meets
Y
, then
Y\cap P
is either a one-point space or all of
P
. Then there exists a retraction
r:X\to Y
defined as follows: if
y\in Y
, then
r(y)=y
and if
x\in X\backslash Y
, then
r(x)=y
where
x
and
y
are endpoints of an arc
A\subseteq X
with the property that
A\cap Y=\{y\}
. Moreover, the following hold:

(1)

if
x_{1},x_{2}
lie in the same connected component of
X\backslash Y
, then
r(x_{1})=r(x_{2})
,

(2)

if
\alpha:[0,1]\to X
is path whose image meets
Y
, then
\operatorname{Im}(r\circ\alpha)\subseteq\operatorname{Im}(\alpha)
,

(3)

r
is non-expansive when
Y
is equipped with the subspace metric.

Proof.

First, we show that
r
is well-defined. Given a point
x\in X\backslash Y
, find an injective path
\alpha:[0,1]\to X
with
\alpha(0)=x
and
\alpha(1)\in Y
. Since
Y
is closed,
s=\min\{t\in[0,1]\mid\alpha(t)\in Y\}
exists and
A=\alpha([0,s])
is an arc with endpoints
x
and
\alpha(s)\in Y
and
A\cap Y=\{\alpha(s)\}
. We show that the endpoint in
Y
is unique. For
i\in\{1,2\}
, suppose
A_{i}\subseteq X
is an arc with endpoint
x
and
y_{i}\in Y
and such that
A_{i}\cap Y=\{y_{i}\}
. Suppose, to obtain a contradiction that
y_{1}\neq y_{2}
. Now
A_{1}\cup A_{2}
is a Peano continuum that meets
Y
in the two-point set
\{y_{1},y_{2}\}
. Let
B_{1}\subseteq A_{1}\cup A_{2}
be an arc with endpoints
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
. Since
Y
is path-connected, we may find an arc
B_{2}\subseteq Y
with endpoints
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
. Since
C=B_{1}\cup B_{2}
is a simple closed curve, we must have
C\subseteq P
for some piece
P
of
X
. However, since
B_{1}
is not contained entirely in
Y
, our hypothesis about how
Y
meets the pieces of
X
forces
P
to be a degenerate piece of
X
but this contradicts the fact that
\{y_{1},y_{2}\}\subseteq P
. Therefore, we must have
y_{1}=y_{2}
and the function
r
is well-defined.

Next, we prove (1). Suppose
x_{1},x_{2}
lie in the same connected component of
X\backslash Y
. Find arcs
A_{i}
with endpoints
x_{i}
and
y_{i}\in Y
and with the property that
A_{i}\cap Y=\{y_{i}\}
so that
r(x_{i})=y_{i}
. Find an arc
B\subseteq X\backslash Y
with endpoints
x_{1}\in A_{1}
and
x_{2}\in A_{2}
. There exists an arc
A_{2}^{\prime}\subseteq A_{1}\cup B
with endpoints
x_{2}
and
y_{1}
. Since
A_{2}^{\prime}\cap Y=\{y_{1}\}
, the well-definedness of
r
from the previous paragraph gives
y_{1}=r(x_{2})=y_{2}
.

Next, we check that
r
is continuous. Let
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\to x
be a convergent sequence in
X
. Since the connected components of
X\backslash Y
are open, the previous paragraph makes the case where
x\in X\backslash Y
trivial. Suppose that
x\in Y
. Since
r
is the identity on
Y
, we may focus on the case where
x_{n}\in X\backslash Y
for all
n
. Let
V
be a path-connected open neighborhood of
r(x)=x
in
X
. Find
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
x_{n}\in V
for all
n\geq N
. For each
n\geq N
, let
B_{n}\subseteq V
be an arc with endpoints
x_{n}
and
x
. Since
x_{n}\in X\backslash Y
and
Y
is closed, we may find subarcs
A_{n}\subseteq B_{n}
,
n\geq N
with endpoints
x_{n}
and
y_{n}\in Y
and such that
A_{n}\cap Y=\{y_{n}\}
. Thus
r(x_{n})=y_{n}\in A_{n}\subseteq B_{n}\subseteq V
for all
n\geq N
, proving that
\{r(x_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\to x
.

For (2), suppose that
\alpha:[0,1]\to X
is a path whose image meets
Y
. Then
K=\alpha^{-1}(Y)
is non-empty and closed. if
t\in K
, then
r(\alpha(t))=\alpha(t)
. Pick a point
t\in[0,1]\backslash K
and find a closed interval
I
with endpoints
t
and
k\in K
and such that
I\cap K=\{k\}
. Now
\alpha(I)
is a Peano continuum containing
\alpha(t)
and for which
\alpha(I)\cap Y=\{\alpha(k)\}
. Thus there exists an arc
A\subseteq\alpha(I)
with endpoints
\alpha(t)
and
\alpha(k)
. The definition of
r
now gives
r(\alpha(t))=\alpha(k)
. This proves that
\operatorname{Im}(\alpha\circ r)\subseteq\operatorname{Im}(\alpha)
.

For (3), recall that
d
is a path-diameter metric. Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
. If
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
lie in the same connected component of
X\backslash Y
, then (1) gives
r(x_{1})=r(x_{2})
and thus
0=d(r(x_{1}),r(x_{2}))\leq d(x_{1},x_{2})
. Suppose
x_{1},x_{2}
do not both lie in a single connected component of
X\backslash Y
. Let
\alpha:[0,1]\to X
be a path from
x_{1}
to
x_{2}
. Then the image of
\alpha
must meet
Y
and (2) implies that
r\circ\alpha:[0,1]\to X
is a path from
r(x_{1})
to
r(x_{2})
satisfying
\diam(\operatorname{Im}(r\circ\alpha))\leq\diam(\operatorname{Im}(\alpha))
. Thus,
d(r(x_{1}),r(x_{2}))\leq d(x_{1},x_{2})
holds.
∎

The next corollary follows from combining (1) and (2) of Lemma
3.12
.

Corollary 3.13
.

Let
Y_{1},Y_{2}
be disjoint subspaces of
X
meeting the hypotheses of Lemma
3.12
and let
r_{1}:X\to Y_{1}
and
r_{2}:X\to Y_{2}
be the retractions from the conclusion of Lemma
3.12
. If
\alpha:[0,1]\to X
is any path with
\alpha(0)\in Y_{1}
and
\alpha(1)\in Y_{2}
, then
\{r_{1}(Y_{2}),r_{2}(Y_{1})\}\subseteq\operatorname{Im}(\alpha)
.

Corollary 3.14
.

If
Y
is a subspace of
X
meeting the hypotheses of Lemma
3.12
, then the subspace metric on
Y
is a path-diameter metric on
Y
.

Proof.

Given
y_{1},y_{2}\in Y
and
\epsilon>0
, we can find a path
\alpha:[0,1]\to X
from
y_{1}
to
y_{2}
with
\diam(\operatorname{Im}(\alpha))<d(y_{1},y_{2})+\epsilon
. Since
\operatorname{Im}(\alpha)
meets
Y
, (2) of Lemma
3.12
implies that
r\circ\alpha:[0,1]\to Y
is a path with
\diam(\operatorname{Im}(r\circ\alpha))\leq\diam(\operatorname{Im}(\alpha))<d(y_{1},y_{2})+\epsilon
.
∎

Corollary 3.15
.

If
\beta:[0,1]\to X
is an injective path, then for every piece
P\in\mathscr{P}
,
\beta^{-1}(P)
is connected.

Proof.

If there exists a
P\in\mathscr{P}
for which
\beta^{-1}(P)
is not connected, consider canonical the retraction
r:X\to P
from Lemma
3.12
. Since
\beta^{-1}(P)
is closed and not connected, there exists an open interval
(a,b)
, which is a connected component of
[0,1]\backslash\beta^{-1}(P)
. Then (1) of Lemma
3.12
implies that
\beta(a)=r(\beta(a))=r(\beta([a,b]))=r(\beta(b))=\beta(b)
, contradicting the assumption that
\beta
is injective.
∎

Proposition 3.16
.

If
X
is compact and
\mathscr{P}=\{P\}
, i.e.
X
has a single piece
P
, then
P
is a deformation retract of
X
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be the collection of connected components of
X\backslash P
. For each
U\in\mathcal{U}
, we have
\overline{U}\cap P=\{x_{U}\}
for some point
x_{U}
. Since
\overline{U}
is a dendrite (using Prop.
3.9
and the fact that
X
is compact) and all such spaces are point-wise contractible, we may choose a contraction
H_{U}:\overline{U}\times[0,1]\to\overline{U}
so that
H(x,0)=x
,
H(x,1)=x_{U}
, and
H(x_{U},t)=x_{U}
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Define
H:X\times[0,1]\to X
so that
H(x,t)=x
if
x\in P
and
H(x,t)=H_{U}(x,t)
if
x\in\overline{U}
. Certainly,
H
is well-defined as a function. The only non-trivial case to check in order to verify the continuity of
H
is if
\{(x_{n},t_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\to(x,t)
is a convergent sequence in
X\times[0,1]
where
x_{n}\in U_{n}
for pairwise-distinct
U_{1},U_{2},U_{3},\dots\in\mathcal{U}
. Let
V
be a neighborhood of
H(x,t)
in
X
. Since each element of
\mathscr{U}
is open in
X
, we must have
x\in P
and thus
H(x,t)=x
. Thus there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
x_{n}\in V
for all
n\geq N
. Since
H(x_{n},t_{n})\in\operatorname{Im}(H_{U_{n}})\subseteq\overline{U_{n}}
, it suffices to show that
\overline{U_{n}}\subseteq V
for all but finitely many
n
. Supposing otherwise, we may find
N<n_{1}<n_{2}<n_{3}<\cdots
and points
a_{i}\in U_{n_{i}}\backslash V
. Since
X
is compact we may assume that
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\to a
in
X
. Since all the
U_{n_{i}}
are distinct, we must again have
a\in X
. If
r:X\to P
is the retraction from Lemma
3.12
, we have
r(x_{n_{i}})=r(a_{i})
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
\{r(x_{n_{i}})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\to x
and
\{r(a_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\to r(a)=a
, we must have
a=x\in V
. Since
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\to a
, we have
a_{i}\in V
for sufficiently large
i
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Extending Proposition
3.16
to the non-compact case is not necessary for our purposes but seems possible with a more technical argument. We prove the following consequence of Proposition
3.16
in significant generality since it will be required for a situation in which non-metrizable quotient spaces appear.

Corollary 3.17
.

Let
B
be a path-connected Hausdorff space and
A\subseteq B
be a closed, path-connected, subspace such that every simple closed curve in
B
is contained in
A
. Then the inclusion
i:A\to B
induces an isomorphism on fundamental groups.

Proof.

Let
\beta:S^{1}\to B
be a loop based at a point in
A
. Then
Y=\operatorname{Im}(\beta)
is a Peano continuum. We claim that
Q=Y\cap A
is path-connected. If
(a,b)
is a connected component of
U=\beta^{-1}(B\backslash A)
, then we must have
\beta(a)=\beta(b)
or previously used arguments would allow us to construct a simple closed curve in
B
that is not contained in
A
. Define
\alpha:[0,1]\to A
so that
\alpha(t)=\beta(t)
if
t\in[0,1]\backslash U
and if
t\in(a,b)
for some connected component
(a,b)
of
U
, then
\alpha(t)=\beta(a)
. Then
\beta
is continuous because
\alpha
is continuous and
\operatorname{Im}(\alpha)=Q
. We conclude that
Q
is path-connected. It follows that
\{Q\}
is a disjoint tree-grading on
Y
. By Proposition
3.16
,
Q
is a deformation retract of
Y
where the resulting retraction
r:Y\to Q
, which is a homotopy equivalence, gives
r\circ\beta=\alpha
. Thus
\beta
is homotopic to
\alpha
in
B
, proving that
i:A\to B
is
\pi_{1}
-surjective.

Let
\gamma:S^{1}\to A
be a loop such that there exists a map
g:\mathbb{D}\to B
with
g|_{S^{1}}=\gamma
. Then
Y=\operatorname{Im}(g)
is a Peano continuum. Choose a surjective path
\alpha:[0,1]\to Y
. Using the argument from the previous paragraph it follows that if
Q=Y\cap A
, then
\{Q\}
is a disjoint tree-grading on
Y
. Again, the retraction
r:Y\to Q
from Lemma
3.12
is a homotopy equivalence. The map
r\circ g:\mathbb{D}\to A
satisfies
r\circ g|_{S^{1}}=r\circ\gamma=\gamma
and thus
\gamma
is inessential in
A
. This proves that
i:A\to B
is
\pi_{1}
-injective.
∎

Corollary 3.18
.

Let
(B,b_{0})
be a based path-connected Hausdorff space and suppose
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{m}
are pairwise-disjoint, closed, path-connected subspaces of
B
such that every simple closed curve in
B
is contained in
A_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
. Then there exists paths
\alpha_{i}:[0,1]\to B
from
b_{0}
to a point
a_{i}\in A_{i}
such that the inclusion maps
A_{i}\to B
induce an isomorphism
\theta:\ast_{i=1}^{m}\pi_{1}(A_{i},a_{i})\to\pi_{1}(B,b_{0})
given by
\theta([\gamma])=[\alpha_{i}\cdot\gamma\cdot\alpha_{i}^{-1}]
for
[\gamma]\in\pi_{1}(A_{i},a_{i})
.

Moreover, if
\beta_{i}:[0,1]\to B
are arbitrarily chosen paths from
b_{0}
to
a_{i}
, then the homomorphism
\theta^{\prime}:\ast_{i=1}^{m}\pi_{1}(A_{i},a_{i})\to\pi_{1}(B,b_{0})
given by
\theta^{\prime}([\gamma])=[\beta_{i}\cdot\gamma\cdot\beta_{i}^{-1}]
for
[\gamma]\in\pi_{1}(A_{i},a_{i})
is injective.

Proof.

For each
i\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
, let
V_{i}
be the connected component of
B\backslash\bigcup_{j\neq i}A_{j}
containing
A_{i}
. Applying Corollary
3.17
to the pair
(V_{i},A_{i})
, we see that the inclusion
(A_{i},a_{i})\to(V_{i},a_{i})
induces an isomorphism on fundamental groups. The sets
V_{i}
cover
B
. Moreover,
V_{i}\cap V_{j}
is uniquely arcwise-connected and therefore simply connected whenever
i\neq j
and
V_{i}\cap V_{j}\neq\emptyset
. Reorder the sets
V_{i}
so that
W_{k}=V_{1}\cup V_{2}\cup\cdots\cup V_{k}
is connected for each
k\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
. Now, the recursive application of the standard van Kampen Theorem to the sets
W_{k}
and
V_{k+1}
for
k\in\{1,2,\dots,m-1\}
shows that
\theta
is an isomorphism for some paths
\alpha_{i}
resulting from the recursion and a final basepoint-change conjugation.

For the second statement, we recall the following property of free products: if
h_{1},h_{2},\dots,h_{m}\in G=\ast_{i=1}^{m}\pi_{1}(A_{i},a_{i})
, then the homomorphism
\phi:G\to G
determined by mapping
\phi(g)=h_{i}gh_{i}^{-1}
for
g\in\pi_{1}(A_{i},a_{i})
is injective. For given paths
\beta_{i}
, we define
\phi
by setting
h_{i}=\theta^{-1}([\beta_{i}\cdot\alpha_{i}^{-1}])
for
i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. Since
\theta^{\prime}=\theta\circ\phi
, it follows that
\theta^{\prime}
is injective.
∎

3.4.
Parameterizations of disjointly tree-graded spaces

Here, we give a structural characterization of disjointly tree-graded spaces that will be crucial in the proof of our main result.

Definition 3.19
.

Let
(X,\mathscr{P})
be a disjointly tree-graded space. A
parameterization
of
(X,\mathscr{P})
is a triple
(T,V,q)
where
T
is a topological tree,
V\subseteq T
is a closed totally disconnected subset, and
q:X\to T
is a continuous surjection such that
\mathscr{P}=\{q^{-1}(v)\mid v\in V\}
and
|q^{-1}(t)|=1
if
t\in T\backslash V
.

First, we show that every disjointly tree-graded space admits a parametrization.

Lemma 3.20
.

Let
(X,\mathscr{P})
be a disjointly tree-graded space,
T
be the quotient space of
X
by collapsing each piece to a point, and
q:X\to T
be the quotient map. Then
T
is Hausdorff, path-connected, locally path-connected and
V=q(\mathbf{Pc}(X))
is closed and totally disconnected.

Proof.

Since the properties of being path-connected and locally path-connected space are closed under forming quotient spaces,
T
has these properties. We check that
T
is Hausdorff. By definition,
\mathbf{Pc}(X)
is closed in
X
and
q
is defined so that
\mathbf{Pc}(X)
is saturated with respect to
q
. Hence,
V
is closed in
T
. Moreover,
q
is bijective on the open set
\mathbf{Tr}(X)=q^{-1}(T\backslash V)
and so
q
maps
\mathbf{Tr}(X)
homeomorphically onto
T\backslash V
. In particular,
T\backslash V
is a disjoint union of open topological
\mathbb{R}
-trees. Suppose
a,b
are distinct points of
T
. Let
x\in q^{-1}(a)
and
y\in q^{-1}(b)
. Since
x
and
y
do not lie in a single piece of
X
, Proposition
3.7
gives a point
z\in\mathbf{Tr}(X)
such that
x
and
y
lie in distinct components of
X\backslash\{z\}
. Say that
C_{x}
and
C_{y}
are these connected components respectively. Then
C_{x}
and
C_{y}
are disjoint, open, and saturated with respect to
q
(since pieces are path-connected) and thus
q(C_{x})
and
q(C_{y})
are disjoint open neighborhoods of
a
and
b
in
T
. Moreover, if
x
and
y
lie in distinct pieces of
X
, then
q(C_{x})\cap V
and
q(C_{y})\cap V
are disjoint clopen neighborhoods in
V
. This completes the proof that
T
is Hausdorff and
V
is totally disconnected.
∎

Lemma 3.21
.

Let
(X,\mathscr{P})
be a disjointly tree-graded space and
q:X\to T
be the quotient map collapsing each piece to a point. For every arc
A\subseteq T
, there exists an arc
B\subseteq X
such that
q(B)=A
.

Proof.

Since
V
is totally disconnected, note that
A\cap(T\backslash V)\neq\emptyset
. Let
\alpha:[0,1]\to T
be an injective path parameterizing
A
and set
a_{i}=\alpha(i)
for
i\in\{0,1\}
. Let
\beta:[0,1]\to X
be an injective path with
\beta(i)\in q^{-1}(a_{i})
for
i\in\{0,1\}
and set
B=\operatorname{Im}(\beta)
.

Let
U=\alpha^{-1}(T\backslash V)
and
M=\beta^{-1}(\mathbf{Tr}(X))=(q\circ\beta)^{-1}(T\backslash V)
. We first prove that
\alpha(U)=q(\beta(M))
. Let
u\in U
and
a=\alpha(u)\in T\backslash V
. Then there is a unique
b\in\mathbf{Tr}(X)
with
q(x)=a
. If
b\notin B
, then
B
must lie in a component
C
of
X\backslash\{b\}
. But then
A
lies in the component
q(C)
of
T\backslash\{b\}
, which cannot occur. Therefore,
b\in B
. Then there exists
m\in M
such that
\beta(m)=b
and thus
a=q(\beta(m))\in q(\beta(M))
. Thus
\alpha(U)\subseteq q(\beta(M))
. For the other inclusion, let
m\in M
. We have
b=\beta(m)\in\mathbf{Tr}(X)
and
a=q(b)\in T\backslash V
. If
a\notin A
, then
A
lies in a single connected component
D
of
T\backslash\{a\}
. Then
q^{-1}(a_{0})\cup q^{-1}(a_{1})
lies in the connected component
C
of
X\backslash\{b\}
satisfying
q(C)=D
. Since
\{b_{1},b_{2}\}\subseteq C
and
b\in B\cap\mathbf{Tr}(X)
violates Proposition
3.7
(which applies since
\beta(0)
and
\beta(1)
do not lie in a single piece of
X
). Therefore,
a\in A
. In particular,
q(\beta(m))=q(b)=a=\alpha(u)
for unique
u\in U
. This proves the other inclusion.

Since
U
is dense in
[0,1]
, we have
A=\alpha(\overline{U})
and since
[0,1]
is compact and
\alpha
is continuous, we have
\alpha(\overline{U})=\overline{\alpha(U)}
. Thus
A=\overline{\alpha(U)}=\overline{q(\beta(M))}\subseteq q(B)
. We also have
q(\beta(\overline{M}))\subseteq\overline{q(\beta(M))}=\overline{\alpha(U)}\subseteq A
. If
(s,t)
is a component of
[0,1]\backslash\overline{M}
, then
\beta
maps
[s,t]
into a piece of
X
and
q(\beta([s,t]))=q(\beta(s))\in q(\beta(\overline{M}))\subseteq A
. This completes the proof that
q(B)=A
.
∎

Lemma 3.22
.

Let
(X,\mathscr{P})
be a disjointly tree-graded space. If
T
is the quotient space of
X
given by collapsing each piece to a point, then
T
is a topological tree.

Proof.

According to Lemma
3.20
, it suffices to show that
T
does not contain a simple closed curve. Suppose that
C\subseteq T
is a simple closed curve. Let
A_{1},A_{2}
be arcs such that
C=A_{1}\cup A_{2}
and such that
A_{1}\cap A_{2}=\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
is a two-point set. As before, let
q:X\to T
denote the quotient map. By Lemma
3.21
, there exist arcs
B_{1},B_{2}\subseteq X
such that
q(B_{i})=A_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. For
i,j\in\{1,2\}
, let
b_{i,j}
be the endpoint of
B_{i}
in
B_{i}\cap q^{-1}(a_{j})
. In the case that
a_{1}\in V
, an initial segment of
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
may lie in the piece
q^{-1}(a_{1})
, By deleting this segment, we may assume that
B_{i}\cap q^{-1}(a_{1})=\{b_{i,1}\}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. Making the same adjustment when
a_{2}\in V
, we may assume that
B_{i}\cap q^{-1}(a_{2})=\{b_{i,2}\}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. Since the interiors of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are disjoint, our adjustments to the ends of
B_{i}
ensures that the interiors of
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are disjoint. Fix
j\in\{1,2\}
. If
b_{1,j}=b_{2,j}
, let
C_{j}=\{b_{1,j}\}
. On the other hand, if
b_{1,j}\neq b_{2,j}
, then
q^{-1}(a_{j})
is a piece of
X
and we may find an arc
C_{j}\subseteq q^{-1}(a_{j})
with endpoints
b_{1,j}
and
b_{2,j}
. With
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
defined,
D=B_{1}\cup B_{2}\cup C_{1}\cup C_{2}
is a simple closed curve in
X
for which
q(D)=C
. Thus
D
must lie in a single piece of
X
, which forces
C
to be a single point; a contradiction.
∎

Example 3.23
.

The topological tree
T
constructed as the quotient of
X
in the previous two lemmas need not be a topological
\mathbb{R}
-tree. For example let
X=\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}\cup\mathbb{Z}\times[0,1]
having single piece
P=\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}
. Then the quotient space
T=X/P
is an infinite wedge of arcs with the weak topology and is not first countable.

Example 3.24
.

The quotient map
q:X\to T
constructed in the previous two lemmas need not be a closed map. For instance, if
X=[0,1]\times\{0\}\cup\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{1/n\}\times[0,1]
with pieces
P_{n}=\{1/n\}\times[0,1]
, then we may identify
T=[0,1]\times\{0\}
,
V=\{0\}\cup\{1/n\mid b\in\mathbb{N}\}
, and
q:X\to T
with vertical projection. In this case,
q
is not a closed map.

Next, we show that a disjointly tree-graded space can be completely recovered from a given parameterization.

Lemma 3.25
.

Let
T
be a uniquely arcwise-connected Hausdorff space,
V\subseteq T
be a closed totally disconnected subset, and let
q:Y\to T
be a map from a space
Y
with the following properties:

(1)

every fiber of
q
is path-connected,

(2)

q
is injective on
Y\backslash q^{-1}(V)
,

If
\alpha:[0,1]\to Y
is an injective path, then
q\circ\alpha:[0,1]\to T
is monotone. Moreover, if
\alpha:S^{1}\to Y
is an injective loop, then
q\circ\alpha:S^{1}\to T
is constant.

Proof.

Supposing
\alpha
is injective, let
a\in T
. If
a\in T\backslash V
, then
\alpha^{-1}(q^{-1}(a))
is contains a single point whenever it is non-empty. If
a\in V
, then
C=q^{-1}(a)
is path-connected by assumption. Suppose that
|\alpha^{-1}(C)|>1
. Let
s=\min(\alpha^{-1}(C))
and
t=\max(\alpha^{-1}(C))
. We claim that
\alpha^{-1}(C)=[s,t]
. Supposing otherwise,
\alpha^{-1}(C)
is a proper closed subset of
[s,t]
. Choose a connected component
(c,d)
of
[s,t]\backslash\alpha^{-1}(C)
. Then
\alpha|_{[c,d]}:[c,d]\to Y
is a path with
\alpha^{-1}(C)=\{c,d\}
and
q\circ\alpha|_{[c,d]}
is a loop in
T
based at
a
that maps
(c,d)
into a connected component
T^{\prime}
of
T\backslash\{a\}
. Since
T^{\prime}
is uniquely arcwise-connected and
V
is totally disconnected, we may find
c<c^{\prime}<d^{\prime}<d
such that
q\circ\alpha(c^{\prime})=q\circ\alpha(d^{\prime})\in T^{\prime}\backslash V
. Since
q:Y\backslash q^{-1}(V)\to T\backslash V
is injective, we have
\alpha(c^{\prime})=\alpha(d^{\prime})
; a contradiction.

For the second statement, suppose
\alpha:S^{1}\to Y
is injective. We may cover
S^{1}
by various arcs and apply the first part of the lemma to see that
q\circ\alpha
is monotone. Additionally, since
T
is Hausdorff,
q\circ\alpha
is quotient onto it’s image. Since a Hausdorff quotient of
S^{1}
by a monotone quotient map is a simple closed curve and
T
is uniquely arcwise-connected,
q\circ\alpha
must be constant.
∎

Lemma 3.26
.

Suppose
X
is a path-connected, locally path-connected metrizable space,
T
is a topological tree,
V\subseteq T
is a closed totally disconnected subspace, and
q:X\to T
is a quotient map with path-connected fibers and such that the fiber of each point in
T\backslash V
is degenerate. Then
\mathscr{P}=\{q^{-1}(v)\mid v\in V\}
is a disjoint tree-grading on
X
for which
(T,V,q)
is a parameterization.

Proof.

The elements of
\mathscr{P}
are pairwise-disjoint by construction. Since
V
is closed in
T
,
q^{-1}(V)=\bigcup\mathscr{P}
is closed in
X
. Since
V
is totally disconnected and each element of
\mathscr{P}
is path-connected, it follows that the elements of
\mathscr{P}
are the path-components of
\bigcup\mathscr{P}
. Moreover, since
T
,
V
, and
q:X\to T
meet the hypotheses of Lemma
3.25
, we see that if
\alpha:S^{1}\to X
parameterizes a simple closed curve, then
q\circ\alpha:S^{1}\to T
must be constant. Thus
\operatorname{Im}(\alpha)
must lie in some non-degenerate fiber of
q
, namely, some element of
\mathscr{P}
.
∎

The above lemmas combine to give the following statement, which shows that one could give an equivalent definition of disjointly tree-graded spaces in terms of parameterizations.

Theorem 3.27
.

Suppose
X
is a path-connected, locally path-connected metrizable space. Every disjoint tree-grading
\mathscr{P}
on
X
admits a parameterization
(T,V,q)
for
(X,\mathscr{P})
where
q
is a topological quotient map. Conversely, if
(T,V,q)
is a triple where
T
is a topological tree,
V\subseteq T
is a closed totally disconnected subspace, and
q:X\to T
is a continuous surjection with path-connected fibers and such that the fiber of each point in
T\backslash V
is degenerate, then
\mathscr{P}=\{q^{-1}(v)\mid v\in V\}
is a disjoint tree-grading on
X
for which
(T,V,q)
is a parameterization.

Remark 3.28
.

Lemma
3.22
shows that we may always choose the map
q:X\to T
in a parameterization
(T,V,q)
to be a topological quotient map if we wish to do so. In Section
5
, we will show that we may also choose
T
to be a topological
\mathbb{R}
-tree and
q
to be non-expansive. Since we do not require that
q
is always a quotient map, the topological tree
T
in a parameterization need not be uniquely determined up to homeomorphism. The ability to recognize a disjoint tree-grading on a space without making specific demands on
T
and
q
will be important later on.

4.
Grade-preserving maps and graded subspaces

The following is the appropriate notion of a morphism of tree-graded spaces.

Definition 4.1

Let

Definition 4.2
.

Let
(X,\mathscr{P})
be a disjointly tree-graded space. A
graded subspace
of

is a disjointly tree-graded space
(Y,\mathscr{Q})
where
Y
is a path-connected, locally path-connected subspace of

and
\mathscr{Q}=\{P\cap Y\mid P\in\mathscr{P}\text{ s.t. }P\cap Y\neq\emptyset\}
.

Remark 4.3
.

The set
\mathscr{Q}
in Definition
4.2
is always a well-defined tree-grading on
Y
. Since
Y
is assumed to be path-connected, it follows from Lemma

that the retraction

and

Definition 4.4
.

Let
(Y,\mathscr{Q})
be a graded subspace of
(X,\mathscr{P})
.

(1)

We say that
(Y,\mathscr{Q})
is a
full
graded subspace of
(X,\mathscr{P})

\mathscr{Q}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
.

(2)

We say that
(Y,\mathscr{Q})
is a

if whenever

,

.

A special case of Lemma
3.12
is the following lemma.

Lemma 4.5
.

If
(Y,\mathscr{Q})
is a sectional graded subspace of
(X,\mathscr{P})
where
Y
is closed in
X
, then there is a canonical non-expansive retraction

.

In the next proposition, we show that if
(Y,\mathscr{Q})
is a graded subspace of

, then we can always enlarge

by replacing each non-degenerate piece of

with the piece of

Proposition 4.6
.

Suppose that
(Y,\mathscr{Q})
is a graded subspace of
(X,\mathscr{P})
and
\mathscr{R}\subseteq\mathscr{Q}
. Define
\mathscr{P}_{0}=\{P\in\mathscr{P}\mid\exists R\in\mathscr{R}\text{ s.t. }R\subseteq P\}

\mathscr{Q}_{0}=(\mathscr{Q}\backslash\mathscr{R})\cup\mathscr{P}_{0}

\mathscr{Q}_{0}
is a disjoint tree-grading on

such that we have graded subspace inclusions

is closed in

.

Proof.

It is straightforward to check that

in

for all

. If infinitely many

, then

. So we may assume that
a_{n}\in(\bigcup\mathscr{P}_{0})\backslash Y
for all

. If infinitely many
a_{n}
lie in some

is closed in

. Thus we may find point

in

Lemma 4.9
.

If
(X,\mathscr{P})
is a disjointly tree-graded space where

(1)

has at most countably many non-degenerate pieces of null diameter,

(2)

\mathbf{Tr}(X)

Let

\mathscr{U}
be any finite open cover of

\mathscr{U}

such that each

does not lie in any single element of

. If
\lambda>0
is a Lebesgue number for

, then
\diam(P_{i})\geq\lambda
for all

. Find

with

\{a_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\to a
and
\{b_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\to b

. Find path-connected open sets

. Now we can find injective paths

from

to

and
\beta:[0,1]\to U_{b}
from

to

. Let

Now, find a sequence

Since

∎

5.
Metric quotients of disjointly tree-graded spaces

Fix a disjointly tree-graded space
(X,\mathscr{P})
and let
\mathscr{Q}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
. Let
X_{\mathscr{Q}}
denote the quotient set obtained by identifying each
P\in\mathscr{P}\backslash\mathscr{Q}
to a single point and let
\Gamma_{\mathscr{Q}}:X\to X_{\mathscr{Q}}
denote the surjection making these identifications. For each
P\in\mathscr{P}\backslash\mathscr{Q}
, let
y_{P}
denote the point in
X_{\mathscr{Q}}
, which is the image of
P
under
\Gamma_{\mathscr{Q}}
. For the sake of simplicity, we identify
X\backslash\bigcup\{P\mid P\in\mathscr{P}\backslash\mathscr{Q}\}
with
X_{\mathscr{Q}}\backslash\{y_{P}\mid P\in\mathscr{P}\backslash\mathscr{Q}\}
so that
\Gamma_{\mathscr{Q}}
is the identity on
X\backslash\bigcup\{P\mid P\in\mathscr{P}\backslash\mathscr{Q}\}
and so that
\Gamma_{\mathscr{Q}}(P)=y_{P}
when
P\in\mathscr{P}\backslash\mathscr{Q}
.

Because we do not demand that grade-preserving maps are non-expansive, we have occasional need to view
X_{\mathscr{Q}}
the topological quotient space of
X
. We write
X_{\mathscr{Q}}^{qt}
to denote
X_{\mathscr{Q}}
equipped with the quotient topology inherited from
\Gamma_{\mathscr{Q}}
. If
\mathscr{Q}=\emptyset
, then
X_{\mathscr{Q}}^{qt}
is precisely the topological tree from Section
3.4
. The same argument used in the proof of Lemma
3.20
shows that
X_{\mathscr{Q}}^{qt}
is Hausdorff for any
\mathscr{Q}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
.

We also wish to endow
X_{\mathscr{Q}}
with a disjoint tree grading
\mathscr{P}_{\mathscr{Q}}=\mathscr{Q}\cup\{\{y_{P}\}\mid P\in\mathscr{P}\backslash\mathscr{Q}\}
for which the map
\Gamma_{\mathscr{Q}}:X\to X_{\mathscr{Q}}
would be grade-preserving. However,
X_{\mathscr{Q}}^{qt}
need not be first countable. Thus we must explicitly define a path-diameter metric
\rho
on
X_{\mathscr{Q}}
such that
\Gamma_{\mathscr{Q}}:X\to X_{\mathscr{Q}}
is a metric quotient map in the following sense.

Definition 5.1
.

A function
q:(X,d)\to(Y,\rho)
of metric spaces is a
metric quotient map
if
q
is a non-expansive surjection with the property that for every non-expansive map
g:(X,d)\to(X^{\prime},d^{\prime})
where
g
is constant on the fibers of
q
, there exists a unique non-expansive map
f:(Y,\rho)\to(X^{\prime},d^{\prime})
such that
f\circ q=g
.

This section is devoted to proving the following theorem.

Theorem 5.2
.

For any
\mathscr{Q}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
, there exists a path-diameter metric
\rho
on
X_{\mathscr{Q}}
so that when
X_{\mathscr{Q}}
is given the topology induced by
\rho
,
\mathscr{P}_{\mathscr{Q}}
is a disjoint tree-grading on
X_{\mathscr{Q}}
and
\Gamma_{\mathscr{Q}}:(X,d)\to(X_{\mathscr{Q}},\rho)
is a grade-preserving, metric quotient map.

Since
\mathscr{Q}
is fixed, we simplify our notation by writing
Y
for
X_{\mathscr{Q}}
and
\Gamma:X\to Y
for the map
\Gamma_{\mathscr{Q}}
. Let
d
be a path-diameter metric inducing the topology of
X
. We take
\rho:Y\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
be the standard quotient pseudometric
[
6
]
on
Y
inherited from
(X,d)
by the map
q
:
\rho(y,z)
is the infimum of all sums
\sum_{i=1}^{n}d(a_{i},b_{i})
taken over the set of all finite chains
a_{1},b_{1},a_{2},b_{2},\cdots,a_{n},b_{n}
in
X
where
\Gamma(a_{1})=y
,
\Gamma(b_{n})=z
, and
\Gamma(b_{i})=\Gamma(a_{i+1})
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
.

Proposition 5.3
.

The quotient pseudometric
\rho
on

is a metric.

Proof.

We check that
\rho
is positive definite. Suppose
y
and

are distinct points in
Y
. Then there exists a point
s_{1}\in\mathbf{Tr}(X)
such that
\Gamma^{-1}(y)
and
\Gamma^{-1}(z)
lie in distinct connected components of
X\backslash\{s_{1}\}
. Since
\mathbf{Tr}(X)
is open in
X
, we may find
\epsilon>0
such that
\overline{B_{\epsilon}(s_{1})}\subseteq\mathbf{Tr}(X)\backslash(\Gamma^{-1}(y)\cup\Gamma^{-1}(z))
. Since

is a path-diameter metric,
\overline{B_{\epsilon}(s_{1})}
is uniquely-arcwise-connected. In particular, there is an arc

with endpoints

Consider a finite chain

in

\Gamma(a_{1})=y
,
\Gamma(b_{n})=z

d(a_{1},b_{1})+\cdots+d(a_{j-1},b_{j-1})+d(a_{j},b_{j+1})+d(a_{j+2},b_{j+2})+\cdots+d(a_{n},b_{n})

Thus, we may assume that

or

\mathbf{Tr}(X)

Proof.

Suppose to obtain a contradiction that

Definition 6.1

A space

8.
Generalized covering maps

An important tool in our proof is the generalized covering space theory of Fischer-Zastrow
[
18
]
, which uses the usual lifting property of covering maps as a definition.

Definition 8.1
.

A map
p:E\to X
is a
generalized covering map
if

(1)

E
is path-connected and locally path-connected,

(2)

for every path-connected, locally path-connected space
Y
, based map
f:(Y,y)\to(X,x)
, and point
e\in p^{-1}(x)
such that
f_{\#}(\pi_{1}(Y,y))\leq p_{\#}(\pi_{1}(E,e))
, there exists a unique map
\widetilde{f}:(Y,y)\to(E,e)
such that
p\circ\widetilde{f}=f
.

If, in addition,
p_{\#}(\pi_{1}(E,e_{0}))
is a normal subgroup of
\pi_{1}(X,p(e_{0}))
for some
e_{0}\in E
, then we refer to
p
as a
generalized regular covering map
. If
E
is simply connected and
X
is path-connected, then we refer to
p
as a
generalized universal covering map
and
E
as a
generalized universal covering space
of
X
.

Remark 8.2
.

Note that every generalized covering map has the unique path lifting property and therefore all of it’s fibers are
T_{1}
.

Remark 8.3
.

Formally, we do not require that a generalized covering map be surjective since the definition permits
X
to have multiple path-components. However, a generalized covering map
p:E\to X
maps surjectively onto a path-component of
X
.

Remark 8.4

(A basis for the topology of
E
)
.

If
p:E\to X
is a generalized covering map, the topology of
E
can be identified with the so-called “whisker topology”
[
3
, Lemma 5.10]
. Let

and

be an open neighborhood of

in
X
. Then a basic neighborhood of

in

Example 8.5

(Locally path-connected coreflections)
.

An important example of a generalized covering map is one which is bijective but need to be a homeomorphism. For any space

be the space with the same underlying set as

but with the topology generated by the path-components of the open sets in

is referred to as the

]

is locally path-connected, the identity

Remark 8.6

(Discreteness of fibers)
.

If

is a generalized covering map, then

is a Serre fibration with unique path lifting. However, the fibers of

need not be homeomorphic to each other. In fact, some fibers will may be discrete while others are non-discrete. A fiber

is discrete if and only if for every

, there exists an open neighborhood

of

in

such that if

is the inclusion, then

. Moreover, if there exists a path-connected and locally path-connected open neighborhood

of

such that

for all

, then

is evenly covered by
p
(it is straightforward to check that

when

and

homeomorphically onto

). Hence, if

is a locally path-connected space and

is semilocally simply connected at

, then

admits a neighborhood

that is evenly covered by

. For example, if

is a disjointly tree-graded and

is a generalized covering map, then each connected component

of

is evenly covered by

.

When

\widetilde{d}(a,b)=\inf\{\diam_{d}(\operatorname{Im}(p\circ\widetilde{\alpha}))\mid\widetilde{\alpha}\in\mathcal{P}(E,a,b)\}.

Lemma 8.9
.

If
p:E\to X
is a generalized covering map and
d
is a compatible metric on
X
, then
\widetilde{d}
is a compatible path-diameter metric on
E
such that

is non-expansive. Moreover, if
Y
is a path-connected space,
\widetilde{f}:Y\to E
is a map, and
f=p\circ\widetilde{f}
, then
\diam(\operatorname{Im}(f))=\diam(\operatorname{Im}(\widetilde{f}))
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{P}(E,b,a)=\{\widetilde{\alpha}^{-1}\mid\widetilde{\alpha}\in\mathcal{P}(E,a,b)\}
, we have
\widetilde{d}(a,b)=\widetilde{d}(b,a)
. If
\widetilde{\alpha}\in\mathcal{P}(E,a,b)
and
\widetilde{\beta}\in\mathcal{P}(E,b,c)
, then
\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot\widetilde{\beta}\in\mathcal{P}(E,a,c)
where
\diam(\operatorname{Im}(p\circ(\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot\widetilde{\beta})))\leq\diam(\operatorname{Im}(p\circ\widetilde{\alpha}))+\diam(\operatorname{Im}(p\circ\widetilde{\beta}))
. The triangle inequality follows. If
a=b
, then the constant path gives
\widetilde{d}(a,b)=0
. Suppose
a\neq b
in
E
. If
p(a)\neq p(b)
, then any path between the points has diameter at least
d(p(a),p(b))
and thus
\widetilde{d}(a,b)>0
. Suppose
p(a)=p(b)=x
and, to obtain a contradiction, that
\widetilde{d}(a,b)=0
. Then there exists a sequence of paths
\widetilde{\alpha_{n}}:[0,1]\to E
,

from
a
to
b
such that
\alpha_{n}=p\circ\widetilde{\alpha}_{n}
is a loop based at
x
and
\{\diam(\operatorname{Im}(\alpha_{n}))\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\to 0
. Let

be the line segment with endpoints
(0,0)
and

and set

. Define

to be the path
\alpha_{n}
on

. Since the diameters of the paths
\alpha_{n}
are null,
f
is continuous. Moreover, since

is a dendrite, there is a unique lift
\widetilde{f}:(H,(0,0))\to(E,a)
, which is defined to be
\widetilde{\alpha_{n}}
on

. But
\{h_{n}\}\to(0,0)
in

,
\widetilde{f}(0,0)=a
, and
\widetilde{f}(h_{n})=b
for all

. This contradicts the fact from Remark
8.2
that

is

. Thus

and we conclude that

is a metric.

To see that

, notice that for every

, we have

.

To see that

is non-expansive, note that

for all

. Hence,

.

For the last statement, we have
\diam(\operatorname{Im}(f))\leq\diam(\operatorname{Im}(\widetilde{f}))
since

is non-expansive. Let

and find a path

.

Finally, we check that

is a path-diameter metric. Given

is simply connected and the pieces of

are retracts of

is simply connected. For (4), note that since each piece

of

is a retract of

is locally path-connected and simply connected. If

9.
Inverse limits of disjointly tree-graded spaces

Here, we show that certain inverse limits of disjointly tree-graded spaces inherit a natural disjoint tree-grading. A basic fact we will use is the following.

Lemma 9.1
.

Let
\varprojlim_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(T_{n},g_{n+1,n})
be the inverse limit of a sequence of uniquely arcwise-connected Hausdorff spaces
T_{n}
and continuous functions
g_{n+1,n}:T_{n+1}\to T_{n}
. Then
\varprojlim_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(T_{n},g_{n+1,n})
contains no simple closed curve. Moreover, if
T_{lim}
is a path component of
\varprojlim_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(T_{n},g_{n+1,n})
, then
\operatorname{{\bf lpc}}(T_{lim})
is a topological tree.

Proof.

Let
g_{n}
denote the projection maps of the limit. If
C\subseteq\varprojlim_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(T_{n},g_{n+1,n})
is a simple closed curve, then
D_{n}=g_{n}(C)
is a dendrite and
C
is homeomorphic to the inverse limit of the sequence
(D_{n},(g_{n+1,n})|_{D_{n+1}})
of dendrites. However, standard results in Continuum Theorem such as
[
22
, Exercise 2.19]
can be used to show that an inverse limit of dendrites cannot contain a simple closed curve.
∎

Definition 9.2
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

be a disjointly tree-graded space and

be a grade-preserving map. We say that a sequence

for all

.
