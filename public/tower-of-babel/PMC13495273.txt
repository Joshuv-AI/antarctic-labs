Macrophage immunometabolism in stroke: a view from single-cell and nano technologies
Authors: Yajun Zhu, Zichao Huang, Xiaoguo Li, Jiru Zhou, Xingwei Lei, Fuming Liang, Jin Yan, Hongji Deng, Xiaochuan Sun, Zongduo Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStroke induces profound neuroinflammation in which macrophages play a complex dual role, contributing to both injury and repair. The traditional M1/M2 classification is increasingly recognized as oversimplified. Advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have revealed a spectrum of dynamic macrophage subpopulations with distinct functional and metabolic states, fundamentally reshaping our understanding of post-stroke immunity.Main bodyThis review synthesizes recent insights into macrophage heterogeneity from a single-cell perspective, highlighting novel subsets such as an LCP1⁺ population defined by coupled glycolipid metabolism. We discuss how metabolic reprogramming, including glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, cholesterol metabolism, hypoxia‑driven gradients, and mitochondrial dynamics, critically underpins macrophage polarization. Glycolysis fuels pro-inflammatory (M1-like) responses, whereas oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation support anti-inflammatory and reparative (M2-like) functions. We further explore innovative nano‑therapeutic strategies, including engineered liposomes, exosomes, and responsive polymeric nanoparticles, that enable spatiotemporally precise modulation of macrophage activity. Based on these advances, we propose an integrative framework that directly links scRNA‑seq‑defined macrophage subsets to their metabolic pathways, druggable targets, and tailored nano‑interventions. We also critically examine clinical translation barriers and prioritize actionable targets (e.g., CCR2, PPARγ, Nrf2) for future stroke therapy.ConclusionsThe convergence of single‑cell genomics, immunometabolism, and nanotechnology offers a transformative path toward precision immunomodulation in stroke. Moving beyond the static M1/M2 dichotomy to target macrophage subpopulations and their metabolic drivers guided by an integrated framework holds significant promise for developing more effective therapies.

Targeting macrophage plasticity in stroke therapy

Stroke represents a major global health challenge, being a leading cause of death and long-term disability worldwide [1–3]. It triggers a powerful neuroinflammatory response in which macrophages play a complex dual role, contributing to both acute injury and subsequent repair [4–6]. For years, this duality was framed by the simplistic M1 (pro-inflammatory)/M2 (anti-inflammatory) dichotomy [7, 8]. However, recent breakthroughs, particularly from single-cell RNA sequencing, have unveiled a far more complex landscape [9]. We now know the brain hosts a spectrum of dynamic macrophage subsets, each with distinct functions and metabolic programs. This heterogeneity challenges the traditional binary classification and calls for a revised framework that captures the full complexity of macrophage biology in stroke.

Concurrent with this single‑cell revolution, the field of immunometabolism has established that metabolic reprogramming directly commands macrophage polarization. Glycolysis fuels pro‑inflammatory responses, whereas oxidative phosphorylation and cholesterol metabolism underpin anti‑inflammatory and reparative functions [10–12]. Recognizing that specific metabolic pathways drive distinct subset behaviors opens new opportunities for therapeutic intervention. Moreover, innovative nano‑delivery systems, including engineered liposomes, exosomes, and responsive polymeric nanoparticles, enable spatiotemporally precise modulation of macrophage activity and phenotype, overcoming many limitations of conventional drug administration.

In this review, we synthesize recent insights from scRNA‑seq to redefine macrophage heterogeneity in stroke, integrate these findings with immune-metabolic mechanisms, and explore how nano‑therapeutic strategies can be rationally designed to target specific macrophage subsets and their metabolic drivers. By moving beyond the static M1/M2 dichotomy toward a unified framework linking subset identity, metabolic state, and nano‑intervention, we aim to provide a roadmap for precision immunomodulation in stroke therapy.

Heterogeneity of macrophages and novel subtyping from a single-cell perspective

Anatomical classification

Macrophages primarily encompass microglia, border-associated macrophages (BAMs, see Glossary), and monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) in the central nervous system (CNS) [4]. Microglia, as the resident macrophages of the brain, are widely distributed throughout the parenchyma. They play a crucial role in maintaining homeostasis of CNS and regulating neuroinflammation through communication and collaboration with other brain cells and peripheral immune cells [13]. Microglia are the first immune cells activated, undergoing rapid morphological and functional changes (e.g., phenotypic activation, hypertrophy, and releasing of inflammatory mediators) and participating in early inflammatory responses as well as tissue damage and repair following stroke [14]. BAMs are located at CNS borders rather than within the parenchyma and can be classified into three categories based on anatomical location: meningeal macrophages (MMs), perivascular macrophages (PVMs), and choroid plexus macrophages (CPMs) [4]. Current understanding of BAMs’ physiological functions is limited. Compared to microglia, BAMs exhibit heterogeneous ontogeny and likely regulate brain homeostasis and disease progression via mechanisms distinct from microglia. Studies suggested that PVMs and MMs might play significant roles in immune regulation, waste clearance, and modulation of BBB’s permeability [9, 15, 16]. However, the physiological role of CPMs remains poor understanding. Gerganova et al. hypothesized that CPMs might act as a “gatekeeper” controlling the infiltration of peripheral immune cells into the brain, which potentially mediated disease progression through interactions with other immune cells [17]. Within the CNS, infiltrating MDMs primarily originate from the differentiation of bone marrow-derived Ly6C+ monocytes. They infiltrate the brain via a CCR2-dependent mechanism and exacerbate inflammatory brain injury after stroke [4, 18–20] (Fig. 1).

Traditionally phenotypic classification of M1/M2

Based on distinct inflammatory properties, macrophages are conventionally classified as classically activated M1-type (the pro-inflammatory phenotype) or alternatively activated M2-type (the anti-inflammatory phenotype). The dynamic balance between these phenotypes plays a pivotal role in regulating tissue inflammation, injury, and repair [21]. The polarization of M1 macrophage can be activated via signaling pathways such as METTL14/STING [22], PKR/STAT1 [23], IRF5 [24], CSF1R [25], and RhoA/ROCK [26] following stroke. Activated M1 macrophages secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) and neurotoxic mediators (e.g., iNOS) to exacerbate inflammatory brain injury. Conversely, M2 macrophage activation relies on signaling molecules including PI3K/Akt [27], mTOR [8], JAK2/STAT3 [28], PPARγ [29], and Slc25a1 [12]. They promote tissue repair by secreting anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-4 and IL-10 [25, 30]. Furthermore, M1 macrophages can be identified by molecular markers including CD32, CD16, MHC-II, CD80, and CD86, whereas markers of M2 include CD206, CD163, and Arg1 [17, 21]. However, macrophages exhibit a dynamic phenotypic continuum across different stroke stages, which renders this simplistic binary classification inadequate for comprehensively reflecting their complex post-stroke biology. Additionally, novel technologies like single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have revealed numerous kinds of macrophage subsets with unique molecular signatures [31, 32]. These findings underscore the need to move beyond the traditional framework of M1/M2 to establish a more precise macrophage classification system in order to guide stroke research.

Novel macrophage classification based on scRNA-seq technology

Recent advances in scRNA-seq technology have provided unprecedented insights into macrophage heterogeneity (Table 1). Both microglia and macrophages co-regulate neuroinflammation and repair through phenotypic specialization into different functional subsets following stroke. Microglia, as resident cells, predominantly initiate a pro-inflammatory response in the early phase before transitioning towards a protective role in later stages. Macrophages, primarily derived from peripheral infiltration, predominantly mediate inflammatory recruitment and contribute to tissue repair [32]. Therefore, targeting specific macrophage subsets holds promise as a novel therapeutic strategy for stroke.

Novel macrophage classification based on scRNA-seq technology

Microglia differentiate into multiple functional subtypes following stroke: (1) Homeostatic Subtype: Characterized by high expression of low-inflammatory genes such as P2RY12, TMEM119, and Gpr34. This population maintains immune homeostasis and performs baseline surveillance functions [31, 33–35]. (2) Pro-inflammatory/Activated Subtype: Identified by markers including Lgals3, SPP1, ISG15, and IRF7 [31]。These cells exacerbate neuroinflammation through the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IFN-γ, IL-1β) and disrupt the integrity of BBB via secretion of MMP12 [36]. (3) Proliferative Subtype: Exhibiting elevated expression of cell cycle genes (Birc5, Top2a). These microglia replenish immune cells in the lesioned brain tissue through localized proliferation [36]. (4) Anti-inflammatory/Reparative Subtype: Defined by high expression of ARG1, IL-10, and LILRB4. They suppress inflammation while promoting tissue repair through phagocytosis of debris and facilitation of remyelination [37]. (5) DAM-like Subtype: Expressing the markers (e.g., SPP1, LPL, CST7) of disease-associated microglia (DAM) alongside antioxidant genes (Prdx1, Mt1/2, Lilrb4a, and Ch25h). This subtype contributes to antioxidant defense, tissue protection, and plays a significant role in post-stroke immunomodulation [34].

Macrophages exhibit significant heterogeneity after stroke, primarily comprising the following subtypes: (1) Antigen-Presenting Subset: Including BAMs and peripherally infiltrated monocytes/macrophages, characterized by high expression of MHC class II molecules (H2-Eb1, H2-Aa, H2-Ab1) and the antigen presentation-associated gene Cd74 [35, 38]. (2) Chemokine-Enriched Subset: Defined by prominent expression of chemokines (CCL1, CCL2, CCL3, CXCL1, CXCL2, and CXCL10). These cells recruit diverse immune cells into the brain, contributing to immunomodulation [31, 35, 39]. (3) Phagocytic Subset: Mediateing clearance of apoptotic cells and myelin debris through activation of the C1q complement system [35, 39]. (4) Metabolic Subset: Regulating oxidative phosphorylation and hypoxia-responsive metabolic pathways via high expression of genes (COX7B, COX8A, UQCR11), thereby modulating M1/M2 macrophage polarization [40]. Additionally, macrophages also encompass pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory/reparative subsets, which will not be detailed here.

Dynamic changes in macrophage subsets across stroke stages

Role of macrophages in the acute phase of stroke

Rapid macrophage recruitment after ischemic stroke: the pivotal role of CCR2

C-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2), a key member of the C-C chemokine receptor family, plays a central role in the inflammatory cascade of ischemic brain injury by mediating the chemotactic recruitment of monocytes/macrophagesC-C [41]. Early studies demonstrated that upregulation of CCR2 expression in the spleen and brain strongly correlates with enlarged infarct volume after ischemia. Blockade of CCR2 signaling significantly reduces the migration of blood-derived macrophages into the ischemic core and suppresses neutrophil infiltration, which indicates that this receptor is a critical checkpoint regulating immune cell recruitment after stroke [42, 43]. Furthermore, the monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1)/CCR2 axis dominates early inflammatory activation following ischemia [41]. Multiple researches have shown that ischemic injury induces rapid release of MCP-1 from endothelial cells [44–46]. MCP-1 binding to CCR2 drives macrophages to penetrate the BBB and accumulate at the lesion site. This process can be blocked by agents such as progesterone or agomelatine, which inhibit MCP-1 secretion from endothelial cells [44–46].

Pharmacological interventions targeting the MCP-1/CCR2 axis include traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) and probiotic therapy, offering unique therapeutic strategies. Baicalin, a key flavonoid from Scutellaria baicalensis, directly binds to CCR2, blocking ligand-receptor interaction, while concurrently inhibiting macrophage release of inflammatory mediators such as NO and IL-6 [47]. In addition, Taohong Siwu Decoction (THSWD), a classical TCM formula, downregulates expression of MCP-1/CCR2, reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α), and increases anti-inflammatory IL-4, ultimately inhibiting macrophage polarization and rebalancing inflammatory cytokines to remodel the inflammatory microenvironment [18]. The multi-target properties of these TCM components illustrate alternative approaches to modulating the MCP-1/CCR2 axis. However, due to their pleiotropic effects and the lack of subset-specific validation, they are currently considered exploratory concepts rather than near-term translational strategies for stroke. As an exploratory concept, a recent study showed that the gut probiotic Lactobacillus reuteri can reduce peripheral immune cell infiltration into the brain by suppressing expression of MCP-1, which suggested a potential gut-brain axis modulation of CCR2 [48]. However, this approach remains at an early stage and is not yet a clinically actionable strategy.

It is noteworthy that CD36 critically regulates the expression of the MCP-1/CCR2 axis. Kim et al. progressively demonstrated that knockout of CD36 gene significantly reduces CCR2-mediated monocyte-macrophage infiltration and improves neurological outcomes, which confirmed the central role of the CD36/MCP-1/CCR2 pathway in the inflammatory cascade [49–51]. Thus, CCR2 integrates upstream CD36 signaling and MCP-1 ligand regulation, orchestrating processes from early immune recruitment to subsequent amplification of inflammatory signals, thereby forming a multitiered inflammatory regulatory network (Fig. 2).

The dual roles of macrophages following hemorrhagic stroke: pro-inflammation and phagocytosis

Hemorrhagic stroke (HS) primarily includes spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) and subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). Macrophages exhibit a dual role in neuro-inflammatory regulation and resolution of hematoma in the acute phase of HS. They drive the early inflammatory response via the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-mediated signaling pathway while simultaneously relying on CD36-associated pathways to promote absorption of hematoma. The dynamic balance between these processes influences disease outcome (Fig. 2).

TLR4 is a central regulatory hub controlling the neuroinflammatory storm after HS [52]. Firstly, distinct subtypes of peroxiredoxin (Prxs) activate TLR4 at specific early time points following ICH, inducing neuroinflammation. Prxs2 upregulates TLR4 expression on microglia/macrophages, activating NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation, and inducing the release of pro-inflammatory mediators such as TNF-αwithin 24 h post-ICH [53]. Subsequently, Prxs1 further upregulates TLR4 on macrophage surface, persistently amplifying the inflammatory response and exacerbating inflammatory brain injury at 72 h [54]. Secondly, heme, a red blood cell metabolite and endogenous TLR4 ligand, enhances activation of microglial/macrophage and increases pro-inflammatory cytokine release via NF-κB activation following both ICH and SAH. A Study showed that heme enhanced microglial activation through TLR4, subsequently activating NF-κB via the MyD88/TRIF signaling axis to trigger an inflammatory cascade after ICH [55]. In SAH models, Akamatsu et al. also demonstrated that heme-mediated upregulation of TLR4’s expression played a significant role in early neuroinflammation and neuronal apoptosis [56]. Furthermore, Xie Y et al. found that biglycan expression inducing activation of M1 microglial also depends on TLR4/NF-κB signaling in early stage of SAH [57]. Conversely, Islam et al. proposed that promoting expression of Lyn kinase in macrophage can suppress the TLR4-driven inflammatory storm [58].

CD36 is a key molecule regulating the phagocytic function of macrophages during early hematoma clearance in HS. Firstly, activation of Mac receptor promotes the transition of macrophages from a pro-inflammatory to a reparative phenotype via the Akt/Nrf2 pathway, accelerating the absorption and resolution of hematoma after ICH [59]. Nrf2 is a phagocytosis-associated transcription factor primarily regulating expression of CD36. Luo Z et al. demonstrated that the IRG1/itaconate axis enhanced phagocytosis of macrophage to erythrocyte by activating the Nrf2/CD36 axis [60]. Liu J et al. further revealed that the Nrf2/CD36 pathway was positively regulated by AMPK in phagocytosis of macrophage and can be inhibited by LRRC8A anionic channel [61]. Additionally, the SUMO and the IL-4/STAT6 signaling axis also contribute to macrophage-mediated clearance of hematoma, and promote pathological repair of brain [62, 63].

Macrophages balance tissue repair and scar formation in stroke progression

Phenotype switching of macrophage in tissue repair

MDMs become key effectors of tissue repair through phenotype switching in the chronic phase of stroke. Multiple studies indicate that MDMs possess higher phagocytic activity compared to microglia in IS [64, 65]. They function to clear apoptotic cells and tissue debris while promoting inflammation resolution. In addition, Ju H et al. further identified a subset of CD45-high-expressing MDMs exhibiting the highest phagocytic activity [65]. CCR2 is the key receptor mediating MDMs homing to cerebral lesions. A study found that they can promote the infiltration of pro-inflammatory MDMs into parenchymal early after stroke [66]. Later, expression of CCR2 decreases while CX3CR1’s expression increases under the induction of cytokines like IL-4 and IL-13, facilitating an anti-inflammatory role. Chen D et al. further demonstrated that IL-13 primarily promoted M2 polarization of microglia/macrophages (expressing Arg1, CD206) by inhibiting phosphorylation of STAT3 [67]. It enhanced axonal remyelination, repair of white matter, and improved neurological function in long-term IS. In HS research, Min H et al. discovered that soluble factors released by glial cells can drive CX3CR1+ macrophages towards the M2 phenotype [68]. These M2 macrophages potentially promote repair of neurovascular unit, axonal remyelination, and remodeling of white matter by phagocytosing cellular debris and secreting neurotrophic factors (e.g., BDNF, IGF) and TGF-β1. Furthermore, modulating activation of Liver X Receptor (LXR) also promotes polarization of M2 macrophage, reducing white matter injury by stimulating oligodendrocyte proliferation [69]. Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) participates in the long-term phenotypic conversion of microglia/macrophages in both IS and HS, promoting neurogenesis and angiogenesis. It also enhances phagocytic function by upregulating IL-10, thereby accelerating resolution of hematoma [29, 70].

Dual regulatory mechanisms of macrophage involvement in scar formation

Fibrotic scar formation inhibits axonal regeneration and represents a significant pathological process impacting tissue reconstruction and functional recovery after IS. While the classical marker of M2 macrophage (Arg1) promotes anti-inflammatory effects, it may also induce excessive fibrosis. Research indicated that the peak expression of Arg1 at 7 days post-stroke can induce fibrotic scar hyperplasia and pathological synaptic pruning in the peri-infarct border zone [71]. Yang Q et al. identified Sonic Hedgehog (Shh) as a key biomarker driving polarization of M2 microglial, with its expression peaking at 14 days after stroke [72]. Furthermore, Huang JG et al. proposed that activation of the Shh/TGF-β1 signaling pathway in M2 macrophages via IL-4 also played a crucial role in fibrotic scar formation and vascular regeneration [73]. Functional balance of macrophages during the progressive phase of stroke relies on the polarization of M2 macrophages mediating repair of neurovascular unit while simultaneously suppressing excessive fibrotic scarring. Therefore, targeting key molecules regulating macrophage phenotype switching at distinct temporal stages (such as CCR2, PPARγ, and LXR) may represent a potential strategy to optimize tissue repair in stroke.

Molecular mechanisms of macrophages’ phenotype regulation by metabolic reprogramming

Metabolic Reprogramming of Macrophage refers to the adaptation and alteration of their energy metabolism during differentiation and activation processes. The polarization of M1 macrophage primarily relies on the glycolytic pathway to meet the short-term energy demands of acute inflammation in the early phase of stroke. This is accompanied by impaired tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. In contrast, M2 polarization is more dependent on oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid oxidation, cholesterol metabolism, and glutamine metabolism pathways [10–12, 74]. Furthermore, the advancement of scRNA-seq technology has unveiled the existence of metabolically heterogeneous macrophage subsets, providing novel insights of immune-metabolic regulation in stroke (Fig. 3).

Metabolic regulation mechanisms of M1 macrophage polarization

A hallmark metabolic feature of M1 macrophage polarization is enhanced glycolysis. It was demonstrated that activation of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling axis significantly increased glycolytic flux in microglia, concurrently promoting M1 polarization and exacerbating neuroinflammation following ischemia-reperfusion injury [7]. Key enzymes within the glycolytic pathway are also crucial for the polarization of M1 macrophage. On the one hand, Hexokinase 2 (HK2) plays a central role in - neuroinflammation mediated by microglia after stroke. Its elevated expression sustains glycolytic flux, providing the energetic foundation for M1 polarization [11]. On the other hand, Pyruvate Kinase M2 (PKM2) further regulates the interconnected glucose metabolism-inflammation network in M1 macrophages. Zhang L et al. found that deficiency of Sirt5 enhanced glycolysis in MDMs via succinylational modification of PKM2 following stroke [75]. This modification increased the expression of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β and CD16 while decreasing the anti-inflammatory marker Arg1. PKM2 also amplifies the pro-inflammatory effects of M1 macrophages by promoting the secretion of High Mobility Group Box 1 and the chemokine CXCL16, which establishes a “metabolic-inflammatory” positive feedback loop [76, 77]. Furthermore, an integrated metabolomic-transcriptomic analysis of human macrophages suggests that enhanced tryptophan catabolism is a metabolic signature of pro-inflammatory macrophages. It is associated with the upregulation of tryptophan membrane transporters and key catabolic enzymes, IDO1 and IL4I1 [78].

Metabolic remodeling pathways in polarization of M2 macrophage

The restoration of the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation serves as the metabolic foundation for macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype. Li Y et al. demonstrated that the mitochondrial citrate transporter Slc25a1 can mediate deacetylation of histone H3/H4 after stroke, which in turn suppressed transcriptional regulation of γ-aminobutyric acid transaminase hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α). It can restore disrupted TCA cycle activity and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation in macrophages, ultimately promoting polarization of M2 Macrophage [12]. This reveals a novel pathway where metabolic reprogramming regulates phenotype switching of macrophage through epigenetic modifications.

The cholesterol metabolism network exerts a dual regulatory effect on polarization of M2 Macrophage. One study confirmed that impaired cholesterol metabolism reduced oxidative phosphorylation levels in macrophage after IS [79]. Conversely, exogenous cholesterol sulfate promoted the polarization toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype by increasing NADPH levels, accompanied by upregulated expression of IL-10, IL-4, and Arg1 [79]. Xiao J et al. further discovered that IL-4 and IL-13 induced Ch25h expression via phosphorylation of STAT6 [80]. This enzyme is significantly enriched in M2 macrophage subsets and drives the anti-inflammatory program by remodeling cholesterol metabolism. Additionally, Chen et al. confirmed that accumulation of cholesterol specifically attenuated the macrophage response to IFNγ, while lipolysis and fatty acid oxidation contributed to polarization of macrophages towards an anti-inflammatory phenotype via IL-4 [78].

Metabolically heterogeneous subsets of macrophage revealed by scRNA-seq technology

The scRNA-seq-defined macrophage subsets described in section. "Novel macrophage classification based on scRNA-seq technology" (Table 1) are not only phenotypic classifications, but also underpinned by distinct metabolic programs. For example, the LCP1⁺ subset co-enriches fatty acid and glycolytic pathways, representing a “glycolipid‑coupled” metabolic state that diverges from the conventional M1/M2 paradigm [81]. Conversely, the metabolic subset characterized by high expression of COX7B, COX8A, and UQCR11 reflects a state of enhanced oxidative phosphorylation, a metabolic configuration commonly linked to anti‑inflammatory or reparative functions in macrophages [40]. Similarly, the DAM‑like subset’s enrichment in Ch25h and LPL points to cholesterol and lipid metabolic reprogramming as a defining feature of its phenotype [82, 83]. Below, we highlight two additional metabolically heterogeneous subsets identified by scRNA‑seq that illustrate how amino acid and coordinated glycolipid metabolism shape macrophage polarization.

Firstly, the coordinated regulation of fatty acid and glycolytic metabolism defines a distinct functional phenotype of macrophage. Wang Y et al. utilized scRNA-seq technology to identified a macrophage subset characterized by high expression of Lymphocyte Cytosolic Protein 1 (LCP1), which exhibited enrichment in fatty acid and glycolytic metabolic pathways [81]. Concurrently, these cells demonstrated enhanced oxidative phosphorylation and ATP synthesis pathways. Their further investigation revealed that knockdown of LCP1 significantly attenuated ischemic brain injury [81]. It suggests that this subset may sustain a pro-inflammatory phenotype through “glycol-lipolytic coupling”. And its metabolic signature diverges from the conventional M1/M2 paradigm. Notably, the LCP1⁺ metabolic subset identified by scRNA‑seq in section. "Novel macrophage classification based on scRNA-seq technology" (Table 1) precisely corresponds to the molecular basis of the “glycol‑lipolytic coupling” phenotype described here. Secondly, the branched-chain aminotransferase (BCAT) within the amino acid metabolic pathway emerges as a novel regulatory nexus for macrophage’s polarization. Papathanassiu et al. employed scRNA-seq technology to identify a macrophage subset with elevated BCAT expression [84]. Impeding BCAT activity was found to reduce the M1-polarized transcriptome profile in murine peritoneal macrophages. Notably, amino acid metabolism in macrophages remains unreported in stroke research. The potential role of branched-chain amino acid metabolism in shaping macrophage phenotypes via modulation of inflammatory transcriptional programs warrants further investigation in stroke.

Beyond these subset-specific metabolic signatures, systems-level metabolic regulation, particularly hypoxia-driven gradients and mitochondrial dynamics, profoundly shapes macrophage polarization across the post-stroke brain. The ischemic core and penumbra exhibit markedly different oxygen tensions, generating a spatial metabolic gradient. In the severely hypoxic core, HIF-1α stabilization drives a glycolytic switch in microglia/macrophages, sustaining pro-inflammatory activation while suppressing oxidative phosphorylation [85]. This metabolic adaptation is reversible: targeting HIF-1α or its downstream effector PKM2 with small molecules (TEPP-46) has been shown to inhibit glycolysis, promote M2 polarization, and reduce infarct volume in experimental stroke [85]. At the subcellular level, mitochondrial dynamics, including fission, fusion, and mitophagy, critically determine macrophage functional outcomes. Ischemic stroke induces mitophagy-related gene expression changes in microglial subpopulations, with single-cell analysis revealing that ATP5J expression dynamically links mitochondrial dysfunction to disease-associated microglial states [86]. Similarly, SPTLC2 has been identified as a key driver of microglial activation and energy metabolism reprogramming after ischemic stroke, highlighting the interplay between mitochondrial impairment and inflammatory polarization [87]. These findings collectively suggest that restoring mitochondrial homeostasis via enhancing mitophagy or modulating mitochondrial biogenesis pathways represents a promising therapeutic avenue for reprogramming post-stroke macrophages.

Nano drug delivery systems: an innovative strategy for precise modulation of macrophages’ polarization

Nanocarriers (e.g., liposomes, exosomes, biomimetic nanocarriers, and polymeric nanoparticles) demonstrate significant potential as the drug delivery systems (DDS) for treating brain injury in stroke (Table 2). Through various modifications, nanoparticles can enhance drug concentration at lesion sites and even achieve cell-targeted delivery for precise treatment [88]. Consequently, leveraging their tunability and targeting specificity, nano-DDS have emerged as an innovative strategy for modulating polarization of macrophages after stroke.

Liposomal DDS: modulating macrophages’ activation and polarization

As a first-generation nanocarrier, liposomes possess a phospholipid bilayer structure conferring exceptional biocompatibility and low immunogenicity, which establishes them as a pivotal drug delivery vehicle for targeting the CNS after stroke [89]. Clodronate-liposomes complex serves as a highly effective tool for depleting peripheral macrophages. Furthermore, some studies demonstrated that local intrastriatal injection achieved microglial elimination within 3 days, thereby blocking inflammation after stroke [90–92]. In addition, liposomal DDS prolongs the circulation time of encapsulated drugs and increases the distribution within the brain. Wang H et al. found that 9-aminoacridine-loaded liposomes suppressed post-ischemic microglial activation via the IL-10/SOCS3 signaling pathway, which depended on enhancing the bioavailability of 9-aminoacridine [93]. It significantly reduced ischemic brain injury and promoted long-term neurological recovery.

Because of being fabricated by coating liposomal surfaces with components of macrophage membrane, macrophage membrane modified liposomes acquire advantages including enhanced penetration for BBB, targeted delivery to ischemic lesions, evasion of the uptake by mononuclear phagocyte system, and improved bioavailability [94, 95]. These properties significantly potentiate the therapeutic efficacy of TCM in IS. For instance, macrophage membrane modified liposomes loaded with baicalin enhance the therapeutic effect of this herbal component [95]. This “drug-loaded liposome + membrane modification” strategy effectively addresses the challenge of poor brain targeting inherent in conventional drugs. Baicalin‑loaded macrophage‑mimetic liposomes interfere with the MCP‑1/CCR2 chemokine axis, thereby reducing the recruitment and glycolytic activation of pro‑inflammatory CCR2⁺ monocyte‑derived macrophages.

What’s more, Liposome modification with targeting ligands further enables precise recognition to macrophage. Because phosphatidylserine possesses inherent targeting capability towards microglia/macrophages. Han R et al. successfully constructed an IL-10-conjugated phosphatidylserine modified liposome (PSL-IL-10) system [96]. This system not only specifically targets microglia, suppressing their activation, and downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, but also upregulates expression of CD36 via the IL-10/STAT3 signaling pathway, enhancing macrophage phagocytic function and accelerating clearance of hematoma. Thus, it targets the CD36⁺ phagocytic subset and its Nrf2‑dependent oxidative metabolism.

Exosome-RNA delivery systems: modulating phenotypic transformation of macrophage

Exosomes, lipid bilayer vesicles 30–100 nm in diameter, can carry molecules such as RNA and proteins to participate in pathological regulation after stroke. Specifically, RNA modified exosomes represent a novel strategy for intervening in neuroinflammation by precisely modulating the M1/M2 phenotype switch in macrophage [97]. Exosomes produced by adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) can improve the microenvironment and exert neuroprotective effects in stroke. Furthermore, ADSC-derived exosomes can precisely regulate the chemotaxis, metabolism, and polarization signaling pathways of macrophages, leading to enhanced therapeutic efficacy by carrying various non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) including circRNAs and miRNAs [98, 99]. A Study demonstrated that ADSC-derived exosomes loaded with circ-Rps5 can directly target microglia [98]. They promoted a phenotypic shift toward M2 macrophage reduced neuronal damage by regulating the genes’ expression of M1/M2. In addition, ADSC‑derived exosomes carrying circ‑Ptpn4 or miR‑30d‑5p promote the transition of pro‑inflammatory macrophages toward a reparative phenotype by upregulating Nrf2‑driven antioxidant metabolism and suppressing hypoxia‑induced glycolytic/autophagic programs [99, 100]. Thus, ADSC-derived exosomes carrying non-coding RNAs are crucial for anti-inflammatory therapy in stroke, as they remodel macrophage metabolism and function through transcriptional regulation.

Differences in RNA cargo across exosomes from distinct cells confer context-dependent effects on inflammatory regulation after stroke. Brain microvascular endothelial cell (BMEC)-derived exosomes loaded with miR-3613-3p promoted the polarization of M1 microglial by inhibiting RC3H1, exacerbating neuroinflammation [101]. Conversely, in type 2 diabetic stroke models, the exosomes carrying miR-126 induced the differentiation of M2 macrophage and promoted neural repair [102]. These indicated the cellular microenvironment influenced the regulatory direction of BMEC-derived exosomes. Furthermore, exosomes from macrophages stimulated by lipopolysaccharide directly skew microglia toward the M2 phenotype. When loaded with edaravone, these exosomes targeted ischemic regions, prolonged drug half-life, and increased bioavailability, achieving a synergistic “RNA regulation + drug delivery” effect [103, 104]. This reduces ROS production, thereby blunting the glycolytic drive of pro‑inflammatory microglia and facilitating their shift toward an OXPHOS‑dependent, reparative state.

In addition, exosomes engineered to overexpress CCR2 enable dual regulation of chemotaxis and polarization in macrophage. Research revealed that CCR2-overexpressing exosomes derived from human mesenchymal stem cells suppressed expression of MCP-1, reducing brain infiltration of blood-derived macrophages while simultaneously promoting polarization of M2 microglial [105]. This resulted in significantly greater improvement in cognitive function compared to plain exosomes after stroke. These CCR2‑overexpressing exosomes directly interfere with the MCP‑1/CCR2 chemokine axis, decreasing the recruitment of CCR2⁺ glycolytic macrophages and favoring the metabolic reprogramming of resident microglia toward an anti‑inflammatory phenotype.

High-targeting polymer nanoparticles conjugated with drug

Polymeric nanoparticles (PNPs), featuring customizable surface ligand modifications and optimized size distribution, achieve targeted delivery to the CNS through precise interactions with the blood-brain barrier (BBB), macrophage-specific receptors, or microenvironmental markers at lesion sites in stroke [106]. Their application in phenotypic modulation of macrophage offers an innovative approach for inflammatory intervention in IS.

On the one hand, addressing the pathological feature of burst release of reactive oxygen species (ROS) after stroke, ROS-responsive PNPs achieve lesion-specific accumulation via the change of microenvironment. ROS‑responsive PNPs, including 18β-glycyrrhetinic acid (GA)-conjugated diethylaminoethyl-dextran and quercetin‑modified nanoparticle system, attenuate the pro‑inflammatory glycolytic program of microglia by suppressing HMGB1 nuclear translocation and reducing oxidative stress, thereby promoting a metabolic switch toward OXPHOS‑dependent M2 polarization [107, 108]. These systems enable precise intervention in the inflammatory microenvironment via a cascade of “ROS sensing, controlled drug release, and phenotype remodeling”.

Furthermore, macrophage membrane-camouflaged phosphorus dendrimer/fibronectin nanocomplexes exploit membrane protein homotypic targeting to traverse the BBB. These complexes loaded with edaravone reduces ROS levels by inhibiting oxidase activity of NADPH and promotes polarization of M2 macrophage, exerting enhanced neuroprotective effects [109]. This edaravone‑loaded biomimetic system targets NADPH oxidase activity, thereby reducing ROS‑driven glycolytic activation in pro‑inflammatory macrophages and facilitating their repolarization to a repair‑associated metabolic state. Notably, unlike generalized antioxidant supplementation, this nanocarrier‑based approach achieves macrophage‑subset‑ specific metabolic modulation, which is a prerequisite for clinical translation in stroke.

On the other hand, Surface-engineered PNPs achieve precise macrophage targeting via specific recognition of surface markers. Davis et al. demonstrated that a nanodelivery system, modified by CD11b antibody, loaded with leukemia inhibitory factor prolonged its half-life and enhanced suppression of proliferation in M1 macrophage by targeting CD11b+ macrophages in peripheral blood and spleen [110]. This “antibody targeting, cellular internalization, and functional modulation” strategy mitigates systemic immunosuppression risks associated with conventional drug administration, potentially enhancing the therapeutic potential for localized suppression of neuroinflammation after stroke.

An integrated framework linking macrophage subsets, metabolic programs, and nano-interventions

scRNA-seq has unveiled the striking heterogeneity of macrophage subpopulations in stroke. This heterogeneity, in turn, underpins their divergent metabolic reprogramming and polarized functional states, ranging from pro‑inflammatory to reparative phenotypes. The recognition that specific metabolic pathways drive distinct polarization outcomes has naturally prompted efforts to target these pathways therapeutically. Among the available intervention tools, nano‑delivery systems offer unique advantages, including cell‑subset targeting, microenvironmental responsiveness, and the ability to cross the blood‑brain barrier. Therefore, an integrated framework that explicitly connects a defined macrophage subset, its dominant metabolic pathway, a druggable molecular node, and a tailored nano‑strategy is essential to accelerate clinical translation. Although few studies to date have rigorously followed this entire chain, the literature summarized above allows us to outline several proof‑of‑concept frameworks, as illustrated below (Fig. 4).

LCP1⁺ glycolipid‑coupled subset in ischemic stroke

scRNA‑seq has identified a macrophage subset characterized by high expression of LCP1, which exhibits concurrent enrichment of fatty acid and glycolytic metabolic pathways alongside enhanced oxidative phosphorylation [81]. This “glycolipid‑coupled” metabolic signature sustains a pro‑inflammatory phenotype, and LCP1 knockdown significantly attenuates ischemic brain injury. Key glycolytic enzymes, Hexokinase 2 (HK2) and Pyruvate Kinase M2 (PKM2), are essential for maintaining this metabolic state [11, 75]. ROS‑responsive polymeric nanoparticles, such as those conjugated with 18β‑glycyrrhetinic acid or quercetin, have been shown to accumulate in ischemic lesions and modulate microglial polarization [107]. These platforms could be rationally designed to deliver HK2 or PKM2 inhibitors specifically to LCP1⁺ macrophages, thereby disrupting their pro‑inflammatory metabolic program.

CCR2⁺ monocyte‑derived macrophages in ischemic stroke

Rapid recruitment of CCR2⁺ monocyte‑derived macrophages via the MCP‑1/CCR2 axis dominates early neuroinflammation after ischemic stroke [41–43, 46]. Blocking this axis reduces brain infiltration and infarct volume. The exosomes overexpressing CCR2 derived from mesenchymal stem cells have been shown to suppress MCP‑1 expression, reduce macrophage infiltration, and promote M2 polarization [105]. Alternatively, antibody‑modified nanoparticles such as CD11b‑targeted formulations provide a blueprint for cell‑subset‑specific delivery [110]. Similar strategies could be adapted using anti‑CCR2 antibodies to achieve precision targeting of this pathogenic subset.

CD36⁺ phagocytic subset in hemorrhagic stroke

In the acute phase of intracerebral hemorrhage, CD36 on macrophages mediates hematoma clearance via the Nrf2 signaling axis [60, 61]. Enhancing this pathway promotes resolution of hematoma and improves neurological outcomes. Phosphatidylserine‑modified liposomes loaded with IL‑10 (PSL‑IL‑10) have been engineered to target microglia/macrophages, and they upregulate CD36 expression through the IL‑10/STAT3 pathway, thereby augmenting phagocytic function [96]. This strategy directly links the CD36⁺ phagocytic subset, its Nrf2‑dependent metabolic/phagocytic machinery, and a clinically testable nano‑intervention.

These examples demonstrate that scRNA‑seq‑derived atlases serve as a molecular map to identify metabolically distinct macrophage subsets and their actionable nodes, while nano‑engineering provides the precision‑guided munition to modulate these nodes in a spatiotemporally controlled manner. The convergence of these three domains including single‑cell genomics, immunometabolism, and nanotechnology thus offers a coherent and actionable framework for next‑generation stroke immunotherapy.

Clinically actionable targets and translational landscape

The integration of scRNA‑seq and immunometabolism has illuminated several macrophage‑associated targets that are particularly amenable to clinical translation. Based on the evidence reviewed above, we propose a priority ranking of these targets according to their preclinical validation, druggability, and proximity to clinical application (Table 3). Among these, CCR2 and PPARγ stand out as the most clinically actionable due to the availability of existing drugs with favorable safety profiles that could be repurposed for stroke. However, direct evidence from stroke‑specific trials remains limited. Notably, a Phase I/II clinical trial has evaluated the safety of intrathecal autologous M2 macrophage transplantation in nonacute stroke patients, reporting no serious adverse events and significant neurological improvement compared to matched controls [111]. Although this cell‑based approach differs from pharmacological or nanomedicine strategies, it validates the therapeutic principle of leveraging macrophage reparative functions. Importantly, among the interventions discussed in this review, direct targeting of the CCR2/CCL2 axis, PPARγ activation, Nrf2 regulation, and nanocarrier‑based delivery platforms (Table 3; Fig. 4) are considered high‑priority near‑term translational strategies due to their strong preclinical validation and druggability. In contrast, approaches such as probiotic modulation of CCR2, generalized antioxidant supplementation without targeted delivery, and multi‑target TCM formulations remain mechanistically informative. But they are currently exploratory, lacking subset‑specific validation for stroke clinical trials.

Clinical translation challenges of nano‑therapeutics for stroke

Despite the impressive preclinical efficacy of nano‑delivery systems summarized above, their translation into clinical stroke therapy faces formidable obstacles. Firstly, the BBB remains a major bottleneck. Although various targeting strategies have been developed, even the most advanced nanoparticles achieve only a small fraction of the injected dose in the brain parenchyma, with > 90% of small‑molecule drugs and virtually all large biomacromolecules blocked by the BBB [112]. Secondly, rapid clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), particularly in the liver and spleen, severely limits nanoparticle circulation half‑life and bioavailability. This issue is compounded by the difficulty of achieving controlled and sustained drug release at the lesion site, especially given the narrow therapeutic window of stroke intervention [113]. Thirdly, a striking gap exists between the abundance of preclinical studies and the paucity of clinical trials. In the last five years, large number of articles have described preclinical nanomedicine‑based approaches for acute ischemic stroke, yet only a handful of clinical trials have involved nanoparticles, with mixed outcomes [114]. Key translational barriers include insufficient characterization of nanomaterial storage stability, biodistribution, organ toxicity, and pharmacokinetics; wide variability in in vivo models and outcome measures across studies, making cross‑comparison difficult; and the predominant focus on ischemia‑reperfusion models rather than clinically relevant thrombolysis‑adjunct scenarios [114]. Moreover, the FDA/EMA has approved over 50 nanomedicines for various diseases, but only one (NanoTherm® for brain tumors) has received approval for a CNS indication, underscoring the unique challenges posed by the brain [112, 115]. Addressing these obstacles will require not only improved nano‑engineering, such as MPS‑evading surface modifications and more robust BBB penetration strategies, but also standardized preclinical protocols and a clearer focus on clinically actionable endpoints.

Conclusions and perspectives

In summary, the dynamic regulatory mechanisms of macrophages, encompassing cellular infiltration, phenotype polarization, inflammatory responses, and tissue repair, are integral to the entire pathological cascade following stroke. scRNA-seq technology serves as a pivotal tool for deciphering and refining the immune landscape of macrophage heterogeneity in stroke. Metabolic reprogramming-immune crosstalk reveals that glycolysis sustains the M1-skewed phenotype, while oxidative phosphorylation and cholesterol metabolism underpin M2-mediated repair functions. Amino acid metabolism represents a promising novel therapeutic target. Strategies such as biomimetic liposomes for efficient penetration for BBB, exosomes loaded with non-coding RNAs to modulate macrophage polarization, and ROS-responsive PNPs for lesion-specific drug delivery constitute innovative translational approaches for stroke therapy targeting macrophages.

However, key aspects of macrophage regulation in stroke demand further investigation. (1) Spatiotemporal scRNA-seq atlases require integration with epigenomic and proteomic data to identify driver genes governing macrophage subset transitions. (2) The mechanisms by which metabolic reprogramming regulates epigenetic modifications via protein post-translational modifications need elucidation. (3) There still exists a striking paucity of research and discussion on metabolic-immune regulatory mechanisms in macrophages within HS, representing a critical area for future exploration. (4) Hurdles remain in translating nanomaterial-driven stroke therapies, including controlling the in vivo pharmacokinetics and immunogenicity of nanocarriers, and balancing pro-reparative macrophage targeting with the risk of promoting fibrotic scar formation. Among these future directions, the most urgent translational priority is to establish clinical proof-of-concept for high-confidence strategies, including CCR2/CCL2 axis modulation, PPARγ activation, Nrf2 regulation, and nanocarrier-based subset-specific delivery. Exploratory concepts such as probiotics, generalized antioxidants, or TCM formulations, while mechanistically interesting, should be prioritized only after more rigorous validation in stroke-relevant models.
