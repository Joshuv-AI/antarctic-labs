Study of mothers’ perceptions of breastfeeding support in Lithuania: is Baby-Friendly Hospital status associated with better breastfeeding support?
Authors: Snieguole Kaseliene, Skirmante Sauliune, Olga Mesceriakova, Vilma Jasiukaitiene, Greta Jonkiene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreastfeeding support provided by healthcare professionals plays an important role in successful breastfeeding initiation and continuation. Previous studies have shown that breastfeeding-supportive maternity care and implementation of the Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative (BFHI) are associated with improved breastfeeding outcomes and more positive maternal experiences [1, 2]. However, evidence regarding mothers’ perceptions of breastfeeding support in Lithuania remains limited, and little is known about potential differences between Baby-Friendly and non-Baby-Friendly hospitals. The aim of this study was to evaluate maternal perceptions of breastfeeding support provided in Lithuanian maternity hospitals and to compare breastfeeding support between Baby-Friendly and non-Baby-Friendly hospitals. In addition, a secondary aim was to explore the internal structure of the newly developed questionnaire using exploratory factor analysis to facilitate interpretation of breastfeeding support domains.MethodsThis cross-sectional comparative observational study was conducted in June 2022. Women (2–6 months postpartum) who had given birth to a healthy full-term infant in Lithuania completed an anonymous online questionnaire distributed via national social media parenting groups. Differences in breastfeeding support between Baby-Friendly and non–Baby-Friendly hospitals were analysed using descriptive statistics and appropriate statistical tests (Mann-Whitney U and χ² tests). Exploratory factor analysis was performed to examine the internal structure of the breastfeeding support questionnaire.ResultsA total of 572 women aged 18–45 years participated in the study; 65.7% delivered in a Baby-Friendly Hospital (BFH), and 76.2% were still breastfeeding at the time of the survey. Overall, breastfeeding support was evaluated as moderate, with a median total score of 65.0 out of 110 points (IQR 49–81). The highest-rated services were those provided by healthcare professionals (HCPs) during the first hours after childbirth (mean score 3.76 (SD 1.1) out of 5), while the lowest-rated were services related to HCPs’ support for continued breastfeeding after discharge (mean score 2.40 (SD 1.0) out of 5). Most women reported that they had been given the opportunity for early breastfeeding and skin-to-skin contact; however, upon discharge, they received insufficient information from HCPs regarding peer support groups for breastfeeding mothers, the risks associated with the use of feeding bottles, pacifiers, and nipple shields, and the involvement of other family members in supporting breastfeeding. The evaluation of breastfeeding support and breastfeeding success were associated with hospital status – women who delivered in BFH rated the services more positively and were more likely to report continued breastfeeding compared to those who delivered in non-BFH (p < 0.05).ConclusionsBreastfeeding support in Lithuania is rated at a moderate level – women expressed satisfaction with early professional support following childbirth; however, they reported a lack of information and continuity of support to ensure successful breastfeeding at home. The findings indicate that delivery in BFH is associated with higher service ratings and greater breastfeeding success. These results highlight the need to strengthen the role of HCPs in providing ongoing breastfeeding support and to encourage hospitals to implement baby-friendly practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13006-026-00868-z.

Background

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), only approximately 44% of infants worldwide are exclusively breastfed for the first six months of life [3], while in Lithuania in 2023 this figure reached 53% [4]. Breastfeeding success is often determined by factors such as early initiation of breastfeeding, skin-to-skin contact [5, 6] – placing the newborn on the breast and breastfeeding within the first hour of life [7]; staying with the baby in the same room [8], breastfeeding on demand [9], a breastfeeding-friendly environment [10], and the provision of adequate information before mother and newborn are discharged from the hospital [11]. Individualized and tailored assistance provided to mothers of healthy newborns prolongs both the duration of breastfeeding and exclusive breastfeeding, while also reducing maternal breastfeeding-related challenges [12]. Several studies have shown that the implementation of the Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative (BFHI) plays an important role in promoting exclusive breastfeeding and increasing mothers’ satisfaction with the care they receive. From the mother’s perspective, hospitals designated as BFHI provide more effective breastfeeding support [13, 14]. Women who are satisfied with the postnatal care demonstrate higher parental self-efficacy, initiate breastfeeding earlier, breastfeed more effectively, experience fewer maternal and infant health problems, and consequently have lower rates of rehospitalization [15–17].

To ensure provision of optimal conditions for the initiation and continuation of breastfeeding in the maternity institutions, the WHO and the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) launched the BFHI in 1991. The core goals of the BFHI are to ensure that mothers are able to make informed decisions regarding infant feeding, to support early breastfeeding initiation, to encourage exclusive breastfeeding during the first six months of life, to discontinue the provision of free or low-cost breast milk substitutes to hospitals, and, when appropriate, to incorporate broader maternal and infant health care considerations [18]. Evidence from scientific literature suggests that the BFHI can improve breastfeeding outcomes, including the initiation of breastfeeding, and extend the duration of breastfeeding for up to one year after birth [19, 20]. As the Ten Steps to Successful Breastfeeding currently serve as a standard of care for optimizing breastfeeding initiation and support, they may also be used as a basis for evaluating the quality of breastfeeding support.

Women’s perceptions of breastfeeding support represent an important indicator of maternity care quality. Perceived support influences maternal confidence, breastfeeding self-efficacy, and continuation of breastfeeding. Therefore, evaluating maternal perceptions provides insights not only into clinical practice but also into the effectiveness of breastfeeding promotion policies [1, 13].

According to the latest available data from 2019, in Lithuania, the BFHI has been implemented in 17 hospitals [21]; however, although about half of mothers intend to breastfeed for one year, a large proportion still encounter challenges, as more than 41% discontinue breastfeeding before 6 months and 57.8% before 1 year of age [22]. Many mothers lack the necessary skills and information related to lactation, including proper positioning and attachment, as well as the ability to assess whether the infant is feeding effectively [22, 23].

The aim of this study was to evaluate maternal perceptions of breastfeeding support provided in Lithuanian maternity hospitals and to compare breastfeeding support between Baby-Friendly and non-Baby-Friendly hospitals. In addition, a secondary aim was to explore the internal structure of the newly developed breastfeeding support questionnaire using exploratory factor analysis to facilitate interpretation of breastfeeding support domains. The psychometric analyses were performed to support interpretation of the study findings rather than to formally validate the instrument. This study seeks to identify existing service gaps and propose potential improvements that could contribute to better breastfeeding practices and enhanced maternal support nationwide. It is expected that the findings will support the development of more effective health policies and improvements in clinical practice, ensuring optimal conditions for mothers and their infants.

Methods

Study design and setting of the study

This study was conducted as a cross-sectional comparative observational study. In June 2022, women who had given birth to a healthy, full-term newborn were surveyed using an anonymous online questionnaire distributed through social media groups. The questionnaire link was shared in large Lithuanian parenting groups on social media platforms, including groups organized according to the birth month of infants, which are typically used by mothers raising infants of similar age.

In Lithuania, postpartum hospital stay typically lasts 2–3 days after vaginal birth and 3–5 days after caesarean section. The BFHI was institutionalized nationally in 2014, when 17 maternity hospitals were recognized as Baby-Friendly [21]. As accreditation status may change over time, hospital classification in this study was based on official records valid at the time of each participant’s childbirth.

Convenience sampling was applied. During the recruitment period, approximately 1,800 women were exposed to the survey invitation through the social media groups. Of the 700 women who initiated the questionnaire, 572 completed it fully and met all inclusion criteria; these responses were included in the final analysis. Incomplete questionnaires or responses not meeting these eligibility criteria were excluded from the analysis. The response rate among participants who started the questionnaire was 81.7%, while the overall participation rate relative to the estimated number of women exposed to the invitation was 38.9%.

According to data from the Lithuanian Institute of Hygiene, 23,033 women gave birth in Lithuania in 2020. The required sample size was calculated using the OpenEpi sample size calculator, assuming a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error. Based on these parameters, a minimum sample of 378 women was required. The final analytic sample of 572 participants exceeded this threshold, ensuring adequate statistical power.

Research instrument

Based on the Ten Steps to Successful Breastfeeding recommendations [18], the authors developed a questionnaire specifically for the purposes of this study to assess breastfeeding support in maternity hospitals. The questionnaire consisted of: (1) 22 statements describing postnatal care of the mother and newborn, maternal education on breastfeeding and newborn care, the provision of breastfeeding-related information by HCPs, and the recommendations given at the time of hospital discharge for the mother and infant; and (2) general questions on the respondents’ sociodemographic characteristics, number of pregnancies, current breastfeeding status (exclusive or partial), antenatal preparation for breastfeeding (including participation in breastfeeding classes during pregnancy, acquisition of practical breastfeeding knowledge, previous breastfeeding experience, and intention to breastfeed), and the status of the hospital where the respondent gave birth (Baby-Friendly or non-Baby-Friendly). The statements in the first part of the questionnaire were rated using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

To compare the total scores of factors consisting of a different number of items, the mean total scores were calculated. The total possible score on the breastfeeding support questionnaire ranged from 22 to 110 points, with higher scores indicating more positive perceptions of breastfeeding support provided in maternity hospitals.

Statistical data analysis

Data were analysed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (IBM SPSS Statistics) version 29.0. The distribution of nominal and ordinal variables was expressed as percentages. For descriptive presentation in tables, Likert scale responses were dichotomized into two categories: “agree” (agree/strongly agree) and “disagree” (disagree/strongly disagree). Neutral responses were not included in percentage calculations. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test; distributions were considered normal when p > 0.05. For normally distributed continuous variables, the mean and standard deviation (SD) were reported, whereas for variables that did not meet the assumption of normality, the minimum and maximum values, median, and interquartile range (IQR; the difference between the 25th and 75th percentiles) were presented. To determine which breastfeeding-related service support factors were rated most favourably, mean factor scores were calculated (the total score of the factor divided by the number of items comprising the factor).

Differences between two independent groups were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test and the χ² test, while associations between two related groups of mean factor scores were evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for related samples. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

An exploratory factor analysis was used to examine the internal structure of the breastfeeding support questionnaire and to support the interpretation of composite scores. As no predefined questionnaire dimensions were established during instrument development, exploratory factor analysis was performed to identify underlying dimensions within the questionnaire items. Principal Component Analysis with Varimax rotation was performed. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity were used to assess the appropriateness of the sample for the factor analysis. Eigenvalues above one and the scree plot were used to determine the number of factors. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated to assess the internal consistency of the first part of the questionnaire.

Results

Characteristics of the study participants

The distribution of respondents according to sociodemographic characteristics, pregnancy, and childbirth experience is presented in Table 1. The study sample comprised 572 women aged 18–45 years, with a mean age of 29.5 years (SD 4.7) and a median age of 30 years. The largest proportion of respondents (314, 54.9%) were aged 25–31 years. Half of the respondents (288, 50.3%) had a university-level higher education, and more than half (332, 58.0%) resided in large cities. 438 (76.6%) of respondents were married, while 128 (22.4%) were cohabiting with a partner. Most of participants were employed prior to pregnancy and maternity leave (469, 82.0%), and most (484, 84.6%) reported that their monthly income was sufficient to cover living expenses. For nearly two thirds of the respondents (359, 62.8%), this was their first pregnancy, and 65.7% (n = 375) gave birth in a BFH. The majority (432, 75.5%) of women reported that they were still breastfeeding (exclusive or partial) their infant at the time of the survey.

Overall evaluation of breastfeeding support in maternity hospitals

Overall, breastfeeding support was evaluated as moderate. The median total score was 65.0 (IQR 49–81) out of a possible 110 points. Support provided during the first hours after childbirth received the highest ratings, whereas support ensuring continuity of breastfeeding after hospital discharge received the lowest ratings.

Breastfeeding support across the identified support factors

Three factors representing different aspects of breastfeeding support were identified through exploratory factor analysis and used to facilitate interpretation of mothers’ evaluations.

The first factor (F1), entitled “HCPs support to the mother after birth”, consisted of 12 items describing the type of breastfeeding support that a mother may require from HCPs immediately after childbirth. The possible total score for this factor ranged from 12 to 60 points. Respondents scored between 12 and 60 points, with a median of 37.0 (IQR 27–47).

In addition, respondents’ evaluations of each individual item within the factor were analysed separately. Table 2 presents the proportions (%) of respondents who agreed or strongly agreed (agree) and those who disagreed or strongly disagreed (disagree) with each statement. The highest levels of agreement within F1 dimension were observed for the statements: “I was encouraged to breastfeed my baby as often and for as long as he/she wanted, both during the day and at night” (63.8%) and “I received breastfeeding-related advice from HCPs when I requested it” (53.5%). In contrast, the lowest levels of agreement were reported for the statements: “I was informed how to manage sore or damaged nipples” (31.1%) and “I was informed about how to manage breastfeeding problems (e.g., mastitis, milk stasis)” (22.6%).

The second factor (F2), entitled “HCPs support for successful future breastfeeding”, consisted of 11 items describing the type of breastfeeding support that mothers may require from HCPs to ensure successful breastfeeding in the future. The total possible score for this factor ranged from 11 to 55 points. In the study sample, participants scored between 11 and 55 points, with a median of 25.0 (IQR 17–34).

Within this factor, disagreement exceeded agreement for all statements. The highest levels of disagreement were reported for the following statements: “Before discharge from the hospital, I was informed about peer support groups for breastfeeding mothers” (71.9%), “I was informed about the risks associated with the use of feeding bottles, pacifiers, and nipple shields” (69.6%), and “HCPs encouraged my partner/husband and family members to become involved in supporting breastfeeding” (65.4%). In contrast, the statement “HCPs encouraged me to ask questions about breastfeeding, lactation, and newborn care” received the lowest level of disagreement (41.8%); however, even for this statement, the proportion of respondents who disagreed remained higher than the proportion who agreed (36.3%).

The third factor (F3), entitled “HCPs support during the first hours after birth”, comprised four items describing maternal and newborn care during the first hours after delivery. The possible total score for this factor ranged from 4 to 20 points. Study participants scored between 4 and 20 points, with a median of 17.0 (IQR 14–19).

The highest proportion of respondents agreed with the statement “I was able to stay with my newborn 24 hours a day” (89.7%). Although the statement “HCPs paid special attention to breastfeeding during the first hours after birth” received the lowest level of agreement among the four items (39.2%), this still represented more than one third of all respondents.

The highest mean score was obtained for factor related to support during the first hours after childbirth (F3) (mean 3.76, SD 1.1), while the lowest mean score was found for the factor related to HCPs support for successful future breastfeeding (F2), with a mean score of 2.40 (SD 1.0) (Fig. 1). Evaluation of breastfeeding support according to hospital status (Baby-Friendly vs. non–Baby-Friendly)

The evaluation of breastfeeding support was significantly associated with hospital status (p < 0.001; Table 3). Women who delivered in BFH rated the services more favourably than women who delivered in non–Baby-Friendly hospitals. All services assigned to F1 (HCPs support to the mother after birth) and F2 (HCPs support for successful future breastfeeding) received significantly higher scores in BFH. A particularly pronounced difference was observed in the evaluation of the statement “Before discharge from the hospital, I received information on where to seek consultation on breastfeeding and lactation after returning home”, which was rated two points higher in BFH compared with non– BFH (median 3 vs. 1, respectively). However, although the overall composite score for F3 (HCPs support during the first hours after birth) was higher among women who delivered in BFH, a statistically significant difference at the item level was observed only for the statement “HCPs paid special attention to breastfeeding during the first hours after childbirth.”. No statistically significant differences were observed for the remaining items in this factor.

Additional analyses showed that higher breastfeeding support scores were reported by multiparous women, women with previous breastfeeding experience, and women who had decided during pregnancy to breastfeed their infant. Psychometric properties of the breastfeeding support questionnaire

The internal consistency of the breastfeeding support questionnaire was high (Cronbach’s α = 0.942). The data were suitable for factor analysis: KMO = 0.938, Bartlett’s test of sphericity p < 0.001. As no predefined questionnaire dimensions were established during instrument development, exploratory factor analysis was performed to identify underlying dimensions within the questionnaire items. Three factors explaining 63.05% of the total variance were identified, with factor loadings ranging from 0.428 to 0.859.

Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the identified dimensions were 0.946 for F1 (HCPs support to the mother after birth), 0.921 for F2 (HCPs support for successful future breastfeeding), and 0.759 for F3 (HCPs support during the first hours after birth).

Discussion

This study examined maternal perceptions of breastfeeding support in Lithuanian maternity hospitals and compared evaluations between Baby-Friendly and non-Baby-Friendly institutions. Three principal findings emerged: (1) overall breastfeeding support was perceived as moderate; (2) support during the first hours after birth was rated more positively than continuity of support after discharge; and (3) delivery in a Baby-Friendly Hospital was associated with more favourable perceptions of breastfeeding support and a higher likelihood of ongoing breastfeeding.

In the present study, mothers evaluated the overall quality of breastfeeding support as moderate, indicating considerable room for improvement. Similar associations between breastfeeding support and breastfeeding outcomes have been reported in previous studies. For example, Monroe et al. reported that creating a hospital environment supportive of breastfeeding could yield positive breastfeeding outcomes for both women and newborns, enabling women to achieve their antenatal breastfeeding goals and increasing the likelihood of exclusive breastfeeding among newborns [24]. Similar results were reported by Beauregard et al., who found that women who received breastfeeding-supportive healthcare services after childbirth were more likely to achieve their prenatal breastfeeding goals [2].

The findings of this study also indicate that breastfeeding support provided immediately after childbirth was evaluated most favourably. In particular, most mothers reported early initiation of breastfeeding, skin-to-skin contact, and continuous rooming-in with their newborn. These practices correspond to the recommendations of the WHO and UNICEF Ten Steps to Successful Breastfeeding, which emphasize early initiation, skin-to-skin contact, and uninterrupted mother–infant contact as key elements of effective breastfeeding support [18]. In the study conducted by Jaafar et al., rooming-in was identified as one of the key determinants of successful breastfeeding [8]. Numerous studies have confirmed the importance of skin-to-skin contact as a determinant of successful early initiation and continuation of breastfeeding [5, 6, 25, 26]. In a 2015 Lithuanian study on factors influencing maternal decisions to breastfeed, newborns who were placed on the mother’s chest immediately after birth and breastfed during the first day of life were significantly more likely to be breastfed [27]. These findings indicate that early postpartum support and assistance in Lithuania are provided relatively effectively, which is critical, as the first hours after birth are vital for both maternal and newborn health, as well as for breastfeeding success and duration.

At the same time, the present study revealed weaknesses in breastfeeding support related to preparation for continued breastfeeding after hospital discharge. Participants reported insufficient information about peer support groups, breastfeeding problem management, and the risks associated with bottles and pacifiers. These findings highlight deficiencies in continuity of breastfeeding support after hospital discharge. The previously mentioned study by Penkauskaite demonstrated that newborns whose mothers received sufficient breastfeeding support and counselling from nurses were significantly more likely to be breastfed [27]. Other studies have similarly confirmed that counselling by HCPs during pregnancy and the breastfeeding period can effectively improve breastfeeding practices [17, 26, 28].

The Ten Steps also emphasize the importance of discussing breastfeeding management and its benefits with pregnant women and their families. Nevertheless, the present study revealed that HCPs insufficiently encourage the involvement of other family members in breastfeeding support. Previous research has consistently shown that family support plays a crucial role in breastfeeding success; supportive behaviour from the infant’s father and the grandmother’s prior breastfeeding experience are particularly influential [29, 30]. Well-informed and supportive family members can help mothers overcome breastfeeding-related difficulties and provide essential emotional support. Conversely, lack of information and support may substantially undermine mothers’ confidence and motivation to continue breastfeeding. Without adequate guidance and encouragement, mothers may feel insecure and doubtful of their ability to breastfeed. Therefore, continuous support – including education on the benefits of breastfeeding, practical guidance, and emotional support – is essential for strengthening maternal self-efficacy and sustaining breastfeeding.

Finally, the present study demonstrated that the BFHI, which aims to motivate facilities providing maternity and newborn services worldwide to implement the Ten Steps to Successful Breastfeeding, makes a significant contribution to improving the quality of healthcare services and promoting the continuity of breastfeeding. Women who gave birth in BFH evaluated breastfeeding-related healthcare services more positively and were more likely to report continued breastfeeding compared with those who delivered in hospitals without this designation. Similar findings have been reported in studies conducted in other countries [31–33]. The WHO guidelines for BFHI implementation highlight strategies aimed at proportionally expanding the global coverage of this initiative and ensuring its sustainability over time. These guidelines emphasize the full integration of the program into national healthcare systems to ensure that all facilities providing maternity care implement the Ten Steps to Successful Breastfeeding [18]. In Lithuania, these recommendations are implemented by the Breastfeeding Promotion Committee, established by an order of the Minister of Health of the Republic of Lithuania in 2014, whose objective is to increase the proportion of infants exclusively breastfed up to six months of age and to promote continued breastfeeding up to two years of age and beyond by ensuring timely and high-quality support [21, 34]. It is essential to ensure that all maternity care facilities nationwide actively implement BFH policies. Therefore, the consistent implementation of the Ten Steps to Successful Breastfeeding across all maternity care facilities remains a key factor in improving the quality of breastfeeding support and ensuring sustainable breastfeeding support in the postpartum period.

Additional analyses suggested that some maternal and obstetric characteristics, including parity, previous breastfeeding experience, intention to breastfeed during pregnancy, and mode of childbirth, were associated with mothers’ evaluations of breastfeeding support. Women with previous childbirth and breastfeeding experience generally reported more favourable evaluations of breastfeeding support, while vaginal birth was associated with higher ratings of support related to the first hours after childbirth and successful future breastfeeding. These findings are consistent with previous research showing that maternal experience and childbirth characteristics may influence breastfeeding experiences and perceptions of support [1, 13, 14, 22, 24].

In addition to the substantive findings, exploratory factor analysis helped identify three interpretable factors representing different aspects of breastfeeding support and facilitated interpretation of mothers’ evaluations. The questionnaire also demonstrated good internal consistency, suggesting that it may be useful for assessing maternal perceptions of breastfeeding support in future research and practice settings. Nevertheless, further studies are needed to evaluate the psychometric properties of the questionnaire in different populations.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first study conducted in Lithuania to comprehensively evaluate breastfeeding support and compare differences between BFH and non-Baby-Friendly institutions. A questionnaire based on the Ten Steps to Successful Breastfeeding was developed to assess mothers’ perceptions of these services. The findings of this study may contribute to improving the quality of breastfeeding support and to increasing both maternal motivation to breastfeed and the prevalence of exclusive breastfeeding in Lithuania.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. The study employed convenience sampling and online recruitment, which may limit representativeness and introduce selection bias. As the survey was conducted online and access was limited to users of social networking platforms, women who do not use social media were not included in the sample. However, according to data from the Official Statistics Portal, in 2022, 89.5% of individuals aged 25–34 years used social media; similarly, 88.3% of women aged 16–24 years and 85.1% of women aged 25–54 years were social media users in the same year [35]. As the majority of participants in this study were young (23–34 years), educated women who are interested in their own and their infant’s health and actively use social media, it can be assumed that the sample reasonably reflects the population of women who have given birth to a healthy, full-term newborn and are 2–6 months postpartum.

Another limitation relates to the timing of data collection. Participants were surveyed 2–6 months postpartum, which may introduce recall bias when reporting their perceptions of breastfeeding support received in maternity hospitals.

Although the questionnaire included several items related to antenatal preparation for breastfeeding, it did not comprehensively assess the quality or content of antenatal breastfeeding counselling and support received during pregnancy. Therefore, the potential influence of prenatal breastfeeding counselling on mothers’ perceptions of breastfeeding support could not be fully evaluated.

Importantly, due to the cross-sectional observational design of the study, causal inferences cannot be made. Although women who delivered in BFH reported more favourable evaluations of breastfeeding support and higher rates of ongoing breastfeeding, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects.

In addition, although selected maternal, obstetric, and breastfeeding-related characteristics were explored in additional analyses, adjusted multivariable analyses were not performed. Women who delivered in BFH were slightly younger than those who delivered in non-BFH, and some characteristics, including parity, previous breastfeeding experience, intention to breastfeed during pregnancy, and mode of childbirth, were associated with breastfeeding support scores. Therefore, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded.

Conclusions

Mothers in Lithuania evaluated breastfeeding support provided in maternity hospitals as moderate. Support during the first hours after childbirth was perceived more positively, whereas support related to continued breastfeeding after hospital discharge was evaluated less favourably. In particular, mothers reported insufficient information about breastfeeding support resources and limited involvement of family members in breastfeeding support. The findings indicate that delivery in BFH is associated with higher service ratings and greater breastfeeding success, although maternal and obstetric characteristics may also influence mothers’ perceptions of breastfeeding support. These results highlight the need to strengthen the role of HCPs in providing ongoing breastfeeding support and to encourage hospitals to implement baby-friendly practices.
