Management of Bouveret syndrome with lithotripsy and transgastric stone retrieval
Authors: Adedoyin Ojo, Cheryl Loh, Gerda Seidel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBouveret syndrome describes an ectopic migrating gallstone resulting in gastric outlet obstruction via a cholecystoduodenal/cholecystogastric fistula. Due to its rarity, no current guidelines or formal consensus exist regarding its management or follow-up. We present a rare case of Bouveret syndrome in a man in his mid 80s. Attempts to endoscopically remove the impacted stone that caused the gastric outlet obstruction failed. We subsequently treated him with lithotripsy and gastrotomy alone with no further interventions, and contribute this case to existing literature regarding this complication of gallstone disease.

Background

Bouveret syndrome is a rare complication, encompassing 1%–3% of gallstone disease,1 first described by French physician Léon Bouveret in 1896. It describes gastric outlet obstruction secondary to impacted ectopic gallstone(s) migrating through bilioenteric fistulation of which there are two major types: cholecystoduodenal and cholecystogastric. It presents typically in the elderly (median age: 74 years), females (female-to-male ratio 7:1) with a similar vignette to that of cholelithiasis.1 Manifestations of Bouveret syndrome are vague in history and physical examination, involving nausea and vomiting, abdominal pain/distension, weight loss, haematemesis and melaena.2 Other possible symptoms and signs include constipation, early satiety, dysphagia, diarrhoea, signs of dehydration, fever and jaundice.3

We present a case of Bouveret syndrome in a patient initially managed as cholecystitis with only later identification of gastric outlet obstruction due to a stone.

Case presentation

A man in his mid 80s presented with a 4-day history of post-prandial vomiting without associated abdominal pain, reflux, bowel or urinary symptoms. He had a background of several months of progressive dysphagia (predominantly with liquids), odynophagia and some weight loss. He was unable to comply with his medications for benign prostatic hyperplasia and chronic kidney disease as he was struggling with his mental health. He was allergic to penicillin, widowed and lived alone.

On initial clinical assessment, he had stable observations. He was afebrile, had a soft abdomen with present bowel sounds but vomited multiple times.

Investigations

On admission, he had a normal full blood count, C-reactive protein was 13 mg/L and he had a mildly raised total bilirubin (25 μmol/L). A CT abdomen/pelvis with intravenous iodine-based contrast reported a distended and thick-walled gallbladder fistulating to the adjacent gastric pylorus/gastroduodenal junction with large non-calcified gallstones within the gallbladder and distal stomach. It also reported no intrahepatic or extrahepatic biliary pathology, bowel dilatation or signs of mechanical obstruction, and this patient was treated for acute cholecystitis (figure 1).

Despite 2 days of antiemetics (metoclopramide and ondansetron), vomiting persisted prompting re-consideration. A repeat CT scan was performed and this showed collapse of the bowel immediately distal to the calculus, suggesting obstruction secondary to impaction (figure 2).

(a, b) Second CT scan showing distended stomach and collapse of the bowel immediately distal to the calculus suggesting obstruction secondary to impaction.

Of note, this patient also had a brief period of hypokalaemia of 2.9, likely secondary to persistent emesis.

He was reviewed by the mental health team as he had a low mood and suicidal ideation.

Treatment

Due to frailty and patient wishes, he was initially managed for acute cholecystitis with intravenous antibiotics (teicoplanin, metronidazole and gentamicin), a proton pump inhibitor and antiemetics. As symptoms persisted, a nasogastric tube was inserted draining 630 mL of bilious fluid and, at this point, the case was re-reviewed with concerns of gastric outlet obstruction. Repeat CT imaging highlighted the obstructing 35 mm gallstone in the duodenum (figure 2a and b). An American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program score adjusted for frailty and hypokalaemia predicted a 30% risk of a serious complication, 22% risk of death and 42% likelihood for discharge to rehabilitation/care centre post laparotomy. His National Emergency Laparotomy Audit (NELA) score for mortality was 5.78%.

Due to his high-risk status, an endoscopic attempt was made the following day to disimpact the gallstone which was unsuccessful (figure 3). The stone was too solid to be broken down endoscopically with a large grasping forceps and a Roth net could not get around it as it was impacted.

First endoscopic attempt which was unsuccessful because the stone was too solid to be broken down endoscopically with a large grasping forceps and a Roth net could not get around it as it was impacted.

After optimising his nutritional status and correcting electrolyte imbalance, he was taken to the operating theatre. A repeat endoscopic attempt was performed using an EHL lithotripter (Boston Scientific Bipolar Electrohydraulic Lithotripter) to break down the large gallstone impacted in D1 (first part of the duodenum). Multiple stones were visible within the fistula and D2 (second part of the duodenum) was not easily visualised. Due to the size and number of the remaining gallstones, there were concerns that further obstruction was very likely and retrieving these large fragmented stones endoscopically may fail. Thus, a decision was made to proceed to laparoscopy for trans-gastric removal of gallstones from the fistula and duodenum.

A gastrotomy was performed and the remaining gallstones were removed (figure 4). Additional oesophagogastroduodenoscopy (OGD) and cholecystoscopy (via the fistula) confirmed all stones were retrieved. Free flow of bile was seen to enter D2.

Extracted gallstones following mechanical lithotripsy and laparoscopic gastrotomy next to a laparoscopic 40 mm Johann forceps.

A two-layered gastroplasty was done with no leak evident on air leak test. Cholecystectomy and fistula repair were not performed.

Outcome and follow-up

Postoperatively, he was monitored in the intensive care unit for recovery and confirmation of resolution of obstruction. This was confirmed with water-soluble contrast study and eventual opening of his bowels on post-operative day 4. His post-operative recovery was delayed as he developed hospital-acquired pneumonia. He was deemed surgically fit for discharge on post-operative day 13 and discharged to a care home shortly after.

This patient remained asymptomatic at follow-up 10 months post surgical intervention.

Discussion

Abdominal ultrasound scan can be useful in identifying a gallstone but it is limited in Bouveret syndrome, making CT the imaging modality of choice with sensitivities between 79.1% and 93%, followed by MRI and OGD in cases of isoattenuating gallstones. Serial imaging is sometimes required to confirm mechanical obstruction as the pathology evolves.2 Diagnosis can be missed on OGD in 69% of cases where obstruction might be identified without visualisation of a deeply embedded obstructing stone.3 4 Most cases report visualising Rigler’s triad on CT: bowel obstruction, pneumobilia and ectopic gallstone ileus5 with other general findings involving duodenal dilatation, cholecystoduodenal fistula, dilated stomach and gastric outlet/pyloric obstruction.3

Due to the rarity of the condition, consensus among authors regarding approach to surgical management is heavily debated. Treatment is skewed in favour of an endoscopic approach due to consideration of the typical co-morbid status of the affected patient, despite a lower success rate of stone removal relative to surgical intervention. Minimally invasive techniques involve simple endoscopic stone extraction (though typically ineffective in stones measuring >2 cm),5 mechanical lithotripsy, electrohydraulic lithotripsy, laser lithotripsy, extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL) and duodenal stenting.6 In cases where an underlying malignancy is suspected, biopsy should be obtained during endoscopy, balancing the risk of perforation and the patient’s wishes.5 6 In a large systematic review of management of Bouveret syndrome, 61% of initial approaches by minimally invasive techniques failed, requiring subsequent surgical management by laparoscopic or open enterolithotomy/gastrolithotomy via gastrostomy, with more current literature estimating the requirement for surgery in 90% of cases. Factors associated with lower success rates with endoscopic therapy include increasing gallstone size ≥4 cm, impaction in the distal duodenum and mechanical retrieval alone without lithotripsy or ESWL. Even stones ≤4 cm can be difficult to retrieve if deeply impacted in the distal duodenum.4

Following stone removal, there is further debate for an interval intervention involving cholecystectomy with or without fistula repair either immediately (one-stage procedure) or electively (two-stage procedure) to prevent recurrence of Bouveret syndrome and general gallstone disease. The recurrence rate of Bouveret syndrome in patients without cholecystectomy and fistula repair has not been explicitly investigated but a two-stage approach may be required in treatment of Bouveret syndrome, in parallel with increasing contradictory evidence of spontaneous fistula closure after removal of the obstructing stone.4 7 Spontaneous closure of the fistula, however, has been debated in itself to promote a dysfunctional scleroatrophic gallbladder more prone to infection,5 though this has not been explicitly investigated either. It is generally accepted here to consider the patient’s own condition and wishes, weighing up the risks of recurrence versus the risk of associated mortality of further intervention, reported at 22.2% in immediate cholecystectomy and 16.9% in elective cases.4

In a review of patients with gallstone ileus, it has been reported that natural closure of the fistula occurs in 61.5% of patients with 85% recurrence in patients without. Though this was not a focused exploration into recurrence in cases of Bouveret syndrome, it may support a somewhat conservative approach for follow-up in patients without cholecystectomy.9 In patients with recurrence, endoscopy may be considered for further retrieval of impacted gallstones.1

Conclusion

Bouveret syndrome is an extremely rare diagnosis and a complication of gallstone disease which is often not immediately recognised due to its vague presentation and limitations of visualisation on endoscopy and ultrasound imaging. While CT imaging remains the mainstay for diagnosis, in patients where visualisation on endoscopy is successful, endoscopy also lends itself as a possible therapeutic adjunct. Initial approach by minimally invasive multimodal endoscopic techniques is favoured due to the typical affected patient being frail or having considerable co-morbidity. Most cases are ultimately resolved by enterolithotomy/gastrotomy, followed by cholecystectomy with or without fistula repair with considerations for the risks versus benefits of a one-stage or two-stage approach to resolution. Patients who do not undergo subsequent cholecystectomy may need to be closely monitored and followed up for signs of gallstone recurrence.

Patient’s perspective

In February of 2025, I was coughing up bile and was brought to hospital via an ambulance. When I got there, I had a scan and gave me stuff to make me less sick. They tried to do a [procedure] to get rid of [the stone] which didn’t work, so they had to do another where they had to anaesthetise me; so they put me out but that also didn’t work, so they cut me open and cut out what I presume was blocking the entry/exit of my gallbladder. I think overall, the operation itself was successful. The surgeon who did my operation said I might have some side effects but the side effects I have are with things I expect when you get older – I don’t really walk as much but mainly because there is nowhere to go. Ever since then, I’ve been trying to recover. No problems with eating and drinking but I don’t get hungry. I didn’t get any pain, but I get a lot of wind in my stomach and get a lot of loose stools, but this is not different from before the operation. I have not been constipated or felt the way I have before I went into hospital.
