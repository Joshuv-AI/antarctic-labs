The impact of a brief digital abstinence intervention on digital distractions and time management among nursing students: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Mohammed Nasser Albarqi, Mohammed Yousef Almulhim, Mai Adel Albana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDigital distraction and excessive engagement with instant-reward technologies, including social media and streaming platforms, are associated with reduced attention control, procrastination, and impaired self-regulation among university students. Nursing students face academic and clinical demands that require planning, prioritization, and sustained concentration. This study examined whether a brief structured digital abstinence intervention was associated with short-term improvement in time management behaviors among undergraduate nursing students.MethodsA quasi-experimental pre-post control group study with a supplementary exploratory qualitative component was conducted among 90 undergraduate nursing students at King Faisal University, Saudi Arabia, during the 2024–2025 academic year. Participants were allocated to an intervention group (n = 45) or a control group (n = 45) using matched allocation based on academic year, GPA category, and gender. The intervention group completed a structured 48-hour digital abstinence protocol restricting non-academic digital activities; the control group continued usual routines. Time management was assessed before and immediately after the intervention using the Time Management Behavior Scale (TMBS), and written reflections from the intervention group were analyzed thematically.ResultsCompared with the control group, the intervention group showed greater short-term improvement in TMBS total change scores (+ 6.7 +/- 4.5 vs. +0.3 +/- 3.6; mean difference = 6.4; 95% CI: 4.69 to 8.11; Cohen’s d = 1.57; p < 0.001). Improvements were also observed for goal setting/prioritization, task mechanics, time control, and organizational preference. Qualitative reflections suggested increased awareness of digital habits, improved academic focus, and more intentional study-time use. Correlations between TMBS improvement and GPA, baseline device-use category, and academic level were exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously.ConclusionsA brief structured digital abstinence intervention was associated with short-term improvement in self-reported time management behaviors among undergraduate nursing students. Because the study was quasi-experimental, single-site, self-reported, and limited to an immediate 48-hour post-test, causal and long-term claims should be interpreted cautiously. Future randomized multicenter studies should include objective screen-time measures and longer follow-up.Clinical trial registrationNCT07467057 Retrospectively registered (11-3-2016).Keywords: Academic performance, Behavioral intervention, Digital abstinence, Digital distraction, Digital self-Regulation, Nursing students, Self-regulated learning, Time management

Introduction

In the contemporary academic landscape, nursing students face an unprecedented convergence of academic pressure, clinical responsibilities, and digital distractions [30]. As digital technologies become integral to modern life, the omnipresence of smartphones, social media platforms, and streaming services has introduced new challenges to cognitive focus, productivity, and mental well-being among university students, particularly those in demanding fields like nursing [28]. Time management, a cornerstone of academic success and professional readiness in nursing education, is increasingly compromised by behavioral patterns associated with digital overstimulation and instant gratification [5, 33].

Time management is not only essential for effective learning and clinical performance but also for stress reduction and overall psychological well-being [17]. Numerous studies have linked effective time management with academic achievement, reduced burnout, and enhanced emotional regulation in nursing students [6]. However, the ability to plan, prioritize, and execute academic tasks is often undermined by the compulsive use of digital devices—a behavioral trend linked to the brain’s reward system, particularly dopamine-mediated pathways [10, 29]. The addictive nature of modern digital environments—designed to exploit reward-based learning mechanisms—has been associated with reduced attention spans, procrastination, and impaired goal-directed behaviors [32].

Digital platforms can reinforce frequent checking and rapid task switching through notifications, variable rewards, and immediate feedback. These mechanisms may contribute to habitual non-academic device use, procrastination, and reduced sustained attention in some students [19]. However, the present study did not measure dopamine activity, reward sensitivity, executive function, or neurobiological outcomes; therefore, the intervention is conceptualized here as a behavioral digital self-regulation strategy rather than a neurochemical intervention.

Emerging evidence suggests that behavioral interventions limiting digital engagement may enhance cognitive control and reduce procrastination in student populations [4]. However, studies explicitly investigating structured digital abstinence protocols within academic contexts—particularly in nursing education—remain scarce [5]. Most available research focuses on digital digital abstinence programs broadly, with few addressing the neurobehavioral underpinnings or directly linking such interventions to time management outcomes [21]. Moreover, poor time management in nursing students has far-reaching implications, extending beyond the academic domain into clinical practice, where prioritization and multitasking are essential competencies [26]. Inability to manage time effectively can result in increased stress, lower academic performance, reduced clinical confidence, and diminished readiness for real-world nursing responsibilities [3]. As such, developing and validating simple, low-cost interventions that address the root behavioral contributors to poor time management—such as digital overuse and hedonic distractions—may offer valuable strategies for both educators and students [35].

The theoretical foundation of this study was Self-Regulated Learning Theory, which emphasizes learners’ active planning, monitoring, and regulation of cognition, behavior, and environment during academic tasks. Within this framework, excessive non-academic digital engagement may interrupt forethought and performance-control processes by reducing attention to academic goals and weakening environmental structuring [25]. The hypothesized pathway was that a short period of structured digital abstinence would reduce immediate distraction, increase self-monitoring of digital habits, and support improved time management behaviors, particularly goal setting, task mechanics, and time control [18]. Other perspectives, such as attention-control and behavioral reinforcement models, are discussed only as supportive explanatory lenses rather than as measured neurobiological mechanisms.

In this manuscript, the term structured digital abstinence intervention refers to a planned, temporary reduction of non-academic digital and highly stimulating leisure activities. The term does not imply literal “detoxification” from dopamine or any directly measured neurobiological recalibration. The intervention was designed to increase awareness of automatic digital habits, reduce immediate distractions, and support intentional allocation of study time.

Aim of the study

The aim of this study was to evaluate whether a brief structured digital abstinence intervention was associated with short-term improvement in time management behaviors among undergraduate nursing students. The intervention sought to reduce non-academic digital distractions and promote self-monitoring, planning, prioritization, and intentional study-time use.

Methodology

Study design

This study used a quasi-experimental pre-post control group design with a supplementary exploratory qualitative component. Quantitative data were used to compare changes in time management behaviors between intervention and control groups, while written reflections from the intervention group were analyzed to contextualize students’ experiences of the intervention. The qualitative component was not intended to confirm effectiveness but to provide supportive explanatory insight. The study was reported with attention to TREND guidance for nonrandomized intervention studies and COREQ-informed reporting principles for the qualitative reflections [12, 31].

Setting

The study was conducted at the College of Nursing, King Faisal University, located in Al-Ahsa, Saudi Arabia. This institution offers a comprehensive undergraduate nursing program, and the study targeted students in their second and third years, who are typically engaged in intensive academic and clinical training. The college setting provided an ideal environment for evaluating the behavioral and cognitive impacts of the intervention within a high-demand learning context.

Sample and sampling

The study targeted undergraduate nursing students enrolled in the Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN) program at King Faisal University, Saudi Arabia, during the academic year 2024–2025. This population was selected due to the demanding nature of their academic and clinical responsibilities, which makes effective time management particularly critical. Additionally, the integration of digital technology in both educational and personal life has rendered this demographic particularly susceptible to the negative effects of overstimulation and digital distractions.

A total of 90 students were recruited through convenience sampling, a non-probability sampling technique chosen for its relevance in intervention-based educational research. This approach enabled the researchers to intentionally select participants who met specific inclusion criteria and were likely to benefit from or contribute meaningfully to the evaluation of the structured digital abstinence intervention.

Eligible students were invited to an initial briefing session through email and class announcements. Interested participants completed a short screening questionnaire to confirm eligibility. Because randomization was not feasible in the educational setting, participants were allocated using matched allocation to balance academic year, GPA category, and gender across groups. Allocation was conducted by a researcher who was not involved in classroom teaching or grading. Participants were allocated as individuals rather than as classes. Allocation concealment was not possible, and contamination between groups could not be fully excluded because students studied in the same institution; this is acknowledged as a limitation.

The sample size of 90 students was determined based on previous quasi-experimental studies evaluating behavioral interventions in educational settings, with an anticipated medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5), 80% statistical power, and a significance level of α = 0.05. An additional 10% was added to the target recruitment number to account for potential attrition; however, all participants completed both pre- and post-test assessments, yielding a 100% response rate.

Demographic data such as age, gender, academic year, cumulative GPA, and average daily screen time were collected to describe the sample and assess baseline equivalence between the two groups.

This sample provided a basis for estimating the short-term association between a structured digital abstinence intervention and self-reported time management behaviors among nursing students; however, the nonrandomized single-site design limits causal inference and generalizability.

Data collection tools

The primary outcome was time management behavior, assessed using the Time Management Behavior Scale (TMBS).

The TMBS was not developed for this study. It was based on the published Time Management Behavior Scale originally developed by Macan et al., for college students and subsequently examined in psychometric studies. The English scale was translated into Arabic for this study using forward translation, expert review, and back-translation procedures [1, 20]. A bilingual nursing-education panel reviewed the translated version for semantic clarity and cultural appropriateness. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never to 5 = always), with higher scores indicating stronger time management behavior. Because no established minimal clinically important difference is available for the TMBS, results are interpreted statistically and educationally rather than as clinically significant.

Four TMBS subdomains were scored: Goal setting and priorities, Task mechanics, Time control, and Organizational preference. Subscale and total scores were calculated by summing item responses within each domain.

Before data collection, the questionnaire package was pilot-tested with nursing students who were not included in the main study to confirm clarity and cultural appropriateness. Internal consistency reliability was calculated in the present sample for the TMBS total scale and subdomains. The immediate post-test required students to answer based on the previous two days; this short recall frame was selected to match the 48-hour intervention but is acknowledged as a measurement limitation because the TMBS was originally designed to assess broader time management tendencies.

Data collection procedure

The data collection process for this study was carried out in three sequential phases: pre-intervention assessment, intervention implementation, and post-intervention assessment, over one week during the second semester of the 2024–2025 academic year.

Recruitment and orientation

Eligible students were identified based on inclusion and exclusion criteria and invited to participate via institutional email and in-class announcements. Interested students attended a brief orientation session conducted by the principal investigator, where the purpose, procedure, expectations, and ethical considerations of the study were clearly explained. Participants were then assigned to either the intervention group (n = 45) or control group (n = 45) using matched allocation based on academic level and GPA to reduce selection bias.

During the orientation, participants received a Participant Information Sheet and signed an informed consent form. They were informed of their right to withdraw at any stage without any academic or personal repercussions.

Pre-intervention assessment

Before the intervention began, all participants were asked to complete a socio-demographic questionnaire and the Arabic version of the Time Management Behavior Scale (TMBS) to assess their baseline time management skills. This data was collected using printed surveys in a monitored classroom setting to ensure a quiet and distraction-free environment. Participants were assigned unique identification codes to maintain confidentiality and facilitate anonymous matching between pre- and post-test data.

Implementation of the structured digital abstinence intervention

Participants in the intervention group received standardized written and verbal instructions describing the 48-hour structured digital abstinence protocol. The period extended from Thursday 6:00 PM to Saturday 6:00 PM to reduce academic disruption while allowing observation of short-term behavioral changes. The protocol focused on avoiding non-academic digital activities while permitting essential academic, family, clinical, and emergency communication.

A daily check-in message via university email (not messaging apps) was sent to participants in the intervention group to provide reminders and answer questions. Adherence was assessed using self-reported reflection logs in which students recorded protocol completion, perceived deviations, and barriers. No objective screen-time analytics or app-monitoring tools were used. No punitive measures were applied for non-adherence, and students were asked to report deviations honestly. No serious harm or distress was reported; transient discomfort, boredom, or restlessness was recorded by some participants and is reported in the qualitative findings.

The control group received no digital restriction instructions and continued their usual routines during the same 48-hour period. To reduce assessment differences, control participants also completed brief reflection logs describing their general academic behaviors and time management practices without any imposed restrictions.

Post-intervention assessment

At the end of the 48-hour intervention period, both groups completed the post-intervention TMBS in the same setting as the baseline assessment. Participants were instructed to answer based on their behavior and experiences during the previous two days. This immediate post-test was used to capture short-term perceived change; it does not establish persistence of behavior change.

Intervention-group written reflections were used for supplementary thematic analysis. Reflections were collected using prompts asking students to describe: (1) challenges experienced during the abstinence period, (2) perceived changes in study focus or time use, (3) strategies used to manage urges to check devices, and (4) whether they would repeat or recommend the strategy.

All paper-based instruments were securely collected, coded, and transferred to a password-protected digital database for statistical analysis. The personal identifiers were kept separate from the response sheets to ensure data privacy.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27. Descriptive statistics summarized demographic variables and TMBS scores. Baseline comparability between groups was examined descriptively and, where appropriate, using independent-samples t-tests or chi-square tests. Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Paired t-tests compared pre- and post-intervention TMBS scores within each group, and independent-samples t-tests compared change scores between groups. Mean differences, 95% confidence intervals, and Cohen’s d effect sizes were reported to support interpretation of magnitude. Normality of change scores was assessed using visual inspection and the Shapiro-Wilk test. Because multiple TMBS domains were analyzed, subdomain analyses were interpreted as secondary and exploratory. The exploratory path model was reframed as a regression-based exploratory analysis because the intervention group sample size (n = 45) was not adequate for a robust structural equation model. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Qualitative analysis: Written reflections from intervention participants were analyzed using inductive thematic content analysis. Two researchers independently read the reflections, generated initial codes, compared coding decisions, and resolved differences through discussion. Codes were grouped into themes that reflected repeated patterns across participants. Frequency counts were used only to describe how often themes appeared and were not treated as quantitative outcome measures. Representative quotes were selected to illustrate each theme. Trustworthiness was supported through investigator triangulation, maintenance of an audit trail of coding decisions, and use of verbatim participant statements.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of King Faisal University before participant recruitment and data collection (KFU-ETHICS4341) in September 2024. All participants provided written informed consent. Participation was voluntary, and students were informed that refusal or withdrawal would not affect grades, academic standing, or relationships with faculty. Data were anonymized using unique identification codes, stored securely, and reported only in aggregate form. Students could withdraw at any time without penalty and were debriefed after data collection.

Discussion

This study examined whether a brief structured digital abstinence intervention was associated with short-term improvement in self-reported time management behaviors among undergraduate nursing students. The findings showed greater immediate improvement in TMBS total and several subdomain scores among intervention participants compared with controls. These findings are consistent with a self-regulated learning interpretation in which reducing non-academic digital distractions may support planning, monitoring, and intentional allocation of study time [9, 24]. However, because the study was quasi-experimental, nonrandomized, single-site, and based on self-report, the results should be interpreted cautiously as associations rather than definitive causal effects.

The primary finding suggests that the structured digital abstinence intervention was associated with statistically significant improvement in time management behaviors. The language of clinical significance has been removed because no established minimal clinically important difference for the TMBS was identified [8]. The immediate improvement may reflect reduced exposure to digital interruptions, increased awareness of habitual device checking, and greater intentionality in study planning; however, expectancy effects, Hawthorne effects, and social desirability may also have contributed because students knew they were taking part in a behavioral challenge [2, 13].

The largest gains were observed in goal setting/priorities and time control, which are central components of self-regulated learning. These results are compatible with the idea that temporarily reducing non-academic digital engagement may help students structure their environment and focus on planned academic tasks [11]. Nevertheless, the intervention was only 48 h long, and the post-test was conducted immediately afterward; therefore, the findings cannot demonstrate sustained behavioral change [22].

Qualitative reflections provided supportive context for the quantitative findings. Students described greater awareness of automatic phone checking, improved study engagement, and initial discomfort during the abstinence period. These reflections help explain possible behavioral mechanisms but do not prove effectiveness [27]. The reflections were brief written logs rather than in-depth interviews, and the frequency counts should be viewed as descriptive indicators rather than quantitative prevalence estimates [15, 23].

The exploratory regression-based findings should be interpreted with particular caution. Baseline device-use category showed a negative association with TMBS change, suggesting that students with higher baseline non-academic digital use may have experienced smaller improvement or greater difficulty adhering to the protocol [14]. GPA showed only a marginal association, and age, gender, and academic level were not significant predictors. Given the small intervention-group sample, these results should be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger studies [16].

The findings contribute to nursing education by highlighting digital self-regulation as a potentially useful component of student support and study-skills programs. The discussion has been revised to avoid direct neurobiological claims because dopamine activity, executive functioning, reward processing, and attentional biomarkers were not measured [34]. Future research should evaluate whether digital abstinence, digital self-monitoring, or blended digital-hygiene interventions produce sustained improvements beyond short-term awareness [7].

Implications of the study

The findings have implications for nursing education and student support services. A brief structured digital abstinence activity may be used as a low-cost reflective exercise to help students identify digital habits, plan study time, and practice environmental self-regulation. However, it should be presented as a flexible educational strategy rather than a mandatory or universally feasible intervention.

Educators and curriculum designers may consider integrating digital self-regulation content into orientation courses, academic-skills workshops, or wellness programs. Implementation should allow essential communication for family, clinical, work, and academic obligations, particularly for students with caregiving duties or clinical placements.

At the institutional level, digital distraction can be addressed through supportive measures such as technology-aware study-skills training, optional screen-time self-monitoring, and protected study spaces. These approaches should be balanced with students’ legitimate need for digital access in modern nursing education.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be considered. First, the quasi-experimental nonrandomized design, convenience sampling, and single-institution setting limit causal inference and generalizability. Second, allocation concealment was not possible, and contamination between groups may have occurred because students studied within the same college. Third, the intervention lasted only 48 h and outcomes were measured immediately afterward, preventing conclusions about sustainability or long-term academic impact.

Fourth, all outcomes were self-reported. Students may have overreported improvement due to expectancy, social desirability, recall bias, or Hawthorne effects. Fifth, adherence was assessed using reflection logs rather than objective screen-time analytics or app-use monitoring, so actual reductions in digital activity could not be independently verified.

Sixth, the TMBS was administered with a two-day recall frame to match the intervention period, although the instrument was originally designed to assess broader time management tendencies. This may have affected measurement validity for immediate short-term change. Seventh, the qualitative component consisted of brief written reflections rather than interviews or focus groups, limiting depth and nuance.

Finally, the exploratory regression-based analysis was limited by the small intervention-group sample and should be interpreted only as hypothesis-generating. Future randomized controlled studies with larger multicenter samples, objective digital-use tracking, longer follow-up, and stronger measurement-validation procedures are recommended.

Conclusion

This study suggests that a brief structured digital abstinence intervention may be associated with short-term improvement in self-reported time management behaviors among undergraduate nursing students. The intervention appeared feasible and was perceived by many students as helpful for increasing awareness of digital habits and supporting focused study time. However, the findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the nonrandomized design, self-reported outcomes, lack of objective adherence monitoring, and immediate post-intervention assessment. Future research should test longer and more flexible digital self-regulation interventions using randomized designs, objective screen-time measures, and longitudinal follow-up before firm conclusions about effectiveness or sustainability are drawn.
