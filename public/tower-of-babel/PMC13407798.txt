Clinically Amyopathic Dermatomyositis: Epidemiology, Clinical Presentation, Pathophysiology, Treatment, and Prognosis: A Systematic Review
Authors: Jucier Gonçalves Júnior, Jamily Izabel Alves dos Santos, Samuel Katsuyuki Shinjo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionWe conducted a systematic review on the epidemiology, clinical presentation, pathophysiology, treatment, and prognosis of clinically amyopathic dermatomyositis (CADM).MethodsA qualitative systematic review was performed from October 1988 to January 2026 according to PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines, using seven electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, Virtual Health Library, PsycInfo, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library. Studies were eligible if at least one combination of search terms appeared in the title, were written in English, Portuguese, or Spanish, and addressed the epidemiology, pathogenesis, diagnosis, or treatment of CADM. Systematic reviews, theses, dissertations, letters, editorials, studies with unclear methodology, and articles addressing classic or paraneoplastic dermatomyositis were excluded.ResultsA total of 106 studies comprising 816 patients were included. CADM is more frequent among females and most prevalent in the fifth and sixth decades of life. The most common cutaneous findings were Gottron’s signs, heliotrope rash, and the V-neck sign. Interstitial lung disease (ILD) was the most frequent extracutaneous, extramuscular manifestation. Elevated ferritin, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were common. The most frequent autoantibodies were anti-MDA-5 (Anti-Melanoma Differentiation-Associated gene 5) and anti-Ro-52. Treatment most often combined pulse glucocorticoids with human immunoglobulin, cyclophosphamide, rituximab, or cyclosporine. Poorer prognosis was associated with higher ferritin, CRP, and LDH levels, and with the presence of ILD.ConclusionsCADM is a serious, complex disease with a distinct clinical and serological profile, requiring further conceptual refinement and multicenter studies to better characterize this DM phenotype.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40744-026-00863-0.

Key Summary Points

Clinically amyopathic dermatomyositis (CADM) predominantly affects middle-aged women and presents with characteristic cutaneous findings.Interstitial lung disease is the most frequent systemic manifestation and a key determinant of prognosis.Anti-Melanoma Differentiation-Associated gene 5 (Anti-MDA-5) positivity and elevated ferritin, C-reactive protein (CRP), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels are associated with worse outcomes.Early recognition and combined immunosuppressive therapy are essential to improve clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM), or systemic autoimmune myopathies, are rare, serious, and complex disorders characterized by an immune-mediated attack on muscle tissue. The incidence of IIMs ranges from 0.2 to 2 per 100,000 inhabitants, with a prevalence of 2–25 per 100,000 inhabitants. This group includes polymyositis (PM), dermatomyositis (DM), inclusion body myositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), anti-synthetase syndrome (ASyS), overlap myositis, and cancer-associated myositis [1–3].

Clinically amyopathic dermatomyositis (CADM) accounts for up to 20% of classic DM cases and is defined by a complete or partial absence of clinical and laboratory muscle involvement despite typical cutaneous manifestations [4]. The pathophysiology of CADM is not fully understood. A subgroup of patients has anti-MDA-5 autoantibodies, which can cause severe pulmonary and cutaneous involvement, contributing to increased morbidity and mortality [5, 6].

The survival rate of patients with CADM at 5 and 10 years can range from 80 to 73%, while mortality can reach 50% at 6 to 12 months in cases with anti-MDA-5 [7, 9]. However, in patients with positivity for the anti-MDA-5 autoantibody, mortality can be significantly higher, reaching up to 50% within a period of 6 to 12 months [7]. A retrospective Chinese study including 125 patients reported a 53.2% mortality rate 3 months after interstitial lung disease (ILD) diagnosis [8]. A population-based study in the United States found a 28% mortality rate over six years of follow-up [9].

Despite recent scientific advances, further studies are needed to better understand CADM. Therefore, our objective was to conduct a systematic literature review guided by the following question: What practical contributions does the current scientific literature offer regarding the epidemiology, clinical presentation, pathophysiology, treatment, and prognosis of CADM? Our hypothesis is that, although CADM is a subtype of DM, it has distinct pathophysiological profiles associated with different prognoses.

Methods

Literature Review

A systematic review of the literature was conducted according to PRISMA guidelines using seven electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, Virtual Health Library (VHL), PsycInfo, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library. Electronic searches used the following MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) terms: #1 “amyopathic dermatomyositis;” #2 “epidemiology;” #3 “physiopathology;” #4 “autoantibodies;” #5 “signs and symptoms;” #6 “magnetic resonance imaging;” #7 “prognosis;” #8 “therapeutics.” These descriptors were combined using the Boolean operator “AND.”

The supplementary Material presents the complete search strategy. The search period spanned from October 1, 1988 to January 1, 2026. Due to the scarcity of articles on this topic, 1988 was chosen as the starting point because it is the earliest relevant publication identified in the databases.

This review followed the PICO framework, in which “P” represents patients with CADM; “I” denotes epidemiology, pathophysiology, clinical features, and treatment in patients with CADM; “C” refers to comparison groups without CADM; and “O” signifies the impact of CADM on the health of patients with IIMs.

Data collection was carried out from January 5, 2026 to January 9, 2026. Manuscripts were initially screened based on title and abstract. Tools from the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute were used to assess the quality of each study (Table 1), including the Study Quality Assessment Tools. The Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies and the Quality Assessment Tool for Case Series Studies were applied to observational cross-sectional, case–control and cohort studies, and to case reports and case series, respectively. These tools classify studies as “good,” “fair,” or “poor” based on the presence or absence of methodological elements relevant to each study design.

Eligibility Criteria

Articles were included if: (i) contained at least one combination of the search terms in the title or abstract; (ii) had full-text online access; (iii) were written in English, Portuguese, or Spanish; and (iv) addressed the epidemiology, pathophysiology, clinical presentation, treatment, or prognosis of patients with CADM. Systematic reviews of research, theses, dissertations, letters, editorials and studies with unclear methodology are excluded. Also excluded are articles that address classical or paraneoplastic DM, including patients with a history of malignancy five years before, at the time of, or five years after diagnosis.

Ethics Statement

As this is a systematic review of the literature, approval by a Human Research Ethics Committee was not required, in accordance with Resolution 510/16 of the Brazilian National Health Council. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors. This review was prospectively registered in PROSPERO under number CRD420261287002.

Results

According to the search strategy applied across the seven databases, 7405 records were identified, of which 106 met the eligibility criteria (Fig. 1). The sample consisted predominantly of case series and case reports (84.9%), followed by cohort studies (14.2%) and case–control studies (0.9%). Most studies were classified as “good” (88.7%) [10–103], while the remainder were classified as “fair” (11.3%) [8, 104–114].Fig. 1PRISMA flow diagram

PRISMA flow diagram

Regarding geographical distribution, most studies were conducted in Asian countries (66.0%), followed by North America (14.2%), Europe (14.1%), South America (3.8%), and Africa (1.9%).

A total of 816 patients with CADM were included. Most were female (65.0%), with a mean age of 50.4 years.

The most frequently reported cutaneous manifestations were Gottron’s sign (53.4%), heliotrope rash (36.5%), and V-neck sign (33.8%). Arthralgia was present in 23.0% of cases, mechanic’s hands in 21.3%, and Gottron’s papules in 16.7%. Other manifestations included skin ulcers (9.0%), calcinosis (2.5%), Holster sign (2.0%), and alopecia (1.5%). Reverse Gottron’s sign was described in only four patients (0.5%). ILD was present in most of the sample (77.3%), with RP-ILD observed in 31.9% of cases. Raynaud’s phenomenon (5.4%), dysphagia (1.5%), and vasculopathy (0.6%) were reported in a minority of cases.

Antinuclear antibodies (ANA) were present in 27.0% of cases. The most prevalent autoantibodies were anti-MDA-5 (51.3%) and anti-Ro-52 (21.2%). Less frequently, the following autoantibodies were identified: anti-Jo-1 (4.6%), anti-PL-7 (3.8%), anti-PL-12 (2.4%), anti-TIF1-γ (2.3%), anti-Mi-2 (1.5%), anti-EJ (1.5%), and anti-Ku (1.3%).

CK levels remained within the normal range in 64.3% of patients with available data. In contrast, elevated ferritin levels were identified in 47.8% of cases, with values ranging from 13 to 7800 ng/ml. Elevated LDH levels were observed in 75.5% of the sample with available information. Similarly, the AST and ALT showed elevation in 53.2% and 51.7% of cases, respectively.

MRI to assess muscle involvement was available for 64 patients, with 34.4% showing signs of myositis and 65.6% showing no signs of inflammation. EMG was reported in 108 patients, with normal results in 79.6%.

Chest HRCT was available for 32.5% of patients, 97.7% of whom showed abnormalities. The most frequently reported findings were ground-glass opacities (21.5%), interstitial pneumonia (15.9%), consolidations (7.9%), septal thickening (3.4%), organizing pneumonia (3.0%), and bronchiectasis (2.6%).

Muscle biopsy was performed in 75 patients, with 69.3% showing no findings suggestive of myositis. Skin biopsy was performed in 9.0% of the sample and revealed features consistent with DM in 98.6% of cases, including inflammatory cell infiltrates (35.6%), degeneration (15.1%), hyperkeratosis (9.6%), and interface dermatitis (8.2%).

Regarding treatment, most patients received glucocorticoids (GC) (89.5%), mainly methylprednisolone—MP (17.4%), prednisolone (15.2%), or prednisone (9.4%). Immunosuppressive therapy was the second most common approach (86.7%), including cyclosporine—CsA (23.2%), cyclophosphamide—CTX (19.9%), tacrolimus—FK (15.0%), mycophenolate mofetil—MMF (12.1%), methotrexate—MTX (5.1%), azathioprine—AZA (5.1%), and thalidomide (1.6%).

Immunobiological therapy was used in 25.5% of patients, predominantly intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (73.7%) and rituximab (26.3%). Antimalarials were prescribed for 15.4% of patients, including hydroxychloroquine—HCQ (42.1%) and chloroquine—CQ (2.3%), and other topical therapies were used in 11.5%.

Extracorporeal blood purification therapies were used in 7.4% of patients, including polymyxin B-immobilized fiber column direct hemoperfusion (52.6%), plasmapheresis (31.6%), extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (10.5%), and DNA immunoadsorption (5.3%).

Use of antifibrotic agents (6.3%), antibiotics (3.3%), and Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors (3.1%) was also reported, although less frequently.

Regarding prognosis, 71.2% of patients improved clinically and survived following the reported therapeutic approaches; however, 28.8% died, and most of these deaths were associated with the presence of ILD (72.1%).

For heuristic purposes, the results were organized into the following topics: “Clinical and epidemiological characterization of patients with CADM,” “Pathophysiology of CADM,” “Laboratory and imaging findings in CADM,” and “Therapeutic approaches and prognosis of CADM.”

Discussion

Clinical and Epidemiological Characterization of Patients with CADM

In our study, CADM was more prevalent among women over 50 years of age, with most cases reported in Asian populations, followed by North American and European cohorts.

CADM typically presents with absent or minimal muscle involvement, along with characteristic cutaneous lesions such as Gottron’s signs, heliotrope rash, shawl sign, and V-neck erythema [42, 69]. In the literature, Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash are described as the most frequent cutaneous manifestations [10, 18, 87, 92]. However, in the present review, Gottron’s signs were reported more frequently than Gottron’s papules, likely reflecting a limitation in the standardization of reports, as many studies did not clearly differentiate between Gottron’s signs and Gottron’s papules [8, 10, 11, 17, 105, 108] (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Clinical and serological features of CADM. ANA antinuclear antibodies, ILD interstitial lung disease, MDA-5 melanoma differentiation-associated gene 5

Other cutaneous manifestations include erythematous plaques, poikiloderma, nail changes, periungual telangiectasia, cuticular dystrophy or hypertrophy, calcinosis, and Raynaud’s phenomenon [62, 92]. Other typical clinical findings include arthralgia and mechanic’s hands [50], observed in 21% and 23% of patients in our sample, respectively. Calcinosis is more frequently described in pediatric patients [94]. In our sample, calcinosis was observed in 2.5% of patients, while Raynaud’s phenomenon occurred in 5.4%.

Several skin manifestations have been associated with anti-MDA-5 positivity, including skin ulcerations, inverted Gottron’s papules, panniculitis, and alopecia [75]. Despite the high prevalence of anti-MDA-5 in our population, only 1.5% of patients presented with alopecia, and inverted Gottron’s sign was documented in only four individuals [74, 90, 94, 97]. This discrepancy is likely related to the retrospective nature of many studies, limited access to autoantibody testing, short follow-up in some cohorts, and incomplete reporting of specific cutaneous features in anti-MDA-5-positive patients [15, 16].

Although cutaneous manifestations are central to the suspicion of CADM, they can be nonspecific, contributing to delayed diagnosis. Aissaoui et al. described a 45-year-old patient with CADM who developed rapidly progressive ILD and presented with atypical cutaneous lesions, which delayed timely therapeutic intervention. This underscores the importance of considering CADM in patients with diffuse ILD, particularly when cutaneous changes and articular involvement coexist [77].

In contrast to classic DM, which typically presents with proximal muscle weakness and overt myositis, CADM shows little or no muscle involvement. Consequently, dysphagia is uncommon [92] and was reported in only 1.5% of patients in our review.

ILD is a frequently reported manifestation in CADM, identified in 74.6% of patients included in this review. Among cases with pulmonary involvement, the rapidly progressive form of ILD was observed in 31.9%. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as they may be overestimated because the available literature predominantly includes patients with more severe conditions, in whom ILD, especially in its rapidly progressive form, is more prevalent [7, 8, 34, 35, 67, 86].

According to the literature, ILD—especially in its rapidly progressive form—shows a strong association with positivity for the anti-MDA-5 antibody and is linked to a worse prognosis [75]. In our review, 42.4% of patients with ILD tested positive for the anti-MDA-5 antibody, while 54.7% of those with the rapidly progressive form of ILD were positive for this antibody. Diagnosis is primarily based on respiratory clinical manifestations, such as dry cough and dyspnea, symptoms frequently reported in this population [8, 10, 53]. Histopathologically, ILD can present different patterns, including diffuse alveolar damage and usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) [64].

Pathophysiology of CADM

The specific mechanisms involved in the pathogenesis of CADM are not yet fully understood; however, it is believed that the disease results from the interaction of genetic, environmental, and immunological factors [87]. Among genetic factors, variations in the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) system are noteworthy, while environmental triggers such as viral infections may initiate the aberrant immune response [78].

In CADM, autoantibodies directed against intracellular autoantigens, particularly those involved in antiviral responses, play a central role in disease pathophysiology. Among these, anti-MDA-5 autoantibodies are notable for targeting MDA-5, a double-stranded RNA helicase encoded by the IFIH-1 (Interferon Induced with Helicase C Domain 1) gene. Under physiological conditions, MDA-5 acts as a pattern-recognition receptor for pathogen-associated molecular patterns, especially viral RNA, promoting activation of the type I interferon pathway and production of inflammatory cytokines [62, 75] (Fig. 2).

The presence of anti-MDA-5 autoantibodies may interfere with this mechanism by directly binding to MDA-5 or forming immune complexes, leading to persistent activation of type I interferon-mediated inflammation. This sustained inflammatory response favors recruitment of immune cells, including CD4⁺ T lymphocytes, dendritic cells, and B cells, and leads to endothelial dysfunction and vasculopathy, particularly in highly vascularized tissues such as the skin and lungs [92]. This predominance of vascular involvement, rather than skeletal muscle damage, helps explain the minimal or absent muscle symptoms characteristic of CADM, as well as the high frequency of cutaneous manifestations and the strong association with ILD [50, 95].

Furthermore, the anti-MDA-5 antibody is considered mutually exclusive with anti-aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase (anti-ARS) antibodies, which are a group of autoantibodies frequently identified in patients with classic DM and PM [15]. In contrast to anti-MDA-5, which is generally associated with an unfavorable prognosis, anti-ARS antibodies are more commonly related to chronic forms of ILD and show a better response to corticosteroid treatment [15, 18]. In our study, positivity for anti-ARS antibodies was observed in approximately 12% of patients, a lower frequency than that described for the most prevalent autoantibodies.

Another autoantibody frequently detected in DM, including amyopathic forms, is anti-Ro-52. This autoantibody targets the regulatory protein Ro-52 and is often found in individuals with anti-MDA-5-positive DM, particularly when ILD is present. Studies indicate that coexistence of anti-Ro-52 and anti-MDA-5 is associated with a higher prevalence of rapidly progressive ILD and a more aggressive clinical phenotype [72, 95, 112] (Fig. 2).

In a cohort of 125 patients, all were anti-MDA-5-positive, and anti-Ro-52 was the second most frequently identified autoantibody [8]. Consistently, in our review, anti-MDA-5 (51.3%) and anti-Ro-52 (21.2%) were highly prevalent, which supports published data and underscores their relevance in the pathogenesis and clinical expression of CADM. However, the cross-sectional design, small sample sizes, and lack of standardized diagnostic criteria for CADM in these studies—reflecting the rarity of the disease—must be considered when interpreting these findings.

Laboratory and Imaging Tests in CADM

Serum muscle enzyme levels are usually normal or only mildly elevated in CADM, including CK, aldolase, LDH, and AST [87]. Among patients with available CK data in our review, 64.31% had normal levels.

Elevated ferritin levels have been associated with worse outcomes in CADM, particularly in patients with DILD, increased LDH, and anti-MDA-5 positivity; however, specific cutoff values and risk estimates vary across studies [10, 18, 72]. In a study by Okabayashi et al. involving 14 patients with CADM and rapidly progressive ILD, ferritin levels were significantly lower among survivors compared with non-survivors [15]. In our review, elevated ferritin was observed in 47.8% of patients with available measurements, reinforcing the usefulness of this laboratory marker as a practical, low-cost tool for disease monitoring in CADM [24].

Furthermore, elevated LDH levels were observed in 75.5% of the sample, supporting its association with increased inflammatory activity and systemic impairment. Elevated AST levels were identified in 53.2% of patients with available values and have also been reported in the literature as a marker of worse prognosis [77].

HRCT is fundamental for evaluating ILD in CADM. This modality typically reveals ground-glass opacities and reticular patterns suggestive of interstitial involvement, being crucial for the early detection of lung disease [95]. In our sample, tomographic abnormalities were observed in 97.9% of the 265 patients with available high-resolution CT results, with ground-glass opacities as the most frequent finding (21.5%). These high values may be due to the high proportion of patients with Inclusion Body Myositis in the included studies [14, 43, 72, 99].

The literature indicates that MRI, EMG, and muscle biopsy are of limited value for diagnosing CADM [42, 64, 86, 87, 104]. In our review, muscle MRI was performed in 64 patients and was normal in 65.6% of cases. Of the 108 patients who underwent EMG, 79.6% had no abnormalities. Muscle biopsies were performed in 75 patients and did not show changes suggestive of myositis in 69.3%. However, the limited use of these tests in the included studies, along with variability in access to specialized equipment and expertise, may affect the generalizability of these findings.

Skin biopsy is widely used as a supportive diagnostic tool in CADM and typically reveals vacuolar interface dermatitis, dermal mucin deposition, epidermal atrophy, predominantly leukocytic perivascular inflammatory infiltrates, endothelial damage, and reduced capillary density [69, 75, 78, 92]. In our review, skin biopsy was performed in only 73 patients but assisted diagnosis in 98.9% of these cases by demonstrating changes compatible with DM [50, 56, 75, 113].

Therapeutic Approaches and Prognosis of CADM

Treatment of CADM is not standardized and often depends on local experience and regional protocols. Nonetheless, high-dose GC (0.5–1.0 mg/kg/day) combined with immunosuppressive agents is generally considered first-line therapy [19].

In our review, GC were prescribed in 89.5% of cases. Methylprednisolone was the most frequently reported, followed by prednisolone and prednisone, likely reflecting the severity of clinical presentations [92]. In many reports, however, the specific GC was not specified, limiting precise characterization of drug distribution within this class [10, 12, 15, 17, 23, 76, 111].

Immunosuppressants were used in 86.7% of cases. IVIG was the most frequently employed immunobiologic (73.7%). IVIG has demonstrated efficacy when combined with GC [88]. For example, Hamada-Ode et al. reported a 62-year-old Japanese patient with rapidly progressive ILD who showed clinical improvement after high-dose IVIG, suggesting a potential role for IVIG in refractory CADM with rapidly progressive ILD [43].

Among conventional immunosuppressive drugs, cyclosporine—CsA (23.2%) was most frequently used, followed by cyclophosphamide (19.9%). CsA is often chosen for severe cases and those associated with ILD [42, 77]. However, Ando et al. described a 58-year-old man with CADM and ILD who did not respond to CsA but improved with tacrolimus (FK), suggesting FK as an alternative in refractory cases [35]. In anti-MDA-5-positive CADM with ILD, cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate mofetil, FK, and CsA have all been used [19, 64, 78].

In our review, most patients received IVIG, MP, FK, CTX, and CsA [58, 62, 102, 112]. These findings align with the included studies, which often report more severe cases of CADM, frequently with pulmonary involvement. Therefore, these results primarily reflect this subgroup of patients, while milder cases without pulmonary involvement can be managed with less aggressive therapies [16, 18, 22, 52, 77].

Other therapeutic strategies have been reported, including plasmapheresis, polymyxin B–immobilized fiber column direct hemoperfusion, rituximab, and inhibitors such as tofacitinib (90). Tofacitinib has shown efficacy in a Japanese patient with anti-MDA-5-positive DM complicated by ILD [63], as well as in a 38-year-old patient with CADM and anti-MDA-5 positivity [94]. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation may be considered as supportive therapy for patients with severe respiratory failure [53].

The prognosis of CADM is heterogeneous and largely dependent on clinical phenotype. The presence of ILD [17, 78, 95], elevated ferritin, CRP, and LDH levels, and anti-MDA-5 positivity are consistently associated with poorer outcomes [15, 18]. Respiratory complications related to ILD are the leading cause of death, accounting for 72.1% of fatalities in our review, underscoring the need for early recognition and aggressive management of pulmonary involvement [72].

Despite the high prevalence of ILD (74.6%), overall survival in our review exceeded 70%. This may be partly explained by the predominance of case reports describing patients who improved clinically [36, 37, 47, 49, 65, 72], as well as publication bias toward favorable outcomes. Moreover, some cohort studies, although including many patients with ILD, did not clearly report mortality rates, which may have led to underestimation of true mortality in this review [105, 107, 108].

Limitations of this review include: (i) the retrospective and cross-sectional design of most studies; (ii) small sample sizes; (iii) missing data, particularly regarding autoantibody profiles and detailed clinical descriptions; (iv) lack of standardized treatment protocols; and (v) the inability to perform a meta-analysis due to heterogeneity of study designs and outcomes.

Conclusions

CADM is a relatively common subtype of IIM among women in their fifth and sixth decades of life. Gottron’s sign, heliotrope rash, and the V-neck sign are the most prevalent cutaneous features. ILD and RP-ILD are common and strongly associated with poor prognosis. Anti-MDA-5 and anti-Ro-52 are the autoantibodies most frequently associated with CADM. Ferritin is an important biomarker linked to ILD and adverse outcomes, with reported values ranging from 13 to 7800 ng/ml. CK, MRI, EMG, and muscle biopsy have limited diagnostic utility due to their frequent normal or nonspecific findings. The extensive use of IVIG, cyclosporine, cyclophosphamide, and rituximab reflect the severity and therapeutic complexity of CADM. Overall, the presence of ILD, elevated ferritin, CRP, and LDH levels, and anti-MDA-5 positivity are consistently associated with a worse prognosis. The main cause of death is respiratory failure secondary to ILD.
