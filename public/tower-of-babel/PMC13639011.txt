Ex vivo heart perfusion induces time dependent inflammatory activation in human donor hearts
Authors: Selena S Li, Bill Michaud, Asishana A Osho, David A D’Alessandro, Shannon N Tessier, Anthony Rosenzweig, S Alireza Rabi, James S Guseh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEx vivo heart perfusion (EVHP) has enabled the use of hearts donated after circulatory death (DCD), expanding the donor pool but with persistently higher rates of rejection and graft dysfunction. The molecular consequences of EVHP, however, remain incompletely defined. We quantified 11,083 proteins in perfusate from 20 human DCD hearts undergoing EVHP using an aptamer-based proteomic platform. Among the most strongly upregulated proteins were inflammatory cytokines IL-6 (log₂FC = 3.5, FDR = 8.9 × 10−6), IL-8 (CXCL8; log₂FC = 2.8, FDR = 4.7 × 10−6), and CCL2/MCP-1 (log₂FC = 2.8, FDR = 2.0 × 10−6). Levels of these cytokines correlated strongly with perfusion duration (Spearman ρ = 0.77–0.78, FDR < 5 × 10−5). Pathway analysis demonstrated enrichment of innate immune signaling, including interleukin-1 signaling (normalized enrichment score (NES) = 2.14, adjusted p = 0.0016) and leukocyte chemotaxis pathways. These cytokine findings were validated in an independent human cohort using multiplex immunoassay and reproduced in a swine DCD perfusion model. Together, these data identify a conserved, time-dependent innate immune response during EVHP and suggest that inflammatory activation may represent one mechanism linking prolonged perfusion to graft dysfunction after DCD heart transplantation.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Cardiology, Diseases, Immunology, Medical research

Introduction

Heart transplantation remains the definitive therapy for end-stage heart failure but is constrained by a limited donor pool. Traditionally, only hearts from brain-dead donors were used for transplantation, but innovative techniques for evaluation and transportation of donor hearts, including ex-vivo heart perfusion (EVHP), have enabled expansion of the donor pool through use of hearts donated after circulatory death (DCD)1–3. The current standard for EVHP is the Organ Care System (OCS), a transport device that received FDA approval following a multicenter trial and has shown promise in DCD heart recovery2–4 in single-center and national studies1–3,5,6.

EVHP is used in approximately 70% of DCD heart donations7 but its direct effects on organ quality remain poorly understood. Remarkably, ex-vivo perfused hearts have been found to have increased rates of acute rejection8, and longer duration of EVHP is associated with reduced survival among DCD heart recipients, raising questions about the effect of EVHP itself on the donor organ9. The OCS perfuses the heart in an unloaded Langendorff mode10, and potential contributors to injury include non-physiologic coronary perfusion, edema formation, contact with artificial surfaces, hemolysis, and other consequences of prolonged perfusion.

Our group previously reported metabolomic changes during EVHP using the OCS platform11. Building on this work, we now apply high-throughput proteomics to characterize the “secretome” of the DCD heart and its perfusate during EVHP, as reflected by circulating protein levels.

Results

Ex-vivo heart perfusion (EVHP) of 20 human DCD donor hearts led to widespread proteomic changes. Among 11,083 proteins quantified by SomaScan, 2347 were significantly increased and 2323 decreased at end-perfusion compared to initial perfusion (FDR < 0.05) (Fig. 1A, Suppl. Table 1). Eight proteins were significantly upregulated (FDR < 0.05) with FC > 1.2: IL-6, CCL2/MCP-1, CXCL8/IL-8, ABHD5, ASB9, MANEAL, TNNT2/TNNC1, MYH7, and LDB3. The most upregulated proteins were IL-6 (log₂FC = 3.5, FDR = 8.9 × 10⁻⁶), CXCL8/IL-8 (log₂FC = 2.8, FDR = 4.7 × 10−6), and CCL2/MCP-1 (log₂FC = 2.8, FDR = 2.0 × 10−6). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of upregulated proteins identified predominant activation of inflammatory pathways, including interleukin-1 signaling (NES = 2.14, adj. p = 0.0016), neutrophil chemotaxis (NES = 2.02, adj. p = 0.0045), and monocyte chemotaxis (NES = 1.97, adj. p = 0.021) (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 2). Fold change thresholds were used for visualization (Fig. 1A), and not for interpretation or pathway analysis, to avoid discarding biologically meaningful signals.

These findings were validated in an independent cohort of nine donor hearts (3 DBD, 6 DCD) perfused on OCS and analyzed by Luminex immunoassay. Unlike DBD hearts, DCD hearts undergo an obligatory period of warm ischemia time (WIT). Next, we investigated if the increase in inflammatory marker is unique to DCD. IL-6, IL-8, and CCL2 levels significantly increased during EVHP in all hearts with similar trends between DCD and DBD hearts (Fig. 3). There were no statistically significant differences between changes in cytokines in DCD and DBD hearts (IL-6: P = 0.286, IL-8: P = 0.643, CCL2: P = 0.095).

Inflammatory cytokines measured by 15-Plex Luminex immunoassay in an independent cohort of 3 hearts donated after brain death (DBD) and 6 additional hearts after circulatory death. Cytokine levels were compared to time on ex-vivo perfusion assessed by linear regression. IL-6, IL-8, and CCL2 demonstrated the strongest correlation with time on ex-vivo perfusion. Inflammatory profiles were similar between DBD and DCD hearts.

To assess whether cytokine induction was attributable to DCD-associated warm ischemia, we performed linear regression using warm ischemic time as the predictor and cytokine levels as outcomes. No significant associations were observed at either perfusion timepoint, suggesting that EVHP—rather than ischemic exposure—was the primary driver of the observed inflammatory signal within the protocol-constrained WIT range ( < 30 min) (Suppl. Fig. 2).

Finally, the robust cytokine response was validated in a swine DCD model using our in-house EVHP system, demonstrating that key cytokine responses are reproducible in an independent experimental system. While CCL2 is not represented in the porcine Luminex panel, the most pronounced changes were observed for IL-6 (fold change [FC] 377.04, P = 1.45 × 10−6) and IL-8 (CXCL8; FC 855.25, P = 2.65 × 10−5), both of which increased by nearly three orders of magnitude from initial to end-of-perfusion samples (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Our study identifies three key findings regarding the immunologic effects of ex-vivo heart perfusion (EVHP) in human DCD donor hearts. First, EVHP induces a robust inflammatory response, marked by significant increases in IL-6, IL-8, and CCL2/MCP-1. Second, the induction of these cytokines is time-dependent and correlates strongly with perfusion duration, suggesting that EVHP itself, independent of warm ischemia, drives activation of proinflammatory networks. Third, pathway-level analyses reveal upregulation of innate immune responses—including monocyte recruitment, neutrophil chemotaxis, and interleukin-1 signaling.

Despite corticosteroids present in the perfusate, we observed approximately threefold increases in IL-6, IL-8, and CCL2. These cytokines are known mediators of leukocyte activation and trafficking: IL-8 promotes neutrophil recruitment and monocyte adhesion12; IL-6 and CCL2 are produced by both endothelial and immune cells and are involved in monocyte/macrophage mobilization13,14. Consistent with these roles, gene set enrichment analysis highlighted IL-1 signaling and leukocyte chemotaxis as dominant pathways upregulated during perfusion. These observations were replicated in an independent human cohort that included both DCD and brain-dead donor hearts, where similar inflammatory cytokine increases were observed using a different assay platform, and while CCL2 was not included in the swine panel, IL-6 and IL-8 increases were validated across species and in an independent experimental system using a swine DCD model on our in-house perfusion system. This consistency across technical and biological replication supports the robustness and generalizability of our findings.

Our findings are consistent with prior data from ex vivo lung and kidney perfusion, where similar inflammatory cytokines—including IL-6, IL-8, and CCL2—were elevated in perfusate and tissue samples. Transcriptomic profiling of human lungs on ex-vivo perfusion revealed marked upregulation of CCL2 (MCP-1) and CXCL8 (IL-8) in endothelial and immune cells15, with protein-level confirmation of IL-8 elevation16. Similarly, ex vivo kidney perfusion in a porcine model led to increased perfusate concentrations of IL-6 and IL-8, along with other cytokines17. These parallels suggest that innate immune activation may be a shared response across organ types during normothermic perfusion.

Importantly, we found no correlation between cytokine levels and warm ischemic time, arguing against warm ischemia as the primary driver of the observed immune response. While ischemia-reperfusion injury is known to activate similar pathways, the proportional relationship between cytokine levels and perfusion duration suggests a distinct contribution from the EVHP process itself. This was further supported by increases in cardiac troponin and IL-1 signaling that did not correlate with perfusion time, suggesting different underlying mechanisms.

These findings may help explain the higher rates of acute rejection and graft dysfunction observed in DCD heart recipients. Whether these cytokine responses are adaptive or maladaptive remains unknown, but they raise the possibility that immune modulation during EVHP could improve post-transplant outcomes. Indeed, prior work in a porcine model demonstrated that cytokine adsorption during EVHP improved contractility of DCD hearts18. Clinical use of an adsorption filter during ex-vivo lung perfusion demonstrated decreased cytokines (IL-6, IL-10, CCL2/MCP-1, and GCSF) with improved in-hospital and 1-year mortality after transplantation19. The clinical impact of cytokine accumulation during ex-vivo perfusion and its potential modulation require further investigation.

This study has limitations. Our cohort lacked a non-perfused DCD control group, as static cold storage does not permit comparable sampling. Similarly, in the swine experiments, there is no sham group or non-perfused group, for the same reason. In comparing DCD and DBD hearts, we are limited by the small sample size of our DBD cohort, as it is less common for DBD hearts to undergo EVHP in the clinical setting. In our targeted validation, the swine Luminex panel does not include CCL2, our top protein, though it does include our other top cytokines (IL-6 and IL-8). Sample size was limited, and future studies should incorporate mechanistic animal models and examine whether pharmacologic modulation of inflammation during EVHP can improve graft performance.

In conclusion, EVHP represents an important advancement to preserve, reanimate, and evaluate donor organs, allowing for expansion of the donor pool and utilization of otherwise undesirable organs. However, the effect of EVHP itself on the heart remains poorly understood, and early studies demonstrate potentially increased morbidity including acute rejection and primary graft dysfunction. Our study demonstrates that EVHP leads to a robust inflammatory response, characterized by significant increases in IL-6, IL-8, and CCL2, along with proinflammatory pathways involving innate immune activation and monocyte/macrophage recruitment. Further work is needed to delineate adaptive versus maladaptive immune responses and to understand the role of the inflammatory response on transplanted organ function.

Methods

Direct procurement of organs

The study includes human donor hearts accepted by our institution for transplantation and procured via direct procurement and perfusion (DPP). Our institution’s full DCD procurement protocol has previously been published20. Under DPP protocol, 30,000 IU of heparin is administered prior to extubation. After extubation, determination of circulatory death, and a “no touch” interval of 2–5 min, sternotomy is performed followed by rapid drainage of 1–1.5 L of donor blood from the right atrium. The donor heart is flushed with cold del Nido preservation solution, and cardiectomy is performed. The heart is then instrumented and perfused on the FDA-approved Organ Care System (OCS) machine and reperfused using sub-normothermic (34 °C), leukocyte-reduced whole donor blood1. A Priming solution, including 250 mg of methylprednisolone and 10,000 IU of heparin, as well as Maintenance solution is also added to the blood to make up the perfusate21. All human donor hearts were successfully transplanted.

Study design

This study was approved by the Massachusetts General Hospital Institutional Review Board (protocol #2022P000405, approved on March 1, 2022) and complies with all relevant ethical regulations in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent for recipient data was obtained from all recipients. Informed consent for the collection of blood samples from donor hearts on OCS was obtained from donor families. In association with EVHP of 20 human DCD hearts on the OCS system, perfusate samples were drawn from the OCS circuit at two timepoints: an early timepoint (ranging 0–2 h) and at end-perfusion (ranging 3–6 h). Donor and recipient clinical characteristics (demographics, comorbidities, infection status, etc.) were recorded in a deidentified intramural heart transplant database (IRB protocol #2017P001969, approved on September 29, 2017).

Sample processing

Samples were stored in room air and transported using 10 mL BD Vacutainer CPT Sodium Citrate tubes. Plasma was separated from peripheral blood cells using centrifugation at 1500 relative centrifugal force for 15 min at room temperature, and stored at −80 °C.

Proteomic Profiling

The SOMAScan aptamer-based assay has previously been described with clinical validity and analytic accuracy in recent studies for high-throughput proteomic analysis22–25. SOMAScan uses synthetic DNA aptamers that bind specific protein targets; the aptamers are the quantified by microarray, and signal intensity is used as a proxy for protein abundance. Using this platform, perfusate samples were sent for proteomic profiling using the 11 K SOMAScan assay (SOMAlogic, Boulder, CO), which includes 11,037 protein analytes corresponding with 10,070 distinct human proteins. Protein abundance is reported in SOMAmer reagent relative fluorescence units (RFU). Assay data are normalized according to standard SOMAScan protocol26.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using R software27 and SOMAScan Data Delve Statistics Tool. Protein RFU values were log10-transformed and Z-score calculated for statistical testing. Paired student t-test was performed comparing end-timepoint values to initial-timepoint values to determine significant protein level changes as a result of EVHP. Fold change (ratio of RFU values between end and initial timepoints) was calculated and log2-transformed. Spearman’s test was performed as a nonparametric measure of correlation between individual protein levels and time on EVHP (range 0–6 h). False discovery rate (FDR) was calculated from p-values using the R package qvalue, with FDR ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), using the R fgsea package, and Panther overrepresentation analysis were performed for Gene Ontology biological processes28,29. Ranking was determined by -log10(p-value) multiplied by log2(fold change) for each protein, using statistically significant proteins for GSEA and overrepresentation analysis.

Targeted Technical Validation (Human Hearts)

Perfusate samples from an additional 9 donor hearts (3 brain dead donors, and 6 DCD) were obtained and processed as described above. Serum samples were sent for analysis using the Luminex Human Focused 15-Plex Discovery Assay (Eve Technologies, Calgary, Canada) for targeted validation of key cytokine responses using a different assay platform. Cytokine levels were analyzed in duplicates, averaged, and compared to time on EVHP using linear regression. To assess potential differences in cytokine responses between DCD and DBD hearts, we compared protein changes from initial to end perfusion in each cohort and used Wilcoxon rank sum testing to determine statistically significant differences.

Targeted Biological Validation (Swine Hearts)

Targeted validation was performed in swine hearts as a separate mammalian model system, providing biological replication. Hearts from six Yorkshire swine (30–35 kg, age 3–4 months, mixed sex) were procured using our previously described DCD protocol30. Yorkshire swine were chosen for the anatomical and physiological similarity to the human heart for ex-vivo perfusion31,32. Under our previously described protocol, sternotomy was performed and 300 IU of heparin was administered. Following extubation, 19 minutes of functional warm ischemic time was observed (MAP < 30 mmHg). The heart was vented and flushed with 1 L of del Nido cardioplegia. Cardiotomy was performed in standard fashion, hearts were instrumented and placed on the normothermic machine perfusion system. Hearts were perfused under Langendorff conditions using a base perfusate containing 0.96% Krebs-Henseleit Buffer, 0.02% hydrocortisone (50 mg/mL), 0.5% heparin (1000 U/mL), and 1 L of isolated red blood cells (1:2) among other additives30. Perfusate samples were collected after 30 minutes and 6 hours of perfusion. Pro‑inflammatory cytokines were measured using the porcine Luminex 13‑Plex Discovery Assay (Eve Technologies, Calgary, Canada) for targeted validation of cytokine results. Cytokine concentrations at 30 min versus 6 h were compared using paired t-test. An alpha level of 0.0045 was considered significant, using Bonferroni correction for multiple hypothesis testing.
