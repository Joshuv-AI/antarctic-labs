Water-mediated interfacial modulation for enhanced selectivity in nitroarene hydrogenation over Pt/Fe2O3 catalysts
Authors: Anqi Li, Lin Li, Xiaoli Pan, Hongying Zhuo, Xiaofeng Yang, Aiqin Wang, Tao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The selective hydrogenation of nitroarenes containing multiple reducible groups to aromatic amines is of considerable importance in fine chemical synthesis. Despite extensive efforts being devoted to catalyst design, the role of water, ubiquitous as a byproduct and a common solvent in these reactions, remains greatly underexplored. In this work, a well-defined PtNP/Fe2O3-sheet model catalyst, featuring uniformly dispersed Pt nanoparticles (NPs) on the (001) facet of Fe2O3 nanoplates, was synthesized to specifically elucidate the effect of water on nitroarene hydrogenation. The selectivity for 3-aminostyrene exhibits a marked enhancement in the presence of water, reaching over 98% at an optimal 10 vol% water content. This interfacial blocking effect promotes preferential hydrogenation of the nitro-group over the low-valent Fe sites at the Pt–Fe2O3 interface. These findings provide atomic-level insights into the key role of water in modulating catalytic performance, offering a distinctive way of optimizing solvents by leveraging the interfacial blocking of water.

Introduction

The selective hydrogenation of nitroarenes with multiple reducible groups to aromatic amines, which are key intermediates in the production of pharmaceuticals, pigments, and polymers, has attracted considerable interest due to its high efficiency and environmental friendliness.1–5 IB-group metals (e.g. Au, Ag) can achieve high chemoselectivity for nitro reduction, but suffer from low hydrogenation activity, often requiring harsh reaction conditions (elevated temperatures and pressures) that limit practical applications.6–8 In contrast, Pt-group metals (PGMs) provide superior hydrogenation activity but often cause indiscriminate reduction of multiple functionalities. To address this selectivity–activity trade-off, substantial efforts have been devoted to enhancing the chemoselectivity of PGM-catalyzed nitro hydrogenation. Effective strategies including heteroatom doping,9–14 alloying or single-atom dispersion of PGMs,15–20 and electronic/structure modulation by supports such as Fe2O3 (ref. 21) have been proved to suppress undesired side reactions and promote selective nitro reduction.

In nitroarene hydrogenation, water serves as both a key byproduct and a common solvent; unfortunately, the effect of water in modulating the chemoselectivity remains elusive or has even been ignored in past studies. In fact, there has been increasing evidence that water plays an important role in various catalytic processes22 through distinct mechanisms,23–29 including stabilizing reactants or transition states via co-adsorption and hydrogen-bonding, redirecting pathways through proton transfer and dynamic hydrogen-bond networks, and even directly participating in hydrogenation via water dissociation. For example, Zhao et al.23 compared furfural hydrogenation in cyclohexane and water over a supported Pd catalyst, and found that hydrogen can be taken up as a proton via surface charge separation and transferred in water, which lowers the activation barrier for CO hydrogenation and enhances selectivity toward furfuryl alcohol. Liu et al.25 reported that in the selective oxidation of methane to methanol on a CeO2–Cu2O catalyst, water-derived OH species occupy the active Ce sites, suppressing the O2 activation pathway that would convert CH4 to CO or CO2 and directing the conversion of CH4 to CH3OH.

Likewise, such water-induced modulation of catalytic reactivity has also been reported in nitro-group reduction reactions.30–38 For example, the introduction of a small amount of water into ethanol was found to markedly enhance the activity for the hydrogenation of p-chloronitrobenzene to p-chloroaniline over Ru/SiO2 catalysts,33 as well as for 3-nitrostyrene hydrogenation over Co–N–C catalysts.37 Moreover, Yang et al.36 demonstrated that trace amounts of water dramatically promote H2 dissociation and accelerate H/D exchange while simultaneously weakening nitrobenzene adsorption to mitigate the inhibitory surface binding, thereby improving the performance of Ru/DMSN-C3 in nitrobenzene hydrogenation to cyclohexylamine. In contrast, for metals supported on reducible oxides, the active sites for nitro-group adsorption are widely recognized to be associated with the oxygen vacancies (Ov) at the metal–support interface.39–44 However, the effect of water in steering chemoselectivity, particularly the underlying mechanism via modulation of interfacial catalytic behaviors, has rarely been addressed and remains poorly understood.

To elucidate the role of water in modulating the hydrogenation of 3-nitrostyrene at the metal–support interface, we herein synthesized a well-defined Pt/Fe2O3 catalyst with Pt nanoparticles uniformly dispersed on the (001) facet of Fe2O3 nanoplates. Both in situ generated and externally introduced water were found to markedly enhance the selectivity toward 3-aminostyrene. Combined kinetic analysis, in situ infrared spectroscopy, and theoretical calculations reveal that water dissociation occurs readily at the Pt–Fe2O3 interface, generating OH species that selectively block Pt sites responsible for –CC– hydrogenation while promoting preferential reduction of –NO2 groups to amines over the low-valent Fe sites adjacent to the Pt–Fe2O3 interface, demonstrating water-mediated interfacial modulation for enhanced selectivity in nitroarene hydrogenation over Pt/Fe2O3 catalysts.

Methods

Catalyst preparation

An Fe2O3 nanosheet support was synthesized by a hydrothermal method.45–49 In a typical synthesis process, 1.365 g of FeCl3·6H2O was dissolved in 50 mL ethanol, then 3.5 mL ultrapure water was added to the solution. Subsequently, 4 g of sodium acetate (CH3COONa) was added followed by stirring for 10 min. The resulting mixture was transferred into a 120 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and heated at 180 °C for 12 h. The solid was then recovered by centrifugation, washed with ultrapure water and ethanol, dried at 80 °C for 12 h, and calcined in a muffle furnace at 300 °C for 2 h to obtain the Fe2O3 nanosheet support, denoted as Fe2O3-sheet.

PtNP/Fe2O3-sheet was synthesized by a colloid-deposition method.50–52 A solution of H2PtCl6·6H2O (1 g) in ethylene glycol (50 mL) was mixed with 50 mL of a 0.5 mol per L NaOH solution in ethylene glycol. The mixture was then heated to 140 °C under a flow of Ar with vigorous stirring for 3 h to yield dark-brown colloidal Pt nanoparticles (3.77 mg PtNP per mL ethylene glycol). Afterward, 1 g of Fe2O3-sheet was mixed with 49 mL ethylene glycol, and then a certain amount of colloidal Pt nanoparticles was added with stirring at 80 °C for 4 h. The resulting precipitate was filtered and washed with 1 L hot water and then dried at 80 °C overnight and finally calcined at 400 °C for 6 h to obtain PtNP/Fe2O3-sheet.

Catalytic performance test

The chemoselective hydrogenation of 3-nitrostyrene was performed in a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave. Before the reaction test, the catalyst (40 mg) was pre-reduced in situ using 10 bar hydrogen in 1 mL ethanol at 80 °C for 1 h. Then, 3-nitrostyrene (0.55 mmol), internal standard n-octanol (100 µL) and 1 mL solvent (x mL water, (1 − x) mL ethanol) were added to the autoclave. Next, the reactor was successively purged with nitrogen and hydrogen five times to remove air, followed by being sealed and charged with 4 bar hydrogen. Then, the reaction was carried out at 40 °C in a water bath with vigorous stirring. The products were analyzed using a gas chromatograph (GC, Agilent 7890B) equipped with an HP-5 column and a flame ionization detector.

The conversion (conv.) of the substrate (3-NS: 3-nitrostyrene), the selectivity (sel.) of the product (3-AS: 3-aminostyrene, 3-ENB: 3-ethylnitrobenzene and 3-EA: 3-ethylaniline) and the turnover frequency (TOF) were calculated using the following equations:where Δn3-NS, n3-NS, n3-AS, n3-ENB, and n3-EA denote the consumed amount of 3-NS, the initial amount of 3-NS, the amount of target product 3-AS, the amount of byproduct 3-ENB, and the amount of byproduct 3-EA. nPt and t represent the exposed Pt sites in the catalyst and the total reaction time, respectively. It is noted that the present TOF values are normalized by the initial exposed Pt active sites of the fresh catalyst after pre-activation at 80 °C, without considering the dynamic change due to water blocking.

For the recycling experiments, the used catalyst was washed with ethanol several times followed by drying under a vacuum at room temperature before being submitted to the next run.

Characterization

X-ray diffraction analysis was performed on a PANalytical X' pert diffractometer equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 0.154 nm) with a scanning angle (2θ) of 10–90°, operated at 40 kV and 40 mA. ICP-OES measurements were conducted on an IRIS Intrepid II XSP instrument (Thermo Electron Corporation). Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface areas of the catalysts were measured by nitrogen adsorption at −196 °C using a Micromeritics ASAP 2460 instrument. High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) was conducted on a JEM-ARM300F instrument, while high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) was performed on a JEM-2100 instrument operating at 120 kV.

H2-TPR was performed on an Auto Chem II 2920 automatic catalyst characterization system. For each measurement, 120 mg of catalyst was loaded into a U-shaped quartz reactor and pretreated in Ar at 120 °C for 30 min to remove adsorbed carbonates and hydrates. After being cooled to −50 °C, the flowing gas was then switched to 10 vol% H2/Ar and the catalyst was heated to 600 °C at a rate of 10 °C min−1.

In situ infrared spectroscopy for CO adsorption on the samples was performed on a Bruker VERTEX70 spectrometer equipped with an MCT detector at a resolution of 4 cm−1. Before measurements, the catalyst samples in powder form were pre-reduced at 80 °C in a 30 vol% H2/He flow for 1 h, followed by cooling to room temperature in a He stream and then the background signal was collected. Afterwards, the CO/He was introduced into the cell until adsorption saturation and then the gas was switched to He to remove gaseous CO. The spectra were recorded in the range of 3000–1000 cm−1 during the whole process. For the in situ infrared spectroscopy measurements for 3-nirtrostyrene adsorption and hydrogenation, the catalyst samples were prereduced at 80 °C in a 30 vol% H2/He flow for 1 h, followed by evacuation. Afterwards, the samples were cooled to 40 °C under vacuum and then the background signal was collected. Subsequently, 3-nitrostyrene vapor was introduced into the reactor until saturation. After evacuation, the spectra for 3-nitrostyrene adsorption were recorded. To investigate the effect of water adsorption on 3-nitrostyrene adsorption, water vapor was introduced into the cell prior to 3-nitrostyrene. For hydrogenation, H2 was introduced after 3-nitrostyrene adsorption. The spectra were recorded during the whole adsorption and hydrogenation process.

DFT calculations

All the density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP 6.1.0).53,54 The PBE functional with generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was used to describe the electron exchange and correlation and the projector augmented wave (PAW) method was employed to describe the interactions between atomic cores and electrons.55,56 A Gamma grid of size 1 × 1 × 1 was used to sample the surface Brillouin zone. The energy cutoff for the plane-wave expansion was set as 400 eV. The convergence criterion for the self-consistent electronic minimization was set as 10−5 eV and the ground-state atomic geometries were obtained by minimizing the forces on the atoms to below 0.02 eV Å−1. The calculations were carried out with the DFT + U method. The transition states were obtained by relaxing the force below 0.03 eV Å−1 by using the DIMER method.57

The PtNP/Fe2O3-sheet catalyst was modeled using a Pt10 cluster supported on an oxygen-terminated α-Fe2O3 (001) surface. The support was represented by a 3 × 3 supercell consisting of four Fe layers and three O layers. To maintain charge neutrality, the bottom O layer was passivated with hydrogen atoms. A vacuum gap of 20 Å was introduced along the z-direction to prevent spurious interactions between periodic images. During geometry optimization, the bottom O layer and the two underlying Fe layers were fixed at their bulk positions, while all other atoms were fully relaxed. The Pt10 cluster was deposited on the surface and one oxygen atom adjacent to the cluster was removed to simulate the oxygen vacancy generated under reduction conditions.

Results and discussion

Iron oxide with uniform hexagonal nanoplates and an average width of 230 nm and a thickness of 25 nm was synthesized via the hydrothermal method as previously reported.45–49 High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images and the corresponding Fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns reveal lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.25 nm, consistent with the (110) and (2−10) planes, indicating the predominant exposure of the basal (001) plane (Fig. 1a). The preformed Pt NPs with an average size of 1.9 nm were found to be preferentially located on the (001) surface (Fig. 1a, b and S1). The absence of diffraction peaks of Pt species in the XRD patterns (Fig. S2) confirms the high dispersion of Pt. Hydrogen temperature-programmed reduction (H2-TPR) and chemisorption were performed to investigate the reduction behavior of PtNP/Fe2O3-sheet. Notably, the presence of PtNP greatly facilitates the reduction of Fe2O3 to Fe3O4, lowering the reduction temperature from 333 °C to 230 °C (Fig. 1c). More interestingly, a new peak appears at a much lower temperature of 40 °C, which is attributed to the reduction of oxidized Pt species together with adjacent interfacial Fe2O3. The corresponding H2 consumption reaches 9.4 µmol per µmolPt-total (Table S1) which is approximately 4.7 times higher than that required for PtOx reduction alone, indicating concomitant reduction of the interfacial Fe sites to low-valent Fe. The hydrogen chemisorption experiments show that, after pre-reduction by H2 at 80 °C, the catalyst exhibits a H/Pt ratio of 3.6, consistent with the reduction of Pt accompanied by hydrogen spillover to neighboring FeOx species. Accordingly, a pre-reduction temperature of 80 °C was chosen prior to catalytic testing in the selective hydrogenation of 3-nitrostyrene (Fig. 2a).

The role of water in the catalytic performances of 3-nitrostyrene hydrogenation was then explored by using the PtNP/Fe2O3-sheets as a model catalyst. Notably, the reduction of the nitro-group to 3-aminostyrene inherently generates water as a byproduct. To investigate its influence, the time-evolution of the reaction products was monitored. As shown in Fig. 2b, the selectivity in ethanol was ∼60% at the initial stage of the reaction, which was found to increase sharply with time at conversions below 15%, and then plateaued at around 90%. Such a trend might arise from the kinetic change associated with variations in substrate concentrations. To exclude this possibility, the concentration of 3-nitrostyrene was varied from 0.28 mol L−1 to 0.20 mol L−1 under the comparable water-generation conditions corresponding to the initial conversions, and the selectivity toward 3-aminostyrene remained nearly constant (Fig. S3a). The variations in H2 pressure also have minimal impact on the chemoselective reduction of the nitro-group (Fig. S3b). These observations imply that the trace amounts of in situ generated water may play a decisive role in promoting the selective hydrogenation of the nitro-group. Indeed, the initial selectivity is significantly enhanced upon introducing water as a co-solvent. As shown in Fig. 2c, the reaction immediately achieves 97% selectivity for 3-aminostyrene and maintained excellent stability over time. High selectivity (>95%) is preserved over a wide range of water content (5–37.5 vol%), with an optimal value exceeding 98% at 10 vol% H2O (200 µL) (Fig. 2d). Nevertheless, increasing water fraction leads to a decrease in the hydrogenation activity. Specifically, the turnover frequency (TOF) decreased from 11 058 h−1 to 6900 h−1 at 10 vol% water content in ethanol solvent (Fig. S4). In addition, the stability and reusability of our PtNP/Fe2O3-sheet catalyst were assessed in 10 vol% water/ethanol (Fig. S5). The conversion remained nearly constant at approximately 60%, while the selectivity toward 3-aminostyrene consistently exceeded 97% throughout all four cycles. Correspondingly, XRD analysis (Fig. S2) confirms that the α-phase of Fe2O3 remains unchanged under all the tested conditions, and the Pt particles exhibited negligible variation in size distributions during the reaction (Fig. S6), indicating the robust resistance of Pt active sites against deactivation under the reaction conditions. Overall, these findings demonstrate that the presence of water, both as an in situ generated byproduct and as a solvent during the nitrostyrene hydrogenation, affords enhanced selectivity toward 3-aminostyrene with excellent stability, albeit at the expense of overall hydrogenation activity.

To elucidate the catalytic mechanism underlying the water-dependent reactivity of PtNP/Fe2O3 in the 3-nitrostyrene hydrogenation, the reaction orders with respect to H2 and 3-nitrostyrene were determined in the absence and presence of trace water in a solvent. As shown in Fig. 3a and b, the introduction of water increases the positive reaction order of H2 from 1.41 to 1.70, indicating that water suppresses the rate of H2 activation over PtNP/Fe2O3 catalysts, and consequently renders the overall rate more dependent on H2 pressure. In contrast, the reaction order for 3-nitrostyrene in ethanol was measured to be −0.24, implying that the higher substrate concentrations inhibit the reaction due to its strong chemisorption on the catalyst surface. This inhibitory effect is further amplified by the presence of water, with the apparent reaction order for 3-nitrostyrene decreasing to −0.33, indicative of a more competitive adsorption of 3-nitrostyrene on the catalyst surface under aqueous conditions. Furthermore, the apparent activation energy was found to decrease from 32.8 kJ mol−1 in ethanol to 25.4 kJ mol−1 in the mixed solvent (10 vol% water) (Fig. S7), indicating that water intrinsically promotes hydrogenation activity, whereas the lower overall activities suggest that water may block active sites for 3-nitrostyrene hydrogenation. Kinetic isotope effect (KIE) measurements (Fig. 3c) provide further mechanistic insight. The H2O/D2O KIE is essentially unity (1.03), indicating that water does not directly participate in the rate-determining step, although it may still influence the activation and reaction of H2 and 3-nitrostyrene. By comparison, the H2/D2 KIE increases from 1.24 in ethanol to 1.72 upon the addition of 10 vol% water, indicating that H2 activation (or H-involving elementary steps) becomes more kinetically significant in the presence of water.58–60

The role of water in regulating the selectivity between nitro-group and –CC– bond hydrogenation was further examined by using nitrobenzene and styrene as separate probe substrates. As shown in Fig. 3d, the introduction of 10 vol% water results in only a moderate decrease in the TOF for nitrobenzene hydrogenation (∼38%, from 6780 h−1 to 4140 h−1). In contrast, styrene hydrogenation is drastically suppressed, with the TOF decreased by ∼99.7%. This striking disparity highlights the preferential inhibition of –CC– hydrogenation by water. Such suppression of alkene hydrogenation is also supported by kinetic measurements. As shown in Fig. S8, the reaction order with respect to H2 increases from 1.18 to 1.45 for nitrobenzene hydrogenation and from 1.26 to 2.02 for styrene hydrogenation upon 10% water addition. These results indicate that H2O impedes H2 dissociation kinetics, with a substantially stronger impact on the H2 activation as well as styrene hydrogenation. This pronounced difference likely arises from the involvement of distinct active sites for nitro-group and –CC– bond hydrogenation in nitrostyrene, with water preferentially blocking the sites responsible for alkene hydrogenation.

CO-DRIFTS was employed to probe the nature of active sites on PtNP/Fe2O3-sheet. As shown in Fig. 4a, the fresh PtNP/Fe2O3-sheet catalyst exhibits CO vibrational bands at 2110, 2073, and 2044 cm−1, which are assigned to linearly adsorbed CO on Ptδ+ sites at the Pt–Fe2O3 interface, on metallic Pt0 sites and on low-coordination Ptδ− sites on the Pt nanoparticles, respectively.61–64 In addition, a broad band at 1847 cm−1 is observed, corresponding to the bridge-bonded CO species on Pt nanoparticles. The coexistence of Ptδ+ and metallic Pt species is further corroborated by XPS analysis. As shown in Fig. S9, the Pt 4f7/2 peak can be deconvoluted into two peaks at 72.8 eV and 71.6 eV, corresponding to the Ptδ+ and Pt0 species,65 respectively, with the Ptδ+ species accounting for approximately 23% of the total Pt. After reaction in ethanol solvent, the relative intensity of the Pt0-associated band at 2073 cm−1 decreases markedly while that of the band at 2110 cm−1 remains unchanged, suggesting partial coverage of Pt0 sites by the adsorbed reactants or intermediates. Notably, when the reaction was performed in an ethanol/water mixture, the CO adsorption band at 2073 cm−1 further diminished, while the band at 2110 cm−1 disappeared completely. This observation indicates that water preferentially blocks Ptδ+ sites at the Pt–Fe2O3 interface.

Such an interfacial blocking mechanism by H2O-derived OH is further demonstrated by in situ FT-IR spectroscopy during 3-nitrostyrene hydrogenation over PtNP/Fe2O3-sheet. As shown in Fig. 4b, the adsorption of 3-nitrostyrene produces two bands at 1533 and 1350 cm−1, corresponding to the asymmetric and symmetrical stretching vibrations of the ONO group,7,9,66 respectively, along with a band at 1614 cm−1 attributed to the –CC– stretching vibration.17,67 Upon introduction of H2, this band red-shifts to 1608 cm−1, which can be attributed to the adsorption of H species on Pt, leading to reduced surface coverage of –CC– groups and enhanced d-π-donation. With continued H2 exposure, the bands of vs(NO2) and vas(NO2) decline rapidly, accompanied by the emergence of bands at 1492 cm−1 (–NH2) and 1463 cm−1 (–NOH), indicative of nitro-group hydrogenation. Meanwhile, a gradual attenuation of the v(CC) band is observed, although the overall signal intensity increases owing to the formation of water.

In contrast, when the catalyst was pre-exposed to water prior to H2 introduction (Fig. 4c), the v(CC) band red-shifts to 1607 cm−1, indicating reduced –CC– surface coverage as a result of Pt site blocking by the adsorbed water. Additionally, the relative intensity of the vs(NO2) band (1346 cm−1) is significantly weaker than that of vas(NO2) (1533 cm−1), consistent with partial N–O bond cleavage in –NO2 to generate a nitroso intermediate, as reported previously.17,68 Upon introduction of hydrogen under these conditions, the nitro groups (1533 and 1346 cm−1) diminish, while the v(CC) band shows negligible attenuation, demonstrating that water effectively suppresses the hydrogenation of –CC– groups. These results provide direct evidence for a water-induced interfacial site-blocking mechanism that governs the selective hydrogenation of 3-nitrostyrene over PtNP/Fe2O3-sheet.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were finally performed to validate the proposed water-derived interfacial blocking mechanism. A Pt10 nanocluster anchored on the oxygen-terminated Fe2O3(001) surface with a neighboring oxygen vacancy was employed as the model system. This model captures the partially reduced Pt–Fe2O3 interface in the practical PtNP/Fe2O3-sheet catalyst, while also accounting for the facile formation of adjacent oxygen-vacancy (Ov) sites due to their relatively low formation energy (Fig. S10). Three types of Pt sites potentially involved in hydrogenation were considered, including the low-coordinated Pt site (Ptδ−) and the metallic Pt site (Pt0) on the nanocluster, as well as the oxidized Pt site (Ptδ+) with the neighboring oxygen vacancy (Ov) at the Pt–Fe2O3 interface. Bader charge analysis yields charges of −0.12|e|, 0.05|e|, and +0.36|e| for these different Pt sites, respectively. Correspondingly, the CO vibrational frequency decreases with increasing electron density on Pt (Table S2), in good agreement with the CO-DRIFTS results, thereby confirming multiple Pt coordination environments.

As shown in Fig. 5a, H2 dissociation occurs on both the Pt nanocluster and Pt–Fe2O3 interface of Pt10/Fe2O3(001), with exothermic energies of −1.67 eV and −2.98 eV, respectively, indicating that the interface serves as the primary site for H2 activation (Fig. S11). The resulting H species on the Pt nanocluster are thermodynamically driven to spill over to interfacial FeOx, with an energy preference of 1.91 eV (Fig. S12), consistent with the enhanced H2 uptakes observed in H2-chemisorption experiments. As for 3-nitrostyrene, it prefers to be adsorbed on the Pt nanocluster via the –CC– or the aromatic ring, with adsorption energies as high as −2.02 and −3.06 eV, respectively (Fig. 5b). Such strong binding effectively blocks Pt surface sites, in line with the experimentally observed negative reaction order with respect to 3-nitrostyrene and the positive dependence on H2 pressure. In contrast, adsorption through the –NO2 groups on Pt sites is significantly weaker, with exothermic energies of only −1.35 eV. Nevertheless, a distinct adsorption mode of the –NO2 group is identified on Fe sites adjacent to oxygen vacancies, with an adsorption energy of −0.75 eV (Fig. 5c). These results indicate that Pt sites in PtNP/Fe2O3 predominantly catalyze –CC– hydrogenation, accounting for the intrinsically low selectivity toward nitro-group reduction, whereas –NO2 activation preferentially occurs at the low-valent Fe sites neighboring oxygen vacancies at the Pt–Fe2O3 interface.

In the presence of water, the adsorption and dissociation of H2O on Pt10/Fe2O3−x(001) are expected to play a decisive role in 3-nitrostyrene hydrogenation. As shown in Fig. 5d, the most favorable pathway involving the spontaneous dissociation of H2O was observed at the Pt–Fe2O3 interface, with a large exothermicity of −3.19 eV, exceeding that of both H2 and 3-nitrostyrene adsorption. Meanwhile, the resulting OH species preferentially accumulate at the perimeter of the Pt cluster (Fig. S13), forming Pt–OH moieties that effectively block interfacial Ptδ+. This site blocking suppresses both –CC– adsorption and H2 activation, accounting for the reduced overall reactivity and the diminished propensity for –CC– hydrogenation. However, the adsorption of the –NO2 group at the Fe sites remains essentially unaffected by the Pt–OH species. As a result, the reaction preferentially proceeds via Ov-associated Fe sites, where nitro-group activation is preserved. Overall, these results demonstrate that water-derived interfacial hydroxylation selectively passivates Pt active sites while retaining Ov-mediated –NO2 activation, thereby greatly suppressing CC hydrogenation and enhancing nitro-group selectivity.

Accordingly, in the hydrogenation of 3-nitrostyrene, water is far more than an inert medium or a passive byproduct. Instead, it actively participates in the reaction network, exerting a profound influence on both catalytic activity and chemoselectivity. At the mechanistic level, its presence lowers the overall apparent activation barrier, indicating that water intrinsically promotes hydrogenation activity.23,69,70 However, this promotional role is complicated by the pronounced tendency of water to undergo dissociative chemisorption at the metal–support interface of the PtNP/Fe2O3 catalysts. Specifically, water readily dissociates at the interface, generating hydroxyl species that occupy the adjacent coordinatively unsaturated Pt sites at interfaces. This interfacial hydroxyl coverage introduces a critical trade-off between activity and selectivity. On the one hand, the occupation of Pt active centers by these OH adsorbates directly attenuates the available surface sites for hydrogen dissociation, resulting in a decreased turnover frequency when normalized to the exposed Pt surface area. Specifically, our TOF values are normalized by the initial Pt sites estimated by the Pt nanoparticle sizes. However, because water preferentially adsorbs and occupies a fraction of these Pt sites, the actual number of accessible Pt active sites for substrate activation is inevitably lower than this initial estimate, thereby leading to an underestimated apparent TOF. On the other hand, the PtNP/Fe2O3 catalyst exhibits a distinct functional-group discrimination, wherein the –CC– moieties preferentially adsorb onto Pt terraces and step edges via π-backbonding, whereas the nitro-groups show a strong affinity for the electron-rich Ov-associated Fe sites through Lewis acid–base interactions. The selective uptake of water-derived species on the Pt surface effectively creates a steric blockade that impedes the adsorption and activation of the –CC– bonds. Crucially, this site-blocking effect does not extend to the Fe–Ov sites, leaving the activation of the nitro functionality largely unperturbed. As a consequence, the competitive hydrogenation of the –CC– bond is selectively suppressed, while the hydrogenation of the nitro group proceeds preferentially. Ultimately, the presence of water markedly enhances the chemoselectivity toward the desired product 3-aminostyrene, underscoring its indispensable role in steering the reaction pathway within this bifunctional catalytic system.

Conclusions

In this study, we elucidate the role of H2O in modulating the hydrogenation of 3-nitrostyrene over a well-defined PtNP/Fe2O3-sheet catalyst featuring uniformly dispersed Pt nanoparticles on the (001) facet of Fe2O3 nanosheets. The presence of H2O was demonstrated to markedly enhance chemoselectivity toward the reduction of the –NO2 group, achieving >98% selectivity to 3-aminostyrene at an optimal 10 vol% H2O, accompanied by a moderate decrease in overall hydrogenation activity and a corresponding change in reaction kinetics. Mechanistically, H2O induces an interfacial blocking effect via competitive adsorption and facile dissociation at the Pt–Fe2O3 interface. The resulting hydroxyl species selectively occupy interfacial Pt sites, thereby suppressing H2 activation and –CC– adsorption. In contrast, nitro-group hydrogenation occurring at the low-valent Fe sites adjacent to oxygen vacancies remains largely unaffected, thereby favoring hydrogenation of the –NO2 group albeit with a trade-off in overall hydrogenation activity. This work provides an atomic-level insight into how water, either as a by-product or a solvent, governs the reactivity and selectivity of nitroaromatic hydrogenation over Pt/Fe2O3 catalysts. These findings offer a general mechanism for understanding water-mediated interfacial modulation, and provide important guidance for catalyst design and process optimization in chemoselective hydrogenation reactions.
