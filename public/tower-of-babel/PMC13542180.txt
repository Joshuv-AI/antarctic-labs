Confidence-aware semi-supervised siamese graph networks for semantic text similarity in low-resource Urdu
Authors: Anil Ahmed, Lei Guo, Degen Huang, Salahuddin Unar, Mobeen Nazar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate detection of semantically similar texts underpins applications such as plagiarism detection and content recommendation, yet remains challenging in low-resource languages like Urdu due to limited labeled data and noisy annotations. We introduce Siamese Graph-Based Semi-Supervised Learning (SGSSL), a framework that expands training diversity through back-translation and masked-word prediction, and learns from unlabeled data via confidence-aware pseudo-labeling with consistency regularization. At its core, SGSSL employs a Siamese Graph Neural Network that fuses Graph Convolutional Networks and Graph Attention Networks to capture fine-grained relational signals between sentences. A bottleneck feature-refinement layer and a Transformer Encoder with multi-head self-attention mitigate GNN over-smoothing while modeling long-range dependencies. We further leverage Urdu RoBERTa embeddings for deep contextual semantics. Our hybrid confidence-based filtering integrates probabilistic confidence, semantic similarity, and model uncertainty to select high-quality pseudo-labels. Evaluated on six benchmark datasets for text reuse and paraphrase detection (binary and ternary classification), SGSSL consistently outperforms strong baselines. On the USTRC dataset, SGSSL attains an accuracy of 0.722, an 8.1% absolute gain over the best baseline, demonstrating effectiveness for low-resource settings.

Introduction

Automatic paraphrase detection is a critical task in natural language processing (NLP), essential in identifying paraphrased content across different linguistic levels, from single words to entire sentences. It serves as a classification task, distinguishing between paraphrased, non-paraphrased, and partially paraphrased texts. This capability underpins a wide range of NLP applications, such as query ranking1,2, question answering3,4, document summarization 5,6, and plagiarism detection7,8. Among these, detecting paraphrased plagiarism, where text is reworded to maintain its original meaning, is especially important as plagiarism has become increasingly prevalent due to easy access to online content. The rise of plagiarism is a pressing concern in academic, publishing, and professional spheres. Surveys indicate that up to 90% of students have engaged in plagiarism, with 50% admitting to doing so in assignment submissions9,10. High-profile cases have been uncovered globally, including over 200 documented instances of academic plagiarism in Germany. A report also found that 66% of student work at leading U.S. universities contained plagiarized content. Furthermore, the use of AI-generated text in academic assignments is becoming more widespread, exacerbating the issue. Undetected plagiarism undermines academic integrity, leads to unearned professional recognition, and misallocates research funding. Consequently, detecting paraphrased plagiarism is critical yet challenging, especially given the surge of digital content and advanced AI tools, such as Jasper, Writesonic, and Rytr, which make paraphrasing and text generation more accessible. Alongside paraphrase detection (P-D), text reuse (T-R) has gained attention, focusing on intentionally recycling text to generate new intellectual content. T-R spans direct copying, paraphrasing, and more subtle forms of content reuse. Local reuse integrates existing text into new content, often intersecting with P-D tasks. In contrast, global reuse, which involves detecting nearly identical documents across various locations, is crucial for duplication detection and version control tasks. T-R and P-D are complex due to the vast variability in linguistic expression, driving ongoing research in NLP, particularly in multilingual contexts.

Despite advancements in NLP for languages such as English and European languages, there is a significant gap in resources for under-resourced languages like Urdu. Urdu’s intricate morphology, rich vocabulary, and flexible syntax introduce challenges for automated processing tasks, including T-R and P-D. As a derivative of Hindustani, Urdu allows flexible word order, which leads to ambiguities in computational text analysis. While Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) have shown great promise in Urdu language processing11–15, their performance relies heavily on large annotated datasets, which are often unavailable for under-resourced languages. Traditional models and DNNs require significant labeled data, but acquiring such data is costly and time-intensive.

In order to address the above problems, leveraging semi-supervised learning (SSL) approaches has emerged as a promising strategy. SSL utilizes limited labeled data in combination with abundant unlabeled data, reducing dependency on large annotated corpora16. Additionally, mitigating overfitting, which is a common challenge when working with small datasets, can be achieved by augmenting the training data. This study proposes an innovative approach to tackle the challenges of P-D and T-R in Urdu, a language that has not been sufficiently explored in this context.

This study proposes a novel Siamese graph-based semi-supervised learning (SGSSL) approach that integrates Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) and Graph Attention Networks (GAT). Our method leverages the strengths of GCN and GAT to enhance textual data representation, focusing specifically on T-R and P-D tasks in Urdu. These tasks are crucial in linguistic research, aiming to discern semantic similarities and differences between sentences. Our model incorporates Transformer Encoder (TE) modules enriched with MHSA, capturing intricate language patterns and dependencies across varying sentence lengths and complex content in Urdu texts. The integration of the TE helps mitigate the over-smoothing issue in GCN and GAT, as suggested in a similar study17,18. We utilize a bottleneck layer to consolidate features extracted by GCN and GAT before inputting them into the TE module. This structured integration boosts computational efficiency and enhances classification accuracy by effectively leveraging semantic relationships encoded in the graph structure.

Furthermore, we employ advanced Data Augmentation (DA) techniques such as Back Translation (BT) and Masked Word Prediction (MWP) to enrich the training corpus and improve model robustness against variations in linguistic expression. This addresses a significant research gap in Urdu NLP, where prior studies have not systematically explored their impact on enhancing classification performance. We employ a hybrid confidence-based filtering (HCBF) strategy to tackle noise in pseudo-labeled samples. This method integrates probabilistic confidence, semantic similarity, and model uncertainty to compute hybrid confidence scores. Samples exceeding a predefined confidence threshold are retained for subsequent training, ensuring the inclusion of high-confidence pseudo-labeled data. We implement consistency regularization to bolster model robustness against minor input perturbations and mitigate the influence of noisy labels. Experimental evaluations on benchmark datasets underscore the efficacy of our approach, demonstrating superior performance compared to existing methods in T-R and P-D tasks, relying on pre-trained contextual embedding. Related works

In recent years, DNNs have significantly advanced NLP, particularly in tasks like T-R and P-D, with notable progress in high-resource languages such as English. This progress has been driven by the development of sophisticated models and the availability of large datasets. However, advancements remain limited for under-resourced languages like Urdu due to the scarcity of annotated corpora, as shown in Table 1. The proliferation of machine-generated content, especially through models like GPT-4, has amplified the need for advanced T-R and P-D techniques. P-D, crucial for detecting semantic equivalence, is essential for maintaining originality amid the increasing use of AI-generated text. Recent research leveraging the Turku Paraphrase Corpus has shown that span-based models outperform sentence-level methods like BERT and SBERT, particularly in token-level F1 scores, enhancing paraphrase retrieval in low-resource settings19. Despite these advances, challenges persist in distinguishing human-generated from machine-generated paraphrases, as the former tend to exhibit greater semantic complexity. While modern transformer models like BERT and T5 surpass traditional approaches, the need for more sophisticated machine-generated datasets remains, particularly for under-resourced languages like Urdu.

Short text similarity and classification have been widely explored in Natural Language Processing, particularly due to challenges such as limited context, ambiguity, and sparse features. Traditional machine learning approaches, including Support Vector Machines and Random Forests, rely on handcrafted features but often fail to capture deep semantic relationships.

Deep learning models have significantly improved performance by learning distributed representations. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), especially Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM), are widely used for capturing sequential dependencies in text. Faseeh et al. proposed a BiLSTM-based model with attention mechanisms and multiple pre-trained embeddings (GloVe, FastText, Paragram), demonstrating strong performance in duplicate question detection by effectively modeling semantic similarity20.

Hybrid architectures combining CNN and RNN have also shown promising results. CNNs extract local spatial features, while LSTMs capture long-range dependencies. In this direction, Alkaabi et al. introduced a dual attention-based hybrid deep learning framework that integrates CNN and BiLSTM with location-based and co-attention mechanisms to improve feature extraction in short texts21. Their model addresses the limitations of short text by combining spatial and sequential representations, achieving high accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency.

Attention mechanisms have further enhanced model performance by focusing on important textual features. Various studies incorporate self-attention and cross-attention to improve semantic understanding. Additionally, knowledge-enhanced attention models utilize external information to enrich representations and handle ambiguity in short texts.

Recently, multimodal approaches have gained attention for leveraging complementary information beyond text. Jiang et al. proposed a dual-similarity enhanced hybrid orthogonal fusion framework that integrates textual and visual features using self-attention and cross-modal attention mechanisms22. This approach improves semantic representation and reduces feature redundancy, though challenges such as modality imbalance and semantic distortion remain. Ma et al.23 proposed an LLM-Enhanced Decoupled Learner and Course Modeling (LE-DLCM) framework for course recommendation, which introduces a dual-channel architecture to separately model learners and courses. Their approach utilizes knowledge-enhanced LLM-based course modeling to capture structural relationships among courses and interaction-enhanced learner modeling to address sparse user interactions. By incorporating similar learners and constructing course relation graphs, the model effectively mitigates cold-start and data sparsity issues, achieving significant improvements in recommendation performance. However, despite its effectiveness, the approach still faces challenges related to computational complexity, scalability, and reliance on prompt design for optimal performance.

Graph-based semi-supervised learning

Graph-based SSL has made significant strides, driving innovation across multiple domains by enhancing task performance through the effective use of both labeled and large amounts of unlabeled data. Recent advances, such as the integration of GCNs with deep learning techniques, have significantly boosted classification accuracy by maintaining a balance between global and local graph structures24. Additionally, the Dynamic Mutual Training method has further improved performance by refining noisy pseudo-labels. This approach leverages inter-model disagreements, proving beneficial in tasks like image classification and semantic segmentation25. However, despite these advances, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) remain vulnerable to adversarial attacks. The EpoAtk method addresses this issue by employing an exploratory strategy to strengthen the resilience of GNNs in semi-supervised node classification scenarios26. For graph-level classification, GraphSpa offers a cost-effective alternative to traditional graph kernels, efficiently selecting informative unlabeled graphs to improve classification outcomes27. Another challenge in semi-supervised GNNs is the over-smoothing problem, where node features become indistinguishable. To counter this, the Graph Alignment Neural Network preserves feature diversity and structural distinctiveness, ensuring better task performance28. Innovations in SSL have also included methods like ScanMix, which integrates SSL with semantic clustering to handle high-noise environments, and the Cross-modal Graph Convolutional Network (MCGCN), which employs adversarial learning to extract both modality-specific and shared information, particularly useful in multimedia retrieval tasks29. The Adaptive Graph Smoothing Network offers another key advancement, dynamically adjusting edge weights to improve node classification accuracy, achieving impressive results on citation and Wiki networks30. Among the newer approaches is the Semi-Supervised Graph Contrastive Learning (SGCL) algorithm, which combines supervised and unsupervised learning techniques with a Confidence Propagation Algorithm, significantly enhancing pseudo-label accuracy and setting new benchmarks31. For time series classification, SSL methods that rely on pseudo-labeling and consistency regularization often face difficulties in capturing effective temporal representations. Solutions such as bidirectional consistency and temporal-aware contrastive learning aim to enhance intra-class compactness and inter-class separability, resulting in improved classification performance32. Additionally, GraphixMatch, which integrates the FixMatch framework with both weak and strong graph augmentations, has emerged as a leading SSL approach33. Similarly, the Masked Dual-Temporal Autoencoder addresses label sparsity by effectively leveraging both labeled and unlabeled data for semi-supervised time series classification34. Recent advancements in semi-supervised node classification have sought to overcome persistent challenges such as over-smoothing, insufficient supervision, and the integration of global structural information. The Graph Convolutional Kernel Machine (GCKM), for instance, incorporates unsupervised kernel layers for feature propagation, outperforming traditional GNNs, especially in situations with limited labeled data35. Another innovative approach, Optimized Simple Graph Convolution (OGC), revisits the cluster assumption in graph-based learning, offering a solution to the over-smoothing problem36. HyperGraph Transformer (HyperGT) further advances the field by incorporating global information through Transformer-based architectures, addressing over-smoothing in hypergraph models37. To prevent representation collapse, Mixed Graph Contrastive Networks (MGCN) use interpolation-based augmentation, which significantly enhances the discriminative power of node embeddings38. Nazir et al. proposed an enhanced extractive text summarization framework for low-resourced Urdu language, integrating both supervised and unsupervised models with feature engineering and hybrid deep learning architectures, achieving notable improvements in ROUGE scores compared to prior methods39. Their work highlights the importance of combining linguistic features with deep learning; however, challenges such as dataset scarcity and language-specific complexities still limit performance.

In the domain of graph neural networks, Chitla et al. introduced GDNConv, a novel graph deformation network designed to improve robustness in noisy graph structures by incorporating dynamic edge weighting, attention-based deformation, and multi-level aggregation40. This approach significantly enhances representation learning and outperforms traditional GNN models, though it still faces challenges related to computational complexity in large-scale graphs. Similarly, Chitla et al. proposed a Modified Graph Convolutional Network (MGCN) that integrates adaptive edge weighting, residual connections, and scalable attention mechanisms to address over-smoothing and rigid graph structures41. While effective, these models indicate that balancing scalability and robustness remains an open problem in graph-based learning. For information retrieval tasks, Panchumarthi et al. developed a Modified Cross Encoder for two-stage passage ranking, leveraging pre-trained transformer models and a hybrid scoring mechanism combining sigmoid and cosine similarity42. Their model demonstrated improved ranking accuracy over traditional approaches such as BM25 and bi-encoders, though the computational cost of cross-encoder architectures continues to be a limitation for real-time systems. In text classification using graph-based learning, Wang et al. proposed PaSIG, a pre-trained semantic interaction-based inductive graph neural network that models semantic relationships between texts using heterogeneous graph structures and gated fusion mechanisms43. This method effectively improves classification accuracy and supports inductive learning; however, the complexity of graph construction and dependency on pre-trained models may impact efficiency.

Furthermore, Yin et al. introduced TextGT, a double-view graph transformer that combines graph neural networks with transformer architectures to jointly model syntactic and sequential representations for aspect-based sentiment analysis44. This hybrid approach successfully alleviates the over-smoothing problem common in GNNs and improves performance, though integrating dual representations increases model complexity. Lastly, the Neighbours-to-Neighbours Contrastive GCN (NNC-GCN) improves classification performance through local neighborhood contrast learning without the need for complex architectures45. These innovations collectively push the boundaries of semi-supervised learning, tackling both local and global challenges in graph-structured data. They underscore the evolving potential of SSL to produce more accurate, robust, and efficient models across a variety of applications.

Oversmoothing mechanism

Oversmoothing in GCNs is a phenomenon that arises when multiple layers are stacked. This stacking results in the representations of nodes from distinct clusters becoming excessively similar, ultimately degrading the overall performance of the model. The problem of over-smoothing was first highlighted in the work of Li and colleagues46. Following this, Xu and others observed that the rate of oversmoothing can differ based on the characteristics of the nodes; specifically, nodes located near the edges of the graph tend to experience slower oversmoothing effects47. To address the over-smoothing issue, several solutions have been proposed. Notably, Kipf and collaborators, along with Xu and others, introduced methods that draw inspiration from residual networks. These approaches incorporate residual connections to help mitigate the over-smoothing effect48,49. Although these techniques demonstrate potential, they are not entirely sufficient for completely resolving the problem. Another approach to tackling over-smoothing is presented in the context of FAGCN50. Unlike previous methods that focus primarily on residual connections, FAGCN enhances node representation by aggregating information from first-order neighbors across both high and low frequencies. This strategy enables the central node to capture more informative features, thereby improving its distinction from neighboring nodes. By integrating diverse frequency information, FAGCN not only alleviates the effects of over-smoothing but also enriches node representations. This leads to enhanced model performance and clearer differentiation among clusters. The over-smoothing effect can significantly hinder a network’s ability to distinguish between different data points. Nodes, regardless of their unique attributes or community affiliations, tend to converge toward similar representations. This issue becomes particularly pronounced in semi-supervised learning scenarios, where a lack of labeled data presents a major challenge. Consequently, achieving high classification accuracy becomes increasingly difficult, as the subtle distinctions critical for effective learning are diminished. To combat the over-smoothing problem, the structure of the proposed model integrates TE with Graph GNNs. This design aims specifically to mitigate the adverse effects of over-smoothing, thereby improving the model’s capability to learn from data.

Siamese networks and plagiarism detection

Several studies have leveraged Siamese network architectures for P-D tasks in the context of low-resource languages. One example is the work on Indonesian poetry, where a Siamese MaLSTM model was used to detect sentence similarity51. The model achieved 91.6% accuracy in detecting semantic similarities between lines of poetry, demonstrating the effectiveness of Siamese networks in identifying plagiarism. This model combined word embeddings (FastText) with tokenization and stop-word removal techniques. Although successful, the authors noted the need for refinement in understanding poetic structures and styles, which are often more nuanced than traditional prose. A study by52 proposed a modularity-based Siamese Simple Graph Convolutional Network (MS-SGC) to address the over-smoothing issue in traditional GCNs. Over-smoothing occurs as the model depth increases, leading to diminished performance. Inspired by the modular structure of brain networks, the authors integrated spectral clustering to detect and preserve modularity in graphs, reducing cross-module connection weights and improving both locality and global balance. Extensive experiments showed that MS-SGC improved node classification accuracy by up to 2% on the Pubmed dataset, outperformed several SOTA models, and reduced computational complexity in node clustering and community prediction tasks. Additionally53, proposed an enhanced, attentive Siamese LSTM network for low-resource text plagiarism detection, particularly focusing on Tibetan-Chinese cross-lingual cases. The study addressed the challenges of limited training data by utilizing translation-based DA to expand the corpus, thus improving the model’s performance in detecting plagiarism, especially in cases involving semantic rewriting and translation-based plagiarism. The paper also introduced a pre-detection module that employs abstract document vectors to enhance detection efficiency. Experimental results demonstrated that the proposed model achieved strong performance, with a significant Pearson correlation coefficient in both Tibetan and Chinese text plagiarism detection tasks, highlighting its potential effectiveness in low-resource language settings.

Urdu text reuse and paraphrase detection

Urdu, being a low-resource language, has witnessed relatively few advancements in T-R and P-D. To the best of our knowledge, all previous efforts in this domain have relied on supervised learning (SL) approaches, and no attempts have been made to explore semi-supervised learning (SSL) in this area. One of the pioneering efforts was the development of the Urdu Paraphrase Plagiarism Corpus (UPPC)54, which contains 160 documents, including 75 paraphrased (P) and 65 non-paraphrased (NP) texts. Despite its value, UPPC has limitations, such as a small dataset size, a focus on celebrity-related texts, and controlled experimental conditions that may not represent real-world plagiarism scenarios. More recent efforts by Hafeez et al.12 introduced the Urdu Sentential Paraphrase (USP) corpus, comprising 4900 sentences with labeled paraphrased and non-paraphrased data. The researchers used a feature-fusion technique combining sentence transformers and n-grams, achieving an F1 score of 0.855, indicating its effectiveness in detecting paraphrases in Urdu text. A significant leap in Urdu P-D came with the development of the Semi-Automatically Generated Urdu Sentential Paraphrase Corpus (SUSPC)55, which employed DNNs-based models such as WENGO and D-TRAPPD. D-TRAPPD, a hybrid model combining convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, significantly outperformed previous methods, achieving an F1 score of 96.80% for P-D. Aslam et al.7 proposed a BiLSTM-based framework for Urdu paraphrase detection to address morphological complexity and limited resources. They also developed the Urdu Paraphrased Corpus (UPC) and demonstrated improved performance over CNN, LSTM, and BERT models. This study laid the foundation for future research, emphasizing the importance of deep learning (DL) architectures in improving Urdu NLP tasks. In the context of T-R, significant progress has been made with corpora, such as the Urdu Short Text Reuse Corpus (USTRC)56 and the COUNTER corpus57. The USTRC contains 2684 manually labeled short text pairs, categorized into verbatim, paraphrased, and non-paraphrased texts, enabling improved T-R detection in Urdu. The study evaluating USTRC found that character n-gram overlap, combined with the J48 classifier, was the most effective approach for detecting T-R. On the other hand, the COUNTER corpus advanced T-R detection in Urdu news articles, with the GST mML1 model achieving an F1 score of 0.81 for binary classification. A more recent development is the creation of the UTRD-Phr-23 corpus58, a large-scale dataset containing 25001 manually annotated text pairs designed to train and evaluate T-R models. The authors proposed a novel model integrating eight pre-trained sentence transformers, achieving an F1 score of 0.63 in T-R tasks. This demonstrates the robustness of transformer-based models in detecting T-R in low-resource languages like Urdu. While supervised learning has been the dominant approach in Urdu T-R and P-D research, there is a growing interest in SSL due to its ability to leverage both labeled and unlabeled data. SSL has shown promise in reducing the reliance on large labeled datasets, often scarce in low-resource languages. However, SSL techniques face challenges, such as error propagation from noisy labeled data and managing noisy predictions59. Recent advancements in GNNs for SSL tasks provide new opportunities for improving T-R and P-D tasks in Urdu. GNNs, including GCNs and GATs, have successfully enhanced feature extraction and the learning of relationships between text nodes. Specifically, models such as Text-GCN60 and TextING61, which construct graphs representing documents and words, allow for the joint learning of word and document embeddings, thereby improving T-R and P-D tasks. A recent study62 that combined a Siamese Network with graph clustering techniques demonstrated improvements in detecting query similarities, achieving a 6% performance improvement on benchmark datasets. Despite significant advances in NLP for high-resource languages, Urdu remains under-researched in the domains of T-R and P-D. Although some progress has been made with corpora like UPPC, USP, and USTRC, the lack of large-scale, diverse datasets and benchmark models has limited the development of robust T-R and P-D systems for Urdu. The application of SSL, particularly in conjunction with GNNs, presents a promising direction for future research. Proposed framework

In this section, we start by outlining the problem formulation. Next, we detail the proposed HBCF mechanism, which selectively incorporates high-confidence pseudo-labeled data into the training set. Following this, we describe our approach to edge construction and pre-processing. We then discuss the DA techniques employed. Finally, we provide a comprehensive explanation of the proposed SGSSL model.

Problem formulation

In the domain of T-R and P-D tasks, challenges persist due to sparse digital resources, especially in languages like Urdu. The proposed SGSSL model addresses low accuracy caused by limited annotated data and noise in pseudo-labeled samples by employing an HCBF strategy. SGSSL is an SSL method that integrates a DA technique to enhance the training dataset and improve overall performance. It represents sentence pairs as graphs G=(V, E), leveraging contextual embeddings x from Urdu Roberta and establishing edges E based on a semantic similarity threshold θ =0.5.Fig. 1The proposed SGSSL model integrates GCN and GAT within a Siamese framework for SSL text classification. HCBF mechanism

This study introduces a novel SGSSL approach with a hybrid confidence-based filtering (HCBF) mechanism, as shown in Algorithm 1. The purpose of HCBF is to improve pseudo-label quality by jointly considering three complementary signals: (i) predictive confidence, (ii) semantic similarity, and (iii) model uncertainty. In contrast to confidence-only filtering, this design reduces the chance of retaining overconfident but unreliable pseudo-labels.

In parallel, we compute a semantic similarity score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_i$$\end{document} using Sentence Transformer embeddings. In this way, HCBF acts as a reliability-aware pseudo-label selection mechanism that is more robust than filtering based only on prediction confidence.

Uncertainty estimation and hybrid score construction

To clarify the “hybrid” nature of HCBF, the uncertainty term is explicitly estimated using Monte Carlo Dropout rather than a deterministic confidence proxy. Specifically, dropout is kept active at inference time, and each unlabeled instance is evaluated through N stochastic forward passes. This produces a distribution of predicted class probabilities, from which predictive variance is computed as the uncertainty estimate. Therefore, the uncertainty term captures the model’s epistemic uncertainty, i.e., instability caused by uncertainty in model parameters when labeled data are limited.

This choice is particularly suitable for semi-supervised learning because pseudo-labels may appear highly confident in a single forward pass while remaining unstable across repeated stochastic passes. By including the variance-based uncertainty term in Eq. (7), HCBF penalizes such unreliable samples even when their maximum softmax confidence is high. Consequently, the filtering mechanism becomes more conservative and better aligned with pseudo-label reliability.

The final hybrid confidence score integrates three complementary signals: (i) confidence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_i$$\end{document}, which reflects the model’s belief in the assigned pseudo-label; (ii) semantic similarity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_i$$\end{document}, which captures consistency with class-level semantic structure; and (iii) uncertainty \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_i$$\end{document}, which quantifies prediction instability under MC Dropout. This formulation makes the filtering criterion mathematically transparent and explains why HCBF performs better than confidence-only pseudo-label selection.

Data processing

To efficiently analyze text, it is essential to convert raw text into numerical data. We enhanced T-R and P-D models with high-quality training data and diverse DA through BT and MWP. Text preprocessing included cleaning input by removing punctuation, whitespace, numbers, and stop words, which optimized tasks and reduced algorithm iterations. We used Python tools NLTK (https://www.nltk.org/) and SpaCy (https://spacy.io/) to handle complex text and ensure the semantic meaning of sentences was preserved. The preprocessed text was tokenized, integer-encoded, and padded into fixed-length vectors for model training, ensuring the models perform well, especially in complex languages like Urdu. Postgraduate experts selected the most suitable augmented samples to maximize model accuracy.

Data augmentation

DA entails the application of semantically invariant transformations as a regularization technique. These transformations generate additional data samples while preserving the integrity of class labels. The scope of DA goes beyond image classification and extends to various domains such as speech recognition, machine translation, and, more recently, text classification. Augmenting data is a critical approach to improving the overall adaptability of models and preventing overfitting. We present our enhanced approach for T-R and P-D by discussing the proposed DA strategy. The annotation is costly and time-consuming, while DL approaches demand a large corpus. To address this problem, we developed methods for generating additional annotations efficiently. Our study utilized two adopted methods: BT and MWP.

Back-translation

DA enhances DNN performance by generating new samples from existing data, which is especially useful when dealing with small datasets or low-resource languages. BT is a common DA technique where sentences are translated from one language to another and then back to the original language to create paraphrases. In this study, we apply BT to the T-R and P-D corpus by translating Urdu sentences into English and back into Urdu, as this method effectively preserves meaning. We employed multiple BT strategies using the Transformers library, which includes machine translation models like Marian (https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/en/model_doc/marian/) and Flore 200 (https://github.com/facebookresearch/flores/), as well as the Google Translation API. Mask word prediction

We present a DA technique utilizing Masked Language Modeling (MLM) for Urdu based on two pre-trained models from the Hugging Face library: Roberta Urdu (https://huggingface.co/urduhack/roberta-urdu-small/) and Albert Urdu (https://huggingface.co/sibt-rj/albert-large-urdu/). The Roberta Urdu model is highly effective in processing Urdu news articles, featuring 125.98 million parameters and a large vocabulary of 52,000. It is designed for tasks like sentiment analysis and summarization within the Urdu news domain. Conversely, the Albert Urdu model, with 11.69 million parameters and a vocabulary of 30,020, is optimized for educational and encyclopedic content, particularly Urdu Wikipedia articles. Both models share an architecture of 12 attention heads and a hidden dimension of 768, facilitating broad contextual understanding.

Our DA methodology begins by masking random words in sentences from our Urdu corpus, replacing them with a <mask> token but keeping the target word intact. This approach allows the models to predict suitable replacements from five contextually relevant options, enhancing linguistic comprehension. We augment this by increasing batch sizes and prolonging training durations, further tuning the models with diverse parameters to optimize performance. By predicting ten potential replacements (five from each model) for every masked word and subsequently transforming these predictions into embeddings using the Roberta-Urdu model, we can compute similarity scores between the embeddings of the original masked words and their predicted counterparts. The most contextually appropriate word is then selected to replace the original, creating varied training sentences that significantly boost the efficacy of Urdu T-R and P-D tasks.

SGSSL framework

We propose a novel SSL approach named SGSSL, as represented in Fig. 2, utilizing Siamese GCN and GAT to classify sentence pairs effectively. Each sentence pair is embedded using the Urdu Roberta model, and edges in the graph are constructed based on semantic similarities between words, enabling robust contextual representation. To comprehensively evaluate our model, we conduct experiments using various combinations of GCN and GAT architectures (i.e., GAT-GAT, GCN-GCN, GAT-GCN, and GCN-GAT) across six widely used T-R and P-D benchmark datasets. Our SSL approach integrates labeled and unlabeled data to enhance the model’s learning process and improve performance in scenarios with sparsely labeled data. The concatenated features from the Siamese network are processed through a bottleneck layer, implemented as a linear transformation, to reduce dimensionality and mitigate the risk of overfitting. This dimensionality reduction is crucial for handling high-dimensional data efficiently. Following this, the features are fed into a TE module, which includes MHSA, dropout, RMSNORM, and Feedforward layers. These components collectively enhance the model’s ability to capture intricate dependencies and patterns within the data. The final prediction is achieved through a SoftMax layer, providing a probabilistic classification output, as detailed in Algorithm 2. Our proposed method demonstrates significant advantages, including improved accuracy, robustness to sparse data, and efficient handling of complex sentence structures. The SSL framework boosts performance by leveraging unlabeled data and ensures scalability and adaptability to various datasets and applications. This innovative approach holds substantial impact potential for advancing sentence pair classification and related NLP tasks.

Graph convolutional networks

GCNs play a crucial role in GNNs, contrasting with conventional graph embedding methods by conducting end-to-end training to derive compact vector representations. These representations are utilized for tasks like node classification, graph classification, and link prediction. Over recent years, integrating neural network techniques has driven substantial progress in applying GNNs to recommender systems, facilitating the modeling of associative connections between users and items where traditional algorithms face challenges. Moreover, GNNs have shown significant effectiveness in tasks within NLP. Originally proposed by Kipf et al.48, GCNs operate directly on graph structures, generating embedding vectors for nodes based on their neighborhood properties and learning graph-based structural information. The GAT enhances this by learning node attribute associations through attention mechanisms, improving classification capabilities. A GCN is a neural network architecture designed to handle graph-structured data. It operates on a graph G = (V, E), where V is the set of nodes, and E is the set of edges. The adjustable MHSA layers in the encoder allow flexible weighting of node features, facilitating the effective extraction of structural and contextual information in graph data. Additionally, the encoder’s weight matrices and activation functions optimize the attention and feedforward networks, making the model more robust in handling complex graph structures. Overall, the Transformer’s design, incorporating TE blocks with MHSA, dropout, RMSNorm, and feedforward layers, overcomes the limitations of traditional GNNs and enhances feature extraction and stability, making it highly effective for Urdu T-R and P-D tasks requiring long-range relationship analysis and segment prioritization.

Datasets

We evaluated our approach on six publicly available benchmark datasets covering Urdu text reuse detection (T-R) and paraphrase detection (P-D) at document, sentence, and phrasal levels. These datasets were selected to ensure diversity in text granularity, task formulation, and label complexity. Specifically, UPPC and COUNTER contain document-level pairs, USP, SUSPC, and USTRC contain sentence-level pairs, and UTRD-Phr-23 focuses on phrase-level similarity. Among these datasets, UPPC, USP, SUSPC, and UTRD-Phr-23 are evaluated in the binary setting, whereas COUNTER and USTRC are evaluated in both binary and ternary settings. The ternary setting is more challenging because it requires discrimination among fine-grained categories such as verbatim, partially paraphrased, and paraphrased. To ensure a uniform semi-supervised learning (SSL) protocol, each dataset was partitioned into a testing subset (10%) and a training subset (90%). The training subset was further divided into 20% labeled and 80% unlabeled samples. The unlabeled subset was incorporated into training through SSL, while the labeled subset was used to guide supervised optimization. In addition, data augmentation (DA) was applied only to the training portion of the data, thereby preserving the integrity of the test set. Data preprocessing and reproducibility protocol

Before model training, all datasets were processed using a unified preprocessing pipeline to ensure consistency across experiments. First, all text pairs were normalized to a consistent Unicode form to reduce variation caused by Urdu script encoding differences. We then removed extra white spaces, normalized punctuation, and filtered malformed or empty samples. Duplicate text pairs, if present, were removed to avoid unintended information leakage across training and testing partitions.

For sentence- and phrase-level datasets, token sequences were preserved as much as possible in order to retain lexical and syntactic variation important for paraphrase and text reuse detection. For document-level datasets, text was segmented into the corresponding pair representation used by the encoder. No test sample was augmented or modified during preprocessing. All data augmentation techniques, including back translation (BT) and masked word prediction (MWP), were applied only to the training data after the train/test split.

To improve reproducibility, all baseline models and all SGSSL variants were evaluated using the same preprocessing pipeline, identical train/test partitions, and the same labeled/unlabeled division strategy. The labeled and unlabeled subsets were created only from the training split, ensuring that no test instances contributed to graph construction, pseudo-label generation, or augmentation. This protocol preserves experimental fairness and prevents data leakage.

After preprocessing, each dataset was converted into a graph representation for SGSSL. Nodes correspond to textual units and their augmented counterparts, while edges were constructed using semantic similarity scores derived from contextual embeddings. The same graph construction procedure and thresholding strategy were applied consistently across datasets unless otherwise noted in the sensitivity analysis.

Evaluation methodology

In this study, we employed Accuracy (ACC) and Macro-F1 as our evaluation metrics. Accuracy, denoted as ACC, measures the proportion of correctly predicted instances out of the total number of instances. In the context of multi-class classification, the F1 score can be decomposed into Micro-F1 and Macro-F1, each calculated using different methodologies. Micro-F1 aggregates precision and recall across all classes, treating them collectively as a single entity. It is calculated as:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} & \text {Micro-Precision} = \frac{\sum _i TP_i}{\sum _i (TP_i + FP_i)} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} & \text {Micro-Recall} = \frac{\sum _i TP_i}{\sum _i (TP_i + FN_i)} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} & \text {Micro-F1} = \frac{2 \times (\text {Micro-Precision} \times \text {Micro-Recall})}{\text {Micro-Precision} + \text {Micro-Recall}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$TP_i$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FP_i$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FN_i$$\end{document} represent true positives, false positives, and false negatives for class i, respectively. Impact of HCBF on SSL models

Integrating HCBF into SSL models addresses noisy pseudo-labeled data, which can hinder learning. By filtering out low-confidence labels, HCBF ensures more reliable training data, directly enhancing performance metrics. For instance, comparing Table 6 (SSL without HCBF) and Table 8 (SSL with HCBF) without DA, the GAT-GAT+TE model’s accuracy increases from 0.764 to 0.799, and its F1 rises from 0.741 to 0.782 on the UPPC dataset, demonstrating HCBF effectiveness in reducing label noise.

Enhancement through DA

DA plays a crucial role in expanding training data diversity, especially beneficial in SSL with limited labeled data. Introducing DA techniques like BT and MWP enriches the training set, improving model generalization. For example, in the GAT-GAT+TE model, accuracy improves from 0.799 to 0.822, and F1 from 0.782 to 0.796 on the UPPC dataset when DA is applied alongside HCBF, highlighting DA’s contribution to enhancing model robustness.

Combined impact of HCBF and DA

Combining HCBF and DA yields a synergistic effect, maximizing SSL model performance. This integrated approach cleans the training data of low-confidence labels and enriches the dataset with diverse examples, leading to significant improvements across all datasets. For instance, the GAT-GAT+TE model with both HCBF and DA achieves an F1 of 0.796 and accuracy of 0.822 on the UPPC dataset, outperforming all baseline models and demonstrating the combined benefits of HCBF and DA.

Computational complexity analysis

In addition to reporting hyper-parameter settings, we analyze the computational complexity and empirical runtime characteristics of the proposed SGSSL framework to provide a clearer assessment of its practical deployment cost. Since SGSSL combines Siamese graph encoders, a Transformer Encoder (TE), a bottleneck feature-refinement layer, and the Hybrid Confidence-Based Filtering (HCBF) mechanism, evaluating only predictive performance is insufficient. Therefore, we complement the accuracy-based analysis with both theoretical complexity and empirical efficiency measurements.

The overall complexity of SGSSL is dominated by three main components: the Siamese graph encoder, the TE module, and the bottleneck feature-refinement layer. A lower threshold creates a denser graph by connecting weakly related samples, which may introduce noisy edges and lead to over-smoothing during message passing. In contrast, a higher threshold yields a sparser graph, which may remove useful semantic relations and restrict label propagation between related nodes.

Among all tested settings, θ =0.5 provides the best balance between connectivity and selectivity, yielding the strongest overall F1 and accuracy.

These findings justify our choice of θ =0.5 in the main experiments. Hybrid score components

To further clarify how the HCBF mechanism is mathematically constructed, we performed a component-wise ablation of the hybrid score on the best-performing backbone without DA, i.e., GAT-GAT+TE. In this analysis, we progressively evaluated four pseudo-label filtering variants: (i) confidence-only filtering, (ii) confidence + semantic similarity, (iii) confidence + uncertainty, and (iv) the full hybrid score integrating confidence, semantic similarity, and MC-dropout uncertainty. The goal of this ablation is to determine whether the uncertainty term contributes meaningful gains beyond deterministic confidence and semantic consistency.

Table 16 shows that confidence-only filtering already improves over the SGSSL baseline, indicating that pseudo-label selection based on predictive confidence is beneficial. However, its gains remain limited because high softmax confidence does not always correspond to reliable pseudo-labels, particularly in low-resource semi-supervised settings. Adding semantic similarity produces further improvement by favoring pseudo-labels that are semantically aligned with the corresponding class structure. Likewise, incorporating uncertainty with confidence provides a larger gain than confidence alone, suggesting that predictive stability across stochastic forward passes is a stronger indicator of pseudo-label reliability than confidence by itself.

Most importantly, the full hybrid formulation consistently achieves the best results across all datasets. For example, on UPPC, the estimated F1 improves from 0.741 for the baseline SGSSL model to 0.759 with confidence-only filtering, 0.770 with confidence + similarity, 0.774 with confidence + uncertainty, and 0.782 with the full HCBF score. A similar trend is observed on USP, USTRC, and UTRD-Phr-23, where the full hybrid score produces the strongest gains. Analysis of ternary classification difficulty

To further investigate the gap between binary and ternary classification, we qualitatively examined the error patterns on the ternary datasets. We observed that most difficult cases are concentrated around the partially paraphrased category, which naturally overlaps with both verbatim and paraphrased samples. If a pair retains long lexical spans with only minor edits, the model may confuse verbatim with partially paraphrased. Conversely, if the semantic meaning is preserved but the wording is more substantially altered, the model may confuse partially paraphrased with paraphrased.

This challenge is more pronounced in Urdu because morphological variation, free word order, and synonym substitution can change the surface form without fully changing the semantic relation between the two texts. As a result, the intermediate class becomes less compact and more heterogeneous than the two endpoint classes. Comparison with baselines

Table 18 provides a detailed comparative analysis of the proposed SGSSL framework against several baseline models, including encoder-only LLMs, encoder-decoder LLMs, decoder-only LLMs, and graph-based models, across six standard datasets in a SSL configuration. The results demonstrate that SGSSL consistently outperforms these baselines, particularly in tasks requiring a deep understanding of inter-document relationships and hierarchical information. Below, we provide a detailed comparison, analyzing the strengths and limitations of each baseline category and highlighting the technical benefits of the SGSSL model. Encoder-only models such as BERT, ALBERT, and RoBERTa are based on deep bidirectional transformers, trained primarily using MLM. These models excel at capturing contextual embeddings within individual text sequences but are inherently limited when it comes to modeling relationships between different samples, which is crucial for SSL tasks. For instance, BERT achieves a test accuracy of 0.453 on the UPPC dataset, while the best-performing encoder-only model, ELECTRA, achieves 0.509 on the USP dataset. Despite their competitive performance in binary classification tasks (e.g., ELECTRA scoring 0.563 on COUNTER (Binary)), these models underperform on datasets requiring complex inter-sample relationships, such as SUSPC, where ELECTRA scores only 0.444. In contrast, the proposed SGSSL framework, which incorporates GATs, is better suited for semi-supervised tasks. Siamese GATs allow SGSSL to capture both local and global dependencies between data points, which is critical in leveraging the limited labeled data available in SSL. On SUSPC, SGSSL with HCBF and DA achieves a test accuracy of 0.753, significantly outperforming all encoder-only models. This demonstrates that the ability to model relationships between text samples through graph structures provides a considerable advantage in complex classification tasks. Encoder-decoder models, such as Simple Transformer and mT5, are designed for sequence-to-sequence tasks, utilizing cross-attention mechanisms between the encoder and decoder. These models are highly versatile and often perform better than encoder-only models on tasks requiring the generation or transformation of sequences. For instance, mT5 achieves an accuracy of 0.594 on the COUNTER (Ternary) dataset. However, similar to encoder-only models, encoder-decoder models do not inherently capture the relationships between different data points in the same way that graph-based models do. Their performance lags on tasks where understanding document-to-document relationships is key, such as USTRC (Ternary), where mT5 scores 0.548, compared to SGSSL’s 0.693. The SGSSL model’s superior performance on these datasets is attributed to its use of graph structures to model the inter-sample relationships, in conjunction with HCBF and DA techniques. By integrating hierarchical contextualization with graph-based learning, SGSSL effectively bridges the gap between local text-level understanding and global structural relationships, enabling it to outperform encoder-decoder models on a range of tasks. Decoder-only models like GPT-2 and Llama3 are primarily designed for autoregressive text generation, focusing on sequential token prediction. These models excel in tasks that involve generating coherent and fluent text but are less suited to tasks that require deep contextual understanding of relationships between different data points. For example, while GPT-2 performs well on the USP dataset with a score of 0.600, it struggles with more complex classification tasks, such as USTRD-Phr-23, where it achieves only 0.322. The SGSSL model’s architecture is designed to address these limitations by leveraging GATs, which capture both local dependencies within documents and global structural relationships across the dataset. On the USTRD-Phr-23 dataset, SGSSL achieves a significantly higher accuracy of 0.486, highlighting the importance of graph-based modeling in capturing inter-sample relationships, particularly in multi-class classification tasks. Graph-based models, such as TextGT, Text-GCN, and TextING, are specifically designed to capture the relationships between textual data points using GNNs. These models perform well in SSL tasks due to their ability to propagate label information across the graph structure, utilizing both labeled and unlabeled data. For instance, TextING achieves a high accuracy of 0.753 on the UPPC dataset, showcasing the strength of GNNs in semi-supervised settings. However, traditional graph-based models often do not leverage hierarchical or contextualized features, limiting their effectiveness in certain complex tasks. The SGSSL framework enhances the graph-based learning paradigm by incorporating HCBF and DA. These additions allow SGSSL to outperform even the best graph-based models, such as TextING, on datasets like UPPC (SGSSL achieving 0.796) and USTRC (Ternary) (SGSSL scoring 0.693 vs. TextING’s 0.612). This improvement is largely due to SGSSL’s ability to integrate both local contextual features and global relational structures, leading to more robust performance across diverse datasets. The proposed SGSSL model, particularly in its most advanced configuration (with both HCBF and DA), demonstrates the highest performance across all datasets. SGSSL leverages GATs to capture complex relationships between data points, while HCBF component that allows for better utilization of both labeled and unlabeled data in SSL scenarios. Discussion

The SGSSL framework effectively addresses Urdu T-R and P-D by integrating graph-based and attention-based mechanisms. Leveraging node aggregation from Siamese GNNs and MHSA from TE, SGSSL captures both semantic relationships and structural dependencies while mitigating over-smoothing issues inherent in standard GCN and GAT. The bottleneck layer reduces dimensionality, filters noise, and preserves salient features, thereby enhancing computational efficiency and minimizing overfitting risks.

The synergy between the local aggregation capabilities of GCN and GAT and the global context modeling of TE modules significantly enhances task-specific performance. Empirical evaluations demonstrate that this integrated methodology substantially improves model robustness, particularly in scenarios with limited labeled data. A pivotal advancement within SGSSL is the HCBF mechanism, which synthesizes probabilistic confidence, semantic similarity, and model uncertainty into a composite confidence score. This composite score enhances pseudo-label reliability by effectively filtering out unreliable data, addressing a fundamental challenge in SSL. The bottleneck feature-refinement layer plays a crucial role in balancing efficiency and performance. By compressing high-dimensional embeddings into a compact representation using a linear projection, it reduces redundancy while preserving essential semantic information. This design enables faster convergence and improves computational efficiency without introducing additional non-linear complexity.

From a deployment perspective, the proposed SGSSL framework introduces additional computational overhead compared to the baseline Siamese GAT configuration due to the inclusion of TE, DA, and HCBF modules. Nevertheless, the measured inference latency remains within a practically manageable range, suggesting that the framework is feasible for real-world offline and near-real-time applications. This trade-off is justified by the substantial improvement in classification performance and robustness observed across all benchmark datasets.

Practically, the SGSSL framework is highly applicable to NLP tasks in under-resourced languages, including plagiarism detection, content verification, and paraphrase identification. Its capability to manage Urdu’s morphological complexity is further bolstered by leveraging contextual embeddings from the pre-trained Urdu RoBERTa model. Additionally, the integration of DA techniques such as BT and MWP enhances data diversity and mitigates overfitting, thereby increasing the model’s robustness in real-world applications. An important direction for future research is the integration of Explainable AI (XAI) techniques into the SGSSL framework. Although the proposed model achieves strong performance, it operates as a black-box system, making it difficult to interpret why two texts are classified as verbatim, partially paraphrased, or paraphrased. Incorporating XAI methods such as attention visualization, gradient-based attribution techniques (e.g., Integrated Gradients or SHAP), and graph-specific explanation approaches (e.g., GNNExplainer) can provide insights into which tokens, phrases, or graph connections contribute most to the model’s decisions. Such interpretability is particularly important in applications like plagiarism detection and content verification, where users require transparent and justifiable predictions. Therefore, enhancing the SGSSL framework with explainability mechanisms represents a promising direction for improving both trustworthiness and usability.

Despite its strengths, the SGSSL model’s reliance on substantial computational resources may limit its applicability in resource-constrained environments. Nonetheless, its robustness and effectiveness with minimal labeled data present notable advantages for organizations with limited annotation resources. Future research should investigate additional DA techniques and advanced filtering mechanisms to further bolster SSL models’ robustness and accuracy. Moreover, extending the framework to other NLP tasks and optimizing it for computational efficiency will enhance its applicability and practical utility.

Conclusion

This study introduces the SGSSL framework, a novel approach for addressing text reuse and paraphrase detection challenges in low-resource languages, with a focus on Urdu. By integrating Siamese Graph Neural Networks with Transformer-based architectures, SGSSL combines graph-based methodologies (GCN and GAT) with MHSA, effectively capturing complex linguistic patterns inherent in under-resourced languages. The incorporation of strategies such as BT, MWP, and the HCBF technique enhances the framework’s accuracy, particularly in settings with limited labeled data. These components collectively enable SGSSL to discern nuanced linguistic differences, crucial for accurately distinguishing paraphrased and reused content. However, the framework encounters challenges with very short text pairs, where limited contextual information compromises accuracy, as demonstrated by performance on datasets like UTRD-Phr-23. Additionally, the computational intensity of the models restricts deployment in resource-constrained environments, such as mobile devices or embedded systems. Addressing these limitations is essential for real-world applicability and scalability.

Despite these challenges, the SGSSL framework holds significant implications for NLP, providing a robust foundation for extending its application to other low-resource languages with unique linguistic characteristics. Its potential applications in plagiarism detection, content verification, and paraphrase identification make it a valuable asset for both academic research and industry practices. Future work should focus on refining the framework by exploring additional DA techniques, enhancing filtering mechanisms, and optimizing computational efficiency to facilitate deployment across diverse operational contexts. Expanding SGSSL to support a broader range of low-resource languages will further validate its generalizability and adaptability, establishing it as a pivotal tool in advancing NLP technologies for under-resourced linguistic communities. In addition, incorporating Explainable AI (XAI) techniques is a promising direction for future work. While SGSSL demonstrates strong predictive performance, improving its interpretability would allow users to better understand the reasoning behind similarity decisions. Techniques such as attention analysis, feature attribution methods, and graph-based explanation models can be integrated to highlight influential words, semantic relationships, and graph structures that drive classification outcomes. This would enhance the transparency, trust, and practical adoption of the framework in real-world applications.
