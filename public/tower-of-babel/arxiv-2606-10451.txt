Arbitrage-free Data Pricing
Yihang Wu, Zhengyu Jin, Yicheng Fu, Jinfei Liu, Kui Ren

Abstract
Driven by the rising value of data in applications such as advertising, finance, and machine learning, markets for data products have become increasingly important. Data markets mainly sell two kinds of products: datasets and machine learning models. Since these products can be replicated at negligible marginal cost, sellers naturally version them through query access and noisy model releases. Versioning immediately raises an arbitrage problem: a buyer may combine cheaper purchases and recover a more informative product at a lower total price. Existing work on query and model pricing studies arbitrage-freeness when buyer values are treated as exogenous, whereas the literature on selling information derives value from the buyer’s decision problem but ignores arbitrage-freeness. Accordingly, we study the seller’s optimal data pricing problem where buyers value data through Bayesian decision making and we impose arbitrage-freeness constraints. We first interpret query and model pricing as special cases of information pricing, and formulate the general arbitrage-free information selling problem, show the computational hardness and give a branch-and-bound algorithm based on McCormick relaxations. We then consider threshold utilities where buyers have a positive value if and only if the experiment is sufficiently informative. Under this condition, we find that the arbitrage-freeness can be characterized by Blackwell dominance, which in turn unifies the arbitrage-free conditions for query pricing [ 15 ] and model pricing [ 11 ] . Finally, we characterize the revenue-maximizing pricing under restricted query and model menus.



1
Introduction

The growing demand for data in advertising, finance, logistics, and machine learning has given rise to active markets for data products. Two main product forms in data markets are datasets and machine learning models
[
31
]
. On the dataset side, Bloomberg and Xignite sell financial information through terminals, data feeds, and APIs, while AWS Data Exchange, Snowflake Marketplace, and Dawex broker subscriptions and licenses for large catalogs of third-party data products. On the model side, sellers increasingly monetize models rather than raw data itself: AWS SageMaker and Microsoft Azure offer pre-trained models and model training services, while large language model (LLM) companies like OpenAI, Anthropic and Google sell LLM access through token-metered APIs and subscriptions.

From an economic perspective, buyers purchase data because it improves their decisions. Consider an advertiser deciding how to allocate a campaign budget across demographic groups, channels, and regions. A dataset describing the attributes and historical behavior of a target population helps the advertiser identify which consumers are likely to respond and therefore how the budget should be split. As another example, consider a retailer planning inventory and procurement for next month. A predictive model that uses historical transactions, promotions, seasonal patterns, and local demand indicators to forecast sales directly improves ordering, staffing, and production decisions. In both cases, the value of the product comes from its effect on a downstream decision problem. This suggests a natural viewpoint: selling data is fundamentally the sale of information that is useful for decision making.

Compared with ordinary goods, data has a distinctive cost structure. Producing the first useful unit may require substantial expenditure on collection, cleaning, labeling, and model training, whereas reproducing additional units is often nearly costless. For information goods with this structure,
[
30
]
argue that versioning is an especially effective selling strategy. In dataset markets, a natural form of versioning is query-based access
[
20
,
19
,
15
]
: rather than sell only the full database, the seller offers different queries so that buyers with heterogeneous needs can acquire only the portions of the dataset relevant to their tasks. In model markets, the analogous strategy is to release multiple versions with different noise levels or accuracies
[
11
,
25
]
. Buyers who do not need the best model may still be willing to pay for a cheaper, lower-quality version, whereas they would decline to buy if only the top version were offered. Tiered model and API services, large language model (LLM) services, follow the same logic.

Once products are versioned, however, a new problem appears:
arbitrage
. If the menu is not designed carefully, a buyer may register multiple identities or repeatedly purchase lower-tier products, combine them, and recover the information contained in a higher-tier product at a lower total price.

Example 1.1

(Query arbitrage)
.

Consider an advertiser who wants to purchase a table of target users for a marketing campaign. Let the underlying dataset be partitioned into two disjoint subsets
D=D_{\mathrm{male}}\cup D_{\mathrm{female}}
, where
D_{\mathrm{male}}
and
D_{\mathrm{female}}
contain the male and female target users, respectively. Suppose the seller offers three deterministic query products:

Q_{\mathrm{male}}(D)=D_{\mathrm{male}},\qquad Q_{\mathrm{female}}(D)=D_{\mathrm{female}},\qquad Q_{\mathrm{all}}(D)=D.

If the posted prices are
p(Q_{\mathrm{male}})=1000,p(Q_{\mathrm{female}})=1000,p(Q_{\mathrm{all}})=3000
, then a buyer who wants the full table never purchases
Q_{\mathrm{all}}
directly. By buying
Q_{\mathrm{male}}
and
Q_{\mathrm{female}}
, the buyer recovers
Q_{\mathrm{male}}(D)\cup Q_{\mathrm{female}}(D)=D=Q_{\mathrm{all}}(D)
at total cost
2000<3000
. Hence the menu admits arbitrage.

Example 1.2

(Model arbitrage)
.

Consider a buyer who wants a model for predicting next month’s sales. Let
y^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}
denote the prediction produced by the seller’s best trained model. The seller versions this model by adding Gaussian noise and offers
M_{1}=y^{\star}+Z_{1}
, where
Z_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, at price
500
, and
M_{2}=y^{\star}+Z_{2}
, where
Z_{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,2)
, at price
200
. Suppose repeated purchases are independent conditional on
y^{\star}
. Then if the buyer purchases two copies of
M_{2}
,

M_{2}^{(1)}=y^{\star}+Z_{2}^{(1)},\qquad M_{2}^{(2)}=y^{\star}+Z_{2}^{(2)},\qquad Z_{2}^{(1)},Z_{2}^{(2)}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,2),

their average is
\frac{M_{2}^{(1)}+M_{2}^{(2)}}{2}=y^{\star}+\frac{Z_{2}^{(1)}+Z_{2}^{(2)}}{2}
. Because the two noises are independent,
\frac{Z_{2}^{(1)}+Z_{2}^{(2)}}{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Thus the average of two cheaper
M_{2}
purchases has exactly the same distribution as the higher-quality product
M_{1}
, but costs only
200+200=400<500
. This is again arbitrage.

Existing work on arbitrage-free pricing for query products
[
10
]
and model products
[
11
,
25
]
typically studies revenue maximization under the assumption that the buyer’s value for each data product is exogenously given. However, in practice the seller only has incomplete information about the buyer’s downstream task, prior information, or payoff function. Moreover, once value is tied to decision making, the arbitrage-free pricing rules studied in previous work need not be sufficient. The two examples just mentioned are cases of exact reconstruction: the cheaper products always yield the expensive one, so arbitrage arises for every buyer preference. But the absence of such an exact reconstruction relation does not imply the absence of arbitrage. There may still exist priors and decision problems under which combining products is profitable. Section
4.1
makes this distinction precise.

A parallel literature on selling information studies precisely the case in which the buyer’s value comes from a concrete Bayesian decision problem
[
1
,
3
,
9
,
12
]
. However, that literature typically does not impose arbitrage constraints across multiple purchased experiments. In Section
2.2
, we unify versioned datasets and machine learning models as statistical experiments. Then Example
2.7
shows that if arbitrage constraints are ignored, a menu that is optimal under standard information selling constraints may still admit arbitrage behavior of buyers, thereby reducing the seller’s revenue.

1.1
Our contributions

Accordingly, we investigate the seller’s optimal data menu design and pricing problem in a setting where pricing satisfies arbitrage-free constraints and where the value of data is derived from the improvement it brings to the buyer’s decision utility. Specifically, our contributions are as follows:

First, we formulate arbitrage-free (AF) constraints for information selling in Section
2
. In this formulation, buyers may purchase several products (a bundle), including repeated copies of the same product, and each bundle induces a composite experiment. We prove that incentive compatibility and individual rationality are special cases of AF constraints, and we establish a revelation principle adapted to AF constraints: without loss of generality, one may restrict attention to direct menus that are arbitrage-free against all bundles.

Second, in Section
3
, we show that repeated purchases can strictly increase informativeness indefinitely, so exact arbitrage-freeness in general induce an infinite set of constraints. We further prove that detecting a profitable bundle deviation is NP-hard even in a highly structured binary signal environment. Motivated by this hardness, we formulate the
h
-bounded problem as a direct mathematical program, where buyers are only allowed to purchase bundles of size at most
h
. The resulting program is multilinear because bundle recommendations are products of per-experiment recommendation probabilities. We then give an equivalent tensor lift that replaces these multilinear terms with linear marginal constraints and bilinear minor constraints, yielding a branch-and-bound formulation based on McCormick relaxations.

Third, in Section
4.1
, we identify threshold utilities as a regime in which arbitrage-freeness becomes much more tractable: for threshold types, the AF constraints are equivalent to a purely informational condition that no cheaper bundle may Blackwell-dominate the assigned experiment for each buyer type in the direct menu. We give explicit constructions of buyer’s decision problems showing that subset-query menus and Gaussian model menus can realize threshold utilities. As a consequence, we recover the arbitrage-free conditions for query and model pricing in previous works as special cases, thus unifying them under the same framework and indicating the arbitrage-free conditions for query and model pricing in previous works are not sufficient for AF in the general setting.

Fourth, Section
4.3
studies the revenue-maximization problem once the seller is restricted to some query and model menus under threshold utilities. We first prove that optimal arbitrage-free pricing remains NP-hard for general query menus. We then identify structured cases in which the problem becomes more tractable. For laminar dataset menus, we derive an exact dynamic program with running time
O(|\mathcal{F}|V^{3})
when values are integral. For additive-precision model menus, we reduce the problem to monotone subadditive chain pricing in
[
11
]
, which implies coNP-hardness of exact optimization and has an
O(n^{2})
dynamic program with a 1/2 approximation guarantee.

The notion of arbitrage-free (AF) data pricing was first introduced by

[

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Model

Basic setup.

We consider a monopoly data seller selling data to a data buyer who faces a decision problem under uncertainty. The state of the world
\omega
is drawn from a finite set
\Omega
, and the data seller can observe the true state
\omega
. The buyer has a private type
\theta
drawn from a finite set
\Theta
, and chooses an action
a
from a finite set
A
. The buyer’s ex-post utility from taking action
a
when the state is
\omega
and the type is
\theta
is given by
u_{\theta}(\omega,a)\in[0,1]
. The pair
(\omega,\theta)\in\Omega\times\Theta
is drawn from a publicly known prior distribution
\mu
. We write
\lambda_{\theta}:=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mu(\omega,\theta)>0
as the prior mass of type
\theta
, and
\mu_{\theta}(\omega):=\Pr[\omega\mid\theta]=\frac{\mu(\omega,\theta)}{\lambda_{\theta}}
as the prior belief of type
\theta
buyer about the state
\omega
.

Experiment.

The data seller offers a menu of experiments (or signaling schemes) about the state
\omega
. An Experiment
E=(S_{E},K_{E})
is a finite signal space
S_{E}
and a Markov kernel
K_{E}(\cdot\mid\omega)\in\Delta(S_{E}),\forall\omega\in\Omega
. When the state of the world is
\omega
, the experiment draws
s\sim K_{E}(\cdot\mid\omega)
and sends
s
to the buyer.

[
3
,
9
]
treat the experiments as decision information that directly affects the buyer’s payoff under uncertainty, and we will prove in Section
2.2
that query answers and model predictions can also be represented in the same framework. Thus the experiment provides a unifying framework for all different types of data products.

Denote the menu posted by the seller as
\mathcal{M}=\{(E_{j},t_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N}
, where
E_{j}
is an experiment and
t_{j}
is its price. In our setting, the buyer may purchase several experiments simultaneously (including repeated purchases of the same experiment), or equivalently create multiple accounts and buy experiments separately under different identities. When a buyer purchases multiple experiments, we assume that conditional on the true state of the world, the seller sends independent signals across purchases. This independence assumption is natural: because the buyer can always split purchases across different accounts, each purchase can be viewed as a separate draw from the corresponding experiment. The following definition formalizes the information generated by an arbitrary finite bundle of purchases.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
B=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell})\in\Theta^{\ell}
be a list of indices of experiments in the menu. The composite experiment associated with
B
, denoted by
E_{B}:=\bigotimes_{r=1}^{\ell}E_{i_{r}}
has signal space
S_{B}:=\prod_{r=1}^{\ell}S_{E_{i_{r}}}
and kernel

K_{E_{B}}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{\ell}\mid\omega)=\prod_{r=1}^{\ell}K_{E_{i_{r}}}(s_{r}\mid\omega),\forall(s_{1},\ldots,s_{\ell})\in S_{B},\omega\in\Omega.

Besides, the empty bundle gives the uninformative experiment.

Based on the notation above, the interaction between the buyer and the seller proceeds as follows:

1.

The seller posts a menu
\mathcal{M}
.

2.

The true state
\omega
and the buyer’s type
\theta
are realized.

3.

The buyer selects a composite experiment
E_{B}
from the menu and pays the corresponding total price
t_{B}=\sum_{j\in B}t_{j}
.

4.

The seller sends the buyer a signal
s\in S_{B}
from the experiment
E_{B}
according to the distribution
K_{E_{B}}(\cdot\mid\omega)
.

5.

The buyer chooses an action
a
and obtains utility
u_{\theta}(\omega,a)
.

The value of a composite experiment.

Before purchasing any experiment, the buyer chooses the action that maximizes expected utility based on the prior belief
\mu_{\theta}
and obtains expected utility

U_{\theta}^{0}=\max_{a\in A}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mu_{\theta}(\omega)u_{\theta}(\omega,a).

After receiving a signal
s
from experiment
E_{B}
, the buyer updates his belief to

\mu_{\theta}(\omega\mid s)=\frac{\mu_{\theta}(\omega)K_{E_{B}}(s\mid\omega)}{\sum_{\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega}\mu_{\theta}(\omega^{\prime})K_{E_{B}}(s\mid\omega^{\prime})},

and chooses the action that maximizes expected utility based on the posterior belief
\mu_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s)
, obtaining expected utility

u_{\theta}(s)=\max_{a\in A}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\frac{\mu_{\theta}(\omega)K_{E_{B}}(s\mid\omega)}{\sum_{\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega}\mu_{\theta}(\omega^{\prime})K_{E_{B}}(s\mid\omega^{\prime})}u_{\theta}(\omega,a).

Since the probability of receiving signal
s
is
\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mu_{\theta}(\omega)K_{E_{B}}(s\mid\omega)
, the expected utility of experiment
E_{B}
to type
\theta
is

W_{\theta}(E_{B})=\sum_{s\in S_{E_{B}}}\max_{a\in A}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mu_{\theta}(\omega)K_{E_{B}}(s\mid\omega)u_{\theta}(\omega,a).

Thus the value of experiment
E
to type
\theta
is the increase in expected utility from purchasing
E
compared to purchasing nothing:

V_{\theta}(E)=W_{\theta}(E)-U_{\theta}^{0}.

AF and responsive menu.

A direct menu
\mathcal{M}=\{E_{\theta},t_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
assigns an experiment
E_{\theta}
and a price
t_{\theta}
to each type
\theta
. We call a direct menu
\mathcal{M}

arbitrage-free (AF)
if for every type
\theta
, the intended experiment
E_{\theta}
is the optimal composite experiment for type
\theta
:

V_{\theta}(E_{\theta})-t_{\theta}\geqslant V_{\theta}(E_{B})-t_{B},\forall B\neq(\theta),\theta\in\Theta.

(1)

Note that AF is a natural extension of incentive compatibility (IC, where
B
is a singleton) to the setting with composite experiments. Besides, if
B=\varnothing
, then the AF constraint reduces to individual rationality (IR), which requires that the buyer’s utility from purchasing the intended experiment is at least the utility from purchasing nothing.

The next proposition is an extension of the classical revelation principle to the setting with composite experiments, and it shows that we can focus on direct menus without loss of generality. The omitted proofs in this section are deferred to Appendix
A
.

Proposition 2.2

(Revelation principle)
.

For any menu
\mathcal{M}
, there is a direct menu
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
that has the same expected seller revenue and is AF.

Besides AF, we also need to impose obedience constraints for the intended types to restrict the number of signals that the seller needs to design for each experiment. We call a menu
responsive
if for every type
\theta
, every signal
s\in S_{E_{\theta}}
of the intended experiment
E_{\theta}
leads type
\theta
to a different optimal action. The following proposition shows that we can focus on responsive direct menus without loss of generality.

Proposition 2.3

(Proposition 1 of
[
3
]
)
.

For any AF direct menu
\mathcal{M}
, there is a responsive direct menu
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
that has the same expected seller revenue and is AF.

The proof is the same as the original one, so we omit it here. This proposition implies that for each type
\theta
, the seller only needs to design an experiment
E_{\theta}
whose signals can be identified with recommended actions. If the menu is responsive, then the buyer will always follow the recommendation of the intended experiment, and we call the buyer
obedient
.

Remark 2.4

(Relation to
[
3
,
9
,
1
,
12
]
)
.

Our model is closely related to several standard formulations in the literature on selling information. As in
[
3
,
9
]
, we study the sale of a menu of experiments. The difference is that in their model all buyer types share the same utility function, that is,
u_{\theta}(\omega,a)=u(\omega,a),\forall\theta\in\Theta
, whereas our model allows the utility function to depend on the buyer’s type.

The works
[
1
,
12
]
study generic interactive protocols for selling information. Such protocols are more general than experiment menus: they may sell either an experiment ex ante or a realized signal ex post. When all buyer types have the same prior, namely
\mu_{\theta}=\mu,\forall\theta\in\Theta
, the optimal generic interactive protocol reduces to a menu of experiments, so our model captures that case as well. In contrast, when buyers have heterogeneous priors, the optimal generic interactive protocol need not be representable as a menu of experiments. Extending our results to this more general setting is an interesting direction for future work.

2.2
Arbitrage-free data pricing

In Section
3
, we will show that the general arbitrage-free information selling problem is hard to solve directly. Thus in Section
4
, we will focus on a special case where the arbitrage constraints can be reduced to the following intuitive and more tractable condition.

Query pricing.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a database-instance space (all possible database instances), a (deterministic) query
Q
is a map
Q:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{A}_{Q}
. For every database instance
D
, the query
Q
can be written as an experiment
E_{Q}
with signal space
\mathcal{A}_{Q}
and kernel

K_{Q}(a\mid D)=\mathbf{1}\{Q(D)=a\},\forall a\in\mathcal{A}_{Q},D\in\mathcal{D}.

To state the arbitrage constraints in query pricing, we need to compare the informativeness of different queries. A natural way is to look at the partitions of
\mathcal{D}
induced by the queries
[
15
]
.

Definition 2.5

(query-induced partition)
.

Define the equivalence relation

D\sim_{Q}D^{\prime}\iff Q(D)=Q(D^{\prime}),

and let
P_{Q}
denote the corresponding partition of
\mathcal{D}
.

If
P_{Q_{1}}
refines
P_{Q_{2}}
, then
Q_{1}
is more informative than
Q_{2}
. This is quite intuitive. For example, consider the query that returns the entire database, which is the most informative query. The induced partition places each database instance in its own singleton cell; hence it is the finest partition. By contrast, consider a constant query that returns a fixed value on every database instance. Such a query conveys no information at all, and its induced partition consists of a single cell containing the entire database-instance space; hence it is the coarsest partition.

[
15
]
prove that a pricing function
p
is arbitrage-free if and only if it satisfies the following conditions for every pair of queries
Q_{1},Q_{2},Q
:

\displaystyle P_{Q_{1}}\preceq P_{Q_{2}}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow p(Q_{2})\leqslant p(Q_{1}),

\displaystyle P_{Q}=P_{Q_{1}}\vee P_{Q_{2}}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow p(Q)\leqslant p(Q_{1})+p(Q_{2}),

where
P_{Q_{1}}\preceq P_{Q_{2}}
means that
P_{Q_{1}}
refines
P_{Q_{2}}
, and
P_{Q_{1}}\vee P_{Q_{2}}
is the coarsest common refinement of
P_{Q_{1}}
and
P_{Q_{2}}
.

Model pricing.

Let
\theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the trained model. In model pricing
[
11
,
25
]
, the seller versions this model by releasing Gaussian noisy instances of
\theta^{*}
: a model product with noise level
\delta>0
returns

\hat{\theta}=\theta^{*}+Z,\qquad Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\delta I_{d}/d),

where
I_{d}
is the
d
-dimensional identity matrix. This noisy model can be viewed as an experiment
E_{\delta}
with signal space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and kernel
1
1

1

The signal space is not a finite set, but this does not affect the discussion since we can substitute the integral for the summation in the related expressions.

K_{E_{\delta}}(\hat{\theta}\mid\theta^{*})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\delta/d)^{d/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{d}{2\delta}\|\hat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}\right),\forall\hat{\theta},\theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

To state the arbitrage constraints in model pricing, denote the precision of noise as
x=1/\delta
. If
x_{1}\geqslant x_{2}
, then the model with precision
x_{1}
is intuitively more informative than the model with precision
x_{2}
.
[
11
]
prove that a pricing function
p
is arbitrage-free if and only if it satisfies the following conditions for every pair of precisions
x_{1},x_{2}
:

\displaystyle x_{1}\leqslant x_{2}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow p(x_{1})\leqslant p(x_{2}),

\displaystyle p(x_{1}+x_{2})

\displaystyle\leqslant p(x_{1})+p(x_{2}).

Blackwell order.

As discussed above, query and model pricing are special cases of information pricing since queries and noisy models can be represented as experiments. In Section
4
, we will show that the arbitrage constraints in query pricing and model pricing can be unified through the Blackwell order on experiments.

Definition 2.6

(Blackwell dominance)
.

Experiment
E
Blackwell-dominates experiment
F
, written
E\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}F
, if there is a Markov kernel
\Gamma:S_{E}\to\Delta(S_{F})
such that

K_{F}(t\mid\omega)=\sum_{s\in S_{E}}\Gamma(t\mid s)K_{E}(s\mid\omega),\forall t\in S_{F},\omega\in\Omega.

We write
E\sim_{\mathrm{B}}F
when both
E\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}F
and
F\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}E
hold.

Blackwell’s theorem
[
8
]
says that
E\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}F
if and only if
E
gives weakly higher value than
F
for every prior and decision problem. In particular,

E\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}F\Longrightarrow W_{\theta}(E)\geqslant W_{\theta}(F),\forall\theta\in\Theta.

The following examples illustrate that if we ignore arbitrage constraints, then a menu that is optimal under standard information selling constraints may admit arbitrage behavior of buyers, thereby reducing the seller’s revenue.

Example 2.7
.

Let
\Omega=\{\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3},\omega_{4}\}
and
A=\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}\}
. Payoffs are

U=\begin{array}[]{c|cccc}&a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}&a_{4}\\
\hline\cr\omega_{1}&1&0&0.1&0\\
\omega_{2}&0&1&0&0.1\\
\omega_{3}&0.1&0&1&0\\
\omega_{4}&0&0.1&0&1\end{array}.

Consider the experiments
E_{1}:\{\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\}\ \text{vs.}\ \{\omega_{3},\omega_{4}\}
(where the signal indicates whether the state lies in the top or bottom two rows of the payoff table),
E_{2}:\{\omega_{1},\omega_{3}\}\ \text{vs.}\ \{\omega_{2},\omega_{4}\}
, and the fully informative experiment
E_{3}
. The three type priors are

\theta^{A}=(0.75,0,0.25,0),\qquad\theta^{B}=(0.75,0.25,0,0),\qquad\theta^{C}=(0.4,0.2,0.2,0.2).

A direct calculation of posterior optimal actions gives the surplus table

\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}&E_{1}&E_{2}&E_{3}\\
\hline\cr\theta^{A}&9/40&0&9/40\\
\theta^{B}&0&1/4&1/4\\
\theta^{C}&9/50&11/50&29/50\end{array}.

Set prices
t_{1}=9/40,t_{2}=1/4,t_{3}=29/50
. Then Type
\theta^{A}
buys
E_{1}
and gets payoff
0
, Type
\theta^{B}
buys
E_{2}
and gets payoff
0
, and Type
\theta^{C}
buys
E_{3}

However,

3
General Arbitrage-free Information Selling

In this section, we will give a formal formulation of the general AF information selling problem based on equation (
1
). We first show that finding an arbitraging bundle is computationally hard. Motivated by this hardness, we restrict attention to bundles of size at most a constant
h
. We formulate the resulting
h
-bounded problem as a mathematical program and show that it is a multilinear optimization problem, which makes it hard to solve. We then rewrite the program in a form that can be solved by a branch-and-bound algorithm.

3.1
Arbitrage is hard

Equation (
1
) requires that, for every type, no bundle of experiments yields higher utility than the intended experiment. In fact, the size of such a bundle is unbounded, because a buyer may purchase the same experiment repeatedly, and repeated purchases can strictly increase information. The following examples illustrate this point. The omitted proofs in this section are deferred to Appendix
B
.

Example 3.1
.

Let
\Omega=\{0,1\}
, let the signal space be
S=\{0,1\}
, and consider the binary symmetric experiment

K_{E}=\begin{pmatrix}1-\varepsilon&\varepsilon\\
\varepsilon&1-\varepsilon\end{pmatrix},\quad 0<\varepsilon<1/2.

Consider the decision problem in which the buyer wishes to guess the state
\omega
, receives payoff
1
for a correct guess and
0
otherwise, and has a uniform prior on
\Omega
. Then for every odd
m
,
E^{\otimes(m+2)}\succ_{B}E^{\otimes m}
.

Therefore the buyer’s bundle space is infinite:
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}:=\bigcup_{\ell\geqslant 0}\Theta^{\ell}
. However, if we allow all possible bundles, then the AF requirement in equation (
1
) becomes an infinite family of constraints, so the information selling problem no longer admits a finite explicit optimization formulation.

A natural restriction is to consider only bundles of size at most some fixed constant
h
, namely
\mathcal{B}_{h}:=\bigcup_{\ell=0}^{h}\Theta^{\ell}
. This restriction is reasonable since (1) in practical markets the seller may restrict the number of purchases from a buyer, and a buyer cannot realistically create arbitrarily many accounts; (2) the discussion below shows that finding an arbitraging bundle is computationally hard even in a highly structured special case.

To state the hardness result, we consider the following special class of menus. Let
U
be a finite set, and let the state space be
\Omega:=\{\bot\}\cup U
. For each set
S_{j}\subseteq U
, the menu contains a binary experiment
E_{j}
with signal space
\{0,1\}
and kernel

K_{E_{j}}(1\mid\omega)=\mathbf{1}\{\omega\in S_{j}\},\qquad K_{E_{j}}(0\mid\omega)=1-K_{E_{j}}(1\mid\omega).

Thus
E_{j}
tells the buyer whether the realized state belongs to
S_{j}
. In addition, the menu contains a designated experiment
E_{\theta}
for type
\theta
, also with signal space
\{0,1\}
, defined by

K_{E_{\theta}}(0\mid\bot)=1,\qquad K_{E_{\theta}}(1\mid u)=1\quad\forall u\in U.

Hence
E_{\theta}
reveals perfectly whether the realized state is the special state
\bot
or an ordinary state in
U
. We consider the following buyer decision problem. There is a single buyer type
\theta
with prior

\mu_{\theta}(\bot)=\frac{1}{2},\qquad\mu_{\theta}(u)=\frac{1}{2|U|}\quad\forall u\in U,

and two actions
A=\{a_{0},a_{1}\}
. For some parameter
M>0
, the buyer’s utility is

u_{\theta}(\bot,a_{0})=M,\qquad u_{\theta}(\bot,a_{1})=0,

u_{\theta}(u,a_{0})=0,\qquad u_{\theta}(u,a_{1})=M\quad\forall u\in U.

Thus the buyer’s objective is simply to decide whether the state is
\bot
or belongs to
U
.

The next theorem shows that even in this simple binary signal environment, finding a profitable bundle deviation is NP-hard.

Theorem 3.2
.

Consider instances of the form described above. Deciding whether there exists a bundle
B
of experiments from
\mathcal{M}
such that
V_{\theta}(E_{B})-t_{B}>V_{\theta}(E_{\theta})-t_{\theta}
is NP-hard.

Motivated by this hardness result, we then focus on the case in which the buyer’s bundle space is
\mathcal{B}_{h}:=\bigcup_{\ell=0}^{h}\Theta^{\ell}
,
where
h
is a fixed constant. We call this restriction the
h
-bounded AF requirement. We next give a mathematical programming formulation of the
h
-bounded AF information selling problem and analyze its structure.

3.2
The
h
-bounded AF program

We now write the
h
-bounded AF problem. By Proposition
2.3
, we focus on AF and responsive direct menus. Let
m=|A|
, identify each signal of the intended experiment for type
\theta
with a recommended action
a_{i}
, and let
\pi_{\omega,i}(\theta)
be the probability that experiment
\theta
recommends action
a_{i}
in state
\omega
. The utility of type
\theta
that chooses
E_{\theta}
and is obedient is

\widehat{W}_{\theta}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mu_{\theta}(\omega)\pi_{\omega,i}(\theta)u_{\theta}(\omega,a_{i}).

Fix a bundle
B=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{\ell})\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
. Its total price is
t_{B}:=\sum_{r=1}^{\ell}t(\theta_{r})
, with the convention
t_{\varnothing}=0
. For a nonempty bundle
B
, a joint recommendation is a vector
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell})\in[m]^{\ell}
. Let
\Pi^{B}_{\omega,\mathbf{i}}
be the probability that the composite experiment
E_{B}
recommends
\mathbf{i}
in state
\omega
, then conditional independence across purchases implies

\Pi^{B}_{\omega,\mathbf{i}}:=\prod_{r=1}^{\ell}\pi_{\omega,i_{r}}(\theta_{r}).

(2)

For the empty bundle, we use the convention that its signal space is
\{\varnothing\}
and
\Pi^{\varnothing}_{\omega,\varnothing}:=1,\forall\omega\in\Omega
.

For every type
\theta
, bundle
B\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
, and bundle signal
\mathbf{i}
, define an auxiliary variable

y^{B}_{\mathbf{i}}(\theta)=\max_{a\in A}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mu_{\theta}(\omega)\Pi^{B}_{\omega,\mathbf{i}}u_{\theta}(\omega,a),

which is the value of the deviation to bundle
B
when the joint recommendation is
\mathbf{i}
. Then the
h
-bounded AF information selling problem is

\max\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\lambda_{\theta}t(\theta)

subject to

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{\theta}-t(\theta)

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{\mathbf{i}}y^{B}_{\mathbf{i}}(\theta)-t_{B},

\displaystyle\forall\theta\in\Theta,\ \forall B\in\mathcal{B}_{h}\setminus\{(\theta)\},

\displaystyle y^{B}_{\mathbf{i}}(\theta)

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mu_{\theta}(\omega)\Pi^{B}_{\omega,\mathbf{i}}u_{\theta}(\omega,a),

\displaystyle\forall\theta\in\Theta,\ \forall B\in\mathcal{B}_{h},\ \forall\mathbf{i},\ \forall a\in A,

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mu_{\theta}(\omega)\pi_{\omega,i}(\theta)u_{\theta}(\omega,a_{i})

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mu_{\theta}(\omega)\pi_{\omega,i}(\theta)u_{\theta}(\omega,a_{j}),

\displaystyle\forall\theta\in\Theta,\ \forall i,j\in[m],

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\pi_{\omega,i}(\theta)

\displaystyle=1,\pi_{\omega,i}(\theta)\geqslant 0,

\displaystyle\forall\theta\in\Theta,\forall\omega\in\Omega,\forall i\in[m]

\displaystyle t(\theta)

\displaystyle\geqslant 0,

\displaystyle\forall\theta\in\Theta,

The objective is the expected revenue of the seller. The first constraint is the AF requirement restricted to bundles of size at most
h
. The second constraint is the definition of the auxiliary variable
y^{B}_{\mathbf{i}}(\theta)
. The third constraint enforces obedience of the intended recommendation. The fourth constraint is the feasibility constraint for the experiment variables. For the last constraint, if
h\geqslant 2
, the nonnegativity constraint is redundant: if
t(\theta)<0
, then the bundle
B=(\theta,\theta)
is strictly profitable, since
E_{\theta}\otimes E_{\theta}\succeq_{B}E_{\theta}
and hence
V_{\theta}(E_{\theta}\otimes E_{\theta})-2t(\theta)>V_{\theta}(E_{\theta})-t(\theta)
. Thus
t(\theta)\geqslant 0
is needed only if one also wishes the formulation to cover the degenerate case
h=1
.

This formulation treats ordinary IC and IR as special cases of the same AF system. If
B=(\theta^{\prime})
is a singleton bundle with
\theta^{\prime}\neq\theta
, the first constraint is exactly the usual one-experiment deviation constraint. If
B=\varnothing
, then
t_{B}=0
,
\mathbf{i}=\varnothing
, and the second constraint reduces to
y^{\varnothing}_{\varnothing}(\theta)\geqslant\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mu_{\theta}(\omega)u_{\theta}(\omega,a),\forall a\in A
, so the first constraint becomes
\widehat{W}_{\theta}-t(\theta)\geqslant U^{0}_{\theta}
, which is precisely IR.

3.3
A branch-and-bound algorithm for
h
-bounded AF program

Equation (
2
) defines
\Pi^{B}_{\omega,\mathbf{i}}
as the product of the terms
\pi_{\omega,i_{r}}(\theta_{r})
, and is therefore multilinear. As a result, the mathematical program above is not a linear program, but a multilinear optimization problem, which is well known to be challenging to solve in general. We therefore need to reformulate the program in order to obtain a tractable solution approach.

If we treat
\Pi^{B}_{\omega,\mathbf{i}}
as an independent variable, the constraint
y^{B}_{\mathbf{i}}(\theta)\geqslant\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mu_{\theta}(\omega)\Pi^{B}_{\omega,\mathbf{i}}u_{\theta}(\omega,a)
is linear. Then we need to add constraints to ensure that
\Pi^{B}_{\omega,\mathbf{i}}
is indeed the product of the
\pi_{\omega,i_{r}}(\theta_{r})
terms.

For every
B
and
\omega
,
(\Pi^{B}_{\omega,\mathbf{i}})_{\mathbf{i}}
can be viewed as a nonnegative tensor in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{[m]^{\ell}}
. For
r\in[\ell]
, let
\mathbf{i}_{-r},\mathbf{j}_{-r}\in[m]^{[\ell]\setminus\{r\}}
, and write
(a,\mathbf{i}_{-r})
for the full multi-index whose
r
-th coordinate is
a
and whose remaining coordinates are given by
\mathbf{i}_{-r}
. Proposition
3.3
below shows that equation (
2
) is equivalent to the following constraints:

\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in[m]^{\ell}:i_{r}=a}\Pi^{B}_{\omega,\mathbf{i}}=\pi_{\omega,a}(\theta_{r}),\qquad\forall r\in[\ell],\ \forall a\in[m],

(3)

\Pi^{B}_{\omega,(a,\mathbf{i}_{-r})}\Pi^{B}_{\omega,(b,\mathbf{j}_{-r})}=\Pi^{B}_{\omega,(a,\mathbf{j}_{-r})}\Pi^{B}_{\omega,(b,\mathbf{i}_{-r})},\qquad\forall r\in[\ell],\ \forall a,b\in[m],\ \forall\mathbf{i}_{-r},\mathbf{j}_{-r}.

(4)

Proposition 3.3
.

For every
B
,
\omega
, and
\mathbf{i}
, the equation (
2
) holds if and only if the constraints (
3
) and (
4
) hold.

The proof of this proposition is linear algebra calculation and is deferred to Appendix
B
. Note that the constraints (
3
) are linear, while the constraints (
4
) are bilinear. The next step is to handle the bilinear constraints.

McCormick envelopes and branch-and-bound algorithm.

Proposition
3.3
reduces all nonlinearity in the lifted
h
-bounded AF program to bilinear equalities of the form

\Pi^{B}_{\omega,(a,\mathbf{i}_{-r})}\Pi^{B}_{\omega,(b,\mathbf{j}_{-r})}=\Pi^{B}_{\omega,(a,\mathbf{j}_{-r})}\Pi^{B}_{\omega,(b,\mathbf{i}_{-r})}.

Fix one such constraint and abbreviate

x:=\Pi^{B}_{\omega,(a,\mathbf{i}_{-r})},\quad y:=\Pi^{B}_{\omega,(b,\mathbf{j}_{-r})},\quad x^{\prime}:=\Pi^{B}_{\omega,(a,\mathbf{j}_{-r})},\quad y^{\prime}:=\Pi^{B}_{\omega,(b,\mathbf{i}_{-r})}.

Introduce auxiliary variables
u
and
v
for the two products, then we have
u=xy
and
v=x^{\prime}y^{\prime}
. By McCormick envelopes
[
27
]
, for a generic bilinear term
z=xy
with bounds
x\in[L_{x},U_{x}]
and
y\in[L_{y},U_{y}]
, the convex hull over that box is described by

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\geqslant L_{x}y+L_{y}x-L_{x}L_{y},

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\geqslant U_{x}y+U_{y}x-U_{x}U_{y},

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\leqslant U_{x}y+L_{y}x-U_{x}L_{y},

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\leqslant L_{x}y+U_{y}x-L_{x}U_{y}.

For our application, we use the natural bounds

0\leqslant\pi_{\omega,i}(\theta)\leqslant 1,\qquad 0\leqslant\Pi^{B}_{\omega,\mathbf{i}}\leqslant 1,\qquad 0\leqslant y^{B}_{\mathbf{i}}(\theta)\leqslant 1,\qquad 0\leqslant t(\theta)\leqslant 1.

The last bound follows from the AF constraint and
u_{\theta}(\omega,a)\in[0,1]
.

Based on the McCormick envelopes, we can relax the bilinear constraints to linear inequalities, thus obtaining a linear programming (LP) relaxation of the lifted
h
-bounded AF program. Then we can apply a branch-and-bound algorithm
[
21
]
. The algorithm proceeds as follows.

1.

Solve the LP relaxation.
If the LP is infeasible, prune the node. Otherwise its objective value is an upper bound of the original program over the current node.

2.

Check exact feasibility.
Let
(\pi,t,y,\Pi)
be the LP solution. Compute the residual of every constraint (
4
):

\Delta^{B,\omega,r}_{a,b,\mathbf{i}_{-r},\mathbf{j}_{-r}}:=\Pi^{B}_{\omega,(a,\mathbf{i}_{-r})}\Pi^{B}_{\omega,(b,\mathbf{j}_{-r})}-\Pi^{B}_{\omega,(a,\mathbf{j}_{-r})}\Pi^{B}_{\omega,(b,\mathbf{i}_{-r})}.

If all residuals are zero (or below a chosen tolerance), then Proposition
3.3
implies that the current solution is feasible for the original
h
-bounded AF program, so its revenue is a valid lower bound and can update the incumbent solution.

3.

Prune by bounds.
If the LP upper bound is no better than the incumbent lower bound, prune the node.

4.

Choose a violated minor and branch.
Select a minor with large residual
|\Delta|
. Among the four tensor entries appearing in that minor, choose one variable whose current interval is widest, say
\Pi^{B}_{\omega,(a,\mathbf{i}_{-r})}\in[L,U]
. Split the node into two children by branching at the midpoint:

\Pi^{B}_{\omega,(a,\mathbf{i}_{-r})}\in\left[L,\frac{L+U}{2}\right]\qquad\text{or}\qquad\Pi^{B}_{\omega,(a,\mathbf{i}_{-r})}\in\left[\frac{L+U}{2},U\right].

Rebuild the McCormick envelopes for every bilinear term involving that variable.

5.

Repeat until convergence.
Continue exploring nodes until the global upper bound and incumbent lower bound differ by at most the target tolerance
\varepsilon
.

Although the branch-and-bound algorithm is guaranteed to find an
\varepsilon
-optimal solution in finite time, its worst-case running time is exponential in the number of bilinear constraints. We leave the empirical evaluation of this algorithm and the design of more efficient algorithms for future work.

4
Blackwell arbitrage-free under Threshold Utilities

Section
3
studies the general AF menu design problem. However, the AF constraints are difficult to analyze. In this section, we focus on a special case in which the AF constraints can be reduced to the more intuitive and tractable Blackwell arbitrage-free condition.

4.1
Threshold utilities collapse AF to Blackwell AF

Definition 4.1

(threshold utility)
.

A buyer type
\theta
has
threshold utility
if there exist a target experiment
T_{\theta}
and a value
v_{\theta}>0
such that

V_{\theta}(E)=\begin{cases}v_{\theta},&E\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\theta},\\
0,&E\not\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\theta}.\end{cases}

Threshold utilities model buyers who need a product to clear a concrete operational threshold. For a dataset example, a lender may need both income and debt variables for every applicant in a target pool before a screening rule is usable: if one required field is missing, the workflow cannot be deployed. For a model example, a firm may buy a prediction API or an LLM service only if it reaches a minimum accuracy or safety benchmark needed for deployment. In all of these cases, the buyer values information that is sufficient for the task, and treats information that is still insufficient as simply not useful for that task.

A natural question is whether threshold utilities can be explicitly realized in a decision problem. The next two propositions give explicit realizations of threshold utilities in subset query (the query returns a deterministic subset of the database) and model selling regimes. The omitted proofs in this section are deferred to Appendix
C
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Fix a finite set
U
, and let the database-instance space be
\mathcal{D}:=2^{U}
. For each
R\subseteq U
, define the deterministic query

Q_{R}(D):=D\cap R,\qquad D\in\mathcal{D},

and let
E_{R}:=E_{Q_{R}}
be the induced experiment. Fix nonempty target sets
S_{1},\dots,S_{n}\subseteq U
and values
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}>0
. Then there exists an explicit decision problem such that for each type
\theta_{j}
and each subset query
R\subseteq U
,

V_{\theta_{j}}(E_{R})=\begin{cases}v_{j},&E_{R}\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}E_{S_{j}}\ \text{(equivalently, }S_{j}\subseteq R\text{)},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proposition 4.3
.

Fix precisions
0<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{m}
, and for each
x_{i}
let
G_{x_{i}}:=G_{dx_{i}I_{d}}
, equivalently

Y=w+\varepsilon,\qquad\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(dx_{i})^{-1}I_{d}),

where
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the unknown model parameter. Fix threshold levels
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}
and values
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}>0
. Then there exists an explicit decision problem such that for each buyer type
\theta_{j}
and each offered model
G_{x_{i}}
,

V_{\theta_{j}}(G_{x_{i}})=\begin{cases}v_{j},&x_{i}\geqslant a_{j},\\
0,&x_{i}<a_{j}.\end{cases}

Based on the definition of threshold utility, we then show that the AF constraints for any type with threshold utility are equivalent to Blackwell AF defined below.

Definition 4.4

(Blackwell AF)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a family of experiments closed under garbling and composition. A price function
p:\mathcal{E}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is Blackwell arbitrage-free (Blackwell AF) if there do not exist experiments
E_{1},\ldots,E_{k},F\in\mathcal{E}
such that

E_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes E_{k}\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}F\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{r=1}^{k}p(E_{r})<p(F).

The next proposition gives a characterization of Blackwell AF that is analogous to the characterizations of query and model AF in Section
2.2
. The proof is trivial and is deferred to Appendix
C
.

Proposition 4.5
.

A price function
p
is Blackwell AF if and only if it satisfies:

1.

Blackwell monotonicity
: if
E\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}F
, then
p(E)\geqslant p(F)
;

2.

parallel subadditivity
: for every
E_{1},E_{2}
,
p(E_{1}\otimes E_{2})\leqslant p(E_{1})+p(E_{2})
.

The next theorem is the main result of this section. It says that for any type with threshold utility, the AF constraints are exactly the Blackwell AF constraints.

Theorem 4.6
.

Fix a direct menu
M=\{(E_{\theta},t_{\theta})\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
, and suppose every type
\theta
has threshold utility in the sense of Definition
4.1
where
E_{\theta}=T_{\theta}
. Assume also that the assigned experiment is individually rational, so
t_{\theta}\leqslant v_{\theta}
. Then for each type
\theta
, the following are equivalent:

1.

\theta
has no profitable bundle deviation from its assigned experiment;

2.

every bundle
B
satisfying
E_{B}\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}E_{\theta}
has total price at least
t_{\theta}
.

Proof.

1
\Rightarrow
2 is trivial according to the definition of Blackwell dominance. For 2
\Rightarrow
1, we show that profitable deviations are exactly the cheaper Blackwell substitutes. Under the threshold assumption, if
E_{B}\not\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}E_{\theta}
, then
V_{\theta}(E_{B})=0
, and since prices are nonnegative and
t_{\theta}\leqslant v_{\theta}
, such a bundle cannot be better than the assigned net utility
v_{\theta}-t_{\theta}
. Hence AF is exactly the requirement that no bundle
B
with
E_{B}\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}E_{\theta}
has total price less than
t_{\theta}
.
∎

This theorem shows that, under threshold utilities, which are common in practice, the AF constraints can be reduced to the more intuitive Blackwell AF condition. Outside the threshold-utility regime, AF is a strictly stronger constraint than Blackwell AF. The following example illustrates this point.

Proposition 4.7
.

There is an information selling instance in which the optimal revenue achieved by a Blackwell AF mechanism is strictly greater than the optimal revenue achieved by an AF mechanism.

4.2
Query and model AF as special cases of Blackwell AF

Next, we show that the AF definitions used in query pricing and model pricing in the previous literature (Section
2.2
) are both special cases of Blackwell AF. On the one hand, this establishes Blackwell AF as a unifying framework; on the other hand, it indicates that the query and model AF notions in the existing literature do not necessarily align with general AF.

We first show that query AF is exactly the restriction of Blackwell AF to deterministic queries.

Proposition 4.8
.

For deterministic queries
Q_{1},Q_{2},Q
:

1.

E_{Q_{1}}\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}E_{Q_{2}}\Longleftrightarrow P_{Q_{1}}\preceq P_{Q_{2}}
, where
P_{Q_{1}}\preceq P_{Q_{2}}
means that
P_{Q_{1}}
refines
P_{Q_{2}}
;

2.

E_{Q}\sim_{\mathrm{B}}E_{Q_{1}}\otimes E_{Q_{2}}\Longleftrightarrow P_{Q}=P_{Q_{1}}\vee P_{Q_{2}}
.

Consequently, Proposition
4.5
restricted to deterministic queries reproduces exactly the query AF condition.

We then consider model AF. For
J\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{d}
, let
G_{J}
denote the Gaussian experiment
Y=\theta+\varepsilon,\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,J^{-1})
. Note that this setting generalizes the setting of
[
11
]
. If we restrict
J
to
J=dxI_{d}
, where

is the scalar precision, then we recover the AF definition of
[
11
]
.

Proposition 4.9
.

For Gaussian location experiments:

1.

G_{J_{1}}\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}G_{J_{2}}
if and only if
J_{1}\succeq J_{2}
in the Loewner order (i.e.,
J_{1}-J_{2}
is positive semidefinite);

2.

G_{J_{1}}\otimes G_{J_{2}}\sim_{\mathrm{B}}G_{J_{1}+J_{2}}
.

Hence, a price rule on this Gaussian family is Blackwell AF if and only if its coordinate representation
\bar{p}(J):=p(G_{J})
is monotone in the Loewner order and subadditive under matrix addition.

Information-theoretic interpretation.

The mutual information between the underlying state and the signal of an experiment is a natural measure of informativeness. The next proposition shows that mutual information pricing is Blackwell AF, and hence is a valid AF pricing rule under threshold utilities.

Proposition 4.10
.

Fix a prior
\mu
on the underlying state
W
. For any experiment
E
, let
Y_{E}
denote its signal and define

p_{\mu}(E):=I_{\mu}(W;Y_{E}).

where
I_{\mu}(W;Y_{E})
is the mutual information between
W
and
Y_{E}
under the prior
\mu
. Then
p_{\mu}
is Blackwell-monotone and subadditive under independent composition.

Corollary
4.11
gives the explicit form of mutual information pricing for query and model.

Corollary 4.11
.

Under the price rule
p_{\mu}(E)=I_{\mu}(W;Y_{E})
:

1.

for a deterministic query
Q
on the possible underlying database

p_{\mu}(E_{Q})=I(D;Q(D))=H(Q(D))
, where

is the Shannon entropy of the query answer under the prior
\mu
.

2.

for a Gaussian experiment
G_{J}
with prior
\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{0})
,
p_{\mu}(G_{J})=\frac{1}{2}\log\det(I+\Sigma_{0}J)
.

Note that
p_{\mu}(E_{Q})
is consistent with the Shannon-entropy pricing discussed in
[

. Moreover, the map
J\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\log\det(I+\Sigma_{0}J)
is monotone in the Loewner order and subadditive under matrix addition.

4.3
Pricing under structured menus

We now turn from the Blackwell characterization to the pricing problem faced by a seller who has committed to a native menu class. However, even under threshold utilities, Blackwell arbitrage-free does not make pricing tractable in general. But for the two main product classes we care about: queries and models, there are structured settings in which the seller can compute optimal arbitrage-free prices efficiently, or obtain provable approximation guarantees.

Blackwell pricing remains hard in general.

The difficulty persists even when every buyer requires at most two elementary data fields. To prove this, we reduce from maximum independent set. Given a graph
G=(V,E)
with
n:=|V|
, take
U:=V
and let
E_{R}
be the deterministic experiment from Proposition
4.2
that reveals exactly the fields in
R\subseteq U
. The candidate products are the singleton experiments
E_{\{v\}}
for vertices and the pair experiments
E_{\{u,v\}}
for edges.

We now define the instance. For each vertex
v\in V
, create one buyer type with target experiment
E_{\{v\}}

Tree-structured dataset menus.

Many dataset sellers organize their products through a natural hierarchy. For example, a mobility-data provider may sell a national dataset, regional datasets, city-level datasets, and neighborhood-level datasets. In this case, a broader product is assembled from disjoint child products, while products on different branches concern separate parts of the database.

Formally, let
U
be a finite set of elementary data fields. A family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{U}
is
laminar
if for any
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
we have either
A\subseteq B
,
B\subseteq A
, or
A\cap B=\varnothing
. Such a family admits a natural rooted-tree representation
T
: each node
u
corresponds to a set
S_{u}\in\mathcal{F}
, the leaves correspond to singletons, and for every internal node
u
the children
\mathrm{Ch}(u)
are pairwise disjoint and satisfy
S_{u}=\bigcup_{w\in\mathrm{Ch}(u)}S_{w}
. For each node
u
, there is a buyer type
\theta_{u}
with mass
b_{u}>0
and value
v_{u}>0
; this type obtains value
v_{u}
from any deterministic experiment
E_{R}
if and only if
S_{u}\subseteq R
, and otherwise obtains value
0
.

To identify arbitrage opportunities, for a finite posted menu
M=\{(E_{j},\tau_{j}):j=1,\ldots,N\}
and any target experiment
F
, define the
indirect bundle cost

c_{M}(F):=\inf\Bigl\{\sum_{r=1}^{k}\tau_{j_{r}}:E_{j_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes E_{j_{k}}\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}F,\ k\geqslant 0\Bigr\}.

In particular, if
c_{M}(E_{j})<\tau_{j}
for a posted product
E_{j}
, then a buyer can bypass its direct price by purchasing a cheaper bundle.

In the laminar-tree instance, auditing arbitrage is easy: one can compute the indirect bundle costs
c_{M}(E_{u})
for all nodes
u
in linear time
O(|\mathcal{F}|)
via two traversals:

1.

Direct prices.
Let
\tau(u)
be the posted price of node
u
, and set
\tau(u)=+\infty
if
u
is not directly offered.

2.

Postorder.
Compute the cheapest cost of synthesizing
E_{u}
using only products in the subtree of
u
:

h(u)=\begin{cases}\tau(u),&\text{if $u$ is a leaf},\\
\min\{\tau(u),\sum_{w\in\mathrm{Ch}(u)}h(w)\},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

3.

Preorder.
Let
r
be the root. Set
c_{M}(r)=h(r)
, and for every non-root node
u
set

c_{M}(u)=\min\{h(u),c_{M}(\mathrm{par}(u))\}.

Having seen that buyers can efficiently audit arbitrage on a laminar tree, we now show that the seller can also compute an optimal Blackwell-arbitrage-free pricing rule efficiently in this instance.

Assume the values are integral and satisfy
v_{u}\in\{0,1,\ldots,V\}
. Define
q(u)
as the total price paid to obtain
E_{u}
after buyers optimally bundle purchases.
In a laminar tree, the Blackwell arbitrage-free constraints localize: a vector
q
is feasible if and only if it satisfies:
q(w)\leqslant q(u)
for every child
w\in\mathrm{Ch}(u)
and
q(u)\leqslant\sum_{w\in\mathrm{Ch}(u)}q(w)
for every internal node
u
.

For each node
u
and candidate price
x
, let
F_{u}(x)
be the maximum revenue obtainable from the subtree rooted at
u
, conditional on setting
q(u)=x
. The local contribution of type
\theta_{u}
is
r_{u}(x):=b_{u}x\mathbf{1}[x\leqslant v_{u}]
. If
u
is a leaf then
F_{u}(x)=r_{u}(x)
. If
u
is internal, the children prices
x_{w}:=q(w)
must satisfy
0\leqslant x_{w}\leqslant x
for all
w\in\mathrm{Ch}(u)
and
\sum_{w\in\mathrm{Ch}(u)}x_{w}\geqslant x
, hence

F_{u}(x)=r_{u}(x)+\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant x_{w}\leqslant x\ \forall w\in\mathrm{Ch}(u)\\
\sum_{w\in\mathrm{Ch}(u)}x_{w}\geqslant x\end{subarray}}\sum_{w\in\mathrm{Ch}(u)}F_{w}(x_{w}).

For each fixed
u
and
x
, the inner maximum is a knapsack-style convolution over the children, indexed by the capped sum of child prices. Evaluating all states bottom-up takes
O(|\mathcal{F}|V^{3})
time, and the optimal revenue is
\max_{0\leqslant x\leqslant V}F_{r}(x)
at the root
r
.

Model menus: additive precision.

As discussed above, independently purchased noisy model releases add precision, and a Blackwell arbitrage-free model price must be monotone and subadditive in precision. Even in this scalar-precision setting, computing the exact revenue-maximizing Blackwell arbitrage-free price is hard. Nevertheless, an efficient dynamic program achieves a 1/2 approximation. The proof is the same as the proof of Theorems 7 and 10 in
[

]
, so we omit it and refer readers to that paper for details.

Proposition 4.13
.

For scalar-precision model menus with threshold buyers:

1.

with Blackwell arbitrage-free, exact optimization is coNP-hard;

2.

an
O(n^{2})
dynamic program returns a Blackwell arbitrage-free menu with revenue at least 1/2 of the exact optimum.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

We study the seller’s optimal data pricing problem when the value of data is derived from its effect on the buyer’s decision utility and when buyers may arbitrage by combining multiple purchases. Our framework models versioned datasets and noisy machine-learning models in a common experiment-based language, establishes direct-menu reductions for bundle-robust design, and formalizes the general arbitrage-free problem. We show that the general problem is computationally hard and admits a multilinear
h
-bounded formulation with a tensor-lifted branch-and-bound implementation. We then identify threshold utilities as a regime in which the full arbitrage-free constraints collapse to Blackwell arbitrage-free, thereby recovering the standard query and model pricing conditions as special cases. Finally, under menu restrictions and threshold utilities, we characterize a computational frontier: general deterministic set menus remain hard, laminar dataset menus admit exact dynamic programming, and additive-precision model menus reduce to monotone subadditive chain pricing with known hardness and approximation guarantees.

Several directions remain for future work. First, as noted in Section
2
, it would be natural to extend the analysis from experiment menus to more general interactive information selling mechanisms. Second, as noted in Section
3.3
, the
h
-bounded branch-and-bound formulation provides an exact benchmark but may be computationally expensive in the worst case, so designing faster algorithms and carrying out a systematic empirical evaluation are important next steps. Third, it would be useful to develop practical pricing algorithms for other realistic market environments, such as overlapping data subsets, or LLM services with multiple quality, latency, context-window, or safety tiers.

1/4
. Type
H
wants to guess the ordered pair
(X,Y)
. It receives payoff
1
if both bits are guessed correctly and
0
otherwise, so the maximum value of any experiment for type
H
is
3/4
. Besides, each buyer type has the same uniform prior on
\{0,1\}^{3}
, and the three type masses are
\lambda_{A}=\lambda_{B}=\lambda_{H}=1
.

Consider three experiments
E_{X},E_{Y},F
. Experiment
E_{X}
reveals
X
,
E_{Y}
reveals
Y
, and
F
reveals the full state
(X,Y,Z)
. The posted prices are
t_{X}=1/4,t_{Y}=1/4,t_{F}=3/4
. If type
A
buys
E_{X}
, type
B
buys
E_{Y}
, and type
H
buys
F
, then the Blackwell AF constraints (note that
E_{X}
and
E_{Y}
cannot be used to construct a cheaper Blackwell substitute for
F
) and IR constraints are satisfied, and the menu obtains total revenue
1/4+1/4+3/4=5/4
. No IR menu can obtain more, because the price for each type cannot exceed its maximum value. Therefore the Blackwell AF optimum is
5/4
.

However, the AF constraints are not satisfied. Type
H
can buy
E_{X}
and
E_{Y}
, learn
(X,Y)
, thus obtaining the same value as from
F
, but pay only
1/2
. We now prove that this deviation forces a revenue loss for
any
AF menu. Consider any direct menu with assigned experiments
E_{A},E_{B},E_{H}
and prices
t_{A},t_{B},t_{H}
satisfying AF. Let
S=t_{A}+t_{B}
. IR gives
t_{A}\leqslant 1/4,t_{B}\leqslant 1/4,t_{H}\leqslant 3/4
. For any experiment
E
, let
p_{X}(E)
be the maximum probability of correctly guessing
X
after observing
E
under type
A
’s prior. Then the value of
E
for type
A
is
V_{A}(E)=(p_{X}(E)-1/2)/2
.
Since type
A
buys
E_{A}
at price
t_{A}
, IR implies
V_{A}(E_{A})\geqslant t_{A}
, hence
p_{X}(E_{A})\geqslant 1/2+2t_{A}
. Similarly, if
p_{Y}(E_{B})
is the best probability of guessing
Y
after
E_{B}
, then
p_{Y}(E_{B})\geqslant 1/2+2t_{B}
.

Now let type
H
buy the bundle
E_{A}\otimes E_{B}
. It can use the optimal rule for guessing
X
from
E_{A}
and the optimal rule for guessing
Y
from
E_{B}
, and then output the pair of guesses. Let
C_{X}
be the event that the first rule guesses
X
correctly and
C_{Y}
the event that the second rule guesses
Y
correctly. By the union bound,
\Pr[C_{X}\cap C_{Y}]\geqslant(1/2+2t_{A})+(1/2+2t_{B})-1=2S
. Type
H
can also ignore all purchased signals and get outside success probability
1/4
. Therefore
V_{H}(E_{A}\otimes E_{B})\geqslant\max\{0,2S-1/4\}
. AF for type
H
requires
V_{H}(E_{H})-t_{H}\geqslant V_{H}(E_{A}\otimes E_{B})-S
.
Since
V_{H}(E_{H})\leqslant 3/4
, we get
3/4-t_{H}\geqslant\max\{0,2S-1/4\}-S
. If
S<1/8
, then total revenue is at most
S+3/4<7/8<1
. If
S\geqslant 1/8
, then the maximum term equals
2S-1/4
, so
3/4-t_{H}\geqslant S-1/4
, hence
t_{H}\leqslant 1-S
. Thus
t_{A}+t_{B}+t_{H}=S+t_{H}\leqslant 1
.
So every AF menu has total revenue at most
1
. The bound is tight: post
(E_{X},1/4)
,
(E_{Y},1/4)
, and
(F,1/2)
. Therefore the AF optimum is
1
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.8
.

We first prove part (1). Assume that
E_{Q_{1}}\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}E_{Q_{2}}
. Then there is a post-processing map from the output of
Q_{1}
to the output distribution of
Q_{2}
. If
Q_{1}(D)=Q_{1}(D^{\prime})
, the input to this post-processing map is identical under
D
and
D^{\prime}
. Therefore the post-processed output distribution must also be identical. Since
E_{Q_{2}}
is deterministic, this implies
Q_{2}(D)=Q_{2}(D^{\prime})
. Every cell of
P_{Q_{1}}
is contained in a cell of
P_{Q_{2}}
, so
P_{Q_{1}}
refines
P_{Q_{2}}
.

Conversely, suppose
P_{Q_{1}}
refines
P_{Q_{2}}
. Then
Q_{2}
is constant on every fiber of
Q_{1}
. Hence there exists a deterministic map
g:\mathcal{A}_{Q_{1}}\to\mathcal{A}_{Q_{2}}
such that
g(Q_{1}(D))=Q_{2}(D)
for every
D\in\mathcal{D}
.
Applying
g
to the signal of
E_{Q_{1}}
produces exactly the signal of
E_{Q_{2}}
, so
E_{Q_{1}}\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}E_{Q_{2}}
.

We next prove part (2). The product experiment
E_{Q_{1}}\otimes E_{Q_{2}}
generates two conditionally independent degenerate signals under database
D
, namely
Q_{1}(D)
and
Q_{2}(D)
. Hence it is Blackwell-equivalent to the deterministic pair-query experiment
E_{(Q_{1},Q_{2})}
that outputs
(Q_{1}(D),Q_{2}(D))
.

The pair query
(Q_{1},Q_{2})
induces exactly the common refinement partition
P_{Q_{1}}\vee P_{Q_{2}}
. Hence, if
P_{Q}=P_{Q_{1}}\vee P_{Q_{2}}
, then
Q
and the pair query
(Q_{1},Q_{2})
induce the same partition. By part (1), this implies
E_{Q}\sim_{\mathrm{B}}E_{(Q_{1},Q_{2})}\sim_{\mathrm{B}}E_{Q_{1}}\otimes E_{Q_{2}}
.

Conversely, if
E_{Q}\sim_{\mathrm{B}}E_{Q_{1}}\otimes E_{Q_{2}}
, then
E_{Q}\sim_{\mathrm{B}}E_{(Q_{1},Q_{2})}
. Applying part (1) in both directions yields
P_{Q}=P_{(Q_{1},Q_{2})}=P_{Q_{1}}\vee P_{Q_{2}}
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.9
.

We prove the two structural statements.

We first prove part (1). Suppose
J_{1}\succeq J_{2}
. Since inversion reverses the Loewner order on positive definite matrices,
J_{2}^{-1}-J_{1}^{-1}\succeq 0
. Given a sample
Y_{1}=\theta+\varepsilon_{1}
from
G_{J_{1}}
, draw independent noise
\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,J_{2}^{-1}-J_{1}^{-1})
and output
Y_{1}+\eta
. Conditional on
\theta
, this output has distribution
\mathcal{N}(\theta,J_{1}^{-1}+J_{2}^{-1}-J_{1}^{-1})=\mathcal{N}(\theta,J_{2}^{-1})
.
Thus
G_{J_{2}}
is obtained from
G_{J_{1}}
by post-processing, and
G_{J_{1}}\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}G_{J_{2}}
.

Conversely, suppose
G_{J_{1}}\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}G_{J_{2}}
. Fix any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
t>0
, and place on the unknown state
\theta
the Gaussian prior
\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,tvv^{\top})
.
By Proposition
4.10
, Blackwell dominance implies that the mutual-information price of
G_{J_{1}}
is at least that of
G_{J_{2}}
under this prior. Since
Y_{r}=\theta+\varepsilon_{r}
with
\varepsilon_{r}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,J_{r}^{-1})
for
r=1,2
, the marginal law of
Y_{r}
is
\mathcal{N}(0,tvv^{\top}+J_{r}^{-1})
, while
Y_{r}\mid\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,J_{r}^{-1})
. Therefore

I(\theta;Y_{r})=\frac{1}{2}\log\det\!\bigl((tvv^{\top}+J_{r}^{-1})J_{r}\bigr)=\frac{1}{2}\log\det(I+t\,vv^{\top}J_{r})=\frac{1}{2}\log\bigl(1+t\,v^{\top}J_{r}v\bigr),

where the last step is the matrix determinant lemma. Hence
1+t\,v^{\top}J_{1}v\geqslant 1+t\,v^{\top}J_{2}v
. Since
t>0
and
v
are arbitrary, we obtain
v^{\top}(J_{1}-J_{2})v\geqslant 0
for every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which proves
J_{1}\succeq J_{2}
.

We next prove part (2). Let
Y_{r}=\theta+\varepsilon_{r}
, where
\varepsilon_{r}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,J_{r}^{-1})
for
r=1,2
, with
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}
independent conditional on
\theta
. Set
J:=J_{1}+J_{2}
and define the weighted average
T:=J^{-1}(J_{1}Y_{1}+J_{2}Y_{2})
. Then
T=\theta+J^{-1}(J_{1}\varepsilon_{1}+J_{2}\varepsilon_{2})
, and the covariance of the noise term is

J^{-1}(J_{1}J_{1}^{-1}J_{1}+J_{2}J_{2}^{-1}J_{2})J^{-1}=J^{-1}(J_{1}+J_{2})J^{-1}=J^{-1}.

Hence
T
is distributed as
G_{J}
, so
G_{J_{1}}\otimes G_{J_{2}}\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}G_{J}
.

For the reverse Blackwell domination, define the residual
R:=Y_{1}-Y_{2}=\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}
.
The covariance between
T-\theta
and
R
is

\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(J^{-1}(J_{1}\varepsilon_{1}+J_{2}\varepsilon_{2}),\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}\bigr)=J^{-1}(J_{1}J_{1}^{-1}-J_{2}J_{2}^{-1})=0.

Since the vector is jointly Gaussian,
T
and
R
are independent conditional on
\theta
; moreover
R\sim\mathcal{N}(0,J_{1}^{-1}+J_{2}^{-1})
, which does not depend on
\theta
. Given only a draw
T
from
G_{J}
, a garbling can independently sample such an
R
and reconstruct

Y_{1}=T+J^{-1}J_{2}R,\qquad Y_{2}=T-J^{-1}J_{1}R.

These equations are obtained by solving
T=J^{-1}(J_{1}Y_{1}+J_{2}Y_{2})
and
R=Y_{1}-Y_{2}
. The reconstructed pair has the same conditional Gaussian distribution as the original pair
(Y_{1},Y_{2})
. Therefore
G_{J}\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}G_{J_{1}}\otimes G_{J_{2}}
, proving Blackwell equivalence.

The final pricing characterization is Proposition
4.5
applied to the coordinate identity
G_{J_{1}}\otimes G_{J_{2}}\sim_{\mathrm{B}}G_{J_{1}+J_{2}}
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.10
.

If
E\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}F
, then
Y_{F}
is obtained from
Y_{E}
by post-processing, so
W\to Y_{E}\to Y_{F}
is a Markov chain. By the data processing inequality,
I_{\mu}(W;Y_{E})\geqslant I_{\mu}(W;Y_{F})
.

Now let
E_{1},E_{2}
be independently issued conditional on
W
, with signals
Y_{1},Y_{2}
. Chain rule gives
I_{\mu}(W;Y_{1},Y_{2})=I_{\mu}(W;Y_{1})+I_{\mu}(W;Y_{2}\mid Y_{1})
. Because
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
are conditionally independent given
W
,
H(Y_{2}\mid W,Y_{1})=H(Y_{2}\mid W)
.
Therefore

I_{\mu}(W;Y_{2}\mid Y_{1})=H(Y_{2}\mid Y_{1})-H(Y_{2}\mid W,Y_{1})\leqslant H(Y_{2})-H(Y_{2}\mid W)=I_{\mu}(W;Y_{2}).

Combining these identities yields
I_{\mu}(W;Y_{1},Y_{2})\leqslant I_{\mu}(W;Y_{1})+I_{\mu}(W;Y_{2})
, which is the desired subadditivity.
∎

Proof of Corollary
4.11
.

For part (1), a deterministic query satisfies
H(Q(D)\mid D)=0
. Hence
p_{\mu}(E_{Q})=I(D;Q(D))=H(Q(D))-H(Q(D)\mid D)=H(Q(D))
.
This is exactly the Shannon-entropy price for instance-independent query pricing discussed in
[
15
]
.

For part (2), let
Y=\theta+\varepsilon
, where
\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{0})
and
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,J^{-1})
is independent of
\theta
. Then
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{0}+J^{-1})
and
Y\mid\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,J^{-1})
. Using the Gaussian differential-entropy formula
h(Z)=\frac{1}{2}\log\det((2\pi e)^{d}\Sigma_{Z})
, we obtain

p_{\mu}(G_{J})=I(\theta;Y)=h(Y)-h(Y\mid\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\log\det\!\bigl((\Sigma_{0}+J^{-1})J\bigr)=\frac{1}{2}\log\det(I+\Sigma_{0}J).

To verify the claimed order properties, first note that if
J_{1}\succeq J_{2}
, then Proposition
4.9
gives
G_{J_{1}}\succeq_{\mathrm{B}}G_{J_{2}}
. Proposition
4.10
therefore implies
\frac{1}{2}\log\det(I+\Sigma_{0}J_{1})\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\log\det(I+\Sigma_{0}J_{2})
. Similarly, Proposition
4.9
gives
G_{J_{1}}\otimes G_{J_{2}}\sim_{\mathrm{B}}G_{J_{1}+J_{2}}
, so Proposition
4.10
yields

\frac{1}{2}\log\det(I+\Sigma_{0}(J_{1}+J_{2}))\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\log\det(I+\Sigma_{0}J_{1})+\frac{1}{2}\log\det(I+\Sigma_{0}J_{2}).

Restricting to
J=dxI_{d}
gives the isotropic scalar family
p_{\mu}(G_{dxI_{d}})=\frac{1}{2}\log\det(I+dx\Sigma_{0})
, which is the one-dimensional precision pricing rule induced by the log-determinant information value.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.12
.

Let
n:=|V|
,
m:=|E|
, and fix any Blackwell-arbitrage-free menu
M
supported on the candidate products. Let
q(R):=c_{M}(E_{R})
be the induced indirect price of obtaining the set
R
. By definition of
c_{M}
,
q
is monotone under inclusion and subadditive under union: if
R\subseteq R^{\prime}
, then any bundle that dominates
E_{R^{\prime}}
also dominates
E_{R}
, so
q(R)\leqslant q(R^{\prime})
, and if bundles dominate
E_{R_{1}}
and
E_{R_{2}}
then their concatenation dominates
E_{R_{1}\cup R_{2}}
, so
q(R_{1}\cup R_{2})\leqslant q(R_{1})+q(R_{2})
. Define
q_{v}:=q(\{v\})
,
q_{uv}:=q(\{u,v\})
, and
I:=\{v\in V:q_{v}\leqslant 1\}
.
A vertex type contributes at most
1
if
v\in I
and contributes
0
otherwise, so vertex revenue is at most
|I|
.

For an edge
e=\{u,v\}
, if both endpoints lie in
I
, then subadditivity gives
q_{uv}\leqslant q_{u}+q_{v}\leqslant 2
.
The
B
corresponding edge types therefore contribute at most
2B
. If at least one endpoint is outside
I
, they contribute at most
3B
. Hence

\operatorname{Rev}(M)\leqslant|I|+2B|E[I]|+3B(m-|E[I]|)=3Bm+|I|-B|E[I]|.

If
I
is not independent, then
|E[I]|\geqslant 1
, and therefore
\operatorname{Rev}(M)\leqslant 3Bm+n-(n+1)=3Bm-1
.
If
I
is independent, then
|I|\leqslant\alpha(G)
and
|E[I]|=0
, so the earlier bound gives
\operatorname{Rev}(M)\leqslant 3Bm+\alpha(G)
. If
I
is not independent, the displayed inequality gives
\operatorname{Rev}(M)\leqslant 3Bm-1\leqslant 3Bm+\alpha(G)
. Therefore every Blackwell-arbitrage-free menu earns at most
3B|E|+\alpha(G)
.

This upper bound is attainable. Let
I^{\star}
be a maximum independent set. The seller offers
E_{\{v\}}
at price
1
for every
v\in I^{\star}
, offers every edge product
E_{\{u,v\}}
at price
3
, and offers no other products. Blackwell arbitrage-free holds because the singleton products sold at price
1
correspond to an independent set, so no edge product can be synthesized from a cheaper bundle of singleton purchases. The resulting revenue is
3B|E|+|I^{\star}|=3B|E|+\alpha(G)
, matching the upper bound.
∎
