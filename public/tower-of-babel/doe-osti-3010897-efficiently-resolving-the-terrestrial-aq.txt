Final Technical Report: Efficiently Resolving the Terrestrial‐Aquatic Interface in E3SM
with Sub‐Grid Methods to Improve Coastal Simulations

DOE Award Number: DE-SC0021105
Recipient Organizations: University of Notre Dame, Argonne National Laboratory, Los Alamos
National Laboratory

Major goals

The broad goal of this project was to extend the capabilities of MPAS‐Ocean, the ocean
component of DOE’s Energy Exascale Earth System Model (E3SM), to better resolve the
hydrodynamics of the terrestrial‐aquatic interface without significantly increasing computing
resource requirements. Specifically, our goal was to develop and implement approaches that
allow for finer scales so that we can accurately connect the upland hydrologic system with the
ocean and estuarine systems. This involves better resolving the hydraulic and hydrologic
processes across the terrestrial‐aquatic interface, often referred to as the “coastal zone,” which
consists broadly of an intertidal zone and an adjacent low lying coastal floodplain with a
complex dendritic network of hydraulically efficient channels connected to estuarine systems
and back bays often cut off from the ocean by barrier islands that are intersected with high
energy inlets. The processes driving flow across the terrestrial‐aquatic interface include upland
and floodplain hydrology, tides, wind and atmospheric pressure (including coastally generated
storm surge across continental shelves and within estuaries and bays), wind waves and water
temperature and salinity gradients. Since current technology of MPAS‐Ocean resolves the
ocean and its coast with unstructured mesh elements no finer than 6 km which are not
anticipated to be finer than 2 km in the near future, these complex small scale systems across
the terrestrial‐aquatic interface would not be resolved at available mesh resolutions in the
foreseeable future. Directly resolving these scales in MPAS‐Ocean would require coastal mesh
resolution two orders of magnitude finer than currently employed, a prohibitive number of
degrees‐of‐freedom. The latter is not a viable option given current and expected computing
capabilities for climate applications requiring century‐long simulations.

Instead of attempting to improve mesh resolution beyond feasible limits, our goal was to
implement recently‐developed sub-grid techniques for coastal inundation. These techniques
use integral properties of high‐resolution bathymetry, topography, and land cover to introduce
corrections into coarser resolution models, and can improve accuracy greatly near
the land‐water interface while having no significant effects in deep waters. Our methods target
both the lack of resolution in the continuity equation as well as in the momentum equations, an
important advancement in sub-grid scale theories and implementation. With these methods, a
sub‐grid scale channel is able to not only connect the flow network correctly in terms of
conveyance, but the frictional resistance can also be accounted for. Additional overhead
associated with sub-grid techniques is relatively small since computationally intensive additional
quantities to be evaluated from given high resolution bathymetry/topography and bottom
roughness data can be precomputed as lookup tables. The methods, developed originally for
depth integrated storm surge modelling, were incorporated into the external two‐dimensional

barotropic mode of the MPAS‐Ocean. The subgrid techniques implemented were focused on
the small-scale features within and across the coastal floodplain all of which are vertically well
mixed. Therefore subgrid implementation within MPAS-O was focused on a single vertical layer,
the appropriate number of layers across the targeted implementation region.

Based on our work in the Advanced CIRCulation (ADCIRC) system, a finite element based
coastal model used by NOAA, the USACE, and FEMA, we anticipated seeing simulations
applying O(2‐6 km) MPAS‐Ocean mesh resolution with sub‐grid corrections yielding results
that retain much of the accuracy of much higher‐resolution O(125m to 1km) simulations but with
a fraction of the computational cost. This would result in significantly better water levels, flows
and transport processes across the terrestrial aquatic interface.

Tasks

Task 1: Development, implementation, verification and validation of subgrid methods into
MPAS-Ocean. In this task, we worked closely with Steven Brus of ANL, Mark Petersen and
Darren Engwirda1 of LANL both in the development and seamless integration in the E3SM
modelling ecosystem.

Task 2: Advances/collaboration on tidal modeling in MPAS-Ocean in order to improve inner shelf
tides which penetrate into the coastal floodplain. At the start of this project, we joined a DOE
group led by Andrew Roberts, Mark Petersen at LANL and Steven Brus at ANL focused on
improving tides within the MPAS‐Ocean system. Since tides are a critical driver at the
terrestrial‐aquatic interface, it is essential to represent them with high skill on the inner
continental shelf in order for the sub‐grid methods we are implementing across the floodplain to
work correctly. We focused on applying into MPAS-Ocean successful features from NOAA’s
Storm and Tide Operational Forecasting System (STOFS) 2D+ Global, an operational total water
level forecasting system based on the ADCIRC hydrodynamic model developed for NOAA by
the Notre Dame team. STOFS 2D+ Global features which were investigated and ported to
MPAS-Ocean included variable resolution to optimize efficiency, delineating the impact of
bathymetry across the globe, and tidal dissipation mechanisms including bottom boundary layer
dissipation and internal tide dissipation. In addition, we investigated parameterizing the energy
lost due to internal tide wave drag in the inclusion of baroclinic forcing in the barotropic model.

Accomplishments

Task 1: Development, implementation, verification and validation of sub-grid methods in
MPAS-Ocean.

We have successfully incorporated our sub-grid strategies, which were developed for both Finite
Volume and Finite Element hydrodynamic models (Kennedy et al., Ocean Modelling, 2019; JL

 Presently at the Commonwealth scientific and industrial research organisation, Canberra, Australia

Woodruff et al., Ocean Modelling, 2021), into a single-layer mode of MPAS-Ocean, appropriate
for the vertically well mixed flows across the coastal floodplain and within the floodplain’s
network of small scale dendritic conveyances.

In the model development, we generalized a single layer, barotropic shallow water equation in
vector-variant form to a ‘sub-grid-aware’ system of equations. A subset of closures proposed in
Kennedy et al 2019, is considered in the generalization; this includes the partial volume
corrections and a simple conical flow of constant friction slope for the sub-grid velocity to the
correction of the bottom friction term. In the subgrid-aware system, for a given grid-scale sea
surface height (SSH), the layer thickness and bottom friction coefficients are defined based on
the high-resolution topography/bathymetry and land cover/use. We investigated a suitable
implementation of these variables in the so-called TRiSK discretization scheme on a polygonal
C grid (Ringler et al. (2010)) used in the MPAS-Ocean model. The evaluation of these variables
was made computationally efficient (~log(M) where M in a number of input levels) through the
use of look up tables with SSH as inputs. We modified the wetting/drying treatment in
MPAS-Ocean to accommodate the presence of partially wet not only within mesh cells but also
along the cell edges. Note that the modified treatment, which ensures an ability to preserve the
lake-at-rest state (i.e. well-balanced) and that there are no unwanted mass fluxes from dry mesh
cells, was demonstrated to benefit the inundation scheme of the standard solution. Note the
computer implementation was done so that it permits the applications of the subgrid corrections
in select portions of the computational domain.

The MPAS-Ocean implementation with sub-grid corrections was extensively verified and
validated using test cases with time-dependent moving wetting/drying fronts. The test cases
range from idealized test cases with analytical solutions to real world tropical storm-induced
inundation. The verification against test problems with exact solutions demonstrates the
sub-grid solution converges approximately at the same rate with the standard solution. The level
of accuracy in the sub-grid solution is indeed consistently much higher on the same given mesh.
Owing in part to the use of lookup tables, the subgrid solution requires only slightly more
computing time than its standard counterpart; this results in the sub-grid solution being superior
in cost-per-accuracy performance. The sub-grid MPAS-Ocean was applied to simulate
inundation induced by Hurricane Sandy, a very challenging problem to accurately simulate the
storm with a coarse grid model. We used a global domain with a mesh resolution of 1.25 km in
the North Atlantic Bight coastal areas, in which the sub-grid corrections were applied, and
varying resolution of 45km to 2.5km elsewhere. The time series at selected water level stations
in the left panel demonstrates the excellent performance of the MPAS-Ocean sub-grid. The
sub-grid model more accurately captures the observed high water levels, especially in western
Long Island Sound, the Delaware River, and upper Chesapeake Bay. These areas correspond
to locations where gauges are located in complex channels and streams. We found that the
quality of the DEM is essential in the sub-grid simulations; in some cases, deficiencies in the
DEM used are likely to be the limiting factor in the accuracy of the computed water levels at
certain stations.

The computer implementation was fully integrated in the E3SM github repository2 and test cases
used can be found in the E3SM COMPASS (Configuration Of Model for Prediction Across
Scales Setups) infrastructure3.

The results were disseminated to communities of interest at national and international
conferences as well as DOE internal meetings (see the list of presentations in the Publications
section). We have completed a draft of the manuscript entitled “A sub-grid correction scheme for
accurate km-scale coastal inundation in the Energy Exascale Earth System Model” to be
submitted to Journal of Advances in Modeling Earth Systems, an American Geophysical Union
peer review journal, for publication (the draft is being uploaded with this report).

Task 2: Advances/collaboration on MPAS‐Ocean and ADCIRC global tidal processes

The Notre Dame group joined a bi‐weekly DOE E3SM ‐ Tides group meeting since our work
interfaces closely with the efforts to incorporate global tides and storm surges into the E3SM
model. The MPAS‐Ocean tides group was led by Andrew Roberts and Mark Petersen both at
LANL and includes Steven Brus (formerly a Ph.D. student and postdoc in our Notre Dame lab,
then a post‐doc at LANL and now a staff scientist at ANL), Brian Arbic from the University of
Michigan, Kristin Barton4 (a Ph.D student at the University of Michigan), and Nairita Pal5 (a
postdoc at LANL). ND group’s ADCIRC work on developing a global storm tide model was in
part done as part of this project for a purpose of cross comparison of sub-grid MPAS-Ocean with
a high resolution model. This work involved investigating optimal mesh efficient and accurate
global tidal modeling, establishing the role of topography/bathymetry on global tides, and
investigating internal wave drag and boundary layer dissipation. Below are summaries of our
results/findings:

 ●​ Through extensive experimentation on mesh generation parameters and targets, the
 Notre Dame group found that topographic length scales and mesh size gradation rate
 (i.e. the adjacent mesh size ratio) are critical in improving computational efficiency and
 accuracy of the tidal simulations. Specifically, resolving continental shelf breaks and
 mid‐ocean mountain ridges was critical in achieving accurate solutions and resulting in
 ADCIRC based meshes that vary between 25 km and 2 km for a global shell and 25 km
 and 80 m for integrated global/coastal floodplain meshes. These optimized targeted
 resolution meshes are two to four orders of magnitude more efficient than applying
 constant mesh resolution of either 2 km or 80 m across the entire globe. These
 techniques for mesh generation were ported to MPAS-Ocean.
 ●​ Internal tidal energy generation/dissipation as well as boundary layer dissipation in
 critical inlet systems were impacted by both resolution and how well bathymetry was
 resolved. We quantified the impact of bathymetric data sets on global tidal solutions. The
 bathymetric depth and ice thickness of the Ronne ice shelf in Antarctica dramatically

 impacted the tidal solutions in the Atlantic, likely due to semi‐diurnal resonance along
 the north south axis of that water body. The bathymetry of the Foxe Basin north of
 Hudson bay also strongly impacted tides in the Atlantic. Our results provide excellent
 guidance on what bathymetric data sets to use in the global tidal models. This guidance
 was used to advance MPAS-Ocean.
●​ We examined two different internal tide relationships as well as two different
 implementations of the quadratic drag law, Chezy and Manning n. We found that a
 Manning n based drag law and a directional internal tide law were more consistent
 across the globe and led to optimized dissipation coefficients that matched the physics
 very well. Specifically while most shelves and inland waterbodies led to good results with
 a Manning n of 0.022, high energy regions like the Hudson Bay, the Bay of Fundy,
 Georges Bank, the Patagonian shelf, the inner European shelf, and the Australian
 shelves had consistently much higher optimal Manning n's on the order of 0.028. Lastly,
 island chains such as the Aleutians and Ryukyu Islands have very large associated
 dissipation parameters reflecting the unresolved high energy eddying that occurs as very
 fast tidal flows pass through these island chains. We found that the boundary layer
 dissipation was particularly highly focused geographically with only 1.4% of the ocean
 accounting for 90% of the boundary layer dissipation while the internal tide dissipation
 was more diffuse with 16.7% of the ocean accounting for 90% of the internal tide
 dissipation.
●​ Overall the optimized mesh, bathymetry, and friction coefficients led to a global deep
 water root‐mean‐square difference compared to the data‐assimilated TPXO8 model for
 the M2 tide of 1.94 cm, the most accurate global tidal model to date. Also of significance
 was that boundary layer dissipation in our optimized model accounted for 1.83 TW and
 internal tide dissipation accounted for 1.49 TW for a total of 3.31 TW, slightly less than
 the widely accepted 3.5 TW. Our dissipation estimates exclude lateral viscous
 dissipation, which likely plays a role in the high energy dissipation regions. Previous
 studies attributed between 0.9 and 1.18 TW to internal tide dissipation and as such, our
 estimates continue the trend of an increasingly larger proportion of the total energy
 dissipation being attributed to internal tide dissipation. Our larger proportion of the total
 being attributed to internal tide dissipation is largely related to the high mesh resolution
 over steep topographic gradients.
●​ We investigated the inclusion of baroclinic forcing (in the form of depth-integrated
 baroclinic pressure gradient) and internal wave drag in the barotropic model. We
 demonstrated that such the inclusion requires modification of the internal wave drag term
 to prevent excess degradation of tidal results compared to the barotropic model. We
 proposed the modification that scaling the internal tide dissipation by an easy to
 calculate dissipation ratio; the resulting model from this model improve substantially, with
 complex root mean square errors (RMSE) of 2.27 cm in the deep ocean and 12.16 cm in
 shallow waters for the M2 tidal constituent. Although this investigation was done for a
 one-way heterogeneously coupled model of a coarse ocean model and high resolution
 barotropic model, the finding and method proposed is applicable to an external mode of
 ocean models using the mode-splitting technique. These methods are now being

 applied to MPAS-Ocean by Steven Brus and Coleman Blakely at ANL under a DOE
 Early Career Award.

(Above works were published in Blakely et al., JGR Ocean, 2022 and Blakely et al., JAMES,
2024)

Our ADCIRC developments were ported over to the MPAS‐Ocean tidal models developed
between LANL and ANL. Our ADCIRC work on developing optimal meshes for global tidal
models were integrated into DOE's MPAS‐Ocean tidal models. A coarser ADCIRC style mesh
focused on steep topography and with 45 km to 5 km resolution has led to the best overall
MPAS‐Ocean tidal results to date (see Barton et al., JAMES, 2022). While not as good as the
ADCIRC solutions (5.4 cm complex M2 tide difference in deep water compared to TPXO8),
implementation taking into account the Antarctic ice shelf water column heights, as seen in
ADCIRC, continues to refine the MPAS-Ocean solution (see Pal et al., GMD, 2023). Although
not attempted, it can be expected a more sophisticated internal tide model dissipation model,
possibly a further refined mesh, and regional optimization of the friction parameters would
continue to refine the MPAS‐Ocean solution to be as accurate as the ADCIRC solution. Work
from this collaboration appeared in Barton et al., JAMES, 2022, Brus et al., Ocean Modelling,
2022, and Pal et al., GMD, 2023. (Note that the collaboration led to a fruitful spin off work in
incorporating inline self attraction and loading into ADCIRC and STOFS 2D+ Global, which is
expected to be tremendously useful for a community of ADCIRC developers/users as well as
NOAA and the USACE.)
