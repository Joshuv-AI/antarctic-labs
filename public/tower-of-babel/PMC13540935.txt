Motivations and experiences in controlled human infection trials vs. phase I clinical trials: a survey study
Authors: Victor M Cnossen, Olivia A C Lamers, Rogier P van Leeuwen, Paul Zimmer-Harwood, Meta Roestenberg, Ingrid M C Kamerling, Marie-Astrid Hoogerwerf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundControlled human infection (CHI) trials deliberately expose healthy participants to a pathogen. Although they share design features with phase I clinical trials, CHI trials may differ in participant burden and administrative burden. Literature on CHI participant motivations and trial experience compared to phase I participants is limited.MethodsWe conducted a survey comparing motivation, decision-making, and ethical views among CHI and phase I participants, using a previously validated questionnaire. Descriptive and comparative analyses assessed motivational drivers and ethical attitudes.ResultsIn total, 70 CHI and 98 phase I participants were included. Distribution of motivation was different for CHI compared to phase I participants, with reimbursement being a less prominent motivator, and more altruistic factors reported. CHI participants experienced more severe symptoms and reported symptoms as an important factor in the decision to participate. Views on key ethical principles such as reimbursement and burden of participation were similar between participant groups.ConclusionOverall, findings suggest CHI participants experienced more symptoms than phase I participants, but that CHI participation was a well-informed decision, with symptom burden taken into account. This informs future CHI trial design and warrants further research on symptoms and participant experience in CHI trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12910-026-01534-z.Keywords: Participant experience, Participant motivation, Controlled human infection model, Clinical trials, Phase I trials, Questionnaire, Survey, Participant-reported outcomes

Introduction

Clinical trials are essential for assessing the efficacy and safety of novel pharmaceutical compounds. Phase I trials are the first step in clinical development of new products, with the aim to assess safety, tolerability and pharmacology of new compounds in small populations of (usually) healthy participants [1]. In controlled human infection (CHI) trials, a pathogen is administered to healthy participants in a controlled setting. Historically, CHI studies have contributed to studying disease pathophysiology and immunology; more recently, they are also being implemented to evaluate vaccine or drug efficacy in a more time-and cost-efficient manner [2]. CHI studies can ‘de-risk’ the clinical development pipeline by allowing ineffective candidates to ‘fail fast’, and facilitating the selection of candidates to proceed to larger phase II and III trials. Moreover, in case of a new pandemic, CHI trials are a potential tool for the emergency testing of drugs and vaccines. This could shorten timelines towards vaccine approval [3–5].

Although CHI and phase I trials share many similarities, including a usually healthy participant population being closely monitored for safety, some differences exist. In phase I trials, primary objectives generally include assessment of safety, pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of the investigational product; the risk of adverse events is minimized by careful selection of a low starting dose, and dose escalation is often guided by extensive review of safety data. Primary endpoints of CHI trials vary between different pathogens: they may include signs of infection or colonisation, or occurrence of clinical symptoms. The World Health Organization (WHO) states that, with appropriate safeguards in place, conventional phase I clinical trials and CHI-studies are broadly comparable in terms of participant health risks [2]. Nevertheless, the discussion on whether CHI trials are similar or different from conventional trials with healthy volunteers has not been concluded, with experts arguing for and against [6, 7]. Moreover, although phase I trials are considered a standard form of research, these studies are also not without debate [8].

Ethical aspects of CHI trials are subject to ongoing discussions within the scientific community, with regulatory authorities and with the general public [7–13]. Importantly, in any of these discussions, the involvement of both CHI and phase I participants viewpoints remains limited. However, motivational factors, decision-making and ethical views of CHI participants have been characterized before in using both quantitative and qualitative methods, and even in publications written by former CHI participants [14–20]. Since phase I trials are considered standard research practice, it is of interest to assess how CHI participant views and motivations compare to those of phase I participants. By investigation of these differences and similarities, understanding of phase I and CHI trials is deepened, informing the debate on acceptability of research on healthy individuals in general, and CHI trials in particular.

In this study, we conducted a survey among CHI and phase I clinical trial participants, comparing motivational drivers, the decision-making process and ethical perceptions regarding trial participation between groups.

Methods

Design

In this two-centre, cross-sectional questionnaire study, participants of CHI studies conducted at the Leiden University Medical Centre (LUMC) in Leiden, The Netherlands, and participants of phase I trials at the Centre for Human Drug Research (CHDR) (a non-for-profit contract research organisation in Leiden, The Netherlands) were included. CHI trial participants were asked to complete the questionnaire between September 2020 and December 2022. After collection of the CHI participant data, it was decided to approach phase I trial participants for comparison with CHI data; this was done between April 2024 and November 2024.

CHI trial participants either participated in controlled human malaria or schistosomiasis infection trials with healthy volunteers; three of four CHI trials were conducted in an outpatient setting, with between 17 and 37 visits in total over the course of 26 to 54 weeks. One malaria CHI trial consisted of an in-house period of 3 days, followed by 21 visits over the course of 5 weeks. Phase I participants participated in various trials at CHDR, including first-in-human, single or multiple ascending dose studies of investigational products targeting neurological, immunological or psychiatric disorders, all with healthy volunteers. The trials consisted of a screening visit, an in-house period between 3 and 15 days, followed by 1 to 5 follow-up visits; total study duration was between 7 and 10 weeks. For an overview of the trial activities, refer to the Supplementary Appendix.

Questionnaire

The survey questionnaire used in this survey was previously developed based on existing literature and topics of ethical debate [14]; no formal validation methods were employed. The questionnaire consists of 37 items, distributed over several domains: the Risk Propensity Scale (RPS, which consists of seven statements on daily life risks, rated between 1 and 9) [21]; motivational factors for participation, factors playing a role in decision-making to participate, influence of others, experience of participation, symptoms, trust in study staff, informed consent, right to withdraw and financial compensation. The questionnaire was slightly modified for phase I participants to match the context of the clinical trials (e.g., ‘Contributing to developing countries’ to ‘Contributing to new treatments’); these modifications were not expected to affect the comparison between the two study groups. The sections with (semi-) quantitative data (RPS, motivational and decision factors) were adapted from previous studies [21, 22]. For the full questionnaire, refer to Supplementary Appendix.

For CHI participants, questionnaires were sent via e-mail, with a maximum of two reminder e-mails; data was processed anonymously using Castor Electronic Data Capture (EDC) [23]. Phase I participants were invited to complete the questionnaire either via post mail, or during a follow-up visit at the clinical trial site; data was processed anonymously using PROMASYS.

Primary endpoints were the ranking of motivational factors and factors contributing to the decision to participate. Secondary objectives were to explore views on ethical principles such as informed consent, right to withdraw and financial compensation, and to compare experience of participation and motivational factors between the two participant groups.

Statistics

Frequencies and percentages were calculated for all variables and ordinal factors and were ranked from highest to lowest. Categorical variables were compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests; ordinal and continuous variables were analysed using Mann-Whitney U tests. Distributions of ranked factors were compared using Chi-square tests. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed in RStudio v. 4.5.1.

Ethics

This survey was conducted in accordance with local law, ICH GCP guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation in this survey was entirely voluntary and without additional compensation for completing the questionnaire. Participants were informed of the survey purpose and data handling; informed consent was implied by completion of the questionnaire. Informed consent for participation in the respective CHI or phase I trials was provided separately by all participants. The study protocol for this survey study (independent from the clinical trial protocols) was reviewed by the Medical Ethical Committee Leiden, Den Haag, Delft (METC-LDD), which determined that the research was not subject to the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO).

Results

Of 98 CHI participants approached, 70 (71.4%) completed the questionnaire; of these, 61.4% participated in the malaria CHI trial and 38.6% in the schistosomiasis CHI trial. Of 184 phase I participants approached, 98 (53.3%) completed the questionnaire; of these, 50.0% participated in a trial with a neurological compound, 21.4% with a psychiatric compound, and 1.0% in a trial with an immunological compound. For 27.6% of participants, the compound category was not recorded because the anonymized questionnaire did not indicate which specific trial participants had joined; however, all phase I participants were enrolled in trials with neurological, psychiatric or immunological compounds. Table 1 provides an overview of the demographics of both participant groups. Compared to phase I participants, CHI participants were more often female (CHI 48.6%; phase I 32.7%; p = 0.06) and of younger age (p = 0.005). Moreover, phase I participants were unemployed more frequently (CHI 1.4%; phase I 49.0%) rather than being working (CHI 37.1%; phase I 18.4%) or a student (CHI 61.4%; phase I 32.7%) (overall p < 0.001). Finally, more phase I participants had previously participated in a clinical trial (CHI 35.7%; phase I 77.6%; p < 0.001).

Motivation

In both groups, reimbursement was rated most often as an important motivator for participation (‘very important’ for 50.0% of CHI participants and 60.2% of phase I participants). In both groups, other important motivational factors were being able to contribute to science, contribution to new treatments, and personal interest and curiosity [Figure 1A]. Distribution of motivational factors was similar between the groups p = 0.59). However, selection of the single most important motivational factor was different between CHI and phase I participants. For 34.5% of CHI participants, reimbursement was most important; 20.2% found it most important to contribute to science, and 17.6% to contribute to new treatments for developing countries. For phase I participants, 58.8% of participants ranked reimbursement as most important, followed by contribution to science (19.1%) and to contribute to new treatments for patients in The Netherlands (9.2%) (overall p < 0.001) [Figure 1B].

Decision-making

When asked to rate factors taken into consideration in the decision to participate, CHI participants most frequently reported trust in the study team (34.3% ‘very important’; 42.9% ‘important’), followed by reimbursement (22.9% ‘very important’; 47.1% ‘important’). For phase I participants, reimbursement was most frequently reported (42.9% ‘very important’; 39.8% ‘important’ (p = 0.02 for comparison with CHI, followed by trust in the study team (43.9% ‘very important’; 29.6% ‘important’ (p = 0.22 for comparison with CHI)) [Figure 1C].

Severity and expectation of symptoms

Median perceived overall symptom severity score (numeric response scale between 0 and 10) was higher for CHI participants (2 [interquartile range (IQR) 1–4]) than for phase I participants (1 [IQR 0–2]) (p = 0.000191) [Figure 2A]. Among CHI participants, 52.9% reported that their experienced symptoms were as expected, compared to 72.4% of phase I participants [Figure 2B]; 22.8% of CHI participants experienced less symptoms than expected, and 14.3% reported more symptoms than expected. The remaining CHI (10%) and phase I (27.6%) participants did not specify how symptoms exactly differed from their expectations. For both CHI and phase I participants, 100% of participants considered the burden and experienced symptoms associated with their participation justifiable by the prospective scientific and personal benefit.

Experience of dosing/inoculation

The anticipated risk involved in inoculation or dosing was considered small by most participants (CHI 75.7%; phase I 56.6%). Reflecting on the experience of dosing or inoculation, both participant groups reported an overall neutral (CHI 50.0%; phase I 46.9%) or positive feeling (CHI 20.0%; phase I 46.9%); 25.7% of CHI participants considered the inoculation ‘exciting’, compared to only 5.1% of phase I participants. Only 2 CHI participants (2.9%) reported experiencing fear, whereas no phase I participants did [Figure 2C].

Risk Propensity Scale (RPS)

Median RPS scores [IQR] were 4.57 [4.14–5] for CHI participants vs. 4.14 [3.57–4.57] for phase I participants (p = 0.000041), indicating a significantly higher propensity to risks for CHI participants.

Influence of others

CHI and phase I participants reported similar rates of discussing their participation with friends (CHI 25.7%; phase I 27.3%), parents (CHI 24.3%; phase I 24.1%) or partners (CHI 18.0%; phase I 19.2%) (no statistically significant differences). Most participants in both groups received positive reactions from others (CHI 76.5%; phase I 89.3%); CHI participants also received negative reactions significantly more often than phase I participants (70.6% vs. 51.1% (p = 0.02). However, most participants reported that reactions from others did not affect their decision to participate (CHI 90.0% and phase I 95.5%).

Information and screening

CHI participants regarded the informed consent & screening visit as more important than phase I participants: only 14.3% of CHI participants considered it as unimportant, compared to 37.8% of phase I participants (p < 0.001) [Figure 3A]. CHI participants attributed most value to discussing risks and expected symptoms during the visit, while phase I participants found the appointment schedule and lifestyle instructions most important [Figure 3B]. The right to withdraw during the trial was equally reported as important between the participant groups (ranked ‘very important’ by CHI 45.7%; phase I 52.0%).

Reimbursement

Views on reimbursement were generally similar for all participants. Reimbursement was considered fair and appropriate by most participants (CH 81.4%; phase I 89.6%). The role of reimbursement was mostly perceived as a motivation to participate (CHI 40.2%; phase I 38.6%), followed by payment for risks and burden (CHI, 24.6%; phase I 22.8%) and compensation for time and effort (CHI 8.2%; phase I 17.5%). Participants mainly reported using the received reimbursement for living expenses (CHI 27.8%; phase I 32.3%), vacations (CHI 23.3%; phase I 23.1%) or paying off debt (CHI 10.0%; phase I 9.2%). A vast majority of both CHI and phase I participants reported not being willing to participate without financial reimbursement (CHI 91.4%; phase I 94.9%).

Discussion

In this survey study, it was found that the most important motivational factors and the factors influencing decision-making are different between phase I and CHI participants; for phase I participants, reimbursement was a more prominent factor, than for CHI participants. Moreover, CHI participants experienced more symptoms, found the inoculation procedure more exciting, and considered the screening process more important than phase I participants. However, views on other important ethical aspects, including reimbursement, risks versus benefits, reactions of others and the right to withdraw, were similar between participant groups.

The overall distribution of motivational factors was similar between CHI and phase I participants. However, the single most important factor was distributed differently between groups: reimbursement was a more prominent factor for phase I participants, while for CHI participants the ability to contribute to science or help developing countries played a bigger role. This suggests that CHI trials attract participants with a more altruistic incentive compared to phase I clinical trials.

CHI participants experienced more (and more severe) overall symptoms compared to phase I participants. The occurrence of symptoms is an aspect of CHI trials recognized as different from phase I studies. This survey indicates that CHI participants consider these symptoms in their decision to participate, and view symptoms as key information to discuss during the screening visit [Figure 3B]. Their decision to participate therefore reflects a conscious risk-benefit assessment: while being aware that more symptoms may be expected compared to phase I trials, this risk is accepted. This is in line with results from earlier trials assessing decision-making in CHI participants [15–20]. Moreover, the higher RPS scores of CHI participants further support this risk-tolerating profile, which corresponds to earlier findings by Hoogerwerf et al. when comparing CHI participants to a control population of university students [14]. It may be argued that the higher occurrence of symptoms in CHI trials, when compared to phase I trials, requires specific attention during the informed consent process; this is acknowledged by the WHO [2].

Inoculation with a pathogen in a CHI trial was considered more exciting by the participants, than dosing of a conventional medicinal product by participants in phase I studies. This difference could be attributed to the difference in trial methods; administration of the phase I therapeutics was primarily done via tablets taken orally, while CHI inoculation was performed using mosquito bites (malaria) or dermal application of larvae (schistosomiasis).

This survey assessed the perception of reimbursement and the role of financial compensation in decision-making. Over 90% of both CHI and phase I participants reported that they would not participate in the trial without reimbursement. In addition, most participants indicated that they spent their compensation on vacations, living expenses or paying off debts, suggesting financial need as a possible driver of participation. This can reflect financial need, but may also indicate that payment offsets time away from work and other opportunity costs. In addition, this may suggest that despite precautions to avoid undue influence of financial compensation, it remains one of the main motivational factors for participation in clinical trials. However, this does not necessarily entail undue influence or coercion; the reimbursement supported voluntary participation, without autonomy or informed consent being compromised [24]. Moreover, this survey suggests that participants were well-informed and did not volunteer against their better judgment. Prior research by Grady et al. supports this: while reimbursement may initially attract individuals to participate in a clinical trial, many other motivational factors are considered in the decision to participate, and participants regard risks and burden as more important in their decision-making than financial compensation [22, 25]. Therefore, reimbursement may be regarded as a facilitating factor of other, more altruistic motivations, rather than considering financial compensation and altruism as dichotomous.

Moreover, undue influence is avoided as long as reimbursement fees remain proportional to the trial burden and costs involved, which is reflected in established frameworks for compensation of research participants [26–28]. Research oversight experts, including institutional review boards (IRBs) and research ethics committees (RECs), increasingly recognize that monetary undue influence is rare among consent-capable adult participants, that underpayment is more common and ethically troubling than overpayment, and that reducing compensation can undermine justice and fairness without meaningfully protecting participants [29, 30]. Although financial compensation may be argued to pose a risk of undue influence in contexts of extreme poverty, where non-participation and loss of reimbursement could have detrimental consequences, [31, 32] malaria CHI studies in low- and middle-income settings suggest that participants’ motivations and perceptions of payment are strongly shaped by local context and do not, by themselves, indicate undue influence [33, 34].

This survey study has several limitations. First, there was a difference in response rates between study populations (CHI: 71.4%, phase I: 50.5%), which may be the result of different methods to approach participants – CHI participants were approached via email, while phase I participants were approached via post mail or during a follow-up visit at the clinical site. This may result in a selection bias; participants having negatively experienced their trial participation could be less likely to respond, resulting in an overly positive evaluation of the motivation and decision-making of CHI participants compared to phase I participants. However, the reverse may be possible as well: the questionnaire may have been regarded as a platform to express any negative experiences for participants. Furthermore, social desirability of the answers may have affected the self-reported study results. Although results were processed anonymously, a proportion of questionnaires were administered during a follow-up visit at the phase I clinical trial site, in presence of clinical staff.

Another potential limitation concerns the comparability between the participant groups. First, sex and age distribution were significantly different, as well as the occupation and proportion of participants with a medical background. Moreover, phase I participants were more often unemployed, while more CHI participants were students. This may reflect the fact that phase I trials included in-house stays of between 3 and 17 days (see Supplementary Appendix), and required full-time commitment, while CHI trials were performed mostly in an outpatient setting and therefore offered more flexibility. This difference in burden is also reflected in the higher average reimbursement received by phase I participants than by CHI participants (€3100 vs. €2056) [27, 28]. These differences may be a potential confounder of the survey results; as phase I studies require more time and have a higher reimbursement, they may inherently select for participants that are in financial need and are willing to be flexible in their schedules. Subsequently, the outpatient setting of most CHI trials results in lower compensation fees and may therefore generally attract a less money-oriented, more altruistic participant population. Furthermore, the date at which the respective surveys were conducted could affect the results of this study. The CHI surveys were carried out up to four years before the phase I surveys, and shortly after the COVID-19 pandemic of 2020. Evidence suggests that the pandemic increased public awareness of the value of medical research, and interest to participate in clinical trials [31–35]. This effect may have been stronger during the CHI surveys than at the time the phase I surveys were conducted, potentially confounding the differences in altruistic motivations between the two participant groups, independent of the trial characteristics. Further research is warranted with regards to symptoms of CHI participants. This survey study demonstrates that the symptom burden after administration of a pathogen is a key factor in decision-making of CHI participants, and is reflected upon in a different manner compared to phase I participants. A better understanding of symptoms, and the perception and acceptability of symptoms to participants is crucial to further knowledge on CHI participant experience, and to inform the design of future CHI trials.

Conclusion

We present a survey study assessing motivation and decision-making in participants of phase I and CHI trials, as well as propensity to risk-taking behaviour, experiences of trial participation and attitudes towards financial compensation. When compared to conventional phase I participants, CHI participants found reimbursement a less important motivator, and CHI participants experienced more trial-related symptoms, although they reported being well-informed about the symptoms and having received appropriate compensation. Phase I and CHI participants were similar in their views on essential ethical aspects of participation in clinical trials. This provides valuable insights into the CHI trial population and supports decisions in future study design. More research on symptom burden and perception is warranted to deepen understanding of CHI participant experience.
