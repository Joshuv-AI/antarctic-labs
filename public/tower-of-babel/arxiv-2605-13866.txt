AI Alignment Amplifies the Role of Race, Gender, and Disability in Hiring Decisions

Alignment changes how models use qualification signals: alignment increases returns to skills and work experience overall, but relatively more so for female and Black candidates. Meanwhile, the absence of qualification signals harms marginalised groups more, particularly for disabled candidates, differences that may explain the asymmetry of alignment effects across groups we observe.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence systems increasingly participate in decisions that were once exclusively human, from advising on medical diagnoses
27
to shaping judicial outcomes
28
to screening job candidates
32
. As these systems shape more consequential outcomes, people have begun to examine whether they inherit the cognitive and social biases documented in decades of psychology and economics research

12
,
26

Asymmetric effects across marginalised groups

We measure the hiring advantage or penalty associated with each demographic group by regressing pairwise decisions on candidate attribute differences (Methods III; Supplementary Information, ‘Derivations’). Every instruction-tuned model favours female candidates over otherwise identical male candidates, and all but two favour Black candidates (Fig. 1). The effects of disability show no comparable pattern: the coefficients are heterogeneous in sign, and the average effect is significantly negative. Pre-trained models show the same directional asymmetry but with smaller magnitudes. 4
Mechanisms of demographic effects

The human studies of discrimination distinguish two broad mechanisms: the use of demographic identity in inference about productivity when individual signals are noisy or absent (statistical discrimination
37
,
6
,
2
,
3
,
9
), or a direct preference for certain groups regardless of qualifications (taste-based discrimination
7
). Taste-based discrimination is notoriously difficult to identify directly in any setting, since it requires distinguishing pure preference from inference; it is often treated as the residual of demographic effects after accounting for statistical discrimination, whether Bayesian or non-Bayesian
10
,
9
. Statistical discrimination, by contrast, admits more direct empirical tests. We therefore focus on statistical discrimination, testing two complementary specifications: a differential-returns specification, in which we test whether the returns to qualifications differ depending on the candidate’s group, and an information-asymmetry specification, in which we test whether the absence of skills signals disproportionately harms marginalised groups.

We find that models reward the same qualifications more generously when held by marginalised-group candidates: all three groups receive significantly higher returns to general skills, while female and Black candidates also receive higher returns to work experience—a channel where disabled candidates instead face a penalty (Extended Data Table 6). The matched-pair design confirms that alignment causally amplifies these differential returns—more than doubling the return to general skills for each group and introducing a work-experience premium for female and Black candidates (Extended Data Table 7).

Our experimental design also offers a natural test of information asymmetry (Methods I). When work experience is absent, general skills—which are part of the occupation’s role requirements (Supplementary Information, ‘Resume’)—become unobservable, and models are forced to infer them from the remaining signals. In the pooled analysis, pre-trained models penalise all candidates equally when skills signals are missing, whereas instruction-tuned models impose additional penalties on female and disabled candidates (Extended Data Table 8). The matched-pair design confirms that alignment causally amplifies demographic penalties under information asymmetry for all three groups: female (
-0.053
,
P=0.002
), Black (
-0.056
,
P=0.004
), and disabled (
-0.064
,
P<0.001
) candidates (Extended Data Table 9).

Alignment thus amplifies the returns to general skills for all three historically-marginalised groups relative to their counterparts, and the returns to work experience for female and Black candidates but not disabled candidates, while simultaneously introducing higher information-asymmetry penalties for historically-marginalised groups that are largest for disability. Evaluated at the sample mean of qualifications, the expected alignment-induced differential-return advantage is
+0.099
for female,
+0.109
for Black, but only
+0.080
for disabled candidates; the expected information-asymmetry penalty is
-0.011
,
-0.011
, and
-0.013
, respectively (Supplementary Information, ‘Calculation’). In both channels, disability receives the least favourable alignment effect, which may help explain the overall asymmetry across the three groups.

5
Discussion

The asymmetry between female, Black, and disabled candidates—documented across 27 models and 177 occupations covering nearly half of U.S. employment—is already present in pre-trained models but is dramatically amplified by post-training alignment, which increases advantages for female and Black candidates by 325% and 330% and worsens the disability penalty by 171%. Compared with human employers
8
,
29
,
38
,
4
,
31
, language models reverse the direction of racial discrimination, attenuate the disability penalty by 90%, but amplify the female advantage by 190%.

While language models produce different outcomes from human employers, the underlying behavioural patterns are consistent with the human literature. Models reward the same credentials more generously for marginalised-group candidates, but penalise them more when qualification signals are absent—consistent with statistical discrimination
37
,
6
,
2
and also evidenced in human experiments
10
.

These relative effects do not affect the three groups equally. Disabled candidates do not receive the same alignment-induced work-experience advantage compared to female or Black candidates, and they face the largest information-asymmetry penalty. Together, these may help explain why the aggregate alignment effect on female and Black candidates is positive and large in magnitude, but negative for disabled candidates.

The broader implication is that AI does not reproduce the outcomes of human discrimination but produces a fundamentally different pattern of results. Existing regulations, e.g. the EU AI Act
20
and New York City’s Local Law 144
34
, often implicitly assume AI will replicate historical biases; our findings suggest the question is no longer whether AI discriminates against marginalised groups, but how alignment reshapes the relative treatment across them.

Methods

I. Audit design

Occupation sample.
We draw occupation-level data from four public U.S. sources: the ONET database
33
(required education, work experience, general skills, and technology-related skills), the 2024 Current Population Survey
41
(labour-force participation rates by gender and race), the May 2024 Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics
42
(hourly wages and total employment), and the Labor Force Characteristics report
43
(disability prevalence by occupation). Disability statistics are reported at the mid-level occupational category and mapped to detailed occupations via CPS classifications. We retain the intersection of occupations present in all four sources with non-suppressed values, yielding a final sample of 177 occupations with complete information on skills, education, experience, wages, and demographic composition.

Candidate profiles.
For each occupation, ONET provides the five most important skills (ranked by survey-based importance ratings), distributions of required education and work experience, and in-demand technology skills. We generate candidate profiles by sampling independently from these occupation-specific distributions. Each skill level is drawn from a normal distribution centred on the occupation’s entry-level requirement and bounded at 0–7, with a common coefficient of variation (
=0.24
) calibrated to the dispersion observed in U.S. standardised test scores
18
,
1
to prevent unrealistic concentration or fat tails in the skill distribution. Education and work experience are sampled from the ONET distributions, with the “Less than a High School Diploma” category excluded and its probability redistributed, as this level may signal that a candidate is underage and trigger refusal to evaluate rather than a hiring preference. GPA is drawn from a normal distribution with the same coefficient of variation, mean
=3.0
for high school graduates
35
and
3.15
for college graduates
39
, bounded at 0–4. For occupations with in-demand technical certifications, technology skills are assigned with 50% probability; for occupations without such requirements, technology skills are set to zero for all candidates.

Resume construction.
Each numeric candidate profile is converted into a natural-language resume in two stages. In the first stage, GPT-4o generates two occupation-level mappings, each verified manually: (a) a career ladder of four job titles spanning entry-level to senior positions along the same occupational track, and (b) a mapping from each education level to the most relevant degree field. In the second stage, the candidate’s attributes are assembled into a formatted resume. Years of experience determine the number of positions (1 if
<2.5
years, 2 if
<6
, 3 if
<10.5
, 4 otherwise); job titles are drawn from the career ladder in descending seniority; and employment periods are computed backward from a fixed reference date. For candidates with work experience, GPT-4o generates 3–4 bullet-point descriptions per position, conditioned on the candidate’s skill names, levels, and occupation. All prompts enforce a neutral style with no names, pronouns, or demographic references (Supplementary Information). For candidates without work experience, the work-experience section is omitted entirely—this is the source of the information-asymmetry variation exploited in our analysis.

Demographic counterfactuals.
Each baseline resume is replicated into eight variants spanning all
2\times 2\times 2
combinations of gender (male, female), race (White, Black or African American), and disability status (reports a disability, reports no disability). The only difference between variants is a standardised demographic information block inserted at the top of the resume, which states the candidate’s gender, race/ethnicity, and disability status alongside a Right to Work declaration. All productivity-relevant attributes—skills, education, work experience, GPA, and resume content—are held constant across the eight variants (Supplementary Information, ‘Resume’).

Pairwise matching.
For each occupation, we generate 100 candidate profiles and their
100\times 8=800
demographic variants. We then randomly sample 400 pairs per occupation (with seed
=42
), where each pair draws two distinct candidate profiles—ensuring the candidates differ in qualifications, not only in demographics—and one demographic variant per candidate uniformly at random. Presentation order is randomised independently. This yields
177\times 400=70{,}800
pairwise comparisons per model. Because pairs are sampled with replacement from the set of possible combinations, 21 pairs (0.03%) are drawn twice; dropping these duplicates has no effect on results (Methods III).

Hiring task.
Each model receives a three-part prompt for every pairwise comparison: (1) occupation-specific role requirements, extracted from ONET and rewritten by GPT-4o to produce professional language; (2) two candidate resumes; and (3) selection instructions asking the model to output “1” or “2” to indicate its choice. All prompts and a role requirement example are provided in the Supplementary Information.

II. Models

We evaluate 27 language models. The 19 instruction-tuned models are: Meta Llama
24
(Llama 3.1-8B, 3.2-3B, 3.3-70B), Alibaba Qwen
45
,
46
(Qwen2.5-3B, 2.5-7B, 2.5-72B, Qwen3-14B, 3-30B-A3B Instruct, 3-30B-A3B Thinking, 3-235B-A22B Instruct, 3-235B-A22B Thinking), OpenAI GPT
40
(GPT-5, GPT-5-mini, GPT-5-nano, GPT-OSS-120B), Google Gemini
23
(Gemini 2.5 Pro, 2.5 Flash, 2.5 Flash-Lite), and AI2 OLMo (OLMo-3-7B). The 8 pre-trained models without post-training alignment are: Meta Llama (Llama 3-70B, 3.1-8B, 3.2-3B), Alibaba Qwen (Qwen2.5-7B, 2.5-72B, Qwen3-14B, 3-30B-A3B), and AI2 OLMo (OLMo-3-7B-base). All nine Llama, Qwen, and OLMo instruction-tuned models that have publicly available base checkpoints are paired with their corresponding base versions; the only exception is Qwen2.5-3B, whose base model was excluded because it could not reliably parse the hiring task. The resulting nine matched pairs enable within-architecture comparisons that isolate the causal effect of alignment. All models are accessed via their respective APIs using default inference settings: temperature, top-
p
, maximum token length, web search, and reasoning mode are not explicitly specified and left at each provider’s defaults.

III. Statistical analysis

Main specification.
Each pairwise hiring decision produces a binary outcome (select candidate
i
or
j
). We regress the log odds of selecting candidate
i
over candidate
j
on the differences in their characteristics:

\log\frac{P(\text{select }i)}{P(\text{select }j)}=\sum_{d}\beta_{d}\,\Delta d_{ij}+X_{ij}^{\prime}\delta+\lambda\,\Delta p_{ij},

(1)

where
\beta_{d}
captures the hiring advantage (positive) or penalty (negative) associated with belonging to group
d
, holding qualifications
X
and presentation order constant (Supplementary Information, ‘Derivations’). GPA and the average of the five general skills (skill avg.) are
z
-scored (demeaned and divided by the sample standard deviation) before differencing; education (in years), work experience (in years), and technology skills (binary; zero for occupations without technical requirements) enter as raw differences. Demographic indicators are binary. Because the dependent variable is the difference in log odds between candidates
i
and
j
, the intercept cancels and is omitted. All regressions cluster standard errors at the occupation level (177 clusters), with degrees of freedom set to
G_{\text{occ}}-1=176
. We do not cluster at the model level because the number of models (8–19) is too small for reliable cluster-robust inference
15
; between-model variation is instead addressed through the random-effects meta-analysis reported in Fig. 3. As a robustness check, we also estimate two-way cluster-robust standard errors (occupation
\times
model) following Cameron, Gelbach & Miller
14
; our main results are robust to this specification. Coefficients are in log-odds units; the corresponding percentage change in hiring odds is
(e^{\beta}-1)\times 100\%
.

Occupation-level heterogeneity.
We extend the aggregate specification (equation
1
) to test whether demographic effects vary systematically across the labour market. We interact each demographic indicator with three occupation-level characteristics: log hourly wage (log-transformed then
z
-scored; from OES), total employment (log-transformed then
z
-scored; from OES), and the occupation-specific group participation rate converted to a within-sample percentile rank (female rate from CPS, Black rate from CPS, disability rate from BLS). This specification pools all observations across instruction-tuned or pre-trained models separately, with standard errors clustered at the occupation level. A positive interaction indicates that the demographic advantage (or penalty) is amplified in occupations where the characteristic is higher.

Mechanisms investigation.
We extend the aggregate specification (equation
1
) to investigate two potential mechanisms of demographic effects. In the differential-returns specification, we add interactions between each qualification and each demographic group to test whether the returns to qualifications differ by group. In the information-asymmetry specification, we add an indicator
v=\mathbf{1}[\text{work exp.}=0]
, its interactions with demographics to test whether models penalise candidates differently by group when qualification signals are absent, its interactions with qualifications to control for structural reweighting of remaining signals, and a conditional skill average
(1-v)\times\overline{\text{skill}}
that reflects the fact that general skills are unobservable to the model when work experience is absent. Each specification pools all observations across instruction-tuned or pre-trained models separately, with standard errors clustered at the occupation level.

Alignment identification.
We pool observations from nine matched instruct–base pairs that share the same architecture and interact every regressor with an instruction-tuning indicator. The coefficient on each
\times
instruct interaction estimates the causal effect of alignment on that variable, holding model architecture fixed. Standard errors are clustered at the occupation level. We apply this approach to three specifications: the aggregate specification (equation
1
), identifying the causal effect of alignment on overall demographic preferences; the cross-occupation specification, testing whether alignment introduces occupation-level gradients in demographic effects; and the mechanism specifications, testing whether alignment causally amplifies differential returns and information-asymmetry penalties.

Meta-analysis.
To aggregate per-model demographic coefficients into a single pooled estimate, we use DerSimonian–Laird random-effects meta-analysis
19
. This approach treats each model’s coefficient as a draw from a distribution of true effects, estimating both the mean effect and the between-model heterogeneity variance
\tau^{2}
. The pooled estimate therefore reflects the average effect one would expect from a randomly drawn model of the same type (instruction-tuned or base). Because the number of models is small (
K=19
for instruction-tuned,
K=8
for base), we apply the Hartung–Knapp adjustment
25
, which replaces the standard normal critical values with
t(K-1)
quantiles. This correction yields more conservative confidence intervals and
P
-values.

Nonparametric robustness.
Our logistic specification assumes that each variable enters the log-odds index linearly and additively. To test whether any nonlinearity or interaction effects affect our conclusions, we estimate XGBoost (gradient-boosted trees with logistic loss; 300 rounds, max depth 6, learning rate 0.1, subsample 0.8)
16
and random forest (500 trees, minimum leaf size 20)
11
classifiers on the same randomly paired candidate profiles used in the main analysis, but using raw candidate-level features
(X_{i},X_{j})
rather than pairwise differences. To obtain log odds ratios comparable to the logistic regression coefficients, we compute the average marginal effect on the log-odds scale via counterfactual prediction: for each pairwise comparison, we set a candidate’s demographic indicator (e.g.,
female_1
) to 1 versus 0 while holding all other features at their observed values, predict the log-odds under each scenario, and average the difference across all pairs. Five-fold cross-validation confirms no overfitting (train–test logloss gap
<5
% for XGBoost,
<15
% for random forest). For inference, we use an occupation-level cluster bootstrap with
B=1{,}000
iterations
13
: in each iteration, we resample 177 occupations with replacement, retrain the model on the bootstrap sample, and recompute the log odds ratios on the full dataset. Bootstrap standard errors and
P
-values follow from the empirical distribution across iterations, and are asymptotically equivalent to the occupation-level cluster-robust standard errors used in our main analysis
13
. All three methods reveal the same pattern.

IV. Human correspondence study benchmarks

We use Lippens et al.
31
as the primary human benchmark, a meta-analysis of 361 correspondence experiments (2005–2020) across multiple countries that reports estimates for gender, race, and disability status. The reported discrimination ratios (DR) are: female DR
=1.04
(95% CI 1.01–1.07), Black DR
=0.68
(0.64–0.73), and disability DR
=0.59
(0.53–0.66). All values are converted to log discrimination ratios for comparison with AI model coefficients in Fig. 3.

The result of the human comparison is invariant to the choice of benchmark. For race, Quillian et al.
38
meta-analyse 28 U.S. field experiments (1989–2015) and report that White applicants receive 36% more callbacks than equally qualified African Americans (implied DR
\approx 0.74
). Kline et al.
29
, published after both meta-analyses, confirm persistent racial discrimination in 83,000 applications to Fortune 500 firms (DR
\approx 0.87
). For disability, Ameri et al.
4
find that applicants disclosing a disability receive 26% fewer expressions of employer interest (DR
\approx 0.74
)—more conservative than the Lippens et al. pooled estimate but still substantially larger than any AI effect we observe. Substituting any of these alternative benchmarks leaves the AI–human comparison qualitatively unchanged.
