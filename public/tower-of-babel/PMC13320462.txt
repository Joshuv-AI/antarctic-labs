Ethnic differences in health outcomes of Chinese children and adolescents: A comparative meta-analysis
Authors: Hailiang Ran, Junwei Peng, Jin Lu, Yusan Che, Die Fang, Qiongxian Li, Yuanyu Shi, Yandie He, Guiqing Zheng, Shuqing Liu, Yuanyuan Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIncongruent results have been reported regarding ethnic disparities in health-related outcomes amomg Chinese children and adolescents. We widely searched eight databases involving English- and Chinese-languages through Jun 4, 2023. A total of 152 observational studies were included in the meta-analysis. Compared to Han children and adolescents, ethnic minorities had increased risks of anemia (odds ratio [OR]: 1.41, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.15–1.73), iron deficiency anemia (OR: 3.49 [1.66–7.34]), stunting (OR: 1.78 [1.48–2.12]), underweight (OR: 1.54 [1.30–1.82]), self-harm (OR: 1.16 [1.06–1.27]), but decreased risk of obesity (OR: 0.81 [0.69–0.95]). Subgroup analyses showed effect modification by regions in ethnic differences of health. Despite substantial efforts, differences in health-related outcomes between ethnic minority and Han youths in China remain evident and likely reflect underlying structural inequalities rather than the independent effect of ethnicity. Addressing these disparities will require interventions targeting underlying social and environmental determinants of health.Subject areas: health sciences, medicine, medical specialty, pediatrics, patient social context, ethnic issue

Introduction

About 10%–20% the global population consists of ethnic minorities, and worse health outcomes are usually observed in this vulnerable group.1 Malnutrition, higher all-cause mortality, and shorter life expectancy have been commonly documented among ethnic minorities, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).2 However, most studies examining ethnic inequalities in health have been conducted in high-income countries. High-quality evidence from LMICs is scarce, especially from Asia, Latin America, and Africa, the regions where over 80% of the world’s ethnic minorities reside.2,3

China is a middle-income country with one of the largest ethnic minority populations in the world.2 In the Chinese context, ethnic minority groups are officially recognized populations that differ from the Han majority in language, culture, customs, and living environments and are relatively small in population size.4 The 2020 national population census estimated that 125 million residents in China belonged to ethnic minority groups, accounting for 8.89% of the total population.5 Over the past decades, tremendous efforts have been made by the Chinese government to improve the health of ethnic minorities, with achievements that have gained international recognition.6,7 Nevertheless, disparities in health outcomes between ethnic minority and Han populations have been consistently reported in adults.2,8 By contrast, evidence among children and adolescents remains fragmented and inconsistent. Take nutrition status for example, some studies have suggested no significant ethnic differences,9,10 whereas newly published evidence reported worse nutrition in ethnic minorities.11,12 The same contradiction also exists for mental health outcomes.13,14

Nutrition, mental health, and behavioral health are among the major health concerns in childhood and adolescence.15,16 Although these outcomes arise through distinct proximal mechanisms, they may be shaped by shared upstream determinants. From a social determinants of health perspective, differences in socioeconomic development, geographic context, and access to healthcare and education can exert influences across multiple domains of health.17,18,19 In China,OPT DEL With attribute : 0 ethnic group is closely linked to these structural conditions. Differences in health outcomes between ethnic groups should therefore be interpreted within the broader context of structural inequalities. A framework that considers multiple health outcomes may provide a more complete understanding of population-level disparities, consistent with previous studies that have examined ethnic differences across multiple health indicators.8 However, quantitative evidence synthesizing ethnic differences across diverse health domains among Chinese children and adolescents remains limited.

We therefore performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to compare ethnic differences in major health-related outcomes, including nutrition status, mental health, self-harm, suicidal behaviors, and unintentional injuries among Chinese children and adolescents. We estimated the overall ethnic differences, and further investigated individual and environmental factors which may influence these differences.

Results

Study selection

Initially, 48,447 records were identified through database searches. After duplicates were removed, we screened 28,217 records and reviewed 245 full-text articles (Figure 1). To avoid missing newly published studies, we updated our search from November 21, 2022, to Jun 4, 2023, using the identical search terms and databases, and identified 7 additional studies.

Quality assessment of studies included in the systematic review

The risk of bias was evaluated using an adapted version of the Newcastle Ottawa scale incorporating items from the National Institute for Clinical Excellence Quality Appraisal. Studies were classified as high risk of bias if there were five or more items reported high or unclear risk of bias.

Results of syntheses

The meta-analysis indicated that ethnic minority children and adolescents tended to have higher odds of anemia (OR 1.41 [95% CI 1.15–1.73]), iron deficiency anemia (OR 3.49 [95% CI 1.66–7.34]), stunting (OR 1.78 [95% CI 1.48–2.12]), and underweight (OR 1.54 [95% CI 1.30–1.82]) than Han children and adolescents (Figures 3A–3C), but reported lower odds of obesity (OR 0.81 [95% CI 0.69–0.95]), overweight and obesity (OR 0.72 [95% CI 0.57–0.90]) (Figures 3D and 3F). No statistically significant differences were observed for wasting (OR 1.11 [95% CI 0.95–1.29]), malnutrition (OR 0.91 [95% CI 0.69–1.21]), or overweight (OR 0.93 [95% CI 0.83–1.04]) (Figure 3C-3E). Heterogeneity across nutritional outcomes was generally high (I2 = 84.3%–98.9%), indicating substantial variability across studies, except for iron deficiency anemia (I2 = 39.2%).

In subgroup analysis, the ethnic disparity in stunting was more pronounced in western China than in central and eastern China (OR 1.89 [95% CI 1.51–2.38] vs. OR 1.15 [95% CI 0.90–1.46]; p = 0.0024; Figure 4A). However, a lower risk of obesity (OR 0.75 [95% CI 0.62–0.91] vs. OR 1.19 [95% CI 0.94–1.50]; p = 0.0180) was observed. A greater ethnic disparity in anemia was also observed in the western regions than in the central and eastern regions (OR 1.46 [95% CI 1.16–1.84] vs. OR 1.21 [95% CI 0.60–2.45]; p = 0.0131). The risk for underweight ethnic minority children in the west and central and eastern regions was similar (OR 1.48 [95% CI 1.21–1.81] vs. OR 1.37 [95% CI 0.01–255.43]; p = 0.1665). Community-based survey tended to report higher odds of anemia (OR 1.57 [95% CI 1.23–2.01] vs. OR 1.00 [95% CI 0.68–1.46]; p = 0.0045) and underweight (OR 1.73 [95% CI 1.50–1.99] vs. OR 1.06 [95% CI 0.63–1.79]; p = 0.0001) in ethnic minorities than school-based survey (Figure 4B). For subgroup analyses by the number of study sites, data from 27 multi-site studies were available, and the pooled OR for anemia remained elevated (OR 1.79 [95% CI 1.50–2.14]; Figure 4C).

Meta-analysis suggested that ethnic minority children and adolescents showed a higher odds of self-harm (OR 1.16 [95% CI 1.06–1.27]), whereas no differences were found for anxiety, depression, and suicide-related outcomes (Figures 3G–3J). Heterogeneity across these outcomes was generally high (I2 = 70.5%–96.5%), except for self-harm (I2 = 18.2%). Due to limited data availability, subgroup analyses for mental health outcomes were restricted. No clear differences were observed for anxiety across regions, study settings, or study designs. For depressive symptoms, some subgroup analyses suggested higher odds among ethnic minority populations in community-based or multi-site studies, but these estimates were derived from single studies (Figure 4D).39

Meta-analysis suggested no difference in odds of unintentional injuries (OR 1.09 [95% CI 0.69–1.74], Figure 3J) between ethnic minorities and Han majority. Statistical heterogeneity was high (I2 = 92.1%). Subgroup analysis by region indicated a lower odds of unintentional injuries (OR 0.87 [95% CI 0.78–0.97]; Figure 4A) among ethnic minority children from Western China.

Sensitivity analysis

In sensitivity analyses, we used two strategies to test the robustness of our findings. First, leave-one-out analysis showed that no individual study significantly influenced pooled OR for any health outcome (Figures S15–S32). Second, 11 of the 18 outcomes involving high risk of bias studies were analyzed together with low risk of bias studies, and the pooled ORs for these outcomes remained unchanged (Figures S4–S14).

Meta-regression and publication bias

Meta-regression was performed for outcomes with at least 10 included studies when data were available on publication year, sex proportion, GDP per capita. Overall, publication year, sex proportion, and GDP per capita were not significantly associated with the pooled estimates for most outcomes (Table S9). Funnel plots or Begg’s test supported insignificant publication bias for all the outcomes (Figures S1–S3).

Discussion

The current meta-analysis identified differences in several health-related outcomes between ethnic minority and Han children and adolescents in China. Compared with Han children and adolescents, ethnic minority groups tended to show higher risks of anemia, stunting, underweight, and self-harm in many included studies, although the magnitude and direction of these associations varied across settings. However, ethnic differences were not significant for wasting, malnutrition, overweight, depression, unintentional injuries, and suicidal behaviors. In addition, region posed prominent influence on ethnicity-related health outcomes in Chinese children and adolescents. These findings highlight population-level disparities, while also reflecting substantial between-study variability. Given the high heterogeneity observed across analyses, these pooled estimates are more appropriately interpreted as average effects across diverse populations and settings, rather than precise estimates of a single underlying association.

The substantial heterogeneity observed across outcomes is best understood as a consequence of synthesizing evidence across diverse populations and settings. In this study, such variability likely reflects differences in the populations being compared, the contexts in which studies were conducted, and the methods used to measure outcomes. Ethnic minority populations in China are not a homogeneous . They differ in culture, diet, language, geographic setting, and access to health and educational resources.4,180,181 Combining these populations within a single analytical category may therefore introduce genuine between-study variation. In addition, ethnicity in China is closely linked to broader structural conditions, including regional economic development, healthcare access, and geography, particularly because many minority populations are concentrated in less developed western regions.4,8 As a result, between-study differences may reflect variation in structural disadvantage rather than a uniform association with ethnicity itself. Differences in sampling frames, study settings, and outcome definitions may also have contributed to the observed heterogeneity.

This study observed that the double burden of malnutrition (DBM), defined as the coexistence of undernutrition and overweight or obesity, differs across ethnic groups among Chinese children and adolescents. Stunting and iron deficiency anemia were more prevalent among ethnic minority children and adolescents, whereas the odds of obesity was higher among Han majority children and adolescents. This pattern may reflect differences in the stage of nutritional transition across populations. In more economically developed regions, such as central and eastern China, the prevalence of overweight and obesity among children and adolescents has increased substantially in recent decades.182,183 In contrast, evidence from less developed regions, including areas with a high concentration of ethnic minority populations, suggests that undernutrition remains a more prominent concern.184,185 Accordingly, the lower odds of obesity observed among ethnic minority children and adolescents may indicate an earlier stage of nutritional transition, in which undernutrition persists while the rise in obesity has not yet fully emerged. This interpretation is consistent with patterns observed in low- and middle-income countries, where different population subgroups experience distinct manifestations of nutritional imbalance.186 These differences are likely shaped by broader structural factors, including economic development, and access to healthcare.

The health and well-being of ethnic minorities have always been priorities in China. In the past several decades, the Chinese government has launched a series of programs aimed at benefiting ethnic minority populations. Specifically, the sustained poverty alleviation initiatives and the New Cooperative Medical Scheme have contributed to improvements in the physical and mental health of ethnic minority populations by increasing access to public health services and local health care.9,187 Despite these efforts and achievements, the present findings suggest that ethnic minority children and adolescents continue to experience a higher burden of certain adverse health outcomes. Several analytical studies ascribed this ethnic health gap primarily to underlying structural inequalities, including disparities in economic development, healthcare accessibility, and living environments.180,181 This interpretation is supported by our subgroup analyses, which showed notable regional variation, with more pronounced disparities in western China. Economic development in western China lags behind eastern China. Meanwhile, ethnic minority groups are primarily concentrated in western China. Children and adolescents in these settings may therefore experience overlapping social disadvantages, including lower socioeconomic status, more limited healthcare resources, and geographic isolation, which may jointly contribute to poorer health outcomes.188 At the same time, this regional pattern should be interpreted with caution, because geographic context and ethnic distribution are partly overlapping in China. Accordingly, the observed regional differences may reflect not only regional context itself, but also the geographic concentration of ethnic minority populations.

Incongruent results were reported between school-based and community-based studies, which may seem puzzling given the extremely high coverage of compulsory education and school enrollment rates in China.6 A reasonable explanation could be non-response bias, since school-based studies can only include students who were currently present at school, those with health issues might be absent temporarily or even dropped out, which could lead to underestimation in prevalence of health-related outcomes.189 Inconsistent results were also observed between single-site and multi-site studies, probably caused by substantial ethnic heterogeneity in China.5 Thereby, a one-size-fits-all approach to address health problems and needs of the youths, particularly those from ethnic minority groups, should be avoided.

In recent years, China has implemented a range of policies to address the DBM among children and adolescents.190 The pattern of nutritional transition observed in this study provides relevant insights for other low- and middle-income countries with multi-ethnic populations. Our findings suggest that public health strategies should be tailored to the distribution of nutritional risks across population subgroups. In regions with a high concentration of ethnic minority populations, where undernutrition remains prevalent, interventions should prioritize early-life nutrition, including maternal nutritional support and school-based feeding programs.17,191 In contrast, among Han children and adolescents, prevention strategies should emphasize integrated approaches that combine school-based, family-based, and individual-level interventions.192 More broadly, effective responses to the DBM require context-specific resource allocation and interventions guided by underlying structural conditions, rather than ethnicity alone.

Limitations of the study

The current meta-analysis was comprehensive in scope, and the low risk of publication bias further supports the robustness of the pooled estimates. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, a high level of heterogeneity among included studies was found, suggesting variability across study populations, settings, and measurement approaches. Although subgroup analyses and meta-regression did not fully account for the observed heterogeneity, this variability likely reflects differences across populations, contexts, and study methods, as discussed previously. Data on key population characteristics, including age distribution and urban-rural context, were not consistently reported across studies. In particular, the lack of detailed age-specific estimates and joint information on ethnicity and urban-rural setting precluded further examination of these factors as potential contributors to between-study variation. Second, most studies in our review had a cross-sectional design, thus causal inference cannot be drawn. Third, we pooled unadjusted effect estimates for health outcomes, as only a limited number of studies reported estimates adjusted for covariates. Therefore, the combined results may be substantially influenced by residual confounding, particularly from socioeconomic, geographic, and healthcare access factors that are closely linked to ethnicity in China. The observed disparities are thus likely to reflect underlying structural inequalities, including differences in health literacy, economic development, and healthcare accessibility between populations. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive evidence of population-level disparities rather than causal effects of ethnicity. Fourth, ethnic minority populations in China are highly heterogeneous, differing substantially in geographic distribution, socioeconomic conditions, and cultural practices. However, most included studies aggregated diverse minority groups into a single category, which may obscure important between-group differences and contribute to the observed heterogeneity. Therefore, the pooled estimates should be interpreted as average differences across heterogeneous populations rather than effects applicable to any specific minority group. Future epidemiological studies based on nationally representative sample of children and adolescents from ethnic backgrounds and incorporating a wider range of psychological and behavioral problems are needed to provide stronger evidence in understanding ethnic disparities in health-related outcomes for Chinese youths.

In conclusion, this study highlights substantial differences in nutritional and behavioral health outcomes between ethnic minority and Han children and adolescents in China. These differences likely reflect broader structural inequalities rather than the independent effect of ethnicity itself. Addressing these disparities will require coordinated efforts targeting underlying social and environmental determinants of health.

Method details

Search strategy and selection criteria

The present study was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines (Table S1).193 The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (No. CRD42022378595). We searched 5 English databases (Web of Science, PsycINFO, MEDLINE, PubMed, Embase) and 3 Chinese databases (Wanfang, VIP, China National Knowledge Infrastructure [CNKI]) from database inception and November 21, 2022, without language restrictions. We tailored search strategies for each database. The references lists of the included studies were also reviewed. Detailed search terms and methods are reported in the supplemental information (Table S3). We also updated our searches to incorporate all evidence from November 21, 2022 to Jun 4, 2023, to evaluate whether these newly published studies might alter the pooled estimates.

We searched for observational studies that compared health-related outcomes between ethnic majority and minority Chinese children and adolescents aged below 18 years. In China, the ethnic majority group is Han, representing 91.11% of the total population.5 All other ethnicities are ethnic minorities. We further excluded studies with a sample size of less than 100, studies in which less than 50% of participants were aged below 18 years, and studies in which the mean or median age was above 18 years. The major health outcomes of interest included nutrition (anemia, stunting, wasting, underweight, overweight, obesity), mental health (depression, anxiety, conduct disorders), self-harm, suicidal behaviors (suicide ideation, plan, and attempt), and unintentional injuries. The inclusion criteria for each outcome and the final definitions of outcomes were summarized in the supplemental information (Table S2).

Literature screening and data extraction

The titles and abstracts of the studies were independently screened by two authors (HR and JP). After that, the full texts of the remaining studies were independently assessed by two authors (YC and DF). Any disagreements regarding the inclusion or exclusion of studies were resolved through discussion between the reviewers or by consulting a senior researcher (YX). For each study included in the review, three pairs of authors (HR and JP; YC and DF; QL and YS) independently extracted data on general study details and design, participant characteristics, sample size, and outcome measures (mean scores with standard deviations, frequency, proportions, crude or adjusted odds ratios, crude or adjusted risk ratios) using a data extraction table, which was provided in the supplemental information (Table S4). The same authors also assessed the risk of bias. If study subjects were described in more than one article, then only the most recent data were extracted. In addition, if a study reported results from the repeated survey, only the latest data or effect estimates were extracted. When a single study reported more than one eligible outcome, outcome-specific effect sizes were extracted separately for the corresponding meta-analyses. These effect sizes were not assumed to be statistically independent.

Literature quality assessment

The risk of bias was evaluated using an adapted version of the Newcastle Ottawa Scale that incorporated items from the National Institute for Clinical Excellence Quality Appraisal.194,195 Studies were classified as having a high risk of bias if five or more items were rated as having a high or unclear risk of bias. This tool and cutoff were validated by a previous study.196 Detailed items for risk of bias were provided in the supplemental information (Table S5).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All meta-analyses were performed in R statistical system (Version: 4.5.2, The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with the “metafor” package (Version: 4.8-0). Studies rated as having a high risk of bias were excluded from the main meta-analyses. The pooled odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated for binary outcomes. The pooled standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% CIs were adopted for quantitative outcomes. To account for the inclusion of multiple effect sizes from the same study, we applied robust variance estimation using the “clubSandwich” package (Version 0.6.0).197 This approach accounts for statistical dependence among effect sizes contributed by the same study, including those arising from multiple reported outcomes, thereby enhancing power and providing more precise estimates. Random-effects model was adopted to synthesize results. We used the I2 statistic to estimate heterogeneity. Funnel plots and Begg’s test were used to evaluate potential publication bias.

We planned subgroup analyses comparing school-based versus community-based studies and multi-site versus single-site studies. In addition, comparisons between regions (western China versus central and eastern China) were conducted to evaluate the influence of socioeconomic and environmental context on ethnic differences in health outcomes among children and adolescents. For regional classification, studies were categorized according to the standard economic zone classification defined by the National Bureau of Statistics of China.198 Western China includes Chongqing, Sichuan, Guizhou, Yunnan, Tibet, Shaanxi, Gansu, Qinghai, Ningxia, Xinjiang, Guangxi, and Inner Mongolia. All remaining provinces and municipalities were grouped into central and eastern China. For each outcome, subgroup analyses were conducted based on the geographic location of the study population. The provinces represented in each subgroup varied depending on data availability and are summarized in the supplemental information (Table S6).

We conducted a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the robustness of our findings using the leave-one-out method and by repeating analyses using all eligible studies (including studies with a high risk of bias), because studies classified as having a high risk of bias were excluded from the main meta-analysis. Additionally, meta-regression was adopted to assess whether age, sex, and provincial gross domestic product per capita (GDP per capita) were associated with the pooled estimates.
