Human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells ameliorate Diabetic Kidney Disease by restoring macrophage efferocytosis
Authors: Shiwen Wu, Wanying Xu, Jiaqian Yao, Renjie Li, Yanfang Yang, Jianhong Jin, Xueqian Peng, Wenhong Liu, Zhiwei Xu, Ai Mi, Hui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic Kidney Disease (DKD) is a major complication driven by chronic inflammation and impaired tissue homeostasis. While mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) show promise, the precise mechanisms by which human adipose-derived MSCs (hASCs) modulate macrophage-mediated resolution of inflammation remain to be fully elucidated.MethodsWe integrated single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis of human DKD kidneys with in vivo evaluations in db/db mice and in vitro co-culture models. We employed transcriptomic and molecular approaches to systematically investigate how hASCs impact macrophage functional states.ResultsscRNA-seq revealed a significant dysregulation of phagocytosis and efferocytosis pathways in human DKD macrophages. In vivo, hASCs effectively homed to injured kidneys, improved renal filtration, and attenuated pathological injury. Rather than a simple binary pro-inflammation to anti-inflammatory switch, hASC treatment restored a comprehensive efferocytic program involving multiple functional stages: chemotaxis (GPR132), recognition/engulfment (PARP9, ELMO1, RAC1), and lysosomal digestion/exhaution and polarisation (LAMP1, LIPA, PPAR-γ, ABCA1). Introduction

Diabetic Kidney Disease (DKD), a major microvascular complication of diabetes, represents the leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) globally [1]. The pathogenesis of DKD is multifactorial, yet chronic inflammation is widely recognized as a pivotal driver of renal dysfunction. Elevated systemic and intra-renal expression of proinflammatory cytokines correlates strongly with albuminuria and disease progression [2, 3]. Given the central role of inflammation in driving disease progression, a deeper understanding of its underlying mechanisms is essential to identifying effective interventions.

Human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hASCs) have emerged as a promising autologous cell therapy due to their abundance, ease of harvest, and potent immunomodulatory properties. MSCs exert therapeutic effects primarily through homing to injured tissues and paracrine signaling, which facilitate tissue regeneration and modulate immune responses [4]. Preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated that MSC administration yields favorable outcomes in diabetes and its complications with minimal adverse effects. MSCs protect podocytes from high glucose-induced apoptosis via the secretion of trophic factors such as epidermal growth factor (EGF) and transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1), thereby attenuating renal injury [5, 6]. Furthermore, MSCs reduce inflammatory mediators in both renal tissue and serum, contributing to overall renal improvement [7].

In DKD, hyperglycemia induces the apoptosis of renal parenchymal cells, including podocytes and mesangial cells. This cell death triggers macrophage infiltration and immune activation, sustaining a local inflammatory state [8]. A central function of macrophages in this context is efferocytosis, the efferocytosis clearance of apoptotic cells, which is essential for immune homeostasis. Efficient efferocytosis promotes macrophage polarization from a pro-inflammatory to an anti-inflammatory phenotype, facilitating inflammation resolution [9]. However, single-cell transcriptomic studies reveal that the immune landscape in DKD is characterized by a skewed pro-inflammation/anti-inflammatory ratio and altered cellular crosstalk. While MSCs-derived exosomes have been reported to promote anti-inflammatory polarization, the specific role of efferocytosis in DKD pathogenesis, and the mechanisms by which hASCs modulate this process, remain incompletely defined [10–15].

Therefore, this study integrates public single-cell sequencing data from human DKD specimens with in vivo and in vitro models to investigate how hASCs restore macrophage efferocytic function and mitigate DKD. Our work aims to delineate the underlying molecular pathways and provide new insights for future therapeutic development.

Results

hASCs significantly improve disease progression in Diabetic Kidney Disease mice

Compared with the control group, mice in the model group exhibited significant weight gain and persistent hyperglycemia (fasting blood glucose, FBG), consistent with the diabetic characteristics of type 2 diabetes. Concurrently, hASCs effectively treated excessive weight gain and significantly reduced fasting blood glucose levels (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1A, B). Furthermore, the model group exhibited significantly elevated glycated blood glucose (GSP) levels indicative of hyperglycemic control, whereas hASC treatment demonstrably reduced GSP levels (Fig. 1C). Regarding insulin levels (Fig. 1F), the model group exhibited elevated insulin levels, suggesting insulin resistance; insulin levels showed a downward trend after hASC treatment, indicating potential improvement in insulin resistance.

Analysis of biochemical markers of renal dysfunction revealed significantly elevated serum urea nitrogen (BUN) and serum creatinine (CREA) levels in DKD samples (P < 0.001), indicating reduced glomerular filtration rate. hASC transplantation significantly mitigated these abnormalities, with the high-dose group (hASCs-H) demonstrating particularly pronounced effects (Fig. 1D, E).

The renal protective effect of hASCs was further confirmed by analysis. Mice in the model group exhibited marked polyuria and elevated 24-hour urinary protein levels, typical clinical features of DKD (Fig. 1G, J). Notably, hASC treatment significantly reduced urine output and urinary protein excretion (P < 0.001) while markedly restoring urinary creatinine (U-CREA) clearance capacity (Fig. 1I). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that hASCs effectively ameliorate DKD by improving glycemic control and renal filtration function.

hASCs can modulate Diabetic Kidney Disease through homing effects and paracrine actions

The homing and paracrine capacity of hASCs were assessed by fluorescence imaging and ELISA. We first confirmed in vitro that fluorescence intensity correlated with cell number (Fig. S1A) and ruled out potential interference of fur on imaging signals in vivo (Fig. S1B). Following intravenous injection, No fluorescence signals were detected in the control group or model group; therefore, these two groups will not be discussed in subsequent experiments. Concurrently, the distribution of hASCs in the remaining three groups (hASCs-L, hASCs-M, hASCs-H) was tracked and observed over a 7-day period. Significant renal accumulation was observed on day 3, with signals weakening thereafter; by day 7, fluorescence was detectable only in the high-dose (hASCs-H) group (Fig. 2A-C). Based on existing in vivo imaging data, there is sufficient evidence to demonstrate the homing effect of mesenchymal stem cells in mice [16, 17]. To investigate whether this homing effect exhibits dose dependency, ex vivo imaging was performed on mouse kidneys harvested at the end of day seven post-injection. Ex vivo imaging of kidneys on day 7 further confirmed this dose-dependent enrichment (Fig. 2D-E).

Compared with the normal control group, VEGF and EGF levels in the kidneys of the model group were significantly reduced (P < 0.001), reflecting microvascular injury and impaired repair capacity caused by DKD. However, hASC transplantation significantly restored the expression levels of both growth factors. Notably, the hASCs-H group exhibited a marked recovery in VEGF and EGF levels (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2F-G).

In summary, hASCs may mitigate the progression of diabetic nephropathy by improving the renal microenvironment through time- and dose-dependent homing mechanisms, as well as by elevating VEGF and EGF levels in the kidneys.

hASCs significantly ameliorate renal lesions in Diabetic Kidney Disease

As shown in the figure, compared with the normal-sized, bright red kidneys in the control group, the kidneys in the model group exhibited significantly increased volume and a darker color, demonstrating pronounced renal hypertrophy. Statistical analysis confirmed that the total kidney weight in the model group was significantly increased (P < 0.01). However, hASC transplantation — particularly in the high-dose hASCs-H group — significantly reversed this pathological increase, reducing kidney weight to near-normal levels. This indicates that hASCs effectively attenuated DKD-induced hypertrophic lesions (Fig. 3A, B).

Therefore, we further evaluated renal pathological changes using H&E, PAS, and Masson staining (Fig. 3C). H&E staining revealed marked mesangial expansion, tubular vacuolar degeneration, and inflammatory cell activation in the model group. PAS staining further revealed marked glycogen deposition and perinephric thickening in the model group. Masson trichrome staining correspondingly reflected the depth of renal fibrosis (blue areas), suggesting the formation of renal fibrosis. Corresponding semi-quantitative pathological scores indicated that all relevant damage scores were significantly impaired in the model group (P < 0.001). Notably, hASC treatment exhibited a dose-dependent improvement in these pathological alterations. Compared to the model group, the hASCs-H group demonstrated significantly reduced mesangial expansion and collagen deposition, with markedly restored pathological scores, confirming the significant anti-fibrotic and tissue-repairing capabilities of hASCs (Fig. 3D-F).

Simultaneously, we examined the ultrastructure of the glomerular filtration barrier using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). In the control group, podocyte foot processes exhibited orderly arrangement with optimal basement membrane thickness. In contrast, the model group demonstrated extensive foot process fusion and loss, along with irregular thickening of the basement membrane (indicated by red arrows) [18]. Following hASC intervention, podocyte morphology significantly improved, podocyte foot process fusion decreased, and basement membrane thickness normalized. Simultaneously, thickening of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM) and widening and regression of foot processes are more pronounced (Fig. 3G, H, I). These findings indicate that hASCs effectively maintain structural integrity of the glomerular filtration barrier.

Single-cell profiling reveals macrophage efferocytosis impairment in Diabetic Kidney Disease

To investigate the cellular pathogenesis of DKD, we integrated four single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets from the GEO database (GSE131882, GSE195460, GSE131685, GSE151302), comprising five DKD and nine normal control (NC) human kidney samples [19]. After quality control to exclude cells with high mitochondrial gene content (> 15%) and data normalization, we performed principal component analysis and UMAP clustering using the top 2000 highly variable genes.

UMAP visualization showed well-integrated data without significant batch effects, enabling robust cross-dataset analysis (Fig. 4A, B). We identified 13 distinct renal cell populations — including proximal and distal convoluted tubule cells (PCT, DCT), connecting tubule-principal cells (CNT_PC), podocytes (PODO), endothelial cells, fibroblasts, T cells, B cells, and macrophages — Annotation of Cell Populations Using Classically Marked Genes (Fig. 4C).

Analysis of Cellular Tissue Differences revealed significant differences in both cellular composition and gene expression profiles between DKD and NC samples (p < 0.05, |log₂FC| > 0.5). Macrophages (All types) demonstrated the most prominent alterations, exhibiting substantial proportional changes and hosting the highest number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among all cell types, suggesting their pivotal role in DKD progression (Fig. 4D, E). Gene Ontology enrichment analysis covering processes such as immune responses and the extracellular matrix (Fig. 4F).

KEGG enrichment analysis identified the “Phagosome” and “Efferocytosis” pathways (highlighted in red), suggesting that efferocytosis functions of macrophages in the kidneys of DKD patients may be impaired (Fig. 4G). Expression heatmap analysis further highlighted dysregulation of core efferocytosis genes — including RAC1, ELMO1, ABCA1, and LIPA — in DKD macrophages compared to controls (Fig. 4G).

In summary, single-cell analysis suggests that DKD progression may be closely associated with impaired cell death function in macrophages.

Transcriptomic analysis of hASCs improving Diabetic Kidney Disease via promoting macrophage efferocytosis

Pharmacodynamic evaluation confirmed the robust therapeutic effect of hASCs in diabetic nephropathy (DKD), the hASCs-H group demonstrated the most effective treatment outcomes. As shown in the figure, immunofluorescence staining revealed significantly enhanced CD86 (red) fluorescence intensity in the model group (model), while CD206 (green) fluorescence was faint, indicating promotion of the most pro-inflammatory cells (Fig. 5A). The treatment group exhibited dose-dependent (L, M, H) significant suppression of CD86 expression and enhanced expression of CD206. This finding was further validated at the protein level (Fig. 5B). Western blot quantification (Fig. 5C, D) revealed that compared to the control group, the model group exhibited high expression of CD86 and low expression of Arg1 (P < 0.001). hASCs activation significantly altered this phenotype, with the high-dose group (hASCs-H) exhibiting a marked effect: it promoted a significant upregulation of Arg1 protein levels and a significant decrease in CD86 protein levels. In summary, hASCs effectively promote the transition of macrophages from the pro-inflammatory phenotype to the anti-inflammatory phenotype, that findings are consistent with previous studies [14].

To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we performed transcriptomic sequencing of renal tissues from each experimental group(control, model, hASCs-H). Principal component analysis and differential gene expression patterns demonstrated clear separation among the three groups (Fig. 5E, F). With thresholds set at p < 0.05 and |log₂FC| > 1.5, we identified 122 significantly upregulated and 122 downregulated genes in hASCs-treated mice compared to the DKD model group. Functional characterization revealed that these differentially expressed genes were primarily enriched in: Biological processes: immune response and immune system regulation, suggesting hASCs modulate the renal immune microenvironment; Molecular functions: chemokine activity and receptor binding, indicating regulation of immune cell infiltration; Cellular components: extracellular space organization, implicating extracellular matrix remodeling. KEGG pathway analysis identified cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, phagosome formation, PPAR signaling, and efferocytosis as key pathways through which hASCs ameliorate DKD pathology. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further confirmed that hASC treatment significantly enhanced macrophage efferocytosis — a crucial process for clearing apoptotic cells and resolving inflammation (Fig. 5G-J).

In summary, hASCs may improve DKD by enhancing macrophage phagocytosis, thereby modulating local renal immune responses and promoting tissue repair.

As shown in the figure, compared with the control group, the levels of the antioxidant enzyme glutathione (GSH) in the kidney tissue of the model group were significantly reduced, while the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), a peroxidation product, were significantly increased (P < 0.001), indicating a severe oxidative imbalance in the kidney tissue (Fig. 6A, B). This reveals that hASCs can effectively attenuate high-glucose-induced oxidative stress in the kidney.

Inflammatory factors are key drivers of DKD fibrosis [20]. ELISA results showed that pro-inflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-1β were significantly upregulated in the model group, while anti-inflammatory factors IL-10 and TGF-β1 were abnormally downregulated. hASCs significantly reversed this pattern by reducing TNF-α and IL-1β while upregulating IL-10 and TGF-β1, shifting the immune microenvironment toward an anti-inflammatory state (Fig. 6F–H).

Additionally, we detected the proinfammatory mediator and during Efferocytosis “Find-me” signaling molecule lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), LPC levels were significantly elevated in the model group (Fig. 6N), while hASC intervention restored these levels to near-normal levels.

To assess apoptotic cells in renal tissue, TUNEL staining was performed. As shown in Fig. 6, model group kidneys exhibited numerous TUNEL-positive cells (green fluorescence), indicating widespread apoptosis. In contrast, the hASCs-treated group demonstrated a significant reduction in apoptotic cells (P < 0.001) (Fig. 6C, D). Although Bcl-2/Bax family proteins exhibit complex regulatory patterns, the model group showed decreased Bcl-2 expression and elevated levels of pro-apoptotic proteins (BAX, Caspase 3). Treatment with hASCs significantly reversed these changes except for Bcl-2. Concurrently, Active-Caspase 3 — a key protein in the apoptotic process — was significantly elevated in the model group but markedly decreased in the hASCs-treated group, highly consistent with TUNEL results(Fig. 6E-M).

Finally, immunofluorescence analysis detected the outward flipping of phosphatidylserine (PS), a hallmark of early apoptosis and efferocytosis signaling (“eat-me” signal) [21]. The model group exhibited strong PS fluorescence, suggesting extensive accumulation of unphagocytosed apoptotic cells, whereas hASC treatment markedly reduced PS signal intensity (Fig. 6O, P).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that hASCs not only inhibit apoptosis but may also promote efficient efferocytosis of apoptotic cells by macrophages, thereby maintaining microenvironmental homeostasis in renal tissue.

hASCs significantly improve efferocytosis function in macrophages from DKD mice

To observe and verify the presence of efferocytosis within hASCs, we performed double immunofluorescence staining of renal tissue using TUNEL (macrophages, green) and F4/80 (macrophages, red). As shown in the figure, the model group (model) exhibited numerous scattered TUNEL-positive cells interspersed with F4/80 fluorescence signals throughout the renal tissue. The hASCs-treated group (especially the high-dose group) not only significantly reduced total TUNEL-positive signals but also revealed macrophages in contact with or engulfing apoptotic cells (co-localization areas), directly confirming the occurrence of macrophage phagocytosis in vivo (Fig. 7A).

Efferocytosis is a complex, multi-step process. We further examined the expression of key associated proteins. IHC and Western blot results revealed abnormally high expression of the “digestion” protein and lysosomal marker LAMP1 in the model group during phagocytosis, suggesting increased lysosomal load or dysfunction [22]; hASC treatment significantly reduced LAMP1 levels, indicating potential restoration of lysosomal abnormalities (Fig. 7B, C, E).

Additionally, Western blot analysis revealed significant upregulation of efferocytosis-associated proteins, “Eat me and Transform” Signaling Molecules — GPR132, ELMO1, PARP9, and RAC1, “Exhaustion and Polarisation” Signaling Molecules — LIPA in the model group, potentially reflecting compensatory yet ineffective cytokine responses during DKD progression. hASCs effectively downregulated these proteins (P < 0.001), restoring cellular signaling to near-normal levels. Concurrently, the DKD model caused significant dysregulation in the expression of key load-regulating factors PPAR-γ (A critical mediator coupling efferocytosis with anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization) and ABCA1 (cholesterol efflux transporter) (P < 0.001), indicating impaired macrophage function. Excitingly, hASCs significantly restored PPAR-γ, and ABCA1 expression levels (P < 0.001) (Fig. 7D, F–L).

This suggests hASCs may achieve tissue repair by restoring macrophage efferocytosis function, thereby modulating the intracellular microenvironment of renal tissue cells.

hASCs significantly promote macrophage efferocytosis in vitro

To investigate the regulation of macrophage efferocytosis by hASCs, we established an in vitro co-culture system (Fig. S2). Based on CCK-8 viability assays and Western blot analysis of the renal injury marker WT-1, a glucose concentration of 55 mM was selected to model DKD under high glucose (HG) conditions (Fig. S3A–C). hASC treatment significantly attenuated oxidative stress in target cells under HG, but not under normal glucose (NG) conditions (Fig. S3D, E). Expression of the mesenchymal stem cell marker CD105 and the secreted factors EGF and VEGF confirmed that hASCs mitigated podocyte injury through migration and paracrine actions (Fig. S3F–H).

We exposed podocytes to a hyperglycemic (HG) environment and co-cultured them with macrophages and hASCs. Flow cytometry (Annexin V-FITC/PI) analysis revealed significantly elevated apoptosis rates in the HG group compared to the normal glucose (NG) group (P < 0.001). However, co-culture with hASCs markedly reduced HG-induced apoptosis (Fig. 8A-B). This finding was confirmed at the protein level. Western blot analysis revealed that HG induced upregulation of pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and Active-Caspase 3, while downregulating the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2. hASC intervention effectively reversed the expression pattern of these apoptosis-related proteins, significantly reducing the cleaved activation of Active-Caspase 3 (P < 0.001), further confirming the direct protective effect of hASCs against high-glucose-induced apoptosis (Fig. 8C-D).

Next, we examined the effect of hASCs on macrophage polarization under high-glucose conditions. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that macrophages in the HG group predominantly exhibited strong CD86 (red, pro-inflammation marker) expression with weak CD206 (green, anti-inflammatory marker) expression. In stark contrast, co-culture with hASCs significantly suppressed CD86 fluorescence intensity and enhanced CD206 expression, suggesting a shift toward an anti-inflammatory macrophage phenotype (Fig. 8F). Protein quantitative analysis corroborated the fluorescence findings: hASCs significantly downregulated the HG-induced pro-inflammation marker CD86 and upregulated the anti-inflammatory marker Arg1 (P < 0.01), confirming that hASCs can correct HG-induced macrophage polarization imbalance in vitro (Fig. 8G-I).

To directly assess hASCs’ impact on macrophage phagocytosis of apoptotic cells, we established an in vitro phagocytosis model using dual staining with TUNEL (marking apoptotic cells, green) and F4/80 (marking macrophages, red). In the NG and NG+hASCs groups, macrophage phagocytosis of apoptotic bodies was observable (yellow colocalization arrows). HG treatment significantly reduced this colocalization, indicating impaired efferocytosis function. Notably, co-culture with hASCs significantly restored macrophage uptake of apoptotic cells under HG conditions (increased yellow spots) (Fig. 8E). hASC treatment significantly restored the levels of these key proteins (P < 0.001) (Fig. 8J-L), highly consistent with in vivo results, indicating that hASCs reestablished the impaired efferocytosis function of macrophages.

To investigate the causal relationship between hASCs and the rescue of macrophage phagocytosis in Diabetic Kidney Disease (DKD), we introduced the specific PPAR-γ inhibitor GW9662 into a high-glucose (HG) co-culture system. As demonstrated by TUNEL staining in Supplementary (Fig. S4A and B), although hASCs significantly reduced high-glucose-induced podocyte apoptosis, this protective effect was abolished upon GW9662 addition, with apoptosis rates reverting to levels comparable to the high-glucose group. Result reveal that under HG conditions, hASCs upregulate the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 and downregulate the pro-apoptotic protein Bax. However, PPAR-γ inhibition significantly reversed the expression of these proteins (Fig. S4C-E). We subsequently investigated whether macrophage phenotypic switching was hASC-dependent. As anticipated that hASC treatment in a high-glucose environment suppressed expression of the pro-inflammation marker CD86 and upregulated expression of the anti-inflammatory marker Arg1 (Fig. S4F-H ). Notably, this immunomodulatory shift was effectively blocked by GW9662. In the presence of the inhibitor, macrophages failed to polarize toward the anti-inflammatory phenotype, suggesting PPAR-γ may be a key target for hASC-driven macrophage polarization. To further validate the molecular mechanism, we assessed the expression of endocytosis-related proteins. Results confirmed the efficacy of the inhibitor: ELMO1 levels were significantly elevated in the GW9662-treated group( Fig. S4I, J, and K). Notably, while hASCs restored ELMO1 abnormally overexpressed in the high-glucose group, GW9662 treatment blocked this restoration. These findings suggest that hASCs may primarily improve impaired macrophage efferocytosis function, thereby alleviating diabetic nephropathy.

Discussion

Current clinical management of DKD primarily involves combination therapy, typically comprising renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) blockers — such as ACE inhibitors or angiotensin II receptor blockers — together with sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors (e.g., empagliflozin), supplemented with nifedipine when necessary. Although this strategy can modestly delay disease progression, its utility is limited by associated adverse effects, including hyperkalemia, orthostatic hypotension, and genitourinary tract infections [23]. A recent clinical trial reported that a single intravenous infusion of allogeneic MSCs in adults with DKD led to stable or improved estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) at 12 weeks post-treatment. The treatment exhibited a favorable safety profile, with no reports of infusion-related acute adverse events, treatment-related adverse events, serious adverse events, or persistent donor-specific anti-HLA antibodies. In line with these clinical observations, our study also detected no significant adverse effects following administration of hASCs, further supporting the safety of MSC-based therapy in DKD [24].

Efferocytosis, the efferocytosis clearance of apoptotic cells, is a crucial homeostatic process mediated by professional phagocytes such as macrophages, dendritic cells, and neutrophils. This process occurs through a coordinated sequence: apoptotic cells release “find-me” signals to recruit phagocytes; phagocytes then recognize “eat-me” signals on target cells and initiate engulfment; internalized cellular material is subsequently degraded via lysosomal digestion; and finally, metabolic byproducts are released extracellularly for reuse. Completion of efferocytosis promotes macrophage polarization from a pro-inflammatory to an anti-inflammatory phenotype, facilitating inflammation resolution and tissue repair [25–27]. However, the regulation of efferocytosis within the inflammatory renal microenvironment of DKD remains poorly understood. Elucidating its molecular mechanisms in DKD will require advanced approaches such as single-cell RNA sequencing.

Macrophages have been established as direct mediators of diabetic kidney injury, with their glomerular infiltration levels correlating closely with clinical and prognostic indicators of DKD [28, 29]. Consistent with these reports, we observed significant alterations in renal macrophage proportions and a marked increase in macrophage-related differentially expressed genes in DKD model mice, reinforcing their central role in disease pathogenesis.

Our study provide evidence that the restoration of macrophage efferocytic function is a critical component of the hASC-mediated therapeutic response in DKD. The role of hASCs extends beyond altering macrophage polarization; more significantly, they remodel the transcriptional programs and phagocytic characteristics of macrophages while modulating the chronic inflammatory microenvironment within renal tissue. The efferocytosis process and its associated lipid metabolism (potentially mediated by PPAR-γ) appear to facilitate the transition of macrophages from a pro-inflammatory transcriptional signature toward an anti-inflammatory (pro-resolving) program. Concurrent RNA-seq analysis revealed that hASC-induced alterations significantly remodel rescue the functional program of macrophages in the high-glucose environment of diabetes. While macrophage plasticity has historically been described using the M1/M2 dichotomy, recent advances in single-cell transcriptomics reveal that this binary classification oversimplifies macrophage heterogeneity, particularly in chronic metabolic diseases like DKD. Instead of a simple phenotypic switch, our scRNA-seq and RNA-seq data demonstrate that hASCs orchestrate a comprehensive shift in macrophage transcriptional programs. By rescuing the efferocytic signature and activating the PPAR-γ-mediated inflammation-resolution pathway, hASCs restore the functional integrity of macrophages rather than merely altering their static polarization states. However, recent consensus and single-cell transcriptomic advances indicate that this binary classification oversimplifies the highly dynamic and heterogeneous nature of macrophages in vivo, particularly in complex chronic metabolic diseases like DKD [30, 31]. In lipotoxic and hyperglycemic microenvironments, rather than strictly committing to an M1 or M2 fate, macrophages adopt specific, multi-dimensional transcriptional programs driven by metabolic and environmental cues [32]. In our study, hASC treatment did not merely induce a generic pro-inflammatory-to-anti-inflammatory switch; rather, it restored the efferocytic signature and inflammation-resolution pathways. The robust upregulation of PPAR-γ and ABCA1 mirrors the specialized transcriptional reprogramming required to handle the metabolic load of engulfed apoptotic bodies. As highlighted by recent studies, successful efferocytosis inherently couples target engulfment with lipid efflux (via ABCA1) and the production of pro-resolving mediators. Thus, our findings demonstrate that hASCs ameliorate DKD by rejuvenating this comprehensive inflammation-resolution transcriptional program, promoting the efficient clearance of necrotic debris and contributing to the improvement of tissue homeostasis.

Efferocytosis is a cornerstone of tissue homeostasis. In DKD, the failure of this process transforms macrophages into Pro-inflammatory cells, produce pro-inflammatory factors. The accumulation of LPC (“find-me” signal) and PS (“eat-me” signal) in our model group confirms this functional blockade. anti-inflammatory macrophages are generally more efferocytosis, but crucially, the act of efferocytosis itself (and the subsequent lipid metabolism via PPAR-γ) actively drives pro-inflammation macrophages toward anti-inflammatory polarization (often termed “metabolic adaptation”). Crucially, we our transcriptional and data implicate PPAR-γ as a potential molecular bridge. hASCs successfully restored PPAR-γ expression, thereby facilitating the “metabolic adaptation” required for sustained efferocytosis [33–35]. In the context of metabolic diseases, “lysosomal overload” or stress is common. The elevated LAMP1 levels observed in our model group likely reflect an accumulation of lysosomes attempting to deal with undigested cargo (e.g., lipids and apoptotic bodies) due to the Lysosomal dysfunction. Then, hASCs facilitate the efficient degradation of lysosomal cargo, relieving lysosomal stress and returning lysosomal to baseline levels [22, 36, 37]. While previous studies have highlighted the role of MSCs in anti-inflammatory polarization, our work provides new insights into the potential upstream events: the restoration of efferocytosis is strongly associated with this phenotypic shift. The dose-dependent efficacy of hASCs, particularly in reducing fibrosis and proteinuria, underscores their clinical potential.

Notably, although our findings confirm that the enhancement of efferocytosis mediated by hASCs may be a key mechanism in improving DKD, their therapeutic effects are not limited to this pathway alone. Previous studies indicate that MSCs can home to pancreatic islets via hematopoietic niche mechanisms and restore β-cell mass in type 2 diabetic models. These collective observations suggest that the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of MSCs comprises multiple synergistic mechanisms [38, 39]. Meanwhile, Extensive research indicates that the apoptotic bodies, extracellular vesicles, and exosomes secreted by these cells also exhibit significant therapeutic effects [28, 40–42].

Our study provides the evidence linking MSCs therapy, Diabetic Kidney Disease progression, and macrophage efferocytosis. We demonstrate that hASCs may enhance the clearance of apoptotic cells by modulating efferocytosis signalling pathways via multiple targets, thereby providing new mechanistic insights and potential therapeutic strategies for the management of DKD. Although our work validates hASCs-mediated efferocytosis enhancement at multiple regulatory levels, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the specific secretome components (such as specific exosomes, miRNAs, or cytokines) initiating macrophage phagocytosis remain unidentified. Second, Although this study provides compelling evidence for the association between enhanced efferocytosis clearance and renal protection, the absence of specific knockout or overexpression experiments in efferocytosis clearance pathways — such as RAC1, ELMO1, LAMP1, PPAR-γ, and ABCA1 — prevents further validation and identification of core proteins essential for macrophage efferocytosis clearance. Finally, although the db/db mouse model mimics many features of human DKD, it lacks the full spectrum of advanced human renal pathology, such as advanced glomerulosclerosis. Therefore, the translation of these results to clinical settings requires caution.

Conclusion

We found that impaired macrophage phagocytosis may be a key pathogenic event in diabetic nephropathy. hASC therapy significantly ameliorates / rescues this defect, which contributes to / correlates with the improvement of renal pathology in db/db mice. Crucially, the mechanism by which hASCs ameliorate DKD extends beyond general immunomodulation to involve the comprehensive enhancement of macrophage efferocytosis function, associated with the modulation of multiple target pathways.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

This study involved three cell lines: hASCs (identification report available in hASCs Expert Report) sourced from Cyagen (Guangzhou) Biotechnology Co., Ltd., mouse macrophages from the School of Pharmacy, Zhejiang University of Chinese Medicine, and Conditionally immortalized mouse podocytes (MPC5) from the School of Pharmacy, Zhejiang University of Chinese Medicine [43]. hASCs were cultured in MSCs-specific mediu, Macrophages and MPC5 were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, 25 mM glucose, Gibco, CN). (To maintain their self-renewal and osteogenic potential, hASCs from the 3rd to 5th passages were used in all experiments). Mouse macrophages and mouse podocytes were cultured in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, CN) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, CN). All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 [44]. All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma and underwent STR analysis.

Cell Co-culture and treatment

A Transwell system (PET membrane, 24 mm, 8.0 μm pore size) (NEST, CN) was used to co-culture hASCs, renal podocytes, and macrophages, modeling the efferocytosis process in vitro. Podocytes were first exposed to 55 mM glucose in DMEM for 48 h to mimic a hyperglycemic renal microenvironment and induce apoptosis [45]. After one PBS wash, these podocytes were co-seeded with normoglycemic-cultured macrophages in the lower chamber at a 5:1 ratio. The lower chamber was then maintained under high- or low-glucose conditions according to experimental groups, while the upper chamber contained either hASCs-specific medium or hASCs themselves, the number of hASCs is consistent with the number of macrophages(Grouping: NG, NG+hASCs, HG, HG+hASCs, the schematic diagram is shown in Fig. S2).

To evaluate the direct association between human Adipose-Derived Mesenchymal Stem Cell intervention and macrophage phagocytosis, GW9662 (a PPAR-γ inhibitor) was selected for validation experiments based on preliminary research findings. After pre-treating macrophages with 5 µM GW9662 for 2 h, all subsequent cell treatments and procedures were identical to the previous protocol [46]. The experiment was repeated with the following groups: NG, HG, HG+hASCs, and HG+hASCs+GW9662.

Flow cytometry

After co-culture, collect the bottom chamber cells from each group. Wash the cells three times with PBS and resuspend them in a cell suspension at 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. Cell were harvested and stained with antibodies against CD86-PE-Cy7 (pro-inflammation marker), CD206-Alexa Fluor 647 (anti-inflammatory marker), and F4/80-PE (BD, US). Apoptosis was evaluated using an Annexin V-FITC/PI kit (Vazyme, CN). Data were acquired on a flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software.

CCK8

Cell viability was assessed using a CCK-8 assay kit (Biosharp, CN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, MPC-5 cells were cultured in 96-well plates at a density of 5000 per well for 48 h. After the corresponding treatments, 10 µL CCK-8 detection reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h. A microplate reader was used to measure the absorbance at 450 nm wavelength to determine the cell viability [47].

Immunofluorescence

In the co-culture system, cells from the lower chamber were seeded onto coverslips in 24-well plates. After 24 h, they were washed three times with PBS (5 min per wash). The coverslips were then carefully removed (cells facing upward) and fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min. Following fixation, samples were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 5 min, then blocked with 10% goat serum for 1 h. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C. After PBS washing, samples were incubated with HRP-labeled secondary antibodies (HuaBio, CN) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. After another PBS wash, cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI [48]. Cover slips were sealed with anti-fluorescence quenching medium. Final imaging was performed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus Fluoview FV 1000, US).

Animal experiments

A total of 50 male mice were used in this study: 10 healthy db/m mice and 40 diabetic db/db mice (all aged 5 weeks; Cyagen (Suzhou) Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). The sample size was determined based on common practice (10 mice per group) without formal power calculation. All procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhejiang University of Chinese Medicine (IACUC-202501-16). Mice were housed under standard conditions (20 ± 2 °C, 45–55% humidity, 12-hour light-dark cycle) with free access to food and water.

At 14 weeks of age, db/m mice were assigned to the Control group . db/db mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 10/group) using a random number table: Model (untreated diabetes), hASCs-L (low-dose, 2.5 × 10⁵ cells/mouse), hASCs-M (medium-dose, 5 × 10⁵ cells/mouse), and hASCs-H (high-dose, 1 × 10⁶ cells/mouse). Mice received weekly intravenous injections of hASCs or PBS (Biosharp, CN) (Control and Model groups) for 4 weeks under SPF conditions.

Body weight and fasting blood glucose were measured weekly. After 4 weeks, 24-hour urine samples were collected using metabolic cages for biochemical analysis. Blood samples were concurrently collected for serum analysis. Urine samples and Blood samples after being treated with other reagents, GSP, BUN, CREA, INS, U-BUN, U-CREA and GLU of mouse were detected according to the manufacturer’s protocol by a commercial kit. Following all measurements, mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation for subsequent tissue collection. All experiments were conducted using randomly selected samples. Following all measurements, mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation for subsequent tissue collection. Euthanasia is carried out in accordance with the 2020 guidelines of the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA). All experiments were conducted using randomly selected samples. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

However, throughout the study, outcome assessors and data analysts remained blinded to group allocations, with the exception of the operators administering the interventions.

Fluorescent in vivo and ex vivo imaging

To track the in vivo distribution and kinetics of hASCs, the cells were labeled with a V-22,887 membrane dye according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The labeled cells were resuspended in PBS at the desired concentration and administered to mice via tail vein injection [49]. Whole-body fluorescence imaging was performed at 0, 1, 3, and 7 days post-injection. On day 7, major organs were harvested for ex vivo imaging. Fluorescence signals were quantified using the IVIS® Spectra System and Living Image Software (PerkinElmer, US).

During in vivo imaging procedures, anesthesia was administered in accordance with AVMA guidelines (2020). Induction was achieved with 3%–5% isoflurane, followed by maintenance at 1.5% – 2.5%, with the concentration adjusted as needed based on the respiratory rate and reflex responses of the mice.

Antibodies and reagents

All reagents and primary antibodies (including those for Western blotting, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence) are listed in the Supplementary Table.

Tissue injury, glycogen, fibrosis, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence staining

Mouse were sacrificed and the kidneys were collection. The specimens were immediately isolated and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Then they were dehydrated using graded alcohols, followed by clearing with xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax for preparation of subsequent 4 μm sections. Subsequently, the slides were routine deparaffinization and rehydration. Finally, they were proceeded to histological staining with hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E), Periodic acid -Schiff (PAS), and Masson. IFTA was scored as previously described: 0 = no glomerular/tubular damage, 1 < 25% of the glomerular/tubular area was affected, 2 = 25% – 50% of the glomerular/tubular area was affected, and 3 > 50% of the glomerular/tubular area was affected.

After deparaffinization and rehydration, the sections were washed with PBS for 3 times. Then they were blotted with 10% goat serum for 15 min at room temperature. Subsequently, immunofluorescence analysis of in the kidney tissue was performed using the primary antibody to incubate for 12 h at 4 °C. After washing, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies and diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate. The sections were analyzed with a Leica microscope.

After deparaffinization and rehydration, the sections were washed with PBS for 3 times. Then they were blotted with 10% goat serum for 15 min at room temperature. Subsequently, immunofluorescence analysis of in the kidney tissue was performed using the primary antibody to incubate for 12 h at 4 °C. After pre-cooled washing with PBS for three washes, the secondary antibodies were applied for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, nuclei were stained with DAPI for 5 min and image acquisition is carried out by a fluorescence microscope (Olympus Fluoview FV 1000, US) and quantified using ImageJ software.

Transmission electron microscopy(TEM)

The mouse kidney tissues were fixed by the 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution for 2–4 h at 4 °C. Then they were washed in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) 3 times for 15 min each time. After fixation, we used a graded series of ethanol to dehydrate the samples and eventually dehydrated by absolute acetone. Subsequently, absolute acetone and the final epoxy resin mixture were used to infiltrate the samples. Then the samples were embedded, ultrathin sectioned, and stained. After the above processes, images were captured via a TEM [50]. Morphometric Analysis Quantitative analysis of Glomerular Basement Membrane (GBM) and podocyte foot process was performed using ImageJ software [51, 52].

Single-cell public data analysis

To investigate the cellular pathogenesis of DKD, we integrated four single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets from the GEO database (GSE131882, GSE195460, GSE131685, GSE151302), comprising five DKD and nine normal control (NC) human kidney samples [17]. After quality control to exclude cells with high mitochondrial gene content (> 15%) and data normalization, the “Harmony” algorithm was used for batch correction and dataset integration. We performed principal component analysis and UMAP clustering using the top 2000 highly variable genes.

RNA-sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from kidney tissues, and RNA integrity and quantity were assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Library preparation involved RNA denaturation and enrichment, RNA fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, end repair of cDNA fragments, PCR amplification, purification of PCR products, and cDNA library construction. The Illumina DNBseq platform (HiSeq 2500) was utilized for sequencing the cDNA library. Raw reads containing sequencing adapters, low-quality reads, and reads with an unknown base ratio exceeding 5% were filtered out using SOAPnuke software (v1.5.2) to generate clean reads. DESeq2 was applied for differential expression analysis, and the differential expression genes were identified with a threshold of |log2 (fold change)| ≥ 1.5 and a p value < 0.05. Subsequently, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses of differentially expressed genes were carried out using R software (v4.3.1). Library preparation and Illumina sequencing were conducted at Novogene Biotechnology Co., Ltd., (Shenzhen, CN).

TUNEL and immunofluorescence staining of tissue sections

Paraffin-embedded kidney tissue sections were dewaxed, rehydrated were subjected to TUNEL staining (Proteintech, CN) by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark, followed by three PBS washes. Sections were blocked with 10% goat serum (1 h) to minimize nonspecific binding and then incubated overnight with primary antibody. After washing, sections were incubated with a fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h in the dark, counterstained with DAPI, and mounted with an antifade medium. Images were acquired using a Leica fluorescence microscope.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay(ELISA)

The levels of VEGF, EGF, GSH, MDA, TNF-α, IL-10, TGF-β1, IL-1β and LPC in kidney samples were quantified using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits, in accordance with the provided protocols.

Western blotting

Cell or tissue lysates were prepared in RIPA buffer containing 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitors, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 ×g for 10 min. Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA kit (Epizyme, CN). Samples (20–40 µg) were separated via SDS-PAGE, transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Cytiva, US), and blocked with 5% BSA for 2 h. The membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody, then with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. The immunoreactive protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (epizyme.CN) and analyzed using ImageJ software. Blots were imaged using a chemiluminescence system (ShenhuaBio, CN).

Statistical analysis

Graphical representations were created using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (Dotmatics). All results are expressed as the mean ± SEM. The differences among multiple groups were compared by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.
