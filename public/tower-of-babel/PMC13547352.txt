Authors: Pegah Tagheie Karaji, Nemat Nyamoradi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this paper, we study an SIR model with a nonlinear incidence rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\beta _0 e^{-\mu } SI}{1+\alpha I}$$\end{document}, which incorporates both a saturated incidence term and the exponential factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e^{-\mu }$$\end{document}, where μ denotes the mask-wearing rate. We derive the equilibria of the system and compute the basic reproduction number \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_0$$\end{document}. We also investigate the local and global stability of the model equilibria. To account for environmental and behavioral variability, bounded multiplicative random perturbations are introduced into the transmission parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _0$$\end{document}, and the resulting model is examined numerically under different perturbation intensities. In addition, we analyze the sensitivity of the basic reproduction number \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_0$$\end{document}. Furthermore, Pontryagin’s maximum principle is employed to determine the optimal levels of two control strategies, namely vaccination and treatment. Finally, numerical simulations are performed to illustrate the theoretical findings and support the conclusions.

Introduction

Infectious diseases remain one of the most significant challenges in public health, and accurately predicting their behavior is essential for designing effective interventions. Mathematical epidemiological models, particularly the classical SIR, SEIR, SIS model, serve as powerful tools for analyzing disease transmission dynamics and evaluating different control strategies1–11. The SIR model with vital dynamics is described by the following system1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \frac{dS}{dt}=\Lambda -\beta S(t)I(t)-m S(t),\\ \frac{dI}{dt}=\beta S(t)I(t)-(\gamma +m)I(t),\\ \frac{dR}{dt}=\gamma I(t)-m R(t),\\ \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where S(t), I(t), and R(t) denote the densities of susceptible, infected, and recovered individuals at time t, respectively. The susceptible population is generated at a rate Λ, while γ represents the recovery rate. The parameter m corresponds to the natural death rate affecting the susceptible, infected, and recovered classes, and β denotes the transmission rate of the disease. Social behaviors can significantly influence the trajectory of a disease outbreak or its eventual eradication. When individuals in a community adopt preventive measures such as wearing masks or practicing social distancing, the spread of the disease decreases noticeably. In many epidemic models, authors considered the saturated incidence rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\beta SI}{(1+\alpha I)}$$\end{document}12–15. The term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{1}{1+\alpha I}$$\end{document} represents these preventive behaviors within the society. As the number of infected individuals rises, people modify their behavior and strictly adhere to health protocols, such as self-isolation or social distancing. Consequently, even with a high number of infected individuals, the frequency of effective contacts leading to disease transmission does not grow linearly; instead, it becomes limited or, in other words, saturates. It is evident that as the prevalence of mask-wearing increases, the transmission rate decreases, and vice versa16,17. Inspired by these findings, we considered mask use through the factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e^{-\mu }$$\end{document}, which acts as a moderator reducing the transmission rate. which acts as a moderator reducing the transmission rate. Although different functional forms could be used to represent the protective effect of masks, the exponential form was chosen because it remains positive, decreases with increasing mask adherence, and provides a simple phenomenological description of the cumulative reduction in transmission. Although the SIR model is a fundamental framework for studying the dynamics of infectious diseases, its deterministic form cannot capture real-world uncertainties. For example, natural factors such as sudden changes in social behavior, population movements, climate change, or non-compliance with health protocols may constantly and randomly disrupt disease transmission, causing the contact rate to fluctuate. Bounded multiplicative random perturbations reflects these rapid and unpredictable environmental and social fluctuations in the model transmission rate. Therefore, the authors have investigated models18–22 incorporating perturbations to describe fluctuations that significantly influence the system dynamics.

Also, some authors in23–27 have applied the optimal control strategy to minimize the number of infected individuals and the costs of the disease simultaneously and have found that the optimal combination of two interventions: vaccination of the susceptible population and interventional treatment of the infected population can reduce the peak of infection and control the epidemic faster. Ultimately, optimal control theory provides a systematic method for designing effective and cost-effective intervention strategies.

Additionally, we can employ a logistic growth model for the susceptible population. The logistic term bS(1-S/K) is introduced as an effective bounded recruitment mechanism for the susceptible population. Although human populations do not strictly exhibit logistic growth during short-term epidemic outbreaks, such logistic-type recruitment assumptions are widely used in epidemiological modeling to represent bounded population dynamics and saturation effects and to prevent unrealistic unbounded population growth28,29. In the present work, K denotes the environmental carrying capacity of the susceptible population. In the context of a human epidemic, this parameter should be interpreted in a broader sense than its classical ecological meaning. Specifically, K represents the effective maximum population level that can be sustained under social, environmental, and resource-related constraints. Thus, K provides a natural upper bound for the susceptible population and contributes to maintaining biologically meaningful bounded dynamics. This approach accounts for the fact that the susceptible population relies on finite resources and does not exhibit unbounded exponential growth. The term (1 - S/K) represents the density-dependent inhibitory effect. When S is small, S/K approaches zero, leading to rapid exponential growth at rate bS. Conversely, as S approaches K, the term (1 - S/K) tends toward zero, indicating that there is no remaining space or resources for new individuals, which severely constrains the growth rate of the susceptible population. Although saturated incidence functions, epidemic models with perturbations, and optimal control strategies have been extensively studied in the literature, most existing works consider these mechanisms separately. In particular, nonlinear incidence structures and healthcare constraints have been incorporated in epidemic models, emphasizing the role of limited resources in disease dynamics19. However, few studies integrate behavioral factors such as mask-wearing, transmission variability, and optimal intervention strategies within a unified framework. In this work, an SIR model with logistic growth, a saturated incidence rate, and a mask-dependent transmission coefficient is proposed and analyzed. In addition, bounded multiplicative random perturbations are introduced into the transmission rate to represent environmental and social uncertainties. The model is further complemented by sensitivity analysis and an optimal control problem involving vaccination and treatment strategies. This unified framework allows a comprehensive investigation of how preventive behavior, perturbations, and control measures jointly influence disease dynamics. Bounded multiplicative random perturbations and qualitative behavior

When analyzing epidemic models with oscillatory behavior, random effects are introduced to account for environmental variability in the transmission process. Following the approach in31, bounded multiplicative random perturbations are incorporated only into the transmission rate parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _0$$\end{document}, while all other parameters remain deterministic. Specifically, the perturbed transmission rate is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _0(t) = \beta _0 \cdot \eta (t)$$\end{document}, where η (t) represents instantaneous bounded random fluctuations of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _0$$\end{document} at time t, and\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \eta (t) \sim \mathcal {U}[1-\eta _{\max },\,1+\eta _{\max }], \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The resulting system is simulated using an explicit Euler scheme with time step dt, leading to a discrete-time perturbed dynamical system in which only the transmission rate is affected by random variations. The proposed discrete-time randomly perturbed system preserves biological feasibility under non-negative initial conditions. Since all state variables represent population densities and the incidence terms remain non-negative for biologically relevant parameter values, the solutions remain well-defined in the positive region. To ensure numerical feasibility, any negative values generated during the Euler iterations are projected onto zero at each time step, guaranteeing that all state variables remain biologically meaningful throughout the simulations. Furthermore, although only the transmission rate is subject to bounded multiplicative random perturbations, the perturbations intensity is restricted within \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[1-\eta _{\max },\,1+\eta _{\max }]$$\end{document}, ensuring that the system trajectories remain bounded for all considered parameter values and simulation times. While rigorous analytical results on long-term behavior under random perturbations are beyond the scope of this work due to the discrete and randomly perturbed nature of the system, numerical simulations indicate that the qualitative dynamics of the deterministic model are preserved. In particular, disease-free and endemic states persist under perturbations, with solutions exhibiting bounded fluctuations around the deterministic trajectories.

The term I(T) is considered a terminal cost, as reducing the number of infected individuals at the conclusion of the control program, compared to reducing it at each time unit, appears to be a more realistic and economical approach. This approach is economically more justifiable when the minimum number of infected individuals achieved at the terminal time is sufficient to eradicate the disease even without continued interventions. We set15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \zeta =\lbrace (u_1(t), u_2(t)) \quad | \quad 0 \le u_1 \le {u_1}_{max} \le 1, 0 \le u_2 \le {u_2}_{max} \le 1, t \in [0, T] \rbrace . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The total vaccination expenses can include the price of the vaccine, cold storage costs, and other operational expenditures. Treatment-related costs may encompass medical diagnosis expenses, the cost of pharmaceuticals, and inpatient costs at healthcare facilities32,33. Assuming that costs follow a nonlinear pattern, we utilized a quadratic model to estimate the cost function. Using Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle, the optimal controls are obtained in terms of the state and adjoint variables and are restricted by upper and lower bounds. The terms \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_1 u_1^2$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_2 u_2^2$$\end{document} represent the cost of applying each control over time. The adjoint system has terminal conditions due to the presence of a terminal cost in the objective function. Finally, the optimality system consists of the state equations, adjoint equations, and the optimal control expressions, which together determine the best control strategy.

Our objective is to find an optimal strategic control \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(u^*_1, u^* _2) \in \zeta$$\end{document} that leads to the minimization of both vaccination and treatment costs throughout the specified time period, as well as the minimization of the number of infected individuals at the terminal time.

Discussion

The results obtained in this study provide qualitative insight into the transmission dynamics of infectious diseases under preventive measures, bounded multiplicative random perturbations and optimal control interventions. The analytical results show that the basic reproduction number \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_0$$\end{document} acts as a threshold parameter governing disease eradication or persistence. In addition, the numerical simulations indicate that increasing mask-wearing compliance reduces disease transmission, while early vaccination strategies are generally more effective than treatment-dominated interventions in reducing the infected population.

The numerical investigations further demonstrate that bounded multiplicative random perturbations in the transmission rate may influence the epidemic trajectories and lead to fluctuations around the corresponding deterministic solutions. However, the effects of these perturbations are assessed only through numerical simulations. No analytical results concerning persistence, extinction thresholds, invariant distributions, or long-term stability under perturbations are established in the present work. Therefore, conclusions regarding perturbation effects should be interpreted within the scope of the numerical experiments.

It should also be noted that the uncertainty assessment is based solely on local sensitivity analysis. Although this approach identifies the relative influence of model parameters on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_0$$\end{document}, more comprehensive global uncertainty quantification techniques, such as Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS), Partial Rank Correlation Coefficients (PRCC), and Sobol sensitivity analysis, may provide a broader assessment of parameter uncertainty.

Furthermore, the study is primarily theoretical in nature. The parameter values were selected based on biological considerations and information available in the literature, but the model was not calibrated or validated using epidemiological data. Consequently, the model is intended to provide qualitative understanding of disease dynamics rather than predictive epidemiological forecasts.

Future work may focus on incorporating population heterogeneity, age structure, spatial effects, and healthcare capacity limitations, as well as performing data-driven parameter estimation and validation to improve the realism and applicability of the model.

Conclusion

In this paper, we investigated an SIR model with a nonlinear incidence rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\beta _0e^{-\mu }SI}{1+\alpha I}$$\end{document} which incorporates both a saturated incidence term and the exponential factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e^{-\mu }$$\end{document}, where μ represents the mask-wearing rate. We derived the equilibria of the system and calculated the basic reproduction number \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_0$$\end{document}. We investigated the local and global stability of the model equilibria. Bounded multiplicative random perturbations were introduced into the transmission rate parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _0$$\end{document}, and the system dynamics were studied under different perturbation intensities. It should be emphasized that the effect of bounded multiplicative random perturbations is investigated only through numerical simulations. Additionally, the sensitivity index of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_0$$\end{document} was evaluated. The uncertainty analysis in this work is based exclusively on local sensitivity methods. To determine optimal intervention strategies, we applied Pontryagin’s maximum principle to characterize the optimal levels of two control variables: vaccination and treatment. Our results in Figs. 8b, 9b and 10b indicate that when the vaccination control weight is greater than the treatment control weight, more treatment is applied and vice versa. When the two weights are equal, a larger amount of vaccination control should be applied initially to reduce the susceptible population below a certain threshold, after which treatment control gradually becomes more dominant while vaccination decreases. The results in Figs. 8a, 9a and 10a show that when the cost of treatment control is lower and treatment is applied more intensively, the peak of the infectious population reaches approximately 23. In contrast, when the cost of vaccination control is lower and vaccination is used more intensively, the infectious population peak reduces to approximately 18. Overall, our findings highlight that, within this model, applying vaccination control from the onset of the outbreak is more effective and reasonable for reducing infection levels. From an epidemiological perspective, the results emphasize the importance of preventive measures in controlling disease transmission.

In particular, increasing mask-wearing compliance reduces the effective transmission rate and consequently decreases the spread of infection. Furthermore, the sensitivity analysis identifies the parameters that most strongly influence the basic reproduction number, providing useful information for the design of effective disease-control strategies. The numerical results also demonstrate that early vaccination is generally more effective than treatment-dominated interventions in reducing the epidemic burden.

The present study has several limitations. The perturbation effects were investigated only through numerical simulations. Moreover, the uncertainty analysis was restricted to local sensitivity methods, and the model was not calibrated using epidemiological data. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as qualitative insights into disease dynamics rather than predictive epidemiological outcomes.
