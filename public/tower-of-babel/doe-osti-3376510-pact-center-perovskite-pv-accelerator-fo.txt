Executive Summary
The Perovskite PV Accelerator for Commercializing Technologies (PACT) center was
established in July 2021 as a national resource to accelerate the commercialization of
perovskite photovoltaic (PV) technology in the United States. Since its inception, PACT
has been led by Sandia National Laboratories (Sandia) in partnership with the National
Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR), formerly known as NREL. From FY20-FY23, Los
Alamos National Laboratory (LANL), CFV Labs, Black & Veatch (B&V), and the Electric
Power Research Institute (EPRI) were part of the project team. LANL brought expertise
in perovskite PV device designs and processing, CFV Labs (now GroundWork
Renewables) provided initial indoor and outdoor measurement hardware technology,
B&V led the initial effort on perovskite PV bankability, and EPRI worked on reviewing
testing standards, identifying commercialization gaps, and helping to run PACT’s Industry
Advisory Board, a group including representatives from commercial testing labs,
independent engineering firms, insurance companies, state regulators, and electric
utilities. To source perovskite PV module samples, PACT contracted with the University
of North Carolina (UNC), the University of Toledo, the University of Washington, and
SLAC/Stanford University to provide a steady stream of research-grade perovskite mini
modules, enabling protocol development in advance of commercial module availability.
The project period ran from July 1, 2021, through December 31, 2025, including a No-
Cost Extension. Starting in FY25, the project was continued as a Core Capability in the
Lab Call portfolio and continues at a reduced budget with only Sandia and NLR as funded
recipients. Notably, starting in FY25 PACT expanded its scope beyond MHP modules to
accept all emerging PV mini module technologies for testing, including organic PV (OPV)
and all-thin-film tandems, with the aim of supporting commercialization across the broader
emerging PV ecosystem. With this change in scope the program was renamed the PV
Accelerator for Commercializing Technologies, dropping perovskite from the name.
Perovskite PV has emerged as one of the most promising next-generation solar
technologies, with certified cell efficiencies surpassing 26% and rapid advances in silicon-
perovskite tandem architectures. However, significant uncertainty regarding long-term
outdoor durability represented a critical barrier to commercial deployment and project
financing. The PACT center directly addressed these gaps by providing a test bed for
field testing of perovskite PV modules, accelerated qualification testing protocols, and a
suite of analytical tools to understand degradation modes in perovskite modules under
realistic field conditions.
Key accomplishments of the PACT center over the project period include:
• Established a comprehensive multi-site outdoor field-testing program at Sandia
 (Albuquerque, NM) and NLR (Golden, CO) deploying perovskite mini modules on both
 two-axis trackers and fixed-tilt racks under real-world climatic conditions.

 modules (458 from five universities and three research centers & 353 from nine
 different companies, six of which are based in the U.S.)

Background
Metal halide perovskite (MHP) photovoltaics have undergone an extraordinary rise from
laboratory curiosity to a contender for next-generation solar module technology. Since
their initial demonstration as PV absorbers in 2009, certified perovskite cell efficiencies
have climbed from 3.8% to almost 28% in 2025 (~0.5 cm2), a pace of improvement
unmatched in the history of photovoltaics. The unique combination of high absorption
coefficient, long carrier diffusion lengths, bandgap tunability, and solution processability
has fueled intense global research interest and substantial commercial investment in
perovskite technology, both as standalone single-junction devices and as the top cell in
silicon-perovskite tandem architectures that have already exceeded 34% certified
efficiency (~1 cm2) [1, 2].
Despite remarkable cell-level progress, the translation of high-efficiency laboratory cells
to durable, commercially deployable modules has proven significantly more challenging.
A fundamental gap exists between the performance of perovskite cells tested on a
timescale of hours to days in a laboratory and the 25–30-year product lifetime expected
for commercial deployment. While substantial improvements in material stability have
been achieved through compositional engineering, interface passivation, and
encapsulation strategies [3, 4], the outdoor operational lifetime of perovskite modules
remains poorly characterized and a significant concern for investors and project
developers.
When PACT started, in addition to the lack of field demonstration, a critical gap was the
absence of standardized methods for measuring and reporting perovskite module field
performance and for conducting qualification testing. The metastable nature of perovskite
devices, which displays as performance changes over time in response to illumination
history, temperature, and electrical bias, creates fundamental measurement challenges
not addressed by existing standards such as IEC 61215. Ion migration within the
perovskite layer can produce hysteresis in current-voltage (J-V) characteristics and time-
dependent shifts in the maximum power point that can easily produce inaccurate power
ratings when standard silicon-based measurement protocols are applied without
modification [5-7]. Furthermore, no standardized preconditioning protocol exists to define
a reproducible "stable state" for perovskite modules prior to performance measurement,
which is a prerequisite for any accurate before-and-after comparison after accelerated
stress testing.
Outdoor performance characterization of perovskite modules prior to PACT was limited
to a handful of academic studies, most reporting rapid degradation within weeks to
months of field deployment. Khenkin et al. [8] and the International Summit on Organic
Photovoltaic Stability (ISOS) community established consensus protocols for stability
reporting but noted wide variability in reported lifetimes and difficulty in comparing results
across different packaging strategies and environments. Cheacharoen et al. [9] and

 8

others demonstrated the importance of encapsulation quality for outdoor stability, but the
field lacked systematic multi-site, multi-module studies with consistent measurement
protocols and transparent reporting.
The bankability of perovskite PV (its ability to attract commercial project financing)
requires not only technical performance data but also standardized qualification testing
and independent third-party assessment from credible testing organizations. The
absence of bankability assessment frameworks specific to perovskite PV, combined with
the lack of a long operating track record, represents a major barrier to early market entry.
Existing qualification standards (IEC 61215, IEC 62804, IEC 61730) were developed for
mature silicon and CdTe technologies and require substantial adaptation before they can
be meaningfully applied to perovskite modules.
The PACT center was conceived precisely to address these gaps. Building on the model
of successful DOE-funded validation centers for emerging PV technologies, PACT
assembled a team with complementary expertise spanning PV characterization, outdoor
testing, accelerated stress testing, failure analysis, performance modeling, and
bankability assessment. By developing and sharing open-access protocols, conducting
multi-year field testing campaigns, and engaging directly with U.S. and foreign industry
and international standards bodies, PACT aimed to catalyze the U.S. perovskite PV
industry's transition from laboratory to market.

Introduction
The PACT center operated under a Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO) organized
around five primary tasks. The center began operations on July 1, 2021 under DOE Award
DE-EE0038050, with Sandia National Laboratories serving as the prime recipient. NLR
was awarded under DOE Award DE-EE0038049 and served as an equal partner in the
project. Los Alamos National Laboratory was awarded under DOE Award DE-EE0038355
and worked primarily with Sandia to review cell-to-module losses and investigate field
performance from MHP modules. The original project period ran through June 30, 2025;
the project period was revised in FY24 to end September 30, 2024, and subsequently a
No-Cost Extension was approved through December 31, 2025. The project team
comprised Sandia (prime), NLR, LANL (removed from project in mid-project
restructuring), CFV Labs, Black & Veatch, EPRI, and four university subcontractors
(University of North Carolina, University of Toledo, University of Washington, and
SLAC/Stanford University).

Task 1: Program Management and Outreach
Task 1 covered the administrative and communications infrastructure of the PACT center,
including: website development (https://pvpact.sandia.gov) and communications
(Subtask 1.1); subcontract management (1.2); development and revision of module
acceptance criteria (https://pvpact.sandia.gov/download/863) (1.3); DOE reporting and
annual planning (1.4); Industry Advisory Board management (1.5); perovskite industry
outreach (1.6); and data management (https://pvpact.sandia.gov/download/747) (1.7).
Annual workshops, webinars, and participation in national and international conferences
were recurring activities (https://pvpact.sandia.gov/events/).

Task 2: Test Capabilities and Field Test Campaigns
Task 2 focused on establishing the physical infrastructure for field testing and managing
the module sample pipeline. This included development of module handling and
packaging protocols (https://pvpact.sandia.gov/download/892), design and fabrication of
custom load-and-measurement hardware for outdoor MPPT and IV tracing,
commissioning of field sites at Sandia (two-axis tracker and fixed tilt), NLR (fixed tilt), and
CFV Labs (fixed tilt), and management of module samples from university and industry
partners.

Task 3: Performance Testing
Task 3 addressed the metrology challenges of characterizing perovskite module power
output and energy production. Subtasks covered: dark storage and preconditioning
protocols (3.1); power rating using continuous solar simulators (3.2); flash simulator
characterization (3.3); and outdoor performance characterization and modeling (3.4).

 10

Task 4: Reliability and Qualification Testing
Task 4 aimed to develop accelerated stress testing protocols to provide information about
early failures and gradual degradation faster than field testing alone. Subtasks included:
definition of accelerated testing conditions (4.1); power loss testing (4.2); failure mode
characterization (4.3); fault tree analysis (4.4); and PVQAT engagement for standards
development (4.5).

Task 5: Bankability and Stakeholder Engagement
Task 5, led by Black & Veatch, implemented a three-tiered bankability program: Tier 1
(education and outreach), Tier 2 (customized bankability road mapping), and Tier 3 (full
bankability assessments). Broader stakeholder engagement including workshops and
webinars was also covered in this task. The bankability task was scaled back and
removed from project scope in FY24, as described in detail in the results section.

Project Results and Discussion
Task 1: Program Management and Outreach

1.1 Team Formation and Partner Management
Sandia placed subcontracts with all major partners within the first budget period, including
CFV Labs for developing monitoring hardware and solar simulator software solutions,
Black & Veatch for bankability program leadership, EPRI for an environmental health and
safety assessment of MHPs and IAB management, and four university partners for
perovskite mini module supply. The team evolved substantially over the project period in
response to technical findings and industry conditions. SLAC/Stanford University was
added in Year 2 as a fourth university partner to increase module compositional diversity.
LANL was removed from the project during the mid-project restructuring, due to the key
staff member at the lab leaving for a university faculty position.
After the first two years of the project, it became clear that the technical readiness level
of perovskite PV modules (between 3-4) was lower than initially anticipated and it was
decided that the existing U.S. companies developing the technology were not yet ready
for the focused bankability support that DOE and PACT had envisioned. In FY24 PACT
scaled back activities so it could focus on outdoor field testing and testing protocol
development. The University of Washington and SLAC/Stanford subcontracts were
closed out. EPRI and Black & Veatch scopes were reduced substantially following the
decision to discontinue the bankability task. UNC and University of Toledo continued as
primary module suppliers through the end of the project with revised contracts to increase
module delivery rates. During the No-Cost Extension, the team focused on finishing
capability buildouts at Sandia and NLR, adding many new outdoor testing channels and
two new large area continuous solar simulators.

1.4 Industry Advisory Board
The PACT IAB was established in Year 1 with 13 members from downstream stakeholder
sectors: PV test labs (2), independent engineers (2), PV plant owners/utilities (2),
financiers and insurers (2), environmental/sustainability specialists (3), and regulators (2).
This composition was deliberately chosen to represent the perspectives of technology
adopters and regulators rather than manufacturers — providing independent feedback on
PACT's activities and the commercial readiness of perovskite PV. IAB meetings were held
regularly throughout the project, initially bimonthly and later revised to quarterly with
alternating themes of reviewing PACT technical progress and discussing the broader

 13

state of the perovskite PV industry. The IAB consistently affirmed the value of Sandia and
NLR's role as trusted, independent sources of testing and validation, a finding that directly
supported the continuation of PACT as a core capability. In recent meetings, the IAB
recommended that PACT consider adding safety assessments for emerging module
technologies, specifically citing concern that existing safety test protocols (e.g., portions
of IEC 61215 and IEC 61730) may be insufficient for mechanically stacked 4-terminal
tandem modules, a configuration being actively developed by several PACT industry
partners.

1.5 Industry Outreach and Engagement
Direct engagement with U.S. perovskite PV module manufacturers was a continuous
activity throughout the project. PACT developed a multi-party NDA and MTA to formalize
the exchange of proprietary module samples and published two sets of module
acceptance criteria (research and industry versions) revised multiple times as technology
evolved. A key outreach deliverable was the report "PV Module Manufacturing and
Testing: Industry Requirements for Perovskite PV Modules," which answered the
frequently asked question of what standard tests are required to commercialize a new PV
module technology, a need that multiple companies identified as high priority. PACT
engaged with at least seven U.S. companies, six of which have sent modules to PACT
for testing.

1.6 Data Management
A comprehensive data management plan was published in Q1 of the project. PACT
leveraged the NLR Perovskite PV Data Hub, which became operational in Year 2,
providing automated daily data transfer from all field sites, common variable naming
conventions, and data quality assurance routines. This infrastructure supports both the
US-MAP and PACT programs and positions both datasets for long-term preservation and
community access. We eventually stopped using the datahub and switched to AWS for
data sharing between NLR and Sandia and Box for data exchange between PACT and
our industry and research partners. Key to the PACT data management is a double-blind
system that ensures that only a small number of people know which modules come from
which providers. During day-to-day operations, the PACT team refer to modules and
module batches using only their PACT ids (e.g., P-0001-03). Later in the project, PACT
adopted common, standardized data file formats for sharing MPP and IV datasets
between labs and partners. PACT also started utilizing Amazon Web Services S3 data
storage buckets to facilitate a unified data analysis platform and started to use a common
set of analysis and plotting scripts. The common data format milestone (Milestone 1.2.2
of the FY25–27 program) was completed on December 31, 2024, six months ahead of its
June 2025 due date, which enabled the development of common reporting and plotting
tools such as the “bubble” T80 plots and “bluebar” daily efficiency plots early, too.

Task 2: Test Capabilities and Field Test Campaigns

2.1 Module Sample Pipeline
A foundational challenge was securing a reliable supply of perovskite mini modules prior
to commercial module availability from industry. PACT contracted with four universities to
supply modules over the first three years of the project. Most university modules followed
a standardized 150 mm × 150 mm glass/glass package based on the procedure by
Gaulding et al. [4] using a desiccated PIB edge seal. Early batches used a desiccant-only
package without bulk polymer encapsulant; later batches transitioned to full polymer
encapsulation based on PACT's early field performance results.
As of December 31, 2025, PACT had received and characterized a total of 811 MHP
modules (458 from five universities and three research centers & 353 from nine different
companies, six of which are based in the U.S.

2.2 Outdoor Field Testing Infrastructure
PACT initially established three complementary outdoor field test sites:
Sandia National Laboratories (Albuquerque, NM)
A dedicated two-axis solar tracker was commissioned for PACT testing, originally
configured with 16-channel uMPPT load-and-measurement boxes designed by CFV
Labs. The tracker provides maximum irradiance stress throughout the day and is more
stressful than fixed-tilt deployment. Additional fixed-tilt racking was also available for
industry module testing. Sandia fielded both university and industry mini modules
throughout this initial period. The uMPPT load-and-measurement boxes lasted about two
years in the field before experiencing performance and reliability issues. In addition,
PACT started to receive larger modules that exceeded the electrical range of the
uMPPTs. Sandia decided to replace the uMPPT boards with commercial off-the-shelf
programable loads from BK Precision. These loads come in a wide electrical range that
can accommodate very small to full-sized modules. Three load models are used: two
high-voltage models (500 V, 30 A, 500 W) and (500 V 15A, 300W) and a lower-voltage
model (80 V, 20 A, 250 W), accommodating modules from minimodule to full-sized
formats. The system was commissioned on March 1, 2025, controlled by custom
LabVIEW software. By December 31, 2025, Sandia had built 40 channels on two two-
axis trackers and 56 channels on fixed-tilt racks (Figure 1). The loads configured on
equipment racks are shown in Figure 2.

2.3 University Module Field Test Results
University modules were deployed in cohorts across more than 20 batches over the
project period, representing diverse perovskite compositions, transport layer materials,
and packaging configurations. Field test results varied dramatically — some modules
degraded to near-zero efficiency within days, while others showed relative stability for
months — providing a rich, systematic dataset for understanding the drivers of perovskite
module durability.
A pivotal finding was the critical importance of module packaging architecture. Early
batches using desiccant-only glass/glass packages (no bulk polymer encapsulant)
exhibited rapid degradation, with post-mortem analysis confirming moisture ingress as

 18

the primary cause — evidenced by phase segregation of the perovskite absorber into
lead iodide and delta-phase Cs/FA constituents [4]. In contrast, batch P-0014 (UNC),
packaged with a full PIB polymer encapsulant covering the device area, demonstrated
dramatically superior outdoor performance: T80 of approximately 140 days at NLR — the
longest outdoor lifetime recorded for any university module batch in the PACT program
as of early 2024[10, 11]. Modules from this batch also showed initial efficiency of 16–
17%, among the highest recorded in the PACT field program.

2.4 Industry Module Field Test Results
Obtaining modules from U.S. industry partners proved more challenging and took longer
than originally anticipated. Companies that had indicated readiness to provide modules
in early 2022 experienced delays due to internal development priorities and
manufacturing scale-up challenges. By mid-FY23, PACT began receiving modules from
multiple industry partners, and by the end of FY24Q1, five industry companies had
contributed approximately 54 modules to the program.
Testing of early industry modules revealed performance and degradation characteristics
generally consistent with the university module dataset, with important nuances: (1)
modules utilizing polymer encapsulants generally exhibited longer field lifetimes; (2) two
companies’ modules achieved the milestone of sustaining >12% efficiency for at least 10
weeks in the field; and (3) modules from one company (P-0039) showed slow enough
degradation to enable detailed indoor performance characterization, which was the most
promising early dataset for performance modeling work in Task 3. As more batches were
received from industry, module size, efficiency and reliability all have increased. PACT
has received modules approaching 8,000 cm2 (1245 mm*635 mm), however most
companies are focusing on efficiency and reliability before scaling up module size.

Task 3: Performance Testing and Modeling

3.1 Metastability and the “Wake-Up” Phenomenon
One of the most technically significant findings of the PACT center was a thorough
characterization of metastable behavior in perovskite PV modules and its implications for
performance measurement. Metastability of this nature was not a new discovery for
perovskites but the focus on modules over small cells and the number and variety of MHP
module technologies tested at PACT provides an unprecedented opportunity to observe
a range of metastable behavior. Metastability in perovskite devices, which is driven
primarily by the slow redistribution of mobile ions within the perovskite lattice, produces
time-dependent shifts in power output in response to illumination history, bias voltage,
temperature, and dark storage [6-8].
A particularly important observation was what we term the "wake-up" phenomenon: Some
perovskite modules stored in the dark overnight exhibit a systematically lower open-circuit
voltage (Voc) at the start of the following morning's illumination, which recovers over a
period of minutes to hours before stabilizing.
We ran multiple days of indoor testing on P-0039-12, the earliest batch received by PACT
that lasted long enough to repeat light soaking tests without substantial degradation. We
have exposed this module to repeated cycles of constant irradiance while holding the
temperature of the module fixed. Figure 7 shows the maximum power voltage for several
of these tests. Below are some observations about these tests. The MPP current did not
change significantly during these tests.
 • For all the tests shown, the MPP voltage starts low and increases over time with
 the rate of increase decreasing during the test. The starting voltage appears to
 depend on whether the module has been exposed to light for at least a day before
 the test.
 • The orange curve is for a test (1,000 W/m2 at 25 °C) after the module had been
 stored in the dark for several days. Note it starts off at a lower voltage and almost
 reaches the end voltage shown by the brown and blue curves.
 • Brown and blue curves are for identical tests (1,000 W/m2 at 25 °C) separated by
 about 1 month but each preceded by similar tests run during preceding days.
 • Red and green curves are for identical tests (1,000 W/m2 at 35 °C). The red test
 was one day after the orange test. It appears that the module has not quite
 stabilized compared with the green test, which is preceded by more than a day of
 testing.
 • Purple curve is a test that started at one condition (1,000 W/m2 at 35 °C) and then
 switched at 200 minutes to a lower temperature (1,000 W/m2 at 25 °C). Note that
 the voltage decreases because of decreasing the temperature. This suggests that
 this module has a positive Vmp temperature coefficient. In fact, this is true only for

 Vmp, not Voc. The effect seems to be that only fill factor increases with temperature.
 We are doing more work to elucidate this behavior and will report in future reports.
 • Another pattern to note is the voltage appears to increase (starts fast and slows

3.2 Preconditioning Protocol Development
PACT developed and publicly released a Prototype Preconditioning Protocol (Version
0.1) for perovskite modules, the first standardized approach to bringing a perovskite
module to a defined and reproducible state prior to performance measurement. The
protocol specifies alternating cycles of 8 hours of illumination at 1000 W/m² and 50°C
module temperature, followed by 16 hours of dark storage at room temperature, with
stability defined as ≤5% change in total energy between cycles. The protocol was
intentionally designed with lenient termination criteria, acknowledging that many early-
generation perovskite modules could not withstand the protocol without degrading, an
honest reflection of the current technology readiness level. This early protocol provided a
best first guess at what would be needed for this technology but lacked any substantial
data validation.
Testing of the preconditioning protocol to provide validation of the approach on PACT
mini modules demonstrated that early-generation modules (with no polymer encapsulant)
failed during the protocol itself, confirming that module durability must improve before
standardized preconditioning testing can be routinely applied. The technical basis

 24

document supporting the protocol development (Milestone 3.3.3) was delivered to DOE
on schedule. As we’ve understood the metastabilities present in these modules further,
the team has continued to work towards a new protocol. This work continues in the PACT
core program.

3.3 Performance Model Development
Milestone 3.3.1 was not completed. The aim of this milestone was to develop a perovskite
performance model predicting daily energy output to within ±6% mean absolute error. The
root cause, documented in the milestone 3.3.2 delivery to DOE, was that the module
technology studied had not yet reached sufficient stability to enable reliable, repeatable
indoor characterization as a foundation for model calibration. Modules that degraded
rapidly in the field did not yield a stable performance signal from which meaningful model
parameters could be extracted.
Despite this, significant progress was made toward understanding the physics of
perovskite performance modeling. The brief by Silverman et al. [12] documented diurnal
performance variations in perovskite modules and their relationship to metastable effects,
providing essential groundwork for future model development. LANL contributed optical
simulations for four-terminal (4T) perovskite/silicon tandem configurations, providing
quantitative analysis of current mismatch as a function of incidence angle and perovskite
absorber thickness, with direct implications for energy yield modeling of tandem systems.
The Deceglie et al. (2025) paper [13], based on outdoor testing of over 150 MHP modules
from 14 partners, formally documented the intraday efficiency variations that distinguish
MHP modules from silicon and confirmed that current PV performance models are
inadequate for predicting MHP energy yields, motivating development of time-dependent
models accounting for light exposure history and temperature.
Performance testing protocol development during FY25 included two additional activities:
a round-robin indoor characterization exercise with IMEC (Belgium), and thermal
response testing of industry modules.
Two MHP mini modules and a reference c-Si module were measured at both IMEC and
Sandia using forward and reverse IV scans at 1,000 W/m² and at 25°C and 40°C, with
multiple sessions at each lab on separate days. Reference c-Si results were highly
consistent between labs (Vmp and Imp differences <1.2%; Pmp <0.5%). In contrast, the two
MHP modules showed substantial inter-lab variation attributed to metastable
performance characteristics and small protocol differences between labs; for one module,
percent differences in Pmp ranged from 6.7% (reverse scan at 25°C) to 47% (forward scan
at 25°C).
Thermal response testing was conducted on two MHP modules (batch P-0097) from an
industry partner. Modules were subjected to temperature cycles from 15°C to 75°C under
constant 1,000 W/m² illumination, in three test variants: a single cycle, three consecutive

 25

cycles, and three cycles preceded by a 60-minute preconditioning dwell at 55°C (LT-55).
A reference c-Si module produced well-behaved linear results consistent with published
temperature coefficients, validating the protocol. The MHP module results showed
negative temperature coefficients for all electrical parameters but with significant
hysteresis between heating and cooling legs. The first heating cycle to 75°C appeared to
stabilize metastability in subsequent cycles; however, a 60-minute LT-55 preconditioning
step was not sufficient to replicate this stabilization, indicating that brief high-temperature
exposure may be required to achieve repeatable temperature coefficient measurements
on MHP modules. Figure 8 shows variability of results when a module was exposed to
three consecutive thermal cycles while being MPP tracked. These results highlight the
critical role of metastability management in establishing reliable temperature response
characterization protocols.

Task 4: Reliability and Qualification Testing

4.1 Evolution of Accelerated Testing Approach
Task 4 was the most technically active area of the center and generated the most
significant findings regarding the current state of perovskite module reliability. The initial
approach drew on the standard IEC 61215 and 63209 tests, including damp heat (DH),
UV exposure, and thermal cycling (TC) and tried to adapt them for perovskite technology.
While these tests provided useful information about failure modes, a critical finding was
that no early-generation PACT modules could be classified as "free from major failure"
within the first year of outdoor service. This result, while initially disappointing from a
milestone perspective, is itself an important scientific finding: It quantified, in a rigorous
and reproducible way, the current state of perovskite module durability and provided
unambiguous direction for the highest-priority reliability improvements needed.
The inability to meet Milestone 3.4.1 was thoroughly documented in the Milestone 3.4.2
delivery to DOE. The root cause was straightforward: All modules tested during the initial
years degraded past T80 within one year of field deployment, many much faster. This
precluded identification of a stress test that "passes" a module that also "passes" in the
field, because no modules initially passed in the field. The program appropriately
redirected focus toward identifying the dominant degradation mechanisms and
developing a test that reproduces them at an accelerated rate.

4.2 Light and Elevated Temperature: A Critical Stressor
A pivotal insight that emerged from the program was confirmation that the combination of
light and elevated temperature is the dominant degradation driver for the perovskite
module types tested at PACT and that this combined stressor is not represented in current
IEC qualification test sequences for PV modules. IEC 61215 includes damp heat, thermal
cycling, and UV tests, but does not include a combined light-and-heat test at temperatures
representative of module operating conditions in the field. This observation about light
and elevated temperature had been well documented in small cell testing but had not yet
been confirmed for modules. PACT’s results provided a diverse data set of modules to
confirm this importance and also began to provide understanding on how this testing may
relate to field performance.
PACT demonstrated this through a systematic sequential test: Modules were exposed to
(1) dark at low temperature, (2) light at low temperature, (3) dark at high temperature, and
(4) light at high temperature. Results were unambiguous: These early modules showed
little or no degradation in the dark at elevated temperature (50°C) but degraded rapidly
when simultaneously exposed to light and heat. Crucially, the degradation modes
observed after indoor light-and-heat testing matched those observed in field-exposed
modules, providing strong scientific justification that this indoor test reproduces field-
relevant failure mechanisms.

 27

This finding was demonstrated for two distinct module types:
• Encapsulant-free glass/glass modules (desiccant-only): Failed rapidly both outdoors
 and after the 50°C light-and-heat test step. Indoor and outdoor samples showed
 consistent degradation modes (voids and delamination at the C60/BCP interface)
 confirmed by cross-sectional SEM.
• POE-encapsulated modules (P-0038/P-0014, UNC): Showed slower but consistent
 degradation under both field and indoor light-and-heat testing. Performance analysis
 and DLIT imaging both indicated series resistance increase along P3 scribes as the
 dominant failure mode in this more durable module type.
These results established the scientific basis for a light-and-elevated-temperature
qualification test protocol as an essential complement to existing IEC tests. Milestone
3.4.4, documenting the completion of the light-and-temperature test sequence for two
module types, was delivered on schedule.
In line with milestone 1.3.1 and further work in the PACT Core Program, a manuscript
was submitted and subsequently published [14]. This study evaluated light and elevated
temperature (LT) testing as an accelerated test for MHP solar modules. This research is
the first to directly compare LT and field testing across multiple device designs and
research labs. We found that LT testing at 55°C gives confidence in whether modules
with a moisture-proof package will retain their efficiency over 10 weeks of field operation.
This finding validates LT testing as a field-relevant accelerated test for MHP modules,
providing a tool for quickly developing more durable modules. Furthermore, this study
offers a foundational framework for developing a comprehensive qualification test for the
certification of MHP solar modules.

The test sequence is summarized in Figure 9 and Table 1 summarizes the tested modules
and the results. Notably, batches that passed both LT-55 and the package test also
passed 10 weeks of field testing, and batches that failed either accelerated test also failed
field testing sooner than 10 weeks.

While this work represents a step towards validated accelerated testing procedures for
MHP modules this is still a baby step. With more modules reaching 1 year or more in the
field we are turning our focus to accelerated tests that provide confidence nearing 1 year
in the field. The perovskite community is aligning on higher temperature longer duration
LT testing as the first step; however, this approach is often done in cells and has no
complimentary field studies to determine its relevance. Additionally, we anticipate these
stressors alone will not be sufficient for longer duration qualification. This is a key focus
of the PACT core program.

4.3 Failure Mode Characterization
PACT developed and applied a comprehensive failure analysis toolkit, yielding a portfolio
of characterization results with significant value for understanding how perovskite
modules fail in the field and under accelerated testing. Below are three journal articles
produced by members of the PACT team (and other collaborators) that highlight PACT
work in this area.
EL, PL, and DLIT imaging (Schall et al. [15]):
EL imaging after UV and thermal cycling exposures showed distinctly different spatial
degradation patterns despite similar overall performance losses measured by standard
IV characterization. UV-stressed modules showed degradation concentrated in specific
sub-cell regions, while TC-stressed modules showed different spatial distributions of
defects. This demonstrated that imaging modalities can diagnose the cause of failure —
a finding with significant practical value for failure triage and root cause analysis in
manufacturing quality control.
Post-mortem destructive analysis (Ulicna et al. [10]):
This paper outlines the findings from a comprehensive study on the reliability and
degradation mechanisms MHP PV modules, conducted at the PACT center.
Encapsulated MHP modules were tested outdoors in Golden, Colorado, to evaluate their
performance and longevity.
Key findings indicate that the choice of module encapsulation scheme is critical for
enhancing MHP module reliability. Three different packaging architectures were
compared: desiccated polyisobutylene (PIB) edge seal only, PIB blanket, and polyolefin
elastomer (POE) encapsulant. The results demonstrated rapid failure in modules with
edge seal only, while modules utilizing POE encapsulation exhibited significantly
improved lifetimes, highlighting the importance of eliminating air gaps in module
packaging.
Nondestructive characterization techniques, including spatially resolved imaging and
current-voltage (IV) analysis, identified scribe regions as critical areas of resistive losses.
The PIB blanket modules showed accelerated degradation patterns compared to the POE
encapsulated modules, attributed to chemical reactions at the scribes and changes in
interface strength.
Additionally, indoor accelerated stress testing revealed greater electrochemical corrosion
in POE-encapsulated samples, particularly near the scribes, where interactions between
the copper electrode and volatile iodine species led to the formation of copper iodide
(CuI).
These findings underscore the necessity of outdoor testing and failure analysis as
foundational steps toward developing effective indoor accelerated stress tests for MHP
technology. The work highlights the need for further investigation into the interactions

 30

between encapsulant formulations and MHP device stacks, as well as the optimization of
material selection and processing conditions to mitigate degradation mechanisms.
Overall, this study contributes valuable insights to the ongoing efforts at the PACT center
to advance the commercialization of MHP PV technology.
Stability and Reliability Challenges (Baumann et al. [16])
Baumann et al. [16] provide a comprehensive review of the stability and reliability
challenges facing perovskite solar cells (PSCs) and perovskite-containing modules, with
emphasis on degradation mechanisms and mitigation strategies relevant to
commercialization. The review distinguishes between intrinsic instability of the perovskite
absorber and extrinsic degradation caused by environmental and operational stressors
such as heat, light, moisture, oxygen, electrical bias, and mechanical loading.
A central theme is the role of mobile ions in accelerating degradation. Ion migration
contributes to phase segregation, interfacial reactions with charge transport layers and
electrodes, hysteresis, and irreversible performance loss under illumination, thermal
stress, and reverse bias. We also describe how precursor residues, crystallographic
phase instability, thermal decomposition, and strain from thermal expansion mismatch
can initiate failure. At the device and module levels, degradation is further exacerbated
by reactive metal electrodes, transport-layer incompatibilities, laser scribing damage,
packaging limitations, potential-induced degradation (PID), and vulnerability to reverse
bias during shading.
The review highlights a broad mitigation toolbox: compositional engineering for
intrinsically stable perovskites; defect passivation; suppression of ion migration through
additives, blocking layers, and 2D/3D interface designs; improved transport layers and
inert or barrier-protected electrodes; low-temperature, chemically compatible
encapsulation approaches; and module designs that better manage shading and reverse-
bias stress. The authors conclude that commercialization will require not only high
efficiency, but also reproducible reliability testing, robust qualification protocols, and multi-
year outdoor validation at module scale.

4.4 Acceleration Factor Analysis
Repins et al. [17] developed an analytical framework for estimating acceleration factors
(AFs) for oxygen-induced photooxidation of MAPI-based perovskites under common
accelerated test conditions. Key findings: (1) Standard dark damp heat tests do not
accelerate photooxidation of MAPI; (2) AFs for most common tests are low, meaning
these tests primarily identify early failures rather than predict long-term lifetime; (3) for
MAPI photooxidation, oxygen diffusion through the package edge seal limits the
degradation rate — a result that simplifies the analysis and demonstrates the value of
edge seal engineering. This work provides a conceptual framework for accelerated test
interpretation that extends beyond MAPI to other perovskite compositions.

Task 5: Bankability and Stakeholder Engagement

5.1 Tier 1 Outreach and Education
The bankability task, led by Black & Veatch, established the programmatic framework for
evaluating commercial readiness of perovskite PV from a project finance perspective. A
Tier 1 bankability webinar was hosted on April 6, 2022, covering the fundamentals of
bankability assessment, the five key assessment areas (design, performance/reliability,
manufacturing, company organization, and installation/O&M), and the structure of the
PACT bankability program. This webinar was among the first public educational efforts
focused specifically on perovskite PV bankability.
Sandia, LANL, and EPRI worked on two studies concerning the environmental and safety
issues with lead halide perovskites. Below are summaries of this work.
Environmental and health risks of perovskite solar modules (Torrence et al. [18])
Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have emerged as one of the most promising next-
generation photovoltaic (PV) technologies, achieving power conversion efficiencies
exceeding 25% at the lab scale and offering the prospect of very low-cost
manufacturing through solution-based and vapor-deposition processes. However, the
highest-performing formulations rely on lead-containing absorber layers, and the water
solubility of lead halide perovskites poses potential environmental and human health
risks that must be understood and addressed before the technology can be deployed
commercially at scale. This 2023 review article—authored by researchers at Sandia
National Laboratories, the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI), and Los Alamos
National Laboratory—provides a comprehensive, lifecycle-oriented risk assessment of
lead halide PSC modules, covering hazards at every stage from manufacturing through
end-of-life disposal.
The authors structure their analysis around the full module lifecycle: production,
transportation, installation, in-field operation, and disposal. During manufacturing,
workers may be exposed to toxic solvents (e.g., dimethylformamide, N-methyl-2-
pyrrolidone) and lead-containing precursor solutions through inhalation or skin contact;
the authors note that existing Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA)
and EPA regulations provide a baseline of worker protection, but that perovskite-specific
guidelines remain limited. Accidental module breakage during transportation or
installation represents another exposure pathway, with lead-halide compounds
dissolving rapidly in rainwater and potentially contaminating soil and groundwater.
During operation, intact and properly encapsulated modules are considered low-risk, but
storm damage, hail, fire, or flooding events can compromise encapsulation integrity and
cause lead leaching.
A central contribution of the paper is a compilation and statistical analysis of published
lead-leaching data from PSC cells and modules tested under a variety of conditions.

 33

The authors found that unencapsulated perovskite films and broken encapsulated
modules consistently leach lead at concentrations that exceed regulatory safe limits—
including the EPA's Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP) threshold of 5
mg/L for hazardous waste classification under the Resource Conservation and
Recovery Act (RCRA). Importantly, modules incorporating lead sequestration layers
(e.g., mesoporous carbon or thiol-functionalized polymers) demonstrated substantially
reduced leaching, in many cases remaining below regulatory limits even when broken.
The analysis revealed high variability in leaching results across studies, attributable to
differences in substrate type (glass vs. polymer), encapsulant, perovskite composition,
and test protocol—underscoring the need for standardized testing methods.
The article also reviews air emissions risks, particularly during fire events, where lead
can be volatilized and released as lead oxide particulates. The authors highlight that
glass-glass encapsulation shows promise in containing lead during high-temperature
events, whereas polymer-backed modules are more vulnerable. With respect to end-of-
life management, the paper notes that perovskite modules are likely to be classified as
hazardous waste under current RCRA regulations unless sequestration layers are
included, significantly increasing disposal costs and logistics. The authors conclude with
a set of forward-looking recommendations: development of standardized leaching test
protocols specific to perovskite modules, expansion of recycling infrastructure and take-
back programs, accelerated research into lead-free or lower-toxicity perovskite
compositions (acknowledging that tin-based alternatives carry their own toxicity
concerns), and broader adoption of lead sequestration layers as a near-term mitigation
strategy. The paper makes a compelling case that resolving these issues is essential
not only for environmental stewardship but also for the commercial bankability of the
technology.
Managing potential environmental and human health risks of lead halide perovskite
photovoltaic modules (Rencheck et al. [19])

Building directly on the risk assessment framework established by Torrence et al. [18],
this 2024 article by researchers at Sandia National Laboratories and EPRI shifts focus
from identifying risks to actively managing them. The paper provides an in-depth
examination of the U.S. regulatory landscape governing lead halide perovskite solar
modules (PSMs) across their lifecycle, and then proposes concrete experimental
research directions and engineering solutions needed to bring the technology into
compliance and support its commercial deployment.
The authors begin by cataloging the array of federal regulations and standards that
apply to PSMs at different lifecycle stages. These span EPA regulations under RCRA
(governing hazardous waste characterization and disposal), Clean Air Act provisions
(air emissions during manufacturing and fire events), Clean Water Act requirements
(stormwater and wastewater discharge), OSHA workplace exposure standards (for lead

 34

and organic solvents), and Department of Transportation hazardous materials transport
rules. The authors also identify relevant international frameworks, including the
European Union's Restriction of Hazardous Substances (RoHS) Directive and Waste
Electrical and Electronic Equipment (WEEE) Directive, noting that compliance
requirements may differ significantly across markets. This regulatory mapping is
presented as a practical resource for module manufacturers, project developers, and
policymakers seeking to understand the full compliance burden associated with PSM
commercialization.
A major contribution of the paper is its identification of critical gaps in current knowledge
that prevent definitive risk characterization and regulatory compliance assessment.
Three scenario-specific risk areas are examined in detail: fire events, in-field module
damage, and end-of-life leach testing. For fire scenarios, the authors note that while
some studies suggest glass encapsulation can contain lead at typical fire temperatures,
there are insufficient data on real-world fire conditions (e.g., wildfire, rooftop structure
fires) specific to PSM configurations. For in-field damage—such as hail strikes,
mechanical breakage, or flood inundation—there is a lack of standardized methods to
assess lead release under realistic environmental conditions. For end-of-life disposal,
current TCLP protocols were designed for municipal solid waste landfill scenarios and
may not accurately reflect the leaching behavior of PSMs, which have a distinct
chemical composition and degradation profile compared to conventional PV
technologies.
To address these gaps, the paper proposes a structured research agenda,
recommending specific experimental designs for each risk scenario. These include fire-
simulation testing protocols that capture air emissions and post-fire leachate from
representative PSM configurations, standardized mechanical damage and outdoor
weathering tests coupled with leaching analyses, and adapted leach test methodologies
(potentially building on or modifying TCLP/SPLP protocols) to better represent PSM
end-of-life conditions. The authors also review lower technology readiness level (TRL)
mitigation strategies currently being investigated, including novel lead-sequestration
materials (such as metal-organic frameworks and functionalized polymer interlayers),
advanced encapsulation schemes, and module-level recycling approaches that recover
lead and other valuable materials. The paper concludes that proactively addressing
these environmental and health risk management challenges—through targeted
research, updated test standards, and scalable mitigation technologies—will be critical
to establishing the commercial viability and social license needed for large-scale PSM
deployment in the United States and globally.

5.4 Decision to Discontinue Bankability Task
In FY24, following a comprehensive review of industry module performance data, PACT
and DOE jointly decided to remove the bankability task from the project scope. The
determining factor was that none of the industry modules under test had demonstrated
the technical performance thresholds specified in the TWP; specifically, sustaining >12%
efficiency for at least 10 weeks in the field. This decision was based at the time on an
evaluation of whether the technology had reached the requisite maturity for investment-
grade services. Looking forward, module durability must first be demonstrated on many
samples from a batch before bankability can be meaningfully assessed. The educational
materials and criteria developed under this task remain valuable community resources,
and the bankability program could be reactivated if module durability improves.

Conclusions
The PACT center has successfully built the scientific and practical infrastructure needed
to evaluate perovskite PV module performance, reliability, and commercial readiness.
The center's findings represent a significant and honest assessment of where the
technology stands, providing the community with data and tools needed to address the
remaining barriers to commercialization. Key conclusions include:
• Module durability was initially limited by packaging architecture. Polymer encapsulant
 (PIB/POE bulk layer) dramatically extended outdoor module lifetimes. Modules
 without these encapsulants declined to T80 in days-to-weeks compared to much
 longer lifetimes for encapsulated modules.
• Light combined with elevated temperature is an important degradation driver for MHP
 module. This stressor is not captured in current IEC qualification test sequences and
 must be incorporated into any fit-for-purpose perovskite qualification standard. Indoor
 light-and-heat testing reproduces a degradation mode observed in the field, providing
 the scientific basis for an accelerated qualification test.
• Metastability is a consistent, quantifiable, and manageable feature of perovskite
 module behavior. The "wake-up" phenomenon and its temperature dependence have
 been characterized but not yet to a degree sufficient to include in a PV performance
 model.
• Failure mode characterization demonstrates two dominant failure pathways: (1) series
 resistance increase at scribe interconnects due to delamination and interface
 degradation; and (2) material degradation due to moisture ingress. These are
 distinguishable by power-dependent IV analysis and by imaging (EL, PL, DLIT),
 enabling root cause identification.
• The U.S. perovskite module industry has not yet reached bankability maturity, but
 clear progress was observed across the project period. The technical barriers are
 identified and addressable, and the trajectory of industry module performance was
 positive throughout the project.

Path Forward
FY25–27 PACT: PV Accelerator for Commercializing Technologies
Continuation
Evidence of PACT's positive impact is DOE's decision to continue the program as a core
laboratory capability under FY25–27 funding. A combined Sandia/NLR proposal and
Technical Work Plan for the FY25–27 PACT program with a combined total budget of
approximately $6M over three years was submitted in February 2024 and accepted. The
continuation program builds directly on the scientific foundation established in this project
period, with priorities including: validation of qualification test protocols that screen for
longer than 1 year of field performance; performance model development as module
technology matures; continued field testing of industry and tandem modules; and, if
industry performance thresholds are met, reactivation of bankability services. The FY25–
27 program also broadens PACT’s technology scope beyond MHP to include all emerging
PV minimodule technologies (organic PV, all-thin-film tandems, and others meeting
PACT’s acceptance criteria). The program’s overarching mission is framed around
technology readiness: PACT helps companies advance from TRL-3 (manually fabricated
prototype minimodules) toward TRL-6 (automated pilot-scale production with small
system testing), at which point companies become eligible for DOE’s PV Proving Ground
program, which offers field testing of full-sized pilot modules in multiple climates.

Technology Transfer and Standardization
All PACT-developed protocols are publicly available on the PACT website and have been
widely downloaded and adopted by researchers, industry, and commercial test labs
worldwide. The primary mechanism for formal technology transfer is through PVQAT TG-
8 and IEC TC82 standards development activities. PACT's finding that combined light-
and-elevated-temperature testing is critical for perovskite qualification is expected to
become a formal test requirement in the emerging perovskite PV qualification standard.

Intellectual Property
No patent applications or invention disclosures resulted from this work. All protocols and
methods developed under PACT have been intentionally released as open-access
publications and documents to maximize community adoption and accelerate
standardization.
