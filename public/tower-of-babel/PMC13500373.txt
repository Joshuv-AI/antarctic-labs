Comprehensive diagnosis and management of Musladin–Lueke Syndrome in a Beagle in Japan
Authors: Mayuka KOIKE, Fumitaka SHITAMORI, Ikuma SUZUKI, Manabu MOCHIZUKI, Akihito IKEDA, Shingo MAEDA, Yasuyuki MOMOI, Tomohiro YONEZAWA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Musladin–Lueke syndrome (MLS) is an inherited connective tissue disorder in Beagle caused by a homozygous missense mutation in the ADAMTSL2 gene. It is rare, but recognized globally. This report presents the first case in Japan definitively diagnosed via genetic testing. The 6-month-old male Beagle exhibited characteristic “tiptoe” gait, joint stiffness, lateral deviation of the eye socket, and undescended testes. At 10 months of age, bilateral Cryptorchidectomy was performed, and echocardiography revealed no abnormalities. At 12 months of age, the patient demonstrated progressive forelimb stiffness, chronic digital abrasions, and mild left atrial enlargement on echocardiography. As no curative treatment exists, we implemented symptomatic management using custom-made orthotic boots, which significantly improved the patient’s quality of life. The high probability that this condition remains underdiagnosed in Japan underscores the necessity to raise awareness among veterinary clinicians.

Musladin-Lueke syndrome (MLS) is a rare autosomal recessive connective tissue disorder first recognized in Beagles in the 1970s [1]. The disease is caused by a homozygous missense mutation in the ADAMTSL2 gene (c.660C >T; p.R221C), which leads to characteristic fibrosis of the skin and joints, as well as musculoskeletal, dermatological, and occasionally behavioral abnormalities. Although MLS shares clinical similarities with human geleophysic dysplasia (GD), which also results from recessive ADAMTSL2 mutations, the prognosis in affected dogs is comparatively less severe, with many surviving into adulthood [9].

Several reports from Europe, North America, and South America have described the clinical features and genetic basis of MLS in Beagles, and the breed has also been proposed as a potential in vivo model for human GD research [1, 8]. While the existence of MLS is occasionally acknowledged in veterinary medicine in Japan, to our knowledge, clear case reports remain still unpublished. Furthermore, there have been no reports of treatments for this disease, including symptomatic treatments. In this paper, a case of MLS in Japan is reported, in which a diagnosis was made based on detailed clinical and genetic findings, and symptomatic treatment was administered.

A 6-month-old, intact male Beagle, weighing 9.0 kg was presented to the Veterinary Medical Center of the University of Tokyo. His chief complaint was “tiptoe gait,” a characteristic ballerina-like gait in MLS, affecting all four limbs. According to the owner, tiptoe gait had been observed since approximately 3 months of age, with progressive worsening. The dog showed no abnormalities in activity level or appetite. According to previous reports, the normal ROM for the carpal joint in young, healthy Beagles is extension at 215° and flexion at 63° [4]. The carpal joints in this case exhibited significant restrictions, with extension limited to 180° and flexion restricted to 90°, which clearly demonstrate a reduction in the ROM for both extension and flexion. Additionally, a slight decrease in ROM was noted in the tarsal and stifle joints, while the subject maintained the ability to achieve palmar/plantar contact with the paw pads, unlike the thoracic limbs. Slight discomfort was detected bilaterally in the hip joints during extension with no numerical abnormalities identified. No abnormalities were observed in the shoulder or elbow joints. No hyperextension was observed in any of the joints. Lateral deviation of the eye socket was also identified (Fig. 1D). There were palmar pad abrasions on the left and right forelimbs, respectively, likely caused by abnormal weight-bearing due to persistent tiptoe gait (Fig. 1E, 1F). Radiographs of the extremities revealed no bony abnormalities (Supplementary Material 2). Additionally, no testes were palpable in the scrotum. Complete blood count, serum biochemistry, and thoracic radiography revealed no abnormalities. No obvious abnormalities were found during the neurological examination while the conscious proprioception in the limbs and the spinal reflex of the extensor carpi radialis muscle were not clearly evaluated due to limited ROM (Supplementary Material 3). Based on the fact that the dog was a young Beagle and showed characteristic physical examination findings, MLS was suspected. A follow-up evaluation was conducted at 12 months of age to assess clinical stability. Physical examination revealed gradual progressive stiffness in the forelimbs. The subsequent progression of the disease involved a further decrease in the range of motion of the carpal joint while there was no sign of muscle rigidity or immobility of the forelimb. Due to a lack of physiological paw pad contact, the patient suffered from chronic abrasions on the digits of the forelimbs. No curative or effective treatment for MLS has been reported worldwide yet. In this case, symptomatic management was attempted to address the patient’s impaired ambulation. To mitigate abrasions on the digits of the forelimbs, a custom-made orthotic device (Toyo Sogu Iryoukigu Seisakujo, Tokyo, Japan) was developed. (Fig. 4C). Since the ROM limitation in the hind limbs was not severe and the dog was still able to land on its paws, we decided that only the forelimbs were suitable for the devices. The device features a distal plastic shell for protection, integrated into a soft fabric sock-like structure for secure attachment. The use of these orthotics during outdoor ambulation on surfaces such as asphalt significantly reduced signs of discomfort. The patient demonstrated excellent tolerance to the device (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Material 5).

MLS is an established inherited connective tissue disorder in Beagles, characterized by progressive fibrosis of the skin and joints, distinctive craniofacial morphology, and a characteristic digitigrade gait [3, 9]. Subsequent genetic studies have demonstrated that MLS is caused by a homozygous missense mutation in the ADAMTSL2 gene, implicating dysregulation of extracellular matrix organization and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling in disease pathogenesis [8]. ADAMTSL2 is known to interact with fibrillin microfibrils and regulate the bioavailability of TGF-β, a mechanism shared by multiple human connective tissue disorders [3, 6]. In particular, mutations in ADAMTSL2 cause human GD, which is characterized by joint stiffness, short stature, distinctive facial features, and cardiovascular disease [3, 8]. The patient in this study exhibited the characteristic craniofacial morphology, digitigrade gait, and bilateral abdominal cryptorchidism that have been consistently described in previous reports [1,2,3], thereby supporting the notion that these findings represent typical phenotypic features of the syndrome. To our knowledge, this is the first case in Japan to be definitively diagnosed with MLS via genetic testing; the identified mutation was identical to those previously reported [1,2,3, 9]. While the occurrence of an identical mutation in Japan could theoretically arise from a spontaneous event, it is more plausible that a population harboring this mutation was introduced from overseas. Although familial information was not available in this case, the identified mutation may already be present within Japanese Beagle populations. While there have been anecdotal reports suggesting the presence of Beagles exhibiting MLS-like symptoms in Japan, neither comprehensive research nor systematic genetic screening has been conducted to date. Considering the high probability that this condition remains underdiagnosed; it is imperative to raise awareness among Japanese veterinary clinicians and to establish clinical recognition of the disorder.

Cardiovascular involvement in MLS has shown considerable interindividual variability, with reported findings ranging from subclinical changes to overt cardiac disease [2, 9]. While MLS is generally considered to manifest clinically shortly after birth and subsequently reach a plateau, the frequency and progression of specific clinical signs exhibit significant inter-individual variability. The mild left atrial enlargement observed during the follow-up of this case supports the interpretation that some manifestations may be progressive. Specifically, there is a clinical concern regarding the potential development of progressive fibrosis in the cardiac valves and myocardium over time. Given the latent risk of developing overt cardiovascular disease, long-term monitoring—including serial echocardiographic evaluations—is highly recommended for the management of MLS-affected patients.

In the present report, comprehensive diagnostic imaging, including CT and MRI, was performed for the first time in an MLS case. Although neurological abnormalities are not considered defining features of MLS, sporadic cases presenting with epileptic seizures have been reported [1, 2]. Although no direct association between ADAMTSL2 mutations and structural abnormalities of the central nervous system has been established, alterations in extracellular matrix composition have been suggested to influence cerebrospinal fluid dynamics and ventricular morphology in other developmental or fibrotic disorders [10]. While seizure episodes were not observed in this case, MRI revealed mild to moderate ventricular enlargement. To date, no definitive association has been established between structural brain abnormalities and mutations in the ADAMTS or ADAMTSL gene families, thus, it remains unclear whether these findings reflect a broader phenotypic spectrum of this syndrome. Further investigation in additional cases will be necessary to clarify this issue.

To date, no curative or disease-modifying treatment for MLS has been established worldwide. In the present case, the use of custom-made orthotic boots significantly improved the patient’s QoL by mitigating digital abrasions on the forelimbs. Since the patient exhibited lameness in the absence of neurological deficits, this orthotic intervention should be highly effective. Although such an approach is just symptomatic, we propose that the refinement and standardized application of orthotics should be considered as a primary management strategy for MLS. Follow-up examinations revealed abrasions caused by increased internal friction. This was attributed to both the deterioration of the cushioning material from long-term use and an increased frequency of running, likely facilitated by the improved comfort provided by the orthotic device. These findings suggest the necessity for strategies to minimize internal friction and the timely replacement of worn cushioning.

Given the marked phenotypic and molecular overlap between canine MLS and human GD, MLS has been proposed as a naturally occurring large-animal model for studying fibrotic connective tissue disorders associated with dysregulated TGF-β signaling [6, 8]. Similar pathogenic mechanisms have been implicated in other connective tissue disorders, including Marfan syndrome, Weill–Marchesani syndrome, and acromicric dysplasia, all of which share clinical features such as joint limitation, cardiovascular abnormalities, and excessive extracellular matrix deposition [3, 5, 6]. The concentration of MLS within the Beagle breed is likely attributable to founder effects and genetic drift in their breeding history. This breed-specificity contrasts with human geleophysic dysplasia (GD), which shows no reported ethnic predilection; however, this specificity does not invalidate MLS as a disease model. While human GD occurs across diverse populations due to various mutations in the ADAMTSL2 gene [7], the uniform genetic background of Beagles, in which a specific mutation has been fixed [1], offers a distinct advantage. It allows for the observation of the molecular pathology such as TGF-β signaling dysregulation caused by ADAMTSL2 deficiency [6] without interference from external genetic noise. Although polygenic factors governing Beagle skeletal and connective tissue phenotypes may influence clinical presentation, the MLS-affected dog remains a highly homologous model, exhibiting joint contractures and skin fibrosis remarkably similar to human GD. This is particularly significant given that mouse models often face limitations due to neonatal lethality [3], which precludes the study of adult pathogenesis. Consequently, while MLS is a breed-specific condition derived from a particular lineage, its underlying biological processes are fundamentally continuous with human disease. Therefore, the MLS Beagle model possesses substantial medical and scientific validity as a preclinical model for elucidating the pathogenesis of GD across all human populations. We anticipate that further investigation into this canine model will provide valuable insights into the pathophysiology of related human conditions and may facilitate the development of novel therapeutic interventions for these challenging disorders.
