Metrnl ameliorates ulcerative colitis by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation via ASC
Authors: Ruilong Kou, Yueming Xia, Wenqin Wei, Yu Chen, Jinzhou Wang, Zhiwei Qin, Guangwei Zhu, Liangsheng Lin, Dingming Zheng, Jianxin Ye, Bin Lan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ulcerative colitis (UC) is characterized by the dysregulation of inflammatory pathways, leading to extensive tissue damage and chronic inflammation. NLRP3 inflammasome promotes inflammatory cytokine maturation and pyroptosis. Meteorin-like protein (Metrnl) has anti-inflammatory effects; however, its specific mechanisms in regulating these pathways remain unclear. This study investigated the effect of Metrnl on UC via regulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome through regulation of its key component, apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing CARD (ASC). NCM460 and FHC cells were treated with TNF-α to establish a cell validation model. Cells with Metrnl interference or overexpression were intervened with three inflammasome activators (ATP, nigericin, or alum), and a caspase-1 inhibitor (belnacasan). The inflammatory factor levels, ASC mRNA and protein expressions, pyroptosis-related proteins, and inflammasome were detected. Interactions between ASC, Metrnl and inflammasome were determined by protein immunoprecipitation. UC models were constructed using wild-type and Metrnl knockout mice. Histopathological changes and differences in pyroptosis-related protein and inflammasome expressions were examined. Metrnl expression was significantly decreased in colitis, and the inflammatory factors IL-1β and LDH expressions increased at the cellular and animal levels, promoting ASC expression and pyroptosis, and activating the inflammasome. Metrnl interference accelerated inflammation and pyroptosis, which were reversed by Metrnl overexpression. Interactive relationships between Metrnl and ASC were noted. Metrnl may mediate ASC protein expression, and reduce IL-1β expression and pyroptosis in colon epithelial cells through the inhibition of ASC-NLRP3 binding. In vivo studies with DSS-induced colitis models confirmed the significance of these findings. After Metrnl knockout, the colon of normal mice was shorter, and Metrnl-/- UC mice showed more severe colon tissue damage, reduced goblet cells, increased inflammation, and accumulation of pyroptosis protein expression, indicating that Metrnl knockout increases susceptibility to UC. Metrnl interferes with key inflammatory signaling pathways in UC by inhibiting the interaction between ASC and NLRP3, suggesting its potential in UC treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56656-6.

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Symptoms of UC include abdominal pain, bloody stools, rectal bleeding, and fatigue. UC patients also have extraintestinal manifestations such as peripheral arthritis, conjunctivitis, and weight loss, which severely affect normal human behavior1. The pathogenesis of UC involves complex immune responses and intestinal barrier dysfunction. In North America, 400 out of 100,000 people suffer from colitis. UC patients have a low life expectancy and an increased risk of colectomy and colorectal cancer. It is considered a common disease worldwide2–4. Currently, the treatment of UC includes surgical and nonsurgical methods, but owing to the high degree of recurrence and postoperative complications such as infections, intestinal obstruction, and pouchitis, innovative treatments for UC are still needed.

Metrnl (meteorin-like protein) is a protein with multiple biological functions. The adipokine Metrnl can ameliorate chronic colitis in Il-10–/– mice5. Metrnl encodes a novel immunoregulatory cytokine that is involved in inflammatory responses6. In recent years, Metrnl has been shown to have anti-inflammatory and intestinal barrier protective effects7. In IBD, including Crohn’s disease and UC, Metrnl may have therapeutic effects through its anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties7,8. Some studies have confirmed that Metrnl is highly expressed in the gastrointestinal tract of humans and mice and is specifically expressed in the intestinal epithelium8. Another study reported that Metrnl is highly expressed during gastrulation in early embryos9, which also adds to the evidence that Metrnl regulates colorectal endothelial cell function. Immunohistochemical analysis of healthy colon tissue and colon cancer tissue from 30 patients revealed that Metrnl expression was significantly lower in colon cancer tissue than in healthy colon tissue10, indicating that Metrnl may have potential for clinical diagnosis and treatment. Previous study reported that low serum Metrnl levels may be related to impaired endothelial function11. Based on previous research, Metrnl is believed to be associated with the early stages of the disease. At present, there is still a gap in the study of Metrnl in UC, and the role of Metrnl in UC is still unclear. It is of great significance to study the regulation and underlying mechanism of Metrnl in UC.

The NOD-, LRR- and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome is an important inflammatory signaling complex that is activated after recognizing multiple danger signals and triggering downstream inflammatory responses. It is believed to play a key role in the occurrence and development of IBD such as UC12,13. Apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a caspase activation and recruitment domain (ASC) is among the key components of the NLRP3 inflammasome. ASC is expressed mainly in neutrophils, macrophages, lymphocytes, monocytes, osteoblasts and dendritic cells. It mediates the interaction between NLRP3 activation and caspase-1 through oligomerization, thereby promoting the maturation and release of the inflammatory factors interleukin (IL)−1β and IL-1814,15.

This study investigated the molecular mechanism through which Metrnl affects ASC expression at the animal and cellular levels, explored its role in inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and evaluated the ability of this pathway to ameliorate UC symptoms and promote intestinal recovery.

Materials and methods

Animals and modeling

Twenty-seven six-week-old male C57BL/6J mice weighing 21–22 g were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, P. R. China (license number: SCXK (Su) 2018–0008). One male and two female Metrnl knockout (Metrnl-eKO1) mice (20–25 g) were obtained from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. (Shanghai, P. R. China) [license number: SCXK (Shanghai) 2017–0010]. The mice were bred at a female-to-male ratio of 2:1 at ZHBY Biotech Co., Ltd. (Zvast-Bio, Jiangxi, P.R. China), and the offspring were used for subsequent experiments. The mice were raised in an environment with a temperature of 20–26 °C and a humidity of 40–70%. The humane endpoint guidelines are included in Supplementary Table 1.

UC model construction: After adaptive feeding, the mice were fed 2.5% dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) solution for 5 days, during which the DSS solution was replaced once, and were returned to normal drinking water on the sixth day. All the experiments were conducted under aseptic conditions. After modeling, the mice were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (45 mg/kg), followed by blood collection from the heart after unconsciousness, for euthanasia.

The Metrnl gene knockout mice were bred in a female: male ratio of 2:1 until 12 offspring Metrnl homozygous knockout mice were obtained. Then, the 12 Metrnl homozygous knockout mice and 12 wild-type mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6/group): (1) wild-type control group (WT), (2) wild-type model group (WT-UC), (3) Metrnl homozygous knockout control group (Metrnl-/-), and (4) Metrnl homozygous knockout model group (Metrnl-/- UC). The WT-UC group and the Metrnl-/- UC group were used to establish UC models, while the WT group and the Metrnl-/- group were fed normal water. The mice were weighed daily after 5 days of model establishment for 7 consecutive days, and samples were taken 7 days later for subsequent experiments.

In vitro cell experiments

NCM460 cells and FHC cells were purchased from Beijing Beina Chuanglian Biotech Institute, Beijing, P. R. China (BNCC BeNa Culture Collection), and FuHeng Biology, Shanghai, P. R. China, respectively. NCM460 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) complete medium (containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin), and FHC cells were cultured in FH-FHC specialized culture medium. The cells were passaged when the cell density reached 80%−90%. The cell culture supernatant was discarded, and the cells were washed twice with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and digested with 0.25% trypsin [containing 0.02% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)]. When the cells became round, the culture medium was added to terminate the digestion, and the cell suspension was collected into a 10 mL centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 3 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were resuspended in the culture medium. The cell suspension was distributed in prepared culture dishes at a ratio of 1:3 and labeled, and the cells were cultured in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator.

Metrnl interference and overexpression lentiviruses (Zvast-bio) were constructed and packaged, and infection was carried out at an infection index of 100. One milliliter of complete medium containing 1X transfection reagent (Lipofectamine 3000; Invitrogen, L3000015) was added to complete the cell transfection. After treatment with 2 µg/mL puromycin for 72 h, stable cell lines were obtained via expansion. A total of 1 µg/mL tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) (Novoprotein Scientific (Shanghai) Inc., C008) was added to the cells for 24 h to simulate the cell inflammation model16. At the same time, different activators—5 mM adenosine triphosphate (ATP)17(MCE, B25057), 20 µM nigericin18(MCE, HY-127019), or 300 µg/mL alum19(Invitrogen, 77161)—were added to activate the inflammasome, and a caspase-1 inhibitor, belnacasan (MCE, HY-13205; 1 µg/mL) was used, and treated the cells for 24 h. The overexpression plasmid vector used was FV115, and its basic structure is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

The negative control for the overexpression experiment was an empty vector that did not contain the target gene fragment. The sense and antisense strands for the shRNAs used in the study are presented in Table 1.

Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining

The paraffin sections of the colon tissue were obtained from the wild-type and Metrnl gene knockout mice. After baking, dewaxing, and hydration, the colon tissue paraffin sections were stained with hematoxylin staining solution (ZSGB Bio, ZLI-9610) for 3–5 min. After being rinsed with running water, the sections were differentiated with 1% hydrochloric acid and alcohol and then stained with bluing solution and eosin (Solarbio, G1100) for 3–5 min. The sections were dehydrated, mounted, and observed under a microscope (BX43, Olympus). The score was recorded according to the degree of colon tissue damage, which ranged from 0 to 4: 0, normal colon; 1, mild damage (less than 25% damage); 2, moderate damage (25%−50% damage); 3, extensive damage (50%−75% damage); and 4, extreme damage (greater than 75% damage). The individual scores for each criterion were summed to obtain the final damage score.

Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining

After baking, dewaxing and hydration, the paraffin sections of the colon tissue were rinsed with tap water for 3 min and then with distilled water twice for 1 min each time. Subsequently, periodic acid solution was added. The sections were allowed to stand at room temperature for 7 min and then rinsed with tap water for 1 min and with distilled water twice for 1 min each. Schiff reagent (Solarbio, G1281) was added, and the samples were placed in the dark at room temperature and stained for 15 min. Then, the sections were rinsed with tap water for 10 min, hematoxylin staining solution was added, and the samples were stained for 2 min. Differentiation with acid differentiation solution was performed for 2–5 s; the samples were rinsed with tap water for 10 min, and finally with distilled water for microscopic examination. The score was recorded according to the degree of colon tissue damage, which ranged from 0 to 4: 0, normal colon; 1, mild damage (less than 25% damage); 2, moderate damage (25%−50% damage); 3, extensive damage (50%−75% damage); and 4, extreme damage (greater than 75% damage). The individual scores for each criterion were summed to obtain the final damage score.

Immunofluorescence

After the colon tissue paraffin sections were baked, dewaxed, and hydrated, the cells were subjected to antigen retrieval via citric acid buffer. The cells were permeabilized at room temperature for 10 min with 0.5% Triton X-100 and then blocked at 37 °C with bovine serum albumin (BSA; Solarbio) for 30 min. The treated cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min, and blocked with 5% BSA at 37 °C for 30 min. Then, the cells were incubated with antibodies against CD4 (Bioss), F4/80, LY6G (Proteintech), and lysozyme (Abcam) overnight at 4 °C. After the samples were washed, a cyanine 3 (cy3) (1:200) fluorescent secondary antibody was added, and the samples were incubated at 37 °C for 45 min, counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Keygen, KGA215-50), mounted, and observed under a fluorescence microscope (CKX53, Olympus).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) detection

An ELISA detection kit was used according to the instructions of the kit to measure the levels of IL-1β (MM-0040M1), IL-6 (MM-0163M1), and TNF-α (MM-0132M1) in colon tissues and cells, and the optical density (OD) and expression levels were detected via an automatic microplate reader (WD-2012B, Beijing Liuyi).

Western blot (WB) detection

A certain amount of colon tissue or cells was obtained, radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (C1053; Applygen Technologies Inc., Beijing) was added, and total protein was extracted. Centrifugation was performed at 12,000 rpm via a high-speed centrifuge at 4 °C for 10 min, the supernatant was obtained, and the total protein was quantified via a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein quantification kit. After the protein sample was denatured, sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) was conducted for 2 h, and then the sample was transferred to the membrane at a 300 mA constant flow for 80 min. The polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore) was blocked with skim milk powder and incubated with the following primary antibodies: mouse monoclonal anti-actin (1/2000, ZSGB-Bio, cat. no. TA-09), rabbit anti-NLRP1 (1/1000, Proteintech, cat. no. 12256-1-ap), rabbit anti-NLRP3 (1/500, Affinity, cat. no. df7438), rabbit anti-NLR family caspase activation and recruitment domain-containing protein 4 (NLRC4, 1/1000, Affinity, cat. no. df13673), rabbit anti-gasdermin D (GSDMD, 1/2000, Affinity, cat. no. af4013), rabbit anti-Metrnl (1/1000, Thermo, cat. no. PA5-113001), mouse anti-caspase-1 (1/2000, Thermo, cat. no. MA5-32909), and rabbit anti-ASC (also known as PYCARD). The next day, the PVDF membrane was incubated with the secondary antibody IgG (H + L) (1/2000, ZSGB-Bio, goat anti-mouse, cat. no. ZB-2305; goat anti-rabbit ZB-2301) at room temperature for 2 h. Then, the PVDF membrane was soaked with luminescent liquid and placed in an ultrahigh-sensitivity chemiluminescence imaging system (Chemi Doc™ XRS+, Bio-Rad Laboratories (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.) for development. ImageJ v.1.8.0 (NIH, USA) was used for quantification.

Protein co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

An immunoprecipitation kit (C600689; Sangon Biotech) was used to detect the binding of ASC with NLRP1 and NLRP3. The cells were lysed with cell lysis buffer, and after ultrasonic treatment, part of the lysate was taken as the input sample. A total of 700 µL of lysis buffer was incubated with ASC antibody at 4 °C for 4 h, and IgG protein (Beyotime) was used as the negative control. The incubated mixture was transferred to a centrifuge column containing protein A/G agarose, incubated at 4 °C overnight, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 s at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the agarose beads were washed with IP buffer and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 s. Fifty microliters of loading buffer was added, the mixture was incubated in a 95 °C water bath for 5 min, and the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 s. The centrifuged products were collected, and the protein expression levels of NLRP1 and NLRP3 were detected via WB.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.0 software was used for graph generation and statistical analysis. All the experiments were repeated 3 times, and the quantitative results are expressed as the means ± standard deviations (¯x̄ ± s). Quantitative numerical comparisons between multiple groups were conducted via one-way analysis of variance, whereas pairwise comparisons were conducted via Tukey’s method. P < 0.05, P < 0.01, and P < 0.001 indicate statistically significant, highly statistically significant, and very highly statistically significant differences, respectively.

Results

The onset of UC was related to a decrease in the Metrnl protein expression

We found that Metrnl protein expression was significantly decreased in the UC mouse model. As shown in Fig. 1A and B, on the day the model was established, the Metrnl protein expression of the mice in the model group was significantly lower than that of the WT (control) group, while Metrnl mRNA expression showed no significant changes. To explore the sustained role of Metrnl in UC, we extended the observation time after model establishment and found that Metrnl protein expression continued to decrease significantly at 12 d post-modeling (P < 0.001). At 24 d post-modeling, Metrnl protein expression was also decreased (P < 0.05), however, compared to 12 d post-modeling, Metrnl protein expression exhibited a rebound phenomenon. Meanwhile, Metrnl mRNA expression showed a decreasing trend at 12 d post-modeling, but no significant differences were observed among groups. These results suggest that Metrnl plays an important role in UC and could serve as a potential therapeutic target for UC. Since the decrease in Metrnl protein expression was more significantly at 12 d post-modeling, we selected the mice euthanized at this time point for subsequent experiments.

Metrnl inhibited the inflammatory response and pyroptosis at the cellular level

On the basis of the findings at the animal level, the possible mechanism of Metrnl in UC was further explored. The experiments were designed to interfere with and overexpress Metrnl in the human colonic epithelial cell lines FHC and NCM460, and inflammasome activators were added to detect the expression of inflammation- and pyroptosis-related proteins. We selected effective interference sequences and successfully verified the resulting overexpression vectors (Fig. 2A). sh-2 was selected for subsequent experiments in both cell lines. FHC and NCM460 cells were transfected separately. After interference with Metrnl, the levels of IL-1β and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) showed an increasing trend but not significant, while overexpression of Metrnl decreased the levels of IL-1 β and LDH, whereas the inflammasome activators reversed the overexpression effect and promoted inflammation (Fig. 2B). Interference with Metrnl promoted the release of IL-1β and LDH, and overexpression of Metrnl reduced the levels of IL-1β and LDH, whereas the activator reversed the effect of Metrnl overexpression and promoted inflammation (Fig. 2B). In the model group and the Metrnl interference group, the fluorescence intensities of NLRP1, NLRP3, and ASC in the two groups of colonic epithelial cells increased, and the fluorescence intensities of NLRP1, NLRP3, and ASC in the overexpression group decreased. Compared with that in the model group, the fluorescence in the activator treatment group was greater (Fig. 2C).

Furthermore, we investigated the changes in the expression of proteins closely related to the NLRP3 inflammasome and pyroptosis pathways in each treatment group. In the model group, the protein expression of ASC, NLRP1, NLRP3, NLRC4, GSDMD, GSDMD N-terminal domain (GSDMD N), caspase-1, procaspase-1, and caspase-P20 increased. After Metrnl interference, the expression of GSDMD N, NLRP1, NLRP3, NLRC4, caspase-1, procaspase-1, and caspase-P20 in FHC cells was significantly increased, but the effect on NCM460 cells was not clear, and there were no significant changes except for increases in the expression of NLRP1 and caspase-P20. This might be because these proteins had already increased in the model, and on this basis, further increases were not obvious. In colon epithelial cells overexpressing Metrnl, the expression of the NLRP3 inflammasome and pyroptosis-related proteins was inhibited to varying degrees, suggesting that Metrnl may be a key target in the regulation of the inflammatory response in UC. The addition of different inflammasome activators aggravated the occurrence of ASC oligomerization and pyroptosis in colon epithelial cells, regardless of whether they were subjected to Metrnl interference or Metrnl overexpression; however, interestingly, the activators, under the Metrnl treatment conditions, the protein expression of NLRP1, NLRP3, NLRC4, caspase-1, pro-caspase-1, caspase-P20, GSDMD, and GSDMD N significantly increased, and the effect on the activation of the inflammatory pathway was more significant (Fig. 3).

Co-immunoprecipitation experiments verified the interaction between Metrnl and ASC

On the basis of the above results, we sought to confirm the interaction between Metrnl and the pyroptosis protein ASC and designed a protein co-IP experiment to verify whether Metrnl and ASC interact in FHC and NCM460 cells. The IP results revealed that the abundance of the Metrnl protein was detected in the ASC precipitate in both the model and overexpression groups, indicating that ASC and Metrnl interact with each other. Moreover, we detected the protein expression of NLRP3 in the precipitate, suggesting that ASC and NLRP3 also interact with each other (Fig. 5).

Histopathological experiments at the animal level revealed that Metrnl deficiency promoted the onset of UC

Overall, the findings at the animal level demonstrated that Metrnl deficiency promotes the onset of UC. Therefore, we knocked out the Metrnl gene at the animal level to determine its potentially important role in colitis. Gene identification was performed on the tails of weaned mice, and homozygous knockout mice were selected for grouping and modeling (Fig. 6A). Colon length measurements in each group of mice revealed that inducing UC in WT and Metrnl-/- mice significantly shortened the colon length (Fig. 6B), and from in vitro colon morphology observation, it was found that, compared with the WT-UC group, the colon morphology of mice in the Metrnl-/- UC group was more abnormal (Fig. 6C). The colons of WT and Metrnl-/- mice were pathologically stained. The mucosal epithelium of the colon tissue of the WT control group mice was intact, the glands were orderly, the structure was clear, there were no ulcers, and there was no inflammatory cell infiltration. The colon tissue of the WT model group exhibited ulcers, mucosal damage, glandular structural disorder, obvious edema in the mucosa and submucosa, and inflammatory cell infiltration. Colon tissue damage was more obvious in the Metrnl-/- mouse model group. PAS staining showed a decrease in goblet cells in the model group, with the Metrnl-/- mouse model group exhibiting the greatest decrease (Fig. 6D). These findings indicate that Metrnl knockout increases susceptibility to UC. Compared with the WT mice, the Metrnl-/- mice showed more severe colitis, which confirms that Metrnl is involved in the development and growth of the colon, and plays an important role in UC.

Metrnl knockout could exacerbate the inflammatory response

We then verified the difference in inflammation levels between WT and Metrnl-/- mice, and the results revealed that the serum levels of the inflammatory factors IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 were significantly increased in the model mice (Fig. 7A). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that the expression levels of lamina propria (CD4+), macrophages (F4/80), neutrophils (LY6G), and Paneth cells (lysozyme) in the colon of the Metrnl-/- mouse model increased (Fig. 7B). The mRNA levels of NLRP3, NLRC4, and caspase-1 in the colon tissue of the Metrnl-/- mouse model were significantly higher than those in the WT mouse model (Fig. 7C). The WB results revealed that NLRP1, NLRP3, NLRC4, caspase-1 and GSDMD protein expression tended to increase in the Metrnl-/- mouse model compared with the WT mouse model (Fig. 7D). These findings indicate that the absence of Metrnl exacerbates inflammation and promotes pyroptosis.

Discussion

UC affects mainly the inner layer of the colon and is characterized by long-term inflammation and ulceration of the colonic mucosa; UC usually begins in the rectum and may spread upward to different parts of the colon but rarely affects the small intestine (unlike Crohn’s disease, which can affect the entire digestive tract). Drug therapy for UC may be accompanied by a series of side effects, such as vomiting, allergies, decreased immune function, and effects on liver and kidney function, which vary depending on the type of drug used.

Metrnl was originally discovered in muscle and adipose tissue and is involved in various physiological processes, such as energy metabolism, anti-inflammation, and tissue repair20. In patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), Lee et al.21 and Dadmanesh et al.22 detected low serum levels of Metrnl, but other studies reported significantly increased serum levels of Metrnl23,24, possibly related to different study populations, disease processes, or treatment cycles.

The inflammatory regulatory role of Metrnl in various other diseases has also gradually been revealed, such as improving cardiac function recovery after heart failure, reducing cardiac inflammatory cell infiltration, and reducing the expression of proinflammatory factors, thereby alleviating the inflammatory response after cardiac injury25. In chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) patients, high Metrnl expression plays an important role in balancing the inflammatory response and may serve as a key therapeutic target26. Interestingly, Xu et al.27 reported that Metrnl relieves endoplasmic reticulum stress by activating adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK)-p21-activated kinase 2 (PAK2) signal transduction in H9C2 cells, thereby alleviating MI/R injury-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis.

Limited research data exists on the role of Metrnl in the development of UC inflammation, and the underlying mechanism by which Metrnl affects the inflammatory response in UC remains unknown. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to discover that regulating Metrnl expression in colonic epithelial cells can affect ASC oligomerization and the expression of NLRP3-pro-Caspase1-related pathway proteins, thereby influencing the formation of inflammasome and regulating pyroptosis. Abnormal epithelial cell death in UC leads to impaired intestinal barrier function, which in turn triggers an inflammatory response, and pyroptosis may play a key role in this process. The NLRP3 inflammasome is a multimolecular complex composed of the adaptor ASC protein, which contains the caspase recruitment domain ASC and the effector protein caspase-1. After the NLRP3 inflammasome is activated, it interacts with and binds to assemble into the cytoplasmic NLRP3-ASC-procaspase-1 complex, which results in cleavage and promotes the release of IL-1β and IL-1828. Moreover, after NLRP3 senses the danger signal in the cell, it recruits and activates caspase-1, which not only cleaves proinflammatory factor precursors to mature and release them but also activates the GSDMD protein, inducing pyroptosis and further exacerbating inflammation and tissue damage. Lv et al.13 demonstrated that lonicerin protects against colitis through the NLRP3-ASC-procaspase-1 pathway and proposed its use as an anti-inflammatory agent for colitis. The expression of Metrnl affects the protein expression of the NLRP3-ASC-procaspase-1 complex, providing new ideas for the treatment of UC. This study revealed that in UC, Metrnl can interact with ASC to inhibit inflammasome formation and prevent the expansion of inflammatory responses. This discovery may reveal a new role for Metrnl in regulating immune homeostasis, especially its potential to inhibit excessive inflammatory responses. The specific binding of Metrnl to ASC not only prevents ASC from oligomerizing to form the scaffold structure of the inflammasome but also reduces the interaction between NLRP3 and ASC, thereby inhibiting the activation of caspase-1 and the secretion of proinflammatory factors such as IL-1β. This mechanism effectively reduces the inflammatory cascade and alleviates inflammatory damage to intestinal tissue, providing a new theoretical basis for the treatment of UC.

In addition, the results of this study revealed that the mRNA content of Metrnl in Metrnl-/- mice was significantly reduced and that pathological tissue manifestations and NLRP3/Caspase1 pathway protein expression were increased in UC, suggesting that Metrnl plays an important role in UC. Li et al.8 reported that the mRNA content of Metrnl in specific knockout Metrnl-/- mice was significantly reduced, which is consistent with our results. However, the body weight, food intake, and blood glucose did not differ between the WT and the Metrnl-/- mice, indicating that Metrnl may not significantly affect the intestinal nutrient absorption under physiological conditions. Moreover, the length, permeability and histology of the intestine did not change in Metrnl-knockout mice, which indicates that, unlike our results, Metrnl did not affect intestinal development or structure. However, Metrnl is undeniably involved in the development of UC. In subsequent studies, we may supplement the detection of pyroptosis in some immune cells.

Although the findings demonstrated that Metrnl can interact with ASC to influence pyroptosis and inhibit the occurrence and development of UC, limitations still exist. In terms of the experimental design, the effect of Metrnl on ASC transcriptional levels was not investigated, the interaction mechanism between Metrnl and ASC was not studied, and disease activity index (DAI) scores were not determined for UC mice. Therefore, in the future, we will verify the effect of Metrnl on ASC mRNA levels, further explore whether Metrnl directly interacts with ASC using GST Pull-down experiments, and investigate the binding region of Metrnl and ASC through molecular docking and spatial conformation. In this study, GSDMD-N expression was detected to verify the occurrence of pyroptosis; in future studies, we may also explore its effect on IL-18 levels. In addition, we will investigate the effects of Metrnl on NLRP1 and NLRC4 and the underlying mechanism to elucidate the effects of Metrnl on pyroptosis.

Conclusion

Metrnl promotes the regeneration and repair of intestinal epithelial cells by activating related signaling pathways, which is very important for accelerating the recovery of damaged intestinal tissue during treatment. The interaction between Metrnl and ASC plays a key anti-inflammatory role in UC, providing a new perspective for understanding the pathogenesis of IBD and developing new treatment strategies. Future studies should explore the specific mechanism of action of Metrnl, the optimal administration method, its safety and efficacy, and whether it can be used as an adjuvant therapy or a new therapeutic target to improve the prognosis of patients with non-UC IBD.
