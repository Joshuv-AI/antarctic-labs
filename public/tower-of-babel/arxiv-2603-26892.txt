One-loop Amplitudes:
String Methods, Infrared Regularization, and Automation

Abstract




We calculate field theory loop amplitudes by string methods, applied to half-maximal 4-point one-loop graviton amplitudes.
Infrared divergences are regulated similarly to
soft-collinear effective field theory (SCET): new mass scales are introduced, here by higher-point kinematics.
We use an analytically continued single-valued polylogarithm as generating function. The Feynman integrals for the new tensor structures are infrared finite. We provide code as a step towards automation.

1
Introduction

The main theme of this paper is how to handle infrared (soft and collinear) divergences in string and field theory.
Let us first very briefly outline the big picture. Weinberg’s 1965
soft theorem
is clearly explained in Schwartz’s textbook
[
48
]
, Ch. 9.
Consider an
n
-point amplitude. If we add one photon of momentum
k
and polarization
e
to the
i
th leg with momentum
p_{i}
, this produces the factor

\frac{p_{i}\cdot e}{p_{i}\cdot k}\;.

(1.1)

Weinberg emphasized that we should expect the infrared limit
p_{i}\cdot k\rightarrow 0
where the denominator vanishes to be cancelled in the total
S
-matrix element, by gauge invariance.
For example, in
[
48
]
, Ch. 20 there is an explicit computation of the 4-point function of leptons
at one-loop, and it is shown how the infrared divergences of this one-loop amplitude are cancelled by a 5-point function at tree-level, where the extra external state is a photon.

It is not so convenient for perturbation theory to have cancellations across different orders (tree-level vs. one-loop). There are now many methods that improve on
this classic cancellation from QED.
One modern example that has some resemblance to the spirit (if not detail) of this paper is the
hard S-matrix
constructed by
Hannesdottir and Schwartz
[
33
]
. They work at fixed order of perturbation theory, say one-loop, but systematically use cuts
to reproduce the above cancellation. The philosophy is that of soft-collinear effective field theory (SCET). There, one
gives up full manifest Lorentz invariance, which provides additional kinematic variables. In practice, these variables appear as low-energy mass scales
in Feynman integrals, that regulate those integrals, sometimes completely. This is a simple but crucial calculational point. Here,
we keep full manifest Lorentz invariance but harvest additional invariants from higher-point functions.

In more detail: we introduce a method to compute the field theory (worldline) limit of string theory amplitudes at fixed order of perturbation theory, using as generating functions certain Feynman integrals with
external masses
. The use of generating functions in this context is of course not new, in fact we essentially recycle the Bern-Dixon-Kosower (BDK) differentiation method from 1993
[
9
]
.
Using external masses as regulators is also hardly new. But the combination of computing
massless
string amplitudes in terms of amplitudes with external
masses
is if not completely new, then at least unusual. (To avert confusion: we are
not
computing amplitudes with massive external string states,
the masses are only regulators.)

Allowing for external masses might seem like introducing complications in an already complicated calculation. But we claim the situation is the opposite:
a key feature of our approach is that Feynman diagrams with all masses generic, “all-mass diagrams”, sometimes
enjoy greatly increased symmetry compared to when some masses are taken to zero, and postponing the taking of the soft/collinear limit to the very end of the calculation increases

simplicity
, not complexity. In particular, as is well known, the scalar
D
-point function in exactly

D
dimensions (so the 4-point function in
D=4
, but not in
D=4-2\epsilon
) has

dual conformal invariance
, as explained for example in
[
47
,
12
]
. To be clear, this is a generic property
of the nonsupersymmetric Feynman integral. In fact, it is an important feature of the formalism in this paper that it makes no use of supersymmetry.
(We do apply it to an example with a specific amount of supersymmetry, but we believe that it could equally well be applied to any piece in
the supersymmetry decomposition of a nonsupersymmetric loop amplitude.)

In string theory, there are other methods that bear some resemblance to ours, for example momentum from a pinch
[
49
,
50
]
,
and chiral splitting
[
2
]
. We do not make explicit connections to these or other string methods in this paper, so this is an interesting question for the future.

There is of course a vast literature on graviton loop amplitude more broadly. However, most of it is in a helicity basis,
that for reasons that will become clear, we do not employ. (One obvious reason is there is no dimension-independent helicity basis.) Early papers calculating the maximally supersymmetric one-loop 4-point graviton amplitude are
[
31
,
32
]
. Another early paper
that computed the one-loop pure gravity 4-point function in helicity basis is Dunbar-Norridge in 1994
[
24
]
.
We use some details from
[
51
]
that used string theory to recreate and extend the Dunbar-Norridge helicity result.
A few other papers that make progress on and summarize previous results are

[
28
,
27
,
45
]
. We make particular use of
[
10
]
, which gives useful comments on the basis of curvature tensors.
The alternative point of view in
[
19
]
, using representation theory to constrain the nonsupersymmetric S-matrix,
is very relevant to our calculations, but we do not attempt a complete match
to their formalism in this paper. We do use explicit analytic continuations in field theory, in particular
[
17
,
13
,
22
,
21
]
.

Finally, we rely heavily on
[
5
,
6
]
: in a nutshell, the main technical result of this paper is to perform the Feynman integrals whose integrands were constructed in those two papers.

2
Review of string computation

In this section we give a relatively brief review of how correlation functions in worldsheet conformal field theory are assembled to an
integrand
of the four-point one-loop scattering amplitudes of the open- and closed-string at half-maximal supersymmetry. The rest of this paper, after this review, is about
integrating
this integrand. The computation will trace the work in sections 4 and 6 of
[
5
]
. The code used in these calculations is available from

the repository

.

In the main body of this work, however, the main part of the algorithm is presented in a programming-language-agnostic procedure.

2.1
Spin summations

2.1.1
Open-string half-maximal supersymmetry spin sums

In this review section, we will focus on the half-maximal
n
-point case and start with the open string.

The open-string integrand has a correlation function of the form

\braket{V_{1}^{(0)}(z_{1})\dots V_{n}^{(0)}(z_{n})}_{\nu}\ ,

(2.1)

where
\nu
labels the spin structure and gauge bosons are represented by vertex operators
with polarization
e^{m}
and momenta
k^{m}

1
1

1

Here we absorbed a factor of
i
in
X^{m}
. Compare
the standard form
e^{ik\cdot X}
in a vertex operator.

V^{(0)}(e,k)\equiv e_{m}(\partial X^{m}+(k\cdot\psi)\psi^{m})e^{k\cdot X},

(2.2)

and the
(0)
superscript denotes zero superghost picture. (For non-string theorists: this is a discrete gauge choice for the vertex operator
representing the external gauge boson, among an infinity of choices. The worldsheet quantum fields
X
and
\psi
are bosons and fermions, respectively.)

For the purposes of this review, let us focus on contributions to (
2.1
) with only worldsheet fermions
\psi
. They are of the form

\braket{[(e_{i}\cdot\psi_{i})(k_{i}\cdot\psi_{i})]\dots[(e_{j}\cdot\psi_{j})(k_{j}\cdot\psi_{j})]}_{\nu}\ ,

(2.3)

where
\psi_{i}\equiv\psi(z_{i})
. Relabel the indices in (
2.3
) and concentrate exclusively on the fermions themselves, factoring
out the constant factors
e_{i}
and
k_{i}
:

\braket{(\psi\psi)_{1}\,(\psi\psi)_{2}\dots(\psi\psi)_{m}}_{\nu}\ .

(2.4)

Pairwise contractions produce closed cycles of the two-point function (Szegö kernel)
S_{\nu}
,

S_{\nu}(x_{1})\,S_{\nu}(x_{2})\dots S_{\nu}(x_{m})\ ,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}=0\ .

(2.5)

The Szegö kernel has a representation in terms of Jacobi theta functions:

S_{\nu}(z)\equiv\frac{\vartheta_{1}^{\prime}(0,\tau)\,\vartheta_{\nu}(z,\tau)}{\vartheta_{\nu}(0,\tau)\,\vartheta_{1}(z,\tau)}\ .

(2.6)

In
[
5
]
, the summation over
\nu
was performed for the reduced supersymmetric sector, namely the half- and quarter-maximal even sectors.
This is done by rewriting the orbifold-twisted partition functions in terms of fermion Green’s functions with the twist vector as the argument:

\mathcal{I}_{n,1/2}^{e}(\vec{v}_{k})=\frac{1}{\Pi_{n}}\,\sum_{\nu=2}^{4}(-1)^{\nu-1}\Bigg[\frac{\vartheta_{\nu}(0,\tau)}{\vartheta_{1}^{\prime}(0,\tau)}\Bigg]^{4}\,S_{\nu}(kv)S_{\nu}(-kv)\,\braket{V_{1}^{(0)}(z_{1})\dots V_{m}^{(0)}(z_{m})}_{\nu}\ ,

(2.7)

where the Koba-Nielsen factor
\Pi_{n}
is given in eq. (
2.18
).
The two
S_{\nu}
combine with the explicit Jacobi theta functions to form the K3
\times\,T^{2}
orbifold partition function: these two
S_{\nu}
can be thought of as background field representation of the nontrivial background.
The fermion Wick contractions in (
2.7
) will yield the same cycles as (
2.4
):

\sum_{\nu=2}^{4}(-1)^{\nu-1}\Bigg[\frac{\vartheta_{\nu}(0,\tau)}{\vartheta_{1}^{\prime}(0,\tau)}\Bigg]^{4}\,S_{\nu}(kv)S_{\nu}(-kv)\,S_{\nu}(x_{1})\,S_{\nu}(x_{2})\dots S_{\nu}(x_{m})\equiv\mathcal{G}_{m+2}(\gamma,-\gamma,x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\ ,

(2.8)

where
{\mathcal{G}}
is just notation for the left-hand side, and the orbifold twist vector is:

\gamma\equiv kv\ ,\quad\gamma_{i}\equiv kv_{i}\ ,

(2.9)

and we have
\gamma_{1}=-\gamma_{2}
for K3
\times\,T^{2}
orbifold compactifications. Note that we are relating the half-maximally supersymmetric spin sum for
m
two-point functions to that of maximal supersymmetry with
m+2
two-point functions,
hence the notation
\mathcal{G}_{m+2}(\gamma,-\gamma,x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
.

To write this out explicitly, we follow the notation of
[
14
]
where a doubly-periodic function
f^{(n)}
for each non-negative integer
n
is defined by
using a non-holomorphic Kronecker-Eisenstein series as generating function (closely related to the Szegö kernel
S_{\nu}
)

\Omega(z,\alpha,\tau)\equiv\exp\Big(2\pi i\alpha\frac{{\rm Im\,}z}{{\rm Im\,}\tau}\Big)\frac{\vartheta_{1}^{\prime}(0,\tau)\vartheta_{1}(z+\alpha,\tau)}{\vartheta_{1}(z,\tau)\vartheta_{1}(\alpha,\tau)}\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha^{n-1}f^{(n)}(z,\tau)\ ,

(2.10)

and the functions
f_{i}
so generated begin with:

\displaystyle f^{(0)}(z,\tau)

\displaystyle\equiv 1\ ,\quad f^{(1)}(z,\tau)=\partial\ln\vartheta_{1}(z,\tau)+2\pi i\,\frac{{\rm Im\,}z}{{\rm Im\,}\tau}\ ,

(2.11)

\displaystyle f^{(2)}(z,\tau)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2}\Big\{\Big(\partial\ln\vartheta_{1}(z,\tau)+2\pi i\,\frac{{\rm Im\,}z}{{\rm Im\,}\tau}\Big)^{2}+\partial^{2}\ln\vartheta_{1}(z,\tau)-\frac{\vartheta_{1}^{\prime\prime\prime}(0,\tau)}{3\vartheta_{1}^{\prime}(0,\tau)}\Big\}\ ,

(2.12)

which are actually all the
f^{(i)}
we need for the purposes of this paper.
Here,
f^{(1)}
is the only singular term of (
2.10
) with a simple pole at lattice points
z=n+m\tau
with
m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. We will suppress the dependence on the modular parameter
\tau
in the following. (For the reader who is not comfortable with this type of function, we only ask for patience: in the next sections, taking the field theory limit will collapse them to polynomials.)

By using results from the maximally supersymmetric case (cf. (3.20)-(3.22) in
[
5
]
), we find:

\mathcal{G}_{2+2}(\gamma,-\gamma,x_{1},x_{2})=1\ ,

(2.13)

and

\mathcal{G}_{3+2}(\gamma,-\gamma,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}f_{i}^{(1)}\ ,

(2.14)

since
f^{(1)}(\gamma)=-f^{(1)}(-\gamma)
, and

\mathcal{G}_{4+2}(\gamma,-\gamma,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=F^{(2)}_{1/2}(\gamma)+\sum_{i=1}^{4}f_{i}^{(2)}+\sum_{1\leq j<k}^{4}f_{j}^{(1)}\,f_{k}^{(1)}\ .

(2.15)

For the interested reader, this can be seen as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{4+2}(\gamma,-\gamma,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})

\displaystyle=f^{(2)}(\gamma)+f^{(2)}(-\gamma)+\sum_{i=1}^{4}f_{i}^{(2)}+f^{(1)}(\gamma)f^{(1)}(-\gamma)

(2.16)

\displaystyle+f^{(1)}(\gamma)\sum_{i=1}^{4}f_{i}^{(1)}+f^{(1)}(-\gamma)\sum_{i=1}^{4}f_{i}^{(1)}+\sum_{1\leq j<k}^{4}f_{j}^{(1)}f_{k}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=2f^{(2)}(\gamma)-(f^{(1)}(\gamma))^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{4}f_{i}^{(2)}+\sum_{1\leq j<k}^{4}f_{j}^{(1)}\,f_{k}^{(1)}\ ,

where we used even- and oddness of
f^{(2)}
and
f^{(1)}
, respectively, and

F^{(2)}_{1/2}(\gamma)\equiv 2f^{(2)}(\gamma)-(f^{(1)}(\gamma))^{2}\ .

(2.17)

We see that the orbifold twist

\gamma
only appears in spin sums with at least four fermion insertions,
\mathcal{G}_{4+2}
.

2.2
Automating the worldsheet Wick contractions

This algorithm of Wick’s theorem application is based on
[
54
]
. We put the operators into a list (with the exponentials being placed last), that we call
list
, and apply the following recursive steps:

1.

Extract the pair-wise contraction of the first and
k
th
elements of
list
with the appropriate sign, according to the number of anti-commuting operators that had to be switched. Start with
k=2
.

2.

Alter the
list
according to the following criteria: if the two-point function of the contracted elements does not contain an operator, drop the two elements from the
list
. The other case manifests only in the bosonic sector, where contractions of
\partial X
with the exponentials retain the exponentials. In this case drop only the non-exponential element from the
list
.

3.

Apply the procedure to the altered
list
.

4.

Run until
k
equals the length of
list
.

5.

Break if
list
is empty or if it contains only the exponentials. In the latter case, the correlation function is the Koba-Nielsen factor

\Pi_{n}\equiv\biggl\langle\prod_{r=1}^{n}e^{k_{r}\cdot X(z_{r})}\biggr\rangle=\prod_{1\leq i<j}^{n}e^{s_{ij}G_{ij}}\,.

(2.18)

2.3
Closed-string four-point scattering amplitudes

The integration measure of
n
-point string scattering amplitudes on the torus in
D=4
or
6
uncompactified dimensions is given by

\int d\rho^{D}{{}_{12...n}}=\frac{(\alpha^{\prime})^{n}V{{}_{D}}}{8N}\int_{\mathcal{F}}\frac{\text{d}^{2}\tau}{(4\pi^{2}\alpha^{\prime}\tau_{2})^{D/2}}\int_{\mathcal{T}(\tau)^{n}}\text{d}^{2}z_{1}\text{d}^{2}z_{2}...\text{d}^{2}z_{n}\,\delta^{2}(z_{1},\bar{z}_{1})\Pi_{n}\ ,

(2.19)

as in eq.(3.61) in
[
5
]
2
2

2

There is a factor of
(\alpha^{\prime})^{n}
missing in that formula.
. The torus modulus
\tau
is integrated over the fundamental domain,

\mathcal{F}:\left|\mathrm{Re}(\tau)\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\text{ and }\left|\tau\right|\geq 1\ ,

(2.20)

and the insertions are integrated over the torus
\mathcal{T}(\tau)
, parametrised by
\tau
.

The integrand for a half-maximal supersymmetric orbifold is the sum of the maximally supersymmetric term and the orbifold twisted terms, eqs. (3.64)-(3.67) in
[
5
]
, so that the amplitude becomes

\mathcal{M}_{1/2}(1,2,\dots,n)=\int\text{d}\rho_{12\dots n}^{D}\ \Bigg\{\Gamma\,\mathcal{J}_{n,\text{max}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,k^{\prime}=0\\
(k,k^{\prime})\neq(0,0)\end{subarray}}^{N-1}\!\hat{\chi}_{k,k^{\prime}}\,\mathcal{J}_{n,1/2}(\vec{v}_{k,k^{\prime}})\Bigg\}\ ,

(2.21)

where
\Gamma
is the sum over winding and KK momentum states,
\vec{v}_{k,k^{\prime}}\equiv(k+k^{\prime}\tau)(v,-v)
and
\hat{\chi}_{k,k^{\prime}}
denotes the degeneracies of twisted states, see discussion in appendix A in
[
5
]
.

To calculate the closed-string
four-point
one-loop amplitudes in half-maximal supersymmetry, we divide and conquer: divide each sector of the amplitude into the contributions from the zero-mode contractions of left-and right moving bosonic operators, and contributions from the left and right correlation functions.

2.3.1
Closed-string separated correlators

Due to the exponentials remaining in the correlation function after a contraction with a bosonic operator, we can think of them as appearing in both left- and right-moving bosonic correlation functions. We only need to remember that when combining the two contributions there is only one Koba-Nielsen factor
\Pi_{4}
, not two.
Hence, the complete contribution from the non-zero-modes (non-Z.M.) to the half-maximal closed-string correlator is

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{4,1/2}(\vec{v}_{k,k^{\prime}})\Big|_{\text{non-Z.M.}}

\displaystyle=\Big\{\mathcal{J}_{4,1/2}^{e,\tilde{e}}(\vec{v}_{k,k^{\prime}})+\mathcal{J}_{4,1/2}^{e,\tilde{o}}(\vec{v}_{k,k^{\prime}})+\mathcal{J}_{4,1/2}^{o,\tilde{e}}(\vec{v}_{k,k^{\prime}})+\mathcal{J}_{4,D=6}^{o,\tilde{o}}\Big\}\Bigg|_{\text{non-Z.M.}}

(2.22)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{4,1/2}^{e}\,\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{4,1/2}^{e}+\mathcal{I}_{4,1/2}^{e}\,\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{4,D=6}^{o}+\mathcal{I}_{4,D=6}^{o}\,\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{4,1/2}^{e}+\mathcal{I}_{4,D=6}^{o}\,\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{4,D=6}^{o}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{4,1/2}\,\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{4,1/2}\ ,

where
\mathcal{I}_{4,1/2}^{e}
and
\mathcal{I}_{4,D=6}^{o}
refer to the contribution of even and odd spin structures,and are given explicitly in eqs. (4.22) and (4.48) in
[
5
]
, respectively. The
D=6
delineation in the odd terms points to the fact that they only occur in this case and drop out for
D=4
, and where
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{4,1/2}
is understood to map
e_{i}^{m}\rightarrow\tilde{e}_{i}^{m}
and
f_{ij}^{(1)}\rightarrow\bar{f}_{ij}^{(1)}

2.3.2
Closed-string zero-mode bosonic contractions

In the four-point case we only need to consider up to two zero-mode contractions since otherwise there would not be enough world-sheet fermions left in the correlator in order to lead to a non-vanishing result after spin structure summation. In the case of left-right interactions sector by sector, one gets:

\mathcal{J}_{4,1/2}^{e,\tilde{e}}\Big|_{\text{Z.M.}}=\zeta\ \mathcal{I}_{4,1/2}^{m,\,e}\,\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{4,1/2}^{m,\,e}+\frac{1}{2}\zeta^{2}\ \mathcal{I}_{4,1/2}^{mn,\,e}\,\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{4,1/2}^{mn,\,e}\ ,

(2.23)

\mathcal{J}_{4,1/2}^{e,\tilde{o}}\Big|_{\text{Z.M.}}=\zeta\ \mathcal{I}_{4,1/2}^{m,\,e}\,\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{4,D=6}^{m,\,o}+\frac{1}{2}\zeta^{2}\ \mathcal{I}_{4,1/2}^{mn,\,e}\,\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{4,D=6}^{mn,\,o}\ ,

(2.24)

\mathcal{J}_{4,D=6}^{o,\tilde{o}}\Big|_{\text{Z.M.}}=\zeta\ \mathcal{I}_{4,D=6}^{m,\,o}\,\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{4,D=6}^{m,\,o}+\frac{1}{2}\zeta^{2}\ \mathcal{I}_{4,D=6}^{mn,\,o}\,\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{4,D=6}^{mn,\,o}\ ,

[

The above conspire together to

\mathcal{I}_{4,1/2}^{m}=\mathcal{I}_{4,1/2}^{m,\,e}+\mathcal{I}_{4,D=6}^{m,\,o}\ ,\qquad\mathcal{I}_{4,1/2}^{mn}=\mathcal{I}_{4,1/2}^{mn,\,e}+\mathcal{I}_{4,D=6}^{mn,\,o}\ ,

4
Field theory limit

The field theory limit of string amplitudes is computed by examining the S-matrix in the low-energy limit, i.e.,
\alpha^{\prime}\rightarrow 0
, which sends the massive states to infinity, while simultaneously degenerating the genus-one surface to a worldline by sending
\tau_{2}\equiv\mathrm{Im}(\tau)\rightarrow\infty
. The limits are taken such that
\alpha^{\prime}\tau_{2}
is kept finite and reduces to the worldline length,
\alpha^{\prime}\tau_{2}\xrightarrow[\text{}]{\text{f.t}}t
, in the Schwinger parametrization of the corresponding field theory diagrams.
3
3

3

In order to avoid the proliferation of meanings of the arrow sign,

will henceforth denote field theory limits only.
Defining
\nu_{i}
by
\mathrm{Im}(z_{i})\equiv\tau_{2}\,\nu_{i}
identifies them with the proper times in the worldline reduction of the original string worldsheet.

Outlook

In essence, this paper is about connecting a few existing amplitude techniques used in field theory and string theory, and implementing
them in such a way that they can at least in principle be automated.
We have merely scratched the surface of this topic, so there are many directions left unexplored.

Perhaps the most glaring omission is that of helicity amplitudes. It would be straightforward to plug in the
helicity expressions for the Berends-Giele currents from
[
6
]
here. But doing only this would be incomplete:
to take our method seriously, one should recalculate amplitudes in a massive helicity formalism, like
[
1
]
,
and then take the soft/collinear limit.

On a related note, we intentionally structured this work so it will apply straightforwardly to six dimensions,
where the hexagon amplitude is a priori the generating function. (We say “a priori”, because here it
was the box, but we got away with using the triangle, since for all-mass, it is related to the box.)
The massless helicity formalism in
D=6

[
18
,
8
]

is related but not identical to some massive helicity formalisms in
D=4
, so this could usefully be connected
to the previous question.

Clearly there is much left to do about details of KLT relations at one-loop. The amplitudes we study here
do not obviously show double-copy structure after integration, but see e.g.
[
49
,
50
]

for an argument that this should be possible to make manifest.

One aspect of amplitudes that was important in this work is the sub-hierarchy of graph topologies
for each topology (“bubbly box”, etc.). It would be interesting to learn whether techniques like in
[
34
]

to represent this graphically could make this more manifest.

As mentioned in section
6.1
, there is by now an extensive literature on single-valued polylogarithms and generalizations thereof,
the work most closely related to that here being
[
46
]
. We have nothing new to say about it here,
but we believe that our work could help connect these powerful mathematical methods
to more conventional Feynman integral techniques used in particle physics.

Then there is the purely mathematical side of amplitudes. In
[
16
]
, Brown discusses Feynman integrals from the point of view of cohomology. He effectively also uses all-mass objects, like here. It would be interesting to understand this connection better.

Appendix A
Berends-Giele currents at one-loop

For the purposes of this paper, a sufficient motivation for introducing the kinematic factors
C_{i|jkl}
,
C^{m}_{i|jkl}
and
C^{mn}_{i|jkl}

is that they compactly represent our amplitude of interest. However, here we provide a little more detail
on the background, for readers that are not familiar with Berends-Giele currents. We hope this can entice such readers to study the original papers,
where motivation for the structure of these objects is provided from resummation of Feynman diagrams in field theory,
and from operator product expansions (OPEs) and BRST invariance in string theory.

Linearized gauge transformations take polarizations
e_{i}^{m}
into momenta
k_{i}^{m}
:

\delta e_{i}^{m}=k_{i}^{m}\ .

(A.1)

The gauge-invariant linearized field strength in terms of the polarization vector

is:

f_{i}^{mn}\equiv k_{i}^{m}e_{i}^{n}-k_{i}^{n}e_{i}^{m}\ ,\ \ \ \ \ \ \delta f_{i}^{mn}=0\ .

(A.2)

The purpose of this appendix is to illustrate how to construct multiparticle generalizations of the gauge-invariant linearized field strength (
A.2
).
The strategy of Berends-Giele
[
4
]
is to allow one off-shell leg and weaken the demand of invariance under gauge transformations
to only covariance. Also, following BCJ, we want to weaken the requirement of
no negative powers of Mandelstams, i.e. allow some nonlocal-looking expressions
in intermediate steps, but only in judiciously chosen places. Then in the final step, we demand that gauge variations
and negative powers of certain Mandelstams
cancel, and assemble physical, gauge-invariant kinematic factors
C_{i|jkl}
,
C^{m}_{i|jkl}
and
C^{mn}_{i|jkl}
for one-loop amplitudes.

19

19

As discussed in
[
5
]
,

is only gauge-pseudo-invariant in
D=6
. Here we focus our discussion on
D=4
. Moreover, with “physical” we mean that the only allowed poles in Mandelstam variables are those which appear in a local theory, i.e. a theory based on a local Lagrangian. For instance,
C_{1|234}
in (
A.23
) below is equal to the color ordered tree level 4-point partial gluon amplitude and consists of the sum of contact terms and exchange terms with simple poles. Hence, all terms in the bracket in (

D.2.1
Expanding hypergeometric representations

Hypergeometric representations are not necessarily the most convenient. But it is a standard language that symbolic manipulation software like Mathematica likes to use.

Polylogarithms and the Lerch function

Here is the Lerch function:

\Phi(z,s,\alpha)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{z^{n}\over(n+\alpha)^{s}}

(D.20)

Note that it starts at zero,
so if we set
\alpha=1
(
\epsilon=0
) it reduces to
1/z
times a polylogarithm:

\Phi(z,s,1)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{z^{n}\over(n+1)^{s}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{z^{n-1}\over n^{s}}={1\over z}\,{\rm Li}_{s}(z)\;.

(D.21)

To connect with e.g. (
D.19
) above, note that one of the specific hypergeometric
function of interest in the basic amplitude calculations has arguments
a=1
,
b=\epsilon
,
1+\epsilon
, so it is a Lerch function:

{}_{2}F_{1}(1,\epsilon;1+\epsilon;z)\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{(1)_{n}(\epsilon)_{n}\over(1+\epsilon)_{n}}{z^{n}\over n!}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{n!\,\epsilon\over n+\epsilon}{z^{n}\over n!}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\epsilon\over n+\epsilon}{z^{n}}=\epsilon\,\Phi(z,1,\epsilon)

(D.22)

since these particular Pochhammer symbols combine:

\displaystyle(1)_{n}

\displaystyle={\Gamma(1+n)\over\Gamma(1)}=n!

(D.23)

\displaystyle(\epsilon)_{n}

\displaystyle={\Gamma(\epsilon+n)\over\Gamma(\epsilon)}

\displaystyle(1+\epsilon)_{n}

\displaystyle={\Gamma(1+\epsilon+n)\over\Gamma(1+\epsilon)}={(\epsilon+n)\Gamma(\epsilon+n)\over\epsilon\Gamma(\epsilon)}={n+\epsilon\over\epsilon}(\epsilon)_{n}\;.

There is an overall
1/\epsilon
in the usual representation of the scalar box, so in fact

\Phi(z,1,\epsilon)
does seem like a more natural way to express it
than
(1/\epsilon)\cdot{}_{2}F_{1}(1,\epsilon,1+\epsilon;z)
. In particular,
the middle argument of

\Phi
is redundantly equal to 1, but it can be deformed to
s\neq 1
for zeta function regularization, that the hypergeometric function doesn’t provide,
since by definition it has only integer powers.

What
is the leading term of the hypergeometric function in the above form
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
? It is trivial to see that
\Phi(z,1,\epsilon)
has a pole at
\epsilon=0
of residue one:

\Phi(z,1,\epsilon)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{1\over n+\epsilon}{z^{n}}={1\over\epsilon}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{1\over n+\epsilon}{z^{n}}={1\over\epsilon}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{0})\;,

(D.24)

so in fact
{}_{2}F_{1}(1,\epsilon,1+\epsilon;z)\rightarrow 1
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, which we will be able to use below.

Here is a minor generalization: for any
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b,c;z)
with
a=1
and
c=b+1
, there will be a representation
in terms of
\Phi
.

Lerch as generating function of polylogs

It is easy to expand
\Phi
in the third argument,
since this follows directly from the sum representation:

{d\over d\alpha}\Phi(z,s,\alpha)=-s\Phi(z,s+1,\alpha)

(D.25)

so the
4-2\epsilon
expansion is

\Phi(z,s,1+\epsilon)=\Phi(z,s,1)-s\epsilon\Phi(z,s+1,1)+\ldots={1\over z}\left({\rm Li}_{s}(z)-s\epsilon{\rm Li}_{s+1}(z)+\ldots\right)

(D.26)

and in particular we want
s=1
, and we bring up the
z
in front
and find the simple result

z\Phi(z,1,1+\epsilon)={\rm Li}_{1}(z)-\epsilon{\rm Li}_{2}(z)+\ldots

(D.27)

so
z\Phi(z,1,1+\epsilon)
generates polylogarithms.

Now note the general shift relation in the last argument:

\Phi(z,s,1+\alpha)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{z^{n}\over(n+1+\alpha)^{s}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{z^{n-1}\over(n+\alpha)^{s}}={1\over z}\left(\Phi(z,s,\alpha)-{1\over\alpha^{s}}\right)\;,

(D.28)

where we explicitly subtracted the
n=0
term in the definition of
\Phi
. Inverting, we see that

\Phi(z,s,\epsilon)=z\Phi(z,s,1+\epsilon)+{1\over\epsilon^{s}}

(D.29)

so the form we had in (
D.22
) for
the hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{1}(1,\epsilon,1+\epsilon;z)
has the expansion

\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}(1,\epsilon;1+\epsilon;z)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\,\Phi(z,1,\epsilon)

(D.30)

\displaystyle=1+\epsilon z\Phi(z,1,1+\epsilon)

\displaystyle=1+\epsilon{\rm Li}_{1}(z)-\epsilon^{2}{\rm Li}_{2}(z)+\ldots\ ,

where we inserted the polylogarithm-generating expansion (
D.27
) for
z\Phi(z,s,1+\epsilon)
.

D.3
Triangles in
D=4
- simplified differentiation method

In the triangle there are three Feynman parameters, call them
a_{1},a_{2}
and
a_{3}
. The tensor triangle

depends on the three virtualities

through the second Symanzik polynomial (in

(D.32)

Here we assume that although the virtualities
p_{i}^{2}
can be arbitrarily small, they are all nonzero.
Then all integrals considered here are finite, so we can freely move derivatives through integral signs.

Claim
: With
u=\mathsf{p}_{1}^{2}/\mathsf{p}_{3}^{2}
,
v=\mathsf{p}_{2}^{2}/\mathsf{p}_{3}^{2}
, the tensor triangles
are obtained from the scalar triangle as

I_{3}[a_{1}^{m}a_{2}^{n}](u,v)={(-1)^{m+n}\over(m+n)!}\left(u^{m}{\partial^{m}\over\partial u^{m}}\right)\!\left(v^{n}{\partial^{n}\over\partial v^{n}}\right)I_{3}[1](u,v)\;.

(D.33)

Here’s the proof.
In Duplancic-Nizic
[
25
]
eq. (25), we find the
double Mellin representation of the all-mass
scalar
triangle in
D=4
(we suppress the Feynman
i\varepsilon
for now):

I_{3}[1](u,v)={1\over\mathsf{p}_{3}^{2}}\int_{C_{s}}\!ds\int_{C_{t}}\!dt\;M^{2}(s,t)\,u^{-s}v^{-t}

(D.34)

for Mellin contours
C_{s}
and
C_{t}
, that are vertical contours in the complex
s
and
t
planes, respectively,
with real parts between 0 and 1.
The Mellin kernel
M^{2}
couples the Mellin variables
s
and
t
:

M^{2}(s,t)=\Big(\Gamma(s)\Gamma(t)\Gamma(1-(s+t))\Big)^{2}

(D.35)

This Mellin kernel is a product of two equal factors, hence the name
M^{2}
.
In
[
25
]
eq. (25), one factor
M
arises from a something that looks suspiciously like a string tree integral, but over the two remaining Feynman parameters (i.e. after using the delta function):

M(s,t)=\int_{0}^{1}da_{1}\;a_{1}^{s-1}(1-a_{1})^{-s}\int_{0}^{1}d\tilde{a}_{2}\;\tilde{a}_{2}^{t-1}(1-\tilde{a}_{2})^{-(s+t)}=\Gamma(s)\Gamma(t)\Gamma(1-(s+t))\;.

(D.36)

The original Feynman parameter
a_{2}
is related to
\tilde{a}_{2}
as
a_{2}=(1-a_{1})\tilde{a}_{2}
.

Now consider the all-mass
tensor
triangle (
D.31
). We need to generalize the
integral (
D.36
) to insertion of arbitrary positive integer powers
a_{1}^{m}a_{2}^{n}
of the original Feynman parameters. This is easy:

\int_{0}^{1}da_{1}\;a_{1}^{m}(1-a_{1})^{n}a_{1}^{s-1}(1-a_{1})^{-s}\int_{0}^{1}d\tilde{a}_{2}\;\tilde{a}_{2}^{n}\,\tilde{a}_{2}^{t-1}(1-\tilde{a}_{2})^{-(s+t)}={\Gamma(s+m)\Gamma(t+n)\Gamma(1-(s+t))\over(m+n)!}\;.

(D.37)

Now we make the trivial observation that differentiating

in (

\displaystyle\left(u^{m}{\partial^{m}\over\partial u^{m}}\right)\!\left(v^{n}{\partial^{n}\over\partial v^{n}}\right)u^{-s}v^{-t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{m}s(s+1)\cdots(s+m-1)u^{-s}\cdot(-1)^{n}t(t+1)\cdots(t+n-1)v^{-t}

(D.38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{m+n}{\Gamma(s+m)\Gamma(t+n)\over\Gamma(s)\Gamma(t)}u^{-s}v^{-t}\;.

Now combine the forefactor in (
D.38

D.36

D.36

D.37

D.33
).
