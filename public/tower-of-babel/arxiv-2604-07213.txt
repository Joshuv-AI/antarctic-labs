Diffusion Processes on Implicit Manifolds
Victor Kawasaki-Borruat, Clara Grotehans, Pierre Vandergheynst, Adam Gosztolai

Abstract
High-dimensional data are often modeled as lying near a low-dimensional manifold. We study how to construct diffusion processes on this data manifold in the implicit setting. That is, using only point cloud samples and without access to charts, projections, or other geometric primitives. Our main contribution is a data-driven SDE that captures intrinsic diffusion on the underlying manifold while being defined in ambient space. The construction relies on estimating the diffusion’s infinitesimal generator and its carré-du-champ (CDC) from a proximity graph built from the data. The generator and CDC together encode the local stochastic and geometric structure of the intended diffusion. We show that, as the number of samples grows, the induced process converges in law on the space of probability paths to its smooth manifold counterpart. We call this construction Implicit Manifold-valued Diffusions (IMDs), and furthermore present a numerical simulation procedure using Euler–Maruyama integration. This gives a rigorous basis for practical implementations of diffusion dynamics on data manifolds, and opens new directions for manifold-aware sampling, exploration, and generative modeling.

Background on diffusion processes on manifolds

Here we provide the necessary background on constructing stochastic processes on Riemannian manifolds.

3.1
Calculus on Riemannian manifolds

Let (
\mathcal{M}
, g) be a complete,
d
-dimensional, connected Riemannian manifold without boundary. For each
z\in\mathcal{M}
, the Riemannian metric
g
defines an inner product on the tangent space
T_{z}\mathcal{M}

(
26
)

\langle U,V\rangle_{g}:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}g_{ij}U^{i}V^{j},

(2)

for vectors
U(z),V(z)\in T_{z}\mathcal{M}
, where
g_{ij}
denotes the metric tensor in local coordinates.
The metric
g
allows defining the gradient
\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}
of a function
b\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
in local (manifold-centric) coordinate basis
(\partial_{1},\dots,\partial_{d})\in T_{z}\mathcal{M}

\left(\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}b\right)^{i}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}g^{ij}\partial_{j}b,

(3)

where
g^{ij}:=g_{ij}^{-1}
is the Riemannian co-metric.
The associated Laplace–Beltrami operator is then defined as

\Delta_{\mathcal{M}}b:=\operatorname{div}(\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}b).

(4)

To work with data densities, we assume
\mathcal{M}
is oriented and define a volume form
\textnormal{dvol}_{g}
, and the corresponding unit volume
\textnormal{Vol}(\mathcal{M}):=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\textnormal{dvol}_{g}
, which gives rise to a measure on
\mathcal{M}
.

We will call variables in local coordinates, e.g.,
z:=(z^{1},\dots,z^{d})\in\mathcal{M}
as intrinsic and the corresponding differential operators
\nabla_{\mathcal{M}},\Delta_{\mathcal{M}}
will define the intrinsic geometry. We may also define extrinsic variables
x:=(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
through an isometric embedding into Euclidean space
F:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
1

1

F
’s existence is guaranteed by Nash’s isometric embedding theorem for large enough
n
.
We will drop subscripts to imply the usual Euclidean differential operators (
\nabla,\Delta
).

3.2
Stochastic processes on manifolds from infinitesimal generators

We now discuss the construction of stochastic processes over manifolds by adopting an operator-theoretic perspective.
For the differential operators
\nabla_{\mathcal{M}},\Delta_{\mathcal{M}}
it is a classical result
(
60
)
that for any smooth test function
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
, the SDE from Eq. (
1
) has an associated partial differential equation description

\partial_{t}f=Lf,

(5)

where
L
is called the infinitesimal generator

L=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a^{ij}(z)\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{j}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}b^{i}(z)\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{i}},

(6)

with
b(z)
is a drift field and
a(z)=\sigma\sigma^{\top}
is the local noise covariance, both restricted to
T_{z}\mathcal{M}
.
Note that the scaled Laplace-Beltrami operator
\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\mathcal{M}}
is a special case for
b(z)=0
,
\sigma(z)=a(z)=\mathbb{I}
, and corresponds to an isotropic Brownian motion
dZ_{t}=dB_{t}^{\mathcal{M}}
on
\mathcal{M}
whose invariant law is the volume measure
\textnormal{dvol}_{g}

(
37
;
33
;
3
)
.

While
\nabla_{\mathcal{M}},\Delta_{\mathcal{M}}
allow defining a stochastic process on the manifold, these objects are generally not available for implicit manifolds. Instead, one may construct these objects directly from data based on diffusion geometry
(
41
)
, a formalism that recovers differential calculus over manifolds using local operators that can be estimated from data.
The central object in diffusion geometry is the
carré-du-champ (CDC)

\Gamma(f,h)=L(fh)-f{L}h-h{L}f,

(7)

which relates the data-induced operator
\Gamma
to the infinitesimal generator
L
of a stochastic process.
Note that for
\Delta_{\mathcal{M}}
the CDC reduces to the usual Riemannian metric in Eq. (
2
)
(

)
.
Further, as the following theorem from Ref.

to an equivalent ambient-space

generating

(

)

Let

be ambient coordinates,

\tilde{a}^{ij}:=\Gamma(f^{i},f^{j}),\quad\tilde{v}^{i}:=Lf^{i},

The operator

should be understood as an ambient representation of the intrinsic generator

Theorem

4
Data-driven construction of diffusions on implicit manifolds

Given that any diffusion on
\mathcal{M}
with second-order generator
L
can be represented in the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
via
\Gamma
(Theorem
3.1

X_{N}:=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

\textnormal{dvol}_{g}

\mathcal{M}

Our construction is based on random walks on graphs. For a global neighbourhood radius
\epsilon>0
, we define the edge-defining binary matrix

W_{ij}:=\mathbf{1}\left\{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\epsilon\right\},

(12)

which we symmetrize by imposing
W_{ij}\overset{!}{=}W_{ji}
. We denote
m_{i}:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}W_{ij}
the node-wise normalization constant, and the associated row-stochastic random walk matrix is given by

P_{ij}:=\frac{W_{ij}}{m_{i}}.

(13)

We can now define the discrete random walk graph Laplacian (RWGL),

(\mathsf{L}_{N}u)(x_{i}):=\frac{c}{\epsilon^{2}}\sum_{i\sim j}\frac{W_{ij}}{m_{i}}\left(u(x_{i})-u(x_{j})\right),

(14)

where
u:X_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function over the nodes, and
c>0
is a fixed scaling constant
(
30
)
. Eq. (
14
) corresponds to the standard RWGL
\mathsf{L}_{N}=I_{N\times N}-P

(
22
)
, up to the constant factor
\frac{c}{\epsilon^{2}}

Proving the convergence of
\mathsf{L}_{N}
to the corresponding continuous operator requires interpolating the Laplacian

\mathsf{L}:=\mathsf{P}_{N}^{*}\,\mathsf{L}_{N}\,\mathsf{P}_{N}:H\to H,

(15)

where
\mathsf{P}_{N}:H\to H_{N}
and
\mathsf{P}_{N}^{*}:H_{N}\to H
are identification maps between the Hilbert spaces of
L^{2}
-functions on the data
H_{N}:=L^{2}(X_{N},\mathbb{R})
and on the manifold
H:=L^{2}(\mathcal{M},\mathbb{R})

Intuitively,
\mathsf{P}_{N}
is already present through the action of the embedding
F
, and
\mathsf{P}_{N}^{*}
acts as a nearest-neighbour approximator, mapping a function assignment

to

, where
x_{j}=\argmin_{x\in X_{N}}\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}
is the closest data point.

We may now define a data-driven version of the SDE (

d\mathsf{X}_{t}=\biggl(\mathsf{L}(\mathsf{X}_{t})+b(\mathsf{X}_{t})\biggr)dt+\mathsf{\Gamma}^{1/2}(\mathsf{X}_{t})dW_{t},

(16)

where
\mathsf{\Gamma}
is the CDC operator (

from Eq. (

By Theorem

6
Experiments

We evaluate IMDs on both synthetic and real-world data to highlight three key properties:

1.

Geometric Fidelity.
The IMD, despite being agnostic to the geometry of
\mathcal{M}
, does not deviate from its surface. Moreover, we show that the error does not accumulate.

2.

Statistical Fidelity.
The law of the generated process on
C[0,T]
matches its theoretical counterpart, providing experimental evidence for Theorem
4.1
.

3.

Better Generalization.
The tangential dynamics that IMDs provide are strictly orthogonal to the "normal" directions of traditional score-based generative models (SGMs). We demonstrate on the MNIST dataset that IMDs allow for a smooth interpolation between two datapoints, which is inaccessible to SGMs.

We refer the reader to Appendix
F
for details on the experimental setups.

7
Limitations and Outlook

Higher dimensions

The computation of the graph Laplacian relies on a kernel
k_{\epsilon}
, which inherently suffers from the curse of dimensionality of kernel methods
(
64
)
. We however note that recent work proposes a neural surrogate to compute the CDC
\Gamma
, offering an interesting computationally tractable option for larger datasets
4
.

Non-asymptotic rates

Deriving finite sample bounds on the convergence of Theorems
4.1
and
5.1
is a clear extension of this work, but requires significant additional theoretical analysis and is thus outside of the scope of this work. We note that some non-asymptotic spectral convergence rates for Laplacians are derived in
(
68
)
for the flat torus.

Generative Modeling

We believe the interplay of tangent and normal directions to be a very important tool in the analysis of generative modeling under the manifold hypothesis. An interesting avenue for future work would involve combining both to consider noisy data, and the evolving geometry along generative dynamics. Furthermore, we believe that extending IMDs to endpoint-conditioned processes (conditioned diffusion, Schrödinger bridges) may offer computationally inexpensive and stable alternatives for exploring data manifolds.

8
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced Implicit Manifold-valued Diffusions, a mathematically rigorous methodology to simulate stochastic processes of the diffusion type on data manifolds. We demonstrated that IMDs converge to their smooth theoretical counterpart in the large sample limit, enabling simulations of Langevin-type processes on manifolds only defined through point clouds. Moreover, the proposed tangential dynamics on the data manifold complement current literature on score-based sampling, and provide evidence that tangential manifold dynamics enable coherent cross-basin exploration beyond nearest-neighbour memorization-like behavior.

under the embedding map

. By the continuous mapping theorem, these processes inherit smoothness, whereby

\mathsf{X}_{t}\implies Y_{t}\qquad\text{in }C([0,T];F(\mathcal{M})),

(50)

proving the claim.

∎

Appendix F
Implementation Details

F.1
Graph Construction

All experiments (except for MNIST) used the hard-cutoff kernel of bandwidth
\epsilon>0
from Eq. (
12
). In the case of the MNIST dataset, we used a fully-connected Gaussian kernel

k_{\epsilon}(x_{i},x_{j}):=\exp\left(\frac{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right).

(60)

This non-compact kernel is not covered by the current convergence proof, although prior work
30
indicates the current framework should appeal to such kernels. We thus disclaim here that the MNIST experiments should be interpreted as empirical evidence, rather than fully theorem-backed instantiations. We compute the random walk graph Laplacian in the same way in both cases, using Eq. (
14
).

The CDC operator is computed by considering its action on coordinate functions. At each timestep
\ell
of Algorithm
5.1
, we extend
\mathsf{L}_{N}
to
\mathsf{L}
as in Eq. (
15
) by using a nearest-neighbour (NN) or
k
-NN lookup.

Remark 4
.

Note that the NN lookup is generally controllable as per Assumption
1
, and
k
-NN lookup is a Lipschitz map. As we are directly considering the extrinsic setting of
F(\mathcal{M})
, all functions are already considered lifted.

Remark 5
.

We also do not use the Diffusion Maps algorithm
23
, as the variable-bandwidth graph Laplacian construction does not fit into the framework presented in Appendix
C
. We believe this to be an important future direction for rigorous IMD constructions, but leave it for future work.

F.2
Score Model

We train a noise-conditional score network

[
38
]
following the multi-scale noise formulation of
57
,
58
using denoising score matching.
The synthetic data’s network’s training is parametrized with a fully-connected residual architecture on a noise-prediction loss. For MNIST we parametrize the score-model with a U-Net encoder-decoder architecture with skip connections at each spatial resolution
[
55

42
]
.

For the noise conditioning, we embed the noise level via a Gaussian random Fourier feature layer

and inject it as a bias into the first linear layer (MLP for synthetic data) or as a per-channel bias at every convolutional block (U-Net for MNIST). We use a geometric schedule of noise levels with 20 different noise levels ranging (

) for the synthetic examples and 100 noise levels ranging (

Training Details

For the synthetic data, we trained a 3 residual layer network of 128 hidden dimensions for 200 epochs with a fixed learning rate of
2\times 10^{-4}
on the Adam optimizer. For MNIST, we trained a three-level U-Net operating on single-channel
28\!\times\!28

The noise scale

F.3
Euler-Maruyama Parameters

As the Euler-Maruyama scheme is fairly simple, we only discuss choice os step size and an extra parameter we call the speedup. All experiments were performed with a step size
h=10^{-3}
. However, as certain datasets were not normalized (as is the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
), we included a speedup term, which effectively stretches the step size. This was done in order to avoid increasing the "physical time"

1.0
for the sphere,
100.0
on the swiss roll and
10.0
on MNIST. The particular number of steps was chosen per experiment, depending on what aspect of IMDs was to be highlighted.

G.2

To test IMDs on implicit manifolds, we considered the Swiss roll dataset. Because neighbours in Euclidean distance may not correspond to those in geodesic distance, unconstrained or projection-based dynamics may induce transitions across nearby folds that are distant along the manifold.
