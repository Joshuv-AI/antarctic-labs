ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the deadliest cancers due to its asymptomatic progression, late‐stage diagnosis, and treatment resistance. Efforts in early detection have centered on identifying imaging features and liquid biopsy biomarkers capable of detecting PDAC and its high‐grade precursors before clinical symptoms arise in patients at elevated risk of PDAC development. Classical imaging‐based surveillance strategies, aligned with current guidelines, form the foundation of screening high‐risk individuals, while organ‐specific fluid analyses—such as cyst fluid and pancreatic juice—offer promising complementary tools with enhanced specificity. Recent advances in radiomics, liquid biopsy, microbiome, and multi‐omics profiling are expanding the frontier of early detection. Despite advances, significant challenges persist. Precursor lesions are difficult to non‐invasively diagnose; are not radiologically or endosonographically visible, or cannot be definitively graded prior to resection. Though biomarkers show promise for early detection, they present unique challenges: early‐stage neoplasias release low levels of many biomarkers and thresholds for these biomarkers that define malignant transformation—and thus guide surgical intervention—remain poorly established. Multi‐institutional initiatives like the Pancreatic Cancer Early Detection consortium (PRECEDE) are critical to bridging discovery and clinical translation. Our international cohort study of high‐risk individuals was designed to discover and validate diagnostic biomarkers according to the PROBE study design. Continued collaboration, technological integration, and patient‐centered approaches are essential to transform early detection research into tangible survival benefits for those at risk of PDAC.

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is the most common form of pancreatic cancer and its public health burden is considerable, with projections suggesting it will become the second leading cause of cancer‐related mortality by 2030 [1, 2, 3]. While long‐term survival rates have shown modest improvement, it has the poorest prognoses among solid tumors, with a 5‐year survival rate of just 13% [2]. Early detection represents an important path to improved outcomes. Currently, only about 15%–20% of PDAC patients are diagnosed with localized, potentially curable disease [2], largely due to the non‐specific nature and late‐onset of symptoms, combined with no general population screening recommendations due to the low disease incidence [4, 5]. Conceptual frameworks like Define–Enrich–Find can help focus screening efforts on high‐risk populations, reducing false positives and improving efficiency [6]. In this model, individuals with elevated risk—such as those with familial or genetic predisposition, new‐onset diabetes, chronic pancreatitis, or relevant lifestyle factors—are first identified [7, 8]. They are then further enriched using clinical risk models or biomarkers to determine who should undergo confirmatory imaging.

Targeted screening in high‐risk individuals is feasible [9] and recommended clinically for individuals with a lifetime risk exceeding 5%–10% [10]. This includes carriers of germline mutations associated with hereditary cancer syndromes (e.g., BRCA1, BRCA2, CDKN2A, STK11, ATM and genes related with Lynch Syndrome) and those with a family history of PDAC that meet the criteria for familial pancreatic cancer (FPC) [11]. Evidence from recent studies confirms the benefit of screening in these groups: a significantly higher proportion of stage I tumors are detected (31% vs. 10%), with improved 5‐year survival (50% vs. 9%) and reduced PDAC‐specific mortality (43% vs. 86%) in screened compared to unscreened Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program controls [12]. This analysis was adjusted for lead‐time bias, which is a significant concern in screening and early detection studies and can artificially inflate perceived survival rates and clinical benefit. This benefit is largely limited to tumors detected via screening and not to those that emerge between screening examinations, which tend to be more aggressive cancers.

Sensitive and accurate biomarkers are crucial to improve screening programs and diagnose pancreatic cancer at a potentially curable stage [10]. The successful translation of early detection biomarkers into routine clinical practice requires rigorous validation, standardized study designs, and stakeholder collaboration. One of the most foundational efforts in this space has been the Early Detection Research Network (EDRN), initiated in 2000, which established biomarker development and validation guidelines. These include the PRoBE (prospective‐specimen collection, retrospective‐blinded‐evaluation) study design framework, which emphasizes clinical context, performance criteria, appropriate sample size, and biomarker type [13, 14]. The PRoBE approach promotes the use of high‐quality biospecimens, rigorous blinding, and standardized reference sets—critical components that have accelerated the pace and reliability of biomarker discovery and validation [15, 16].

The PRECEDE consortium [9, 17] aims to address this need by validating promising early detection biomarkers through a large, international multi‐center cohort of high‐risk individuals, supported by prospectively collected, high‐quality biospecimens, clinical data, and imaging. Currently, the PRECEDE study is the largest PROBE compliant study for patients at high risk for PDAC. This review aims to provide a comprehensive, expert‐driven overview of liquid biopsy‐based biomarkers (Table 1) and imaging modalities for early detection and to highlight hurdles and key strategies for their successful clinical implementation.

Challenges in Early Detection of PDAC

The most common PDAC precursor lesions—pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasias (PanINs)—pose a fundamental problem. These lesions are microscopic, undetectable by current imaging or biopsy methods, and can only be confirmed by histopathology. Autopsy studies suggest that PanINs are highly prevalent in the general aging population, found in up to 86% of adult pancreata [25]. Yet, the biological trigger that transforms a low‐grade PanIN into a high‐grade lesion (PanIN‐3) and eventually invasive cancer remains unknown.

PanINs, together with the mucinous cystic pathway to PDAC, most commonly intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs) with high‐grade dysplasia (HGD), represent the true biological targets for early detection. Like PanIN‐3, these advanced cystic lesions are limited in number and often asymptomatic. While imaging can detect IPMNs, distinguishing those with malignant potential remains difficult. Dysplastic burden and mutation accumulation likely increase as lesions progress, potentially enhancing biomarker shedding—but the kinetics of this process are still poorly understood [26]. An additional challenge lies in tumor size and biomarker dilution. Early tumors (< 20 mm) are thought to release minimal amounts of circulating biomarkers, making their detection by liquid biopsy methods difficult [27].

Imaging‐Based Surveillance of High‐Risk Individuals

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) are the primary imaging modalities for monitoring high‐risk individuals for the emergence and growth of pancreatic cysts, IPMN, and pancreatic cancer. Screening strategies may include annual EUS, alternating EUS and MRI, or MRI alone, depending on patient preference and local resources [11]. EUS offers excellent sensitivity, particularly for small, solid lesions, and allows for concurrent tissue and cytologic sampling. Nevertheless, it is invasive, operator‐dependent, and carries risks related to sedation [11]. MRI, by contrast, is non‐invasive, widely accessible, and provides high‐resolution soft tissue contrast without radiation exposure. Limitations include operator dependency, motion and other imaging artifacts, reduced sensitivity in certain cases, cost, and incompatibility with pacemakers or severe claustrophobia [28]. MRI should be performed with intravenous contrast and at least a 1.5‐T magnet, though 3‐T systems may offer enhanced diagnostic performance [11]. CT scans, while less sensitive for detecting small or cystic lesions, serve as practical alternatives for patients who cannot undergo MRI or EUS [28, 29]. Imaging methods such as MRI/MRCP can detect PDAC ≥ 10 mm, at which point the five‐year survival rate roughly doubles compared to tumors ≥ 50 mm [30]. While techniques like EUS‐FNA or core needle biopsy can sample even smaller lesions (≥ 5 mm) [31], these are invasive, operator‐dependent, and challenging to implement broadly for widespread screening.

In individuals at elevated genetic risk, screening typically begins between ages 40–50 (30–35 years for Peutz–Jeghers syndrome) or 10 years prior to the earliest pancreatic cancer diagnosis in the family [11, 29]. Longitudinal imaging using EUS or MRI has been shown to enable early‐stage detection and improve survival in high‐risk groups [12]. Furthermore, CT scans performed for other medical reasons may be useful for incidental early diagnosis of PDAC, but this is not a reliable approach.

Clinical and Metabolic Indicators for Early Detection of PDAC

Clinical indicators for early detection of PDAC reflect physiological and metabolic alterations that may precede symptomatic disease and could be detected through imaging, laboratory values, or subtle clinical changes. While imaging modalities remain central in identifying structural abnormalities, blood‐based biomarkers—including fasting blood glucose, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), circulating lipids, and carbohydrate antigen 19‐9 (CA 19‐9)—have received growing attention as part of early detection strategies in high‐risk individuals (Table 1).

The International Cancer of the Pancreas Screening (CAPS) Consortium guidelines advise CA 19‐9 testing only when imaging findings raise suspicion for malignancy [10]. Nonetheless, recent data suggest that longitudinal trends in CA 19‐9, rather than isolated values, may provide early warning signals. Serial analysis of samples from the Prostate, Lung, Colorectal and Ovarian (PLCO) Cancer Screening Trial showed rising CA 19‐9 levels up to 2 years before PDAC diagnosis, supporting its potential role in early detection [18]. CA 19‐9 also has limited specificity and may be elevated in benign conditions such as cholangitis or biliary obstruction [32]. Furthermore, approximately 10% of the population are FUT3‐null and do not express CA19‐9 [19]. Conversely, individuals who are FUT2‐null may overexpress CA 19‐9, resulting in false positives. Adjusting CA 19‐9 reference ranges to account for FUT2 and FUT3 status improved detection sensitivity from 52.7% to 60.8% [19], thus personalized CA 19‐9 interpretation based on genetic background may improve its utility in surveillance. A recent study using pre‐diagnostic samples showed that integrating serial CA 19‐9 with TIMP1 via a parametric empirical Bayes algorithm increased sensitivity from 27% to 37% at 98.5% specificity and extended lead time by approximately 7 months [20].

Metabolic markers such as fasting blood glucose (FBG) and Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) have been explored in the context of new‐onset diabetes (NOD), which may precede the diagnosis of a PDAC by several months to 3 years and confers a 0.85% three‐year risk of PDAC in individuals over the age of 50 [21] and a 2.3‐fold increased risk compared to long‐standing diabetes [22]. The CAPS Consortium recommends routine FBG or HbA1c testing in HRIs, with further diagnostic evaluation if NOD is detected [10]. However, two recent studies from the Dutch Pancreatic Cancer Surveillance Program found no association between FBG or HbA1c levels and early PDAC detection in HRIs, suggesting that routine metabolic monitoring may offer limited diagnostic value in surveillance programs [23, 24].

While symptoms such as weight loss, abdominal or back pain, jaundice, or gastrointestinal disturbances may be associated with PDAC, these are typically vague and often appear only in advanced disease. For example, abdominal pain—the most frequently reported symptom—occurs in only 40%–60% of patients and usually reflects later stages [33]. Consequently, reliance on symptoms for early detection remains inadequate.

Emerging data suggests that a preclinical phase of PDAC may exist, marked by subtle clinical or biochemical changes that go unrecognized by current screening strategies. Retrospective analysis of electronic medical records has identified potential early signals—such as altered lab values, symptom patterns, or imaging findings—that preceded diagnosis by months to years [34]. Identifying and integrating these early changes into surveillance protocols may improve early detection and clinical outcomes.

Nucleic Acid–Based Biomarkers

Nucleic acid–based biomarkers, including DNA and RNA, are released from tumor and associated stromal cells into the bloodstream. Cell‐free DNA (cfDNA) is relatively stable and detectable in plasma, making it a valuable analyte for noninvasive cancer detection (Table 2). These nucleic acid biomarkers encompass somatic mutations (e.g., KRAS) and may also capture epigenetic changes such as DNA methylation and hydroxymethylation, which reflect tumor evolution and progression. In contrast, cell‐free RNA is more labile but can be protected and recovered from extracellular vesicles (EVs), including those circulating in plasma or internalized by platelets.

Recent years have seen the emergence of multi‐cancer early detection (MCED) tests, which aim to screen asymptomatic individuals for multiple cancer types simultaneously using cfDNA features such as somatic mutations, methylation patterns, and fragmentomics [39, 40]. One example is the IO‐ctDNA assay, which combines KRAS mutation detection with four protein markers—CA 19‐9, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), and osteopontin (OPN)—achieving an overall sensitivity of 64% and a specificity of 99.5% [35]. However, its sensitivity drops below 50% for stage I and IIa pancreatic cancers, limiting its utility for early detection.

The Avantect assay from ClearNote Health, based on 5‐hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) differential profiling of cfDNA, demonstrated a sensitivity of 68.3% for stage I and II PDAC and a specificity of 96.9% [36]. Similarly, GRAIL's targeted methylation‐based MCED test showed sensitivity around 63% for stage I and 83.7% for stage II pancreatic cancers in a case‐control study [37]. While promising, these performance metrics still fall short of the sensitivity needed for reliable early detection in clinical practice. Notably, the Verita system demonstrated a higher sensitivity—95.5% for stage I and 96.0% for stage II—with 95.5% specificity [38]. However, these results are from an early study with a small sample size (22 stage I and 25 stage II patients), so further validation in accordance with PROBE design is needed.

Screening for PDAC—where early detection is critical for improved survival—biomarkers must achieve high sensitivity and specificity, specifically in stage I potentially curable disease. MCED tests based on cfDNA offer the potential to identify early‐stage cancers that may be missed by conventional imaging. A 1 mm tumor may contain ~100,000 cells, while detection thresholds for circulating free DNA often require tens of millions of mutated cells per milliliter of blood [41]. Even when KRAS mutations are detected, interpretation is complicated by the multifocal and genetically heterogeneous nature of early pancreatic precursor lesions, many of which harbor independent KRAS variants. Given their small size and limited biomarker shedding, it is unclear whether a detected KRAS mutation reflects a high‐grade lesion, an invasive cancer, or a low‐grade precursor [42, 43]. Moreover, a mutation in isolation provides no information on the organ of origin, leaving clinicians with an uncertainty decision to continue monitoring, rescan, or biopsy the patient. However, molecular assays may help shift the stage distribution of PDAC diagnoses away from late‐stage disease and toward a window of curability. Furthermore, a recent article suggests that elevated cfDNA levels in early‐stage PDAC may largely reflect systemic responses, such as increased neutrophil turnover, rather than direct tumor shedding. Thus, highlighting the importance of distinguishing tumor‐derived from non‐tumor‐derived cfDNA in early detection strategies but also suggesting that systemic responses may serve as sensitive and indirect early detection biomarker [44].

Protein‐Based Biomarkers

Protein biomarkers released into circulation may circulate freely or be encapsulated in EVs and are typically detected using antibodies or affinity reagents targeting specific epitopes. A 2018 study identified a 29‐protein blood‐based biomarker signature that distinguished early‐stage PDAC (stage I and II) from healthy controls with an AUC of 0.96 across Scandinavian and US cohorts [45]. This was followed in 2022 by validation of the IMMray PanCan‐d test, which combines an 8‐protein panel with CA 19‐9 and achieved up to 89% sensitivity and 99% specificity, even accounting for Lewis antigen–null individuals [46]. More recently, PancreaSure, a serum biomarker panel, distinguished early‐stage PDAC (n = 202) from high‐risk controls (n = 864) with 78.5% sensitivity and 93.5% specificity, significantly outperforming CA 19‐9 alone. The test performed consistently across stage I and II cases and among controls with or without genetic/familial risk, with 93.7% of samples classified with full diagnostic certainty [47]. These studies support the utility of proteomic panels in high‐risk populations for noninvasive early detection.

Among individual protein markers, serum levels of Syndecan‐1 (SDC1), a transmembrane heparan sulfate proteoglycan, were significantly higher in PDAC compared to healthy controls across discovery (n = 59) and validation (n = 76) cohorts, with ROC AUCs of 0.847 and 0.844, respectively (95% CI 0.747–0.947 and 0.757–0.932; p < 0.001) [48]. MMP‐7, a matrix metalloproteinase involved in ECM remodeling and cell signaling, is markedly overexpressed in pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN) and PDAC [49] and high MMP‐7 levels have been linked to tumor progression and reduced survival in PDAC [50]. In a case–control study, newly diagnosed PDAC patients (n = 121) showed significantly elevated serum levels of MMP‐7 and SDC1 compared to those with benign pancreatic cysts (n = 66) or healthy controls (n = 48). The combination of SDC1, MMP‐7, and CA 19‐9 provided superior diagnostic accuracy (AUC 1) compared to any marker alone [51]. Notably, MMP‐7 was also elevated in benign cystic disease versus controls (p = 0.02), highlighting its broader pathophysiological relevance as well as potential challenges for clinical implementation. However, most PDAC cases in this study were late‐stage, so further evaluation in early‐stage cohorts is needed to validate these findings.

A promising innovation is PAC‐MANN, a serum protease activity–based test that achieved 98% specificity and 73% overall sensitivity. When combined with CA 19‐9, early‐stage PDAC detection sensitivity increased to 85% [52], suggesting this approach may enhance detection during a potentially curative window. PAC‐MANN features include accuracy, high throughput, low cost, and the potential for an at‐home assay kit for initial PDA detection in high‐risk patients.

Together, these studies exemplify best practices in biomarker validation. While current protein‐based assays show high specificity and strong diagnostic promise, particularly in combination panels, moderate sensitivity in early‐stage PDAC remains a limitation (Table 3). Larger, prospective studies are needed to confirm their clinical utility across diverse populations and real‐world settings.

Metabolite‐Based Biomarkers

A 2018 study reported a nine‐metabolite panel combined with CA19‐9 that distinguished PDAC from chronic pancreatitis with an AUC up to 0.96 [54], and a 2023 follow‐up reduced this to four metabolites plus CA19‐9 while maintaining 82.4% accuracy and enabling detection of resectable PDAC at diagnosis [55]. These findings were validated in the large, multicenter METAPAC trial (n = 1129), which assessed two signatures: the 12‐analyte i‐Metabolic and the 4‐analyte m‐Metabolic. The i‐Metabolic panel achieved an AUC of 0.846 with 90.4% specificity and 67.5% sensitivity, while the m‐Metabolic panel reached similar performance (AUC 0.846, 93.6% specificity, 59.9% sensitivity) and showed particular utility in patients with new‐onset diabetes [53]. These signatures may help stratify high‐risk individuals and reduce unnecessary interventions (Table 3).

Biomarker Detection in Pancreatic Fluids

Analysis of organ‐specific fluids—such as pancreatic cyst fluid and pancreatic juice—offers a promising approach to enhance the specificity and sensitivity of pancreatic cancer detection, especially in early stages (Table 4). Compared to blood‐based tests, which may miss low‐abundance markers, fluid obtained directly from the pancreatic environment can provide more concentrated and lesion‐specific biomarker profiles.

Molecular profiling of cyst fluid has further advanced with the development of genomic‐based panels, such as PancreaSeq, which has undergone extensive study to optimize the identification of advanced precursor lesions (HGD or invasive adenocarcinoma). A 74‐gene DNA/RNA version achieved 95% sensitivity and 100% specificity for detecting cystic precursor neoplasms and 82% sensitivity with 100% specificity for advanced neoplasia [56]. A larger, multi‐institutional prospective study using a streamlined 22‐gene version of PancreaSeq analyzed over 1800 pancreatic cyst fluid samples, demonstrating 90% sensitivity and 100% specificity for identifying mucinous cysts. When combined with cytopathology, this panel achieved up to 93% sensitivity and 95% specificity for detecting advanced neoplasia [57], suggesting that PancreaSeq significantly improves diagnostic accuracy while maintaining high specificity.

Pancreatic juice can be collected from the duodenum at the time of an EGD, EUS, and/or ERCP [58] and a study identified elevated levels of IL‐8, NGAL, and MUC5AC in pancreatic juice from high‐risk individuals with suspected PDAC. However, the diagnostic performance of these biomarkers was inferior to serum CA 19‐9, which remained the most accurate single marker in the cohort. These findings highlight the promise of pancreatic juice as a diagnostic fluid; though further validation in prospective surveillance settings is warranted [59].

Challenges in Biomarker Discovery and Clinical Validation

The search for biomarkers that detect PDAC before metastasis begins is both urgent and technically demanding. Currently available tests, including most liquid biopsies, do not yet offer the sensitivity or specificity needed for clinical screening and are best used in combination with imaging or clinical features [72]. An ideal biomarker would detect advanced precursor lesions such as PanIN‐3 or HGD IPMN, providing a window for curative resection prior to cellular invasion and thus prior to micro‐metastasis. However, identifying such markers is complicated by limited access to early‐stage tissues and by biological variability across patients and precursor lesions.

One major barrier is the lack of validated cohorts for biomarker discovery. Recruiting individuals with stage I PDAC, or those with known high‐grade precursor lesions, is difficult due to their rarity and asymptomatic presentation [9]. Programs like PRECEDE are actively working to address this gap by prospectively collecting data and biospecimens from high‐risk populations. Even promising biomarkers face hurdles in clinical translation. Few have undergone prospective validation across diverse high‐risk cohorts. Standardization of protocols, sample handling, and interpretation is needed to ensure reproducibility. Moreover, it is increasingly clear that multi‐analyte panels—drawing on blood, cyst fluid, pancreatic juice, or even stool—may be necessary to improve sensitivity while preserving specificity. Finally, integrating these biomarkers into real‐world practice requires careful clinical trial design, with endpoints that reflect not only diagnostic accuracy but also meaningful patient outcomes—such as earlier‐stage diagnosis, increased resectability, and ultimately improved survival.

Future Directions

Innovative technologies in quantitative imaging and liquid biopsy are poised to transform early detection of PDAC, such as radiomics and artificial intelligence (AI)‐driven analytics, which leverage high‐dimensional image features and machine learning to identify early malignancy signatures that may elude visual detection (Table 4).

Radiomics applied to CT, MRI, and EUS has shown potential in distinguishing early PDAC from healthy pancreatic tissue [69], and in stratifying risk among patients with IPMNs. These tools can accurately differentiate IPMNs with HGD or invasive carcinoma from those with low‐grade lesions [70]. Integration of radiomic analysis into surveillance programs could significantly enhance early detection, but clinical implementation remains limited by the lack of standardized and commercially available algorithms, particularly for MRI and EUS modalities.

In 60 branch duct IPMNs, human experts using needle‐based confocal laser endomicroscopy (nCLE) achieved modest dysplasia stratification (AUC 0.59–0.64; κ = 0.29), while an AI model (nCLE‐AI) reached AUC 0.70 alone and 0.85 when combined with revised Fukuoka criteria [73]. In 35 histologically confirmed IPMNs (18 HGD/invasive), EUS‐nCLE AI outperformed AGA and Fukuoka guidelines, achieving 83.3% sensitivity, 82%–88% specificity, and 82.9%–85.7% accuracy [73], accurately risk‐stratifying lesions and suggesting AI can enable real‐time automated HGD detection.

The deep learning model PANDA, trained on > 3000 annotated cases and validated in > 20,000 patients, outperformed radiologists on non‐contrast CT and was non‐inferior to contrast‐enhanced CT for lesion subtype differentiation, highlighting its potential as a scalable, low‐cost PDAC screening tool [71]. Additionally, AI models using clinical histories from 6 million Danish and (24,000 PDAC) 3 million US (3900 PDAC) citizens predicted pancreatic cancer up to 36 months in advance with AUROC 0.8834, enabling early identification of high‐risk individuals [34].

Analyzing cfDNA fragmentomics—including fragment size, end motifs, and genomic alterations—may improve detection of low‐shedding tumors [68]. Circular RNAs (circRNAs), with their covalently closed structure, are highly stable, sometimes disease‐specific, and detectable even in harsh conditions. Emerging evidence shows circRNAs' potential for PDAC detection, monitoring via extracellular vesicles, and predicting treatment response, making them a promising avenue for early diagnosis [60, 61].

Microbiome composition in the gut and oral cavity varies with diet, geography, and ethnicity, influencing baseline diversity and biomarker studies. Multiple studies have demonstrated that PDAC is associated with gut and oral dysbiosis, including reduced alpha diversity and shifts in key taxa across cohorts [74, 75, 76]. Specific oral pathogens and altered oral/gut ratios correlate with PDAC risk, with diagnostic models reaching AUC 0.9. Metagenomic classifiers of stool microbiota, especially combined with CA 19‐9, achieved AUC up to 0.94, while circulating microbial EVs provided predictive performance exceeding AUC 0.95, highlighting the microbiome's promise for early PDAC detection pending further validation.

Furthermore, specific oral pathogens are associated with increased PDAC risk [62, 77], with diagnostic models achieving high accuracy (AUC 0.9). Combined oral/gut analysis further supports this potential: a decreased Gammaproteobacteria oral/gut ratio was associated with PDAC and high‐risk pancreatic lesions [78]. Additionally, metagenomic classifiers built on stool microbiota performed well across multiple validation cohorts, especially when combined with CA 19‐9 (AUC up to 0.94) [63]. Circulating microbial EVs have also emerged as stable, non‐invasive biomarkers, with profiling approaches achieving predictive performance exceeding AUC 0.95 [66, 67]. These findings support the microbiome's value for PDAC detection, though further validation is required before clinical integration.

In a nested case–control study including 99 prediagnostic PDAC cases and 198 matched controls from ethnically diverse cohorts, a urinary biomarker panel of LYVE1, REG1B, and TFF1 (PancRISK) combined with CA19‐9 improved early detection of PDAC, identifying cases up to 2 years before diagnosis with AUCs of 0.89 within 1 year and 0.77 within 2 years [64]. A subsequent study in an Egyptian cohort of 75 PDAC and 50 healthy controls, urinary qPCR analysis showed significantly increased expression of these proteins in early‐stage PDAC, supporting their potential as a non‐invasive diagnostic biomarker panel with high reported sensitivity and specificity [65].

Wearable smart devices offer promise for early PDAC detection by continuously monitoring subtle physiological and molecular changes. Advanced biosensors can detect tumor markers—cfDNA, ctDNA, mutated KRAS, and CA 19‐9—in noninvasive biofluids, while simultaneously capturing early systemic signals like reduced heart rate variability and pre‐diagnostic body composition changes [79, 80, 81, 82, 83]. By combining tumor‐ and host‐derived signals in a sensitive, noninvasive, and user‐friendly format, wearables may enable detection of PDAC at an earlier, more treatable stage.

Strategies for Clinical Implementation

To facilitate the clinical translation of cfDNA testing for early detection, there is a pressing need for internationally recognized standards of quality and consistency across all stages of testing, from sample processing to data interpretation [84]. Robust study design and statistical validation are fundamental to developing reliable early detection biomarkers for PDAC that can withstand regulatory scrutiny and deliver meaningful clinical benefit [85], whilst ensuring equitable access to testing, mitigating patient anxiety from screening results [86] with standardized and robust economic evaluations to inform clinical and policy decisions [87]. Involving high‐risk individuals and patient advocates in early detection programs ensures strategies reflect real‐world needs, values, and concerns, enhancing relevance, accessibility, trust, and shared decision‐making [88].

Together, these initiatives illustrate the critical infrastructure and collaborative ethos required to move early detection biomarkers from bench to bedside in a way that is scientifically rigorous, patient‐centered, and clinically impactful, which is something that the PRECEDE consortium aims to achieve.

Conclusion and Outlook

The path toward effective early detection of PDAC is rapidly evolving, driven by advances in biomarker discovery, imaging technologies, and liquid biopsy platforms. However, translating these innovations into clinical benefit requires rigorous validation in well‐characterized, high‐risk populations. The PRECEDE consortium represents a pivotal step in this direction. This large, international, multi‐center cohort of individuals at elevated risk for PDAC provides the critical infrastructure to evaluate promising biomarkers in a real‐world context. Its standardized and prospective collection of high‐quality biospecimens, imaging, and clinical data allows for robust, reproducible validation of diagnostic tools across diverse populations.
