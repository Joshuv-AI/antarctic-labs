No quantum advantage implies improved bounds and classical algorithms for the binary paint shop problem
Mark Goh, Lara Caroline Pereira dos Santos, Matthias Sperl

Abstract
The binary paint shop problem (BPSP) is an APX-hard optimization problem in which, given n n car models that occur twice in a sequence of length 2 ​ n 2n , the objective is to find a colouring sequence such that each car model pair is painted differently while minimizing the number of times the paint is swapped along the sequence. A recent classical heuristic, known as the recursive star greedy (RSG) algorithm, is conjectured to achieve an expected paint swap ratio of 0.361 0.361 , thereby outperforming the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) with circuit depth p = 7 p=7 . Since the performance of the QAOA with logarithmic circuit depth is instance independent, the average paint swap-ratio is upper-bounded by the QAOA, with numerical evidence suggesting a bound that lies between 0.255 0.255 and 0.283 0.283 . To provide hardware-relevant comparisons, we additionally implement the BPSP on a D-Wave Quantum Annealer Advantage 2, obtaining a minimum paint swap ratio of 0.320 0.320 . Given that the QAOA with logarithmic circuit depth does not exhibit a quantum advantage for sparse optimization problems such as the BPSP, this implies the existence of a classical algorithm that outperforms both the RSG algorithm and logarithmic depth QAOA. We provide numerical evidence that the Mean-Field Approximate Optimization Algorithm (MF-AOA) is one such algorithm that beats all known classical heuristics and quantum algorithms to date with a paint swap ratio of approximately 0.2799 0.2799 .



I
Introduction

Quantum computing is expected to offer a computational speedup for certain optimization problems. For instance, Grover’s algorithm for unstructured search
[
18
]
and Shor’s algorithm for factoring
[
28
]
both achieve an asymptotic speedup against the best classical algorithm for their respective problem. However, these algorithms require fault-tolerant quantum computers which are out of reach in the current Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ)
[
26
]
era of quantum computing. As a result, there is a growing interest in quantum algorithms that can demonstrate a quantum advantage with the current hardware. A leading candidate is the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)
[
15
]
. Inspired by quantum adiabatic computing
[
14
]
, the QAOA is a heuristic algorithm that, given
p
layers, provides an approximate solution for combinatorial optimization problems. Typically, these problems are framed as either an Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) or more generally Polynomial/Higher-order Unconstrained Binary Optimization (PUBO/HUBO) with binary variables
x\in\{0,1\}
. Equivalently, under a change of basis, one can express these problems as an Ising model with spin variables
z\in\{-1,1\}
, where the cost function can be expressed as

\displaystyle H(z)=\sum_{i}h_{i}z_{i}+\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}z_{i}z_{j},

(1)

where
h_{i}
and
J_{ij}
are real-valued numbers with
h_{i}
being the local magnetic field and
J_{ij}
the coupling strength between spins
z_{i}
and
z_{j}
.
Additionally, one can use quantum annealers to solve such combinatorial optimization problems. Quantum annealing (QA), as an open quantum adiabatic hardware system, has been made available by D-Wave and it is known to be an efficient QUBO and Ising solver for small problem sizes
[
5
,
1
,
20
,
25
,
34
,
27
]
.

In a previous application of the QAOA on the binary paint shop problem (BPSP), it was found that the QAOA outperforms the recursive greedy algorithm, the best classical heuristic at the time of publication, requiring a depth of only
p=7

[
29
]
. Since then, a new recursive star greedy algorithm was developed that is conjectured to outperform
p=7
QAOA
[
19
]
. Furthermore, it has been recently argued that for sparse optimization problems such as the BPSP, there can be no quantum advantage offered by the QAOA with logarithmic circuit depth when compared to message passing algorithms
[
9
]
. This implies that even if the QAOA outperforms the newly created recursive star greedy algorithm at constant depth, there exists a classical algorithm that would outperform the QAOA. Here we investigate the hypothesis that the Mean-Field Approximate Optimization Algorithm (MF-AOA), a classical algorithm inspired by the QAOA and developed in Ref.
[
23
]
, is one such algorithm which could outperform logarithmic depth QAOA in the BPSP.

II
Background

II-A

Binary Paint Shop Problem

The paint shop problems are a set of various optimization problems in the automobile industry, where given a sequence of cars with a certain constraint on how to paint them, the number of times the paint colour is swapped must be minimized. The problem was first introduced in Ref.
[
13
]
, where it was proven to be an NP-complete problem if either the number of colours or the number of car body types is unbounded. Loosely speaking, in decreasing order of difficulty, there are three main types of paint shop problems: i) the multi-car multi-colour paint shop problem, where we have multiple occurrences of each car body type with multiple paint colours, ii) the multi-car paint shop problem, where we restrict to only two colouring, and iii) the binary paint shop problem (BPSP), where each car type occurs only twice. This paper focuses on the BPSP, defined as the following:

Definition II.1

(Binary Paint Shop Problem (BPSP))

Let
A=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
be the set of car body types. Let
S=(s_{1},\dots,s_{2n})
be a sequence of car instances such that each
a_{i}\in A
appears exactly twice in
S
. Denote the two paint colours as the set
C=\{0,1\}
. The BPSP aims to find a colouring sequence
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{2n})\in\{0,1\}^{2n}
that minimises the number of colour changes
\Delta_{C}
between consecutive cars with the constraint that for each car body type, the two occurrences must be coloured differently (i.e., for any car body type
a_{i}
, if
s_{j}=s_{k}=a_{i}
for
j\not=k
, we have
f_{j}\not=f_{k}
). This can be expressed mathematically as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathrm{minimize}\;&\Delta_{C}=\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}[f_{i}\not=f_{i+1}],\\
\text{subject to }\;&f_{i}\not=f_{j}\quad\forall i,j:s_{i}=s_{j},i\not=j,\end{aligned}

(2)

where
[\cdots]
denotes the Iverson bracket.

Example II.2

Consider the following instance of an
n=3
BSPS problem with the sequence

\displaystyle S=(a_{1},a_{2},a_{1},a_{3},a_{3},a_{2}).

A valid solution corresponds to the colouring

\displaystyle f^{\prime}=(0,0,1,1,0,1),\quad\Delta_{C}^{\prime}=3.

However, this is not optimal since we have

\displaystyle f^{\prime}=(0,1,1,1,0,0),\quad\Delta_{C}^{\prime}=2.

The BPSP is an NP-complete problem in its decision form
[
13
]
and APX-hard as an optimization problem
[
7
]
. Thus, unless P = NP, no polynomial-time algorithm can find an arbitrary approximate solution for all instances, let alone solve them exactly. In order to evaluate the performance of various algorithms, consider the following expression: for fixed
n
, let the normalised expectation of
\Delta_{C}
over all possible sequences of length
2n
be given by
\mathbb{E}\Delta_{C}/n
. Denote the average performance of an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on the BPSP be
\mathbb{E}\mathcal{A}(\Delta_{C}/n)
. The performance of a classical greedy algorithm and recursive greedy algorithm have been found in Ref.
[
3
]
to produce an expected colour change of

greedy

\displaystyle:\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{A}\left(\frac{\Delta_{C}}{n}\right)=0.5+\mathcal{O}(1/n),

recursive greedy

\displaystyle:\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{A}\left(\frac{\Delta_{C}}{n}\right)=0.4+\mathcal{O}(1/n).

This raises an interesting question. Namely, what is the limit of
\mathbb{E}\Delta_{C}/n
in the limit
n\rightarrow\infty
? It was conjectured in Ref.
[
21
]
that
\mathbb{E}\Delta_{C}/n=o(n)
but this has been proven false in Ref.
[
2
]
where they proved the following theorem.

Theorem II.3
(Modified Theorem 1.4 of Ref.
[
2
]
)

The expected colour change
\mathbb{E}\Delta_{C}/n
is lower bounded by

\displaystyle 2H^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)-o(1)=0.220\ldots-o(1),

(3)

where
H^{-1}
is the inverse of the binary entropy function. In addition, it is upper bounded by
0.4+o(1)
.

Remark II.4

The authors further noted that the lower bound can be slightly improved to 0.227, and they provided an outline of the proof.

The authors of Ref.
[
19
]
independently proved a weaker lower bound of 0.214 and slightly improved the upper bound to
0.4-\epsilon
for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
. In addition, they developed a new recursive star greedy (RSG) algorithm that is conjectured to have an upper bound of
0.361
. Even if their conjecture is true, there is still a substantial gap between the upper and lower bound.

II-B

BPSP as a QUBO

The algorithm to convert any instance of the BPSP into an Ising problem (more precisely, a weighted MaxCut problem) was developed in Ref.
[
30
]
and further made mathematically rigorous independently in Ref.
[
24
]
and Ref.
[
31
]
. For completeness, we describe the steps in transforming the BPSP into an Ising problem. Each car body type
i
is associate with a single qubit
z_{i}
with the sign of the spin corresponding to the colour assigned to the first instance of car body type in the sequence. Let
\Omega:S\rightarrow A
be a map that labels each
s_{i}
to an instance of a car body type in
A
. The couplings between the spins are calculated by going down the car sequence
S
and adding the coupling between adjacent cars
s_{i}
and
s_{i+1}
depending on their occurrence and car body type. If both car body types are distinct and
s_{i}
and
s_{i+1}
are both the first or second appearance of their car type, a coupling strength of
-1
between the corresponding spins in the Ising formulation is added. If one of the car is the first occurrence of their car type and the other the second occurrence, a coupling strength of
+1
is added between the corresponding spins. If both cars are of the same body type, a constant is added since we have
Z_{i}Z_{i}=1
for all
i
. Finally, a factor of
1/2
is added for each term so that the energy of the Ising formulation corresponds to the number of colour changes. Thus, for
n
car body types, the BPSP-Ising Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{BPSP}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\left(J_{s_{i}s_{i+1}}^{\prime}Z_{\Omega(s_{i})}Z_{\Omega(s_{i+1})}+1\right)

(4)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}J_{s_{i}s_{i+1}}^{\prime}Z_{\Omega(s_{i})}Z_{\Omega(s_{i+1})}+\frac{2n-1}{2},

(5)

where
J_{s_{i}s_{i+1}}^{\prime}
is the coupling strength assigned by the relation between adjacent cars
s_{i}s_{i+1}
as defined above and
\Omega(s_{i})
the car body type of the
i
-th car in the sequence.

The equation above can be further simplified by converting the sum over adjacent cars in the sequence into a sum over car body types
A
. Doing so results in a simplified Hamiltonian given by

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{BPSP}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}J_{ij}Z_{i}Z_{j}+\frac{c^{\prime}}{2},

(6)

where
J_{ij}
is the effective coupling of the Ising spins corresponding to the
i
and
j
car body type,
E
is the set of edges in the corresponding Ising graph, and
c^{\prime}=2n-1+\#\text{adjacent pairs of the same car type}
.

Remark II.5

Note that the matrix
J
is a triangular matrix rather than a symmetric matrix which is typical for Ising problems due to the directed graph nature of BPSP. If one wishes to use a symmetric formulation, then the factor of
1/2
has to be changed to a factor of
1/4
.

As we are interested in averaging over all sequences
S
in the limit
n\rightarrow\infty
to find the average colour change
\mathbb{E}\Delta_{C}/n
, we note that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}c^{\prime}/n=1
. Furthermore, it has been shown previously that in the large
n
limit, the coupling strength only takes on the values
J_{ij}\in\{-1,+1\}
, with -1 occurring with a probability of
2/3
and +1 with a probability of
1/3
and that the underlying graph approaches that of a 4-regular tree
[
30
]
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{H_{\mathrm{BPSP}}}{n}=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}J_{ij}Z_{i}Z_{j}+1,

(7)

where
|E|=2n
.

II-C

Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm

The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm
[
15
]
is a quantum variational algorithm for finding approximate solutions to combinatorial optimization problems. The problem is mapped into an Ising formulation typically encoded as a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) problem or more generally a Higher order/Polynomial Unconstrained Binary Optimization (HUBO/PUBO) problem. Focusing on the BPSP, the Hamiltonian can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{c}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}J_{ij}\hat{Z}_{i}\hat{Z}_{j},

(8)

where
\hat{Z}_{i}
is the Pauli
Z
operator acting on the
i
-th qubit and
J_{ij}
is the coupling strength between the
i
-th and
j
-th qubits. The constant term has been dropped since it adds only an overall phase factor to the quantum state and a shift in the energy.

The unitary operators of the QAOA are defined as

\displaystyle\hat{U}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\exp{-i \beta\sum_j \hat{X}_j},

(9)

\displaystyle\hat{U}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\exp{-i \gamma\hat{H}_c},

(10)

where
\gamma,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
\hat{X}_{i}
is the Pauli
X
operator acting on the
i
-th qubit.

The QAOA ansatz state is then defined by the number of layers
p
and
2p
variational parameters

\displaystyle\ket{\bs{\gamma},\bs{\beta}}=\prod_{i}^{p}\hat{U}(\beta_{i})\hat{U}(\gamma_{i})\ket{+},

(11)

where
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

The objective function to be optimized in the QAOA is typically the expected energy

\displaystyle E(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\expectationvalue{H_c}{\bs{\gamma},\bs{\beta}}.

(12)

II-D

Mean-Field Approximate Optimization Algorithm

The Mean-Field Approximate Optimization Algorithm (MF-AOA)
[
23
]
is a quantum-inspired classical limit of the QAOA, where the time evolution of the quantum spins is replaced by classical spin dynamics via the mean-field approximation.

For a concrete example of how the MF-AOA works, consider an Ising model with spins
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\{-1,1\}^{N}
with a local magnetic field
h_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle H_{h,q}(\boldsymbol{Z})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{i}Z_{i}+\sum_{j,k}J_{jk}Z_{j}\dots Z_{k},

(13)

where we excluded the normalising constant for ease of notation.

As a classical algorithm inspired by the QAOA, one similarly uses a problem Hamiltonian and a driving Hamiltonian of the following form:

H_{P}=-H_{h,q}(\boldsymbol{Z}),

(14a)

H_{D}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Delta_{i}X_{i},\quad\Delta_{i}>0,

(14b)

where the positivity of
\Delta_{i}
ensures that the ground state of
H_{D}
is given by
\ket{+}^{\otimes N}
.

The classical spin vector is defined as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=(n_{i}^{x}(t),n_{i}^{y}(t),n_{i}^{z}(t))

(15)

\displaystyle=(\text{Tr}[\rho X_{i}(t)],\text{Tr}[\rho Y_{i}(t)],\text{Tr}[\rho Z_{i}(t)]),

(16)

with
\rho
being the density matrix in the mean-field approximation
\rho=\otimes_{i=1}^{N}\rho_{i}
.

In the mean-field approximation, the total Hamiltonian has the form

\displaystyle H(t)=-\gamma(t)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[h_{i}+\sum_{j}J_{ij}n_{j}^{z}\right]n_{i}^{z}-\beta(t)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Delta_{i}n_{i}^{x},

(17)

where
\beta(t)
and
\gamma(t)
are piecewise-constant functions. Define also the effective magnetization as

\displaystyle m_{i}(t)=h_{i}+\sum_{1\leq j\leq N}J_{ij}n_{j}^{z}(t).

(18)

The dynamics of this system boil down to solving the
3N
differential equations for each spin in its effective magnetic field

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{n}(t)=\boldsymbol{n}(t)\times\boldsymbol{B}(t),

(19)

with
B_{i}(t)=\langle 2(1-s(t)),0,2s(t)m_{i}(t)\rangle
.

Remark II.6

For the BPSP, without an external field (i.e.,
\forall i\;h_{i}=0
) the effective magnetization remains 0. In order for the algorithm to work, one could fix the last spin of the state, i.e.,
n_{N}^{z}=1
in order to break the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry.

The piecewise solution to the mean-field Hamiltonian is thus given by

\displaystyle n_{i}(p)=\prod_{t=1}^{p}V_{i}^{D}(t)V_{i}^{P}(t)n_{i}(0),

(20)

with

\displaystyle V_{i}^{D}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\cos{(2\Delta_i \beta_t)}&\sin{(2\Delta_i \beta_t)}\\
0&-\sin{(2\Delta_i \beta_t)}&\cos{(2\Delta_i \beta_t)}\end{pmatrix},

(21a)

\displaystyle V_{i}^{P}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\cos{( 2m_{i,t-1}\gamma_t)}&\sin{(2 m_{i,t-1}\gamma_t)}&0\\
-\sin{( 2m_{i,t-1}\gamma_t)}&\cos{( 2m_{i,t-1}\gamma_t)}&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.

(21b)

Since the QAOA is guaranteed to find the exact solution by reduction to the adiabatic algorithm, it suffices to take linear functions of
\gamma
and
\beta
as in Ref.
[
32
]
for a sufficiently large number of time step
T
to ensure the algorithm achieves a near optimal performance.

We now explain the algorithm. Starting with
N
classical spins in the
x
-axis, for each time step, it applies the mean-field evolution using
eq.

21
and calculates the new effective magnetization using
eq.

18
. Once finished, it rounds off the
z
component of each spin vector to obtain the bitstring
\sigma^{*}=\left(\text{sign}(n_{1}^{z}),\dots,\text{sign}(n_{N}^{z})\right)
.

II-E

Related Work

There have been a few other works on the performance of QAOA variants on the BPSP. Reference
[
24
]
examines the performance of Recursive-QAOA (RQAOA) on the BPSP and found that parameter transfer showed no noticeable reduction in solution quality for both QAOA and RQAOA. In addition, it was found that RQAOA outperformed the standard QAOA. Reference
[
31
]
similarly studies the performance of RQAOA and eXpressive-QAOA (XQAOA) with a circuit depth
p=1
, where not only XQAOA outperformed RQAOA, but the authors also provided evidence that the performance of RQAOA diminishes as the problem size increases.

Regarding quantum annealing, Ref.
[
34
]
studies the application of QA on the multi-car paint shop problem, where it was found to exhibit an advantage on small problem instances but it loses any advantage in the asymptotic limit, performing comparably to a simple greedy algorithm. A recent work studied the performance of Decoded Quantum Interferometry (DQI) and benchmarked it against a state of the art classical algorithm concluding that it is unlikely for DQI to exhibit a quantum advantage on the BPSP
[
6
]
.

Methods

III-A

Application of the QAOA to the BPSP

In the large
n
limit, this problem is equivalent to the Max-2-XORSAT problem. It is known that up to logarithmic-depth, the performance of the QAOA on sparse instances of Max-2-XORSAT and MaxCUT are the same
[
9
,
10
]
. An algorithm to evaluate the performance of the QAOA on
(D+1)
-regular trees was developed in Ref.
[
4
]
for a constant depth
p
, where the expectation value of the QAOA is given by

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{Z_i Z_j}{\bs{\gamma},\bs{\beta}}=-\frac{2v_{p}(D,\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\sqrt{D}}.

(22)

This algorithm has a time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(p16^{p})
and a memory complexity of
\mathcal{O}(4^{p})
.

Since every edge in the graph is locally tree-like up to logarithmic depth, the validity of
eq.

22
can be extended from constant depth to logarithmic depth
p\sim\mathcal{O}(\log n)

[
17
]
. Thus, the performance of log-depth QAOA on
eq.

7
can be simplified to

\displaystyle\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{J}\expectationvalue{\frac{H_{\mathrm{BPSP}}}{n}}{\bs{\gamma},\bs{\beta}}=1-\frac{2\nu(3,\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\sqrt{3}}.

(23)

We point out to the reader that in evaluating
\nu(3,\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})
, the unitary operator
\hat{U}(\boldsymbol{\gamma)}
used in Ref.
[
4
]
is slightly different. Instead of using the Problem Hamiltonian (here
H_{\mathrm{BPSP}}
), a scaled driving Hamiltonian with
Z_{i}Z_{j}/\sqrt{D}
rather than
Z_{i}Z_{j}/2
is used. In our numerical simulation, we followed the convention of Ref.
[
4
]
and thus, our values of
\gamma
differ by a constant factor from the known literature values. The angles used in our simulation can be found in appendix
A
.

Remark III.1

At the time of writing, the authors were recently made aware that the algorithm to evaluate
\nu(D,\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})
is sub-optimal. By applying tensor network techniques in evaluating

, one can improve the time complexity to

[

Quantum Annealing

Next, we implement the BPSP via quantum annealing using the Ising formulations in

4

6
. Firstly, we apply the problem to the D-Wave QA Advantage 2, system 2.1, located in the central European region. For every

III-C

Simulation of MF-AOA

We perform a numerical simulation of the MF-AOA modifying some of the codes that were developed in the original paper
[
22
]
. To estimate the algorithm’s performance in the large
n
limit, we randomly generated 1000 instances of the BPSP with problem sizes up to
n=10000
. Note that given a problem with size
n
, there is the question of deciding the number of time-step (aka. the runtime of the algorithm)
T
. One could, in principle, set
T
to be arbitrarily large but we found that when we set
T\gg n
, the results were worse than simply setting
T=n
. For example, for
n=2000
instances, 4 different runs of the MF-AOA were done with
T\in\{1000,2000,5000,10000\}
. We found that most instances had the best results using
T=2000
while only a few had slightly better results with
T=5000
. Thus, simply setting
T
to be arbitrarily large does not seem to be the optimal solution. In order to simplify the analysis, we shall set
T=\max(1000,n)
.

\displaystyle 1-\frac{2\nu(3,\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\sqrt{3}}\approx 0.269\quad\text{with}

(26)

\displaystyle 0.255\leq

\displaystyle 1-\frac{2\nu(3,\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\sqrt{3}}\leq 0.283.

(27)

Using the fitted value gives us an expected value of 0.269, while the range of values comes about from the uncertainty of the fit.

Similarly, assuming the validity of the fit, this implies that QAOA requires only a constant depth to surpass the RSG algorithm with a depth of
p\approx 10
.

IV-C

MF-AOA

Following the extrapolation of the QAOA’s result in
section

IV-A
, we make a conjecture about the performance of the MF-AOA:

Conjecture IV.1

In the limit
n\rightarrow\infty
, AMP algorithms such as the MF-AOA achieve a performance of approximately

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\Delta_{C}/n=1-\frac{2\nu(3,\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\sqrt{3}}\gtrsim 0.269.

(28)

Remark IV.2

We use
\gtrsim
because the value is likely an overestimation of the parameter fitting, which can similarly be observed if one attempts a similar fit with the Sherrinton–Kirkpatrick model with values up to
p=7
resulting in an overestimation of the optimal solution and similarly with values of up to
p=80
as seen in Ref.
[
8
]
.

We found that the MF-AOA had an expected paint swap ratio of
\mathbb{E}\Delta_{C}/n=0.27993
with a standard deviation of
\mathbf{s}=0.001469
for
n=10000
, outperforming all known classical heuristics, applications of the QAOA and QA to the BPSP. In general, both the expected paint swap ratio and the standard deviation decreases as
n
increases. Further details can be found in
fig.

1
and
table

IV
. Consistent with the findings of Ref.
[
9
]
, we conjecture that AMP algorithms are optimal for average-case sparse optimization problems.

The difficulty with proving conjecture
IV.1
is that, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, the performance guarantee of AMP algorithms is only well understood when, loosely speaking, one takes the large
n
limit followed by setting the average degree of the graph to infinity (i.e.,
\lim_{D\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}
). For the BPSP, the degree is fixed at
D=4
. The performance of AMP algorithms on MaxCut is known
[
12
]
to be bounded by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+\Pi_{2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{D}}+o_{D}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}\right),

(29)

where
\Pi_{2}=0.763\dots
is the ground state energy of the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick model.

Converting the cut ratio to a paint swap ratio, this gives us a value of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Delta_{C}/n

\displaystyle=1-\Pi_{2}+o_{D}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}\right)

(30)

\displaystyle=0.2368\dots+o_{D}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}\right).

(31)

For
D=4
, the sub-leading term is not suppressed and cannot be ignored leading to a correction that is difficult to quantify. Similarly, assuming that the bounds for MaxCut and Max-2-XORSAT (and hence BPSP) are similar, then using the known upper-bound of 0.8683 and lower-bound of 0.8333 for Maxcut on a 4-regular graph
[
11
]
, the expected paint swap ratio would be lower-bounded by 0.2634 and upper-bounded by 0.3334. Our numerical results for the expected paint swap ratio of approximately 0.27993 fits within these bounds which gives us good reason to believe that the algorithm is near-optimal.

V
Discussion and Conclusion

In this paper, we have analyzed the performance of the QAOA, QA and the MF-AOA for solving the BPSP. We have shown that the QA outputs a minimum paint swap ratio of
0.320
at
n=50
, outperforming constant depth QAOA, but it loses accuracy for larger problem sizes.

Similarly, we numerically estimated the performance of logarithmic depth QAOA and estimate that it has an average paint swap ratio between
0.255
and
0.283
. However, given that there is no quantum advantage for logarithmic depth QAOA when compared to AMP algorithms for sparse optimization problems such as the BPSP
[
9
]
, this implies the existence of a suitably applied AMP algorithm that would achieve significantly better performance.

We have provided numerical evidence that the MF-AOA is able to outperform all known classical heuristics, QA and logarithmic depth applications of the QAOA for the BPSP. While we conjecture that AMP algorithms are optimal with respect to the BPSP, it is of further work to provide a rigorous proof of the performance of AMP algorithms for the BPSP.

Given that AMP algorithms seem to work well for the BPSP and unconstrained optimization problems in general
[
9
]
, it would be of further interest to study whether such algorithms work in more generalized constrained optimization problems, such as the multi-car paint shop problem.

While the results using the D-Wave BQM solver indicate a performance improvement over the MF-AOA, we cannot conclusively affirm this for two reasons. Firstly, we only used a sample size of 50 instances for the D-Wave BQM solver due to limitations in computing time, whereas the MF-AOA had a sample size of 1000 instances. Secondly, we did not fully optimise the MF-AOA and only used linear functions of
\gamma
and
\beta
as in Ref.
[
32
]
. It could be that optimising the MF-AOA schedule could lead to an improvement in the results. We did not attempt to optimise over the MF-AOA schedule since the focus of this paper was to demonstrate that efficient classical algorithms exist that outperform all known classical heuristics. An important remark is that the BQM is a hybrid solver and is therefore expected to perform comparably to an efficient classical algorithm, while benefiting from an additional quantum optimization and acceleration. Nevertheless, larger scale experiments with the D-Wave solvers are of interest and planned for future investigations.

Lastly, while there is no quantum advantage for logarithmic depth QAOA, it does not rule out an advantage at higher depth (e.g., linear depth). One possible extension would be to identify instances where the MF-AOA fails to find the optimal solution and the depth required by the QAOA to outperform the MF-AOA in expectation value.
