A natural language framework for non-conforming hybrid polytopal methods in Gridap.jl
Jordi Manyer †, Jai Tushar ♯, Santiago Badia †

Abstract
. Hybrid finite element methods such as hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin, hybrid high-order and weak Galerkin have emerged as powerful techniques for solving partial differential equations on general polytopal meshes. Despite their diverse mathematical origins, these methods share a common computational structure involving hybrid discrete spaces, local projection operators and static condensation. This work presents a comprehensive framework for implementing such methods within the Gridap finite element library. We introduce new abstractions for polytopal mesh representation using graph-based structures, broken polynomial spaces on arbitrary mesh entities, patch-based local assembly for cell-wise linear systems, high-level local operator construction and automated static condensation. These abstractions enable concise implementations of hybrid methods while maintaining computational efficiency through Julia’s just-in-time compilation and Gridap’s lazy evaluation strategies. We demonstrate the framework through implementations of several non-conforming polytopal methods for the Poisson problem, linear elasticity, incompressible Stokes flow and optimal control on polytopal meshes.

2.
A motivating example: The Poisson problem with HHO

To fix ideas and demonstrate the abstractions offered by the library, we consider the following Poisson problem on a polytopal Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
with boundary
\partial\Omega
: find

(2.1)

\displaystyle u\in H^{1}(\Omega)\ :\ -\Delta u

\displaystyle=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\quad u=g\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

for given source data
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
(to avoid some technicalities in the presentation) and boundary data
g\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega)
. The weak formulation is: find

(2.2)

u\in H^{1}_{g}(\Omega)\ :\ a(u,v)=l(v)\quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

where
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
H^{1}_{g}(\Omega)
denote the standard Sobolev spaces with functions in
H^{1}(\Omega)
such that their trace on
\partial\Omega
is zero and
g
, respectively, and the bilinear and linear forms are defined as

a(u,v)\doteq\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v,\quad l(v)\doteq\int_{\Omega}fv.

We now discretise this problem using the
hho
method, closely following
[
26
, Chapter 2]
. This exposition will highlight the method’s key features and the computational building blocks shared across the broader family of hybrid methods.

2.1.
Hybrid discrete spaces

Given a polytopal mesh
\mathcal{M}_{h}
of
\Omega
consisting of a finite collection of non-overlapping polytopal elements
T
, we denote by
\mathcal{T}_{h}
the set of mesh cells and by
\mathcal{F}_{h}
the set of mesh facets. For polynomial degrees
k,l\geq 0
, the
hho
method introduces a
hybrid
discrete space defined as

(2.3)

\underline{U}_{h}^{k,l}\doteq\mathbb{P}^{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\times\mathbb{P}^{l}(\mathcal{F}_{h}),

where

\mathbb{P}^{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})

and

\mathbb{P}^{l}(\mathcal{F}_{h})
denote the globally discontinuous piecewise polynomial spaces of degree
k
and
l
on the mesh cells and facets, respectively. An element
\underline{u}_{h}\in\underline{U}_{h}^{k,l}
is thus a tuple
\underline{u}_{h}=((u_{T})_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}},(u_{F})_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}})
, where
u_{T}
represents cell-wise polynomials and
u_{F}
represents facet (skeleton) polynomials. This hybrid structure—pairing volumetric
dof
with
dof
on the mesh skeleton—is the first common feature shared by
hdg
,
hho
,
wg
and related methods. The local spaces on each cell
T
can be seen in Figure
1
.

Figure 1
.
Illustration of the local
hho
discrete space on a hexagonal cell
T
for polynomial degrees
(k,l)=(0,0),(1,1),(2,2)
. The space consists of polynomials defined on the cell (grey) and polynomials defined on each facet (black).

Having defined the hybrid discrete spaces, we now describe the local operators that map between them.

2.2.
Local operators and cell-wise integration

The
hho
method relies on operators defined locally on each cell
T
and its surrounding facets. These operators are constructed by solving cell-wise linear systems that involve integrals over the cell and its facets—a second common feature of hybrid methods.

The primary building block is the
local reconstruction operator

\mathcal{R}_{T}:\underline{U}_{h}^{k,l}(T)\to\mathbb{P}^{k+1}(T)
, which computes a higher-order polynomial approximation inside
T
from the local trace unknowns
\underline{v}_{T}=(v_{T},(v_{F})_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}(T)})\in\underline{U}_{h}^{k,l}(T)
. This operator is uniquely defined by

(2.4)

\displaystyle\int_{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}_{T}\underline{v}_{T}\cdot\nabla w

\displaystyle=\int_{T}\nabla v_{T}\cdot\nabla w+\sum_{F\in\partial T}\int_{F}(v_{F}-v_{T})\nabla w\cdot\bm{n}_{TF}\quad\forall w\in\mathbb{P}^{k+1}(T),

(2.5)

\displaystyle\int_{T}\mathcal{R}_{T}\underline{v}_{T}

\displaystyle=\int_{T}v_{T},

where
\bm{n}_{TF}
denotes the unit outward normal to facet
F
from cell
T
.
Computing
\mathcal{R}_{T}\underline{v}_{T}
requires assembling a small local linear system involving integrals over
T
and its facets
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}(T)
, then solving this system to determine the coefficients of the reconstructed polynomial.

Additionally, the method employs the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projectors onto cells and facets, namely
\pi_{T}^{0,k}:L^{2}(T)\to\mathbb{P}^{k}(T)
and
\pi_{F}^{0,l}:L^{2}(F)\to\mathbb{P}^{l}(F)
such that

(2.6)

\displaystyle\int_{T}\pi_{T}^{0,k}w\,v=\int_{T}w\,v\quad\forall v\in\mathbb{P}^{k}(T),\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{F}\pi_{F}^{0,l}w\,v=\int_{F}w\,v\quad\forall v\in\mathbb{P}^{l}(F).

These projectors can also be computed locally via a small system involving cell or facet integrals. We also define the so-called difference operators
\delta_{T}^{k}:\underline{U}_{h}^{k,l}(T)\to\mathbb{P}^{k}(T)
and
\delta_{TF}^{l}:\underline{U}_{h}^{k,l}(T)\to\mathbb{P}^{l}(F)
by

\displaystyle\delta_{T}^{k}\underline{v}_{T}=\pi_{T}^{0,k}(\mathcal{R}_{T}\underline{v}_{T}-v_{T}),\quad\text{and}\quad\delta_{TF}^{l}\underline{v}_{T}=\pi_{F}^{0,l}(\mathcal{R}_{T}\underline{v}_{T}-v_{F}).

Note that local operators, like the weak gradient in the
wg
methods, can also be readily computed in this framework. With these local operators in hand, we can now formulate the discrete problem and describe the assembly process.

2.3.
Cell-local assembly and static condensation

The global discrete problem is assembled from cell-local contributions, which is a third defining characteristic of hybrid methods. Denoting by
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{{\rm bd}}
the set of boundary facets, the
hho
approximation of (
2.2
) reads: find
\underline{u}_{h}\in\underline{U}^{k,l}_{h,g}\doteq\{\underline{u}_{h}\in\underline{U}_{h}^{k,l}:u_{F}=\pi_{F}^{0,l}(g)\ \forall F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{{\rm bd}}\}
such that

(2.7)

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt\qquad a_{h}(\underline{u}_{h},\underline{v}_{h})=l_{h}(\underline{v}_{h})\quad\forall\underline{v}_{h}\in\underline{U}^{k,l}_{h,0}\doteq\{\underline{v}_{h}\in\underline{U}_{h}^{k,l}:v_{F}=0\ \forall F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{{\rm bd}}\},

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad a_{h}(\underline{u}_{h},\underline{v}_{h})=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left[a_{T}(\underline{u}_{T},\underline{v}_{T})+S_{T}(\underline{u}_{T},\underline{v}_{T})\right],\quad l_{h}(\underline{v}_{h})=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}l_{T}(\underline{v}_{T}),

with
\underline{u}_{T}=\underline{u}_{h}|_{T}
denoting the restriction of
\underline{u}_{h}
to cell
T
and its surrounding facets. The local bilinear form
a_{T}(\underline{u}_{T},\underline{v}_{T})
represents the consistency term,
S_{T}(\underline{u}_{T},\underline{v}_{T})
provides stabilisation and
l_{T}(\underline{v}_{T})
is the local load term. The involved local forms are defined as

(2.8)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\hskip 71.13188pt\qquad a_{T}(\underline{u}_{T},\underline{v}_{T})=\int_{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}_{T}\underline{u}_{T}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{R}_{T}\underline{v}_{T},\\
&S_{T}(\underline{u}_{T},\underline{v}_{T})=h_{T}^{-1}\sum_{F\in\partial T}\int_{F}(\delta_{TF}^{l}-\delta_{T}^{k})\underline{u}_{T}\cdot(\delta_{TF}^{l}-\delta_{T}^{k})\underline{v}_{T},\quad\text{and}\quad l_{T}(\underline{v}_{T})=\int_{T}fv_{T}.\end{aligned}

where
h_{T}
denotes the diameter of cell
T
.
In the facet integrals defining
S_{T}
, the cell polynomial
\delta_{T}^{k}\underline{v}_{T}\in\mathbb{P}^{k}(T)
is understood as restricted to
F
.
The evaluation of these forms requires integrating over the cell
T
and its facets, producing a local matrix and vector for each cell that couples the cell unknowns
u_{T}
with the facet unknowns
(u_{F})_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}(T)}
.

The computational efficiency of hybrid methods stems from
static condensation
: since the cell unknowns appear only in their respective local contributions, they can be eliminated locally to obtain a reduced global system involving only the facet unknowns. This condensation process involves:

(1)

For each cell
T
, assembling the local system coupling cell and facet
dof
;

(2)

Solving locally for the cell unknowns in terms of the facet unknowns (a Schur complement operation);

(3)

Assembling a reduced global system on the mesh skeleton
\mathcal{F}_{h}
only.

The reduced system is typically much smaller than the full system, especially for high polynomial degrees, and once solved, the cell unknowns can be recovered efficiently via local solves.

2.4.
Towards a unified computational framework

The preceding exposition of
hho
for the Poisson problem reveals a computational structure that is shared across the broader family of hybrid methods (
hdg
,
hho
,
wg
). From an implementation perspective, these methods require the same fundamental building blocks:

(1)

Hybrid discrete spaces:

dof
distributed across mesh entities of different dimensions—typically cells and facets—forming a product space structure. The implementation must handle data structures that associate unknowns with both volumetric elements and the mesh skeleton.

(2)

Local integration on cells and facets:
All methods require the computation of integrals over each cell
T
and its boundary facets
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}(T)
. These integrals appear in the definition of local operators (e.g., reconstructions, projections) and in the evaluation of local bilinear and linear forms. The implementation must provide efficient quadrature rules and basis function evaluations on general polytopes.

(3)

Local operator assembly and solution:
Many hybrid methods define auxiliary operators (reconstructions, projections, stabilisations) that are computed by assembling and solving small local linear systems on each cell. The framework must provide mechanisms to assemble these systems from user-defined variational forms and solve them efficiently.

(4)

Cell-local assembly and static condensation:
The global discrete system is assembled by iterating over cells, computing local contributions that couple cell and facet unknowns, and then condensing out the cell unknowns to form a reduced global system on the skeleton. The implementation must automate this condensation process and manage the assembly of the reduced system.

Despite the diversity of hybrid methods, the computational workflow is remarkably uniform: for each cell, integrate local forms, solve local systems to compute auxiliary operators, assemble local matrices and vectors, perform static condensation and contribute to the global skeleton system. This uniformity suggests that a
generic computational framework
can be designed to support the implementation of a wide range of hybrid methods without requiring low-level, method-specific code for each variant.

The goal of this work is to present such a framework, built on top of the Gridap
fe
library, that abstracts the common computational patterns while remaining flexible enough to accommodate the specific features of different hybrid methods. By providing high-level abstractions for hybrid spaces, local operators and static condensation, the framework enables users to implement complex hybrid discretisations with minimal effort, focusing on the mathematical formulation rather than low-level implementation details. We remark that standard
dg
methods are also accommodated as a special case: by omitting facet unknowns, local reconstruction operators and static condensation, the framework reduces to a conventional
dg
assembly. In the following sections, we describe the design and implementation of this framework and demonstrate its application to several hybrid methods.

3.
Introduction to Gridap.jl

Gridap
[
10
,
29
]
is an open-source
fe
framework written entirely in the Julia programming language
[
12
]
for the numerical approximation of
pde
. Being written exclusively in Julia, Gridap sidesteps the two-language problem common in scientific computing—where performance-critical code is written in C++ or Fortran while user interfaces are provided in Python—by leveraging Julia’s
jit
compilation to generate efficient, problem-specific machine code at runtime. This allows researchers to extend the library and develop new numerical methods entirely in a high-level language, without sacrificing performance.

Unlike
fe
libraries that follow traditional object-oriented designs (e.g., Deal.II
[
4
]
) or rely on domain-specific form compilers (e.g., FEniCS
[
24
]
), Gridap adopts a software architecture based on Julia’s multiple dispatch paradigm and lazy evaluation. Rather than explicitly iterating over mesh cells, users build objects representing elemental quantities (such as stiffness matrices or load vectors) for all cells simultaneously. These objects are typically
lazy
: the underlying data is computed on-the-fly when needed rather than stored in memory for all cells at once, reducing memory requirements. This design hides assembly loops from user code, yielding a compact syntax that closely resembles mathematical weak forms—a standard Poisson or Stokes problem can be solved in 10–20 lines of code.

Gridap also provides a modular low-level interface based on a small number of fundamental abstractions that stem from mathematical concepts: arrays (
AbstractArray
), callable objects (
Map
), physical fields (
Field
), tensor values (
MultiValue
) and linear functionals (
Dof
). These abstractions can be combined and extended via multiple dispatch, allowing advanced users to customise the library’s behaviour without modifying its core. A comprehensive explanation of these software abstractions is provided in
[
29
]
, and tutorials can be found in
[
23
]
.

4.1.
General representation of polytopes

We follow the framework used in
[
9
]
to represent general polytopes using rotation systems — a subset of planar graphs in which the neighbours of each vertex are arranged in a cyclic order. These graph-based representations enable efficient storage, flexible topological queries and straightforward integration with our
fe
assembly pipeline. For the sake of completeness, we reproduce here the main results.

For a given graph
G
, the vertex set of
G
is denoted as
\texttt{vert}(G)
and its adjacencies are represented by
\texttt{adj}(G)
. The set of vertices adjacent to a vertex
\alpha\in\texttt{vert}(G)
is denoted by
\texttt{adj}(G)(\alpha)
. In a rotation system
R
, for a vertex
\alpha\in\texttt{vert}(R)
and an adjacent vertex
\beta\in\texttt{adj}(R)(\alpha)
, there exists a clearly defined preceding and succeeding vertex within
\texttt{adj}(R)(\alpha)
due to the cyclic ordering. Consequently, we can define
\texttt{next}(\alpha,\beta)
as the vertex that follows
\beta
in the cyclic ordering
\texttt{adj}(R)(\alpha)
.

A polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is then defined as a rotation system whose vertices are points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In 2D, each boundary vertex has exactly two neighbors, so the rotation system reduces to a simple closed loop, recovering the classical polygon representation. In 3D, the cyclic ordering encodes the local face structure around each vertex, and the boundary of
P
forms an oriented closed surface composed of polygons. In Figure
2
, we illustrate all three of these representations for a scutoid, a type of polyhedron recently discovered in living cells that helps pack them efficiently into curved tissues.

4.2.
Polytopal meshes

Traditional simplicial or hexahedral meshes can be efficiently represented by storing a single reference polytope, a list of vertex coordinates and a connectivity table that maps each element to its vertices. With this information, geometrical maps can be computed that map the reference polytope to each of the physical ones. This approach minimises memory usage by avoiding redundant storage of geometric information.

The same strategy does not work for general polytopal meshes for two fundamental reasons. First, each polytope may be unique, lacking a single reference element. Second, a practical geometrical map from one general polytope to another with the same topology cannot, in general, be defined. Consequently, general polytopal meshes require new data structures that, while more straightforward in design, are potentially more memory-intensive than their traditional counterparts.

To this end, we provide a new
PolytopalGridTopology
structure, which stores all the necessary information to represent the topology of a polytopal mesh. This topology is then paired with a
FaceLabeling
, which assigns physical labels to each face of the mesh, to produce a
PolytopalDiscreteModel
object. We currently provide a low-level constructor for
PolytopalDiscreteModel
, as well as several higher-level methods for generating polytopal meshes: Voronoi tessellation from a background simplicial mesh in 2D (illustrated in Figure
3
), and direct conversion of existing simplicial or quadrilateral meshes in both 2D and 3D via the constructor
PolytopalDiscreteModel(model)
. Although not demonstrated in this work, the framework also supports the construction of arbitrarily agglomerated meshes
[
7
]
.

We now describe how to extract topological information from a polytopal mesh. Consider a polytopal mesh
\mathcal{M}_{h}
of a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
. For any dimension
0\leq d\leq D
, we refer to
d
-dimensional entities of the mesh as
d
-faces. In particular,
D
-faces correspond to mesh cells (or elements), while
(D-1)
-faces correspond to mesh facets.

We denote by
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{d}
the set of
d
-faces of
\mathcal{M}_{h}
. In particular, we have that
\mathcal{T}_{h}\doteq\mathcal{F}_{h}^{D}
and
\mathcal{F}_{h}\doteq\mathcal{F}_{h}^{D-1}
. We will use
T
,
F
and
F^{d}
to denote generic elements of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
,
\mathcal{F}_{h}
and
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{d}
respectively.

Given a
d
-face
F^{d}\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{d}
, we denote by
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{s}(F^{d})
the set of
s
-faces around
F^{d}
, defined as

(4.1)

\mathcal{F}_{h}^{s}(F^{d})\doteq\begin{cases}\{F^{s}\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{s}:F^{s}\subset\overline{F^{d}}\}&\text{if }0<s<d,\\
\{F^{d}\}&\text{if }s=d\\
\{F^{s}\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{s}:F^{d}\subset\overline{F^{s}}\}&\text{if }d<s\leq D.\end{cases}

In particular, the boundary of
F^{d}
is given by
\partial F^{d}\doteq\mathcal{F}_{h}^{d-1}(F^{d})
. For example, the boundary of a cell
T
consists of its facets:
\partial T=\mathcal{F}_{h}^{D-1}(T)
.
In Gridap, this information can be accessed through the
get_faces(object, d, s)
API, which is defined both at the polytope (see Listing
4.1
) and mesh levels. We refer to the provided tutorials
[
23
]
for a more detailed explanation.

4.3.
Broken polynomial spaces

Having established how to represent and query polytopal mesh topology, we now introduce the
fe
spaces defined on these meshes.
All discontinuous and hybrid non-conforming methods rely on broken polynomial spaces defined on the
d
-dimensional faces of the mesh. Within the hybrid methods presented in this paper, we will only be dealing with polynomial spaces on cells (
d=D
) and faces (
d=D-1
). The implementation does, however, support the use of this machinery on faces of arbitrary dimension, opening the door to more complex constructions such as the conforming
vem

[
16
]
or the discrete de Rham sequence on polytopal meshes
[
18
]
.

We will begin by introducing local polynomial spaces. We denote by
\mathbb{P}^{k}
the space of polynomials of degree at most
k
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
, be an open bounded connected set. The local polynomial space
\mathbb{P}^{k}(X)
is defined as the restriction of
\mathbb{P}^{k}

4.4.
Local assembly and solves

Assembling and solving local (usually cell-wise) linear systems is at the heart of hybrid methods. These operations are used, for instance, to statically condense the cell
dof
or to compute local projections such as the
hho
reconstruction operator. As part of this library expansion, we provide a high-level flexible framework to integrate, assemble and solve local
fe
problems defined on subsets of mesh geometrical entities, called
patches
.

Although all the local solves needed in this work are defined on a single cell and its surrounding facets, we designed our code to support arbitrary patch definitions. The framework can, for instance, accommodate multi-cell patches, interface patches between cells sharing multiple faces and overlapping patches where a mesh entity belongs to multiple patches. This flexibility enables applications beyond hybrid methods, such as patch-based smoothers for geometric multigrid solvers, similar to the PCPATCH framework developed in
[
21
]
. Examples of this framework being used within multigrid solvers can be found as part of the GridapSolvers package
[
25
]
. However, such applications are beyond the scope of this work.

Our framework revolves around three new objects. First, a
PatchTopology
that defines, for each local problem to be solved, the set of mesh entities (patch) whose contributions have to be considered. Second,
PatchTriangulation
objects that take
D
-dimensional slices of these patches where the different contributions can be defined and integrated. Finally, a
PatchAssembler
that gathers all the contributions from different
PatchTriangulation
objects and assembles them into an array of local matrices, vectors or matrix-vector pairs (local linear systems).

A
PatchTopology
is defined by providing an underlying
GridTopology
object and, for each dimension, a list of the
d
-faces that belong to each patch. For instance, in Listing
4.4
(line 29), we define patches around each cell
T
of a polytopal mesh
\mathcal{M}_{h}
, where each patch comprises the cell
T
itself and all its surrounding faces, i.e.,
\{T,(\mathcal{F}_{h}^{d}(T))_{d<D}\}
.

Integration in Gridap revolves around
Triangulation
objects, which represent single-dimensional slices of a mesh (i.e., collections of
d
-faces for a fixed
d
) where integral contributions are defined. We provide two constructors for
Triangulation
objects on patches: a
PatchTriangulation
that contains the
D
-faces for each patch and a
PatchBoundaryTriangulation
that contains, for each patch, the
(D-1)
-faces lying on the patch boundary. Internally, the faces are concatenated for efficient integration, but enough information is retained to facilitate patch-wise integration. Listing
4.4
(lines 30-31) illustrates how to create both types of

objects for the patches defined above, which will be used to define the constrained local
rhs
(
rhs

lhs
(
lhs
) for the

2.4
) (lines 35-40).

Finally, we provide a
PatchAssembler
object that takes care of assembling local contributions defined on the different
PatchTriangulation
objects into local arrays. It supports both single-variable and multi-variable problems, and, in the latter case, it allows local block assembly. Listing

(lines 43-47) shows how to use the

and

contain the local system matrices, split into

blocks corresponding to the different test and trial variables. Using Gridap’s

Paired with the local assembly of linear systems, we provide several local maps to solve these systems. The most basic is the
LocalSolveMap

However, implementing more specialised local solvers that exploit the structure of the local systems is straightforward. In Listing

(lines 50-74) we show how to implement a custom local solver for the

4.5.
Local operators

Local operators encapsulate the common pattern of computing discrete projections through local linear solves. Like in the example showcased in Listing
4.4

hho

(k,l)

4.4
can be abstracted away.

To encompass all the possibilities, we provide a new

to solve each one of these systems and an output discontinuous

to which the resulting local solutions belong.

There are two main signatures, both showcased in Listing

and two bilinear forms corresponding to the left- and right-hand sides of the projection, which in this case are both the

-mass bilinear form.
The second signature is for local operators that require the assembly of linear systems on patches. It is used (lines 13–39) to implement the
hho

defined in (

), replacing most of the manual assembly shown in Listing

A subtle but important detail is the use of

Within the
hho

fe
spaces. For instance, the input space to the
hho

and facet

4.6.
Static condensation

A defining feature of hybrid methods is the ability to eliminate cell unknowns locally through static condensation, yielding a reduced global system involving only the skeleton (facet)
dof
. This process requires assembling local contributions that couple cell and facet unknowns, then performing a Schur complement operation on each cell to eliminate the cell
dof
before global assembly. Our framework automates this procedure by building on the patch assembly infrastructure introduced in Section
4.4
.

A key challenge is that local operators such as
\mathcal{R}_{T}
map from the hybrid space
\underline{U}_{h}^{k,l}(T)
to a reconstruction space
\mathbb{P}^{k+1}(T)
defined on the cell. The resulting reconstructed basis is attached to all hybrid
dof
within the patch, which has to be accounted for during assembly.

To address this, we introduce the
PatchFESpace
construct. Given a space
X
and a
PatchTopology
, the
PatchFESpace
, illustrated in Listing
4.6
(line 57) by
Xp = PatchFESpace(X,ptopo)
, creates a view of
X
that gathers all
dof
in each patch. In the
hho
case,
Xp
gathers, for each cell
T
, the local
dof
from the cell and all its surrounding facets, providing the assembler with the correct global
dof
indices for contributions involving the reconstructed basis.

This leads to a natural
2\times 2
block structure when assembling forms that mix the original and reconstructed bases. On the one hand, the consistency term
a_{T}
only involves the reconstruction operator and can therefore be assembled using only
Xp
. On the other hand, the stabilisation term
S_{T}
involves both the original and reconstructed bases. This requires that we decompose the facet residual appearing in the stabilisation. Specifically, for any
\underline{u}_{T}\in\underline{U}_{h}^{k,k}
and any facet
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}(T)
, consider

s_{TF}(\underline{u}_{T})\doteq(\delta_{TF}^{k}-\delta_{T}^{k})\underline{u}_{T},

where
\delta_{T}^{k}\underline{u}_{T}\in\mathbb{P}^{k}(T)
is understood as restricted to
F
in facet expressions. Using the definitions of
\delta_{T}^{k}
and
\delta_{TF}^{k}
, we can write, on each facet
F
,

\displaystyle s_{TF}(\underline{u}_{T})=S_{1}(\underline{u}_{T})+S_{2}(\mathcal{R}_{T}\underline{u}_{T}),

with

\displaystyle S_{1}(\underline{u}_{T})\doteq(u_{T})|_{F}-u_{F},\quad\text{and}\quad S_{2}(\mathcal{R}_{T}\underline{u}_{T})\doteq\pi_{F}^{0,k}\bigl((\mathcal{R}_{T}\underline{u}_{T})|_{F}\bigr)-\bigl(\pi_{T}^{0,k}(\mathcal{R}_{T}\underline{u}_{T})\bigr)|_{F}.

The terms
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are attached to the original and reconstructed
dof
, respectively. The stabilisation can then be rewritten as

(4.5)

\displaystyle S_{T}(\underline{u}_{T},\underline{v}_{T})

\displaystyle=h_{T}^{-1}\sum_{F\in\partial T}\int_{F}\bigl(S_{1}(\underline{u}_{T})+S_{2}(\mathcal{R}_{T}\underline{u}_{T})\bigr)\cdot\bigl(S_{1}(\underline{v}_{T})+S_{2}(\mathcal{R}_{T}\underline{v}_{T})\bigr)

(4.6)

\displaystyle=h_{T}^{-1}\sum_{F\in\partial T}\int_{F}S_{1}(\underline{u}_{T})\cdot S_{1}(\underline{v}_{T})+h_{T}^{-1}\sum_{F\in\partial T}\int_{F}S_{1}(\underline{u}_{T})\cdot S_{2}(\mathcal{R}_{T}\underline{v}_{T})

(4.7)

\displaystyle+h_{T}^{-1}\sum_{F\in\partial T}\int_{F}S_{2}(\mathcal{R}_{T}\underline{u}_{T})\cdot S_{1}(\underline{v}_{T})+h_{T}^{-1}\sum_{F\in\partial T}\int_{F}S_{2}(\mathcal{R}_{T}\underline{u}_{T})\cdot S_{2}(\mathcal{R}_{T}\underline{v}_{T})

Lines 66–72 in Listing
4.6
pass these four blocks to

\times

(X, X)
for contributions involving only original

(Xp, Xp)
for terms involving the reconstruction on both trial and test sides and the mixed pairs
(X, Xp)
and
(Xp, X)
for cross terms. The
PatchAssembler
ensures that contributions are placed at the correct locations in the global matrix based on the
dof

5.2.
More involved examples

Now we discuss some more complex standard examples from the literature, showcasing the proposed framework’s flexibility and ease of extension to new formulations.

5.2.1.
hho
for linear elasticity

Linear elasticity is a vector-valued problem whose
kernel contains the so-called rigid body modes. The
hho
formulation for linear elasticity
[
19
]
closely resembles the one for the Poisson problem introduced above, with the main changes being the modification of the local reconstruction operator to account for rigid body motions and the use of vector-valued spaces. We will closely follow the formulation introduced in
[
26
, Chapter 7]
. Listing
5.2.1
shows the implementation of this example. To add some variation to the previous example, we will consider the mixed-order
hho
formulation. Given a polynomial degree
k\geq 0
, we define our hybrid space (lines 23-31) as

(5.1)

\underline{\bm{U}}_{h}^{k+1,k}=\left[\mathbb{P}^{k+1}\right]^{d}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\times\left[\mathbb{P}^{k}\right]^{d}(\mathcal{F}_{h}).

The local reconstruction operator
\mathcal{R}_{T}^{\mathrm{sym}}:\underline{\bm{U}}_{h}^{k+1,k}(T)\to\left[\mathbb{P}^{k+1}(T)\right]^{d}
is now uniquely defined by

(5.2)

\displaystyle(\nabla_{s}\mathcal{R}_{T}^{\mathrm{sym}}\underline{v}_{T},\nabla_{s}w)_{T}

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{s}v_{T},\nabla_{s}w)_{T}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}(T)}(v_{F}-v_{T},\nabla_{s}w\cdot\bm{n}_{TF})_{F}\quad\forall w\in\left[\mathbb{P}^{k+1}\right]^{d}(T),

(5.3)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{R}_{T}^{\mathrm{sym}}\underline{v}_{T},1)_{T}

\displaystyle=(v_{T},1)_{T}

(5.4)

\displaystyle(\nabla_{s}\mathcal{R}_{T}^{\mathrm{sym}}\underline{v}_{T},1)_{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}(T)}\frac{1}{2}(\bm{n}_{TF}\otimes v_{F}-v_{F}\otimes\bm{n}_{TF},1)_{F}

Note that this new reconstruction operator solves a modification of the vector Poisson problem (see (
2.4
)) in which we replace the gradient by the symmetric gradient
\nabla_{s}
and add rigid-body motions to the kernel space. The implementation of this operator can be found in Listing
5.2.1
. Constraints are enforced via Lagrange multipliers, using cell-wise constant vector-valued spaces with one component per constraint:
\Lambda 1
enforces the mean value (component-wise,
d
equations), while
\Lambda 2
enforces the rigid body modes (
d(d+1)/2
equations) using inlined functions
\alpha
(component-wise contraction) and
\beta(\bm{n},v)=(1/2)(\bm{n}\otimes v-v\otimes\bm{n})
.

The advantage of mixed-order
hho
for the elasticity problem is that we can replace
\mathcal{R}_{T}^{\mathrm{sym}}
by a simpler gradient reconstruction operator
\mathcal{G}_{T}:\underline{\bm{U}}_{h}^{k+1,k}(T)\to\left[\mathbb{P}^{k+1}(T)\right]^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
mapping to the subspace of symmetric 2-tensors, defined by

(5.5)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{G}_{T}\underline{v}_{T},\bm{\tau})_{T}

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{s}v_{T},\bm{\tau})_{T}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}(T)}(v_{F}-v_{T},\bm{\tau}\cdot\bm{n}_{TF})_{F}\quad\forall\bm{\tau}\in\left[\mathbb{P}^{k+1}(T)\right]^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}.

The implementation of this operator can also be found in Listing
5.2.1
.

Back in Listing
5.2.1
, the discretised problem then reads (lines 50-72): find
\underline{u}_{h}\in\underline{\bm{U}}_{h}^{k+1,k}
such that
a_{h}(\underline{u}_{h},\underline{v}_{h})=l_{h}(\underline{v}_{h})\quad\forall\underline{v}_{h}\in\underline{\bm{U}}_{h}^{k+1,k}
. The involved local forms are defined by

\displaystyle a_{T}(\underline{u}_{T},\underline{v}_{T})=\left(\mathcal{G}_{T}\underline{u}_{T},\bm{C}:\mathcal{G}_{T}\underline{v}_{T}\right)_{T},

\displaystyle S_{T}(\underline{u}_{T},\underline{v}_{T})=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}(T)}h_{F}^{-1}\int_{F}\left((\pi_{F}^{0,k}u_{T}-u_{F})\cdot(\pi_{F}^{0,k}v_{T}-v_{F})\right),\quad\text{and}\quad l_{T}(\underline{v}_{T})=\int_{T}fv_{T}\,dx,

where
\bm{C}
is the elasticity tensor, which we take as the isotropic elasticity tensor defined by the Lamé parameters
\lambda
and
\mu
(lines 9-12). Convergence results for this method can be found in Figure

5.2.2.
hho
for the incompressible Stokes problem

Another typical example of a vector-valued problem is the incompressible Stokes problem
[
1
]
, which models the flow of an incompressible viscous fluid at low Reynolds numbers. We will closely follow the formulation introduced in
[
26
, Chapter 8]
. Listing
5.2.2
shows the implementation of this example.

For the velocity field, we take
\underline{\bm{U}}_{h}^{k+1,k}
like in (
5.1
) (lines 16-23), while for the pressure field we take the broken polynomial space
\mathbb{P}^{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
, split into its mean-value and zero-mean value subspaces. As directly imposing a global zero-mean value constraint on the pressure space is not scalable, we instead consider a cell-wise zero-mean pressure space and enforce global zero-mean value via a Lagrange multiplier. This leads to the following pressure spaces (lines 25-30):

(5.6)

P_{h}=\hat{P}_{h}\oplus\bar{P}_{h}\oplus\Lambda\ ,\quad\hat{P}_{h}=\left\{q_{h}\in\mathbb{P}^{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h}):(q_{h},1)_{T}=0\ ,\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\}\ ,\quad\bar{P}_{h}=\mathbb{P}^{0}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\ ,\quad\Lambda=\mathbb{P}^{0}(\Omega).

The subspace
\hat{P}_{h}
can be statically condensed together with the cell velocity unknowns, leaving only the mean-value pressure unknowns
\bar{P}_{h}
and the Lagrange multiplier
\Lambda
in the global system. Thus, we use
BlockMultiFieldStyle(2,(2,3))
(lines 32-34) to structure the five-field space as a two-block system
(\bm{U}_{h}\times\hat{P}_{h},U_{h}^{\partial}\times\bar{P}_{h}\times\Lambda)
, where the first block contains all the variables that will be statically condensed.

To reconstruct the velocity field, we will use a vector-valued version of the reconstruction operator introduced in (
2.4
). Given
\underline{v}_{T}\in\underline{\bm{U}}_{h}(T)
, the local velocity reconstruction operator
\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{T}:\underline{\bm{U}}_{h}^{k+1,k}(T)\to\left[\mathbb{P}^{k+1}(T)\right]^{d}
is uniquely defined by

(5.7)

\displaystyle(\nabla\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{T}\underline{v}_{T},\nabla\bm{w})_{T}

\displaystyle=-(v_{T},\Delta\bm{w})_{T}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}(T)}(v_{F}-v_{T},\nabla\bm{w}\cdot\bm{n}_{TF})_{F}\quad\forall\bm{w}\in\left[\mathbb{P}^{k+1}(T)\right]^{d},

(5.8)

\displaystyle(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{T}\underline{v}_{T},1)_{T}

\displaystyle=(v_{T},1)_{T}

To reconstruct the divergence, we will introduce a new local operator
\mathcal{D}_{T}:\underline{\bm{U}}_{h}^{k+1,k}(T)\to\mathbb{P}^{k}(T)
defined by

(5.9)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}_{T}\underline{v}_{T},q)_{T}

\displaystyle=(\nabla\cdot v_{T},q)_{T}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}(T)}{\left((v_{F}-v_{T})\cdot\bm{n}_{TF},q\right)_{F}}\quad\forall q\in\mathbb{P}^{k}(T)

The implementation of these operators can be found in Listing
5.2.2
. A subtle detail is that, when using these operators within Listing
5.2.2
, the input space of the operators is
\underline{\bm{U}}_{h}
(i.e.
YR
in lines 45-47) while the solution space of the problem is
\underline{\bm{U}}_{h}\times P_{h}
. Thus, we need to recast the five-field variables into the velocity reconstruction operator’s input space
\underline{\bm{U}}_{h}
(then recast the resulting bases back into the original space). To do so, we wrap our operators (lines 55-60) within a function
adapt_ids
(lines 48-54). Alternatively, one could create a new local map, in the spirit of Listing
4.4
, that is prepared to handle the five fields.

Going back to Listing
5.2.2
, the discretised problem reads (lines 65-99): find
(\underline{u}_{h},p_{h})\in\underline{\bm{U}}_{h}^{k+1,k}\times P_{h}
such that
a_{h}((\underline{u}_{h},p_{h});(\underline{v}_{h},q_{h}))=l_{h}(\underline{v}_{h})\quad\forall(\underline{v}_{h},q_{h})\in\underline{\bm{U}}_{h}^{k+1,k}\times P_{h}
, where the local forms are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pta_{T}(\underline{u}_{T},p_{T};\underline{v}_{T},q_{T})=\int_{T}\left(\nabla\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{T}\underline{u}_{T}:\nabla\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{T}\underline{v}_{T}-p_{T}\mathcal{D}_{T}\underline{v}_{T}-q_{T}\mathcal{D}_{T}\underline{u}_{T}\right),

\displaystyle S_{T}(\underline{u}_{T},\underline{v}_{T})=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}(T)}h_{F}^{-1}\int_{F}\left((\pi_{F}^{0,k}u_{T}-u_{F})\cdot(\pi_{F}^{0,k}v_{T}-v_{F})\right),\quad\text{and}\quad l_{T}(\underline{v}_{T})=\int_{T}\bm{f}\cdot v_{T}\,dx.

5.2.3.
Optimal control

Optimal control problems governed by
pde
involve finding a control function that minimises a cost functional while satisfying the constraints imposed by the
pde
. We consider a prototypical example of a distributed optimal control problem governed by the Poisson equation
[
28
]
. Given a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with boundary
\partial\Omega
, we seek to minimise the cost functional

\displaystyle\min_{z\in Z_{ad}}J(u,z)=\frac{1}{2}\|u-u_{d}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|z\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

subject to the state equation

\displaystyle-\Delta u=z+f\quad\text{in}\;\Omega,\quad u=0\quad\text{on}\;\partial\Omega,\quad z\in Z_{ad}:=\{z\in L^{2}(\Omega):z_{a}\leq z(x)\leq z_{b}\text{ a.e. in }\Omega\},

where
u
is the state variable,
z
is the control variable,
u_{d}
is the desired state,
\alpha>0
is a regularisation parameter and
z_{a}<z_{b}
are real numbers. It is well known that the above distributed optimal control problem is equivalent to solving the following
kkt
(
kkt
) optimality system:
(u,p,z)\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times Z_{ad}

\displaystyle a(u,v)=(z+f,v),\quad a(v,p)=(u-u_{d},v),\quad(p+\alpha z,w-z)\geq 0,

for all
v,w\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and
w\in Z_{ad}
. Here,
p
is the adjoint variable which solves the corresponding adjoint equation.
We approximate the
kkt
system using a mixed order
hho
method using the implicit characterisation of the control variable
z=\min\{z_{b},\max\{z_{a},-(1/\alpha)p\}\}
. The discrete
kkt
system reads: find
(\underline{u}_{h},\underline{p}_{h})\in\underline{U}_{h}^{k+1,k}\times\underline{U}_{h}^{k+1,k}
such that

\displaystyle a_{h}(\underline{u}_{h},\underline{v}_{h})=l_{h}(v_{\mathcal{T}_{h}})+(\min\{z_{b},\max\{z_{a},-(1/\alpha)p_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\}\},v_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}),\quad a_{h}(\underline{w}_{h},\underline{p}_{h})=(w_{\mathcal{T}_{h}},u_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}-u_{d}),

for all
\underline{v}_{h},\underline{w}_{h}\in\underline{U}_{h}^{k+1,k}
. The optimal control can be post-processed using the characterisation
z_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}=\min\{z_{b},\max\{z_{a},(-1/\alpha)p_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\}\}\in Z_{ad}
. The following code snippet (see Listing
5.2.3
) shows the implementation of this example.

6.
Conclusion

We have presented a comprehensive framework for implementing non-conforming hybrid polytopal
fe
methods within the Gridap library. By introducing abstractions for polytopal mesh representation, broken polynomial spaces, patch-based local assembly, local operators and static condensation, we enable researchers to implement complex hybrid methods with minimal code while maintaining computational efficiency.

The framework’s design leverages Julia’s multiple dispatch and Gridap’s lazy evaluation to achieve a high-level declarative programming model that closely resembles mathematical notation. Complete implementations of
dg
,
hdg
and
hho
methods require around 100 lines of code, dramatically reducing the implementation burden compared to traditional approaches. Despite this brevity, the generated code achieves performance comparable to hand-optimised implementations through
jit
compilation.
Although not discussed in this work, the framework also supports distributed-memory parallelism through GridapDistributed
[
8
]
and scalable solvers through GridapSolvers
[
25
]
, enabling large-scale simulations on polytopal meshes
[
5
,
7
]
.

The numerical examples presented in Section
5
demonstrate that the framework successfully handles methods of varying complexity, from simple
dg
discretisations to sophisticated
hho
formulations involving multiple local reconstruction operators. The convergence results confirm that the implementations achieve optimal rates on polytopal meshes, validating both the mathematical formulations and the computational framework.

Beyond the specific methods demonstrated here, the abstractions introduced in this work are sufficiently general to support a broader class of polytopal discretisations. The patch assembly framework can accommodate overlapping patches and multi-cell configurations, enabling applications to patch-based multigrid smoothers and other advanced techniques.

Future work will focus on extending the framework to support more variants of hybrid methods, such as
vem
or the discrete de Rham sequence on polytopal meshes
[
18
]
.
