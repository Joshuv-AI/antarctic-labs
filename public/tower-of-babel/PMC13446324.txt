Distribution of Research and Clinical Productivity among US Ophthalmologists, 2021 to 2023: A Cross-Sectional Analysis
Authors: Muhammad Z. Chauhan, Ahmed Shakarchi, Xiaoran Zhang, Porter Jones, Alexander Kwok, Jawad Muayad, Asad Loya, Abdelrahman M. Elhusseiny, Paul H. Phillips, Jennifer E. Thorne, Ahmed B. Sallam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo quantify the association between research output and Medicare-weighted clinical volume among ophthalmologists and to examine variation by gender, career stage, and institution.DesignCross-sectional study linking PubMed publications (January 1, 2021–December 31, 2023) to the National Plan and Provider Enumeration System and Medicare Part B claims data.SubjectsUS ophthalmologists with at least one PubMed-indexed publication during 2021 to 2023 (N = 2914). The clinician-scientist model underpins many of ophthalmology’s transformative advances over the past few decades, from antivascular endothelial growth factor therapy to modern retinal imaging. However, the field faces simultaneous pressures: (1) an expanding literature, more than 18 000 ophthalmic articles were published globally in 2022 (∼50 articles per day), with US institutions producing the plurality1,2 and (2) rising demand for eye-care services as the population ages.1,3 Workforce projections estimate that by 2035 only 70% of the ophthalmology care needed in the United States will be met, the second-worst adequacy among 38 specialties.4

These trends raise concerns that escalating clinical volumes could erode time for scholarship among clinician scientists. Historic work in radiology documented a significant inverse relationship between relative value unit (RVU) production and academic output,5 yet contemporary data for ophthalmology are scarce. Simultaneously, gender equity remains a priority. Although women now comprise nearly half of US ophthalmology residents, they are under-represented in senior ranks and leadership.6, 7, 8 Recent surveys also show women ophthalmologists have almost twice the odds of burnout compared with men,9 underscoring workload and cultural challenges.

Finally, bibliometric analyses reveal striking institutional concentration: Harvard, Johns Hopkins, and a few other centers dominate publication metrics.1 Whether this concentration extends to combined clinical-research productivity is unknown.

We conducted a national, cross-sectional analysis of ophthalmologists who published linking fractional authorship scores to Medicare RVUs. We sought to (1) quantify the relation between research and clinical volume, (2) assess differences by gender and career stage, and (3) map geographic and institutional patterns.

Methods

Study Design and Data Sources

We conducted a cross-sectional observational study of ophthalmologists who published peer-reviewed articles between 2021 and 2023. We identified physicians using the 2023 National Plan and Provider Enumeration System file published by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services. To identify eligible participants, we retrieved publication data for all physicians with at least one publication during this period. We calculated each physician’s publication score using a weighted algorithm system that takes into account journal impact factor (multiplier based on the publication’s journal) and assigns fractional authorship credit based on contribution. In this system, first and senior authors received greater weight, while co-authors received partial credit. For example, a sole-authored article contributes 1.0 point, whereas multiauthored articles contribute proportionally less per author. This scoring method accounted for both the number of publications and the author’s role, yielding a continuous measure of academic productivity. The weighting scheme for author positions was as follows: single author: 100% of the score; 2 authors: first author = 60% and last author = 40%; 3 or more authors: first author = 45%, last author = 30%, and the remaining authors shared 25%, divided equally among them.

We obtained each physician’s year of medical school graduation to calculate years in practice, along with their primary hospital affiliation and state of practice. To assess clinical volume, we analyzed work RVUs derived from Medicare Part B claims. Using a nationally representative 5% sample of physician claims from 2021 to 2023, we identified services performed by each ophthalmologist. We excluded physicians who performed <10 services during this timeframe. For each physician, we summed the work RVUs and multiplied the total by 20 (since a 5% sample represents one-20th of the Medicare population) to estimate annual RVUs across the full Medicare population. This imputed RVU value serves as a proxy for each ophthalmologist’s overall Medicare patient care volume, including surgeries, clinic visits, and procedures. Because these data do not include commercial, Medicaid, self-pay, Veterans Affairs, capitated plan, or other non-Medicare encounters, this measure was not intended to estimate all-payer clinical volume. Rather, it served as a standardized proxy for the Medicare component of ophthalmic clinical workload, including clinic visits, procedures, and surgeries.

We excluded 230 physicians (≈8% of the sample) with missing geographic data (i.e., no recorded state or location) from location-based analyses, yielding 2684 ophthalmologists with complete data for comparative analyses. By design, the study cohort included only ophthalmologists who had published at least one peer-reviewed article between 2021 and 2023. We extracted peer-reviewed articles from the PubMed database and matched physician names to author names using a combination of name and affiliation matching algorithms. We excluded letters to the editor from the analysis. This study did not meet the criteria for human subjects research as defined by our institution, as it did not include patient data. Therefore, it did not require institutional review board approval or informed consent. Our study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Outcomes and Statistical Analysis

The primary outcomes were the publication score (academic/research productivity) and total RVUs (clinical productivity) for each physician. We defined a composite metric of publication score × RVU as the product of the publication score and total RVUs—this captures ophthalmic productivity in both realms over the study period. We conducted a sensitivity analysis in which each metric was z-standardized and summed before analysis (z = [publication score – μ]/σ + [RVU – μ]/σ). We performed descriptive analyses of publication scores across categories of interest: gender (male vs. female), years since medical school graduation, hospital affiliation, and state of practice. We assessed group differences in continuous outcomes with appropriate statistical tests (Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for 2-group comparisons and Kruskal-Wallis tests for multiple groups, as appropriate given skewed distributions). We examined the correlation between publication score and clinical volume (RVUs) using Spearman’s rank correlation. We also fitted a multivariable linear regression model predicting publication score from key covariates (RVUs, gender, and years in practice) to assess independent associations. All tests were 2-sided with a significance threshold of P < 0.05. We used Stata/MP 17.0 (StataCorp LP) for data management and analyses. We reported results as means with standard deviations (SDs) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) as appropriate. We conducted all analyses in aggregate and maintained the confidentiality of individual physicians.

Results

Cohort Characteristics

Eligible for analysis were a total of 2914 ophthalmologists with peer-reviewed publications from 2021 to 2023 (after excluding those without geographic data, n = 2684 for location-based analysis). Of these, 1764 (65.7%) were male, and 920 (34.3%) were female (Table 1). The cohort represented a wide range of career stages: graduation years ranged from 1964 to 2024, with a median of 2004 (∼19 years in practice). Physicians were affiliated with 622 distinct hospitals across 49 states. The distribution of research output was highly skewed; the median publication score was 0.41 (IQR, 0.04–2.19), while the mean was 2.50 (SD, 6.62). In terms of clinical volume, ophthalmologists in the sample managed a substantial workload of Medicare patients. Publication Score versus Clinical Volume

There was a small negative correlation between research productivity and clinical workload in this cohort. The Spearman correlation between an individual’s publication score and total RVUs was r = –0.13 (P < 0.0001) (Fig 1). A multivariable linear regression adjusting for gender and years in practice, RVUs had a small negative coefficient (each additional 1000 RVUs associated with a 0.094-point decrease in publication score; P < 0.001). Gender Differences

The median publication score for women was 0.52 (IQR, 0.05–2.19) versus 0.36 (IQR, 0.03–2.18) for men (P = 0.25), indicating no statistically significant difference in publication output between genders. In contrast, female physicians had markedly lower patient care volumes: median total RVUs were 1833 (621–3682) for women, compared to 3750 (1295–7594) for men (P < 0.001). Female ophthalmologists in this publishing cohort had substantially fewer Medicare-weighted patient encounters than her male counterpart during 2021 to 2023. As a result, the composite publication score × RVUs—which reflects dual clinical and research productivity—was lower for women (median [IQR]: 649 [17–3830] vs. 892 [22–6444] for men, P < 0.001). However, the single highest composite score in the entire data set was achieved by a female ophthalmologist.

Experience and Career Stage

Ophthalmologists in their first decade (1–10 years; n = 574) recorded the lowest output—median publication score of 0.20 (IQR, 0.00–1.50), median imputed RVUs of 1574 (562–3579), and median score × RVU of 286 (1–1838). Those 11 to 20 years out (n = 815) doubled research output (score, 0.42) and more than doubled clinical volume (RVUs, 3554), lifting the combined metric to 982. Peak productivity occurred at 21 to 30 years (n = 545): median publication score of 0.60, the highest median RVUs 4414, and the maximal score × RVU composite of 1436. Beyond 30 years (n = 750), research output remained high (score, 0.64), while clinical workload tapered modestly (RVUs, 3275), yielding a slightly lower—but still robust—composite metric of 1158. All 3 measures differed significantly across the 4 groups (P < 0.001). There was a positive association between years in practice and publication score (P < 0.001) and with RVUs (P < 0.001).

Institutional and Regional Variation

Maryland had the highest mean publication score of any state (mean, 5.35; median, 2.28), coinciding with the presence of Johns Hopkins in Baltimore (as well as the National Eye Institute in Bethesda). Massachusetts was a close second (mean, 5.00; median, ∼0.90), reflecting the impact of institutions like Harvard/Massachusetts Eye and Ear. Other states with high average publication scores included Pennsylvania (mean, 3.55), Minnesota (3.33), and California (2.51) (Fig 3). We also observed geographic differences in the composite score × RVU metric. Ophthalmologists in Maryland achieved by far the highest median combined metric of any state (median score × RVU, ∼6111). Discussion

In this analysis of more than 2900 US ophthalmologists who published research during the study period, we found that clinical productivity and clinical research productivity are largely independent dimensions. Contrary to the notion that a busy clinical practice precludes academic output, our data show no strong trade-off between seeing patients and publishing,many ophthalmologists manage to do both to varying degrees and some excel in both arenas. This is an important finding for the ophthalmology community. This suggests that engaging in research does not inherently require sacrificing clinical volume and vice versa. However, we also observed that only a small subset achieved high levels in both domains simultaneously. These “dual high performers” mainly clustered at major academic centers, implying that certain environments and support systems (e.g., abundant research funding, large patient referral networks, and sufficient protected time) are conducive to maximizing both clinical and scholarly productivity.

Additionally, we found that male and female ophthalmologists in our cohort had equal academic output, but women had lower Medicare RVU totals. Women constituted one-third of publishing ophthalmologists and matched men in publication score despite 50% lower Medicare volumes, echoing subspecialty-specific analyses where productivity gaps disappeared after adjusting for career duration.6 Female ophthalmologists, on average, performed fewer surgeries and patient visits in Medicare compared to their male peers,a finding consistent with other studies of procedure volumes.10 Combined with higher burnout prevalence among women,9 these findings support initiatives such as equitable RVU targets, mentorship, and flexible pathways to tenure. Several factors could contribute to the above findings: women are disproportionately represented in certain subspecialties (such as pediatrics) that involve fewer Medicare-aged patients.11 They may more often practice part-time or in academic roles with more nonclinical responsibilities, and there may be systemic biases or differences in referral patterns affecting female physicians’ caseloads. Some existing data suggest a mid-career dip for women’s productivity relative to men,12 potentially reflecting family leave or part-time work periods.

We found that career length strongly influences productivity profiles. Early-career ophthalmologists (first 10 years in practice) generally focus on building clinical expertise and establishing themselves; their publication rates tend to be modest as they lack extensive data or large research teams. Mid-career ophthalmologists (roughly 11–20 years in practice) often represent a peak in both clinical and research productivity for those who remain in academia. These physicians have accumulated experience and possibly research funding, and they may have protégés or research team members contributing to output. Indeed, ophthalmologists 11 to 20 years in practice in our cohort had double the publication score of the youngest group and more than double the clinical RVUs. Many mid-career faculty attain leadership in clinical trials, publish review articles or book chapters, and take on mentees, all boosting publication counts. However, mid-career can also be a time of intense clinical responsibility (high surgical volumes, administrative roles) and personal obligations, which may temper research time.

The analysis revealed that academic output in ophthalmology is highly concentrated in certain institutions and regions. Our geographic and institutional analysis reinforces bibliometric evidence of concentration.1 Five hospitals delivered one-quarter of all publication credit and one-third of combined output. Concentration can accelerate innovation by clustering expertise but risks leaving large regions academically underserved. Collaborative research networks and registry-based studies may mitigate this imbalance. Many faculty at these centers have research as part of their job description and promotion criteria, which naturally drives higher publication counts compared to community practitioners whose primary (or sole) focus is patient care. The presence of residents and fellows can also boost publication output at academic centers (trainees often contribute to faculty-led projects). In contrast, ophthalmologists practicing in nonacademic settings may publish only occasionally, if at all, given fewer incentives or resources to do so. It is worth noting that our study only included those with at least one publication in 2021 to 2023. Thus, community ophthalmologists who never published were outside our sample. Even so, among the community-based doctors who did publish, their contributions were usually minimal (e.g., a single case report or a low-impact collaborative study). This dichotomy suggests that research in ophthalmology remains somewhat siloed in academic medicine and bridging this gap (through community engagement in research or expanding multicenter studies) could be beneficial.

Geographically, the state-by-state differences largely mirrored the distribution of academic centers. States like Maryland and Massachusetts stood out for their high research output and many high-performing physicians, whereas several smaller or more rural states had scant representation in the research sphere. Notably, Maryland’s median combined productivity was exceptionally high. This implies a cluster of ophthalmologists there who are managing large patient volumes while still publishing significantly. We suspect this is driven by the unique environment at Johns Hopkins (which has high surgical volume faculty who also do research) and possibly by the proximity of federal research institutions (some ophthalmologists in Maryland may have roles at the National Institutes of Health, although National Institutes of Health clinicians often have lower RVUs by virtue of seeing fewer patients). Other states with high combined metrics—Minnesota, Alabama, Iowa, and Tennessee—each host well-known ophthalmology departments (Mayo Clinic, University of Alabama, University of Iowa, Vanderbilt, etc.) where clinician-scientists are actively practicing. In contrast, states without major academic ophthalmology departments showed far fewer such dual-role physicians. This regional imbalance may have downstream effects on patient care and innovation. Patients in high-academic states might have readier access to clinical trials and cutting-edge treatments, whereas those in low-research regions might need referral to academic centers for advanced care. From a policy perspective, encouraging collaborations or tele-research networks could help disseminate research opportunities more evenly across regions and expand patient research populations to more rural areas.

A strength of this study is the comprehensive, data-driven approach linking objective measures of academic output and clinical workload across a large national sample of ophthalmologists. By quantifying publication credit with a weighted score (rather than a simple count) and taking into account journal impact factor, we accounted for the magnitude and nature of contributions, and by using Medicare RVUs, we obtained a standardized measure of clinical activity. The use of Medicare data is a validated approach to gauge physician volume, and focusing on Medicare work RVUs captures the bulk of surgical and adult ophthalmology practice in the United States (Medicare covers the demographic that undergoes most cataract surgeries, intravitreal injections, etc.). Nevertheless, we acknowledge several limitations. First, the publication score was confined to 2021 to 2023 and does not reflect a physician’s entire career output or impact; a senior ophthalmologist who published landmark papers decades ago but nothing in the past 3 years would appear as zero in our data set. Conversely, a flurry of publications in a short timeframe might overemphasize overall publication activity. Second, the RVU-based clinical volume measure was derived from Medicare Part B claims and therefore does not capture all-payer clinical activity. We did not directly estimate or adjust for commercial patient volume. This limitation may differentially affect physicians whose practices include larger proportions of commercially insured adults, pediatric patients, refractive or cosmetic services, or other care not billed through Medicare. Medicare RVU totals may underestimate overall clinical workload for some clinicians and may partly reflect differences in payer mix, subspecialty, and practice setting rather than clinical effort alone. These considerations are also relevant when comparing our findings with the Association of University Professors of Ophthalmology or other academic productivity benchmarks. Association of University Professors of Ophthalmology compensation survey data are confidential, chair-accessible, and designed for academic compensation benchmarking, whereas our study used physician-level claims to quantify traditional Medicare fee-for-service clinical activity. It is important to point out that for most ophthalmologists (especially those in comprehensive or adult subspecialties), Medicare constitutes a large share of their surgical volume.13 Third, because publication scores (0 – ≈6) and RVUs (hundreds to thousands) are on different scales, the multiplicative composite could in principle be dominated by the larger-scale variable. We therefore conducted a sensitivity analysis in which each metric was z-standardized and summed before analysis. All key associations—correlations, gender and career-stage contrasts, and institutional rankings—remained materially unchanged, confirming that our findings are robust to the choice of scaling. Additionally, because the study period overlapped with the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic and recovery era, pandemic-related disruptions may have affected both axes of our analysis: reduced patient access, surgical delays, staffing constraints, regional waves, and patient avoidance of care may have lowered Medicare-weighted RVUs, particularly in 2021, whereas remote work, coronavirus disease-focused scholarship, and altered manuscript submissions and publication patterns. Finally, unmeasured confounders—such as subspecialty, academic rank, or part-time status—were not directly accounted for in our analysis. These factors likely intersect with gender, institution, and years of experience in influencing productivity.

In summary, we found wide variability in productivity among publishing ophthalmologists, with no inherent trade-off between clinical and academic output. Practice setting and institutional support emerged as key drivers of success in both domains. Women matched men in research despite lower Medicare volumes, highlighting the need for policies addressing workload balance and career development. Our findings can inform departmental policies and career mentorship, emphasizing that a balance of clinical and research excellence is attainable. Fostering environments that support clinician-scientists with mentorship, protected time, and funding14 may enable more ophthalmologists to reach their full potential in both caring for patients and advancing the science of ophthalmology.
