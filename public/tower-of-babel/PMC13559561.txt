Pan-cancer characterization of HMGA1 reveals its oncogenic role in tumor microenvironment and stemness: functional validation in pancreatic cancer migration and invasion
Authors: Huan Liu, Shiyang Dai, Chaoxiang Lv, Mingming Deng, Xiaopan Zou, Xiaoling Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHMGA1 is a chromatin-associated oncogenic factor implicated in tumor progression, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), stemness, and metastasis. However, its pan-cancer expression and prognostic patterns, epigenetic activation, and relationship with malignant-cell stemness/plasticity and tumor microenvironment (TME) remodeling in pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) remain incompletely defined. This study aimed to systematically characterize HMGA1 across cancers and clarify its clinical and biological relevance in PAAD.MethodsPan-cancer transcriptomic, clinical, genetic, methylation, immune, and stemness data were integrated from multiple public databases. PAAD single-cell RNA sequencing data were analyzed to localize HMGA1 expression, infer malignant-cell pseudotime, calculate a stemness module score, and assess ligand-receptor communication using CellChat. Public HMGA1-knockdown RNA sequencing data were reanalyzed to evaluate transcriptional remodeling. The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-PAAD expression and methylation data were used to assess TME-remodeling, immune-suppression, stemness/plasticity, cytokine/chemokine, checkpoint, and promoter-methylation features. HMGA1 expression and function were further examined using immunohistochemistry (IHC), quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction, Western blotting, wound-healing assays, and Transwell migration and invasion assays.ResultsHMGA1 was upregulated in most tumor types, and high expression was associated with unfavorable survival in multiple cancers, including PAAD. In PAAD, HMGA1 was enriched in malignant epithelial cells and positively correlated with pseudotime (Spearman’s rho =0.594), while the stemness module score increased along pseudotime (rho =0.748). HMGA1-high malignant cells showed markedly stronger CellChat-inferred outgoing communication, predominantly involving extracellular matrix (ECM)-receptor, adhesion-related, and selected immunomodulatory ligand-receptor axes. HMGA1 knockdown was associated with broad remodeling of EMT, TGF-β, Hedgehog, IL6/JAK/STAT3, KRAS, and cancer stem cell/stemness-related programs rather than uniform suppression of these programs. HMGA1 promoter methylation was inversely correlated with HMGA1 expression (rho =−0.633) and the TME-remodeling score (rho =−0.347). HMGA1 was associated with selected mediators, including PPIA, PLAU, ANXA1, LGALS9, TGFB1, CD276, and CD47, but not with a generalized checkpoint-high phenotype. Functionally, HMGA1 knockdown significantly reduced pancreatic cancer (PC) cell migration and invasion.ConclusionsThese findings support an association-based model in which promoter hypomethylation-associated HMGA1 activation is linked to malignant epithelial stemness/plasticity, ECM/adhesion-dominant TME remodeling, selected immunomodulatory programs, and aggressive PAAD phenotypes. Further mechanistic and clinical validation is required before HMGA1 can be used for therapeutic stratification or immunotherapy-response prediction.Keywords: Pan-cancer analysis, HMGA1, tumor microenvironment (TME), tumor stemness, pancreatic cancer (PC)

Highlight box

Key findings

• HMGA1 was broadly upregulated across cancers and was associated with unfavorable prognosis, particularly in pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD). In PAAD, HMGA1 was enriched in malignant epithelial cells and associated with late pseudotime, higher stemness/plasticity scores, promoter hypomethylation, and enhanced extracellular matrix (ECM)/adhesion-dominant outgoing communication. HMGA1 knockdown suppressed pancreatic cancer cell migration and invasion. HMGA1 was associated with selected immunomodulatory mediators rather than a generalized checkpoint-high phenotype.

What is known and what is new?

• HMGA1 is a chromatin-associated oncogenic factor involved in tumor progression, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, stemness, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. Previous studies have documented HMGA1 dysregulation in several cancers, but its pan-cancer profile and its epigenetic, cellular, and microenvironmental context in PAAD remain incompletely characterized.

• This study integrates pan-cancer transcriptomic and prognostic analyses, promoter methylation analysis, single-cell trajectory inference, CellChat ligand-receptor analysis, public HMGA1-knockdown RNA sequencing reanalysis, The Cancer Genome Atlas-PAAD signature analysis, and in vitro functional assays. The findings support an association-based model linking promoter hypomethylation-associated HMGA1 activation to malignant epithelial stemness/plasticity, ECM/adhesion-dominant TME remodeling, selected immunomodulatory programs, and aggressive PAAD phenotypes.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• HMGA1 may represent a candidate prognostic biomarker and therapeutic vulnerability in PAAD. However, methylation editing, rescue experiments, tumor–immune co-culture, in vivo studies, and immunotherapy-treated cohorts are required before clinical translation.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) is one of the most aggressive malignancies of the digestive system (1). Due to its silent onset and rapid progression, the majority of patients are diagnosed with locally advanced or metastatic disease, with limited overall treatment benefit (2). Although advances in surgical techniques and multimodal treatment strategies have offered hope for some patients, the overall prognosis of PC remains largely unchanged, with a 5-year survival rate hovering around 10% (3,4). On the one hand, PC cells exhibit high malignancy and tend to metastasize early (5,6); on the other hand, the characteristic desmoplasia interacts with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), creating both physical and immunological barriers that not only limit effective drug delivery but also undermine the efficacy of immunotherapy (7-11). Accordingly, clarifying the key molecular events underlying PC initiation, progression, and metastasis, and identifying novel biomarkers for molecular classification, prognostic assessment, and targeted intervention (5,12,13), have become a central scientific challenge in basic and translational PC research.

High mobility group (HMG) proteins are important non-histone chromatin-binding proteins that play pivotal roles in dynamic remodeling of gene expression networks by altering chromatin architecture and regulating transcription complex assembly (14,15). HMGA family members (including HMGA1 and HMGA2) are prominently expressed during embryonic development but are expressed at very low levels or silenced in most adult normal tissues (16,17). Increasing evidence in recent years indicates that HMGA1 is abnormally activated in various solid tumors (18), where it functions as a key oncogene by promoting tumor cell proliferation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), stemness maintenance, metastasis, and treatment resistance (19,20). However, a systematic pan-cancer characterization of HMGA1 remains limited, and its expression patterns, prognostic significance, genetic alterations, and epigenetic regulation have not been comprehensively delineated (20,21). More importantly, the specific relationships between HMGA1 and the complex TME, cancer stemness properties, and invasive phenotypes in PC—a highly malignant tumor—remain to be further validated (22,23).

Here, we first performed a systematic pan-cancer analysis of HMGA1 expression patterns, prognostic significance, genetic alterations, promoter methylation, TME associations, and tumor stemness features. We then focused on PAAD and integrated bulk transcriptomic analysis, promoter methylation analysis, single-cell trajectory inference, CellChat ligand-receptor analysis, public HMGA1 knockdown RNA-seq data, and in vitro functional assays. This framework was designed to clarify whether HMGA1 is associated with malignant epithelial stemness/plasticity, extracellular matrix (ECM)/adhesion-dominant TME remodeling, selected immune-related mediator programs, and clinically relevant outcomes in PAAD, while avoiding overinterpretation of cross-sectional transcriptomic associations as direct causality. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0937/rc).

Methods

Data mining and preprocessing

Pan-cancer transcriptomic data (RNA-seq in HTSeq-FPKM format) and corresponding clinical annotations—including overall survival (OS), disease-free interval (DFI), progression-free interval (PFI), tumor stage, and histological grade—were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) (24). To ensure comparability across diverse samples, all expression matrices were normalized prior to downstream analyses. For cancer types lacking sufficient matched normal tissue samples within the TCGA cohort, the “Gene_DE” module of the TIMER2 web server (http://timer2.cistrome.org/) was utilized as an external reference to validate differential expression. For the primary cancers of interest—liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) and pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD)—the GEPIA2 platform (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#index) was employed to further evaluate stage-specific differences in HMGA1 expression.

Differential expression analysis of HMGA1

The messenger RNA (mRNA) expression levels of HMGA1 were evaluated across multiple cancer types by comparing tumor tissues with their adjacent normal counterparts. Depending on data distribution characteristics, two-group comparisons were performed using either Student’s t-test (for normally distributed data) or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (for non-normally distributed data). For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied. Results were visualized using box plots or violin plots. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. The false discovery rate (FDR) approach was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Survival analysis and cox regression modeling

Patients in each cancer cohort were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the median HMGA1 expression level. Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves were generated, and the statistical significance of differences in survival between the two risk groups was evaluated using the log-rank test. To systematically determine the pan-cancer prognostic value of HMGA1, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were utilized to analyze the association between its expression and various survival endpoints, including OS, disease-specific survival (DSS), DFI/disease-free survival (DFS), and PFI/progression-free survival (PFS) (25). The analytic results were presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and the results were visualized using forest plots.

TME scoring and immune subtype analysis

The ESTIMATE algorithm was employed to calculate the StromalScore and ImmuneScore for each tumor sample, followed by correlation analyses with HMGA1 expression levels. Depending on the data distribution, either Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients (R) and their corresponding P values were determined. Concurrently, differential HMGA1 expression was evaluated across the established TCGA immune subtypes (C1–C6, which encompass wound-healing, IFN-γ-dominant, and inflammatory phenotypes, among others) (26). For pairwise comparisons among multiple groups, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was used to correct for FDR.

Tumor stemness correlation analysis

Publicly available tumor stemness indices from the TCGA cohorts—specifically the DNA methylation-based stemness score (DNAss) and the RNA expression-based stemness score (RNAss)—were downloaded and integrated. Correlation analyses were conducted to evaluate the associations between HMGA1 expression levels and these two stemness metrics. Scatter plots overlaid with linear regression trendlines were generated to visualize the data, reporting the correlation coefficients and statistical significance.

Promoter methylation and its correlation with expression

To assess HMGA1 promoter methylation, we used the UALCAN portal based on TCGA methylation data generated with the Illumina HumanMethylation450 platform. The average methylation level of the HMGA1 promoter was compared between tumor and normal tissues. We further evaluated the association between HMGA1 promoter methylation and HMGA1 mRNA expression using Pearson or Spearman correlation analysis, and the results were displayed as scatter plots.

Genetic alteration landscape and survival comparisons

The genetic alteration profile of HMGA1 across cancers was examined using the cBioPortal database. Alteration types, including mutations, copy-number amplifications, and deep deletions, were collected and summarized to characterize the overall alteration landscape of HMGA1 across pan-cancer. Based on HMGA1 alteration status, patients were divided into altered and unaltered groups. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with the log-rank test was then performed to compare OS, DSS, DFS, and PFS between the two groups.

Immune cell infiltration and single-cell expression localization analysis

The TIMER2 platform was used to evaluate the relationship between HMGA1 expression and the infiltration levels of several immune cell types, including B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, neutrophils, macrophages, and dendritic cells. To further determine the cellular source of HMGA1 expression at single-cell resolution, publicly available PC single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets were analyzed through the TISCH2 platform. Based on the cell-type annotations, the distribution of HMGA1 expression across different cell populations was evaluated.

Drug sensitivity analysis

Drug sensitivity data for the NCI-60 cell lines were retrieved from the CellMiner database (https://discover.nci.nih.gov/cellminer/) to calculate the correlation between HMGA1 expression levels and cellular responses to various antitumor agents (expressed as Z-scores) (27). Data analysis and visualization were conducted using the “limma”, “ggpubr”, and “ggplot2” packages in R software (version 4.4.0). Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed, with the sign of the correlation coefficient (R) indicating a positive or negative correlation. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Cell culture and HMGA1 knockdown

Human PC cell lines PANC-1 and AsPC-1 were obtained from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2, using high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. HMGA1-targeting knockdown plasmids and their corresponding empty control vectors were synthesized and provided by the Nanjing Public Protein/Plasmid Library. Cell transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA; Cat. No. L3000015) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After transfection, PANC-1 and AsPC-1 cells were incubated for 48 hours to ensure efficient knockdown of HMGA1 expression. At 48–72 hours post-transfection, the knockdown efficiency was verified at both the mRNA and protein levels via reverse-transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and Western blot analysis, respectively.

Western blot analysis

Protein extraction from PANC-1 and AsPC-1 cells was performed using RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) that was supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 1 hour. Immunoblotting was performed using primary antibodies: rabbit monoclonal anti-HMGA1 (EPR7839; Abcam, Waltham, MA, USA; dilution 1:1,000) and rabbit polyclonal anti-β-actin (Abcam, Waltham, MA, USA; dilution 1:2,000). This was followed by incubation with appropriate secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized on X-ray film (Huadong Medicine, Hangzhou, China) and quantified using imaging software.

Reverse-transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Reverse transcription was performed using the SuperScript™III First-Strand Synthesis SuperMix kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to generate complementary DNA (cDNA). Gene expression was quantified by reverse-transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). Primer sequences were designed and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China).

Wound healing assay

PANC-1 and AsPC-1 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 6×105 cells per well. The following day, the cells were transfected with either the HMGA1 knockdown plasmid (shHMGA1) or the negative control plasmid (shNC). Once the cell reached >90% confluence, a linear wound was generated by scratching the cell monolayer with a sterile 200 µL pipette tip. The wells were then gently washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove detached cells. Cellular migration into the wounded area was observed and photographed under an inverted microscope at 0, 6, 12, and 24 hours after wounding to monitor wound closure.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Transfected cells were resuspended in 200 µL of serum-free medium and seeded at an appropriate density into the upper chambers of Transwell inserts (8 µm pore size; Corning, USA). The lower chambers were filled with 500 µL of DMEM or RPMI-1640 medium containing 20% FBS, which served as a chemoattractant. After 48 hours of incubation, non-migrating cells on the upper surface of the membrane were carefully removed using a cotton swab. Cells that had migrated to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and photographed under an inverted microscope. The cells were then counted in five randomly selected fields of view. For the invasion assay, Matrigel (BD Biosciences, Bedford, MA, USA) was diluted 1:8 in serum-free medium and applied to coat the upper surface of the Transwell membrane prior to the experiment. The inserts were incubated at 37 ℃ to allow the matrix to polymerize. Subsequent steps for cell seeding, incubation, fixation, staining, and counting were identical to those described for the migration assay.

Immunohistochemical staining

Six pairs of PAAD tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues were obtained from Jilin Provincial People’s Hospital. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jilin Provincial People’s Hospital (approval No. 2024129). All patients provided written informed consent prior to sample collection. Patient information was anonymized and de-identified to ensure privacy and confidentiality. IHC was performed using a rabbit monoclonal anti-HMGA1 antibody (EPR7839; Abcam, UK, Cat. No. ab129153) at a dilution of 1:500. Staining intensity and percentage of positive cells were evaluated independently by two experienced pathologists blinded to clinical information using a semi-quantitative scoring system (28).

Single-cell pseudotime, CellChat, and HMGA1 knockdown RNA-seq reanalysis

For PAAD single-cell reanalysis, malignant epithelial cells were extracted from the processed single-cell object and reclustered (labeled as clusters 0–3) to evaluate subcluster structure, HMGA1 expression, stemness module score, and pseudotime trajectory. The stemness module score was calculated using a curated gene set including EPCAM, KRT19, MUC1, CD44, PROM1, ALDH1A1, SOX2, NANOG, KLF4, CXCR4, CD24, ITGA6, LGR5, VIM, ZEB1, SNAI1, and SNAI2. Monocle 3 was used for pseudotime inference, with the HMGA1-low/stemness-low malignant cluster selected as the root state. Spearman correlation and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to evaluate the relationships among pseudotime, HMGA1 expression, and stemness module score.

CellChat was used to infer ligand-receptor communication among HMGA1-high malignant cells, HMGA1-low malignant cells, and non-malignant cell populations. HMGA1-high and HMGA1-low malignant cells were defined according to HMGA1 expression strata within the malignant-cell compartment. Outgoing communication strength, communication number, target-cell distribution, and representative ligand-receptor pairs were extracted for comparison. Particular attention was given to ECM-receptor, adhesion-related, and immunomodulatory axes involving LAMC2, LAMB3, LAMA3, COL6A1, COL6A2, CEACAM6, PPIA, BSG, ANXA1, FPR family receptors, APP, CD74, TREM2, TYROBP, LGALS9, CD44, HAVCR2, PLAU, and PLAUR.

Public HMGA1 knockdown RNA-seq data from GSE222890 were reanalyzed using DESeq2. The contrast was defined as shHMGA1 versus shControl; therefore, positive log2 fold-change or positive normalized enrichment score indicates higher expression or enrichment after HMGA1 knockdown. Differential expression analysis was combined with gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) for Hallmark pathways and a custom CSC/stemness marker gene set. These analyses were interpreted as transcriptional remodeling after HMGA1 perturbation rather than as uniform suppression of cancer stemness.

TCGA-PAAD integrative signature, methylation, cytokine/chemokine, and checkpoint analyses

For TCGA-PAAD integrative signature analysis, stemness/plasticity, TME-remodeling, and immune-suppression scores were calculated using the average z-scored expression of curated gene sets. The TME-remodeling signature was constructed from genes supported by CellChat-derived ECM/adhesion and ligand-receptor axes, whereas immune-suppression and checkpoint-related signatures were analyzed as reference immune features. Spearman correlation analysis and HMGA1-high/low group comparison were used to evaluate the coupling among HMGA1 expression, TME remodeling, immune suppression, and stemness/plasticity.

For methylation integration, TCGA-PAAD Illumina HumanMethylation450 beta values were analyzed. Nineteen HMGA1 promoter-associated CpG probes located in TSS1500, TSS200, 5’UTR, or first-exon regions were identified according to 450K annotation. The mean beta value of these promoter-associated probes was used as the HMGA1 promoter methylation score. Spearman correlation was used to assess relationships among promoter methylation, HMGA1 expression, TME-remodeling score, immune-suppression score, and stemness/plasticity score. Cox regression and median-based Kaplan-Meier analysis were used to evaluate the prognostic relevance of promoter methylation.

To examine immune-related mediators and immunotherapy-relevant molecular features, curated cytokine/chemokine and checkpoint/immunomodulatory gene panels were analyzed in TCGA-PAAD. Spearman correlation with HMGA1 expression and HMGA1-high/low Wilcoxon comparisons were performed. These analyses were integrated with CellChat results and interpreted as transcriptomic associations rather than direct evidence of cytokine secretion, immune-cell recruitment, or clinical immune checkpoint inhibitor response.

Statistical analysis

Bioinformatics analyses were mainly performed in R software (version 4.4.0), and most figures were generated using the ggplot2 package. Unless otherwise specified, two-group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Student’s t-test according to data distribution, and correlations were evaluated using Spearman or Pearson correlation analysis as appropriate. Survival differences were assessed using Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank tests, and Cox proportional hazards regression was used for survival modeling. All in vitro experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses for experimental data were completed in GraphPad Prism 9.0. A P value <0.05 or an FDR <0.05 was considered statistically significant where applicable. Statistical significance was indicated as follows: *P<0.05, **P<0.01, and ***P<0.001.

Results

Pan-cancer expression landscape of HMGA1

To characterize HMGA1 expression patterns across diverse human malignancies, we initially compared its mRNA levels between tumor and matched normal tissues using the TCGA pan-cancer cohort. As illustrated in Figure 1A,1B, HMGA1 was significantly upregulated in most cancer types, suggesting its potential as a broad-spectrum oncogenic driver. Specifically, elevated HMGA1 expression was observed in neoplastic tissues compared to their normal counterparts in bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC), cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (PCPG), rectum adenocarcinoma (READ), sarcoma (SARC), thyroid carcinoma (THCA), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), and uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC) (Figure 1C). By contrast, kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC) showed an opposite pattern, suggesting that HMGA1 dysregulation is not consistent across tumor types.

Prognostic implications of HMGA1 expression across human cancers

To evaluate the prognostic value of HMGA1, we first carried out univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis using TCGA pan-cancer data. HMGA1 expression was significantly associated with OS in several cancer types. In most tumors, HMGA1 behaved as a risk factor, with HR values above 1, whereas in a small number of cancers it appeared to have a protective effect, with HR values below 1, indicating that its prognostic significance may differ among tumor types (Figure 2A,2B). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed a similar pattern. Patients with high HMGA1 expression had significantly shorter OS in ACC, HNSC, KIRP, LGG, LIHC, LUAD, MESO, SARC, and STAD. Notably, the same association was observed in PAAD, where increased HMGA1 expression was significantly associated with worse OS (Figure 2C-2L).

We then examined the relationship between HMGA1 expression and DFS across 33 cancer types (Figure 3A). Higher HMGA1 expression was significantly associated with shorter DFS in both LIHC and PAAD (Figure 3B,3C). To further evaluate the stability of these prognostic associations, we analyzed multiple independent cohorts from the PrognoScan database. As shown in Table 1, HMGA1 was associated with poor prognosis in lung cancer, bladder cancer, and breast cancer, but showed a favorable prognostic association in colorectal cancer and ovarian cancer. When the prognostic impact of HMGA1 was compared across tumor types, LIHC and PAAD consistently showed the strongest negative associations across several survival endpoints, including OS, DSS, DFI, and PFI (Table 2). These findings indicate that HMGA1 is not only associated with OS but may also be involved in tumor recurrence and disease progression, particularly in LIHC and PAAD.

Genetic alteration landscape of HMGA1 and its clinical significance

To elucidate the genomic basis underlying aberrant HMGA1 expression, we queried the cBioPortal database to characterize its pan-cancer genetic alteration landscape. HMGA1 alterations were predominantly characterized by copy number amplifications, with minor fractions of mutations and deep deletions, and frequencies varying substantially across cancer types (Figure 3D). The distribution of mutation sites revealed that several missense mutations clustered near the region encoding the AT-hook DNA-binding domain, with the R58W mutation exhibiting the highest frequency (Figure 4A). Survival analyses stratified by genetic alteration status showed no significant OS difference between the altered and unaltered cohorts (Figure 4B). DFS differed significantly between the groups (Figure 4C, P=3.73e−05), whereas no significant. DSS difference was observed (Figure 4D). PFS also differed significantly (Figure 4E, P=2.82e−05). These findings link HMGA1 alterations more closely to recurrence and progression than to OS or DSS. This suggests that HMGA1 genetic alterations are closely linked to tumor progression risk, underscoring their clinical utility for predicting relapses rather than OS.

Epigenetic regulation of HMGA1 and its prognostic value

To investigate the epigenetic mechanisms driving HMGA1 transcriptional upregulation, we first compared promoter methylation levels between tumor and normal tissues in LIHC and PAAD. In both malignancies, promoter methylation was significantly reduced in tumor tissues compared with normal controls (Figure 5A,5B), and average promoter methylation was inversely associated with HMGA1 mRNA expression (Figure 5C,5D). We further expanded the PAAD methylation analysis at the promoter-probe level using TCGA-PAAD 450K data. Nineteen HMGA1 promoter-associated CpG probes located in TSS1500, TSS200, 5’UTR, or first-exon regions were identified, and the average beta value of these probes was used to represent HMGA1 promoter methylation.

HMGA1 promoter methylation showed a strong inverse correlation with HMGA1 expression in PAAD (Spearman’s rho =−0.633, P<2.2×10−16), supporting promoter hypomethylation- associated transcriptional activation of HMGA1. Survival analyses indicated that in LIHC, the associations between HMGA1 expression and OS, DFI, or PFI did not reach statistical significance (Figure 5E-5G), whereas elevated HMGA1 expression was significantly correlated with worse OS, DFI, and PFI in PAAD (Figure 5H-5J). Together, these data indicate that promoter hypomethylation-associated HMGA1 activation is a clinically relevant epigenetic event in PAAD.

Diagnostic efficacy of HMGA1 and its association with the TME and stemness

Having established the association between HMGA1 expression and clinical outcomes in LIHC and PAAD, we next evaluated its diagnostic value and its relationship with TME and stemness-related features. ROC analysis demonstrated that HMGA1 distinguished tumors from normal tissues in both LIHC [area under the curve (AUC) =0.866] and PAAD (AUC =0.803) (Figure 6A,6B). HMGA1 expression was also associated with immune subtype distributions in both malignancies (Figure 6C,6D). Correlation analysis with ESTIMATE-derived scores showed that HMGA1 expression was positively associated with ImmuneScore in LIHC but showed limited association with bulk ImmuneScore or StromalScore in PAAD (Figure 6E-6H). Regarding tumor stemness, HMGA1 showed context-dependent relationships with DNAss and RNAss across tumor types; in LIHC, HMGA1 was negatively associated with DNAss but positively associated with RNAss (Figure 6I,6J), whereas in PAAD, HMGA1 was positively associated with both stemness indices (Figure 6K,6L). Because bulk stemness indices may be influenced by tumor purity and stromal composition, these results were interpreted together with the single-cell trajectory, HMGA1 knockdown RNA-seq, and TCGA signature analyses rather than as standalone evidence of direct stemness regulation.

Single-cell sequencing and immune infiltration correlation analysis

To delineate the cellular context in which HMGA1 is expressed, we analyzed single-cell RNA-seq data and immune infiltration correlations. Public single-cell datasets from LIHC and PAAD showed that HMGA1 was predominantly expressed in malignant cells, with this pattern especially pronounced in PAAD (Figure 7A,7B). In LIHC, HMGA1 expression was positively correlated with the infiltration of multiple immune cell types, whereas in PAAD, bulk immune infiltration correlations were weaker and more selective (Figure 7C,7D). In the analysis, we therefore interpreted PAAD HMGA1 biology as primarily malignant epithelial-cell centered, but not exclusively cell autonomous. Subsequent malignant-cell pseudotime and CellChat analyses showed that HMGA1-high malignant epithelial cells were associated with stemness/plasticity-enriched states and enhanced ECM/adhesion-dominant communication with selected TME populations.

Single-cell RNA sequencing plots show the cellular distribution of HMGA1 expression in LIHC (A) and PAAD (B). TIMER-based immune infiltration correlations are shown in LIHC (C) and PAAD (D). Malignant epithelial cells were reclustered and analyzed for subcluster structure (E), HMGA1 expression (F), stemness score (G), pseudotime (H), and pseudotime distribution between HMGA1-low and HMGA1-high malignant cells (I). These analyses support predominant malignant-cell localization of HMGA1 in PAAD and motivated the malignant-cell pseudotime and CellChat analyses. LIHC, liver hepatocellular carcinoma; PAAD, pancreatic adenocarcinoma; UMAP, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection.

To further clarify the relationship between HMGA1 and malignant-cell stemness/plasticity in PAAD, we performed refined single-cell analysis of the malignant epithelial compartment (Figure 7E). HMGA1 expression and stemness module score were then projected onto the same UMAP embedding to visualize their distribution across the reclustered malignant-cell states (Figure 7F,7G). HMGA1-high malignant cells were enriched in a late pseudotime state compared with HMGA1-low malignant cells (Figure 7H). Quantitatively, HMGA1 expression was positively correlated with pseudotime (Spearman’s rho =0.594, P<2.2×10−16; Figure S1A), and the stemness module score also increased along pseudotime (Spearman’s rho =0.748, P<2.2×10−16; Figure S1B). HMGA1-high malignant cells showed markedly higher pseudotime values than HMGA1-low malignant cells (median pseudotime: 18.3 vs. 0.802; Wilcoxon P<2.2×10−16; Figure 7I). Subsequently, we examined whether HMGA1-high malignant cells exhibited altered tumor-microenvironment communication. CellChat analysis revealed that HMGA1-high malignant cells showed substantially stronger outgoing communication than HMGA1-low malignant cells (Figure S1C). HMGA1-high malignant cells mainly communicated with ductal/epithelial and acinar populations, with additional interactions toward myeloid and T/NK populations (Figure S1D). Representative ligand-receptor axes included LAMC2/LAMB3/LAMA3-CD44 or integrin complexes, COL6A1/COL6A2-CD44, CEACAM6-CEACAM6, PPIA-BSG, ANXA1-FPR family receptors, APP-CD74/TREM2-TYROBP, LGALS9-related interactions, and PLAU-PLAUR. These findings suggest that HMGA1-high malignant epithelial cells are associated with ECM-receptor, adhesion-related, and immunomodulatory communication programs rather than with generalized immune infiltration alone (Figure S1E). These results support an association between HMGA1-high malignant epithelial cells and a stemness/plasticity-enriched malignant-cell state.

HMGA1 knockdown reveals stemness/plasticity-related transcriptional remodeling

To address whether HMGA1 perturbation is linked to stemness/plasticity-related transcriptional programs, we reanalyzed public HMGA1 knockdown RNA-seq data from GSE222890 (Figure 8A). Because the contrast was defined as shHMGA1 versus shControl, positive normalized enrichment scores indicate enrichment after HMGA1 knockdown. GSEA showed significant enrichment of EMT [normalized enrichment score (NES) =2.818, FDR =9.0×10−10], TGF-β signaling (NES =1.913, FDR =5.42×10−4), Hedgehog signaling (NES =1.851, FDR =0.0046), IL6/JAK/STAT3 signaling (NES =1.585, FDR =0.0147), KRAS signaling (NES =1.777, FDR =2.42×10−4), and a custom CSC/stemness marker gene set (NES =1.620, FDR =0.0404) (Figure 8B). To integrate the single-cell and knockdown transcriptomic findings with patient-level data, we calculated TME- remodeling, immune-suppression, and stemness/plasticity scores in TCGA-PAAD (Figure 8C). HMGA1 expression was positively correlated with the TME-remodeling score, and HMGA1-high tumors showed significantly higher TME-remodeling scores than HMGA1-low tumors (Figure 8D). The TME-remodeling score was also positively correlated with the stemness/plasticity score (Spearman’s rho =0.250, P=7.86×10−4; Figure 8E). These findings indicate that HMGA1 perturbation is associated with broad remodeling of stemness/plasticity-related transcriptional programs, but they do not support a simple uniform suppression model.

Furthermore, we further integrated HMGA1 promoter methylation with the TME and stemness analyses. Nineteen HMGA1 promoter-associated CpG probes were annotated from TCGA-PAAD 450K methylation data and summarized in Figure S2A and Table S1. The mean HMGA1 promoter methylation beta value was strongly and inversely correlated with HMGA1 expression (Spearman’s rho =-0.633, P<2.2×10−16; Figure 8F) and negatively correlated with TME-remodeling score (Spearman’s rho =−0.347, P=2.47×10−6; Figure 8G), indicating that lower promoter methylation was associated with HMGA1 transcriptional activation and stronger ECM/adhesion-dominant TME-remodeling features. Promoter methylation was not significantly associated with bulk stemness/plasticity score (Spearman’s rho =0.136, P=0.07; Figure 8H). In survival analysis, promoter methylation as a continuous variable was associated with a lower risk of death. In the median-based Kaplan-Meier analysis, although statistical significance was not reached, the methylation-high group still showed a protective trend (HR =0.689, P=0.08; Figure 8I). These findings place promoter hypomethylation upstream of HMGA1 transcriptional activation and TME remodeling, while indicating that methylation should not be interpreted as directly driving bulk immune suppression or stemness.

To further connect HMGA1 expression with immune-related microenvironmental features, we analyzed a curated cytokine, chemokine, and immunomodulatory ligand panel in TCGA-PAAD. HMGA1 was positively correlated with PPIA (rho =0.581), PLAU (rho =0.446), IL18 (rho =0.399), LIF (rho =0.339), ANXA1 (rho =0.338), IL11 (rho =0.322), LGALS9 (rho =0.278), TGFB1 (rho =0.265), and CSF2 (rho =0.188), all with FDR <0.05 (Figure S2B). HMGA1-high tumors showed significantly higher expression of selected immune-related mediators, including IL11, PLAU, LIF, PPIA, IL18, ANXA1, and LGALS9, whereas several classical chemokines, including CXCL12, CCL4, CCL2, CCL5, CSF1, and CSF3, were lower in HMGA1-high tumors or negatively correlated with HMGA1 (Figure S2C). Thus, HMGA1-high PAAD tumors were associated with a selective immune-related mediator pattern rather than uniform upregulation of chemokines. Additionally, we also analyzed checkpoint-related and immunotherapy-relevant genes. HMGA1 was positively correlated with PPIA (rho =0.581), PLAU (rho =0.446), ANXA1 (rho =0.338), CD276 (rho =0.333), LGALS9 (rho =0.278), TGFB1 (rho =0.265), and CD47 (rho =0.224), all with FDR <0.05 (Figure S3A). HMGA1-high tumors showed higher expression of PLAU, CD276, PPIA, ANXA1, LGALS9, and TGFB1 (Figure S3B). The overall checkpoint score was not associated with HMGA1 expression (rho =−0.047, P=0.53) or HMGA1-high/low grouping (P=0.30; Figure S4A), and several T-cell checkpoint or exhaustion-related genes, including PDCD1, CTLA4,LAG3, TIGIT, HAVCR2, PDCD1LG2, and BTLA, were negatively associated with HMGA1 or lower in HMGA1-high tumors. Exploratory HMGA1/PD-L1 combined survival stratification was not statistically significant (global P=0.20; Figure S4B). Therefore, HMGA1 should be interpreted as a marker of selected immunomodulatory and TME-remodeling-related programs, not as a predictor of generalized checkpoint activation or clinical immunotherapy response.

Correlation between HMGA1 expression and drug sensitivity

To explore the clinical therapeutic implications of HMGA1, we analyzed the correlation between its expression and responses to various anti-tumor agents using the CellMiner/NCI-60 database. The results indicated that HMGA1 expression levels were significantly associated with responses to several antimetabolite drugs. Specifically, HMGA1 expression exhibited significant positive correlations with the reactivity to methotrexate (Figure 9A), 6-thioguanine (Figure 9B), 6-mercaptopurine (Figure 9C), floxuridine (Figure 9D), dacarbazine (Figure 9E), cytarabine (Figure 9F), fludarabine (Figure 9G), econazole nitrate (Figure 9H), marizomib (Figure 9I), idebenone (Figure 9J), vorinostat (Figure 9K), clofarabine (Figure 9L), and cytarabine hydrochloride (Figure 9M). Conversely, a significant negative correlation was observed with the response to mithramycin (Figure 9N). While these findings provide preliminary evidence that HMGA1 expression may influence the efficacy of certain chemotherapeutics, it is important to note that the NCI-60 panel comprises diverse cell lines from multiple cancer types, and Z-score definitions may vary across platforms. Therefore, these correlations serve primarily as candidate references requiring further validation in PC-specific models.

HMGA1 knockdown suppresses PC cell migration and invasion

To further validate the biological function of HMGA1 in PC progression, we initially evaluated its expression levels and subcellular localization in clinical PC specimens. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and IHC confirmed that the HMGA1 protein was highly expressed in tumor tissues—predominantly localized to the nucleus—whereas it was virtually undetectable in adjacent non-tumor tissues (Figure 10A). We then established HMGA1 knockdown models in PANC-1 and AsPC-1 cells. RT-qPCR and Western blot results showed that sh-HMGA1 transfection effectively reduced HMGA1 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels, indicating successful knockdown (Figure 10B,10C). Wound healing assays showed that silencing HMGA1 markedly suppressed the migratory ability of PC cells (Figure 10D-10F). Consistently, Transwell assays demonstrated that the numbers of both migrated cells and Matrigel-invading cells significantly decreased after HMGA1 knockdown (Figure 10G,10H). Together, these results indicate that HMGA1 plays an important role in promoting the migration and invasion of PC cells, and further support its potential value as a therapeutic target and metastasis-related biomarker in PC.

Discussion

Through a systematic pan-cancer analysis coupled with multi-omic and in vitro functional analyses, this study comprehensively elucidates the expression profile and clinical significance of HMGA1 across diverse human malignancies, with a focus on PAAD. HMGA1 is markedly upregulated in most cancer types and its overexpression is associated with unfavorable outcomes. In PAAD, the data support an association-based regulatory model in which promoter hypomethylation is linked to HMGA1 transcriptional activation. Activated HMGA1 is predominantly localized in malignant epithelial cells, where it is associated with stemness/plasticity-enriched states, enhanced ECM/adhesion-dominant TME remodeling, selected immunomodulatory mediator programs, and aggressive functional phenotypes such as migration and invasion. This model is intentionally interpreted as an integrative association framework rather than as proof of a linear causal pathway.

The advent of large-scale public repositories, such as TCGA, has established pan-cancer analyses as an indispensable approach for uncovering universal principles of tumorigenesis (29-31). Our pan-cancer transcriptomic profiling reveals that HMGA1 is significantly overexpressed in the vast majority of epithelial malignancies, including PAAD. This observation aligns with its identity as a chromatin architectural factor that is typically silenced post- embryogenesis (14,20). Such broad-spectrum reactivation suggests that HMGA1 likely orchestrates core malignant phenotypes shared across diverse tumors, including uncontrolled proliferation, dedifferentiation, and the acquisition of metastatic potential (18,32-34). Interestingly, HMGA1 exhibits a paradoxical downregulation in certain cancers, such as KIRC. This divergence from its typical expression pattern points to a tumor-type-dependent contextuality in HMGA1 biology, warranting further mechanistic investigation.

Survival analysis further highlighted the clinical significance of HMGA1. In our study, high HMGA1 expression was associated with worse OS in multiple cancer types, and in PAAD, it was also closely related to DFI and PFI, suggesting a link with postoperative recurrence and disease progression. Considering the high recurrence rate and aggressive clinical behavior of PC (35,36). Biomarkers that can help identify patients at higher risk of relapses are of considerable clinical value. Our findings suggest that HMGA1 may serve not only as a prognostic marker, but also as a molecule involved in PC progression. This view is further supported by the genetic alteration analysis. Although HMGA1 alterations, particularly copy number amplification, were not significantly associated with OS, they were clearly associated with poorer DFS and PFS, suggesting that HMGA1 dysregulation may be more closely involved in tumor recurrence and progression than in overall mortality.

The expanded methylation analysis places HMGA1 promoter hypomethylation upstream of the HMGA1-associated regulatory network in PAAD. Nineteen promoter-associated CpG probes were identified, and their mean beta value showed a strong inverse association with HMGA1 expression. Lower promoter methylation was also associated with higher TME-remodeling score and poorer survival-related features. These findings suggest that promoter hypomethylation may contribute to HMGA1 transcriptional activation, which in turn is linked to ECM/adhesion-dominant microenvironmental remodeling. However, promoter methylation was not significantly associated with bulk stemness/plasticity score or immune-suppression score. Therefore, the methylation-HMGA1 axis should not be interpreted as a simple promoter hypomethylation-immune suppression-stemness cascade. Experimental methylation editing, chromatin accessibility assays, and functional rescue studies will be required to determine whether HMGA1 promoter hypomethylation directly drives this oncogenic regulatory program.

A key contribution of the study is the refined interpretation of HMGA1 biology in PAAD. Single-cell data indicate that HMGA1 is primarily enriched in malignant epithelial cells, supporting a malignant-cell-centered role. However, the newly added pseudotime and CellChat analyses show that this role is not restricted to cell-autonomous migration and invasion. HMGA1-high malignant cells were enriched in late pseudotime states with increasing stemness module scores, and they exhibited markedly stronger outgoing communication through ECM-receptor, adhesion-related, and immunomodulatory ligand-receptor axes. These findings support a model in which HMGA1-high malignant epithelial cells couple stemness/plasticity-associated states with TME remodeling. This interpretation replaces the earlier overly narrow conclusion that HMGA1 acts without extensive microenvironmental involvement in PAAD.

Tumor stemness and plasticity are core drivers of intratumor heterogeneity, metastatic dissemination, and therapeutic resistance. In the original analysis, HMGA1 showed positive associations with established stemness indices in PAAD. The analysis adds important nuance. At the single-cell level, HMGA1-high malignant epithelial cells were located in a late pseudotime state, and stemness module score increased along pseudotime. Public HMGA1 knockdown RNA-seq data further showed remodeling of EMT, TGF-β, Hedgehog, IL6/JAK/STAT3, and CSC/stemness-related programs. However, the bulk TCGA stemness/plasticity signature did not uniformly increase with HMGA1 expression. Therefore, we now interpret HMGA1 as being associated with malignant epithelial stemness/plasticity in a cell-context-dependent manner, with stronger support from cell-type-resolved and perturbation-associated analyses than from a single bulk stemness score.

The immunotherapy-related results should be interpreted with caution. Because no PAAD cohort with matched immune checkpoint inhibitor treatment outcomes was available, the current study cannot determine whether HMGA1 predicts clinical response to immunotherapy. The checkpoint analysis only indicates that HMGA1 is associated with selected immunotherapy-relevant molecular features, including PPIA, PLAU, ANXA1, CD276, LGALS9, TGFB1, and CD47. Prospective PAAD immunotherapy cohorts and functional tumor-immune co-culture models will be required to test whether HMGA1 has predictive value for immune checkpoint blockade.

The cytokine/chemokine and checkpoint analyses further refine the interpretation of the HMGA1-associated microenvironment. HMGA1-high PAAD tumors were associated with increased PPIA, PLAU, IL18, LIF, ANXA1, IL11, LGALS9, TGFB1, and CD276-related features, many of which were consistent with CellChat-derived ligand-receptor axes such as PPIA-BSG, PLAU-PLAUR, ANXA1-FPR, and LGALS9-related interactions. In contrast, several classical chemokines, including CXCL12, CCL2, CCL4, CCL5, and CSF1, were reduced or negatively associated with HMGA1. Similarly, HMGA1 was not associated with CD274/PD-L1 expression or an overall checkpoint-high phenotype, and several T-cell checkpoint or exhaustion-related genes were lower in HMGA1-high tumors. These findings suggest a selective immunomodulatory mediator program rather than generalized immune suppression or broad checkpoint activation.

Recent literature further supports the biological context of the framework. A 2025 PC study reported that FOSL1 promotes malignant progression partly through HMGA1 and that HMGA1 knockdown inhibits PC cell stemness, invasion, migration, and multidrug efflux-related programs (37). Recent reviews also emphasize the clinical relevance of integrating biomarkers, TME modulation, and immunotherapy in PC (38), the central role of immunosuppressive and cytokine/chemokine-driven microenvironmental networks in PC progression and therapy resistance (39), and the need to reprogram the PC TME to overcome immunotherapy resistance (40). In addition, recent evidence on PC epigenetic dysregulation highlights the value of DNA methylation-based biomarkers and epigenetic regulation in disease progression and clinical translation (41). These studies are consistent with our conservative interpretation that HMGA1 is linked to promoter hypomethylation, malignant-cell plasticity, ECM/adhesion-dominant TME remodeling, and selected immunomodulatory programs, while further experimental validation remains necessary.

Despite systematically delineating the role of HMGA1 in pan-cancer and PAAD contexts, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, many findings are derived from public databases such as TCGA and GEO; inherent selection biases and batch effects cannot be fully excluded. Second, although in vitro assays confirmed the pro-migratory and pro-invasive functions of HMGA1 in PC cells, the present study did not include sphere-formation assays, CSC marker validation, cytokine secretion assays, methylation editing, co-culture experiments, in vivo metastasis models, or immune checkpoint blockade-treated PAAD cohorts. Third, single-cell pseudotime, CellChat ligand-receptor inference, methylation integration, and signature-based analyses are cross-sectional and computational; they support association-based models but cannot establish definitive causal direction. Future studies should validate the HMGA1 promoter methylation-HMGA1 activation-TME remodeling-stemness/plasticity axis using methylation editing, chromatin accessibility profiling, rescue experiments, tumor-immune co-culture systems, spatial transcriptomics, cytokine/chemokine assays, and clinically annotated immunotherapy cohorts.

Conclusions

This study systematically reveals the pan-cancer expression pattern of HMGA1 and its clinically relevant oncogenic role in PAAD. HMGA1 is overexpressed across multiple tumor types, and in PAAD this activation is associated with promoter hypomethylation and unfavorable clinical outcomes. The analyses support an association-based model in which HMGA1 is enriched in malignant epithelial cells and is linked to stemness/plasticity-enriched states, ECM/adhesion- dominant TME remodeling, selected cytokine/chemokine and checkpoint-related mediator programs, and enhanced PC cell migration and invasion. These findings identify HMGA1 as a prognostic biomarker candidate and potential therapeutic vulnerability in PAAD, while emphasizing that causal mechanisms and immunotherapy-predictive value require further experimental and clinical validation.
