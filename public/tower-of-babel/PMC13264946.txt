Pregnant women’s perceptions and experiences of social media communication for antenatal care: a scoping review
Authors: Fabrice Djouma Nembot, Collins Buh Nkum, Blake Nkemngu Afutendem, Dora Ateudjieu, Deborah Kakapen, Zacheus Ebongo Nanje, Fancis Duhamel Nang Nang, Jerome Ateudjieu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo map and synthesise existing evidence on pregnant women’s perceptions and experiences of social media communication for antenatal care (ANC).DesignScoping review.Data sourcesFour electronic databases (PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science and Google Scholar) alongside ‘grey’ and supplementary searches were conducted between December 202–January 2026.Study selectionAll studies reporting pregnant women’s perceptions or experiences of social media communication for ANC.Data extraction and synthesisData were extracted independently by two reviewers using a structured charting framework. Extracted data were synthesised using a descriptive and narrative approach, with pregnant women’s perceptions and experiences analysed through reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsSix studies met the inclusion criteria. Across platforms including WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram and WeChat, pregnant women generally perceived social media communication as acceptable and beneficial, particularly for accessing trustworthy information, reassurance between visits, peer support and flexible engagement. Experiences varied by platform, moderation model and context. Key challenges included limited personalisation, variability in moderators’ capacity and responsiveness, digital literacy barriers, data affordability, privacy concerns and sociocultural influences. Equity-related considerations were recurrent, highlighting the potential for uneven experiences if digital communication is not carefully designed and standardised.ConclusionsSocial media communication is generally experienced positively by pregnant women as a complement to routine ANC, particularly when professionally moderated and responsive to women’s informational needs. However, variability in experiences and equity-related challenges underscore the need for further research and careful implementation. This scoping review provides a preliminary mapping of the evidence and identifies priorities for future qualitative synthesis, primary research and the development of inclusive, person-centred digital ANC communication strategies.Keywords: Pregnancy, Social Media, Perception

Introduction

Antenatal care (ANC) is a cornerstone of maternal and newborn health, providing an opportunity for timely health education, risk identification and psychosocial support during pregnancy. Effective communication between pregnant women and healthcare providers is central to the quality of ANC, particularly during a period characterised by heightened information needs, emotional vulnerability and frequent uncertainty.1 2 In recent years, digital health technologies have increasingly been integrated into ANC delivery, with social media platforms such as WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram and WeChat emerging as widely used channels for health information exchange, peer interaction and provider-patient communication, especially in contexts where access to in-person care may be constrained.3 4

Pregnancy has been consistently described as a period of intensified information seeking, during which women actively seek guidance on symptoms, lifestyle behaviours and fetal development beyond what is provided during routine clinic visits. Evidence suggests that pregnant women frequently turn to digital and online resources to supplement facility-based care, valuing accessibility, immediacy and the ability to revisit information as needed. Trust and credibility are particularly salient during this period, with women expressing stronger preferences for information that is perceived as reliable and linked to health professionals or recognised health institutions.2 5

Social media platforms differ from other digital health modalities by enabling group-based interaction, peer exchange and asynchronous communication. These features have facilitated their adoption for antenatal education, appointment reminders, emotional support and direct communication with healthcare providers. The use of social media for ANC expanded rapidly during periods of service disruption, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, further accelerating experimentation with digitally mediated models of care. While a growing body of research has examined the feasibility and acceptability of digital ANC interventions, much of this work has focused on clinical or behavioural outcomes rather than on women’s lived experiences of engaging with social media as a communication tool.4

Several systematic and scoping reviews have synthesised evidence on mobile health, telehealth and digital interventions for maternal and child health. These reviews commonly report positive effects on knowledge, service utilisation or psychosocial outcomes but often aggregate heterogeneous digital modalities including websites, mobile applications, SMS, teleconsultations and social media.6,8 As a result, the specific experiential dimensions of social media communication such as peer interaction, moderation practices, message retrievability and platform-specific affordances remain underexplored. Moreover, existing reviews have paid limited attention to equity-related considerations, including digital literacy, data affordability, privacy concerns and sociocultural influences, which are critical to understanding differential experiences of digital ANC communications.3

The available primary studies on social media communication for ANC are diverse in design, platform and setting and are often exploratory in nature. This heterogeneity, combined with variation in reported outcomes and contexts, makes a scoping review particularly appropriate. A scoping approach allows for systematic mapping of the breadth and characteristics of the evidence, identification of key concepts and gaps, and clarification of how pregnant women experience social media-based communication across platforms and settings, without restricting inclusion based on study design or outcome measures.

The objective of this scoping review is to map and synthesise existing evidence on pregnant women’s perceptions and experiences of social media communication for ANC. Specifically, the review addresses the following question: what is known about how pregnant women perceive and experience social media communication used to support ANC? Using a Population-Concept-Context framework which is a structured tool used primarily in scoping reviews to define the research scope by identifying the specific group of interest (Population), the core phenomenon being explored (Concept), and the setting or geographic location (Context),9 the review focuses on pregnant women (population), their perceptions and experiences of social media communication for ANC (concept), across all healthcare settings and geographic contexts (context). By centring women’s perspectives, this review aims to contribute to a clearer understanding of how social media is experienced within ANC and to inform future research, implementation, and policy discussions related to person-centred and equitable digital maternal health interventions.

Methods

Protocol and registration

Our protocol was drafted using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis Protocols extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR),10 which was revised by the research team. The final protocol was registered with the Open Science Framework (DOI: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/Z6KBS).

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were defined a priori using a Population Concept Context (PCC) framework, consistent with PRISMA-ScR guidance.10

Population

Studies were eligible if they included pregnant women as primary participants. Studies that included postpartum women were retained only when pregnancy-related perceptions or experiences were explicitly reported. Studies focusing exclusively on healthcare providers or other stakeholders without reporting pregnant women’s perspectives were excluded. This criterion was selected to ensure that the synthesis remained centred on women’s lived experiences during pregnancy.

Concept

The concept of interest was social media communication for ANC, defined as the use of interactive social media platforms to deliver information, support or communication related to pregnancy or antenatal services. Platforms of interest included WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram, WeChat and Telegram. Studies focusing solely on SMS, voice calls or standalone mobile applications without a social media component were excluded. This restriction was applied to isolate features specific to social media, such as group interaction and asynchronous communication.

Context

All healthcare and geographic settings were eligible for inclusion. No restrictions were applied based on country income level or health system type. This inclusive approach was adopted to capture diverse experiences and identify geographic and contextual gaps in the literature.

Types of evidence and publication characteristics

Eligible sources included qualitative studies, mixed-methods studies, surveys and programme evaluation reports that reported pregnant women’s perceptions, experiences, acceptability or perspectives of social media-based antenatal communication. Randomised or non-randomised trials were included only if experiential outcomes were reported. Conference abstracts without full-text availability were excluded.

No language restrictions were applied. Although no non-English studies met the inclusion criteria, the review process allowed for their inclusion. Where potentially eligible non-English studies were identified, translation was planned using professional translation tools and, when necessary, reviewed by multilingual members of the research team. No restrictions were applied based on year of publication, as social media use in ANC is a relatively recent and evolving phenomenon, and the review aimed to capture the full temporal scope of available evidence.

Information sources

A comprehensive literature search was conducted on 15 December 2025. The following electronic databases were searched: PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science. These databases were selected to ensure broad coverage of biomedical, public health, nursing, midwifery and qualitative research literature.

In addition to database searching, grey literature was sought through manual screening of reference lists of included studies, scanning relevant organisational and institutional websites (eg, WHO, UNICEF). Reference list screening was also conducted for all included articles to identify additional relevant sources.

Search

A comprehensive search strategy was developed to maximise sensitivity for studies exploring pregnant women’s perceptions and experiences of social media communication for ANC. The search combined terms related to pregnancy (eg, pregnant women, antenatal women), social media platforms (eg, WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram, WeChat, Telegram), ANC (eg, prenatal care, maternal health services) and qualitative or experiential outcomes (eg, perceptions, experiences, acceptability, interviews, focus groups).

The social media platforms included in this review were selected based on their global reach, functional characteristics and documented relevance in digital health communication. WhatsApp is among the most widely used messaging applications globally, with particularly high penetration in sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America and parts of Europe. Its low data requirements, end-to-end encryption and capacity for group-based, asynchronous messaging make it especially suitable for delivering health information and facilitating peer and provider interaction in resource-constrained settings.3 11 12 Facebook remains one of the largest social networking platforms worldwide, offering diverse functionalities such as private groups, multimedia content sharing and community engagement features that have been widely used in health promotion and behavioural interventions, although its usage patterns vary across age groups and regions.11 13 Instagram, also widely adopted, is primarily oriented toward visual communication, emphasising images and short videos, and is often used for health information dissemination, though it may be less conducive to sustained peer interaction compared with messaging platforms.14 WeChat is highly prevalent in China and represents a multifunctional ecosystem integrating messaging, social networking and service delivery, including healthcare services, thereby enabling direct communication between patients and providers within formal health system structures.15 Telegram, while less frequently represented in maternal health studies, offers similar group-based communication features with enhanced privacy settings and the ability to support large groups, which may be advantageous for scalable health communication interventions.12 These platforms were included because they support interactive, group-based and asynchronous communication, which are central to social media-based ANC. Their differing functionalities and regional dominance provide important insights into how platform-specific affordances and contextual factors shape pregnant women’s experiences of digital health communication.

No restrictions were applied on publication date or study design. The complete and reproducible search strategies for all databases, including MeSH terms and database-specific adaptations, are provided in online supplemental file 1. The search was conducted by the review team and was not formally peer reviewed. Selection of sources of evidence

All records retrieved from the searches were imported into Rayyan for management,16 deduplication and screening. Automatic deduplication was performed within Rayyan and supplemented by manual verification.

Title and abstract screening were conducted independently by two reviewers (BNA and DK) using a standardised screening guide. Records were excluded at this stage if they clearly met one or more exclusion criteria. Full texts of potentially relevant articles were then retrieved and assessed independently by the same reviewers for final inclusion.

Discrepancies at either the title and abstract stage or the full-text stage were resolved through discussion. When consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (FDNN) adjudicated the decision. The screening process and reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were documented and will be reported using a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram.

Data charting process

Data charting was conducted using a structured extraction matrix developed iteratively by the review team. The charting form was piloted on a subset of included studies to ensure clarity and consistency, and minor revisions were made prior to full extraction.

Two reviewers (BNA and DK) independently charted data from all included sources. Charted data were compared and discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with arbitration by FDNN when necessary. Authors of included studies were contacted on a case-by-case basis if clarification was required to support accurate charting.

Data items

The following data items were extracted from each included source: Study metadata (author, year, country, setting); study design and methods; social media platform(s) and communication formats; intervention or communication characteristics (eg, moderation model, duration, frequency); participant characteristics (eg, age, parity, education where reported); pregnant women’s reported perceptions and experiences; reported benefits, challenges and acceptability; equity-related observations (eg, digital literacy, access, privacy, sociocultural influences); author-identified limitations and research gaps.

Data items involving interpretation (eg, thematic categorisation of experiences) were discussed and agreed on by the review team to ensure consistency.

Critical appraisal of individual sources of evidence

Consistent with PRISMA-ScR guidance,10 no formal critical appraisal of included sources was conducted. The objective of the review was to map the scope and nature of the evidence rather than to assess methodological quality or confidence in findings. Findings were not weighted or excluded based on study quality.

Synthesis of results

Charted data were synthesised using a descriptive and narrative approach. Study characteristics and intervention features were summarised in tables to map the evidence base. Pregnant women’s perceptions and experiences were synthesised using a reflexive thematic analysis approach following Braun and Clarke, combining inductive coding from the data with deductive organisation guided by the review objectives and PCC framework,17 18 with findings organised across key experiential domains such as informational support, peer interaction, trust and moderation, communication formats, equity considerations and continuity of care.

Results are presented using a combination of narrative text and tabular summaries to provide a comprehensive overview of the available evidence and to identify gaps for future research. No dedicated qualitative data analysis software (eg, NVivo or Dedoose) was used; coding and theme development were conducted manually using Microsoft Excel and Microsoft Word to support reflexive thematic analysis.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Selection of sources of evidence

After database searching, a total of 196 records were identified from PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and grey literature sources. Following removal of 15 duplicate records, 181 records remained for title and abstract screening. At this stage, 168 records were excluded for not meeting the eligibility criteria, leaving 13 full-text articles to be retrieved and assessed for eligibility. Of these, 7 articles were excluded for the following reasons: irrelevant population (n=3), irrelevant concept (n=3) and full text unavailability (n=1). No additional studies were identified through backward or forward citation tracking. The remaining six studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final synthesis, as summarised in the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (figure 1).

Synthesis of results

Social media platforms and patterns of use

Across the included studies, pregnant women described their experiences of social media communication primarily in relation to how different platforms fit into their daily lives, supported reassurance between antenatal visits, and enabled or constrained interaction with health professionals and peers.14 15 19 20 In addition to these relational dimensions, women highlighted the practical convenience of having antenatal information consolidated in one accessible digital space. Women’s accounts emphasised familiarity, ease of use, opportunities for timely reassurance and the ability to locate, revisit and review previously shared information when questions or uncertainties arose during pregnancy.15 20

Across studies using WhatsApp, pregnant women consistently described the platform as an accessible and familiar means of communicating about pregnancy-related concerns. In the Kenyan study, women reported that moderated WhatsApp groups enabled them to ask questions, share experiences and receive support from both peers and health workers, contributing to sustained participation.19 Women valued the low threshold for engagement, noting that communication could occur alongside daily activities without the need for additional clinic visits. In the Brazilian Godmother Project, pregnant women similarly experienced WhatsApp as a direct and reassuring link to nurse educators during pregnancy.20 Beyond real-time exchanges, women appreciated the ability to scroll back through prior messages and educational materials within the group, allowing them to re-check guidance and refresh information as needed. Being assigned to gestational age-specific groups allowed women to interact with others facing similar concerns, which they described as comforting and relevant. Women emphasised the benefit of timely responses to questions as well as the reassurance of knowing that previously shared advice remained accessible between antenatal visits, reinforcing continuity of care.20

Pregnant women’s experiences with Instagram reflected a combination of appreciation and constraint. Participants generally perceived Instagram content as visually appealing and informative, and valued professionally designed posts that supported understanding of pregnancy-related lifestyle information.14 However, women also described reluctance to actively contribute, particularly when participation involved sharing personal photos or experiences. Perceptions of what constituted acceptable or ‘Instagram-worthy’ content reduced some women’s willingness to post. In addition, women noted that Instagram’s interface made it more difficult to sustain interaction and to develop a sense of community when compared with other platforms, limiting its role as a space for mutual exchange and positioning it more as a channel for receiving information.14

In the Chinese qualitative study,15 pregnant women described WeChat as a convenient extension of ANC that enabled rapid access to nurse-vetted information and reassurance. Women reported using WeChat not only to clarify symptoms and seek guidance but also to scroll through earlier conversations or educational messages to re-check recommendations at later points in pregnancy. This ability to revisit previously shared information was perceived as particularly valuable during periods of uncertainty or when similar concerns re-emerged. At the same time, women’s engagement varied according to digital literacy.15 Some reported difficulties navigating features such as downloading educational materials or opening links, which constrained their participation. Family and cultural contexts further shaped experiences, as women described negotiating relatives’ expectations or concerns regarding the legitimacy and safety of communicating with nurses through social media. Despite these challenges, trust in nurses and hospital-affiliated communication generally supported continued use.15

Experiences with Facebook also varied across studies. In Taiwan, pregnant women using Facebook to access a prenatal mindful yoga programme valued the flexibility to view video content at home and at convenient times, which supported engagement in pregnancy-related self-care.21 Some women preferred Facebook over Digital Video Disc (DVDs) because of ease of access, while others favoured DVDs due to larger screen size, highlighting individual differences in how digital content was experienced.21 In the US-based pilot intervention, pregnant women reported mixed experiences with Facebook groups. While the platform itself was accessible, some women did not perceive the group component as the most useful aspect of the intervention and suggested that greater opportunities for peer interaction or alternative platforms might enhance engagement, underscoring how platform features influenced perceived value of social media-based support.

Communication formats and delivery modalities

Pregnant women described social media communication for ANC as occurring through formats and delivery modalities that needed to fit within the rhythms of everyday life during pregnancy.15 20 Group-based and provider-led interactions were experienced as particularly valuable when they allowed flexible access to information and reassurance alongside work, household responsibilities and caregiving.21 22 Across platforms, short text messages, images, infographics, videos and voice notes were perceived as practical and manageable formats, enabling women to engage with antenatal information at their own pace and to return to content when needed.14 15 20

In the Godmother Project, pregnant women expressed clear preferences for short texts, short videos and images or infographics, emphasising that concise formats supported understanding and facilitated rapid retrieval of information during moments of uncertainty.20 Women valued content that could be quickly consulted rather than lengthy messages requiring sustained attention, particularly when seeking clarification between scheduled antenatal visits. Similarly, in the WeChat-based study, pregnant women reported that step-by-step explanations, visual materials and voice messages enhanced comprehension, especially for women with lower digital literacy or limited confidence navigating written information.15

Experiences with Instagram further highlighted how format features shaped women’s engagement. While visually appealing posts were appreciated, pregnant women consistently emphasised that clarity and relevance were more important than aesthetic presentation.14 Instagram Stories were perceived as engaging and interactive, but some women noted that their temporary nature limited usefulness for antenatal education they wished to revisit. As a result, static posts were preferred over Stories for information intended for repeated consultation. Women also commented on the timing of content delivery, indicating that posts or Stories shared in the evening were more likely to be viewed, as daytime hours were often constrained by work or household responsibilities.14

Perceptions of intervention duration and posting frequency further shaped pregnant women’s experiences. In the Instagram-based intervention, women reported that the short-term duration of the programme and the regular but limited number of daily posts were acceptable and not experienced as burdensome.14 In contrast, in longer-running WhatsApp-based initiatives such as the Godmother Project, women expressed sustained satisfaction with ongoing access to moderated groups across pregnancy, particularly when communication remained responsive and content was aligned with their gestational stage.20 Across studies, pregnant women’s accounts indicate that social media communication for ANC was most positively experienced when content was concise, appropriately timed during the day and delivered at a frequency that supported engagement without contributing to information overload.

Social media communication content

Across studies, pregnant women emphasised the importance of social media content that addressed immediate, practical and pregnancy-specific concerns.1415 19,22 Women consistently reported that the information shared through social media platforms was trustworthy, useful and relevant.

Across platforms, women generally perceived the content as clear and helpful. In the Godmother Project, women agreed that the information posted by nurse educators was trustworthy, useful, relevant and easy to retrieve, and many indicated high overall satisfaction with the educational content provided.20 Women valued having confidence in the credibility of the information, especially in contrast to the broader and often unreliable pregnancy-related content encountered on the internet. In the Instagram study, participants reported that posts included useful pregnancy-related information presented in an understandable way.14 However, some women also expressed critical reflections on content originality. A subset of participants noted that certain posts did not contain novel information, as they had previously encountered similar messages on other pregnancy-related Instagram accounts. These women voiced a desire for more personalised and tailored content, including guidance that reflected different stages of pregnancy, more specific exercise routines appropriate for varying gestational phases or dietary advice aligned with different eating patterns or personal needs.14

Preferences for personalisation were echoed in other contexts. In the Godmother Project, women reported frequent engagement with content related to fetal development, preparation for labour and delivery, breastfeeding, postpartum care and newborn care, particularly when information was timely and aligned with their gestational stage. The availability of a virtual educational library containing materials in multiple formats enabled pregnant women to revisit information as needed and supported understanding when content aligned with concerns they were encountering at specific points in pregnancy.20

Equity-related differences in how content was experienced were also evident. In the WeChat-based study, older women or those with lower levels of education reported greater difficulty interpreting digital instructions or navigating written materials, which sometimes limited their ability to fully benefit from the information provided.15 These women described relying more heavily on visual materials, voice messages or assistance from family members to understand content.

Peer support during social media based antenatal communication

Peer interaction emerged as a central experiential component of social media-based antenatal communication, with informational supports identified as the most frequently cited benefit by pregnant women. In WhatsApp-based interventions, women described group environments as spaces where they could learn from the experiences and responses of other pregnant women, which helped them interpret symptoms, anticipate challenges and feel reassured that their concerns were shared by others. This exchange of experiential knowledge was perceived as distinct from but complementary to information provided by health professionals.19 20

Beyond informational support, women reported additional psychosocial benefits associated with peer interaction. Participants described forming new social connections through group participation, which in some cases extended beyond pregnancy-related discussions. Women reported increased confidence in their roles as expectant or new mothers and described feeling encouraged to seek care after reading about or discussing similar experiences within the group.19 In the Godmother Project, pregnant women characterised the group environment as supportive and confidence-building, reinforcing professional guidance while also providing emotional reassurance through shared experiences.20

In contrast, pregnant women using Instagram reported more limited opportunities for peer interaction. Some participants perceived the platform as less conducive to sustained group exchange and community-building. Women in the Instagram-based intervention explicitly suggested strengthening the social dimension of the programme by offering more structured opportunities for interaction with other participants, such as additional group meetings, online group fitness classes, group walks or friendly competitions, indicating a desire for more active and relational forms of peer engagement.22

Health professionals and groups’ moderators

Across studies, platforms were primarily moderated by nurses or healthcare providers, though details varied.1419,21 From pregnant women’s perspectives, the involvement of health professionals and group moderators was central to building trust and sustaining engagement with social media-based antenatal communication. Women consistently described moderated WhatsApp and WeChat groups as safe and credible spaces where nurses or trained staff provided reliable information, answered questions and addressed misinformation. In the Godmother Project, participants reported satisfaction with the availability of nurse educators (‘Godmothers’) and agreed that their presence contributed to feelings of reassurance and confidence during pregnancy.20 Women valued the ability to receive personal attention when needed, particularly in moments of uncertainty, and several described the availability of a responsive moderator as especially important during first pregnancies.

At the same time, women’s experiences reflected nuanced expectations regarding the nature and timeliness of professional responses. While many participants appreciated personalised support, some expressed dissatisfaction when responses were perceived as insufficiently direct. For example, some women reported frustration when questions about specific issues, such as the safety of medications during pregnancy, were met with general advice to consult a doctor rather than clear guidance, which they felt reduced the usefulness of the interaction.20

Women’s experiences also varied depending on moderators’ capacity and digital skills. Not all nurses were reported to have the time or digital proficiency to tailor responses or content to individual needs. As a result, some pregnant women received comprehensive and supportive digital guidance, while others felt their concerns were addressed too briefly or appeared dismissed.15 19 20 Feedback on the role and activity of group administrators was therefore mixed. In the Kenyan WhatsApp study, some interviewees provided positive feedback on the administrator’s engagement and responsiveness, whereas others expressed dissatisfaction with the frequency and timeliness of responses, highlighting inconsistencies in moderator activity and availability.19

In addition to responsiveness, women commented on the tone and personal nature of moderation. In the Instagram-based intervention, some pregnant women perceived the content feed as somewhat impersonal and suggested that moderators could enhance engagement by sharing selective aspects of their own lives relevant to the intervention, such as photos of meals or physical activity routines. Women indicated that such personal sharing could humanise moderators and foster a stronger sense of connection, suggesting that relational presence was an important component of how professional support was experienced in social media-based ANC.14

Data privacy and cultural considerations

Pregnant women reported varying levels of concern regarding data privacy and confidentiality in relation to social media-based antenatal communication. Some women voiced mild apprehension about sharing personal health information, such as test results, through social media platforms, particularly when considering potential data security risks or phone sharing within households. For example, women described situations in which they personally felt comfortable sharing information but were influenced by partners’ concerns about hacking or data storage on personal devices, which shaped how and what information they chose to share digitally.15

Cultural considerations also influenced pregnant women’s experiences of social media communication for ANC. In the WeChat-based study, some women described needing to reassure family members, particularly older relatives, about the legitimacy and appropriateness of communicating with nurses through social media rather than in person. Women reported that family members sometimes questioned whether medical advice should be delivered digitally, prompting them to explain that social media communication was part of routine care and that nurses remained trustworthy sources of information.15

Despite these privacy-related and cultural concerns, many women indicated that trust in provider-led and institutionally affiliated social media groups mitigated apprehensions and supported continued participation. When communication channels were clearly linked to health facilities or known health professionals, women described feeling reassured about the credibility and safety of the information shared, which outweighed residual concerns about data privacy or non-traditional modes of interaction.15 20

Acceptability and willingness to continue participation

Overall, pregnant women’s experiences of social media communication for ANC were largely positive, with high acceptability and expressed willingness to sustain engagement over time.22 In WhatsApp-based interventions, women not only reported intentions to continue participating but also expressed a desire for the groups to be maintained and expanded to include other pregnant women who might benefit from similar support.19 Some women described actively recommending the programme to peers, reflecting perceived value beyond their own participation.

In the Godmother Project, pregnant women similarly reported strong acceptability and a willingness to continue engaging with the programme throughout pregnancy and into the postpartum period.20 Women’s positive experiences contributed to sustained demand, and the initiative was subsequently incorporated into routine antenatal services. As a result of its perceived success and continued interest from participants, the Godmother Project was scaled up and is now offered to all women interested in perinatal education and support, alongside existing in-person educational sessions, as part of a blended care approach.20

Discussion

This scoping review synthesised evidence on pregnant women’s perceptions and experiences of social media communication for ANC, in line with the review’s primary objective of mapping how such platforms are experienced, perceived, and valued by women during pregnancy. The available evidence, drawn predominantly from qualitative and mixed-methods studies, indicates that social media-based antenatal communication is generally acceptable and perceived as beneficial when it complements routine facility-based care.

Across the included studies, several consistent experiential themes emerged. Informational support was the most frequently reported and highly valued aspect of social media communication. Pregnant women appreciated receiving clear, relevant and trustworthy information related to pregnancy, particularly when content was moderated or delivered by healthcare professionals. This pattern is consistent with broader evidence showing that pregnancy is a period of intensified information seeking, and that women tend to place higher trust in professional sources than in unmoderated digital sources; where digital communication is clearly linked to trusted professionals, it can better meet women’s expectations for credible guidance during a time of high informational need.2 4

Peer support and social connectedness were prominent experiential benefits. Women described learning from other pregnant women’s experiences, normalising pregnancy-related concerns, and gaining reassurance through shared discussions. These peer-mediated interactions were associated with increased confidence as expectant mothers and, in some cases, encouragement to seek timely ANC. Such findings are consistent with recent evidence highlighting the role of digitally mediated peer support in promoting psychosocial well-being during pregnancy.6 23

At the same time, women’s participation in group-based social media spaces raised important considerations related to confidentiality and privacy. Although many women valued group interaction, participation in shared digital spaces entails potential risks, including inadvertent disclosure of sensitive personal or health information, screenshots or message forwarding outside the group, and uncertainty regarding who can access or retain shared content. Recent reviews of digital maternal health interventions note that privacy concerns are particularly salient during pregnancy, when women may share intimate health details and psychosocial concerns.24 25

Additional risks linked to group-based formats include the potential spread of misinformation between peers, unequal participation dynamics where some voices dominate discussions, and discomfort among women who prefer more private communication channels. Evidence from digital health equity research suggests that these risks may disproportionately affect women with lower digital literacy or those in shared-phone households, who may have limited control over confidentiality.3

To mitigate these risks while preserving the benefits of peer interaction, several protective strategies have been proposed in the recent literature. These include the use of clear group rules and confidentiality agreements,26 active moderation to discourage sharing of identifiable information, guidance on respectful communication,25 and reminders about not forwarding group content outside the platform. Structuring groups to remain relatively small and homogeneous by gestational stage may also enhance trust and reduce unintended disclosure.23 For patients and communities, these considerations underscore the importance of maintaining interactive and participatory elements within digital antenatal interventions while embedding safeguards that protect confidentiality and promote safe, inclusive engagement.24 25

Women’s experiences highlighted the importance of communication formats and delivery modalities. Short videos, infographics, images, voice notes and short text were preferred for their clarity and ease of use.4 6 Pregnant women valued the ability to access information asynchronously, revisit previous messages, and have antenatal content consolidated in one place.6 Preferences for posting frequency, timing, and format (eg, posts vs Stories) varied, but evening delivery and concise content were commonly perceived as more compatible with daily routines.2 These considerations have practical implications for healthcare providers and programme designers seeking to optimise engagement while avoiding information overload and unintended exclusion.3 4 When developing social media-based content for antenatal education, attention should be paid to content size and data requirements, particularly in resource-limited settings. Evidence from maternal digital health research indicates that large video files and high-bandwidth content can pose barriers for women with limited internet connectivity or constrained financial resources for mobile data, potentially reducing engagement and exacerbating inequities.3 24 Recent reviews of digital maternal health interventions in low- and middle-income countries emphasise that optimising content for low bandwidth, using compressed media, and prioritising text and image-based formats can improve accessibility and inclusiveness without compromising informational value.3 27

Trust and moderation emerged as central determinants of positive experiences. The presence of active and responsive moderators, particularly nurse educators, enhanced women’s trust in the information shared and contributed to feelings of reassurance.23 While most women expressed satisfaction with moderator availability, some reported dissatisfaction when responses were perceived as insufficiently personalised or overly generic.4 This variability highlights the need for adequate training, clear role definitions and workload considerations for health professionals involved in digital communication, a concern also noted in recent qualitative research on digital maternal health services.4 Importantly, the findings also suggest a risk of inequity when multiple health professionals’ moderate parallel groups without standardised content and response protocols. In the WeChat-based programme, not all nurses had the time or digital skills to customise content; consequently, some pregnant women received comprehensive digital guidance, while others felt their concerns were dismissed or addressed too briefly.15 Standardising core educational content, triage rules for clinical questions and minimum response expectations may help reduce variability in women’s experiences across groups.

At the same time, women’s accounts indicate that professional moderation alone is insufficient if response delays or limited capacity of moderators constrain individualised follow-up. Delayed replies and brief responses were experienced as frustrating in some settings, especially when women sought time-sensitive reassurance. During scale-up phases, two complementary strategies may help mitigate workload while preserving women’s experience of responsiveness: (1) maintaining smaller groups (e.g., fewer than 10 women per moderated group) to support timely interaction and personalised follow-up and (2) integrating chatbot-supported frequently asked questions to provide immediate, standardised responses for non-urgent informational needs, while reserving health professional time for complex or high-risk concerns.27,29

Finally, equity-related considerations were evident across studies. Older or less educated women reported greater difficulty interpreting written digital content, while privacy concerns and sociocultural expectations influenced how some women engaged with digital platforms.15 24 These findings align with recent scoping reviews documenting digital divides in maternal health.3 24 Recent interventions and reviews have documented practical strategies to mitigate inequities in social media-based maternal health communication. These include offering multimodal content (combining text, images, audio and short videos) to accommodate varying literacy levels, simplifying language and avoiding medical jargon, and providing optional voice notes or audio explanations for women with lower reading proficiency.27 In addition, women living in remote or underserved settings, those facing language barriers, and those from diverse cultural backgrounds may experience additional challenges engaging with social media-based antenatal communication. Future interventions should therefore incorporate culturally tailored and linguistically appropriate content, including context-specific dietary recommendations and low-bandwidth multimodal communication strategies, to ensure more inclusive and equitable access to antenatal information and support.4 To improve access to more effective communication programmes, future research should also explore whether communication needs and experiences differ according to obstetric characteristics, including parity, pregnancy risk status, and pre-existing maternal conditions.3

Collectively, the findings are relevant to multiple knowledge users. For policymakers, the evidence supports integrating social media communication into ANC strategies as a complementary approach, particularly in settings with constrained health system capacity. For healthcare providers, the review highlights the need to balance responsiveness and personalisation with workload and sustainability. Findings suggest that effective moderation may require both clinical knowledge and digital communication skills. For pregnant women and communities, the findings affirm the potential of social media platforms to enhance access to information, reassurance and peer support when implemented in a trustworthy and inclusive manner.

Strengths and limitations

This scoping review has several methodological strengths. It followed PRISMA-ScR guidance with a prospectively registered protocol, applied a comprehensive and systematic search strategy across multiple databases and grey literature sources, and used independent dual screening and data charting with third-reviewer arbitration. The explicit focus on pregnant women’s perceptions and experiences enabled detailed mapping of experiential and equity-related dimensions that are often underrepresented in effectiveness-focused reviews.

Nevertheless, some limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. The available evidence base remains relatively limited in size and scope, with a small number of studies meeting the inclusion criteria and a concentration on specific platforms and settings, which may limit transferability to other contexts or populations. Evidence from low-income and francophone African settings remains sparse despite widespread use of social media platforms in these regions. In addition, although grey literature searching and citation tracking were undertaken, some unpublished programme evaluations or locally implemented initiatives may not have been captured. Finally, consistent with PRISMA-ScR guidance, no formal critical appraisal of included studies was conducted; while appropriate for the exploratory aims of a scoping review, this limits assessment of methodological quality and confidence in individual findings and highlights the need for future systematic reviews.

Conclusions

This scoping review mapped the available evidence on pregnant women’s perceptions and experiences of social media communication for ANC. Overall, women perceived professionally moderated social media platforms as acceptable and valuable complements to routine ANC, particularly for accessing trustworthy information, reassurance between visits and peer interaction during a period of heightened informational and emotional needs.

The findings align with broader literature showing that pregnancy is a time of intensified information seeking and that women tend to place greater trust in professionally affiliated digital sources than in unmoderated online content. By focusing specifically on social media, this review highlights how features such as group-based interaction, message retrievability and asynchronous communication shape women’s engagement and perceived usefulness.

At the same time, the review underscores challenges in scaling social media-based antenatal communication. Variability in moderators’ capacity, digital skills and response timeliness, alongside equity-related factors such as digital literacy, privacy concerns and sociocultural influences, may lead to uneven experiences if not addressed. Future research should prioritise more focused qualitative syntheses and primary studies in underrepresented settings, particularly low-income countries, and explore implementation strategies that balance responsiveness, inclusivity and sustainability, including standardised content, low-bandwidth design and complementary digital tools.
