Experimental evaluation of CO2 mineral trapping in Albian glauconite-bearing sandstone, Mangyshlak Basin, Kazakhstan
Authors: Mahmoud Leila, Nazira Kuzhamratova, Branimir Šegvić, Alina Shchepetkina, Natalya Khan, Veronika Slipko, Milovan Fustić
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glauconite-bearing sandstone reservoirs represent promising targets for potential subsurface CO2 storage via mineral trapping. Nevertheless, previous studies commonly indicate that substantial CO2 sequestration through glauconite carbonation occurs on a geological timescale. In this study, we present results of a controlled experimental assessment of CO2 mineral trapping in Albian glauconite-bearing sandstones from the Mangyshlak Basin (southwestern Kazakhstan). The studied unit, composed of very fine to fine-grained sandstones, is commonly 30–40 m thick and extends hundreds of kilometers laterally. A 30-day CO2 injection batch experiment was performed at 100°C and 150 bar using a brine/rock ratio of 20. The aqueous phase exhibited a sustained increase in Fe, Na, K, Mg, and Si ions, followed by a modest late-stage decline in Mg, Fe, and Si ions, implying mineral dissolution followed by ion consumption through formation of secondary minerals. These results are supported by petrographic comparison of pre- and post-experiment mineral assemblages, which confirm the dissolution of glauconite clasts, feldspars, albite, and K-feldspar overgrowths, with minor precipitation of ankerite. Although the experiments clearly demonstrate significant dissolution of glauconite, the precise mechanisms governing the partitioning and incorporation of Fe and Mg into newly formed carbonate and/or secondary phyllosilicate phases require further mineralogical and geochemical investigations. The current results demonstrate that nascent laboratory scale glauconite carbonation can be feasible under high temperature–pressure conditions and significant concentration of Fe2+.

Introduction

Glauconite is a Fe- and K-rich, illite-like marine phyllosilicate characterized by interstratified smectitic and micaceous layers1–4. It is commonly described as a dioctahedral 2:1 ferruginous green clay mineral that forms during early marine diagenesis through progressive glauconitization, involving the transformation of Fe3+-rich smectitic precursors toward more mature glauconitic compositions5–7. Glauconite is widely distributed on continental shelves and has been documented across a broad range of geological periods, with the oldest confirmed occurrences dating back to the Late Paleoproterozoic8. Approximately 73% of recorded glauconite occurrences are associated with Cretaceous–Quaternary strata that developed during major episodes of eustatic sea-level rise9,10. The glauconite genesis is closely linked to transgressive systems, where low sedimentation rates promote the slow, in-situ authigenic transformation of precursor materials (e.g., fecal pellets, iron-rich smectite) into glauconite9,11,12.

Glauconitic minerals typically form during syn-depositional to very early diagenetic processes near the sediment–water interface, under conditions of low sedimentation rate, prolonged grain residence time, and suboxic pore-water chemistry (e.g3,9,13). However, following the mica nomenclature framework and recent discussions on the glauconite–Fe-illite–Fe-smectite problem, “glauconite” should not be treated as a single, compositionally uniform phase, but rather as part of a continuum of Fe- and K-rich dioctahedral phyllosilicates, including glauconite-smectite, Fe-illite–smectite, and related mixed-layer phases6,14–17. During glauconitization, precursor Fe-rich/K-poor expandable phases progressively evolve toward more K-enriched, mica-like compositions, with increasing structural ordering and decreasing expandability7,13,18. This maturation trend is commonly reflected by increasing K2O contents, from nascent glauconitic phases with K2O < 4 wt% to highly evolved glauconite with K2O contents approaching or exceeding ~ 8 wt%5,7,13,15,17–20. In this study, the term “glauconitic phases” is therefore used cautiously to refer to the Fe- and K-bearing green phyllosilicates identified petrographically and supported by XRD and SEM–EDS observations. These phases are important because their octahedral and interlayer sites may host Fe(III), Fe(II), Mg, Al, and K, making them potential sources of reactive cations during CO2–brine–rock interaction. Dissolution of glauconitic phases under CO2-rich conditions may release Fe2+ and Mg2+ that can contribute to the precipitation of Fe–Mg carbonate minerals, including siderite, ankerite, and ferroan dolomite21,22. Despite the recognized potential of glauconitic phases for CO2 mineral trapping, experimental constraints on CO2–brine–glauconitic sandstone reactions remain limited, and previous studies have mainly documented glauconite dissolution without clear secondary carbonate precipitation. As a result, full understanding for the geochemical pathways, reaction kinetics, and mineralogical evolution of glauconite-bearing sandstones for carbon storage is still limited. The few available laboratory studies consistently demonstrate measurable glauconite dissolution under CO2-rich conditions,however, none have reported the formation of secondary carbonate minerals23–25.

Geochemical simulations by Zhang et al26. provide additional context, suggesting that complete dissolution of glauconite and subsequent carbonate precipitation may require several thousand years (~ 10,000 years). Importantly, these models highlight that the extent and rate of carbonation are strongly influenced by several factors, including the initial chemical composition and maturity of glauconite, as well as reaction temperatures. Less evolved glauconite is predicted to release more divalent cations (e.g., Fe(II)) and, therefore, to be more susceptible to carbonation. Temperature exerts a dominant control on the kinetics of glauconite-CO2-water reactions. At elevated temperatures (~ 80°C), representative of deeper sedimentary storage layers, geochemical kinetic models predict that carbonate precipitation may proceed on timescales shorter than two months under favorable water–rock ratios and CO2 fugacity. Despite these predictions, robust experimental validation remains lacking, and laboratory-scale demonstrations of efficient carbonation in glauconite-bearing sandstones under controlled conditions have yet to be reported.

The present study investigates Albian (Lower Cretaceous) glauconite-bearing sandstones of the Mangyshlak Basin, southwestern Kazakhstan (Fig. 1). These sandstones are developed basin-wide27, covering at least several thousand square kilometers. A characteristic feature of the Albian sandstones is objet-trouvé, i.e., meter-scale, carbonate-cemented concretions, together with siderite-filled trace fossils such as Ophiomorpha, Thalassinoides, and Skolithos (Fig. 2). These features provide important evidence for localized carbonate precipitation within the sedimentary succession. The preferential concentration of siderite within burrow networks suggests that biogenic sedimentary structures acted as permeable micro-reactors, promoting focused fluid flow, enhanced water–rock interaction, and localized Fe–carbonate precipitation. Geologic setting

The Mangyshlak Basin is bounded by the Mangyshlak Mountains to the north and northeast, the Karabogaz Arch to the south, and the Central Caspian Arch to the west (Fig. 1). The basin is intersected by a set of major steep northwest-striking faults that form a network of depressions (troughs), which progressively deepen to the west and are separated by a series of step-faults. The origin of faults is poorly defined. Most likely, the faults form part of a large strike-slip zone on the Turan domain, which extends from the Central Ustyurt Fault to Kopet Dag in Iran (Fig. 1)28,29. The tectonic history of the basin includes several distinct stages. During the Late Permian and Triassic, the back-arc rift zone created accommodation space for the accumulation of deep-marine clastics, mixed carbonate clastics, shallow-water carbonates, and shoreface deposits (Fig. 3A). The Late Triassic to Early Jurassic collision of Cimmerian terranes and the southern Eurasian margin caused the closure of Paleo-Tethys Ocean, resulting in the structural inversion and uplift of the Mangyshlak area (Fig. 3). Sediments sourced from the uplifted terrains formed vast fluvio-deltaic deposit above the basin-scale Triassic-Jurassic unconformity (Fig. 3A)28,30–32. Accommodation space for Cretaceous and Cenozoic shelf and coastal sediments was primarily controlled by tectonic subsidence, with additional influence from eustatic sea-level changes. As a result of the collision of India and southern Eurasia, uplift was reactivated in the late Cenozoic and has continued to the present day33–35. The present-day Mangyshlak Mountains were most likely formed by transpression along the major Central Ustyurt strike-slip fault (Fig. 1)29, with the central part comprised of steeply dipping, erosion-resistant, metamorphosed Permo-Triassic strata of the central Mangyshlak uplift (Fig. 1).Fig. 3(A) Stratigraphic chart of the Mangyshlak Basin, including dominant lithologies and basin evolution phases (modified after28). The basin fill comprises a relatively continuous succession of Permian to Cenozoic strata (Fig. 3). While the petroleum-bearing Jurassic (e.g., the Uzen field) and Lower to Middle Triassic marine clastic and carbonate units are relatively well documented in the literature28,31,32,37,38, other strata, including Cretaceous, have attracted limited attention so far. Unlike the underlying Jurassic and Triassic deposits, Cretaceous sediments are not divided into formations. Thus, literature commonly refers to these intervals using international stage and sub-stage subdivisions (Fig. 3B). The most detailed are the biostratigraphic studies27,36, while the sedimentological studies are limited to oil-hosting Aptian and Albian strata of the Dunga oil field (Fig. 137–39). The Cretaceous strata are mostly comprised of marine clastics with carbonate interbeds (Berrisian to Valanginian), terrestrial sediments (Hauterivian), marine clastics (Aptian to Turonian), and carbonates and chalk deposits (Turonian -Maastrichtian, Fig. 3B). The processes forming erosional unconformities found at the base of the Berrisian and Valanginian units and, thus, stratigraphic gaps (Fig. 3B) are poorly understood36.

The Albian strata are exposed at many localities across the Mangyshlak Mountains (Fig. 1) and are separated by an erosional surface from the Aptian strata (Fig. 3B). The erosion was followed by a rapid deepening caused by a major transgression. Thus, the overlying sediments are comprised of fine-grained sediments accumulated during the slow shoaling of the shelf basin. The top of the Albian is marked by an erosional unconformity defined by detrital phosphate nodules, phosphatized burrows, and molluscs (Fig. 3B). The Albian succession is proposed to be complete in terms of ammonite stratigraphy40. However, it contains numerous small biostratigraphic gaps which are usually marked by intraformational, up to 15 cm thick detrital “phosphates” (Fig. 3B). These thin “phosphate” beds are commonly comprised of broken, up to 10 cm long, phosphatized burrow clasts. However, some beds also contain variable amounts of cm-scale broken and/or fractured phosphate nodules and phosphatized molluscs, as well as pieces of wood.

The correlation of detailed biostratigraphy and lithofacies between numerous Albian outcrops, including Shakh-Bogota, Sulu-Kapy, Shyrkala-Airakty, Kush, Aksyrtau-Koksyrtau, and Besakty outcrop sections (Fig. 1) allowed Marcinowsky et al27. to create a regional profile and document strong basin-scale litho-stratigraphic correlation. Although not mapped in detail in the subsurface, direct outcrop-subsurface correlation of the Albian strata with the Dunga Oil Field38, regional cross-sections28,31, and identification in drilled wells (Fig. 1) suggests their significant, basin-scale extent (Fig. 1).

Outcrop characterization, sampling, and glauconite content estimates

The Albian exposure at the Koksyrtau outcrop (Figs. 1–3) was described and logged in detail in the field using a conventional facies analysis approach, while the elevation-grain size cross-plot was constructed using the Wentworth grain size scale (Fig. 3)27. A digital 3-D model (Fig. 1B) was created using terrestrial photogrammetry from tens of photos acquired by a UAV, used to verify the elevation of shifted log intervals (Figs. 1B and 3). A total of 12 samples were collected along the logged section for further in-lab estimates of glauconite content and verification of grain-size estimates under the stereomicroscope.

Considering the limited variation in the glauconite content (Fig. 6), Sample #12 collected from the middle of the logged section and containing ~ 7 vol% glauconite, was selected as representative for batch-reactor experiments to evaluate its potential for CO2 mineral trapping via changes in aqueous geochemistry and new mineral formation, if any. The sandstone sample is poorly consolidated and essentially uncemented in nature. Mechanical sieving was therefore conducted to obtain a well-defined grain-size fraction suitable for the experimental investigations. The sample was sieved, and the fine-grained sandstone fraction with particle sizes < 0.25 mm was carefully separated and retained for subsequent experimental analyses. Before the batch experiment, the sample underwent comprehensive petrographic and mineralogical characterization to constrain its modal composition and to identify the dominant and accessory mineral phases. Petrography and mineralogy

Because of the unconsolidated nature of the collected sand samples, loose grains were poured into the epoxy, and following solidification, were cut and prepared for investigation under the petrographic Zeiss microscope. While this approach implies that thin sections are not representative for interpreting potential structures or porosity, they are suitable for interpreting the mineral composition. Petrographic examination was conducted on five polished thin sections prepared from the selected glauconitic sandstone (Sample #12). The sections were characterized using plane-polarized light (PPL) optical microscopy to document textural relationships, grain mineralogy, and diagenetic features. This optical investigation was complemented by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to resolve fine-scale mineralogical and microstructural characteristics. SEM analyses were performed at the Core Facilities Department of Nazarbayev University, Kazakhstan, using a JSM-IT200 scanning electron microscope equipped with Everhart–Thornley (ET) type secondary electron detector and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) system for qualitative to semi-quantitative elemental analysis. Prior to analysis, the EDS system was calibrated using standard factory and elemental reference calibration procedures to ensure accurate peak position and detector response. Spectral acquisition and processing were performed using the integrated JEOL/EDS software package, including automatic background subtraction, peak deconvolution, and ZAF-based matrix correction procedures for semi-quantitative elemental determination. Both point analyses and selected area scans were conducted on representative mineral grains and reaction surfaces. Before SEM examination, thin sections were carbon-coated to ensure adequate electrical conductivity. Imaging and microanalytical measurements were acquired at an accelerating voltage ranging between 20–30 kV, achieving a spatial resolution of approximately 3.0 nm.

Whole-rock mineralogical composition of the selected sample was performed on the fine-grained (< 0.25 mm) fraction using X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. The XRD measurements were performed on the sandstone sample before and after experimentation (Fig. 5) at the Core Facilities Department of Nazarbayev University, Kazakhstan, enabling direct assessment of mineralogical modifications induced by CO2-rock interactions. XRD analyses were carried out on finely powdered rock material using a SmartLab diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (Rigaku). The analyses were conducted using an operating tube voltage of 40 kV and a tube current of 30 mA. Samples were gently disaggregated, oven-dried at low temperature, and pulverized to a fine homogeneous powder using an agate mortar to minimize contamination and preferred orientation effects. The powdered material was back-loaded into a standard sample holder to obtain a randomly oriented powder mount. XRD patterns were collected over a 2θ range of 02–100° with a step size of 0.01–0.02° 2θ and a scan rate of 1–5° min⁻1. Mineral phase identification was performed using the PDF-4 + reference database in combination with Bruker DIFFRAC.EVA software. Semi-quantitative mineral abundances were estimated using the Reference Intensity Ratio (RIR) method, following established procedures41,42. In this approach, the relative abundance of each identified crystalline phase was calculated from the integrated intensities of selected diagnostic reflections, corrected using the corresponding RIR values available in the PDF-4 + database. The RIR results are therefore considered semi-quantitative because they depend on peak overlap, crystallinity, grain-size effects, preferred orientation, background subtraction, and the availability and accuracy of reference intensity values. Accordingly, the XRD-derived mineral percentages were used primarily to compare relative mineralogical changes before and after the experiment rather than as absolute mineral abundances.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was carried out at the Core Facilities Department of Nazarbayev University, Kazakhstan, using a Nexsa spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) equipped with a monochromated low-power Al Kα radiation source (hν = 1486.6 eV). The analysis was performed in finely ground powder without additional mineral separation or enrichment. Therefore, the obtained spectra represent the average chemical composition and oxidation states within the outermost near-surface layers of the analyzed polymineral particles. Photoelectrons were detected using a 180° double-focusing hemispherical analyzer fitted with a 128-channel detector. Because the investigated sample is electrically non-conductive, charge neutralization was employed using a flood gun during the measurements to compensate for surface charging effects.

Batch reactor experiment

A batch CO2-water-glauconite sandstone interaction experiment was conducted using 8 g of powdered whole-rock material suspended in 160 mL of deionized water. The water–rock suspension was transferred into a sealed PARR batch reactor vessel, and CO2-water–rock experiments were performed at a CO2 partial pressure (pCO2) of 150 bar and a constant temperature of 100°C for 30 days (Fig. 6). The experiment was temporarily halted every 5 days to extract 5 mL aliquots of the aqueous phase for chemical analysis. The collected liquid was then analyzed for major cation concentrations using Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES) with an iCAP 6300 system (Thermo Scientific) at the Core Facilities Department of Nazarbayev University, Kazakhstan. All measured major cations exhibited an analytical uncertainty of ± 0.1%.

Results and interpretations

Sedimentology, lithology, and glauconite content of the Koksyrtau Albian Section

The Albian section at Koksyrtau is comprised of very fine to fine-grained sandstone, occasionally interbedded with thin siltstone and one coquina layer (Fig. 6). Although most of the exposure appears structureless probably due to well-sorted sandstones, rare observations of the hummocky, cross-bedded, and wave-rippled structures coupled with the presence of the bivalves, fish skeletons, and molluscs confirm a shallow-marine depositional setting. The section also contains abundant meter-scale spherical concretions (Figs. 1–3). The centers of the concretions contain either “encased” wood fragments (Fig. 2) or detrital phosphates probably transported from land following major storms. The sample used for the batch reactor experiment was collected from planar-laminated, medium-grained sandstone and is considered representative of the overall Albian section lithology, particularly in terms of glauconite content (5%–7%; Fig. 6).

Petrographic characterization of the studied glauconitic sandstone

The studied glauconitic sandstone is composed predominantly of sub-angular to rounded, moderately sorted detrital grains (Fig. 7). Framework constituents predominantly include monocrystalline quartz, subordinate feldspar, minor lithic fragments, and relatively common glauconite grains (Fig. 7A–F). Feldspars occur mainly as microcline and orthoclase, which are largely fresh and only weakly altered, whereas plagioclase is rare (Fig. 7B, D). Mineralogical composition and chemical composition of glauconite

XRD and petrography

The XRD analysis of the studied glauconitic sandstone reveals a mineral assemblage dominated by quartz (68%), with subordinate albite (5%), and mica, glauconite (~ 25%) (Fig. 8). Quartz is the most abundant phase, as evidenced by a series of intense and sharp reflections at interplane values at 4.27 Å, 3.35 Å, 1.82 Å, 1.54 Å, and 1.38–1.34 Å. The sharpness and high intensity of reflections indicate a highly crystalline quartz and minimal structural disorder, which is consistent with the petrographic observation of abundant monocrystalline quartz grains (Fig. 7). Albite is identified by distinct but comparatively lower-intensity reflexes at 3.76 Å and 3.20 Å. The presence of albite corroborates petrographic observations of feldspars. The data further indicate that glauconite in this sample occurs predominantly as an interstratified glauconite-smectite (G–S), as evidenced by the position of the first basal reflection at 12.12 Å (Fig. 8), which is intermediate between the ~ 10 Å spacing of non-expandable glauconitic mica layers and the ~ 14–15 Å spacing typical of smectite43. The observed 001 spacing implies a partially expandable structure corresponding to an estimated ~ 40%–50% smectitic component, assuming fully disordered (R0) interstratification44,45. Further confirmation of G–S interlayering comes from the presence of a dioctahedral 060 reflection near ~ 1.50 Å (Fig. 8), which is diagnostic of Fe-rich dioctahedral phyllosilicates46,47. The identification of mixed-layer glauconite–smectite in the sample fits well the established models of glauconite maturation, involving progressive reduction of expandable layers during burial and diagenesis15,18.Fig. 8X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) patterns show that quartz, albite, and glauconite represent the main mineral phases in the investigated glauconitic sandstone before the experiment.

The SEM–EDS results highlight pronounced textural and chemical heterogeneity within the studied glauconitic sandstone (Fig. 9). Glauconite occurs in the form of rounded to lobate grains as well as compacted pellets and commonly exhibits a relatively homogeneous lamellar to fibrous microfabric (Fig. 9A–D). The pellets are internally massive and do not contain obvious biogenic nuclei or lithic inclusions. In particular, no diagnostic relics of foraminiferal tests or other skeletal precursors have been observed. Chemically, glauconite is dominated by Si and Al with variable contributions from Fe, K, Mg, Na, and Ca, defining a compositional spectrum that bridges Fe-rich smectitic-glauconitic compositions11. The measured Fe contents span ~ 2–10 wt%, whereas K typically ranges between ~ 2 and 5 wt%. Minor alkalis and alkaline earths are also detected, with Na, Mg, and Ca (Fig. 9B, D).

XPS results and Fe speciation

The XPS survey spectra of the studied glauconitic sandstone is presented in Fig. 10A. The sample exhibits pronounced peaks corresponding to O 1 s (~ 530 eV), Fe 2p (~ 710–725 eV), Si 2p (~ 102–103 eV), Si 2 s (~ 150 eV), Mg 2p (~ 49–50 eV), and Mg 2 s (~ 88–90 eV). The presence of magnesium is additionally confirmed be the Mg KLL Auger transition located near ~ 305 eV and a weak Mg 1 s signal at ~ 1302–1303 eV. The intense O 1 s peak corresponds to lattice oxygen in the silicate framework. Fe 2p signal indicates the presence of iron and silicon-related peaks (Si 2p and Si 2 s) confirm the silicate framework of the mineral48,49. A distinct Al 2p peak (~ 74–76 eV) is observed, suggesting the presence of aluminum-containing aluminosilicate phases or partial substitution of Al in the tetrahedral sites of the glauconite structure. Specifically, the separation between Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2 components was fixed at approximately 13.1 eV, consistent with reported spin–orbit splitting values for iron51,52.

The Fe 2p region (Fig. 10C) shows the expected doublet structure, with the 2p3/2 feature near 712 eV and the 2p1/2 feature around 725 eV53,54. Deconvolution revealed Fe2+ species at 710.1 eV in the 2p3/2 region, accompanied by corresponding 2p1/2 contributions at 712.1 eV with associated higher-energy components within the 723.7–724.0 eV range. In addition, satellite feature typical of Fe3+ oxides are observed approximately 6–7 eV above the main Fe peaks53,55,56.

Experimental results

Chemistry of the aqueous solution

The time-resolved aqueous chemistry of the closed-system CO2-water-glauconitic sandstone experiment reveals a pronounced, non-linear evolution of dissolved cations over the 30-day reaction period (Fig. 11). During the early stage of the experiment (Days 1–10), concentrations of all monitored cations (i.e., Na, Mg, Si, Fe, and K) increased rapidly by up to several orders of magnitude. The particularly strong early increase in Na suggests efficient mobilization from exchangeable sites and easily leachable surfaces, whereas the concurrent rise in Mg, Si, and Fe indicates early alteration of silicate and Fe-bearing phases, including glauconite.Fig. 11Measured concentration of the major cations (i.e., Mg, F, Si, K, and Na) in the aqueous solution during the conducted experiment.

Measured concentration of the major cations (i.e., Mg, F, Si, K, and Na) in the aqueous solution during the conducted experiment.

Following this initial high-reactivity phase, the system evolved toward a quasi-steady-state regime after approximately 10 days of reaction. From Day 10 onward, Na concentration abruptly decreased, indicating its incorporation into secondary solid phases (Fig. 11). Mg showed a similar pattern, with element concentrations increasing modestly, stabilizing, and eventually decreasing, consistent with a balance between continued mineral dissolution and reduced reaction rates imposed by evolving solution chemistry and surface passivation. In contrast, K concentration remained low throughout the experiment despite a modest increase after Day 10, suggesting that released K+ is efficiently re-incorporated into residual glauconite structures or newly forming K-bearing aluminosilicate phases, rather than accumulating in a solution.

Dissolved Si displays a distinct temporal trend characterized by a rapid increase during the early reaction stage, followed by a slight decrease, and stabilization at later times. The initial rise in Si reflects active silicate dissolution under CO2-acidified conditions, whereas the subsequent decline indicates the onset of secondary controls on dissolved silica concentrations. Fe exhibits the most pronounced non-conservative behavior in the system. Dissolved Fe concentrations increased during Days 10–20, consistent with the mobilization of Fe from glauconite and other Fe-bearing phases during early alteration. However, by Day 30, Fe concentrations decreased sharply, indicating the development of an efficient sink for dissolved Fe at later stages of the experiment. In a closed CO2 system, this decline most plausibly reflects removal of Fe from solution through the precipitation of secondary Fe-bearing phases, such as Fe-carbonates or incorporation into newly forming clay minerals. Although oxidation during sampling cannot be entirely excluded, the decoupling of Fe behavior from that of Na and Mg strongly suggests that secondary mineral formation controls Fe mobility during the later reaction stages.

Post-experiment solid fraction (glauconite) characterization

The analysis of post-experiment solid fraction/residue shows that glauconite clasts in post-experiment exhibit modest depletion in the more readily exchangeable/soluble cations (i.e., Na, K, Mg, and Ca; Fig. 12A–B). In contrast, Al concentrations increased systematically across all analyzed clasts, whereas Si showed a marked decrease. Fe displayed a non-uniform trend, increasing in some clasts but decreasing in others, implying that Fe mobility is controlled by micro-heterogeneity at the grain scale. Discussion

Glauconite reactivity

The variations in aqueous chemistry observed during the experiment (Fig. 11), together with the marked compositional heterogeneity recorded within the reacted glauconite clasts (Fig. 12), demonstrate the high reactivity of glauconitic sandstone under aqueous CO2 conditions. In such systems, the dissolution of CO2 leads to the rapid formation of carbonic acid, resulting in acidic conditions that promote proton-driven dissolution of reactive mineral phases57,58 and facilitate the desorption and exchange of weakly bound interlayer and surface cations from glauconite and associated clay minerals59,60. Previous studies26,61 have demonstrated that glauconite dissolution is strongly pH-dependent, with acidic conditions enhancing mineral dissolution and the mobilization of ions from the glauconite structure. However, in contrast to the pronounced pH-swing behavior documented during mineral trapping experiments involving mafic and ultramafic lithologies57,58,62,63, such pH swing has not been reported to date in glauconite–aqueous CO2 interaction experiments. This is mostly related to the incomplete dissolution of glauconite under experimental conditions, which limits alkalinity production, prevents a subsequent increase in solution pH, and ultimately suppresses or strongly constrains carbonate mineral precipitation.

In contrast, the divergent trends in Fe concentrations within individual glauconite clasts (Fig. 12) indicate heterogeneous reaction pathways operating at the grain scale. This variability may be attributed to several, potentially coupled, processes: (1) spatially variable redox conditions and differences in Fe speciation (Fe(II)/Fe(III)) within and between glauconite clasts (e.g.,23,25), (2) localized precipitation of secondary Fe-bearing phases, such as Fe oxides or Fe-rich clay minerals on selected grain surfaces (Fig. 13A, B), resulting in apparent Fe enrichment despite net dissolution elsewhere64,65, and/or 3) intrinsic compositional and textural heterogeneity in the precursor glauconite which can strongly influence reaction kinetics and dominant dissolution–precipitation pathways25. These observations highlight the complexity of Fe behavior during glauconite alteration under CO2-rich conditions. Systematic characterization of Fe speciation during dissolution, coupled with reactive-transport experiments explicitly accounting for the role of glauconite microporosity and internal heterogeneity, is required to further understand the dominant controls on glauconite reactivity and Fe release in CO2-brine-glauconite systems.Fig. 13Thin section (A–B) and SEM (C–D) photomicrographs demonstrating the post-experiment alterations, dissolution, and growth of different mineral phases within the studied glauconitic sandstone. Quantification of Fe2+/Fe3+ and impact on glauconite carbonation

Iron speciation exerts a fundamental control on the reactivity of glauconite and the extent of its carbonation during interaction with CO2-bearing aqueous fluids. This control arises because ferrous iron (Fe2+) serves as the primary cationic precursor for Fe-carbonate precipitation, particularly siderite and Fe-bearing carbonate phases such as ankerite23,25,66,67. Consequently, the efficiency of glauconite-mediated mineral carbonation is strongly influenced by the relative proportions of structural Fe2+ and Fe3+ within the glauconite lattice. Carbonation of glauconite proceeds through a redox-coupled dissolution–reprecipitation mechanism in which partial destabilization of the glauconite structure releases iron from octahedral sites during reaction with CO2-rich fluids. The liberated iron is subsequently redistributed into secondary mineral phases depending on prevailing redox conditions and fluid chemistry. Under sufficiently reducing conditions, the dominant reaction pathway promotes precipitation of Fe-carbonates, primarily siderite and subordinate ankerite, accompanied by recrystallization of the residual clay toward a Fe-depleted glauconitic phase. In contrast, systems dominated by ferric iron (Fe3+) tend to suppress efficient carbonate mineralization because Fe3+ is poorly incorporated into carbonate structures and instead preferentially partitions into Fe(III) oxyhydroxides or oxide phases. Therefore, the availability of Fe2+ within the glauconite structure and its release during dissolution represents a key geochemical control on both the kinetics of reaction and the overall capacity for CO2 mineral trapping in glauconite-bearing systems.

Mineral dissolution and precipitation of new phases

Development of dissolution grooves on albite and K-feldspar overgrowths provide a microtextural evidence for post-experimental feldspar dissolution (e.g.,25, Fig. 11C, D). These textures are consistent with the time-progressive enrichment of Na+ and K+ in the aqueous phase. In CO2-charged systems, feldspar dissolution is typically initiated by proton-promoted hydrolysis, whereby acidic fluids attack Al–O–Si linkages and liberate alkali cations alongside dissolved silica species68–70. Accordingly, the observed Na+ and K+ release is compatible with acid-driven dissolution pathways widely documented for detrital and authigenic feldspars during diagenesis68,69,71,72. These reactions often evolve toward secondary clay precipitation (i.e., kaolinite, illite)68,73,74. However, the present experiment does not show petrographic or mineralogical evidence for clay neoformation, despite the feldspar dissolution signatures. Instead, feldspar overgrowths are observed (Fig. 13C, D), and bulk mineralogical data indicate increased amounts of albite and K-feldspar after the experiment (Fig. 14,Table 2). The dissolution–precipitation processes can be reconciled by considering that feldspar alteration in closed or semi-closed systems commonly proceeds via coupled dissolution-reprecipitation and localized re-equilibration rather than net mineral loss. Compared with published kinetic estimates from geochemical modeling, the occurrence of measurable carbonation on laboratory timescales highlights the strong dependence of glauconite carbonation on temperature, water/rock ratios, and Fe2+/Fe3+content. Using geochemical calculations, Zhang et al26. predicted that complete carbonation of glauconite reacting with aqueous CO2 at a 10:1 water/rock ratio would require ~ 17.5, 11.9, 7.1, and 3.8 kyr at 35°C, 45°C, 60°C, and 80°C, respectively. In contrast, the current expriment demonstrates that detectable carbonate mineralization can occur within days to weeks when temperatures approach ~ 100°C and when the effective water/rock ratio is high. This disparity is consistent with the strong temperature sensitivity of kinetic rate constants (i.e., Arrhenius-type behavior) and the capacity of higher water/rock ratios to maintain far-from-equilibrium conditions through continued fluid–mineral interaction.

Beyond temperature and water/rock ratio, these results further suggest that lithological and compositional attributes exert first-order controls on the extent of mineral trapping in glauconitic sandstones. Pyrite and other Accessory sulfides may influence carbonation indirectly by modulating redox state and acidity: sulfide oxidation (where applicable) can generate acidity and sulfate, potentially suppressing carbonate saturation and/or diverting Ca2+ into competing sulfate phases67. Accordingly, the absence of pyrite and other sulfides in the studied sandstone may have been promoted the carbonation and precipitation of ankerite. In addition, the initial composition and maturity of glauconite is possibly critical. Less evolved glauconite tends to host higher proportions of exchangeable and structurally bound reactive cations (e.g., Fe, Mg, Ca) and, therefore, offers a larger cation supply for carbonate growth, enhancing CO2 trapping potential22,26,67. Conversely, more evolved glauconite or more silica-rich/passivated grain surfaces may limit cation liberation and restrict carbonate yields, even at elevated temperatures.

Overall, the combined XRD and textural evidence demonstrates that glauconitic sandstones can mineralize some CO2 on experimentally short timescales (≤ 1 month), albeit at low extent under the specific conditions tested. The limited ankerite abundance (~ 1 wt%) indicates that carbonation is not simply controlled by reaction time but is probably constrained by one or more rate-limiting factors, including 1) insufficient sustained supply of divalent cations probably due to partial surface passivation or near-equilibrium dissolution, 2) redox-dependent availability of Fe2+ required for ankerite stoichiometry, and/or 3) competition with alternative sinks for Ca-Mg-Fe (e.g., adsorption/ion exchange on clays, incorporation into secondary silicates)13,22–24,67. Accordingly, resolving the controls on the slight carbonation observed herein requires targeted experiments that systematically vary Fe speciation, glauconite compositional maturity, and host-sandstone mineralogy, coupled with time-resolved fluid chemistry and saturation-index tracking. Such work would clarify whether carbonation in glauconitic sandstones is primarily limited by cation supply (silicate dissolution), nucleation/growth kinetics of carbonate phases, or competing secondary mineral pathways. Defining these uncertainties would strengthen predictive models for CO2 mineral trapping efficiency in glauconite-bearing sandstone reservoirs.

Implications for co2 sequestration and storage

Permanent mineral trapping of CO2 in glauconite-bearing sandstones has been investigated based on laboratory experiments and reactive-transport/geochemical modeling13,25,61,66. However, the literature also emphasizes that the efficiency of this trapping pathway is strongly dependent on reaction kinetics, surface passivation, and redox state of Fe in glauconite clasts. Fernandez-Bastero et al61. argue that glauconite alteration may exert only a limited control on long-term CO2 mineralization because initial dissolution can generate an amorphous, silica-rich alteration rind that progressively passivates reactive surfaces and retards further cation release. In contrast, Zhang and Tutolo67 propose that glauconite carbonation may represent a high-potential mineral trapping mechanism as divalent cations are liberated from glauconite (notably Fe(II) and Mg2+, and in some systems Ca2+ from associated phases) and can be incorporated into carbonate minerals.

Consistent with previous results, the current experiment provides the first direct evidence that carbonate mineralization can occur in laboratory conditions and on several weeks timescales in glauconite-bearing sandstones. The precipitation of minor carbonate phases, including neo-formed ankerite, indicates that aqueous CO2-rock interaction mobilized Fe, Mg, and Ca and that these cations were subsequently incorporated into a solid carbonate sink (Fig. 15). This observation is significant for two reasons. First, it demonstrates that at least transient conditions of carbonate supersaturation can be achieved during the experiment. Second, the formation of ankerite is likely attributed to the availability of Fe in a carbonate-compatible valence state (Fe2+), linking mineral trapping directly to Fe speciation and supporting the broader notion that redox conditions may amplify mineralization efficiency in glauconite-bearing systems23,24,67. However, further investigation is still needed to quantify the control of Fe speciation on the extent of glauconite carbonation under various experimental conditions.

The study findings have important implications for CO2 subsurface storage widespread glauconitic sandstone reservoirs present in many sedimentary basins worldwide. For example, in southwestern Kazakhstan, Albian glauconitic sandstones are regionally extensive and occur at relatively shallow burial depths (< 1500 m), making them promising sites for engineered CO2 storage. Shallow to moderate depths can be advantageous operationally and economically76,77, while the presence of glauconite introduces a plausible mineral-trapping pathway that can progressively convert dissolved CO2 into stable carbonate minerals, enhancing long-term storage security beyond purely physical trapping. Importantly, the present experimental results also indicate that CO2-brine-rock interaction can substantially dissolve framework grains, including feldspars and glauconite. Such dissolution generates secondary porosity and increases pore connectivity, potentially improving effective storage capacity and injectivity. Nevertheless, the coupling between secondary porosity generation through dissolution and carbonate precipitation warrants careful consideration in reservoir-scale assessments. Dissolution may enhance porosity and permeability, whereas carbonate precipitation can locally occlude pores or reduce permeability if precipitation is focused at pore throats, along flow paths, or near injection wells13,23,24. Accordingly, quantifying the impacts of both processes is crucial for evaluating the CO2 storage capacity of glauconitic-sandstone reservoirs.
