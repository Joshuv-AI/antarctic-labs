Nonparametric undirected graphical model selection using diffusion models
Hyeok Kyu Kwon, Myeonggu Kang, Minwoo Chae, Wanjie Wang, [2mm] Department of Industrial and Management EngineeringPohang University of Science and Technology (POSTECH), Pohang, 37673, South Korea

Abstract
Undirected graphical models provide a fundamental framework for representing conditional independence structures among high-dimensional random variables. While undirected graphical model selection has become a central problem in high-dimensional statistics, most existing methods are restricted to parametric settings. In this paper, we develop a nonparametric approach to undirected graphical model selection based on diffusion models. Recent work has shown that diffusion models can adapt to the unknown graph structure of the underlying distribution, yet utilizing these models for explicit graph estimation remains unexplored. To bridge this gap, we introduce a novel diffusion-based method for nonparametric undirected graphical model selection. We establish the model selection consistency of the proposed method and demonstrate its empirical performance through extensive simulations and two real data analyses. Keywords: Diffusion models, Model selection consistency, Nonparametric estimation, Undirected graphical models

To date, most approaches to graphical model selection rely on parametric assumptions about the underlying distribution.

While nonparametric graph recovery is widely recognized as an important statistical problem, it has long been regarded as statistically challenging. This difficulty arises because it is intrinsically linked to estimating the high-dimensional density

For instance, classical minimax rates for

This reflects the fundamental difficulty of nonparametric density estimation and related inference problems.
Moreover, existing nonparametric approaches to undirected graphical model selection are closely tied to the estimation of the Hessian of
\log p_{0}

Recent advances in diffusion models and score-based generative modeling offer a new perspective on high-dimensional density estimation.
Rather than directly estimating the density
p_{0}

2.2

Beyond their empirical success in generative modeling (e.g., images, videos, and language), recent theoretical developments have analyzed diffusion models from the perspective of nonparametric density estimation
(

31
,
14
,
53
,
2
,
51
,
9

These works show that diffusion-based estimators can adapt to various low-dimensional structures of the underlying distribution
P_{0}
, thereby outperforming classical nonparametric methods in structured settings.
In particular, it has been established that when
p_{0}
admits a factorization into low-dimensional components, diffusion models can avoid the full curse of dimensionality, achieving convergence rates governed by the intrinsic dimensional complexity of
p_{0}
rather than the ambient dimension

(
31

14

Notably, the Hammersley–Clifford theorem guarantees such a factorization whenever
p_{0}
is Markov with respect to a sufficiently sparse undirected graph

While these works
(
31
,
14
)
demonstrate that diffusion models adapt to unknown undirected graph structures, their theoretical results are developed in the context of nonparametric density estimation and do not directly guarantee strong performance in graphical model selection.
In particular, to the best of our knowledge, diffusion models have not been investigated for the purpose of recovering the underlying graph structure.
Nevertheless, since diffusion models adapt to undirected graph structures at the level of density estimation, it is natural to expect that diffusion models, if properly leveraged, can outperform existing approaches to graph recovery.

Motivated by this, we propose a novel diffusion-based method for nonparametric undirected graphical model selection.
The proposed procedure first estimates a family of score functions, following the standard diffusion modeling framework, and then recovers the graph structure using samples generated from the corresponding score estimators.
A key ingredient of our approach is Tweedie’s formula (see (
3.1
)), which enables graph recovery without directly estimating second-order derivatives of
\log p_{0}
.
Instead, the procedure relies on estimating certain covariance matrices from the generated samples, which can be done via simple Monte Carlo methods.

We establish rigorous theoretical guarantees and provide strong empirical evidence for the proposed method.
In particular, under mild regularity conditions, we establish selection consistency of the proposed procedure in a fixed-
D

Although extending the theory to settings where
D

Since the existing theoretical understanding of diffusion-based density estimation is also largely restricted to the fixed-
D
regime, we leave the study of diverging-

Instead, we present extensive experimental results demonstrating that our method substantially outperforms existing nonparametric approaches to graphical model selection.

Preliminaries

2.1
Undirected graphical models

Undirected graphical models, also called Markov random fields, provide a convenient framework for representing conditional independence relationships.
For comprehensive reviews, we refer to the literature
(
32
,
28
,
13
)
.

In general, the pairwise Markov property does not uniquely determine an undirected graph from
P_{0}
.
Specifically, if a graph
G
satisfies the pairwise Markov property with respect to
{\bf X}_{0}
, then any graph obtained by adding edges to
G
also satisfies the same property.
We therefore focus on the graph
G_{0}=([D],E_{0})
defined by the condition that, for all
i\neq j\in[D]
,
(i,j)\notin E_{0}
if and only if
X_{0,i}
and
X_{0,j}
are conditionally independent given
{\bf X}_{0,-(i,j)}
.
We call
G_{0}
the
conditional independence graph
of
P_{0}

(
13
)
.
By construction,
G_{0}
is the unique graph with the smallest edge set among all undirected graphs satisfying the pairwise Markov property.
Throughout the paper, our goal is to estimate
G_{0}
.

If
P_{0}
admits a strictly positive density
p_{0}
with respect to a product measure on
\mathcal{X}
, then, by the celebrated Hammersley–Clifford theorem
(
20
,
32
)
, the density factorizes as

p_{0}({\bf x})=\prod_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}g_{C}({\bf x}_{C}),\qquad{\bf x}\in\mathcal{X},

(2.1)

for some functions
g_{C}
, where
{\bf x}_{C}=(x_{c})_{c\in C}
and
\mathcal{C}_{0}
denotes the set of all (maximal) cliques in the graph
G_{0}
.
Here, a clique is a fully connected subset of the vertex set.

This factorization often yields convenient characterizations of the graph structure in certain distribution families.
Indeed, if
p_{0}
is strictly positive and twice continuously differentiable on a suitably regular domain
\mathcal{X}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
, then

(i,j)\notin E_{0}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{\partial^{2}\log p_{0}({\bf x})}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}=0\quad\forall{\bf x}\in\mathcal{X},\qquad i\neq j\in[D].

(2.2)

This equivalence holds, for example, when
\mathcal{X}
is
[-1,1]^{D}
,
(-1,1)^{D}
, or
{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
; see Lemma 2 and its proof in
50
.
Motivated by this characterization, several recent works
(
66
,
4
,
37
)
focus on estimating the Hessian of
\log p_{0}
to recover the graph
G_{0}
.
A detailed review of these works, along with classical parametric methods and their semiparametric extensions, is provided in Section
A.1
.

2.2
Diffusion models

Let
({\bf X}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the standard Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process defined by the stochastic differential equation (SDE)

{\rm d}{{\bf X}}_{t}=-{{\bf X}}_{t}{\rm d}t+\sqrt{2}{\rm d}{\bf B}_{t},\quad{\bf X}_{0}\sim P_{0},

(2.3)

where
({\bf B}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a standard
D
-dimensional Brownian motion.
Although we focus on this OU process for simplicity, our main result (Theorem
4.3
) extends to more general time-inhomogeneous diffusion processes, including the widely used DDPM
(
22
)
and EDM
(
24
)
frameworks.
For the OU process, the conditional distribution of
{\bf X}_{t}
given
{\bf X}_{0}={\bf x}_{0}
is Gaussian with mean vector
\mu_{t}{\bf x}_{0}
and covariance matrix
\sigma_{t}^{2}{\bf I}_{D}
, where
\mu_{t}=e^{-t}
and
\sigma_{t}^{2}=1-\mu_{t}^{2}
.
Hence, the marginal distribution of
{\bf X}_{t}
, denoted by
P_{t}
, has Lebesgue density
p_{t}
given by

p_{t}({\bf x})=\int\phi_{\sigma_{t}}({\bf x}-\mu_{t}{\bf y}){\rm d}P_{0}({\bf y}).

Let
{\bf f}_{0}({\bf x},t)=\nabla\log p_{t}({\bf x})
.
For each fixed
t
, the map
{\bf x}\mapsto{\bf f}_{0}({\bf x},t)
is the score function corresponding to the marginal density
p_{t}
.
By convention, we also refer to the map
({\bf x},t)\mapsto{\bf f}_{0}({\bf x},t)
as the score function.

For a non-random
\overline{T}>0
, let
({\bf Y}_{t})_{t\in[0,\overline{T})}
be the reverse-time process defined by
{\bf Y}_{t}={\bf X}_{\overline{T}-t}
.
It is well known
(
1
)
that, under mild assumption on
P_{0}
, the reverse-time process is also a diffusion process satisfying

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\bf Y}_{t}=\Big[{\bf Y}_{t}+2{\bf f}_{0}\big({\bf Y}_{t},\overline{T}-t\big)\Big]{\rm d}t+\sqrt{2}{\rm d}{\bf B}_{t},\quad{\bf Y}_{0}\sim P_{\overline{T}}.

Note that the Brownian motion in this SDE is not the same as that appearing in the SDE (
2.3
).
However, for notational simplicity, we use the same notation
{\bf B}_{t}
throughout the paper.

Diffusion models exploit this reverse process to construct an implicit estimator of
P_{0}
.
Specifically, once an estimator
\widehat{{\bf f}}
of the score function
{\bf f}_{0}
is available, one can simulate the reverse process from the standard Gaussian distribution to generate samples from the estimated distribution.
This initialization is justified because
P_{t}
converges rapidly to the standard Gaussian distribution as
t\to\infty

(
3
)
.

The score function can be estimated via score matching
(
23
,
58
,
49
)
.
Let
\mathcal{F}
be a class of Borel-measurable functions
({\bf x},t)\mapsto{\bf f}({\bf x},t)
used to model the score function
{\bf f}_{0}
.
In practice,
\mathcal{F}
is typically chosen as a class of (deep) neural networks.
To avoid potential singularity issues, we estimate
{\bf f}_{0}(\cdot,t)
only for
t\in[\underline{T},\overline{T}]
, where
\underline{T}>0
is sufficiently small.
By a well-known identity due to
58
, the objective function

{\bf f}\mapsto\int_{\underline{T}}^{\overline{T}}{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf f}({\bf X}_{t},t)-{\bf f}_{0}({\bf X}_{t},t)\|_{2}^{2}]{\rm d}t

has the same minimizer over
\mathcal{F}
as

{\bf f}\mapsto{\mathbb{E}}[\ell_{{\bf f}}({\bf X}_{0})],

where

\ell_{{\bf f}}({\bf x})={\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf f}(\mu_{T}{\bf x}+\sigma_{T}{\bf Z},T)+\sigma_{T}^{-1}{\bf Z}\|_{2}^{2}]

denotes the loss function.
Here,
{\bf Z}
is a
D
-dimensional standard Gaussian random vector,
T
is uniformly distributed on
[\underline{T},\overline{T}]
, and
{\bf Z}
and
T
are independent.

This equivalence naturally leads to the following empirical risk minimization (ERM) estimator based on
n
observations
{\bf X}^{1},\ldots,{\bf X}^{n}
:

\widehat{\bf f}\in\argmin_{{\bf f}\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{{\bf f}}({\bf X}^{i}).

(2.4)

Note that the loss function
\ell_{{\bf f}}(\cdot)
is not directly tractable because it involves expectation with respect to
T
and
{\bf Z}
.
In practice, the solution to (
2.4
) is approximated by stochastic gradient descent.
Specifically, since the objective
{\mathbb{E}}[\ell_{{\bf f}}({\bf X}_{0})]
can be written as an expectation over the independent random variables
{\bf X}_{0}
,
T
, and
{\bf Z}
, one forms stochastic approximations by sampling
{\bf X}_{0}
from the empirical distribution and drawing
T
and
{\bf Z}
independently from their respective distributions.

Let
(\widehat{\bf Y}_{t})_{t\in[0,\overline{T}-\underline{T}]}
be the solution to the SDE

{\rm d}\widehat{\bf Y}_{t}=\Big[\widehat{\bf Y}_{t}+2\widehat{\bf f}\big(\widehat{\bf Y}_{t},\overline{T}-t\big)\Big]{\rm d}t+\sqrt{2}\,{\rm d}{\bf B}_{t},\quad\widehat{\bf Y}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{D},{\bf I}_{D}),

(2.5)

and set
\widehat{\bf X}_{t}=\widehat{\bf Y}_{\overline{T}-t}
for
t\in[\underline{T},\overline{T}]
.
Then, for each
t\in[\underline{T},\overline{T}]
, the distribution of
\widehat{\bf X}_{t}
estimates
P_{t}
.
Moreover, since
P_{\underline{T}}
is close to
P_{0}
when
\underline{T}
is small, the distribution of
\widehat{\bf X}_{\underline{T}}
serves as an implicit estimator of
P_{0}
.

In particular, one can obtain samples from the (marginal) distribution of
\widehat{\bf X}_{\underline{T}}
by solving the SDE (
2.5
) up to
t=\overline{T}-\underline{T}
, for example via the Euler–Maruyama discretization
(
27
,
49
)
.
More importantly, for any
({\bf x},t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D}\times(\underline{T},\overline{T}]
, sampling from the conditional distribution of
\widehat{\bf X}_{\underline{T}}
given
\widehat{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}
is straightforward by the Markov property of the SDE (
2.5
). This conditional sampling is the key property exploited in the following section to define the graph estimator.

3
Diffusion-based graphical model selection

In this section, we introduce a diffusion-based method for nonparametric undirected graphical model selection.
Our method estimates the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}({\bf x})
from samples of the conditional distribution of
\widehat{\bf X}_{\underline{T}}
given
\widehat{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}
.
Notably, the method requires no additional training given the score function estimator
\widehat{\bf f}
defined in (
2.4
).
We begin with the relationship between
\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}({\bf x})
and the conditional independence graph
G_{0}
.

Throughout the paper, we assume that
p_{0}
is strictly positive and twice continuously differentiable on
\mathcal{X}=[-1,1]^{D}
.
For each
i,j\in[D]
, let
H_{ij}({\bf x},t)=\partial^{2}\log p_{t}({\bf x})/(\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j})
.

The equivalence (
2.2
) implies that, in principle, the graph
G_{0}
can be recovered by analyzing
H_{ij}({\bf x},t)
at
t=0
.
However, this characterization does not directly extend to
t>0
, since conditional independence is generally not preserved under Gaussian perturbation.
Nevertheless, for each fixed
{\bf x}
, the map
t\mapsto H_{ij}({\bf x},t)
is continuous, and hence
H_{ij}({\bf x},t)
converges to
H_{ij}({\bf x},0)
as
t\to 0
.
One may therefore expect that the condition
(i,j)\notin E_{0}
is nearly equivalent to
|H_{ij}({\bf x},t)|
being sufficiently small for all
{\bf x}
.
At the same time,
t
should not be taken too large; as
t\rightarrow\infty
,
{\bf X}_{t}
rapidly approaches the standard Gaussian, and hence
|H_{ij}({\bf x},t)|
converges to zero for every
(i,j)
and
{\bf x}
.
An illustrative example is provided in Section
A.2
.

For
t>0
,
H_{ij}({\bf x},t)
can be estimated from the score function estimator
\widehat{\bf f}
, without any additional training.
Specifically, the second-order Tweedie’s formula states that

\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}({\bf x})=\sigma_{t}^{-4}\mu_{t}^{2}\text{Cov}\big[{\bf X}_{0}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}\big]-\sigma_{t}^{-2}{\bf I}_{D}\qquad\forall t>0,

(3.1)

see Lemma 1 of
60
.

The advantage of this formula is that
H_{ij}({\bf x},t)
can be estimated by estimating the conditional covariance of
{\bf X}_{0}
given
{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}
.
Since the distribution of
\widehat{\bf X}_{t}
estimates that of
{\bf X}_{t}
for
t\in[\underline{T},\overline{T}]
, and
\underline{T}
is sufficiently small,
H_{ij}({\bf x},t)
can be estimated by

\displaystyle\widehat{H}_{ij}({\bf x},t)=\sigma_{t}^{-4}\mu_{t}^{2}\text{Cov}_{n}\big[\widehat{X}_{\underline{T},i},\widehat{X}_{\underline{T},j}\mid\widehat{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}\big]\qquad\forall i\neq j\in[D],

where
\text{Cov}_{n}[\cdot,\cdot]=\text{Cov}[\cdot,\cdot\mid{\bf X}^{1},\ldots,{\bf X}^{n}]
denotes the covariance conditional on the
n
observations. This conditional covariance can be readily estimated via Monte Carlo sampling using trajectories simulated from the reverse process (
cf.
(
2.5
)).

We have introduced the pointwise estimator
\widehat{H}_{ij}({\bf x},t)
of
H_{ij}({\bf x},t)
for each
({\bf x},t)
.
Recall that, when
t
is small, one expect that the condition
(i,j)\notin E_{0}
is nearly equivalent to
|H_{ij}({\bf x},t)|
being sufficiently small for all
{\bf x}
.
This suggests graph estimation by thresholding
{\mathbb{E}}_{n}[|\widehat{H}_{ij}({\bf U}_{t},t)|]
for a suitable random vector
{\bf U}_{t}
, where
{\mathbb{E}}_{n}[\cdot]={\mathbb{E}}[\cdot\mid{\bf X}^{1},\ldots,{\bf X}^{n}]
denotes the expectation conditional on the
n
observations.

For
t\in[\underline{T},\overline{T}]
and
\tau>0
, we define the undirected graph estimator
\widehat{G}_{t,\tau}=([D],\widehat{E}_{t,\tau})
by

(i,j)\notin\widehat{E}_{t,\tau}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{\mathbb{E}}_{n}\big[\big|\widehat{H}_{ij}({\bf U}_{t},t)\big|\big]\leq\tau,\qquad i\neq j\in[D].

(3.2)

Here,
\tau
and
t
are allowed to depend on
n
, and the distribution of
{\bf U}_{t}
will be specified later.
The overall procedure is summarized in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Diffusion-based graph estimation

1:

Input:
Data
{\bf X}^{1},\ldots,{\bf X}^{n}
, parameters
t,\tau
, and model class
\mathcal{F}
.

2:

Solve the problem (
2.4
) and obtain the score function estimator
\widehat{\bf f}\in\mathcal{F}
.

3:

Compute
{\mathbb{E}}_{n}\big[|\widehat{H}_{ij}({\bf U}_{t},t)|\big]
for all
i\neq j
.

4:

Set
(i,j)\in\widehat{E}_{t,\tau}
if and only if
{\mathbb{E}}_{n}\big[|\widehat{H}_{ij}({\bf U}_{t},t)|\big]>\tau
.

5:

Return
\widehat{G}_{t,\tau}=([D],\widehat{E}_{t,\tau})
.

Note that evaluating the target estimator
\widehat{G}_{t,\tau}
involves numerical errors from two sources.
First, sampling from the conditional distribution of
\widehat{\bf X}_{\underline{T}}
given
\widehat{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}
requires discretizing the underlying SDE, for example via the Euler–Maruyama method
(
27
,
49
)
, which introduces discretization error.
Second, both the covariance appearing in the definition of
\widehat{H}_{ij}({\bf x},t)
and the outer expectation in
{\mathbb{E}}_{n}[|\widehat{H}_{ij}({\bf U}_{t},t)|]
are approximated from finitely many simulated samples, which introduces Monte Carlo error.
Various works study discretization errors in diffusion models
(
45
,
5
,
10
)
.
Moreover, the errors induced by approximating the covariance and the expectation can be controlled by standard Monte Carlo bounds.
We assume these numerical errors to be negligible in our theoretical analysis.

4.1
Assumptions

For a multi-index
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{D})^{\top}\in({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})^{D}
, let
{\rm D}^{\bm{\alpha}}
denote the mixed partial derivative operator
\partial^{\bm{\alpha}_{\cdot}}/(\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{D}^{\alpha_{D}})
, where
\alpha{{}_{\cdot}}=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\alpha_{i}
.
For any
\beta,K>0
, let
\mathcal{H}^{\beta,K}(A)
denote the class of
\beta
-Hölder functions on
A\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
, consisting of all functions
g:A\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha_{\cdot}\leq\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\sup_{{\bf x}\in A}|({\rm D}^{\bm{\alpha}}g)({\bf x})|+\sum_{\alpha_{\cdot}=\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf x},{\bf y}\in A\\
{\bf x}\neq{\bf y}\end{subarray}}\frac{|({\rm D}^{\bm{\alpha}}g)({\bf x})-({\rm D}^{\bm{\alpha}}g)({\bf y})|}{\|{\bf x}-{\bf y}\|_{\infty}^{\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}\leq K,

where
\lfloor\beta\rfloor
denotes the largest integer strictly smaller than
\beta
.
Throughout the paper, we will impose the following assumption on
p_{0}
:

(
S
) There exist constants
\beta>2
and
K>0
such that
\log p_{0}\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta,K}([-1,1]^{D})
.

This condition arises naturally because our characterization of the graph structure relies on the equivalence (
2.2
), which in turn requires the second-order smoothness of
\log p_{0}
.
To the best of our knowledge, existing methods for nonparametric undirected graphical model selection rely on substantially more restrictive assumptions
(
39
,
38
,
62
,
40
,
4
,
66
,
37
)
.
Detailed reviews of these works are provided in Section

.

4.2
Edge identification via Hessian components

In this subsection, we present one of the main results of the paper, showing that the edges of
G_{0}
can be identified through the Hessian of
\log p_{t}
.
Given an undirected graph
G
,
let
d_{G}(i,j)
denote the minimum length of a path from vertex
i
to vertex
j
in
G
.
By convention, we set
d_{G}(i,i)=0
and
d_{G}(i,j)=\infty
if there is no path between
i
and
j
in
G
.
For simplicity, we write
d(i,j)=d_{G_{0}}(i,j)
.
Note that for all
i\neq j\in[D]
,

(i,j)\notin E_{0}

if and only if

d(i,j)>1
.
Theorem
4.1
shows that,
{\rm{Cov}}[X_{0,i},X_{0,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}]
decays with a rate depending on
d(i,j)
as
t\to 0
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\gamma\in[0,1)
, and suppose that the density
p_{0}
satisfies assumption (
S
).
Then, for every
t\leq C_{1}
and
\|{\bf x}\|_{\infty}\leq\mu_{t}\gamma
, we have
\sigma_{t}/\mu_{t}\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\Big|{\rm{Cov}}\big[X_{0,i},X_{0,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}\big]\Big|\leq C_{2}\sigma_{t}^{(2d(i,j)+2)\wedge(\beta+2)\wedge 5},

for all
i,j\in[D]
, and

\displaystyle\Big|{\rm{Cov}}\big[X_{0,i},X_{0,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}\big]\Big|\geq\Big|\big({\bf H}^{d(i,j)}\big)_{ij}\Big|\big(\sigma_{t}/\mu_{t}\big)^{2d(i,j)+2}-C_{2}\sigma_{t}^{(2d(i,j)+4)\wedge(\beta+2)\wedge 5},

for all
i,j\in[D]
with
d(i,j)<\infty
, where
C_{1}=C_{1}(\beta,K,D,\gamma)
and
C_{2}=C_{2}(\beta,K,D)
are positive constants, and

{\bf H}=(H_{ij}({\bf x}/\mu_{t},0))\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D\times D}.

Since
\sigma_{t}=\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}
and
\mu_{t}=e^{-t}
, we have
\sigma_{t}\asymp\sqrt{t}
and
\mu_{t}\asymp 1
for sufficiently small
t
.
Hence, Theorem
4.1
implies that

\displaystyle\Big|{\rm{Cov}}\big[X_{0,i},X_{0,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}\big]\Big|\asymp t^{2}\qquad\forall(i,j)\in E_{0},

provided that
H_{ij}({\bf x}/\mu_{t},0)
is nonzero, whereas

\displaystyle\Big|{\rm{Cov}}\big[X_{0,i},X_{0,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}\big]\Big|\lesssim t^{\frac{(\beta+2)\wedge 5}{2}}\qquad\forall(i,j)\notin E_{0}\text{ and }i\neq j.

Consequently, the conditional covariance decays at a faster rate for non-edges
(i,j)\notin E_{0}
than for edges
(i,j)\in E_{0}
.
Theorem
4.1
immediately yields the following corollary, which characterizes the decay rate of
H_{ij}({\bf x},t)
according to whether
(i,j)\in E_{0}
.

Corollary 4.2
.

Suppose that the density function
p_{0}
satisfies assumption (
S
).
Let
\gamma,C_{1},C_{2}
be the constants defined in Theorem
4.1
.
Then, for every
t\leq C_{1},\|{\bf x}\|_{\infty}\leq\mu_{t}\gamma
, and
i,j\in[D]
, we have

\displaystyle\big|H_{ij}({\bf x}/\mu_{t},0)\big|-C_{2}\sigma_{t}^{\tilde{\beta}}\leq\big|H_{ij}({\bf x},t)\big|\leq C_{2},\quad\text{if }d(i,j)=1,

and

|H_{ij}({\bf x},t)|\leq C_{2}\sigma_{t}^{\tilde{\beta}}
if
d(i,j)\geq 2
,
where
\tilde{\beta}=(\beta-2)\wedge 1
.

Corollary
4.2
implies that, for small
t
and suitable
{\bf x}\in[-\mu_{t}\gamma,\mu_{t}\gamma]^{D}
, the pointwise value
|H_{ij}({\bf x},t)|
can be used to characterize conditional independence.
Specifically, we have
|H_{ij}({\bf x},t)|\lesssim t^{\tilde{\beta}/2}
for
(i,j)\notin E_{0}
with
i\neq j
, whereas
|H_{ij}({\bf x},t)|\asymp 1
for
(i,j)\in E_{0}
.
This pointwise characterization, however, requires that
|H_{ij}({\bf x}/\mu_{t},0)|>0
for every
(i,j)\in E_{0}
.
One can instead characterize conditional independence through the averaged Hessian
{\mathbb{E}}[|H_{ij}({\bf U}_{t},t)|]
, provided that
{\mathbb{E}}[|H_{ij}({\bf U}_{t}/\mu_{t},0)|]>0
for every
(i,j)\in E_{0}
.

Throughout this section, we take
{\bf U}_{t}
to be the uniform random vector on
[-\mu_{t}\gamma,\mu_{t}\gamma]^{D}
for each
t\geq 0
.
Note that this choice is flexible: our main consistency result (Theorem
4.3
) continues to hold if the uniform distribution is replaced by any continuous distribution on
[-\mu_{t}\gamma,\mu_{t}\gamma]^{D}
whose density is uniformly bounded from above for
t\lesssim 1
.
Practical choices of
{\bf U}_{t}
used in our experiments are discussed in Section
C
.

Note that
{\bf U}_{0}
is the uniform distribution on
[-1,1]^{D}
.
The equivalence (
2.2
) then implies that
{\mathbb{E}}[|H_{ij}({\bf U}_{0},0)|]>0
for every
(i,j)\in E_{0}
, provided that
\gamma
is sufficiently close to
1
.
Consequently,
C_{\gamma}>0
can always be ensured by such a choice of
\gamma
, where
C_{\gamma}=\min_{(i,j)\in E_{0}}{\mathbb{E}}[|H_{ij}({\bf U}_{0},0)|]
.
We therefore fix
\gamma\in(0,1)
such that
C_{\gamma}>0
.
When
E_{0}
is empty, we set
C_{\gamma}=1
; any fixed positive constant suffices for our main results.

Let
\widetilde{T}={\widetilde{T}}(\beta,\underline{T},C_{1},C_{2},C_{\gamma})>0
be a (small enough) constant such that
2C_{2}\sigma_{{\widetilde{T}}}^{\tilde{\beta}}<C_{\gamma}
,
{\widetilde{T}}\leq C_{1}
and
{\widetilde{T}}\geq\underline{T}
.
Then, for every
t\leq{\widetilde{T}}
and
i\neq j\in[D]
, Corollary
4.2
implies that

(i,j)\notin E_{0}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big|H_{ij}({\bf U}_{t},t)\big|\big]\leq C_{2}\sigma_{t}^{\tilde{\beta}}.

(4.1)

This novel characterization is the key to choosing the threshold
\tau
in the estimator
\widehat{G}_{t,\tau}
and to proving graph selection consistency in Section
4.3
.

A closely related analysis of the relationship between
H_{ij}({\bf x},t)
and
d(i,j)
was given by
19
.
Under stronger assumptions than those of Corollary
4.2
, they derived a sharper upper bound on
|H_{ij}({\bf x},t)|
.
Specifically, Theorem 2.1 of
19
states that, if
p_{0}
is log-concave and twice continuously differentiable, then
|H_{ij}({\bf x},t)|\lesssim\sigma_{t}^{2d(i,j)-2}
.
This bound matches with Corollary
4.2
when
d(i,j)=1
and is strictly stronger if
d(i,j)>1
.
In contrast, our result relaxes the log-concavity assumption and also provides a lower bound on the Hessian entry, leading to the characterization of conditional independence in (
4.1
).

4.3
Estimator consistency

For two undirected graphs
G_{1}=([D],E_{1})
and
G_{2}=([D],E_{2})
, we write
G_{1}=G_{2}
if and only if
E_{1}=E_{2}
.
We say that the estimator
\widehat{G}_{t,\tau}
is consistent if

{\mathbb{P}}(\widehat{G}_{t,\tau}\neq G_{0})=o(1).

With appropriate choices of
\tau
and
t
, the following theorem provides a non-asymptotic upper bound on
{\mathbb{P}}(\widehat{G}_{t,\tau}\neq G_{0})
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose that
p_{0}
satisfies (
S
), and let
\tilde{\beta},\gamma,C_{\gamma},C_{2},{\widetilde{T}}
be the constants defined in Section
4.2
.
For each
t\geq 0
, let
{\bf U}_{t}
be the uniform random vector on
[-\mu_{t}\gamma,\mu_{t}\gamma]^{D}
.
Let
\epsilon_{n}>0
be given with
\epsilon_{n}<1/e
, and suppose that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\int_{\underline{T}}^{{\widetilde{T}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{D}}\big\|\widehat{\bf f}({\bf x},t)-{\bf f}_{0}({\bf x},t)\big\|_{2}^{2}p_{t}({\bf x}){\rm d}{\bf x}{\rm d}t\right)^{1/2}\right]\leq\epsilon_{n},

(4.2)

and

\sup_{{\bf f}\in\mathcal{F}}\sup_{{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D}}\big\|{\bf f}({\bf x},t)\big\|_{\infty}\leq\sigma_{t}^{-1}\sqrt{\log(1/\epsilon_{n})},\quad\forall t\in[\underline{T},{\widetilde{T}}].

(4.3)

For each
t\leq{\widetilde{T}}
, let
\tau>0
be chosen to satisfy

C_{2}\sigma_{t}^{\tilde{\beta}}<\tau<C_{\gamma}-C_{2}\sigma_{t}^{\tilde{\beta}}.

Then, for every
t\in[\underline{T},{\widetilde{T}}]
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\widehat{G}_{t,\tau}\neq G_{0}\Big)\leq C_{3}\left[\frac{\underline{T}}{(t^{2-\tilde{\beta}/2}\eta_{t,\tau,1})\wedge(t^{2}\eta_{t,\tau,2})}+\frac{\epsilon_{n}\{\log(1/\epsilon_{n})\}^{3/2}}{t^{2}(\eta_{t,\tau,1}\wedge\eta_{t,\tau,2})}\right],

where
C_{3}=C_{3}(\beta,K,D,\gamma)
,
\eta_{t,\tau,1}=\tau-C_{2}\sigma_{t}^{\tilde{\beta}}
and
\eta_{t,\tau,2}=C_{\gamma}-C_{2}\sigma_{t}^{\tilde{\beta}}-\tau
.

The condition (
4.2
) is a key assumption for establishing consistency; it specifies the convergence rate of
\widehat{\bf f}
to the true score function
{\bf f}_{0}
, which in turn governs the convergence rate of the graph estimator
\widehat{G}_{t,\tau}
to
G_{0}
.
Several works on diffusion models establish (
4.2
) under (
S
), together with additional technical assumptions.
For example, when
\mathcal{F}
is taken to be a class of sparse neural networks, the rate
\epsilon_{n}\asymp n^{-\beta/(2\beta+D)}
is achievable up to a logarithmic factor
(
45
)
.
Moreover, the improved rate
\epsilon_{n}\asymp n^{-\beta/(2\beta+d)}
can be attained up to a logarithm factor by taking
\mathcal{F}
to be a class of sparse weight-sharing networks
(
31
)
or fully connected networks
(
14
)
.
Here,
d=\max_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}|C|
denotes the maximum clique size of
G_{0}
.
The condition (
4.3
) is a technical assumption that controls the tail probability of the solution to the SDE (
2.5
), which is also imposed in the aforementioned works
(
45
,
31
,
14
)
.

By Theorem
4.3
, once we take
\underline{T}\asymp n^{-c_{0}}
for a sufficiently large constant
c_{0}>0
, the estimator
\widehat{G}_{t,\tau}
is consistent whenever

\frac{\epsilon_{n}\{\log(1/\epsilon_{n})\}^{3/2}}{t^{2}(\eta_{t,\tau,1}\wedge\eta_{t,\tau,2})}=o(1).

(4.4)

When
\epsilon_{n}=o(1)
, condition (
4.4
) is easily verified by taking
t
and
\tau
to be constants, for example,
t={\widetilde{T}}
and
\tau=C_{\gamma}/2
.
As another example, if
\beta\geq 3
and
\epsilon_{n}\asymp n^{-c_{1}}
, then the choice
t=n^{-c_{1}/4}
and
\tau=2n^{-c_{1}/32}
satisfies (
4.4
).

Note that the constant
C_{\gamma}
represents the minimum signal of dependence between variables.
We have implicitly assumed that this signal is of constant order; however, it is natural to consider settings in which two variables are conditionally dependent, but the strength of their dependence is weak. Such weak dependence can be accommodated by allowing
C_{\gamma}\to 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
Building on the previous example, let
c_{1}=\beta/(2\beta+d)
,
\epsilon_{n}=n^{-c_{1}}
,
t=n^{-c_{2}}
and
\tau=2C_{2}\sigma_{t}^{\widetilde{\beta}}
, where
c_{2}=2c_{1}/(4+\widetilde{\beta})-\delta/\widetilde{\beta}
for a sufficiently small
\delta>0
.
Then, condition (
4.4
) is satisfied if
C_{\gamma}\gtrsim n^{-\frac{\tilde{\beta}\beta}{(\tilde{\beta}+4)(2\beta+d)}+\delta}
, which implies that the graph estimator
\widehat{G}_{t,\tau}
remains consistent even in this weak dependence scenario.

5
Practical tuning strategy

While Theorem
4.3
provides sufficient conditions for
(t,\tau)
to guarantee the consistency of
\widehat{G}_{t,\tau}
, these parameters depend on unknown quantities such as
d
and
\beta
. In particular, selecting an appropriate threshold
\tau
poses a significant challenge in practical applications. To address this inherent limitation, this section discusses practical procedures for aggregating information across a range of timescales
t
and employing a heuristic to determine the threshold for edge selection. A detailed discussion accompanied by an illustrative example is provided in Section
C
.

For each fixed
t
, let
\widetilde{H}_{ij}(t)
be the standardized value of
{\mathbb{E}}_{n}[|\widehat{H}_{ij}({\bf U}_{t},t)|]
, where the standardization is performed across all pairs
\{(i,j):i,j\in[D],\,i<j\}
. Instead of directly thresholding
{\mathbb{E}}_{n}[|\widehat{H}_{ij}({\bf U}_{t},t)|]
for a single
t
, we leverage information across multiple time points
t_{1}<\cdots<t_{M}
, chosen to be sufficiently small. Empirically, we have observed that it suffices to select
t_{i}
’s such that
\sigma_{t_{i}}/\mu_{t_{i}}\leq 0.5
. We set
M=30
in all our experiments. We then apply
K
-means clustering with
K=2
to the
M
-dimensional vectors

\{\big(\widetilde{H}_{ij}(t_{1}),\ldots,\widetilde{H}_{ij}(t_{M})\big):i,j\in[D],\,i<j\}.

For each
i<j
, we let
(i,j)\in\widehat{E}
(and consequently
(j,i)\in\widehat{E}
) whenever the cluster containing
(i,j)
corresponds to the one with the larger centroid. As illustrated in Section
6
, this simple rule works surprisingly well across all our numerical experiments.

7
Real data analysis

In this section, we apply our diffusion-based method for undirected graphical model selection to two real datasets that have not been explored in the existing literature.
We first apply our method to image data, where the recovered graph has a transparent interpretation, and then to stock price data, where the dependence structure among companies is less obvious and of independent interest.

7.1
Image analysis

A long-standing tradition in image analysis is to represent an image as a local undirected graph, in which nearby pixels are directly connected, and distant pixels are connected only through their neighbours.
This locality has long been exploited in classical image processing
(
36
)
and underlies the design of modern architectures such as convolutional neural networks
(
29
,
18
)
.
Although recent empirical work suggests that conditioning on neighbouring pixels weakens the dependence between distant pixels
(
55
,
56
)
, the conditional independence graph of image data has not been formally investigated, to the best of our knowledge.
We apply the proposed method to the MNIST dataset
(
35
)
, which consists of
50000
training images, each of size
28\times 28=784
pixels; see Section
E.1
for further details.

The left panel of Figure
2
displays the resulting estimated graph.
Each vertex is either isolated or connected only to its immediate neighbours, which aligns with the locality principle described above.
Edges are concentrated near the center of the image, where most non-zero pixel values reside.
Out of the
\binom{784}{2}=306936
possible edges, only
640
are recovered, indicating that the conditional independence graph of MNIST is highly sparse.

A more striking feature is the emergence of diagonal edges. In addition to horizontal and vertical neighbors, the estimated graph contains a substantial number of diagonal edges connecting upper-right to lower-left pixels, but markedly fewer connecting upper-left to lower-right pixels.

To illustrate this phenomenon, we examine the estimated local Hessian values around a fixed anchor pixel.
Since each pixel of the
28\times 28
grid can be identified by its position in the
784
-dimensional vector, we fix the anchor at
i=(15,16)
and report the values of
\widetilde{H}_{ij}(t)
for all
j\in[28]\times[28]
with
j\neq i
in the right panel of Figure
2
.
Here, the upper-left pixel corresponds to
(1,1)
and the lower-right pixel to
(28,28)
.
The four pixels at
\ell^{1}
-distance one from
i
, namely
(14,16)
,
(16,16)
,
(15,15)
, and
(15,17)
, are clearly identified as strong candidates for inclusion in the edge set.
Among the pixels at
\ell^{1}
-distance two from
i
, the next strongest candidates are
(16,15)
and
(14,17)
, which correspond precisely to the upper-right-to-lower-left diagonal direction.
This pattern aligns with the asymmetry of diagonal edges observed in the estimated conditional independence graph.

We conjecture that this asymmetry reflects the predominant slant of MNIST digits, which tend to lean from the upper right toward the lower left. This slant arises mainly because the digits are handwritten, and the majority of writers are right-handed, typically writing digits from the upper right toward the lower left. While the horizontal and vertical neighbors can be readily explained by the general properties of natural images, these diagonal edges reflect a property specific to MNIST images themselves. This example shows that our model captures even such an unanticipated structure, suggesting its potential for broader applications.

We have shown that the MNIST data set admits a low-dimensional, sparse graphical structure.
Although the ambient dimension is
D=784
, Figure
2
reveals that the maximum clique size in the estimated graph is only
d=4
.
This provides empirical support for the sparse graphical model assumptions employed in
31
,
14
to explain the success of diffusion models on high-dimensional structured data.

8
Conclusion

We have proposed a diffusion-based estimator of the conditional independence graph and shown that it is theoretically consistent and empirically competitive across a variety of data distributions.
We close by discussing two directions for future work.

First, as briefly noted in Section
1
, a natural next step is to extend our analysis to high-dimensional settings in which the dimension
D
grows with the sample size
n
.
Given that diffusion models have demonstrated strong empirical performance even when
D\gg n
, we expect that our framework can be extended to such scenarios with more delicate analysis.

Second, it would be interesting to extend our framework to discrete distributions, for which diffusion models based on continuous-time Markov chains (CTMCs) have recently achieved remarkable success in modeling high-dimensional discrete data such as natural language
(
41
,
25
)
.
Since our framework relies on the connection between the SDE solution and conditional independence via Tweedie’s formula, investigating an analogous connection in the CTMC setting is a natural direction.

A.1
Related work

In this subsection, we review existing methods for undirected graphical model selection.
We also discuss existing work on nonparametric density estimation relevant to our method.

The factorization (
2.1
) often yields convenient characterizations of the graph structure in certain distribution families.
For example, in the Gaussian case with covariance matrix
\Sigma\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D\times D}
, the condition
(i,j)\notin E_{0}
is equivalent to
(\Sigma^{-1})_{ij}=0
for all
i\neq j\in[D]
; see Proposition 5.2 of
32
.
Thus, Gaussian graphical models recover the graph
G_{0}
by estimating the precision matrix
(
12
,
43
,
64
,
16
)
.
This idea extends to semiparametric settings, since conditional independence is invariant under componentwise monotone transformations
(
39
)
.
Accordingly, the precision matrix of the transformed Gaussian random vector is estimated after suitable marginal transformations
(
39
,
38
,
62
)
; these methods are known as nonparanormal graphical models.

While nonparanormal models offer greater flexibility, they still fundamentally rely on a latent Gaussian structure. To move beyond such constraints, it is natural to investigate whether the graph structure can be characterized in a fully nonparametric manner.
To the best of our knowledge, however, existing methods rely on restrictive assumptions.
For example,
G_{0}
is restricted to be an undirected acyclic graph (forest), which in particular implies
d\leq 2
, where
d
denotes the maximum clique size of
G_{0}
.
They exploit the fact that, for this graph class, the factorization (
2.1
) admits an explicit representation in terms of the univariate and bivariate marginal densities of
p_{0}
.

The methods most closely related to ours are those that exploit the equivalence (
2.2
) to recover
G_{0}
through the Hessian of
\log p_{0}

(
4
,
66
,
37
)
.
These methods first estimate
\log p_{0}
or
\nabla\log p_{0}
under suitable regularization and then differentiate to obtain a pointwise estimator of
\nabla^{2}\log p_{0}({\bf x})
.
The graph estimator is then constructed by thresholding the estimated entries of the matrix
{\mathbb{E}}[|\nabla^{2}\log p_{0}({\bf U})|]
for a suitable random vector
{\bf U}
, where
|\cdot|
is applied componentwise to the matrix.
The SING model
(
4
)
assumes that
p_{0}
is the density of the pushforward of a standard normal random vector under a monotone lower-triangular map parameterized by tensor-product Hermite polynomials.
It establishes consistency by estimating the transport map within the same model class and then taking second-order derivatives of the logarithm of the induced pushforward density.
While selection consistency is not established in
66
, the graph is estimated by first learning
\nabla\log p_{0}({\bf x})
with a neural network via score matching
(
23
)
, and then differentiating the learned score to estimate
\nabla^{2}\log p_{0}
.
The L-SING model
(
37
)
estimates the conditional density of
X_{0,i}
given the remaining variables for each
i\in[D]
and uses the Hessian of the corresponding log-conditional density to construct the graph.

A common limitation of these works is that they all require pointwise estimation of the Hessian of
\log p_{0}
, which is intrinsically more difficult than estimating the density or its score; see Section 6 of
31
and the references therein.
Our method circumvents this difficulty by using diffusion models to estimate the Hessian of
\log p_{t}
instead of that of
\log p_{0}
.
This is made possible by the minimax optimality of diffusion models in nonparametric density estimation.

The minimax optimal rate for estimating a standard smooth density in the
\beta
-Hölder class is well known to be
n^{-\beta/(2\beta+D)}

(
54
,
17
)
, for example, under the total variation distance.
Various modern deep generative models also attain this rate up to logarithmic factors
(
30
,
45
,
47
)
.
Moreover, diffusion models can attain the rate
n^{-\beta/(2\beta+d)}
for every
\beta>0
and
d\leq D

(
31
,
14
)
, which is faster than the standard rate
n^{-\beta/(2\beta+D)}
.
In fact, for any constants
\beta>0
and
d_{0}\leq D
, the rate
n^{-\beta/(2\beta+d_{0})}
is minimax optimal over densities in the
\beta
-Hölder class whose conditional independence graph has maximum clique size at most
d_{0}

(
31
)
.
This suggests that diffusion models are natural candidates for density estimation regardless of whether the underlying graph
G_{0}
is sparse.

B.1
Proof outlines

In this subsection, we outline the key ideas behind the proofs of Theorems

.
We begin with Theorem
4.1
.

B.1.1
Theorem
4.1

Note that

\displaystyle{\rm{Cov}}\big[{\bf X}_{0}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}\big]=\left(\frac{\sigma_{t}}{\mu_{t}}\right)^{2}\mbox{{\rm Cov}}\big[{\bf Z}_{0}\big],

where
{\bf Z}_{0}
is the conditional random vector
(\mu_{t}{\bf X}_{0}-{\bf x})/\sigma_{t}
given
{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}
.
Here, we suppress the dependence on
{\bf x}
and
t
by treating them as fixed.
Using the above identity, it suffices to derive upper and lower bounds for the covariance of
{\bf Z}_{0}
instead.

We first approximate
{\bf Z}_{0}
by a random vector
\widetilde{\bf Z}
whose covariance can be characterized explicitly.
Since the conditional density of
{\bf X}_{0}
given
{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}
is proportional, up to normalization, to the map
{\bf x}_{0}\mapsto p_{0}({\bf x}_{0})\phi_{\sigma_{t}}({\bf x}-\mu_{t}{\bf x}_{0})
, the Lebesgue density of
{\bf Z}_{0}
is proportional to the map

\displaystyle{\bf z}\mapsto\exp\big(u_{0}({\bf z})\big),\qquad u_{0}({\bf z})=\log p_{0}\left(\frac{{\bf x}+\sigma_{t}{\bf z}}{\mu_{t}}\right)-\frac{\|{\bf z}\|_{2}^{2}}{2},

with support
S=\{{\bf z}:\|{\bf x}+\sigma_{t}{\bf z}\|_{\infty}\leq\mu_{t}\}
.
By assumption (
S
),
u_{0}
is twice continuously differentiable on
S
.
Since
\mathbf{0}_{D}\in S
, let
\widetilde{u}:{\mathbb{R}}^{D}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be the second-order polynomial given by the second-order Taylor expansion of
u_{0}({\bf z})
at
{\bf z}=\mathbf{0}_{D}
. Then,

\big|u_{0}({\bf z})-\widetilde{u}({\bf z})\big|\lesssim\sigma_{t}^{\beta\wedge 3}\|{\bf z}\|_{\infty}^{\beta\wedge 3}\qquad\forall{\bf z}\in S.

(B.1)

Define
\widetilde{\bf Z}=(\widetilde{Z}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{Z}_{D})
to be the Gaussian random vector with density proportional to the map
{\bf z}\mapsto\exp(\widetilde{u}({\bf z}))
.
Then, the difference between the covariances of
{\bf Z}_{0}
and
\widetilde{\bf Z}
can be bounded as

\Big|{\rm Cov}\big[Z_{0,i},Z_{0,j}\big]-{\rm Cov}\big[\widetilde{Z}_{i},\widetilde{Z}_{j}\big]\Big|\lesssim\sigma_{t}^{\beta\wedge 3}\qquad\forall i,j\in[D].

(B.2)

Here, the error is mainly driven by the Taylor expansion error in (
B.1
), while the mismatch between the supports of
\widetilde{\bf Z}
and
{\bf Z}_{0}
, namely
{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
and
S
, is negligible.
Specifically,
{\mathbb{P}}(\widetilde{\bf Z}\notin S)
is sufficiently small, since
\|{\bf x}\|_{\infty}\leq\mu_{t}\gamma<\mu_{t}
and the set
S
is sufficiently large for small
t
.

We now derive upper and lower bounds for the covariance of
\widetilde{\bf Z}
.
Since
\widetilde{\bf Z}
is Gaussian, its covariance matrix is given by the inverse of the negative Hessian of its log-density.
By construction,
\nabla^{2}\widetilde{u}({\bf z})=\nabla^{2}u_{0}(\mathbf{0}_{D})=(\sigma_{t}/\mu_{t})^{2}{\bf H}-{\bf I}_{D}
for every
{\bf z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
and hence

\displaystyle\mbox{{\rm Cov}}\big[\widetilde{\bf Z}\big]=\bigg({\bf I}_{D}-\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\mu_{t}^{2}}{\bf H}\bigg)^{-1}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2k}}{\mu_{t}^{2k}}{\bf H}^{k},

where the last equality holds for sufficiently small
t
by the Neumann series expansion.

The key connection between the graph distance and the covariance appears here.
By the equivalence (
2.2
), the condition

H_{ij}({\bf x}/\mu_{t},0)\neq 0
implies
(i,j)\in E_{0}
for
i\neq j\in[D]
.
The essential point is that
({\bf H}^{k})_{ij}=0
for every
k<d(i,j)
; see Lemma
B.1
.
The proof proceeds by contradiction: if
({\bf H}^{k})_{ij}\neq 0
for some
k<d(i,j)
, then there exists a path of length
k
from vertex
i
to
j
, contradicting the definition of
d(i,j)
.
Therefore, all terms with
k<d(i,j)
vanish in the
(i,j)
th entry of the series expansion, which yields

\displaystyle\mbox{{\rm Cov}}\big[\widetilde{Z}_{i},\widetilde{Z}_{j}\big]=\sum_{k\geq d(i,j)}\big({\bf H}^{k}\big)_{ij}\big(\sigma_{t}/\mu_{t}\big)^{2k}

and

\displaystyle\Big|{\rm Cov}\big[\widetilde{Z}_{i},\widetilde{Z}_{j}\big]-\big({\bf H}^{d(i,j)}\big)_{ij}\big(\sigma_{t}/\mu_{t}\big)^{2d(i,j)}\Big|\lesssim\sigma_{t}^{2d(i,j)+2}.

Combining this with (
B.2
) and the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\Big|{\rm Cov}\big[Z_{0,i},Z_{0,j}\big]-\big({\bf H}^{d(i,j)}\big)_{ij}\big(\sigma_{t}/\mu_{t}\big)^{2d(i,j)}\Big|\lesssim\sigma_{t}^{(2d(i,j)+2)\wedge\beta\wedge 3}.

The assertion then follows from the relation
\mbox{{\rm Cov}}[X_{0,i},X_{0,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}]\asymp\sigma_{t}^{2}\mbox{{\rm Cov}}[Z_{0,i},Z_{0,j}]
.

B.1.2
Theorem
4.3

Here, we provide a sketch of the proof of Theorem
4.3
.
For every
t\leq{\widetilde{T}}
, Corollary
4.2
implies that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big|H_{ij}({\bf U}_{t},t)\big|\big]-\tau\geq C_{\gamma}-C_{2}\sigma_{t}^{\tilde{\beta}}-\tau>0\qquad\forall(i,j)\in E_{0}.

Combining this with Markov’s inequality, the false negative rate is bounded as

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\Big({\mathbb{E}}_{n}\big[\big|\widehat{H}_{ij}\big({\bf U}_{t},t\big)\big|\big]\leq\tau\Big)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\Big|{\mathbb{E}}_{n}\big[\big|\widehat{H}_{ij}\big({\bf U}_{t},t\big)\big|\big]-{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big|H_{ij}\big({\bf U}_{t},t\big)\big|\big]\Big|\geq{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big|H_{ij}\big({\bf U}_{t},t\big)\big|\big]-\tau\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathcal{E}_{ij,t}}{C_{\gamma}-C_{2}\sigma_{t}^{\tilde{\beta}}-\tau}\qquad\forall(i,j)\in E_{0},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{ij,t}={\mathbb{E}}\left[\Big|{\mathbb{E}}_{n}\big[\big|\widehat{H}_{ij}\big({\bf U}_{t},t\big)\big|\big]-{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big|H_{ij}\big({\bf U}_{t},t\big)\big|\big]\Big|\right],\qquad i\neq j\in[D],

is the estimation error for the
(i,j)
th entry of the averaged Hessian at time
t
.
Similarly, the false positive rate is bounded as

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\Big({\mathbb{E}}_{n}\big[\big|\widehat{H}_{ij}\big({\bf U}_{t},t\big)\big|\big]>\tau\Big)\leq\frac{\mathcal{E}_{ij,t}}{\tau-C_{2}\sigma_{t}^{\tilde{\beta}}}\qquad\forall(i,j)\notin E_{0}\text{ with }i\neq j.

Therefore, it suffices to derive an upper bound on
\mathcal{E}_{ij,t}
.

Recall that
H_{ij}({\bf x},t)
and
\widehat{H}_{ij}({\bf x},t)
are defined through the conditional covariances of
{\bf X}_{0}
given
{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}
and of
\widehat{\bf X}_{\underline{T}}
given
\widehat{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}
, respectively, scaled by
\sigma_{t}^{-4}\mu_{t}^{2}
.
By the triangle inequality,

\mathcal{E}_{ij,t}\leq\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big|H_{ij}\big({\bf U}_{t},t\big)\big|\big]-{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big|\widetilde{H}_{ij}\big({\bf U}_{t},t\big)\big|\big]\Big|+{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big|\widetilde{H}_{ij}\big({\bf U}_{t},t\big)\big|\big]-{\mathbb{E}}_{n}\big[\big|\widehat{H}_{ij}\big({\bf U}_{t},t\big)\big|\big]\Big|\Big],

(B.3)

where
\widetilde{H}_{ij}({\bf x},t)=\sigma_{t}^{-4}\mu_{t}^{2}\text{Cov}[X_{\underline{T},i},X_{\underline{T},j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}]
.
The first term on the right-hand side of (
B.3
) measures the truncation error of the conditional covariance at time
\underline{T}
, scaled by
\sigma_{t}^{-4}\mu_{t}^{2}
.
More precisely, for
i\neq j\in[D]
, we have

\displaystyle\Big|\text{Cov}\big[X_{\underline{T},i},X_{\underline{T},j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}\big]-\text{Cov}\big[X_{0,i},X_{0,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}\big]\Big|\lesssim\frac{\underline{T}}{t^{2}}\Big|\text{Cov}\big[X_{0,i},X_{0,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}\big]\Big|.

Combining this with Theorem
4.1
, the first term on the right-hand side of (
B.3
) is bounded above, up to a constant, by
\underline{T}/t^{2}
for
(i,j)\in E_{0}
and by
\underline{T}/t^{2-\tilde{\beta}/2}
for
(i,j)\notin E_{0}
with
i\neq j
.

The second term on the right-hand side of (
B.3
) measures the difference between the averaged conditional covariances of
\widehat{\bf X}_{\underline{T}}
given
\widehat{\bf X}_{t}
and
{\bf X}_{\underline{T}}
given
{\bf X}_{t}
.
Given
{\bf x}\in[-\mu_{t}\gamma,\mu_{t}\gamma]^{D}
, we first consider the pointwise difference.
For
s\in[\underline{T},t]
, let
\widehat{p}_{s\mid t}(\cdot\mid{\bf x})
and
p_{s\mid t}(\cdot\mid{\bf x})
denote the Lebesgue densities of the conditional random vectors
\widehat{\bf X}_{s}
given
\widehat{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}
and
{\bf X}_{s}
given
{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}
, respectively.
Then, based on well-known results
(
7
,
34
,
10
)
, the total variation distance between the solutions of the two SDEs can be bounded by controlling the difference between their drift terms, as

\displaystyle\Big\{d_{\rm TV}\big(p_{\underline{T}\mid t}(\cdot\mid{\bf x}),\widehat{p}_{\underline{T}\mid t}(\cdot\mid{\bf x})\big)\Big\}^{2}\leq\int_{\underline{T}}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{D}}\big\|\widehat{\bf f}({\bf z},s)-{\bf f}_{0}({\bf z},s)\big\|_{2}^{2}p_{s\mid t}({\bf z}\mid{\bf x}){\rm d}{\bf z}{\rm d}s.

This requires control of the
conditional

L^{2}
-error between
\widehat{\bf f}(\cdot,s)
and
{\bf f}_{0}(\cdot,s)
, where the expectation is taken with respect to the conditional distribution of
{\bf X}_{s}
given
{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}
for
s\in[\underline{T},t]
.
However, condition (
4.2
) only guarantees the
marginal

L^{2}
-error, where the expectation is taken with respect to the marginal distribution of
{\bf X}_{s}
for
s\in[\underline{T},t]
.
Note that
p_{t}({\bf x})\gtrsim 1
whenever
\|{\bf x}\|_{\infty}\leq\mu_{t}
, since

is bounded from below on its support; see Lemma 6 of

.
Combining this with Bayes’ rule yields

\displaystyle p_{s\mid t}({\bf z}\mid{\bf x})\lesssim\phi_{\sigma_{t-s}}({\bf x}-\mu_{t-s}{\bf z})\,p_{s}({\bf z}),

which in turn implies that
{\mathbb{E}}[p_{s\mid t}({\bf z}\mid{\bf U}_{t})]\lesssim p_{s}({\bf z})
.
Thus, after taking expectation with respect to
{\bf U}_{t}
, the conditional
L^{2}
-error can be controlled by the marginal

-error, yielding

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[d_{\rm TV}\big(p_{\underline{T}\mid t}(\cdot\mid{\bf U}_{t}),\widehat{p}_{\underline{T}\mid t}(\cdot\mid{\bf U}_{t})\big)\Big]\lesssim\epsilon_{n}.

The difference between the covariances can then be controlled by the total variation distance, provided that the tail probabilities of both densities
p_{\underline{T}\mid t}(\cdot\mid{\bf x})
and
\widehat{p}_{\underline{T}\mid t}(\cdot\mid{\bf x})
are sufficiently small.
Combining this with condition (
4.3
) and several basic lemmas based on Itô’s formula
(

, the tail bounds introduce an additional logarithmic factor

in the convergence rate.

B.2
Proof of Theorem
4.1

We begin by stating and proving a key lemma used in the proof of Theorem
4.1
.
This lemma shows that the truncation error of the Neumann series expansion can be controlled by the graph-induced sparsity pattern of the matrix.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
K>0
be given, and consider an undirected graph
G
with vertex set
\{1,\ldots,D\}
.
Suppose that a symmetric matrix
{\bf H}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D\times D}
satisfies
|H_{ij}|\leq K
for all
i,j\in[D]
and
H_{ij}=0
when
d_{G}(i,j)>1
.
Then, for every
0\leq\epsilon<(DK)^{-1}
,

\displaystyle\left|\left\{\left({\bf I}_{D}-\epsilon{\bf H}\right)^{-1}\right\}_{ij}-\epsilon^{d_{G}(i,j)}\left({\bf H}^{d_{G}(i,j)}\right)_{ij}\right|\leq\frac{(\epsilon DK)^{d_{G}(i,j)+1}}{1-\epsilon DK},\quad\forall i,j\in[D]\text{ with }d_{G}(i,j)<\infty,

and
\{({\bf I}_{D}-\epsilon{\bf H})^{-1}\}_{ij}=0
when
d_{G}(i,j)=\infty
.

Proof.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have
(\sum_{i=1}^{D}|x_{i}|)^{2}\leq D\|{\bf x}\|_{2}^{2}
for any
{\bf x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{D})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D}.

A simple calculation yields that

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{\top}{\bf H}{\bf x}=\sum_{i,j\in[D]}H_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}\leq K\sum_{i,j\in[D]}|x_{i}||x_{j}|=K\|{\bf x}\|_{1}^{2}\leq DK\|{\bf x}\|_{2}^{2}.

It follows that, for any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{\top}\left({\bf I}_{D}-\epsilon{\bf H}\right){\bf x}=\|{\bf x}\|_{2}^{2}-\epsilon\left(\sum_{i,j\in[D]}H_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}\right)\geq(1-\epsilon DK)\|{\bf x}\|_{2}^{2}.

Hence, for every
0\leq\epsilon<(DK)^{-1}
, the matrix
{\bf I}_{D}-\epsilon{\bf H}
is positive-definite.
Let

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}=\left({\bf I}_{D}-\epsilon{\bf H}\right)^{-1}.

Then, for every
N\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
, a simple calculation yields that

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}-\sum_{k=0}^{N}\epsilon^{k}{\bf H}^{k}=\epsilon^{N+1}\bm{\Sigma}{\bf H}^{N+1}

because
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\sum_{k=0}^{N}\epsilon^{k}{\bf H}^{k})=({\bf I}_{D}-\epsilon{\bf H})(\sum_{k=0}^{N}\epsilon^{k}{\bf H}^{k})={\bf I}_{D}-\epsilon^{N+1}{\bf H}^{N+1}.

For any
i,j\in[D]
,

\displaystyle\left|\left(\bm{\Sigma}{\bf H}^{N+1}\right)_{ij}\right|=\left|{\bf e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}{\bf H}^{N+1}{\bf e}_{j}\right|\leq\left\|{\bf e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}\left\|{\bf H}^{N+1}{\bf e}_{j}\right\|_{2}\leq\left|\mkern-3.0mu\left|\mkern-3.0mu\left|\bm{\Sigma}\right|\mkern-3.0mu\right|\mkern-3.0mu\right|_{2}\left|\mkern-3.0mu\left|\mkern-3.0mu\left|{\bf H}\right|\mkern-3.0mu\right|\mkern-3.0mu\right|_{2}^{N+1},

where
{\bf e}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{D}
denotes the standard basis vector whose
i
-th element is one and all other elements are zero.
Here,
|\mkern-3.0mu|\mkern-3.0mu|\cdot|\mkern-3.0mu|\mkern-3.0mu|_{2}
denotes the spectral norm (or
\ell^{2}
operator norm).
Since
{\bf x}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}{\bf x}\geq(1-\epsilon KD)\|{\bf x}\|_{2}^{2}
for any
{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
, the smallest eigenvalue of
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}
is larger than
1-\epsilon KD
; therefore,
|\mkern-3.0mu|\mkern-3.0mu|\bm{\Sigma}|\mkern-3.0mu|\mkern-3.0mu|_{2}\leq 1/(1-\epsilon KD)
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\left|\mkern-3.0mu\left|\mkern-3.0mu\left|{\bf H}\right|\mkern-3.0mu\right|\mkern-3.0mu\right|_{2}=\sup_{\|{\bf x}\|_{2}\leq 1}\|{\bf H}{\bf x}\|_{2}=\sup_{\|{\bf x}\|_{2}\leq 1}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{D}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{D}H_{ij}x_{j}\right)^{2}}\leq K\sup_{\|{\bf x}\|_{2}\leq 1}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{D}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{D}|x_{j}|\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq DK\sup_{\|{\bf x}\|_{2}\leq 1}\|{\bf x}\|_{2}=DK.

Combining the last three displays, we have

\left|\Sigma_{ij}-\sum_{k=0}^{N}\epsilon^{k}({\bf H}^{k})_{ij}\right|\leq\frac{(\epsilon DK)^{N+1}}{1-\epsilon DK}

(B.4)

for every
0\leq\epsilon<(DK)^{-1}
and
i,j\in[D]
.

Note that

\displaystyle({\bf H}^{k})_{ij}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{0},\ldots,i_{k}\in[D]\\
i_{0}=i,i_{k}=j\end{subarray}}\prod_{l=0}^{k-1}H_{i_{l}i_{l+1}}

for
k\geq 1
, and
({\bf H}^{0})_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
, where
\delta_{ij}
denotes the Kronecker delta.
For
i,j\in[D]
with
i\neq j
and
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
with
k<d_{G}(i,j)
, we have
({\bf H}^{k})_{ij}=0
.
The proof proceeds by contradiction.
Suppose that
({\bf H}^{k})_{ij}\neq 0
.
Then, there exists a sequence
(i^{*}_{0},\ldots,i^{*}_{k})
such that
\prod_{l=0}^{k-1}H_{i^{*}_{l}i^{*}_{l+1}}\neq 0
with
i_{0}^{*}=i
and
i_{k}^{*}=j
.
This implies the existence of a path of length
k
from vertex
i
to
j
, which contradicts the assumption
k<d_{G}(i,j)
.

For
i,j\in[D]
with
d_{G}(i,j)<\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{d_{G}(i,j)}\epsilon^{k}({\bf H}^{k})_{ij}=\epsilon^{d_{G}(i,j)}\left({\bf H}^{d_{G}(i,j)}\right)_{ij}.

Combining with (
B.4
), we have

\displaystyle\left|\Sigma_{ij}-\epsilon^{d_{G}(i,j)}\left({\bf H}^{d_{G}(i,j)}\right)_{ij}\right|\leq\frac{(\epsilon DK)^{d_{G}(i,j)+1}}{1-\epsilon DK}

for every
0\leq\epsilon<(DK)^{-1}
and
i,j\in[D]
with
d_{G}(i,j)<\infty
.
Moreover,
({\bf H}^{k})_{ij}=0
for any

Since

Proof of Theorem

.

Let

be the conditional density of

given

, given as

\displaystyle p_{0\mid t}({\bf x}_{0}\mid{\bf x})=p_{0}({\bf x}_{0})\exp\left(-\frac{\|{\bf x}-\mu_{t}{\bf x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)\left\{\int_{[-1,1]^{D}}p_{0}({\bf y})\exp\left(-\frac{\|{\bf x}-\mu_{t}{\bf y}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right){\rm d}{\bf y}\right\}^{-1}

for
{\bf x}_{0}\in[-1,1]^{D}
.
Throughout this proof, we fix

Let
u_{0}(\cdot)=\log p_{0}(\cdot)
and
{\bf Z}_{0}=(\mu_{t}{\bf X}_{0}-{\bf x})/\sigma_{t}
.
Then, the density of
{\bf Z}_{0}
(conditional on
{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}

\displaystyle p_{{\bf Z}_{0}}({\bf z})=\exp\left(u_{0}\left(\frac{{\bf x}+\sigma_{t}{\bf z}}{\mu_{t}}\right)-\frac{\|{\bf z}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\right)\left\{\int_{S}\exp\left(u_{0}\left(\frac{{\bf x}+\sigma_{t}{\bf y}}{\mu_{t}}\right)-\frac{\|{\bf y}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\right){\rm d}{\bf y}\right\}^{-1},\quad{\bf z}\in S,

where

.
Note also that

\displaystyle{\rm{Cov}}\big[X_{0,i},X_{0,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}\big]=\left(\frac{\sigma_{t}}{\mu_{t}}\right)^{2}\text{Cov}\big[Z_{0,i},Z_{0,j}\big],

where
{\bf Z}_{0}=(Z_{0,1},\ldots,Z_{0,D})
.

We approximate
u_{0}
by its second-order Taylor expansion.
Specifically, for every

,

u_{0}\left(\frac{{\bf x}+\sigma_{t}{\bf z}}{\mu_{t}}\right)=u_{0}\left(\frac{{\bf x}}{\mu_{t}}\right)+\frac{\sigma_{t}}{\mu_{t}}\left(\nabla u_{0}\left(\frac{{\bf x}}{\mu_{t}}\right)\right)^{\top}{\bf z}\quad+\left(\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{2\mu_{t}^{2}}\right){\bf z}^{\top}\nabla^{2}u_{0}\left(\frac{{\bf x}}{\mu_{t}}\right){\bf z}+r({\bf z}),

(B.5)

where

\displaystyle r({\bf z})=\left(\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\mu_{t}^{2}}\right)\sum_{\alpha.=2}\left\{\left({\rm D}^{\bm{\alpha}}u_{0}\right)\left(\frac{{\bf x}+\xi\sigma_{t}{\bf z}}{\mu_{t}}\right)-\left({\rm D}^{\bm{\alpha}}u_{0}\right)\left(\frac{{\bf x}}{\mu_{t}}\right)\right\}\frac{{\bf z}^{\bm{\alpha}}}{\bm{\alpha}!}

for a suitable

Here,
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{D})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{D}

{\bf z}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\prod_{k=1}^{D}z_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}
.
Since
u_{0}\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta,K}_{D}([-1,1]^{D})
, there exists a positive constant
D_{1}=D_{1}(D,K)
such that

|r({\bf z})|\leq D_{1}\left(\frac{\sigma_{t}}{\mu_{t}}\right)^{\beta\wedge 3}\|{\bf z}\|_{\infty}^{\beta\wedge 3}.

(B.6)

Let
{\bf H}=(H_{ij})=\nabla^{2}u_{0}({\bf x}/\mu_{t})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D\times D}
.
Since

, we have
|H_{ij}|\leq K
for all

.
Hence, for any

\displaystyle{\bf y}^{\top}{\bf H}{\bf y}=\sum_{i,j\in[D]}H_{ij}y_{i}y_{j}\leq\sum_{i,j\in[D]}K|y_{i}||y_{j}|=K\|{\bf y}\|_{1}^{2}\leq DK\|{\bf y}\|_{2}^{2},

where the last inequality holds by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.
This bound further implies that

(B.7)

where
\kappa_{t}=1-DK(\sigma_{t}/\mu_{t})^{2}
.
Let
D_{2}=D_{2}(D,K)
be a positive constant such that
(\sigma_{t}/\mu_{t})^{2}<(DK)^{-1}
for every
t\leq D_{2}
.
Then, for every
t\leq D_{2}

0<\kappa_{t}\leq 1

Combining this with Lemma
B.1
, for every

\left|\widetilde{\Sigma}_{ij}-\left(\frac{\sigma_{t}}{\mu_{t}}\right)^{2d(i,j)}\left({\bf H}^{d(i,j)}\right)_{ij}\right|\leq\frac{(1-\kappa_{t})(DK)^{d(i,j)}}{\kappa_{t}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{t}}{\mu_{t}}\right)^{2d(i,j)},\quad\text{if }d(i,j)<\infty,

(B.8)

and
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{ij}=0
if

, where

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}=(\widetilde{\Sigma}_{ij})=\left\{{\bf I}_{D}-\left(\frac{\sigma_{t}}{\mu_{t}}\right)^{2}{\bf H}\right\}^{-1}.

Motivated by the quadratic approximation of

in (

Accordingly, we decompose the covariance as follows:

We proceed by deriving upper and lower bounds for

,

For any

&\leq(2\mu_{t}\gamma)^{-D}\frac{\mu_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}\int_{S}\Big|\text{Cov}\big[X_{0,i},X_{0,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}\big]-\text{Cov}\big[X_{s,i},X_{s,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}\big]\Big|{\rm d}{\bf x},\end{split}

(B.22)

where the last inequality holds by the triangle inequality.
We proceed by bounding the difference between the two conditional covariance terms; see (
B.25
).

Fix
{\bf x}_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D},{\bf x}_{t}\in[-\mu_{t}\gamma,\mu_{t}\gamma]^{D}
, and
{\bf x}_{0}\in[-1,1]^{D}
.
A simple calculation yields that

\displaystyle p_{t\mid s,0}({\bf x}_{t}\mid{\bf x}_{s},{\bf x}_{0})=\frac{p_{t,s,0}({\bf x}_{t},{\bf x}_{s},{\bf x}_{0})}{p_{s,0}({\bf x}_{s},{\bf x}_{0})}=\frac{p_{s\mid t,0}({\bf x}_{s}\mid{\bf x}_{t},{\bf x}_{0})p_{t,0}({\bf x}_{t},{\bf x}_{0})}{p_{s,0}({\bf x}_{s},{\bf x}_{0})}=\frac{p_{s\mid t,0}({\bf x}_{s}\mid{\bf x}_{t},{\bf x}_{0})p_{t\mid 0}({\bf x}_{t}\mid{\bf x}_{0})}{p_{s\mid 0}({\bf x}_{s}\mid{\bf x}_{0})}.

Here,
p_{s,0}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the joint density of
({\bf X}_{s},{\bf X}_{0})
, and
p_{s\mid 0}(\cdot\mid{\bf x}_{0})
denotes the conditional density of
{\bf X}_{s}
given
{\bf X}_{0}={\bf x}_{0}
.
Since the process
({\bf X}_{u})_{u\geq 0}
is Markov, we have

\displaystyle p_{t\mid s,0}({\bf x}_{t}\mid{\bf x}_{s},{\bf x}_{0})=p_{t\mid s}({\bf x}_{t}\mid{\bf x}_{s}).

Combining the last two displays yields that

\displaystyle p_{s\mid t,0}({\bf x}_{s}\mid{\bf x}_{t},{\bf x}_{0})=\frac{p_{t\mid s}({\bf x}_{t}\mid{\bf x}_{s})p_{s\mid 0}({\bf x}_{s}\mid{\bf x}_{0})}{p_{t\mid 0}({\bf x}_{t}\mid{\bf x}_{0})}.

Note that the conditional distribution of
{\bf X}_{t}
given
{\bf X}_{s}={\bf x}_{s}
is Gaussian with
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t-s}{\bf x}_{s},\sigma_{t-s}^{2}{\bf I}_{D})
, and that the conditional distribution of
{\bf X}_{s}
given
{\bf X}_{0}={\bf x}_{0}
is
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{s}{\bf x}_{0},\sigma_{s}^{2}{\bf I}_{D})
.
A direct calculation yields that

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\|{\bf x}_{t}-\mu_{t-s}{\bf x}_{s}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma_{t-s}^{2}}-\frac{\|{\bf x}_{s}-\mu_{s}{\bf x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma_{s}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=C({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{t})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\left({\bf x}_{s}^{\top}{\bf x}_{s}\right)\left(\frac{\mu_{t-s}^{2}}{\sigma_{t-s}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{s}^{2}}\right)-2{\bf x}_{s}^{\top}\left(\frac{\mu_{t-s}{\bf x}_{t}}{\sigma_{t-s}^{2}}+\frac{\mu_{s}{\bf x}_{0}}{\sigma_{s}^{2}}\right)\right\}\right),

where
C({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{t})
is a constant depending only on
{\bf x}_{0}
and
{\bf x}_{t}
.
Recall that
\mu_{t}=e^{-t}
and
\sigma_{t}=\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}
.
Therefore, the coefficient of the quadratic term is

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{t-s}^{2}}{\sigma_{t-s}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{s}^{2}}=\frac{\mu_{t-s}^{2}\sigma_{s}^{2}+\sigma_{t-s}^{2}}{\sigma_{t-s}^{2}\sigma_{s}^{2}}=\frac{e^{-2t+2s}(1-e^{-2s})+1-e^{-2t+2s}}{\sigma_{t-s}^{2}\sigma_{s}^{2}}=\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{\sigma_{t-s}^{2}\sigma_{s}^{2}}=\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{t-s}^{2}\sigma_{s}^{2}}.

Hence, the conditional distribution of
{\bf X}_{s}
given
{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}
and
{\bf X}_{0}={\bf x}_{0}
is Gaussian with

\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{\sigma_{s}^{2}\mu_{t-s}{\bf x}_{t}+\sigma_{t-s}^{2}\mu_{s}{\bf x}_{0}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}},\frac{\sigma_{t-s}^{2}\sigma_{s}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\bf I}_{D}\right).

(B.23)

To derive the conditional covariance of
{\bf X}_{s}
given
{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}
, we apply the law of total covariance:

\displaystyle\text{Cov}\big[X_{s,i},X_{s,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\Big[\text{Cov}\big[X_{s,i},X_{s,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t},{\bf X}_{0}\big]\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad+\text{Cov}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}\big[X_{s,i}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t},{\bf X}_{0}\big],{\mathbb{E}}\big[X_{s,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t},{\bf X}_{0}\big]\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\Big].

By (
B.23
), we have

{\mathbb{E}}\big[X_{s,i}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t},{\bf X}_{0}={\bf x}_{0}\big]=\frac{\sigma_{s}^{2}\mu_{t-s}x_{t,i}+\sigma_{t-s}^{2}\mu_{s}x_{0,i}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}},\quad\forall i\in[D],

(B.24)

and

\displaystyle\text{Cov}\big[X_{s,i},X_{s,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t},{\bf X}_{0}={\bf x}_{0}\big]=\left(\frac{\sigma_{t-s}^{2}\sigma_{s}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)\delta_{ij},\quad\forall i,j\in[D],

where
{\bf x}_{t}=(x_{t,i}),{\bf x}_{0}=(x_{0,i})
, and
\delta_{ij}
denotes the Kronecker delta.
Combining the last three displays, we have

\displaystyle\text{Cov}\big[X_{s,i},X_{s,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]=\left(\frac{\sigma_{t-s}^{2}\sigma_{s}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)\delta_{ij}+\left(\frac{\sigma_{t-s}^{4}\mu_{s}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}\right)\text{Cov}\big[X_{0,i},X_{0,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big],\quad\forall i,j\in[D].

Hence, for all
i\neq j\in[D]
,

\displaystyle\Big|\text{Cov}\big[X_{s,i},X_{s,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]-\text{Cov}\big[X_{0,i},X_{0,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]\Big|=\Big|1-\frac{\sigma_{t-s}^{4}\mu_{s}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}\Big|\Big|\text{Cov}\big[X_{0,i},X_{0,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]\Big|.

A simple calculation yields that

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}^{4}-\sigma_{t-s}^{4}\mu_{s}^{2}

\displaystyle=1-2e^{-2t}+e^{-4t}-e^{-2s}(1-2e^{-2t+2s}+e^{-4t+4s})

\displaystyle=1-e^{-2s}+e^{-4t}-e^{-4t+2s}

\displaystyle=(1-e^{-4t+2s})(1-e^{-2s})=\sigma_{s}^{2}(1-e^{-4t+2s}).

Since
0<s\leq t
and
0<e^{-4t+2s}<1
, we have
|1-\sigma_{t}^{-4}\sigma_{t-s}^{4}\mu_{s}^{2}|\leq\sigma_{t}^{-4}\sigma_{s}^{2}
.
Therefore, for all
i\neq j\in[D]
,

\displaystyle\Big|\text{Cov}\big[X_{s,i},X_{s,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]-\text{Cov}\big[X_{0,i},X_{0,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]\Big|\leq\frac{\sigma_{s}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}\Big|\text{Cov}\big[X_{0,i},X_{0,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]\Big|.

Combining this with Theorem
4.1
, for all
i\neq j\in[D]
, we have

\Big|\text{Cov}\big[X_{s,i},X_{s,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]-\text{Cov}\big[X_{0,i},X_{0,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]\Big|\leq D_{1}\sigma_{s}^{2}\sigma_{t}^{(2d(i,j)-2)\wedge{\tilde{\beta}}},

(B.25)

where
D_{1}=D_{1}(C_{2})>0
.
Combining this with (
B.22
), and noting that
\mu_{t}^{2}\leq 1
, we have

\left|{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big|H_{ij}\big({\bf U}_{t},t\big)\big|\big]-{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big|\widetilde{H}_{ij}\big({\bf U}_{t},t\big)\big|\big]\right|\leq D_{1}\sigma_{s}^{2}\sigma_{t}^{(2d(i,j)-2)\wedge{\tilde{\beta}}-4},\quad\forall i\neq j\in[D].

We now focus on deriving (

For all
i\neq j\in[D]
, we have

\begin{split}&{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big|\widetilde{H}_{ij}\big({\bf U}_{t},t\big)\big|\big]-{\mathbb{E}}_{n}\big[\big|\widehat{H}_{ij}\big({\bf U}_{t},t\big)\big|\big]\right|\right]\\

where the last inequality holds by the triangle inequality.
We proceed by bounding the difference between the two conditional covariance terms.

By the triangle inequality, for all

\begin{split}&\Big|\text{Cov}\big[X_{s,i},X_{s,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]-\text{Cov}_{n}\big[\widehat{X}_{s,i},\widehat{X}_{s,j}\mid\widehat{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]\Big|\\

\begin{split}&\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\big[X_{s,i}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]{\mathbb{E}}\big[X_{s,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]-{\mathbb{E}}_{n}\big[\widehat{X}_{s,i}\mid\widehat{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]{\mathbb{E}}_{n}\big[\widehat{X}_{s,j}\mid\widehat{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]\Big|\\

(B.29)

We proceed by bounding the differences in the conditional first and second moments; see (

Let

(B.30)

Let
\widehat{p}_{s\mid t}(\cdot\mid{\bf x}_{t})
denote conditional the density of
\widehat{\bf X}_{s}
given
\widehat{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}
and the observations
{\bf X}^{1},\ldots,{\bf X}^{n}
.
A simple calculation yields that

\displaystyle\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\big[X_{s,i}X_{s,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]-{\mathbb{E}}_{n}\big[\widehat{X}_{s,i}\widehat{X}_{s,j}\mid\widehat{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{D}}y_{i}y_{j}\left\{p_{s\mid t}({\bf y}\mid{\bf x}_{t})-\widehat{p}_{s\mid t}({\bf y}\mid{\bf x}_{t})\right\}{\rm d}{\bf y}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{D}}\big|y_{i}y_{j}\big|\big|p_{s\mid t}({\bf y}\mid{\bf x}_{t})-\widehat{p}_{s\mid t}({\bf y}\mid{\bf x}_{t})\big|{\rm d}{\bf y}

\displaystyle\leq 2Ld_{\rm TV}\left(p_{s\mid t}(\cdot\mid{\bf x}_{t}),\widehat{p}_{s\mid t}(\cdot\mid{\bf x}_{t})\right)+\int_{|y_{i}y_{j}|>L}\big|y_{i}y_{j}\big|\left\{p_{s\mid t}({\bf y}\mid{\bf x}_{t})+\widehat{p}_{s\mid t}({\bf y}\mid{\bf x}_{t})\right\}{\rm d}{\bf y},\quad\forall i,j\in[D],

denotes the total variation distance between two probability density functions

and
q

{\mathbb{R}}^{D}

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\int_{|y_{i}y_{j}|>L}\big|y_{i}y_{j}\big|p_{s\mid t}({\bf y}\mid{\bf x}_{t}){\rm d}{\bf y}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{D}}\big|y_{i}y_{j}\big|1\big(|y_{i}y_{j}|>L\big)p_{s\mid t}({\bf y}\mid{\bf x}_{t}){\rm d}{\bf y}

\displaystyle\int_{|y_{i}y_{j}|>L}\big|y_{i}y_{j}\big|\widehat{p}_{s\mid t}({\bf y}\mid{\bf x}_{t}){\rm d}{\bf y}\leq\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}_{n}\big[\widehat{X}_{s,i}^{2}\widehat{X}_{s,j}^{2}\mid\widehat{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\big[|\widehat{X}_{s,i}\widehat{X}_{s,j}|>L\mid\widehat{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]},

where
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(\cdot)
denotes the conditional probability given the
n
observations
{\bf X}^{1},\ldots,{\bf X}^{n}
.

Since
\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:|xy|>a\}\subseteq\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:|x|>\sqrt{a}\}\cup\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:|y|>\sqrt{a}\}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|X_{s,i}X_{s,j}\big|>L\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\Big)\leq{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|X_{s,i}\big|>\sqrt{L}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\Big)+{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|X_{s,j}\big|>\sqrt{L}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\Big).

Combining the last three displays yields that for all
i,j\in[D]
, we have

\begin{split}&\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\big[X_{s,i}X_{s,j}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]-{\mathbb{E}}_{n}\big[\widehat{X}_{s,i}\widehat{X}_{s,j}\mid\widehat{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]\Big|\\
&\leq 2Ld_{\rm TV}\left(p_{s\mid t}(\cdot\mid{\bf x}_{t}),\widehat{p}_{s\mid t}(\cdot\mid{\bf x}_{t})\right)\\
&+\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}\big[X_{s,i}^{2}X_{s,j}^{2}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]\Big\{{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|X_{s,i}\big|>\sqrt{L}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\Big)+{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|X_{s,j}\big|>\sqrt{L}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\Big)\Big\}}\\
&\quad+\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}_{n}\big[\widehat{X}_{s,i}^{2}\widehat{X}_{s,j}^{2}\mid\widehat{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]\Big\{{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\Big(\big|\widehat{X}_{s,i}\big|>\sqrt{L}\mid\widehat{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\Big)+{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\Big(\big|\widehat{X}_{s,j}\big|>\sqrt{L}\mid\widehat{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\Big)\Big\}}.\end{split}

Similarly, for all
i\in[D]

\begin{split}&\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\big[X_{s,i}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]-{\mathbb{E}}_{n}\big[\widehat{X}_{s,i}\mid\widehat{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]\Big|\\

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[X_{s,i}^{2}X_{s,j}^{2}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]\leq 25,\quad\forall i\neq j\in[D],\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbb{E}}\big[X_{s,i}^{2}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]\leq 5,\quad\forall i\in[D].

\begin{split}&\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}\big[X_{s,i}^{2}X_{s,j}^{2}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\big]\Big\{{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|X_{s,i}\big|>\sqrt{L}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\Big)+{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|X_{s,j}\big|>\sqrt{L}\mid{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}_{t}\Big)\Big\}}\\

Appendix C
Implementation details of the proposed method

In this section, we provide practical implementation details of our method, complementing the description in Section
5
.

Recall that the forward process
({\bf X}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in Section
2.2
is the standard OU process (
2.3
).
Our main result (Theorem
4.3
) extends to more general time-inhomogeneous diffusion processes, including the forward processes underlying the DDPM
(
22
,
49
)
and EDM
(
24
)
frameworks.
In our implementation, we adopt the DDPM framework.
Specifically, the forward process is discretized as follows: for
\overline{T}\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
t\in\{1,\ldots,\overline{T}\}
,

\displaystyle{\bf X}_{t}\mid{\bf X}_{0}={\bf x}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t}{\bf x}_{0},\sigma_{t}^{2}{\bf I}_{D}),

where
\mu_{t}=\sqrt{\prod_{l=0}^{t-1}(1-\alpha_{l})}
,
\sigma_{t}=\sqrt{1-\mu_{t}^{2}}
, and
\alpha_{l}=\alpha_{\min}+l(\alpha_{\max}-\alpha_{\min})/(\overline{T}-1)
for
l\in\{0,1,\ldots,\overline{T}-1\}
.
We set
\alpha_{\min}=0.001
,
\alpha_{\max}=0.02
, and
\overline{T}=500
; the corresponding values of
\mu_{t}
and
\sigma_{t}
are displayed in Figure
A2
.

Within this DDPM framework, the score function is estimated by a suitable class of deep neural networks.
Since the consistency of the graph estimator holds for sufficiently small
t
by Theorem
4.3
, we only consider the timesteps
t_{1}<\cdots<t_{M}
such that
\sigma_{t_{i}}/\mu_{t_{i}}\leq 0.5
for
t_{i}\in\{2,\ldots,\overline{T}-1\}
, which corresponds to
M=30
as shown in Figure
A2
.
We exclude the timestep
t=1
because, in the DDPM implementation, Gaussian noise is typically not added at the last step of the reverse process, which makes the corresponding conditional covariance zero.
Although our graph estimation procedure only requires the score function at
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{M}\}
, we train it over the entire range
\{1,\ldots,\overline{T}\}
as in standard diffusion model training, which enables empirical evaluation of the score function estimator by sampling from the marginal distribution.
The simulation studies in Section
6
and the network analysis in Section
7.2
share the same deep neural network class, while the image data analysis in Section
7.1
uses a different one.
Detailed descriptions are provided in Sections
D.1
,
E.1
, and
E.2
, respectively.

Once the score function estimator is given, the values
{\mathbb{E}}_{n}[|\widehat{H}_{ij}({\bf U}_{t},t)|]
are approximated by Monte Carlo sampling with respect to a suitable random vector
{\bf U}_{t}
.
Specifically, for each
t
, we first draw
N_{1}
samples from the distribution of
{\bf U}_{t}
; then, conditioning on each such sample
{\bf x}
, we draw
N_{2}
samples from the conditional distribution of
\widehat{\bf X}_{\underline{T}}
given
\widehat{\bf X}_{t}={\bf x}
and compute the corresponding sample covariance.
In our experiments, we take the distribution of
{\bf U}_{t}
to be the empirical distribution of the perturbed observations
\mu_{t}{\bf X}^{i}+\sigma_{t}{\bf Z}^{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, where
{\bf Z}^{1},\ldots,{\bf Z}^{n}
are i.i.d. copies of
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{D},{\bf I}_{D})
.
We set
N_{1}=128
and
N_{2}=5000
; this sampling and the computation of the sample covariance can be performed efficiently on a GPU.

As discussed in Section
5
,
\widetilde{H}_{ij}(t)
is the standardized value of
{\mathbb{E}}_{n}[|\widehat{H}_{ij}({\bf U}_{t},t)|]
across all pairs
\{(i,j):i,j\in[D],\ i<j\}
for each
t\in\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{M}\}
.
Figure
A3
illustrates the effect of this standardization on the simple
3
-dimensional Gaussian example from Section
A.2
, for sample sizes
n\in\{10,20,50,500\}
.
Figure

shows that
{\mathbb{E}}_{n}[|\widehat{H}_{ij}({\bf U}_{t},t)|]
approaches
|H_{ij}(t)|
as
n
increases.
For very small
t
, however, the error remains large; in particular,
{\mathbb{E}}_{n}[|\widehat{H}_{13}({\bf U}_{t},t)|]
does not converge to zero as
t\to 0
, even though
H_{13}(0)=0
.

By contrast, Figure
3(b)
shows that
\widetilde{H}_{ij}(t)
approaches the corresponding standardized version of
|H_{ij}(t)|
.
The
30
-dimensional vectors
(\widetilde{H}_{ij}(t_{1}),\ldots,\widetilde{H}_{ij}(t_{30}))
cluster naturally into two groups,
\{(1,3)\}
and
\{(1,2),(2,3)\}
, even for moderate sample sizes (
n\geq 20
).
This observation motivates the clustering-based procedure applied to the standardized
30
-dimensional vectors.

Exploiting multiple values of

jointly, rather than committing to a single

As shown in Figure

Nevertheless, clustering on the

More generally, the value of

Varying

Non-Gaussian: Gaussian copula

D.2
Other baselines

We compare DDPM with several existing baselines, which we group into nonparametric methods and parametric (or semi-parametric) methods.
For the nonparametric baselines, we consider SING
(
4

and L-SING
(
37

, both briefly reviewed in Section

and accompanied by publicly available implementation code.
For the parametric and semi-parametric baselines, we consider the graphical lasso (GLASSO) and the nonparanormal (NPN), both also discussed in Section
A.1
.

The performance of SING depends heavily on the polynomial order
p
of the transport map.
Note that
p=1
corresponds to a well-specified model for the Gaussian distributions considered here, since the reference measure is standard normal.
Although
p=3
is taken in
4
when estimating the graph underlying the
10
-dimensional Butterfly distribution, SING with
p=3
does not scale to the dimension
D=20
used in our experiments; over seven hours were required for a single run, even at
n=300
.
We therefore report only the results for
p=1
and adopt the remaining choices, including the regularization parameters and the edge-selection thresholds, from the authors’ code; results for various
p
in lower-dimensional settings are also reported.

For L-SING, we likewise follow the implementation details provided in the accompanying code, in which the transport map between the reference measure and the conditional distribution is parameterized by structured neural networks.
We set the edge-selection threshold to
0.2
, which yields the best performance across the experiments reported in
37
.

For selecting the regularization parameter in GLASSO, we follow the publicly available code of
42
.
Specifically, for each candidate value of the regularization parameter, we first estimate the underlying conditional independence graph and then compute the unpenalized maximum likelihood estimator constrained to this graph.
The estimator with the lowest EBIC is selected, following the procedure of
15
.

For NPN, we first estimate the marginal transformation using the implementation of
39
provided in
65
.
We then apply the same GLASSO procedure described above to the transformed observations.

D.3
Performance measures

For each data distribution, we vary the training sample size over
n\in\{100,\allowbreak 200,\allowbreak 300,\allowbreak 400,\allowbreak 500,\allowbreak 1000,\allowbreak 2000,\allowbreak 5000,\allowbreak 10000,\allowbreak 20000,\allowbreak 50000\}
.
At each sample size, every method described above produces an estimated graph
\widehat{G}=([D],\widehat{E})
, which estimates the true conditional independence graph
G_{0}=([D],E_{0})
.
Recall that
G_{0}
is either
G_{\rm pair}
or
G_{\rm chain}
in our setting, depending on the data distribution.
To compare the methods, we measure the discrepancy between
\widehat{G}
and
G_{0}
through three metrics, the Hamming distance (HD), the true positive rate (TPR), and the false discovery rate (FDR), defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{HD}(G_{0},\widehat{G})=\frac{|E_{0}\setminus\widehat{E}|+|\widehat{E}\setminus E_{0}|}{2},\quad\mathrm{TPR}(G_{0},\widehat{G})=\frac{|\widehat{E}\cap E_{0}|}{|E_{0}|},\quad\mathrm{FDR}(G_{0},\widehat{G})=\frac{|\widehat{E}\setminus E_{0}|}{|\widehat{E}|}.

The factor of
2
in HD accounts for the inclusion of each undirected edge as both
(i,j)
and
(j,i)
in the edge sets.
When
\widehat{E}
is empty, we set
\mathrm{FDR}(G_{0},\widehat{G})=0
by convention.
Each experiment is repeated
10
times, and we report the average of each metric.

D.4
Performance results

The overall performance results are summarized in Figure
A5
.
DDPM and L-SING are the only methods that perfectly recover the graphs across all four data distributions, and DDPM is more sample-efficient than L-SING in every setting.
The two methods exhibit similar TPRs, but DDPM attains a substantially lower FDR across the full range of sample sizes.
This finding aligns with the optimality of diffusion models in learning distributions with sparse graphical structure
(
31
)
, which our methodology directly exploits.
We also experimented with various thresholds for L-SING, but DDPM consistently outperformed it across all values considered.

The parametric baselines GLASSO and NPN fail to recover the conditional independence graph of the Butterfly distribution, as also reported in the SING and L-SING analyses
(
4
,
37
)
.
On the Gaussian distributions, where GLASSO and NPN are correctly specified, they recover the true graph with
\mathrm{HD}<1
on average for
n\geq 100
in the high-correlation regime and for
n\geq 300
in the low-correlation regime.
On the Gaussian copula, NPN is correctly specified and likewise recovers the true graph with
\mathrm{HD}<1
on average for
n\geq 100
, while GLASSO is misspecified and fails to recover the graph.

Notably, DDPM is competitive with the corresponding correctly specified models in every setting, suggesting that DDPM can serve as a unified alternative to parametric methods across data distributions.
DDPM achieves
\mathrm{HD}<1
on average for
n\geq 100
in the high-correlation Gaussian setting and for
n\geq 400
in the low-correlation Gaussian setting.
For the Gaussian copula setting, DDPM attains
\mathrm{HD}<1
on average for
n\geq 1000
, which is slightly less sample-efficient than the correctly specified NPN model.
While DDPM tends to produce more false edges than NPN at smaller sample sizes, the residual false edges are not arbitrary.
As shown in Figure
1
, the values
\widetilde{H}_{ij}(t)
are closely aligned with the graph distance
d(i,j)

.
This implies that although DDPM does not perfectly recover the graph

For

shows that the gap between true and false edges widens further, which explains the consistency of DDPM observed for

Finally, we comment on SING.
Perfect graph recovery on the Butterfly distribution with polynomial order

is reported in

We also experimented with

At lower dimensions, SING with
p=3
perfectly recovers the graph for the Butterfly distribution at large sample sizes, but fails to recover the graph for the Gaussian copula; see Figure

By contrast, DDPM is consistent in both settings and is more sample-efficient than SING for every value of
