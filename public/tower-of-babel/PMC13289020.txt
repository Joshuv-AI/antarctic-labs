Validation of the Coercive-Composite Abuse Scale (C-CAS) in an Australian sample of women experiencing intimate partner abuse
Authors: Kelsey Hegarty, Cynthia Brown, Elizabeth McLindon, Kelly M FitzPatrick, Laura Tarzia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesCoercive control is a complex behavioural phenomenon where severity, frequency and patterns need to be examined collectively. However, approaches to measuring coercive control to date have demonstrated limited understanding of these patterns of behaviour by a partner or ex-partner that reflect an individual woman’s experience. The aim of this study was to develop a holistic contemporary valid self-report measure of coercive control by partners.DesignStandard instrument development, with academic and lived experience feedback and psychometric testing, using secondary data analysis, to develop the Coercive-Composite Abuse Scale (C-CAS).SettingAustralian survey of women: feedback from experts.ParticipantsData for scale development from 854 adult women in Australia, where items assessed 5-year and 12-month abuse frequency. Feedback from five survivors and eight expert researchers.Primary/secondary outcome measuresScale reliability and validity; robustness of subscales; association with fear of a partner.ResultsDeveloped from existing scales and informed by academic and lived-experience expertise, the C-CAS factor analysis revealed a four-factor structure accounting for 57% of variance – Psychological Control (13 items, α=0.928), Sexual and Reproductive Coercion (6 items, α=0.887), Severe Coercive Control (8 items, α=0.820) and Technology-Facilitated Threats (3 items, α=0.754), showing excellent internal consistency. Evidence of the scale’s face, content and construct validity was demonstrated, with further testing needed to confirm these findings. The factors combined into four key patterns- three of combined abuse and psychological control, which were experienced alone by some women. Our hypotheses asserting distinct patterns of abuse types for individuals, with severe combined patterns eliciting greater fear of a partner/ex-partner, were confirmed.ConclusionsThe C-CAS, a self-report measure of coercive control experiences among women, has demonstrated initial reliability and validity and is suitable for use in population or clinical studies. Additional validation with diverse samples is required.Keywords: Health Surveys, MENTAL HEALTH, Psychometrics, PUBLIC HEALTH

Introduction

Since the 1970s, feminist scholars have described coercive control as a pattern of behaviours in a relationship aimed at dominating and controlling another person, often used by men against their female partners.1,5 Coercive control can include physical and sexual violence, psychological abuse (deprivation of human rights, social isolation, intimidation, monitoring and harassment), threats to harm, financial abuse, reproductive coercion, mental health and substance abuse coercion and technology-facilitated abuse.4 Stark1 has argued that when coercion and control occur together, this abuse erodes autonomy, resulting in social entrapment. Even without physical or sexual violence, coercive control causes women to be fearful for their own safety and that of others close to them, including children, family members and pets.6 Coercive control is a prevalent form of intimate partner violence and has major effects on health.7 8

Johnson’s typology of violence9 10 differentiates between two main types of intimate partner violence: coercive controlling violence (or intimate terrorism) involving violent and non-violent tactics aimed at maintaining power over one’s partner and situational couple violence resulting from arguments or situations that escalate to physical acts that are not part of a larger motive to control one’s partner. While situational couple violence may be equally experienced by men and women, it is important to distinguish this type of partner violence from the pattern of coercive and controlling behaviour that is predominantly experienced by heterosexual women.11 Coercive controlling violence is more associated with fear, as well as death and significant harm, than isolated physical incidents of violence.4 However, coercive control is challenging to measure12 and has been conceptualised and operationalised in varied ways, resulting in a lack of generalisable prevalence findings.4

In policy, practice and research, coercive control is often taken to mean psychological abuse and measured separately from physical and sexual abuse.4 Many prevalence studies4 make bespoke measures of non-physical coercive control or use validated measures of psychological violence only; for example, Psychological Wife Maltreatment Inventory,13 Multidimensional Measure of Emotional Abuse,14 Women’s Experiences of Battering.15 Some authors specifically set out to measure control or coercion, but most of these scales are quite lengthy.16 These include Controlling Behaviours Scale,17 Coercion in Intimate Partner Relationships Scale,18 Checklist of Controlling Behaviours19 and the Coercive Control Measure.20 Shorter scales include the Psychological Abuse in Relationships scale (PARS)21 and the Coercive Control UK Scale,22 23 which also measures impact (fear, unable to leave, changes in routine, give up work or education and lose contact with children). While these psychological scales are useful for an in-depth understanding of this type of violence,21 they need to be used in combination with physical and sexual violence measures for a comprehensive definition of coercive control.21 Further, other forms of coercion, such as reproductive coercion and mental health and substance abuse coercion, are infrequently measured within these scales.4 12 24

A 2022 systematic review12 evaluated 87 measures of intimate partner violence, including screening tools, severity or high-risk measures, criminal recidivism measures, attitudinal measures and different behavioural types of violence. The review concluded that no existing measure could differentiate between intimate terrorism and situational couple violence.12 Scales attempting to measure a broad definition of physical, sexual and psychological forms of abuse included the Index of Spouse Abuse (30 items, Physical and Non-Physical dimensions), the Measure of Wife Abuse25 (60 items, Physical abuse, Verbal abuse, Psychological abuse and Sexual abuse) and the Abusive Behaviour Inventory26 (30 items, Psychological and Physical abuse). These have been criticised for neglecting many physically aggressive acts12 and ignoring some non-physical behaviours (eg, isolation from family) that typically occur in coercive control against women. Situational couple violence is often measured by the Revised Conflict Tactics Scale,27 with the authors themselves and other scholars28 29 suggesting it does not capture coercive control.

More than 20 years ago, the Composite Abuse Scale (CAS-30 items) was developed to measure the multidimensional patterns of coercive control and is scored for an individual’s holistic experiences of abuse.30 Four patterns (Severe Combined Abuse, Physical and Emotional Abuse/Harassment, Emotional Abuse/Harassment alone and Physical Abuse alone) reveal that physical and sexual abuse is usually combined with psychological abuse tactics that cause fear.31 Physical violence by male partners is rarely experienced by women survivors in isolation.31 A 15-item shorter version, CAS-SF,32 was developed in response to criticisms, including the need for a shorter scale in public health surveys, updated language (eg, replacing ‘rape’ with ‘forced sex’) and inclusion of technology-facilitated abuse. However, the shorter scale lacks the ability to generate nuanced patterns of coercive control that cause fear for survivors.

Development of a contemporary, well-validated, multidimensional measure of coercive control is the next logical step in furthering research on coercive control and intimate partner abuse (IPA). Use of the term IPA is deliberately employed to signal forms of intimate partner violence that are central to coercive control. This paper presents preliminary development and validation of a new revised CAS to measure coercive control (Coercive-CAS, C-CAS). Our hypotheses were that (1) An individual would experience distinct patterns of types of abuse, (2) These patterns would involve multiple types of abuse apart from psychological IPA which would also occur by itself and (3) When compared with single types of abuse, the combined patterns of abuse—particularly severe combinations of abuse—would elicit greater fear of a partner/ex-partner. Preliminary guidelines for scoring of the C-CAS are presented.

Method

Guided by standard scale development methods,33,35 this study involved input by five expert IPA researchers from the SAFE team at the University of Melbourne and nine individuals with coercive control lived experience, generating evidence of content and face validity. Factor analysis assessed internal consistency and construct validity based on hypotheses informed by previous research.30 The aim was to establish the structure, practical length and reliability of a new scale measuring coercive control in intimate relationships.33 36 37 The reporting of the study was informed by the STROBE guidelines for observational research.38

Development of survey items

A total of 130 items (online supplemental appendix 1) were sourced from other scales—CAS,30 Intimate Partner Sexual Violence Scale,39 PARS,21 Technology-facilitated Abuse in Relationships Scale (TAR)40 and Reproductive Coercion items.41 Nine lived-experience female IPA survivors reviewed the items for resonance with their experiences, after which several items were added and others modified.

Data collection

A nation-wide, English-speaking sample of women aged 18 and over residing in Australia was recruited to complete an online survey between February and April 2022. Recruitment was managed by iLink, a commercial research company, using a panel of individuals registered for research. Participation was voluntary, with advice that the survey addressed sensitive topics and would remain confidential. Informed consent was obtained, and participants received panel points (redeemable as vouchers) and were entered into a draw to win one of two iPads.

Prospective participants were screened for recent experiences of IPA. Eligibility required reporting that, within the past 5 years, a partner or ex-partner had engaged in one or more of the following behaviours: made them feel afraid; controlled or belittled them; isolated them from others; monitored, manipulated or harassed them; threatened harm to them or others; used physical violence (eg, hitting, slapping and kicking) or pressured them into unwanted sexual activity. Eligible participants completed an online survey covering experiences of IPA (including physical, psychological, sexual, reproductive and technology-facilitated), health, service use and demographics. For all 130 behaviours, participants reported their experiences with a partner/ex-partner (in relationships lasting longer than 1 month) within the last 5 years (yes/no). For each item endorsed, they were asked to indicate frequency over the past 12 months using a Likert-type scale: Not in the past 12 months, Once, A few times, Monthly, Weekly or Daily/almost daily. Those who had experienced at least one behaviour from the list of 130 behaviours over the past 12 months (n=854) were included in the analyses. Analytical methods were aligned with the non-probability sampling strategy, and no weighting or clustering adjustments were required.

The survey took approximately 30 min to complete and included optional guided self-care activities. The following instructions were undertaken to ensure the safety of participants: introductory information alerting participants that the survey contained questions about emotional well-being, including IPA; administration in a private setting and a list of support services was provided to all participants irrespective of IPA experiences.

Content validity

The authors and five expert IPA researchers reviewed these 130 items against Stark’s definition of coercive control using an iterative rating and consensus process over three meetings;1 66 items were identified as constituting coercive control (online supplemental appendix 1) and factor analysed.

Factor analysis

Using IBM SPSS V.29.0.0.0 for Windows, missing values and limitations were assessed,33 37 and common factor analysis (initially principal axis factoring (PAF) extraction, later in combination with promax rotation) was employed to identify the underlying dimensions of the 66 items.3742,44 A participant’s data was included in the analysis if they indicated having experienced any of the 66 behaviours at least once in the last 12 months. Assumptions of normality and linearity, multicollinearity and singularity were assessed across the 854 participants who reported on the 66 behaviours in the past 12 months.

Construct validity

Hypotheses about the patterned nature of the items and their association with fear of a partner or ex-partner were tested.

Face and content validity

The 30-item scale was reviewed by five IPA survivors and eight expert researchers. Post-analysis refinements were made to ensure consistency across the scale (see Online supplemental appendix 1 for original wording)—Forced, threatened or pressured me to get pregnant when I did not want to be was revised to remove its second-person tense.51 56 Parentheses were added to two items to enhance clarity50 53 Threatened to share my sexual orientation or gender identity publicly (eg, if I would not have sex with them) and Choked or strangled me (eg, during sex without consent). Finally, the item Threatened to distribute nude image(s) of me, originally loading on Factor 2, was reassigned to Factor 4 due to its clearer conceptual fit, improving the scale’s coherence and interpretability.52 57 Factor 1 was named Psychological Control (13 items), Factor 2 Sexual and Reproductive Coercion (6 items), Factor 3 Severe Coercive Control (8 items) and Factor 4 Technology-facilitated Threats (3 items). Taking approximately 5 min to complete, the scale was titled the C-CAS.

Scoring

The following suggestions for scoring were developed based on the author’s experiences of developing the CAS,30 PAR58 and TAR.40 There are four key steps in the C-CAS scoring and categorisation process: (a) Calculate item scores (0–5), (b) Calculate factor subscale scores, (c) Use cut points (Psychological Control ≥3, Sexual and Reproductive Coercion ≥1, Severe Coercive Control ≥1, Technology-Facilitated Threats ≥2 and (d) Categorisation of abuse pattern (see figure 1). Applying cut points enables the type of abuse experienced by each respondent to be categorised into a pattern. Using the four-factor subscales above, there are 15 possible combinations of abuse type that participants may experience (table 3). These 15 combinations can be reduced to four major patterns of abuse experienced by women (table 3). In this categorisation process, Severe Coercive Control takes precedence over other forms of abuse, that is, any participant who has experienced Severe Coercive Control with or without other types of abuse falls into this combined category (Pattern 1). The second pattern includes all participants who have experienced Sexual and Reproductive Coercion in combination with Psychological Control and/or Technology-facilitated Threats. The third pattern contains all participants who have experienced Technology-Facilitated Threats either by themselves or with Psychological Control. Participants who score on Psychological Control but no other forms of abuse fall into the final pattern. Full details of the C-CAS can be found at www.saferfamilies.org.au.

Construct validity

Our hypotheses of the patterns and their association with fear of partner/ex-partner in the last 12 months were confirmed (table 3). Apart from Psychological Control, the four factors occurred mostly in combination with other types of abuse. Second, fear was most commonly reported by participants experiencing combined abuse patterns: three-quarters (77.6%, n=243) in Severe Coercive Control combined pattern and two-thirds (67.6%, n=50) in Sexual and Reproductive Coercion combined pattern. Half of the participants (49.6%, n=136) in the Psychological Control alone pattern felt afraid of their partner/ex-partner in the last 12 months.

Discussion

This study provides the first empirical validation of a 30-item C-CAS designed to capture the multi-dimensional nature of coercive control in intimate partner relationships. Developed from existing scales and informed by academic and lived experience expertise, the C-CAS factor analysis revealed a four-factor structure—Psychological Control, Sexual and Reproductive Coercion, Severe Coercive Control and Technology-Facilitated Threats—accounting for 57% of variance and demonstrating excellent internal consistency (α=0.945). Evidence of the scale’s face, content and construct validity was demonstrated, with further testing needed to confirm these findings. The four factors combined into four key patterns of abuse, and our hypotheses asserting distinct patterns of abuse types for individuals, with severe combined patterns eliciting greater fear of a partner/ex-partner, were confirmed.

Regarding the factors, Psychological Control comprises 13 items reflecting classic markers of coercive dominance such as isolation, control, emotional and financial abuse. The high internal consistency demonstrates its coherence and aligns with previous findings from the PARS.21 The second factor, Sexual and Reproductive Coercion, includes six items that demonstrate strong reliability, capturing behaviours often not included in scales measuring IPA. The Severe Coercive Control factor groups together behaviours such as threats of lethal violence, deprivation of essential resources and weapon use. Contributing a modest portion of variance, this factor represents a lower-prevalence but theoretically and materially important dimension of coercive control.59 The Technology-Facilitated Threats factor, consisting of three items, achieved acceptable reliability.

These factors revealed four patterns of abuse, where in three patterns the factors combined together for an individual woman, with one pattern, Psychological Control, alone not combining with other factors for some women. There were only a few survivors who experienced Sexual and Reproductive Coercion alone or Technology-Facilitated Threats alone. As anticipated, fear was most common among women who experienced the Severe Coercive Control combined pattern, and least common among women reporting a single abuse type (ie, Psychological Control alone). These findings support our theoretical understanding of coercive control as a pattern of behaviours causing fear and provide further evidence of the limitations of measuring types of abuse in isolation.

Prior attempts to assess coercive control have focused narrowly on psychological abuse,13 failed to consider a comprehensive definition of coercive control60 or produced instruments impractical in length for large-scale surveys.25 The C-CAS bridges these gaps by integrating physical, sexual, psychological, reproductive, mental health and substance use coercion and technology-facilitated behaviours into a robustly developed, single, concise instrument that can be administered in approximately 5 min. Compared with the CAS30 and its short-form derivative,32 the C-CAS contributes two notable advances. The C-CAS adopts a pattern-based scoring system that aligns with current evidence positioning coercive control as a constellation of behaviours rather than isolated incidents.24 61 When IPA is measured in this way, it foregrounds women survivors’ lived experience of abuse, coercion and control. In contrast, measures of situational couple violence and research disaggregating abuse types (rather than synthesising them into coherent patterns) do not reflect women’s experiences. These approaches have impeded understanding and illustration of IPA’s gendered prevalence, dimensions and impacts.62 By aggregating related items into coherent patterns, the C-CAS more closely reflects the lived reality of survivors, facilitating more nuanced analyses of gendered risk factors, trajectories and long-term outcomes. Second, it measures a wide definition of coercive control incorporating technology-facilitated threats, reproductive, mental health and substance use coercion and financial abuse, acknowledging the increasingly common ways perpetrators exploit technology and various aspects of autonomy to humiliate, coerce, threaten, monitor or control survivors. These enhancements respond directly to gaps identified in a 2022 systematic review of IPV measures,12 which highlighted a paucity of tools that simultaneously capture the breadth of tactics while remaining feasible for population-level research.

Strengths and limitations

The study’s strengths include a large, geographically diverse Australian sample of 854 women survivors, enhancing the generalisability of findings, and a rigorous mixed-methods development process that incorporated survivor input, expert review and sequential factor-reduction steps. Recruitment through an online, English-speaking panel may under-represent survivors from diverse backgrounds, including those without internet access, which may limit the generalisability of findings. Further, social desirability bias may have led to under-reporting and influenced findings. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference regarding fear outcomes; further longitudinal investigation may clarify this relationship. As technology evolves, additional items—such as threats involving deep-fake technology—may necessitate ongoing iterative testing to maintain the currency of the C-CAS. Finally, we did not conduct confirmatory factor analysis due to insufficient sample size for a split-sample approach. Further research should undertake this psychometric validation.

Implications

To obtain better estimates of the prevalence and impact of coercive control, we need population research reflecting coercive control as a complex behavioural phenomenon where severity, frequency and patterns are examined collectively. Future research should include how these patterns occur, in which relationships and over time, similar to previous longitudinal research.63 Coercive control has been largely understood as a chronic social issue, characterised not by episodes of physical violence only, but by psychological abuse used by cisgender men to control their female partners.64 Further validation of C-CAS is needed, including across diverse samples (genders, language spoken) and qualitative cognitive testing with survivors.65 By foregrounding the interplay between psychological, sexual, reproductive, severe and technology-facilitated abuse, the C-CAS provides a psychometrically sound, pragmatic tool for capturing a fuller spectrum of coercive control in intimate relationships in future research.
