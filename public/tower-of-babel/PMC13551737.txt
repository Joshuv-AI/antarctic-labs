Anopheles gambiae biting behavior and malaria transmission risk in Simiw, a community located in the coastal forest zone of the Central Region of Ghana

Background

Despite significant public health interventions, malaria remains a major health concern in Africa [1]. The primary vectors responsible for malaria transmission in sub-Saharan Africa are Anopheles gambiae s.l. and An. funestus [2]. While studies in Ghana have reported sympatric populations of An. gambiae s.l. and An. funestus in certain areas, An. gambiae s.l. is generally the predominant species [3]. Within the An. gambiae s.l. complex, An. coluzzii and An. gambiae s.s. are the most efficient and prevalent species across all ecological zones [4–6]. An. funestus, the sole species of the An. funestus complex found in Ghana, is mainly observed in forest zones and some Sahel savanna regions [7].

Malaria transmission in sub-Saharan Africa, including Ghana, shows strong seasonality driven by rainfall and temperature pattern. Anopheles mosquito density and breeding increase during and soon after the rainy season when suitable habitats such as puddles and temporary pools are abundant [8–10]. In the dry season, vector populations and transmission rates decline due to fewer breeding sites and shorter mosquito survival [11, 12]. These seasonal fluctuations significantly influence malaria transmission dynamics and should be considered when planning surveillance and control interventions [13, 14].

The widespread implementation of long-lasting insecticide-treated nets (LLINs) and indoor residual spraying (IRS) has contributed to a significant reduction in global malaria morbidity and mortality rates [15]. However, these vector control methods have exerted selective pressure, leading to changes in vector behavior. In areas where IRS and LLINs are extensively used, many Anopheles mosquitoes previously known for indoor feeding and resting have shifted towards outdoor behavior [5, 16]. This adaptation has potentially compromised the effectiveness of major malaria interventions, thereby impeding malaria elimination efforts in many Sub-Saharan African countries [17].

In addition to these behavioral changes, widespread insecticide use has also driven the emergence and spread of resistance-associated mutations, particularly the knockdown resistance (kdr) mutations, including kdr-west (L1014F) and kdr-east (L1014S), which confer resistance to pyrethroids used in LLINs and IRS [18, 19]. These adaptations have collectively undermined the efficacy of current vector control interventions, posing a significant challenge to malaria elimination efforts across many Sub-Saharan African countries [20].

Traditionally, malaria vector biting activity has been known to start in the early evening, continue through the night, and extend into the early morning [21]. Biting typically peaks late in the evening when human activity decreases and people are asleep, declining in the early morning as daily activities resume [5, 22]. However, recent reports suggest a slight shift in this pattern, with an increase in Anopheles mosquito biting during early evening and early morning hours [22, 23].

Given that malaria transmission is closely linked to vector abundance, transmission capability, insecticide resistance, and biting behavior, understanding these factors in all malaria-endemic areas is crucial for effective malaria elimination strategies [2]. This study therefore aimed to investigate the vector abundance, biting behavior, Plasmodium parasite infectivity, and the presence of insecticide resistance mutations, particularly kdr-west (L1014F) and kdr-east (L1014S) among malaria vectors in the coastal forest zone of Ghana’s Central Region.

Methodology

Study site

The present study was carried out in the Simiw community (5°17′08.10" N, 1°33′75.53" W), a rural community situated in the Komenda-Edina-Eguafo-Abirem (KEEA) District located in the Coastal Forest of the Central region of Ghana (Fig. 1). This region experiences two rainy seasons, from April to June and from September to October, with an average annual rainfall of 730 mm and an average temperature of 27.6 °C. The relative humidity is generally high, ranging from 65% in the midafternoon to 95% at night. The rainfall pattern and landscape of Simiw facilitate the formation of stagnant waters, and the temperature and humidity create a favorable environment for mosquitoes to breed.

Map of Ghana and Central Region showing location of the study sites

Mosquito collections

Host-seeking adult mosquitoes were collected using human landing catches (HLC) during both the dry and rainy seasons of 2021. A total of four surveys were conducted: two in the dry season (February and November) and two in the rainy season (May and August), ensuring a representative sample from each season. To minimize selection bias and ensure representative sampling across the study site, the community was divided into four segments, and during each survey, three households were randomly selected from each segment for mosquito collection over four consecutive nights. In each house, two trained volunteers were stationed indoors and two outdoors to collect mosquitoes landing on their legs between 18:00 and 06:00, with collections recorded hourly. Different houses were selected each night to ensure a representative sample of the Anopheles vector population across the study site. The houses from the initial survey were revisited in subsequent surveys to allow for comparisons based on season and location.

Identification of mosquito species

Mosquitoes collected were identified morphologically under a stereomicroscope (Olympus, SZ60, Japan) using the identification keys developed by Maureen Coetzee [24]. A sub-sample of An. gambiae s.l. collected throughout the study period was selected randomly based on season and location (indoor or outdoor) for further identification of An. gambiae complex members by PCR and RFLP-PCR. The legs of each mosquito were used for DNA extraction, as described by Scott et al. [25]. Four sets of primers (An. gambiae, An. arabiensis, An. melas, and An. gambiae s.l. universal primer) were used in PCR to identify members of the An. gambiae s.l. species complex. An. gambiae s.s. and An. coluzzii were distinguished by PCR–RFLP using the method described by Fanello et al. [26].

Detection of insecticide resistance mutations

Genomic DNA extracted from the legs of the Anopheles mosquito samples was used to assess insecticide resistance mutations using a TaqMan SNP genotyping probe-based assay [27]. The targeted markers included the kdr-west (Vgsc-1014F) and kdr-east (Vgsc-1014S) mutations associated with pyrethroid resistance. The same set of samples were also genotyped for the Ace-1 (G119S) mutation, which confers resistance to organophosphates and carbamates.

Sporozoite detection

The heads and thoraces of mosquito samples were used to detect the presence of Plasmodium falciparum sporozoites using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) detecting the cytochrome oxidase I (COX-I) gene as described by Echeverry et al. [28] Samples selected for sporozoite detection were analyzed singly.

Data analysis

Descriptive analysis was conducted to compare the abundance of malaria vectors across different seasons, biting locations, and times. A generalized linear model (GLM) was employed to examine the effects of mosquito behavior, season, and sampling period on the abundance of Anopheles mosquitoes. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 15 software (StataCorp, 2017). The sporozoite infection rate (IR) was expressed as the proportion of mosquitoes positive for Plasmodium sporozoite, that is the number of sporozoite positive mosquitoes divided by total number of mosquitoes tested. Human Biting Rate (HBR) was calculated as the total number of mosquitoes collected via HLC per the total number of collectors per nights collected. The entomological inoculation rates (EIR) were calculated as the product of the human biting rate and the sporozoite rate and was expressed as the number of infective bites received per person per year (ib/p/y). The frequencies of the kdr L1014F and Ace 1 G119S mutations were determined using the formula: F(kdr) = (2RR + RS)/2n. Maps were downloaded from google and modified with Adobe Photoshop CS6 to show locations of study sites.

Ethical clearance

This study received scientific and ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Noguchi Memorial Institute for Medical Research (NMIMR), College of Health Sciences, University of Ghana. Verbal and written informed consent were obtained from opinion leaders of selected study sites, heads of households, and HLC collectors. To minimize the risk of mosquito bites, collectors were trained to catch mosquitoes before they could pierce their skin with their proboscis. Prior to mosquito collection, all volunteers were screened for malaria and provided with prophylactic medication to prevent infection. They were advised to report any malaria-like symptoms for immediate testing and treatment. Follow-up malaria testing was conducted 4 weeks after the collection period. Additionally, volunteers were recruited from within the study communities to provide support and ensure safety supervision throughout the night.

Results

Seasonal distribution and abundance of malaria vectors

Overall, 4,173 mosquitoes belonging to four different genera were collected through the sampling period; dry season (n = 2,230) [Anopheles = 1,673/2,230, Aedes = 17/2,230, Culex = 184/2,230, Mansonia = 356/2,230] and rainy reason (n = 1,943) [Anopheles = 1,591/1,943, Aedes = 37/1,943, Culex = 190/1,943, Mansonia = 125/1,943]. Surprisingly, higher abundance of mosquitoes was collected during the dry season than the rainy season (X2 = 0.193, P = 0.66). In total, 78.2% (n = 3,264/4,173) of mosquitoes collected were Anopheles, 1.3% (n = 54/4,173) Aedes, 8.9% (n = 374/4,173) Culex, and 11.5% (n = 481/4,173) Mansonia. Of the Anophelines were An. gambiae s.l. [98.7% (3,223/3,264)], An. funestus [0.2% (6/3,264)] and other Anophelines [1.1% (35/3,264)] (Table 1).

Seasonal distribution of mosquito species

Indoor and outdoor distribution of malaria vectors

Anopheles mosquitoes collected indoors were lightly more abundant than those collected outdoors in both seasons [dry season (indoor = 854/1,673; outdoor = 819/1,673), rainy season (indoor = 822/1,943; outdoor 769/1,943)]. On the contrary, other mosquito species collected (Aedes, Culex and Mansoni) were more abundant outdoors than indoors. An. funestus collected in this study were only found indoors during the rainy season (Table 1).

Species discrimination in the An. gambiae s.l.

Of the total An. gambiae s.l. collected, a subsample of 858 were selected for molecular species discrimination. Overall, An. coluzzii dominated with 79.0% (678/858), followed by An. gambiae s.s. 17.6% (151/858), hybrid of An. gambiae and An. coluzzii 1.9% (16/858) and An. melas 1.5% (13/858) (Table 1). There was no notable association between the abundance of An. gambiae s.l. species and the season or location (indoor and outdoor).

Biting times of An. gambiae s.l. in the study site

In this study, the indoor and outdoor biting patterns of An. gambiae s.l. exhibited distinct peak times. Indoor biting activity among malaria vectors was highest during the early morning (EM) hours, specifically between 04:00 and 05:00 h, accounting for 16.89% (278/1,646) of the total indoor bites, followed by 01:00–03:00 h at 12.58% (207/1,646). In contrast, the highest presence of An. gambiae s.l. outdoors was recorded between 22:00 and 04:00, corresponding to the late evening period, with slight density variations across different hours. Outdoor biting activity peaked, with the highest proportion occurring between 02:00 and 03:00 h (13.32%, 210/1,577), followed by 00:00–01:00 h (12.11%, 191/1,577).

Overall, biting activity of An. gambiae s.l. increased from the early evening (EE) through the late evening (LE) hours. Notably, biting activity began to decline after the early morning (EM) peak indoors (04:00–05:00 h) and the late evening (LE) peak outdoors (02:00–03:00 h).

The abundance of host-seeking An. gambiae s.l. was higher indoors (51.1%, 1,646/3,223) compared to outdoors (48.9%, 1,577/3,223) (Fig. 1). The mosquitoes exhibited a preference for biting during the early morning (B = 1.15, 95% CI 1.11–1.20, P = 0.000) and late evening (B = 1.00, 95% CI 0.97–1.05, P = 0.000) hours.

Insecticide resistance genotypes in An. gambiae s.l.

High frequencies of insecticide resistance mutations, Vgsc-1014F and G119S, were detected among An. gambiae s.l. populations (Table 2). The Vgsc-1014F mutation occurred most frequently across all species and in both indoor and outdoor collections. Its frequency was slightly higher in hybrids (1.0) and An. melas (1.0) than in An. gambiae s.s. (0.9) and An. coluzzii (0.9), indicating near fixation of this resistance allele in the population (Table 2).

Sporozoite infection rates and entomological transmission risk

Of the 1,659 An. gambiae s.l. tested, 13 were positive for Plasmodium falciparum cytochrome oxidase I (COX-1) [Dry (in = 9, out = 2), Rainy (in = 2, out = 0)]. Human biting rate (HBR) appeared slightly higher in the dry season, 17.27 mosquitoes/person/night (m/p/n) compared to the rainy season, 16.30 m/p/n. In both seasons indoor HBR was slightly higher than outdoor [Dry (indoor = 17.56 m/p/n, outdoor = 16.98 m/p/n), Rainy (indoor = 16.73 m/p/n, outdoor = 15.88 m/p/n).

The overall entomological inoculation rate (EIR) measured in this study is 52.69 infective bite per person per year (ib/p/yr). Higher EIR was measured during the dry season (76.73 ib/p/yr) than the rainy season (15.25ib/p/yr) (Table 3). Notably, sporozoite-infected mosquitoes were more abundant indoors than outdoors, with estimated EIRs of 78.05 ib/p/yr indoors compared with 13.93 ib/p/yr outdoors.

Discussion

Effective malaria control and elimination strategies rely on consistent monitoring and comprehension of local vector diversity and behavioural patterns [29]. Changes in the biting and resting patterns of malaria vectors [5, 16], coupled with rising insecticide resistance [30, 31] and the emergence of new vector species in Ghana [32, 33] highlight the need for further investigation into vector behaviour and diversity in different ecological zones and locations. This research examined the biting patterns of malaria vectors and evaluated key entomological transmission risk factors in Simiw, a community within the coastal forest of Ghana's Central region. The study revealed that indoor and outdoor malaria vector biting activities reached their peak during early morning and late evening hours respectively, with slightly higher abundance of host-seeking An. gambiae s.l. recorded indoors than outdoors.

Generally, mosquito populations, particularly Anopheles species, tend to increase during the rainy season when the availability of larval habitats such as puddles and temporary pools is high [12–14, 34, 35]. However, contrary to this expectation, the present study recorded higher mosquito densities, including Anopheles gambiae s.l., during the dry season. The Simiw community is geographically divided, with one part situated on highland terrain and the other in a lowland area near the Kakum River. The lowland area remains wet throughout the year, providing permanent breeding habitats, which could explain this observation. This finding diverges from numerous studies conducted in Nigeria, southern Mozambique [36] southern Tanzania [37] and various locations in Ghana [5, 6, 11], where malaria vector populations were more abundant in the rainy season than the dry season. However, it aligns with observations made by Forson et al. [6] in their Konongo (Ghana) study site, where they recorded higher abundance of An. gambiae s.l. in the dry season as compared to the rainy season.

This study found higher indoor biting densities of An. gambiae s.l. compared to outdoor biting. While high outdoor biting densities might be expected due to malaria vector control interventions such as long-lasting insecticidal nets and indoor insecticides [38], the rise in insecticide resistance across West Africa, including Ghana, against almost all insecticides employed in their control allows host-seeking Anopheles mosquitoes to withstand these interventions. Consequently, they may seek blood meals indoors, where hosts are more readily available, potentially increasing indoor biting densities over time [39]. These findings corroborate studies by Degefa et al. [40] in western Kenya and Akuoko et al. [5] in Ghana, both of which reported higher indoor biting densities. However, these contrast with results from Kabbale et al. [41] in Uganda's Kamuli district, where outdoor biting densities were higher.

Furthermore, indoor An. gambiae s.l. mosquitoes displayed a clear peak in biting activity during early morning (EM) hours (04:00–05:00), while outdoor An. gambiae s.l. mosquitoes had their peak activity in the late evening (LE) hours (02:00–03:00) (Fig. 2). Traditionally, the late evening period (LE), often referred to as the classical biting time, has been recognized as the peak biting time for An. gambiae s.l. mosquitoes, as observed outdoors in this study. The observed early morning (EM) peak in biting activity among indoor mosquitoes could be attributed to the widespread use of insecticide-treated nets, which act as a barrier for host-seeking mosquitoes during the night. Consequently, indoor mosquitoes may only gain access to humans in the early morning when people emerge from their nets to begin their day. This contrasts with the findings of Akuoko et al. [5] who reported a peak biting period in the late evening (LE), around 02:00, for both indoor and outdoor mosquitoes. Biting patterns are shaped by several factors, including the availability of blood sources [42], implementation of control measures such as indoor residual spraying (IRS) and long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs) [5, 16], and local human behavioral patterns [43]. This variation in biting times complicates malaria control efforts.

This research identified a diverse array of malaria vectors, including An. coluzzii, An. gambiae s.s., An. melas, An. funestus, and hybrids of An. gambiae s.s. and An. coluzzii. Among these, An. coluzzii was the dominant species, constituting 79% of all identified specimens. The extensive vector diversity observed surpasses that reported in other studies in Ghana [16, 44, 45], suggesting potentially enhanced vectorial capacity in the study area. Previous research in Ghana's coastal regions typically identified fewer vector species. For instance, Akuoko et al. [5] found only three primary malaria vectors, while Kudom [44] in Cape Coast also in the central region of Ghana identified three species, with An. coluzzii similarly predominating.

Genotypic analysis detected two resistance mutations among An. gambiae s.l., L1014F (kdr-West) and G119S (Ace-1). The L1014F mutation, conferring resistance to pyrethroids and DDT, was highly prevalent across all species: An. coluzzii (0.9), An. gambiae s.s. (0.9), An. melas (1.0), and hybrids (1.0), indicating near fixation and possible gene flow maintaining this allele. The L1014S (kdr-East) mutation was absent in all species, suggesting it is not yet established in the area. The G119S mutation, conferring resistance to organophosphates and carbamates, occurred at moderate frequencies in An. coluzzii (0.2), An. gambiae s.s. (0.4), An. melas (0.2), and hybrids (0.3). Similarly, Akuoko et al. [5] reported moderately high frequencies of L1014F and G119S in An. gambiae s.s. and An. coluzzii, although the values were lower than those observed in this study. Forson et al. [16] also reported comparable patterns among outdoor-resting An. gambiae s.s. and An. coluzzii.

Key entomological indices used to assess malaria transmission risk, including the human biting rate (HBR) and entomological inoculation rate (EIR), demonstrated pronounced spatial and seasonal heterogeneity in transmission intensity within the study area. Although HBR was only slightly higher during the dry season than the rainy season, indoor HBR consistently exceeded outdoor HBR across both seasons, indicating sustained and preferential human–vector contact indoors throughout the year. Notably, sporozoite-positive An. gambiae s.l. mosquitoes were disproportionately concentrated indoors, resulting in a substantially higher indoor EIR (78.05 ib/p/yr) compared with outdoor transmission (13.93 ib/p/yr). The overall annual EIR was estimated at 52.69 infective bites per person per year, with transmission intensity markedly higher during the dry season than the rainy season. These findings highlight the dominant contribution of indoor transmission to malaria risk in the community, despite the presence of vector control interventions. While studies from Ethiopia have reported higher outdoor HBRs [46], others, including Bradley et al. [47] in Equatorial Guinea, similarly observed greater indoor biting rates. Consistent with our findings, a study conducted in Mali reported indoor EIRs that were approximately three times higher than outdoor EIRs, underscoring the continued importance of indoor transmission in sustaining malaria burden in endemic settings [48].

Limitations of the study

This study inferred malaria transmission risk from entomological indicators (EIR, HBR, and SIR) without incorporating clinical or parasitological data, limiting the link between vector indices and actual disease burden. Data on LLIN ownership, usage, and IRS coverage in Simiw were unavailable, hindering assessment of intervention pressure on mosquito behavior and resistance. Additionally, mosquito sampling was limited to four surveys in 2021, which may not capture interannual variations in vector abundance and biting behavior influenced by climatic changes.

Conclusions and recommendation

This study found a notably high abundance of An. gambiae s.l. during the dry season, with increased indoor biting density observed in both the dry and rainy seasons. Several malaria vector species, An. coluzzii, An. gambiae s.s., An. melas, An. funestus, and hybrids of An. gambiae s.s. and An. coluzzii were identified. An. gambiae s.l. mosquitoes exhibited peak biting indoors in the early morning (04:00–05:00) and outdoors in the late evening. Two insecticide resistance mutations were detected, L1014F (kdr-west) and G119S (ace-1), with L1014F being more common. The Human Biting Rate (HBR) was higher during the dry season, with greater activity indoors than outdoors.

Given the high indoor biting activity and widespread insecticide resistance, reliance on indoor interventions alone may be inadequate. Integrated vector management combining environmental control, larval source reduction, and new-generation tools such as dual-active nets or spatial repellents should be adopted, alongside continuous surveillance to guide adaptive malaria control.
