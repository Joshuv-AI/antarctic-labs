Overview of Thyristors
 A thyristor is a solid-state semiconductor switch made up of four alternating p and n type
layers. When the switch is turned on by a small pulse, current can flow as long as the device stays
on. When the voltage drops below the turn on level or the current reverses, the switch will turn off.
Thyristors only allow current to flow in one direction. One of the benefits of a thyristor device is in its
capacity to conduct large voltages with a small device and a small turn-on pulse requirement.
Another distinguishing feature of thyristors compared to other semiconductor devices is that it can
only be in the on or off state, there’s no in-between state for the device to exist within. These
properties make thyristors very useful as switch devices, especially in applications where current
flow is only needed in one direction and could potentially be damaging in the opposite direction.
Currently, thyristors are most commonly used to control very high power loads and they are
available commercially in many designs to control different amounts of current.

 The four-layer alternating structure seen in Figure 1 creates 3 junctions between the n and p
type layers. These junctions are what block current from flowing until certain conditions are met. In
this device, the anode is considered the ‘top’ p-type layer in the material stack and the cathode is
the ‘bottom’ n-type layer in the stack. The gate is connected to the p-layer above the cathode. The
anode, cathode, and gate need to all be connected properly for current to be able to flow. The
cathode and anode need to be connected to the negative and positive terminals respectively, but
this along will not allow current flow. Junctions 1 and 3 from Figure 1 will be forward biased but
junction 2 will remain reverse biased until the gate received a positive voltage. The positive gate
voltage breaks down the depletion region at junction 2 which turns the device fully on and allows
current to flow. Once current is flowing, the gate can be disconnected without the device turning
off. Current will continue to flow until it drops below the holding or latching current, at which time
the junction 2 depletion region re-forms.

 Operationally, a thyristor is turned to the on state when a small gate current is applied as a
pulse. This allows large amounts of current to begin flowing from the anode to the cathode. The
thyristor will remain in the on state until the current drops below a threshold, called the latching or
holding current, at which point the thyristor will switch off and current will no longer flow. When the
thyristor is switched off, the electrons and holes that were conducting in the on state will
recombine. The device can be activated again once this recombination is complete. A typical IV
curve can be seen in Figure 2 below.

 Commonly, thyristor fabrication involves epitaxial growth of one layer at a time to create the
alternating n and p regions. Our approach will evaluate both epitaxially grown thyristor structures
and ion implanted thyristor structures. 2 different sets of silicon wafers were procured for
fabrication of thyristors. Within each set, there are 3 distinct doping concentrations for a total of 6
different wafer types. 1 set of wafers has 4 alternating n and p layers grown epitaxially and will be
used to evaluate the epitaxial thyristor structure. The second set of wafers has a p layer epitaxially
grown on an n-base. This set will be the base for thyristor fabrication with ion implantation. By
starting with these different wafer epitaxy geometries, we should be able to compare the implanted
and grown devices directly.

 The ion implant into the n-type base wafers will create the 4-layer alternating p and n
regions. This structure can be seen in Figure 3 below. Additionally, the p+ and n+ implanted regions
need to be aligned from the top side and back side of the device. P+ implant on the top side need to
align with n+ implant from the backside and vice versa to create the vertical alternating n-p-n-p
structure, also shown in the device schematic below. Ohmic metal contacts will create the anode
and cathode, and edge bevel will reduce the forward breakdown voltage, allowing device operation
at higher currents.

 Along with the vertical thyristor regions, this structure will also create vertical diode
sections that will be alternating with the thyristor sections. This structure can be seen in Figure 3.
Previously published work on thyristors with this similar structure showed improved switching
ability of thyristors with this alternating structure (https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0015284). This
structure will be used in this work to increase the efficiency of the devices and reduce the switching
time.

Evaluation of 1-dimensional epitaxially grown thyristors

 Our first task in this effort was to build a 1-dimensional thyristor from wafers that had
epitaxially grown alternating n and p regions. 3 sets of wafers with differing dopant levels were
acquired and the structure of these wafers is shown in Figure 4. Since the p-n-p-n structure was
already in place, all that was needed to fabricate a thyristor was metal contacts to create the anode
and cathode. Chrome-gold contacts were blanket deposited on both sides of the wafer using
sputter deposition. The deposition included a sputter clean of the wafers in-situ before any metal
was deposited. After metal, the wafers were diced to the size and shape of the final devices. First,
the samples were diced into squares. The squares were then further diced into circles of multiple
diameters. A sidewall etch was done with HNA to smooth any damage that occurred on the device
edges during dicing. Finally, a dielectric, silicone-based gel was applied to the edges and the
devices were cured and tested. Figure 5 shows the 1D thyristor devices after dicing, before edge
coating.

 Electrical measurements taken on the 1-dimensional thyristors can be seen in Figure 6.
Devices were made from 3 wafers with different n and p doping concentrations in the 4 layer
epitaxial structure. The measurements show that there is a large amount of variation between
devices from the same wafer, as well as no clear trend between the wafers that would indicate an
optimal concentration. The variation between devices from the same wafer is likely attributed to the
edge treatment of the devices. After dicing, the edges of the silicon are very chipped.

 To singulate these devices from the wafer they were processed on, multiple dicing steps are
completed. First, the circular devices are singulated as squares with one device per square. Next,
circles are individually diced out of each square die. All dicing is done with resin blades containing
diamond grit and the dicing is completed by chipping away at the silicon. This leaves very rough and
chipped side walls for the devices which can lower the breakdown voltage of the device. Uneven
side walls can short the device during testing and greatly lower the performance. Mitigation
strategies for this issue will be discussed in a later section.

Design and validate ion implantation schemes

 Because this thyristor design depends on ion implanted p-n diodes, verification and
validation of the implant process is a crucial step in the process development. It was first
necessary to determine the required implant doses needed to get the desired implant
concentrations at the desired implantation depth. To begin, Stopping and Range of Ions in Matter
(SRIM) simulations were run to determine how our desired implant ions, boron (p+) and

 7

phosphorous (n+), travel through silicon, seen in Figure 7. Simulations were run at implant energies
between 5 and 180keV and the resultant data was used in box implant models.

 (A) (B)

 Figure 7: SRIM simulation of target dopants moving through silicon at 180 keV
 (A) Boron (p-type); (B) Phosphorous (n-type); boron is a smaller and lighter atom than
 phosphorous and therefore has a deeper penetration depth at the same incident energy. These
 plots suggest that a boron implant and phosphorous implant on the same region should have
 boron as the carrier as it should fully compensate the phosphorous implant.

 Data from the SRIM models was input into an excel spreadsheet that calculated the
necessary doses to create a box profile at the desired concentration and depth. The box profile is
the sum of implants at multiple energies and doses, and they add up to a desired concentration
that reaches a desired depth into the silicon. For this application, we wanted a dopant
concentration of around 1E20 cm-3 for each ion, and we wanted the implant as deep into the silicon
as possible. As the SRIM models show, boron can reach deeper into silicon than phosphorous, and
this is reflected in the box profiles shown in Figure 8.

 Another consideration in the ion implant process is the masking material for implants. The
implant mask material needs to be able to stop our maximum dose of ions to leave un-implanted
regions and allow patterning for the implant. SiO2 is commonly used as an implant mask and was
compared to various options to evaluate how deep ions would travel. Metal is shown to stop ions
very well in Figure 9, with most metal implant masks only needing a thickness of around 500 nm to
be effective at stopping ion implants. Figure 9 also shows that SiO2 needs a thickness around 1000
nm to be effective as an implant mask. Another option that was explored was using thick
photoresist as a mask. This would simplify the fabrication of the devices as only 1 fabrication step
(lithography) would be needed compared to 3 steps for oxide (deposition, lithography, etching).
Additionally, we were having some consistency problems with the PCVD tool used to deposit the

 9

oxide and the tool was down for multiple weeks. Using photoresist as a mask would allow us to
continue processing without worrying about the status of the PCVD tool. After speaking with the
vendor who completes the ion implant process for us, it became clear that with our higher implant
concentrations we were seeking, photoresist would not be a viable option. In order to keep the
photoresist un-damaged, implants would need to be longer and at lower implant doses. While this
might work, the cost would be significantly higher, and we could not guarantee that the mask would
stay undamaged enough to be effective.

 After all the options were considered, SiO2 as an implant mask was clearly the best option,
even with the tool being down. While the mask needed to be thicker the alternative options,
deposition of SiO2 is much quicker. Including tool pump down time, 1000 nm of SiO2 can be
deposited in less than an hour while metal depositions would take a couple hours. Additionally,
removing the SiO2 mask is much easier than removing a metal mask. SiO2 etched very quickly in
BOE 6:1 while metal etchants take longer and we have often seen them leave some sort of residue
on the parts.

 As previously mentioned, our PCVD tool was not operational when it was needed for this
fabrication. As an alternative to PCVD deposition, we attempted to deposit oxide with a low
pressure chemical vapor deposition (LPCVD) furnace equipped with tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS)
gas. This process would be a good option for oxide deposition because it is done at lower
temperatures, up to 850C instead of over 1000C. the lower temperature process is ideal to avoid
prematurely activating the ion implant. This furnace is newer and unfortunately we had not run this
process before. Test runs were completed and we struggled to get an oxide layer thicker than
1000um. We didn’t feel confident in this process and decided that it was not usable for this
fabrication without extensive characterization that we didn’t have time for. Ultimately, we were able
to use the cleanroom at Stanford to deposit PCVD oxide on our wafers.

 To validate the ion implant process and make sure the dopant concentrations reach the
required levels, test implants were completed based on the simulations. Initially, phosphorous and
boron implants were done independently and sent out to be profiled with spreading resistance
measurements. As seen in Figure 10, the oxide mask was sufficient for blocking implant. The
doping in the regions that were masked was at the level of the base wafer, and the n+ and p+
concentrations in the implanted regions matched the desired level. Various activation anneals were
also done on the samples and are shown below in Figure 10. These were all completed in a rapid
thermal annealer under Argon with anneal temperature and anneal time as the variables. In the
fabrication process for full devices, one activation anneal will be used to activate both implants so
we looked at the activation data from both the n and p implants to choose the best option. Of all the
tested options, 900C for 10 minutes was chosen based on the results. While a longer anneal could
drive the implants deeper into the silicon, we want to ensure that the p implant will be significantly
deeper than the n implant so the net doping will be p where both have been implanted.

Develop edge treatment of fabricated thyristors to increase
device breakdown voltage

 Development of the edge treatment for the final devices is critical for the eventual
functionality of the device as discussed previously. There are 2 steps after the bulk of the
fabrication is complete and the individual devices are singulated that improve the performance of
the device. First the devices are wet etched to mitigate the dicing damage. The edges of the device
are then protected with the previously mentioned dielectric silicone-based gel. These steps
decrease sidewall damage and increase sidewall uniformity to improve device performance.

 Similar devices that were previously fabricated in our group use an HNA etch (110:70:40 mL
Acetic acid : Nitric acid : Hydrofluoric acid) to smooth chipping and roughness that occurs from the
dicing process. While the HNA etch works well to smooth edge chipping, there is still room for
improvement. We wanted to test other etches that are reported to smooth chipping in silicon and
determine if HNA is the best option or if another etch can provide better smoothing. Silicon etch,
NH4F:H2O:HNO3, 5ml:60ml:126ml, was tested as an alternative to HNA. These etches have very
different etch rates; HNA etch is much faster, while Si etch is much slower. As seen in Figure 12,
both etches smoothed the diced surfaces but a 10 min HNA etch resulted in much smoother
surfaces. However, HNA etch has been seen to be inconsistent and volatile with etching results
varying considerably between devices. There is also worry that the HNA etch could be etching the
contact metal on the devices and damaging them more than helping. We attempted to use
photoresist as a protective layer on the metal but the resist etched and came off the metal surface
after only a few minutes. Further tests will be conducted on the etched parts to explore these initial
results and see if longer HNA etches will improve or degrade device performance.

 Additionally, tests are being run to explore the feasibility of switching from a dice and wet
etch process to a dry etch only for device singulation. Reactive ion etch (RIE) and deep reactive ion
etch (DRIE) will be tested and evaluated for efficacy in this process. Hopefully these etches will
provide a faster singulation process with less edge damage than dicing.

 This device will ideally have a beveled edge profile which will lower the electric field on the
edge of the device, increasing the breakdown voltage by forcing breakdown to occur towards the
interior of the device where the breakdown can be significantly higher and where the electric field
will be much more uniform. So far, only one method has been attempted to achieve the desired
edge bevel. Special dicing blades with manufactured angles were tested and the dice profiles can
be seen in Figure 13, Creating a bevel by dicing is not ideal because we would be limited in angle by
what blades are available. It would also add a step to our dicing singulation process which already
is slow. We are exploring creating the edge bevel with a wet or dry etch currently. If it works, this
would be a tunable bevel angle and would hopefully leave a smoother surface.

Fabricate 2-Dimensional thyristor devices

 Developing the fabrication process for the full device has been challenging, especially since
there are multiple fabrication steps that will need to be completed by external vendors. In laying out
the process flow, consideration was taken in reducing the time that wafers were away with outside
vendors and minimizing extraneous steps. One of the steps we were able to remove for efficiency
was patterning the wafer for n+ implant. Based on box implant simulations, implanting p+ in a
region that was already implanted n+ will result in a p+ implanted region. The p+ implant will more
than compensate for the previously implanted n+. This was previously shown in Figure 11 but can
be seen more clearly in Figure 14.

 18

 Figure 14: Box profile of n+, p+, and double implants

 All the process steps previously discussed in this paper will have been used in the
fabrication of the full thyristor devices. Specially implanted wafers were thinned with two different
polish schemes. One set of wafers is being thinned from the backside (n-base) to a final thickness
of 360um. The second set is being thinned to a thickness of 410um from the backside (n-base) and
50um from the frontside (p-base) to a final thickness of 360um. These parameters were determined
from the thicknesses of the doped regions in the wafers. Our process steps will begin with the
thinned 8 inch wafers and they will first be diced into 2.8 in squares. The external vendor that does
the ion implants has size restrictions in the implant tool and cannot accommodate an 8 in wafer.
Additionally, most of the tools in our cleanroom are configured for 4 in or smaller wafers so the 2.8
in squares will be better for processing.

 We decided to process both single and double sided devices in the first run. The difference
between these can be seen in Figure 15. The primary difference is that the single sided devices do
not have patterned shunts on the backside and instead are just blanket p-type under the metal
contact.

 After initial dicing of the wafers into 2.8in squares they were sent out for a blanket
phosphorous (n-type) ion implant on both sides. When the wafers were returned, they were
patterned and the silicon was etched with reactive ion etching to create alignment marks to be
used in the remainder of the processing. PCVD oxide was then deposited on both sides of the
wafers to be an oxide mask for the p-type ion implant. The oxide was then patterned on both sides
starting with the topside. Both sides of the wafer were spun with resist so that one side could be
patterned and the other side would be protected. The topside was patterned first with lithography
using a mask file I designed in a maskless aligner tool. Lithography opened the pattern in the
photoresist and the oxide was then etched in 6:1 buffered oxide etch. This process was then

 20

repeated, protecting the frontside with photoresist and patterning the backside. Once the wafer
was fully patterned, it was sent back to the vendor for the boron (p-type) implant. After the final
implant, activation was completed by annealing at 900C for 10 min in argon atmosphere in a
graphite susceptor. The graphite susceptor increases the uniformity of the anneal to improve the
activation. Finally, gold-tin ohmic contacts were deposited on both sides of the devices using
lithography and a lift-off process.

 Once the bulk of the fabrication was complete, the devices were singulated from the wafer
by dicing into squares. These die were then further diced into circles with a slightly larger diameter
than the metal contacts. This is to avoid dicing through any metal which could end up on the sides
on the device and affect the performance. After dicing, a sidewall etch was done to smooth the
roughness that occurs during dicing. Finally, a dielectric epoxy was applied to the sidewalls to
increase uniformity of the sidewalls.

Testing results and further improvements
 Initial test results on 2-dimensional thyristor devices show a significant difference in
performance between single- and double-sided devices, and double-sided devices with and
without a sidewall etch; this is all shown in Figure 17. Considering first the single and double sided
un-etched devices on the far left of the plot, the double-sided device performed better in that it was
able to reach a higher voltage. The comparison between un-etched double-sided device (3 Double
no etch, green line) and the etched devices from the same wafer (3-1 Double, 3-2 Double, 3-3

 22

Double) shows a significant increase in the amount of voltage the devices can reach once the
sidewalls are etched. This solidifies the importance of having edge treatments on the sidewalls of
the devices that increase the uniformity of said sidewall.

 While the results of these tests are promising and are moving in the right direction, we
would like to be able to reach voltages 5x higher than what we achieved here. The main factors that
will allow us to do that will be edge treatments and carrier profiles. We have received newer wafers
with improved carrier profiles that should improve device performance immediately. Additionally,
the devices tested here did not have an edge bevel yet as we are still trying to develop that process.
We are also still working to determine the best etching process for the sidewalls, whether it’s a wet
or dry etch, or some combination. The downside of the HNA etch can be seen here, as the double
sided devices 1, 2, and 3 from wafer 3 all underwent the same etch and same processing but show
a lot of variation in performance.

Conclusion
 We have successfully fabricated and tested both 1 and 2 dimensional thyristors, and single-
and double-sided thyristors. We continue to work on improvements including the doping schemes
of the wafers we start with, improving sidewall uniformity, and refining processing steps. We are
making great progress and have shown that a thyristor device can be fabricated using ion implants
to create alternating n and p regions.
