Project Overview
Understanding how matter behaves under extreme compression is essential for predicting
fusion experiments and the properties of stars and planets. Recent experiments at the National
Ignition Facility suggest that widely used models underestimate how readily tightly bound
electrons separate from atoms in dense materials such as carbon and beryllium. These
discrepancies affect predictions of how matter absorbs and transports energy. This project
aimed to resolve these differences and establish a stronger experimental foundation for
modeling matter under extreme conditions.
The project combined x-ray scattering, which enables probing the behavior of electrons in
dense plasmas, with precision radiography, which measures x-ray absorption. Through new
analysis methods were developed to determine plasma temperature with less dependence on
theoretical models, and thus reducing uncertainty in the interpretation of x-ray scattering
measurements. Together, these measurements and methods are intended to clarify the
relationship between electron release and energy absorption, test competing theoretical
predictions, and guide improvements to models used to predict the behavior of dense matter.

Mission Impact
This research is expected to strengthen Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory capabilities in
precision plasma measurements and predictive modeling under extreme conditions. By
addressing uncertainties in material properties essential to stockpile stewardship simulations, it
supports National Nuclear Security Administration efforts to maintain and enhance the safety,
security, and effectiveness of the United States nuclear weapons stockpile. The work also
advances Department of Energy fusion research and energy-security goals by improving the
scientific foundation for predicting fusion experiments. New measurement and analysis
capabilities could enable research collaborations connecting experiments, atomic physics, and
computational modeling, while developing tools to address future national security challenges.
The findings may also inform Space Administration research on stellar and planetary interiors,
where extreme compression governs material behavior and energy transport.
