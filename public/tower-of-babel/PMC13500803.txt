Occupational hazard awareness and safety-related knowledge among EMS students: evidence of a potential gap
Authors: Yasir Almukhlifi, Abdullah Alharbi, Lara Alghamdi, Monia Bin Mahfouz, Lama Khan, Krzysztof Goniewicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emergency Medical Services (EMS) trainees are exposed to multiple occupational hazards during their training; however, the extent to which their awareness translates into safe professional behaviours remains unclear. This study aimed to assess the level of occupational hazard awareness and safety-related knowledge among EMS students in the Makkah region, Saudi Arabia. A cross-sectional study was conducted among 96 EMS students from three academic institutions. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire adapted from previously published instruments assessing demographic characteristics, awareness of occupational hazards, safety-related measures, and sources of information. Descriptive statistics and inferential analyses using non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U, Kruskal–Wallis), Chi-square/Fisher’s exact tests, and Spearman correlation analysis were applied. Overall, students demonstrated moderate to high awareness of occupational hazards, particularly regarding hand hygiene and personal protective equipment (PPE). Lower awareness was observed for ergonomic risks and procedures for reporting occupational injuries. Physical (83.3%) and chemical (81.3%) hazards were most frequently recognized, while ergonomic hazards were least identified (26.0%). A total of 63.5% of participants reported encountering aggressive patients at least once per year. No statistically significant association was observed between academic progression and awareness (p = 0.14). A weak, non-significant correlation was found between awareness and self-reported safety-related measures (r = 0.182, p = 0.076), suggesting a limited association between knowledge and reported safety behaviours. Although EMS students demonstrate generally high awareness of occupational hazards, this may be insufficient to ensure safe behaviour, as reflected by self-reported safety-related measures. These findings highlight the need for educational strategies that better support the application of knowledge in practice.

Introduction

Healthcare workers, including paramedics, operate in inherently high-risk environments and are routinely exposed to a broad spectrum of occupational hazards, including physical, biological, chemical, and psychological risks. Epidemiological data indicate that the rate of occupational injuries in healthcare reaches 16.8%, compared to 4.8% in other industries, placing healthcare workers at more than twice the risk of work-related harm1. Within this group, paramedics represent a particularly vulnerable population due to the unpredictable, uncontrolled, and time-pressured nature of prehospital care2.

Paramedics are exposed to multiple and often overlapping hazards, including infectious diseases, musculoskeletal injuries, chemical exposures, and severe psychological stress associated with long working hours and emotionally demanding situations3,4. Frequent contact with bodily fluids places them at increased risk of infections such as hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and HIV, with studies reporting that 27% of paramedics experience direct exposure and 21% sustain needle-stick injuries5. In addition, emergency response driving introduces a significant risk of road traffic incidents, further compounding occupational vulnerability6.

Despite the recognized importance of occupational safety, evidence suggests substantial gaps in adherence to safety practices. Up to 52% of paramedics report improper disposal of infectious materials, often attributed to operational pressure, while 38% do not consistently use essential personal protective equipment (PPE), such as masks or goggles5,7,8. Notably, these behaviours coexist with a strong professional commitment to patient care, as the majority of paramedics report continuing interventions even in high-risk exposure scenarios7. This tension highlights a critical issue in occupational health: awareness of risk does not necessarily translate into consistent protective behaviour.

Current Emergency Medical Services (EMS) education primarily focuses on clinical competencies, such as patient assessment, treatment, and transport, while comparatively less emphasis is placed on self-protection and occupational risk management2. Although knowledge of safety measures is often acquired during academic training, with approximately 46.75% of paramedics attributing their understanding to formal education, this knowledge may not be sufficient to ensure safe practice7. Importantly, awareness and safety behaviours are influenced by multiple factors, including experience, workload, and contextual pressures, suggesting that education alone may not fully address occupational risk.

The transition from student to professional paramedic represents a critical and often challenging phase. Newly qualified paramedics frequently report difficulties adapting to the operational realities of emergency care, including high expectations, exposure to patient suffering and death, and the need for rapid decision-making under pressure2,9. Early professional experiences are commonly described as overwhelming, with emotional regulation identified as a key challenge2,9. These factors further complicate the relationship between knowledge, behaviour, and safety in practice.

Recent research increasingly emphasises the need to integrate structured training in both occupational safety and psychological resilience into EMS education. This includes not only knowledge acquisition but also behavioural reinforcement, emotional preparedness, and organisational support mechanisms10. However, despite these advances, there remains a limited understanding of how awareness of occupational hazards among EMS students translates into safety-related behaviours and preparedness for real-world practice.

Given the high-risk profile of paramedic work and the potential disconnect between knowledge and practice, it is essential to evaluate occupational hazard awareness at the early stages of professional development. Assessing students’ awareness provides critical insight into the foundations upon which future safety behaviours are built and may help identify gaps in current educational approaches.

Therefore, this study aims to assess the level of occupational hazard awareness and safety-related knowledge among EMS students in the Makkah region, Saudi Arabia. Using a structured survey, the study examines awareness of key hazard categories, including physical, chemical, biological, and psychological risks, as well as students’ understanding and application of safety measures.

By identifying strengths and gaps in students’ awareness, this study seeks to contribute to the development of more effective, evidence-based educational strategies that not only improve knowledge but also support the translation of awareness into safe professional practice. Ultimately, this approach may contribute to enhancing occupational safety, reducing risk exposure, and strengthening the resilience of the future paramedic workforce.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional study design was employed to assess awareness of occupational hazards among EMS students. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire adapted from previously published instruments assessing occupational hazard awareness and safety-related measures among healthcare workers and students11. Items were selected and modified to reflect the EMS student context, including prehospital hazards, PPE use, hand hygiene, injury reporting, and exposure to aggressive patients.

The study was conducted between December 2023 and May 2024 in the Makkah region, Saudi Arabia, and included students from three academic institutions: King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences (KSAU-HS), Umm Al-Qura University, and Al-Ghad International College. A convenience sampling strategy was applied to recruit participants.

Eligible participants were students aged 18–30 years who were currently enrolled in Emergency Medical Services (EMS) programs (paramedic training), actively attending classes, and who provided informed consent. These programs are designed to prepare students for prehospital emergency care roles and include both theoretical instruction and structured clinical training components. As part of their curriculum, students participate in clinical training and field placements with varying levels of involvement ranging from observational roles in earlier years to supervised participation in patient care during advanced stages of training and internship. Students who did not complete the questionnaire, withdrew consent, or were not actively participating in the educational program were excluded.

The estimated population of EMS students in the Makkah region is approximately 150 individuals. Based on this population, the sample size was calculated using a finite population correction approach, assuming a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and a response distribution of 50%. The calculation was performed using the Raosoft sample size calculator, yielding a minimum required sample size of approximately 109 participants. A total of 96 completed responses were obtained. Therefore, the achieved sample size was below the calculated minimum, which may have reduced statistical precision and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Data collection and variables

Data were collected using a structured online questionnaire composed of four sections.

The first section gathered demographic information, including age, gender, academic year, and university affiliation. The second section consisted of 10 items measured on a 5-point Likert scale (ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”) and assessed participants’ perceptions of occupational hazards and safety practices. The third section included 7 items evaluating knowledge of safety precautions, while the fourth section comprised 4 multiple-choice questions assessing awareness of types and sources of occupational hazards.

The questionnaire was distributed via an electronic flyer containing study information, including objectives, inclusion criteria, and a survey link. The questionnaire was administered in English, as all participants were enrolled in academic programs delivered in English. The survey was disseminated through university communication channels in February 2024 and remained open until April 2024.

The questionnaire was reviewed for content validity by subject-matter experts prior to dissemination. The instrument was adapted to reflect the EMS student context, including prehospital occupational hazards, PPE use, hand hygiene, injury reporting procedures, and exposure to aggressive patients. A pilot test was subsequently conducted among a small group of EMS students (n = 7) to assess the clarity, comprehensibility, and feasibility of the questionnaire. Minor modifications were introduced based on participant feedback to improve wording and interpretability of the items. Internal consistency of the adapted instrument was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient.

Awareness and safety-related measures were derived from questionnaire responses by aggregating item scores within each domain. For Likert-scale items, responses were assigned numerical values (1–5), and composite scores were calculated by summing the responses for each domain. These scores were treated as continuous variables for analytical purposes. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR) due to non-normal distribution.

Statistical analysis

Data were exported from the online survey platform and initially processed using Microsoft Excel for data cleaning and organization. The cleaned dataset was subsequently analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and response distributions. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the data did not meet the assumptions of normal distribution, and given the ordinal nature of Likert-scale responses, non-parametric tests were applied. Continuous variables are presented as median and IQR.

Inferential analysis was conducted using non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis) to compare differences between groups. Associations between categorical variables were examined using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationship between awareness and safety measures. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Awareness levels were derived from questionnaire responses and categorized for analytical purposes based on response distributions. Incomplete responses were excluded from the final analysis.

Ethical considerations and data security

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC), Jeddah, Saudi Arabia (Ref. No. SP23J/136/08).

Participation in the study was voluntary. Prior to accessing the questionnaire, participants were provided with an information sheet explaining the study purpose, procedures, and confidentiality measures, and informed consent was obtained electronically from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study. The survey was anonymous, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any stage without consequences.

All data were securely stored on password-protected devices accessible only to the research team, and confidentiality of participant information was strictly maintained throughout the study. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Results

Participant characteristics

The internal consistency of the adapted questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient prior to analysis. The awareness scale (10 items) demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.85), while the safety-related measures scale (7 items) demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.89). The reliability analysis supported the internal consistency of the adapted instrument.

A total of 96 EMS students participated in the study. The majority of respondents were female (59.4%), and most participants were aged 21–23 years (71.9%).

In terms of academic progression, the largest groups were third-year (32.3%) and fourth-year students (28.1%), followed by interns (27.1%). Awareness of occupational hazards and safety measures

Overall, EMS students demonstrated varying levels of awareness across different categories of occupational hazards.

The highest levels of awareness were observed in relation to basic preventive practices. Most participants reported strong familiarity with hand hygiene and the use of personal protective equipment (PPE), with over 70–80% indicating agreement or strong agreement.

Moderate levels of awareness were identified for electrical and mechanical hazards, with approximately 60–65% of respondents reporting familiarity with risks such as fire hazards, electrical shocks, and Hepatitis B vaccination.

In contrast, lower levels of awareness were observed for biological and chemical hazards, with only around half of participants indicating familiarity. The lowest levels were related to hearing loss, headache risks, and procedures for reporting occupational injuries, where fewer than half of respondents reported strong awareness.

These findings highlight uneven distribution of awareness across hazard categories, with notable gaps in less visible and less emphasized occupational risks. Detailed responses are presented in Table 2.

Exposure to occupational risks and threats

EMS students reported high recognition of commonly encountered occupational hazards. Physical hazards were identified by 83.3% of participants, followed by chemical (81.3%) and biological hazards (64.6%), while ergonomic hazards were least frequently recognized (26.0%).

In terms of exposure, 63.5% of students reported encountering aggressive patients at least once per year. Patient-related threats were the most frequently reported, including patient violence (52.1%) and exposure to infectious diseases (41.7%).

Other commonly reported threats included bystander violence (34.4%), risk of burnout (34.4%), and physiological burden associated with long working hours (33.3%). Detailed findings are presented in Table 3.

Sources of information

Media was the most frequently reported source of information on occupational hazards (55.2%), followed by pre-employment orientation (42.7%) and clinical exposure through ward rounds (41.7%). Fewer students reported formal professional workshops as a source of information (28.1%), while only a small proportion indicated other sources (7.3%).

These findings suggest that EMS students rely predominantly on informal and experiential sources of information, rather than structured educational programs, for knowledge related to occupational hazards. Detailed results are presented in Table 4.

Relationship between awareness and safety

A weak positive correlation was observed between awareness of occupational hazards and safety measures (r = 0.182); however, this relationship was not statistically significant (p = 0.076).

These findings suggest that higher levels of awareness do not necessarily translate into improved safety-related knowledge or practices among EMS students, suggesting a potential disconnect between theoretical knowledge and its application in practice.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of occupational hazard awareness and safety-related knowledge among EMS students in the Makkah region, Saudi Arabia, offering important insights into how theoretical understanding translates into preparedness for real-world practice. Overall, the findings indicate moderate to high levels of awareness across most hazard categories, with particularly strong familiarity observed in basic preventive measures such as hand hygiene and the use of PPE. These results are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that EMS personnel and students generally report high levels of knowledge regarding standard safety practices12,13. However, this awareness was not uniformly distributed, and important gaps were identified, particularly in relation to less visible or less emphasized risks.

Lower levels of awareness were observed for hazards such as hearing loss, headaches, and procedures for reporting occupational injuries. Similar patterns have been reported in prior research, where less obvious or cumulative risks tend to be underrepresented in training programs despite their long-term impact on health and occupational functioning14. In the present study, awareness of biological and chemical hazards was also moderate rather than high, which aligns with previous findings indicating that these risk categories are often insufficiently addressed in educational settings15. These results suggest that current EMS curricula may prioritise immediate and acute risks while underemphasising chronic, indirect, or system-related hazards, such as ergonomics and reporting mechanisms.

The distribution of hazard recognition further supports this interpretation. While most students identified physical and chemical hazards, ergonomic risks were markedly under-recognised. This finding is consistent with previous research highlighting limited awareness of ergonomic hazards among healthcare students, despite their significant contribution to musculoskeletal disorders and long-term occupational burden16,17. The discrepancy between recognition of acute versus cumulative risks suggests a structural gap in occupational health education, where training remains focused on visible and immediate dangers rather than comprehensive risk profiles.

Recent pedagogical research further supports this interpretation, indicating that improving occupational safety requires more than traditional, knowledge-based instruction. Active learning approaches and sector-specific simulations have been shown to reduce early-career vulnerability to occupational injury by better preparing individuals for real-world operational demands18. In particular, simulation-based training that reflects the complexity and pressures of actual work environments may help bridge the gap between hazard awareness and safe performance. These findings suggest that integrating structured experiential learning into EMS education may enhance the translation of theoretical knowledge into effective safety-related behaviours.

Exposure-related findings also provide important context for understanding students’ preparedness. A substantial proportion of participants reported encountering aggressive patients, reflecting the well-documented prevalence of violence in prehospital care settings19. It should be noted that students’ exposure to such risks occurs within the context of clinical training and field placements, with varying levels of involvement depending on their stage of education. Patient-initiated violence, along with bystander aggression, represents a significant occupational hazard that is frequently reported in EMS literature. The presence of these experiences already at the student level underscores the need for early integration of de-escalation training and situational awareness strategies within educational programs. Consequently, exposure findings should be interpreted within the context of supervised clinical training rather than fully independent professional practice. Furthermore, the identification of burnout risk and physiological burden associated with long working hours highlights the multidimensional nature of occupational risk, encompassing both physical and psychological domains.

Although some differences in exposure patterns were observed across demographic groups in the original dataset, these were not retained as central analytical findings in the present model, as the primary focus of the study was on awareness and its relationship with safety behaviour. Nonetheless, existing literature suggests that gender and experience may influence the perception and reporting of occupational stressors, with some studies indicating higher levels of reported stress and burnout among female EMS personnel14. This contextual evidence remains relevant for interpreting the broader implications of the findings, particularly in relation to occupational health support systems.

An important finding of this study is that although higher awareness scores were observed among students in advanced academic years, these differences were not statistically significant. While a trend toward increased awareness with academic progression was noted, this pattern should be interpreted with caution. Similar trends have been reported in previous literature, indicating that students in more advanced years may demonstrate higher levels of awareness, which is consistent with the role of clinical exposure and experiential learning in shaping professional competence2. However, this pattern did not reach statistical significance in the present study.

This interpretation is supported by the central finding of the study: the weak and non-significant correlation between awareness and safety measures (r = 0.182, p = 0.076). This result suggests that higher levels of awareness do not necessarily translate into improved safety-related practices among EMS students. It should be noted that safety practices were assessed indirectly through self-reported measures rather than direct observation of behaviour. However, given the relatively small sample size (n = 96), the study may have been underpowered to detect a weak association, and this finding should therefore be interpreted with caution. Similar conclusions have been reported in previous research, where knowledge alone has been found to be an insufficient determinant of safe behaviour, with factors such as workplace culture, availability of resources, and operational pressure playing a critical role in shaping compliance20. The present findings therefore reinforce the notion that occupational safety cannot be addressed solely through knowledge-based education. This suggests that the observed knowledge–behaviour gap is not merely a result of insufficient awareness, but may reflect deeper structural and behavioural barriers within training environments and clinical practice settings.

In addition, behavioural science frameworks suggest that safety behaviour in high-risk clinical environments should be understood as a multidimensional construct rather than a direct outcome of hazard awareness alone. Evidence indicates that compliance with safety practices is influenced by a complex interaction of factors, including perceived institutional support, organisational safety culture, and engagement in reporting behaviours21. These dimensions may help explain the non-linear relationship observed in the present study, where increased awareness did not translate into significantly improved safety-related measures. This perspective highlights the importance of considering organisational and behavioural determinants when interpreting the gap between knowledge and practice.

The discrepancy between awareness and behaviour highlights a critical gap in EMS training. While students appear to acquire theoretical knowledge of hazards, this knowledge may not be effectively translated into practical application. This gap may be explained by several factors, including limited opportunities for skills-based training, lack of reinforcement in clinical settings, and the influence of contextual pressures during real-world practice. As previously suggested in the literature, effective occupational safety education should integrate not only knowledge acquisition but also behavioural training, simulation-based learning, and organisational support mechanisms10.

The findings related to sources of information further support this interpretation. The predominance of media and informal sources over structured educational programs suggests that students may rely on fragmented or non-standardised information channels. While media can play an important role in disseminating knowledge, its dominance as a primary source raises concerns regarding the consistency and reliability of occupational safety education22–24. Previous studies have highlighted the growing influence of digital media in shaping health-related knowledge, while also emphasising the need for critical evaluation and integration within formal curricula25–27. The present results suggest that educational institutions should strengthen structured training and ensure that key occupational safety competencies are delivered through formal, standardised frameworks.

Taken together, these findings indicate that while EMS students demonstrate a generally high level of awareness, important gaps remain in both the scope of this awareness and its translation into safe practice. The results highlight the need for a shift in educational approaches, from knowledge-focused models to more comprehensive strategies that integrate behavioural training, experiential learning, and system-level support.

Addressing this gap is essential not only for improving individual safety practices but also for enhancing the overall resilience and preparedness of the future EMS workforce. However, these findings should be interpreted in light of methodological considerations, including the variability in students’ clinical exposure and the use of self-reported measures to assess safety-related behaviours. Future research may benefit from applying more advanced analytical approaches, such as regression modelling, to better identify predictors of safety-related behaviours and further explore the relationship between awareness and practice.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths that enhance the robustness and relevance of its findings. First, the use of a structured questionnaire based on previously published instruments supports the reliability and consistency of data collection. Second, the inclusion of EMS students from multiple academic institutions within the Makkah region provides a broader perspective on occupational hazard awareness across different educational settings. Additionally, the study addresses an underexplored area by focusing specifically on EMS trainees, contributing novel insights into the early stages of professional development in a high-risk healthcare domain.

At the same time, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the use of self-reported data may introduce response bias, including social desirability bias, as participants may overreport adherence to recommended safety practices. Furthermore, some questionnaire items relied on subjective perceptions, which may affect the accuracy of responses. Importantly, safety-related practices were not directly measured but inferred from self-reported measures, representing a major limitation of the study and limiting the ability to assess actual behaviour.

Second, the use of convenience sampling and the predominance of participants from a single institution may limit the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. Differences in institutional training and safety culture may also have influenced the results. In addition, although 96 completed responses were obtained, this number was below the calculated minimum sample size of approximately 109 participants for the estimated study population. This may have reduced statistical precision and increased the risk of sampling bias, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings.

Third, variability in students’ clinical exposure and level of involvement in patient care may affect the validity of reported exposure to occupational hazards, particularly in relation to patient-related risks. Additionally, potential confounding factors, such as academic year and level of clinical exposure, were not controlled for in the analysis and may have influenced the observed relationships.

Finally, the cross-sectional design of the study restricts the ability to establish causal relationships between awareness and safety-related behaviours. The observed associations should therefore be interpreted with caution, as they may be influenced by unmeasured contextual and organisational factors.

Conclusions

This study shows that EMS students demonstrate generally moderate to high awareness of occupational hazards, particularly in relation to basic preventive measures such as hand hygiene and PPE use. However, important gaps remain in the recognition of less visible risks, including ergonomic hazards and procedures for reporting occupational injuries.

A weak and non-significant association between awareness and self-reported safety-related measures suggests that knowledge alone may be insufficient to ensure safe behaviour in clinical settings. This finding should be interpreted with caution, as safety practices were not directly observed and the sample size may have limited the ability to detect weak associations.

These findings highlight the need for educational approaches that extend beyond knowledge-based instruction and incorporate behavioural training, active learning, and simulation-based experiences that reflect real-world conditions of prehospital care. Strengthening structured and standardised training may improve preparedness for occupational risks among EMS students.
