AI‐Revealed Transition From Density‐ to Connectivity‐Controlled Mechanics in Hierarchical Porous Materials
Authors: Milad Masrouri, Federica Buccino, Laura Maria Vergani, Zhao Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHierarchical porous architectures enable lightweight, damage‐tolerant materials, yet their performance depends on how mechanical properties scale with density. Classical cellular‐solid theory captures the density‐controlled scaling law, but it remains unclear whether microscale defect topology drives its breakdown. Here, in situ synchrotron tomography, diffusion‐based generative modeling, and micromechanical simulations are combined to reveal an AI‐enabled, scale‐dependent transition in stiffness‐density scaling in trabecular bone with hierarchical porous structures. At the millimeter scale, the effective Young's modulus follows cellular‐solid behavior, E∝(BVTV)2, where BVTV is defined as bone volume divided by total volume. At the lacunar (microscale) level, simulations on thousands of lacunar microstructures, augmented by statistically faithful AI‐generated ensembles, exhibit a markedly steeper dependence, E∝(BVTV)4−6. This hypersensitivity arises from long‐range interactions between void‐induced stress fields that merge into system‐spanning weak corridors, shifting load transfer from porosity control to matrix connectivity and promoting guided crack initiation and propagation. By preserving higher‐order morphology while expanding microstructural sampling, AI generation enables ensemble‐level quantification of this connectivity‐controlled regime under data‐limited imaging. These results establish a mechanism by which microscale defect topology can dominate stiffness in hierarchical porous materials.Keywords: generative AI, hierarchical porous materials, lacunar network, micromechanics, stiffness‐density scaling, trabecular bone

Introduction

Hierarchical porous architectures that span stochastic foams, architected lattices, and biological tissues enable lightweight stiffness and damage tolerance by redistributing load and arresting cracks across nested structural levels [1, 2]. Across both engineered and biological systems, multiscale structure–mechanics coupling enables materials such as nacre, dentin, and bone to combine stiffness with resilience through load redistribution and crack‐path control [3, 4]. Because relative density is often the most accessible descriptor of porous architectures, stiffness–density scaling relations underpin property maps, reduced‐order surrogates, and design rules. A central question is whether these density‐based scaling laws remain invariant across hierarchical levels, or whether they shift when the governing architecture changes character.

Classical cellular‐solid theory provides the canonical baseline for density‐controlled mechanics. In the Gibson–Ashby framework, the normalized Young's modulus follows a power‐law dependence on relative density, with the exponent reflecting deformation mode and network topology [5]. This description captures trabecular bone at the millimeter scale, where bone volume fraction (BV/TV; bone volume/total volume) serves as a practical density proxy [6]. The resulting stiffness–density relations consolidate the view that trabecular bone behaves mechanically as a bending‐dominated cellular solid [5, 7, 8]. Bone is therefore a uniquely constrained model to interrogate when density‐controlled scaling may break down, because its hierarchy spans nanoscale mineralized collagen to microscale osteocyte lacunae and millimeter‐scale trabecular networks (Figure 1a) [9, 10, 11]. At small scales, nanoscale mechanical heterogeneity promotes energy dissipation and influences damage, emphasizing that hierarchy can alter governing mechanisms, not only property magnitudes [12, 13].

At the micron scale, bone departs fundamentally from open‐cell cellular‐solid assumptions. The mineralized matrix is perforated by the lacunar–canalicular network (LCN), an interconnected system of lacunae and canaliculi embedded within an otherwise continuous load‐bearing solid, introducing microscale porosity, anisotropy, and spatial correlations [14]. Osteocytes inhabit this network and regulate remodeling, making LCN morphology a component of “bone quality” beyond trabecular density; consistent with this role, LCN architecture links to in vivo mechanosensation and remodeling response [15]. Lacuna‐scale micromechanics further shows that lacunar geometry can amplify local strains relative to applied strains, indicating that these voids are mechanically active features rather than dilute defects [16]. Moreover, lacunae are biologically regulated and altered by aging and disease [17], and High‐resolution synchrotron imaging reveals systematic, pathology‐dependent changes in lacunar morphology, yet how these collective changes translate into tissue‐level mechanics remains unresolved [15, 18]. Experimental modulation of the lacunar–canalicular space can measurably reduce tissue stiffness, highlighting the potential for disproportionate stiffness loss from modest microscale porosity changes [19]. Therefore, these observations motivate the hypothesis that classical density‐controlled scaling may not extrapolate to the lacunar scale, and that a distinct connectivity‐controlled regime may emerge (Figure 1b).

Understanding the mechanistic origin of this transition requires explicitly linking osteocyte‐scale architecture to stress redistribution and failure, beyond what can be inferred from porosity alone. A mechanistic route to such a regime shift is provided by connectivity, percolation, and stress‐field interactions in embedded‐void systems. Classical elasticity predicts localized stress concentrations around isolated inclusions; however, when voids are embedded in a continuous matrix and sufficiently close for their stress fields to overlap, interactions become collective and load paths can reorganize into connected weak corridors [20]. In random elastic networks, elastic and rigidity percolation predict critical scaling of macroscopic elastic constants governed primarily by connectivity rather than porosity alone near a threshold [21, 22]. Testing for a microscale connectivity‐controlled stiffness law, however, requires statistical power: high‐resolution synchrotron microtomography (HR‐µCT) and in situ testing can resolve 3D microarchitecture and damage (Figure 1c,d), but experimental throughput and computationally expensive postprocessing constrain sample availability across physiopathological states [23]. As a result, lacunar‐scale studies often operate in narrow windows of void content, leaving open whether observed sensitivity reflects local perturbations or a broader scaling transition [19, 24].

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) offers a principled route to overcome data scarcity by learning microstructure distributions and enabling ensemble‐level sampling while preserving higher‐order morphology. Early models such as variational autoencoders (VAEs) [25] and generative adversarial networks (GANs) [26] enabled scalable learning of high‐dimensional image distributions and were widely adopted for biomedical image synthesis [27, 28, 29, 30] and for materials microstructure reconstruction and property‐guided generation [31, 32]. However, VAEs often oversmooth samples [33], whereas GANs can be difficult to train and may suffer from mode collapse, limiting diversity and reliability when long‐range correlations matter [34]. Denoising diffusion models replace adversarial training with a stable denoising process, typically improving mode coverage, fidelity, and conditional controllability (Figure 1e), enabling state‐of‐the‐art biomedical image synthesis [35] morphology‐preserving microstructure generation and evolution [36, 37, 38, 39, 40]. These capabilities directly align with lacunar‐scale mechanics, where higher‐order spatial correlations strongly influence load transfer and failure.

Here, trabecular bone is used as a model hierarchical porous solid to test whether density‐controlled scaling remains invariant across length scales or transitions to a connectivity‐controlled regime. In situ synchrotron tomography is combined with class‐conditional diffusion generation and elastic‐network micromechanics to quantify this transition across three physiopathological classes (healthy, osteoporotic, and COVID‐19‐affected) (Figure 1) [41]. Three advances are established: (i) stiffness–density scaling is shown to diverge between trabecular and lacunar architectures, indicating a scale‐dependent regime shift beyond classical cellular‐solid assumptions; (ii) diffusion‐augmented ensembles expand microstructural sampling for estimating scaling, variability, and class‐conditioned microstructure under data‐limited tomography; and (iii) a stress‐corridor mechanism is identified in which void‐induced stress‐field overlap reorganizes load transfer into connected weak corridors, shifting control of stiffness and damage initiation from porosity to matrix connectivity.

Results and Discussion

The central hypothesis is that hierarchical porous mechanics undergoes a scale‐dependent transition. Trabecular bone follows classical density‐controlled cellular‐solid scaling, whereas the osteocyte lacunar network introduces connectivity‐controlled micromechanics driven by long‐range stress‐field interactions between lacunae. To test this mechanism under data‐limited synchrotron imaging, a class‐conditional denoising diffusion probabilistic model (ccDDPM) is first validated to generate statistically consistent trabecular cross‐sections that preserve lacunar microstructural features. Real and AI‐generated (synthetic) ensembles are then coupled to micromechanical simulations to reveal a qualitative transition in stress redistribution and crack pathways. This enables quantitative scaling analysis that identifies amplified lacunar‐scale stiffness–density sensitivity and a peak sensitivity near an intermediate corridor‐forming regime. Finally, architecture‐driven effects are examined using composition‐controlled 3D‐printed replicas as a proof‐of‐concept comparison and leveraged to train a multimodal surrogate that rapidly predicts and maps local mechanical competence.

Diffusion Generation Reproduces Trabecular Context and Class‐Consistent Lacunar Microstructure

A ccDDPM model is trained on high‐resolution synchrotron µCT trabecular cross‐sections from three physiopathological classes (healthy, osteoporotic, and COVID‐19‐affected) [41], such that generation learns the joint distribution of trabecular morphology (strut geometry and connectivity) and embedded lacunar‐scale void features (density, shape, and spacing). Model fidelity is assessed by comparing real and synthetic samples both within each class and across classes (Figure 2). Qualitatively, generated cross‐sections closely match the global trabecular topology and thickness variation, while the zoomed insets show that lacunar voids are reproduced with realistic placement, size, and shape at the level required for downstream segmentation and simulation (Figure 2a,b).

To quantify similarity during training, the Fréchet inception distance (FID) [42] decreases rapidly and converges toward the real‐real baseline, indicating that real‐synthetic differences approach intrinsic variability between independent real subsets (Figure 2c). Lacunar statistics are then computed using a consistent segmentation pipeline: a convolutional neural network (CNN) trained on trabecular bone samples spanning all three physiopathological classes is used to segment lacunae within both real and generated trabecular networks [43]. This enables controlled, like‐for‐like extraction of lacunar density and shape descriptors from real and synthetic images. Lacunar density is defined as the areal lacunar fraction within each cross‐section (total lacunar area divided by mineralized matrix area), and oblateness is computed from an ellipse fit as the minor‐to‐major axis ratio.

Morphological comparisons further assess whether AI‐generated images preserve the lacunar features observed in the synchrotron‐derived dataset. At the image level, lacunar density shows class‐dependent trends, while real and AI‐generated images remain closely matched within each class (Figure 2d,e). At the lacuna level, oblateness exhibits similar class‐wise trends, with small real‐synthetic shifts in mean values for some conditions but substantially overlapping distributions (Figure 2f,g). These comparisons support the use of AI‐generated images for downstream microstructural sampling and mechanical simulation.

This interpretation is consistent with a recent large‐scale three‐dimensional SR‐µCT study of human femoral head trabecular bone, which analyzed 48 female donors (n = 16 per group) and approximately 8.96 million lacunae using AI‐assisted lacunar segmentation and hierarchical statistical modeling [44]. The study reported a robust osteoporotic lacunar remodeling signature, including increased lacunar volume, surface area, semi‐minor axis length, increased oblateness trends, and reduced sphericity. It also reported numerically higher lacunar number density and lacunar volume fraction in osteoporotic bone, although lacunar density did not differ significantly among groups. These larger‐cohort 3D findings are directionally consistent with the elevated osteoporotic lacunar‐density and shape trends observed in the present 2D analysis, while emphasizing that patient‐level interpretation requires appropriately powered donor‐level statistics. Differences in the COVID‐19 ranking between the two studies may reflect the two‐dimensional nature of the present descriptors, the smaller donor cohort, and heterogeneity in post‐COVID‐19 clinical presentation and time since infection.

Additional analyses in the Supporting Information further evaluate class conditioning and generative diversity. Cross‐class FID values are larger than the corresponding within‐class real–synthetic values (Figure S1), consistent with preservation of class‐conditioned image characteristics rather than convergence toward a single averaged distribution. A pairwise image‐similarity matrix shows no repeated high‐similarity blocks or duplicated patterns (Figure S2), supporting the diversity of the generated images and the absence of evident mode collapse.

Elastic‐Network Micromechanics Reveals a Transition in Stress and Energy Localization

To isolate architectural effects from compositional variability, tiles of size 160 × 160 µm2 were randomly cropped from mineralized trabecular regions after bone masking, excluding marrow pores and outer trabecular boundaries, to sample lacunar microstructure. Each tile was converted into an elastic network model with identical constituent properties, treating lacunae explicitly as voids (Figure 3a). A total of 3000 tiles were mechanically simulated, comprising 1500 synchrotron‐derived and 1500 AI‐generated tiles, with 500 tiles per physiopathological condition from each source. The two ensembles were first analyzed separately to compare their mechanical responses and stiffness–density scaling. After morphological and mechanical consistency was established, the expanded dataset was used for the downstream regime and surrogate‐model analyses. Simulations reveal a qualitative transition in stress redistribution as lacunar architecture varies. At low lacunar density, stress concentrations remain localized around individual lacunae and multiple alternative load paths remain available in the surrounding bone matrix (Figure 3b). In this regime, lacunae act as largely isolated perturbations and crack paths are repeatedly deflected, consistent with classical solutions for noninteracting defects (e.g., Kirsch for circular holes and Goodier for spherical inclusions), where stiffness decreases gradually with porosity [20, 45]. As lacunar density increases (reduced spacing and increased alignment), neighboring stress fields overlap and organize into connected, highly stressed ligaments that link adjacent lacunae (Figure 3c). Load becomes concentrated within these “weak corridors,” reducing redundancy in load transfer and increasing sensitivity to the connectivity of the remaining mineral matrix rather than to porosity alone [46, 47]. This stress‐corridor formation provides a mechanistic route for amplified stiffness degradation and corridor‐guided failure at the lacunar scale. Complementary local strain‐energy‐density fields show the same architecture‐dependent transition (Figure S4). At low lacunar density, deformation energy remains localized around individual lacunae, whereas at higher lacunar density, neighboring high‐energy regions overlap and form connected corridors across the mineral matrix. This energetic representation supports the interpretation that the weak‐corridor pattern is not specific to the von Mises stress measure and instead reflects collective deformation localization arising from lacunar spatial organization.

In Situ Synchrotron Radiation Micro Computed Tomography (SR‐µCT) Links Stress Corridors to Crack Trajectories

In situ SR‐µCT imaging of damage evolution provides a qualitative experimental comparison with the architecture‐dependent crack behavior observed in the elastic‐network simulations (Figure 3d). S1–S3 contain relatively isolated or weakly organized lacunae and exhibit crack deviation or branching as cracks interact with individual lacunae and local heterogeneities. These specimens therefore correspond to the low‐lacunar‐density regime, in which stress and strain‐energy localization remain confined around isolated lacunae and multiple competing load‐bearing pathways remain available.

By contrast, S4 contains a more corridor‐like lacunar arrangement and exhibits a comparatively straighter crack trajectory adjacent to this lacunar corridor. This behavior is qualitatively consistent with the simulated high‐lacunar‐density regime, where neighboring stress or strain‐energy fields overlap and form connected weak corridors that provide preferential paths for progressive rupture. Exact point‐by‐point agreement between simulated and experimental crack paths is not expected, because the simulations isolate lacunar topology in a homogeneous two‐dimensional elastic network under uniaxial tension, whereas the in situ experiments involve three‐dimensional trabecular architecture under compression, out‐of‐plane crack propagation, trabecular boundaries, and additional tissue‐level heterogeneities. Accordingly, the comparison supports the shared mechanistic trend that isolated lacunae promote crack deflection and branching, while corridor‐like lacunar organization can guide straighter crack propagation.

This qualitative correspondence supports the interpretation that corridor‐like pathways are mechanically relevant architectural features expressed under load, rather than artifacts of the elastic‐network visualization. It also links microscale topology to failure mode: when load‐bearing connectivity is preserved, cracks are repeatedly deflected and fragmented; when deformation localizes into weak corridors, cracks follow preferential paths, effectively bypassing deflection‐driven toughening.

Amplified Stiffness‐Density Scaling at the Lacunar Scale

With validated real and synthetic ensembles and a mechanistic picture of stress redistribution, stiffness‐density scaling is quantified across scales. At the trabecular scale, image‐guided failure assessment (IGFA) based on in situ SR‐µCT compression of 15 specimens yields an effective Young's modulus consistent with density‐controlled cellular‐solid mechanics of a bending‐dominated open‐cell lattice, with E∝(BVTV)n and n = 2.02 (Figure S5; Experimental Section and Supporting Information). In contrast, lacunar‐scale simulations exhibit a markedly steeper dependence of effective modulus on local BV/TV (Figure 3e). As shown in Figure S6, log–log regression of lacunar‐scale modulus versus BV/TV yields substantially elevated scaling exponents (n = 5.75 for real microstructures and n = 4.78 for synthetic microstructures), indicating that stiffness sensitivity at the osteocyte scale cannot be explained by density alone.

Because all simulations employ identical constituent properties, the scaling amplification arises purely from microarchitecture. This establishes an architectural origin for the lacunar‐scale regime transition, consistent with the stress‐corridor mechanism in which overlapping void‐induced stress fields eliminate redundant load paths and concentrate deformation into a small fraction of the matrix. Importantly, these corridors also act as precursors for failure: consistent with straighter crack trajectories in lacuna‐rich regions (Figure 3d), cracks preferentially propagate along corridor‐like paths, effectively bypassing deflection‐driven toughening. This geometry‐driven sensitivity is consistent with experimental observations that physiological expansion of the lacunar‐canalicular space can reduce cortical tissue modulus without detectable changes in bulk mineralization [19].

To contextualize the lacunar‐scale amplification relative to prior osteocyte‐scale measurements, published stiffness‐density datasets are replotted in the same log–log representation (Figure S7). A cohesive finite‐element analysis of osteocyte lacunae reported by Sang et al. yields an apparent exponent n ≈ 2.37(R 2 ≈ 0.36) over a narrow BV/TV window, whereas microindentation during lactation‐associated expansion of the lacunar‐canalicular space reported by Kaya et al. exhibits an extremely steep local sensitivity (n ≈ 16.71, R 2 ≈ 0.99) [19, 24]. These comparisons support a transition from density‐controlled behavior toward a connectivity‐controlled regime as lacunar topology reorganizes load transfer.

Peak Sensitivity in an Intermediate Corridor‐Forming Regime Indicates a Rapid Connectivity Transition

To quantify what governs the amplified lacunar‐scale scaling, microstructures are stratified into isolated (sparse), corridor‐forming (intermediate), and clustered (dense) lacunar‐density regimes, and stiffness‐BV/TV scaling is evaluated within each regime (Figure 3f). Here, “corridor‐forming” is used operationally to denote the intermediate regime in which lacuna‐induced stress fields begin to organize into system‐spanning weak corridors. The scaling exponent peaks in the intermediate corridor‐forming regime (n = 5.81), while remaining substantially lower in the sparse (n = 3.93) and dense (n = 3.84) regimes, indicating that stiffness sensitivity is maximized near a rapid connectivity transition. In this regime, overlapping stress fields and the loss of redundant load paths amplify stiffness degradation as corridors emerge and coalesce.

This non‐monotonic sensitivity is a hallmark of connectivity‐controlled mechanics. At low lacunar density, lacunae are too isolated to reorganize global load transfer while at high lacunar density, the matrix is broadly compliant and further porosity changes produce smaller relative shifts. Near the intermediate regime, however, load‐bearing connectivity changes rapidly as load‐bearing pathways are severed, producing disproportionate mechanical consequences from modest architectural perturbations. Similar transitions from distributed damage to brittle failure driven by defect networks have been reported in porous brittle materials such as concrete and rocks [46, 48].

Consistent with this interpretation, the present image‐level analysis shows elevated lacunar porosity in osteoporotic bone relative to healthy tissue (Figure 2d), placing osteoporotic microstructures closer to a regime in which mechanical response becomes increasingly controlled by the connectivity of the remaining load‐bearing mineral matrix [49]. Because the present cohort contains five donors per group, these disease‐associated trends should be interpreted as exploratory at the patient level. The central mechanical conclusion is therefore not that a specific clinical group is definitively separated by lacunar density, but that modest changes in lacunar‐scale architecture can produce disproportionate stiffness loss when they shift the matrix toward a corridor‐forming connectivity regime.

Composition‐Controlled 3D Printing Provides Proof‐of‐Concept Support for Architecture‐Driven Stiffness Ranking

To isolate architectural effects from tissue composition, trabecular architectures were 3D printed from a single material and tested under compression (Figure S8). Volumes of interest (VOI) were selected to have comparable BV/TV across the three physiopathological classes, thereby controlling density while isolating topology (Experimental Section). Despite identical constituent properties, printed replicas reproduce the stiffness ranking observed in the selected native specimens (Figure 4a), with the healthy architecture stiffest, followed by COVID‐19‐affected and osteoporotic architectures. Although absolute apparent moduli differ between printed replicas and native tissue due to different constituent stiffnesses, normalizing by the constituent modulus enables direct comparison: E∗=EEs, with E s, print = 3 GPa and E s, bone = 5 GPa (Figure 4b). Because only one selected architecture and one printed replica were tested per condition, this comparison is presented as a proof‐of‐concept architectural demonstration rather than a statistically replicated validation. The osteoporotic architecture remains more compliant than the healthy architecture despite being fabricated from the same material, supporting the interpretation that mechanical competence is strongly influenced by architecture rather than composition alone.

This comparison complements lacunar‐scale findings by showing that connectivity effects persist across hierarchical levels. Trabecular topology governs millimeter‐scale stiffness, while lacunar topology governs microscale stiffness sensitivity and corridor‐guided failure. These results support a unified multiscale picture in which stiffness loss is governed not only by reduced density but by architectural connectivity spanning from trabecular organization down to the lacunar network.

Multimodal Surrogate Enables Fast Prediction and Mapping of Local Mechanical Competence

While micromechanical simulations and the proof‐of‐concept 3D‐printed comparison support the proposed architecture–mechanics relationship, they are not scalable for rapid evaluation of large bone regions, and density‐only metrics (e.g., BMD) cannot capture microscale architectural effects. A multimodal surrogate was therefore trained to infer local mechanical properties directly from lacunar tiles by fusing an image‐based encoder with physics‐aligned lacunar descriptors (Figure 4c). The model fuses two complementary inputs: an image encoder that learns pixel‐level morphology and connectivity patterns, and a structured set of physically interpretable tabular lacunar descriptors (i.e., local lacunar density and shape statistics). These tabular descriptors are derived from lacuna segmentation masks predicted by a CNN model adopted from Buccino et al. that was trained on a library of SR‐µCT images processed through a standardized postprocessing workflow (denoising, threshold‐based bone masking, morphological cleanup, and expert‐labeled lacunae) [43]. The surrogate is trained on 160 × 160 µm2 tiles drawn from both real SR‐µCT data and synthetic microstructures, each paired with effective properties from elastic‐network simulations (Experimental Section). The fused model consistently outperforms image‐only and feature‐only baselines for predicting Young's modulus, strength, and toughness (Figure 4d), indicating that pixel‐level morphology and interpretable descriptors provide complementary, nonredundant information. Parity plots show strong agreement between surrogate predictions and simulation ground truth across the full range of values (Figure 4e).

Applied in a sliding‐window fashion, the surrogate produces spatial maps of local stiffness that reveal pronounced microscale heterogeneity: compliant regions coincide with lacuna‐rich zones, whereas stiffer regions track continuous mineral corridors (Figure 4f). These recovered patterns mirror the connectivity‐driven stress localization identified in simulations and experiments, indicating that the surrogate internalizes the same architectural mechanisms governing lacunar‐scale stiffness.

Conclusion

This work identifies an AI‐revealed, scale‐dependent transition in hierarchical porous mechanics, using trabecular bone as a model biological material whose architecture spans open‐cell lattices and embedded‐void networks. While trabecular‐scale stiffness follows classical density‐controlled cellular‐solid scaling, lacunar‐scale stiffness becomes connectivity‐controlled: overlap of void‐induced stress fields reorganizes load transfer into system‐spanning weak corridors, amplifying stiffness sensitivity and guiding crack propagation. By combining in situ synchrotron tomography with class‐conditional diffusion generation and micromechanics, expanded microstructural ensembles are obtained under data‐limited imaging, enabling quantitative scaling analysis and mechanistic interpretation beyond what density alone can provide.

From a biomedical perspective, these findings motivate the future development of connectivity‐aware descriptors of bone mechanical competence beyond density alone. However, the present study is primarily mechanistic rather than diagnostic. Each cohort contained five female donors, and the COVID‐19–affected cohort was heterogeneous with respect to clinical presentation and time since infection. In addition, images and individual lacunae were nested within donors, AI‐generated images increased microstructural sampling without increasing biological replication, and the 3D‐printing comparison involved one selected architecture per condition. Larger, prospectively characterized cohorts with donor‐level statistical validation and replicated physical testing will therefore be required before this framework can be evaluated for disease classification, fracture‐risk assessment, or patient‐specific clinical applications. The present results should therefore be interpreted as identifying a mechanistic link between lacunar architecture and local mechanical competence, rather than establishing a clinically powered diagnostic marker. Moreover, the ability to predict spatially resolved mechanical properties from multimodal inputs offers new opportunities for the design and optimization of orthopedic implants, scaffolds, and biomaterials that better match local bone mechanical functions [50].

Although established here in bone, the mechanism is not bone‐specific. A transition from density‐ to connectivity‐controlled mechanics is expected whenever defects are embedded within a continuous load‐bearing matrix and their interaction range becomes comparable to defect spacing, so that stress fields overlap and redundant load paths are rapidly lost. This condition arises broadly in hierarchical porous solids and architected materials, including additively manufactured lattices containing process‐induced pores, sintered ceramics with intergranular void networks, cementitious and geological materials with coupled pore‐microcrack populations, and porous polymers with evolving damage cavities. The AI‐enabled ensemble paradigm introduced here provides a general route to (i) learn physically realistic microstructure distributions under limited imaging, (ii) identify the regime boundaries where scaling laws change, and (iii) generate connectivity‐aware property maps and surrogates for design and mechanical assessment. More broadly, the results suggest that robust prediction of mechanical competence in hierarchical porous materials will require connectivity‐resolved descriptors, beyond volume fraction, particularly near intermediate regimes where sensitivity peaks and failure precursors emerge.

Experimental Section

Trabecular Bone Sample Collection and Preparation

Human femoral heads were collected from patients undergoing primary hip arthroplasty at San Raffaele Hospital (Milan, Italy), with institutional Ethics Committee approval (13 May 2020) and written informed consent from all participants (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT04787679). Trabecular bone specimens were obtained from age‐matched female donors and grouped into three cohorts: healthy controls, osteoporotic, and COVID‐19–affected. Inclusion criteria included age ≥18 years, primary hip replacement, and availability of a routine preoperative CT scan. After fixation, segments were embedded in epoxy, maintained in saline, and machined into 4 × 4 × 14 mm specimens for mechanical testing and synchrotron imaging. Cohort demographics and clinical characteristics (age, sex, T‐score, and BV/TV) are summarized in Table S1, and full clinical criteria and sample handling protocols are provided in the Supporting Information.

In Situ Synchrotron Mechanical Testing and IGFA

In situ mechanical testing was performed at the ELETTRA synchrotron (Trieste, Italy) using a custom Micro‐Compression Device (MCD). After an initial preload of 2–3 N, specimens were tested under displacement‐controlled compression in incremental steps of 0.1 mm at 0.1 mm s− 1, with each step followed by a 3 min relaxation period at fixed displacement. Load was recorded at 10 Hz and monitored in real time. IGFA alternated tomographic scans with micro‐compression steps to visualize crack nucleation, propagation, and final failure.

Propagation‐based phase‐contrast SR‐µCT was conducted at the SYRMEP beamline using a white‐beam configuration. Samples were positioned 150 mm from the detector and irradiated with 25.6 keV X‐rays using a 1.5 mm silicon filter. Projections were recorded with a 16‐bit water‐cooled Orca Flash 4.0 sCMOS camera (2048 × 2048 pixels) coupled to a 17 µm GGG scintillator. Scans employed half‐acquisition over 360° in fly mode (voxel size 1.6 µm, exposure 0.1 s, 1800 projections, field of view 3.28 × 3.28 mm2). Flat‐field and dark‐field images were acquired for correction. The estimated radiation dose was ∼165 Gy, below levels reported to affect bone mechanical properties. Projections were processed using a single‐distance phase‐retrieval approach with δ/β = 20 ± 1 (optimized for crack and lacunae visualization) and reconstructed using filtered back‐projection in SYRMEP Tomo Project (STP) v1.41. Additional acquisition parameters and validation procedures are provided in Supporting Information.

Class Conditional Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model

A ccDDPM was implemented for single‐channel SR‐µCT trabecular cross‐sections and conditioned on three clinical class labels: healthy, osteoporotic, and COVID‐19–affected. Training used a cosine noise schedule with 1000 diffusion steps (offset s = 0.008) and a v‐prediction objective for improved stability on grayscale images. The denoiser was a compact U‐Net with an encoder–decoder structure (channels 64 → 128 → 256 → 512, mirrored during upsampling) and skip connections. Each stage contained two residual blocks with GroupNorm and SiLU activations, and single‐head self‐attention was inserted at 64 × 64 resolution and at the 32 × 32 bottleneck to improve long‐range coherence. Conditioning was injected via FiLM at each residual block using a sinusoidal time embedding combined with a learned class embedding. Classifier‐free guidance was implemented by replacing the class label with a null token in 10% of training batches and applied during sampling (see Supporting Information and Figure S9). Training used AdamW (learning rate 3 × 10− 4, weight decay 10− 4) with automatic mixed precision, global gradient‐norm clipping (0.5), and an exponential moving average of weights (decay 0.999) for evaluation and sampling. Image fidelity was evaluated using FID and morphology statistics.

Lacuna Segmentation and Morphological Descriptors

Lacunae were segmented from 2D SR‐µCT slices using a CNN trained on a library of synchrotron images spanning all three classes and processed through a standardized workflow (denoising, threshold‐based bone masking, and morphological cleanup; Supporting Information). Two descriptors were extracted from both real and synthetic images: lacunar density, defined as the lacunar area fraction (total lacunar area divided by mineralized matrix area within each cross‐section), and oblateness, defined as the minor‐to‐major axis ratio from an ellipse fit to each lacuna as a proxy for shape anisotropy.

Multiple images and individual lacunae were obtained from each donor and were therefore treated as nested observations. Because each clinical cohort contained five independent donors, image‐ and lacuna‐level p‐values are reported only as exploratory comparisons. These statistics were used descriptively to evaluate class‐wise morphology trends and real–synthetic agreement, not as independently powered patient‐level clinical hypothesis tests.

Elastic‐Network Simulation and Stiffness Extraction

Real and synthetic SR‐µCT images were used to extract 2D tiles of size 160 × 160 µm2, randomly cropped from full trabecular cross‐sections to avoid manual selection bias. Cropping was performed within the segmented mineralized bone mask (threshold‐based bone masking and morphological cleanup; Section 4.4 and Supporting Information), such that each accepted tile lay fully inside mineralized trabeculae and contained no marrow/background pixels. Tiles intersecting marrow voids or trabecular boundaries were rejected and resampled. For each physiopathological class, 500 tiles were sampled from the real dataset and 500 from the synthetic dataset (3000 tiles total). Each tile was binarized and converted into a 2D bead–spring elastic network aligned to the image grid (hexagonal/triangular lattice), with pixels labeled as lacunae removed to form voids and bone pixels forming the connected network. Springs followed a Morse potential (parameters in Table S2) calibrated to reproduce trabecular tissue–like stiffness and strength, ensuring that simulated differences arose from geometry/topology rather than constituent‐property variation [51]. All 3000 tiles were subjected to the same elastic‐network simulation procedure. Synchrotron‐derived and synthetic results were retained with source labels so that their mechanical responses and stiffness–density scaling could first be evaluated separately.

Micromechanical simulations were performed in LAMMPS under uniaxial tension with periodic boundary conditions to approximate an infinite medium. Networks were energy‐minimized (conjugate gradient) and equilibrated at 300 K using a Nosé–Hoover thermostat. Tension was imposed via box deformation at a constant engineering strain rate with incremental loading and relaxation between steps (details in Supporting Information). Effective Young's modulus was defined as the initial linear slope of the stress–strain response. Per‐atom virial stress components were recorded and converted to local equivalent stress using the nominal nodal volume of the triangular lattice. In addition, incremental local strain energy density was calculated from the change in per‐atom conservative potential energy relative to the equilibrated unloaded configuration. The resulting fields were visualized in OVITO. Crack initiation and propagation were governed directly by progressive Morse‐bond extension and rupture. Von Mises stress and strain energy density were used only as visualization metrics and were not imposed as failure criteria.

3D Printing of Trabecular Bone Replica

To isolate architectural effects from composition, macroscopic trabecular replicas were fabricated by additive manufacturing. VOI were extracted from SR‐µCT scans of healthy, COVID‐19–affected, and osteoporotic donors and selected to have comparable BV/TV values (28.3, 27.4, and 28.7, respectively; BV/TV computed volumetrically as V bone/V totalfrom the segmented 3D VOIs). The segmented trabecular architectures were converted to surface meshes, uniformly scaled, and 3D printed using an Objet260 Connex3 (Stratasys) printer with VeroBlue resin, a rigid photopolymer with near‐isotropic elastic properties. All specimens were printed under identical conditions and cleaned to remove support material. One replica was fabricated and mechanically tested for each selected architecture (n = 1 per condition); therefore, this experiment was interpreted as a proof‐of‐concept architectural comparison rather than a statistically replicated validation. Compression tests were then performed under displacement control to measure effective stiffness (setup details in Figure S8 and Supporting Information).

Multimodal Surrogate Model for Mechanical Prediction

A multimodal neural network was trained to predict local mechanical properties—Young's modulus, ultimate strength, and toughness (defined as the area under the stress–strain curve)—from lacunar tiles and associated lacunar descriptors. The surrogate dataset comprised the simulated structure–property pairs from both synchrotron‐derived and synthetic tiles. Source labels and physiopathological classes were retained during dataset construction, and the data were divided into training, validation, and test subsets before model fitting. The architecture fused image embeddings extracted with a ResNet‐based encoder with a feature vector encoded by a multilayer perceptron (MLP), enabling joint learning from spatial morphology and engineered descriptors. Images were standardized to the range [−1, 1], and tabular descriptors were standardized feature‐wise using training‐set statistics. The model was trained using a multi‐task Huber loss, and image‐only and feature‐only baselines were implemented for ablation. Training used AdamW (learning rate 3 × 10− 4, weight decay 10− 4), a batch size of 64, and up to 300 epochs with early stopping (patience 20). Complete training details and validation procedures are provided in the Supporting Information.
