PEIRA : Learning Predictive Encoders through Inter-View Regressor Alignment
Michael ArbelUniv. Grenoble Alpes, InriaCNRS, Grenoble INP, LJK michael.arbel@inria.fr, Basile TerverEcole Normale Supérieure / PSLInria Paris basile.terver@ens.psl.edu Jean PonceEcole Normale Supérieure / PSLNew York University jean.ponce@ens.fr

Abstract
Non-contrastive self-supervised learning (SSL) is an effective framework for predictive representation learning, but popular (and in practice effective) methods such as SimSiam, BYOL, I-JEPA or DINO, which rely on a form of self-distillation to train a teacher-student network, remain poorly understood as they typically do not minimize a well-defined objective. We analyze the dynamics of a variant of the Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) using a regularized linear regressor to predict the learned representations of two views of the data from one another, and fully characterize its stability: non-collapsed stable equilibria align with leading nonlinear canonical correlation subspaces, while collapsed equilibria may also be stable attractors. Motivated by this result, we introduce PEIRA, a non-contrastive SSL method with an explicit objective defined through the trace of the optimal linear regressor. We show that its only stable equilibria are nontrivial global minimizers and recover the same canonical correlation subspaces, with regularization selecting the effective dimension. Experiments on ImageNet-1K and CIFAR-10 show PEIRA is competitive with VICReg and LeJEPA baselines, and qualitative empirical results support the theory.

Introduction

Non-contrastive
self-supervised learning has emerged as a successful approach to representation learning, achieving strong empirical performance across vision and video understanding
[
Grill et al. 2020
,
Caron et al. 2021
,
Chen and He 2021
,
Bardes et al. 2022
,
Bardes et al. 2024
,
Assran et al. 2025
]
.
In this setting, one learns encoders of different views of the same data so the corresponding features predict each other using a learned inter-view regressor, thereby avoiding the need to mine negative examples as in
contrastive
methods
[
Chen et al. 2020
,
He et al. 2020
]
. These approaches are, however, susceptible to
representational collapse
, in which the learned encoders and predictor respectively degenerate to constants and the identity
[
LeCun 2022
]
. A large body of work has studied and sought to prevent collapse.
Among non-contrastive methods, one can distinguish
regularization-based
approaches, such as Barlow Twins
[
Zbontar et al. 2021
]
, VICReg
[
Bardes et al. 2022
]
, and LeJEPA
[
Balestriero and LeCun 2025
]
, from
self-distillation
methods, such as SimSiam
[
Chen and He 2021
]
, BYOL
[
Grill et al. 2020
]
, DINO
[
Caron et al. 2021
,
Oquab et al. 2023
,
Siméoni et al. 2025
]
, I-JEPA
[
Assran et al. 2023
]
and V-JEPA
[
Bardes et al. 2024
]
.
The latter use mechanisms such as stop-gradient and exponential moving averages, leading to dynamics that differ from standard gradient descent and may avoid collapse
[
Tian et al. 2021
]
.
Regularization-based methods, like VICReg or Barlow Twins, have been connected to the recovery of canonical correlation subspaces
[
Balestriero and LeCun 2022
,
Chapman et al. 2024
]
, providing a useful interpretation of the representations they learn.
By contrast,
self-distillation
methods remain poorly understood despite their empirical success: beyond restricted analyses
[
Tian et al. 2021
,
Tang et al. 2023
,
Littwin et al. 2024
,
Ponce et al. 2026
]
, the learned representations and their collapse behavior are still not fully characterized.

In this work, we close this gap for a self-distillation variant of JEPA dynamics that learns nonlinear encoders coupled with an optimal regularized linear predictor. We establish, to our knowledge, the first rigorous connection between these dynamics and nonlinear canonical correlation analysis (CCA)
[
Michaeli et al. 2015
]
. The dynamics converge to representations spanning a nonlinear CCA subspace, with effective dimension selected implicitly by regularization. We characterize stable equilibria, identifying those that encode predictive structure and those that collapse. This extends prior analyses of non-contrastive self-distillation, which are restricted to linear encoders and assumptions such as isotropic covariance or orthogonal initialization
[
Tian et al. 2021
,
Tang et al. 2023
,
Littwin et al. 2024
]
.
The characterization motivates a new unconstrained objective,
Predictive Encoders by Inter-View Regressor Alignment
(
PEIRA
), for JEPA-style learning, with the same statistical target that rules out collapse as a stable solution, both theoretically and empirically.
PEIRA
aligns encoders by maximizing the trace of the best regularized linear predictor between views while controlling feature scale.
We analyze its dynamics and equilibria, showing that it recovers the same nonlinear CCA subspaces as the considered self-distillation dynamics, again with regularization-induced spectral selection, while excluding collapse through the objective rather than training heuristics. Finally, we give a scalable stochastic implementation for parametric encoders inspired by stochastic compositional optimization
[
Wang et al. 2017
]
, which avoids differentiating through the optimal predictor and achieves competitive performance on SSL image representation learning. Specifically, the main contributions of this paper are threefold:

1. We present a full theoretical characterization of the dynamics of
JEPA-style self-distillation learning in terms of canonical correlation
analysis (CCA) when the predictor is linear and the encoders are
arbitrary square-integrable functions. This includes identifying all
equilibria of the corresponding dynamics
(
Proposition

4.1
)
and
characterizing the stable ones, which include the collapsed zero
solution
(
Proposition

4.2
)
.

2. We introduce PEIRA, a novel approach to self-supervised learning with a well-defined unconstrained objective. We characterize the equilibria of its dynamics and show that PEIRA recovers the same CCA subspaces as self-distillation
(
Proposition

4.3
)
. Unlike self-distillation, however, all stable equilibria of PEIRA are nontrivial global minimizers of the objective
(
Proposition

4.4
)
.

3. We propose a scalable stochastic training procedure for
PEIRA

(
Figure

1
)
, for parametric encoders (in practice neural networks), and demonstrate in
Section

6
that it delivers competitive performance with respect to baselines like VICReg and LeJEPA on ImageNet-1K and CIFAR-10 benchmarks.
Experiments on these benchmarks also confirm the theoretical claims about spectral alignment, show strong correlation between the PEIRA objective and downstream performance, and demonstrate robustness of the method to variations in hyperparameter values.

Related work

Canonical correlation analysis and self-supervised learning.

CCA is the classical framework for extracting maximally correlated components across paired views under a whitening constraint
[
Hardoon et al. 2004
,
Andrew et al. 2013
,
Fukumizu et al. 2007
]
. Recently, several SSL methods have incorporated CCA-inspired structure to prevent collapse. Whitening-based losses
[
Ermolov et al. 2021
]
and their analysis
[
Huang et al. 2024
]
do so through explicit decorrelation, while penalized relaxations
[
Zhang et al. 2021
,
Chang et al. 2018
]
improve practicality but lose exact CCA recovery guarantees.
Chapman et al. 2024
introduced an unconstrained objective for CCA with provable recovery in the linear setting, and applied it to SSL. However, its quartic (4th order polynomial) dependence on the representation can make optimization prone to instabilities
[
Shi et al. 2023
,
De Sa et al. 2015
]
.
A few works also established connections between some existing SSL methods and CCA.
Balestriero and LeCun 2022
show that VICReg and Barlow Twins recover spectral embedding methods, while
Chapman et al. 2024
further rigorously establish that VICReg recovers CCA subspaces, in the linear setting, and show that it can still collapse. Similarly,
Lyu et al. 2021
show that maximizing correlation under decorrelation constraints recovers shared components, and discuss similarities with methods such as BYOL and Barlow Twins. However, these works stop short of providing a formal CCA recovery theory for self-distillation methods.

Self-distillation methods for non-contrastive SSL.

Theory for these methods remains confined to highly simplified regimes, typically linear models with isotropic data or orthogonal initialization
[
Tian et al. 2021
,
Tang et al. 2023
,
Littwin et al. 2024
]
.
Tang et al. 2023
have further shown that, provided the training procedure converges, the corresponding equilibrium spans a linear eigenspace of the cross-covariance matrix. Related analyses with shallow neural networks still rely on simplified data models
[
Wen and Li 2022
]
. Extending these results is difficult: even in the linear case, relaxing isotropy leads to hard-to-characterize equilibria and unclear convergence, since the dynamics are not the gradient flow of any global objective
[
Ponce et al. 2026
]
.

3
From Self-Distillation to
PEIRA

Following the JEPA approach to self-supervised representation learning,
we are interested in learning encoders of different views of the data such that the encoded views predict each other well.
In general, this problem can be formulated as follows:
Given a distribution
\mathbb{P}
of data pairs
(x,y)
(the views) in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
, the goal is to learn square-integrable and in general
nonlinear
encoders
U:\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
V:\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
for views
x
and
y
of the data using a predictive objective. We consider a regularized

symmetric
1

1

Tang et al. 2023
,
Grill et al. 2020
, and naturally accommodate views in different spaces. There is no loss of generality due to symmetrization and all our results can be adapted to asymmetric objectives, provided the views are interchangeable.
objective which involves a
linear
prediction operator
P:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
represented as a
k\times k
matrix

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{P}(P,U,V)=\mathcal{P}_{U\triangleright V}(P,U,V)+\mathcal{P}_{V\triangleright U}(P,V,U)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert P\right\rVert_{F}^{2},\end{gathered}

(1)

where
\left\lVert P\right\rVert_{F}
is the Frobenius norm of
P
, whereas
\mathcal{P}_{U\triangleright V}
and
\mathcal{P}_{V\triangleright U}
are directed objectives encouraging
P
to linearly predict one encoded view from the other.
Specifically, defining
\left\lVert U\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert U(x)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}]
and

\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert V(y)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}]
, we define these objectives as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{U\triangleright V}(P,U,V)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert PU(x)-V(y)\right\rVert^{2}\right]+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|U\|_{2}^{2},

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{V\triangleright U}(P,V,U)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert PV(y)-U(x)\right\rVert^{2}\right]+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|V\|_{2}^{2}.

where expectations are taken w.r.t. the data distribution
\mathbb{P}
. Here, the symbol
U\triangleright V
means that
V
is treated as a
target/teacher
view for prediction, whereas
U
is a
source/student
view.
Ideally, we would like to learn
P
,
U
and
V
by minimizing
\mathcal{P}
w.r.t. these variables.
Following
[
Tang et al. 2023
]
, we consider the fast-predictor regime where the regressor is optimized much faster than the encoders.
This regime will allow us to provide a precise characterization of equilibria of the dynamics under consideration, by exploiting a closed form expression of the regressor.
Namely, for each pair of encoders
(U,V)
, the regressor is set to its optimal value,

P_{U,V}=\arg\min_{P\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}}\mathcal{P}(P,U,V),

(3)

which can be computed in closed form as the solution of a ridge regression problem.
Thus, minimizing
\mathcal{P}
w.r.t.
P
,
U
and
V
is equivalent
to minimizing w.r.t.
U
and
V
the
residual
objective function

\mathcal{P}_{\text{res}}(U,V)=\mathcal{P}_{U\triangleright V}(P_{U,V},U,V)+\mathcal{P}_{V\triangleright U}(P_{U,V},V,U)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert P_{U,V}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

(4)

Optimizing either
\mathcal{P}
or
\mathcal{P}_{\text{res}}
is known to be prone to trivial solutions, e.g.,
U
and
V
and
P
are all equal to
0
, leading to representation
collapse.
To address this, self-distillation methods such as SimSiam, BYOL, V-JEPA or DINO update the encoders using a so-called
semi-gradient

[
Sutton and Barto 2018
, Chapter 9.3]
, that is, a proxy for the actual gradient obtained by applying either a stop-gradient operation or exponential moving averages to the
teacher
view. In this way, the
student
view is trained to predict the
teacher
view, rather than both views being updated through the full gradient of the objective.
Although this heuristic works well empirically, the learned representations remain poorly understood. In
Section

4.1
, we show that the self-distillation dynamics derived from the objective
\mathcal{P}_{\text{res}}
recovers nonlinear CCA subspaces, but is still prone to (partial) collapse.

We propose instead to minimize with respect to
U
and
V
a slightly different objective which also depends on the optimal regressor
P_{U,V}
but which
is guaranteed to
prevent collapse. Let us first note that
P_{U,V}
can be written in closed form as a matrix-valued
signal-to-noise ratio
(SNR):

P_{U,V}=\Sigma_{U,V}(N_{U,V}+\lambda I)^{-1},\,\,\text{where}\,\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\Sigma_{U,V}=\mathbb{E}[V(y)U(x)^{\top}+U(x)V(y)^{\top}],\\
N_{U,V}=\mathbb{E}[U(x)U(x)^{\top}+V(y)V(y)^{\top}],\\
\end{array}\right.

(5)

where the matrix
\Sigma_{U,V}
measures shared cross-view signal, while
N_{U,V}
measures within-view variability.
The trace of the predictor appears as a natural scalar summary of the SNR as it sums the contributions of different eigen-modes of
P_{U,V}
and maximizing it should favor representations that share most information between views. Thus, instead of
\mathcal{P}_{\text{res}}
, we propose to minimize the
PEIRA
(for Predictive Encoders through Inter-view Regressor Alignment)
objective:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(U,V)=-\frac{1}{2}\Tr\!\bigl(P_{U,V}\bigr)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(\left\lVert U\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right).

(6)

When both encoders are linear, minimizing the negative trace of
P_{U,V}
is closely related to ratio-trace optimization for dimensionality reduction
[
Wang et al. 2007
,
Jia et al. 2009
]
discussed in
Appendix

A
.
The quadratic regularization on
U
and
V
controls feature scale, preventing the negative trace term from being decreased merely by enlarging their norms.
The trace of the regressor can be written as the Frobenius scalar product between the symmetric matrices
\Sigma_{U,V}
and
(N_{U,V}+\lambda I)^{-1}
. Therefore, minimizing
\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}
should also encourage an
alignment
between the eigenbases of the signal and noise matrices
\Sigma_{U,V}
and
N_{U,V}
. Such an alignment has been shown to arise implicitly for some self-distillation methods with linear encoders
[
Tian et al. 2021
]
.

4
Canonical structure and stability of the learning dynamics

We characterize the equilibria of the

self-distillation dynamics
arising from the objective
\mathcal{P}_{\text{res}}
in
Equation

4
and the critical points of
PEIRA
introduced in
Equation

6
in terms of nonlinear CCA.

Nonlinear CCA identifies pairs of functions of the two views that are maximally correlated after normalization by
whitening constraint (see
Appendix

A
for a formal definition).
These directions capture the shared signal between the views, whereas directions with small canonical correlation correspond to signal components that are only weakly correlated across views.
To make the characterizations in this section independent of any particular model architecture, we do not impose parametric restrictions on the encoders.
Instead, we work directly in the natural infinite dimensional function spaces of square-integrable encoders,
\mathcal{U}=L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X};\mathbb{R}^{k})
,
\mathcal{V}=L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{Y};\mathbb{R}^{k}),
where
\mathbb{P}_{X}
and
\mathbb{P}_{Y}
denote the marginals of the joint distribution
\mathbb{P}
.
Parametric models, such as deep networks, are introduced only later as function approximators in these spaces (see the
capacity hypothesis
in
[
Huh et al. 2024
]
).
We use the following spectral form of nonlinear CCA throughout the analysis.

Definition 4.1

(Nonlinear Canonical Correlations)
.

The distribution
\mathbb{P}
is said to admit a nonlinear canonical correlation decomposition, in the sense of
Michaeli et al. 2015
, if there exist orthonormal families
(\psi_{i})_{i\leq R}
and
(\xi_{i})_{1\leq i\leq R}
of
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
and
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{Y})
, and a non-increasing sequence
(c_{i})_{1\leq i\leq R}\in(0,1]
, where
R
may be infinite, such that
\mathbb{E}[\psi_{i}(x)\xi_{i}(y)]=c_{i}
and

\mathbb{E}[\psi_{i}(x)\xi_{j}(y)]=0\quad\text{for }i\neq j
.
Moreover, the families exhaust all cross-correlated directions: any square-integrable function of
x
orthogonal to all the
\psi_{i}
’s is uncorrelated with every square-integrable function of
y
, and conversely for functions of
y
orthogonal to all the
\xi_{i}
’s.
The functions
\psi_{i}
and
\xi_{i}
are called the nonlinear canonical directions, and the scalars
c_{i}
are the canonical correlations, which quantify the strength of the shared signal along the
i
-th direction.

\displaystyle U^{\star}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\sum_{i=1}^{r^{\sigma}}f_{1}(c_{i},\lambda)\psi_{i}(x){\bf{q_{i}^{\sigma}}},\quad V^{\star}(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\sum_{i=1}^{r^{\sigma}}\rho_{\sigma}f_{1}(c_{i},\lambda)\xi_{i}(y){\bf{q_{i}^{\sigma}}}

7
Conclusions

We have analyzed the JEPA dynamics in the simplified setting (but arguably closer to the practical case where
P
,
U
and
V
are all nonlinear) where the predictor is linear although the encoders are arbitrary nonlinear square-integrable functions, identified the corresponding equilibria and their stability, and proposed an alternative objective where maximizing the trace of the optimal predictor provably leads to nontrivial global optimizers. We have also demonstrated through preliminary experiments with standard benchmarks the promise of this approach. We now briefly discuss its limitations and future work.

Limitations.
From a theoretical viewpoint, we have assumed throughout an optimal predictor. Relaxing our analysis to suboptimal predictors is, in principle, possible using the theory of singularly perturbed systems
[
Habets 1974
,
Arbel and Mairal 2022
]
. This would also allow an analysis of discrete-time stochastic updates of
PEIRA
, but is left for future work. Our proofs further assume distinct canonical correlations which are simplifying assumptions that can be relaxed, but would result in more complicated characterization. From an empirical point of view, the experiments presented in this paper are preliminary. Although they have demonstrated competitive performance on benchmarks like ImageNet-1K, more thorough experiments are needed to establish
PEIRA
as a viable alternative to BYOL, SimSiam, VICReg, V-JEPA 2, and SIGReg for example, notably through experiments on more complex tasks such as video classification
[
Goyal et al. 2017
,
Kay et al. 2017
]
. Code will be released upon publication.

Future work.
Most (but not all) of our discussion and proofs are valid for encoders that belong to Hilbert spaces of infinite dimension, and predictors that are continuous linear operators over these. We plan to investigate next how much of the theory remains valid in this general setting and whether using reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces might lead to a practical computational solution.
As shown by recent work on so-called world models
[
Sobal et al. 2025
,
Terver et al. 2026b
]
, learning action models in the form of control encoders and conditioning predictors on these models has led to new approaches to robotic planning. We also plan to investigate how PEIRA could be used in this setting, the simplest but probably unsatisfactory instance being to concatenate the state and control features.

A.1
Nonlinear canonical correlation analysis

We start by briefly recalling population nonlinear CCA

[
Lancaster 1958
,
Michaeli et al. 2015
]

maximizing inter-view correlation under orthonormality constraints.
Specifically, these are solutions to the following constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{(U,V)\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[U(x)^{\top}V(y)\right],\qquad\text{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[U(x)U(x)^{\top}\right]=I,\quad\mathbb{E}\left[V(y)V(y)^{\top}\right]=I,

(16)

Such representations can be thought of as

in the sense that
V(y)
is most predictable from
U(x)
and vice versa.
We need the following mild regularity assumption on the data distribution to ensure nonlinear CCA admits a spectral characterization
[
Michaeli et al. 2015
]

(
A
)

The density ratio

\mathbb{P}
with respect to

\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{Y}
exists and is integrable under
\mathbb{P}
, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}[f(x,y)]<\infty
.

This is a mild assumption (see
Remark

A.1
) ruling out degenerate cases such as a deterministic dependence between

and
y
. Under
(A)
, CCA admits a spectral characterization in terms of the canonical directions
(\psi_{i},\xi_{i})_{1\leq i\leq R}
introduced in

4.1

Specifically, for
k\leq R
, a solution to the CCA problem is obtained by stacking the first
k

canonical directions:

\displaystyle U(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}\psi_{i}(x)\qquad V(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}\xi_{i}(y),

(17)

where
(f_{i})_{1\leq i\leq k}
are the columns of the
k\times k
identity matrix. Accordingly, the top-
k
canonical subspaces are the spans of these directions.

Remark A.1
.

(A)
holds, for example, on compact domains when

and

are obtained by independently perturbing latent paired samples

through kernels whose densities are uniformly bounded above and below. Indeed, if

and

f(x,y)=\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}\leq\frac{M_{X}M_{Y}}{m_{X}m_{Y}}

\displaystyle\left\lvert\langle w,Aw^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\left\lVert w^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}},\qquad\forall\,w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}.

is compact and admits a spectral decomposition. Specifically, letting
I=\mathbb{N}
if
\mathcal{H}
has infinite dimensions or
I=\{1,\dots,\dim(\mathcal{H})\}
otherwise, there exists an orthonormal basis
(\zeta_{i})_{i\in I}
of
\mathcal{H}
and eigenvalues
(s_{i})_{i\in I}

satisfying
A\zeta_{i}=s_{i}\zeta_{i}
.
Let
I_{+}
be the index set of positive eigenvalues, and
\sigma
be an ordering of the indices in
I_{+}
such that
s_{\sigma(1)}\geq\dots\geq s_{\sigma(R)}>0
, with
R
possibly infinite (in which case
s_{\sigma_{j}}\rightarrow 0
). Set
c_{j}=s_{\sigma_{j}}
and
(\psi_{j},\xi_{j})=\sqrt{2}\zeta_{\sigma_{j}}
. Then if
c_{j}
are all distinct,
(\psi_{j})_{j=1}^{R}
and
(\xi_{j})_{j=1}^{R}
are orthonormal families of
L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
and
L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{Y})
satisfying
\mathbb{E}\left[\psi_{j}(x)\xi_{j^{\prime}}(y)\right]=c_{j}\delta_{j=j^{\prime}}
and that exhaust all cross-correlated directions (see
Definition

4.1
).
Moreover, any index
i\in I
corresponding to negative eigenvalue is of the form
\left\lvert s_{i}\right\rvert=c_{j}
for some
j\in\{1,\dots,R\}
, and its eigenvector
\zeta_{i}
satisfies
\sqrt{2}\zeta_{i}=(\psi_{j},-\xi_{j})
. The converse is also true: for any index
j\in\{1,\dots,R\}
there exists an index
i\in I
with negative eigenvalue
s_{i}
satisfying
\left\lvert s_{i}\right\rvert=c_{j}
.

A.3
Top-
k
subspaces and ratio-trace optimization.

As illustrated through CCA, one often only needs to recover a
top-
k
subspace representation
, i.e. an element
W=(U,V)\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V}
whose
k
components span the same subspace as the
k
largest generalized eigenvalues of a given GEVD problem.
For a GEVD problem of finite dimension
D
and when
A\succeq 0
, this subspace was characterized as the span of rows of any maximizer of the ratio-trace objective

[
Wang et al. 2007
,
Jia et al. 2009
]
:

\displaystyle\max_{W\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times D}}Tr\Big((WAW^{\top})(WBW^{\top})^{-1}\Big).

(20)

While
Equation

20
appears to offer an appealing route to direct GEVP via iterative optimization, it is
basis-invariant
, i.e. for any invertible
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
,
W
and
QW
attain the same value. Consequently, the above objective is flat along re-parameterizations within the optimal subspace and is typically ill-conditioned for iterative optimization.

The regularization we introduce in the linear predictor considered by
PEIRA
results in a modified
ratio-trace
objective that breaks this invariance and becomes smooth. The resulting criterion selects a particular subset of optimal subspaces, generally without enforcing
B
-orthonormality.

Proposition B.3

(

(

(

Coercivity.

(

Lower bound.

(

(

The dynamical system in

are given by:

\displaystyle g(s,\lambda)=\begin{cases}\left(\sqrt{s}-\lambda\right)_{+}^{\frac{1}{2}}&s\geq 0\\
0&s<0\end{cases},\qquad f_{\epsilon}(s,\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\lvert s\right\rvert+\epsilon\sqrt{s^{2}-4\lambda}\right)\mathds{1}_{s^{2}-4\lambda\geq 0}.

(44)

The functions
g
and
f_{\epsilon}
are spectral filters as they can filter out some eigendirections based on the value of the corresponding eigenvalue. For the case
\kappa=1
, we will call
f_{1}
the
stable filter
and
f_{-1}
the
unstable filter
for reasons that will be clear in
Section

B.5
.

The key idea behind the proof of
Proposition

B.6
is to obtain an algebraic equation satisfied by the operator
M=(\mathbf{w}^{\star})^{\top}\mathbf{w}^{\star}
. From such equation, we then show that a certain polynomial of
M
must commute with the diagonal operator
S
(containing the eigenvalues of the cross-covariance operator
A
). The fact that all eigenvalues of
S
are distinct ensures that such polynomial must also be diagonal. Then, after showing that the polynomial defines a bijective spectral map, we are able to deduce that
M
must also be diagonal. This diagonal structure implies that the fixed point
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
must satisfy an orthogonality constraint which allows decoupling the critical point equation it satisfies into scalar equations. The fact that
M
is of rank at most
k
implies that at most
k
of these equations can have non-zero solution.
Solving these equations explicitly yields the desired characterization.

B.5
Stability Analysis of Critical Points of
F^{\kappa}

We are now interested in characterizing which critical points of the vector field
F^{\kappa}
are stable for a dynamics of the form
\dot{\mathbf{w}}=-\lambda F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})
, i.e. critical points for which the Jacobian of
F^{\kappa}
has only non-negative real parts of the eigenvalues, and those that are unstable (negative real part).
The key ingredient to obtain such a characterization is to find a diagonalization of the Jacobian
\nabla F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})
at any critical point
\mathbf{w}
of
F^{\kappa}
which allows studying stability: either verify that all eigenvalues are non-negative (stable equilibrium) or identify a negative eigenvalue (unstable equilibrium).

Eigen decomposition of the Jacobian of the vector field
F^{\kappa}
.

The following proposition, whose proof is deferred to
Section

F.2
, provides the desired decomposition.

Proposition B.7

(Diagonalization of the Jacobian at critical points)
.

Assume all non-zero eigenvalues of
S
to be distinct and let
\mathbf{w}
be a critical point of
F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})
, which, by virtue of
Proposition

B.6
, necessarily has the form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}=Q\underbrace{\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}E_{p,i(p)}\right)}_{\mathbf{w}_{0}}M^{\frac{1}{2}},

(45)

where
Q
is an orthogonal matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
,
r
is an integer between
0
and
k
,
I_{sup}=\{i(1),\dots,i(r)\}\subset I_{+}\cup I_{-}
an increasing sequence of integers greater or equal to
1
and
M
is a positive semi-definite diagonal operator on
\ell^{2}(I)
whose only non-zero diagonal coefficients
(m_{i(p)})_{1\leq p\leq r}
are positive roots of the equations
(x+\lambda)^{2}=x^{\kappa}s_{i(p)}^{\kappa+1}
of unknown
x
for
1\leq p\leq r
.
Then the Jacobian
\nabla F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})
at
\mathbf{w}
of the vector field admits an eigendecomposition of the form:
(\bar{E}_{p,j},\mu_{p,j})_{1\leq p\leq k,j\in I}
, satisfying:

\displaystyle\bar{E}_{p,j}=\begin{cases}QE_{p,j},&p>r\text{ or }j\notin I_{sup}\text{ or }(p\leq r\text{ and }j=i(p))\\
(1-c_{p,p^{\prime}})^{\frac{1}{2}}QE_{p,i(p^{\prime})}+c_{p,p^{\prime}}^{\frac{1}{2}}QE_{p^{\prime},i(p)},&p,p^{\prime}\leq r\text{ and }j=i(p^{\prime})>i(p)\\
c_{p,p^{\prime}}^{\frac{1}{2}}QE_{p,i(p^{\prime})}-(1-c_{p,p^{\prime}})^{\frac{1}{2}}QE_{p^{\prime},i(p)},&p,p^{\prime}\leq r\text{ and }j=i(p^{\prime})<i(p),\end{cases}

(46)

\displaystyle\mu_{p,j}=\begin{cases}1-\lambda^{-2}s_{j}\mathds{1}_{\kappa=0},&j\notin I_{sup}\text{ and }p>r\\
\delta_{p,j},&j\notin I_{sup}\text{ and }p\leq r\\
\frac{4m_{j}}{m_{j}+\lambda}-2\mathds{1}_{\kappa=1},&j=i(p)\text{ and }p\leq r\\
\gamma_{p,p^{\prime}},&p,p^{\prime}\leq r\text{ and }j=i(p^{\prime})>i(p)\\
0,&p^{\prime}\leq r\text{ and }j=i(p^{\prime})\text{ and }\left(\left(p\leq r\text{ and }j<i(p)\right)\text{ or }p>r\right)\end{cases}

(47)

with
c_{p,p^{\prime}}
,
\gamma_{p,p^{\prime}}
and
\delta_{p,j}
given by:

\displaystyle c_{p,p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{i(p^{\prime})}}{m_{i(p^{\prime})}+m_{i(p)}},

(48)

\displaystyle\gamma_{p,p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(m_{i(p)}+m_{i(p^{\prime})}\right)\left(\left\lvert s_{i(p)}\right\rvert^{\frac{\kappa+1}{2}}m_{i(p)}^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}+\left\lvert s_{i(p^{\prime})}\right\rvert^{\frac{\kappa+1}{2}}m_{i(p^{\prime})}^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}-s_{i(p)}s_{i(p^{\prime})}\mathds{1}_{\kappa=1}\right)}{\left(m_{i(p)}+\lambda\right)\left(m_{i(p^{\prime})}+\lambda\right)}

\displaystyle\delta_{p,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{(s_{i(p)}-s_{j})}{\lambda(m_{i(p)}+\lambda)}\left(1+\left(m_{i(p)}s_{i(p)}-1\right)\mathds{1}_{\kappa=1}\right).

A striking fact about the above proposition is that most eigenvectors are simply
rotated
versions of the canonical vectors
E_{p,j}
, with rotation
Q
corresponding to the arbitrary orthogonal matrix defining the critical point
\mathbf{w}
. The remaining ones are linear combinations of two rotated versions of paired canonical vectors
E_{p,i(p^{\prime})}
and
E_{p^{\prime},i(p)}
on which
\mathbf{w}
admits non-zero components.
The main proof strategy here is to first derive the Jacobian expression at the critical point, and express it in terms of the operator
M=\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{w}
which must be diagonal by virtue of
Proposition

B.6
. The diagonal structure implies some terms involve commuting factors which are key to simplifying the expressions. Applying such Jacobian to each rotated canonical basis element of the form
\tilde{E}_{p,j}
and employing suitable linear algebra identities results in two types of expressions: either an expression colinear to the original element
\tilde{E}_{p,j}
, or if
j=i(p^{\prime})
and
p\leq r
it is a linear combination of it and another rotated canonical basis element
\tilde{E}_{p^{\prime},i(p)}
. We then further show that the Jacobian is stable under the subspace spanned by those two elements, which allows recovering the eigenvectors and eigenvalues corresponding to such subspace.

Stability of equilibria.

Using the above eigen decomposition, we can study the stability of any critical point
\mathbf{w}
by investigating the sign of the eigenvalues associated to each eigenvector of the Jacobian of the vector field
F^{\kappa}
at that point. The following propositions, proven in
Section

F.3
, provide such characterization for each case
\kappa=0
and
\kappa=1
separately.

Proposition B.8

(Stability of maximal top-
r
critical points
(\kappa=0)
)
.

Assume
\kappa=0
,
0<\lambda<1
,
k\geq 1
, and that all non-zero eigenvalues of
S
are distinct.
Let
r_{\mathrm{max}}
be the largest integer such that
0<r_{\mathrm{max}}\leq\min(k,R)
and
\sqrt{s_{i(r_{\mathrm{max}})}}>\lambda
and denote by
I_{sup}=\{i(1),\dots,i(r_{\mathrm{max}})\}
the set of indices of the
r_{\mathrm{max}}
largest positive eigenvalues of
A
by decreasing order.
Let
M
be the positive semi-definite diagonal operator on
\ell^{2}(I)
whose only positive diagonal elements
(m_{i(p)})_{1\leq p\leq r_{\mathrm{max}}}
are given by:

\displaystyle m_{i(p)}=\sqrt{s_{i(p)}}-\lambda.

(49)

Then, for any orthogonal matrix
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
, the following critical point is stable for
F^{0}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}=Q\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}E_{p,i(p)}\right)M^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(50)

Conversely, the only stable critical points of
F^{0}
are of the above form for any orthogonal matrix
Q
. All other critical points of
F^{0}
are unstable as the Jacobian of
F^{0}
at these points necessarily admits a negative eigenvalue.

The proof requires verifying that all eigenvalues of the identified stable critical points are non-negative. To verify all the other critical points are unstable, we identify precisely unstable directions which are of two types depending on the form of the critical points: type I or II.
Type I critical points are those that skip an eigen direction of
A
of index
j_{0}
associated to a positive eigenvalue
s_{j_{0}}
while including another direction
i(p_{0})
with smaller eigenvalue
s_{i(p_{0})}
. These possess an unstable direction associated to negative eigenvalue
\mu_{p_{0},j_{0}}
of
\nabla F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})
.
Type II critical points contain
r<k
eigen components without gap, but still miss an eigen direction
j_{0}
with small positive eigenvalue that is not filtered out by the spectral filter, i.e.
g(s_{j_{0}},\lambda)>0
, with
g(s,\lambda)=(\sqrt{s}-\lambda)_{+}^{\frac{1}{2}}
. These possess an unstable direction associated to negative eigenvalue
\mu_{r+1,j_{0}}
of
\nabla F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})
.

Proposition B.9

(Stability of the top-
r
critical points
(\kappa=1)
)
.

Assume
\kappa=1
,
\lambda\leq 1
and that all non-zero eigenvalues of
S
are distinct.
Let
r^{+}
and
r^{-}
be any non-negative integers in
[0,R]
such that
r=r^{+}+r^{-}\leq k
.
Denote by
I_{sup}^{+}=\{i(1),\dots,i(r^{+})\}
and
I_{sup}^{-}=\{i(r^{+}+1),\dots,i(r)\}
, the sets of indices of the
r^{+}
largest positive eigenvalues and
r^{-}
smallest negative eigenvalues of
A
satisfying
\left\lvert s_{j}\right\rvert\geq 2\sqrt{\lambda}
for all
j\in I_{sup}=I_{sup}^{+}\cup I_{sup}^{-}
.
We use the convention that
I_{sup}^{+}=\emptyset
if
r^{+}=0
and
I_{sup}^{-}=\emptyset
if
r^{-}=0
. Let
M
be the positive semi-definite diagonal operator on
\ell^{2}(I)
whose diagonal elements
m_{j}=0
for
j\notin I_{sup}
, whereas the elements
(m_{i(p)})_{1\leq p\leq r}
are given by:

\displaystyle m_{i(p)}^{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\lvert s_{i(p)}\right\rvert+\sqrt{s_{i(p)}^{2}-4\lambda}\right)\mathds{1}_{s_{i(p)}^{2}-4\lambda\geq 0}.

(51)

Then, for any orthogonal matrix
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
, the following critical point is stable for
F^{1}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}=Q\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}E_{p,i(p)}\right)M^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(52)

Conversely, the only stable critical points of
F^{1}
are of the above form for any orthogonal matrix
Q
and any
0\leq r^{+},r^{-}\leq R
satisfying
r^{+}+r^{-}\leq k
. All other critical points are unstable.

The proof of
Proposition

B.9
proceeds similarly as that of
Proposition

B.8
. To verify all the other critical points are unstable, we identify precisely unstable directions which are, again, of two types depending on the form of the critical points: type I or II.
Type I critical points are those that include an eigen direction of
A
of index
i(p_{0})
but weighted by the unstable spectral filter
f_{-1}
instead of the stable one
f_{1}
. These possess an unstable direction associated to negative eigenvalue
\mu_{p_{0},i(p_{0})}
of
\nabla F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})
.
Type II critical points all use the stable spectral filter
f_{1}
but present a gap in their spectra: they miss an eigen direction of index
j_{0}
of eigenvalue
s_{j_{0}}
but contain an eigendirection indexed by
i(p_{0})
of eigenvalue
s_{i(p_{0})}
with the same sign as
s_{j_{0}}
but smaller in absolute value:
\left\lvert s_{j_{0}}\right\rvert>\left\lvert s_{i(p_{0})}\right\rvert
. These critical points possess an unstable direction associated to negative eigenvalue
\mu_{p_{0},j_{0}}
of
\nabla F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})
.

C.1.2
Minimizers span thresholded canonical correlation subspaces

Proof of
Proposition

4.4
.

By
Proposition

B.1
, we have the following equivalence between the gradient flow
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in
\mathcal{W}
and an ODE defined on its components
(\mathbf{w}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in the eigenbasis of

:

\displaystyle\dot{W}_{t}=-\nabla\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(W_{t})\iff\dot{\mathbf{w}}_{t}=-\lambda F^{0}(\mathbf{w}_{t}),

(57)

where the vector field

is defined in

. This is a direct consequence of

which ensures that the only stable critical points of
F^{0}
are of the form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{\star}=\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}\left(\sqrt{s_{\sigma(i)}}-\lambda\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}_{+}q_{i}\otimes e_{\sigma(i)}

(58)

with
r_{\mathrm{max}}
the largest integer such that
\sqrt{s_{\sigma(r_{\mathrm{max}})}}>\lambda
and
r_{\mathrm{max}}\leq\min(R,k)
. These are the coordinates of the following elements
(U^{\star},V^{\star})\in\mathcal{W}
which are the critical points of

described in

4.3
:

\displaystyle U^{\star}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}q_{i}\left(\sqrt{c_{i}}-\lambda\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}_{+}\psi_{i}(x),\qquad V^{\star}(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}q_{i}\left(\sqrt{c_{i}}-\lambda\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}_{+}\xi_{i}(y).

(59)

We will now show that these stable solutions are precisely the global minimizers of
W\mapsto\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(W)
.
Since
(A)
holds, we know by
Proposition

A.1
that
A
is a compact operator and that there exists an orthonormal basis
\mathcal{B}=(\zeta_{i})_{i\in I}
of
\mathcal{H}
consisting of eigenvectors of
A
, i.e.
A\zeta_{i}=s_{i}\zeta_{i}
, with
(s_{i})_{i\in I}
being the eigenvalues of
A
.

We first treat the case where
k\leq dim(\mathcal{H})
.
Consider
W\in\mathcal{W}
. Such element can be viewed as a Hilbert-Schmidt operator from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. It is therefore compact, and thus, by the spectral theorem for compact operators
[
Gohberg et al. 2003

, it admits a singular value decomposition of the form:

\displaystyle W=Q\Sigma T^{*},

(60)

where
Q\in O(k)
,
\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k})
, and

T:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathcal{H}
is an isometric embedding. Then

\displaystyle WW^{T}=Q\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{k}^{2})Q^{*},

(61)

and, by cyclicity of the trace,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(W)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\Bigl(T^{*}AT\,\operatorname{diag}\Bigl(\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda}\Bigr)\Bigr)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma^{2}_{i}.

(62)

Moreover, let us decompose
A
into the difference of two positive semi-definite operators
A_{+}
and
A_{-}
, i.e.
A=A_{+}-A_{-}
, with
A_{+}
containing all non-negative eigendirections, while
A_{-}
contains the absolute value of the negative ones. More precisely,
A\zeta_{i}=A_{+}\zeta_{i}=s_{i}\zeta_{i}
and
A_{-}\zeta_{i}=0
for all indices

such that
s_{i}\geq 0
, whereas
A\zeta_{i}=-A_{-}\zeta_{i}=s_{i}\zeta_{i}
and

, for all indices such that

. It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\Bigl(T^{*}AT\,\operatorname{diag}\Bigl(\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda}\Bigr)\Bigr)=

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\Bigl(T^{*}(A_{+}-A_{-})T\,\operatorname{diag}\Bigl(\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda}\Bigr)\Bigr)

(63)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\Bigl(T^{*}A_{+}T\,\operatorname{diag}\Bigl(\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda}\Bigr)\Bigr),

since

is positive semi-definite.
For fixed
(\sigma_{i})
, the numbers

[
Grigorieff 1991

is upper-bounded by the sum of products of their singular values ordered by non-increasing order:

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\Bigl(T^{*}A_{+}T\,\operatorname{diag}\Bigl(\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda}\Bigr)\Bigr)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tilde{s}_{i}d_{\beta(i)},

(64)

where

\beta(1),\dots,\beta(k)
is a re-indexing to ensure that

Thus, by Courant-Fisher’s theorem

\displaystyle\sup_{T^{\star}T=I}\operatorname{tr}\Bigl(T^{*}A_{+}T\,\operatorname{diag}\Bigl(\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\lambda}\Bigr)\Bigr)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}s^{+}_{i}d_{\beta(i)}.

(65)

63

\displaystyle\inf_{W}\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(W)\geq\min_{\sigma_{1},\dots\sigma_{k}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(-s^{+}_{i}\frac{\sigma_{\beta(i)}^{2}}{\sigma_{\beta(i)}^{2}+\lambda}+\lambda\sigma^{2}_{\beta(i)}\right)=\min_{\sigma_{1},\dots\sigma_{k}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{s_{i}^{+}}(\sigma_{\beta(i)}),

(66)

(67)

\displaystyle U(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{i\in D}q_{i}\left(\sqrt{c_{i}}-\lambda\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}_{+}\psi_{i}(x),\qquad V(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{i\in D}q_{i}\left(\sqrt{c_{i}}-\lambda\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}_{+}\xi_{i}(y),

(71)

where

(q_{i})_{i\in D}
is an orthonormal family in

. Evaluating

at such point yields:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(W)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in D}(\sqrt{c_{i}}-\lambda)_{+}^{2}.

(72)

The above quantity is minimized exactly when

corresponds to the top
r_{\max}
-singular values of

, since these are assumed to be distinct. These are also equal to the top-

positive eigenvalues of

. Since, we assumed, without loss of generality that

The case when

C.1.3
The gradient of
PEIRA
’s objective matches that of the auxiliary loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}

Proof of

5.1
.

Recall the auxiliary loss defined in the proposition, which we can express in terms of the operator

:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}(U,V;P,Q)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left(\left(QP+\lambda I\right)\mathbb{E}\left[U(x)U(x)^{\top}+V(y)V(y)^{\top}\right]\right)

(73)

\displaystyle-\Tr\left(Q\mathbb{E}\left[U(x)V(y)^{\top}+V(y)U(x)^{\top}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left(\left(QP+\lambda I\right)WW^{\top}-QWAW^{\top}\right),

where we replaced expectations by
WW^{\top}
and
WAW^{\top}
. For
P
and
Q
such that
Q
and
QP
are symmetric, we get by direct differentiation w.r.t.
W

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{W}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}(W;P,Q),\delta W\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}=-Tr\left(\delta W\left(QWA-(QP+\lambda I)W\right)^{\top}\right).

In particular, the above expression holds when
P=P_{W}
and

\partial_{W}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}(W,P_{W},Q_{W})=-Q_{W}WA+(Q_{W}P_{W}+\lambda I)W

\displaystyle\partial_{W}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}(W,P_{W},Q_{W})

\displaystyle=-Q_{W}WA+(Q_{W}P_{W}+\lambda I)W

\displaystyle=\lambda W\left(I-\left(W^{\top}W+\lambda I\right)^{-1}A\left(W^{\top}W+\lambda I\right)^{-1}\right).

The above expression matches that of

from

. Thus, we have shown that

Appendix D
Proofs of the reduction to coordinate representations

D.1
Proof of
Proposition

B.1
: Expressions of the gradient of
PEIRA
’s objective

Proof of
Proposition

B.1
.

Let
(U,V)
be in
\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V}
. We start by expressing the objective in terms of the operator
A
, then its gradient.

Expression of the objective.

Recall the expression of
\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(U,V)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(U,V)=-\frac{1}{2}\Tr\!\bigl(P_{U,V}\bigr)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|U(x)\|_{2}^{2}+\|V(y)\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(81)

A first step is to express
P_{U,V}
in closed form in terms of
W
and operator
A
.
As the solution of the linear regression problem in
Equation

3
,
P_{U,V}
admits the following expression:

\displaystyle P_{U,V}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbb{E}\left[U(x)V(y)^{\top}+V(y)U(x)^{\top}\right]\right)\left(\mathbb{E}\left[U(x)U(x)^{\top}+V(y)V(y)^{\top}\right]+\lambda I\right)^{-1}

(82)

\displaystyle=WAW^{\top}\left(WW^{\top}+\lambda I\right)^{-1}.

Therefore,
\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(U,V)
can be written as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(U,V)=\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(W)=-\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left(WAW^{\top}\left(WW^{\top}+\lambda I\right)^{-1}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\Tr\left(WW^{\top}\right).

(83)

Hence,
\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}
is a rational function of
W
. To prove smoothness, recall that by definition,
A
is a bounded operator. Hence,
WAW^{\top}
is a smooth function of
W
, i.e. infinitely differentiable.
Moreover, since
\lambda>0
, then
WW^{\top}+\lambda I
is smooth and invertible for any
W
, therefore
W\mapsto Q_{W}=(WW^{\top}+\lambda I)^{-1}
is smooth by composition with the inverse map. Finally,
\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}
is also smooth by composition of smooth functions.

Gradient expression.

The expression of the gradient is obtained by direct differentiation along any direction
\delta W\in\mathcal{W}

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(W),\delta W\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}Tr\left(\left(\delta WAW^{\top}+WA\delta W^{\top}\right)Q_{W}\right)+\lambda Tr(\delta WW^{\top})

(84)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}Tr\left(WAW^{\top}Q_{W}\left(\delta WW^{\top}+W\delta W^{\top}\right)Q_{W}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-Tr\left(\delta WAW^{\top}Q_{W}\right)+\lambda Tr(\delta WW^{\top})

\displaystyle+Tr\left(\delta WW^{\top}Q_{W}WAW^{\top}Q_{W}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-Tr\left(\delta W\left(AW^{\top}Q_{W}-W^{\top}Q_{W}P_{W}-\lambda W^{\top}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-Tr\left(\delta W\left(Q_{W}WA-(Q_{W}P_{W}+\lambda I)W\right)^{\top}\right).

This shows that the gradient is given by:

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(W)=-Q_{W}WA+Q_{W}P_{W}W+\lambda W.

(85)

To get the second expression, recall that
P_{W}=WAW^{\top}(WW^{\top}+\lambda I)^{-1}=WAW^{\top}Q_{W}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(W)

\displaystyle=-Q_{W}WA+Q_{W}P_{W}W+\lambda W

(86)

\displaystyle=Q_{W}WA\left(W^{\top}(WW^{\top}+\lambda I)^{-1}W-I\right)+\lambda W

\displaystyle=Q_{W}WA\left((W^{\top}W+\lambda I)^{-1}W^{\top}W-I\right)+\lambda W

\displaystyle=-\lambda Q_{W}WA(W^{\top}W+\lambda I)^{-1}+\lambda W

\displaystyle=-\lambda W\left(W^{\top}W+\lambda I\right)^{-1}A\left(W^{\top}W+\lambda I\right)^{-1}+\lambda W

\displaystyle=\lambda W\left(I-\left(W^{\top}W+\lambda I\right)^{-1}A\left(W^{\top}W+\lambda I\right)^{-1}\right),

where we used the following property
(WW^{\top}+\lambda I)^{-1}W=W(W^{\top}W+\lambda I)^{-1}
in the third and fifth lines.

To show that the gradient is Lipschitz, we will show that its Hessian is bounded over
\mathcal{W}
. Let us first express the action of the Hessian on a direction
\delta W
by differentiating the second expression of the gradient along such direction. For conciseness we define
R=(W^{\top}W+\lambda I)^{-1}
and get:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(W)[\delta W]=

\displaystyle\lambda\delta W\left(I-RAR\right)+\lambda WR\left(\delta W^{\top}W+W^{\top}\delta W\right)RAR

(87)

\displaystyle+\lambda WRAR\left(\delta W^{\top}W+W^{\top}\delta W\right)R.

Let us now find an upper bound
C
on the operator norm of the Hessian. To this end it suffices to find an upper bound
\left\lVert\nabla^{2}\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(W)[\delta W]\right\rVert
of the form
\left\lVert\nabla^{2}\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(W)[\delta W]\right\rVert\leq C\left\lVert\delta W\right\rVert
. Standard operator norm inequalities yield:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla^{2}\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(W)[\delta W]\right\rVert\leq

\displaystyle\lambda\left(\left\lVert RAR\right\rVert_{op}+1\right)\left\lVert\delta W\right\rVert

(88)

\displaystyle+\lambda\left\lVert WR\right\rVert_{op}\left\lVert WRAR\right\rVert_{op}\left\lVert\delta W\right\rVert+\lambda\left\lVert WRW^{\top}\right\rVert_{op}\left\lVert RAR\right\rVert_{op}\left\lVert\delta W\right\rVert

\displaystyle+\lambda\left\lVert WRAR\right\rVert_{op}\left\lVert WR\right\rVert_{op}\left\lVert\delta W\right\rVert+\lambda\left\lVert WRARW^{\top}\right\rVert_{op}\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{op}\left\lVert\delta W\right\rVert.

Define
C_{0}
as:

\displaystyle C_{0}=

\displaystyle\lambda\left(1+\left\lVert RAR\right\rVert_{op}+2\left\lVert WR\right\rVert_{op}\left\lVert WRAR\right\rVert_{op}\right)

(89)

\displaystyle+\lambda\left(\left\lVert WRW^{\top}\right\rVert_{op}\left\lVert RAR\right\rVert_{op}+\left\lVert WRARW^{\top}\right\rVert_{op}\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{op}\right)

We have shown, so far that
\left\lVert\nabla^{2}\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(W)\right\rVert_{op}\leq C_{0}
. It remains to show that
C_{0}
is uniformly bounded w.r.t.
W
. To this end, let us further upper-bound
C_{0}
:

\displaystyle C_{0}\leq

\displaystyle\lambda\left(1+\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{op}\left(\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{op}^{2}+3\left\lVert WR\right\rVert_{op}^{2}\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{op}+\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{op}^{2}\left\lVert WRW^{\top}\right\rVert_{op}\right)\right)

(90)

Recalling that
R=(W^{\top}W+\lambda I)^{-1}
, we directly have that
\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{op}\leq\lambda^{-1}
. Moreover, since
W
has finite dimensional range (of dimension
k
), it must be a compact operator, so that it admits a singular-value decomposition, by the spectral theorem for compact operators
[
Gohberg et al. 2003
]
. Using such a decomposition, we can show that the singular values of
WR
and
WRW^{\top}
are given by
\frac{w_{i}}{w_{i}^{2}+\lambda}
and
\frac{w_{i}^{2}}{w_{i}^{2}+\lambda}
where
w_{i}
are the singular values of
W
. These quantities are uniformly bounded by
\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}
and
1
respectively. Consequently, we have
\left\lVert WR\right\rVert_{op}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}
and
\left\lVert WRW^{\top}\right\rVert_{op}\leq 1
. Finally, recalling that
\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{op}\leq 1
by
Equation

19
it follows that:

\displaystyle C_{0}\leq\lambda\left(1+\frac{11}{4\lambda^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{4}{\lambda},

(91)

where we used that
\lambda<1
. This allows us to conclude that the gradient
\nabla\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}
is Lipschitz, with Lipschitz constant
\frac{4}{\lambda}
.

Components of the gradient in the eigenbasis of
A
.

Using the decomposition in
Equation

24
of
WAW^{\top}
and
WW^{\top}
in such basis and recalling the expression of the gradient
\nabla\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}(W)
above, we directly get the desired expression for its components
\mathbf{g}
in the basis
(\zeta_{i})_{i\in I}
in terms of
\mathbf{w}
and the diagonal operator
S
which is given by the vector field
F^{0}
. Equivalence between the ODE in
\mathcal{W}
and the ODE of its coordinates in
\mathbb{W}
follows directly.

E.1
Proof of convergence to critical points for general gradient-like dynamical systems

For convenience, let us recall here the statement of the general convergence result before proceeding with the proof.

Proposition E.1
.

Let
F
be a smooth vector field defined on a separable Hilbert space
\mathbb{W}
and consider the following dynamical system:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{w}}_{t}=-F(\mathbf{w}_{t}),\qquad\mathbf{w}_{0}\in\mathbb{W}.

(97)

Assume there exists a real analytic function
L
defined over
\mathbb{W}
such that the following holds:

(
A
)

Dissipation.

L
is a Lyapunov function for the system, i.e.
\dot{L}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\leq 0
for all
t\geq 0
.

(
B
)

Sufficient decrease.
There exists
c_{max}>0
such that
\left\lVert F(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}\leq-c_{max}\dot{L}(\mathbf{w}_{t})
for
t\geq 0
.

(
C
)

Relative bound.
There exists a non-increasing real-valued function
\Omega:[0,+\infty)\rightarrow(0,+\infty)
such that
\Omega(\left\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}\right\rVert)\left\lVert\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}\leq\left\lVert F(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}
.

(
D
)

Coercivity.

L
is coercive, i.e.
L(\mathbf{w})\rightarrow+\infty
when
\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert\rightarrow+\infty
.

(
E
)

Lower bound.

L
admits a finite lower bound.

(
F
)

Radial growth.
The vector field
F
, gradient
\nabla L
and Hessian
\nabla^{2}L
all have radial growth, i.e. there exists a non-decreasing real-valued function
\omega:[0,+\infty)\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
such that
\max\left(\left\lVert F(\mathbf{w})\right\rVert,\left\lVert\nabla L(\mathbf{w})\right\rVert,\left\lVert\nabla^{2}L(\mathbf{w})\right\rVert\right)\leq\omega(\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert)
for all
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{W}
.

(
G
)

Fredholm property.
For any
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{W}
, the hessian
\nabla^{2}L(\mathbf{w})
can be decomposed as the sum of a boundedly invertible operator
R
and a bounded compact operator
K
.

(
H
)

Palais-Smale condition.
Any bounded sequence
(\mathbf{w}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of
\mathbb{W}
such that
\left\lVert\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{n})\right\rVert\rightarrow 0
admits a convergent subsequence.

Then the dynamical system in
Equation

97
is defined at all times and converges to a critical point of the vector field
F
.

Proof of
Proposition

E.1
.

Let
\mathbf{w}_{t}
be a solution to the ODE and
L
be a Lyapunov function, which exists by
(A)
.

Local existence.

Since
F
is a smooth vector field, it is therefore locally Lipschitz. Hence, by Cauchy-Lipschitz theorem
[
Teschl 2000
, Theorem 1.9]
, the ODE
Equation

97
admits a unique maximal solution starting from a point
\mathbf{w}_{0}
, continuous in time and defined on a maximal interval
[0,T)
with
T>0
possibly equal to
+\infty
. We will later show that
T=+\infty
.

Bounded trajectories and velocities.

We know by
(A)
that
L
is a Lyapunov function for the ODE
Equation

97
. Therefore,
\mathbf{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
remains in the level set
\{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{W}|L(\mathbf{w})\leq L(\mathbf{w}_{0})\}
. Moreover, since
L
is coercive by
(D)
, such level set must be bounded so that
\mathbf{w}_{t}
is also bounded, i.e. there exists
b>0
so that
\left\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}\right\rVert\leq b
for all
0\leq t<T
.
To show that
\dot{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
is bounded, recall that
F(\mathbf{w})
is bounded over the centered ball of radius
b
by
(F)
. In particular,
t\mapsto\left\lVert F(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert
is also bounded, since we have just established that
\mathbf{w}_{t}
belongs to such a ball for all
0\leq t<T
. Consequently,
t\mapsto\dot{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
remains also bounded for all
0\leq t<T
, since, by definition
\dot{\mathbf{w}}_{t}=-F(\mathbf{w}_{t})
.

Global existence.

Let us now show that
T=+\infty
. By contradiction, assume that
T
is finite. We have shown that
\dot{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
is bounded for all
0\leq t<T
. Hence, there exists a positive constant
M
such that
\left\lVert\dot{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\right\rVert\leq M
for all
0\leq t<T
. Hence, for any
0\leq s<t<T
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}_{s}\right\rVert\leq\int_{s}^{t}\left\lVert\dot{\mathbf{w}}_{\tau}\right\rVert\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\leq M(t-s).

(98)

Thus
(\mathbf{w}_{t})_{t<T}
is Cauchy as
t\uparrow T
. Since
\mathbb{W}
is complete, there exists
\mathbf{w}_{T}\in\mathbb{W}
such that
\mathbf{w}_{t}\rightarrow\mathbf{w}_{T}
as
t\uparrow T
.
Applying the local existence theorem again with initial condition
\mathbf{w}_{T}
extends the solution beyond time
T
, contradicting the maximality of
T
. Therefore,
T=+\infty
.

Convergence of the energy gradient
\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})
to
0
.

First, by
(C)
, we know that
\Omega(\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{t})\left\lVert\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}\leq\left\lVert F(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}
. Since
\left\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}\right\rVert
is bounded by
b>0
and
\Omega
is non-increasing, it follows that
0<\Omega(b)\leq\Omega(\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{t})
. Denoting by
c_{min}=\Omega(b)
, we have shown that:

\displaystyle c_{min}\left\lVert\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}\leq\left\lVert F(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}.

(99)

Moreover, further using
(B)
, it follows that
\left\lVert\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}\leq-\frac{c_{max}}{c_{min}}\dot{L}(\mathbf{w}_{t})
. Therefore, by integrating such inequality between
0
and any positive time
T
, it follows that:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{c_{max}}{c_{min}}\left(L(\mathbf{w}_{0})-L(\mathbf{w}_{T})\right).

(100)

Now recall that, by
(A)
and
(E)
,
t\mapsto L(\mathbf{w}_{t})
is non-increasing with
L
being lower-bounded. Therefore,
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})
must converge to a finite limit
L_{\infty}
satisfying
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\geq L_{\infty}
for all
t\geq 0
. Using that
L(\mathbf{w}_{T})\geq L_{\infty}
in
Equation

100
implies that
\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left\lVert\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
is finite. Such an integrability condition alone is not enough to deduce that
\left\lVert\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}
converges to
0
. However, provided that
\left\lVert\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}
is uniformly continuous, we can deduce convergence to
0
of
\left\lVert\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}
by application of Barbalat’s theorem
[
Barbălat 1959
]
. We obtain uniform continuity by
Lemma

E.1
which applies here, as
\mathbf{w}_{t}
and
\dot{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
are bounded and
\mathbf{w}\mapsto\nabla L(\mathbf{w})
and
\mathbf{w}\mapsto\nabla^{2}L(\mathbf{w})
are radially bounded by
(F)
.

Existence of cluster points.

We wish to establish the existence of a cluster point for the dynamics, i.e., a point
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
such that there exists a subsequence along the trajectory
\mathbf{w}_{t}
converging towards it.
Let
(t_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be any increasing sequence of positive real numbers with
t_{n}\rightarrow+\infty
. We know that
(\mathbf{w}_{t_{n}})_{n\geq 1}
is bounded since we established that
t\mapsto\mathbf{w}_{t}
is bounded. By
(H)
, we deduce that
(\mathbf{w}_{t_{n}})
admits a convergent subsequence, thus establishing the existence of a cluster point as the limit point of such a subsequence. If
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
is a cluster point, then it must be a critical point of
\nabla L
. Indeed, we have already established that
\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\rightarrow 0
. Hence, by continuity of
\nabla L
, we directly get that
\nabla L(\mathbf{w}^{\star})=0
.

A Lojasiewicz-Simon’s inequality.

For any
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{W}
, we know, by
(G)
, that the Hessian
\nabla^{2}L(\mathbf{w})
can be decomposed as the sum of a boundedly invertible operator
R
and a bounded compact operator
K
.
The operator
R
is necessarily a Fredholm operator of index
0
, i.e.
ker(R)=\{0\}
and
ker(R^{*})=\{0\}
and
R
has a closed range. Moreover, by stability of Fredholm operators to compact perturbations, e.g.
[
Conway 2019
, Theorem 3.11]
, it follows that
\nabla^{2}L(\mathbf{w})=R+K
is also Fredholm with index
0
. Moreover, by assumption,
L
is analytic. Therefore, by direct application of
[
Feehan and Maridakis 2020
, Theorem 1]
, it follows that
L
satisfies a desired Lojasiewicz–Simon gradient inequality near its critical points. In other words, if
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
is a critical point of
\nabla L
, then there exist finite constants
Z>0
,
0<\sigma<1
and
\frac{1}{2}\leq\theta<1
so that for any
\mathbf{w}
satisfying
\left\lVert\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right\rVert\leq\sigma
, it must hold that:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla L(\mathbf{w})\right\rVert\geq Z\left\lvert L(\mathbf{w})-L(\mathbf{w}^{\star})\right\rvert^{\theta}.

(101)

Trapping near a cluster point.

Let
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
be an accumulation point of
\mathbf{w}_{t}
, which was shown to exist. We have also shown that
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})
converges to a finite value
L_{\infty}
with
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\geq L_{\infty}
. Here we are only interested in the case where
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})>L_{\infty}
at all times. The other will be treated separately. To show that the trajectory remains close to the same cluster points, we will use a trapping argument near
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
following the scheme from
[
Bolte et al. 2014
]
for Lyapunov functions satisfying a Lojasiewicz-Simon inequality.
Fix
\epsilon>0
. Since
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
is an accumulation point of
\mathbf{w}_{t}
, and that
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\rightarrow L_{\infty}>-\infty
, there exists
T>0
so that
\left\lVert\mathbf{w}_{T}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right\rVert\leq\epsilon
and
0\leq L(\mathbf{w}_{t})-L_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
for all
t\geq T
. By choosing
\epsilon<\sigma
, we can guarantee that
Equation

101
holds. Let us show that
\left\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right\rVert\leq\sigma
for any
t\geq T
. Consider
T_{max}
, the largest time greater than
T
for which
\left\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right\rVert\leq\sigma
for all
t\in(T,T_{max})
. We need to show that
T_{max}=\infty
. It is clear by continuity of
\mathbf{w}_{t}
that
T_{max}>T
so that the interval
(T,T_{max})
is non-empty. Recalling that
\dot{\mathbf{w}}_{t}=-F(\mathbf{w}_{t})
, the following holds, for all
t\in(T,T_{max})
:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\dot{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\right\rVert=\left\lVert F(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{c_{min}^{-1}}\frac{\left\lVert F(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert}

(102)

\displaystyle\leq-\sqrt{c_{min}^{-1}}c_{max}\frac{\dot{L}(\mathbf{w}_{t})}{\left\lVert\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert}

\displaystyle\leq-Z^{-1}\sqrt{c_{min}^{-1}}c_{max}\frac{\dot{L}(\mathbf{w}_{t})}{\left\lvert L(\mathbf{w}_{t})-L(\mathbf{w}^{\star})\right\rvert^{\theta}},

\displaystyle=-Z^{-1}\sqrt{c_{min}^{-1}}c_{max}\frac{\dot{L}(\mathbf{w}_{t})}{\left(L(\mathbf{w}_{t})-L_{\infty}\right)^{\theta}},

where we used
Equation

99
for the first line, then
(B)
for the second line. The third inequality, uses
Equation

101
, whereas the last one uses that
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\geq L_{\infty}=L(\mathbf{w}^{\star})
, where the equality
L_{\infty}=L(\mathbf{w}^{\star})
follows by continuity of
L
and uniqueness of the limit
L_{\infty}
. As we are in the case where
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})-L_{\infty}>0
, we must have
\left\lVert\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert>0
thanks to
Equation

101
, so that the above upper-bounds are always finite. Integrating the above inequality over an interval
(t,s)
with
T\leq t\leq s<T_{max}
yields:

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{s}\left\lVert\dot{\mathbf{w}}_{\tau}\right\rVert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\left(\left(L(\mathbf{w}_{t})-L_{\infty}\right)^{1-\theta}-\left(L(\mathbf{w}_{s})-L_{\infty}\right)^{1-\theta}\right)

(103)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\left(L(\mathbf{w}_{t})-L_{\infty}\right)^{1-\theta}

where
\alpha=\frac{1}{1-\theta}Z^{-1}\sqrt{c_{min}^{-1}}c_{max}<\infty
.
Consequently, the above inequality implies, in particular, that:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{w}_{T}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right\rVert+\int_{T}^{t}\left\lVert\dot{\mathbf{w}_{\tau}}\right\rVert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

(104)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{w}_{T}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right\rVert+\alpha\left(L(\mathbf{w}_{T})-L_{\infty}\right)^{1-\theta}\leq\epsilon+\alpha\epsilon^{1-\theta}.

We can choose
\epsilon
small enough so that
\epsilon+\alpha\epsilon^{1-\theta}\leq\frac{\sigma}{2}
, thus yielding
\left\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right\rVert\leq\frac{\sigma}{2}
for all
T\leq t<T_{max}
. If, by contradiction,
T_{max}
were finite, then, by continuity of
\mathbf{w}_{t}
we would also have
\left\lVert\mathbf{w}_{T_{max}}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right\rVert\leq\frac{\sigma}{2}
contradicting the definition of
T_{max}
. Therefore
T_{max}=+\infty
and
\left\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right\rVert\leq\frac{\sigma}{2}
for all
t\geq T
.

Convergence to an accumulation point.

To prove convergence towards
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
, we consider two separate cases depending on whether
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})
reaches its lower bound
L_{\infty}
or not.

•

Case 1
: There exists
t_{0}\geq 0
so that
L(\mathbf{w}_{t_{0}})=L_{\infty}
.

Let
t\geq t_{0}
. Since
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})
is non-increasing by
(A)
, it holds that
L_{\infty}=L(\mathbf{w}_{t_{0}})\geq L(\mathbf{w}_{t})
. On the other hand, we have
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\geq L_{\infty}
so that
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})=L_{\infty}
. As this equality holds for any
t\geq t_{0}
, it must be that
\dot{L}(\mathbf{w}_{t})=0
for all
t\geq t_{0}
. Along with
(B)
, we deduce that
\left\lVert F(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}\leq-c_{max}\dot{L}(\mathbf{w}_{t})=0
for all
t\geq t_{0}
. Consequently,
\mathbf{w}_{t}
remains constant, but since we know that
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
is an accumulation point of the trajectory, it must hold that
\mathbf{w}_{t}=\mathbf{w}^{\star}
for all
t\geq t_{0}
, which, a fortiori, implies convergence of
\mathbf{w}_{t}
towards
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
.

•

Case 2
:
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})>L_{\infty}
for all
t\geq 0
.

We have already established that
\left\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right\rVert\leq\sigma
for all
t\geq T
, so that
Equation

101
always holds and we can use
Equation

103
.
Let
t\geq T
and consider a sequence
\mathbf{w}_{s_{n}}
converging towards
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
with
s_{n}
increasing and greater than
t
. Such sequence always exists as
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
is an accumulation point of
\mathbf{w}_{t}
. Recalling that
\mathbf{w}_{s_{n}}-\mathbf{w}_{t}=\int_{t}^{s_{n}}\dot{\mathbf{w}}_{t^{\prime}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}
and using
Equation

103
, it follows that for any
n\geq 1
:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{w}_{s_{n}}-\mathbf{w}_{t}\right\rVert\leq\int_{t}^{s_{n}}\left\lVert\dot{\mathbf{w}}_{t^{\prime}}\right\rVert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\leq\alpha\left(L(\mathbf{w}_{t})-L_{\infty}\right)^{1-\theta}.

(105)

Taking the limit
n\rightarrow+\infty
yields:
\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\star}-\mathbf{w}_{t}\right\rVert\leq\alpha\left(L(\mathbf{w}_{t})-L_{\infty}\right)^{1-\theta}
, which holds for any
t\geq T
. Furthermore, since
L(\mathbf{w}_{t})-L_{\infty}\rightarrow 0
,
\alpha>0
and
1-\theta>0
, we deduce that
\mathbf{w}_{t}\rightarrow\mathbf{w}^{\star}
. By continuity of
\nabla L
and
F
, we also have that
\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t})\rightarrow\nabla L(\mathbf{w}^{\star})
and
F(\mathbf{w}_{t})\rightarrow F(\mathbf{w}^{\star})
. Recall also that
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
is a critical point of
\nabla L
, i.e.
\nabla L(\mathbf{w}^{\star})=0
. Finally, by
(B)
, we get

\displaystyle\left\lVert F(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}\leq-c_{max}\dot{L}(\mathbf{w}_{t})=c_{max}\langle\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{t}),F(\mathbf{w}_{t})\rangle,

(106)

taking the limit to infinity yields
\left\lVert F(\mathbf{w}^{\star})\right\rVert^{2}\leq 0
. Thus
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
is a critical point of
F
.
∎

Lemma E.1
.

Consider a differentiable map
t\mapsto\mathbf{w}_{t}
with values in a separable Hilbert space
\mathbb{W}
with
\mathbf{w}_{t}
and
\dot{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
bounded. Let
G
be a smooth map on
\mathbb{W}
so that
G
and
\nabla G
have radial growth, i.e. there exists a non-decreasing function
\omega:[0,+\infty)\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
so that
\left\lVert G(\mathbf{w})\right\rVert,\left\lVert\nabla G(\mathbf{w})\right\rVert\leq\omega(\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert)
for all
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{W}
. Then
t\mapsto\left\lVert G(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}
is uniformly continuous.

Proof.

We know that
\mathbf{w}_{t}
is bounded, hence, there exists a positive real number
b
so that
\mathbf{w}_{t}
is included in a centered ball of radius
b
for all
t
. As
G
and
\nabla G
have radial growth, by assumption, they must be bounded on such a ball. Therefore, one can find a positive constant
L
so that the following holds:

\displaystyle\left\lVert G(\mathbf{w}_{t})-G(\mathbf{w}_{s})\right\rVert\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{W}\\
\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert\leq b\end{subarray}}\left\lVert\nabla G(\mathbf{w})\right\rVert\left\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}_{s}\right\rVert\leq L\sup_{t^{\prime}\geq 0}\left\lVert\dot{\mathbf{w}}_{t^{\prime}}\right\rVert\left\lvert t-s\right\rvert,\qquad\left\lVert G(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert\leq L

(107)

Since, by assumption,
\sup_{t^{\prime}\geq 0}\left\lVert\dot{\mathbf{w}}_{t^{\prime}}\right\rVert<+\infty
, we deduce that
t\mapsto G(\mathbf{w}_{t})
is Lipschitz and bounded. We can therefore deduce that
t\mapsto\left\lVert G(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}
is Lipschitz thanks to the inequalities:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\left\lVert G(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}-\left\lVert G(\mathbf{w}_{s})\right\rVert^{2}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert G(\mathbf{w}_{t})-G(\mathbf{w}_{s})\right\rVert\left(\left\lVert G(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert+\left\lVert G(\mathbf{w}_{s})\right\rVert\right)

(108)

\displaystyle\leq 2L^{2}\sup_{t^{\prime}\geq 0}\left\lVert\dot{\mathbf{w}}_{t^{\prime}}\right\rVert\left\lvert t-s\right\rvert.

A fortiori,
\left\lVert G(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}
is uniformly continuous.
∎

E.2
Proof of the auxiliary results of
Propositions

B.4
,
B.1
,
B.2
,
B.3
and
B.4

Existence of a Lyapunov function

Proof of
Proposition

B.4
(Existence of a Lyapunov function).

Differentiating
L^{\kappa}
w.r.t.
\mathbf{w}
yields:

\displaystyle\nabla L^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})=2\mathbf{w}\Phi(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{w}),\qquad\Phi(M)=(M+\lambda I)^{2}-S(MS)^{\kappa}.

(109)

Note the vector field
F^{\kappa}
can be expressed in terms of
\Phi
as follows:

\displaystyle F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})=

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}B(M)\Phi(M)B(M),\qquad M=\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{w},\qquad B(M)=(M+\lambda I)^{-1}.

(110)

Introducing
M_{t}=\mathbf{w}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{t}
for conciseness and computing the time derivatives of
L^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w}_{t})
yields:

\displaystyle\dot{L}^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w}_{t})

\displaystyle=Tr\left(\nabla L^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w}_{t})^{\top}\dot{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\right)

(111)

\displaystyle=-2\lambda Tr\left(\Phi(M_{t})M_{t}B(M_{t})\Phi(M_{t})B(M_{t})\right)

\displaystyle=-2\lambda Tr\left(\Phi(M_{t})B(M_{t})M_{t}\Phi(M_{t})B(M_{t})\right)

\displaystyle=-2\lambda Tr\left(B(M_{t})^{\frac{1}{2}}M_{t}\Phi(M_{t})B(M_{t})\Phi(M_{t})B(M_{t})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=-2\lambda Tr\left(M_{t}B(M_{t})^{\frac{1}{2}}\Phi(M_{t})B(M_{t})\Phi(M_{t})B(M_{t})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=-2\lambda Tr\left(M_{t}Q_{t}^{2}\right)=-2\lambda\left\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}Q_{t}\right\rVert^{2},

where we defined
Q_{t}=B(M_{t})^{\frac{1}{2}}\Phi(M_{t})B(M_{t})^{\frac{1}{2}}
and used that
M_{t}
always commutes with
B(M_{t})
.

Sufficient decrease.

First, recall that by definition of
F^{\kappa}
, we have that:

\displaystyle\left\lVert F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=Tr\left(B\Phi BMB\Phi B\right),

(112)

where, we used that
M=\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{w}
,
B=B(M)=(M+\lambda I)^{-1}
and
\Phi=\Phi(M)
for conciseness.
Recalling that
M
and
B
are PSD and commute, by construction, and that
B\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}I
, it follows:

\displaystyle\left\lVert F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=Tr\left(B\Phi B^{\frac{1}{2}}M^{\frac{1}{2}}BM^{\frac{1}{2}}B^{\frac{1}{2}}\Phi B\right)

(113)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{-1}Tr\left(B\Phi B^{\frac{1}{2}}M^{\frac{1}{2}}M^{\frac{1}{2}}B^{\frac{1}{2}}\Phi B\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}Tr\left(M^{\frac{1}{2}}B^{\frac{1}{2}}\Phi B^{\frac{1}{2}}BB^{\frac{1}{2}}\Phi B^{\frac{1}{2}}M^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{-2}Tr\left(M^{\frac{1}{2}}B^{\frac{1}{2}}\Phi B^{\frac{1}{2}}B^{\frac{1}{2}}\Phi B^{\frac{1}{2}}M^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-2}Tr\left(MB^{\frac{1}{2}}\Phi B\Phi B^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)=-\frac{1}{2\lambda^{3}}\dot{L}^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w}_{t}).

Relative error.

Similarly, using again that
B
and
M
commute:

\displaystyle\left\lVert F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=Tr\left(B\Phi M^{\frac{1}{2}}B^{2}M^{\frac{1}{2}}\Phi B\right)

(114)

\displaystyle\geq\sigma_{min}(B^{2})Tr\left(B\Phi M^{\frac{1}{2}}M^{\frac{1}{2}}\Phi B\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sigma_{min}(B^{2})Tr\left(M^{\frac{1}{2}}\Phi B^{2}\Phi M^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sigma_{min}(B^{2})^{2}Tr\left(M^{\frac{1}{2}}\Phi\Phi M^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{min}(B^{2})^{2}Tr\left(\Phi M\Phi\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sigma_{min}(B^{2})^{2}\left\lVert\nabla L^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})\right\rVert^{2},

where we used that
B^{2}\geq\sigma_{min}(B^{2})I
the smallest singular value of
B^{2}
. Moreover, simple estimates show that
\sigma_{min}(B^{2})\geq\frac{1}{(\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert^{2}+\lambda)^{2}}
. Hence, we have established the relative error bound:

\left\lVert F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})\right\rVert^{2}\geq\frac{1}{4(\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert^{2}+\lambda)^{4}}\left\lVert\nabla L^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})\right\rVert^{2}.

∎

Growth of the vector field
F^{\kappa}
.

Proof of
Lemma

B.1
.

The bound holds by direct application of operator norm properties. Specifically, for
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{W}
, and denoting by

, we have:

\displaystyle\left\lVert F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert\left(1+\left\lVert RS(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{w}S)^{\kappa}R\right\rVert_{op}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert\left(1+\underbrace{\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{op}^{2}}_{\leq\lambda^{-2}}\left\lVert S\right\rVert_{op}^{1+\kappa}\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{op}^{\kappa}\right)

This establishes that

is radially bounded by the function

Growth, coercivity and lower boundedness of the energy
L^{\kappa}
.

Proof of
Lemma

B.2
(Finite lower bound on the energy).

Let us first show that
L^{\kappa}
is lower-bounded. Let
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{W}
. Then
\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\top}
is a
k\times k
PSD matrix. Denote by
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k}
its singular values. Then

\displaystyle Tr\left((\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\top}+\lambda I)^{3}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\sigma_{i}+\lambda)^{3}\;\geq\;\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_{i}^{3}+k\lambda^{3}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{k^{2}}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbb{W}}^{6}+k\lambda^{3},

(116)

using
\sum\sigma_{i}^{3}\geq k^{-2}(\sum\sigma_{i})^{3}=k^{-2}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbb{W}}^{6}
by Jensen’s inequality. For the second term of
L^{\kappa}
, we use the following upper-bound:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\Tr\left(\left(\mathbf{w}S\mathbf{w}^{\top}\right)^{\kappa+1}\right)\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert S\right\rVert_{op}^{\kappa+1}\left\lVert\mathbf{w}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{W}}^{2(\kappa+1)}

(117)

Hence, with
r=\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbb{W}}
,

L^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})\geq\frac{1}{3k^{2}}r^{6}-\frac{1}{\kappa+1}\left\lVert S\right\rVert_{op}^{\kappa+1}r^{2(\kappa+1)}+\frac{k\lambda^{3}}{3}.

(118)

The right-hand side is bounded below for
r\geq 0
, as long as
\kappa<2
(positive leading coefficient), so
L^{\kappa}
is bounded below. From the above inequality, we also get that
L^{\kappa}
is coercive.

To show that
\nabla L^{\kappa}
and
\nabla^{2}L^{\kappa}
are radially bounded, recall that
L^{\kappa}
is a smooth polynomial, so that
\nabla L^{\kappa}
and
\nabla^{2}L^{\kappa}
are also smooth polynomials and are thus, a fortiori, radially bounded.

Hessian decomposition.

Proof of
Lemma

B.3
(Hessian decomposition).

First, by direct differentiation, we have that:

\displaystyle\nabla L^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})=2\mathbf{w}\left(\left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{w}+\lambda I\right)^{2}-S\left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{w}S\right)^{\kappa}\right)=2\left(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\top}+\lambda I\right)^{2}\mathbf{w}-2\mathbf{w}S\left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{w}S\right)^{\kappa}.

(119)

Hence, differentiating the above along a direction
v
yields:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}L^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})(\mathbf{w}^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\underbrace{2\left(\left(\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\mathbf{w}^{\top}+\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\prime\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\top}+\lambda I\right)+\left(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\top}+\lambda I\right)\left(\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\mathbf{w}^{\top}+\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\prime\top}\right)\right)}_{Q(\mathbf{w}^{\prime})}\mathbf{w}

(120)

\displaystyle+2\left(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\top}+\lambda I\right)^{2}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}-\underbrace{\left(2\mathbf{w}^{\prime}S\left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{w}S\right)^{\kappa}+2\delta_{\kappa=1}\mathbf{w}S\left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}+\mathbf{w}^{\prime\top}\mathbf{w}\right)S\right)}_{K_{1}(\mathbf{w}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\top}+\lambda I\right)^{2}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}+Q(\mathbf{w}^{\prime})\mathbf{w}-K_{1}(\mathbf{w}^{\prime})

By assumption, we know that
S
is a bounded compact operator, hence,
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\mapsto K_{1}(\mathbf{w}^{\prime})
is also bounded and compact as a sum of compositions of bounded and compact operators. Moreover

is a

lives in the range of the

\mathbf{w}
. Hence,
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\mapsto Q(\mathbf{w}^{\prime})\mathbf{w}
has a finite range and is thus, a fortiori, compact. Finally, note that

is an invertible

is invertible with a bounded inverse. We have shown, so far, that
\nabla^{2}L^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})
is decomposed as the sum of a boundedly invertible operator

Palais-Smale condition.

defined on

For the second term, we know that

Lemma E.2
.

Let
S
be a bounded compact operator on
\ell^{2}(I)
,
and
(B_{n})_{n\geq 1}
a bounded sequence of bounded operators on
\ell^{2}(I)
. Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a family of self-adjoint bounded operators satisfying:

\|X_{n}-B_{n}S\|\to 0.

Then
(X_{n})
admits a convergent subsequence in operator norm.

Proof.

Let
(P_{m})_{m\geq 1}
be a family of finite-rank orthogonal projections in
\ell^{2}(I)
such that
P_{m}
converges to the identity as
m
increases. Since
S
is compact, necessarily,
\|S(I-P_{m})\|\to 0
.
Moreover, we have that
X_{n}-B_{n}S\to 0
and
(B_{n})
is bounded. Hence,
(X_{n})
is bounded and

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|X_{n}(I-P_{m})\|\leq\sup_{n}\|B_{n}\|\,\|S(I-P_{m})\|\to 0\qquad(m\to\infty).

(124)

Moreover, by assumption
X_{n}
is self-adjoint, i.e.,
X_{n}=X_{n}^{\ast}
, so that

\|(I-P_{m})X_{n}\|=\|X_{n}(I-P_{m})\|,

and

\displaystyle\|X_{n}-P_{m}X_{n}P_{m}\|

\displaystyle=\|X_{n}(I-P_{m})+(I-P_{m})X_{n}P_{m}\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert X_{n}(I-P_{m})\right\rVert+\left\lVert(I-P_{m})X_{n}\right\rVert\left\lVert P_{m}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\lVert X_{n}(I-P_{m})\right\rVert.

Therefore, combining the above upper-bound with
Equation

124
yields:

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|X_{n}-P_{m}X_{n}P_{m}\|=0.

(125)

We now show that the sequence
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is relatively compact in the operator norm, meaning that its closure is a compact set. To achieve this, we only need to show that
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
admits a finite
\epsilon
-cover for any
\epsilon>0
. Fix
\epsilon>0
, then
Equation

125
ensures there exists
m>0
so that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|X_{n}-P_{m}X_{n}P_{m}\|\leq\epsilon/4
.
Consequently, there exists
N>0
so that for all
n\geq N
:

\displaystyle\|X_{n}-P_{m}X_{n}P_{m}\|\leq\epsilon/2

(126)

Denote by
P_{m}\ell^{2}(I)
the finite dimensional space of projections of
\ell^{2}(I)
by the operator
P_{m}
. Then the restrictions
Q_{n}
of
P_{m}X_{n}P_{m}
to
P_{m}\ell^{2}(I)
are stable, i.e.,
Q_{n}
are operators from
P_{m}\ell^{2}(I)
to itself. Moreover,
(Q_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a bounded family of bounded operators on the finite-dimensional space
P_{m}\ell^{2}(I)
. Therefore,
(Q_{n})_{n}
is relatively compact and thus admits a finite
\epsilon/2
-cover
\tilde{Q}_{1},\dots,\tilde{Q}_{K}
, for some
K>0
with
\tilde{Q}
bounded operators on
P_{m}\ell^{2}(I)
satisfying:

\displaystyle\min_{1\leq l\leq K}\left\lVert Q_{n}-\tilde{Q}_{l}\right\rVert\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

(127)

By abuse of notation, we identify
\tilde{Q}_{l}
with its unique extension on
\ell^{2}(I)
satisfying
\tilde{Q}_{l}\mathbf{w}=\tilde{Q}_{l}P_{m}\mathbf{w}
for any
\mathbf{w}\in\ell^{2}(I)
. Hence, combining
Equations

126
and
127
,we have shown that for all
n\geq N
:

\displaystyle\min_{1\leq l\leq K}\left\lVert X_{n}-\tilde{Q}_{l}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert X_{n}-P_{m}X_{n}P_{m}\right\rVert+\min_{1\leq l\leq K}\left\lVert Q_{n}-\tilde{Q}_{l}\right\rVert\leq\epsilon.

(128)

Consequently,
\{\tilde{Q}_{1},\dots,\tilde{Q}_{K}\}\cup\{X_{1},\dots,X_{N}\}
is a finite
\epsilon
-cover of
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
w.r.t. the operator norm. This establishes that
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is relatively compact and a fortiori that
X_{n}
admits a convergent subsequence.
∎

\mathbf{w}
from
Equation

45
and properties of the canonical basis, it holds that:

\displaystyle E_{p,j}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=1}^{r}E_{p,j}^{\top}E_{q,i(q)}=E_{p,j}^{\top}E_{p,i(p)}\mathds{1}_{1\leq p\leq r},

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{p,j}^{\top}\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle=m_{i(p)}^{\frac{1}{2}}E_{p,j}^{\top}E_{p,i(p)}\mathds{1}_{1\leq p\leq r}.

(143)

Furthermore, recall that right-multiplication of
E_{p,j}
with a diagonal operator
T
yields
E_{p,j}T=t_{j}E_{p,j}
, where
t_{j}
is the
j
-th diagonal coefficient of
T
. Hence, we can express
H_{1}
,
H_{2}
and
H_{3}
as:

\begin{gathered}H_{1}=\left(1-r_{j}^{2}g_{j}\right)\tilde{E}_{p,j}\\
H_{2}=m_{j}^{\frac{1}{2}}m_{i(p)}^{\frac{1}{2}}\beta_{i(p),j}Q\mathbf{w}_{0}E_{p,j}^{\top}E_{p,i(p)}\mathds{1}_{1\leq p\leq r}\\
H_{3}=m_{i(p)}\beta_{i(p),j}Q\mathbf{w}_{0}E_{p,i(p)}^{\top}E_{p,j}\mathds{1}_{1\leq p\leq r},\end{gathered}

(144)

where we introduced
\beta_{i,j}
for conciseness:

\displaystyle\beta_{i,j}=r_{i}r_{j}^{2}g_{j}+r_{j}r_{i}^{2}g_{i}-r_{j}r_{i}s_{j}s_{i}\mathds{1}_{\kappa=1}.

(145)

Moreover, defining
i^{-1}(i(p))=p
for
1\leq p\leq r
, the following identity holds:

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{0}E_{p,j}^{\top}E_{p,i(p)}

\displaystyle=E_{i^{-1}(j),i(p)}\mathds{1}_{j\in I_{sup}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{0}E_{p,i(p)}^{\top}E_{p,j}

\displaystyle=E_{p,j}.

(146)

Using the above in
Equation

144
and writing
q=i^{-1}(j)
and
l=i(p)
allows us to express the Jacobian as follows:

\displaystyle\nabla F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})(\tilde{E}_{p,j})=

\displaystyle\left(1-r_{j}^{2}g_{j}+m_{l}\beta_{l,j}\mathds{1}_{p\leq r}\right)\tilde{E}_{p,j}+m_{j}^{\frac{1}{2}}m_{l}^{\frac{1}{2}}\beta_{l,j}\tilde{E}_{q,l}\mathds{1}_{p\leq r,j\in I_{sup}}.

(147)

We distinguish two cases depending on whether
j\in I_{sup}
and
p\leq r
or not.

Case
j\notin I_{sup}
or
p>r
.

The second term in
Equation

147
vanishes and we get that
\tilde{E}_{p,j}
is an eigenvector of
\nabla F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})
with eigenvalue
\mu_{p,j}
given by:

\displaystyle\mu_{p,j}

\displaystyle=\left(1-r_{j}^{2}g_{j}+m_{i(p)}\beta_{i(p),j}\mathds{1}_{1\leq p\leq r}\right)

(148)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&j\in I_{sup},p>r\\
1-\lambda^{-2}s_{j}^{\kappa+1}m_{j}^{\kappa},&j\notin I_{sup},p>r\\
1-\lambda^{-1}\frac{s_{j}^{\kappa+1}m_{j}^{\kappa}}{m_{i(p)}+\lambda}+\lambda^{-1}m_{i(p)}\left(1-\frac{s_{i(p)}s_{j}}{m_{i(p)}+\lambda}\mathds{1}_{\kappa=1}\right),&j\notin I_{sup},p\leq r\end{cases},

where we used that
m_{j}=0
and
m_{i(p)}
satisfies the equation
(m_{i(p)}+\lambda)^{2}=s_{i(p)}^{\kappa+1}m_{i(p)}^{\kappa}
.

Case
j\in I_{sup}
and
p\leq r
.

If
j=i(p)
, then
j^{\prime}=j
and
p^{\prime}=p
so that
\tilde{E}_{p,j}=\tilde{E}_{p^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
. Hence,
\tilde{E}_{p,j}
is again an eigenvector of
\nabla F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})
associated to eigenvalue
\mu_{p,j}
given by:

\displaystyle\mu_{p,j}

\displaystyle=1-\underbrace{r_{j}^{2}g_{j}}_{=1}+2m_{j}\beta_{j,j}=2m_{j}\left(2r_{j}^{3}g_{j}-r_{j}^{2}s_{j}^{2}\mathds{1}_{\kappa=1}\right)

(149)

\displaystyle=4r_{j}m_{j}-2\underbrace{r_{j}^{2}m_{j}s_{j}^{2}\mathds{1}_{\kappa=1}}_{=\mathds{1}_{\kappa=1}}=\frac{4m_{j}}{m_{j}+\lambda}-2\mathds{1}_{\kappa=1}.

If
j\neq i(p)
, then
\tilde{E}_{p,j}
and
\tilde{E}_{i^{-1}(j),i(p)}
are two different orthogonal elements of
\mathbb{W}
. Moreover, setting
p^{\prime}=i^{-1}(j)
and
j^{\prime}=i(p)
, and noticing that
i^{-1}(j^{\prime})=p
and
i(p^{\prime})=j
, it follows that
\tilde{E}_{i^{-1}(j^{\prime}),i(p^{\prime})}=\tilde{E}_{p,j}
. Therefore, applying
\nabla F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})
to
\tilde{E}_{p^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
yields the following expression:

\displaystyle\nabla F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})[\tilde{E}_{p^{\prime},j^{\prime}}]=

\displaystyle\left(1-r_{j^{\prime}}^{2}g_{j^{\prime}}+m_{i(p^{\prime})}\beta_{i(p^{\prime}),j^{\prime}}\right)\tilde{E}_{p^{\prime},j^{\prime}}+m_{j^{\prime}}^{\frac{1}{2}}m_{i(p^{\prime})}^{\frac{1}{2}}\beta_{i(p^{\prime}),j^{\prime}}\tilde{E}_{i^{-1}(j^{\prime}),i(p^{\prime})}

(150)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1-r_{j^{\prime}}^{2}g_{j^{\prime}}+m_{i(p^{\prime})}\beta_{i(p^{\prime}),j^{\prime}}\right)\tilde{E}_{i^{-1}(j),i(p)}+m_{j^{\prime}}^{\frac{1}{2}}m_{i(p^{\prime})}^{\frac{1}{2}}\beta_{i(p^{\prime}),j^{\prime}}\tilde{E}_{p,j}.

We have shown so far that
span\left(\tilde{E}_{p,j},\tilde{E}_{p^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\right)
is a stable subspace of dimension
2
for
\nabla F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})
.
Moreover,
i^{-1}(j)\leq r
and
p\leq r
, so that, by definition,
m_{j}
and
m_{j^{\prime}}
are positive and satisfy the equation
r_{i}^{-2}=(m_{i}+\lambda)^{2}=s_{i}^{\kappa+1}m_{i}^{\kappa}=g_{i}
for
i\in\{j,j^{\prime}\}
. Consequently, the terms
1-r_{j^{\prime}}^{2}g_{j^{\prime}}
and
1-r_{j}^{2}g_{j}
appearing in the expressions of
\nabla F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})[\tilde{E}_{p^{\prime},j^{\prime}}]
and
\nabla F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})[\tilde{E}_{p,j}]
vanish and
\nabla F^{\kappa}(\mathbf{w})
restricted to the
2
-dimensional subspace
span\left(\tilde{E}_{p,j},\tilde{E}_{p^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\right)
admits the matrix representation:

\displaystyle\beta_{j^{\prime},j}\begin{pmatrix}m_{j^{\prime}},&m_{j^{\prime}}^{\frac{1}{2}}m_{j}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
m_{j^{\prime}}^{\frac{1}{2}}m_{j}^{\frac{1}{2}}&m_{j}\end{pmatrix}.

(151)

The above matrix admits eigenvalues
\mu_{p,j}^{0}=0
and
\mu_{p,j}^{1}=\beta_{j^{\prime},j}\left(m_{j}+m_{j^{\prime}}\right)=\gamma_{p,p^{\prime}}
with associated eigenvectors
\bar{E}^{0}_{p,j}
and
\bar{E}^{1}_{p,j}
:

\displaystyle\bar{E}^{0}_{p,j}=\frac{1}{(m_{j}+m_{j^{\prime}})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left(m_{j}^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{E}_{p,j}-m_{j^{\prime}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{E}_{p^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\right),\quad\bar{E}^{1}_{p,j}=\frac{1}{(m_{j}+m_{j^{\prime}})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left(m_{j^{\prime}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{E}_{p,j}+m_{j}^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{E}_{p^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\right).

(152)

∎

Stability of critical point of
F^{0}
.

Proof of
Proposition

B.8
.

We will first prove that for any critical point
\mathbf{w}
of the form in
Equation

50
, the Jacobian
\nabla F^{0}(\mathbf{w})
has only non-negative eigenvalues, whereas for any other critical point, there exists a negative eigenvalue.
We will heavily rely on the eigen-decomposition of
\nabla F^{0}(\mathbf{w})
from
Proposition

B.7
which establishes that all its eigenvalues are of the form:

\displaystyle\mu_{p,j}=\begin{cases}1-\lambda^{-2}s_{j},&j\notin I_{sup}\text{ and }p>r\\
\delta_{p,j},&j\notin I_{sup}\text{ and }p\leq r\\
\frac{4m_{j}}{m_{j}+\lambda},&j=i(p)\text{ and }p\leq r\\
\gamma_{p,p^{\prime}},&p,p^{\prime}\leq r\text{ and }j=i(p^{\prime})>i(p)\\
0,&p^{\prime}\leq r\text{ and }j=i(p^{\prime})\text{ and }\left(\left(p\leq r\text{ and }j<i(p)\right)\text{ or }p>r\right),\end{cases}

(153)

with
\delta_{p,j}
and
\gamma_{p,p^{\prime}}
given by:

\displaystyle\delta_{p,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{(s_{i(p)}-s_{j})}{\lambda(m_{i(p)}+\lambda)}

(154)

\displaystyle\gamma_{p,p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(m_{i(p)}+m_{i(p^{\prime})}\right)\left(\sqrt{s_{i(p)}}+\sqrt{s_{i(p^{\prime})}}\right)}{\left(m_{i(p)}+\lambda\right)\left(m_{i(p^{\prime})}+\lambda\right)}.

Critical points of the form in
Equation

50
are stable.

To see this, it suffices to check that all eigenvalues
\mu_{p,j}
of the Jacobian of
F^{0}
in
Equation

153
are non-negative. We treat each case separately. We only need to check the cases where
j\notin I_{sup}
as, for all the other ones,
\mu_{p,j}
is clearly non-negative.

•

j\notin I_{sup}\text{ and }p>r
.
In this case we have
\mu_{p,j}=1-\lambda^{-2}s_{j}
. We need to show that necessarily
s_{j}\leq\lambda^{2}
. Recall that
r
is the maximal integer such that
s_{i(r)}>\lambda^{2}
and
0\leq r\leq k
. We distinguish two cases, either
r=k
or
r<k
. If
r<k
, then having
s_{j}>\lambda^{2}
contradicts the maximality of
r
, since we could include
s_{j}
to
I_{sup}
. The case
r=k
is excluded here, since we would have
p>k
, which is impossible since
p
must always be smaller than
k
. Consequently,
\mu_{p,j}\geq 0
.

•

j\notin I_{sup}\text{ and }p\leq r
.
In this case
\mu_{p,j}=\delta_{p,j}
. We need to show that
s_{i(p)}\geq s_{j}
.
Indeed,
s_{i(1)},\dots,s_{i(r)}
consists of the
r
highest eigenvalues, hence if
s_{j}>s_{i(p)}
then
j
must be in
I_{sup}
which contradicts the condition
j\notin I_{sup}
.

Strict saddles characterization.

Consider a critical point
\mathbf{w}
of
F^{0}
. By
Proposition

B.6
, it admits a general decomposition of the form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}=Q\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}E_{p,i(p)}\right)M^{\frac{1}{2}},

(155)

where
Q
is an orthogonal matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
,
r
is an integer between
0
and
k
,
I_{sup}=\{i(1),\dots,i(r)\}
is an increasing sequence of integers greater than or equal to
1
, and
M
is a positive semi-definite diagonal operator on
\ell^{2}(I)
whose only non-zero diagonal coefficients
(m_{i(p)})_{1\leq p\leq r}
are given by:

\displaystyle m_{i(p)}^{\frac{1}{2}}=g(s_{i(p)},\lambda),\qquad g(s,\lambda)=(\sqrt{s}-\lambda)^{\frac{1}{2}}_{+}.

(156)

We are interested in critical points that are not of the form of
Equation

50
. Two possibilities arise:

•

Case 1
: There exists
j_{0}\notin I_{sup}
and
1\leq p_{0}\leq r
with
s_{j_{0}}>s_{i(p_{0})}
.

•

Case 2
: There exists
j_{0}\notin I_{sup}
so that
s_{i(p)}>s_{j_{0}}>\lambda^{2}
for all
p
such that
1\leq p\leq r
and the rank
r
satisfies
r<k
.

We will treat each case separately by identifying a negative eigenvalue of the Jacobian
\nabla F^{0}(\mathbf{w})
.

Case 1.

Consider the eigenvalue
\mu_{p_{0},j_{0}}
. Since
p_{0}\leq r
and
j_{0}\notin I_{sup}
,
\mu_{p_{0},j_{0}}
must take the form:

\displaystyle\mu_{p_{0},j_{0}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{p_{0},j_{0}}=\frac{(s_{i(p_{0})}-s_{j_{0}})}{\lambda(m_{i(p_{0})}+\lambda)}<0.

(157)

\mu_{p_{0},j_{0}}
is negative since by assumption
s_{j_{0}}>s_{i(p_{0})}
.
Therefore, the considered critical points cannot be stable since the Jacobian admits a negative eigenvalue.

Case 2.

Set
p_{0}=r+1\leq k
and consider the eigenvalue
\mu_{p_{0},j_{0}}
of the Jacobian
\nabla F^{0}(\mathbf{w})
. Since
j_{0}\notin I_{sup}
and
p_{0}>r
, then
\mu_{p_{0},j_{0}}
must be equal to:

\displaystyle\mu_{p_{0},j_{0}}=1-\lambda^{-2}s_{j_{0}}.

(158)

By assumption
s_{j_{0}}>\lambda^{2}
, therefore
\mu_{p_{0},j_{0}}<0
. Therefore, such critical point cannot be stable.

Stability of critical point of
F^{1}
.

Proof of
Proposition

B.9
.

We will first prove that for any critical point
\mathbf{w}
of the form in
Equation

52
, the Jacobian
\nabla F^{1}(\mathbf{w})
has only non-negative eigenvalues, whereas for any other critical point, there exists a negative eigenvalue.
We will heavily rely on the eigen-decomposition of
\nabla F^{1}(\mathbf{w})
from
Proposition

B.7
which establishes that all its eigenvalues are of the form:

\displaystyle\mu_{p,j}=\begin{cases}1,&j\notin I_{sup}\text{ and }p>r\\
\delta_{p,j},&j\notin I_{sup}\text{ and }p\leq r\\
\frac{4m_{j}}{m_{j}+\lambda}-2,&j=i(p)\text{ and }p\leq r\\
\gamma_{p,p^{\prime}},&p,p^{\prime}\leq r\text{ and }j=i(p^{\prime})>i(p)\\
0,&p^{\prime}\leq r\text{ and }j=i(p^{\prime})\text{ and }\left(\left(p\leq r\text{ and }j<i(p)\right)\text{ or }p>r\right),\end{cases}

(159)

with
\delta_{p,j}
and
\gamma_{p,p^{\prime}}
given by:

\displaystyle\delta_{p,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda(m_{i(p)}+\lambda)}m_{i(p)}s_{i(p)}(s_{i(p)}-s_{j}),

(160)

\displaystyle\gamma_{p,p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(m_{i(p)}+m_{i(p^{\prime})}\right)\left(\left\lvert s_{i(p)}\right\rvert m_{i(p)}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left\lvert s_{i(p^{\prime})}\right\rvert m_{i(p^{\prime})}^{\frac{1}{2}}-s_{i(p)}s_{i(p^{\prime})}\right)}{\left(m_{i(p)}+\lambda\right)\left(m_{i(p^{\prime})}+\lambda\right)}.

Critical points of the form in
Equation

52
are stable.

Here
I_{sup}=I_{sup}^{+}\cup I_{sup}^{-}
.
To see why these critical points are stable, it suffices to check that all eigenvalues
\mu_{p,j}
of the Jacobian of
F^{1}
in
Equation

159
are non-negative. We treat each case separately.

•

j\notin I_{sup}\text{ and }p>r
.
In this case
\mu_{p,j}=1>0
.

•

j\in I_{sup}\text{ and }\left(p>r\text{ or }\left(p\leq r\text{ and }j<i(p)\right)\right)
.
This corresponds to the last case in
Equation

159
for which
\mu_{p,j}=0\geq 0
.

•

j=i(p)\text{ and }p\leq r
.
By definition of the critical point,
m_{i(p)}
is assumed to be positive and of the form
m_{i(p)}^{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\lvert s_{i(p)}\right\rvert+\sqrt{s_{i(p)}^{2}-4\lambda}\right)
. This is only possible if
\left\lvert s_{i(p)}\right\rvert\geq 2\sqrt{\lambda}
, which, in turn, can only happen if
\lambda\leq\frac{1}{4}
since
\left\lvert s_{i}\right\rvert\leq 1
for all
i\in I
.
In this case,
m_{i(p)}^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert s_{i(p)}\right\rvert\geq\sqrt{\lambda}
, so that
m_{i(p)}\geq\lambda
. Recalling that
\mu_{p,j}=\frac{4m_{j}}{m_{j}+\lambda}-2=2\frac{m_{i(p)}-\lambda}{m_{i(p)}+\lambda}
it follows that
\mu_{p,j}\geq 0
.

•

j\notin I_{sup}\text{ and }p\leq r
.
In this case, by assumption
m_{i(p)}>0
which is only possible if
s_{i(p)}\neq 0
. If
s_{i(p)}s_{j}\leq 0
, then necessarily
s_{i(p)}(s_{i(p)}-s_{j})>0

\mu_{p,j}=\delta_{p,j}>0
. If

cannot be such that

. Otherwise,

would belong to the set

, which contains the
r^{+}
highest positive and
r^{-}

. Thus, necessarily
\left\lvert s_{j}\right\rvert<\left\lvert s_{i(p)}\right\rvert
(non-zero eigenvalues are assumed to be distinct). Therefore,
s_{i(p)}(s_{i(p)}-s_{j})>0
and
\mu_{p,j}=\delta_{p,j}>0
.

•

p,p^{\prime}\leq r\text{ and }j=i(p^{\prime})>i(p)
.
In this case,
\mu_{p,j}=\gamma_{p,p^{\prime}}
as defined in
Equation

160
. By definition of the critical point,
m_{i(p)}
and
m_{i(p^{\prime})}
are positive and given by an equation of the form in
Equation

51
, so that
m_{i(p)}^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert s_{i(p)}\right\rvert
and
m_{i(p^{\prime})}^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert s_{i(p^{\prime})}\right\rvert
. Consequently, it follows that:

\mu_{p,j}=\gamma_{p,p^{\prime}}\geq\frac{\left(m_{i(p)}+m_{i(p^{\prime})}\right)\left(\left\lvert s_{i(p)}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert s_{i(p^{\prime})}\right\rvert^{2}-2s_{i(p)}s_{i(p^{\prime})}\right)}{2\left(m_{i(p)}+\lambda\right)\left(m_{i(p^{\prime})}+\lambda\right)}\geq 0.

Strict saddles characterization.

Consider a critical point
\mathbf{w}
of
F^{1}
. By
Proposition

B.6
, it admits a general decomposition of the form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}=Q\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}E_{p,i(p)}\right)M^{\frac{1}{2}},

(161)

where
Q
is an orthogonal matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
,
r
is an integer between
0
and
k
,
I_{sup}=\{i(1),\dots,i(r)\}
is an increasing sequence of integers greater than or equal to
1
, and

is a positive semi-definite diagonal operator on
\ell^{2}(I)
whose only non-zero diagonal coefficients

are given by:

\displaystyle m_{i(p)}^{\frac{1}{2}}=f_{\epsilon_{p}}(s_{i(p)},\lambda),\qquad f_{\epsilon}(s,\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\lvert s\right\rvert+\epsilon\sqrt{s^{2}-4\lambda}\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\lvert s\right\rvert^{2}-4\lambda\geq 0}.

(162)

where

can take any values in
\{-1,1\}
. We are interested in critical points that are not of the form of

. Two possibilities arise:

•

so that

and

•

Case 2
: For all

, we have

and there exists

and

This subsection records the closed-form values that the auxiliary loss

and the trace-form objective

Lemma G.1

(Closed-form value of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}
at the closed-form regressor)
.

For any
(U,V)\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V}
, evaluating
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}
of

Proposition

5.1
at the closed-form

P^{\star}=\Sigma_{U,V}(N_{U,V}+\lambda I)^{-1}
and

Q^{\star}=(N_{U,V}+\lambda I)^{-1}
of
Equation

5

gives

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}(U,V;P^{\star},Q^{\star})=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\bigl[\Tr(N_{U,V})-\Tr(\Sigma_{U,V}(Q^{\star})^{2})\bigr].

(165)

Proof.

From
Equation

73
,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}(U,V;P,Q)=\tfrac{1}{2}\Tr\bigl((QP+\lambda I)N_{U,V}-Q\Sigma_{U,V}\bigr)
.
Setting
P=P^{\star}=\Sigma_{U,V}Q^{\star}
and using the algebraic identity

N_{U,V}Q^{\star}=I-\lambda Q^{\star}
(which follows from
N_{U,V}=(N_{U,V}+\lambda I)-\lambda I
),

\displaystyle\Tr(Q^{\star}P^{\star}N_{U,V})

\displaystyle=\Tr\bigl(Q^{\star}\Sigma_{U,V}Q^{\star}N_{U,V}\bigr)=\Tr\bigl(Q^{\star}\Sigma_{U,V}(I-\lambda Q^{\star})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\Tr(Q^{\star}\Sigma_{U,V})-\lambda\Tr(\Sigma_{U,V}(Q^{\star})^{2}),

where the last equality uses trace cyclicity (
Q^{\star}
symmetric). Substituting,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}(U,V;P^{\star},Q^{\star})=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[\Tr(Q^{\star}\Sigma_{U,V})-\lambda\Tr(\Sigma_{U,V}(Q^{\star})^{2})+\lambda\Tr(N_{U,V})-\Tr(Q^{\star}\Sigma_{U,V})\bigr],

(166)

which simplifies to (
165
).
∎

Equilibrium values of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\text{PEIRA}}
.

The identity (
165
) of
Lemma

G.1

holds for any
(U,V)
. At the stable equilibrium of
Proposition

4.3
,
the spectral filter of
Proposition

4.4
forces

and
\Sigma_{U,V}
to be simultaneously diagonalizable, with

respectively on every active mode
i
and zero elsewhere; in particular,

\Sigma_{U,V}(Q^{\star})^{2}
shares its eigenvalues with

on the
active subspace and both matrices vanish on its orthogonal complement, so

\Tr(N_{U,V})=\Tr(\Sigma_{U,V}(Q^{\star})^{2})
and

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}(U,V;P^{\star},Q^{\star})=0
. The trace-form
objective of

G.3
Effective rank vs.
\lambda
on ImageNet-1K

This subsection tracks the entropy-based effective rank of
PEIRA
’s
backbone and projector embeddings during ImageNet-1K pretraining as a
function of
\lambda
, both at end of training and along the trajectory.

Metric.

We track the entropy-based effective rank as a basis- and label-free summary of representational diversity, sensitive to partial collapse without requiring a labelled probe; this is the standard SSL diagnostic introduced by
Garrido et al. 2023a
. Concretely, for an embedding batch
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
with singular values

\sigma_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{d}
and
p_{i}=\sigma_{i}/\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}
,

\erank(Z)\;=\;\exp\!\left(-\!\sum_{i:p_{i}>0}p_{i}\log p_{i}\right)\;\in\;[1,d].

(167)

Since
\sigma_{i}(Z)=\sqrt{N\,\lambda_{i}(\hat{\Sigma}_{Z})}
with

\hat{\Sigma}_{Z}=Z^{\!\top}\!Z/N
,
\erank
is a one-number summary of the empirical
covariance eigenspectrum.

What is reported.

Figure

G.1
reports end-of-training (epoch
\geq 95
)

\erank
of the backbone (
d{=}2048
) and the projector (
d{=}256
) across

\lambda\in\{0.025,0.05,0.1,0.2,0.5\}
on the
PEIRA
configuration of

Table

G.1
(
1
–
4
seeds per cell,
13
runs total). The
per-epoch backbone-rank trajectory is in
Figure

3
(middle) of
the main text.

Observations.

•

Projector
\erank
sits at
\sim\!248
across every
\lambda
,
within
\sim\!3\%
of the
256
-d cap.

•

Backbone
\erank
is
\sim\!1314
at
\lambda\in\{0.025,0.05\}
and

\sim\!1283
–
1290
at
\lambda\in\{0.1,0.2,0.5\}
, a
\sim\!30
-unit
(
\sim\!2\%
) drop, then a plateau.

•

\erank
stays clear of any collapse regime across the full

20\times

\lambda
span.

Interpretation.

Recall from
Section

4
that
R
denotes the number of
canonical correlations of the data distribution (
Definition

4.1
) and

r_{\mathrm{max}}\leq\min(k,R)
is the largest rank supporting a
non-collapsed equilibrium of
Proposition

4.4
. We draw three conclusions from the
\erank
measurements above. First, the projector (
d{=}256
) sits within
\sim\!3\%
of its dimensionality cap, i.e. the projector output dimension
k=256
that bounds
r_{\mathrm{max}}
: the projector spectrum is set by this cap rather than by
\lambda
whenever
k
binds, which is the regime our sweep operates in. Second, for
\lambda
small relative to the leading
\sqrt{c_{i}}
,
Proposition

4.4
predicts a gradual contraction of the active eigenvalues
\tfrac{1}{4}(\sqrt{c_{i}}-\lambda)^{2}
without a strict-rank drop; the mild
\sim\!2\%
backbone contraction we observe across the
20\times

\lambda
span is consistent with this regime, although we do not claim to observe a change in
r_{\mathrm{max}}
itself. Third,
Proposition

4.4
together with
Proposition

4.3
rules out collapse as a stable equilibrium of the
PEIRA
dynamics, and the absence of any
\erank
collapse on real data corroborates this. Two caveats: the
\lambda
window stays well below the regime in which
Proposition

4.4
would predict visible mode killing (
\lambda\to\sqrt{c_{1}}
), so these numbers do not test the strict-rank prediction
r_{\mathrm{max}}=\#\{i:\sqrt{c_{i}}>\lambda\}
; and
\erank
is a one-number entropy summary, so we do not resolve the full eigenspectrum here.
