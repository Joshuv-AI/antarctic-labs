ABSTRACT
It is paramount to accurately access the risk of facilities and machinery that use hydrogen. Towards
this effort, this report surveys and compares existing models for the ignition of unintended hydrogen
releases, typically leaks. These models are primarily formed on expert experience and perhaps a
few points of data. As such, this report provides suggestions for improvement in ignition probability
modeling, both in terms of experimentation and incident collection as well as modeling. These
modeling approaches are then demonstrated on data gathered from the HIAD incident database.
This report concludes with a discussion of validation techniques for current and future models once
higher quality data becomes available.

1. INTRODUCTION

A quantitative risk assessment can help to inform safety requirements and system design choices
for hydrogen systems. A typical risk assessment analysis consists of two components: first, a
delineation of the possible outcomes and the probability of their occurrence, and second, some
measure of the consequence of each event (in terms of human casualties, property damage, etc.).
A common figure used to summarize these considerations is an event sequence diagram, illustrated
in Figure 1-1, which is taken from [6, Figure 2-1]. The focus of this report is to survey, scrutinize,
and suggest efforts towards improving the probabilities of ignition used in event sequence diagrams
such as Figure 1-1 to produce the more accurate and informative risk assessments.
This report has the following structure. In Chapter 2, we begin with an outline of the current state
of knowledge regarding the underlying physical mechanisms for hydrogen self-ignition. We then
present and compare the models for immediate and delayed present in the literature. Due to the
dearth of data and knowledge of the underlying physics, many of these models are constructed
using largely expert opinion and perhaps a few data points. As such, these models can result
in very different ignition probabilities. The focus of Chapter 3 is then to provide suggestions
for improvement, both in data collection as well as modeling and validation. Regarding data
collection, we refer to several prior studies on hydrogen ignition and provide suggestions for future
experimentation. Similarly, we suggest improvements for incident databases, such as HIAD [4]
and H2Tools [23], so that their entries are more amenable to analysis. The chapter concludes with
suggestions and demonstrations of modeling approaches and validation techniques to attempt when
higher quality data becomes available.

2. SURVEY OF EXISTING METHODS

There are currently many models for the ignition of hydrogen, most naturally divided into models
for immediate ignition and delayed ignition. We define immediate ignition of hydrogen as an
ignition that occurs quickly after a release, and before a sizable vapor cloud or liquid pool forms.
A delayed ignition of hydrogen is defined as the ignition of a hydrogen release that occurs with
some time delay after the release, allowing for a vapor cloud or liquid pool to form. This distinction
in the literature is important as it determines the physical inputs to each model, as well as the
physical outcomes due to the ignition event. For instance, delayed ignition models tend to take
into account cloud area, time, and some assessment of ignition sources present in the surrounding
environment. On the other hand, immediate ignition models focus on temperature and pressure. It
is important to note that there are significantly fewer models for immediate ignition than delayed
ignition. Additionally, there are some models that do not make a distinction between these two
types.

2.1. Fundamental Aspects of Hydrogen Ignition

Before presenting the current models for immediate and delayed ignition, it is important to introduce
some relevant physical properties of hydrogen. Hydrogen has a lower flammability limit of 4%
and an upper flammability limit of 75% by volume in air. Its minimum ignition energy (MIE)
is reported to be between 0.011 and 0.017 mJ, and its autoignition temperature (AIT) is reported
between 752-1085 ◦ F [10, Table 2]. In this report, we will use the lower endpoint of these ranges
for the MIE and AIT in calculations and models.
Despite the above physical characterization, hydrogen has been observed to ignite with no obvious
ignition sources present and at temperatures below its autoignition temperature [1]. This behavior
is concerning for safety and risk assessment. In response, [1] proposes five potential mechanisms
for self-ignition of hydrogen leaks:
 • Joule-Thomson heating: above temperatures of -80 ◦ C (in particular, at ambient tempera-
 ture), compressed hydrogen will heat upon expansion to atmospheric pressure.
 • Electrostatic discharge: since hydrogen has a low MIE, it can be ignited by spark discharges,
 brush discharges, and corona discharges.
 • Diffusion ignition: shock wave formation upon the release of compressed hydrogen into low
 pressure air can cause heating past the AIT.
 • Sudden adiabatic compression: adiabatic compression may increase gas temperature past
 its AIT.

 15

 • Hot surface ignition: contact of a vapor cloud with a hot surface may heat the gas past its
 AIT.

There is evidence in favor and against each of these hypotheses. For instance, [2] provides numerical
evidence for the diffusion mechanism via shock-wave formation, and the many studies summarized
in [24] similarly present evidence in favor of shock-wave formation. At the same time, [1] notes that
the heat provided by Joule-Thomson heating at most relevant pressures is not sufficient to produce
ignition. After an analysis of specific incidents, [1] concludes that potential mechanisms cannot
account for all self-ignition events. Moreover, [1] claims that compression ignition, Joule-Thomson
expansion, diffusion ignition, and hot surface ignition are unlikely ignition mechanisms for hydrogen
leaks at ambient temperature. However, [1] does remark that multiple ignition mechanisms could
be present together.

Of course, it is also essential in risk assessment to acknowledge the possibility of hydrogen ignition
caused by contact with a direct ignition source. Many models, particularly for delayed ignition, take
into account ignition sources in the nearby environment. That being said, assigning a numerical
quantity to the “strength” of a potential ignition source is subject to a great deal of subjectivity and
will certainly affect downstream modeling. Moreover, the strength of an ignition source can change
depending on conditions such as the weather, time of day (daytime or night time), whether the leak
occurs indoors or outdoors, and, if considering a stationary installation, whether it is onshore or
offshore– see [7, Chapter 2] and [14] for more discussion. Many authors present look-up tables for
ignition strength of various potential sources [15, 14, 12, 10].

2.2. Ignition Models

Before introducing the models for hydrogen ignition present in the literature, there are a couple of
important remarks to make. First, many of these models were formed to predict the probability of
ignition for a wide variety of flammable liquids and gases. While many of these models do include
substance-specific physical characteristics (such as AIT, MIE), they are not tailored to hydrogen
and thus may not be completely applicable. Additionally, these models do not present the ranges
of input parameters for which they are valid– for instance, many of these models will produce a
probability of ignition for any collection of inputs, regardless of how physically feasible the inputs
are. Lastly, and perhaps most importantly, the rationale for the parameter values presented in these
models are not always clear. In many cases they were informed (at least partially) by expert opinion.
When parameter estimates were formed based on data, many models did not mention this fact nor
did they reference the data used. While this omission is frustrating from a modeling perspective, it
is important to acknowledge that it occurs due to the sensitive nature of these data. Ultimately, this
issue highlights the importance of better data collection to foster the development of data-informed
models and more accurate risk assessment.

We now present and compare models for the immediate and delayed ignition of hydrogen leaks
with the goal of identifying points of consensus and disagreement in the literature.

temperature, especially near the AIT. This influence is heightened, and observed at tem-
peratures far less than the AIT, in models that include a temperature adjustment for MIE.
Equivalence ratio and pressure are also influential in the models, although pressure appears
to have a weaker influence. The ER becomes especially influential as it diverges from 1.

2.2.2. Delayed Ignition Models

There are many models for the delayed ignition of an unintended flammable liquid release. As
remarked earlier, most delayed ignition models take into account some assessment of the ignition
potential of the environment around the leak– this assessment is necessarily subjective. Thus, even
though many authors present tables of the ignition strengths of various materials to aid in risk
assessment, such as [15, 14, 12, 10], it is important to note that such an assessment is not an exact
science, i.e. a data source or methodology for the derivation of these values is not presented. That
being said, all the models we present consider ignition strength as a parameter in the interval [0, 1]
with a stronger ignition source having a larger value associated to it.

We now present and compare models for delayed ignition. We note that some of the models were
not proposed in their initial studies as models for “delayed ignition,” but since the models have
parameters for quantities such as cloud area and time, we consider the situation being modeled
in alignment with the notion of delayed ignition. The first model we consider is due to Simmons

2.3. Takeaways

For immediate ignition, the consensus of the few existing models is that temperature and pressure
should play an important role. The importance of these two measurements are rooted in the
postulated mechanisms for hydrogen self-ignition. That being said, these models can result in

 25

very different probabilities if one chooses to adjust the MIE based on AIT or ER. While the
adjustment based on ER is justified via experimentation, the basis for the formula provided for
temperature adjustment is less clear. Similarly, there is not a strong basis for how to combine
the probabilities due autoignition and static discharge aside from either producing more or less
conservative probabilities.
Regarding delayed ignition, there appears to be much more disagreement among the many existing
models, with some combinations of physical parameters resulting in predicted delayed ignition
probabilities near 0 for some models and near 1 for others. That being said, the factors that appear
to be important in the many models include cloud area, strength and quantity of ignition sources,
probability ignition source is present, time, and flow rate.
Lastly, for both immediate and delayed ignition there is a great need to investigate the relevancy
of flow rate in these models. Flow rate is the sole input in the tables provided by [22] as well
as [5], but it does not appear in the base form of the delayed ignition models investigated in this
report. That being said, [10, Section 6.2] does introduce a multiplier term involving the flow rate.
Nevertheless, a less ad hoc manner of incorporating flow rate with the remaining models, ideally
in a way suggested by data or physical intuition, would certainly help the rectify which inputs are
more essential to include in a model for delayed ignition.

3. RECOMMENDATIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

As illustrated in the previous section, the state of knowledge regarding the ignition of hydrogen
is not particularly robust, casting doubt on the downstream application of these probabilities in
complicated, multifaceted risk assessments. Improving confidence in the ignition probabilities
will require the collection of higher quality data, both from laboratory experiments and incident
reports. Once this high quality data is obtained, current models can be validated and further
models can be fit as replacements if the data suggests. Lastly, additional data will enable better
uncertainty quantification in the predicted ignition probabilities, providing decision makers with a
more complete risk assessment on which to base their high-consequence decisions.

3.1. Data Collection

Data regarding the ignition of hydrogen come from two settings: experiments performed in a
laboratory and incident reports for an event, typically recorded in a database. These settings
vary drastically; in a laboratory one has (or attempts to) control for as many factors as possible.
As such, well-designed laboratory experiments can help isolate and understand the five proposed
mechanisms for hydrogen self-ignition outlined by [1]. However, a leak or ignition event in a
stationary facility, vehicle, or other structure occurs with potentially little to no warning, and thus
its causes and associated physical measurements (pressure, temperature, leak diameter, etc.) have
to be deduced after the fact, which cannot always be done with acceptable accuracy. While incident
reports can speculate about the causes of ignition events (such as static electricity or prolonged
contact of a cloud with a hot surface), the cause cannot always be known for certain. That being
said, incident databases can allow for assessment of outcomes, such as whether there was a jet flame,
fire, deflagration, detonation, or explosion. Hence, incident reports are more helpful for outcome
prediction rather than identifying the potential physical mechanisms for hydrogen ignition. As such,
this report considers these two types of data separately, providing separate recommendations.

3.1.1. Laboratory Experiments

The most desirable laboratory experiments for producing a probabilistic model of hydrogen ignition
serve two purposes. First, they advance the state of knowledge regarding the causal mechanisms of
ignition. For example, they investigate the validity of the proposed self-ignition mechanisms in [1].
Second, they account for the stochastic nature of ignition by performing replicates for each set of
tested conditions. Examples of studies with these properties include [16, 8, 20, 18]. [16] presents
experiments that aim to isolate the roles of the diffusion and static discharge mechanisms for self-
ignition. Similarly, [20] studies the probability of ignition for lean hydrogen-air mixture, reporting

 27

probabilities as a function of hydrogen concentration and Reynolds number (at a fixed reference
point), and [8] performs multiple tests at various equivalence ratios for hydrogen-methane-air
mixtures to gauge minimum ignition energy. While perhaps not as immediately applicable, [18]
also carefully considers the stochastic nature of ignition by developing a notion of “flammability
factor” that closely matches the ignition probability of a turbulent hydrogen jet.

Many experimental setups fail to capture the stochastic nature of hydrogen ignition. Instead, many
reports just observe conditions that produce ignition. While this can be helpful for providing
evidence for or against the proposed mechanisms of self-ignition, it is difficult to translate these
results to probabilistic ignition models that could be used in risk assessments. For instance, a recent
review article [24] pools results and experimental setups many of which appear to be focused on
quantifying the effect of tube length, diameter, geometry, as well as rupture disk and presence of
obstacles on hydrogen ignition. The results of these studies may be helpful for mitigating ignition
likelihood when designing a facility or other structure. In addition, [24] is able to reason the
likely mechanisms of hydrogen self-ignition at play from the trends that emerge from these studies.
However, many of these studies do not address the stochastic nature of hydrogen ignition; for
instance, in [24, Figure 2] only a critical pressure of self-ignition is plotted. Similarly, [21] gives a
detailed experimental setup to determine how close an ignition source must be to a hydrogen leak
to cause an ignition, but only reports a maximum distance at which ignition occurs. That being
said, these experiments could be performed again with replicates to account for this fact.

It is important to remark that laboratory experiments for hydrogen ignition may struggle to ad-
equately capture the range of conditions which may be desired to be accounted for in a risk
assessment. For instance, corrosion of components or releases caused by impact have effects that
are difficult or impossible to model in a laboratory setting. That being said, laboratory experiments
are a necessary first step towards expanding the state of knowledge, especially with understanding
the role of the proposed self-ignition mechanisms.

Altogether, we present the following suggestions for further experimentation:

 • Experiments should be designed to isolate and study a specific cause of hydrogen self-ignition.

 • Experiments should recognize the stochastic nature of hydrogen self-ignition, performing
 several trials (ideally randomized replicates) for each set of experimental conditions.

 • Ideally should include readings for quantities appearing in many pre-existing models, such
 as temperature (ambient and potentially process), pressure, and flow rate.

Lastly, there appears to be no work towards understanding how the different hypothetical ignition
sources interact with each other. A conservative approach to combining these probabilities would
be to add them together, but additional experimentation could perhaps inform a more suitable
combination method. For instance, the formula for immediate ignition developed in [7] insists
that ignition due to static discharge can only occur if autoignition does not. A different approach
altogether would be to not attempt to work with the explicit causes for self-ignition but instead a
collection of associated readings (temperature, pressure, flow rate, leak diameter, etc.) and build a
model from those considerations. However, such a model would be less physically interpretable.

 28

3.1.2. Incident Reporting

In contrast to laboratory experiments in which hydrogen ignition is observed in closely monitored
circumstances, another source of data for hydrogen ignition is that provided to voluntary ignition
databases. Two large databases for unintended hydrogen releases are provided by [4], which is
maintained by the Joint Research Centre of the European Commission, and [23], which is main-
tained by Pacific Northwest National Laboratory. Both databases consist of voluntarily submitted
event descriptions of hydrogen releases, which may or may not have ultimately ignited. Both
databases include written descriptions of the events and their consequences, frequently listing the
specific component that failed, relevant circumstances leading up the event, personnel injuries or
causalities, and damage to environment or surroundings. Additionally, there are sometimes as-
sociated measurements (regarding temperature, pressure, amount released, etc.) as well as likely
mechanism of ignition, if it occurred.
There are several important limitations to using incident databases that are important to address
before analyzing their data. First, as has been emphasized, the incident data is voluntarily sub-
mitted to these databases, so these data cannot be understood as a comprehensive collection of
all unintended hydrogen releases nor as a representative subset of all such events. For instance,
it is reasonable to suspect that small releases not resulting in ignition are under-reported in these
databases, as they may be ultimately viewed as inconsequential for the reporting facility. However,
these events are essential to accurately develop ignition probabilities that are not unreasonably
conservative. Similarly, there may be business or other incentives to under-report large releases
(at least to these publicly available databases), again rendering it difficult to trust any models for
ignition probability resulting from these databases. Additionally, different incident reporters may
characterize the same event differently, so the labels associated to an event may not be consistent.
For instance, in the HIAD database, an outcome of “fire” frequently appears in event descriptions
with little indication to size or other attributes. Similarly, the physical measurements associated
to each event (pressure, flow rate, etc.) may not be known to a reasonable degree of accuracy to
include in downstream computations or may only include one or two significant digits. Indeed,
many incident reports do not include any physical measurements at all.
We present the following suggestions for future incident reporting:
 • Develop and employ standard definitions for crucial terms in the event description. While
 notions such as “jet flame” and “explosion” may be clear, an event description of “fire” is
 less helpful and can include a wide range of events of vastly different severity.
 • For physical measurements attributed to the event, allow for the reporting of a range or some
 indication of uncertainty or number of significant figures along with a statement of the units.
 Additionally, it should be clear if the reported values are for typical operation or near the
 moment of incident.
 • The inclusion of as many events as possible, especially non-ignition events.
We acknowledge the difficulty in the last recommendation especially. However, the inclusion of all
non-ignition events are essential in providing realism and reducing the overall conservativeness of
the ignition probabilities. Given a specific site with a detailed incident history, it would perhaps be

 29

more realistic to instead produce site-specific ignition probabilities from such an incident history,
specialized to the various properties of the site. Lastly, we remark that while it would be impossible
to retroactively implement these guidelines for events that have already occurred, following these
guidelines going forward could help glean maximum knowledge from future events.

3.2.1. Construction of New Models

In the setting of this report, we typically desire to model either a binary outcome (ignition or no
ignition) or a short list of consequences of interest (no ignition, jet flame, deflagration, explosion,
etc.). As such, binary and multiclass classification algorithms provide a natural class of algorithms
to first consider when constructing new models. Due to the high consequences associated with
ignition, it is important that these models err on the side of being conservative while still providing
a nuanced view of the risk involved. This fact highlights the importance of building models
that are highly interpretable and provide uncertainty quantification (in the form of confidence or
prediction intervals) so that decision makers are provided with as much information as possible.
This constraint renders many deep learning approaches as not particularly desirable. Binary
classification models such as logistic and probit regression [9] provide both desirable features. As
generalized linear models, these models are very interpretable, and they can be fit in a Bayesian
framework, allowing for very detailed uncertainty quantification– see [9, Chapter 21] for more
details. Additionally, Gaussian process classifiers, while producing less interpretable models (as the
method is nonparametric), can also enable uncertainty quantification– see [13] for a comprehensive
overview of Gaussian processes.
As illustrated in Tables 2-3 and 2-5, there are a range of inputs that one many want to measure and
include in a new model for ignition probability. Feature selection criteria (such as AIC, BIC, etc.)
and sensitivity analysis can help modelers identify and remove models that do not have much of an
influence on the predicted ignition probabilities or are otherwise superfluous. On the other hand, it
is important that variable selection is done closely with subject-matter experts so that as much of
the underlying physics is captured in the models as possible.
From the delayed ignition models investigated in this report, we see that there is disagreement on
what “model form” is correct. For this reason, as well as the absence of strong physical evidence to
choose a specific model form, nonparametric models such as Gaussian process (GP) classifiers can

 30

be a great starting point for modeling. [13, Chapter 3] provides a great introduction to using GPs
for classification tasks. Since GPs are nonparametric, they produce very flexible models that adjust
to peculiarities in the data that a modeler may miss if they were to fit a parametric model, such as a
linear regression. That being said, there is a risk of overfitting and potential loss of interpretability
of results.

Another commonly used binary classification performance metric is the area under the Receiver

to the “chance model”– that is, the model that equally at random assigns data points to one of the
two classes. This line can provide a helpful reference to determine if a model is performing better
than chance.

4. CONCLUSION

In conclusion, there is much room for improvement in the state of knowledge regarding hydrogen
ignition, regarding both the underlying physical mechanisms and modeling. While [1] proposes
five potential mechanisms for hydrogen self-ignition, there is still a need to experimentally quantify
the role each mechanism plays in ignition as well as how the different mechanisms interact with
each other. Similarly, while databases such as HIAD [4] and H2Tools [23] provide the hydrogen
and risk assessment community a great service by collating a large number of incidents in a publicly
available database, there are still ways to mitigate subjectivity in outcome description and make
the entries as amenable to statistical analysis as possible, maximizing the knowledge gleaned from
these incidents. Advances in the knowledge of the physical foundations of ignition and the quality
of incident reports will enable improved modeling, allowing for the creation of data-informed
models that rely less on expert opinion, which we observed can result in vastly different ignition
probabilities for the same collection of circumstances. To provide the most information-rich risk
assessments, future modeling should also aim to provide uncertainty quantification in the form
of prediction or confidence intervals for their ignition probabilities. As illustrated in Figure 4-1,
improvements in the state of knowledge regarding hydrogen ignition will be cyclical, with new data
resulting in new models, in turn begetting new insight that can inform further experimentation.
