 ABSTRACT

This report fulfills the requirement for milestone M3AT-26OR0605091, “Provide status on activities on
molten salt intrusion and wetting studies,” due September 30, 2026. The report contains updates and
molten salt intrusion and wetting behavior of molten salts on graphite surfaces as well as a list of
presentations and participation in conferences and meetings.

 1. SALT INTRUSION STUDIES

During FY 2026, several key activities were undertaken in support of the ongoing research on molten salt
intrusion and wetting studies. First, the team continued to address the edits requested by reviewers for the
manuscript previously submitted for publication. This editorial process involved three cycles of revisions
before the document was ultimately accepted. Simultaneously, coordination efforts were made to
facilitate neutron computed tomography (nCT) imaging of three graphite samples that had been exposed
to FLiBe and one pristine sample used as a reference. These samples were provided by Kairos Energy.
Analysis of the results is ongoing, and publication of the results is planned. In addition to these efforts,
work progressed toward developing a reliable procedure for measuring the contact angle of molten salt on
graphite surfaces. Early measurements revealed significant variability, prompting the team to conduct
detailed and controlled experiments using highly oriented pyrolytic graphite (HOPG). These experiments
were designed to better understand the variables affecting measurement accuracy and reproducibility.
Finally, preparations were initiated for long-term salt exposure experiments using static capsules. With
these planned exposures, we aim to further investigate the effects of molten salt on graphite materials
over extended periods. More details of these activities are presented in the following sections.

1.1 SALT INTRUSION STUDIES: PUBLISHED WORK

Manuscript Title: Viewing is understanding: Graphite microstructure effects on infiltrated molten salt
distribution revealed by 3D neutron tomography

Authors: Jisue Moon, Nidia C. Gallego, Cristian I. Contescu, James R. Keiser, Yuxuan Zhang, Hassina
Bilheux

Publishing details:
 Journal: Carbon, Published by Elsevier
 Journal Impact Factor: 12.7
 Date: January 2026
   Article: 120903
   Volume: 246
   OSTI ID: 3007919
   https://doi-org.ornl.idm.oclc.org/10.1016/j.carbon.2025.120903

Abstract:

Molten salt infiltration in the pore network of nuclear graphite may cause unwanted changes to graphite’s
local structure and mechanical and thermal properties. A detailed and comprehensive understanding of
molten salt intrusion (distribution across sample cross section and penetration depth) is needed to assess
its effects. In this work, we report on an improved methodology for the use of neutron imaging (computed
tomography) to evaluate salt penetration and distribution of a wide range of graphite grades with diverse
microstructures. Neutron tomography data were acquired on the same graphite sample before and after
salt intrusion; the 3D reconstructed volumes were digitally co-registered and subtracted. The difference in

 1

neutron attenuation coefficient represents direct visualization of FLiNaK (LiF–NaF–KF) salt distribution
in the salt-impregnated graphite samples. This improved methodology was applied to investigate the
effect of exposure times (12 h and 336 h) and of graphite microstructure when exposed to FLiNaK at 750
°C and 3 bar (gauge) pressure, starting from flowing argon at near atmospheric pressure. The results show
that medium-grained and fine-grained graphites evolve to equilibrium at significantly different rates: fast
salt uptake in medium-grained graphites produces salt deposits throughout the volume of graphite
specimens, whereas salt infiltration in fine-grained graphites is much slower and limited to exposed
surfaces.

Conclusions:

The molten FLiNaK salt infiltration of six graphite grades with various microstructures was investigated
by neutron tomography. The graphite samples were exposed to molten FLiNaK at 750 °C and 3 bar
(gauge) pressure for 12 and 336 h.

The results at the longest impregnation time (336 h) indicate that, for the temperature and pressure used in
this study, salt impregnation in fine grain graphite grades (IG-110, 2114, NBG-25, and ETU-10) was
limited to a narrow subsurface zone, whereas for medium grain graphite grades (PCEA and NBG-18), the
impregnated salt reached farther into the core of the sample.

Although minor differences were observed for fine grain graphite samples as a function of exposure time,
medium grain graphite samples saw a significant difference in the impregnation behavior between the
samples exposed for 12 h and those exposed for 336 h. This difference indicates that the two structurally
different graphite types evolve toward equilibrium at significantly different rates. The limited data suggest
that slow infiltration rates might be expected if the initial contact of graphite with molten salt occurs near
atmospheric pressure (a practical scenario) rather than in a vacuum (as investigated by previous studies).

The visualizations generated with the neutron tomography provided valuable insights into the distribution
of the infiltrated salt within the sample, highlighting significant differences between fine/superfine
graphite samples and medium grain graphite samples. These differences were evident in both the quantity
of infiltrated salt and its penetration depth and distribution—information that cannot be obtained through
gravimetric measurements alone.

1.2 ONGOING ACTIVITIES

1.2.1 Neutron Computed Tomography Imaging of FLiBe-Exposed Graphite Samples

Three graphite samples that had been exposed to FLiBe, along with a pristine graphite reference sample,
were provided by our collaborator Kairos for evaluation of salt intrusion behavior. The samples
represented a range of FLiBe salt uptake levels: 0%, 3.5%, 9.3%, and 11.7%. To facilitate imaging, our
team submitted a user proposal to the High Flux Isotope Reactor Multimodal Advanced Radiography
Station (MARS) beamline and managed the shipping of the samples. Special handling procedures were
required due to the presence of beryllium in the samples.

All four samples have been successfully imaged, and 3D reconstructed volumes have been obtained.
Detailed analysis of the reconstructed data is currently underway. The results of this analysis will be
published once the evaluation is complete.

1.2.2 Long-Term Exposure of Graphite to Molten Salt Using Static Capsule Technique

The long-term behavior of graphite in molten salt environments is not yet well understood. Previous
experiments by our team have involved exposing graphite samples to molten salt for up to 2 weeks
(336 h). However, additional data covering longer exposure durations is necessary to better understand
graphite’s performance under these conditions. To address this need, the static capsule process is being
implemented. This approach involves placing multiple graphite samples and exposing them to molten salt
in sealed capsules. The sealed environment allows for controlled testing conditions and supports the
evaluation of cyclic exposures, including repeated heating and cooling cycles. During FY 2026,
cylindrical graphite samples were prepared and the necessary materials for capsule fabrication were
acquired. The total number of samples to be exposed, as well as the temperature and duration of each
experiment, will be determined based on future funding allocations.

1.3 WETTING STUDIES: MEASUREMENT OF CONTACT ANGLE (WORK ACCEPTED
 FOR PUBLICATION)

Manuscript Title: High-temperature surface energy and interfacial energy of molten FLiNaK–highly
oriented pyrolytic graphite (HOPG) system from selected contact angle measurement.

Authors: Jisue Moon, Gang Seob Jung, Nidia C. Gallego

Publishing details:
 Journal: Industrial & Engineering Chemistry Research – ACS Publications
 Journal Impact Factor: 3.9
 Date: September 18, 2026
   https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.iecr.6c02718.
 OSTI ID: not available yet

Abstract

Accurate high-temperature surface and interfacial energies are essential for understanding molten salt
interactions with nuclear graphite. This study examined how initial pellet geometry and temperature affect
the wetting of FLiNaK on highly oriented pyrolytic graphite (HOPG). Sessile-drop experiment using 1.5
and 3 mm pellets showed that precursor geometry influences transient contact angles and the time
required to reach a common long-time value. Capillary forces dominated, while minor gravity effects for
3mm pellets could not be excluded. Observed variations were mainly attributed to droplet shape evolution
and contact-line pinning. The Neumann equation of state was used to estimate HOPG surface energy,
yielding approximately 145 mN/m at 500 °C and 120 mN/m at 800 °C. Solid-liquid interfacial energies
were consistently 30-40 mN/m below solid-gas values, indicating partial wetting dominated by physical
interactions. These findings establish a reproducible method and show that contact angle convergence
must be demonstrated before interfacial energy analysis.

Conclusions:

This study investigated the wetting behavior of molten FLiNaK on HOPG across a temperature range of
500–800 °C. Sessile drop experiments revealed that apparent contact angles were influenced by the initial
droplet geometry, underscoring the need for careful control of pellet preparation and measurement
protocols when assessing high-temperature wetting behavior.

By applying the Neumann EOS, we extracted surface energy values for HOPG from the contact angle and
surface tension data. The results showed only a modest decrease in surface energy (γs) with temperature,

 3

with values spanning approximately 145 mN/m at 500 °C to 120 mN/m at 800 °C. Sensitivity analysis
confirmed that the observed trend is robust within experimental uncertainty, although the absolute values
remain semiempirical due to the extrapolation of the EOS interaction parameter β beyond its original
ambient temperature derivation.

These findings provide two key insights. First, they establish a reproducible methodology for extracting
solid surface energies in molten salt environments using single-liquid data—a necessity given the
impracticality of multi-liquid methods at high temperatures. Second, they provide a reliable baseline for
the HOPG basal plane against which the more complex wetting behavior of nuclear-grade graphite can be
evaluated thoroughly. Because the performance of graphitic materials in MSRs depends on their
resistance to salt infiltration and interfacial degradation, a clear understanding of the intrinsic surface
energetics of HOPG is an essential step toward interpreting and predicting the behavior of practical
graphite components. Future work should extend this analysis to polycrystalline nuclear-grade graphite
materials and evaluate the role of surface chemistry and irradiation damage on salt wetting and
infiltration.
