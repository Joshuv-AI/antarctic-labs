Symptoms of depression, suicidal ideation, and personality traits in a sample of medical students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Stipe Vidović, Niko Morović, Ivanka Maduna, Marija Heffer, Dunja Degmečić, Marija Jelić Vuković, Slavica Kozina, Zenon Pogorelić
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedical education is associated with substantial psychological demands, and accumulating evidence indicates a high prevalence of depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation among medical students, in some studies exceeding levels observed in non-medical student populations. However, data from Croatia remain limited, and the role of personality traits as potential vulnerability or protective factors remains insufficiently explored. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation among medical students and to examine their associations with personality traits. We hypothesized that higher levels of depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation would be associated with lower levels of emotional stability, conscientiousness, and extraversion.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted among medical students at the University of Split between 15 October and 15 November 2025. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). Personality traits (extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, emotional stability, and intellect/imagination) were measured using the International Personality Item Pool–50 (IPIP-50).ResultsA total of 278 students were included (72.3% female; median age 22 years, IQR 20–24). At least mild depressive symptoms were reported by 61.2% of participants, while 9% exhibited moderately severe to severe symptoms. Suicidal ideation was reported by 12.2%. Female students reported higher depression scores compared with males (p < 0.001). Students with suicidal ideation showed significantly lower levels of extraversion (p < 0.001), conscientiousness (p < 0.001), and emotional stability (p < 0.001). Depression scores were negatively correlated with emotional stability (ρ = −0.468, p < 0.001), conscientiousness (ρ = −0.28, p < 0.001), agreeableness (ρ = −0.12, p < 0.05), and extraversion (ρ = −0.216, p < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, emotional stability (β = −1.088, p < 0.001), conscientiousness (β = −0.284, p = 0.007), and extraversion (β = −0.164, p = 0.047) emerged as the strongest independent correlates of depressive symptom severity.ConclusionDepressive symptoms and suicidal ideation were highly prevalent among medical students. Depressive symptom severity was negatively associated with several personality traits, particularly emotional stability, conscientiousness, and extraversion, which emerged as the strongest correlates. These findings highlight the relevance of personality traits in understanding depressive symptoms among student population and support the need for further longitudinal research and mental health prevention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08329-x.Keywords: Depression, Suicidal ideation, Personality traits, Medical students, Students, Mental health, Mental disorders

Background

Depression is a major global public health concern, affecting more than 300 million people worldwide and representing one of the leading causes of disability and diminished quality of life [1]. It is frequently associated with suicidal behaviors, including suicidal ideation, suicide planning, and suicide attempts [1, 2]. Suicide remains one of the leading causes of death among young adults aged 15–29 years, highlighting the profound individual and societal burden associated with depressive disorders [3].

Medical students constitute a population that may be particularly vulnerable to adverse mental health outcomes. Compared with age-matched peers in the general population, they appear to experience a higher burden of depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation [4]. The structure of medical education is often characterized by sustained academic pressure and substantial emotional demands, including excessive workloads, high performance expectations, sleep disruption, chronic stress, and prolonged clinical exposure [2, 5–7]. During clinical training, repeated encounters with human suffering, severe illness, and death may further compound psychological distress [2, 5–7]. Collectively, these factors are associated with an elevated prevalence of depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation among medical students [2, 8].

Individual differences in vulnerability to depression and suicidality may, in part, be explained by personality traits. Within the Five-Factor Model—extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, neuroticism, and intellect/imagination—personality traits have been recognized as important psychological determinants of mental health outcomes [9–13]. In particular, high neuroticism, characterized by heightened emotional reactivity, negative affectivity, and stress sensitivity, has been consistently associated with depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation [14]. Conversely, extraversion, conscientiousness, and emotional stability have been linked to greater emotional resilience, more effective coping strategies, and improved psychological well-being [10, 15]. By contrast, findings regarding agreeableness and intellect/imagination remain less consistent, with considerable heterogeneity across studies [15, 16].

Medical students are exposed to prolonged academic, emotional, and clinical stressors, within which personality traits may play a key role in shaping stress appraisal, emotion regulation, and help-seeking behavior [17, 18]. Despite a growing body of research examining personality and mental health, studies focusing specifically on medical students remain limited, particularly within national contexts [9, 19, 20].

In Croatia, existing research indicates a high prevalence of depressive symptoms among medical students, both during and following the COVID-19 pandemic, with reported rates of suicidal ideation exceeding 10% [5, 21–24]. However, prior studies have primarily examined sociodemographic and lifestyle correlates of depression, while the role of personality traits as potential vulnerability or protective factors for depressive symptoms and suicidality has received limited empirical attention [5, 21–24].

Therefore, the present study aimed to quantify the prevalence of depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation among medical students in Croatia and to examine their associations with personality traits conceptualized within the Five-Factor Model.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a cross-sectional study conducted among medical students at the School of Medicine, University of Split, Croatia.

Recruitment method

Participants were recruited via official university email accounts. An electronic invitation was sent to all medical students enrolled at the School of Medicine, University of Split. The invitation included a Participant Information Sheet outlining the study objectives, design, data collection procedures, and overall methodology. Participation was voluntary, the questionnaire was fully anonymous, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without providing a reason. Only students who chose to participate and completed the questionnaire were included in the final study sample. The questionnaire was administered using Google Forms and accessed via a secure link included in the invitation email. Prior to completing the questionnaire, participants were required to provide electronic informed consent (Supplement File 1). The initial invitation was distributed on 15 October 2025, and the survey remained open until 15 November 2025, at which point data collection was completed. The recruitment procedure followed a standardized survey-based approach commonly used in studies of student mental health, and data for the present study were collected independently [5, 24, 25].

Questionnaire

The study questionnaire was administered in Croatian and consisted of 73 items organized into three sections, with an estimated completion time of 15–20 min. An English translation of the questionnaire is provided in Supplementary File 1.

The first section assessed sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics. Collected variables included sex, age, body weight and height, year of study, place of residence, living arrangements, and self-perceived financial status. Lifestyle factors included alcohol consumption, smoking status, and use of psychoactive substances. Participants also reported whether they had ever considered seeking, or had previously sought, professional psychological or psychiatric help. The sociodemographic and lifestyle items were adapted from instruments used in our previous studies among medical students [5, 24, 25].

The second section assessed depressive symptoms using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [26]. The PHQ-9 is a widely used self-report instrument consisting of nine items corresponding to the diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder as defined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM). The instrument has demonstrated good psychometric properties and a largely unidimensional factorial structure across numerous international studies [26]. Each item evaluates the frequency of depressive symptoms experienced over the preceding two weeks and is rated on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). Total PHQ-9 scores were calculated by summing responses across all items, yielding a score range from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating greater severity of depressive symptoms. Established cut-off values were applied to classify depression severity as none-minimal (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), moderately severe (15–19), and severe (20–27). Item 9 of the PHQ-9, which assesses thoughts of self-harm or suicide, was used as an indicator of suicidal ideation, with a score of ≥ 1 indicating the presence of suicidal thoughts/ ideation. A Croatian translation of the PHQ-9 is publicly available and has been used in previous studies conducted in Croatian samples, demonstrating good internal consistency [23, 26]. Internal consistency of the PHQ-9 in the present sample was good (Cronbach’s α = 0.87).

The third section assessed personality traits using the International Personality Item Pool–50 (IPIP-50) questionnaire, which measures the five major personality dimensions: extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, emotional stability, and intellect/imagination [9, 20]. The IPIP-50 consists of 50 items, with 10 items assigned to each personality dimension. Participants rated their agreement with each statement on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very inaccurate) to 5 (very accurate). Negatively keyed items were reverse-scored prior to analysis. Final scores for each personality trait were calculated as the mean of the corresponding 10 items, resulting in a possible score range from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater expression of the respective trait. A Croatian version of the IPIP markers has previously been examined in a Croatian sample, demonstrating that the instrument reproduces the expected five-factor structure and shows satisfactory psychometric properties [27]. Accordingly, the Croatian version of the IPIP-50 was used in the present study [27]. Internal consistency coefficients in the current sample were satisfactory, with Cronbach’s α values of 0.850 for Extraversion, 0.795 for Agreeableness, 0.826 for Conscientiousness, 0.895 for Emotional Stability, and 0.806 for Intellect/imagination.

Sample size

Sample size estimation was performed using Cochran’s formula with finite population correction. Assuming a conservative expected prevalence of 50%, a 95% confidence level, and a margin of error of 5%, and accounting for the finite size of the target population 555, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 228 participants.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using visual inspection of histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test and inspection of Q–Q plots. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean (standard deviation [SD]) and were compared using the independent samples t-test, whereas non-normally distributed variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Effect sizes for non-parametric comparisons were estimated using the rank biserial correlation (r) and Cohen’s d for parametric comparisons. Sex differences across the five personality traits were further examined using multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA). Effect sizes were expressed as partial eta squared (η²ₚ). Differences in categorical variables were evaluated using the χ² test. Associations between depression score (PHQ-9 total score) and personality traits were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ). The strength of correlations was interpreted according to the magnitude of the coefficients. Associations between personality trait scores and PHQ-9 scores were analyzed using bivariate and multivariable linear regression analyses, with the PHQ-9 score specified as the dependent variable. The regression analyses were conducted within an exploratory framework to examine potential associations between personality traits and depressive symptoms. Personality traits that were significantly associated with PHQ-9 scores in the bivariate analyses were subsequently included in the multivariable regression model. Normality of residuals was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of Q–Q plots, while homoscedasticity was evaluated by inspection of residual plots. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Jamovi (version 2.6; The jamovi project, 2025).

Consequently, the final sample comprised 278 participants, thereby meeting the minimum required sample size for statistical analysis.

Discussion

The present study identified a substantial burden of depressive symptoms and suicidality among medical students at the University of Split. Furthermore, analyses examining the relationship between depression and personality traits revealed significant negative associations between levels of depressive symptoms and the traits of extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, and emotional stability.

The results of the present study indicate that nearly six out of ten participants reported at least mild depressive symptoms, while almost one-tenth exhibited moderately severe to severe symptoms of depression. It should be noted, however, that the majority of these cases fell within the mild symptom range, whereas only a smaller proportion of students reported moderately severe to severe symptoms. Accordingly, these findings should primarily be interpreted as reflecting an elevated burden of depressive symptoms rather than the prevalence of probable clinical depression. The high levels of depressive symptoms observed in this study are comparable to those reported in previous cross-sectional studies conducted among medical students in Croatia during and after the COVID-19 pandemic [5, 21–24]. Findings from a recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Paiva et al. (2025), encompassing 1,655 studies examining depressive symptoms among students worldwide, indicate that approximately 35% of students reported mild, 25% moderate, and 13% severe depressive symptoms, which is broadly consistent with the distribution observed in the present study, suggesting that depressive symptoms represent a substantial concern across student populations [28]. Moreover, our findings align with evidence demonstrating similarly elevated levels of depressive symptoms among healthcare students during and following the COVID-19 pandemic [28]. Compared with pre-pandemic estimates, the prevalence of depressive symptoms of this study appears to be higher, supporting prior evidence that depressive symptom burden increased during the pandemic and remained elevated in the post-pandemic period [28, 29]. The substantial burden of depressive symptoms observed in this study may reflect a combination of general student-related stressors and factors specific to medical training. Medical education is characterized by intensive academic workload, frequent examinations, early and prolonged clinical exposure, limited opportunities for recovery, and heightened emotional demands, which may contribute to psychological distress. However, because the present study did not include a comparison group of students from other academic disciplines, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that medical students necessarily experience a greater burden of depressive symptoms than students in general. These stressors may also fluctuate across the academic year, with examination periods potentially representing particularly demanding phases for students [6, 30].

When stratified by sex, female students reported significantly higher levels of depressive symptoms compared with their male counterparts. This finding is consistent with evidence from previous cross-sectional studies conducted in Croatia that have documented sex-specific differences in depression prevalence [5, 22–25]. Moreover, similar patterns have been reported in large-scale meta-analyses and systematic reviews, which consistently demonstrate a greater burden of depressive symptomatology among female students [28, 29]. The observed sex-related disparity likely arises from the complex interplay of biological, psychological, and social mechanisms operating simultaneously rather than from a single explanatory pathway [31–33]. Existing literature suggests that differences in neuroendocrine functioning, emotional regulation, coping patterns, and help-seeking behavior, together with unequal exposure to academic demands and gendered social expectations, may collectively contribute to the consistently higher levels of depressive symptomatology observed among female medical students [29, 31–33]. However, the absence of a significant sex effect in the multivariate model indicates that these observed differences may be attenuated when individual personality traits are taken into account. In particular, emotional stability and conscientiousness, both strongly associated with depressive symptom severity, may partially account for the apparent sex-related differences in depressive symptoms.

Consistent with existing meta-analytic evidence, suicidal ideation was reported by slightly more than one-tenth of participants in the present sample [2]. Students who endorsed suicidal ideation exhibited significantly higher levels of depressive symptoms, underscoring the close link between depression severity and suicidality. The prevalence observed in this study closely mirrors that reported among medical students in other Croatian settings [24]. Notably, a substantial proportion of participants reported having considered seeking professional mental health care, highlighting both the extent of psychological distress and the need for accessible, targeted mental health services within medical training environments.

The observed associations between depressive symptom severity and personality traits are consistent with contemporary models linking personality characteristics to negative affective states [11, 34, 35]. One possible explanation for the association between depressive symptoms and extraversion is that lower levels of extraversion may be associated with reduced engagement in rewarding social interactions, as well as a greater tendency toward social withdrawal and rumination, which may contribute to the persistence or intensification of depressive symptoms [11, 36]. Furthermore, the relationship between depressive symptoms and agreeableness may be explained by the fact that lower levels of agreeableness can reflect interpersonal difficulties and conflict-prone interaction styles, leading to more frequent experiences of interpersonal conflict, distrust, and perceived social isolation. These factors may, in turn, exacerbate social stressors implicated in depression [37]. In line with the multivariate regression findings of the present study, conscientiousness emerged as an independent negative predictor of depressive symptom severity. Lower conscientiousness is likely linked to difficulties in self-regulation, goal-directed behavior, and the use of adaptive coping strategies [11, 38]. Challenges in maintaining structure, delaying immediate gratification, and fulfilling obligations may foster feelings of overwhelm, failure, and demotivation, thereby increasing vulnerability to depressive symptoms [11, 38]. In contrast, higher conscientiousness may function as a protective factor by supporting effective organization, persistence, and adaptive management of academic and daily demands, which may mitigate the development or exacerbation of depressive symptoms [11, 38]. Notably, emotional stability emerged as the strongest independent predictor of depressive symptom severity in the present study, even after adjustment for age, sex, and other personality traits. Low emotional stability (i.e., high neuroticism) is a well-established risk factor for depression, reflecting heightened emotional reactivity, elevated negative affectivity, and maladaptive stress appraisal processes [11, 39, 40]. It is important to emphasize that these associations are likely bidirectional. While personality traits may contribute to vulnerability to depressive symptoms, depressive states may also transiently influence self-reported personality characteristics. This consideration is particularly relevant for emotional stability (the inverse of neuroticism), which conceptually overlaps with core features of depression such as negative affectivity and emotional reactivity and may therefore be partly influenced by current mood state. This reciprocal relationship represents an inherent limitation of cross-sectional study designs and highlights the need for longitudinal research to disentangle trait vulnerability from state-dependent effects.

From an applied perspective, these findings underscore the potential utility of brief, psychometrically robust self-report measures of personality traits and depressive symptoms as efficient screening tools for identifying individuals at elevated risk for adverse mental health outcomes [11, 41, 42]. Incorporating personality assessment into routine mental health screening may facilitate earlier identification of vulnerability profiles, inform stratified prevention strategies, and support more personalized intervention planning, particularly in non-clinical or resource-limited settings [43]. In this context, personality or temperament-based screening approaches implemented during medical training could help identify students who may be at increased risk of psychological distress or suicidality and who might benefit from targeted psychological support or preventive interventions.

Strengths, limitations and further directions

The present study has several strengths. It contributes to the limited body of evidence on depressive symptoms, suicidal ideation, and personality traits among medical students in Croatia. The final sample exceeded the minimum required size, supporting the robustness of the statistical analyses. In addition, the use of validated instruments (PHQ-9 and IPIP-50), both demonstrating good internal consistency in the present sample, strengthens the reliability of the findings. Furthermore, by examining both depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation in relation to Big Five personality traits, this study provides a more comprehensive insight into psychological factors associated with student mental health.

Notwithstanding these strengths, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. Due to its cross-sectional design, temporal relationships between the examined variables could not be established, thereby precluding any causal inferences. Data collection was conducted during the October–November period of the academic year. Although this timing was determined by logistical considerations rather than by a predefined assumption regarding stress levels, psychological distress among students may fluctuate across the academic year, particularly during examination periods, which in Croatian medical schools are typically scheduled in February, June–July, and September. Therefore, the observed levels of depression and anxiety symptoms may not fully capture potential temporal variations associated with examination-related academic stress. Future studies should employ longitudinal designs with repeated measurements across different periods of the academic year, including examination sessions, to better understand temporal patterns in student mental health.

Depressive symptoms and personality traits were assessed using self-reported questionnaires (PHQ-9 and IPIP-50), which may be subject to reporting and recall bias, as well as social desirability effects. Although these instruments are well validated, future studies would benefit from incorporating structured clinical interviews conducted by psychiatrists or clinical psychologists to enhance diagnostic accuracy. In addition, given that a substantial proportion of participants fell within the mild symptom range, PHQ-9 categories should be interpreted as reflecting symptom burden rather than clinically confirmed depressive disorders. Mild scores may capture transient distress or situational reactions (e.g., short-term academic strain) rather than persistent depressive pathology, and therefore prevalence estimates may partly reflect subclinical symptomatology. Suicidal ideation was assessed using a single PHQ-9 item, which may not fully capture the complexity, severity, or temporal dynamics of suicidality. In addition, this item does not distinguish between passive and active suicidal ideation nor assess suicidal intent; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as indicating the presence of suicidal thoughts rather than a comprehensive evaluation of suicidality. The use of more comprehensive and multidimensional instruments could provide a more nuanced assessment of suicidal thoughts and behaviours in future research.

The present study focused exclusively on the associations between depressive symptoms, suicidality, and personality traits, while other potentially relevant variables were not assessed. A more comprehensive research approach is therefore recommended, incorporating factors such as sociodemographic characteristics, academic workload and stressors, physical activity, dietary habits, sleep quality, social media use, and adverse childhood experiences or trauma, all of which may influence mental health outcomes. In addition, anxiety symptoms were not assessed in the present study. Given the well-established comorbidity between major depressive disorder and generalized anxiety disorder among student populations, the inclusion of validated measures of anxiety and stress (e.g., the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 [GAD-7] and the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale-21 [DASS-21]) would provide a more comprehensive assessment of students’ mental health in future studies [44, 45].

Furthermore, given the cross-sectional nature of the study, it remains unclear whether personality traits represent stable vulnerability factors for depressive symptoms and suicidality or whether current depressive states transiently influence self-perceived personality profiles. Longitudinal research is needed to disentangle these potentially bidirectional relationships.

Finally, this was a single-center study conducted on a relatively small sample of medical students, and the response rate was 56%, which, although acceptable for survey-based research, introduces the possibility of non-response bias, as non-respondents may differ from respondents in their attitudes or experiences. Furthermore, participation in the study was voluntary, and the final sample consisted only of students who agreed to complete the questionnaire. This self-selection may introduce selection bias, as students experiencing greater psychological distress may have been either more motivated to participate or, conversely, less likely to respond due to stigma or emotional burden. Consequently, the reported prevalence of depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation may have been either overestimated or underestimated. In addition, the sample was predominantly female (72%). Although a higher proportion of female students is commonly observed in medical education, the sex imbalance in the present sample should be considered when interpreting the generalizability of the findings. These factors limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should employ multicenter designs, include students from other medical faculties across Croatia, including the Faculty of Medicine in Zagreb, the Faculty of Medicine in Rijeka, the Faculty of Medicine in Osijek, and the School of Medicine at the Catholic University of Croatia, and extend the sample to include non-medical students to enable broader comparisons, enhance generalizability, and improve external validity. The aforementioned limitations are consistent with those reported in previous cross-sectional studies [5, 21–24].

Conclusions

This study identified a substantial burden of depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation among medical students, with most reported symptoms falling within the mild range. Depressive symptom severity was negatively associated with several personality traits, particularly emotional stability, conscientiousness, and extraversion. These findings highlight the relevance of personality traits in understanding depressive symptoms among medical students and support the need for further multicenter longitudinal research and the development of structured mental health screening and prevention programs within student populations.
