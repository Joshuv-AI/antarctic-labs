Protein consumption during pregnancy and risk of gestational diabetes mellitus: Insights from a multi‐center case–control study
Authors: Marzieh Zare, Elnaz Daneshzad, Hatav Tehrani, Razieh Tabaeifard, Maryam Mahmoudi, Leila Azadbakht
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) poses major health risks for mothers and infants. Evidence on the association between dietary protein intake and GDM risk remains inconsistent. This study investigated dietary protein quantity and quality in relation to GDM.MethodsA hospital‐based case–control study was conducted in Isfahan, Iran, involving 200 cases and 263 controls. Dietary intake was assessed using three 24‐h dietary records, and protein intake was categorized into plant‐ and animal‐based sources. Multivariate logistic regression models were applied to evaluate associations.ResultsHigher plant protein intake was associated with a significantly lower risk of GDM (OR = 0.34; 95% CI: 0.20–0.59). Similarly, higher seafood (OR = 0.40; 95% CI: 0.24–0.68) and egg intake (OR = 0.59; 95% CI: 0.35–0.98) were inversely associated with GDM risk. In contrast, total protein, animal protein, poultry, dairy protein, aromatic amino acids, and branched‐chain amino acids showed no significant or meaningful trend with GDM risk.ConclusionsOur study did not identify significant associations between total or animal protein intake during pregnancy and GDM risk. However, higher consumption of plant protein, seafood, and eggs was associated with lower odds of GDM in multivariable‐adjusted models that accounted for BMI, total energy intake, socioeconomic status, and other relevant confounders. Given the observational case–control design, these findings cannot establish causality, and the possibility of residual confounding remains. Nevertheless, the results suggest that protein‐intake quality may play a role in GDM risk, warranting confirmation in prospective cohorts and randomized trials.Keywords: diabetes, pregnancy, proteins

INTRODUCTION

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), the predominant metabolic disorder in pregnancy, entails carbohydrate intolerance, resulting in varying degrees of elevated blood sugar levels, and is typically first identified during pregnancy 1 , 2 , 3 . GDM impacts 3–14% of pregnancies, with a notable rise in prevalence observed in both developed and developing countries 4 , 5 . Women with GDM face up to a 40% incidence of postpartum prediabetes 6 and an 18–50% higher risk of type 2 diabetes within 5 years, underscoring the long‐term metabolic burden associated with the condition 3 , 5 , 7 . Complications include polyhydramnios, postpartum depression, preeclampsia, labor induction, hypertension, dyslipidemia, thrombotic events, and cardiovascular diseases 5 , 7 , 8 . Additionally, infants face doubled risks of type 2 diabetes, with associated complications like macrosomia and a propensity for impaired glucose tolerance and obesity 3 , 5 , 7 .

GDM risk factors include a history of gestational diabetes, congenital anomalies, macrosomia, body mass index (BMI) ≥25 kg/m2, history of stillbirth, abortion, or preterm delivery, age, diet, and lifestyle 9 , 10 , 11 .

Previous studies have found that carbohydrate intake is related to GDM 12 , 13 . Recent evidence highlights that both the quality and quantity of dietary protein intake may influence GDM risk 14 . Some studies report that a higher intake of total protein and animal protein in pregnancy is related to a higher risk of GDM while the intake of plant protein before and during pregnancy has no association with GDM 15 . Conversely, other studies—particularly from Asian populations—have reported that both plant‐based and animal‐based proteins increase GDM risk 16 . The observed discrepancies may be attributed to variations in dietary patterns, protein sources, genetic predisposition, metabolic differences among populations, and differences in confounder adjustment.

The heightened predisposition to GDM in this context is ascribed to the integral involvement of specific amino acids and protein‐derived metabolic pathways in the pathogenesis of insulin resistance, serving as gluconeogenic precursors that stimulate hexosamine biosynthesis or activate the mTOR signaling pathway 17 .

Given the contradictory nature of previous evidence and the limited data available in Iranian pregnant women—the present study aims to address these gaps by using detailed 24‐h dietary records and comprehensive adjustment for major confounders to assess the association between specific protein sources and GDM risk.

Conducted in light of the divergent quantities and qualities of protein intake across diverse societies, as well as the limited and contradictory nature of existing research, this study sought to determine the relationship between quantities and qualities of dietary protein intake and GDM, with the ultimate goal of advancing scientific understanding in this domain.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and subjects

Comprehensive details regarding the study design, as well as the inclusion and exclusion criteria, have been previously documented in our hospital‐based case–control study 18 . Briefly, in the period between September 2015 and May 2016, a total of 200 cases and 263 controls were recruited for the study in Isfahan, Iran. Sampling was conducted through a non‐random convenience sampling method. Cases were selected from Azzahra Hospital, nutrition clinics, and Shahid Beheshti Hospital, while controls were drawn from outpatients seeking treatment at Azzahra and Shahid Beheshti Hospitals in Isfahan. Controls comprised individuals receiving medical care at the hospital without requiring overnight stays. All participants provided informed written consent, and the study received approval from the Isfahan University of Medical Sciences in Iran. Also, the present study complies with the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The study included expectant mothers aged 22–44 years with singleton pregnancies, and participants were specifically between 25 and 28 weeks of gestation. All participants underwent standard initial screening between weeks 25–28, including fasting glucose and a 1‐h postprandial glucose test. Women with fasting glucose ≥95 mg/dL or 1‐h postprandial glucose ≥140 mg/dL were diagnosed with GDM by medical doctors 19 . A full 2‐h OGTT was not performed as part of the diagnostic protocol in the participating hospitals. Participants were newly diagnosed pregnant women not using insulin or medication therapy. Controls consisted of non‐GDM pregnant individuals with normal FG or 1‐h postprandial glucose levels. GDM pregnant women were matched with non‐GDM pregnant counterparts during the specific gestational period. The study excluded women with diseases, medication or hormone therapy usage, and participants with incomplete daily food records or abnormal energy intake (less than 800 or more than 4,200 kcal per day) 20 .

Dietary data

The dietary assessment involved the averaging of three 24‐h dietary records, including two weekdays and one weekend day. Participants received guidance from a skilled dietitian to complete the dietary food record forms, utilizing a food album to accurately estimate portion sizes. Raw and cooked coefficients were applied to all foods, and the dietitian verified form accuracy, addressing incomplete records via phone follow‐ups. Portion sizes were converted to grams using household measures for Iranian foods, and dietary intake was evaluated using the Nutritionist IV software (First Databank Division, the Hearst Corporation, San Bruno, CA, USA), adapted for Iranian food items. Dietary assessment was conducted during weeks 25–28 of gestation, following GDM diagnosis.

Assessment of protein intake

Each protein‐rich food item was classified as either of plant or animal origin, and the protein intake from each category was calculated by summing the protein content (in grams per day) of the corresponding food items. Total daily protein intake was then determined by aggregating the protein content from both plant and animal sources. Protein intake from poultry, dairy, eggs, and seafood was assessed individually. Furthermore, the consumption of aromatic amino acids and branched‐chain amino acids was evaluated.

Mixed dishes (e.g., stews, soups, rice‐based dishes, and sandwiches) were disaggregated into their individual ingredients using standard household measures and Iranian recipe databases within the Nutritionist IV software. Protein content from each ingredient was then allocated to the respective protein source category (plant, animal, poultry, dairy, egg, or seafood). This approach ensured accurate classification of protein intake from complex foods.

Assessment of other variables

Participant weight was measured using a SECA scale, and height was measured with a wall tape. BMI was calculated, and blood pressure was measured twice after a 5‐min rest. The final recorded value for blood pressure was the average of two measurements. Blood samples were collected after a 12‐h overnight fast and analyzed for various parameters using an autoanalyzer. Socioeconomic status (SES) was determined based on education, car and house ownership, and family size. Physical activity was assessed using a self‐reported questionnaire in which participants recorded the type and duration of their daily activities. MET values were assigned according to the Compendium of Physical Activities developed by Ainsworth et al. 21 , and total physical activity was expressed as MET‐h/day. Smoking habits were assessed, with all participants reported as nonsmokers. Additionally, participants were questioned about supplement intake, and all of them took iron supplements.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables between cases and controls were compared using the independent samples Student's t‐test, while categorical variables were assessed using the chi‐square test. The dietary intake of the case and control groups was assessed using Analysis of Covariance (ancova), with adjustments made for energy intake. A multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to investigate the relationship between intake of protein, its sources, along with BCAAs and aromatic amino acids, and the risk of GDM, considering both unadjusted and adjusted models. A crude model was initially conducted, followed by two adjusted models aimed at mitigating potential confounding variables. Model 1 included adjustments for age, BMI, SES, energy intake, and number of offspring, while Model 2 further adjusted for physical activity, dietary fiber intake, and the intake of multivitamin supplements. To assess the trend of odds ratios, each tertile was treated as an ordinal variable. Statistical analyses were conducted utilizing SPSS software (version 16, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL), with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05.

Table 3 displays the crude and multivariable‐adjusted odds ratios (ORs) along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for GDM across the tertiles of protein and amino acids. In the crude model, individuals in the highest tertile of plant protein intake exhibited a significantly reduced likelihood of GDM risk (OR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.29–0.73), a trend that persisted even after adjusting for multiple variables (OR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.20–0.59), indicating a 66% lower risk. A notable trend was observed for GDM risk across plant protein tertiles in both crude and adjusted models. Similarly, high seafood consumption was associated with decreased GDM risk in both crude (OR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.31–0.77) and adjusted models (OR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.24–0.68), with individuals in the highest tertile showing a 60% lower risk. A significant trend was noted for GDM risk across tertiles of seafood consumption in both unadjusted and adjusted models. Additionally, elevated egg intake was linked to reduced GDM risk in the unadjusted model (OR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.33–0.89) and after adjustment (OR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.35–0.98), indicating a 41% lower risk for individuals in the highest tertile. In the crude model, a significant trend was observed for GDM risk across tertiles of egg consumption. However, in the adjusted models, no significant trend was observed. There was no statistically significant link found between total protein, animal protein, poultry, dairy, BCAAs, and aromatic amino acids with the risk of GDM.

DISCUSSION

In this case–control study, we did not detect any notable association between maternal intake of protein during pregnancy and the risk of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). However, we did identify a noteworthy inverse correlation between the consumption of plant protein, seafood, and eggs with the risk of GDM. On the other hand, there were no significant findings regarding the relationship between animal protein, poultry, dairy, aromatic amino acids, and branched‐chain amino acids (BCAA) consumption with the risk of GDM.

Although women with GDM reported higher absolute protein intake, total protein intake was not associated with GDM risk after multivariable adjustment. This discrepancy is likely explained by the heterogeneity of protein sources, as total protein does not differentiate between plant‐based and animal‐based proteins. Our findings indicate that only specific sources—namely plant protein, seafood, and eggs—were inversely associated with GDM risk, whereas other major contributors to total protein showed no meaningful association. Moreover, GDM cases consumed higher total energy and lower carbohydrate and fiber, suggesting that accompanying dietary patterns and residual confounding may have influenced crude differences in protein intake. Therefore, assessing protein quality and source provides more informative insights than evaluating total protein alone.

Our findings were in line with another Iranian study that found no association between maternal protein intake and the risk of GDM 22 . While a Chinese study observed a significant positive relationship between total protein intake and GDM 15 . This inconsistency might be due to different sources of protein intake 23 . Therefore, we assessed the source of protein intake and observed a significant reversed association between plant protein and the risk of GDM. Other studies revealed negative 24 , positive 16 , or no association between plant protein intake and GDM risk 25 . One proposed mechanism is that potentially lower intake of heme iron (which can act as a potent pro‐oxidant and increase oxidative stress in tissues such as pancreatic beta cells) or higher intake of L‐arginine in the diet might justify better glucose regulation in this context compared to others, due to increased intake of plant‐based proteins in the diet 22 , 26 .

Regarding the consumption of seafood, there are conflicting research findings indicating either a negative correlation with the risk of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) 27 , an increased risk 16 , or no discernible association with GDM 22 , 24 . We observed an inverse relationship between the consumption of seafood and the risk of GDM. The variation in the types of fish and other seafood consumed across different geographical regions could potentially explain this discrepancy 16 . Also, the inverse association between seafood intake and GDM risk observed in our study may be partially explained by the metabolic effects of long‐chain omega‐3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA), which are abundant in fish 28 . Omega‐3 PUFAs act as precursors to anti‐inflammatory eicosanoids and play a critical role in regulating systemic inflammation 28 . Evidence indicates that, at comparable energy intakes, lean seafood can reduce fasting and postprandial markers of insulin resistance and improve insulin sensitivity in insulin‐resistant adults 29 . Cohort findings also suggest that moderate maternal fish consumption during pregnancy is associated with reduced inflammation and improved metabolic outcomes in offspring, whereas higher prenatal exposure to mercury, a known contaminant in some fish species, may exert the opposite effect 30 . These competing nutritional benefits and risks may contribute to the heterogeneity observed across studies evaluating seafood intake and GDM risk.

While some studies have found a positive association between egg consumption and GDM risk 31 , 32 , others have reported a lower risk of GDM with higher egg consumption 33 . In the present study, we observed a reverse relationship between egg consumption and the risk of GDM. It is important to mention that dietary cholesterol primarily affects serum cholesterol levels in individuals with abnormal lipid profiles, with relatively large increments having minimal impact on total or low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol concentrations in others 34 , 35 . Moreover, according to findings from a meta‐analysis study, there exists a dose–response positive correlation between the consumption of eggs and the risk of developing cardiovascular diseases and diabetes 36 . Hence, the contradiction in the results of studies regarding the association between egg intake and gestational diabetes may be attributed to the disparity in egg consumption levels, with studies demonstrating a positive relationship often involving higher daily egg intake (more than 7 eggs per week) 31 , whereas other studies, including the present one, and others reporting contrasting findings typically involve lower egg consumption (maximum one egg per week in over half of the studied population) 33 .

In the current study, mean protein intake was approximately 63–68 g/day, which appears relatively moderate compared with some Asian cohort studies reporting higher total protein‐intake levels and positive associations with GDM risk 15 , 16 . These differences suggest that the metabolic impact of dietary protein may depend not only on protein source but also on the overall quantity consumed and the accompanying dietary pattern 23 . Therefore, variations in habitual protein intake across populations may partly explain inconsistencies among previous findings 23 .

The current research has some limitations. The first limitation lies in the case–control design, which cannot establish causal relationships. Second, the conclusions drawn from this study may not apply to populations without GDM. Third, although dietary intake was assessed using three non‐consecutive 24‐h dietary records, this method remains susceptible to recall bias and underreporting, especially among pregnant women. Future studies would benefit from incorporating pregnancy‐specific validated food frequency questionnaires (FFQs) to better capture habitual dietary patterns. Fourth, given that participants were recruited using a non‐random convenience sampling approach from hospitals and nutrition clinics, the possibility of selection bias cannot be excluded. This sampling framework may overrepresent women who are more health‐conscious or more engaged with the healthcare system, thereby limiting the generalizability of our findings. Future research should employ population‐based random sampling to enhance external validity. Fifth, dietary data were collected following GDM diagnosis, which may have introduced bias, as women may modify their dietary behaviors after receiving a diagnosis 37 . This is a recognized limitation in case–control studies. An important strength of the current study is the adequate sample size and the careful adjustment for potential confounding variables.

In conclusion, our study did not identify any correlation between total or animal dietary protein intake during pregnancy and the risk of GDM. However, there appears to be an inverse association between plant protein, seafood, and egg consumption and GDM risk. This suggests that the quality of dietary protein intake during pregnancy may influence GDM risk. Further clinical trials and prospective studies are necessary to elucidate this relationship more precisely.
