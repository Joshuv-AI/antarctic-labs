Addressing Weight Stigma in Healthcare Settings Using an Obesity Checklist: A Mixed-Method Development Study
Authors: Giang T. Nguyen, Jade H. Thorne, Stephanie Goodlet, Lisa Davies, Emilie Combet Aspray, Dimitris Papamargaritis, Alexander Dimitri Miras, Sarah Le Brocq, Suzy Cooke, Jill Harland, Nicola Heslehurst
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionExperiencing weight stigma negatively impacts health yet remains present in healthcare environments. Hospital environments must be inclusive for people living with obesity (PLwO). The aim of this study was to develop and refine a checklist to increase obesity inclusivity in UK National Health Service (UK NHS) hospital environments, with input from healthcare professionals (HCPs), researchers, PLwO, and their carers and family members (C/FM).MethodsThis study used a sequential mixed-methods approach. Phase 1 used a converging coding matrix to integrate existing checklists and reviewed language for inclusivity. In phase 2, HCPs, researchers, PLwO, and C/FM were recruited from professional and patient networks to provide feedback on the integrated checklist using a mixed-methods survey (February-March 2025) and qualitative semi-structured interviews (May-July 2025). Descriptive statistics and deductive and inductive framework analyses were used to identify patterns in the data to inform refinement of the checklist and implementation recommendations (Phase 3).ResultsSearches identified five checklists, which were integrated, resulting in categories for reading materials, furniture, equipment, and access and infrastructure. Sixty-nine participants completed the survey, and eighteen were interviewed. Overall, participants considered the checklist comprehensive. Integration of the qualitative survey and interview data resulted in four themes: (1) recommendations and thoughts about checklist components; (2) wording, language, images, and structure; (3) patient impact and care experiences; (4) implementation barriers and support. Primary implementation barriers related to funding, space, outdated infrastructure, and deep-rooted weight bias. Management buy-in, training, education, endorsement, and accreditation schemes were considered facilitators. Twenty recommendations were developed to support hospitals with implementation.ConclusionFindings indicate that a clear, practical, and inclusive checklist could support hospitals in reducing weight stigma and improving care experiences for PLwO. The collaboratively developed and refined checklist offers a practical tool for hospitals to use. However, support will be required for effective and complete implementation.

Introduction

Weight stigma, the discriminatory acts and beliefs aimed at individuals due to their body size, is prevalent across many contexts, including education, media, healthcare and among healthcare professionals (HCPs) [1–4]. Weight stigma negatively impacts health. Physiological impacts include increasing obesity, lower weight loss, increased cortisol, and C-reactive protein [5–7]. Experiencing weight stigma is also associated with mental distress [8, 9] and social phobia [6, 8]; associations are independent of body mass index, suggesting that stigma is the key risk factor [6, 10, 11].

Many people living with obesity (PLwO) report stigmatising healthcare experiences [4, 12], not being treated with dignity and respect [13], and stigmatising language and physical environments [2]. The inadequacy and systemic discriminatory nature of equipment are well established [14–17]. For example, chairs that are too small for individuals seeking bariatric surgery [15], insufficient lifting equipment, inaccessible scales [14], and a lack of appropriately sized computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging scanners [17]. These environments may compromise patient safety, negatively affect clinical outcomes [17], exacerbate health inequalities and reflect a broader systemic failure within healthcare systems [18, 19].

The value of addressing weight stigma within the hospital environment is well recognised [2, 20, 21]. Key areas to be addressed include reading materials [1, 2, 22, 23], furniture [2, 24, 25], equipment [2, 25, 26], and access and infrastructure [2, 27]. For example, weight stigma can be avoided by providing reading materials that use non-stigmatising images and language [2, 23–25]; inclusive furniture such as seating being available in all areas without noticeable separation [28]; and availability of appropriately sized gowns, needles, blood pressure cuffs, step stools with handlebars, and scales with higher weight capacity [2, 27–29]. Accessibility of hospital departments should be considered, including space in corridors and examination rooms [2, 29], as well as inclusivity in other healthcare settings, such as prayer spaces, the pharmacy, food service areas, and the reception [28].

Weight stigma checklists can be used to standardise practice, enhance safety, and ensure consistent delivery of evidence-based care for PLwO. They provide a simple, structured means of identifying gaps, guiding action, and supporting quality improvement [30]. Applying a checklist approach to weight-inclusive environments, therefore, offers a practical way to reduce weight stigma in the hospital environment, yet comprehensive, evidence-based checklists to evaluate and improve the inclusivity of hospital environments are lacking. This research aimed to develop and refine a weight stigma checklist to increase obesity inclusivity in UK NHS hospital environments, with input from HCPs, researchers, PLwO and their carers and/or family members (C/FM).

Methods

This study draws on international research on medical checklist development [31–34], using stage 2 of a five-stage framework for checklist development and implementation [32]: (1) conception; (2) determination of content and design; (3) testing and validation; (4) induction, training, and implementation; and (5) ongoing evaluation, revision, and potential retirement. Stage 2 was initially undertaken as a quality improvement project by Northumbria NHS Trust (online suppl. Table 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552921), who developed and implemented a weight stigma checklist to support an obesity-inclusive hospital environment. A limitation of the internally developed checklist was its localised context, which may reduce generalisability to NHS services across the UK. A structured, three-phase approach was employed to further develop and refine the checklist. Phase 1 integrated existing checklists using a convergence coding matrix to create an initial draft of a checklist. Phase 2 engaged stakeholders: HCPs, researchers, PLwO, and their C/FM via an online survey and interviews to explore content, feasibility of implementation, inclusivity, and relevance to lived experience. Phase 3 refined the checklist and produced recommendations for NHS validation and implementation. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the GRAMMS framework for mixed-method studies (see online suppl. File 2).

Phase 1: Integrating Existing Checklists and Reviewing Language

Literature searches including weight, stigma, checklist, and hospital terms were conducted in Google Scholar to identify existing checklists and resources that could inform the content of an integrated checklist suitable for the UK NHS hospital context. A convergence coding matrix [35, 36] (online suppl. Material 1 Table 2) was used to systematically search for agreement and disagreement between the content of the identified checklists and resources. This process includes triangulation of the data within the existing checklists to identify areas of convergence (agreement in the content between two or more checklists), complementarity (additional value added by one or more checklists), dissonance (contradiction between the content of two or more checklists), and silence (unique information in one checklist not reflected in any others) [35, 36]. The integrated checklist was reviewed against weight-related terminology international consensus statements to ensure appropriate language and framing were applied [2, 20].

Phase 2: Feedback on Checklist Content

A two-stage sequential mixed-methods study was conducted, including (1) a cross-sectional online survey to assess checklist usability, and (2) follow-up qualitative interviews with a subset of survey respondents. The qualitative component was prioritised to deeply explore lived experiences, feasibility, inclusivity, and implementation barriers.

Recruitment and Data Collection

Recruitment utilised convenience sampling methods, targeting professional and patient networks, with recruitment material shared using social media (online suppl. Material 1 Table 3). Eligibility criteria for participants were any HCPs, researchers working in obesity and/or stigma, PLwO (self-identified as living with obesity), and C/FM residing in the UK or Republic of Ireland.

The survey (online suppl. Material 1 Table 4) was developed using evidence-based recommendations [37, 38], including simple instructions, survey length <20 min, and using open and closed questions. The questionnaire comprised respondent demographics, followed by structured sections on each domain of the draft checklist. Question pathways were tailored to participant groups: HCPs and researchers were asked about clinical feasibility, relevance, and implementation considerations, while PLwO and C/FM were asked about lived experiences, perceived inclusivity, and the usefulness or clarity of proposed checklist items. If a participant identified as both an HCP or researcher, and PLwO or C/FM, the pathways were combined to include all sections. The survey was conducted via Jisc Online Survey [39] in February and March 2025.

Respondents to the survey were asked if they were interested in participating in follow-up interviews. Purposive sampling among respondents prioritised HCPs and PLwO given the direct experience with care in hospital environments. One-off semi-structured interviews were conducted using Microsoft Teams between May and July 2025, lasting between 25 and 65 min. Interview questions (online suppl. Material 1 Table 5) were developed to further explore survey results. The participant information was shared in advance, and verbal consent was obtained prior to the interview. Interviews continued until meaning saturation was achieved, where data collected were adequate to address the research question [40]. Participants received a GBP 25 thank-you voucher. Interviews were transcribed verbatim and pseudo-anonymised before analysis. Transcripts were stored on the secure Newcastle University network in accordance with institutional data-governance policies, with access restricted to Newcastle University authors.

Quantitative survey data were analysed using SPSS [41] using descriptive statistics. As respondents could self-identify with more than one population group, numbers reported may be greater than the total number of participants. Data were assessed for normality using a Shapiro-Wilk normality test, and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation and median and interquartile range accordingly.

Framework analysis was used to analyse qualitative survey and interview data, involving data familiarisation, framework development, coding, charting, and interpretation [42]. Anonymised transcripts were uploaded to Taguette [43], and coding was inductive and deductive to ensure alignment with the study objectives while capturing participants’ views and experiences [42]. Two researchers (J.T. and G.N.) independently undertook familiarisation and coding, then integrated findings through triangulation. Triangulation followed published protocols [35, 36] and included methodological triangulation (integration of quantitative and qualitative data), investigator triangulation (independent analyses by two researchers), and data triangulation across multiple sources and population groups [35, 36]. Findings are presented narratively and supported by verbatim quotations.

Phase 3: Checklist Revision and Implementation Recommendations

Survey and interview data informed the final revision of the checklist. Any feedback in conflict was resolved through professional judgement among the research team and evidence-based rationale.

While this research focussed on stage 2 of the checklist development framework (i.e., determination of content and design), the subsequent stages relate to testing, validation, and implementation [32]; therefore, this study also focused on implementation to inform future stages. Phase 3 of this study draws together the quantitative and qualitative results related to implementation (analysed as per survey and interview data described in phase 2). These data were used to produce strategic recommendations for future validation of the checklist and for implementation in NHS hospital settings.

Results

Phase One

Searches identified four relevant checklists and resources to supplement the locally developed Northumbria Healthcare NHS Foundation Trust Checklist. These included an equipment and environment checklist for offices and clinical settings developed by the Provincial Bariatric Resource Team, Alberta Health Services, in Canada [24]; a clinical environment checklist designed for accommodating PLwOs in ambulatory settings in the USA [25]; an assessment of the clinical space checklist developed by Obesity Canada [44], which has been adopted by the Association for the Study of Obesity (ASO) on the Island of Ireland [45]; and the European ASO (EASO) Collaborating Centres for Obesity Management (COMs) criteria, which sets out the essential requirements for an obesity specialist service [46].

Several areas of convergence, complementarity, dissonance, and silence were identified between the five data sources (online suppl. Material 1 Table 2). For example, there was convergence between data sources’ recommendations that reading materials should be considered and not stigmatise language; complementarity between checklists relating to the bed requirements (e.g., specifics related to pressure relief mattresses); dissonance between the recommendations for equipment weight capacity; and silence with only one data source including recommendations related to bariatric wheelchairs. The results of the integration process formed the basis of the initial checklist categories and contents, which were (1) reading materials, for example, relating to stigmatising images and language; (2) furniture, for example, seating; (3) equipment, for example, examination beds and other medical equipment; and (4) access, for example, the distance to hospital departments (online suppl. Material 1 Table 6).

Phase Two

A total of 69 participants completed the online survey (n = 51 HCPs, n = 8 researchers, n = 21 PLwO, and n = 4 C/FM). As some respondents identified with more than one role (n = 12), fifty-six respondents completed the HCP/researcher branch of the survey, and twenty-two completed the PwLEO/C/FM branch (Table 1; online suppl. Material 1 Table 7). The mean age was similar for survey and interview participants, respectively (47 and 48 years); the majority were female (84% and 82%) and based in England (82% and 83%). Eighteen survey participants took part in the interviews (n = 11 HCPs and n = 10 PLwO).

Overall, most survey respondents indicated that no important items were missing from the checklist (75%–85.7% across the four checklist categories for HCPs and researchers; 82%–100% for PLwO and C/FM), needed removing from the checklist (93%–100% HCPs and researchers), or that wording changes were required (79%–100% HCPs and researchers) (Table 2). Among respondents who did feel items were missing, these included inclusivity of “easy-read” materials suitable for people with learning difficulties, explaining the complexity of obesity, including period product disposal bins, gynaecology seats, and rest zones on the route to the hospital (HCPs and researchers), not making assumptions about people’s weight, and the provision of psychological support (PLwO and C/FM). The majority of PLwO and C/FM respondents felt that the reading materials and access sections of the checklist would not have made a difference to how they experienced hospital care or would have made it more positive (59% and 36%, respectively) and that the equipment section would have made a positive impact or no difference (55% and 46%, respectively). Results were split on whether the furniture section would have made their experience more positive or no difference to their experience (50% each).

PLwO and C/FM indicated which checklist items were most important to make hospital environments inclusive (online suppl. Material 1 Table 8). Priorities for reading materials related to using non-stigmatising images and language and not using humour, blame or oversimplifying obesity, sensationalist or combative language. For seating, having options with or without arms, no separation or visible difference to other seating styles, and the dimensions and cushioning of the seating were considered important. The placement and space around the toilets and beds and weight capacity were important, as well as having appropriately sized gowns and blood pressure cuffs. Related to access, the distance to the department and addressing the outpatient environments were considered most important.

Theme 1: Recommendations and Thoughts about the Checklist Components. Participants described the importance of reading materials being evidence based, using non-stigmatising images and language, reflecting diverse body types and not reinforcing stereotypes. Materials should also include positive messaging that extended beyond risks, diet and exercise, for example, including mental health, sleep and self-care, and be available in different formats for disability inclusivity, language and for content to be culturally relevant. They also discussed the potential for causing harm if materials were not fit for purpose and the importance of meaningful involvement with PLwO.

“A doctor’s surgery had these little health TV shows, and one of them was about obesity. I was the only person in the doctors’ waiting room who had obesity, and there were loads of people in there. It was all about how bad it is, and it was just really excruciating.” (PLwO, P11)

A recurring theme was the stigma associated with bariatric furniture. Participants felt that stigma could be reduced by ensuring bariatric furniture is visually indistinguishable from standard items. Additionally, the placement of period product bins in toilets being too close to the pedestal, options to be seated in the shower, and the need for movable seating to make it easier to have space between chairs were considered important.

“So, we often have a bariatric chair, but then the relative that comes along with a similar weight then has to go on the standard chair, and it can make the patient and the relative feel really uncomfortable.” (HCP, P17)

The lack of size-inclusive equipment, such as gowns, chairs, beds, hoists, scales, and blood pressure cuffs, was deemed to significantly impact patient dignity, comfort, and access to care. Additional equipment considerations were discussed, including mammogram machines and smear equipment, and staff skills for procedures such as phlebotomy. There was an emphasis on ensuring that the provision of the size-inclusive equipment was seamless and available upon arrival to avoid delays and stigma and that details on equipment weight limits could be communicated in advance to allay anxieties before attending hospital appointments.

“The dignity pants… it’s knowing that if there aren’t any hospital ones that fit you, you can actually wear your own to keep your own dignity. Then the compression stockings or socks. They should be readily available, but some people won't be able to put them on, so it’s making sure that there’s support there to actually put them on or take them off because the people might not be able to bend down and that sort of thing” (PLwO, P54)

Multifaceted barriers to accessibility within healthcare environments for PLwO who have mobility challenges were noted, including narrow corridors, inaccessible stair-only areas, insufficient lift access, and inadequate parking facilities. Suggestions for improvements included having parking spaces to accommodate doors being wide open, the journey to hospital departments having handrails and seating en route, and a bus stop near the hospital entrance. Participants also suggested using digital tools to improve accessibility, including advanced online booking of wheelchairs or porters, and virtual consultations to remove the stigma of having to request assistance at reception. Participants also emphasised that respectful, non-stigmatising communication, especially at initial points of contact like the reception, can significantly impact a patient’s willingness to seek care.

“Maybe just to have navigational aids as well, signs to say, if you come in the main entrance, how far the next department would be. Some hospitals […] they’ve got like lines. So, if you need to go to a certain department, […] they’ve painted like a yellow line to that department, and it tells you every now and again how far it is still to go to this department. […] Then having benches along the way, it may be as well for people who might need to take rest if it’s a very far department.” (HCP + PLwO, P46)

Theme 2: Wording, Language, Images, and Structure. The practical usability and clarity of the checklist were discussed, including the importance of logical sequencing and a preference for structured formats, including tick boxes, numbered sections, and headings, to enhance readability and navigation. Suggestions for improvements included digital adaptability, such as online versions that generate summaries of incomplete items. There were also suggestions to tailor the checklist for specific healthcare settings (e.g., maternity, emergency, paediatrics).

Participants highlighted the thorough and comprehensive nature of the checklist, frequently describing it “very detailed,” “global,” and “all-encompassing,” reflecting real-life experiences and addressing previously overlooked needs. However, participants also expressed concern about the length deterring implementation. The tension between depth and usability was a recurring theme, with some suggesting that the checklist could benefit from having a summary version or be presented differently, such as with a subheading for different clinical areas.

“So overall, looking at the checklist, it's very comprehensive. So there’s been a lot of thought that's gone into it, and I concur with most of what has been consolidated and put into this list.” (PLwO, P43)

“The only issue is obviously that by nature it’s relatively long, but that’s a balance because of course you don’t want to miss stuff out that’s important, and that’s the only danger is people might look at it and go and what, 7 pages, I’ll do that another day. But you can't have it both ways, I suppose, and it is important that it is comprehensive.” (HCP, P6)

Theme 3: Patient Impact and Care Experiences. Both positive and negative examples of previous care and how the checklist may make a difference. The systemic inadequacies in hospital environments were described for PLwO as physical infrastructure, equipment, furniture, and attitudes with multiple examples of explicit and implicit weight bias in care experiences. These included dismissive language, assumptions about behaviours and health, and a lack of empathy. PLwO described how this led to emotional distress, lack of dignity, avoidance of medical care, and, in some cases, delayed diagnoses. HCPs also described the systemic nature of weight bias, noting that patients could be defensive or emotionally vulnerable due to prior negative experiences. The use of certain terminology, such as “morbidly obese,” and the insistence on discussing weight against the patient’s wishes were considered unacceptable.

“I feel worthless enough when you're at that point of asking for help, and then when you’re going to go into an environment that is possibly uncomfortable, possibly causes anxiety, you’re feeling even more worthless and a bit more of a failure as well because if I don’t care about myself and I’m going into an environment that doesn’t meet my needs, they don’t care about me either. So what is the point?” (PLwO, P40)

Examples of barriers to providing equitable and dignified care for PLwO included standard equipment and furniture failing to be suitable for larger bodies, which signalled institutional stigma. PLwO echoed these concerns when describing negative experiences. The emotional toll on patients includes shame, anxiety, and avoidance of medical services.

“Absolutely all the time you walk into your clinic, and they just don’t have the right rooms. I mean, I run obesity clinics for crying out loud, and I can’t find appropriate tables, appropriate chairs […] if I cannot find this in my specialised obesity clinic, what happens in the cardiology clinic, or the orthopaedic clinic, or the respiratory clinic? People are made to feel uncomfortable. People are made to feel unwelcome. Again, it happens all the time […] not excusable.” (HCP, P13)

“The new policy is you have to put a gown on when you have a DEXA scan. Now, I’ve always had DEXA scans where I've been able to keep my clothes on. Okay, so that’s alright, I will put a gown on […] only one size […] and you can see because they’re a particular colour and the larger size ones are green, […] They behaved as if they’d never heard that this was a problem before, which is not a nice experience either to feel like you’re the only one. […] “We don’t know what to do”. Honestly, it was a horrible experience. I ended up in scrubs and nobody else was wearing scrubs. So, I looked completely different from everybody else” (PLwO, P24)

While there were fewer positive experiences of hospital care shared during interviews, examples included having privacy and a sense of ownership when PLwO were able to weigh themselves and support with putting on compression socks. Proactive care planning, such as anticipating patient needs and offering psychological support, was considered crucial in shaping positive experiences. Some HCPs described attempts to mitigate institutional inadequacies by proactively adapting their environments and communication strategies. PLwO described positive experiences related to having inclusive care, thoughtful language, and appropriately sized equipment and furniture, which made them feel “normal,” respected and more engaged with healthcare services. Participants emphasised the importance and value of this checklist and described how many items would improve their hospital care experience, particularly related to equipment. The reading materials section was described as illustrating that obesity was being “taken seriously” and conveyed a more neutral and less sensationalised approach to obesity. PLwO described how the checklist would make them feel like they “mattered” were less of a “nuisance” adding to staff workload and that experiences would be less embarrassing and upsetting.

“This is a very important, inspirational step in the right direction for people living with obesity.” (HCP + PLwO, P27)

Over time, I’ve had a lot of issues myself with things that haven’t been right […] it’s something I feel that really needs to be to be done so that people don’t feel ashamed, uncomfortable, or even don’t go at their appointments because of it” (PLwO, P40)

Theme 4: Implementation Barriers and Support. Survey results indicated that HCPs and researchers perceived multiple barriers to implementing the checklist, particularly regarding furniture, access, and equipment, with hospitals likely requiring additional implementation support (online suppl. Material 1 Table 9). Qualitative survey and interview data identified several implementation challenges and support requirements.

Overall, participants were positive about the checklist, describing it as sensible, comprehensive, and necessary. They highlighted its potential to raise awareness, guide practical improvements, and foster inclusive care. The checklist was perceived as a practical tool for gap analysis, enabling services to identify areas of non-compliance and plan targeted improvements. While questions were raised about whether the checklist might inadvertently reinforce obesity, the majority viewed the checklist as a compassionate and respectful step toward equitable healthcare.

“I would absolutely love this as a priority because I work in weight management. I think we should set an example to the rest of the hospital, community and within the community services, but also the hospital setting, you know, and we ourselves are still not getting it right all the time. So we need to lead by example. We would absolutely make this a priority.” (HCP, P17)

“If I think about myself, if those sorts of things are thought of, wider seats, it just it shows that there’s care and compassion for people with excess weight in that hospital, in that clinic, by that professional as well. So I definitely think it would have a positive experience on the patient.” (HCP + PLwO, P46)

However, there were multiple complex barriers to implementation identified. Participants described how implicit and explicit weight bias, stigma, and negative culture and attitudes within clinical environments, that mirrored those seen in wider society, would be barriers to implementation. There was discussion of pervasive and deeply embedded weight stigma within healthcare settings, which manifests in dismissive language, inadequate equipment, judgemental attitudes, and assumptions about personal responsibility and health status. HCPs described the need for a cultural shift, with obesity care moving beyond a niche interest area to become mainstream practice, empathetic practice, and evidence-based practice. While some progress was noted, particularly in specialist services, widespread stigma, implicit bias, and outdated assumptions such as the oversimplified “eat less, move more” concept were deemed to hinder effective and compassionate care.

“This is great to clearly share the fundamentals of equipment and resources required as outlined in the tables [checklist]. My biggest concern is the cultural change required alongside this in the NHS to achieve this.” (HCP, P4)

“When you even consider bringing in sort of specialist equipment that’s got the right weight capacity and things, or just generally shape and size is sufficient, then people are like, why should I do that for somebody? Because there’s still that perception that person is ignorant, lazy, chooses to eat badly, all those kinds of old-fashioned ideas about where overweight and obesity comes from.” (HCP, P36)

Participants described a lack of awareness and understanding within healthcare systems and HCPs of the complexity of obesity. There were suggestions for systemic changes to increase awareness, including education and training incorporating empathy, inclusive language, the biological and psychological underpinnings of obesity, and including narratives from PLwO was considered a powerful method to achieve this. There was strong support for making weight stigma training a mandatory component of professional development. Having an accreditation system in place for sites that comply with the checklist was also considered a strategy to incentivise hospital management.

“I think that people who are responsible for resourcing the environments have a responsibility of care to people and to risk manage the environments of providing care and to have an awareness that when they’re procuring equipment for their areas where they’re delivering care that if they don’t provide the right equipment, then they create a risk in the environment.” (HCP, P36)

Financial constraints were considered a key barrier to implementation. Infrastructure limitations, including outdated hospital buildings and restricted clinical space, were also highlighted, alongside the budgets required to adapt these environments. Certain settings, such as Accident and Emergency department were perceived as especially challenging; however, participants emphasised that the checklist remained essential in these contexts. The NHS was described as reactive and overstretched, with a prevailing “firefighting” culture that prioritises immediate pressures over long-term, inclusive improvements. Hence, time constraints, competing demands, and limited prioritisation of training were considered barriers. Procurement challenges were raised, including uncertainty about sourcing appropriate equipment and furniture, and the limited availability of specialist items through existing NHS suppliers.

“I think you would start you'll struggle […] because the equipment is so expensive, you know the plus size equipment is so expensive, I think that’s difficult.” (HCP + PLwO, P32)

Participants identified several facilitators to implementation, including securing buy-in from multiple stakeholders and adopting a combined top-down and bottom-up approach. Management support was viewed as dependent on the availability of supporting data, such as risk register entries, incident reports, and evidence demonstrating equipment-related benefits. Practical enablers included guidance on procurement (e.g., lists of suitable suppliers), support for reconfiguring clinical spaces, and understanding local needs alongside forecasting future demand, supported by appropriate funding streams and strategic planning (e.g., inclusion in business cases). Pilot projects were suggested as a pragmatic starting point, with participants emphasising the need to move beyond reliance on individual champions and embed shared responsibility. Regulatory and system-level drivers, including government support and inclusion within national standards (e.g., care quality commission), were consistently identified as critical for gaining traction within NHS settings. Without such enforcement, initiatives are considered to be at risk of being deprioritised amid competing demands. Accountability mechanisms, including penalties for non-compliance, and endorsement from influential bodies or inclusion in commissioning criteria were viewed as key levers to support implementation.

“I think that there needs to be a national policy to make sure that new hospitals, or those having major rebuilding, are taking these points into consideration.” (HCP, P15)

“You need the managers to buy in, and you need the procurement teams, the people who spend the money to buy in. But you also need people on the wards, leaders on the wards to see it as a positive to have those things and to kind of encourage staff to know about them and to kind of roll them out in the same way that they would any other equipment to help people.” (HCP + PLwO, P32)

Finally, participants noted the need for multi-channel communication strategies using accessible formats to reach broader audiences, including clinical staff and patients. There was discussion around using awareness days and social media platforms and targeting decision-makers in communications to facilitate implementation.

“I think you need to reach to the audience that’s not listening to you usually. So yes, you can share this to the enthusiasts […] but I think the challenge will be to get it to the people who are not your listeners […] I think there is a need for a clear dissemination strategy and that should be multi-pronged. It can’t be just one thing and that could be a variety of events, and I think you need some national leadership events. You need some traction with some advisory boards.” (HCP, P13)

Phase Three

The synthesis informed checklist revisions and implementation recommendations. The final checklist includes specific, measurable standards across the four checklist domains. Changes included adding items such as reading materials on the complexity of obesity, adjustable beds for comfort and staff access, and new categories like dignity underwear, daily living equipment, and commodes (online suppl. Implementation recommendations for supporting checklist adoption in NHS hospital environments included external support such as funding, procurement resources, information banks, accreditation, regulatory frameworks, data collection, and checklist review (Table 3). Recommendations for hospital-based action to support checklist implementation include those related to infrastructure changes, multi-stakeholder support and champions, the whole patient journey and psychological safety, culture change, training and development, and gap analysis (Table 4).

Discussion

This study has developed a checklist and implementation recommendations to address weight stigma and promote inclusivity in hospital environments, incorporating clinical, academic, and lived experience expertise. The final checklist comprises four domains related to inclusive and non-stigmatising reading materials, furniture, equipment, and access and infrastructure. There was a consistent message from HCPs and PLwO that inappropriate equipment and furniture were major areas of inadequacy and had the potential to negatively impact patient dignity, comfort, safety, and care outcomes. This aligns with evidence showing clinics frequently lack equipment designed for higher weight capacities and that patient comfort, dignity, and care can be compromised when equipment is unavailable [25, 47, 48]. Appropriate and accessible equipment has been recommended as a critical component of inclusive clinical practice to reduce weight stigma and promote care engagement. This aspect of the checklist should be a clinical priority.

The perspectives of PLwO and C/FM were central in shaping the checklist to ensure it reflected real-world needs. Respondents emphasised that staff behaviours and attitudes had a major influence on their dignity, safety, and overall care experiences. The checklist focused primarily on environmental and equipment factors and does not directly address these interpersonal aspects. Evidence from healthcare settings shows that weight stigma can manifest through verbal and non-verbal staff behaviours, affecting patient trust, engagement, and perceived quality of care [49]. Therefore, staff training and cultural change must accompany checklist adoption to meaningfully reduce weight stigma and ensure respectful and inclusive care.

There are additional steps in the checklist framework for continued development and improvement, including testing and validation; induction, training, and implementation; and ongoing evaluation, revision, and potential retirement) [32]. While the next steps in the framework were outside the remit of this study, we explored potential implementation barriers and facilitators to inform future work. Participants identified time and financial constraints as significant barriers to implementation, reflecting broader challenges in obesity care delivery [50], and the importance of engaging management and appointing a responsible lead to oversee adoption and compliance. These findings align with evidence that leadership, organisational commitment, and stakeholder engagement are critical for the successful implementation of obesity-related guidelines [51]. Overall, these perspectives highlight that while the checklist is a practical tool, its effectiveness will depend on dedicated resources, long-term planning, and strategic integration into hospital systems.

The phased approach taken in this study is a strength. By integrating existing checklists and reviewing their language, then collecting stakeholder feedback through surveys and interviews, and finally refining the tool and generating implementation recommendations, the study combined existing evidence with lived and professional expertise to produce a checklist that is practical and theoretically robust. The inclusion of diverse respondents across geographies, professional roles, and lived experiences should enhance generalisability. However, the sample sizes of different survey group respondents were small and heterogeneous. The integration of qualitative interviews added depth, enabling us to build on the survey responses by exploring some developing themes and achieving meaning saturation. The qualitative component added important perspectives, particularly in highlighting themes around content, language, care experiences, and implementation barriers. While the checklist was developed to be relevant to the UK NHS context, the existing checklists integrated in phase 1 were from the USA and Canada. Therefore, elements of the checklist should be internationally transferable to similar contexts, although they may need some edits to language, spelling or specific items in the checklist (e.g., kg versus lbs for equipment capacity). Further research would be needed to explore transferability to contexts dissimilar to the UK, for example, low- or middle-income countries. Additionally, the checklist developed in this study adds to the existing checklists and resources available base by providing a holistic and comprehensive overview of requirements for entire hospital environments, rather than being limited to specific or specialist services.

Adherence to international consensus statements and GRAMMS reporting standards improved the robustness of the study. However, the study was cross-sectional and captured perspectives at a single point in time; therefore, it cannot account for changes in attitudes, practices, or system-level developments over time. In addition, completing the survey before the interview may have shaped participants’ responses, focussing discussion within the four checklist domains. Future research should incorporate the final stages of the checklist development framework, including testing, validation, induction, training, implementation, ongoing evaluation (including acceptance and useability), and revision [32]. Longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate the checklist’s real-world effectiveness and its impact on inclusivity, patient experience, and stigma reduction.

Conclusion

This study developed a comprehensive, multiple stakeholder-informed checklist to support obesity inclusivity, reduce weight stigma, and improve hospital care experiences for PLwO. Overall, findings indicate that the checklist offers a clear, practical, and inclusive tool; however, support will be required for successful implementation, including resources, leadership engagement, training, and cultural change.
