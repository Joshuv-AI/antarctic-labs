Interplay between genomic architecture alterations and GDF6 regulation: A candidate mechanism in Nablus mask-like facial syndrome
Authors: Alexandru Marian Bologa, Luiza Dimos, Anca Gabriela Pavel, Maria Stratan, Petruța Elisabeta Bână, Danae Stambouli, Iustin Butnariuc, Radu-Alexandru Truică, Ina-Ofelia Focşa, Vasilica Plăiașu, Andreea Țuțulan-Cuniță
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNablus mask-like facial syndrome (NMLFS) is a very rare disorder associated with 8q22.1q22 microdeletions and characterized by a distinctive facial phenotype and variable neurological, cardiological, and genital anomalies. However, not all affected individuals with overlapping deletions present the mask-like phenotype, thus suggesting the involvement of an additional regulatory mechanism. Here, we perform a comparative analysis of previously reported affected individuals (n = 30) with deletions in the 8q21q22 region, stratified by the presence or absence of the NMLFS phenotype, and including an affected individual from Romania with an 8q21.13q22.1 microdeletion. We investigated the 3D genomic architecture, focusing on topologically associating domains (TADs) and regulatory elements, and identified a recurrent pattern of candidate TAD fusion significantly associated with NMLFS. This configuration brings a cluster of ultra-conserved non-coding elements (UCNEs) in the proximity of GDF6, a known regulator of craniofacial development. Thus, we propose a mechanism involving TAD fusion and enhancer hijacking affecting the candidate gene GDF6 that may underlie the facial anomaly and provide insight into the variable expressivity in 8q22.1 microdeletion syndrome.

Introduction

Partial deletions of the long arm of chromosome 8 have been associated with multiple developmental disorders, including 8q21.11 microdeletion syndrome (MIM: 614230) and Nablus mask-like facial syndrome (NMLFS; MIM: 608156). NMLFS is a rare condition characterized by a particular dysmorphic facial phenotype, intellectual disability, and speech impairment and has been linked to a deletion affecting a minimal critical region at 8q22.1. However, patients with similar deletions without the typical mask-like appearance have been described, suggesting that the deletion alone may not be sufficient to determine the particular facial phenotype.1 Several affected individuals who harbor deletions spanning the 8q21q22 have been reported, with variable clinical presentations. Urraca et al. described an 8q21 deletion in an individual with dysmorphic facies, psychosis, and psychomotor delay.2 An 8q21q22 deletion was reported in an infant with congenital brain and heart defects and developmental delay.3 Nevado et al. report an individual with an 8q22.2 deletion who displays intellectual disability, absent speech, seizures, and characteristic dysmorphism.4

In the clinical context, coding variants have received the most attention in disease interpretation, whereas the contribution of non-coding elements is poorly described due to insufficient data. In recent years, research has shifted toward regulatory elements and their involvement in certain diseases, leading to the identification of bona fide clusters of regulatory non-coding elements, such as enhancers and super-enhancers, that act through physical contact mediated by chromatin folding.5,6,7 Genetic elements located on the same chromosome interact in cis, typically within spatially defined genomic compartments known as topologically associating domains (TADs). These TADs are altered by structural variants such as deletions, duplications, etc.8,9

Here, we aim to investigate the genetic mechanisms underlying NMLFS by analyzing the potential effects of deletion-induced chromatin conformation changes, focusing on TAD and enhancer organization. We analyzed data collected from the literature on 13 affected individuals with deletions at 8q21q22 and the characteristic mask-like appearance of NMLFS and on 17 with deletions in the same region without the mask-like facies. We also included an additional affected individual from Romania who has an 8q21.13q22.1 microdeletion.

Material and methods

Clinical description

A 2-year-old girl was referred for genetic investigations due to suspected Kabuki syndrome. She was the first and only child of a couple, conceived through in vitro fertilization. She was born at 38 weeks of gestation, delivered by cesarean section, with an Apgar score of 9. At birth, she weighed 3,880 g, with a length of 51 cm and an occipitofrontal circumference (OFC) of 35 cm. At 2 years, she weighed 13.5 kg and had an OFC of 46 cm (25th centile). She presented facial dysmorphism suggestive of Kabuki phenotype, including epicanthus, long eyelashes, arched eyebrows, and long palpebral fissures, along with hyperactivity, attention deficit with short attention span, global developmental delay, speech delay, and joint laxity. Transfontanellar, cardiac, and abdominal ultrasounds performed during her first weeks of life showed normal results.

Written informed consent for publication was obtained from the child’s legal representatives.

Peripheral blood was collected from the child, and DNA was extracted using the Thermo Fisher Scientific PureLink Genomic DNA Isolation Kit. DNA concentration was assessed using the Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and copy-number variation (CNV) analysis were performed on an Illumina platform, according to the manufacturer’s protocols. A total of 327,500,508 reads were generated with a mean sequence quality score of 36. Reads were mapped to the reference genome GRCh37, and data analysis was performed on the Congenica platform (Wellcome Trust), employing the Sentieon DNAseq pipeline for variant calling and ExomeDepth10 for CNV calling. Variants with a population frequency below 3% were analyzed based on bioinformatic algorithm predictions and available annotations from dedicated genetic databases (ClinVar, gnomAD, LOVD, and DECIPHER) and classified according to ACMG guidelines.

Microarray analysis was performed using the Affymetrix Cytoscan 750K single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) array. All procedures were carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Affymetrix). CNVs were annotated using available databases, including DECIPHER, ClinVar, Franklin, VarSome, and DGV, and classified according to ACMG guidelines.

A total of 30 affected individuals from the literature reporting deletions in the 8q21q22 region were collected. Of these, 13 were reported to exhibit the characteristic mask-like appearance of NMLFS, while 17 with deletions in the same region were reported to lack the mask-like facies; we added our affected individual to the latter. Deletions described in the recruited affected individuals were detected using microarray and/or next-generation sequencing (NGS) techniques; the coordinates were collected as described in the reference articles, as there was no reliable option to infer the maximal range. Genomic coordinates of all analyzed affected individuals were collected in BED format and converted to GRCh38 using the LiftOver tool from the University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC) Genome Browser.11 The UCSC Genome Browser was employed for data visualization.12

TAD coordinates were obtained from 3D Genome Browser13 as a GRCh37-based BED file derived from Hi-C analysis of the cell line GM12878 (GM12878_Lieberman-raw_TADs.txt), a widely used model for genetic and transcriptomic research.14 The UCSC LiftOver was used to convert coordinates to GRCh38.

All analyses were performed on BED using bedtools (intersect function) and the Bash programming language. We numerically labeled each TAD, and domains spanning both deletion breakpoints were considered as potentially fused following the deletion.

RStudio Build 467 (2024.12.0) was used to compute Fisher’s exact test (fisher.test function) values for each pair of predicted fused TADs identified.

In order to visualize the 3D genomic context before and after the deletion event, we employed TADeus2 (https://tadeus2.mimuw.edu.pl/), a tool designed for the evaluation of chromatin conformation changes on chromosome build GRCh38.15

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from the legal guardian in accordance with the current ethics guidelines, protocols for patient confidentiality, and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

WGS analysis did not identify pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in KMT2D or KDM6A (the two main genes associated with Kabuki syndrome) or other relevant genes. However, a heterozygous 12.3 Mb deletion was identified on chromosome 8 (chr8:82119575–94392575 [GRCh37]; chr8:81207340–93380347 [GRCh38]), spanning the 8q21.13–q22.1 region. The deletion was confirmed by SNP array (Figure 1), with a slight offset of the breakpoint’s coordinates: arr[GRCh38] 8q21.13q22.1(81,204,165_93,388,533)x1. No other pathogenic or likely pathogenic CNVs were detected using WGS or the SNP array. According to WGS data, the deletion spans a total of 34 OMIM genes (42 RefSeq genes and 17 non-coding elements), with 6 morbid genes: CA2 (MIM: 611492), CNGB3 (MIM: 605080), IMPA1 (MIM: 602064), NBN (MIM: 602667), OTUD6B (MIM: 612021), and PMP2 (MIM: 170715); no deletion breakpoint resides in a protein-coding gene that could lead to gene truncation. According to the ClinGen Dosage Sensitivity platform, no evidence for dosage pathogenicity was identified for any of the deleted genes, except NBN (haploinsufficiency [HI] score of 30, indicating association with an autosomal-recessive condition). According to DECIPHER, the deleted region comprises another 4 genes predicted to exhibit haploinsufficiency, namely, WWP1, MMP16, RIPK2, and RUNX1T1; however, the clinical phenotype cannot be, apparently, explained by the loss of any of these genes, as none of them is an OMIM morbid gene or associated with any disease in DECIPHER.

Among the 6 morbid genes located in the deleted area, 5 genes (CA2, CNGB3, IMPA1, NBN, and OTUD6B) are associated with autosomal-recessive disorders. Given that large heterozygous deletions may unmask recessive variants on the remaining allele, we analyzed these loci, but no pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were found. The 6th morbid gene is PMP2, associated with Charcot-Marie-Tooth type 1G disease (CMT1G; MIM: 618279), a condition with autosomal-dominant (AD) inheritance. However, deletion of the entire PMP2 gene has not been associated with Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease. Overall, this deletion is classified as pathogenic based on ACMG criteria and tools such as Franklin and VarSome.

Although no known CNV syndrome has been associated with the deleted region, the closest comparable to our case are 8q21.11 and 8q22.1 microdeletion syndromes; the latter is sometimes defined as NMLFS, regardless of the presence of the mask-like facies.

To investigate the genetic factors that may lead to variable expression of the facial phenotype, we recruited a total of 31 individuals (including our affected individual) with deletions in the region of interest (Table S1). Affected individuals were selected based on molecular evidence (microarray and/or WGS) of a deletion in the 8q21q22 genomic region and available phenotypic description. We classified the individuals into two categories: one with a specific NMLFS phenotype and a second with a non-NMLFS phenotype, including our patient. In NMLFS, a pattern of fused TADs was observed: in all but one individual, the centromeric breakpoint is located in TAD94, while the telomeric breakpoints are located in either TAD98 or -99, with only one breakpoint in TAD100. Nine out of 13 have deletion breakpoints within the same TADs (94 and 99). Conversely, our analysis did not identify any individuals without NMLFS who harbor candidate fusion between 94 and 99 TADs, nor any clear pattern of TAD fusion. However, in this group, there are 2 notable affected individuals, P4 and P15, whose centromeric deletion breakpoint is just outside TAD94, toward TAD93, resulting in an apparent loss of TAD94.

Fisher’s exact test demonstrated a significant association between TAD94–TAD99 fusion and the NMLFS phenotype (p = 0.00056) (Table S2). This result suggests that fusion of 94 and 99 TADs (at coordinates chr8:91512772–93662772 and chr8:95312772–96237772 in GRCh38, respectively) is more likely to occur in individuals with NMLFS compared to the non-NMLFS group. Notably, TAD94 is deleted in 12 of the 18 individuals without NMLFS. Similar to the NMLFS group, TAD94 is partially preserved in P1, P10, P12, P14, and P18 but with a key difference, as shown in Figure 2: the remaining telomeric region of TAD94 (consecutive to deletion) consistently appears to be fused with a genetic element located centromeric to the deletion, while the telomeric sequence of the chromosome remains intact. The absence of the majority of TAD94 in the non-NMLFS group suggests that a regulatory element located within this TAD may influence the facial aspect when repositioned.

To identify candidate regulatory elements underlying this mechanism, we analyzed H3K27Ac data indicating active enhancer regions, PhyloP scores indicating highly conserved DNA sequences, NCBI RefSeq Functional Elements, and UCNE clusters in relation to genes potentially relevant for the mask-like facies. This analysis suggests that TAD94 is associated with a UCNE cluster annotated as RUNX1T1_cluster, which may act as one or multiple enhancers (Figure 2). Following the deletion of the region between TAD94 (with the preservation of its centromeric end) and TAD99, the remaining part of the RUNX1T1_cluster may be repositioned in very close proximity to GDF6, potentially leading to an increase in GDF6 expression as a result of altered 3D chromatin structure.

Several exceptions to this hypothesis were observed: PN2, reported to have blepharophimosis and tight nasal skin and suspected of Schwartz-Jampel syndrome, has the entire candidate UCNE cluster deleted; the deletion identified in PN3 and PN4, both presenting with blepharophimosis and tight, glistening facial skin, encompasses GDF6, an observation also noted by Raas-Rothschild et al.16,17 All of these may constitute limitations of data analysis in relation to phenotype variability.

To further explore whether enhancer element(s) located centromeric to TAD94 might be brought into close proximity to the GDF6 gene, we used TADeus2 to visualize the Hi-C matrix and TAD distribution within the critical region before the deletion (Figure S1). The syntenic mode shows the initial genomic context for the region of interest, with the deletion breakpoints marked by dotted red lines, while the breakpoint mode shows the rearrangement following deletion. The detailed view generated by TADeus2 revealed that the deletion spans multiple TADs, with the centromeric TADs containing enhancer-like features, while the telomeric TAD contains the GDF6 gene. In the NMLFS group (Figure S1B), except for PN2, PN3, and PN4, following deletion, the discrete repositioning of the UCNE cluster in relation to GDF6 can be observed, with the centromeric part of the UCNE cluster moving closer to the GDF6, without any interposing genetic elements (in PN2, the whole cluster is deleted, while in PN3 and PN4, GDF6 is lost). Thus, the distance between the telomeric breakpoint, representing the closest part of the UCNE cluster, and GDF6 varies in these individuals between 11.2 (PN11) and 856.8 (PN1) kb, with an average of 320 kb. Meanwhile, in non-NMLFS patients (Figure S1A), the UCNE cluster is either completely lost or, when its telomeric region remains, other genetic elements are interposed between the UCNE cluster and GDF6; alternatively, GDF6 may be lost itself. In any case, the close, direct proximity between the UCNE cluster and GDF6 is not observed.

Discussion

The 8q22.1 microdeletion syndrome is a poorly understood condition with a highly heterogeneous clinical phenotype. One of its most striking characteristics is that only a subset of patients present with a mask-like facial phenotype, suggesting the hypothesis that supplementary genetic, epigenetic, or environmental factors might be involved.1,18 Interestingly, 8q22.1 microduplication syndrome (Leri pleonosteosis), a disease with phenotypic features that partially overlap with Nablus syndrome, is reported to result from overexpression of GDF6 and SDC2 as a consequence of the gene dosage effect.19,20

Despite opposite copy-number changes, both conditions exhibit overlapping features, including blepharophimosis, abnormal palpebral fissures, stiff joints with contractures, hand anomalies, and facial rigidity, suggesting perturbation of a common developmental connective tissue regulatory network. In Leri pleonosteosis, duplication of 8q22.1 likely results in increased gene dosage and triplosensitivity, contributing to dysregulation of extracellular matrix homeostasis via the link between TGF-β/SMAD signaling, GDF6, and SDC2, placing it within the “TGF-β-pathies” group of disorders.19 The TGF-β/SMAD pathway is known to play a role in organ fibrosis, including skin, which may be involved in the mask-like facies.21,22 By contrast, the more complex craniofacial phenotype observed in Nablus syndrome may not be fully explained by the contiguous gene deletion alone, supporting the contribution of position effects mediated by altered chromatin architecture and enhancer dysregulation following deletion. Together, these observations support a model in which reciprocal structural variants at 8q22.1 might share common biological pathways through distinct dosage and position-dependent mechanisms.

To better understand the mechanistic factors at work in 8q22.1 microdeletion syndrome, we collected data from 30 individuals reported in the literature, with deletions in the same cytogenomic region as our patient, 8q21q22, and categorized them into two groups based on the presence of the mask-like phenotype. Aside from this feature, the clinical presentation of the patients was widely variable (Table S1). Almost all of them exhibited variable facial dysmorphism, which was more specific in the NMLFS group. The non-NMLFS group showed developmental delay ranging from mild to moderate, as well as isolated speech delay or cognitive impairment; three individuals exhibited autistic behavior. In the NMLFS group, three individuals had normal intelligence, while the remaining individuals had varying degrees of developmental delay.

Genital anomalies were present in both groups, though more prevalent in the NMLFS group. Cardiac defects were more frequent in the non-NMLFS group, with five affected, whereas only one person in the NMLFS group had a small atrial septal defect that closed spontaneously. Obesity was reported in three individuals in the non-NMLFS group but in none of the NMLFS group.

Additional clinical features were observed in both groups. In the non-NMLFS group, these included recurrent infections, hearing loss, craniosynostosis, various facial dysmorphisms, ophthalmological anomalies, limb anomalies or dysmorphisms, café-au-lait spots, joint hyperlaxity, hypotonia, and feeding problems in infancy. The NMLFS group also presented with acquired microcephaly, macrocephaly, dental anomalies, and other abnormalities.

In this context, our patient presents a limited number of dysmorphic features (high-arched eyebrows, long eyelashes, and epicanthus), developmental delay, attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and joint laxity, despite having a large loss of genetic material.

Often, genomic analyses, especially in a diagnostic context, focus on the linear organization of the genome, in which genes and regulatory elements are organized as a sequence. Yet, chromatin is folded into complex 3D structures that bring together genomic elements into close spatial proximity. Recent studies23,24 indicate that TAD integrity is essential for the proper functioning of the genome, playing an important role in the regulation of gene expression. Within TADs, genes and regulatory elements are physically brought together, and structural variants spanning these regions may disturb this interaction and could lead to gene expression dysregulation associated with developmental diseases and cancer.15,25

Given previous reports of TAD-altering mechanisms in abnormal limb development and cardiac anomalies,25 we investigated for suitable candidate interactions in 8q21q22 deletions that might explain the variability of the mask-like facies. By comparing the BED file of TADs derived from the GM12878 cell line with the BED files of patients with NMLFS and non-NMLFS patients, followed by the analysis of the disrupted and fused TADs consecutive to deletion events, we identified the potentially relevant TAD94_99 fusion pattern that was significantly associated with the NMLFS group (Tables 1 and S2), supporting the hypothesis that this fusion may be correlated with the investigated phenotype.

Visualization of the genomic data using the UCSC Genome Browser showed that TAD94 encompasses a region of UCNEs in multiple vertebrate species: a UCNE cluster annotated as RUNX1T1_cluster (ID = 552 in UCNEbase). This cluster is associated with the genes C8orf83, C8orf87, FLJ46284, LINC00535, RUNX1T1, and SLC26A7 and comprises 41 elements.6 Notably, UCNEs have been proposed to act as transcriptional regulators of essential developmental genes, and the Nablus phenotype is strongly correlated with developmental problems. Conversely, TAD99 contains GDF6. Nine of the telomeric breakpoints of the deletion found in the 13 individuals with NMLFS are located in this TAD, in proximity to GDF6, resulting in the repositioning of the UCNE cluster in TAD94 closer to this gene, with an average distance between them of ∼320 kb. The 5′ end of GDF6 is located near the telomere; however, the gene has been reported to be regulated by cis-regulatory elements within an approximately 500 kb region at the 3′ end of the gene.26 In the study conducted by Bademci et al. on ear development abnormalities,26 deletions located within ∼350 kb centromeric to GDF6 disturb gene expression, suggesting the presence of one or multiple enhancers mapping to the deleted region. The centromeric 900 kb flanking sequence of GDF6 maps to a gene desert, a genomic region frequently associated with regulatory elements controlling gene expression during embryonic development.27

The TADeus2 tool enabled visualization of the interaction matrix in the region of interest prior to deletion, highlighting the breakpoints located centromeric to GDF6, the only dosage-sensitive gene in the region. Following deletion, the UCNE cluster, which is disrupted by the centromeric breakpoint, appears to be repositioned in proximity to GDF6, potentially enabling this gene to hijack regulatory elements from this enhancer group (Figures 3 and S1B).

Given the association of GDF6 with developmental disorders usually accompanied by skeletal abnormalities and intellectual disability28,29,30 and its dosage sensitivity, GDF6 is a strong candidate for the NMLFS phenotype. In most individuals with NMLFS, both breakpoints of the deletions are located within non-coding regions: the telomeric one is an average distance of approximately 320 kb from GDF6, while the centromeric breakpoint disrupts the UCNE RUNX1T1_cluster region. Figure 3 and the cross-comparison between NMLFS and non-NMLFS groups illustrate the effect of TAD fusion following the deletion and support the observation that multiple enhancers, possibly within the RUNX1T1_cluster, are brought in close spatial proximity to GDF6, indicating its putative key role in this rare genetic condition.

Taken together, our results suggest that NMLFS-inducing deletions consistently span specific TAD boundaries, resulting in interactions between unrelated strong enhancers and the GDF6 promoter or regulatory elements, potentially leading to its abnormal (including ectopic) expression. GDF6 has previously been reported to be required for normal development of the middle ear, joints and ligaments, and skull bones in humans and mice.28,29,30 Moreover, GDF6 plays an important role in the patterning of the epidermis, and it can bind directly to and block the effects of noggin, a protein that controls neural development.31 Variants in GDF6 have been associated with several developmental abnormalities of the skeleton, eye, and other organs, including Klippel-Feil syndrome.32 It has also been reported that ectopic GDF6 expression promoted stem cell differentiation toward the mesodermal lineage,33 from which many of the anatomical structures affected by NMLFS originate.

Thus, we propose a mechanism for NMLFS similar to that described in adult-onset AD leukodystrophy (ADLD) disease,24 Liebenberg syndrome,34,35 and facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy (FSHD) disease,36 in which deletions disrupting TAD boundaries may result in aberrant expression of certain genes through the TAD fusion effect. In this context, the deletion may be sufficient to contribute to the mask-like facies when its breakpoints enable fusion between the UCNE cluster and the regulatory landscape of GDF6, although additional modifying factors cannot be excluded.

Our findings resulted from an opposite approach to that of Overhoff et al.,37 who suggest that unaffected individuals should not be used to define the critical region of a structural variant. In our case, precisely this comparison allowed us to detect the pattern of TAD fusion.

Our study has several limitations. First, the number of probands is limited, due to the rarity of the disease, and phenotypic documentation, including facial photographs, was not available for all reported affected individuals. Second, the published reports describing 8q21q22 deletions are heterogeneous in terms of the molecular biology techniques used for detection, including both microarray and NGS-based approaches. Since no reliable option was available to determine the maximal deletion ranges from array-generated data, we used the coordinates reported in the reference articles. Third, another limitation stems from the absence of detailed cytogenetic characterization of the derivative chromosomes carrying the deletions, as the loss of genetic material is often accompanied by more complex structural rearrangements undetectable by microarray or NGS,38 which may greatly impact TAD organization and function. Four, our study relies primarily on bioinformatic analyses, and although further functional validation would strengthen this model, the recurrent genomic pattern observed across independent cases provides support for this mechanism.

In conclusion, our findings support the previously proposed hypothesis1 that GDF6 may be involved in the genetic determination of NMLFS phenotype and provide insight into a potential mechanism involving hijacking of enhancers of the UCNE RUNX1T1_cluster located within the 8q21.3q22.1 region. Beyond this specific model, our study may contribute to a broader understanding of the fundamental genetic concepts, such as incomplete penetrance, variable expressivity, and position effect, in the clinical context of structural variants.
