Methodological quality of health literacy instruments used among school children in Asian countries: a systematic review
Authors: Nur Aziemah Mohd Fauzi, Mahmathi Karuppannan, Li Shean Toh, Luke Sawyers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThis study aimed to examine the methodological quality of measurement properties of health literacy instruments used in children, specifically in Asian countries, and to identify the most suitable instrument for field application.MethodsThis systematic review was conducted following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Health literacy studies conducted between 2013 and 2023 and involving children aged 6–18 years old were identified through searches in PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science. Only studies that assessed methodological qualities were included. Quality appraisal was conducted using COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health status Measurement Instruments (COSMIN).ResultA total of 10 studies met the inclusion criteria from the screening process. The quality of measurement properties in these studies varied. With 9 items on the COSMIN checklist and 10 studies assessed, this resulted in a total of 90 checklist items evaluated. Based on this, 25 items were classified as very good (primarily for structural validity and general criteria), 7 as adequate (mainly for content validity), 14 as inadequate (mostly for internal consistency), 10 as doubtful (mostly for reliability) and 34 as unknown (primarily for measurement error, cross-cultural validity and responsiveness). Nearly 40% (37.7%) of measurement quality ratings were either unknown or not reported. Among the tools assessed, the 8-item Health Literacy Assessment Tool Chinese version (C-HLAT-8) demonstrated very good quality across most measurement properties compared to others. C-HLAT-8 includes functional, interactive and critical health literacy domains and has been assessed in children aged 11–17 in China.ConclusionDespite the global interest in health literacy, studies focusing on methodological quality in the context of school children and Asian countries remain limited. The C-HLAT 8 emerges as a promising tool for field application due to its strong methodological quality and adaptability.KEYWORDS: Systematic review, health literacy, school children, methodological quality

Background

Health literacy reflects an individual’s knowledge, personal skills and confidence to take actions that enhance both personal and community health by changing lifestyles and living conditions (World Health Organization, n.d.). The concepts of health literacy encompass several dimensions, including cognitive aspects (how knowledge is used), behavioural factors (health-related choices), socioeconomic and environmental contexts and the type, quantity and quality of health care received. Low health literacy has been associated with poorer health knowledge, more complex health behaviours, poor health outcomes, inadequate use of health services and increased health care costs (Buhr & Tannen, 2020). A systematic review conducted by Shoyemi et al., in 2024 found that higher health literacy can improve health outcomes and enhance healthcare delivery (Shoyemi et al., 2024).

Promoting health literacy from an early age is therefore crucial. From a public health perspective, children represent an essential target group due to their developing cognitive, emotional and physical abilities. Health education interventions are particularly effective at this stage as health-related behaviours and attitudes are developing. Hence, improving health literacy among children and adolescents can foster lifelong healthy habits and reduce future health risks (Nutbeam, 2000).

Despite growing attention to health literacy research, there are limited systematic reviews evaluating the quality of measurement tools used among adolescents and children in Asian countries. For example, the review by Rajah et al. (2019) reported a low level of health literacy in five Southeast Asian countries. However, it focused only on assessing health literacy levels and did not evaluate the instruments used. Validated and reliable tools for measuring health literacy in Asian populations, especially among younger groups, remain scarce.

Interest in health literacy research has grown considerably, as reflected by a nearly tenfold increase in related publications from Asian countries over the past decade in the Web of Science database (Duong & Chang, 2024). These studies cover diverse health literacy topics, yet there remains a paucity of research focusing on children. As mentioned by Duong and Chang (2024) only four studies have examined health literacy among high school students, and none have addressed children below 12 years of age. Therefore, this systematic review aims to examine the methodological quality of measurement properties of health literacy instruments used among children, specifically in Asian countries, and to identify the most suitable instrument for field application.

Methods

We developed a review protocol before commencing the study, following the systematic review procedures outlined in the Cochrane Handbook (PROSPERO registration number: CRD42023476643) (Higgins et al., 2024). The review was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement to maintain methodological transparency and reporting quality (Elsman et al., 2024).

Literature searching techniques

Electronic searches were conducted in the following databases: PubMed, SCOPUS and WOS. Additionally, hand searching of the indexes published article was also performed (Page et al., 2021). The keywords used were ‘health literacy’ (health literacy, health education, health knowledge), ‘children’ (child, adolescent, youth) and questionnaire (instrument, measure, survey, tool, screen, assess, test, psychometric, evaluate). All keywords were combined using the Boolean operators ‘AND’ and ‘OR’.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The search strategy employed comprehensive terms developed using the Population, Intervention, Comparison(s), Outcome and Study design (PICOS) framework. The keywords were generated according to PICOS elements (Table 1). However, no comparison component was applied in this review. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are tabulated in Table 2.

Study selection

All references retrieved from the PubMed, SCOPUS and WOS databases (n = 24,202) were imported into Rayyan.AI web tool. Duplicate records were removed prior to the screening process. Titles and abstracts of the identified papers were screened by two authors (NAMF and MK) based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies or uncertainties were resolved through discussion.

In the first stage, both authors (NAMF and MK) screened the titles and abstracts to identify potentially relevant studies. In the second stage, the full-text of the selected articles was assessed independently by the same authors to determine final eligibility. Only studies reporting the development and/or validation of health literacy instruments were included in the review.

Data extraction and synthesis

Data extraction was conducted by NAMF and independently reviewed by MK to ensure accuracy. Extracted information included publication year, author(s), study design, sample size, health literacy instrument(s) and key outcomes were extracted from each included study. Any disagreements were resolved through consensus between the two authors.

Risk of bias assessment

The included studies were subsequently assessed for risk of bias using the COnsensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health Status Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) study design checklist for Patient-Reported Outcome Measurement instruments (PROM) version July 2019.

The COSMIN assessment comprised nine domains: (1) general design, (2) content validity, (3) structural validity, (4) hypothesis testing, (5) cross-cultural validity, (6) internal consistency, (7) measurement error, (8) reliability, and (9) responsiveness. For studies involving translation into another language, the quality of the translation process was also evaluated.

Each domain was rated as ‘very good’, ‘adequate’, ‘doubtful’ or ‘inadequate’. Domains that were not applicable to a study were categorised as ‘not applicable’. This approach was based on Terwee et al.; in a situation where one of the sub-items is rated at the lowest score, the domain will be categorised as the lowest. For example, although other sub-items were ‘very good’, but one was ‘doubtful’, the whole domain will be categorised as ‘doubtful’ (Terwee et al., 2012).

The overall assessment was conducted into three stages: (1) methodological quality assessment, (2) quality of the included studies and (3) overall rating ‘best evidence synthesis’. The second stage followed Terwee et al.’s method, which classifies measurement quality as ‘positive’, ‘negative’, ‘indeterminate’ or ‘unknown’ (Terwee et al., 2012). The first stage was conducted independently by both authors, while the second stage was conducted by NAMF and verified by MK. The third stage was conducted based on the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework adapted by the COSMIN group (Guo, Armstrong, et al., 2018).

In ‘best evidence synthesis’, each measurement property is rated as positive, negative, conflicting or unknown, and given a level of evidence (strong, moderate or limited). The overall rating is determined in three steps. First, the methodological quality of each study is checked using the COSMIN checklist, and studies with ‘poor’ quality are excluded. Second, each measurement property is judged using Terwee’s quality criteria. Third, the results from different studies on the same instrument are compared to see whether they are consistent (Guo et al., 2018). However, as this review only involved one instrument once, the last process was not conducted.

Results

This review focused on studies examining health literacy among adolescents aged below 18 years of age in Asian countries. The database search identified a total of 24,202 publications that met the initial search criteria (PubMed = 11,481; Scopus = 11,297; Web of Science = 1424). An additional 16 articles were identified through manual searching.

After removing duplicates (n = 11,105) and excluding 12,924 studies that were qualitative, systematic reviews or unrelated topics, 173 articles remained for screening. At this stage, both researchers (NAMF and MK) independently screened the titles to exclude irrelevant papers. Subsequently, 24 articles were selected for abstract screening.

Upon reviewing abstracts, 16 articles were excluded, leaving 157 articles for full-text analysis. Of these, 46 studies were assessed for eligibility based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Finally, 10 articles were included in the final systematic review as they specifically addressed the development and/or validation of health literacy instruments. In contrast, the China study (Guo et al., 2018) recruited participants exclusively from urban areas.

Description of instruments

Among the 10 reviewed articles, five instruments were newly developed and not adapted from any existing instruments. These include Knowledge-Based Health Literacy (funHLS-YA) from Japan. Korean Health Literacy Instrument for children (KHLI-C), Taiwan Children’s Health Literacy Test (TCHL), Health Literacy Assessment Tool for 9–10 Years Old children from Thailand (HLAT-31) and Health Literacy Measure for Adolescents (HELMA) from Iran. Only one study, by Tsubakita et al. (2020) in Japan, compared two instruments: funHLS-YA and Health Literacy Scale for Japanese Adults 14 items (HLS-14). In this study, funHLS-YA measured functional health literacy, while HLS-14 assessed perceived health literacy (Tsubakita et al., 2020).

The mode of assessment varied among instruments. The KHLI-C, funHLS-YA and TCHL used objective multiple-choice questions, requiring respondents to select the correct answer. In contrast, HLAT-31, Health Literacy Assessment Scale for Adolescents (HAS-A), HLS-14, HELMA, and Health Literacy for School-Aged Children – Turkish Version (HLSAC) employed a Likert-scale format to assess respondents’ agreement. The C-HLAT-8 also used multiple-choice items, but it applied a differential scoring system, where each response carried a specific weight. A higher total score indicated a greater health literacy.

Participants and measurement scope

This systematic review includes both children and adolescents due to the limited availability of tools specifically developed for younger children. Therefore, the included studies comprised both children and adolescents, with several instruments primarily validated among adolescent populations. The majority of the studies reported the age group or age range of their participants. However, the study that developed theHLSAC presented only the mean age of the population studied (Haney, 2020). Another study using the Short-Form Health Literacy Instrument (HLS-SF-12) included participants aged above 15 years (Duong et al., 2019). The HLS-SF-12 was included due to the definition of children by the WHO, which was less than 17 years old (World Health Organization, 2024).

Each article also described the scope and the type of health literacy measured by its respective instruments. Some studies classified their instruments according to Nutbeam’s health literacy model (Nutbeam, 2000), while some followed the domains proposed by Sorensen et al. (2012). According to Nutbeam, health literacy consists of three types, which are functional, interactive and critical. Communication is also used interchangeably as interactive in some literature (Nutbeam, 2000). Meanwhile, Sorensen et al., on the other hand, defined health literacy across three domains: health care, disease prevention, and health promotion (Sorensen et al., 2012).

However, HAS-A includes only a part of Nutbeam’s health literacy model for functional and communication domains and adds an additional domain of confusion. The confusion domain refers to the extent to which adolescents experience difficulty or uncertainty in understanding the health information they receive (Sarhan et al., 2024).

Among the instruments reviewed, KHLI-C and funHLS-YA measured functional health literacy exclusively (Park & Kim, 2021; Tsubakita et al., 2020). While TCHL, HELMA and C-HLAT-8 measured functional, interactive and critical health literacy components (Ghanbari et al., 2016; Guo et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2016). The HAS-A instrument measured functional, communication (interactive) and confusion domains.

Instrument structure and administration

The number of items across the instruments ranged from 8 items to 44. The shortest instrument was C-HLAT-8, which consisted of 8 items, while the longest was HELMA. Eight instruments used a Likert-type response scale, ranging from four to six points. Only five studies reported the time required for participants to complete the questionnaires. The shortest time was recorded for C-HLAT-8, while the longest was for TCHL.

Methodological quality

Using nine items on the COSMIN checklist, a total of 90 items were evaluated across the ten included studies. The methodological quality of the included studies is presented in Table 4. Nearly 38% (37.7%) of the measurement quality ratings were ‘not applicable’ as it is not reported in the study.

Nearly all studies (n = 9) received a ‘very good’ for general methodological quality, which reflects clear research aims, adequate descriptions of patient-reported outcome instrument measures and well-defined study populations (Mokkink et al., 2019). The reported Cronbach’s α coefficients for internal consistency ranged from 0.604 to 0.93, with the lowest value found in the ‘confusion’ subscale of the Taiwan-HAS-A (Chou et al., 2023), and the highest in HELMA (α = 0.93) (Ghanbari et al., 2016).

All studies also assessed the instrument’s reliability. Five studies demonstrated a ‘very good’ reliability rating, as they reported intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). Three studies were rated as ‘doubtful’ and two as ‘inadequate’. The C-HLAT 8 was the only study that reported measurement error and responsiveness, receiving a ‘very good’ rating for measurement error but a ‘doubtful’ rating for responsiveness due to a small retest sample size (n = 39) (Guo et al., 2018).

For content validity, six studies were rated as ‘adequate’, two as ‘inadequate’ and two as ‘doubtful’. In terms of structural validity, seven studies achieved ‘very good’ ratings, one was ‘adequate’ and the two were ‘doubtful’. Only three studies conducted hypothesis testing: funHLS-YA (Tsubakita et al., 2020) and C-HLAT 8 (Guo et al., 2018) were rated ‘very good’, while TCHL (Liu et al., 2016) was rated ‘doubtful’. Cross-cultural validity was only assessed in HLS-SF-12, which involved data from multiple countries and was rated as ‘very good’.

Translation quality was assessed only in studies that involved adaptation and translation into another language. Five studies evaluated these aspects: C-HLAT-8 (Guo et al., 2018) and Health Literacy Assessment Scale for Adolescents in Arabic (HAS-A-AR) (Sarhan et al., 2020) were rated as ‘very good’, HLS-SF-12 was rated as ‘adequate’ (Duong et al., 2019), HLSAC (Haney, 2018) was rated as ‘doubtful’ and HAS-A (Chou et al., 2023) received an ‘inadequate’ rating.

Discussion

Quality of the tools

Health literacy research among children and adolescents in Asian countries remains limited (Rajah et al., 2019), especially in studies comparing the methodological quality of instruments. Using the COSMIN checklist, this review critically appraised the psychometric quality of existing tools. In this review, it is noted that all studies assessed general properties, internal consistency, reliability, content validity and structural validity, while the least frequently evaluated measurement properties were cross-cultural validity, measurement error and responsiveness, with only one study examining each of these. There was no evidence indicating that the studies were required to assess all criteria outlined in the COSMIN checklist (Wei et al., 2018).

This review includes both multiple-choice questions and the Likert-scale report. Combining both in one review may have obscured methodological differences and should be considered when interpreting findings. However, objective and subjective instruments differ in their measurement properties. This is due to the fact that objective tools are more prone to performance-related measurement error, while self-reported measures rely on internal consistency and may be influenced by respondent perception (Mokkink et al., 2019). In line with COSMIN recommendations, internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s α for continuous scores, while the Kuder–Richardson Formula 20 (KR-20) was applied for dichotomous items, ensuring that the method of reliability estimation corresponded to the measurement scale (Mokkink et al., 2019).

Three instruments, C-HLAT 8, HELMA and HLS-SF-12, demonstrated strong methodological quality and robust psychometric properties. Both HELMA and HLS-SF-12 received positive ratings across most COSMIN items but lacked assessment of measurement error and hypothesis testing. The C-HLAT 8 performed well in all items except measurement error and structural validity, although it was the only instrument that reported measurement error analysis. Therefore, in this review, HELMA, C-HLAT-8 and HLS-SF-12 show strong psychometric properties.

To establish sound psychometric quality, instruments must demonstrate validity, reliability and responsiveness to ensure that they measure the intended construct (validity), maintain stability over time (reliability) and are sensitive to change (responsiveness) (Dinh & Bonner, 2025). According to the COSMIN checklist, psychometric studies require a sufficient sample size and should address how missing items are managed in the data. For internal consistency, it is crucial to assess unidimensionality and to report Cronbach’s α or KR-20 to determine the quality of internal consistency. Content validity must be evaluated for comprehensiveness and relevance, with an adequate number of participants representing both relevant professionals and the target population. For structural validity, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) is considered superior to exploratory factor analysis (EFA). In addition, sample size is an essential criterion, where having more than 100 participants or at least seven times the number of items is regarded as ‘very good’ according to COSMIN requirements (Terwee et al., 2012). Missing items were accounted for within the domain of structural validity. The method used to handle missing items should be clearly described to ensure a ‘very good’ rating, while failure to do so will result in a ‘doubtful’ rating (Mokkink et al., 2019). Conversely, the HLAT-31 was rated weakest, showing an ‘inadequate’ score for general and internal consistency, and ‘doubtful’ for structural validity and reliability.

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the COSMIN checklist, while the quality of the reported measurement properties was evaluated using Terwee’s criteria. In simple terms, the COSMIN checklist determines ‘how good the study is?’ whereas Terwee’s criteria define ‘how good the instrument is?’. For example, for internal consistency, the COSMIN checklist requires that Cronbach’s α be reported in the study as one of the criteria for a ‘very good’ rating. In contrast, according to Terwee’s criteria, Cronbach’s α must be above 0.7 to receive a ‘+’ rating (Terwee et al., 2012). Therefore, a study may achieve a ‘very good’ rating in COSMIN because it reports Cronbach’s α, but receive a ‘−’ rating in Terwee’s criteria if the Cronbach’s α is less than 0.7. This combined approach allows for a comprehensive assessment of both the methodological rigour of the studies and the performance of the instruments (Guo et al., 2018). The example is given in Supplemental Appendix 4.

Domain and dimension tested

Health literacy instruments can be unidimensional, measuring a single construct or multidimensional, assessing multiple domains (Rouquettea et al., 2021). In this review, KHLI-C, Health Literacy Assessment Tool for 9–10 years old (31 items), from Thailand and HLSAC were identified as unidimensional. KHLI-C and Health Literacy Assessment Tool for 9–10 years old (31 items), from Thailand, measured functional health literacy, whereas HLSAC focused solely on the health promotion domain. The funHLS-YA also focused on functional health literacy only. The remaining instruments were multidimensional, encompassing multiple aspects of health literacy.

Unidimensional tools offer simplicity and convenience for both the target population and researchers, as they are efficient to administer and easy to interpret (Nutbeam, 2000). They are particularly useful for rapid screening. In contrast, multidimensional tools measure several domains, such as functional, interactive and critical health literacy. Because they assess multiple aspects of health literacy, they provide a clearer reflection of an individual’s strengths and weaknesses across different domains. Although they require a more detailed assessment, the domain-specific insights generated by multidimensional tools are more beneficial for planning and implementing public health interventions (Ishikawa et al., 2008). The selection of an instrument depends on various aspects to ensure it meets the study objectives, and each instrument should align with the health literacy framework from which it is derived (Barros et al., 2023).

Cross-cultural adaptation

Only HLS-SF-12 achieved a ‘very good’ rating for cross-cultural validity according to the COSMIN checklist. This instrument was tested in six countries, namely Indonesia, Kazakhstan, Malaysia, Taiwan, Myanmar and Vietnam. Following Beaton’s guideline, cross-cultural adaptation is required when an instrument is used in a setting different from its origin or in another language. This process involves five stages: translation, synthesis, back translation, expert committee review and pilot testing (Beaton et al., 2000).

Five instruments underwent cross-cultural adaptation: Taiwan-HAS-A, C-HLAT 8, HAS-A-AR, Turkish-HLSAC and HLS-SF-12. Among these, only C-HLAT 8 explicitly followed Beaton’s guideline. HAS-A-AR and C-HLAT 8 demonstrated ‘very good’ translation quality, and Turkish-HLSAC rated the ‘doubtful’ result as it relied solely on the back translation technique. HLS-SF-12 received an ‘adequate’ rating, as the involvement of the original developer was not mentioned. Finally, Taiwan-HAS-A was rated ‘inadequate’ because it did not report whether the target population was consulted regarding the relevance, comprehensiveness and comprehensibility of the tool. Culturally adapted tools must adequately reflect the original version (Wei et al., 2018). To achieve this, high-quality translation and cultural adaptation include pre-testing in the target population. These are crucial to ensure conceptual equivalence across languages, even though these processes can be time-consuming and costly (Beaton et al., 2000).

Target population

All included studies used self-reported instruments validated among school children. In this review, eight studies used Likert-scale formats, while two studies employed objective multiple-choice questions. Evidence suggests that children as young as eight years can reliably respond to four- or five-point Likert scales (Blasco-Belled et al., 2023). Given that most studies were conducted more than a decade ago, the use of these formats remains appropriate for school children.

Field application for primary school children in Malaysia

In Malaysia, primary school children range from 7 to 12 years of age. The initial plan was to identify suitable instruments for children aged 7–12 years. However, according to Abdullah et al., approximately 90% of students have mastered language skills at the age of 9 only (Abdullah, Mohd Asraf, & Mat Zamin, 2016). In addition, most of the studies included in this review involved children aged 10–12 years. Therefore, the target population was revised to children aged 10–12 years.

Although the C-HLAT-8 was validated in individuals aged 11–17 years old, it provides a useful conceptual basis for adaptation in the younger population. Based on this review, C-HLAT-8 appears to be the potential future applicability after validation in the local context. It demonstrated strong psychometric properties and was validated among the population in the age range of close to the target population. Although the C-HLAT-8 showed a ‘doubtful’ rating for responsiveness according to the COSMIN checklist, other sub-items for the domain have a ‘very good’ rating. Responsiveness is critical for field application; however, evidence on responsiveness was limited or not reported for most of the included instruments. Therefore, the recommendation of C-HLAT-8 was primarily based on its demonstrated strengths in validity, reliability and feasibility rather than responsiveness alone.

These findings align with a previous systematic review by Guo et al. (2018). Furthermore, C-HLAT-8 is considered an appropriate instrument for measuring health literacy in school settings for three reasons: (1) it includes the influence of family and friends; (2) it is concise yet measures functional, critical and interactive health literacy and (3) it is self-administered and time-efficient (Guo et al., 2018).

Strengths and limitations

This review included only studies that focused on the development and validation of instruments. As a result, instruments applied or adapted for children but not formally validated may have been excluded. Additionally, the search was limited to articles published in English, which may have introduced language bias and led to the omission of relevant studies published in other languages.

Nevertheless, this review is among the few that specifically focus on studies conducted in Asian countries, and target children.

To date, no systematic review has focused exclusively on children below 12 years of age. This limits the direct applicability of the findings to primary school populations. Furthermore, the review provides a detailed appraisal of the methodological quality and measurement properties of health literacy instruments, offering valuable insights for future research and practice.

Conclusion

Despite growing global interest in measuring health literacy, research among school children in Asia remains scarce. Nearly 40% of reviewed studies employed instruments with good methodological quality, with more than half exhibiting unreported measurement properties. Future research should prioritise the rigorous assessment of psychometric quality to ensure strong validity and reliability. Among the existing instruments, C-HLAT-8 shows promise and may serve as a candidate measure for future validation among Malaysian primary school populations. This review highlights the need for age-appropriate and culturally relevant instruments to assess health literacy among children. Using validated tools such as the C-HLAT-8 can help clinicians and educators identify children with low health literacy and implement targeted health education strategies to improve health outcomes.
