Spectral embedding through weak* limit of finite-dimensional approximations
Fabrice Nonez

Abstract
. The scope of this text is to study a process that induces another proof of the Spectral Embedding Theorem: that any densely defined symmetric operator can be extended by a multiplication operator through an embedding of the Hilbert space into an L 2 L_{2} space. Furthermore, that process is meant to be used for specific operators, where natural spectral embeddings or equivalences may be found. That process has previously been considered in [ nonez2024spectralequivalencesnonstandardsamplings ] and in [ goldbring2025nonstandardapproachdirectintegral ] , where it has been introduced through nonstandard techniques. Our contribution aims to be the reformulation of the theory through classical analysis arguments, without the use of nonstandard techniques nor ultraproducts.



1.
Overview and motivations

First, we state the central theorem of this paper, which we also call the Spectral embedding Theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(Spectral Theorem for symmetric operators)
.

Suppose that
H
is a separable real or complex Hilbert space, and that
A
is a densely defined symmetric operator on
H
. Then, there exists a compact metric space
\hat{\Omega},
a probability measure
\hat{\mu}
on
\operatorname{Borel}(\hat{\Omega})
, an isometry
\hat{U}:H\rightarrow L_{2}(\hat{\Omega},\hat{\mu})
and a square-integrable function
\hat{m}:\hat{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
generating multiplication operator
\hat{T}
on
L_{2}(\hat{\Omega},\hat{\mu})
such that
\hat{U}\circ A\subset\hat{T}\circ\hat{U}.

Remark 1.2
.

By the Appendix of
[
nonez2024spectralequivalencesnonstandardsamplings
]
(which only uses elementary techniques), if
A
is self-adjoint, it is a corollary that:

•

\hat{U}\circ A=\hat{T}\circ\hat{U}.

•

\hat{U}(H)
reduces for
\hat{T}
, meaning that
\operatorname{proj}_{\hat{U}(H)}\hat{T}=\hat{T}\operatorname{proj}_{\hat{U}(H)}
.

•

for
V\in\operatorname{Borel}(\mathbb{R}),
if
\hat{P}(V)
is the multiplication operator on
L_{2}(\hat{\Omega},\hat{\mu})
induced by
\mathbf{1}_{\hat{m}^{-1}(V)}
and if
P(V)=\hat{U}^{*}\hat{P}(V)\hat{U},
then
P:\operatorname{Borel}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\{\text{ projections on $H$ }\}
is the spectral measure of
A
. This establishes the spectral measure version of the Spectral Theorem.

While the Spectral Theorem is not usually stated for merely symmetric operators instead of self-adjoint, it is already known that any symmetric operators has a self-adjoint extension if we allow the space to be larger, and as such this version is equivalent to the usual formulation for self-adjoint operators.

The aim of this paper lies the description of a ”process”. This process has a set of parameters that is the ”
A
-converging sampling-scale-sequence” (which we define below). Then, it induces resulting objects
\hat{\Omega}
,
\hat{\mu}
,
\hat{U}
and
\hat{m}
that satisfy the Spectral embedding Theorem.

This process was first introduced in
[
nonez2024spectralequivalencesnonstandardsamplings
]
, and then it was adapted in
[
goldbring2025nonstandardapproachdirectintegral
]
to show the direct integral version of the Spectral Theorem. Both of these papers describe this process through nonstandard analysis, and fundamentally rely on concepts such as internal sets and Loeb measures to establish its validity. The goal here is to create a description of it that achieves the same aim as in
[
nonez2024spectralequivalencesnonstandardsamplings
]
, sharing the same underlying idea, while only using tools and ideas of classical functional analysis to do it.

Counterintuitively, we think that this showcases one of the strengths of nonstandard analysis: even if one aims for a classical method, it is often a good strategy to try nonstandard methods first, then try to extract classical methods from them.

There are two main motivations for this process. The first one is to provide an interesting proof of the spectral theorem, which is done by showing there are general parameters that will work for any symmetric densely-defined
A
. Since no complex analysis, resolvent theory or Cayley transform are involved, the proof is the same whether the Hilbert space is real or complex. Instead, the proof relies on the finite-dimensional version of the spectral theorem.

Aside from both papers mentioned above, this paper shares ideas with
[
2
]
, whereas finite dimensional approximations of bounded self-adjoint operators with cyclic vectors are used to construct a suitable measure from their ultraproduct, noting that any self-adjoint bounded operator can split into subspaces with cyclic vectors.

Another proof that uses nonstandard/ultraproduct finite dimensional approximations to prove the Spectral Theorem is found in
[
GOLDBRING2021590
]
, which itself inspired the work done in
[
nonez2024spectralequivalencesnonstandardsamplings
]
, while another, short proof, can be found in
[
matsunaga2024shortnonstandardproofspectral
]
. One difference with all of these papers is that we aim to not use any nonstandard analysis nor ultraproduct machinery (both are conceptually equivalent).

As far as we know, there has not been many recent new proofs of the Spectral Theorem outside of ultraproducts/nonstandard analysis. One we could find is
[
leinfelder2017secondlookageometric
]
, which aimed to revisit and simplify their work in
[
3
]
. One reason we think the proof presented in our paper is an interesting addition to the literature is that it directly results in the suitable multiplication operator, even in cases where the resulting isometry is not surjective, which is certain if
A
has no self-adjoint extensions. This contrasts with the classical method of splitting the Hilbert space into subspaces with cyclic vectors.

The second motivation is to help study specific operators. This is done by tweaking the parameters so that not only they respect the criteria, but they are ”well-suited” for the operator. When done right, the resulting objects can be explicit in their description, and allow precise study and calculations concerning the operator. To observe this phenomenon we consider the shift operator and the differential operator. Object of future research will be to consider other classes of operators.

We note that the work done
[
2
]
and
[
hyttinen2024approximationsfeynmanpathintegrals
]
also aims to apply ultraproducts methods to specific operators, notably ones that come from quantum mechanics. We think that the methods we present here (and in
[
nonez2024spectralequivalencesnonstandardsamplings
]
) can help in this endeavor.

2.
Key ideas and conventions

We present here a (hopefully) intuitive summary of the process presented in this text.

First, we start with the separable Hilbert space
H
, and symmetric operator
A
on
H
. We then consider a sequence, each of the form
(H_{n},A_{n},\Omega_{n})
, where
H_{n}
is some finite-dimensional subspace of
H
,
A_{n}
is some symmetric application on
H_{n}
with fixed orthonormal eigenbasis given by
\Omega_{n}.
We want the the sequence to approximate
A
in some sense.

That sense is
not
that
A_{n}
should converge to
A
in any of the usual operator topologies, which is hard to achieve when
A
is unbounded. Instead, we want
A
to be ”approximable” by the
A_{n}
: whenever
x\in\operatorname{dom}(A),
there exists some sequence
(x_{n}\in H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for which the sequence of pairs
(x_{n},A_{n}x_{n})
converge to
(x,Ax)
.

The idea is that just as the measure of Theorem
1.1
would be on the set of eigenvectors if
A
was decomposable, we want to construct a sequence of measures
\mu_{n}
on each
\Omega_{n}.
We have to do this judiciously, which is why we introduce ”scales” which we define in Section
3
. The problem then becomes that of defining
\hat{\Omega}
, which would be some ”limit” of
\Omega_{n}
.

In
[
nonez2024spectralequivalencesnonstandardsamplings
]
, it was done intrinsically using a nonstandard hull. The natural idea in classical analysis would then be to use Gromov-Hausdorff limit, using convergent subsequence if necessary, but some technical issues made the method less appealing in some cases. Instead, the scale we define induces natural maps
X_{n}:\Omega_{n}\rightarrow Q
, where
Q
is the Hilbert cube. Then,
\hat{\Omega}
can be naturally defined as the set of limit points of
X_{n}(\Omega_{n}).

However, we find that considering
Q
itself as our actual metric space of Theorem
1.1
makes the proof even simpler, as the measure
\hat{\mu}
on
\operatorname{Borel}(Q)
naturally appears as a weak* limit point of the pushforward measures
X_{n}^{\#}(\mu_{n})
. We can then show that
\hat{\mu}
is supported on
\hat{\Omega}
. We note that defining
\hat{\mu}
this way relies on Banach-Alaoglu and Riesz-Markov Theorems.

From there, we find that the scale induces a natural isometry
\hat{U}:H\rightarrow L_{2}(\hat{\Omega},\hat{\mu}).
The rest is then the proof that the required square-integrable
\hat{m}:\hat{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
exists by using Riesz representation theorem for Hilbert space if necessary. One notes that with the further examples, where explicit solutions are desired, the use of subsequences and representation theorems will not be required.

2.1.
Conventions

From now on, we assume that
H
is a separable
\mathbb{K}
-Hilbert space, where
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}.
Furthermore, we assume that
A
is a densely-defined symmetric operator, with no other properties unless stated.

We will note the left-linear inner product of
x
and
y
with
\langle x,y\rangle
, while we note pairs of these elements with
(x,y).
When
\mu
is a measure on some set
\Omega
, and
h:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{K}
is a square-integrable function, we may abuse the notation and also note
h
as its
L_{2}(\Omega,\mu)
-class.

Considering
\overline{\mathbb{D}}=\{z\in\mathbb{K}\;|\;|z|\leq 1\},
the closed unit disk of
\mathbb{K}
, we note the space
Q=\overline{\mathbb{D}}^{\mathbb{N}}
equipped with its usual product topology. Since
Q
is known to be homeomorphic to
[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
no matter the value of
\mathbb{K},
we will refer to
Q
as the Hilbert cube, a known compact metrizable space. We finally note the natural projections
\pi_{j}:Q\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{D}}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}.

3.
Sampling-scale sequences

We now define the main objects concerning our process.

Definition 3.1
.

A sequence of triplets
(H_{n},A_{n},\Omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is called a
sampling-sequence
for
A
if for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(1)

H_{n}<H
and
\operatorname{dim}(H_{n})\in\mathbb{N}
;

(2)

A_{n}:H_{n}\rightarrow H_{n}
is a symmetric linear operator;

(3)

\Omega_{n}
is an orthonormal eigenbasis of
A_{n}
,

and if

(4)

for any
x\in\operatorname{dom}(A)
, there exists a sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that each
x_{n}\in H_{n}
,
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}x_{n}=x
and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}A_{n}x_{n}=Ax.

Proposition 3.2
.

There exists a sampling sequence for
A
.

Proof.

Since the graph
G(A)\subset H\times H
is a separable space, let
(g_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
\operatorname{dom}(A)
such that the sequence
(g_{k},Ag_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is dense in
G(A)
. Then, for
n\in\mathbb{N},
let
H_{n}=\operatorname{span}(\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}),
a finite dimensional subspace of
\operatorname{dom}(A)\subset H
, and let
A_{n}=(\operatorname{proj}_{H_{n}}\circ A)|_{H_{n}}:H_{n}\rightarrow H_{n}.
It is clear that
A_{n}
is a linear operator on
H_{n}
, and symmetry holds since

\langle A_{n}x,y\rangle=\langle\operatorname{proj}_{H_{n}}Ax,y\rangle=\langle Ax,y\rangle=\langle x,Ay\rangle=\langle x,\operatorname{proj}_{H_{n}}Ay\rangle=\langle x,A_{n}y\rangle

holds for any
x,y\in H_{n}.
Thus, by the finite dimensional version of the Spectral Theorem, let
\Omega_{n}
be some orthonormal eigenbasis of
A_{n}
. We show that
(H_{n},A_{n},\Omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
forms a sampling sequence for
A
. We already know that Properties (
1
), (
2
) and (
3
) hold, so we only need to show (
4
).

We note that
\{g_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is dense in
H
. Indeed, we have that for
x\in\operatorname{dom}(A)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\|x-g_{k}\|\leq\|(x,Ax)-(g_{k},Ag_{k})\|,
and as such
\{g_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is dense in
\operatorname{dom}(A)
, which itself is dense in
H
.

Let
x\in\operatorname{dom}(A).
Then, for
n\in\mathbb{N},
let
x_{n}\in H_{n}
be the element such that
(x_{n},A_{n}x_{n})=\operatorname{proj}_{G(A_{n})}(x,Ax).
We show that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(x_{n},A_{n}x_{n})=(x,Ax).
Let
\epsilon>0
, and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\|(g_{k},Ag_{k})-(x,Ax)\|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}.
Then, let
l\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\|Ag_{k}-g_{l}\|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}.
For any
n\geq l
, we have, since
g_{l}\in H_{n},

\|Ag_{k}-\operatorname{proj}_{H_{n}}Ag_{k}\|\leq\|Ag_{k}-g_{l}\|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Therefore, if
N=\max\{l,k\}
, we have that for any
n>N
,

\displaystyle\|(x,Ax)-(x_{n},A_{n}x_{n})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|(x,Ax)-(g_{k},A_{n}g_{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|(x,Ax)-(g_{k},Ag_{k})\|+\|(g_{k},Ag_{k})-(g_{k},A_{n}g_{k})\|

\displaystyle=\|(x,Ax)-(g_{k},Ag_{k})\|+\|Ag_{k}-\operatorname{proj}_{H_{n}}Ag_{k}\|<\epsilon.

Thus,
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(x_{n},A_{n}x_{n})=(x,Ax).
Since
x\in\operatorname{dom}(A)
is arbitrary, we conclude that Property (
4
) holds, and
(H_{n},A_{n},\Omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
forms a sampling sequence for
A
.
∎

Next, we need another relatively peculiar object, the low-dimensional-biased scale.

Definition 3.3
.

The sequence of finite sequences of pairs
((e_{j}^{(n)},c_{j}^{(n)})_{j=1}^{N_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is called a
low-dimensional-biased scale
if, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(5)

N_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
, with
e_{j}^{(n)}\in H\setminus\{0\}
and
c_{j}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
for any
j\leq N_{n}
;

(6)

\sum_{j=1}^{N_{n}}c_{j}^{(n)}\|e_{j}^{(n)}\|^{2}=1
,

and if

(7)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}N_{n}=\infty
;

(8)

for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
we have
e_{j}:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}e_{j}^{(n)}
exists in
H\setminus\{0\}
and
c_{j}:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}c_{j}^{(n)}
exists in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
;

(9)

\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is dense-spanning in
H
;

(10)

\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}c_{j}\|e_{j}\|^{2}=1.

The idea of such scales is that they will allow to construct suitable measures on each
\Omega_{n}
, as well as metric structures on the
\Omega_{n}
that allow interactions between each other. Thus, we will need a few more compatibility conditions.

Definition 3.4
.

The sequence
S=(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=(H_{n},A_{n},\Omega_{n},(e_{j}^{(n)},c_{j}^{(n)})_{j=1}^{N_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is called an
A
-sampling-scale
sequence if
(H_{n},A_{n},\Omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
forms a sampling sequence for
A
while
((e_{j}^{(n)},c_{j}^{(n)})_{j=1}^{N_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
forms a low-dimensional-biased scale, and if

(11)

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
j\leq N_{n},

e_{j}^{(n)}\in H_{n}
;

(12)

there exists
C\in\mathbb{R}
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\sum_{j=1}^{N_{n}}c_{j}^{(n)}\|A_{n}e_{j}^{(n)}\|^{2}<C
;

(13)

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in\Omega_{n},
there exists
j\leq N_{n}
such that
\langle e_{j}^{(n)},f\rangle\neq 0.

Finally, the
A
-sampling-scale sequence is called
strong
if

(14)

For any
j\in\mathbb{N},
the sequence
(A_{n}e_{j}^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges.

Remark 3.5
.

One can show directly that for any
A
-sampling-scale sequence,
e_{j}\in\operatorname{dom}(A^{*})
and that
A^{*}e_{j}
is the weak limit of
(A_{n}e_{j}^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, hence the term ”strong” for Property (
14
). We will not use that here, and so we omit the proof.

We end this section by showing that not only do
A
-sampling-scale sequences exist, but that a suitable scale exists given any sampling sequence.

Proposition 3.6
.

For any sampling sequence
(H_{n},A_{n},\Omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for
A
, there exists a scale
((e_{j}^{(n)},c_{j}^{(n)})_{j=1}^{N_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=(H_{n},A_{n},\Omega_{n},(e_{j}^{(n)},c_{j}^{(n)})_{j=1}^{N_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a strong
A
-sampling-scale sequence.

Proof.

Let
(H_{n},A_{n},\Omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be an arbitrary sampling sequence for
A
.

Let
(g_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be a Hilbert basis of
H
such that for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
,
g_{j}\in\operatorname{dom}(A)
. It exists, since
H
is separable and
\operatorname{dom}(A)
is dense. Given
j\in\mathbb{N}
, let
(g_{j}^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence given by Property (
4
) of Definition
3.1
used on
g_{j}
. As such,
g_{j}^{(n)}\in H_{n}
,
g_{j}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{j}^{(n)}
and
Ag_{j}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}A_{n}g_{j}^{(n)}
. Since
\dim(H_{n})>0
for each
n
, and since
g_{j}\neq 0
for each
j
, we can assume without loss of generality that
g_{j}^{(n)}
is always nonzero.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
N_{n}:=n+1
. For
j\in[N_{n}]
, let

e_{j}^{(n)}=\begin{cases}g_{j}^{(n)}\quad\text{ if }j\leq n\\
\sum_{f\in\Omega_{n}}f\quad\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

Furthermore, for such
j
, let

\displaystyle a_{j}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{j}\max\left(\|e_{j}^{(n)}\|^{2},\|A_{n}e_{j}^{(n)}\|^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle c_{j}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{l=1}^{N_{n}}a_{l}^{(n)}\|e_{l}^{(n)}\|^{2}}a_{j}^{(n)}.

We first establish that
((e_{j}^{(n)},c_{j}^{(n)})_{j=1}^{N_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a low-dimensional-biased scale. Properties (
5
), (
6
) and (
7
) are directly verified. Furthermore, it is clear that for
j\in\mathbb{N},

\displaystyle e_{j}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}e_{j}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{j}^{(n)}=g_{j},

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}A_{n}e_{j}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}A_{n}g_{j}^{(n)}=Ag_{j},

and that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}a_{j}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{2^{j}\max(1,\|Ag_{j}\|^{2})}=:a_{j}.
Since
0<a_{j}^{(n)}\|e_{j}^{(n)}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2^{j}}
, we use the Dominated Convergence Theorem to conclude that

c_{j}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}c_{j}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{\sum_{l\in\mathbb{N}}a_{l}\|g_{l}\|^{2}}a_{j}>0.

Thus, Property (
8
) holds.
e_{j}=g_{j}
ensures that Property (
9
) holds, while Property (
10
) follows from that last equality. Thus,
((e_{j}^{(n)},c_{j}^{(n)})_{j=1}^{N_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a low-dimensional biased scale.

We now show that
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a strong
A
-sampling-scale sequence. Property (
11
) follows from the definition of
e_{j}^{(n)}
and Property (
13
) follows from the fact that for any
f\in\Omega_{n},

\langle e_{j}^{n+1},f\rangle=1.
Finally, we have that for any
n\in\mathbb{N},

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N_{n}}c_{j}^{(n)}\|A_{n}e_{j}^{(n)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{l=1}^{N_{n}}a_{l}^{(n)}\|e_{l}^{(n)}\|^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{n}}a_{j}^{(n)}\|A_{n}e_{j}^{(n)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{a_{1}^{(n)}\|e_{1}^{(n)}\|^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{n}}\frac{1}{2^{j}}<\frac{1}{a_{1}^{(n)}\|e_{1}^{(n)}\|^{2}}.

That last sequence converges to
\frac{1}{a_{1}\|e_{1}\|^{2}}
and as such is bounded above, thus Property (
12
) holds. Therefore,
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is an
A
-sampling-scale sequence. Since we have already established that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}A_{n}e_{j}^{(n)}=Ag_{j}
for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we conclude that Property (
14
) holds and
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is strong.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

Given
S=(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, an
A
-sampling-scale sequence, it is direct to show that any subsequence
S^{\prime}=(S_{n_{m}})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is also an
A
-sampling-scale sequence.

4.
The finite dimensional isometries

We now fix the (not necessarily strong)
A
-sampling-scale sequence

S=(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=(H_{n},A_{n},\Omega_{n},(e_{j}^{(n)},c_{j}^{(n)})_{j=1}^{N_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}.

We also denote the eigenvalue function of
A_{n}
by
\lambda_{n}:\Omega_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.

Definition 4.1
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mu_{n}:\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with

\mu_{n}(V)=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{n}}c_{j}^{(n)}\|\operatorname{proj}_{\operatorname{span}(V)}e_{j}^{(n)}\|^{2}=\sum_{f\in V}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{n}}c_{j}^{(n)}|\langle e_{j}^{(n)},f\rangle|^{2}.

Remark 4.2
.

For
f\in\Omega_{n}
, we also note
\mu_{n}(f):=\mu_{n}(\{f\})=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{n}}c_{j}^{(n)}|\langle e_{j}^{(n)},f\rangle|^{2}.

The following is a direct result from Properties (
6
) and (
13
).

Proposition 4.3
.

We have that
\mu_{n}
is a probability measure on
\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{n})
. Furthermore,
\mu_{n}(f)>0
holds for any
f\in\Omega_{n}
.

Thus, as vector spaces, we have that
L_{2}(\Omega_{n},\mu_{n})=\mathbb{K}^{\Omega_{n}}
. This allows us to define the following unitary maps.

Definition 4.4
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N},
let
U_{n}:H_{n}\rightarrow L_{2}(\Omega_{n},\mu_{n})
be given by

(U_{n}(x))(f)=\frac{\langle x,f\rangle}{\sqrt{\mu_{n}(f)}}.

Proposition 4.5
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
U_{n}
is a unitary map. Furthermore, for any given
x\in H_{n}
, we have
\lambda_{n}\cdot U_{n}(x)=U_{n}(A_{n}x).
Finally,
|(U_{n}(e_{j}^{(n)}))(f)|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{c_{j}^{(n)}}
whenever
j\leq N_{n}
and
f\in\Omega_{n}.

Proof.

Linearity of
U_{n}
is a direct result from its definition. Furthermore, for any
x\in H_{n}
,

\displaystyle\|U_{n}(x)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{n}}|U_{n}(x)|^{2}d\mu_{n}=\sum_{f\in\Omega_{n}}\left|\frac{\langle x,f\rangle}{\sqrt{\mu_{n}(f)}}\right|^{2}\mu_{n}(f)=\sum_{f\in\Omega_{n}}|\langle x,f\rangle|^{2}=\|x\|^{2}.

Thus, as a norm-preserving linear map,
U_{n}
is an isometry. Since
\dim(H_{n})=|\Omega_{n}|,

U_{n}
is unitary.

For the second part, let
x\in H_{n}.
We note, for any
f\in\Omega_{n}
, that

(\lambda_{n}\cdot U_{n}(x))(f)=\lambda_{n}(f)\frac{\langle x,f\rangle}{\sqrt{\mu_{n}(f)}}=\frac{\langle x,A_{n}f\rangle}{\sqrt{\mu_{n}(f)}}=\frac{\langle A_{n}x,f\rangle}{\sqrt{\mu_{n}(f)}}=(U_{n}(A_{n}x))(f).

Thus,
\lambda_{n}\cdot U_{n}(x)=U_{n}(A_{n}x).
Finally, let
j\leq N_{n},
and let
f\in\Omega_{n}.
We have

\displaystyle|(U_{n}(e_{j}^{(n)}))(f)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c_{j}^{(n)}}\frac{c_{j}^{(n)}|\langle e_{j}^{(n)},f\rangle|^{2}}{\mu_{n}(f)}\leq\frac{1}{c_{j}^{(n)}},

proving the inequality.
∎

We can now define our sequence of embeddings, as described in Section
2
.

Definition 4.6
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N},
let
X_{n}:\Omega_{n}\rightarrow Q
be defined by

\pi_{j}\circ X_{n}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{c_{j}^{(n)}}U_{n}(e_{j}^{(n)})\quad\text{ if $j\leq N_{n}$ }\\
0\quad\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

for
j\in\mathbb{N}.

We can now define the limit space
\hat{\Omega}
as well.

Definition 4.7
.

We define
\hat{\Omega}\subset Q
with

\hat{\Omega}=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\overline{\bigcup_{m=n}^{\infty}X_{m}(\Omega_{m})}.

In other words,
x\in\hat{\Omega}
if and only if there exists a subsequence
(\Omega_{n_{m}})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
and a sequence
(f_{m}\in\Omega_{n_{m}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
x=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}X_{n_{m}}(f_{m}).

Remark 4.8
.

It is clear that
\hat{\Omega}
is a compact subset of
Q
. If
X_{n}(\Omega_{n})
converges under Hausdorff metric, then
\hat{\Omega}
is the limit. In fact,
\hat{\Omega}
is the union of all Hausdorff limit points of
(X_{n}(\Omega_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}.

We now define the notion of convergence for sampling-scale sequences.

Definition 4.9
.

We say that the
A
-sampling-scale sequence
S
is
convergent
if for any continuous
h:Q\rightarrow\mathbb{K},
the sequence

\left(\int_{\Omega_{n}}h\circ X_{n}d\mu_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

converges.

Remark 4.10
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N},
let
\hat{\mu}_{n}=X_{n}^{\#}(\mu_{n})
be the push-forward probability measure of
\mu_{n}
on
\operatorname{Borel}(Q)
through
X_{n}
. Then,
S
is convergent if and only if
(\hat{\mu}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is weak* convergent in
C(Q)^{\prime}
.

Proposition 4.11
.

Any
A
-sampling-scale sequence
S=(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has a convergent subsequence
S^{\prime}=(S_{n_{m}})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
. In particular, there always exists a convergent
A
-sampling-scale sequence.

Proof.

Since
Q
is a compact metrizable space,
C(Q)
is a separable Banach space. By Alaoglu’s Theorem, the unit ball of
C(Q)^{\prime}
is a metrizable compact space under weak* topology.

For
n\in\mathbb{N},
we define
\hat{\mu}_{n}
as per Remark
4.10
. Then, since each
\hat{\mu}_{n}
is a probability measure, we have that
\|\hat{\mu}_{n}\|=1
in
C(\hat{\Omega})^{\prime}
. Therefore, there exists a weak* convergent subsequence
(\hat{\mu}_{n_{m}})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
. Using Remark
4.10
, we conclude that
S^{\prime}=(S_{n_{m}})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is convergent.
∎

5.
Resulting probability measure

We now assume that the fixed
A
-sampling-scale sequence

S=(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=(H_{n},A_{n},\Omega_{n},(e_{j}^{(n)},c_{j}^{(n)})_{j=1}^{N_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

is convergent. We now define the measure
\hat{\mu}
on
\operatorname{Borel}(Q)
using the following, which is a consequence of the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem.

Proposition 5.1
.

There exists a unique probability measure
\hat{\mu}:\operatorname{Borel}(Q)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that for any continuous function
h:Q\rightarrow\mathbb{K}
,

\int_{Q}hd\hat{\mu}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega_{n}}(h\circ X_{n})d\mu_{n}.

In general, one cannot calculate
\hat{\mu}(V)
directly as the limit of
\hat{\mu}_{n}(V)=\mu_{n}(X_{n}^{-1}(V))
, as
\hat{\mu}
can have a disjoint support from all the finite measures. This is why the use of continuous functions was required to define convergence. The next proposition, however, is useful for such direct calculations of
\hat{\mu}
using values of
\mu_{n}
.

Proposition 5.2
.

If
V
is an open subset of
Q
, then
\hat{\mu}(V)\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{n}(X_{n}^{-1}(V)).

Proof.

Let
V
be an open subset of
Q
. Since
Q
is a compact metric space and
\hat{\mu}
is a probability measure on its Borel sets, we have that
\hat{\mu}
is Radon. Thus, we know
\hat{\mu}(V)=\sup(\{\hat{\mu}(K)\;|\;K\text{ is a compact subset of $V$ }\}).
However, since
Q
is a metric space, for any compact subset
K
of
V
, there exists a continuous
h:Q\rightarrow[0,1]
supported in
V
such that
h|_{K}=1
, thus
\hat{\mu}(K)\leq\int_{Q}hd\hat{\mu}\leq\hat{\mu}(V).
Therefore, we have

\hat{\mu}(V)=\sup(\{\int_{Q}hd\hat{\mu}\;|\;h:Q\rightarrow[0,1]\text{ is continuous and supported in $V$ }\}).

For any such continuous
h
supported in
V
with values in
[0,1]
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{Q}hd\hat{\mu}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega_{n}}(h\circ X_{n})d\mu_{n}\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega_{n}}(\mathbf{1}_{V}\circ X_{n})d\mu_{n}=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{n}(X_{n}^{-1}(V)).

Thus, by definition of
\sup
,
\hat{\mu}(V)\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{n}(X_{n}^{-1}(V)).

∎

Corollary 5.3
.

We have that
\hat{\mu}
is supported on
\hat{\Omega}
. In other words,
\hat{\mu}(Q\setminus\hat{\Omega})=0.

Proof.

Since
\hat{\Omega}=\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\overline{\bigcup_{m=k}^{\infty}X_{m}(\Omega_{m})},
it is sufficient to establish that for any
k
,
\hat{\mu}(Q\setminus\overline{\bigcup_{m=k}^{\infty}X_{m}(\Omega_{m})})=0.
We have, for any
n\geq k,
that
X_{n}^{-1}\left(Q\setminus\overline{\bigcup_{m=k}^{\infty}X_{m}(\Omega_{m})}\right)
is empty, thus
\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{n}\left(X_{n}^{-1}\left(Q\setminus\overline{\bigcup_{m=k}^{\infty}X_{m}(\Omega_{m})}\right)\right)=0.

Since
Q\setminus\overline{\bigcup_{m=k}^{\infty}X_{m}(\Omega_{m})}
is open, we have
\hat{\mu}\left(X_{n}^{-1}\left(Q\setminus\overline{\bigcup_{m=k}^{\infty}X_{m}(\Omega_{m})}\right)\right)=0
from Proposition
5.2
.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

Corollary
5.3
allows us to identify
L_{2}(\hat{\Omega},\hat{\mu})=L_{2}(Q,\hat{\mu}),
with all relevant objects of Theorem
1.1
restricting naturally on
\hat{\Omega}
as long as they have been suitably defined on
Q
. However, it remains useful to work in
Q
to first establish such definitions.

We end this section with another consequence of Proposition
5.2
, which we will use in the next section, after having proven the following lemma.

Lemma 5.5
.

For any
\epsilon>0,
there exists
J_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N},

\sum_{J_{\epsilon}<j\leq N_{n}}c_{j}^{(n)}\|e_{j}^{(n)}\|^{2}<\epsilon.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon>0.
By Property (
10
), let
J_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\sum_{j=1}^{J_{0}}c_{j}\|e_{j}\|^{2}>1-\frac{\epsilon}{2}.
Then, using Properties (
7
) and (
8
) on each
j\leq J_{0}
, let
K\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
n>K
, we have
N_{n}\geq J_{0}
and
c_{j}^{(n)}\|e_{j}^{(n)}\|^{2}>c_{j}\|e_{j}\|^{2}-\frac{\epsilon}{2J_{0}}
whenever
j\leq J_{0}.
For any such
n
, we then have
\sum_{j=1}^{J_{0}}c_{j}^{(n)}\|e_{j}^{(n)}\|^{2}>1-\epsilon,
thus
\sum_{j=J_{0}+1}^{N_{n}}c_{j}^{(n)}\|e_{j}^{(n)}\|^{2}<\epsilon
by Property (
6
). Let
J_{\epsilon}=\max(\{N_{n}\}_{n=1}^{K}\cup\{J_{0}\}).
Then, for any
n\in\mathbb{N},
we have

\sum_{J_{\epsilon}<j\leq N_{n}}c_{j}^{(n)}\|e_{j}^{(n)}\|^{2}\leq\begin{cases}\sum_{j=J_{0}+1}^{N_{n}}c_{j}^{(n)}\|e_{j}^{(n)}\|^{2}&<\epsilon\quad\text{ if $n>K$ }\\
0&<\epsilon\quad\text{ otherwise, }\end{cases}

completing the proof.
∎

Proposition 5.6
.

Given
O=(0)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in Q,

\hat{\mu}(\{O\})=0.

Proof.

For
k,l\in\mathbb{N},
let
E_{k,l}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{l}\pi_{j}^{-1}(D_{\frac{1}{k}})\subset Q,
where
D_{\frac{1}{k}}
is the open disk of radius
\frac{1}{k}
in
\mathbb{K}
centered at
0
. By definition of the product topology, we have that
E_{k,l}
is an open subset of
Q
containing
O
for any
k,l
. Thus, using proposition
5.2
, we always have
\hat{\mu}(\{O\})\leq\hat{\mu}(E_{k,l})\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{n}(X_{n}^{-1}(E_{k,l})).

Furthermore, upon inspection, we have that for any such
k,l\in\mathbb{N},

X_{n}^{-1}(E_{k,l})=\bigcap_{j=1}^{l}(\pi_{j}\circ X_{n})^{-1}(D_{\frac{1}{k}})=\bigcap_{j=1}^{\min(l,N_{n})}U_{n}(e_{j}^{(n)})^{-1}(D_{\frac{1}{k\sqrt{c_{j}^{(n)}}}}),

and so
|\langle e_{j}^{(n)},f\rangle|^{2}<\frac{\mu_{n}(f)}{k^{2}c_{j}^{(n)}}
whenever
f\in X_{n}^{-1}(E_{k,l})
and
j\leq\min(l,N_{n}).

Let
\epsilon>0
, and per Lemma
5.5
let
J_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\sum_{J_{\epsilon}<j\leq N_{n}}c_{j}^{(n)}\|e_{j}^{(n)}\|^{2}<\epsilon
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}.
We have, for any
n
such that
N_{n}>J_{\epsilon}
(which is guaranteed for large enough
n
by Property (
7
) of Definition
3.3
) and any
k\in\mathbb{N}
:

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(X_{n}^{-1}(E_{k,J_{\epsilon}}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{n}}c_{j}^{(n)}\sum_{f\in X_{n}^{-1}(E_{k,J_{\epsilon}})}|\langle e_{j}^{(n)},f\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{J_{\epsilon}}c_{j}^{(n)}\sum_{f\in X_{n}^{-1}(E_{k,J_{\epsilon}})}|\langle e_{j}^{(n)},f\rangle|^{2}+\sum_{J_{\epsilon}<j\leq N_{n}}c_{j}^{(n)}\|e_{j}^{(n)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\sum_{j=1}^{J_{\epsilon}}c_{j}^{(n)}\sum_{f\in X_{n}^{-1}(E_{k,J_{\epsilon}})}|\langle e_{j}^{(n)},f\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\sum_{j=1}^{J_{\epsilon}}c_{j}^{(n)}\sum_{f\in X_{n}^{-1}(E_{k,J_{\epsilon}})}\frac{1}{k^{2}c_{j}^{(n)}}\mu_{n}(f)

\displaystyle=\epsilon+\frac{J_{\epsilon}}{k^{2}}\mu_{n}(X_{n}^{-1}(E_{k,J_{\epsilon}}))\leq\epsilon+\frac{J_{\epsilon}}{k^{2}}.

Thus, we have that
\hat{\mu}(\{O\})\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{n}(X_{n}^{-1}(E_{k,J_{\epsilon}}))\leq\epsilon+\frac{J_{\epsilon}}{k^{2}}.
Since
k
is arbitrary, we have that
\hat{\mu}(\{O\})\leq\epsilon.
Since
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary, we have that
\hat{\mu}(\{O\})=0.

∎

6.
Induced Isometry

As with the previous section, we assume that the fixed
A
-sampling-scale sequence

S=(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=(H_{n},A_{n},\Omega_{n},(e_{j}^{(n)},c_{j}^{(n)})_{j=1}^{N_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

is convergent, with the probability measure
\hat{\mu}
on
\operatorname{Borel}(Q)
defined the same way. The goal of this section is to introduce a suitable isometry
\hat{U}:H\rightarrow L_{2}(Q,\hat{\mu})
and real-valued function
\hat{m}
satisfying Theorem
1.1
, completing the process.

We now define an important sequence of continuous functions on
Q
.

Definition 6.1
.

For
j\in\mathbb{N},
let
V_{j}:Q\rightarrow\mathbb{K}
be given by
V_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{j}}}\pi_{j}.

Remark 6.2
.

It is clear that for each
j
,
V_{j}
is continuous on
Q
. Furthermore, as
Q
is compact and
\hat{\mu}
is a probability measure, it is also clear that
V_{j}
is square integrable. Finally, we note that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
j\leq N_{n}
,
V_{j}\circ X_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{c_{j}^{(n)}}{c_{j}}}U_{n}(e_{j}^{(n)}).

We can now define the natural isometry
\hat{U}:H\rightarrow L_{2}(Q,\hat{\mu})
as well, which is done using the following proposition.

Proposition 6.3
.

There is a unique linear isometry
\hat{U}:H\rightarrow L_{2}(Q,\hat{\mu})
such that for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
,
\hat{U}(e_{j})=V_{j}.

Proof.

The uniqueness of such an isometry holds by density of
\operatorname{span}(\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}})
, which is established by Property (
9
) of Definition
3.3
. Therefore, we only need to show the existence of such
\hat{U}
.

The most important part is proving that for any
j,l\in\mathbb{N},

\langle V_{j},V_{l}\rangle=\langle e_{j},e_{l}\rangle.
We calculate that for such
j
and
l
, since
V_{j}\overline{V_{l}}
is continuous on
Q
:

\displaystyle\langle V_{j},V_{l}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{Q}V_{j}\overline{V_{l}}d\hat{\mu}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega_{n}}\left(V_{j}\overline{V_{l}}\right)\circ X_{n}d\mu_{n}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{\frac{c_{j}^{(n)}}{c_{j}}\frac{c_{l}^{(n)}}{c_{l}}}\int_{\Omega_{n}}U_{n}\left(e_{j}^{(n)}\right)\overline{U_{n}\left(e_{l}^{(n)}\right)}d\mu_{n}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle U_{n}\left(e_{j}^{(n)}\right),U_{n}\left(e_{l}^{(n)}\right)\right\rangle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\langle e_{j}^{(n)},e_{l}^{(n)}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle e_{j},e_{l}\rangle.

The last equality, as well as the fourth, are due to Property (
8
) of Definition
3.3
. Thus, we have that for any
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in\mathbb{K},

\|\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}e_{j}\|^{2}=\|\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}V_{j}\|^{2}.
Therefore, there is a well-defined linear isometry
\hat{U}:\operatorname{span}(\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}})\rightarrow L_{2}(Q,\hat{\mu})
such that
\hat{U}(\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}e_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}V_{j}
, which extends to
\overline{\operatorname{span}(\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}})}=H
.
∎

To complete the intended process, we now only need to define the suitable
\hat{m}
, which is done through the following theorem.

Theorem 6.1
.

There exists a unique real-valued
\hat{m}\in L_{2}(Q,\hat{\mu})
such that for any
x\in\operatorname{dom}(A)
,
\hat{U}(Ax)=\hat{m}\cdot\hat{U}(x)
as elements of
L_{2}(Q,\hat{\mu}).

The rest of this section will be dedicated to proving this theorem. First, we need a few tools.

We want to be able to construct
\hat{m}
as the ”limit” of the eigenvalue functions
\lambda_{n}
, and do it in such a way that
\lambda_{n}\cdot U_{n}(x_{n})
”converges” to
\hat{m}\cdot\hat{U}(x)
whenever
x_{n}\in H_{n}
converges to
x\in\operatorname{dom}(A)
, with
U_{n}(x_{n})
itself ”converging” to
\hat{U}(x).
To hope to have that, we need to define a proper convergence notion.

Definition 6.4
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N},
let
\Phi_{n}:L_{2}(\Omega_{n},\mu_{n})\rightarrow C(Q)^{\prime}
, with

(\Phi_{n}(g_{n}))(h)=\int_{\Omega_{n}}g_{n}(h\circ X_{n})d\mu_{n}.

Furthermore, we say that a bounded sequence
(g_{n}\in L_{2}(\Omega_{n},\mu_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

S
-converges if
(\Phi_{n}(g_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in
C(Q)^{\prime}
in the weak* topology. In other words,
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

S
-converges if the
L_{2}
norms are uniformly bounded, and if
\left(\int_{\Omega_{n}}g_{n}(h\circ X_{n})d\mu_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges for any
h\in C(Q).

Proposition 6.5
.

For any
S
-convergent sequence
(g_{n}\in L_{2}(\Omega_{n},\mu_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a unique
g\in L_{2}(Q,\hat{\mu})
such that for any
h\in C(Q),

\int_{Q}hgd\hat{\mu}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(\Phi_{n}(g_{n}))(h).
Furthermore,
\|g\|\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|g_{n}\|
. Finally, if each
g_{n}
is real-valued, so is
g
.

Proof.

Let
\iota:C(Q)\rightarrow L_{2}(Q,\hat{\mu})
be the natural inclusion, which has dense image. Furthermore, let
M=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|g_{n}\|\in\mathbb{R}.
We want to define
\Phi:\iota(C(Q))\rightarrow\mathbb{K}
with
\Phi(\iota(h))=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(\Phi_{n}(g_{n}))(h).
To show that such a definition is possible, we note, for any
h\in C(Q),

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega_{n}}g_{n}(h\circ X_{n})d\mu_{n}\right|^{2}\leq\int_{\Omega_{n}}|g_{n}|^{2}d\mu_{n}\int_{\Omega_{n}}|h\circ X_{n}|^{2}d\mu_{n}=\|g_{n}\|^{2}\int_{\Omega_{n}}|h|^{2}\circ X_{n}d\mu_{n}.

Therefore,
|\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(\Phi_{n}(g_{n}))(h)|^{2}\leq M^{2}\int_{Q}|h|^{2}d\hat{\mu}=M\|\iota(h)\|^{2},
and so we have that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(\Phi_{n}(g_{n}))(h)=0
whenever
\iota(h)=0.
From that, we conclude that
\Phi
is indeed a well-defined bounded linear relation on
\iota(C(Q))
with
\|\Phi\|\leq M.
Since
\iota(C(Q))
is dense in
L_{2}(Q,\hat{\mu}),
Riesz representation theorem for Hilbert space ensures that there is a unique
g\in L_{2}(Q,\hat{\mu})
such that for any
h\in C(Q),

\Phi(\iota(h))=\langle\iota(h),\overline{g}\rangle
, thus

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(\Phi_{n}(g_{n}))(h)=\int_{Q}hgd\hat{\mu}.

The theorem further ensures that
\|g\|=\|\overline{g}\|=\|\Phi\|\leq M.

Finally, if each
g_{n}
is real valued, then for any
h\in C(Q)
, we have
\overline{h}
is also continuous, and

\displaystyle\langle\iota(h),\overline{g}\rangle

\displaystyle=\overline{\langle\iota(\overline{h}),g\rangle}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\overline{\int_{\Omega_{n}}g_{n}(\overline{h}\circ X_{n})d\mu_{n}}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(\Phi_{n}(g_{n}))(h)=\langle\iota(h),g\rangle.

Thus, by density of
\iota(C(Q))
,
g=\Re(g)
is real-valued.
∎

Definition 6.6
.

If
(g_{n}\in L_{2}(\Omega_{n},\mu_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

S
-converges, we say that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}^{(S)}g_{n}\in L_{2}(Q,\hat{\mu})
is the unique element given by the previous proposition.

Remark 6.7
.

By definition of convergence of
A
-sampling-scale sequence, we know that
\lim^{(S)}_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{n}}=1_{Q}.
In fact, for any
h_{0}\in C(Q)
,
h_{0}=\lim^{(S)}_{n\rightarrow\infty}h_{0}\circ X_{n},
noting that
\int_{\Omega_{n}}|h_{0}\circ X_{n}|^{2}d\mu_{n}\rightarrow\int_{Q}|h_{0}|^{2}d\hat{\mu},
ensuring the sequence
(h_{0}\circ X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded.

We can now state our suitable condition, that if
(x_{n}\in H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence for which
x=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}x_{n}
in
H
, then
\hat{U}(x)=\lim^{(S)}_{n\rightarrow\infty}U_{n}(x_{n}).
Since we are going to work with
\lambda_{n}\cdot U_{n}(x_{n}),
we will say a bit more.

Proposition 6.8
.

Suppose
(g_{n}\in L_{2}(\Omega_{n},\mu_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
S
-convergent sequence with
g=\lim^{(S)}_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{n},
and that
(x_{n}\in H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
x\in H.
Suppose further that
(g_{n}\cdot U_{n}(x_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded (as respective elements of
L_{2}(\Omega_{n},\mu_{n})
). Then, we have
g\cdot\hat{U}(x)\in L_{2}(Q,\hat{\mu}),
and
g\cdot\hat{U}(x)=\lim^{(S)}_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{n}\cdot U_{n}(x_{n}).

Proof.

For this proof, since norms may present some ambiguity, we will use
\|\;\|_{0}
to denote the
\sup
norm on bounded functions, while
\|\;\|_{\mu}
will denote the
L_{2}
norm with respect to measure
\mu
.

We first show that
\Phi_{n}(g_{n}\cdot U_{n}(x_{n}))
weak* converges, with

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(\Phi_{n}(g_{n}\cdot U_{n}(x_{n})))(h)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega_{n}}(h\circ X_{n})g_{n}\cdot U_{n}(x_{n})d\mu_{n}=\int_{Q}hg\cdot\hat{U}(x)d\hat{\mu}

for any
h\in C(Q)
. We now fix such
h
. Also, we fix
M\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\|g_{n}\|_{\mu_{n}}\leq M
for all
n
. We note that by Proposition
6.5
,
\|g\|_{\hat{\mu}}\leq M
as well.

Let
\epsilon>0
. By Property
9
of Definition
3.3
, let
\{a_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}\subset\mathbb{K}
with
\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}e_{j}-x\right\|<\frac{\epsilon}{4M\|h\|_{0}+1}
. Then, let
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
n>N
,
N_{n}>k
and:

•

\left|\int_{Q}g\left(h\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}V_{j}\right)d\hat{\mu}-\int_{\Omega_{n}}g_{n}\cdot\left(h\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}V_{j}\right)\circ X_{n}d\mu_{n}\right|<\frac{\epsilon}{4}
;

•

\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}e_{j}^{(n)}-x_{n}\right\|<\frac{\epsilon}{4M\|h\|_{0}+1}
;

•

for each
1\leq j\leq k

\int_{\Omega_{n}}(h\circ X_{n})d\mu_{n}=\int_{[0,1]}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{2n}h(X_{n}(f_{k}^{(n)}))\mathbf{1}_{[\frac{k}{2n+1},\frac{k+1}{2n+1})}\right)d\mu.

Since the sequence of functions in that last integral is bounded and pointwise convergent to
h\circ X
on
[0,1),
we conclude from the Dominated Convergence Theorem that
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is convergent, with
\hat{\mu}=X_{\#}(\mu),
the pushforward measure.

•

We can then calculate that for any

and

(V_{j}\circ X)(t)=e^{2\pi il_{j}\cdot t},
and so for any

(U(g_{l}))(t)=e^{2\pi ilt}.
That

•

We calculate that
\lambda_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\left(U_{n}(e_{2}^{(n)})+U_{n}(e_{3}^{(n)})\right).
Thus, it follows from Proposition
6.8
and Remark

that
\hat{m}=\frac{1}{2}\left(V_{2}+V_{3}\right),
and so we have
m(t)=(\hat{m}\circ X)(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{2\pi il_{2}t}+e^{2\pi il_{3}t}\right)=\cos(2\pi t)=
for any

•

Finally, that
U(Az)=m\cdot U(z)
always hold directly follows from the definitions of

8.
Example: Differential operator on
\mathbb{R}

Here, we consider another example, that of the derivative for compactly supported smooth functions on
\mathbb{R}
. We think it is maybe the most important one, as it opens the door to further research on some classes of differential operators. Just like with the shift operator, there is a natural known spectral embedding, which is given by the Fourier Transform. Here, we find that up to measure-preserving map and normalization, our process naturally outputs this embedding.

Let
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
and
H=L_{2}(\mathbb{R},\mu),
with
\mu
being the Lebesgues measure on
\mathbb{R}.
Furthermore, let
\operatorname{dom}(A)=C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),
and let
A=-i\frac{d}{dx}.
It is known that
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is dense in
H
, and it is quickly verified though integration by parts that
A
is symmetric.

8.1.
Finding the sampling sequence

Our intuition is that to expect the most explicit and natural resulting objects, one should be meticulous in choosing the sampling most ”tailored” to the given operator. In the case of the shift operator, we only had to make minor modifications on
A_{n}
when compared with the generic case described in Proposition
3.2
, so that
\Omega_{n}
could have an explicit description. Here, we think that the better strategy is to construct the sampling with inspiration from numerical methods of the derivative.

Given
n\in\mathbb{N},
and
l\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
-n!^{2}\leq l<n!^{2},
let
s_{l}^{(n)}=[\frac{l}{n!},\frac{l+1}{n!}).
Then, let
H_{n}=\operatorname{span}(\{\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}\}_{l=-n!^{2}}^{n!^{2}-1}).
we note that
(\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}})_{l=-n!^{2}}^{n!^{2}-1}
forms on orthogonal basis of
H_{n}
, all having the same norm
\|\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}\|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}.
Thus, we can define
LS_{n}
and
RS_{n}
on
H_{n}
to be the left and right shift operators mod
2n!
with respect to that basis. Specifically, like in the previous section, they are extending by linearity the relations

RS_{n}\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{1}_{s_{-n!^{2}}^{(n)}}\quad\text{ if $l=n!^{2}-1$ }\\
\mathbf{1}_{s_{l+1}^{(n)}}\quad\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}\quad\text{ and }\quad LS_{n}\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{1}_{s_{n!^{2}-1}^{(n)}}\quad\text{ if $l=-n!^{2}$ }\\
\mathbf{1}_{s_{l-1}^{(n)}}\quad\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

Then, we define
A_{n}=-i\frac{LS_{n}-RS_{n}}{2/n!}
on
H_{n}
. Finally, let
\Omega_{n}=\{f_{k}^{(n)}\}_{k=-n!^{2}}^{n!^{2}-1}
with

f_{k}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n!}}\sum_{l=-n!^{2}}^{n!^{2}-1}e^{2\pi il\frac{k}{2n!^{2}}}\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}.

We first prove the following:

Proposition 8.1
.

We have that
(H_{n},A_{n},\Omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
forms a sampling sequence for
A
. Furthermore,
\lambda_{n}(f_{k}^{(n)})=n!\sin\left(\pi\frac{k}{n!^{2}}\right).
Finally,
LS_{n}
and
RS_{n}
are unitary and adjoint of each other, with
LS_{n}f_{k}^{(n)}=e^{2\pi i\frac{k}{2n!^{2}}}f_{k}^{(n)}
for any
f_{k}^{(n)}\in\Omega_{n}.

Proof.

Property (
1
):
As the span of a finite subset of
H
,
H_{n}
is a finite-dimensional subspace of
H
by definition. Of note, we have earlier established that
(\sqrt{n!}\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}})_{l=-n!^{2}}^{n!^{2}-1}
is an orthonormal basis of
H_{n}
, and so
\dim(H_{n})=2n!
.

Property (
2
):
Since
LS_{n}
and
RS_{n}
both permute orthonormal basis of
H_{n}
given by
(\sqrt{n!}\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}})_{l=-n!^{2}}^{n!^{2}-1}
, they both are unitary. Furthermore, they are inverses of each other, thus adjoint of each other. Thus,
LS_{n}-RS_{n}
is antisymmetric, making
A_{n}
symmetric.

Property (
3
):
We first establish that
\Omega_{n}
is an orthonormal basis of
H_{n}
. We have, for
-n!^{2}\leq k<n!^{2},
that
\|f_{k}^{(n)}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2n!}\sum_{l=-n!^{2}}^{n!^{2}-1}\frac{1}{n!}=1.
Furthermore, for
-n!^{2}\leq k_{2}<k_{1}<n!^{2},
we have, though geometric sums, that

2n!^{2}\langle f_{k_{1}}^{(n)},f_{k_{2}}^{(n)}\rangle=\sum_{l=-n!^{2}}^{n!^{2}-1}\left(e^{2\pi i\frac{k_{1}-k_{2}}{2n!^{2}}}\right)^{l}=0.

Thus,
\Omega_{n}
is orthonormal. Since
|\Omega_{n}|=2n!^{2}=\dim(H_{n}),
we have that
\Omega_{n}
is indeed an orthonormal basis of
H_{n}
.

Just like with last section, we simplify the next calculation by considering the permutation
\phi
on
\{l\}_{l=-n!^{2}}^{n!^{2}-1}
for which
LS_{n}\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}=\mathbf{1}_{s_{\phi(l)}^{(n)}}
, noting
\phi(l)\equiv l-1
modulo
2n!^{2}
. We then calculate, for
-n!^{2}\leq k<n!^{2},

\displaystyle LS_{n}f_{k}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n!}}\sum_{l=-n!^{2}}^{n!^{2}-1}e^{2\pi il\frac{k}{2n!^{2}}}\mathbf{1}_{s_{\phi(l)}^{(n)}}

\displaystyle=e^{2\pi i\frac{k}{2n!^{2}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n!}}\sum_{l=-n!^{2}}^{n!^{2}-1}e^{2\pi i\phi(l)\frac{k}{2n!^{2}}}\mathbf{1}_{s_{\phi(l)}^{(n)}}

\displaystyle=e^{2\pi i\frac{k}{2n!^{2}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n!}}\sum_{l=-n!^{2}}^{n!^{2}-1}e^{2\pi il\frac{k}{2n!^{2}}}\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}

\displaystyle=e^{2\pi i\frac{k}{2n!^{2}}}f_{k}^{(n)}.

Using that
RS_{n}
and
LS_{n}
are inverses of each other, we find
RS_{n}=e^{-2\pi i\frac{k}{2n!^{2}}}f_{k}^{(n)}.
Therefore,
A_{n}f_{k}^{(n)}=n!\frac{e^{2\pi i\frac{k}{2n!^{2}}}-e^{-2\pi i\frac{k}{2n!^{2}}}}{2i}f_{k}^{(n)}=n!\sin\left(\pi\frac{k}{n!^{2}}\right)f_{k}^{(n)}.
We thus note that
\Omega_{n}
is an orthonormal eigenbasis of
A_{n}
, with
\lambda_{n}(f_{k}^{(n)})=n!\sin\left(\pi\frac{k}{n!^{2}}\right).

Property (
4
):
Let
u\in\operatorname{dom}(A)=C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}).
Furthermore, given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

u_{n}=\sum_{l=-n!^{2}}^{n!^{2}-1}u(\frac{l}{n!})\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}\in H_{n}.

We establish that in
L_{2}(\mathbb{R},\mu),

u=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}u_{n}
and
Au=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}A_{n}u_{n},
which is sufficient to conclude the property as
u
is arbitrary.

First, let
r\in\mathbb{R}
for which
r\geq 1
and
\operatorname{supp}(u)\subset[-r+1,r-1].
We first establish that all
u_{n}
are supported in
[-r,r].
We have that for
t\in[-n!,n![\supset\operatorname{supp}(u_{n}),

u_{n}(t)=u(\frac{l_{n}(t)}{n!}),
where
-n!^{2}\leq l_{n}(t)<n!^{2}
is the unique such integer for which
t\in s_{l_{n}(t)}^{(n)}.
Noting that
\operatorname{diam}(s_{l}^{(n)})=\frac{1}{n!}\leq 1
, for any
t\notin[-r,r]
but still inside
[-n!,n![,

l_{n}(t)\notin[-r+1,r-1],
and so
u_{n}(t)=0.
It follows that
u
and all
u_{n}
are supported in
[-r,r]
.

We set
M\in\mathbb{R}
such that
|u^{\prime}(t)|\leq M
for all
t\in[-r,r],
which exists since
u
is smooth. Let
n
large enough that
[-r,r]\subset[-n!,n![.
For any such
n
, we have that for any
t\in[-r,r]
,
|u(t)-u_{n}(t)|=|u(t)-u(l_{n}(t))|\leq M|t-l_{n}(t)|\leq\frac{M}{n!}.
Thus,

\|u-u_{n}\|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u-u_{n}|^{2}d\mu=\int_{[-r,r]}|u-u_{n}|^{2}d\mu\leq 2r\frac{M^{2}}{n!^{2}}.

We conclude that
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
u
.

We have that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
for which
n!>r,

u(t)=0
for any
t\notin(-n!,n!-\frac{1}{n!}).
Thus, we have that
u_{n}=\sum_{l=-n!^{2}+1}^{n!^{2}-2}u(\frac{l}{n!})\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}
and thus

LS_{n}u_{n}=\sum_{l=-n!^{2}+1}^{n!^{2}-2}u(\frac{l}{n!})\mathbf{1}_{s_{l-1}^{(n)}}=\sum_{l=-n!^{2}}^{n!^{2}-3}u(\frac{l+1}{n!})\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}=\sum_{l=-n!^{2}}^{n!^{2}-1}u(\frac{l+1}{n!})\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}.

The same way,

RS_{n}u_{n}=\sum_{l=-n!^{2}+1}^{n!^{2}-2}u(\frac{l}{n!})\mathbf{1}_{s_{l+1}^{(n)}}=\sum_{l=-n!^{2}+2}^{n!^{2}-1}u(\frac{l-1}{n!})\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}=\sum_{l=-n!^{2}}^{n!^{2}-1}u(\frac{l-1}{n!})\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}.

Therefore, we calculate, for such
n
,

\displaystyle A_{n}u_{n}

\displaystyle=-i\frac{1}{2/n!}(LS_{n}u_{n}-RS_{n}u_{n})

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{l=-n!^{2}}^{n!^{2}-1}\frac{u(\frac{l+1}{n!})-u(\frac{l-1}{n!})}{2/n!}\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{l=-n!^{2}}^{n!^{2}-1}u^{\prime}(c_{l}^{(n)})\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}},

where the existence of
c_{l}^{(n)}\in(\frac{l-1}{n!},\frac{l+1}{n!})
is guaranteed by the Mean Value Theorem. Since
u
is supported in
[-r+1,r-1]
,
\operatorname{supp}(u^{\prime})\subset[-r+1,r-1]
as well. Similarly as before, for any
t\in[n!,n![\setminus[-r,r],
we have
c_{l_{n}(t)}^{(n)}\notin[-r+1,r-1]
and so
(A_{n}u_{n})(t)=-iu^{\prime}(c_{l_{n}(t)}^{(n)})=0,
since
|t-c_{l_{n}(t)}^{(n)}|<\frac{2}{n!}\leq 1
. Thus, for any
n
such that
n!>r\geq 1
,
A_{n}u_{n}
is supported in
[-r,r].

If
M_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
is such that
|u^{\prime\prime}(t)|\leq M_{2}
for all
t\in[-r,r],
then we have that

|(A_{n}u_{n})(t)-(Au)(t)|=|(-iu^{\prime}(c_{l_{n}(t)}^{(n)}))-(-iu^{\prime}(t))|=|u^{\prime}(t)-u^{\prime}(c_{l_{n}(t)}^{(n)})|\leq M_{2}\frac{2}{n!}.

Thus,

\|A_{n}u_{n}-Au\|^{2}=\int_{[-r,r]}|A_{n}u_{n}-Au|^{2}d\mu\leq 4M_{2}r\frac{1}{n!}.

Therefore,
(A_{n}u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
Au
, ending the proof.
∎

8.2.
Finding a suitable scale

While we could use Proposition
3.6
to consider a generic scale, we meticulously build one here that helps simplify much of the calculations.

First, let
E:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
with
E(t)=\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}e^{-t^{2}}.
We have that
E\in H
is even, with
\|E\|=1.
We also note that
E
is smooth, with all derivatives bounded and in
H
.

Furthermore, let
(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be an integer sequence such that
0<L_{n}<n!^{2}-1
and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{n!^{\frac{3}{2}}}{L_{n}}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{L_{n}}{n!^{2}}=0.
For example,
L_{n}=\lfloor n!^{\frac{5}{3}}\rfloor
works. Then, let

E^{(n)}:=\sum_{l=-L_{n}}^{L_{n}}E\left(\frac{l}{n!}\right)\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}+\frac{i}{n!}\mathbf{1}_{s_{0}^{(n)}}\in H_{n}\setminus\{0\}.

Proposition 8.2
.

We have that
E=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}E^{(n)}
and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}A_{n}E^{(n)}=-iE^{\prime}.
Furthermore, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in\Omega_{n},

\langle E^{(n)},f\rangle\neq 0.

Proof.

We first show that
E=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}E^{(n)}.
We calculate, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\|E-E^{(n)}-\frac{i}{n!}\mathbf{1}_{s_{0}^{(n)}}\|^{2}=\|E\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-\frac{L_{n}}{n!},\frac{L_{n}+1}{n!}[}\|^{2}+\sum_{l=-L_{n}}^{L_{n}}\left\|\left(E-E\left(\frac{l}{n!}\right)\right)\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}\right\|^{2}.

Since both
-\frac{i}{n!}\mathbf{1}_{s_{0}^{(n)}}
and
E\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-\frac{L_{n}}{n!},\frac{L_{n}+1}{n!}[}
converge to
0
, using Monotonic Convergence Theorem and the fact that
\frac{L_{n}}{n!}
goes to infinity, we have that it is sufficient to establish
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{l=-L_{n}}^{L_{n}}\left\|\left(E-E\left(\frac{l}{n!}\right)\right)\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}\right\|^{2}=0
to prove
E=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}E^{(n)}.
Since
E^{\prime}
is bounded, let
M\in\mathbb{R}
such that
|E^{\prime}(t)|\leq M
holds for
t\in\mathbb{R}.
Then, by Mean Value Theorem, we have that whenever
|l|\leq L_{n}
and
t\in s_{l}^{(n)},

|E(t)-E(\frac{l}{n!})|\leq\frac{M}{n!}.
Therefore, for such
l
,
\|\left(E-E\left(\frac{l}{n!}\right)\right)\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}\|^{2}\leq\frac{M^{2}}{n!^{3}},
and so

\sum_{l=-L_{n}}^{L_{n}}\left\|\left(E-E\left(\frac{l}{n!}\right)\right)\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{(2L_{n}+1)M^{2}}{n!^{3}}.

Since
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{L_{n}}{n!^{2}}=0,
we get
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{l=-L_{n}}^{L_{n}}\left\|\left(E-E\left(\frac{l}{n!}\right)\right)\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}\right\|^{2}=0,
and
E=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}E^{(n)}.

Next, since
-n!^{2}<-L_{n}<L_{n}<n!^{2}-1
, we have
A_{n}\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}=-i\frac{n!}{2}(\mathbf{1}_{s_{l-1}^{(n)}}-\mathbf{1}_{s_{l+1}^{(n)}})
whenever
-L_{n}\leq l\leq L_{n}.
Thus,

\displaystyle A_{n}\left(E^{(n)}-\frac{i}{n!}\mathbf{1}_{s_{0}^{(n)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=-L_{n}}^{L_{n}}E\left(\frac{l}{n!}\right)A_{n}\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-in!}{2}\sum_{l=-L_{n}}^{L_{n}}E\left(\frac{l}{n!}\right)\left(\mathbf{1}_{s_{l-1}^{(n)}}-\mathbf{1}_{s_{l+1}^{(n)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{-in!}{2}\left(\sum_{l=-L_{n}}^{L_{n}}\left(E\left(\frac{l+1}{n!}\right)-E\left(\frac{l-1}{n!}\right)\right)\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}+R_{n}\right),

where

R_{n}=E(\frac{-L_{n}}{n!})\mathbf{1}_{s_{-L_{n}-1}^{(n)}}-E(\frac{L_{n}+1}{n!})\mathbf{1}_{s_{L_{n}}^{(n)}}+E(\frac{L_{n}}{n!})\mathbf{1}_{s_{L_{n}+1}^{(n)}}-E(\frac{-L_{n}-1}{n!})\mathbf{1}_{s_{L_{n}}^{(n)}}.

We have that

\|n!R_{n}\|^{2}=n!\left(|E(\frac{-L_{n}}{n!})|^{2}+|E(\frac{L_{n}+1}{n!})|^{2}+|E(\frac{L_{n}}{n!})|^{2}+|E(\frac{-L_{n}-1}{n!})|^{2}\right),

and so
\|n!R_{n}\|^{2}\leq 4n!\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}e^{-2(\frac{L_{n}}{n!})^{2}}
. Since
\frac{L_{n}}{n!^{\frac{3}{2}}}\rightarrow\infty
, for large enough
n
we have
\frac{L_{n}}{n!}>\sqrt{n!},
and so
\|n!R_{n}\|^{2}\leq 4\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}n!e^{-2n!},
which implies
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n!R_{n}=0.
We can also calculate that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}A_{n}(\frac{i}{n!}\mathbf{1}_{s_{0}^{(n)}})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{1}_{s_{-1}^{(n)}}-\mathbf{1}_{s_{1}^{(n)}})=0.

Therefore, if
F_{n}:=iA_{n}\left(E^{(n)}-\frac{i}{n!}\mathbf{1}_{s_{0}^{(n)}}\right)-\frac{n!}{2}R_{n},
then
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}=E^{\prime}
if and only if
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}A_{n}E^{(n)}=-iE^{\prime}.

Then, by the Mean Value Theorem, we have elements
c_{l}^{(n)}\in(\frac{l-1}{n!},\frac{l+1}{n!})
such that

\displaystyle F_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=-L_{n}}^{L_{n}}\frac{E\left(\frac{l+1}{n!}\right)-E\left(\frac{l-1}{n!}\right)}{2/n!}\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}=\sum_{l=-L_{n}}^{L_{n}}E^{\prime}(c_{l}^{(n)})\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}.

Using
M_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
as any bound on
E^{\prime\prime},
we can use the Mean Value Theorem again to establish that
|E^{\prime}(t)-E^{\prime}(c_{l}^{(n)})|\leq\frac{2M_{1}}{n!}
whenever
t\in s_{l}^{(n)},
thus

\displaystyle\|E^{\prime}-F_{n}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|E^{\prime}\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-L_{n},L_{n}[}\|^{2}+\sum_{l=-L_{n}}^{L_{n}}\|(E^{\prime}-E^{\prime}(c_{l}^{(n)}))\mathbf{1}_{s_{l}^{(n)}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|E^{\prime}\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-L_{n},L_{n}[}\|^{2}+4M_{1}^{2}\frac{2L_{n}+1}{n!^{3}}.

Therefore, since
E^{\prime}\in H
, we have
E^{\prime}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n},
and so
-iE^{\prime}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}A_{n}E_{n}.

For the last part, we calculate, for any
f_{k}^{(n)}\in\Omega_{n},

\displaystyle\langle E^{(n)},f_{k}^{(n)}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!\sqrt{2n!}}\sum_{l=-L_{n}}^{L_{n}}e^{-2\pi il\frac{k}{2n!^{2}}}E\left(\frac{l}{n!}\right)+\frac{i}{n!^{2}\sqrt{2n!}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!\sqrt{2n!}}\left(E(0)+\frac{i}{n!}+\sum_{l=1}^{L_{n}}\left(e^{-2\pi il\frac{k}{2n!^{2}}}E\left(\frac{l}{n!}\right)+e^{2\pi il\frac{k}{2n!^{2}}}E\left(\frac{-l}{n!}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!\sqrt{2n!}}\left(\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}+\frac{i}{n!}+2\sum_{l=1}^{L_{n}}\cos\left(2\pi l\frac{k}{2n!^{2}}\right)E\left(\frac{l}{n!}\right)\right).

Thus,
\operatorname{Im}(\langle E^{(n)},f_{k}^{(n)}\rangle)=\frac{i}{n!^{2}\sqrt{2n!}}\neq 0,
and so
\langle E^{(n)},f_{k}^{(n)}\rangle\neq 0.

∎

Then, let
(q_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be a count of
\mathbb{Q},
with
q_{1}=0.
Furthermore, for
r\in\mathbb{R},
let
\mathcal{T}_{r}:H\rightarrow H
be the translation by
r
, so that
(\mathcal{T}_{r}g)(t)=g(t-r).
We note that
\mathcal{T}_{r}
is a unitary map on
H
. Then, for
j\in\mathbb{N},
we define
g_{j}=\mathcal{T}_{q_{j}}E
. We have that each
g_{j}\in H
is smooth, with
\|g_{j}\|=\|E\|=1.

Using the Fourier Transform, one could prove the following. We do find relevant to include the schema of an elementary proof, which is taken from
[
nonez2024spectralequivalencesnonstandardsamplings
]
.

Proposition 8.3
.

The set
\{g_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is dense-spanning in
L_{2}(\mathbb{R},\mu).

Idea of Proof.

For this part (and this part alone) we will denote a given function
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
by
f(x)
. Let
\tilde{H}=\overline{\operatorname{span}(\{g_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}})}\subset H.
We also define, for
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C},

P_{k}f
to be the
2k
-periodic extension of
f|_{[-k,k)}.
Since
g_{j}(x)=E(x)\cdot e^{2q_{j}x}e^{-q_{j}^{2}},

\tilde{H}
must contain:

•

E(x)\cdot e^{qx}
, for any
q\in\mathbb{Q}
;

•

E(x)\cdot e^{tx}
, for any

, using
E(x)\cdot e^{q_{n}x}
when
q_{n}\rightarrow t
;

•

E(x)\cdot e^{tx}x

E(x)\cdot\frac{e^{(t+1/n)x}-e^{tx}}{1/n}

•

E(x)\cdot e^{tx}x^{k}

•

E(x)\cdot e^{itx}
for any

\displaystyle=\Psi^{(K)}(\hat{\nu}^{(K)}(f_{k}^{(K)}))=\operatorname{st}_{Q}(X_{K}(f_{k}^{(K)})),
