Optimal dismantling of directed networks
Authors: Xueming Liu, Jiawen Hu, Yumei Wang, Yang-Yu Liu, Hai-Tao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNetwork dismantling aims to identify a set of critical nodes whose removal rapidly fragments a network’s connectivity and functionality, with applications in controlling epidemics, halting rumor spread, and disrupting criminal networks. While previous studies have mainly focused on undirected networks, many real-world systems are directed, such as the World Wide Web and global trade networks. In directed networks, the giant strongly connected component captures mutual reachability and enables feedback loops that sustain system functionality. Here we introduce a centrality measure called network incoherence centrality and develop a trophic analysis-based dismantling method in which nodes are removed in descending order of their scores. Tested on synthetic networks and 14 real-world directed networks, our method consistently outperforms existing approaches. It also triggers the largest connectivity avalanches, highlighting its ability to pinpoint structurally critical nodes. These findings advance understanding of structure-function relationships in directed networks and inform the design of more resilient systems.Subject terms: Complex networks, Applied mathematics

Introduction

Network dismantling (ND) involves finding the minimum set of critical nodes whose removal can rapidly fragment a network into isolated clusters1,2. ND has attracted considerable attention3–8 due to its extensive real-world applications, such as stopping the spread of epidemics and rumors9, and disrupting the communication in criminal or malware networks10,11.

To identify critical nodes in networks, various heuristic methods based on centrality measures have been proposed. At the individual node level, nodes are assessed based on centrality measures, such as degree12,13, closeness14, and betweenness15. At the level of node sets, the collective influence (CI) algorithm1, which leverages optimal percolation theory, has been developed to identify key groups of nodes. Additionally, advanced algorithms like belief propagation-guided decimation (BPD)11 and the Min-Sum algorithm16 address the optimal decycling problem, providing a more refined approach to pinpointing a minimal, yet highly influential set of critical nodes.

Recently, several machine learning-based methods have been developed for ND. Notably, the deep reinforcement learning-based FINDER17, the deep learning-based Directed Network Disintegrator (DND)18, and the supervised learning-based GDM19 have shown potential in handling the complexities of large-scale networks. However, most of these methods are primarily designed for undirected networks. Among them, DND is the only approach tailored for directed networks, but its performance still lags behind heuristic methods, such as CoreHD20, in certain scenarios, and its black-box nature limits interpretability.

In contrast, many real-world networks are inherently directed21,22, including gene regulatory networks23, traffic systems24, and the World Wide Web25, which often exhibit strong asymmetry and structural heterogeneity26. Directed networks comprise multiple giant components—namely the giant weakly connected component (GWCC), the giant in-component (GIN), the giant out-component (GOUT), and the giant strongly connected component (GSCC)—that capture different aspects of connectivity and functionality27–29. Among them, the GSCC uniquely supports mutual reachability, feedback loops, and closed-circuit signaling required for synchronization, computation, and homeostatic regulation that are essential for sustained functionality in many real-world systems30–32. Consequently, the GSCC is widely used as a key structural indicator in studies of directed network percolation and robustness33–36.

Fortunately, the recent development of trophic analysis37 has provided a principled way to quantify directionality and hierarchy in general directed networks, offering interpretable tools to support more effective dismantling strategies. Trophic analysis was originally developed to study species’ trophic levels in ecology38,39 and has since been widely applied across various domains, including food web exploration40, epidemic spreading in social networks41, infrastructure resilience assessment42,43, and economic interactions37. These applications have advanced the understanding of directed networks by providing quantitative tools for analyzing complex systems.

Trophic analysis reveals a strong correlation between a network’s strong connectivity, global directionality—defined as the dominant orientation of links from lower to higher trophic levels—and hierarchical organization, quantified by trophic incoherence. Hierarchical structures are prevalent in real-world networks, where nodes’ trophic levels are often indicative of their functional roles37. Notably, “backward" links—where the target node’s trophic level is not higher than the source node’s—play a crucial role in shaping global directionality. Their removal can significantly disrupt hierarchical ordering and strong connectivity44,45, presenting a promising avenue for effectively dismantling directed networks.

Based on trophic analysis, we developed an interpretable ND method tailored for directed networks, whose connectivity is characterized by its GSCC size34. In our trophic analysis-based dismantling (TAD) method, nodes are removed in descending order according to their network incoherence (NI) centrality scores. When applied to various networks, our TAD method outperforms existing state-of-the-art methods in terms of fragmenting directed networks. Further investigation of the ND process reveals that TAD results in the largest “avalanche” compared to other methods, which helps explain its superior performance. In addition, we systematically investigated the general applicability of TAD and found that its performance improves first and then deteriorates as the trophic incoherence rises. This work offers a comprehensive analysis and explanation of the observed results, thereby enhancing the understanding of ND across diverse scenarios.

Results

Overview of the TAD method

To address the intricate role of hierarchy and directionality in network robustness, we introduce TAD, an ND method based on trophic level analysis. This method ranks nodes using a trophic-based centrality measure and removes them sequentially in descending order of this measure. The procedure consists of three main steps, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Given a directed network, we first compute the trophic level hi of each node based on the trophic level analysis. Trophic levels quantify the hierarchical position of nodes in a directed network, with the fractional trophic level hi measuring relative hierarchy along the dominant direction of flow. Higher values of hi correspond to nodes positioned higher in the hierarchy. Because only relative trophic positions are meaningful, we shift the solution such that min(hi)=0, ensuring non-negative values, while the maximum hi is determined by the network topology.

A directed link (i → j) is defined as a backward link if the trophic level of its target node is lower than that of its source, i.e., hj − hi < 0. These backward links play a critical role in maintaining strong connectivity by violating the feed-forward hierarchical organization implied by trophic ordering and thereby introducing directed cycles45. Therefore, we anticipate that systematically identifying and removing the nodes associated with such links is an effective strategy to dismantle the GSCC.

Furthermore, the presence of backward links gives rise to trophic incoherence of the network, which is defined as F=∑ijAij(hj−hi−1)2∑ijAij, with A being the adjacency matrix37. The trophic incoherence of a network F quantifies how much a directed network deviates from a perfectly hierarchical structure. When F = 0, all links satisfy hj − hi = 1 and are perfectly aligned with the trophic ordering, such that the network admits a strict feed-forward structure and is acyclic. As F increases, departures from this feed-forward organization become more pronounced, reflecting stronger violations of trophic ordering and facilitating the emergence of directed cycles (see Supplementary Information Note 1 for more details). For large values of F, trophic levels become increasingly compressed, and the network departs substantially from feed-forward organization, typically exhibiting dense cyclic structure.

This metric effectively captures each node’s influence on the overall incoherence of the network, providing a principled basis for prioritizing node removal during the dismantling process.

Dismantling synthetic networks using TAD

To evaluate the effectiveness of TAD, we compared it against eight baseline methods, including classical centrality-based (HD46, HDA47, PageRank48, Control Centrality49), structural optimization (MinSum16, CoreHD20), and machine learning-based approaches (FINDER17, DND18). These methods encompass both classical heuristics and modern AI-driven strategies. Details of each method are presented in the Methods section.

We tracked the evolution of the GSCC size during the progressive removal of nodes in synthetic directed scale-free (SF) networks34 with varying degree exponents λ, where the same λ is used for both the in-degree and out-degree distributions. As shown in Fig. 2a, TAD (dark blue lines) consistently induces a faster collapse of the GSCC compared to other methods, demonstrating its superior dismantling performance.

To quantify dismantling efficiency, we plotted the curve of the remaining GSCC size as a function of the fraction of nodes removed, and then calculated the area under this curve (AUC). A smaller AUC value indicates a more effective dismantling. As presented in Fig. 2B, TAD consistently achieves the lowest AUC across all λ values. For instance, when λ = 2.2, the AUC for TAD is 0.02, compared with 0.04 for the second-best method, PageRank. Similarly, at λ = 3.2, TAD yields an AUC of 0.13, while the second-best result is 0.18.

Moreover, the dismantling advantage of TAD becomes more pronounced as λ decreases. A lower degree exponent λ is typically associated with greater degree heterogeneity, reflecting broader heavy-tailed degree distributions that are commonly observed in real-world networks with 2 < λ < 3 50. This trend suggests that TAD is particularly effective in dismantling highly heterogeneous directed networks.

We also tested its performance on synthetic Erdős-Rényi (ER) networks with varying average degrees. As detailed in Supplementary Information Note 3, TAD remains highly effective in ER networks with moderate link density. However, as the average degree increases, the network becomes more cyclic and trophically incoherent, reducing the advantage of hierarchy-based strategies, such as TAD—a phenomenon that will be further examined in the next section. These results underscore the structural dependence of TAD’s effectiveness and highlight the importance of considering trophic properties when selecting dismantling strategies.

TAD induces the largest maximum avalanche

During ND, we observe an “avalanche” phenomenon, characterized by an abrupt and discontinuous reduction of the GSCC induced by a single node removal. In TAD, such avalanches arise from the targeted removal of nodes involved in multiple backward links that form directed cycles sustaining mutual reachability. Disrupting these cycles can simultaneously break mutual reachability among many nodes, leading to a large-scale fragmentation of the GSCC in a single step. Accordingly, larger avalanches indicate more effective dismantling of directed network connectivity.

As shown in Fig. 3a, we highlighted the maximum avalanche triggered by different methods. Across different networks (Fig. 3b–e), TAD stands out by consistently inducing the largest maximum avalanche. In contrast to methods such as CoreHD, PageRank, and FINDER, which cause a more gradual and less disruptive fragmentation, TAD leads to a sharper and faster breakdown of the network’s connectivity, demonstrating its superior ability to dismantle directed networks.

TAD induces the largest maximum avalanche by targeting nodes that contribute most to network incoherence. Backward links violate the trophic hierarchy, in which edges predominantly point from lower to higher levels and define a consistent directional organization. By introducing edges from higher to lower levels, backward links create directed cycles that sustain mutual reachability. Because the GSCC consists of nodes that are mutually reachable, such cycles are necessary for maintaining the GSCC. By targeting nodes with a high number of backward links (significant trophic level differences), TAD effectively dismantles critical feedback structures in the network, leading to an abrupt breakdown of strong connectivity, analogous to structural percolation transitions in directed networks51. The ability of TAD to induce the largest maximum avalanche is meaningful as it offers a powerful tool for analyzing the vulnerability and resilience of complex networks, shedding light on the potential weaknesses of networks in various real-world systems.

To quantify the structural determinants of avalanche magnitude, we analyzed the dependence of the maximum avalanche on trophic incoherence F (see Supplementary Information Note 4 for details). Across both ER and SF networks, avalanche size exhibits a non-monotonic dependence on F, peaking at intermediate incoherence levels. While F controls the abundance of feedback structures, avalanche magnitude is shaped by how backward links and directed cycles are organized within the GSCC: when feedback is concentrated around a limited set of nodes, their removal disrupts overlapping cycles and triggers large collapses, whereas diffuse feedback leads to gradual fragmentation.

This structural regime naturally points to a nodal-level mechanism. The structurally pivotal nodes sustaining feedback and mutual reachability are not necessarily high-degree hubs and may therefore be weakly emphasized by centrality-based methods, such as PageRank or CoreHD. Consequently, TAD identifies a subset of critical nodes that only partially overlaps with those selected by other approaches. These TAD-exclusive nodes are enriched in backward links spanning trophic-level differences and participate in directed cycles that maintain strong connectivity (see Supplementary Information Note 5 for details).

Performance of TAD across networks with varying trophic incoherence

Our TAD method is designed based on nodes’ network incoherence centrality, prioritizing node removal according to the number and trophic level difference of backward links. On the network level, different networks may have different trophic incoherence F, which could influence TAD’s performance. Investigating how TAD performs across networks with varying F is crucial for assessing its effectiveness and generalizability in different structural configurations.

We compared the performance of TAD with other dismantling methods in networks with varying trophic incoherence F, as shown in Fig. 4. In ER networks with an average degree 〈k〉 = 20 (see Supplementary Information Note 6 for the generation of ER networks with varying F), as shown in panel (A), TAD achieves the lowest AUC for networks with F≤0.62, indicating its superior dismantling capability in networks with moderate hierarchical structure. As F increases beyond this point, the efficiency of TAD gradually decreases. In SF networks with a degree exponent λ = 2.8, shown in b, we analyzed cases where F≤0.6 and extended the analysis to networks with F > 0.6 by reversing some links to artificially increase trophic incoherence (see Supplementary Information Note 7 for more details). The pattern observed in SF networks is consistent with that in ER networks: TAD exhibits the best performance at low F, but its advantage diminishes as F grows. When F exceeds 0.67, TAD is no longer the top-performing method.

This trend reflects structural changes associated with trophic incoherence. For low to moderate F, hierarchical organization remains visible, enabling TAD to exploit trophic-level imbalances to fragment the GSCC efficiently. As F increases, backward links initially introduce additional structural vulnerabilities. However, when incoherence becomes high, directional hierarchy is obscured, weakening the effectiveness of hierarchy-based dismantling.

To further quantify the structural effects, we systematically compared dismantling efficiency across networks with varying trophic incoherence F, degree exponent λ (in SF networks), and average degree 〈k〉 (in ER networks) (see Supplementary Information Note 8 for more details). The results confirm a non-monotonic dependence on F, with peak performance emerging at intermediate incoherence levels. While λ and 〈k〉 influence the absolute difficulty of dismantling, they do not reproduce the same performance pattern, indicating that the observed trend is specifically associated with how trophic violations shape mutual reachability within the GSCC.

The superior performance of TAD arises from its ability to exploit hierarchical directionality in real-world networks. Nodes with high NI centrality participate in backward links that span trophic levels and sustain directed cycles. Removing these nodes simultaneously disrupts multiple cycles, leading to rapid loss of mutual reachability and fragmentation of the GSCC.

Together, these results show that TAD not only achieves strong quantitative performance across diverse real-world networks but also provides a mechanistic understanding of which nodes structurally sustain directed connectivity. By exploiting directional hierarchy as a structural prior, TAD is particularly effective in systems where link directionality encodes functional constraints.

Discussion

Dismantling directed networks is a fundamental problem with wide-ranging applications, from epidemic control and rumor suppression to infrastructure protection and social system regulation. Unlike undirected networks, directed networks are governed by asymmetric interactions and hierarchical organization, making their structural fragility and functional resilience more complex to assess. Existing dismantling methods, largely developed for undirected graphs, often fall short in capturing these features.

To address this gap, we developed the TAD method—an interpretable and efficient dismantling strategy tailored for directed networks. Grounded in trophic level analysis, TAD prioritizes node removal based on the network incoherence centrality, a measure to quantify the trophic level difference across backward links, thereby targeting the nodes that most disrupt the network’s hierarchical integrity. This mechanism leverages directional and hierarchical cues that are often overlooked by traditional or learning-based methods.

Our results demonstrate that TAD consistently outperforms state-of-the-art dismantling methods across both synthetic and real-world networks, particularly in systems with high degree heterogeneity or well-defined global directionality. In real-world directed networks from multiple domains, TAD achieves the lowest dismantling AUC in all cases. Notably, it induces the largest maximum avalanches, revealing its capacity to identify structurally critical nodes whose removal causes cascading breakdowns in connectivity.

A key insight from this work is the structural dependence of dismantling effectiveness. In synthetic networks, TAD performs best when trophic incoherence is low to moderate—where hierarchical organization remains but is not entirely acyclic. As incoherence increases and the network becomes more cyclic and disordered, the relative advantage of hierarchy-based strategies gradually declines, emphasizing the need to tailor dismantling approaches to different structural regimes. Remarkably, in contrast to synthetic cases, TAD maintains its superiority in real-world networks, consistently outperforming all baseline methods even when the trophic incoherence F is very high. This difference suggests that real-world networks, even at high F, may preserve localized hierarchical organization or structural constraints not captured by purely synthetic random constructions.

Beyond its empirical performance, TAD offers mechanistic interpretability by identifying nodes that bridge trophic levels, bottleneck directional flow, or anchor backward connectivity. The avalanche-like collapses it triggers reflect abrupt structural breakdowns in directed connectivity. This connection provides a physical perspective on the dismantling dynamics and enriches our understanding of robustness in directed networks.

In summary, TAD not only advances the dismantling of directed networks by integrating hierarchical structure into the dismantling strategy but also provides a generalizable framework to explore the structure-function relationship in complex systems. Its ability to predict and induce critical transitions further enhances its applicability to domains that require precise, efficient, and interpretable control over networked systems.

Methods

Network dismantling

Given a directed network G=(V,E) with N=∣V∣ nodes and ∣E∣ directed links, the goal of ND is to identify a set of nodes S⊆V whose removal completely fragments the network, such that the resulting structure contains no GSCC. To assess the effectiveness of a dismantling strategy, we evaluate how rapidly the GSCC collapses as nodes are sequentially removed. Specifically, after removing k nodes {v1, v2, . . . , vk}, we define the remaining connectivity as fk=∣GSCC(G\{v1,...,vk})∣/N. The overall dismantling performance is measured by the AUC:

where K is the total number of node removals required to eliminate the GSCC. A smaller AUC indicates a more efficient dismantling strategy that disrupts strong connectivity with fewer removals.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the dismantling procedure proceeds by ranking nodes using a given method and removing them sequentially, monitoring the GSCC size after each step. In this work, we use AUC as the unified metric to compare the performance of TAD and other baseline methods across a variety of directed networks.

Computation of trophic level

Trophic levels define the hierarchical position of nodes in a directed network. In ecological food webs, for example, energy flows from nodes at lower trophic levels, such as plants, to those at higher levels, such as carnivores38. This concept can be naturally extended to general directed networks.

For a network with N nodes, let A denote the adjacency matrix, where Aij = 1 if there is a directed link from node i to node j, and Aij = 0 otherwise. The trophic levels are obtained by solving the linear system

where h is the vector of trophic levels37, υi=kiin−kiout is the difference between the in-degree and out-degree of node i, and Λ = diag(u) − A − AT is a Laplacian-like matrix of the directed network, with ui=kiin+kiout.

Equation (3) defines trophic levels through a Laplacian-like balance between incoming and outgoing connections. In general, nodes with relatively stronger incoming connectivity tend to be placed higher in the trophic ordering, while nodes with stronger outgoing connectivity tend to occupy lower hierarchical positions. Through the operator Λ, this formulation relates hi to local degree asymmetry while simultaneously enforcing a global self-consistent structure, thereby integrating local connectivity imbalance into a coherent hierarchical organization.

Since the matrix Λ is singular, the solution for h is defined up to an additive constant. We therefore shift the solution such that min(hi)=0, ensuring non-negative trophic levels. The resulting linear system can be efficiently solved using standard numerical methods, such as LU decomposition or iterative solvers, for large networks.

Generate networks with a high level of trophic incoherence

In synthetic SF networks generated with a fixed degree distribution exponent λ, the trophic incoherence F rarely exceeds 0.6 due to the natural emergence of globally directed structures. However, to systematically evaluate the performance of dismantling methods in networks with higher structural disorder, we construct SF networks with elevated trophic incoherence while preserving their degree distributions.

To achieve this, we adopt a minimal perturbation strategy that reverses the direction of a small number of selected links to increase F. Specifically, we iteratively select one directed link (i → j) and reverse its direction (j → i) only if the operation increases the number of cycles in the network, thereby increasing the global incoherence. This process allows us to gradually increase F without significantly altering other topological features, such as degree distribution or density.
