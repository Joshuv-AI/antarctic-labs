Can LLMs understand LilyPond? A benchmark for symbolic music generation and understanding
Matteo Spanio, Mohammad Torabi, Andrea Poltronieri, Antonio Rodà

Abstract
Symbolic music evaluation for large language models remains fragmented across representations, datasets, and metrics. We introduce LilyBench, a LilyPond-based benchmark that jointly evaluates symbolic music generation and music understanding on the same family of open-weight LLMs. The benchmark includes a 200-prompt generation suite and ten understanding tasks adapted from ABC-Eval, covering syntax, metadata prediction, structural sequencing, and music recognition. Generation quality is evaluated using compile rate, MusPy descriptor distributions via Jensen–Shannon similarity, and LilyBERT-based Fréchet Music Distance (FMD). Experiments on four open-weight models show that executable LilyPond generation is achievable in zero-shot settings, while structural understanding tasks remain challenging despite strong performance on composer and genre recognition. Our experiments also reveal systematic disagreements between descriptor-based and embedding-based metrics, suggesting that symbolic music evaluation benefits from metric triangulation rather than single-score ranking. We release the benchmark, prompt bank, and evaluation code to support future research in symbolic music generation and understanding at https://github.com/CSCPadova/lilybench .

Introduction

The choice of music representation determines which constraints a language-model system can check automatically and which musical knowledge stays latent
9
;
15
. Among textual score formats, ABC notation has received the most attention, both for generation
16
;
2
and understanding
20
;
18
; LilyPond
8
is less studied, despite exposing a richer score language closer to code that fits full-score generation and structural reasoning. The BMdataset corpus and LilyBERT encoder
14
make it a practical substrate for LLM work.

We ask whether current open-weight LLMs can generate well-formed LilyPond matching a curated Baroque reference, whether the same models can answer structural, metadata, style, and quality questions from raw LilyPond, and how descriptor-based (Jensen–Shannon) and embedding-based (Fréchet Music Distance) distributional metrics differ when ranking generation quality. To answer these, we introduce
LilyBench
, a benchmark that pairs a generation evaluation with an understanding suite of ten tasks adapted from ABC-Eval
20
, evaluated on the same four open-weight backbones: Phi-4, Qwen2.5-Coder-14B, DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Lite, and Codestral-22B.

(3)

2
Related work

MIDI is the dominant symbolic representation for music generation
7
, but it is performance-oriented and exposes neither score structure nor variable reuse. Among text-based formats, ABC has received the most attention: as a “second language” for LLaMA-2
18
, in full pretrain/finetune/RL pipelines
16
, with Russell-quadrant emotion conditioning
21
, and as the substrate for the ABC-Eval understanding benchmark
20
. LilyPond
8
has typically been used as a rendering target rather than a model substrate; the BMdataset corpus and LilyBERT encoder
14
make it usable for LLM work. Objective evaluation is more mature for raw audio, where Fréchet Audio Distance
6
and CLAP-based similarities are standard; symbolic music has lagged, mixing parseability checks, handcrafted descriptors, and manual assessment
5
. Current symbolic evaluation falls into two distributional families. Descriptor distributions aggregate MusPy’s twelve symbolic features
3
via Jensen–Shannon divergence
12
. Embedding distributions use symbolic encoders such as MusicBERT
19
and CLaMP
17
as spaces in which Fréchet Music Distance (FMD)
13
ports the FID/FAD construction. To our knowledge, no prior work has run JS and FMD head-to-head on the same models and corpus. Music benchmarks for LLMs consistently report a recognition-vs-reasoning gap
18
;
22
, and the closest precedent for our understanding suite is ABC-Eval
20
, a 10-task benchmark over ABC notation.
LilyBench
mirrors its task taxonomy, ports each task to LilyPond, and pairs it with a generation benchmark, so the same backbone is evaluated on both sides from the same notation.

3
Methodology

LilyBench
couples a generation benchmark with an understanding benchmark, both run on the same four backbones and reference corpus.

We evaluate four open-weight backbones, one general-purpose and three code-specialised. Phi-4
1
(14 B dense) is the general-purpose baseline. The code-centric checkpoints are Qwen2.5-Coder-14B
4
(14 B), DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Lite
23
(16 B MoE,
\approx
2.4 B active), and Codestral-22B
10
(22 B). Sizes are not matched; we test whether code pretraining transfers to LilyPond.

The reference corpus is BMdataset
14
: 391 Baroque works yielding 2,645 self-contained LilyPond files. We split at the work level to prevent leakage; the held-out test split is the in-domain reference and Mutopia the out-of-domain one. Understanding additionally uses EMOPIA for emotion (120 records over Russell quadrants) and synthetically corrupted Mutopia scores for error detection (220 records over five corruption categories).

Generation setup

All cells use the same 200-prompt stratified bank.
Zero-shot

Few-shot

Few-shot (Ablation)
: three hand-written A-minor examples, retained because they expose a JS/FMD disagreement (section

5
Results

JS reads MusPy descriptors from rendered MIDI, FMD reads LilyBERT embeddings from raw LilyPond text. The two metrics agree when alignment to BMdataset is genuine: Phi-4 few-shot improves over zero-shot on both JS(test) and FMD(test) (0.933 to 0.701), and Codestral-22B few-shot achieves the lowest in-domain FMD (0.696) with a higher JS than its zero-shot baseline. They diverge in two opposite directions. When a model collapses to a narrow but well-formed subdistribution, JS rewards it while FMD penalises it: Codestral-22B with the hand-written A-minor demos scores the table’s maximum JS(test) (89.44) yet its FMD is worse than zero-shot, because the MusPy descriptors land near corpus means in A minor while LilyBERT detects the surface form as foreign. The opposite happens for DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Lite zero-shot, where FMD(test) is second-best in the row (0.887) but JS(test) is only 55.39: the model reproduces LilyPond surface form better than the rendered musical statistics. JS is interpretable but exploitable by narrow outputs; FMD is compile-independent and harder to fool, but does not localise which musical property changed. We therefore read both alongside compile rate, instead of fusing them into a single rank.

Tables
2
and
3
share the same four backbones, so the two halves compare directly. Generation strength does not track understanding strength. Phi-4 and Qwen2.5-Coder-14B lead on both, but Codestral-22B, the best zero-shot compiler (79.3%), is the weakest understander (0.474), with most of the gap on the format-sensitive tasks (
metadata_prediction
,
emotion_recognition
). The asymmetry matches the modality-bias diagnostics in Audio-LLMs
11
: fluency can decouple from semantic competence.

Within understanding, the recognition–reasoning gap is sharper for LilyPond than for ABC. ABC-Eval reports about 90% on basic syntax and about 25% on sequence-level reasoning for the best proprietary system
20
; on LilyPond, our open-weight 14–22 B models reach 0.68–0.96 on three recognition tasks (
music_captioning
,
composer
,
genre
), but never lift
bar_count
above 0.03 exact or
error_detection
above 0.041 macro-F1. The harder structural tasks survive the move to a richer notation, consistent with prior work
18
;
22
.

6
Conclusion

LilyBench
is the first symbolic music benchmark built on LilyPond and the first unified evaluation of LLM music generation and understanding on a shared backbone set. The four open-weight models produce executable LilyPond zero-shot, recognise composer, genre, and captions at 0.68–0.96 accuracy, and adapt to demonstrations when given training-distribution exemplars. The methodological contribution is a direct comparison of Jensen–Shannon descriptor similarity and LilyBERT-based Fréchet Music Distance on the same reference corpus: the two metrics agree when alignment is genuine and diverge in two complementary directions, motivating triangulation with compile rate rather than fusion. We release the prompt bank, understanding benches, synthetic corruption set, and evaluation code for full reproducibility. Natural extensions include chain-of-thought and reasoning-focused models
22
on the structural tasks (especially
error_detection
, where ABC-Eval
20
reports about a 40% gain), broadening the corpus beyond Baroque so that BMdataset and LilyBERT do not over-determine the FMD reference, and adding audio-LLM modality
11
to test whether the recognition–reasoning gap narrows when listening is paired with reading.
