ABSTRACT
This report details the calibration procedures that were used for the spectral and radiometric part
of the Dragonfly’s Eye for Viewing Lightning (DrEVL) radiometers. Our goal was to develop
a highly repeatable and low-cost spectral radiometer that could be deployed at numerous sites to
collect a catalog of lightning data. Maximizing the re-usability of that data requires a well-calibrated
instrument.

1. OVERVIEW

The calibration of spectral radiometers requires care because all lenses, optical filters, photodiodes,
and analog amplifier gains influence the sampled signal. Appropriate sources and instrumentation
must be selected to achieve a high-accuracy calibration with low error. For instance, the dynamic
range available from a source can limit the range over which a calibration can be performed
inducing errors at large or small observed fluences. The Dragonfly’s Eye for Viewing Lightning
(DrEVL) radiometer systems have seven spectral channels with nearly identical receive optics but
slightly different optical filters, optical coatings, spectral filters, and corresponding photodiodes
(see Table 1-1) with differing electronic gains [1]. In general the radiometer can be configured
with different detector materials and filters to achieve the desired spectral response. Figure 1-1
shows a schematic representation of a DrEVL radiometer. Each DrEVL system has seven spectral
channels and all components but the microprocessor system are replicated seven times in a single
instrument. Here, we present the methodology we use for calibration of these systems.

2.1. Governing equation

The response of each spectral channel 𝑖 of the DrEVL spectral radiometer is modeled here for a
homogeneous irradiance of the input optics. This idealization assumes that the aperture of the
radiometer is filled by the source. The radiometric signal 𝑠𝑖 , in counts, from an at-aperture spectral
radiance 𝐿 source (𝜆), in µW · cm−2 ·sr−1 ·nm−1 , is:

where 𝑠dark,𝑖 (𝑇) is the dark signal of channel 𝑖 in counts at a temperature, 𝑇, 𝑅𝑖 is band-integrated
radiometric response function of the channel in counts · µW−1 ·cm2 ·sr, and 𝐺 𝑖 (𝜆) is the normalized
spectral response function of the channel [2, 3]. For the sake of simplicity -and since the calibration
is performed at room temperature in a laboratory setting- we will ignore the temperature dependence
of the response.
The DrEVL spectral radiometer is designed to measure the radiance (𝐿 source (𝜆)) of lightning flashes
across different wavebands [1]. In order to support this field measurement, we must characterize
the spectral and radiometric response functions of each channel, that is, we must find 𝐺 𝑖 (𝜆) and 𝑅𝑖
in a controlled setting. Sections 2.2 and 2.3 describe the approaches we have taken to characterize
the spectral (𝐺 𝑖 (𝜆)) and radiometric (𝑅𝑖 ) response functions of each DrEVL channel.

2.2. Spectral response function

A calibration setup was constructed and employed to measure the spectral response of each DrEVL
channel across a wide range of wavelengths. Figure 2-1 presents a block diagram of this setup for
reference. A quartz-tungsten halogen (QTH) lamp was first coupled to the input slit of an imaging
spectrometer using 𝑓 -matched optics with 𝑓 = 3.9. The output slit of the spectrometer was set
such that the full-width half-maximum (FWHM) spectral bandwidth of the output light was 1 nm.
During measurement a given DrEVL channel was aligned to the output slit at an approximately
6 inch standoff distance. This distance ensured that the light output by the spectrometer would fill
the aperture of the channel.
Because a diffractive grating is used in our spectrometer, we had to take additional care to account for
higher-order diffraction. Higher-order diffraction is an optical phenomenon where comparatively
low wavelength light is mixed in with the output of a diffractive spectrometer when its grating is
positioned at a whole number multiple of that comparatively low wavelength [4]. For example,

2.2.1. Measurement of the DrEVL response

During testing, the DrEVL channel under test was regularly prompted to record its response in
counts using an onboard “snapshot” command, which collects and averages 10 000 samples. The
collected average and corresponding standard deviation were saved in a table corresponding to
each wavelength step to form a spectrally dependent response and noise characterization. At the
start of a measurement the spectrometer grating was moved to an initial wavelength position, and
the DrEVL unit was prompted to record the response of the channel under test. For each channel
the spectrometer grating was swept through the entire anticipated response wavelengths of the
photodiode detector material in 1 nm increments, and the channel response was recorded at each
wavelength. This spectral sweep almost always includes wavelengths that would be blocked by the
optics but were included for completeness. The wavelength ranges were to 300 nm to 1200 nm for
silicon channels and 800 nm to 1600 nm for InGaAs channels.
The need to account for higher-order diffraction resulted in each channel being measured multiple
times to ensure only first-order diffracted light was considered in the final results. For example, the
broadband silicon channel (CH00) was first measured from 300-610 nm with no filter, then from
590-760 nm with the FEL0600 filter installed on the spectrometer output, from 740-1060 nm with
the FEL0750 filter installed, then finally from 1040-1200 nm with the FEL1050 filter installed.
The resulting datasets were then stitched together at the cut-on wavelength on the filter to create a
single response curve.

2.2.2. Calculating spectral response function from DrEVL response and ground
 truth

Every measurement taken on a DrEVL system was then repeated on a calibrated power meter from
Thorlabs, resulting in a "ground truth" irradiance measurement that corresponded to the DrEVL
channel response measurements. The DrEVL channel responses (in counts) were then divided by
the ground truth irradiance values (in µW · cm−2 ·nm−1 ) at each wavelength to generate datasets of
the spectral irradiance responsivity of each channel (in counts · µW−1 ·cm2 ·nm). It is important to
note that neither the QTH lamp nor the ground truth power meter in this part of the calibration
process are NIST-traceable, instead they are traceable only to the manufacturer. To obtain a NIST-
traceable absolute radiometric responsivity measurement (i.e., system response where 𝐿 source (𝜆)
is NIST-traceable), we then took DrEVL units to a different laboratory with a NIST-traceable
integrating sphere source (see Section 2.3). Responsivity values taken during this measurement
were instead normalized to their individual peak values, creating a dataset capturing the normalized
spectral responsivity of each channel (see Figure 2-2) with nm resolution. This normalized value
is equivalent to 𝐺 𝑖 (𝜆) in Equation (2.1). Please note that prior to normalization these values could
have been converted to units of spectral radiance responsivity (in units counts · µW−1 ·cm2 ·sr · nm)
using Equation 2.3 (see Section 2.4). However, because the conversion from irradiance to radiance
is a linear division this conversion would be redundant with the normalization and was skipped.

of to their own maximum. A tabular output of this figure is provided in Appendix B of this

2.3.1. Measurement of the DrEVL response and calculation of the radiometric
 response function

During measurement the DrEVL unit was again prompted to record the response of each channel in
counts using the “snapshot” command. The recorded average and corresponding standard deviation
for each channel were saved to a table. This measurement was repeated for multiple illumination
configurations on the integrating sphere, generating a dataset where channel responses (𝑠𝑖 ) were
correlated to the different source radiance values (𝐿 source (𝜆)). With this data collected, it is possible
to solve Equation 2.2 for the radiometric response function (𝑅𝑖 ) of each channel.

2.4. Modifications to perform UV calibration

Calibration of the UV channel CH06 was performed much the same way as the non-UV channels;
however, instead of using a QTH source or the integrating sphere, a deuterium lamp (type L2D2
L6309 from Hamamatsu) was used as the source for both spectral and radiometric calibrations.
Except for the different source, the procedure for spectral calibration is identical to that described
in Section 2.2. Radiometric calibration was performed by placing the UV channel of a DrEVL
unit approximately 150 mm from the aperture of the deuterium lamp and recording the channel
response. Various neutral density (ND) filters were then placed between the source and the channel,
and the channel response was recorded at each interval.

Upon completion of the measurements, the DrEVL unit was swapped for a calibrated UV power
meter (S130VC from Thorlabs) with the same filter as the DrEVL UV channel. The measurements

2.5. Calibration validation

At this point, all system-specific parameters (𝐺 𝑖 (𝜆) and 𝑅𝑖 ) have been either directly measured
or calculated, fully calibrating the system. To confirm the accuracy of the calibration we can
validate it by using the DrEVL system to predict the total radiance (𝐿 𝑠𝑜𝑢𝑟𝑐𝑒 ) in the spectral range
of a given channel (𝐺 𝑖 (𝜆)) of a known source not used in the calibration process. To validate the
performance of non-UV channels we again employed the integrating sphere, however we used a
different lamp than was used during the calibration process, providing us with a different NIST-
traceable radiometric spectrum. We aligned the DrEVL unit to the integrating sphere as described
in Section 2.3 and prompted recording using the “snapshot” command, collecting a measurement
of the new lamp across all non-UV channels. Separately, we solved Equation 2.1 using the known
output of this new lamp as 𝐿 𝑠𝑜𝑢𝑟𝑐𝑒 , using the system-specific 𝐺 𝑖 (𝜆) and 𝑅𝑖 values found during
the calibration process. We then compared the measured and predicted responses to calculate the
difference between the values and found that they were within approximately 3% of each other for
all channels. This data can be found in the “Radiometric validation” column of Table 2-2.
To further improve confidence in the system, we also validated using the pre-normalized spectral
response function found in Section 2.2. This validation was done by first converting ground
truth irradiance measurements to radiance using Equation 2.3, where 𝜃 was the half angle of the
output of the imaging spectrometer. Measured system response could then be divided by this
ground truth radiance value to calculate the absolute spectral responsivity of each channel with nm
resolution. From here, it was possible to multiply each channel’s absolute spectral responsivity by
the radiometric spectrum of the new lamp to predict the output of the calibrated DrEVL unit. Using
this approach we found that the measured and predicted responses were again within approximately
3% of each other for all channels. This agreement demonstrates that the spectral response function
captured in Section 2.2 also appropriately captures the performance of the DrEVL unit. However,
since the setup lacks NIST-traceability it is still less preferable than the approach described in
Section 2.3. The data for this approach can be found in the “Spectral validation” column of
Table 2-2.

presents a handful of blackbodies from 4000 to 6000 K, which emit significant proportions of visible
and NIR light as part of their spectral radiance. By measuring the radiance across several narrow
and broad spectral bands and assuming a Planck distribution it becomes possible to reconstruct the
shape of the signal. This technique can be used for remote pyrometric measurements of blackbody
sources.
