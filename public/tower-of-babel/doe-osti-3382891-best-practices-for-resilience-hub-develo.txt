FEBRUARY 2026

Executive Summary
The Carbon League and its community partners in East St. Louis, Illinois, have identified five facilities to
serve as resilience hubs. These hubs are intended to support the local community through a range of
services and resources during blue-sky (everyday), gray-sky (pre-event), and black-sky (emergency)
conditions. Transforming these facilities into fully functional resilience hubs requires a broad operational
improvement and programmatic planning roadmap. This memo outlines best practices to guide the
development of these resilience hubs in East St. Louis, including recommendations for infrastructure
services; safety and physical protection; community services; operational protocols; and a phased
implementation strategy aligned with realistic funding and capacity constraints. Infrastructure
recommendations include strengthening electric power, communications, water, sanitation, and
transportation/logistics capabilities, all of which are essential for hubs that may serve as cooling and
warming centers, distribution points, and information hubs during emergencies. Safety recommendations
focus on accessibility, emergency action planning, indoor air quality, and secure storage of critical
equipment.
A phased roadmap provides guidance from immediate, low-cost readiness actions to long-term
optimization and community integration. Performance metrics and maintenance protocols ensure
continuous improvement and operational readiness. This guidance draws on best practices that can be
used to support the development of resilient, community-centered hubs capable of enhancing public
safety, health, and well-being during everyday operations and emergencies alike.

1. Project Overview
East St. Louis, Illinois, is receiving technical assistance from Argonne National Laboratory under the U.S.
Department of Energy (DOE) Energy to Communities (E2C) Expert Match Program to support local
resilience planning and community infrastructure improvements. The Carbon League, a community-based
organization, requested this technical assistance in establishing five facilities that will serve as resilience
hubs in neighborhoods facing challenges in hazard mitigation and emergency preparedness, including
constrained infrastructure capacities, limited local resources, and an aging housing stock increasingly
vulnerable to flooding events. The Carbon League is coordinating across city agencies, utilities, health
organizations, and community groups to develop a strategy that incorporates retrofit planning, housing
resilience, and other related community services [1][2].
This memo provides a framework for strengthening five preselected resilience hubs in East St. Louis,
with an emphasis on establishing the services, systems, and functions needed to support residents during a
wide range of conditions. The hubs, which include church and school facilities, will serve neighborhoods
experiencing concentrated hardship, high vulnerability to flooding hazards, and limited access to
conventional emergency resources. A detailed and actionable plan is necessary to ensure that these
facilities are capable of supporting residents not only during acute disruptions but also in daily
community needs [1][3].
 • Purpose of the memo. This document outlines a strategy for resilience hub development that
 incorporates physical infrastructure upgrades, operational redundancies, community service
 delivery models, and phased implementation pathways. Although the sites for these hubs have
 already been identified, a coordinated and layered approach to safety, infrastructure continuity,
 and service delivery is required to ensure these hubs meet the needs of the community [3].
 • Need for a holistic approach. Because resilience hubs must operate under both everyday and
 emergency conditions, the facilities must be designed to provide continuity of lifeline services
 even when neighboring infrastructure is disrupted. DOE and Federal Emergency Management
 Agency (FEMA) guidance note that resilience requires integration across energy,

 communications, transportation, and water systems to prevent cascading failures and ensure
 continuity of critical functions [4][7]. A facility hardened against flood hazards but lacking
 redundant power or equipped with energy systems but lacking indoor air-quality controls will not

 • Scope of recommendations. The memo’s recommendations span four principal categories: (1)

 dependable logistics capabilities, protected critical equipment, and responsive programming for

 • Alignment with E2C program goals. The E2C Expert Match Program emphasizes light-touch,

 communities with the tools to advance their own energy resilience goals. This memo aligns with
 those objectives by delivering practical, immediately usable recommendations that reflect both
 recognized best practices and the specific vulnerabilities of East St. Louis [3].

2. Resilience Hub Characteristics
Resilience hubs are multipurpose, community-serving facilities that support residents during emergencies
while providing relevant resources and services during normal conditions. They are designed to
strengthen social cohesion, reduce vulnerability to hazards, enhance community capacity, and maintain
essential lifeline services during disruptions. Guidance from USDN, DOE, FEMA, and the National
Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) consistently emphasizes that resilience hubs must integrate
infrastructure resilience with social resilience, ensuring that the facility is both technically robust and
socially embedded [1][3][4][24].
 • Community-centered design. A resilience hub is successful only when it is grounded in
 community requirements, trust, and participation. USDN highlights that hubs function most
 effectively when community members are engaged in decision-making, programming, and
 governance. This involvement ensures that hubs reflect local histories, norms, and priorities,
 promoting a sense of ownership and increasing the likelihood of use during emergencies.
 Community-centered design also supports better governance and improved outcomes in
 neighborhoods affected by risk [1].
 • Multipurpose functionality. Unlike conventional shelters or emergency sites, resilience hubs
 operate throughout the year. During blue-sky conditions, they serve as community assets offering
 digital access, education, workforce development, after-school activities, health workshops, and
 meeting spaces. FEMA research demonstrates that facilities with established everyday uses are
 more likely to serve as trusted emergency resources because residents feel familiar with the space
 and staff [4]. This dual function strengthens both preparedness and community cohesion.
 • Service-oriented infrastructure. Resilience hubs must maintain access to electricity, clean
 water, sanitation, communications, and transportation even when local infrastructure is
 compromised. DOE guidance stresses that hubs should be equipped with islandable power
 systems (i.e., photovoltaics [PV] coupled with battery energy storage systems [BESS]) that
 sustain critical loads during outages. Communications infrastructure should also include
 redundant pathways to ensure connectivity when cellular networks fail or overload [3][7].
 • Access-focused operations. Hubs should be physically accessible in line with the requirements
 of the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) (e.g., compliant entrances, safe parking lots, clear
 signage) and socially accessible (e.g., multilingual materials, culturally appropriate

 programming). FEMA and USDN emphasize that resilience hub design should components that
 enable every member of the community that might potentially require services to be able
 physically access and understand information around and within these facilities [1][4].

3. Context for East St. Louis Resilience Hubs
East St. Louis faces persistent infrastructural, socioeconomic, and environmental challenges that heighten
vulnerability to extreme weather hazards and infrastructure disruptions. These challenges make the
development of resilience hubs particularly critical, as hubs can address both short-term emergency needs
and underlying disparities affecting health, safety, and well-being. The city’s infrastructure condition,
social vulnerability indicators, and hazard exposure patterns all reinforce the need for robust and
accessible resilience hubs [5][6][20].
 • Flood hazard exposure. Many areas of East St. Louis lie within historical floodplains or
 experience recurrent flooding due to insufficient stormwater drainage, outdated pumping systems,
 and proximity to the Mississippi River. Flood events frequently damage electrical equipment,
 building interiors, and heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems, rendering
 facilities unusable precisely when residents most need shelter and services. The Centers for
 Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration
 (NOAA) guidance recommends elevating critical equipment above base flood elevation,
 installing barriers, and sealing penetrations to reduce flood impacts [5][6]. These safety measures
 are essential to ensuring operational continuity at resilience hubs.
 • Aging infrastructure and energy insecurity. Much of East St. Louis’ building stock is outdated
 and in disrepair, with aging electrical panels, compromised HVAC systems, damaged roofs, and
 limited insulation. DOE resilience studies indicate that communities with aging infrastructure
 face higher likelihoods of extended outages, equipment failure, indoor health hazards, and costly
 energy burdens [3]. These risks disproportionately affect low-income residents who cannot afford
 repairs or backup systems, making resilience hubs a critical lifeline for sheltering, cooling, device
 charging, and communications.
 • Transportation and mobility vulnerabilities. Major arterial roads frequently become
 impassable during severe storms due to flooding, snow, or debris. U.S. Department of
 Transportation (DOT) emergency transportation guidance indicates that communities without
 robust mobility networks face barriers to reaching emergency services, distribution centers, and
 healthcare providers [20]. As such, resilience hubs must incorporate transportation and logistics
 planning to ensure that residents, staff, volunteers, and supply trucks can safely access the site.
 • Socioeconomic and public health disparities. East St. Louis experiences high poverty rates,
 chronic disease prevalence, lower rates of vehicle ownership, and elevated energy burdens.
 USDN and FEMA both highlight that social vulnerability significantly increases risk during
 extreme events, particularly when access to cooling, sanitation, communications, and medical
 device charging is limited [1][4]. These disproportionate impacts make resilience hubs crucial for
 ensuring access to life-saving services during disruptions.
 • Existing community-based leadership. The selected hubs (churches and one school) are trusted
 institutions embedded in their neighborhoods. FEMA and USDN note that hubs anchored in faith-
 based or community-based institutions often perform exceptionally well because they leverage
 existing relationships, volunteer networks, and communication channels. Residents are more
 likely to seek support from familiar institutions during crises, making these facilities strong
 candidates for hub development [1][4].

4. Facility Considerations for Implementation
Establishing a functional resilience hub requires detailed assessment of each facility's structural
conditions, environmental risk exposure, circulation patterns, and operational readiness. Even though sites
have already been selected, the design and implementation of energy, communications, water, sanitation,
and logistical upgrades must be informed by these baseline considerations. National guidance from
FEMA, DOE, and the National Laboratory of the Rockies emphasizes that resilience infrastructure cannot
succeed without alignment between facility conditions and the resilience functions expected of the
hub.[3][4][7]
 • Structural readiness. Facility conditions determine whether resilience equipment, especially PV,
 BESS, HVAC improvements, and sanitation upgrades, can be safely installed and operated. DOE
 laboratory guidance recommends comprehensive assessments of roof integrity, load-bearing
 capacity, electrical panel condition, grounding, and wiring age before microgrid equipment is
 mounted or connected.[7][9] In East St. Louis, where many buildings are older and may have
 deferred maintenance, it is critical to verify that roofs can support PV arrays, that electrical panels
 have sufficient breaker space for critical load circuits, and that wiring is up to code. Structural
 modifications may be required to support heavier equipment or to elevate critical systems above
 projected flood levels. These upgrades not only enhance facility safety but also reduce the risk of
 equipment damage during extreme weather events or prolonged outages.
 • Environmental exposure. East St. Louis experiences recurring flood events due to riverine
 flooding, inadequate drainage, and aging stormwater systems. CDC and NOAA guidance
 recommends elevating electrical equipment, including inverters, battery storage units,
 communication panels, and HVAC controls, well above historical and projected flood
 elevations.[5][6] Facilities should assess water intrusion pathways, seal penetrations, and ensure
 that mechanical equipment is not located below grade. Additionally, facilities should consider
 windborne debris, hail exposure, and extreme temperature fluctuations, all of which can damage
 PV panels, batteries, and exterior conduits. This environmental exposure assessment should be
 revisited regularly as extreme weather conditions evolve and community hazard maps are
 updated.
 • Accessibility, parking, and pedestrian flow. For resilience hubs to serve as reliable resource
 distribution centers during emergencies, they must allow efficient vehicle and pedestrian
 circulation. DOT’s emergency transportation operations guidance stresses the importance of
 ADA-compliant access routes, clearly marked pedestrian lanes, and designated loading/unloading
 areas for trucks delivering water, food, or sanitation supplies.[20] In a high-demand scenario,
 hubs may need to process large numbers of residents arriving on foot, by car, or via paratransit
 services. Clear signage, directional pathways, and lighting redundancy improve safety during
 nighttime operations or during gray-sky conditions when power may be unstable.
 • Internal circulation and service zoning. The internal layout of the hub should support cooling
 and warming services, device charging, hydration stations, volunteer staging, medical triage, and
 information distribution. FEMA guidance on mass care highlights the importance of
 distinguishing “clean zones” for vulnerable individuals, designated intake areas, and
 sheltering/supply distribution areas to prevent congestion and confusion during emergencies.[17]
 In East St. Louis facilities that may have limited large open spaces, modular reconfiguration,
 including movable dividers, collapsible tables, or temporary barriers, can support multifunction
 operations across changing conditions.
 • Operational readiness and staffing capacity. Facility considerations must include an
 understanding of staff familiarity with building systems, emergency procedures, and equipment
 operation. Research by NIST and the U.S. Department of Homeland Security (DHS) underscores
 that facilities best able to maintain continuity during crises are those where operators, volunteers,

 4

 and community members routinely practice drills, understand building systems, and are
 empowered to activate emergency protocols.[24][25] In this context, the East St. Louis hubs
 should plan for regular training, documentation reviews, and scheduled testing of all systems.
 • Training on Incident Command System. The Incident Command System structure supports
 organized operations across all three modes. Key roles include hub coordinator, facilities/energy
 lead, communications lead, volunteer manager, and safety officer. Regular testing, drills, and
 equipment inspections are required for operational readiness. NIST and DHS recommend
 quarterly exercises and after-action reviews to reinforce continuous improvement and identify
 gaps [24][25]. Activation triggers should follow National Weather Service alert categories and
 utility outage notifications [21].

5. Safety Measures
Safety measures reduce a facility’s vulnerability to hazards by ensuring protection of the building, its
occupants, and critical equipment. They are the foundation on which resilience measures are built:
Without protection against hazards, redundant systems may fail prematurely, become inaccessible, or
expose the community to risk. FEMA, DOE, and USDN all emphasize that hardening, particularly for
facilities in flood- or outage-prone areas, is essential for resilience hub functionality.[1][3][16]
 • Structural and environmental hardening. Structural hardening measures strengthen the
 building envelope against wind, water, and extreme temperature fluctuations. Recommended
 improvements include roof reinforcement, installation of impact-resistant window films, sealing
 of wall penetrations and utility conduits, and installation of secondary drainage systems. These
 measures reduce water infiltration risks and protect interior assets from windborne debris.
 According to DOE guidance, facilities intended to host critical energy systems such as PV and
 BESS must maintain a structurally sound roof and wall assembly to reduce the likelihood of
 equipment detachment, leaks, or thermal instability.[7][9]
 • Flood protection and equipment elevation. Because flood hazards are a known risk in East St.
 Louis, the elevation of critical electrical and mechanical equipment is essential. FEMA
 recommends elevating systems to at least 3 ft above base flood elevation or placing equipment on
 higher floors when feasible.[4] Flood barriers, such as deployable flood gates, can protect
 entryways while backflow preventers reduce sewer infiltration. Electrical rooms should be sealed,
 ventilation openings screened, and sump pumps connected to backed-up circuits. These measures
 ensure that the facility remains operational when many surrounding buildings cannot.
 • Physical security and access control. During emergencies, community facilities can become
 focal points for high traffic, resource distribution, or periods of heightened stress. Security
 measures, including exterior lighting with battery backup, reinforced doors, high-security locks,
 and optional closed-circuit television (CCTV), reduce the likelihood of theft, vandalism, or
 accidental damage to critical equipment. DHS guidance stresses the importance of controlled
 access zones during resource distribution to limit crowding and ensure effective allocation.[12]
 Facilities should implement visitor registration protocols during black-sky operations and
 maintain volunteer identification systems.
 • Indoor environmental quality (IEQ) and thermal protection. FEMA and the American
 Society of Heating, Refrigerating and Air-Conditioning Engineers (ASHRAE) recommend that
 resilience hubs maintain safe air quality even when the power grid is compromised.[15][16]
 Portable high efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters, designated clean-air rooms, and backup-
 powered ventilation systems minimize exposure to airborne contaminants during wildfires,
 chemical spills, or extreme heat events. Adequate insulation and weatherization can extend the
 duration of passive survivability such that the facility remains habitable without powered heating
 or cooling for a limited period.

These safety improvements ensure the survival of the hub’s infrastructure during extreme events. Without
them, even high-quality PV, BESS, or communications systems may fail due to water damage,
overheating, wind damage, or security breaches. FEMA emphasizes that safety measures are most
effective when combined with operational plans, regular maintenance, and hazard-based
prioritization.[4][16]

6. Resilience Measures
Resilience measures ensure that hubs remain operational when primary systems fail. These measures
provide continuity of lifeline infrastructure services (i.e., electricity, water, sanitation, communications,
and transportation) during outages or hazards. National laboratory and federal guidance emphasize that
resilience depends on redundancy, diversity of systems, and alternative pathways for service
continuity.[3][7][11]
6.1. Electric Power Resilience
 • Critical considerations. DOE identifies electricity as the most essential lifeline service for
 resilience hubs because nearly all other services, including communications, water pumping,
 HVAC, indoor air quality, refrigeration, and lighting, depend on power.[3][7] East St. Louis’
 aging grid infrastructure increases the probability of outages, voltage drops, and long restoration
 times. Therefore, identifying and separating Tier 1 critical loads, including medical equipment,
 communications, refrigeration, lighting, HVAC for safe rooms, and charging circuits, is essential
 to ensuring hub operability during grid failures.
 • PV-plus-BESS integration. Solar PV coupled with BESS provides clean, reliable backup power,
 reduces operational costs, and offers significant autonomy during outages. DOE recommends
 designing systems to maintain at least 8–12 hours of autonomy for Tier 1 loads, with the potential
 for extended runtime through solar recharging.[3] In the East St. Louis hubs, the pre-estimated
 costs for PV ($X) and BESS ($Y) provide a baseline for implementation, but safety measures
 such as equipment elevation, ventilation, and secure mounting are prerequisites for installation.
 • Backup generators and islanding strategies. Islanding capability ensures that the hub remains
 powered even when the surrounding grid fails. The Institute of Electrical and Electronics
 Engineers (IEEE) standards for backup (and, eventually, microgrids) safety require that transfer
 switches, critical load panels, and inverters be configured to isolate the hub’s circuits.[10] This
 arrangement must be paired with clear hub-specific operational protocols so staff know how to
 activate, monitor, and troubleshoot the system.
6.2. Communications Resilience
 • Redundant communications pathways. Communications systems are often the first to fail
 during extended outages, particularly when cellular networks become congested. DHS and its
 Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) recommend layering communications
 pathways (e.g., Wi-Fi, cellular boosters, radios, hotspots, and satellite devices) to ensure
 continuity.[11][12] For example, a resilience hub should maintain portable hotspot kits, handheld
 radios for on-site coordination, and at least one satellite messaging device for black-sky
 scenarios.
 • Device charging as a core service. Many residents rely on phones for communication,
 navigation, and access to emergency updates. Therefore, charging stations must be tied to backed-
 up circuits. FEMA mass care guidance identifies charging as a “top-tier essential service” in
 emergency shelters and resilience hubs because communication breakdowns can increase
 isolation, anxiety, and health risk.[17]

 6

6.3. Water and Sanitation Resilience
 • Potable water access. The Environmental Protection Agency’s (EPA’s) drinking water resilience
 and response guidance emphasizes the need for potable water redundancy when grid-dependent
 pumping or treatment systems fail.[13] Facilities should maintain portable water storage,
 filtration systems, and a plan for bulk water deliveries. Hydration stations should be positioned
 near cooling or warming areas to manage heat-related illness risks.
 • Sanitation continuity. CDC and FEMA highlight sanitation continuity as a core resilience
 function.[14][16] When wastewater pumping or sewer systems fail, hubs must rely on portable
 toilets or hygiene stations to maintain safety standards. Facilities with electric pumps must tie
 these systems to backed-up circuits.
 • IEQ. ASHRAE emergency sheltering standards recommend HEPA filtration, humidity
 management, and temperature stabilization to protect vulnerable populations during outages.[15]
 Backup-powered HVAC systems or “safe rooms” can significantly reduce heat- or cold-related
 health risks.
6.4. Transportation and Logistics Resilience
 • Access routes for residents and staff. DOT guidance indicates that transportation continuity is
 essential for effective service delivery.[20] Hubs must maintain accessible pathways, ADA-
 compliant entrances, and clear signage to manage high volumes of residents during emergencies.
 • Loading and staging areas. FEMA’s Community Points of Distribution (CPOD) Manual
 provides detailed layouts for truck circulation, pallet staging, vehicle queues, and resource
 handoff points.[19] Hubs should designate areas for receiving supplies, distributing goods, and
 managing traffic safely during black-sky operations.

7. Community Programs
Community programs form the public-facing core of resilience hubs. These services must be designed to
support residents in blue-sky, gray-sky, and black-sky conditions. FEMA and USDN emphasize that
successful resilience hubs provide continuous services, not merely emergency services, because daily
engagement builds familiarity and trust, which are essential during crises.[1][4]
7.1 Blue-Sky Services
 • Community programming. Hubs should provide educational workshops, after-school activities,
 community meals, and health screenings during normal operations. These services help reduce
 social isolation, strengthen community cohesion, and increase residents’ awareness of hub
 functions.[1]
 • Preparedness education. FEMA and DOE highlight the importance of everyday preparedness
 education, such as home energy safety, emergency kit preparation, and severe weather awareness,
 to improve community readiness.[4][3]
7.2 Gray-Sky Services
 • Resource staging and readiness. During periods of elevated risk, such as severe weather
 watches or flood warnings, hubs should pre-position supplies, test equipment, notify volunteers,
 and prepare communication materials. CISA recommends testing communications pathways
 during these periods.[11]
 • Cooling/warming preparation. Hubs must prepare hydration stations, safe rooms, and backup
 HVAC systems for activation during extreme heat or cold events.[15]

 7

7.3 Black-Sky Services
 • Emergency sheltering. FEMA mass care guidance requires hubs to support cooling/warming,
 hydration, sanitation access, and first aid during disruptions.[17] Even if hubs do not serve as
 overnight shelters, they are expected to provide life-sustaining services.
 • Charging, communications, and information. Hubs offer device charging, Wi-Fi hotspots, and
 information centers that distribute updates and support residents in accessing benefits or
 assistance.[12]
 • Distribution and logistics. Drawing from FEMA CPOD guidance, hubs may distribute food,
 water, sanitation kits, and medical supplies. Clear routing minimizes congestion and ensures
 effective service delivery.[19]

8. Phased Implementation Roadmap
The phased implementation roadmap provides a structured sequence for advancing resilience hubs from
initial readiness through full operational capability and long-term integration into community
infrastructure. Phased approaches align with DOE and FEMA best practices, which emphasize
prioritizing actions that reduce the greatest risks early while structuring long-term investments in ways
that reflect available funding, workforce capacity, and community readiness.[3][4] A phased plan ensures
that resilience hubs can deliver meaningful benefits even before all improvements are completed,
particularly in resource-constrained communities like East St. Louis.
Because funding availability, contractor capacity, and internal staffing constraints are real considerations,
a phased approach enables hubs to address the most critical vulnerabilities first. DOE’s Energy
Transitions Initiative Playbook stresses that phasing is essential for small or under-resourced
communities, allowing them to scale upgrades over time without losing strategic direction.[22] In East St.
Louis, phasing allows hubs to focus initially on critical load support, basic communications resilience,
and flood protection while planning for more complex upgrades such as full microgrids or advanced
logistics capabilities in later phases.
8.1. Phase 1: Immediate Actions
Phase 1 focuses on actions that require minimal investment but deliver rapid improvements in safety,
awareness, and operational readiness. These steps reduce the likelihood of catastrophic failure during
early disruptions and establish the foundational understanding required for more substantial upgrades.
 • Critical load inventory. Identifying and mapping critical loads (e.g., lighting, refrigeration,
 communications, HVAC zones, medical devices) is essential for designing PV-plus-BESS
 systems and ensuring emergency continuity. DOE guidance stresses that critical load
 identification is a prerequisite for backup generator implementation and islanding capability.[3]
 • Transfer switch installation and electrical readiness. Installing manual or automatic transfer
 switches allows the facility to safely isolate circuits during outages and connect backup sources.
 This is an essential first step before any BESS installation and prevents backfeeding hazards.[10]
 • Water access and sanitation readiness. Hubs should identify potable water sources, confirm
 restrooms’ ability to function without grid power, and develop contingency solutions (portable
 toilets, hygiene stations). EPA and CDC recommend preparing basic water resilience measures
 before extended outages occur.[13][14]
 • Basic security and exterior lighting. Installing or verifying battery-backed exterior lighting
 improves safety for nighttime operations and supports resource distribution during emergencies.
 FEMA mass care guidelines note that lighting is one of the most critical support functions during
 emergency sheltering.[17]

 8

 • Staff and volunteer familiarization. Staff should be briefed on hub functions, emergency
 procedures, and communication channels. NIST emphasizes training as a key early-phase activity
 to support continuity and resilience.[24]
8.2. Phase 2: Short-Term Improvements
Phase 2 builds on the foundation established in Phase 1 by strengthening the facility’s ability to operate
during moderate disruptions. Actions in this phase typically involve moderate financial investment, but
deliver substantial gains in operational reliability.
 • Solar-ready wiring and partial BESS installation. Wiring upgrades (e.g., conduit installation,
 new breaker panels, grounding improvements, and critical load subpanels) prepare the facility for
 PV and BESS. Partial BESS enables limited autonomy, supporting lighting, communications, and
 essential HVAC loads.[7]
 • Initial deployment of PV/BESS/electric vehicle (EV) infrastructure. The preliminary cost
 estimates for solar PV, BESS, and EV charging can guide early procurement decisions. Even
 small PV-plus-BESS systems improve resilience and reduce operational costs.
 • Filtration systems and cooling/warming readiness. Installing high-quality filtration systems
 (e.g., MERV-13) and portable cooling/warming units supports IEQ, as recommended by
 ASHRAE for emergency sheltering.[15]
 • Parking lot circulation improvements. Marking lanes, adding ADA-compliant signage, and
 improving drop-off points enable more efficient gray- and black-sky operations. DOT guidance
 highlights circulation as a low-cost improvement with major operational benefits.[20]
 • Installation of basic access control measures. Reinforced doors, lock upgrades, and secure
 storage for equipment reduce risk during emergencies. DHS emphasizes early investment in
 physical security for community-serving facilities.[12]
8.3. Phase 3: Medium-Term Improvements
Phase 3 establishes the resilience hub as a fully capable emergency resource center with the infrastructure
needed to maintain operations during significant disruptions.
 • Islandable solar-plus-storage systems. Fully integrated PV-plus-BESS with automated transfer
 switches provide reliable backup power. DOE guidance indicates that islandable microgrids offer
 significantly improved resilience compared to standalone backup generators, especially in
 communities prone to repeated outages.[3]
 • Advanced communications redundancy. Satellite messaging units, high-gain cellular boosters,
 and radio networks enable communication when local infrastructure collapses. CISA emphasizes
 communications redundancy as essential for emergency coordination and public information.[11]
 • Hardened loading zones and logistics staging. Installing bollards, signage, reinforced
 pavement, and lighting supports truck access and nighttime distribution. FEMA’s CPOD manual
 provides templates for distribution site layout that can be applied here.[19]
 • Water storage, sanitation upgrades, and backup pumping. Permanent water storage tanks,
 improved filtration units, and sanitation systems powered by backed-up circuits provide essential
 services during extended outages. EPA and CDC emphasize that water and sanitation continuity
 is a core determinant of community health during emergencies.[13][14]
 • Flood-protected equipment installation. BESS, HVAC controls, electrical panels, and
 communication devices should be elevated or otherwise hardened to withstand anticipated flood
        conditions.[5][6]

8.4. Phase 4: Long-Term Improvements
Phase 4 ensures long-term resilience, continuous improvement, and integration into broader community
infrastructure and governance.
 • Local workforce development. Local workforce capacity is critical for long-term system
 maintenance and community ownership.[22] Training programs in solar installation, battery
 maintenance, HVAC, or electrical systems can be integrated into hub operations.
 • Expansion of IEQ and environmental controls. Adding real-time IEQ monitors, automated
 filtration, and zoned HVAC enhances protection for vulnerable residents during extreme heat or
 wildfire events.[15]
 • Inter-hub coordination and data dashboards. A network of hubs sharing energy data, resource
 availability, and operational status supports citywide resilience, consistent with NIST’s system-
 level resilience goals.[24]
 • Institutionalized partnership agreements. Collaboration with the city, the county, utilities, and
 nonprofits can formalize supply deliveries, communications protocols, and disaster logistics
       roles.[23]
 • Long-term modernization and capital upgrades. Structural retrofits, roof replacement,
 mechanical system modernization, and advanced microgrid controls transform hubs into enduring
 resilience assets.

9. Performance, Maintenance, and Continuous Improvement
The resilience hub system must incorporate structured performance metrics, maintenance protocols, and
continuous improvement processes. Federal guidance from NIST, DHS, and FEMA stresses that
resilience is a long-term practice driven by routine evaluation and system testing.[16][24][25]
 • Performance metrics. Metrics should evaluate autonomy hours (energy), communications
 uptime, water availability, transportation throughput, HVAC reliability, sanitation continuity,
 ADA accessibility, and effective service delivery. NIST recommends aligning performance
 indicators with community-defined outcomes and hazard-specific needs.[24]
 • Maintenance protocols. Regular PV inspections, BESS cycling, generator testing, water
 filtration maintenance, drainage clearing, lighting checks, and HVAC maintenance are essential
 to operational readiness. EPA and CDC emphasize sanitation system maintenance as a core
 requirement for emergency operations.[13][14]
 • Life-cycle costs, warranties, and financial planning. Planning for resilience hub upgrades
 should explicitly account for long-term life-cycle costs, including routine maintenance,
 component replacement, software subscriptions, etc. Capital planning should incorporate
 manufacturer warranties and operations and maintenance requirements as well as insurance
 premiums, liability coverage, and local tax assessments associated with improvements.[3][24]
 • Continuous improvement cycle. FEMA and DHS recommend a structured after-action review
 process after exercises or real events.[16][25] Findings should inform annual hub updates, staff
 training, and equipment replacement planning.
 • Mutual aid and interjurisdictional coordination. Resilience hubs should be part of formal and
 informal mutual aid frameworks that enable both the receipt and provision of assistance during
 emergencies, including sharing supplies, staff, or facilities with neighboring jurisdictions. Pre-
 established mutual aid and coordination protocols improve response efficiency and support
 broader recovery efforts when capacity allows.[4][23]
