Giant mediastinal lipoma with bilateral hemithoracic extension in a young woman: a case report
Authors: Reza Jalli, Bijan Ziaian, Asal Seifaei, Seyed Sina Dehghani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMediastinal lipomas are rare benign tumors that usually remain asymptomatic until they reach a considerable size. Giant mediastinal lipomas can cause progressive respiratory symptoms and present diagnostic challenges by mimicking malignant lipomatous tumors.Case presentationWe report a 33-year-old Iranian woman presenting with progressive dyspnea for one year. Chest radiography and contrast-enhanced CT revealed a giant, well-encapsulated fat-density mass in the anterior mediastinum, measuring approximately 214 × 123 × 159 mm, extending to the diaphragmatic level and into the contralateral hemithorax, without invasive features. The patient underwent complete surgical excision via median sternotomy. Intraoperatively, a large encapsulated fatty mass with minimal adhesions and no signs of invasion was identified. Histopathological examination confirmed a benign lipoma composed of mature adipocytes. The postoperative course was uneventful, with complete resolution of symptoms.ConclusionsGiant mediastinal lipomas are rare but should be considered in the differential diagnosis of fat-containing mediastinal masses. Cross-sectional imaging plays a pivotal role in diagnosis, while complete surgical resection provides definitive treatment with excellent outcomes.

Background

Lipomas are common benign mesenchymal neoplasms composed of mature adipocytes, most frequently arising in the subcutaneous tissues of the trunk and extremities. However, mediastinal lipomas are rare neoplasms accounting for less than 2% of all mediastinal tumors and less than 0.1% of all thoracic neoplastic lesions [1]. These tumors typically remain asymptomatic for prolonged periods and are often discovered incidentally on imaging studies performed for unrelated conditions [2]. When symptomatic, they are usually associated with mass effect from their size, leading to respiratory complaints such as dyspnea, chest discomfort, or cough [3]. Despite their benign histology, giant mediastinal lipomas can pose significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges because of their potential to mimic malignant liposarcomas radiologically [4]. Cross-sectional imaging plays a critical role in differentiation, with computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) being particularly valuable for assessing fat density, delineating margins, and identifying suspicious components [5]. Herein, we report a case of a young female patient with a giant mediastinal lipoma extending to the diaphragmatic level, who presented with progressive dyspnea. Giant mediastinal lipomas (including lesions exceeding 20 cm) have been reported in the literature, although such extensive bilateral involvement has been infrequently described [6, 7]. This case highlights the importance of considering lipomas in the differential diagnosis of mediastinal fat-containing masses and emphasizes the role of surgical excision as a definitive and curative treatment.

Case presentation

A 33-year-old Iranian woman with no previous medical history presented to the emergency department with progressive dyspnea for nearly one year, with gradual worsening over the past month. The patient also had no history of metabolic disorders (e.g., Cushing's syndrome) or corticosteroid use. On admission, her vital signs were stable, and physical examination was unremarkable except for reduced breath sounds over the lower lung zones. Vital signs were as follows: blood pressure 118/75 mmHg (measures in both upper extremities), respiratory rate 19–21 breaths/min, and pulse rate 70–74 beats/min. Cardiovascular examination revealed normal heart sounds without murmurs. No signs of superior vena cava syndrome, peripheral edema, or jugular venous distention were observed. Initial chest radiography demonstrated a large low-density mass lesion occupying most of the right hemithorax with some mass effect on the heart and mediastinum associated with ill-defined borders of the heart shadows (Fig. 1). Complementary contrast-enhanced CT images of the chest revealed a large and almost butterfly-shaped fat-containing mass lesion measuring approximately 214 × 123 × 159 mm in the mediastinum, mainly involving anterior and middle compartments (Figs. 2 and 3). The lesion extended laterally and inferiorly, reaching the anterior and posterior aspects of the right hemidiaphragm and the anterior aspect of the left hemidiaphragm. There was no evidence of pressure effect on adjacent organs. The lesion revealed homogeneous hypodensity without any soft tissue components. No signs of infiltration, bronchiectasis, emphysema, nodular opacity, or septal thickening were noted in the lungs. Great vessels and other mediastinal structures were normal. A few benign-appearing lymph nodes were observed in both axillary regions. The patient underwent median sternotomy under general anesthesia. Intraoperatively, a large, encapsulated fatty mass originating from the anterior mediastinum was identified. The tumor showed minimal adhesions to adjacent mediastinal structures, including the pericardium and bilateral pleura, without evidence of invasion. The lesion extended into the right and left hemithorax but maintained clear dissection planes. The mass was completely excised without intraoperative complications. Postoperatively, the patient recovered well and was discharged in stable condition. Histopathological examination of the resected specimen demonstrated a tumor composed of bland mature adipocytes without cytologic atypia or other features of malignancy, consistence with a benign lipoma (Fig. 4). The patient was followed for 18 months with chest radiographs performed every 6 months. Imaging demonstrated normal lung expansion with no residual mass, and her respiratory symptoms resolved completely (Fig. 5). Discussion and conclusions

Mediastinal lipomas are relatively rare entities that usually present in middle-aged adults, with no gender predilection [8]. Most patients remain asymptomatic until the lesion attains a large size, at which point symptoms arise from compression of adjacent thoracic structures. Dyspnea, chest tightness, and cough are the most frequently reported symptoms [9]. In our case, the patient experienced progressive dyspnea for one year, reflecting the slow-growing nature of the tumor.

Radiologically, lipomas appear as homogeneous, well-encapsulated, fat-attenuation lesions without enhancing soft tissue components on CT, consistent with the findings in our patient [10]. MRI can be particularly useful in doubtful cases, as fat suppression sequences confirm the fatty nature of the lesion and help exclude liposarcoma [11]. The absence of nodular or irregular enhancing components is crucial in differentiating benign lipomas from low-grade liposarcomas [12]. The management of mediastinal lipomas depends on their size, symptoms, and diagnostic certainty. Asymptomatic small lesions may be followed conservatively, but large or symptomatic lipomas, or those with indeterminate features, require surgical excision [13]. Both thoracotomy and video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) approaches have been described, with thoracotomy often preferred for giant lipomas due to their size and potential adherence to surrounding structures [14]. Our patient underwent successful open median sternotomy with complete excision. Definitive diagnosis in the present case was established by histopathologic evaluation, which demonstrated bland mature adipocytes without cytologic atypia or other malignant features, supporting the diagnosis of benign lipoma. The prognosis of mediastinal lipomas is excellent after complete resection, with recurrence being rare [15]. Although minimally invasive approaches such as VATS or lateral thoracotomy have been reported for mediastinal lipomas, these techniques are generally reserved for smaller and unilateral lesions. In the present case, the giant size of the tumor (> 20 cm) and its extension into the contralateral hemithorax necessitated a surgical approach that could provide wide bilateral exposure. Therefore, median sternotomy was considered the safest and most appropriate approach to ensure complete resection and avoid intraoperative complications. The patient remains asymptomatic after surgery with complete resolution of dyspnea.

Previously reported cases have shown that mediastinal lipomas may be discovered incidentally or present with symptoms related to mass effect, most commonly dyspnea and compression of adjacent thoracic structures [3, 9, 16]. More severe compressive manifestations have also been reported, including cardiovascular compression and respiratory compromise with cardiac arrest [17, 18]. Bilateral thoracic extension has also been reported in some giant lipomatous mediastinal lesions, although this pattern appears to be less commonly described than unilateral involvement. In the report by Chen et al., giant mediastinal lipomatous tumors involved both chest cavities, whereas Kummari et al. described a giant mediastino-pleural lipoma extending across the anterior mediastinum into both pleural spaces [6, 19]. In comparison, the present case was characterized by giant size, marked bilateral hemithoracic extension, and a nearly butterfly-like appearance on CT, without soft-tissue components or radiologic evidence of invasion. Differential diagnosis is also important in such cases, because bilateral or extensive fat-containing mediastinal lesions may not always represent a simple benign lipoma. Giant bilateral mediastinal lipomatous tumors may include both benign lipoma and well-differentiated liposarcoma, while omental herniation through a diaphragmatic defect may also mimic mediastinal lipoma on imaging [6, 20]. These observations further support the clinical relevance of the present case, particularly with regard to its bilateral extent, characteristic imaging appearance, and complete surgical resection.

The present case contributes to the literature by reporting a giant mediastinal lipoma and highlights the importance of recognizing its radiological hallmarks. The presence of bilateral and extensive adipose tissue in the mediastinum can sometimes occur due to a small defect in the diaphragm and upward extension of abdominal fat (without herniation of the visceral organs), therefore, careful evaluation of the diaphragm is essential, as management differs substantially.
