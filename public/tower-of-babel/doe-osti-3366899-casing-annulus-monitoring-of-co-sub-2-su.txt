 Extended Abstract
Effective and secure carbon subsurface storage, involving the deep underground injection of CO2 into
geological formations where it is permanently trapped, is paramount to mitigating CO2 emissions (Figure
I). Ensuring the integrity of these storage sites and detecting potential leakage through the casing annulus
necessitates robust monitoring. This work provides the first integrated demonstration of a wireless
casing-annulus monitoring architecture that can operate in highly attenuating cement-brine environments
relevant to CO₂ storage.

 Figure I: Illustration of CO2 injection into the subsurface and instrumentation behind the casing for in-situ leak detection.

This project focused on developing and validating a novel sensor system for integration with autonomous
monitoring near the cement reservoir interface. The goal was a fully integrated Technology Readiness Level
(TRL) 4/5 field validation of a distributed wireless intelligent sensor system providing real-time, direct
subsurface formation measurements to enhance fluid movement monitoring in the cemented casing annulus.
Achieving this objective required the development and integration of 1) wireless autonomous microsensor
technology by California Institute of Technology (Caltech); 2) sensor packaging and emplacement
technology by Research Triangle Institute (RTI); and 3) smart well completions using wireless active casing
collars and NOV pipe by the Sandia National Lab (SNL). The collaboration with the Caltech team in this
project aimed to develop millimeter-scale radio frequency identification (RFID) sensors capable of
detecting CO₂, pH, and/or methane levels. These sensors are engineered to be impervious to fluids, allowing
them to be mixed with cement and installed within the casing annulus. They operate using RFID protocols
at frequencies of 902–928 MHz for both power and communication. A Sandia National Laboratories’ team
engaged their expertise in the development of a Smart Collar system designed for the wireless data
collection from these RFID sensors embedded in the cement annulus and transmission of this information
to the ground surface via IntelliPipe/IntelliServ NOV drill pipe. This is accomplished through inductive
coupling at the collar, which facilitates data transfer through each segment of the pipe. Because the system

 6

cannot transmit a direct current signal to power the Smart Collar, both power and communication were
implemented using alternating current and electromagnetic signals at varying frequencies. Furthermore, the
developed microsensor technology had to be demonstrated and validated in comparison with reference
transducer measurements in a field test site at The University of Texas at Austin (UT-Austin). Although
the full sensor suite did not reach field-deployment readiness, the system-level integration achieved in this
project establishes a validated pathway for future incorporation of advanced microsensors.

To address Task 2, the Caltech team focused on developing and validating a System on a Chip (SoC) sensor
platform for monitoring supercritical CO2 sequestration environments. They designed, fabricated, and
modified these SoCs to withstand the harsh conditions of CO2 sequestration field experiments and to
directly measure critical subsurface parameters. The team advanced the SoC microsystems to TRL 4,
producing pH devices for functional lab validation. These test devices were provided to SNL for testing
with their Smart Collar system. The SoCs and test devices developed under this task were also supplied to
Tasks 3, 4, and 5, with technology performance reported to Task 1. A key aspect of this work involved
miniaturizing specialized microsensors to fit onto the 1.2 mm × 1.2 mm complementary metal oxide
semiconductor (CMOS) chip surface. pH sensors were optimized to be less than 0.5 mm in size, operating
within the voltage and current limits of the CMOS potentiostat (Figure II). The functionalization process,
such as the electro-polymerization of polyaniline for pH sensing, was chosen for its compatibility with the
wireless platform. This method allowed for the deposition of the specific emeraldine phase of polyaniline,
which is sensitive to pH changes through variations in conductivity. The chip's communication protocols
were also designed to handle multiple chips.

To fulfill Subtask 2.1, individual wired sensors were functionalized and demonstrated adequate sensitivity
in laboratory conditions. The Caltech team developed miniaturized temperature, pH, and CO2 sensors that
are compatible with 1 mm CMOS radiofrequency (RF) tags. Figure III shows calibration curves of pH and
CO2 sensors, taken on the lab-bench with wired electronic driving circuitry. The CO2 sensors, based on

 8

potentiostatic measurements from the solid electrolyte Na3Zr2Si2PO12 (NASICON) were found to be
sensitive to 1% CO2 concentrations, but also very sensitive to humidity. Consequently, these sensors are
unlikely to work in the wet environment envisioned for this project. The pH sensors, based on conductivity
changes in polyaniline with pH, were found to be very sensitive to pH, and robust at temperatures up to 80
°C and high-pressure environments.

 Figure III: Calibration curves of pH sensors (above) and CO2 gas sensors (below). These sensors were fabricated and bench-
 top tested and designed to be integrated onto CMOS wireless chips.

For Subtask 2.2, the developed wireless RF tag sensors at Caltech were tested for both pH and temperature
measurements. The temperature sensors were integrated directly onto CMOS wireless systems and
successfully measured temperature in American Petroleum Institute (API) brine at 100 °C. The temperature
range of these sensors was adjustable by selecting different materials (Germanium or Silicon) and
optimizing deposited layer thicknesses. The findings showed that thin poly-silicon conductors, deposited
onto the CMOS chips via evaporation, provided the highest sensitivity to temperature changes. These robust
wireless temperature sensors were validated on hotplates (Figure IV) and in boiling API brine. The team
further demonstrated communication functionality in 120 °C brine on a hotplate in the lab, and conducted
hotplate temperature measurements up to 175 °C. While temperature was measured with integrated CMOS
wireless chips using the RF tag power/communications readers, CO2 concentration was only measured at
room temperature in the lab under controlled humidity.

To fulfill Subtask 2.3, the Caltech team successfully demonstrated multiplexed communication between
the developed RF readers and multiple CMOS sensors. These sensors, when placed within the reader’s
antenna range, transmitted measured data, enabling a system where readers can interrogate several sensors
and consolidate data using RF tag addressing. This was visually represented by the tag signal and response
 10

signal from the CMOS tag, confirming communication between two wireless devices (Figure VI). The
Caltech team also confirmed wireless RF tag communication through a 2.54 cm-thick glass barrier with
both the reader and temperature sensors (Figure V). This result was independently reproduced by a group
at Boston University, which received one of Caltech’s RF tag readers. Lifetime tests on reader/sensor
systems for temperature sensors showed reliable performance up to 150 °C (Figure VII). While the
communication electronics on the RF tag may temporarily fail between 150 °C and 200 °C, the CMOS
sensor itself remains intact, allowing readings to be obtained once the temperature drops below 150 °C.
The Caltech team plans to complete lifetime measurements on the developed polyaniline pH sensors, which
have demonstrated high robustness at elevated pressures and temperatures up to 80 °C. The Caltech team
also supplied SNL temperature-sensitive microsensors. However, tests revealed that the communication
range between the microsensors and the Caltech reader was limited to millimeters when submerged in
brine-soaked cement. To address this, it was decided that larger microsensors would be explored to extend
the powering and communication range instead of producing more of the current microsensor chips. The
team is prepared to functionalize these larger microsensors with miniaturized pH and temperature sensing
capabilities.

Task 3 at RTI focused on developing and applying surface coatings to improve the downhole emplacement
and functionality of microsensors in subsurface reservoirs. The team addressed the core technical challenge
of accurately placing sensors against formation walls and maintaining their integrity in harsh conditions.
Through three subtasks, the team progressively advanced the development and deployment readiness of
surface coatings and encapsulation technologies for downhole microsensors, with each spanning sequential
budget periods to facilitate iterative development, validation, and scale-up.

To fulfill Subtask 3.1, RTI designed and applied functional coatings to enhance sensor durability, mobility,
and selective exposure. This involved three key areas:

• Superhydrophobic Nanoparticle (SHNP) Coatings: RTI developed a water-borne, nanoparticle-based
 superhydrophobic coating system. The team synthesized silica nanoparticles in two sizes (~30 nm and
 ~50 nm) using a sol-gel process, creating a self-assembling structure with controlled porosity and
 surface roughness. To achieve low surface energy, RTI incorporated perfluorinated functional groups
 using 1H,1H,2H,2H-perfluorodecyltriethoxysilane. They applied these coatings via a one-sided spray
 method, ensuring access to the sensing element. Contact angle testing confirmed a significant
 improvement in hydrophobic performance. RTI then successfully transferred this coating process to
 functional and phantom sensor substrates from Caltech collaborators.

• UV-Curable Fluorinated Acrylate Copolymer Coatings: To boost mechanical robustness and adhesion,
 RTI developed a UV-curable fluorinated acrylate system. They applied these coatings to diced Si₃N₄
 wafers and optimized them for a 5-minute UV cure cycle. Contact angle testing demonstrated excellent
 water repellency (126°) on coated wafers, a significant improvement over uncoated control (41°). RTI
 subsequently applied this acrylate formulation to actual microsensor packages (1 mm × 1 mm & 1 mm
 × 3 mm), observing no loss in sensor functionality after application.

• Magnetic Nanoparticle Integration: RTI extended its coating platform to include sub-120 nm ferrite
 nanoparticles, enabling guided sensor emplacement via magnetic manipulation. These
 superparamagnetic particles offer directional control under external magnetic fields and avoid
 agglomeration due to their field-dependent magnetism.

To accomplish Subtask 3.2, RTI advanced the testing and validation of the previously developed surface
coatings and encapsulation approaches. The team aimed to assess their impact on sensor mobility,
placement, and functional integrity in reservoir-representative conditions. Specifically, they sought to
validate how coated microsensors behaved in oilfield fluids, how magnetic properties could aid sensor
navigation, and how encapsulation could support sensor emplacement during downhole operations. This
work included:

• Mobility Testing of Coated Dummy Sensors: RTI used 2 mm × 2 mm silicon nitride dummy sensors to
 study how various coatings affected movement through deionized water (DI), seawater (SW), and oil
 simulant (Isopar-L). The team tested both one-sided and two-sided coating configurations. SHNP and
 silane coatings-maintained hydrophobicity, floating under static conditions in DI and SW, though they
 sank when disturbed. Two-sided silane coatings showed recoverable buoyancy, likely due to their
 uniform application. In contrast, acrylate coatings presented edge-bleed issues during curing, even with

 12

 pipette deposition, as their viscosity decreased at elevated temperatures, making consistent application
 challenging.

• Integration of Magnetic Nanoparticles: To enable guided mobility using magnetic fields, RTI
 incorporated ferrite nanoparticles (50–100 nm) into acrylate and epoxy coating systems. These
 paramagnetic fillers allowed the sensors to respond to external magnetic fields without retaining
 residual magnetization. Dummy sensors encapsulated in ferrite-filled epoxy responded to magnetic
 fields from approximately 3 cm away, demonstrating the feasibility of magnetic manipulation for
 downhole guidance.

• Encapsulation Strategies for Buoyancy and Protection: Due to the limitations of thin-film spray
 coatings (especially with acrylates), RTI shifted to injection molding techniques for encapsulation.
 Testing showed precise control of buoyancy using three color-coded encapsulated sensor formulations.
 The team validated buoyancy using Quik-gel, a bentonite-based drilling mud simulant, where sensors
 consistently floated or suspended based on their specific gravity. During sonication, sensors self-
 associated with glass vial surfaces due to weak electrostatic interactions, suggesting compatibility with
 reservoir flow and emplacement environments.

• Lab-Scale Emplacement Simulation: RTI constructed a lab-scale sensor emplacement demo to mimic
 downhole conditions. Using a peristaltic pump, the team injected sensors, suspended in Quik-gel, into
 a dual polycarbonate tubing system. Injected phantom sensors successfully emplaced along the outer
 tubing wall. After decanting the gel, they injected lightweight cement using the same setup. Crucially,
 the emplaced sensors remained in place and undamaged throughout the cementing process, validating
 both their mechanical stability and the cement-rejection performance of the coated backsides.

To fulfill Subtask 3.3, RTI initially aimed to find a vendor for surface coatings for microsensors destined
for field tests. However, the team shifted its research focus to developing and evaluating coating strategies
for available commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) pH sensors. The goal was to enhance their performance in
both liquid and gaseous environments. Despite initial challenges in sourcing suitable COTS CO₂ or pH
sensors, Caltech provided several pH sensors, allowing RTI to progress into coating development and
functional evaluation. The RTI team focused on developing robust, biocompatible hydrogel-based coating
explicitly tailored for the Caltech pH sensors. This coating supported real-time, accurate pH measurements.
Hydrogels were selected for their advantageous moisture retention, biocompatibility, and ability to regulate
ion diffusion across a wide pH range, which helps preserve sensor stability and longevity in chemically and
biologically diverse environments. RTI shortlisted and rigorously evaluated three hydrogel formulations.
Their criteria included film uniformity, optical transparency, pH resistance, curing behavior, and adhesion
to improve bonding. The first formulation, a polyaniline-based hydrogel dispersed in calcium alginate,
exhibited poor adhesion and mechanical stability, resulting in coating failure and its exclusion from further
testing. The second formulation, a Methocel-Calcium Alginate composite, adhered well but exhibited
drawbacks such as uneven coating thickness, high viscosity, long curing times, and marginal chemical
stability under extreme pH conditions, which limited its practical scalability. The third and most promising
candidate was the Calcium Carboxymethyl Cellulose–Calcium Alginate (CCMC-CA) hydrogel. This
formulation provided excellent transparency, moisture retention, biocompatibility, uniform and smooth
coating layers, and strong adhesion regardless of substrate pretreatment. Stability tests in harsh acidic (pH
1) and basic (pH 14) environments confirmed the CCMC-CA hydrogel’s resilience, with no observable
swelling, flaking, or delamination, even after mechanical agitation. This indicated its suitability for robust
 13

sensor deployment in industrial, biomedical, and environmental settings. For subsequent electrochemical
validation, RTI used open-circuit potential measurements across a range of buffered pH solutions. This
allowed the team to assess the coated sensor’s sensitivity, stability, and dynamic response. Comparative
studies between coated and uncoated sensors revealed that the CCMC-CA coating enhanced
electrochemical responsiveness, surface passivation, and stability. The coated sensors demonstrated
superior signal stability and reduced drift over extended pH cycling. Notably, the coated sensors exhibited
strong pH sensitivity with rapid equilibration and excellent reversibility through repeated acidic, neutral,
and basic cycles, confirming the chemical robustness and reproducibility of the hydrogel interface.

The coating and emplacement work demonstrated that buoyancy control, surface-energy tuning, and
selective sensor positioning are technically feasible, providing a practical roadmap for future
cement-embedded sensor deployment.

Task 4 at SNL focused on developing the well infrastructure's electronics and mechanical systems,
specifically aiming to establish a wireless power and communication link from the surface to sensors
embedded in the casing annulus, bypassing the need for wires in the flow path or annulus. This task
advanced these components from TRL 3 to 4, with demonstrations utilizing COTS sensors. Building on
SNL's Real-time Subsurface Event Assessment and Detection (ReSEAD) initiative and Laboratory
Directed Research and Development (LDRD) efforts, the goal was to push the technology from TRL 3 to
5, culminating in the Smart Collar prototype shown in Figure VIII. Success for Task 4 hinges on several
key metrics: enabling stable NOV pipe power transmission to charge capacitor banks at approximately 2
V, with a minimum energy storage capacity of 25 Joules to power downhole electronics and support
communication; ensuring the downhole electronics package performs all designed functions, including data
interpretation, transmission, and sensor queries; providing the capability to power and interface with RFID
sensors through representative annular materials; and finally, successful field installation and demonstration
at an approved site.

Task 5 aimed to demonstrate the feasibility of proof-of-concept (POC) of sensors from Task 2 and Smart
Collars, developed in Task 4, in a field lab at TRL 5, and to report technology performance. Initial design
for the POC was for vertical wellbore conditions; however, due to a) scarcity of the sensors that Caltech
was to develop, and the need to perform a much more quantitative characterization of sensors in connection
with casing Collar, UT devised and demonstrated the POC in a new horizontal trench demonstration.

UT-Austin succeeded in all metrics of the final implementation of this task: 1) modeling of spatial and
temporal distribution of injected fluids in the formation; 2) vendor contracting and construction for
assessment of feasibility of field experiment; and 3) the system installation and demonstration at the Devine
test site.

To fulfill Subtask 5.1, UT-Austin initially designed several field experiment setups, including vertical
single-well or cross-well injection of pH-adjusted fluids to assess the feasibility of effective pH changes in
a possible cross-borehole configuration, UT-Austin built a dynamic reservoir model of the geometry of the
injection formation and injection and observation wells. The model was filled with appropriate porosity,
permeability, and other relevant formation properties obtained from fluid samples and previously cored

The project work directly addresses the critical need for effective monitoring of CO2 stored in the
subsurface. By enabling robust and continuous surveillance, the team’s advancements are crucial for
ensuring the long-term integrity and safety of carbon subsurface storage efforts. This capability allows for
the early detection of potential CO2 leakage or migration, which is crucial for preventing environmental
harm and maintaining public trust in carbon capture and storage technologies. Furthermore, the valuable
data provided by the monitoring system will inform regulatory compliance and significantly enhance the
understanding of complex subsurface behavior. Building on these foundational contributions, future work
will focus on advancing and validating wireless sensor technologies for challenging subsurface and other
extreme environments, with an emphasis on enhanced leak detection in CO2 geological/subsurface storage.

Limitations & Lessons Learned
The development and field-demonstration of an integrated wireless sensing system for casing-annulus
monitoring revealed several technical, environmental, and integration-related constraints that shaped the
achievable Technology Readiness Level (TRL4) within the award period. These limitations also yielded
important lessons that inform appropriate next steps (outside the scope of this award). These include:

Microsensor Development (Task 2)

 • Communication range in conductive media: Millimeter-scale CMOS RFID sensors designed for
 ~900 MHz operation experienced severe attenuation in brine-filled cement, limiting
 communication to millimeters.
 • pH sensor material stability: Early spin-coated PANI films drifted and degraded above ~60 °C;
 even improved electrodeposited PANI required protective coatings and remained sensitive to
 humidity and handling.
 • CO₂ sensing via NASICON: Although responsive in dry laboratory conditions, NASICON sensors
 were highly humidity-sensitive and required high-temperature sintering incompatible with CMOS
 integration.
 • Insufficient sensor quantities: Sensor availability was inadequate for bulk emplacement in cement
 or for iterative integration-testing with the Smart Collar.

 • Ultra-small antennas at UHF are fundamentally mismatched to lossy, high-permittivity cement and
 require either larger antennas or lower-frequency platforms.
 • Miniaturization improves pH sensitivity but places higher demands on packaging, environmental
 isolation, and RF power delivery.
 • CMOS-compatible CO₂ sensing will require alternative materials or architectures.
 • Early alignment of sensor fabrication cadence with systems-integration milestones is essential.

Coating & Emplacement Technologies (Task 3)

 • Acrylate coatings suffered from edge-bleed during curing, reducing repeatability and making
 two-sided applications difficult.
 • Hydrogel formulations varied widely in adhesion, mechanical stability, and chemical durability;
 only the CCMC-CA hydrogel met the minimum operational criteria.
 • Magnetic steering worked at bench scale, but field-relevant forces, distances, and geometries were
 not validated.
 • Emplacement efficiency in simulated annuli (~30%) demonstrated feasibility but also highlighted
 variability in transport behavior.
 21

 • One-sided, functionally selective coatings are feasible and mechanically robust when carefully
 formulated.
 • Encapsulation via injection molding provides superior density control, mechanical protection, and
 coating uniformity versus thin-film methods.
 • Buoyancy control and wall-affinity behavior are tunable, but real-well emplacement requires more
 controlled geometry and repeatable fluid rheology.

Smart Collar & Wired Pipe Integration (Task 4)

 • High attenuation through cement prevented communication with Caltech’s sensors even when
 using high-power, custom RF readers.
 • Antenna detuning occurred in response to moisture and brine ingress, making UHF patch antennas
 extremely sensitive to environmental state.
 • Power harvesting constraints: The AC-harvested supercapacitor bank required long recharge times
 (12–24 hrs) and degraded during field use.
 • Multiple reflection boundaries in vertical configurations produced extremely low SNR, preventing
 successful interrogation of embedded chips.

 • Horizontal trench configurations reduce multipath loss and enable realistic, field-representative RF
 evaluation at low risk.
 • The NOV wired-pipe system is effective for low-frequency power/communications but cannot
 overcome UHF attenuation without larger antennas or alternative sensing approaches.
 • Off-the-shelf RFID temperature sensors provided a successful surrogate path for validating the
 Smart Collar’s power/communication chain.
 • Reliable antenna-cement coupling requires early co-design between sensor RF architecture and
 downhole materials.

Field Demonstration (Task 5)

 • Unavailable microsensors prevented the originally planned instrumented vertical-well
 demonstration.
 • Cross-borehole chemistry detection proved unrealistic at shallow depths and small pressure
 differentials.
 • Cement-interface access constraints at DFPS prevented retrofitting wells for prototype-scale
 geometry and spacing.
 • Tank/collar integration required iterative mechanical adaption to ensure watertight seals and
 physical alignment.

 22

 • The horizontal trench-and-tank configuration provided a highly controllable, permit-free,
 field-representative platform for RF testing and CO₂ exposure experiments.
 • Reference CO₂ and pH instruments are essential for benchmarking early-TRL sensors in dynamic
 aqueous environments.
 • The Smart Collar–NOV Pipe data path performs reliably under field conditions when paired with
 commercially supported sensing hardware.

System-Level Integration Across All Tasks

 • TRL advancement was uneven across partners: the Smart Collar reached ~TRL4-5, RTI coatings
 reached TRL3–4, whereas microsensors remained at TRL3–4 for temperature and lower for CO₂.
 • Ultra-miniaturized sensors, patch antennas, and cement-annulus geometries impose mutually
 conflicting constraints on frequency, range, and power.
 • Supply-chain disruptions (reader chip obsolescence) and limited sensor inventory produced delays
 and required redesigns.

 • Early co-design between electronics, materials, emplacement, and field geometries is mandatory
 for UHF-frequency downhole systems.
 • Cement’s dielectric and conductive properties dominate all RF design considerations—more so
 than originally anticipated.
 • Successful integration requires harmonizing sensor availability, RF architecture,
 cement-environment constraints, and deployment geometry.

Overall, the project advanced multiple subsystems toward field-readiness, proved the viability of wireless
annular monitoring in cemented environments, and clarified the remaining sensor-level developments
needed to realize a fully integrated CO₂-leak-detection platform.

Introduction
Carbon capture and storage (CCS) require durable, long-term containment of CO₂ within the target reservoir.
Conventional monitoring approaches (e.g., surface seismic campaigns, periodic logging) are typically indirect,
costly, and intermittent, making it difficult to detect early changes near the injection interval where risks
originate.

This project develops a miniaturized, wireless, subsurface sensor system designed for continuous or
on-demand measurements adjacent to the wellbore and caprock. By placing low-power sensing nodes close
to the critical seal, the system can track parameters such as temperature, pressure-proxy signals, and chemistry
(e.g., brine pH shifts that accompany CO₂ dissolution), enabling earlier detection and intervention.

The work adapts low-power resonant RFID principles expressly for harsh, conductive wellbore
environments—cement, brine, and steel—and integrates with Sandia National Laboratories’ Smart Casing
Collar and RTI’s emplacement approaches, with testing support at UT-Austin’s DFTS. Design, fabrication,
and validation activities under this DOE effort were solely directed at CCS well-integrity monitoring.
Certain foundational circuits and coupling concepts originated from earlier academic research in unrelated
fields. Early investigations prior to this project explored RF resonant coupling across progressively smaller
platforms. Initial experiments used large 20 MHz wire-wound coils tested in air, deionized water, and saline
to characterize resonance behavior in conductive media. Subsequent PCB-based planar inductors enabled
refinements in coil geometries, alignment tolerances, and backscatter telemetry concepts. Finally, integrated
on-chip inductors demonstrated the feasibility of millimeter-scale resonant structures operating near 900
MHz. These insights informed the pH and CO2 sensor design considerations during this project to adapt
resonant RFID methods for the harsher cement and brine environments relevant to CCS wellbore monitoring.
At DOE's direction, we have removed the details of earlier works and the methodologies employed from the
report.

All tasks, prototypes, and tests in this project addressed subsurface communications, environmental
robustness, and well-integrity sensing relevant to CCS operations.

Materials and Methods
System Overview and Design Objectives

This project developed a passively powered, wireless microsensor platform for continuous subsurface
monitoring in environments relevant to carbon capture and storage (CCS). The primary design objectives
were to (1) enable long-term operation without on-board batteries, (2) support sensing in conductive and
high-dielectric media such as brine-saturated cement, and (3) achieve sufficient power transfer and
communication reliability at millimeter-scale sensor dimensions compatible with wellbore deployment.

The resulting system integrates CMOS electronics, resonant power harvesting components, sensor interfaces,
and telemetry circuitry into a compact sensor node. All architectures, fabrication, testing, and validation
activities described below were conducted under this DOE award.

Wireless Power Transfer Architecture

Wireless power delivery is achieved using near-field inductive coupling between an external reader coil and
an on-chip resonant LC circuit. The reader energizes the coupling field at a frequency near 900 MHz, chosen
to balance antenna size, coupling efficiency, and attenuation in conductive media.

Figure 2-1 shows a schematic block diagram of the microsensors’ control circuit design and CMOS sensor
system architecture. The sensor chip rectifies the induced AC signal using CMOS-based rectifier stages and
regulates the resulting DC voltage to power on-chip analog and digital subsystems. Power optimization
focused on minimizing static current consumption while maintaining sufficient voltage headroom for sensor
operation, signal conditioning, and telemetry.

Power transfer efficiency and alignment tolerance were experimentally evaluated under representative test
conditions, including air, deionized water, and saline solutions used to emulate the electromagnetic properties
of CCS wellbore environments.

Figure 2-1: Block diagram of the RF-powered CMOS sensor readout chain. The system consists of a TIA (transimpedance
amplifier) that converts the sensor-generated current into a voltage signal, a potentiostat that biases and controls the
electrochemical sensor interface, a reference generator that provides stable bias and reference voltages, and an ADC (analog-to-
digital converter) that digitizes the sensor signal for wireless backscatter communication to the RF reader.

 30

Backscatter Telemetry and Data Communication
The same resonant structure used for power harvesting also supports data telemetry through load-shift keying
(LSK). In this scheme, the sensor modulates its effective load impedance, producing corresponding changes
in the reflected RF power observed at the reader (Figure 2-2).

Figure 2-2. System-level architecture of the RF-powered wireless sensor node and reader interface. The reader-side TX PCB
(transmit printed circuit board) generates the RF power signal, which is impedance-matched and monitored using an envelope
detector. Power and data are coupled wirelessly to the sensor through a resonant RF link. On the sensor side, the received signal
is processed by a power management unit (PMU) for energy harvesting, a demodulator for downlink control, and a modulator
for uplink backscatter communication. A control circuit interfaces with the electrochemical sensor, consisting of WE (working
electrode), RE (reference electrode), and CE (counter electrode).

Sensor data are encoded as pulse-width-modulated bit streams that are demodulated by the reader electronics
and decoded for display and storage. This approach eliminates the need for active RF transmission from the
sensor, reducing power consumption and system complexity while enabling reliable bidirectional
communication over short stand-off distances (Figure 2-3).

Figure 2-3. Demonstration of passive wireless power delivery and backscatter telemetry. Oscilloscope traces show reader
interrogation signals and corresponding sensor responses encoded via pulse-width modulation, confirming reliable bidirectional
communication without on-board power storage.

 31

Integrated Coil and Resonator Design
On-chip inductors were implemented in the top metal layers of the CMOS process to maximize quality factor
(Q) while minimizing substrate losses. Coil geometries were selected based on trade studies evaluating
conductor width, spacing, number of turns, and proximity effects.

Electromagnetic simulations were used to assess resonant frequency stability, parasitic effects, and coupling
behavior in the presence of nearby metal structures and lossy surrounding media. Integrated metal–insulator–
metal (MIM) capacitors were used to tune the resonance and to compensate for detuning introduced by
environmental loading (Figure 2-4 and 2-5).

These design considerations ensured consistent power transfer and telemetry performance under conditions
expected for embedded subsurface operation.

Figure 2-4. Simplified electrical model of the on-chip resonant inductor used for wireless power transfer and telemetry. The model
captures inductive, resistive, and parasitic capacitive elements that influence resonance behavior and coupling efficiency in
conductive environments.

Electrochemical Sensor Interface and Potentiostat Design
Electrochemical sensing was enabled using a low-power integrated potentiostat architecture optimized for
amperometric measurements. The potentiostat maintains a controlled potential difference between the
working and reference electrodes while measuring the resulting current flowing through the sensing element
(Figure 2-2 and 2-5).

To reduce noise and improve stability at small electrode dimensions, the working electrode is held at a fixed
potential, while current is conveyed through current-copying circuits that isolate the electrochemical interface
from downstream readout and telemetry electronics.

Measured sensor currents are digitized using low-power readout circuitry and serialized for wireless
backscatter transmission.

 32

Figure 2-5: RF-tag sensor with 20,000 transistors on board, made by 65 nm CMOS process. This system enables the measurement
of current through a potentiostat circuit.

Temperature Sensor Implementation
Temperature sensing was implemented using thin-film resistive elements fabricated directly on the CMOS
platform. Platinum, silicon, and germanium-based resistive materials were evaluated based on linearity,
sensitivity, and long-term stability at elevated temperatures.

Resistive elements were tailored to match the biasing and dynamic range of the on-chip potentiostat and
analog-to-digital conversion circuitry. Calibration was performed using controlled hot-plate tests across a
temperature range relevant to subsurface environments, and encapsulation strategies were applied to improve
corrosion resistance in saline conditions.

pH Sensor Fabrication and Optimization

pH sensing was achieved using interdigitated gold electrodes functionalized with electropolymerized
polyaniline (PANI). Changes in solution pH produce reversible changes in polymer conductivity, enabling
amperometric pH measurement.

Electrode geometries, polymer deposition parameters, and film thickness were optimized to maximize
sensitivity while maintaining long-term stability. Wired benchtop experiments were used to evaluate sensor
response, drift, and repeatability prior to integration with wireless CMOS platforms. Protective coating
strategies were evaluated to improve robustness and enable sustained operation in chemically aggressive
environments (see Chapter 3).

Environmental Testing and Validation

All system characterization was conducted using test configurations representative of CCS wellbore
conditions. Test media included air, deionized water, and saline solutions designed to emulate conductive and
dielectric properties encountered in cemented annular environments.

 33

Testing quantified power transfer efficiency, resonant detuning, telemetry reliability, alignment tolerance,
sensor sensitivity, and operational stability. Results informed iterative refinement of coil design, power
management circuitry, and sensor integration (See also TASK 4).

System Integration and Deployment Readiness

Fully integrated sensor systems were fabricated and tested to verify end-to-end operation, including wireless
power harvesting, sensing, data encoding, and telemetry. Representative sensor units and reader hardware
were delivered to project collaborators at Sandia for integration with intelligent casing collar test platforms
and further validation under field-relevant conditions.

Results
Wireless Power and Communications
Over the past 5 years, the inductively coupled sensor chips were refined for the continuous monitoring of
temperature and pH. With a size of approximately 1.2 mm × 1.2 mm (Figure 2-5) and a power consumption
of 5 μW, the sensors can be separated from their reader up to 5 mm in a conducting solution that attenuates
RF power and report new data every 100 milliseconds to a reader.

In this system, the function of the reader includes (a) supplying power to the sensor chip through near-field
inductive coupling, (b) collecting data periodically by using an active RF-tag approach, and (c) transmitting
that information to a computer or smartphone for display and storage through a wireless interface. To
minimize the sensor's size to the smallest possible volume, on-chip batteries were eliminated. Wireless
power and communications occur by matching, in near field, a set of two inductive coils. One of the coils
is located on the reader and the other on the sensor. Current flow through the reader coil induces a
corresponding current in the sensor coil when these are critically coupled at a frequency of 800-900MHz.
Communication is established via an RF tag system, in which the reader sends a tag signal to the sensor,
and the sensor, in turn, responds with an updated current reading. By monitoring the RF-power transmitted
from the reader to the chip, it is possible to establish a very efficient two-way communications link, in
which reflected power is modulated by the sensor chip. To communicate with the reader, the sensor circuit
changes the frequency of its inductive coil in short pulses, resulting in changes in the reflective power
measured by the reader. The current sensor of the working electrode is read, amplified and digitized by the
CMOS circuit. The measured electrochemical current is then transmitted as an 8-bit binary number back to
the reader (Figure 2-6) by using a pulse-width modulated format. This figure shows a typical back-reflected
signal from one of the chips in an experiment, displayed on an oscilloscope. The oscilloscope trace shows
the transmission of 1s and 0s within an 8-bit + parity format from the chip to the reader. Wider pulses
encode 1s whereas shorter pulses encode 0s. As each sensor can respond to a separate 9 bit tag signal,
several sensors can be deployed in proximity yet measured separately.

 34

Figure 2-6: Wireless operation of the integrated system: Oscilloscope traces on left show the tag signal to wake up the CMOS tag
(shown on right). In response, back-scattered bit-stream contains measured sensor response using a pulse width modulation format.

The first year of the program was dedicated to characterizing the wireless temperature probes and benchtop
testing of first-generation pH and resistivity sensors. Complete and functional temperature sensors were
sent to the collaborators at Sandia Laboratories for evaluation. To enable the Sandia team to communicate
with these sensors, a prototype reader was designed and built, which was delivered in 2021. Also, programs
were written for the reader to optimize the reader operation frequency between 860 MHz and 928 MHz for
minimal loss and critical coupling between the sensor and the reader antennas. The parameters for the
CMOS sensor interrogation, such as the duty cycle, gain, time delay, and communications frequency, could
all be modified through this program. The programs work well and were handed over to the Sandia Labs
team in 2022, but difficulty in downloading software from unsecured servers delayed their implementation.
The wireless temperature sensors were also calibrated by measuring changes in the conductivity on a
hotplate. The calibration efforts were completed, and calibration curves were provided to Sandia Labs along
with 10 functional wireless CMOS temperature sensors.
In the first year of the program, pH sensors based on measuring conductivity changes in thin polyaniline
(PANI) films were also worked on. The lifetimes and stability of these pH sensors were targeted for
improvement, as they exhibit excessive drift when measuring pH over several days. Optimization of the
circuitry for the temperature and pH sensors was also undertaken to improve the overall system's power
efficiency. Several approaches were tested, including the use of Ag/AgCl reference electrodes to help
stabilize the on-chip Pt/PtO reference electrode system. The down selection of the most effective and robust
approach was intended by testing different strategies to make the PANI pH sensors more robust, but this
work was only partially successful, as pH sensors can’t withstand temperatures above 60 °C.

The simplest sensors that were developed by using the potentiostat circuit have been temperature probes.

different buffers. The most miniature pH sensors are now approximately 0.05 mm × 0.05 mm in scale.
Between the newly stabilized PANI chemistry and miniaturized sensors, conductivity drift has been reduced,

The wired pH sensors were evaluated using benchtop experiments based on polyaniline resistance changes.
Also, wired sensors were microfabricated on a wafer scale with porous gold and platinum electrochemical

chemistries for maximum selectivity and performance is expected to be down selected and de-risked.
To functionalize the electrodes to become pH sensors, a thin layer of Polyaniline (PANI) or polypyrrole
(PPY) were used. These polymers are known to change in conductivity when the layers are immersed in
different pH environments. In acid solutions, the polymer is doped with H+ ions. If it is in the emeraldine
salt phase of PANI, which is electrically conductive, the conductivity changes with the pH. The resulting
surface charge controls the resistance. When the polymer is exposed to basic solutions, the captured H+ ions
are neutralized by OH-, resulting in the opposite effect and the resistivity of the polymer increases. The
optimized sensors measure the change in resistance of an emeraldine PANI layer, deposited on top of an
interdigitated gold contact geometry as shown in Figure 2-10.

pH Sensor mechanism
As has been reported before (Goswami et al, 2023), PANI undergoes reversible protonation and
deprotonation as the pH of the surrounding environment changes. In acid environments, H+ ions lead to the
protonation of the imine (N=) groups within the PANI backbone. This changes the electronic structure of
PANI allowing it to be electrically conductive. In alkaline environments, OH- ions lead to interacting with
H+ ions of the imine groups, leading to their removal within the PANI backbone. This deprotonation causes
PANI to lose its electrical conductivity and adopt the emeraldine base state (Goswami et al, 2023).

PANI sensors exist in three possible states: Leucoemeraldine is the fullest reduced state, Pernigraniliine is
the fullest oxidized state, and emeraldine is the half-oxidized state. The emeraldine state is generally desired
for sensing pH, and the PANI states can be controlled during electrochemical deposition through cyclic
voltammetry. A process of depositing these films was developed from a 0.05M solution of aniline in sulfuric
acid onto gold electrodes with a potential window of -0.2V-1V versus a standard electrode at a scan rate of
20mV/s.

During the polymerization reaction, different states of aniline can be observed by measuring a
voltammogram. Figure 2-11 shows this current/voltage plot after 15 scans and characteristic peaks
corresponding to the oxidation and reduction processes can be observed: During oxidation, leucoemeraldine
(A) is converted to emeraldine base (B) and to pernigraniline (C). During reduction, pernigraniline (C’) is

NASICON as a Carbon Dioxide Sensor
In year 2 of the project, difficulties were encountered in adhesion and stability of the polyaniline pH probes,
resulting in short lifetime. The original goal was to use polyaniline films deposited by spin-coating onto
potentiostats, and these films are much less stable than the electrodeposited films that were developed later
in the program. The pH sensors did not work well at temperatures above 60°C, and the polyaniline layers
became less sensitive to pH changes with time. This resulted in a pH sensor lifetime of only several days
rather than years. Therefore, other alternative sensor chemistries were explored to measure CO 2 directly.
As part of this effort, developing electrochemical sensors were focused on for direct measurement of CO2
by using Na3Zr2Si2PO12 (NASICON) (Hunter et al, 2006). NASICON is a solid electrolyte originally
developed in the 1960s for sodium batteries and has since then been used to measure carbon dioxide at
temperatures as high as 500°C. Inorganic electrolytes are more temperature stable than polymer layers and
enable the lifetimes that are required by this project. The CMOS potentiostat sensor and reader electronics

 41

required to detect changes in carbon dioxide with the NASICON electrolyte is identical to the original
design of pH sensors. Therefore, the idea was to bond the NASICON sensor onto CMOS chips by using
wafer bonding techniques. Since NASICON requires high temperature sintering (at temperatures above
1000°C), monolithic integration is out of the question as the silicon CMOS electronics would be
deteriorated at such high temperatures. It was expected to integrate the NASICON solid electrolyte with
Na2CO3/BaCO3 auxiliary electrolytes that can be integrated before the bonding process. Interdigitated
metal finger electrodes deposited onto the NASICON before wafer bonding and the resistivity of the sensor
layer could be matched to the 2.5 MΩ impedance that the CMOS potentiostat circuit requires.
As inorganic solid electrolyte carbon dioxide sensors were explored, work from sensor groups that had been
developed over the past 20 years was adapted. One group led by Gary Hunter (Hunter et al, 2006) at NASA,
has reduced the size of NASICON carbon dioxide sensors by using thin film technology (Figure 2-16) 20
years ago. The resulting solid electrolyte sensors have the advantage of being stable up to 500°C, and
therefore it is expected to have decadal lifetimes as long as the surface is encapsulated and its corrosion is
prevented.

Methane Detection by Tunable Laser Absorption Spectroscopy (TLAS)
TLAS has evolved over the past 40 years into a very sensitive gas analysis technique for remote sensing.
TLAS competes with mass spectroscopy in both gas sensitivity and selectivity yet lends itself to
miniaturization. In TLAS, the emission wavelength of a single mode laser is thermally tuned across the
absorption line of the gas to be measured (Figure 2-19).

Figure 2-19: TLAS concept and measurements, including concentration vs. signal response plot showing 1ppm sensitivity and
absorption detector response to chirped laser signal.

TLAS is presently used for remote sensing of gases with characteristic absorption peaks in the near infrared,
where semiconductor lasers are readily available. The advantage of TLAS over gas chromatography, mass
spectroscopy and other techniques is its small size, simplicity and specificity, as the narrow absorption peak
identifying the gas of interest is scanned. By repeatedly temperature-tuning the single mode laser over the
absorption peak by pulsing the laser at kHz frequencies, TLAS can generate a frequency doubled signal if
the tuning range straddles the gas absorption line of interest. This higher frequency signal results from the
gas absorption attenuating the laser light in the middle of the scan, resulting in two pulses (one at the
beginning and another at the end) for each scan. This doubled frequency can be detected with a lock-in
photo-detector circuit and results in the quantitative determination of gas concentrations with ppm to ppb
sensitivity for most interesting gases. A prototype fiber-coupled TLAS system has been developed to detect
methane with 1 ppm sensitivity (Figure 2-20).

Discussion
This program evaluated the feasibility of millimeter-scale wireless RF tag sensors for downhole
monitoring applications, with a focus on temperature, pH, carbon dioxide, and methane sensing in
electrically and dielectrically challenging environments such as cement and brine. The work builds on
prior demonstrations of RF-powered CMOS sensor platforms and extends them toward subsurface use
cases where regulatory RF power limits applicable to medical or surface applications no longer apply.
Operating at depth enables substantially higher reader power levels and, in principle, improved
communication ranges compared to earlier short-range resonant demonstrations.

The wireless sensing architecture is based on an RF tag concept in which the sensor device is powered by
an external reader and communicates measured data via back-reflected microwave pulse trains generated
by modulating the antenna quality factor (Q) on the CMOS chip. This approach was successfully
implemented and characterized across multiple media, including water, brine, and phosphate buffer
solutions. The platform was readily adapted to temperature sensing by incorporating silicon- or
germanium-based resistive elements into the on-chip potentiostat circuitry, demonstrating the flexibility
of the CMOS sensor architecture. These wireless temperature sensors, along with a reader system, were
transitioned to partners at Sandia National Laboratories for further optimization, including extension
toward carbon dioxide monitoring in cementitious environments. Independent work initiated by the AEC
in late 2024 showed that RF frequencies of 134KHz were suitable for communication of about 5cm in
lossy media using COTS sensors about 9mmx2mm in size (DOE asked not to include this AEC data in
the report and results have been excluded from this report).

A key outcome of this effort was the identification of practical limits on RF-powered sensor operation as
reader power levels increase. Because the same RF carrier is used both for power delivery and for
backscatter-based data transmission, increasing reader power leads to self-jamming, where the sensor
signal becomes difficult to distinguish from the interrogation waveform unless additional RF isolation or
more advanced antenna and reader designs are employed. Experimental studies were conducted to
quantify the onset of self-jamming and to evaluate approaches for broadening the usable power window

 45

through electronics upgrades and power attenuation studies in conductive media. These results showed
that, in realistic subsurface environments, achievable communication ranges are constrained by a
combination of RF losses and self-jamming effects, especially at the high frequencies selected. Extending
wireless operation to centimeter-scale distances in cement would require a substantial redesign of antenna
geometries and increased reader gain. Because antenna design for high-dielectric, conductive media was
outside the core expertise of the Caltech team, the RF tag sensors and reader development were
transitioned to Sanda, while continued support was provided for adapting the sensor devices to cement-
specific dielectric and conductivity conditions (see Chapter 4).

The program also yielded important insights into the impact of sensor miniaturization on chemical
sensing performance. Early in the project, wireless pH sensors based on conventional glass electrode
designs were explored in collaboration with Shell Research Laboratories. These devices proved
unsuitable for extreme-pressure environments due to mechanical failure. This limitation motivated the
development of solid-state pH sensors based on conducting polymers such as polyaniline and polypyrrole.
A significant and somewhat unexpected finding was that miniaturization of these polymer-based pH
sensors not only improved pressure robustness, but also enhanced sensitivity and response time.
Lithographically defined interdigitated electrode geometries fabricated with gold electrodes exhibited a
clear logarithmic relationship between pH and resistance, with excellent sensitivity despite the very small
sensor footprint. These micro-pH sensors were successfully integrated within the 1.2 mm × 1.2 mm
CMOS wireless sensor chips and demonstrated good stability and operational lifetimes. Further
improvements in stability are expected through surface coatings developed in collaboration with RTI, and
the small sensor dimensions enable the use of redundant sensor arrays to extend operational lifetime even
further.

Carbon dioxide sensors based on NASICON solid-state electrolytes were also investigated. While
promising responses were observed for CO₂ concentrations on the order of 1% in dry nitrogen, these
sensors exhibited strong sensitivity to humidity, which limits their applicability in aqueous or cement-
based environments. In addition, the high-temperature sintering required for NASICON fabrication
complicates integration into millimeter-scale wireless platforms. As a result, NASICON-based CO₂
sensors were deemed unsuitable for the wet subsurface environments targeted in this project.

Methane sensing was addressed using tunable laser absorption spectroscopy, achieving part-per-million
sensitivity. Although these sensors demonstrated excellent performance, their reliance on fiber coupling
and semiconductor laser sources makes them incompatible with the constraints of an RF-powered
wireless casing-collar system. Nevertheless, because the methane sensor head contains no electronics or
materials prone to degradation, this approach represents a robust alternative for long-term methane
monitoring. The demonstrated sensitivity and relatively low system cost suggest strong potential for
safety and environmental monitoring applications, particularly for rapid leak detection, even if integration
into the present wireless platform is not practical.

Overall, this project established the viability of millimeter-scale RF-powered wireless temperature and pH
sensors for operation in conductive and high-dielectric environments, while also defining the key
technical barriers that must be addressed to extend communication range and expand sensing modalities.
 46

The results highlight both the promise of highly miniaturized wireless chemical sensors for subsurface
monitoring and the critical importance of antenna design, RF isolation, and environmental coupling in
realizing practical downhole wireless sensor systems.

Future Work
There are many applications in which temperature, pressure, and chemistry must be measured in extreme
environments. Sensors have been developed that can resist some of these environments without having to
be encapsulated in hermetically sealed systems. This makes the microsensors especially useful when
information is required at high pressures. For example, prior to this work, there were a few inexpensive
methods for measuring pH in high-pressure vessels. Based on the experience gained in this work, the
Caltech team will continue to send sensors with the latest miniaturized geometries to RTI for evaluation
and protective coating, and will develop more advanced, capable integrated systems that can be monitored
wirelessly for a long time. Commercially available wireless RF platforms can be modified with sensors
developed in this work to extend the communication range. Advances in this communications technology
will aid efforts to introduce microsensors into many new applications.
Given the excellent sensitivity of the miniaturized pH sensors in this work, the size of the electrode patterns
can be further reduced. Smaller electrode patterns provide even higher pH sensitivity and faster response
times. The components of solid-state sensors, such as polyaniline for pH sensing and miniaturized platinum
resistance sensors for temperature measurement, have been tested, and recipes for generating such devices
have been optimized. All the sensors developed show promising results and can be integrated onto Caltech’s
millimeter-scale microsensor platform. They easily withstand the high pressures encountered and are more
robust and sensitive than larger systems currently commercially available. More work remains to be done
on optimizing the communication link between the readers and the microsensors when the material between
them is highly conducting and absorbing.

Introduction
This project focuses on advancing the development and deployment readiness of microsensor encapsulation
and coating technologies designed for high-pressure, high-temperature (HPHT) well environments. The
effort targets the progression of sensor systems from early-stage formulation and lab-scale testing to scaled
application for future field demonstrations, with a focus on achieving Technology Readiness Levels (TRL)
2–4 across various components.

In Task 3.1 (BP1), RTI developed specialized coating formulations and surface preparation techniques for
microsensors, including the application of tunable and hermetic encapsulations. These materials were
initially applied to non-functioning sensor substrates to demonstrate early-stage proof-of-concept (TRL
2/3). The coatings were engineered not only for protection under HPHT conditions but also to enable
specific functional characteristics, such as buoyancy control and targeted emplacement within the wellbore
environment. Discreet chemistries were employed to achieve these attributes, and best-performing
materials were down-selected by the end of BP1. These optimized coatings were then applied to functioning
sensors and phantoms, culminating in the creation of sensor-on-a-chip (SoC) systems.

Task 3.2 (BP2) supports this development with the design and assembly of a custom test rig capable of
simulating relevant sensor placement in downhole conditions. This rig was used to validate the
emplacement control mechanisms provided by the surface coatings, including buoyancy-driven transport,
selective emplacement at the annulus/reservoir interface, and rejection from non-target zones such as
cement layers. The test demonstrates the ability to manipulate surface energy interactions between the
injection fluid and sensor coating, thereby achieving targeted delivery.

Finally, during Task 3.3 (BP3), the project team made a strategic decision to shift its focus from scaling up
coatings for microsystem test sets to prioritizing the development and evaluation of a new semi-pervious
sensor coating design that enables pH measurements using Caltech sensors. As a result, research and
development activities related to scale-up were paused to allow for alignment with the updated technical

compatibility with the latest coating formulations.

Encapsulations and Sensor Coatings
Initially, RTI focused on the synthesis, application, and characterization of glass slides coated with a
superhydrophobic material formulation that would drive the coated sensors toward the formation wall
downhole. Later in BP1, such coatings were applied to actual silicon substrates provided by the Caltech
collaborators. The superhydrophobic nanoparticle-based system that was developed utilizes a water-based
spray technique, as described in a recent Advanced Materials publication (Gu et al., 2020). Such coatings
were applied to only one side of the microsensor, leaving the opposite face open and accessible for the
sensing elements to interact with the surrounding environment.
The nanoparticle-based technical approach involves the synthesis of two distinct sizes of silica
nanoparticles, specifically 30 nm and 50 nm, using a sol-gel technique. The two particle sizes self-assemble
into short chains of differing lengths to provide a porous surface in tandem with very low surface energy
provided by the perfluoro functional groups present on the 30 nm silica particles via incorporation of
1H,1H,2H,2H-perfluorodecyltriethoxysilane (Figure 3-1) into the sol-gel self-assembled coating. The
coatings were applied to the surface of 1” × 3” glass slides and tested to determine the contact angle for
deionized water. Contact angle measurement results were compared to those for an uncoated clean glass
slide and a cleaned glass slide coated with the commercial fluorinated surface release coating system,
Frekote 770-NC (Figure 3-2). Uncoated glass slides exhibited a contact angle of 56 °, while the fluorinated
Frekote and silica nanoparticle coatings had similar contact angles of approximately 119 °.

research labs at Caltech as representative sensor materials for surface functionalization testing. In addition,

latter as inexpensive non-functional substrates to develop the coating materials and application approach.

determine the mobility performance of this lower-cost coating material in comparison to the more time-
consuming multi-step (i.e., a 3-day, 3-step set of reactions) synthesis required to fabricate the SHNPs.

The SHNP and silane coating design elements will provide sensor mobility through the subsurface space
as it gathers data. Similarly, the acrylate polymer is a low-surface-energy, hydrophobic coating system that
can also provide mobility while controlling the overall specific gravity of the coated sensor. The polymer
can be formulated with buoyant or weighted fillers to target a specific gravity of interest. This requires that
the acrylate is present in sufficient volume to achieve the target buoyancy of interest, resulting in thicker
coatings compared to the flat monolayer coatings offered by small molecule SHNP and silane chemistries.

Scale-up coatings for microsystem test sets

The original goal of this subtask was to identify a vendor capable of applying the appropriate surface coating
to microsensors intended for use in the field test demonstration. However, the RTI team shifted focus toward
coating a commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) CO2 or pH sensor and evaluating its performance in both liquid
and gas environments. Although the team was initially unable to identify a suitable COTS CO2 or pH sensor
for testing, several pH sensors were ultimately received from Caltech, enabling the team to proceed with
coating and performance evaluation efforts.

The RTI team focused on engineering a robust, biocompatible coating layer that can interface seamlessly
with the pH sensors from the Caltech while supporting accurate, real-time pH measurements. After
extensive research and experimental activities to advance the development of the appropriate coating
formulations, hydrogel-based coatings were successfully developed for use in the pH sensor platforms.
Hydrogels offer several compelling advantages for this purpose, including their moisture-retentive
properties, biocompatibility, and ability to act as controlled diffusion media for ions in solution of wide pH
window. These features not only preserve the functional stability of the sensor but also enhance its
operational lifespan, particularly in diverse chemical or biological environments.

Three hydrogel systems were shortlisted for evaluation. Each was chosen for its unique chemical structure
and functional potential to contribute to the overall performance of the coated sensors. The evaluation
 61

criteria included film uniformity, transparency, pH resistance, curing behavior, and, most importantly,
adhesion to the sensor surface under different treatment conditions. For all formulations, coatings were
attempted on both untreated sensor substrates and those pre-treated with 3-
glycidyloxypropyltrimethoxysilane (3-GPS), a silane coupling agent that is widely used to enhance bonding
between organic/inorganic layers and silicon-based surfaces.

 Figure 3-17: Sensor coating using PANI-based hydrogel.

The PANI-based hydrogel was the first candidate for the coating evaluation (Figure 3-17). This formulation
was composed of PANI polymer dispersed in a calcium alginate matrix, with the addition of deionized
water and standard crosslinking promoters. The conductive nature of polyaniline made this formulation a
potentially attractive option for facilitating electrochemical pH measurements. However, its real-world
performance was significantly limited by poor mechanical adhesion. When applied to the sensors surface,
both with and without 3-GPS surface treatment, the hydrogel failed to form a stable bond and flaked off
after curing. The observed detachment likely resulted from a combination of substrate incompatibility and
internal stresses developed during drying and curing. Due to these critical limitations, the PANI-based
hydrogel was unsuitable for further testing.

The second hydrogel formulation investigated was the Methocel-Calcium Alginate (MCA) composite. This
system was designed for its thermos-gelling capabilities and relatively larger pore structure, which can
facilitate rapid ion diffusion. When applied to the sensor surface, the formulation adhered well under both
treated (i.e., pre-treatment with 3-GPS) and untreated conditions. Despite this promising adhesion
performance, the high viscosity of the formulation led to uneven coating thickness and inconsistent film
morphology. In addition, extended curing times and marginal stability under extreme pH conditions were
observed. These factors collectively limit the practicality of MCA for scalable pH sensors manufacturing
and consistent field performance (Figure 3-18).

 Figure 3-18: Sensor coated with MCA formulation.

Alginate (CCMC-CA) hydrogel. The CCMC-CA hydrogel displayed several key advantages that are critical
for sensor coating applications. These included high transparency, effective moisture retention, intrinsic
biocompatibility, and excellent adhesive characteristics. Coatings applied with this formulation exhibited
strong adhesion to the sensors surface, regardless of the use of 3-GPS surface pretreatment or not. The
resulting hydrogel layers were uniform in thickness, smooth in texture, and optically transparent, which is
particularly important for optical or hybrid optical-electrochemical sensor platforms (Figure 3-19).

Next, attention was focused on advancing the functional validation of the CCMC-CA hydrogel formulation.
One of the primary objectives was to assess the electrochemical performance of the coated sensors using
the Open Circuit Potential (OCP) technique. These experiments were performed using CH Instruments, a
reliable platform for precise electrochemical analysis. The OCP technique allowed to monitor the potential
response of the hydrogel-coated sensors across a range of buffered pH solutions. These measurements
provide critical information on the linearity, stability, sensitivity, and drift characteristics of the hydrogel
interface over time. Initially, these experiments were carried out with uncoated sensors reproducing the data
collected by Caltech team to validate the consistency of pH response to determine reproducibility across
multiple sensors. Then, a similar experiment was repeated with coated sensors, which sensed significantly
lower OCPs in different pH ranges which were expected due to limited and controlled ionic conductivities
through the coating.

Figure 3-21 illustrates the open circuit potential (OCP) measurement comparing the electrochemical
behavior of an uncoated and a coated sensor in response to sequential pH changes over a 600-second period
within the potential window of -1 V to +1 V potential window. The setup consisted of a freshly anodized
Ag/Ag⁺ electrode as the reference electrode, the sensor (either coated or uncoated) as the working electrode,
and a platinum (Pt) wire as the counter electrode and the experiment was carried out using a CH Instrument
600E. The experiments were conducted in three distinct pH environments to evaluate the sensors’ stability
and responsiveness under varying proton concentrations: pH = 4 (0 to 200 seconds), pH = 7 (200 to 400
seconds), and pH = 10 (400 to 600 seconds). During the initial stage (0–200 s, pH 4), both sensors showed
relatively stable potentials. The uncoated sensor (blue curve) maintained a potential around -0.60 V, while
the coated sensor (orange curve) held slightly more negative at approximately -0.65 V. This initial acidic
condition showed both sensors in their baseline acidic response state. At around 200 seconds, when the pH
was adjusted to neutral (pH = 7), a sharp drop in potential was observed for both sensors. The uncoated
sensor exhibited a more pronounced shift, decreasing to nearly -0.78 V, while the coated sensor also

 64

dropped but to a lesser extent (approximately -0.70 V). This suggests that the uncoated sensor is more
sensitive to sudden pH changes and experiences a perturbation in its interfacial potential.

 Figure 3-21: OCP data for coated and uncoated sensors in potential window of -1 V to +1 V.

In addition, both sensors stabilized at new steady-state potentials under neutral conditions. The coated
sensor demonstrated better stabilization, and less drift compared to the uncoated one, indicating improved
surface buffering or slower proton exchange kinetics due to the coating. At the 400-second mark, the
electrolyte was adjusted to alkaline conditions (pH = 10), and a clear divergence in behavior was observed.
The coated sensor underwent a significant increase in potential, eventually reaching approximately -0.38
V, while the uncoated sensor experienced only a modest shift, stabilizing around -0.60 V. The greater
potential response of the coated sensor in the basic environment suggested enhanced pH sensitivity and
dynamic electrochemical behavior, likely due to functional groups within the coating that interact more
effectively with hydroxide ions. This OCP study highlighted that the coated sensor exhibits enhanced
electrochemical responsiveness and stability across a wide pH range compared to the uncoated counterpart.
The coating appears to confer improved proton sensitivity, faster interfacial equilibrium, and better surface
passivation, making it a strong candidate for pH sensing applications across varied environments. However,
both coated and uncoated sensors were not following an increase or decrease in the OCP pattern which
could be used to define pH-OCP correlation.

We repeated similar study, narrowing the potential window keeping all other parameters constant. Figure
3-22 illustrates the OCP response of a coated sensor subjected to repeated pH cycling between acidic (pH
4), neutral (pH 7), and basic (pH 10) environments. The solutions used were phosphate buffered solutions
(PBS) of analytical grade. The measurements were performed using a conventional three-electrode setup
comprising a freshly anodized Ag/Ag⁺ electrode as the reference, the coated sensor as the working
electrode, and a platinum (Pt) wire as the counter electrode. The OCP was recorded continuously as the
solution pH was systematically altered, with the experiment designed to assess the reversibility, sensitivity,
and dynamic electrochemical behavior of the coated sensor in real-time. The applied potential window

spanned from –0.5 V to +0.5 V, although the sensor’s actual response was confined within approximately
–0.2 V to +0.18 V, indicating stable operation within the designed range as shown in the data provided by
the Caltech team.

Initially, beginning around 600 seconds, the sensor was immersed in a pH 4 buffer solution. Here, the OCP
progressively increased and stabilized near +0.17 V, suggesting that the surface of the coated sensor is
protonated in the acidic environment. This response likely arises from the interaction of H⁺ ions with
functional groups embedded in the sensor's coating layer. The sensor reached a steady-state potential
quickly, indicating rapid charge redistribution and favorable proton transport at the sensor–electrolyte
interface. As the system transitioned to pH 7 (around 800 seconds), the OCP began to drop, stabilizing
around 0 V. This shift reflected a decrease in proton activity and a re-equilibration of the surface-bound
species as the sensor adjusted to the neutral environment. Importantly, the transition occurred smoothly
without erratic fluctuations, indicating that the coating layer supports a stable and gradual electrochemical
response to changing ionic conditions. At approximately 900 seconds, the solution was replaced within a
pH 10 buffer, resulting in a sharp decline in the OCP to around –0.17 V. This drop is consistent with
deprotonation processes at the sensor interface, where OH⁻ ions dominate and interact with the coating to
form a more negatively charged surface. The magnitude and direction of this potential shift provide direct
evidence of the sensor's responsiveness to basic environments and its capacity to distinguish between
proton-rich and hydroxide-rich conditions.

The process was then repeated in a second cycle starting at ~1200 seconds, where the solution was again
adjusted to pH 4. The OCP quickly returned to its original acidic-state value near +0.17 V, demonstrating
excellent reproducibility and chemical reversibility of the sensor’s coated interface. The transitions to pH
7 (~1400 s) and pH 10 (~1600 s) again produced the same stepwise potential drops observed in the first
cycle, closely retracing the earlier path. This consistent cyclic performance highlights the robustness and
memory-free response of the coated sensor, even under repeated pH perturbations. The sensor's ability to
recover its original potential in each cycle without drifting or hysteresis was especially notable. It indicates
that the coating is chemically stable, does not undergo irreversible reactions with the buffer components,
and maintains its electroactive properties across a broad pH range. The observed potential window
(spanning approximately –0.2 V to +0.18 V) lies comfortably within the operational design limits (–0.5 V
to +0.5 V), ensuring the reliability of long-term sensing without approaching degradation thresholds. This
OCP study provides compelling evidence that the coated sensor demonstrates strong pH sensitivity, rapid
electrochemical equilibration, and excellent cycle-to-cycle reproducibility.

Through these formulation screening, adhesion testing, and environmental/electrochemical stability
assessments, the CCMC-CA hydrogel has emerged as a strong candidate for further development. Its
physical resilience, chemical resistance, and excellent adhesion characteristics position it as a suitable
material for robust sensor platforms. The upcoming OCP-based electrochemical validation and subsequent

Conclusion
This work represents a significant advancement in the development and validation of multifunctional
coatings and encapsulation strategies for microsensor systems designed for deployment in subsurface
environments such as CO2 sequestration sites. The team successfully formulated a nanoparticle-based
superhydrophobic coating to enhance surface repellency and support sensor transport and emplacement
within complex fluid environments. In parallel, a photocured fluorinated acrylate copolymer system was
developed, forming a durable, crosslinked polymer network capable of withstanding the chemical and
thermal stresses typical of downhole conditions.

These advanced coatings were applied to fully functional microsensors with dimensions of 1 mm × 1 mm
and 1 mm × 3 mm. Post-coating evaluations confirmed that sensor performance remained unaffected, with
no damage or degradation observed—demonstrating both the mechanical robustness of the coating and its
compatibility with delicate microsensor components. Building on this success, the coating platform was
further enhanced through the integration of magnetic nanoparticles, offering a novel method for guiding
and positioning sensors within the subsurface environment. This innovation has the potential to improve
emplacement precision and overall system reliability.

In addition, the team encapsulated dummy sensors with buoyancy-compensating acrylate formulations,
enabling precise density control and transport behavior. Lab-scale testing under simulated wellbore
conditions demonstrated reliable movement of the coated sensors through mud-filled casing annuli,
successful emplacement at the reservoir/annulus interface, and rejection from cement layers due to

 67

engineered surface properties. These results represent a major technical milestone, confirming Technology
Readiness Level 3 (TRL 3) for sensor emplacement during lab-simulated cementing processes.

Despite an initial change in scope—from identifying a commercial coating vendor to internally evaluating
COTS available sensor platforms—the team adapted effectively to meet project goals. The acquisition of
pH sensors from Caltech enabled continued progress, allowing practical testing of coating formulations in
representative CO2-rich (lightly acidic) environments. These efforts have provided key insights into coating
performance, sensor durability, and system integration requirements, laying a strong foundation for
advancing to higher technology readiness levels and preparing for future field-scale demonstrations.

The group identified, shortlisted, and evaluated three hydrogel coating systems. The polyaniline (PANI)-
based hydrogel exhibited delamination from the sensor substrate, even when pre-treated with 3-
glycidyloxypropyltrimethoxysilane (3-GPS), a widely used silane coupling agent, and was rejected. The
Methocel-Calcium Alginate (MCA) composite hydrogel adhered better, but its high viscosity resulted in
uneven coating thickness and instability under extreme pH conditions, which also precluded its use. The
most promising formulation tested was the Calcium Carboxymethyl Cellulose–Calcium Alginate (CCMC-
CA) hydrogel. It exhibited several key advantages, including high transparency, effective moisture
retention, and excellent adhesive characteristics. This formulation was used to coat a pH sensor and
demonstrate its changes in open circuit potential at pH levels of 4, 7, and 10.

Collectively, the achievements of this task directly support core project objectives and demonstrate the
viability of robust, coated microsensor systems for long-term performance monitoring in geologically
complex and chemically challenging environments.

Introduction
Carbon dioxide (CO2) and other environmental gases are being captured, and solutions are being identified
to effectively store the gases. One effective approach for achieving long-term storage involves injecting
CO2 into the subsurface through boreholes drilled to depths ranging from 2,000 to 4,000 meters, as
illustrated in Figure 4-1. To prevent the surrounding rock from collapsing, a water-filled steel casing is
utilized, allowing CO2 to be injected through tubing within the casing. Sensors are strategically positioned
both above and below the cap rock to monitor for CO2 and ensure that none of the stored gas escapes back
into the atmosphere. These instruments continuously monitor for leaks between the cap rock, casing
annulus, and casing throughout the injection period, which can last up to 20 years, and for an additional 20
or more years after the borehole is sealed.

 70

 Figure 4-1: Illustration of CO₂ injection into the subsurface.

Monitoring fluids at significant depths presents considerable challenges, particularly in preventing the
formation of leakage pathways back to the surface over the course of more than 40 years of operation.
Current technologies for communicating with downhole instrumentation, including fiber optics and
electrical wiring, are not ideally suited for this application. Each of these solutions has inherent drawbacks,
such as susceptibility to damage during installation, the potential to create leak pathways, and long-term
degradation. To address these challenges, a collaborative effort involving The University of Texas at Austin
(UT-Austin), Sandia National Laboratories (SNL), the California Institute of Technology (Caltech), and
the Research Triangle Institute (RTI) aims to demonstrate a subsurface system capable of detecting fluids
in the casing annulus using a combination of the NOV pipe and wireless sensors embedded in the cement
sheath of the casing annulus.

The primary goal of this project was to advance a fully integrated system for validating fluid detection in
the casing annulus to Technology Readiness Level (TRL) 5. The system will leverage a combination of
technologies, including Pipe (NOV IntelliServ) for communication and power, wireless radio frequency
identification (RFID) sensors for fluid detection within the cement, and a custom Smart Collar to relay
RFID data to the surface (Figure 4-2). SNL is responsible for developing the Smart Collar, which will
collect data from the RFID sensors and transmit it to the surface.

Task 4 work focused on the Smart Collar technology being developed by SNL. For this demonstration,
NOV Pipe (IntelliPipe/IntelliServ, NOV) will facilitate communication and power between the surface and

Simultaneously, bi-directional communication will be employed to capture sensor data, with the onboard
Smart Collar microcontroller relaying this information back to a computer located at the ground surface.

The integration of these advanced technologies—NOV Pipe communication, wireless coated RFID sensors,
and the innovative Smart Collar—represents a significant advancement in the monitoring of subsurface gas

storage. By enabling real-time detection of potential leaks and providing continuous data on the conditions
within the casing annulus, this system enhances the reliability and safety of carbon subsurface storage
efforts. The Smart Collar's ability to wirelessly power the RFID sensors and transmit critical information
to the surface without creating leakage pathways ensures effective monitoring over extended periods. This
comprehensive approach not only addresses the challenges associated with traditional monitoring methods
but also instills confidence in the long-term integrity of CO₂ storage sites. Ultimately, the successful
implementation of this integrated system will contribute to the broader goal of ensuring that captured gases
are securely stored and do not re-enter the atmosphere. This chapter will review the efforts from SNL to
develop the Smart Collar prototype, establish communication and power through the NOV Pipe, and
facilitate communication with the RFID sensors.

Materials and Methods
This section provides an overview of the Smart Collar electronics, NOV Wired Pipe, subsurface experiment
configuration, custom antenna, RFID sensor, and housing.

NOV-Internally Wired Pipe
To establish an electrical connection between the Smart Collar and surface instrumentation, NOV pipe
(IntelliPipe™ for IntelliServ™ system) was utilized. This 32-foot drill string incorporates a wire that uses
inductive coupling at the joints to create an electrical path. Due to the nature of inductive coupling, a direct
current (DC) signal cannot propagate through the pipe. The NOV Pipe serves both communication and
power functions for the Smart Collar. Although typically used for drilling applications, it is repurposed here
as a casing. The NOV Pipe has been characterized using a vector network analyzer (VNA), revealing an
attenuation range of 5–20 dB from 3.5 to 28 MHz across five segments of pipe. The S-parameter data,
including gain and reflection data as a function of frequency, is shown in Figure 4-4.

 Figure 4-4: Insertion loss and port 1 reflection for the NOV Pipe.

A pair of off-the-shelf Ethernet-over-Coax (EoC) modules were selected based on the performance of the
NOV Pipe. These modules operate between DC and 28 MHz and are flexible enough to allow for the
selection of an optimal operating frequency within that range. They can also function effectively with more
than 60 dB of attenuation between them. Testing on the NOV pipe demonstrated data rates exceeding 80

Smart Collar System Block Diagram
The development of the Smart Collar electronic system has undergone several iterations, as illustrated in
Figures 4-6 through 4-9. These changes were driven by various methods of communication and power
transmission through the Wired Pipe, the energy-harvesting mechanisms to power the Smart Collar, and
the communication with the RFID sensors. The four primary designs are summarized below, highlighting
the challenges encountered and the corresponding modifications made.

The original block diagram for the Smart Collar (Figure 4-6) is divided into three key elements: 1)
communication and power from the surface to the Smart Collar, 2) energy harvesting, and 3) RFID
communication. In Element 1, communication was designed to operate from DC to 30 MHz using an off-
the-shelf EoC module, while an alternating current (AC) signal was generated from 35 to 45 MHz. These
two signals were combined for input into the NOV pipe and subsequently split within the Smart Collar
using low-pass filters (LPF) and high-pass filters (HPF). A second EoC module within the Smart Collar
facilitated communication with the microcontroller. The AC signal was converted to a digital current using
a bridge rectifier and supercapacitors. Element 2 involved the DC signal passing through a buck-boost
converter to provide stable 12 V and 5 V sources for the EoC, microcontroller, and RFID electronics.
Element 3 comprised an off-the-shelf RFID reader paired with Wolfspeed’s RF power amplifier
(CMPA0527005F) to boost the RF signal to the RFID sensor. The RF amplifier utilized Pasternack’s RF
circulator (PE83CR007) at both the input and output, allowing the signal generated by the RFID reader to

 74

be amplified from 1 watt to 9 watts while enabling the reflected signal from the RFID sensor to return to
the RFID reader, as the amplifier functions as an RF isolator.

 Figure 4-6: Smart Collar block diagram Design 1.

In transitioning from Design 1 to Design 2 (Figure 4-9), Element 1 underwent modifications to the operating
frequencies for communication and power. During testing of the 35-45 MHz signal, high-speed diodes
suitable for the bridge rectifier could not be identified for efficient full-wave rectification of the AC signal.
Consequently, lower frequency diodes were required, leading to the selection of a 4 MHz diode (within the
low-loss frequency band of the Wired Pipe) for effective rectification. The chosen diodes were Silicon
Carbide Schottky diodes (Toshiba TRS2E65F, S1Q) with low parasitic capacitance (8.7 pF), enabling rapid
switching and high-frequency rectification. Figure 4-10 shows the operating bands for the AC power signal
and the EoC communication band.

As such, a 4 MHz signal is generated at the surface to power the Smart Collar. To accommodate the
frequency change of the power AC signal, the EoC signal would then operate from the 10-28 MHz (lower
end is 10 MHz to prevent overlap between the AC signal and the communication signal). The EoC has an
automatic feature to adjust the operating frequency and amplification to accommodate for the performance
of the connection between the two EoC, thus allowing the unit to continue to relay information with a small
degradation of the operating data rate. Element 3 of the circuit was altered due to the inability to
communicate with the Caltech RFID sensors through the cement and other media between the RFID
antenna and RFID sensor. The idea was to have two antennas, one dedicated for 1-watt communications at
902-928 MHz and second antenna tuned at a different frequency that is producing high RF power primarily
for powering the RFID sensor. The hypothesis was that the RFID sensor was not obtaining enough energy
to power up the internal electronics of the RFID sensor.

Subsurface Experiment Configuration
Early in the project, the UT-Austin team designed a field demonstration scheme to conduct a field
experiment with the Smart Collar in a vertical well, allowing for the flexibility to change the samples near
the Smart Collar. This configuration involves a well that accommodates both the Smart Collar/NOV pipe
and a parallel 5-inch PVC pipe filled with water. A 1-inch plunger (pipe) containing a cement-encased
RFID sensor would be positioned in the water, oriented perpendicular to the Smart Collar. This technique,
developed by UT-Austin, is illustrated in Figure 4-11.

 Figure 4-11: Illustration of the vertical well configuration for the Smart Collar experiment.

In this setup, the RF signal must propagate through various materials, including Delrin, sand/rock/dirt,
PVC, water, and cement, before reaching the RFID sensor. Each of these materials presents impedance
mismatch boundaries, leading to significant propagation losses and reflection points. Although the signal
transmitted from the Smart Collar can potentially reach the RFID sensor through all these media, the RFID
sensor must also reflect the signal back to the Smart Collar. This reflection introduces additional attenuation
and noise due to the multiple reflections at each media interface, severely degrading the signal-to-noise
ratio (SNR). Figure 4-12 illustrates the RF signal propagation through these materials.

 78

 Figure 4-12: Illustration of RF signal propagation through various media.

Given these concerns, multiple laboratory experiments were conducted to establish communication
between the Smart Collar and the RFID device. Off-the-shelf RFID tags were utilized in these experiments
due to persistent communication issues with Caltech’s RFID sensors, even in air. Various setups were
created to demonstrate communication, including different materials, device positions, RFID devices, and
reader power levels. The final experimental setup involved a 6.5-inch diameter PVC pipe representing the
Smart Collar shell and a second 6.5-inch diameter PVC pipe with slits to simulate the parallel pipe for
positioning the cement-covered RFID device. An image of this updated setup is shown in Figure 4-13. The
experiments employed FEIG Electronic’s RFID reader, designed for 2 watts of output power, along with

optimizing the setup, communication was not successfully established for the vertical configuration, even
with the off-the-shelf RFID sensor featuring a significantly larger antenna than Caltech’s RFID sensor.

Caltech RFID Sensor and Reader
A significant challenge in this project has been establishing reliable communication with Caltech’s RFID
sensors. Early on, Sandia assumed that Caltech utilized a standard RFID protocol, prompting the acquisition
of small form-factor RFID readers that could fit within the Smart Collar housing. However, attempts to
communicate with the sensors using off-the-shelf readers were unsuccessful because a custom protocol,
specific to Caltech’s sensors, was developed, requiring the use of Caltech’s proprietary RFID reader.
Fortunately, this custom reader is compact enough to potentially fit within the Smart Collar housing, but it
was the only unit available at the time.

 81

Through collaboration with Caltech, communication with the sensors was successfully established using
their custom reader, but only when the sensor was positioned just a couple millimeters away from the
antenna. Given that cement is a significantly lossy medium compared to air, it was needed to demonstrate
longer-distance communication capabilities. To achieve this, the topology proposed in block Diagram 1
(Figure 4-6) was implemented, which included a power amplifier and two RF circulators to boost the output
power of the reader from 1 watt to 9 watts. Incremental adjustments were made to the power amplifier to
mitigate the risk of damaging the reader and the sensor. Despite these efforts, improved communication
range was not observed, and attempts were continued to increase the power level, which ultimately resulted
in damage to the reader. This setback caused significant delays as both Sandia and Caltech worked to repair
the unit. It was discovered that the original chip used in the reader had been discontinued due to a product
line acquisition by another company. Eventually, Sandia was able to repair the unit by sourcing an
integrated circuit (IC) with a different part number from the new company.

With the reader functional again (Figure 4-16), it was decided to reverse-engineer the unit, as the software
documentation was lacking. Collaboration was undertaken with a former Caltech student involved in the
reader's development, and through this, data were able to be interpreted. By utilizing two RF circulators, an
RF mixer, and a 16-bit resolution oscilloscope placed between the antenna and the reader, the transmit and
receive signals between the reader and the sensor were successfully tapped into and observed.

 Figure 4-16: RFID reader communicating with RFID sensors.

Communication between the devices is achieved by modulating the length of time for the on and off states.
The sensor communicates by loading the RF signal, which either reduces or increases the amount of energy
transmitted back to the reader. The difference between the loaded and non-loaded states is typically 2 to 3
mV, as illustrated in Figures 4-17 and 4-18. With a solid understanding of the communication mechanism,
Sandia decided to assemble a custom RFID reader using off-the-shelf RF components. This approach allows
for greater adaptability to handle high reflected energy from the surrounding materials and sensors.

attaching the RFID sensor to an external large antenna, and/or 2) designing a custom antenna specifically
tuned for cement, allowing for direct contact with the cement and eliminating the plastic shell between the
antenna and the cement. Given the challenges associated with designing, fabricating, and packaging an
antenna for the RFID sensor, a focus on developing a custom cement-tuned antenna was opted for.

critical data for designing the custom-tuned antenna. The cement was characterized using the open-probe

To report, the powering mechanism of the Smart Collar will be reviewed. As discussed in the previous
section and illustrated in Figure 4-12, the Smart Collar requires smart power management to effectively
harvest the AC signal propagating through the NOV pipe and power the internal electronics when sufficient
energy is available. Starting from a worst-case scenario of 0% charge in the supercapacitors, energy is
gradually harvested as power transfers down the Wired Pipe. Buck-boost circuits are employed to elevate
the low power output from the Wired Pipe. A Torex Semiconductor XC6138NARJMR-G power
management integrated circuit (PMIC) and an Infineon Technologies CY8CKIT-059 PSoC microcontroller
ensure that all electronics remain in an 'open' state (no current flow), allowing electrons to primarily flow
into the supercapacitors. Once the supercapacitors reach approximately 9 V, the PMIC activates the
microcontroller, which checks the voltage levels of the supercapacitors. After this check, the
microcontroller enters an ultra-low power sleep mode, periodically checking the voltage level. When the
voltage reaches 11.5 V, the microcontroller allows charging to continue for an additional 30 minutes to

 94

account for voltage relaxation, which helps achieve a more uniform state of charge within the
supercapacitors.

Upon completion of charging, the microcontroller activates a latching relay (TXS2SA-L-4.5V-Z) to power
the RFID Inc. reader (S4-8030-04) and collect two sets of RFID temperature sensor data before quickly
deactivating the RFID reader and storing the data within the microcontroller. Given that the reader draws
more than 1 amp of current, the supercapacitors can only power the system for a brief period, necessitating
the capture of two samples. After a few seconds, the microcontroller activates the Ethernet-over-Coax
(EoC) and Arduino via another latching relay. The microcontroller relays the information to the Arduino,
which converts the serial data into an Ethernet dataset, with the EoC converting this data for transmission
through a coaxial cable (in this application, the Wired Pipe). Once the message is relayed, the EoC and
Arduino are immediately deactivated. After two minutes, the microcontroller checks the voltage level of
the supercapacitors to repeat the data collection process from the RFID sensors for transmission to the
surface. At the other end of the Wired Pipe, a coax splitter facilitates communication and power. As
previously noted, a 4 MHz AC signal is utilized to transmit power to the Smart Collar. It is important to
mention that when the Smart Collar's charge is fully depleted, it may take 12-24 hours to fully recharge
through several segments of Wired Pipe. The surface EoC converts the coaxial data back to Ethernet
protocol. For simplicity, an Arduino on the surface continuously monitors the Ethernet port on the EoC to
capture the transmitted data.

Conclusion
This work advanced the development of wireless power and communication systems for downhole
environments, aiming to eliminate the need for additional wiring in the wellbore. The core achievement
was establishing a robust link from the surface to sensors embedded in the casing annulus, moving the
technology from an initial TRL 3 to a TRL 4-5 demonstration with the creation of the Smart Collar
prototype.

The methodology focused on iterative development and rigorous testing. Specialized NOV pipe and
essential materials were acquired to build borehole instrumentation and power/communication systems for
laboratory evaluation. This foundational work allowed to progress to procuring NOV pipe for field
deployment and developing the Smart Collar instrumentation. The Smart Collar was designed to
communicate wirelessly with commercial sensors, marking a critical step in preparing the technology for
field laboratory demonstrations. The culmination of this effort involved comprehensive field experiments,
where the developed technology was integrated into a full system, demonstrating its functional readiness in
a representative downhole setting.

We successfully enabled stable NOV pipe power transmission, reliably charging capacitor banks to about
12 V with an energy storage capacity of at least 25 Joules to power downhole electronics and support
communication. The downhole electronics package performed all its designed functions, including data
interpretation, transmission, and sensor queries. Crucially, the capability to power and interface with RFID
sensors was provided through representative annular cement materials, and the project culminated in the
successful design, fabrication, and field installation and demonstration of the Smart Collar prototype.

The implications of this work are substantial for subsurface reservoir monitoring. By demonstrating a
reliable wireless power and communication link, this technology paves the way for more efficient, durable,
and easily deployable sensor networks in harsh downhole and subsurface environments. The ability to
wirelessly power and communicate with sensors through the casing annulus reduces operational
complexities, enhances data acquisition capabilities, and ultimately supports more effective management
of subsurface resources. This breakthrough offers a significant step towards real-time, comprehensive
monitoring, crucial for optimizing recovery and ensuring operational safety.

Introduction and Summary
This chapter documents the design, preparation, and field demonstration of a low-risk, field-representative
testbed to evaluate wireless data communication and sensing for casing-annulus monitoring. The
demonstration validates Smart Collar communication through NOV wired pipe and wireless sensor
response under controlled exposure to CO₂-bearing fluids and temperature transients, using commercial
reference instruments for benchmarking.

Field validation is a critical step in demonstrating the performance of wireless CO₂-sensing technologies
under conditions that approximate the complexities of a carbon sequestration well. Unlike controlled
laboratory testing, field deployments must account for constraints imposed by geology, existing
infrastructure, communications paths, hydraulic conditions, and sensor survivability. Accordingly, the
design of the field experiment for this project evolved significantly as the technical maturity of the
microsensors, practical deployment considerations, and site-specific constraints became clearer.

The initial field concept centered on a cross-borehole injection and monitoring experiment at the Devine
Fracture Pilot Site (DFPS), where multi-well spacing, high-resolution subsurface characterization, and
existing instrumentation offered an attractive platform for testing. During this phase, reactive-transport
simulations were used to estimate whether pH-adjusted fluids injected from one well would generate a
measurable pH signal at an offset observation well. Commercial membrane-based CO₂ probes (Vaisala
GMP343 and Pro-Oceanus Mini-CO₂) were planned as reference instruments to benchmark any cross-well
signals, with AquaTroll-600 pH and temperature measurements providing additional context. However,
delays in microsensor development and the lack of sufficient sensors for bulk emplacement into cement,
combined with uncertainties in the detectability of cross-well chemical perturbations at shallow depth, made
this multi-well configuration increasingly impractical.

To preserve the ability to evaluate RF communication, powering, and CO₂ detection in a controlled
environment, the team transitioned to a single-borehole validation strategy. This approach simplified
hydraulic control and eliminated dependence on multi-well interactions while still allowing sensors to be
exposed to CO₂-bearing fluids. As field planning advanced, it became apparent that more precise control
over sensor spacing relative to the Smart Collar was required to ensure interpretable RF performance
measurements. Additionally, the existing wells at DFPS could not be modified to accommodate the tailored
hardware interfaces and sensor placement geometries needed for the prototype system.

 108

These constraints led to the development of a simplified horizontal trench-based field setup, engineered
specifically to support the first full demonstration of the Smart Collar and wireless CO₂ sensors under
field-representative conditions. In this final configuration, a 105-ft NOV casing string was installed within
a shallow trench and backfilled with native soil to emulate downhole electromagnetic properties while
providing full accessibility to the hardware. A modified 1,000-gallon water tank at the casing terminus
enabled controlled introduction of CO₂-saturated water or gas, allowing sensor response and RF
communication performance to be evaluated in a stable, repeatable environment. This configuration offered
substantial advantages: flexible sensor positioning, clear RF signal paths, simplified hydraulic isolation,
and the ability to modify components as the design matured—none of which were feasible in the original
multi-well plan.

Through this staged design evolution—from cross-borehole to single-borehole, and ultimately to a
horizontal trench—the field experiment became an adaptable, low-risk test platform capable of delivering
definitive data on CO₂-sensor performance and Smart Collar communication behavior.

What This Demonstration Accomplished:

 • End-to-end data link: Verified Smart Collar → NOV pipe → surface data pathway under
 field-representative conditions.
 • Wireless sensing response: Demonstrated RF temperature measurements with high agreement to a
 reference, confirming data integrity through the full chain.
 • CO₂ exposure control & referencing: Established repeatable CO₂/pH transients and validated
 reference CO₂ probes for subsequent microsensor benchmarking.
 • Practical testbed: The horizontal trench-and-tank configuration provided precise sensor–collar
 positioning, straightforward access, and a no-permit path to scalable, repeatable experiments.

The following sections describe the preparation, construction, instrumentation, and execution of this field
demonstration in detail. In order to simplify, the details of the reactive transport modeling have been moved

Field Experimental Setup Design Modifications
UT-Austin chose the Devine Fracture Pilot Site (DFPS), located within the 100-acre Devine Field Test Site
(DFTS), about 45 miles southwest of San Antonio, Texas, for the CO2 and pH sensor field tests. This site
is an ideal testbed because it already has the necessary infrastructure, including multiple wells for injecting
fluids, monitoring conditions, and cross-referencing pressure, temperature, and salinity data. This test site
also boasts a detailed subsurface characterization with extensive stratigraphic information from logs and
cores. The UT-Austin’s Advanced Energy Consortium (AEC) previously installed an inverted five-spot
well setup at DFPS, each about 300 ft deep. The central steel-cased well is designed for high-pressure fluid
injections. The four surrounding observation wells are equipped with PVC casings and feature electrical
resistivity tomography (ERT), along with permanent fiber optic cables for both temperature (DTS) and

 109

acoustic sensing (DAS). This existing infrastructure has been crucial in pinpointing the exact location of a
known fracture anomaly, filled with a contrast agent, at a depth of 175 ft, as documented by Ahmadian et
al. (2018, 2019). During the verification of this anomaly, multiple observation wells were drilled, logged,
and completed. Specifically, two 200-ft-deep wells (DMWs 1, 2) were screened and gravel-packed at the
170 to 180-ft depth interval. Two additional wells (DMWs 3, 4) were screened and gravel-packed at the
130 to 140-ft depth interval to monitor fluid containment during deeper injections. All these wells are
exclusively available for the team's use in this work. The DFPS offers several practical benefits. The
Principal Investigator (PI) manages the site, and existing permits are in place for the team's use. Its
convenient day-trip distance from UT-Austin makes logistics straightforward. Furthermore, its proximity
to the Eagle Ford play provides access to a large base of oilfield operators and service providers. The DOE
authorized the use of the DFPS for these proposed studies, allowing the team to proceed with field test
planning.

Figure 5-1 illustrates the original proposed instrumented well. This well would be constructed by drilling

Water Tank Experiments in Lab to Evaluate Reference Probe Reliability
To better understand measurements of the microsensors under development in this project within varied
field conditions, laboratory evaluations of critical experimental design components were conducted. The
initial aim was to use reference pH sensors for these evaluations. However, due to technical issues
(specifically delamination at high temperatures) encountered with the pH microsensors under construction,
project manager approval was received to focus solely on CO2 sensing. To validate CO2 microsensor
readings, the performance of two reference probes, the Vaisala GMP343 and Pro-Oceanus Mini-CO2, was
assessed, both before and after CO2 injection and with changes in the working fluid. According to the
available literature, these probes are among the most accurate and stable probes in the favorable small size
to be deployed in confined spaces such as wellbores and capable of measuring CO2 concentrations in both
water and gas phases. When submerged in water, both sensors measure the aqueous-phase CO2
concentration based on the partial pressure of CO2 gas diffused through a membrane from the aqueous
phase into a cavity that is equipped with a non-dispersive infrared detector (NDIR). The reading accuracy
is ± (5 ppm+2% of reading) and ±2% of the maximum range for the GMP343 CO 2 probe and Mini-CO2
sensor, respectively.

For these evaluations, probes were immersed in a 55-liter tank containing tap water or a 200-gallon tank
containing water-saturated coarse sand, with CO2 bubbled from the bottom. To achieve high dissolved CO2
concentrations, a microbubble generation system was implemented using either six series-connected
FluvalTM ceramic CO2 diffusers (Figure 5-8a) or a Deutsche 300-mm stainless steel carbonation stone
(Figure 5-8b). A diffusion stone is a porous metal device commonly used in the brewing industry to
introduce CO2 into beverages and made of stainless steel or sintered materials with tiny, precise pores. As
shown in Figure 5-8, the diffusers were separated adequately from the probes to minimize the interference

 117

of the readings by the gas bubbles and rather expose the probes to the dissolved CO2 in water, considering
that the target was to measure the dissolved CO2 concentration in water. Nitrogen (N2) bubbling was used
to reduce CO2 concentration. pH with an In Situ AquaTroll 600 transducer was also monitored, as CO 2
presence acidifies water. The two primary testing media—a water-filled tank and a sand tank—simulated
the expected field conditions.

The inputs to these Phreeqc models are pressure, temperature, density, and redox, recorded by the Aqua
Troll 600 transducer, as well as the expected partial pressure of CO2 in the aqueous phase, which is equal
to the measured partial pressure by the CO2 probes. The Phreeqc input files for these batch-test models are
presented in Appendix 1. These models predicted pH of 5.6, 5.4, and 5.4 in States 1 through 3, whereas the
recorded pH by the Aqua Troll 600 transducer in these states were 5.5, 4.5, and 4.8. These values show a
small discrepancy between measured and modeled pH before CO2 bubbling, and overestimation of pH by
the model after CO2 bubbling. This overestimation can be due to unknown species in the aqueous phase
that may have generated H+ and consequently lowered the pH but were not considered in the models.

The mechanisms that could lead to the absence of a ubiquitous positive correlation between CO2
concentration inside the probe chamber and dissolved CO2 concentration in water were further investigated.
One mechanism could have a strong diurnal effect due to steep daily temperature changes throughout this
experiment. Figure 5-38c shows a good negative correlation between the ambient temperature and the
dissolved CO2 concentration in water through time. This negative correlation is consistent with the decrease
of CO2 solubility in water with temperature increase and vice versa (Weiss, 1974). The mild increase of the
dissolved CO2 concentration in water during 1/18/2024 20:00 ‒ 1/19/2024 13:45 (Observation 5) despite
the relatively large increase of the CO2 concentration inside the probe chamber may originate from the
increase of temperature during this interval leading to less solubility of CO2 in water.

These results demonstrate the strong role of temperature in CO2 dissolution in water and consequent pH
changes in addition to the role of the gaseous CO2 in the probe chamber that is available for dissolution in
water. The temperature effect on pH changes may seem dominant at low CO2 concentrations and cold
temperatures. At high CO2 concentrations (above 20,000 ppmv), as investigated in the previous
experiments, the role of CO2 concentration in changing pH seems dominant. The initial remaining water at
the bottom of the tank may have contributed to this large difference. Thereby, removing the entire tank
water and refilling the tank with fresh tap water may resolve this issue.

 Discussion
 The field test campaigns on January 15-16 and March 17-19, 2025, resulted in informative results,
 confirming the Smart Collar’s operational reliability, sensor accuracy, and environmental adaptability. The
 developed app streamlined data acquisition for complex tests involving pH adjustment, CO2 injection,
 heating, and water circulation. The Smart Collar consistently provided repeatable temperature
 measurements, even under rapid environmental changes. The agreement between the Smart Collar RF
 sensors and the AquaTroll-600 reference transducer validates the system’s calibration accuracy. Notably,
 communication remained stable during fluid injection near the NOV pipe, demonstrating the Smart Collar's
 resilience to hydrological interference—a critical attribute for subsurface monitoring in applications such
 as carbon sequestration and pipeline integrity assessment. The successful simulation of temperature
 monitoring during carbonation, heating, and environmental mixing suggests the potential for the Smart
 Collar to operate in and monitor multiple in-situ parameters. The findings from this campaign will inform
 future hardware improvements, aid in calibrating predictive models, and support larger-scale field testing
 in operational settings.

 Field testing such monitoring systems in CO2 sequestration environments requires careful consideration of
 the challenging subsurface conditions, including high pressure, high temperatures (HPHT), and chemically
 reactive fluids. These conditions are typical of deep saline aquifers or depleted hydrocarbon reservoirs,
 where CO2 is injected 800 m below the surface. At these depths, CO2 becomes supercritical, exhibiting
 properties of both a gas and a liquid, which enhances its mobility and penetration through porous media.

 152

Pressures typically range from 10 to 20 MPa and temperatures from 30°C to 100°C, varying with the
geological setting. Beyond thermodynamic challenges, the geochemical environment has a significant
impact on material performance and system design. Injected CO2 dissolves in formation brines, forming
carbonic acid, which creates a corrosive, low-pH environment (as low as pH 3–4) for unprotected materials.
These brines are also highly saline, often exceeding 100,000 ppm total dissolved solids, posing risks of
chemical degradation, scaling, or general sensor drift. Consequently, any monitoring system must utilize
HPHT sensors coated with robust, chemically resistant materials that can withstand prolonged mechanical
stress and exposure to geochemical conditions. Testing efforts must therefore validate performance under
representative HPHT and geochemical scenarios, including buoyancy control, transport behavior, and the
ability to target specific geologic zones. Before field deployment, simulating these environmental factors
at a laboratory scale or pilot site is crucial.

Conclusion
To accurately predict subsurface pH changes from acid injection, core samples collected from 135 and 175
ft deep at the Devine Fracture Pilot Site (DFPS) were analyzed using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)
and Energy-Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS) imaging. The elemental analysis from EDS helped identify
potential reactants with the acidic fluids that were planned to inject at these two depths. These identified
reactions serve as crucial inputs for the computational models. To further support the reactive-transport
numerical model, hydrothermal experiments with Devine core samples were conducted. These experiments
focused on understanding how a Dry Acid aqueous solution affects the dissolution and transformation of
K-feldspar in sandstones from both 135 and 175 ft. After one week, the capsules were opened, solid
products dried and prepared for post-reaction SEM analysis. Based on the combined EDS and SEM
analyses of the DFPS rock samples, the reactive transport model was adjusted. K-feldspar and kaolinite
were the dominant reactive minerals in acidic solutions, with negligible calcite present, which differed from
the initial assumptions.

A comprehensive hydrogeological model for the DFPS was successfully developed. This model
incorporates the locations of existing injection and observation wells, which are screened and gravel-packed
at depths of 135 ft and 175 ft. The estimated petrophysical properties were integrated for the proposed
injection zones, based on available stratigraphic data, and a new instrumented monitoring well was
positioned between existing observation wells 3 and 4. Complementing this, a reactive fluid transport model
was developed that could simulate the injection of pH and salinity-adjusted water slugs. To accelerate these
computationally intensive simulations, parallel processing was leveraged by installing the commercial
reservoir simulation program CMG on the Stampede-2 Supercomputer at the Texas Advanced Computing
Center (TACC). Several acid injection test cases were then completed to evaluate the parallel-processing
speed-up. Finally, the reactive fluid transport models utilized determined ion concentrations from both the
resident and injected water to simulate acidic and pH-neutral solution injections at either 135 ft or 175 ft
depths. These injection scenarios were specifically designed to observe and track pH changes across all
associated monitoring wells. Using the mineral composition obtained from geochemical analyses led to
numerical convergence issues. To achieve stable simulations and produce reasonable pH contours, the
volumetric fraction of these minerals was subsequently reduced in the latest models. The initial estimations
of effective pH in monitoring wells were consistent with the injected fluid's pH, providing a valuable

 153

starting point for further validation. However, due to high-temperature delamination issues with the pH
microsensors, project approval was received to shift the focus entirely to CO2 sensing for subsequent work.

To validate the readings of the CO2 microsensors targeted for development in this project, a robust
workflow was established comparing their measurements against reference transducers. For this purpose,
two high-precision CO2 probes were acquired, modified, and rigorously tested in both water and fully
saturated sand tanks, using both deionized and tap water, at the Bureau of Economic Geology. To simulate
downhole conditions for these tests, a laboratory setup was constructed: a water tank filled halfway with a
sand pack containing two installed screened PVC pipes for deploying a gas diffusion stone and pH and CO2
probes. Gas bubbling experiments in these tanks confirmed the reliability of these probes for dissolved CO2
concentration measurement. An adjacent pH probe was also used to ensure accurate CO2 concentration
readings. Phreeqc batch-test models were developed and validated against these pH measurements. The
gas-bubbling experiment was repeated using the water-saturated sand pack to evaluate the sensors'
capability in monitoring CO2 concentration changes resulting from atmospheric CO2 dissolution in water,
prior to active CO2 bubbling. The performance of the Pro-Oceanus Mini-CO2 probe was also rigorously
evaluated during this process.

The CO2 sensors were successfully deployed adjacent to the NOV pipe at the DFTS, building on the
extensive laboratory experience. This site proved ideal for sensor tests due to several key advantages. From
a practical standpoint, the DFTS is managed by the PI, has existing permits for the team's use, and is
conveniently located a day trip away from UT-Austin. Its proximity to the Eagle Ford play also provided
easy access to numerous oilfield operators and service providers. The DOE authorized the use of the DFTS
for these studies, allowing the team to proceed with field test planning.

The field test setup included these activities: 1) identifying an appropriate open space at the DFTS for the
construction of the field experimental setup, 2) receiving the pipe joints and a specialized water tank, 3)
excavating a 109-ft trench, 4) constructing support structures, 5) carefully positioning and connecting the
drilling pipes within the trench, 6) placing the water tank for sensor access, 7) installing an injection water
line, 8) backfilling the trench while maintaining access to pipe ends, and 9) implementing safety measures
around the test site. The specialized polyethylene water tank was modified by Plastic Welding and
Fabrication in Austin, allowing the instrumented Smart Collar to pass through the tank and ensure fluid-
tight sealing. To validate the readings from five radiofrequency (RF) sensors over a Smart Collar at the end
of the NOV pipe, the performance of a reference temperature probe was evaluated. Due to the complications
of producing pH microsensors by Caltech within the budgeted project timeframe before field deployment,
cm-sized COTS RF temperature sensors were used in those studies.

The field studies demonstrated the repeatability of temperature measurement by RF sensors by comparing
these measurements with a reference transducer reading. The strong agreement between the RF sensors'
temperature readings and those from the reference probe consistently demonstrated the Smart Collar's
reliable RF communication and the NOV pipe's dependable signal transmission. Further testing confirmed
that water flow in a PVC pipe adjacent to the induction couplings of buried NOV pipes had minimal impact
on RF signal communication. This demonstrated that the system maintains robust signal integrity even in
the presence of nearby fluid flow. The reliability of Pro-Oceanus Mini-CO2 and Vaisala GMP343 reference
CO2 probes was also confirmed in field conditions, and a correlation was recorded between their CO2

 154

concentration readings and pH changes measured by an AquaTroll-600 transducer. The decrease of
dissolved CO2 concentration was also measured due to heating of the carbonated water.

Recommendations
Due to the high frequency (900 MHz) for which the Caltech sensors were designed, communication with
the casing collar was in mm range in dry conditions but dropped precipitously in brine and a lossy liquid
environment. Thus, larger COTS temperature sensors (cm-scale) were obtained, designed for 900 MHz
frequency (see section 4). These worked better in the demonstration. UT-Austin secured seed funding from
the AEC and is pursuing additional sensor development and improvements to increase communication
distance and sensor miniaturization. The preliminary lab results obtained with AEC’s mm-sized RF sensors
have extended the interrogation in brine to 5 cm, which was the target for this DOE project. UT/AEC plans
to conduct additional field tests with developed sensors and a reference transducer once sensor development
is complete. Repeating the field tests is planned with the newly developed UT temperature sensors and a
reference transducer at the DFTS in 2026.

Hydrothermal Experiments in Externally Heated Cold-Seal Pressure
Vessels
To introduce injected fluid into the cement-mud interface, using existing wells at the DFPS was considered.
The EDS analysis of representative DFPS rock thin sections revealed a crucial insight: calcite, previously
assumed to be a primary reactive phase, was nearly absent at the depths studied. This indicates that calcite
is unlikely to drive reactions with acidic fluids at the DFPS. Instead, the analysis identified kaolinite and
K-feldspar as dominant rock species that are likely more critical for defining the major geochemical changes
expected during field acid treatment. Consequently, the geochemical models were updated to incorporate
reactions involving these minerals in an acidic solution:

Water dissociation reaction: 'OH-' + 'H+' ⇆‘H2O’,
K-feldspar dissolution reaction: KAlSi3O8 + 4H+ ⇆ 2H2O + K+ + Al3+ + 3SiO2(aq),
Kaolinite dissolution reaction: Al2Si2O5(OH)4 + 6H+ ⇆ 5H2O + 2Al3+ + 2SiO2(aq),
where KAlSi3O8 and Al2Si2O5(OH)4 represent chemical formulas of k-feldspar and kaolinite, respectively.

The volumetric content of kaolinite from EDS of multiple DFPS rock samples from 135 ft depth and
unconsolidated soil samples from 175 ft depth are as follows:

 1) 3%, obtained from DMW 4 at 135 ft depth;
 2) 15%, obtained from coarse-grained sample, collected from DMW 4 at 175 ft depth; and
 3) 5%, obtained from fine-grained samples collected from DMW 4 at 175 ft depth.

K-feldspar concentration is constant at 7% in all these cases.

The addition of Dry Acid (sodium bisulfate, NaHSO4) could trigger dissolution of K-feldspar and
precipitation of kaolinite as a bi-product via the combination of two reactions:

K-feldspar + 4H+ = 2H2O + K+ + Al3+ + 3SiO2(aq)
5H2O + 2Al3+ + 2SiO2(aq) = Kaolinite + 6H+

Dry Acid was diluted in deionized water to create a 12.5 wt% Dry Acid (NaHSO4) + H2O solution,
according to the reaction:

NaHSO4(s) + H2O(l) → Na+(aq) + SO2−4(aq) + H3O+(aq)

The formation of hydronium ion (H3O+) is responsible for the slightly acidic nature of the solution.

 164

conditions:

 week
 3) 135 ft sandstone immersed in 12.5 wt% Dry Acid + H2O solution at 200°C and 350 bars for one

 4) 175 ft sandstone immersed in 12.5 wt% Dry Acid + H2O solution at 100°C and 350 bars for one
 week
 5) 175 ft sandstone immersed in 12.5 wt% Dry Acid + H2O solution at 200°C and 350 bars for one
 week.

experiments. Reactions at more accurate reservoir conditions will be performed at a later stage. Assuming

Hydrogeological Simulations to Validate Prospective Subsurface pH
Sensing
A reactive-flow hydrogeological model of the DFPS was created using the CMG software program by
Computer Modelling Group Ltd. (2020), using the CMG-Builder™ and CMG-GEM™ packages (Figure
A1-13). The DFPS was a prime spot for a new instrumented well, and the model was informed by lab tests
that helped pinpoint reactive minerals. This hydrogeological model included: 1) the existing injection and
observation wells, which are screened and gravel-packed at depths of 135 ft (DMWs 3, 4) and 175 ft
(DMWs 1, 2); 2) estimated petrophysical properties for the planned injection zones, based on available
stratigraphy data; 3) a new proposed instrumented monitoring well positioned between Devine Monitoring
Wells (DMWs) 1 and 2 or 3 and 4; and 4) the ability to simulate injecting water with adjusted pH and
salinity. This model spans an area of 1355 ft × 1355 ft, extending from the surface down to a depth of 310
ft. It utilizes a structured grid with 380,666 gridblocks, centered around the injection well. Grid refinements
were incorporated at the injection depths of 135 ft and 175 ft (Figure A1-13). For single-phase simulations,
this number of gridblocks strikes a good balance between computational efficiency and sufficient
resolution, especially around the injection well(s) and the fractured zone. This model is set up with assumed
initial hydrostatic pressure and geothermal temperature gradients from the gathered data during the previous
coring and drilling studies and hydrogeological injection tests that had been conducted for the DFPS. In
these simulations, the porosity and permeability values were used from UT/BEG’s Wilson 9 core
collection, which had been subjected to laboratory geomechanical tests during the design phase of hydraulic
fracturing of the DFPS in 2017 (Ahmadian et al., 2018). Because Wilson 9 well is very close to the injection
well and demonstrates a similar stratigraphy at the depth of interest, it was initially assumed that the
geomechanical values of the injection site will be similar to those at Wilson 9 well. The permeability of the
hydraulically fractured zone in the model is estimated from the previous injections into this zone.

A high concentration of hydrogen ion (H+) was assumed in the injected water to evaluate the model
capabilities in acidic conditions, as was proposed in the SOPO. The reactive transport models include
provisions for dissolution and precipitation of solid phases (minerals and salts) which are governed by the
mass action law (see https://www.aqion.de/site/168). The mineral dissolution and precipitation reaction rate
is expressed by Transition State Theory (TST) in the CMG-GEMTM simulation package (Computer
Modelling Group Ltd., 2020).

Among two options for the proposed instrumented monitoring well location, it was decided to place the
new instrumented monitoring well between DMWs 3 and 4. This location was chosen based on the
simulation results of a non-reactive salinity transport model, primarily due to the significant difference in
the predicted size of the elevated salinity zone: it is expected to be 120 ft in diameter at 175 ft deep, but
only 45 ft in diameter at 135 ft deep. By positioning the well here, salinity changes from injection will be
observable at both the 175 ft and 135 ft depths.

To prepare for injecting into chemically equilibrated media in contact with resident water in the reactive-
flow model, 11-day simulations were run at idle conditions before injection began. The focus was on
aqueous phase reactions, assuming near-zero gas phase saturation in the aqueous phase and no gas content

 168

in the injected fluid. The injection of two slugs on consecutive days, each for 12 hours, was simulated using
the layer-cake hydrological model (Figure A1-13). The first slug had a pH of 3, and the second a pH of 7
(Figure A1-14). Simulation injected both slugs at a rate of 110 barrels/day (3.2 US gallons/minute) and
contained ions at concentrations based on water samples listed in Tables A1-1 through A1-3, in various
units. The resident water ion molality was also incorporated from Tables A1-2 through A1-3 into these
models. For reactive minerals, only K-feldspar and kaolinite were included, assuming all other minerals
were geochemically inactive.

Incorporating reactions into the hydrogeological model significantly increased computational costs by
requiring many more time steps. To improve numerical convergence, the injection rate was linearly
increased from zero to 110 barrels per day (BPD) [3.3 gallons per minute (gpm)] over an 8-hour injection
period. Also, to accelerate the computationally intensive reactive fluid transport models, parallel processing
was leveraged. The CMG software was installed on the Lonestar-5 Supercomputer at the Texas Advanced
Computing Center (TACC), and several acid injection test cases were run to quantify the speedup gained
from parallel processing.

Our initial attempts to model actual mineral concentrations were unsuccessful due to numerical model
convergence issues. This was likely caused by the active conversion of kaolinite to K-feldspar in contact
with resident water even before the acidic slug injection. This mineral content disturbance led to significant
porosity changes, which may have contributed to the drastic decrease of model convergence rate. To resolve
this, the K-feldspar and kaolinite content were reduced to 3% and 0.5%, respectively, in the simulation.
Additionally, the simulation effort focuses solely on injection into the fracture zone at a depth of 175 ft.

Hydrothermal Experiments in Externally Heated Cold-Seal Pressure Vessels

Based on the mineralogical composition of analyzed samples from 135 ft and 175 ft, the following mineral
phases will be the dominant reactants during acidic fluid injection:

At 135 ft depth:

 • Potassium feldspar (K-feldspar): SEM-EDS mapping indicates these feldspars contain very little
 sodium, leading to use potassium-rich endmembers (e.g., orthoclase, microcline, adularia,
 KAlSi3O8) in physical experiments and computational models.
 • Kaolinite (Al2Si2O5(OH)4)

At 175 ft depth:

 • Kaolinite (Al2Si2O5(OH)4)
 • K-feldspar (KAlSi3O8)
    •   Chlorite ((Mg,Fe)3(Si,Al)4O10(OH)2·(Mg,Fe)3(OH)6)
    •   Biotite (K(Mg,Fe)3(AlSi3O10)(F,OH)2)

K-feldspar commonly alters to the more stable kaolinite, while biotite can readily transform into chlorite
through natural chemical weathering in aqueous fluids or hydrothermal alteration. This conversion of biotite
to chlorite is particularly common under hydrothermal conditions.
 175

Given the relative abundance of these potentially reactive minerals, the dominant reaction at both the 135
ft and 175 ft injection depths was expected to be the conversion of K-feldspar to kaolinite. While typically
a very slow process during natural chemical weathering (Drever et al., 1992), this reaction can be
significantly accelerated in acidic environments. Recent laboratory experiments have shown this
transformation can occur rapidly, within hours to days, under acidic conditions (Yuan et al., 2018). Since
the formation of fine-grained kaolinite has the potential to occlude porosity and reduce permeability, proper
evaluation and quantification of K-feldspar breakdown to kaolinite at the injection site is critically
important.
