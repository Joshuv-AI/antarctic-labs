Serial AI-Enabled Electrocardiogram Trajectories During Heart Failure Treatment
Authors: Ah-Hyun Yoo, Hak Seung Lee, Kyung-Hee Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial intelligence–enabled electrocardiogram (AI-ECG) detects left ventricular systolic and diastolic dysfunction at single time points, but longitudinal behavior during heart failure treatment remains unexplored. We examined whether paired serial AI-ECG scores track echocardiographic and biochemical trajectories.MethodsIn this retrospective 4-patient case series, previously validated deep learning AI-ECG models generated left ventricular systolic dysfunction and left ventricular diastolic dysfunction scores from serial 12-lead ECGs, paired with ejection fraction, E/e′, and N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide across treatment courses spanning revascularization, valve replacement with resynchronization therapy, percutaneous coronary intervention, and rhythm control.ResultsSerial score changes were broadly directionally concordant with echocardiographic and biochemical changes. Case-level and within-case discordances occurred, including persistent left ventricular systolic dysfunction elevation after resynchronization and bidirectional movement coinciding with rhythm transitions—consistent with rhythm- and pacing-related effects on ECG morphology.ConclusionsThese hypothesis-generating observations support the feasibility of longitudinal paired AI-ECG analysis and highlight the need for rhythm- and confounder-aware design in future studies.

Background

Artificial intelligence–enabled electrocardiogram (AI-ECG) has demonstrated robust diagnostic performance for detecting left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVSD) and diastolic dysfunction (LVDD) from standard 12-lead ECGs, establishing its role as a point-in-time screening tool.1 Whether AI-ECG outputs behave informatively when applied longitudinally during heart failure treatment remains largely unexplored.Take-Home Messages•Serial artificial intelligence–enabled ECG left ventricular systolic dysfunction and left ventricular diastolic dysfunction scores showed longitudinal changes broadly directionally concordant with echocardiographic and biochemical changes during heart failure treatment in 4 patients with diverse etiologies.•Case-level discordances and rhythm- or device-related ECG changes were also observed, supporting the need for larger longitudinal studies with rhythm- and confounder-aware design before artificial intelligence–enabled ECG can be considered a serial monitoring biomarker in heart failure.

Echocardiography is constrained by cost and accessibility. Prior longitudinal AI-ECG work has been reported in cardiac amyloidosis and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and recent studies have extended AI-ECG to filling-pressure estimation and prognostic trajectories.2,3 Heart failure care—spanning revascularization, valve intervention, device therapy, and rhythm control—offers a heterogeneous setting in which the longitudinal behavior of AI-ECG scores has not been systematically described.4 Here, we present a hypothesis-generating case series exploring whether serial AI-ECG LVSD and LVDD scores track echocardiographic and biochemical trajectories across 4 illustrative patients.

Methods

Patient selection

From a single center (Incheon Sejong Hospital), we identified patients with heart failure who underwent at least 3 serial 12-lead ECGs during routine follow-up (March 2017 to January 2025) with concurrent echocardiographic and N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide measurements. From 1,033 eligible patients, 4 were purposively selected to illustrate 4 distinct treatment modalities—surgical revascularization, valve replacement with subsequent device therapy, percutaneous coronary intervention, and rhythm control—across a range of heart failure etiologies. Cases were not selected on the basis of AI-ECG score behavior or the degree of AI-ECG–echocardiographic concordance. As an illustrative case series, this selection is not statistically representative of the broader cohort and is subject to selection bias.

AI-ECG analysis

Previously validated deep learning AI-ECG models generated LVSD and LVDD probability scores (range: 0-100), developed against ejection fraction (EF) ≤40% and E/e′ >15, with predefined screening cutoffs of 9.7 and 20.8, respectively.5,6 Model architecture has been described previously. In this longitudinal context, changes in serial AI-ECG scores, rather than threshold crossings, were the primary focus, with published cutoffs shown only as visual reference lines.

Comparators and reporting

For each patient, all available serial ECGs during the treatment course were analyzed. AI-ECG scores were paired with the temporally closest echocardiographic measurements (EF, E/e′) and N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide, with date offsets recorded. Rhythm (sinus, atrial fibrillation [AF], or ventricular pacing), QRS duration, and procedural events were recorded at each time point, given their potential to influence ECG morphology independently. Model output was generated automatically, blinded to clinical and echocardiographic readers.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Incheon Sejong Hospital (ISH 2024-09-001), with a waiver of informed consent granted given the retrospective study design.

Case 1

A 78-year-old man with ischemic cardiomyopathy (left main, 3-vessel disease) underwent off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting. At baseline (sinus rhythm, QRS 134 ms), both AI-ECG scores were elevated despite an EF of 49%: The LVSD score was 42.5 (above the 9.7 cutoff, though EF was just above the EF ≤40% training threshold) and the LVDD score was 57.2. Following revascularization, the LVSD score dropped sharply to 0.5 within 2 weeks, ahead of overt echocardiographic change, while the LVDD score declined more gradually (57.2 → 17.5 at 3 years). Case 2

A 78-year-old man with low-flow, low-gradient severe aortic stenosis underwent aortic valve replacement (AVR), followed by pacemaker implantation 3 days later and cardiac resynchronization therapy–defibrillator (CRT-D) upgrade 7 months later. At baseline, both AI-ECG scores were markedly elevated (LVSD: 80.2, LVDD: 53.2; EF: 18%, native sinus rhythm with QRS 187 ms). EF gradually recovered to 36% at 1 year and 52% at last follow-up (∼23 months), with the LVDD score initially declining to 10.2. The LVSD score, in contrast, transiently dropped to 46.5 early after AVR but returned to and remained at baseline levels (80.0 and 80.9). Case 3

A 73-year-old man with 2-vessel coronary artery disease underwent percutaneous coronary intervention to the mid left anterior descending artery. At baseline (sinus rhythm with ambiguous atrial activity suggesting possible ectopic atrial tachycardia; QRS 101 ms), all parameters were markedly abnormal: EF was 25%, E/e′ was 30, LVSD score was 94.9, and LVDD score was 48.7. At intermediate follow-up (∼18 months), EF improved to 54%, E/e′ normalized to 10, and AI-ECG scores fell to LVSD 10.4 and LVDD 26.3—the latter still above the cutoff despite E/e′ normalization. At last follow-up (∼28 months), LVDD further declined to 11.7 (below cutoff), while LVSD and EF showed mild parallel deterioration (LVSD: 12.7, EF: 48%). Both score trajectories tracked echocardiographic changes across 2 follow-up windows (Figure 1C).

Case 4

A 66-year-old woman with dilated cardiomyopathy and AF was evaluated across multiple rhythm states over ∼6 years. At baseline (AF, QRS 89 ms) EF was 35%, LVSD score was 64.7, and LVDD score was 33.1. Following medical optimization (∼30 months), sinus rhythm was restored (QRS 109 ms), EF improved to 56%, and both scores declined markedly (LVSD: 1.9, LVDD: 3.9). AF subsequently recurred (∼43 months), with clinical and echocardiographic deterioration (EF: 34%, E/e′: 18.8); both scores increased correspondingly (LVSD: 40.0, LVDD: 22.6). At an assessment before radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) (∼47 months, AF, QRS 109 ms), EF further declined to 28% and the LVSD score peaked at 71.8, although the LVDD score and E/e′ were paradoxically more reassuring at the same time point (LVDD: 15.4, E/e′: 9.1)—illustrating that the 2 AI-ECG axes need not move together within a single time point. After successful RFCA (∼70 months), sinus rhythm was restored, EF recovered to 43%, and both scores normalized (LVSD: 1.7, LVDD: 3.8). Whether these trajectories reflect ventricular function, AF-related ECG features, or both cannot be determined from a single case (Figures 1D, 2C, and 2D).

Discussion

This hypothesis-generating case series describes the longitudinal behavior of serial AI-ECG LVSD and LVDD scores in 4 heart failure patients with diverse etiologies. Across cases, serial score changes were broadly directionally concordant with echocardiographic and biochemical changes, although case-level discordances and rhythm- or device-related ECG changes were evident.

Several observations emerged. First, in cases 1, 3, and 4, changes in the AI-ECG LVSD score generally paralleled changes in LVEF over time. Because the LVSD model was originally trained against an EF-based label, this concordance reflects within-construct consistency; nevertheless, the magnitude and timing of score changes—at times preceding overt echocardiographic change, as in case 1—suggest that serial scoring may capture information not fully reduced to a single EF measurement. A complementary pattern was observed for LVDD scores, which declined from 57.2 to 17.5 in case 1 and from 48.7 to 11.7 in case 3, crossing below the screening cutoff only with extended follow-up—suggesting that threshold crossing may be a function of follow-up duration rather than a property of any single time point. Discordant AI-ECG findings at a single time point should not be dismissed as noise; apparent false-positives have been associated with elevated downstream risk in other contexts.4

Second, case 2 illustrated a more complex pattern that does not reduce to simple LVDD-LVSD divergence. Both baseline scores were markedly elevated (LVSD: 80.2, LVDD: 53.2), with severe systolic dysfunction (EF: 18%) and a widened native QRS (187 ms). Over the AVR → pacemaker → CRT-D course, EF gradually recovered to 52%; the LVDD score initially declined to 10.2 at 1 year but rose to 31 at last follow-up despite continued EF and biomarker improvement, while the LVSD score transiently dropped early but returned to and remained at near-baseline levels (∼80). This pattern admits 3 nonmutually exclusive interpretations: 1) an EF of 36% to 52% remains near the abnormal range defining the model's training label, so persistent LVSD elevation is not necessarily discordant with continued systolic dysfunction; 2) progressive transition from native QRS 187 ms to atrioventricular dual-paced rhythm with narrowing QRS (135 → 106 ms) substantially altered ECG morphology in ways that can influence AI-ECG output independently of ventricular recovery; and 3) the 2 models may exhibit different sensitivities to procedural and rhythm-related changes. The second consideration aligns with prior work showing that ECG-based deep learning itself captures CRT-related signal, indicating that paced ECGs are not interpretively neutral inputs.7

Third, case 4 showed bidirectional score movement across initial improvement, deterioration coinciding with recurrent AF, and recovery following RFCA. AI-ECG output moved in both directions over a long observation window, with LVDD broadly paralleling the LVSD trajectory across rhythm transitions (33.1 → 3.9 → 22.6 → 15.4 → 3.8). However, the same trajectory is confounded by rhythm itself: AF directly alters multiple features of the surface ECG that AI-ECG models can plausibly weigh, including QRS-level metrics that varied measurably between rhythm states (Figures 2C and 2D), so attributing score changes specifically to ventricular functional change is not possible from these data. Of note, at the pre-RFCA assessment, the LVSD score peaked while the LVDD score and E/e′ were comparatively reassuring at the same time point, illustrating that the 2 axes did not move together within a single time point. Disentangling treatment effect from rhythm effect will require dedicated analyses in cohorts with concurrent rhythm and ventricular function changes.

ECGs are inexpensive, widely available, and frequently obtained during routine heart failure follow-up, making the longitudinal use of AI-ECG outputs an attractive question to study. Whether such ECG-derived scores can complement existing monitoring tools across heterogeneous heart failure populations—and under what conditions, including rhythm and pacing states, they remain interpretable—remains to be defined.2,3,8,9

Several caveats temper these observations. The within-construct concordance and rhythm/pacing confounding discussed above are central interpretive limitations. In addition, screening cutoffs may not be the most informative summary of longitudinal data; as illustrated in cases 1 and 3, LVDD threshold crossing was a function of follow-up duration, so we present cutoffs as visual reference lines rather than decision thresholds. AI-ECG in this longitudinal context is best understood as complementary to—rather than substitutive for—echocardiography and biomarker testing. Taken together, we frame this work as hypothesis-generating evidence of longitudinal directional concordance, not as a demonstration that AI-ECG functions as an independent treatment-response biomarker. Moreover, because AI-ECG scores are derived from the ECG, serial changes in their output may reflect evolving cardiac electrical activity—including rhythm, pacing, and conduction remodeling—rather than changes in ventricular function per se; a change in a serial score should therefore not be assumed to indicate a change in systolic or diastolic function until rhythm- and conduction-related contributions have been considered.

Beyond the conceptual caveats above, this study has specific limitations. First, this is a small, 4-patient retrospective case series from a single center, subject to selection bias despite explicit non–AI-based selection criteria. Second, ECG-echocardiography pairing was opportunistic rather than protocol-driven, and intervals between paired measurements varied. Third, although rhythm, pacing status, QRS duration, and postprocedural conduction changes were recorded, the descriptive nature precluded formal statistical adjustment for these confounders or quantification of their contribution to score changes. Fourth, associations between AI-ECG score changes and downstream clinical outcomes such as heart failure hospitalization or mortality were not evaluated. Larger prospective multicenter studies with standardized ECG-echocardiography timing, blinded readers, prespecified rhythm and pacing handling, and confounder-adjusted longitudinal models are needed to determine whether serial AI-ECG scores independently predict, and whether acting on them alters, clinical outcomes in heart failure.

Conclusions

Serial AI-ECG LVSD and LVDD scores showed longitudinal changes broadly directionally concordant with echocardiographic and biochemical changes in 4 illustrative patients with diverse heart failure etiologies, although case-level discordances and rhythm- or device-related confounders were also observed. Because the LVSD and LVDD models were trained against EF- and E/e′-based labels, this longitudinal concordance reflects internal (within-construct) consistency rather than independent biological validation. Moreover, because these scores are derived from the ECG, serial changes may partly reflect evolving cardiac electrical activity—rhythm, pacing, and conduction remodeling—rather than ventricular function alone. These hypothesis-generating findings motivate larger longitudinal studies that explicitly address rhythm, pacing, and procedural confounders before AI-ECG can be considered a serial monitoring biomarker in heart failure.
