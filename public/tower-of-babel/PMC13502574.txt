Radical‐Mediated Dispersion Breaks Aggregation Limits in Carbon Thermoelectrics
Authors: Shanshan Zhou, Xiao‐Lei Shi, Meng Li, Wenyi Chen, Tianyi Cao, Nan‐Hai Li, Min Zhang, Prashant Sonar, Qian Liu, Zhi‐Gang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCarbon‐based materials, particularly single‐walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs), are promising candidates for flexible thermoelectric applications due to their excellent electrical conductivity and mechanical robustness. However, severe self‐aggregation of SWCNTs leads to suboptimal and degraded thermoelectric performance. Conventional dispersion strategies have proved largely ineffective in overcoming this limitation. Here, we present a pioneered radical‐mediated dispersion (RMD) strategy, enabled by a rationally designed small molecule, OTN, which incorporates a donor‐acceptor conjugated backbone and pendant free‐radical terminals. The RMD strategy mechanism functions through dual interactions: The donor‐acceptor backbone enhances π‐interactions with SWCNTs, while the pendant radicals facilitate radical‐radical interactions to further suppress nanotube aggregation. This synergistic molecular design enables OTN‐SWCNT hybrid films to achieve a high power factor of 30.1 µW cm−1 K−2, far exceeding previous reports, while maintaining excellent free‐standing mechanical flexibility. Furthermore, a nine‐leg thermoelectric device assembled from these films delivers a normalized power density of 0.653 µW cm−2 K−2, representing one of the best performances for CNT‐based thermoelectrics to date. This pioneering molecular design, together with the derived innovative RMD strategy overcomes the long‐standing aggregation of SWCNTs and is anticipated to open new avenues for advancing carbon‐based thermoelectric materials toward practical, flexible energy‐harvesting applications.Keywords: carbon nanotube, device, electrical conductivity, organic, thermoelectric

Introduction

Thermoelectric materials, which directly convert temperature gradients into electricity via the Seebeck effect [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6], offer a promising pathway toward sustainable energy generation. Among them, carbon‐based materials are considerably attractive owing to their potential in flexible thermoelectric devices (F‐TEDs) for wearable electronics. In particular, single‐walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) stand out benefiting from their one‐dimensional structure, which confers exceptional electrical conductivity (σ) and mechanical flexibility [7, 8, 9], two essential attributes for wearable technologies. Moreover, SWCNTs address key drawbacks of counterpart thermoelectric materials, such as the brittleness of conventional inorganic materials and the poor air stability of organic conjugated systems [10, 11, 12, 13]. With scalable production of SWCNTs now achievable, the global research priority has shifted toward enhancing their thermoelectric performance to reach commercially competitive levels.

The performance of thermoelectric materials is typically evaluated by the dimensionless figure of merit, ZT = S 2 σT/κ, where S is the Seebeck coefficient, T the absolute temperature (K), and κ the thermal conductivity [14, 15, 16]. Since S and σ are interdependent and often inversely correlated, and κ is largely intrinsic to the material, the product of S 2 σ (i.e., power factor) is frequently used as the key metric to assess the potential of thermoelectric films for device application. SWCNTs‐based thermoelectric materials universally exhibit S 2 σ below 10 µW cm−1 K−2, which falls far short of the ∼30 µW cm−1 K−2 threshold benchmarked against high‐performance inorganic and commercial thermoelectric materials [5]. Surpassing this threshold is essential to enable extensive research into advanced device engineering and practical applications. The major obstacle to boosting the S 2 σ of SWCNTs‐based materials beyond this threshold lies in the severe aggregation of nanotubes, which impedes the enhancement of S without sacrificing σ [17, 18, 19, 20, 21].

Current strategies to mitigate SWCNTs aggregation include ultrasonication, surface modification, and hybridization [22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. Ultrasonication provides a straightforward dispersion method, however, SWCNTs tend to reaggregate once the mechanical agitation ceases due to strong intertube interactions [27]. Surface modification, while effective in some cases, often demands careful selection of functional groups and involves complex reaction and purification processes [28, 29]. In contrast, hybridization presents a more versatile and practical approach, as it allows flexible selection of external compounds [5]. Various materials have been employed to form SWCNTs hybrids for enhanced dispersion and thus improved S 2 σ. For instance, in PEDOT:PSS/SWCNTs hybrid films, phase separation of PEDOT and PSS via specific treatments introduces defects in SWCNTs, thereby reducing conductivity while enhancing the S [20, 30], achieving S 2 σ as high as 5.01 µW cm−1 K−2. Conjugated polymers can wrap around SWCNTs through π‐interactions, promoting effective dispersion, combining with their inherent dopability [31, 32, 33], enabling polymer/SWCNTs hybrid films to achieve S 2 σ of around 4.5 µW cm−1 K−2. Conjugated small molecules utilize similar π‐interactions for efficient dispersion, but can readily mix more uniformly with SWCNTs [29], leading to higher S 2 σ values of up to 7.98 µW cm−1 K−2. Notably, Kim et al. recently reported an exceptional small molecule/SWCNTs hybrid film with an S 2 σ of 21.6 µW cm−1 K−2 [34]. However, minor structural modifications to the small molecule significantly reduced the S 2 σ to below 10 µW cm−1 K−2. These findings collectively indicate that current dispersion strategies for SWCNTs‐based thermoelectric materials remain inadequate. Most reported systems still exhibit S 2 σ values below 10 µW cm−1 K−2, with only rare cases exceeding 20 µW cm−1 K−2. This underscores the pressing need for more robust design principles capable of ensuring efficient SWCNTs dispersion and providing a rational framework for systematic improvement of S 2 σ. Conceiving innovative strategies that can readily boost the S 2 σ of SWCNTs‐based thermoelectric materials beyond 30 µW cm−1 K−2 would not only break the shackles of existing approaches, but also open new avenues for materials optimization, propelling progress toward next‐generation wearable electronics.

Results and Discussion

In this work, to more efficiently overcome the aggregation challenge of SWCNTs and substantially enhance their thermoelectric performance, we designed and synthesized a novel organic π‐conjugated small molecule, OTN (3,6‐di(naphthalen‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐bis(8‐((2,2,6,6‐tetramethylpiperidin‐1‐oxyl)octyl))‐2,5‐dihydropyrrolo[3,4‐c]pyrrole‐1,4‐dione, structure shown in Figure 1a). Guided by a donor‐acceptor backbone design, OTN promotes strong π‐interactions with SWCNTs, thereby enabling effective dispersion. More innovatively, flexible alkyl‐linked 2,2,6,6‐tetramethylpiperidin‐1‐yl)oxyl (TEMPO) free radical terminals were incorporated, which engage in radical‐radical interactions to form non‐conjugated flexible domains. These domains sterically hinder SWCNTs reaggregation, further improving dispersion (Figure 1a). As a result, OTN‐SWCNT hybrid thin films were fabricated, exhibiting a high S 2 σ of 30.1 µW cm−1 K−2, far surpassing previously reported SWCNTs‐based thermoelectric materials, including pristine SWCNTs, polymer/SWCNTs, and small molecule/SWCNTs hybrids (Figure 1b and Table S1). More importantly, this high S 2 σ value demonstrates the potential of radical strategy to advance thermoelectric systems based on commercially available SWCNTs, enabling comparable performance to that of intrinsically highly aligned CNT‐based materials [26, 35]. To further identify the role of free radicals, we employed a non‐radical analogue, O‐DPPN (3,6‐di(naphthalen‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐dioctyl‐2,5‐dihydropyrrolo[3,4‐c]pyrrole‐1,4‐dione), previously reported in our earlier work [36]. Under identical film fabrication conditions, although the O‐DPPN improved the σ of the SWCNTs networks, it simultaneously reduced the S, which is consistent with the conventional conductivity‐Seebeck trade‐off. In contrast, the TEMPO‐radical‐containing OTN enabled the simultaneous enhancement of both σ and S, resulting in a S 2 σ of twice than that of the non‐radical analogue. These results suggest that the incorporated radicals play a more efficient role in improving SWCNTs dispersion than the π‐interactions between OTN backbone and SWCNTs. This innovative free radical design principle and the significant role of radical‐radical interactions in dispersing SWCNTs define our strategy as radical‐mediated dispersion (RMD). Notably, the RMD strategy is highly versatile and accessible, as both the π‐conjugated backbone and free radical terminals can be readily modified, offering extensive opportunities to materials innovation for mitigating SWCNTs aggregation and stimulating interests on SWCNTs toward practical applications.

Beyond their exceptionally high S 2 σ, the OTN‐SWCNT hybrid films also exhibit remarkable mechanical flexibility, with negligible resistance variation even after 1000 bending cycles at bending radius (r) of 2.5 mm and press‐release folding cycles at r = 0 (vide infra). It is particularly worth noting that in many wearable applications (such as powering flexible sensors), the primary requirements for thermoelectric materials include high σ to enable rapid response, a large S 2 σ, and excellent mechanical flexibility. From this perspective, our high‐performance OTN‐SWCNT films demonstrate significant potential for wearable applications. Therefore, a nine‐leg flexible thermoelectric generator (F‐TEG) was assembled from the hybrid films and achieved a normalized power density (ω n) of 0.653 µW cm−2 K−2, the highest reported among SWCNTs‐based F‐TEGs (Figure 1c and Table S2), and comparable to that of many inorganic‐based F‐TEGs (Table S3) [45, 46]. These results evidently demonstrate the strong practical potential of our RMD strategy, pushing SWCNTs‐based thermoelectric research into the next stage of application development.

Details of the synthesis (Schemes S1, S2) and characterization of OTN are provided in Supporting Information. High‐resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) confirmed the molecular mass of OTN with a negligible deviation of −2.2 ppm (Figure S1). 1H and 13C NMR (nuclear magnetic resonance) of pristine and quenched OTN with pentafluorophenylhydrazine shown in Figures S2–S4 further confirmed the high purity and chemical structure. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) revealed decomposition and melting transition temperatures of 281 °C (5% weight loss) and 123 °C, respectively (Figures S5, S6). Ultraviolet‐visible (UV–vis) absorption spectra exhibited a red shift from solution to film, indicative of π–π stacking or ordered molecular packing, typically arising from the planar conjugated backbone and radical‐radical interactions (Figure S7 and Table S4). The OTN‐SWCNT hybrid films were fabricated via a simple three‐step process (Figure S8). First, OTN and SWCNTs were mixed in chlorobenzene at specific weight ratios to form homogeneous suspension through stirring and ultrasonication. The suspension was then drop‐cast into a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) mold (3 cm × 4 cm) and naturally evaporated to form flexible thin films. Second, the as‐prepared films were post‐treated with 0.1 M aqueous NaBH4 solution and subsequently dried in a vacuum oven [19, 47]. Finally, the dried films were cold‐pressed to obtain the OTN‐SWCNT hybrid thin films for thermoelectric performance evaluation. It's worth mentioning that the OTN‐SWCNT films fabrication and subsequent performance and device characterizations were all conducted under ambient air conditions.

To optimize performance, we systematically varied the OTN content in the x wt% OTN‐SWCNT hybrid films (x = 0, 5, 7.5, 10, 12.5, 15, and 17.5). As x increased from 0 to 10, both the σ and S improved, yielding a maximum S 2 σ of 8.65 µW cm−1 K−2 at 298 K. Further increasing x to 12.5 and beyond led to a decline in S while σ remained largely unchanged, resulting in a reduced S 2 σ (Figure 2a). This deterioration could be attributed to intensified radical‐radical interactions among OTN molecules, which diminish their effectiveness in dispersing SWCNTs. We next investigated the influence of cold‐pressing pressure (ranging from 5.6 to 8.6 tons) on the thermoelectric performance of the 10 wt% OTN‐SWCNT films (Figure 2b). Increasing the pressure from 5.6 to 7.6 tons significantly enhanced σ and maintaining consistent S, reaching a peak S 2 σ of 30.1 µW cm−1 K−2 at 7.6 tons. Further, increasing the pressure to 8.6 tons caused a reduction in S despite continuous growth in σ, resulting in unchanged S 2 σ. This variation in thermoelectric parameters is consistent with the behavior observed for pristine SWCNTs films under different cold‐pressing pressures, as shown in Figure S9. Notably, the optimized S 2 σ of pristine SWCNTs films is less than 10 µW cm−1 K−2, that is, substantially lower than the 30.1 µW cm−1 K−2 achieved by the OTN‐SWCNT hybrid films, highlighting the high effectiveness of the OTN‐enabled RMD strategy. The selection of 5.6 tons as the initial pressure was guided by film thickness considerations that films pressed at or above this pressure exhibited a consistent thickness of ∼2.5 µm (Figure S10a–c). Below 5.6 tons, the film thickness was significantly high (Figure S11), while at 8.6 tons, the films tore upon pressure releasing (Figure S10d), preventing reliable thickness measurement. These observations indicate that film densification saturates above 5.6 tons and remains stable up to 7.6 tons. Beyond this threshold, excessive pressure damages the film microstructure, resulting in unreliable electrical measurements. Under the optimized pressing pressure of 7.6 tons, the thermoelectric properties of x wt% OTN‐SWCNT films (x = 0, 5, 7.5, 10, 12.5, and 15), presented in Figure S12, further confirmed that the optimal OTN content remained at 10 wt%, consistent with the trend observed for the unpressed films (Figure 2a). More importantly, the reproducibility of the optimized 10 wt% OTN‐SWCNT films was evaluated by preparing samples from different batches. The σ, S, and S 2 σ parameters exhibited highly consistent across all samples (Figure S13), demonstrating excellent reproducibility. These reproduced films were subsequently used for the fabrication of F‐TEDs for wearable applications (vide infra). To further elucidate the transport behavior of the x wt% OTN‐SWCNT hybrid films, the carrier concentration (n) and mobility (μ) were measured as a function of x at room temperature (Figure 2c). Compared with pristine SWCNTs, all OTN‐SWCNT hybrid films exhibited a substantial decrease in n, while the μ gradually increased with increasing x, reaching a maximum at x = 10 before declining at higher OTN contents. Notably, the reduced n did not lead to a significant increase in S, while the variation in μ closely followed the trends of σ and S 2 σ (Figure S12). This is because the S in semiconducting SWCNT networks cannot be described solely by the n. Instead, network‐dependent transport effects, including the density‐of‐states (DOS) distribution, nanotube‐junction transport, and energetic disorder, have been shown to significantly influence charge and thermoelectric transport in SWCNT networks. Consequently, the dependence of S on n is considerably weaker than that predicted by conventional semiconductor models [48, 49]. For example, a unified transport model was developed for semiconducting SWCNT networks and demonstrated that energetic disorder plays a critical role in determining both σ and the S [48]. The model showed that both n and energetic disorder influence charge transport, while increasing disorder causes deviations from simple band‐transport behavior. These findings indicate that the thermoelectric transport properties of SWCNT networks, including S, cannot be fully described by n alone. Similarly, it was reported that the S in semiconducting SWCNT networks is strongly influenced by the width of the DOS distribution and network transport characteristics [49]. The results further demonstrated that, the thermoelectric response of SWCNT networks depends not only on n, but also on network‐dependent transport effects. More recently, experimental results showed that the observed trends in μ and S in semiconducting SWCNT networks can be explained using a random‐resistor‐network model incorporating impurity‐band formation arising from overlapping Coulomb potentials [50]. The results further revealed that thermoelectric transport in SWCNT networks depends not only on n, but also on impurity‐band‐related electronic structure effects.

Besides, it should also be noted that the n of our pristine SWCNT films is on the order of 1022 cm−3, which is higher than the typical optimized n range (1019–1021 cm−3) reported for conventional thermoelectric semiconductors [51]. Under such highly doped conditions, the dependence of S on n may not strictly follow the simple relationships commonly used for homogeneous semiconductors. In SWCNT networks, thermoelectric transport is additionally influenced by energetic disorder, DOS distribution, and network‐dependent transport effects. Consequently, a substantial decrease in n does not necessarily lead to a proportional increase in S. In our system, SEM and TEM observations clearly demonstrate that OTN induces significant debundling of SWCNTs and improves network uniformity. This indicates substantial changes in bundle size, nanotube packing, and junction distribution. Since charge transport in SWCNT films is governed not only by transport along individual nanotubes but also by transport across numerous nanotube‐nanotube junctions, such morphological changes are expected to alter the percolation pathways and junction transport characteristics of the network. Consequently, although n decreases substantially after OTN incorporation, the S increases only moderately from 47 to 54 µV K−1. Therefore, the observed moderate increase in S despite a substantial decrease in n does not contradict the carrier‐concentration effect. Rather, it is consistent with previous reports showing that the S of SWCNT networks is governed by both n and network‐dependent transport effects rather than by n alone. The enhanced μ strongly supports our central conclusion that the radical‐mediated debundling effect of OTN effectively improves SWCNTs dispersion, thereby facilitating intertube charge transport and suppressing bundle‐induced carrier scattering. Meanwhile, the improved dispersion and redistribution of SWCNTs bundles likely reduce the local carrier density per unit area, leading to the experimentally observed decrease in n. Consequently, the OTN‐SWCNT system exhibits an unusual transport behavior with simultaneously reduced n and enhanced μ.

The exceptionally high S 2 σ observed for the 10 wt% OTN‐SWCNT hybrid film prompted us to further evaluate its ZT to explore its potential for practical applications. Beyond S 2 σ, the κ is a critical parameter in determining ZT, which was determined using the relation κ = DρC p, where D is the thermal diffusivity, ρ is the density, and C p is the specific heat capacity [52]. We first measured the C p of OTN‐SWCNT films with different OTN contents (Figures S14–S19). The addition of OTN led to a noticeable reduction in C p compared to pristine SWCNTs (1.23 J g−1 K−1 at 298 K), with the 10 wt% film showing the lowest value of 0.83 J g−1 K−1. Next, we measured the D of films with varying OTN contents (Figures S20–S25), showing values ranging from 3.67 to 1.34 × 10−5 m2 s−1 as summarized in Figure S26. The reduced D upon OTN incorporation is likely associated with the radical‐mediated debundling and improved dispersion of SWCNTs, which increases intertube phonon scattering and interfacial thermal resistance, thereby suppressing heat transport across the nanotube network. The ρ, determined using a drainage method, remained nearly unchanged at 0.63‐0.66 g cm−3, likely due to the relatively low OTN content in the composites. Combined with the C p, D, and ρ, these parameters yielded the corresponding κ for each composition (Figure 2d), with the 10 wt% film exhibiting the lowest value of 7.00 W m−1 K−1. The reduced κ upon incorporating OTN could be attributed to both the intrinsically low κ of the organic molecules and the enhanced interfacial phonon scattering between OTN and SWCNTs. These effects were optimized in the 10 wt% OTN‐SWCNT film. Combining the S 2 σ and κ values, we calculated the ZT values at 298 K for all films and found that the 10 wt% OTN‐SWCNT film achieved a ZT of ∼0.13 (Figure 2d), among the highest reported for SWCNTs‐based thermoelectric materials (Table S5) [5, 19, 34, 53].

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the excellent thermoelectric performance of the OTN‐SWCNT hybrid thin films, we conducted scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis on x wt% OTN‐SWCNT samples with varying OTN contents (x = 0, 5, 7.5, 10, 12.5, and 15) (Figures S27, S28). As the OTN content increased up to 10 wt%, the average bundle diameter of the SWCNTs steadily decreased from 25 nm to 7.5 nm. However, with further increases beyond 10 wt%, the bundle diameter began to rise again. This trend shown in Figure 3a suggests that the 10 wt% OTN‐SWCNT film achieved the optimal dispersion that was further confirmed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images shown in Figure S29, consistent with the obtained highest S 2 σ. To further investigate the dispersion behavior in the 10 wt% OTN‐SWCNT film, dynamic SEM imaging was performed after each post‐treatment step (Figure S30a–c). Compared to pristine SWCNTs, the average bundle diameter was reduced from 25 nm to approximately 13 nm upon the addition of OTN and further decreased to 7.5 nm following NaBH4 treatment (Figure S30d–f). These morphological trends correlate well with the measured film thickness evolution (Figure S30g–i). Specifically, the pristine SWCNTs film exhibited a thickness of 12.61 µm, which increased to 15.36 µm after the addition of 10 wt% OTN, and further to 19.98 µm after NaBH4 post‐treatment. After cold pressing, both the pristine and 10 wt% OTN‐SWCNT films were compressed to a comparable thickness of approximately 2.5 µm (Figure S31). These results collectively demonstrate a stepwise enhancement in SWCNTs dispersion and film uniformity, culminating in a densely packed microstructure after cold pressing, key factors contributing to the superior thermoelectric performance of the OTN‐SWCNT films.

Conjugated molecules are known to improve SWCNTs dispersion via π‐interactions, and our donor‐acceptor design strategy further strengthens these interactions, thereby achieving more effective SWCNTs dispersion. However, the specific role of free radicals in this process remains unclear. To probe this, we performed electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements to characterize the behavior of TEMPO free radicals in the 10 wt% OTN‐SWCNT films. Interestingly, an unexpected yet promising phenomenon was observed that post‐treatment with NaBH4 influenced the radical‐radical interactions. As shown in Figure 3b, the OTN solution exhibited a typical laterally zygomorphic triplet signal, characteristic of isolated TEMPO free radicals. Upon hybridization with SWCNTs to form the OTN‐SWCNT thin film, a notable integration of EPR peaks was observed (Figure 3c), indicating the occurrence of radical‐radical interactions. In general, a higher degree of peak integration reflects stronger radical‐radical interactions [54]. For pristine, the triplet EPR peak in dilute OTN solution merged into a singlet when transitioning from solution to solid state (Figure 3b), indicating strong radical‐radical interactions. However, for the OTN‐SWCNT hybrid film, the lower triplet peak evolved into a single peak, while the upper triplet peak merged into a nearly symmetric doublet, suggesting partial interactions alongside residual free radicals. This behavior also indicates that OTN molecules were not self‐aggregated, but interacted directly with SWCNTs. Following NaBH4 post‐treatment, the lower single peak became narrower, and the upper doublet showed a distinct tendency toward merging into a single peak. These spectral changes, on the one hand, indicate that the NaBH4 treatment is insufficient to quench the TEMPO radicals, which is further supported by quantitative spin‐counting analysis showing comparable spin numbers of 4.7 × 1016 and 5.6 × 1016, respectively, before and after NaBH4 treatment (Figure S32). On the other hand, they suggest the formation of additional radical‐radical exchange interactions, which may slightly increase the spin population and, more importantly, generate nonconjugated domains that suppress SWCNTs aggregation, thereby enabling further improved dispersion (Figure S33). Finally, the strong SWCNTs dispersion induced by the radical‐mediated interactions was further confirmed by TEM characterization through direct comparison with the non‐radical analogue sample (O‐DPPN, with structure shown in Figure S34). The TEM images reveal a stepwise debundling evolution from pristine SWCNTs to O‐DPPN‐SWCNT and finally to OTN‐SWCNT as shown in Figure S35. Although the non‐radical O‐DPPN partially reduces SWCNTs aggregation primarily through π‐interactions, the TEMPO‐radical‐containing OTN induces substantially finer nanotube bundles. These structural differences are ultimately reflected in the thermoelectric performance. As shown in Figure S34, compared with pristine SWCNTs, the 10 wt% O‐DPPN‐SWCNT and 10 wt% OTN‐SWCNT hybrid films exhibit ∼50% and ∼200% enhancements in S 2 σ, respectively.

Beyond the improved dispersion, the enhancement in the S 2 σ of OTN‐SWCNT hybrid films can be rationalized by considering the effective charge carriers involved in transport, which can be examined by investigating their doping characteristics. Raman spectroscopy was first performed on x wt% OTN‐SWCNT films with varying OTN content (x = 0, 5, 7.5, 10, 12.5, and 15) (Figure S36a). All samples exhibited weak D bands at ∼1341 cm−1, indicating minimal defect intensity, and prominent G bands at ∼1593 cm−1, corresponding to the in‐plane stretching vibration of sp2‐hybridized carbon atoms. Additionally, the second‐order 2D bands at ∼2676 cm−1 confirmed high‐quality nanotube stacking and interlayer separation [55, 56, 57]. As shown in Figures S37–S39 and Figure 3d, compared with pristine SWCNTs, the OTN‐SWCNT hybrid films showed a red shift in all three characteristic peaks (D, G, and 2D), signifying electrons transfer from SWCNTs to OTN, indicative of p‐type doping. To quantify the defect levels, the G‐to‐D intensity ratio (IG/ID) was calculated for each film (Figure S36b). Pristine SWCNTs exhibited a high IG/ID ratio of 32, reflecting their high purity and low defect content. Upon OTN incorporation, all hybrid films exhibited reduced IG/ID ratios (Figure 3d), implying that OTN introduces additional surface defects that enhance phonon scattering and thereby improve the S. To further probe the influence of post‐treatment and cold pressing on the doping behavior, Raman spectra were recorded for 10 wt% OTN‐SWCNT films after NaBH4 treatment and cold pressing (Figures S36c and 3e). The hybrid films exhibited clear red shifts in the D, G, and 2D peaks relative to pristine SWCNTs, consistent with p‐type doping. However, no additional spectral shifts were observed after post‐treatment and cold pressing, indicating that the doping originates exclusively from the presence of OTN and remains unaffected by subsequent processing steps. To corroborate the p‐type doping behavior, UV–vis‐NIR spectroscopy was performed on the OTN‐SWCNT films. Pristine SWCNTs exhibit characteristic van Hove singularity transitions: S11 and S22 (from semiconducting tubes) and M11 (from metallic tubes) [58]. With increasing OTN content up to 10 wt%, the S11 peak at 1946 nm was suppressed (Figure S36d), accompanied by the emergence of new shoulder peaks at the range of 1834–1903 nm (Figure 3f), indicative of charge transfer from SWCNTs to OTN (i.e., p‐doping). Moreover, a new absorption feature appeared at 1360 nm in the S22 region, and the M11 peak exhibited a blue shift (Figure 3f), further supporting the presence of p‐type doping. Together, the Raman and UV–vis‐NIR results provide compelling evidence of electrons transfer process from SWCNTs to OTN, resulting in effective p‐type doping. Consequently, although the overall n decreases due to improved SWCNTs dispersion as measured above (Figure 2c), the doped carriers become more effective, thereby enhancing the electrical performance of the OTN‐SWCNT hybrid films.

With the achievement of such a high S 2 σ, and the elucidation of underlying mechanism, it is compelling to explore the potential of OTN‐SWCNT films for F‐TEGs applications. We first evaluated the mechanical flexibility of the fabricated x wt% OTN‐SWCNT thin films with varying OTN contents (x = 0, 7.5, 10, and 12.5). Flexibility tests were conducted under various bending cycles and r. As shown in Figure 4a, the resistance change (ΔR/R 0) percentage of all films under a r of 5 mm remained below 1% that is within the error range, indicating excellent mechanical stability. To further evaluate their flexibility under more stringent bending conditions, ΔR/R 0 of the 10 wt% OTN‐SWCNT thin film was measured at different r (r = 2.5, 3.0, 3.5, and 4.0 mm) over 1000 bending cycles (Figure 4b). In all cases, ΔR/R 0 remained below 1%, even at the smallest radius of 2.5 mm, confirming the outstanding mechanical flexibility of the film. Finally, the film was folded into a corrugated shape (equal to r = 0 mm, Figures 4c and S40) to evaluate its adaptability to irregular surfaces. This press‐release cycling test corresponds to repeated folding/unfolding under an effective r = 0 mm, where sharp creases were simultaneously generated at multiple positions of the freestanding film. Encouragingly, the resistance of OTN‐SWCNT film remained consistent upon 1000 press‐release cycles, indicting robust flexibility and uniformity across the entire film. Beyond simple resistance measurements, key thermoelectric parameters (σ, S, and S 2 σ) were systematically evaluated. As shown in Figure S41, these parameters exhibit negligible variation after 1000 bending cycles or press‐release cycles, demonstrating excellent mechanical stability. Collectively, these results highlight its strong potential for integration into F‐TEGs.

A practical F‐TEG was thus fabricated using the 10 wt% OTN‐SWCNT films as p‐type legs and copper as the n‐type legs to evaluate device applicability. To establish a sufficient temperature difference (ΔT), a single p‐n junction was first constructed in a vertical‐leg configuration on a cylindrical support (Figure 4d, top). Specifically, both p‐ and n‐type legs were affixed to the surface of a vertically mounted cylinder, and silver conductive adhesive was employed to connect the legs due to its high flexibility and low contact resistance after curing. These individual legs were then assembled to a 3 × 3 leg F‐TEG by connecting multiple p‐n junctions with copper wires (Figure 4d, bottom), yielding a total device resistance of approximately 27 Ω (see Supporting Video 1). The F‐TEG was integrated into a closed‐loop circuit, and hot and cold sources were applied to the respective ends of the device to establish a ΔT (Figure S42). Upon operation under an applied ΔT, the electrical circuit topology with the current flow pathway is illustrated in Figure S43. The open‐circuit voltage (V oc) of the 9‐leg vertical F‐TEG exhibited a clear linear dependance on ΔT (Figure 4e), confirming stable and reliable operation. Further, the output power (P) and voltage (V) as functions of current (I) were evaluated under precisely controlled ΔT values of 15.7, 19.8, 24.8, and 34.7 K, as monitored by thermocouples (Figures 4f,g and S44). At a ΔT of 34.7 K, the device delivered a peak P of 0.90 µW and a V oc of 13.94 mV, corresponding to a high‐power density (ω) of 782.53 µW cm−2 and a ω n of 0.653 µW cm−2 K−2. To gain insight into the thermal behavior, heat distribution within the device was simulated (Figure 4h). At ΔT = 34.7 K, the simulated hot‐ and cold‐side temperatures were approximately 105 and 65 °C, respectively, closely matching the thermocouple measurements (Figure S44d), indicating the reliability of collected data. Further, the thermal stability of the device was evaluated by continuously monitoring the V at 80 °C in air for 6 h. As shown in Figure S45 and Supporting Video 2, only slight fluctuations were observed, demonstrating the excellent thermal and air stability of the assembled device under continuous operation conditions. Moreover, the device performance under extreme conditions was also explored to broaden its application scenarios. Liquid nitrogen was applied to the cold side, and the device operating in a closed circuit achieved a high V oc of 28.6 mV (Figure S46 and Supporting Video 3). The temperature, monitored using a thermocouple, reached as low as 104 K (Supporting Video 4). Together with the above measurements using a hot source exceeding 374 K, the results demonstrate the excellent robustness of the device across a wide temperature range, particularly impressive under cryogenic environments. Finally, when the F‐TEG was worn on the forearm to simulate wearable scenarios, a V of 2.5 mV under a ΔT of 6.2 K (Figures 4i and S47) is achieved, highlighting its practical potential for body‐heat‐powered F‐TEDs.

Conclusions

In summary, we designed and characterized a small molecule (OTN) featuring a donor‐acceptor‐type conjugated backbone and pendant TEMPO free radical terminals to enhance the thermoelectric performance of SWCNTs‐based films. By systematically optimizing the OTN content, the 10 wt% OTN‐SWCNT hybrid film achieved a record‐high S 2 σ of 30.1 µW cm−1 K−2 at 298 K under ambient air conditions, enabled by NaBH4 post‐treatment and cold pressing at 7–8 tons. SEM and TEM analysis revealed significantly improved SWCNTs dispersion upon OTN incorporation and post‐treatment, attributed to the synergistic effects of the conjugated backbone and TEMPO free radical side chains. The donor‐acceptor backbone strengthened π‐interactions with SWCNTs, promoting uniform dispersion, while the flexible TEMPO free radicals formed radical‐radical interactions, further suppressing SWCNTs aggregation. Given this exceptional room‐temperature S 2 σ obtained in air, the application potential of the OTN‐SWCNT hybrid film in F‐TEGs was further explored, with device fabrication and characterization performed under ambient air as well. The hybrid films exhibited excellent mechanical reliability, maintaining consistent resistance after 1000 bending cycles with a small r of 2.5 mm and 1000 press‐release cycles upon folding into corrugated shape (equal to r = 0). Meanwhile, a simple F‐TEG fabricated using the optimized hybrid films delivered a P of 0.90 µW and a V oc of 13.94 mV, corresponding to a ω n of 0.653 µW cm−2 K−2, the highest reported to date among SWCNTs‐based F‐TEGs. Remarkably, this device operated reliably across a wide temperature range from 114 to 374 K, highlighting its robustness under diverse conditions. Our findings and proposed mechanism establish an RMD strategy, that is, both accessible and effective for addressing the long‐standing challenge of SWCNTs aggregation, thereby advancing SWCNTs‐based thermoelectrics toward application development. We anticipated that this strategy will be widely adopted, marking a new stage in the development of SWCNTs‐based thermoelectric materials with S 2 σ readily exceeding 30 µW cm−1 K−2.
