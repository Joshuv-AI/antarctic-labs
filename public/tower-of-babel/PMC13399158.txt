A community-based model for multimodal brain-health screening and Alzheimer’s disease and related dementia risk prediction in mid-sized cities and surrounding rural communities: a mixed-methods evaluation
Authors: Amy M Kemp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesRural adults (those residing in nonmetropolitan counties) face persistent barriers to brain-health assessment that may help prevent or delay cognitive decline. This study reports protocol and pilot feasibility findings from year 1 of a 3-year longitudinal project evaluating Building a Rural Aging and Intervention Network: Factors that Impact Trajectories (BRAIN-FIT), a community-embedded program to improve access to multidomain screening and awareness of modifiable dementia risk factors.Research Design and MethodsUsing a mixed-methods design, 119 adults aged 45 or older completed a single-day interdisciplinary screening incorporating clinical-standard measures (cardiovascular, physical, lifestyle, social–emotional, sensory, cognitive, and speech–language health), followed by individualized-results review. 63 participants completed a 4-week post-event behavior change evaluation.ResultsBRAIN-FIT, delivered across 7 community sites in 3 cities through 18 events, enrolled 163 adults (aged 46–95; M = 67.5) with socioeconomic and clinical heterogeneity. Engagement was strong: 119 consented to longitudinal participation, 95% completed all stations and rated the program highly feasible (3.7–4.4 out of 5). Screening identified prevalent modifiable risk factors: elevated cardiometabolic indicators, reduced physical endurance, sleep disturbance, and sensory impairment. Cognitive and language performance remained largely within functional limits, suggesting engagement during a preventive window.Discussion and ImplicationsCommunity-embedded multimodal screening appears to be a viable program for identifying actionable brain-health risk prior to substantial decline. By reaching diverse adults outside traditional care pathways, BRAIN-FIT advances a pragmatic population-health approach to dementia prevention. Longitudinal follow-up will evaluate sustained behavior change, strengthen referral integration, and inform hybrid effectiveness–implementation trials to promote healthier aging trajectories.

Building a Rural Aging and Intervention Network: Factors that Impact Trajectories (BRAIN-FIT) is a feasible and accepted community-based model for multidomain brain-health screening in aging adults. Delivered in trusted settings, it integrates cardiovascular, lifestyle, sensory, mobility, and speech-language indicators into a unified prevention framework. By incorporating cognitive-communication markers—early but often overlooked signs of neurologic change—the program reflects current understanding of cumulative Alzheimer’s disease and related dementia risk. Screenings are conducted by a supervised interprofessional student workforce. Embedded within a longitudinal data system, BRAIN-FIT reduces barriers to early detection, particularly in rural areas, and generates real-world evidence to inform targeted prevention and healthy aging initiatives.

Background and objectives

In rural and underserved communities—here operationalized as nonmetropolitan (nonmetro) counties (U.S. Census Bureau, 2026)—access to specialized care (e.g., cardiology or neurology) is limited (Barreto et al., 2021), and early risk identification of Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRDs) remains a persistent challenge (Xu et al., 2022). Existing screening practices often rely heavily on self-report or single-domain cognitive measures, which do not capture the multidimensional contributors to cognitive aging. Multimodal screening, including speech, language, hearing, mobility, psychosocial well-being, and lifestyle behaviors, offers a richer understanding of early cognitive-communication changes. By considering methods of monitoring domains of functioning potentially impacted by cognitive decline (e.g., independence, participation) (Savundranayagam et al., 2005), early intervention can be systematically examined to inform impactful treatment practices (Lanzi et al., 2021). This, in turn, can support adults in maintaining chosen participation factors as they age.

Current tools do not adequately support clinicians in identifying which individuals in diverse populations are most vulnerable or in determining when and how to intervene beyond a global assessment of cognitive decline (Roitsch et al., 2021). To address this gap, Building a Rural Aging and Intervention Network: Factors that Impact Trajectories (BRAIN-FIT) was developed as a community-embedded, multimodal screening, and monitoring platform designed to support early risk identification and longitudinal tracking of cognitive-communication health in underserved populations. BRAIN-FIT integrates clinical, behavioral, and communication-focused measures within a single accessible framework, with health domains informed by the Brain Care Score (Singh et al., 2023) and established modifiable risk factors for dementia (Livingston et al., 2024). As such, prevention frameworks increasingly emphasize the importance of risk identification and intervention starting in mid-life (e.g., ages 40–65) rather than limiting screening efforts to older adulthood. Despite this, most community-based screening initiatives and clinical workflows remain focused on adults aged 60 and older, missing a critical window for early, potentially modifiable change.

The present study adopts a mid-life–inclusive approach (≥45 years) to align with prevention science and to capture individuals at a stage when multidomain interventions may have the greatest long-term impact. This approach also supports a continuum model of brain health, in which early risk identification in mid-life is linked to ongoing monitoring and intervention in later life. The long-term objective of this program is to establish a sustainable model for longitudinal monitoring that can identify risk trajectories, inform timely intervention, and support population-level brain health for communities with limited access to specialty care.

The current study represents the initial phase of this longitudinal program and aims to (a) describe the BRAIN-FIT protocol, (b) evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of community-based multimodal screening, and (c) provide preliminary quantitative and qualitative data to inform longitudinal validation and future implementation for nonmetro areas.

Research design and methods

Study design

This embedded mixed-methods non-control trial design combined quantitative screening data with qualitative participant feedback. The approach facilitated the examination of brain health risks across multiple domains of cognitive aging, as well as the development of methods for delivering accessible, sustainable screening, education, and self-management interventions. These data represent the pilot year one data of a longitudinal study. Washington State University (WSU) Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved the study (IRB 21003), and informed consent was obtained in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration.

Participants and setting

Adults aged 45 or older were recruited from community centers, community clinics, low-income housing, and community partner networks in rural and suburban Washington and Idaho between June 9, 2025 and June 27, 2025. The population is considered primarily nonmetro, with most events occurring in nonmetropolitan counties (12/18), defined as counties outside metropolitan areas (U.S. Census Bureau, 2026). Locations were identified through independent senior living networks, collaborations, and word of mouth. In total, seven facilities agreed to host the BRAIN-FIT events (four low-income living housing, two community centers, and one community health clinic). All facilities advertised the events with IRB-approved flyers and provided physical space on the selected dates. Participants were provided a sign-up link to schedule a dedicated appointment at any location, or they could “walk in” to an appointment. Facilities with members or residents that exceeded 50 had events on 2 or 3 days, while smaller facilities had 1-day events. In total, 18 events (6 hr each) were held over 21 days. Two community centers held 3 days of events, one housing location held 2 days, and the community clinic held 7 days. Eligible participants included anyone aged 45 or older who could provide informed consent in English or Spanish, could see and hear in a typical conversational environment, and, if relevant, complete an Evaluation for Informed Consent. Exclusion criteria included non-English or Spanish-speaking status or inability to provide informed consent. The lower age threshold was selected to align with evidence supporting mid-life as a critical period for identifying and modifying risk factors associated with later-life cognitive decline (Livingston et al., 2024). Participants could choose to participate in the longitudinal monitoring of their data.

Electronic consent is stored separately from the health survey measures. The de-identified electronic records on REDCap (Harris et al., 2019) are securely stored and accessible only to designated project staff. Prior to screening, participants met individually with a trained researcher in a semi-private area to review study information and complete informed consent. Information was presented in plain language, and participants were given the opportunity to independently review the consent form and ask questions before providing written consent. Only individuals who demonstrated the capacity to consent or had a proxy to provide consent proceeded to screening. Additionally, participants could choose to complete the health screening program without consenting to the research or complete a selection of the program without penalty or loss of benefits. As part of this feasibility study, it should be noted that participants in the screening did not have to agree to have their health screening data stored for research purposes. For these participants, assent was given for standardized screening, and only a unique ID was provided. No other information about the participant was collected for research storage, meaning that participant data from each health station were provided to the participant but not stored or used for analysis.

Procedure

To support feasibility evaluation, the BRAIN-FIT screening model was designed as a structured, multi-station workflow that standardized participant flow, data collection, and staff roles across events. See Figure 1 for the workflow. Each event included a check-in station, five domain-specific screening stations, and a check-out/counseling station. After completing informed consent, participants were assigned a unique identifier and rotated through screening stations in a randomized order using a REDCap-based system to minimize order effects and manage participant flow.

BRAIN-FIT protocol procedure from training to longitudinal tracking.

Trained interprofessional student clinicians (speech-language pathology, nursing, and neuropsychology) administered all screenings under the supervision of licensed clinicians. All student clinicians completed a full-day, team-based training session prior to the events, which included reviewing all screening protocols, hands-on practice, and competency-based calibration across disciplines. Each station followed standardized administration, scoring, and data entry procedures to ensure consistency across administrators and sites.

Upon completion of all stations, participants received a structured results review and counseling session conducted using a semi-structured interview protocol. Follow-up interviews were similarly guided by semi-structured procedures to ensure consistency while allowing for individualized responses. Interviews are included in Supplementary Material. Screenings took between 45 and 90 min, with most of the variation occurring in the review of results (5–25 min) and at bottleneck stations, which increased wait time (5–20 min). Participants could then choose to complete a 4-week follow-up with research staff. All participants were entered into a raffle for one of 50 $25 gift cards.

For each domain screened, a corresponding educational handout was provided as part of the intervention (Sánchez-Polo et al., 2019). Handouts were developed using a standardized template at a reading level below 5th grade and included (a) a brief explanation of the screening domain and its relevance to brain health, (b) actionable self-management strategies, and (c) locally tailored care navigation information (e.g., provider names, phone numbers, addresses, and insurance or sliding-scale options). Handouts were matched 1:1 to each domain screened (e.g., blood pressure, sleep, etc.)

The inclusion of simplified educational content and locally relevant care information was informed by prior work demonstrating that individuals often struggle to understand health information and navigate available services, particularly in underserved settings (Bhattad & Pacifico, 2022; Marca-Frances et al., 2020). Handouts were then elaborated by graduate trainees using any available clinical guidelines (e.g., American Diabetes Association Professional Practice Committee et al., 2026; American Heart Association, 2026; Fazio et al., 2018) that followed the predefined criteria and were reviewed for accuracy and clarity by clinical supervisors and members of the community advisory board. These materials were intended to support comprehension and facilitate connections to care within the local context.

Health screening measures

At the health fair, we collected demographic characteristics, self-reported medical history, and insurance information. All participants who completed the health fair completed clinical screening assessments derived from the Brain Care Score and literature on modifiable risk factors (Livingston et al., 2024) to provide objective measures of modifiable risk factors. These are further described in Table 1. These included standard clinical screenings equivalent to Brain Care Score Factors: physical (blood pressure, hemoglobin A1C, cholesterol, body mass index [BMI]), lifestyle (nutrition, alcohol, smoking, aerobic activities, sleep), and social–emotional (stress, social relationships, meaning in life). Based on the Livingston et al. (2024) update on modifiable risk factors, we also collected data on the highest level of education, hearing, depression, history of traumatic brain injury, and vision. Finally, a cognitive screening station, using the Five-Minute Cognitive Test (Zhang et al., 2019), and a speech-language station were added. Speech and language samples (intelligibility, language sample, naming, acoustic features) were added to address another forthcoming project on noninvasive biomarkers. In combination, these were collected to better understand this community’s strengths and needs to support healthy aging.

Event counseling

After completing all screenings, participants could review their results with a student clinician. These interactions were recorded and semi-structured to yield results focused on the broad theme of contributing factors to brain health. Factors were categorized as “protective” (within or above standard limits) or “modifiable” (below normal limits) based on the standardized score criteria. These were presented in a 1-page report with individual and normed scores and visually through highlighting in green (protective) and pink (modifiable). Discussions were led by the student clinician, who asked which factors stood out to the participant, followed by questions about barriers or facilitators to care or change (see Supplementary Material for interview questions). All areas of interest and modifiable factors were correlated with a 1-page educational sheet and provided to the participant. Participants who elected to participate in the follow-up were asked to pick a target from the handouts to focus on over the next 4 weeks.

Follow-up interview

Participants who elected to complete the 4-week follow-up were contacted by the research team (three graduate speech and hearing sciences [SHS] students, two undergraduate SHS students, and the principal investigator [PI]) via email or phone. Follow-up interviews focused on self-reported behavior change and more in-depth feedback on the event (see Supplementary Material for interview questions). All participants completed the follow-up on the phone.

Implementation outcome measures

After completing the BRAIN-FIT program and the follow-up interview, participants completed feasibility measures both in quantitative and qualitative formats. Immediately after the BRAIN-FIT screening, all participants were prompted to complete a 12-question survey with validated measures (implementation outcome measures) to assess the feasibility, relevance, and appropriateness (Weiner et al., 2017). The counseling session also included a question that prompted feedback on the event (“Was there anything you would like to provide feedback on for this event to improve it?”). Any feedback that emerged organically in counseling was also captured during the qualitative analysis.

At the follow-up interview, participants completed the Designing for AcceleRated Translation-Intervention (DART-I; Kemp et al., 2026) and a semi-structured interview targeting acceptability, feasibility, and relevance (Weiner et al., 2017). The DART-I is an evaluation of implementation potential in which end users rate an intervention across seven domains (cost, safety, effectiveness, clinical demand, patient values, clinical utility, and relative advantage). Higher scores in any domain (out of five) indicate greater implementation potential, as reflected in the composite score.

Analysis

Quantitative

Survey responses were scored and managed in REDCap, a secure, web-based electronic data capture platform hosted by WSU. Data were exported into IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac, Version 31 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and Python (Van Rossum, 2026) for analysis.

Descriptive statistics (frequencies, means, standard deviations, and ranges) were calculated to characterize participant demographics, health history, and performance across the screening instruments. Group differences were evaluated using chi-square tests of independence for categorical variables, including age group, socioeconomic status (SES), education level, and geographic screening location. Independent-samples t-tests were used to assess changes in behavioral health outcomes from pre- to post-program, including overall change scores and differences by health behavior type (e.g., physical activity, cognition, mood). All tests were two-tailed with α = .05.

Qualitative

Counseling and follow-up interviews were analyzed using a rapid qualitative analysis (RQA) approach (Austin & Stuart, 2015; Taylor et al., 2018), which allowed the team to efficiently summarize key content areas while maintaining analytic rigor appropriate for early-phase implementation research. Atlas.ti qualitative software (version 25.2.1 for Mac; ATLAS.Ti Scientific Software Development GmbH, 2025) was used to organize, code, and summarize the data. A deductive coding template was developed based on the program’s targeted health domains and anticipated participant response patterns. Each interview was summarized using structured matrices that captured (a) the health topic discussed, (b) indicators of perceived benefit, challenge, or need for follow-up support, and (c) feedback on the event itself. The following provides the steps involved for RQA used in this study.

Each transcript was reviewed in full for familiarization and summarized into the structured matrix, with exemplar quotes referenced where they illustrated key points. Individual interview summaries were then combined into a cross-case matrix, with one row per participant and predefined columns for health domains (e.g., mood, cognition, physical activity, sleep, social engagement), perceived outcomes, and feedback on the event. The cross-case matrix supported systematic comparison across participants and facilitated identification of recurrent patterns, areas of divergence, and thematic relationships.

In parallel, we conducted a sentiment analysis (Birjali et al., 2021; Medhat et al., 2014) using the Atlas.ti sentiment analysis algorithm to characterize the emotional valence of participant responses coded to correspond with the above domains. Statements were coded as positive, negative, or neutral based on lexical, contextual, and affective indicators within the transcripts. The sentiment codes were integrated with the deductive RQA categories to identify patterns in participants’ experience with the event itself and across health-domain outcomes (e.g., mood, cognition, physical activity, sleep, social engagement). This allowed us to quantify broad trends in participant satisfaction and constructive criticisms while retaining the contextual nuance captured during qualitative review. The full RQA matrix is available in the Supplementary Material.

The combined RQA and sentiment analysis procedures provided an efficient yet robust framework for understanding participants’ experiences during the program. To enhance rigor, we maintained an audit trail documenting analytic decisions, revisions, and representative excerpts supporting theme development and participant representation in the matrix. Two trained coders, including the PI as a third reviewer (A.M.K.), completed the RQA. Any disagreements were resolved through consensus. Inter-rater reliability was achieved on 25% of transcripts with a Cohen’s kappa of 87.6%. A more detailed qualitative thematic analysis of feedback collected from the qualitative study is underway and will be reported in a future paper.

Results

Consistent with recommendations for feasibility and pilot studies, the sample was neither intended nor powered to detect small-to-moderate intervention effects (Leon et al., 2011; Thabane et al., 2010). Instead, the sample size was determined by the number of eligible individuals who participated in the community-based screening events during the study period, which had prior approval from WSU’s IRB to enroll up to 300 individuals. Analyses should therefore be interpreted as preliminary and descriptive, with emphasis on feasibility, acceptability, and signals of potential efficacy rather than definitive inferential outcomes.

One hundred and sixty-three people self-enrolled in the health fair across 18 events. Thirty people had prior appointments; the remaining 80% of the study participants were considered walk-ins. Of those enrolled, 73% (n = 119) agreed to participate in the research portion (a longitudinal study), and 95% (n = 114) completed all stations. Eighty-eight percent (n = 105) consented to a follow-up interview after 4 weeks, and 60% (n = 63) ultimately completed it. Of the 44 individuals who were not interested in the research, 16 (36%) still completed the full health fair, but their data were not stored or included in the dataset. The remaining (n = 28) only completed one station, primarily cardiovascular and hearing. Due to low attrition within those who enrolled in the research study, we did not evaluate differences between completers and non-completers.

Geographic locations were organized into three major cities and the PI’s lab, which is housed in a community clinic. Average age was 67.53 (SD = 10.23). Participants at all locations were primarily female (n = 81; 68%), White (n = 109; 89.1%), retired (n = 66; 55.5%), and reported Medicare as their primary insurance (n = 72; 60.5%). Most of the sample reported low (n = 47; 39.5%) or middle (n = 39; 32.8%) SES according to Washington’s Income Standards. There were differences between people at each location for education [F(1, 3) = 13.5, p < .001], income [F(1, 3) = 22.55, p < .001], and cognitive scores [F(1, 3) = 3.33, p = .022]. It should be noted that, although there are group differences, some were intentional for reaching diverse populations, as Major City 1 was deliberately recruited from low-income housing.

Counseling interviews were offered to all participants; however, a substantial number were not captured due to inconsistent staff use of the recording device (n = 22) or participant refusal to be recorded (n = 14). Eighty-three (69.7%) recordings were captured after the events. After reviewing for data quality, two were lost due to recording quality. In total, 81 counseling interviews were completed. Rapid qualitative analysis focused primarily on health topics, with a sentiment analysis completed for content units within health topic codes. The primary health topics discussed (with incidence greater than 75% of participants) were sensory, cardiovascular, lifestyle, and social–emotional. When giving examples, participants will be referred to as “BF[participant number].” BF to indicate “BRAIN-FIT,” and their ID number to maintain anonymity. Most participants agreed with their results in all domains (“yea, I wasn’t sure if my hearing was declining, but this confirmed it!”—BF222), but were neutral in their responses for cognition, speech, and language (“Ok. [language score] makes sense.”—BF301). Some participants (n = 18) showed contradictions or dismissals of results in cardiovascular, hearing, and social–emotional outcomes. For example, a participant, BF219, stated, “I don’t think the physical test captured what I can do. I’m active enough to pass.” In this instant, the participant received a “refer,” but did not agree that their abilities or results were not considered “within functional limits.” Additional details on this were explored in the feasibility section to better understand whether the comments were related to self-beliefs or to the standardized assessment implementation.

When asked about barriers to making changes to health domains screened, participants primarily discussed lifestyle factors, followed by sensory, physical, and social–emotional health. These barriers centered on feelings of resource availability, including time, money, transportation, and specialty services (“Well, when my son gets back, he can take me to the doctor, but he’s gone this summer.”—BF271). However, other personal factors, such as time, dedication, and motivation, were also noted (“I just have a hard time sticking to it [healthy diet].”—BF338). The only health domain discussed in terms of benefits to change includes social–emotional. These included increasing interactions with others, staying positive, and supporting friends and family (“This makes me feel like I should be going to poker night again, it will keep me sharp!”—BF203). Follow-up interviews included self-reported behavior change for each health domain screened. Of the 53 (44.5% sample) follow-ups completed, 31 (43%) reported taking at least one action in the last 4 weeks because of their health screenings (“I really wanted to increase this score for next year, so I started walking every other day.”—BF223). Participants were asked to expand on their behavior changes made to better understand the relationship between the screening results, handout suggestions, and reported change (“I liked how what I could do was right here. You gave me the ideas of where to start.”—BF338). For people who reported changing a behavior, cardiovascular health (e.g., A1C and weight loss), physical health (e.g., walking more), and sleep quality were the areas of health discussed most often in conversations (“I started paying attention to how many hours [of sleep] I was getting. It makes a difference!”—BF290). Participants reported that the handouts supported their decision-making and felt that making the changes was easier than in the past.

Implementation outcomes

During these pilot recruitment windows, the enrollment and completion of nearly 120 participants demonstrate the program’s acceptability. The high number of walk-in participants demonstrated the effectiveness of the recruiting format. Retention of 95% of participants demonstrates interest and demand for comprehensive health screenings within rural health settings. Among those who declined to participate in the research, the primary reasons were time commitment, lack of interest in unselected stations, and established care.

However, some data were lost due to workflow assessments, primarily due to inefficient station flow, staff training, and technical errors. Although 114 participants completed all stations, 25 (21.9%) were lost due to data loss. Specifically, we lost data for the physical station (n = 8), which evaluated endurance and fall risk; the lifestyle station (n = 8), which evaluated sleep, nutrition, and mood; and the hearing station (n = 9). Specific contributors to data loss included inconsistent use of digital survey platforms by staff (e.g., delayed or omitted data entry during high-throughput periods), limited familiarity with data entry protocols among newly trained personnel, and interface constraints within the data tracking system (e.g., inability to navigate backward after advancing). In addition, inefficient station flow led participants to progress past stations during periods of bottlenecking (e.g., long wait times), with some not returning to complete missed stations.

Event counseling

At the end of the health screening, participants were asked to rate their immediate feedback of the event using the Implementation Outcome Measures (IOMs; Weiner et al., 2017) of appropriateness, acceptability, and feasibility. We collected 97 responses (85.1%). IOMs are scored on a 5-point scale, with higher scores indicating greater perceptions of appropriateness, acceptability, and feasibility. The BRAIN-FIT program was rated as moderately acceptable (3.78; SD = 1.89), appropriate (3.76; SD = 1.89), and feasible (3.7; SD = 1.88).

Responses to the immediate feedback from the event were mostly positive (74%; “yes, this was great that it was all just here in one place.”—BF236), highlighting the program’s strengths and the student–clinician staff they interacted with. Program strengths included the wide range of health information provided, the ease of completing health screenings, and the comfort of the setting where the information was shared. If participants offered suggestions for improvement immediately after the event, these were recorded. Specifically, feedback was given to increase the amount of time for the full event (“The flyer says 60 minutes, but this took me more like 90.”—BF302) and how the flow of stations was set up (“I was waiting for hearing for a while when physical had no one there. I wish I could have done that while I was waiting.”—BF289).

Feedback on specific stations was coded in the qualitative interviews, which primarily included participants who felt the physical and hearing screenings were inaccurate. For the physical assessment, the Six-Minute Walk Test was used, and participants felt the results underestimated their abilities, either because the total laps completed were miscalculated or the laps themselves were poorly measured (“I think a walking test is fine for assessing [physical ability]. It just didn’t seem like it was calibrated correctly.”—BF278). During the hearing screening, although participants were told it was a speech-in-noise assessment, some still felt it did not accurately reflect their hearing capabilities (“I wish the room would have been a little bit quieter. I might have done better on the hearing test.”—BF204). These perspectives were expanded in the follow-up qualitative interviews.

Follow-up interviews

Results from the DART-I, which evaluated seven domains related to implementation potential (Kemp et al., 2025; Ramsey et al., 2019), indicated that participants rated BRAIN-FIT as having high implementation potential (4.41 out of 5; SD = 0.41). All domains scored between 4.25 and 4.63, with no scores below 3. This indicates that participants rate BRAIN-FIT as “ready” for larger-scale implementation.

Interviews were coded based on statements related to feasibility, acceptability, and relevance, as well as a section on suggestions for improvement. Participants consistently described the program as feasible, emphasizing the convenience of on-site delivery within familiar community settings. The location reduced common barriers such as transportation, mobility challenges, and navigating clinical environments (“It was great it was here. It’s the getting them [my neighbors] to get out, to get something like that is very challenging because they don’t have access to transportation.”—BF324). Participants noted that the clear organization, flexible station flow, and ability to complete multiple screenings in one visit made participation easy and efficient:…how you gave us the sheet and depending who was available to take me next, you know, you have five areas to cover. I might go from a three to five to one to whatever, but it very organized how you did that. (BF218)

The program was perceived as highly acceptable, with participants highlighting the professionalism, friendliness, and attentiveness of staff and student clinicians. Immediate feedback from screening tools and clear explanations further reinforced confidence in the process and contributed to a positive overall experience (“At the health fair, you can see all of those different people in one hour versus making five different appointments to get the same tests on at different times on different days.”—BF344).

Participants also emphasized the program’s relevance to their health and aging-related concerns. Screenings were described as empowering, providing a meaningful snapshot of current health and increasing awareness of modifiable risk factors (“The whole thing is that you’re doing these modifiable risks, so there’s hope in identifying what is needed.”—BF211). Participants valued being asked questions they found personally meaningful and noted that the program addressed domains often overlooked in routine medical visits, such as cognition, hearing, and social-emotional health (“You were asking questions that mattered to me and you’re willing to listen and take into consideration what I had to say.”—BF299). Discussion and implications

This pilot study provides early evidence supporting the feasibility and acceptability of a comprehensive, multimodal brain-health screening program delivered within rural community settings. BRAIN-FIT was intentionally designed to function outside traditional clinical infrastructure, and findings suggest that this community-embedded approach is both operationally viable and well accepted by participants. The interdisciplinary structure allowed adults to receive individualized education alongside actionable health insights, reinforcing the role of accessible, prevention-oriented models in promoting brain health among aging populations.

From an implementation perspective, the program demonstrated meaningful geographic reach across multiple mid-sized cities, surrounding rural and nonmetro areas, and affiliated clinical sites. Geographical variation in rurality appeared to influence both feasibility and participant reach. Although the largest number of participants was observed in mid-sized metropolitan areas, most screening events occurred in nonmetropolitan communities (12/18, 66.7%). Of the total sample (N = 119), 70 participants (58.8%) were recruited from metro-based events, while 49 participants (41.2%) were recruited from nonmetro sites. Despite lower per-site volume, nonmetro events demonstrated substantial cumulative reach across distributed locations. These findings suggest a tradeoff between site-level efficiency and distributed access: while metro sites may support higher throughput per event, rural and nonmetro deployments enable broader population engagement by reducing geographic and structural barriers to care. From a feasibility perspective, implementation in more rural settings required additional coordination (e.g., staffing, travel, and workflow adaptation), but did not preclude successful delivery.

These findings align with previous work demonstrating that rural populations face structural and logistical barriers to accessing care, including travel burden, limited service availability, and system-level inefficiencies (Boring, 2017; Villapiano et al., 2017). Qualitative evidence on chronic condition management suggests that geographic proximity alone is insufficient to ensure access, and that other factors, such as care coordination, cultural fit, and perceived quality, also influence engagement (Golembiewski et al., 2022). In this context, the present findings support that integration within a trusted, accessible community may mitigate some of these barriers and facilitate engagement.

Importantly, the sample reflected a broad age range (46–95 years; M = 67.5), substantial income variability, and heterogeneous neurological histories, including individuals with prior traumatic brain injury (21.5%), other neurological conditions (20.2%), and stroke (5%). This clinical heterogeneity strengthens the ecological validity of findings and supports the program’s applicability to aging populations with complex health profiles and addressing health aging in mid-life.

Nearly 40% of participants reported annual incomes between $25,000 and $50,000, suggesting successful engagement of lower-income adults, a population often underrepresented in preventive brain-health initiatives. The educational distribution further reflects diversity in health literacy contexts (Aljafri et al., 2025), with representation spanning from high school education to professional degrees. Together, these demographic features enhance confidence in the program’s pragmatic relevance and its readiness for implementation across varied community strata.

At the same time, the racial composition of the sample was predominantly White (89.1%), with limited representation of Black and Hispanic/Latino participants. This demographic pattern reflects broader regional population characteristics but constrains conclusions regarding cultural generalizability (Peters et al., 2022). Additionally, women comprised approximately two-thirds of the sample, a common pattern in preventive health and community-based screening research (Steinberg et al., 2021), but one that underscores the need for targeted recruitment strategies to enhance male participation in future implementation phases.

Preliminary qualitative findings suggest that the screening and counseling process supported health-related behavior change among 43% of participants, with a need to support continued verification of these changes. By integrating multidomain screening with individualized education and goal-setting, the program advances a model aligned with chronic care and self-management frameworks. Chronic health conditions are both more prevalent and associated with higher mortality in rural residents compared to urban counterparts (Boring, 2017; Garcia, 2017; Villapiano et al., 2017), indicating that self-management in rural areas is needed to support overall health and wellness. Contemporary self‑management frameworks conceptualize self‑management as a set of processes that include problem solving, decision making, resource utilization, partnership with providers, and taking action (Lorig & Holman, 2003). These have shown increased effectiveness and sustainability when embedded in community-based self-management of chronic care interventions (Collinsworth et al., 2014; Walton et al., 2012; Zolezzi et al., 2022). Embedding preventive services within trusted community settings may enhance both access and sustained engagement, which are core determinants of successful implementation (Mendel et al., 2008).

Multidomain health screening further revealed several modifiable risk factors relevant to brain-health trajectories in this aging cohort (Livingston et al., 2024). On average, participants demonstrated elevated cardiometabolic risk indicators, including blood pressure within the prehypertensive range, a mean BMI in the overweight category (Qiu & Fratiglioni, 2015), and borderline malnutrition risk scores. Physical endurance, as measured by the Six-Minute Walk Test, fell below recommended targets (Enright, 2003), while global sleep scores suggested clinically meaningful sleep disturbance at the population level (Buysse et al., 1991). Sensory screening identified notable rates of potential vision impairment, and nearly one-quarter of participants did not pass the hearing screening—both factors increasingly recognized as modifiable contributors to later-life cognitive decline (Liu & Lee, 2019; Livingston et al., 2024; Wahl et al., 2013).

Importantly, cognitive and language performance remained largely within functional limits for most participants, suggesting that BRAIN-FIT is successfully engaging adults during a potentially preventive window rather than after substantial impairment has emerged (O’Donnell et al., 2015; Ritchie et al., 2024). Taken together, these findings reinforce the value of multimodal community screening for identifying early, actionable risk profiles that may otherwise remain undetected in routine care.

From an implementation perspective, the co-occurrence of cardiometabolic, sensory, lifestyle, and sleep-related risks supports the need for integrated rather than siloed prevention models (Braithwaite et al., 2018). Future directions will prioritize longitudinal follow-up to determine whether individualized education and goal-setting translate into sustained health behavior change and downstream cognitive outcomes. Additionally, these data suggest that strengthening referral pathways—particularly for vision, hearing, sleep, and metabolic management—may enhance the clinical impact of community-based screening programs (Del Rosario et al., 2025; Zuckerman et al., 2013). As BRAIN-FIT expands, incorporating repeat assessments will be critical for evaluating risk modification over time (Rigaud et al., 2024) and positioning the program within hybrid effectiveness–implementation trials focused on delaying or reducing dementia risk (Curran et al., 2012).

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. The racial and ethnic homogeneity of the sample limits generalizability to more diverse populations and highlights the need for culturally tailored outreach and partnership strategies in subsequent phases. While income variability was achieved, the underrepresentation of some racial groups suggests structural or relational barriers that warrant attention in future implementation cycles (Jones et al., 2026).

The predominance of female participants may also reflect differential help-seeking behavior or community engagement patterns in aging populations (Holdcroft, 2007; Umberson et al., 2022). Future work should examine gender-specific barriers to participation to improve representativeness and reach (Hernandez et al., 2018). Finally, although the sample included individuals with heterogeneous neurological histories, longitudinal validation of behavioral and cognitive outcomes remains ongoing. Future expansion will prioritize deliberate recruitment strategies to enhance racial, ethnic, and gender diversity, strengthen representativeness, and further evaluate sustained outcomes across demographically varied communities.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, measurement-related challenges were identified during implementation, including variability in administration across stations, reliance on brief screening tools, and participant burden associated with completing multiple domains within a single session. These factors may have influenced the precision and completeness of some measures, particularly in high-throughput community settings. Second, data loss occurred for a subset of participants (21.9%), primarily due to workflow inefficiencies, variability in staff training, and technical constraints in data capture systems (e.g., inconsistent use of digital entry platforms, inability to navigate backward in the tracking interface, and missed stations during bottlenecks). Although most participants completed all stations, missing data were most common in the physical, lifestyle, and hearing domains, which may introduce bias in domain-specific findings.

Importantly, these limitations reflect early-stage implementation within a pragmatic, community-based model and informed iterative refinements to protocol and workflow. In subsequent years of the pilot, strategies to reduce data loss include enhanced standardized staff training, real-time monitoring of data completeness, improved digital infrastructure (e.g., flexible data entry interfaces with error-checking and back-navigation), and optimization of station flow to minimize bottlenecks and ensure completion of all screening components. Additionally, future iterations will evaluate the feasibility of reducing measurement burden and prioritizing core domains to enhance data quality while maintaining multidomain coverage.

Conclusion

BRAIN-FIT demonstrates a feasible model for multimodal brain-health screening embedded in community settings. The demographic breadth achieved across age, income, and neurological complexity supports the program’s pragmatic relevance, while identified gaps in racial and gender representation provide a clear roadmap for refinement. Importantly, multidomain screening revealed clustering of modifiable risk factors within an otherwise largely cognitively functional cohort, underscoring the value of community-based screening to identify individuals during a potentially preventive window. Early detection of these risks may enable timely education, referral, and behavior change efforts to support healthier cognitive aging trajectories. These findings establish a foundation for longitudinal evaluation and progression toward a fully powered hybrid effectiveness–implementation trial aimed at expanding equitable brain-health promotion across aging populations.
