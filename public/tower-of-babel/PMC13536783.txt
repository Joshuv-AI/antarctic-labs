Comprehensive comparison of extraction space closure during canine retraction using nickel-titanium closed-coil springs versus elastomeric power chains during orthodontic treatment – a split-mouth randomized controlled trial
Authors: Munnal Gulzar, Laiba Amin, Raif Ilyas, Mubassar Fida, Rashna Hoshang Sukhia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe two widely used methods for orthodontic tooth movement with sliding mechanics are elastomeric power chains (EPC) and nickel-titanium closed-coil springs (NCCS).ObjectivesTo compare the rate of extraction space closure during canine retraction, degree of root resorption, dental plaque accumulation and gingival health using NCCS versus EPC in subjects undergoing first premolar extractions.Trial designSingle-centered, prospective, split-mouth randomized controlled trial.MethodsFor canine retraction, NCCS and EPCs were randomly allocated to the right and left quadrants of both maxillary and mandibular arches in 46 subjects. Canine retraction, root resorption, plaque accumulation and gingival health were recorded at four points in time. Within groups, pairwise comparison and between-group comparisons were done using Wilcoxon signed rank test. Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) analysis was employed to identify the factors significantly influencing the rate of extraction space closure during canine retraction and the extent of root resorption.BlindingThe study investigators, patients recruited, and assessors were not blinded during the trial due to the visible nature of the orthodontic appliances used.ResultsNCCS exhibited faster rates in total canine retraction and during the T0-T1 (p = 0.039) and T2-T3 (p = 0.003) intervals. Root resorption exhibited non-significant differences. Whereas, NCCS demonstrated higher plaque retention at T1 (p = 0.013), T2 (p = 0.016) and T3 (p = 0.033). GI differed significantly between groups, with higher values with NCCS at T1 (p = 0.046) and EPC at T2 (p = 0.039). Extraction space closure rate displayed a significant difference between the T1-T2 and T2-T3 stages with NCCS (p = 0.025). GEE showed a significant positive association of age with root resorption (β = 0.939, CI = 0.312, 1.565, p = 0.003).ConclusionNCCS demonstrated a significantly higher rate of extraction space closure and plaque accumulation compared to EPC. However, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups with respect to root resorption and gingival health. Furthermore, the degree of root resorption was found to increase significantly with the advancing age.Trial registrationRegistered at clinicaltrials.gov under the identifier NCT06567730 on 23rd August 2024.

Introduction

In patients presenting with severe dental crowding and/or pronounced proclination of the anterior teeth, extraction of the first premolars followed by canine retraction is a widely accepted and commonly practiced treatment approach [1–3]. Canine retraction, however, is a prolonged phase of orthodontic therapy, and accelerating this process can significantly reduce overall treatment duration while minimizing potential adverse effects associated with prolonged appliance use [4, 5].

The most common techniques used for canine retraction are sliding and loop mechanics, which have been discussed frequently in literature [6]. Both techniques offer distinct advantages, including reduced friction and enhanced control over space closure through the use of loops [7]. Whereas, while using sliding mechanics, one can have the benefits of reduced wire bending time, control of arch form and root alignment [7]. However, both techniques are associated with specific limitations. With loop mechanics, the additional chair side time, skills, soft tissue irritation, root resorption, bacterial build up and uncontrolled tipping present a challenge [8, 9]. Friction and binding of wire to the bracket slot results in slow tooth movement and anchorage loss with sliding mechanics [9].

Despite its inherent limitations, sliding mechanics remains one of the most commonly employed techniques for canine retraction [10]. The two widely used methods for sliding teeth include elastomeric power chains (EPC) and nickel-titanium (Ni–Ti) closed-coil springs (NCCS) [11]. EPCs are widely favored in orthodontic practice because of their ease of use, effectiveness and patient comfort by allowing periods of regeneration [12]. EPCs exert heavy interrupted forces when they return to their original length after being stretched, with decay of forces as they revert, requiring a greater number of appliance activation [13].

In contrast, NCCS apply light continuous forces and deliver a constant force over a period that leads to more efficient tooth movement and steadier space closure, with reduced number of appliance activation [14, 15]. In a study by Barsoum et al. [16] NCCS demonstrated force reduction after 18 hours and remained constant for about 21 days. Badran et al. [17] also found that NCCS were less tolerated than elastomeric power chains by patients but produced an average of 0.5 mm more tooth movement than the EPCs during the 12-week study period.

Although several clinical trials have compared canine retraction mechanics using NCCS and EPC, most have primarily focused on the rate of tooth movement and selected side effects, such as root resorption, tipping, and rotation [18]. Therefore, a gap remains in the literature regarding a comprehensive evaluation that simultaneously assesses extraction space closure rate, root resorption, plaque accumulation, and gingival health within the same population using conventional techniques in a routine clinical setting. The present study addresses this gap by integrating these biological and periodontal parameters in a single cohort, thereby reducing the risk of confounding that may arise when different outcomes are examined in separate populations. Evaluation of canine retraction efficiency using NCCS and EPC is critical for optimizing orthodontic treatment outcomes, while concurrent assessment of root resorption and periodontal indices is essential for maintaining periodontal health and overall treatment quality.

The present trial assessed the rate of extraction space closure, canine root resorption, plaque accumulation, and gingival health during canine retraction using NCCS and EPC. The null hypothesis stated that no statistically significant differences would be observed between the two force delivery systems with respect to extraction space closure rate, canine root resorption, plaque accumulation, or gingival health in patients undergoing first premolar extractions.

Material and methods

Trial design

The study was a single-center, prospective, split-mouth randomized controlled trial with 1:1 allocation ratio. An approval from the Ethical Review Committee (2024–9110–28139) was obtained at a tertiary care hospital and prospectively registered at clinicaltrials.gov under the identifier NCT06567730. The trial was conducted and reported following the CONSORT statement and no changes were made after the trial started.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using standard estimate where n = 8(CV2)/(PC2) [1 + (1-PC)2], PC is the proportionate change in means (PC = (μ0 − μ1)/μ0) and CV is the coefficient of variation (CV = σ0/μ0 = σ1/μ1). Based on previously reported data [8], the mean ± SD values for total canine retraction were 3.88 ± 2.66 mm for EPC and 5.45 ± 1.53 mm for NCCS, corresponding to an estimated PC of approximately 39%. The CV was conservatively assumed to change by 68% or less, reflecting the expected biological variability in orthodontic tooth movement reported in prior studies and ensuring adequate statistical power under clinical conditions. Using these parameters, the study required a minimum sample size of 33 (N) patients in each group. With an inflation of 40% for an estimated loss to follow up/non-response rate, 46 (N) participants were included in the study. As this was a split‑mouth study in which each participant received both interventions across all four quadrants, a total of 184 canine observations were obtained (four per patient). These observations were inherently paired, and the patient was considered the unit of analysis. This sample size provided 80% power to detect the stated effect size at a two‑sided 5% level of significance.

Participants, eligibility criteria, and settings

Patients visiting the orthodontic clinics at the Aga Khan University Hospital Karachi, aged between 13—40 years and undergoing fixed orthodontic treatment were recruited in the study from September 2024 to March 2025. This age range was selected to represent individuals with fully erupted permanent dentition and active orthodontic treatment needs (The split-mouth study design ensured that age-related biological variability in tooth movement was controlled, as each participant served as their own control during canine retraction). Informed consent was obtained prior to recruitment. Detailed information regarding the study was provided to the participants and they were given the choice to either accept or refuse their inclusion in the study. The eligibility criteria included patients with Angle’s Class I malocclusion, with the presence of a full set of erupted permanent teeth (except for third molars), requiring bilateral first premolar extractions as part of a predetermined treatment plan for dentoalveolar protrusion, with or without mild crowding. All patients had completed initial leveling and alignment prior to extraction and initiation of canine retraction, with a standardized anchorage requirement across participants. Posterior anchorage reinforcement was achieved using a uniform clinical protocol in all cases. Patients with severe crowding requiring early extraction for substantial space utilization during alignment were excluded. Patients with systemic diseases that can affect tooth movements, uncontrolled periodontal disease, craniofacial syndromes, pregnant or lactating mothers, patients on medications that can affect tooth movements, patients with nickel allergies and patients with bracket failures greater than three times per bracket during the study were excluded. All participants were instructed to distribute mastication bilaterally during the study period to minimize asymmetric functional loading.

Interventions

Following bonding of the maxillary and mandibular arches with 0.022-inch Roth prescription (SKY DENT Inc., Manhattan, New York, USA), 0.012 Ni–Ti archwires were inserted for initial levelling and alignment. Levelling and alignment proceeded till 0.017 × 0.025″ stainless steel archwires were passively engaged, indicating completion of this phase. Subsequently, all first premolars were extracted under local anesthesia. Canine retraction was initiated one week after premolar extraction, allowing for initial soft tissue healing. All cases required maximum anchorage, which was standardized by ligating the second premolar, first molar and second molar to serve as a single anchorage unit prior to initiation of canine retraction. Since a split-mouth randomization design was used, where each participant received both interventions, allocation was performed at the side level in each arch (right/left), with one side randomly assigned to NCCS and the contralateral side assigned to EPC in a 1:1 allocation ratio (Fig. 1). In the NCCS group, the spring (SKY DENT Inc., Manhattan, New York, USA) was activated to apply a force of 150–200 g, removed, adjusted and retied at each 4-week follow-up visit (Fig. 2). The power chains (SKY DENT Inc., Manhattan, New York, USA) were refreshed at each 4-week follow-up visit in the EPC group to maintain a comparable force of 150–200 g (Fig. 3). At each follow-up, both force systems were standardized to deliver 150–200 g using a calibrated digital force gauge. The gauge was aligned along the intended line of canine retraction, and the force-delivery system was secured once the target force range was achieved. In the EPC group, the distal end was attached to the permanent first molar, while in the NCCS group, the distal end was attached to the permanent second molar, which formed part of the posterior anchorage unit. In both groups, the force was applied directly to the canine hook. To prevent any unintended distal rotation or tipping of the canine during retraction, the distal wing of the canine bracket was ligated using a stainless-steel ligature.

Participants exhibiting any adverse reactions associated with NCCS, including contact dermatitis, rash, swelling, erythema or irritation of the skin or oral mucosa, had the appliance discontinued and the patient was excluded from the study.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the rate of extraction space closure during canine retraction. Secondary outcomes included assessment of root resorption, plaque accumulation, and gingival health parameters, which were evaluated to assess the biological and periodontal response to the applied orthodontic forces. The rate of canine retraction was evaluated by measuring the distance between the mesial surface of the second premolar and the distal surface of the canine at each follow-up visit using a 100 mm calibrated scale. Canine movement was calculated as the difference between sequential measurements recorded at predefined time points i.e. T0 – T1, T1 – T2, T2 – T3 and as the overall change from baseline to the final assessment T0 – T3.

The Plaque Index (PI) was noted using the grading system used to assess the amount of plaque present at the gingival margins of the canine [17]. The PI consists of four grades including Grade 0, 1, 2 and 3. The increase in grade indicates the increased amount of plaque along the gingiva. Gingival health of the canine was assessed using the Gingival Index (GI) proposed by Loe [19]. It consists of four grades including Grade 0, 1, 2 and 3. The increase in grade indicates the compromised gingival health of the teeth.

Periapical radiographs (PA) were obtained to evaluate root resorption by measuring the length of each canine from the cusp tip to the root apex. To reduce variability, all periapical radiographs were acquired by a single operator using a cone indicator. Each canine radiograph included the entire crown and root, extending approximately 1 mm beyond the apex, with both the mesial and distal aspects clearly visible. Any projection discrepancies were corrected using the method described by Kocadereli et al. [20], implemented through SIDEXIS XG 2.63 for image analysis (Fig. 4). A single investigator first measured the total tooth length, followed by the root measurement from cervical area till the root apex. The change in root length was calculated by subtracting the second root length measurement from the first measurement i.e. T0 – T1, T1 – T2, T2 – T3 and finally T0 – T3.Fig. 4Root resorption calculation

Root resorption calculation

Radiographic measurement (root resorption) and clinical measurements (space closure, plaque accumulation and gingival health) were recorded at four points in time. Baseline measurements were obtained at the initiation of canine retraction (T0). Follow‑up assessments were conducted at standardized 4‑week intervals, designated as T1 (4 weeks), T2 (8 weeks), and T3 (12 weeks) following the commencement of canine retraction. All participants were observed for an identical total follow‑up period of 12 weeks.

Harms

NCCS were meticulously evaluated to ensure they did not impinge on the surrounding soft tissues. Throughout the study period, no participants reported any adverse effects or allergic reactions. Furthermore, no significant complications or serious harm were observed.

Randomization and blinding

Block randomization was performed to code the type of retraction method in this trial and to determine the sequence of its administration to each participant. This was controlled by a research faculty member who was not involved in the study. The randomization codes were written on white papers folded three times and then placed in sealed envelopes.

During the recruitment of the participants in the trial, the sealed envelope for a participant was opened by the enrolling team member and they were allotted an intervention according to the ascending order sequence already generated. Blinding was not feasible for this trial due to the visible nature of the orthodontic appliances used. The study investigators, patients recruited, and assessors were not blinded during the trial.

Reliability analysis

To ensure measurement consistency, 10% of the sample was randomly selected and measured by the same examiner after a two-week interval. The repeated measurements included the root length (cusp tip to apex) on standardized periapical radiographs. Intra-examiner reliability for these continuous outcomes was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient [two-way mixed-effects, absolute agreement, single measure; ICC(3,1)] with 95% confidence intervals.

For the ordinal outcomes (Plaque Index and Gingival Index), intra-examiner agreement was evaluated using quadratically weighted Cohen’s kappa with 95% confidence intervals. The examiner was calibrated on a pilot set before data collection.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). The significance level was kept at p ≤ 0.05 for all statistical analyses. The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to check the normality of the data and displayed a non-normal distribution. Mean age was reported for both the groups. Frequencies were reported for categorical variables, such as gender. For non-parametric paired analyses, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare NCCS and EPC groups for canine retraction, root resorption, PI, and GI, and to perform within-group pairwise comparisons of canine retraction and root resorption across consecutive time intervals. A Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) was conducted to determine the impact of various factors on canine retraction and root resorption.

Results

A total of 46 participants were enrolled in the study, comprising 22 males and 24 females, with a mean age of 19.23 ± 5.49 years (Table 1). All 46 participants completed the study and were included in the final analysis. No losses to follow-up were recorded. Each participant contributed data for both intervention sides. The total canine retraction achieved in the duration of three months for NCCS was 5.00 mm (IQR: 4, 5), while for EPC it was 4.00 mm (IQR: 3, 4). During T2-T3 interval, the median rate was 2.00 mm/month for NCCS (IQR: 1, 2) and 1.00 mm/month for EPC (IQR: 1, 2), with the difference also reaching statistical significance (p = 0.003) (Table 2). Discussion

This split-mouth randomized-controlled trial evaluated and compared the clinical performance of NCCS and EPC for extraction space closure during canine retraction over a three-month period. Although several studies have investigated these force delivery systems, the evidence remains inconclusive, and few studies have assessed extraction space closure alongside root resorption and periodontal variables within the same population [5, 9, 21–23]. In this trial, the null hypothesis stating that the rate of extraction space closure, plaque accumulation, and gingival health during canine retraction is the same with NCCS and EPC was rejected. However, the null hypothesis asserting no significant differences in root resorption between the two methods was accepted.

Over the three-month retraction period, NCCS demonstrated a greater overall reduction in extraction space than EPC, with the difference most evident during the first two months. This contrasts with the findings of Barsoum et al. [16] who reported no clinically or statistically significant difference in retraction rate between NCCS and EPC. Interestingly, a significantly faster rate of retraction with NCCS was observed in the third month, which may reflect the more consistent and continuous force delivery associated with coil springs [24]. However, this differs from the observations of Badran et al. [17], who noted a reduced rate of space closure following the second activation of NCCS and attributed this to reactivation of the same coil spring, whereas the elastomeric chain was replaced.

Taken together, the present findings suggest a modest tendency toward accelerated tooth movement and greater space closure with the use of springs, as the NCCS group achieved 5 mm of canine retraction compared to 4 mm in the EPC group during the three month trial. This pattern is consistent with Hashemzadeh et al. [8] who reported greater distal canine movement with NCCS (5.45 mm) than EPC (3.88 mm) in adolescents over a 4 month period, and with Badran et al. [17] who observed a small but statistically significant benefit of coil springs over power chains at 12 weeks, with coil springs achieving approximately 0.5 mm greater canine retraction compared to power chains. A systematic review by Sebastian et al. [9] likewise reported a slight overall advantage for NCCS, with a mean difference of approximately 0.24 mm favoring springs. Although the findings indicate a statistically significant advantage of NCCS over EPC in early-phase extraction space closure, the magnitude of this difference appears clinically modest and is unlikely to substantially influence overall treatment duration. Therefore, the observed difference should be interpreted as a limited early-phase effect rather than a clinically decisive advantage. From a routine clinical perspective, NCCS may offer practical advantages related to reduced need for frequent replacement or activation, potentially decreasing chairside adjustments. However, this should be balanced against considerations such as oral hygiene maintenance, patient comfort, and cost.

Root resorption is an important adverse effect of retraction. Aras et al. [25] concluded that intermittent forces, by allowing periods of rest, promote cellular regeneration and consequently reduce root resorption compared to continuous forces. In contrast, Dindaroğlu et al. [26] reported no significant differences in the extent or severity of root resorption when comparing continuous and interrupted force applications. In the present study, despite the use of a more continuous force delivery system with NCCS (0.70 mm) and an intermittently renewed force system with EPC (0.80 mm), no statistically significant difference in early radiographically detectable root change was observed between groups. This is consistent with Le et al. [27] who reported comparable reductions in canine root length with NCCS (0.90 ± 0.60 mm) and EPC (0.92 ± 0.69 mm), and Barsoum et al. [16] who similarly found no meaningful difference in root resorption between these modalities. Collectively, the findings support the conclusion that NCCS is not associated with a greater extent of early detectable root change than EPC when equivalent orthodontic forces are applied. The observed similarity in resorption levels may be attributed to the application of light, continuous forces (150–200 g) over short retraction durations, conditions known to minimize apical remodeling.

Previous studies have linked NCCS to potential oral hygiene challenges due to their auxiliary configuration and the additional plaque-retentive surfaces they introduce [28]. In the present study, plaque accumulation differed significantly between the two groups, with higher plaque scores observed in the NCCS group compared with the EPC group. This contrasts with Badran et al. [17], who reported no significant intergroup differences in plaque index scores at any time point, although participants reported greater difficulty maintaining oral hygiene with springs. This increased difficulty in oral hygiene maintenance, together with the greater tendency of NCCS to promote microbial colony formation, may explain the higher plaque accumulation observed with NCCS [29]. Conversely, recent studies have indicated that elastomeric power chains may be associated with an increased bacterial load, potentially compromising gingival health [30, 31]. Gingival health was broadly comparable between groups, however, transient differences were observed, with higher GI scores in the NCCS group during the first month and in the EPC group during the second month. Overall, these findings reinforce that fixed appliances predispose to plaque retention, and that oral hygiene maintenance is likely a dominant determinant of plaque and gingival outcomes during space closure.

Orthodontic tooth movement may vary with patient-related factors, such as age (with adolescents typically exhibiting slightly faster movement than adults) and potentially gender. However, the split-mouth design employed in this study effectively controlled for individual variability. The age range and gender distribution in the sample were comparable, and existing research and the recent trial indicates that gender does not have a consistent influence on canine retraction rate [23]. Of particular importance, because allocation occurred within the same individual (each patient acted as their own control), age and gender could not systematically differ between intervention conditions, thereby minimizing their potential confounding influence. Nevertheless, GEE analysis demonstrated a positive association between increasing age and the degree of radiographically detected root resorption. This finding is supported by the literature, which attributes the trend to reduced periodontal vascularity and increased bone density in older individuals [26, 32].

Strengths

This study shares many strengths with the recent research cited above. First, the split-mouth randomized design controlled for inter-subject variability as each patient received both appliances on opposite sides, ensuring that factors like skeletal pattern, bone density, masticatory and oral hygiene habits were held constant across interventions, thereby reducing the biological variability.

Second, the protocol was purposefully structured to reflect routine orthodontic practice by employing commonly used force delivery systems and anchorage reinforcement strategies during canine retraction, thereby enhancing external validity and improving the applicability of the findings to real‑world clinical settings.

Third, the study evaluated extraction space closure, root resorption, plaque accumulation, and gingival health within the same cohort, reducing confounding that may occur when outcomes are assessed across different populations. Finally, EPC and NCCS were applied under standardized clinical protocols, with continuous verification and calibration of the delivered force to 150–200 g at each visit. Outcome assessment followed a prespecified measurement protocol with reliability and agreement analyses (e.g., ICC (3,1) and weighted κ, each with 95% CIs), enhancing the transparency and reproducibility of the outcomes.

Limitations

However, several limitations merit discussion. Although participants were instructed to chew bilaterally, individual masticatory habits could not be objectively monitored, and preferential functional loading cannot be completely excluded. In addition, while split-mouth randomized trials control many confounders, patient blinding was not possible due to the visible nature of the orthodontic appliances used and clinicians could not be blinded during appliance placement. This introduces the possibility of performance and detection bias and the limitation was carefully considered in interpreting the findings. Nonetheless, the potential impact of non-blinding was mitigated by the application of both interventions within the same patient under identical treatment conditions and standardized activation protocols, and by the inherent reduction in inter-individual variability afforded by the split-mouth design, thereby strengthening internal validity and minimizing confounding effects that could otherwise amplify bias in parallel-group designs. To further reduce detection bias, all outcomes were assessed using standardized and calibrated measurement procedures, and intra-examiner reliability was confirmed to ensure measurement consistency. Because the primary outcome was quantitatively measured, the influence of subjective interpretation was minimized, however, subtle bias related to treatment awareness cannot be completely ruled out. Future studies incorporating automated measurements or independent blinded digital analyses may further strengthen the evidence.

Furthermore, anchorage loss was not evaluated in the study. Therefore, the observed reduction in extraction space should be interpreted as overall space closure rather than isolated canine displacement. This study was designed to compare canine retraction mechanics in a routine orthodontic clinical practice, rather than to quantify absolute orthodontic tooth movement independent of anchorage effects. Therefore, conventional clinical measurements and standardized periapical radiographs were used rather than advanced imaging modalities, to maintain clinical relevance and minimize radiation exposure. Nevertheless, three‑dimensional imaging (e.g., CBCT) would provide more sensitive assessment of root changes and angular movement, whereas two‑dimensional radiographs may underestimate subtle root surface alterations. In addition, the outcomes related to root changes and periodontal parameters reflect short‑term observations and may not represent long‑term orthodontic sequelae, therefore, findings should be interpreted as early‑phase responses. Longitudinal studies with extended follow-up are necessary to evaluate whether these initial differences persist and to better understand their implications for long-term tooth stability and root resorption. Finally, larger samples and multi‑center recruitment would enhance generalizability, and inclusion of patient‑reported outcomes and treatment efficiency measures would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of clinical performance.
