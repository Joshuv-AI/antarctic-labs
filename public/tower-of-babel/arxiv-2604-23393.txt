Asymptotic theory of rerandomization for survival analysis
Xinyuan Chen, Fan Li

Abstract
Rerandomization systematically reduces chance imbalance and can improve the efficiency of the average treatment effect estimator in randomized experiments. While the asymptotic properties of finite-dimensional M-estimators under rerandomization have been established, existing theory does not directly address survival outcomes under censoring, where the target estimand involves infinite-dimensional functional parameters. This article establishes the uniform weak convergence of treatment-specific survival function estimators under rerandomization and stratified rerandomization. We prove that the Kaplan-Meier and inverse probability of censoring weighted Kaplan-Meier estimators converge to tight limiting processes with reduced pointwise asymptotic variances. Furthermore, we prove that the pointwise asymptotic variance of the debiased machine learning survival function estimator remains invariant under rerandomization, a consequence of the Neyman orthogonality. Simulations and a real data example are used to illustrate the theoretical results. Our results characterize the geometric interplay between restricted randomization designs and analysis-stage covariate adjustment for functional target estimands in survival analysis.



Keywords:
Bahadur representation; Covariate adjustment; Debiased machine learning; Inverse probability of censoring weighting; Randomized experiments; Rerandomization

Introduction

Rerandomization
(
13
)
is an attractive experimental design strategy that restricts randomization to allocations that satisfy pre-specified covariate balance criteria, thereby improving baseline balance and potentially improving the statistical efficiency for inference. While the majority of the theoretical literature operates within the finite-population design-based framework
(
9
;
11
;
10
;
21
;
25
;
22
;
7
, e.g.,)
, recent work by
17
offered an important generalization of the rerandomization theory to super-population estimands and a wider class of estimators, by characterizing the asymptotic behavior of finite-dimensional M-estimators for scalar average causal effect estimands under a sampling-based perspective. Furthermore,
20

1

A comprehensive review of historical foundations and theoretical developments of rerandomization, with a focus on the average treatment effect estimand, is provided in
8
.

Beyond simple scalar estimands, causal survival analysis has been central in randomized experiments, where time-to-event outcomes such as overall and progression-free survival serve as primary endpoints in many pivotal trials. The existing asymptotic theory for rerandomization, however, does not directly apply to this setting. More importantly, causal survival analysis inherently targets infinite-dimensional functional parameters—most typically the treatment-specific/counterfactual survival function
(
4
)
—and is subject to right-censoring and time-evolving risk sets. Characterizing the impact of rerandomization on causal survival analysis thus requires advancing the technical machinery from standard multivariate central limit theorems to the uniform weak convergence of empirical processes. Motivated by this methodological gap, we seek to establish the variance-reduction geometry and uniform limiting processes for estimating treatment-specific survival functions under rerandomization.

We investigate the asymptotic implications of rerandomization on three causal survival estimators: the Kaplan-Meier (KM) estimator, the inverse probability of censoring weighted Kaplan-Meier (IPCW-KM) estimator, and the recently established debiased machine learning (DML) estimator
(
23
)
, which leverages the efficient influence function (EIF) to achieve flexible covariate adjustment in the analysis stage. Our first finding is that under rerandomization, all three estimators remain uniformly consistent and preserve their Bahadur representations under simple randomization. However, under rerandomization, the KM and IPCW-KM estimators converge uniformly to tight limiting processes with reduced pointwise asymptotic variances unless the outcome counting and at-risk processes are uncorrelated with the rerandomization covariates. In contrast, rerandomization does not reduce the pointwise asymptotic variance of the DML estimator, provided the rerandomization covariates are included in the set of covariates adjusted for in the nuisance outcome model; hence, rerandomization is asymptotically ignorable in the analysis stage with flexible covariate adjustment. These results, for the first time, expand the asymptotic analysis of
17
and the insights under rerandomization to the infinite-dimensional functional domain.

To understand why rerandomization reduces the variance of the KM and IPCW-KM estimators but not the DML estimator, we appeal to a geometric decomposition. By the Hilbert space projection theorem, each estimator’s limiting process decomposes into a projection onto the subspace spanned by the rerandomization covariates and an independent Gaussian process residual orthogonal to that subspace. Hence, the randomness of the projection onto the rerandomization covariate subspace arises solely from the covariates, with the projection processes being deterministic. The rerandomization constraint restricts the stochastic variability of this projection component, whereas the residual component remains unaffected, effectively clamping the stochastic process cloud into a tighter spindle. For the KM and IPCW-KM estimators, this projection is non-trivial because the outcome counting and at-risk processes are generally correlated with the rerandomization covariates. In particular, although the IPCW-KM estimator adjusts for covariates to account for censoring under the covariate-dependent censoring assumption, the outcome counting and at-risk processes remain unadjusted, so the variance-reduction implication of rerandomization for the KM estimator applies equally even in the presence of censoring weights. For the DML estimator, however, the Neyman orthogonality of the EIF renders the projection asymptotically zero, leaving no variance to clamp. We formalize this geometric picture and its implications for both pointwise and uniform inference of the three typical causal survival estimators.

In developing the new asymptotic theory when introducing rerandomization to causal survival analysis, we assume that the true survival functions of the outcome and censoring times are càdlàg (right continuous with left limits) to allow greater generality. The rest of the article is organized as follows. Section

3

4

5
presents the numerical studies to illustrate the asymptotic analysis under rerandomization, including simulations and the analysis of a real-life dataset. Section
6

2.2
Simple randomization and rerandomization

Let
A^{*}=a\in\{0,1\}
denote the treatment assignment under simple randomization, where
\mathbb{P}(A^{*}=a)=\pi_{a}\in(0,1)
with
A^{*}\perp\mathcal{C}
. Compared to simple randomization, rerandomization controls imbalance on a subset of
p^{\mathrm{rr}}
baseline covariates,
\bm{Z}^{\mathrm{rr}}\subseteq\bm{Z}

(
13
)
, which involves the following steps. First, one independently generates
(A_{1}^{*},\ldots,A_{n}^{*})
from a Bernoulli distribution with
\mathbb{P}(A_{i}^{*}=a)=\pi_{a}\in(0,1)
under simple randomization. Then, the imbalance statistic on
(\bm{Z}_{1}^{\mathrm{rr}},\ldots,\bm{Z}_{n}^{\mathrm{rr}})
and its variance estimator are computed as

\displaystyle\bm{I}_{n}^{*}=\frac{1}{n_{1}^{*}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{*}\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{rr}}-\frac{1}{n_{0}^{*}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-A_{i}^{*})\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{rr}},

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}}}(\bm{I}_{n}^{*})=\frac{1}{n_{1}^{*}n_{0}^{*}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{rr}}-\overline{\bm{Z}}^{\mathrm{rr}}\right)\left(\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{rr}}-\overline{\bm{Z}}^{\mathrm{rr}}\right)^{\top},

where
n_{a}^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(A_{i}^{*}=a)
and
\overline{\bm{Z}}^{\mathrm{rr}}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{rr}}
. Lastly, given a pre-specified balance threshold
0<c<\infty
, one checks whether the realized assignment satisfies the balance criterion with
\bm{I}_{n}^{\ast\top}\{\widehat{{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}}}(\bm{I}_{n}^{*})\}^{-1}\bm{I}_{n}^{*}<c
. If true, the final treatment assignment is
(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})=(A_{1}^{*},\ldots,A_{n}^{*})
; otherwise, the steps are repeated until the first randomization scheme satisfying the balance criterion. It is worth mentioning that the above procedure considers the Mahalanobis distance to define the balance criterion, and thus
\bm{I}_{n}^{\ast\top}\{\widehat{{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}}}(\bm{I}_{n}^{*})\}^{-1}\bm{I}_{n}^{*}
follows a chi-squared distribution asymptotically. A key analytic implication is that rerandomization restricts the space of acceptable assignments to those satisfying
\bm{I}_{n}^{\ast\top}\{\widehat{{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}}}(\bm{I}_{n}^{*})\}^{-1}\bm{I}_{n}^{*}<c
, thereby inducing correlation among the treatment assignments
(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})
.

Assumption 3
(Rerandomization)

(i) The covariance matrix of the imbalance criterion
\widehat{{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}}}(\bm{I}_{n}^{*})
is positive definite, and
n\widehat{{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}}}(\bm{I}_{n}^{*})\overset{p}{\to}(\pi_{1}\pi_{0})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Z}^{\mathrm{rr}}}
, where
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Z}^{\mathrm{rr}}}\equiv{\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}}(\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{rr}})
, which is also positive definite. (ii) Let
\mathcal{E}_{n}=\{\bm{I}_{n}^{\ast\top}\{\widehat{{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}}}(\bm{I}_{n}^{*})\}^{-1}\bm{I}_{n}^{*}<c\}
denote the acceptance set for rerandomization. Then,
\mathcal{E}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{n}
with
\mathbb{P}(\partial\mathcal{E})=0
, where
\partial\mathcal{E}
is the boundary of
\mathcal{E}
.

Assumption
3
ensures that the acceptance set
\mathcal{E}_{n}
and its limit
\mathcal{E}
are not degenerate and there are no discontinuities on the boundary of
\mathcal{E}
. Conditional on
(\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{n})
, the estimated covariance matrix of the imbalance criterion is

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}}(\bm{I}_{n}^{*})=\left(\frac{1}{n_{a}^{*}}+\frac{1}{n_{1-a}^{*}}\right)\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{rr}}-\overline{\bm{Z}}_{i}^{\mathrm{rr}}\right)\left(\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{rr}}-\overline{\bm{Z}}_{i}^{\mathrm{rr}}\right)^{\top}\equiv\left(\frac{1}{n_{a}^{*}}+\frac{1}{n_{1-a}^{*}}\right)\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Z}^{\mathrm{rr}},n}.

To prepare for the subsequent asymptotic analyses, we further define

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{n}^{*}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\pi_{1}\pi_{0}}(A_{i}-\pi_{1})\left\{\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{rr}}-\mathbb{E}(\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{rr}})\right\},

(1)

and note that
\sqrt{n}\bm{I}_{n}^{*}=\bm{B}_{n}^{*}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. Lemma S1 in Section S2.1 of the Supplementary Materials shows that
\mathcal{E}_{n}=\{\bm{B}_{n}^{*\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}^{*},n}^{-1}\bm{B}_{n}^{*}<c\}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}^{*},n}=(\pi_{1}\pi_{0})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Z}^{\mathrm{rr}},n}
. Therefore, the limiting acceptance set for rerandomization can be written as
\mathcal{E}=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{\mathrm{rr}}}:\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}^{*}}^{-1}\bm{x}<c\}

\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}^{*}}=(\pi_{1}\pi_{0})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Z}^{\mathrm{rr}}}
.

2.3
Three typical survival estimators and their properties under simple randomization

Under the stable treatment and value assumption (SUTVA), we have
T_{i}=A_{i}T_{i}(1)+(1-A_{i})T_{i}(0)

C_{i}=A_{i}C_{i}(1)+(1-A_{i})C_{i}(0)

Y_{i}(t)=\mathbbm{1}(X_{i}\geq t)

\mathcal{O}_{i}=(A_{i},X_{i},\Delta_{i},\bm{Z}_{i})
with
\mathcal{O}_{i}(a)=\{a,X_{i}(a),\Delta_{i}(a),\bm{Z}_{i}\}

and
\Delta_{i}(a)\equiv\mathbbm{1}\{T_{i}(a)\leq C_{i}(a)\}

Under completely independent censoring, Assumption
2
(i) and (iii), we can estimate

\displaystyle\widehat{\Lambda}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{km}}(t)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(A_{i}=a)\mathrm{d}N_{T,i}(s)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(A_{i}=a)Y_{i}(s)},\quad\widehat{S}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{km}}(t)=\Prodi_{[0,t]}\left\{1-\mathrm{d}\widehat{\Lambda}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{km}}(s)\right\},

\Sigma_{T,a}^{\mathrm{km}}(s,t)=\mathbb{E}\{\varphi_{T,a}^{\mathrm{km}}(s,\mathcal{O})\varphi_{T,a}^{\mathrm{km}}(t,\mathcal{O})\}

(
19

\displaystyle\widehat{\Lambda}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{ipcw}}(t)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{a}(s,\bm{Z}_{i})\mathrm{d}N_{T,i}(s)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{a}(s,\bm{Z}_{i})Y_{i}(s)},\quad\widehat{S}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{ipcw}}(t)=\Prodi_{[0,t]}\left\{1-\mathrm{d}\widehat{\Lambda}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{ipcw}}(s)\right\},

\widehat{S}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{ipcw}}(t)
is uniformly consistent, admits a Bahadur representation with influence function

\mathbb{G}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{ipcw}}(\cdot)

DML estimator.

3
Asymptotic survival analysis under rerandomization

3.1
KM and IPCW-KM estimators

We first introduce regularity conditions for establishing the uniform consistency of
\widehat{S}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{km}}(t)
and
\widehat{S}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{ipcw}}(t)
for
t\in[0,\tau]
under rerandomization.

(C1)

S_{T,a}(t)
and
S_{C,a}(t)
are càdlàg functions on
[0,\tau]
. For
\mathbb{P}
-almost all
\bm{z}\in\mathfrak{Z}
,
S_{T,a}(t|\bm{z})
and
S_{C,a}(t|\bm{z})
are càdlàg functions on
[0,\tau]
.

(C2)

Let
\widehat{S}_{C,a}(t|\bm{z})
be an estimator for
S_{C,a}(t|\bm{z})
. Under simple randomization, for
\mathbb{P}
-almost all
\bm{z}\in\mathfrak{Z}
,
\sup_{t\in[0,\tau]}|\widehat{S}_{C,a}(t|\bm{z})-S_{C,a}(t|\bm{z})|\overset{p}{\to}0
, and there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\inf_{t\in[0,\tau]}S_{C,a}(t|\bm{z})\geq\epsilon
and
\inf_{t\in[0,\tau]}\widehat{S}_{C,a}(t|\bm{z})\geq\epsilon/2
.

Condition (C1) states that the marginal survival functions and conditional survival functions for
\mathbb{P}
-almost all
\bm{z}\in\mathfrak{Z}
are càdlàg on
[0,\tau]
, which goes beyond the class of continuous survival functions for greater generality. Condition (C2) involves two components. First, it assumes the existence of a uniformly consistent estimator
\widehat{S}_{C,a}(t|\bm{z})
for the conditional censoring survival function
S_{C,a}(t|\bm{z})

3.2
Geometric illustration of the variance reduction property

We provide a geometric illustration of the orthogonal decomposition of the limiting processes of the KM estimator under rerandomization, as established in Theorem
2

rerandomization covariates
\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{rr}}=(Z_{i1},Z_{i2})^{\top}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbf{I}_{2})

with shape

and scale
\lambda_{i}(a)=\exp(-\eta_{i}/1.5)

C_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,15)
is drawn independently of the potential event times.

We set the longest follow-up time to be

, and

by computing the empirical influence functions

\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{L}\leq c
. The unrestricted, orthogonal residual process

is generated as a mean-zero Gaussian process parameterized by the residual covariance matrix extracted from the empirical influence functions. We simulate 250 independent trajectories, partitioning them into paths that satisfy the rerandomization constraint

Results in Section
3

D\in\bm{Z}
be a categorical (or discretized continuous) baseline covariate encoding the randomization strata with domain
\mathcal{D}=\{1,\ldots,\overline{\mathcal{D}}\}

\overline{\mathcal{D}}
is fixed with
\mathbb{P}(D=d)>0
for
d\in\mathcal{D}
. Assumption
1
implies that
D_{1},\ldots,D_{n}
are i.i.d. Following the design in
20

d
, a unit has probability
\pi_{a[d]}\in(0,1)
to be randomized to treatment
a
, with
\pi_{1[d]}+\pi_{0[d]}=1
for
d\in\mathcal{D}

The stratified rerandomization design combines stratified randomization and rerandomization as follows. First, generate
(A_{1}^{\star},\ldots,A_{n}^{\star})
by stratified randomization based on observed strata
(D_{1},\ldots,D_{n})
and parameter
\pi_{a[D_{i}]}\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\bm{I}_{n}^{\star}=\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\frac{n_{[d]}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(D_{i}=d)\left\{\frac{A_{i}^{\star}\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{rr}}}{n_{1[d]}^{\star}}-\frac{(1-A_{i}^{\star})\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{rr}}}{n_{0[d]}^{\star}}\right\},

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}}}(\bm{I}_{n}^{\star})=\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\frac{n_{[d]}}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n_{1[d]}^{\star}n_{0[d]}^{\star}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(D_{i}=d)\left(\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{rr}}-\overline{\bm{Z}}_{[d]}^{\mathrm{rr}}\right)\left(\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{rr}}-\overline{\bm{Z}}_{[d]}^{\mathrm{rr}}\right)^{\top},

\overline{\bm{Z}}_{[d]}^{\mathrm{rr}}=n_{[d]}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(D_{i}=d)\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{rr}}
. Lastly, if
\bm{I}_{n}^{\star\top}\{\widehat{{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}}}(\bm{I}_{n}^{\star})\}^{-1}\bm{I}_{n}^{\star}<c
for a pre-specified balance threshold
c
, set the final treatment
(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})
to
(A_{1}^{\star},\ldots,A_{n}^{\star})

\bm{Z}^{\mathrm{rr}}\setminus D
. Similar to
\bm{B}_{n}^{*}
in (

5
Numerical studies

5.1
Simulation design

We conduct simulation studies to illustrate our asymptotic results, considering three randomization procedures: simple randomization, rerandomization, and stratified rerandomization. We generate the covariate vector
\bm{Z}_{i}=(Z_{i1},Z_{i2},D_{i})^{\top}
, where
Z_{i1}\sim\mathcal{N}(1,1)
,
D_{i}|Z_{i1}\sim\mathcal{B}(p_{i})
, a Bernoulli random variable with
p_{i}\equiv\mathbb{P}(D_{i}=1|Z_{i1})=0.4+0.2\mathbbm{1}(X_{i1}<1)
, and
Z_{i2}|Z_{i1},D_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. The rerandomization covariates
\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{rr}}=(Z_{i1},Z_{i2})^{\top}
. The treatment assignment
A_{i}\in\{0,1\}
is generated with
\pi_{1}=\pi_{0}=0.5
under three designs: (i) the simple randomization: draw
A_{i}\sim\mathcal{B}(0.5)
independently; (ii) rerandomization: draw
A_{i}^{*}\sim\mathcal{B}(0.5)
, compute the Mahalanobis distance
\bm{I}_{n}^{\ast\top}\{\widehat{{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}}}(\bm{I}_{n}^{*})\}^{-1}\bm{I}_{n}^{*}
based on
\bm{Z}^{\mathrm{rr}}
, and accept the allocation if
\bm{I}_{n}^{\ast\top}\{\widehat{{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}}}(\bm{I}_{n}^{*})\}^{-1}\bm{I}_{n}^{*}<1.83
(the approximate 40th percentile of a
\chi^{2}_{2}
distribution, yielding a 40% acceptance rate); and (iii) stratified rerandomization: draw
A_{i}^{\star}\sim\mathcal{B}(0.5)
within the strata defined by
D_{i}
, compute the stratified Mahalanobis distance
\bm{I}_{n}^{\star\top}\{\widehat{{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}}}(\bm{I}_{n}^{\star})\}^{-1}\bm{I}_{n}^{\star}
on
\bm{Z}^{\mathrm{rr}}
, and accept if
\bm{I}_{n}^{\star\top}\{\widehat{{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}}}(\bm{I}_{n}^{\star})\}^{-1}\bm{I}_{n}^{\star}<1.83
.

For outcome and censoring time generation, we consider two scenarios: in scenario I (proportional hazards with covariate-dependent censoring), the outcome time
T_{i}(a)\sim\mathrm{Weibull}\{1.5,\lambda_{i}(a)^{-1/1.5}\}
, where
\lambda_{i}(a)=0.1\exp(-0.5a+0.5Z_{i1}-0.5Z_{i2}+0.5D_{i}+0.5aZ_{i1}-0.5aZ_{i2})
for
a=0,1
, and the censoring time
C_{i}\sim\text{Exp}\{0.05\exp(0.2Z_{i1}+0.2D_{i})\}
; in scenario II (accelerated failure time with completely independent censoring), the outcome time
\log T_{i}(a)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i}(a),\sigma^{2})
, where
\mu_{i}(a)=-1-0.5a+Z_{i1}-Z_{i2}+D_{i}
for
a=0,1
, and the censoring time
C_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,20)
. In both scenarios, we set
\tau=5
. The parameters described above are calibrated such that the resulting mean observed event rate is approximately 50% in Scenario I and 70% in Scenario II, while the proportion of censoring prior to
\tau=5
lies between 20% and 30% in both scenarios. The parameters of interest are
S_{T,1}(t)=\mathbb{P}\{T(1)>t^{*}\}
at
t^{*}=1,2,3,4
, where the true values are approximated via Monte Carlo integration over a simulated super-population of
n=10^{6}
units using the true data-generating process parameters. In Scenario I, we consider the IPCW-KM and DML estimators due to covariate-dependent censoring. In Scenario II, we consider the KM and DML estimators, as the censoring is completely independent. For the IPCW-KM estimator, we fit a Cox PH model for the censoring time, and for the DML estimator, we adopt a five-fold cross-fitting scheme given in Section
2.3
by setting
K=5
. The implementation of the DML estimator is conducted via the
R
package
survSuperLearner
. We execute this for sample sizes
n=100
and
400
with 1000 replicates.

5.2
Pointwise and uniform inference

The pointwise and uniform inference procedures follow
23
, and a detailed methodological discussion of variance estimation is not particularly interesting, given its similarity to prior work. We thus provide descriptions of the procedures for obtaining pointwise CIs and the uniform CBs.

To obtain pointwise CIs, for
{\mathrm{est}}={\mathrm{km}},{\mathrm{ipcw}}
, define
\sigma_{T,a}^{{\mathrm{est}},2}(s,t)\equiv\Sigma_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(s,t)
under simple randomization,
\sigma_{T,a}^{{\mathrm{est}},2}(s,t)\equiv\Sigma_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(s,t)-\{1-\kappa(c)\}\bm{\Sigma}_{T,\bm{B}^{*},a}^{\mathrm{est}}(s)^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}^{*}}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{T,\bm{B}^{*},a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)
under rerandomization, and
\sigma_{T,a}^{{\mathrm{est}},2}(s,t)\equiv\Sigma_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(s,t)-\{1-\kappa(c)\}\bm{\Sigma}_{T,\bm{B}^{\star},a}^{\mathrm{est}}(s)^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}^{\star}}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{T,\bm{B}^{\star},a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)
under stratified rerandomization. Then, a natural estimator of
\sigma_{T,a}^{{\mathrm{est}},2}(s,t)
is
\widehat{\sigma}_{T,a}^{{\mathrm{est}},2}(s,t)\equiv\widehat{\Sigma}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(s,t)
for the simple randomization design,
\widehat{\sigma}_{T,a}^{{\mathrm{est}},2}(s,t)\equiv\widehat{\Sigma}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(s,t)-\{1-\kappa(c)\}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{T,\bm{B}^{*},a}^{\mathrm{est}}(s)^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{B}^{*}}^{-1}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{T,\bm{B}^{*},a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)
for the rerandomization design, and
\widehat{\sigma}_{T,a}^{{\mathrm{est}},2}(s,t)\equiv\widehat{\Sigma}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(s,t)-\{1-\kappa(c)\}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{T,\bm{B}^{\star},a}^{\mathrm{est}}(s)^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{B}^{\star}}^{-1}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{T,\bm{B}^{\star},a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)
for the stratified rerandomization design. For the DML estimator, define
\sigma_{T,a}^{{\mathrm{dml}},2}(s,t)\equiv\Sigma_{T,a}^{\mathrm{dml}}(s,t)
with
\widehat{\sigma}_{T,a}^{{\mathrm{dml}},2}(s,t)\equiv\widehat{\Sigma}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{dml}}(s,t)
, since neither rerandomization nor stratified rerandomization alters its covariance function. Note that
\widehat{\sigma}_{T,a}^{{\mathrm{dml}},2}(s,t)
is obtained following the same cross-fitting scheme given in Section
2.3
with
K=5
. The specific forms of
\widehat{\sigma}_{T,a}^{{\mathrm{est}},2}(s,t)
(
{\mathrm{est}}={\mathrm{km}},{\mathrm{ipcw}}
) under different designs are obtained by replacing the population expectations in the expressions with their empirical counterparts. For
\widehat{\sigma}_{T,a}^{{\mathrm{dml}},2}(s,t)
, we recycle the point estimates with the previous cross-fitted EIFs, i.e.,
\widehat{\sigma}_{T,a}^{{\mathrm{dml}},2}(s,t)=\mathbb{P}_{n,K}\{\widehat{\varphi}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{eff}}(s,\mathcal{O})\widehat{\varphi}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{eff}}(t,\mathcal{O})\}
, where
\widehat{\varphi}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{eff}}(t,\mathcal{O})=\widehat{\varphi}_{T,a}^{{\mathrm{eff}},\ast}(t,\mathcal{O})+\widehat{S}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{dml}}(t)
.

With
\widehat{\sigma}_{T,a}^{{\mathrm{est}},2}(t)\equiv\widehat{\sigma}_{T,a}^{{\mathrm{est}},2}(t,t)
, the pointwise Wald-type asymptotic
(1-\alpha)
-level CI for
S_{T,a}(t)
is
\widehat{S}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\cdot\widehat{\sigma}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)/\sqrt{n}
, where
z_{1-\alpha/2}
is the
(1-\alpha/2)
-quantile of the standard normal distribution. As suggested in
23
, constructing Wald-type intervals on the logistic probability scale can improve finite-sample coverage. Specifically, define
\mathrm{expit}(x)\equiv\exp(x)/\{1+\exp(x)\}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathrm{logit}(u)\equiv\log(u)-\log(1-u)
for
u\in(0,1)
, with
\widetilde{\sigma}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)\equiv\widehat{\sigma}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)/[\widehat{S}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)\{1-\widehat{S}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)\}]
. Then, the transformed Wald-type interval
[l_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t),u_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)]\equiv\mathrm{expit}[\mathrm{logit}\{\widehat{S}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)\}\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\cdot\widetilde{\sigma}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)/\sqrt{n}]
. If
\widehat{S}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)=0
, set
l_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)=0
and
u_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)=\min_{s}\{u_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(s):u_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(s)>0\}
, whereas if
\widehat{S}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)=1
, set
l_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)=\min_{s}\{l_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(s):l_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(s)>0\}
and
u_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)=1
.

For uniform inference, we construct fixed-width CBs as
\widehat{S}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)\pm\widehat{c}_{a,\alpha}^{\mathrm{est}}/\sqrt{n}
, where
\widehat{c}_{a,\alpha}^{\mathrm{est}}
is any consistent estimator of the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the supremum of the absolute value of the limiting mean-zero Gaussian process to which
\{\sqrt{n}\{\widehat{S}_{T,a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)-S_{T,a}(t)\}:t\in\mathcal{T}\}
weakly converges, for a restricted compact and continuous time domain
\mathcal{T}\subset[0,\tau]
. To obtain
\widehat{c}_{a,\alpha}^{\mathrm{est}}
, we simulate sample paths of a mean-zero Gaussian process with covariance function
(s,t)\mapsto\widehat{\sigma}_{T,a}^{{\mathrm{est}},2}(s,t)
, and then set
\widehat{c}_{a,\alpha}^{\mathrm{est}}
as the sample
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the uniform norm over
\mathcal{T}
of these sample paths. Specifically, in our simulations, we evaluate simultaneous coverage over
\mathcal{T}=[1,4]
, obtaining the uniform CBs using 50 equally spaced time points over this interval. The monotonicity of the CBs is ensured via isotonic regression
(
24
)
.

5.3
Simulation results

Simulation results are reported in Tables
1
and
2
for settings with sample size
n=100
and in Web Tables 1 and 2 in Section S7 of the Supplementary Materials for sample size
n=400
. All estimators are asymptotically consistent, and the empirical coverage probabilities of the pointwise 95% CIs attain their nominal levels. The uniform 95% CBs are conservative across the considered scenarios. These findings provide empirical corroboration for Theorems
1
,
3
,
5
, and
7
.

In Scenario I, under covariate-dependent censoring, the IPCW estimator yields larger empirical standard errors (ESEs) under simple randomization than under rerandomization and stratified rerandomization, demonstrating that rerandomization and stratified rerandomization reduce the uncertainty of the IPCW-KM estimator. In Scenario II, under completely independent censoring, the KM estimator exhibits the same pattern, indicating that the variance-reduction properties of rerandomization and stratified rerandomization persist in this setting. These findings provide empirical confirmation of Theorems
2
and
6
. The variance reduction effect is more pronounced in scenario II because of the stronger effect of
\bm{Z}
and
D
in the data-generating process.

In comparison, under both scenarios, the DML estimators exhibit comparable ESEs under all three randomization designs, corroborating Theorems
4
and
8
, which state that rerandomization and stratified rerandomization do not reduce the variance of the DML estimator due to the Neyman orthogonality of the EIF. Interestingly, the DML estimator is empirically more efficient than the IPCW-KM or the KM estimator under rerandomization and stratified rerandomization, yielding smaller ESEs across the considered scenarios. This indicates that, in nonlinear regression settings, covariate adjustment at the analysis stage can remain critically important and can dominate the efficiency gains attributable to design-based adjustment via rerandomization.

We additionally illustrate the theoretical developments using a real data example based on data from a randomized clinical trial evaluating hormonal therapy and chemotherapy duration in patients with node-positive breast cancer. The trial was conducted by the German Breast Cancer Study Group
(

6
Discussion

The uniform limiting processes established in Theorems
2
and
6
reveal a fundamental geometric connection between design-stage constraints and analysis-stage efficiency. The variance reduction achieved by rerandomization is mathematically equivalent to clamping the stochastic variation of the estimator’s projection onto the restricted covariate subspace. For the KM and IPCW-KM estimators, this geometric clamping deflates the uniform CBs. However, as demonstrated in Theorems
4
and
8
, this geometric advantage is nullified when utilizing the DML estimator. Because the EIF is Neyman-orthogonal to the baseline covariates, its projection onto the rerandomization subspace is asymptotically zero. Consequently, when the analysis-stage estimator achieves the semiparametric efficiency bound, restricting the design space offers no further asymptotic variance reduction.

In practice, however, the KM estimator remains the most conventional and widely reported choice for survival analysis in clinical trials, and this operational boundary carries an important interpretive caveat. When rerandomization or any other form of constrained randomization has been employed, interval estimates derived from the unadjusted KM estimator (that is, without incorporating the rerandomization correction to the variance) will be conservative, in the sense that the resulting CBs are wider than asymptotically necessary. Investigators reporting such estimates should therefore acknowledge that the coverage is achieved with excess interval width, and that the corrected variance estimator derived from our limiting theory should be used to obtain sharper inference. To facilitate adoption, we provide
R
code implementing the rerandomization-corrected variance estimator for both the KM and IPCW-KM estimators, and the DML estimator at
https://github.com/erxc/Surv_rerand
.

The simulation studies underscore the finite-sample reality of these geometric bounds. The magnitude of the variance reduction is bounded by the prognostic power of the rerandomization covariates. In settings where the covariates exhibit weaker correlation with the survival outcome, the geometric deflation factor
\rho_{a}^{\mathrm{est}}(t)
approaches zero. While the exactness of the limiting processes holds regardless of the covariate signal, substantive reductions in the CB “spindle” require the pre-specification of highly prognostic baseline metrics. Several extensions to our current results warrant further investigation. First, our theory development assumes a fixed number of rerandomization covariates,
p^{\mathrm{rr}}
. Extending this geometry to high-dimensional regimes where
p^{\mathrm{rr}}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty

(
21
)
would require adapting the Mahalanobis distance constraint and establishing uniform Donsker conditions under diverging dimensions. Second, our results focus on standard rerandomization with a fixed set of strata. Extending the current asymptotic framework to finely stratified rerandomization designs
(
1
;
3
)
would require establishing uniform weak convergence of survival function estimators under designs where the number of strata grows with sample size, presenting an additional technical challenge from the finite-dimensional M-estimation setting considered in prior works. This will be pursued in future research.
