Competition for Survival and the Maximum Entropy Production Principle in Self-Organized Silver Particle Chains Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Albert Han, Jiri Kataman-Kustwan, Alexey Bezryadin

Abstract
The maximum entropy production (MEP) principle is a hypothetical law of physics which dictates that complex systems, far from equilibrium, evolve into an ordered dissipative structure (DS) which generates as much entropy per second as possible, under given constraints. The MEP is a promising principle in non-equilibrium physics, but more precise, quantitative experimental tests are needed for the MEP principle to become universally accepted. An important problem is whether the natural competition for resources, well known in biology, limits the ability of dissipative structures (DS) to achieve the maximum of the entropy production rate (EPR). Here, we investigate this competition between DS experimentally, by performing high precision electrical measurements on suspensions of silver particles under strong electric fields. To establish the hypothetical impact of competition between the DS on the MEP effect, precise electrical measurements of the Joule heating effect are performed on two Ag suspension samples connected in parallel, exposing them to the same voltage. The samples are able to self-organize, dissipate energy, generate entropy, and they compete with each other for resources, i.e., electrical current, which is limited by a series resistor. We find that usually only one of the two samples is able to develop a dissipative structure, i.e., a conducting chain of Ag particles. Our findings are as follows. (1) There is a competition between the two samples, which prevents both systems from achieving their maximum possible contribution to the global entropy production rate. (2) Due to this competition, we find that only one out of two samples can self-organize, while the other deteriorates and approaches zero EPR. (3) The global EPR, i.e., the entropy produced by the samples and the energy supply circuit, is also reduced from its possible maximum due to the competition between the sub-systems. Based on these observations, we propose that the competition effect constitutes an essential constraint that must be incorporated into formulations of the MEP. This principle parallels real-world phenomena, reflecting the competition for resources observed among species and individual organisms in natural systems. We also examine the global implications of the MEP and propose that it serves as a driving mechanism propelling the hypothetical ascent of civilizations along the Kardashev scale.



I
Introduction

The notion of competition in biology is understood as an interaction between organisms or species in which they require resources, the supply of which is limited. The functioning, i.e., the entropy production, of one of the organisms reduces the amount of the resource available to the other organism. Thus, "successful" organisms cause a strong entropy production in the environment, while the "unsuccessful" ones might go extinct or have their contribution to entropy production minimized.

The thermodynamics of equilibrium systems is well understood and has a governing extremum principle, namely, the second law of thermodynamics. However, the thermodynamics of systems far from equilibrium remains poorly understood, and no universally accepted extremum principle exists for such systems. Several proposals have been put forward, typically invoking the maximization of power dissipation and, consequently, entropy production

Given the usefulness of the second law of thermodynamics and other extremum principles in physics, it is natural to look for an analogous principle that governs systems far from equilibrium, as many phenomena of great interest take place far from equilibrium. One can argue, for example, that life itself is a non-equilibrium phenomenon
33
. The key idea discussed over the last few decades is that systems which can self-organize do so in such a way as to generate as much heat and entropy as possible, given the constraints. Such maximum entropy production (MEP) principles have been applied in many different fields, including climate science, biology, and physics, and has shown much promise.

Examples are found in the Earth’s climate, specific biological systems, selforganized electrorheological fluids

One notable application of the MEP includes the calculations of Paltridge, who showed that maximizing the entropy production in Earth’s atmosphere produces temperature distributions that agreed surprisingly well with the measured values.

Similar calculations have been done for the atmospheres of other planets like Mars and Saturn’s moon Titan

4

11

The MEP principle, like any foundational concept in physics, must be validated by in a series of reproducible, precise experimental measurements—an area that remains underdeveloped. The lack of high-resolution quantitative measurements of the entropy production in non-equilibrium systems capable of self-assembly has hindered empirical support for the MEP principle, leaving it somewhat philosophical. In the previous experiments mentioned above and in many natural processes, the system is too complex to calculate entropy production analytically or to measure EPR with high accuracy. The absence of accurate entropy production measurements has limited its acceptance within the broader scientific community. Our research aims to address this gap by introducing a model system that exhibits compelling self-assembly behavior and enables highly precise quantification of the heat generated and the entropy produced per second.

In this paper, we focus on the competition concept. Competition in biology is understood as an interaction between organisms or species in which both require resources, the supply of which is limited. Examples of such supplies include food, water, territory, and sunlight
3
. According to Lang and Benbow, competition reduces the fitness of the organisms involved. This is because the functioning of one organism reduces the amount of the resource available to the other.
15

Based on the hints provided by biological organisms, we hypothesize that "successful" subsystems generate more entropy within themselves and drive strong entropy production in the environment, while "unsuccessful" subsystems might become extinct or have their contribution to the entropy production rate greatly reduced. This factor may reduce the global entropy production rate for the entire population of all organisms or subsystems.

Here, we perform precise electrical measurements of the entropy production rate by dissipative structures formed out of silver (Ag) nanowires (and/or silver flakes) suspended in isopropanol and subjected to a strong electric field bias. Non-equilibrium conditions are imposed by placing two metallic electrodes in the suspension and biasing them with a high voltage. The voltage and the current are precisely measured during the experiments. The main goal is to clarify the impact of the competition effect on the global entropy production rate of the entire system. Therefore, the setup involves two self-assembly subsystems arranged in parallel electrical connection within the circuit. From our precise measurements of the current and the voltage, the Joule heating is calculated. The entropy production rate is evaluated as the heat released by the Joule heating divided by the temperature. Similar experiments were done previously using carbon nanotubes and a nonpolar solvent
5
;
4
;
6
, but there was only one DS in the previous experiments, and so the competition effect has not been investigated until now.

In the present experiments, we observe that Ag nanowire/Ag flake suspensions evolve into an electrically conducting dissipative structure, thus confirming previous observations
5
;
4
;
6
. During the evolution of the DS, a chain involving many Ag nanowires/flakes connected electrically is formed. Thus, the heat generated by the system increases with power, and therefore, the EPR increases in time. Initially, most of the entropy is generated by the DS as its electrical resistance is higher than the internal resistance,
R_{series}
, of the voltage supply circuitry. As the evolution progresses, most of the entropy production shifts into the energy supply circuitry, which is modeled by a fixed resistor (
R_{series}
) connected in series with the voltage source.

The competition effect is the main result reported here. To test it, we make a system that contains two competing sub-systems, namely two containers with self-assembly suspension, each equipped with a pair of electrodes. These two samples are connected in parallel. Systematic experiments show that only one of the samples achieves the maximum contribution to the global entropy production rate. The other sample drops to the minimum EPR. Thus, we propose a modification to the MEP principle. Our results demonstrate that competition for resources makes it impossible for all subsystems to succeed and contribute their potential maximum EPR to the global EPR. Also, we conclude that the global EPR is not maximized due to the competition. This last conclusion means that if all subsystems were successful and achieved their maximum EPR contribution, then the global EPR would be higher.

The setup is similar to previous experiments

To prepare each sample, approximately 10 ml of solution was placed into a glass vial equipped with two stainless steel electrodes,

Such imitations mimic real-life situations in which resources are limited and the EPR cannot be set to infinity.

Before and between experiments, Ag Nanowires were suspended in isopropanol by shaking the container until the solution was visibly homogeneous. The Ag flake samples were sonicated until visibly homogeneous. We have noticed that ultrasound sonication can break nanowires and change the samples qualitatively. Thus ultra-sonication was not used to re-agitate samples with Ag nanowires. The concentrations tested were chosen to ensure that significant self-assembly was possible, i.e, a dissipative structure (DS) could form between the electrodes, but the initial concentration was low enough that the DS significantly increased the entropy production. This environment mimics biological systems, where organic materials are not overabundant.

First, measurements were performed on single samples to verify that these Ag nanoparticles (Ag nanowires and/or Ag flakes) were able to self-assemble under the present conditions, similar to previously observed self-assembly in single-wall carbon nanotube suspensions

;
4
;
6

In Fig.

, the ammeter "A" represents the same device, the Keithley 6517B, which can measure current with high precision, down to the level of a few fA (
10^{-15}
A). In most experiments, self-assembly was observed and the entropy production increased drastically. The DS is an electrically conducting chain of silver particles (nanowires or flakes), schematically shown in Fig.
1
as a chain-like structure formed by red rectangles. The DS (silver chain) forms between the electrodes (blue) due to the effect of the applied voltage and the corresponding electric field, which polarizes the nanowires (red).

The DS (the chain) is electrically conducting, so that its formation drastically decreases the resistance between the electrodes (blue). Formation of DS strongly decreases the electrical resistance between the electrodes. It thus increases the Joule heating power
P_{global}=V_{source}I
in the circuit, and, correspondingly increases the total (global) entropy production rate (EPR),
dS_{global}/dt=P_{global}/T
. Here
I
is the current in the circuit,
V_{source}
is the voltage provided by the voltage source,
T
is the temperature of the environment, and
t
is the time. The global entropy production is the sum of the entropy generated by the sample and by the energy supply circuit, i.e., the series resistor
R_{series}
. The experiments are conducted at room temperature, so
T=295
K. Therefore the total entropy production is

dS_{global}/dt=IV_{source}/T

(1)

The total Joule heating and the total entropy production rate of the circuit is directly proportional to the current, since the source voltage is fixed. Therefore, the maximum entropy production state occurs when
I
is largest, or the total resistance of the circuit is smallest. The absolute maximum of the total dissipated power,
P_{max,global}=V^{2}_{source}/R_{series}
, and the corresponding entropy production rate is

(dS_{global}/dt)_{max}=(V^{2}_{source}/R_{series})(1/T_{room})

(2)

Note that this EPR cannot be achieved since it would require the resistance of the DS (Ag chain) to become zero, which is not realistic. However, the sample can always evolve closer and closer to this maximum since more Ag particles can join the chain and make it more and more conducting over time. Such process is frequently observed in the experiments.

The power dissipated within the sample only is
P_{sample}=IV_{sample}
. This is done by setting a fixed source voltage (
V_{source}
) and by measuring the current in the circuit (
I
). The voltage drop on the resistor is calculated using Ohm’s law, since the series resistor is know precisely.

The entropy production rate (EPR) by the sample itself is
dS_{sample}/dt=P_{sample}/T
. Interestingly, the entropy production rate within the sample (
dS_{sample}/dt
) can (and frequently does) achieve its theoretical maximum. This happens under the resistance matching condition, namely when the resistance of the dissipative structure (
R_{sample}
) equals the resistance of the series resistor, i.e., when
R_{sample}=R_{series}
, in which case
V_{sample}=V_{source}/2
. Note that
V_{sample}+V_{resistor}=V_{source}
, where
V_{resistor}=IR_{series}
is the voltage drop on the resistor. So, the maximum power which can be dissipated within the sample is
P_{max,sample}=(V_{source}/2R_{series})(V_{source}/2)
. The corresponding maximum value of the EPR within the sample is

(dS_{sample}/dt)_{max}=(V_{source}^{2}/4R_{series})(1/T)

(3)

In the the general case, the formula for the entropy production by the sample equals the Joule power dissipated by the sample divided by the temperature of the environment

dS_{sample}/dt=R_{sample}[V_{source}/(R_{series}+R_{sample})]^{2}/T

(4)

This is a non-monotonic function with a maximum at the resistance matching point,
R_{series}=R_{sample}
. Based on this, we can distinguish two stages of the evolution. In the first stage
R_{sample}>R_{series}
and in the second stage
R_{sample}<R_{series}
. The direction of the evolution is predominantly to decrease
R_{sample}
. In the first stage the power dissipated by the same increases with time (as long as
R_{sample}
goes down). In the second stage of the evolution, when
R_{sample}<R_{series}
, the power dissipated in the sample decreases and the power dissipated in the resistor increases, so the total dissipated power increases as well.

Note again that, to simplify the discussion, we assume the sample’s temperature equals the room temperature. This is justified because the currents flowing through the sample are usually very small, of the order of
\mu A
, which corresponds to a low level of heating in general.

This circuit is used to test the competition effect between the two DS, which can potentially form in each sample. The samples are prepared as was described above. One of the electrodes of each sample is linked to the series resistor, while the other electrode is connected to ground through a much smaller (10k

.

By connecting our samples in parallel, we are able to quantitatively study the impact of the competition between the two potential dissipative structures on the MEP principle. The samples are set to compete for the limited resources (the voltage and the electrical current). The competition occurs since as one sample self-assembles and increases its electrical conductance, the voltage on both samples drops, simply because they are connected in parallel and therefore they have the same voltage applied to them. In such a scenario, the second sample, which is a little slower in its evolution, might not be able to self-assemble at all, as the actual measurements confirmed.

III.1
Single-Sample Experiments

Single-sample measurements on Ag nanowires and Ag flakes suspensions were performed to verify their self-assembly abilities and to serve as a baseline for the following two-sample measurements. An example image of self-assembled dissipative structure (composed of Ag nanowires), namely a bundle of nanowires, is shown in Fig.
3
. The source voltage was
V_{source}=18
V and the series resistor was
R_{series}=15.9
k
\Omega

4

P_{max,sample}=(0.5V_{source})(V_{source}/2R_{series})
, which occurs if the resistance matching condition is satisfied. The evolution progressed to the point when the resistance matching condition is satisfied. Indeed, at the beginning of the evolution the normalized power is of the order of 0.01 while it is close to 1 at the end of the evolution time.

We have performed many such tests and observed that Ag nanowires suspended in isopropanol are able to self-assemble under voltage bias between
9V
and
30V
while the series resistors tested were in the range between
10k\Omega
and
500k\Omega
. However, not every sample tested in this range was able to successfully self assemble. The competing process is the precipitation of the suspended nanowires on the bottom of the container. It was observed that higher voltages enable a faster evolution time and thus a higher probability of the DS formation, which has to happen before most of the Ag nanowires precipitated.

The concentrations tested were in the range between
0.025
and
1.5\frac{mg}{ml}
. With such concentration the initial resistance of the suspension is much higher than the series resistor. If a sufficient voltage is applied,
V_{source}>9V
, the suspension exhibits self-assembly and the resistance drops by a few orders of magnitude, this drastically increasing the entropy production rate (EPR).

The maximum EPR cannot be achieved instantaneously. Thus, the maximum entropy production principle has to be formulated accordingly. It takes a certain time for the dissipative structure to develop and become efficient entropy producer
6
. We will call this time the evolution time,
t_{e}
. We define the "evolution time",
t_{e}
, as the time interval measured from the moment when the voltage is applied to the moment when
R_{sample}=R_{series}
for the first time. Equivalently the same condition can be formulated as the time when
P_{sample}=P_{max,sample}
for the first time. In other words,
t_{e}
is the time when the first stage of the evolution ends and the second stage begins. For example, in Fig.
4
the evolution time was about 1000 seconds.

Another possible definition for the evolution time is the time at which the sample resistance exhibits a sharp decrease, or the time where
\frac{dR_{sample}}{dt}
is at its minimum, while its absolute value is at its maximum. We label this time
t_{e}^{{}^{\prime}}
. As seen in Fig.
8
, this metric behaves very similarly to
t_{e}
. This metric was used as some samples were not able to achieve the matching condition, but still exhibited self assembly.

It turns out that combined exponential and activation functions can also be used to approximate the evolution time. First, we observe that a simple exponential dependence of the form
t_{e}\sim exp(-\alpha V_{source})
does not make sense since at zero voltage it predicts a finite evolution time while one naturally expects an infinite evolution time if the driving force (
V_{source}
) is zero. Therefore we propose a function of the form
t_{e}\sim\exp(-\beta V_{source})\exp(\gamma/V_{source})
, which combines an exponential high rate (
\exp(\beta V_{source})
) of the occurrence of DS at high voltages and the activation behavior at low voltages. The activation law means that the DS nucleation rate is very low (
\exp(-\gamma/V_{sourc})
) at low driving force. Note that the rate of occurrence of DS is inversely proportional to the evolution time. Qualitatively, this makes sense since at high driving force the evolution happens very quickly while at low driving force (source voltage) the evolution time is extremely long, which is reflected by the exponential activation factor. The activation factor suggests that to form a dissipative structure the system has to overcome a certain free energy barrier. A comparison of this combined activation law and exponential decay formula to the data is shown in Fig.

The Ag flakes have a lower resistance on average and thus they can produce structures approaching the global maximum of EPR. A self-assembly of Ag flakes into a DS structure was observed, similar to Ag nanowires. An example of the self assembly is shown in Fig.

dS_{global}/dt\approx(dS_{global}/dt)_{max}

The phase transition from almost no entropy production to a high level of entropy production happens extremely fast for both Ag Nanowire and Ag flake samples. For Ag Flakes, this dynamic phase transition has been investigated using a fast voltage digitizer (picoscope) and the results are shown in Fig.

Ag nanowires and Ag flakes showed similar types of behavior under the voltage bias. However, using similar concentrations and series resistors, Ag flakes required a higher voltage to self-assemble. Voltages between
40-300V
were required to observe a self-assembly and an EPR increase, while lower voltages showed no self-assembly. Samples were measured under the voltage bias until all Ag nanoparticles precipitated, after which no self-assembly was possible. This suggests that the lack of self-assembly was not due to the time the experiment was run. A reasonable explanation for this difference is the increased size of Ag flakes. Ag flakes used had
\sim 20\mu m
diameter, while Ag nanowires used had
\sim 100nm
diameter and
\sim 150\mu m
length. A rough estimate suggests that Ag flakes would weigh about
\sim 10^{-8}g
per particle, while Ag nanowires would weigh about
\sim 10^{-11}g
per particle. The gravity effect is the factor which competes with self-assembly and it makes it less probable for the heavier Ag flakes to form a DS. A conclusion from this is that the EPR maximization principle might be valid only of the driving force is sufficiently strong to overcome competing destructive effects, such as thermal fluctuations and gravity in our model systems.

This dissipated energy is very low if
R_{sample}\gg R_{series}
. This is the case at the beginning of the first stage of the evolution when there is no DS and all metallic particles are disorganized. also, the dissipated energy approaches zero as
R_{sample}\ll R_{series}
. This is the case at the end of the second stage of the evolution, if the evolution was successful and Ag particles were able to organize into a chain with high electrical conductivity. In the second stage of the evolution, i.e., when
R_{sample}<R_{series}
, the EPR increases globally, while the EPR generated by the DS decreases as the structure becomes more and more organized and its resistance drops.

The global EPR,
dS_{global}/dT=(1/T)IV_{source}
, is proportional to the current since the voltage of the voltage source is fixed. Typically we find that throughout both evolution stages the resistance decreases, the current increases and therefore
dS_{global}/dt
increases. If the evolution is fulfilled completely then at the end of the second evolution stage the sample resistance is negligible compared to the series resistance. Thus the global entropy production approaches its absolute maximum, which is give in Eq.

.
This absolute maximum is never achieved exactly since its realization requires zero resistance of the DS. (Zero resistance of the dissipative structure might be possible if the particles are superconducting but this is obviously not the case in the presented experiments.)

III.2
Two-Sample Competition Experiments

The main focus of this paper is the demonstration of the competition effect in artificial complex systems with the ability of self-organization. The competition effect is observed if two samples are connected in parallel. The physical implication of the parallel connection is that both samples are always biased with the same voltage. Moreover, if one of the samples is able to form a DS, its resistance drops, as discussed above, and so the voltage on both samples decreases drastically, just because the samples are connected in parallel. The corresponding circuit diagram for the two-sample experiments is shown in Fig.
2
.
This electrical circuit also proved a model system in which resources are limited. Such a limit is achieved by including a resistor in series with the voltage source. Therefore the electrical current, which is the "resource" for which the samples "compete" is limited. The largest possible current is the same as with one sample, namely
I_{max,total}=V_{source}/R_{series}
. Thus, the entropy produced by the entire system is limited by the same maximum value as in the case with one sample, given in Eq.
2
.

An typical example of such two-sample experiment (referred to as "Experiment 1") is presented in Fig.
11
, where two samples (connected in parallel), containing Ag flakes (concentration of 2.87mg/ml), were measured in series with a resistor
R_{series}=500k\Omega
while the source voltage was
V_{source}=80V
. The electrical current in each sample is plotted versus time in Fig.
11
. At time
t\approx 19
s a sharp current increase is observed in sample "a" (red curve). At the exact same moment the current through the sample "b" dropped almost to zero (blue curve) and remained at zero level permanently.

In this example (Experiment 1; Fig.
11
) the sample "a" (red curve) behaves as a "competitive dominant" since it is able to "out-compete" the other sample. In this model experiment the samples "compete" for the electrical current which is limited by the maximum possible value of
I_{max,total}=V_{source}/R_{series}
. The sample which gets more electrical current generates more entropy, either within itself or it causes more entropy generation in the series resistor. This is in agreement with the competitive exclusion principle, primarily known in ecology. It sates that if two species compete for the same limiting resource, the species that uses the resource more efficiently will eventually exclude the less efficient competitor from that niche. In the example considered the sample "a" is able to increase its electrical conductance faster than sample "b". Because of this the voltage on both samples drops. Yet, sample "a" is able to maintain its level of self-organization while sample "b" is not able to form a dissipative structure. This is because, as sample "a" achieves a high conductivity (at
t\approx 19
s), the voltage becomes too low for the other sample (sample "b") to self-organize into a DS. As a result, sample "a" (the red curve is Fig.
11
) gets almost all of the available electrical current and wins the competition to generate more entropy. This explanation implicitly assumes that a larger driving force (i.e., sample voltage) is needed to self-assemble the dissipative structure than to maintain it in a functional stable state. This appears to be the case based on the reported experiments.

The photographs of the samples corresponding to the Experiment 1, discussed above, are shown in Fig.

The sample "b" is on the right; it does not show any structure. This observation confirms that among the two competing sample only one sample can "survive" (Fig.
10

10
b).

This dissipative structure competition phenomenon has been confirmed by performing experiments at various voltages and concentrations, as shown in Fig.
12
. We observe, consistently, that if one sample forms a dissipative structure, the current in the second sample drops and it looses the chance to form a dissipative structure, for the same reason, i.e., the reduction of the driving force (the sample voltage). Only at very high voltages and concentrations can both samples form sustained dissipative structures (the case of almost unlimited resources), as shown in Fig.
12
d.

At a lower probability, we sometimes also observe that the sample with the dissipative structure can swap during the self-assembly process, but only one dissipative structure was active at a time. An example of this can be seen in Fig.
13
, where the dissipative structure swaps a few times from the "blue" sample to the "red" sample and back. This switching behavior suggests that the two samples are in competition, as the evolution of the "red" sample dissipative structure prevents the "blue" sample from organizing into a dissipative structure and vice versa. In essence, the samples compete for the possibility to generate the maximum possible amount of entropy per unit time.

We this competition effect prevents the global entropy production from achieving its absolute maximum since if two dissipative structures would be active simultaneously they would be able to generate more entropy per unit time compared to the case when just one dissipative structure is active, assuming they have similar resistances. This is due to the fact that the global entropy production is inversely proportional to the circuit resistance, which would decrease is both samples self assemble. It is also true that the competition effect is only important if one of the dissipative structures achives a high level of efficiency, i.e., if it is able to reduce its resistance and make it much lower than the series resistor. In other words, the condition for the strong competition is
R_{sample,a}\ll R_{series}
or
R_{sample,b}\ll R_{series}
.

The MEP principle states that the entropy production rate should achieve its allowed maximum. However, we observe competitive behavior, where one sample inhibits the self-assembly of the other. Therefore, the competition effect might require a correction to the MEP maximum. Note that competition, in principle, can occur even within one sample, between the different segments of the sample. Thus we suggest that this competition phenomenon is quite ubiquitous.

Discussion

IV.1
Stages of evolution in single-sample experiments

Experiments show that as the system self-organizes it goes through multiple stages. Initially the metallic suspended particles (silver nanowire or micro-flakes) are disorganized and not connected to each other. Thus the conductivity of the fluid and the current passing through it is very low. Therefore, the entropy production rate is near zero. This is true for both
dS_{sample}/dt
and
dS_{global}/dt
. Thus, by design of our experiments, at the initial stages of evolution the entropy production rate (EPR) is always much smaller than the allowed absolute maximum.

Next, the system undergoes an evolution in which the conducting particles orient themselves along the applied electric field and form continuous and electrically conducting chains, as seen in Fig.
3
, Fig.
5
b, and Fig.
10
. Initially the chains are short but with time they form bundles which eventually link the electrodes and make the conductance of the sample much higher compared to the initial state. If the concentration of the metallic particles and their individual conductivities are sufficiently high, and the applied electric field is sufficiently strong to overcome destructive effects (e.g., gravity and thermal fluctuations), then the particles organize into a dissipative structure which eventually achieves the maximum possible entropy production rate (EPR) by the sample itself. In our case, this means that the Joule heat released by the Ag chain itself is at the maximum possible level.

A simple calculation shows that the maximum power dissipated in the sample is achieved when the sample resistance equals the series resistor,
R_{sample}=R_{series}
. Under this "impedance-matching" condition, the EPR of the sample itself is its its maximum, i.e.,
dS_{sample}/dt=(dS_{sample}/dt)_{max}
, as defined in Eq.
3
. This is the end of the first stage of the evolution, which occurs when the local EPR maximum is achieved, i.e., when the matching condition,
R_{sample}=R_{series}
, is realized. At the end of the first evolution stage the EPR of the sample is
(dS_{sample}/dT)_{max}=(P_{sample})_{max}/T
. This entropy is generated as the heat flows from the sample to the environment, assumed to have the temperature
T
.

To characterize the evolution time scale quantitatively we introduced the "evolution time",
t_{e}
, which was defined as the time needed for the dissipative structure to achieve the local EPR maximum, i.e. the time at which
R_{sample}=R_{series}
for the first time. (By our definition, see below, this point in time corresponds to the end of first evolution stage and the beginning of the second and final evolution stage.) Our numerous tests showed that such evolution time decays as function of the applied voltage, which is the driving force in these experiments. This decay and be modeled by both a powerlaw trend or an activation-law and exponential decay formula. The results are summarized in Fig.
8
and Fig.
9
.

There are in fact two evolution stages. In the first stage, when
R_{sample}>R_{series}
, the EPR increases both within the sample as well as globally. In the second stage, when
R_{sample}<R_{series}
, the global EPR continues to increase while the sample EPR decreases, because the voltage now drops mostly on the series resistor. As the system achieves the maximum EPR within the sample, the evolution does not stop at this point, although it might slow down since the voltage on the sample at this point is reduced by 50%, due to the voltage drop on the resistor. In general, the evolution continues as the system attempts to maximize the global entropy production rate. The global EPR includes both the heat generated by the sample and the heat generated by the energy supply circuit. An important fact is that after the local EPR maximum is achieved, and if the evolution continues further (i.e., the flux or the current allowed by the dissipative structure continues to increase), the global entropy production rate continues to increase while the local EPR begins to decreases. Thus the signature of the second evolution stage is that the local EPR decreases while the global EPR continues to increase. Thus, we observe a tendency, which we suspect is quite general, that advanced dissipative structures tend to shifts the heat generation from themselves to outer elements, i.e., to their energy supply system. In our case, at the second evolution stage, more heat is generated in the series resistor than in the self-assembled silver particle chain (see the derivation of Eq.
3
and Eq.
4
). Note that such two stages of evolution have been observed previously in Ref.
4
and it was also concluded that the first stage is characterized by a series of avalanches, while the second stage was much more stable.

In the second stage of evolution (
R_{sample}<R_{series}
and so
V_{sample}<V_{series}
), the global entropy production continues to grow to its absolute maximum, which is set by the energy supplie circuit (
V_{source}
and
R_{series}
) and is given in Eq.
2
. The global entropy production is maximized when
R_{sample}
is exactly zero, however, this condition is never realized in practice. Therefore, under favorable conditions, the systems evolves to the global EPR maximum but cannot achieve it exactly. Indeed, in the second (final) evolution stage we frequently observe
R_{sample}\ll R_{series}
(see Fig.
5
), provided that the suspension has enough conducting particles. The Joule heating power produced by the supply circuit is
P_{outside}=R_{series}I^{2}
and the heat power produced by the dissipative structure is
P_{inside}=R_{sample}I^{2}
. (The current,
I
, is the same in the sample and in the resistor.) All this heat goes to the environment at temperature
T\approx 295K
, so the entropy production of the sample is
S_{inside}/dt=R_{sample}I^{2}/T
and the EPR of the resistor is
S_{outside}/dt=R_{series}I^{2}/T
. At the end of the second evolution stage the EPR in the sample itself is much lower than the EPR the dissipative structure causes or induces in energy source.

The analogy between our model systems and biological organisms is noteworthy. At earlier stages of evolution, organisms primarily generate entropy within their own bodies. As evolution progresses, however, a larger fraction of entropy production shifts to external systems. Human civilization provides a clear example of this transition. With the development of technologies to harness chemical energy (e.g., fire and power plants), nuclear energy, and electrical energy (e.g., data centers), most entropy production associated with human activity now occurs outside the body, within engineered infrastructures and energy networks.

A similar pattern is observed in our experiments. As Ag particles evolve toward a highly conductive dissipative structure (DS) during the second stage of evolution, the effective point of entropy production (EPR) shifts to the external energy supply circuit, specifically the series resistor. In contrast, systems at an earlier evolutionary stage dissipate energy primarily internally.

By analogy, animals—representing an earlier stage—mainly dissipate energy through internal metabolic processes such as digestion. Humans, representing a more advanced stage, increasingly rely on external systems such as factories, power plants, and data centers, which dominate overall energy dissipation and entropy production. Consequently, the conversion of free energy (ultimately derived from the Sun) now occurs predominantly outside the human body, within industrial and technological systems. At present, the rate of entropy production in these external systems far exceeds that within human bodies.

IV.2
The competition effect in two-samples experiments

The main focus of this paper is on systems in which two samples are connected in parallel. In such configurations, Ag flake suspensions compete for a shared resource—namely, the free energy supplied by the voltage/current source—which is required for the formation of dissipative structures and the associated entropy production.

Initially, when both samples exhibit high resistance, the voltage across each is approximately equal to the source voltage,
V_{\text{source}}
. However, once one sample undergoes self-assembly and forms a dissipative structure with high electrical conductance, its voltage drops sharply. Because the samples are connected in parallel, the voltage across both must remain equal. Consequently, the voltage across the second sample decreases as well, suppressing its ability to form a dissipative structure.

As a result, the system exhibits a symmetry-breaking effect: the formation of a dissipative structure in one sample inhibits its formation in the other, such that only a single sample develops a dissipative structure under these conditions.

This is an example of a "winner takes all" scenario. This winner is the sample in which the dissipative structure evolves first. This sample takes the entire resource, which is the electric current, which is limited by the resistor. A strong voltage (electric field, to be more precise) is needed to form a continuous and conducting chain of Ag particles. The voltage polarizes the particles and then they, acting as electric dipoles, tend to form chains. The current, on the other hand, allows the chain to survive. Indeed, if a break develops, the current creates a high concentration of opposite charges around the break point, which produce a force to heal the break. Once a structure forms it needs the current to sustain its functionality. If the current is limited, only one dissipative structure can exist, at least in our experimental settings.

Another interesting question is the effect of such "winnertakes all" competition effect and the MEP principle. Assuming that the conductivity of each Ag chain has an upper limit,
G_{1,max}
, one concludes that if the competition effect would be absent and two chains are formed then the total conductivity would be twice larger, i.e.,
2G_{1,max}
. Then the total current would be higher and more entropy would be produced per second. In such simplified view the competition effect prevents the system from reaching the global entropy production rate. In the presence of such competition The local EPR is not maximized either. To be more precise, it is maximized on the winner sample, but it remains near zero in the sample which does not win the competition. This suggests that in larger systems that consist of multiple dissipative structures, the MEP may be acting as an additional constraint, that might reduce the entropy production.

The hypothesis that the competition effect can prevent the entire system from reaching the absolute global maximum of the entropy production is in agreement with our experiments. Typically if we measure just one sample then at the end of the second stage of the evolution is current is not significantly different from the maximum current
V_{source}/R_{series}
. For example,in Fig.
4
, the resistance of the sample is less than 1% in comparison to the series resistor. Therefore the current is less than the maximum current also by less than 1%. Yet, in the experiments with two competing samples the final current is roughly 3% to 5% less than the maximum possible current
V_{source}/R_{series}
. Indeed, in Fig.
12
, the series resistor was 500k
\Omega
, and the voltages were (Fig.
12
a): 100V, (Fig.
12
b): 180V, (Fig.
12
c): 300V, (Fig.
12
d): 120V. The corresponding maximum theoretical currents (corresponding to the global maximum EPR) are 0.2mA, 0.36mA, 0.6mA, 0.24mA. The experimentally achieved maximum currents are 0.19mA, 0.348mA, 0.58mA, 0.23mA. Thus it appears that the system containing two competing samples could not approach the
(dS_{global}/dt)_{max}
as closely as one-sample systems could.

Previously, theoretical models have been proposed
2
in which two types of dissipative structures compete for free energy. Now we report a similar behavior, although our system has only one type of dissipative structure (i.e., Ag particle chains). This competitive behavior resembles natural systems, as animals and plants must compete for free energy in terms of food and sunlight (for plants). Our experiments also show that the "winners" and the "loosers" are not set forever. For example in Fig.
13
the "blue" sample develops a highly conducting dissipative structure at
t=50

t=150

300V

IV.3
Constraints and the MEP Principle

The question of constraints in formulations of the MEP principle is somewhat difficult because of the possibility of circular reasoning. Whatever is the entropy production rate, one can always say that it is the possible maximum and that the constraints are just such that the observed EPR has to be considered as the maximum possible value. To avoid circular definitions, one needs an independent way to establish the constraints on the EPR in any given system. A related question is whether the maximum EPR is a constant or is it a function of time. We take the point of view that the EPR maximum,
(dS/dt)_{max}
, is a constant for a given system. Then, an immediate conclusion is that EPR is not always at the maximum possible level. There reason for this is that it takes a finite time for the dissipative structure to form and to achieve perfection. Therefore, at the beginning if evolution the entropy production is always lower than the maximum possible value allowed by the constraints. We find the evolution time increases if the driving force (voltage) is reduced (Fig.
8
) following a power-law dependence. Currently, there many current definitions for the MEP
21
;
23
;
7
;
9
but the evolution time is not typically considered as a constraint. Our current experiments and similar previous experiments
4
;
6
, show the maximum EPR cannot be achieved immediately, but can be reached through the system’s evolution. Our work suggests that the evolution time is accurately modeled by a power-law trend, similar to previous works
6
. We also observe that an activation-law and exponential-decay formula also accurately models the evolution time as a function of the source voltage.

We will now comment on promising formulations of the MEP in relation to our results. One promising formulation has been suggested recently: "In a local physico-chemical system with a thermodynamic condition far from equilibrium, a dynamic structure called dissipative structure (DS) would be stabilized in chemical or physical space, which produces maximum amount of entropy among the possible modes, and keeps itself in a low entropy state by disposing of the produced entropy out into the reservoir."
32
Our experiments suggest that this formulation should apply to the global entropy production, since not every sample forms the DS and, also, within each sample, not all locations are occupied by DS and thus the local EPR is not maximized at every segment of the sample. This matches biological organisms, where animals, which dissipate large amounts of entropy, are concentrated to certain locations, but usually are not evenly distributed. Thus, the entropy production density is probably not maximized for every point in space, and is very close to zero for most points, while the global EPR might be maximized since the number of animals might be controlled by the amount of food available globally. Additionally, the above definition uses a notion of a critical parameter. We find that there are indeed some critical parameters, namely the driving voltage and the concentration. If the voltage is too low the dissipative structure cannot form. Similarly, if the concentration of Ag particles is too low we don’t observe any dissipative structure. Also, the gravitational force plays a role of a critical parameter since not all silver particles have a chance to participate, as many precipitate to the bottom of the sample glass bottle (vial).

One of the first formulations of the MEP was stated by Ziegler in his orthogonality principle
35
36
. It is widely accepted that Ziegler’s principle is valid in the linear regime, however, some formulations of the MEP extend Ziegler’s principle beyond the linear regime
22
. Ziegler’s principle requires a fixed force and elementary system. Our system is elementary since it only has one flux and one force, namely, the current and the voltage. If we consider just the sample, then Ziegler’s principle wouldn’t apply, as the force, voltage, isn’t constant. However, if we consider the entire circuit, the voltage is constant. Our system is nonlinear due to the nonlinear nature of the nanowire self assembly, which can be seen in the sharp jumps in entropy production. This means that an extension of Ziegler’s principle to the nonlinear regime would imply that the entropy production of the entire circuit is maximized, which we observe in the second stage of evolution. This suggests that generalizations of Ziegler’s principle are promising given that the second stage of evolution is the final stage we observed. But, again, our observations suggest that DS does not occupy every segment of the sample and therefore EPR should be maximized globally, not locally.

Another constraint on the MEP is the competition effect. When two samples are connected in parallel, they are observed to compete for resources, which, at the end, lowers the overall entropy production. This suggests that as the complexity of a system increases the competition effect lowers the entropy production from its maximum value, an affect formulations of the MEP must take into account.

In a general sense the competition effect is present even within one sample, but it is not simple to quantify. Indeed, different areas of the same sample compete for the same resources (silver particles, electric field, electric current) which are needed to develop and maintain the dissipative structure. Yet, at the end of the evolution process, only a small volume of the sample is actually occupied by a functional dissipative structure.

Based on our results we suggest the following modifications to the general MEP. First, the entropy production is limited not only by the existing constraints but also by the allowed evolution time and the competition effect. Additionally, it is observed in our experiments that, due to the competition, only one sample is able to generate entropy, while the theoretical maximum of the global EPR would be approached more closely if both samples are equally efficient for the entropy production. These corrections are significant since the evolution time increases sharply with reduced driving force, following a power law trend, and the competition is ubiquitous in the natural environment. The competition effect as a filter selecting DS with a stronger internal entropy production or a larger contribution to the global entropy production.

IV.4
Possible Global Implications: Kardashev scale and the Maximum Entropy Production (MEP) Principle

The Kardashev scale classifies civilizations by their ability to harness energy at increasing global scales: planetary (Type I), stellar (Type II), and galactic (Type III)
12
. In this framework, a Type I civilization utilizes the amount of energy arriving from the Sun to Earth, a Type II civilization harnesses the full energy output of the Sun (e.g., via a Dyson sphere or a similar structure), and a Type III civilization exploits energy on the scale of the Milky Way galaxy.

The human civilization is often estimated to be below Type I, and more specifically
K\approx
0.7, as we do not yet utilize the full energy budget of Earth, although its energy consumption has historically grown rapidly, roughly exponentially. From a thermodynamic perspective, biological life can be viewed as a self-organized, non-equilibrium process driven primarily by solar radiation and all the free energy sources it produces
16
. The MEP suggests that such non-equilibrium systems tend to evolve toward states that increase their entropy production and approach the maximum set by constraints. Yet, according to Kardashev scale, these constraints are not absolute as life forms can, in principle, spread to other planets or even other star systems. This perspective may imply a tendency for an ever increasing energy utilization.

We notice an interesting analogy between the Kardashev scale and the dissipative structures, as they tend to shift the entropy production away from them. Indeed, in the first stage of the evolution the newly formed chains of silver particles generate heat inside themselves. Yet, in the second stage of the evolution the heat and the entropy production is shifted closer to the energy source, namely to the resistor. Similarly, in Kardashev type of evolution the free energy usage shifts from biological organisms to the planetary artificial structures and then to the star (i.e., the Dyson sphere).

We conclude that the following formulation of the Maximum Entropy Production (MEP) Principle works the best:
with given free energy sources, a non-equilibrium system, able to self-organize, will tend to develop competing and localized dissipative structures with tend to increase their entropy production rate and tend to approach the maximum set by the free energy sources.

When interpreted alongside the Kardashev scale, the MEP Principle implies that the progression of technological civilizations may reflect an increasing capacity to dissipate energy gradients (free energy sources), particularly through the transformation of concentrated energy sources such as stellar radiation into low temperature thermal radiation with high entropy. In such view, the constraints are not rigid since the spacial dimensions and the scope of the dissipative structure can increase, in principle, indefinitely. Energy consumption on the Kardashev scale is not just “progress”; it is the macroscopic enabler of higher entropy-production rates.
Intelligent civilizations (and their technospheres) are the most sophisticated dissipative structures yet known. They emerge and ascend the Kardashev scale precisely because MEP selects configurations that dissipate free energy gradients fastest. Ascending Kardashev levels is therefore not optional or cultural—it is the thermodynamic attractor for any long-lived technological biosphere.

We propose that MEP principle is the driving variational principle that forces Kardashev ascent and might even predicts specific observable consequences. Any technological civilization that survives its initial “entropy bottleneck” (
K\approx
0.7–0.8, the current human phase of rapid but possibly unsustainable dissipation) will inevitably reach full Type I (
K=1
) status within 200–500 years and Type II status (
K=2
) within 3,000–10,000 years after that. This is illustrated in Fig.

Reversible computation minimizes entropy production in the computational subsystem (good engineering), but MEP cares about the global rate across the entire open system and its environment. Together, these frameworks imply that as civilizations expand from planetary to stellar to galactic energy use, they may also accelerate the dissipation of energy gradients, transforming concentrated sources into dispersed waste heat (i.e., convert thermodynamic free energy into heat). In this sense the evolution might continue in many stages since new sources of free energy can be harnessed, by expanding the entropy producing systems over the surface of the Earth and, eventually, beyound the Earth limits, and each new source contributes to the EPR. In this view, the progression along the Kardashev scale can be interpreted as the stages of the entropy production, although the relationship remains theoretical, since the MEP Principle is not universally validated and the search for its exact formulation continues.

The Sun outputs a luminosity of approximately

\dot{Q}_{\odot}\approx 3.8\times 10^{26}\ \mathrm{W}
. If this energy is converted entirely into heat, it generates entropy at a rate that depends on the temperature of the environment into which the energy is dumped. For instance, dumping this energy at the Sun’s surface temperature (
T_{\odot}\approx 5800\ \mathrm{K}
) produces

\dot{S}\approx\frac{\dot{Q}_{\odot}}{T_{\odot}}\approx 6.6\times 10^{22}\ \mathrm{J\ K^{-1}\ s^{-1}}.

However, if the energy is ultimately radiated into the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) at
T_{\mathrm{CMB}}\approx 2.725\ \mathrm{K}
, the resulting entropy production rate is much higher:

\dot{S}\approx\dot{Q}_{\odot}\left(\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{CMB}}}-\frac{1}{T_{\odot}}\right)\approx 1.4\times 10^{26}\ \mathrm{J\ K^{-1}\ s^{-1}}.

Currently, global primary energy consumption is approximately
P_{\mathrm{human}}\sim 2\times 10^{13}\ \mathrm{W}
, or about 20 TW. The rate of increase of humanity’s energy use is small on a per-second basis, roughly
10^{4}\ \mathrm{W\ s^{-1}}
, corresponding to long-term growth of
1
–
2\%
per year. At this rate, simple exponential growth calculations suggest that humanity could, in principle, reach solar-scale energy consumption (
\dot{Q}_{\odot}
) within 1500–3000 years.

However, such growth is constrained by planetary heat dissipation. Energy used on Earth eventually converts to heat and must be radiated away according to the Stefan–Boltzmann law:

P_{\mathrm{rad}}=\sigma AT^{4},

where
A
is the effective radiating area and
T
the surface temperature. Exceeding Earth’s natural radiative capacity would rapidly increase global temperatures, making the planet uninhabitable. To circumvent terrestrial thermal limits, humanity must move energy-intensive processes into space. Space-based radiators can dump excess heat directly into the cold vacuum.

The ultimate strategy to maximize entropy production involves capturing a large fraction of the Sun’s energy output in space, for example through a Dyson swarm or sphere. This would allow humanity to use solar energy on an astronomical scale. A possible practical purpose is to build an artificial intelligence (AI) system at the planetary scale or even at the solar system scale. This construct might radiate waste heat into the cosmic background, thus generating entropy at a rate near the theoretical maximum:

\dot{S}_{\mathrm{max}}\sim\frac{\dot{Q}_{\odot}}{T_{\mathrm{CMB}}}.

While humanity is far from utilizing even a tiny fraction of solar energy, the MEP principle suggests a natural thermodynamic incentive to expand energy capture off-planet. Heat dissipation constraints prevent unbridled growth on Earth, but space-based energy infrastructure—including coherent energy transport, radiators, and Dyson-type structures—provides a feasible path toward maximizing entropy production. This framework links planetary energy use, space-based megastructures, and universal thermodynamics, highlighting the ultimate physical limits and long-term trajectory of civilization. If Kardashev levels are taken into consideration, then it becomes clear that the main principle of the evolution of the dissipative structure is not only to maximize EPR but also to find new source of free energy so the dissipative structure expands and continues to grow indefinitely.

When considering the MEP in regard to human civilization, the competitive effect mentioned above is participially prevalent. Though each individual human can be modeled as a dissipative structure, it is more insightful to consider each country as its own dissipative structure when considering global entropy production. Countries today compete for free energy, which potentially limits the entropy production of each individual country. Additionally, this limits the overall entropy production of the Earth, as competition might create inefficiencies and destruction in the free energy paths. A critique of this analogy would be that such competition filters out inefficient methods and systems, thus potentially increasing the overall entropy production. However, in our model system with two competing samples, we observed competition to reduce the maximum obtainable entropy production at the final stage of evolution. Thus we suggest that the competition accelerates the entropy production at the initial evolution stages, by allowing more efficient systems to grow and spread, while it decreases EPR at the final stage.

V
Conclusion

We study entropy production is a nonlinear, non-equilibrium system able of self-organization. The energy supply circuit is such that the maximum dissipation is limited. We distinguish the entropy production within the sample,
dS_{sample}/dt=(1/T)IR_{sample}^{2}
, and the global entropy production,
dS_{global}/dt=(1/T)IV_{source}
, which includes the sample and the energy source. The entropy production within the dissipative structure,
dS_{sample}/dt
, typically can achieve its maximum value if the applied voltage is sufficiently high, although the time required for the first stage of the evolution to be completed increases rapidly if the applied voltage is reduced, following a power-law trend. In the second evolution stage the conductance of the dissipative structure continues to increase and the current approaches its absolute maximum,
I\rightarrow I_{max}=V_{source}/R_{series}
. So, in the final evolution stage the global entropy production approaches its maximum given the constraints, yet the power dissipated within the sample approaches zero. In other words, as
t\rightarrow\infty
, we observe
dS_{global}/dt\rightarrow(dS_{global}/dt)_{max}
while
dS_{sample}/dt\rightarrow 0
. This appears to be in analogy with living organisms as animals tend to produce most entropy within their organisms while more advanced human civilization produces most entropy in outside constructs such as power plants and data centers etc.

We observe and quantify the effect of competition between dissipative structures (DS) in a setting where two samples are connected in parallel and thus always exposed to the same voltage, by design. In this case the two DS compete for the electrical current, which sustains them and allows them to produce entropy. The competition is manifest in that the self assembly of a dissipative structure in one sample usually prohibits formation of DS in the other sample. These results mimic biological systems, where dissipative structures (organisms) compete with each other for free energy and resources.

We observed that when two competing parallel samples the maximum achieved entropy production was somewhat lower compared to just one sample. Thus it appears that the competition reduces the entropy production at the end of the evolution. But in the beginning of the evolution the competition acts as a filter which eliminate DS which produce less entropy in themselves or globally.

Expanding on this biological analogy, we examine the potential implications of the maximum entropy production principle (MEP) for human energy consumption. We suggest that human technological development is guided by MEP, as it tends to increase energy throughput, making humanity’s progression along the Kardashev scale a plausible thermodynamic trend.
