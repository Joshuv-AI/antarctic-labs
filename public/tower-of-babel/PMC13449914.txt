Intramasseteric dermoid cyst: Imaging clues to a rare masticator space lesion
Authors: Mohamed Bouallou, Issam Berrajaa, Achraf Amine Sbai, Drissia Benfadil, Azeddine Lachkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dermoid cysts are benign developmental lesions arising from ectodermal inclusion during embryogenesis. Their occurrence in the head and neck is uncommon, and localization within the masseter muscle is exceptionally rare. We report the case of a 35-year-old woman presenting with painful right cheek swelling and trismus initially misdiagnosed as cellulitis. Contrast-enhanced CT and MRI revealed a well-circumscribed intramasseteric lesion containing intralesional fat and calcifications suggestive of a dermoid cyst, initially raising the differential diagnoses of intramuscular hemangioma and deep facial abscess. Complete surgical excision was performed through an external mandibular approach. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis by demonstrating a cyst lined with keratinizing stratified squamous epithelium with sebaceous glands. The postoperative course was uneventful with no evidence of recurrence at 12-month follow-up. This case highlights the importance of imaging in identifying the characteristic features of a dermoid cyst and in guiding the diagnostic approach to cystic lesions of the masticator space, thereby helping distinguish this rare entity from vascular and infectious mimics.

Introduction

Dermoid cysts are uncommon benign developmental lesions that arise from ectodermal sequestration during embryologic development. They belong to the spectrum of inclusion cysts and are characterized histologically by a cystic cavity lined with keratinizing stratified squamous epithelium containing dermal adnexal structures such as sebaceous glands, hair follicles, and sweat glands [1]. Although dermoid cysts may occur in various anatomical regions, their distribution within the head and neck accounts for approximately 7% of reported cases [2].

Within this region, dermoid cysts show a marked predilection for midline locations, particularly the floor of the mouth, the periorbital region, and the nasal dorsum [3]. In contrast, involvement of the deep facial spaces remains exceedingly rare. Among these atypical locations, the masticator space, and especially the masseter muscle, represents an exceptionally uncommon site. Because of this unusual localization, intramasseteric dermoid cysts may pose significant diagnostic challenges and are often misinterpreted clinically or radiologically as other cystic or vascular lesions.

The clinical presentation of dermoid cysts is usually characterized by a slow-growing, painless mass that may remain asymptomatic for years. However, when located in deep facial compartments, these lesions may present with nonspecific symptoms such as facial swelling, pain, or trismus, occasionally mimicking infectious or vascular conditions. In such situations, imaging plays a pivotal role in establishing an accurate diagnosis and guiding appropriate therapeutic management [4].

Cross-sectional imaging modalities, particularly CT and MRI, are essential for the evaluation of deep facial masses. The identification of characteristic imaging features, including intralesional fat, calcifications, and heterogeneous internal components, can strongly suggest the diagnosis and significantly narrow the differential diagnosis [5].

In this report, we describe a rare case of an intramasseteric dermoid cyst presenting as a painful cheek swelling initially misdiagnosed as cellulitis. We highlight the key radiological findings that enabled the correct preoperative diagnosis and discuss the relevant differential diagnoses and surgical management in light of the current literature.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old woman with no significant past medical history was referred to our Department of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery for evaluation of a progressively enlarging swelling of the right cheek. The patient denied any history of facial trauma, insect bite, dental procedures, or systemic diseases such as diabetes mellitus.

The symptoms began approximately 5 weeks prior to admission, when the patient noticed a gradually increasing painful swelling in the right jugal region. The lesion was initially associated with local erythema, warmth, and tenderness. She first consulted a general practitioner and was treated empirically for presumed right jugal cellulitis with oral amoxicillin–clavulanic acid (3 g/d) and paracetamol (3 g/d) for 15 days. However, despite this treatment, the swelling persisted and progressively increased in size, with the subsequent development of trismus. In view of the lack of clinical improvement, the patient was referred to our department for further evaluation and management.

On general examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable, conscious, and afebrile. Otolaryngological examination revealed a diffuse swelling of the right cheek resulting in mild facial asymmetry. Palpation identified a soft, poorly compressible jugal mass without fluctuation or evidence of cutaneous fistulization (Fig. 1). Discussion

Dermoid cysts are rare benign developmental lesions that arise from ectodermal inclusions during embryogenesis or, less commonly, from post-traumatic implantation of epithelial elements. Histologically, they are defined by a cystic cavity lined with keratinizing stratified squamous epithelium containing dermal adnexal structures such as sebaceous glands, hair follicles, and sweat glands [6]. These adnexal structures constitute the key histopathological feature distinguishing dermoid cysts from epidermoid cysts, which lack dermal appendages, and from teratoid cysts, which contain derivatives from all 3 germ layers.

In the head and neck region, dermoid cysts account for approximately 7% of all cases and are most commonly encountered in midline locations, particularly the floor of the mouth, periorbital region, and nasal dorsum [2,3]. Localization within the masticator space, especially the masseter muscle, is exceptionally rare, with only a limited number of cases reported in the literature [7].

The pathogenesis of intramasseteric dermoid cysts is thought to be primarily congenital. During embryologic development, ectodermal remnants may become entrapped within deep mesenchymal tissues during fusion of the branchial arches and craniofacial structures [6]. These epithelial inclusions can remain dormant for many years before progressively enlarging through the accumulation of keratinous debris and sebaceous secretions. Less commonly, dermoid cysts may result from acquired implantation of epithelial elements following trauma or surgical procedures [8]. In our patient, the absence of any history of facial trauma or surgery strongly favored a congenital origin.

Clinically, dermoid cysts are typically slow-growing and may remain asymptomatic for years. Their presentation depends largely on size and anatomical location [2]. Superficial lesions are often detected early due to cosmetic deformity, whereas deeply located lesions, such as those arising within the masticator space, may remain clinically silent until they reach a size sufficient to cause facial asymmetry, compress adjacent structures, or induce functional impairment such as trismus. Our patient presented with pain, erythema, and local warmth, features that initially suggested cellulitis and resulted in inappropriate antibiotic treatment. Such inflammatory-like presentations, although uncommon, have been previously reported and may delay the correct diagnosis [9].

A similar case of a cystic lesion arising within the masticator space has been reported by Han et al., describing an intramuscular epidermal cyst of the masseter muscle in a 61-year-old man presenting with a long-standing, painless cheek swelling. MRI findings showed a multilobulated cystic lesion with low T1 signal, high T2 signal, and thin rim enhancement, leading to an initial misdiagnosis of lymphatic malformation [7]. The definitive diagnosis of epidermal cyst was established only after surgical excision and histopathological analysis.

In contrast, our case exhibited a more acute and misleading clinical presentation, as well as more specific imaging features, notably the presence of intralesional fat and calcifications, which are highly characteristic of dermoid cysts.

This comparison highlights the diagnostic value of imaging, particularly the identification of fat components, in distinguishing dermoid cysts from other cystic lesions of the masticator space and avoiding potential diagnostic pitfalls.

Imaging plays a pivotal role in the evaluation of deep facial masses and is essential for accurate diagnosis and preoperative planning. Ultrasonography, although useful for superficial lesions, has limited utility in the assessment of deep spaces such as the masticator compartment due to acoustic shadowing from the mandible and limited tissue penetration [4]. Therefore, cross-sectional imaging modalities, particularly CT and MRI, are essential for precise lesion characterization and surgical planning. CT is particularly useful for detecting intralesional fat and calcifications, which represent characteristic features of dermoid cysts. Typically, these lesions appear as well-circumscribed low-attenuation masses containing fat-density components and occasional calcified foci corresponding to keratin debris or dystrophic calcifications [10]. A classic imaging appearance is the “sack-of-marbles” sign, reflecting multiple intracystic lipid globules [5]. In our case, CT demonstrated a well-defined intramasseteric lesion containing internal calcifications, serpiginous structures, and a fatty component.

MRI offers superior soft tissue contrast and multiplanar capability, making it the imaging modality of choice for characterizing lesions of the masticator space. The signal characteristics of dermoid cysts are variable and depend on their internal composition. The presence of lipid material typically results in high signal intensity on T1-weighted images, while T2-weighted images may show heterogeneous signal due to the admixture of fat, keratin, and calcified components [11]. Calcifications usually appear as signal voids, and enhancement after contrast administration is generally minimal. In our patient, MRI findings were particularly contributive, demonstrating a well-defined lesion with heterogeneous signal intensity combining fatty and calcified components, thereby strongly supporting the diagnosis and significantly narrowing the differential diagnosis.

The differential diagnosis of cystic lesions involving the masseter muscle is broad and encompasses vascular, lymphatic, infectious, and developmental entities.

In this context, intramuscular hemangioma represents an important diagnostic consideration, as it is the most frequently reported benign vascular lesion involving the masseter muscle in several published series. Although intramuscular hemangiomas account for less than 1% of all hemangiomas, approximately 10–14% occur in the head and neck region, with the masseter muscle being the most commonly involved site [12]. These lesions typically appear as poorly defined intramuscular masses containing serpiginous vascular channels, frequently associated with phleboliths. Following contrast administration, they usually demonstrate intense and homogeneous enhancement, often accompanied by flow voids on T2-weighted MRI sequences [13]. However, the absence of vascular flow voids and the presence of intralesional fat and calcifications argued strongly against this diagnosis.

Among developmental cystic lesions, epidermoid cyst constitutes an important differential diagnosis, particularly in atypical locations such as the masticator space. Epidermoid cysts typically present as well-defined lesions with homogeneous signal intensity and without intralesional fat. A key distinguishing feature is the presence of marked diffusion restriction on diffusion-weighted imaging, reflecting dense keratin content [14]. In addition, histopathological examination in our case revealed sebaceous glands within the cyst wall, definitively excluding an epidermoid cyst and confirming the diagnosis of a dermoid cyst.

Beyond vascular lesions, lymphatic malformations (lymphangiomas) were also considered, particularly given their potential to involve deep facial spaces and present as soft cheek swellings. According to the literature, lymphangiomas typically manifest as multiloculated cystic lesions with thin internal septations and often demonstrate fluid–fluid levels on MRI [15]. They are predominantly diagnosed in pediatric populations and are rarely identified de novo in adults. In contrast, the lesion in our patient was well circumscribed, oval in shape, and lacked both a multiloculated architecture and fluid–fluid levels. Furthermore, the presence of intralesional fat is not a feature of lymphatic malformations. These imaging characteristics, combined with the patient’s age, made this diagnosis unlikely.

Anatomically, branchial cleft cysts were also considered. However, their typical location makes this diagnosis improbable in the present case. These cysts are classically situated along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle within the lateral cervical region and are not expected to arise within the masticator space [16]. In addition, the presence of fat and calcifications within the lesion further excluded this possibility.

Finally, infectious collections such as abscesses were initially suspected based on the clinical presentation, which included pain, erythema, local warmth, and trismus. Similar misleading presentations have been reported in the literature, particularly in cases of deep-seated dermoid cysts associated with inflammatory changes [17].

Histopathological examination remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis. The presence of a cyst wall lined by keratinizing stratified squamous epithelium associated with dermal adnexal structures confirms the diagnosis of a dermoid cyst [1]. In our case, the identification of keratinized epithelium associated with sebaceous glands confirmed the dermoid nature of the lesion.

Complete surgical excision without rupture of the cyst wall is considered the treatment of choice for dermoid cysts. The selection of the surgical approach depends primarily on the lesion’s size, anatomical location, and relationship with adjacent structures [18]. Two principal surgical approaches have been described in the literature: the intraoral (endobuccal) approach and the external (transcutaneous) approach. The intraoral approach is usually preferred for small lesions with extension toward the oral cavity, as it avoids external scarring. However, it may provide limited surgical exposure for deeply located lesions. In contrast, the external approach offers wider surgical access and allows safer dissection of deeply located lesions within facial compartments such as the masticator space, while facilitating preservation of the facial nerve branches [19]. Careful identification and protection of the facial nerve and its branches (particularly the buccal and marginal mandibular branches) are essential during dissection in the masticator space to prevent postoperative facial weakness.

In the present case, an external mandibular approach was selected because of trismus and the absence of intraoral extension. This approach provided excellent exposure, allowed meticulous dissection with preservation of adjacent structures, and resulted in a satisfactory cosmetic outcome without facial nerve dysfunction. No intraoperative or postoperative complications such as facial nerve injury, hematoma, infection, or salivary fistula were observed.

From a cosmetic standpoint, the external mandibular approach resulted in a discreet and well-healed scar, which was well tolerated by the patient and did not lead to visible facial asymmetry or aesthetic complaint during follow-up.

The prognosis of dermoid cysts following complete surgical excision is excellent, with very low recurrence rates reported in the literature. In a recent series of head and neck epidermoid and dermoid cysts including 22 patients, recurrence was observed in only 9.5% of cases, all of which were successfully treated with secondary surgery, confirming the overall favorable outcome after complete excision [20]. Importantly, several studies emphasize that recurrence is almost exclusively associated with incomplete removal of the cyst wall, with reported recurrence rates rising dramatically to 50–100% in cases of residual epithelial lining [21]. Our patient experienced an uneventful postoperative course and remained free of recurrence at 12 months of follow-up, consistent with the favorable outcomes reported in the literature.

Overall, this case highlights the importance of recognizing characteristic imaging features, particularly intralesional fat and calcifications, in suggesting the diagnosis of dermoid cyst in unusual locations. Increased awareness of this rare entity is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and ensure optimal management.

Conclusion

Intramasseteric dermoid cysts are exceptionally rare entities that may present with misleading clinical features, mimicking inflammatory or vascular lesions. Cross-sectional imaging, particularly CT and MRI, plays a pivotal role in identifying characteristic features such as intralesional fat and calcifications, which strongly suggest the diagnosis. Recognition of these imaging clues is essential for accurate preoperative diagnosis and appropriate surgical planning. Complete surgical excision remains curative and is associated with an excellent prognosis.
