Convergence Rates for Latent Mixing Measures in Infinite Homoscedastic Location-Scale Mixture Models


Abstract
We study posterior contraction rates for mixing measures in homoscedastic location-scale mixture models with infinitely many components. While posterior convergence at the level of densities is well understood, ensuring convergence of the latent mixing measure is more challenging and has remained an open problem in settings where both location and scale parameters are unknown. We address this by deriving novel lower-bounds that connect the L 1 L^{1} distance between mixture densities to discrepancies, based on the Wasserstein distances and the operator norm, between the underlying mixing measures and scale matrices. Our approach combines the dual formulation of the W 1 W_{1} distance with functional-analytic approximation techniques. This leads to general inequalities, whose strength is determined (i) by the smoothness of the mixture kernel via the rate of decay of its characteristic function, and (ii) by a key lower-bound on the L 1 L^{1} metric involving the operator norm discrepancy between scale parameters. Moreover, a novel PDE inversion condition yields a sharper inequality for important ordinary-smooth cases. We specialize these bounds to popular mixtures based on multivariate Gaussian, Cauchy, and Laplace kernels. As a consequence, we obtain first-of-their-kind contraction rates in the context of Dirichlet process mixtures with an unknown scale parameter shared across components. As a byproduct of our inequalities, we can distinguish the convergence behavior of the location mixing measure from that of the scale parameter across a range of kernel choices, leading to nuanced insights into their respective rates.

Introduction

Mixture modeling is a central tool in modern statistical analysis, due to its flexibility and the interpretability of the implied generative process. Concretely, one models data
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as arising from a density of the form

p_{G}(x):=\int_{\Theta}f(x\mid\theta)G(d\theta),

where
G
, a probability measure on the parameter space
\Theta
, is known as the
mixing measure
, while
\{f(\cdot\mid\theta):\theta\in\Theta\}
is a family of probability density kernels indexed by
\Theta
. The statistical problem, then, consists in estimating
G
and the resulting density
p_{G}
. A popular methodological choice involves modeling
G
as discrete, i.e.,
G=\sum_{j=1}^{k}w_{j}\delta_{\theta_{j}}
with
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
and
(w_{j})_{j=1}^{k}
a sequence of positive weights summing to one. This formulation is appealing for two reasons. First, it is highly expressive: for instance, any density can be approximated in the
L^{1}
sense by discrete mixtures with suitable kernels. Second, from a generative perspective, it induces a latent partition of the data into at most
k
groups
j=1,\ldots,k
, according to which component
f(\cdot\mid\theta_{j})
generates each observation, which provides a natural basis for clustering.

Mixture models have received substantial attention in statistics and machine learning over the last decades, especially within the Bayesian community
(
McLachlan and Peel, 2000
;
Fraley and Raftery, 2002
)
following the introduction of Bayesian nonparametric models that place flexible priors, such as the Dirichlet process
(
Ferguson, 1973
)
, on discrete mixing measures with
k=\infty

(
Ferguson, 1983
;
Lo, 1984
;
Neal, 2000
)
. The appeal of the nonparametric approach is that it allows for flexible density estimation and clustering schemes, often making both the data partition and the underlying mixing measure the two primary objects of interest
(
Nguyen, 2013
;
Wade, 2023
)
. This flexibility has also led to computational methods for handling the resulting infinite-dimensional structure
(
Escobar and West, 1995
;
MacEachern and Müller, 1998
;
Walker, 2007
)
, as well as to extensive theoretical analysis. This article offers several new contributions to such line of work, which we now briefly review.

1.1
Related literature

Much of the existing theory has focused on the asymptotic behavior of Bayesian mixtures for density estimation. To introduce the problem, define the
L^{1}
distance
\|p-p^{\prime}\|_{1}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|p(x)-p^{\prime}(x)|dx
between densities
p
and
p^{\prime}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and suppose that
X_{i}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}p_{G_{0}}
for some unknown mixing measure
G_{0}
. A natural question is whether, given a prior
\Pi
supported on a suitable space of mixing measures
\mathscr{G}
with
G_{0}\in\mathscr{G}
, the posterior distribution

\Pi(A\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}):=\frac{\int_{A}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{G}(X_{i})\Pi(dG)}{\int_{\mathscr{G}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{G^{\prime}}(X_{i})\Pi(dG^{\prime})}

concentrates in
L^{1}
neighborhoods of
p_{G_{0}}
as
n\to\infty
. This question has been studied extensively, both for fixed but arbitrarily small neighborhoods
(
Ghosal et al., 1999
;
Barron et al., 1999
;
Walker and Hjort, 2001
;
Walker, 2004
;
Lijoi et al., 2005b
;
Tokdar, 2006
;
Wu and Ghosal, 2010
;
Chae and Walker, 2017
;
Bariletto et al., 2025
;
Ghosal and Van der Vaart, 2017
, leading to
L^{1}
posterior consistency,)
, and for shrinking neighborhoods, yielding
L^{1}
posterior contraction rates
\omega_{n}
satisfying

\Pi\left(G:\|p_{G}-p_{G_{0}}\|_{1}\geq K\omega_{n}\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\right)\to 0

(1)

in
P_{G_{0}}^{n}
-probability for all large enough
K>0
as
n\to\infty
; here,
P_{G}^{n}
denotes the
n
-fold product measure induced by
P_{G}(dx):=p_{G}(x)dx
, while
(\omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a positive vanishing sequence. Investigating sufficient conditions for posterior behavior like in Equation (
1
), as done by
(
Ghosal et al., 2000
;
Genovese and Wasserman, 2000
;
Shen and Wasserman, 2001
;
Ghosal and van der Vaart, 2001
;
Walker et al., 2007
;
Ghosal and van der Vaart, 2007a
;
Ghosal and van der Vaart, 2007b
;
Giné and Nickl, 2011
;
Tokdar, 2013
;
Scricciolo, 2011
;
Canale and De Blasi, 2017
;
Ghosal and Van der Vaart, 2017
;
Castillo, 2024
)
, provides useful information on the asymptotic rate at which the posterior concentrates around the ground truth
p_{G_{0}}
, and yields guidance on model choices that support efficient density estimation.

However,
L^{1}
contraction rates do not fully characterize posterior behavior in terms of clustering and contraction to the true mixing measure, since the evolution of the mixture components is a more refined phenomenon than
L^{1}
convergence of the density. Several works have addressed this problem, focusing for instance on the recovery of
k_{0}
, the true number of components
(
Miller and Harrison, 2013
;
Miller and Harrison, 2014
;
Ascolani et al., 2023
)
, and on the allocation of data to redundant components in overfitted mixture models
(
Rousseau and Mengersen, 2011
)
. A prominent strand of literature, pioneered by
Nguyen, 2013
, studies posterior contraction towards
G_{0}
using the Wasserstein distances to measure discrepancy between mixing measures
(
Guha et al., 2021
;
Ho and Nguyen, 2016a
;
Ho and Nguyen, 2016b
;
Rousseau and Scricciolo, 2024
, see also)
. Concretely, if
\rho
is a metric on
\Theta
, the corresponding
Wasserstein distance of order
r\in[1,\infty)
is defined as

W_{r}(G,G^{\prime}):=\left(\inf_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(G,G^{\prime})}\int_{\Theta\times\Theta}\rho(\theta,\theta^{\prime})^{r}\pi(d\theta,d\theta^{\prime})\right)^{1/r},

where
\mathcal{P}(G,G^{\prime})
is the set of probability measures
\pi
on
\Theta\times\Theta
with marginals
G
and
G^{\prime}
. Besides their solid grounding in the rich field of optimal transport
(
Villani, 2008
)
, the appeal of the
W_{r}
metrics in the study of mixture models lies in their ability to capture discrepancies between mixing measures that may be discrete and have disjoint supports, a feature not shared by many classical probability metrics. These features have motivated significant recent interest in optimal transport metrics within the Bayesian nonparametric community, for instance to measure dependence between random measures
(
Catalano et al., 2021
;
Catalano et al., 2024
;
Catalano and Lavenant, 2025
)
.

Convergence analyses for mixing measures with infinitely many atoms, such as those arising from a Dirichlet process prior, have been presented only for a restricted class of mixtures, namely
location mixtures
, where
f(x\mid\theta)=g(x-\theta)
for
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and some density kernel
g
. Location mixtures have important applications, for instance in deconvolution problems with additive noise of known scale, as studied in a Bayesian nonparametric framework by
Nguyen, 2013
;
Gao and van der Vaart, 2016
;
Scricciolo, 2018
;
Rousseau and Scricciolo, 2024
; see also
Caillerie et al., 2011
;
Dedecker and Michel, 2013
;
Dedecker et al., 2015
for non-Bayesian analyses using Wasserstein distances. However, restricting variation to a location parameter severely limits flexibility in learning the underlying density and mixing structure, often motivating the adoption of more complex model designs.

1.2
Contributions of the article

A more flexible and popular alternative to location mixtures are
location-scale mixtures
associated to families of kernels of the form

f(x\mid\theta,\Sigma)=|\Sigma|^{-1/2}g\left((x-\theta)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\theta)\right),

where
\theta\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a location parameter,
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a positive-definite symmetric scale matrix (with
|\Sigma|
denoting its determinant), and
g
is a typically symmetric kernel. Despite their prominence in applications, the posterior behavior of nonparametric mixing measures with a prior on both the location and scale parameters has, to the best of our knowledge, never been studied before. Our goal is to provide, for the first time, such an analysis for priors supported on mixing measures of the form

G(d\theta,dS)=P(d\theta)\times\delta_{\Sigma}(dS),

that is,
homoscedastic location-scale mixtures
in which the matrix
\Sigma
is shared across components and, unlike in previous work, estimated from data rather than fixed at the unknown value
\Sigma_{0}
. Concretely, we aim to establish posterior contraction rates of the form

\Pi\left(G:\mathcal{D}(G,G_{0})\geq K\varepsilon_{n}\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\right)\to 0

(2)

in
P_{G_{0}}^{n}
-probability for all large enough
K>0
as
n\to\infty
, where
G_{0}=P_{0}\times\delta_{\Sigma_{0}}
and
\mathcal{D}
is a meaningful discrepancy between mixing measures
G
and
G_{0}
. To achieve this, after verifying an
L^{1}
contraction rate
\omega_{n}
as in Equation (
1
), a key step is to derive a lower-bound of the form
\|p_{G}-p_{G_{0}}\|_{1}\gtrsim\mathcal{D}(G,G_{0})
.

\|p_{G}-p_{G_{0}}\|_{1}\gtrsim\varphi(W_{1}(P,P_{0}))
for some increasing function
\varphi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
. Existing approaches, pioneered by
Nguyen, 2013
, establish this kind of inequality by first convolving
P
and

with a suitable smoothing kernel
K_{\delta}
, and then exploiting the triangle inequality

W_{1}(P,P_{0})\leq W_{1}(P,P*K_{\delta})+W_{1}(P*K_{\delta},P_{0}*K_{\delta})+W_{1}(P_{0}*K_{\delta},P_{0})

(3)

to bound
W_{1}(P,P_{0})
by handling each term separately
(
Nguyen, 2013

.

While this strategy represents the state of the art in the location mixture setting, it fails in the more general location-scale case, as the notion of convolution has no obvious extension to bound discrepancies of the form
W_{1}(P\times\delta_{\Sigma},P_{0}\times\delta_{\Sigma_{0}})
where different scales
\Sigma
and
\Sigma_{0}
are involved.

To fill this gap, our first contribution is to develop new proof techniques that approach the problem from a functional analytic perspective. Our starting point is the observation that, for many location-scale mixtures of interest, an inequality of the form

\|p_{G}-p_{G_{0}}\|_{1}\gtrsim\Psi(\|\Sigma-\Sigma_{0}\|_{\mathrm{op}}),

(4)

for some strictly increasing function
\Psi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
, is available under mild parameter boundedness conditions. Moreover, the well-known Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality theorem
(
Villani, 2008
)
allows to rewrite

W_{1}(P,P_{0})=\inf_{h\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathcal{X})}\int_{\mathcal{X}}h(\theta)\big(P(d\theta)-P_{0}(d\theta)\big),

where
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathcal{X}):=\{h\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\,:\,\forall\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X},\;|h(\theta)-h(\theta^{\prime})|\leq\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|\}
and the infimum is attained at some
h^{*}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
. Our strategy, then, is to develop a functional approximation of
h^{*}
that enables the use of Fourier transforms and the inversion theorem to analyze
W_{1}(P,P_{0})
. In fact, since
\mathcal{X}
will be assumed to be a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, leveraging Fourier inversion requires to first extend
h^{*}
to a function
\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}
defined on the whole
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and then to approximate
\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}
with another function
\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}^{*}
, where
\Lambda>0
controls the approximation accuracy, that guarantees sufficient regularity. This constitutes a key technical step in our analysis.

With these approximations in place, we rewrite

\displaystyle W_{1}(P,P_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}^{*}(\theta)\big(P(d\theta)-P_{0}(d\theta)\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(\theta)-\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}^{*}(\theta)\right)\big(P(d\theta)-P_{0}(d\theta)\big).

The rest of our argument then proceeds in two steps:

1.

we show that the difference
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(\theta)-\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}^{*}(\theta)\big)\big(P(d\theta)-P_{0}(d\theta)\big)
represents a controlled approximation error of order
\Lambda^{-1}
, then

2.

we bound the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}^{*}(\theta)\big(P(d\theta)-P_{0}(d\theta)\big)
, which is amenable to a Fourier-analytic treatment owing to the regularity of
\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}^{*}
. In particular, a careful application of the Fourier inversion theorem, together with Equation (
4

\|p_{G}-p_{G_{0}}\|_{1}\gtrsim\varphi(W_{1}(P,P_{0}))+\Psi(\|\Sigma-\Sigma_{0}\|_{\text{op}}),

(5)

for a certain increasing function
\varphi
.

While the specific form of the function
\Psi
is a direct consequence of the requirement in Equation (
4

\varphi
can be characterized in terms of the properties of the density kernel
f
. In particular,
\varphi
is related to the smoothness of

(i)

In the

\alpha>0

\varphi(t)=\min\{\exp(-C/t^{\alpha}),(\Psi\circ\Xi^{-1})(\exp(-C/t^{\alpha}))\}

C>0
, where
\Xi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is another function (introduced later) determined by

(ii)

In the

\beta>0
, where
\log\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)\asymp-\log(1+\|\xi\|^{\beta})

\varphi(t)=\min\{t^{d+\beta+1},(\Psi\circ\Xi^{-1})(Ct^{d+p+1})\},

for some constant

, where
p\geq 1
is determined by

and introduced later. In this case, the dependence on

and

(iii)

In the presence of a
partial differential equation (PDE) inversion condition of order
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
(involving the existence of an appropriate differential operator
\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}
such that
\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}p_{G}=P

), we obtain that

\varphi(t)=\min\{t^{\beta},\Psi(Ct^{\beta})\},

To summarize, the main contributions of the article are as follows.

•

Using the dual formulation of the
W_{1}
metric, together with functional-analytic approximation techniques, we develop new tools to lower-bound the
L^{1}
distance between homoscedastic location-scale mixture densities in terms of the
W_{1}
distance between the underlying location mixing measures and the operator norm distance between the underlying scales. The resulting inequalities depend on the smoothness of the mixture kernel and, in the ordinary-smooth case, can be significantly sharpened by verifying a novel and widely applicable PDE inversion condition;

•

We specialize these abstract inequalities to common kernel choices, obtaining explicit bounds for multivariate Gaussian, Cauchy, and Laplace mixtures;

•

Combining these results with existing
L^{1}
contraction theory, for the first time in the literature we derive posterior convergence rates for mixing measures and scale parameters in infinite homoscedastic location-scale mixture models with Dirichlet process priors, focusing on the Gaussian and Laplace cases.

The rest of the article is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces notation and collects a few preliminary results. Section
3
develops abstract inequalities of the form in Equation (
5
) under different smoothness assumptions on the mixture kernel. Section
4
specializes these inequalities to commonly used kernels. Section
5
builds on the previous sections to derive posterior contraction rates for homoscedastic location-scale infinite mixture models with Dirichlet process priors. Section
6
concludes. Complete proofs are provided in the appendices.

2
Notation and preliminaries

Throughout the article, we assume that a sample of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued observations
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
is available and drawn i.i.d. from an underlying mixture distribution
P_{G_{0}}
(for
G_{0}=P_{0}\times\delta_{\Sigma_{0}}
) with density

p_{G_{0}}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x\mid\theta,\Sigma_{0})\,P_{0}(d\theta),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(endowed with its natural Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
). The specific form of the kernel
f
will vary throughout, with

\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\xi^{\top}x}f(x\mid 0,\Sigma)\,dx,\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

representing the associated characteristic function; with a slight abuse of notation, we also write
\Phi_{\mu}(\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\xi^{\top}x}\mu(dx)
for any probability measure
\mu

Prior and posterior distributions, which we have already defined and denoted respectively by

A key feature of the Dirichlet process is the implied stick-breaking representation

3
Inverse Bounds for Mixing Measure Discrepancies

In this section, we derive lower-bounds for the
L^{1}

p_{G}
and

G=P\times\delta_{\Sigma}
and
G^{\prime}=P^{\prime}\times\delta_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
, in terms of suitable functions of the
W_{1}
distance between the mixing measures
P
and
P^{\prime}
, and the operator norm distance between the scale matrices
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}

We begin by stating two assumptions that will be used throughout.

Assumption 1
.

There exists a strictly increasing function
\Psi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
such that, for any
G=P\times\delta_{\Sigma}
and

\mathscr{G}

\displaystyle\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\geq C\Psi(\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}),

for a positive quantity
C
that may depend on
R
,
\lambda_{\min}
,
\lambda_{\max}
, and
d
.

The specific form of
\Psi
depends on the choice of kernel, but the requirement itself is mild: since the location space
\mathcal{X}
is compact, a small
L^{1}

4

Assumption 2

There exist an exponent
p\geq 1
and a strictly increasing function
\Xi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
such that, for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

|\Phi_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(\xi)-\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|\leq C\Xi(\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}})\|\xi\|^{p}\max\{|\Phi_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(\xi)|,|\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|\},

for a positive quantity
C
that may depend on
R
,
\lambda_{\min}
,
\lambda_{\max}
, and
d
.

Assumption

to vary in a controlled manner with respect to
\Sigma

and a linear one in the magnitude of the characteristic function itself.

We are now in a position to state our main results. Towards that goal, we will consider three settings separately: when the kernel

is super-smooth, when it is ordinary smooth, and when the mixture satisfies a certain PDE inversion condition.

3.1
Super-smooth kernel

We first consider the case in which the mixture kernel
f
is super-smooth. For the next definition, recall that
\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)
denotes the characteristic function, evaluated at
\xi
, associated with the density
f(\cdot\mid 0,\Sigma)
.

Definition 1
.

The density kernel
f
is
super-smooth of order
\alpha>0
if

\exp(-c_{1}\|\xi\|^{\alpha})\leq|\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|\leq\exp(-c_{2}\|\xi\|^{\alpha})

for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{+}_{d}(\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max})
, where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are constants depending only on
\lambda_{\min}
,
\lambda_{\max}
, and
d
.

That is, super-smoothness requires an exponential decay of
\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)
as a function of
\|\xi\|
. This condition is satisfied by several commonly used kernels, including the Gaussian and Cauchy kernels considered in Section
4
. Combined with Assumptions
1
and
2
, super-smoothness yields the following inverse bound.

Theorem 2
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, if the kernel
f
is super-smooth of order
\alpha
, there exist positive quantities
C,\widetilde{C},C^{\prime}
, depending on
R
,
\lambda_{\min}
,
\lambda_{\max}
,
p
, and
d
, such that

\displaystyle\|p_{G}

\displaystyle-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\geq C^{\prime}\bigg[\Psi(\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}})

\displaystyle+\min\left\{\exp\left(-\frac{C}{W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{\alpha}}\right),\;(\Psi\circ\Xi^{-1})\left(\widetilde{C}\exp\left(-\frac{C}{W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{\alpha}}\right)\right)\right\}\bigg]

(6)

for any
G=P\times\delta_{\Sigma}
and
G^{\prime}=P^{\prime}\times\delta_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
in
\mathscr{G}
.

The bound in Theorem
2
depends on
\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
only through the function
\Psi

1

W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})\equiv w
is more complex, entering through terms of the form

\Psi\circ\Xi^{-1}

Beyond the lower-bound results and their implications for statistical estimation of mixture models, a central contribution of this article is the development of new techniques to derive such bounds, based on functional approximations in the dual formulation of
W_{1}
and tools from Fourier analysis and PDEs. Therefore, we now outline the main steps of the argument, deferring the full proof to the appendices.

Proof sketch of Theorem
2
.

The proof consists of five steps. We first use the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality theorem to represent
W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})
as an integral of an optimal
1
-Lipschitz function
h^{*}
, which we extend to all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
via a McShane–Whitney argument. We then construct a spectrally truncated approximation of
h^{*}
using a Jackson kernel, controlling the resulting approximation error. Fourier inversion and Fubini’s theorem allow us to express the integral in terms of characteristic functions, which we split into two contributions via the triangle inequality. Each contribution is then bounded using the super-smoothness assumption and Assumptions
1
and
2
. Optimizing over the truncation parameter and rearranging finally yields the claimed inequality. Below is a more detailed discussion of each step.

Step 1: McShane–Whitney extension.
By the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality theorem
(
Villani, 2008
)
, for every
P
and
P^{\prime}
there exists
h^{*}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
attaining

W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}h^{*}(\theta)\,(P(d\theta)-P^{\prime}(d\theta)).

Replacing
h^{*}
with
h^{*}(\cdot)-h^{*}(0)
leaves the integral unchanged and ensures
|h^{*}(x)|\leq\|x\|
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Our goal is to analyze
W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})
via Fourier inversion, which requires extending
h^{*}
to all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and working with a sufficiently regular approximation thereof. We first extend
h^{*}
to a globally
1
-Lipschitz continuous function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
via the McShane–Whitney infimal convolution
(
Heinonen, 2001
;
McShane, 1934
;
Whitney, 1934
)

\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x):=\inf_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\Big\{h^{*}(y)+\|x-y\|\Big\},

and then multiply it by a compactly supported and infinitely differentiable cutoff
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
\eta(x)=1
for
x\in\bar{B}(0,R)
and
\eta(x)=0
for
x\not\in B(0,2R)

(
Lee, 2013
)
. The resulting function
\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}
belongs to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, is globally
(1+2RM_{\eta})
-Lipschitz continuous (where
M_{\eta}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\nabla\eta(x)\|<\infty
), and preserves the property

W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)\,(P(d\theta)-P^{\prime}(d\theta)).

Step 2: Spectral truncation.
To apply Fourier inversion, we convolve
\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}
with a rescaled Jackson kernel
K_{\Lambda}(x):=\Lambda^{d}K(\Lambda x)
, whose Fourier transform is supported on
\bar{B}(0,\Lambda)
, to obtain
\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}:=\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}*K_{\Lambda}

(
DeVore and Lorentz, 1993
;
Butzer and Nessel, 1971
)
. The Lipschitz continuity of
\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}
implies the approximation bound

\|\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}-\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}(x)-\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(x)\big|\leq(1+2RM_{\eta})M_{K}/\Lambda,

where
M_{K}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|K(x)\,dx<\infty
. Combined with
\sup_{A\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}|P(A)-P^{\prime}(A)|\leq 2
, this gives

W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})\;\leq\;\frac{C_{0}}{\Lambda}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}(\theta)\,(P(d\theta)-P^{\prime}(d\theta))

(7)

for a constant
C_{0}>0
depending only on
R
and
f
.

Step 3: Fourier inversion.
Since
\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathcal{F}[\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}]
is supported in
\bar{B}(0,\Lambda)
and bounded, the Fourier inversion and Fubini’s theorems yield

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}(\theta)\,(P(d\theta)-P^{\prime}(d\theta))=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\bar{B}(0,\Lambda)}\mathcal{F}[\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}](\xi)\,\big(\Phi_{P}(\xi)-\Phi_{P^{\prime}}(\xi)\big)\,d\xi.

Writing
\Phi_{P}(\xi)-\Phi_{P^{\prime}}(\xi)=\Phi_{p_{G}}(\xi)/\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)-\Phi_{p_{G^{\prime}}}(\xi)/\Phi_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(\xi)
and applying the triangle inequality, the right-hand side is bounded above by
C_{R}
times

\int_{\bar{B}(0,\Lambda)}\frac{|\Phi_{p_{G}}(\xi)-\Phi_{p_{G^{\prime}}}(\xi)|}{|\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|}\,d\xi\;+\;\int_{\bar{B}(0,\Lambda)}\frac{|\Phi_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(\xi)-\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|}{|\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|}\,d\xi,

(8)

where
C_{R}>0
depends only on
R
and
d
.

Step 4: Bounding via super-smoothness.
For the first term in Equation (
8
), we use
|\Phi_{p_{G}}(\xi)-\Phi_{p_{G^{\prime}}}(\xi)|\leq\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}
and the lower-bound
|\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|\geq\exp(-c_{1}\|\xi\|^{\alpha})
from the super-smoothness assumption to get

\int_{\bar{B}(0,\Lambda)}\frac{|\Phi_{p_{G}}(\xi)-\Phi_{p_{G^{\prime}}}(\xi)|}{|\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|}\,d\xi\;\lesssim\;\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\cdot\Lambda^{d}\exp(c_{1}\Lambda^{\alpha}).

For the second term, Assumption
2
and the super-smoothness bounds together yield

\int_{\bar{B}(0,\Lambda)}\frac{|\Phi_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(\xi)-\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|}{|\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|}\,d\xi\;\lesssim\;\Xi(\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}})\cdot\Lambda^{d+p}\exp((c_{1}-c_{2})\Lambda^{\alpha}).

Step 5: Conclusion.
Substituting these bounds into Equation (
7
) and using Assumption
1
to replace
\Xi(\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}})
with
(\Xi\circ\Psi^{-1})(\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1})
, we obtain

W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})\;\lesssim\;\frac{C_{0}}{\Lambda}+\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\cdot\Lambda^{d}e^{c_{1}\Lambda^{\alpha}}+(\Xi\circ\Psi^{-1})(\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1})\cdot\Lambda^{d+p}e^{(c_{1}-c_{2})\Lambda^{\alpha}}.

Setting

and applying the elementary bound

and

, we rearrange to obtain that either

\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\;\gtrsim\;\exp\!\left(-\frac{C}{W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{\alpha}}\right),

or

\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\;\gtrsim\;(\Psi\circ\Xi^{-1})\!\left(\exp\!\left(-\frac{C}{W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{\alpha}}\right)\right),

which together with Assumption
1
give Equation (
6

3.2
Ordinary-smooth kernel

The second setting we consider involves an ordinary-smooth kernel

Definition 2
.

The density kernel
f

ordinary-smooth of order

c_{1}(1+\|\xi\|^{\beta})^{-1}\leq|\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|\leq c_{2}(1+\|\xi\|^{\beta})^{-1}

\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{+}_{d}(\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max})
, where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are constants depending on
\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}
, and
d
.

Unlike in the super-smooth case, ordinary-smooth kernels are characterized by a polynomial decay of the characteristic function, corresponding to a substantially lower degree of smoothness. This leads to the following inverse bound.

Theorem 3
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, if the kernel
f
is ordinary-smooth of order
\beta
, there exist positive constants
C
and
C^{\prime}
, depending on
R
,
\lambda_{\min}
,
\lambda_{\max}
,
p
, and
d
, such that

\displaystyle\|p_{G}

\displaystyle-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\geq C^{\prime}\Big[\Psi(\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}})

\displaystyle+\min\Big(W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{d+\beta+1},\;(\Psi\circ\Xi^{-1})\big(CW_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{d+p+1}\big)\Big)\Big]

(9)

for any
G=P\times\delta_{\Sigma}
and
G^{\prime}=P^{\prime}\times\delta_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
in
\mathscr{G}
.

The structure of the bound in Theorem
3
is similar to its super-smooth counterpart in Theorem
2
, in terms of the role played by the functions
\Psi
and
\Xi
in mediating the dependence on
\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
and
W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})
. However, the
W_{1}
component of the bound is significantly improved under the ordinary-smooth assumption: the inverse-exponential dependence in the super-smooth case is replaced by a polynomial one, with
\beta
worsening the rate as it increases. A drawback of Theorem
3
, however, is the explicit dependence on the dimension
d
, which may inflate the polynomial degree. Intuitively, this reflects the fact that the ordinary-smooth assumption imposes only mild conditions on the tail behavior of the characteristic function of
f
, whereas in many cases of interest (e.g., Laplace mixtures, as shown in Section
4

Proof sketch of Theorem
3
.

The proof follows the same structure as that of Theorem
2
, with Steps 1–3 carrying over verbatim. The key differences arise in Step 4, where the polynomial decay of the characteristic function under ordinary smoothness leads to polynomial rather than exponential bounds, and in Step 5, where the choice of truncation parameter and the subsequent rearrangement differ accordingly. We therefore focus on these two steps.

Step 4’: Bounding via ordinary smoothness.
For the first term in Equation (
8

|\Phi_{p_{G}}(\xi)-\Phi_{p_{G^{\prime}}}(\xi)|\leq\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}
and the lower-bound
|\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|\geq(1+c_{1}\|\xi\|^{\beta})^{-1}

\int_{\bar{B}(0,\Lambda)}\frac{|\Phi_{p_{G}}(\xi)-\Phi_{p_{G^{\prime}}}(\xi)|}{|\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|}\,d\xi\;\lesssim\;\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\cdot\Lambda^{d+\beta}.

For the second term, Assumption
2

|\Phi_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(\xi)-\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|\lesssim\Xi(\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}})\|\xi\|^{p}\max(|\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|,|\Phi_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(\xi)|).

Under ordinary smoothness, the ratio
\max(|\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|,|\Phi_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(\xi)|)/|\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|
is bounded above by
c_{1}/c_{2}
, uniformly in
\xi
, so that

\int_{\bar{B}(0,\Lambda)}\frac{|\Phi_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(\xi)-\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|}{|\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|}\,d\xi\;\lesssim\;\Xi(\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}})\cdot\Lambda^{d+p}.

Note that, in contrast to the super-smooth case, both bounds are now purely polynomial in
\Lambda

Step 5’: Conclusion.
Substituting these bounds into Equation (
7

1

\Xi(\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}})

(\Xi\circ\Psi^{-1})(\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1})

W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})\;\lesssim\;\frac{C_{0}}{\Lambda}+\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\cdot\Lambda^{d+\beta}+(\Xi\circ\Psi^{-1})(\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1})\cdot\Lambda^{d+p}.

Setting

\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\;\gtrsim\;W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{d+\beta+1},

or

\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\;\gtrsim\;(\Psi\circ\Xi^{-1})\!\left(CW_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{d+p+1}\right).

These two cases, combined with Assumption
1
, give Equation (
9
).
∎

3.3
Kernel with PDE inversion

To remedy the dependence on
d
in the inverse bound based on ordinary-smooth kernels, we now introduce the following PDE inversion assumption. Before proceeding, as we are about to work with differential operators defined in the weak functional-analytic sense of distributions, we refer the reader to, e.g., Chapter 9 of
Folland, 1999
for a concise introduction to the topic.

Definition 3
.

For any
\nu=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, set
|\nu|:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\nu_{j}
and define the distributional derivative operator
\partial^{\nu}:=\frac{\partial^{|\nu|}}{\partial x_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\dots\partial x_{d}^{\nu_{d}}}
. Then the family
\mathscr{P}
satisfies a
PDE inversion condition of order
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
if there exists a linear differential operator

\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}=\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\,:\,0\leq|\nu|\leq\beta}c_{\nu}(\Sigma)\partial^{\nu}

such that

1.

there exists
\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
with
|\nu|=\beta
such that
c_{\nu}(\Sigma)\neq 0
,

2.

there exists a constant
L>0
, independent of
\nu
, such that the coefficients
\Sigma\mapsto c_{\nu}(\Sigma)
are
L
-Lipschitz continuous from
\mathbb{S}^{+}_{d}(\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max})
to
\mathbb{R}
, and

3.

the equation
\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}p_{G}=P
holds in the sense of distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. That is, for every test function
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have

\langle p_{G},\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}^{*}\phi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\,P(dx),

where
\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}^{*}:=\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\,:\,0\leq|\nu|\leq\beta}(-1)^{|\nu|}c_{\nu}(\Sigma)\partial^{\nu}
is the formal adjoint of
\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}
, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the natural pairing between distributions and test functions.

To the best of our knowledge, the formulation of PDE inversion conditions of the above kind in the context of mixture model theory is new. As it turns out, conditions of this form are especially well-suited to our problem, as they provide a convenient way to invert the convolution
p_{G}(x)=P*f(x\mid\cdot,\Sigma)
by means of a differential operator of finite order
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
, whose coefficients depend smoothly on the scale matrix
\Sigma
. Exploiting this relationship---which, as shown below, holds for Laplace mixtures
6
6

6

Though we do not pursue this here, the PDE inversion condition of Definition
3
is applicable to other kinds of mixtures, such as those driven by shifted exponential and fixed-shape Gamma kernels.
—allows one to relate differences between the mixture densities
p_{G}
and
p_{G^{\prime}}
directly to differences between the corresponding mixing measures
P
and
P^{\prime}
and the scale parameters
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
. Finally, we emphasize that the use of distribution theory is necessary here, since the mixing measure
P
may be purely atomic, as is the case almost surely under a Dirichlet process prior, and differentiation must be interpreted in a weak sense via test functions
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Before presenting an inverse bound based on the PDE inversion condition, we relate it to the ordinary smoothness of the kernel, thereby justifying its use as a substitute for obtaining sharper inequalities.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that
f(x\mid 0,\Sigma)=|\Sigma|^{-1/2}g(x^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}x)
for some
g:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
. If
\mathscr{P}
satisfies the PDE inversion condition of even order
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
, then
f
is ordinary-smooth of order
\beta
.

That is, when the index
\beta
is even (as it is the case for Laplace mixtures, cf. Theorem
8
below), the PDE condition is a proper strengthening of ordinary-smoothness of the kernel
f
. This, as we show next, plays a crucial role in sharpening the inverse bound obtained in Theorem
3
.

Theorem 4
.

Under Assumption
1
, if
\mathscr{P}
satisfies a PDE inversion condition of order
\beta
, there exist positive quantities
C
and
C^{\prime}
, depending only on
R
,
\lambda_{\min}
,
\lambda_{\max}
, and
d
, such that

\displaystyle\|p_{G}

\displaystyle-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\geq C^{\prime}\left[\Psi(\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}})\;+\;\min\left\{W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{\beta},\;\Psi\big(CW_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{\beta}\big)\right\}\right],

(10)

for any
G=P\times\delta_{\Sigma}
and
G^{\prime}=P^{\prime}\times\delta_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
in
\mathscr{G}
.

Theorem
4
improves upon Theorem
3
by removing the explicit dependence on the dimension
d
in the exponent. That is, while the bound in Theorem
3
describes a dimension-dependent relationship under simple ordinary-smoothness, the PDE inversion condition yields, remarkably, a dimension-free rate governed solely by the smoothness order
\beta
. This shows that, for mixtures satisfying this condition, the efficiency in recovering the mixing measure is determined by the smoothness properties of the kernel (with higher smoothness leading to weaker bounds) rather than by the ambient dimension.

Proof sketch of Theorem
4
.

The proof shares Step 1 with Theorems
2
and
3
, producing a compactly supported, globally Lipschitz continuous function
\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)\,(P(d\theta)-P^{\prime}(d\theta))
. The key departure begins in Step 2”, where we use a smooth mollifier rather than a Jackson kernel to construct the approximation
\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}
, which grants additional regularities needed to invoke the PDE inversion condition. Steps 3”–5” then exploit this condition directly in the spatial domain, bypassing Fourier inversion entirely and leading to dimension-free bounds.

Step 2”: Mollifier approximation.
Rather than convolving with a Jackson kernel as in the previous proofs, we convolve
\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}
with a scaled mollifier
\psi_{\Lambda}(x):=\Lambda^{d}\psi(\Lambda x)
, where
\psi(x)=C_{\psi}^{-1}\varrho(x)
is a normalized bump function with
C_{\psi}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varrho(x)\,dx
and

\varrho(x)=\begin{cases}\exp\!\left(-\dfrac{1}{1-\|x\|^{2}}\right)&\text{if }\|x\|<1,\\
0&\text{if }\|x\|\geq 1,\end{cases}

so that
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(x)\,dx=1
. We then define

\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}(x):=(\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}*\psi_{\Lambda})(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(x-y)\,\psi_{\Lambda}(y)\,dy.

The resulting function
\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}
belongs to
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which makes it an admissible test function for the distributional PDE inversion condition. The Lipschitz continuity of
\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}
again implies
\|\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}-\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}\|_{\infty}\leq(1+2RM_{\eta})M_{\psi}/\Lambda
, where
M_{\psi}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|\psi(x)\,dx<\infty
, and combined with
\sup_{A\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}|P(A)-P^{\prime}(A)|\leq 2
this yields

W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})\;\leq\;\frac{C_{0}}{\Lambda}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}(\theta)\,(P(d\theta)-P^{\prime}(d\theta))

(11)

for a constant
C_{0}>0
depending only on
R
and
f
.

Step 3”: PDE inversion.
Since
\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}p_{G}=P
and
\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}p_{G^{\prime}}=P^{\prime}
hold in the sense of distributions, and
\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is an admissible test function, we may write

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}(\theta)\,(P(d\theta)-P^{\prime}(d\theta))

\displaystyle=\langle\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda},\,\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}p_{G}-\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}p_{G^{\prime}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{T}^{*}_{\Sigma}\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda},\,p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\rangle+\langle(\mathcal{T}^{*}_{\Sigma}-\mathcal{T}^{*}_{\Sigma^{\prime}})\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda},\,p_{G^{\prime}}\rangle,

where
\mathcal{T}^{*}_{\Sigma}:=\sum_{0\leq|\nu|\leq\beta}(-1)^{|\nu|}c_{\nu}(\Sigma)\partial^{\nu}
is the formal adjoint of
\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}
, and we added and subtracted
\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}p_{G^{\prime}}
to isolate the two sources of discrepancy. We label the two resulting terms
I_{1}
(observation penalty) and
I_{2}
(scale mismatch).

Step 4”: Bounding
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
.
For
I_{1}
, Hölder’s inequality gives
|I_{1}|\leq\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\|\mathcal{T}^{*}_{\Sigma}\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}\|_{\infty}
. To bound
\|\mathcal{T}^{*}_{\Sigma}\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}\|_{\infty}
, we use the fact that
\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}=\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}*\psi_{\Lambda}
to transfer one derivative onto
\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}
and the remaining ones onto
\psi_{\Lambda}
. Young’s inequality and the scaling identity
\|\partial^{\nu}\psi_{\Lambda}\|_{1}=\Lambda^{|\nu|-1}\|\partial^{\nu}\psi\|_{1}
then yield
\|\partial^{\nu}\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}\|_{\infty}\lesssim\Lambda^{|\nu|-1}
for all
\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
with
1\leq|\nu|\leq\beta
. Since the coefficients
c_{\nu}(\Sigma)
are uniformly bounded over
\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{+}_{d}(\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max})
, we conclude

|I_{1}|\;\lesssim\;(1+\Lambda^{\beta-1})\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}.

For
I_{2}
, we use
\|p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}=1
together with the
L
-Lipschitz continuity of
\Sigma\mapsto c_{\nu}(\Sigma)
from the PDE inversion condition, which gives
|c_{\nu}(\Sigma)-c_{\nu}(\Sigma^{\prime})|\leq L\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
. Applying the same derivative bounds as for
I_{1}
, we obtain

|I_{2}|\;\lesssim\;(1+\Lambda^{\beta-1})\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

Note that both bounds are now dimension-free, in contrast to those obtained in the proof of Theorem
3
.

Step 5”: Conclusion.
Substituting the bounds on
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
into Equation (
11
) and using Assumption
1
to replace
\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
with
\Psi^{-1}(\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1})
, we obtain

W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})\;\lesssim\;\frac{C_{0}}{\Lambda}+(1+\Lambda^{\beta-1})\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}+(1+\Lambda^{\beta-1})\Psi^{-1}(\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}).

Two cases arise depending on the size of
W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})
. If
W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})
is bounded away from zero, setting
\Lambda=1
and rearranging yields
\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\gtrsim\min\{W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{\beta},\,\Psi(CW_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{\beta})\}
directly. If instead
W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})
is small, setting
\Lambda\asymp W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{-1}
and rearranging leads to the same bound. In both cases, combining with Assumption
1
gives Equation (
10
).
∎

4
Examples

We now specialize the general inequalities from Section
3
to specific choices of the mixture kernel
f
. In particular, we focus on three canonical cases, namely Gaussian, Cauchy, and Laplace kernels, which exemplify the different assumptions considered in our theory.

4.1
Gaussian kernel

The multivariate Gaussian kernel, which is arguably the most widely adopted choice in applied mixture modeling, is given by

f(x\mid\theta,\Sigma)=\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(x-\theta)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\theta)\right)}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}|\Sigma|^{\frac{1}{2}}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

for some
(\theta,\Sigma)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{S}_{d}^{+}(\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max})
. The following theorem shows that it belongs to the class of super-smooth kernels and provides the corresponding explicit inverse bound.

Theorem 5
.

The multivariate Gaussian kernel is super-smooth of order
2
. Moreover, if
f
is Gaussian, there exists a constant
M>0
, depending only on
R
,
\lambda_{\min}
, and
\lambda_{\max}
, such that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold with

\displaystyle\Psi(t)=\exp\left(-M\frac{\log(1/t)}{t}\right),\quad\Xi(t)=t,\quad\text{and}\quad p=2.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\;\geq\;C^{\prime}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{C\exp\big(\tilde{C}/W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{2}\big)}{W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{2}}\right)\;+\;\exp\left(-M\frac{\log(1/\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}})}{\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}\right)\right],

for any
G=P\times\delta_{\Sigma}
and
G^{\prime}=P^{\prime}\times\delta_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
in
\mathscr{G}
, where
C,C^{\prime},\tilde{C}>0
depend on
R
,
\lambda_{\min}
,
\lambda_{\max}
, and
d
.

As a preview of the posterior contraction results in Section
5
, Theorem
5
shows how an
L^{1}
estimation rate
\omega_{n}
for
p_{G_{0}}
propagates to
P_{0}
and
\Sigma_{0}
. In particular, one obtains

W_{1}(P,P_{0})\lesssim\left(\log\log(1/\omega_{n})\right)^{-1/2},\quad\|\Sigma-\Sigma_{0}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\lesssim\frac{\log\log(1/\omega_{n})}{\log(1/\omega_{n})}.

These rates are quite slow, reflecting the strong smoothing effect induced by the Gaussian kernel. This will serve as a benchmark when comparing with other kernel choices.

Before moving on to other kernel types, we briefly focus on the Gaussian setting where the scale matrices under consideration are of the form
\Sigma=\sigma^{2}I_{d}
, so that
\mathscr{G}
consists of mixing measures
G=P\times\delta_{\sigma^{2}I_{d}}
with

Theorem 6

For an isotropic Gaussian mixture, there exist positive constants

\displaystyle\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\;\geq\;C^{\prime}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{C}{W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{2}}\right)\;+\;\exp\left(-M\frac{\log(1/|\sigma^{2}-\sigma^{\prime 2}|)}{|\sigma^{2}-\sigma^{\prime 2}|}\right)\right],

and
G^{\prime}=P^{\prime}\times\delta_{\sigma^{\prime 2}I_{d}}

.

While Theorem

4.2
Cauchy kernel

We next consider the multivariate Cauchy kernel,

f(x\mid\theta,\Sigma)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1+d}{2}\right)}{\pi^{\frac{d+1}{2}}|\Sigma|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left[1+(x-\theta)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\theta)\right]^{\frac{d+1}{2}}},

Theorem 7

The multivariate Cauchy kernel is super-smooth of order

2

\displaystyle\Psi(t)=t^{2},\quad\Xi(t)=\sqrt{t},\quad\text{and}\quad p=1.

\displaystyle\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\;\geq\;C^{\prime}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{C}{W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})}\right)+\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\right],

4.3
Laplace kernel

Finally, we consider the multivariate Laplace kernel

\displaystyle f(x\mid\theta,\Sigma)=\frac{2\left(\frac{(x-\theta)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\theta)}{2}\right)^{\frac{2-d}{2}}B_{\frac{d-2}{2}}\left(\sqrt{2(x-\theta)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\theta)}\right)}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}|\Sigma|^{\frac{1}{2}}},

Theorem 8

The multivariate Laplace kernel is ordinary-smooth of order

\displaystyle\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\;\geq\;C^{\prime}\left[W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{2}+\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\right],

5
Posterior asymptotics for Dirichlet process mixtures

Equipped with the general inequalities obtained in Section
3
and their specializations in Section
4
, we now derive posterior contraction results for mixing measures in Bayesian nonparametric homoscedastic location-scale mixture models. As discussed earlier, our approach proceeds in two steps:

1.

We verify an
L^{1}
posterior contraction rate
\omega_{n}
for density estimation, namely such that

\Pi\left(G:\|p_{G}-p_{G_{0}}\|_{1}\geq K\omega_{n}\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\right)\to 0

in
P_{G_{0}}^{n}
-probability for all large
K>0
as
n\to\infty
, for a given kernel
f
defining the density
p_{G}(x)=P*f(x\mid\cdot,\Sigma)
(as usual, with
G=P\times\delta_{\Sigma}
). Such results are well studied in the literature on Bayesian asymptotics and will be borrowed accordingly;

2.

We use the inverse inequalities developed in the previous sections to translate the
L^{1}
rate
\omega_{n}
into contraction rates for
W_{1}(P,P_{0})
and
\|\Sigma-\Sigma_{0}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
.

We provide explicit results for multivariate Gaussian mixtures and univariate Laplace mixtures under Dirichlet process priors, as these are settings where suitable
L^{1}
contraction rates are available. In contrast, we do not include any explicit result for Cauchy mixtures, despite the bounds obtained in Theorem
7
. This is because, to the best of our knowledge, no
L^{1}
posterior contraction results are currently available for infinite Cauchy mixtures. This gap appears to stem from limitations in the existing techniques used to estimate the entropy numbers of such model classes, which are in turn essential to establish posterior contraction rates (cf. the discussion in Section
2
). In particular, standard approaches, originating from
Ghosal and van der Vaart, 2001
, rely on approximating infinite mixtures by finitely supported ones via moment-matching strategies, which fail in their current form with heavy-tailed kernels. As a consequence, entropy bounds and posterior contraction rates for infinite Cauchy mixtures constitute an open problem which is beyond the scope of this article.

In the Gaussian case, combining our inequalities with Theorem 9.9 in
Ghosal and Van der Vaart, 2017

(
Tokdar, 2013
, see also)
, which establishes the
L^{1}
contraction rate
\omega_{n}=n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{(d+1)/2}
, yields the following result.

Proposition 2
.

Assume that
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots\overset{\textnormal{i.i.d.}}{\sim}p_{G_{0}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\cdot\mid\theta,\Sigma_{0})\,P_{0}(d\theta)
, where
f
is the
d
-variate Gaussian kernel,
P_{0}(\bar{B}(0,R_{0}))=1
for some
R_{0}\in(0,\infty)
, and
\Sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{+}_{d}(\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max})
for some
0<\lambda_{\min}<\lambda_{\max}<\infty
. Consider a homoscedastic Gaussian location-scale mixture prior
\Pi
on
G=P\times\delta_{\Sigma}
, where
P\sim\mathrm{DP}(a,H)
(for some
a>0
) with base measure
H
having a strictly positive and continuous density supported on
[-R,R]^{d}
for some
R\in[R_{0},\infty)
, and
\Sigma
has a prior with positive continuous density supported on
\mathbb{S}^{+}_{d}(\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max})
. Then the following statements hold true.

(a)

For
K>0
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\Pi\Bigg(G

\displaystyle\;:\;\exp\bigg(-\frac{C\exp\big(\tilde{C}/W_{1}(P,P_{0})^{2}\big)}{W_{1}(P,P_{0})^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle+\exp\bigg(-M\frac{\log(1/\|\Sigma-\Sigma_{0}\|_{\mathrm{op}})}{\|\Sigma-\Sigma_{0}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}\bigg)\;\geq\;Kn^{-1/2}(\log n)^{\frac{d+1}{2}}\;\Big|\;X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\Bigg)\to 0

in
P_{G_{0}}^{n}
-probability as
n\to\infty
, where the positive constants
M
,
C
, and
\tilde{C}
may depend on
R
,
\lambda_{\min}
,
\lambda_{\max}
, and
d
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\Pi\Bigg(G\,:\,W_{1}(P,P_{0})\geq K\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log\log n}}\;\Big|\;X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\to 0,

\displaystyle\Pi\Bigg(G\,:\,\|\Sigma-\Sigma_{0}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq K\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\;\Big|\;X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\to 0

in
P_{G_{0}}^{n}
-probability for all large
K>0
as
n\to\infty
.

(b)

In the isotropic Gaussian mixture setting, the above convergence statements may be replaced by

\displaystyle\Pi\Bigg(G

\displaystyle\;:\;\exp\bigg(-\frac{C}{W_{1}(P,P_{0})^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle+\exp\bigg(-M\frac{\log(1/|\sigma^{2}-\sigma_{0}^{2}|)}{|\sigma^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{0}|}\bigg)\;\geq\;Kn^{-1/2}(\log n)^{\frac{d+1}{2}}\;\Big|\;X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\Bigg)\to 0

and

\displaystyle\Pi\Bigg(G\,:\,W_{1}(P,P_{0})\geq K\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log n}}\;\Big|\;X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\to 0,

\displaystyle\Pi\Bigg(G\,:\,|\sigma^{2}-\sigma_{0}^{2}|\geq K\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\;\Big|\;X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\to 0.

in
P_{G_{0}}^{n}
-probability as
n\to\infty
.

Proposition
2
highlights a key feature of the Gaussian setting. Despite the essentially parametric
L^{1}
rate for density estimation, the super-smooth nature of the Gaussian kernel in general leads to slow, double logarithmic rates for recovering the mixing measure
P_{0}
and logarithmic rates for the scale matrix
\Sigma_{0}
. The double logarithmic rate is however turned into a milder (but nonetheless slow) logarithmic one in the isotropic case. On the other hand, the dependence on the dimension

, which appears at the exponent of the logarithmic factor in the

rate, only affects lower-order terms and does not influence the leading behavior of the
W_{1}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}
rates (in the fixed-

regime considered here). Finally, Proposition
2
highlights that the rate of recovery for
\Sigma_{0}

5.1
Mixing measure priors beyond the Dirichlet process

We conclude this section by noting that, although the results above are stated for Dirichlet process mixtures, the underlying methodology is not restricted to this specific prior choice. The arguments rely on two ingredients: (i) the inverse inequalities developed in Sections
3
and
4
, and (ii) available
L^{1}
posterior contraction rates for the corresponding mixture models. Since the inequalities are general, they can be combined with contraction results for a wide range of alternative priors on the mixing measure.

Examples include Pitman–Yor processes
(
Perman et al., 1992
;
Pitman and Yor, 1997
)
, normalized inverse-Gaussian processes
(
Lijoi et al., 2005a
)
, more general normalized random measures with independent increments
(
Kingman, 1967
;
Regazzini et al., 2003
;
Nieto-Barajas et al., 2004
;
Lijoi and Prünster, 2010
)
, Gibbs-type priors
(
Gnedin and Pitman, 2006
;
De Blasi et al., 2015
)
, and species sampling models
(
Kingman, 1978
;
Pitman, 1996
)
. For instance, the inequalities developed here could be combined with the contraction rates established in
Scricciolo, 2014
for Pitman–Yor and normalized inverse-Gaussian mixtures. We do not pursue these extensions further, as they do not introduce additional conceptual difficulties given the generality of our framework.

6
Discussion

We studied posterior contraction for mixing measures in homoscedastic location-scale mixture models with infinitely many components. We developed a general approach to derive lower-bounds for the
L^{1}
distance between mixture densities in terms of discrepancies between the corresponding mixing measures. Our method relies on the dual formulation of the
W_{1}
distance in terms of 1-Lipschitz functions, combined with functional approximation and Fourier inversion techniques, and yields inequalities whose strength reflects the smoothness of the mixture kernel and, in the ordinary-smooth case, the applicability of a novel PDE inversion condition. These results provide a unified route from
L^{1}
convergence to contraction at the level of both the mixing measure and the shared scale parameter, and apply to a range of commonly used kernels in the context of Dirichlet process mixtures.

In particular, we point out a connection with deconvolution problems, which have motivated much of the previous work on mixing measure convergence, as they can be framed, in the mixture setting, as the recovery of a signal distributed according to the unknown mixing measure when observations are contaminated by additive noise drawn from the mixture kernel
(
Gao and van der Vaart, 2016
;
Scricciolo, 2018
;
Rousseau and Scricciolo, 2024
)
. As noted earlier, existing contributions focus on the location-only mixture case, which in deconvolution terminology corresponds to the setting where the noise scale is known. Our results can be interpreted as characterizing recovery of the mixing measure in the more challenging scenario where the noise scale is also unknown. Moreover, in contrast to other multivariate deconvolution analyses
(
Rousseau and Scricciolo, 2024
)
, our approach does not rely on independence assumptions across noise coordinates and instead accommodates general covariance structures. New results on
L^{1}
density estimation for infinite Cauchy mixtures would also make it possible, when combined with our inequality in Theorem
7
, to fully characterize the currently unexplored problem of deconvolution with heavy-tailed Cauchy error distribution.

The functional-analytic techniques we developed to handle the dual formulation of the
W_{1}
metric are quite general and open several directions for future work. A natural extension is to heteroscedastic location-scale mixtures, where the scale parameter varies across components. In addition, our results for different kernel choices reveal significant heterogeneity, with Laplace mixtures achieving substantially faster rates than Gaussian mixtures. This raises the question of whether, when the primary objects of interest are the mixing measure and the scale (as in the deconvolution problems discussed above), it may be advantageous to use a misspecified kernel with lower smoothness, potentially improving recovery rates—perhaps up to an approximation error—when the true kernel is very smooth. A natural setting to investigate this question is the class of exponential power kernels considered for density estimation by
Scricciolo, 2011
, where the smoothness can be tuned continuously and misspecification can be studied in a systematic way. Finally, it would be of interest to extend our results to models with more complex prior structures on the mixing measure, such as those inducing dependence across multiple mixing measures
(
Teh et al., 2006
;
Rodriguez et al., 2008
;
Lijoi et al., 2014
;
Franzolini et al., 2025
, among many others)
or repulsion across atoms to achieve well-separated components
(
Petralia et al., 2012
;
Xu et al., 2016
)
.

Recall that the probability measures

. The shift also leaves the optimal transport integral unchanged, which implies that

By Hölder’s inequality,

|I_{1}|\leq\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\,\|\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}^{*}\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}^{*}\|_{\infty}.

(32)

We bound
\|\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}^{*}\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}^{*}\|_{\infty}
by the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}^{*}\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq|c_{0}(\Sigma)|\,\|\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}^{*}\|_{\infty}+\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\,:\,1\leq|\nu|\leq\beta}|c_{\nu}(\Sigma)|\,\|\partial^{\nu}\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}^{*}\|_{\infty}.

Applying the Fourier transform to both sides of
\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}p_{G}=P
and evaluating at
\xi=0
(where
\Phi_{p_{G}}(0)=\Phi_{P}(0)=1
) gives
c_{0}(\Sigma)\equiv 1
. By Young’s inequality and
\|\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq 2R
(see the proof of Lemma
3
), we have
\|\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq 2R
. For the higher-order terms, we claim that
\|\partial^{\nu}\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{\mathrm{der}}\Lambda^{|\nu|-1}
for all
\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
such that
1\leq|\nu|\leq\beta
and some
C_{\mathrm{der}}<\infty
. Indeed, fixing any canonical direction
e_{m}
with
\nu_{m}\geq 1
and writing
\nu^{\prime}=\nu-e_{m}
, the convolution structure gives

\partial^{\nu}\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}^{*}=(\partial_{x_{m}}\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*})*(\partial^{\nu^{\prime}}\psi_{\Lambda}).

By Young’s inequality and
\|\nabla\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq 1+2RM_{\eta}
(Lemma
3
),

\|\partial^{\nu}\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq(1+2RM_{\eta})\|\partial^{\nu^{\prime}}\psi_{\Lambda}\|_{1}.

A change of variables and the chain rule give
\|\partial^{\nu^{\prime}}\psi_{\Lambda}\|_{1}=\Lambda^{|\nu^{\prime}|}\|\partial^{\nu^{\prime}}\psi\|_{1}\leq C_{\psi,\beta}\Lambda^{|\nu|-1}
, where
C_{\psi,\beta}:=\max_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:1\leq|\nu^{\prime}|\leq\beta}\|\partial^{\nu^{\prime}}\psi\|_{1}<\infty
. Hence
C_{\mathrm{der}}:=C_{\psi,\beta}(1+2RM_{\eta})
.

Since the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
lie in
[\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}]
and the coefficients
c_{\nu}(\Sigma)
depend smoothly on
\Sigma
, we have
\max_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:1\leq|\nu|\leq\beta}|c_{\nu}(\Sigma)|\leq M_{\beta}<\infty
(where
M_{\beta}
is independent of
\Sigma
). Setting
C_{1}^{\prime\prime}:=C_{\mathrm{der}}M_{\beta}\binom{d+\beta}{d}+2R
, for
\Lambda\geq 1
we obtain

\|\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}^{*}\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{1}^{\prime\prime}(1+\Lambda^{\beta-1}),

and therefore

|I_{1}|\leq C_{1}^{\prime\prime}(1+\Lambda^{\beta-1})\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}.

(33)

Bound on
I_{2}

By Hölder’s inequality,

|I_{2}|\leq\|(\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}^{*}-\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}^{*})\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}^{*}\|_{\infty}.

(34)

The Lipschitz continuity of the coefficients
\Sigma\mapsto c_{\nu}(\Sigma)
gives
|c_{\nu}(\Sigma)-c_{\nu}(\Sigma^{\prime})|\leq L\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
uniformly in
\nu
. Combining with the derivative bound obtained before and setting
C_{2}^{\prime\prime}:=CL\binom{d+\beta}{d}
, we get

\displaystyle\|(\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}^{*}-\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}^{*})\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}^{*}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,1\leq|\nu|\leq\beta}|c_{\nu}(\Sigma)-c_{\nu}(\Sigma^{\prime})|\,\|\partial^{\nu}\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X},\Lambda}^{*}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}^{\prime\prime}\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}(1+\Lambda^{\beta-1}),

(35)

and therefore
|I_{2}|\leq C_{2}^{\prime\prime}\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}(1+\Lambda^{\beta-1})
.

Conclusion

Combining Equations (
31
), (
33
), and (
35
), and using
\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\Psi^{-1}(\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}/C_{0})
for some
C_{0}>0
(by Assumption
1
), we obtain, for
\Lambda\geq 1
,

\displaystyle W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})\leq\frac{C_{1}^{\prime}}{\Lambda}+C_{1}^{\prime\prime}(1+\Lambda^{\beta-1})\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}+C_{2}^{\prime\prime}\Psi^{-1}(\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}/C_{0})(1+\Lambda^{\beta-1}).

(36)

We now distinguish two cases based on the size of
W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})
.

Case 1:
W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})\geq 2C_{1}^{\prime}
.

Setting
\Lambda=1
in Equation (
36
) and using
W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})-C_{1}^{\prime}\geq W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})/2
, we get

W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})\leq 4C_{1}^{\prime\prime}\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}+4C_{2}^{\prime\prime}\Psi^{-1}(\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}/C_{0}).

Hence either
W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})\leq 8C_{1}^{\prime\prime}\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}
or
W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})\leq 8C_{2}^{\prime\prime}\Psi^{-1}(\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}/C_{0})
. Since
\mathrm{supp}(P),\mathrm{supp}(P^{\prime})\subseteq\bar{B}(0,R)
implies
W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})\leq 2R
, we have
W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})\leq(2R)^{1-\beta}W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{\beta}
. Combining, there exist constants
C_{\mathrm{I}}^{\prime\prime}
and
c_{\mathrm{I}}^{\prime\prime}
such that

\displaystyle\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\geq C_{\mathrm{I}}^{\prime\prime}\min\Big\{W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{\beta},\,\Psi\big(c_{\mathrm{I}}^{\prime\prime}\,W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{\beta}\big)\Big\}.

(37)

Case 2:
W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})<2C_{1}^{\prime}
.

Setting
\Lambda=2C_{1}^{\prime}/W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})>1
in Equation (
36
) and letting
C_{1,\mathrm{II}}^{\prime\prime}:=4C_{1}^{\prime\prime}(2C_{1}^{\prime})^{\beta-1}
and
C_{2,\mathrm{II}}^{\prime\prime}:=4C_{2}^{\prime\prime}(2C_{1}^{\prime})^{\beta-1}
, we obtain

1\leq C_{1,\mathrm{II}}^{\prime\prime}W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{-\beta}\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}+C_{2,\mathrm{II}}^{\prime\prime}W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{-\beta}\Psi^{-1}(\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}/C_{0}).

Hence either the first or the second summand is at least
1/2
, yielding respectively
\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\geq W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{\beta}/(2C_{1,\mathrm{II}}^{\prime\prime})
or
\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\geq C_{0}\,\Psi(W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{\beta}/(2C_{2,\mathrm{II}}^{\prime\prime}))
. Combining, there exist constants
C_{\mathrm{II}}^{\prime\prime}
and
c_{\mathrm{II}}^{\prime\prime}
such that

\displaystyle\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\geq C_{\mathrm{II}}^{\prime\prime}\min\Big\{W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{\beta},\,\Psi\big(c_{\mathrm{II}}^{\prime\prime}\,W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{\beta}\big)\Big\}.

(38)

Combining Equations (
37
) and (
38
) with Assumption
1
, we conclude that

\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\gtrsim\min\Big\{W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{\beta},\,\Psi\big(C\,W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})^{\beta}\big)\Big\}+\Psi(\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}),

where the implicit constant and
C
depend on
R
,
\lambda_{\min}
,
\lambda_{\max}
, and
d
. This completes the proof.

Proof of Theorem
5

We split the proof in four parts, as follows.

Verification of Assumption
2
.

The characteristic function of the Gaussian kernel is
\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)=\exp(-\xi^{\top}\Sigma\xi/2)
. Setting
\psi(t):=e^{-t/2}
, so that
\psi^{\prime}(t)=-\psi(t)/2
, the mean value theorem gives a
\tau\in(0,1)
such that

\Phi_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(\xi)-\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)=\psi(\xi^{\top}\Sigma^{\prime}\xi)-\psi(\xi^{\top}\Sigma\xi)=\psi^{\prime}(\xi^{\top}\Sigma(\tau)\xi)\cdot\xi^{\top}(\Sigma^{\prime}-\Sigma)\xi,

where
\Sigma(\tau)=(1-\tau)\Sigma^{\prime}+\tau\Sigma
. Hence,

\displaystyle|\Phi_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(\xi)-\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\,\psi(\xi^{\top}\Sigma(\tau)\xi)\,\|\Sigma^{\prime}-\Sigma\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\xi\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\max\big\{|\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|,|\Phi_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(\xi)|\big\}\|\Sigma^{\prime}-\Sigma\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\xi\|^{2},

so Assumption
2
holds with
p=2
and
\Xi(t)=t
.

Verification of Assumption
1
: univariate case.

Consider two univariate mixture densities

p_{G}(x)=\int f(x\mid\theta,\sigma^{2})\,P(d\theta)\qquad\text{and}\qquad p_{G^{\prime}}(x)=\int f(x\mid\theta,\sigma^{\prime 2})\,P^{\prime}(d\theta),

where
f(\cdot\mid\theta,\sigma^{2})
denotes the univariate Gaussian kernel with mean
\theta
and variance
\sigma^{2}
. Without loss of generality, suppose
\sigma^{2}\geq\sigma^{\prime 2}
and set
\gamma^{2}:=\sigma^{2}-\sigma^{\prime 2}
. By the semigroup property of Gaussian convolutions,
f(\cdot\mid 0,\sigma^{2})=f(\cdot\mid 0,\gamma^{2})*f(\cdot\mid 0,\sigma^{\prime 2})
, so that

p_{G}=P*f(\cdot\mid 0,\sigma^{2})=\big(P*f(\cdot\mid 0,\gamma^{2})\big)*f(\cdot\mid 0,\sigma^{\prime 2})=:\tilde{P}*f(\cdot\mid 0,\sigma^{\prime 2}),

where
\tilde{P}
is the distribution of
X+\gamma Z
with
X\sim P
and
Z\sim f(\cdot\mid 0,1)
, independent of each other.

For a positive integer
k
to be specified later, consider the Hermite polynomial

H_{2k}(x;\sigma^{\prime 2}):=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim f(\cdot\mid 0,1)}\big[(x+i\sigma^{\prime}Z)^{2k}\big].

By Lemma
6
,
\int_{\mathbb{R}}H_{2k}(x;\sigma^{\prime 2})\,f(x\mid\theta,\sigma^{\prime 2})\,dx=\theta^{2k}
, so that

\displaystyle\Delta M_{2k}

\displaystyle:=\int\theta^{2k}\,\tilde{P}(d\theta)-\int\theta^{2k}\,P^{\prime}(d\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}H_{2k}(x;\sigma^{\prime 2})\big(p_{G}(x)-p_{G^{\prime}}(x)\big)\,dx.

We now lower-bound
\Delta M_{2k}
. Since
\tilde{P}
is the law of
X+\gamma Z
,

\displaystyle\int\theta^{2k}\,\tilde{P}(d\theta)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{2k}{2j}\mathbb{E}_{P}\big[X^{2k-2j}\big]\gamma^{2j}\mathbb{E}\big[Z^{2j}\big]\geq\gamma^{2k}(2k-1)!!.

Using
(2k)!\geq(2k/e)^{2k}
and
k!\leq k^{k}
, we get
(2k-1)!!=\frac{(2k)!}{2^{k}k!}\geq(2k/e^{2})^{k}
. Since
P^{\prime}
is supported on
[-R,R]
, we have
\int\theta^{2k}\,P^{\prime}(d\theta)\leq R^{2k}
, and therefore

\Delta M_{2k}\geq\left(\frac{2k}{e^{2}}\right)^{k}\gamma^{2k}-R^{2k}.

(39)

To obtain an upper-bound on
\Delta M_{2k}
, set
V:=\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}
and
\Lambda:=R+L
, for a
L>0
to be specified. From the definition of
H_{2k}
,

|H_{2k}(x;\sigma^{\prime 2})|\leq 2^{k-1}\big(x^{2k}+\sigma^{\prime 2k}(2k-1)!!\big)\leq 2^{k-1}\big(x^{2k}+\lambda_{\max}^{k}2^{k}k^{k}\big).

For any choice of
|x|>\Lambda
,
\theta\in[-R,R]
and
\sigma\in[\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}]
, the Gaussian kernel satisfies
f(x\mid\theta,\sigma^{2})\leq C_{0}\exp(-(|x|-R)^{2}/2\lambda_{\max}^{2})
with
C_{0}=(2\pi\lambda_{\min}^{2})^{-1/2}
, so that
|p_{G}(x)|,|p_{G^{\prime}}(x)|\leq C_{0}\exp(-(|x|-R)^{2}/2\lambda_{\max}^{2})
. Splitting the integral at
|x|=\Lambda
and applying Lemma
7
to bound the tail, we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta M_{2k}\leq C_{2}^{k}\big(V+e^{-L^{2}/2\lambda_{\max}^{2}}\big)\big(R^{2k}+L^{2k}+k^{k}\big),

(40)

where
C_{2}>0
depends only on
\lambda_{\min}
,
\lambda_{\max}
, and
R
. Combining Equations (
39
) and (
40
) gives

k^{k}\gamma^{2k}\leq R_{1}^{k}+C_{3}^{k}\big(V+e^{-L^{2}/2\lambda_{\max}^{2}}\big)\big(L^{2k}+k^{k}+R^{2k}\big),

(41)

where
R_{1}:=(Re)^{2}/2
and
C_{3}:=C_{2}e^{2}/2
.

We now tune
L
and
k
. Set
L:=\lambda_{\max}(2\log(1/V))^{1/2}
, so that
e^{-L^{2}/2\lambda_{\max}^{2}}=V
and

k^{k}\gamma^{2k}\leq R_{1}^{k}+2C_{3}^{k}V\big(R^{2k}+(2\lambda_{\max}^{2})^{k}(\log(1/V))^{k}+k^{k}\big).

(42)

Set
k:=\lfloor\log(1/V)/\log\log(1/V)\rfloor
. If
V<e^{-e}
, one has
k\leq\log(1/V)
, so that
V(\log(1/V))^{k}\leq 1
and
Vk^{k}\leq 1
. Substituting into (
42
) and absorbing all terms into a geometric bound yields

\gamma^{2}\leq C_{4}\frac{\log\log(1/V)}{\log(1/V)},

where
C_{4}:=R_{1}+4C_{3}R^{2}+2C_{3}+4C_{3}\lambda_{\max}^{2}
. If
V\leq e^{-e^{8C_{4}}}
(so that
\log\log(1/V)\geq 8C_{4}
) then, using
e^{t}\geq t^{2}/2
, we have that
C_{4}\log\log(1/V)\leq(\log(1/V))^{1/2}
, and thus
\gamma^{2}\leq(\log(1/V))^{-1/2}
. Hence and

V\geq\exp\!\left(-2C_{4}\frac{\log(1/\gamma^{2})}{\gamma^{2}}\right),

(43)

whenever
V\leq C_{5}:=\min\!\left\{e^{-e},e^{-e^{8C_{4}}}\right\}.

On the other hand, if
V\geq C_{5}
, then, noting that the function
t\mapsto t\log t
attains its minimum
-e^{-1}
at
t=e^{-1}
, we have that

V\geq C_{5}\geq C_{6}\exp\!\left(-2C_{4}\frac{\log(1/\gamma^{2})}{\gamma^{2}}\right),

(44)

where
C_{6}:=C_{5}e^{2C_{4}/e}
.
Combining Equations (
43
) and (
44
) yields, with
M:=2C_{4}
and
C^{\prime}:=\min\{1,C_{6}\}
,

\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\geq C^{\prime}\exp\!\left(-M\frac{\log(1/\gamma^{2})}{\gamma^{2}}\right).

(45)

Thus, Assumption
1
is verified in the univariate case with
\Psi(t)=\exp(-M\log(1/t)/t)
.

Verification of Assumption
1
: multivariate case.

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|u\|=1
be the principal unit eigenvector of
\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}
, so that
u^{\top}(\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime})u=\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
. Define the projected mixture densities

p_{G,u}(y):=\int f\big(y\mid u^{\top}\theta,\,u^{\top}\Sigma u\big)\,P(d\theta),\qquad p_{G^{\prime},u}(y):=\int f\big(y\mid u^{\top}\theta,\,u^{\top}\Sigma^{\prime}u\big)\,P^{\prime}(d\theta),

which represent the marginal densities of
u^{\top}X
under
p_{G}
and
p_{G^{\prime}}
respectively. By Lemma
8
,

\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\geq\|p_{G,u}-p_{G^{\prime},u}\|_{1}.

Setting
\sigma^{2}:=u^{\top}\Sigma u
,
\sigma^{\prime 2}:=u^{\top}\Sigma^{\prime}u
, and
\gamma^{2}:=|\sigma^{2}-\sigma^{\prime 2}|=\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
, the univariate bound (
45
) gives

\|p_{G,u}-p_{G^{\prime},u}\|_{1}\geq C^{\prime}\exp\!\left(-M\frac{\log(1/\gamma^{2})}{\gamma^{2}}\right)=C^{\prime}\exp\!\left(-M\frac{\log(1/\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}})}{\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}\right),

which establishes Assumption
1
with
\Psi(t)=\exp(-M\log(1/t)/t)
.

Conclusion.

With Assumptions 1 and 2 verified for
\Psi(t)=\exp(-M\log(1/t)/t)
,
\Xi(t)=t
,
p=2
, and super-smoothness of order
\alpha=2
being obvious given the form of the Gaussian characteristic function, an application of Theorem
2
completes the proof.

Proof of Theorem
6

Before delving into the details of the proof, we remark that the key challenge in generalizing the argument below to the case of general
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
matrices is due to the fact that, if we were to move from
\Sigma
to
\Sigma^{\prime}
using the semi-group property, we would need to guarantee that
\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}
is a positive definite matrix, which is not always true unless we have further knowledge on the structure of
\Sigma
. For instance, as we do below,
\Sigma=\sigma^{2}I_{d}
works to this end, as positive-definiteness of
\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}
can be guaranteed by assuming, without loss of generality, that
\sigma>\sigma^{\prime}
.

Therefore, without loss of generality, assume that
\sigma>\sigma^{\prime}
. Define the variance gap
\Delta:=\sigma^{2}-\sigma^{\prime 2}>0
and
\widetilde{P}=P*f(\cdot\mid 0,\Delta\cdot I_{d})
, so that
\widetilde{P}
is a continuous probability measure with a smooth density given by

\widetilde{P}(dy)=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{(2\pi\Delta)^{d/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|y-\theta\|^{2}}{2\Delta}\right)P(d\theta)\right)dy.

Due to the Gaussian semigroup property, we have
p_{G}(x)\equiv\int f(x\mid\theta,\sigma^{\prime 2}I_{d})\widetilde{P}(d\theta)
. To estimate
W_{1}(P,\tilde{P})
, we consider the coupling
(X,Y)
such that
X\sim P
,
Y=X+Z
where
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Delta\cdot I_{d})
and independent from
X
, which means that

W_{1}(P,\widetilde{P})\leq\mathbb{E}[\|Y-X\|_{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\|Z\|_{2}]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\|Z\|_{2}^{2}]}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\Delta I_{d})}=\sqrt{d\Delta}.

Therefore, an application of the triangle inequality leads to

\displaystyle W_{1}(P,P^{\prime})\leq W_{1}(P,\widetilde{P})+W_{1}(\widetilde{P},P^{\prime})\leq\sqrt{d\Delta}+W_{1}(\widetilde{P},P^{\prime}).

(46)

Using Lemma
10
, there exists a function
h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, with
h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(0)=0
, which is a solution of Kantorovich-Rubinstein dual problem:

W_{1}(\widetilde{P},P^{\prime})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)(\widetilde{P}(d\theta)-P^{\prime}(d\theta)).

Lemma
2
guarantees the existence of a cutoff function
\eta:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
in
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
which takes values in
[0,1]
, evaluates identically to
1
on the closed ball
\bar{B}(0,R)
(where
P^{\prime}
is supported), and vanishes outside the open ball
B(0,2R)
. Hence, given
\eta
, we denote

\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(x):=h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(x)\eta(x)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. As we show in Lemma
3
,
\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and it is globally
(1+2RM_{\eta})
-Lipschitz continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
M_{\eta}=\|\nabla\eta\|_{\infty}
. By the properties of
\eta
and noting that the support of
P
and
P^{\prime}
are in
B(0,R)
, we also clearly have that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)(\widetilde{P}(d\theta)-P^{\prime}(d\theta))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)-\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta))(\widetilde{P}(d\theta)-P^{\prime}(d\theta))+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)(\widetilde{P}(d\theta)-P^{\prime}(d\theta))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash B(0,R)}(h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)-\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta))(\widetilde{P}(d\theta)-P^{\prime}(d\theta))+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)(\widetilde{P}(d\theta)-P^{\prime}(d\theta))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash B(0,R)}(h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)-\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta))\widetilde{P}(d\theta)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)(\widetilde{P}(d\theta)-P^{\prime}(d\theta))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash B(0,R)}(h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)-\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta))(\widetilde{P}(d\theta)-P(d\theta))+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)(\widetilde{P}(d\theta)-P^{\prime}(d\theta)).

It is clear that
h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)-\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)=h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)(1-\eta(\theta))
. Therefore,
\nabla(h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)-\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta))=(1-\eta(\theta))\nabla h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)-h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)\nabla\eta(\theta)
, so that

\|\nabla(h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}-\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}})\|_{\infty}\leq\|(1-\eta)\nabla h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}\|_{\infty}+\|h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}\nabla\eta\|_{\infty}.

Since
h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have
|h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)|\leq\|\theta\|_{2}
. Furthermore, as
\eta
is stricly supported on
B(0,2R)
, we have

\|h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}\nabla\eta\|_{\infty}\leq 2RM_{\eta}.

Furthermore, as
\|\nabla h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
and
\eta\in[0,1]
, we have

\|(1-\eta)\nabla h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}\|_{\infty}\leq 1.

Putting these bounds together, we arrive at

\|\nabla(h^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}-\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}})\|_{\infty}\leq 1+2RM_{\eta}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash B(0,R)}({h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)-\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta))(\widetilde{P}(d\theta)-P(d\theta))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}({h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)-\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta))(\widetilde{P}(d\theta)-P(d\theta))

\displaystyle\leq(1+2RM_{\eta})W_{1}(\widetilde{P},P),

(47)

where the final inequality is due to the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality theorem while the equality is due to
{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)=\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\theta)
when
\theta\in B(0,R)
.

Moving to
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widetilde{h}^{*}(\theta)(\widetilde{P}(d\theta)-P^{\prime}(d\theta))
, we can bound this term using the spectral bandlimiting via the Jackson kernel as in the proof of Theorem
2
. In particular, we consider the multivariate Jackson kernel
(
DeVore and Lorentz, 1993
;
Butzer and Nessel, 1971
)
.

K(x):=\bigg(\frac{3}{8\pi\sqrt{d}}\bigg)^{d}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathrm{sinc}^{4}\bigg(\frac{x_{i}}{4\sqrt{d}}\bigg),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\mathrm{sinc}(u):=\sin u/u
, which by construction satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x)dx=1
. For any
\Lambda>0
, define the function
K_{\Lambda}(x):=\Lambda^{d}K(\Lambda x)
. By Lemma
4
,
\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{F}[K])\subseteq\bar{B}(0,1)
, which implies that
\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{F}[K_{\Lambda}])\subseteq\bar{B}(0,\Lambda)
. Consider the function

Proof of Theorem
7

We split the proof in four parts, as follows.

Verification of Assumption
2
.

The characteristic function of the Cauchy kernel is
\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)=\exp(-\sqrt{\xi^{\top}\Sigma\xi})
. Setting
\psi(t):=e^{-t}
, so that
\psi^{\prime}(t)=-\psi(t)
, the mean value theorem gives a
\tau\in(0,1)
such that

\Phi_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(\xi)-\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)=\psi^{\prime}\!\left((\xi^{\top}\Sigma(\tau)\xi)^{1/2}\right)\left((\xi^{\top}\Sigma^{\prime}\xi)^{1/2}-(\xi^{\top}\Sigma\xi)^{1/2}\right),

where
\Sigma(\tau):=(1-\tau)\Sigma^{\prime}+\tau\Sigma
. Using the inequality
|\sqrt{a}-\sqrt{b}|\leq\sqrt{|a-b|}
, we get

\displaystyle|\Phi_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(\xi)-\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq\psi\!\left((\xi^{\top}\Sigma(\tau)\xi)^{1/2}\right)|\xi^{\top}(\Sigma^{\prime}-\Sigma)\xi|^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\max\big\{|\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|,|\Phi_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(\xi)|\big\}\|\Sigma^{\prime}-\Sigma\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}\|\xi\|,

so Assumption
2
holds with
C=1
,
p=1
, and
\Xi(t)=\sqrt{t}
.

Verification of Assumption
1
: univariate case.

Consider two univariate mixture densities

p_{G}(x)=\int f(x\mid\theta,\sigma)\,P(d\theta)\qquad\text{and}\qquad p_{G^{\prime}}(x)=\int f(x\mid\theta,\sigma^{\prime})\,P^{\prime}(d\theta),

where
f(\cdot\mid\theta,\sigma)
is the univariate Cauchy kernel with location
\theta
and scale
\sigma
. Without loss of generality, suppose
\sigma^{\prime}>\sigma
. Setting
V:=\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}
, since
\mathcal{F}[p_{G}](\xi)=\Phi_{P}(\xi)\exp(-\sigma|\xi|)
,

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{Re}\big(\mathcal{F}[p_{G}](\xi)\big)-\mathrm{Re}\big(\mathcal{F}[p_{G^{\prime}}](\xi)\big)

\displaystyle=\exp(-\sigma|\xi|)\int\cos(\xi\theta)\,P(d\theta)-\exp(-\sigma^{\prime}|\xi|)\int\cos(\xi\theta)\,P^{\prime}(d\theta).

Using
1-\xi^{2}\theta^{2}/2\leq\cos(\xi\theta)\leq 1
and
|\theta|\leq R
for
\theta\in\mathrm{supp}(P)\cup\mathrm{supp}(P^{\prime})
,

1-\tfrac{1}{2}R^{2}\xi^{2}\leq\int\cos(\xi\theta)\,P(d\theta)\leq 1.

(56)

Hence, for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle\geq\exp(-\sigma|\xi|)\left(1-\tfrac{1}{2}R^{2}\xi^{2}\right)-\exp(-\sigma^{\prime}|\xi|)

\displaystyle=\exp(-\sigma|\xi|)\big(1-\exp(-(\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma)|\xi|)\big)-\tfrac{1}{2}R^{2}\xi^{2}\exp(-\sigma|\xi|).

(57)

Applying
1-e^{-x}\geq x-x^{2}/2
for
x\geq 0
and
e^{-\sigma|\xi|}\geq 1-\sigma|\xi|
to the first term, and
e^{-\sigma|\xi|}\leq 1
to the second, we obtain for
|\xi|\leq 2/(\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma)
,

V\geq|\xi|(\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma)-\xi^{2}\left(\sigma(\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma)+\tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma)^{2}+R^{2}\right).

The right-hand side is maximized at

|\xi|=\xi_{0}:=\frac{\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma}{2\sigma(\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma)+(\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma)^{2}+2R^{2}},

which satisfies
\xi_{0}\leq 2/(\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma)
. Substituting and simplifying,

V\geq\frac{(\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma)^{2}}{4\sigma(\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma)+2(\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma)^{2}+4R^{2}}\geq\frac{(\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma)^{2}}{6\lambda_{\max}^{2}+4R^{2}}=:C_{0}(\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma)^{2}.

This confirms Assumption
1
in the univariate case with
\Psi(t)=t^{2}
.

Verification of Assumption
1
: multivariate case.

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the principal eigenvector (thus, of unit norm) of
\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}
, so that
u^{\top}(\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime})u=\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
. Define the projected mixture densities

\displaystyle p_{G,u}(y)

\displaystyle:=\int f\big(y\mid u^{\top}\theta,\,(u^{\top}\Sigma u)^{1/2}\big)\,P(d\theta),

\displaystyle p_{G^{\prime},u}(y)

u^{\top}X
under
p_{G}
and
p_{G^{\prime}}

8
,

\|p_{G}-p_{G^{\prime}}\|_{1}\geq\|p_{G,u}-p_{G^{\prime},u}\|_{1}.

\sigma:=(u^{\top}\Sigma u)^{1/2}

\sigma^{\prime}:=(u^{\top}\Sigma^{\prime}u)^{1/2}

. Then,

with

Proof of Theorem
8

We split the proof into five parts, as follows.

PDE inversion condition.

The characteristic function of the multivariate Laplace kernel is
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)=(1+\frac{1}{2}\xi^{\top}\Sigma\xi)^{-1}
, so
\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)^{-1}=1+\frac{1}{2}\xi^{\top}\Sigma\xi
. By the linearity of the Fourier transform and the identity
\mathcal{F}[\partial^{\nu}p_{G}](\xi)=(-i)^{|\nu|}\xi^{\nu}\mathcal{F}[p_{G}](\xi)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}p_{G}](\xi)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}[p_{G}](\xi)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\Sigma_{jk}(-\xi_{j}\xi_{k})\mathcal{F}[p_{G}](\xi)=\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\xi^{\top}\Sigma\xi\right)\mathcal{F}[p_{G}](\xi)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)^{-1}\mathcal{F}[p_{G}](\xi)=\mathcal{F}[P](\xi),

where the last equality uses
\mathcal{F}[p_{G}](\xi)=\Phi_{P}(\xi)\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)
. By uniqueness of the Fourier transform,
\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}p_{G}=P
in the sense of distributions, and similarly
\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}p_{G^{\prime}}=P^{\prime}
. The Lipschitz continuity condition on the coefficients is immediate, since the only non-constant coefficients are
c_{\nu}(\Sigma)=-\Sigma_{jk}/2
for
\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
with
|\nu|=2
, which depend linearly on
\Sigma
.

Verification of Assumption
2
.

Setting
\psi(t):=(1+t/2)^{-1}
, so that
|\psi^{\prime}(t)|=(1/2)|\psi(t)|^{2}\leq(1/2)|\psi(t)|
, the mean value theorem implies that

\displaystyle|\Phi_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(\xi)-\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|

\displaystyle=|\psi^{\prime}(\xi^{\top}\Sigma(\tau)\xi)|\cdot|\xi^{\top}(\Sigma^{\prime}-\Sigma)\xi|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\,\psi(\xi^{\top}\Sigma(\tau)\xi)\,\|\Sigma^{\prime}-\Sigma\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\xi\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\max\big\{|\Phi_{\Sigma}(\xi)|,|\Phi_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(\xi)|\big\}\|\Sigma^{\prime}-\Sigma\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\xi\|^{2},

for some
\tau\in(0,1)
, where we set
\Sigma(\tau):=(1-\tau)\Sigma^{\prime}+\tau\Sigma
. Hence Assumption
2
holds with
p=2
and
\Xi(t)=t
.

Verification of Assumption
1
: univariate case.

Consider two univariate mixtures

p_{G}(x)=\int f(x\mid\theta,\sigma)\,P(d\theta)\qquad\text{and}\qquad p_{G^{\prime}}(x)=\int f(x\mid\theta,\sigma^{\prime})\,P^{\prime}(d\theta),

where
f(\cdot\mid\theta,\sigma)
is the univariate Laplace kernel with location
\theta
and scale
\sigma
. The PDE inversion condition gives
\mathcal{T}_{\sigma}p_{G}=P
and
\mathcal{T}_{\sigma^{\prime}}p_{G^{\prime}}=P^{\prime}
, where
\mathcal{T}_{\sigma}:=\mathrm{Id}-\frac{\sigma}{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}

Let

be a non-negative bump function supported on
(R+1,R+2)
with
\|h\|_{1}=1
; the key property here is that
\mathrm{supp}(h)
is disjoint from
[-R,R]\supseteq\mathrm{supp}(P)\cup\mathrm{supp}(P^{\prime})
. Therefore
\langle P,h\rangle=\langle P^{\prime},h\rangle=0
, and the PDE inversion gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{G}(x)\,\big(\mathcal{T}_{\sigma}h(x)\big)\,dx=\langle\mathcal{T}_{\sigma}p_{G},h\rangle=\langle P,h\rangle=0.

On the other hand, decomposing
\mathcal{T}_{\sigma}=\mathcal{T}_{\sigma^{\prime}}+(\mathcal{T}_{\sigma}-\mathcal{T}_{\sigma^{\prime}})
and using
\langle p_{G^{\prime}},\mathcal{T}_{\sigma^{\prime}}h\rangle=\langle P^{\prime},h\rangle=0
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{G^{\prime}}(x)\,\big(\mathcal{T}_{\sigma}h(x)\big)\,dx=\langle p_{G^{\prime}},(\mathcal{T}_{\sigma}-\mathcal{T}_{\sigma^{\prime}})h\rangle=-\tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{G^{\prime}}(x)\,h^{\prime\prime}(x)\,dx,

since
\mathcal{T}_{\sigma}-\mathcal{T}_{\sigma^{\prime}}=-\frac{\sigma-\sigma^{\prime}}{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}
. Subtracting and applying Hölder’s inequality,

V\,\|\mathcal{T}_{\sigma}h\|_{\infty}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}|\sigma-\sigma^{\prime}|\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x)\,p_{G^{\prime}}^{\prime\prime}(x)\,dx\right|.

(58)

For
x\in(R+1,R+2)

\theta^{\prime}\in[-R,R]

x-\theta^{\prime}\geq 1>0

f(x\mid\theta^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})=(2\sigma^{\prime})^{-1/2}\exp(-(x-\theta^{\prime})/\sqrt{\sigma^{\prime}/2})

f^{\prime\prime}(x\mid\theta^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})=\frac{2}{\sigma^{\prime}}\,f(x\mid\theta^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}).

Hence
p_{G^{\prime}}^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{2}{\sigma^{\prime}}p_{G^{\prime}}(x)
for

Lemma 1
.

Define the extended function
\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
via the infimal convolution operator over the domain
\mathcal{X}
:

\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x):=\inf_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\Big\{h_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(y)+\|x-y\|\Big\},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The following properties hold:

(a)

\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)=h_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
;

(b)

\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}
is a globally 1-Lipschitz continuous function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, meaning
\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

Since
h_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
and
h_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(0)=0
, we have
|h_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(y)|\leq\|y\|\leq R
for all
y\in\mathcal{X}
, so
h_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(y)+\|x-y\|\geq-R
uniformly in
y
. Hence the infimum defining
\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)
is finite for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Part (a).

For any
x\in\mathcal{X}
, taking
y=x
in the infimum gives
\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)\leq h_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)
. Conversely, the
1
-Lipschitz property of
h_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}
on
\mathcal{X}
gives
h_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)-h_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(y)\leq\|x-y\|
for all
y\in\mathcal{X}
, so
h_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)\leq h_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(y)+\|x-y\|
for all
y\in\mathcal{X}
, and taking the infimum over
y
yields
h_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)\leq\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)
. Combining both inequalities,
\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)=h_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

Part (b).

For any
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
y\in\mathcal{X}
, the triangle inequality gives
\|u-y\|\leq\|u-v\|+\|v-y\|
. Adding
h_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(y)
to both sides and taking the infimum over
y\in\mathcal{X}
,

\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(u)\leq\|u-v\|+\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(v).

Swapping the roles of
u
and
v
and combining yields
|\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(u)-\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(v)|\leq\|u-v\|
for all
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so
\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Define the isotropic bump function
\varrho:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\varrho(x):=\begin{cases}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{1-\|x\|^{2}}\right)&\text{if }\|x\|<1\\
0&\text{if }\|x\|\geq 1.\end{cases}

Then define the normalized mollifier
\psi(x)=\frac{1}{C_{\varrho}}\varrho(x)
, where
C_{\varrho}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varrho(x)dx\in(0,\infty)
, and its
\epsilon=\frac{R}{2}
-scaled mollifier as

\psi_{\epsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d}}\psi\left(\frac{x}{\epsilon}\right).

Then define the cutoff function
\eta:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\eta(x):=(\mathbf{1}_{A}*\psi_{\epsilon})(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{1}_{A}(x-y)\psi_{\epsilon}(y)\,dy,

where
A=\overline{B}(0,R+\epsilon)=\overline{B}\left(0,\frac{3R}{2}\right)
. Then the following properties hold:

(a)

\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

(b)

\eta(x)=1
for all
x\in\overline{B}(0,R)
,
\eta(x)=0
for all
x\notin B(0,2R)
, and
0\leq\eta(x)\leq 1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
;

(c)

M_{\eta}:=\|\nabla\eta\|_{\infty}<\infty
.

Proof.

The function
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
g(t)=e^{-1/t}
for
t>0
and
g(t)=0
for
t\leq 0
belongs to
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
by Lemma 2.20 in
Lee, 2013
. Since
\varrho(x)=g(1-\|x\|^{2})
is a composition of
g
with a smooth polynomial,
\varrho\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. It is strictly positive on
B(0,1)
and vanishes on
\|x\|\geq 1
, so
\mathrm{supp}(\varrho)=\bar{B}(0,1)
is compact and
\varrho\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Hence
C_{\varrho}\in(0,\infty)
and
\psi
is well-defined with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(x)\,dx=1
. The scaled mollifier satisfies
\psi_{\epsilon}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\mathrm{supp}(\psi_{\epsilon})=\bar{B}(0,\epsilon)
, and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi_{\epsilon}(x)\,dx=1
by the substitution
y=x/\epsilon
.

Part (a).

For any multi-index
\nu
,
\partial^{\nu}\eta(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{1}_{A}(y)\,(\partial^{\nu}\psi_{\epsilon})(x-y)\,dy
, which is continuous since
\partial^{\nu}\psi_{\epsilon}
is smooth, bounded, and compactly supported. Hence
\eta\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The support satisfies

\mathrm{supp}(\eta)\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\mathbf{1}_{A})+\mathrm{supp}(\psi_{\epsilon})=\bar{B}\!\left(0,\tfrac{3R}{2}\right)+\bar{B}\!\left(0,\tfrac{R}{2}\right)=\bar{B}(0,2R),

so
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Part (b).

Since
\mathbf{1}_{A}\geq 0
and
\psi_{\epsilon}\geq 0
, we have
\eta(x)\geq 0
. Since
\mathbf{1}_{A}\leq 1
, we have
\eta(x)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi_{\epsilon}(y)\,dy=1
.

For
x\in\bar{B}(0,R)
and any
y\in\mathrm{supp}(\psi_{\epsilon})
, the triangle inequality gives
\|x-y\|\leq\|x\|+\|y\|\leq R+R/2=3R/2
, so
x-y\in A
and
\mathbf{1}_{A}(x-y)=1
. Hence
\eta(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi_{\epsilon}(y)\,dy=1
.

For
x\notin B(0,2R)
and any
y\in\mathrm{supp}(\psi_{\epsilon})
,
\|x-y\|\geq\|x\|-\|y\|\geq 2R-R/2=3R/2
, so
x-y\notin A
and
\mathbf{1}_{A}(x-y)=0
. Hence
\eta(x)=0
. By continuity,
\eta(x)=0
on
\|x\|=2R
as well.

Part (c).

Since
\eta(x)=1
for
\|x\|<R
and
\eta(x)=0
for
\|x\|>2R
, we have
\nabla\eta(x)=0
outside the compact annulus
\bar{B}(0,2R)\setminus B(0,R)
. As
\eta\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the function
x\mapsto\|\nabla\eta(x)\|
is continuous and attains its maximum on this compact set, giving
M_{\eta}:=\|\nabla\eta\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\nabla\eta(x)\|<\infty
.
∎

Lemma 3
.

For
\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x):=\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)\eta(x)
, the following properties hold:

(a)

\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

(b)

\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}
is globally
(1+2RM_{\eta})
-Lipschitz continuous, namely

\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad|\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)-\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(y)|\leq(1+2RM_{\eta})\|x-y\|;

(c)

\|\mathcal{F}[\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}]\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2+1)}(2R)^{d+1}
.

Proof.

Part (a).

Since
\mathrm{supp}(\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*})\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\eta)\subseteq\bar{B}(0,2R)
, and
\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}
is
1
-Lipschitz with
\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(0)=0

|\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)|\leq|\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)|\cdot\eta(x)\leq\|x\|\leq 2R
for all

. Therefore,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)|\,dx\leq\int_{\bar{B}(0,2R)}2R\,dx=\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2+1)}(2R)^{d+1}<\infty,

so
\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Part (b).

Since
\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, Rademacher’s theorem gives
\|\nabla\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)\|\leq 1
for almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the product rule gives
\nabla\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)=\eta(x)\nabla\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)+\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)\nabla\eta(x)
almost everywhere. By the triangle inequality,

\|\nabla\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)\|\leq|\eta(x)|\|\nabla\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)\|+|\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)|\|\nabla\eta(x)\|\leq 1+2R\,M_{\eta},

where we used
\eta(x)\leq 1
,
\|\nabla\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)\|\leq 1
,
|\bar{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)|\leq\|x\|\leq 2R
on
\mathrm{supp}(\nabla\eta)\subseteq\bar{B}(0,2R)
, and
\|\nabla\eta\|_{\infty}=M_{\eta}
. Taking the essential supremum,
\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}
is globally
(1+2RM_{\eta})
-Lipschitz.

Part (c).

By the triangle inequality and part (a),

|\mathcal{F}[\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}](\xi)|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(x)|\,dx\leq\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2+1)}(2R)^{d+1}.

∎

h^{*}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
h^{*}(0)=0
that attains the Kantorovich-Rubinstein dual problem:

\sup_{h\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(x)\,\mu(dx)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(x)\,\nu(dx)\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h^{*}(x)\,\mu(dx)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h^{*}(x)\,\nu(dx).

Proof.

Let
(h_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a maximizing sequence for the Kantorovich-Rubinstein dual problem. Since adding a constant to any
h_{n}
does not affect
\int h_{n}\,d\mu-\int h_{n}\,d\nu
(as
\mu
and
\nu
are both probability measures), we may assume without loss of generality that
h_{n}(0)=0
for all
n
. Consequently,
|h_{n}(x)|=|h_{n}(x)-h_{n}(0)|\leq\|x\|
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so the family
(h_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly bounded and equicontinuous on every compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We now apply a diagonal argument with the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem. Applying Arzelà-Ascoli on
\bar{B}(0,1)
, we extract a subsequence
(h^{1}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(h_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging uniformly on
\bar{B}(0,1)
. Iteratively, for each
k\geq 1
, by Arzelà-Ascoli on
\bar{B}(0,k+1)
, we extract a subsequence
(h^{k+1}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(h^{k}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging uniformly on
\bar{B}(0,k+1)
. The diagonal sequence
(h^{n}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
then converges uniformly on every compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to some pointwise limit
h^{*}
. Since uniform-on-compacts limits of
1
-Lipschitz functions are
1
-Lipschitz,
h^{*}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and clearly
h^{*}(0)=0
.

It remains to show that
h^{*}
attains the supremum. We have
h^{n}_{n}(x)\to h^{*}(x)
pointwise, and the bound
|h^{n}_{n}(x)|\leq\|x\|
holds uniformly in
n
. Since

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|\,\mu(dx)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|\,\nu(dx)<\infty,

the Dominated Convergence Theorem (applied separately to
\mu
and
\nu
) yields

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h^{n}_{n}(x)\,\mu(dx)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h^{n}_{n}(x)\,\nu(dx)\;\longrightarrow\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h^{*}(x)\,\mu(dx)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h^{*}(x)\,\nu(dx).

Since
(h_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a maximizing sequence, the left-hand side converges to
W_{1}(\mu,\nu)
, so
h^{*}
attains the supremum. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 11
.

Let
x,y,M>0
satisfy
x\leq My\log y
and
x\geq e^{1/M}
. Then
y\geq x/(M\log x)
.

Proof.

Define
f(t):=t\log t
for
t>0
. Since
f^{\prime}(t)=\log t+1
, the function
f
is decreasing on
(0,e^{-1})
and increasing on
(e^{-1},\infty)
, with global minimum
f(e^{-1})=-e^{-1}
. From
x>0
and
x\leq My\log y
, we have
y\log y>0
, hence
y>1
.

The hypothesis
x\geq e^{1/M}
gives
\log x\geq 1/M
, hence
M\log x\geq 1
, which implies
x/(M\log x)\leq x
. Therefore

f\!\left(\frac{x}{M\log x}\right)=\frac{x}{M\log x}\log\!\left(\frac{x}{M\log x}\right)\leq\frac{x}{M\log x}\log x=\frac{x}{M}.

Combined with
x/M\leq y\log y=f(y)
, this gives
f(x/(M\log x))\leq f(y)
.

We conclude with a brief case analysis. If
x/(M\log x)\leq 1
, then since
y>1
, the conclusion
y\geq x/(M\log x)
holds trivially. Otherwise,
x/(M\log x)>1
, and both
x/(M\log x)
and
y
lie in
(1,\infty)\subset(e^{-1},\infty)
, where
f
is strictly increasing; hence
f(x/(M\log x))\leq f(y)
implies
x/(M\log x)\leq y
.
∎
