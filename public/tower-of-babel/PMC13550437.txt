Measuring the spatial lag effect of Tobler’s First Law of Geography in Earth system predictive learning
Authors: Pingting Zhou, Yafei Liu, Kaiqi Chen, Min Deng, Qiangjun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prediction of complex geographical phenomena is central to geoscience, underpinning climate adaptation, disaster risk reduction, and resource management. A foundational principle guiding predictive learning is Tobler’s First Law (TFL) of Geography. Although widely embedded in models ranging from spatial autoregression to deep learning, TFL is rarely examined in terms of its lag effect, how spatial and temporal lags jointly shape predictability. This gap constrains efforts to identify informative neighborhood sizes and memory lengths for predictive modeling. Here we introduce a predictability-based causal emergence framework that quantifies the information gain from incorporating different spatial and temporal lags. Applying this framework to global 2 m temperature reanalysis data, we find that the most informative spatiotemporal context is regionally heterogeneous under the current experimental setting. Our results provide a principled approach to refining TFL in predictive modeling, offering new insights into how spatiotemporal lag effects contribute to the emergence of predictability in complex geographical phenomena.

Introduction

Spatiotemporal prediction aims to forecast the unknown states in space and time, which means, in Earth system science, to forecast future Earth phenomena from weather to climate change1,2. The development of accurate and efficient spatiotemporal prediction models remains one of the most critical research challenges in Earth system science, as these models are essential for addressing a series of global challenges3,4. For instance, more accurate wind forecasts generated by NOAA’s High-Resolution Rapid Refresh (HRRR) model are estimated to have saved consumers at least USD 200 million per year5. Additionally, the Global Commission on Adaptation reports that providing just twenty-four hours of advance warning for extreme weather events can reduce resulting damages by approximately 30%6, underscoring the substantial societal and economic value of improving spatiotemporal prediction capabilities.

To design more accurate and efficient spatiotemporal prediction models, an effective predictive learning method is indispensable7–9. Predictive learning is a process that maps the past observations of a dynamic system into its future states10–12. This process includes extracting invariant patterns from observations and making predictions, which is implemented through a structure comprising an encoder, a temporal module and a decoder13–15. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the encoder extracts spatial patterns inherent in observations, such as spatial dependence and spatial variation. In practice, this process is commonly implemented using models such as convolutional neural networks (CNN) for regular grid data, graph convolutional networks (GCN) for irregular spatial structures, or vision transformers (ViT) for global spatial attention16–18. The temporal module extracts temporal patterns, such as trends, periodicity and seasonality. Typical implementations include recurrent neural networks (RNN), long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, and Transformers, which are capable of modeling sequential dependencies and temporal dynamics19–21. The decoder then synthesizes these extracted patterns to generate future predictions. Common decoder architectures employ fully connected layers (FCL) for direct mapping, transposed convolutional networks (DeCNN) to reconstruct high-dimensional spatial maps, or sequence-to-sequence (Seq2Seq) architectures for temporal decoding22,23. In Earth system predictive learning, spatial patterns are often more complex and have thus attracted greater research attention than temporal patterns. Among these, spatial dependence as articulated by Tobler’s First Law (TFL) of Geography is the most fundamental, as it is a component considered in all Earth system predictive learning methods24,25.

TFL states that “Everything is related to everything else, but near things are more related than distant things24.” During predictive learning, it implies that the state of a spatial unit can be inferred from the states of its spatial neighbors. Traditional spatial econometric models, such as the spatial lag, spatial error, and spatial Durbin models, incorporate this dependence through autoregressive approaches26–28. These models generate predictions by integrating observations from the target unit with aggregated observations from its neighbors within a regression framework. Conversely, geostatistical models like simple kriging and cokriging capture spatial dependence by estimating a variogram29,30. This variogram is used to compute a covariance matrix that quantifies the dependence between pairs of spatial units31. These covariances are subsequently used as weights in a prediction function, ensuring that units with stronger covariances exert a greater influence on the prediction32. Recently, deep learning approaches have introduced convolutional structures to model spatial dependence. Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) handle irregular spatial data, while Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are applied to regular data33,34. Both GCNs and CNNs operate through a consistent three-step procedure: selecting spatial neighbors, quantifying the weights for these neighbors, and generating a prediction as a weighted aggregation13,35. Ultimately, accurately capturing spatial dependence remains a fundamental challenge and common requirement for all predictive learning tasks within the Earth system3,36.

To capture spatial dependence effectively, many efforts have been made in existing studies by considering the nonlinear, nonstationary, and multivariate issues in spatial neighborhood modeling37,38. However, the underlying effect of spatial lag on neighborhood construction is rarely examined explicitly in predictive contexts39,40. Spatial lag value represents the radius of the considered neighbors. Since many Earth phenomena can be represented as time series of spatial raster data along the temporal dimension, this spatial lag is defined as spatial neighborhood order in this study. A spatial lag of 1 corresponds to first-order neighbors, whereas a spatial lag of 2 corresponds to second-order neighbors. Some prediction models predefine this spatial lag as a constant, which can lead to incorrect dependence learning26,41,42. Others employ data-driven optimizers to adjust the spatial lag adaptively, but this often limits the interpretability of the results43–45. Consequently, a statistical measure to determine the optimal spatial lag is highly desirable. Such a measure would enhance both the accuracy and interpretability of dependence learning in predictive research.

Numerous statistical methods exist for quantifying spatial dependence, including Moran’s I, Geary’s C, and variogram-based metrics46–48. These methods have proven highly successful for spatial analysis like spatial clustering, association mining, and anomaly detection. However, they are not specifically designed for predictive learning due to two key limitations: (1) they are correlation-based rather than predictability-oriented, and (2) they overlook the spatial lag effect. Specifically, these methods measure spatial dependence by calculating correlations between locations, but high correlation does not necessarily imply predictive contribution49. Predictive learning, which bridges past and future states, requires the integration of time intervals into the assessment of spatial dependence50,51. Consequently, an effective spatial dependence measure for predictive learning should capture how past states propagate through space to influence future outcomes, shifting the focus from correlation to predictability52,53. Furthermore, the spatial lag is a critical parameter for this predictability54. An optimal spatial lag, which is influenced by the target phenomenon and forecast horizon, can maximize predictability. Yet, current literature provides limited guidance for determining this optimal lag value55,56.

Therefore, the objective of this study is to quantify the incremental predictive contribution of spatial neighborhoods to predictive performance in Earth system modeling and to identify the most informative spatial context for prediction using information-theoretic measures. To achieve this goal, we develop a novel measure for quantifying the spatial lag effect. Grounded in the principles of predictability and causal emergence, the proposed method is applicable to both predictive learning and spatial lag optimization. Specifically, we introduce a measure of Effective Information (EI) based on transfer entropy to quantify the contribution of past observations from one spatial unit to forecasting the future state of another unit57,58. Building upon this formulation, we further define an EI-based Causal Emergence (CE) measure to identify the optimal spatial lag by systematically evaluating predictive information gain across spatial distances. Through an empirical analysis of global 2 m temperature reanalysis data, the proposed framework provides a quantitative way to characterize the spatial lag effect of TFL in Earth system prediction.

Quantifying spatial lag effect of TFL

We next examined how predictive performance depends on the spatial extent of the neighborhood. Spatial lags from the first to the tenth order were evaluated under a fixed one-hour forecast horizon. The distribution of EI across lag orders shows a clear distance-decay pattern (Fig. 3a). Both the median and the upper tail of EI decrease monotonically as spatial lag increases, indicating a progressive reduction in predictive contribution from more distant neighbors.

This behavior is further reflected in the global EI fields (Fig. 3b–d). At the first-order spatial lag, EI values are high and spatially coherent across large regions, indicating strong predictive contributions from immediately adjacent units. As the spatial lag increases to orders five and ten, EI weakens and becomes increasingly fragmented, with elevated values confined to a limited number of regions. Under a fixed temporal lag of one hour, these patterns indicate that predictive information propagates most efficiently over short spatial distances, while contributions from more distant neighborhoods diminish rapidly with increasing lag, potentially reflecting both intrinsic spatial processes and the increasing redundancy associated with larger neighborhoods.

Selecting the most informative spatial lag with causal emergence

To determine the effective spatial and temporal boundaries of predictive context, we computed Causal Emergence (CE), which quantifies the marginal information gain when expanding the neighborhood in space or time. The formal mathematical definition and computation of CE are provided in the Methods section. Spatial CE maps reveal a consistent divergence between land and ocean regions (Fig. 4a-c). Over land, CE values are predominantly positive, suggesting that expanding the spatial neighborhood can provide additional predictive information. Over ocean, CE values are largely negative, and negative regions expand with increasing spatial lag, indicating that extending the neighborhood beyond immediate neighbors may reduce marginal predictive information.

Temporal CE exhibits a complementary pattern (Fig. 4d-f). Land regions show positive temporal CE at short temporal expansions, while ocean regions display weaker or near-neutral temporal gains. As temporal lag increases, positive CE over land diminishes, indicating reduced marginal benefit from longer temporal memory. The combined spatiotemporal CE heatmap (Fig. 4g) exhibits an empirical maximum at spatial lag one and temporal lag three under the current dataset and experimental design. The mean EI at this configuration is significantly greater than zero across spatial units (95% CI: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:[0.1655,\:0.1876]$$\end{document}; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p\:<\:10^{-150}$$\end{document}), indicating a robust predictive contribution of spatial context. However, this significance does not imply that the identified maximum is unique across all configurations. Although the baseline diurnal cycle has been removed from the data, CE retains a structured daily pattern across temporal lags, indicating that the most informative predictive context varies systematically over the day.

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that the predictive contribution implied by TFL is neither static nor spatially uniform when evaluated in a predictive context. By quantifying the predictive contribution of spatial neighborhoods through EI, we show that proximity alone does not necessarily guarantee predictive value. Instead, the usefulness of nearby observations varies systematically over space and time, reflecting constraints on how predictive information propagates across the Earth’s surface. The observed diurnal modulation of EI, characterized by stable predictability during most hours but pronounced reductions at specific times dominated by oceanic regions, indicates that the predictive utility of spatial information fluctuates even after removal of the baseline diurnal cycle. This suggests that predictability is not determined solely by local temporal persistence, but also by the broader environmental organization that governs how information is shared among neighboring locations.

The monotonic decay of EI with increasing spatial lag provides empirical evidence for a distance-decay pattern in predictive information. Immediate neighbors contribute the majority of usable spatial information, while the marginal contribution of more distant neighborhoods diminishes rapidly under a fixed forecast horizon. This pattern highlights an intrinsic limitation of spatial context in short-term prediction: although environmental processes are spatially continuous, predictive signals do not propagate instantaneously across space. The gradual rather than abrupt decline of EI further suggests that spatial influence weakens progressively rather than disappearing beyond a single characteristic scale. Within this framework, spatial dependence operates as a finite-range constraint on prediction, governed by the speed and coherence of information transmission rather than by geometric distance alone.

Differences between land and ocean further reveal that predictive information is organized unevenly across the Earth’s surface. Causal Emergence analysis indicates that expanding spatial or temporal context can yield additional predictive information over land, whereas oceanic regions often reach saturation rapidly, with larger spatial neighborhoods introducing redundancy rather than new information. This contrast likely reflects differences in surface heterogeneity and environmental structure. Terrestrial environments are shaped by fragmented land surfaces, variable topography, and diverse surface properties, which distribute predictive signals across broader spatial and temporal contexts. Marine environments, in contrast, tend to exhibit smoother spatial structures in which useful predictive information is concentrated locally. As a result, the effective spatiotemporal footprint of predictive dependence varies substantially between land and ocean.

These findings suggest that, in predictive modeling, TFL can be interpreted from a dynamic perspective related to predictive information flow rather than a fixed spatial rule. The most informative predictive configuration identified here, combining immediate spatial proximity with limited temporal memory, indicates that useful predictive context is both scale-dependent and time-dependent. In practical terms, this implies that the predictive contribution of spatial neighborhoods in Earth system prediction should not be treated as a static neighborhood assumption, but as an emergent property shaped by environmental structure and information propagation constraints. By providing a quantitative framework to identify effective spatiotemporal context, this study links a classical geographical principle with modern information-theoretic analysis and offers a systematic basis for improving data-driven prediction of environmental variables.

While our framework effectively captures local distance-decay, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the observed macroscopic decay of EI may be partly influenced by structural effects of the lag construction. As neighborhood size increases, redundancy among neighboring units and the dimensionality of the conditioning set al.so increase, which can reduce the marginal predictive contribution of additional neighbors. At the same time, the most informative spatial lag exhibits clear spatial heterogeneity, suggesting that predictive information operates across multiple spatial scales rather than a single uniform range61. Furthermore, although CE provides a useful diagnostic of marginal predictive gain, it reflects changes in conditional predictive contribution rather than formal causal structure62,63. In particular, negative CE values may arise from redundancy, noise, or estimation effects, rather than indicating intrinsic physical mechanisms. In addition, the current analysis is based on a single variable, a single month, and a fixed spatial resolution, which limits the generality of the conclusions.

Recent advances in spatiotemporal predictive modeling, including transformer-based architectures for long-range forecasting and learning-based frameworks such as neural architecture search and meta-learning, have significantly improved predictive performance64–66. The framework proposed in this study is complementary to these approaches, as it provides an information-theoretic perspective for quantifying the predictive contribution of spatial and temporal context, which can inform model design and interpretation. Future work will extend this framework to incorporate nonlocal dependencies, apply causal network learning approaches, and evaluate robustness across multiple variables, longer time periods, and different spatial resolutions67,68.

Effective Information as a measure of predictive dependence

Effective Information (EI) was used to quantify the predictive contribution of the spatial neighborhood at a specific spatiotemporal lag. This formulation expresses the reduction in uncertainty of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{X}_{t}^{\left(i\right)}$$\end{document} gained by knowledge of the spatial neighborhood \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{X}_{t-\tau\:}^{{N}_{k}^{\left(i\right)}}$$\end{document}, given the unit’s own past \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{X}_{t-1}^{\left(i\right)}$$\end{document}. As this formulation is based on conditional mutual information, EI is closely related to transfer entropy and can be interpreted as a measure of directional predictive dependence. In line with applied information-theoretic studies in geoscience, such measures quantify predictive information flow rather than implying mechanistic causality70. Moreover, their estimation and interpretation may be affected by finite-sample effects, high-dimensional conditioning, and estimator-related biases, particularly when the spatial lag increases.

In practice, the probability density functions required to compute entropy and mutual information are generally unknown. As the spatial lag increases, the predictive neighborhood expands, resulting in a higher-dimensional conditioning space. Binning-based estimation methods may therefore suffer from discretization bias and sparse-sample effects. To avoid imposing parametric assumptions on the data distribution, we estimate these quantities using the Kraskov–Stögbauer–Grassberger (KSG) estimator, a nonparametric k-nearest-neighbor method widely used for mutual information estimation in continuous systems71. The KSG estimator adapts to local data density by evaluating the distance between each sample and its k-th nearest neighbor in the joint state space. However, the effects of high-dimensional conditioning, redundancy among neighboring units, and finite-sample bias are not fully eliminated and are therefore considered in the interpretation of the results and discussed as limitations.

Causal Emergence (CE) and optimal scale selection

While the term Causal Emergence (CE) originates from literature describing macroscopic causal supersedence, in this study, it is utilized as a diagnostic of predictability rather than a measure of causality57. CE was computed to assess the marginal predictive gain associated with shifting the spatiotemporal context. It is defined as a finite difference of EI across neighboring lag configurations. Spatial CE quantifies the change in EI when expanding the spatial lag from k-1 to k at a fixed temporal lag.6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}C{E}_{S}\left(k\right)=EI\left(k,\tau\:\right)-EI\left(k-1,\tau\:\right) \end{array}$$\end{document}

Positive CE values indicate that the expanded spatiotemporal context provides additional predictive information beyond the smaller or more recent context, while negative values indicate a reduction in marginal predictive contribution.
