Chance-Constrained Correlated Equilibria for Robust Noncooperative Coordination
Jaehan Im, Ufuk Topcu, David Fridovich-Keil

Abstract
Correlated equilibria enable a coordinator to influence the self-interested agents by recommending actions that no player has an incentive to deviate from. However, the effectiveness of this mechanism relies on accurate knowledge of the agents’ cost structures. When cost parameters are uncertain, the recommended actions may no longer be incentive compatible, allowing agents to benefit from deviating from them. We study a chance-constrained correlated equilibrium problem formulation that accounts for uncertainty in agents’ costs and guarantees incentive compatibility with a prescribed confidence level. We derive sensitivity results that quantify how uncertainty in individual incentive constraints affects the expected coordination outcome. In particular, the analysis characterizes the value of information by relating the marginal benefit of reducing uncertainty to the dual sensitivities of the incentive constraints, providing guidance on which sources of uncertainty should be prioritized for information acquisition. The results further reveal that increasing the confidence level is not always beneficial and can introduce a tradeoff between robustness and system efficiency. Numerical experiments demonstrate that the proposed framework maintains coordination performance in uncertain environments and are consistent with the theoretical insights developed in the analysis.



I
Introduction

Multi-agent systems often consist of self-interested agents whose decisions collectively determine system-level outcomes.
In such environments, agents optimize their own objectives independently, which often leads to inefficient outcomes at equilibrium. As a result, designing coordination mechanisms that improve system-level performance without requiring cooperation remains a central challenge in decentralized decision-making systems.

The correlated equilibrium concept
[
4
,
3
]
provides a coordination mechanism that can improve outcomes in noncooperative games. In this mechanism, a coordinator samples a joint action from a probability distribution and privately recommends an action to each player. If the distribution satisfies the correlated equilibrium conditions, no player can reduce its cost by unilaterally deviating from the recommendation. This incentive-compatibility property enables a coordinator to influence the behavior of self-interested agents while respecting their autonomy. The correlated equilibrium concept has been widely studied as a coordination tool in various multi-agent systems
[
16
,
20
,
13
,
15
,
12
]
, as illustrated in the air mobility coordination example in
Figure

1
.

However, in many practical applications, the coordinator may not have accurate knowledge of the players’ cost structures
[
7
,
12
,
1
,
2
]
.
Under such uncertainty, the incentive compatibility conditions derived from the nominal cost model may no longer hold. Consequently, players may have incentives to deviate from the recommended actions, which undermines the effectiveness of the correlated equilibrium.

Fig. 1:

Illustration of the vertiport occupancy coordination scenario.
Aircraft arrive through multiple approach queues, where each queue represents a self-interested agent that dispatches aircraft and determines which combination of vertiports to occupy or yield.
A central coordinator broadcasts correlated recommendations that suggest vertiport usage while preserving agents’ autonomy.
The interaction forms a noncooperative coordination game in which agents may follow or deviate from the recommended actions.

We study a
chance-constrained correlated equilibrium
(CC-CE) that explicitly accounts for uncertainty in agents’ costs.
In CC-CE, the incentive compatibility conditions are required to hold with a user-specified confidence level.
By incorporating uncertainty directly into the equilibrium constraints, CC-CE generates recommendations that remain incentive-compatible under cost uncertainty with quantifiable confidence.
Our recent work has explored the use of CC-CE for coordination under uncertainty
[
15
]
, primarily focusing on empirical performance. However, the theoretical role of uncertainty in shaping coordination outcomes and incentive constraints remains largely unexplored.

The contributions of this paper are threefold. First, we derive sensitivity results that quantify the value of information in individual incentive constraints and identify the constraints that act as information bottlenecks in coordination.
Second, we analyze how the confidence level used in CC-CE affects system performance, revealing a tradeoff between robustness and efficiency.
Third, we provide numerical experiments that illustrate how these theoretical insights explain the coordination behavior observed in practice.

II
Related work

The correlated equilibrium concept
[
4
,
3
]
has been widely studied as a mechanism for coordinating noncooperative agents, as it allows a coordinator to recommend actions while preserving each agent’s autonomy
[
16
,
20
,
13
,
12
,
15
]
. However, correlated equilibrium recommendations can be sensitive to uncertainty in the underlying payoff structure, such as estimation errors or stochastic perturbations
[
13
,
20
]
.

Existing work addressing uncertainty in correlated equilibria largely follows two directions. One line of research studies
Bayes correlated equilibria
, which model payoff uncertainty through states and information structures
[
10
]
. In this framework, agents face incomplete information about payoff-relevant states, and incentive compatibility is defined in expectation with respect to players’ beliefs
[
10
,
9
,
19
]
.

Another line of work incorporates uncertainty directly into agents’ cost functions through robust or distributionally robust formulations
[
5
,
14
,
18
,
17
]
. These approaches enforce worst-case guarantees over admissible uncertainty sets. While this framework provides strong robustness, it often leads to nonconvex optimization problems
[
18
,
17
]
and can become overly conservative when uncertainty reflects stochastic fluctuations around nominal costs.

A different perspective appears in bounded-rationality models such as quantal response equilibria and their correlated variants
[
23
,
8
]
. These models introduce stochastic response rules and define equilibrium as a fixed point of noisy best responses.
Here, randomness arises from the response mechanism rather than from explicit reliability constraints on deviation incentives that arise in the setting of correlated equilibrium problems with uncertain agent objectives.

III
Preliminaries

III-A

Correlated equilibrium

Consider a finite strategic game with agent set
N=\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Each agent
i\in N
chooses an action from a finite action set

X_{i}
. The joint action space is defined as

\mathcal{X}:=\prod_{i\in N}X_{i}
.
Let
z\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
denote a joint probability distribution
over action profiles. A correlated equilibrium can be interpreted
as a coordination device that recommends actions to agents
according to the distribution
z
. Upon receiving a recommendation,
each agent decides whether to follow it or deviate to another action.

Let
J_{i}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the cost of agent
i
.
We define the deviation cost for agent
i
as

\Delta J_{i}(x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime},x_{-i}):=J_{i}(x_{i},x_{-i})-J_{i}(x_{i}^{\prime},x_{-i}),

(1)

where
x_{-i}\in\prod_{j\neq i}X_{j}
denotes the joint actions
of all agents except
i
.
This quantity represents the cost change experienced by agent
i

if the recommended action
x_{i}
is replaced with a deviation
x_{i}^{\prime}

while other agents play
x_{-i}
.

Given a recommendation
x_{i}
, the conditional expected deviation
margin under
z
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{-i}\sim z(\cdot|x_{i})}

\displaystyle[\Delta J_{i}(x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime},x_{-i})]

(2)

\displaystyle=\textstyle{\sum_{x_{-i}}z(x_{-i}|x_{i})\Delta J_{i}(x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime},x_{-i})}.

This expectation measures whether the deviation is profitable on
average when recommendations are drawn from the joint distribution.
We recall the classical definition of correlated equilibrium
[
4
,
3
]
.

Definition 1
(Correlated equilibrium)

A distribution
z\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
is a correlated equilibrium if

\mathbb{E}_{x_{-i}\sim z(\cdot|x_{i})}[\Delta J_{i}(x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime},x_{-i})]\leq 0,\quad\forall i,

(3)

for all suggestions
x_{i}
, and for all deviations
x_{i}^{\prime}\neq x_{i}
.

Intuitively, once an agent receives a recommendation, no unilateral
deviation yields a lower expected cost. Therefore following the
recommendation is rational.

III-B

Coordination via correlated equilibrium

The objective of coordination is to select a feasible equilibrium that
leads to a desirable system-level outcome. Let

J_{\text{sys}}:\Delta(\mathcal{X})\to\mathbb{R}
denote a system-level cost function.

A correlated equilibrium specifies a probability distribution over
joint actions, so we evaluate the system outcome in expectation.
Consequently, many coordination objectives admit a
linear combination of outcome costs with respect to the distribution
z
.
For example, we can express the utilitarian system cost as

J_{\text{sys}}(z)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}z(x)\left(\sum_{i\in N}J_{i}(x)\right)
.

We formulate the coordination task as the following equilibrium
selection problem

\displaystyle\min_{z\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})}

\displaystyle J_{\text{sys}}(z)

(4)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle z\text{ satisfies the constraints \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:ceDef}.}

Because both the objective and the constraints are linear in
z
,

Equation

4
becomes a linear program.

IV
Correlated equilibrium

under cost uncertainty

IV-A

Deviation uncertainty model

In practical coordination settings, the coordinator may not know the
exact deviation costs of agents due to modeling errors, operational
variability, or incomplete information about agent preferences.
To capture this uncertainty, we introduce a stochastic
model for the deviation cost.

Recall that
\Delta J_{i}(x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime},x_{-i})
denotes the deviation cost defined in
Equation

1
. From the coordinator’s perspective, this quantity is not perfectly known and this uncertainty is modeled as

\Delta J_{i}(x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime},x_{-i})=\Delta\bar{J}_{i}(x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime},x_{-i})+\eta_{i},

(5)

where
\Delta\bar{J}_{i}
represents the nominal estimate of the
deviation cost and
\eta_{i}
is an agent-level uncertainty term.
In this work we assume
\eta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{i}^{2})
, where

\sigma_{i}\geq 0
characterizes the uncertainty level associated with agent
i
.
The disturbance
\eta_{i}
is assumed to be common across all deviation costs for agent
i
.
This captures agent-level modeling uncertainty that shifts all deviation margins for agent
i
in a coherent way.

Under the noise model
Equation

5
, the expected deviation cost perceived by the coordinator becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{-i}\sim z(\cdot|x_{i})}

\displaystyle[\Delta J_{i}(x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime},x_{-i})]

(6)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x_{-i}\sim z(\cdot|x_{i})}[\Delta\bar{J}_{i}(x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime},x_{-i})]+\eta_{i}.

Thus even if the nominal expected deviation margin is negative,
uncertainty in the deviation cost may cause the realized margin
to become positive, leading agents to deviate from the recommended action, cf.
Equation

3
.

IV-B

Chance-constrained correlated equilibrium

To ensure robust coordination under uncertainty, we require the
deviation condition to hold with high confidence.
We adopt the CC-CE formulation from our prior work
[
15
]
.

Definition 2
(Chance-constrained correlated equilibrium)

For confidence level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, a distribution

z\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
is a chance-constrained correlated equilibrium (CC-CE) if

\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{x_{-i}\sim z(\cdot|x_{i})}[\Delta\bar{J}_{i}(x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime},x_{-i})]+\eta_{i}\leq 0\Big)\geq\alpha,\quad\forall i,

(7)

for all recommendations
x_{i}
, and all deviations
x_{i}^{\prime}\neq x_{i}
.

This condition guarantees that deviations remain unprofitable with probability at least
\alpha
.

Under the noise model
Equation

5
with
\eta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{i}^{2})
, the chance constraint
Equation

7
admits
a deterministic equivalent. Grouping all deviation constraints into an index
c=(i,x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime})
, we write

\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x_{-i}\sim z(\cdot|x_{i})}[\Delta\bar{J}_{i}(x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime},x_{-i})]}_{=:m_{c}(z)}+\underbrace{\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)}_{=:q(\alpha)}\sigma_{i(c)}\leq 0,

(8)

where
i(c)
denotes the agent
associated with constraint
c
, and
\Phi^{-1}
denotes the inverse
cumulative distribution function of the normal distribution.
The term
m_{c}(z)
denotes the nominal deviation margin, while

q(\alpha)\sigma_{i(c)}
is the uncertainty margin.

To compute an uncertainty-aware coordination outcome, we solve the coordination problem
Equation

4
while replacing the classical constraints with the CC-CE constraints
Equation

8
. This yields a robust equilibrium selection problem that accounts for uncertainty in deviation costs.

V
Solution geometry and sensitivity analysis

We analyze the geometric structure of the feasible set of the
chance-constrained correlated equilibrium (CC-CE) and its sensitivity
to uncertainty parameters. The goal is to understand which incentive
constraints act as bottlenecks for coordination and how reducing
uncertainty can improve the achievable outcome. Concretely, we ask:
(i) which constraints become active and restrict coordination
performance, (ii) whether these bottlenecks arise from the game
structure or from uncertainty in cost information, and (iii) which
sources of uncertainty are most valuable to reduce.

V-A

Bottleneck origin analysis

We first ask which constraints are actually responsible for limiting
the achievable coordination performance once uncertainty is taken into
account.

Recall the CC-CE coordination problem is subject to

g_{c}(z;\alpha,\sigma_{i(c)})=m_{c}(z)+q(\alpha)\sigma_{i(c)}\leq 0,\quad\forall c,

(9)

where
c=(i,x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime})
indexes a deviation constraint and
i(c)
denotes
its associated agent, cf.
Equation

8
.
Geometrically, the CC-CE constraints
Equation

7
translate the facets of the classical correlated equilibrium polytope (
m_{c}(z)\leq 0
) by an uncertainty margin (
q(\alpha)\sigma_{i(c)}
).
Let
z^{\star}
be an optimal solution for
Equation

4
. Then some incentive constraints may become active and restrict the achievable coordination performance, where we define the active set as
\mathcal{A}:=\{c\mid g_{c}(z^{\star};\alpha,\sigma_{i(c)})=0\}
.

For
c\in\mathcal{A}
, the condition

m_{c}(z^{\star})+q(\alpha)\sigma_{i(c)}=0
highlights the role of the
uncertainty margin
q(\alpha)\sigma_{i(c)}
, which shifts the nominal
constraint
m_{c}(z)\leq 0
inward, as illustrated in
Figure

2
.
The magnitude of
q(\alpha)\sigma_{i(c)}
therefore reflects how
strongly uncertainty displaces the feasible set from the nominal equilibrium boundary. Large values indicate that the constraint is likely driven by
uncertainty in the cost information, whereas small values suggest that
the bottleneck primarily reflects the underlying game structure.

V-B

Information benefit:
\sigma
–sensitivity analysis

While the previous subsection distinguishes structural and information
driven bottlenecks qualitatively, it does not quantify the benefit of
reducing a given uncertainty source.
We now quantify the value of reducing uncertainty by analyzing how the
optimal system outcome changes with respect to the uncertainty levels

\sigma_{i}
. The following result characterizes the marginal benefit of reducing agent-level uncertainty.

Theorem 1
(
\sigma
–sensitivity)

Let
J^{\star}_{\text{sys}}(\sigma)
denote the optimal system cost of the CC-CE coordination
problem for a fixed confidence level
\alpha
, and let
\lambda_{c}^{\star}

denote the optimal dual variable associated with constraint
c
.
Then,

\textstyle{\frac{\partial J_{\text{sys}}^{\star}}{\partial\sigma_{i}}=q(\alpha)\Lambda_{i},\quad\forall i\in N},

(10)

where

\Lambda_{i}:=\sum_{c\in C_{i}}\lambda_{c}^{\star}
,
and
C_{i}
denotes the set of CC-CE constraints associated with agent
i
.

Proof:

For fixed
\alpha
, recall that the CC-CE coordination problem is

\min_{z\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})}J_{\text{sys}}(z)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad m_{c}(z)+b_{c}(\sigma)\leq 0,\ \forall c,

(11)

where
b_{c}(\sigma)=q(\alpha)\sigma_{i(c)}
.

Since the problem is a linear program, standard sensitivity
results imply that the derivative of the optimal value with respect
to the constraint margin
b_{c}
equals the corresponding dual
multiplier
\lambda_{c}^{\star}

[
6
]
. Thus,

\textstyle{\frac{\partial J^{\star}_{\text{sys}}}{\partial b_{c}}=\lambda_{c}^{\star}}.

(12)

Applying the chain rule,

\displaystyle\textstyle{\frac{\partial J_{\text{sys}}^{\star}}{\partial\sigma_{i}}}

\displaystyle=\textstyle{\sum_{c}\frac{\partial J_{\text{sys}}^{\star}}{\partial b_{c}}\frac{\partial b_{c}}{\partial\sigma_{i}}}=\textstyle{\sum_{c}\lambda_{c}^{\star}q(\alpha)\bm{1}\{i(c)=i\}}

(13)

\displaystyle=\textstyle{q(\alpha)\sum_{c\in C_{i}}\lambda_{c}^{\star}},

where
\bm{1}
is an indicator function.
Define
\Lambda_{i}:=\sum_{c\in C_{i}}\lambda_{c}^{\star}
.
Substituting this completes the proof.
∎

This result shows that the marginal performance gain from reducing
agent-level uncertainty is proportional to the dual multipliers as above of the
associated CC-CE constraints.

Corollary 1
(Constraint-level sensitivity)

The sensitivity of the optimal coordination
outcome with respect to the uncertainty of constraint
c
is

\textstyle{\frac{\partial J_{\text{sys}}^{\star}}{\partial\sigma_{i(c)}}=q(\alpha)\lambda_{c}^{\star}}.

(14)

Proof:

The result follows directly from
Theorem

1
.
∎

Thus,
\Lambda_{i}
quantifies the aggregate value of reducing uncertainty
for agent
i
, whereas
\lambda_{c}^{\star}
captures the contribution of
an individual constraint to that value.

V-C

Information acquisition prioritization

Fig. 2:

Illustration of CC-CE feasible-set contraction in the decision space
z\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
for two constraints
c\in\{1,2\}
.
Uncertainty shifts the nominal constraints
m_{c}(z)
inward by
q(\alpha)\sigma_{i(c)}
, yielding the chance-constrained boundaries
g_{c}(z;\alpha,\sigma_{i(c)})
.
Constraint
c=1
(blue) has small uncertainty and represents a structural bottleneck, while constraint
c=2
(red) corresponds to an information bottleneck.
In this illustration, the optimal solution
z^{\ast}
lies at the intersection where both constraints are active, with dual sensitivities
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
.
This highlights that the value of reducing uncertainty depends jointly on the dual sensitivity and the uncertainty level.

The marginal improvement in the
optimal coordination outcome obtained by relaxing a constraint is
proportional to the corresponding dual multiplier
\lambda_{c}
, as shown in
Corollary

1
.
Classically,
\lambda_{c}
can be interpreted as the “shadow price” associated
with constraint
c
.

However, this quantity alone does not determine which information
should be prioritized. As indicated by
Equation

9
, each
constraint margin consists of a structural component
m_{c}(z^{\star})

and an uncertainty component
q(\alpha)\sigma_{i(c)}
.

To quantify the value of information, we combine the sensitivity
result of
Section

V-B
with the magnitude of the
uncertainty per
Section

V-A
. A first-order approximation
of the improvement from eliminating
\sigma_{i(c)}
is

\textstyle{\Delta J_{\text{sys}}^{\star}\approx-\sigma_{i(c)}\frac{\partial J_{\text{sys}}^{\star}}{\partial\sigma_{i(c)}}}.

(15)

Substituting
Equation

14
, we obtain

\Delta J_{\text{sys}}^{\star}\approx-q(\alpha)\sigma_{i(c)}\lambda_{c}^{\star}.

(16)

Since
q(\alpha)
is constant for fixed confidence level, we approximate
the potential information benefit of constraint
c
by

\text{InfoGain}_{c}:=\sigma_{i(c)}\lambda_{c}^{\star}.

(17)

This expression suggests that the potential benefit of reducing
uncertainty depends on both the dual sensitivity of the constraint
and the magnitude of the associated uncertainty.
In particular, even if the shadow price

\lambda_{c}^{\star}
is large, the achievable improvement may remain
small when
\sigma_{i(c)}
is negligible, indicating a structural
bottleneck. Conversely, constraints with both large
\sigma_{i(c)}
and
large
\lambda_{c}^{\star}
correspond to uncertainty that significantly
limits coordination performance and therefore represent the most
valuable targets for information acquisition (cf.,
Sections

V-B
and
V-A
).

V-D

Confidence benefit:
\alpha
–
J_{\text{sys}}
tradeoff

Finally, we view the confidence level
\alpha
itself as a design knob.
Intuitively, increasing
\alpha
reduces the probability of deviation,
but also tightens the CC-CE constraints and can worsen coordination
cost. We now make this tradeoff precise.

Theorem 2
(
\alpha
sensitivity)

Let
J_{\text{sys}}^{\star}(\alpha)
denote the optimal value of the CC-CE coordination
problem for fixed uncertainty levels
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i\in N}
. Under Gaussian
uncertainty, the sensitivity of the optimal system cost with respect to
the confidence level
\alpha
is

\textstyle{\frac{dJ_{\text{sys}}^{\star}}{d\alpha}=\frac{1}{\phi(q(\alpha))}\sum_{i}\Lambda_{i}\sigma_{i}},

(18)

where
q(\alpha)=\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)
and
\phi
denotes the probability
density function of the standard normal distribution.

Proof:

Recall that the CC-CE constraints contain the margin

b_{c}(\alpha,\sigma)=q(\alpha)\sigma_{i(c)}
. From

Theorem

1
, the derivative of the optimal value with
respect to
b_{c}
equals the corresponding dual multiplier

\lambda_{c}^{\star}
. Applying the chain rule yields

\textstyle{\frac{dJ_{\text{sys}}^{\star}}{d\alpha}=\sum_{c}\lambda_{c}^{\star}\frac{\partial b_{c}}{\partial\alpha}=\sum_{c}\lambda_{c}^{\star}\sigma_{i(c)}\frac{dq(\alpha)}{d\alpha}}.

(19)

For Gaussian uncertainty,

\frac{dq(\alpha)}{d\alpha}=\frac{1}{\phi(q(\alpha))}

[
22
]
.
Grouping constraints by agent yields

\sum_{i}\Lambda_{i}\sigma_{i}
,
which completes the proof.
∎This theorem leads to the following corollary.

Corollary 2
(
\alpha
sensitivity as information gain)

Under Gaussian uncertainty,

\textstyle{\frac{dJ_{\text{sys}}^{\star}}{d\alpha}=\frac{1}{\phi(q(\alpha))}\sum_{c}\text{InfoGain}_{c}}\,,

(20)

where
\text{InfoGain}_{c}
denotes the information value associated with
constraint
c
, defined in
Equation

17
.

Proof:

Substituting
Equation

17
into
Equation

18
completes the proof.
∎

Theorem

2
and
Corollary

2
show that the
sensitivity of the optimal coordination cost with respect to
\alpha

scales with the aggregate information gain across constraints.
Increasing
\alpha
enlarges the uncertainty margin in the incentive
constraints, shrinking the feasible coordination region and thereby
increasing the coordination cost.

However, increasing
\alpha
also reduces the probability of deviation.
To capture this effect, let
C_{\text{dev}}
denotes the expected system loss incurred when deviations occur. Consider an effective system cost

J_{\text{eff}}(\alpha)=J_{\text{sys}}(\alpha)+(1-\alpha)C_{\text{dev}},

(21)

which additionally considers the deviation risk to the system cost.

Theorem 3
(Necessary condition for optimal
\alpha
)

A necessary first-order condition for an optimal confidence level
\alpha^{\star}
that minimizes the effective system cost
J_{\text{eff}}
satisfies

\textstyle{\frac{1}{\phi(q(\alpha^{\star}))}\sum_{c}\text{InfoGain}_{c}=C_{\text{dev}}},

(22)

under Gaussian uncertainty and fixed
\sigma_{i}
for all agents
i
.

Proof:

Differentiating the effective cost gives

\textstyle{\frac{dJ_{\text{eff}}}{d\alpha}=\frac{dJ_{\text{sys}}^{\star}}{d\alpha}-C_{\text{dev}}}.

(23)

Any stationary point therefore satisfies

\frac{dJ^{\star}}{d\alpha}=C_{\text{dev}}.

Substituting
Equation

20
from

Corollary

2
completes the proof.
∎

This result highlights the nuanced role of the confidence parameter.
Increasing
\alpha
reduces deviation risk
(1-\alpha)C_{\text{dev}}
but simultaneously shrinks
the feasible coordination region. This result shows that increasing the confidence level does not always improve performance.

VI
Numerical experiment

VI-A

Vertiport occupancy scenario

We consider a vertiport occupancy coordination scenario for air mobility, illustrated in
Figure

1
.
Aircraft arrive through multiple queues, and each queue is modeled as a self-interested agent that determines which vertiports to occupy by deploying the aircraft in its queue at each decision step.
Specifically, for each vertiport the agent chooses a binary action: occupy the vertiport or yield it to others.

Let
x_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
denote the occupancy decision of agent
i
, where
m
denotes the number of vertiports,

x_{iv}=1
indicates that agent
i
attempts to occupy vertiport
v
and

x_{iv}=0
indicates that the agent yields it.
Let
N_{v}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{iv}
denote the number of agents occupying
vertiport
v
under joint action
x
.
The nominal cost of each agent,
\bar{J}_{i}(x)
, represents the accumulated delay in minutes.
Yielding a vertiport incurs a waiting penalty of
5 minutes per yielded vertiport.
If multiple agents simultaneously occupy the same vertiport, each involved agent incurs a congestion penalty proportional to the number of simultaneous occupancies, yielding the total cost:

\small{\textstyle{\bar{J}_{i}(x)=5\gamma\sum_{v=1}^{m}x_{iv}(N_{v}(x)-1)+5\sum_{v=1}^{m}(1-x_{iv})}}.

(24)

The penalty parameter
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 1}
controls the severity of congestion penalties.
When multiple agents attempt to occupy the same vertiport, the resulting delay
is amplified by the factor
\gamma
, capturing the operational impact of
uncoordinated decisions. Larger values of
\gamma
therefore correspond to
scenarios in which deviations from coordinated recommendations lead to more
severe system-level delays, thereby implicitly increasing
C_{\text{dev}}
(c.f.,
Theorem

2
).

The coordinator selects a recommendation distribution
z
over joint actions to minimize the weighted agent cost

\textstyle{J_{\text{sys}}(z)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim z}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\bar{J}_{i}(x)\right]},

(25)

where
\omega_{i}\propto 1/\sigma_{i}
assigns smaller weight to agents with higher uncertainty.
Thus, the coordinator prioritizes reducing delay for agents whose cost estimates are more reliable.

Uncertainty in the incentive constraints is modeled through Gaussian perturbations in the deviation costs, following
Equation

5
.
Specifically, each agent’s deviation cost
\Delta J_{i}(\cdot)
includes an additive noise term
\eta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{i}^{2})
.

VI-B

Baselines and evaluation metrics

We compare the proposed chance-constrained correlated equilibrium (
CC-CE
) coordination method with the following baselines:

1.

NE
: Outcome without coordination, represented by a Nash equilibrium.
If multiple equilibria exist, one equilibrium is randomly selected.

2.

Naive-CE
: correlated equilibrium-based coordination that ignores uncertainty and solves the coordination problem in
Equation

4
.

We evaluate coordination performance using two metrics.

1.

Expected system cost
: the expected total delay experienced by all agents under the recommendation distribution, defined by
Equation

25
.

2.

Realized system cost
: the total delay computed using the same cost function in
Equation

25
but evaluated at the realized joint action after agents respond to the recommendation under cost perturbations. This metric captures the performance degradation caused by deviations.

VII
Conclusion

We studied uncertainty-aware coordination in multi-agent systems using chance-constrained correlated equilibria (CC-CE).
We showed that uncertainty contracts the feasible correlated equilibrium region and that active incentive constraints can arise either from structural properties of the game or from uncertainty in cost information.
Using dual sensitivity analysis, we derived an information-gain metric that identifies which uncertainty sources most strongly limit coordination performance.
We also analyzed the role of the confidence level
\alpha
, which improves robustness against deviations but may restrict coordination flexibility.
Numerical experiments confirmed these insights, showed that coordinated outcomes consistently outperform uncoordinated ones, and demonstrated that prioritizing uncertainty reduction using the proposed metric improves coordination performance.
Future work includes a deeper analysis of the role of the confidence level and extending the proposed framework to larger coordination problems with diverse uncertainty structures.
