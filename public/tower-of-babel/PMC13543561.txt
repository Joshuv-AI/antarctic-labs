The effect of the COVID-19 pandemic on surgical volume and residency training program in pediatric otolaryngology
Authors: Nir Tsur, Elchanan Zloczower, Tara Coreanu, Tom Fischer, Yaakov Eyal, Roy Hod
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic significantly disrupted healthcare systems worldwide, prompting substantial changes in medical services, including elective surgical procedures. This study examines the impact of the pandemic on residency training in a tertiary pediatric otolaryngology department in Israel.ObjectiveTo assess the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on surgical volume and the training experiences of pediatric otolaryngology residents, with a focus on changes in surgical case types and frequencies.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective chart review of fifteen residents who completed pediatric otolaryngology rotations at Schneider Children’s Medical Center between 2019 and 2021. The study period was divided into three years: pre-pandemic (2019), first pandemic year (2020), and second pandemic year (2021). Continuous variables were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate.ResultsA total of 1,992 surgical procedures were performed during the study period: 614 (31%) in 2019, 874 (43%) in 2020, and 504 (26%) in 2021. The proportion of urgent procedures increased significantly across the study period, rising from 10.3% in 2019 to 11.9% in 2020 and 20.6% in 2021 (p < 0.001). Foreign body–related surgeries increased to 7% of all procedures in 2021 compared with 4% in both 2019 and 2020 (p = 0.03), whereas infection-related procedures remained relatively stable. The mean number of surgeries performed per resident declined from 64.1 in 2019 and 72.8 in 2020 to 40.0 in 2021, although this reduction did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.204).ConclusionThe COVID-19 pandemic led to considerable fluctuations in surgical volume and case types, significantly affecting pediatric otolaryngology training. These findings highlight the need for adaptable training strategies to maintain educational quality during healthcare crises.Keywords: COVID 19, Surgical Training, Otolaryngology Residency, Pediatric ENT Surgery

Introduction

The residency training program (RTP) in otolaryngology in Israel spans six years and encompasses comprehensive medical and surgical training across the specialty’s broad subspecialties. During the pediatric otolaryngology rotation, residents primarily gain experience with common procedures, including tonsillectomy, adenoidectomy, and ventilation tube insertion. As with other surgical disciplines, a key component of the RTP is acquiring the skills necessary to perform surgeries independently. According to the Israeli Medical Association and the Israeli Society of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery (ISOHNS), residents must perform over 200 surgeries as the primary surgeon across the entire six-year residency program (not limited to pediatric ENT) to qualify for the oral board clinical certification exam. The pediatric otolaryngology rotation contributes substantially to this operative logbook through high-volume core procedures (e.g., adenotonsillectomy and ventilation tube insertion).

In February 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic reached Israel, prompting the government to implement widespread public health measures, including school closures, movement restrictions, and multiple national lockdowns [1]. These interventions significantly altered hospital operations. Elective surgeries were postponed, and physicians, including otolaryngology residents, were reassigned to COVID-19 wards to support the national response [2]. Consequently, surgical training opportunities were reduced, particularly in elective procedures.

Educational disruptions extended beyond the operating room. Academic activities such as teaching sessions, tumor boards, and radiologic reviews were suspended due to social distancing policies. As a surgical specialty heavily reliant on procedural volume, otolaryngology residency training was especially affected by the decline in elective surgeries. Moreover, the high risk of viral transmission during upper airway procedures presented a unique challenge for otolaryngologists compared to other surgical fields [3]. While surgical specialties like general surgery reached 95% of baseline by late 2020, otolaryngology remained a global outlier, showing a sustained 10% volume deficit through 2021 due to the perceived risk of aerosol-generating procedures in the upper aerodigestive tract [4].

Although several studies have explored the broader impact of the pandemic on medical training, data specifically addressing pediatric otolaryngology are limited. For instance, a survey of pediatric otolaryngology fellowship directors found that 77.2% believed COVID-19 significantly disrupted training [5]. Similarly, a nationwide survey in the U.S. reported that 98.3% of otolaryngology centers experienced reduced clinical activity [6]. Krishnan et al. documented a 9.9% decrease in otolaryngologic surgeries in 2021 compared to 2019 [7].

The present study aims to assess the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the RTP in our pediatric otolaryngology unit, focusing on changes in surgical volume and case type during the pandemic years.

Methods

This retrospective study was conducted at Schneider Children’s Medical Center, the largest tertiary pediatric hospital in Israel. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained, and the requirement for patient consent was waived due to the study’s retrospective design and the use of de-identified data. A clinical trial registration number is not applicable due to the retrospective design of the study.

Data were extracted from the hospital’s computerized surgical records for the period between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2021. Surgical cases performed by fifteen otolaryngology residents, at various stages of their training, were included.

The study period was divided into three calendar years:

2019 (pre-pandemic baseline),

2019 (pre-pandemic baseline),

2020 (the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic),

2020 (the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic),

2021 (the Second pandemic year).

2021 (the Second pandemic year).

The primary outcome was the number of surgical procedures performed per resident annually. Secondary outcomes included the mean procedure duration, surgical urgency, and procedure type (e.g., injury-related vs. infection-related).

Urgent surgeries were defined by clinical indication (e.g., foreign body extraction, abscess drainage) or by timing (procedures initiated during evening or night shifts). Infection-related surgeries were defined as operations necessitated by infectious etiologies, including mastoidectomy, endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS), and abscess drainage. The full list of surgical procedures analyzed is summarized in appendix table.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize study variables. Continuous variables are presented as mean (SD) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Comparisons of continuous variables across the three study years (2019, 2020, 2021) were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When the overall ANOVA was statistically significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted with multiple testing adjustment (Tukey or Bonferroni, as appropriate). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were small. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SPSS (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).’

Figure 1 illustrates the monthly distribution of surgeries across the three study years alongside the cumulative number of national COVID-19 cases. For instance, only 8 procedures were performed in April 2020, compared to 57 in April 2019 and 63 in April 2021 (p = 0.03). Across the study period, the proportion of urgent surgeries increased from 10.3% in 2019 to 11.9% in 2020 and 20.6% in 2021 (p < 0.001) (Table 1). This shift reflects a growing relative contribution of time-sensitive, non-deferrable cases during the pandemic. In contrast, the anatomical subsite distribution showed a different pattern. Otologic procedures decreased from 41.2% of total surgeries in 2019 to 36.8% in 2020 and 33.1% in 2021 (p = 0.017) (Table 2).

Surgical volume per resident

In 2021, the average number of surgeries performed per resident dropped by 41.5%, from 64.1 in 2019 and 72.8 in 2020 to 40.0 in 2021. Although notable, this reduction did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.203). The decrease was primarily driven by a significant reduction in elective surgeries performed during morning shifts. In 2021, residents performed an average of 33.3 morning-shift surgeries, compared to 65.2 in 2019 and 58.9 in 2020 (p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study demonstrated a significant decline in the total number of pediatric otolaryngologic surgeries and in surgeries performed per resident during the second year of the COVID-19 pandemic (2021), compared to both the pre-pandemic year (2019) and the first pandemic year (2020). While several international studies reported a recovery of elective surgical activity by 2021, particularly in private healthcare systems and surgical subspecialties [8–10], our findings align with others [7, 11] that suggest ongoing disruption, especially within Israel’s public health system.

During the pandemic years, surgical activity increasingly consisted of urgent or time-sensitive procedures, while high-volume elective surgeries—particularly ventilation tube insertion—declined. Consistent with this pattern, the proportion of urgent surgeries increased significantly during the pandemic, particularly in 2021 (p < 0.001), reflecting the prioritization of time-sensitive, non-deferrable procedures during periods of restricted operating capacity and heightened infection-control measures. However, despite this overall shift toward urgent procedures, a substantial proportion of ambulatory surgical activity was maintained in our cohort. This finding may appear counterintuitive compared with healthcare systems such as those in the United States, where elective procedures were widely cancelled during the pandemic. This difference may be explained by the unique structure of the Israeli healthcare system, which utilizes a mixed public–private model in which elective pediatric ENT procedures are often outsourced to private facilities under the ‘Waiting Times Shortening Program’ (WTSP) [11]. During the 2020 surges, private-sector activity was reduced due to resource prioritization, redirecting part of the elective caseload back to tertiary public centers. In 2021, as private institutions resumed operations, patients opted for private care to avoid the perceived risk of crowded public wards, contributing to the substantial volume drop observed at our center during the second pandemic year [12, 13]. This shift likely contributed to the substantial drop in surgical volume within our center.

Although not statistically significant, we observed a decline in infection-related surgeries in 2021, mirroring global trends of reduced viral respiratory infections during the pandemic [14–16]. This can be attributed to widespread behavioral and public health changes—such as mask mandates, improved hand hygiene, physical distancing, and lockdowns—which have been shown to suppress the transmission of multiple respiratory pathogens [17–19]. These measures may have indirectly contributed to reduced surgical burden from infectious diseases, including pediatric otitis media and sinusitis, as noted in previous studies from Israel [20–22].

Beyond systemic shifts between the public and private sectors in Israel, a major contributor to the decreased surgical volume was the significant reduction in common childhood illnesses resulting from pandemic-related non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs). The global implementation of social distancing, mandatory masking, and school closures effectively suppressed the transmission of respiratory pathogens such as RSV and Influenza, which are the primary risk factors for pediatric ear and sinus disease [23]. This “viral hiatus” disrupted the pathogenic chain in which viral upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs) lead to Eustachian tube dysfunction and subsequent bacterial infections. In Canada, for instance, the rate of bilateral myringotomy and tube insertion decreased by 2.9 times during the pandemic due to reduced viral transmission [24]. Similar trends were reported in Shanghai, where daily outpatient attendance for acute otitis media (AOM) dropped by over 60% compared with pre-pandemic baselines, and in São Paulo, Brazil, where quarantine measures were associated with a massive drop in diagnosed AOM cases [25]. Furthermore, our own local data from Israel corroborated a more than 2-fold reduction in AOM episode rates during the initial pandemic phase [21]. This suppressed epidemiological landscape likely reduced the overall pool of children requiring surgical interventions for chronic or recurrent infectious conditions, thereby contributing to the sustained volume deficit observed in our cohort during the second pandemic phase.

Interestingly, our data revealed a significant increase in foreign body-related surgeries in 2020, particularly after the initial lockdown. This trend is consistent with reports of increased domestic accidents and foreign body ingestions in children during lockdowns [26, 27]. Although some studies found no increase in emergency room visits for foreign bodies [28], the rise in surgical interventions in our study suggests that the cases encountered may have been more severe or required operative management. Simultaneously, trauma-related procedures decreased—likely due to reduced outdoor activities and school closures, as reported in orthopedic literature [29, 30].

From a residency training perspective, the most marked decline occurred in 2021, with a 32.2% reduction in overall surgeries and a 41.5% drop in procedures per resident, compared to 2019. Although the difference in surgical volume per resident did not reach statistical significance, the trend is concerning, particularly given the hands-on nature of surgical education. In 2020, despite the pandemic, residents maintained surgical exposure because patient load shifted to public institutions. However, in 2021, the redistribution of cases to the private sector, combined with ongoing pandemic-related constraints, sharply curtailed training opportunities.

Similar trends have been observed across other surgical residency programs during the pandemic, highlighting the vulnerability of hands-on medical education in crisis settings [31–34]. This underscores the need to rethink surgical training models. Encouragingly, the pandemic also catalyzed innovation: e-learning platforms, mobile applications [35, 36], telemedicine [37], and immersive technologies like virtual and augmented reality [38–40] have emerged as valuable adjuncts to traditional training. Incorporating these modalities into otolaryngology residency programs could help mitigate the impact of future disruptions and modernize the educational framework.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size of fifteen residents is relatively small and represents a heterogeneous group at varying stages of training. This heterogeneity means the pandemic’s impact on surgical skill acquisition may vary significantly between a junior resident reassigned to internal medicine wards and a senior resident facing a deficit in index cases required for board certification. Training seniority was not formally analyzed, and therefore, the potential differential effects of the pandemic on junior versus senior residents could not be assessed. Data were extracted from routine clinical documentation without research-specific inclusion criteria, relying solely on ICD-coded diagnoses. Additionally, differences in institutional policy and local pandemic dynamics may have influenced surgical trends beyond what this study captured.

Conclusions

Our study revealed a substantial decline in pediatric otolaryngologic surgical volume during the second year of the COVID-19 pandemic (2021), with a corresponding reduction in surgical opportunities for residents. This decline likely resulted from systemic shifts in patient distribution between the public and private healthcare sectors. These changes underscore the importance of developing flexible and resilient training models. Additionally, the observed increase in foreign body–related surgeries during pandemic restriction periods represents an association in our dataset and should not be interpreted as causal; further multi-center work is needed to confirm whether this pattern is consistent and to clarify contributing factors. As the medical community prepares for future challenges, integrating technological innovations into residency training will be essential to maintaining educational standards during crises.
