On the density of rational lines on diagonal cubic hypersurfaces, II
Scott Parsell, Kiseok Yeon

Abstract
. In this paper, we establish the expected asymptotic formula for the number of rational lines on a diagonal cubic hypersurface in 18 or more variables, improving on recent work of the second author. This is achieved via a refined mean value estimate for minor arcs that non-trivially exploits a shifting variables argument in both underlying dimensions.



1.
Introduction

The problem of establishing the existence of linear spaces on algebraic varieties has a long history, beginning with work of Brauer
[
9
]
and Birch
[
3
]
. The inductive nature of these arguments, however, does not immediately yield quantitative bounds on the number of variables required, and in fact the first general explicit bounds appeared many years later in work of Wooley
[
19
]
. Subsequent work of Brandes
[
7
]
establishes asymptotic formulae for the number of linear spaces on a variety defined by a system of homogeneous polynomial equations of the same degree, and Brandes
[
8
]
further specializes to the case of rational lines on the hypersurface defined by a single equation.

The case in which the underlying variety is defined by a system of cubic forms has received significant attention, dating to work of Lewis and Schulze-Pillot
[
11
]
. Refinements due to Wooley
[
18
]
show for instance that the hypersurface defined by a cubic form in at least 37 variables contains a rational line. Brandes and Dietmann
[
6
]
later showed that 31 variables suffice in the non-singular case, and this was further improved to 29 in very recent work of Brandes, Dietmann, and Leep
[
5
]
, who also removed the non-singularity hypothesis.

When attempting to harness the full power of the circle method, one often restricts attention to the case of diagonal forms. Suppose that
k\geq 3
, and consider the hypersurface defined by the additive equation

(1.1)

\sum_{i=1}^{s}c_{i}z^{k}_{i}=0,

where
c_{i}\ (1\leq i\leq s)
are non-zero integers. Explicit results concerning linear spaces of arbitrary dimension satisfying (
1.1
) have origins in work of Arkhipov, Karatsuba, and Chubarikov
[
1
]
, with later refinements occurring in
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
, and
[
12
]
. The special case of lines on a diagonal hypersurface of degree
k
is examined in
[
14
]
and
[
22
]
, for example, and a detailed study of the cubic case began with work of the first author
[
13
]
.

To describe further progress, we set up some additional notation.
Our goal is to count pairs of vectors
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s})\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
and
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{s})\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
such that the line
l:\boldsymbol{x}+t\boldsymbol{y}
is contained in the hypersurface defined by
(\ref{hypersurface}).

It follows from the Binomial Theorem that such pairs
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

are precisely the solutions of the system

(1.2)

\sum_{i=1}^{s}c_{i}x_{i}^{k-j}y_{i}^{j}=0\qquad(0\leq j\leq k).

We therefore let
N_{s}(X):=N_{s,k}(X;\boldsymbol{c})
denote the number of solutions
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2s}\cap[-X,X]^{2s}
to
(\ref{system})
.

By making clever use of the cubic case of Vinogradov’s mean value theorem due to Wooley
[
21
]
, Zhao
[
24
]
established the expected asymptotic formula for
N_{s,3}(X)
whenever
s\geq 21
, improving on the bound
s\geq 29
due to the first author
[
17
]
.
Following the work of Bourgain, Demeter, and Guo
[
4
]
on the two-dimensional cubic Parsell-Vinogradov system, one can now recover Zhao’s result in a routine manner by completing the system (
1.2
)
to obtain the required mean value estimate. Very recently, the second author
[
23
]
was able to save two additional variables by devising a multi-dimensional version of Wooley’s shifting variables argument (see
[
20
]
) that leverages the bounds of
[
4
]
to obtain a mean value estimate restricted to minor arcs. The surplus minor arc savings permits an interpolation with lower moments of the relevant exponential sum, thus reducing the total number of variables required.

The purpose of the present paper is to refine the shifting variables argument of
[
23
]
to more fully exploit the two-dimensional nature of the problem.
Our main achievement is a stronger mean value estimate that exploits non-trival summation over both integer shifts, subject to a suitable minor arc condition. It transpires that this allows us to save an extra variable, and we thus obtain the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Whenever
s\geq 18
, one has

N_{s,3}(X)=\sigma X^{2s-12}+O(X^{2s-12-\tau}),

for some
\tau>0
, where
\sigma=\sigma_{\boldsymbol{c}}
is a positive constant depending on the coeffcients
\boldsymbol{c}
.

As indicated above, this sharpens
[
23
, Theorem 1.1]
, where the same conclusion was obtained for
s\geq 19
. Here
\sigma
represents a product of local densities, which is positive for
s\geq 14
as a consequence of
[
13
]
, Lemma 5.1.

We mention that a straightforward generalization of our methods, combined with the results of Guo and Zhang
[
10
]
on two-dimensional Parsell-Vinogradov systems, produces analogous results for larger
k
. In this instance, we would save roughly
k/3
variables over what follows directly from
[
10
]
, in which the total number of variables required to obtain the expected asymptotic formula for
N_{s,k}(X)
is of order
k^{3}
. Since the savings is not especially compelling and does not illuminate any new ideas, we choose not to provide details in this direction.

Throughout we use Vinogradov’s notation, where
f\ll g
means that
f(x)\leq Cg(x)
for some sufficiently large constant
C>0.
We may use
f=O(g)
with the same meaning. We also make liberal use of vector notation, writing
a\leq|{\bf x}|\leq b
to mean that each component of
{\bf x}
lies in
[a,b]
. Finally, we use
\varepsilon
to denote a small positive number, whose value may change from statement to statement.

2.
Mean value estimates on minor arcs

Our main object of interest is the exponential sum

(2.1)

F(\boldsymbol{\alpha}):=\sum_{1\leq x,y\leq X}e(\alpha_{1}x^{3}+\alpha_{2}x^{2}y+\alpha_{3}xy^{2}+\alpha_{4}y^{3}),

with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\alpha_{4}).

For
s\in\mathbb{N}
we further define

J_{s}(X)=J_{s,2,3}(X)
to be the number of integer solutions of the system

\sum_{i=1}^{s}x_{i}^{d-j}y_{i}^{j}=\sum_{i=s+1}^{2s}x_{i}^{d-j}y_{i}^{j}\qquad(0\leq j\leq d,\ 1\leq d\leq 3)

satisfying
1\leq\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\leq X
.
In this section we aim to estimate restricted mean values of the exponential sum
(\ref{deg k sum})
by relating them to
J_{s}(X)
, for which sharp bounds are available
due to Bourgain, Demeter, and Guo
[
4
]
.
The particular estimate we need here, which follows from
[
4
, Theorem 1.5]
, is

(2.2)

J_{10}(X)\ll X^{20+\varepsilon}.

In the shifting variables argument of
[
23
]
one replaces
x
by
x-z_{1}
and
y
by
y-z_{2}
and averages over
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
. In analyzing the effects of these shifts on the cubic equations, it transpires that the linear forms

(2.3)

\displaystyle L_{1}(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle:=3h_{1}\alpha_{1}+2h_{2}\alpha_{2}+h_{3}\alpha_{3}

\displaystyle L_{2}(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle:=h_{1}\alpha_{2}+2h_{2}\alpha_{3}+3h_{3}\alpha_{4}

play a prominent role, as they arise naturally from the partial translation invariance inherent in a Vinogradov-type system with only the quadratic equations inhomogeneous. It is therefore useful to define the corresponding exponential sum

(2.4)

\displaystyle T(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}):=\sum_{1\leq z_{1},z_{2}\leq X}\biggl|\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{h}}|\leq sX^{2}}e(-z_{1}L_{1}({\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{\alpha})-z_{2}L_{2}({\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{\alpha})-\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot{\boldsymbol{h}})\biggr|,

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in[0,1)^{4}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in[0,1)^{3}

From the argument of
[
23
]
, Lemma 3.1, we extract the following fundamental lemma, which relates mean values of
F(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
to pointwise estimates for
T(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
s
be a natural number, and let
\mathfrak{B}
denote any measurable subset of
[0,1)^{4}
. Then one has

\int_{\mathfrak{B}}|F(\boldsymbol{\alpha})|^{2s}\,d\boldsymbol{\alpha}\ll(\log X)^{4s}\,J_{s}(2X)\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{B}\\
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in[0,1)^{3}\end{subarray}}|T(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})|.

Proof.

On inspecting the proof of
[
23
, Lemma 3.1]
, one finds that the argument leading to the inequality (3.21) is unchanged if the set
{\mathcal{M}}_{l}(H)
is replaced by an arbitrary measurable set
{\mathfrak{B}}\subseteq[0,1)^{4}
. Hence the statement of the lemma follows immediately upon inserting the bound (3.22) from
[
23
]
into (3.21).
∎

We now seek mean value estimates on various sets of minor arcs, and Lemma
2.1
reduces the problem to obtaining suitable estimates for
T(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.
We start by defining a set of one-dimensional major arcs

{\mathfrak{M}}(Q)=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a\leq q\leq Q\\
(q,a)=1\end{subarray}}{\mathfrak{M}}_{q,a}(Q),

where

{\mathfrak{M}}_{q,a}(Q)=\{\alpha\in[0,1):|q\alpha-a|\leq QX^{-3}\}.

Further define
{\mathfrak{m}}(Q)=[0,1)\setminus{\mathfrak{M}}(Q)
, and for
l=1,2,3,4
write

{\mathcal{M}}_{l}(Q)=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in[0,1)^{4}:\alpha_{l}\in{\mathfrak{m}}(Q)\}.

By inserting the bound for
T
obtained in (3.27) of
[
23
]
, that is

(2.5)

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal{M}_{l}(Q)\\
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in[0,1)^{3}\end{subarray}}|T(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})|\ll X^{8+\varepsilon}(Q^{-1}+X^{-1}),

into Lemma
2.1
, we recover the estimate of
[
23
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
Q
and
X
be positive numbers with
Q\leq X^{3/2}
, and suppose that
s
is a natural number. Then for
1\leq l\leq 4
one has

\int_{{\mathcal{M}}_{l}(Q)}|F(\boldsymbol{\alpha})|^{2s}\,d\boldsymbol{\alpha}\ll X^{8}\cdot J_{s}(2X)\cdot(Q^{-1}+X^{-1})(\log X)^{4s+1}.

Proof.

This is essentially the statement of
[
23
, Lemma 3.1]
. We note here that the factor of
X^{\varepsilon}
occurring at (3.24) may in fact be replaced by
\log(2r)\ll\log X
, and the result follows on inserting the ensuing bound (3.27) into our Lemma
2.1
.
∎

Our objective is now to establish a sharper bound for
T(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
than that provided in Lemma
2.2
by employing a further dissection of the minor arcs

\mathcal{M}_{l}(Q)
into secondary major and minor arcs.
Before proceeding, we offer a brief heuristic motivation for this refinement.
Recall the definitions (
2.3
) and (
2.4
).
Suppose, hypothetically, that the term
2h_{2}\alpha_{2}
were absent from
L_{1}(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),
so that

L_{1}(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{\alpha})=3h_{1}\alpha_{1}+h_{3}\alpha_{3}.

Then, it follows by summing over
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
that the exponential sum
T(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
would satisfy

T\ll X^{2}\sum_{1\leq z_{1},z_{2}\leq X}\min\left\{X^{2},\frac{1}{\|3\alpha_{1}z_{1}+\alpha_{2}z_{2}+\beta_{1}\|}\right\}\min\left\{X^{2},\frac{1}{\|2\alpha_{3}z_{2}+\beta_{2}\|}\right\}.

Furthermore, if
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{3}
belong to the minor arcs
\mathfrak{m}(Q)
, then by summing over
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
in succession and following the standard argument
[
2
, Lemma 3.2]
, we would obtain

T(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\ll X^{8}(Q^{-1}+X^{-1})^{2}(\log X)^{2}.

This represents a superior bound to (
2.5
), provided that both
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{3}
are in
\mathfrak{m}(Q).

In the actual case where
2h_{2}\alpha_{2}
is present, we can approximate this behavior when
\alpha_{2}
lies in a suitable major arc. Let
q_{2}
be a natural number such that
\|q_{2}\alpha_{2}\|
is sufficiently small, and write
h_{2}=q_{2}l+r
with
0\leq r\leq q_{2}-1
. When summing over
h_{1}
and
l
, the term
2h_{2}\alpha_{2}=2(q_{2}l+r)\alpha_{2}
remains nearly constant
(\text{mod}\ 1)
because
\|q_{2}\alpha_{2}\|
is small. This stability allows us to apply the aforementioned argument over the variables
h_{1}

and
l
to achieve additional savings, provided
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
lies on suitably defined secondary minor arcs.

In Lemma
2.4
below, we formalize this heuristic to provide a refined bound for
T(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
. Before stating the result, we define the relevant secondary major and minor arc dissections and introduce the necessary notation.
Consider a fixed
H\leq X^{3/2}
and
L\leq H^{1/2}
.
We define a modified set of major arcs by

\mathfrak{M}(L;H):=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq a\leq r\leq L\\
(r,a)=1\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{M}_{r,a}(L;H),

where

\mathfrak{M}_{r,a}(L;H):=\{\beta\in[0,1):\ |r\beta-a|\leq LHX^{-3}\}.

For each
\alpha\in\mathfrak{M}(H)
, let
q=q_{\alpha}
denote the smallest natural number for which
\|q\alpha\|\leq HX^{-3}
, and define

\mathfrak{L}^{(\alpha)}(L;H):=\left\{\beta\in[0,1):\ q\beta\ (\text{mod}\ 1)\in\mathfrak{M}(L;H)\right\}.

Additionally, define

(2.6)

\mathfrak{l}^{(\alpha)}(L;H)=[0,1)\setminus\mathfrak{L}^{(\alpha)}(L;H).

It is worth emphasizing that these sets
\mathfrak{L}^{(\alpha)}(L;H)
and
\mathfrak{l}^{(\alpha)}(L;H)
depend on
\alpha\in\mathfrak{M}(H).

With above definitions in mind, for
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
1\leq m,n\leq 4,
we define

(2.7)

\mathcal{L}^{*}_{m,n}(L;H):=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in[0,1)^{4}:\ \alpha_{m}\in{\mathfrak{M}}(H),\ \alpha_{n}\in\mathfrak{l}^{(\alpha_{m})}(L;H)\}.

Define

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{l}(L;H)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{L}_{2,3}^{*}(L;H)\ \ \ \text{when}\ l=1,3\\
&\mathcal{L}_{3,2}^{*}(L;H)\ \ \ \text{when}\ l=2,4\\
\end{aligned}\right..

Let
Q,H,L
, and
X
be positive numbers with
Q\leq H\leq X^{3/2}
and
L\leq H^{1/2}
. We shall show in the proof of Lemma
2.4
that the exponential sum
T(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
has a sharper bound over
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal{M}_{l}(Q)\cap\mathcal{L}_{l}(L;H)
with
1\leq l\leq 4,
compared to the bound (
2.5
). Eventually, in Lemma
2.4
, we shall have a sharper mean value estimate for
F(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
over the set
\mathcal{M}_{l}(Q)\cap\mathcal{L}_{l}(L;H)
, compared to
Lemma
2.2
.

Prior to this, we verify that the measure of the set on which we’re forced to use the weaker estimate (
2.5
) for
T
embedded in Lemma
2.2
is indeed small.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
H,L
and
X
be positive numbers with
H\leq X^{3/2}
and
L\leq H^{1/2}
.
Consider the set

\mathfrak{L}(L;H):=\{(\alpha,\beta)\in[0,1)^{2}:\ \alpha\in\mathfrak{M}(H),\ \beta\in\mathfrak{L}^{(\alpha)}(L;H)\}.

Then, the set
\mathfrak{L}(L;H)
is measurable and

\text{mes}\left(\mathfrak{L}(L;H)\right)\ll L^{2}H^{3}X^{-6}.

Proof.

First, we claim that for a given
\alpha\in\mathfrak{M}(H)
, the measure of
\mathfrak{L}^{(\alpha)}(L;H)
is bounded above by
L^{2}HX^{-3}.
Recall the denominator
q=q_{\alpha}.
With this, let
A\subseteq[0,q)
be a set such that
x\in A
if and only if

x\ (\text{mod}\ 1)
is in
\mathfrak{M}(L;H).
Observe that this set
A
has the property that

\text{mes}(A)=q\cdot\text{mes}(\mathfrak{M}(L;H)).

Meanwhile, we see by the definition of
\mathfrak{L}^{(\alpha)}(L;H)
that
\mathfrak{L}^{(\alpha)}(L;H)=\frac{1}{q}A
. Therefore, we conclude that

(2.9)

\displaystyle\text{mes}\bigl(\mathfrak{L}^{(\alpha)}(L;H)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\text{mes}(A)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\cdot q\cdot\text{mes}(\mathfrak{M}(L;H))

\displaystyle=\text{mes}(\mathfrak{M}(L;H))

\displaystyle\leq L^{2}HX^{-3}.

Next, we shall show that the set
\mathfrak{L}(L;H)
is also measurable set. Consider the decomposition

\mathfrak{M}(H)=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq H\end{subarray}}A_{n},

where

A_{n}=\{\alpha\in\mathfrak{M}(H):\ q_{\alpha}=n\}.

Notice that each set
A_{n}
is a measurable set and
A_{n}
are pairwise disjoint for all
n
.
Therefore, we deduce that

(2.10)

\displaystyle\text{mes}\left(\mathfrak{L}(L;H)\right)

\displaystyle=\text{mes}\left(\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq H\end{subarray}}\{(\alpha,\beta)\in[0,1)^{2}:\ \alpha\in A_{n},\ \beta\in\mathfrak{L}^{(\alpha)}(L;H)\}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq H\end{subarray}}\text{mes}\left(\{(\alpha,\beta)\in[0,1)^{2}:\ \alpha\in A_{n},\ \beta\in\mathfrak{L}^{(\alpha)}(L;H)\}\right).

Meanwhile, we see that the dependence of the set
\mathfrak{L}^{(\alpha)}(L;H)
on
\alpha
is related only to natural numbers
q_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, we find that for fixed
n\in[1,H]\cap\mathbb{N}
, the sets
\mathfrak{L}^{(\alpha)}(L;H)
are equal for all
\alpha\in A_{n}
. Then, we infer by the bound (
2.9
) that

(2.11)

\displaystyle\text{mes}\left(\{(\alpha,\beta)\in[0,1)^{2}:\ \alpha\in A_{n},\ \beta\in\mathfrak{L}^{(\alpha)}(L;H)\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq L^{2}HX^{-3}\cdot\text{mes}(A_{n}).

Thus, we conclude by (
2.10
) and
(\ref{measure bound 2})
that

\displaystyle\text{mes}\left(\mathfrak{L}(L;H)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq H\end{subarray}}\text{mes}\left(\{(\alpha,\beta)\in[0,1)^{2}:\ \alpha\in A_{n},\ \beta\in\mathfrak{L}^{(\alpha)}(L;H)\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq H\end{subarray}}L^{2}HX^{-3}\cdot\text{mes}(A_{n})

\displaystyle\leq L^{2}HX^{-3}\cdot\text{mes}(\mathfrak{M}(H))

\displaystyle\leq L^{2}H^{3}X^{-6},

which completes the proof.
∎

Let
Q
be a positive number. Since
\mathcal{M}_{l}(Q)
is measurable, we infer by Proposition
2.3
together with the definition of
\mathcal{L}_{l}(L;H)
that
\mathcal{M}_{l}(Q)\cap\mathcal{L}_{l}(L;H)
is measurable. The following lemma provides an upper bound for the mean value of
F(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
over the set
\mathcal{M}_{l}(Q)\cap\mathcal{L}_{l}(L;H)
, which is a sharper bound than that in Lemma
2.2
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
Q,H,L
, and
X
be positive numbers with
Q\leq H\leq X^{3/2}
and
L\leq H^{1/2}
, and suppose that
s
is a natural number. Then for
1\leq l\leq 4
one has

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}_{l}(Q)\cap\mathcal{L}_{l}(L;H)}|F(\boldsymbol{\alpha})|^{2s}\,d\boldsymbol{\alpha}

\displaystyle\ll X^{8}\cdot J_{s}(2X)\cdot\left(Q^{-1}+X^{-1}\right)\bigl(L^{-1}+X^{-1/2}\bigr)(\log X)^{4s+2}.

Proof.

We see by reversing the roles of
x
and
y
in the exponential sum
F(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
that the bound for the cases
l=3,4
immediately follows by the bound for the cases
l=1,2
. Thus, it suffices to restrict our attention to the latter cases.

We shall consider the case
l=1
and
l=2
, separately.
First, consider the mean value

\mathcal{I}_{1}:=\int_{\mathcal{M}_{1}(H)\cap\mathcal{L}^{*}_{2,3}(L;H)}|F(\boldsymbol{\alpha})|^{2s}\,d\boldsymbol{\alpha}.

It follows by Lemma
2.1
with
\mathfrak{B}=\mathcal{M}_{1}(Q)\cap\mathcal{L}^{*}_{2,3}(L;H)
that

(2.12)

\mathcal{I}_{1}\ll\,(\log X)^{4s}\,J_{s}(2X)\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(Q)\cap\mathcal{L}^{*}_{2,3}(L;H)\\
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in[0,1)^{3}\end{subarray}}|T(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})|.

Recall the definition (
2.7
) of
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{2,3}(L;H)
. For a given
\alpha_{2}\in\mathfrak{M}(H),
recall the definition of
q=q_{\alpha_{2}}
, so that
\|q\alpha_{2}\|\leq HX^{-3}
. With this
q
, write

(2.13)

h_{2}=q\lfloor X^{2}/H\rfloor r_{1}+qr_{2}+r_{3},

for some
r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}
with

(2.14)

\displaystyle-sH/q\leq

\displaystyle r_{1}\leq sH/q

\displaystyle 0\leq

\displaystyle r_{2}\leq\lfloor X^{2}/H\rfloor-1

\displaystyle 0\leq

\displaystyle r_{3}\leq q-1.

Notice here that
r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}
are uniquely determined for a given
h_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
with
|h_{2}|\leq sX^{2}
.
Recall the definition (
2.4
) of
T(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
, and write the summation over
h_{2}
in
T(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
as three sums over
r_{1},r_{2}
and
r_{3}
using the representation
(\ref{representation h2}).
Then, for a given
\alpha_{2}\in\mathfrak{M}(H)
, one has

(2.15)

T(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\ll X^{2}\sum_{1\leq z_{1},z_{2}\leq X}\bigl|S_{1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},{\bf z})\,S_{2}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},{\bf z})\bigr|,

where

(2.16)

\displaystyle S_{1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},{\bf z})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{|h_{1}|\leq sX^{2}}e(-3\alpha_{1}h_{1}z_{1}-\alpha_{2}h_{1}z_{2}-\beta_{1}h_{1})

\displaystyle\ll\min\left\{X^{2},\frac{1}{\|3z_{1}\alpha_{1}+z_{2}\alpha_{2}+\beta_{1}\|}\right\}

and

(2.17)

\displaystyle S_{2}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},{\bf z})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{|h_{2}|\leq sX^{2}}e(-2\alpha_{2}h_{2}z_{1}-2\alpha_{3}h_{2}z_{2}-\beta_{2}h_{2})

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{r_{1},r_{3}}\biggl|\sum_{r_{2}}e(-2q\alpha_{2}r_{2}z_{1}-2q\alpha_{3}r_{2}z_{2}-q\beta_{2}r_{2})\biggr|

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{r_{1},r_{3}}\biggl|\sup_{I\subseteq\left[1,X^{2}/H\right]}\sum_{r_{2}\in I}e(-2q\alpha_{3}r_{2}z_{2}-q\beta_{2}r_{2})\biggr|

\displaystyle\ll H\cdot\min\left\{\frac{X^{2}}{H},\frac{1}{\|2q\alpha_{3}z_{2}+q\beta_{2}\|}\right\}.

Here the supremum of
I
is over subintervals in
\left[1,X^{2}/H\right]
, and we used summation by parts in order to eliminate the factor
-2q\alpha_{2}r_{2}z_{1}
in the third inequality of (
2.17
), together with the fact that

\|2q\alpha_{2}z_{1}\|\leq 2X\|q\alpha_{2}\|\leq 2HX^{-2}.

We remark here that the range of the summation over
r_{2}
in the second inequality of (
2.17
) may not be over the full interval

We now have the tools needed to prove Theorem
