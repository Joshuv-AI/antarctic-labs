Fast-Dissolving
and Highly Soluble Dasatinib Multicomponent
Crystals Efficiently Inhibit Leukemia Cell Viability

Luan F Diniz, Luiz C G Diógenes, Paulo S Carvalho Jr, Ezequiel A D Junior, Alexia D Nascimento, Maria E Cortés, Rubén Dario Sinisterra, Renata Diniz, Christian Fernandes

Abstract

Dasatinib (DAS) is
a biopharmaceutical classification
system (BCS)
class II drug that is used orally to treat chronic myelogenous leukemia.
However, its very low and pH-dependent aqueous solubility severely
limits its oral bioavailability. To address this biopharmaceutical
limitation, we designed more soluble multicomponent crystalline forms
(MCCs) of DAS. Two novel salt solvate forms were successfully obtained
via solvent evaporation through reactions with maleic and fumaric
acids. A comprehensive solid-state evaluation of these salt solvate
forms was conducted by using X-ray diffraction (SCXRD, PXRD), spectroscopy
(FT-IR and 1H NMR), and thermal (TG and DSC) analyses.
Both MCCs of DAS have displayed substantial enhancements in buffered
aqueous solubility (up 1470-fold) and intrinsic dissolution rate (up
312-fold) compared with the parent drug. Furthermore, the DAS MCCs
exhibited an increased cytotoxicity and selectivity toward tumor cells.
These fulfillments highlight the potential of crystalline salt engineering
to enhance the unsatisfactory oral bioavailability and anticancer
efficacy of the DAS.

Introduction

Crystal-engineered multicomponent
pharmaceutical crystals have
become a consolidated, highly effective, and cost-effective approach
for enhancing the active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs).
−

 Its extensive application in developing innovative APIs and repositioning
of older ones has allowed this approach to gain widespread and unanimous
acceptance in the pharmaceutical field.
−

 Creating custom crystalline salt structures
by incorporating API-complementary counterion into the crystal lattice
while maintaining covalent bonds ensures the drug’s pharmacological
activity and provides a pathway for optimizing its physicochemical
properties, mainly aqueous solubility and dissolution rate.
−

 For basic, ionizable, and weakly soluble APIs, such as dasatinib
(DAS, ), pharmaceutically
acceptable carboxylic acids are typically utilized for drug protonation,
turning it into novel soluble salts.

DAS is a Biopharmaceutical
Classification System
(BCS) class II
drug, which is orally active. It is a multitargeted tyrosine kinase
inhibitor used in the treatment of chronic myelogenous leukemia. Although DAS is ionizable, this API is marketed
in its neutral form, exhibiting low and pH-dependent aqueous solubility
as well as a slow dissolution rate. Consequently,
DAS has a poor oral bioavailability of 14%. Also, its very short half-life of 3 to 5 h requires frequent dosing,
which affects patient compliance due to the occurrence of systemic
side effects. To overcome these biopharmaceutical
issues, several approaches have been employed to date, including salt
solvate crystallization,
,
 cyclodextrin complexation, nanocrystal-based formulations, amorphous solid dispersions, self-nanoemulsifying systems, polymeric
micelles, and hybrid exosomes. For DAS, these investigations have demonstrated
that significantly enhancing the drug’s solubility and dissolution
rate positively benefits its pharmacokinetic profile.

From a
crystal engineering perspective, prior studies on DAS have
concentrated on describing the solid-state structure of multicomponent
crystals (MCCs) without an accompanying proper pharmaceutical evaluation.
Sarceviča et al. and Trimdale-Deksne
et al. successfully determined the crystal
structures of twenty-three DAS solvates formed with various organic
solvents. These solvents included alcohols such as isopropanol, n-butanol, n-pentanol, and isoamyl alcohol, along with dimethyl sulfoxide,
dimethylformamide, ketones like cyclohexanone and 4-methyl-2-pentanone,
esters, chloroform, and nitromethane, among others. They classified
the solvates into three structural packing groups based on molecular
conformation, hydrogen bonding patterns, and layer arrangements but
reported no solubility or dissolution data. Additionally, three recent
publications
,,
 described DAS MCCs with carboxylic acid coformers, although only
one of these is an unambiguously confirmed salt. Mondal et al. reported dasatinib succinate hydrate, confirming
the protonation of DAS through single-crystal X-ray diffraction. They
also investigated dasatinib-l-glutamic acid and dasatinib-4-hydroxybenzoic
acid MCCs, although the ionization states of these were not established
crystallographically. The other two studies, by Abdul Azeeze et al. and Saladi et al., respectively, reported assumed cocrystals (with 4-hydroxybenzoic
acid, 4-chlorobenzoic acid, and sorbic acid) and solvates (with hexanoic,
heptanoic, and octanoic fatty acids).

While these cited studies
provided adequate solid-state characterization,
only one of them has undoubtedly elucidated the crystal structure
of the only known DAS salt to date (succinate hydrate). Regarding
biopharmaceutical performance, the available solubility and dissolution
data for DAS in scientific reports are incomplete and do not adequately
address conditions relevant to physiological processes. For these
reasons, our work is unique. It stands out from recent studies of
DAS cocrystallization because it elucidates salt solvate crystal structures
and characterizes them energetically and conformationally, unlike
prior reports. In addition, it carefully outlines the API biopharmaceutical
profile under conditions that closely resemble physiological conditions.
Finally, it evaluates the cytotoxicity and selectivity toward tumor
cells.

Given the poor water solubility and slow dissolution
rate of DAS,
which considerably restrict its oral bioavailability, we have focused
on an effective crystal engineering strategy to develop two highly
soluble and faster-dissolving salt solvates, aiming to enhance the
biopharmaceutical profile of DAS. These salts solvated have been obtained
from the drug’s reaction with pharmaceutically acceptable acids,
maleic and fumaric. Besides a thorough solid-state characterization
by single-crystal and powder X-ray diffraction (SCXRD and PXRD), energy
framework (EF) calculations, Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy,
proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR), thermogravimetry
(TG), and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), we also performed
equilibrium solubility as well as dissolution studies. A cytotoxicity
evaluation employing the human leukemia cell line THP-1 was conducted
to reveal the enhanced anticancer activity of the new DAS MCCs. All
outcomes acquired provide vital insights into the oncological use
of DAS, as its solvate salts have been proven to be biopharmaceutically
advantageous compared to the parent DAS form.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Dasatinib (purity >99%),
organic solvents (analytical, spectroscopic, or chromatographic grade),
and the coformers (maleic and fumaric acids) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich
Brazil and used as received. Ultrapure water was obtained from a Millipore
Direct-Q3 system and was used directly.

Preparation
of DAS Solid Forms

Dasatinib fumarate salt solvate (DAS-HFum-sol): 200 mg of DAS (0.498 mmol) and
50 mg of fumaric acid (0.498 mmol)
were manually ground in a pestle mortar until a homogeneous mass was
formed by adding 2 mL of hot methanol. The resulting ground material
was redissolved in 10 mL of hot methanol for recrystallization. Suitable
colorless plate crystals of DAS-HFum-sol were observed within 1–2
days by slow evaporation of solvent at room temperature.

Dasatinib maleate salt solvate (DAS-HMal-sol): 200 mg of DAS (0.498 mmol) and 50 mg of maleic acid (0.498 mmol)
were mechanochemically reacted in a pestle mortar by adding 2 mL of
hot ethanol until a homogeneous system was formed. Suitable plate
crystals of DAS-HMal-sol appeared within 1–2 days by recrystallizing
this material in 10 mL of hot ethanol, maintained under slow evaporation
at room temperature.

X-ray Diffraction Analysis

Single-crystal
X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) data were collected at room temperature
(298 ± 2 K) on a Rigaku XtaLAB Synergy diffractometer with a
HyPix detector system using a Cu (λ = 1.54187 Å) microfocus
radiation source. The acquisition, indexing, integration of the reflections,
and unit cell determinations were performed with CrysAlisPro software.
Using Olex2, the structures were solved
by intrinsic phasing with SHELXT and
refined by full-matrix least-squares minimization with SHELXL. The non-hydrogen atoms were refined anisotropically.
All hydrogen atoms were located from electron-density difference maps
and were positioned geometrically and refined using the riding model
[Uiso(H) = 1.2U
eq or 1.5U
eq]. PLATON/SQUEEZE was used to remove disordered
solvent. Crystallographic representations
have been created through the MERCURY 4.3 and CrystalExplorer 21.5 programs.
All CIF files are available at www.ccdc.cam.ac.uk under the
CCDC numbers 2528707 and 2528708.

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) data were acquired at room temperature
on an XRDynamic 500 Anton Paar diffractometer, operating at 40 kV
of voltage and 50 mA of current, in a Bragg–Brentano geometry
and using CuKα radiation (λ = 1.54187 Å). The diffractograms
were collected over an angular range of 3–45° (2θ)
with a step size of 0.02° (2θ) and a constant counting
time of 20 s per step.

Energy Framework Calculations

The
energy frameworks (EFs) were computed based on density functional
theory (B3LYP/6–31G­(d,p)) molecular wave function using CrystalExplorer
21.535 software and CIF files as input.
The total intermolecular interaction energy (E
tot) is given as the sum of the electrostatic (E
ele), polarization (E
pol),
dispersion (E
dis), and exchange–repulsion
(E
rep) components, with scale factors
of 1.057, 0.740, 0.871, and 0.618, respectively. Intermolecular interactions
between two adjacent molecules were ignored when the closest interatomic
distance was higher than 3.8 Å. The resulting EFs were graphed
by linking the centers of mass of molecules so that the cylinder thicknesses
are set in proportion to the magnitude of the intermolecular interaction
energies.

Spectroscopy Analysis

Fourier transform
infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded on a PerkinElmer Spectrum One
spectrometer equipped with an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) device.
In measurements, 64 scans over a range of 4000–650 cm–1 were collected with a spectral resolution of 4 cm–1.

The 1H NMR data were obtained at room temperature
on a Bruker Avance DRX400 spectrometer, operating at 400 MHz, with
a spectrum resolution of 0.12 Hz, a zg 30 pulse program with ns 16,
d1 1s, an acquisition time of 4.09 s, and a spectral width of 20 ppm.
For the acquisition and processing of 1H NMR spectra, the
TopSpin 4.0 software package was used. The phase and baseline were
manually corrected, and the TMS signals were calibrated at 0.00 ppm.
Proton chemical shifts (δ) were given in ppm and coupling constants
(J) in Hz. For the NMR data collection, approximately
10 mg of DAS samples was dissolved in 600 μL of DMSO-d
6 and transferred to NMR tubes.

Thermal Analysis

Thermogravimetric
(TG) analysis was carried out using a Shimadzu TGA-60 thermobalance.
Approximately 5.0 mg of the samples was placed in open alumina pans
and heated under N2 flow (50 mL min–1) from 25 to 500 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C min–1. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) curves were
acquired using a Shimadzu DSC-60 instrument. The samples (2.0 ±
0.2 mg) were placed in open aluminum pans and heated under a N2 atmosphere (50 mL min–1 at a heating rate
of 10 °C) until the degradation temperature of each DAS sample.
The obtained thermal data were analyzed and plotted using the Shimadzu
TA-60 software.

Ultra-Performance Liquid
Chromatography

The quantitative analysis of DAS solid forms
from solubility, dissolution,
and stability studies was conducted on a Waters Acquity UPLC system
equipped with a diode array detector (DAD). Empower 3.0 software was
used for the acquisition and analysis of chromatograms. A Zorbax Eclipse
Plus C18 column (100 mm length × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm particle
size), maintained at 35 °C, was used for separation. The mobile
phase consisted of a mixture of ammonium acetate buffer pH 4.0 and
acetonitrile (75:25, v/v), delivered at a flow rate of 0.6 mL min–1. DAS was detected at 323 nm, and the injection volume
was 5 μL. The method validation followed the ICH Q2 (R2) guideline. The parameters selectivity, linearity, precision,
accuracy, and limits of quantitation and detection were evaluated,
and the main results are summarized in Table S1.

Equilibrium Solubility and Intrinsic Dissolution

Equilibrium solubilities of DAS solid forms were determined through
the shake-flask method at 37 ± 0.5
°C in five dissolution media: 0.01 M HCl solution (pH 2.0), acetate
buffer (pH 3.6), acetate buffer (pH 4.5), phosphate buffer (pH 5.4),
and phosphate buffer (pH 6.8). Suspensions were prepared in triplicate
by stirring at 150 rpm an excess of DAS solid phases into 2 mL of
each dissolution medium, being maintained at 150 rpm for 24 h to ensure
saturation. Afterward, the suspensions were filtered through a 0.45
μm syringe filter and diluted in their respective dissolution
media before being quantified by UPLC-DAD. Upon completing the equilibrium
solubility tests, the solid sediment’s identity was confirmed
through PXRD analysis, and the pH of each dissolution medium was measured
using a pH meter.

Intrinsic dissolution experiments were conducted
in triplicate using a rotating disk dissolution apparatus. Approximately
200 mg of each DAS solid form was compressed with a hydraulic pump
at 1 kN for 1 min to create nonporous and compact disks measuring
0.5 cm2, with one side featuring a flat surface. The disk
attachments were rotated at 100 rpm while submerged in 500 mL of acetate
buffer at pH 3.6, preheated to 37.0 ± 0.5 °C. At specific
time intervals (5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min), 5 mL
of dissolution medium was withdrawn and filtered through a 0.45 μm
syringe filter. The concentration of dissolved DAS was then measured
using UPLC-DAD. Any possible phase transitions during the dissolution
experiments and disk compression were monitored through PXRD from
the undissolved solids.

Stability Studies

DAS solid forms
were exposed under accelerated degradation conditions specified by
ICH, i.e., at 40 °C and 75% relative humidity (RH), for 180 days
to evaluate the drug’s solid-state stability. To achieve these
conditions, a calibrated climate chamber, model 420/CLD-300 from Nova
Ética, has been employed. At the same time, the photostability
of DAS samples was checked through a photodegradation test. Irradiation
of samples was conducted at 20 °C for 5 days in a photostability
chamber EF/0,2/R-F-220 Mecalor equipped with UV and visible lamps,
achieving a light intensity of 1.2 million lux h–1 (visible) and 200 W h m–2 (UV), as recommended
by ICH guidance. In both stability assessments,
the DAS content has been confirmed by UPLC-DAD evaluations.

Cell Lines and Culture Conditions

Fibroblasts L929
and human leukemia THP-1 cell lines were grown in
cell culture flasks containing Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s
medium (DMEM) and Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium (RPMI), respectively,
both containing high glucose (4500 mg L–1), supplemented
with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% antibiotic/antimycotic (penicillin
10,000 units mL–1/streptomycin 10 mg mL–1), 1% l-glutamine, and 1% nonessential amino acids. All
cells were incubated in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 and a temperature of 37.5 °C.

In Vitro Cytotoxicity Tests

The cell viability
of L929 and THP-1 cell lines in indirect contact
with DAS solid forms was evaluated by the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2yl)-2,5-diphenyl
tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. L929 and THP-1 cells were treated
with DAS commercial form (DAS-H2O) and DAS-HFum-sol/DAS-HMal-sol
salt solvate forms at concentrations of 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, and 200
μg mL–1 for 24, 48, and 72 h. Following treatments,
cells were incubated with 10 μL of MTT solution at 5 mg/mL,
and 90 μL of phenol red-free DMEM was added to each well to
form the formazan crystals over 4 h. The absorbance of the formazan
crystals, dissolved overnight in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), was
measured at 570 nm using a spectrophotometer.

Results and Discussion

Crystal Structure Description

DAS
has several functional groups, enabling the formation of diverse supramolecular
motifs. Its molecular structure comprises a pyrimidine–thiazole
core attached to a hydroxyl-piperazine and Cl-phenylacetamide moieties,
which includes eight hydrogen bond acceptors and three hydrogen bond
donors ().
The piperazine nitrogen in DAS can participate in proton–acceptor
interactions (pK

a
 = 6.8),
as observed by its known salt solvate forms. Salt formation with carboxylic
acids is highly expected
,
 (Table S2), offering a promising strategy to enhance the DAS
solubility and dissolution rate. By enabling ionization and providing
hydrogen-bond donors, which otherwise require hydration, this strategy
provides a robust alternative to solvation. DAS has been reported to form numerous solvates and even multicomponent
salt forms; nevertheless, the 3D structures of many of these salt
solvate systems are rarely known, and the relationship between structure
and solubility remains unexplored. Herein, we elucidated the crystal
structure of two DAS solvated salts and correlated the relationship
between crystallographic features, the impact of coformer molecule
insertion, intermolecular interactions, the energy framework, and,
most importantly, the drug’s aqueous solubility, dissolution
rate, cytotoxicity, and selectivity toward tumor cells.

To further
increase the solubility and dissolution rate of DAS, we have prepared
MCCs with fumaric and maleic acids, successfully yielding the DAS-HFum-sol
and DAS-HMal-sol salt solvates. Toward understanding the structural
attributes driving the stabilization of these systems in the solid
state, we provide detailed crystallographic evaluations below. Figure S1 illustrates the asymmetric units (ASUs)
of DAS salt solvates, whereas and S3 summarize the crystallographic
data and H-bond geometric parameters, respectively. Figure S2 shows the electron density maps, confirming the
N atom protonation site of the DAS molecule and, thereby, the salt
formation.

Dasatinib Fumarate (DAS-HFum-sol)
Salt Solvate

The DAS-HFum-sol crystallizes in the monoclinic
space group P21/c (Z’ = 1, ) with one ionized
DAS+ cation,
a hydrogen-fumarate anion (HFum–), and a methanol
solvent molecule in the asymmetric unit (Figure S1a). Methanol acts as a lattice solvent stabilizing the DAS+:HFum– pair through hydrogen bonding. We
acknowledge that a solvated salt containing methanol might limit its
pharmaceutical application. However, the multiple attempts at solvent
exchange in the synthetic process of this salt form did not yield
a novel DAS MCC, as we had intended. Salt formation occurs through
the transfer of a proton from the acid to the piperazine moiety of
the DAS molecule. The ionic pair forms through the R
2
2(8) synthon,
which involves the amine-hydroxyl portion of the DAS+ cation
and the carboxylate moiety of the counterion (a). In the salt solvate form, the DAS+ cation adopts a conformation in which the aromatic pyrimidine–thiazole
core and the mean plane of the pyrimidine ring are 70.9(2)°,
oriented relative to each other. The hydroxyl group adopts a synclinal
orientation relative to the N atom of the pyrimidine ring (N–C–C–O
= −40.2(3)°). Due to steric hindrance, the chlorophenyl
ring adopts an almost orthogonal orientation concerning the central
aromatic system, leading to syn-periplanar orientation between the
amide oxygen and the thiazole sulfur atom (O–C–C–S
= 16.7(2)°).

The reported fumarate displays a conformation that
differs from
its hydrated (commercial form) and succinate salt counterparts.
,
 The hydrated and succinate forms exhibit a more extended conformation
of the ionized group relative to the central molecular core (see Figure S3). In the DAS-HFum-sol, the ionic pairs
are centrosymmetrically linked through a homosynthon involving the
thiazole rings of DAS+ cations, forming a planar unit (b). These dimeric
units are further connected via NH•••O H-bonds
(N7–H7•••O2, 2.958(2)­Å, 172.09(11)°)
between amide groups of adjacent cations, resulting in a 2D sheet
extending along the [010] direction. Within this sheet, neighboring
planar units are oriented nearly orthogonally, forming an angle of
87.09(2)° between the mean planes of adjacent dimers (c). As the anions
are located on the exterior of these motifs, adjacent fumarate anions
are then bridged by methanol molecules, providing the assembly of
the sheets into a 3D structure as shown in d. The packing of these 2D sheets leads to
the formation of polar domains, enriched by the presence of the anions,
whereas the solvent molecules play a crucial role in stabilizing the
overall crystal structure.

Dasatinib Maleate (DAS-HMal-sol)
Salt Solvate

The structural determination of DAS-HMal-sol
presented manifold
challenges. Pronounced disorder effects (disordered ethanol solvent
molecules and substitutional disorders) needed to be modeled, with
only the substitutional disorder being properly treated. In the final
refinement model, the disordered ethanol that fills the solvent-accessible
channels of the crystal structure has been removed by a SQUEEZE procedure.
Despite our repeated efforts to obtain high-quality single crystals,
all solids exhibited twinning. An appropriate twin law has been found;
nevertheless, the refinement did not converge to an acceptable structural
model. Thus, the most reliable and satisfactory model for the DAS-HMal-sol
salt solvate is based on the twinned and “squeezed”
structure. The identity of the compound was unambiguously confirmed
through SCXRD analysis, as shown in its asymmetrical unit representation
(Figure S1b). DAS-HMal-sol crystallizes
in the monoclinic space group C2/c with Z’ = 1 ( and a). Interestingly, its unit cell volume is approximately twice that
of DAS-HFum-sol. Despite the larger unit cell, the calculated density
of DAS-HMal-sol (1.275 g cm–3) is slightly lower
than that of DAS-HFum-sol (1.370 g cm–3), highlighting
the difference in crystal packing between the two salt solvates.

A notable structural distinction
lies in the conformation
of the
DAS+ cation. In the maleate salt solvate, the DAS+ cation exhibits an antiorientation of the nitrogen atom from the
pyrimidine ring relative to the adjacent carbon bearing the methyl
group, with a N–C–C–C torsion angle of 176.7(5)°.
Additionally, within the piperazine moiety, the nitrogen and hydroxyl
atoms adopt a torsion angle of 51.4°. The conformations of DAS
in both the fumarate and maleate salt solvates differ significantly
from those observed in the DAS hydrate and succinate salt (Figure S3). Interestingly, in the structure of
the DAS-HMal-sol salt solvate, there is no direct hydrogen bonding
between the cation and the anion. However, similar to the fumarate
salt solvate, ionic pairs assemble into dimeric units through a homosynthon
formed by interactions involving the central portion of the cations
(b). These
planar units extend into a 2D sheet via NH•••O
H-bonds (N7–H7•••O2, 2.818(5)­Å, 157.67(26)°)
along the [010] direction. Within this motif, adjacent dimers are
oriented at an angle of 66.01(2)° relative to each other, as
measured between their mean planes (c). Notably, the anions remain aligned within
this motif and do not orient themselves outside of it. The motifs
are arranged in an antiparallel manner due to the OH•••COO
H-bonds (O1–H1•••O6, 2.853(8)­Å, 137.04(41)°)
from adjacent dimers. As a result, the packing of these 2D sheets
generates a continuous anionic channel throughout the structure, creating
solvent-accessible regions. As shown in d, the crystal packing of DAS-HMal-sol gives
rise to a significantly more pronounced hydrophilic domain compared
to that observed in the fumarate salt solvate.

Infrared spectroscopy
has confirmed the proton transfer process and accurately identified

the new DAS salt solvates. presents the FT-IR spectra of both DAS monohydrate

 This API exhibits characteristic

Salt formation has been confirmed since these typical

Proton Nuclear Magnetic Resonance

The 1H NMR experiments have been used to determine the
stoichiometric ratio between DAS and the salt formers by analyzing
the integral values of each signal, thereby verifying the sample’s
purity in solution. Assignments of the proton chemical shifts and
spectra interpretation were carried out using DAS 1H NMR
spectra reported in the literature.
,,
 The 1H NMR spectra of both DAS solvated
salts (Figures S4 and S5) exhibited all
the signals required for the DAS molecule, besides the expected signals
corresponding to the maleate and fumarate anions assigned to the =CH
olefinic group at 6.05 and 6.62 ppm. Additionally, the integral values
in the 1H NMR spectra of the new DAS salts solvated confirm
that the crystal structures contain DAS+ cation and the
corresponding counterion in a 1:1 ratio, attesting to the purity of
these crystalline phases in solution. It is worth mentioning that
typical signals of methanol and ethanol molecules appeared in the
spectra, confirming the solvatomorphic nature of the new DAS MCCs.
For ethanol, the characteristic signals were observed at approximately
1.0 ppm (−CH3), 3.4 ppm (−CH2),
and 4.5 ppm (−OH). For methanol, the typical signals were found
at around 3.2 ppm (−CH3) and 5.0 ppm (−OH),
with the last signal being discrete and broad.

Powder
X-ray Diffraction

Powder
X-ray diffraction (PXRD) is an undoubtedly useful characterization
tool for demonstrating the formation of multicomponent crystals from
a crystallization screening. Changes
in the 2θ peak positions observed in the experimental diffractograms,
compared to the starting materials, indicate the establishment of
a new and unambiguous crystalline phase. PXRD confirms that the single
crystal mounted for the SCXRD data collection is representative of
the synthesized bulk sample and is also used herein to check the purity
of the DAS salt solvates. First, as presented in a, both experimental PXRD patterns
of DAS maleate and fumarate are entirely distinct from the DAS raw
material. Second, experimental patterns exhibit excellent agreement
with the theoretical ones, calculated from the CIF files obtained from the refinements. Hence, we confirm that all
single crystals formed correspond to pure and unique salt solvate
crystalline phases, which also represent the entire synthesized sample.

Thermal
Characterization

The thermal
profile of DAS solid forms was revealed by DSC and TG, as shown in b. DSC and TG thermograms
of the DAS commercial monohydrate form were included for comparison.
The DSC curve of parent DAS exhibits two well-defined endothermic
peaks at 96.3 °C (T
onset = 73.01
°C) and 285.6 °C (T
onset = 281.50
°C) assigned to the dehydration and melting/degradation processes
of the API, respectively. The early peak has been accompanied by an
initial mass loss of approximately 3.1% at around 100 °C on the
TG curve, consistent with the release of one water molecule from the
crystal structure. The second melting/degradation peak correlated
with a gradual mass loss in the TG curve after 287 °C. After
dehydration, DAS remains stable in the anhydrous form. The new solvated salts DAS-HMal-sol and DAS-HFum-sol
exhibited distinct thermal behavior compared to pure DAS, reflecting
their more diverse and less cohesive salt crystal packing.

The
thermal profiles of DAS-HMal-sol and DAS-HFum-sol salt solvates are
similar, showing two endothermic peaks in DSC curves and a gradual
thermal decomposition in TG thermograms after ∼180 °C.
The first DSC peak is assigned to the salt desolvation, while the
second one (between ∼192–194 °C) is attributed
to the salt melting/degradation process. For DAS-HMal-sol, it is noted
that residual solvent molecules, specifically ethanol, remaining in
accessible voids of the structure are released close to 134.0 °C,
as evidenced by the discrete endothermic event and mass loss at ∼2.5%
in the DSC and TG curves, respectively. As previously demonstrated
in the crystallography and 1H NMR spectroscopy sections,
ethanol molecules are not only present in the DAS-HMal-sol crystal
structure but also fill, nonhomogeneously, part of the solvent-accessible
voids, being consistent with a nonstoichiometric solvated salt (1:1:0.35,
drug:coformer:solvent). For DAS-HFum-sol, desolvation occurred only
at 166.4 °C since the methanol molecules in the structure are
strongly H-bonded to fumarate anions. In this temperature range, a
mass loss of ∼4.5% is observed in the corresponding TG curve.
This value corresponds to the release of approximately one molecule
of methanol from the crystal structure, which aligns with the stoichiometry
of 1:1:1 (drug:coformer:solvent), which provides a solvent weight
loss theoretical value of about 5.0%. Therefore, based on the results
from the DSC and TG curves and degradation onset temperatures, the
following order of thermal stability for DAS solid forms has been
established: DAS-H2O > DAS-HFum-sol ≈ DAS-HMal-sol.

Equilibrium Solubility and Intrinsic Dissolution
Profiles

Aqueous solubility is a crucial property of solid
drug products, significantly affecting the bioavailability of an API
and, consequently, the pharmacological response. It is well established that the salt formation strategy
readily fine-tunes this attribute, especially for ionizable APIs,
and is extensively used for this purpose.
,
 For
DAS, which displays low and pH-dependent solubility, the synthesis
of more soluble salt forms is a primordial requirement for enhancing
its oral bioavailability. Thus, the equilibrium solubility plot of
unmodified commercial DAS-H2O and its reported salt solvate
forms in media covering physiological conditions is depicted in a. Overall, remarkable
improvements in drug solubility were achieved in all dissolution media
assessed, achieving our central target. In terms of stability, the
new DAS-solvated salts did not significantly affect the pH of the
dissolution media after the solubility test. PXRD data revealed that
the structural identity of the solid residues and the undissolved
disks from the solubility and dissolution experiments remained unchanged,
excluding the occurrence of phase transitions and proving the stability
of the newly formed solids after dissolution/solubilization. The stability
data mentioned are not shown here.

Indeed, DAS solubility is very low and pH-dependent,
consistent
with data described in the literature with the highest values in acidic media. The new DAS-HMal-sol and
DAS-HFum-sol salt solvates, which followed this pH-dependent solubility
trend, achieved solubilities that were remarkably superior across
all dissolution media evaluated (). Corroborating our expectations, DAS-HMal-sol showed
a significant API solubility improvement, reaching an impressive 1470-fold
increase at pH 4.5. At pH values of 5.4 and 6.8, DAS-HMal-sol was
approximately 465 and 838 times more soluble than DAS-H2O. Although more discretely, DAS-HFum-sol also provided considerable
enhancements in the solubility of DAS in all dissolution media, being
around 85 times more soluble compared to the parent drug at pH 6.8.
Hence, the following solubility order can be established: DAS-HMal-sol
> DAS-HFum-sol > DAS-H2O.

In addition
to conducting solubility studies, we also
determined
the intrinsic dissolution rates (IDRs) of the DAS solid forms to establish
the effect of the solid-state changes on drug dissolution kinetics.
The pH 5.4 chosen closely resembles the pH of the intestinal portion,
where DAS is most absorbed in vivo. The new salt solvate forms of maleate and fumarate presented
more favorable IDRs than hydrated DAS (). The DAS-HMal-sol (0.572 mg cm–2 min–1) and DAS-HFum-sol (1.558 mg cm–2 min–1) exhibited a 114- and 312-fold faster dissolution
rate, respectively, compared to DAS-H2O (0.005 mg cm–2 min–1), as illustrated in b. Surprisingly,
the DAS-HFum-sol salt solvate possibly exhibited a further “spring-parachute”
effect in the initial hours of intrinsic
dissolution experiments, and this eventual metastability of the dissolved
species can justify the very high IDR found for this salt solvate.
Accordingly, the following IDR order can be established: DAS-HFum-sol
> DAS-HMal-sol > DAS-H2O.

Both solubility and
intrinsic dissolution tests revealed a remarkable
increase in drug solubility upon salt formation, and this can be correlated
with some crystallographic features of these solvated salts. The solid-state
arrangement of DAS with maleate and fumarate counterions derived from
the coformers, rich in atoms O and highly avid for hydrogen bonds,
enhances the occurrence of polar supramolecular synthons and contributes
to the emergence of hydrophilic regions in the crystal structures.
From an energetic framework point of view, the insertion of coformers
into the crystal lattice generates less efficient 3D arrangements
and, consequently, less energetically stable ones (see next ). These combined
solid-state attributes justify, in part, the higher IDRs and water
solubility of these new DAS salt solvates compared with the DAS neutral
form. This result confirms that the salt formation of DAS has a noteworthy
influence on the solubility and IDR of the API. Further, it indicates
that both solvated salts tend to readily solubilize in the medium
mimicking the physiological one, which may favor drug absorption.

Stability Outcomes

In addition to
improved solubility and dissolution, the DAS crystal forms also demonstrated
high solid-state stability. Stability data for newly synthesized and
unstressed DAS solid forms compared to samples exposed to accelerated
degradation conditions are summarized in . All initial samples have a high purity
of almost 100% of DAS. The DAS solvated salt forms retained almost
entirely their original form after 5 days of exposure to UV radiation,
proving that solvated salts DAS-HMal-sol and DAS-HFum-sol are also
highly photostable. Resemble stability results have been found after
DAS solid samples are maintained for 180 days at 40 °C and 75%
RH, indicating that high temperature and relative humidity have low
degradative potential for the samples.

Energy Framework Analysis

To understand
the energetic contributions of supramolecular motifs in stabilizing
interaction forces in DAS MCCs, energy framework studies have been
utilized. Figures S6–S8 depict the
relative magnitude, and illustrates the topology of the interaction energies for
DAS solid forms. The DAS+ cation, the counterparts maleate/fumarate
anions, and solvent/water molecules are stabilized by both Coulombian
(red) and dispersive (green) energies. Solvent molecules (water, methanol,
and ethanol) have been considered in the calculations to evaluate
their contribution to crystal lattice stabilization. For DAS-HMal,
EF calculations conducted both with and without the solventspecifically,
disordered ethanol in the solvent-accessible structural channelsproduced
very similar results. This suggests that, for this particular salt
structure, the solvent has a minimal effect on the crystal lattice
stabilization. Due to the difference in supramolecular architectures,
each DAS MCC exhibits a unique and irregular energy topology in the
solid state. Nevertheless, a thorough examination of the stabilizing
interaction magnitude for each MCC may clarify the reason for the
high aqueous solubility and lower thermal stability of DAS after its
salt formation. It is well established that crystal structures efficiently
packed are more thermally stable. On the other hand, the aqueous solubility
of the API is generally lower. The DAS
commercial form corroborates this statement; we only need to observe
its lower energy framework and high thermal stability (), besides its low aqueous solubility
(). Following
this premise, both DAS-HMal-sol and DAS-HFum-sol forms are significantly
less stable in terms of energy framework, and this possibly directly
reflects their high aqueous solubilities, rapid IDRs, and low thermal
stabilities. Despite the nontriviality between the interaction energy
framework and the drug’s physicochemical properties, our results
reinforce that aqueous solubility and thermal stability are properties
that are prone to being inversely proportional to lattice energy in
crystals.

Leukemia
Cell Line Viability

The
cytotoxicity evaluation of the DAS solid forms in the fibroblast L929
culture demonstrated that none of the MCCs showed toxicity to this
cell line. The percentages of cell viability found for the DAS MCCs
in all concentrations tested (20–200 μg/mL) and after
the three exposure periods (24, 48, and 72 h) were superior to 80%,
indicating high cytocompatibility (grade 0, noncytotoxic) according
to the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 10993–5. In contrast, when human THP-1 leukemia cells
were exposed to DAS MCCs, under the same conditions as L929 fibroblasts,
we observed significant reductions in cell viability, reaching up
to 56% of inhibition at the highest concentrations and over prolonged
periods tested. DAS in its native form (DAS-H2O) was considerably
less effective at inhibiting THP-1 cell viability than its solvated
salt forms. Overall, as presented in , the new DAS solvated salts are predominantly
classified as grade 1 (slightly cytotoxic), with some points reaching
grade 2 (moderately cytotoxic) according to ISO 10993–5. This
suggests that there are subcytotoxic effects that do not cause appreciable
cell death. In summary, the cytotoxicity analysis undertaken indicates
a partial selectivity toward tumor cells, suggesting metabolic or
cellular stress effects without causing significant apoptosis or necrosis.

Conclusions

A crystalline salt engineering
strategy has yielded two MCCs of
DAS with the pharmaceutically acceptable maleic and fumaric carboxylic
acids. Both DAS-HMal-sol and DAS-HFum-sol forms substantially increased
DAS solubility and dissolution rate, making these solvated salts useful
and applicable for enhancing poor oral bioavailability. Structural
elucidation by SCXRD revealed 3D supramolecular arrangements enriched
with polar domains and stabilized mainly by H-bonds. Spectroscopic
(FT-IR and 1H NMR), PXRD, and thermal (DSC and TG) analyses
have indicated the formation of unique and highly pure salt solvates.
Moreover, in the solubility and intrinsic dissolution tests, a remarkable
improvement in these essential attributes has been achieved by new
DAS solid forms. DAS-HMal-sol, for instance, displayed a solubility
approximately 1470 times higher at pH 4.5 compared with parent DAS.
In terms of cytotoxicity, the DAS MCCs maintained/enhanced the viability
of L929 fibroblasts while slightly reducing the cell viability of
the human leukemia cell line THP-1, suggesting an interesting selectivity
toward tumor cells. Therefore, our investigation demonstrates that
the salt formation of DAS with maleic and fumaric acids provides the
central requirement for enhancing the oral bioavailability of DAS,
i.e., high and rapid solubilization of the API in physiologically
like media.
