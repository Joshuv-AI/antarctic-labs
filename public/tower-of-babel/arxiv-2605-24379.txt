On the complexity of extensions of non-archimedean Polish groups admitting a compatible complete left-invariant metric Thanks: Research is partially supported by the National Natural Science Foundation of China (Grant No. 12271264).
Longyun Ding, Xu Wang

Abstract
. In this article, motivated by a problem asked by Allison and Panagiotopoulos [ 1 ] , we study a problem concerning the complexity of group extensions within a hierarchy (denoted by α \alpha -CLI and L- α \alpha -CLI) on the class of non-archimedean CLI Polish groups: Given a non-archimedean Polish group G G and one of its closed normal subgroup N N , suppose N N and G / N G/N are α \alpha -CLI and β \beta -CLI, respectively. Is G G always ( α + β ) (\alpha+\beta) -CLI? We provide a positive answer under a certain additional assumption. We then construct two examples yielding negative answers: (1) for each countably infinite ordinal α \alpha , there exists a group G G that is not α \alpha -CLI, but G G has a 1 1 -CLI normal subgroup N N such that G / N G/N is proper α \alpha -CLI; (2) there exists a proper 3 3 -CLI group U U that has an abelian normal subgroup N N such that U / N U/N is also abelian. These examples also provide negative answers to the original problem raised by Allison and Panagiotopoulos. Finally, we show that if N N and G / N G/N are α \alpha -CLI and β \beta -CLI with β > 0 \beta>0 , respectively, then G G is β ⋅ ( ω ⋅ α + 1 ) \beta\cdot(\omega\cdot\alpha+1) -CLI, which gives an upper bound on the complexity of the extended group.

1.
Introduction

A
Polish
group is a topological group with the underlying topology Polish. A
non-archimedean
Polish group is a Polish group that has an open neighborhood basis of the identity element consisting of open subgroups. A theorem of Becker and Kechris (cf.
[
2
, Theorem 1.5.1]
) states that a Polish group
G
is non-archimedean iff
G
can be embedded as a closed subgroup of
S_{\infty}
, i.e., the permutation group on
\omega
. A metric
d
on a group
G
is said to be
left-invariant
if
d(gh,gk)=d(h,k)
for any
g,h,k\in G
. A CLI group is a topological group that admits a compatible complete left-invariant metric.

Malicki
[
6
]
introduced the notion of the orbit tree for closed subgroups of
S_{\infty}
.
He proved that a closed subgroup of
S_{\infty}
is CLI iff its corresponding orbit tree is well-founded.
Moreover, he showed the class of all CLI Polish groups is
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{1}
non-Borel.
Subsequently, Xuan
[
7
]
modified the definition of the orbit tree, and further discussed the relationship between the rank of an orbit tree and topological aspects of a non-archimedean Polish group. He showed that a non-archimedean Polish group is locally compact iff its corresponding orbit tree has finite rank.
Later, Ding and Wang
[
3
]
also modified the definition of the orbit tree, by which they introduced a hierarchy on the class of all non-archimedean CLI Polish groups, denoted by
\alpha
-CLI and L-
\alpha
-CLI.
Allison and Panagiotopoulos
[
1
]
defined the notion of
\alpha
-balancedness for general Polish groups.
They used it to give a hierarchy that stratifies the class of all CLI Polish groups. In detail, given a Polish group
G
, they defined
\mathrm{rk}(V,U)
for
V,U\subseteq_{1}G
, where
V\subseteq_{1}G
indicates that
V
is an open neighborhood of identity element of
G
. By letting
\mathrm{rk}(G)=\sup\{\mathrm{rk}(V,G)+1:V\subseteq_{1}G\}
, they proved that
G
is CLI iff
\mathrm{rk}(G)
is a countable ordinal. For any ordinal
\alpha
,
G
is said to be
\alpha
-balanced if
\mathrm{rk}(G)\leq\alpha
. As an application of the notion of
\alpha
-balancedness, they further proved that the class of all CLI Polish groups forms a
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}^{1}
-complete set.

A well-known result states that for a Polish group
G
and a closed normal subgroup
N
of
G
,
G
is CLI iff both
N
and
G/N
are CLI (cf.
[
5
, Theorem 2.2]
). Allison and Panagiotopoulos in
[
1
]
raised the following problem:

Problem 1
.

Let
H
be a closed normal subgroup of a topological group
G
. Is it true that

\mathrm{rk}(G)\leq\sup\{\mathrm{rk}(V,H;H)+\mathrm{rk}(G/H):V\subseteq_{1}H\}?

Here
\mathrm{rk}(V,H;H)
means that the underlying topological group for function
\mathrm{rk}(V,H)
is
H
.

Instead of solving this problem directly, we ask a similar problem:

Problem 2
.

Let
N
be a closed normal subgroup of a non-archimedean Polish group
G
. Suppose
N
is
\alpha
-CLI and
G/N
is
\beta
-CLI, where
\alpha,\beta<\omega_{1}
. Is
G

(\alpha+\beta)
-CLI?

To build a connection between these two problems, we first discuss the relationship between a non-archimedean Polish group being
\alpha
-balanced and being
\alpha
-CLI. We show that

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G
be a non-archimedean Polish group and
\alpha<\omega_{1}
. Then
G
is
\alpha
-CLI iff
G
is
(1+\alpha)
-balanced.

In view of this theorem, a positive answer to Problem 1 would yield a positive answer to Problem 2. In case of the semidirect products, we obtain a positive answer to Problem 2 under a certain assumption (see Theorem
4.3
). We then apply this theorem to two classes of examples: the wreath products
G\wr H
and the semidirect products
G\ltimes{\mathbb{Z}}^{\omega}
.

However, these results cannot be generalized to all non-archimedean CLI Polish groups. In fact, the following result allows us to construct a counterexample to Problem 2:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a countably infinite group, and let
G
be a closed subgroup of
{\mathrm{Aut}}(\Gamma)
. If
G
is proper
\alpha
-CLI, then
A=G\ltimes_{\rho}\Gamma
is proper L-
\alpha
-CLI, where
\rho:G\to{\mathrm{Aut}}(\Gamma)
is given by

\rho(g)(\gamma)=g(\gamma)
for
g\in G
and
\gamma\in\Gamma
.

Consequently, we may choose a proper
\alpha
-CLI group
G
for some
\alpha\geq\omega
, and define
A=G\ltimes_{\rho}\Gamma
. Then
A
is proper L-
\alpha
-CLI, and hence not
(1+\alpha)
-CLI.

In
[
3
]
, we define the notion
{\mathrm{rank}}(G)
so that
{\mathrm{rank}}(G)\leq\alpha
iff
G
is L-
\alpha
-CLI . Note that in Theorem
1.2
we have
{\mathrm{rank}}(A)={\mathrm{rank}}(G)=\alpha
and
{\mathrm{rank}}(\Gamma)=0
. One may expect that

{\mathrm{rank}}(G)\leq{\mathrm{rank}}(N)+{\mathrm{rank}}(G/N)

holds for all non-archimedean Polish groups
G
and their closed normal subgroups
N
. But this is refuted by the following more fundamental counterexample:

Theorem 1.3
.

There exists a non-archimedean Polish group
U
that is proper
3
-CLI, although
U
has a closed normal subgroup
N
such that both
N
and
U/N
are abelian, thus
1
-CLI.

Surprisingly, despite the negative answer to Problem 2, we still managed to find an upper bound for the complexity of group
G
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
G
be a non-archimedean Polish group,
N
be a proper closed normal subgroup of
G
. If
N
is
\alpha
-CLI and
G/N
is
\beta
-CLI, then
G
is
\beta\cdot(\omega\cdot\alpha+1)
-CLI.

Note that this upper bound is quite large. For instance, even if both
N
and
G/N
are
1
-CLI, the upper bound in the above theorem for
G
is
(\omega+1)
-CLI.
Therefore, we would like to know whether the upper bound
\beta\cdot(\omega\cdot\alpha+1)
-CLI given in the above theorem is optimal.

Preliminaries

Let
\alpha

\omega(\alpha)=\max\{0,\lambda:\lambda\leq\alpha\mbox{ is a limit ordinal}\}.

Then

Let

Let

. For a subgroup

of

A topological space is

if it is separable and completely metrizable. A topological group is
Polish
if its underlying topology is Polish. Let
G
be a Polish group and
X
a Polish space. An action of

on
X

G\curvearrowright X
, is a map

that satisfies
a(1_{G},x)=x
and
a(gh,x)=a(g,a(h,x))
for
g,h\in G
and
x\in X
. The pair
(X,a)
is called a
Polish
G
-space
if
a
is continuous. When the action is understood from context, we write
g\cdot x
instead of

.
The
orbit equivalence relation

E_{G}^{X}
is defined as
xE_{G}^{X}y\iff\exists g\in G\,(g\cdot x=y)
. Note that the
E_{G}^{X}
-equivalence class of
x
is
G\cdot x=[x]_{G}=\{g\cdot x:g\in G\}
, which is also called the
G
-orbit
of
x
. The class of all
G
-orbits is
X/G=\{[x]_{G}:x\in X\}
. Similarly, for
A\subseteq X
, the
E_{G}^{X}
-saturation of
A
is
G\cdot A=[A]_{G}=\{g\cdot x:g\in G\land x\in A\}
.

Let
E,F
be two equivalence relations on sets
X,Y
, respectively. A
homomorphism
from
E
to
F
is a function
f:X\to Y
satisfying

\forall\,x,y\in X\left(x\,E\,y\implies f(x)\,F\,f(y)\right);

and a
reduction
from
E
to
F
is a function
f:X\to Y
satisfying

\forall\,x,y\in X\left(x\,E\,y\Longleftrightarrow f(x)\,F\,f(y)\right).

In particular, if there exists a reduction from
E
to
F
which is a bijection, we say that
E
is
isomorphic
to
F
.

Let
<
be a binary relation on a set
T
. We say that
(T,<)
is a
tree
if

(1)

\forall s\in T\,(s\not<s)
,

(2)

\forall s,t,u\in T\,((s<t\land t<u)\implies s<u)
,

(3)

\forall s\in T\,(|\{t\in T:t<s\}|<\omega\land\forall\,t,u<s\,(t=u\lor t<u\lor u<t))
.

For
s\in T
, we define the
length
of
s
as
{\rm lh}(s)=|\{t\in T:t<s\}|
. It is clear that
s<t
implies
{\rm lh}(s)<{\rm lh}(t)
. For
n\in\omega
, we denote the
n
-th level of
T
by

L_{n}(T)=\{s\in T:{\rm lh}(s)=n\}.

Each element in
L_{0}(T)
is called a
root
of
T
.

Let
(T,<)
be a tree. We say that
T
is
well-founded
if any non-empty subset of
T
contains at least a maximal element, or equivalently (under AC), if
T
contains no infinite strictly increasing sequence. Let
T
be a well-founded tree. We define the rank function
\rho_{T}
on
T
inductively as

\rho_{T}(s)=\sup\{\rho_{T}(t)+1:s<t\land t\in T\}

for
s\in T
. If
\rho_{T}(s)=0
, we say that
s
is a
terminal
of
T
. Next we define

\rho(T)=\sup\{\rho_{T}(s)+1:s\in T\}.

So
\rho(T)=0
iff
T=\emptyset
. It is clear that
\rho(T)=\sup\{\rho_{T}(s)+1:s\in L_{0}(T)\}
. If
L_{0}(T)=\{s_{0}\}
is a singleton, then
\rho(T)=\rho_{T}(s_{0})+1
is a successor ordinal.

For
s\in T
, we define

T_{s}=\{t\in T:s=t\vee s<t\}.

Since
L_{0}(T_{s})=\{s\}
, we have
\rho(T_{s})=\rho_{T}(s)+1
. For the convenience of discussion, we let
T_{s}=\emptyset
whenever
s\notin T
. Note that
\rho(T_{s})
is always a non-limit ordinal regardless of
s\in T
or not.

We list some facts about computing the rank of nodes in a well-founded tree.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
T
be a well-founded tree, then for all
s\in T
,

\displaystyle\rho_{T}(s)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\rho_{T}(t)+1:s<t\land t\in T\land{\mathrm{lh}}(t)={\mathrm{lh}}(s)+1\}.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
(T,<)
be a well-founded tree,
k\in\omega
. For any
s\in L_{k}(T)
and
m>k
, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{T}(s)\leq\sup\{\rho_{T}(t)+1:t\in L_{m}(T)\}+(m-k-1).

Let
(S,<)
and
(T,<)
be two trees. A map
\phi:S\to T
is said to be an
order-preserving map
if

\forall s,t\in S\,(s<t\implies\phi(s)<\phi(t)).

It is said to be an
order-preserving embedding (isomorphism)
if it is injective (bijective) and

\forall s,t\in S\,(s<t\iff\phi(s)<\phi(t)).

If an order-preserving map
\phi
additionally satisfies
{\mathrm{lh}}(\phi(s))={\mathrm{lh}}(s)
for each
s\in T
, then we say
\phi
is
Lipschitz
. Note that if there is an order-preserving map
\phi:S\to T
and
T
is well-founded, then
(S,<)
is also well-founded; moreover, we have
\rho(S)\leq\rho(T)
.

Next, we recall the definition of
\alpha
-CLI groups. We begin by reviewing orbit trees. Given a set

\mathcal{E}=(E_{n})
be a decreasing sequence of equivalence relations on

. We define the

of

T_{\mathcal{E}}^{X}=\{(n,C):n\in\omega\wedge C\mbox{ is a nonsingleton $E_{n}$-class}\},

ordered by

(n,C)<(m,D)\iff n<m\land C\supseteq D.

We recall a useful criterion to determine whether there exists an order-preserving map between orbit trees.

Proposition 2.3

(
[

, Proposition 3.4]
)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{E}=(E_{n})
and

(2)

Let

3.
\alpha

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose

Let

\rho(T_{\mathcal{H}}^{X})\leq\rho(T_{\mathcal{G}}^{X})

Proof.

Define

\theta(x)=x
. For each

and

\rho(T_{\mathcal{H}}^{X})\leq\rho(T_{\mathcal{G}}^{X})

∎

For a closed subgroup
G

G_{\langle n\rangle}=\{f\in G:\forall\,i<n\left(f(i)=i\right)\}.

For any

It follows that

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
G
be a closed subgroup of
S_{\infty}
, and
\mathcal{G}=(G_{\langle n\rangle})\in{\mathrm{dgnb}}(G)
. Then we have

\rho(\mathcal{G})=\rho(T_{\mathcal{G}}^{\omega}).

Proof.

Fix a

, and define
\delta:G\to G^{k}
as
\delta(g)=(g,g,\dots,g)
. Clearly,
\delta(G)
is a closed subgroup of

. Let
\mathcal{G}^{k}=(G_{\langle n\rangle}^{k})\in{\mathrm{dgnb}}(G^{k})
and
\mathcal{H}^{k}=(\delta(G)\cap G_{\langle n\rangle}^{k})\in{\mathrm{dgnb}}(\delta(G))
. By
[
3
, Lemma 4.3]
, we obtain
\rho(T_{\mathcal{G}^{k}}^{\omega^{k}})=\rho(T_{\mathcal{G}}^{\omega})
. Then according to Lemma
3.1
, we have
\rho(T_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{\omega^{k}})\leq\rho(T_{\mathcal{G}}^{\omega})
.

Consider an action of
G
on
\omega^{k}
, which is defined as

g\cdot(a_{0},\dots,a_{k-1})=(g(a_{0}),\dots,g(a_{k-1}))

for
g\in G
and
(a_{0},\dots,a_{k-1})\in\omega^{k}
.
Let
E_{n}=E_{G_{\langle n\rangle}}^{\omega^{k}}
and
F_{n}=E_{\delta(G)\cap G_{\langle n\rangle}^{k}}^{\omega^{k}}
.
It is easy to see that
E_{n}
is isomorphic to
F_{n}
for each
n\in\omega
, witnessed by the identity map on
\omega^{k}
. Therefore, using Proposition
2.3
(2), we can see that
T_{\mathcal{G}}^{\omega^{k}}
is isomorphic to
T_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{\omega^{k}}
.

Next, define
\theta:G/G_{\langle k\rangle}\to\omega^{k}
as
\theta(gG_{\langle k\rangle})=(g(0),\dots,g(k-1))
. For each
n\in\omega
, let
E_{n}^{\prime}=E_{G_{\langle n\rangle}}^{G/G_{\langle k\rangle}}
and
F_{n}^{\prime}=E_{G_{\langle n\rangle}}^{\omega^{k}}
. Then for any
n\in\omega
, it is straight forward to check that,
\theta
is a reduction of
E_{n}^{\prime}
to
F_{n}^{\prime}
. So by Proposition
2.3
again, we have

\rho(T_{\mathcal{G}}^{G/G_{\langle k\rangle}})\leq\rho(T_{\mathcal{G}}^{\omega^{k}})=\rho(T_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{\omega^{k}}).

Consequently, we have
\rho(\mathcal{G})=\sup_{k}\rho(T_{\mathcal{G}}^{G/G_{\langle k\rangle}})\leq\rho(T_{\mathcal{G}}^{\omega})
. Conversely, by
[
3
, Corollary 3.12]
we have
\rho(T_{\mathcal{G}}^{\omega})\leq\rho(\mathcal{G})
. Thus
\rho(\mathcal{G})=\rho(T_{\mathcal{G}}^{\omega})
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
G
be a closed subgroup of
S_{\infty}
. Then for any ordinal
\alpha>0
and
a,n\in\omega
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G_{\langle n\rangle})\leq\alpha

\displaystyle\iff\exists\,m\geq n\,\forall\,b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a\,(\mathrm{rk}(G_{b},G_{\langle m\rangle})<\alpha).

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
. Since
\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G_{\langle n\rangle})\leq\alpha
, there exists a
W\subseteq_{1}G
such that for all
g\in G_{\langle n\rangle}
,
\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},gWg^{-1})<\alpha
. Now we can find an
m\geq n
with
G_{\langle m\rangle}\subseteq W
. Then by Proposition
2.7
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{rk}(G_{g^{-1}(a)},G_{\langle m\rangle})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{rk}(g^{-1}G_{a}g,G_{\langle m\rangle})=\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},gG_{\langle m\rangle}g^{-1})

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},gWg^{-1})<\alpha.

So for any
b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a
we have
\mathrm{rk}(G_{b},G_{\langle m\rangle})<\alpha
.

(\Leftarrow)
. For any
g\in G_{\langle n\rangle}
, we have
\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},gG_{\langle m\rangle}g^{-1})=\mathrm{rk}(G_{g^{-1}(a)},G_{\langle m\rangle})<\alpha
by Proposition
2.7
. Hence
\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G_{\langle n\rangle})\leq\alpha
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
G
be a closed subgroup of
S_{\infty}
. Then

\mathrm{rk}(G)=\sup\{\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G)+1:a\in\omega\}.

Proof.

For any
V\subseteq_{1}G
, we can find an
m>0
such that
G_{\langle m\rangle}\subseteq V
. Now by Proposition
2.7
we have

\mathrm{rk}(V,G)\leq\mathrm{rk}(G_{\langle m\rangle},G)\leq\max\{\mathrm{rk}(G_{0},G),\dots,\mathrm{rk}(G_{m-1},G)\}.

Thus

\displaystyle\mathrm{rk}(G)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\mathrm{rk}(V,G)+1:V\subseteq_{1}G\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup\{\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G)+1:a\in\omega\}\leq\mathrm{rk}(G).

This shows
\mathrm{rk}(G)=\sup\{\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G)+1:a\in\omega\}
.
∎

We are now ready to describe the connection between
\alpha
-balanced groups and
\alpha
-CLI groups.

Theorem 3.5
.

Suppose
G
is a closed subgroup of
S_{\infty}
. Let
\mathcal{G}=(G_{\langle n\rangle})\in{\mathrm{dgnb}}(G)
and
T=T_{\mathcal{G}}^{\omega}
, then for any natural number
l\geq 1
, we have

\rho_{T}(n,G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a)<\omega\cdot l\iff\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G_{\langle n\rangle})\leq l

for
(n,G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a)\in T
.

Proof.

We will prove the statement by induction on
l
.

(1)
l=1
.

(
\Rightarrow
). Suppose
\rho_{T}(n,G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a)=k<\omega
, then for any
b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a
, we can see that
G_{\langle n+k+1\rangle}\cdot b
is a singleton. Hence
G_{b}\supseteq G_{\langle n+k+1\rangle}
. This means
\mathrm{rk}(G_{b},G_{\langle n+k+1\rangle})=0
. It follows from Lemma
3.3
that
\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G_{\langle n\rangle})\leq 1
.

(
\Leftarrow
). Since
\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G_{\langle n\rangle})\leq 1
, by Lemma
3.3
we can find an
m\geq n
, such that for any
b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a
,
G_{b}\supseteq G_{\langle m\rangle}
holds. Hence for any
b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a
, we have
G_{\langle m\rangle}\cdot b=\{b\}
. Applying Proposition
2.2
, we can deduce that

\rho_{T}(n,G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a)\leq\max\{0,m-n-1\}<\omega.

(2)
l>1
.

(
\Rightarrow
). Suppose
\rho_{T}(n,G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a)=\omega\cdot(l-1)+k
, then for any
m>n+k
and any
b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a
,
\rho_{T}(m,G_{\langle m\rangle}\cdot b)<\omega\cdot(l-1)
holds. From the induction hypothesis we obtain
\forall\,b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a\,(\mathrm{rk}(G_{b},G_{\langle m\rangle})\leq l-1)
. Thus by Lemma
3.3
,
\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G_{\langle n\rangle})\leq l
holds.

(
\Leftarrow
). Since
\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G_{\langle n\rangle})\leq l
, by definition and Proposition
2.7
(1), we can find an
m>n
such that for any
g\in G_{\langle n\rangle}
,
\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},gG_{\langle m\rangle}g^{-1})\leq l-1
holds. Next, using Proposition
2.7
(2) we obtain
\mathrm{rk}(G_{g^{-1}(a)},G_{\langle m\rangle})\leq l-1
. This implies
\forall\,b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a\,(\mathrm{rk}(G_{b},G_{\langle m\rangle})\leq l-1)
. According to the induction hypothesis, we have
\rho_{T}(m,G_{\langle m\rangle}\cdot b)<\omega\cdot(l-1)
for
b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a
. Then using Proposition
2.2
,

\displaystyle\rho_{T}(n,G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a)

\displaystyle\leq\sup\{\rho_{T}(m,G_{\langle m\rangle}\cdot b)+1:b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a\}+(m-n-1)

\displaystyle\leq\omega\cdot(l-1)+(m-n-1)<\omega\cdot l.

This finishes the induction.
∎

Theorem 3.6
.

Suppose
G
is a closed subgroup of
S_{\infty}
. Let
\mathcal{G}=(G_{\langle n\rangle})\in{\mathrm{dgnb}}(G)
and
T=T_{\mathcal{G}}^{\omega}
, then for any ordinal
\alpha\geq\omega
, we have

\rho_{T}(n,G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a)<\omega\cdot(\alpha+1)\iff\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G_{\langle n\rangle})\leq\alpha.

Proof.

We will prove the statement by induction on
\alpha\geq\omega
.

(1)
\alpha=\omega
.

(\Rightarrow)
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\rho_{T}(n,G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a)=\omega\cdot\omega+k
for some
k<\omega
. Then for any
m>n+k
and any
b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a
,
\rho_{T}(m,G_{\langle m\rangle}\cdot b)<\omega\cdot l_{b}
for some
0<l_{b}<\omega
. By Theorem
3.5
, we have
\mathrm{rk}(G_{b},G_{\langle m\rangle})\leq l_{b}
for all
b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a
. Next applying Lemma
3.3
, we can see that
\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G_{\langle n\rangle})\leq\omega
.

(\Leftarrow)
. By Lemma
3.3
and Proposition
2.7
(1), there exists an
m>n
such that for all
b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a
,
\mathrm{rk}(G_{b},G_{\langle m\rangle})\leq l_{b}
for some
0<l_{b}<\omega
. So by Theorem
3.5
we have
\rho_{T}(m,G_{\langle m\rangle}\cdot b)<\omega\cdot l_{b}
. Applying Proposition
2.2
, we can obtain that

\displaystyle\rho_{T}(n,G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\sup\{\rho_{T}(m,G_{\langle m\rangle}\cdot b)+1:b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a\}+(m-n-1)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\omega\cdot\omega+(m-n-1)

\displaystyle<\,

\displaystyle\omega\cdot(\omega+1).

(2)
\alpha>\omega
is a successor ordinal.

(\Rightarrow)
. Let
\alpha=\beta+1
. Note that there is an
m>n
such that for all
b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a
,
\rho_{T}(m,G_{\langle m\rangle}\cdot b)<\omega\cdot\alpha=\omega\cdot(\beta+1)
holds. Then applying the induction hypothesis we can see that
\mathrm{rk}(G_{b},G_{\langle m\rangle})\leq\beta
for each
b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a
. Thus
\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G_{\langle n\rangle})\leq\alpha
follows from Lemma
3.3
.

(\Leftarrow)
. By Lemma
3.3
and Proposition
2.7
(1) again, we can choose an
m>n
so that

\forall\,b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a\,(\mathrm{rk}(G_{b},G_{\langle m\rangle})\leq\beta).

Now by induction hypothesis we have
\rho_{T}(m,G_{\langle m\rangle}\cdot b)<\omega\cdot(\beta+1)
. Hence using Proposition
2.2
we obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{T}(n,G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a)

\displaystyle\leq\sup\{\rho_{T}(m,G_{\langle m\rangle}\cdot b)+1:b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a\}+(m-n-1)

\displaystyle\leq\omega\cdot(\beta+1)+(m-n-1)

\displaystyle<\omega\cdot(\alpha+1).

(3)
\alpha>\omega
is a limit ordinal.

(\Rightarrow)
. Choose an
m>n
such that for any
b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a
,
\rho_{T}(m,G_{\langle m\rangle}\cdot b)<\omega\cdot\alpha
. Then there is an ordinal
\omega\leq\beta_{b}<\alpha
such that
\rho_{T}(m,G_{\langle m\rangle}\cdot b)<\omega\cdot(\beta_{b}+1)
. From the induction hypothesis we obtain
\mathrm{rk}(G_{b},G_{\langle m\rangle})\leq\beta_{b}<\alpha
. Thus
\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G_{\langle n\rangle})\leq\alpha
.

(\Leftarrow)
. Similarly, we can find an
m>n
such that for any
b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a
,
\mathrm{rk}(G_{b},G_{\langle m\rangle})<\alpha
. Let
\beta_{b}<\alpha
be an ordinal with
\mathrm{rk}(G_{b},G_{\langle m\rangle})\leq\beta_{b}
. Then from the induction hpothesis we can see that
\rho_{T}(m,G_{\langle m\rangle}\cdot b)<\omega\cdot(\beta_{b}+1)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\rho_{T}(n,G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a)

\displaystyle\leq\sup\{\rho_{T}(m,G_{\langle m\rangle}\cdot b)+1:b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a\}+(m-n-1)

\displaystyle\leq\sup\{\omega\cdot(\beta_{b}+1):b\in G_{\langle n\rangle}\cdot a\}+(m-n-1)

\displaystyle\leq\omega\cdot\alpha+(m-n-1)<\omega\cdot(\alpha+1).

∎

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
G
be a closed subgroup of
S_{\infty}
,
\omega\leq\alpha<\omega_{1}
be an ordinal,
n\in\omega
. Then

(1)

G
is
n
-CLI iff
G
is
(n+1)
-balanced;

(2)

G
is
\alpha
-CLI iff
G
is
\alpha
-balanced.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(G_{\langle n\rangle})\in{\mathrm{dgnb}}(G)
and
T=T_{\mathcal{G}}^{\omega}
.

(1)
(\Rightarrow)
. By definition and Lemma
3.2
we have
\rho(T)=\rho(\mathcal{G})\leq\omega\cdot n
. So we have
\rho_{T}(0,G\cdot a)<\omega\cdot n
for each
a\in\omega
. Then from Theorem
3.5
we can see that
\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G)\leq n
for any
a\in\omega
. It follows from Lemma
3.4
that
G
is
(n+1)
-balanced.

(\Leftarrow)
. For any
a\in\omega
, we have
\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G)\leq n
. So by Theorem
3.5
,
\rho_{T}(0,G\cdot a)<\omega\cdot n
holds. Hence
\rho(T)\leq\omega\cdot n
. This means
G
is
n
-CLI.

(2) Case 1.
\alpha=\omega
.

(\Rightarrow)
. By Lemma
3.2
, for any
a\in\omega
, we have
\rho_{T}(0,G\cdot a)<\omega\cdot\omega
. So we can find an
l_{a}\in\omega
such that
\rho_{T}(0,G\cdot a)<\omega\cdot l_{a}
. Then Theorem
3.5
implies that
\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G)\leq l_{a}
. Hence
G
is
\omega
-balanced by Lemma
3.4
.

(\Leftarrow)
. For any
a\in\omega
, we have
\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G)=l_{a}<\omega
for some
l_{a}
. So we can get
\rho_{T}(0,G\cdot a)\leq\omega\cdot l_{a}
from Theorem
3.5
. This shows that
\rho(T)\leq\omega\cdot\omega
, i.e.,
G
is
\omega
-CLI.

Case 2.
\alpha>\omega
.

(\Rightarrow)
. By definition and Lemma
3.2
again we obtain
\rho(T)\leq\omega\cdot\alpha
. So for any
a\in\omega
, we have
\rho_{T}(0,G\cdot a)<\omega\cdot\alpha
. Let
\rho_{T}(0,G\cdot a)=\omega\cdot\beta_{a}+k_{a}
with
k_{a}<\omega
. Note that
\beta_{a}<\alpha
. Now for those
a\in\omega
with
\beta_{a}\geq\omega
, we have
\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G)\leq\beta_{a}
by Theorem
3.6
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{rk}(G)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G)+1:a\in\omega\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup\{\omega,\beta_{a}+1:a\in\omega\land\beta_{a}\geq\omega\}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha,

i.e.,
G
is
\alpha
-balanced.

(\Leftarrow)
. By Case 1, we can assume that
\mathrm{rk}(G)>\omega
. For each
a\in\omega
, let
\mathrm{rk}(G_{a},G)=\beta_{a}
. Then from Lemma
3.4
, we have

\omega<\sup\{\beta_{a}+1:a\in\omega\}=\mathrm{rk}(G)\leq\alpha;

and for those
a\in\omega
with
\beta_{a}\geq\omega
, we can obtain
\rho_{T}(0,G\cdot a)<\omega\cdot(\beta_{a}+1)
by Theorem
3.6
. So

\displaystyle\rho(T)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\rho_{T}(0,G\cdot a)+1:a\in\omega\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup\{\omega\cdot(\beta_{a}+1):a\in\omega\}

\displaystyle\leq\omega\cdot\alpha.

This shows that
G
is
\alpha
-CLI.
∎

We can combine these results into a single statement.

Let
G
be a closed subgroup of

,

. Then
G
is
\alpha
-CLI iff
G
is
(1+\alpha)
-balanced.

Since each non-archimedean Polish group
G
embeds as a closed subgroup of

, we conclude the following theorem.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
G
be a non-archimedean Polish group,

. Then

is

G
is
(1+\alpha)
-balanced.

We now turn to a problem that was asked by Allison and Panagiotopoulos in
[
1
]
.

Problem 1
.

Let
H
be a closed normal subgroup of a topological group
G
. Is it true that

\mathrm{rk}(G)\leq\sup\{\mathrm{rk}(V,H;H)+\mathrm{rk}(G/H):V\subseteq_{1}H\}?

Here
\mathrm{rk}(V,H;H)
means that the underlying topological group for function
\mathrm{rk}(V,H)
is
H
.

Inspired by this problem, we consider an analogous problem under the setting of
\alpha
-CLI.

Problem 2
.

Let
N
be a closed normal subgroup of a non-archimedean Polish group
G
. Suppose
N
is
\alpha
-CLI and
G/N
is
\beta
-CLI,
\alpha,\beta<\omega_{1}
. Is
G

(\alpha+\beta)
-CLI?

Assume that
N,G/N,G
are proper
\alpha
-CLI,
\beta
-CLI,
\gamma
-CLI, respectively. If Problem
1
has a positive answer, then applying Theorem
3.9
yields

\displaystyle 1+\gamma

\displaystyle=\mathrm{rk}(G)\leq\sup\{\mathrm{rk}(V,H;H)+\mathrm{rk}(G/H):V\subseteq_{1}H\}

\displaystyle=\sup\{\mathrm{rk}(V,H;H)+1+\beta:V\subseteq_{1}H\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{rk}(H)+\beta=1+\alpha+\beta.

So
\gamma\leq\alpha+\beta
. This observation shows that if Problem
1
has a positive answer, then so does Problem
2
.

In the following section we devote ourselves to solve Problem
2
.

4.
Positive Results

In this section, we provide a positive answer to Problem
2
under an additional assumption.

Recall the definition of semidirect product of groups. For two non-archimedean Polish groups
G
,
H
and a homomorphism
\rho:G\to{\mathrm{Aut}}(H)
, the
semidirect product

G\ltimes_{\rho}H
is the set
G\times H
together with the multiplication

(x_{1},y_{1})(x_{2},y_{2})=(x_{1}x_{2},y_{1}\rho(x_{1})(y_{2})),

where
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\in G\times H
. In addition, if the map
(x,y)\mapsto\rho(x)(y)
is continuous, then the following proposition shows that,
G\ltimes_{\rho}H
equipped with the product topology is also a non-archimedean Polish group.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
G,H
be two non-archimedean Polish groups, and
\rho:G\to\mathrm{Aut}(H)
be a homomorphism. Let
A=G\ltimes_{\rho}H
and endow it with the product topology. Suppose the map
(x,y)\mapsto\rho(x)(y)
is continuous, then
(1_{G},1_{H})
has a neighborhood basis consisting of open subgroups of the form
G^{\prime}\times H^{\prime}
, where
G^{\prime}
and
H^{\prime}
are open subgroups of
G
and
H
respectively.

Proof.

Let
(G_{n})\in{\mathrm{dgnb}}(G)
,
(H_{n})\in{\mathrm{dgnb}}(H)
. Fix an
n\in\omega
, and define
H_{n}^{\prime}=\bigcup_{k\in\omega}(\rho(G_{n})(H_{n}))^{k}
. We claim that
G_{n}\times H_{n}^{\prime}
is a subgroup of
G\times H
.

For
(g,a),(h,b)\in G_{n}\times H_{n}^{\prime}
, there are
k,l\in\omega
such that

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\rho(x_{1})(y_{1})\cdots\rho(x_{k})(y_{k}),

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=\rho(x_{1}^{\prime})(y_{1}^{\prime})\cdots\rho(x_{l}^{\prime})(y_{l}^{\prime})

for some
x_{1},\dots,x_{k},x_{1}^{\prime},\dots,x_{l}^{\prime}\in G_{n}
and
y_{1},\dots,y_{k},y_{1}^{\prime},\dots,y_{l}^{\prime}\in H_{n}
. Thus we have

\displaystyle(g,a)(h,b)

\displaystyle=(gh,a\rho(g)(\rho(x_{1}^{\prime})(y_{1}^{\prime})\cdots\rho(x_{l}^{\prime})(y_{l}^{\prime})))

\displaystyle=(gh,a\rho(gx_{1}^{\prime})(y_{1}^{\prime})\cdots\rho(gx_{l}^{\prime})(y_{l}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\in G_{n}\times(\rho(G_{n})(H_{n}))^{k+l},

and

\displaystyle(g,a)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(g^{-1},\rho(g^{-1})(\rho(x_{k})(y_{k})^{-1}\cdots\rho(x_{1})(y_{1})^{-1}))

\displaystyle=(g^{-1},\rho(g^{-1}x_{k})(y_{k}^{-1})\cdots\rho(g^{-1}x_{1})(y_{1}^{-1}))

\displaystyle\in G_{n}\times(\rho(G_{n})(H_{n}))^{k}.

This proves the claim.

Now since
(x,y)\mapsto\rho(x)(y)
is continuous, for each
n\in\omega
there is a
k\geq n
such that
\rho(G_{k})(H_{k})\subseteq H_{n}
. This implies that
G_{k}\times H_{k}^{\prime}\subseteq G_{n}\times H_{n}
. Also, note that
G_{k}\times H_{k}^{\prime}
is open, so we can find an
l\in\omega
such that
G_{l}\times H_{l}\subseteq G_{k}\times H_{k}^{\prime}
.
Using these facts interchangeably, we can find a strictly increasing sequence
(k_{n})
such that
(G_{k_{n}}\times H_{k_{n}}^{\prime})\in\mathrm{dgnb}(A)
.
So
(G_{k_{n}}\times H_{k_{n}}^{\prime})
is the desired neighborhood basis of
(1_{G},1_{H})
.
∎

Next we give a useful lemma in estimating the rank of the semidirect product of non-archimedean Polish groups.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(T,<)
be a well-founded tree,
\alpha<\omega_{1}
and
T_{1}\subseteq T
. Suppose
T_{1}
is downward closed under
<
, i.e.,

\forall\,x\in T_{1}\,\forall\,y\in T\left(y<x\implies y\in T_{1}\right).

Assume additionally that for any
y\in T\setminus T_{1}
we have
\rho_{T}(y)<\alpha
. Then for
x\in T_{1}
, we have

\rho_{T}(x)\leq\alpha+\rho_{T_{1}}(x).

In particular,
\rho(T)\leq\alpha+\rho(T_{1})
.

Proof.

The proof goes by induction on
\rho_{T_{1}}(x)
for
x\in T_{1}
. First, if
\rho_{T_{1}}(x)=0
, then either
\rho_{T}(x)=0
, which obviously satisfies the inequality; or
\rho_{T}(x)>0
, then

\rho_{T}(x)=\sup\{\rho_{T}(y)+1:y>x\land y\in T\}.

Since
\rho_{T_{1}}(x)=0
, we can see that
y>x\land y\in T
implies
y\in T\setminus T_{1}
. So
\rho_{T}(y)+1\leq\alpha
. This shows that
\rho_{T}(x)\leq\alpha=\alpha+\rho_{T_{1}}(x)
. The indution step of the proof follows easily.

Next we estimate the rank of the tree
T
. Clearly, we have

\displaystyle\rho(T)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\rho_{T}(x)+1:x\in T\}

∎

This theorem gives a negative answer to Problem

6.
Upper bound on group extensions

In this section, our goal is to establish a general formula that bounds the complexity of
G
, in terms of the levels of
N
and
G/N
, where
N
is a closed normal subgroup of a non-archimedean Polish group
G
.

The following is a well-known result in group theory.

Proposition 6.1
.

Suppose
G
is a Polish group,
N
is a closed normal subgroup of
G
, and
H
is a clopen subgroup of
G
. Then
HN/N
is isomorphic to
H/H\cap N
.

Proof.

Assume
\pi:G\to G/N
is the quotient map. Then
\pi
is surjectively open. Thus
\pi(H)
is an open subgroup of
G/N
.

Consider
\pi\mathord{\upharpoonright}H:H\to\pi(H)
. Note that it is a surjectively and continuously open homomorphism. Also, we know that

g\in\ker(\pi\mathord{\upharpoonright}H)\Leftrightarrow g\in H\land\pi(g)=N\Leftrightarrow g\in H\cap N.

Hence
H/H\cap N
is isomorphic to
\pi(H)=HN/N
.
∎

For a group
G
and its normal subgroup
N
, we denote by
[h]_{N}
the left/right coset
hN=Nh
.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
H
be a non-archimedean Polish group,
K
a closed normal subgroup of
H
, and
Y
a countable discrete Polish
H
-space. Let
\pi
be the quotient map from
H
to
H/K
. We define an action of
H/K
on
Y/K
by

[h]_{K}\cdot[y]_{K}=[h\cdot y]_{K},

where
h\in H
,
y\in Y
. Then

(1)

this group action is well-defined and continuous;

(2)

if
H^{*}
is a subgroup of
H
, then for any
y\in Y
,

\displaystyle\bigcup\pi(H^{*})\cdot[y]_{K}=H^{*}K\cdot y

and

\displaystyle(H^{*}K\cdot y)/K=\pi(H^{*})\cdot[y]_{K}.

Proof.

The proof is just a routine verification.
∎

Definition 6.3
.

Let
G
be a non-archimedean Polish group, and

a countable discrete Polish
G
-space. For

,
\mathcal{G}=(G_{n})\in{\mathrm{dgnb}}(G)

Below we verify

Lemma 6.4
.

Let

\pi:G\to G/N

(1)

is well-defined;

(2)

is the least

such that
\pi(G_{p})\cdot[x]_{N}=\{[x]_{N}\}

(3)

Proof.

(1) We only need to prove that there exists a

. Let

(2) This follows immediately from the definition of

(3) Take any

∎

For

To proceed, we introduce the following auxiliary definition.

Definition 6.6
.

Let
G
be a non-archimedean Polish group,
\mathcal{G}=(G_{n})\in{\mathrm{dgnb}}(G)
, and
X
a countable discrete Polish
G
-space. We define
(T_{\mathcal{G}}^{X})^{+}=T_{\mathcal{G}}^{X}\cup\{(0,G\cdot x):x\in X\}
.

In this definition, we add all singleton
G
-orbits to the orbit tree. This will be used in the proof of Lemma
6.8
. Note that
\rho(T_{\mathcal{G}}^{X})=\rho((T_{\mathcal{G}}^{X})^{+})
if
T_{\mathcal{G}}^{X}
is nonempty.

For a binary relation
R
on a set

, recall that
R
is
well-founded
if each nonempty subset of
A
has a
R
-minimal element. Given a well-founded relation
R
on
A
, for each
x\in A
, we can define the
rank
of

in
A
as

\rho_{R}(x)=\sup\{\rho_{R}(y)+1:y\in A\land y\,R\,x\}.

Note that
x\in A
is a
R
-minimal element iff
\rho_{R}(x)=0
. Next we define the
rank
of
R
by

\rho(R)=\sup\{\rho_{R}(x)+1:x\in A\}.

These notions coincide with previous definitions of rank for trees, if we treat a well-founded tree
(T,<)
as a well-founded relation
(T,>)
.

Let
R,S
be two binary relations on
A,B
, respectively. A function
f:A\to B
is said to be a
homomorphism
from
(A,R)
to
(B,S)
if

\forall\,x_{1},x_{2}\in A\left(x_{1}\,R\,x_{2}\implies f(x_{1})\,S\,f(x_{2})\right).

If such a homomorphism
f
exists and
S
is well-founded, then
R
is also well-founded. Moreover, by induction one can show that
\rho(R)\leq\rho(S)
.

Before diving into the details, we briefly outline our approach. We begin by defining a tree
(T_{B},<_{T_{B}})
. Next, we replace each node in
T_{B}
with a new tree, and lexicographically order the resulting set. Denoting the resulting set and order by
A
and
R
, respectively, we show that
(A,R)
is well-founded. We then construct a homomorphism from
(T_{\mathcal{G}}^{X},>)
to
(A,R)
. It follows that the rank of
R
is an upper bound for the rank of
T_{\mathcal{G}}^{X}
.

Now, we define a tree

T_{B}=\{(q_{x,n},N_{q_{x,n}}\cdot x)\in T_{\mathcal{N}}^{X}:x\in X\land n\in\omega\}\cup\{\emptyset\},

which is ordered by

C<_{T_{B}}D\iff(C=\emptyset\land D\neq\emptyset)\lor(C<D).

Here
C,D\in T_{B}
, and
<
is the order of the tree
T_{\mathcal{N}}^{X}
. Clearly,
\emptyset
is the
<_{T_{B}}
-least element in
T_{B}
, and thus from
[

, Corollary 3.12]
,

\rho(T_{B})=\rho_{T_{B}}(\emptyset)+1\leq\rho(T_{\mathcal{N}}^{X})+1\leq\rho(\mathcal{N})+1.

Next we define a function
\Phi
on
T_{B}
as

\displaystyle\Phi(\emptyset)

\displaystyle=(T_{\pi(\mathcal{G})}^{X/N})^{+},

\displaystyle\Phi(q_{x,n},N_{q_{x,n}}\cdot x)

\displaystyle=(T_{\pi(\mathcal{G}_{q_{x,n+1}})}^{N_{q_{x,n}}\cdot x/N_{q_{x,n+1}}})^{+}

for
x\in X
and
n\in\omega
. For any
x,y\in X
and

, when
(q_{x,n},N_{q_{x,n}}\cdot x)=(q_{y,n},N_{q_{y,n}}\cdot y)
, we can see that

by Lemma

(4). Therefore,

(T_{\pi(\mathcal{G}_{q_{x,n+1}})}^{N_{q_{x,n}}\cdot x/N_{q_{x,n+1}}})^{+}=(T_{\pi(\mathcal{G}_{q_{y,n+1}})}^{N_{q_{y,n}}\cdot y/N_{q_{y,n+1}}})^{+}.

This shows that
\Phi
is well-defined.

Let

A=\bigcup_{s\in T_{B}}\{s\}\times\Phi(s).

For
s\in T_{B}
, we denote by

the order of the tree
\Phi(s)
. We define a relation
R
on
A
by

(t,D)\,R\,(s,C)\iff s<_{T_{B}}t\lor(s=t\land C<_{\Phi(t)}D),

where
(s,C),(t,D)\in A
.

Lemma 6.7
.

R
is a well-founded relation on
A
. Furthermore, we have

\rho(R)\leq\sup\{\rho(\Phi(s)):s\in T_{B}\}\cdot\rho(T_{B}).

Proof.

By Proposition
6.1
, for any
n\in\omega
,
G_{n}/N_{n}
is isomorphic to
G_{n}N/N
, and
G_{n}N/N=\pi_{0}(G_{n})
is an open subgroup of
G/N
. Since
G/N
is CLI,
G_{n}/N_{n}
is also CLI. Thus by Theorem
2.4
, for each
s\in T_{B}
,
\Phi(s)
is a well-founded tree. Similarly,
T_{B}
is a well-founded tree because
N
is a CLI group. Then it is trivial to see that
R
is well-founded.

Put
\beta=\sup\{\rho(\Phi(s)):s\in T_{B}\}
. Then
\beta>\rho_{\Phi(s)}(C)
for all
(s,C)\in A
. Next, we will prove

\rho_{R}(s,C)\leq\beta\cdot\rho_{T_{B}}(s)+\rho_{\Phi(s)}(C)

by induction on
\rho_{R}(s,C)
for
(s,C)\in A
.
If
\rho_{R}(s,C)=0
, then the inequality clearly holds. Now suppose the inequality holds for any
(t,D)\in A
with
\rho_{R}(t,D)<\rho_{R}(s,C)
. Then

\displaystyle\rho_{R}(s,C)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\rho_{R}(t,D)+1:(t,D)\,R\,(s,C)\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup\{\beta\cdot\rho_{T_{B}}(t)+\rho_{\Phi(t)}(D)+1:s<_{T_{B}}t\lor(s=t\land C<_{\Phi(t)}D)\}

\displaystyle=\sup(\{\beta\cdot\rho_{T_{B}}(t)+\rho_{\Phi(t)}(D)+1:s<_{T_{B}}t\land D\in\Phi(t)\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\cup\{\beta\cdot\rho_{T_{B}}(s)+\rho_{\Phi(s)}(D)+1:C<_{\Phi(s)}D\})

\displaystyle\leq\sup\{\beta\cdot\rho_{T_{B}}(s)+\rho_{\Phi(s)}(D)+1:C<_{\Phi(s)}D\}

\displaystyle=\beta\cdot\rho_{T_{B}}(s)+\rho_{\Phi(s)}(C).

This finishes the induction.

Finally, from the inequality above we can see that

\displaystyle\rho(R)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\rho(s,C)+1:(s,C)\in A\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup\{\beta\cdot\rho_{T_{B}}(s)+\rho_{\Phi(s)}(C)+1:(s,C)\in A\}

\displaystyle\leq\beta\cdot\rho(T_{B}).

For

.

For any

, the image of

. Moreover, for any

Let

.

For

So
\rho_{T}(p,U_{p}\cdot XU_{n})=\omega
.

(3) For
X^{\prime}U_{n}\in U_{n}\cdot XU_{n}
, we have
R^{n}(u^{\prime})=R^{n}(u)
. Using (2) and Lemma
7.4
(4) we obtain

\rho_{T}(N_{2}(X^{\prime})-1,U_{N_{2}(X^{\prime})-1}\cdot X^{\prime}U_{n})=\rho_{T}(N_{2}(X)-1,U_{N_{2}(X)-1}\cdot X^{\prime}U_{n})=\omega.

This implies
\rho_{T}(n,U_{n}\cdot XU_{n})=\omega+(N_{2}(X)-n-1)
.

(4) By (3), the only remaining case is
n\geq R^{n}(u^{\prime})
. But
N_{2}(X^{\prime})=n
in this case. So by (1) we have
\rho_{T}(n,U_{n}\cdot X^{\prime}U_{n})<\omega
.

(5) We can find a sequence
(A_{k})
in
U_{n-1}
, such that
R^{n}(a_{k}+u)\to\infty
and
R^{n}(b_{k}+v)=R^{n}(v)
. Now we have
N_{2}(A_{k}X)\to\infty
. Then by (3) we obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{T}(n-1,U_{n-1}\cdot XU_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\sup\{\rho_{T}(n,U_{n}\cdot X^{\prime}U_{n})+1:X^{\prime}U_{n}\in U_{n-1}\cdot XU_{n}\}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\,\sup\{\rho_{T}(n,U_{n}\cdot A_{k}XU_{n})+1:k\in\omega\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\omega\cdot 2.

Applying (4) we can see that
\rho_{T}(n-1,U_{n-1}\cdot XU_{n})=\omega\cdot 2
.
∎

Lemma 7.6
.

U
is proper
3
-CLI.

Proof.

Fix an
n\geq 1
. Then by Lemma
7.5
(5) we have
\rho(T_{\mathcal{U}}^{U/U_{n}})=\omega\cdot 2+n
. Thus
\rho(\mathcal{U})=\omega\cdot 3
. This shows that
U
is proper
3
-CLI.
∎

Finally, let

\displaystyle N=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&0&d&f\\
0&1&0&e\\
0&0&1&0\\
0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\in U:d,e,f\in G\right\}.

Clearly,
N
is an abelian normal closed subgroup of
U
. It is routine to check that

N
and

are
1
-CLI. This finishes the construction of our counterexample.
