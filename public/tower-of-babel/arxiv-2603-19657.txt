Model Selection and Parameter Estimation of Multi-dimensional Gaussian Mixture Model
Xinyu Liu, Hai Zhang

Abstract
In this paper, we study the problem of learning multi-dimensional Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs), with a specific focus on model order selection and efficient mixing distribution estimation. We first establish an information-theoretic lower bound on the critical sample complexity required for reliable model selection. More specifically, we show that distinguishing a k k -component mixture from a simpler model necessitates a sample size scaling of Ω ⁡ ( Δ − ( 4 ​ k − 4 ) ) \Omega(\Delta^{-(4k-4)}) . We then propose a thresholding-based estimation algorithm that evaluates the spectral gap of an empirical covariance matrix constructed from random Fourier measurement vectors. This parameter-free estimator operates with an efficient time complexity of 𝒪 ⁡ ( k 2 ​ n ) \mathcal{O}(k^{2}n) , scaling linearly with the sample size. We demonstrate that the sample complexity of our method matches the established lower bound, confirming its minimax optimality with respect to the component separation distance Δ \Delta . Conditioned on the estimated model order, we subsequently introduce a gradient-based minimization method for parameter estimation. To effectively navigate the non-convex objective landscape, we employ a data-driven, score-based initialization strategy that guarantees rapid convergence. We prove that this method achieves the optimal parametric convergence rate of 𝒪 p ( n − 1 / 2 ) \mathcal{O}_{p}(n^{-1/2}) for estimating the component means. To enhance the algorithm’s efficiency in high-dimensional regimes where the ambient dimension exceeds the number of mixture components (i.e., d > k d>k ), we integrate principal component analysis (PCA) for dimension reduction. Numerical experiments demonstrate that our Fourier-based algorithmic framework outperforms conventional Expectation-Maximization (EM) methods in both estimation accuracy and computational time.



1
Introduction

The Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) is a fundamental statistical framework utilized extensively across machine learning, pattern recognition, and robust statistics. It provides a powerful mechanism for modeling complex, heterogeneous data distributions originating from distinct sub-populations. The GMM represents a probability distribution as a weighted sum of Gaussian components, each characterized by its mean and covariance matrix. Formally, each independent observation
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from a
k
-component GMM is generated according to the density:

p(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\mathcal{N}(x;\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i}),

(1)

where
w_{i}
is the mixing weight such that
w_{i}>0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}=1
. The mean vector and the covariance matrix of the
i
-th component are denoted as
\mu_{i}
and
\Sigma_{i}
, respectively. Given the i.i.d. samples drawn from the mixture distribution, the challenge is to learn the underlying model.

The learning problem for GMMs is generally categorized into three highly interconnected tasks: (1)
Clustering
, which infers the latent component assignment for each sample; (2)
Parameter Estimation
, which recovers the precise values of the weights, means, and covariances up to a global permutation; and (3)
Density Estimation
, which seeks to approximate the true probability density function under specific statistical metrics such as the Kullback-Leibler divergence or the Wasserstein distance.

In the context of clustering, objective functions such as
k
-means are generally NP-hard to optimize globally, even for
k=2
clusters (see
[
2
]
). Traditional approximation methods, such as Lloyd’s algorithm
[
15
]
, are highly sensitive to initialization and dimension. Recent theoretical advancements have established that perfectly clustering high-dimensional Gaussian mixtures fundamentally depends on the separation distance
\Delta=\min_{i\neq j}\|\mu_{i}-\mu_{j}\|_{2}
. For instance,
[
17
]
proved that the critical threshold for the exact recovery of a two-component mixture with covariance
\sigma^{2}I_{d}
explicitly depends on the ambient dimension
d
:

\Delta^{2}>\sigma^{2}\left(1+\sqrt{1+\frac{2d}{n\log n}}\right)\log n.

(2)

For parameter estimation, the high-dimensional regime severely exacerbates the ”curse of dimensionality.” Traditional iterative methods like the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm (
[
6
]
) often suffer from exponentially slow convergence rates or become trapped in spurious local optima when the dimension
d
is large and the overlap between components is significant. To bypass the non-convexity of the likelihood landscape, method-of-moments and spectral approaches have gained prominence.
[
20
]
proposed a spectral algorithm that projects samples onto the subspace spanned by the top principal components, successfully learning mixtures of spherical Gaussians provided the component centers exhibit sufficient separation.
[
11
]
later developed an efficient method-of-moments estimator using the spectral decomposition of low-order observable moments; this approach precludes the need for explicit minimum separation assumptions, but operates under the strict non-degeneracy condition that the component mean vectors are linearly independent (spanning a
k
-dimensional subspace).

The fundamental information-theoretic limits of parameter estimation for Gaussian mixtures were quantified by
[
16
]
. They demonstrated that learning general GMMs necessitates a sample size and runtime that depend exponentially on the number of components
k
. Specifically, by constructing two distinct 1-dimensional Gaussian mixtures with
k
components that are separated in parameter space by
\Omega(1/k)
yet exhibit an exponentially small statistical distance bounded by
\mathcal{O}(e^{-\Omega(k)})
, they proved that distinguishing such models intrinsically requires a sample size exponential in
k
. Moving beyond the 1-dimensional worst-case scenario to high-dimensional spaces,
[
18
]
later established a sharp phase transition for the sample complexity of
k
spherical GMMs. They proved that parameter recovery requires super-polynomial samples if the spatial separation
\Delta
falls below
o(\sqrt{\log k})
, whereas a polynomial number of samples is strictly sufficient if
\Delta=\Omega(\sqrt{\log k})
. Furthermore,
[
7
]
established the optimal minimax rate for estimating the mixing distribution of high-dimensional location mixtures under the Wasserstein distance, proving that the worst-case error is bounded by
\mathcal{O}\left((d/n)^{1/4}+n^{-1/(4k-2)}\right)
, highlighting the severe degradation in statistical efficiency when the model components are severely overlapping.

A critical limitation in nearly all the aforementioned high-dimensional clustering and parameter estimation algorithms is the strict requirement that the model order
k
(the true number of components) is known exactly a priori.
In practice, however,
k
is rarely known, and its accurate estimation, formally termed as a model selection problem, is of great importance.
Selecting the optimal
k
dictates the fundamental information-theoretic tradeoff between model expressivity and statistical efficiency. Underestimating
k
forces disparate subpopulations to merge, violating the structural assumptions of the data-generating process and destroying the consistency of subsequent parameter estimates. Conversely, overestimating
k
leads to over-parameterization, resulting in spurious clusters and loss of identifiability.

The likelihood ratio test (LDR) can be used to determine the model order. However, the asymptotic result may fail, see
[
10
]
. Some alternatives have been proposed, including the modified likelihood ratio test (MLRT)
[
5
,
3
]
, the EM test
[
13
]
and the penalized method
[
8
]
. Another widely used method is based on the information criteria such as BIC (
[
19
]
) and AIC (
[
1
]
). The properties of AIC and BIC are investigated in
[
12
]
. The above methods all require separate fitting for each candidate model, which can be costly in high-dimensional scenarios. Another practical method is to infer
k
by the eigengap in the sample covariance matrix. However, this method lacks theory and is poorly understood.

1.1
Problem Setting

Consider
n
independent samples drawn from a
d
-dimensional Gaussian mixture distribution with a unified covariance matrix:

x_{j}\sim\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\Sigma),\ j=1,2,\cdots,n.

(3)

We assume that the covariance matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is known as prior information. This scenario is referred to as the Gaussian location mixture if
\Sigma=\sigma^{2}I_{d}
. We define the separation distance
\Delta
and the minimal weight
w_{\min}
of the model (
3
) as

\Delta=\min_{1\leq i<i\leq k}\left\lVert\mu_{i}-\mu_{j}\right\rVert,\quad w_{\min}=\min_{1\leq i\leq k}w_{i}.

This model (
3
) can also be written in a convolution form:

\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\delta_{\mu_{i}}*\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma).

(4)

Here
\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\delta_{\mu_{i}}
is known as the mixing distribution. In this paper, we study the problem of learning model (
3
) from
n
i.i.d. samples. More specifically, we aim to determine the model order
k
and estimate the mixing distribution
\nu
from the samples.

1.2
Our Contributions

The contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows. We establish an information-theoretic lower bound on the sample complexity required for reliable model order selection in multi-dimensional GMMs. By embedding a worst-case one-dimensional moment-matching configuration into
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we show that distinguishing a
k
-component mixture from a simpler model requires a critical sample complexity of at least
\Omega(\Delta^{-(4k-4)})
.

To address the model selection problem, we propose an algorithm to infer
k
by utilizing Fourier measurements. Specifically, we evaluate the empirical characteristic function of the observed data at randomly sampled frequency vectors to construct an empirical Fourier covariance matrix. The method then estimates
k
by analyzing the spectral gap of this matrix via a principled thresholding procedure. Compared to traditional methods based on information criteria or the log-likelihood ratio, our method is more computationally efficient, and the theoretical guarantee for correctly estimating
k
in large sample scenarios is provided. Furthermore, we prove that this estimator is consistent and demonstrate that its sample complexity matches our established theoretical lower bound, confirming its minimax optimality with respect to the separation distance.

For mixing distribution estimation, we propose an algorithm that minimizes the projection error of the Fourier steering vectors parametrized by the Gaussian mean
\mu
onto the spectral subspace obtained from the empirical Fourier covariance matrix. The minimization is executed via gradient descent, with a data-driven and score-based initialization strategy that ensures rapid convergence. The effectiveness of our method in the case when the ambient data dimension is greater than the component number
k
is further enhanced by employing principal component analysis (PCA) for dimension reduction. The estimation error of the mean parameters is investigated. Numerical experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed algorithm compared with the EM algorithm in multiple scenarios, including both low- and high-dimensional cases.

Paper Organization and Notations

In Section
2
, we first discuss the sampling complexity of learning the model order of GMMs, establishing a lower bound for this complexity based on the statistical indistinguishability of closely located Gaussian components. We then introduce a Fourier approach for model selection of GMMs. The proposed estimator is proven to be consistent, and its sampling complexity order matches the established lower bound in Section
2
. In Section

, we propose our algorithm for estimating the mixing distribution with a focus on estimating the means. The estimator is based on a gradient-descent method with a data-driven, score-based spectral initialization. The error of the estimator is investigated, and the dimension reduction technique could improve the efficiency for high-dimensional mixtures. In Section
4
, numerical studies are provided to illustrate the effectiveness of our algorithms. We conclude our work with limitations and future works in Section
5
.

2
Model Selection and Sampling Complexity

In this section, we first introduce the critical sampling complexity for learning the model order of GMMs. We focus on quantifying the minimal sample size required to distinguish a complex model from a simpler one, which serves as the theoretical motivation for our proposed Fourier-based approach.

We then introduce a Fourier-based approach for model order selection. Using the exponential form of the covariance-compensated characteristic function of the GMM, we construct a latent Fourier covariance matrix from random Fourier measurement vectors. We characterize the algebraic and structural properties of the covariance matrix, explicitly quantifying its singular value spectrum as a function of the underlying model parameters. Leveraging this spectral characterization, we then develop a statistically consistent estimation procedure for the true model order
k
, accompanied by finite-sample complexity guarantees.

2.1
Indistinguishable Probability Models

Given a complex probability model
P
with density
p
and a simpler probability model
Q
with density
q
, we say that the model
P
is distinguishable with confidence
1-\delta
at sample size
n
from the model
Q
if the average log-likelihood of the samples under
P
is strictly greater than that under
Q
:

\mathbb{P}_{X_{i}\sim P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log p(X_{i})>\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log q(X_{i})\right)\geq 1-\delta.

(5)

Equivalently, we say that the model
P
is indistinguishable with probability at least
\delta
from the model
Q
if:

\mathbb{P}_{X_{i}\sim P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log p(X_{i})\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log q(X_{i})\right)\geq\delta.

(6)

We note that the independent and identically distributed samples
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are drawn from the true complex model
P
. Intuitively, this indistinguishability quantifies the scenario where the available data do not provide enough statistical evidence to definitively prefer
P
over the simpler model
Q
. If
P
and
Q
are sufficiently similar relative to the sample size
n
, the simpler model
Q
may effectively explain the data without loss in modeling fidelity.

2.2
Critical Sample Complexity for Model Selection

To characterize the hardness of model selection, we formalize the set of models that cannot be statistically differentiated from the ground truth given a finite sample size. This allows us to define the critical sample complexity required to rule out all simpler approximations.

Let
P
be the true
k
-component GMM with a unified covariance matrix
\Sigma
. Given
n
i.i.d. samples drawn from
P
, we identify the set of candidate models that remain plausible explanations for the data. We define the set of simpler
\delta
-admissible models for
P
at sample size
n
as:

\mathcal{Q}_{simpler}=\left\{Q\in\bigcup_{1\leq j\leq k-1}\mathcal{G}_{j,\Sigma}:\mathbb{P}_{X_{i}\sim P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log p(X_{i})\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log q(X_{i})\right)\geq\delta\right\},

(7)

where
\mathcal{G}_{j,\Sigma}
denotes the space of all
j
-component GMMs with covariance
\Sigma
. If
\mathcal{Q}_{simpler}
is non-empty, there exists at least one simpler model that is statistically indistinguishable from the true model
P
given the available data. In such a scenario, reliable model selection is information-theoretically impossible.

We formally define the critical sample complexity, denoted as
N^{*}(P,\delta)
, as the minimal sample size required to ensure that no simpler model is admissible:

N^{*}(P,\delta)=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\mathcal{Q}_{simpler}=\emptyset\}.

(8)

2.3
A Lower Bound via Moment-Matching

To establish a lower bound on this critical sample complexity for multi-dimensional GMMs, we construct a ”hard” scenario by embedding a worst-case one-dimensional component configuration into
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Consider a
k
-component GMM
P\sim\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\sigma^{2}I_{d})
. Suppose all component means lie on a single one-dimensional line in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined by a unit vector
v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. We arrange the means uniformly on an interval
[-R,R]
such that:

\mu_{i}=\left(-R+\frac{2(i-1)R}{k-1}\right)v,\quad\text{for }i=1,\dots,k.

(9)

The separation distance is exactly
\Delta=\|\mu_{i}-\mu_{i+1}\|_{2}=\frac{2R}{k-1}
.

To understand the sample complexity required to identify that
P
has
k
components, we must determine how easily it can be approximated by a simpler
(k-1)
-component model
Q
. Because
Q
possesses
2(k-1)-1=2k-3
free parameters (mixing weights and means) restricted along the direction
v
, it can be strategically chosen to exactly match the first
2k-3
moments of
P
projected onto
v
.

As established in
[
14
]
, when the components merge (
R\to 0
), matching these
2k-3
moments causes the Kullback-Leibler divergence between
P
and
Q
to vanish at a rate bounded by the
(2k-2)
-th moment. This yields the following lower bound on the sample complexity:

Theorem 2.1
.

(Theorem 2.4 in
[
14
]
)
Consider the true
k
-component GMM
P
constructed above with separation distance
\Delta
. For any
0<\delta<1/2
, if the sample size
n
satisfies:

n<C_{k,w}[\Phi^{-1}(2\delta)]^{2}\frac{1}{\Delta^{4k-4}}

(10)

where
C_{k,w}
is a constant depending only on
k
and the weights, then there exists a
(k-1)
-component GMM
Q
such that
\mathbb{P}_{X_{i}\sim P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log p(X_{i})\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log q(X_{i})\right)\geq\delta
.

This theorem establishes that distinguishing a
k
-component model from a
(k-1)
-component model requires a critical sample complexity of at least:

N^{*}(P,\delta)=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\Delta^{4k-4}}\right).

(11)

If the actual sample size
n<N^{*}
, the information contained in the samples is intrinsically insufficient for any estimator to resolve the true model order with high probability.

2.4
Fourier Measurements and Latent Fourier Covariance Matrix

The Fourier transform for the density of (
3
) is given by

\phi(t)=\exp\left(-\frac{t^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma t}{2}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\exp\left(\iota\langle\mu_{i},t\rangle\right),

(12)

which is also known as the characteristic function of the GMM. It can be empirically estimated from i.i.d. samples
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
by the empirical characteristic function:

\hat{\phi}_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp(\iota\langle x_{j},t\rangle).

(13)

To isolate the spectral contribution of the component means, we introduce the following covariance-compensated empirical characteristic function

\hat{y}_{n}(t)=\exp\left(\frac{t^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma t}{2}\right)\hat{\phi}_{n}(t)=\exp\left(\frac{t^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma t}{2}\right)\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp(\iota\langle x_{j},t\rangle)

(14)

and refer to it as the
Fourier measurement
henceforth.
We denote

y(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}e^{\iota\langle\mu_{i},t\rangle}

and call it the
latent spectral signal
. Consequently, the Fourier measurement can be decomposed as

\hat{y}_{n}(t)=y(t)+e_{n}(t),

(15)

where
e_{n}(t)
can be regarded as the noise term due to the finite sample size
n
. The noise level
\left\lVert e_{n}(t)\right\rVert_{\infty}
can be quantified in a probability sense by the following proposition:

Proposition 2.2
.

For any fixed
\epsilon>0
, we have the concentration that

\mathbb{P}\left(|e_{n}(t)|\geq\epsilon\right)\leq 4\exp\left(-\frac{n\epsilon^{2}}{4e^{2t^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma t}}\right)\leq 4\exp\left(-\frac{n\epsilon^{2}}{4e^{2\left\lVert t\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\sigma_{\min}(\Sigma)}}\right),

where
\sigma_{\min}(\Sigma)
denotes the minimum singular value of
\Sigma
. Then for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, if the sample size
n\geq\frac{4}{\epsilon^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right){\exp\left(2\left\lVert t\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\sigma_{\min}(\Sigma)\right)}
, with probability
1-\delta
, we have that
|e_{n}(t)|<\epsilon
.

Next, we use the latent spectral signal
y(t)
to demonstrate the main idea behind our algorithm for model selection. We first randomly generate
L
frequency vectors
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}
in the Fourier space that follow a prescribed distribution
\mathcal{D}
(eg. a uniform distribution in a bounded ball, or Gaussion distribution). Define the
latent spectral vector
at the generated points as

{\bm{y}}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}y(t_{1})&y(t_{2})&\cdots&y(t_{L})\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}.

Given that
y(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}e^{\iota\langle\mu_{i},t\rangle}
, we can express
{\bm{y}}_{0}
as

{\bm{y}}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}e^{\iota\langle\mu_{1},t_{1}\rangle}&e^{\iota\langle\mu_{2},t_{1}\rangle}&\cdots&e^{\iota\langle\mu_{k},t_{1}\rangle}\\
e^{\iota\langle\mu_{1},t_{2}\rangle}&e^{\iota\langle\mu_{2},t_{2}\rangle}&\cdots&e^{\iota\langle\mu_{k},t_{2}\rangle}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
e^{\iota\langle\mu_{1},t_{L}\rangle}&e^{\iota\langle\mu_{2},t_{L}\rangle}&\cdots&e^{\iota\langle\mu_{k},t_{L}\rangle}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}\\
w_{2}\\
\vdots\\
w_{k}\end{bmatrix}=:\Phi{\bm{w}}.

We also introduce a
Fourier steering vector
for each mean vector
\mu
:

\varphi_{L}(\mu)=[e^{\iota\langle\mu,t_{1}\rangle},e^{\iota\langle\mu,t_{2}\rangle},\cdots,e^{\iota\langle\mu,t_{L}\rangle}]^{\mathrm{T}}.

The matrix
\Phi
can then be written as

\Phi=[\varphi_{L}(\mu_{1}),\varphi_{L}(\mu_{2}),\cdots,\varphi_{L}(\mu_{k})].

To extract the Fourier steering vector of the means
\mu_{j}
’s, we form a
latent Fourier covariance matrix
of the random latent spectral vectors which are obtained by translating the Fourier points
t_{i}
’s. More precisely, let
\{v_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
be a set of translation directions and denote
v_{0}=0\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define

{\bm{y}}_{m}=\begin{bmatrix}y(t_{1}+v_{m})&y(t_{2}+v_{m})&\cdots&y(t_{L}+v_{m})\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{L},

Then

{\bm{y}}_{m}=\Phi{\bm{w}}_{m},\ m=0,\cdots,M,

where

{\bm{w}}_{m}=\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}e^{\iota\langle\mu_{1},v_{m}\rangle}&w_{2}e^{\iota\langle\mu_{2},v_{m}\rangle}&\cdots&w_{k}e^{\iota\langle\mu_{k},v_{m}\rangle}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{k}.

The latent Fourier covariance matrix of the Fourier vectors
\{{\bm{y}}_{m}\}_{m=0}^{M}
is then defined by

C:=\frac{1}{M+1}\sum_{m=0}^{M}{\bm{y}}_{m}{\bm{y}}_{m}^{*}=\frac{1}{M+1}\sum_{m=0}^{M}\Phi{\bm{w}}_{m}{\bm{w}}_{m}^{*}\Phi^{*}=\Phi W\Phi^{*}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{L\times L},

where the
W=\frac{1}{M+1}\sum_{m=0}^{M}{\bm{w}}_{m}{\bm{w}}_{m}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
. The crux of the algorithm is: In the noiseless case with
L\geq k
, the image space of the latent Fourier covariance matrix
C
coincides with the image space of
\Phi
, which is spanned by
k
Fourier steering vectors
\varphi_{L}(\mu_{1}),\varphi_{L}(\mu_{2}),\cdots,\varphi_{L}(\mu_{k})
. This image space can be decomposed by the orthogonal basis from the singular value decomposition with the following property:

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose that
M+1,L\geq k
and
rank(
\Phi
)
=
rank(
W
)
=k
, then the latent Fourier covariance matrix
C
has the singular value decomposition as

C=[U_{1}\quad U_{2}]\textbf{diag}\left(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\cdots,\sigma_{k},0,\cdots,0\right)[U_{1}\quad U_{2}]^{*},

where
U_{1}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{L\times k},U_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{L\times(L-k)}
and
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{k}>0
.

Remark 2.4
.

The condition that rank(
\Phi
)
=
rank(
W
)
=k
is equivalent to that

•

\varphi_{L}(\mu_{1}),\cdots,\varphi_{L}(\mu_{k})
are linearly independent;

•

{\bm{w}}_{0},{\bm{w}}_{1},\cdots,{\bm{w}}_{M}
span the space
\mathbb{C}^{k}
(or equivalently, the matrix
[{\bm{w}}_{0},\dots,{\bm{w}}_{M}]
has rank

).

The above two conditions are easily satisfied by choosing different
t_{l}
’s and
v_{m}
’s if all
\mu_{i}
’s are distinct.

The column space of
U_{1}
spans the image space. In practice, the latent spectral signal
y(t)
is approximated by
\hat{y}_{n}(t)
and the latent Fourier covariance matrix
C
by
\hat{C}
with SVD as:

\hat{C}:=\frac{1}{M+1}\sum_{m=0}^{M}\hat{\bm{y}}_{m}\hat{\bm{y}}_{m}^{*}=[\hat{U}_{1}\quad\hat{U}_{2}]\textbf{diag}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{1},\hat{\sigma}_{2},\cdots,\hat{\sigma}_{k},\hat{\sigma}_{k+1},\cdots,\hat{\sigma}_{L}\right)[\hat{U}_{1}\quad\hat{U}_{2}]^{*},

where
\hat{\bm{y}}_{m}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{y}_{n}(t_{1}+v_{m})&\hat{y}_{n}(t_{2}+v_{m})&\cdots&\hat{y}_{n}(t_{L}+v_{m})\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}
denotes the
random Fourier measurements vector
and we refer to
\hat{C}
as the

.
Due to finite sample size, the empirical Fourier measurements deviate from the latent spectral signal. Consequently, the spectrum of the empirical Fourier covariance matrix
C
is perturbed, thereby obscuring its true underlying rank
k
.

2.5
Signal Strength Analysis

The ability to detect the correct model order
k
depends fundamentally on the spectral properties of the latent Fourier covariance matrix
C
. Specifically, the magnitude of the smallest non-zero singular value
\sigma_{k}(C)
. If
\sigma_{k}(C)
is too small relative to the sampling noise, the
k
-th component becomes indistinguishable from the noise floor.

In this subsection, we derive a lower bound for the singular values of
C
in terms of model parameters: the minimum separation distance
\Delta
and the mixing weights
w_{\min}
. First, we establish that the high-dimensional separation of the means is preserved when projected onto a one-dimensional subspace. This allows us to reduce the analysis of the singular values to a univariate problem.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\{\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a set of distinct points with minimum separation distance
\Delta=\min_{i\neq j}\|\mu_{i}-\mu_{j}\|_{2}
. Let
\{t_{1},\dots,t_{J}\}
be a set of
J
independent random vectors drawn uniformly from the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
For any confidence parameter
\delta\in(0,1)
, if the number of sampled directions satisfies

J\geq\lceil\log_{2}(1/\delta)\rceil,

(16)

then with probability at least
1-\delta
, there exists at least one index
n\in\{1,\dots,J\}
such that the projected separation satisfies:

\min_{i\neq j}|\langle\mu_{i}-\mu_{j},t_{n}\rangle|\geq\frac{\Delta}{k^{2}\sqrt{d}}.

(17)

Using this geometric property, we now estimate the lower bound of the
k
-th singular value.
We adopt the following sampling strategy to generate frequency vectors in the Fourier space. We first generate
J
i.i.d. unit directions
t_{1},\cdots,t_{J}\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
. For each direction
t_{j}
, we then construct a set of
S
colinear measurement points
t_{j,s}=s\tau t_{j}
for
s=1,\cdots,S
with step size
\tau>0
. This yields a total of
L=JS
Fourier measurement points and admits the following theoretical guarantee:

Proposition 2.6

In practice, we compute the empirical Fourier covariance matrix

Given the singular value relation, the model order can be determined through a thresholding scheme for a chosen threshold

\hat{k}=\argmax_{1\leq l\leq L-1}\{l:\hat{\sigma}_{l}\geq\epsilon\}.

Remark 2.8
.

The above theorem states that suppose the threshold is properly chosen. Then, consistent selection is achievable if the sample size satisfies:

n\gtrsim\frac{1}{w_{\min}^{4}\Delta^{4k-4}}\log(1/\delta).

The remark above implies that for exact model order recovery using our Fourier-based thresholding approach, the sufficient sample size must scale as:

n=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{w_{\min}^{4}\Delta^{4k-4}}\log(1/\delta)\right).

Comparing this sufficient condition to the fundamental lower bound established in Theorem

\Delta

\Delta

Mixing Distribution Estimation

In this section, we estimate the mixing distribution under the assumption that the model order
k
is known. Our proposed methodology is inspired by the MUltiple SIgnal Classification (MUSIC) algorithm in array signal processing and line spectral estimation which relies on the geometric properties of spectral subspaces.
Specifically, by exploiting the algebraic structure of the latent Fourier covariance matrix
C
, we first estimate the
spectral subspace
spanned by the Fourier steering vectors associated with the true component means of the GMM. Subsequently, we localize these component means by minimizing the orthogonal projection distance between parameterized Fourier steering vectors and the estimated spectral subspace.

Given that the resulting continuous optimization landscape is inherently highly non-convex, a good initialization strategy is imperative to prevent convergence to spurious local minima. To this end, we introduce a data-driven, score-based initialization scheme. This approach selects initial candidates directly from the empirical samples by maximizing a score function, defined as the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the sample’s associated Fourier steering vector projected onto the estimated spectral subspace. In the case of
k<d
, where the number of components is smaller than the ambient data dimension, we apply Principal Component Analysis (PCA) as a preliminary dimensionality reduction step.

3.1

k\geq d

In this section, we present our algorithm for the case

. Recall that in the noiseless case, the Fourier steering vectors
\varphi_{L}(\mu_{1}),\varphi_{L}(\mu_{2}),\cdots,\varphi_{L}(\mu_{k})

\{\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\cdots,\mu_{k}\}=\{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\mathcal{P}_{U_{1}}(\varphi_{L}(\mu))=\varphi_{L}(\mu)\},

where
\mathcal{P}_{U_{1}}(\cdot)
is the projection operator which projects the parameterized Fourier steering vector onto the spectral subspace

Unlike the classical MUSIC algorithm, which relies on an exhaustive grid search to identify steering vectors that are orthogonal to the noise subspace (the orthogonal complement of the spectral subspace), we adopt a continuous optimization strategy. This approach circumvents the prohibitive computational complexity associated with multi-dimensional grid scanning when

. Accordingly, the component means are estimated by identifying the
k
local minimizers that achieve the smallest values for the objective function defined via the following
orthogonal complement subspace projector

\hat{J}(\mu)=\left\lVert\mathcal{P}_{\hat{U}_{1}}\varphi_{L}(\mu)-\varphi_{L}(\mu)\right\rVert_{2},\ \text{where}\ \mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

It is obvious that we have
J(\mu^{*}):=\left\lVert\mathcal{P}_{U_{1}}\varphi_{L}(\mu^{*})-\varphi_{L}(\mu^{*})\right\rVert_{2}=0
for any
\mu^{*}\in\{\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{k}\}

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigate the model selection and mixing distribution estimation of the multi-dimensional Gaussian mixture models (
3
) with a known unified covariance matrix across the Gaussian components. For model selection, we established an information-theoretic lower bound demonstrating that distinguishing a
k
-component mixture requires a sample size scaling of
\Omega(\Delta^{-(4k-4)})
. We subsequently proposed a singular value thresholding approach with a
\mathcal{O}(k^{2}n)
time complexity and achieved a sample complexity that matches this lower bound. For mixing distribution estimation, conditioned on the correct model order,
we introduced a gradient-based minimization method that employs a data-driven, score-based initialization strategy to ensure rapid convergence. We prove that this method achieves the optimal parametric convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}_{p}(n^{-1/2})
. Finally, the computational and statistical efficiency of the proposed methods are demonstrated through extensive numerical experiments.

While our framework provides efficient estimation methods and theoretical guarantees for models with a unified covariance structure, extending these results to GMMs with arbitrary, component-specific covariances presents significant challenges and requires new insights. For mixtures with unrestricted covariances, the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm currently remains the standard empirical approach, despite its known issues, such as convergence to local optima and sensitivity to initialization. Developing computationally efficient algorithms with provable theoretical guarantees for this general setting remains an important avenue for future research.

Proof of Proposition

Note that

\hat{y}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{t^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma t}\cos\langle x_{j},t\rangle+\iota\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{t^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma t}\sin\langle x_{j},t\rangle.

Applying Hoeffding’s inequality to the real and imaginary parts, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{t^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma t}\cos\langle x_{j},t\rangle-e^{t^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma t}\text{Re}\left\{\phi(t)\right\}\right|>\epsilon\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{n\epsilon^{2}}{2e^{2t^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma t}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{t^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma t}\sin\langle x_{j},t\rangle-e^{t^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma t}\text{Im}\left\{\phi(t)\right\}\right|>\epsilon\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{n\epsilon^{2}}{2e^{2t^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma t}}\right).

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|e^{t^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma t}\hat{\phi}_{n}(t)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\exp\left(\iota\langle\mu_{i},t\rangle\right)\right|>\epsilon\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\left|e^{t^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma t}[\hat{\phi}_{n}(t)-\phi(t)]\right|>\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{t^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma t}\cos\langle x_{j},t\rangle-e^{t^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma t}\text{Re}\left\{\phi(t)\right\}\right|>\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{t^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma t}\sin\langle x_{j},t\rangle-e^{t^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma t}\text{Im}\left\{\phi(t)\right\}\right|>\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4\exp\left(-\frac{n\epsilon^{2}}{4e^{2t^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma t}}\right)\leq 4\exp\left(-\frac{n\epsilon^{2}}{4e^{2\left\lVert t\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\sigma_{\min}(\Sigma)}}\right).

(30)

If we choose

, then (

A.2
Proof of Lemma
2.5

Let
v
be drawn uniformly from
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. For any fixed unit vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the random variable
Z=\langle u,v\rangle
represents the projection of a random spherical vector onto a fixed axis. The probability density function of
Z
is given by:

f_{Z}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{\Gamma((d-1)/2)}(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}},\quad t\in[-1,1].

(31)

Consider a pair of points with normalized difference vector
u_{ij}=(\mu_{i}-\mu_{j})/\|\mu_{i}-\mu_{j}\|_{2}
. The failure condition is that the projection magnitude falls below
\epsilon=\frac{c_{k,d}^{*}}{\Delta}=\frac{1}{k^{2}\sqrt{d}}
. The probability of this event is:

p_{\text{pair}}=\mathbb{P}(|Z|\leq\epsilon)=\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}f_{Z}(t)\,dt.

(32)

Since
(1-t^{2})\leq 1
for all
t
, the density is bounded by its maximum at
t=0
:

f_{Z}(t)\leq C_{d}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{\Gamma((d-1)/2)}.

(33)

We apply the Gautschi’s inequality for the ratio of Gamma functions:
\frac{\Gamma(x+1)}{\Gamma(x+1/2)}<\sqrt{x+1/2}
. Setting
x=(d-2)/2
, we obtain:

C_{d}<\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{d}{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{d}{2\pi}}<\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{d}\quad(\text{for }d\geq 2).

(34)

Thus, the integral is bounded by the length of the interval times the maximum density:

p_{\text{pair}}\leq\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{d}\,dt=2\epsilon\cdot\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{d}=\epsilon\sqrt{d}.

(35)

Substituting
\epsilon=\frac{1}{k^{2}\sqrt{d}}
, we get:

p_{\text{pair}}\leq\frac{1}{k^{2}\sqrt{d}}\cdot\sqrt{d}=\frac{1}{k^{2}}.

(36)

Let
E_{\text{fail}}
be the event that any of the
\binom{k}{2}
pairs fails to meet the separation threshold. By the union bound:

\mathbb{P}(E_{\text{fail}})\leq\binom{k}{2}p_{\text{pair}}<\frac{k^{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{k^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}.

(37)

This shows that a single random direction succeeds with probability at least
1/2
. If we sample
M
independent directions
v_{1},\dots,v_{M}
, the probability that
all
of them fail is:

\mathbb{P}(\{\text{All Fail}\})\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{M}.

(38)

Setting
(1/2)^{M}\leq\delta
yields
M\geq\log_{2}(1/\delta)
.

A.3
Proof of Proposition
2.6

Without loss of generality, we assume that

\min_{i\neq j}\left\lvert\langle\mu_{i}-\mu_{j},t_{1}\rangle\right\lvert\geq\frac{\Delta}{k^{2}\sqrt{d}}.

By defining the diagonal matrix
\tilde{D}=\textbf{diag}\left(\exp(\iota\langle\mu_{1},\tau t_{0}\rangle),\exp(\iota\langle\mu_{2},\tau t_{0}\rangle),\cdots,\exp(\iota\langle\mu_{k},\tau t_{0}\rangle)\right)
, we have

C=\Phi W\Phi^{*}=\tilde{\Phi}\tilde{D}W\tilde{D}^{*}\tilde{\Phi}^{*},

(39)

where
\tilde{\Phi}
takes the form as

\tilde{\Phi}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
e^{\iota\langle\mu_{1},\tau t_{0}\rangle}&e^{\iota\langle\mu_{2},\tau t_{0}\rangle}&\cdots&e^{\iota\langle\mu_{1},\tau t_{0}\rangle}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
e^{\iota(k-1)\langle\mu_{1},\tau t_{0}\rangle}&e^{\iota(k-1)\langle\mu_{2},\tau t_{0}\rangle}&\cdots&e^{\iota(k-1)\langle\mu_{k},\tau t_{0}\rangle}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\end{bmatrix}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}V\\
R\end{bmatrix}.

Here
V\in{\mathbb{C}}^{k\times k}
is a Vandermonde matrix with nodes
e^{\iota\langle\mu_{1},\tau t_{0}\rangle},e^{\iota\langle\mu_{2},\tau t_{0}\rangle},\cdots,e^{\iota\langle\mu_{k},\tau t_{0}\rangle}
.

Since
\tilde{D}
is a diagonal matrix with entries on the unit circle, it is a unitary matrix. Therefore, the singular values of the rotated weight matrix
W^{\prime}=\tilde{D}W\tilde{D}^{*}
are identical to those of
W
:

\sigma_{k}(W^{\prime})=\sigma_{k}(W)\geq w_{\min}^{2}.

(40)

Now we consider the singular values of
\tilde{\Phi}
. We have the lower bound:

\sigma_{k}(\tilde{\Phi})=\lambda_{k}(\tilde{\Phi}^{*}\tilde{\Phi})=\lambda_{k}(V^{*}V+R^{*}R)\geq\lambda_{k}(V^{*}V)=\sigma_{k}(V).

(41)

To derive the lower bound of Vandermonde matrix
V
, we first have

\sigma_{k}(V)=\frac{1}{\left\lVert V^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}\left\lVert V^{-1}\right\rVert_{\infty}}.

(42)

By Lemma
B.1
, it has

\|V^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leq\max_{1\leq j\leq k}\prod_{m\neq j}\frac{2}{|e^{\iota\tau\langle\mu_{j},t_{0}\rangle}-e^{\iota\tau\langle\mu_{m},t_{0}\rangle}|}.

(43)

Notice that
\left\lvert\tau\langle\mu_{j},t_{0}\rangle-\tau\langle\mu_{m},t_{0}\rangle\right\lvert\leq\tau\Delta\leq\pi
, we have

\left|e^{\iota\tau\langle\mu_{j},t_{0}\rangle}-e^{\iota\tau\langle\mu_{m},t_{0}\rangle}\right|\geq\frac{2}{\pi}\left|\tau\langle\mu_{j}-\mu_{m},t_{0}\rangle\right|\geq\frac{2\tau\Delta}{\pi k^{2}\sqrt{d}}.

(44)

For
1\leq j\leq k
, it follows that

\displaystyle\prod_{m\neq j}\frac{2}{\left|e^{\iota\tau\langle\mu_{j},v\rangle}-e^{\iota\tau\langle\mu_{m},v\rangle}\right|}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\pi k^{2}\sqrt{d}}{\tau\Delta}\right)^{k-1}.

Substituting this into the upper bound (
42
):

\|V^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leq\left(\frac{\pi k^{2}\sqrt{d}}{\tau\Delta}\right)^{k-1}.

(45)

Therefore,

\sigma_{k}(V)\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}\left\lVert V^{-1}\right\rVert_{\infty}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\left(\frac{\tau\Delta}{\pi k^{2}\sqrt{d}}\right)^{k-1}.

(46)

Finally, combining the bounds for the measurement matrix and the weight matrix:

\sigma_{k}(C)\geq\sigma_{k}^{2}(\tilde{\Phi})\sigma_{k}(W^{\prime})\geq\sigma_{k}^{2}(V)\sigma_{k}(W)\geq C_{k,d}(\tau\Delta)^{2k-2}\cdot w_{\min}^{2},

(47)

where
C_{k,d}=\frac{1}{k(\pi k^{2}\sqrt{d})^{2k-2}}
.
This completes the proof.

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{m=0}^{M}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert{\bm{e}}_{m}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{3L}\right)-M

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{m=0}^{M}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert{\bm{e}}_{m}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{3L^{3/2}}\right)-M

. Therefore, if the sample size

The objective function is
J(\mu)=\|\Pi_{U_{1}^{\perp}}\varphi_{L}(\mu)\|_{2}
, where
\Pi_{U_{1}^{\perp}}
is the orthogonal projection onto the noise subspace. We have

J(\mu_{j}+\delta)=\Pi_{U_{1}^{\perp}}\varphi_{L}(\mu_{j}+\delta)=\Pi_{U_{1}^{\perp}}\Big(\varphi_{L}(\mu_{j})+\nabla\varphi_{L}(\mu_{j})[\delta]+O(\|\delta\|^{2})\Big).

\Pi_{U_{1}^{\perp}}\varphi_{L}(\mu_{j})=0

\delta\mapsto\Pi_{U_{1}^{\perp}}(\nabla\varphi_{L}(\mu_{j})[\delta])

\nabla\varphi_{L}(\mu_{j})[\delta]\notin U_{1}\quad\text{for all }\delta\neq 0.

\delta

\{\varphi_{L}(\mu_{1}),\dots,\varphi_{L}(\mu_{k})\}
.

\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k},\beta

t
