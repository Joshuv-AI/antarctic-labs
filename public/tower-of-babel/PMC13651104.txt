QTL mapping and haplotype analysis reveal candidate genes for seedling-stage salinity tolerance in rice

Wei Xu, Chunyan Ju, Xiaoding Ma, Bing Han, Xiaohong Guo, Zhengwu Zhao, Longzhi Han, Di Cui

Abstract

IntroductionSoil salinization is a major constraint on rice production, and severe salinity levels lead to pronounced yield losses. Therefore, elucidating the genetic architecture of salinity tolerance and identifying candidate genes underlying this trait are crucial for molecular design breeding. MethodsIn this study, a population of 253 recombinant inbred lines (RILs) derived from the salt-sensitive japonica cultivar Jileng 1 (JL1) and the salt-tolerant indica cultivar Milyang 23 (MY23) was evaluated for seedling-stage salinity tolerance under 0.9% NaCl stress. ResultsUsing multiple phenotypic indices and a high-density genetic map constructed from whole-genome resequencing data, we identified 43 QTLs associated with seedling-stage salinity tolerance, including 11 major-effect loci, each explaining more than 10% of the phenotypic variance. By integrating functional annotation, haplotype analysis, RNA-seq-based co-expression network analysis, and qRT-PCR validation, OsFBX29 (LOC_Os01g59910) was prioritized as a strong candidate gene associated with seedling-stage salinity tolerance. Lines carrying the Hap2 haplotype exhibited enhanced salinity tolerance, and qRT-PCR analysis revealed significantly lower OsFBX29 expression levels (p < 0.05) in Hap2-carrying lines after salt treatment, suggesting a potential association between reduced OsFBX29 expression and enhanced salinity tolerance. DiscussionThese findings provide new insights into the genetic architecture of seedling-stage salinity tolerance and offer potential genetic resources for breeding salinity-tolerant rice varieties.

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is a major staple crop that is highly vulnerable to salinity stress, particularly during early developmental stages such as seedling establishment (). Salinity stress generally refers to the excessive accumulation of soluble salts in soil or irrigation water, which imposes both osmotic stress and ionic toxicity on plants. In rice production systems, soil electrical conductivity (EC) above approximately 4 dS m-1 can substantially restrict plant growth and productivity, and more severe salinity levels have been associated with pronounced yield losses (). At the physiological and biochemical levels, salinity stress inhibits water uptake and seedling establishment, disrupts Na+/K+ homeostasis, reduces chlorophyll accumulation and photosynthetic capacity, promotes reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and induces membrane damage (; ). Therefore, improving seedling-stage salinity tolerance in rice is essential for stable crop establishment in saline-alkaline soils and for maintaining yield stability. Although salinity tolerance at the reproductive stage is more directly associated with grain yield and remains an important target for salinity-tolerance breeding (), seedling-stage tolerance is also critical because early salt injury can impair seedling survival, stand establishment, and subsequent crop performance in saline soils (; ). Moreover, the seedling stage is a sensitive developmental phase during which genotypic differences in salinity responses can be effectively detected. Phenotyping at this stage under controlled hydroponic conditions also provides a rapid, reproducible, and scalable approach for screening large populations and identifying salinity-tolerance loci (; ). Therefore, the present study focused on seedling-stage salinity tolerance.

Salinity tolerance in rice is a complex quantitative trait governed by multiple genes. Previous quantitative trait locus (QTL) studies have identified numerous loci associated with salinity tolerance at both the seedling and reproductive stages, with many investigations focusing on seedling-stage responses (; ; ). In linkage analyses using biparental populations, the major seedling-stage QTL Saltol was mapped to chromosome 1 in an IR29 × Pokkali RIL population and has since been widely used in breeding and functional validation studies (). In a panel of 308 F4 families derived from Sahel 317 and Madina Koyo, seedling-stage screening under salt stress was combined with construction of a high-density genetic map using 3,698 SNPs; composite interval mapping subsequently identified 13 QTLs associated with salt injury score (SIS), seedling height, shoot dry weight, and root length, distributed across chromosomes 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 10, and 12 (). Using an F2 population derived from the salt-tolerant landrace Kalarata and the salt-sensitive cultivar Azucena, followed by seedling-stage phenotyping of F3 families, a linkage map based on 151 SSR and InDel markers was developed, and 13 QTLs were detected across 16 traits (). In addition, reciprocal mapping populations derived from the Bangladeshi salt-tolerant landrace Horkuch and the high-yielding cultivar IR29 were analyzed using an F2:3 design, and genetic maps constructed with DArTseq enabled the identification of 14 QTLs associated with salinity tolerance at the seedling and reproductive stages ().

Despite the large number of salinity-tolerance QTLs mapped to date, only a limited number of loci have been resolved to causal genes by map-based cloning with clear mechanistic evidence. Among these, SKC1 was shown to encode the HKT-type sodium transporter OsHKT1;5, which regulates Na+ transport and maintains K+ homeostasis under salt stress, representing a landmark example of the transition from QTL to functional gene (). Subsequent functional studies demonstrated that OsHKT1;5 promotes Na+ retrieval from the xylem in vascular tissues of roots and stems, contributing to internal ion redistribution, restricting Na+ delivery to young leaves and reproductive organs, and thereby alleviating salt toxicity and enhancing salinity tolerance (). Similarly, qSE3 was cloned and identified as the K+ transporter gene OsHAK21, which promotes seed germination and seedling establishment under salt stress (). These cloned loci have revealed that ion transport, particularly Na+ transport, K+ uptake, and Na+/K+ homeostasis, represents an important mechanism underlying rice salinity tolerance.

However, for most reported salinity-tolerance QTLs, the specific molecular processes they regulate—such as Na+ exclusion, K+ uptake or retention, Na+/K+ homeostasis, osmotic adjustment, ROS detoxification, and stress-signaling pathways—remain incompletely understood, and many QTLs remain genetically broad and have not been sufficiently narrowed to specific candidate genes (; ; ). In this study, we used the salt-sensitive japonica cultivar JL1 as the female parent and the salt-tolerant indica cultivar MY23 as the male parent to develop a recombinant inbred line (RIL) population comprising 253 F10 lines through continuous selfing using the single-seed descent method. Using a previously developed high-density genetic map based on whole-genome resequencing data, we performed QTL mapping for seedling-stage salinity tolerance and prioritized candidate genes associated with this trait. This study provides a foundation for elucidating the genetic basis of seedling-stage salinity tolerance in rice and offers valuable genetic resources for breeding salt-tolerant rice varieties.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and population development

The salt-sensitive japonica cultivar JL1 and the salt-tolerant indica cultivar MY23 were used as the female and male parents, respectively. The F1 plants and their progeny were continuously selfed using the single-seed descent method to develop a recombinant inbred line (RIL) population comprising 253 F10 lines. The RIL population was grown at the Changping Experimental Station of the Institute of Crop Sciences, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, under standard local agronomic management practices.

Phenotype evaluation and statistical analysis

Seeds were dried at 50 °C for 2 days to break dormancy. For each line, 30 plump seeds were surface-sterilized with 1.4% sodium hypochlorite for 15 min and then rinsed three times with distilled water. Seeds were soaked at 35 °C for 24 h and subsequently germinated at 35 °C for a further 24 h. Twelve uniformly germinated seeds were selected and sown in 96-well plates, and seedlings were grown in a controlled growth chamber at 50% relative humidity under a 12 h light/12 h dark photoperiod (30 °C during the light period and 25 °C during the dark period). After 4 days of growth, seedlings were transferred to Yoshida nutrient solution, which was renewed every 2 days. At the two-leaf-one-heart stage, seedlings were transferred to Yoshida nutrient solution supplemented with 0.9% (w/v) NaCl (approximately 154 mM NaCl) to impose salinity stress, a concentration previously used for seedling-stage salinity-tolerance evaluation in rice (; ). The salt-treatment solution was renewed every 2 days to maintain stable treatment conditions throughout the experiment. Salinity injury was scored daily from 2 to 7 days after the onset of salt treatment, generating six time-point traits: 2dSST, 3dSST, 4dSST, 5dSST, 6dSST, and 7dSST, according to the 1–9 scale described by , with lower scores indicating stronger salinity tolerance and higher scores indicating more severe salt injury. The single-plant salinity tolerance score (SST) was calculated as the average salinity-injury score of individual plants for each genotype. Chlorophyll content (CH) was measured using a SPAD meter. The DLR-derived salinity tolerance score (DSS) was assigned according to dead leaf rate thresholds: DSS = 1 for DLR ≤ 20%, 3 for 20% < DLR ≤ 40%, 5 for 40% < DLR ≤ 60%, 7 for 60% < DLR ≤ 80%, and 9 for 80% < DLR ≤ 100%. The SI-derived salinity tolerance score (ISS) was assigned according to salinity index thresholds: ISS = 1 for SI ≤ 15%, 3 for 15% < SI ≤ 30%, 5 for 30% < SI ≤ 60%, 7 for 60% < SI ≤ 85%, and 9 for 85% < SI ≤ 100%. All measurements were performed with three independent biological replicates.

Linkage map construction and QTL detection

A high-density genetic linkage map based on whole-genome resequencing data had been developed for this RIL population in our previous study (). The previously developed map comprised 2,921 bin markers spanning 1,408.03 cM, with an average genetic length of 117.34 cM per chromosome. The average inter-marker genetic distance was 0.48 cM. QTL detection in this study was performed using the BIP module in QTL IciMapping (). Permutation tests with 1,000 permutations were conducted separately for each phenotypic trait. The empirical LOD thresholds were all ≤ 2.5 across traits; therefore, a common threshold of LOD ≥ 2.5 was used to retain QTLs for further analysis. To reduce false-positive results, QTLs detected repeatedly across related traits or adjacent time points, especially those with relatively high LOD scores and/or PVE values, were interpreted with greater confidence and were prioritized for candidate-gene discovery, whereas isolated trait-specific QTLs were treated as putative loci requiring further validation. Additive effects and the proportion of phenotypic variance explained (PVE) by each QTL were subsequently estimated. QTL nomenclature followed the guidelines of . The linkage map was visualized using the LinkageMapView package in R ().

SNP annotation and haplotype analysis

Using the IRGSP-1.0 japonica reference genome, SNPs were subjected to quality control according to the genotyping and marker-filtering criteria. Sites with missing parental genotypes were excluded, and SNPs with parental base support ≥5 and progeny base support ≥3 were retained for downstream analyses. A total of 2,822,607 SNPs were annotated using SnpEff (). Nonsynonymous SNPs within the coding sequences and polymorphic SNPs within the 2-kb upstream promoter regions of candidate genes were included in site-wise haplotype analyses. At each retained SNP site, RILs carrying the two parental homozygous genotypes were assigned to Hap1 and Hap2, respectively (), whereas lines with heterozygous or missing genotypes were excluded. Phenotypic differences between Hap1 and Hap2 at each site were evaluated using Welch’s t-test.

RNA-seq and qRT-PCR validation

A representative salt-tolerant line and a salt-sensitive line were selected from the RIL population. Leaf samples were collected from these lines at two time points: 0 h (before treatment) and 6 h after the onset of 0.9% NaCl treatment, with three biological replicates per line per time point. Samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using an RNA extraction kit (Novogene, Beijing, China). Poly(A)-enriched libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. Raw reads were quality-filtered using fastp (v0.20.0) () and aligned to the IRGSP-1.0 rice reference genome using STAR (v2.7.9a) (), with gene models annotated according to MSU v7.0. Gene-level read counts were quantified using featureCounts (), and differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (). Genes with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1 were defined as differentially expressed genes. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was conducted using normalized expression data from the representative salt-tolerant and salt-sensitive lines to identify gene modules associated with the salt-stress response. Modules significantly correlated with treatment conditions (|r| ≥ 0.90, p < 0.001) were selected for further analysis. To account for potential variation among representative lines, module–condition associations were interpreted as group-level co-expression patterns and were used only as supporting evidence for candidate-gene prioritization. Network visualization was performed in Cytoscape (v3.7.1) (). To validate the RNA-seq results, quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using four representative salt-tolerant lines and four representative salt-sensitive lines. Leaf samples were collected at 0 h and 6 h after the onset of 0.9% NaCl treatment. qRT-PCR was conducted using THUNDERBIRD SYBR qPCR Mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan), with primer sequences provided in . Each sample was assayed in three technical replicates across three biological replicates. The ubiquitin gene (UBI) was used as the internal reference. Relative transcript levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method (), and statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-test in R (v4.2.2). Before the t-test, the normality of residuals was assessed, and no significant deviation from normality was detected. Because only one pre-specified pairwise comparison between the treatment group and the corresponding control group was performed for each target gene, multiple-comparison correction was not applied.

Results

Assessment of seedling-stage salinity tolerance

After 7 days of salt treatment, MY23 seedlings showed markedly higher vigor and better growth than JL1 (). Consistent with this observation, salinity tolerance scores of the two parents clearly diverged from day 2 to day 7: scores for JL1 and MY23 were 5.67 and 2.33, 7.00 and 3.67, 9.00 and 3.67, 9.00 and 4.33, 9.00 and 4.33, and 9.00 and 4.33, respectively (; ). For the female parent JL1, the values of seedling survival rate (SSR), chlorophyll content (CH), dead leaf rate (DLR), DLR-derived salinity tolerance score (DSS), salinity index (SI), SI-derived salinity tolerance score (ISS), and single-plant salinity tolerance score (SST) were 16.67%, 0.99, 84.44%, 8.33, 96.30%, 9.00, and 8.67, respectively; the corresponding values for the male parent MY23 were 69.44%, 22.06, 36.03%, 3.67, 64.81%, 6.33, and 5.83, respectively. Collectively, these results confirmed that MY23 exhibited substantially greater salinity tolerance than JL1.

In the RIL population, mean salinity tolerance scores from day 2 to day 7 were 3.76, 4.64, 5.65, 6.16, 6.43, and 6.77, respectively (; ). Mean values of SSR, CH, DLR, DSS, SI, ISS, and SST were 58.71%, 5.94, 71.24%, 6.78, 83.44%, 7.91, and 7.31, respectively. These salinity-related traits exhibited substantial phenotypic variation across the population, with coefficients of variation ranging from 11.57% to 84.70%, reflecting rich genetic diversity among the lines (). Further correlation analysis was performed among the salinity-related traits in the RIL population (). DLR, DSS, SI, ISS, and SST were significantly positively correlated with each other (r = 0.79–0.98, P < 0.01), with the highest correlation observed between DLR and DSS (r = 0.98, P < 0.01). Similarly, salinity tolerance scores at different evaluation stages (2dSST–7dSST) exhibited highly significant positive correlations (r = 0.60–0.96, P < 0.01). Conversely, SSR and CH exhibited significant negative correlations with DLR, DSS, SI, ISS, SST, and 2dSST–7dSST, with correlation coefficients ranging from −0.53 to −0.90 (P < 0.01).

QTL mapping of salinity tolerance

Using a high-density linkage map developed in our previous study (), QTL mapping was performed based on multiple seedling-stage salinity-related indices, including 2dSST to 7dSST, SSR, CH, DLR, DSS, SI, ISS, and SST (; ). In total, 43 QTLs were detected on chromosomes 1, 2, 3, 9, 10, and 12, with LOD scores ranging from 2.53 to 47.99 and phenotypic variance explained (PVE) ranging from 0.69% to 19.78%. Of these, 11 major QTLs each showed PVE > 10%.

Five QTLs were associated with 2dSST, among which q2dSST1 and q2dSST9 explained 14.37% and 12.08% of the phenotypic variance, respectively. For 3dSST, three QTLs were identified, with the highest contribution from q3dSST9 at 17.86%. Four QTLs were associated with 4dSST and 5dSST, with q4dSST9 and q5dSST9 contributing the most at 14.07% and 14.19%, respectively. Five QTLs were associated with 6dSST, with q6dSST9 contributing the most at 10.93%. Three QTLs were linked to 7dSST, with q7dSST9 showing the highest contribution at 19.14%. Two QTLs were associated with SSR, with qSSR1 and qSSR9 explaining 8.30% and 16.10% of the phenotypic variance, respectively. Three QTLs were associated with each of CH, DLR, DSS, and SST, with qSST9 showing the highest contribution at 13.50%. Two QTLs were linked to SI, and three to ISS, with qISS9 contributing the most at 19.78%.

Overlapping QTL intervals were observed among several salinity-related traits, including traits measured at adjacent time points. These overlapping intervals were referred to as co-localized or positionally clustered QTL regions for candidate-region prioritization. As interval overlap alone does not necessarily indicate a shared causal locus, these co-localized or positionally clustered regions were used only for candidate-gene prioritization based on repeated detection, LOD scores, and PVE values. On chromosome 1, the QTL clusters were arranged from top to bottom on the linkage map as follows: qCH1 and qSST1; q3dSST1–1 and qISS1; q4dSST1-1, q5dSST1-1, q6dSST1-1, q7dSST1, qDLR1, and qDSS1; q2dSST1, q3dSST1-2, and qSSR1; and q4dSST1-2, q5dSST1-2, and q6dSST1-2. On chromosome 9, two positionally clustered QTL regions were identified: q2dSST9 and q3dSST9; and q4dSST9, q5dSST9, q6dSST9, q7dSST9, qSSR9, qCH9, qDLR9, qDSS9, qSI9, qISS9, and qSST9. On chromosome 10, the co-localized QTL clusters were arranged from top to bottom as follows: q5dSST10, q6dSST10, qDLR10, and qISS10; and q4dSST10, q7dSST10, qCH10, qDSS10, and qSST10. Based on their repeated detection across related traits or traits measured at adjacent time points, together with relatively high LOD scores and PVE values, QTL regions on chromosomes 1, 9, and 10 were prioritized for subsequent candidate-gene discovery and haplotype analysis ().

Haplotype analysis of candidate genes

More than 300 genes were identified within the three major QTL intervals on chromosomes 1, 9, and 10 (), including 43 homologs of genes previously reported to be involved in salinity tolerance or salt stress responses. Of these 43 genes, seven harbored nonsynonymous SNPs based on SnpEff annotation. Based on nonsynonymous SNP screening and functional annotation, four candidate genes potentially associated with salinity tolerance were prioritized. Among these, OsFBX29 (LOC_Os01g59910) and OsFBX339 (LOC_Os09g35680) encode F-box domain-containing proteins. OsMYB21 (LOC_Os01g63460) encodes a putative MYB-family transcription factor, whereas OsSub59 (LOC_Os09g36110) encodes a putative subtilisin homolog.

SNPs located in the coding sequences and 2-kb promoter regions of the four candidate genes were identified by comparing JL1 and MY23. For haplotype analysis, RILs carrying the homozygous JL1-derived genotype were classified as Hap1, whereas those carrying the homozygous MY23-derived genotype were classified as Hap2. For example, based on nonsynonymous variation in the CDS, Hap1 and Hap2 of OsFBX29 were represented by T (n = 174) and C (n = 72), respectively; those of OsMYB21 were represented by T (n = 196) and C (n = 53); those of OsFBX339 were represented by A (n = 161) and G (n = 87); and those of OsSub59 were represented by GGCCGGCA (n = 111) and GGCCGATC (n = 50), respectively ().

For OsFBX29, one nonsynonymous SNP was identified in the coding region at Chr1:34,645,688 (T>C), together with 18 SNPs in the 2-kb promoter region spanning Chr1:34,646,430 to Chr1:34,648,040 (). Based on significance tests of 6dSST between the two haplotypes defined by each SNP, 12 SNPs showed significant differences (P < 0.05; ). Similarly, significant differences in 6dSST were also detected between the two haplotypes defined by respective SNPs in OsFBX339, OsMYB21, and OsSub59 (P < 0.05; -).

Gene co-expression network analysis

RNA-seq was performed on salt-sensitive (S) and salt-tolerant (R) lines at 0 h and 6 h after salt treatment. WGCNA identified seven co-expression modules, among which the turquoise module showed a significant association with salt treatment (; ). This module contained 6,385 genes, including known salt stress-related genes such as OsRLCK311 () and OsMSRA4.1 (). GO enrichment analysis revealed that genes in the turquoise module were predominantly enriched in transcriptional regulation and RNA processing categories, including transcription by RNA polymerase II, regulation of RNA polymerase II-dependent transcription, RNA splicing, and mediator complex, suggesting a role in transcriptional reprogramming during the salt stress response ().

WGCNA analysis showed that OsFBX29, OsSWEET6b (LOC_Os01g42090), and OsRAV9 (LOC_Os01g04800) were assigned to the turquoise module. Among these genes, only OsFBX29 was located within the prioritized QTL regions and was further supported by haplotype analysis. Therefore, OsFBX29 was prioritized as a strong candidate gene associated with seedling-stage salinity tolerance.

Expression analysis of OsFBX29 under salt stress

RNA-seq analysis showed that OsFBX29 expression increased in the salt-sensitive line, whereas a slight decreasing trend was observed in the salt-tolerant line (). To further validate the expression pattern of the prioritized candidate gene OsFBX29, qRT-PCR analysis was performed. OsFBX29 displayed an expression pattern consistent with both the RNA-seq expression trend and the phenotypic contrast between tolerant and sensitive lines. At 6 h after salt treatment, OsFBX29 expression was significantly downregulated in the salt-tolerant lines but significantly upregulated in the salt-sensitive lines (). Based on the 6 h/0 h expression ratio, mean OsFBX29 expression decreased to approximately 0.73-fold in the salt-tolerant lines but increased to approximately 2.15-fold in the salt-sensitive lines relative to 0 h. The relative expression range in the salt-tolerant lines (0.63–0.81) was markedly lower than that in the salt-sensitive lines (1.72–2.76) under salt stress. These results indicate that Hap2-carrying tolerant lines exhibited reduced OsFBX29 expression under salt stress, whereas Hap1-carrying sensitive lines showed increased expression. This expression pattern is consistent with the haplotype-associated phenotypic differences and provides additional expression-based support for prioritizing OsFBX29 as a candidate gene associated with seedling-stage salinity tolerance.

Discussion

Seedling-stage salinity tolerance in rice

Salinity stress is a major environmental constraint affecting rice growth, development, and yield (; ; ). In recent years, substantial progress has been made in identifying salinity-tolerance-related genes and dissecting the genetic basis of salinity tolerance in rice (; ; ; ). Because the seedling stage is particularly sensitive to salinity stress, phenotypic evaluation at this stage provides an important basis for elucidating the genetic architecture of salinity tolerance (). Previous studies using RIL populations have identified multiple QTLs associated with seedling-stage salinity tolerance, including the Saltol locus in IR29 × Pokkali (), qST1 and qST3 in Milyang 23 × Gihobyeo (), and several QTLs in At354 × Bg352 (). Collectively, these findings indicate that seedling-stage salinity tolerance in rice is a complex quantitative trait governed by multiple loci.

In the present study, the RIL population derived from the cross between the salt-sensitive japonica cultivar JL1 and the salt-tolerant indica cultivar MY23 exhibited substantial phenotypic variation under 0.9% NaCl treatment at the seedling stage. The continuous distribution and clear transgressive segregation of salinity-tolerance-related traits among the RILs indicate that seedling-stage salinity tolerance in this population is a quantitatively inherited trait governed by multiple genetic factors (). This phenotypic variation further suggests that the parental lines carry contrasting alleles for salinity tolerance, supporting the suitability of this population for QTL mapping and candidate-gene identification. In addition, this study evaluated rice salt tolerance under a single salinity level of 0.9% NaCl, which presents certain experimental limitations. The growth responses and physiological regulatory mechanisms of rice germplasm vary significantly under mild, moderate, and severe salt stress conditions. Accordingly, the results of this study are most applicable to moderately saline soils with salt concentrations close to 0.9% NaCl. Three salt-tolerant RIL lines (RIL 69, RIL 143, and RIL 248) were identified based on their consistently favorable performance across the evaluated seedling-stage salinity-tolerance-related traits (). However, in highly saline-alkali soils with salt concentrations substantially higher than 0.9% NaCl, the observed tolerance advantages may not be consistently expressed. Future studies will incorporate multiple salinity gradients to establish a more comprehensive stress evaluation system and to identify superior germplasm adapted to different levels of saline-alkali environments.

Identification and comparison of salinity-tolerance QTLs in rice

In this study, QTL mapping identified 43 QTLs associated with seedling-stage salinity tolerance based on multiple phenotypic indices measured at different time points. To clarify the novelty of these loci, the QTL intervals identified in the present study were compared with previously reported salinity-tolerance-related QTLs and genes based on available physical positions or marker-defined regions anchored to the rice Nipponbare reference genome (). Among the 43 QTLs, 23 QTLs (53.49%) showed no clear positional correspondence with previously reported loci and were therefore considered potentially novel. In addition, 11 QTLs each explained more than 10% of the phenotypic variation, suggesting that several loci detected here may have relatively large effects on seedling-stage salinity tolerance. Among the major-effect QTLs, qISS9 showed the largest effect, explaining 19.78% of the phenotypic variation. Comparison with earlier studies revealed several genomic regions that showed overlap with previously reported salinity-tolerance-related loci. mapped qPH1.1s on chromosome 1 within the RM246–RM431 interval, corresponding to approximately 27.33–38.89 Mb, which overlapped with qCH1 and qSST1, identified in the present study. Similarly, the QSkc1 locus reported by in the RM297–RM315 region of chromosome 1, corresponding to approximately 32.09–36.73 Mb also overlapped with qCH1 and qSST1. On chromosome 10, the SalTol10–1 locus reported by in the RM25092–RM25519 region, corresponding to approximately 5.34–16.79 Mb overlapped with several QTLs identified here, including qCH10, q4dSST10, q7dSST10, qDSS10, and qSST10 within the 12.93–13.14 Mb interval; qDLR10, q5dSST10, q6dSST10, and qISS10 within the 13.72–13.94 Mb interval; and q2dSST10 within the 15.74–15.92 Mb interval. In addition, qSKC10, previously localized by to the RM311–RM467 region on chromosome 10, also overlapped with qCH10, q4dSST10, q7dSST10, qDSS10, and qSST10 identified in the present study.

These overlapping genomic regions provide supporting evidence for the relevance of several genomic regions identified in this study to seedling-stage salinity tolerance, whereas the identification of 23 potentially novel QTLs highlights the added value of the present study and provides additional candidate regions for fine mapping and molecular breeding.

OsFBX29 is associated with seedling-stage salinity tolerance

In this study, QTL mapping combined with haplotype analysis, expression profiling, and co-expression network analysis prioritized OsFBX29 as a strong candidate gene associated with seedling-stage salinity tolerance. Among the haplotypes detected at this locus, Hap2-carrying lines exhibited significantly enhanced salinity tolerance compared with Hap1-carrying lines. qRT-PCR analysis further showed that OsFBX29 expression was reduced in Hap2-carrying tolerant lines but increased in Hap1-carrying sensitive lines under salt stress, providing additional expression-based support for the association between OsFBX29 and seedling-stage salinity tolerance.

OsFBX29 encodes an F-box domain-containing protein and belongs to the large F-box family of ubiquitination regulators in plants (; ). F-box proteins typically function as the substrate-recognition subunits of the SCF (SKP1–CUL1–F-box) E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, recruiting specific target proteins and promoting their degradation via the 26S proteasome pathway. Through this mechanism, F-box proteins play important roles in hormone signaling and abiotic stress responses (; ; ; ). OsFBX257 has been reported to enhance salt tolerance in transgenic rice and Arabidopsis, whereas OsFBX148 enhances salinity tolerance when heterologously overexpressed in Arabidopsis, possibly by regulating Na+ distribution and maintaining ROS homeostasis (; ). Given its F-box domain and the reported roles of salt-related F-box proteins, OsFBX29 may be involved in salt-stress regulation through ubiquitin-mediated protein turnover, providing a plausible biological context for its association with salinity tolerance. Notably, the reduced expression of OsFBX29 observed in Hap2-carrying tolerant lines further supports its potential association with seedling-stage salinity tolerance.

In summary, phenotypic and expression analyses supported a potential association between variation at the OsFBX29 locus and seedling-stage salinity tolerance, with lower OsFBX29 expression observed in Hap2-carrying tolerant lines under salt stress.

Conclusion

In this study, we identified 43 QTLs associated with seedling-stage salinity tolerance in rice. Through integrated analyses encompassing gene annotation within QTL intervals, haplotype analysis, co-expression network analysis, and qRT-PCR validation, OsFBX29 was prioritized as a promising candidate gene associated with seedling-stage salinity tolerance. Nevertheless, its biological function and causal role in salinity tolerance require further functional validation through genetic approaches in future studies. Our findings provide new insights into the genetic basis of seedling-stage salinity tolerance and offer useful candidate regions and genes for future fine mapping, functional characterization, and salinity-tolerance breeding.
