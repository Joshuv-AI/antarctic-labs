Development and validation of a predictive model for postoperative complications in thymoma patients
Authors: Zhendong Lu, Fangchao Liu, Dongjie Yan, Jinghui Wang, Teng Ma, Hongyun Ruan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop and validate an intervention model for the risk factors of postoperative complications in patients with thymoma.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical data of patients who underwent thymoma resection surgery at our hospital between June 2006 and July 2024. Postoperative complications were systematically categorized according to the Clavien-Dindo classification (Grade II-V), including but not limited to: ① 30-day mortality, ② respiratory failure (defined as mechanical ventilation > 48 h or unplanned reintubation), ③ myocardial injury (troponin elevation > 5×ULN).Whether complications occurred was divided into two groups. The data were randomly divided into a modeling cohort (70%) and a validation cohort (30%). Logistic regression was used to identify risk factors for postoperative complications, and model performance was evaluated using ROC curves and calibration plots.ResultsA total of 26.1% (66/253) of thymoma patients developed postoperative complications. Multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed that Masaoka-Koga stage, Myasthenia Gravis, intraoperative blood loss, forced expiratory volume in 1 second, and albumin were significant predictors in the model. The model demonstrated an area under the ROC curve of 0.84 (95% CI: 0.78–0.91) in the modeling cohort and 0.76 (95% CI: 0.63–0.89) in the validation cohort. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test showed: modeling cohort (χ2 = 5.0997, P = 0.6478), validation cohort (χ2 = 4.9946, P = 0.7582). Decision curve analysis indicated that the risk thresholds for the modeling and validation cohorts were 10%-70% and 10%-60%.ConclusionThis study developed a risk prediction model for postoperative complications in thymoma patients, demonstrating strong discriminative ability (C-statistic 0.82, 95% CI 0.78–0.86) and calibration (Brier score 0.11) upon internal validation. The model may aid in identifying high-risk patients for targeted preventive measures, though its impact on actual complication rates requires prospective intervention trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-026-04294-4.Keywords: Thymoma, Surgical Resection, Postoperative Complications, Intervention model, Nomogram

Background

The thymus is a critical immune organ in the human body, and thymoma, originating from thymic epithelial cells, has an incidence rate of 1.3–3.2 per 100,000, with a slightly higher incidence in China at 4.09/10 thousand compared to Western countries [1, 2]. Thymomas tend to grow insidiously and invasively, often in close proximity to vital organs, and are commonly associated with paraneoplastic syndromes. The gold standard surgical approach for thymoma is a median sternotomy, which disrupts the integrity of the sternum and is associated with significant trauma, contributing to a relatively high postoperative mortality rate [2–4].

Surgical treatment is the primary modality for thymoma patients, as tumor resection alleviates symptoms, reduces metastasis, and improves long-term survival [4, 5]. However, postoperative complications occur in up to 25–48% of patients, making them a leading cause of postoperative mortality [6, 7]. Some patients may develop chronic respiratory failure postoperatively and require long-term invasive mechanical ventilation. Currently, there is a lack of comprehensive research on the risk factors for postoperative complications in thymoma surgery. we aim to construct a intervention model for postoperative complications in thymoma patients. We hope that this model will assist in identifying high-risk patients preoperatively, allowing for personalized interventions that can help reduce the risk of postoperative complications and mortality.

Materials and methods

We retrospectively analyzed clinical data from patients who underwent thymoma resection in the Department of Thoracic Surgery between June 2006 and July 2024, extracted from our hospital’s electronic medical records. In this study, postoperative complications of thymoma were defined as any of the following occurring from the time of surgery until discharge: postoperative death, myasthenic crisis, respiratory muscle paralysis, respiratory failure, infections of the lungs or other organs, and organ dysfunction (heart, liver, or kidney). A total of 187 patients were included in the group without complications, and 66 patients were included in the group with complications.

Inclusion Criteria: (I) Postoperative pathological diagnosis confirming thymoma. (II) Preoperative arterial blood gas analysis showing no respiratory failure. (III) Presence of comorbid conditions, provided the diseases were well-controlled. (IV) MG Classification Criteria: Osserman grades I-II are eligible for inclusion; pulmonary function quantitative criteria (FEV1%pred ≥ 50% and DLCO%pred ≥ 40%). (V) Complete clinical data available for analysis.

Exclusion Criteria: (I) Severe cardiac dysfunction (NYHA III-IV or LVEF < 40%). (II) Advanced renal impairment (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m² or dialysis-dependent). (III) History of other malignancies within the past 5 years. (IV) Recent immunosuppression (systemic agents within 30 days prior to surgery). (V) Laboratory-confirmed active infection (CRP > 10 mg/L or PCT ≥ 0.5 ng/mL).VI. preoperative assessment must confirm no active myasthenic crisis(Preoperative MGFA Clinical Classification ≥ IV or requiring mechanical ventilation).To minimize selection bias, we included consecutive thymoma resection patients during the study period, excluding only those with absolute contraindications to surgery (chronic cardiac/renal insufficiency) or competing mortality risks (concurrent malignancies).

This study was designed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Chest Hospital, affiliated with Capital Medical University, which granted a waiver of written informed consent for this retrospective study (Ethical Approval No: Clinical Research 2019(85)).

Incidence of postoperative complications: The proportion of patients who experienced Clavien-Dindo grade II or higher complications within 30 days after surgery. Grading Criteria: Grade I: Any deviation from the normal postoperative course requiring only basic interventions (e.g., antipyretics, antiemetics). Grade II: Requires pharmacological treatment (including blood transfusion, TPN, etc.). Grade III: Requires surgical, radiological, or endoscopic intervention. IIIa: Intervention performed without general anesthesia. IIIb: Intervention performed under general anesthesia. Grade IV: Life-threatening complications. IVa: Single-organ dysfunction. IVb: Multi-organ dysfunction. Grade V: Death. Two independent surgeons should perform grading. Discrepant cases should be adjudicated by a third senior surgeon,

Baseline Data Collection: General clinical data collected included gender, age, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, and major comorbidities (including hypertension, coronary artery disease, chronic lung disease, and diabetes). Tumor size (in cm) was measured based on the maximum diameter of the tumor during surgery. Thymoma was staged according to the Masaoka-Koga system (Stage I, II, III, and partial Stage IV). Pathological classification followed the WHO system (A, AB, B1, B2, B3). The Osserman MG staging system was used to categorize myasthenia gravis (MG) as Stage 0 (no MG) and Stage ≥ 2 (MG).

Pulmonary function parameters included the percentage of predicted forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1%pred) and the percentage of predicted diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO%pred). Laboratory tests included hemoglobin, serum albumin, and C-reactive protein levels. Chest CT scans were reviewed to assess the invasion of surrounding organs, tissues, and blood vessels.

Surgical Information: Surgeon experience (≥ 5 years vs. <5 years in thymoma resection). Anesthetic protocols (No neuromuscular reversal agents were administered in any protocol). VATS procedures utilized Two ports (10 mm, 12 mm). Open surgeries employed a 10–15 cm anterolateral incision. All patients received ICU monitoring. Common surgical approaches included left or right video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, median sternotomy, and left or right thoracotomy. Intraoperative variables collected included blood loss volume and surgical duration. Additional surgical details included whether the tumor invaded the phrenic nerve, resulting in intraoperative resection; whether the tumor involved the pericardium, portions of the lung, lymph nodes, or adjacent adipose tissue.

Statistical analysis

The number and proportions of patients, as well as various demographic and clinical characteristics, were summarized in tables to identify potential factors associated with thymoma surgery. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and intergroup comparisons were performed using the t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median with interquartile range (25th, 75th percentiles), and group comparisons were made using the Z-test. Categorical data were presented as frequency or percentage (%), and intergroup comparisons were performed using the χ2 test, descriptive statistics were applied to baseline data. Patients were randomly divided into a modeling cohort and a validation cohort at a 7:3 ratio. To convert continuous variables into categorical ones, the area under the curve (AUC) method was used to determine cut-off values for the variables (Fig. 1A), with the exception of age and BMI. For age, the median was chosen as the cutoff, while BMI was categorized according to the internationally recognized weight classification standards: BMI < 18.5 kg/m² was considered underweight, 18.5 kg/m² ≤ BMI < 24.0 kg/m² was classified as normal weight, 24.0 kg/m² ≤ BMI < 28.0 kg/m² as overweight, and BMI ≥ 28.0 kg/m² as obese [8]. After all variable conversions were completed, we further assessed whether there were significant differences between these categorical variables in the training set (modeling cohort) and the validation set (validation cohort).Because this is an exploratory retrospective study, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed. Instead, the sample size was determined by including all consecutive eligible patients during the study period (2006–2024) to maximize statistical power. To ensure the robustness of the multivariable logistic regression model and prevent overfitting, we strictly adhered to the widely accepted “events per variable” (EPV) criterion. An EPV of 10 or greater is generally recommended for predictive modeling. In our study, 66 patients experienced the outcome (major complications). Consequently, the maximum number of candidate predictors allowed in the final model was constrained to 6. Our final model included 5 predictors, yielding an EPV of 13.2, which adequately fulfills the requirements for statistical power and model reliability.

Flowchart of complications in thymoma patients

Logistic regression was used to identify risk factors for postoperative complications in thymoma patients. In the univariate logistic regression analysis, variables with a p-value < 0.1 were considered potentially associated with the study outcomes and were further analyzed using backward stepwise logistic regression. A regression model with the minimum Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was used to construct a nomogram [8]. Multicollinearity was assessed through correlation tests and variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis [9]. To further evaluate model stability and assess potential optimism, we implemented four rigorous resampling techniques specifically within the modeling cohort: Leave-Group-Out Cross-Validation (LGOCV): Bootstrap = 1000, Split ratio = 0.7; K-fold Cross-Validation (K-fold CV): K = 5; Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV); Bootstrapping: K = 5, Bootstrap = 1000.Accuracy and Kappa values were used to assess model consistency during these resampling processes. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to evaluate the discriminative ability of the model, while calibration was assessed using calibration curves and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (HLGT) [10]. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was employed to evaluate the clinical benefit of the nomogram [11]. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.2.0. The following R packages were utilized: “mass” (for stepwise regression); “rms” (for constructing the nomogram); “car” (for calculating VIF); “pROC” (for plotting the ROC curve); “rms” (for calculating and plotting calibration curves and performing the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test); and “rmda” (for decision curve analysis).

Results

Sample Characteristics: A total of 253 patients were included in this study, and their characteristics were categorized for statistical analysis, Fig. 1. Postoperative complications included: 2 deaths within 30 days of surgery, 33 patients of respiratory failure, 26 patients of postoperative infections, 3 patients of heart failure, 1 case of liver dysfunction, and 1 case of renal dysfunction. Among the patients who developed respiratory failure postoperatively, 64.7% (22/33) had severe respiratory failure, with the duration of invasive mechanical ventilation ranging from 12 to 252 hours. Preoperative comorbidities included 54 patients of hypertension, 18 patients of diabetes, 16 patients of previous benign tumors, 9 patients of gastrointestinal diseases, 10 patients of cardiovascular diseases, 10 patients of respiratory diseases, and 6 patients of gallbladder diseases. All patients underwent tracheal intubation and general anesthesia, with no anesthesia-related adverse events. Muscle relaxants were not used during induction, and all patients safely returned to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) after surgery.

Compared to the group without postoperative complications, the complication group had a higher proportion of male patients (44.39% vs. 60.61%) and patients with myasthenia gravis (MG) (7.49% vs. 24.24%) (P = 0.023 and < 0.001, respectively). The forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1%pred) and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO%pred) were significantly lower in the complication group (P = 0.002 and 0.013, respectively). Tumor size (maximum diameter) was significantly larger in the complication group (median: 6.0 cm vs. 5.0 cm in the non-complication group; P = 0.007). Similarly, operation time (120 vs. 100 min, P = 0.001) and intraoperative blood loss (100 vs. 50 mL, P < 0.001) were higher in the complication group.Regarding surgical approach, thoracoscopic surgery was predominantly performed in the group without complications, while thoracotomy was more common in the complication group (72.73% vs. 36.36%, P < 0.001). In the univariate regression analysis of establishment modeling cohort, multiple risk factors for postoperative complications were identified. These included Masaoka-Koga stage, pathological type, MG, maximum tumor diameter, operation time, blood loss volume, surgical method, clearing range, albumin (Alb), FEV1%pred, DLCO%pred, and C-reactive protein (CRP), all of which were significantly associated with postoperative complication risk (P < 0.05).

After variable selection through multivariate logistic regression, the best predictors for postoperative complication risk were identified as Masaoka-Koga stage, MG, blood loss volume, Alb, and FEV1%pred. These variables were found to be independent risk factors for postoperative complications in thymoma patients (P < 0.05), with MG (Myasthenia Gravis) being the most significant factor associated with an increased risk of complications (OR: 7.43, 95% CI: 2.23–24.78, P = 0.001), Fig. 2 and Table 3.

Model Validation and Evaluation: The intervention model was applied to assess the risk of postoperative complications in the training cohort. The results indicated that the model demonstrated good discriminative ability, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.84 (95% CI: 0.78–0.91) in the modeling cohort and 0.76 (95% CI: 0.63–0.89) in the validation cohort (Fig. 3). The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was also used to evaluate model calibration, with results showing a good agreement between predicted and observed risks (modeling cohort: χ2 = 5.0997, P = 0.6478; validation cohort: χ2 = 4.9946, P = 0.7582). The model demonstrated high calibration, and calibration curves in both the training and validation cohorts showed good alignment, Fig. 4A-B.To assess the clinical benefit of the model, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed. The decision curve analysis for both the training and validation cohorts is shown in Fig. 4C-D. To evaluate the reliability of the modeling process, four resampling methods were applied to the training data. The results demonstrated high consistency across all frameworks, with Accuracy values stabilized around 0.79 and Kappa values ranging between 0.40 and 0.44, Table 4. This indicates that the selected predictors are fundamentally robust and that the model is resilient to random data fluctuations.The nomogram predicted postoperative complications, and when the risk threshold was set between 10% and 70% in the training cohort and 10% and 60% in the validation cohort, clinical intervention based on the model could provide potential benefits for thymoma patients after surgery.

Discussion

While prior models have focused on MG or general complications post-thymectomy, our study specifically integrates Masaoka-Koga stage, intraoperative blood loss, and dynamic pulmonary function metrics (FEV1%pred/DLCO%pred) into a unified nomogram. This approach addresses the multifactorial nature of thymoma complications, particularly for patients with advanced-stage tumors or compromised respiratory reserve, which were underrepresented in earlier studies [12, 13]. Additionally, our model provides clinically actionable risk thresholds (10–70%) via decision curve analysis, enabling tailored preoperative optimization.

Thymomas are often asymptomatic in their early stages, but as the tumor gradually increases in size, patients may develop a range of clinical symptoms and often present with other immune-related diseases, significantly complicating treatment and increasing the patient’s risk of mortality [14]. Surgery is the preferred treatment for thymomas; however, postoperative complications such as infections and hemorrhages cannot be overlooked [15]. Therefore, constructing an accurate intervention model for postoperative complications in thymoma patients is crucial. This nomogram, internally validated in a cohort of 253 patients, demonstrated moderate but statistically significant discriminative ability (C-index 0.72, 95%CI 0.68–0.76) and calibration. While these findings suggest its potential to guide personalized risk assessment, external validation and prospective impact studies are needed before clinical implementation.

The nomogram model incorporates multiple preoperative and intraoperative variables, covering the patient’s disease characteristics (Masaoka-Koga stage, MG), physiological status (lung function, albumin levels), and intraoperative blood loss. It provides a comprehensive risk assessment for postoperative complications. Among these, a higher Masaoka-Koga stage (III or IVa) directly impacts prognosis. Analysis of intraoperative blood loss, lung function, and nutritional status offers important insights for systemic risk assessment of postoperative complications.

A key predictor to focus on is myasthenia gravis (MG), as 75%−80% of MG patients have thymic abnormalities, and 30% of thymoma patients are diagnosed with MG. The presence of MG significantly increases the risk of postoperative complications [16–20]. MG is an autoimmune disorder characterized by muscle weakness and fatigue, acquired autoimmune disease with impaired transmission at the neuromuscular junction mediated by anti-acetylcholine receptor binding antibody (ARAb), cellular immunity-dependent, complement-involved, resulting in impaired transmission of acetylcholine (ACHR) receptors at the neuromuscular junction in respiratory muscles. This leads to impaired neuromuscular transmission at the respiratory muscles, resulting in respiratory dysfunction, and in severe patients, carbon dioxide retention and type II respiratory failure [19, 22, 24].FEV1%pred (ventilatory capacity) and DLCO%pred (gas exchange) are essential for predicting postoperative respiratory complications, while hypoalbuminemia increases infection and poor wound healing risks due to impaired immunity [21, 23,24].The clinical utility of this nomogram lies in its real-time. stratification and versatile applicability.

Intraoperative factors such as prolonged operation time and increased blood loss are associated with a higher risk of postoperative complications [25]. Blood loss exceeding 85 mL is an independent predictor for the occurrence of complications. The reasons for significant intraoperative blood loss and extended surgery time include large surgical incisions and substantial exudate. Moreover, excessive blood loss can lead to postoperative hypoproteinemia. If blood albumin is not replenished in a timely manner, low colloid osmotic pressure may cause capillary leakage. Additionally, infection of the surgical wound, release of inflammatory mediators, and endothelial damage to capillaries can further exacerbate abnormal fluid exudation, delaying postoperative recovery and increasing the risk of complications.

Through decision curve analysis, we further evaluated the clinical utility of the nomogram. The results suggest that, within certain risk thresholds, the nomogram can effectively predict postoperative complications and guide clinical intervention, ultimately benefiting patients by improving surgical safety. This provides scientific decision support for clinicians in developing personalized treatment plans and postoperative management strategies. In specific clinical scenarios, the model constructed in this study, based on preoperative examination data, offers a convenient and efficient tool for predicting the risk of postoperative complications. Based on the predicted risk, clinicians can adjust the surgical and anesthetic plans according to the patient’s individual risk profile. For high-risk patients, more conservative surgical approaches or more meticulous anesthetic management may be considered to reduce the incidence of postoperative complications. Additionally, identifying high-risk factors for complications preoperatively allows for more intensive monitoring and care postoperatively. Early identification of high-risk patients helps clinicians design targeted postoperative care plans, schedule critical tests, and anticipate potential interventions to ensure early detection and management of complications, ultimately improving patient outcomes.

However, our study has certain limitations. First, it is based on retrospective data from a single center and has not been validated with large external datasets, which may limit the model’s external applicability and generalizability across different medical institutions and populations. Although internal validation showed robustness, the absence of external validation remains a key limitation. Our ‘validation cohort’ was derived from random split-sample validation, which may overestimate generalizability. Future studies should utilize the ITMIG multicenter database for external validation. Second, the study uses composite complications as the outcome measure, while different types of postoperative complications may have distinct pathophysiological mechanisms and influencing factors. While predictors such as myasthenia gravis and pulmonary function are individually well-established, relying solely on subjective clinical judgment to weigh these disparate factors can lead to high inter-observer variability. Due to limitations in retrospective data collection, we were unable to distinguish de novo postoperative myasthenic crisis from exacerbations of pre-existing MG, which may introduce potential misclassification bias.The primary value of our nomogram lies in its ability to synthesize these established multi-dimensional variables into a single, objective, and quantifiable probability score. Furthermore, rather than merely evaluating predictive accuracy (AUC), we utilized DCA to explicitly quantify clinical utility. Our DCA results demonstrate that within a broad range of threshold probabilities (10%–70%), using this nomogram to guide preventive interventions yields a higher net clinical benefit than treating all or no patients, thereby proving its superiority over ad-hoc clinical judgment.This approach may result in the oversight of some important specific intervention factors, which could potentially affect the model’s intervention accuracy for particular types of complications.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study developed a predictive intervention model for postoperative complications in thymoma patients, demonstrating robust discriminative ability and good calibration in internal validation using bootstrap resampling. While these results are promising, the model’s generalizability should be further confirmed through prospective, multicenter external validation involving diverse patient populations (e.g., varying surgical approaches and comorbidities). If validated, this model could support personalized postoperative management strategies, potentially improving clinical outcomes. However, until larger-scale studies are conducted, its application in routine practice should be implemented with caution and ideally within a decision-support framework.
