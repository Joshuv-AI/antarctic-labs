Single-copy stabilizer learning: average case and worst case Preprint: APS/123-QED
Gyungmin Cho, Dohun Kim

Abstract
We study single-copy stabilizer learning, the problem of identifying a stabilizer group of dimension n − t n-t from an n n -qubit quantum state ρ \rho . We obtain two complementary results. First, in the average case, logarithmic-depth local Clifford circuits suffice to efficiently learn almost all stabilizer groups with t = O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) t=O(\log n) , instead of the linear-depth measurements required in previous approaches. We support this result with numerical simulations for systems of up to 100 qubits. Second, we show that, in the worst case, any adaptive single-copy measurement scheme requires a number of samples that scales exponentially in t t . Together with existing results on two-copy learning, our findings suggest that, for large t t , identifying Pauli symmetries of a quantum system exhibits a quantum advantage in the learning setting.



I
Introduction

Stabilizer states are a central class of quantum states, defined as the common eigenspace of an abelian subgroup of the Pauli group. An
n
-qubit stabilizer state is uniquely specified by
n
independent commuting Pauli operators. Stabilizer states play an important role across quantum information science and many-body physics. In quantum information, most quantum error-correcting codes admit a stabilizer description, making these states fundamental for fault-tolerant quantum computation
Gottesman 1997
. In many-body physics, they also provide canonical examples of topological order, such as the logical states of the toric code
Kitaev 2003
.

t
-doped stabilizer states provide a simple interpolation between stabilizer states and more general quantum states. Any stabilizer state can be prepared from
\ket{0^n}
by a Clifford circuit generated by {CNOT, Hadamard, Phase}. If a small number of non-Clifford gates, such as
T
gates, are inserted, some of the Pauli stabilizer structure is typically lost. The resulting state is no longer stabilized by
n
independent commuting Pauli operators, but may still retain a large abelian Pauli subgroup. In this paper, we refer to such a state as a
t
-doped stabilizer state
when the remaining Pauli stabilizer subgroup has dimension
n-t
.

There are several reasons to study such states. In quantum information, Clifford-generated ensembles form a state
3
-design but not a state
4
-design
Kueng and Gross 2015
, so accessing fourth or higher moments requires some non-Clifford resource. This leads to circuits consisting of Clifford gates together with a small number of non-Clifford gates
Haferkamp et al. 2023
, such as
T
gates, and hence to
t
-doped stabilizer states. In many-body physics, Pauli symmetry subgroups appear in settings such as symmetry-protected topological phases
Zeng et al. 2015
, and related Pauli stabilizer structure can also emerge effectively in low-energy sectors even when it is not explicit at the Hamiltonian level
Brell et al. 2011
;
Kitaev 2006
.

As quantum devices continue to improve, they enable the preparation and study of increasingly complex quantum states that are difficult to analyze by direct classical means
Evered et al. 2025
;
Will et al. 2025
. In such situations, identifying the symmetries of the underlying state provides useful information.

In particular, for a typical quantum state, the expectation values of all non-identity Pauli operators are exponentially small with high probability
Huang et al. 2021
. Therefore, the presence of a Pauli operator with a large expectation value already constitutes significant information about the state. The challenge, however, is that there are
4^{n}-1
non-identity Pauli operators on
n
qubits, so determining such a symmetry by a brute-force search would require exponential samples and time.

To circumvent this exponential barrier, previous works introduced Bell difference sampling
Grewal et al. 2025
;
Hangleiter and Gullans 2024
;
Leone et al. 2024
;
Gross et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2025
, which allows one to extract information about the stabilizer groups using poly(
n
) samples and time resources. However, these approaches require entangled measurements across two copies of the state [Fig.
1
(a)]. Moreover, on hardware with limited qubit connectivity, implementing the Bell-measurement circuit may incur a depth overhead that grows with the system size.

To alleviate these hardware constraints, computational difference sampling was introduced in the single-copy setting
Grewal et al. 2025
. This removes the need for quantum memory, but requires the application of a random Clifford circuit before measurement, which has circuit depth
O(n)
in the absence of ancilla qubits
Maslov and Roetteler 2018
[Fig.
1
(b)]. This raises the question of whether one can learn large Pauli symmetry groups using only single-copy measurements while substantially reducing the required circuit depth, say,
O(\log n)
[Fig.
1
(c)].

Prior works crucially rely on the fact that the random Clifford group acts transitively on the set of non-identity Pauli operators
Chia et al. 2024
;
Grewal et al. 2025
. As a result, all such operators lie in a single orbit, and many averaging arguments reduce to analyzing a single representative together with uniform counting over its orbit.

In contrast, for shallow-depth circuits, locality imposes strong constraints on how Pauli operators can propagate. Consequently, the Pauli space no longer forms a single orbit but instead decomposes into multiple, geometry-dependent orbits. In this setting, the standard transitivity-based arguments break down.

To address this, we develop a framework that reduces the problem to cases determined by the relevant symplectic intersection patterns, and then analyzes each case via a refined counting argument. A key technical ingredient is an extension of the counting methods for Lagrangian subspaces
Kueng and Gross 2015
to general isotropic subspaces.

II
Preliminaries

Throughout this work, we ignore global phases of Pauli operators and identify unsigned

n
-qubit Pauli operators with binary vectors in
V:=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
.
For
a=(a_{x},a_{z})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\times\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, we define the corresponding Weyl operator by

W_{a}:=i^{a_{x}\cdot a_{z}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}X^{(a_{x})_{j}}Z^{(a_{z})_{j}}
, where
a_{x}\cdot a_{z}
in the phase factor is evaluated over the integers. Under this identification, multiplication of unsigned Pauli operators corresponds to addition in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
. For a state
\rho
, we define
\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho):=\{a\in V:\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{a})^{2}=1\}
, and define the
stabilizer dimension
of
\rho
to be
\dim\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
.

The vector space
V
is equipped with the canonical symplectic bilinear form. For
a=(a_{x},a_{z})
and
b=(b_{x},b_{z})
in
V
, we define
[a,b]:=a_{x}\cdot b_{z}+a_{z}\cdot b_{x}\pmod{2}
, where
\cdot
denotes the standard inner product over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. In particular,
W_{a}
and
W_{b}
commute if and only if
[a,b]=0
. A
S\subseteq V
is called
isotropic
if
[a,b]=0
for all
a,b\in S
. An isotropic subspace is called
Lagrangian
if it has maximal dimension, i.e., if
\dim(S)=n
. Let
\mathcal{I}_{n-t}(V)
denote the set of all isotropic subspaces of
V
of dimension
n-t
. For subspaces
A,B\subseteq V
, we define their sum by
A+B:=\{a+b:a\in A,\ b\in B\}
. We will also use the symplectic complement of a subspace
A^{\perp}:=\{x\in V:[x,a]=0\text{ for all }a\in A\}
.

We employ the block-Clifford ensemble
\mathcal{C}_{k}\coloneq\mathrm{Cl}(k)^{\otimes n/k}
, which has appeared previously in randomized measurements
Cho and Kim 2025a

and quantum state tomography
Cho and Kim 2025b
;
Brandão et al. 2020
. For a Clifford ensemble
\mathcal{C}
and a Pauli operator

, we define the Pauli collision coefficient
Bertoni et al. 2024
;
Cho and Kim 2025a
by

m_{P}\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{C\sim\mathcal{C}}[\mathbf{1}\{C(P)\in\mathcal{Z}\}],

(1)

where
C(P)=CPC^{\dagger}
,
\mathcal{Z}=\{I,Z\}^{n}=\{(0^{n},z):z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\}
, and
\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}
denotes the indicator function. For
\mathcal{C}_{k}
with
k=\Omega(\log n)
, we have [Appendix
D
]

\min_{P}m_{P}=\Theta(2^{-n}).

(2)

Since implementing a unitary in
\mathrm{Cl}(k)
requires depth
O(k)

Maslov and Roetteler 2018
, choosing
k=O(\log n)
allows us to realize
\mathcal{C}_{k}
using a circuit of depth
O(\log n)
.

III
Computational difference sampling

Given an
n
-qubit quantum state
\rho
, computational difference sampling
Grewal et al. 2025
consists of
drawing two independent computational-basis outcomes
x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
from
\rho

and returning their bitwise sum
x+y\pmod{2}
.
We denote the resulting distribution by
r_{\rho}
.

For our analysis, it is convenient to regard the outcome space as
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\{(x,0^{n}):x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
, rather than
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
itself.
Under this identification, for any
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
,

r_{\rho}(a,0^{n})=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\mathrm{tr}(\rho\ket{x}\bra{x})\,\mathrm{tr}(\rho\ket{x+a}\bra{x+a}).

(3)

Equivalently,
r_{\rho}(a,0^{n})
is the probability that two independent
computational-basis samples from
\rho
differ by
a
.
For a subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, we write
r_{\rho}(A)\coloneqq\sum_{x\in A}r_{\rho}(x)
.

The embedding of the outcome space into
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
is used only
to match the symplectic formalism adopted throughout the paper.
In the actual implementation, one may work directly over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, which yields a
more efficient classical post-processing.

Computational difference sampling itself is well defined for an arbitrary quantum state
\rho
.
However, in prior work, its analysis has typically relied on properties specific to pure states
Grewal et al. 2025
.
Here we extend the framework so that it also applies to mixed states.

Learning stabilizer group

We begin by recalling the definition of stabilizer-group learning, which was introduced by previous works

Definition 1

(

Let

This problem arises naturally as a subroutine for quantum state tomography (QST) on states with stabilizer dimension

Indeed, once such a subspace

Our first main result can be stated as follows. A detailed proof is given in the Appendix

V
Worst case

The algorithm based on our shallow-depth measurements does not work for all inputs
\rho
. A representative counterexample is the
n
-qubit GHZ state. Among the generators of its stabilizer group
S_{\mathrm{GHZ}}
,
n-1
have weight
2
, while the remaining one has weight
n

T_{0}=\langle Z_{1}Z_{2},\dots,Z_{n-1}Z_{n}\rangle=\{(0^{n},z):\sum_{i}z_{i}\equiv 0\pmod{2}\}

S_{\mathrm{GHZ}}\setminus T_{0}
has weight
n
, making this an especially hard instance for our approach. In this case, for
C\sim\mathcal{C}_{k}

\Pr_{C}\!\left(C(S_{\mathrm{GHZ}}\setminus T_{0})\cap\mathcal{Z}\neq\emptyset\right)\leq(2/3)^{n/k}.

(7)

A proof is given in the Appendix
F
. Therefore, for the Clifford ensemble
\mathcal{C}_{k}
,

must scale at least linearly with
n
. Nevertheless, this obstruction can be bypassed by employing a single round of adaptivity. Indeed, one can first learn an
(n-1)
-dimensional subgroup

without much difficulty. Since
|T_{0}^{\perp}/T_{0}|=4
, it then suffices to identify which of the four candidates is correct. This avoids the need to draw exponentially many unitaries. Moreover, this adaptive step still uses only constant-depth circuits, so the overall depth remains
O(\log n)
.

VII
Lower bound

Known single-copy algorithms for learning an
(n-t)
-dimensional stabilizer group
require a number of samples that scales exponentially in
t

Grewal et al. 2025
;
Chia et al. 2024
. It is therefore natural
to ask whether this dependence is merely an artifact of existing techniques, or whether
it is in fact inherent to the single-copy setting. The following theorem shows that the
latter is the case: in the worst case, any algorithm based on single-copy measurements
must use at least
\Omega(2^{t})
samples. Consequently, when
t=\omega(\log n)
, both
the sample complexity and the overall runtime become super-polynomial.

Theorem 2

(Single-copy lower bound on
t
-dependence)
.

Any algorithm that uses potentially adaptive single-copy measurements for

(\epsilon,\delta,t)
-stabilizer group learning must, in the worst case, use at least

\Omega(2^{t})
samples.

A detailed proof is given in the Appendix
H
, where we reduce our stabilizer group learning problem to a previously studied single-copy Pauli testing problem and invoke the known lower bound
Huang et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2024
.

Combining this hardness result for classical learners with the sample-efficient Bell difference sampling protocol for quantum learners
Grewal et al. 2025
;
Hangleiter and Gullans 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025
;
Leone et al. 2024
;
Gross et al. 2021
yields a quantum advantage for learning Pauli symmetries of quantum systems. This separation is notable for several reasons. First, once a candidate symmetry group is found, it can be verified efficiently. Second, the quantum algorithm is not only sample-efficient but also computationally efficient, in contrast to previously known examples where the advantage appears only at the level of sample complexity
Huang et al. 2021
. Finally, identifying symmetries of quantum states is a natural problem in many-body physics, giving this learning problem direct physical motivation and potential applications.

VIII
Discussion and Outlook

We studied the problem of learning stabilizer groups using single-copy measurements. On the positive side, we showed that shallow-depth measurements suffice to efficiently learn almost all stabilizer groups, using a refined counting argument that extends from Lagrangian to general isotropic subspaces in symplectic vector spaces. On the negative side, we established a limitation of arbitrary single-copy measurements by showing that, in the worst case, learning can require exponentially more samples than in the two-copy setting
Grewal et al. 2025
;
Hangleiter and Gullans 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025
when
t
is large.

A natural direction for future work is to understand whether, by allowing adaptivity, one can learn stabilizer groups using only shallow-depth measurements even in the worst case. It is also interesting to study what can be proved when the measurements are further restricted. As we observed in numerical experiments and previous work
Arunachalam et al. 2022
, even single-qubit measurements are already very effective.
Another interesting direction is to consider variants of the block-Clifford ensemble used in this work. If the block-Clifford ensemble is arranged in a brickwork pattern, one obtains a two-layer block-Clifford ensemble, which can be used to construct approximate unitary designs
Schuster et al. 2025
. It would be interesting to understand whether the two-layer block-Clifford ensemble is sufficient in the worst case.

Appendix A
Related work

Multi-copy and single-copy stabilizer learning.

Previous work on stabilizer learning has explored both multi-copy
Grewal et al. 2025
;
Leone et al. 2024
;
Hangleiter and Gullans 2024
;
Montanaro 2017
and single-copy
measurement settings
Grewal et al. 2025
;
Chia et al. 2024
. Multi-copy methods are often more efficient, but typically rely
on entangled measurements across different copies of the state and may require coherent
quantum memory. Single-copy approaches avoid this requirement and are therefore more
appealing from an experimental point of view, but existing methods usually rely on
random Clifford measurements implemented by circuits of depth
O(n)
. In contrast, our
result shows that, for almost all stabilizer groups
\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
of dimension

n-t
with
t=O(\log n)
, one can learn the hidden Pauli symmetries without coherent
quantum memory and without using linear-depth measurement circuits.

State hidden subgroup problem.

Our learning problem can be viewed as a special case of the recently introduced state
hidden subgroup problem
Hinsche et al. 2025
;
Bouland et al. 2025
, which asks for the hidden symmetry subgroup of an unknown
quantum state. From this perspective, learning stabilizer groups is one representative
example, alongside other tasks such as identifying cuts across which the state is
unentangled and learning hidden translation symmetries. The common theme is that one
aims to recover a symmetry of the state rather than a complete classical description
of it. The standard algorithmic approach to this framework is based on Fourier sampling
Bouland et al. 2025
.
However, implementing it in full generality typically relies on generalized phase
estimation, which requires auxiliary registers and controlled group actions and can
therefore be demanding in practice. More recent work
Hinsche et al. 2025
has shown that, in several
important cases, the same structure can be realized through simpler measurement primitives, including two-copy routines such as Bell sampling and Bell difference sampling.

Stabilizer testing.

A related line of work considers stabilizer testing
Hinsche and Helsen 2025
;
Gross et al. 2021
rather than stabilizer learning. Here the
task is only to determine whether an unknown state is a stabilizer state or is
\epsilon
-far from the
stabilizer family, so the problem is generally easier than full learning. Nevertheless, as in
learning, the measurement model plays a decisive role. Two-copy protocols based on Bell
difference sampling suffice with
O(1)
copies
Gross et al. 2021
, while single-copy protocols are inherently
more costly. Recent work
Hinsche and Helsen 2025
showed that single-copy stabilizer testing can be carried out
using computational difference sampling with
O(n)
copies, and also proved a lower bound
of
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
copies for any single-copy algorithm. This again highlights the gap
between single-copy and multi-copy access.

Randomized measurements and quantum state tomography.

The block-Clifford ensemble
\mathcal{C}_{k}=\mathrm{Cl}(k)^{\otimes n/k}
used in our work
has also appeared in recent studies on randomized measurements
Cho and Kim 2025a
and quantum state tomography
Cho and Kim 2025b
.
In the randomized-measurement setting, such ensembles provide a hardware-efficient way to
estimate a variety of quantities, ranging from linear observables such as
\mathrm{tr}(O\rho)
to
nonlinear ones such as the purity
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{2})
. In this line of work, the choice of unitary ensemble affects not only the sample complexity,
but also the cost of the classical post-processing. While earlier approaches
Bertoni et al. 2024
;
Schuster et al. 2025
;
Hu et al. 2025
often
prioritized sample efficiency, they could require more delicate or approximate post-processing. Recent work
Cho and Kim 2025a
showed that the block-Clifford ensemble

\mathcal{C}_{k}=\mathrm{Cl}(k)^{\otimes n/k}
admits efficient classical post-processing while maintaining optimal sample complexity guarantees in many cases. This efficient post-processing also makes it possible to extend hybrid quantum-classical learning schemes, previously developed for single-qubit measurements
Huang et al. 2022
, to the setting of shallow-depth randomized measurements.

The same Clifford ensemble
\mathcal{C}_{k}
has also been used for quantum state tomography
Cho and Kim 2025b
. Previous optimal single-copy tomography protocols typically relied on linear-depth Clifford measurements
Lowe and Nayak 2025
. By contrast, recent work showed that
\mathcal{C}_{k}
with
k=O(\log n)
already suffices to achieve optimal sample complexity in the full-rank case, thereby reducing the required measurement depth to
O(\log n)
without sacrificing performance guarantees.

Appendix B
Weyl representation and symplectic geometry

In the main text, we ignore global phases of Pauli operators and identify unsigned

n
-qubit Pauli operators with binary vectors in

V:=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\times\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}.

For
a=(a_{x},a_{z})\in V
, we define the corresponding
Weyl operator by

W_{a}:=i^{a_{x}\cdot a_{z}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}X^{(a_{x})_{j}}Z^{(a_{z})_{j}},

(8)

where the exponent
a_{x}\cdot a_{z}
in the phase factor is evaluated over the integers.
Modulo global phase, every
n
-qubit Pauli operator is represented uniquely by a vector

a\in V
. Thus, multiplication of unsigned Pauli operators corresponds to addition in

\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
.

The vector space
V
is equipped with the canonical symplectic bilinear form

[a,b]:=a_{x}\cdot b_{z}+a_{z}\cdot b_{x}\pmod{2},\qquad a=(a_{x},a_{z}),\;b=(b_{x},b_{z})\in V,

(9)

where
\cdot
denotes the standard inner product on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.
This form encodes the commutation relations of Weyl operators via

W_{a}W_{b}=(-1)^{[a,b]}W_{b}W_{a}.

(10)

In particular,
W_{a}
and
W_{b}
commute if and only if
[a,b]=0
.
Thus, commutation among Pauli operators is naturally captured by the symplectic geometry of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
.

For
A\subseteq V
, we define its symplectic complement by

A^{\perp}:=\{x\in V:[x,a]=0\text{ for all }a\in A\}.

(11)

A subset
A\subseteq V
is called
isotropic
if
[x,y]=0
for all
x,y\in A
, that is, if all Weyl operators
\{W_{x}:x\in A\}
commute pairwise.
An isotropic subspace is called
Lagrangian
if it has maximal possible dimension, namely
\dim(A)=n
.

We will use the following standard facts for subspaces
A,B\subseteq V
:

•

(A^{\perp})^{\perp}=A
.

•

\dim(A)+\dim(A^{\perp})=2n
; equivalently,
|A|\,|A^{\perp}|=4^{n}
.

•

A\subseteq B
if and only if
B^{\perp}\subseteq A^{\perp}
.

•

(A+B)^{\perp}=A^{\perp}\cap B^{\perp}
.

Moreover, if
A
is isotropic, then
A\subseteq A^{\perp}
, and therefore
\dim(A)\leq n
.

Fact 2
.

Let
A,B\subset V
be subspaces. Then

(A+B)^{\perp}=A^{\perp}\cap B^{\perp}.

(12)

Fact 3
.

Let
A,B,C\subset V
be subspaces such that
C\subseteq A
. Then

A\cap(B+C)=(A\cap B)+C.

(13)

We denote by
\mathrm{Sp}(V)
the symplectic group of
V
, namely

\mathrm{Sp}(V):=\{\phi\in\mathrm{GL}(V):[\phi(x),\phi(y)]=[x,y]\text{ for all }x,y\in V\},

(14)

where
\mathrm{GL}(V)
is the group of invertible linear maps on
V
.

For two
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-vector spaces
A
and
B
, we denote by

\mathrm{Hom}(A,B)

(15)

the space of all
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear maps from
A
to
B
.
In particular, if
\dim A=a
and
\dim B=b
, then
\mathrm{Hom}(A,B)
is an

\mathbb{F}_{2}
-vector space of dimension
ab
, and hence

|\mathrm{Hom}(A,B)|=2^{ab}.

(16)

In particular, a subspace
L\subset V
is Lagrangian when it is isotropic and has dimension
n
.
Equivalently, if
L\subseteq V
is Lagrangian, then
L=L^{\perp}
.

We will frequently use the coordinate subspaces

\mathcal{X}:=\{(x,0^{n}):x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\},\qquad\mathcal{Z}:=\{(0^{n},z):z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\},

(17)

both of which are Lagrangian subspaces of
V
.

We will also use the following standard character-sum identities.
For a subspace
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, define its orthogonal complement with respect
to the standard inner product by

A^{\perp_{s}}:=\{b\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}:a\cdot b=0\text{ for all }a\in A\}.

(18)

Then, for every
b\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
,

\sum_{a\in A}(-1)^{a\cdot b}=|A|\,\mathbf{1}\{b\in A^{\perp_{s}}\}.

(19)

Similarly, for a subspace
A\subseteq V=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
and every
b\in V
,

\sum_{a\in A}(-1)^{[a,b]}=|A|\,\mathbf{1}\{b\in A^{\perp}\}.

(20)

For proofs in the following sections, we repeatedly use the quotient map

\pi:M^{\perp}\to M^{\perp}/M,\qquad\pi(x)=x+M,

(21)

where
M\subset V
is isotropic. For any Lagrangian subspace
L\subset V
, the image

\pi(L\cap M^{\perp})=(L\cap M^{\perp}+M)/M

(22)

is a Lagrangian subspace of
M^{\perp}/M
, since it is isotropic and has dimension
n-\dim M
.

The Weyl operators form an orthogonal basis of the operator space on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}

with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product. More explicitly,

\mathrm{tr}(W_{a}W_{b})=2^{n}\delta_{a,b}.

(23)

Therefore any
n
-qubit state
\rho
admits the Weyl expansion

\rho=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{a\in V}\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{a})\,W_{a}.

(24)

This expansion shows that the coefficients
\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{a})
provide a complete description
of the state in the Weyl basis.

Finally, following the notation in the main text, we define

\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho):=\{a\in V:\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{a})^{2}=1\}.

(25)

Thus,
\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
records the unsigned Pauli symmetries of
\rho
.
Because
[a,b]=0
is equivalent to commutation of
W_{a}
and
W_{b}
, the set

\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
is naturally viewed as an isotropic subspace of the
vector space
V
.

We will also use the Gaussian binomial coefficient. For integers
0\leq r\leq m
and a prime power
q
, define

\binom{m}{r}_{q}:=\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\frac{q^{m-i}-1}{q^{r-i}-1}.

(26)

Equivalently,
\binom{m}{r}_{q}
is the number of
r
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
.
In particular, when
q=2
,
\binom{m}{r}_{2}
counts the number of
r
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
. We will use the following standard properties of the Gaussian binomial coefficients:

\displaystyle\quad 1.\quad\binom{m}{r}_{2}=\binom{m}{m-r}_{2},

(27)

\displaystyle\quad 2.\quad\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}(1+2^{i}t)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}2^{\binom{i}{2}}\binom{m}{i}_{2}\,t^{i}.

(28)

Fact 4
.

The number of Lagrangian subspaces of
V=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
is
\prod_{i=1}^{n}(2^{i}+1)
.

Lemma 3

(The number of isotropic subspaces)
.

Let
m\in[n]
, and let
\mathcal{I}_{m}(V)
denote the set of isotropic subspaces of

V=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
of dimension
m
.
Then

|\mathcal{I}_{m}(V)|=\binom{n}{m}_{2}\prod_{i=n-m+1}^{n}(2^{i}+1).

(29)

Proof.

We count ordered bases
(v_{1},\dots,v_{m})
of a
m
-dimensional isotropic subspace. The first vector
v_{1}
can be any nonzero vector of
V
, so there are
2^{2n}-1
choices.
Suppose inductively that
v_{1},\dots,v_{i}
have already been chosen and span an isotropic subspace

U_{i}
of dimension
i
. Since
U_{i}
is isotropic, we have
U_{i}\subseteq U_{i}^{\perp}
, and
\dim(U_{i}^{\perp})=2n-i
. To keep the span isotropic, the next vector
v_{i+1}
must lie in
U_{i}^{\perp}\setminus U_{i}
.
Hence, the number of choices for
v_{i+1}
is
|U_{i}^{\perp}\setminus U_{i}|=2^{2n-i}-2^{i}
. Therefore, the total number of ordered linearly independent
m
-tuples spanning an isotropic subspace is

\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}(2^{2n-i}-2^{i}).

(30)

Each
m
-dimensional isotropic subspace has

\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}(2^{m}-2^{i})

(31)

ordered bases. Dividing, we obtain

\displaystyle|\mathcal{I}_{m}(V)|

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}(2^{2n-i}-2^{i})}{\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}(2^{m}-2^{i})}

(32)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{2^{2n-2i}-1}{2^{m-i}-1}

(33)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{(2^{n-i}-1)(2^{n-i}+1)}{2^{m-i}-1}

(34)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{2^{n-i}-1}{2^{m-i}-1}\right)\left(\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}(2^{n-i}+1)\right)

(35)

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{m}_{2}\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}(2^{n-i}+1)

(36)

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{m}_{2}\prod_{i=n-m+1}^{n}(2^{i}+1).

(37)

For
m=n
, this recovers Fact
4
.
∎

Appendix C
Computational difference sampling

Computational difference sampling
Grewal et al. 2025
uses two independent copies of
\rho
. Measuring each copy in the computational basis gives outcomes
b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, and the procedure outputs their bitwise mod-
2
sum
b_{1}+b_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. In the main text, we denote the resulting distribution by
r_{\rho}
. Unlike Bell difference sampling, the two measurements are performed independently, so this procedure can be implemented in the single-copy setting and requires no quantum memory or ancillary qubits.

The natural domain of
r_{\rho}
is
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. However, to match the Weyl representation in the sympectic geometry, it is convenient to regard
r_{\rho}
as a distribution on
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\{(x,0^{n}):x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
. Then, for
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
,

r_{\rho}(a,0^{n})=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\mathrm{tr}\bigl(\rho\ket{x}\bra{x}\bigr)\,\mathrm{tr}\bigl(\rho\ket{x+a}\bra{x+a}\bigr).

(38)

The next lemma rewrites
r_{\rho}
in terms of Pauli expectation values.

Lemma 4
.

For every
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
,

r_{\rho}(a,0^{n})=2^{-n}\sum_{b\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{a\cdot b}\,\mathrm{tr}(\rho Z^{b})^{2}.

(39)

Proof.

Using
X^{a}\ket{x}=\ket{x+a}
, we have

\displaystyle r_{\rho}(a,0^{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\mathrm{tr}(\rho\ket{x}\bra{x})\,\mathrm{tr}(\rho X^{a}\ket{x}\bra{x}X^{a})

(40)

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{b\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\mathrm{tr}(\rho Z^{b})\,\mathrm{tr}(\rho X^{a}Z^{b}X^{a})

(41)

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{b\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{a\cdot b}\,\mathrm{tr}(\rho Z^{b})^{2},

(42)

where
X^{a}=\bigotimes_{i}X^{a_{i}}
,
Z^{b}=\bigotimes_{i}Z^{b_{i}}
, and
a\cdot b=\sum_{i}a_{i}b_{i}
. In the second line, we used

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\ket{x}\bra{x}\otimes\ket{x}\bra{x}=2^{-n}\sum_{b\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}Z^{b}\otimes Z^{b}.

∎

For
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, write
r_{\rho}(A)\coloneqq\sum_{x\in A}r_{\rho}(x)
. We next compute the mass of an
X
-type subspace under
r_{\rho}
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
H=H_{X}\times 0^{n}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, where
H_{X}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is a subspace. Then

r_{\rho}(H)=\frac{|H+\mathcal{Z}|}{2^{n}}\,p_{\rho}((H+\mathcal{Z})^{\perp}),

(43)

where
p_{\rho}(S)\coloneqq\sum_{x\in S}p_{\rho}(x)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4
,

\displaystyle r_{\rho}(H)

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{b\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\sum_{a\in H_{X}}(-1)^{a\cdot b}\,\mathrm{tr}(\rho Z^{b})^{2}

(44)

\displaystyle=2^{-n}|H_{X}|\sum_{b\in H_{X}^{\perp_{s}}}\mathrm{tr}(\rho Z^{b})^{2}

(45)

\displaystyle=|H_{X}|\,p_{\rho}(0^{n}\times H_{X}^{\perp_{s}})

(46)

\displaystyle=\frac{|H+\mathcal{Z}|}{2^{n}}\,p_{\rho}((H+\mathcal{Z})^{\perp}),

(47)

where
H_{X}^{\perp_{s}}\coloneqq\{b\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}:a\cdot b=0\text{ for all }a\in H_{X}\}
. Since
H+\mathcal{Z}=H_{X}\times\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, we have
|H+\mathcal{Z}|=2^{n}|H_{X}|
, and since
(H+\mathcal{Z})^{\perp}=0^{n}\times H_{X}^{\perp_{s}}
, the last equality follows.
∎

The next lemma identifies the orthogonal complement that will appear in the support statement.

Lemma 6
.

Let
H\coloneqq(\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)\cap\mathcal{Z})^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{X}
. Then

(H+\mathcal{Z})^{\perp}=\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)\cap\mathcal{Z}.

(48)

Proof.

Using
(A+B)^{\perp}=A^{\perp}\cap B^{\perp}
, we get

\displaystyle(H+\mathcal{Z})^{\perp}

\displaystyle=H^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{Z}

(49)

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)\cap\mathcal{Z}+\mathcal{X})\cap\mathcal{Z}

(50)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)\cap\mathcal{Z},

(51)

where we used
\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{Z}=\{0\}
in the last step.
∎

Corollary 2

(Support of computational difference sampling)
.

If
r_{\rho}(x)>0
, then

x\in(\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)\cap\mathcal{Z})^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{X}.

(52)

Proof.

Let
H\coloneqq(\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)\cap\mathcal{Z})^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{X}
. By Lemma
5
,

r_{\rho}(H)=\frac{|H+\mathcal{Z}|}{2^{n}}\,p_{\rho}((H+\mathcal{Z})^{\perp}).

By Lemma
6
,
(H+\mathcal{Z})^{\perp}=\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)\cap\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
. Hence

r_{\rho}(H)=\frac{|H+\mathcal{Z}|\cdot|(H+\mathcal{Z})^{\perp}|}{4^{n}}=1.

Therefore, the support of
r_{\rho}
is contained in
H
.
∎

Appendix D
Clifford ensembles

In the main text, instead of the full random Clifford ensemble
\mathrm{Cl}(n)
, we consider the block-Clifford ensemble

\mathcal{C}_{k}\coloneqq\mathrm{Cl}(k)^{\otimes n/k},

(53)

where we assume throughout the paper that
k
divides
n
. Here, each block has size
k
, and the Clifford circuit acting on each block is sampled independently from
\mathrm{Cl}(k)
. The ensemble
\mathcal{C}_{k}
is useful for randomized measurements and quantum state tomography because of its tensor-product structure and because it forms a group.

For a given unitary ensemble
\mathcal{C}
, an important quantity in our analysis is the Pauli collision probability

m_{P}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{C\sim\mathcal{C}}\left[\mathbf{1}\{C(P)\in\mathcal{Z}\}\right],

(54)

where
\mathcal{Z}=\{I,Z\}^{n}=\{(0^{n},z):z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\}
. For
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, this quantity is given by

\displaystyle m_{P}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{C\sim\mathcal{C}_{k}}\left[\mathbf{1}\{C(P)\in\mathcal{Z}\}\right]

(55)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n/k}\mathbb{E}_{C_{i}\sim\mathrm{Cl}(k)}\left[\mathbf{1}\{C_{i}(P_{i})\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}\}\right]

(56)

\displaystyle=(2^{k}+1)^{-w_{k}(P)},

(57)

where
C=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n/k}C_{i}
,
P=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n/k}P_{i}
, and
\mathcal{Z}_{k}=\{I,Z\}^{k}=\{(0^{k},z):\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\}
. Here,

w_{k}(P)\coloneqq\left|\{i\in[n/k]:P_{i}\neq I_{k}\}\right|

(58)

denotes the number of blocks on which
P
acts nontrivially, and
I_{k}
is the
2^{k}\times 2^{k}
identity matrix.

Since
w_{k}(P)\leq n/k
for all
P
, we obtain

\min_{P}m_{P}=(2^{k}+1)^{-n/k}=2^{-n}(1+2^{-k})^{-n/k},

(59)

where equality is attained when
P
is nontrivial on every block. Now let
f(x)=(1+x^{-1})^{x}
for
x>0
. Since
f(x)
is monotone increasing and
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=e
, it follows that

e^{-n/(2^{k}k)}2^{-n}<\min_{P}m_{P}<2^{-n}.

(60)

Therefore, if the block size
k
is chosen so that
2^{k}k=\Omega(n)
, then
e^{-n/(2^{k}k)}=\Omega(1)
. In particular, when
k=\Omega(\log n)
, we have

\min_{P}m_{P}=\Theta(2^{-n}).

(61)

Since implementing a unitary in
\mathrm{Cl}(k)
requires depth
O(k)

Maslov and Roetteler 2018
, choosing
k=O(\log n)
allows us to realize
\mathcal{C}_{k}
with circuit depth
O(\log n)
.

Appendix E
Performance guarantees of the learning algorithm

In this section, we give a detailed proof of Theorem
1
from the main text. We first recall the targeted learning problem.

Definition 2

(
(\epsilon,\delta,t)
-stabilizer group learning)
.

Let
\rho
be an
n
-qubit state with stabilizer dimension
n-t
. An algorithm outputs an isotropic subspace
\widehat{S}
such that, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

1.

\widehat{S}\supseteq\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
,

2.

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{S}}\!\left[\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{x})^{2}\right]\geq 1-\epsilon
.

We analyze Algorithm
1
, which learns
\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
using only single-copy shallow-depth measurements. The proof has two parts: first, we show that random Clifford circuits from
\mathcal{C}_{k}
reveal enough information to recover
\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
; second, we bound the number of computational difference samples needed to recover the hidden Pauli symmetries in each selected basis.

Before proving the theorem, we first collect several results about averages over uniformly random isotropic subspaces
S\sim\mathcal{I}_{n-t}(V)
that will be used below.

Fact 5

(Theorem 4 in
Kueng and Gross 2015
)
.

Let
L_{0}\subset V\coloneq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
be a fixed Lagrangian subspace. Then, for each
0\leq l\leq n
, the number of Lagrangian subspaces
L\subset V
such that
\dim(L_{0}\cap L)=l
is

\left|\left\{L\subset V:\dim(L\cap L_{0})=l\right\}\right|=\binom{n}{l}_{2}\,2^{(n-l)(n-l+1)/2}.

(62)

In particular, the total number of Lagrangian subspaces of
V
is

\prod_{i=1}^{n}(2^{i}+1).

(63)

Definition 3
.

Let
L_{0}\subset V\coloneq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
be a fixed Lagrangian subspace, and let

L
be sampled uniformly from the set of all Lagrangian subspaces
\mathcal{I}_{n}(V)
.
We define the generating function by

\displaystyle G_{n}(s)

\displaystyle\coloneq\sum_{l=0}^{n}\Pr_{L}\bigl(\dim(L\cap L_{0})=l\bigr)s^{l}

(64)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{n}\frac{\binom{n}{l}_{2}\,2^{(n-l)(n-l+1)/2}}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(2^{i}+1)}\,s^{l}

(65)

\displaystyle=\frac{s^{n}}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(2^{i}+1)}\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\left(1+\frac{2^{i+1}}{s}\right),

(66)

where the second equality uses Fact
5
, and the last equality follows from Eq. (
28
).
In particular, we will use the special values

\displaystyle G_{n}(2)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n+1}}{2^{n}+1},

(67)

\displaystyle G_{n}(4)

\displaystyle=\frac{6\cdot 4^{n}}{(2^{n}+1)(2^{n}+2)}.

(68)

Lemma 7
.

Let
L\subset V\coloneq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
be a fixed Lagrangian subspace. Then, for every

0\leq m\leq n
,

\bigl|\{S\in\mathcal{I}_{m}(V):\dim(S\cap L)=0\}\bigr|=\binom{n}{m}_{2}\,2^{m(m+1)/2}\,2^{m(n-m)}.

(69)

Proof.

Fix a Lagrangian complement
M
of
L
, so that

V=L\oplus M.

(70)

Write each
x\in V
uniquely as
x=x_{L}+x_{M}
with
x_{L}\in L
and
x_{M}\in M
. Let
S\in\mathcal{I}_{m}(V)
satisfy
S\cap L=\{0\}
and let
\pi_{M}:V\to M,\pi(x)=x_{M}
. Then
\pi_{M}|_{S}:S\to M
is injective,
since
\ker(\pi_{M}|_{S})=S\cap L=\{0\}
. Thus

A\coloneq\pi_{M}(S)

(71)

is an
m
-dimensional subspace of
M
. Moreover,
\pi_{M}|_{S}:S\to A
is an isomorphism, so
there is a unique linear map
\phi:A\to L
such that

S=\Gamma(\phi)\coloneq\{a+\phi(a):a\in A\}.

(72)

Conversely, every pair
(A,\phi)
with
A\subseteq M
,
\dim A=m
, and

\phi\in\mathrm{Hom}(A,L)
gives such a subspace. Hence it suffices to count those

\phi
for which
\Gamma(\phi)
is isotropic.

For
a,b\in A
, since
A\subset M
and
\phi(A)\subset L
, and both
L
and
M
are
isotropic, we have

[a+\phi(a),\,b+\phi(b)]=[a,\phi(b)]+[\phi(a),b].

(73)

Thus
\Gamma(\phi)
is isotropic if and only if

[a,\phi(b)]=[b,\phi(a)]\qquad\text{for all }a,b\in A.

(74)

Now
A^{\perp}\cap L
is a subspace of
L
of dimension
n-m
. Choose a complementary
subspace
B\subset L
such that

L=B\oplus(A^{\perp}\cap L),

(75)

so
\dim B=m
. Then every
\phi\in\mathrm{Hom}(A,L)
decomposes uniquely as

\phi=\phi_{B}+\phi_{\mathrm{free}},

(76)

where
\phi_{B}\in\mathrm{Hom}(A,B)
and

\phi_{\mathrm{free}}\in\mathrm{Hom}(A,A^{\perp}\cap L)
. Since

[A,A^{\perp}\cap L]=0,

(77)

the map
\phi_{\mathrm{free}}
does not affect the isotropy condition, so only
\phi_{B}
matters.

Fix bases of
A
and
B
. Then
\phi_{B}
is represented by an
m\times m
matrix, and
the condition

[a,\phi_{B}(b)]=[b,\phi_{B}(a)]

(78)

means exactly that this matrix is symmetric. Hence the number of possible
\phi_{B}
is the number of symmetric

m\times m
matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, namely

2^{m(m+1)/2}.

(79)

Also,
\phi_{\mathrm{free}}
can be chosen arbitrarily in

\mathrm{Hom}(A,A^{\perp}\cap L)
. Therefore, the number of possible
choices for
\phi_{\mathrm{free}}
is

|\mathrm{Hom}(A,A^{\perp}\cap L)|=2^{m(n-m)}.

(80)

Therefore, for each fixed
A\subseteq M
with
\dim A=m
, the number of isotropic subspaces

S
with
\pi_{M}(S)=A
and
S\cap L=\{0\}
is

2^{m(m+1)/2}2^{m(n-m)}.

(81)

Finally, the number of
m
-dimensional subspaces
A\subseteq M
is
\binom{n}{m}_{2}
, so

\bigl|\{S\in\mathcal{I}_{m}(V):\dim(S\cap L)=0\}\bigr|=\binom{n}{m}_{2}\,2^{m(m+1)/2}\,2^{m(n-m)},

(82)

as desired.
∎

Extending the Lagrangian counting result in Fact
5
to isotropic subspaces
S\in\mathcal{I}_{m}(V)
, we obtain the following.

Lemma 8
.

Let
L\subset V\coloneq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
be a fixed Lagrangian subspace. Then, for every

0\leq l\leq m\leq n
,

\bigl|\{S\in\mathcal{I}_{m}(V):\dim(S\cap L)=l\}\bigr|=\binom{n}{l}_{2}\binom{n-l}{m-l}_{2}\,2^{(m-l)(m-l+1)/2}\,2^{(m-l)(n-m)}.

(83)

Proof.

Fix
T=S\cap L
with
\dim(T)=l
. There are
\binom{n}{l}_{2}
such subspaces
T\subset L
. For each fixed such
T
, we have

S\cap L=T\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad S_{T}\cap L_{T}=\{0\},

(84)

where
S_{T}=S/T
and
L_{T}=L/T
. Therefore, for fixed
T
,

|\{S\in\mathcal{I}_{m}(V):S\cap L=T,\ \dim(T)=l\}|=|\{S_{T}\in\mathcal{I}_{m-l}(W):S_{T}\cap L_{T}=\{0\}\}|,

(85)

where
W=T^{\perp}/T
and
\dim(W)=2(n-l)
. Applying Lemma
7
with

m\leftarrow m-l
and
n\leftarrow n-l
completes the proof.
∎

Using Lemma
8
, we can compute higher moments of the intersection size with a fixed Lagrangian
L
for a uniformly random
S\in\mathcal{I}_{m}(V)
, as stated below.

Lemma 9
.

For a fixed Lagrangian
L\subset V\coloneq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
and
c\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbb{E}_{S\in\mathcal{I}_{n-t}(V)}[|S\cap L|^{c}]=\prod_{i=1}^{c}\frac{2^{-c+i}+2^{-t}}{2^{-c+i}+2^{-n}}.

(86)

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S\in\mathcal{I}_{n-t}(V)}[|S\cap L|^{c}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{l}^{n-t}\Pr(\mathrm{dim}(S \cap L)=l)\,2^{cl}

(87)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{t}_{2}\prod_{i=t+1}^{n}(2^{i}+1)}\sum_{l=0}^{n-t}\binom{n}{l}_{2}\binom{n-l}{n-t-l}_{2}2^{(n-t-l)(n-t-l+1)/2}2^{(n-t-l)t}2^{cl}

(88)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\prod_{i=t+1}^{n}(2^{i}+1)}\sum_{l=0}^{n-t}\binom{n-t}{l}_{2}2^{(n-t-l)(n-t-l+1)/2}2^{(n-t-l)t}2^{cl}

(89)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\prod_{i=t+1}^{n}(2^{i}+1)}\sum_{l=0}^{n-t}\binom{n-t}{l}_{2}2^{l(l+1)/2}2^{tl}2^{c(n-t-l)}

(90)

\displaystyle=2^{c(n-t)}\frac{\prod_{i=0}^{n-t-1}(2^{i+t+1-c}+1)}{\prod_{i=t+1}^{n}(2^{i}+1)}

(91)

\displaystyle=2^{c(n-t)}\frac{\prod_{i=t-c+1}^{t}(2^{i}+1)}{\prod_{i=n-c+1}^{n}(2^{i}+1)}

(92)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{c}\frac{2^{-c+i}+2^{-t}}{2^{-c+i}+2^{-n}}

(93)

as desired. The second line follows from Lemma
3
and Lemma
8
, the third from
Eq. (
27
), the fourth by the change of variables

l\leftarrow n-t-l
, and the fifth from Eq. (
28
).
∎

These higher-moment estimates will be used to control tail bounds for averages over
S\in\mathcal{I}_{n-t}(V)
. In particular, for
c=O(1)
, the corresponding higher moments remain
O(1)
.

Corollary 3
.

\mathbb{E}_{S\in\mathcal{I}_{n-t}(V)}|S\cap L|^{2}=1+\frac{3}{2^{t}}+\frac{2}{2^{2t}}-O(2^{-n}).

(94)

Proof.

Substituting
c=2
into Lemma
9
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S\in\mathcal{I}_{n-t}(V)}|S\cap L|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2^{-1}+2^{-t})(1+2^{-t})}{(2^{-1}+2^{-n})(1+2^{-n})}

(95)

\displaystyle=\frac{(2^{-1}+2^{-t})(1+2^{-t})}{2^{-1}}\bigl(1-O(2^{-n})\bigr)

(96)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{3}{2^{t}}+\frac{2}{2^{2t}}-O(2^{-n}),

(97)

as claimed.

Lemma 10
.

Let
L_{1},L_{2},L_{1}^{\prime},L_{2}^{\prime}\subset V\coloneq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
be Lagrangian subspaces such that

\dim(L_{1}\cap L_{2})=\dim(L_{1}^{\prime}\cap L_{2}^{\prime}).

(98)

Then, for uniformly sampled isotropic subspace
S\in\mathcal{I}_{m}(V)
,

\mathbb{E}_{S}\,\bigl[|S\cap L_{1}|^{2}|S\cap L_{2}|^{2}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{S}\,\bigl[|S\cap L_{1}^{\prime}|^{2}|S\cap L_{2}^{\prime}|^{2}\bigr].

(99)

Proof.

Let

\ell:=\dim(L_{1}\cap L_{2})=\dim(L_{1}^{\prime}\cap L_{2}^{\prime}).

(100)

Choose symplectic bases

\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n},f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\{e_{1}^{\prime},\dots,e_{n}^{\prime},f_{1}^{\prime},\dots,f_{n}^{\prime}\}

(101)

of
V
such that

\displaystyle L_{1}

\displaystyle=\langle e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\rangle,

(102)

\displaystyle L_{2}

\displaystyle=\langle e_{1},\dots,e_{\ell},f_{\ell+1},\dots,f_{n}\rangle,

(103)

\displaystyle L_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\langle e_{1}^{\prime},\dots,e_{n}^{\prime}\rangle,

(104)

\displaystyle L_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\langle e_{1}^{\prime},\dots,e_{\ell}^{\prime},f_{\ell+1}^{\prime},\dots,f_{n}^{\prime}\rangle.

(105)

These bases satisfy

[e_{i},e_{j}]=[f_{i},f_{j}]=0,[e_{i},f_{j}]=\delta_{ij},

(106)

and similarly,

[e_{i}^{\prime},e_{j}^{\prime}]=[f_{i}^{\prime},f_{j}^{\prime}]=0,[e_{i}^{\prime},f_{j}^{\prime}]=\delta_{ij},

(107)

for all
i,j\in[n]
.

Define a linear map
\phi:V\to V
by

\phi(e_{i})=e_{i}^{\prime},\phi(f_{i})=f_{i}^{\prime},

(108)

for each
i\in[n]
. Then for any
x,y\in V
,

[x,y]=[\phi(x),\phi(y)],

(109)

so
\phi\in\mathrm{Sp}(V)
. By construction,

\phi(L_{1})=L_{1}^{\prime},\phi(L_{2})=L_{2}^{\prime}.

(110)

Since
\phi
preserves the symplectic form, it maps isotropic subspaces to isotropic
subspaces and hence induces a bijection on
\mathcal{I}_{m}(V)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S}\,[|S\cap L_{1}^{\prime}|^{2}|S\cap L_{2}^{\prime}|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S}\,[|S\cap\phi(L_{1})|^{2}|S\cap\phi(L_{2})|^{2}]

(111)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S}\,[|\phi^{-1}(S)\cap L_{1}|^{2}|\phi^{-1}(S)\cap L_{2}|^{2}]

(112)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S}\,[|S\cap L_{1}|^{2}|S\cap L_{2}|^{2}],

(113)

as desired.
∎

We next state two lemmas that will serve as key ingredients in the proof.

Lemma 11
.

Let
L_{1},L_{2}\subset V\coloneq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
be Lagrangian subspaces such that

K\coloneq L_{1}\cap L_{2}
with
\dim(K)=\kappa
. Then, for uniformly sampled

S\sim\mathcal{I}_{m}(V)
,

\displaystyle\mu(n,\kappa,m)

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{S}\bigl[\,|S\cap L_{2}|^{2}\bigm|S\cap L_{1}=\{0\}\,\bigr]

(114)

\displaystyle=1+3(1-2^{\kappa-n})\frac{2^{m}-1}{2^{n}-1}

\displaystyle\quad+2(1-2^{\kappa-n})(1-2^{\kappa-n+1})\frac{(2^{m}-1)(2^{m-1}-1)}{(2^{n}-1)(2^{n-1}-1)}

(115)

\displaystyle\leq 1+3\cdot 2^{m-n}+2^{2m-2n+1}.

(116)

Proof.

Since

|S\cap L_{2}|=\sum_{x\in L_{2}}\mathbf{1}\{x\in S\},

(117)

we have

\displaystyle|S\cap L_{2}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in L_{2}}\mathbf{1}\{x\in S\}\mathbf{1}\{y\in S\}

(118)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in L_{2}}\mathbf{1}\{\langle x,y\rangle\subseteq S\}.

(119)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mu(n,k,m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in L_{2}}\Pr_{S}\!\bigl(\langle x,y\rangle\subseteq S\,\bigm|\,S\cap L_{1}=\{0\}\bigr).

(120)

We group the pairs
(x,y)\in L_{2}\times L_{2}
according to the dimension

d\in\{0,1,2\}
of the subspace
U_{d}\coloneq\langle x,y\rangle
.
We also use the fact that if any one of
x
,
y
, or
x+y
lies in
L_{1}\cap L_{2}=K
, then the corresponding term is zero, and that the conditional probability above depends only on
d
, not on the choice of
(x,y)
.
For
d\in\{1,2\}
, let
U_{d}\subseteq L_{2}
be any fixed
d
-dimensional subspace satisfying

U_{d}\cap L_{1}=\{0\}
, and define

p_{d}:=\Pr_{S}\!\bigl(U_{d}\subseteq S\,\bigm|\,S\cap L_{1}=\{0\}\bigr).

(121)

Then

\mu(n,\kappa,m)=1+3(2^{n}-2^{\kappa})p_{1}+(2^{n}-2^{\kappa})(2^{n}-2^{\kappa+1})p_{2}.

(122)

It remains to compute
p_{d}
. By definition,

p_{d}=\frac{\bigl|\{S\in\mathcal{I}_{m}(V):U_{d}\subseteq S,\ S\cap L_{1}=\{0\}\}\bigr|}{\bigl|\{S\in\mathcal{I}_{m}(V):S\cap L_{1}=\{0\}\}\bigr|}.

(123)

The denominator is given by Lemma
7
:

\bigl|\{S\in\mathcal{I}_{m}(V):S\cap L_{1}=\{0\}\}\bigr|=\binom{n}{m}_{2}2^{m(m+1)/2}2^{m(n-m)}.

(124)

For the numerator, since
U_{d}
is isotropic, the quotient

W:=U_{d}^{\perp}/U_{d}

(125)

is a symplectic vector space of dimension
2(n-d)
, and the map

S\mapsto S/U_{d}
gives a bijection between

\{S\in\mathcal{I}_{m}(V):U_{d}\subseteq S\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{I}_{m-d}(W).

Set

\overline{L}_{1}:=(L_{1}\cap U_{d}^{\perp}+U_{d})/U_{d}\subseteq W.

(126)

Since
L_{1}
is Lagrangian and
U_{d}\cap L_{1}=\{0\}
, the subspace
\overline{L}_{1}

is Lagrangian in
W
. Moreover, for every isotropic
S
containing
U_{d}
,

S\cap L_{1}=\{0\}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\overline{S}\cap\overline{L}_{1}=\{0\},

(127)

where
\overline{S}\coloneq S/U_{d}
.

Indeed, since
U_{d}\subseteq S
,

\displaystyle\overline{S}\cap\overline{L}_{1}

\displaystyle=(S/U_{d})\cap((L_{1}\cap U_{d}^{\perp}+U_{d})/U_{d})

(128)

\displaystyle=(S\cap(L_{1}\cap U_{d}^{\perp}+U_{d}))/U_{d}

(129)

\displaystyle=(S\cap L_{1}+U_{d})/U_{d}.

(130)

If
S\cap L_{1}=\{0\}
, then clearly
\overline{S}\cap\overline{L}_{1}=\{0\}
.
Conversely, if
\overline{S}\cap\overline{L}_{1}=\{0\}
, then

S\cap L_{1}\subseteq U_{d}
, and since
L_{1}\cap U_{d}=\{0\}
, it follows that

S\cap L_{1}=\{0\}
.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\bigl|\{S\in\mathcal{I}_{m}(V):U_{d}\subseteq S,\ S\cap L_{1}=\{0\}\}\bigr|

(131)

\displaystyle=\bigl|\{\overline{S}\in\mathcal{I}_{m-d}(W):\overline{S}\cap\overline{L}_{1}=\{0\}\}\bigr|.

(132)

Applying Lemma
7
in
W
, with
n
replaced by
n-d

and
m
replaced by
m-d
, we obtain

\bigl|\{S\in\mathcal{I}_{m}(V):U_{d}\subseteq S,\ S\cap L_{1}=\{0\}\}\bigr|=\binom{n-d}{m-d}_{2}2^{(m-d)(m-d+1)/2}2^{(m-d)(n-m)}.

(133)

Combining (
124
) and (
133
), we obtain

p_{d}=2^{-dn+d(d-1)/2}\frac{\binom{n-d}{m-d}_{2}}{\binom{n}{m}_{2}}.

(134)

In particular,

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\frac{2^{m}-1}{2^{n}-1},

(135)

\displaystyle p_{2}

\displaystyle=2^{-2n+1}\frac{(2^{m}-1)(2^{m-1}-1)}{(2^{n}-1)(2^{n-1}-1)}.

(136)

Substituting these into (
122
) gives

\displaystyle\mu(n,\kappa,m)

\displaystyle=1+3(1-2^{\kappa-n})\frac{2^{m}-1}{2^{n}-1}

(137)

\displaystyle\quad+2(1-2^{\kappa-n})(1-2^{\kappa-n+1})\frac{(2^{m}-1)(2^{m-1}-1)}{(2^{n}-1)(2^{n-1}-1)}

(138)

\displaystyle\leq 1+3\cdot 2^{m-n}+2^{2m-2n+1}.

(139)

This proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 12
.

Let
L_{1},L_{2}\subset V\coloneq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
be Lagrangian subspaces such that

K\coloneq L_{1}\cap L_{2}
with
\dim(K)=\kappa
. Then,

\displaystyle\gamma(n,m,\kappa)

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\mathcal{I}_{m}(L_{1})}\left[|S\cap K|^{2}\right]

(140)

\displaystyle=1+3(2^{\kappa}-1)\frac{2^{m}-1}{2^{n}-1}+(2^{\kappa}-1)(2^{\kappa}-2)\frac{(2^{m}-1)(2^{m-1}-1)}{(2^{n}-1)(2^{n-1}-1)}

(141)

\displaystyle\leq 1+3\cdot 2^{\kappa+m-n}+4^{\kappa+m-n}.

(142)

Proof.

Since

|S\cap K|=\sum_{x\in K}\mathbf{1}\{x\in S\},

(143)

we have

\displaystyle|S\cap K|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in K}\mathbf{1}\{x\in S\}\mathbf{1}\{y\in S\}

(144)

\displaystyle=1+3\sum_{x\in K\setminus\{0\}}\mathbf{1}\{x\in S\}+\sum_{x\neq y\in K\setminus\{0\}}\mathbf{1}\{x\in S\}\mathbf{1}\{y\in S\}.

(145)

Taking expectation, we obtain

\displaystyle\gamma(n,m,k)

\displaystyle=1+3\sum_{x\in K\setminus\{0\}}\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\mathcal{I}_{m}(L_{1})}\left[\mathbf{1}\{x\in S\}\right]

(146)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{x\neq y\in K\setminus\{0\}}\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\mathcal{I}_{m}(L_{1})}\left[\mathbf{1}\{x\in S\}\mathbf{1}\{y\in S\}\right].

(147)

Since the probability depends only on the dimension of the span,
this becomes

\displaystyle\gamma(n,m,\kappa)

\displaystyle=1+3(2^{\kappa}-1)\Pr_{S}(\langle x\rangle\subseteq S)

(148)

\displaystyle\quad+(2^{\kappa}-1)(2^{\kappa}-2)\Pr_{S}(\langle x,y\rangle\subseteq S),

(149)

where
x\neq y\in K\setminus\{0\}
.

Now let
U_{d}\subseteq L_{1}
be a fixed
d
-dimensional subspace. The number of

m
-dimensional subspaces of
L_{1}
containing
U_{d}
is

\binom{n-d}{m-d}_{2}
, and the total number of
m
-dimensional subspaces of
L_{1}

is
\binom{n}{m}_{2}
. Therefore,

\Pr_{S}(U_{d}\subseteq S)=\frac{\binom{n-d}{m-d}_{2}}{\binom{n}{m}_{2}}.

(150)

In particular,

\displaystyle\Pr_{S}(U_{1}\subseteq S)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{m}-1}{2^{n}-1},

(151)

\displaystyle\Pr_{S}(U_{2}\subseteq S)

\displaystyle=\frac{(2^{m}-1)(2^{m-1}-1)}{(2^{n}-1)(2^{n-1}-1)}.

(152)

Substituting these into Eq. (
149
) gives

\displaystyle\gamma(n,m,\kappa)

\displaystyle=1+3(2^{\kappa}-1)\frac{2^{m}-1}{2^{n}-1}+(2^{\kappa}-1)(2^{\kappa}-2)\frac{(2^{m}-1)(2^{m-1}-1)}{(2^{n}-1)(2^{n-1}-1)}

(153)

\displaystyle\leq 1+3\cdot 2^{\kappa+m-n}+4^{\kappa+m-n}.

(154)

This proves the claim.
∎

E.1
Part I: Recovery of the Weyl support

In the first part, we show that, when
t=O(\log n)
, with high probability over independently sampled

C_{1},\dots,C_{m}\sim\mathcal{C}_{k}
, the resulting measurement bases are sufficient to recover the full stabilizer group. Concretely, we show that

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)\cap C_{i}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{Z})\bigr)=\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)

(155)

holds with high probability.

This conclusion does not hold in the worst case for non-adaptive shallow-depth measurements. For example, as noted in the main text,
\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho_{\mathrm{GHZ}})
may fail to be recovered from polynomially many sampled bases. However, such worst-case obstructions occur only for an exponentially small fraction of
\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)\in\mathcal{I}_{n-t}(V)
. For almost all target subspaces with
t=O(\log n)
, the following lemma provides the required recovery guarantee.

Lemma 13

(Intersection with
\mathcal{Z}
)
.

Assume that
t=O(\log n)
and
k=\Omega(\log n)
. Let
C
be sampled independently from
\mathcal{C}_{k}
. Then, for all but a

2^{-\Omega(n)}
fraction of
S\in\mathcal{I}_{n-t}(V)
, the following holds:
for every codimension-one subspace
T\subset S
,

\Pr_{C}\!\left(C(S\setminus T)\cap\mathcal{Z}\neq\emptyset\right)\geq\Omega(2^{-t}).

(156)

Proof.

Define
X_{C}=X_{C}(S,T):=|C(S\setminus T)\cap\mathcal{Z}|
. Then

\Pr_{C}\!\left(C(S\setminus T)\cap\mathcal{Z}\neq\emptyset\right)=\Pr_{C}(X_{C}>0).

(157)

Since
X_{C}
is nonnegative and finite, Paley–Zygmund’s inequality gives

\Pr_{C}(X_{C}>0)\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{C}[X_{C}]^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{C}[X_{C}^{2}]}.

(158)

For the numerator, Lemma
14
gives
\mathbb{E}_{C}[X_{C}]\geq\Omega(2^{-t})
, and hence

\mathbb{E}_{C}[X_{C}]^{2}\geq\Omega(2^{-2t}).

(159)

For the denominator, Corollary
5
implies that, for all but a
2^{-\Omega(n)}
fraction of
S\in\mathcal{I}_{n-t}(V)
,

\mathbb{E}_{C}[X_{C}^{2}]\leq O(2^{-t}).

(160)

Combining Eq. (
159
) and Eq. (
160
) proves the claim.
∎

Corollary 4

(Recovery of the Weyl support)
.

Assume that
t=O(\log n)
and
k=\Omega(\log n)
. Let

C_{1},\dots,C_{m}
be sampled independently from
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, where

m=O(n2^{t})
. Then, with high probability over the choice of

C_{1},\dots,C_{m}
, the following holds for all but a
2^{-\Omega(n)}

fraction of
S\in\mathcal{I}_{n-t}(V)
:

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(S\cap C_{i}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{Z})\bigr)=S.

(161)

Proof.

Fix
S\in\mathcal{I}_{n-t}(V)
for which Lemma
13

holds for every codimension-one subspace of
S
. For each
i\in[m]
, define

S_{i}:=S\cap C_{i}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{Z}),\qquad T_{i}:=\sum_{j=1}^{i}S_{j},

(162)

with the convention
T_{0}=\{0\}
.

Suppose that
T_{i-1}\subsetneq S
. Since
T_{i-1}
is a proper subspace of
S
,
there exists a codimension-one subspace
T\subset S
such that

T_{i-1}\subseteq T
. If

C_{i}(S\setminus T)\cap\mathcal{Z}\neq\emptyset,

(163)

then there exists
x\in S\setminus T
such that

x\in S\cap C_{i}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{Z})=S_{i}
. In particular,
x\notin T_{i-1}
, and hence

T_{i}\supsetneq T_{i-1}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pr\!\left(T_{i}\supsetneq T_{i-1}\mid T_{i-1}\subsetneq S\right)

\displaystyle\geq\Pr\!\left(C_{i}(S\setminus T)\cap\mathcal{Z}\neq\emptyset\right)

(164)

\displaystyle\geq\Omega(2^{-t}),

(165)

where the last line follows from Lemma
13
.

Thus, as long as
T_{i-1}\neq S
, the dimension increases by at least one with
probability at least
\Omega(2^{-t})
. Since
\dim(S)=n-t
, it follows by a
standard Chernoff-bound argument that after
m=O(n2^{t})
independent samples,
we have
\dim(T_{m})=n-t
with high probability. Because
T_{m}\subseteq S
, this implies

T_{m}=S
, i.e.,

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(S\cap C_{i}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{Z})\bigr)=S.

(166)

This proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 14
.

Let

and let

Fact 6

(Chebyshev’s inequality and a simple consequence)
.

Let
X
be a real-valued random variable with finite variance and

\mathbb{E}[X]>0
. Then, for any
a>0
,

\Pr\!\left(|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|\geq a\right)\leq\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X)}{a^{2}}.

In particular, for any constant
c>1
,

\Pr\!\left(X\geq c\,\mathbb{E}[X]\right)\leq\Pr\!\left(|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|\geq(c-1)\mathbb{E}[X]\right)\leq\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X)}{(c-1)^{2}\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}}.

Therefore,

\Pr\!\left(X<c\,\mathbb{E}[X]\right)\geq 1-\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X)}{(c-1)^{2}\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}}.

Equivalently, for any fixed constant
c>1
,

\Pr\!\left(X<O(\mathbb{E}[X])\right)\geq 1-O\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X)}{\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}}\right).

Lemma 15
.

Let

We first bound

Lemma 16
.

Let

Lemma 17
.

Let
A,B\subset V\coloneq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
be Lagrangian subspaces, and let

K\coloneq A\cap B
with
\dim(K)=\kappa
.
If
S
is sampled uniformly from
\mathcal{I}_{n-t}(V)
, then

\mathbb{E}_{S}\bigl[|S\cap A|^{2}|S\cap B|^{2}\bigr]\leq f_{t}^{2}+O(2^{\kappa-n})+O(4^{\kappa-n}).

(188)

Proof.

We first condition on the dimension of
S\cap A
. Writing

a:=\dim(S\cap A),

(189)

we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S}\bigl[|S\cap A|^{2}|S\cap B|^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=0}^{n-t}\Pr_{S}\!\bigl(\dim(S\cap A)=a\bigr)\,\mathbb{E}_{S}\!\bigl[|S\cap A|^{2}|S\cap B|^{2}\,\bigm|\,\dim(S\cap A)=a\bigr]

(190)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=0}^{n-t}\Pr_{S}\!\bigl(\dim(S\cap A)=a\bigr)\,2^{2a}\,\mathbb{E}_{S}\!\bigl[|S\cap B|^{2}\,\bigm|\,\dim(S\cap A)=a\bigr].

(191)

We next analyze the conditional expectation inside the sum.
Conditioning further on the actual subspace
M=S\cap A
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{S}\!\bigl[|S\cap B|^{2}\,\bigm|\,\dim(S\cap A)=a\bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{M\sim\mathcal{I}_{a}(A)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{S}\!\bigl[|S\cap B|^{2}\,\bigm|\,S\cap A=M\bigr]\right].

(192)

Here we used that, conditional on
\dim(S\cap A)=a
, the intersection

M=S\cap A
is uniformly distributed over the
a
-dimensional subspaces of
A
.

Fix such a subspace
M\subset A
with
\dim(M)=a
, and define

W\coloneq M^{\perp}/M,\qquad S_{M}\coloneq S/M,\qquad A_{M}\coloneq A/M,\qquad B_{M}\coloneq(B\cap M^{\perp}+M)/M.

(193)

Figure 3:

Quotient reduction.
Left: for a fixed isotropic subspace
S
, let
M=S\cap A
.
Right: after passing to the quotient space
M^{\perp}/M
, the subspaces
S_{M}=S/M
,
A_{M}=A/M
, and
B_{M}=(B\cap M^{\perp}+M)/M
encode the remaining intersection structure. In particular, conditioning on
S\cap A=M
reduces the analysis of
S\cap B
to that of
S_{M}\cap B_{M}
in the quotient symplectic space.

Since
A
and
B
are Lagrangian subspaces of
V
, both
A_{M}
and
B_{M}
are Lagrangian subspaces of the symplectic space
W
[Fig.
3
]. Applying the first isomorphism theorem to the restriction of the quotient map
\pi:M^{\perp}\to W
to
S\cap B
, we obtain

(S\cap B)/(M\cap K)\cong(S\cap B+M)/M=S_{M}\cap B_{M}.

(194)

Consequently,

|S\cap B|=|M\cap K|\,|S_{M}\cap B_{M}|.

(195)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S}\!\bigl[|S\cap B|^{2}\,\bigm|\,S\cap A=M\bigr]

\displaystyle=|M\cap K|^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{S}\!\bigl[|S_{M}\cap B_{M}|^{2}\,\bigm|\,S\cap A=M\bigr].

(196)

Indeed, the map
S\mapsto S/M
gives a bijection between
\{S\in\mathcal{I}_{n-t}(V):S\cap A=M\}
and
\{S_{M}\in\mathcal{I}_{n-t-a}(W):S_{M}\cap A_{M}=\{0\}\}
. Since the original distribution is uniform, the induced distribution on the quotient is also uniform.
Hence

\mathbb{E}_{S}\!\bigl[|S_{M}\cap B_{M}|^{2}\,\bigm|\,S\cap A=M\bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{S_{M}\sim\mathcal{I}_{n-t-a}(W)}\!\bigl[|S_{M}\cap B_{M}|^{2}\,\bigm|\,S_{M}\cap A_{M}=\{0\}\bigr].

(197)

Applying Lemma
11
in the quotient space
W
gives

\mathbb{E}_{S_{M}\sim\mathcal{I}_{n-t-a}(W)}\!\bigl[|S_{M}\cap B_{M}|^{2}\,\bigm|\,S_{M}\cap A_{M}=\{0\}\bigr]\leq f_{t},

(198)

where
f_{t}=1+3/2^{t}+2/2^{2t}
.
Consequently,

\mathbb{E}_{S}\!\bigl[|S\cap B|^{2}\,\bigm|\,S\cap A=M\bigr]\leq f_{t}\,|M\cap K|^{2}.

(199)

Substituting this bound into Eq. (
192
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S}\!\bigl[|S\cap B|^{2}\,\bigm|\,\dim(S\cap A)=a\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq f_{t}\,\mathbb{E}_{M\sim\mathcal{I}_{a}(A)}\bigl[|M\cap K|^{2}\bigr].

(200)

By Lemma
12
,

\mathbb{E}_{M\sim\mathcal{I}_{a}(A)}\bigl[|M\cap K|^{2}\bigr]\leq 1+3\cdot 2^{\kappa+a-n}+4^{\kappa+a-n}.

(201)

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}_{S}\!\bigl[|S\cap B|^{2}\,\bigm|\,\dim(S\cap A)=a\bigr]\leq f_{t}\bigl(1+3\cdot 2^{\kappa+a-n}+4^{\kappa+a-n}\bigr).

(202)

Plugging this estimate into Eq. (
191
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S}\bigl[|S\cap A|^{2}|S\cap B|^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq f_{t}\sum_{a=0}^{n-t}\Pr_{S}\!\bigl(\dim(S\cap A)=a\bigr)\Bigl(2^{2a}+3\cdot 2^{\kappa-n}2^{3a}+4^{\kappa-n}2^{4a}\Bigr)

(203)

\displaystyle=f_{t}\Bigl(\mathbb{E}[2^{2a}]+O(2^{\kappa-n})\,\mathbb{E}[2^{3a}]+O(4^{\kappa-n})\,\mathbb{E}[2^{4a}]\Bigr),

(204)

where the expectation is taken with respect to the distribution of

a=\dim(S\cap A)
. Finally, since
A
is Lagrangian, Lemma
9
and
Corollary
3
imply that

\mathbb{E}[2^{2a}]=\mathbb{E}_{S}[|S\cap A|^{2}]=f_{t}+O(2^{-n}),

(205)

while
\mathbb{E}[2^{3a}]
and
\mathbb{E}[2^{4a}]
are both
O(1)
.
Hence

\mathbb{E}_{S}\bigl[|S\cap A|^{2}|S\cap B|^{2}\bigr]\leq f_{t}^{2}+O(2^{\kappa-n})+O(4^{\kappa-n}),

(206)

as claimed.
∎

Lemma 18
.

Let
S\in\mathcal{I}_{n-t}(V)
, and define
\beta(S):=\mathbb{E}_{C}[|C(S)\cap\mathcal{Z}|^{2}]
, where
C
is sampled from
\mathcal{C}_{k}
. Then

\mathbb{E}_{S}[\beta(S)^{2}]\leq f_{t}^{2}+O\left(\left(\frac{2}{2^{k}+1}\right)^{n/k}\right),

(207)

where
f_{t}=1+3/2^{t}+2/2^{2t}
.

Proof.

We expand the second moment as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S}[\beta(S)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S}\mathbb{E}_{C_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{C_{2}}\bigl[|C_{1}(S)\cap\mathcal{Z}|^{2}\,|C_{2}(S)\cap\mathcal{Z}|^{2}\bigr]

(208)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{C_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{C_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\bigl[|S\cap C_{1}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{Z})|^{2}\,|S\cap C_{2}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{Z})|^{2}\bigr]

(209)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{n}\Pr_{C_{1},C_{2}}\bigl(\dim(C_{1}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{Z})\cap C_{2}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{Z}))=l\bigr)\,\mathbb{E}_{S}[|S\cap A|^{2}|S\cap B|^{2}]

(210)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{n}\Pr_{C}\bigl(\dim(C^{\dagger}(\mathcal{Z})\cap\mathcal{Z})=l\bigr)\,\mathbb{E}_{S}[|S\cap A|^{2}|S\cap B|^{2}],

(211)

where in the third line we used Lemma
10
, and
A,B\subset V
are Lagrangian subspaces satisfying
\dim(A\cap B)=l

Let
G_{k}(s)
be the generating function defined in Definition
3
for the block size
k
, and let
G(s):=G_{k}(s)^{n/k}
. Then

\Pr_{C}\bigl(\dim(C^{\dagger}(\mathcal{Z})\cap\mathcal{Z})=l\bigr)=[s^{l}]\,G(s),

(212)

where
[s^{l}]\,G(s)
denotes the coefficient of
s^{l}

\displaystyle G(2)=G_{k}(2)^{n/k}=\sum_{l=0}^{n}\Pr_{C}\bigl(\dim(C^{\dagger}(\mathcal{Z})\cap\mathcal{Z})=l\bigr)\,2^{l},

(213)

\displaystyle G(4)=G_{k}(4)^{n/k}=\sum_{l=0}^{n}\Pr_{C}\bigl(\dim(C^{\dagger}(\mathcal{Z})\cap\mathcal{Z})=l\bigr)\,4^{l}.

Applying Lemma

, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S}[\beta(S)^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq f_{t}^{2}+O\left(2^{-n}G_{k}(2)^{n/k}\right)+O\left(4^{-n}G_{k}(4)^{n/k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq f_{t}^{2}+O\left(\left(\frac{2}{2^{k}+1}\right)^{n/k}\right)+O\left(\left(\frac{6}{(2^{k}+1)(2^{k}+2)}\right)^{n/k}\right)

E.2
Part II: Reconstruction from computational difference samples

In Part I, we showed that, after sampling
m=O(n2^{t})
Clifford circuits
C_{1},\dots,C_{m}\sim\mathcal{C}_{k}
, one obtains measurement bases that are sufficient
to recover
\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
. This, however, does not by itself identify the generators
of
\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
. It remains to show that, in each such basis, sufficiently many
computational difference samples allow us to learn the corresponding hidden Pauli
symmetries, and that combining the resulting subspaces yields a sufficiently accurate
approximation to
\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
.

The role of Part II is precisely to establish this second step. For each

\rho_{i}:=C_{i}\rho C_{i}^{\dagger}
, we use computational difference sampling to construct a
subspace
\widehat{H}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
that captures most of the mass of the
distribution
r_{\rho_{i}}
. We then show that the subspaces

C_{i}^{\dagger}\bigl((\widehat{H}_{i}+\mathcal{Z})^{\perp}\bigr)
can be aggregated so as to
produce a subspace
\widehat{S}
satisfying

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{S}}[\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{x})^{2}]\geq 1-\epsilon
. In particular, this
part extends the computational difference sampling analysis from the pure-state setting
Grewal et al. 2025
to
general mixed-state inputs.

Fact 7

(Finding a heavy-weight subspace
Grewal et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2025
)
.

Let
\epsilon>0
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\mathcal{D}
be a distribution over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}
.
Suppose
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
are
m
i.i.d. samples drawn from
\mathcal{D}
.
Fix
0<\delta<1
. If
m\geq\frac{2\log(1/\delta)+2d}{\epsilon}
, then with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\mathcal{D}\!\left(\operatorname{span}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\right)\geq 1-\epsilon.

(218)

Applying this fact to the computational difference sampling distribution
r_{\rho}
yields the following.

.

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
be
m
i.i.d. computational difference samples drawn from
r_{\rho}
, and let
A=\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\rangle
. If

m\geq\frac{2\log(1/\delta)+2n}{\epsilon},

(219)

then with probability at least

(220)

Proof.

This follows directly from Fact

.

Lemma 19

(Sum of heavy-weight subspaces)
.

Let
S_{1},S_{2}\subset V\coloneq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
be subspaces satisfying

\frac{1}{|S_{i}|}\sum_{x\in S_{i}}\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{x})^{2}\geq 1-\epsilon_{i},\qquad i\in\{1,2\}.

(221)

Let
S:=S_{1}+S_{2}
. Then

\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{x\in S}\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{x})^{2}\geq 1-(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}).

(222)

Proof.

For a subspace
A\subset V
, define

P_{A}\coloneq\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{x\in A}W_{x}\otimes\overline{W_{x}},

(223)

where the bar denotes entrywise complex conjugation in the computational basis.

We first show that
P_{A}
is an orthogonal projector. Since the Weyl operators are
defined only up to their projective phase, for any
x,y\in V
there exists a phase

\gamma(x,y)
with
|\gamma(x,y)|=1
such that

W_{x}W_{y}=\gamma(x,y)W_{x+y}.

(224)

Taking the complex conjugate gives

\overline{W_{x}W_{y}}=\overline{\gamma(x,y)}\,\overline{W_{x+y}}.

(225)

Hence the phase cancels in the tensor product:

(W_{x}\otimes\overline{W_{x}})(W_{y}\otimes\overline{W_{y}})=W_{x+y}\otimes\overline{W_{x+y}}.

(226)

Therefore,

\displaystyle P_{A}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|A|^{2}}\sum_{x,y\in A}(W_{x}\otimes\overline{W_{x}})(W_{y}\otimes\overline{W_{y}})

(227)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|A|^{2}}\sum_{x,y\in A}W_{x+y}\otimes\overline{W_{x+y}}

(228)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{z\in A}W_{z}\otimes\overline{W_{z}}

(229)

\displaystyle=P_{A}.

(230)

Since each qubit Weyl operator
W_{x}
is Hermitian,
W_{x}\otimes\overline{W_{x}}
is
also Hermitian. Hence
P_{A}^{\dagger}=P_{A}
, and so
P_{A}
is an orthogonal projector.

Next, we relate this projector to the Pauli weights. For any subspace
A\subset V
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\!\left[(\rho\otimes\overline{\rho})P_{A}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{x\in A}\mathrm{tr}\!\left[(\rho\otimes\overline{\rho})(W_{x}\otimes\overline{W_{x}})\right]

(231)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{x\in A}\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{x})\,\mathrm{tr}(\overline{\rho}\,\overline{W_{x}})

(232)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{x\in A}\left|\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{x})\right|^{2}.

(233)

Since both
\rho
and
W_{x}
are Hermitian,
\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{x})
is real. Thus

\mathrm{tr}\!\left[(\rho\otimes\overline{\rho})P_{A}\right]=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{x\in A}\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{x})^{2}.

(234)

Applying this identity to
A=S_{i}
, the assumptions become

\mathrm{tr}\!\left[(\rho\otimes\overline{\rho})P_{S_{i}}\right]\geq 1-\epsilon_{i},\qquad i\in\{1,2\}.

(235)

We now compute the product
P_{S_{1}}P_{S_{2}}
. Using the same phase-cancellation
identity as above,

\displaystyle P_{S_{1}}P_{S_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|S_{1}||S_{2}|}\sum_{x\in S_{1},\;y\in S_{2}}(W_{x}\otimes\overline{W_{x}})(W_{y}\otimes\overline{W_{y}})

(236)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|S_{1}||S_{2}|}\sum_{x\in S_{1},\;y\in S_{2}}W_{x+y}\otimes\overline{W_{x+y}}

(237)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|S_{1}||S_{2}|}\sum_{s\in S}N_{s}\,W_{s}\otimes\overline{W_{s}},

(238)

where

N_{s}\coloneq\left|\{(x,y)\in S_{1}\times S_{2}:x+y=s\}\right|.

(239)

For every
s\in S=S_{1}+S_{2}
, we have
N_{s}=|S_{1}\cap S_{2}|
. Indeed, if

(x_{0},y_{0})
is one solution to
x+y=s
, then all solutions are exactly

(x_{0}+u,y_{0}+u),\qquad u\in S_{1}\cap S_{2}.

(240)

Therefore,

\displaystyle P_{S_{1}}P_{S_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|S_{1}\cap S_{2}|}{|S_{1}||S_{2}|}\sum_{s\in S}W_{s}\otimes\overline{W_{s}}

(241)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{s\in S}W_{s}\otimes\overline{W_{s}}

(242)

\displaystyle=P_{S},

(243)

where we used

|S|=|S_{1}+S_{2}|=\frac{|S_{1}||S_{2}|}{|S_{1}\cap S_{2}|}.

(244)

In particular,
P_{S_{1}}
and
P_{S_{2}}
commute.

Since
P_{S_{1}}
and
P_{S_{2}}
are commuting orthogonal projectors, they are
simultaneously diagonalizable. Thus there exists an orthonormal basis

\{\ket{\psi_j}\}_{j}
such that

\displaystyle P_{S_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}a_{j}\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j},

(245)

\displaystyle P_{S_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}b_{j}\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j},

(246)

\displaystyle P_{S}=P_{S_{1}}P_{S_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}a_{j}b_{j}\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j},

(247)

where
a_{j},b_{j}\in\{0,1\}
. Since

a_{j}b_{j}\geq a_{j}+b_{j}-1

(248)

for all
j
, we obtain the operator inequality

P_{S}\succeq P_{S_{1}}+P_{S_{2}}-I,

(249)

where
A\succeq B
means that
A-B
is positive semidefinite. Finally, since
\rho\otimes\overline{\rho}
is positive semidefinite, taking the
trace against it preserves the semidefinite order. Hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\!\left[(\rho\otimes\overline{\rho})P_{S}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{tr}\!\left[(\rho\otimes\overline{\rho})P_{S_{1}}\right]+\mathrm{tr}\!\left[(\rho\otimes\overline{\rho})P_{S_{2}}\right]-\mathrm{tr}(\rho\otimes\overline{\rho})

(250)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\epsilon_{1})+(1-\epsilon_{2})-1

(251)

\displaystyle=1-(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}).

(252)

Using again

\mathrm{tr}\!\left[(\rho\otimes\overline{\rho})P_{S}\right]=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{x\in S}\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{x})^{2},

(253)

we obtain

\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{x\in S}\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{x})^{2}\geq 1-(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2})

(254)

as desired.
∎

Appendix F
Worst-case instance: GHZ

Our learning algorithm does not succeed for every input whose stabilizer group
\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
lies in
\mathcal{I}_{n-t}(V)
. As noted in the main text, the
n
-qubit GHZ state
provides a representative hard instance. Let

\rho_{\mathrm{GHZ}}=\ket{\mathrm{GHZ}}\!\bra{\mathrm{GHZ}},

where
\ket{\mathrm{GHZ}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0^n}+\ket{1^n})
and let
S_{\mathrm{GHZ}}
denote its stabilizer group, generated by

S_{\mathrm{GHZ}}=\left\langle Z_{1}Z_{2},\,Z_{2}Z_{3},\,\dots,\,Z_{n-1}Z_{n},\,\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right\rangle.

Define

T_{0}:=\left\langle Z_{1}Z_{2},\,Z_{2}Z_{3},\,\dots,\,Z_{n-1}Z_{n}\right\rangle=\{(0^{n},z)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}:\textstyle\sum_{i}z_{i}\equiv 0\pmod{2}\}.

Then

S_{\mathrm{GHZ}}\setminus T_{0}=\{(1^{n},z)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}:\textstyle\sum_{i}z_{i}\equiv 0\pmod{2}\}.

If
C
is sampled uniformly from
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, then we obtain

\Pr_{C}\!\left(C(S_{\mathrm{GHZ}}\setminus T_{0})\cap\mathcal{Z}\neq\emptyset\right)\leq(2/3)^{n/k}.

(278)

This shows that, under the ensemble
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, one would need exponentially many
independent random Clifford circuits in order to reveal all generators in
S_{\mathrm{GHZ}}
.

There are two points worth emphasizing about this observation. First, the bound in
Eq. (
278
) is proved only for the Clifford ensemble
\mathcal{C}_{k}=\mathrm{Cl}(k)^{\otimes n/k}
used throughout this paper. It does not rule out
the possibility that a different choice of shallow-depth Clifford ensemble could avoid this
exponential suppression. Understanding whether such an ensemble exists remains an interesting direction for future work.

Second, even within the ensemble
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, the GHZ example does not by itself imply
that shallow-depth measurements are fundamentally insufficient. As discussed in the main
text, once one allows a single adaptive step, the full stabilizer group of the GHZ state can
still be learned using
O(\log n)
-depth measurements without any exponential increase in the
number of samples. This suggests that adaptivity may substantially enlarge the class of states
that can be learned efficiently in the shallow-depth setting. Determining how much adaptivity
is necessary remains another interesting open question.

We now introduce the lemma needed to prove Eq. (
278
).

Lemma 20
.

Let
x_{0}=(1^{n},0^{n})\in V\coloneq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}
, and let
C
be sampled uniformly from
\mathrm{Cl}(n)
. Then

\Pr_{C}\!\bigl(C(x_{0}+\mathcal{Z})\cap\mathcal{Z}\neq\emptyset\bigr)=\frac{2}{3}.

(279)

Proof.

We first replace the random Clifford
C
by the random Lagrangian subspace

L:=C^{\dagger}(\mathcal{Z})
. Since
C
is sampled uniformly from
\mathrm{Cl}(n)
, the
subspace
L
is distributed uniformly over the set of Lagrangian subspaces of
V
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pr_{C}\!\bigl(C(x_{0}+\mathcal{Z})\cap\mathcal{Z}\neq\emptyset\bigr)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{C}\!\bigl((x_{0}+\mathcal{Z})\cap C^{\dagger}(\mathcal{Z})\neq\emptyset\bigr)

(280)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{L}\!\bigl((x_{0}+\mathcal{Z})\cap L\neq\emptyset\bigr)

(281)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{L}\!\bigl(x_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}+L\bigr).

(282)

Next, consider the quotient map

\pi:V\to V/\mathcal{Z},\qquad\pi(x)=x+\mathcal{Z}.

(283)

We claim that

x_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}+L\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\pi(x_{0})\in\pi(L),

(284)

where
\pi(L)=\{l+\mathcal{Z}:l\in L\}
. Indeed, if
x_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}+L
, then
x_{0}=z+l
for some
z\in\mathcal{Z}
and

l\in L
, so
\pi(x_{0})=x_{0}+\mathcal{Z}=l+\mathcal{Z}\in\pi(L)
. Conversely, if
\pi(x_{0})\in\pi(L)
, then
x_{0}+\mathcal{Z}=l+\mathcal{Z}
for some

l\in L
, which means that
x_{0}-l\in\mathcal{Z}
, or equivalently

x_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}+L
.

Thus,

\displaystyle\Pr_{L}\!\bigl(x_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}+L\bigr)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{L}\!\bigl(\pi(x_{0})\in\pi(L)\bigr)

(285)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\Pr_{L}\!\bigl(\dim(L\cap\mathcal{Z})=r\bigr)\,\Pr_{L}\!\bigl(\pi(x_{0})\in\pi(L)\mid\dim(L\cap\mathcal{Z})=r\bigr).

(286)

By Fact
5
,

\Pr_{L}\!\bigl(\dim(L\cap\mathcal{Z})=r\bigr)=\frac{\binom{n}{r}_{2}\,2^{(n-r)(n-r+1)/2}}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(2^{i}+1)}.

(287)

Moreover, conditioned on
\dim(L\cap\mathcal{Z})=r
, the image
\pi(L)
is uniformly distributed over all
(n-r)
-dimensional subspaces of
V/\mathcal{Z}
, since the subgroup
\{\phi\in\mathrm{Sp}(V):\phi(\mathcal{Z})=\mathcal{Z}\}
acts transitively on that set. Therefore,
since
\pi(x_{0})\neq 0
, we have

\Pr_{L}\!\bigl(\pi(x_{0})\in\pi(L)\mid\dim(L\cap\mathcal{Z})=r\bigr)=\frac{\binom{n-1}{n-r-1}_{2}}{\binom{n}{n-r}_{2}}=\frac{2^{\,n-r}-1}{2^{n}-1}.

(288)

Hence,

\displaystyle\Pr_{L}\!\bigl(x_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}+L\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{\binom{n}{r}_{2}\,2^{(n-r)(n-r+1)/2}}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(2^{i}+1)}\cdot\frac{2^{\,n-r}-1}{2^{n}-1}

(289)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n+1}+1}{3(2^{n}-1)}-\frac{1}{2^{n}-1}

(290)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3}

(291)

as desired.
∎

We can now prove Eq. (
278
).

Proof of Eq. (
278
).

Since

S_{\mathrm{GHZ}}\setminus T_{0}=x_{0}+T_{0}\subseteq x_{0}+\mathcal{Z},

we obtain

\displaystyle\Pr_{C}\!\bigl(C(S_{\mathrm{GHZ}}\setminus T_{0})\cap\mathcal{Z}\neq\emptyset\bigr)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{C}\!\bigl(C(x_{0}+T_{0})\cap\mathcal{Z}\neq\emptyset\bigr)

(292)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{C}\!\bigl(C(x_{0}+\mathcal{Z})\cap\mathcal{Z}\neq\emptyset\bigr).

(293)

Now let
C=C_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes C_{n/k}
be sampled uniformly from

\mathcal{C}_{k}=\mathrm{Cl}(k)^{\otimes n/k}
. Since
x_{0}+\mathcal{Z}
factorizes across the

n/k
blocks as

x_{0}+\mathcal{Z}=(x_{0,k}+\mathcal{Z}_{k})^{\otimes n/k},

where

x_{0,k}=(1^{k},0^{k})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2k},\qquad\mathcal{Z}_{k}=\{I,Z\}^{k}=\{(0^{k},z)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2k}:z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\},

the event factorizes over
n/k
blocks as well. Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pr_{C}\!\bigl(C(x_{0}+\mathcal{Z})\cap\mathcal{Z}\neq\emptyset\bigr)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n/k}\Pr_{C_{i}}\!\bigl(C_{i}(x_{0,k}+\mathcal{Z}_{k})\cap\mathcal{Z}_{k}\neq\emptyset\bigr)

(294)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{n/k},

(295)

where the last equality follows from Lemma
20
applied on each

k
-qubit block. This proves Eq. (
278
).
∎

To recover the hidden parity, we use the Fast Walsh–Hadamard Transform (FWHT), which computes all empirical Walsh correlations once the sample frequency is estimated.
Given samples

\displaystyle\quad 1.\ \widehat{S}\supseteq\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho),

(305)

\displaystyle\quad 2.\ \frac{1}{|\widehat{S}|}\sum_{x\in\widehat{S}}\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{x})^{2}\geq 1-\epsilon,

(306)

\displaystyle\quad 3.\ \widehat{S}\text{ is isotropic}.

(307)

We will show that the existence of such an algorithm would imply an efficient
single-copy protocol for a previously studied hypothesis-testing problem that is known
to require
\Omega(2^{t})
samples. This yields the desired lower bound.

Consider the following two families of states:

\displaystyle\rho_{A}

\displaystyle=\ket{0^{n-t}}\bra{0^{n-t}}\otimes\frac{I}{2^{t}},\qquad

\displaystyle\text{with probability }\frac{1}{2},

(308)

\displaystyle\rho_{B}

\displaystyle=\ket{0^{n-t}}\bra{0^{n-t}}\otimes\frac{I+P}{2^{t}},\qquad

\displaystyle\text{with probability }\frac{1}{2},

(309)

where
P
is randomly chosen from
\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes t}\setminus\{I^{\otimes t}\}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho_{A})

\displaystyle=\langle Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n-t}\rangle,

(310)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho_{B})

\displaystyle=\langle Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n-t},P\rangle,

(311)

where, in the second line,
P
is understood as acting on the last
t
qubits.

Assume now that
\epsilon<1/2
. Then condition 2 implies

\frac{1}{|\widehat{S}|}\sum_{x\in\widehat{S}}\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{x})^{2}>\frac{1}{2}.

(312)

We claim that, for both
\rho=\rho_{A}
and
\rho=\rho_{B}
, this forces

\widehat{S}=\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho).

Indeed, by condition 1 we already have
\widehat{S}\supseteq\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
.
Suppose, for contradiction, that
\widehat{S}
strictly contains
\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
, and let

x\in\widehat{S}\setminus\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
. Since
\widehat{S}
is a subspace containing

\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
, it also contains the entire coset
x+\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
.
Moreover, because
x\notin\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
, none of the elements of this coset belong to

\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
. Therefore,

\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{y})^{2}=0\qquad\text{for all }y\in x+\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho),

whereas

\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{y})^{2}=1\qquad\text{for all }y\in\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho).

Since the two cosets
\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
and
x+\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
have the same size,
it follows that

\frac{1}{|\widehat{S}|}\sum_{y\in\widehat{S}}\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{y})^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2},

(313)

contradicting condition 2. Hence
\widehat{S}=\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
.

It follows that any algorithm satisfying the above three conditions can distinguish

\rho_{A}
from
\rho_{B}
: its output has dimension
n-t
in the first case and
n-t+1

in the second.

We now express such an algorithm as an adaptive single-copy measurement protocol.
Let

\{F_{k}^{<i}\}_{k}

denote the POVM used on the
i
-th copy, where the choice of POVM may depend on all
previous
i-1
outcomes. Since both
\rho_{A}
and
\rho_{B}
are tensor products with the same
pure state on the first
n-t
qubits, distinguishing
\rho_{A}
from
\rho_{B}
is equivalent
to distinguishing their reduced states on the last
t
qubits using the induced POVMs

G_{k}^{<i}:=\mathrm{tr}_{1,\dots,n-t}\!\Big[\bigl(\ket{0^{n-t}}\bra{0^{n-t}}\otimes I\bigr)F_{k}^{<i}\Big].

(314)

Each
\{G_{k}^{<i}\}_{k}
is a valid POVM on
t
qubits. Therefore the original task reduces to
the following adaptive single-copy property-testing problem on
t
qubits:

\displaystyle\rho_{A}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{I}{2^{t}},\qquad

\displaystyle\text{with probability }\frac{1}{2},

(315)

\displaystyle\rho_{B}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{I+P}{2^{t}},\qquad

\displaystyle\text{with probability }\frac{1}{2}.

(316)

where
P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes t}\setminus\{I^{\otimes t}\}
.

This is the single-copy Pauli-learning/testing problem studied in previous work
Chen et al. 2024
;
Huang et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2022
,
for which an
\Omega(2^{t})
lower bound is known. Hence any algorithm satisfying conditions 1–3 above must use at least
\Omega(2^{t})
copies in the worst case. If we restrict the algorithm to be non-adaptive like ours, then we can get a tighter
\Omega(t2^{t})
lower bound
Huang et al. 2021
.

We summarize the discussion as follows:

Lemma 21

(Single-copy lower bound on the
t
-dependence)
.

Let
\epsilon<1/2
. Any potentially adaptive single-copy algorithm that outputs
an isotropic subspace
\widehat{S}
satisfying

1.

\widehat{S}\supseteq\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
,

2.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\widehat{S}|}\sum_{x\in\widehat{S}}\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_{x})^{2}\geq 1-\epsilon
,

for every
n
-qubit state
\rho
with
\dim\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)=n-t
, must use
at least
\Omega(2^{t})
copies in the worst case.

Previous single-copy learning algorithms
Grewal et al. 2025
;
Chia et al. 2024
were established mainly for pure-state inputs,
so, taken in isolation, a lower bound based on mixed-state hypotheses could be viewed
as an artifact of the particular mixed-state construction used in the proof of the
lower bound. By contrast, combining the extension to mixed states in
Lemma
5
with previous single-copy learning
algorithms
Grewal et al. 2025
;
Chia et al. 2024
shows that the relevant
parameter is the dimension of
\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)
, rather than whether the input
state is pure or mixed. This suggests that the exponential dependence on
t
is
intrinsic to the single-copy task of identifying hidden Pauli symmetries, rather than
being a consequence of the mixed-state instances used in the lower-bound construction.
