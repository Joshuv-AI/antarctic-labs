‘I don’t think they involve me in any way’: Qualitative study of patient centrality in chronic non-communicable disease management in Ghana
Authors: Hubert Amu, Farrukh Ishaque Saah, Selasi Amu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic non-communicable diseases (CNCDs) pose a significant and growing challenge to public health systems worldwide, including Ghana. The complexities of their management underscore the critical importance of patient engagement in navigating CNCD care. Individuals with CNCDs find themselves at the forefront of their own treatment journeys and need to be empowered to make informed decisions and actively participate in their healthcare. The paradigm shift towards patient-centred CNCD care places patients at the heart of their healthcare experiences. We, therefore, explored patient centrality in CNCD care in Ghana.MethodsThis phenomenological study involved 82 adults living with CNCDs and 30 healthcare professionals accessing and providing care, respectively, at the Korle-Bu and Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospitals. Data were collected between January and May 2019 using in-depth interview guides exploring experiences and perceptions of empowerment and involvement in CNCD management, guided by an adapted Wagner’s Chronic Care Model. Reflexive thematic analysis was employed to analyse the data using ATLAS.ti V.7.5.7.ResultsPatient centrality was inadequately practised during routine CNCD management, with predominantly low levels of patient empowerment and involvement reported. Most patients were not informed about their conditions and healthcare professionals were at times reluctant to engage with patients’ questions. A few who felt empowered noted education on management options and were invited to choose their preferences. Similarly, patient involvement in management decision-making was limited, with most patients feeling uninvolved in decisions relating to medication and exercise regimens and their changes. A few, however, noted being consulted on changes to medication.ConclusionsHealthcare professionals and patients recognise the importance of the centrality of the patient in CNCD management, but their understanding of how and the extent of patient centrality differs. Patient empowerment through information sharing, education, and participation in care decision-making is key to achieving optimal involvement, and treatment protocols should acknowledge patients as partners to encourage collaborative discussions between patients and healthcare professionals.

Introduction

Chronic non-communicable diseases (CNCDs) represent a significant global health challenge, responsible for a substantial proportion of annual deaths worldwide.1 According to Hajat and Stein,2 one in three people worldwide suffers from one or more CNCDs. Low and middle-income countries (LMICs), including those in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), are undergoing an epidemiological transition characterised by a shift from communicable diseases to chronic, non-communicable diseases.1 3 Research indicates growing burdens of CNCDs in SSA, including diabetes, cardiovascular diseases and mental health disorders.4 5 Despite this burden, treatment coverage remains critically low, fewer than 1 in 10 people with diabetes in LMICs receive guideline-based comprehensive treatment.6 A pooled analysis of national surveys across 100 countries found that attainment of global diabetes management targets remains far below what is needed, particularly in LMICs.7 SSA is projected to experience a significant increase in CNCD-related mortality.3 Most adults in the region are exposed to at least one risk factor for CNCDs, such as tobacco use, unhealthy diet or high blood pressure, according to SSA’s CNCD risk factor surveillance over the past decade.8

The UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and the WHO Global NCD Action Plan 2013–2020 highlight global recognition of the need to reduce NCD-related mortality, with a target to reduce their mortality by 30% by 2030,9 but many SSA countries remain underprepared.10 Health systems will need to be prepared to handle the evolving patterns of disease burden to meet these targets; however, based on the action plan’s NCD policy indicators, countries in SSA lack the necessary policies in place to help them do so, with most systems underdeveloped, disjointed, under-resourced and incapable of meeting the growing NCD load.11 Evidence from primary healthcare assessments in LMICs confirms that ambitious disease-targeting policies are often unsupported by the necessary technical, administrative and financial resources, and that patients consistently receive limited disease-specific information.12 Treatment hurdles that are now present will become more noticeable if the prevalence of NCDs rises.11 13 Consequently, patient-centred care has been a key paradigm shift in improving management outcomes for CNCDs.

Patient engagement plays a pivotal role in shaping healthcare outcomes and fostering collaboration between patients and healthcare professionals. Active patient engagement improves outcomes, resource use and satisfaction while reducing hospitalisation and poor self-care. Engaged patients experience higher satisfaction levels while proactively managing their health, leading to better outcomes and quality of life.14 Engagement fosters a sense of partnership, mutual respect and shared decision-making (SDM). Patients who understand their therapies are more likely to adhere to prescribed protocols.15 Chronically ill patients with low engagement levels face a higher risk of hospitalisation, emergency care, readmission and poor self-care behaviour.1618

Patient engagement is a dynamic process shaped by cognitive, emotional and behavioural factors.19 20 Beyond adherence, it reflects patients’ active role as codecision-makers in their care.19 20 Outside mere adherence, patients become codecision-makers, assuming responsibility for their well-being and contributing to care planning. Limited access to information and passive professional engagement often hinder patients’ ability to participate in CNCD management.2123

Patient information, shared care and decision-making are essential in CNCD management.14 Emana et al highlight a positive correlation between health information-finding ability and patient engagement.24 As individuals become more adept at accessing relevant health information, their likelihood of active participation in decision-making increases. Informed patients are better equipped to ask questions, evaluate treatment options and make sound decisions regarding their medical conditions.25 When patients feel empowered to make choices aligned with their values and preferences, engagement naturally follows.

In Ghana, where the burden of CNCDs continues to escalate, effective management strategies are imperative. Central to this endeavour is patient engagement. Despite the recognition of patient centrality, some individuals face barriers due to passive engagement by healthcare professionals. Consequently, making informed decisions and self-managing their conditions becomes challenging. Additionally, the extent to which patient-centrality, empowerment, involvement and engagement are achieved in CNCD management appears to be lacking. This study explored patient centrality in CNCD management within the Ghanaian context.

Conceptual framework on patient centrality in chronic illness management

Wagner’s Chronic Care Model was selected as the guiding framework because it is one of the most extensively validated frameworks for organising chronic disease management globally.26 Unlike purely biomedical models, it explicitly positions patients as active participants within a responsive health system and addresses both individual and system-level factors that shape chronic care. Its applicability across diverse health system contexts, including LMICs, makes it suitable for the Ghanaian tertiary hospital setting.26 This study, therefore, adapts the model to emphasise patient centrality in the management of CNCDs, highlighting six interrelated elements that support empowerment and involvement of patients: community engagement and patient advocacy, health system integration and patient-centred care, patient self-management and empowerment, delivery system design, SDM and patient education and empowering information systems (figure 1).

Conceptual framework.

Community engagement and patient advocacy involve mobilising communities to support people with CNCDs, promoting health literacy, reducing stigma and advancing policies that prioritise patient needs.26 Additionally, health system integration and patient-centred care focus on partnerships between patients and healthcare professionals. Care plans respect patient values and preferences, and SDM is embedded in treatment. Integration across levels of care ensures continuity and responsiveness.26

Patient self-management and empowerment build on Wagner’s model by emphasising knowledge, skills and confidence for effective self-care through education, goal setting and supportive follow-up.26 Again, delivery system design stresses flexibility in care provision. Options such as coordinated care, convenient scheduling and telemedicine allow services to align with patient needs and daily lives.26

SDM and patient education ensure that patients are informed about their conditions, treatment options and likely outcomes. Decision-support tools and education promote dialogue and enable patients to make informed choices that align with their goals.26 Empowering information systems enhance communication among healthcare professionals and give patients access to health records, treatment plans and educational resources. Patient portals and health applications encourage active participation and monitoring of progress.26

Together, these elements create a cycle of patient-centred care. Community advocacy shapes system responsiveness, while integrated services and education empower patients for self-management. Flexible delivery systems and supportive technologies strengthen engagement, allowing patients to take ownership of their health and influence wider community and policy initiatives.26

Materials and methods

Study design

The study adopted a qualitative approach, employing a phenomenological design.27 The design enabled in-depth information to be obtained from the study participants,28 29 as the study sought to understand the experiences of people living with CNCDs who use CNCD management services. The design was specifically used to address how and to what extent individuals are central to the management of CNCDs by healthcare professionals. Reporting of this study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist, provided as online supplemental file 1.

Study setting

The study was conducted at Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital (KBTH) and the Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital (KATH). These are the two biggest hospitals in Ghana and receive both primary and referral cases from all over the country. These facilities were chosen because they receive all CNCD-referred cases across the country, which offered the study the opportunity to cover extensively all major CNCDs. The two are the leading national referral centres in Ghana.

Population

The study’s target population comprised adults living with CNCDs and accessing services related to their conditions from KBTH and KATH who have been living with the CNCD condition for at least 6 months. For this study, CNCDs are defined as conditions of long duration that generally progress slowly and are not passed from person to person, including but not limited to diabetes, hypertension, stroke, cancers, glaucoma, chronic kidney disease, sickle cell disease, asthma and chronic lung disease.30 Both inpatients and outpatients accessing CNCD-related care at the study facilities were eligible for inclusion. For in-patients, only those who were very active could communicate clearly and were not in any form of pain were included. Those whose conditions had impaired their ability to understand, consent to participate and respond to the interview questions were excluded. Healthcare professionals who attended to patients with CNCDs at the hospitals were included to obtain expert views on management practices. Only those who had worked in the facility and unit for at least 6 months before the study were eligible.

Sampling

A purposive sampling technique was employed in selecting the study participants. For people living with CNCDs, three approaches were applied: (1) individuals attending condition-specific outpatient clinics (eg, diabetes at KATH and glaucoma at KBTH) were invited after their routine care; (2) during general outpatient clinics, patients presenting with CNCDs of interest were identified and recruited after consultation; and (3) inpatients were selected with assistance from nurses-in-charge and review of ward registers to identify those who met the inclusion criteria. While no strict numerical quotas were set per condition, recruitment aimed to achieve balanced representation across conditions between the two facilities, such that approximately equal numbers of patients with each condition were recruited from KBTH and KATH, respectively. The healthcare professionals were approached in their respective units during data collection, and those who consented were included. Healthcare professionals were purposively selected to represent all clinical units providing CNCD care at both facilities, including physiotherapy units (managing stroke, arthritis and related conditions), diabetes and hypertension clinics, ENT clinics (managing glaucoma and related conditions), radiotherapy and chemotherapy units (managing cancers and immune-related conditions) and dialysis units (managing chronic kidney and organ-related conditions). This approach ensured representation of the diverse professional perspectives involved in CNCD management, including medical doctors, nurses, physiotherapists and optometrists.

Overall, 82 people living with CNCDs and 30 healthcare professionals participated in the study. In qualitative phenomenological research, sample size is guided by the principle of purposive sufficiency rather than statistical power.27 The sample of 82 people living with CNCDs was determined by saturation, whereby no new themes emerged from additional interviews, while also ensuring a balanced representation across conditions and facilities. At the first facility, interviews were conducted until data saturation was achieved and then the resulting sample size per condition was matched at the second facility to ensure balanced cross-facility comparability. The 30 healthcare professionals were similarly selected to represent all clinical units providing CNCD care across both facilities.

Procedures

Data were collected using two in-depth interview guides (online supplemental file 2): one for patients and one for healthcare professionals. The guides were structured in three parts. The first section collected sociodemographic information, the second explored experiences and perceptions of patient empowerment in CNCD management and the third explored patient involvement in CNCD management decision-making. Follow-up probes were used to explore participants’ experiences, perceptions and examples in greater depth. While Wagner’s Chronic Care Model informed the conceptual framing of the study, the interview guides were deliberately kept broad rather than structured around the model’s six elements. The instruments were validated by two experts in qualitative research and CNCDs. Both interview guides were in English. To ensure linguistic clarity, consistency and cultural appropriateness, pilot interviews were conducted with six CNCD patients prior to full data collection (three in Twi and three in Ga-Dangme) by two research assistants who are public health graduates and native speakers of these languages. Feedback from the pilot was used to refine the interview guides before commencement of the main study.

Data collection took place between January and May 2019. Three trained field assistants supported the process following a 2-day training on the study objectives and instruments. Participants were interviewed face-to-face in private spaces within the hospitals to ensure confidentiality. While all interviews with healthcare professionals were conducted in English, 22 of the patient interviews were conducted in local languages (Twi and Ga-Dangme) and the remainder in English. The field assistants who conducted these interviews were bilingual native speakers of Twi and Ga-Dangme, respectively, ensuring accurate communication and cultural sensitivity throughout the data collection process. Each interview took about 40 min. Interviews were audio-recorded with permission, and field notes were taken to capture contextual details.

Data analysis

Interviews were transcribed verbatim, translated into English where necessary, and proofread for accuracy. Data were analysed thematically using a reflexive approach with ATLAS.ti V.7.5.7. The analysis began with repeated readings of the transcripts to ensure familiarity, followed by coding. Two researchers (HA and FIS) independently coded the data, applying both a priori codes derived from study objectives and inductive codes emerging from the data. The codebooks were compared and harmonised through discussion to enhance reliability. Codes were then organised into subthemes and themes, which were refined by merging, separating or discarding categories until a coherent thematic structure was established. Final themes were clearly defined, and representative quotes were selected to illustrate key findings. Although themes were generated inductively from participants’ lived experiences, Wagner’s Chronic Care Model was subsequently used as an interpretive framework to contextualise and discuss the findings, particularly regarding patient empowerment and involvement in CNCD management.

In defining themes around patient involvement, the analysis distinguished between active and passive participation. Patients were considered actively involved when healthcare professionals consulted them in decision-making, offered choices between management options and provided adequate information about their conditions. Patients were considered passively involved when their opinions were not sought in management decisions, and they were not given sufficient information about their care.

Results

A total of 82 people living with CNCDs and 30 healthcare professionals participated in the study. Of those approached, 23 patients (10 at KBTH and 13 at KATH) declined participation citing reasons of lack of interest, discomfort at the time of data collection or lack of time. The majority were nurses (66.7%), held first-degree qualifications and had between one and 5 years of practice. A comparative majority of the professionals (46.7%) had 1–5 years of practice. Table 2 shows that the people living with CNCDs were evenly split between KATH and KBTH, with over half (51.1%) aged 60 years or older and 57.3% female. Most patients were married, Christian, and had at least some formal education, though nearly 1 in 10 had none. Thematic results

Table 3 presents patient centrality in CNCD management in two broad themes: patient empowerment and patient involvement in decision-making. The emergent themes of patient empowerment and patient involvement in decision-making align most closely with three of Wagner’s six framework elements: patient self-management and empowerment, SDM and patient education and health system integration and patient-centred care. While the framework also encompasses community engagement and patient advocacy, delivery system design and empowering information systems, these elements were not prominently foregrounded in participants’ accounts and therefore did not emerge as distinct themes. This reflects the inductive nature of the analysis, in which themes were derived from participants’ lived experiences rather than imposed from the framework. It shows that while most people living with CNCDs reported low empowerment, few felt empowered to contribute to their care choices. Most participants also described passive involvement in their care.

Patient empowerment in CNCD management

Patient empowerment in CNCD management refers to the extent to which patients are supported to participate actively in decisions about their care and to engage in self-management. In this study, participants described varied experiences of empowerment. While some reported receiving adequate information and involvement in decision-making, most indicated limited empowerment in their care. Two subthemes emerged: low empowerment and good empowerment.

Low level of empowerment

Several participants expressed frustration at not receiving clear explanations from healthcare professionals. Many people living with CNCDs described limited empowerment due to poor communication, with some reporting that their diagnoses were recorded only in their files and never explained to them. Attempts to ask questions were often discouraged, as healthcare professionals perceived this as challenging authority, leaving patients excluded from decision-making. Confusion about medication use and lack of clarity on disease progression further undermined patients’ confidence and contributed to low involvement in their care. The following quote illustrates this experience.

Okay! I have not asked the doctor what is wrong with me, and he has also not told me anything about my condition. Everything is in my file… When the nurses look inside the file, they give me medications to take.

–KATH, Diabetes/hypertension comorbid patient, Male, 40–49 years

Others felt discouraged from asking questions, as healthcare professionals perceived this as overstepping boundaries. A male patient from KBTH posited;

I don’t think they (healthcare professionals) involve me in any way… anytime you try to ask questions, some of them will perceive it as going beyond your boundaries, and then they would start getting angry and behaving in some way.

–KBTH, Prostate cancer patient, Male, 40–49 years

A recurring challenge was a lack of clarity about medications. Some patients struggled to differentiate drugs and dosages, leading to confusion and potential non-adherence:

…The medicines are too many; I am unable to see or know which one is for what or which one to take in the morning and which one to take in the evening. As for the pressure (hypertension) medicine, I take it in the evening. But for the stiffness, I don’t know its medicine.

–KATH, Diabetes/hypertension comorbid patient, Female, 60–69 years

Healthcare professionals acknowledged these gaps, noting the need to demonstrate drug use and explain prognosis:

…Some of them don’t see the need to use their drug, and then even the timing. So today, for instance, I was telling them how to use the drug. I demonstrated how to use the drugs.

–KBTH, Nurse, Female, 30–39 years

Some of the patients do not appreciate the stage of their condition. For example, with stroke management, …once the patient reaches a plateau, there is nothing much you can do to improve the condition… But some of the patients don’t understand these things. They will keep you asking questions like “Why am I not improving, or why is this hand not working?”

–KBTH, Physiotherapist, Female, 40–49 years

Good level of empowerment

Some participants described experiences of genuine empowerment, where healthcare professionals provided clear information, sought informed consent and involved patients in treatment planning. Patients valued collaborative decision-making, respect for autonomy and education that enabled them to manage their conditions more confidently. These practices fostered trust and active participation in care, as illustrated below:

I first asked them what they thought of my opinion, and the madam even said my opinion was also good. And the madam taught me another one, which helped my hand.

–KATH, Stroke/hypertension comorbid patient, Male, 30–39 years

When the doctor comes around, he asks me about whatever problem my eyes give when I go home, or he asks me if there has been any other problem with my eyes.

–KATH, Glaucoma patient, Male, 60–69 years

This was corroborated by some of the healthcare professionals:

…We involve them in terms of treatment planning and prognosis. We make them aware of what is happening and the options that we have. We seek what is best for them, and we seek their opinion. Sometimes, because they cannot come three times a week because of financial problems, we adjust. The goal is to make them comfortable and help them understand the treatment and likely outcomes.

–KBTH, Physiotherapist, Female, 40–49 years

At the first assessment, we explain what we are going to do and educate them on the process. …Even with the subsequent visits, the patient’s consent is key. If a patient comes and says, “Today I’m not in the mood or not feeling well,” we suspend the session. …Usually, we discuss with the patient that, as for today, this is what we are going to do. So once the patient is okay with it, we go ahead with the therapy.

–KBTH, Physiotherapist, Female, 30–39 years

Patient involvement in CNCD management

Patient involvement refers to the extent to which individuals participate in decisions about their care. In this study, most people living with CNCDs described being only passively involved, with limited opportunities to influence management decisions. A few, however, reported more active participation in treatment planning.

Passive involvement of participants

Most patients reported being only passively involved in their care. They were usually not consulted on treatment decisions, medication changes or lifestyle recommendations, but rather received instructions from healthcare professionals. While some acknowledged that these directives were useful, many expressed dissatisfaction and wished their opinions had been considered. They noted:

No! They just let me go and see the doctor. …He will write some medicines for me. After that, they only told me I could go and come back when the medicine was finished. They don’t ask me to choose any medicine or the kind I want.

–KATH, Diabetes patient, Female, 60–69 years

No, they don’t ask me such things (management options).

–KATH, Stroke patient, Male, 70–79 years

No, I have not asked or said anything (an opinion about the treatment) before. They just let me go and see the doctor, and then he will write medicines for me.

–KATH, Diabetes/hypertension comorbid patient, Female, 60–69 years

…Nobody here has discussed anything concerning what food I should eat or any medicine with me. …They just told me to go and do it.

–KATH, Stroke/hypertension comorbid patient, Male, 30–39 years

Active involvement of participants in care

A few patients and healthcare professionals described practices that reflected active patient involvement. This occurred in three main ways: open communication where patients could share experiences and preferences about medications; a holistic approach that considered age, work and individual circumstances in planning care; and collaborative goal setting where patients were educated, consulted and involved in decisions about their treatment. These practices allowed patients to feel heard and to contribute meaningfully to their care.

Discussion

This study explored the practice of patient centrality in CNCD management from the perspectives of people living with CNCDs and healthcare professionals in two tertiary hospitals. The findings highlight predominantly low levels of patient empowerment and involvement, though a few instances of good empowerment and active participation were noted. These results raise important questions about how healthcare systems support or constrain patient-centred care in CNCD management.

Patient-centred care is a broad organisational and philosophical approach to healthcare that extends beyond individual empowerment and involvement. It encompasses respect for patients’ values and preferences, coordination and integration of care, provision of information and education, physical comfort, emotional support, involvement of family and friends, and continuity and transition of care.31 32 While empowerment and involvement are critical dimensions of person-centred care (PCC), they represent only part of what patient-centred care requires in practice. In the context of CNCD management, achieving genuine PCC demands systemic commitment, embedding patient partnership into institutional culture, clinical protocols and health system design rather than leaving it to the discretion of individual healthcare professionals. Our findings suggest that even these two dimensions of PCC remain inadequately realised in Ghana’s tertiary CNCD care settings, underscoring how far the broader PCC agenda remains from full implementation.

Patient empowerment involves providing patients with the knowledge, skills and confidence necessary to participate effectively in their care.3335 The literature emphasises patient empowerment as a crucial aspect of CNCD management. Our study found that patients with CNCDs experience varying levels of empowerment, echoing the findings of previous research.36 37 Some were frustrated at the lack of clear explanations from healthcare professionals, leading to feelings of exclusion from decision-making. This is consistent with studies indicating that inadequate information and communication barriers hinder patient empowerment.36 38 Additionally, the negative attitudes of healthcare professionals towards patient involvement, as observed in our study, have been identified as barriers to empowerment in other studies.3941 However, this is in contrast to the finding of a review study that noted that generally, healthcare professionals had favourable attitudes towards patient empowerment and involvement.23

Conversely, our study also identified instances where patients felt empowered when provided with comprehensive information and support. This aligns with the literature, which suggests that patient empowerment is associated with improved health outcomes, enhanced patient satisfaction and reduced healthcare costs.35 42 Moreover, empowering patients through education and self-management strategies has been shown to facilitate long-term disease management and improve patients’ ability to cope with uncertainties associated with chronic diseases.38 43 Beyond individual outcomes, patient empowerment has been identified as a mechanism for improving patient safety, empowered patients are more likely to question care decisions, identify errors and contribute to safer care environments.44 From a health systems perspective, empowerment also contributes to greater efficiency, with empowered patients demonstrating better treatment adherence, reduced emergency care utilisation and lower overall healthcare costs.45

The challenges and facilitators of patient empowerment identified in this study underscore the complexity of CNCD management and the need to address both patient-level and system-level factors. Achieving empowerment requires a shift towards patient-centred care, where healthcare professionals promote active engagement through effective communication, education, self-management support and SDM.353746 47 At the same time, systemic barriers such as cultural, educational and socioeconomic disparities must be tackled to enable long-term patient involvement.36 46 Empowering patients as active partners in their care, as advocated by the WHO, has the potential to improve health outcomes, reduce the burden of chronic diseases and advance health equity.38 46 Tailored interventions, such as patient education, collaborative goal-setting and involving patients in service design, are, therefore, essential to strengthening CNCD management.47 48

This study revealed low patient involvement, with most patients reporting they were not consulted in the management of their conditions and largely accepted a passive role. This may reflect patients’ perceptions of themselves as subordinates to healthcare professionals and their fear of being labelled as difficult if they asked questions or made suggestions.14 49 Some patients may also prefer a limited role in decision-making.50 At the same time, healthcare professionals’ limited engagement could partly stem from concerns about patients’ limited health literacy and their ability to make informed choices about complex treatments. However, even in such cases, it remains essential that patients are given clear explanations of their management options, so they can make informed contributions. These findings mirror the broader Ghanaian healthcare culture, where patients are often regarded as passive stakeholders in care decisions.

SDM, a collaborative process in which clinicians and patients work together to make healthcare decisions that align with the patient’s values and preferences, remains aspirational rather than routine in Ghana’s CNCD care context. Our findings mirror those from other LMICs, where hierarchical patient–provider relationships, limited health literacy, time constraints and resource-scarce environments collectively constrain the practice of SDM.22 51 52 In Ghana specifically, the dominance of a biomedical, provider-directed model of care, combined with cultural norms that position healthcare professionals as unquestioned authorities, creates structural and attitudinal barriers to SDM that go beyond individual provider behaviour.5355 Similar patterns have been documented in other SSA countries, where patients’ deference to medical authority and fear of being perceived as difficult inhibit active participation in care decisions.5658 Addressing SDM in Ghana, therefore, requires not only provider training but also broader cultural and systemic shifts that reposition patients as legitimate partners in the clinical encounter.

Several factors may hinder patients’ willingness or ability to actively participate in CNCD management, including resilience, the nature of their conditions, healthcare professional attitudes, task demands and healthcare setting-related barriers.50 The WHO recommends that patients be encouraged to ask questions, voice concerns and engage in developing strategies for their care to improve understanding and use of relevant tools.14 Promoting patient involvement, therefore, requires systemic changes such as SDM frameworks, comprehensive patient education and improved communication. Evidence shows that active patient participation enhances physical functioning, adherence to preventive measures, treatment compliance and overall health outcomes.40 43 Incorporating patient preferences into decision-making also fosters safer self-care behaviours, greater adherence and fewer avoidable readmissions.59 Interventions that prioritise clear information and collaborative planning can strengthen patients’ perceived control and empower them to manage their conditions more effectively.

The conceptual framework guiding this study highlights patients’ active participation within the healthcare delivery system, emphasising that management actions should reflect patients’ cultural backgrounds, values and levels of comprehension.26 Ideally, this would ensure that patients are included in decision-making and have opportunities to voice their preferences. However, our findings indicate that patient roles remain largely passive, reflecting a gap between the framework’s ideals and actual practice. This disconnect may stem from systemic issues within the health delivery system, including entrenched paternalism among healthcare workers and the high workloads that limit opportunities for meaningful patient engagement.14

The challenges identified in this study are not unique to Ghana but reflect broader structural features of health systems in LMICs that constrain the implementation of patient-centred care. Underfunded health systems with high patient-to-provider ratios, inadequate infrastructure, fragmented service delivery and limited investment in health information systems create conditions in which PCC becomes structurally difficult even when healthcare professionals are motivated to practise it.12 In such contexts, the burden of chronic disease management falls disproportionately on patients and families, often without adequate information, support or involvement in care decisions. Health system strengthening efforts in LMICs must, therefore, explicitly incorporate PCC principles, including patient empowerment, SDM and community engagement, into health system design rather than treating them as add-ons to clinical care. Without structural enablers, individual-level interventions to improve patient centrality will remain insufficient.

Overall, the findings underscore the multifaceted nature of patient centrality in CNCD management. The gap between the conceptual framework’s ideals of active, culturally responsive participation and the observed reality of predominantly passive involvement highlights both systemic and interpersonal challenges. Barriers such as limited information provision, negative professional attitudes and structural constraints call for targeted improvements in practice. Strengthening patient education, enhancing communication and fostering a culture of respect and partnership can help bridge this gap. At the same time, the examples of active involvement observed in this study demonstrate that patient-centred care is achievable. Building on these promising practices will be crucial for repositioning patients as active partners rather than passive recipients in the management of CNCDs.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its inclusion of both people living with CNCDs and healthcare professionals, which enhanced the breadth of perspectives and strengthened the validity of the findings. It is also one of the few studies to provide insights into patient centrality in CNCD management within Ghana’s health system, offering practical implications for clinical care and policy. However, the use of purposive sampling may have introduced selection bias, limiting the representativeness of participants and the generalisability of findings. Additionally, while education level and other socioeconomic characteristics were captured for all patient participants, the qualitative phenomenological design did not allow for a systematic comparison of perspectives across sociodemographic subgroups. Future mixed-method studies should explore how socioeconomic factors, particularly education level, shape patient involvement and empowerment in CNCD management in Ghana and similar settings. Also, responses may have been influenced by social desirability bias, particularly in relation to sensitive aspects of patient–provider interactions.

Conclusions

This study highlights persistent gaps in patient empowerment and involvement in CNCD management in Ghana, with most patients remaining passive participants due to inadequate information, limited communication and hierarchical professional attitudes. Nonetheless, instances of active patient involvement demonstrate that patient-centred approaches are feasible when healthcare professionals foster respectful communication, SDM and tailored education. Strengthening patient centrality in CNCD care aligns with global priorities under SDG 3: Good Health and Well-being and the Universal Health Coverage agenda, which emphasise equity, participation and people-centred care. Embedding patient empowerment into routine practice will ensure that healthcare systems in Ghana and similar contexts can improve CNCD outcomes while advancing global commitments to sustainable and equitable healthcare.
