Oral anticoagulation and left atrial appendage closure in dialysis atrial fibrillation: between stroke prevention and bleeding hazard
Authors: Simonetta Genovesi, Toshiki Kuno, Jürgen Floege, Carmine Zoccali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAtrial fibrillation (AF) is frequent in dialysis patients and associates with a substantial increase in stroke, cardiovascular events, and mortality, yet the net clinical benefit of oral anticoagulation (OAC) in this setting remains uncertain. Dialysis patients were excluded from pivotal vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) and direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) trials, and observational data are conflicting. The PRO position argues that in carefully selected patients with very high thromboembolic risk and acceptable bleeding risk, thromboembolic prophylaxis—pharmacological with OAC or non-pharmacological with left atrial appendage closure (LAAC)—can reduce stroke and possibly mortality. This view is supported by several cohort studies suggesting lower ischemic events and improved survival when OAC is maintained, and international normalized ratio control is good, and by emerging data that LAAC may offer similar stroke protection with less bleeding and lower mortality than OAC or no prophylaxis. The CON position stresses that many large studies show no clear stroke reduction and consistently higher major bleeding with warfarin versus no OAC, that small randomized controlled trials of DOACs versus VKAs reveal very high absolute bleeding without clear efficacy gains, and that VKAs may aggravate vascular calcification. Both positions agree on the need for individualized, shared decision-making and for adequately powered randomized trials comparing OAC and LAAC with no specific therapy in dialysis AF.

INTRODUCTION

Atrial fibrillation (AF) in dialysis patients is a central unresolved problem in cardio-nephrology. In the general AF population, large randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have established that oral anticoagulation (OAC) confers a clear net clinical benefit in patients with elevated Congestive heart failure, Hypertension, Age ≥ 75, Diabetes, Stroke (doubled) (CHADS2) and the Congestive heart failure or left ventricular dysfunction Hypertension, Age ≥ 75 (doubled), Diabetes, Stroke (doubled)-Vascular disease, Age (CHA2DS2VA) scores, and this is reflected in current European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/Heart Rhythm Society (ACC/AHA/HRS) guidelines [1, 2].

By contrast, patients on dialysis were systematically excluded from the pivotal trials of vitamin K antagonist (VKAs) and direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) [1, 2]. Yet AF is common in this population and is consistently associated with higher all-cause mortality, cardiovascular death, and stroke compared with dialysis patients without AF [3–5]. At the same time, dialysis patients have a high baseline risk of major bleeding—around 4%–5% per year or more in large cohorts—due to uremic platelet dysfunction, frequent heparin exposure, and comorbidities [6].

Consequently, guideline recommendations for dialysis patients with AF are weak, stress individualized decisions, and acknowledge that the net clinical benefit of anticoagulation is uncertain [1, 2]. The debate polarizes between two positions. The PRO position, articulated by Simonetta Genovesi, argues that thromboembolic prophylaxis—pharmacological with OAC and non-pharmacological with left atrial appendage closure (LAAC)—can reduce stroke and possibly mortality in carefully selected dialysis patients with AF, and that the real question is not “OAC yes or no” but “thromboembolic prophylaxis yes or no.” The CON position, presented by Toshiki Kuno, cautions against routine OAC use, emphasizing the lack of RCTs testing OAC in AF patients on dialysis, the substantial bleeding burden and possible long-term harms of VKAs, and the potential role of non-pharmacologic strategies and aggressive risk-factor control [7].

PRO: IN FAVOR OF THROMBOEMBOLIC PROPHYLAXIS, INCLUDING ORAL ANTICOAGULATION AND LEFT ATRIAL APPENDAGE CLOSURE, IN DIALYSIS PATIENTS WITH ATRIAL FIBRILLATION

In non-dialysis AF, cardiology guidelines recommend OAC for almost all patients with a CHA₂DS₂-VA(Sc) score ≥2 because anticoagulation substantially reduces ischemic stroke risk and improves survival [1, 2]. Dialysis patients with AF are at least as vulnerable: they have a high AF prevalence and incidence and a consistently higher risk of stroke, cardiovascular events, and death than dialysis patients in sinus rhythm [3–5]. On the face of it, these patients appear to be prime candidates for stroke prophylaxis.

The difficulty is that their bleeding risk is also greatly increased. In international cohorts of patients with kidney failure, major bleeding occurs at rates of roughly 4%–5% per year, even before systemic OAC is considered [6]. Because robust RCTs comparing OAC with no OAC in dialysis AF are lacking, clinicians must rely on observational data and small RCTs using surrogate endpoints. A trial comparing the efficacy and safety of VKA versus no treatment in patients with kidney failure, AVKDIAL (NCT02886962), was discontinued due to under-recruitment. Three RCTs are currently underway comparing warfarin-VKA (DANWARD, NCT03862859), apixaban (SACK, NCT05679024), and VKA or edoxaban (VISIONAIRE, NCT06402851) with no OAC. The three studies started in 2019, 2023, and 2024, respectively, and are still in the enrollment phase. The expected completion dates are January 2027, December 2028, and December 2026. While awaiting the results of these trials, we should ask ourselves whether it is correct to deny thromboembolism prophylaxis to patients with kidney failure and AF because the risk of bleeding outweighs the benefit.

Evidence for and against OAC in dialysis AF

Over the last two decades, multiple cohort studies and registry analyses have examined VKAs (phenprocoumon, warfarin) and, more recently, DOACs in dialysis AF. Their findings are heterogeneous but not uniformly negative. In a study by Olesen [8], VKA treatment was associated with a decreased risk of stroke or systemic thromboembolism among patients with kidney failure on dialysis, without an increased risk of bleeding. Similarly, in hemodialysis patients with incident AF, VKAs use was marginally associated with reduced risk of ischemic stroke, with a signal toward reduced mortality [9]. Reduced all-cause and cardiovascular mortality were also observed in a study by Genovesi [10]. Furthermore, in another study [11], VKAs were associated with lower all-cause mortality and ischemic stroke, without significantly increasing the risk of bleeding in hemodialysis patients with AF. As to DOACs, the use of apixaban vs no OAC soon after newly diagnosed AF went along with lower risks of ischemic stroke, a composite thromboembolic end point, and all-cause mortality but higher rates of clinically meaningful bleeding [12]. Shah et al., however, found that VKA therapy did not prevent stroke and increased the risk of bleeding [13].

The contrast is well illustrated by two nationwide Korean studies using similar administrative data. Kim et al., employing propensity score matching to balance OAC users and non-users, observed significant reductions in ischemic stroke and all-cause mortality, without increased major bleeding, among patients treated with OAC [14]. In another analysis, Park et al., using Cox regression with non-users as the reference group, reported higher thromboembolic events and mortality in VKA-treated patients than in non-users. [15] These discrepancies likely reflect differences in analytical approach, unmeasured confounding, and background cardiovascular care, rather than a simple binary truth about OAC.

A key methodological issue is treatment persistence and quality. In routine practice, only a minority of dialysis AF patients ever receive OAC, and more than half of those initiated on VKA discontinue within the first year. Bleeding is an important reason, but so are labile international normalized ratio (INR), comorbidities, and clinical uncertainty. In studies that distinguish between “ever-treated” and “continuously treated” patients, persistent OAC use is more often associated with lower thromboembolic risk and, in some cohorts, lower mortality, compared with no OAC or early discontinuation [9–11]. This suggests that, at least in selected patients where OAC can be maintained and monitored, there may be a real benefit.

For VKAs in particular, the quality of INR control is critical. In hemodialysis patients with AF, higher mortality. and bleeding are closely linked to time spent outside the therapeutic range, whereas patients with stable, well-controlled INR experience fewer hemorrhagic events and better survival.[16] Because many observational datasets lack granular INR information, analyses may overestimate the inherent risk of warfarin and underestimate the impact of poor management.

DOACs have been used increasingly in advanced CKD. Network meta-analyses suggest that, in AF in dialysis patients, DOACs as a class offer stroke prevention similar to VKAs, with different bleeding profiles. Apixaban has attracted particular interest because of lower renal clearance and pharmacokinetic data indicating acceptable exposure in hemodialysis patients. It has been reported that apixaban 2.5 mg twice daily in patients on hemodialysis yields drug levels similar to those observed with the standard dose (5 mg twice daily) in patients with preserved kidney function [17]. Available studies generally suggest that apixaban has non-inferior safety and effectiveness outcomes compared to VKAs in AF patients on dialysis [18]. On the other hand, apixaban at standard dose was associated with significantly lower risks of stroke/systemic embolism and death as compared with either reduced dose apixaban or warfarin in a large study based on an outcome-analog propensity score [19]. A meta-analysis by Kuno [20] showed that apixaban and no OAC carried a significantly lower risk of major bleeding compared to warfarin, dabigatran, and rivaroxaban. However, an RCT by Pockorney [21] had inadequate power to draw any conclusion regarding rates of bleeding comparing apixaban and warfarin.

From the PRO perspective, the totality of observational evidence does not support a categorical “OAC never” stance. Instead, it points to a situation where, in some dialysis AF patients, particularly those with very high thromboembolic risk and manageable bleeding risk, carefully managed OAC can reduce stroke and possibly mortality, whereas in others the bleeding risk or frailty clearly outweighs potential gains.

LAAC as a non-pharmacologic alternative to OAC

Thromboembolic prophylaxis in AF is not limited to pharmacological measures. In non-valvular AF, most thrombi originate in the left atrial appendage. LAAC has therefore emerged as a non-pharmacological alternative to systemic anticoagulation. In a study in kidney failure patients with AF, LAAC was safe and effective and associated with reduced mortality compared with OAC or no therapy [22]. In patients with preserved kidney function, RCTs and pooled analyses show that LAAC is non-inferior to OAC for stroke prevention, with the prospect of fewer long-term bleeding events in patients at high risk [23].

In dialysis and advanced CKD, observational and registry-based studies suggest that LAAC is feasible but more procedurally demanding than in non-CKD populations. Meta-analyses show higher peri-procedural complications, including vascular and pericardial events, and more early bleeding episodes in patients with kidney failure as compared to patients without [24]. However, when longer-term outcomes are examined, the picture is more favorable. Analyses of Medicare claims data comparing the outcomes and safety of OACs with LAAC among individuals with kidney failure and AF found that LAAC was associated with improved survival and reduced bleeding risk [25].

In a recent systematic review and meta-analysis, LAAC in patients with AF and kidney failure was associated with a low frequency of thromboembolic events and bleeding when compared with standard OAC and no treatment. Overall mortality was moderate and primarily attributable to underlying comorbidity rather than the procedure itself [26]. Similar results were registered in another meta-analysis based on 28 studies (including three randomized controlled trials) that enrolled 144 630 AF patients on dialysis. In this study, the risks of thrombotic events in any DOAC and VKA were comparable to no OAC, whereas LAAC was associated with a lower risk of thrombotic events [27]. In a national database including 14 849 patients with kidney failure on dialysis and AF, in propensity-matched analyses, the composite outcome of stroke or systemic embolism, major bleeding, and all-cause mortality was higher in those treated with apixaban or warfarin compared with those who underwent LAAC [28].

While these are largely not randomized data, they point to LAAC as a promising option for patients at simultaneously high thromboembolic and high bleeding risk, or with contraindications to long-term use. A practical unresolved issue is the optimal post-procedural antithrombotic regimen. Many centers prescribe a short course of dual antiplatelet therapy or reduced-dose OAC after LAAC, and most bleeding complications cluster in this early phase. Determining the minimal effective regimen in kidney failure remains an important research priority.

Risk stratification

Risk prediction tools such as CHA₂DS₂-VASc/CHA₂DS₂-VA(Sc) and HAS-BLED were not developed in dialysis populations. Nonetheless, recent systematic reviews suggest that stroke prediction models retain acceptable discriminative performance in AF patients with kidney disease, including those on dialysis, even if calibration in the extreme risk range is imperfect [29]. Bleeding scores perform less well, and clinical factors such as prior major bleeding, labile INR, concomitant antiplatelet therapy and frailty must be weighed carefully.

Patients with previous stroke or systemic embolism, or with very high CHA₂DS₂-VA(Sc) scores, are those most likely to gain from effective prophylaxis. The algorithm starts from stroke risk CHA₂DS₂-VA(Sc) and clinical factors and bleeding risk, then considers the feasibility of high-quality INR control in the case of VKAs, availability and suitability of DOACs, and candidacy for LAAC. Patients with extreme bleeding risk, limited life expectancy or severe frailty are directed toward conservative management without prophylaxis. In contrast, patients with very high thromboembolic risk and acceptable bleeding profiles may receive (VKAs or DOACs) or LAAC, with the choice guided by patient preference, logistical considerations and multidisciplinary discussion.

This approach reframes the question: rather than asking “yes or no?” in isolation, it asks “in this individual patient, is any form of thromboembolic prophylaxis justified, and if so, which one is safest and most effective?”

CON: AGAINST ROUTINE ORAL ANTICOAGULATION IN DIALYSIS PATIENTS WITH ATRIAL FIBRILLATION

From the CON standpoint, the core problem is the combination of very high bleeding risk and a lack of randomized data showing that OAC improves outcomes compared with no prophylaxis in dialysis AF. Dialysis patients have major bleeding rates of 4%–5% per year or higher, depending on comorbidities and concomitant medications [6].

The trials that established the benefit of VKAs and DOACs in AF excluded dialysis patients [1, 2]. This means that the strong net clinical benefit seen in the general AF population cannot be simply extrapolated to kidney failure. Acknowledging this, both ESC and ACC/AHA/HRS guidelines assign only weak (class IIb) recommendations to in dialysis, underscoring that the benefit–risk balance is unknown and decisions must be highly individualized.

Observational data failed to show a clear reduction in ischemic stroke with VKAs versus no AF in hemodialysis patients [13]. Several large cohorts report neutral or even higher stroke rates among warfarin-treated patients, together with consistently higher rates of major bleeding [13, 20].

VKAs may also promote vascular calcification and calciphylaxis by blocking Matrix Gla Protein, a vitamin K-dependent inhibitor of vascular calcification, which is particularly relevant in kidney failure, where calcific vasculopathy is common and devastating.

Experience with DOACs is not reassuring enough to justify routine use. These drugs started to be used in CKD and in kidney failure patients at a time when no studies about their safety and efficacy in these populations were available [31]. Before 2020, no randomized data existed for DOACs in dialysis AF, but observational analyses suggested that VKAs, dabigatran, and rivaroxaban were associated with significantly higher bleeding risk compared with apixaban and no anticoagulant [20]. Although apixaban has shown a more favorable bleeding profile than warfarin in kidney failure in Medicare data, and may be associated with lower mortality, a randomized trial comparing apixaban and VKA in patients with AF on hemodialysis with long follow-up, no differences were observed in safety or efficacy outcomes [32]. In this trial 1-year stroke and systemic embolism rates were similar in the two arms, whereas major or clinically relevant non-major bleeding occurred in about one-third of patients, without a statistically significant safety advantage for apixaban.

The Vitamin K Antagonists in Renal Disease and Vascular Calcification (VALKYRIE) Study compared low-dose rivaroxaban (10 mg once daily) with a VKA and found fewer life-threatening or major bleeding events with rivaroxaban, but no clear benefit in ischemic stroke or systemic embolism [33]. Taken together, these RCTs suggest that some DOAC regimens may be at least as safe as, or safer than, warfarin, but they do not demonstrate a net benefit of OAC per se compared with no OAC.

From a mechanistic perspective, several features of kidney failure further undermine the case for routine. Repeated systemic heparinization during hemodialysis increases bleeding risk and may already contribute to partial stroke prophylaxis. Dialysis patients often have subclinical vitamin K deficiency [34]. Uremic platelet dysfunction, frail vasculature, and frequent invasive procedures amplify the clinical impact of even modest degrees of over-anticoagulation.

LAAC, as a non-pharmacologic alternative, may, in the long term, strike a better balance between stroke prevention and bleeding risk. As noted in the PRO section, observational and registry data in dialysis AF suggest that LAAC is associated with fewer major bleeding events and lower mortality than OAC, with similar or lower stroke rates [26–28]. However, LAAC in kidney failure carries higher peri-procedural risks, and th e need for at least short-term antithrombotic therapy after implantation means that early bleeding remains a significant concern. Thus, while LAAC is attractive, the evidence in dialysis patients remains limited and largely non-randomized.

Across these studies, absolute bleeding risks are consistently high in anticoagulated patients, reductions in ischemic stroke compared with no treatment are inconsistent or absent, and no strategy has yet demonstrated an unequivocal net clinical benefit in robust randomized comparisons.

Given this evidence, the CON position holds that routine OAC in dialysis patients with AF is not justified. Apixaban, although widely used, is approved in this setting mainly on the basis of pharmacokinetic studies in a small number of hemodialysis patients and extrapolation from non-dialysis cohorts [17]. Until adequately powered randomized studies show that a specific regimen yields a clear net benefit over no OAC, the default strategy in many dialysis AF patients should be to avoid routine OAC. Instead, efforts should focus on optimizing modifiable cardiovascular risk factors, individualizing dialysis and hemodynamic management, and considering LAAC only in carefully selected patients at centers with adequate expertise.

MODERATORS’ CONCLUSION

Both the PRO and CON perspectives agree on the basic facts: AF is frequent and prognostically adverse in dialysis patients, and associated with increased risk of stroke, cardiovascular events, and death. At the same time, dialysis patients have a high intrinsic bleeding risk, related among others to repetitive heparin exposure, subclinical vitamin K deficiency and uremia-associated hemostatic abnormalities. There are still no large, definitive RCTs comparing any regimen with no OAC in dialysis AF, so the true net benefit or harm of OAC remains unknown.

Where the positions diverge is mainly in how to interpret the available observational and limited randomized evidence, and how much weight to give to potential benefits versus well-documented harms. The PRO view stresses that in selected high-risk patients—particularly those with previous stroke or very high CHA₂DS₂-VA(Sc) scores—observational data suggest that OAC can reduce thromboembolic events and possibly mortality, especially when treatment is maintained, and anticoagulation quality is good. It also emphasizes that LAAC appears promising in dialysis AF, with emerging data showing lower thromboembolic events, fewer bleeding events, and lower mortality compared with VKA therapy or no prophylaxis, even though peri-procedural risks are higher in kidney failure.

The CON view highlights that many large observational studies and meta-analyses have failed to demonstrate stroke reduction and have consistently shown higher bleeding and, at times, higher mortality with VKAs versus no treatment, and that even with DOACs, absolute bleeding rates remain very high. It underscores that RCTs comparing DOACs with VKAs in dialysis AF are small, underpowered, and lack a no-treatment arm, and that none have established a net clinical benefit of OAC compared with non-anticoagulant strategies.

In practice, a rigid “always” or “never” policy is unlikely to be correct. A more credible way forward is a genuinely individualized, shared decision-making approach informed by the best available data and structured guidance, such as the EuDial algorithm (Fig. 1). This requires careful assessment of stroke risk [CHA₂DS₂-VA(Sc) and clinical history], bleeding risk (including previous major hemorrhage, frailty, and INR instability), technical feasibility of LAAC, life expectancy, and patient values. For some patients—particularly those with extreme bleeding risk or limited prognosis—no long-term prophylaxis will be appropriate. For others, especially those at very high thromboembolic risk, a trial under close surveillance or referral for LAAC evaluation may be justified, preferably within or aligned with prospective registries and clinical trials. However, a significant problem of Fig. 1 is that many patients fall in between the relatively severe categories shown on top and end up in a grey zone, such as old, frail patients who fall repeatedly, patients on concomitant low-dose aspirin, and patients with Erythropoietin Stimulating Agent-hyporesponsiveness.

Whenever possible, nephrologists and cardiologists should collaborate to enroll dialysis patients with AF into ongoing and future randomized studies comparing DOACs with no OAC and LAAC versus pharmacological or no prophylaxis. Only such trials can definitively resolve the current equipoise. Until then, clinical judgment, transparent communication with patients, and multidisciplinary collaboration remain essential. And finally we should remember that not treating a condition can be difficult to defend but causing iatrogenic harm is definitely worse.
