Microsurgical clip ligation of pericallosal, anterior choroidal, and 2 middle cerebral artery aneurysms through simultaneous transsylvian and interhemispheric approaches: illustrative case
Authors: Mustafa Motiwala, Eva M. Wu, Earl D. Jones, Nickalus R. Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDThe presence of multiple intracranial aneurysms is not infrequent. Surgical strategies to manage this condition vary, including staged procedures and multiple craniotomies.OBSERVATIONSA 40-year-old female presented with headache and syncope and was found to harbor 4 aneurysms involving the pericallosal, anterior choroidal, and bilateral middle cerebral arteries. Given her young age and the presence of multiple aneurysms across distinct vascular territories, microsurgical clip ligation via a single incision and dual craniotomies was selected over endovascular treatment.LESSONSDual ipsilateral craniotomies through a single incision can provide safe and effective corridors for microsurgical treatment of multiple aneurysms across distinct vascular territories. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE2633

Multiple intracranial aneurysms are identified in 14%–34% of patients and occur more frequently in women. Their presence is associated with conditions such as autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, atherosclerosis, and arterial fenestrations.1,2 Higher rates of coexisting aneurysms are observed in the distal anterior cerebral artery (ACA) and pericallosal regions. Microsurgical management may require multiple craniotomies through a single incision, a strategy previously described but not illustrated in a case report with operative video.1–4

Illustrative Case

The patient is a 40-year-old female who presented to the emergency department with headache and syncope. She had a past medical history significant for a patent foramen ovale. As seen in Fig. 1, CT angiography showed 2 right middle cerebral artery (MCA) aneurysms as well as a distal ACA aneurysm. Formal digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was performed that showed 4 separate aneurysms: 2 MCA aneurysms, 1 distal ACA aneurysm, and 1 anterior choroidal aneurysm. The patient was allowed to recover for 3 months while being worked up for a hypercoagulable disorder and undergoing a course of anticoagulation therapy. The rationale for the procedure included a patient with multiple aneurysms, the patient’s young age, and her previous strokes from her cerebral angiography study as well as potential hypercoagulable disorder. The risk of the procedure included bleeding, infection, seizures, CSF leakage, cranial neuropathy, stroke, vascular injury, and death. The benefit was the reduction of the risk of rupture of the multiple aneurysms over her lifetime. The alternatives for treatment included endovascular treatment with flow diversion or coil embolization, microsurgical treatment with clip ligation, or observation. Microsurgical clip ligation was chosen due to the patient’s embolic stroke as well as the multiple aneurysms that were amenable for treatment through a single approach. The setup included a Mayfield head holder, operative microscope, microsurgical instruments, aneurysm clips, micro-Doppler ultrasonography, indocyanine green angiography, and neuronavigation in order to tailor the craniotomies. Neuromonitoring was not utilized as it was not believed to be necessary. Positioning involved a more neutral turn of the head compared to a standard anterior circulation aneurysm clipping (Fig. 3). A single pterional flap incision was slightly extended to cross the sagittal sinus. As seen in Video 1, the key surgical steps included a pterional incision followed by a right pterional craniotomy with a right-sided sylvian fissure split, identification of the MCA and anterior choroidal aneurysms, and clip ligation of these aneurysms. The order of aneurysm treatment in the pterional corridor followed a superficial to deep approach. These steps were followed by an interhemispheric approach through a bifrontal craniotomy with splitting of the interhemispheric fissure, identification of the distal ACA aneurysm, and ligation of the distal ACA aneurysm (Video 1).

The patient had complete aneurysm occlusion on postoperative angiography with preservation of all outflow and parent vasculature (Fig. 4). She remained neurologically intact. Ultimately, she was seen in the outpatient clinic and was started on anticoagulation therapy for her hypercoagulable disorder at her postoperative follow-up visit. Discussion

Observations

The technical considerations of this case included dual ipsilateral craniotomies through a single incision in order to treat all 4 aneurysms through the same surgical sitting. They also included the positioning of the patient and the use of neuronavigation in order to tailor the craniotomies especially in the interhemispheric corridor. As seen in Table 1, surgical treatment of patients with multiple aneurysms through one incision and two craniotomies can be associated with favorable outcomes. With a case series of 12 patients, Burkhardt et al.1 deemed this approach a “squeeze play” craniotomy and determined that it was safe to treat multiple unruptured aneurysms in a single-stage surgery. Dunn et al.3 described 9 patients with aneurysms in lateral and axial locations treated safely in a single-stage operation using separate pterional and frontal craniotomies through a single incision with excellent outcomes. Furthermore, Choque-Velasquez et al.2 reported a case of a 58-year-old woman who underwent a single-stage operation for clipping of 7 intracranial aneurysms that was safely and successfully performed. Finally, Hong et al.4 wrote of 2 cases involving patients who underwent clipping of multiple aneurysms via keyhole approaches and a single incision and showed favorable outcomes. Notably, these publications did not feature surgical footage to illustrate these techniques. Lessons

To our knowledge, this is the first report in the literature that includes an operative video to depict the clipping of multiple aneurysms through dual ipsilateral craniotomies and one incision. The patient had no complications, suggesting that this method is a safe and effective procedure for surgical treatment of multiple aneurysms in distinct vascular territories. Limitations included the lack of long-term follow-up, but at the time of this writing, no adverse outcomes were noted.
