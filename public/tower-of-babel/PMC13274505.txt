Coaching in undergraduate and graduate medical education: a scoping review of success factors, barriers, and outcomes
Authors: Binata Mukherjee, Jenna Pfleeger, Haidee Custodio, Maria Renee Roca, Gurupreet Khalsa, Clista Clanton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeWanting to implement a sustainable learner coaching program in academic medicine and prior exposure to coaching initiatives raised questions, including planning, implementation and assessment, and resource allocation. This scoping review examines coaching across medical learners, reported program elements, success factors, and barriers.MethodsFollowing the PRISMA-ScR guidelines, which were informed by Levac et al. on Arksey and O’Malley’s framework and Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis, PubMed, Scopus, and ERIC were searched through May 2024. Articles were screened, the data were extracted and coded. The mixed methods analysis included descriptive statistics and thematic analysis. Themes/domains were developed and refined through consensus.ResultsOf 801 articles screened, 141 were included in the analysis. Coaching publications in academic medicine have increased, peaking in 2023 (n = 20). Most articles focused on competency-based coaching (n = 91), followed by learner wellness (n = 26). Coaching was more frequently studied in GME (n = 92), with overlaps in UME (n = 64) and faculty (n = 43). Six domains emerged: overview/general, coaching program related, coaching process related, coaching outcomes, coaching success factors, and coaching barriers. The articles reflected a broad conceptualization of coaching in academic medicine. Coaching programs varied in structure but commonly aimed to support competency, wellness, interpersonal skills, and self-directed learning. Reporting on coach recruitment, training, and compensation was inconsistent. Coaching processes incorporated reflection, goal setting, and observation. The coaching outcomes encompassed skills development and personal and professional growth. Success factors included institutional support and coach training; barriers involved time constraints, role ambiguity, and coachee readiness.ConclusionsCoaching is increasingly used in academic medicine to address diverse and evolving learner needs across settings, within the United States and internationally. This review reveals the conceptual breadth and structural variability of coaching programs and the flexibility of coaching as a practice for individualizing learner success. The findings offer insights and serve as a resource for institutions seeking to develop, implement, or refine coaching initiatives.

Introduction

Coaching is a process [1] in which coachees are inspired to maximize their potential through facilitation and inquiry, sometimes alluded to as developmental coaching [2]. Coaching also entails reaching performance outcomes benefiting coachees [1] and ultimately the organizations in which they work. Essentially, coaching is about changing a behaviour [3]. The use of coaching has a long-standing history in sports, music, and business, where it has traditionally been used for learning [4], performance improvement [4], and personal growth [4,5]. The increasing complexity of educating undergraduate and graduate learners within a time-constrained curriculum, focusing on competency-based medical education (CBME) [6], self-directed learning (SDL) [7], and learner wellness [8], has propelled educators to explore coaching to support learning and growth. Consequently, coaching has grown in popularity in medical education over the past few decades, particularly in surgical specialties, where it has been shown to improve technical and non-technical skills [9,10]. Despite this growing popularity, best practices for coaching in medical education are unclear.

The nuanced [1] interpretation of coaching has likely contributed to challenges in evaluating coaching programs' true impact on learner development. Although coaching shares some similarities with more traditional relationships, such as mentoring, precepting, advising, counselling, teaching, and giving feedback, coaching is distinct [11,12]. These concepts, particularly mentoring, advising, and giving feedback, have been used interchangeably with coaching in the medical literature, leading to confusion and difficulty in accurately characterizing coaching interventions, synthesizing research findings, and establishing the best [12–14] practices. Unlike other professional relationships that may involve directive guidance or problem-solving, coaching assumes that the coachee is their own expert [15], self-reflective [13], and able to find learner-centred [12] solutions. In addition, studies have shown that coaching in medical education can positively impact both learners and faculty serving as coaches [16–18].

There has been an increase in institutional coaching programs and scholarly interest in coaching in academic medicine. However, the literature includes limited high-quality studies, with reported outcomes being mostly self-evaluated or self-reported, and a lack of validated tools for programmatic evaluation [13]. Thus, it is not obvious how coaching is defined, implemented, or assessed across the continuum of undergraduate medical education (UME) and graduate medical education (GME).

In response to these gaps and with the aim of informing coaching program development at our own institution, we conducted a scoping review of the literature on coaching in academic medicine. We chose to perform a scoping review because this type of evidence synthesis includes multiple study types and identifies gaps in the literature, which can inform future research on the topic [19]. Our objectives were to discern current practices of coaching learners across the UME and GME, identify reported program elements, success factors, and barriers. This scoping review will highlight opportunities for program development, coach training, and future research.

Method

Institutional review board approval was not required for this scoping review. We adhered to the steps of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [19]. The PRISMA-ScR flow diagram can be found in Figure 1, and the checklist can be found in Supplementary Appendix 1. Further, this review is supported by Levac’s update of Arksey and O’Malley’s work on scoping review methodology [20]. Data extraction was guided by the scoping review research questions. Synthesizing the extracted data was guided by Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis (Figure 2) [21]. We elected not to register the protocol. Consensual agreement was achieved among team members during each stage of the research through an iterative process [22].

Reflexivity

The team brought various perspectives to the scoping review, arising from their diverse professional backgrounds, personal experiences, and different levels of exposure to coaching. For example, it became apparent during data extraction that clinical members of the team considered professionalism and professional identity formation (PIF) to be overlapping, whereas non-clinical team members did not. We engaged in discussion to challenge each of our perspectives and develop a consensus while acknowledging that complete objectivity is unrealistic. BM, a certified leadership coach, has developed and led an academic medicine coach training program at her institution, now in its fifth year. She coaches medical students, residents, and faculty. She is involved in a leadership curriculum for undergraduate medical students and graduate business students. CC, a medical librarian and the library liaison for the College of Medicine (COM) and the health system, participated in the coach training program. GK teaches graduate courses in qualitative research and has research experience in coaching. HC, JMP, and MRR are clinician educators for medical students and residents, and participated in the coach training program.

Formulating the research question

Through group discussion, we developed our research questions and eligibility criteria utilizing a modified PICOS framework [23]. The questions were then further refined during the process of piloting the data extraction tool. The following questions guided our inquiry: (1) How is coaching for undergraduate and graduate learners being used in academic medical centres? (2) What elements of academic coaching programs are being reported in the literature? (3) What factors contribute to success? and (4) What are the barriers to success?

Identifying relevant studies

CC conducted searches in PubMed (1946–present), Scopus (2000–present), and ERIC (Education Resources Information Center, 1966–present). A comprehensive search strategy utilized both controlled vocabulary Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keywords such as undergraduate medical education, graduate medical education, coach, coached, coaching, and mentoring. Since we wanted to capture the whole gamut of medical education, including UME/GME, coaching programs, and coach training, we were intentional about using the term education instead of students, residents, and fellows. The first search was conducted for English-language, peer-reviewed articles published from the inception of each database to November 18, 2020, and updated on May 15, 2024. We decided not to include a grey literature search. The full search strategy is included in Supplementary Appendix 2. Selected articles compiled by the research team prior to the database searches were also included. Research team members reviewed article bibliographies to identify articles not included in the searches, and also consulted with subject matter experts to identify additional relevant articles. The articles were imported into Rayyan, a platform for managing systematic and other reviews, for screening and selection [24].

Article selection

The articles selected for inclusion were qualitative, quantitative, or mixed methods primary studies, secondary research syntheses, and narratives (designated as ‘other’) on academic coaching related to competency-based learning (acquiring skills), professionalism/professional identity formation, and wellness in UME or GME learners. We also included articles on faculty development related to coaching learners, but excluded those that focused on other aspects of faculty development and faculty/learner coaching of patients. While the articles were restricted to the English language, we included studies from any country. We excluded articles that focused on coaching in other health science professions (allied health, nursing, dentistry, veterinary medicine). Also excluded were articles that primarily described activities related to role modelling, supervision, preceptorship, advising, sponsoring, counselling, mentoring, or giving feedback, unless coaching was also integral. Opinion/perspective pieces and commentaries were excluded. Articles on the coaching of patients were excluded. Articles were also excluded if the study population was faculty or attending level physicians, unless they pertained to coach training for UME or GME coaching activities. Each article title and abstract was independently assessed by two reviewers (BM, HC, JMP, and MRR) using the identified inclusion criteria for eligibility. The research team met regularly to discuss the process, answer questions, and clarify issues. CC and GK helped resolve article selection discrepancies during these team meetings. Records that met the inclusion criteria were loaded as full-text articles into a Google shared drive for review.

Data items, data charting, and recording process

Data items selected for extraction were developed through discussion. The items collected were article characteristics, including country, institution, Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) region, article research question(s), and purpose(s) of the coaching program for all articles. If an article was marked for inclusion, additional data items collected were: study time frame, methodology (qualitative, quantitative, mixed method, secondary research, or other, with branching logic to further describe each category), study population (UME, GME, and faculty), size of study, intervention (type, duration, implementation, and comparator), outcomes as reported by authors, factors for success in academic coaching programs, and barriers to success in academic coaching programs. The data extraction sheet is available in Supplementary Appendix 3. The data extraction sheets for five articles were piloted independently by four team members (BM, HC, JMP, and MRR). In the full-text review stage, each article was independently assessed by two reviewers (BM, CC, HC, JMP, and MRR) for inclusion in the final data set using our data extraction sheet designed in the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) platform [25]. Branching logic on the data extraction sheet helped streamline the full data collection process for the articles marked for inclusion. The research team (BM, CC, HC, JMP, and MRR) piloted 20 articles each to ensure that the charting process was consistent and reliable, and subsequently finalized the form. Three team members were each assigned a third of the articles to chart, and two members were assigned half the articles, thus ensuring that each article was blindly reviewed by two team members. After completion of the data extraction process, the team met frequently to resolve conflicts on article inclusion in the review. The steps undertaken by the research team ensured the trustworthiness of the collected data. A critical appraisal of the sources of evidence was not performed, as the intention of this review was to report what the authors of the selected articles stated on the topic.

Synthesis of results

This mixed methods analysis included enumerating the characteristics of the included articles, such as the purpose of coaching, study population, and publication year (Figure 3), and a thematic analysis [21] to address the scoping review research questions. Codes were identified using an inductive approach. All identified codes, along with the respective text segments, were compiled and arranged into themes. The themes were aggregated into code groups and reviewed iteratively and consensually. A data dictionary of the code groups was created and further modified throughout the data analysis. The code groups were later assigned to domains (Figure 4) that were linked but not restricted to the research questions only. To ensure that the themes identified were relevant to the research questions, a random selection of themes was cross-checked with the articles.

Consultation

To increase the rigour of the study and to ensure that we identified the relevant literature, BM consulted with coaches who are also educators and/or researchers in academic medicine within the AAMC and the researchers’ group at the Institute of Coaching [26] (n = ~25 coaches). She presented the review process and early findings, asking for input regarding potential areas of improvement/refinement to strengthen the research synthesis. While many articles focused on performance improvement and development of skills, there was inconsistency among the reviewers regarding the inclusion of articles focused on surgical skills. After this consultation and based on their input, we broadened our original interpretation of skills beyond SDL/self-regulated (SRL)/master adaptive (MAL) [7,27,28] learning to include surgical skills.

The number of articles published on coaching in academic medicine is increasing, beginning in 2016 (n = 11). The greatest number of articles in our study range was published in 2023 (n = 20). Articles on competency-based coaching (n = 91) exceeded that of other categories, followed by wellness for learners (n = 26). There were more articles encompassing the different purposes of coaching in GME (n = 92, including overlaps with UME and faculty), followed by UME (n = 64) and faculty (n = 43). The greatest number of articles was published in the United States (n = 85), with the Northeast Group on Educational Affairs (NEGEA) in the AAMC region representing the highest number of articles published (n = 34) (Figure 3).

We identified six domains: overview/general, coaching program related, coaching process related, coaching outcomes, coaching success factors, and coaching barriers. Each of the domains represents a set of aggregated code groups and themes derived from multiple individual codes (Table 1).

Thematic analysis of included articles

The ‘overview/general’ domain pertains to the code groups definitions of coaching, article research question(s), and the number of schools offering coaching programs (Figure 4). Striking was the lack of inclusion of a definition of coaching in most of the articles. One systematic review of 35 articles in the surgery literature identified only 17 studies that defined coaching [138]. A wide range of concepts is represented by the term ‘coaching’ (Table 1). The three types of research questions most represented in the articles reviewed focused on learner skills/competencies [72], development of the coach [37], and understanding and application of coaching in medical education [125].

‘Coaching program related’ domain includes the code groups program history, coaching aim, coaching topic, program description, program duration, funding source, number of coaches, number of coachees per faculty, coach recruitment, coach selection, coach training, and coach compensation. The themes of wellness and resilience, interpersonal/psychosocial skills, and learning orientation, or facilitating the process of becoming self-directed and self-regulated learners, were commonly occurring coaching aims. We originally combined learning orientation with competency-based skills development for grouping coaching aims. During coding, the theme learning orientation emerged as distinct from performance/competency-based coaching.

The most commonly reported method of coaching was in groups [113]. Additionally, we observed instances of in-person [128], remote [54], synchronous [113], and asynchronous [61], including written coaching [53]. The duration of coaching varied depending on expected outcomes, with the majority lasting less than one year [123], including unscheduled [132] coaching, which we assigned the theme ‘seize the opportunity’ [84]. Very few articles reported funding sources for coaching programs, with some evidence of funding by the American Medical Association (AMA) [49]. The number of coaches reported in the programs ranged from 1 to 57 [39], with one to three coaches being the most common [103]. The coachee–coach ratio most reported was 6:1. The recruitment and selection of coaches were inconsistent across programs, often based on an application process and coaching experience. Coach training focused on the development of coaching skills, including emotional intelligence and reflective thinking. Some articles reported program-specific training addressing institutional needs. The training duration varied widely. Interestingly, the delivery of training ranged from using newsletters and providing a guide for coaching conversations to in-person sessions. The coaches’ compensation ranged from nothing to a percentage of full-time equivalent (FTE), a fixed amount, or simply formal recognition.

The domain ‘coaching process related’ includes the code groups, number of sessions, meeting frequency, session duration, coaching model, coaching process, coach characteristics, and coaching measure. The broadly reported themes of coaching focused on coachee development and/or performance, with medicine-specific models such as R2C2 [152], and PRACTICE [138], and general coaching models such as SMART goals [161], and GROW [162] being cited. Commonly used practices include reflection; setting, refining, and attaining goals; observing the coachees; and giving feedback. Relationship-based processes were also important. The use of positive psychology and giving responsibility to coachees to determine goals and developmental plans was noted. Faculty/clinicians were most frequently serving as coaches, although people from diverse backgrounds, including subject-matter experts, were also coaches. Coaches’ demonstration of interpersonal/psychosocial skills was frequently described. The code group coaching measure did not reveal a consistency in the process used to measure coaching outcomes, with themes ranging from assessing performance in surgical skills to non-technical skills, the coaching process, and satisfaction and well-being.

The ‘coaching outcomes’ domain contains the code groups program, student, resident/fellow, and coach. The outcomes of coaching programs included themes such as skills development, personal and professional growth, and well-being. Interestingly, coaching contributed to the wellness of the coach, not just the coachee. Coaches felt that coaching positively impacted their teaching, self-awareness, self-evaluation, giving feedback, confidence, and understanding of their roles. Negative emotional outcomes for coaches, such as burnout and the inability to mitigate coachees’ challenges, were reported.

‘Coaching success factors’ and ‘coaching barriers’ include the code groups program, coaching, student, resident/fellow, and coach. Themes identified were that coaches, residents/fellows, and students valued building trusting relationships, counterbalancing hierarchy through coaching. Factors contributing to the success of coaching programs include institutional support, such as funding and dedicated time for coaching, organizational culture, and how coaches were trained. notably, success resulted from both standardizing and individualizing the coaching process. Barriers to the success of coaching programs indicate that limited time for coaching could impact outcomes. The theme of role conflict was a barrier from a program perspective if roles were not clearly defined for the coach, specifically in their understanding of coaching vs. mentoring vs. teaching. Also problematic was if a coach served as an evaluator for a coachee. GME norms [140] and the lack of resiliency skills sometimes hindered [97] the process of coaching. The attitudes, receptiveness, and readiness of coachees were reported as barriers. In several studies, unproductive coach-coachee relationships were mentioned as negatively impacting coaching results. The time factor barriers for both coaches and coachees included not having enough time for scheduling, preparation, or coaching sessions.

Discussion

The aim of this scoping review was to understand how coaching is being used and reported in the literature. Considering the richness and vastness of the findings, our intention is to collate relevant ideas pertaining to our research questions, which we discuss by focusing on selected significant findings in each paragraph sequentially. While there have been proposed archetypes [163] and models [73,152] for coaching across the medical education curriculum, especially for surgical coaching [138], we approached the literature broadly in order to extract relevant data as reported by the authors of included articles that answered our research questions.

Coaching has gained significant popularity within the last decade across the continuum of medical education in the face of significant challenges in learner development [50]. Although coaching is being included in academic medical centres, significant variations exist in how it is being integrated [106,137]. Our review did not identify the total number of institutions offering coaching programs. Although surgery [5] was the earliest adopter with a focus on skills-based coaching, the scope of coaching now encompasses a wide variety of needs among multiple specialties. In both UME and GME, coaching emphasizes the skills needed for autonomy in learning [7,27,28,148]. Our findings of success factors and barriers in coaching may provide a roadmap for coaching programs in addition to identifying systemic concerns pertaining to institutional support for coaching and the culture of medicine.

How coaching differed from feedback in medical education was raised by the coaches we consulted with. Feedback is defined as the ‘transmission of evaluative or corrective information about an action, event, or process to the original or controlling source.’ [164] Coaching is a non-hierarchical partnership between the coach and coachee in a non-judgmental safe space. The difference, based on our analysis, hinges on coachee goal-setting, self-paced achievement of goals, and reflection on progress, rather than the coach’s directions. The use of coaching skills such as building trust, listening, encouraging reflection, and providing a safe space is extremely important.

The multiple references to learning orientation (SDL/SRL/MAL) in the literature were striking, highlighting the need to teach students how to learn. Coaching in medical education is not just about the development of competencies (outcomes), but also about facilitating coachees’ ownership of their learning (process), which is a Liaison Committee on Medical Education (LCME) standard (page 8) in medical education [165]. Coaching is performed in a variety of ways to meet multiple needs; we propose the determination of a coaching definition broad enough to allow for a unified understanding that will support program development and evaluation, yet specific enough to make a distinction between coaching and mentoring/advising/feedback.

Considering the high prevalence of burnout in academic medical centres [166–168], it is not surprising that wellness was the second-highest reported type of coaching program in our analysis. Coaching for wellness was represented in both UME and GME, although in greater numbers in GME coaching programs. In both UME and GME, coaching, owing to its individualized approach, supports wellness within the complex, prescribed, and hierarchical nature of the learning environment. This further emphasizes the need for institutional support for coaching programs.

A slightly higher number of programs used group coaching than individual coaching. The lead author’s experience coaching in UME is that students seem to be more comfortable with group coaching than with one-on-one coaching, indicating perhaps a lack of readiness to be coached and willingness to be vulnerable. This observation supports our finding that the theme attitude, receptiveness, and readiness of the coachee [93] is a success factor in coaching programs. Given the wide variety of ways in which coaching is integrated and programs are implemented, it is important to identify the specific needs of learners as well as the institution’s needs and the extent of resources and support available. The lack of compensation for coaches [40] has impacted the implementation and sustainability of programs in academic medicine. According to the lead author, BM, the coaching program for medical students at our institution, MedJag Career Success, has stalled primarily because faculty were not receiving protected time or additional compensation for coaching.

Since the review provides evidence that coaching is primarily done by medical faculty [41], it is important that they be trained to transition from the ingrained professional identity and mindset of a clinician/educator/advisor/problem solver/expert to being a coach-educator. In BM’s experience of providing coach training to over 80 faculty, there is a mindset continuum from resistance to giving up control [169] of how learning happens, to allowing for learners’ self-agency and pacing. The coach-educator mindset necessitates tolerance of ambiguity. The discomfort is understandable considering the accreditation and regulatory requirements of skills-based, time-constrained medical education. Coaching in medical education should allow for the coachee’s self-directed [7] and self-regulated [27] learning. As observed by BM, the switch from a clinician mindset to a coaching mindset was noticed by some but not all faculty during the 12-week coach training program at our institution. A limited number of programs used non-medical/peer coaches to address coachees’ comfort level to speak up, corroborating the need for embodying a different behaviour in the coaching relationship. We found a few reports of developing self-as-a-coach [170] or coach identity in academic medicine coach training [170]. Although training in coaching skills was the dominant theme in the code group coach training, we found mostly vague descriptions of coaching skills development. When coaching skills development was mentioned, the skills were not necessarily aligned with effective competencies identified in the coaching literature [171]. However, pragmatic approaches to learning coaching skills, including role-playing [58], role-modelling [47], and shadow coaching were represented [38]. Consistent with our findings, BM’s experience in 5 years of coach training is that the biggest struggle for coach trainees is to step out of an advisor role and become a listener, endeavouring to partner with the learner. Since medical faculty are the main resource for coaching in the traditionally hierarchical environment of medicine, the medical coach community would benefit from more research on coach training and faculty-coachee relationships.

One of the barriers to effective coaching included learners’ lack of readiness to be coached [4,67], which could be due to individual characteristics and/or institutional culture. This indicates that a coaching program in academic medicine needs to be intentional about understanding the needs of coachees, their attitudes, and their receptiveness toward self-reflection rather than outward-focused professional development. Coaching is not for everyone, and therefore, should be voluntary for the learner. Additionally, there is such a wide variation in learner populations that it would be useful if research on coaching programs better identified learner characteristics, such as cultural differences between international medical graduates and American medical graduates, that could affect learner outcomes.

Setting, refining, and attaining personal goals were frequently documented, yet few reports showed whether or how learners were achieving their goals through coaching. The learner’s pace of goal achievement may be variable, and therefore, more appropriate for PIF, wellness, and communication skills. For more time-constrained areas such as professionalism and milestone achievement, and in light of the ‘do no harm’ premise of patient care, learning at one’s own pace is less realistic. However, this does not mean that coaching is not effective in both areas. Evaluations primarily used standardized metrics such as CBME/competency goals, which may be appropriate for surgical skills development but not necessarily for learning at one’s own pace, emphasizing that the coaching approach cannot be standardized. This is corroborated by our finding that there is little consistency in evaluation methods or the use of standardized instruments at both the program and individual levels. Thus, standardized program evaluations may be unrealistic given varied institutional needs and the individualized nature of coaching. Coaching’s goal is to embed behavioural changes that improve the personal and professional lives of coachees [3]. The validation of an instrument to measure the impact of coaching on learners, regardless of the focus of coaching, would be useful. There is evidence outside of medicine that coaching is effective for leadership development; [1] however, the culture and context of academic medicine involve varied facets, including patient care, leading care teams, medical education, producing scholarship, and meeting regulatory requirements. Specifics regarding what works in academic medicine coaching to make coaching worth the investment at both the institutional and individual levels are lacking.

The strength of coaching research is diluted because so many concepts are represented by the term coaching. The International Coaching Federation defines coaching as ‘partnering with clients in a thought-provoking and creative process that inspires them to maximize their personal and professional potential’ [172]. In medical education, the coaching definition has been proposed as ‘the art and science of facilitating sustainable change and growth of medical students and residents to realize their full potential as master adaptive learners. Key areas of change and growth include optimal learning, well-being, professional development and performance, and leadership’ [173]. Our findings reflected a wide array of coaching definitions, which were frequently and confusingly conflated with mentoring, advising, and teaching. The use of variable definitions of coaching impedes the advancement [1] of academic coaching, particularly in the areas of coach training, program implementation, and assessment.

Finding articles that met our search criteria was challenging. While ‘mentoring’ was an exclusion in our search criteria, ‘mentoring’ was used as a MeSH term in searching the Medline database because ‘coaching’ is not a MeSH [174] term, but is listed as an entry term that maps to mentoring. The MeSH definition of ‘mentoring’ highlights hierarchy in the relationship, which is contrary to coaching: ‘The art and practice of training, guiding, COUNSELING, and providing support to a less experienced, trained, or knowledgeable person’ (emphasis in original). Owing to the conflation of mentoring and coaching, we included a number of hand-searched articles on coaching that were not captured in the database searches. This conflation of terms may have resulted in missed articles, yet as the analysis progressed, no new themes emerged; we are confident that the analysis reached saturation pertaining to our research questions. The scoping project leads us to believe that a MeSH term for coaching is needed, but until a unified definition of coaching is developed and widely accepted, it is unlikely to happen.

Identifying coaching processes and practices that are effective is still needed before coach competencies in academic medicine can be established. Research linking coaching programs to physician retention, learner capabilities for self-directed learning, and patient outcomes would enhance the stakeholder buy-in needed to implement effective coaching programs. The questions of interest to us for future research include: How should coaches be trained to embody coaching practice? How long should a coaching relationship last? What aspects of coaching would positively impact learners? Are there coaching frameworks or models most appropriate for academic medicine? What is the long-term impact of coaching on the learner? How will the use of artificial intelligence impact the field of coaching in medical education?

Our study has limitations common to scoping reviews and thematic analysis. It is possible that we did not find every available article related to coaching in academic medicine that addressed our questions. The lack of a MeSH term for coaching, the use of mentoring and coaching interchangeably in the literature, and not including grey literature, may have led to missing some relevant information. Considering that we used the search term ‘education’ and not student, resident, or fellow, may have resulted in missing some articles. However, thematic analysis reached saturation, indicating adequate comprehensiveness of our findings. Despite our constant reflexivity, coding and interpretation of data are influenced by our own experiences, and therefore, it is possible that other researchers might have variations in some of the findings.

Conclusion

Coaching is being increasingly used in academic medicine to address a wide variety of complex learner needs both within the United States and internationally. This scoping review reveals the flexibility of coaching as a process and practice for individualizing learning and learner success, complementary to the medical curriculum at both the undergraduate and graduate levels. We believe our work will be useful for institutions that are considering starting a coaching program or those with existing programs.
