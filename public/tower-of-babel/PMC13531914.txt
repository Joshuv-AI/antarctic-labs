Evaluation of dietary iron exposure across iron compounds and fortification levels in Saudi Arabia: simulation modeling study
Authors: Omar Alhumaidan, Rehab Alawad, Sarah Alkhunein, Mohammed Alsaif, Faisal Albuhayjan, Maram Binwatyan, Nawaf Sharahili, Rabaa AlYumni, Abdulaziz Alsuwaylihi, Mohammed Baghdadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIron fortification is a key public health policy in Saudi Arabia, intended to improve iron intake and reduce the prevalence of inadequate iron intake. An effective policy design should support improved intake among at-risk groups while avoiding excessive intakes in other groups. This study evaluated the anticipated impact of the current fortification policy by examining its ability to balance nutritional adequacy and safety in the Saudi population. MethodsThis simulation modelling study followed the guidelines of the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations and the World Health Organization to simulate and model the impact of iron fortification. The modeled usual iron intake was determined, and the population segments with the highest and lowest intakes relative to this average were identified for benefit–risk assessment. All fortification scenarios in SFDA.FD 2539:2022 were evaluated to assess iron adequacy in low-intake groups, reduction in inadequacy prevalence, and risk of exceeding UL in high-intake groups.ResultsAt baseline, the modeled mean usual iron intake was 13.0 mg/day for males and 12.0 mg/day for females. Across the simulated fortification scenarios, intake increased, reaching up to 24.3 mg/day in males and 23.6 mg/day in females at the 60-ppm level of fortification. The prevalence of inadequate intake decreased across all scenarios. It declined from 5.99% to 0.2% in males and from 10.56% to 0.3% in females, respectively. Intake above the upper limit (UL) was negligible. It was observed only at 60 ppm, affecting 0.7% of males and 0.4% females. Based on these findings, a fortification level of 30 ppm may achieve population-level adequacy while maintaining a minimal risk of exceeding UL.ConclusionsThe simulation of fortification effects at levels prescribed by current legislation suggests that these levels may reduce inadequate intake. The risk of excess remains minimal, particularly at 30 ppm. This study provides an initial overview of these anticipated impacts.Keywords: Iron fortification, Wheat flour, Dietary exposure, Probabilistic modeling, Nutrient inadequacy, Saudi Arabia

Background

Iron is an essential micronutrient required for oxygen transport, cellular energy production, and various metabolic processes [1]. Iron deficiency is the most prevalent micronutrient deficiency worldwide, affecting approximately two billion people [2]. According to a recent estimate from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 report, iron deficiency affects 16.7% of the global population, corresponding to 16,434 cases per 100,000 people, based on age-standardized prevalence [3, 4].

Recent studies in Saudi Arabia have confirmed that iron deficiency remains a significant public health issue, particularly in high-risk groups. A 2022 systematic review of 20,900 individuals found that the prevalence of iron deficiency anemia ranged from 9.8% in young children to 75.3% in pregnant females, with a pooled prevalence of approximately one-third of the study population [5]. The National GBD estimates for Saudi Arabia in 2021 consistently showed an age-standardized prevalence of 10,748 per 100,000 people, which is one of the lowest in the Middle East and North Africa regions [6].

Iron fortification legislation is used as a public health intervention to address iron deficiency by deliberately adding iron to selected food vehicles, thereby increasing the daily iron intake of the population [7]. This approach must be accompanied by a public health impact assessment that evaluates the benefits and risks of the legislation. The goal is to support improved iron intake among segments of the population at risk of nutritional inadequacy while avoiding harm to those at risk of iron toxicity or exceeding safe iron intake thresholds [8].

Balancing risks and benefits in this context require estimating a fortification scenario that increases daily iron intake in individuals below the average requirement (AR). At the same time, it should avoid excessive intake in those with adequate or high consumption that could exceed the upper level (UL) [8].

AR and UL values serve as key reference points when assessing fortification policies. They are based on health and clinical endpoints that reflect nutrient adequacy and potential toxicity [9–11]. Specifically, the AR for iron was derived using a factorial approach. This approach estimates physiological requirements by quantifying iron losses and determining the amount of iron needed to replace them. [9]. The AR represents the average requirement across a population and serves as a cutoff point for assessing nutritional adequacy. An individual is considered at risk of deficiency if their intake falls below this threshold [10].

The establishment of a value representing the safe upper intake level of iron aims to mitigate the risk of systemic iron overload [11]. This threshold functions as a reference point for identifying potential toxicity in studies evaluating the effects of iron fortification legislation [10].

In Saudi Arabia, several standards and regulations govern wheat flour fortification, the most recent being the Saudi Food and Drug Authority’s (SFDA) technical regulation SFDA.FD 2539:2022 [12]. This regulation mandates the addition of iron, folic acid, vitamin A and vitamin B. The regulation specifies the minimum and maximum fortification levels of iron, ranging from 15–60 ppm, depending on the compound used for fortification [12].

Given the importance of iron fortification, it is essential to provide data to assess the public health impact of current policies [13]. This includes estimating the total dietary iron exposure at the national level across different iron compounds and fortification levels [13]. This assessment will support decision-makers in monitoring and improving existing iron fortification policies in Saudi Arabia.

This study quantitatively evaluated the projected public health impact of existing iron fortification policies. It modeled whether the mandated fortification levels could improve iron intake among population groups below the AR, while ensuring that exposure remains within safe limits for groups with adequate or higher intake. The analysis was based on national dietary exposure estimates and applied established reference values for adequacy and safety (AR and UL), in line with FAO and WHO guidelines [14]. Furthermore, the study aimed to identify the fortification level that minimizes the combined risk of inadequacy and excess at the population level.

Methods

Study design overview

The main components of the analytical fortification impact evaluation methodology used in this study were based on the FAO/WHO guidelines for food fortification with micronutrients [14]. The methodology encompasses several critical steps: (1) identifying the modeled usual intake of targeted micronutrients within the population; (2) determining the population subgroups most at risk of insufficient or excessive estimated intake of specific micronutrients; (3) assessing the modeled usual intake of the selected food vehicle among these high-risk groups; (4) simulating the impact of fortifying food vehicles with varying levels of micronutrients; and (5) modelling the expected prevalence of iron intake inadequacy and toxicity using the cut-point approach.

Uncertainty and variability

According to the reviewed FAO/WHO guidelines on food fortification methodology, the food balance sheet (FBS) is a possible data source for the usual intake at the population level [14]. In cases where national nutrition survey data are available, these should be used as the primary data source instead of relying on the modeled usual intake. However, owing to the absence of a national dietary survey in Saudi Arabia, this study used the modeled usual intake as the main source of data. Thus, a level of uncertainty may exist in the estimation of food consumption, and the simulation and modeling processes should be able to capture the variance and uncertainty associated with individual consumption patterns.

In this study, a probabilistic modeling approach was adopted to address variance and uncertainty. Unlike deterministic methods that use fixed inputs, the probabilistic approach accounts for uncertainty and variability by modeling inputs and parameters as statistical distributions [20]. Consequently, the outputs are presented as probability distributions, interpreting the simulated impact and accounting for data and assumption uncertainty [21].

Thus, nutrient intake, concentration, and resulting exposure were modeled as probabilistic distributions to support the fortification impact evaluation, which was performed using a five-step assessment framework.

Consequently, all variables are presented in Table 1 to correspond with the probabilistic nature of the simulation analysis performed in this study. All input parameters used in the modeling process are specified in Appendix 1. This approach was designed to incorporate uncertainty into the results and did not serve as a measure of usual intake.

Fortification specification

In Saudi Arabia, the iron fortification of wheat flour is implemented in accordance with the National Technical Regulation for Food Fortification (SFDA.FD 2539:2022) [22]. This regulation specifies the use of four iron compounds for fortification, with clearly defined minimum and maximum levels based on flour extraction rates.

According to SFDA.FD 2539:2022, iron chelate (NaFeEDTA) is permitted in both low- and high-extraction flour at levels ranging from 15 to 40 ppm. For low-extraction flour, the other three authorized compounds were ferrous sulfate (20–60 ppm), ferrous fumarate (20–60 ppm), and electrolytic iron (40–60 ppm). These values were used as input parameters in the probabilistic model to estimate dietary iron exposure and evaluate the potential impact of fortification under various regulatory scenarios.

Fortification compounds and levels were determined based on the flour extraction rate, as low-extraction flour is more refined and contains significantly less natural iron because of the removal of the bran and germ. High-extraction flour retains more of the original components of the grain, resulting in a higher iron content; however, both types are fortified with NaFeEDTA to ensure adequate dietary iron intake.

Although fortification levels were defined based on flour extraction rates, the analysis simulated all possible scenarios to ensure comprehensive coverage. This included modeling various extraction levels, compound types, and fortification concentrations to reflect the full range of potential outcomes.

Saudi market flour fortification

To determine the fortification level applied to flour products in the Saudi market, all available nutritional data from the four main Saudi flour mills were analyzed. These mills, now operated by four separate companies, were selected as they represent the primary suppliers in the market following privatization [23–26]. The iron content of fortified wheat products ranged from 15 to 30 ppm, with a median of 30 ppm [27–30]. Given that unfortified wheat contains approximately 10 ppm of iron, fortification contributes an additional 20 ppm, resulting in a total concentration of around 30 ppm in final products [27–30].

Determining the subgroups most at risk of insufficient or excessive intakes

To identify at-risk groups, a percentile-based segmentation approach was applied within the simulation framework, following the distribution presented in FAO and WHO guidelines [14]. Modeled individuals between the 2.5th and 50th percentiles were classified as at risk of deficiency, reflecting lower-than-average intake. In contrast, modeled individuals between the 75th and 97.5th percentiles were classified as at risk of excess or toxicity, reflecting higher-than-average intake.

For each segment, the total dietary exposure was calculated independently, enabling a more targeted evaluation of nutritional risk across the distribution. These exposure estimates were compared with the AR and UL of intake. The AR for iron was set to 6 mg for males and 7 mg for females, whereas the UL was set to 40 mg for both sexes [31].

The AR values used in this study assume an iron absorption of 15%. These values were derived from Allen et al., where requirements are presented under three absorption scenarios (15%, 10%, and 5%). The 15% scenario was chosen for three reasons. First, it aligns with the assumption in national specification SFDA.FD 2539:2022 [12]. Second, it fits national dietary patterns, as shown by aggregate consumption data [15–17]. Third, the iron fortification compounds in the specification have high bioavailability, with values from 0.75 to 1.2, in the higher absorption range [14, 22].

Assessing the modeled usual intake of the selected food vehicle

To model the usual intake of wheat flour, the same approach and Eqs. 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 were applied at the individual food item level. This allowed the model to calculate the baseline dietary exposure to iron from wheat flour and the average amount of flour consumed per person per day. Estimating iron intake inadequacy and toxicity using the cut-point method

In the final estimation stage, the approach changes from earlier steps. Instead of calculating indicators for each percentile and dividing individuals, overall prevalence was calculated using the cutoff point method [14, 32, 33].

In this study, the cut-point method was applied using a simple probability approach, where usual intake was modeled as a distribution and the average requirement was treated as a fixed value without assuming a coefficient of variation (e.g., 15%) [32, 33]. This approach was used for iron, as requirement distributions may be skewed across individuals, particularly among females [14, 33]

As this analysis relied on national-level aggregated data rather than repeated individual-level dietary measurements, the simple probability approach was applied. The full probability approach was not used, as it requires modeling both requirements and usual intakes as distributions based on individual-level data.

This method estimates population proportions relative to the AR and UL [14, 32, 33]. The area below the AR represents the prevalence of inadequacy, while the area above the UL represents the prevalence of excessive intake.

Based on scenario modeling, iron fortification at 15 ppm was associated with a prevalence of inadequacy of 2.05% in males and 3.6% in females. Continued fortification at 20, 30, 40, and 60 ppm was associated with progressive reductions in the prevalence of inadequacy. In males, the prevalence decreased to 1.52%, 0.86%, 0.51%, and 0.20%, respectively. In females, it decreased to 2.6%, 1.5%, 0.9%, and 0.3%, respectively. In contrast, the proportion exceeding the UL of 40 mg/day remained negligible across scenarios. No excessive intake was estimated up to 40 ppm. At 60 ppm, 0.7% of males and 0.4% of females exceeded the UL, indicating a maintained safety margin.

In contrast, the proportion of the population exceeding a UL of 40 mg/day remained negligible in all simulated scenarios. No cases of excessive intake of up to 40 ppm were found. At 60 ppm, only 0.7% of males and 0.4% of females exceeded the UL, supporting the safety margin of the fortification strategy.

The difference in these results compared with dividing the population into percentile groups in the subgroup analyses is because the prevalence analysis in this section considered the entire population across all levels. This approach contrasts with previous subgroup analyses that relied only on data between the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. The prevalence analysis included results below the 2.5th and above the 97.5th percentiles. These excess percentages were not observed within the percentile groups analyzed in Table 4, indicating that excess intake occurred only at the extreme tail of the distribution and not between the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the distribution.

Discussion

Key findings

The findings of this simulation modeling study suggest that iron fortification of wheat flour in Saudi Arabia may reduce the prevalence of inadequate intake. This was consistent across the four SFDA-approved iron compounds. In subgroup risk analysis, iron exposure relative to the AR and UL showed that inadequacy was present in the lower percentiles under baseline conditions. This was observed at the 2.5th and 5th percentiles among both males and females.

Following fortification, iron intake levels increased across percentiles in both sexes, mostly above the AR and below the UL. The simulation indicated males could achieve adequacy at 15 ppm, while females might need ≥ 30 ppm to eliminate deficiency in lower percentiles, reflecting sex-based differences in iron needs. Risk analysis showed iron exposure from the 2.5th to 97.5th percentile may not exceed the UL for either sex under any fortification scenario.

In the modeled prevalence analysis, including intake data below the 2.5th percentile and above the 97.5th percentile, the prevalence of inadequacy among females was expected to decrease from 10.56% before fortification to 0.3% at the highest fortification level (60 ppm). For males, the reduction ranged from 5.99% to 0.2%. A proportion exceeding the UL was observed only at the highest fortification level, reaching 0.4% in females and 0.7% in males. These indicate individuals exceeding the UL weren't captured in the subgroup risk analysis, as no percentile values between the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles showed iron exposure surpassing the UL. The UL for iron was based on evidence identifying gastrointestinal distress as the critical adverse effect of excessive intake [31]. These findings highlight the need for further investigation to better characterize this subgroup and assess whether intake may remain above the UL over time. This would support a more detailed evaluation of potential health impacts.

The findings of this dietary intake analysis matched iron deficiency prevalence estimates from biomarker data. A study of 3,432 adults aged ≥ 18 years (mean age: 37.7 ± 11.7 years; female: 51.2%) in all 13 Saudi Arabian regions found iron deficiency was the most common micronutrient deficiency, affecting 23.2% of participants [34]. Normal serum ferritin levels are 13–150 ng/mL in males and 30–400 ng/mL in females [34]. This estimate aligns with previous reports indicating a 35% prevalence [35]. Additionally, the higher risk of iron deficiency among females compared to males aligns with previous evidence, placing Saudi Arabia among countries with the highest anemia prevalence in reproductive-age females in the Middle East [34, 36].

Implications on the current iron policy

A simulation modeling study suggested that 30 ppm may address inadequate intake with minimal excess risk. Using a probabilistic approach, 30 ppm may ensure adequate intake without exceeding the UL for all population segments between the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. Furthermore, market assessment shows that most wheat flour products align with the 20-ppm scenario, enhancing iron adequacy, except for females at the 2.5th percentile, whose intake is 6.7 mg/day, without toxicity risk. Although this study offers an overview of the impact and intake estimates, a national survey is recommended to assess current exposure levels and verify alignment with these estimates.

Another factor influencing result interpretation is compliance rate. As this study was a modeling exercise to estimate fortification impact, it assumed 100% compliance with the requirement. This assumption estimated the policy's potential effects before implementation. However, actual compliance may differ. Future studies should use compliance data from Saudi Arabia's regulatory monitoring to estimate fortification's actual impact. Such analyses would also compare expected modeled impact with observed real-world outcomes.

Implications on iron status and anemia in Saudi Arabia

Based on a previous meta-analysis, each 1 mg/day increase in dietary iron intake may increase serum ferritin by 0.58 mg/L, scaled from a 10 mg/day reference [37].

This suggests that the modeled improvements in iron intake could correspond to potential increases in serum ferritin concentration at the population level. However, clinical outcomes such as hemoglobin levels or anemia prevalence cannot be directly inferred from intake modeling alone. Such outcomes would require population-based biomarker data obtained from national nutrition surveys to evaluate iron status according to the cut-off points adopted in the FAO/WHO guidelines [14, 38, 39].

Advantages and disadvantages of the study approach

This study utilized a probabilistic modeling framework to estimate dietary iron exposure across intake percentiles, sexes, and broad age groups. Stratified analysis helped identify general patterns of inadequacy and excess. Scenario testing across multiple iron compounds and fortification levels enhanced the relevance of the results for national regulatory planning.

Nevertheless, this study has limitations. Although stratified by intake percentiles, estimates came from national data, limiting risk identification in small or distinct subpopulations, like remote areas or specific cities, where dietary behaviors or food access differ from national averages. For instance, in Saudi Arabia, younger adults may face more iron deficiency than older individuals, a factor beyond this study's scope [34, 36].

The lack of national dietary survey data required the use of aggregated data, which may not fully capture individual variability or differences across subpopulations [15–17]. This limitation becomes more relevant when applying the model at levels below the national scale, where local variability may be greater. It may also affect estimates at the lower and upper tails of the distribution and could lead to under- or overestimation of inadequacy and excess. The AR value was selected based on population-level assumptions and may not reflect variation across age groups or sensitive populations [22]. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as an initial population-level assessment.

In addition, dietary supplement intake was not included, which may underestimate total iron exposure, particularly among individuals with higher supplement use. This may also lead to underestimation of the proportion exceeding the UL. Although supplement use is common in Saudi Arabia, available data on frequency, duration, and dosage remain limited [40], and more detailed data would improve exposure estimation in future analyses.

A limitation involves using McCance and Widdowson’s 'composition of foods integrated dataset due to the lack of a comprehensive national database for all required food items [18]. Although the dataset was modified to exclude foods not culturally relevant or available in the Saudi market, and items were adapted to better reflect the Saudi food context, identifying culturally appropriate foods involved researchers’ judgment (Appendix 2), introducing structural uncertainties. These supports using a probabilistic approach to account for uncertainty and highlights the need to develop and update the Saudi food composition database for future comparisons with this study's results.

Conclusions

This study developed a probabilistic model to assess dietary iron exposure under different fortification scenarios. It provides the first population-level estimate of the expected impact of iron fortification policy in Saudi Arabia. The model analysis suggests that the current fortification policy could improve iron adequacy for both males and females, as the risk of excessive intake remains low across the 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles. Although this study provides an initial population-level assessment of the expected impact and intake, it can serve as a foundation for designing more detailed and targeted surveys to further examine and validate exposure patterns.
