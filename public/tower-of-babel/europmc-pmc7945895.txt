The Life and Works of Gilles De Corbeil

By Stephen D’Irsay

Published in: Annals of Medical History

﻿THE LIFE AND WORKS OF GILLES DE CORBEIL
By STEPHEN D’IRSAY, M.D.
NEW HAVEN, CONN.
NO field is more fascinating in
medical academic history than the
study of the relationships be-
tween the various centers of
medical learning in the Middle Ages. One
followed the other in carrying the torch,
in dispensing long-inherited lore; and they
flourished according to the spirit which
dominated the age. When Salerno began
to decline, Montpellier already flourished
and Paris rose to leadership. The history
of Paris is full of deep interest; the timid
beginnings of the great school at Monte
Sainte Geneviève, under the archways of
Notre-Dame, and the secluded but resound-
ing halls of Saint Victor were to mark the
growth of the famous Studium when Salerno
was going slowly down.
The medical learning cultivated at
Salerno had a very direct influence upon
the new school, a very direct human
influence in the person of Gilles de Corbeil.
It was this scholar of Salerno who became
the dominating medical personality in Paris
at the end of the twelfth and the beginning
of the thirteenth century. Turning to him,
ample occasion will be given to illustrate
the Salernitan origins of some phases of
Parisian learning. I shall undertake to
sketch the life and the works of Gilles de
Corbeil,1 or Aegidius Corboliensis, archiater,
physician-in-ordinary to King Philip
Augustus (i 180-1223).2
The external facts of his life are rather
little known. He was born in the small town
of Corbeil, in the He de France, about
twenty-five miles southeast from Paris.
This place was the seat of a noble clan, the
Counts of Corbeil.3 The date of his birth is
unknown, though it can be placed in the
middle of the twelfth century. The out-
standing event in his earlier life and that
which determines his interest for us is
that he pursued medical studies in Salerno,4
the medical school of which must have
reached the acme of its reputation during
his youth.5 His master there, among others,
was Romoald,3 court physician to Pope
Clement 111. To this master, whose influ-
ence over him was lasting, Gilles dedicates
one of his works, the “De Iaudibus et
virtutibus compositorum medicaminum.”6
He mentions some other men, too, as his
teachers. We hear of Musandinus,7 also
known as Petrus de Musanda, author of the
booklet “Summula de preparatione ciborum
et potuum infirmorum secundum Musandi-
num,”8 a little work indicating the special
direction of Salerno, which was toward
prevention and dietetics. That master is
eulogized by his grateful pupil again and
again.9 There are other men besides Musan-
dinus who are discernible as Gilles’ teachers,
Maurus10 and Urso (Orso11). Of the influ-
ence of the early Salernitans, as Gario-
pontus, Petroncellus, Trotula (?), Johannes
Afflatius and Alphanus upon this belated
disciple of the sinking school we cannot say
anything. Their names are not mentioned.
On the other hand we hear of a Platearius;
to which member of the family this refers,
however, we can but guess. It is prob-
ably Matthaeus, whose “Circa instans”
(preserved together with the famous “Anti-
dotarium Nicolai”)12 formed the standard
collection of pharmaceutical material exist-
ing throughout the next centuries. That the
materia medica of Aegidius was created both
formally and contextually under the influ-
ence of this Platearius can be discerned
in these lines of the “De Iaudibus”:
Vellem quod Medicae doctor Platearius artis
Munere divino vitales carperet auras:
Gauderet metricis pedibus sua scripta Iigari,
Et numeris parere meis . . . vv. 110-113.
Of his fellow-students we know of a
certain John (Johannes Castalius), another
pupil of Musandino; this is none else but
the Cardinal Johannes de Sancto Paulo,
himself an author of a medical work,13
the very Salernitan “Breviarium” and
Papal legate in later years to France.14
Those years of study in Salerno must
have been numerous and their terminus
ante quem is the year of Romoald’s death
(in 1181).15 His more mature works, like
the “De Iaudibus et virtutibus,” were written
when he was no longer in Salerno.Gilles did
not have to witness the sack and destruc-
tion of that city16 by the Emperor Henry vi
(in 1193) which event he never ceases to
lament. Notwithstanding, the years of his
stay in Salerno gave him plenty of oppor-
tunity to absorb that Constantino-Salerni-
tan Galenism which characterizes his works,
reflects his “early Arab” learning and which
will be very evident to us after having dis-
cussed those works. Gilles’ enthusiasm for
his old school is boundless and persisting;17
however occasionally we also hear criti-
cisms which, incidentally, throw an interest-
ing light upon some features of contempo-
rary medical education. He complains about
the youth of the students of medicine
aspiring to mastership and particularly
mentions Salerno in this connection:
What a deviation from the ancient custom
and from good judgment if I may say so are the
present regulations in Salerno which tolerate
with aequanimity that immature growing plants
of physicians, undeveloped children should
obtain Hippocratic rights and endorse and
construct Machaonian laws! Far better way to
learn would be for them to be spanked; it would
be better for them to hang on the old teacher’s
lips than to ask for the honors of a chair of which
they are not worthy.18
This statement started a controversy
whether the criticism applies to Salerno
only or to a more general condition, pre-
vailing also in Paris.19 Of course, at the
time when the “De Iaudibus et virtutibus,”
the poem containing the incriminated state-
ment, was written (around the year 1195),
there were no regulations concerning the
ages of the licentiates in Paris in operation.
The Studium, as we know, was not yet
formed and organized,20 the University
was in statu nascendi.
Outside of the considerable number of
direct references, we find a quantity of
indirect indications demonstrating the
Salernitan origin of Aegidius’ knowledge.
The whole construction of his work, both
in its form and in its contents, bears the
Salernitan stamp so strongly that this in
itself would make his person and work
worth studying. The slender and mostly
intangible threads connecting the Gulf of
Amalfi with the banks of the Seine assume
in his personality a human and vividly
real existence.
After leaving Salerno, our Magister
taught for a while in Montpellier and had
there most disagreeable experiences.21
Montpellier was at that time becoming the
stronghold of the late Arabs; the learning
there was more dogmatic and there was
much opposition to the supposedly rather
empirical teaching of Salerno. It is not
easy to perceive very much difference
between the two schools, although Salerno
was under “early Arab” influence which
made for freer observation and less aprioristic
thinking. Gilles, with his enthusiasm for
Salerno, was bodily driven out of the city;
his career in the South was ended.22 Thence,
after passing the both geographically and
intellectually intermediate stage of Mont-
pellier, he went to Paris, where we see him
becoming canon of the Cathedral23 and
archiater to the King.
The royal physicians, usually called
medici regis or physici principis, were
influential dignitaries.24 They could always
approach the King’s person, enter his apart-
ments day and night, without permission.
It is not wonderful then that during this
close contact they acquired much influence
with their royal masters which procured
them impunity and rendered it possible
for them to voice their opinions freely, if
they had any to voice. That Gilles took
ample advantage of this situation and
indeed that some forms of his activity
cannot be explained otherwise, but by
being carried out under the royal shield,
we shall presently see.
The designation “ archiater,” by which
Gilles and many of his colleagues are known,
appears first in Gregory of Tours who
applies it to Reoval,25 physician to Sigebert
and to Marileif,26 the primus medicorum,
physician to Chilperic i. In Capetian times
Obison is on record27 as having been physi-
cian to Louis vi. It may be added that
other sovereigns, too, had their archiatres.28
Guilleaume de Jumieges mentions one John
of Chartres29 attached to the person of
Henry i of England. In fact, some kings
commanded both personal physicians and
surgeons-in-ordinary,30 the former always
clerics, the latter laymen. The only excep-
tion to the clerical status was to be found
in the Jews, some of whom were very
prominent physicians through all periods
of the Middle Ages, as is well known; one
of them, Sedecius,31 is heard of as medicus
to Charles the Bald. The clerical medici
all belonged to the secular clergy; the
regulars were prohibited from practicing
medicine by the Councils of Reims
(1131),32 the Lateran (second Council)33
and Tours (1163)34 which were supported
by the authority of Hugo of St. Victor.35
Although it would seem that, according
to these regulations, it could not have been
possible for Gilles to practice medicine had
he been a monk, nevertheless this remains
a controversial question.36 In many of the
early editions he appears as Aegidius Mona-
chus,37 sometimes Delphensis, at other
times “natione Graeci,” confused with
Gilles of Athens, an eighth century Bene-
dictine, and with Joannes de Sancto Aegi-
dio, a late Dominican. The confusion with
the Greek Benedictine is probably due to
an ambiguous passage in “De Iaudibus.”38
The two positions of royal archiater and
Benedictine monk seem to be incompatible;
there is strong evidence that he was closely
connected with the King and also that he
practiced the art of medicine;39 it appears
reasonable therefore to drop the designation
“Monachus.”
When Gilles commenced his activities
as a teacher of medicine in Paris, the art
as a systematized science was new in that
city.40 The great collection of disconnected
norms and rules which Gilles’ Salernitan
learning represented was consolidated into
that great body of learning which issued
from the Patristic tradition and developed
in the medical scholasticism of the
thirteenth century fully. But Gilles found
things in statu nascendi, as we have said,
and had ample opportunity to participate
in the growth of them, in the very city
which, of all others, was most destined to
become the center of all science and learn-
ing.41 He must have flourished in Paris
before the year 1198, as it was in that year
that Gilles de Paris, his poet-namesake,
finished his Carolinian epic which contains
an eulogy of the Magister.42 However, a
passage in “De signis et sinthomatibus
egritudinum” in which reference is made to
the Horatian “Dens Theoninus,”43 the false
friend’s slandering tongue, shows that,
besides such enthusiastic friends as the
poet, he must also have had enemies in
Paris. In “De Iaudibus” one Zoile is being
referred to:44 with this person the author
wishes to get even. But what the contro-
versy between them was and who this
Zoile might have been, we do not know.
There is another rancour in the Magister’s
heart, however, the object and also the
reason of which we know fairly well. His
bitter hatred against the prelate so impor-
tant in the history of the University of Paris,
Cardinal Guala di Bichiero45 is often em-
phasized. The reason for the invectives
hurled against the Cardinal is most likely
to be sought in the enmity between that
Prince of the Church and Gilles’ royal
master,46 together with the general resent-
ment of the Parisian scholars incited by the
Cardinal’s drastic rules imposed upon
them.47 Aegidius was a great patriot48 and an
ardent devotee of the rising Paris49 and the
Papal Legate’s actions could very well have
excited his feelings.
Master Gilles seemingly was one of
those who are at home in the “gentle art of
making enemies.” He must have had many
in the ranks of the regular clergy who as a
whole are fiercely denounced in the great
satiric poem “Ierapigra ad purgandos pre-
Iatos.”50 Incidentally, this is another form
of evidence for the contention that he
could not have been a religious himself.
This interesting poem was known to
contemporaries, as is witness Guilleaume Ie
Breton who mentions it in his Philippides,51
although it was lost later and even the
meaning of its title, which is rather plain,52
was not understood. It is directed against
the clergy and especially against the regular
clergy in a very violent way, denouncing
the simony, cupidity, avarice and various
other abuses observable amongst ecclesias-
tics; he pillories the debauchery and irregu-
lar life in some monasteries, reminding one
of the first assailant of monasticism in
satire, Rutilius Claudius Namatianus:
“Squalet Iucifugis insula plena viris.”53
There was always much attack on that
system by saintly people, like Raoul de
Tortaire54 and Vincent of Beauvais,55 the
former a century before Aegidius, and
unsaintly young people, witness the collec-
tion of Walter Map.55“ Thus there was
nothing original in Gilles’ satire, except
perhaps that his is one of the most
venomous attacks ever launched against
churchmen.56
It is probable that the King, whose
Gallican spirit always rejoiced in fearless
minds, protected his favorite courtier and
physician, and looked upon these attacks
with a certain glee. Gilles well understood
the courtier’s arts, he knew how to sway
his master toward his own ends and he
probably deftly used his confidential posi-
tion to promote his personal advancement.57
Outside of the material that can be
gleaned from his own writings concerning
his life and character, and occasional, rare
references like that of Gilles de Paris,
medieval records omit to pay attention
to his person. In fact, as we have seen, he
was constantly confused by scribes, early
commentators, editors and printers with
other homonymies, which shows that he
was very little known until modern times.
The medieval attitude toward personal
importance and the preservation of names
was different from that of later periods.
The works alone counted. They embodied
all that a man was worth and the world
was not concerned with his personal im-
mortality. The author merged into his writ-
ings, the precise origin of which was of
but little importance. An individual’s work
was part of the work of others; the whole
body of knowledge was a large stream to
which each contributed his share. In the
massive flow of this stream the provenience
of the individual waves is no more dis-
cernible. Later posterity, which happens to
be interested in personalities, is placed at a
disadvantage through this attitude of our
ancestors.
Our Magister probably did not survive
his patron Philip Augustus and the date
of his death must be set down in the early
twenties of the thirteenth century.
Aegidius was a learned physician, grown
up under the shadows of the Cassino monk
and brought up on sound medical tradition.
He was wandering much and spreading
joyously his learning. His teaching was
greatly enhanced by the facility and pleas-
ingness of his poetical vein. The content of
that teaching we shall discuss presently.
Here we must rest content with knowing
that all his knowledge was derived from
the Salernitan civitas Hippocratica and in
his person we find most or all of the learning
that Paris owes Salerno.
Personally he was a vain and ambitious
man. He admonishes his students often to
read his work rather than the lascivious
“Ars amatoria” of Ovid. (It seems that
Naso must have been a popular poet with
the twelfth century student—nothing unu-
sual!) But in all respects we can discern in
him the child of his age, with all the charac-
teristic naïveté, enthusiasm and eagerness.
He is a good Christian; despises the Albi-
gensian heresy;68 is moral and has a high
conception of his profession, advocating
the unselfish help of the poor; this he con-
siders the real joy in medicine. Although
ambitious, he has enough of Christian
humility to know that nothing is good that
is done without the aid of others, that no
work can be born in singleness:
Nemo sua propria nitens virtute decenter
Complet opus, cuius magno sub pondere posset
Multorum quamvis perfecta fatiscere virtus.69
His work truly is the expression of a tradi-
tion and a school and therein lies its
importance.
I shall now sketch his medical writings.60
At the opening of the thirteenth century,
we have in Paris a complete edifice of
classical learning, slowly growing in the
sign of rationalism, clothed in the scholastic
garb. To this is added the great labor of
the early Arabs, introduced by Gilles, and
possibly other vehicles too. The interchange
of men and ideas was vigorous at this time:
Robert of Sicily, duke of Normandy, Saler-
no’s Maecenas, brought some material
with him (the introduction of the famous
“Regimen Sanitatis” is attributed to him);
Adelard of Bath was probably also instru-
mental in bringing some of the Arab works
to Paris.61 However, we know that all the
Arab books used at this period were parts
of the Constantinian Corpus62 and we
equally know that this “Corpus” was part
and parcel of Salerno’s teaching which
finds its expression in the oeuvre of Gilles.
Gilles, on the other hand, entered Paris
in the early eighties of the twelfth century,
as we have seen, and it is therefore super-
fluous to assume any other channel, in
regard to Salernitan transmission.
The works of Gilles de Corbeil were all
clad in poetic form. That form was the
Leonine verse; pure hexameters interspersed
with distichs. The style of verse was
prevalent in the academic circles of the
twelfth century,63 but in scientific litera-
ture more than anywhere it was applied
in Salerno. Archbishop Alphanus,64 one of
the earliest teachers in the school, made use
of it and how well it was developed in
later centuries we can easily see from the
• “Regimen.” The form of verse, however
faulty it was prosodically, was indeed well
adapted to popularize and distribute scien-
tific writings in wide circles.
The following is the list of Gilles’ medical
works: “De Pulsibus”; “De Urinis”; “De
Iaudibus et virtutibus compositorum medi-
caminum” and “De signis et sinthomati-
bus egritudinum.” It is possible that some
minor writings can be added to these.
These didactic poems were well known
throughout the Middle Ages and some of
them enjoyed extreme popularity. The two
books “De Pulsibus” and “De Urinis”
particularly were used as textbooks, which
is indicated by their wide distribution in
time and space.65 They reappear as “Versus
Egidii” again and again; the students in
the University of Paris used them.66 The
“De Urinis” is more or less a restatement
of Theophilis (Protospatharius)67 who was
the standard uroscopic author. It is a link
in the uroscopic chain visible from Theo-
philus to Michael de Savonarola.68 “De
Urinis” (together with “De Pulsibus”)
is usually associated in early thirteenth
century manuscripts with the following
writings: Constantine’s “Viaticum” (Abu-
Jafar-Ahmed’s Zad-el-Mou^affir),69 (the
“Libri Isaac” (in Constantinian rendering:
“Libri febrium, dietarum et urinarum”),
the “Antidotarium Nicolai” and some
Galenic treatises. Greek works constantly
associated with Gilles are the “Aphorisms”
of Hippocrates, Philaretes’ “De Pulsibus”
and, naturally, Theophilus’ “De Urinis”;
Arabian works are the famous “Isagoge”
of Johannitius (Hunein-ibn-Ishaq), the “Ad
Almansorem” of Rhazes and the “Pan-
tegni.”70 In later (fifteenth century) manu-
scripts Gilles is always found in the com-
pany of his numerous commentators who
arose after the fourteenth century. The
more important ones are Gilbertus Anglicus,
Gentilis da Fulgineo (Gentile da Foligno),71
Gualterius de Agilo,72 and in the late Quat-
torcento Avenantius de Camerino.73 The
associations of the Aegidian manuscripts
are becoming more and more typical of the
full medical scholasticism and late Arab-
ism74: Avicenna, John of St. Amand, Ber-
nard of Gordon, Petrus de Tussignano’s
comments to the “Ad Almansorem” and
so forth.75 In one manuscript we find him
associated with the Salernitan Alphanus.76
It is clear from all this that the two books,
“De Urinis” and “De Pulsibus” rose to
prominence together with the classical and
early Arab authors (which, in the main,
the world owes to the African) and that
they maintained their popularity through-
out three centuries during the sway of the
Neo-Arab doctrinarism. What we call Saler-
nitan learning is after all chiefly Constan-
tinian Arabism; Gilles’ books, both in
regard to their contents and to their subse-
quent fate depended entirely on the con-
tents and fate of the Constantinian
collection.77
The other works seem to have been
far less popular, although their importance
is much higher. The “De Iaudibus et virtu-
tibus compositorum medicaminum,” closely
connected with the Salernitan stock of
materia medica to which the “ Antidotarium
Nicolai” (Praepositi) and the “Circa in-
stans” of Platearius belong and which
superseded it completely, is extant in only
one manuscript.78
The “De signis et sinthomatibus egri-
tudinum” had been considered lost for a
long period, since its description by Von
Murr.79 One part of it had been found and
described by Daremberg in 1853,80 the
whole luckily discovered by Rose in the
Amplonian collection (Erfurt) and pub-
lished in 1907.81 The “De Physiognomiis”
was originally intended to be a part of this.82
Outside of these medical works, we know
the satirical poem “Ierapigra,” referred
to in the previous section,83 with which the
list of extant Aegidian books is exhausted.
Let us say a few words concerning the
printed editions. The “De Urinis” has
been first printed by Cerdone de Windisch-
graetz in Padua, 1483.84 In printed form
it retained its tremendous popularity and
reached eight editions, besides being repub-
lished in Lyons and Venice.85 This popu-
Iarity was shared by the pulse treatise.86
The four others, however, were not printed
until the eighteenth and nineteenth centu-
ries, the treatise on materia medica (“De
Iaudibus, etc.”) being first published by
Polycarpe Leyser,87 the diagnostic works
(“De signis” and the “Physiognomies”) by
Daremberg and Rose, as we have seen.88
The “Ierapigra” was discovered only in
1837.89
Gilles’ place in the texture of learning
can be determined much better by con-
sidering the later treatises (the “Ierapigra”
of course excepted) than by a discussion
of the earliest two works, which are gener-
ally acknowledged to be but Theophilus
and Philaretes, respectively, in metric form.
The origins of the “De Iaudibus” are
to be found in the Salernitan “Antido-
tarium.”90 That “Antidotarium,” as has
been recently shown,91 is very probably
nothing but the post-Constantinian form
of the great collection of pharmaceutical
norms that prevailed in the early Middle
Ages, which existed in Salerno and were
later strongly colored by Arab influence.
These norms are to some extent preserved
in some early medieval antidotaries: the
London,92 Bamberg,93 Glasgow,94 Reich-
enau,95 St. Gall96 and Berlin97 collections,98
together with the codex of Breslau.99 In a
slow process, through long and devious
routes of Hebrew100 and Jewish-Arabic
translations, these norms, ultimately
derived from the Hippocratic Corpus,
Dioscorides, Scribonius Largus and Apu-
Ieius,101 accumulated in the south of Italy;
there they underwent thorough systemati-
zation. This was the work of several
generations of medical men, from Gario-
pontus,102 the author of a “Passionarius,”
in the early eleventh to the Platearius
family in the twelfth103 century and Nicho-
Iaus Praepositus104 who flourished when the
twelfth century turned into the thirteenth.
There is no mention in Gilles’ book of
this Nicolaus and it would be correct to
say that both authors had their roots in
that large and indefinite collection of pre-
scriptions which issued from the classics and
the late Romans and found its way to
Salerno to be enlarged upon by Salernitan
masters. It is not possible to say how much
of popular medical lore, of purely Western
provenience, enters the “De Iaudibus,”
or whether there is any connection between
it and the legendary “Macer Floridus,”105
a Western pharmaceutic collection, or the
numerous “Bestiaries” carrying much ther-
apeutic advice. But it is certain, on the
other hand, that the post-Constantinian
Salernitan sources and not the early ones
mentioned above were utilized. For there
is in it much Arab lore, such as we find in
Abu-Jafar-Ahmed’s “ Zad-el-Mou^affir”96;
the various syrups are an Arab specialty,107
similarly Sandalum, Kebuli, Zedoar and
other material that was unknown to the
West before Constantine’s work. The “ Pan-
tegni”108 (another part of the Const ant inian
corpus) contains many of the prescriptions
that we encounter in both “De Iaudibus”
and the “Antidotarium.”
Nearly all of the prescription material
in Gilles can be discovered in one of the
Pre-Salernitan collections mentioned above,
in the “Zad-el-Mousaffir,” the “Pantegni”
or in Nicholaus. We have before us the
pure Salernitan pharmacopea, in a pleasing
poetic form. The great number of emplastra,
electuaria, the Diacalamentum, Diapras-
sium, Unguentum Agrippa, emplastrum
apostolicum, and so forth are present in all
these works; even the aphrodisiac electu-
arium diasatyrion109 is contained in some
of the early collections.
Thus, this long poem110 conveys to us the
pharmaceutic lore which is Salerno’s pride.
It was through Gilles and through the
“Antidotarium” that Paris got acquainted
with that lore and it is very possible that
the priority belongs to Gilles, as the “Anti-
dotarium” was not known to him.111
Furthermore, the work of Nicolaus is not
heard of in Paris for another century.112
The “De signis et sinthomatibus egri-
tudinum”113 is based on Galenic pathology.
It will be enough to demonstrate in a few
examples Gilles’ dependence on Galen and
the early followers of Galenism. The
symptomatology of the so-called emi-
triteus114 and the pathogenesis of sincope116
are clear examples of this connection. Still
more interesting and illuminating, because
it gives an insight into the intricate develop-
ment of astrological ideas, is Gilles’ account
of the causes of epilepsy.116 Epilepsy, owing
to its mysterious character, often caused
scientific men to resort to fanciful and far-
fetched explanations117 and it is not wonder-
ful that it became a fruitful playground for
astrology. The sacra passio is due immedi-
ately to humors which overflow the brain.118
But these humors are regulated in definite
ways by the changes of the moon. Thus was
it also conceived by Gilles’ contemporary,
Johannes de Sancto Paulo.119 From where
did these two disciples of Salerno derive
this idea? It is first of all present in the
“Pantegni.”120 The sources of that book,
however, which professes to be an exposi-
tion of Galen, are not quite clear. Galen
concedes the role of the humors and
moisture in the production of epilepsy121
and there is no doubt that in this he is the
preceptor of Gilles. Ali-ibn-el-Abbas, the
author of the “Pantegni,” must have stood
under the influence of Johannitius, or
Hunein-ben-Ishaq, whose translation of the
pseudo-Galenic commentary to the “Heb-
domadai” of Hippocrates is crowded with
astrological lore.122 That author was highly
influential throughout the Middle Ages, his
“Isagoge ad Tegni Galeni”123 was partly
responsible for Galen’s continued prevalence.
There is, however, a statement in Galen
which could have served well as the starting
point for astrological speculation in regard
to epilepsy,124 and this, together with the
fact that this speculation was never sub-
merged and reappeared time and again in
the Hermetic writings of medieval Greek
astrologers,125 upon whom various Arabs
constantly relied, explains the origin of
Gilles* astrological notions quite satis-
factorily. Contrast the Galenic origin of
this vague astrology with the Hippocratic
origin128 of the empirical prognosis of the
disease.127 Nevertheless, Gilles’ statements
are probably derived not immediately from
Hippocrates, but through the medium of
Galen.128
The quatrihumoral theory of Galen is
used on every occasion.129 The action of
abundance of one of the humors, of flegma
insipidum or of salsum, is both the cause of
some diseases and serves as a basis for
diagnosis; the color of the urine,130 the
character of breathing (stertorous respira-
tion is described for instance) and the
quality of the pulse are all indications of
imperfect “digestion” and prevalence of
some humors, as is also pallor of the face,
tympanites or soporousness. Very often
the expressions themselves are literally
those of Galen.131 But this solid Galenic
foundation by no means excludes either
powers or results of acute observation, as
exemplified in the description of the apo-
plectic shock:
All the motive powers destroyed and only sup-
planted
By the motion of lungs, inspiring the air which will
help the
Heart to perform its task . . .
Senses abandon their work and the tongue, mind’s
witness, is silent,
Never revealing the secrets of soul which now hide
in seclusion,
The eye sees nought any more, the form nor colors
of all things,
Nor does the ear give hearing to voices floating
around it . . .
No perception is left of the sweetness of odors of

One of the most interesting descriptions
is the one concerning the Signa fehris
pestilentialis, the portion of the “De Signis”
unpublished by Rose. We find a rather good
symptomatology there: great thirst, pain
in the mouth, dry tongue, “splen tumi-
dus,”133 carnis tepor (défense musculaire?)
and dyspnea with polypnea.134 Much of the
influence of Aretaeus the Cappadocian135 and
of Rhazes136 is recognizable here. It is here
that the famous expression atra mors occurs
perhaps for the first time in its long history :
Quam subito solet atra sequi mors significabunt
Pestiferam, quisquis Iegis, hec sinthomata febrem.137

Personal observation is always discerni-

The short poem, “De Physiognomiis,”
intended to form part of “De Signis,”
deserves now a very brief consideration.143
This poem is a descendant of the Hermetic
literature; Gilles himself attributes his phys-
iognomical sources to Aristotle,144 Pole-
mon,145 and similarly to Rhazes.146 The
so-called Aristotelian physiognomic treatise
is now known not to be Aristotle’s work.147
There is only one reference in the writings
of the Stagirite to that subject148 upon
which probably medieval identification was
based. However, it appears that the real
source of Gilles was Apuleius149 and that of
Apuleius, Polemon the Rhetor,150 as can
readily be illustrated in a few illuminating
parallels. Rose traced some of these con-
nections and his work may be supple-
mented by establishing a connection
between Gilles, the pseudo-Aristotle and
Adamantius,151 also an author of a physi-
ognomic treatise. All these formed seemingly
a great body or coherent system of physi-
ognomic norms152 which formed part of the
Graeco-Arab tradition represented amply
in our author. The whole of the work is
characterized, as all similar writings are,
by the curious symbolism and fantastic
analogies drawn, respectively construed
from observed facts. Such an example is
given below, at the same time demonstrat-
ing the connections between Gilles,153 Pole-
mon,154 Adamantius155 and the pseudo-
Aristotle.156 These relationships can be
followed without break in the texts.157
The continuity of the traditional lore is
nicely apparent in the series of passages
relating to the physiognomic importance
and significance of the neck.158 That this
was a strongly Arabized £Iore is shown,
outside of the references to Rhazes,159 in
the presence of passages which have a
counterpart in Rhazes for instance, but
none in the Apuleian text.160 The Apuleian
text is the Western form of the Greek
(Pseudo-Aristotelian-Polemonic) tradition
which fell into the hands of the Arabs.
The Latin form apparently did not share
this fate. Gilles however, in Salerno, was
in the fortunate position to get acquainted
with both and that he availed himself of
this opportunity, this work amply brings
out in strong relief.
This kind of physiognomic literature
was very popular throughout the Middle
Ages. Treatises were composed in the
thirteenth century (e.g., the “Physiogno-
mies” of Michael Scot161) and even later.
This desire for the symbolic, for the pene-
tration of the mysterious barriers between
the body and soul which the medieval mind
so persistently craved, explains the longev-
ity of this literature. It explains also the
longevity of Gilles’ poem.
This brings us to the end of this short
survey of the sources and nature of the
Aegidian books. Their importance, to state
this again, lies not with their originality.
They are not original. Their importance
lies in two facts: first, that they represent
the Salernitan medical tradition which it
helped to perpetuate in the West; second,
that they were widely used as textbooks
and thus influenced medical thought and
practice for three hundred years. These
considerations justify the acquaintanceship
of this medieval author.
