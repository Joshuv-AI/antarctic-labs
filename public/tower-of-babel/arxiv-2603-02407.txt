Collapse and transition of a superposition of states under a delta-function pulse in a two-level system
Ariel Edery

Abstract
Under a time-dependent perturbation it is common to calculate the transition probability in going from from one eigenstate to another eigenstate of a quantum system. In this work we study the transition in going from a linear superposition of eigenstates to an eigenstate under a delta-function pulse (which acts at t = 0 t=0 ). We consider a two-level system with energy levels E 1 E_{1} and E 2 E_{2} and solve the coupled set of first order equations to obtain exact analytical expressions for the coefficients c 1 ​ ( t > 0 ) c_{1}(t>0) and c 2 ​ ( t > 0 ) c_{2}(t>0) of the final state. The expressions for the final coefficients are general in the sense that they are functions of the interaction strength β \beta and the coefficients α 1 \alpha_{1} and α 2 \alpha_{2} of the initial superposition state which are free parameters constrained only by | α 1 | 2 + | α 2 | 2 = 1 |\alpha_{1}|^{2}+|\alpha_{2}|^{2}=1 . This opens up new possibilities and in particular, allows for a “collapse” scenario. We obtain a general analytical expression for the transition probability P α 1 , α 2 → 2 P_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\to 2} in going from an initial superposition state to the second eigenstate. Armed with this general expression we study some interesting special cases. With a delta-function pulse, the transitions are abrupt/instantaneous and we show that they do not depend on the energy gap E 2 − E 1 E_{2}-E_{1} and hence on the relative phase between the two eigenstates. For specific multiple values of the interaction strength β \beta , we show that the system ends up in a definite eigenstate i.e. probability of unity. Such a transition can be viewed as a “collapse” since a superposition of states transitions abruptly to a definite eigenstate. The collapse of the wavefunction is familiar in the context of a measurement. Here it occurs via a delta-function pulse in Schrödinger’s equation. We discuss how this differs from a collapse due to a measurement.



1
Introduction

Under a time-dependent interaction
H^{\prime}(t)
a quantity of interest is the transition probability in going from one eigenstate to a second eigenstate of a time-independent Hamiltonian
H^{0}
. In such a case, the particle starts off in an initial state which is an eigenstate which could, for example, be the ground state of an atom, molecule, etc. One is however free to consider transitions where the initial state is a
linear superposition of eigenstates
. This opens up an interesting scenario where an interaction can cause an abrupt/instantaneous transition from a linear superposition of states to a definite eigenstate i.e. with unit probability. This transition could then be viewed as a “collapse”. The collapse of the wavefunction is familiar in the context of a measurement, where the wavefunction, which is a linear superposition of eigenstates, is said to collapse (basically instantaneously) to a definite eigenstate. A collapse in the context of Schrödinger’s equation would occur when a specific time-independent interaction, such as a delta-function pulse, causes an abrupt transition from a linear superposition of eigenstates to a definite eigenstate. In other words, the initial state evolves (albeit abruptly) towards a definite eigenstate. We discuss later how this collapse scenario under a delta-function pulse differs from a real measurement with an apparatus.

In this work, we study the effects of a time-dependent interaction on a two-level system consisting of two energy eigenstates of a time-independent Hamiltonian
H^{0}
. We consider transitions where the initial state is a linear superposition of the two eigenstates. A delta-function pulse acts on this initial state and causes an abrupt/instantaneous transition. The delta-function pulse is treated as a sequence of functions
q_{n}(t)
, where the pulse width gets smaller and its height gets taller as
n
increases while the area under the pulse, its integral over time, stays constant. In the limit as
n
tends to infinity,
q_{n}(t)
approaches a Dirac delta function. The
q_{n}(t)
are actually defined via their integrals and in calculations
q_{n}(t)
will typically appear in the integrand of an integral. One does not encounter any infinities in calculations because one does not take the infinite
n
limit of
q_{n}(t)
itself but of the integral. The integral yields a finite function of
n
and taking the infinite
n
limit of that function yields a finite result. Though our motivation for using a delta-function pulse is to cause an abrupt/instantaneous transition, it has recently been pointed out
[
1
]
that it also leads to one of the few quantum dynamical systems that can be solved exactly, analytically.

The two-level system we consider has energies
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
(one can assume
E_{2}>E_{1}
). The coefficients appearing in the linear superposition associated with the initial state are
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
where
|\alpha_{1}|^{2}
and
|\alpha_{2}|^{2}
are the probabilities of measuring
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
respectively before the onset of the interaction. The time-dependent interaction
\hat{H}^{\prime}
has off-diagonal elements
H^{\prime}_{12}=H^{\prime}_{{21}^{*}}=\beta\,q_{n}(t)
where
\beta
is the interaction strength and
q_{n}(t)
approaches a delta-function pulse at
t=0
in the large (infinite)
n
limit. For our purpose, the diagonal elements of
\hat{H}^{\prime}
are not required and for simplicity we set them to zero:
H^{\prime}_{11}=H^{\prime}_{{22}}=0
. One goal is to solve for the two coefficients appearing in the final state after the delta-function pulse acts at
t=0
; these are labeled
c_{1}(t>0)
and
c_{2}(t>0)
. The coupled set of first order equations that mix
c_{1}(t)
and
c_{2}(t)
can be turned into second order equations for either
c_{1}(t)
or
c_{2}(t)
separately. We will see that this can be solved exactly in the large (infinite)
n
limit i.e. for the delta-function pulse. For concreteness, our calculations are performed using Gaussians for the sequence of functions
q_{n}(t)
. We obtain
exact analytical expressions
for
c_{1}(t>0)
and
c_{2}(t>0)
as a function of the interaction strength
\beta
and the initial coefficients
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
. In appendix A, we solve the coupled set of first order equations using a different method that does not involve combining them into a second order equation. The expressions obtained for
c_{1}(t>0)
and
c_{2}(t>0)
using this method match our original expressions providing a strong confirmation of our exact analytical results.

An important physical consequence of using the delta-function pulse is that the coefficients
c_{1}(t>0)
and
c_{2}(t>0)
do not depend on the energy gap
E_{2}-E_{1}
between the two states. The coupled equations for the coefficients contain the relative phase between the two eigenstates, a term
e^{i\,\omega_{0}\,t}
where
\omega_{0}=(E_{2}-E_{1})/\hbar
. In the limit as
n
tends to infinity, which yields a delta-function pulse at
t=0
, the coefficients have no dependence on
\omega_{0}
and hence on the energy gap. The relative phase is lost in an abrupt transition. Interestingly, the interaction with the environment in the decoherence framework leads to a very rapid loss in the relative phase relationship of the original pure state. This is somewhat intriguing and discussed further in the conclusion.

Armed with the exact expressions for
c_{1}(t>0)
and
c_{2}(t>0)
we calculate the transition probability
P_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\to 2}
in going from the initial superposition of states to the second eigenstate under the delta-function pulse. The expression for the probability is
general
in the sense that they are functions of the coefficients
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
associated with the initial state. These are free parameters constrained only by
|\alpha_{1}|^{2}+|\alpha_{2}|^{2}=1
. Our general expression is used to study some interesting special cases. It is also used to evaluate cases previously studied in order to compare results. A quantity of particular interest that has been studied in the past is the probability
P_{1\to 2}
for a transition from the first eigenstate to the second eigenstate (see
[
11
]
). We can obtain this by setting
\alpha_{2}=0
and
|\alpha_{1}|=1
in our general expression. We find that our results are in agreement with previous work in the limit as the pulse width tends to zero i.e. limit of a delta-function pulse. In previous work where a pulse of finite width was considered, the energy gap appears in the expression
P_{1\to 2}
. However, we show that it disappears in the limit as the pulse width tends to zero. This confirms in an explicit and independent fashion our finding that the that the coefficients
c_{1}(t>0)
and
c_{2}(t>0)
do not depend on the energy gap
E_{2}-E_{1}
for a delta-function pulse.

Our general expressions for the probabilities allow us to explore a special transition: a “collapse” to a definite eigenstate (assume for now that it is the first eigenstate). For specific multiple values of the interaction strength
\beta
, the initial superposition transitions abruptly (collapses) under the delta-function pulse to the first eigenstate with unit probability i.e.
|c_{1}(t>0)|^{2}=1
. We show that the values of
|\beta|
that cause such a collapse are determined by
|\alpha_{1}|
and we provide a plot of
|\beta|
as a function of
|\alpha_{1}|
. The magnitude of the slope increases with
|\alpha_{1}|
and we discuss the implications of this.

When the delta-function pulse with a particular value of the interaction strength
\beta
acts on the initial superposition and causes an abrupt transition (collapse) to a definite eigenstate, say the first eigenstate, we know a priori that the energy is
E_{1}
. This is not exactly equivalent to performing a measurement with an apparatus. Though a measurement will also collapse the superposition to a definite eigenstate and yield a single energy, the energy could be
E_{1}
(with probability
|\alpha_{1}|^{2}
) or energy
E_{2}
(with probability
|\alpha_{2}|^{2}
). In other words, we do not know a priori which of the two energies we will measure and the Born rule determines the probability of each. In a measurement, “collapse” and the “Born rule” go hand in hand. For the delta-function pulse, “collapse” does not involve the Born rule. Another important difference between a collapse induced by a measuring apparatus and a collapse due to a delta-function pulse in Schrödinger’s equation is that the former is an irreversible process while the latter is reversible. We show explicitly that the collapse scenario with the delta-function pulse is reversible in the sense that one can get back to the original initial state if one uses the
negative
of the interaction used for the original collapse.

[
1
]
. The system studied consisted of a quantum dynamical model where the Hamiltonian includes diagonal elements with a delta-function pulse
V(t)
and off-diagonal elements with constant coupling. The author obtains an exact analytical solution something which is rare for systems where the energy gap follows a pulse-like profile (for analytical results in other two-level model systems see

[

). The analytical results in
[

agree strongly with those obtained numerically for short finite duration pulses and reveal criteria for suppressed transmission (i.e. criteria for no transition so that it remains in the initial state) even though the delta-function pulse does not satisfy the slowly-varying constraint on which such criteria were previously found
[
8
]
. The exact solution also provides a way to analyze suppressed transmission in more complex systems like those that are harmonically driven (e.g.
[
9
,
10
]
). The quantum dynamical model in
[
1
]
differs from ours in a few regards and the focus of the study is different. First, the energy gap varies with the pulse whereas in our case it is constant. Secondly, the coupling is constant whereas in our case it is time-dependent i.e. the delta-function pulse appears in the off-diagonal elements (i.e. as part of the coupling). These differences lead to significantly different analytical expressions for the two coefficients
c_{1}(t)
and
c_{2}(t)
of the final state. The transition probabilities studied in
[
1
]
are between two quasi-states
\left|1\right\rangle
and
\left|2\right\rangle
whereas we focus on transition probabilities between a superposition of two eigenstates and an eigenstate. In particular, if the final state is a definite eigenstate, the transition can then be regarded as a “collapse”. Other work involving transitions in a two-level system have concentrated on transition probabilities between two eigenstates (or quasi-states) and used finite pulses instead of delta-function pulses (see
[
11
]
for a review). In our work, a delta-function pulse is a physical requirement and not optional as it is the interaction that causes an abrupt/instantaneous transition.

Our paper is organized as follows. In section 2 we derive the well-known coupled set of first-order equations for the coefficients under a time-dependent perturbation. This section is naturally brief. In section 3 we solve the coupled set of equations for a delta-function pulse acting at
t=0
and obtain general analytical expressions for the coefficients
c_{1}(t>0)
and
c_{2}(t>0)
. In appendix A we derive the same expressions using a different method. We also obtain a general formula for the transition probability
P_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\to 2}
and apply it to special cases of interest. Section 4 is devoted entirely to the “collapse” scenario. Section 5 is the conclusion where we summarize and discuss our results.

2
Two-level system under a time-dependent perturbation

We begin by deriving a well-known exact expression in time-dependent perturbation theory; this will not only help establish the notation but will allow us to explain more clearly the new results we obtain. The derivation in this section will naturally be brief. We consider a two-level system where
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are eigenstates of the time-independent Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{0}
:

\hat{H}^{0}\,\psi_{1}=E_{1}\,\psi_{1}\quad;\quad\hat{H}^{0}\,\psi_{2}=E_{2}\,\psi_{2}\,.

(1)

\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are orthonormal

\left\langle\psi_{a}|\psi_{b}\right\rangle=\delta_{ab}

(2)

where
a
and
b
can take on the values of
1
or
2
. The wavefunction
\Psi(t)
(space dependence may exist but is not stated explicitly) can be written as usual as a linear combination of the two states with their time-dependent phase factor:

\Psi(t)=c_{1}\,\psi_{1}\,e^{-iE_{1}\,t/\hbar}+c_{2}\,\psi_{2}\,e^{-iE_{2}\,t/\hbar}\,.

(3)

Normalization implies that
|c_{1}|^{2}+|c_{2}|^{2}
=1. Let
\hat{H}^{\prime}(t)
be a time-dependent perturbation so that the full Hamiltonian is

\hat{H}=\hat{H}^{0}+\hat{H}^{\prime}(t)\,.

(4)

Since
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
form a complete set we can still express
\Psi(t)
as a linear combination of them except that now the coefficients
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
depend on time
[
12
]
:

\Psi(t)=c_{1}(t)\,\psi_{1}\,e^{-iE_{1}\,t/\hbar}+c_{2}(t)\,\psi_{2}\,e^{-iE_{2}\,t/\hbar}\,.

(5)

We prefer not to absorb the phase factors into
c_{1}(t)
and
c_{2}(t)
since the phase factors would be present without a time-dependent perturbation. The goal is to now solve for
c_{1}(t)
and
c_{2}(t)
by applying Schrödinger’s equation:

i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=\hat{H}\,\Psi\,.

(6)

Substituting (
4
) and (
5
) into (
6
) and using (
1
) yields the equation

\displaystyle i\,\hbar\,(\dot{c_{1}}\,\psi_{1}\,e^{-iE_{1}\,t/\hbar}+\dot{c_{2}}\,\psi_{2}\,e^{-iE_{2}\,t/\hbar})=c_{1}\,\hat{H}^{\prime}\,\psi_{1}\,e^{-iE_{1}\,t/\hbar}+c_{2}\,\hat{H}^{\prime}\,\psi_{2}\,e^{-iE_{2}\,t/\hbar}\,.

(7)

It is convenient to define

H^{\prime}_{ab}=\left\langle\psi_{a}|\hat{H}^{\prime}|\psi_{b}\right\rangle

(8)

where
H^{\prime}_{ba}=(H^{\prime}_{ab})^{*}
since
\hat{H}^{\prime}
is a hermitian operator.
By taking the inner product with
\psi_{1}
and with
\psi_{2}
separately in (
7
) and using the orthonormality condition (
2
) we obtain the following equations:

\dot{c_{1}}=\frac{-i}{\hbar}\Big(c_{1}\,H^{\prime}_{11}+c_{2}\,H^{\prime}_{12}\,e^{-i(E_{2}-E_{1})\,t/\hbar}\Big)

(9)

\dot{c_{2}}=\frac{-i}{\hbar}\Big(c_{1}\,H^{\prime}_{22}+c_{1}\,H^{\prime}_{21}\,e^{i(E_{2}-E_{1})\,t/\hbar}\Big)\,.

(10)

Equations (
9
) and (
10
) are
exact
[
12
]
; no approximations have been made. We will be using these exact equations in the next section.

3
Expressions for
c_{1}(t)
and
c_{2}(t)
after a delta-function pulse at
t=0

Consider the time-dependent perturbation

\hat{H}^{\prime}=\hat{V}\,q_{n}(t)

(11)

where
q_{n}(t)
is a sequence of functions labeled by the positive integer
n
and depends on time only. The operator
\hat{V}
is time-independent so that the time-dependence of
\hat{H}^{\prime}
stems from
q_{n}(t)
only. The operator
\hat{V}
acts on the time-independent states
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
. The functions
q_{n}(t)
are well-behaved functions that have a maximum at
t=0
. They have the following properties:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}q_{n}(t)\,dt=1\quad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}

(12)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}q_{n}(t)\,f(t)\,dt=f(0)\,.

(13)

The first property (
12
) states that, regardless of the value of
n
, the “area” under the curve is always unity. The second property (
13
) applies to any well-behaved function
f(t)
. Note that the infinite limit in (
13
) is taken after the integral is performed; the integral is a function of
n
and finite. When the infinite
n
limit is taken of the integral, it yields also a finite result. So no infinities are ever encountered. Since the limit yields the result
f(0)
regardless of the function
f(t)
, this implies that the functions
q_{n}(t)
become increasingly concentrated at
t=0
as
n
increases; they become thinner and higher near
t=0
to maintain an area equal to one. The sequence of functions
q_{n}(t)
with properties (
12
) and (
13
) can be viewed as representing a Dirac
\delta(t)
at
t=0

[
13
]
. An example of a sequence of well-behaved functions

having properties (
12
) and (
13
) are the Gaussians

q_{n}(t)=\frac{n}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,e^{-n^{2}\,t^{2}}\quad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\,\,.

(14)

The above function is symmetric about
t=0
and for a given positive integer
n
, has its maximum at
t=0
. It becomes increasingly concentrated at
t=0
as
n
increases; it becomes thinner and taller about
t=0
while preserving its area. The Gaussians (
14
) act as a Dirac delta function at
t=0
in the large
n
limit.

We are now ready to investigate the effects of the perturbation
\hat{H}^{\prime}=\hat{V}\,q_{n}(t)
. From (
8
) we have
H^{\prime}_{ab}=V_{ab}\,q_{n}(t)
. Let
V_{12}=V_{21}^{*}=\beta
and
V_{11}=V_{22}=0
(
V_{11}
and
V_{22}
are not necessary to create transitions between states so setting them to zero simplifies things). We will refer to
\beta
as the interaction strength. It follows that
H^{\prime}_{12}=\beta\,q_{n}(t)
,
H^{\prime}_{21}=\beta^{*}\,q_{n}(t)
and
H^{\prime}_{11}=H^{\prime}_{22}=0
. It is convenient to define the positive quantity
\omega_{0}=(E_{2}-E_{1})/\hbar
. Then equations (
9
) and (
10
) for
\hat{H}^{\prime}=\hat{V}\,q_{n}(t)
reduce to

\dot{c_{1}}=\frac{-i}{\hbar}\Big(c_{2}\,\beta\,q_{n}(t)\,e^{-i\,\omega_{0}\,t}\Big)

(15)

\dot{c_{2}}=\frac{-i}{\hbar}\Big(c_{1}\,\beta^{*}\,q_{n}(t)\,e^{i\,\omega_{0}\,t}\Big)\,.

(16)

The time-dependent interaction
\beta\,q_{n}(t)
acts as a delta-function pulse at
t=0
in the large (infinite)
n

t<0
to the final state at

. The coefficients for the initial state are constant in time and denoted
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
:

\Psi(t<0)=\alpha_{1}\,\psi_{1}\,e^{-iE_{1}\,t/\hbar}+\alpha_{2}\,\psi_{2}\,e^{-iE_{2}\,t/\hbar}\,.

(17)

where
|\alpha_{1}|^{2}+|\alpha_{2}|^{2}=1
. This means that in (
5
)

c_{1}(t<0)=\alpha_{1}\text{ and }c_{2}(t<0)=\alpha_{2}\,.

(18)

After the delta-function pulse acts at
t=0
, the coefficients in the final state are
c_{1}(t>0)
and
c_{2}(t>0)
and are also constant:

\Psi(t>0)=c_{1}(t>0)\,\psi_{1}\,e^{-iE_{1}\,t/\hbar}+c_{2}(t>0)\,\psi_{2}\,e^{-iE_{2}\,t/\hbar}

(19)

The coefficients
c_{1}(t>0)
and
c_{2}(t>0)
in the final state will usually be different from the coefficients
c_{1}(t<0)=\alpha_{1}
and
c_{2}(t<0)=\alpha_{2}
of the initial state. However, in both cases the coefficients are constant, independent of time since the time-dependent interaction acts only at
t=0
in the large (infinite)
n

Our goal is to solve the coupled set of first order equations (
15
) and (
16
) for
c_{1}(t>0)
and
c_{2}(t>0)
in terms of
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
and the interaction strength

. We will use the Gaussians (
14
) for

. We begin by taking the derivative of

with respect to time. We can express
\dot{c_{2}}

\frac{\ddot{c_{1}}}{n^{2}}+\dot{c_{1}}\,\big(2\,t+\tfrac{i\,\omega_{0}}{n^{2}}\big)+\frac{1}{\pi\,\hbar^{2}}\,c_{1}\,|\beta|^{2}\ e^{-2\,n^{2}\,t^{2}}=0\,.

(20)

Note that in the above equation the term
(2\,t+\tfrac{i\,\omega_{0}}{n^{2}})
tends to
2\,t
in the large (infinite)
n
limit. We can therefore simply leave

in the brackets to solve the equations in the large

\displaystyle b_{1}\cos\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{2\,\hbar}\Big)-b_{2}\sin\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{2\,\hbar}\Big)

(26)

\displaystyle i\,b_{2}\,\frac{|\beta|}{\beta}\cos\left(\frac{|\beta|}{2\,\hbar}\right)+i\,b_{1}\frac{|\beta|}{\beta}\sin\left(\frac{|\beta|}{2\hbar}\right)

Solving the above two equations yields

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}\cos\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{2\,\hbar}\Big)-i\,\alpha_{2}\,\frac{\beta}{|\beta|}\sin\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{2\hbar}\Big)

\displaystyle b_{2}

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{1}\,\sin\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{2\,\hbar}\Big)-i\,\alpha_{2}\,\frac{\beta}{|\beta|}\,\cos\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{2\,\hbar}\Big)\,.

\displaystyle c_{1}(t>0)

\displaystyle c_{2}(t>0)

where we used

c_{2}(t>0)
are constant, independent of time, as there is no longer a time-dependent interaction after the delta-function pulse at
t=0
. A non-trivial check on our final expressions (
30
) and (
31
) is that
|c_{1}(t>0)|^{2}+|c_{2}(t>0)|^{2}
should equal unity. In other words, the total probability should be conserved after the interaction. Summing the squares of the coefficients yields

|c_{1}(t>0)|^{2}+|c_{2}(t>0)|^{2}=(|\alpha_{1}|^{2}+|\alpha_{2}|^{2})\Big[\,\cos^{2}\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{\hbar}\Big)+\sin^{2}\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{\hbar}\Big)\,\Big]=|\alpha_{1}|^{2}+|\alpha_{2}|^{2}=1

(32)

where we used the fact that the initial state is normalized i.e.
|\alpha_{1}|^{2}+|\alpha_{2}|^{2}=1
. The sum of the square of the coefficients is indeed unity confirming that the total probability has been conserved after the interaction. In appendix A, using a different method, we derive the expressions (
A.13
) and (
A.14
) for
c_{1}(t>0)
and
c_{2}(t>0)
respectively. These match the expressions (
30
) and (
31
) obtained above. This provides another strong confirmation of our exact expressions.

3.1
Transition probability: general expression

After the delta-function pulse at
t=0
has acted on the system, a measurement of the energy (at
t>0
), yields either
E_{1}
with probability
|c_{1}(t>0)|^{2}
or
E_{2}
with probability
|c_{2}(t>0)|^{2}
. Since
|c_{1}(t>0)|^{2}=1-|c_{2}(t>0)|^{2}
we can evaluate
|c_{2}(t>0)|^{2}
only. This is the probability of a transition to the second energy eigenstate starting with the initial state (
17
) which is a linear superposition of the two eigenstates with coefficients
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
. We can therefore label this transition probability as
P_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\to 2}
. Multiplying (
31
) by its complex conjugate we obtain

\displaystyle P_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\to 2}

\displaystyle=|c_{2}(t>0)|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\alpha_{2}|^{2}\,\cos^{2}\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{\hbar}\Big)+|\alpha_{1}|^{2}\,\sin^{2}\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{\hbar}\Big)+i\,|\beta|\,\sin\Big(\frac{2\,|\beta|}{\hbar}\Big)\Big(\frac{\alpha_{1}^{*}\,\alpha_{2}}{2\,\beta^{*}}-\frac{\alpha_{2}^{*}\,\alpha_{1}}{2\,\beta}\Big)\,.

(33)

The above result depends on the interaction strength
\beta
, the coefficients
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
but
not
on the energy gap
E_{2}-E_{1}\,.
The expression is
general
in the sense that it is valid for any
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
(with constraint
|\alpha_{1}|^{2}+|\alpha_{2}|^{2}=1
).

Let us now look at a case where the last term in (
33
) is zero. This can occur, for example, when
\sin\Big(\frac{2\,|\beta|}{\hbar}\Big)=0
and hence when
|\beta|=\frac{\ell\,\pi\,\hbar}{2}
where
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Substituting
|\beta|=\frac{\ell\,\pi\,\hbar}{2}
into (
33
) we obtain

\displaystyle P_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\to 2}

\displaystyle=|\alpha_{2}|^{2}\,\cos^{2}\Big(\frac{\ell\,\pi}{2}\Big)+|\alpha_{1}|^{2}\,\sin^{2}\Big(\frac{\ell\,\pi}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}|\alpha_{1}|^{2}&\text{ if }\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\text{ is odd}\\
|\alpha_{2}|^{2}&\text{ if }\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\text{ is even}\,.\end{cases}

(34)

This is quite a remarkable result. It states that if
|\beta|=\tfrac{\ell\,\pi\,\hbar}{2}
with
\ell
a positive even integer, then the probability is
|\alpha_{2}|^{2}
which is the same as the probability of measuring
E_{2}
in the initial state.
It is as if no interaction occurred
. If we divide the positive even integers into
\ell=4\,n
and
\ell=4\,n-2
where
n
is a positive integer then the initial state stays the same if
\ell=4\,n
but changes sign if
\ell=4\,n-2
. Let us see this. If one substitutes the interaction
|\beta|=\tfrac{\ell\,\pi\,\hbar}{2}
into the equations (
30
) and (
31
) for the coefficients
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
respectively, one obtains
(c_{1},c_{2})=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
if
\ell=4\,n
and
(c_{1},c_{2})=(-\alpha_{1},-\alpha_{2})
if
\ell=4\,n-2\,.
In the latter case, the interaction changes the state by flipping its sign but this does not affect the probabilities [however, it is worth noting that the sign change (a phase shift of
\pi
) can be detected
relative to another state
via quantum interference]. If
\ell
is odd something interesting happens: the initial roles of
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are basically switched under the interaction. The probability of measuring
E_{2}
becomes
|\alpha_{1}|^{2}
and the probability of measuring
E_{1}
becomes
|\alpha_{2}|^{2}
. A worthwhile question is whether such switching can find some application (practically, this would require an ultra-short pulse with a specific value of the strength).

In the literature there have been studies of transitions in two-level systems with finite pulses (see
[
11
]
for a review). A quantity that has been calculated is the probability
P_{1\to 2}
of a transition from the first eigenstate with energy
E_{1}
to the second eigenstate with energy
E_{2}
. The initial state in such a transition has
|\alpha_{1}|=1
and
\alpha_{2}=0
. Substituting these values into (
33
) yields

P_{1\to 2}=\sin^{2}\big(\tfrac{|\beta|}{\hbar}\big)\,.

(35)

The above is our result for a delta-function pulse. The result for a finite pulse
\Omega(t)
of width
T
is (see
[
11
]
for details on the derivation):

P_{1\to 2}=\frac{\sin^{2}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\pi\,\Omega_{0}\,T\big)}{\cosh^{2}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\pi\,\Delta_{0}\,T\big)}\,

(36)

where
\Delta_{0}
is the constant energy gap,
\pi\,\Omega_{0}\,T
is the dimensionless (positive) area of the pulse
\Omega(t)
(integral over time of the pulse) and
\Omega_{0}
is the amplitude or height of the pulse with dimensions of frequency. By comparing the coupled first order equations, the real finite pulse
\Omega(t)
corresponds to our quantity
2\,\beta\,q_{n}(t)/\hbar
with
n
assumed large. The integral over time yields
2\,|\beta|/\hbar
which becomes the dimensionless area identified with
\pi\,\Omega_{0}\,T
(we took
\beta
here to be positive and real to obtain a positive real area. Hence
\beta
was replaced by
|\beta|
for purposes of comparison). The delta-function pulse is equivalent to taking the limit as the pulse width
T
tends to zero while maintaining the area under the pulse. Therefore the area
\pi\,\Omega_{0}\,T
remains constant under this limit (i.e. as
T
gets smaller,
\Omega_{0}
gets larger so that their product stays constant). Evaluating (
36
) in the
T\to 0
limit we obtain

\lim_{T\to 0}\frac{\sin^{2}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\pi\,\Omega_{0}\,T\big)}{\cosh^{2}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\pi\,\Delta_{0}\,T\big)}=\sin^{2}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\pi\,\Omega_{0}\,T\big)=\sin^{2}\big(\tfrac{|\beta|}{\hbar}\big)

(37)

where we used the fact that the area
\pi\,\Omega_{0}\,T
had been identified with the area
\tfrac{2\,|\beta|}{\hbar}
. We also used that
\lim_{T\to 0}\cosh^{2}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\pi\,\Delta_{0}\,T\big)=1
. Note that the energy gap
\Delta_{0}
no longer appears in the result after this limit. This confirms what we had previously found: that
\omega_{0}=(E_{2}-E_{1})/\hbar
which contains the energy gap does not appear in the coefficients
c_{1}(t>0)
and
c_{2}(t>0)
in the delta-function limit where
n\to\infty
. In (
37
) we recovered our result (
35
) for the delta-function pulse as a limiting case of the finite pulse. This provides yet another confirmation of our general expressions (
30
) and (
31
).

4
Collapse scenario

The collapse of the wavefunction is something we usually encounter in the context of a measurement. For concreteness, let us assume one is measuring the energy. The collapse of the wavefunction would then be defined as the abrupt/instantaneous change from a linear superposition of energy eigenstates, the wavefunction, to a definite energy eigenstate. In this section, we will see that the delta-function pulse we considered in this paper can create a collapse, that is an abrupt/instantaneous transition from a linear superposition of eigenstates to a definite eigenstate.

The initial state of the two-level system is a
linear superposition of two eigenstates
given by

\Psi(t<0)=\alpha_{1}\,\psi_{1}\,e^{-iE_{1}\,t/\hbar}+\alpha_{2}\,\psi_{2}\,e^{-iE_{2}\,t/\hbar}

(38)

where
|\alpha_{1}|^{2}+|\alpha_{2}|^{2}=1
. After a delta-function pulse at
t=0
one obtains the final state

\Psi(t>0)=c_{1}(t>0)\,\psi_{1}\,e^{-iE_{1}\,t/\hbar}+c_{2}(t>0)\,\psi_{2}\,e^{-iE_{2}\,t/\hbar}

(39)

where
c_{1}(t>0)
and
c_{2}(t>0)
are given by the expressions (
30
) and (
31
) respectively and are a function of
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
and the interaction strength
\beta
. We will consider a collapse from an initial state, a superposition of two eigenstates, to the single energy eigenstate
\psi_{1}
. We will determine what interaction strength
\beta
leads to such a collapse as a function of
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
.

A collapse to the eigenstate
\psi_{1}
means that in the final state one has
c_{2}(t>0)=0
(and hence
|c_{1}(t>0)|^{2}=1
). Using (
31
), this implies that

\alpha_{2}\cos\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{\hbar}\Big)-i\,\alpha_{1}\,\frac{|\beta|}{\beta}\,\sin\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{\hbar}\Big)=0

(40)

which yields the equation

\tan\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{\hbar}\Big)=\dfrac{-i\,\beta\,\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{1}\,|\beta|}\,.

(41)

The left hand side of this equation is real which requires the right hand side to be real also. It is convenient to square the above equation by multiplying the right hand side by its complex conjugate. This yields

\displaystyle\tan^{2}\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{\hbar}\Big)=\dfrac{|\alpha_{2}|^{2}}{|\alpha_{1}|^{2}}=\dfrac{1-|\alpha_{1}|^{2}}{|\alpha_{1}|^{2}}=\dfrac{1}{|\alpha_{1}|^{2}}-1\,.

(42)

Since
\tan^{2}(x)+1=\sec^{2}(x)
the above equation reduces to the simple equation

\cos\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{\hbar}\Big)=\pm|\alpha_{1}|\,.

(43)

It is convenient to define
k=\beta/\hbar
where
k
is a dimensionless quantity. So
k
can be viewed as
\beta
in units of
\hbar
. We will work with
k
from now on instead of
\beta
and refer to
k
as the interaction strength. The solution to (
43
) can be expressed as

|k|=\cos^{-1}\big(\pm|\alpha_{1}|\big)\,.

(44)

In
\cos^{-1}(x)
,
x
can take on values ranging from
-1
to
1
inclusively. We define here
\cos^{-1}(x)
to have a finite range from
0
to
\pi
inclusively (
0
at
x=1
and
\pi
at
x=-1
). Since
\pm\,|\alpha_{1}|
ranges from
-1
to
1
,
\cos^{-1}(\pm\,|\alpha_{1}|)
ranges between
0
and
\pi
which is non-negative. This is consistent with the fact that
|k|
in (
44
) is also non-negative. For now,
|k|
ranges from
0
to
\pi
(we will extend this range later). For a given value of
|\alpha_{1}|
, there are two distinct values of
|k|
in this range corresponding to the
\pm
sign in (
44
): the positive sign yields a value of
|k|
in the range between
0
and
\pi/2
inclusively and the negative sign yields a value of
|k|
in the range between
\pi/2
and
\,\pi
inclusively (
|\alpha_{1}|=0
is the exception and yields one value for
|k|
which is
\pi/2
).

Below we plot
|k|
as a function of
|\alpha_{1}|
in the range
0
to
\pi
.

Figure 1
:
Plot of
|k|=|\beta|/\hbar
vs.
|\alpha_{1}|
. The are two plots corresponding to the two signs in (
44
). The solid line plot corresponds to the positive sign and runs from
\pi/2
to
0
whereas the dashed plot corresponds to the negative sign and runs from
\pi/2
to
\pi
.

Note that for both plots, the slope becomes steeper as
\alpha_{1}
increases towards unity. Let us quantify this by taking the derivative of (
44
):

\dfrac{d\,|k|}{d\,|\alpha_{1}|}=\mp\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{1}|^{2}}}

(45)

where the minus/plus sign corresponds to the solid/dashed plots respectively. For equal intervals
d|\alpha_{1}|
, the magnitude of
d|k|
for both plots increases with
|\alpha_{1}|
and reaches a maximum as
|\alpha_{1}|
approaches
1
(the slope diverges there). This implies that if one has a set of initial states whose modulus of
\alpha_{1}
has a spread of
d|\alpha_{1}|
, the magnitude of
d|k|
is greater if
|\alpha_{1}|
is greater. There will therefore be a greater spread of possible values of the interaction that lead to the collapse of a state in the set if
|\alpha_{1}|
is larger.

We can extend the range of
|k|
all the way to infinity by adding
2\,\pi\,n
to
\pm\,\hbar\,\cos^{-1}(\pm\,|\alpha_{1}|)
where
n
is a positive integer:

|k|=\begin{cases}&\cos^{-1}\big(\pm|\alpha_{1}|\big)\\
&2\,\pi\,n\,\pm\,\cos^{-1}(\pm\,|\alpha_{1}|)\quad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\,.\end{cases}

(46)

The above gives all the possible values for the modulus of
k
that leads to a collapse. We have one more equation stemming from the the fact that the right hand side of (
41
) must be real. Replacing
\beta
by
k\,\hbar
this yields

\dfrac{k}{k^{*}}=-\dfrac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{1}^{*}}\,\dfrac{\alpha_{2}^{*}}{\alpha_{2}}\,.

(47)

For simplicity define the right hand side above as the complex number

z=-\dfrac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{1}^{*}}\,\dfrac{\alpha_{2}^{*}}{\alpha_{2}}

(48)

and define
R
as any of the values of
|k|
given by the positive real quantities in (
46
). We therefore have
|k|=R
and
k/k^{*}=z
. We can therefore solve for
k
. The final result for the interaction strength
k
that yields a collapse is

k=\pm R\,\sqrt{z}

(49)

where
R
has a value given by (
46
) and
z
is given by (
48
). The interaction strength
k
is a function of
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
whereas its modulus
|k|
is a function of
|\alpha_{1}|
only.

4.1
A reversible process

Since the collapse here takes place via the potential in Schrödinger’s equation, the process should be reversible. That means we should be able to show that the same interaction (but negative) applied to the collapsed state brings us back to our original state. The collapsed state is now the new initial state with coefficients labeled as
\alpha^{new}_{1}
and
\alpha^{new}_{2}
. Since the collapsed state corresponds to the first eigenstate only (no superposition) we have
\alpha^{new}_{2}=0
. We also have
|\alpha^{new}_{1}|=1
. We would however like to obtain
\alpha^{new}_{1}
itself, not just its modulus. Recall that the initial superposition of states (
38
) with coefficients
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
transitioned to the final state (
39
) with coefficients
c_{1}(t>0)
and
c_{2}(t>0)
under the delta-function pulse. Since the final state during collapse corresponds to the first eigenstate this implied that
c_{2}(t>0)=0
. This condition led to the equations (
41
) and (
43
) for the
\beta
that yields a collapse. Though we know that
|c_{1}(t>0)|=1
we now want to obtain
c_{1}(t>0)
itself given by (
30
) using the equations (
41
) and (
43
). This will be
\alpha^{new}_{1}
:

\displaystyle c_{1}(t>0)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}\cos\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{\hbar}\Big)-i\,\alpha_{2}\,\frac{|\beta|}{\beta^{*}}\,\sin\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{\hbar}\Big)

\displaystyle=\cos\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{\hbar}\Big)\,\Big(\alpha_{1}-i\,\alpha_{2}\,\frac{|\beta|}{\beta^{*}}\,\tan\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{\hbar}\Big)\,\Big)

\displaystyle=\pm|\alpha_{1}|\,\Big(\alpha_{1}+\frac{|\alpha_{2}|^{2}}{\alpha_{1}^{*}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{\alpha_{1}}{|\alpha_{1}|}

(50)

where we used
|\alpha_{2}|^{2}=1-|\alpha_{1}|^{2}
. Note that
\beta
above is the original interaction strength that led to the collapse. The right hand side of (
41
) is real and we used its complex conjugate instead in the above derivation.

Therefore the new initial state is given by

\alpha^{new}_{1}=\pm\frac{\alpha_{1}}{|\alpha_{1}|}\text{ and }\alpha^{new}_{2}=0\,.

(51)

After a new interaction
\beta^{new}
acts on this new initial state we obtain the new final state with coefficients
c^{new}_{1}(t>0)
and
c^{new}_{2}(t>0)
given by (
30
) and (
31
) but with the new initial state coefficients
\alpha^{new}_{1}
and
\alpha^{new}_{2}
respectively. This yields

\displaystyle c^{new}_{1}(t>0)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{new}_{1}\cos\Big(\frac{|\beta^{new}|}{\hbar}\Big)-i\,\alpha^{new}_{2}\,\frac{|\beta^{new}|}{\beta^{new^{*}}}\,\sin\Big(\frac{|\beta^{new}|}{\hbar}\Big)

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{\alpha_{1}}{|\alpha_{1}|}\,\cos\Big(\frac{|\beta^{new}|}{\hbar}\Big)

(52)

and

\displaystyle c^{new}_{2}(t>0)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{new}_{2}\cos\Big(\frac{|\beta^{new}|}{\hbar}\Big)-i\,\alpha^{new}_{1}\,\frac{|\beta^{new}|}{\beta^{new}}\,\sin\Big(\frac{|\beta|}{\hbar}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mp\,i\,\frac{\alpha_{1}}{|\alpha_{1}|}\,\frac{|\beta^{new}|}{\beta^{new}}\,\sin\Big(\frac{|\beta^{new}|}{\hbar}\Big)\,.

(53)

Reversing the collapse process means that this new final state has to be equal to the original initial state:
c^{new}_{1}(t>0)=\alpha_{1}
and
c^{new}_{2}(t>0)=\alpha_{2}
. We therefore obtain the following two equations:

\displaystyle\pm\frac{\alpha_{1}}{|\alpha_{1}|}\,\cos\Big(\frac{|\beta^{new}|}{\hbar}\Big)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}

(54)

\displaystyle\mp\,i\,\frac{\alpha_{1}}{|\alpha_{1}|}\,\frac{|\beta^{new}|}{\beta^{new}}\,\sin\Big(\frac{|\beta^{new}|}{\hbar}\Big)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{2}\,.

(55)

The first equation (
54
) yields

\cos\Big(\frac{|\beta^{new}|}{\hbar}\Big)=\pm\,|\alpha_{1}|\,.

(56)

Dividing the second equation (
55
) by the first equation (
54
) yields

\tan\Big(\frac{|\beta^{new}|}{\hbar}\Big)=\frac{i\,\beta^{new}\,\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{1}\,|\beta^{new}|}\,.

(57)

Comparing (
56
) to (
43
) and (
57
) to (
41
) we see that

\beta^{new}=-\beta

(58)

where
\beta
is the original interaction strength that led to the collapse. This means that the collapse process is reversible using the negative of the original interaction strength. This shows that the abrupt/instantaneous collapse of a superposition of two eigenstates to a definite eigenstate using a delta-function pulse in Schrödinger’s equation is a completely reversible process.

5
Conclusion

In this paper we investigated general transitions under a delta-function pulse in a two-level system where the initial state was a linear superposition of two eigenstates. This system was solved exactly using two different methods (one of them presented in appendix A) and the general analytical expressions (
30
) and (
31
) were obtained for the coefficients
c_{1}(t>0)
and
c_{2}(t>0)
respectively in the final state. These expressions were a function of the initial coefficients
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
as well as the interaction strength
\beta
. They are general in the sense that one could choose any value for
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
(within the constraint that
|\alpha_{1}|^{2}+|\alpha_{2}|^{2}=1
). Previous work had focused mainly on the transition probability
P_{1\to 2}
in going from the first to the second eigenstate. We obtained a general expression
P_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\to 2}
given by (
33
) for the transition probability to the second eigenstate starting from a general initial state. This opened up new possibilities where under a certain interaction strength, the sign of the initial state flips or the roles played by
|\alpha_{1}|^{2}
and
|\alpha_{2}|^{2}
in the initial state are switched in the final state (e.g.
|\alpha_{1}|^{2}
becomes probability of measuring
E_{2}
instead of
E_{1}
).

Most importantly, under a delta-function pulse, we were able to investigate a “collapse” scenario where an initial superposition of two eigenstates transitions abruptly to a definite eigenstate i.e. with probability of unity. A collapse scenario requires three conditions to be met: the initial state must be in a general superposition (i.e. not fixed to a particular eigenstate), the final state must be a definite eigenstate and the transition has to be abrupt or basically instantaneous. The delta-function pulse ensured the latter condition, the analytical expressions for the coefficients
c_{1}(t>0)
and
c_{2}(t>0)
could be used for a general initial state and with specific values of the interaction strength
\beta
one could reach a definite eigenstate in the final state. We showed that the values of
\beta
that lead to a collapse to the first eigenstate obey the equations (
43
) and (
41
) with the modulus
|k|=|\beta|/\hbar
depending on
|\alpha_{1}|
only. We plotted
|k|
as a function of
|\alpha_{1}|
where
|\alpha_{1}|
runs from
0
to
1
inclusively. The magnitude of the slope in the plot increases with
|\alpha_{1}|
which means that for a spread
d|\alpha_{1}|
of a set of initial states, the spread
d|k|
in interaction strengths that lead to a collapse is greater when
|\alpha_{1}|
is larger. Simply put, there is a greater spread of possible values of the interaction that lead to the collapse of a state in the initial set if
|\alpha_{1}|
is larger.

The collapse of a state means that it has transitioned abruptly from a linear superposition of eigenstates to a definite eigenstate. What we saw is that this can happen under a delta-function pulse for particular values of the interaction strength. A measurement can also be viewed as inducing a collapse, where the initial state, a linear superposition of eigenstates, transitions abruptly to a definite eigenstate. However, an important difference is that you do not know a priori which eigenstate you will end up in. All you know is that the probability of ending up in a given eigenstate is governed by the Born rule. A measurement is also an irreversible process whereas we showed that the collapse under a delta-function pulse is a reversible process.

An interesting feature of a delta-function pulse is that it leads to transition probabilities that do not depend on the energy gap
E_{2}-E_{1}
. In section
3.1
we saw explicitly that the dependence on the energy gap that exists for finite pulse widths disappears in the limit as the width tends to zero. This means that the relative phase
e^{i\,\omega_{0}\,t}
where
\omega_{0}=(E_{2}-E_{1})/\hbar
has no effect when the interaction is a delta-function pulse. This brings to mind the decoherence viewpoint (for a review see
[
14
]
), where the interaction of a pure state with the environment leads to a rapid, exponentially decreasing loss of the phase relationships in the pure state. The loss is thought to occur rapidly due to the large number of degrees of freedom involved in the macroscopic environment and measuring apparatus. This connection with the decoherence paradigm is intriguing and worth exploring in more detail in the future.

Appendix A
A different method for solving the coupled set of first order equations

In section
3
we solved the coupled set of first order equations (
15
) and (
16
) that mix the coefficients
c_{1}(t)
and
c_{2}(t)
by rewritting them as a second order equation (
21
) for
c_{1}(t)
. In the large (infinite)
n
limit (delta-function pulse) we obtained the final expressions (
30
) and (
31
) for
c_{1}(t>0)
and
c_{2}(t>0)
respectively. In this section we solve the coupled set of first order equations using a different method. Instead of combining them into a second order equation we solve two separate first order equations. We obtain the same expressions as (
30
) and (
31
) for
c_{1}(t>0)
and
c_{2}(t>0)
respectively. For ease of reference we rewrite the coupled equations here:

\dot{c_{1}}=\frac{-i}{\hbar}\Big(c_{2}\,\beta\,q_{n}(t)\,e^{-i\,\omega_{0}\,t}\Big)

(A.1)

\dot{c_{2}}=\frac{-i}{\hbar}\Big(c_{1}\,\beta^{*}\,q_{n}(t)\,e^{i\,\omega_{0}\,t}\Big)\,.

(A.2)

In the large
n
limit,
q_{n}(t)
is concentrated near
t=0
. The functions
e^{\pm\,i\,\omega_{0}\,t}
appearing in (
A.1
) and (
A.2
) are smooth functions that are perfectly well-behaved at
t=0
so that in the large
n
limit, they can be set to unity. The error in this approximation decreases as
n
gets larger and vanishes for the delta-function pulse we consider (corresponding to the infinite
n
limit). We begin by dividing (
A.1
) by (
A.2
) and obtain

\displaystyle\dfrac{\dot{c_{1}}}{\dot{c_{2}}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{c_{2}\,\beta}{c_{1}\,\beta^{*}}

\displaystyle\beta^{*}\,\dot{c_{1}}\,c_{1}

\displaystyle=\beta\,\dot{c_{2}}\,c_{2}\,

\displaystyle\beta^{*}\,\dfrac{d\,c_{1}^{2}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\beta\,\dfrac{d\,c_{2}^{2}}{dt}\,.

(A.3)

Integrating both sides of (
A.3
) yields

\displaystyle\beta^{*}\,\int_{-\infty}^{t}\dfrac{d\,c_{1}^{2}}{dt^{\prime}}\,dt^{\prime}\,
