 ABSTRACT
 Self-powered neutron detectors (SPND) are small, robust, in-core radiation
sensors that have the potential to operate in high-temperature environments
(above 600°C). However, previous experiments indicated temperature-related
effects in the form of displacement currents. Given the recent irradiation at The
Ohio State University Research Reactor using a delayed-response rhodium-based
SPND alongside a prompt-response tantalum-based SPND, it was concluded that
only low-energy electrons from gamma interactions were the cause of these
temperature effects.
 A series of heated gamma irradiations with temperatures up to 750°C of
SPNDs were performed at the Advanced Test Reactor Gamma Facility in order
to better characterize these displacement currents. The results from the
experiments demonstrated a strong relation between the displacement currents
that occurred during temperature ramps to thermoluminescence (TL) glow
peaks—the SPND with Al2O3 insulation has one glow peak within 750°C and the
SPND with MgO has three.
 A demonstration was performed to calculate the trap energy levels of the
displacement currents through TL glow peak analysis. Unfortunately, the
methods employed provided inconsistent results, requiring an updated model
based on ongoing irradiation instead of post-irradiation measurements.

 1. MOTIVATION
 The Advanced Sensor and Instrumentation Program at Idaho National Laboratory has been evaluating
and developing neutron flux sensors capable of operating in high-temperature environments in support of
upcoming advanced reactor designs and demonstration. These advanced reactor designs are expected to
operate at high inlet temperatures near 650°C and above [1]. Previous evaluations performed on rhodium-
based self-powered neutron detectors (SPND) in multiple heated irradiations showed the sensor’s
capability for survivability at temperatures up to 850°C. Temperature effects were observed at
temperatures above 500°C [2-5], but the SPNDs showed capability to provide reasonable responses to
reactor power changes. Normal operation of the SPNDs were also fully recoverable upon cooling below
500°C. These made the SPND a candidate for further testing to characterize the temperature effects. By
understanding the cause of the temperature effects, data analysis methods could be used to separate the
radiation-correlated readings or utilize materials that would reduce or eliminate these effects to extend the
operating temperature of SPNDs for advanced reactor demonstration support.
 2. BACKGROUND
 SPNDs have many advantages as an in-core detector. Their construction is simple and robust—taking
the form of a mineral-insulated, metal-sheathed cable with the lead wire attached to a neutron-sensitive
wire called the emitter (Figure 1).

Figure 1. Sketch overview of an SPND.

 The emitter passively emits electrons in a radiation field proportional to the neutron field strength
resulting in an electric current output that can be read by an electrometer without the need for a high-
voltage power supply. The material melting points and annealing temperatures of many SPND materials
and fabrication processes exceeds 900°C. The omission of a high-voltage power supply makes the
detector less susceptible to leakage current damage. These qualities make the SPND a candidate for high
temperature reactor applications.
 However, initial testing of rhodium-based SPNDs (Rh-SPNDs) at high temperatures at the Neutron
Radiography (NRAD) reactor showed significant temperature effects on the signal. During steady reactor
power, the Rh-SPNDs showed temporary displacement currents during changing temperatures as well as
a decay to a steady-state current offset upon reaching steady temperatures. Additionally, the magnitude of
the displacement currents were of opposite polarities below and above 600°C as shown in Figure 2 [2].

 12

Figure 2. Rh-SPND temperature effects observed at NRAD [2].

 Despite the temperature effects observed at NRAD, the test still concluded that the SPND was
capable of tracking reactor power once a steady-state signal was maintained. This was most clearly
demonstrated in the follow-on testing at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology Reactor (MITR),
where both Rh-SPNDs tested showed linear down-power responses to reactor starting at 750°C as shown
in Figure 3 [3].

Figure 3. Rh-SPND response to reactor down-power starting at 750°C [3].

 But, the most recent irradiation test utilizing Rh-SPNDs and a tantalum-based SPND (Ta-SPND)
provided an additional theory about SPND temperature characteristics at high temperatures (Figure 4) [4,
5]. Within this test, performed at The Ohio State University Research Reactor (OSURR), the Rh-SPND
response to the reactor power was observed to be superimposed above the expected temperature effects,
whereas the Ta-SPND response was a convolution of the reactor power response and temperature effects.
The difference in temperature response strongly suggests that the temperature effects observed could be
primarily from the gamma field, which is discussed below.

 13

Figure 4. Rh- and Ta-SPND response to reactor power at different temperatures.

 As mentioned, SPNDs passively generate an electric current in a radiation field. This is primarily
done through neutron and gamma interactions with the emitter. The types of interactions that could occur
to generate a signal are given in Table 1 [6]. While these interactions can take place throughout the entire
detector body, the emitter has the largest contribution of reaction rates by design. This process thus
generates a net flow of electrons originating from the emitter, transporting through the insulator, and
reaching the collector sheath, resulting in a measurable electric current via a low-current measurement
device such as a picoammeter or electrometer. Given the dominant interaction that generates the electric
current, SPNDs are operationally categorized as delayed-response and prompt-response.

 Delayed-response PNDs’ time responses are dictated by the activation decay half-life from the
(n, β-) interaction, which varies between emitter materials. Separately, prompt-response SPNDs are near
instantaneous—on the order of femto- to picoseconds for the combined process of neutron-captured

 14

prompt-gamma emissions followed by a photoelectric or Compton scattering event to eject an electron
from the emitter.
 While the response times of delayed-response SPNDs are not fast enough to be used for reactor
controls and protection, it is more accurate for steady-state neutron flux measurements since the signal is
directly related to the neutron interaction and its well-known decay constant. Conversely, prompt-
response SPNDs are better applied for transients and reactor control functions with the assumption that
the background gamma field at high reactor power is proportional to reactor power [7]. This also implies
an additional category of self-powered gamma detectors (SPGDs) where the gamma-interaction
contribution is dominant.
 The Rh-SPND is categorized as a delayed-response SPND, utilizing the interaction of
Rh-103(n, β-)Pd-104 to generate a dominant signal (135 barns capture cross-section to Rh-104) with a
42.3-second half-life and a secondary signal (11 barns capture cross-section to Rh-104m) with a
4.4-minute half-life. Separately, the Ta-SPND is a prompt-response SPND that requires an intermediary
gamma-interaction. Based on the material interaction, the energy distribution of the electrons emitted
from rhodium and tantalum differs significantly. A Monte Carlo N-Particle (MCNP) simulation of the
electron energy emitted from a rhodium and tantalum emitter is shown in Figure 5 [8].

Figure 5. MCNP normalized electron energy distribution emitted from rhodium and tantalum emitters [8].

 The higher energy distributed electrons from the Rh-104 beta-particle emission have a higher escape
probability to reach the SPND sheath, which contributes to a signal. The results from observations at
OSURR therefore suggest that the temperature-correlated signals are separate from the high-energy
electrons emitted from Rh-104 decay. Simultaneously, the behavior of the Ta-SPND suggests that
low-energy electrons originating from the intermediary gamma-interaction are affected by temperature
changes. Further investigation of gamma-related signals in an SPND at these temperatures was required,
so another heated irradiation at the Advanced Test Reactor (ATR) Gamma Facility was performed.
 3. GAMMA IRRADIATION SETUP
 The heated gamma irradiation, funded by the Nuclear Science User Facilities Rapid Turnaround
Experiments award, was performed at the ATR Gamma Facility. The Gamma Facility is located at the
ATR canal (Figure 6). By placing discharged fuel from ATR around the gamma tube, dose rates up to
5 × 106 R/hr can be achieved.
 The experimental rig comprises a length-configurable iron core from seven stacked identical 2.188-
inch effective-height iron segments containing holes for sensors. The segments were wrapped in cable

 15

heaters and insulators (Figure 7). Eight type K thermocouples (TC) were distributed across sensor
locations and three axial heights within the iron core for experiment monitoring, heater control, and over-
temperature protection (Figure 8). The different axial heights of the sensors were for measuring the
differences between integrated length of gamma flux and temperature influence. All three heater cables
were wrapped evenly across the length of the iron core for redundancy; therefore, only the mid-axial
height is used to control the heater given the expected temperature gradient. In general, the temperature
gradient (Figure 9) does not change significantly from the target temperature in the bottom half of the iron
core; however, a linear difference from target temperature was measured for the top sensor location
(Figure 10).

 4.1. Multiple 100°C Steps
 The multiple 100°C steps to 700°C were performed to provide a wide range of temperatures to help
determine the onset of temperature effects similar to the test performed at the NRAD reactor. This test
was repeated four times to determine reproducibility. The first multistep trend for all SPNDs is shown in
Figure 11 and Figure 12.
 The qualitative sensor response between all four repeated tests were the same. For Rh-SPND (062),
the initial displacement currents in response to temperature changes below 500°C is negative; the
displacement current becomes positive above 500°C. Separately, the dummy SPNDs have multiple
polarity changes in displacement currents occurring near 400, 500, and 600°C heating steps. This is also
consistent with SPNDs in different height locations due to the temperature gradient tracked by the
monitoring TCs (Figure 12).

analyzed using different TL analysis techniques [9]. The fundamental TL techniques to determine the
energy trap, E, are:
 1. Initial rise method
 2. Peak position method
 a. Based on temperature at the maximum
 b. Based on temperature at maximum shift from various heating rates.

 5. CONCLUSION
 Two types of SPNDs irradiated in a heated rig in the ATR Gamma Facility were analyzed for
temperature-related effects under constant high gamma irradiation. One is a single Rh-SPND with outer
diameter 0.062-inch with Al2O3 insulation; the other is a SPND dummy (without an emitter) with outer
diameter 0.080-inch with MgO insulation.
 From the measured signal responses of both SPND types, to varying temperature changes and heating
rates, it is concluded that the SPND displacement currents are related to TL glow peaks, where electrons
are released from traps of certain energy related to the insulating material. The methods used to determine
the energy of the traps were the initial rise method, the peak temperature estimation, and peak temperature
shift method.
 For the Rh-SPND (062), the Al2O3 insulation only presented one energy trap while heating up to
750°C. The associated energy calculated from the initial rise method provided an average of 0.39 eV.

 29

However, this value was completely different from the peak temperature method of 1.68 eV and the peak
temperature shift method of 2.24 eV. Based on the time of irradiation, however, the data of the 80%
power was collected 20 days prior with a higher gamma flux. Removing the dataset would reduce the
shift method’s calculation to be 1.80 eV.
 Separately, for the SPND dummy (SPD-D1) insulated with MgO, multiple energy traps were
identified. While it was not possible to perform an initial rise method to calculate the energy levels, the
peak temperature method estimated energy levels of all three peaks to be 1.14, 1.62, and 1.86 eV. Similar
to the discussion on the Rh-SPND (062), the shifting temperature method provided -3.61 and 1.36 eV
energy traps for the first two peaks. Removing the 80% heat rate dataset, these values are -1.07 and 1.42
eV.
 The differences in values calculated using these different methods can be attributed to the large signal
noise. As indicated by the peak values near the 1E-10 to 1E-9 A range, the methods to employ the initial
rise method, which requires regression of data at and below 15% of the peak signal can result in large
errors. This also applies to the measurement of peak temperatures based on peak displacement currents
(especially for SPND dummies).
 Despite these issues, the data strongly suggest a relationship between displacement currents and TL
glow curves; and a demonstration of measuring the electron trap energies were partly applicable. New
methods of analyzing these glow curves from the perspective of an ongoing irradiation would need to be
developed to better apply additional analysis techniques.
