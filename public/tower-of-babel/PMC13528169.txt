Aspergillus keratitis after photorefractive keratectomy: a case report
Authors: Alireza Peyman, Nahid Mirzaei, Elaheh Foroughi, Asieh Aslani, Rasoul Mohammadi, Mohsen Pourazizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFungal keratitis is an uncommon condition that can occur after refractive surgery. Herein, we report a case of early postoperative Aspergillus keratitis in an immunocompetent patient following photorefractive keratectomy (PRK).Case presentationWe report a case of a 21-year-old white man with no past ocular or systemic diseases who underwent bilateral PRK for myopia. Seven days after surgery, he presented to his surgeon, complaining of eye redness and blurred vision that had started 3 days prior. First, empirical antibiotic therapy was initiated, and antifungal agents were subsequently added based on the direct microscopic smear examination of corneal scrapings. The polymerase chain reaction test revealed an Aspergillus corneal infection, and after 7 days of antifungal treatment, significant clinical improvement was observed.ConclusionsThis case revealed the necessity of considering atypical microorganisms, such as fungi, in the differential diagnosis of early post-PRK keratitis and highlights the role of direct smear and polymerase chain reaction tests in diagnosis, timely treatment initiation, and subsequently favorable clinical outcomes.

Background

Laser Refractive surgery is a reliable and cost-effective technique for correcting refractive errors, such as myopia, hyperopia, and astigmatism associated with good outcomes, low complication rates, and rapid recovery of function [1, 2]. As refractive errors are becoming more common and technology in this field is developing quickly, the number of patients having keratorefractive surgery has increased significantly in recent years [3]. Infectious keratitis (IK) is a potentially catastrophic complication following refractive surgeries, with an estimated overall incidence of less than 0.02–0.8% [4, 5]. Despite the low incidence, infectious keratitis is a relatively devasting postoperative complication that needs rapid diagnosis and appropriate treatment [6, 7]. While fungal keratitis is extremely uncommon, the most frequent organisms that cause IK are Streptococcus pneumonia, Staphylococcus aureus, and Staphylococcus epidermidis. These bacteria are also found in the normal flora of the eyes [8]. Herein, we report a case of early postoperative Aspergillus keratitis in an immunocompetent patient diagnosed by direct microscopic examination of the sample’s cornea scrape smear and polymerase chain reaction test.

Case presentation

In July 2023, a 21-year-old white man without any past ocular or systemic disease underwent bilateral PRK for myopia correction. The patient had no family history of relevant ocular disease and demonstrated a normal psychosocial profile. According to preoperative examination records, his refraction before PRK had been − 5.0 OD and − 4.5 to − 0.75 × 34 º OS, with a corrected distance visual acuity of 20/20 in both eyes; his slit-lamp examination had been normal; and there were no signs of meibomian gland dysfunction or blepharitis. The trauma history was negative. During the procedure, corneal epithelium was removed using 20% ethanol and a blunt blade; an excimer laser for target correction was performed using the Schwind Amaris excimer laser 1050rs (SCHWIND eye-tech-solutions GmbH, Kleinostheim, Germany), and 0.02% mitomycin-C was placed on the cornea for 45 s, then soft bandage contact lens (AIR OPTIX® AQUA; Alcon Laboratories, Inc., Fort Worth, TX, USA) were fitted. Ciprofloxacin and betamethasone 0.1% (Betasonate, Sina Daru, Tehran, Iran) eye drops were prescribed every 6 h.

On the 7th day after the surgery, the patient presented to his surgeon complaining of eye redness and blurred vision from 3 days ago. Upon referral, his visual acuity was Hand Motion OD and 20/25 OS. Slit-lamp examination revealed a 4 mm × 4 mm corneal epithelial defect with subepithelial infiltration in the right eye (Fig. 1A), and there were bandage contact lenses (BCL) in both eyes. BCLs were immediately removed and sent to the laboratory for microbial evaluation. Due to the initial suspicion of bacterial keratitis, empirical treatment with fortified eye drops ceftazidime (5%) 50 mg/ml and vancomycin (5%) 50 mg/ml (every 5 min for 1 h and then every 1 h) was initiated. The next day, a direct microscope examination of corneal scrapings and a KOH mount were suggestive of fungal infection. Topical voriconazole 10 mg/ml ophthalmic drops (every 5 min for 1 h, then every 1 h) and oral fluconazole (100 mg daily) were added. A PCR specimen from the corneal surface was collected with a needle and sent to the laboratory, confirming a fungal infection with Aspergillus species (Fig. 2). After 72h of antifungal therapy, pain, discharge, conjunctival injection, and infiltration size decreased. In addition, A 50 µm/0.1 mL dose of intrastromal voriconazole was injected, and the patient was discharged. After 7 days, the patient’s symptoms improved significantly. Conclusions and discussion

We report a case of early post-PRK Aspergillus keratitis in a patient without any past ocular or systemic disease. Seven days after surgery, the patient reported experiencing redness and blurred vision in her right eye for the past 3 days and was then referred to our center. Smear of corneal scrap, culture of BCL, and PCR of eye discharge confirmed the Aspergillus fungal infection. The report’s significance lies in four key aspects: first, the occurrence of keratitis attributed to a rare fungal organism; second, the crucial nature of prompt diagnosis and treatment; third, the utilization of PCR as a diagnostic method; and finally, the provision of suitable therapy. In recent decades, keratorefractive surgery has become increasingly prevalent. Infectious keratitis is an uncommon complication following PRK. The systematic review published by Afsharpaiman et al. reported the prevalence of fungi keratitis 0.000041%, MRSA 0.000093%, staphylococci (excluding MRSA) 0.000142%, and viridans streptococci 0.000001% following keratorefractive surgeries [6]. Fungal keratitis was demonstrated to be found in 17–36% of corneal ulcer cases worldwide. In the research by Bharathi et al. 34.4% of participants with keratitis were diagnosed with fungal keratitis [9], and Diaz-Valle et al. found that 37.23% of corneal ulcers were caused by fungal keratitis [10]. The breakdown of the corneal epithelium’s barrier integrity, prolonged use of bandage contact lenses, corneal surgery, dry eye, lack of perioperative antibiotics, and herpes simplex virus (HSV) are predisposing risk factors for infectious keratitis [7]. Furthermore, the immune system’s ability to resist infection may be compromised by topical steroid treatment in the healing process [11].

Cavallini et al. reported a case of post-PRK Fusarium keratitis in a 39-year-old woman which was successfully treated by the topical and systemic antifungal and antibacterial drugs [12]. In the other case report of fungal keratitis following PRK, a 35-year-old male had negative cultures for both bacterial and fungal keratitis; however, a histopathologic examination of specimens revealed the presence of fungal hyphae. The case was associated with poor outcomes, because diagnosis and treatment were delayed in this case. The patient had several risk factors for developing infectious keratitis, including corneal epithelial defect, use of contact lenses following surgery, and regular use of steroid drops following refractive surgery [13]. In another case report, a healthy 29-year-old man contracted fungal keratitis 5 days after PRK. Ten days after the administration of fortified antibiotics and antifungal medications, the fungus culture revealed Bipolaris. The antifungals tapered on day 12 after admission, because the patient’s symptoms improved and the infiltrates were less dense. After 6 months, the patient reported no more symptoms and an improvement in vision [14].

Several factors, including the timely identification of the infection, the effectiveness of pharmacological treatment, and the virulence of the fungus influence fungal keratitis’s course. Managing fungal keratitis presents a significant challenge. In cases of post-PRK fungal keratitis, punctual diagnosis and appropriate treatment are important in the long-term prognosis and visual outcomes. In this patient, timely diagnosis of the organism and administration of systemic and topical antibiotics and antifungal drugs leads to a good result.

It is uncommon to develop fungal keratitis after corneal refractive surgery. However, this infection may have adverse effects if appropriate treatment is delayed. Thus, it is necessary to have a high index of suspicion, particularly when there are several risk factors. This case revealed the necessity to consider atypical microorganisms, such as fungi, in differential diagnosis of early post-PRK keratitis and highlights the role of direct smear and polymerase chain reaction in proper diagnosis, timely initiation of treatment, and subsequently favorable clinical outcomes.
