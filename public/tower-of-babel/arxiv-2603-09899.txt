The SQInstructor: a guide to SQIsign and the Deuring Correspondence with level structures
Giacomo Borin, Luca De Feo, Guido Lido, Sina Schaeffler

Abstract
We explore the use of level structures to generalize the SQIsign signature scheme. We give a general framework where, given the public key and the commitment, the challenge is to exhibit an isogeny between them with an additional requirement, namely to map a chosen level structure to another. We then instantiate the framework using 1-dimensional and 2-dimensional isogenies. In doing that we provide a new explicit Deuring correspondence for supersingular elliptic curves with level structures and solve new constrained norm equations.

Introduction

Isogeny-based cryptography is a promising candidate for post-quantum
cryptography, with several proposals for key exchange
and digital signatures, but also for more advanced primitives such as
threshold and ring signatures.

A mainstay of isogeny-based cryptography is

\mathrm{SQIsign}

[
23
]
, which is so far the most compact signature
scheme participating in NIST’s post-quantum standardization
process
[
36
]
. At its heart, it is based on the

, a
mathematical and algorithmic equivalence between supersingular curves
and orders of a quaternion algebra.

The original instantiation of
\mathrm{SQIsign}
constructs signatures by
solving some quadratic equations in the quaternion algebra, using an
algorithm known as KLPT
[
31
,
24
]
. This approach gives very compact signatures, with a relatively simple
verification procedure, but a quite involved and slow signing
procedure. It also presents an intrinsic difficulty in proving EUF-CMA security,
owing to the limitations of KLPT.
A paradigm shift for
\mathrm{SQIsign}
,
first proposed in
[
21
]
and then improved
[
8
,
26
,
35
]
,
came with the
introduction of
higher-dimensional (HD) isogenies
.
Building upon a line of work started with the SIDH
attacks
[
15
,
34
,
40
]
,
these techniques significantly sped up
\mathrm{SQIsign}
and improved its provable security.

Taking a step back,

level structures
are bases of torsion subgroups of elliptic curves, up to
transformation by a well chosen group of matrices.
They have been used to prove indistinguishability of certain distributions
on supersingular isogeny graphs
[
7

, and to classify hardness assumptions in isogeny-based cryptography
[
22
]
.
However level structures are implicitly featured in many more
isogeny-based protocols, for example in SIKE
[
30
]

or FESTA
[

. Even

itself can be understood in terms of level structures. Indeed, a

The Deuring Correspondence in Cryptography

We recall here some basic facts about the Deuring correspondence and
its applications to cryptography, in particular to SQIsign. We assume some familiarity with elliptic curves, isogenies and
quaternion algebras. For more details, we refer
to
[

,
43
,

,

]
.

Throughout this document we fix a prime
p
and we write
\mathbb{F}_{p}
for
the finite field with
p
elements,
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
for a field extension
of degree
2

\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
for their algebraic closure. Finally, we write
{{\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}}}
for the quaternion algebra ramified at
p

and at infinity.

An equivalence of categories.

If
E,E^{\prime}
are elliptic curves, we write
\mathrm{Hom}(E,E^{\prime})
for the group of
homomorphisms
E\to E^{\prime}
, and
\mathsf{End}(E):=\mathrm{Hom}(E,E)
. As a
\mathbb{Z}
-module,
\mathrm{Hom}(E,E^{\prime})
has finite rank, and the usual degree
map is a positive definite quadratic form on it. In short,
\mathrm{Hom}(E,E^{\prime})
is a Euclidean lattice. On top of that, composition of morphisms makes
\mathsf{End}(E^{\prime})
a ring and

\mathrm{Hom}(E,E^{\prime})
a left
\mathsf{End}(E^{\prime})
-module.

Deuring proved that if
E,E^{\prime}
are supersingular curves in
characteristic
p
, then
\mathrm{Hom}(E,E^{\prime})
has rank
4
and
\mathsf{End}(E^{\prime})
is
isomorphic to a maximal order in
{{\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}}}
. But his correspondence goes even deeper: it shows that isomorphism
classes of supersingular elliptic curves, up to Galois conjugation in

\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}/\mathbb{F}_{p}
, are in one-to-one correspondence with isomorphism
types of maximal orders of
{{\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}}}
(both sets are finite), and that
the integral left ideals of
\mathsf{End}(E)
are in one-to-one
correspondence with the isogenies of
E
, up to post-composition with
isomorphisms. In modern terms, fixing a curve
E_{0}
and an order

\mathcal{O}_{0}\simeq\mathsf{End}(E_{0})
, the functor
\mathrm{Hom}(-,E_{0})
defines an
equivalence of categories between supersingular elliptic curves with
isogenies and invertible left
\mathcal{O}_{0}
-modules with non-zero
homomorphisms.

Theorem 2.1

Let
E_{0}
be a supersingular curve and let
\rho:E_{0}\to E
be an
isogeny. Define the ideal

I_{\rho}:=I_{\rho,E}:=\{\psi\circ\rho:\psi\in\mathrm{Hom}(E,E_{0})\}\subset\mathsf{End}(E_{0})\ ,

(1)

then
\mathsf{n}(I_{\rho})=\deg(\rho)
. Two isogenies
\rho,\rho^{\prime}
define the same ideal if and only if

\rho^{\prime}=\iota\circ\rho
for some isomorphism
\iota
. Any integral ideal of
\mathsf{End}(E_{0})
arises this way.

In what follows, we shall write
I_{\rho}\star E_{0}
for the
isomorphism class of
E
, and sometimes abuse notation and simply
write
E=I_{\rho}\star E_{0}
. Two ideals

yield the same class
I\star E_{0}=J\star E_{0}
if
and only if they are in the same left ideal class, i.e., if and only
if
I=J\alpha
for some
\alpha\in{{\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}}}
. Equivalently, we may write
[I]
for the ideal class of

for the unique isomorphism class of
I\star E_{0}
.

An algorithmic tool.

Deuring’s correspondence also has an algorithmic content that
isogeny-based cryptography has helped unearth. It can be summarized by the mantra:
some problems are hard for
curves, every problem becomes easy in the quaternions.

Concretely, the foundations of isogeny-based cryptography rest on the
famous
isogeny problem
:

Problem 1
(Supersingular Isogeny Problem)

Given supersingular elliptic curves
E,E^{\prime}/\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
, compute an
isogeny between them.

This problem is assumed to be exponentially hard, even for quantum
computers, when at least one of
E
or
E^{\prime}
is chosen at random. But the analogous problem for quaternions, that is to compute a

connecting ideal
between two given maximal orders of
{{\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}}}
,
is solved by basic integer linear algebra. In fact, Kohel, Lauter, Petit and Tignol
[
31
]
showed that even
a more restricted variant of the problem, where the connecting ideal
is requested to have prime-power norm, can be solved in polynomial
time. Their algorithm has become a staple of isogeny-based cryptography,
under the name of KLPT.

This disparity between curves and quaternions suggests a strategy to
solve the isogeny problem: because curves are in (almost) one-to-one
correspondence with maximal order types, if we could effectively
evaluate this correspondence, we could translate isogeny problems to
quaternion problems, solve them and bring the solution back to the
curves. The intuition checks out and leads to the inevitable conclusion that
computing endomorphism rings of supersingular elliptic curves is
generically hard. The problem below is indeed proven polynomial-time equivalent to the
isogeny problem for a random input curve, assuming
GRH
[
44
]
.

Problem 2
(Supersingular Endomorphism Ring Problem)

Given a supersingular elliptic curve
E
defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
,
find a basis for
\mathsf{End}(E)
.

On the other hand, the “converse” problem is now known to be easy:
given a maximal order
\mathcal{O}
of
{{\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}}}
, a supersingular curve such that

\mathsf{End}(E)\simeq\mathcal{O}
may be computed as follows:

1.

Start with a pair
(E_{0},\mathcal{O}_{0})
such that

\mathsf{End}(E_{0})\simeq\mathcal{O}_{0}
;
2
2

2

For any
p
, there exists a
polynomial-time algorithm to produce a supersingular curve
together with its endomorphism ring. Note this does not
contradict the fact that computing endomorphism rings of random
curves is hard.

2.

Use KLPT to compute a connecting ideal
I
between
\mathcal{O}_{0}
and

\mathcal{O}
of norm
\ell^{n}
for some small prime

;

3.

Use an
ideal-to-isogeny

[
27
]
to
compute
E=I\star E_{0}
by evaluating an isogeny of degree

\ell^{n}
. Then
\mathsf{End}(E)\simeq\mathcal{O}
.

Or at least this is how one would have solved the problem before
2022. The discovery of efficient attacks on

[
15

34
,
40
]
brought several

). We will not discuss them as they are not necessary for our purpose.

From the correspondence to cryptography.

Summarizing, it is easy to construct supersingular curves knowing
their endomorphism rings, but once we throw away this information it
is hard to recover it. This defines at the very least a one-way function, but hopefully
something more. The first to observe that one could use the knowledge of the
endomorphism ring as a “trapdoor” in a signature scheme were
Galbraith, Petit and Silva (GPS)
[
28

with SQIsign
[

that the Deuring correspondence truly
showed its full potential.

\mathcal{O}_{0}\simeq\mathsf{End}(E_{0})

The key difference between GPS and SQIsign lies in
Step
2

\approx N^{2}
choices for
P_{\mathsf{ch}}
, though linearly
equivalent points are effectively the same challenge, so the number of
distinct challenges is rather

\approx 1/N
, as opposed to
1/2
for GPS. If
N
is exponentially large, it thus suffices to run a single
iteration of the identification protocol to achieve the desired level
of security.

At first sight, using torsion points as challenges may seem a hack
with little relation to the Deuring correspondence. Looking closer we notice that the set

\Lambda\subset\mathrm{Hom}(E_{\mathsf{pk}},E_{\mathsf{com}})
of isogenies
vanishing on

\varphi_{\mathsf{ch}}:E_{\mathsf{pk}}\to E_{\mathsf{ch}}
be the isogeny
such that
\ker\varphi_{\mathsf{ch}}=\langle P_{\mathsf{ch}}\rangle

\Lambda=\{\psi\circ\varphi_{\mathsf{ch}}:\psi\in\mathrm{Hom}(E_{\mathsf{ch}},E_{\mathsf{com}})\}.

(2)

Some readers will find our explanation unnecessarily contrived: we may
as well have said that
\varphi_{\mathsf{ch}}
is the challenge sent by the
verifier, and that the response is computed from

\mathrm{Hom}(E_{\mathsf{ch}},E_{\mathsf{com}})
. However this point of view, which is the traditional way of presenting
SQIsign, misses two important points: First, if
\varphi_{\mathsf{ch}}
is the
challenge, we need an isogeny-to-ideal algorithm, somewhat
complicating the protocol. This was already observed in
[

. Second, there are potentially other choices of sublattices

\Lambda\subset\mathrm{Hom}(E_{\mathsf{pk}},E_{\mathsf{com}})
that lead to
interesting variants of SQIsign. This is the topic of the present work.

Roadmap.

We start by characterizing which restrictions on
\mathrm{Hom}(E,E^{\prime})
lead to
a generalized Deuring correspondence. It is tempting to impose that a point

is mapped to some

\mathrm{Hom}(E,E^{\prime})

extending previous work of Arpin on

[

]

The rest of the paper will be devoted to adapting SQIsign and its

elliptic curves (aka, one-dimensional isogenies), in the style of
pre-SIDH-attacks SQIsign. Although modern SQIsign has moved away from one-dimensional isogenies
for efficiency, these algorithms are still very much relevant for
SQIsign-like ring signatures
[

]
and chameleon

[

]
. Finally, in
Section

we analyze in detail a modern instantiation
of
\mathrm{SQInstructor}
, using higher dimensional isogenies, and compare it to
the latest version of the NIST candidate SQIsign.

Level structures and the Deuring Correspondence

We start by studying how isogenies map torsion subgroups of elliptic

3.2
Level Structures

Restricting the action of

Given a subgroup

of

-level structure containing it is

\Gamma\cdot\left(\begin{smallmatrix}{P}\\
{Q}\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\left\{\mathsf{M}\cdot\left(\begin{smallmatrix}{P}\\
{Q}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\mid\mathsf{M}\in\Gamma\right\}\ .

(8)

The integer

is referred to as the

For example, the bases of the
E[N]
are
\{\mathrm{Id}\}
-structures,
we call these

. In this work we focus on
the following level structures:

•

Borel level structures
are the level structures relative to the Borel subgroup

\Gamma_{0}=\left\{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}{a}&{b}\\
{0}&{d}\end{smallmatrix}\right]\big|a\cdot d\in{(\mathbb{Z}_{/N})}^{*},b\in\mathbb{Z}_{/N}\right\}\ .

(9)

Borel structures of level

on

are in bijection with cyclic subgroups

of order

: a Borel structure
\Gamma_{0}\cdot\left(\begin{smallmatrix}{P}\\
{Q}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
corresponds to

since

\Gamma_{0}\cdot\left(\begin{smallmatrix}{P}\\
{Q}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
is exactly the set of bases
(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})

with

.

•

Split Cartan level structures
, i.e. diagonal level structures, relative to

\Gamma=\left\{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}{\lambda}&{0}\\
{0}&{\mu}\end{smallmatrix}\right]\mid\lambda,\mu\in{(\mathbb{Z}_{/N})}^{*}\right\}\ .

They correspond to ordered pairs of cyclic subgroups whose direct sum is
E[N]
;
indeed
\Gamma\cdot\left(\begin{smallmatrix}{P}\\
{Q}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
is the set of bases
(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})

with

and
P^{\prime}\in\langle P\rangle
.

•

Scalar level structures
, i.e.

-level structures,
with

{0}&{\lambda}\end{smallmatrix}\right]\mid\lambda\in{(\mathbb{Z}_{/N})}^{*}\right\}\ .

If

{Q}\end{smallmatrix}\right)

-structure, we define

{\alpha(Q)}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\;,

(10)

which is a well defined

-structure, in particular the above definition does not depend on the

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}{P}\\
{Q}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in S

The assumption

however it will be convenient to extend definitions to isogenies of

be the set of all

,
and we define the set of all linear combinations of pairs of points in

{Q}\end{smallmatrix}\right)

{Q}\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\{\gamma\cdot\left(\begin{smallmatrix}{P}\\

We now describe a paradigm for creating

4.1
The Protocol

As usual, we choose a prime

The protocol being a sigma-protocol, we describe its phases one by

Key generation.

Like in
\mathrm{SQIsign}

\Gamma

The exact way in which

Depending on the instantiation,

is coprime to

The verifier picks a uniformly random

The response

such that

\varphi_{\mathsf{rsp}}(S_{\mathsf{pk}})=S_{\mathsf{com}}

This is where we encounter the biggest variability: we may opt to

chains of 1-dimensional isogenies like in earlier versions of

[

currently submitted to NIST, as we will

4.2
Encoding and sampling level structures

The group

If

Zero Knowledge.

To prove zero-knowledge we would need to exhibit a simulator that on
input

produces a random isogeny

However the existence of such a simulator depends on how the response
is computed. Like in
\mathrm{SQIsign}

1-dimensional response, then the distribution of
\varphi_{\mathsf{rsp}}

If instead we use a higher-dimensional method to produce the response,
we are faced with the obstacle that no polynomial time algorithm is
known to compute a large arbitrary degree isogeny from

without knowing its endomorphism ring. The workaround used in
\mathrm{SQIsign}
is to prove security in a model where
the simulator has access to an oracle producing such
isogenies
[
1
]
, or at least where it is
provided non-interactively with a polynomially sized
hint

[
2
]
. We adapt these proof techniques to
\mathrm{SQInstructor}
in

Sections

and

Whatever the case is, if we take Zero-Knowledge for granted, then the
we can apply the Fiat-Shamir transform to the protocol to obtain a
signature scheme as secure as
\mathrm{SQIsign}

to the hardness of

via

The best classical attacks against

The level

Thus we need

5
KLPT for level structures

We now come to the problem of finding an element of

\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{E},\mathbb{E}^{\prime})
of smooth norm. Here we focus solely on extending to the
\mathrm{SQInstructor}
framework the
KLPT algorithm
[

, which computes a connecting ideal of
smooth norm given
\mathcal{O}(E)
and
\mathcal{O}(E^{\prime})
. Then, standard techniques
[

let us
translate this ideal into a 1-dimensional isogeny, to be used, e.g.,
as response

in the identification protocol.

These algorithms lead to larger degree isogenies and less efficient
implementations than what is achievable through higher-dimensional
techniques, but, unlike those, produce outputs that can be efficiently
simulated (under an

Parameters.

Letting

6.2
Lattice shapes and response degree

The response computation described above may fail and restart the
signature when no
\alpha\in I_{\rho,\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{com}}}
of odd norm

\mathsf{n}(\alpha)<B_{\mathsf{rsp}}\cdot\mathsf{n}(I_{\rho,\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{com}}})

is found. Though we set this failure probability to be so low as to be
practically irrelevant, it is not practically negligible and it thus
affects the distribution of responses and consequently the
Zero-Knowledge property. We note that
\mathrm{SQIsign}
also has failure cases that force it to restart,
as analyzed in
[
1
,
13
]
, and those
failure cases also affect
\mathrm{SQInstructor}
, however this failure event is
specific to the design described here, thus we analyze it exclusively.

Because
I_{\rho,\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{com}}}
is isometric to

\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{pk}},\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{com}})
up to a multiplicative factor
\deg\rho
, the probability of failing to find

\alpha
is the same as the probability of failing to find an isogeny
of odd degree
<B_{\mathsf{rsp}}
in
\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{pk}},\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{com}})
. Let
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\alpha_{4})
be the Minkowski-reduced basis of

\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{pk}},\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{com}})
and
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4})
be the norms of the
\alpha_{i}
’s, i.e. the squares of the successive minima
12
12

12

recall that we are using the norm specified
in
Equation

13
.

It is not possible that all the minima are even, otherwise all
the elements of the lattice would be even, contradicting

Lemma

1
.
Thus, the probability of not finding an odd norm vector in the intersection with
the ball of radius
B_{\mathsf{rsp}}
is lower-bounded by
\Pr(\lambda_{4}>B_{\mathsf{rsp}})
.
We estimate this probability via a quantitative analysis of the distribution of
the minima, for which we provide experiments
13
13

13

github.com/SQInstructors/SQInstructor
.
We also refer to
[
1
, Section 9.3.2]
for a similar
analysis.
Recall that:

1.

\mathsf{InStruct}\-\mathsf{Ideal}
returns a left
\mathcal{O}
-ideal
I_{\rho}

where
\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}(E_{\mathsf{pk}})
.

2.

I_{\rho,\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{com}}}=I_{\rho}\cap(\mathbb{Z}+N\mathcal{O})
.

3.

\mathcal{O}
is statistically close to the uniform distribution
over the set of maximal orders of
B_{p,\infty}

(
Table

1
), by the choice of
\deg(\varphi_{\mathsf{sk}})
.

4.

if
C
is

\mathrm{Cov}(\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{pk}},\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{com}}))=[M_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{/N}):\left\langle\Gamma\right\rangle]\cdot\tfrac{1}{4}p
, for scalar level
structures
C=\tfrac{p}{4}N^{3}
, then by Minkowski’s second theorem we have

\frac{1}{55}C^{2}\leq\frac{2^{4}}{9\pi^{4}}C^{2}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{4}\lambda_{i}<\frac{2^{10}}{\pi^{4}}C^{2}<11C^{2}
.

In our experiments, we observe an inverse linear correlation between the first
and the last minima, that means we can approximate

\lambda_{1}\lambda_{4}\approx k_{1}C

for a constant
k_{1}
. Our experiments suggest
k_{1}\approx 0.314
.
We thus focus on the cumulative distribution of
\lambda_{1}
,
i.e.
\Pr(\lambda_{1}\leq x)
,
that is the probability that the shortest vector of
\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{E},\mathbb{E}^{\prime})
has norm at most
x
.

Following the same estimates as in
[
1
]
, we know that there are

\psi(x)=x\prod_{l|x}(1+\tfrac{1}{l})
primitive left
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{E})
-ideals
(i.e. cyclic isogenies mapping curves with level structure)
of norm
x
, that means that the number of primitive ideals of odd norm at most
x

\sum_{2r+1\leq x}\psi(2r+1)
, which asymptotically lies between
\frac{1}{4}x^{2}
and
x^{2}
.
To count also minimal isogenies
\varphi\colon\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{pk}}\to\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
of even degree, (here minimal just means that it is not a multiple of another isogeny
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{com}}\to\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
) we notice that such maps, since they map scalar structures to multiples of scalar structures, at the level of elliptic curves (without level structures) are of the form
\varphi=2^{\delta}\varphi^{\prime}
with
\delta\leq e_{\mathsf{tor}}
and
\varphi^{\prime}
a cyclic, odd-degree isogeny. Moreover if we choose
\varphi^{\prime}
, then
E^{\prime}
is determined and the level structure is determined up to
2^{3\delta}
choices (or
\tfrac{3}{4}2^{3\delta}
if
\delta=e_{\mathsf{tor}}
). Then, fixing
\deg\varphi=2^{2\delta}\deg\varphi^{\prime}=2^{3\delta}(2r+1)
, then the number of such
\phi
is (up to a small constant in the case
\delta=e_{\mathsf{tor}}
)
2^{3\delta}\psi(2r+1)
.
Summing over possible
\delta
and
2r+1
such that
4^{\delta}(2r+1)\leq x
, we see that the possible minimal isogenies
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{com}}\to\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
of degree at most
x
is equal to
\alpha x^{2}
for some
\alpha
bounded from below by
\tfrac{1}{4}
and also bounded from above by some constant (in particular
\alpha\leq 2
if
x/N^{2}
is large enough).

The total number of ideal classes, or equivalently the number of possible choices for
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{com}}

up to isomorphism, is approximately
\tfrac{p}{12}N^{3}=\tfrac{C}{3}
for
scalar level structures.
Thus, if all ideal classes are equally likely and it never happens that two
ideals of norm at most
x
are in the same class, for
x\gg N^{2}
we have

\Pr\left(\lambda_{1}\leq x\right)=k_{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{C}}{x}\right)^{2}\ ,\text{ for }k_{2}\in\left[\frac{3}{2},6\right]\ .

(21)

Thus, combining the last estimate with our correlation hypothesis, we have that

\Pr\left(\lambda_{4}>x\right)=\Pr\left(\lambda_{1}\leq\frac{k_{1}C}{x}\right)\approx k_{3}\left(\frac{\sqrt{C}}{x}\right)^{2}\text{ for }k_{3}=k_{1}^{2}k_{2}\ .

(22)

Our experiments corroborate this estimate, for
k_{3}\approx 0.553794
. Thus, we
can conclude that via fixing
B_{\mathsf{rsp}}=\kappa\sqrt{C}=\kappa\sqrt{p}N^{3/2}
we can bound the restart probability by
\tfrac{k_{3}}{\kappa^{2}}
.
Thus, we have three possible strategies. The aggressive one is to fix
\kappa

to a small
O(1)
constant, having a restart probability of
O(1)
,
getting a more efficient signature. The conservative one is to fix
\kappa=\sqrt[4]{p}
, getting a negligible restart probability in
\lambda
.
The intermediate one is to aim to a restart probability of
2^{-64}
, in line with
[
1
]
and
NIST’s recommendations that the adversary has access to no more
than
2^{64}
signing queries
[
36
]
, thus we fix
\kappa=2^{32}
,
implying

B_{\mathsf{rsp}}=2^{32}\sqrt{p}N^{3/2}\ ,

(23)

that implies, recalling the definition of
N
, that
\log(B_{\mathsf{rsp}})=32+\tfrac{1}{2}(\log(p)+\lambda)
.

6.3
\mathsf{EUF\text{-}CMA}
Security

We are finally ready to discuss the
\mathsf{EUF\text{-}CMA}
security of our
\mathrm{SQInstructor}
signature.
From now we denote by
\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{SQInstructor}}
the identification protocol from

Section

4.1
.
As noted repeatedly,
see
[
21
,
8
,
1
,
6
]
, we cannot sample
efficient representations of large degree isogenies without relying on the
knowledge of the endomorphism ring of the starting curve.
This means that the straightforward simulator for the
\mathrm{SQIsign}
protocol
described above cannot be instantiated in polynomial time.

To overcome this problem in
[
21
]
, and following works on
\mathrm{SQIsign}
variants
[
8
,
26
]
, the authors introduced the notion of

isogeny oracles
: oracles that on input a supersingular elliptic curve and
some parameters, return an efficient representation of a large degree isogeny
from the curve following a given distribution.

Remark 5

In a follow-up the authors of
[
2
]
provide a different
security proof for the
\mathrm{SQIsign}
signature scheme, avoiding the use of
isogeny oracles, but instead introduce the
notion of
hint-assisted
zero knowledge, where the simulator is given
hints sampled from an isogeny distribution. A similar proof can be adapted to
our protocol, but is very technical and would take all our available space.
We focus here on the more intuitive, yet rigorous proof based on oracles,
and give the hint-assisted proof in
for completeness.

As for
[
8
]
, we consider two variants of the oracle:

Definition 5

A
\Gamma
-
uniform target oracle
(
\Gamma\text{-}\mathsf{UTO}
)
for
\Gamma\leq\mathsf{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{/N})
is an oracle taking
as input a supersingular elliptic curve with
\Gamma
-level structure
\mathbb{E}
defined
over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
and an integer
B_{\mathsf{rsp}}

and outputs a random isogeny
\phi:\mathbb{E}\to\mathbb{E}^{\prime}

(in efficient representation) such that:

1.

\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
is distributed sampled from the uniform distribution on
the set of supersingular elliptic curves over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
with level
structure so that there exists an odd-norm isogeny in

\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{E},\mathbb{E}^{\prime})
of degree
\leq B_{\mathsf{rsp}}
;

2.

The conditional distribution of
\varphi
given
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
is uniform
over isogenies
\varphi:\mathbb{E}\to\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
of odd degree
\leq B_{\mathsf{rsp}}

Definition 6
(Definition 23
[
8
]
)

A
fixed degree isogeny oracle
(
\mathsf{FIDIO}
) is an oracle taking as input a
supersingular elliptic curve
E
defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
and an integer
N
,
and outputs a uniformly random isogeny
\phi:E\to E^{\prime}
(in efficient
representation) with domain
E
and degree
N
.

Using these oracles, we can enunciate and prove the following theorem:

Theorem 6.1

The
\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{SQInstructor}}
identification protocol is statistically

non-aborting
honest-verifier zero-knowledge in the
\Gamma\text{-}\mathsf{UTO}
and
\mathsf{FIDIO}
model.
In other words, there exists a polynomial time simulator
\mathsf{Sim}
with access
to a
\Gamma\text{-}\mathsf{UTO}
and a

\mathsf{FIDIO}
that produces random transcripts which are statistically indistinguishable
from honest non-aborting transcripts.

Proof

E_{\mathsf{pk}}
be a random supersingular elliptic curve over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
and

N

\mathsf{Sim}
that using the
\Gamma\text{-}\mathsf{UTO}
and
\mathsf{FIDIO}
returns a transcript

1.

\mathsf{Sim}
sets
S_{\mathsf{pk}}\leftarrow Z\cdot\left(\begin{smallmatrix}{P_{\mathsf{pk}}}\\

\Gamma\text{-}\mathsf{UTO}

to get the
isogeny

\varphi_{\mathsf{rsp}}:(E_{\mathsf{pk}},S_{\mathsf{pk}})\to(E_{\mathsf{com}},S_{\mathsf{com}})
;

2.

\mathsf{Sim}
computes the matrix
M_{\mathsf{ch}}\leftarrow\mathsf{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{/N})/Z

\mathsf{Sim}

∎
