Adaptive discovery of effect modification in matched observational studies
Yu Gui, Dylan S. Small, and Zhimei Ren

Abstract
Understanding effect modification—how treatment effects vary across subpopulations—is practically important in observational studies, as it helps identify which subgroups are likely to benefit from a given treatment. In this paper, we study the discovery of effect modification in matched observational studies, where each treated unit may be matched to multiple controls. We develop a finite-sample valid procedure for identifying and selecting covariate-interpretable subgroups, with exact control of the subgroup-level false discovery rate (FDR). Our method explicitly accounts for unmeasured confounding via sensitivity models, and leverages multiple matched controls to improve statistical power. We demonstrate the favorable performance of our method relative to baseline methods through extensive simulation studies and a real-world application to the economic returns to college education.

Although the young generation has long been encouraged to attend college by the conventional opinion that college education will lead to positive economic returns, some social scientists remain skeptical

, and public skepticism has become increasingly visible in recent discussions

Whether college is worth it may depend on a person’s characteristics, e.g., their interests, their high school grades, or their parents’ education. One approach to understanding the heterogeneity would be to directly model the conditional treatment effect as a function of a person’s characteristics (covariates). However, such a model may rely on parametric or structural assumptions and may not be that interpretable. A more interpretable alternative is to study the heterogeneity through subgroups: rather than attempting to recover the full conditional treatment effect, one seeks to identify subpopulations defined by observed characteristics for which the treatment effect may differ and then identify the subpopulations for which the treatment has a positive effect, e.g., for which subpopulations college has positive returns
(
48
;
10

.
Realizing this goal calls for a data-adaptive method that first identifies candidate subgroups from
the data and then selects the significant ones to report with valid error-rate control, while
relying on weak assumptions about the underlying distribution and remaining
robust to possible unmeasured confounding.

1.2
Adaptive discovery of effect modification with controlled error

Effect modification arises when the treatment effect of interest varies across levels of observed covariates.
Identifying such heterogeneity not only offers insights into effect heterogeneity but may also help define tailored inferential targets for follow-up investigations and decision-making.
In observational studies, treatment effect estimates may be biased by unmeasured confounding, e.g., people who are more likely to attend college may also have parents with higher socioeconomic status (SES) that is not fully measured, and this unmeasured SES may also affect a person’s earnings in ways other than making a person more likely to go to college. Sensitivity analysis can be used to study how sensitive a treatment effect estimate is to unmeasured confounding
(
20
;
53
;
55
)
.
Discovering effect modification can make findings less sensitive to bias from unmeasured confounding
(
37
;
41
)
.

Detecting effect modification via matching.

As alluded to above, in observational studies,
directly defining subgroups and performing inference based on
a model for the conditional treatment effect can be
unreliable when the model is misspecified.
An alternative is
matching

(
19
;
60
;
61
;
57
;
65
)
,
which constructs matched sets of treated and control units that are comparable in observed covariates. The simplest type of matching is pair matching, which constructs matched pairs of treated and control units.
By balancing covariate distributions prior to outcome analysis,
matching reduces overt bias and limits extrapolation to regions with poor overlap, thereby making subsequent subgroup comparisons more interpretable without imposing a fully specified model for the conditional treatment effect.

While it is natural to use the same matched dataset to choose subgroups and make inferences about treatment effects within those subgroups, this reuse of data could invalidate
inference without careful adjustment.
Data splitting is a classical solution:
subgroups are defined with a pilot dataset and
inference is conducted on the identified subgroups using the remaining subset
(
21
;
15
)
.
But this comes with a price: by halving the effective sample size,
sample splitting can lead to a loss in power, especially in small-sample settings.
This motivates the development of a disciplined and fine-grained inferential procedure
that allows selection over data-driven subgroups while retaining valid error control conditional on the identified subgroups.
Due to space constraints, discussion of additional related work on subgroup partition and selection is deferred to Appendix
A
, including CATE-based subgroup selection
(
67
;
47
)
and interactive methods with type-I error control in randomized experiments
(
16
)
.

Quantification of selection error.

Once the candidate subgroups have been specified,
our goal is to select the subgroups in which the treatment has an effect.
To quantify the error rate of subgroup selection,
38
considered

family-wise error rate (FWER)
, i.e., the probability of making at least one false discovery, and
proposed an algorithm that controls the FWER at any given level

\alpha\in(0,1)
with a finite sample.
However, FWER control can be too stringent when the number of subgroups is large, since guarding against even a single false discovery can substantially reduce power
and lead to few selected subgroups.
An alternative error rate quantity that adapts to the scale of the problem is the

false discovery rate (FDR)
, which focuses on the proportion of false discoveries
among the selected subgroups.
Concretely, the goal is to control

\displaystyle\text{FDR}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\sum_{\mathfrak{g}}\mathds{1}\{\text{subgroup }\mathfrak{g}\text{ is selected but has zero effect}\}}{\text{number of selected subgroups}\vee 1}\right]\leq\alpha,

(1)

where
a\vee b=\max(a,b)
, for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.
To achieve finite-sample FDR control,

39
proposed applying the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH)
procedure
(
7
)
to subgroup-level p-values,
e.g., Wilcoxon signed rank p-values.
However, this approach has several limitations.
First, it does not support interactive/adaptive data analysis, in which data analysts
adapt their decisions after viewing (part of) the data.
Second, because these subgroup-level tests are often discrete, the resulting
p-values can be coarse, especially for small subgroups, which in turn can substantially reduce selection power.
More recently,
25
proposed an adaptive multiple testing procedure
for subgroup selection with FDR control that addresses these issues.
Their method, however, is tailored primarily to settings without unobserved confounders,
and can become quite conservative when such confounding is taken into account.
A detailed comparison with
25
is provided in Section
3.5
.

Enhancing power by leveraging multiple matched control units.

Beyond valid error control, it is also crucial to design powerful statistics
that effectively use the observational data.
One potentially valuable source of information is the presence of
multiple matched control units, which commonly arises in relatively large cohorts
with limited treated units
(
63
;
59
;
54
)
.

56
demonstrates that the use of multiple controls often yields a nontrivial reduction in sensitivity to unmeasured biases.
However, in the context of effect modification with data-driven subgroups,
it remains unclear how to effectively leverage multiple control units to improve statistical power while maintaining rigorous error control (see discussion in Section
1.3
under “Multiple controls”).

1.3
Adaptive multiple testing

Our subgroup selection method adopts the adaptive multiple testing
framework, which permits sequential, data-driven decisions based on a masked view of the test statistics, while the remaining information is reserved for rigorous error rate control
(see e.g.,
4
;
13
;
42
;
50
;

;

;

The multiple testing problem induced by our main research question, however,
introduces new challenges that are not addressed by existing methods.
In developing efficient subgroup selection procedures, we
also expand the methodological toolbox of adaptive multiple testing itself.
We outline these challenges below.

Composite null.

In observational studies, valid inference on treatment effects must
account for unmeasured confounding. Under such confounding,
the null hypothesis of no treatment effect is inherently composite.
Existing adaptive multiple testing methods are largely designed for
testing sharp nulls, or for the composite nulls under the additional requirement that
each hypothesis admits a p-value with a non-decreasing
density
(
23
;
14
)
. However, as we show
in Section
3
, the commonly used Wilcoxon signed-rank p-values

do not
satisfy the
non-decreasing condition, making existing composite null-oriented
methods not generally applicable. Meanwhile, applying methods tailored to sharp nulls
often leads to suboptimal statistical power.
Our proposed method directly targets composite nulls without imposing
density conditions on the p-values, achieving substantially improved power
and rigorously controlled FDR; see Figure
1(a)

for a preview of the power comparison between our proposed method,
Ours-NP
,
and the sharp null-oriented method from
25
,
P-screening
;
the performance of the other baseline
(
39
)
,
BH-baseline
, is also included.

Multiple controls.

A central question in this paper is how to
leverage multiple controls to improve statistical efficiency.
When applying existing multiple testing methods that incorporate
multiple controls
(
30
;
33
)
to
the subgroup selection task, we observe a counter-intuitive phenomenon:
power
decreases
as the number of
controls grows (see the
Max

TopGap
,
MedSplit
curves in Figure
1(b)
).
Upon closer inspection, these methods primarily differ in how the test statistics are masked,
yet all their masking schemes “dilute” the signal as the number of controls increases.
Our approach identifies and retains a key piece of information that is lost under
existing masking strategies; by incorporating it into the masking step,
we achieve increasing power as the number of controls grows, as shown
by the
NP

1(b)

Offset effect.

In the adaptive multiple testing framework, FDR control
is typically achieved by forming an FDR estimate
from the unmasked information and choosing the selection
set for which this estimate falls below the target level.
Due to an artifact of this construction—specifically, the granularity of the estimator—these
procedures become effectively powerless unless the number of nonnulls exceeds a certain
threshold. This phenomenon, referred to as the

threshold effect

(
4
;
30
;
46
;
25
)
,
is common across existing adaptive multiple testing methods.
In the context of subgroup selection, it manifests when only a few
(potentially large) subgroups are under consideration,
making reliable selection difficult.
To alleviate this issue, we adopt the
conditional calibration
technique
(
46
;
40
)
, which
boosts power in such regimes while maintaining rigorous
FDR control. As illustrated in Figure
1(c)
, the conditionally calibrated
version (
-cc

Meanwhile, our instantiation of conditional calibration
for composite nulls of this type appears to be new in the literature.

1.4
Our contributions

Our main contributions are summarized below.

(1)

We develop an adaptive framework for selecting data-driven, covariate-interpretable subgroups
with significant treatment effects. Our approach controls the subgroup-level FDR under Rosenbaum’s
\Gamma
-sensitivity model
(
53
)
and accommodates flexible modeling choices.

(2)

Our procedure generalizes beyond matched pairs and applies to
the setting with multiple controls, where a treated unit can be matched with multiple control units.
By leveraging the masked rank of the treated outcome,
it substantially improves the discovery power relative to existing multi-control testing approaches.
The proposed test statistic is based on the likelihood ratio, and we also investigate
its optimality theoretically.

(3)

We develop several techniques to further enhance the power of the subgroup selection procedure,
which may be of independent interest within the broader framework of adaptive multiple testing.

(4)

The empirical advantages of our method
are supported by extensive simulations.
In a real-data study on the economic return to college education,
our method selects strictly more subgroups than the comparison methods across varying values of sensitivity parameters.
The selected subgroups provide insights into the heterogeneity of benefits
from college and corroborate the negative selection theory in sociology that the groups who are least likely to attend college are the ones that would most benefit from it.

2
Problem setup

Consider an observational study with
I
matched sets.
For each
i\in[I]:=\{1,2,\ldots,I\}
, set
i
contains

n_{i}\geq 2
units that share the same covariates
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
;
for any
j\in[n_{i}]
, let
Z_{ij}\in\{0,1\}
denote the assigned treatment, where

Z_{ij}=1
indicates treatment and
Z_{ij}=0
indicates control.
The
Z_{ij}
’s are assumed to be independent across
i
, and
we impose the constraint that
\sum^{n_{i}}_{j=1}Z_{ij}=1
, i.e., each matched set
contains exactly one treated unit.
Let
t_{ij}
and
c_{ij}
denote
the potential outcomes of unit
(i,j)
with and without treatment, respectively, and let
u_{ij}
represent all the unmeasured confounding factors.
We adopt the
stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA)
,
under which the observed outcome can be written as

\displaystyle R_{ij}=Z_{ij}\cdot t_{ij}+(1-Z_{ij})\cdot c_{ij}.

(SUTVA)

Following the convention in the literature of observational studies, we denote

{\mathcal{F}}=\left\{x_{i},u_{ij},t_{ij},c_{ij}\right\}_{i\in[I],j\in[n_{i}]}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\mathcal{Z}}=\left\{Z_{i}:\;i\in[I],\;\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}Z_{ij}=1\right\}.

Inference is conditional on

{\mathcal{F}}
and
{\mathcal{Z}}
throughout, and we do not explicitly write down the conditional events when the context is clear.
Within each matched set, units
(i,j)
and
(i,j^{\prime})
can differ in the odds of being treated due to unmeasured confounding.
In this paper, we adopt Rosenbaum’s
\Gamma
-sensitivity model
(
53
)
to quantify the difference in the odds: for some
\Gamma\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{-1}\leq\frac{\pi_{ij}/(1-\pi_{ij})}{\pi_{ij^{\prime}}/(1-\pi_{ij^{\prime}})}\leq\Gamma,\quad\forall i\in[n]\text{ and }\forall j,j^{\prime}\in[n_{i}],

(2)

where we write
\pi_{ij}=\mathbb{P}(Z_{ij}=1\mid{\mathcal{F}})
for notational simplicity.
Under (
2
), there is

\displaystyle\Gamma^{-1}\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}(Z_{ij}=1\mid{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{Z}})}{\mathbb{P}(Z_{ij^{\prime}}=1{\,|\,}{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{Z}})}\leq\Gamma,\quad\forall i\in[n]\text{ and }\forall j,j^{\prime}\in[n_{i}].

(3)

To identify effect modification, we partition the full cohort into non-overlapping
subgroups using the information in
{\mathcal{F}}
. The guiding principle
is to construct groups that are as homogeneous as possible with respect
to the treatment effect—so that units within a group share the same
treatment effect whereas effects vary across groups.
Formally, denoting
{\mathcal{A}}_{G}
as an algorithm of subgroup partition, we obtain data-driven subgroups

{\mathcal{G}}={\mathcal{A}}_{G}\left({\mathcal{F}}\right)=(\mathfrak{g}_{1},\cdots,\mathfrak{g}_{K})
,
which is deterministic given
{\mathcal{F}}
.

Inferential target.

At the unit level, we consider the null hypothesis:
\forall i\in[I]
,
\forall j\in[n_{i}]
,

\displaystyle H^{(0)}_{ij}:t_{ij}=c_{ij}\text{ versus }H^{(1)}_{ij}:t_{ij}\neq c_{ij}.

(4)

In concrete contexts, we may also be interested in positive effects, in which case we consider
H^{(0)}_{ij}:t_{ij}=c_{ij}
versus
H^{(1)}_{ij}:t_{ij}>c_{ij}
.
At the matched-set level, the null hypothesis for each
i\in[I]
is defined by
the intersection of the individual-level nulls, where
H_{i}^{(0)}:\cap_{j\in[n_{i}]}\;H_{ij}^{(0)}
.
Given subgroups
{\mathcal{G}}=(\mathfrak{g}_{1},\cdots,\mathfrak{g}_{K})
,
we further define the subgroup-level null hypotheses:

\displaystyle H^{(0)}_{\mathfrak{g}}=\bigcap_{i\in\mathfrak{g}}\;H_{i}^{(0)},\qquad\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}.

(5)

Formulating subgroup analysis as a multiple testing problem for
\{H^{(0)}_{\mathfrak{g}}
,
\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}\}
,
our goal is to identify a set of subgroups
{\mathcal{S}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}
that are likely to
have significant treatment effect while controlling the FDR:

\displaystyle\mathrm{FDR}({\mathcal{S}}_{{\mathcal{G}}};{\mathcal{G}})=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\sum_{\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}}\mathds{1}\left\{\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{{\mathcal{G}}},\;H^{(0)}_{\mathfrak{g}}\;\text{true}\right\}}{|{\mathcal{S}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}|\vee 1}\penalty\ \bigg|\penalty\ {\mathcal{A}}_{G}\left({\mathcal{F}}\right)={\mathcal{G}}\right]\leq\alpha,

(6)

for a given nominal level
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Here, the expectation is conditional on the identified subgroups as the output of
{\mathcal{A}}_{G}
,
and post-selection bias should be taken into consideration.

We emphasize that our inferential target is at the subgroup level.
While one could instead conduct inference at the matched-set level (e.g., in
25
),
the resulting discoveries—indexed by
i\in[I]
—can be hard to interpret
and to generalize beyond the observed samples.
For example, in the college education dataset, if a matched pair of graduates from Wisconsin with covariates
rural residence=Yes
,
mother’s education=12 years
, and
number of siblings=3
is selected as a “subgroup”, it is not clear how this result translates into our understanding of the
benefit of college education.
Moreover, for inference at the matched set level, within-subgroup variability cannot be estimated, making the interpretability, replicability, and statistical significance of such findings unclear.

3
Subgroup selection with matched pairs

We begin with the matched-pair setting, i.e.,
n_{i}=2
for all
i\in[I]
.
To fix ideas, we focus on the one-sided unit-level alternative

H_{ij}^{(1)}:t_{ij}>c_{ij}
, together with the induced matched-set- and group-level hypotheses.
Extension to two-sided alternatives is provided to Appendix
C.3
.
In the college education example, this formulation corresponds to identifying subgroups in which college education exerts a positive effect on individuals’ income.

3.1
Construction of test statistics

Suppose the partition
{\mathcal{G}}
is given. For each matched pair
i\in[I]
, define
the treated-control difference in outcomes
Y_{i}=(R_{i1}-R_{i2})(Z_{i1}-Z_{i2})

and decompose it as

\displaystyle L_{i}=\textnormal{sgn}(Y_{i}),\quad W_{i}=|Y_{i}|.

Above, the
sign
statistic
L_{i}
captures the direction of the effect,
while the
magnitude
statistic
W_{i}
estimates its size.
Under
H_{i}^{(0)}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(L_{i}=1\mid W_{i},{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{Z}})=\mathbb{P}(Z_{i1}=\mathds{1}\{c_{i1}-c_{i2}>0\}\mid{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{Z}})\in\left[\frac{1}{1+\Gamma},\frac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma}\right],

(7)

where the equality follows from

W_{i}=|c_{i1}-c_{i2}|
under
H_{i}^{(0)}
and
the final inclusion is a direct consequence of Equation (
3
)
under the
\Gamma
-sensitivity model.

We now pass from the matched-pair level to the group level.
For each
\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}
, denote
{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
a subset of indices
determined solely by
\{W_{i}:\;i\in\mathfrak{g}\}
.
For example, we may define
{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}=\{i\in\mathfrak{g}:W_{i}\geq W_{(k)}\}
,
where
W_{(k)}
is the
k
-th largest value among
\{W_{i}:\;i\in\mathfrak{g}\}
.
Using the units in
{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}
as the group representative, we define:

\displaystyle L_{\mathfrak{g}}\;=\;2\cdot\mathds{1}\!\left\{\,\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}}\,L_{i}\;>\;2\,\eta_{\mathfrak{g}}-|{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}|\,\right\}-1,\qquad V_{\mathfrak{g}}\;=\;\bigl(L_{j}\bigr)_{j\in\mathfrak{g},\;j\notin{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}},

(8)

where the threshold
\eta_{\mathfrak{g}}
is the upper
\Gamma/(1+\Gamma)
-quantile of
the worst-case binomial distribution,

\eta_{\mathfrak{g}}\;:=\;\min\!\left\{\,t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\;:\;\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathrm{Bin}\bigl(|{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}|,\,\tfrac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma}\bigr)>t\right)\;\leq\;\tfrac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma}\,\right\}.

(9)

One special case is when
{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}
contains only

i^{*}(\mathfrak{g})=\underset{i\in\mathfrak{g}}{\arg\!\max}\penalty\ W_{i}
, i.e., the unit in the group with the largest magnitude outcome difference.
In this case,
|{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}|=1
,
the group-level sign statistic reduces to
L_{\mathfrak{g}}=L_{i^{*}(\mathfrak{g})}
,
and
V_{\mathfrak{g}}=(L_{j})_{j\in\mathfrak{g},j\neq i^{*}(\mathfrak{g})}
.

In words,
L_{\mathfrak{g}}
serves as a proxy for the overall direction of the treatment effect
within group
\mathfrak{g}
, while
V_{\mathfrak{g}}
collects the remaining sign information in the group.
Writing
W_{\mathfrak{g}}=(W_{i})_{i\in\mathfrak{g}}
, we define the
bounded skewness condition for
L_{\mathfrak{g}}
conditional on
(V_{\mathfrak{g}},W_{\mathfrak{g}})
.

Definition 1

(
\kappa
-bounded skewness)
.

For any
\kappa>0
, the group-level statistics
(L_{\mathfrak{g}},V_{\mathfrak{g}},W_{\mathfrak{g}})
are
said to satisfy the
\kappa
-bounded
skewness condition if,
\forall\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}
such that
H_{\mathfrak{g}}^{(0)}
is true,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(L_{\mathfrak{g}}=1\mid V_{\mathfrak{g}},W_{\mathfrak{g}},{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{Z}}\big)\leq\kappa\cdot\mathbb{P}\big(L_{\mathfrak{g}}=-1\mid V_{\mathfrak{g}},W_{\mathfrak{g}},{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{Z}}\big).

(10)

The following lemma characterizes the bounded skewness of the design in (
8
).

Lemma 1
.

For each
\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}
such that
H_{\mathfrak{g}}^{(0)}
is true, the group-level statistics
(L_{\mathfrak{g}},V_{\mathfrak{g}},W_{\mathfrak{g}})
defined in (
8
)
satisfy the
\kappa
-bounded skewness condition with
\kappa=\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(|{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}|,\tfrac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma})>\eta_{\mathfrak{g}})/\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(|{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}|,\tfrac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma})\leq\eta_{\mathfrak{g}})
,
which is further bounded by
\Gamma
.

The proof of Lemma
1
is provided in Appendix
B.1
.
The bounded skewness condition enforces a form of partial “orthogonality”
between
L_{\mathfrak{g}}
and
(V_{\mathfrak{g}},W_{\mathfrak{g}})
. In particular, it allows us
to treat
(V_{\mathfrak{g}},W_{\mathfrak{g}})
as fixed—that is, as the masked statistics introduced earlier—and
to estimate the number of null groups
with
L_{\mathfrak{g}}=1
by counting the number of subgroups with
L_{\mathfrak{g}}=-1
as a proxy.

3.2
The subgroup selection procedure

Our proposed method performs subgroup selection through a sequential screening procedure.
In this process, subgroups exhibiting weak evidence of positive treatment effects are progressively
filtered out, while the FDP among the remaining subgroups is monitored. Once the estimated
FDP falls below the target level
\alpha
, the procedure stops, and the remaining subgroups
are declared discoveries.

The key to FDR control is that the sign statistics
L_{\mathfrak{g}}
are (partially)
masked during screening. Instead of directly using their values,
we rely on the remaining information to predict or “guess” them.
Error control follows from keeping the true signs hidden,
while the power of the procedure depends on how accurately we can predict them.
The procedure consists of four main components: (i) initialization, (ii) the screening order, (iii) the FDP estimator, and (iv) the stopping rule. We detail each component in turn.

Initialization.

For each group
\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}
, we generate a variable

\xi_{\mathfrak{g}}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\text{Bern}(\gamma)
,
independent of all other quantities.
These auxiliary binary variables are used to randomly split the groups with

L_{\mathfrak{g}}=-1
, where we define:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}^{-}_{1}=\{\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}:L_{\mathfrak{g}}=-1,\xi_{\mathfrak{g}}=0\},\quad{\mathcal{I}}^{-}_{2}=\{\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}:L_{\mathfrak{g}}=-1,\xi_{\mathfrak{g}}=1\},

(11)

and
{\mathcal{I}}^{+}=\{\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}:L_{\mathfrak{g}}=1\}
.
That is, the groups with negative representative signs are randomly divided into two folds,
while the positive groups are kept intact. By construction,
{\mathcal{I}}^{-}_{1}

consists of groups that appear unlikely to exhibit a positive treatment effect
and will serve as a labeled set of “negative” examples. In contrast,

{\mathcal{I}}^{-}_{2}\cup{\mathcal{I}}^{+}
forms a mixed pool containing both pessimistic
and potentially promising groups.

We then treat
{\mathcal{I}}^{-}_{1}
as (negatively) labeled data and
{\mathcal{I}}^{-}_{2}\cup{\mathcal{I}}^{+}

as unlabeled data to build an initial model
\hat{\mu}_{0}
for
L_{\mathfrak{g}}
.
The model is trained to predict
L_{\mathfrak{g}}
from the group-specific features

(x_{i})_{i\in\mathfrak{g}}
, together with
(V_{\mathfrak{g}},W_{\mathfrak{g}})
.
This setup naturally fits into the semi-supervised learning framework
(e.g.,
69
;
22
), and in principle,
any semi-supervised learning algorithm may be used to fit
\hat{\mu}_{0}
,
provided that the true signs
\{L_{\mathfrak{g}}\}_{\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{I}}^{-}_{2}\cup{\mathcal{I}}^{+}}

remain masked throughout the training process.

Screening order.

Equipped with the initial model
\hat{\mu}_{0}
, we proceed to
examine the groups in
{\mathcal{I}}:={\mathcal{I}}^{-}_{2}\cup{\mathcal{I}}^{+}
.
Let
{\mathcal{O}}_{t}\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}
denote the set of subgroups that have been screened out at step

t\in\{0,1,\ldots,|{\mathcal{I}}|\}
, initialized at
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}={\mathcal{I}}^{-}_{1}
.
At time
t\geq 1
, we use the prediction model
\hat{\mu}_{t-1}
to score
each remaining group:

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\mathfrak{g}}:=\hat{\mu}_{t-1}\big((x_{i})_{i\in\mathfrak{g}},V_{\mathfrak{g}},W_{\mathfrak{g}}\big),\penalty\ \forall\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{t-1},

with the convention that a larger value of
\hat{L}_{\mathfrak{g}}
indicates a higher
likelihood that
L_{\mathfrak{g}}=1
.
We then remove the group with the weakest predicted evidence for a positive effect, updating

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}_{t}={\mathcal{O}}_{t-1}\cup\bigg\{\pi(t):=\underset{\mathfrak{g}\notin{\mathcal{O}}_{t-1}}{\arg\!\min}\penalty\ \hat{L}_{\mathfrak{g}}\bigg\}.

(12)

The prediction model is then updated to
\hat{\mu}_{t}
.
Crucially, its training may only use the information revealed at step
t
, namely,
{\mathcal{M}}_{t}:=\{\{x_{i}\}_{i\in[I]},\{V_{\mathfrak{g}},W_{\mathfrak{g}}\}_{\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}},\{L_{i}:i\in\mathfrak{g}\}_{\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{t}}\}
.
That is, while the covariates, treatment-difference magnitudes
W_{\mathfrak{g}}
, and residual sign information

V_{\mathfrak{g}}
are always available, the sign statistics
L_{\mathfrak{g}}

are only used for groups that have already been screened out. The signs of the unscreened groups remain masked.
The updated prediction model
\hat{\mu}_{t}
is then used to re-score the groups
\mathfrak{g}\notin{\mathcal{O}}_{t}
,
and the screened-out set is updated according to (
12
).

FDP estimate and stopping rule.

At each step
t\geq 0
, we estimate the FDP by

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{FDP}}(t)=\frac{\kappa}{\gamma}\cdot\frac{(1+N_{t})}{1\vee P_{t}},\quad\text{where }P_{t}\coloneqq\sum_{\mathfrak{g}\notin{\mathcal{O}}_{t}}\mathds{1}\{L_{\mathfrak{g}}=1\},\;\;N_{t}\coloneqq\sum_{\mathfrak{g}\notin{\mathcal{O}}_{t}}\mathds{1}\{L_{\mathfrak{g}}=-1\}.

(13)

We define the stopping time as
\tau\coloneqq\inf\{t\in\{0\}\cup[|{\mathcal{I}}|]:\;\widehat{\mathrm{FDP}}(t)\leq\alpha\}

with the convention that
\inf\varnothing=\infty
and
{\mathcal{O}}_{\infty}={\mathcal{G}}
.
We obtain a selected subset of subgroups

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{S}}\coloneqq\left\{\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}:\;\mathfrak{g}\notin{\mathcal{O}}_{\tau},L_{\mathfrak{g}}>0\right\}.

(14)

We conclude with a remark on the role of
\xi_{\mathfrak{g}}
’s.

Remark 1

(The role of
\xi_{\mathfrak{g}}
’s)
.

Our proposed method incorporates external randomness
through the
\xi_{\mathfrak{g}}
’s, which are introduced for sample splitting
to improve the initialization of the prediction model.
This sample splitting strategy takes a special form that follows
29
.
Unlike standard sample-splitting procedures, it only “sacrifices”
the groups with
L_{\mathfrak{g}}=-1
that were not to be selected otherwise.
We emphasize that this randomization is optional: when randomness is undesirable,
it can be “turned off” by setting
\gamma=1
.

3.3
Finite-sample FDR control

To understand why our proposed procedure leads to finite-sample FDR control, we
first examine the intuition behind the FDP estimator.
Recall that
L_{\mathfrak{g}}
satisfies the
\kappa
-bounded skewness condition (Definition
1
).
Consequently, among the subgroups still under consideration (unscreened) at step
t
,
the number of nulls with

L_{\mathfrak{g}}=1
can be upper bounded (heuristically) by those with
L_{\mathfrak{g}}=1
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{g}\notin O_{t}}\mathds{1}\{L_{\mathfrak{g}}=1,H_{\mathfrak{g}}^{(0)}\text{is true}\}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\mathfrak{g}\notin O_{t}}\frac{1}{\gamma}\mathds{1}\{L_{\mathfrak{g}}=1,\xi_{\mathfrak{g}}=1,H_{\mathfrak{g}}^{(0)}\text{is true}\}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\mathfrak{g}\notin O_{t}}\frac{\kappa}{\gamma}\mathds{1}\{L_{\mathfrak{g}}=-1,\xi_{\mathfrak{g}}=1,H_{\mathfrak{g}}^{(0)}\text{is true}\}\leq\frac{\kappa}{\gamma}(1+N_{t}).

It follows that the true false discovery proportion among the unscreened subgroups,

\text{FDP}(t)=\frac{\sum_{\mathfrak{g}\notin O_{t}}\mathds{1}\{L_{\mathfrak{g}}=1,H_{\mathfrak{g}}^{(0)}\text{ is true}\}}{1\vee P_{t}}\lesssim\widehat{\text{FDP}}(t).

In words,
\widehat{\text{FDP}}(t)
serves as a conservative estimate of FDP
(t)
,
and the choice of the stopping time
\tau
ensures
\widehat{\text{FDP}}(\tau)

to be bounded by
\alpha
, which in turn yields control of
\text{FDP}(\tau)
.
Of course, the derivation above is only heuristic, as indicated by the use of the
“
\lesssim
” notation and by informally treating
\tau
as a constant.
The rigorous argument requires a more careful conditioning and leave-one-out analysis,
which is provided in Theorem
1
. In what follows,
we write
{\mathcal{H}}_{0,{\mathcal{G}}}=\{\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}:\;H^{(0)}_{\mathfrak{g}}\;\text{is true}\}

for the collection of null subgroups.

Theorem 1
.

Assume (
SUTVA
) holds. Given the groups
{\mathcal{G}}={\mathcal{A}}_{G}({\mathcal{F}})
, suppose the
group-level sign statistics
(L_{\mathfrak{g}},V_{\mathfrak{g}},W_{\mathfrak{g}})_{\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}}
satisfy the
\kappa
-boundedness
condition in Definition
1
. The selection set
\hat{\mathcal{S}}

produced by (
14
) satisfies

\mathrm{FDR}(\hat{\mathcal{S}};{\mathcal{G}})=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\sum_{\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0,{\mathcal{G}}}}\mathds{1}\{\mathfrak{g}\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}\}}{1\vee|\hat{\mathcal{S}}|}\penalty\ \bigg|\penalty\ {\mathcal{G}}\right]\leq\alpha.

The proof of Theorem
1
is provided in Appendix
B.2
.

3.4
Further power improvement via conditional calibration

When the number of tested groups or the number of
groups with treatment effects is small, our
proposed method in Section
3.2
may be underpowered due
to the “+1” term in the numerator. Indeed, we need
P_{t}
to be
at least
\kappa/(\gamma\alpha)
for
\widehat{\text{FDP}}(t)
to (possibly) drop
below the required level. We alleviate this issue by applying the

conditional calibration
technique
(
46
;
40
)

to our procedure in Section
3.2
as a post hoc adjustment.

To instantiate the conditional calibration framework, we consider
an auxiliary statistic
A_{\mathfrak{g}}=h(L_{\mathfrak{g}},V_{\mathfrak{g}})
for some
nonnegative function
h
and define a modified stopping time

\displaystyle T^{\mathsf{early}}=\inf\bigg\{t\in\{0\}\cup[|{\mathcal{I}}|]:\widehat{\text{FDP}}(t)\leq\alpha\text{ or }P_{t}<\frac{\kappa}{\gamma\alpha}\bigg\},

where the sequential procedure is stopped earlier in the “hopeless” scenario, i.e.,
when
P_{t}<\kappa/(\gamma\alpha)
.
By construction,
T^{\mathsf{early}}
only differs from
T
when
\hat{\mathcal{S}}=\varnothing
. We
augment
\hat{\mathcal{S}}
as
\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathsf{cc}}=\hat{\mathcal{S}}\cup\{\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}\backslash\hat{\mathcal{S}}:E_{\mathfrak{g}}(A_{\mathfrak{g}})\leq 0\}
,
where, with
L_{i}\overset{\textnormal{ind}}{\sim}\text{Bern}(p_{i})
,

\displaystyle E_{\mathfrak{g}}(c):=\sup_{p_{i}\in[l_{\Gamma},u_{\Gamma}],i\in\mathfrak{g}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\mathds{1}\{\mathfrak{g}\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}\text{ or }A_{\mathfrak{g}}\geq c\}}{|\hat{\mathcal{S}}\cup\{\mathfrak{g}\}|}-{{\frac{\alpha\gamma}{\kappa}\cdot}}\frac{\mathds{1}\{\mathfrak{g}\notin{\mathcal{O}}_{T^{\mathsf{early}}},L_{\mathfrak{g}}=1\}}{1+N_{T^{\mathsf{early}}}}\,\Big|\,\{L_{i}\}_{i\notin\mathfrak{g}},\{W_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}},\xi_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}}\}_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{G}}}\bigg]

and
(l_{\Gamma},u_{\Gamma})
refer to the lower and upper bounds on

p_{i}
under the
\Gamma
-sensitivity model.

By construction,
\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathsf{cc}}\supseteq\hat{\mathcal{S}}
, and therefore uniformly improves
the power over the baseline.
The following theorem shows that
\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathsf{cc}}
also controls the FDR.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose the same assumptions of Theorem
1
hold.
The augmented selection set satisfies
\text{FDR}(\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathsf{cc}};{\mathcal{G}})\leq\alpha
;

We provide the proof in Appendix
B.3

and discuss the implementation details in Section
D.1
.

4
Subgroup selection with multiple controls

We previously focused on the case
n_{i}=2
for all
i\in[I]
, i.e.,
each matched set contains one treated and one control unit.
We now extend our proposed method to full matching, where multiple controls can be
matched to a treated unit. As before, we restrict our attention to testing for a positive
treatment effect for exposition.

Under the full-matching setting, the main difference lies in
constructing the matched set-level statistics
(L_{i},W_{i})
.
With multiple controls, each matched set contains richer information about the null distribution,
which can—in principle—enable more powerful procedures.
The key challenge, however, is how to effectively leverage the additional
information provided by multiple controls to improve the statistical power
of subgroup selection. As we will see, a naïve
generalization of the
n_{i}\equiv 2
case can in fact lead to power loss,
while our proposed full-matching variant overcomes the issue.
We detail the construction of
(W_{i},L_{i})
below; the remainder of the subgroup selection
procedure follows as in the
n_{i}=2
case.

4.1
Matched set-level statistics

Recall that in the
n_{i}=2
case, we define the matched set-level sign statistic as
the sign of the treatment-control difference
L_{i}=\textnormal{sgn}((Z_{i1}-Z_{i2})(R_{i1}-R_{i2}))
.
Equivalently, we may write
L_{i}=\mathds{1}\{r_{i}=1\}-\mathds{1}\{r_{i}=2\}
in the absence of ties,
where
r_{i}
is the rank of
the treated outcome among
\{R_{i1},R_{i2}\}
in decreasing order.
This reformulation admits a natural generalization to the
full-matching setting: for each
i\in[I]
, define

\displaystyle L_{i}=\mathds{1}\left\{r_{i}\leq\lfloor n_{i}/2\rfloor\right\}-\mathds{1}\left\{r_{i}\geq\lceil n_{i}/2\rceil+1\right\},

(16)

where
r_{i}
is now the rank of the treated outcome among
\{R_{ij}\}_{j\in[n_{i}]}

in decreasing order, with the ties broken randomly.

Under the
\Gamma
-sensitivity model, if the null hypothesis
H_{i}^{(0)}
is true,
then for each
r\in[n_{i}]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(r_{i}=r\mid\{R_{i(j)}:\;j\in[n_{i}]\}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{i(r)}=1\mid\{R_{i(j)}:\;j\in[n_{i}]\}\right)\in\left[\frac{1}{1+(n_{i}-1)\Gamma},\frac{\Gamma}{n_{i}-1+\Gamma}\right],

(17)

where
R_{i(r)}
denotes the element in
\{R_{ij}:\;j\in[n_{i}]\}
ranked
r
and

Z_{i(r)}
is its corresponding treatment assignment (conditioning on the tie-breaking randomness if there are ties).
This characterization implies that
L_{i}
obeys a (marginal) bounded skewness condition,
formalized in Lemma
2
;
its proof is provided
in Appendix
B.6
.

Lemma 2
.

For any
i\in[I]
, if
H_{i}^{(0)}
is true, then
L_{i}
defined in (
16
) satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(L_{i}=1{\,|\,}{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{Z}})\leq\kappa(n_{i},\Gamma)\cdot\mathbb{P}(L_{i}=-1{\,|\,}{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{Z}}),\text{ where }\kappa(n_{i},\Gamma)=\frac{(n_{i}-1)(\Gamma-1)+{\left\lfloor{n_{i}/2}\right\rfloor}}{\lfloor n_{i}/2\rfloor}.

We now turn to the construction of magnitude statistics.
Recall that when
n_{i}\equiv 2
,
W_{i}=|Y_{i}|
serves as a proxy for the
matched set-level effect magnitude. Directly extending the idea to
multiple controls leads to several natural choices:

\displaystyle(\text{Max})\penalty\ W_{i}=R_{i(1)};\quad(\text{TopGap})\penalty\ W_{i}=R_{i(1)}-R_{i(2)};\quad(\text{MedSplit})\penalty\ W_{i}=R_{i(1)}-R_{i(\lfloor n_{i}/2\rfloor)}.

(18)

Here, option (TopGap) is from
30
,
while option (MedSplit) is from
33
.
However, these direct generalizations may
lead to power loss when
n_{i}
increases, as illustrated in Figure
1(b)

in the introduction.

To understand this seemingly counterintuitive phenomenon, consider the following
thought experiment. For each
i\in[I]
, suppose
c_{ij}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)

and
t_{ij}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}{\mathcal{N}}(\tau,1)

\tau>0
;
the treatment assignment is uniform in
[n_{i}]
.
As
n_{i}\rightarrow\infty
, we have
W_{i}=O_{p}(\sqrt{2\log n_{i}})

for (Max) and (MedSplit) and

for (TopGap), regardless

This gap suggests that the current construction of the magnitude statistics
fails to exploit all available information. To fill in the gap, we introduce the
“masked” rank:

\displaystyle g(r_{i})=\min(r_{i},n_{i}+1-r_{i}).

By construction,

Crucially, the sign statistic

Lemma 3
.

For any

, under

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(L_{i}=1{\,|\,}g(r_{i}),{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{Z}})\leq\Gamma\cdot\mathbb{P}(L_{i}=-1{\,|\,}g(r_{i}),{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{Z}}).

Desiderata for the optimal magnitude statistic

We now turn to the design of

using

Given a masked rank

We study the optimal form of

under a Gaussian model:

\displaystyle R_{ij}{\,|\,}X_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{ind}}}{{\sim}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mu(X_{i})+\tau\mathds{1}\{j=q\},\sigma^{2}),\text{ for }j\in[n_{i}],

unknown

Marginally, the covariate
X_{i}
is generated from some unknown covariate distribution
P_{X}
.
We denote the preimage of the masked rank as
{\mathcal{J}}_{i}=\{r_{q},r_{q^{\prime}}\}

where
r_{q}
is the rank of the treated unit and
r_{q^{\prime}}
is that of the control unit that is masked together with

Recalling the structure of our subgroup selection procedure,
we see that its selection power is driven by the
order
of the groups:
more discoveries can be made if
the groups with negative sign statistics are identified and revealed early in the
screening procedure. Motivated by this, we seek to construct
W_{i}
that
optimally distinguishes between
L_{i}=-1
and
L_{i}=1
.

Concretely, we seek a predictor
\hat{L}_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
, based on
the masked index set
{\mathcal{J}}_{i}
, the order statistic
\tilde{R}_{i}=(R_{i(j)})_{j\in[n_{i}]}
,
and the covariate
X_{i}

that minimizes the following classification error
\ell(\hat{L}_{i})=\mathbb{P}(L_{i}=1,\hat{L}_{i}=-1)+\mathbb{P}(L_{i}=-1,\hat{L}_{i}=1)
.
Theorem
3
establishes that the optimal predictor is the sign of the log-likelihood ratio:

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{i}=\textnormal{sgn}\big(\beta({\mathcal{J}}_{i},\tilde{R}_{i},X_{i})\big),\text{ where }\beta({\mathcal{J}}_{i},\tilde{R}_{i},X_{i})=\log\frac{\mathbb{P}(L_{i}=1\mid{\mathcal{J}}_{i},\tilde{R}_{i},X_{i})}{\mathbb{P}(L_{i}=-1\mid{\mathcal{J}}_{i},\tilde{R}_{i},X_{i})},

(20)

and moreover,
\beta({\mathcal{J}}_{i},\tilde{R}_{i},X_{i})
is a strictly increasing function of
W_{i}^{{\rm NP}}=|R_{iq}-R_{iq^{\prime}}|
.

Theorem 3
.

Under the Gaussian model (
19
), for any fixed
\tau>0
,

,
\mu(\cdot)
, and
\sigma^{2}
, there is

(1)

the predictor
\hat{L}_{i}
defined in (

) minimizes the classification error
\ell(\hat{L}_{i})
;

(2)

the log-likelihood ratio
\beta({\mathcal{J}}_{i},\tilde{R}_{i},X_{i})
is strictly

W^{\rm NP}_{i}
.

See proof in Appendix
B.8
. Theorem
3
suggests
that
\textnormal{sgn}(\beta({\mathcal{J}}_{i},\tilde{R}_{i},X_{i}))
provides the best prediction for
L_{i}
while

preserves its order; we therefore adopt

Appendix A
Additional related work

Subgroup partition and selection.

Subgroup analysis has been of significant interest in a broad range of applications, such as stratified medicine in clinical trials
(
64
;
58
;
45
)
and heterogeneous treatment-effect discovery in program and policy evaluation
(
3
)
.
Identifying subgroups from data is the first emerging question.
There is a rich literature on learning subgroups from data, in particular on tree-based methods, including the CART algorithm
(
66
;
11
)
, causal trees
(
3
)
, and causal forests
(
67
)
.

The data-driven nature of tree-based approaches is a strength, but it can also be a liability: with data-dependent subgroups as inferential targets, how to characterize or relieve the selection bias has become quite challenging, making valid error control elusive without data splitting.

A thread of earlier works assumes a fixed class of subgroups
(
9
;
44
;
18
)
, with which one can perform simultaneous inference over the entire class, which avoids data-driven partitioning and post-selection complications, at the cost of restricting attention to an analyst-chosen subgroup family and limiting the flexibility of subgroup partition.
A generic solution that enables valid inference on subgroups is data splitting
(
21
)
, which is assumption-free but may lead to a loss in discovery power due to a reduced sample size.
Recursive/repeated data splitting
(
17
)
, on the other hand, can be ambiguous about the inferential targets as inference for subgroups is performed on an aggregation of multiple confirmatory datasets.

Post-selection validity and multiplicity adjustment of subgroup selection.

Given a collection of data-driven subgroups,
two key questions arise:
(1) how to identify and select subgroups with nonzero effects using the same dataset, and
(2) how to control the (in-sample) false discoveries, while conditioning on data-driven subgroups?
Several lines of work address these questions in different ways.
The first type of work focuses on the
conditional average treatment effect (CATE)
, i.e.,
the average treatment effect conditional on the observed covariates.
The goal there is to identify a subset
{\mathcal{R}}
in the covariate space such that

\mu({\mathcal{R}})
—the average treatment effect given the event that
X\in{\mathcal{R}}
—exceeds
some pre-specified threshold
\tau_{0}
with probability at least
1-\alpha
.
As demonstrated in the literature, (asymptotically) valid selection of
{\mathcal{R}}
is feasible
when CATE can be estimated efficiently, allowing for the construction of asymptotically valid
confidence bands

(
2
;
32
;
51
;
67
)
.
Recent work further extends these results to classes of CATE functionals under mild shape constraints
(
49
;
47
)
.
More recently,
16
introduces an interactive approach that achieves type-I error
control in search of
{\mathcal{R}}
without data splitting, although its extension to observational data remains an open question.

The error metric
\mathbb{P}(\mu({\mathcal{R}})\leq\tau_{0})
in the aforementioned literature
is defined at the population level.
In observational studies with a finite sample, however,
analysts often seek an
in-sample
notion of error control,
where the error rate is defined conditional on the observed sample.
To this end, earlier work by
37
;
41
;
27

focuses on controlling the type-I error for testing the global null across subgroups,
while
38
propose a subgroup selection approach that controls
the
family-wise error rate (FWER)
among selected subgroups.
Moving to the FDR
(
7
;
31
)
,
which provides a more informative criterion in large-scale multiple testing problems,

39
apply the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure to
subgroup-level p-values for FDR control with data-driven subgroups.
More recently,
25
introduced an interactive procedure for subgroup selection
with FDR control. Their method is the closest to ours, but designed primarily for
the matched-pair setting without unmeasured confounding. A more detailed comparison
is described in Section
3.5
.

B.1
Proof of Lemma
1

By definition,
L_{\mathfrak{g}}=1
if and only if
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}}\mathds{1}\{L_{i}=1\}>\eta_{\mathfrak{g}}
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(L_{\mathfrak{g}}=1\mid V_{\mathfrak{g}},W_{\mathfrak{g}},{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{Z}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}}\mathds{1}\{L_{i}=1\}>\eta_{\mathfrak{g}}\,\Big|\,V_{\mathfrak{g}},W_{\mathfrak{g}},{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{Z}}\right)

(21)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{Bin}\bigl(|{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}|,\tfrac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma}\bigr)>\eta_{\mathfrak{g}}\right),

(22)

where the inequality follows from the fact that, under
H_{\mathfrak{g}}^{(0)}
,
the distribution of
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}}\mathds{1}\{L_{i}=1\}
is stochastically dominated by
\mathrm{Bin}(|{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}|,\tfrac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma})
conditional on
(V_{\mathfrak{g}},W_{\mathfrak{g}},{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{Z}})
.
(
22
) implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(L_{\mathfrak{g}}=-1\mid V_{\mathfrak{g}},W_{\mathfrak{g}},{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{Z}})\geq\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(|{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}|,\tfrac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma})\leq\eta_{\mathfrak{g}}).

(23)

Combining (
22
) and (
23
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(L_{\mathfrak{g}}=1\mid V_{\mathfrak{g}},W_{\mathfrak{g}},{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{Z}})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{Bin}\bigl(|{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}|,\tfrac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma}\bigr)>\eta_{\mathfrak{g}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{Bin}\bigl(|{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}|,\tfrac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma}\bigr)>\eta_{\mathfrak{g}}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{Bin}\bigl(|{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}|,\tfrac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma}\bigr)\leq\eta_{\mathfrak{g}}\right)}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{Bin}\bigl(|{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}|,\tfrac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma}\bigr)\leq\eta_{\mathfrak{g}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{Bin}\bigl(|{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}|,\tfrac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma}\bigr)>\eta_{\mathfrak{g}}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{Bin}\bigl(|{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}|,\tfrac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma}\bigr)\leq\eta_{\mathfrak{g}}\right)}\cdot\mathbb{P}(L_{\mathfrak{g}}=-1\mid V_{\mathfrak{g}},W_{\mathfrak{g}},{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{Z}}).

That is, the group-level statistics
(L_{\mathfrak{g}},V_{\mathfrak{g}},W_{\mathfrak{g}})
we construct
satisfy the bounded skewness condition with
\kappa=\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(|{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}|,\tfrac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma})>\eta_{\mathfrak{g}})/\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(|{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}|,\tfrac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma})\leq\eta_{\mathfrak{g}})
.
Finally, by the choice of
\eta_{\mathfrak{g}}
in (
9
), we have
\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(|{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}|,\tfrac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma})>\eta_{\mathfrak{g}})\leq\Gamma/(1+\Gamma)
and
\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(|{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathfrak{g}}|,\tfrac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma})\leq\eta_{\mathfrak{g}})\geq 1/(1+\Gamma)
, which implies
\kappa\leq\Gamma
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\mathds{1}\{\mathfrak{g}\notin{\mathcal{O}}_{\tau}(\bm{L},\Xi),L_{\mathfrak{g}}=1\}}{1+\sum_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0,{\mathcal{G}}},\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\neq\mathfrak{g}}\mathds{1}\{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\notin{\mathcal{O}}_{\tau}(\bm{L},\Xi),L_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}}=-1\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\penalty\

C.2
Screening by incorporating additional information

In our framework, the sign-magnitude pair is used in a disentangled way:
the sign is used for FDP estimation, while the magnitude determines the screening ordering.
For FDR control, the magnitude statistic is subject to essentially no restriction
beyond a near-independence condition with the sign.
This separation offers substantial flexibility that allows additional considerations
or side information to be incorporated in the design of
W_{\mathfrak{g}}
—provided
they are independent of the treatment assignment.

One practically important consideration is subgroup size.
In many applications, very small subgroups may raise concerns about reproducibility
and/or exacerbate multiplicity issues for classical p-value-based
methods
(
12
;
68
)
.
On the other hand, larger subgroups are also often more relevant in clinical settings,
as they indicate broader applicability of a discovery.

This motivates us to incorporate
|\mathfrak{g}|
, which is independent of
L_{\mathfrak{g}}
,
into the magnitude statistic without affecting the bounded skewness and hence the FDR control.
One straightforward variant is to define
\tilde{W}_{\mathfrak{g}}=|\mathfrak{g}|^{b}\cdot W_{g}
,
where
b>0
is up to practitioners’ choice.
Since
W^{\rm NP}_{\mathfrak{g}}
is shown to be optimal under Gaussian models, replacing it with its counterpart
\tilde{W}^{\rm NP}_{\mathfrak{g}}
tends to exhibit the loss in power (in terms of the
number of selected groups).

C.3
Testing two-sided effects

In practical scenarios, however, the signs of underlying effects are unknown without prior knowledge. Then, to identify affected subgroups, we expect our inferential procedure to be aware of both positive and negative effects, in which the alternative hypothesis takes the form
H^{(0)}_{i}:\;t_{ij}\neq c_{ij}
.

Recalling the procedure targeted at non-negative effects, both the validity and the power of discoveries rely on the desired decomposition
(W_{i},L_{i})
. Concretely, the statistic
W_{i}\cdot L_{i}=Y_{i}=(R_{i1}-R_{i2})(Z_{i1}-Z_{i2})
tends to be positive when the one-sided alternative
t_{ij}>c_{ij}
is true for all
j\in[n_{i}]
and is centered around zero otherwise.
Then, to identify two-sided effects, one solution is to define a statistic
Y_{i}
as a function of
(R_{ij},Z_{ij},X_{i})
such that it is nearly symmetric around zero under the null hypothesis and antisymmetric under the alternative, with which, the decomposition
(W_{i}=|Y_{i}|,L_{i}=\textnormal{sgn}(Y_{i}))
still satisfies the ideal properties in Section
3
.

To start with, it suffices to define a variant
\tilde{R}_{ij}
of
R_{ij}
that can inform the treatment effect such that, under the alternative, with a high probability,
\tilde{R}_{ij}>\tilde{R}_{ij^{\prime}}
if
Z_{ij}=1
regardless of the sign of the effect, and in the meantime,
\tilde{R}_{ij}\approx\tilde{R}_{ij^{\prime}}
both of which are centered around zero under the null.
To achieve this, we need to center the original outcome
R_{ij}
and flip its sign when the effect is negative, which requires knowledge of the null distribution of outcomes.
Suppose we have a predictive model
R_{0}(X_{i})
for
R_{ij}
under the null (i.e.,
R_{0}(X_{i})\overset{d}{\approx}(R_{ij}\mid Z_{ij}=0)
, which is only a function of covariates.
Then, we can define
\tilde{R}_{ij}=(R_{ij}-R_{0}(X_{i}))^{2}
, with which,
Y_{i}
can be defined by

\displaystyle Y_{i}=(\tilde{R}_{i1}-\tilde{R}_{i2})(Z_{i1}-Z_{i2})=(R_{i1}-R_{i2})(R_{i1}+R_{i2}-2R_{0}(X_{i}))(Z_{i1}-Z_{i2}).

(60)

Accordingly, we can follow the one-sided case to define subgroup-level statistics
(W_{\mathfrak{g}},L_{\mathfrak{g}})
for all
\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}
.
We note that, in (
60
), the power of our procedure may rely on the quality of the predictive model
R_{0}
. To avoid double-dipping, the predictive model can be updated at each step
t
in Algorithm
1
using screened data from
{\mathcal{O}}_{t}
.

D.1
Implementation details of conditional calibration

This section describes the implementation details of the conditional calibration
(
CC
) step introduced in Section
3.4
.
Throughout, we take
h(L_{\mathfrak{g}},V_{\mathfrak{g}})=-\log p_{\mathfrak{g},\Gamma}
,
where
p_{\mathfrak{g},\Gamma}
is the sensitivity p-value for group

\mathfrak{g}
resulting from the group-global signed-rank test.

It remains to specify how to compute
E_{\mathfrak{g}}(A_{\mathfrak{g}})
.
Recall that we condition on
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathfrak{g}}=(\{L_{i}\}_{i\notin\mathfrak{g}},\;\{W_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}},\xi_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}}\}_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{G}}})
, which can be viewed as fixed in the computation.
To emphasize the dependence on
(L_{\mathfrak{g}},V_{\mathfrak{g}})
, we write the budget as

B_{\mathfrak{g}}(\ell,v)=\frac{\gamma\alpha}{\kappa}\cdot\frac{\mathds{1}\{\mathfrak{g}\notin{\mathcal{O}}_{T^{\mathsf{early}}(\ell,v)}(\ell,v),\;\ell=1\}}{1+N_{T^{\mathsf{early}}(\ell,v)}(\ell,v)}

and

\displaystyle\zeta_{a}(\ell,v)=\frac{\mathds{1}\{\mathfrak{g}\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}(\ell,v)\}\vee\mathds{1}\{h(\ell,v)\geq a\}}{|\hat{\mathcal{S}}(\ell,v)\cup\{\mathfrak{g}\}|}-B_{\mathfrak{g}}(\ell,v),

(61)

where the respective quantities computed with
(L_{\mathfrak{g}},V_{\mathfrak{g}})

set to
(\ell,v)
.
Then, we can rewrite
E_{\mathfrak{g}}(a)
as

\displaystyle E_{\mathfrak{g}}(a)

\displaystyle=\sup_{p_{i}\in[\ell_{\Gamma},u_{\Gamma}]}\mathbb{E}_{L_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(p_{i}),i\in\mathfrak{g}}\big[\zeta_{a}(L^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{g}},V^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{g}})\big]

(62)

\displaystyle=\sup_{p_{i}\in[\ell_{\Gamma},u_{\Gamma}]}\sum_{\mathbf{L}\in\{-1,1\}^{|\mathfrak{g}|}}\zeta_{a}(\mathbf{L})\cdot\prod_{i\in[|\mathfrak{g}|]}p_{i}^{L_{i}}(1-p_{i})^{1-L_{i}}.

(63)

This is a multi-linear function of
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\cdots,p_{|\mathfrak{g}|})
, thus obtaining the maximum at a certain vertex.
The conditional calibrated selection is then defined by
\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathsf{cc},{\rm full}}=\hat{\mathcal{S}}\cup\{\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}:\;E_{\mathfrak{g}}(h(L_{\mathfrak{g}},V_{\mathfrak{g}}))\leq 0\}
.

A computationally light variant.

As an alternative, we condition on
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathfrak{g}}=(\{L_{i}\}_{i\notin\mathfrak{g}},\;V_{\mathfrak{g}},\;\{W_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}},\xi_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}}\}_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{G}}})
when defining
E_{\mathfrak{g}}(a)
.
Here,
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
treats
V_{\mathfrak{g}}
as fixed and is therefore a finer conditioning than
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathfrak{g}}
.
Concretely, we define

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{\mathfrak{g}}(a)=\sup_{p\in[\ell_{\Gamma},u_{\Gamma}]}\mathbb{E}_{L^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{g}}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(p)}\bigg[\frac{\mathds{1}\{\mathfrak{g}\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}(L^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{g}},V_{\mathfrak{g}})\}\vee\mathds{1}\{h(L^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{g}},V_{\mathfrak{g}})\geq a\}}{|\hat{\mathcal{S}}(L^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{g}},V_{\mathfrak{g}})\cup\{\mathfrak{g}\}|}-B_{\mathfrak{g}}(L^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{g}},V_{\mathfrak{g}})\;\bigg|\;\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathfrak{g}}\bigg],

(64)

where the expectation is
only
over the randomness of
L_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
.
To calculate
\tilde{E}_{\mathfrak{g}}(a)
, we similarly define

\displaystyle\tilde{\zeta}(\ell)=\frac{\mathds{1}\{\mathfrak{g}\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}(\ell,V_{\mathfrak{g}})\}\vee\mathds{1}\{h(\ell,V_{\mathfrak{g}})\geq a\}}{|\hat{\mathcal{S}}(\ell,V_{\mathfrak{g}})\cup\{\mathfrak{g}\}|}-B_{\mathfrak{g}}(\ell,V_{\mathfrak{g}}),

(65)

with which we have

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{\mathfrak{g}}(a)

\displaystyle=\sup_{p\in[\ell_{\Gamma},u_{\Gamma}]}\mathbb{E}_{L^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{g}}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(p)}\big[\tilde{\zeta}(L^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{g}})\big]

(66)

\displaystyle=\sup_{p\in[\ell_{\Gamma},u_{\Gamma}]}\left\{p\cdot\tilde{\zeta}(1)+(1-p)\cdot\tilde{\zeta}(-1)\right\}

(67)

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\tilde{\zeta}(-1)+\ell_{\Gamma}(\tilde{\zeta}(1)-\tilde{\zeta}(-1)),\;\tilde{\zeta}(-1)+u_{\Gamma}(\tilde{\zeta}(1)-\tilde{\zeta}(-1))\right\}.

(68)

Then, we define
\hat{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathsf{cc}}=\hat{\mathcal{S}}\cup\{\mathfrak{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}:\;\tilde{E}_{\mathfrak{g}}(h(L_{\mathfrak{g}},V_{\mathfrak{g}}))\leq 0\}
.

We note that evaluating
\tilde{E}_{\mathfrak{g}}(a)
has constant computational cost,
which is much more lightweight compared with the evaluation of
E_{\mathfrak{g}}(a)
,
whose cost is exponential in
|\mathfrak{g}|
. The trade-off is that the finer
conditioning reduces the remaining randomness and may lead to some
loss in statistical efficiency.
In the main paper, we report the results conditional on fixed
V_{\mathfrak{g}}
;
as discussed below, these two versions are comparable empirically.

D.2
Alternative splitting methods in P-screening

14
generalizes the p-value splitting method in
Section
3.5

to account for conservative (i.e., super-uniform) p-values.
Rather than estimating the number of null p-values in
[0,1/2)

by counting those in
[1/2,1]
, they propose
using p-values in an interval
[\lambda,\nu]\subseteq[0,1]
to infer those in
[0,\underline{\alpha})
,
where
\underline{\alpha}\leq\lambda
accounts for stretching factors.
Concretely, with
0<\underline{\alpha}\leq\lambda<\nu\leq 1
, their proposed splitting is

\displaystyle L_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\mathsf{pscr}}=\begin{cases}1,&p_{\mathfrak{g}}\in[0,\underline{\alpha})\\
-1,&p_{\mathfrak{g}}\in[\lambda,\nu],\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\quad V_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\mathsf{pscr}}=\begin{cases}(\nu-p_{\mathfrak{g}})/\zeta,&p_{\mathfrak{g}}\in[\lambda,\nu],\\
p_{\mathfrak{g}},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(69)

where
\zeta=(\nu-\lambda)/\underline{\alpha}
.
Note that, with
\underline{\alpha}=\lambda=0.5
and
\nu=1
, (
69
) covers
the splitting method in
Section
3.5

as a special example.
Adapting the result of
14
to our setting, we
obtain the following implication: if the subgroup-level p-value
p_{\mathfrak{g}}

has a density that is
non-decreasing
under the null, then—by their theory—the

L^{\mathsf{pscr}}_{\mathfrak{g}}
’s satisfy the bounded skewness condition with
\kappa=\zeta
.
However, with the presence of unmeasured confounding (
\Gamma>1
), the group-level
p-values are not guaranteed to have non-decreasing density, as illustrated by the following example.

Counterexample 2
.

Suppose
|\mathfrak{g}|=3
and
\Gamma=3
. Let
r_{i}=i
, for
i=1,2,3
, and define

\displaystyle S_{\mathfrak{g}}=\sum^{3}_{i=1}r_{i}\mathds{1}\{L_{i}=1\},

where
\mathds{1}\{L_{i}=1\}\overset{\text{ind}}{\sim}\text{Bern}(p_{i})
with
(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})=(\tfrac{3}{5},\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{3}{4})
.
Since
\tfrac{1}{1+\Gamma}\leq p_{i}\leq\frac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma}
, for
\forall i=1,2,3
, the example is compatible
with the sensitivity model. It can be verified that the density
f
of the
randomized p-values resulting from the Wilcoxon signed-rank test defined in (
36
)
satisfies the following:

\displaystyle f(40)=\frac{8}{5}>\frac{6}{5}=f(50).

The above counterexample demonstrates that the density of the signed-rank p-values
need not be non-decreasing under the null; in particular, the bounded skewness is not
guaranteed to hold. Similar to the case in Section
3.5
, P-screening
does not guarantee the FDR control unless the screening order is independent of

V^{\mathsf{pscr}}_{\mathfrak{g}}
.

That said, the overestimation issue can be mitigated by carefully choosing

\lambda
and
\nu
, so that the probability mass in
[0,\underline{\alpha})

is comparable to that in
[\lambda,\nu]
.

F.1
A formal introduction of simulation setup

We begin by describing the data-generating process used throughout this section.
Given the number of matched sets
I
and
n_{i}\geq 2
for
i=1,\dots,I
,
we generate for each matched set
i\in\{1,\dots,I\}

a
d
-dimensional covariate vector shared by all units in the set
from a multivariate Gaussian distribution:
X_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
.

To define the “important” sets, we assign each set
i\in[I]
an importance score

s_{i}=X_{i1}+0.5X_{i2}
.
We then let
t
be the
(1-p_{\mathrm{imp}})
-quantile
of
\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{I}
and define the group label
S_{i}\;=\;\mathbf{1}\{s_{i}\geq t\}\in\{0,1\}
.
Thus,
p_{\mathrm{imp}}
controls the proportion of “important” units, and we fix
p_{\mathrm{imp}}=0.3
throughout this section.
The potential outcomes are generated by
R_{ij}\;=\;\beta^{\top}X_{i}\;+\;\alpha_{u}\,u_{ij}\;+\;\tau(X_{i})\cdot S_{i}\cdot\mathds{1}\{Z_{ij}=1\}\;+\;\varepsilon_{ij}
,
where
\varepsilon_{ij}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,

\alpha_{u}=0.2
, and
u_{ij}\sim{\rm Unif}(0,1)
is the unit-level latent confounder.
The vector
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is sampled once with independent entries

\beta_{j}\sim{\rm Unif}(0,1)
, and is
held fixed across simulation runs.
The heterogeneous treatment effect
\tau(X_{i})
takes the form
\tau(X_{i})=\tau^{*}\cdot\Phi(s^{-1}_{Z}(Z_{i}-\bar{Z}))
, where
Z_{i}=X_{i}^{\top}\eta-1
.
Above,
\tau^{*}=3.0
for random subgroup partition and
\tau^{*}=4.0
for tree-based partition,
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
sampled once with independent entries
\eta_{j}\sim{\rm Unif}(0,1)
,
and
\bar{Z}
and
s_{Z}
are the sample mean and standard deviation of
\{Z_{i}\}_{i\in[I]}
.

To model treatment assignment under unmeasured confounding, we adopt Rosenbaum’s sensitivity model
(
52
)
. Specifically, we define

\pi_{ij}\;=\;\frac{w_{ij}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{i}}w_{i\ell}},\text{ where }w_{ij}\;=\;\Gamma^{\,u_{ij}}\;=\;\exp\bigl((\log\Gamma)\,u_{ij}\bigr),\forall j\in[n_{i}].

We then sample the index of the treated unit
T_{i}
from a multinomial distribution
with parameters
(\pi_{i1},\cdots,\pi_{in_{i}})
and let
Z_{iT_{i}}=1\text{ and }Z_{ij}=0
,
\forall j\neq T_{i}
.
By construction,

\pi_{ij}/\pi_{ik}=\Gamma^{\,u_{ij}-u_{ik}}\in[\Gamma^{-1},\,\Gamma]

for any
j,k
, i.e., treatment probabilities within a set are bounded by
\Gamma
.
In tree-based subgroup partitions, since the final FDR control is free of configurations of the tree, we set the nominal level to be
0.05
to produce a sufficiently large number of subgroups and will later vary the sample size restriction
minsplit
to control the size of subgroups.
