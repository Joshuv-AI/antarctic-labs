Nash Equilibria with Derangement Degree Probabilities
Edan Orzech 1 Martin Rinard 1,2 1 MIT CSAIL 2 National University of Singapore {iorzech,rinard}@csail.mit.edu

Abstract
We prove for every n ≥ 4 n\geq 4 the existence of an n n -player game in normal form with integer payoffs that has a unique Nash equilibrium, which is fully mixed. In the equilibrium, each probability weight is an algebraic number of degree ! n {\mathbin{!n}} (the derangement number), and its minimal polynomial has Galois group S ! n S_{\mathbin{!n}} and ! n + 1 {\mathbin{!n}}+1 nonzero coefficients.

Definitions

We refer to
[
12
,
13
]
for the full details regarding background on normal form games and NEs and
[
1
,
5
,
4
]
for a full algebra background. An
n
-player
2
-action game
G
is defined by
n
payoff functions,
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}:\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
which are extended to
[0,1]^{n}
as
u_{i}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim x}(u_{i}(a))
.
G
has
integer payoffs
if
u_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}
for every
i
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\left\{0,1\right\}
. A NE is defined in the usual way. Given
G
we have
n
multi-affine polynomials defined as
f_{i}(x_{-i})=u_{i}(x_{-i},x_{i}=1)-u_{i}(x_{-i},x_{i}=0)
.
f_{i}(x_{-i})
or in short
f_{i}(x)
is the payoff advantage the first action has over the second action for player
i
given that the other players play
x_{-i}
. We have the following useful lemma:

Lemma 3.1
.

If
x\in\NE(G)
then for every
i
,

\displaystyle f_{i}(x_{-i})>0

\displaystyle~\Rightarrow~x_{i}=1,

\displaystyle f_{i}(x_{-i})<0

\displaystyle~\Rightarrow~x_{i}=0,

(1)

\displaystyle 0<x_{i}<1

\displaystyle~\Rightarrow~f_{i}(x_{-i})=0.

Here, we are interested in fully mixed NEs, in which
x_{i}\in(0,1)
for all
i
. The set of fully mixed NEs of
G
is exactly
\left\{x\in(0,1)^{n}\mid\forall i\in[n]~~f_{i}(x)=0\right\}
.

Games as equation systems:

Define the generic system
\sys(c,x)
as follows: there is a coefficient vector
c=(c_{i,s})_{i\in[n],~s\subseteq[n]\setminus\left\{i\right\}}
which is either
generic
(a vector of symbols) or
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
where
N=n2^{n-1}
, a variable vector
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
that will take values in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}(c)[x]
of the form

\displaystyle f_{i}(c,x)=\sum_{s\subseteq[n]\setminus\left\{i\right\}}c_{i,s}\prod_{j\in s}x_{j}.

When
c
is generic, we may still set some
c_{i,s}
to
0
.
We write
f_{i}^{c}\coloneqq f_{i}(c,\cdot)
.
Given
c
, each polynomial
f_{i}^{c}
has monomial exponents with support
S_{i}=\left\{s\mid c_{i,s}\neq 0\right\}\subseteq\left\{0,1\right\}^{i-1}\times\left\{0\right\}\times\left\{0,1\right\}^{n-i}
. The monomial exponent supports
S_{i}
of the generic
\sys(c,x)
are
S_{i}=\left\{0,1\right\}^{i-1}\times\left\{0\right\}\times\left\{0,1\right\}^{n-i}
for all
i
.

Every
c
(either generic or real) defines a game
\game(c)
uniquely up to affine transformations, which do not affect the game’s best responses or NEs. In particular if
c\in\mathbb{Q}^{N}
then the payoffs in
\game(c)
can be scaled to be integers. Therefore we sometimes identify games as vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Furthermore, the set of fully mixed NEs of
G(c)
is
\left\{x\in(0,1)^{n}\mid\sys(c,x)=0\right\}
.

Proof of Proposition
1.1

The proof consists of
4
steps. In the first, we show that the generic
\sys
has
{\mathbin{!n}}
solutions in
x\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus\left\{0\right\})^{n}
, and the field extension of
\mathbb{Q}(c)
by all the coordinates of these
{\mathbin{!n}}
roots has Galois group

.

Proposition 3.2

(Solution count of
\sys
)
.

Suppose
c_{i,s}
are generic. The number of solutions in
x\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus\left\{0\right\})^{n}
to
\sys(c,x)=0
is
\leq{\mathbin{!n}}
, and
={\mathbin{!n}}
if
c_{i,s}\neq 0
for all
i,s
.

Proof.

Each
f_{i}
’s support of monomial exponents is
S_{i}\subseteq\left\{0,1\right\}^{i-1}\times\left\{0\right\}\times\left\{0,1\right\}^{n-i}
, and its associated Newton polytope is
\delta_{i}\subseteq\Delta_{i}\coloneqq[0,1]^{i-1}\times\left\{0\right\}\times[0,1]^{n-i}
.
Therefore by the BKK theorem
[
2
,
10
]
the number of solutions to
\sys
(in the generic case) is
n!\mv(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n})
where
\mv
is the mixed volume function. By monotonicity,
n!\mv(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n})\leq n!\mv(\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n})
.

To calculate the rhs we refer to the function

\displaystyle V(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\coloneqq\vol_{n}(\lambda_{1}\Delta_{1}+\ldots+\lambda_{n}\Delta_{n})=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}=1}^{n}\mv(\Delta_{i_{1}},\ldots,\Delta_{i_{n}})\prod_{l=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{l}}.

Let
\sum\lambda_{-i}=\sum_{j\neq i}\lambda_{j}
.

\displaystyle\vol_{n}(\lambda_{1}\Delta_{1}+\ldots+\lambda_{n}\Delta_{n})=\vol_{n}\left(\left[0,\sum\lambda_{-1}\right]\times\ldots\times\left[0,\sum\lambda_{-n}\right]\right)=\prod_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}\lambda_{j}.

The upper bound
n!\mv(\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n})
is the coefficient of
\prod_{i}\lambda_{i}
in the rhs which is
{\mathbin{!n}}
.

Equality is obtained if
\delta_{i}=\Delta_{i}
for all
i
, so
c_{i,s}\neq 0
for all
i,s
.
∎

Let
r=\left\{r^{1},\ldots,r^{\mathbin{!n}}\right\}
be the set of solutions to
\sys
where
r^{k}=(r^{k}_{1},\ldots,r^{k}_{n})
. We also denote them sometimes by
r(c)
.

Proposition 3.3

(Galois group of generic roots)
.

Suppose
c_{i,s}\neq 0
and generic for all
i,s
.

\displaystyle\gal(\mathbb{Q}(c)(r^{1}_{1},\ldots,r^{\mathbin{!n}}_{n})/\mathbb{Q}(c))\cong S_{\mathbin{!n}}.

Proof.

We use
[
7
, Theorem 1.5]
. First we need to prove that
S=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})
is reduced and irreducible. We view each
S_{i}
as a subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

S
is reduced if there are no
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that
S_{1}+b_{1},\ldots,S_{n}+b_{n}
are all contained in a proper sublattice of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Since
0,e_{j}\in S_{i}
for all
i\neq j
, every sublattice that contains
S_{1}+b_{1},\ldots,S_{n}+b_{n}
contains
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
(by taking differences). Therefore
S
is reduced.

S
is irreducible if there are no
m>0
and a subset
\left\{S_{i_{1}},\ldots,S_{i_{m}}\right\}\subseteq\left\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}\right\}
that can be shifted into a sublattice of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
of dimension
m
. In the first case,
m=1
. Every
S_{i}
contains
0
and
n-1
of the
n
standard basis elements of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, so the shifted
S_{i}
can only fit into a sublattice of dimension at least
n-1>1
. In the second case,
m>1
. The union of every pair
S_{i},S_{j}
(
i\neq j
) contains
0
and all of
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
. Therefore the shifted
S_{i_{1}},\ldots,S_{i_{m}}
can only fit into a sublattice of dimension
n
(by considering differences of elements in these tuples), namely
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Therefore
S
is irreducible.

Therefore, from
[
7
]
the monodromy group of
\left\{r^{k}\right\}_{k}
is
G\cong S_{n!\mv(\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n})}=S_{\mathbin{!n}}
(last equality by Proposition
3.2
).

Lastly we prove that
G\cong\gal(\mathbb{Q}(c)(r^{1}_{1},\ldots,r^{\mathbin{!n}}_{n})/\mathbb{Q}(c))
. Write in short
\mathbb{Q}(c,r)
and
\mathbb{C}(c,r)
. First observe that
\mathbb{Q}(c,r)/\mathbb{Q}(c)
is a Galois extension, because
r=\left\{r^{k}_{i}\right\}_{k,i}
contains all the conjugates of the
r^{k}_{i}
by definition (as the set/tuple of all solution coordinates to
\sys
). Now, using
[
3
]
,
G\cong\gal(\mathbb{C}(c,r)/\mathbb{C}(c))
.
Let
\sigma\in\gal(\mathbb{C}(c,r)/\mathbb{C}(c))
. Then
\sigma\restriction_{\mathbb{Q}(c)}=id_{\mathbb{Q}(c)}
,
\sigma(\mathbb{Q}(c,r))=\mathbb{Q}(c,r)
and
\sigma\restriction_{\mathbb{Q}(c,r)}\in\gal(\mathbb{Q}(c,r)/\mathbb{Q}(c))
because
\sigma
permutes the root tuples
r^{1},\ldots,r^{\mathbin{!n}}
. Also, if
\sigma,\tau\in\gal(\mathbb{C}(c,r)/\mathbb{C}(c))
and
\sigma\restriction_{\mathbb{Q}(c,r)}=\tau\restriction_{\mathbb{Q}(c,r)}
then
\sigma,\tau
agree on

. They both fix
\mathbb{C}(c)
so we get
\sigma=\tau
. Therefore
\gal(\mathbb{C}(c,r)/\mathbb{C}(c))\lesssim\gal(\mathbb{Q}(c,r)/\mathbb{Q}(c))
. Overall:

\displaystyle S_{\mathbin{!n}}\cong G\lesssim\gal(\mathbb{Q}(c,r)/\mathbb{Q}(c))\lesssim S_{\mathbin{!n}}.

So
\gal(\mathbb{Q}(c,r)/\mathbb{Q}(c))\cong S_{\mathbin{!n}}
.
∎

In the second step, we create a simple game
\game(c_{0})
with a unique NE
\left(\frac{1}{2},\ldots,\frac{1}{2}\right)

such that every

Proposition 3.4

(Simple anchor game with open neighborhood of unique fully mixed NE games)
.

There is an
n
-player 2-action game
g_{0}=\game(c_{0})
with a unique NE
\left(\frac{1}{2},\ldots,\frac{1}{2}\right)
and an open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
c_{0}\in U
and for every
c\in U
,
\game(c)
has a unique NE.

Proof.

Define the game through the polynomials
f_{i}(x)=2x_{i+1}-1
for
i<n
and
f_{n}(x)=1-2x_{1}
. Let
x\in\NE
. If
x_{i+1}=0
then
f_{i}=-1<0
so
x_{i}=0
so we get
x_{1}=\ldots=x_{i+1}=0
and then
f_{n}=1>0
so
x_{n}=1
so
f_{n-1}=1>0
so
x_{n-1}=1
. Therefore we also get
x_{i+1}=\ldots=x_{n}=1
– a contradiction. Similarly we get a contradiction if
x_{i+1}=1
or
x_{1}\in\left\{0,1\right\}
. Therefore all NEs are fully mixed and the unique solution to
f_{1}=\ldots=f_{n}=0
is
x^{0}_{1}=\ldots=x^{0}_{n}=\frac{1}{2}
.

Now we prove the existence of
U
.
Define
F:\mathbb{R}^{N+n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
F(c,x)=(f_{1}^{c}(x),\ldots,f_{n}^{c}(x))
. The matrix
\left[\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}(g_{0},x^{0})\right]_{i,j}
is invertible:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}(g_{0},x^{0})=\frac{\partial f^{c_{0}}_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\frac{1}{2},\ldots,\frac{1}{2}\right)=\begin{cases}2_{j=i+1}&i<n\\
-2_{j=1}&i=n\end{cases}

meaning the matrix is

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\bf 0&2I_{n-1}\\
\hline\cr-2&\bf 0\end{array}\right)

which is invertible. The implicit function theorem gives open neighborhoods
U\ni c_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
W\ni x^{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and a unique function
\varphi:U\to W
such that for all
c\in U
,
\varphi(c)
is the unique solution in

to

. This means that
\varphi(c)
is a fully mixed NE (in

\game(c)
. Wlog take

.

Now we show that wlog

contains only games with a unique and fully mixed NE. Suppose otherwise. Then there is

and

such that

, there is a subsequence

. Since

has a unique NE,
\tilde{x}=x^{0}
so for all large enough

is fully mixed, meaning

P_{i}(t)=\prod_{k=1}^{\mathbin{!n}}(t-r^{k}_{i})

such that
P_{i}=\sum_{k=0}^{\mathbin{!n}}b_{k,i}t^{k}
. In the third step we show that

{\mathbin{!n}}

with

for all

is the minimal polynomial of

Proposition 3.5
.

Suppose
c_{i,s}\neq 0
and generic for all
i,s
. Then
P_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}(c)[t]
.

Proof.

First observe that
b_{k,i}(r^{1}_{i},\ldots,r^{\mathbin{!n}}_{i})
is symmetric in
r^{1}_{i},\ldots,r^{\mathbin{!n}}_{i}
.
\mathbb{Q}(c)
is a perfect field so we have the Galois group
G=\gal(\overline{\mathbb{Q}(c)}/\mathbb{Q}(c))
where
\overline{\mathbb{Q}(c)}
is the algebraic closure of
\mathbb{Q}(c)
. By definition
\sigma(r^{k})=(\sigma(r^{k}_{1}),\ldots,\sigma(r^{k}_{n}))

\sigma(f_{i}^{c}(r^{k}))=f_{i}^{c}(\sigma(r^{k}))=0

\sigma

\left\{r^{1},\ldots,r^{\mathbin{!n}}\right\}
within itself (
\sigma^{-1}(0)=0

\sigma
also permutes each
\left\{r^{1}_{i},\ldots,r^{\mathbin{!n}}_{i}\right\}
within itself. Therefore
\sigma(b_{k,i})=b_{k,i}
. So

is a fixed point of all

, so

. Therefore

Proposition 3.6

(Dense generic
P_{i}
)
.

For every

, the polynomials
b_{k,i}(r)\in\mathbb{Q}(c)[r]

Proof.

Use the notation

and consider
\sys(c,x+\lambda)
. Then it also has
{\mathbin{!n}}
roots and these are
\left\{r^{k}-\lambda\right\}_{k}
. Since
\sys(c,x+\lambda)=\sys(\psi_{\lambda}(c),x)

P^{\psi_{\lambda}(c)}_{i}=\prod_{k}(t-(r^{k}_{i}-\lambda_{i}))=\sum_{k=0}^{\mathbin{!n}}b_{k,i}(r(\psi_{\lambda}(c)))t^{k}

P^{c}_{i}
is monic so
b_{{\mathbin{!n}},i}(r(c))=1

\displaystyle b_{k,i}(r(\psi_{\lambda}(c)))=\sum_{j=k}^{\mathbin{!n}}\binom{j}{k}\lambda_{i}^{j-k}b_{j,i}(r(c))=\binom{{\mathbin{!n}}}{k}\lambda_{i}^{{\mathbin{!n}}-k}+\sum_{j=k}^{{\mathbin{!n}}-1}\binom{j}{k}\lambda_{i}^{j-k}b_{j,i}(r(c)).

To see that

for some

. Its coefficients do not depend on
