Characteristics, treatment patterns, healthcare resource use, and costs among adult patients diagnosed with neurofibromatosis type 1 and plexiform neurofibromas in the United States
Authors: Xiaoqin Yang, Mavis Obeng-Kusi, Theresa Dettling, Ayo Adeyemi, Marianne Cunnington, Qing Liu, Dominique Lejeune, Grace Chen, Mei Sheng Duh, Justin T Jordan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is a progressive rare genetic disorder that frequently involves the development of plexiform neurofibromas (PN). Until recently, there was no approved pharmacological treatment for adults with NF1-PN, and the disease burden is not well understood. This real-world retrospective study aimed to describe treatment patterns, healthcare resource utilization (HRU), and costs among adults with NF1-PN in the US.MethodsData were obtained from the Merative™ MarketScan® Commercial and Medicare Databases (01/01/2016-12/31/2022). Adults diagnosed with NF1 and PN were matched to controls without NF1-PN on sociodemographic characteristics (1:5 ratio). Continuous enrollment for ≥ 12 months before and ≥ 3 months after the index diagnosis (defined as the latter of NF1 or PN diagnosis by ICD-10-CM codes) was required for all patients. Treatment patterns, all-cause, and NF1/PN-specific HRU and costs were characterized during the follow-up period. A generalized linear model with Poisson or Tweedie distribution was used to compare all-cause HRU and costs, respectively, between patients and controls.ResultsThis study included 944 patients with NF1-PN and 4,720 controls. The mean age was 39.6 (standard deviation: 15.6) years and 59.6% were female. Over a mean follow-up of 25.8 months, the most common treatment among patients with NF1-PN was prescription pain medication (69.5%), followed by debulking surgeries (22.9%), cytotoxic chemotherapy (7.0%), radiotherapy (4.8%), and targeted therapies (e.g., MEK inhibitors) (4.7%). All-cause HRU was significantly higher among patients than controls across all settings (1.6 vs. 0.27 inpatient days per patient per year [PPPY], 18.8 vs. 9.1 outpatient visits PPPY, 0.80 vs. 0.53 emergency department visits PPPY), with adjusted incidence rate ratios of 1.4 to 4.2 (all p < 0.001). Among patients with NF1-PN, 53% and 18% of all-cause inpatient days and outpatient visits, respectively, were attributable to an NF1 or PN diagnosis. The adjusted mean total healthcare costs were $23,516 PPPY higher among the NF1-PN ($34,398 PPPY) versus matched control ($6,149 PPPY) cohort, with a cost ratio of 4.3 (p < 0.001).ConclusionsThis real-world study identified a substantially higher HRU and economic burden among adult patients with versus without NF1-PN across all settings, highlighting the need for new treatments to manage NF1-PN among this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04868-8.Keywords: Neurofibromatosis type 1, Plexiform neurofibromas, Treatment patterns, Healthcare resource use, Healthcare costs, Retrospective matched cohort study

Background

Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is a rare, autosomal dominant disorder characterized by tumors of the central and peripheral nervous system, estimated to affect 1 in 3,200 people [1–3]. Patients with NF1 may experience many non-neoplastic consequences of the disease, including cognitive impairment, skeletal abnormalities or vascular disease, as well as manifold neoplastic consequences including cutaneous neurofibromas, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors, or plexiform neurofibromas (PNs), the latter of which are present in up to 50% of patients [1, 2, 4, 5]. PNs are complex nerve sheath tumors that may grow progressively throughout a patient’s lifetime and may be associated with pain, neurologic dysfunction, disfigurement, and decreased quality of life [6–8].

The standard of care for PN treatment among patients with NF1 was historically limited to surgical tumor debulking [9–11]. However, complete tumor resection is generally not feasible because of the large size of many PNs, and their adjacency to major nervous system or vascular structures [9, 10]. Surgery is typically postponed for as long as possible due to the involvement of multiple fascicles of nerves and tissues that increase the risk of neurological and functional damage [9, 10]. Instead, pharmaceuticals are quickly becoming a consideration for first-line treatment of NF1-PN [11, 12], including with inhibition of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MEK) 1/2 proteins of the mitogen-activated protein kinases/extracellular signal-regulated kinases (MAPK-ERK) signaling pathway, which becomes dysregulated upon mutation of the NF1 gene [13, 14]. The MAPK-ERK pathway is critical for control of cellular proliferation, and inhibition thereof results in proliferation arrest and apoptosis, reducing tumor growth [13, 14].

Selumetinib, an oral, highly selective, MEK inhibitor, was approved by the United States (US) Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in April 2020 and November 2025 for pediatric and adult patients with NF1, respectively, who have symptomatic and inoperable PN [15–17]. In the Phase 2 SPRINT trial, 68% of pediatric patients with NF1-PN treated with selumetinib experienced a confirmed partial response, defined as a target neurofibroma volume decrease from baseline of ≥ 20% on consecutive restaging examinations ≥ 3 months apart [18]. Subsequent independent central review confirmed 44% partial response rate at the time of FDA approval [19]. Likewise, in the international, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase 3 KOMET trial, a significantly greater proportion of adult patients with NF1-PN experienced a reduction in PN tumor volume with selumetinib versus placebo treatment, and the safety profile was similar to that of pediatric patients. [20, 21]. Selumetinib led to a rapid onset of response (median: 3.7 months), with an objective response rate (ORR) of 20% for patients randomized to selumetinib compared with 5% for those randomized to placebo [21]. Additionally, patients with a baseline chronic pain score of ≥ 3 on the 11-point Numerical Rating Scale had a clinically meaningful reduction in pain with selumetinib versus placebo treatment [21]. A higher ORR of 64% was observed in a Phase 2 trial of selumetinib among adult patients with a similar study design [22].

Mirdametinib, another MEK inhibitor, was approved by the FDA in February 2025 for adults and children aged ≥ 2 years with NF1 and associated PNs that are symptomatic and not amenable to complete resection. Based on the single-arm Phase 2 ReNeu study, 41% of adults and 52% of children treated with mirdametinib had a confirmed radiographic response by blinded independent central review, defined as ≥ 20% volumetric reduction in target PN on ≥ 2 consecutive scans within 2–6 months [23].

Moreover, both selumetinib and mirdametinib showed improvements in patient-reported outcomes, including pain intensity and pain-interference [22, 23]. Other targeted therapies are not currently FDA approved for the treatment of NF1-PN but have been, or are currently being, assessed for efficacy and safety in clinical trials among adult [24–26], pediatric [27, 28], or both [29–32] populations of patients with NF1-PN.

The disease burden associated with NF1-PN in adults is not well understood, and at the time this study was performed there were no FDA-approved treatments for NF1-PN in the adult patient population. To our knowledge, only one study has previously described healthcare resource utilization (HRU) and costs among adults with NF1-PN, evaluating a combined pediatric and adult NF1-PN population in Japan [33]. Thus, the objectives of this real-world study were to both characterize current treatment patterns and estimate all-cause and NF1/PN-specific HRU and costs among adult patients with NF1-PN in the US.

Methods

Data source

De-identified administrative claims data were obtained from the Merative™ MarketScan® Commercial and Medicare Databases from January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2022. The MarketScan® databases contain claims from various healthcare encounter sources, including inpatient (IP), outpatient (OP), pharmacy, and long-term care services, for approximately 250 million unique individual employees, their spouses, and dependents covered by employer-sponsored private health insurance. MarketScan is a registered trademark of Merative Corporation in the US, other countries, or both. Available data comprises enrollment eligibility and demographic characteristics, as well as medical, surgical, and prescription drug utilization, and expenditure information. This study was considered exempt research under 45 CFR § 46.104(d)(4) as it involved only the secondary use of data that were de-identified in compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), specifically, 45 CFR § 164.514. Additionally, it was conducted according to the standards of Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practice (GPP) and all applicable federal, state, and local laws, rules, and regulations.

Study design and patient population

In this retrospective cohort study, patients with NF1-PN (i.e., both a NF1 and a PN diagnosis) were matched to control patients without NF1-PN at a 1:5 ratio. For patients with NF1-PN, the index date was defined as the first instance of having medical claims for both NF1 and PN, with the latter claim for NF1 or PN determining the index date. Index dates for the matched control cohort were assigned based on the index date of the matched patient with NF1-PN. The baseline period comprised the 12 months prior to the index date, while the follow-up period spanned from after the index date to the earlier of the end of data availability or the end of patient enrollment, with a minimum required follow-up of 3 months to ensure adequate measurement of study outcomes. Patients were censored at the earliest of end of enrollment or the end of data availability (December 31, 2022).

Patients in the NF1-PN cohort were identified as having ≥ 1 medical claim with a diagnostic code for NF1 (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification [ICD-10-CM]: Q85.01) and ≥ 1 medical claim with a diagnostic code for PN (ICD-10-CM: D36.10). NF-1 PN patients were required to be aged ≥ 18 years at the index date and have ≥ 12 months of continuous eligibility prior to the index date and ≥ 3 months of continuous eligibility after the index date. Patients who participated in a clinical trial during the baseline or follow-up period (ICD-10-CM: Z00.6; Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System [HCPCS]: S9988, S9990, S9991, S9992, S9994, S9996) were excluded. Patients who were covered by a capitated health plan were excluded from the cost analyses (Fig. 1).

Patients without NF1-PN, defined as individuals with no recorded diagnosis of NF1 and no recorded diagnosis of PN at any time during the study period, were exact matched to patients with NF1-PN based on the following pre-defined sociodemographic characteristics: exact birth year, sex, region at index date, insurance type at index date, and COVID-19 period at index date (pre-pandemic: prior to March 15, 2020; COVID pre-vaccine: March 15, 2020 to March 8, 2021; COVID post-vaccine: March 9, 2021 or later). Controls were also required to have ≥ 12 months of continuous eligibility prior to the index date.

Study outcomes

Treatments were described for the NF1-PN cohort during the follow-up period and included debulking surgeries and procedures, prescription pain medications, targeted therapies [12, 34], cytotoxic chemotherapy, and radiotherapy (see Additional file 1 for list of codes).

All-cause, NF1/PN-specific (i.e., HRU related to either the NF1 or PN diagnosis), and PN-specific HRU were assessed during the follow-up period for all patients and, separately, for patients with ≥ 1 type of visit (IP admission, emergency department [ED] visit, OP visit). All-cause HRU was described among the NF1-PN and matched control cohorts, separately, while NF1/PN-specific and PN-specific HRU were described among the NF1-PN cohort only. NF1/PN-specific HRU comprised medical claims with NF1 or PN as the primary or secondary diagnosis, while PN-specific HRU comprised medical claims for a specific encounter with PN as the primary or secondary diagnosis. Reported outcomes included HRU associated with IP admissions (proportion of patients with IP admissions, number of IP admissions, total length of stay), ED visits (proportion of patients with ED visits, number of ED visits), and OP visits (proportion of patients with OP visits, number of OP visits [including physical therapy and occupational therapy (PT/OT) visits, imaging procedures, specialist visits [35]; see Additional file 1 for list of codes]) .

All-cause, NF1/PN-specific, and PN-specific healthcare costs were evaluated during the follow-up period, based on definitions equivalent to those applied to the HRU analysis. Reported outcomes included total healthcare costs, total medical costs (IP admission costs, ED costs, OP costs, other medical costs), and OP pharmacy costs.

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographic (age, sex, region, health plan type, COVID-19 period) and clinical (comorbidities, use of pain medications, HRU) characteristics were described among both the NF1-PN and matched control cohorts, separately, during the baseline period or on the index date. Descriptive statistics were reported as mean, standard deviation (SD), median, and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables, and frequency and proportion for categorical variables. Non-matching characteristics were compared between the NF1-PN and matched control cohorts using standardized mean differences (SMD), with an SMD > 10% considered an indicator of imbalance.

Each treatment assessed during the follow-up period was reported as either the number and proportion of patients who received the treatment of interest, or the number of treatments per year using mean, SD, median, and IQR. For radiotherapy, treatment sessions and office visits that occurred on the same day were counted as a single visit; the same rule was applied to the calculation of surgery rates.

All-cause HRU and costs were reported per patient per year (PPPY) for continuous variables, to account for varying lengths of follow-up, or described using frequency and proportions for categorical variables. PPPY estimates assume that observed HRU and costs during the available follow-up period are representative of the patient’s experience over time. To compare all-cause HRU between the NF1-PN and matched control cohorts, adjusted incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with their respective 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using a generalized linear model (GLM) with Poisson distribution. Matching variables, including birth year, sex, region, healthcare plan, and COVID-19 period at the index date, were included in the adjusted model for a doubly robust estimate. Additionally, the Quan-Charlson comorbidity index (Quan-CCI), which was unbalanced between the 2 cohorts and not considered an intermediate factor between NF1-PN and HRU, was adjusted in the regression model. To compare all-cause healthcare costs between the 2 cohorts, adjusted cost difference and cost ratios were estimated using GLM with Tweedie distribution using the same set of covariates as the HRU analysis. The corresponding 95% CIs and p-values were derived using bootstrapping and costs were adjusted for inflation using the Consumer Price Index (CPI) from the US Bureau of Labor Statistics to 2023 US dollars (USD).

Results

Baseline characteristics for patients with NF1-PN and matched controls

A total of 944 patients with NF1-PN were identified (Fig. 1) and matched to 4,720 control patients without NF1-PN. Among patients with NF1-PN and their matched controls, the mean (SD) age was 39.6 (15.6) years, 59.6% were female, and 41.7% of patients were from the South US census region (Table 1). More than half had a preferred provider organization (PPO) coverage (54.7%), and most were identified during the pre-COVID-19 pandemic period (64.1%). A total of 124 (13.1%) patients with NF1-PN and their matched controls were enrolled in health maintenance organization (HMO) or capitated or partially capitated point-of-service (POS) plans and were excluded from the cost analyses.

In the NF1-PN cohort, hypertension was the most frequent NF1-PN-related comorbidity (22.4%), followed by anxiety disorders (19.8%), cancers (17.4%; melanoma 5.2%, soft-tissue sarcoma 4.0%, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor [MPNST] 3.1%), abdominal pain (15.3%), and depression/bipolar disorder (14.5%). Differences in the prevalence of comorbidities between the NF1-PN and control cohorts are presented in Table 1. Comorbidities observed more often among the NF1-PN versus matched control cohort, with a SMD > 20%, included melanoma (5.2% vs. 0.0%), scoliosis (6.1% vs. 0.7%), soft-tissue sarcoma (4.0% vs. 0.0%), MPNST (3.1% vs. 0.0%), neuropathy/peripheral neuropathy (3.8% vs. 0.4%), constipation (6.8% vs. 2.1%), insomnia (8.5% vs. 3.5%), and epilepsy/seizures (3.8% vs. 0.7%). The mean Quan-CCI was significantly higher in the NF1-PN cohort compared to the matched control cohort (0.83 vs. 0.25, SMD: 50.2%).

In total, 11.1% of patients with NF1-PN had ≥ 1 IP admission during the baseline period, while 37.1% had ≥ 1 ED visit, and nearly all (98.3%) had ≥ 1 OP visit. Among the matched control cohort, 4.2% had ≥ 1 IP admission, 29.0% had ≥ 1 ED visit, and 86.5% of patients had ≥ 1 OP visit.

Treatment during the follow-up period

Over a mean (SD) follow-up of 25.8 (19.7) months, 69.5% of patients with NF1-PN received prescription pain medication (Table 2). Other treatments among patients with NF1-PN included debulking surgeries (22.9%), cytotoxic chemotherapy (7.0%), and radiotherapy (4.8%). Targeted therapies (e.g., MEK inhibitors) were less commonly used (4.7%) during this time period prior to MEK inhibitor approval in the US for adults with NF1-PN. Among the 44 patients who received targeted therapies, 31 received MEK inhibitors and 11 received tyrosine kinase inhibitors (see Additional file 1 for list of targeted therapies and associated codes). Among the 216 patients who underwent ≥ 1 debulking surgery, most received only a single procedure during the entire follow-up period; consequently, the mean (SD) annualized rate was 0.55 (0.66) procedures per year over an average follow-up period of more than 2 years. Among the 45 patients with ≥ 1 record of radiotherapy, patients underwent a mean (SD) of 5.7 (9.5) sessions per year. The specific diagnosis for which treatments were given, including chemotherapy and radiation, was also unknown.

All-cause HRU between patients with NF1-PN and matched controls

In total, 20.9% of patients with NF1-PN had ≥ 1 IP admission. Among those with ≥ 1 IP admission, patients experienced a mean (SD) of 2.0 (1.9) IP admissions over the follow-up period, corresponding to an annualized mean (SD) of 0.81 (1.03) IP admissions PPPY and 6.9 (14.7) IP days PPPY over an average follow-up period of more than 2 years (Table 3). After adjusting for potential confounders, including birth year, sex, region, health care plan, COVID-19 period, and Quan-CCI score, patients with NF1-PN had a 3.22 times higher rate of IP admissions PPPY compared to patients without NF1-PN (95% CI: 2.78–3.72, p < 0.001). After adjustment, the mean number of IP days PPPY was 4.19 times higher in the NF1-PN versus matched control cohort (95% CI: 3.97–4.43, p < 0.001).

Healthcare costs

During the follow-up period, the mean all-cause total healthcare costs were $34,398 PPPY in the NF1-PN cohort versus $6,149 PPPY in the matched control cohort, with an adjusted cost difference of $23,516 (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). The adjusted cost ratio indicated that the mean total healthcare costs PPPY were 4.28 times higher for patients with NF1-PN versus those without (95% CI: 3.51–5.24, p < 0.001). The mean total healthcare costs in the NF1-PN cohort were largely driven by OP costs ($13,519 PPPY), followed by IP admission costs ($10,808 PPPY) and pharmacy costs ($8,738 PPPY). Total healthcare costs across all settings were significantly higher in the NF1-PN cohort compared to the matched control cohort, with the largest adjusted differences PPPY observed for OP visits (cost difference: $9,802; cost ratio: 4.13, 95% CI: 3.53–4.86, p < 0.001), followed by IP admissions (cost difference: $7,247; cost ratio: 4.81, 95% CI: 3.28–8.03, p < 0.001) and pharmacy costs (cost difference: $6,797; cost ratio: 4.98, 95% CI: 3.14–6.86, p < 0.001).

Discussion

This large population-based study comprehensively characterized current treatment patterns and estimated both all-cause and NF1/PN-specific HRU and costs across all encounter settings among adult patients with NF1-PN in the US. The most frequently administered treatment among patients with NF1-PN was prescription pain medication (70%), followed by debulking surgeries (23%), cytotoxic chemotherapy (7%), radiotherapy (5%), and targeted therapies (e.g., MEK inhibitors) (5%), indicating substantial needs related to treatment of ongoing symptoms among this patient population. All-cause HRU was consistently and significantly higher among patients with NF1-PN versus those without across all encounter settings (1.6 vs. 0.27 IP days PPPY; 18.8 vs. 9.6 OP visits PPPY). After adjustment for matching variables and comorbidity burden, patients with NF1-PN experienced 1.4 to 4.2 times higher rates of HRU across settings (all p < 0.001). Further, adjusted healthcare costs were $23,516 higher among patients with NF1-PN versus those without ($34,398 vs. $6,149 PPPY), with a cost ratio of 4.3 (p < 0.001), highlighting the significant economic burden associated with NF1-PN.

Patients with NF1-PN often have extensive comorbidities. In the present study, conditions including scoliosis (6.1%), melanoma (5.2%), soft-tissue sarcoma (4.0%), epilepsy/seizures (3.8%), neuropathy/peripheral neuropathy (3.8%), and MPNST (3.1%) were observed among patients with NF1-PN while being rare among the matched control cohort (< 1.0%). These findings are consistent with the results of an earlier real-world study, where the most frequent comorbidities among a combined cohort of pediatric and adult patients with NF1-PN in Japan were epilepsy (8.7%), scoliosis (8.4%), and MPNST (3.8%) [33]. Soft-tissue sarcomas were also frequent, though this may reflect some non-specific coding practices with potential overlap between the sarcomas captured in the MPNST and other soft-tissue sarcoma categories.

The most common treatment among patients with NF1-PN in the current analysis was prescription pain medication, received by 69.5% of patients, highlighting the substantial burden of pain-related symptoms among this population. Previous real-world claims-based studies have also identified high rates of pain medication use among both pediatric and adult patients with NF1-PN, ranging from 44.2% to 58.5% [33, 36, 37] Cytotoxic chemotherapy was observed among 7.0% of patients. As cytotoxic chemotherapy is not an approved treatment for NF1-PN and there is little evidence supporting its efficacy among this population, its use is typically reserved for highly selected patients, such as those requiring treatment for comorbid cancer [2, 38] Thus, its use here likely reflects the increased cancer risk associated with NF1, though a limitation of our dataset is that the diagnosis for which a treatment is given is not specified. A similar caveat applies to the observed rate of radiotherapy (4.8%) as this is rarely used in the treatment of NF1-PN and likely reflects treatment of comorbid malignancies or particularly refractory PNs [11]. Further, the number of radiotherapy sessions reported corresponds to the number of billable events (i.e., claims) versus the clinical fractions or complete radiation courses administered. As no targeted therapies were approved for the adult NF1-PN population at the time of our study conduct, the low prevalence of targeted therapy use (4.7%) was not unexpected. The proportion of patients administered targeted therapies was slightly higher than that previously reported among a Medicaid- (1.6%) or commercially- (1.0%) insured pediatric NF1-PN population prior to selumetinib approval [36, 37] Notably, the introduction of MEK inhibitors, including selumetinib and mirdametinib, represents an important advancement in the treatment of NF1-PN and has the potential to substantially alter treatment patterns as well as HRU and costs. While the use of targeted therapies may be associated with higher pharmacy costs, reductions in other components of HRU, including surgical interventions or symptom-related care, are probable [34, 39]. Accordingly, further real-world research is warranted to evaluate the overall impact of MEK inhibitors on NF1-PN management, HRU, and costs.

This study is the first to quantify and compare the incremental healthcare and economic burden between adult patients with NF1-PN and matched controls in the US. Adjusted all-cause HRU rates were significantly higher among patients with NF1-PN versus those without across IP, ED, and OP settings, ranging from 1.36 to 4.19 fold adjusted differences in rates (all p < 0.001). Patients with NF1-PN had a significantly greater number of total IP days compared to patients without NF1-PN, and the proportion of patients with ≥ 1 IP admission was nearly 3 times higher among the NF1-PN versus the matched control cohort. Together, this may reflect the complexity of clinical management and severity of complications among the adult NF1-PN population. Although a very high proportion of patients both with and without NF1-PN had OP visits, approximately 2 times the proportion of patients among the NF1-PN cohort required ≥ 1 PT/OT, imaging, or specialist visit. Further, the adjusted rate of specialist visits was over 3 times higher among patients with NF1-PN versus those without, highlighting the specific needs of these patients. The absolute burden of HRU was similar to previous research among pediatric patients with NF1-PN in the US, with similar proportions of patients requiring IP visits (~ 13% versus 20.9%) and OP visits (~ 100% versus 99.9%) [36, 37]. The mean number of visits among pediatric patients with NF1-PN requiring healthcare services was also comparable to the adult NF1-PN population in the present study (IP visits: 1.2 to 1.4 PPPY versus 0.8 PPPY; ED visits: 1.3 to 1.6 PPPY versus 1.3 PPPY; OP visits: 17.3 to 18.2 PPPY versus 18.8 PPPY) [36, 37]. Only one other study has previously described HRU among adult patients with NF1-PN, evaluating a combined cohort of pediatric and adult patients in Japan [33]. Over a median follow-up of 33.6 months, 43.8% of patients with NF1-PN required ≥ 1 IP admission and nearly all (99.5%) required an OP visit. A total of 61.6% and 43.2% of patients with NF1-PN required magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography imaging services, respectively. Although HRU rates among Japanese patients without NF1-PN was not measured, the proportion of patients with NF1-PN who accessed healthcare services are in line with findings of the present study and support the substantial economic burden associated with NF1-PN in adult patients across encounter settings.

Among patients with NF1-PN, the proportion of HRU that was directly attributable to an NF1 or PN diagnosis or a PN diagnosis varied across settings. A descriptively higher proportion was attributable to the NF1 or PN diagnosis in the IP setting compared to the OP setting. The proportion of PT/OT visits attributable to an NF1 or PN diagnosis or a PN diagnosis was notably lower compared to imaging and specialist visits, potentially reflecting limited recognition of NF1-PN in driving the need for PT/OT care. It is also possible that the lower proportion of OP versus IP visits attributable to an NF1 and/or PN diagnosis could be due to differences in coding practice to capture the underlying reasons for medical visits in the OP setting.

Consistent with the HRU analyses, all-cause healthcare costs were significantly higher among patients with NF1-PN versus those without across all settings. The mean total all-cause healthcare costs observed among adults with NF1-PN ($34,398 PPPY) were comparable to those previously identified among pediatric patients with NF1-PN covered by commercial insurance in the US ($38,292 PPPY) [36] and descriptively higher than for pediatric patients with NF1-PN insured with Medicaid ($17,275 PPPY) [37]. Taken together, the findings of the present study highlight the economic burden among adults with NF1-PN in the US and reflect the continued unmet medical need and the lack of approved treatments among this population.

This study was subject to several limitations. As with all studies of large claims databases, this analysis was vulnerable to administrative coding inaccuracies, including miscoding and use of rule-out diagnoses, leading to potential misclassification of exposure groups and NF1-PN related outcomes. The study population may be skewed toward patients with more severe NF1-PN, as those with milder disease may not have had relevant ICD-10-CM codes documented in their claims. Treatments were identified based on pharmacy and procedure data, without knowing the specific diagnostic reasoning. Because claims databases only record diagnostic and procedure codes for reimbursement purposes, HRU and cost estimates may be either overestimated or underestimated due to coding variability and billing practices [40]. However, there is no reason to consider that this would have differentially impacted the NF1-PN and matched control cohorts. Clinical measures, such as the number, size, and operability of PNs, as well as the indication for treatment, were not available, limiting the interpretation of disease burden and whether this differentially affects certain patient subgroups. As this was a non-randomized observational study, it is possible that unmeasured confounding was not fully addressed despite matching between NF1-PN and control cohorts. In addition, although patients were matched on geographic region at index date, regional variation in treatment patterns and healthcare costs were not examined. Differential access to specialized NF1 care centers across regions may therefore have contributed to unmeasured heterogeneity in HRU and costs. The study period also spanned the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have influenced healthcare delivery, HRU, and costs over time. While matching on COVID-19 period was used to mitigate potential confounding, year-to-year variations in treatment patterns and expenditures were not separately evaluated. In addition, PPPY estimates assume that observed HRU during the available follow-up period is representative of the patient’s experience over time, though it is possible clinical trajectories may change. A fixed 12-month follow-up duration was not imposed for either cohort, to minimize selection bias related to differential enrollment or attrition patterns. Similarly, a minimum follow-up duration was not imposed for patients without NF1-PN; however, the observed follow-up duration was similar between the NF1-PN and control cohorts. Finally, the generalizability of results is constrained, as findings from this commercially insured population may not be fully applicable to the broader US population of patients with NF1-PN without insurance coverage. However, given the paucity of previous burden of disease data on adults with NF1-PN this study still represents a larger, more diverse population than previously studied.

Conclusions

In this real-world study, HRU and healthcare costs were consistently higher among adults with NF1-PN compared to those without, evidenced across all encounter settings. With no specific approved pharmacological treatments during the study period, these findings reinforce both the substantial economic burden associated with NF1-PN and the need for new treatments and interventions to effectively manage NF1-PN in the adult population.
