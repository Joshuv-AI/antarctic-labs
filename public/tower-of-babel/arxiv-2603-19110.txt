Post-Quantum Cryptography from Quantum Stabilizer Decoding
Jonathan Z. Lu, Alexander Poremba, Yihui Quek, Akshar Ramkumar

Abstract
Post-quantum cryptography currently rests on a small number of hardness assumptions, posing significant risks should any one of them be compromised. This vulnerability motivates the search for new and cryptographically versatile assumptions that make a convincing case for quantum hardness. In this work, we argue that decoding random quantum stabilizer codes—a quantum analog of the well-studied 𝖫𝖯𝖭 \mathsf{LPN} problem—is an excellent candidate. This task occupies a unique middle ground: it is inherently native to quantum computation, yet admits an equivalent formulation with purely classical input and output, as recently shown by Khesin et al. (STOC ’26). We prove that the average-case hardness of quantum stabilizer decoding implies the core primitives of classical Cryptomania , including public-key encryption ( PKE ) and oblivious transfer ( OT ), as well as one-way functions. Our constructions are moreover practical: our PKE scheme achieves essentially the same efficiency as state-of-the-art 𝖫𝖯𝖭 \mathsf{LPN} -based PKE , and our OT is round-optimal. We also provide substantial evidence that stabilizer decoding does not reduce to 𝖫𝖯𝖭 \mathsf{LPN} , suggesting that the former problem constitutes a genuinely new post-quantum assumption. Our primary technical contributions are twofold. First, we give a reduction from random quantum stabilizer decoding to an average-case problem closely resembling 𝖫𝖯𝖭 \mathsf{LPN} , but which is equipped with additional symplectic algebraic structure. While this structure is essential to the quantum nature of the problem, it raises significant barriers to cryptographic security reductions. Second, we develop a new suit of scrambling techniques for such structured linear spaces, and use them to produce rigorous security proofs for all of our constructions.

1.1
Our approach

In this work, we propose an affirmative answer to the aforementioned question by exploring the cryptographic potential of a natural quantum computational assumption: the hardness of decoding
random
quantum stabilizer codes.

Quantum stabilizer codes are among the most central objects in all of quantum information science.
Not only do they form the backbone of all of quantum error correction and quantum fault-tolerance

,

,

]

[

From a complexity-theoretic perspective, stabilizer decoding is a natural quantum analog of classical decoding problems, such as the

[

[

for linear codes. In the

-complete
[
11

This disparity is due to the quantum-mechanical structure of the problem; indeed, the general quantum decoding problem is much more subtle as the input comes in the form of a
quantum
state—it consists of a noisy quantum codeword—and the task is to recover the encoded logical information.

At first glance, therefore, it may seem counterintuitive to base classical cryptography on a quantum decoding problem.
After all, stabilizer decoding appears to involve inherently quantum objects—quantum codewords and quantum noise.
However, recent work
[
65
]
has revealed a surprising equivalence: average-case quantum stabilizer decoding is equivalent (under polynomial-time
quantum
reductions) to a purely
classical
average-case problem, i.e. one that involves only classical inputs and classical outputs, yet retains the essential quantum-mechanical structure and difficulty of the original problem.

This equivalence suddenly opens the door to a tantalizing possibility:
Can we base post-quantum cryptography on the hardness of decoding quantum codes?

Cryptomania Meets Quantum Error Correction.

Driven by this possibility, we show that the

hardness of quantum stabilizer decoding indeed suffices to construct core primitives of

Importantly, our constructions are

Learning Stabilizers with Noise.

The starting point of this work is a recent

The

In subsequent work, Khesin, Lu, Poremba, Ramkumar and Vaikuntanathan

Here we make the case that

First,

Unlike leading post-quantum assumptions, such as

Basing cryptography off of

\bullet

If

\bullet

If
\mathsf{LSN}
is broken, this would represent a major breakthrough in our current understanding of quantum stabilizer codes, with profound downstream implications for many areas of quantum information science.

\bullet

If
\mathsf{LSN}
turns out to be equivalent to
\mathsf{LPN}
, this would reveal deep and unexpected connections between classical and quantum error correction.

Our results

We now briefly summarize our main contributions.
At a high level, we show that the average-case hardness of decoding random quantum stabilizer codes—captured by (the state variant of)
\mathsf{LSN}
—is sufficient to instantiate all of the core primitives of classical

[
58
]
.

Below, we use

to denote the security parameter and block length of the stabilizer code, and we let

Public-key encryption.

In

5
, we construct a

scheme from the hardness of
\mathsf{LSN}
in the low-noise regime with

.
The scheme matches the parameter regime and efficiency of state-of-the-art Alekhnovich-style

-based encryption
[

,
31
]
, with
O(n^{2})
encryption time and

Theorem (informal).

Assuming (the state variant of)
\mathsf{LSN}
is hard in the low-noise regime, there exists an efficient post-quantum

scheme.

Our
\mathsf{PKE}
scheme is formally described in
Section

5
, and we prove its security in

5.4
. We note that our security reduction is highly non-trivial and requires many new technical insights into

and its related variants; these form

Oblivious transfer.

In
Section

6
, we strengthen our scheme to obtain a strongly uniform public-key encryption scheme, which once again rests on the low-noise regime with
p=O(1/\sqrt{n})
.
Using known black-box transformations, this implies round-optimal malicious-secure oblivious transfer:

Theorem (informal).

Assuming (the state variant of)
\mathsf{LSN}
is hard in the low-noise regime, there exists an efficient post-quantum

Since oblivious transfer implies general secure multi-party computation, this establishes that
\mathsf{LSN}

One-way functions.

In

B

This gives symmetric cryptography directly from the hardest possible parameter setting of the problem.

Assuming (the state variant of)

Importantly, this variant of

Taken together, these results show that the hardness of decoding random quantum stabilizer codes suffices to realize symmetric encryption, public-key encryption, and round-optimal malicious-secure multi-party computation. Our constructions match the efficiency of the best known

1.3
Related work

The hardness of decoding random classical linear codes and the closely related
\mathsf{LPN}
(Learning Parity with Noise) problem has been studied extensively in coding theory and cryptography; see e.g.
[
6
,
47
,
62
,
42
,
8
,
4
,
81
,
56
,
73
,
99
,
15
]
.
A common formulation of
\mathsf{LPN}
presents the adversary with a sequence of
n
noisy linear samples
\{(\mathbf{a}_{i},\;\langle\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{x}\rangle+e_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
(where
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
), and hence a significant body of work has been focused on the fine-grained regimes where the number of samples
n
(equivalently, the block length of the code) is significantly larger than the message length.
From a coding-theoretic perspective, this corresponds to decoding a random linear code with vanishing rate
R=k/n
.
For example, BKW-style techniques yield
2^{O(k/\log k)}
-time algorithms when
n
is superpolynomial in
k

[
16
]
, and refinements give
2^{O(k/\log\log k)}
-time algorithms when
n
is slightly superlinear in
k

[
73
]
.
Despite decades of work, however, no subexponential-time algorithm is known in the constant-rate regime
n=\Theta(k)
, which is the regime underlying many important
\mathsf{LPN}
-based cryptographic constructions. In the constant rate regime, the best known algorithms for
\mathsf{LPN}
tend to involve variants of
information set decoding
(ISD) attacks
[
84
,
94
]
which run in exponential time
[
24
]
.

The study of quantum stabilizer decoding has historically followed a different direction.
Most prior work has focused on
worst-case
formulations of the problem, often in the form of maximum-likelihood decoding, and established classical hardness results and complexity-theoretic barriers for these variants
[
57
,
61
,
70
]
.
Only recently the attention shifted to
average-case
formulations that are more suitable for cryptographic applications.
In particular, Poremba, Quek, and Shor introduced the
Learning Stabilizers with Noise
(
\mathsf{LSN}
) problem and provided an initial analysis of its algorithmic and complexity-theoretic properties
[
82
]
. They also constructed a quantum bit commitment scheme from the hardness of the
\mathsf{LSN}
problem.
Subsequent work of Khesin, Lu, Poremba,
Ramkumar and Vaikuntanathan further clarified the landscape by relating average-case stabilizer decoding to classical noisy linear problems like
\mathsf{LPN}
in many parameter regimes
[
65
]
.
This work shows that the problem admits a purely classical description while retaining the symplectic algebraic structure intrinsic to stabilizer codes, thereby providing initial evidence that decoding random stabilizer codes may serve as a meaningful cryptographic hardness assumption.

Outside of quantum error correction, there have been a number of recent attempts at building
quantum
rather than
classical
cryptography from other natural average-case hard computational tasks
[
18
,
67
,
41
,
53
,
54
,
27
,
78
]
. However, unlike in our work, these constructions are inherently quantum (typically requiring quantum communication between multiple quantum parties) and do not give rise to classical cryptographic primitives as in our work.

2
Technical overview

The three primitives on which this paper focuses are one-way functions (
OWF
), public-key encryption (
PKE
), and oblivious transfer (
OT
).
All have been constructed—with a high degree of practical efficiency—from the
\mathsf{LPN}
assumption
[
4
,
31
,
35
,
32
,
43
]
.

Our main goal is to construct each of these primitives in such a way that

(a)

breaking each primitive is at least as hard as decoding a random quantum stabilizer code, and

(b)

each primitive’s efficiency is as good as the state-of-the-art corresponding
\mathsf{LPN}
-based construction, up to small constant factors.

This overview sketches the key technical ingredients for our constructions.
Our general recipe proceeds as follows.
First, we reduce the task of decoding a random quantum stabilizer code to the task of decoding a random
classical
linear code drawn from an ensemble of codes satisfying a certain algebraic structure. This problem is known as
symplectic

\mathsf{LPN}
(
\mathsf{sympLPN}
), and was introduced recently as a technical tool to reduce
\mathsf{LPN}
to average-case quantum stabilizer decoding in the high-noise regime
[
65
]
.
Because
\mathsf{sympLPN}
and
\mathsf{LPN}
appear similar conceptually, one could hope that
\mathsf{LPN}
-based cryptographic constructions would be readily amenable to adaptation into a
\mathsf{sympLPN}
-based protocol, thereby achieving constructions secured by the hardness of quantum error correction.
In reality, this intuition only holds reasonably well for
OWFs
, wherein inverting the
OWF
is essentially always equivalent to solving the underlying computational problem anyway.
Asymmetric cryptographic protocols based on
\mathsf{LPN}
, on the other hand, rely critically on the fact that the encoding matrix of the
\mathsf{LPN}
instance features uniformly random, independent entries as part of the security proofs, and thus break down when this assumption is relaxed.

This breakdown poses a serious technical barrier when proving the security of
\mathsf{sympLPN}
-based schemes.

The second component of our recipe involves overcoming these barriers by introducing an entirely new suite of techniques for scrambling and unscrambling information in linear subspaces, with the algebraic conditions specified by
\mathsf{sympLPN}
.
This approach enables us to rigorously prove the security of our constructions under the hardness of
\mathsf{stateLSN}
, at the cost of significantly increasing the technicality of the proofs relative to those of
\mathsf{LPN}
-based schemes.

2.1
Reduction to an
\mathsf{LPN}
-like Classical Problem

Informally,
\mathsf{stateLSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
is the task of decoding a random quantum stabilizer code with
k
logical qubits,
n
physical qubits, and noise rate
p\in(0,1)
.
Specifically, we draw a Haar-random (i.e. uniformly random) logical state
\ket{\psi}
as well as a random
n
-qubit Clifford operator
\mathbf{C}
.
We also draw a random error operator
\mathbf{E}
from the
depolarizing
distribution
\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n}
—the natural quantum analog of Bernoulli error (i.e. binary symmetric channel) in classical error correction.

The task of
\mathsf{stateLSN}
is to approximately recover the logical state
\ket{\psi}
, given a classical description of the code
\mathbf{C}
and the noisy code state
\mathbf{E}\mathbf{C}(\ket{0^{n-k}}\otimes\ket{\psi})
.
Being a problem with manifestly quantum inputs and outputs,
\mathsf{stateLSN}
as stated cannot be used directly for classical cryptography.
However, if we can produce a
quantum reduction
from
\mathsf{stateLSN}
to a problem with manifestly classical inputs and outputs, then we can at once use this classical problem directly to construct cryptography and be sure that such protocols are secured directly by the hardness of
\mathsf{stateLSN}
.
As a first step, consider a variant of
\mathsf{stateLSN}
wherein the logical state
\ket{\psi}
is a uniformly random
bitstring

\ket{\mathbf{x}}
for
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
, rather than a Haar-random state; thus the output of
\mathsf{LSN}
is classical though the input remains quantum.
This variant is known as
\mathsf{LSN}

[
82
]
, and when
k=O(\log n)
there is a known quantum reduction from
\mathsf{stateLSN}
to
\mathsf{LSN}

[
65
]
.
Importantly, while the hardness of
\mathsf{LPN}
is characterized by the logical dimension
k
—there is a brute-force guess-and-check
\poly(2^{k},n)
-time algorithm to solve it, the hardness of
\mathsf{LSN}
does not appear to depend on
k
. At first glance, this may seem counterintuitive. The reason is that, unlike for
\mathsf{LPN}
, there is simply no efficient method of verifying proposed
\mathsf{LSN}
solutions.
Indeed, for most regimes of
p
,
\mathsf{stateLSN}
is known to be
\mathsf{LPN}
-hard
even for a single logical qubit

[
65
]
.
Hence, the restriction to
k=O(\log n)
does not necessarily come at the cost of hardness.

Moreover,
\mathsf{LSN}
is equivalent (via efficient quantum reductions) to a completely classical problem, which we henceforth also refer to as
\mathsf{LSN}
.
Defining this classical problem requires the notion of symplectic subspaces, which are only well-defined for even-dimensional spaces.
Given bitstrings
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}),(\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
, the
symplectic inner product
is given by

\displaystyle(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\odot(\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime}):=\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}^{\prime}+\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{b}\pmod{2}.

(1)

Then, the classical equivalent of
\mathsf{LSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
is the task of recovering a bitstring
\mathbf{y}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
, given
([\mathbf{A}\,|\,\mathbf{B}],\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{By}+\mathbf{e})
, where
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n},\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times n},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times k},\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
;
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
are random subject to having symplectically orthogonal columns and being jointly full rank, while
\mathbf{e}
is drawn from a symplectic representation of the depolarizing distribution
\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n}
.
Concretely, each pair
(e_{j},e_{n+j})
is i.i.d., being
(0,0)
with probability
1-p
and
(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)
each with probability
p/3
.

While completely classical,
\mathsf{LSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
still differs structurally from
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
in that only a small part of the effective logical state
[\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathbf{y}]
needs to be recovered.
1
1

1

This is due to a uniquely quantum phenomenon called
stabilizer degeneracy

[
60
,
49
]
; roughly speaking, two distinct errors can have the same effect on a codeword.

In
\mathsf{LPN}
, the entire logical state must be recovered.

Surprisingly, however, we are able to reduce
\mathsf{LSN}
to a much more
\mathsf{LPN}
-like problem, known as
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
.
This is the same task as
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(k,2n,p\right)
, except the encoding matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times k}
is uniformly random
subject to
having symplectically orthogonal columns, and the error is drawn from the depolarizing distribution’s symplectic representation.
Thus,
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
and
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(k,2n,p\right)
describe essentially the same objects, but draw them from different distributions.
Note that formally, the
\mathsf{sympLPN}
and
\mathsf{LPN}
variants we consider are the
decision
variants, wherein one is given
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{z})
and must decide if
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e}
(“
structured
”) or
\mathbf{z}
is uniformly random (“
unstructured
”).
The
decision
variant of
\mathsf{LPN}
is the standard cryptographic variant, and is essentially equivalent to the
search
variant
[
64
]
(up to small factors).
Importantly, we only consider the
search
variant of
\mathsf{LSN}
, as their
search
and
decision
variants appear inequivalent in general
[
65
]
and the
search
variant is natural for quantum information processing.

Search
and
decision
variants of
\mathsf{sympLPN}
are not known to be equivalent.

To achieve the first component in our recipe, we show in
Theorem

4.1
that, perhaps surprisingly, there is a simple classical reduction from
\mathsf{LSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
to
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n,p\right)
for any
k=O(\log n)
.
Intuitively,
\mathsf{sympLPN}
appears to be “embedded” in
\mathsf{LSN}
as the
\mathbf{A}
-part of the
\mathsf{LSN}
matrix:
\mathsf{sympLPN}
is of the form
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e})
while
\mathsf{LSN}
takes the form
([\mathbf{A}\,|\,\mathbf{B}],\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{By}+\mathbf{e})
.
Our reduction relies on the simple observation that the noisy codeword part of
\mathsf{LSN}
is
identical
to that of
\mathsf{sympLPN}
when
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{0}
.
If that is the case, then dropping the
\mathbf{B}
part of the
\mathsf{LSN}
matrix yields
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e})
, precisely the form of Decision
\mathsf{sympLPN}
with a
structured
input.
If
\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{0}
, then again dropping the
\mathbf{B}
part of the
\mathsf{LSN}
matrix yields
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{By}+\mathbf{e})
.
In fact, we show that
\mathbf{By}
is marginally negligibly close (in total variation distance) to a uniformly random vector, and thus this instance is negligibly close to an
unstructured

\mathsf{sympLPN}
task.
Therefore, a
\mathsf{sympLPN}
solver can decide whether or not
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{0}
in the
\mathsf{LSN}
problem.
If
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{0}
we are done; if
\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{0}
, we guess a random
\hat{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}\setminus\set{\mathbf{0}}
.
The
\mathsf{sympLPN}
solver hence gives us a minuscule advantage in solving
\mathsf{LSN}
by providing a larger signal only when
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{0}
.
However, since
k=O(\log n)
, even such a small signal gives a solver with a non-negligible overall advantage over completely random guessing for
\mathsf{LSN}
, which turns out to be sufficient to complete the reduction.
Overall, our chain of reductions proceeds as, for
k=O(\log n)
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{stateLSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)\leq_{Q}\mathsf{LSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)\leq_{C}\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n,p\right),

(2)

where
\leq_{Q},\leq_{C}
respectively denote quantum and classical reductions.
Crucially, this reduction works
only
for
\mathsf{sympLPN}
with exactly
n
logical bits (i.e. rate
1/2
).
With even
one
less logical bit, it is no longer clear as to how much easier the
\mathsf{sympLPN}
instance becomes.

2.2
Cryptographic Constructions with
\mathsf{sympLPN}

Having established that (
decision
)
\mathsf{sympLPN}
is at least as hard as
\mathsf{stateLSN}
, we next outline our cryptographic constructions and the technical barriers to a security proof relative to their
\mathsf{LPN}
-based counterparts.
Our
OWF
construction is the most straightforward, and unlike the public-key protocols is based directly on
\mathsf{LSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
, and in the hardest regime wherein
p=\Omega(1)
and
k=\Omega(n)
.
The function is indexed by
[\mathbf{A}\,|\,\mathbf{B}]
, and maps on input
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{e})
to
\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{By}+\mathbf{e}
.
Inverting this function is equivalent to finding
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{e}
from
([\mathbf{A}\,|\,\mathbf{B}],\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{By}+\mathbf{e})
.
This is at least as hard as solving
\mathsf{LSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
, which only requires finding
\mathbf{y}
.
However, even if we had a
\mathsf{LSN}
solver which could find
\mathbf{y}
, it is not clear that we could then also recover
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{e}
.
In fact, if we subtract
\mathbf{By}
out from
\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{By}+\mathbf{e}
, the remaining piece
\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e}
is precisely a
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n,p\right)
task.
We prove, in fact, that breaking this
OWF
is equivalent to solving
both

\mathsf{LSN}
and
search

\mathsf{sympLPN}
.
The
search
variant of
\mathsf{sympLPN}
has no clear relation to
\mathsf{LSN}
, and thus our
OWF
may be more secure than
\mathsf{LSN}
alone.
Moreover, in the regime of
p=\Omega(1)
,
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(pn,2n,p\right)
is known to reduce to
\mathsf{stateLSN}\!\left(k=O(\log n),n,\Theta(p)\right)

[
65
]
, and thus our
OWF
is provably at least as secure as a conventional
\mathsf{LPN}
-based
OWF
.
We defer the full construction and security proof to
Appendix

B
.

On the other hand, the
PKE
and
OT
constructions in this work are built upon
\mathsf{sympLPN}
.
Our starting point is the
\mathsf{LPN}
-based
PKE
scheme in
[
31
]
which is rooted in the seminal scheme of Alekhnovich
[
4
]
, and which remains the state-of-the-art
\mathsf{LPN}
-based construction in terms of security and efficiency.
This particular scheme requires
p=\Theta(1/\sqrt{n})
, because the correctness of the scheme relies heavily on the inner product of two independently sampled noise vectors being
0
with high probability; this occurs precisely when
p=O(1/\sqrt{n})
.
If in the
\mathsf{LPN}
scheme we set
k=\Omega(n)
, then brute-force algorithms require time
2^{O(pn)}
by enumerating all possible errors of about the right weight.
Thus, the security of the scheme scales as
2^{O(\sqrt{n})}
.
2
2

2

While schemes have since been proposed which use variants of high-noise
\mathsf{LPN}

[
100
]
, their security scales quasipolynomially in
n
and are therefore in practice orders of magnitude less secure than Alekhnovich-type schemes.

Much of this construction is readily adaptable to
\mathsf{sympLPN}
in place of
\mathsf{LPN}
, with one very significant barrier.
More precisely, in the process of proving the security of our
\mathsf{sympLPN}
-based scheme (formally described in
Section

5
), we find in
Section

5
that the protocol’s security reduces to the hardness of both
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n,p\right)

and

\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n-1,n,p\right)
.
If this were
\mathsf{LPN}
, this subtlety would be essentially irrelevant—the hardness of
\mathsf{LPN}
provably does not depend on small changes in
k
.
In particular, there is a simple self-reduction from
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
to
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(k-1,n,p\right)
.
For
\mathsf{sympLPN}
, however, this subtlety becomes a significant technical barrier.
In our case, the security of our adapted
PKE
scheme would rely on both
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n,p\right)
and
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n-1,n,p\right)
.
But, as discussed above, a reduction from
\mathsf{stateLSN}
is only known for
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
with
k=n
logical bits, exactly half the number of physical bits
2n
.
In addition, the naïve self-reduction in which we drop one logical bit, which succeeds on
\mathsf{LPN}
, fails completely on
\mathsf{sympLPN}
because the entries are jointly nearly maximally far from uniformly random.
Despite this failure, we find an alternative and substantially more involved approach which successfully reduces
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n,p\right)
to
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n-1,n,p\right)
by introducing new techniques for scrambling symplectic subspaces in a carefully controlled manner.
We outline the key idea for these techniques below, but with this reduction complete we prove the (
IND-CPA
) security of our
PKE
.
Notably, our scheme has the same runtime up to small constant factors as state-of-the-art Alekhnovich-type
\mathsf{LPN}
-based
PKE
, which are quite efficient with
O(n^{2})
time encryption,
O(n)
time decryption.

Finally, we construct malicious-secure (i.e. even if one party deviates arbitrarily from protocol, the other party’s security is still guaranteed)
OT
from the hardness of
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,p\right)
.
To show the
existence
of polynomial-time malicious-secure
OT
from
\mathsf{sympLPN}
is straightforward using our
PKE
construction.
This is because we can readily use our
PKE
scheme to directly obtain two-round
OT
secure against
semi-honest
parties (who do not deviate from protocol but otherwise may try to break security by analyzing the interaction transcript).
There are many works, e.g.
[
48
]
, which transform a semi-honest
OT
scheme and a
OWF
to a malicious-secure
OT
scheme
at the cost
of a large blowup in the round complexity.
Thus, while this simple procedure establishes the existence of malicious-secure
OT
based on
\mathsf{stateLSN}
, it is of little practical relevance.

It is known, however, that the minimum round complexity of malicious-secure
OT
is four.
Furthermore, there is a generic procedure to achieve round-optimal
OT
by way of a particularly structured
PKE
scheme known as a
strongly uniform

PKE
scheme (
SU-PKE
)
[
43
]
.
Informally,
SU-PKE
is the same as
PKE
with the added property that the public key in the scheme is computationally indistinguishable from a uniformly random bitstring.
Since Alekhnovich-type
PKE
based on
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
has public key
(\mathbf{A}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n\times k},\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e})
which is by assumption computationally indistinguishable from the uniformly random bitstring
(\mathbf{A}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n\times k},\mathbf{u}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n})
, they immediately imply round-optimal
OT
.
However, our
\mathsf{sympLPN}
-based
PKE
scheme has a public key which is easily distinguishable from uniformly random because the key takes the form
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e})
, where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times k}
is a uniformly random full-rank matrix subject to having symplectically orthogonal columns.
As a result, the key technical barrier to the construction of round-optimal malicious-secure
OT
from
\mathsf{sympLPN}
is the modification of the
PKE
scheme to have a public key computationally indistinguishable from random.
We achieve this modification in
Section

6
.
Our modification begins with a well-known idea that a randomized algorithm sampling from some not-necessarily-uniform distribution can be replaced with a deterministic algorithm which accepts an additional input—a sufficiently long “seed” string
\mathbf{s}
which is genuinely uniformly random.
In our case, the generation of
\mathbf{A}
can be executed by a deterministic algorithm
\mathcal{A}
which iteratively builds a basis of the subspace symplectically orthogonal from the columns already sampled, and then picking a random linear combination of these basis vectors to be the next column of
\mathbf{A}
.
This linear combination can be chosen with access to
O(n)
random bits.

Thus, we would hope to replace the public key
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e})
with
(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e})
, where
\mathbf{A}
is computed from
\mathbf{s}
.
This replacement has no impact on correctness, as the encrypting party simply first computes
\mathbf{A}
from
\mathbf{s}
using
\mathcal{A}
.
However, while this form appears very much hard to distinguish from uniformly random bits
(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{u}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n})
, we formally know only that
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e})
is indistinguishable from
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{u}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n})
.
Thus, to complete the proof, we must show that given
\mathbf{A}
we can distributionally “invert” the
\mathbf{s}\mapsto\mathbf{A}
algorithm to produce a marginally uniformly random
\hat{\mathbf{s}}
such that
\mathcal{A}(\hat{\mathbf{s}})=\mathbf{A}
.
We give such an inversion algorithm and prove its correctness in
Section

6
.
Thus,
\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e}
is indistinguishable from random given either
\mathbf{A}
or
\mathbf{s}
, so
(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e})
is indeed computationally indistinguishable from uniformly random bits.
We therefore achieve round-optimal
OT
from the hardness of
\mathsf{sympLPN}
, and as a consequence, round-optimal general secure multi-party computation.

2.3
Removing one logical bit in
\mathsf{sympLPN}
via symplectic scrambling

We here outline our solution to the primary technical barrier to the security proof of
\mathsf{sympLPN}
-based
PKE
, namely reducing
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n,p\right)
to
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n-1,n,p\right)
.
If we wished to reduce one logical qubit for
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
, it would be straightforward: given
(\mathbf{A}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n\times k},\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e})
, let
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n\times(k-1)}
be
\mathbf{A}
with the last column removed, and run a
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(k-1,n,p\right)
decider on
(\mathbf{A}^{\prime},\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e})
.
If
x_{k}=0
, then
\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+\mathbf{e}
, where
\mathbf{x}=[\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\,|\,x_{k}]
.
Otherwise,
\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+\mathbf{e}+\mathbf{a}
, where
\mathbf{a}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
is the last column of
\mathbf{A}
—so
\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e}
is uniformly random given
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
.
Hence, a
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(k-1,n,p\right)
decider will output
structured
only when the input is structured with
x_{k}=0
, which occurs with large enough probability to obtain a solver for
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
.

In the case of
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n,p\right)
, however, an analogous technique fails completely.
Given
(\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times n},\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e})
, we might again try to remove the last column of
\mathbf{A}
to obtain
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
.
If
x_{n}=0
, then indeed
\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+\mathbf{e}
is a
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n-1,n,p\right)
structured instance.
However, if
x_{n}=1
, then
\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+\mathbf{e}+\mathbf{a}
, but this time the last column
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
of
\mathbf{A}
has a complicated distribution which depends sensitively on the rest of
\mathbf{A}
.
In other words,
\mathbf{a}
is nearly maximally far from uniformly random given
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
, so the instance is not at all close to unstructured.
Consequently, we have no guarantees on the accuracy of a
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n-1,n,p\right)
decider on this input.

To overcome this barrier, we introduce four techniques which approach the reduction in a completely different way and thereby bypass the above obstruction.
Our first technique arises from the observation that the maximal dimension of a subspace of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
in which all vectors are symplectically orthogonal is
n
.
Thus, a useful geometric interpretation of a
(n-1)
-dimensional symplectically orthogonal subspace—the image of the matrix
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times(n-1)}
in a
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n-1,n,p\right)
instance—is as a hyperplane of codimension
1
within some maximal
n
-dimensional symplectically orthogonal subspace, symplectically orthogonal to some random vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
.
(Technically, there are many possible maximal subspaces which contain this hyperplane, but we ignore this subtlety in this overview.)
Given a
2n\times n
matrix
\mathbf{A}
in a
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n,p\right)
instance, our first step is to reduce the dimensionality by 1 by forcing all vectors in the code to be symplectically orthogonal to some fixed
\mathbf{v}
—this step effectively removes a logical bit.
This is easy to do if the first entry of the error
\mathbf{e}
is 0: we can then easily transform the code space to always have first entry
0
.
Equivalently, the code is symplectically orthogonal to the vector
(0^{n},1,0^{n-1})
.
However, a
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n-1,n,p\right)
instance has a code orthogonal to a
random
vector
\mathbf{v}
, not a fixed one.
Thus, our next step is to design a very sparse random operator
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times 2n}
which “rotates” this orthogonal vector to a random vector, taking its normal hyperplane along with it.
We multiply to produce
(\mathbf{CA},\mathbf{CAx}+\mathbf{Ce})
.
Since
\mathbf{C}
is very sparse, the new error
\mathbf{Ce}
is not irrecoverably blown up, but does have a complicated distribution far from depolarizing.
Hence, our third technique is to apply a “noise symmetrization” operation which, using a combination of noise flooding and permutations, maps
\mathbf{Ce}
to a depolarizing error with only slightly larger noise rate
p^{\prime}
than
p
, while not affecting the distribution of the other objects.
This technique turns out, however, to only work when the
(n+1)
th component of
\mathbf{e}
is 0.

In summary, our approach only works when entries
1
and
n+1
of
\mathbf{e}
are
0
, which does not occur with overwhelming probability.
So, to complete the reduction, our final technique is an “interpolation trick”.
This trick arises from the observation that we are free to add extra noise prior to the start of the reduction if we wish, which will change the probabilities of the reduction’s success.
A simple analysis of these probabilities reveals that the two reductions—one with and one without the preliminary noise flooding—cannot both fail, and thus there must exist a successful reduction from
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n,p\right)
to
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n-1,n,p^{\prime}\right)
for
p^{\prime}
only slightly larger than
p
.

This reduction completes the security proof of our
\mathsf{sympLPN}
-based
PKE
scheme.
Given the many technical obstructions which arise in performing even the simplest
\mathsf{sympLPN}
self-reduction, it is somewhat surprising that a rigorous security proof of
\mathsf{sympLPN}
-based
PKE
scheme is possible at all.

2.4
Comparative Hardness of
\mathsf{LPN}
and
\mathsf{sympLPN}

The fact that
\mathsf{sympLPN}
readily implies all of the core primitives of
Cryptomania
with
\mathsf{LPN}
-level efficiency gives a compelling reason to consider
\mathsf{sympLPN}
as an independent post-quantum assumption, provided that
\mathsf{sympLPN}
is not already equivalent or reducible to
\mathsf{LPN}
.

Prior work has shown a reduction from
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(pn,2n,p\right)
to
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n,6p\right)

[
65
]
.
With
p=O(1/\sqrt{n})
, however, this reduction becomes vacuous, as
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(\sqrt{n},2n,1/\sqrt{n}\right)
can be solved in polynomial time by restricting to the first
\sqrt{n}\times\sqrt{n}
minor of
\mathbf{A}
(where an error rate of
1/\sqrt{n}
implies that there typically only
O(1)
errors on this block) and brute-force enumerating constant-weight errors.
It is therefore not known if
\mathsf{LPN}
reduces to
\mathsf{sympLPN}
in this low-noise regime, or if in fact
\mathsf{sympLPN}
reduces to
\mathsf{LPN}
.
The former reduction would establish
\mathsf{sympLPN}
as being at least as good as
\mathsf{LPN}
for cryptography in light of this work, while the latter reduction would prove that
\mathsf{sympLPN}
adds no value for post-quantum security.
While we leave the former open, we prove in
Appendix

D
a strong barrier against the existence of a
\mathsf{sympLPN}
-to-
\mathsf{LPN}
reduction, suggesting that the assumptions are
inequivalent
.

The most natural approach to a reduction would be to, given
(\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times n},\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e})
, simply remove around
n/2
rows of
\mathbf{A}
.
After all,
\mathbf{A}
has
2n^{2}
entries but only has entropy
\sim\frac{3}{2}n^{2}
since there are
\binom{n}{2}\sim\frac{1}{2}n^{2}
constraints.
More generally, we may wish to multiply
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e}
by some
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m\times 2n}
, producing
(\mathbf{BA},(\mathbf{BA})\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{Be})
, and hope that this instance is close to a
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(m,2n,p^{\prime}\right)
instance for some
p^{\prime}
.
It is not at all clear as to why such a strategy would not work, especially because intuition from the leftover hash lemma
[
59
]
suggests that it in fact may succeed.
Moreover, such a “linear reduction” was used, for example, to successfully produce the first non-trivial random self-reduction for
\mathsf{LPN}
from polylog-weight errors in the worst case to error rate
1/2-1/\poly(n)
in the average case
[
21
]
.
We prove, however, that linear reductions
cannot
reduce
\mathsf{sympLPN}
to
\mathsf{LPN}
.
While the proof is quite technical, the central idea is simple.
We show that for any
fixed

\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m\times 2n}
, the random matrix
\mathbf{BA}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m\times n}
is severely deficient in entropy.
That is, while
\mathbf{BA}
must have entropy about
mn
to be uniformly random (and thus a
\mathsf{LPN}
matrix), it actually only has entropy at most
(1-d)mn
for some constant
d
.
To compensate this deficiency, we must choose
\mathbf{B}
from a sufficiently high-entropy distribution.
Using this result, we then prove that any distribution over
\mathbf{B}
which adds enough entropy so as to randomize
\mathbf{BA}
also irrecoverably blows up the error.
More precisely, in the codeword
(\mathbf{BA})\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{Be}
part of the input, the error
\mathbf{Be}
has error weight larger than
\frac{(1-r-\delta)}{2}m
for any
\delta>0
with overwhelming probability, where
r
is the rate
r=\frac{n}{m}
.
Shannon’s noisy coding converse theorem turns out to imply that this error weight is undecodable even information-theoretically.
Hence, this reduction cannot map into
any
statistically solvable
\mathsf{LPN}
instance.

Our barrier does not imply that no reduction exists, but it shows that the most clearly motivated approach fails.
A reduction would have to proceed with a very different strategy, and it is possible that if such a reduction exists, it could also improve the random self-reductions achievable for
\mathsf{LPN}
.
(The current linear reduction—from worst case with polylog-weight to average case with noise rate
1/2-1/\poly(n)
—has very weak parameters, with the primary barrier to improvement being the same issue that we rigorously derive here.)

2.5
Cryptanalysis

Given the close relationship between
\mathsf{sympLPN}
and
\mathsf{LPN}
, a natural question is how the security of our
\mathsf{sympLPN}
-based schemes compares to that of standard
\mathsf{LPN}
-based schemes in practice.
We focus on our
\mathsf{PKE}
scheme in
Section

5
, as our
OWF
construction may assume the hardest variant of
\mathsf{LSN}
, which is already known to be at least as hard as
\mathsf{LPN}
in most relevant parameter regimes
[
65
]
.

As pointed out in
[
24
]
,
the dominant class of attacks against
\mathsf{LPN}
are information-set decoding (ISD) algorithms, originating in the work of Prange and Stern
[
84
,
94
]
.
These attacks are combinatorial: they exploit the sparsity of the error vector and are largely agnostic to the algebraic structure of the underlying code.
Their running time scales exponentially in the block length, with the exponent governed by the particular noise rate.
Because of this structural insensitivity, we expect ISD-type attacks to serve as the primary
generic
benchmark for
\mathsf{sympLPN}
-based
PKE
.

To enable a meaningful comparison, it is important to place the two schemes on equal footing; for example, standard practice
[
31
]
suggests matching parameters by fixing the decryption success probability to be
0.75
.
Since
\mathsf{sympLPN}
employs depolarizing noise whereas
\mathsf{LPN}
uses Bernoulli noise, we can compare our decryption analysis with that of
\mathsf{LPN}
-based schemes
[
31
]
and equate the corresponding decryption success probabilities:
if
p
and
q
denote the noise rates in
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,p\right)
and
\mathsf{LPN}(n,2n,q)
, respectively, this matching yields

1-\frac{4}{3}p^{2}=(1-2q^{2})^{2},\qquad\text{and hence}\qquad p=\sqrt{3(q^{2}-q^{4})}=\sqrt{3}\,q-O(q^{2}).

(3)

In particular, for the same decryption error probability, say
0.75
, the
\mathsf{sympLPN}
distribution produces errors of slightly larger expected weight (by a constant factor) than the corresponding
\mathsf{LPN}
instance.

From the perspective of ISD, this has two competing effects.
On the one hand, since ISD algorithms are driven primarily by the sparsity of the error vector, the increased error weight suggests that
naïve
ISD attacks should perform somewhat worse against
\mathsf{sympLPN}
than against
\mathsf{LPN}
.
On the other hand, the depolarizing noise in
\mathsf{sympLPN}
exhibits a mild pairwise correlation structure, which induces a small entropy loss relative to fully independent noise.
Naturally, this structure can be exploited by a simple
pair-aware
variant of ISD—e.g., modifications of Prange’s original algorithm
[
84
]
—in which coordinates are processed in pairs
(j,n+j)
rather than independently.
Taken together, these considerations suggest that while off-the-shelf ISD attacks may underestimate the vulnerability of
\mathsf{sympLPN}
, tailored ISD variants should provide an accurate point of comparison, and are expected to achieve performance comparable to that observed for
\mathsf{LPN}
-based
PKE
.
This heuristic is consistent with our preliminary numerical investigations.

Our results so far suggest a qualitative message:
\mathsf{sympLPN}
-based
PKE
schemes are likely to be similarly susceptible to brute-force ISD-attacks as comparable
\mathsf{LPN}
-based schemes—even if the security of
\mathsf{sympLPN}
may ultimately hinge on the symplectic structure of the underlying code and its interaction with the noise model.
This is because ISD’s brute-force approach has little dependence on the actual code distribution itself, and therefore does not distinguish between the random matrices in
\mathsf{LPN}
and the random symplectically orthogonal matrices in
\mathsf{sympLPN}
.
Rather, as discussed above, ISD depends more sensitively on the noise distribution.
We thus believe that, for practical purposes, alternative noise distributions (possibly deviating from our quantum-inspired depolarizing model) could further amplify the security of the scheme.
Exploring such directions, as well as refining the analysis of tailored attacks, remains an interesting avenue for future work.

2.6
Outlook

This paper shows that private-key encryption, public-key encryption, and secure multi-party computation can be constructed entirely from the average-case hardness of decoding quantum stabilizer codes,
\mathsf{stateLSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
.
In the private-key regime, we construct one-way functions from high-rate (
\Omega(1)
), high-noise (
\Omega(1)
)
\mathsf{stateLSN}
.
In the public-key regime, we construct
PKE
and round-optimal malicious-secure
OT
(which then implies round-optimal malicious-secure multi-party computation) from low-rate (
O(\frac{\log n}{n})
), low-noise (
O(1/\sqrt{n})
)
\mathsf{stateLSN}
, whose security thus scales as
2^{\widetilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{n})}
.
The security of
\mathsf{stateLSN}
does not appear to depend on the rate of the code at all
[
65
]
, and the security relative to low noise rate approximately matches the best known security for
\mathsf{LPN}
-based
PKE
schemes, namely
2^{\widetilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{n})}
.

From the construction point of view, a pertinent question is whether public-key encryption can be built directly out of high-rate, low-noise
\mathsf{stateLSN}
.
Our approach has been to first quantumly reduce
\mathsf{stateLSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
with
k=O(\log n)
logical qubits to a classical problem,
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n,p\right)
, and then construct public-key cryptography from
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n,p\right)
.
Whether or not either an improved reduction with larger
k
, or a more direct construction from
\mathsf{stateLSN}
, remains open.
A further open constructive avenue is security in different models.
For example,
OT
secure in the
universal composability
model can be constructed from the hardness of
\mathsf{LPN}

[
32
]
; can an analogous construction be made from the hardness of
\mathsf{sympLPN}
?

A second question concerns improving our understanding of the comparative security between
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n,p\right)
and
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(k,n^{\prime},p^{\prime}\right)
, when
p=O(1/\sqrt{n})
, and thereby understanding how
\mathsf{sympLPN}
stands as a post-quantum security assumption relative to
\mathsf{LPN}
.
Is there a reduction from
\mathsf{LPN}
to
\mathsf{sympLPN}
in this regime, and is there
no
reduction in the opposite direction?
In this work, we have shown significant barriers towards the possibility of a converse reduction—from
\mathsf{sympLPN}
to
\mathsf{LPN}
(which would nullify the motivation to build cryptography from
\mathsf{sympLPN}
)—but a more rigorous proof that no reduction exists would substantially strengthen our hope that
\mathsf{sympLPN}
may remain secure even in a world where
\mathsf{LPN}
is broken.
More generally, understanding precisely how symplectic structure affects the complexity of decoding remains a key open question.

3
Preliminaries

A comprehensive introduction to quantum computation can be found in
[
80
]
. For a detailed introduction to quantum error correction, we refer to
[
49
]
.

Pauli matrices.

The four
2\times 2

Pauli matrices
are denoted as

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\quad\,\,\,\,\mathbf{X}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\quad\,\,\,\,\mathbf{Y}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\quad\mathbf{Z}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(4)

The phase-free Pauli group
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is the group of
n
-qubit Pauli operators
\set{\mathbf{I},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z}}^{\otimes n}
which multiply modulo phase.
Any such
n
-qubit Pauli can be represented in so-called
symplectic form
, via a map

\displaystyle\operatorname{Symp}\,:\,\mathcal{P}_{n}\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}.

(5)

Here, bits
j
and
j+n
in
\operatorname{symp}(\mathbf{P})
indicate which Pauli is on the
j
th qubit of
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
.
The two bits are
(0,1)
if
\mathbf{X}
,
(1,0)
if
\mathbf{Z}
,
(1,1)
if
\mathbf{Y}
, and
(0,0)
if
\mathbf{I}
.
Since
\mathbf{XY}=i\mathbf{Z}
,
\mathbf{YZ}=i\mathbf{X}
, and
\mathbf{ZX}=i\mathbf{Y}
, this representation shows that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Symp}(\mathbf{P}_{1}\mathbf{P}_{2})=\operatorname{Symp}(\mathbf{P}_{1})+\operatorname{Symp}(\mathbf{P}_{2})

(6)

and thus that
\Symp
is an isomorphism (with arithmetic mod 2).
The symplectic representation is equipped with a natural inner product given by

\displaystyle(\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{b}_{1})\odot(\mathbf{a}_{2},\mathbf{b}_{2}):=\mathbf{a}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{b}_{2}+\mathbf{a}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{b}_{1}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2),

(7)

where
\cdot
denotes the standard dot product.
Two phase-free Paulis commute if and only if their symplectic representations have zero symplectic inner product.
We say that such vectors are symplectically orthogonal.
The standard symplectic basis is given by
\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{n},\mathbf{f}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{f}_{n}
, where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
is
1
on the
i
th entry and 0 elsewhere, and
\mathbf{f}_{i}
is
1
on the
(i+n)
th entry and zero elsewhere.
Note that

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{i}\odot\mathbf{e}_{j}=\mathbf{f}_{i}\odot\mathbf{f}_{j}=0,\quad\mathbf{e}_{i}\odot\mathbf{f}_{j}=\delta_{ij}.

(8)

Quantum stabilizer codes.

Letting
\mathrm{U}(2^{n})
denote the unitary group on
n
qubits, we define the Clifford group
\mathcal{C}_{n}
as the set of unitaries which leave the Pauli group invariant under conjugation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n}:=\set{\mathbf{C}\in\mathrm{U}(2^{n})\,}{\,\mathbf{C}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{C}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{P}_{n},\,\forall\,\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}.

(9)

A
\llbracket n,k\rrbracket
quantum stabilizer code is the simultaneous
+1
eigenspace of
n-k
independent commuting
n
-qubit Pauli operators in
\mathcal{P}_{n}
.
Such a code can be succinctly specified by a Clifford operator
\mathbf{C}
(e.g., in the form of a quantum circuit) as well as a choice of logical dimension
k
.
In this case, the code space is given by
\mathbf{C}(\ket{0^{n-k}}\otimes\ket{\psi})
, where
\ket{\psi}\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes k}
is any
k
-qubit logical state.
The stabilizers of the subspace of states
\ket{0^{n-k}}\otimes\ket{\psi}
are
\mathbf{Z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{Z}_{n-k}
—hence,
\mathbf{CZ}_{1}\mathbf{C}^{\dagger},\dots\mathbf{CZ}_{n-k}\mathbf{C}^{\dagger}
are stabilizers for code-vectors
\mathbf{C}(\ket{0^{n-k}}\otimes\ket{\psi})
. A random quantum stabilizer code can be generated by using a uniformly random Clifford operator
\mathbf{C}\sim\mathcal{C}_{n}
as the encoding map
[
82
,
95
]
.

If we ignore phases, a Clifford is defined entirely on how it acts (via conjugation) on the
2n
Paulis
\mathbf{X}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{n},\mathbf{Z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{Z}_{n}
.
Thus, a Clifford modulo phases on Paulis can be represented as a matrix in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times 2n}
.
Since conjugation by a Clifford does not affect the commutation relation of two Pauli operators, the symplectic representation of Cliffords preserves the symplectic inner product.
That is,
(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{v})\odot(\mathbf{Cw})=\mathbf{v}\odot\mathbf{w}
for all
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
if and only if
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times 2n}
represents a Clifford operation.
These matrices are referred to as symplectic matrices.
For a Clifford
\mathbf{C}
, we often abuse notation by referring to its matrix representation as
\mathbf{C}
as well, instead of
\Symp(\mathbf{C})
.

Symplectic linear algebra.

Given a subspace
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
, we denote its
symplectic dual
or
symplectic orthogonal complement
by

\displaystyle S^{\perp}:=\set{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}\,}{\,\mathbf{v}\odot\mathbf{w}=0,\,\,\,\forall\mathbf{w}\in S}.

(10)

As with the standard inner product,
\text{dim}(S)+\text{dim}(S^{\perp})=2n
.
However, unlike the standard inner product,
S^{\perp}\cap S
is not necessarily trivial, e.g., when
S
is
isotropic
as defined below.

Definition 3.1

(Isotropic subspaces and matrices)
.

We say that a subspace
S
of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
is
isotropic
if every pair of points in
S
is symplectically orthogonal, i.e.,
S\subseteq S^{\perp}
.
Note that the maximum dimension of an isotropic subspace is
n
.
A matrix is isotropic if its image is an isotropic subspace. Equivalently, a matrix is isotropic if its columns are pairwise symplectically orthogonal.

We remark that an equivalent characterization of an isotropic matrix
\mathbf{M}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}_{1}\\
\mathbf{M}_{2}\end{bmatrix}
is that
\mathbf{M}_{2}^{\intercal}\mathbf{M}_{1}+\mathbf{M}_{1}^{\intercal}\mathbf{M}_{2}=0
.
In other words,
\mathbf{M}
is isotropic if and only if
\mathbf{M}_{1}^{\intercal}\mathbf{M}_{2}
is symmetric.

Quantum and classical noise models.

The
n
-qubit depolarizing noise distribution with error parameter
p\in(0,1)
is denoted as
\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n}
.
This distribution independently draws a Pauli error on each qubit according to a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{p}
; namely, with probability
1-p
, no error occurs, otherwise, with probability
p
, a uniformly random Pauli
\set{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z}}
is drawn and applied.
Note that
\mathcal{D}_{3/4}
coincides with a uniformly random pair of bits. In slight abuse of notation, we frequently use the same notation in the symplectic representation: we use
\mathbf{e}\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n}
as shorthand notation for first sampling a Pauli operator
\mathbf{E}\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n}
and letting
\mathbf{e}=\Symp(\mathbf{E})
. The resulting error vector
\mathbf{e}

has length
2n
, with each pair
(e_{i},e_{i+n})
drawn independently;
Each such pair is
(0,0)
with probability
1-p
, and the other 3 possibilities each with probability
p/3
.
By contrast, a vector
\mathbf{e}\sim\mathsf{Ber}(p)^{\otimes n}
has length
n
, with each bit being
1
independently with probability
p
, and
0
otherwise.

Haar measure over states.

To generate a random logical state
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
, say of a classical linear code, it suffices to choose
\mathbf{x}
uniformly at random.
Quantumly, one way to analogously generate a random logical state is to choose a uniformly random
k
-qubit state.
The notion of a uniformly random state is given by the Haar measure (see
[
74
]
), formally defined for the unitary group.

Definition 3.2

(Haar measure)
.

The Haar measure
\mu
over the group of
d\times d
unitary matrices
\text{U}(d)
is the unique probability measure which is invariant under translation. That is, for all
\mathbf{V}\in\text{U}(d)
and integrable functions
f
,

\displaystyle\int f(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{U})\,d\mu(\mathbf{U})=\int f(\mathbf{U})\,d\mu(\mathbf{U})=\int f(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V})\,d\mu(\mathbf{U}).

(11)

We refer to a
Haar-random state

\ket{\psi}\sim\mu_{k}
to be a state constructed by sampling
\mathbf{U}\sim\mu
a unitary drawn from the Haar measure over
\text{U}(2^{k})
and then letting
\ket{\psi}=\mathbf{U}\ket{0^{k}}
.

3.1
Learning Stabilizers with Noise

The problem of decoding a random quantum error-correcting code, is known as
Learning Stabilizers with Noise

[
82
]
—the quantum analog of
Learning Parity with Noise
(
\mathsf{LPN}
) which we stated in its
decision
variant: decide whether its input is a true noisy codeword or simply a uniformly random bitstring, as opposed to decoding a given noisy codeword (for a formal definition, see
Appendix

A
).

For
Learning Stabilizers with Noise
, there are two variants which we will discuss, termed
\mathsf{stateLSN}
and
\mathsf{LSN}
.
Both computational problems characterize the hardness of decoding an encoded quantum state, where the stabilizer code is sampled uniformly at random, the starting logical state is sampled from a specified distribution, and a random depolarizing error is applied to the state.
In
\mathsf{stateLSN}
, the logical state is a Haar-random pure state: the problem serves as a natural model for average-case quantum stabilizer decoding.
Meanwhile, for
\mathsf{LSN}
, the logical state is chosen as a uniformly random computational basis state.
Because
\mathsf{LSN}
restricts itself to encoding simpler states, it is easier to characterize.
In particular, it is shown in
[
65
]
that
\mathsf{LSN}
is (quantumly) equivalent to a classical computational task.
Below, we define
\mathsf{stateLSN}
, and then
\mathsf{LSN}
in its equivalent classical form.
We note that in
[
65
]
, various forms of these two problems are discussed, including a Decision version and a variant with more than one sample.
The definitions provided here coincide with Search
\mathsf{stateLSN}
and Search
\mathsf{LSN}
with just one sample.

Definition 3.3

(Learning Stabilizers with Noise, state variant
\mathsf{stateLSN}
)
.

The
\mathsf{state}
variant of the Learning Stabilizers with Noise problem, denoted by
\mathsf{stateLSN}(k,n,p)
, is characterized by integers
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in(0,1)
. Both
p
and
k
can vary with
n
. Given as input a sample of the form

\displaystyle(\mathbf{C},\;\mathbf{E}\mathbf{C}\ket{0^{n-k},\psi}),

(12)

where
\mathbf{C}\sim\mathcal{C}_{n}
is a random
n
-qubit Clifford operator (admitting a classical description via, e.g., a circuit),
\mathbf{E}\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n}
is an
n
-qubit Pauli sampled from a depolarizing distribution with parameter
p
, and
\ket{\psi}\sim\mathfrak{\mu}_{k}
is a Haar random
k
-qubit state, the task is to output a quantum state
\rho_{\psi}
within average fidelity at least
\frac{1}{2^{k}}+\frac{1}{\poly(n)}
of
\ket{\psi}
over the choice of
\ket{\psi}\sim\mu_{k}
; that is,

\displaystyle\underset{\ket{\psi}\sim\mu_{k}}{\mathbb{E}}[\braket{\psi|\rho_{\psi}|\psi}]\geq\frac{1}{2^{k}}+\frac{1}{\poly(n)}.

(13)

Next, we formally define the classical formulation of the
\mathsf{LSN}
problem.

Definition 3.4

(Learning Stabilizers with Noise, classical representation)
.

\mathsf{LSN}(k,n,p)
, is characterized by integers
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in(0,1)
.
Here, the input is

\displaystyle\Big(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}\,|\,\mathbf{B}\end{bmatrix},\;\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}\,|\,\mathbf{B}\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{r}\\
\mathbf{y}\end{bmatrix}+\mathbf{e}\Big)

(14)

where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times n}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times k}
are uniformly random matrices subject to the constraint that
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
are isotropic and that
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}\,|\,\mathbf{B}\end{bmatrix}
is full-rank; where
\mathbf{r}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
and
\mathbf{y}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
are random, and where
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
is a depolarizing error with parameter
p
, i.e.
\Symp^{-1}(\mathbf{e})\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n}
(for brevity, we sometimes write
\mathbf{e}\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n}
instead).
The task is to output
\mathbf{y}
with probability at least
1/2^{k}+1/\poly(n)
.

\mathsf{stateLSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
is always at least as hard as
\mathsf{LSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
, and in fact the two problems are equivalent when
k=O(\log n)
.
These reductions are necessarily quantum, as the input and output of
\mathsf{stateLSN}
include quantum states.

Theorem 3.5

(
\mathsf{stateLSN}
versus
\mathsf{LSN}
,
[
65
]
)
.

Let
k,n,\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in(0,1)
such that
k=O(\log n)
. Then there exist efficient quantum reductions from
\mathsf{LSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
to
\mathsf{stateLSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
and from
\mathsf{stateLSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
to
\mathsf{LSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
.

The problem of
\mathsf{stateLSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
(or, equivalently,
\mathsf{LSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
) with
k=O(\log n)
will be the basis for the hardness of the encryption and oblivious transfer schemes that we present.
However, it is challenging to work directly with these problems because of their complicated setup.
As a result, we will instead define a simpler problem which bears a closer resemblance to
\mathsf{LPN}
.
In the next section, we will prove that in fact
\mathsf{LSN}
with
k=O(\log n)
logical qubits reduces to this simpler problem, so that in combination with
Theorem

3.5
, our schemes are secured solely by the hardness of
\mathsf{stateLSN}\!\left(k=O(\log n),n,p\right)
.

Definition 3.6

(Decision
\mathsf{sympLPN}
)
.

Let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in(0,1)
be parameters. Then,
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
is the task of distinguishing with advantage
1/\poly(n)
between

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A},\;\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e})\quad\text{ or }\quad(\mathbf{A},\;\mathbf{u}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n})

(15)

where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times k}
is a random full-rank isotropic matrix, and where
\mathbf{e}
is a depolarizing error with probability parameter
p
, i.e.
\Symp^{-1}(\mathbf{e})\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n}
(for brevity, we often write
\mathbf{e}\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n}
).
The former case is called
structured
and the latter called
unstructured
.
For convenience, we denote
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n,p\right)
, a parameter regime of particular relevance, as
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,p\right)
.

If
\mathbf{A}
was instead chosen to be a uniformly random matrix, then the above problem would resemble Decision
\mathsf{LPN}
.
A random
2n\times k
matrix is full-rank with at least constant probability, so the isotropy condition is the fundamental difference between
\mathsf{LPN}
and
\mathsf{sympLPN}
.

\mathsf{sympLPN}
was first defined in
[
65
]
as a tool for reducing
\mathsf{LPN}
to
\mathsf{LSN}
—
[
65
]
demonstrates that for any regime of
k
,
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,p\right)
reduces to
\mathsf{LSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
, and moreover that there is a reduction from
\mathsf{LPN}
to
\mathsf{sympLPN}
for certain choices of
p
, namely
p=\omega(n^{-1/2})
.

4
Hardness of
\mathsf{sympLPN}

We will construct our public-key cryptographic protocols directly from
\mathsf{sympLPN}(n,p)
for certain
p
.
As a consequence, our first result is a reduction from
\mathsf{stateLSN}
with very few logical qubits to
\mathsf{sympLPN}
.
Therefore, cryptography built on the hardness of
\mathsf{sympLPN}
source their security from the hardness of decoding random quantum stabilizer codes with Haar-random logical states with logarithmically many qubits.
Classically, there is a polynomial time algorithm which solves
\mathsf{LPN}(k,n,p)
when
k=O(\log n)
by brute force.
However, quantumly, there is substantial evidence that
\mathsf{LSN}(k,n,p)
is exponentially hard (in
n
)
for any

k\geq 1

[
65
]
, intuitively because there is no known efficient way to verify proposed solutions for any
k
and thus one must brute-force over possible errors instead of logical states.
Therefore, the fact that
k=O(\log n)
in our codes does not imply that security is lost.

Our public-key encryption will, however, rely on the hardness of both
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n,p\right)=\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,p\right)
and
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n-1,n,p\right)
.
A reduction from
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
to
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(k-1,n,p\right)
is trivial for
\mathsf{LPN}
, but is surprisingly challenging for
\mathsf{sympLPN}
.
Nonetheless, we will then show how to give such a reduction by introducing some new analytical techniques for symplectic subspaces.
As a consequence, the public-key encryption scheme will only rely on the hardness of
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,p\right)
, as desired.

4.1
Reducing
\mathsf{LSN}
for very few logical qubits to
\mathsf{sympLPN}

Since
\mathsf{stateLSN}
and
\mathsf{LSN}
are equivalent for
k=O(\log n)
(see
Theorem

3.5
), we here reduce
\mathsf{LSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
to
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,p\right)
.

Theorem 4.1

(
\mathsf{LSN}
reduces to
\mathsf{sympLPN}
)
.

Let
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
p\in(0,1)
.
Suppose
\mathcal{O}
is an oracle which solves
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,p\right)
.
Then there exists a polynomial time algorithm which solves
\mathsf{LSN}(k,n,p)
, using a single call to
\mathcal{O}
.

Proof.

Let
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{z})
be an instance of
\mathsf{LSN}
, where
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{Ar}+\mathbf{By}+\mathbf{e}
.
The algorithm queries the oracle
\mathcal{O}
with input
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{z})
.
If the oracle outputs
structured
, then return
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{0}
, while if the oracle outputs
unstructured
, return a uniformly random value of
\mathbf{y}\in\{0,1\}^{k}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
.

We analyze this algorithm in two cases: either
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{0}
or
\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{0}
.
If
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{0}
, then
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{z})=(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{A}\mathbf{r})
is precisely a structured instance of
\mathsf{sympLPN}
, since marginally
\mathbf{A}
is a uniformly random full-rank isotropic matrix.
Now, say that
\mathbf{y}\neq 0
. We can assume without loss of generality that
\mathbf{y}=(1,0,\dots,0)^{\intercal}
.
Indeed, for any given
W=\operatorname{im}\mathbf{B}
, each nonzero vector
W
is equally likely to be the first column of
\mathbf{B}
,
\mathbf{By}
.
The distribution of
\mathbf{By}
for
\mathbf{y}=(1,0,\dots,0)^{\intercal}
and
\mathbf{By}
for uniformly random nonzero
\mathbf{y}
are both just a uniformly random nonzero vector in
W
.

Now, observe that the first column of
\mathbf{B}
is equally likely to be any vector in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}\backslash V
, where
V=\operatorname{im}\mathbf{A}
.
For example,
\mathbf{B}
may be sampled column by column by sampling a new vector linearly independent from
\mathbf{A}
and symplectically orthogonal to the previous columns of
\mathbf{B}
, giving the first column this simple form.
Thus in the
\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{0}
case,
\mathbf{z}\sim(\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e})+\mathbf{b}_{1}
, where
\mathbf{b}_{1}
is the first column of
\mathbf{B}
, uniformly random over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}\setminus V
, and
\frac{|V|}{2^{2n}}=2^{-n}
.
Consequently,
\mathbf{b}_{1}
has total variation distance
2^{-n}
from that of uniformly random, using the fact that the TV distance between uniform distributions over sets
S
and
T\subseteq S
is
1-\frac{|T|}{|S|}
.
Hence, the total variation distance between
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{z})
and
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{u})
, where
\mathbf{u}
is uniformly random, is also
O(2^{-n})=\negl(n)
.
In summary, therefore, if
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{0}
, then the oracle
\mathcal{O}
is given a sample from the
structured
distribution of
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,p\right)
; if
\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{0}
, then
\mathcal{O}
is given a sample drawn from a distribution within
\negl(n)
TV distance from the
unstructured
distribution of
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,p\right)
.

To complete the reduction, we calculate the success probability of our algorithm for
\mathsf{LSN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
.

\mathcal{O}
outputs the correct instance type with probability
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
, for some
\epsilon=\frac{1}{\poly(n)}
. If
\mathbf{y}=0
,
\mathcal{O}
outputs
structured
with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
, and the algorithm is correct in this case.
Meanwhile, if
\mathbf{y}\neq 0
, then
\mathcal{O}
outputs
unstructured
with probability
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon-\negl(n)
, implying that the algorithm guesses a random nonzero
\mathbf{y}
and so is correct with probability
\frac{1}{2^{k}-1}(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon-\negl(n))
.
The probability of correctness over random
\mathbf{y}
is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon\right)+\frac{2^{k}-1}{2^{k}}\frac{1}{2^{k}-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon-\negl(n)\right)\,=\,\frac{1}{2^{k}}+\frac{1}{\poly(n)},

(16)

where the last equality holds because
2^{k}=2^{O(\text{log}n)}=\poly(n)
.

∎

4.2
Reducing logical bits in
\mathsf{sympLPN}
without reducing hardness

In this section, we tighten the hardness of
\mathsf{sympLPN}
by reducing
\mathsf{sympLPN}(n,p)=\mathsf{sympLPN}(n,n,p)
to
\mathsf{sympLPN}(n-1,n,p^{\prime})
for slightly larger
p^{\prime}
.
In particular, this reduction barely changes the parameters, simply reducing the number of logical bits by 1.
However, it is surprisingly technical and essential for our cryptographic constructions.
To give a baseline intuition for this reduction, we show in
Appendix

A
a simple analogous reduction for
\mathsf{LPN}
, which proceeds by simply removing the last column of the code matrix.
Unfortunately, as discussed in
Section

2
, this proof technique does not extend to
\mathsf{sympLPN}
because the last column of the
\mathsf{sympLPN}
code matrix depends heavily on the rest of the matrix.
Thus, our reduction technique proceeds via a completely different approach.

Our proof will proceed in three steps.
We begin with a sample of
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,p\right)
,
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{u})
, where
\mathbf{u}
is either structured (i.e. of the form
\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e}
) or uniformly random.
Letting
V=\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{A})
, we consider the subspace
W:=\text{span}(\mathbf{f}_{1})^{\perp}\cap V
, i.e. the hyperplane of vectors symplectically orthogonal to
\mathbf{f}_{1}
.

W
has dimension
n-1
with overwhelming probability (so we will assume it does), and it contains precisely the vectors in
V
whose first coordinate is zero.
Suppose
\mathbf{u}
is structured.
If the first physical bit has no error, i.e.
e_{1}=0
, then
\mathbf{Ax}\in W
if and only if the first bit of
\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e}
is 0.
We wish to transform
\mathbf{u}
to some
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
such that
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
is of the form
\mathbf{Ax}^{\prime}+\mathbf{e}
but
\mathbf{Ax}^{\prime}\in W
.
If
\mathbf{Ax}\in W
, then no further action is required, and we set
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}=\mathbf{u}
.
If
\mathbf{Ax}
is
not
in
W
, by adding some
\mathbf{v}\in V\backslash W
we can obtain
\mathbf{u^{\prime}}:=(\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{v})+\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{Ax}^{\prime}+\mathbf{e}
such that
\mathbf{Ax}^{\prime}\in W
.
Note that if instead
\mathbf{u}
were unstructured, this transformation would leave it uniformly random.

After this step, under the assumption that
e_{1}=0
, we have almost prepared a sample of
\mathsf{sympLPN}
with
n-1
logical qubits.
Indeed, letting
\mathbf{B}
be a code with
W
as its codespace, we can prepare
\mathbf{u^{\prime}}
so that:

1.

If
\mathbf{u}
is uniformly random, then
\mathbf{u^{\prime}}
is uniformly random.

2.

If
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e}
, then
\mathbf{u^{\prime}}=\mathbf{By}+\mathbf{e}
for uniformly random
\mathbf{y}
.

However,
W
is
not
a uniformly random
(n-1)
-dimensional isotropic vector space, because it is always symplectically orthogonal to a known vector, namely
\mathbf{f}_{1}
.
Therefore,
\mathbf{B}
is not a uniformly random matrix with symplectically orthogonal columns.
In the second step of the reduction, we design a sparse Clifford operator
\mathbf{C}
which randomly rotates the normal vector
\mathbf{f}_{1}
(the hyperplane then follows along).
This operation fully scrambles the code, so that
\mathbf{CB}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times(n-1)}
is a uniformly random matrix with symplectically orthogonal columns.

This second step converts a structured sample
\mathbf{u^{\prime}}
to
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{u^{\prime}}=\mathbf{CBy}+\mathbf{Ce}
, however, which damages the distribution of the noise
\mathbf{e}
.
However, since
\mathbf{C}
is sparse, as long as
e_{n+1}=0
, i.e. no error occurs on the
(n+1)
st physical bit,
\mathbf{Ce}
is low-weight.
In this case, we are able to re-randomize to transform
\mathbf{Ce}
back into depolarizing noise, at the cost of a slightly larger noise parameter
p^{\prime}
.
Now, we may query the
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n-1,n,p^{\prime}\right)
oracle, which will output
structured
or
unstructured
, and the reduction can output the same for the sample
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{u})
.

This completes the reduction, but only under the assumption that a good event occurs, namely that
e_{1}=e_{n+1}=0
.
However, interestingly, this assumption can be completely circumvented.
In the original sample,
(e_{1},e_{n+1})
follow a 1-qubit depolarizing distribution with parameter
p
.
We are, however, free to increase this noise parameter to any
q>p
by adding additional error ourselves.
For example, we could create an alternate reduction by first adding completely random bits to increase the noise on
(e_{1},e_{n+1})
to that of
\mathcal{D}_{3/4}
, before executing the reduction outlined above.
We show that at least one of these two reductions must succeed, or else the reduction could not possibly succeed when the good event
does
occur.
This is a contradiction, and thus either the original reduction or the alternate one with extra noise added must reduce
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,p\right)
to
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n-1,n,p^{\prime}\right)
.

To establish the above argument rigorously, we begin by constructing the sparse, randomizing distribution of Cliffords.
Recall that
\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{n},\mathbf{f}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{f}_{n}
denotes the standard symplectic basis on
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
.

Definition 4.2

(Symplectic hyperplane rotation)
.

For
n\geq 2
, a
random symplectic hyperplane rotation
is an efficiently sampleable ensemble of matrices
\mathcal{R}_{n}
in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times 2n}
constructed as follows.
Sample a uniformly random
\mathbf{r}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
.
Let
k
be the first (positive) index for which
r_{n+k}=1
(if there is no such index, output
\mathbf{C}:=\mathbf{I}
).
Let
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
be the same as
\mathbf{r}
, except the
(n+1)
st and
(n+k)
th entries are swapped.
Let the map
\mathbf{C}_{0}
execute the following map on standard basis vectors.

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{1}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathbf{e}_{1},

(17)

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{1}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathbf{r}^{\prime},

(18)

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{j}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathbf{e}_{j}+(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\odot\mathbf{e}_{j})\mathbf{e}_{1}\quad\text{for $j$ from $2$ to $n$},

(19)

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{j}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathbf{f}_{j}+(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\odot\mathbf{f}_{j})\mathbf{e}_{1}\quad\text{for $j$ from $2$ to $n$}

(20)

Define a matrix
\mathbf{\Pi}
which swaps
(\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{f}_{1})
and
(\mathbf{e}_{k},\mathbf{f}_{k})
, and otherwise for each
j\neq 1,k
acts as identity on
\mathbf{e}_{j},\mathbf{f}_{j}
.
Then, output
\mathbf{C}:=\mathbf{\Pi}\mathbf{C_{0}}
.
Note that
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{f}_{1}=\mathbf{r}
.

This rotation, by uniformly randomizing
\mathbf{f}_{1}
, also randomizes the hyperplane that is symplectically orthogonal to it.
The remaining relations serve only to ensure that the map is a sparse symplectic matrix (i.e., that it preserves the symplectic inner product).
We claim that
\mathbf{C}
is a valid symplectic matrix which maps a random symplectic hyperplane orthogonal to
\mathbf{f}_{1}
to a completely random symplectic hyperplane.

Lemma 4.3

(Randomizing symplectic hyperplanes)
.

Denote by
\mathcal{R}_{n}
the ensemble of random symplectic hyperplane rotations from
Section

4.2
.
Sample
\mathbf{C}\sim\mathcal{R}_{n}
.
Then the following properties hold.

(1)

\mathbf{C}
is a symplectic matrix.

(2)

For
\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{n-1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
a uniformly random basis of a
(n-1)
-dimensional isotropic subspace
V
(see
Section

) which is random subject to the condition that
V

\mathbf{f}_{1}
, let
\mathbf{w}_{i}:=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{v}_{i}
.
Then the distribution of
(\mathbf{w}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{w}_{n-1})
is within total variation distance
\negl(n)
to a uniformly random basis of a random
(n-1)
-dimensional isotropic subspace.

The proof is deferred to
Appendix

C
.
Before we proceed to the reduction, we also require a lemma that total depolarizing noise, applied to only certain qubits, can be scrambled into completely symmetric depolarizing noise on all qubits.
A similar technique was used in

]
to reduce from
\mathsf{LPN}
to
\mathsf{sympLPN}
.

Lemma 4.4

(Noise symmetrization)
.

Let
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
, and let
\pi\sim S^{n}
be a random permutation on
n
elements.
We define
\pi
to act on elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
by executing the same permutation on the first and last set of
n
indices.
Suppose that
(e_{j},e_{n+j})=(0,0)
for all
j
except in a known set
M
of size
m=\omega(\log n)
; for
j\in M
,
(e_{j},e_{n+j})\sim\mathcal{D}_{3/4}
independently (i.e., random bits).
Then, there exists a distribution
\mu_{n}
over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
, sampleable in time
\poly(n)
, such that
\pi(\mathbf{e}+\mathbf{e}^{\prime})
for
\mathbf{e}^{\prime}\sim\mu_{n}
is within total variation distance
\negl(n)
from
\mathcal{D}_{q}^{\otimes n}
, where
q=\frac{m}{n}
.

The proof is deferred as well to
Appendix

C
.
We also record a relevant lemma about the product of depolarizing distributions.

Lemma 4.5

(Depolarizing convolution,
[
65
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
be single-qubit phase-free Paulis such that
\mathbf{P}_{1}\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}
and, independently,
\mathbf{P}_{2}\sim\mathcal{D}_{u}
where
u=\frac{q-p}{1-\frac{4}{3}p}
for any
p\in[0,\frac{3}{4}]
and
q\in[p,\frac{3}{4}]
.
Then
\mathbf{P}_{1}\mathbf{P}_{2}\sim\mathcal{D}_{q}
.

Theorem 4.6

(Reducing by one logical bit in
\mathsf{sympLPN}
)
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
be such that
\frac{3}{4}-p=\Omega(1)
, and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n\geq 2
.
Suppose
\mathcal{O}
is an oracle which solves Decision
\mathsf{sympLPN}(n-1,n,p+\log^{2}n/n)
.
Then there exists an algorithm running in time
\poly(n)
, which solves Decision
\mathsf{sympLPN}(n,p)
, using a single call to
\mathcal{O}
.

Proof.

Suppose first that the input is structured.
That is, we are given
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{u}:=\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e})
, where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times n}
is a uniformly random basis of a uniformly random symplectic subspace,
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
, and
\mathbf{e}\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n}
.
Define
V:=\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{A})
.
By Lemma 6.2 of
[
65
]
, the first
n/2
elements of the first row of
\mathbf{A}
are marginally independently uniformly random bits up to
\negl(n)
total variation distance.
Therefore, with probability
1-\negl(n)
, at least one of the first
n/2
columns of
\mathbf{A}
has first entry 1.
Define
V_{0}\subseteq\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{A})
to be the subspace of
\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{A})
with vectors whose first entry is 0.
Having first entry 0 is equivalent to being symplectically orthogonal to
\mathbf{f}_{1}
.
Conditioning on the
1-\text{negl}(n)
probability event, this is one extra nontrivial constraint, so
\dim(V_{0})=n-1
.
Let
\mathbf{A}_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times(n-1)}
be a random basis matrix for
V_{0}
.
Suppose that a good event occurs, namely that
(e_{1},e_{n+1})=(0,0)
.
Conditioned on this event, either
u_{1}=0
or
u_{1}=1
.
If
u_{1}=1
, choose any element of
V\backslash V_{0}
, and subtract it from
\mathbf{u}
.
If
u_{1}=0
, do nothing at this step.
Denote by
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
the outcome of this step.
Note that conditioned on
\mathbf{A}_{0}
,
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
is equidistributed as
\mathbf{A}_{0}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+\mathbf{e}^{\prime}
, where
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}
and
\mathbf{e}^{\prime}
is independent depolarizing noise on pairs
(2,n+2),\dots,(n,2n)
but
(e^{\prime}_{1},e^{\prime}_{n+1})=(0,0)
.
Thus, we can equivalently express
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}=\mathbf{A}_{0}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+\mathbf{e}^{\prime}
.

Next, we sample and apply a random symplectic hyperplane rotation Clifford
\mathbf{C}
from the ensemble in
Section

4.2
.
With probability
1-\negl(n)
(we condition on this event), there is some index
k\geq 1
, which is the first for which
r_{n+k}=1
, where
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{f}_{1}
.
We apply
\mathbf{C}
to
\mathbf{A}_{0}
and
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
.
By
Section

4.2
,
\mathbf{B}:=\mathbf{CA}_{0}
is statistically indistinguishable from a uniformly random basis of a uniformly random symplectic subspace of dimension
n-1
.
Meanwhile,
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}=\mathbf{Bx}^{\prime}+\mathbf{C}\mathbf{e}^{\prime}
.
The distribution of
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{e}^{\prime}
can be derived from the structural form of
\mathbf{C}
.
Since
\mathbf{e}^{\prime}
is independent depolarizing noise
\mathcal{D}_{p}
on the last
n-1
qubits and
e^{\prime}_{1},e^{\prime}_{n+1}=0
, we may write
\mathbf{e}^{\prime}=\sum_{j=2}^{n}(a_{j}\mathbf{e}_{j}+b_{j}\mathbf{f}_{j})
,
where each
(a_{j},b_{j})\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}
independently. Let
\boldsymbol{\Pi}
be the permutation matrix from
Section

4.2
. Then, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}\mathbf{e}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Pi}\left(c\mathbf{e}_{1}+\sum_{j=2}^{n}(a_{j}\mathbf{e}_{j}+b_{j}\mathbf{f}_{j})\right)

(21)

\displaystyle=c\mathbf{e}_{k}+(a_{k}\mathbf{e}_{1}+b_{k}\mathbf{f}_{1})+\sum_{j\neq k,1}a_{j}\mathbf{e}_{j}+b_{j}\mathbf{f}_{j}

(22)

where the coefficient
c
of
\mathbf{e}_{k}
has a potentially complicated distribution correlated with the
(a_{j},b_{j})
.
That is,
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{e}^{\prime}
takes the form of independent
\mathcal{D}_{p}
noise on all but the
k
th pair, where the distribution is complicated.
To adjust for this, we will add uniformly random bits to the
k
th pair (i.e. sample from
\mathcal{D}_{3/4}
), given by
\mathbf{s}_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
which is
0
on all indices, except uniformly random on
k
and
n+k
.
This step erases any correlation the
k
th pair currently has with the
(a_{j},b_{j})_{j\neq k}
(since this pair is now independently and uniformly random).
The distribution of the error is therefore now
\mathbf{Ce}^{\prime}+\mathbf{s}_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes k-1}\otimes\mathcal{D}_{3/4}\otimes\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n-k}
.
It is convenient to represent this distribution as the sum of two random variables, one
\mathbf{e}^{\prime\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n}
and the other
\mathbf{s}
which is the uniform distribution on the
k
th index and 0 elsewhere.
Now, to satisfy the conditions of
Section

4.2
, choose
m-1
—for
m=\frac{\log^{2}(n)}{1-\frac{4}{3}p}
—indices other than
k
and add a vector
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
which is uniformly random bits on these
m-1
pairs and 0 elsewhere.
Define
\mathbf{t}:=\mathbf{s}+\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
, a vector which has uniform bits on
m
pairs and is
(0,0)
on all other pairs.
We add
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
to our error, producing
\mathbf{e}^{\prime\prime}+\mathbf{t}
.
Note that
\mathbf{e}^{\prime\prime}
and
\mathbf{t}
are independent.
Applying
Section

4.2
, we may add an extra certain random vector
\mathbf{t}^{\prime}
and apply a random permutation
\pi
on the
n
index pairs such that
\pi(\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{t}^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{D}_{m/n}^{\otimes n}
.
On the other hand,
\mathbf{e}^{\prime\prime}
is permutation-invariant, so
\pi(\mathbf{e}^{\prime\prime})\sim\mathbf{e}^{\prime\prime}
.
Let
p^{\prime}=p+\log^{2}n/n
.
We defined
m
such that
m/n=\frac{p^{\prime}-p}{1-\frac{4}{3}p}=\frac{\log^{2}n}{n(1-\frac{4}{3}p)}
; for
p=\frac{3}{4}-\Omega(1)
, this choice ensures that
m=\omega(\log n)
.
Hence, by
Section

4.2
,

\displaystyle\pi(\mathbf{e}^{\prime\prime}+\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{t}^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n}+\mathcal{D}_{m/n}^{\otimes n}=\mathcal{D}_{p^{\prime}}^{\otimes n},

(23)

We conclude that if
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{u})
is (up to
\negl(n)
TV distance), then
(\pi(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}_{0}),\pi(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}_{0}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+\mathbf{e}^{\prime\prime}+\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{t}^{\prime}))
is precisely a structured instance of
\mathsf{sympLPN}(n-1,n,p^{\prime})
, since
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}_{0}
and
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
are jointly follow a permutation-invariant distribution while
\pi(\mathbf{e}^{\prime\prime}+\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{t}^{\prime})
follows precisely the right error distribution.
On the other hand, if the input were instead unstructured, then almost all of the operations we performed on
\mathbf{u}
would leave its distribution—uniform over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
—invariant.
The only step which does not is the first, wherein we alter
\mathbf{u}
to
\mathbf{u^{\prime}}
which yields a uniformly random vector with first bit
0
.
However, we later multiply by
\mathbf{C}
which maps the first bit to the
k
th bit, and then add a uniformly random bit to index
k
, and thus uniform randomness is preserved.
Therefore, this transformation yields a
\mathsf{sympLPN}(n-1,n,p^{\prime})
problem which has negligible total variation distance from structured (resp. unstructured) if the input
\mathsf{sympLPN}(n,p)
instance is structured (resp. unstructured).
We submit our transformed instance to
\mathcal{O}
and output its Decision.

To complete the proof, we must address the cases in which the good event does not occur.
That is, we discuss the cases when
(e_{1},e_{n+1})\neq(0,0)
.
The other cases for the values of
(e_{1},e_{n+1})
are
(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)
.
Let
p_{ij}=\Pr[(e_{1},e_{n+1})=(i,j)]
.
Then
p_{00}=1-p
and
p_{01}=p_{10}=p_{11}=p/3
.
Define
q_{ij}
to be the probability that
\mathcal{O}
outputs
structured
when we receive a structured input wherein
(e_{1},e_{n+1})=(i,j)
; let
\bar{q}
be the probability that
\mathcal{O}
outputs
structured
on an unstructured input.
By the above,
|q_{00}-\bar{q}|=\frac{1}{\poly(n)}
.
In general,

\displaystyle Q_{0}

\displaystyle:=\Pr_{p_{ij}=(1-p,p/3,p/3,p/3)}[\mathcal{O}=\text{{structured}}\,|\,\text{{structured}}]

(24)

\displaystyle=q_{00}(1-p)+\frac{p}{3}(q_{01}+q_{10}+q_{11}).

(25)

Now, consider an alternate reduction wherein we first add uniform noise to bits
1
and
n+1
of
\mathbf{u}
.
This has no effect on unstructured instances, but on structured instances, we may equivalently express the effect of the extra noise by modifying
p_{ij}=1/4
for all
i,j
.
In this case,

\displaystyle Q_{1}

\displaystyle:=\Pr_{p_{ij}=(1/4,1/4,1/4,1/4)}[\mathcal{O}=\text{{structured}}\,|\,\text{{structured}}]

(26)

\displaystyle=q_{00}\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{4}(q_{01}+q_{10}+q_{11}).

(27)

Suppose that both probabilities were negligibly close to unstructured probability, i.e.
|Q_{0}-\bar{q}|=\negl(n)
and
|Q_{1}-\bar{q}|=\negl(n)
.
Define

\displaystyle a:=\frac{1}{1-\frac{4}{3}p},\;b:=-\frac{4}{3}ap.

(28)

Then
a+b=1
and
aQ_{0}+bQ_{1}=q_{00}
.
By the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\poly(n)}

\displaystyle=|q_{00}-\bar{q}|=|aQ_{0}+bQ_{1}-\bar{q}|\leq a|Q_{0}-\bar{q}|+|b|\cdot|Q_{1}-\bar{q}|

(29)

\displaystyle=a\cdot\negl(n)+|b|\cdot\negl(n)=\negl(n),

(30)

where in the last line we used the fact that
\frac{3}{4}-p=\Omega(1)
so that
a=O(1)
and thus
|b|=O(1)
.
This is a contradiction, and therefore either
|Q_{0}-\bar{q}|=1/\poly(n)
or
|Q_{1}-\bar{q}|=1/\poly(n)
.
In other words, at least one of the two reductions solves Decision
\mathsf{sympLPN}(n,p)
with non-negligible advantage.
∎

We may now proceed to construct cryptographic protocols from
\mathsf{sympLPN}
, starting with a simple one-way function, and then constructing public-key cryptography and oblivious transfer schemes that rely on the above result.

Theorem 5.2

.

(31)

.
Our security proof relies on the following indistinguishability lemma, which one can think of as the

of

.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be the security parameter.
Suppose that there exists an efficient (quantum or classical) algorithm which runs in time
\poly(n)
and, with non-negligible advantage, distinguishes

\displaystyle(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{f}\odot\mathbf{H})\quad\text{from}\quad(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{r}),

(32)

where
\mathbf{H}=(\mathbf{B}\,|\,\mathbf{v})\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times(n+1)}
consists of a random full-rank isotropic matrix
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times n}
and a random column vector
\mathbf{v}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
, and where

\mathbf{f}\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n}

and
\mathbf{r}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n+1}
.
Then there exists an efficient (quantum or classical) algorithm which solves Decision
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n-1,p\right)
.

Proof.

Suppose we are given as input the
\mathsf{sympLPN}(n-1,n,p)
instance
(\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times(n-1)},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n})
, where
\mathbf{b}
is either structured with
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{f}
, for
\mathbf{f}\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n}
, or unstructured with
\mathbf{b}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
.
Note that the matrix
\mathbf{A}
has full column rank
n-1
, and thus
S=\mathrm{im}(\mathbf{A})
spans a
(n-1)
-dimensional subspace within
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
. Consequently, the symplectic dual
S^{\perp}
—the space of vectors whose symplectic inner product with vectors in
S
is zero—is a subspace of dimension
n+1
.
Our reduction proceeds as follows:

1.

Sample
\mathbf{u}\sim S^{\perp}\backslash S
.
Let
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times n}
be a random basis for
\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{A})\oplus\text{span}(\mathbf{u})
.
Note that
\mathbf{B}
is isotropic with
\dim\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{B})=n
.
Since
n
is the maximum dimension of an isotropic subspace,
\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{B})=\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{B})^{\perp}
.
Then, let
\mathbf{v}
be a random vector in
S^{\perp}\setminus\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{B})
.
Set
\mathbf{H}=(\mathbf{B}\,|\,\mathbf{v})
.
We argue that
\mathbf{H}
has negligible total variation distance from the random variable mentioned in the claim.

2.

Run the assumed distinguisher on input
(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{b}\odot\mathbf{H})
.

Since each column of
\mathbf{H}
is in
S^{\perp}
,
\mathbf{H}^{\intercal}\odot\mathbf{A}=0
.
Consequently,

•

if
\mathbf{b}
is structured with
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{f}
, our reduction computes

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}\odot\mathbf{H}=(\mathbf{H}^{\intercal}\odot\mathbf{b})^{\intercal}=(\mathbf{H}^{\intercal}\odot(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{f}))^{\intercal}=(\mathbf{H}^{\intercal}\odot\mathbf{f})^{\intercal}=\mathbf{f}\odot\mathbf{H};

(33)

•

if
\mathbf{b}
is unstructured with
\mathbf{b}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
, then our reduction computes
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{b}\odot\mathbf{H}
.

In both cases, assuming a negligible total variation distance between
\mathbf{H}
and the random variable mentioned above, we claim that the input to the distinguisher is negligibly close to the desired distribution.
In the structured case, this is immediate, whereas in the unstructured case it readily follows from the fact that
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{b}\odot\mathbf{H}
is uniformly random over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n+1}
when
\mathbf{b}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
itself is uniform.
This is because
\mathbf{H}
is a full-rank matrix, so that every bitstring in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n+1}
has the same number of pre-images under
\mathbf{H}^{\intercal}
.

We need only justify the assertion in Step 1 regarding the distribution of
\mathbf{H}
.
First, we claim that
\mathbf{B}
is a uniformly random full-rank isotropic matrix.
Indeed,
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times(n-1)}
is a uniformly random full-rank isotopic matrix, and there are exactly
2^{n+1}-2^{n-1}
choices for
\mathbf{u}
no matter what
\mathbf{A}
is.
Hence, the probability
p_{\mathbf{B}}
of sampling any given
\mathbf{B}
that is full rank and isotropic is
\frac{1}{(2^{n+1}-2^{n-1})N_{\mathbf{A}}}
, where
N_{\mathbf{A}}
is the number of full-rank isotropic matrices
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times(n-1)}
.
This probability is equal for any such
\mathbf{B}
, so
p_{\mathbf{B}}
is indeed uniformly distributed.

Next, we claim that
\mathbf{v}
is negligibly close in distribution to a uniformly random vector outside of
\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{B})
.
By definition,
\mathbf{v}
is a random vector, not contained in
\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{B})
, that is symplectically orthogonal to a random
(n-1)
-dimensional subspace
S
of
\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{B})
, namely
\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{A})
.
For any nonzero
\mathbf{v}\notin\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{B})
,
\mathbf{v}
has an orthogonal complement of dimension
2n-1
, and its intersection with
\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{B})
therefore has dimension
n-1
, one less than the maximum possible
n
.
This is because not every element of
\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{B})
is orthogonal to
\mathbf{v}
, as
\mathbf{v}\notin\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{B})=\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{B})^{\perp}
.
For each
\mathbf{v}\notin\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{B})
, therefore, there is exactly one
(n-1)
-dimensional subspace of
\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{B})
which is orthogonal to
\mathbf{v}
.
Say that the number of
(n-1)
-dimensional subspaces of
\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{B})
is
N_{\mathbf{B}}
.
Then the probability of sampling any
\mathbf{v}\notin\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{B})
is
\frac{1}{(2^{n+1}-2^{n})N_{\mathbf{B}}}
, a constant not depending on
\mathbf{v}
as desired, so long as
\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}
which occurs with
\negl(n)
probability.
(
2^{n+1}-2^{n}
, above, is the number of vectors
\mathbf{v}\notin\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{B})
orthogonal to any given
(n-1)
-dimensional
V\subseteq\mathbf{B}
.)

Recall that the total variation distance between uniform distributions over sets
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
where
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
, is given by
1-|\mathcal{X}|/|\mathcal{Y}|
.
Thus, for each
\mathbf{B}
,
\mathbf{v}
has a negligible total variation distance from uniform over all of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
, since it is (negligibly close to) uniform over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}\backslash\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{B})
, which is a
1-\negl(n)
fraction of the full set.
Therefore, the distribution of
\mathbf{H}=(\mathbf{B}\,|\,\mathbf{v})
has negligible total variation distance from one wherein
\mathbf{B}
is a uniformly random full-rank isotropic matrix and
\mathbf{v}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
.
∎

Theorem 5.4

(Security)
.

Let

be the security parameter and
\Sigma

5

Suppose there is a (quantum or classical) algorithm

Then there is a (quantum or classical) algorithm

or Decision
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n-1,p\right)

Proof.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Consider the following hybrid distributions for

•

H
{{}_{0}}:
This is the ciphertext pair

produced by

5

\displaystyle\mathsf{ct}=(\mathbf{f}\odot\mathbf{A},\mathbf{f}\odot\mathbf{b}+\mu)

•

6
Strongly uniform public-key encryption

In this section, we alter our previous
\mathsf{PKE}
construction to upgrade it into a so-called Strongly Uniform Type-A
\mathsf{PKE}
(
SU-PKE
).
While this alteration adds complications to the construction, a
SU-PKE
scheme is known to imply a round-optimal oblivious transfer (
OT
) scheme
[
43
]
, which in turn implies all of secure multiparty computation
[
68
,
63
]
.

Definition 6.1

(Strongly Uniform Type-A Public-Key Encryption)
.

A
\mathsf{PKE}
scheme
(\mathsf{Gen},\mathsf{Enc},\mathsf{Dec})
is called "Strongly Uniform of Type A" if the public key
\mathsf{pk}
generated by
\mathsf{Gen}(1^{n})
is computationally indistinguishable from a uniform distribution over some efficiently sampleable group parameterized by
n
,
G(n)
.

For our purposes, the public key takes values in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m}
for some
m
depending on
n
, and this is the corresponding group.
However, the PKE scheme presented in
Section

5
is not strongly uniform.
The public key is of the form
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e})
—while
\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e}
is computationally indistinguishable from uniform,
\mathbf{A}
is a uniformly random full-rank isotropic, and is thus far from uniform.
Fortunately, it is possible to change the cryptographic protocol to remedy this problem.
To do so, we first prove a lemma that relates the random variable
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times n}
to a uniform distribution over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{4n^{2}}
.

Lemma 6.2

(A uniformly random public key)
.

Sample
\mathbf{s}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{4n^{2}}
and let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times n}
be a uniformly random full-rank isotropic matrix.
There exists a deterministic, polynomial-time classical algorithm
\mathcal{A}
and a polynomial-time, randomized classical algorithm
\mathcal{B}
such that
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{s})
is statistically indistinguishable from
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{A})
is statistically indistinguishable from
\mathbf{s}
, and

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathcal{B},\mathbf{A}}[\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{A}))=\mathbf{A}]=1-\negl(n).

(35)

Proof.

Intuitively,
\mathcal{A}
uses
\mathbf{s}
as a source of randomness to deterministically construct
\mathbf{A}
such that if
\mathbf{s}
is uniformly random, then
\mathbf{A}
is a uniformly random full-rank isotropic matrix.
We construct
\mathcal{A}
as follows.
Given
\mathbf{s}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{4n^{2}}
, we build
\mathbf{A}
column-by-column.
Let
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times i}
be a matrix with
i
columns, and let
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{0}
be an empty matrix.
For
i=1,\dots,2n
, we use a deterministic algorithm to calculate an ordered basis for the symplectic dual space of
\operatorname{im}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i-1})
.
(By convention, the dual of the empty matrix is
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
.)
This orthogonal complement has some dimension
d_{i}\leq 2n
, and we may consume the next
d_{i}
unused bits of
\mathbf{s}
to sample a random vector
\mathbf{w}\in\operatorname{im}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i-1})^{\perp}
.
Then, we can set
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i-1}\,|\,\mathbf{w})
.
This process yields a matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{2n}
, and there are always enough bits of
\mathbf{s}
since there are
2n
steps taking
d_{i}\leq 2n
bits each.

\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{2n}
is almost what we want, except that it has twice as many columns as needed and is not necessarily full rank.

Therefore, we define
\mathbf{A}
by iteratively adding the
i
th column of
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{2n}
if it is linearly independent to all previous columns.
We then pad
\mathbf{A}
with zero columns until all
n
columns are filled in (this is an edge-case event which we will momentarily show occurs with only negligible probability).
Note that the number of columns is never larger than
n
, since there can be at most
n
linearly independent vectors that are symplectically orthogonal.

Now, we claim that
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{s})
is statistically indistinguishable from a uniformly random full-rank isotropic matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times n}
.
Conditioned on the event
L
—that
\mathbf{A}
was not padded with any zero columns—we are constructing a matrix column by column by sampling symplectically orthogonal, linearly independent vectors, which yields precisely the same distribution as
\mathbf{A}
.
Hence, we need only show
\Pr[L]=\negl(n)
.
For the
i
th column of
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{2n}
, the chance that it does not increase the rank from the previous
i-1
columns is precisely
\frac{2^{2n-d_{i}}}{2^{d_{i}}}
.
For
d_{i}\geq n+1
this probability is at most
1/4
.
At
i=1
,
d_{i}
begins at
2n
, and decreases by 1 each time a linearly independent vector is added.
If
\mathbf{A}
does not have
n
columns prior to padding, then
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{2n}
does not have rank
n
, so this event must occur at least
n+1
times.
Each time this event occurs,
d_{i}
does not decrease, which means
d_{i}
decreased at most
n-1
times.
Hence,
d_{i}\geq n+1
for all
i
, and multiplying these events together gives

\Pr[L]\leq\frac{1}{4^{n+1}}=\negl(n).

We next construct algorithm
\mathcal{B}
.
Given an input
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times n}
that is full-rank and isotropic, we will describe a procedure for
i=1,\dots,2n
to build matrices
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
, with
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{0}
empty.

1.

Let
d_{i}:=\dim\operatorname{im}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i-1})^{\perp}
.
With probability
1-\frac{2^{2n-d_{i}}}{2^{d_{i}}}
, let
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i-1}\,|\,\mathbf{v}_{i})
, where
\mathbf{v}_{i}
is the next column of
\mathbf{A}
which has not yet been sampled.

2.

Otherwise, let
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i-1}\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{i})
, where
\mathbf{u}_{i}
is a random vector in the span of the columns in
\mathbf{A}
which have already been sampled.
(If no column has yet been sampled, then
\mathbf{u}_{i}=\mathbf{0}
.)

3.

Notice that the columns are all symplectically orthogonal.
Now, given
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{2n}
, reconstruct the random bits
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
of length
m\leq 4n^{2}
that would have produced
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{2n}
.
Sample the remaining bits uniformly at random to produce
\mathbf{s}
of length
4n^{2}
.
Output
\mathbf{s}
.

By the same reasoning as before,

\negl(n)

Moreover, conditioned on the event

Indeed, applying

\mathbf{s}
.
We will show that it is precisely equal to a uniform distribution over seeds for which applying

yields a full rank
\mathbf{A}
.
To see this, note that for a fixed

\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{2n}

\mathbf{A}
.

.
Then, given a uniformly random

that yields
\mathbf{A}
(this is the converse of the deterministic procedure in

producing

\mathbf{s}

By the procedure of
\mathcal{B}
, every full-rank
\mathbf{A}
has exactly the same number of seeds
\mathbf{s}

\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}_{2n}
, and not the actual values of the first
n
linearly independent columns.

\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{A})
is therefore a uniform distribution over the seeds
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
for which
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime})
is full-rank.
Now, noting that the event
L
that
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{s})
is not full-rank has
\negl(n)
probability, we conclude that there is a negligible fraction of seeds which are not in the preimage of full-rank
\mathbf{A}
, so that the uniform distribution over seeds

has negligible TVD from
\mathbf{s}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{4n^{2}}

∎

Appendix A
Further discussion of
\mathsf{LPN}

For completeness, we here give a formal definition of
\mathsf{LPN}
and give a simple reduction from
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
to
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(k-k^{\prime},n,p\right)
, where

.

Definition A.1

(Decision Learning Parity with Noise,
\mathsf{LPN}
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n\times k}
,
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
, and
\mathbf{e}\sim\mathsf{Ber}(p)^{\otimes n}
, i.e. each
e_{i}
is i.i.d.
1
with probability
p
and
0
with probability
1-p
.
Let
\mathbf{u}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
.

\mathsf{LPN}(k,n,p)
is the task of distinguishing between the two distributional samples

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e})\quad\text{or}\quad(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{u}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n})

(36)

with advantage
1/\poly(n)
.
The former case is called
structured
and the latter
unstructured
.

Lemma A.2

(Reducing a logical bit in
\mathsf{LPN}
)
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
and
k,k^{\prime},n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k>k^{\prime}
.
Let
\mathcal{O}
be an oracle which solves Decision
\mathsf{LPN}(k-k^{\prime},n,p)
with advantage
\delta
.
Then there is an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, running in time
O(\poly(n))
, which solves Decision
\mathsf{LPN}(k,n,p)
with advantage
\delta/2^{k^{\prime}}
using a single call to
\mathcal{O}
.

Proof.

Let the Decision
\mathsf{LPN}(k,n,p)
instance be
(\mathbf{A}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n\times k},\mathbf{u})
.
Define
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n\times(k-k^{\prime})}
by discarding the last
k^{\prime}
columns of
\mathbf{A}
, and define
\mathbf{\bar{A}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n\times k^{\prime}}
to be the last
k^{\prime}
columns of
\mathbf{A}
.
The algorithm will run
\mathcal{O}
on
(\mathbf{A}^{\prime},\mathbf{u})
and output its answer.
Suppose that the
\mathsf{LPN}
instance is structured, so
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{Ax}+\mathbf{e}
for
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
and
\mathbf{e}\sim\mathsf{Ber}(p)^{\otimes n}
.
Let
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{\bar{x}})
with dimensions
k-k^{\prime}
and
k^{\prime}
.
With probability
1/2^{k^{\prime}}
,
\mathbf{\bar{x}}=\mathbf{0}
.
Conditioned on this event,
(\mathbf{A}^{\prime},\mathbf{u})
is precisely a structured instance of
\mathsf{LPN}(k-k^{\prime},n,p)
and
\mathcal{O}
outputs the correct answer.
Otherwise,
\mathbf{\bar{x}}\neq 0
and
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+\mathbf{\bar{A}\bar{x}}+\mathbf{e}
.
But
\mathbf{\bar{A}\bar{x}}
is a uniformly random bitstring since the columns of
\mathbf{\bar{A}}
are uniformly random and
\mathbf{\bar{x}}\neq\mathbf{0}
.
Hence,
\mathbf{u}
is independent from
\mathbf{A}
and marginally
\mathbf{u}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
, i.e.
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{u})
is an unstructured instance.
Let
p_{0}:=\Pr[\mathcal{A}=\text{{structured}}\,|\,\text{{structured}}]
and
p_{1}:=\Pr[\mathcal{A}=\text{{structured}}\,|\,\text{{unstructured}}]
.
Define
q_{0},q_{1}
similarly for
\mathcal{O}
as an oracle solving
\mathsf{LPN}(k-k^{\prime},n,p)
.
Then

\displaystyle p_{0}=\frac{1}{2^{k^{\prime}}}q_{0}+\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{k^{\prime}}}\right)q_{1},\quad p_{1}=q_{1}.

(37)

By definition, the advantage of
\mathcal{A}
is
|p_{0}-p_{1}|
, and by assumption,
|q_{0}-q_{1}|=\delta
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle|p_{0}-p_{1}|=\frac{1}{2^{k^{\prime}}}|q_{0}-q_{1}|=\frac{\delta}{2^{k^{\prime}}}

(38)

as claimed.
∎

This proof implies a reduction from
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
to
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(k-k^{\prime},n,p\right)
for any
k^{\prime}=O(\log n)
logical bits.

Appendix B
One-way function from high-noise
\mathsf{stateLSN}

We here construct a one-way function family (
\mathsf{OWFF}
) such that any algorithm inverting the
\mathsf{OWFF}
implies an algorithm solving
\mathsf{LSN}
.
In fact, this
\mathsf{OWFF}
can be inverted efficiently if and only if there are efficient solvers for both
\mathsf{LSN}
and a Search version of
\mathsf{sympLPN}
(recover the noisy codeword instead of distinguish two distributions) and is therefore potentially more secure than Search
\mathsf{LSN}
itself because no reduction is known in either direction between Search
\mathsf{sympLPN}
and
\mathsf{LSN}
.

Definition B.1

(
\mathsf{OWFF}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{n}:=\set{f_{I}:\mathcal{D}_{I}\to\mathcal{R}_{I}}_{I\in\mathcal{I}_{n}}

from a domain set
\mathcal{D}_{I}
to a range set
\mathcal{R}_{I}
, where
I
is an index drawn from an index set
\mathcal{I}_{n}
which depends on the security parameter
n
.
Then
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is a
one-way function family
(
\mathsf{OWFF}
) if the following conditions hold.

(1)

There exists a classical algorithm
\mathsf{Gen}(1^{n})
, running in time

, which produces an index
I\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
.

(2)

There exists a classical algorithm
\mathsf{Sample}(I)
such that for any
I\in\mathcal{I}_{n}

\mathsf{Sample}(I)
samples a uniformly random element of

in time

(3)

and any

in time

.

(4)

For any (quantum or classical) algorithm
\mathcal{A}
running in time

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathcal{A},I,x}[f_{I}(\mathcal{A}(I,f_{I}(x)))=f_{I}(x)]=\negl(n)

where in the randomness,

and

.

The existence of

is equivalent to the existence of a standard one-way function, which in turn implies pseudorandom generators, pseudorandom functions, and all other primitives of private-key cryptography
[
63
]
.

Construction B.2

(
\mathsf{OWFF}

We instantiate a

candidate based on Search

(

Lemma B.5

Appendix D
Barriers to a reduction from
\mathsf{sympLPN}
to
\mathsf{LPN}

It is known that there exists a reduction from
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(k,n,p\right)
to
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n,p\right)
, for
k=\lfloor pn/6\rfloor

[
65
]
.
This reduction is only meaningful for certain regimes of
p
.

\mathsf{LPN}(k,n,p)
is easy for any
p=O(k^{-1})
, as a
k\times k
sub-block of the encoding matrix has substantial probability of experiencing no error at all, and thus being directly invertible.
Meanwhile, when
p=\Omega(k^{-c})
for
c<1
, there is no known algorithm that runs in polynomial time in
k
.
It follows that the reduction from
\mathsf{LPN}
to
\mathsf{sympLPN}
gives a strong lower bound on the hardness of
\mathsf{sympLPN}
when
p=\Omega\left(n^{-c}\right)
for
c<1/2
.
However, in the regime for our proposed
PKE
and
OT
schemes,
p=\Theta\left(n^{-1/2}\right)
, so the reduction becomes vacuous.
It is therefore open as to whether
\mathsf{sympLPN}
-based schemes inherit security from the hardness of
\mathsf{LPN}
.

A more pressing concern one might raise with low-noise
\mathsf{sympLPN}
-based proposals is, on the other hand, the possibility of a
converse
reduction from
\mathsf{sympLPN}
to
\mathsf{LPN}
with
p=\Theta(1/\sqrt{n})
.
If such a reduction exists, then any low-noise
\mathsf{sympLPN}
-based scheme is no more secure than one based off of low-noise
\mathsf{LPN}
.
We here give show that there are significant barriers to any converse reduction, from
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,p\right)
to
any
parameter-regime of
\mathsf{LPN}
.
Thus, despite their similarities, low-noise
\mathsf{LPN}
and
\mathsf{sympLPN}
currently stand as incomparable post-quantum cryptographic assumptions.
It is unknown as to how the security of the two cryptographic hardness assumptions rigorously compare in theory and in practice.

Given a sample of
\mathsf{sympLPN}
,
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{r})
, where
\mathbf{r}
is either of the form
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}
or
\mathbf{u}
, a natural class of reductions would proceed by preparing

(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\mathbf{r}),

(62)

where
\mathbf{B}
is any random variable taking values in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m\times 2n}
.
If
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{u}
, then
\mathbf{B}\mathbf{r}
is uniformly random (certainly for
m\leq 2n
, depending on the distribution of
\mathbf{B}
for
m>2n
); if
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}
then
\mathbf{B}\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{e}
.
If one could show that
\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}
were statistically close to a uniform distribution, while
\mathbf{B}\mathbf{e}
remained a low-weight error, then this approach would constitute a reduction from
\mathsf{sympLPN}
to
\mathsf{LPN}
.
In order to reduce to an information-theoretically solvable
\mathsf{LPN}
instance, we further require that
m\geq cn
for
c>1
.
(If we allowed
m\leq n
, then one could simply set
\mathbf{B}
to remove the last
(1+\delta)n
rows of
\mathbf{A}
for any
\delta>0
.
Then
\mathbf{BA}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n\times n}
is indeed close to uniformly random, but produces an information-theoretically unsolvable instance of
\mathsf{LPN}
.)

It is not clear at all, a priori, that such a reduction would not exist. A random isotropic code
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times n}
has roughly
\frac{3}{2}n^{2}
bits of entropy, since there are
{n\choose 2}
symplectic orthogonality conditions between the pairs of columns.
Hence, choosing
m=cn
for
1<c<\frac{3}{2}
, it is a priori possible that even for some fixed
\mathbf{B}
,
\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}
could be statistically indistinguishable from a uniform distribution.
However, we demonstrate that the random variable
\mathbf{A}
cannot be randomized in this manner—in fact,
\mathbf{BA}
for any fixed
\mathbf{B}
is severely deficient in entropy.
In what follows, let
H(X)
denote the entropy of a random variable
X
.

Theorem D.1
.

Let
m\geq cn
for
c>1
. Then there exists a constant
d>0
, such that for sufficiently large
n
and any fixed choice of
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m\times 2n}

H(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A})\leq(1-d)mn
.

This is a strong bound because we require
\mathbf{BA}
to be close in total variation distance to a uniformly random
m\times n
matrix in the reduction to
\mathsf{LPN}\!\left(n,m,p^{\prime}\right)
.
To satisfy this closeness,
\mathbf{BA}
must have entropy negligibly close to
mn
.
But by
Theorem

D.1
, any fixed
\mathbf{B}
is off in entropy by at least a constant factor, and thus
\mathbf{B}
itself must be chosen from a distribution random enough to supplement
\Omega(n^{2})
bits of entropy.
Intuitively, when
\mathbf{B}
has that much entropy, it cannot possibly be very sparse, and therefore it should be forced to blow up the error
\mathbf{Be}
in the transformed codeword
(\mathbf{BA})\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{Be}

However, placing this intuition on rigorous grounds to prove the implication requires additional technical subtlety.
Using the entropy deficiency with some additional arguments, we will establish that for any random variable
\mathbf{B}
for which
\mathbf{BA}
is statistically indistinguishable from uniformly random,
\mathbf{Be}
is far from a
\mathsf{Ber}(p)^{\otimes n}
for any
p\leq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\poly(n)}
.
That is, if
\mathbf{B}
can scramble the code enough to match a
\mathsf{LPN}
code instance, then it also scrambles the error past what is even information theoretically decodable.
Therefore, there can be no reduction of this form from
\mathsf{sympLPN}
to
any

\mathsf{LPN}
instance.
This result holds for
\mathsf{sympLPN}
with error rate
p=\omega(1/n)
.
If
p=O(1/n)
, then there is a polynomial-time brute-force error enumeration algorithm to solve
\mathsf{sympLPN}\!\left(n,n,p\right)

Theorem D.2

Let

with
m>cn
for some

Suppose that

\Pr_{\mathbf{B}}\left[\underset{\mathbf{e}\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n}}{\mathbb{E}}[|\mathbf{Be}|]\geq\left(\frac{1-r-\delta}{2}\right)m\right]\geq 1-\negl(n).

In other words, this theorem states that if

has weight lower bounded by
(\frac{1-r-\delta}{2})m

\delta>0

For

Indeed,
\frac{1-r}{2}>H_{2}^{-1}(1-r)

But by Shannon’s noisy coding converse theorem, any error probability

Consequently, the above theorem shows that
\mathbf{Be}
already has weight large enough that further manipulating its noise distribution can only yield Bernoulli noise with probability
p\geq\frac{1-r}{2}>H_{2}^{-1}(1-r)
, at which point the code is no longer decodable.

When
m=\omega(n)
, the result shows that the output error has weight larger than
\frac{1}{2}-\delta
for any constant
\delta
, since
r=o(1)
. This bound is not sufficient to fully rule out decodability.
We believe that it should be possible to do so for any
m=\poly(n)
—however, the primary case of interest for the result was to rule out a reduction where
m=(1+\epsilon)n
, i.e. when the number of rows decreased rather than increasing.
It seems unlikely that reductions that resort to adding many more rows will do a better job of randomizing the code instance without amplifying the error.

In order to prove these theorems, we will begin by introducing new notions in symplectic linear algebra and establishing basic lemmas that will be necessary for the proofs.
First, we define the
radical
of a vector space
V\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
.

Definition D.3
.

For a vector space
V\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
, define the
radical

\operatorname{rad}(V):=V\cap V^{\perp}
, i.e. it is the set of all vectors
\mathbf{v}\in V
such that
\mathbf{v}\odot\mathbf{w}=0
for any
\mathbf{w}\in V
.

For example,
\operatorname{rad}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n})=\{\mathbf{0}\}
, and
\operatorname{rad}(\operatorname{span}(\set{\mathbf{v}}))=\operatorname{span}(\set{\mathbf{v}})
, since
\mathbf{v}
is always symplectically orthogonal to itself.

We will now prove an upper bound on the dimension of
\text{rad}(V)
for a uniformly random
V\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
of a given dimension.
In order to do so, we first establish that a random vector space
V\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
is very unlikely to be isotropic.

Lemma D.4

(Random subspaces are not isotropic)
.

Let
\epsilon>0
be a constant.
If
V\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
is a random subspace of dimension
m\geq\epsilon n
, then there exists a constant
\delta>0
so that

\Pr[V\subseteq V^{\perp}]=2^{-\delta n^{2}}.

(64)

Proof.

Instead of
V
, we consider a random matrix
\mathbf{M}
formed by the following random process: new uniformly random (linearly independent) columns are added to
\mathbf{M}
until it has dimension
m
.
The span of such a random matrix is a uniformly random subspace, since it is obtained by repeatedly adding new random vectors to a given subspace.
However, the chance that the result is isotropic is at most
2^{-\delta_{0}m^{2}}
for some
\delta_{0}>0
.
Indeed, say that
k
columns have been added to
\mathbf{M}
. Then, the chance that the subsequent column lies in the symplectic dual of the previous ones is
\frac{2^{2n-k}-2^{k}}{2^{2n}-2^{k}}
, because the current column span has dimension
k
(none of the vectors in this span can be added) and the symplectic dual has dimension
2n-k
.
Since
m\leq n
for any isotropic vector space, the value of
k
must satisfy
k<n
, and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2n-k}-2^{k}}{2^{2n}-2^{k}}\leq\frac{2^{2n-k}}{2^{2n}-2^{k}}\leq 2\frac{2^{2n-k}}{2^{2n}}=\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}.

(65)

Taking the product of these values from
k=1
to
k=m-1
(the probability is exactly
1
for
k=0
) yields
2^{-\frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{2}}
.
In particular, since
m\geq\epsilon n
, there exists some
\delta
for which
\delta n^{2}\leq\frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{2}
for any
n\geq 1
, and therefore
2^{-\delta n^{2}}\geq 2^{-\frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{2}}
.
∎

We also record a standard result on the number of subspaces
V
satisfying
U\subseteq V\subseteq W
of fixed dimension.

Lemma D.5

(Number of sandwiched subspaces)
.

There exists constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that for any vector spaces
U\subseteq W
over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
of respective dimensions
k
and
m
, the number
N
of vector spaces
V
of dimension
l
for which
U\subseteq V\subseteq W
satisfies

C_{1}2^{(l-k)(m-l)}\leq N\leq C_{2}2^{(l-k)(m-l)}.

(66)

Using the previous two lemmas, we may bound the size of
\operatorname{rad}(V)
for a random subspace
V
of fixed dimension.
Recall that if
W\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
has dimension
t
, then
\dim(W^{\perp})=2n-t
.

Lemma D.6

(Dimension of a random radical)
.

Let
\epsilon>0
and
\alpha>0
be constants. If
V\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
is a random subspace of dimension
m\geq\alpha n
, then there exists a constant
\delta>0
such that

\Pr[\operatorname{dim}(\operatorname{rad}(V))\geq\epsilon n]=2^{-\delta n^{2}}.

(67)

Proof.

Let
k=\lceil\epsilon n\rceil
.
A vector space
V
has radical of dimension at least
\epsilon n
if and only if there is some isotropic subspace
W
with dimension
k
such that
W\subseteq V\subseteq W^{\perp}
.
Indeed, a subspace
W\subseteq V
is contained in the radical of
V
if and only if
V\subseteq W^{\perp}
, i.e. every vector in
W
is orthogonal to every vector in
V
.

The number
N_{k}^{\text{iso}}
of isotropic vector spaces of dimension
k
, by
appendix

D
and
appendix

D
, satisfies
N_{k}^{\text{iso}}\leq C_{2}2^{k(2n-k)-\delta^{\prime}n^{2}}
for some
\delta^{\prime}>0
.
Meanwhile, for some fixed isotropic
W
of dimension
k
, let
M
be the number of vector spaces
V
for which
W\subseteq V\subseteq W^{\perp}
.
By
appendix

D
, since
\dim(W^{\perp})=2n-k
,
M\leq C_{2}2^{(m-k)(2n-m-k)}
.
It follows that the number of vector spaces
V
of dimension
m
with radical of dimension at least
k
has upper bound

MN_{k}^{\text{iso}}\leq C_{2}^{2}2^{k(2n-k)+(m-k)(2n-m-k)-\delta^{\prime}n^{2}}.

(68)

Meanwhile, the total number of vector spaces
N_{m}
of dimension
m
satisfies the lower bound
C_{1}2^{m(2n-m)}\leq N_{m}
.
Hence, the fraction of vector spaces
V
of dimension
m
with radical of dimension at least
k
is at most

\displaystyle\frac{MN_{k}^{\text{iso}}}{N_{m}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{2}^{2}}{C_{1}}2^{k(2n-k)+(m-k)(2n-m-k)-m(2n-m)-\delta^{\prime}n^{2}}

(69)

\displaystyle=D2^{-\delta^{\prime}n^{2}},

(70)

where
D:=\frac{C_{2}^{2}}{C_{1}}
.
This shows that a randomly sampled
V
of dimension
m
has a radical of dimension at least
\epsilon n
with probability at most
D2^{-\delta^{\prime}n^{2}}
. Replacing
\delta^{\prime}
with an appropriate
\delta\geq\delta^{\prime}
, we may remove this constant
D
in the inequality to obtain the desired result.
∎

With these lemmas, we can now prove a technical result that will be instrumental for
Theorem

D.1
.

Lemma D.7

(Symmetrized product of random matrices is nearly full rank)
.

Let
\epsilon>0
be a constant.
Say that
\mathbf{C},\mathbf{B}
are independent, uniformly random
m\times n
matrices, such that
\alpha n\leq m\leq n
for some constant
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
.
Then for sufficiently large
n
, there exists a constant
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle\Pr[\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{C}^{\intercal}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}^{\intercal}\mathbf{C})\leq(1-\epsilon)n]\leq 2^{-\delta n^{2}}.

(71)

Proof.

Express

\mathbf{C}^{\intercal}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}^{\intercal}\mathbf{C}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{C}^{\intercal}&\mathbf{B}^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbf{I}\\
\mathbf{I}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{C}\\
\mathbf{B}\end{bmatrix}

(72)

We first may note that
\mathbf{T}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{C}\\
\mathbf{B}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2m\times n}
is uniformly random, and therefore since
2m\geq n
it has rank at least
\left(1-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)n
with probability
1-2^{-\delta_{0}n^{2}}
for some
\delta_{0}>0
.
To see this, note that in order for
\mathbf{T}
to have rank less than
(1-\frac{\epsilon}{2})n
, then there must be at least
\frac{\epsilon n}{2}
columns that are contained in the span of the previous columns (whose rank is bounded by
(1-\frac{\epsilon}{2})n
).
Each of these
\frac{\epsilon n}{2}
occurrences happens with probability at most
2^{-\epsilon n/2}
, implying that the rank can be less than
(1-\frac{\epsilon}{2})n
with probability at most
(2^{-\epsilon n/2})^{\epsilon n/2}=2^{-\epsilon^{2}n^{2}/4}
.
Hence, setting
\delta_{0}=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4}
yields the desired bound.

Therefore, with this probability,
W:=\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{T})\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2m_{0}}
has dimension at least
\left(1-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)n
.
Let
t=\dim(W)
, and note that
W
is a uniformly random subspace with dimension
t
.
We must now bound the dimension of
\operatorname{rad}(W)
, because this dimension constrains the rank of
\mathbf{C}^{\intercal}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}^{\intercal}\mathbf{C}
.
Indeed, say that some vector
\mathbf{u}
satisfies
(\mathbf{C}^{\intercal}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}^{\intercal}\mathbf{C})\mathbf{u}=0
.
Then, by Eqn. (
72
) this is equivalent to
(\mathbf{T}\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\odot(\mathbf{T}\mathbf{u})=0
for any
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
, i.e. that
\mathbf{T}\mathbf{u}\in\operatorname{rad}(W)
.
It follows that with probability at least
1-2^{-\delta_{0}n^{2}}
,

\displaystyle\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\mathbf{C}^{\intercal}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}^{\intercal}\mathbf{C}))

\displaystyle=\text{dim}(\text{rad}(W))+\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\mathbf{T}))

(73)

\displaystyle\leq\text{dim}(\text{rad}(W))+\frac{\epsilon}{2}n,

(74)

by the rank-nullity theorem.
By
Appendix

D
, there exists some
\delta_{1}
for which the probability that
\dim(\operatorname{rad}(V))\geq\frac{\epsilon}{2}n
is at most
2^{-\delta_{1}n^{2}}
.
Choosing
\delta<\min(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
, we have that
2^{-\delta_{0}n^{2}}+2^{-\delta_{1}n^{2}}\leq 2^{-\delta n^{2}}
for sufficiently large
n
as claimed.
∎

With the tools in place, we may now prove
Theorem

D.1
.
Before we give the proof, we recall the existence of a standard, useful basis for any subspace
V\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
.
The standard symplectic basis
\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{n},\mathbf{f}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{f}_{n}
of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
has convenient orthogonality relations:
\mathbf{e}_{i}\odot\mathbf{e}_{j}=0
,
\mathbf{f}_{i}\odot\mathbf{f}_{j}=0
, and
\mathbf{e}_{i}\odot\mathbf{f}_{j}=\delta_{ij}
.
For a general
V
, there is a similar convenient basis.

Lemma D.8

(Symplectic basis of a subspace)
.

Let
V\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
be a subspace.
There exists a basis
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{k},\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{l},\mathbf{w}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{w}_{l}
such that

(1)

\mathbf{u}_{i}\in V^{\perp}
for
i=1,\dots,k
.

(2)

\mathbf{v}_{i}\odot\mathbf{v}_{j}=0
for
i,j=1,\dots,l
.

(3)

\mathbf{w}_{i}\odot\mathbf{w}_{j}=0
for
i,j=1,\dots,l
.

(4)

\mathbf{v}_{i}\odot\mathbf{w}_{j}=\delta_{ij}
for
i,j=1,\dots,l
.

This basis is almost precisely analogous to
(\mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{f}_{i})
in the case of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
—the
\mathbf{v}_{i}
correspond to the
\mathbf{e}_{i}
, while the
\mathbf{w}_{i}
correspond to the
\mathbf{f}_{i}
.
The additional vectors
\mathbf{u}_{i}
give a basis for
\text{rad}(V)
.

Proof of
Theorem

D.1
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the set of
2n\times n
isotropic matrices, and let
F_{\mathbf{B}}=\{\mathbf{BA}\,|\,\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{S}\}
.
We will start by showing that for some constant
d^{\prime}>0
, for any
\mathbf{B}
,
|F_{\mathbf{B}}|\leq 2^{mn-d^{\prime}n^{2}}
.
This implies that
H(\mathbf{BA})\leq mn-d^{\prime}n^{2}
, and with a short additional argument we will conclude that
H(\mathbf{BA})\leq(1-d)mn
for some
d>0
.

First, note that
|F_{\mathbf{B}}|=|F_{\mathbf{TBS}}|
, where
\mathbf{T}
is any invertible
m\times m
matrix, and
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n\times 2n}
is any matrix that preserves the symplectic inner product.
Indeed, consider the map that takes
\mathbf{M}\in F_{\mathbf{B}}
to
\mathbf{TM}
.
Writing
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{BA}
,
\mathbf{TM}=(\mathbf{TBS})(\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{A})
, and hence
\mathbf{TM}\in F_{\mathbf{TBS}}
because
\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{A}
is an isotropic matrix.
Likewise, the inverse map
\mathbf{M}\mapsto\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{M}
for
\mathbf{M}=(\mathbf{TBS})\mathbf{A}
also places
\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{M}\in F_{\mathbf{B}}
.
Thus,
|F_{\mathbf{B}}|=|F_{\mathbf{TBS}}|
.

Let
W:=\ker(\mathbf{B})\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
.
By
Appendix

D
,
W
has a basis of the form
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{k},\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{l},\mathbf{w}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{w}_{l}
, where
\mathbf{v}_{i}\odot\mathbf{w}_{j}=\delta_{ij}
,
\mathbf{v}_{i}\odot\mathbf{v}_{j}=0
,
\mathbf{w}_{i}\odot\mathbf{w}_{j}=0
, and
\mathbf{u}_{i}\in W^{\perp}
.
Let
\mathbf{S}
be a symplectic matrix which sends the standard symplectic basis vectors
\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{l+k},\mathbf{f}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{f}_{l}
to
\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{l},\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{k},\mathbf{w}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{w}_{l}
.
Then,
\ker({\mathbf{BS}})
is spanned by
\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{l+k},\mathbf{f}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{f}_{l}
, while
\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{BS})
is spanned by the images of
\mathbf{e}_{l+k+1},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{n},\mathbf{f}_{l+1},\dots,\mathbf{f}_{n}
.
By choosing an appropriate invertible
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m}
, we can construct a
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{TBS}
which is a projector onto this latter set of coordinates. As a consequence,
|F_{\mathbf{B}}|=|F_{\mathbf{P}}|
.

It therefore suffices to prove that for any projection
\mathbf{P}
,
|F_{\mathbf{P}}|\leq 2^{mn-d^{\prime}n^{2}}
.
Say that
\mathbf{P}
is a projection onto the coordinates
\mathbf{e}_{l+k+1},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{n},\mathbf{f}_{l+1},\dots,\mathbf{f}_{n}
.
In particular,
m=(n-l)+(n-l-k)=2n-2l-k
.
By our bound on
m
, it follows that
2n-2l-k\geq cn
, i.e.

\displaystyle 2l+k\leq(2-c)n.

(75)

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a uniformly random element of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m\times n}
.
We will consider the conditions under which there exists
\mathbf{N}\in\mathcal{S}
for which
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{PN}
.
We may decompose
\mathbf{N}
as

\mathbf{N}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{B}_{1}\\
\mathbf{B}_{2}\\
\mathbf{B}_{3}\\
\mathbf{C}_{1}\\
\mathbf{C}_{2}\\
\mathbf{C}_{3},\end{bmatrix}

(76)

where
\mathbf{B}_{1},\mathbf{C}_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{l\times n}
,
\mathbf{B}_{2},\mathbf{C}_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k\times n}
, and
\mathbf{B}_{3},\mathbf{C}_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{(n-l-k)\times n}
.
The only condition on
\mathbf{N}
is that its columns are symplectically orthogonal, which translates to the requirement that
\mathbf{D}:=\mathbf{C}_{1}^{\intercal}\mathbf{B}_{1}+\mathbf{C}_{2}^{\intercal}\mathbf{B}_{2}+\mathbf{C}_{3}^{\intercal}\mathbf{B}_{3}
is symmetric.
Then,

\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{PN}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{B}_{3}\\
\mathbf{C}_{2}\\
\mathbf{C}_{3}\end{bmatrix}.

(77)

Hence,
\mathbf{M}\in F_{\mathbf{P}}
if and only if there is some choice of
\mathbf{B}_{1},\mathbf{B}_{2},\mathbf{C}_{1}
for which
\mathbf{D}
is symmetric.
Let
\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n\times n}
be an invertible matrix chosen so that
\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\mathbf{C}_{2}^{\intercal})
is contained in the last
k
coordinate vectors.
This is possible, since
\mathbf{C}_{2}^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n\times k}
has rank at most
k
.
Then, define
\mathbf{D}^{\prime}:=\mathbf{R^{\intercal}DR}=\mathbf{C}_{1}^{\prime\intercal}\mathbf{B}_{1}^{\prime}+\mathbf{C}_{2}^{\prime\intercal}\mathbf{B}_{2}^{\prime}+\mathbf{C}_{3}^{\prime\intercal}\mathbf{B}_{3}^{\prime}
, where
\mathbf{C}_{i}^{\prime}=\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{B}_{i}^{\prime}=\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{R}
.

\mathbf{D}^{\prime}
is symmetric if and only if
\mathbf{D}
is symmetric, and so we need to select
\mathbf{B}_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{B}_{2}^{\prime}
, and
\mathbf{C}_{1}^{\prime}
for which
\mathbf{D}^{\prime}
is symmetric.

By construction of
\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}
,
\mathbf{C}_{2}^{\prime\intercal}\mathbf{B}_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n\times n}
is only nonzero in the last
k
rows, since
\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{C}_{2}^{\prime\intercal}\mathbf{B}_{2}^{\prime})\subseteq\operatorname{im}(\mathbf{R}^{\intercal})
.
It immediately follows that the top
(n-k)\times(n-k)
block of
\mathbf{C}_{1}^{\prime\intercal}\mathbf{B}_{1}^{\prime}+\mathbf{C}_{3}^{\prime\intercal}\mathbf{B}_{3}^{\prime}
must be symmetric.
Equivalently, defining
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}
to be the first
n-k
columns of
\mathbf{C}_{i}^{\prime}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{i}
as the first
n-k
columns of
\mathbf{B}_{i}^{\prime}
,

(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{1}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{1}+\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{1}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{1})+(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{3}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{3}+\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{3}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{3})=\mathbf{0}.

(78)

Notice that for arbitrary
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{1},\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{1}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{1}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{1}+\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{1}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
is the sum of two matrices with rank
l
, and hence must have rank at most
2l
.

Meanwhile, since
\mathbf{M}
is uniformly random,
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{3}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{3}
are uniformly random as well.
Then we claim that
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{3},\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{(n-l-k)\times(n-k)}
satisfy the conditions of
Appendix

D
.
The relevant condition to establish is that

\alpha(n-k)\leq n-l-k\leq(n-k),

(79)

for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
The upper bound on
n-l-k
is immediate.
Meanwhile, by Eqn. (
75
), we write that
l\leq\frac{(2-c)n-k}{2}\leq\frac{(2-c)(n-k)}{2}
, since
2-c\leq 1
.
Thus,

\displaystyle n-l-k

\displaystyle\geq(n-k)-(n-k)\frac{(2-c)}{2}=\left(\frac{c}{2}\right)(n-k).

(80)

By assumption,
c>1
, which gives the desired lower bound with
\alpha:=\frac{c}{2}
.

Now, applying the lemma with
\epsilon:=c-1
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{3}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{3}+\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{3}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{3}
has rank strictly larger than
(1-\epsilon)(n-k)=(2-c)(n-k)
with probability
1-2^{-\delta(n-k)^{2}}
, for some
\delta>0
.
Using Eqn. (
75
), we see that
k\leq(2-c)n
or
n-k\geq(c-1)n
, implying that
2^{-\delta(n-k)^{2}}\leq 2^{-\delta(c-1)^{2}n^{2}}
.
Hence this rank bound holds with probability at least
1-2^{-\delta(c-1)^{2}n^{2}}
.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle(2-c)(n-k)

\displaystyle>(2-c)n-k\geq 2l.

(81)

Consequently, the rank of

\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{1}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{1}+\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{1}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{1},

(82)

for
any
choice of
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{1},\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{1}
(which is
\leq 2l
) is strictly less than that of

\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{3}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{3}+\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{3}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{3}

(83)

with probability
1-2^{-\delta(c-1)^{2}n^{2}}
.
Conditioned on this event,

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{1}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{1}+\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{1}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{1})+(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{3}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{3}+\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{3}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{3})\neq\mathbf{0},

(84)

since the first term must have the same rank of the second term to cancel it out.
By the previous argument, therefore,
\mathbf{M}\notin F_{\mathbf{P}}
.
Now, set
d^{\prime}=\delta(c-1)^{2}
.
Then at most a
2^{-d^{\prime}n^{2}}
fraction of matrices
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m\times n}
lie in

.
Thus,
|F_{\mathbf{P}}|\leq 2^{mn-d^{\prime}n^{2}}
.
It follows that
H(\mathbf{BA})\leq mn-d^{\prime}n^{2}
.

To complete the proof, we observe that
H(\mathbf{A})\leq 2n^{2}
, so by the data processing inequality,
H(\mathbf{BA})\leq 2n^{2}
.
Then, the bound

\displaystyle H(\mathbf{BA})\leq\left(1-\frac{\min(d^{\prime},1)}{3}\right)mn

(85)

always holds, since when
m\leq 3n
,

and when
m\geq 3n
,

.
Setting

completes the proof.
∎

Before proving
Theorem

D.2
, we record a simple result about the distribution of
\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{e}
, where
\mathbf{b}
is any fixed vector and
\mathbf{e}\sim\mathcal{D}^{\otimes n}_{p}
.

Lemma D.9
.

Let
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
be a fixed vector and
\mathbf{e}\sim\mathcal{D}^{\otimes n}_{p}
for
p\leq\frac{3}{4}
.
Then
\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{e}\sim\mathsf{Ber}(q)
, where
\eta(|\mathbf{b}|,p)\leq q\leq\frac{1}{2}
for

\eta(w,p)=\frac{1-\left(1-\frac{4}{3}p\right)^{\frac{w}{2}}}{2}.

(86)

Note that
\eta(w,p)
is non-negative, and is increasing in both
w
and
p
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{e}\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{e}\;=\;\sum_{k\in T}e_{k},

(87)

where
T
is the set of indices where
\mathbf{b}
is nonzero. For any
k\in[n]
, let

\displaystyle e^{\prime}_{k}\;=\;b_{k}e_{k}\;+\;b_{n+k}e_{n+k}.

(88)

By direct calculation,

\displaystyle e_{k}^{\prime}\sim\begin{cases}\mathsf{Ber}(0)&k,n+k\notin T,\\
\mathsf{Ber}(\frac{2}{3}p)&\text{else}.\end{cases}

(89)

For
S=\{\,k\leq n\mid k\in T\text{ or }k+n\in T\,\}
, we may rewrite

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in T}e_{k}=\sum_{k\in S}e^{\prime}_{k}.

(90)

These
e^{\prime}_{k}
are independent Bernoulli random variables with probability
q=\tfrac{2}{3}p
, and note that

\displaystyle|S|\;\geq\;\frac{|\mathbf{b}|}{2}.

(91)

Using the fact that the probability that a
\textsf{Binomial}(m,q)
random variable is odd with probability
\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}(1-2q)^{m}
,

\Pr\left[\sum_{k\in S}e_{k}^{\prime}=1\right]=\frac{1-\left(1-\frac{4}{3}p\right)^{|S|}}{2},

(92)

which satisfies

\frac{1-\left(1-\frac{4}{3}p\right)^{|\mathbf{b}|/2}}{2}\leq\frac{1-\left(1-\frac{4}{3}p\right)^{|S|}}{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}

(93)

as desired.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

D.2
.

Let

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbf{B}_{0}):=\underset{{\mathbf{e}\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n}}}{\mathbb{E}}[|\mathbf{B}_{0}\mathbf{e}|],

(94)

for any
\mathbf{B}_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m\times 2n}
.
Let
\mathbf{B}
be any random variable such that
\mathbf{BA}
has negligible total variation distance from a uniformly random matrix.
Recalling that
r:=\frac{n}{m}
, let
A
be the event that
\mu(\mathbf{B})<(\frac{1-r-\delta}{2})m
.
We will show that
\Pr[A]=\negl(n)
.

We claim that it suffices to show that

\displaystyle H(\mathbf{BA}|A)=mn-\Omega(mn).

(95)

Suppose for contradiction that
\Pr[A]=\frac{1}{\poly(n)}
but Eqn. (
95
) holds.
Then we may describe the probability distribution
h:\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m\times n}\to[0,1]
of
\mathbf{BA}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m\times n}
, as

h=pf+(1-p)g,

(96)

where
p:=\Pr[A]=\frac{1}{\poly(n)}
and
H(f)=mn-\Omega(mn)
.
From this decomposition, there is a resulting bound on entropy of

\displaystyle H(h)

\displaystyle\leq pH(f)+(1-p)H(g)+H_{2}(p)

(97)

\displaystyle\leq p(mn-\Omega(mn))+(1-p)mn+O(p\log(1/p))

(98)

\displaystyle=mn-\Omega(pmn)+O(p\log(n))

(99)

\displaystyle=mn-\frac{1}{\poly(n)},

(100)

where in the second inequality we use the fact that
H_{2}(p)=O(p\log(1/p))
for
p\leq\frac{1}{2}
(which is without loss of generality).

However,
h
is the probability distribution of
\mathbf{BA}
, and if two probability distributions have negligible total variation distance, the difference in entropy is negligible.
Since
h
has negligible distance from uniform by assumption, and the entropy of the uniform distribution is
mn
, this is a contradiction.

We will now bound the entropy
H(\mathbf{BA}|A)
. Define a random variable
\mathbf{\Pi}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m\times m}
, which is the permutation matrix such that the rows of
\mathbf{B^{\prime}}:=\mathbf{\Pi B}
are sorted in descending order by weight.
Note that
H(\mathbf{\Pi BA|A,\Pi})=H(\mathbf{BA|A,\Pi})
since
\mathbf{\Pi}
is a permutation, so that

\displaystyle H(\mathbf{\Pi BA}|A)

\displaystyle\geq H(\mathbf{\Pi BA}|A,\mathbf{\Pi})=H(\mathbf{BA}|A,\mathbf{\Pi})

(101)

\displaystyle\geq H(\mathbf{BA}|A)-H(\mathbf{\Pi}|A).

(102)

Thus,

\displaystyle H(\mathbf{BA}|A)

\displaystyle\leq H(\mathbf{\Pi BA}|A)+H(\mathbf{\Pi}|A)

(103)

\displaystyle\leq H(\mathbf{B^{\prime}A}|A)+m\log(m).

(104)

Since
m=\poly(n)
,
m\log(m)=o(mn)
, it therefore suffices to show that
H(\mathbf{B^{\prime}A}|A)=mn-\Omega(mn)
.

Let
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}_{i}
denote the
i
th row of
\mathbf{B^{\prime}}
.
Then, by linearity of expectation and
Appendix

D
,

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbf{B}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\underset{\mathbf{e}\sim\mathcal{D}_{p}^{\otimes n}}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{b}^{\prime}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{e}]\geq\sum_{i}\eta(|\mathbf{b}^{\prime}_{i}|,p)

(105)

where we recall that
\eta(w,p)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{4}{3}p\right)^{\frac{w}{2}}
.
Because
\mathbf{B^{\prime}e}
and
\mathbf{Be}
have exactly the same weight,

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbf{B})

\displaystyle=\mu(\mathbf{B^{\prime}}).

(106)

Hence, the event
A
is also the event that
\mu(\mathbf{B^{\prime}})<(\frac{1-r-\delta}{2})m
.
The use of
\mathbf{\Pi}
to sort
\mathbf{B}
into
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}
is helpful because it will allow us to split the entropy analysis into two cases: for the first few rows of
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}
, the entropy is large and we will not be able to give a non-trivial bound, but for all remaining rows, we will show that their weight is relatively small, and thus have a much lower entropy.
Define this “cutoff” row to be
k=\lceil m(1-r-\frac{\delta}{2})\rceil
.
We claim that for any
d_{0}>0
, for sufficiently large
n
,
\Pr_{\mathbf{B}}[|\mathbf{b}^{\prime}_{k}|\geq d_{0}n\,|\,A]=0
.
This is because if
|\mathbf{b}^{\prime}_{k}|\geq d_{0}n
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\eta(|\mathbf{b}^{\prime}_{i}|,p)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta(\mathbf{b}^{\prime}_{k},p)\geq k\eta(d_{0}n,p)

(107)

\displaystyle=k\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\left(1-\omega\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)^{d_{0}n}}{2}\right)=\frac{k}{2}-o(k).

(108)

Note that
\frac{k}{2}>\left(\frac{1-r}{2}-\frac{\delta}{4}\right)m
, so this immediately implies that for sufficiently large
n
,
\mu(\mathbf{B})\geq\frac{k}{2}-o(k)>\left(\frac{1-r-\delta}{2}\right)m
, contradicting the conditioned event
A
.
Hence, for sufficiently large
n
,
|\mathbf{b}^{\prime}_{k}|<d_{0}n
, and by construction
|\mathbf{b}^{\prime}_{j}|<d_{0}n
for all
j\geq k
.
Now, we consider the
m-k
bottom rows, which all satisfy this weight bound.

\displaystyle m-k

\displaystyle\geq m\left(r+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)-1\gtrsim m\left(r+\frac{\delta}{4}\right)\geq n\left(1+\frac{c\delta}{4}\right),

(109)

where
\gtrsim
indicates that inequality holds for sufficiently large
n
.
If
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}_{1}
denotes the first
k
rows of
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}_{2}
denotes the remaining
m-k
rows, then by
theorem

D.1
,

\displaystyle H(\mathbf{B}^{\prime}_{2}\mathbf{A}|A)

\displaystyle\leq H((\mathbf{B}^{\prime}_{2},\mathbf{B}^{\prime}_{2}\mathbf{A})|A)=H(\mathbf{B}_{2}^{\prime}\mathbf{A}|\mathbf{B}_{2}^{\prime})+H(\mathbf{B}_{2}^{\prime}|A)

(110)

\displaystyle\leq(m-k)n-d(m-k)n+H(\mathbf{B}_{2}^{\prime}|A)

(111)

\displaystyle\leq(m-k)n-d\left(r+\frac{\delta}{4}\right)mn+H(\mathbf{B}_{2}^{\prime}|A)

(112)

for some
d>0
, that depends on the constant
\frac{c\delta}{4}
.

Each row of
\mathbf{B}_{2}^{\prime}
has weight at most
d_{0}n
.
Thus, the entropy of each row is at most the entropy of a uniformly random length-
2n
bitstring of weight
d_{0}n
, which is
\log\binom{2n}{d_{0}n}\leq nH_{2}(d_{0}/2)
; the entropy overall of
\mathbf{B}_{2}^{\prime}
is at most the entropy of the sum of rows, so

\displaystyle H(\mathbf{B}_{2}^{\prime}|A)\leq(m-k)\log\binom{2n}{d_{0}n}\leq m\log\binom{2n}{d_{0}n}\leq mnH_{2}\left(\frac{d_{0}}{2}\right).

(113)

Choose
d_{0}
sufficiently small so that
H_{2}(d_{0}/2)\leq d^{\prime}/2
, where
d^{\prime}:=d(r+\delta/2)
.
Then

H(\mathbf{B}_{2}^{\prime}|A)\leq\frac{d^{\prime}}{2}mn.

(114)

As a consequence, we obtain that
H(\mathbf{B}^{\prime}_{2}\mathbf{A}|A)\leq(m-k)n-\frac{d^{\prime}}{2}mn
.
Meanwhile, by a trivial dimension bound,
H(\mathbf{B}^{\prime}_{1}\mathbf{A}|A)\leq kn
.
Together, these bounds imply that

\displaystyle H(\mathbf{B^{\prime}A}|A)

\displaystyle\leq H(\mathbf{B}^{\prime}_{1}\mathbf{A}|A)+H(\mathbf{B}^{\prime}_{2}\mathbf{A}|A)

(115)

\displaystyle\leq mn-\frac{d^{\prime}}{2}mn.

(116)

Hence, as desired, we have that
H(\mathbf{B^{\prime}A}|A)=mn-\Omega(mn)
, and as a result
\Pr[A]=\negl(n)
.
∎
