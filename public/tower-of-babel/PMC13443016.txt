Mechanical Shunt Catheter Injury From Repeated Insulin Injections in a Diabetic Patient
Authors: Woo Sel Kim, Yu Shik Shim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMechanical failure of a ventriculoperitoneal shunt catheter due to repeated external trauma is an extremely rare complication. We report a 72-year-old male with type 2 diabetes who developed a purely subcutaneous fluid collection along the catheter tract after 4 years of habitual subcutaneous insulin injections near the shunt path. Notably, despite self-administering insulin daily, the patient was completely unaware of the necessity to avoid the catheter pathway, highlighting a critical gap in routine post-operative counseling. He presented with localized abdominal swelling and erythema without systemic infection. Abdominal computed tomography demonstrated a kinked catheter within a subcutaneous fluid collection, whereas the deeper peritoneal course and distal tip remained completely intact. The distal catheter was externalized, and at revision surgery, the fluid collection was completely excised. The shunt system was replaced using an alternative upper abdominal midline approach via the linea alba, remote from the prior injection zone. This case underscores 2 vital lessons: first, proactive patient education regarding injection site rotation away from implanted hardware is essential; second, in patients requiring lifelong daily abdominal injections, neurosurgeons should prioritize alternative entry sites like the linea alba to ensure long-term shunt integrity.Keywords: Needlestick injuries, Ventriculoperitoneal shunt, Patient education as topic, Abdominal wall

INTRODUCTION

The ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt is the standard treatment for hydrocephalus and remains one of the most frequently performed neurosurgical procedures worldwide. Despite its widespread use, shunt failure occurs in 40%–50% of patients within the first 2 years after surgery, and abdominal complications account for approximately 5%–47% of all VP shunt-related complications.3) Distal shunt complications traditionally involve intra-abdominal pathology, with a reported incidence ranging from 0.25% to 10% across published series. These intra-abdominal complications most commonly present with localized abdominal symptoms such as pain, distension, and a palpable mass, often without prominent neurological deterioration. Recognized predisposing factors include prior abdominal surgery, multiple shunt revisions, low-grade infection, elevated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) protein, and sterile inflammatory responses to shunt components.2,4)

In contrast, complications confined strictly to the superficial subcutaneous plane along the catheter tract—without any peritoneal involvement—are exceedingly rare. While historical literatures have noted subcutaneous fluid accumulations that propagated from pre-existing intraperitoneal pathology through a weakened abdominal wall or involved communicating peritoneal–subcutaneous compartments, a purely isolated subcutaneous fluid collection restricted to the tract due to repetitive local external trauma has been poorly documented. Repeated subcutaneous injections administered in proximity to implanted medical devices are known to cause chronic local tissue injury, including progressive fibrosis and structural disruption of the subcutaneous layer.7) However, the possibility that repetitive needle trauma near a VP shunt catheter could contribute to direct mechanical catheter injury, structural deformation, and localized subcutaneous fluid accumulation has not been adequately addressed.

Furthermore, while neurosurgeons focus heavily on the surgical and technical aspects of shunt placement, post-operative education regarding everyday self-care for patients with chronic comorbidities is often overlooked. We report a case of a localized subcutaneous fluid collection developing approximately 4 years after VP shunt placement in a patient with diabetes mellitus, caused by repeated subcutaneous insulin injections near the distal catheter insertion site. We discuss the critical role of structured patient education and the necessity of customizing surgical planning for patients requiring lifelong daily abdominal injections.

CASE REPORT

A 72-year-old man presented with a one-month history of localized swelling, erythema, and tenderness at the abdominal insertion site of his VP shunt distal catheter. His medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus managed with an insulin pump since 2000, early-stage gastric carcinoma treated with subtotal gastrectomy (Billroth I reconstruction, 2008), and aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) caused by a ruptured left internal carotid artery anterior choroidal segment aneurysm in 2020.

Following the SAH, the patient underwent emergency left pterional craniotomy with aneurysm wrapping. Posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus was subsequently managed with navigation-assisted VP shunt placement in February 2021 (STRATA No Siphon Control [NSC] programmable valve; right parieto-occipital burr hole; peritoneal entry at McBurney’s point; 20 cm distal catheter). The initial valve pressure setting was selected at Performance Level (PL) 1.5. The procedure was uneventful, and the patient was followed by the endocrinology service for insulin pump management thereafter.

Over approximately 4 years following shunt placement, the patient self-administered repeated subcutaneous insulin injections in the right abdominal wall, inadvertently in close proximity to the subcutaneous course of the distal shunt catheter. Neither the patient nor his caregivers had received counselling regarding the location of the shunt tract or the importance of avoiding subcutaneous injections in its vicinity.

Four years after shunt insertion, at a neurosurgical outpatient visit in December 2025, physical examination revealed a well-defined area of induration, erythema, and tenderness measuring approximately 3×4 cm at the abdominal catheter insertion site (FIGURE 1). There was no fever, no signs of peritoneal irritation, and no neurological deterioration. Shunt function was clinically intact.

This site corresponds precisely to the anatomical location where the patient had habitually self-administered subcutaneous insulin injections over the preceding 4 years.

Non-contrast abdominal computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a kinked configuration of the distal shunt catheter at the insertion site, with a well-defined loculated fluid collection (approximately 3×4×2 cm) confined entirely to the subcutaneous layer along the catheter tract (FIGURE 2A). Subsequent contrast-enhanced CT confirmed these findings on sagittal and coronal reconstructions. Crucially, the CT scan clearly delineated that the distal catheter successfully traversed the abdominal wall into the peritoneal cavity without any structural failure in the deeper planes, and the final distal tip remained completely intact within a normal peritoneal cavity free of loculation or effusion (FIGURE 2B). Brain CT showed no ventriculomegaly.

(B) Coronal reconstruction view clearly demonstrating the anatomical relationship of the shunt. The distal segment within the peritoneal cavity and its final distal tip (arrow) show a completely normal configuration, free of any intraperitoneal fluid collection or associated pathology.

Percutaneous aspiration yielded approximately 4 mL of clear, non-purulent fluid. Gram stain of the aspirate revealed no white blood cells and no bacteria. Laboratory workup demonstrated completely unremarkable systemic inflammatory markers: C-reactive protein was 0.22 mg/dL (reference <0.3 mg/dL), erythrocyte sedimentation rate was 34 mm/hr (mildly elevated), and white blood cell count was 8.78×103/μL (within normal range). Microbiological cultures of both the fluid aspirate and the subsequently externalized shunt tip grew Staphylococcus epidermidis in minimal quantities, detected exclusively in the enrichment broth (Thioglycollate medium) after prolonged incubation, consistent with low-level skin flora contamination of the disrupted catheter surface rather than a frank, deep shunt infection. Blood cultures yielded no growth throughout the hospitalization.8)

The patient was admitted, and the distal catheter was externalized at the cervical level at the bedside under aseptic conditions, enabling external CSF drainage and continuous microbiological surveillance. Intravenous cefazolin was commenced empirically and subsequently transitioned to nafcillin (2 g per 4 hours) following infectious disease consultation, based on the confirmed methicillin-susceptible coagulase-negative staphylococcal isolate. CSF drainage from the externalized catheter remained clear throughout.

After 10 days of systemic antibiotic therapy with complete resolution of local inflammatory signs, VP shunt revision was performed in conjunction with the general surgery service. Intraoperatively, the fluid collection and surrounding reactive tissue were found to be entirely confined to the subcutaneous plane along the catheter tract, with no peritoneal involvement. The localized subcutaneous swelling and surrounding inflammatory tissue were completely excised. Both the distal catheter and valve were replaced. To prevent re-injury and ensure maximum safety, the new distal catheter was inserted through a separate upper abdominal midline incision at the linea alba and advanced into the peritoneal cavity to a depth of 20 cm, keeping the STRATA NSC valve pressure setting at PL 1.5. The patient completed a total of 22 days of targeted intravenous antibiotic therapy and was discharged on oral cefdinir. At outpatient follow-up 6 weeks after surgery, the wound was fully healed, local inflammatory signs had completely resolved, and there was no evidence of pseudocyst recurrence.

DISCUSSION

This case highlights a unique and entirely preventable mechanism of mechanical shunt failure: localized subcutaneous fluid collection along a VP shunt tract caused by repeated needle trauma from daily insulin injections. The complete absence of deep-tissue or intraperitoneal extension—as objectively demonstrated by the intact intraperitoneal course and distal tip of the catheter on abdominal CT—substantiates that the mechanical insult was restricted exclusively to the superficial subcutaneous plane where the repetitive needle trauma occurred. It is worth noting that distal shunt malfunctions can manifest through various atypical clinical spectra and secondary soft tissue pathologies surrounding the catheter pathway, even in the absence of complete internal device occlusion.1,5,6,10,11) Rather than focusing on controversial biochemical or nosological definitions of the localized fluid, the primary significance of this case lies in its vital educational and tactical lessons for both patient management and neurosurgical planning.2,9)

The first and most striking finding in this case is the total lack of patient awareness regarding basic shunt care. Despite self-administering multiple subcutaneous insulin injections every single day for 4 years, the user was entirely ignorant of the fact that he was routinely inserting a needle adjacent to—and likely directly into—his implanted shunt catheter. This highlights a critical, systemic gap in post-operative counseling. Neurosurgeons frequently focus extensively on acute post-operative surgical risks but often fail to provide long-term, practical lifestyle education for patients with chronic medical comorbidities like diabetes. For patients requiring habitual subcutaneous drug delivery (such as insulin, low-molecular-weight heparin, or GLP-1 agonists), proactive, structured education must be mandatory before hospital discharge. The precise subcutaneous pathway of the shunt should be clearly demonstrated to the patient and their caregivers, and explicit instructions to rotate injection sites completely away from the hardware must be provided. Routine clinical follow-up should also include inspection of the injection sites to actively prevent accidental needle-related hardware damage.2,7)

The second major clinical lesson involves the need for flexibility in selecting the peritoneal entry site during surgical planning. The standard abdominal entry point for a VP shunt is typically McBurney’s point in the right lower quadrant. However, the lower quadrants of the abdomen happen to be the primary, most habitual sites chosen by diabetic patients for subcutaneous insulin injections due to ease of access and optimal fat thickness. Placing a shunt catheter directly through a patient’s habitual injection zone creates an ongoing, lifelong hazard for accidental mechanical hardware damage.2,9)

Therefore, when planning a VP shunt for a patient with diabetes or any condition requiring chronic abdominal injections, neurosurgeons should actively deviate from standard entry points and employ a customized surgical approach. Specifically, an upper abdominal midline approach via the linea alba should be strongly considered as an optimal alternative. Utilizing the linea alba provides several distinct clinical and technical advantages from the operator’s perspective. First and foremost, this approach offers anatomical isolation from habitual injection zones, as the upper midline area over the linea alba is rarely utilized by patients for daily insulin delivery, thereby effectively safeguarding the shunt catheter from potential needle trauma and subsequent superficial mechanical injury. Furthermore, entering through the relatively fixed, avascular, and rigid fascial structure of the linea alba minimizes the rotational and shearing forces exerted on the catheter by the active abdominal wall musculature during daily patient movement, which significantly reduces the long-term risk of catheter displacement, kinking, or mechanical flow obstruction at the abdominal entry site. Finally, this surgical strategy ensures the avoidance of distorted or compromised tissue, providing a clean and safe surgical field entirely remote from the scarred, hypertrophied, or fibrotic subcutaneous layers frequently found in the lower abdomen of long-term insulin users. By choosing the distal catheter type, insertion location, and length flexibly based on the patient’s pre-existing medical habits, neurosurgeons can proactively eliminate the risk of mechanical catheter damage and ensure stable shunt function.

CONCLUSION

Mechanical catheter injury resulting from repeated daily subcutaneous injections represents a rare but entirely preventable shunt complication. This case clearly demonstrates the paramount importance of structured patient education, emphasizing that individuals requiring ongoing subcutaneous drug delivery, along with their caregivers, must be explicitly counseled regarding the exact anatomical pathway of the shunt tract to strictly avoid accidental needle-induced hardware trauma. Furthermore, it highlights the necessity for flexible, customized selection of surgical entry sites in clinical practice. Rather than dogmatically adhering to standard abdominal entry points like McBurney’s point, which frequently overlap with a patient’s habitual injection zones, neurosurgeons must demonstrate adaptability in surgical planning. Prioritizing alternative entry sites, such as an upper abdominal midline approach via the linea alba, can actively safeguard the long-term safety, flow stability, and structural integrity of the shunt system in this vulnerable patient population.
