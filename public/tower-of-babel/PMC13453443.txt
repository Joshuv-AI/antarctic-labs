Mpox in advanced AIDS with concurrent opportunistic infections: periprocedural management challenges
Authors: Sanjana Murdande, Shikha Mishra, Augustine Munoz, Kasey Fox, Carlos D'Assumpcao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundMpox can be severe and prolonged in people with advanced human immunodeficiency virus. Concurrent opportunistic infections and diagnostic uncertainty may delay definitive therapy and complicate infection control and procedural planning.Presentation of the caseA 42-year-old man with long-standing acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) and inconsistent antiretroviral adherence presented with fever, cough, diffuse lymphadenopathy, and multiple papules on the skin, some being violaceous and crusted and others epithelialized. Imaging showed diffuse lymphadenopathy and pulmonary nodules. Broad differential diagnoses included disseminated coccidiomycosis, Pneumocystis pneumonia, Kaposi sarcoma, human herpesvirus 8–associated disease, and mpox. Cell-free DNA next-generation sequencing demonstrated mpox clade II and concurrent pathogens. Tecovirimat was obtained under the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) expanded-access protocol and administered for 14 days with epithelialization of skin lesions, suggesting resolution of mpox except for persistent mpox polymerase chain reaction positivity of one epithelialized lesion.Our team deferred inpatient lymph node biopsy because neither the CDC nor World Health Organization guidelines provide specific periprocedural isolation workflows (e.g. procedure room selection, anaesthesia considerations, staff personal protective equipment beyond standard recommendations, and postprocedure decontamination) for mpox patients, and we found no state, national, or international guidelines addressing periprocedural isolation precautions for mpox.ConclusionThis case highlights the management complexity of mpox in advanced AIDS with multiple concurrent opportunistic infections. It also underscores operational gaps for time-sensitive procedures in patients requiring mpox isolation precautions, emphasizing the need for practical periprocedural guidance and evolving CDC recommendations.

Introduction

Mpox is a zoonotic orthopoxvirus infection that spreads primarily through close, often skin-to-skin contact and exposure to lesions or contaminated materials. During the ongoing clade II outbreak recognized in 2022, most reported cases have been self-limited; however, severe manifestations have been observed, especially among people with moderate to severe immunocompromise, including advanced human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infections [[1], [2], [3]]. People with advanced HIV (particularly CD4 <200 cells/mm3 and especially <50 cells/mm3) are at a higher risk of disseminated disease, necrotizing lesions, secondary infections, prolonged illness, and death [2,4]. In August 2024, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared an mpox upsurge in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and other African countries a Public Health Emergency of International Concern [5]. Although guidance exists for mpox diagnosis, treatment, and infection prevention in healthcare settings, operational questions remain for patients requiring urgent diagnostic or therapeutic procedures while under isolation precautions [1]. Reports from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and WHO describe transmission risk through direct contact with lesions, contaminated fomites, respiratory secretions, and potentially aerosol-generating activities during close patient care [6,13]. Environmental sampling studies have demonstrated persistent mpox viral DNA on high-touch surfaces, linens, air vents, and reusable medical equipment in patient care environments, with replication-competent virus isolated from selected surfaces for days to weeks, highlighting the potential for environmental contamination and indirect transmission [13,19,20]. In healthcare settings, strict adherence to contact and droplet precautions, appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE), environmental disinfection with Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)-registered hospital-grade disinfectants, and careful handling of contaminated materials have been associated with low rates of nosocomial transmission [6,13]. However, uncertainty persists regarding procedural workflows for patients requiring urgent diagnostic or therapeutic interventions while under mpox isolation precautions, particularly in severely immunocompromised patients with prolonged viral shedding or persistent polymerase chain reaction (PCR) positivity. Methods

This manuscript was prepared in accordance with the CARE case report guidelines. Institutional review board approval and written informed consent for publication were obtained (see Ethics statement on the title page).

Case presentation

Patient information and history

A 42-year-old man with HIV/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) diagnosed in 2007 presented to the emergency department with two to three weeks of fever, cough, and progressive rash and approximately one month of bilateral neck swelling. History included treated syphilis in 2023, coccidioidomycosis diagnosed in 2023, and polysubstance use disorder. The patient reported non-adherence to his medications including bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide (Biktarvy) for several months, resuming approximately one month before presentation.

Clinical findings

On examination, he had oral thrush, bilateral large tender cervical lymphadenopathy, and multiple round violaceous papules with crusting, scaling, and scabbing on the face, nose, hands, legs, and feet (Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4, and 4). Diagnostic assessment

Computed tomography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis demonstrated diffuse lymphadenopathy, innumerable tiny pulmonary nodules, a hepatic hypodensity, and splenomegaly (Figure 5). The working differential included disseminated coccidioidomycosis, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, Kaposi sarcoma, human herpesvirus (HHV) 8–associated multi-centric Castleman disease, disseminated Mycobacterium avium complex infection, Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)–associated lymphoma and mpox. The patient was managed with isolation precautions while diagnostic evaluation proceeded in parallel with empiric therapy.Figure 5CT chest: innumerable bilateral pulmonary nodules. Because of diagnostic uncertainty and concern for concurrent pathogens, plasma cell-free DNA next-generation sequencing (Karius test) was obtained and reported mpox clade II along with coccidioidomycosis, Kaposi sarcoma, EBV and cytomegalovirus. Qualitative PCR was then completed on the lesion, as shown in Figure 1, Figure 4 and 4, and both resulted positive. The lesion pictured in Figure 4 tested positive despite epithelialization.

Therapeutic intervention

Antiretroviral therapy was administered, and given the concern for disseminated coccidioidomycosis, the patient was treated with liposomal amphotericin B. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole was also administered for suspected P. jirovecii pneumonia. Tecovirimat (TPOXX) was obtained under CDC expanded-access investigational protocols and administered for 14 days.

Infection prevention and control measures

The patient was managed with contact and droplet precautions in a single-patient room, with airborne precautions available for aerosol-generating procedures or if clinically indicated. Healthcare personnel used gown, gloves, eye protection, and medical masks for routine care; N95 respirators were available in the event of an aerosol-generating procedure. Environmental cleaning and disinfection were performed using EPA-registered hospital-grade disinfectants with appropriate emerging viral pathogen claims. High-touch surfaces and reusable equipment were cleaned according to institutional infection prevention protocols. Clinical specimens were collected and transported using standard precautions and institutional biosafety procedures. Waste and contaminated disposable materials were handled as regulated medical waste according to institutional policy.

Follow-up and outcomes

At discharge, the patient was counselled on infection prevention including lesion coverage, hand hygiene, avoiding close contact, and not sharing personal items.

During the tecovirimat therapy, skin lesions epithelialized and were clinically considered to be healed. However, the lesion on the back, which was epithelialized on patient presentation, continued to test positive for mpox, suggesting that the patient continued to remain in active infection. Persistent cervical lymphadenopathy raised concern for HHV-8-associated disease or lymphoma; however, inpatient lymph node biopsy was deferred because of unresolved questions regarding periprocedural management and infection prevention in a patient with active mpox.

Although a formal infection prevention and control (IPC) consultation was not obtained, multi-disciplinary discussions among the infectious diseases, primary medical, and procedural teams were conducted to assess healthcare worker exposure risk and procedural feasibility. Considerations included the potential for lesion manipulation, environmental contamination, patient transport requirements, uncertainty regarding aerosol-generating aspects of the procedure, and persistent mpox PCR positivity despite epithelialized lesions. Healthcare worker exposure risk was assessed based on the anticipated degree of direct contact with potentially infectious lesions or body fluids, the likelihood of contamination of procedural surfaces and equipment, the potential need for aerosol-generating interventions, and the ability to implement appropriate PPE and environmental decontamination measures during and after the procedure. Because no institution-specific or national periprocedural workflow guidance for mpox was available, and because the biopsy was diagnostically important but not emergently life-saving, the procedure was deferred with recommendation for outpatient tissue sampling after reassessment of infectious risk. However, the patient was lost to follow-up. He was readmitted to the hospital subsequently with cerebrovascular accidents due to coccidioidomycosis vasculitis and ultimately was discharged on hospice, following which he passed away.

Discussion

This case illustrates three intersecting challenges commonly faced when mpox occurs in advanced AIDS: (1) severe or atypical clinical presentations with high risk for complications, (2) diagnostic uncertainty with frequent coexisting opportunistic infections, and (3) operational gaps in safely performing urgent procedures under mpox isolation precautions.

Severity of mpox in advanced HIV

The CDC notes that patients with advanced HIV (CD4 <200 cells/mm3, especially <50 cells/mm3) are at risk for uncontrolled viral spread and life-threatening disease. Severe disease may include necrotic or confluent lesions, pulmonary nodules, sepsis, and involvement of multiple organ systems, often requiring hospitalization [1,2,6]. A large multi-national case series of mpox in people with advanced HIV reported substantially higher rates of necrotizing lesions, pulmonary involvement, secondary infections, and high mortality among those with CD4 counts <100 cells/mm [6,7].

Prevention and vaccination

The CDC recommends JYNNEOS vaccination for people with known or suspected exposure and for people with risk factors for exposure, including persons with HIV and others at increased risk based on sexual and social history. Vaccination is a key preventive measure that should be incorporated into routine care for eligible patients [[15], [16], [17]].

Treatment considerations

Supportive care and pain control remain central to mpox management. For patients with or at a high risk of severe manifestations (including advanced HIV), the CDC provides interim treatment considerations and access to tecovirimat under expanded-access protocols [1,8].

Randomized, placebo-controlled trials in patients with predominantly mild-to-moderate disease and limited immunocompromise – including the Study of Tecovirimat for Mpox (STOMP) conducted during the clade II outbreak and PALM007 conducted in the Democratic Republic of the Congo for clade I mpox – found oral tecovirimat to be safe but not associated with shorter time to clinical lesion resolution (and no improvement in pain in STOMP). Accordingly, in the USA, tecovirimat is generally accessed through the CDC Expanded Access Investigational New Drug (EA-IND) protocol for patients with, or at high risk for, severe disease; our patient received tecovirimat under this EA-IND pathway [9,10].

Emerging evidence also suggests that tecovirimat resistance can arise, particularly with prolonged or repeated therapy in severely immunocompromised patients, and resistant strains have been documented in the USA [11,12].

Infection prevention and periprocedural considerations

The CDC infection prevention guidance for healthcare settings recommends rapid identification, immediate notification of infection prevention personnel, and implementation of appropriate isolation precautions, including use of PPE and environmental cleaning with EPA-registered-hospital-grade disinfectants with an emerging viral pathogen claim [7].

In practice, patients with advanced AIDS often require diagnostic procedures (e.g. lymph node biopsy) to evaluate for lymphoma or HHV-8-associated disease. Our team deferred inpatient lymph node fine-needle aspiration/biopsy because neither CDC nor WHO guidance provides specific periprocedural isolation workflows (e.g. Operating room (OR)/procedure room selection, anaesthesia considerations, staff PPE beyond standard recommendations, and postprocedure decontamination) for mpox patients, and we found no state, national, or international guidelines addressing periprocedural isolation precautions for mpox.

Elements of such guidelines can be modelled based on the CDC’s Infection Control Guidance: SARS-CoV-2 as of 24th June 2024. Similar periprocedural infection control challenges were encountered during the COVID-19 pandemic, when healthcare systems developed institution-level protocols for aerosol-generating procedures, operating room selection, patient transport, staff minimization, enhanced PPE, and environmental decontamination in the absence of initially standardized guidance [18]. COVID-19 peri-operative frameworks emphasized multi-disciplinary risk assessment, including evaluation of aerosolization risk, procedural urgency, ventilation requirements, and healthcare worker exposure mitigation strategies [18]. Comparable operational concerns have also been described in the management of other high-risk transmissible infections requiring procedural intervention, where balancing diagnostic necessity against occupational exposure risk necessitated institution-specific workflows and enhanced IPC precautions [19,20]. These experiences support the need for similarly structured periprocedural guidance for patients with active mpox, particularly in severely immunocompromised individuals with prolonged viral shedding or persistent PCR positivity.

In this case, the planned lymph node biopsy was diagnostically important however not life-saving. Although the CDC’s infection control principles support deferring elective procedures during active infection, this case highlights the challenge of classifying procedures as elective versus urgent in patients with complex comorbidities. The lack of mpox-specific periprocedural guidance and the concern for spread to surgical staff complicated risk assessment and contributed to deferral to the outpatient setting.

Prolonged viral shedding and persistent PCR positivity have been reported in patients with concurrent advanced HIV, further complicating decisions regarding timing and safety of procedures. Individualized planning and close co-ordination with public health authorities in such cases are essential [14].

Limitations

This report is limited by incomplete outpatient follow-up and the absence of tissue diagnosis for persistent lymphadenopathy. In addition, mpox PCR detects viral DNA and does not distinguish live from non-viable viral DNA. Therefore, persistent PCR positivity may not equate to ongoing infectivity. Clearer clinical guidance on interpreting persistent PCR positivity in such patients would help inform isolation duration and procedural planning.

In conclusion, mpox should remain in the differential diagnosis of rash and systemic illness in patients with advanced HIV, particularly when lesions are confluent, necrotic, or accompanied by lymphadenopathy and pulmonary findings. Early co-ordination with infection prevention teams is essential to safely perform needed diagnostics and interventions. Practical periprocedural guidance for mpox in severely immunocompromised patients – integrating isolation, procedural logistics, and evolving therapeutic evidence – is urgently needed.
