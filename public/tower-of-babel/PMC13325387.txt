Rigorous Modeling and Industrial-Scalable Optimization of Hydroquinone–Catechol Vacuum Distillation: Aspen Plus-RSM Integration for Thermally Labile Systems
Authors: Chenglei Wang, Yihong Zeng, Mingwu Yi, Mengqi Yang, Shusheng Qiu, Yuanjian Xie, Pingxiong Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the critical challenges of high energy consumption and low efficiency in conventional hydroquinone (HQ)–catechol (CAT) purification, this study presents a systematic computational investigation of vacuum distillation via Aspen Plus. Leveraging HQ and CAT’s thermal sensitivity and nonazeotropic behavior (40.0 °C boiling point difference at 101.325 kPa), an accurate thermodynamic framework is built using the NRTL (non-random two-liquid) activity coefficient model, with parameters calibrated via UNIFAC and the Aspen database. A vacuum distillation configuration (column, condenser, reboiler) is initialized with 100 kg·h–1 feed flow, 100 °C inlet temperature, and 50 kPa system pressure, with rigorous vapor–liquid equilibrium and mass transfer simulations conducted via the RadFrac module. Systematic single-factor analysis of core variables (pressure, theoretical plates, feed stage, reflux ratio) is performed, with purity, recovery, and specific energy consumption as metrics. Integrating sensitivity analysis, response surface methodology (RSM), and multiobjective optimization, a predictive model identifies optimal operating conditions: 10 kPa operating pressure, 16 theoretical plates, 11th-tray feed position, 3.143 mass reflux ratio, and an optimized distillate-to-feed (D/F) ratio of 0.092 (reduced from the industrial baseline D/F ratio of 0.121). These optimal conditions yield 99.2% hydroquinone (HQ) purity, 99% catechol (CAT) purity, 98.5% HQ recovery, and 97.8% CAT recovery, with core distillation energy consumption reduced by 23.7% (23.2% for the full process, including vacuum pumps) versus the industrial baseline operating condition.

Introduction

Hydroquinone (HQ, 1,4-dihydroxybenzene) and catechol (CAT, 1,2-dihydroxybenzene) are essential organic chemical feedstocks utilized extensively in pharmaceuticals, dyes, rubber additives, and other industrial applications. Hydroquinone is a crucial precursor in the development of photographic developers and polymer antioxidants, , while catechol serves as a vital intermediate in the synthesis of fragrances, pesticides, and bioactive molecules. As phenolic isomers, the two compounds share highly similar molecular structures, as shown in Figure , which leads to nearly identical physicochemical properties and poses significant challenges for their efficient separation. However, industrial processes frequently produce these compounds as mixed products, necessitating efficient separation methodologies to improve product quality and cost-effectiveness. Conventional separation techniques, such as solvent extraction − and crystallization are hindered by inherent limitations, including suboptimal separation efficiency, excessive energy consumption, and environmental concerns associated with solvent usage. Consequently, the development of sustainable, energy-efficient separation technologies for these thermally sensitive isomers has become an urgent industrial requirement.

Molecular structural formulas of hydroquinone and catechol

Extensive research has been conducted worldwide on the separation of hydroquinone and catechol, with initial efforts concentrating on solvent extraction techniques. Zhu et al. demonstrated the application of ionic liquids for the separation of phenolic compounds, achieving component discrimination through the design of selective extractants; however, challenges related to extractant recovery and regeneration impede its industrial scalability. Although melt crystallization can produce high-purity products, its complex operation and significant energy requirements limit its practical application in continuous processes. ,

In recent decades, distillation technology has gained prominence due to its broad applicability, high separation efficiency, and compatibility with continuous operation. Nevertheless, the high boiling points, similar physicochemical properties, and thermal lability of hydroquinone and catechol present significant challenges for conventional distillation, which often necessitates elevated temperatures that risk material decomposition and coking. , Vacuum distillation has emerged as an effective solution by reducing operating temperatures through pressure adjustment. , Despite its potential, systematic computational optimization of vacuum distillation processes for these isomersparticularly using Aspen Plusremains limited. In terms of optimization methods, while emerging AI-driven approaches − (e.g., AutoML, meta-learning , ) and basic grid search ,, have been applied to simple distillation problems, they face critical limitations in this study’s scenario: AI methods require massive labeled data sets that are impractical to generate with high-cost Aspen Plus simulations, while grid search suffers from the “curse of dimensionality” for multivariable optimization. Aspen Plus, a leading process simulation platform in chemical engineering, enables rigorous modeling of distillation systems through its advanced thermodynamic databases and detailed unit operation modules (e.g., RadFrac). , Complementing this, Response Surface Methodology (RSM) provides a statistical framework to establish nonlinear relationships between operational variables and performance metrics (e.g., purity, recovery, energy consumption), facilitating efficient optimization with minimal experimental trials. , The integration of Aspen Plus’ process simulation capabilities with RSM’s mathematical optimization creates a synergistic approach to address the complex interactions inherent in thermally sensitive separation processes.

While previous studies have explored individual separation techniques for hydroquinone (HQ) and catechol (CAT), systematic investigations combining Aspen Plus-based vacuum distillation modeling with response surface methodology (RSM) optimization are scarce. Furthermore, existing single-tower simulation studies have largely overlooked the operational constraints of multitower cascaded industrial processessuch as feed stability tied to upstream unitsand the critical role of small-scale distillation columns for laboratory research, new product development, and specialty chemical production. This work addresses these gaps by delivering three key novel contributions: (1) the first rigorous simulation of a single tower (within a multitower cascaded vacuum distillation process) for HQ-CAT separation from mixed phenol extraction raffinate, incorporating industrial cascaded constraints; (2) the first integration of the NRTL thermodynamic model and RSM to optimize this cascaded single tower, aligned with upstream feed stability requirements; and (3) a cascaded-process-constrained optimization framework that enhances industrial applicability. Specifically, the core methodological design and evaluation the basis of this study are as follows: (1) optimized variables, three key operational variables with the highest sensitivity, including overhead operating pressure, reflux ratio, and feed tray position, were selected as the core optimization variables through presensitivity analysis; (2) simulated column, wherein the research object is a vacuum packed distillation column for industrial multitower cascaded process, which is rigorously modeled via the RadFrac module of Aspen Plus, with a final optimized number of 16 theoretical plates; (3) validation approach, in which the accuracy of the simulation model is verified by comparing with laboratory-scale test data, with the relative error of key performance indicators less than 1.5%; (4) energy comparison baseline for which the mature operating parameters of the current industrial continuous production of the target HQ-CAT separation system are used as the baseline, to ensure the industrial reference value and practical significance of the energy-saving effect comparison.

Theoretical Basis

Principle of Vacuum Distillation

Vacuum distillation is a separation technique conducted under subatmospheric pressures, utilizing reduced system pressure to lower the boiling point of mixtures, thereby facilitating gas–liquid separation at elevated temperatures. For thermally labile compounds such as hydroquinone and catechol, this method mitigates high-temperature side reactionssuch as decomposition and polymerizationthereby enhancing product quality and yield. Additionally, decreased operating pressure increases the relative volatility between components, improving the thermodynamic driving force for distillation-based separation. According to Raoult’s and Dalton’s law, under the gas–liquid equilibrium, the partial pressure of component (i) in the vapor phase (p i ) equals the product of its liquid-phase mole fraction (x i ) and its saturated vapor pressurepartial: 1 (pisat):⁣pi=xipisat(T)

Overview of Distillation Process Optimization Methods

To clarify the methodological selection logic of this study, this section systematically introduces the principles, advantages and limitations of typical optimization methods for distillation processes, including the grid search method, AI-driven optimization methods, and the RSM method adopted in this study.

Grid Search Optimization Method

Grid search, also known as full factorial traversal method, is the most basic global optimization method. Its core principle is to divide the value interval of each optimization variable into several equal gradient levels, and then combine all levels of all variables to form a complete variable space traversal scheme. The optimal solution is obtained by simulating all combined working conditions and comparing the response values. The advantage of grid search is that the principle is simple, there is no need for complex mathematical modeling, and there is no risk of falling into a local optimal solution; however, its core limitation is the “curse of dimensionality”: for an optimization problem with n variables, if each variable is divided into m levels, m n simulation trials are required. For the five continuous optimization variables in this study, even if only 10 levels are set for each variable, 105 rigorous process simulations are required, which will bring extremely high computational cost and long calculation time. In addition, grid search can only obtain the optimal solution within the set gradient interval, and cannot accurately locate the extreme point of the continuous response surface, resulting in limited optimization accuracy.

AI-Driven Optimization Methods

In recent years, AI-driven , optimization methods have been widely developed in the field of complex chemical process optimization, among which AutoML, gene expression programming and meta-learning are the most representative methods:

AutoML (Automated Machine Learning): AutoML realizes the automatic selection of machine learning models, automatic tuning of hyperparameters, and automatic optimization of feature engineering, which avoids the bias caused by manual experience in traditional machine learning modeling. For high-dimensional and ultracomplex distillation systems, AutoML can construct high-precision proxy models with strong generalization ability; however, its model training requires a large number of labeled sample data, which is not suitable for the scenario in this study where the single simulation cost is high and it is difficult to generate massive samples.

Gene Expression Programming (GEP): GEP is an evolutionary algorithm that combines the advantages of genetic algorithm and genetic programming, which can automatically identify the structural relationship between input variables and output responses, and construct an explicit mechanism model. GEP has significant advantages in the modeling of complex nonlinear systems with unknown mechanism, but in this study, we have completed the rigorous mechanism modeling of the distillation process through Aspen Plus, and there is no need for additional model structure identification, so the application of GEP will bring unnecessary computational complexity.

Meta-Learning: Meta-learning, also known as ″learning to learn″, realizes fast optimization of new tasks by learning the common laws of a large number of similar tasks. For the optimization of multiseries distillation processes, meta-learning can greatly reduce the number of samples required for optimization; however, this study focuses on the optimization of a specific hydroquinone-catechol vacuum distillation system, and there is a lack of a large number of similar pretrained tasks, so the advantages of meta-learning cannot be fully utilized.

Response Surface Methodology (RSM)

Response Surface Methodology (RSM) is a statistical optimization method that combines experimental design and mathematical modeling. Its core principle is to select representative variable combination working conditions through reasonable experimental design, obtain the response values corresponding to each working condition through simulation or experiment, and then use multiple regression fitting to construct a quantitative nonlinear relationship between the optimization variables and the response values, and finally obtain the optimal process parameters through the response surface model. Compared with grid search and AI-driven methods, RSM has the following core advantages for the research scenario of this study: (1) high computational efficiency, where only a small number of simulation trials are required to construct a high-precision response surface model, which greatly reduces the total computational cost; (2) strong interpretability, where the fitted quadratic polynomial model has an explicit mathematical expression, which can clearly explain the interaction between different operating variables, rather than a “black box” model; (3) suitable for thermally labile systems, where reducing the number of simulation iterations can avoid the cumulative error of simulation caused by repeated high-temperature residence time calculation of thermally sensitive materials. Based on the above advantages, this study selects Box–Behnken Design (BBD) combined with RSM as the core optimization method.

Simulation Results and Analysis

Influence of Operating Pressure on Separation Effect

A series of simulations were conducted to investigate the impact of operating pressure on separation efficiency, with pressure systematically varied from 5 to 20 kPa. Analysis of the results revealed a direct correlation between decreasing pressure and increasing relative volatility (α) of hydroquinone and catechol. This phenomenon arises from the differential response of their saturated vapor pressures to pressure reduction: as total pressure decreases, the disparity between the vapor pressures of the two components widens, enhancing their separability according to Raoult’s Law.

The relationship between pressure and relative volatility can be mathematically expressed as 7 αHQ/CT=PHQsatPCTsat where P HQ sat and P CT sat denote the saturated vapor pressures of hydroquinone and catechol, respectively. At 10 kPa, for example, α increases from 1.85 at 20 kPa to 2.30 at 5 kPa, reflecting a 24% improvement in separation potential. This trend, specifically for the hydroquinone–catechol binary system, is consistent with thermodynamic principles governing vacuum distillation, where reduced pressures mitigate thermal degradation risks while enhancing component volatility differences.

Figure a illustrates the direct correlation between decreasing pressure and increasing purity of catechol in the overhead stream. At 20 kPa, the catechol purity was 0.94377, while hydroquinone purity in the bottoms reached 0.985492. Reducing the pressure to 5 kPa enhanced both metrics, with catechol purity rising to 0.99996 and hydroquinone purity to 0.991189. This improvement arises from the amplified relative volatility (α) at lower pressures, which facilitates the preferential vaporization of catechol (the lighter component) into the gas phase.

Figure b, depicting hydroquinone impurity in the distillate, identifies a critical threshold: at 10 kPa, its content in the overhead stream falls below 0.01 (1 wt %), meeting industrial purity requirements. Balancing these results with practical considerations, 10 kPa was selected as the optimal operating pressure.

Recovery rate analysis further supports this choice. While catechol recovery increases monotonically with decreasing pressure, excessively low pressures (e.g., < 5 kPa) risk inducing floodinga hydrodynamic instability caused by high vapor velocities that disrupts mass transfer efficiency. This trade-off highlights the importance of selecting a pressure that maximizes separation while maintaining stable column operation.

Influence of Operating Temperature on Separation Effect

The temperature profile within the distillation column exerts a pronounced influence on product composition. At the column top, elevated temperatures enhance the volatility of heavy components (hydroquinone), leading to their increased presence in the overhead product and a corresponding decrease in catechol purity. Conversely, bottom temperatures dictate the residual light component (catechol) concentration in the bottoms stream; higher bottom temperatures reduce catechol carryover but may induce thermal decomposition of hydroquinonea thermally labile compoundintroducing impurities that compromise bottom product purity.

Given the thermal sensitivity of hydroquinone, this study focuses on temperature effects relevant to purity optimization rather than exploring yield fluctuations caused by potential decomposition. By prioritizing operational temperatures that balance component volatility with thermal stability, the proposed framework ensures reliable prediction of separation performance without exacerbating degradation risks.

Analysis of Unexpected Results and Deviation Mechanism

In the single-factor analysis of operating pressure and reflux ratio, we found an obvious nonlinear deviation phenomenon that deviates from the ideal linear trend: when the overhead operating pressure is lower than 5 kPa, the influence amplitude of reflux ratio on the reboiler heat duty significantly deviates from the linear increasing trend under conventional pressure (10–20 kPa). Specifically, under 10 kPa pressure, a 0.5 increase in reflux ratio leads to an 8% increase in reboiler heat duty; while when the pressure is reduced to 3 kPa, a 0.5 increase in reflux ratio leads to a 32% sharp increase in reboiler heat duty, showing an unexpected nonlinear surge in energy consumption. Meanwhile, under the pressure below 5 kPa, the improvement rate of catechol purity with the decrease of pressure also deviates from the linear growth trend, showing an obvious plateau effect: when the pressure is reduced from 10 to 5 kPa, the catechol purity increases from 0.9898 to 0.99996; while when the pressure is further reduced from 5 to 3 kPa, the catechol purity only increases by 0.00002, with almost no improvement, but the energy consumption increases sharply.

Through in-depth mechanism analysis, the core reasons for the above unexpected deviation phenomenon are clarified as follows:

Nonideal vapor–liquid equilibrium behavior of the system: The hydroquinone-catechol system has strong intermolecular hydrogen bond interaction, and the activity coefficient of the system changes sharply under extremely low pressure and low temperature conditions, leading to the deviation of the vapor–liquid equilibrium ratio from the ideal linear trend. This change amplifies the influence of reflux ratio on the vapor–liquid circulating load in the column, resulting in a nonlinear increase in energy consumption.

Pressure drop effect in the packed column: Under extremely low vacuum conditions, the proportion of pressure drop in the packed column to the overhead operating pressure increases significantly (from 8% under 10 kPa to 22% under 3 kPa), leading to a significant increase in the pressure difference between the top and bottom of the column. The bubble point temperature of the column bottom rises sharply with the increase of pressure, which directly leads to a nonlinear surge in the reboiler heat duty.

Physical property change of the thermally labile system: Under extremely low pressure, the density difference between the vapor and liquid phases in the column decreases significantly, leading to the distortion of the vapor–liquid load distribution in the column, and the mass transfer efficiency of the structured packing decreases. To achieve the same separation effect, a higher reflux ratio is required, which forms a positive feedback of energy consumption increase, leading to the unexpected sharp rise of energy consumption.

This deviation phenomenon also has important guiding significance for industrial operation: the optimal operating pressure range of this system is 5–10 kPa. This pressure range not only avoids the nonlinear sharp increase of energy consumption under ultrahigh vacuum (<5 kPa), but also controls the maximum temperature in the column below 180 °C, which effectively reduces the risk of polymerization and deterioration of the thermally labile hydroquinone and catechol system.

Research and Optimization by Response Surface Methodology

Optimization Objectives and Methods

To minimize energy consumption while maintaining product purity and recovery rate, an integrated optimization strategy combining sensitivity analysis (SA) and response surface methodology (RSM) was employed. Considering alternative optimization approaches widely reported in chemical process optimization, basic grid search, AutoML, gene expression programming and meta-learning were also carefully evaluated in this work. Grid search suffers from the curse of dimensionality for multivariable continuous optimization, requiring massive simulation runs and extremely high computational cost. AI-based methods such as AutoML and meta-learning rely on a large number of labeled data sets for model training, which is impractical for this thermally labile system with high single Aspen Plus simulation cost. Gene expression programming is more suitable for mechanism model structure identification, while rigorous thermodynamic and process modeling has already been completed in this study, making those alternative methods less applicable. Therefore, SA-RSM was finally selected to balance calculation accuracy and computational efficiency.

SA was first conducted to quantify the impact of each operating parameter (e.g., reflux ratio, feed stage, pressure) on the objective function, identifying key variables with the highest sensitivity indices. Subsequently, RSM was applied to construct a quadratic regression model linking these critical factors to the response variables (energy consumption, purity, recovery). The model was validated using ANOVA (F-value = 12.8; p < 0.001) and optimized via numerical search, yielding a 15.3% reduction in specific energy consumption (from 0.124 to 0.105 kwh/kg) while meeting purity targets (catechol ≥ 0.99; hydroquinone ≥ 0.99). This synergistic approach efficiently balances thermodynamic rigor with practical process economics.

All simulation and optimization calculations in this work were performed on a workstation with Intel Core i7-14700K CPU (3.4 GHz, 20 threads) and 64 GB DDR5 memory. The single steady-state simulation convergence time of the distillation column is about 12–18 s; the total computational time for sensitivity analysis and RSM experimental design runs is approximately 14 min; model regression, ANOVA and numerical optimization consume around 45 s; the whole modeling-to-optimization workflow takes about 2.5 h in total. By contrast, grid search with equivalent optimization accuracy would require over 100 h of computational time, further demonstrating the efficiency advantage of the adopted SA-RSM strategy.

Optimization Plan

A preliminary sensitivity analysis was conducted with the tray count held constant at 16, for the purpose of screening potential operating parameters influencing separation performance. Key operating parameters (pressure, reflux ratio, feed location, and feed temperature) were initially assessed.

Feed temperature was ultimately excluded from the RSM optimization framework, as the feed stream to this column is sourced from the effluent of the preceding tower in the multitower cascaded process and is maintained at a constant temperature via intertower heat exchangersan industrial configuration implemented to ensure process continuity and consistent feed characteristics. A supplementary sensitivity check was performed, confirming that minor fluctuations (±2 °C) in the stabilized feed temperature exert negligible impacts on both separation performance and energy consumption, thus validating its designation as a fixed process parameter.

Subsequently, pressure, reflux ratio, and feed location were systematically varied, with corresponding changes in product purity monitored. This analysis confirmed that these three parameters represent the primary drivers of separation performance, thereby justifying their selection as independent variables for RSM implementation.

Response surface methodology (RSM) was subsequently implemented to optimize separation performance, with product purity defined as the primary objective function and pressure, reflux ratio, and feed location designated as independent variables. A simulation-based experimental design matrix was constructed to systematically explore the parameter space, facilitating the development of a quadratic regression model that correlates the independent variables with the response (product purity).

This behavior is rooted in two key mechanisms.1.Reflux Ratio Impact: A higher reflux ratio increases liquid-phase recirculation, enhancing gas–liquid contact on trays and driving more efficient mass transfer. This elevates the liquid-phase concentration of hydroquinone (the heavy component) by providing greater driving force for its absorption from the vapor phase, analogous to increasing the effective number of theoretical trays. The resulting longer residence time of liquid on trays facilitates finer separation, mirroring the effect of multiple sequential distillations.2.Feed Tray Location Effect: A higher feed tray number (closer to the column top) shortens the stripping section, reducing the number of trays available for hydroquinone enrichment in the bottoms stream. In the rectifying section near the top, the vapor phase is enriched in light components (catechol), while the liquid phase contains lower heavy-component concentrations. Placing the feed here disrupts the gas–liquid equilibrium necessary for hydroquinone separation, as the shortened stripping section limits its concentration through successive vapor–liquid exchanges. This misalignment reduces the efficiency of heavy-component recovery, directly lowering hydroquinone purity.

Optimization Results

Regression analysis of simulation results yielded a quadratic model relating objective functions (purity, recovery, energy consumption) to operating parameters. Solving this model identified optimal conditions: 10 kPa operating pressure, reflux ratio of 3.143, and feed stage at the 11th tray.

Under these conditions, the overhead catechol purity reached 0.99, while bottoms hydroquinone purity achieved 0.992. Corresponding recovery rates were 98.5% (catechol) and 97.8% (hydroquinone), with a reboiler heat duty of 12.175 kW. Compared to initial simulation conditions, these results represent improvements of 16.8% in catechol purity, 1.8% in hydroquinone purity, and 19.3% in energy efficiency (reduction in specific energy consumption from 0.152 to 0.124 kWh/kg). The simultaneous enhancement in product quality, recovery efficiency, and energy savings validates the effectiveness of the RSM-based optimization strategy, providing a robust framework for industrial-scale separation of thermally sensitive isomer systems.

Optimization Result Validation

To validate the optimization results, simulated outcomes were first compared with data from the company’s lab-scale (bench-scale) tests (note: pilot-scale tests are currently in preparation and not yet completed). While full pilot-scale experimental verification is planned for future work, the current comparison between simulation and lab-scale results demonstrates close agreement, underscoring the initial reliability and preliminary practical utility of the optimized conditions.

The simulation-lab test alignment confirms that key performance indicators (KPIs)including product purity (catechol: 0.99 vs 0.987; hydroquinone: 0.992 vs 0.989) and reboiler duty (12.175 kW vs 12.320 kW)match within a 1.5% error margin. This consistency validates the Aspen Plus model’s ability to predict separation behavior under controlled lab-scale conditions, providing a critical basis for subsequent process amplification.

To further confirm industrial applicability, a pilot-scale test plan has been formulated: (1) Pilot-scale separation equipment will be commissioned within the next 9 months, focusing on replicating the optimized operating parameters (e.g., reflux ratio, column temperature gradient); (2) continuous operation tests will be conducted to verify KPIs such as long-term product purity stability and energy consumption under scaled-up conditions; (3) pilot-scale data will be used to refine the model and confirm its scalability, with results to be supplemented in follow-up reports.

Despite the absence of pilot-scale data at present, the lab-scale validation already supports the rationality of the optimized parameters for preliminary industrial reference. Additionally, integrating lab-scale data with prepilot process simulation helps mitigate technical risks in thermally sensitive separations (e.g., avoiding overheating-induced product degradation during scale-up), highlighting the practical value of the current research framework.

Conclusions and Prospects

Summary of Research Findings

In this study, aiming at the vacuum distillation separation process of the thermally labile catechol-hydroquinone isomer system, a rigorous steady-state process model was constructed using Aspen Plus with the NRTL activity coefficient model as the thermodynamic framework. Combined with sensitivity analysis and response surface methodology (RSM), multivariable collaborative optimization was completed with unit product energy consumption as the optimization objective and product purity and recovery rate as the constraints. Finally, the purity of both products reaches more than 99%, the recovery rate of target products is over 97.8%, and the core distillation energy consumption is reduced by 23.7% compared with the industrial baseline operating condition. This study demonstrates the successful simulation of hydroquinone and catechol vacuum distillation using Aspen Plus, through a systematic analysis of key process parametersincluding operating pressure, reflux ratio, feed stage, and intracolumn temperature profilesand their impacts on separation efficiency. Thermodynamic modeling with the NRTL activity coefficient model, coupled with rigorous RadFrac simulations, enabled detailed characterization of vapor–liquid equilibrium and mass transfer dynamics in the binary mixture.

Operating pressure emerged as a critical parameter, with reduced pressures (5–20 kPa) enhancing relative volatility αHQ/CT by 24% at 5 kPa compared to 20 kPa, primarily due to divergent responses in component vapor pressures. A reflux ratio of 3.143 and feed stage at the 11th trayidentified via response surface methodology (RSM)optimized mass transfer, achieving target purities of 0.99 (catechol, overhead) and 0.992 (hydroquinone, bottoms) while minimizing reboiler duty (12.175 kW). These conditions balanced the trade-off between separation efficiency and energy consumption, with recovery rates reaching 98.5% (catechol) and 97.8% (hydroquinone).

The study highlights the interactive effects of reflux ratio and feed stage on column section utilization: higher reflux ratios enhanced liquid-phase enrichment in the rectifying section, while lower feed stages prolonged contact in the stripping section, improving heavy-component (hydroquinone) recovery. Laboratory-scale test comparisons validated model predictions within a 1.5% error margin for key performance indicators, underscoring the reliability of the Aspen Plus framework for thermally sensitive isomer separations.

Beyond these performance outcomes, this study delivers three key novel contributions that advance the field: (1) it represents the first rigorous simulation of this specific single tower within a multitower cascaded industrial process, incorporating real-world cascaded constraints such as upstream feed stability; (2) it is the first to integrate the NRTL thermodynamic model with RSM for optimizing this cascaded single tower, ensuring alignment with industrial operational boundaries; and (3) it establishes a cascaded-process-constrained optimization framework that enhances the direct applicability of results to full-scale multitower production. This integrated approach provides a robust foundation for industrial process design, combining thermodynamic rigor with practical optimization to address challenges in high-purity separation of structurally similar compounds.

Research Deficiencies and Prospects

This study acknowledges inherent limitations stemming from the reliance on Aspen Plus’ built-in thermophysical property models and estimated binary interaction parameters, which may introduce minor discrepancies compared to experimental values. These uncertainties necessitate further validation through targeted physical property measurements and empirical data calibration. Additionally, the simulation framework omits practical considerations such as tray efficiency, hydrodynamic limitations (e.g., flooding, entrainment), and nonideal flow patternsfactors critical to real-world column performance.

Future research will focus on three key directions.(1) Model Refinement: Enhancing the Aspen Plus model by incorporating experimentally determined interaction parameters and validated tray efficiency correlations (e.g., O’Connell or Murphree efficiency models) to improve prediction accuracy for complex vapor–liquid dynamics.(2) Experimental Validation: Collaborating with industrial partners to conduct pilot-scale distillation tests, comparing simulated outcomes with real-world data to quantify model reliability and address discrepancies arising from nonideal behavior.(3) Process Innovation: Exploring advanced separation configurations (e.g., reactive distillation, divided-wall columns) and novel operating strategies (e.g., periodic reflux manipulation) to enhance energy efficiency and separation sharpness for thermally sensitive isomer systems.

Key Assumptions of the Proposed Methodology

The simulation and optimization methodology proposed in this study is based on the following core assumptions, which are the preconditions for the establishment of the model and optimization results:

Steady-State Operation Assumption

The simulation is based on the ideal steady-state operation of the industrial process, without considering the dynamic disturbances in actual industrial production, such as feed flow fluctuation, feed composition fluctuation, ambient temperature change, and equipment performance attenuation during long-term operation.

Mass Transfer Efficiency Assumption

The mass transfer efficiency of the structured packed column is calculated by a constant height equivalent to a theoretical plate (HETP) of 0.4 m, without considering the change of packing mass transfer efficiency under different vapor–liquid load conditions, and the influence of nonideal flow such as wall flow and entrainment on mass transfer efficiency.

Adiabatic Operation Assumption

The distillation column is assumed to operate under adiabatic conditions, without considering the heat loss of the column body to the external environment during actual industrial operation.

Phase Equilibrium Assumption

The vapor and liquid phases on each theoretical plate are assumed to be fully mixed and reach complete thermodynamic phase equilibrium at the outlet of the theoretical plate, without considering the mass transfer resistance and nonequilibrium effect in the actual vapor–liquid mass transfer process.

Feed Stability Assumption

The feed stream is assumed to be saturated liquid with stable composition and flow rate, without considering the influence of trace impurities in the actual industrial feed on the phase behavior and separation effect.

Limitations of the Proposed Methodology

Optimization Scope Limitation

This study only optimizes the steady-state operation parameters of the single distillation column, and does not involve the collaborative optimization of the upstream and downstream towers in the multitower cascaded industrial process, nor does it involve the optimization of the dynamic control scheme and antidisturbance ability of the process.

Model Prediction Limitation

The optimization accuracy of the RSM model adopted in this study depends on the variable interval of the experimental design. For extreme operating conditions beyond the design interval (e.g., pressure < 5 kPa or > 20 kPa), the prediction accuracy of the response surface model will decrease.

Industrial Scaling Limitation

The current model verification is only based on laboratory-scale test data, and the pilot-scale industrial test data is not yet available due to corporate confidentiality constraints. There may be deviations between the optimized parameters and the actual requirements of large-scale industrial continuous production.

Future Research Prospects

Model Refinement

Enhancing the Aspen Plus model by incorporating experimentally determined NRTL binary interaction parameters and validated packing mass transfer efficiency correlations, to improve the prediction accuracy of the model for actual industrial nonideal flow and mass transfer processes.

Full-Process Collaborative Optimization

Expanding the optimization scope from a single column to the whole multitower cascaded process, and carrying out collaborative optimization of upstream and downstream operating parameters to achieve further energy saving of the whole process.

Dynamic Control Scheme Development

Combining the steady-state optimization results with dynamic process simulation, developing an industrial automatic control scheme with both low energy consumption and high antidisturbance ability, to ensure the stable operation of the process under actual industrial disturbance conditions.

Pilot-Scale Industrial Verification

Collaborating with industrial partners to carry out pilot-scale continuous production tests, verifying the reliability of the optimized parameters in actual industrial production, and further calibrating the simulation model based on the pilot test data.
