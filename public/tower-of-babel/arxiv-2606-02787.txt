Adaptive Accelerated Mirror Descent in Primal and Dual Spaces
Zeyi Xu, Long Chen

Abstract
We propose Adaptive Accelerated Mirror Descent (AAMD), a flow-based method that combines nonlinear preconditioning, acceleration, and adaptivity in mirror geometry. The key ingredient is an accumulated Lyapunov perturbation budget: local descent failures are allowed as long as the total budget remains nonpositive, so line search is used only when stability is at risk. We prove accelerated convergence under dual relative smoothness/convexity and a mirror-geometry compatibility condition, and obtain an O ⁡ ( 1 / k 2 ) O(1/k^{2}) rate for convex objectives by homotopy under a bounded-sublevel-set assumption. Experiments on relative-smoothness problems show that combining preconditioning, acceleration, and adaptivity gives substantial gains over methods using only part of this structure.



1
Introduction

Gradient descent (GD) is a basic tool in first-order optimization. Its performance can degrade sharply when
f
is ill-conditioned or when the Euclidean geometry poorly matches the problem. Three ideas address this issue: preconditioning, acceleration, and adaptivity. Each is well developed on its own. Much less is known about how to combine all three in nonlinear mirror geometry.

Preconditioning and generalized smoothness.

Standard preconditioning replaces the Euclidean metric by a state-dependent matrix
A_{k}
, giving

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}A_{k}^{-1}\nabla f(x_{k})
. Mirror descent (MD)
[
18
]
generalizes this idea by using a mirror function
\phi
:

\nabla\phi(x_{k+1})=\nabla\phi(x_{k})-\alpha_{k}\nabla f(x_{k}).

(1)

Dual mirror descent (DMD) uses the Fenchel conjugate
\phi^{*}
and gives the primal-space update

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla f(x_{k})).

(2)

DMD includes gradient clipping and coordinate-wise schemes, such as Adam and Adagrad, by allowing larger effective steps along low-curvature directions
[
21
]
. In particular,
14
established the formal duality between MD (
1
) and DMD (
2
).

Acceleration under relative geometry.

Momentum acceleration, pioneered by Nesterov
[
19
]
, achieves the optimal
\mathcal{O}(1/k^{2})
rate for convex smooth problems. Classical acceleration is Euclidean. Its extension to mirror geometry has been studied in several forms
[
17
,
20
]
.
10
studied mirror duality in convex optimization and derived dual accelerated mirror-descent-type methods. ODE discretizations also give accelerated rates
[
11
,
24
]
. However, these rates are of order
\mathcal{O}(L_{f}/k^{2})
and still depend on the Euclidean Lipschitz constant
L_{f}
of
\nabla f
. This constant can be large or infinite, which limits the benefit of mirror geometry.

To avoid this Euclidean dependence,
9
proposed Accelerated Bregman Proximal Gradient (ABPG), whose rate depends on the triangle scaling exponent (TSE). More recently,
5
developed Acc-MD through variable-operator splitting
[
4
]
and proved an accelerated linear rate under relative geometry.

Acceleration in mirror geometry requires extra structure. Relative smoothness alone does not generally imply global accelerated rates
[
7
]
. Existing approaches impose additional geometric conditions, such as the TSE condition in ABPG or the generalized Cauchy–Schwarz condition in
[
4
]
.

Adaptivity without overhead.

Practical methods must adapt to unknown or spatially varying curvature. Classical line search enforces descent at every step, but this may require repeated gradient evaluations. Modern adaptivity, as defined by
[
16
]
, tracks local smoothness using only information already computed. Such schemes exist for Euclidean GD
[
15
,
23
]
, but they have not been fully developed for accelerated mirror methods.

Main idea.

The three ingredients above are not new by themselves. The contribution of this paper is to combine them in one stable algorithmic framework. The method uses mirror geometry as a nonlinear preconditioner, adds acceleration through a primal–dual mirror flow, and adapts local parameters through a Lyapunov stability budget. This combination matters: preconditioning alone changes the geometry but does not accelerate; acceleration alone remains tied to the Euclidean smoothness constant
L_{f}
and may fail to apply when
L_{f}
is large or infinite; adaptivity alone does not exploit the mirror geometry. Their coupling gives a method that is geometry-aware, accelerated under relative geometry, and stable with few backtracking steps.

Contributions.

The paper makes the following contributions.

1. A primal–dual accelerated mirror framework.

We derive an accelerated mirror-descent-type scheme by coupling a dual preconditioned descent step for the primal variable with a primal mirror update for an auxiliary variable. The construction is guided by a continuous-time Lyapunov flow. This flow shows how nonlinear preconditioning and acceleration can be coupled while preserving the energy dissipation property.

The starting point is closely related to dual-space preconditioning and mirror duality
[
14
,
12
,
10
]
. In particular, a related coupling of primal and dual mirror updates was considered by
10
. However, their method does not include adaptivity, and its acceleration rate depends on the Euclidean Lipschitz constant
L_{f}
rather than a relative smoothness constant. The new point here is the flow-based integration of nonlinear preconditioning, acceleration, and adaptive stability control.

2. Adaptive stability through an accumulated perturbation budget.

The main algorithmic ingredient is an accumulated perturbation budget. Instead of imposing a strict descent inequality at every step, the method allows local violations when they are compensated by previous descent. Backtracking is triggered only when the accumulated budget becomes positive. Thus line search acts as a safeguard rather than a repeated per-iteration routine, while global Lyapunov stability is preserved. In our numerical experiments, fewer than ten backtracking steps occur in total, and all appear during the initial transient phase.

3. A combined method with improved practical performance.

The resulting Adaptive Accelerated Mirror Descent (AAMD) method combines nonlinear preconditioning, acceleration, and adaptivity. Numerical experiments show that this combination is stronger than using only one or two of these ingredients.

Limitations.

The results require a mirror map adapted to the objective. The method may not help if
\nabla\phi
or
\nabla\phi^{*}
is expensive to compute, or if the mirror map does not reduce the relative condition number. The accelerated rate also relies on a mirror-geometry compatibility condition, which is automatic in the Euclidean case but may hold only locally or with a large constant for general mirror maps. The present analysis is deterministic and convex. Stochastic and nonconvex extensions are left for future work.

2
Preliminaries

We recall basic facts from finite-dimensional convex optimization and duality; see, e.g.,
[
1
,
22
]
. Throughout the paper,
V
denotes a finite-dimensional Hilbert space and
V^{*}
denotes its dual space. The duality pairing between
\chi\in V^{*}
and
x\in V
is denoted by
\langle\chi,x\rangle
. By the Riesz representation theorem,
V
can be identified with
V^{*}
, in which case the duality pairing coincides with the inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Bregman divergence.

For a continuously differentiable function
f
, the
Bregman divergence
is

D_{f}(y,x):=f(y)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle.

For
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(V)
, convexity is equivalent to
D_{f}(y,x)\geq 0
for all
x,y\in V
. If
f
is strictly convex, then
D_{f}(y,x)=0
if and only if
x=y
. In general,
D_{f}
is not symmetric. Its symmetrization satisfies

D_{f}(y,x)+D_{f}(x,y)=\langle\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle.

A key tool in the analysis is the three-point identity
[
3
]
:

\langle\nabla f(z)-\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle=D_{f}(x,y)+D_{f}(y,z)-D_{f}(x,z).

(3)

For a Legendre function
\phi
, the Bregman divergences of
\phi
and its Fenchel conjugate
\phi^{*}
satisfy

D_{\phi}(x,y)=D_{\phi^{*}}(\nabla\phi(y),\nabla\phi(x)),

(4)

with reversed arguments. The gradient of the Bregman divergence with respect to the first variable is

\displaystyle\nabla D_{f}(\cdot,x)=\nabla f(\cdot)-\nabla f(x),

(5)

\displaystyle\nabla D_{\phi^{*}}(\cdot,\chi)=\nabla\phi^{*}(\cdot)-\nabla\phi^{*}(\chi).

Mirror duality.

Throughout the paper, we assume
\nabla\phi^{*}(0)=0
and
\nabla\phi(0)=0
, which can be achieved by a shift. The mirror function
\phi
is assumed to be Legendre: it is closed, proper, strictly convex, differentiable on
\operatorname{int}\operatorname{dom}\phi
, and its gradient map is one-to-one from
\operatorname{int}\operatorname{dom}\phi
onto
\operatorname{int}\operatorname{dom}\phi^{*}
. Hence

(\nabla\phi)^{-1}=\nabla\phi^{*}.

When we use
(\nabla f)^{-1}=\nabla f^{*}
in the dual formulation, we assume the corresponding Legendre-type condition for
f
on the relevant domain.

For the primal problem
\min_{x\in V}f(x)
, consider the dual mirror descent step for
\min_{\chi\in V^{*}}\phi^{*}(\chi)
with
f^{*}
as the mirror function:

\nabla f^{*}(\chi_{k+1})=\nabla f^{*}(\chi_{k})-\alpha_{k}\nabla\phi^{*}(\chi_{k}).

(6)

This scheme is formally dual to the primal mirror descent update (
1
), and we call it dual mirror descent (DMD). Identifying
x_{k}=\nabla f^{*}(\chi_{k})
, or equivalently
\chi_{k}=\nabla f(x_{k})
, transforms (
6
) into the primal update (
2
).
Thus, parts of the mirror descent analysis can be transferred to DMD.

Relative smoothness in the dual space.

Relative smoothness and relative convexity, first introduced in
[
13
]
, are standard assumptions for mirror descent methods.

Assumption (A1).
There exist constants
0\leq\mu\leq L
such that

\mu D_{\phi}(x,y)\leq D_{f}(x,y)\leq LD_{\phi}(x,y).

(7)

Assumption (A1) is equivalent to the convexity of
f-\mu\phi
and
L\phi-f
.

In parallel, DMD requires the following dual relative smoothness and convexity condition
[
14
]
, also called anisotropic smoothness in
[
12
]
.

Assumption (A1
∗
).
There exist constants
0\leq\mu\leq L
such that

\mu D_{f}(y,x)\leq D_{\phi^{*}}(\nabla f(x),\nabla f(y))\leq LD_{f}(y,x).

(8)

Assumption (A1
∗
) is the standard relative smoothness and convexity condition for the dual pair
(\phi^{*},f^{*})
:
\phi^{*}-\mu f^{*}
and
Lf^{*}-\phi^{*}
are convex. Its equivalence to anisotropic smoothness was proved in
[
12
]
using Moreau envelope arguments.

The constants in (A1) and (A1
∗
) need not be the same. Indeed, the two assumptions are independent, and we specify which one is used in each result. When
\phi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}
, both reduce to the usual Euclidean smoothness and strong convexity assumptions. For nonquadratic
\phi
, Assumptions (A1) and (A1
∗
) can capture non-Euclidean curvature even when
\nabla f
is not Lipschitz. The assumption is useful but restrictive: an adapted mirror map must be available, and both
\nabla\phi
and
\nabla\phi^{*}
must be efficient to compute.

3
Accelerated Primal–Dual Mirror Descent

Continuous-time dynamics.

We combine the primal and dual mirror dynamics

\displaystyle x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=y-x-\beta\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla f(x)),

(9)

\displaystyle\bigl(\nabla\phi(y)\bigr)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(y)-\tfrac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(x).

This flow preconditions the HNAG flow
[
6
]
through a primal–dual mirror coupling. Here
\mu>0
is the relative convexity constant, so that
f-\mu\phi
is convex, and
\beta>0
controls the dual preconditioning strength. The
x
-dynamics contain a dual mirror step, while the
y
-dynamics follow a primal mirror update. The equilibrium of (
9
) is
x=y=x^{\star}
.

To analyze stability, define the Lyapunov energy

E(z):=D_{f}(x,x^{\star})+\mu D_{\phi}(x^{\star},y),\qquad z=(x,y).

(10)

The identity
D_{\phi}(x^{\star},y)=D_{\phi^{*}}(\nabla\phi(y),\nabla\phi(x^{\star}))
will be used to differentiate the second term.

Lemma 3.1

(Energy dissipation)
.

The energy (
10
) along the flow (
9
) satisfies

\displaystyle\langle\nabla E(z),z^{\prime}\rangle={}

\displaystyle-E(z)-\beta\langle\nabla f(x),\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla f(x))\rangle-D_{f-\mu\phi}(x^{\star},x)-\mu D_{\phi}(y,x).

(11)

Consequently, if
f-\mu\phi
is convex, then

E(z(t))\leq e^{-t}E(z(0)),\qquad t\geq 0.

Proof.

Let
\eta=\nabla\phi(y)
. Differentiating
E
gives

\partial_{x}E=\nabla f(x),\qquad\partial_{\eta}E=\mu\bigl(\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(y))-\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x^{\star}))\bigr)=\mu(y-x^{\star}).

Then by the chain rule, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\,{\rm d}}{\,{\rm d}t}E(z)={}

\displaystyle\left\langle{\nabla E(z),z^{\prime}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\left\langle{\nabla f(x),y-x-\beta\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla f(x))}\right\rangle+\mu\left\langle{y-x^{\star},\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(y)-\tfrac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(x)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\left\langle{\nabla f(x),x-x^{\star}}\right\rangle-\beta\left\langle{\nabla f(x),\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla f(x))}\right\rangle+\mu\left\langle{y-x^{\star},\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(y)}\right\rangle

which gives (
11
) by the three-point identity (
3
). If
f-\mu\phi
is convex, then the last two terms in (
11
) are nonpositive. Hence
\frac{\,{\rm d}}{\,{\rm d}t}E(z(t))\leq-E(z(t))
, and Grönwall’s inequality gives the result.
∎

Discretization.

We discretize (
9
) by the following implicit–explicit scheme:

\displaystyle x_{k+1}-x_{k}

\displaystyle=\alpha\bigl(y_{k}-x_{k+1}-\beta\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla f(x_{k}))\bigr),

(12a)

\displaystyle\nabla\phi(y_{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y_{k})

\displaystyle=\alpha\bigl(\nabla\phi(x_{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y_{k+1})\bigr)-\tfrac{\alpha}{\mu}\nabla f(x_{k+1}).

(12b)

The gradient in the
x
-dynamics is treated explicitly in (
12a
), while
x_{k+1}
and
y_{k+1}
are treated implicitly to preserve the energy structure. Equivalently, (
12b
) is a mirror-descent step, and
y_{k+1}
solves

y_{k+1}\in\arg\min_{y}(1+\alpha)\phi(y)-\left\langle\alpha\nabla\phi(x_{k+1})+\nabla\phi(y_{k})-\tfrac{\alpha}{\mu}\nabla f(x_{k+1}),\,y\right\rangle.

(13)

We assume that
\phi
is chosen so that the subproblem (
13
) can be computed efficiently.

Lemma 3.2

(Descent identity)
.

The iterates of (
12
) satisfy

\begin{split}(1+\alpha)E(z_{k+1})-E(z_{k})={}&-D_{f}(x_{k},x_{k+1})-\mu D_{\phi}(y_{k+1},y_{k})\\
&+\alpha\left\langle{\nabla f(x_{k+1}),y_{k}-y_{k+1}}\right\rangle-\alpha\beta\left\langle{\nabla f(x_{k+1}),\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla f(x_{k}))}\right\rangle\\
&-\alpha D_{f-\mu\phi}(x^{\star},x_{k+1})-\alpha\mu D_{\phi}(y_{k+1},x_{k+1}).\end{split}

(14)

Proof.

We present an outline here and refer to Appendix for details. The difference of Lyapunov functions

E(z_{k+1})-E(z_{k})=\left\langle{\nabla E(z_{k+1}),z_{k+1}-z_{k}}\right\rangle-D_{E}(z_{k},z_{k+1}).

The first line on RHS of (
14
) is simply
-D_{E}(z_{k},z_{k+1})
.
Let
G
denote the vector field in (
9
). The scheme (
12
) can be written as a correction of the implicit Euler step:

z_{k+1}-z_{k}=\alpha G(z_{k+1})+\binom{\alpha(y_{k}-y_{k+1})-\alpha\beta\bigl(\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla f(x_{k}))-\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla f(x_{k+1}))\bigr)}{0}.

We follow Lemma
3.1
to calculate
\left\langle{\nabla E(z_{k+1}),\alpha G(z_{k+1})}\right\rangle
. The discrepancy terms are in the second and third lines in (
14
).
∎

4
Adaptive Accelerated Mirror Descent

We now introduce AAMD, which adaptively updates
(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k},\mu_{k})
by monitoring the slack in the Lyapunov dissipation inequality.

Accumulated Stability and Reduced Line Search.

We first consider the case where
\mu>0
is known and rewrite the remaining parameters as
(\alpha_{k},L_{k})
with
1/L_{k}=\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}
.

Lemma 4.1

(Descent inequality)
.

The iterates of (
12
) satisfy

E(z_{k+1})\leq\frac{1}{1+\alpha_{k}}E(z_{k})+b_{k},\qquad b_{k}=b_{k}^{(1)}+b_{k}^{(2)}+b_{k}^{(3)},

(15)

where

\displaystyle b_{k}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L_{k}}D_{\phi^{*}}(\nabla f(x_{k+1}),\nabla f(x_{k}))-D_{f}(x_{k},x_{k+1}),

(16a)

\displaystyle b_{k}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k}\left\langle{\nabla f(x_{k+1}),y_{k}-y_{k+1}}\right\rangle-\frac{1}{L_{k}}D_{\phi^{*}}(\nabla f(x_{k+1}),0)-\mu D_{\phi}(y_{k+1},y_{k}),

(16b)

\displaystyle b_{k}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{L_{k}}D_{\phi^{*}}(0,\nabla f(x_{k}))-\alpha_{k}\mu D_{\phi}(y_{k+1},x_{k+1}).

(16c)

Proof.

Let
g_{k}=\nabla f(x_{k})
. Dropping the negative terms
-\alpha D_{f-\mu\phi}(x^{\star},x_{k+1})
in the last line of (
14
) and applying the three-point identity gives

-\left\langle{g_{k+1},\nabla\phi^{*}(g_{k})}\right\rangle=-D_{\phi^{*}}(g_{k+1},0)-D_{\phi^{*}}(0,g_{k})+D_{\phi^{*}}(g_{k+1},g_{k}).

Rearranging the terms yields (
15
).
∎

The resulting algorithm is summarized in Algorithm
1
. We use a modified adaptive backtracking strategy from
[
2
]
. The perturbation
b_{k}
consists of three computable parts. The term
b_{k}^{(3)}
is always nonpositive and acts as a stability buffer generated by the dual mirror step. When
p_{k}>0
, the current parameters fail to satisfy the descent condition, and we distinguish two cases.

If
b_{k}^{(1)}>0
, then the local smoothness estimate
L_{k}
is too small. We update

L_{k}\leftarrow\max\left\{c_{1}L_{k},\,\frac{D_{\phi^{*}}(g_{k+1},g_{k})}{D_{f}(x_{k},x_{k+1})}\right\},\qquad g_{k}=\nabla f(x_{k}).

If
b_{k}^{(2)}>0
, then the stepsize
\alpha_{k}
is too large. We decrease
\alpha_{k}
by

\alpha_{k}\leftarrow\min\left\{\alpha_{k}/c_{2},\,\frac{\frac{1}{L_{k}}D_{\phi^{*}}(g_{k+1},0)+\mu D_{\phi}(y_{k+1},y_{k})}{\left\langle{g_{k+1},y_{k}-y_{k+1}}\right\rangle}\right\},

until
p_{k}\leq 0
. Here
c_{1},c_{2}>1
are fixed backtracking constants. The second formula is obtained by enforcing
b_{k}^{(2)}=0
. Thus the line search either increases the local curvature estimate or decreases the acceleration parameter, guaranteeing termination.

After an admissible pair
(L_{k},\alpha_{k})
is found, we use the spectral estimates in Lines 13–14 as the initial guess for the next iteration. The ratio defining
L_{k+1}
measures the local relative smoothness along the current step, while
\alpha_{k+1}=\sqrt{\mu/L_{k+1}}
gives the corresponding accelerated parameter.

The global stability is controlled by the following multi-step estimate.

Theorem 4.2

(Multi-step convergence)
.

Assume
f-\mu\phi
is convex with
\mu>0
, and let
\{z_{k}\}
be generated by Algorithm
1
. Define the accumulated perturbation by

p_{-1}=0,\qquad p_{k}=\frac{1}{1+\alpha_{k}}(p_{k-1}+b_{k}).

Then the Lyapunov energy (
10
) satisfies

E(z_{k+1})\leq\left(\prod_{i=0}^{k}\frac{1}{1+\alpha_{i}}\right)E(z_{0}).

Proof.

By induction,

E(z_{k+1})\leq\left(\prod_{i=0}^{k}\frac{1}{1+\alpha_{i}}\right)E(z_{0})+p_{k}.

Since the algorithm enforces
p_{k}\leq 0
for all
k
, the result follows.
∎

Unlike standard line search methods, AAMD triggers backtracking only when
p_{k}
becomes positive. In practice, the negative buffer term
b_{k}^{(3)}
is often large enough that line search is needed only during the initial transient phase. By continuity, the parameters
(L_{k+1},\alpha_{k+1})
are usually good initial guesses for the next iteration. In our numerical experiments, fewer than ten backtracking steps occur in total, all during the initial transient phase.

Remark 4.3
.

In all numerical experiments, we use
c_{1}=2
and
c_{2}=1.5
. The method is not sensitive to these values, since they only serve as safeguards for line-search termination.

The key idea is simple. For a fixed

D_{\phi}(x^{\star},x_{k})\leq\frac{1}{2}R^{2},\qquad\forall k\geq 0.

We use two criteria for halving the perturbation. The first is the decay condition

E_{k}\leq E_{k_{s}}/2

is not directly observable, we use the computable stopping test

\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}\leq\|\nabla f(x_{k_{s}})\|^{2}/2.

With this rule, AAMD-0 works for both

On the boundedness assumption.

The boundedness assumption (
17
) in Theorem
4.4
is needed to convert the homotopy decrease for the regularized problems into an
O(1/k^{2})
rate for the original convex problem. It holds, for example, when the relevant Lyapunov sublevel set is bounded, when
\phi

In Appendix
C

9

\alpha
is sufficiently small. In practice, boundedness can be enforced by projection, constraints, or a trust-region safeguard.

Convergence of iterates.

The preceding results give convergence of the objective value and Lyapunov energy. If the minimizer
x^{\star}
is unique and
\phi
is strongly convex on the relevant sublevel set, then
D_{\phi}(x^{\star},y_{k})\to 0
implies
y_{k}\to x^{\star}
. The update equations and the vanishing energy then give
x_{k}\to x^{\star}
. Without uniqueness, the results imply convergence of the function values and the Bregman distance to the solution set, but not strong convergence of
\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\|
to a fixed minimizer.

Extension to composite problems.

AAMD also extends to composite problems of the form
\min_{x}f(x)+g(x)
, where
f
is smooth and
g
is convex but possibly nonsmooth. The
x
-update is replaced by a proximal gradient step adapted to the mirror geometry:

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\frac{1}{L_{k}(1+\alpha_{k})}\phi\left(-L_{k}(1+\alpha_{k})\left(x-z_{k+1}\right)\right)+\left\langle{\nabla f(x_{k}),x}\right\rangle+g(x)\right\}

(18)

where
z_{k+1}=\frac{1}{1+\alpha_{k}}(x_{k}+\alpha_{k}y_{k})
. The
y
-update uses the subgradient computed in the
x
-update step. The linesearch procedure for
L_{k}
and the spectral update remain the same as in the smooth case. For
\mu=0
, we employ the same homotopy strategy as in the smooth case in Appendix
C
.

This allows the method to handle nonsmooth penalties and simple constraints. The resulting algorithm, AAproxMD, is summarized in Algorithm
3
. Its convergence analysis is given in Appendix

.

Discussion: Relation to Anisotropic Smoothness/Convexity

Laude et al. (2025) proposed the anisotropic descent (smoothness) and convexity conditions
[
12
]
:

Definition A.1
.

The function
f
is said to have the anisotropic convexity property of
\lambda
with respect to the preconditioner function
\phi
if, for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

D_{f}(x,y)\geq D_{\phi_{\lambda}}(x-y^{+},y-y^{+}),

(23)

where
y^{+}=y-\lambda\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla f(y))
, and
\phi_{\lambda}
is the epi-scaling of
\phi
:
\phi_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda\phi(\frac{1}{\lambda}x)
.

Definition A.2
.

The function
f
is said to have the anisotropic descent (smoothness) property of
\lambda
with respect to the preconditioner function
\phi
if, for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

D_{f}(x,y)\leq D_{\phi_{\lambda}}(x-y^{+},y-y^{+}),

(24)

where
y^{+}=y-\lambda\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla f(y))
, and
\phi_{\lambda}
is the epi-scaling of
\phi
:
\phi_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda\phi(\frac{1}{\lambda}x)
.

We now show that
(A1
∗
)
is equivalent to having the anisotropic descent property of
L
(Definition
A.2
) and anisotropic convexity property of
\mu
(Definition
A.1
). Our approach to the dual mirror descent can thus be viewed as an alternative approach with greater simplicity and clarity.

A.1
epi-scaling

The scaling in the dual function leads to the so-called epi-scaling of
\phi
:

(\phi_{\lambda})(x):=\lambda\,\phi\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}x\right),\qquad\lambda>0.

It is called
epi-scaling
because the epigraph of
\phi_{\lambda}
is obtained
by scaling the epigraph of
\phi
by the factor
\lambda
. Indeed, recall that

\operatorname{epi}\phi:=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\;:\;t\geq\phi(x)\}.

For
\lambda>0
and
\phi_{\lambda}(x):=\lambda\,\phi(x/\lambda)
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{epi}\phi_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\{(x,t)\;:\;t\geq\lambda\,\phi(x/\lambda)\}=\{(\lambda y,\lambda s)\;:\;s\geq\phi(y)\}=\lambda\,\operatorname{epi}\phi,

where the last equality is understood as a uniform dilation in

\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

Lemma A.3
.

For
\lambda>0
, the convex conjugate of the epi-scaled function satisfies

(\phi_{\lambda})^{*}=\lambda\,\phi^{*}.

Proof.

\displaystyle(\phi_{\lambda})^{*}(y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x}\Big\{\langle y,x\rangle-\lambda\,\phi(x/\lambda)\Big\}=\lambda\sup_{u}\Big\{\langle y,u\rangle-\phi(u)\Big\}=\lambda\,\phi^{*}(y).

∎

Certain scaling properties hold under epi-scaling:

•

Bregman divergence.

The Bregman divergence associated with
\phi_{\lambda}
satisfies

D_{\phi_{\lambda}}(x,y)=\lambda\,D_{\phi}\!\left(\frac{x}{\lambda},\,\frac{y}{\lambda}\right),\qquad\forall\,x,y.

•

Gradients and Hessians.

If
\phi\in C^{1}
, then

\nabla(\phi_{\lambda})(x)=\nabla\phi\!\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right).

If
\phi\in C^{2}
, then

\nabla^{2}(\phi_{\lambda})(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\,\nabla^{2}\phi\!\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right).

Thus epi-scaling rescales the curvature of
\phi
, not merely its values. In
particular, it modifies smoothness and strong convexity constants while
preserving the overall geometric structure encoded by the Bregman divergence.

A.2
Equivalence of anisotropic smoothness with dual relative smoothness

If
\phi^{*}
is
L
-relatively smooth with respect to
f^{*}
, define the epi-scaled
function
\psi:=\phi_{1/L}
, that is,
\psi(x)=\frac{1}{L}\,\phi(Lx).
By the conjugacy property of epi-scaling, we have

D_{\psi^{*}}(\chi,\eta)\leq D_{f^{*}}(\chi,\eta),

which shows that
\psi^{*}
is
1
-relatively smooth with respect to
f^{*}
.
Therefore, by replacing
\phi
with its epi-scaled version
\phi_{1/L}
, we may
assume without loss of generality that the relative smoothness constant satisfies

L=1
.

Appendix B
Convergence Analysis for Accelerated Primal-Dual Mirror Descent

Convergence Guarantees

Accelerated linear convergence is achieved by ensuring the right-hand side of Lemma
3.2
remains non-positive through proper parameter selection. The following Young-type condition is sufficient for guaranteeing accelerated linear convergence:

Assumption B.1

(Mirror acceleration compatibility)
.

For the iterates generated by (
12
), the mirror map
\phi
and the
stepsize
\alpha
satisfy

\alpha\langle\nabla f(x_{k+1}),y_{k}-y_{k+1}\rangle\leq\frac{1}{L}D_{\phi^{*}}(\nabla f(x_{k+1}),0)+\mu D_{\phi}(y_{k+1},y_{k}).

Theorem B.2
.

Assume
f-\mu\phi
and
Lf^{*}-\phi^{*}
are convex. Let
\{z_{k}\}
be generated
by (
12
) with
\alpha\beta=1/L
. If Assumption
B.1

holds, then the energy decays at a linear rate:

E(z_{k+1})\leq\frac{1}{1+\alpha}E(z_{k}).

(33)

Proof.

As
f-\mu\phi
and
\phi
are convex, the last two terms in (
14
) are non-positive and can be dropped.

We use the three-point identity for
\phi^{*}
to expand the cross term
\left\langle{\nabla f(x_{k+1}),\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla f(x_{k}))}\right\rangle
.

Substituting
\alpha\beta=1/L
. Since
Lf^{*}-\phi^{*}
is convex, dual relative smoothness implies

D_{\phi^{*}}(\nabla f(x_{k+1}),\nabla f(x_{k}))\leq LD_{f}(x_{k},x_{k+1}).

The cross term
\alpha\left\langle{\nabla f(x_{k+1}),y_{k}-y_{k+1}}\right\rangle
is bounded by (
B.1
),
which cancels the remaining positive term in (
14
). Therefore,

(1+\alpha)E(z_{k+1})\leq E(z_{k}),

which yields (
33
).
∎

Next, we discuss several types of mirror maps where the assumptions needed for linear accelerated convergence holds. In all cases, (
B.1
) is satisfied with the step-size

being lower bounded by
\sqrt{\mu/L}
up to some constant, thus guaranteeing accelerated linear convergence:

E(z_{k+1})\leq\left(1+c\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{L}}\right)^{-1}E(z_{k}).

These examples cover a wide variety of mirror maps which are widely used in real practice.

Linear preconditioners

When
\phi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{B^{-1}}^{2}

Proof of Proposition

Proposition D.1

(Dual relative smoothness and convexity of norm-polynomials)
.

Let
\phi,\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex polynomials of the norm:

\phi(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}a_{i}\|x\|^{p_{i}},\qquad\psi(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}b_{j}\|x\|^{q_{j}},

where
2\leq p_{0}<p_{1}<\dots<p_{m}
,
2\leq q_{0}<q_{1}<\dots<q_{n}
, and
a_{i},b_{j}>0
. If
p_{m}>q_{n}
,
then for any
R>0
, there exists
L>0
such that for all
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|y_{1}\|,\|y_{2}\|\geq R
,

D_{\phi^{*}}(y_{1},y_{2})\leq LD_{\psi^{*}}(y_{1},y_{2}).

An intuitive interpretation of Proposition
D.1
is that the dual relative smoothness and convexity of norm-polynomials at the neighborhood of infinity is determined by the highest order term. The proof is done by identifying the leading terms of the convex conjugates.

Before proceeding to the proof, we first give some remarks on the proposition.

Remark D.2
.

Analogously, if
p_{0}<q_{0}
, then for any
R>0
, there exists
\mu>0
such that for all
y_{1},y_{2}
with
\|y_{1}\|,\|y_{2}\|\leq R
,

D_{\phi^{*}}(y_{1},y_{2})\geq\mu D_{\psi^{*}}(y_{1},y_{2}).

Remark D.3
.

Reversing the roles of
\phi
and
\psi
, if
p_{m}<q_{n}
, then for any
R>0
, there exists
\mu>0
such that for all
y_{1},y_{2}
with
\|y_{1}\|,\|y_{2}\|\geq R
,

D_{\psi^{*}}(y_{1},y_{2})\leq\frac{1}{\mu}D_{\phi^{*}}(y_{1},y_{2}).

Combining the two arguments, if
p_{m}=q_{n}
, then
\phi^{*}
and
\psi^{*}
are dual relatively smooth and strongly convex with respect to each other on the domain
\{y:\|y\|\geq R\}
for any

.

The proof of the proposition is given below. It relies on the asymptotic behavior of the convex conjugate of a norm-polynomial, which is also a norm-polynomial with the highest order term determined by the highest order term of the original function.

Proof.

Let
\phi(x)=c\|x\|^{p_{m}}+o(\|x\|^{p_{m}})
with
c>0
and
p_{m}>1
. Its convex conjugate is

\phi^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big\{\langle y,x\rangle-\phi(x)\big\}.

For large

y

x=t\frac{y}{\|y\|}
with

\phi^{*}(y)=\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\{t\|y\|-ct^{p_{m}}+o(t^{p_{m}})\right\}.

Maximizing

yields
t_{*}=\left(\frac{\|y\|}{cp_{m}}\right)^{1/(p_{m}-1)}
, and hence when
\|y\|\to\infty

\phi^{*}(y)=\left(\frac{p_{m}-1}{p_{m}}\right)(cp_{m})^{-1/(p_{m}-1)}\|y\|^{p_{m}/(p_{m}-1)}\big(1+o(1)\big).

Analogously, we have

\psi^{*}(y)=\left(\frac{q_{n}-1}{q_{n}}\right)(dq_{n})^{-1/(q_{n}-1)}\|y\|^{q_{n}/(q_{n}-1)}\big(1+o(1)\big).

Since
p_{m}>q_{n}
, we have
\frac{p_{m}}{p_{m}-1}<\frac{q_{n}}{q_{n}-1}
. Therefore, by comparing the leading terms of
\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}

\nabla^{2}\psi^{*}

Appendix F
Boundedness of iterates

In this section, we show that the iterates of the proposed method are bounded under mild assumptions. We first show that the iterates are bounded unconditionally in the continuous time limit, and then we show that the iterates of the discrete time method are also bounded by approximating the discrete time dynamics with the continuous time dynamics. The main idea is a new Lyapunov function

\mathcal{E}(x,y)=f(x)-f(x^{\star})+\mu D_{\phi}(x,y).

(50)

Different from (
10
), the
y
-part of the Lyapunov function is the Bregman divergence between
x
and
y
, which is computably tractable.

F.1
Continuous time analysis

Consider the continuous time dynamics

x^{\prime}=y-x,

and

(\nabla\phi(y))^{\prime}=\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(y)-\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(x).

Compared to the flow (
9
) in the main text, the flow here does not have the extra gradient descent term. This term is mainly used for balancing the positive terms produced by the discretization, and will be added back in the discrete time analysis. We have the following result.

Theorem F.1
.

Consider the continuous time dynamics defined above. The Lyapunov function defined in (
50
) satisfies the exact identity

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=-2\mu\,\langle\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(y),x-y\rangle.

Therefore,
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\leq 0
and the iterates are bounded.

Proof.

First,

\frac{d}{dt}f(x)=\langle\nabla f(x),x^{\prime}\rangle=\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle.

Now differentiate the Bregman divergence:

D_{\phi}(x,y)=\phi(x)-\phi(y)-\langle\nabla\phi(y),x-y\rangle.

Using the chain rule,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}D_{\phi}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla\phi(x),x^{\prime}\rangle-\langle\nabla\phi(y),y^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle-\left\langle(\nabla\phi(y))^{\prime},x-y\right\rangle-\left\langle\nabla\phi(y),x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}\right\rangle.

The
y^{\prime}
-terms cancel:

-\langle\nabla\phi(y),y^{\prime}\rangle+\langle\nabla\phi(y),y^{\prime}\rangle=0,

so

\frac{d}{dt}D_{\phi}(x,y)=\langle\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(y),x^{\prime}\rangle-\left\langle(\nabla\phi(y))^{\prime},x-y\right\rangle.

Substitute
x^{\prime}=y-x
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}D_{\phi}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(y),y-x\rangle

\displaystyle-\left\langle\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(y)-\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(x),x-y\right\rangle.

Since
y-x=-(x-y)
,

\langle\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(y),y-x\rangle=-\langle\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(y),x-y\rangle.

Hence

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}D_{\phi}(x,y)

\displaystyle=-2\langle\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(y),x-y\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\mu}\langle\nabla f(x),x-y\rangle.

Multiplying by
\mu
,

\mu\frac{d}{dt}D_{\phi}(x,y)=-2\mu\langle\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(y),x-y\rangle+\langle\nabla f(x),x-y\rangle.

Finally,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+\mu\frac{d}{dt}D_{\phi}(x,y)

\displaystyle=-\langle\nabla f(x),x-y\rangle+\langle\nabla f(x),x-y\rangle

\displaystyle-2\mu\langle\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(y),x-y\rangle.

Therefore,

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=-2\mu\,\langle\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(y),x-y\rangle.

By convexity of
\phi
,

\langle\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(y),x-y\rangle\geq 0,

so

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\leq 0.

If
\phi
is strictly convex, then
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=0
iff
x=y
.
∎

F.2
Discrete time analysis

Now, we analyze the discrete time method. The main idea is to approximate the discrete time dynamics with the continuous time dynamics, and then use the boundedness of the continuous time dynamics to show that the iterates of the discrete time method are also bounded. We have the following result.

Theorem F.2
.

Consider the iterative scheme

\displaystyle x_{k+1}-x_{k}

\displaystyle=\alpha(y_{k}-x_{k+1})-\frac{1}{L}\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla f(x_{k})),

\displaystyle\nabla\phi(y_{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y_{k})

\displaystyle=\alpha\big(\nabla\phi(x_{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y_{k+1})\big)-\frac{\alpha}{\mu}\nabla f(x_{k+1}).

Then the Lyapunov function (
50
) satisfies the exact identity

\mathcal{E}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}=-\frac{1}{L}D_{\phi^{*}}(0,\nabla f(x_{k}))+O(\mu).

Therefore, choosing
\mu
sufficiently small, we have

\mathcal{E}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}\leq 0.

Proof.

Expand the Lyapunov difference at
z_{k+1}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}={}

\displaystyle\alpha\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(z_{k+1}),\,\mathcal{G}(z_{k+1})\rangle+\alpha\langle\nabla f(x_{k+1})+\mu(\nabla\phi(x_{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y_{k+1})),\,y_{k}-y_{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle-\frac{1}{L}\langle\nabla f(x_{k+1})+\mu(\nabla\phi(x_{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y_{k+1})),\,\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla f(x_{k}))\rangle

\displaystyle-D_{f}(x_{k},x_{k+1})-\mu(D_{\phi}(x_{k},y_{k})-D_{\phi}(x_{k},y_{k+1})+D_{\phi}(x_{k},x_{k+1})

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle+\langle\nabla^{2}\phi(y_{k+1})(x_{k+1}-y_{k+1}),y_{k}-y_{k+1}\rangle).

The last term is the Bregman divergence of
H(x,y):=D_{\phi}(x,y)
:

\displaystyle D_{H}(z_{k},z_{k+1})

\displaystyle=H(z_{k})-H(z_{k+1})-\langle\nabla H(z_{k+1}),z_{k}-z_{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle=D_{\phi}(x_{k},y_{k})-D_{\phi}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})

\displaystyle-\langle\nabla\phi(x_{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y_{k+1}),\,x_{k}-x_{k+1}\rangle-\langle\nabla^{2}\phi(y_{k+1})(x_{k+1}-y_{k+1}),y_{k}-y_{k+1}\rangle

and 3-point identity for Bregman divergences gives

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\phi(x_{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y_{k+1}),\,x_{k}-x_{k+1}\rangle=D_{\phi}(x_{k},\,y_{k+1})-D_{\phi}(x_{k},\,x_{k+1})-D_{\phi}(x_{k+1},\,y_{k+1}).

We bound each term in the above expression. First, we have

\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(z_{k+1}),\,\mathcal{G}(z_{k+1})\rangle=-2\mu\langle\nabla\phi(x_{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y_{k+1}),\,x_{k+1}-y_{k+1}\rangle=2\mu D_{\phi}^{\text{sym}}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1}).

Next, we assume the following inequality holds:

\langle\nabla f(x_{k+1}),\,y_{k}-y_{k+1}\rangle\leq\frac{1}{L}D_{\phi^{*}}(\nabla f(x_{k+1}),0)+\mu D_{\phi}(y_{k+1},y_{k}).

Then, by the 3-point identity for Bregman divergences

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f(x_{k+1}),\,\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla f(x_{k}))\rangle

\displaystyle=D_{\phi^{*}}\!\left(0,\,\nabla f(x_{k})\right)+D_{\phi^{*}}\!\left(0,\,\nabla f(x_{k+1})\right)-D_{\phi^{*}}\!\left(\nabla f(x_{k+1}),\,\nabla f(x_{k})\right).

By dual relative smoothness, we have

D_{\phi^{*}}\!\left(\nabla f(x_{k+1}),\,\nabla f(x_{k})\right)\leq LD_{\phi}(x_{k},x_{k+1}).

Combining the above estimates, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}

\displaystyle\leq-2\alpha\mu D_{\phi}^{\text{sym}}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})

\displaystyle+\mu\langle\nabla\phi(x_{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y_{k+1}),\,y_{k}-y_{k+1}-\frac{1}{L}\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla f(x_{k}))\rangle

\displaystyle+\mu(D_{\phi}(x_{k},y_{k+1})-D_{\phi}(x_{k},y_{k})-D_{\phi}(x_{k},x_{k+1})+D_{\phi}(y_{k+1},y_{k}))

\displaystyle-\mu\langle\nabla^{2}\phi(y_{k+1})(x_{k+1}-y_{k+1}),y_{k}-y_{k+1}\rangle-\frac{1}{L}D_{\phi^{*}}(0,\nabla f(x_{k})).

The second line can be simplified using the 3-point identity for Bregman divergences:

\displaystyle D_{\phi}(x_{k},y_{k+1})-D_{\phi}(x_{k},y_{k})-D_{\phi}(x_{k},x_{k+1})+D_{\phi}(y_{k+1},y_{k})

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle=\langle\nabla\phi(y_{k})-\nabla\phi(y_{k+1}),\,x_{k}-y_{k+1}\rangle-D_{\phi}(x_{k},x_{k+1})

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}

\displaystyle\leq-2\alpha\mu D_{\phi}^{\text{sym}}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})

\displaystyle+\mu\langle\nabla\phi(x_{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y_{k+1})-\nabla^{2}\phi(y_{k+1})(x_{k+1}-y_{k+1}),\,y_{k}-y_{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle+\mu\langle\nabla\phi(y_{k})-\nabla\phi(y_{k+1}),\,x_{k}-y_{k+1}\rangle-\mu D_{\phi}(x_{k},x_{k+1})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{L}D_{\phi^{*}}(0,\nabla f(x_{k}))-\frac{\mu}{L}\langle\nabla\phi(x_{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y_{k+1}),\,\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla f(x_{k}))\rangle.

To approximate the above expression, we linearize differences of
\nabla\phi
:

\displaystyle\nabla\phi(x_{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y_{k+1})=\nabla^{2}\phi(\xi_{k+1})(x_{k+1}-y_{k+1}),

\displaystyle\nabla\phi(y_{k})-\nabla\phi(y_{k+1})=\nabla^{2}\phi(\zeta_{k+1})(y_{k}-y_{k+1}),

and thus

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\phi(x_{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y_{k+1})-\nabla^{2}\phi(y_{k+1})(x_{k+1}-y_{k+1}),\,y_{k}-y_{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\langle(\nabla^{2}\phi(\xi_{k+1})-\nabla^{2}\phi(y_{k+1}))(x_{k+1}-y_{k+1}),\,y_{k}-y_{k+1}\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\phi(y_{k})-\nabla\phi(y_{k+1}),\,x_{k}-y_{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\langle y_{k}-y_{k+1},\,\nabla^{2}\phi(\zeta_{k+1})(x_{k}-y_{k+1})\rangle.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}

\displaystyle\leq-2\alpha\mu D_{\phi}^{\text{sym}}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})

\displaystyle+\mu\langle\nabla\phi(x_{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y_{k+1})-\nabla^{2}\phi(y_{k+1})(x_{k+1}-y_{k+1}),\,y_{k}-y_{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle+\mu\langle\nabla^{2}\phi(\zeta_{k+1})(y_{k}-y_{k+1}),\,x_{k}-y_{k+1}\rangle-\mu D_{\phi}(x_{k},x_{k+1})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{L}D_{\phi^{*}}(0,\nabla f(x_{k}))-\frac{\mu}{L}\langle\nabla^{2}\phi(\xi_{k+1})(x_{k+1}-y_{k+1}),\,\nabla\phi^{*}(\nabla f(x_{k}))\rangle.

∎
