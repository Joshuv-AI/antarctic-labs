Association between occupational risk factors and lung function among small-scale gold miners in Mongolia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Densenbal Dansran, Odgerel Chimed-ochir, Ichinnorov Dashtseren, Solongo Bandi, Garamjav Khishigdavaa, Tomohiro Ishimaru, Naidansuren Tsendeekhuu, Allabyergyen Myeryemkhaan, Batmunkh Batsan, Ankhil Lkhagvasuren, Amarbuyan Algaa, Ekaterina Fearmark, Jun Pyo Myong, Yasuo Morimoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to assess the association between occupational risk factors and lung function among gold miners in a northern Mongolian province.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 143 gold miners in July 2024. Data were collected through structured interviews, physical examinations, chest X-rays, and spirometry. Occupational exposures included workplace type (administrative, factory, or underground), dust exposure level (low, moderate, high, based on work duration and underground working hours), and N95 mask use. Lung function outcomes were assessed using forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1), evaluated both both as an absolute value (liters) and as impaired lung function, defined by FEV1 <80% of predicted. Multivariable logistic regression was used to examine associations with impaired lung function, and linear regression was applied to assess determinants of absolute FEV1, adjusting for age, sex, and smoking status.ResultsAmong 143 workers, mean FEV1 was 3.39 L; and 23.8% had FEV1 <80% of predicted. No occupational exposure was significantly associated with impaired lung function (FEV1 <80%). However, underground work was significantly associated with reduced absolute FEV1 (β = −0.22 L; p=0.026). N95 mask use was not significantly associated with impaired lung function after adjustment.ConclusionsEarly lung function decline was not captured by the standard threshold. Therefore, monitoring absolute FEV1 may offer a more sensitive measure of early pulmonary changes among miners. Regular health monitoring and enhanced protective measures are urgently needed to prevent occupational lung diseases in resource-limited mining settings, including Mongolia and other developing countries.

Introduction

The modern mining industry in Mongolia officially began on December 25, 1922, with the operation of the Nalaikh coal deposit, the country’s first official mine. A major turning point occurred in the 1990s, when Mongolia transitioned from socialism to democracy and a free-market economy, marking a new era of rapid mining development1). Over the past three decades, gold mining has become one of the country’s most important industries, contributing significantly to foreign exchange reserves and export revenues. Mongolia ranks first in the world in terms of gold reserves and production. Since 1992, the gold production and mining in Mongolia has dramatically increased and the gold purchase by the central bank grew 10 times in the last decade2).

As of 2022, Mongolia had 554 valid gold mining licenses, with 462 for placer mines and 92 for hard rock deposits. A significant portion of Mongolia’s gold production comes from artisanal and small-scale miners, with an estimated 40,000 to 60,000 people engaged in this activity across 14 out of 21 provinces3). However, this sector presents significant occupational health challenges. Miners in these settings are exposed to high levels of dust, gas, and other hazardous substances, often under rotating shifts and physically demanding workloads4,5,6). They often operate in dangerous conditions with limited use of personal protective equipment (PPE), poor access to healthcare, and insufficient insurance coverage7,8). Even when healthcare is technically available, many miners perceive travel to distant clinics as a waste of time. Moreover, small-scale companies rarely conduct regular health checkups for their workers.

Chronic exposure to dust can impair respiratory function and lead to long-term health conditions, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, lung cancer, and pneumoconiosis4,9). Pneumoconiosis accounts for 53.8% of all occupational disability cases10). In Mongolia, between 1980 and 2006, a study found that pneumoconiosis reduced miners’ quality of life by 45%11). These findings underscore the importance of regular monitoring of lung function to detect abnormalities early and prevent further complications.

Lung function is known to decline with age and varies by anthropometric characteristics, such as age, height, and weight12). However, for mining workers, lung function may decline more rapidly due to dust exposures, beyond the normal decrease associated with aging and body composition. Spirometry is widely used to assess pulmonary function. Yet, even when results are near the lower limit of normal, individuals are often classified as “normal,” potentially masking early signs of respiratory impairment13). Therefore, it is crucial to regularly monitor the trajectory of lung function over time, not merely to check whether levels have crossed into the abnormal range, especially among mining workers. Several international studies have examined the relationship between dusty workplaces and lung function among small-scale gold miners, particularly in low-income countries14,15,16). However, no such studies are currently available in Mongolia. Given the country’s unique mining practices, environmental conditions, and healthcare context, exposure levels and lung function outcomes may differ significantly from those observed elsewhere. Therefore, the aim of this study is to assess the correlation between occupational risks, in particular work type, use of PPE, and dust exposure, and pulmonary function among small-scale gold miners in Mongolia. Our findings will provide much-needed localized data to inform occupational health guidelines and contribute to global understanding of lung health in artisanal mining — filling a critical knowledge gap in Central Asia.

Methods

Study setting, design, and subjects

This cross-sectional study was conducted in July 2024 as part of The Mongolian Miners’ Health Study: An International Collaborative Initiative at a gold mining company located in Mongolia’s northern province. Mongolia’s known gold deposits fall into three economic categories: veins, mineralized zones, and placers. The mine involved in this study is a high-grade, narrow-vein gold operation, with gold content in quartz veins ranging from 0.1 g/ton to 746 g/ton1).

A total of 477 workers were employed at the mining site at the time of study. Of these, 147 workers (30.9%) were on duty during the shift when the research team conducted interviews and health examinations. Among them, 143 workers with complete data on lung function tests and work history were included in the final analysis, yielding a response rate of 97.3%. Due to time constraints and logistical challenges, including the remote location of the mining site, which made repeated visits during subsequent shifts impractical, the study was restricted to a single work shift. Consequently, participants were recruited using a convenience sampling approach.

Data collection and measurement

Data were collected through structured interviews using a pre-administered questionnaire, physical examination conducted by pulmonologists, medical imaging, and lung function testing via spirometry. Due to time and logistical constraints, we conducted a cross-sectional survey among workers present during a single scheduled shift at the gold mining site.

The questionnaire consisted of 43 items, organized into six main domains: (1) general information, including demographic information; (2) occupational risk; (3) injury-related information; (4) health-related quality of life; (5) lifestyle factors; and (6) additional information. The physical examination included a thorough respiratory assessment, with anamnesis conducted to collect participants’ current complaints. Chest X-rays were performed for all participants to evaluate structural changes in the lungs, while spirometry tests were conducted to assess lung function.

In the current analysis, we extracted data on demographic characteristics, occupational risk factors, and lung function. Occupational risk factors served as exposure variables and included work duration, daily working hours, workplace type, and the use of PPE. Workplace type was categorized by comparing participants working in factory and underground (undermining) environments to those in administrative roles. Work duration was categorized into two groups: up to 2 years or more than 2 years. Exposure intensity was defined by underground work hours and categorized as up to 6.0 hours or more than 6.0 hours per day. Based on the combination of work duration and intensity, participants were further grouped into three levels of dust exposure: low, medium, and high (Figure 1). PPE usage was defined strictly as the use of N95 dust-proof masks; other types of masks, including gas masks, surgical masks, cotton fabric masks, neck gaiters, and neck cloths, were excluded from this definition.Fig. 1. Demographic variables and smoking status were extracted as confounding variables. Participants who had smoked within the past 10 years were classified as “smokers,” while those who had never smoked or had quit at least 6 months prior were classified as “non-smokers.”

The lung function was assessed using spirometry, a widely accepted method for diagnosing and monitoring lung function. We used the Medikro-Pro, a computer-based spirometer compliant with the American Thoracic Society (ATS) Standards (2019). The spirometry procedure adhered strictly to ATS guidelines, including patient preparation, assessment, and interpretation of results17,18). During spirometry, participants were seated, with feet flat on the floor in a relaxed environment. The spirometry software required the following inputs: participant identification number, name, age, sex, ethnicity (categorized as “Chinese” in the system), address, height, weight, smoking status, and relevant medical history. Participants were informed of the test purpose and given standardized instructions. A disposable mouthpiece flowmeter and a nose clip were used. Each participant performed up to three spirometry attempts unless a valid result was obtained in the first attempt.

We assessed lung function using two FEV1 measurements: absolute FEV1 in liters (L), representing the volume of air forcefully exhaled in 1 second; and FEV1 categorized as normal or abnormal based on the percentage of the predicted value, with FEV1 considered normal if it exceeded 80% of the predicted value19).

Statistical analysis

First, study participants were descriptively analyzed by demographic characteristics and occupational risk factors. Mean FEV1 (in liters) and the proportion of participants with normal FEV1 values were reported across these variables.

To examine the relationship between demographic and occupational factors and lung function, two statistical approaches were applied: multivariable logistic regression and linear regression.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess the association between occupational and demographic factors and the likelihood of having abnormal lung function, defined as FEV1 <80% of the predicted value. The dependent variable in this model was binary: 0 = normal FEV1 (≥80%) and 1 = abnormal FEV1 (<80%). Results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Linear regression analysis was conducted to explore the relationship between occupational and demographic variables and absolute FEV1 values (in liters), providing a continuous measure of lung function. Results from the linear regression were expressed as regression coefficients with 95% CIs, quantifying the associations between predictors and FEV1.

For both logistic and linear regression analyses, three models were constructed: i) model 1 with only occupational exposure variables (eg, dust exposure level, PPE use, and workplace type); and ii) model 2 with occupational exposure variables plus demographic factors (eg, age and sex) and smoking status. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

To assess the robustness of the models, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated to detect multicollinearity among the predictor variables. No significant multicollinearity was observed.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Medical Research of the Mongolian National University of Medical Sciences (2022/3-06) and the Ethics Committee of the University of Occupational and Environmental Health, Japan (ER24-005). Participation in this study was voluntary and non-coercive. Workers were informed that refusal to participate would not result in any penalty, disadvantage, or change in employment conditions. All participants provided written informed consent after reviewing the study details.

Most participants were male (86.7%), and males had a higher mean FEV1 (mean 3.52; standard deviation [SD], 0.68 L) compared to females (mean 2.55; SD, 0.53 L). The proportion of participants with impaired lung function (FEV1 <80% of predicted) was also higher among females (36.8%) than males (21.8%).

The youngest age group (20–29 years), comprising 39.8% of the sample, had the highest mean FEV1 at 3.67 (SD, 0.72) L, with 19.3% of individuals in this group exhibiting FEV1 <80%. In contrast, the oldest group (40–59 years, 20.9% of the sample) had the lowest mean FEV1 (3.05; SD, 0.67 L), although the proportion with FEV1 <80% (23.3%) was slightly lower than in the 30–39 age group.

Regarding occupational risk factors, 76.2% of participants reported wearing N95 masks. These individuals had a higher mean FEV1 (3.43; SD, 0.68 L) compared to those who did not use N95 masks (mean 3.27; SD, 0.90 L). Similarly, the prevalence of impaired lung function was lower among mask users (19.4%) than non-users (36.1%).

In terms of dust exposure, 37.1% of workers were categorized as having low exposure, 41.3% as moderate, and 21.7% as high exposure. The three groups had similar mean FEV1 values, but the percentage of participants with FEV1 <80% varied: 26.4% in the low exposure group, 23.7% in the moderate group, and 19.4% in the high exposure group.

Nearly half of the participants (48.9%) were underground workers. Factory workers had a slightly higher mean FEV1 (3.45 L; SD, 0.65 L) and a lower proportion of abnormal FEV1 (18.2%) compared to those in other work environments.

Discussion

This study examined the association between occupational risks such as work type, N95 respirator use, working duration and intensity and pulmonary function among 143 workers employed in a gold mining company in a Northern province of Mongolia. Pulmonary function was evaluated using two measures: the percentage of predicted FEV1, where values below 80% are considered abnormal, and the absolute FEV1 in liters. The findings did not reveal any association between the percentage of predicted FEV1 and occupational risks. However, we observed a significant decline in absolute FEV1 among underground miners compared to workers at other sites. This result highlights the potential for early, subtle changes in lung function among underground miners that may not yet meet the threshold for “abnormality” based on percentage-predicted FEV1. Consequently, monitoring absolute FEV1 may be a more sensitive approach to detecting early lung function deterioration in occupational settings.

In parallel, radiographic findings from same study population have been reported separately20), demonstrating a silicosis prevalence of 16.3% and significant associations with occupational exposures, particularly high dust exposure and engagement in blasting-related tasks. These findings provide important context for interpreting the present results. While chest X-rays capture structural lung abnormalities associated with silica exposure, spirometry reflects functional impairment. The discrepancy between the strong exposure–disease associations observed in radiographic outcomes and the weaker associations with FEV1-based outcomes in this study suggests that structural changes may occur earlier or be more readily detectable than measurable functional decline.

The significance of this finding is supported by previous studies. Research has shown that miners with initially comparable mean FEV1 levels to reference groups often experience higher rates of FEV1 decline during their working careers, later reporting increased rates of respiratory symptoms and illnesses19,21). Bhakta et al. underscored the limitations of evaluating lung function solely on percentage-predicted FEV1, noting that this approach may overlook gradual declines in absolute FEV122). Additionally, follow-up studies among coal miners revealed that those with declining FEV1 exhibited significantly greater airway resistance and air trapping, further linking these changes to long-term respiratory issues23). Community-based studies have also documented a relationship between lower FEV1 levels and increased mortality risk23,24). Collectively, these findings emphasize the importance of absolute FEV1 as a critical measure for early detection of lung function changes, even before clinical symptoms arise.

Our study reinforces the necessity of regular health monitoring for miners, particularly those working underground, who are at heightened risk of gradual lung function decline. Unfortunately, routine follow-up is often lacking in Mongolia, where mid-sized mining companies typically do not employ occupational health professionals, and workers seek medical care only when symptoms significantly impact their daily lives. Health screenings are often limited to external projects rather than forming part of routine health protocols. This gap in regular monitoring presents a significant challenge for the early detection and prevention of occupational lung diseases. Our findings call for mining companies to prioritize routine health assessments and adopt proactive measures to safeguard the health and well-being of their workforce.

The study also examined the role of N95 respirator use in mitigating lung function decline. Although non-use of N95 respirators was associated with higher odds of impaired lung function (adjusted OR 2.57), this association did not reach statistical significance after adjustment. This finding contradicts with many studies showing that N95 respirators are significantly associated with preventing occupational lung disease including silicosis25). Therefore, the current findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the study may have been underpowered to detect this effect. The exposure distribution was highly imbalanced, with 76.2% of participants reporting N95 use, and the total number of abnormal lung function events was limited. Such exposure skewness increases standard errors, resulting in wider confidence intervals and higher p-values. Therefore, the absence of statistical significance does not necessarily indicate the absence of a clinically meaningful association26). The magnitude of the point estimate suggests a potentially important protective role of N95 respirators, supporting continued promotion of appropriate respiratory protection in mining settings. In addition, the lack of significance could stem from improper mask usage, poor fitness, misreporting, or low-quality masks, since the effectiveness of N95 respirators depends on proper fit, consistent usage, and regular maintenance27). Therefore, the quality of masks must be assured and improper use of mask needs to be checked. Globally, including in Mongolia, workers often face barriers to consistent respirator use, such as physical discomfort, limited awareness of dust exposure risks, and the absence of stringent PPE policies28,29). To address these issues, mining companies should ensure the provision of high-quality masks and enforce strict PPE usage policies. Training programs that educate workers about dust exposure risks and proper respirator use are essential.

Regarding the dust exposure defined based on combination of working duration and intensity working in their own worksite, although the average FEV1 appeared slightly higher in the medium and high dust-exposure groups compared to the low exposure group, this difference was not statistically significant. This unexpected trend may reflect random variation or possible selection factors, such as the healthy worker effect, where individuals with better baseline lung function are more likely to remain in or be assigned to more intensive work. However, given the lack of statistical significance, no definitive conclusion can be drawn, and further research with larger sample sizes and more precise exposure assessment is warranted.

This study has several strengths and limitations. One notable strength is the ability to collect data from a mid-sized mining company, despite the general reluctance of such companies to disclose workers’ health information due to concerns about their reputation. By engaging with the company, the study was able to highlight the importance of regular health monitoring, conveying a crucial message to company managers and health professionals about the benefits of consistent follow-ups in lung function rather than relying on abnormality of lung function via sporadic health checks. However, as a cross-sectional study, our findings are limited to a snapshot of the current situation and cannot establish causality between exposure and lung function outcomes. Additionally, the relatively small sample size of 143 workers may have reduced the study’s statistical power, limiting the precision of results and their generalizability to other mining populations. Future research with larger sample sizes is necessary to confirm these findings and improve their applicability to broader worker populations. In the future, there is a need for larger studies to confirm the findings and improve generalizability.

Conclusion

Our findings contribute to the growing body of evidence that questions the sole reliance on percentage-predicted FEV1 as a measure of lung function. While this metric remains the standard for diagnosing pulmonary dysfunction, it may fail to capture the full spectrum of occupational risks faced by miners. Incorporating absolute FEV1 measurements offers a more sensitive approach for detecting early changes in lung function. Furthermore, transitioning underground miners to less hazardous work environments based on early detection of lung function decline could help prevent occupational lung diseases. Mining companies, particularly in resource-limited settings like Mongolia, must take proactive measures to implement routine health monitoring and protective strategies to safeguard workers’ respiratory health.
