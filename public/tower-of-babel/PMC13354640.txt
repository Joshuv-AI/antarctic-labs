Psychiatric Symptoms Associated with Corticosteroid Use: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Keisuke Kusudo, Yuki Mashima, Hideaki Yasuda, Teruomi Iyo, Hiroyoshi Takeuchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveCorticosteroids (CSs) are widely prescribed but can induce psychiatric adverse effects (depressive, manic, psychotic, and anxiety symptoms). This systematic review aimed to quantify the association between CS use and psychiatric symptoms and to explore potential moderators across clinical contexts.MethodsWe conducted a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)-guided systematic review and meta-analysis. MEDLINE and Embase were searched through 10 October, 2025, and 19 hand-searched records were also screened. We included observational studies in medical CS users and excluded sex steroids, neonatal exposure, prescription-only outcomes, purely objective outcomes, sleep/cognition/delirium-focused studies, CS deficiency, withdrawal, and studies confounded by co-medications; randomized controlled trials were excluded by design. Two reviewers screened studies, extracted data, and assessed risk of bias with the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Certainty of evidence for each pooled outcome was additionally evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach. Pairwise meta-analyses pooled standardized mean differences and odds ratios using random-effects models; I2 quantified heterogeneity. The acute phase was operationally defined as ≤ 8 weeks after CS initiation. An exploratory meta-regression across six studies (seven groups) examined dose, duration, cumulative dose, age, and sex.ResultsSeventy-three studies (total N = 3,759,659) were included (22 cross-sectional, 31 cohort, 12 pre-post, 2 case-control, 2 registry, and 4 prospective patient-as-own-control investigations comparing outcomes during on-CS vs off-CS periods). Compared with non-users, CS use was associated with higher depressive symptom scores (standardized mean difference = 0.92, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.40–1.45; p < 0.001; I2 = 89%; seven studies; n = 1403; eight groups). During the operationally defined acute phase (≤ 8 weeks after CS initiation), manic symptoms were more frequent than depressive symptoms (six studies; n = 1086; odds ratio = 2.42, 95% CI 1.48–3.97; p < 0.001; I2 = 0%). A single-arm meta-analysis estimated psychotic symptoms in 2.4% of CS users (95% CI 0.76–7.26; six studies; n = 1206). Anxiety showed no significant association with CS use (standardized mean difference = 0.13, 95% CI − 0.24 to 0.51; p = 0.26; I2 = 7%; three studies; n = 1251). Overall study quality (Newcastle-Ottawa Scale) was generally moderate to high, whereas certainty of evidence according to GRADE was generally low to very low across pooled outcomes. The exploratory meta-regression suggested that higher daily dose and older age were associated with greater depressive severity; by contrast, cumulative dose, treatment duration, and sex showed no significant associations, and these moderator findings remain exploratory.ConclusionsCorticosteroid use is associated with more severe depressive symptoms, and manic symptoms may predominate during acute treatment; psychotic reactions are uncommon. Dose and age may modulate depressive severity, but these moderator findings should be interpreted cautiously. Routine monitoring for mood and psychotic changes, especially early after initiation and in higher-dose or older patients, is warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40263-026-01298-5.

Key Points

Corticosteroid use is associated with more severe depressive symptoms, while manic symptoms occur more often than depressive symptoms during acute corticosteroid treatment.Psychotic symptoms occur in approximately 2% of corticosteroid users, whereas anxiety symptoms are not significantly associated with corticosteroid use.Higher dose and older age may increase the risk of depressive symptoms, but this finding is based on a limited body of evidence and remains exploratory.

Introduction

Corticosteroids (CSs) are widely used in the treatment of autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [1], allergic disorders such as asthma [2], and malignancies including leukemia [3]. However, the adverse effects associated with CSs frequently complicate clinical decision making. Physical side effects encompass a wide range of issues, such as hypertension, hyperglycemia, gastric ulcers, weight gain, immunosuppression, cutaneous adverse events, and osteonecrosis [4]. Additionally, CSs can induce psychiatric adverse effects through their actions on the central nervous system [5]. These psychiatric symptoms are extensive and include insomnia, anxiety, depression, mania, suicidal ideation, cognitive impairment, and delirium [6, 7]. Several biological mechanisms have been proposed to explain these effects. Dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, impaired neuroplasticity in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, and alterations in neurotransmitter systems such as serotonin, dopamine, and GABA are thought to play important roles [8–10]. Furthermore, the immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory properties of CSs may indirectly contribute to the emergence of psychiatric symptoms [9, 11]. Management of CS-induced psychiatric symptoms typically involves dose reduction or the use of psychotropic medications; however, these strategies often complicate medical management and may hinder appropriate treatment [12].

The incidence of psychiatric side effects associated with CS use varies widely across studies [13]. The risk of developing these symptoms primarily depends on factors such as dose, duration of treatment, and patient age. With regard to dosage, a dose–response relationship has been established for psychiatric adverse effects. Although the evidence underlying this relationship derives primarily from studies published over 50 years ago [14], the findings that minimal risk is associated with doses < 40 mg/day, moderate risk with 40–80 mg/day, and high risk with doses > 80 mg/day have been widely accepted as the standard understanding [15, 16]. Psychiatric symptoms typically emerge within 1–2 weeks after initiation of CS treatment, suggesting a heightened risk during the immediate post-initiation period [12]. Mania is commonly reported early after treatment initiation, indicating that the duration of treatment may influence psychiatric symptomatology [17]. Additionally, age plays a significant role, with older men being more susceptible to delirium, confusion, disorientation, and mania, whereas younger patients are at higher risk of suicide or suicide attempt [7]. These data highlight the importance of considering multiple factors in evaluating the psychiatric symptoms associated with CS treatment.

However, previous studies have encountered challenges because of variability in patient populations, differing methods of psychiatric symptom assessment, and difficulties in establishing appropriate control groups, complicating a comprehensive risk assessment for CS treatment [13, 18]. Furthermore, analyses accounting for duration-dependent changes in psychiatric symptoms and moderators associated with psychiatric symptomatology have been limited [13]. A recent systematic review provided important evidence on the prevalence of psychiatric symptoms including depression, mania, anxiety, and delirium among glucocorticoid users. However, heterogeneity in study design and limited analysis of symptom timing and moderating factors restricted the interpretability of its findings [13].

Therefore, this study aimed to synthesize existing real-world knowledge about CS-associated psychiatric symptoms through a systematic review and meta-analysis. Specifically, the study aimed to comprehensively profile psychiatric symptoms by examining the effects of acute-phase CS treatment on psychiatric symptoms, exploring impacts on depressive symptoms, and analyzing potential moderators.

Methods

For our systematic review and meta-analysis, we searched MEDLINE (1946 to 23 June, 2021) and Embase (1947 to 23 June, 2021). We used the following search terms: (steroid* OR corticosteroid* OR glucocorticoid* OR hydrocortisone OR prednisolone OR dexamethasone OR methylprednisolone OR betamethasone OR triamcinolone OR prednisone OR adrenal cortex hormone*) (mental OR psych* OR hallucinat* OR delusion* OR paranoi* OR disorganiz* OR catatoni* OR mood OR depress* OR dysthymi* OR mania OR manic OR hypomani* OR affective* OR emoti* OR dysphori* OR anxi* OR panic OR obsessi* OR neurosis OR neuroti* OR irritab* OR aggress* OR excit* OR hostil* OR agitat* OR illusion* OR temper OR terror OR hysteri* OR frenz* OR craz* OR overactive OR formication OR behavior* OR outburst OR labil* OR elation OR elate* OR suicid* OR homicid* OR unipolar OR bipolar OR apath*). In the search strategy, we used the term “affective*” rather than “affect*.” This choice was made because “affect*” retrieves a large number of irrelevant records in which the word is used in its general sense (“to influence”), whereas “affective*” more specifically captures psychiatric terms such as “affective disorder” or “affective symptoms,” which were directly relevant to our research question. The search was limited to human studies and English-language publications. We updated the literature search on 10 October, 2025. In addition to the database search, we conducted a hand search consisting of backward citation searching of the included studies and of a prior meta-analysis [13], searching for peer-reviewed full-text publications corresponding to records retrieved as abstract only at full-text assessment, as well as targeted queries in Google Scholar.

Study Selection

Two reviewers (KK and YM) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved studies based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) studies investigating the administration of CSs as a treatment for physical illnesses, (2) studies assessing psychiatric symptoms associated with CS administration as medication, and (3) observational studies. Full-text articles were reviewed, and those meeting at least one of the following exclusion criteria were removed: (1) studies administering only sex steroids or mineralocorticoids; (2) studies involving prenatal or neonatal CS administration; (3) studies using the presence or absence of psychotropic medication prescriptions as an outcome; (4) studies relying solely on objective measures such as magnetic resonance imaging, electroencephalography, or physiological indicators as outcomes; (5) studies evaluating sleep, cognitive function, or delirium as primary outcomes because our study focused specifically on core psychiatric symptoms; (6) studies focusing on conditions related to CS deficiency (e.g., Addison’s disease); (7) studies using only descriptive assessments as outcomes; (8) studies assessing CS withdrawal symptoms; (9) studies in which concurrently administered drugs could also influence psychiatric outcomes; (10) studies available only as abstracts; and (11) case reports or case series. Delirium was excluded because it is strongly influenced by underlying medical conditions and systemic factors, making it difficult to distinguish CS-induced effects from those of comorbid illness. In addition, delirium is classified as a neurocognitive disorder in Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), Fifth Edition, characterized by an acute disturbance of consciousness and a fluctuating course, which is qualitatively distinct from the mood, psychotic, and anxiety symptom domains that constitute the focus of this review. We intentionally excluded randomized controlled trials (RCTs) for the purpose of summarizing them in a different study. If consensus could not be reached, the investigators (KK, YM, TI, HY, and HT) reviewed the full text, and disagreements were resolved through consensus meetings.

Data Extraction

Two investigators (KK and YM) extracted data independently. For the quantitative analysis, we extracted both dichotomous outcomes and continuous outcomes with their standard deviations for psychiatric symptoms. If the standard deviation was not given, we estimated it from the t value. When only the median and interquartile range were available, the mean and standard deviation were estimated using a method based on Box-Cox transformation implemented in the MASS package in R.

In the qualitative review, for cross-sectional studies and case-control studies, we collected the information on clinical and demographic characteristics (medical condition, total number of participants, number of male participants, mean age, and control group information), CS treatment (type, dose, administration route, and duration), evaluation (rating scales), and psychiatric symptoms (depression, mania, psychosis, anxiety, and other). For cohort studies and registry studies, we extracted similar information, along with the evaluation duration or timepoint and the time to symptom onset after CS initiation. In pre-post studies, we collected the same data excluding control group information, focusing on changes before and after CS administration. For studies with other designs, we extracted comparable data, including CS administration schedule instead of administration duration.

Risk of Bias and Certainty of Evidence Assessment

All studies included in the meta-analyses were rated independently by two of the three authors (KK, YM, and TI) using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for assessing risk of bias, using the adaptations for cohort [19] and cross-sectional studies [20, 21]. In addition, the certainty of evidence for each pooled outcome was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [22, 23]. Because only observational studies were included in this review, certainty started at low and was downgraded or upgraded according to standard GRADE domains. Downgrading was considered for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Upgrading was considered for a large magnitude of effect, a dose-response gradient, and situations in which plausible residual confounding would be expected to reduce an apparent association. Risk of bias judgments in the GRADE assessment were informed primarily by the NOS results. Publication bias was assessed only when a sufficient number of studies were available for a given meta-analysis.

Statistical Analysis

We performed the primary meta-analysis when three or more eligible studies were identified. Psychiatric symptoms were categorized into the following five categories, depending on the reported outcomes of each study: depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, psychotic symptoms, manic symptoms, and other symptoms. For the pairwise meta-analysis, we included studies that compared psychiatric symptoms between two distinct groups: individuals who were administered CSs for the treatment of a specific disease (referred to as the ‘CS group’) and those who, despite sharing a similar disease condition as the CS group, did not receive CS treatment (referred to as the ‘reference group’). For continuous outcomes, we pooled standardized mean differences (Hedges’ g) between the CS group and the reference group, and for dichotomous outcomes, we pooled the odds ratio (OR) between them. If studies reported both depressive and manic symptoms induced by CSs within the same cohort during the acute phase, we calculated the within-cohort OR comparing the odds of developing manic symptoms to the odds of developing depressive symptoms. Because both outcomes are derived from the same multinomial sample, we derived the sampling variance of the log OR using the delta method applied to the multinomial distribution, which accounts for the covariance between the two outcome counts (Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM]). The acute phase was operationally defined as the evaluation of psychiatric symptoms within 8 weeks of CS initiation. We adopted this 8-week window as a pragmatic clinical framework for the purposes of this review, referencing prior SLE literature on CS-induced psychiatric disorders and their clinical differentiation from neuropsychiatric SLE [24–26]. If studies reported multiple outcomes in the same symptom category, we employed a three-level random-effects model to pool correlated outcomes within the same study. The three-level structure of the data accounted for multiple estimates within each study (level 1), study-level estimates (level 2), and a pooled estimate across overall studies (level 3). To model this hierarchical structure, a generalized least-squares method was applied to aggregate correlated effect sizes. The between-study variance was estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood method. To calculate the study-level variance-covariance matrix, we assumed the within-study correlation coefficient to be 0.6 [27]. To ensure that the results did not depend on this correlation coefficient, we also we also conducted a sensitivity analysis assuming that the correlation was either 0.4 or 0.8. If studies of specific symptoms did not include a reference group, but provided quantitative data, we performed a single-arm meta-analysis using a generalized linear mixed model. We performed an exploratory meta-regression analysis using possible effect modifiers including age, sex, CS dose, duration of treatment, and cumulative dose. If fewer than ten study groups contributed to any regression, we treated all findings as hypothesis generating. To assess the robustness of the results of the primary meta-analyses, we conducted sensitivity analyses restricted to studies rated as high quality according to the NOS. This sensitivity analysis was applied to each meta-analysis for which three or more high-quality studies were available. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 index. We considered an I2 value of > 50% to indicate significant heterogeneity. If significant heterogeneity was found and sufficient studies were included in the meta-analysis, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to examine the origins of heterogeneity. Publication bias was evaluated by visually inspecting funnel plots and by using the Egger’s test when ten or more studies were included in a meta-analysis. The meta-analysis was performed using R Statistical Software (Version 4.2.3) and metafor and meta R packages (Version 3.0.2). The significance level was set at 0.05 with a two-tailed test for the meta-analysis.

Results

Using database searches, we identified 6409 records. After removing duplicate records, titles and abstracts were screened, and 19 additional studies identified through a hand search were also included in the screening process (Table 1 of the ESM). A total of 109 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 36 were subsequently excluded. Finally, 73 articles (total N = 3,759,659) were included in the qualitative synthesis (22 cross-sectional studies [28–49], 31 cohort studies [6, 7, 14, 24, 50–76], 12 pre-post studies [17, 77–87], 2 case-control studies [88, 89], 2 registry studies [90, 91], and 4 other design studies [92–95]) (Fig. 1). Depressive Symptoms

Quantitative Review

Meta-analysis Comparing Depression Scores Between Corticosteroid (CS) Group and Reference Group

Seven studies were included in the meta-analysis, comprising a total of 1403 participants (CS group: N = 341; reference group: N = 1062). The study populations were heterogeneous, including adults with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [47], rheumatic diseases [43, 55], asthma [37], diabetes mellitus [38], SLE [34], and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [49], but sharing similar disease conditions between the CS group and reference group. Corticosteroid regimens varied across studies, with routes of administration including oral (five studies), inhaled, topical, intramuscular, and combinations. Daily doses ranged from 6.35 to 42 mg (prednisone equivalent), with a median treatment duration of 44 months in studies reporting duration data. The mean age across studies was 49 years (range 13.6–68.1 years). Depressive symptoms were assessed using validated instruments including the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale [37, 38], Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HRSD) [43, 55], Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) [47], Brief Symptom Inventory [47], Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) [49], Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI) [49], and Symptom Checklist-90-R depression subscale [34]. Across seven studies comprising eight groups, a three-level meta-analysis pooling standardized mean differences (SMDs) of depression scores between the CS group and reference group showed that the CS group had significantly higher depression scores than the reference group (Fig. 2). The pooled SMD was 0.92 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.40, 1.45, p < 0.001), suggesting an association between CS use and increased depressive symptoms. Substantial heterogeneity was observed in effect sizes across studies (I2 = 89%), indicating variability in trends among individual studies. Because of the significant heterogeneity detected in the primary meta-analysis, we conducted a leave-one-out meta-analysis. The leave-one-out meta-analysis showed that the study by Gift et al. significantly contributed to the observed heterogeneity (Fig. 2 of the ESM). Excluding the study by Gift et al. did not alter the statistical significance of the pooled SMD (SMD = 0.63, 95% CI 0.34, 0.92, p < 0.01, I2 = 51%), which still indicated significantly greater depressive symptoms in the CS group compared with the reference group. However, significant heterogeneity remained after removing Gift et al. Publication bias was assessed visually using a funnel plot (Fig. 3 of the ESM). As fewer than ten studies were included, reliable assessment of funnel plot asymmetry was not feasible, and Egger’s test was not performed because of insufficient statistical power.

Exploratory Moderator Analyses

Univariable Meta-regression Analysis

A univariable meta-regression analysis was conducted to examine the effects of each moderator. The number of groups included in the analysis varied according to data availability for each moderator: CS dose (seven groups), duration of administration (seven groups), and cumulative dose (seven groups). Study Quality and Certainty of Evidence

Seven studies included in the meta-analysis were evaluated using the NOS (Table 2 of the ESM). One study scored 9, indicating high methodological quality. Four studies scored 8, also indicating high methodological quality. Meanwhile, two studies scored 6, which were evaluated as moderate quality because of limitations in follow-up adequacy.

In the sensitivity analysis using high-quality studies for the pairwise comparison of depressive symptom severity between the CS group and the reference group, the pooled SMD was 0.60 (95% CI 0.31, 0.89), which remained statistically significant and directionally consistent with the primary analysis (Fig. 10 of the ESM). Heterogeneity was significant in this subset (I2 = 61.02%). According to the GRADE approach, the certainty of evidence for this outcome was judged to be very low, mainly because the evidence was derived from observational studies and was further downgraded for serious inconsistency and serious risk of bias (Table 3 of the ESM).

Qualitative Review

Adults

A total of 16 cross-sectional studies conducted among adult populations were evaluated (Table 4). Overall, an association between CS use and depressive symptoms was demonstrated. Specifically, nine studies reported significantly worse depressive symptoms among CS users compared with controls [31, 33, 34, 37, 38, 43, 45–47]. A cross-sectional study (N = 50) of adults with asthma receiving inhaled CSs (ICSs) found that higher ICS dose was directly correlated with greater depressive symptom severity, measured with the BDI and HRSD (p < 0.01), and was also associated with lower Short-Form-36 Item Quality of Life Questionnaire Mental Component scores [44]. A study (N = 96) among patients with primary or metastatic brain tumors, a study using the Dexamethasone Symptom Questionnaire-Chronic reported that greater total cumulative CS dose predicted higher Dexamethasone Symptom Questionnaire-Chronic scores, including the item for depression; the odds of depression increased with cumulative dose (OR 3.06, 95% CI 1.13–8.31; p = 0.028) [36]. A total of 15 cohort studies evaluating depressive symptoms associated with CS use were identified, among which seven provided statistical analyses (Table 5). Overall, findings regarding the relationship between CS use and depressive symptoms have been inconsistent across studies. Specifically, six studies compared depressive symptoms between CS-treated and untreated groups [7, 54, 55, 58, 60, 62], with three reporting no significant differences [54, 58, 62]. However, three studies with longitudinal observation over multiple years identified significant associations between CS administration and depressive symptoms. A prospective study (N = 12) of patients with rheumatic diseases and asthma, and controls without CS treatment, followed for approximately 4.5 years assessed depressive symptoms using the HRSD and reported that CS-treated patients showed a significant increase in HRSD scores from baseline to follow-up (p ≤ 0.05), and had significantly higher HRSD scores compared with controls at follow-up (14.0 ± 9.2 vs 5.3 ± 1.5, p ≤ 0.05) [55]. Another large retrospective cohort study (N = 1,597,680) found a significantly elevated hazard ratio (HR) for depression (HR = 1.83, 95% CI 1.72,1.94, p < 0.001) among CS-exposed patients compared with unexposed individuals with the same underlying medical conditions, in which depression was ascertained using database diagnostic and medication codes [7]. Furthermore, in an approximately 12-year follow-up study (N = 1,105,964) of individuals prescribed oral CS, a within-individual analysis comparing treated and untreated periods revealed a HR of 1.08 (95% CI 1.00, 1.16, p = 0.045) for depressive disorder, diagnosed through unplanned specialist psychiatric healthcare contact, during treatment periods compared with untreated periods [60]. A prospective study (N = 1609) of patients with SLE found that higher dose prednisone (≥ 20 mg/day) significantly increased the risk of depression (rate ratio = 2.3, 95% CI 1.5, 3.5 for 20–39 mg/day; rate ratio = 3.1, 95% CI 1.7, 5.4 for ≥ 40 mg/day), which was defined as persistent documentation of depressive symptoms in clinic notes and/or formal psychiatric diagnosis requiring documented treatment with psychotherapy or antidepressants [52]. In contrast, a retrospective study (N = 93) of hospitalized patients receiving oral prednisolone and referred to consultation-liaison psychiatry found no significant difference in the incidence of Pediatric Patients

Although most cross-sectional studies targeted adult populations, in a cross-sectional study (N = 66) of pediatric patients with IBD (mean age 13.8 ± 2.7 years), those receiving high-dose systemic CSs showed significantly higher depressive symptoms than IBD controls in CS-free remission for ≥ 6 months (CBCL Internalizing and CDI-Parent, p < 0.001; CDI-Self, p < 0.05), with anhedonia the most prominent feature [49] (Table 4). This study specifically evaluated the impact of systemic CS administration in pediatric patients undergoing treatment for IBD and found significantly greater depressive symptoms in CS-treated patients compared with untreated controls.

Additionally, six cohort studies evaluated depressive symptoms, of which three investigated the relation to CS dose (Table 5). In a study (N = 30) of children with CS-sensitive idiopathic nephrotic syndrome who received CS treatment for approximately 7 weeks, depressive symptoms assessed by the CDI significantly worsened from week 1 and persisted through week 7 (p < 0.001), with symptom severity showing a significant positive correlation with CS dose (r = 0.433, p = 0.017) [68]. Two studies by Bender et al. (N = 27 and N = 32) in adolescent patients with severe asthma (mean age 13–14 years) reported that children experienced significantly more depressive symptoms, assessed by the CDI, at high CS doses (mean 61.4–61.5 mg/day) compared with low CS doses (mean 3.3–7.0 mg/day) [72, 73].

A short-term study (N = 60) involving pediatric patients with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), aged 2–17 years, receiving 5-day CS treatment reported no significant changes in depressive/anxious symptoms [86]. However, Singh et al., in a study (N = 75) involving pediatric patients with idiopathic nephrotic syndrome, aged 1–15 years, found that depressive/anxious symptoms significantly worsened after 12 weeks of CS treatment (p < 0.001) and remained significantly elevated at 24 weeks after treatment completion compared with baseline (p < 0.001) [35]. Hall et al., in a study (N = 12) of children with nephrotic syndrome aged 3–8 years, reported that after 4–6 weeks of CS administration, depressive/anxious symptoms did not significantly worsen compared with controls, although depressive/withdrawal symptoms significantly worsened more in the CS group compared with the control group (p = 0.024) [87].

Manic Symptoms

Quantitative Review

Because of the limited number of eligible studies reporting manic symptoms in both the CS group and reference group, we could not conduct a meta-analysis. Therefore, only a qualitative synthesis was performed.

Qualitative Review

Adults

Four cross-sectional studies assessed manic symptoms (Table 4). In severe acute respiratory syndrome survivors (N = 102), a mailed survey across the acute and convalescent phases documented mood instability and manic complaints. Acute pulse CS exposure, including use and total dose, predicted higher factor scores for anxiety and depression and for psychosis. These CS-related effects on affective domains persisted into convalescence. Mania-like symptoms such as euphoric mood were also reported in 7.8% of patients in the acute phase and 10.8% in the convalescent phase [41]. In adults treated with CSs up to 40 mg per day prednisone equivalent (N = 54) compared with healthy controls (N = 51), hypomania and depression were more prevalent on the Hypomania Checklist 16 and the BDI, and Hypomania Checklist 16 scores showed a modest negative correlation with treatment duration (Spearman ρ = − 0.33; p = 0.043) [33]. In a long-term outpatient cohort on prednisone (N = 20) versus controls (N = 14), patient rated Internal State Scale activation scores were significantly higher with prednisone (p < 0.001), whereas clinician rated Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) scores did not differ [43]. In severe asthma, baseline data from a glucocorticoid-dependent clinic cohort (N = 101) documented neuropsychiatric toxicity in 81%, including mania in 52.5%, indicating that activation and mania-like symptoms are common with substantial cumulative exposure [32].

Regarding manic symptoms, 11 cohort studies were identified, with three providing statistical analyses (Table 5). All three studies, which evaluated outcomes over periods of several years, demonstrated a significant exacerbation of manic symptoms following CS administration. A prospective study (N = 12) of patients with rheumatic diseases and asthma, and controls without CS treatment, followed for approximately 4.5 years assessed manic symptoms using the YMRS and reported that CS-treated patients had significantly higher YMRS scores compared with controls at follow-up (p ≤ 0.05) [55]. Similarly, another large retrospective cohort study (N = 1,597,680) of patients who received at least oral CS prescription, CS-treated patients showed a significantly increased risk of mania compared with those with similar underlying conditions not treated with CSs (HR = 4.35, 95% CI 3.67–5.16, p < 0.05), as assessed using database diagnostic and medication codes [7]. Another retrospective cohort study (N = 1,105,964) using a within-individual analysis found an HR = 1.23 (95% CI 1.12, 1.36) for bipolar disorder, diagnosed through unplanned specialist psychiatric healthcare contact, during treatment periods compared with untreated periods [60].

Seven pre-post studies evaluated manic symptoms (Table 6). Five studies reported a consistent pattern in which manic symptoms, assessed using rating scales, emerged or worsened shortly after initiation of CS treatment (approximately 3–14 days) and returned to baseline levels after the treatment ended (at the end of the tapering phase or approximately 10-day to 1-month post-treatment) [17, 78–81]. Another study (N = 50) of patients with ocular diseases assessed manic symptoms using the Association for Methodology and Documentation in Psychiatry scale up to 8 days post-treatment initiation and observed significantly increased manic symptoms at day 8 compared with baseline (p < 0.001), aligning with the previously mentioned pattern [82]. Only one study (N = 8) of patients with dermatologic diseases and multiple sclerosis, using the YMRS, reported no change in manic symptoms [84].

Pediatric Patients

Only one study evaluated manic symptoms in a younger patient specifically in pediatric patients (Table 5). In a retrospective cohort study (N = 1813) of patients with ALL aged 1–25 years, mania was observed in 1 out of 93 pediatric patients presenting with CS-related neuropsychiatric symptoms [74].

A Meta-analysis of Depressive and Manic Symptoms in Acute CS Treatment

Six studies comprising a total of 1086 patients provided data for a quantitative meta-analysis comparing both manic and depressive symptoms within the same cohort during the operationally defined acute phase (≤ 8 weeks) [Fig. 3]. Underlying conditions included SLE in three studies [53, 57, 65] (n = 302), multiple sclerosis in one study [81] (n = 88), various conditions (neoplastic diseases, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, rheumatoid arthritis) in one study [14] (n = 676), and ophthalmic diseases in one study [82] (n = 50).Fig. 3Meta-analysis of depressive and manic symptoms in acute corticosteroid therapy. CI confidence intervals, OR odds ratio

Meta-analysis of depressive and manic symptoms in acute corticosteroid therapy. CI confidence intervals, OR odds ratio

Corticosteroid doses in prednisone equivalent ranged from 31.1 to 59.5 mg/day [14, 57], 0.8 to 1.0 mg/kg/day [53, 65], 75 to 119 mg/day [82], and 1000 to 1250 mg/day [81]. The duration of CS treatment was explicitly reported in two studies: 3–10 days [81] and 8 days [82]. In the remaining studies, the total treatment duration was not specified, and the observation period for psychiatric events ranged from a mean of 28 days [14] to 8 weeks after CS initiation [53, 57]. In studies that reported timing of psychiatric symptom onset, events occurred within 18.2 days on average after CS initiation [65], within 28 days after initiation [53], or within 21 days after initiation or dose escalation [57]. Psychiatric symptom assessment methods differed across studies, including standardized rating scales (Association for Methodology and Documentation in Psychiatry [14], Profile of Mood States [14], BDI-Fast Screen, and Mood Disorders Questionnaire [81], DSM) criteria-based psychiatric evaluation [53, 57, 65], and clinical judgment by physicians [14]. The within-cohort random-effects meta-analysis, accounting for the covariance between manic and depressive symptom counts, revealed that manic symptoms were reported significantly more frequently than depressive symptoms during the acute phase. The pooled OR was 2.42 (95% CI 1.48, 3.97, p < 0.01), demonstrating a statistically significant result. Heterogeneity was low (I2 = 0.0%). Individual study ORs ranged from 1.20 to 7.23. Publication bias was assessed visually using a funnel plot (Fig. 11 of the ESM). As fewer than ten studies were included, reliable assessment of funnel plot asymmetry was not feasible, and Egger’s test was not performed because of insufficient statistical power.

Study Quality and Certainty of Evidence

Six studies were assessed using the NOS (Table 4 of the ESM). Four studies scored 8, indicating high methodological quality across all criteria. Two studies scored 5, reflecting moderate methodological quality because of limitations in comparability of cohorts and follow-up adequacy.

A sensitivity analysis using high-quality studies for the within-cohort comparison of manic versus depressive symptoms yielded a pooled OR of 2.13 (95% CI 1.20, 3.78, p = 0.01), with low heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%), supporting the primary finding that manic symptoms predominate over depressive symptoms during the acute phase (Fig. 12 of the ESM). According to the GRADE approach, the certainty of evidence for this outcome was judged to be low because the evidence was derived from observational studies, although no additional major downgrading was considered necessary for inconsistency, indirectness, or imprecision (Table 3 of the ESM).

Psychotic Symptoms

Quantitative Review

Pooled Proportion of CS-Induced Psychotic Symptoms Based on Single-Arm Meta-analysis

Because of the limited number of eligible studies reporting psychotic symptoms in both the CS group and reference group, we could not conduct a pairwise meta-analysis. A single-arm meta-analysis was conducted on six studies comprising a total of 1206 patients (Fig. 4). The studies enrolled patients with SLE [53, 57, 64], pemphigus [59], ophthalmologic disorders [82], and mixed medical conditions including malignancies and rheumatologic diseases [14]. Sample sizes ranged from 50 to 718 patients, with mean ages that ranged from 34 to 47 years. Corticosteroid doses varied considerably across studies: initial doses ranged from 38.8 to 148.8 mg/day prednisone equivalent, with one study using doses of 60–180 mg/day [59] and another requiring ≥ 40 mg/day [64]. The observation periods for detecting psychiatric symptoms ranged from 8 days to 8 weeks following CS initiation or dose escalation. Among studies reporting onset of psychotic symptoms, the mean time to symptom emergence was 12 days [64] (median), 12.2 days [57], and 12.5 days [53], with ranges of 1–28 days. Diagnostic criteria for CS-induced psychiatric complications varied across studies, with some employing DSM, Third Edition, Revised [82] or DSM, Fourth Edition criteria [53, 57] and others using clinical assessment by psychiatrists [59, 64], and one using nurse monitoring [14]. The pooled proportion of patients who experienced CS-induced psychotic symptoms was 2.4% (95% CI 0.76, 7.26; I2 = 91.8%; k = 6), indicating substantial variability across studies.Fig. 4Pooled proportion of corticosteroid-induced psychotic symptoms based on single-arm meta-analysis. A leave-one-out meta-analysis revealed that omission of Rosenberg et al. produced a markedly different heterogeneity profile. The pooled proportion was 1.97% (95% CI 1.28, 3.02%) with I2 = 0.0%, indicating that Rosenberg et al. was the dominant source of heterogeneity in the primary analysis (Fig. 13 of the ESM). Publication bias was assessed visually using a funnel plot (Fig. 14 of the ESM). As fewer than ten studies were included, reliable assessment of funnel plot asymmetry was not feasible, and Egger’s test was not performed because of insufficient statistical power.

Study Quality and Certainty of Evidence

Six studies were assessed using the NOS (Table 5 of the ESM). Three studies achieved a high score of 8, reflecting strong methodological quality, particularly in aspects like representativeness of the cohort and comparability of groups. In contrast, the other three studies scored lower at 5, indicating moderate quality because of weaknesses in categories such as cohort comparability and adequacy of follow-up.

A sensitivity analysis using high-quality studies for the single-arm meta-analysis of psychotic symptom incidence, the pooled proportion was 2.16% (95% CI 1.03, 4.47), with no evidence of heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%), consistent with the primary estimate (Fig. 15 of the ESM). According to the GRADE approach, the certainty of evidence for this outcome was judged to be very low, mainly because of the observational design, serious inconsistency, and serious imprecision (Table 3 of the ESM).

Qualitative Review

Adults

Two cross-sectional studies evaluated psychotic symptoms (Table 4). In severe acute respiratory syndrome survivors (N = 102), a mailed survey across the acute and convalescent phases found that acute pulse CS exposure (use and total dose) was significantly associated with higher psychosis factor scores during the acute phase; psychotic symptoms were reported in 3.9 % in the acute phase [41]. Among women with SLE (N = 121; CS users N = 33, SLE without CS N = 38, healthy controls N = 50), those receiving CSs showed higher Symptom Checklist-90-R psychoticism scores than both patients with SLE not receiving CSs and healthy controls (p = 0.016) [34].

Three cohort studies statistically evaluated psychotic symptoms associated with CS use, all with evaluation durations exceeding 4 years (Table 5). A prospective study (N = 12) of patients with rheumatic diseases and asthma, and controls without CS treatment, followed for approximately 4.5 years assessed psychiatric symptoms using the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale and reported that CS-treated patients had significantly higher Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale scores compared with controls at follow-up (p ≤ 0.05) [55]. Additionally, a retrospective cohort study (N = 597,257) of individuals (including both those who received CS prescriptions and those who did not) reported a significantly increased risk of early-onset schizophrenia spectrum disorders, identified through registry diagnostic codes, among those exposed to non-systemic glucocorticoids (HR ratio= 1.47; 95% CI 1.25, 1.73) and systemic glucocorticoids (HR ratio = 1.66; 95% CI 1.13, 2.43) compared with unexposed individuals [63]. Another retrospective cohort study (N = 1,105,964) using a within-individual analysis found an HR = 1.17 (95% CI 1.05, 1.30) for schizophrenia-spectrum disorder, diagnosed through unplanned specialist psychiatric healthcare contact, during treatment periods compared with untreated periods [60]. Furthermore, 2 cohort studies involving patients with SLE identified hypoalbuminemia as a risk factor for CS-induced psychosis [24, 57]. Three pre-post studies assessed psychotic symptoms using rating scales during high-dose CS treatment for 2 weeks or less (Table 6). Two studies found no change in psychotic symptoms, while one study reported improvement [17, 82].

Pediatric Patients

Only one study evaluated psychotic symptoms specifically in pediatric patients. In a retrospective cohort study (N = 1813) of patients with ALL aged 1–25 years, psychosis was observed in 48 patients and hallucinations in 14 patients out of 93 pediatric patients presenting with CS-related neuropsychiatric symptoms [74] (Table 5).

Quantitative Review

The meta-analysis included three studies with 1251 participants across diverse clinical populations (diabetes [38], SLE [34], and asthma [37]), but sharing similar disease conditions between the CS group and reference group within each study. Mean ages ranged from 34 to 68 years. Corticosteroid exposure varied in route (inhaled, oral, topical, intramuscular), dose (6.35–10 mg/day prednisone equivalent where specified), and duration (from within 3 months to median 44 months of use). Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale anxiety subscale [37, 38] and the Symptom Checklist-90-R anxiety subscale [34]. Across three cross-sectional studies comparing anxiety scores between the CS group (n = 262) and reference group (n = 989), a pairwise meta-analysis revealed that the pooled SMD was 0.13 (95% CI − 0.24, 0.51, p = 0.26), indicating a non-significant difference (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Meta-analysis comparing anxiety scores between corticosteroid users and nonusers. Study Quality and Certainty of Evidence

Three studies were assessed using the NOS. One study scored 9, indicating high methodological quality (Table 6 of the ESM). Two studies scored 8, also indicating high methodological quality. Qualitative Review

Adults

Anxiety symptoms were evaluated in 12 cross-sectional studies, eight of which reported significantly worse anxiety symptoms among CS-treated patients compared with controls (Table 4). Dose information was provided in six of these studies, and notably, even relatively low-dose CS treatment was associated with increased anxiety symptom scores and a higher prevalence of anxiety disorders [32, 34, 37, 39]. Furthermore, in SARS survivors (N = 102), anxiety symptoms were significantly associated with CS use in both the acute and convalescent phases; prevalence was 26.5% acutely and 13.7% during convalescence [41]. In patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder (N = 160), a cross-sectional study of ICS duration showed an upward trend in anxiety prevalence with longer use that did not reach statistical significance (Spearman’s ρ = 0.152; p = 0.056) [28].

Five cohort studies reported the onset of anxiety associated with CS use (Table 5). The only study that performed statistical analysis, a retrospective cohort study (N = 1,105,964 individuals), found that within-individual analysis revealed a HR of 1.25 (95% CI 1.20–1.31) for anxiety disorder during treatment periods compared with untreated periods [60]. Two pre-post studies evaluated anxiety symptoms using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory, both of which demonstrated significant improvement in anxiety following CS administration (Table 6).

Pediatric Patients

Four cohort studies evaluated anxiety in pediatric patients (Table 5). Two studies, by Bender et al. (N = 27 and N = 32) in adolescent patients with severe asthma (mean age 13–14 years) reported that children experienced significantly more anxiety symptoms, assessed by the Children’s Manifest Anxiety Scale, at high CS doses (mean 61.4–61.5 mg/day) compared with low CS doses (mean 3.3–7.0 mg/day) [72, 73]. In a study (N = 30) of children with CS-sensitive idiopathic nephrotic syndrome who received CS treatment for approximately 7 weeks, anxiety symptoms assessed by the CMAS significantly worsened from week 1 and persisted through week 7 (p < 0.001), with symptom severity showing a significant positive correlation with CS dose (r = 0.388, p = 0.034) [68]. For pre-post studies, no additional findings were identified beyond those previously described in the review of depressive symptoms in pediatric patients (Sects. 1–2) [Table 6].

Other Psychiatric Symptoms

Quantitative Review

Because of the limited number of eligible studies, it was inappropriate to conduct a meta-analysis. Therefore, only a qualitative synthesis was performed.

Qualitative Review

Adults

Other psychiatric symptoms evaluated in included studies comprised those not readily classified into categories such as psychotic, depressive, manic, or anxiety symptoms, as well as symptoms assessed more comprehensively. In cross-sectional studies, other psychiatric symptoms associated with CS treatment included anger or irritability [36], obsessive-compulsive symptoms [34, 44], somatization [34], and behavioral changes [41]. Additionally, in long-term prednisone users (N = 20) versus controls (N = 14), overall psychiatric symptom burden was higher, with higher clinician-rated global severity and a higher patient-reported global state [30, 43]. Among patients with SLE in a low disease activity state (N = 175), higher daily prednisolone dose correlated with poorer emotional health (p = 0.01) [30]. In dermatology settings, patients (N = 54) also self-reported subjective emotional changes during systemic CS therapy [35] (Table 4). Conversely, an ecological US county-level analysis found that higher prescription rates of intranasal CSs were associated with lower suicide rates after adjustment for antidepressant prescribing (p = 0.0004) [40].

In cohort studies, the onset of psychiatric symptoms such as agitation, anger or bad temper, behavioral changes, affect change, mood disorder, emotional lability, irritability, hyperkinesia, and suicide-related symptoms has been reported in association with CS use (Table 5). Among these studies, two retrospective cohort studies performed statistical evaluations. The first study (N = 1,597,680) found significantly elevated HRs for suicide or suicide attempt (HR = 6.89, 95% CI 4.52,10.50) and panic disorder (HR = 1.45, 95% CI 1.15,1.85) among CS-exposed patients compared with unexposed individuals with the same underlying medical conditions, in which suicidal behavior was ascertained using database diagnostic codes and death certificates, and panic disorder was ascertained using database diagnostic codes [7]. The second study (N = 1,105,964) conducted a within-individual analysis comparing treated and untreated periods and found a non-significant HR of 1.06 (95% CI 0.96, 1.17) for suicidal behavior during treatment periods compared with untreated periods [60].

In two pre-post studies, the effects of CS treatment on other psychiatric symptoms were evaluated (Table 6). One study (N = 50) of patients with ocular diseases reported a significant increase in apathetic symptoms (p < 0.02), assessed by the Association for Methodology and Documentation in Psychiatry, following 8 days of CS administration, while levels of vigor and anger, measured by the Profile of Mood States, did not change significantly [82]. Another study assessed eight patients with dermatologic diseases or multiple sclerosis who received CS treatment for more than 10 days [84]. The total mood score, based on seven mood items—sadness, irritability, apathy, emotional lability, crying, social withdrawal, and decreased energy level—was significantly improved at days 3, 7, and 10 following CS initiation [84].

A case-control study including 14,028 individuals who died by suicide and an equal number of matched controls reported that initiation of oral CS treatment was associated with suicide in a dose-dependent manner (Table 7 of the ESM). The study found a seven-fold increased risk of suicide among patients with cancer and a two-fold increased risk among patients treated for other medical conditions [88].

Pediatric Patients

In a cross-sectional study of pediatric IBD (N = 66; CS N = 33, CS-free controls N = 33), parents reported greater difficulties in emotional regulation in children receiving systemic CSs, with higher CBCL Internalizing and CDI Parent totals in the CS group (both p < 0.001) [49] [Table 4]. In cohort studies, the development of psychiatric symptoms such as aggressive behavior, agitation, emotional lability, disorganized speech, suicidal ideation, personality changes, homicidal ideation, and mood changes has been reported in association with CS use (Table 5). In a study of children with CS-sensitive idiopathic nephrotic syndrome (N = 30) who received CS treatment for approximately 7 weeks, aggression as measured by the Aggression Questionnaire significantly worsened from week 1 and persisted through week 7 (p < 0.001) [68]. In contrast, a retrospective study (N = 125) of patients with ALL aged 1–10 years found that behavioral problems and affective disorders, assessed through physician evaluation, did not significantly worsen during CS treatment [75]. Regarding the relationship between dose and psychiatric symptoms, two studies demonstrated a significant association between higher CS dose and greater severity of aggression [68, 70]. A prospective study (N = 10) of patients with idiopathic nephrotic syndrome (mean age 8.2 years) found that CS dose was a strong predictor of aggressive behavior (F = 5.35, p < 0.0001) as measured by the CBCL [70]. In the study by Youssef et al., a significant positive correlation was observed between prednisolone dose and aggression scores (r = 0.438, p = 0.015) [68]. However, two studies by Bender et al. (N = 27 and N = 32) involving adolescent patients with asthma (mean age 13–14 years) reported no significant differences in hyperactivity, attention, impulsivity, or motor control between patients receiving high-dose and low-dose CSs [72, 73].

In three pre-post studies conducted in pediatric populations, significant worsening of aggressive behavior, as assessed by the CBCL, was consistently observed, particularly among children aged 1.5–5 years [85–87] (Table 6). Among children under the age of 8 years, significant increases were also reported in attention problems, emotional reactivity and control difficulties, and impaired inhibition [86, 87]. In a study (N = 75) of children with idiopathic nephrotic syndrome aged 1–15 years, significant worsening was observed in rule-breaking behavior (p < 0.001) and thought problems (p < 0.05) among those aged 6–15 years [85].

One registry study and two other design studies also evaluated other psychiatric symptoms in pediatric patients (Tables 8 and 9 of the ESM).

Discussion

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to examine the psychiatric symptoms associated with CSs. Across the 73 included observational studies, CSs were administered for a wide range of conditions, including SLE, respiratory diseases such as asthma, cancers such as ALL, and dermatologic disorders. Although the majority of studies focused on adult populations, 16 studies included pediatric patients.

The meta-analysis revealed that CS users exhibited significantly more severe depressive symptoms than non-users. Furthermore, our exploratory meta-regression analysis, based on a limited number of studies, suggests that both higher CS dose and older age may be associated with greater severity of depressive symptoms. However, as the analysis included fewer than the recommended ten studies, these findings should be considered preliminary and require further validation. Notably, this meta-analysis suggests that manic symptoms were more likely to emerge than depressive symptoms within the operationally defined 8-week window following CS administration. In contrast, no significant association was found between CS use and anxiety symptoms. Regarding psychotic symptoms, the pooled analysis indicated that psychotic symptoms occurred in approximately 2.4% of CS users, although this estimate should be interpreted with caution given the substantial heterogeneity and its sensitivity to individual studies. The qualitative findings from the systematic review also largely support these results.

CS Dose and Psychiatric Symptoms

Higher doses of CSs have been considered to be associated with an increased risk of psychiatric symptoms [7, 52]. In the present meta-regression analysis, increased CS dose was significantly associated with the worsening of depressive symptoms, although the evidence is limited by the small number of studies. Among studies not included in the meta-regression analysis, three studies, including two large-scale investigations that followed more than 1000 participants for over 10 years, demonstrated a significant association between higher CS doses and depressive symptoms [7, 44, 52]. Although two studies reported no significant association [6, 33], these findings may reflect insufficient statistical power owing to their relatively short observation periods and modest sample sizes of approximately 100 participants. Similar associations between higher CS doses and greater severity of depressive symptoms have also been reported in pediatric populations [72, 73]. Taken together, the results of these quantitative and qualitative reviews suggest a possible association between higher doses of CS and depressive symptoms.

In adults, the relationship between CS dosage and psychiatric symptoms other than depression has been insufficiently investigated. However, a cohort study following 1,597,680 individuals over multiple years demonstrated that the HR for manic symptoms increased with CS dose, suggesting a likely dose-dependent risk for the exacerbation of mania [7]. In pediatric populations, high-dose CS treatment was suggested to be associated with an increased risk of anxiety symptoms [72, 73] and aggressive behavior [68, 70], compared with lower doses.

Regarding the threshold for psychiatric symptom onset, only one identified study, conducted by the Boston Collaborative Drug Surveillance Program, reported minimal risk associated with doses < 40 mg/day, moderate risk with 40–80 mg/day, and high risk with doses > 80 mg/day [14]. This finding, reported over 50 years ago, has provided the basis for the widely accepted understanding that a daily dose of 40 mg/day of prednisone represents a critical cutoff, which persists to the present day [15, 61]. However, it should be noted that these values were not derived through statistical analysis. Future research employing statistical methods to determine the threshold for psychiatric symptom onset is warranted.

CS Administration Duration and Psychiatric Symptoms

Although previous studies have suggested that acute CS treatment may be associated with mania [17], the present meta-analysis provided, to our knowledge, the first statistical evidence that manic symptoms are more likely to occur than depressive symptoms during the acute phase of CS treatment. Notably, the incidence of manic symptoms was not related to CS dose or the use of CS pulse treatment. In the qualitative review, a consistent pattern was observed in which manic symptoms peaked approximately 3–14 days after CS initiation and resolved within 1 month following treatment cessation [17, 78–82]. These findings suggest that clinicians should closely monitor for manic symptoms during the early phase of treatment, and dose reduction may be effective if such symptoms emerge.

Furthermore, findings from cohort studies indicate that the onset of psychiatric symptoms typically occurs 10–20 days after the start of CS treatment, highlighting this period as particularly critical for clinical vigilance [53, 57, 60, 61, 64, 65]. In several pre-post studies, improvements in psychiatric symptoms, particularly depressive and anxiety symptoms, were observed during the early period following CS administration [17, 78, 80, 82–84]. These findings may be explained by the presence of CS-induced manic symptoms or by improvements in the underlying medical condition.

In contrast, although evidence directly linking long-term CS use to psychiatric symptoms is limited, longitudinal studies have suggested that depressive symptoms are the most frequently observed psychiatric manifestation in the context of prolonged CS exposure [7, 43, 55]. This pattern may be explained, at least in part, by biological mechanisms proposed in prior research. Acute disruption of hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis–glucocorticoid receptor signaling can shift monoaminergic and GABAergic transmission, whereas longer-term exposure may affect neuroplasticity and the neuroinflammatory milieu, ultimately favoring the development of depressive symptoms [96–98]. These mechanisms are presented here as a possible interpretive framework for the observed clinical patterns.

CS Cumulative Dose and Psychiatric Symptoms

Several studies have reported an increased risk of depressive symptoms and agitation in association with higher CS cumulative exposure [36, 50, 51]. However, in our multivariable meta-regression analysis, no significant association was found between CS cumulative dose and depressive symptom severity. This may be because of limitations in sample size or variability in outcome assessment methods across studies. Although CS cumulative dose may influence specific psychiatric symptoms, its overall impact on psychiatric outcomes remains unclear.

In addition, whether CS treatment was ongoing or completed at the time of assessment appears to be an important factor, but this distinction was often not clearly reported in the studies that we identified. Furthermore, cumulative dose metrics consider high-dose short-term regimens and low-dose long-term regimens as equivalent, despite their clinical differences. Given this heterogeneity, further investigation is needed to determine whether cumulative dose functions as an independent predictor of psychiatric outcomes, separate from dose and administration duration.

Age and Psychiatric Symptoms

The meta-regression analysis revealed that increasing age was significantly associated with greater severity of depressive symptoms. However, this association derives from an exploratory meta-regression with few study groups (k = 6 studies; seven groups) and between-study variability, and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. This may be related to age-associated alterations in CS pharmacokinetics as well as of the central nervous system, including increased vulnerability of the aging central nervous system [99, 100].

Because of insufficient data, a meta-analysis of psychiatric symptoms in pediatric patients receiving CSs could not be conducted in the present study. In particular, only one study assessed manic or psychotic symptoms in children. The qualitative review indicated that behavioral abnormalities were frequently reported in pediatric populations. This suggests that the psychiatric effects of CSs in children may manifest not only as internalizing symptoms but also as behavioral changes. Taken together, these findings suggest that in older adults, close monitoring of depressive symptoms is warranted, while in children, targeted assessment of behavioral symptoms is particularly important.

Novel Contributions Beyond Previous Research

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Koning et al. also examined neuropsychiatric adverse effects of glucocorticoids using a broadly similar quantitative framework, including pooled proportions and, where possible, comparisons of questionnaire scores between glucocorticoid users and nonusers. Their review likewise identified depressive and manic symptoms as major psychiatric outcomes associated with glucocorticoid use, indicating substantial overlap in scope and findings with the present review [13]. However, the prior review differed from the present review in methodological approach, including study design eligibility, comparator selection, and outcome scope. Specifically, it pooled RCTs with observational studies, included healthy comparators, and restricted outcomes to the acute phase. These distinctions primarily reflect differences in scope and analytic framing, and both reviews should be viewed as complementary rather than competing. In the present review, analyses were limited to observational designs with disease-matched controls, both short-term and long-term outcomes were examined, and additional analytic strategies, including three-level and paired meta-analyses as well as exploratory meta-regression, were applied. We also analyzed studies that reported both manic and depressive outcomes within the same cohort, enabling comparisons under matched moderators (e.g., age, sex distribution, CS dose, and duration) within the same study when examining the incidence rates of depressive and manic symptoms during the acute phase. In addition, we estimated the prevalence of psychotic symptoms and provided controlled comparisons for anxiety symptoms, thereby broadening the symptom domains examined. Finally, we provided a systematic synthesis of psychiatric symptoms in pediatric CS users that offers a complementary perspective to prior pediatric reviews through distinct methodological contributions. While Drozdowicz and Bostwick [101] comprehensively reviewed pediatric adverse effects organized by disease condition and treatment context (neonatology, oncology, general pediatrics), with emphasis on neurodevelopmental outcomes and narrative synthesis of case reports, our study focused on core psychiatric symptoms in school-age children and adolescents systematically categorized by symptom domain across diverse medical conditions. We systematically integrated observational evidence from studies employing standardized rating scales to assess symptom severity, and identified dose-dependent patterns wherein higher CS doses were associated with increased anxiety and aggressive behavior. Although limited in study number, this symptom-based synthesis revealed age-specific clinical patterns and established priorities for future systematic investigation in this understudied pediatric population. Collectively, these refinements contribute to a more comprehensive and clinically informative evidence base for CS-associated psychiatric outcomes.

Research Implications

Only a limited number of studies included in this meta-analysis directly compared psychiatric symptoms between two distinct groups, such as CS users and non-users. Future research employing such comparative designs may provide higher-quality evidence for evaluating the psychiatric effects of CSs. Additionally, further accumulation of research findings on psychiatric symptoms other than depression would allow for the application of meta-regression analyses to other symptom domains.

Although the present meta-regression identified CS dose and age as significant moderators of depressive symptoms, the findings from the qualitative review suggest that cumulative dose and treatment duration of CSs may also play a role in the development of psychiatric symptoms. Well-designed studies that directly examine these variables may offer clinically valuable insights. Additionally, as mentioned above, research to update the threshold of CS dose for psychiatric symptom onset is also warranted. Furthermore, recent studies suggest that CSs may exert therapeutic effects in inflammatory subtypes of psychiatric and cognitive disorders, such as those associated with neural autoantibodies. This potential dual role underscores the need for future research to integrate findings on both the adverse and the potentially beneficial effects of CSs in psychiatry [102, 103].

In pediatric populations, the number of studies eligible for meta-analysis was even more limited. In particular, there is a pressing need for more research on manic and psychotic symptoms in children receiving CS treatment.

Limitations

This study has several methodological limitations. First, it was not pre-registered, which may increase the risk of selective reporting. Second, a medical librarian did not participate in developing the search strategy, which could have limited the comprehensiveness of the literature search despite the use of broad search terms and independent dual screening. In addition to database searching, we performed a hand search, which, while useful for completeness, has inherent limitations in comprehensiveness compared with fully systematic approaches and should be considered when interpreting the results. Furthermore, there was considerable heterogeneity among the included studies in terms of patient populations, types of CSs, doses, treatment durations, and assessment methods. This variability limits the ability to directly compare results across studies.

In the meta-analysis of depressive symptoms, substantial heterogeneity was observed, and heterogeneity remained at a moderate level even after exclusion of an influential study in leave-one-out analyses. This suggests that the observed heterogeneity was not solely driven by a single study but may instead reflect underlying clinical and methodological diversity across studies. Such heterogeneity may be partly attributable to the wide range of underlying diseases included, as well as differences in outcome assessment methods, including the use of various depression rating scales. In addition, differences in age distributions across studies may have contributed to this variability. Consistent with this, our exploratory meta-regression analysis suggested that age may function as a significant moderator of depressive symptom severity, although this finding should be interpreted with caution given the limited number of studies and the potential for ecological bias inherent in study-level meta-regression.

Moreover, the number of included studies was limited for several analyses, which may have reduced statistical power. Accordingly, the findings of this meta-analysis should be interpreted with caution and regarded as exploratory and hypothesis generating rather than confirmatory. In addition, the certainty of evidence was generally low to very low across the pooled outcomes according to the GRADE approach. This mainly reflected the observational nature of the included studies, along with additional concerns regarding risk of bias, heterogeneity, and imprecision in several meta-analyses. Therefore, the pooled estimates should be interpreted with appropriate caution. In the meta-regression analysis, it was not possible to fully control for the influence of underlying diseases or the conditions for which CSs were prescribed. Although meta-regression is generally recommended when at least ten studies are available, the present analysis included only seven study groups. Therefore, the meta-regression findings should be interpreted with caution and considered preliminary. Furthermore, the meta-regression focused exclusively on depressive symptoms, and it remains unclear whether the results are applicable to other psychiatric outcomes. Particularly for depression, findings from the qualitative review may reflect the influence of underlying disease burden on depressive outcomes. By contrast, the meta-regression analyses for depression were conducted within pairwise meta-analyses, comparing CS-treated patients with same-disease reference groups, enabling a more direct assessment of the effect of CSs on depression.

This review also excluded symptom domains such as sleep disturbances, cognitive dysfunction, and delirium, and therefore cannot provide evidence regarding these outcomes. We excluded delirium in particular because it is characterized primarily by acute disturbances in consciousness and cognition rather than core psychiatric symptoms (depression, mania, psychosis, anxiety). Furthermore, delirium is strongly influenced by underlying medical conditions and systemic factors such as infection or metabolic derangements, making it nearly impossible to disentangle the direct psychiatric effects of CSs from the effects of comorbid illness. However, this decision may reduce comparability with prior reviews that included delirium as a CS-induced psychiatric outcome and may limit the generalizability of our findings by excluding a clinically important presentation, particularly in medically complex populations such as patients with SLE.

Finally, because this review focused on observational studies, confounding by indication is an inherent limitation. Patients with more severe disease or poorer prognosis are more likely to receive CSs, and residual confounding by disease severity cannot be fully excluded. However, in the pairwise meta-analyses, the included studies used reference groups with the same underlying disease, which may reduce confounding by indication. Such matching, however, does not eliminate confounding and cannot fully account for other important sources of bias, including differences in disease severity, treatment escalation, comorbid conditions, and time-varying clinical factors. We were also unable to conduct a pairwise meta-analysis comparing manic symptoms between CS users and non-users because of the limited number of eligible studies. Although RCTs are critical for establishing causality, they were not included in this review because our eligibility criteria were intentionally restricted to observational designs to provide a methodologically coherent synthesis of the available real-world literature. A dedicated systematic review using RCT-specific eligibility criteria and synthesis methods would be required to evaluate that evidence appropriately. Evidence from RCTs on this topic has been synthesized in the recent review by Koning et al. [13], which included RCTs within its broader eligibility framework. Importantly, the design-based focus of the present review should not be interpreted as strengthening causal inference.

Conclusions

This study systematically evaluated the risk of psychiatric symptoms associated with CS use. The meta-analysis showed that CS use was associated with depressive symptoms. An exploratory meta-regression revealed that CS dose and patient age could moderate depression severity, although this finding requires confirmation in larger-scale studies because of the small number of studies included in the analysis. In addition, the analysis revealed an elevated risk of manic symptoms during the acute phase of CS treatment. These findings underscore the need for appropriate monitoring and management of psychiatric symptoms in patients undergoing CS treatment.
