Effects of Long-Term Lobeglitazone Treatment on Renal Function and Albuminuria in Korean Patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus: A Multicenter Retrospective Observational Study (LUCKY)
Authors: Hee-Sung Kim, Chang Hee Jung, Jung-Hyun Noh, Eun-Jung Rhee, Heung-Yong Jin, In-Kyung Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis study aimed to evaluate the long-term effects of lobeglitazone on renal function, glycemic control, metabolic parameters, and safety in Korean patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) treated for over 1 year.MethodsThis retrospective, non-interventional, multicenter observational study was conducted at 30 institutions in South Korea. Patients with T2DM who had received lobeglitazone for at least 1 year and had available estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) data were included. Primary outcomes were changes in eGFR and urinary albumin–creatinine ratio (UACR). Secondary outcomes included changes in HbA1c, body weight, lipid profiles, insulin resistance, β-cell function, and adverse events (AEs).ResultsOf the 2743 enrolled patients, 2648 and 2500 were analyzed for safety and efficacy, respectively. Of 2648 patients analyzed for safety, 1655 (62.5%) were male and 993 (37.5%) were female. Baseline eGFR (82.0 ± 22.0 ml/min/1.73 m2) remained stable over 6 years across all chronic kidney disease (CKD) stages. UACR was unchanged in 77–83% of patients throughout follow-up. HbA1c significantly decreased from baseline (− 0.99% at year 1 to − 0.93% at year 6; all p < 0.0001), indicating sustained glycemic control. Lipid profiles and HOMA-IR improved, while HOMA- β remained stable. Overall, 1165 AEs occurred in 531 patients (20.05%), mostly consistent with the established safety profile; events of special interest were rare (< 1%).ConclusionsLong-term lobeglitazone treatment was not associated with deterioration of renal function or worsening of albuminuria status across all CKD stages, while demonstrating sustained glycemic efficacy and a favorable safety profile in real-world clinical practice. These findings support lobeglitazone as a clinically relevant long-term treatment option for patients with T2DM, including those with pre-existing CKD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13300-026-01898-w.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a prevalent metabolic disorder and a leading cause of diabetic kidney disease (DKD), which substantially increases the risk of end-stage kidney disease and cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [1]. In Korea, diabetes accounts for approximately 40% of incident dialysis cases [2], highlighting the clinical and public health importance of preventing DKD progression in patients with T2DM.

The development of DKD follows complex mechanisms involving chronic hyperglycemia, oxidative stress, advanced glycation end-products, and activation of the renin–angiotensin system, with insulin resistance playing a central role in linking metabolic dysfunction to renal injury [3]. Consequently, insulin-sensitizing therapies may confer reno-protective effects beyond glucose lowering. Thiazolidinediones (TZDs), as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ) agonists, improve insulin sensitivity and adipokine profiles, and prior studies of pioglitazone and rosiglitazone have demonstrated modest reductions in albuminuria, suggesting a potential class effect on renal outcomes [4].

Lobeglitazone is a novel TZD developed in Korea that has demonstrated favorable effects on glycemic control, lipid parameters, and insulin resistance indices in phase 3 trials [5, 6]. A 24-week randomized study in Korean patients with T2DM demonstrated improvements in insulin resistance, with a trend toward reduced urine albumin–creatinine ratio (UACR) [5, 6]. Compared with earlier TZDs, lobeglitazone appears to have a more favorable safety profile, including lower rates of weight gain and edema [5, 7, 8]; however, existing evidence is largely limited to short-term clinical trials.

Given the chronic and progressive nature of DKD, long-term real-world data are needed to clarify the renal effects of lobeglitazone across diverse patient populations and stages of chronic kidney disease (CKD). Therefore, this retrospective observational study evaluated the impact of ≥ 1 year of lobeglitazone treatment on renal function in Korean adults with T2DM by assessing longitudinal changes in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and UACR, with subgroup analyses according to baseline CKD stage.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective, multicenter observational study investigated the impact of long-term lobeglitazone use on renal function in Korean patients with T2DM. The study involved approximately 30 endocrinology centers across Korea, with data collected from patients' electronic medical records (EMRs) between February 2014 and December 2023.

Study Population

Eligible participants were adults aged ≥ 19 years with a confirmed diagnosis of T2DM who had received lobeglitazone for at least 360 consecutive days. A maximum of one temporary interruption not exceeding 30 days was permitted during the exposure period. Patients were required to have available serum creatinine (SCr) data obtained within 6 months prior to initiating lobeglitazone and at least once after 12 ± 3 months of treatment, ensuring reliable assessment of renal trajectory over time.

Key exclusion criteria included known contraindications to lobeglitazone (e.g., history of New York Heart Association (NYHA) class III–IV heart failure, active hepatic disease with transaminase elevations > 2.5 × the upper normal limit, or severe renal dysfunction), as well as type 1 diabetes, diabetic ketoacidosis, or recent major illness or surgery.

Data Collection

Data were extracted from EMRs and entered into a centralized electronic case report form. Collected variables included demographic characteristics (age, sex, body mass index, as well as smoking and alcohol history), clinical parameters (blood pressure and pulse rate), diabetes-related factors (duration of disease and concomitant antidiabetic medications), and laboratory results. Renal parameters included SCr and UACR, with eGFR calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) formula [9]. Where UACR values were unavailable, urine microalbumin and creatinine values were used to calculate the ratio.

All parameters were assessed at baseline and annually (± 3 months) during lobeglitazone exposure. Study Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was the longitudinal change in renal function markers—eGFR and UACR—from baseline over the course of treatment. Changes in eGFR were analyzed according to baseline CKD stages, as defined by eGFR categories (G1: ≥ 90, G2: 60–89, G3: 30–59, G4: 15–29, G5: < 15 ml/min/1.73 m2). Changes in albuminuria status were categorized as improvement, no change, or worsening. Improvement was defined as a transition from macroalbuminuria to microalbuminuria or normoalbuminuria, or from microalbuminuria to normoalbuminuria. No change indicated maintenance of the same albuminuria category. Worsening was defined as progression from normoalbuminuria to microalbuminuria or macroalbuminuria, or from microalbuminuria to macroalbuminuria.

Exploratory outcomes included changes in HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose, body weight, insulin resistance assessed by Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR), and β-cell function assessed by Homeostasis Model Assessment of beta-cell function (HOMA-β) at follow-up time points up to 6 years. The incidence of renal or cardiovascular AEs of special interest (AESIs), such as myocardial infarction, stroke, initiation of renal replacement therapy, and bladder cancer, was also investigated.

Sensitivity Analysis

To assess whether the observed stability in renal function could be attributable to concomitant reno-protective therapy rather than to lobeglitazone itself, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to patients who received lobeglitazone without concomitant angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB), or sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) at baseline or at any time during follow-up. Within this subgroup, longitudinal changes in eGFR were analyzed across baseline CKD stages (G1–G4) using the same observed-case approach as in the primary analysis, and the renal trajectory was compared with that of the overall cohort to evaluate consistency.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using standard descriptive and inferential methods. Continuous variables were summarized as means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges, as appropriate. Paired comparisons were performed using the paired t test for normally distributed variables or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test otherwise. Between-group comparisons, such as those across CKD stages, were assessed using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and proportions and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance level of 0.05, without imputation for missing data.

Ethics

All participating institutions independently obtained approval from their respective Institutional Review Boards prior to study initiation, and the study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. The reference numbers of all participating institutional review boards are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The requirement for informed consent was waived by each Institutional Review Board due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified clinical data.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of Study Population

A total of 2743 patients were enrolled in the study. Of these, 2648 patients were included in the safety analysis set, and 2500 patients were included in the efficacy analysis set. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of the participants was 62.6 ± 11.3 years, and 62.5% were male. The average duration of lobeglitazone treatment was 3.0 ± 1.6 years. At baseline, the mean eGFR was 82.0 ± 22.0 ml/min/1.73 m2, indicating relatively well-preserved renal function in the overall cohort. The most common comorbidities were dyslipidemia (62.5%) and hypertension (28.4%). Changes in Body Weight, Insulin Resistance, β-Cell Function, and Lipid Profiles

Mean body weight increased slightly from 67.6 kg at baseline to 69.3 kg at year 1 (an increase of 1.8 kg, p < 0.0001). Thereafter, body weight remained stable, with no further significant increases from year 2 to 6 (Fig. 4A). HOMA-IR, a marker of insulin resistance, decreased from 4.57 at baseline to 2.92 at year 6 (Fig. 4B). Safety Analysis

Among the 2648 patients included in the safety set, a total of 1165 AEs were reported in 531 patients (20.05%). Stroke occurred in 21 patients (0.79%). Bladder cancer was reported in three patients (0.11%), all of whom were newly diagnosed and had no prior history. Other reported events included hypoglycemia (n = 1, 0.04%), fractures (n = 15, 0.57%), and myocardial infarction (n = 3, 0.11%).

Discussion

This multicenter, real-world study evaluated the long-term renal safety, glycemic durability, and metabolic effects of lobeglitazone in Korean patients with T2DM over a follow-up period of up to 6 years. The principal findings indicate that long-term lobeglitazone use was associated with sustained glycemic control, stable renal function across all stages of CKD, and a favorable safety profile in routine clinical practice.

A key finding of this study was the absence of significant eGFR deterioration across all CKD stages, including stages 3 and 4, over up to 6 years of lobeglitazone treatment—groups generally considered at higher risk of progressive renal function loss. Although the observational design of this study precludes causal inference, and the absence of a control group limits direct comparison with the natural history of DKD, these findings are consistent with prior observational data suggesting that TZD-based therapy may not accelerate renal function decline.

Regarding albuminuria, the majority of patients exhibited a stable clinical course, with approximately 80% maintaining their baseline albuminuria category throughout follow-up (Table 2). While the observed stabilization of albuminuria category does not establish active renoprotection—given that UACR reduction, rather than mere stabilization, is the primary therapeutic target for long-term renal protection in DKD. Nevertheless, the maintenance of stable albuminuria over 6 years in a real-world cohort—where concomitant medication use and lifestyle factors were not controlled—may be of clinical relevance when assessing the long-term renal safety of lobeglitazone. A post hoc analysis of a randomized phase III trial previously reported a tendency toward UACR improvement with lobeglitazone [5]; the present long-term data corroborate these observations in a real-world setting over an extended time horizon, though the interpretive limitations of an uncontrolled observational design must be acknowledged.

The renal findings of the present study are biologically plausible in light of emerging non-glycemic mechanisms of lobeglitazone. Beyond glucose lowering, lobeglitazone has demonstrated anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects in experimental models, including suppression of the transforming growth factor-β/Smad signaling pathway, a central mediator of renal fibrosis [10, 11]. Structural and mechanistic studies further suggest that lobeglitazone selectively inhibits cyclin-dependent kinase 5-mediated phosphorylation of PPAR-γ at Ser273—a modification implicated in insulin resistance, inflammation, and tissue injury—without full transcriptional activation of PPAR-γ [12]. This selective modulation may explain how lobeglitazone achieves metabolic and renal benefits at a lower dose with a more favorable safety profile than earlier TZDs [13]. Accordingly, the stable renal parameters observed in our cohort may in part reflect integrated anti-inflammatory, anti-fibrotic, and endothelial-protective effects rather than glycemic improvement alone.

The durability of glycemic control observed in this study was also notable. HbA1c levels decreased significantly within the first year of treatment and remained stable through year 6, with nearly half of the patients achieving the target HbA1c < 7.0% at long-term follow-up (Fig. 3B). Glycemic control was maintained even among patients who did not receive additional glucose-lowering agents throughout the observation period, underscoring the intrinsic durability of lobeglitazone therapy. This sustained efficacy is closely linked to the observed improvement in insulin resistance, as reflected by the significant reduction in HOMA-IR, along with preservation of β-cell function, as indicated by stable HOMA-β values (Fig. 4B). These findings are consistent with the established mechanism of TZDs in alleviating insulin resistance and reducing β-cell workload, thereby supporting long-term metabolic control [5, 7].

From a metabolic standpoint, the modest weight gain observed during the first year of treatment was followed by stabilization without further increase over subsequent years (Fig. 4A). This pattern may help alleviate longstanding concerns regarding TZD-associated weight gain in clinical practice. Additionally, improvements in lipid parameters, including increased HDL cholesterol and reduced LDL cholesterol (Fig. 4C), further support the potential cardiometabolic benefits of lobeglitazone, particularly in a population at high cardiovascular risk.

The safety profile of lobeglitazone in this large real-world cohort was reassuring. The overall incidence of adverse events was consistent with previous reports, and events of special interest—including heart failure, fractures, and bladder cancer—occurred at low rates. Although three incident cases of bladder cancer were identified during follow-up, the incidence rate (0.11%) was numerically lower than that reported in previous pioglitazone trials [14, 15]. These findings align with accumulating real-world data supporting the long-term safety of lobeglitazone and suggest that concerns historically associated with the TZD class may be less pronounced with this agent [16]. Our findings expand upon this literature by providing the longest clinical follow-up data to date, spanning up to 6 years in a specialized tertiary care setting.

In the contemporary treatment landscape, where sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists have become central to DKD management [17], the role of insulin-sensitizing therapies warrants reappraisal. Within the treatment framework outlined by the KDIGO 2026 Clinical Practice Guideline for Diabetes Management in CKD, which prioritizes SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists as foundational pharmacotherapies, thiazolidinediones occupy a later position as additional glucose-lowering agents [17]. In this context, the present findings may be of relevance for patients in whom guideline-directed first- and second-line therapies have already been optimized but further glycemic control remains necessary, or for those in whom SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists are contraindicated, not tolerated, or inaccessible. Furthermore, pharmacokinetic studies have demonstrated minimal drug–drug interactions between lobeglitazone and commonly used antidiabetic agents [18–21], supporting its integration into combination treatment strategies across a broad range of renal function.

This study has several limitations. The retrospective observational design introduces the possibility of selection bias and residual confounding, including lifestyle factors and medication adherence that could not be fully accounted for. In addition, the continuous use of other reno-protective agents during the study period may have influenced renal outcomes despite sensitivity analyses. Furthermore, the study employed an observed-case analysis without imputation for missing data, and the patient cohort decreased substantially over time. Consequently, this observed-case approach and the presence of sporadic missing clinical values led to minor discrepancies in the total number of evaluable patients (n) across different parameters and longitudinal figures at specific intervals. Patients retained through extended follow-up are likely more adherent and clinically stable and may not represent the broader starting population. This concern applies particularly to UACR, where the number of evaluable patients was substantially smaller than the overall efficacy cohort from the outset, limiting the robustness of long-term albuminuria conclusions. Additionally, UACR was collected on an opportunistic basis from routine clinical records rather than as a mandatory study requirement, which further contributed to the higher rate of missing albuminuria data relative to eGFR. Moreover, formal sex-stratified outcome analyses were not performed because the present study was not originally designed or statistically powered to evaluate sex-specific differences in renal outcomes, and such analyses were not prespecified. Finally, as the study population consisted exclusively of Korean patients, generalizability to other ethnic groups may be limited.

Conclusions

This LUCKY study provides real-world evidence that long-term lobeglitazone therapy is not associated with deterioration of renal function or worsening of albuminuria status across all stages of CKD in patients with T2DM, while demonstrating sustained glycemic efficacy and a favorable safety profile over up to 6 years of follow-up. Lobeglitazone may therefore represent a clinically relevant option for patients with T2DM and CKD, particularly in those requiring durable glycemic control without evidence of renal harm. Prospective controlled studies are warranted to further characterize its long-term renal effects.
