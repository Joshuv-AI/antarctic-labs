Decoding functional changes in the brain following ischemic stroke: a multimodal feature approach integrating fNIRS with machine learning and deep learning
Authors: Hong Xu, Cunyuan Luan, Jiawen Yin, Boyang Xu, Yuhui Chen, Huabo Liu, Tao Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIschemic stroke (IS) induces significant structural and functional remodelling of brain networks. While functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) offers high temporal resolution for monitoring cerebral hemodynamics, its potential for capturing complex dysfunction patterns remains underutilized. This study investigated task-evoked and resting-state fNIRS responses to decode neurovascular deficits in IS patients compared to normal controls (NCs).MethodsA total of 160 participants (115 IS patients and 45 NCs) were enrolled and underwent fNIRS recording across three paradigms: resting state, verbal fluency task (VFT), and right-hand gripping task (RHGT). Hemodynamic features including oxyhemoglobin (HbO), deoxy-hemoglobin (HbR), and total hemoglobin (HbT) were extracted for functional connectivity analysis (resting state) and cortical activation assessment (task-based). Three traditional machine learning classifiers (Gaussian Naïve Bayes (GNB), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Logistic Regression (LR)) and a Transformer-based deep learning model were constructed to classify IS patients from NCs. SHAP analysis was performed to identify the most discriminative features. Statistical comparisons between groups were conducted using multiple linear regression models, adjusting for age, sex, brain lesions, and bilateral hand functional status.ResultsDuring resting-state recording, IS patients exhibited widespread reductions in functional connectivity within sensorimotor regions, particularly involving bilateral primary motor cortex (M1), premotor and supplementary motor cortex (PMC), and primary somatosensory cortex (PSC), with the most pronounced alterations observed in HbO- and HbT-derived signals (p < 0.05). During the VFT, IS patients showed significant hemodynamic abnormalities in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and the right Brodmann Area 45 (BA45), characterized by elevated HbR concentrations (p < 0.05). Additionally, a significant increase in HbT was detected at channel 16, predominantly localized in the left frontopolar area (FPA) (p < 0.05). During theRHGT, IS patients exhibited a significant decrease in HbO at channel 26, predominantly localized in the left DLPFC (p < 0.05), along with non-significant downward trends in HbR and HbT (p > 0.05). The Transformer model consistently outperformed all conventional classifiers across three paradigms, achieving peak performance with raw gripping-task data (AUC = 0.86, accuracy = 86%, sensitivity = 100%, specificity = 78%). SHAP analysis identified sensorimotor and prefrontal regions as the most discriminative features for IS classification.ConclusionThis study revealed distinct hemodynamic abnormalities in IS patients across resting-state, VFT, and RHGT paradigms. The Transformer model consistently outperformed traditional classifiers, with SHAP analysis confirming sensorimotor and prefrontal regions as key discriminative features. These findings support fNIRS combined with deep learning as a promising approach for non-invasive post-stroke functional assessment and rehabilitation planning.Keywords: fNIRS, Ischemic stroke, Deep learning, Machine learning

Introduction

Stroke is characterized by high incidence, mortality, and disability rates. It is the second leading cause of death and disability worldwide and the leading cause in China, severely impacting public health and quality of life (Feigin et al., 2021; Ji et al., 2025a; Tu et al., 2023). Among stroke subtypes, ischemic stroke (IS) is the most common, accounting for 69.6% ∼ 70.8% of all cases (Wang et al., 2017). Early identification and assessment of structural and functional changes in the brains of IS patients, coupled with the timely initiation of effective rehabilitation interventions, is pivotal to improving patient outcomes. In particular, early objective evaluation of brain function holds significant importance for determining the optimal window for initiating rehabilitation and formulating individualized intervention strategies.

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) is a non-invasive, optical brain imaging technique that uses near-infrared light to detect changes in blood oxygen levels in the cerebral cortex (Jöbsis, 1977; Yücel et al., 2017). Based on the neurovascular coupling mechanism, fNIRS monitors concentration changes in oxyhemoglobin (HbO), deoxy-hemoglobin (HbR), and total hemoglobin (HbT) to infer cortical physiological states and functional activity (Jöbsis, 1977; Yücel et al., 2017). fNIRS is safe, offers high temporal and spatial resolution, is relatively resistant to motion artifacts, and is well tolerated by participants. Consequently, fNIRS has been widely adopted in studies of motor-related brain activation, including finger movement (Ma et al., 2023), walking and running task (Lim et al., 2022) and motor skill learning (Vermeij et al., 2017).

The canonical hemodynamic response pattern in fNIRS—increased HbO accompanied by decreased HbR—is generally considered indicative of cortical activation (Ferrari and Quaresima, 2012), however, this pattern is not universally observed. Xia et al. reported that the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) exhibited a significant increase in HbR during intermittent theta burst stimulation, while the contralateral DLPFC showed decreased HbR during and after stimulation (Xia et al., 2025a). Furthermore, HbR signals have been shown to exhibit more direct coupling with neuronal activity and shorter temporal delays, potentially rendering them more sensitive to activation responses in certain task contexts such as verbal fluency and motor paradigms (Cui et al., 2010). Takagi et al. comprehensively characterized cortical activation patterns using HbO, HbR, and HbT data during clenched-jaw and resting states, and applied deep learning to distinguish between the two conditions with an accuracy of 90% (Takagi et al., 2020). These findings suggest that a comprehensive analysis integrating HbO, HbR, and HbT data is essential for fully characterizing brain functional changes.

Despite these advantages, fNIRS has several recognized limitations. The signals it acquires can be affected by various physiological noises and head movement artifacts, and traditional analysis methods are insufficient to capture the complex temporal dynamics and dependencies of the signals, thereby reducing the accuracy and robustness of feature extraction (Goldstein et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2022). Moreover, prior work (Goldstein et al., 2025; Lanka et al., 2022) has largely been based on conventional signal processing pipelines, which have notable limitations in analytical precision and interpretability, constraining the translation of fNIRS into clinical settings. More importantly, there is no objective, high-performance functional assessment framework based on fNIRS suitable for IS. This deficiency limits the application of fNIRS in the precision assessment and rehabilitation of IS.

With the advancement of artificial intelligence, machine learning and deep learning algorithms have been increasingly applied in the field of medical image analysis, achieving significant progress in the classification, prediction, and feature extraction of brain functional imaging data (Castiglioni et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022). Compared with traditional statistical methods, machine learning can uncover complex non-linear brain functional patterns through feature-based modelling, while deep learning architectures such as the Transformer can automatically learn discriminative features directly from high-dimensional raw data, reducing reliance on manual feature engineering (Vaswani et al., 2017). By accurately modelling the temporal dependencies in the data, these approaches can improve the accuracy of brain functional state recognition (Bunterngchit et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2022).

In the present study, we employed three standardized task paradigms (resting state, verbal fluency task (VFT), and right-hand gripping task(RHGT)) to acquire fNIRS data. fNIRS-based IS classification models were constructed using machine learning (Gaussian Naïve Bayes (GNB), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Logistic Regression (LR)) and a deep learning architecture (Transformer), with comparative performance evaluations conducted across all models. To enhance model interpretability, we applied the SHAP algorithm to quantify the contributions of key brain functional connectivity features and regional activation features to model classification. Through these analyses, this study aims to delineate characteristic patterns of brain network connectivity and cortical activation in IS patients, providing an evidence base for individualized rehabilitation interventions based on brain functional remodelling.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Hospital (2023BJYYEC-212-01), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. We recruited patients with IS from the Department of Neurology at Beijing Hospital from May 2023 to September 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) onset within 1 month; (3) confirmed diagnosis of IS by brain MRI; (4) alert, hemodynamically stable, and able to cooperate with the examination; (5) right-hand dominant before disease onset. Exclusion criteria included: (1) hemorrhagic stroke; (2) right-hand muscle strength < grade 3 assessed by Medical Research Council (MRC) scale, precluding cooperation with task performance; (3) right-hand musculoskeletal disorders; (4) other neurological diseases affecting neurological function, such as Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, or multiple sclerosis; (5) malignant tumors or severe cardiopulmonary, hepatic, or renal disease, with an expected survival <1 year; (6) alcohol or drug dependence. We concurrently recruited healthy volunteers as normal controls (NCs).

Demographic and clinical data were collected for all participants, including age, sex, medical history (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and coronary heart disease), brain lesions, bilateral hand functional status, as well as smoking and alcohol consumption status. Bilateral handgrip strength was assessed and graded using the 6-point Medical Research Council (MRC) scale, ranging from 0 (no visible contraction) to 5 (normal strength).

fNIRS data acquisition

All fNIRS data were acquired using the NirSmart-6000 A system (Danyang Huichuang Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., China). The lowest point of the cap was aligned with the brow line, and the posterior edge was aligned with the external occipital protuberance. The elastic cap was equipped with 14 source probes and 14 detector probes, with a source–detector separation of 3 cm. The system operates at wavelengths of 730 nm and 850 nm, with a data acquisition sampling rate of 11 Hz. The center of each channel was defined as the primary brain region monitored by that channel. Changes in HbO, HbR, and HbT were recorded across different cortical regions during each task condition. A 35-channel layout was used for resting-state andRHGT recording, and a 19-channel configuration was used for VFT acquisition. Each channel was assigned to a primary brain region based on the spatial correspondence between the channel center and underlying cortical anatomy. For channels spanning multiple brain regions, the region with the largest proportional coverage was designated as the representative region. In the 19-channel configuration, the regions of interest (ROI) primarily include DLPFC, Brodmann Area 45 (BA45), frontopolar area (FPA), and orbitofrontal area (OFA). In the 35-channel configuration, the specific ROIs include primary somatosensory cortex (PSC), primary motor cortex (M1), premotor and supplementary motor cortex (PMC), DLPFC, BA45 and FPA (see Fig. 1).

Task paradigms

(1)Resting-state paradigm

Resting-state paradigm

Participants were instructed to close their eyes and relax for five minutes in a quiet, undisturbed room. Prior to data acquisition, each participant was instructed not to speak or make any unnecessary movements during the recording period. (See Supplementary Materials 1: Fig. S1).

(2)VFT

VFT

The VFT comprised three blocks. The first block was a pre-task rest phase lasting 30 s, during which participants were prompted to repeat ‘1, 2, 3, 4, 5.’ The second block was the word-generation phase, lasting 60 s. Participants were presented with three Chinese characters— for example, ‘Bai,’ ‘Bei,’ or ‘Da’—and were instructed to produce as many words as possible beginning with the given characters. The final block was the post-task rest phase, lasting 70 s. Participants were again prompted to repeat ‘1, 2, 3, 4, 5’ until the recording was complete. (See Supplementary Materials 1: Fig. S1).

(3)RHGT

RHGT

Since all enrolled participants were right-hand dominant before stroke onset, the right-hand gripping task was employed as a standardized motor paradigm to ensure consistency in motor demands across all participants. Given that the cohort included IS patients with both left and right hemispheric lesions, the right hand may represent either the affected or unaffected side depending on individual lesion laterality. This task consisted of five identical blocks, each lasting 35 s. Each block included two stages: active phase (15 s), during which the system issued a start command and the participant was instructed to repeatedly clench and unclench their fist with maximum force; and rest phase (20 s), during which the participant stopped grasping while maintaining the same sitting position and avoiding any unnecessary movements. Before the task began, the experimenter explained the procedure to the participant in detail and ensured that they adopted the correct posture: sitting upright with the right hand placed on the thigh. Throughout the task, the experimenter recorded the participant's grasping performance (see Supplementary Materials 1: Fig. S2).

fNIRS data analysis

(1)fNIRS data preprocessing

fNIRS data preprocessing

Data preprocessing was performed using NirSpark 1.8.7 software, which has been adopted in previous fNIRS studies (An et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026). The preprocessing pipeline proceeded as follows: First, raw optical signals underwent manual quality control to assess detector saturation; no saturation was detected in any participant or channel. Subsequently, data segments containing large motion artifacts, abrupt signal fluctuations, obvious discontinuous noise, or time periods unrelated to the experimental task were excluded. Motion artifacts were then identified using an automated detection algorithm, with signal peaks exceeding 6 standard deviations defined as the threshold for detecting abrupt signal changes (spikes or jumps). The detected artifact segments were further verified using a moving standard deviation method and corrected via cubic spline interpolation to minimize interference caused by head movement or probe displacement. The corrected light intensity signals were subsequently converted into optical density (OD), and the resulting OD time series were band-pass filtered (0.01–0.20 Hz) to remove low-frequency drift, high-frequency instrumental noise, and physiological noise including respiration and cardiac activity. Finally, the filtered OD signals were converted into relative concentration changes in HbO and HbR using the Modified Beer–Lambert Law, and the resulting time series were used for subsequent deep learning modelling. To minimize the impact of peripheral hemodynamic signals on the fNIRS measurements, bandpass filtering, motion artifact correction, and a block-design task paradigm with adequate rest intervals were employed rather than short-distance channels to attenuate systemic physiological fluctuations and isolate task-evoked cortical responses.

(2)Functional connectivity analysis

Functional connectivity analysis

We performed functional connectivity (FC) analysis to assess inter-regional activity patterns across different brain regions under each task condition. Time series were extracted from each channel for HbO, HbR, and HbT signals. Pearson correlation coefficients between all channel pairs were calculated to construct a functional connectivity matrix, reflecting temporal synchrony between channels. The resulting correlation matrix was subjected to Fisher's Z-transformation to improve normality. The resulting Z-values were used as functional connectivity strength features for subsequent between-group comparisons and classification model construction.

(3)Block averaging

Block averaging

For the VFT, there were 3 blocks: a 20–30-s pre-task baseline period, a 60-s word-generation period, and 110–115-s post-task baseline period. The two baseline segments were linearly fitted to calculate the average change in hemoglobin concentration for each channel during the word generation phase; the average of the hemoglobin concentration changes across all channels within each ROI was used as the activation value for that ROI. Subsequently, the average values of HbO, HbR, and HbT changes for each channel and each ROI were used as input features for the machine learning model.

For the RHGT, each trial comprised five blocks, with each block consisting of a 15-s task period followed by a 20-s rest period. The mean signal during the 2 s preceding each task onset was used as the baseline. The activation value for each block was defined as the mean change in hemoglobin concentration during the 15-s task period relative to baseline. The final activation value for each participant was obtained by averaging across the five blocks. At the ROI level, activation values were first calculated for each channel and then averaged across all channels within the same ROI. The mean changes in HbO, HbR, and HbT for each channel and each ROI were used as input features for the machine learning model.

Statistical analysis and evaluation

(1)Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality. For continuous variables that followed a normal distribution, variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD) and compared between groups using the independent-samples t-test; non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (first quartile, third quartile) [M (Q1, Q3)] and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. For categorical variables such as sex, smoking, drinking, and hypertension, the chi-square test was used. We compared fNIRS features between IS and NCs across the three paradigms: resting-state, VFT, and RHGT. To account for potential confounding factors, multiple linear regression analysis was subsequently conducted. In each model, the fNIRS-derived feature served as the dependent variable, with study group as the primary independent variable. Age (continuous), sex (categorical), brain lesion (categorical), and bilateral hand functional status were included as covariates. Bilateral hand functional status was derived from clinical muscle strength grading and dichotomized into impaired (MRC score < 5) and normal (MRC score = 5). Based on brain magnetic resonance imaging findings, brain lesions were classified into four categories: no lesion, posterior circulation (infratentorial) lesions, anterior circulation (AC) subcortical lesions, and AC cortical lesions. The regression coefficient (β) and corresponding p-value of the group variable were used to determine whether significant between-group differences in fNIRS features persisted after adjusting for demographic and clinical covariates. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, and all p-values were two-tailed. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) to evaluate model performance.

(2)Machine learning

Machine learning

To address the sample class imbalance, this study employed the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) on the training data. SMOTE generates new minority-class samples through interpolation in the feature space, thereby augmenting minority-class representation and reducing model bias caused by class imbalance. Subsequently, Minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance (mRMR) feature selection was applied to identify the most relevant features while minimizing redundancy. The Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was then applied, combined with 5-fold cross-validation, to select the most informative features for model construction. Given the substantial imbalance in the sample size ratio between the two groups, the dataset was split into training and test sets at a 7:3 ratio.

Machine learning analyses were conducted using Python 3.12.2 (https://www.python.org/) and the scikit-learn package (scikit-learn.org) in PyCharm 2023.3.4. Three algorithms were employed: GNB, SVM, and LR, to build classification models based on the selected features. GNB is a supervised classification algorithm based on Bayes' theorem, which assigns class labels on the basis of posterior probabilities. SVM constructs a decision hyperplane that maximizes the margin between classes. LR is a generalized linear model that predicts class membership by estimating the probability that an observation belongs to a given class. To enhance the interpretability of the classification models, SHAP values were calculated to quantify the contribution of each feature in the model.

(3)Deep learning

Deep learning

To address the class imbalance, we applied data augmentation by adding Gaussian noise. After augmentation, the expanded training set contained 624 samples (312 IS; 312 NC). Data augmentation served three purposes: increasing the effective sample size, enhancing sample diversity to improve generalization, and balancing the class distribution.

The overall model architecture is shown in Supplementary Materials 1: Fig. S3. We employed two strategies: transfer learning and fine-tuning. Transfer learning improves model performance by applying a model pre-trained on a large dataset to a new task. This approach enables high classification accuracy with limited data, as the pre-trained model has already learned generalizable features. In this study, we used the Transformer as the base model. Fine-tuning was subsequently applied to adapt the model to the fNIRS data to achieve IS-versus-NC classification. After training, model performance was evaluated using a held-out (validation set) to assess classification accuracy. The dataset was divided into training, validation, and test sets at a ratio of 70:15:15.

All experiments were conducted using Anaconda 2.5.2 under Windows 11, with Python 3.9.19 and PyTorch 2.3.1. The CPU was an Intel(R) Core (TM) i5-12600KF; the GPU was an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 Super with (12 GB of VRAM; DDR4 memory; 3600 MHz).

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 160 participants were enrolled in this study, all of whom completed at least one fNIRS task paradigm. Of these, 153 completed the resting-state recording (108 IS patients and 45 NCs), 131 completed the VFT (86 IS patients and 45 NCs), and 140 completed the RHGT (95 IS patients and 45 NCs). Compared with NCs, IS patients were significantly older, with a higher proportion of males and greater rates of smoking and alcohol consumption. The IS group also exhibited significantly higher rates of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and coronary heart disease (all p < 0.05). Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1, Table 2, Table 3. Among the 108 IS patients in the resting-state cohort, 16 (14.8%) had posterior circulation (infratentorial) lesions, 92 (85.2%) had anterior circulation (AC) lesions, of which 50 (46.3%) were purely subcortical and 42 (38.9%) involved the cortex with or without subcortical lesions. Regarding the hemispheric distribution of AC lesions, 38 patients (35.2%) showed right hemispheric involvement, 37 (34.3%) showed left hemispheric involvement, and 17 (15.7%) showed bilateral involvement.

Group comparison of fNIRS metrics

Under resting-state conditions, IS patients exhibited a widespread reduction in functional connectivity strength derived from all three hemoglobin components (HbO, HbR, and HbT) compared to NCs. These changes were predominantly localized within sensorimotor-related regions, particularly manifesting as decreased interhemispheric and intrahemispheric connectivity involving the primary motor cortex (M1), premotor and supplementary motor cortex (PMC), primary somatosensory cortex (PSC) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC). Specifically, for HbO, IS patients showed significantly decreased connectivity compared with NCs in the following connections: Compared with NCs, decreased connectivity was found within bilateral PSC, M1, and PMC, and between left PSC–bilateral M1, left PSC–right FPA, right M1–bilateral PMC, right M1–right DLPFC, right M1–right FPA, left M1–bilateral PMC and right PMC-right DLPFC (p < 0.05). For HbR, significantly diminished connectivity was observed within bilateral M1 and PMC, and between left PSC–right M1 and left M1–right PMC (p < 0.05). For HbT, the differences were more extensive. In addition to the aforementioned connections, significantly decreased connectivity was also found between left PSC–right PMC, left PSC–right DLPFC, left M1–right DLPFC, and left PMC–right DLPFC (p < 0.05). (see Fig. 2 and Supplementary Materials 1: Table S1; detailed results are provided in Supplementary Materials 2: Tables S1A and S1B).

Model classification results

Different models exhibited varying levels of discriminative performance across different tasks and feature sets. Compared with traditional machine learning models, the deep learning approach demonstrated significantly superior classification performance on fNIRS data. For the resting-state task, the Transformer model achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.80, an accuracy of 81%, a sensitivity of 77%, and a specificity of 83%, whereas the other models yielded AUC values ranging from 0.58 to 0.65. In the VFT task, the Transformer model also outperformed the conventional classifiers, achieving an AUC of 0.76, an accuracy of 76%, a sensitivity of 82%, and a specificity of 71%. For the RHGT, the Transformer model attained the highest performance, with an AUC of 0.86, an accuracy of 86%, a sensitivity of 100%, and a specificity of 78%, compared with AUC values of 0.61–0.68 for the other models. Discussion

This study revealed distinct patterns of brain region activity in patients with ischemic stroke compared with healthy individuals across different task conditions. Furthermore, we explored the potential utility of various fNIRS features in assessing brain functional changes in IS patients. IS patients exhibited region-specific activation changes, suggesting the engagement of compensatory mechanisms. Machine learning and deep learning methods were employed to classify IS patients and NCs. Among all models, the deep learning models performed best overall, with the best-performing model being the Transformer trained using raw blood oxygen change data from the RHGT, achieving an accuracy of 86%. These findings may facilitate a deeper understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying post-stroke brain functional changes in IS and support more comprehensive brain function assessment and the development of targeted rehabilitation strategies.

In resting-state fNIRS data, IS patients exhibited a significant breakdown in long-range functional connectivity, particularly within the sensorimotor network. Compared with NCs, IS patients exhibited a declining trend in overall functional connectivity strength calculated from HbO, HbR, and HbT during rest, particularly in interhemispheric and intercortical pathways centered on M1, PMC, and PSC. IS leads to impaired neuronal activity in the lesion area and associated motor networks, disrupting effective information integration between cortices, which manifests as a decrease in interhemispheric motor network connectivity (Grefkes and Fink, 2014). Previous studies have also reported that IS patients exhibit a general decrease in motor network functional connectivity at rest, while some prefrontal-motor cortex connections may show compensatory enhancement, reflecting plasticity-driven reorganization of brain function following IS (Park et al., 2011). Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated a significant reduction in functional connectivity within motor-related brain networks after ischemic stroke, with the most prominent change being decreased interhemispheric connectivity between the bilateral M1, which is closely associated with motor impairment (Chi et al., 2018). The present study further found a significant reduction in functional connectivity between the PSC and the M1, suggesting that the sensorimotor integration process is impaired in patients with ischemic stroke. Previous studies have shown that sensory input plays an important role in motor control and motor learning, and disruption of this functional connectivity may further aggravate motor dysfunction and adversely affect the rehabilitation process (Baker et al., 2023).

Building on these findings, SHAP analysis further revealed the relative contributions of various functional connectivity features in the classification model. The results showed that statistically significant connections (such as right M1–left M1, left PMC-right PMC, left PSC–right M1) also carried high SHAP weights in the model. These features not only exhibited significant differences at the group level but also demonstrated strong discriminative power for individual classification. Functional connectivity features within the motor cortex and between the motor and sensory cortices demonstrate strong potential as biomarkers for assessing motor function in IS patients. Functional connectivity of motor-related brain networks can assess hand function recovery and reflect the severity and recovery potential of motor dysfunction after IS (Hong et al., 2019; Plantin et al., 2021). In a study on biomarkers related to stroke and motor function, changes in connectivity patterns between motor regions combined with machine learning algorithms could effectively distinguish between mild/moderate and severe upper-limb motor dysfunction, with an accuracy of 91.30% (Cai et al., 2024). In addition, functional connectivity within motor and between motor-sensory areas has significant potential for predicting rehabilitation outcomes and guiding individualized rehabilitation intervention plans. Chen et al. used resting-state functional connectivity in bilateral M1 regions to assess the efficacy of a home-based motor training telerehabilitation program for stroke patients, and found that improvements in motor function were accompanied by enhanced interhemispheric functional connectivity in the M1 regions (Chen et al., 2020). Changes in motor network functional connectivity can assess the impact of different transcranial magnetic stimulation protocols on motor recovery in the early stages of IS and provide basis for the development of individualized rehabilitation strategies (Juan et al., 2021). Resting-state motor network functional connectivity not only serves as an important feature to distinguish IS patients from NCs but also reflects patients' motor function status and rehabilitation potential, providing robust neuroimaging evidence for the development of individualized rehabilitation plans (Cassidy et al., 2022; Rizor et al., 2024).

We also extracted cortical activation features from fNIRS data during the VFT and conducted statistical comparisons and SHAP interpretability analyses. Statistical analysis revealed that IS patients exhibited significant hemodynamic abnormalities in the left DLPFC compared with NCs, characterized by a significant increase in HbR and a no-significant decrease in HbO concentrations, indicating reduced cortical activation in this region. Although the right DLPFC did not reach significance at the ROI level, channel-level analysis revealed a consistent pattern of hemodynamic change. These findings suggest that IS not only compromises motor-related cortical areas but also exerts a substantial impact on prefrontal regions critically involved in language generation, executive control, and higher-order cognitive regulation (El Husseini et al., 2023). From a neuroanatomical perspective, the left DLPFC is widely recognized as a core hub for executive function, encompassing working memory, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control (Miller and Cohen, 2001). Reduced activation in this region following IS may directly underlie the executive dysfunction and attentional deficits commonly observed in post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI), which affects approximately 30–50% of stroke survivors and significantly hinders functional recovery and return to daily activities (El Husseini et al., 2023). Notably, the hemodynamic abnormalities observed in right BA45 are of particular interest. BA45, as a key component of Broca's area, is primarily associated with semantic retrieval, phonemic fluency, and language production in the dominant (typically left) hemisphere (Liu et al., 2023). The involvement of the right-hemispheric homologue during the VFT in IS patients may reflect a compensatory recruitment mechanism, whereby the contralesional hemisphere is engaged to support language processing when the dominant hemisphere is functionally compromised (Liu et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023). Additionally, the elevated HbR and decreased HbO observed in right BA45 suggest that this compensatory recruitment may be insufficient or dysfunctional, potentially indicating that bilateral prefrontal language networks are disrupted following IS. This bilateral involvement underscores the complexity of post-stroke language reorganization and suggests that cognitive decline following IS may not be confined to a single hemisphere but rather reflects a broader disruption of the distributed frontally mediated cognitive network, encompassing executive, linguistic, and attentional functions simultaneously.

SHAP analysis further corroborated these statistical findings by revealing the relative contributions of activation features to the classification model. The results demonstrated that activation features in the left DLPFC and right BA45 during the VFT contributed significantly to model classification, indicating that hemodynamic abnormalities in these prefrontal regions possess considerable discriminative power for distinguishing IS patients from NCs at the individual level. This convergence between statistical group-level differences and individual-level classification importance strengthens the evidence for these regions as potential neuroimaging biomarkers for post-stroke cognitive assessment. In previous studies, prefrontal regions have been recognized as playing a crucial role in cognitive control (Aliramezani et al., 2025; Miller and Cohen, 2001). fNIRS is commonly used to monitor frontal cortex activity to reflect changes in higher cognitive functions such as language generation, executive control, and working memory (Liu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025). Liu et al. demonstrated that fNIRS could effectively capture improvements in prefrontal activation and working memory function in IS patients following high-frequency repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) (Liu et al., 2024). Zhang et al. established a diagnostic model using fNIRS multidimensional data based on multi-domain cognitive tests to identify patients with mild cognitive impairment, achieving an accuracy of 80.77% (Zhang et al., 2023). Moreover, the identification of specific prefrontal regions with impaired activation may facilitate the development of individualized neuromodulation protocols, such as targeting the left DLPFC with rTMS or transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) to enhance executive function recovery, or modulating the right BA45 to promote language network reorganization.

During the RHGT, only CH26 in the left DLPFC showed a significant decrease in HbO in IS patients compared with NCs. In addition, at the ROI level, both the left DLPFC and the left FPA exhibited an increasing trend in HbO and a decreasing trend in HbR, although these trends did not reach statistical significance. These findings may reflect the additional recruitment of frontopolar and prefrontal resources during motor task execution in IS patients. Previous studies have demonstrated that the FPA is involved in higher-order motor planning and execution (Mansouri et al., 2017), and may serve a compensatory role in IS patients to offset functional deficits arising from lesions within the motor network (Yang et al., 2024). The DLPFC, beyond its established role in cognitive control, is also closely associated with attention regulation and strategy selection during motor execution (Bigliassi and Filho, 2022; Cao et al., 2022; Mihara et al., 2008). The hemodynamic changes observed in this region suggest that IS patients require additional cognitive control resources to accomplish motor tasks that healthy individuals perform with minimal prefrontal engagement. Meanwhile, neither the decreases in activation in the left M1 and PMC nor the increase in the left PSC reached statistical significance in IS patients in our study. Previous studies have shown a compensatory inhibitory mechanism within the premotor and primary motor cortices following IS (Dennis et al., 2011). The PMC is a critical region for motor preparation and coordination, and alterations in its activity patterns may be closely related to the severity of motor dysfunction and could serve as an important indicator of motor function reorganization in IS patients (Liu et al., 2018). Collectively, these findings suggest that cortical hemodynamic changes during motor tasks extend beyond the core motor regions (PMC and M1) to encompass cognitive control areas such as FPA and DLPFC, reflecting that motor execution after IS depends on the engagement of a broader, distributed cortical network (Dennis et al., 2011; Hensel et al., 2023).

SHAP analysis further corroborated these findings by revealing the relative contributions of cortical activation features during the RHGT to model classification. Across all models, the left PMC, left DLPFC, and left FPA consistently exhibited high contribution levels, aligning well with the results of statistical analyses. In the Transformer model specifically, right M1 demonstrated the highest feature importance, while left BA45, left FPA, and right PSC also contributed substantially to classification. These results collectively indicate that IS patients engage multi-network collaboration during motor task performance, involving not only motor-related regions but also cortical areas subserving cognition and sensation. These observations are consistent with prior research. Yang et al. predicted fall risk in stroke patients using fNIRS-derived activation features from the PMC and DLPFC regions (Yang et al., 2024). Chen et al. reported that IS patients exhibited significant bilateral sensorimotor cortex activation during both unilateral and bilateral fine motor tasks, with more pronounced activation during bilateral coordination (Chen et al., 2024). Previous studies on motor rehabilitation mechanisms have further indicated that activation levels of the motor cortex and prefrontal regions in IS patients are closely associated with motor recovery outcomes (Du et al., 2019; Huo et al., 2024; Ji et al., 2025b; Kim et al., 2024).

In this study, deep learning models, particularly the Transformer, demonstrated superior performance in classifying IS patients versus NCs based on fNIRS signals, exhibiting higher accuracy and generalizability than traditional machine learning approaches (LR, SVM, and GNB) when processing raw time-series data. The best-performing model was the Transformer trained on raw hemoglobin concentration change data from the RHGT, achieving an accuracy of 86%. This finding is consistent with a growing body of evidence highlighting the advantages of deep learning architectures in fNIRS data modelling. Deep neural networks, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, have been shown to automatically extract spatiotemporal features from raw fNIRS signals, substantially enhancing classification performance (Ho et al., 2022). For instance, Ma et al. proposed a 1D improved CNN-ResNet model that classifies fatigue states based on high-dimensional multi-channel fNIRS data, achieving an accuracy of 97.78% (Ma et al., 2025). Ho et al. constructed a CNN-LSTM-based fNIRS framework for multi-level classification of Alzheimer's disease, reaching an accuracy of 87.7%, significantly outperforming random forest and SVM methods (Ho et al., 2022). An increasing number of studies have demonstrated that deep learning possesses superior pattern recognition capabilities across diverse fNIRS applications, including brain disease identification, brain-computer interfaces, and multimodal fusion (Huang et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2024; Rabbani and Islam, 2023; Takagi et al., 2020; Xia et al., 2025b).

Regarding fNIRS feature selection, previous studies have predominantly focused on HbO signals. However, the present study found that features derived from HbR data accounted for a substantial proportion of both statistically significant group differences and high-ranking SHAP contributions across all three paradigms, underscoring the importance of incorporating multiple hemoglobin components. The canonical neurovascular coupling model predicts that cortical activation is accompanied by increased HbO and decreased HbR (Ferrari and Quaresima, 2012). In IS patients, however, this coupling may be disrupted. The prominent HbR alterations observed in our study may reflect several pathophysiological mechanisms: impaired neurovascular coupling, whereby the hemodynamic response fails to adequately compensate for increased metabolic demand, resulting in a relative increase in local deoxygenated hemoglobin; disrupted cerebral autoregulation, leading to altered oxygen extraction dynamics in perilesional and remote cortical regions; and the inherent signal characteristics of HbR itself, which exhibits more direct coupling with neuronal activity and shorter temporal delays than HbO (Cui et al., 2010), rendering it potentially more sensitive to subtle cortical dysfunction following stroke. The predominance of HbR-derived features in both statistical analyses and SHAP-based model contributions suggests that HbR may serve as a more sensitive biomarker for detecting post-stroke cortical dysfunction than HbO alone. These results reinforce the notion that a comprehensive analysis integrating HbO, HbR, and HbT data is essential for fully characterizing brain functional changes in IS patients. The present study extends the application scope of fNIRS combined with deep learning, validates the potential of the Transformer model in multi-task and multi-state signal modelling, and provides a foundation for the future development of intelligent neural rehabilitation support systems.

Limitations

Although this study yielded promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, short-distance channels were not incorporated in the current optode configuration, and the bandpass filter applied (0.01–0.2 Hz) did not exclude Mayer waves (approximately 0.1 Hz). Although the block-design paradigm, baseline correction, and multi-block averaging can attenuate the influence of non-time-locked systemic physiological oscillations, future studies should consider implementing short-distance channel regression or additional filtering techniques to more effectively separate cerebral from extra-cerebral hemodynamic signals.

Certain imbalances in baseline characteristics, such as age and sex, existed between the two groups. Although multiple linear regression was employed to adjust for these covariates, residual confounding effects may still influence the generalizability of our findings. The sample size was relatively modest, and the class imbalance between IS patients and NCs may have affected model training and generalizability, despite the application of SMOTE and data augmentation strategies. All participants were recruited from a single center, which may limit the external validity of the findings. Additionally, the present study employed a standardized RHGT for all participants regardless of lesion laterality. As the cohort included patients with both left and right hemispheric infarctions, the right hand could represent either the affected or unaffected side, potentially introducing heterogeneity in cortical activation patterns. Although bilateral hand functional status was included as a covariate in the statistical analyses, the confounding effects of hemispheric dominance and lesion laterality on fNIRS features were not fully addressed. Future studies should consider stratifying analyses by lesion side or incorporating lesion laterality as an additional covariate in the classification models to disentangle these effects.

This study was cross-sectional in design and thus could not capture the dynamic evolution of brain functional changes over the course of stroke recovery. Longitudinal studies incorporating serial fNIRS assessments at different post-stroke time points would provide more comprehensive insights into the trajectory of cortical reorganization and its relationship with functional recovery. Future studies with larger, more closely matched cohorts are needed to validate these results. Third, the spatial resolution of fNIRS is inherently limited compared with functional MRI, and the channel-to-region assignment approach may not fully capture the complexity of cortical activation patterns. Future studies integrating fNIRS with other neuroimaging modalities may help overcome this limitation. Despite these limitations, the present study provides preliminary evidence supporting the feasibility of combining fNIRS with deep learning for non-invasive post-stroke brain functional assessment.

Conclusions

This study identified distinct hemodynamic abnormalities in IS patients across three fNIRS paradigms: widespread reductions in sensorimotor functional connectivity (involving M1, PMC, and PSC) during the resting state; dysfunction in the left DLPFC and right BA45 during the VFT; and a focal decrease in activation within the left DLPFC during the RHGT. The Transformer model consistently outperformed traditional classifiers, achieving peak performance in the RHGT (AUC = 0.86, accuracy = 86%). SHAP analysis confirmed that sensorimotor and prefrontal features contributed the most discriminative information, with certain statistically non-significant features exhibiting high model importance, suggesting that deep learning can capture nonlinear patterns overlooked by conventional approaches. These findings support the integration of fNIRS with deep learning as a promising tool for non-invasive post-stroke brain functional assessment and individualized rehabilitation planning.
