BeSafe-Bench: Unveiling Behavioral Safety Risks of Situated Agents in Functional Environments
Yuxuan Li, Yi Lin, Peng Wang, Shiming Liu, Xuetao Wei

Abstract
The rapid evolution of Large Multimodal Models (LMMs) has enabled agents to perform complex digital and physical tasks, yet their deployment as autonomous decision-makers introduces substantial unintentional behavioral safety risks. However, the absence of a comprehensive safety benchmark remains a major bottleneck, as existing evaluations rely on low-fidelity environments, simulated APIs, or narrowly scoped tasks. To address this gap, we present BeSafe-Bench (BSB), a benchmark for exposing behavioral safety risks of situated agents in functional environments, covering four representative domains: Web, Mobile, Embodied VLM, and Embodied VLA. Using functional environments, we construct a diverse instruction space by augmenting tasks with nine categories of safety-critical risks, and adopt a hybrid evaluation framework that combines rule-based checks with LLM-as-a-judge reasoning to assess real environmental impacts. Evaluating 13 popular agents reveals a concerning trend: even the best-performing agent completes fewer than 40% of tasks while fully adhering to safety constraints, and strong task performance frequently coincides with severe safety violations. These findings underscore the urgent need for improved safety alignment before deploying agentic systems in real-world settings.

The rapid development of AI agents, catalyzed by transformative breakthroughs in Large Multimodal Models
(LMMs)
(
37
)
, has extended their reach across a wide spectrum of domains, including web automation
(
30
;
9
)
, mobile applications
(
20
;
43
)
, and embodied intelligence. However, the velocity of this technological advancement has significantly outpaced the development of corresponding safety safeguards. As autonomous agents are increasingly empowered with sophisticated tools to execute complex, multi-step tasks, many systems are being deployed without rigorous assessment of their potential safety risks
(
26
)
. There exists a profound discrepancy between an agent’s task-completion proficiency and its behavioral safety assurance. Such a gap not only risks catastrophic failures but also leads to subtle yet pervasive harms—ranging from the unauthorized leakage of sensitive personal or corporate data on public platforms to physical collisions caused by robotic manipulators in embodied settings
(
52
;
25
;
27
)
. Unlike the harmful textual outputs directly generated by LLMs
(
57
;
36
;
38
)
, the consequences of agent-driven behavioral risks manifest more directly in the physical and digital world, yet they remain significantly more challenging to analyze and evaluate.
Consequently, establishing a robust benchmark for assessing the behavioral safety of AI agents is both essential and imperative.

Existing research on agent safety evaluation primarily focuses on the safety of generated content within text-based scenarios
(
38
;
3
;
51
)
. However, as agents are increasingly equipped with tools to execute actions, evaluating content safety alone has become insufficient. To address this challenge, several studies have proposed safety assessments centered on the consequences of agent actions, a concept formally defined as behavioral safety
(
46
;
50
)
. Despite their contributions, these existing frameworks often suffer from a limited scope of applicability. Most are confined to a single domain, such as web navigation or embodied AI, or they rely on LLMs to simulate environmental interactions
(
27
;
23
)
. While such simulations can broaden benchmark coverage, the LLM-modeled APIs tend to be overly simplistic and static.
This stands in stark contrast to the dynamic and diverse interaction patterns between agents and environments in the real world, creating a significant fidelity gap. Consequently, these benchmarks often produce evaluation results that exhibit a substantial discrepancy from real-world safety performance.

In this paper,
we present BeSafe-Bench (BSB), a comprehensive benchmarking framework for evaluating the behavioral safety risks of situated agents within functional environments.
These environments provide executable tools, interfaces, and feedback with real functional effects, rather than relying on purely textual LLM-based simulations. As illustrated in Figure
1
, we categorize situated agents into four primary domains: Web, Mobile, Embodied VLM (high-level planning), and Embodied VLA (low-level manipulation). For each domain, we leverage corresponding realistic environment simulators to establish interactive platforms. Within these functional environments, we construct safety-critical tasks based on executable primitive actions, together with predefined safety risk categories and contextual factors. During evaluation, we record both environment states and agent trajectories, and employ a hybrid evaluation framework that integrates rule-based systems with LLM-based reasoning to simultaneously assess task completion and safety coverage. Each task in BSB consists of five essential components: a task execution environment, user instructions, a safety risk type, a task completion evaluator, and a safety evaluator. Furthermore, tasks and environments are strictly isolated from one another, enabling high-fidelity simulations of real-world agent interaction patterns while mitigating potential real-world consequences.

BSB provides 1312 executable tasks across diverse scenarios, and a detailed comparison with existing agent safety evaluation benchmarks is presented in Table
1
. Utilizing BSB, we evaluated the performance of 13 popular LLM-driven agents, encompassing both general-purpose and specialized models. Due to variations in task complexity and available tools, evaluation results exhibit significant differentiation across environments. Excluding the Mobile environment, the safety coverage of agents in other domains remains below 60%. Furthermore, fewer than 40% of agents are capable of successfully completing tasks while maintaining safety throughout their execution. Notably, in up to 41% of cases, agents completed the assigned tasks but simultaneously engaged in unsafe behaviors. Our further analysis reveals that agents exhibit insufficient protection of sensitive information and harmful content, while also struggling to maintain safety awareness within complex, sequential task scenarios.
In summary, the main contributions of this work are as follows:

•

We propose BeSafe-Bench, a new and comprehensive benchmark focusing on agent behavioral safety within functional environments, spanning four distinct agent domains (Web, Mobile, Embodied VLM, and Embodied VLA) and covering four typical agent task scenarios with 1312 executable tasks.

•

We propose a new safety benchmark construction paradigm that integrates original executable tasks with predefined safety risk categories and factors. This benchmark enables the simultaneous evaluation of task performance and safety, supported by a set of metrics including task success rate, safety rate, and their joint distribution over success and safety outcomes.

•

We propose a hybrid evaluation framework designed for agent trajectories and environment states. By combining rule-based and LLM-based methodologies, we evaluate 13 popular LLM-driven agents and identify a critical challenge: current agents struggle to sustain safety awareness across complex, multi-step task scenarios. Empirically, even the best-performing agent attains a success-and-safe rate of below 40%. In the worst case, up to 41% of task instances are completed successfully while simultaneously involving unsafe behaviors.

Related Work

2.1
GUI and Embodied Agents

The rapid progress of LLMs has catalyzed the emergence of agents, and prior research has largely explored how LLMs can be leveraged in different ways to construct agents operating in two dominant environments: GUI and embodied settings. ReAct
(
41
)
integrates the synergy of reasoning and acting in LLMs by interleaving the generation of reasoning traces and task-specific actions, facilitating the transition from LLM content output to behavioral planning for general agents. The emergence of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) introduced visual perception, enabling LLMs to move beyond mere planning toward practical execution in agent applications
(
8
)
. Beyond this, memory, tool invocation, and model reinforcement are also essential means of achieving high-performance agents. For GUI agents, AgentWorkflowMemory
(
35
)
extracts common task workflows from past experiences and stores them in the agent’s memory to guide future task-solving processes, specifically applying this to web agents. AutoGLM
(
20
)
designs an appropriate GUI interface framework for tool invocation, thereby realizing a high-performing mobile agent in Mobile environments. MobileRL
(
39
)
utilizes reinforcement learning to optimize models, bringing mobile GUI agents to state-of-the-art levels. AgentCPM-GUI
(
53
)
, based on the MiniCPM-V
(
42
)
model, achieves a mobile GUI agent through a combination of techniques, including training for perception-imitation-reasoning and specialized designs for mobile devices. For embodied agents, VLMs can be directly employed for task planning
(
34
)
. Furthermore, certain embodied models such as PaLM-E
(
7
)
, EmbodiedGPT
(
22
)
, RoboBrain
(
11
)
have achieved superior performance through fine-tuning on embodied data atop VLMs. However, a gap remains between planning and execution; thus, Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have been proposed to integrate action decoders with VLMs, enabling the direct generation of action outputs from visual and linguistic inputs
(
56
)
. Although these agents can complete tasks to a certain extent, they present significant safety risks during task execution.

2.2
Safety Evaluation for Agents

To facilitate the safe deployment of agents in real-world applications, a growing body of research has been dedicated to evaluating the safety of agents. SafeBench
(
45
)
assesses the content safety of LMMs in question-answering but does not involve behavioral safety. R-judge
(
46
)
evaluates the risk awareness of LLMs in open-agent scenarios, yet it remains at the stage of behavioral judgment and does not involve behavioral output. ST-WebAgentBench
(
15
)
provides a behavioral safety evaluation of various web agent strategies based on the BrowserGym
(
5
)
web task experimental environment. MobileSafetyBench
(
14
)
evaluates the interaction safety of agents in near-real mobile environments and their various applications based on an Android simulator. OpenAgentSafety
(
32
)
more comprehensively evaluates the safety of various GUI operations of agents in real environments, including web pages, file systems, command lines, and code operations. In addition to safety evaluation in GUI environments, safety evaluation in embodied environments has also been studied. SafeAgentBench
(
44
)
evaluates the safety of action planning for agents in household scenarios. IS-Bench
(
21
)
evaluates action planning safety in dynamic embodied interactions, but focuses on a limited set of scenarios and interaction tasks. SafeLIBERO
(
10
)
and VLA-Arena
(
48
)
evaluate the safety of low-level behavioral operations of embodied agents, but SafeLIBERO only considers a single safety risk, and the safety-related tasks in VLA-Arena are very limited. Despite the existence of various agent benchmarks, no work has been able to effectively unify these scenarios. Although Agent-SafetyBench
(
50
)
and ToolEmu
(
27
)
both consider GUI and embodied scenarios simultaneously, they lack effective behavioral simulation and rely too heavily on LLM-generated or pre-defined tool invocation results. These limitations of existing evaluations motivate our work.

3
Problem Formulation

We model our environment as
\mathcal{E}=\langle S,A,O,I,T\rangle
. The agent decision-making problem is formulated as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP). Here,
S
denotes the environment state space,
A
denotes the action space,
O
denotes the observation space, and
I
represents the instruction (intent) space. The state transition function
T:S\times A\rightarrow S
is deterministically instantiated by the underlying environment dynamics, which may vary across different environments.

Given a task described by a natural-language instruction
i\in I
, the agent selects an action
a_{t}\in A
conditioned on the instruction
i
, the current observation
o_{t}\in O
, the action history
a_{1:t-1}
, and the observation history
o_{1:t-1}
. Executing action
a_{t}
induces a new environment state
s_{t+1}\in S
and yields a corresponding observation
o_{t+1}\in O
. For the four representative categories of agent task scenarios considered in this work, we employ high-fidelity simulators together with real-world tools. The state space (S) corresponds to the instantaneous environment state provided by the simulator, such as the currently loaded web page or a physically grounded embodied scene under simulation. We next introduce the formal modeling of the remaining components. The construction methodology of the instruction space
I
is detailed in Section
4.3
.

3.1
Observation Space

To simulate the observation process of agents in real-world settings, we define the agent observation spaces by drawing on prior work across different environments. Across different environments, agents can acquire environment representations consistent with the real world through interaction. Additional details are provided in Appendix
A
.

3.2
Action Space

Building on previous work on agents across different domains
(
19
;
49
;
31
)
, we design environment-specific executable action spaces and available tools for agents operating in different settings. An overview is provided in Appendix
B
.

For GUI-based agents, the action space of web agents is primarily composed of composite keyboard–mouse interactions, whereas mobile agents are associated with touch-based input modalities, including tapping, swiping, text input, and shortcut operations.

For embodied planning agents, the action space is defined as a predefined set of high-level skills, such as picking and placing objects, opening and closing containers, and other abstract manipulation primitives. For embodied manipulation agents based on VLA models, the action space corresponds to a 7 Degree of Freedom (7-DoF) robotic arm control space, which governs the end-effector’s translation, rotation, and gripper actuation.

4
Benchmark Construction

4.1
Environments

For each category of agent task environments, we employ widely adopted simulators to facilitate high-fidelity interactions. In accordance with the problem formulation established in Section
B
, we strictly define the interaction protocols between the environment and the agents. To ensure rigorous experimental control, different tasks and environments are isolated from one another. Specifically, each simulator is encapsulated within a containerized environment, where a fresh system snapshot is initialized for every assessment. This mechanism guarantees that evaluations remain independent and free from cross-task interference.

For the Web scenario, we utilize WebArena
(
54
)
, an environment for language-guided agents that is highly realistic and reproducible. It focuses on agents performing tasks on the web across fully functional websites from four common domains: e-commerce, social forum discussions, collaborative software development, and content management. For the Mobile scenario, we adopt AndroidLab
(
40
)
, which provides pre-defined Android Virtual Devices (AVDs) and a suite of tasks spanning nine integrated applications, offering an agent-friendly interface for both action execution and performance evaluation.

For embodied planning scenarios, we leverage IS-Bench
(
21
)
framework, which provides standardized agent operation interfaces and formalized risk condition definitions, and integrate it with the OmniGibson
(
16
)
simulation environment to enable both task execution and safety evaluation. OmniGibson is a large-scale embodied simulator that supports diverse environments beyond household scenarios, and its rich set of embodied tasks facilitates our extension of both task design and safety assessment. For manipulation scenarios, we adopt VLA-Arena
(
48
)
, which extends the LIBERO
(
18
)
simulation environment by incorporating the parsing of cost conditions to support safety risk evaluation. LIBERO offers a lifelong robot learning environment that allows flexible customization of scenes and tasks, thereby enabling scalable extensions of our evaluation across different risk settings.

4.2
Risk Categories

Following the taxonomy of content safety and behavioral safety established in existing work
(
51
;
55
;
47
)
, we develop our benchmark around 9 distinct categories of safety risks. The classification and definitions of these risk categories are detailed in Appendix
C
. Recognizing the significant divergence in risk dimensions between GUI-based agents and embodied agents, we employ a decoupled categorization framework tailored to each. Our primary focus is directed toward unintentional safety risks within the agent’s decision-making process. Specifically, we assume a setting where both user intentions and task instructions are devoid of malicious intent or adversarial content, and the interaction lifecycle remains free from malicious third-party adversaries. This refined taxonomy provides broad coverage of real-world deployment scenarios, ensuring the robustness and comprehensiveness of our evaluation framework.

4.3
Tasks and Instructions

Since our benchmark relies on high-fidelity simulators of real-world scenarios, it is imperative that tasks involving safety risks remain both plausible and executable. We begin with the original, feasible tasks provided by the environmental simulators. By leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) for safety-oriented instruction augmentation, we transform these baseline tasks into target instructions that may induce specific safety risks during execution. Specifically, for GUI-based agents, we prompt the LLM to select the most relevant risk category based on the environment and initial instruction. The LLM then performs task rewriting to generate executable safety-risk tasks tailored to the simulator. Furthermore, we mandate the LLM to synthesize corresponding risk-triggering mechanisms, which serve as the ground truth for evaluating whether the agent’s behavior ultimately leads to a safety breach. For embodied agents, a more direct construction approach is adopted by incorporating environmental risk factors. We require the LLM to inject these risk factors into the original tasks, resulting in safety-sensitive instructions. Further implementation details are provided in Appendix
F
. This entire pipeline is powered by GPT-5. This methodology enables a dual-faceted assessment of both task completion and behavioral safety, ultimately forming our Safety Risk Instruction Space.

4.4
Hybrid Evaluation Framework

Metrics
Distinguishing our work from existing literature, we simultaneously consider Success Rate (SR) and Safety Rate (SafetyR). SR is defined as the percentage of tasks where the agent’s final action or response fulfills the original instruction intent. SafetyR represents the proportion of execution trajectories that do not trigger any predefined safety risks. To provide a more granular perspective, we further report the Joint Distribution of SR and SafetyR in our evaluation results.

Rule-based Evaluation
We employ an extensive suite of rule-based evaluators to assess both the agent’s trajectories and its final outputs. Regarding task success, for GUI-based agents, we primarily utilize exact matching or containment logic—for instance, requiring the agent’s response or the final environment state to contain the ground-truth answer or specific entities. For embodied agents, we mainly rely on object state and spatial conditions, such as requiring an object to be opened or placed at a specified target location. For safety assessment, evaluations are conducted based on the specific risk-triggering mechanisms associated with each risk-sensitive instruction.

LLM-based Evaluation
Given the vast action space and environmental states, LLMs are indispensable for performing semantic fuzzy matching. Leveraging GPT-5 as an evaluator, we analyze the environment states and agent trajectories to determine task completion and safety compliance. Prior research has validated the feasibility of using LLMs as safety scorers, with GPT-4o demonstrating evaluative performance comparable to that of human experts.

Our hybrid evaluation methodology ensures comprehensive coverage across diverse task formats and scenarios. Notably, we focus exclusively on manifested safety risks—those resulting from executed agent actions that lead to observable environmental changes. Cases where an agent exhibits risky intent without triggering an actual environmental impact are excluded from this assessment. This dual-track scheme strikes a balance between the precision of deterministic rule-checking and the broad behavioral insights provided by LLM judgment, achieving robust safety evaluation across heterogeneous tasks.

5.1
Experimental Setup

We evaluate BSB across four environments: web-based, mobile GUI, embodied VLM, and embodied VLA. BSB-Web is built on WebArena with containerized websites, where we follow the original action space and task definitions while redesigning safety evaluation protocols. BSB-Mobile assesses agent safety in realistic Android emulator environments using UI-hierarchy-based state representations and atomic mobile actions, covering both closed-source LLM-based agents and an open-source mobile agent. BSB-EmbodiedVLM evaluates the safety of VLMs as decision-making cores for embodied agents through multi-step skill planning executed in simulation, while BSB-EmbodiedVLA focuses on operational safety in physical manipulation by executing agent outputs as control commands in a robotic simulation environment. Across all benchmarks, agents are evaluated under standardized settings to ensure fair and comparable safety analysis. More detailed configurations, task designs, and model specifications are provided in Appendix
D
.

Main Results

BSB-Web

Due to the increased complexity introduced by task instruction rewriting, AWM exhibits a reduced task completion rate compared to the original tasks. Notably, safety risks may still be triggered during successful task execution, and such risks are often difficult to detect, indicating that early termination or interception can be preferable for risk-prone operations, even at the cost of task failure. Overall, the results show that current web agents are highly vulnerable to safety risks during execution, underscoring the need for improved risk mitigation mechanisms.

BSB-Mobile

Compared to baseline tasks in the original simulated environment, agents achieve substantially lower task success rates. Even the best-performing GPT-4-1106-Preview attains only 25.58% SR, while the 9B AutoGLM reaches merely 2.63% SR. These results suggest persistent difficulties in understanding user intent and generating appropriate behaviors. As a result, risk-triggering actions in high-risk tasks are rarely activated, leading to artificially inflated safety coverage (42.11%–79.17%). Additional analysis is provided in Appendix
E
.

BSB-EmbodiedVLM

The evaluated VLMs show significant variance in both task success and safety, with higher SR not necessarily implying better safety. For example, GPT-5 achieves the highest SR but maintains a comparatively low safety rate, with 40.99% of executions being successful yet unsafe. In contrast, RoboBrain2.0, a model specifically designed for embodied interaction, attains a higher safety rate and a more balanced outcome distribution, indicating stronger safety awareness. General open-source VLMs such as Qwen3-VL and InternVL3.5 exhibit similar safety rates despite differences in task success, suggesting that safety performance among these models is largely comparable. Overall, these results reveal a systematic misalignment between planning performance and safety robustness in current VLM-driven embodied planning systems.

BSB-EmbodiedVLA

The proportion of executions that simultaneously achieve task completion and satisfy safety constraints remains relatively low, while a substantial fraction of executions involve unsafe behaviors. Notably, safety violations frequently occur even during successful task executions, rather than merely arising as a byproduct of task failure. Moreover, although different models exhibit clear differences in task success rates, these variations do not translate into commensurate improvements in safety rates. In addition, a considerable proportion of failed executions still trigger safety violations, suggesting that in the absence of effective safety awareness or risk-sensitive termination mechanisms, agents may continue executing along unsafe trajectories. Overall, these findings indicate that current embodied manipulation agents remain predominantly task-driven and have yet to systematically integrate safety constraints into their execution and control processes.

When risk conditions are introduced, task completion rates exhibit a decreasing trend in the majority of risk settings across models, indicating that safety constraints are associated with increased difficulty in task execution, potentially by perturbing the agents’ original action trajectories. Notably, the magnitude of degradation in task completion is generally smaller than that observed in safety compliance. This suggests that agents often continue executing their learned task trajectories without effectively adapting their behavior to respond to or avoid unsafe situations. Under composite risk settings, where multiple safety constraints are imposed simultaneously, all evaluated models exhibit relatively low safety rates, highlighting the challenges posed by compounded risks in embodied manipulation. Overall, these results demonstrate that task completion alone is insufficient to characterize embodied agent performance under safety-critical conditions, and that joint evaluation of completion and safety metrics is necessary for a comprehensive assessment.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce BeSafe-Bench (BSB), a benchmarking framework designed to evaluate the behavioral safety of agents across typical scenarios, including Web, Android, Embodied VLM, and Embodied VLA. By constructing safety-critical tasks within functional environments, we simultaneously assessed the task completion rates and safety coverage of 13 popular agents in complex, sequential task settings. Our findings reveal that even the top-performing agent successfully completed only 35.19% of tasks without violating safety protocols, highlighting a substantial gap between task competence and safety robustness in current agentic systems. Beyond quantifying existing limitations, BeSafe-Bench provides a unified and extensible evaluation paradigm that enables systematic analysis of safety risks across heterogeneous environments and agent architectures. These results emphasize the urgent necessity of establishing more comprehensive protection mechanisms and advanced training methodologies before deploying agentic systems in sensitive real-world applications. We hope that BeSafe-Bench will serve as a foundational resource for the community, facilitating the development of safer and more resilient agents and advancing the reliable deployment of agentic systems in real-world settings.

Appendix D
Experimental Environment

BSB-Web
is constructed on top of WebArena to simulate web-based environments, encompassing domains such as e-commerce platforms, management systems, and online forums. Each website is deployed within an isolated Docker container, with the corresponding container loaded when executing its associated tasks. We adopt the agent action space and task types as defined by WebArena, while redefining the safety evaluation methodology for each task category. For agent selection, we evaluate the state-of-the-art AgentWorkflowMemory (AWM)
(
35
)
architecture implemented on WebArena, using GPT-5 as the underlying language model. During the deployment of website mirrors using WebArena, we identified technical inconsistencies within the provided GitLab and Map platforms. Specifically, the Map service lacked a local Docker implementation and instead relied on Amazon EC2 instances for instantiation. Furthermore, the GitLab platform frequently encountered ”500 Internal Server Error” responses during external access requests, characterized by the message: ”Whoops, something went wrong on our end.” These stability issues introduced significant biases into the evaluation of tasks associated with these two platforms. Consequently, to ensure the integrity of our experimental results, we excluded the data from GitLab and Map in our final analysis, focusing exclusively on results from stable environments such as Shopping, Shopping admin (Online Store CMS), and Reddit..

BSB-Mobile
is designed to evaluate agent safety in realistic mobile GUI environments. All tasks are executed in an Android emulator and span several high-risk application domains. In Bench-Mobile, we adopt a UI-hierarchy-based state representation, where the current screen is parsed into a structured UI tree. Agent actions are restricted to atomic mobile interactions such as clicking, typing, and scrolling, in order to closely approximate real user behavior. We evaluate two representative categories of mobile agents. The first category consists of closed-source agents powered by general-purpose large language models, including GPT-5 and GPT-4-1106-preview
(
1
)
. The second category is an open-source agent specifically designed for mobile scenarios, AutoGLM-9B
(
20
)
. All agents are evaluated on the same task set and under identical execution environments to ensure comparability in safety analysis.

BSB-EmbodiedVLM
is designed to evaluate the safety of VLMs when they serve as the decision-making core of embodied agents. Given a task description and a predefined set of executable skills, the VLM is required to select, sequence, and invoke appropriate skills to accomplish the task. Each task involves multi-step skill planning, and the resulting skill sequence is executed in the OmniGibson simulation environment. Task completion and safety are determined by analyzing the environmental states and execution outcomes observed during simulation. Specifically, we assess whether the task is successfully completed and whether any unsafe states or behaviors are triggered during execution. We evaluate five representative models: GPT-5, Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Instruct, Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Thinking
(
2
)
, InternVL3.5-30B-A3B
(
33
)
, and RoboBrain2.0-32B
(
29
)
. These models span a diverse set of characteristics, including closed-source versus open-source, general-purpose versus embodiment-oriented, and reasoning-enabled versus non-reasoning VLMs, enabling a comprehensive comparative analysis.

BSB-EmbodiedVLA
focuses on evaluating the operational safety of embodied agents during physical manipulation. In this benchmark, the agent’s outputs are directly executed as control commands for a robotic arm in the LIBERO simulation environment, enabling end-to-end interaction with the environment. Task success is determined based on predefined evaluation rules, while risk triggers are explicitly embedded within tasks to assess safety during execution. These triggers are designed to capture unsafe behaviors or states that may arise even when a task is nominally completed. Since VLA models extend VLMs by enabling direct action execution, they are particularly suitable for embodied manipulation scenarios. Accordingly, we select VLA models as the evaluation targets in this benchmark. Specifically, we evaluate the OpenVLA
(
13
)
model, its more efficient successor OpenVLA-OFT
(
12
)

\pi_{0.5}

(

It is worth noting that VLA models typically require downstream task-specific fine-tuning for effective deployment. Accordingly, the OpenVLA and OpenVLA-OFT models evaluated in this benchmark are fine-tuned on the LIBERO-90 task suite, ensuring a reasonable level of task completion performance. In contrast,

Appendix E
Main Results in Android Environments

Within the Android simulation environment implemented via Android-Lab, the process of agent-environment interaction primarily relies on predefined text-based functions. Consequently, the agent’s instruction-following capability is a decisive factor in the successful execution of tasks. We conducted a manual audit and in-depth analysis of the anomalous performance where GPT-4-1106-Preview outperformed GPT-5. Our findings indicate that during multi-round interactions, GPT-5 struggles to strictly adhere to the required output formats and provide correct parameters in the later stages of interaction. This deficiency leads to repetitive or meaningless clicking operations on the Android system, which may impair system usability and subsequently be categorized as unsafe behavior during evaluation. In contrast, AutoGLM exhibited even poorer performance in such scenarios. Conversely, GPT-4-1106-Preview demonstrates consistent instruction-following capabilities throughout long-sequence interactions, strictly generating the required formatted functional actions. This consistency results in superior performance and a higher safety rate.
