Using narrative practices in nursing to enhance compassionate care for older adults: protocol for a qualitative study
Authors: Veselina Hristova Slavova, Anna Petrova Georgieva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionCompassionate care is a core ethical and professional value in nursing, especially in the context of elderly care, where vulnerability, chronic illness and relational dependency are at the forefront. Despite the growing international interest in compassion and narrative medicine, there is limited empirical research examining how nurses experience, construct and sustain compassionate care for older people in everyday practice, especially in Eastern European contexts. Narrative practices offer a valuable methodological lens for capturing the experiences, moral meanings and emotional dimensions of nursing care. The aim of this study is to explore how nurses understand, experience and implement compassionate care for older people and to explore the potential of narrative practices to illuminate facilitators, barriers and professional meaning-making in gerontological nursing.Methods and analysisA qualitative research design based on narrative inquiry and interpretive phenomenological analysis will be used. Semistructured narrative interviews will be conducted with registered nurses working with older people in institutional care settings. The interview guide is informed by international literature on compassionate care, narrative medicine, gerontological nursing and relational ethics. Data will be analysed using reflexive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework supported by NVivo software. Narrative and interpretive elements will be integrated to explore both the content of participants’ stories and the meanings they attribute to their experiences.Ethics and disseminationThe study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Varna (Approval No. 17, 3 July 2025). All participants will provide informed consent and confidentiality will be assured throughout the study. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, academic conferences and professional forums, with the aim of informing nursing education, practice and policy related to compassionate care for older people.Keywords: Aged, Nursing Care, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH, Compassion Fatigue, Nursing research

Introduction

Population ageing and the growing proportion of older adults requiring long-term care have emerged as one of the key public health challenges in the Republic of Bulgaria.1 2 The high vulnerability of this population group, their specific health and social care needs, and the limited capacity of families to provide adequate care in the home setting often lead to the need for institutional care placement.3 4

In Bulgaria, older adults requiring long-term or end-of-life care may be cared for either in residential care homes for older people or in hospices, which differ in their aims and models of care. Residential care homes primarily provide social support and assistance with daily living, with limited on-site medical care. In contrast, hospices deliver specialised palliative care for people with terminal or advanced incurable conditions, with a primary focus on symptom relief, alleviation of suffering and quality of life.

This distinction is relevant for nursing practice, as the intensity of care, required professional competencies and emotional demands placed on nurses differ substantially between these two care settings.

Concerns regarding the quality of institutional care for older adults in Bulgaria have increased in recent years, drawing attention to systemic challenges related to staffing, working conditions and ethical standards of care. Previous national studies highlight difficulties in residents’ adaptation to institutional environments, experiences of loneliness,5 6 communication barriers7 and ethical challenges in care provision.8 At the same time, nurses working in these settings report high emotional and physical workload, low professional recognition, and increased risk of professional distress and burnout.9 10

Despite these challenges, little is known about how nurses themselves narrate and make sense of compassionate care in such contexts, particularly in relation to moral sensitivity and emotional labour.

At the international level, research on residential care for older adults highlights that staff psychological well-being and job satisfaction are key determinants of the quality of care provided. Studies have shown that high emotional demands and limited workplace support represent significant risk factors for staff, underscoring the need for research focused on caregivers’ lived experiences.11 Furthermore, research across various clinical settings has identified links between compassion and nurses’ professional well-being. Studies among nurses in emergency departments indicate that prolonged exposure to intense suffering, high-pressure situations and morally challenging scenarios is associated with an increased risk of compassion fatigue. However, these studies also demonstrate that targeted educational and reflective interventions can effectively support both prevention and recovery.12 Similar findings have been reported among oncology nurses, where resilience-building programmes have been shown to reduce symptoms of compassion fatigue and enhance professional well-being.13 These findings support the understanding that compassion in nursing practice is not an inexhaustible personal resource, but rather a process that requires institutional support and the development of specific skills, particularly in contexts with high emotional and ethical demands, such as care for older adults.

The concept of ‘care’ lies at the heart of the medical and nursing professions and is traditionally regarded as a core value. In practice, however, care is often reduced to adherence to professional standards and protocols, which may limit a patient-centred approach. In this sense, care devoid of compassion risks becoming a purely formal execution of duties.14

Within the context of nursing practice, compassion can be understood as a cognitive-emotional process in which the nurse recognises the patient’s suffering, interprets it as morally significant and is motivated to respond through caring and ethical action. This conceptualisation aligns with Martha Nussbaum’s philosophical view of compassion as a fundamental social emotion,15 as well as with the empirical model of compassion in healthcare proposed by Sinclair et al, which frames compassion as a complex process involving the recognition of suffering, emotional and moral engagement, and deliberate action to alleviate it.16 In this sense, compassion goes beyond a spontaneous emotional reaction, constituting a morally considered choice and action, which, according to Von Dietze and Orb, positions compassionate care as a core dimension of the nurse’s professional identity.17 The understanding of compassionate care is also grounded in a conceptual distinction between empathy and compassion. According to some authors, while empathy involves the cognitive-emotional recognition and understanding of another person’s experiences, compassion adds an active moral and professional response aimed at alleviating suffering.16 18

In the Bulgarian academic literature, empathy is well-documented as a competence that develops through clinical practice and targeted education.19 20 However, there is a lack of dedicated qualitative research examining compassion as a distinct concept and as an active behaviour in everyday nursing practice.21 This gap highlights the need for empirical investigation, particularly within long-term care settings, which justifies the relevance of the present study.

The narrative approach provides a valuable methodological framework for exploring nurses’ lived experiences. Researchers interpret storytelling in nursing practice as a means of making sense of experiences by organising them into coherent narratives that follow a logical structure. This process contributes to the development and reinforcement of professional capacity, encompassing emotional regulation, moral competence and practical skills.22–24 Clark emphasises that narrative functions as a tool for understanding the complexity of care, moral dilemmas and relational aspects of clinical practice, particularly in geriatric contexts.25

Although narrative approaches in healthcare are often associated with patient stories, their potential extends to nurses’ professional narratives. Storytelling about experiences of providing compassionate care enables reflection on the emotional and moral labour inherent in the profession and facilitates a deeper understanding of compassion as both an experienced and practised value.26 27

Despite the existence of international and national research, there is a lack of targeted qualitative, narrative studies exploring how nurses in Bulgaria experience and make sense of compassionate care within long-term and hospice care settings.

This study employs a qualitative narrative design to explore how nurses in Varna, Bulgaria, experience and enact compassionate care in the context of long-term and hospice services. By analysing their narratives, the study aims to illuminate the interaction between individual experience, professional identity and structural factors that either facilitate or hinder compassionate care. In doing so, it addresses a significant gap in the Eastern European literature and offers insights with potential applicability for practice, education and policy in broader international contexts.

Study aim

The main aim of this qualitative narrative study is to explore how nurses working in long-term and hospice care settings for older adults in Varna, Bulgaria, experience, make sense of and enact compassionate care in their daily practice. The study is exploratory in nature, seeking to generate in-depth understanding rather than to test predefined hypotheses.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This study employs a combined qualitative approach, integrating narrative methodology with interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA). The narrative approach is used to elicit rich personal accounts from participants, allowing the construction of individual stories that capture their lived experiences of providing compassionate care. IPA is applied to systematically examine the meanings participants attribute to these experiences, offering an interpretative perspective on their perceptions, emotions and ethical reasoning in caring for older adults. This approach is informed by Husserl’s concept of bracketing and the investigation of experiences from the perspective of the transcendental subject.28

The narrative approach in nursing practice draws on Paul Ricoeur’s philosophical framework, in which individual identity is shaped and understood through the narrative of lived experiences.29 Storytelling enables nurses to integrate personal and professional experience, articulate moral judgements and make sense of the dynamics of care in clinical practice. Combining narrative and phenomenological approaches allows for an in-depth exploration of nurses’ personal, professional and moral experiences.

Participants

Participants will include nurses working in long-term and hospice care for older adults in Varna. They will be selected using purposive sampling, with attention to diversity in professional experience, role and type of services provided. This approach ensures the inclusion of participants capable of offering diverse perspectives and rich insights into the experiences and enactment of compassionate care across different institutional contexts. The expected sample size is approximately 25 participants, deemed sufficient to achieve data saturation.

Reporting

The findings of this qualitative study will be reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist.30 The interview guide was developed following established principles of qualitative interviewing. It is provided as online supplemental file 1 to facilitate transparent reporting in line with COREQ.

Data collection

Data will be collected through semistructured interviews based on a narrative inquiry approach. The interviews will focus on nurses’ experiences in providing compassionate care, the challenges and barriers they encounter, and the strategies they employ for self-care and professional development.

The interview guide was developed based on a targeted review of international literature on compassionate care, narrative practices, gerontological nursing and ethical perspectives.11 14 16–18 It has been refined to ensure alignment with qualitative research standards and to elicit rich, detailed narratives that reflect participants’ lived experiences.

Prior to data collection, the interview guide was informally tested with two registered nurses who also serve as academic faculty at a medical university. This process focused on assessing the clarity, relevance and flow of the questions, as well as their suitability for eliciting narrative accounts from clinical practice. Feedback from this pilot led to minor refinements in wording and the sequence of questions. Data from the pilot testing were not included in the study analysis.

In developing the interview questions, careful attention was paid to the conceptual distinction between empathy and compassion. Empathy refers to the ability to recognise, understand and share the feelings of another, which may or may not result in action. Compassion, on the other hand, entails an active orientation toward alleviating the suffering of others, combining empathic understanding with intentional supportive action.16 18 This distinction guided the formulation of open-ended questions, encouraging participants to describe situations in which they not only perceived patients’ emotional states but also acted to provide relief or support.

The interview guide was designed to maintain depth and consistency across interviews, facilitating thematic and narrative saturation while allowing participants to highlight aspects of their experiences they consider most meaningful. Open-ended questions were supplemented with optional prompts to encourage storytelling without leading the participants.

Interviews will be conducted in Bulgarian, audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. The research team will review the transcripts to ensure accuracy. The semistructured format allows participants to elaborate on their personal experiences while covering key areas related to the study’s research questions.

Data analysis

The data will be analysed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework for thematic analysis31: familiarisation with the transcripts through repeated reading, generation of initial codes, identification and review of themes, defining and naming themes, and the production of a final analytic report with illustrative quotes.

Transcripts will be coded and analysed using NVivo V.15, allowing systematic identification of recurring patterns and extraction of key themes. The combined approach of narrative analysis and IPA enables examination of both the content of participants’ stories and the depth of nurses’ experiences, including their interpretations of compassionate care.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this study. The study participants are registered nurses working in a nursing home and a hospice who will be recruited solely as research participants. The findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications. A summary of the study findings will also be made available to the participating nursing home and hospice and to the participants on request.

Ethics and dissemination

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Varna (Approval No. 17, 3 July 2025). All participants will receive written and verbal information about the study aims, procedures and their rights as participants. Written informed consent will be obtained prior to participation. Participation will be voluntary, and participants will be informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences. Confidentiality and anonymity will be ensured through the use of pseudonyms and the removal of any potentially identifying information from transcripts and reports. Audio recordings and transcripts will be securely stored and accessible only to the research team.

The findings of this study will be disseminated through publication in peer-reviewed open-access journals and presentation at national and international conferences in the fields of nursing, gerontology, palliative care and qualitative health research. In addition, summaries of the findings will be shared with participating institutions and professional nursing communities to support reflection on compassionate care practices in clinical settings.
