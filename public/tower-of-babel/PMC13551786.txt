Emotional intelligence and its relationship with moral distress in nurses: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Leila Ghanbari-Afra, Ali Alizadeh, Abed Asgari, Somayeh Namdari, Seyed Kazem Mousavi, Mohamad Amin Hoshangishayan, Zahra Akbari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionOne of the important occupational challenges of nurses is moral distress, which can negatively affect their mental health and the quality of clinical care. On the other hand, On the other hand, higher emotional intelligence is associated with better coping and professional performance. Therefore, this study was conducted to investigate the relationship between emotional intelligence and moral distress in nurses.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted from July to October 2025, and 210 nurses working in 4 hospitals in Qom, Iran were selected using stratified random sampling. Data collection tools included a demographic questionnaire, the Moral Distress Scale–Revised (MDS-R), and Schutte Self-Report Emotional Intelligence Test (SSEIT), which were completed by self-report. The collected data were analyzed using descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean and standard deviation) and inferential statistics (independent t-test, ANOVA, Pearson correlation and multiple linear regression). A significance level of 5% was considered.ResultsThe mean total scores for emotional intelligence and moral distress among participants were 93.11 ± 20.33 and 188.54 ± 34.19, respectively. Also, there was a significant relationship between the total score and the dimensions of emotional intelligence and moral distress among nurses, and adjusted R² showed that 15.6% of the variance in moral distress was explained by emotional intelligence components.ConclusionThis study found a relationship between emotional intelligence and moral distress among nurses. Therefore, in addition to providing a supportive environment to reduce moral distress, educational programs aimed at improving emotional intelligence may be beneficial to strengthen nurses’ emotional intelligence.Trial registrationNot applicable.

Introduction

Between 1900 and 1920, the concept of cognitive intelligence (IQ) was introduced as a tool to distinguish intelligent people from others, but its limitations soon became apparent, as it became clear that some people, despite having high cognitive intelligence, were not successful in managing behaviour and social interactions [1]. Following these limitations, the concept of emotional intelligence (EI) was introduced. Emotional intelligence includes a set of non-cognitive abilities and skills that help individuals cope with stressful situations and psychological pressures. It also promotes adaptive and prosocial behaviors [2]. In fact, emotional intelligence, as the ability to understand, regulate, and manage one’s own and others’ emotions, can play an important role in how one faces stressful and ethical situations [3]. Therefore, people with higher emotional intelligence are generally better able to regulate negative emotions arising from ethical conflicts. They may also make more balanced decisions and adapt more effectively to challenging workplace conditions [2]. Emotional intelligence has been considered in many fields today, including education, psychology, health, and treatment, especially in nursing [4]. The nursing profession requires strong emotional and social competencies [5]. Nurses are continuously exposed to complex, stressful, and unpredictable clinical situations that demand effective interpersonal communication and emotional regulation [4, 5]. In nursing practice, emotional intelligence has been associated with better decision-making, problem-solving abilities, effective communication, and lower psychological stress [4–6]. In addition, emotional intelligence can affect the mental health of nurses and patients and significantly impact the quality of nursing services [7]. It should be noted that the nursing profession, as one of the main pillars of the health system, is inherently human and ethical and is based on complex emotional and interpersonal interactions [8]. Nurses in clinical settings frequently encounter difficult ethical situations. They must simultaneously manage their own emotions while responding to the needs of patients and their families. In many of these situations, organizational constraints, limited resources, workload pressures, and conflicts between professional values and workplace expectations may prevent nurses from acting according to what they believe is ethically appropriate. This situation leads to a phenomenon called moral distress [9]. Moral distress refers to a situation in which an individual knows what the morally correct action is, but is unable to do it due to personal, organisational, or structural barriers [10]. This phenomenon is highly prevalent in the nursing profession due to the nature of care, close patient contact, and continuous exposure to complex ethical decisions [11]. Factors such as resource shortages, work pressure, conflicts with the treatment team, organisational policies, and management constraints can contribute to the development of moral distress among nurses [10–12]. Moral distress may be accompanied by feelings of guilt, anger, helplessness, and job dissatisfaction. It can also contribute to burnout, reduced quality of care, lower professional commitment, and intention to leave the profession [10, 12, 13]. Therefore, given the vital role of nurses in providing safe, high-quality care, identifying the factors that contribute to moral distress and strategies to reduce it is of great importance [14].

In summary, given the stressful conditions in clinical environments, nurses are constantly exposed to difficult moral decisions and psychological pressures arising from the conflict between professional values and organisational constraints, which can exacerbate moral distress [15]. Therefore, identifying individual factors that influence how nurses respond to these situations is particularly important. Focusing solely on organizational factors may not be sufficient to address this challenge [10]. Emotional intelligence is considered a developable capability. It may contribute to greater resilience, improved ethical decision-making, and reduced negative effects of occupational stress among nurses [16]. Therefore, examining the relationship between emotional intelligence and moral distress can lead to the development of targeted educational programs, strengthening managerial support, and improving the quality of nursing care.

Methods

Study design and sampling

This was a cross-sectional study conducted from July to October 2025 with the aim of investigating the relationship between emotional intelligence and moral distress among nurses. The study population included nurses working in public hospitals affiliated with Qom University of Medical Sciences in Iran, and the research setting was Shohada, Kamkar, Shahid Beheshti, and Fatemeh Masoumeh hospitals. The sample size was calculated using MedCalc version 19.6.1 based on an expected medium effect size (r = 0.25), alpha level of 0.05, and statistical power of 80%, resulting in a minimum sample size of 194 participants. Considering possible attrition, 210 nurses were recruited.

Sampling was conducted using stratified random sampling, with hospitals in the study area divided into four strata. Then, a number of nurses were selected from each class, in proportion to the total number of nurses at that hospital, using simple random sampling. Inclusion criteria were: willingness to participate in the study, having a bachelor’s degree in nursing or higher, having at least six months of experience working in a hospital and not suffering from physical or psychiatric diseases and taking medication in this regard (self-report). Also, failure to complete 10% of the questionnaire questions was considered an exclusion criterion.

Research instruments

Data collection instruments included demographic questionnaires, Moral Distress Scale–Revised (MDS-R), and Schutte Self-Report Emotional Intelligence Test (SSEIT). The demographic questionnaire questions included age, gender, marital status, parenting, economic status, work experience, job category, type of employment, ward of service, and level of education.

The SSEIT is a 33-item tool with five subscales, including self-arousal (7 questions), self-awareness (8 questions), self-control (7 questions), empathy /social awareness (6 questions), and social/communicational skills (5 questions). The items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale from “Never = 1” to “Always = 5”. The range of overall scores is 33–165. People with scores between 132 and 165 are considered to have high emotional intelligence; between 99 and 131, good; between 66 and 98, average; and between 33 and 65, low. Therefore, a range of scores from 132 to 165 is considered high emotional intelligence, 99–131 is considered good, between 66 and 98 is average, and between 33 and 65 is considered low emotional intelligence [17]. The Persian version of the SSEIT has previously demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties among Iranian populations. Mansouri reported satisfactory validity and a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.84 for the Persian version of the instrument [18]. In the present study, content validity was reviewed and confirmed by 10 faculty members with expertise in nursing and behavioral sciences. Internal consistency reliability was also assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which was 0.84.

Also, the Moral Distress Scale-Revised (MDS-R) developed by Allen et al., and contains 21 items. Each item is scored based on the individual’s perception of the situation and calculated according to the frequency and intensity of the moral distress experienced. It is computed in a two-part procedure. Each part is scored based on a 0- to 4-point Likert scale, from 0 (never) to 4 (very frequently) for the frequency of moral distress, and from 0 (none) to 4 (great extent) for the intensity of moral distress. Each item is scored by multiplying its frequency of moral distress by its intensity, yielding a score ranging from 0 to 16. The total score ranges from 0 to 336. Higher scores indicate higher moral distress [19]. The Persian version of the MDS-R has previously been validated among Iranian nurses, with acceptable validity and reliability reported by Merghati Khoiee et al. [20]. Also, in this study, the qualitative content validity of this scale was found to be favorable, and its reliability was calculated to be 0.81 using Cronbach’s alpha.

Because the data were collected using self-report questionnaires, the findings may have been affected by social desirability bias and subjective response tendencies.

Method of implementation

After obtaining the necessary permissions for participation in the study, informed consent was obtained from all participants. The questionnaires, and the researchers’ contact information were then provided to the participants in person. The questionnaires were completed by the participants, while the researchers remained present to answer any questions that arose during the process. Sampling continued until the required sample size was achieved.

Data analysis

After data collection, cases with more than 10% missing data were deleted using listwise deletion. Also, using SPSS version 26, the data were examined for normality and their normality was confirmed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (p-value > 0.05). Therefore, parametric tests were used to analyze the data. Therefore, descriptive indicators of mean, standard deviation, frequency, and frequency percentage were used to describe the demographic characteristics and values of the main research variables. Also, independent t-tests, ANOVA, Pearson correlation, and multiple regression were used to examine the relationships between the main research variables and the demographic characteristics of the participants. The significance level was also set at 5%.

Results

Of the 210 nurses in the study, 16 were excluded because they did not complete the questionnaires. Thus, the information obtained from 194 participants was analysed. According to the findings, the participants’ average age was 36.04 ± 9.24, and their average work experience was 32.11 ± 8.36. Also, 71.2% of them were female, 55.9% were married, and 41.9% had children. In addition, the average total scores for emotional intelligence and moral distress among nurses were 93.11 ± 20.33 and 188.54 ± 34.19, respectively (Table 1).

Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between emotional intelligence and moral distress among nurses. In this section, the study’s main variables were first discussed, followed by an examination of their relationship. According to the findings, the level of emotional intelligence of nurses was at a moderate level, similar to findings from studies conducted in Iran, such as Botshekanan Dehkordi et al. [21], Omidi et al. [2] and Mahmoudian [22]. Also, similar findings were reported in studies by Turjuman et al. in Saudi Arabia [23], Wu et al. in China [24], and Soriano-Vázquez in Peru [25]. Of course, some studies also had different results. For example, in the studies by Cheraghi et al. [5], Al-Oweidat et al. [26] and Jawabreh et al. [27], the level of emotional intelligence among nurses was found to be high, whereas in the studies by Abualruz et al. [28] and Ghadrnezhad et al. [29], it was found to be lower than moderate. In this regard, it should be said that this difference in findings is usually predictable. In fact, differences in the findings of studies on emotional intelligence among nurses are mainly due to variations in theoretical models, measurement tools, sample characteristics, and cultural backgrounds [21, 22, 25]. Studies examine different dimensions of emotional intelligence using different theories or models, which affects the results [21]. Also, differences in measurement tools (questionnaires or performance tests), the type and location of nurses’ work, levels of job stress, statistical analysis methods, and even organisational and national culture can lead some studies to report high levels of emotional intelligence, while others yield different or contradictory results [5, 25]. Also, in this study, there was a relationship among nurses’ age, work experience, and level of education, consistent with findings from similar studies [25, 27, 28]. It should be noted that all three factors lead to increased experience and individual and social growth [7]. With increasing age and work experience, nurses have more opportunities to face difficult situations, manage stress, understand patients’ emotions, and control their emotional reactions. These real-world experiences strengthen the core components of emotional intelligence [2]. On the other hand, higher levels of education improve nurses’ cognitive and communication abilities, help them better understand human behaviour, and increase emotional self-efficacy [21]. As a result, these three variables together indicate professional maturity, emotional cognition, and psychological maturity that naturally accompany increased emotional intelligence [2, 21, 24].

Also, the observed level of moral distress suggests that nurses continue to experience considerable ethical pressure in clinical settings. This finding is consistent with a number of studies conducted in Iran [10, 30] and other countries [31, 32]. However, some other studies have also reported contradictory findings. For example, a systematic review in Iran reported a high level of moral distress among nurses [9], whereas two other studies found it to be low [11, 33]. In other countries, study findings are often contradictory. In general, studies show that social, economic, and organisational factors can contribute to nurses’ moral distress [9, 10]. For example, one study found that nurses’ moral distress increased during the COVID-19 pandemic compared to before the pandemic [34]. In another study, the level of moral distress among nurses was higher in developing countries, aligning with our findings, as nurses with lower economic status reported greater moral distress [15]. Also, organisational factors that contribute to moral distress, such as inadequate management support, unclear policies for resolving ethical conflicts, and an unsupportive organisational culture, can fail to provide the necessary context for acting on ethical principles, thereby increasing nurses’ moral distress [9, 10]. In addition, individual nurse characteristics play an important role in the severity of nurses’ moral distress. In fact, some of these characteristics act as a defensive shield, helping nurses better absorb the shock of ethical trade-offs [31, 34]. In this study, there was also a relationship between some characteristics of the participants, such as age, gender, parenting, economic status, and work experience, and the level of moral distress, which confirms this claim.

The study’s most important finding was the inverse association between emotional intelligence and moral distress; that is, higher emotional intelligence was associated with lower moral distress. This could indicate that nurses with higher emotional intelligence are better able to face and manage complex issues and difficult decisions in the environment, and as a result, experience less stress and moral distress. Some studies align with this study’s findings. For example, in the study by Qiaoling et al., a negative correlation was observed between the total score of emotional intelligence and its dimensions, and moral distress in nurses [35]. In the study by Ali Awad et al., an inverse relationship was also observed between emotional intelligence, moral distress, and work engagement [36]. In addition, Pirizadeh Avansar found a significant relationship between emotional intelligence and moral intelligence among nurses [37]. Also, in the study by Cheraghi et al., emotional intelligence was associated with job performance and job stress [5]. Mousavi et al. confirmed the relationship between emotional intelligence and nurses’ performance [38], and Ye et al. confirmed a positive correlation between this variable and moral sensitivity [39]. Also, Nyamori stated that emotional intelligence significantly affects nurses’ moral decision-making [40]. Wang et al. also reported a positive correlation between emotional intelligence and moral disgust in another study [41]. Contrary to these results, in another study by Cheraghi et al., emotional intelligence had a direct and negative effect on nurses’ moral intelligence; however, this correlation was weak [42]. In explaining the findings of this section, it should be noted that, because emotional intelligence is related to variables such as moral disgust, moral decision-making, and job performance, this construct plays a fundamental role in how nurses deal with moral situations. Nurses with higher emotional intelligence manage emotions arising from disgust or moral conflict more adaptively [41] and act more rationally and balancedly in the moral decision-making process [40]. This not only improves job performance but can also reduce the severity of moral distress [38]. Therefore, emotional intelligence can be considered a protective factor against the negative threats of moral tension in healthcare settings [42].

According to the latest research findings, about One sixth of nurses’ moral distress was explained by emotional intelligence. In line with this, in the study of Qiaoling et al., the variables of age, working hours, title and dimensions of emotional intelligence explained 42% of nurses’ moral distress in the oncology department [35]. In the study by Abualruz et al., about 20% of nurses’ psychological distress was explained by emotional intelligence [28]. In general, it can be concluded that emotional intelligence can play an important role in reducing nurses’ moral distress by empowering them to recognise and manage emotions, helping them manage moral conflicts, and reducing the psychological stress caused by organisational constraints [35, 36].

Strengths and limitations

This research has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causal relationships between emotional intelligence and moral distress. Second, data collection was based on self-report questionnaires, which may be associated with recall bias and social desirability bias. Third, the study was conducted in four hospitals affiliated with Qom University of Medical Sciences, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings and regions. Despite these limitations, the use of stratified random sampling and validated instruments strengthened the methodological rigor of the research.

Conclusion

The results of this study confirmed the relationship between emotional intelligence and moral distress in nurses. Therefore, it is recommended that policymakers and nursing administrators incorporate relevant workshops—such as emotional intelligence management and strategies for preventing moral distress—into continuing nursing education programs. Also, future research can examine the roles of mediating and moderating variables, such as organisational support, resilience, and burnout, in the relationships examined in this study. In addition, by examining variables such as organisational culture and team interactions qualitatively, a more complete perspective can be developed on how to manage moral distress in clinical settings.
