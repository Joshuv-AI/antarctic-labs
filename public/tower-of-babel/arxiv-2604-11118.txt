Distributionally Robust K-Means Clustering
Vikrant Malik, Taylan Kargin, and Babak Hassibi

Abstract
K-means clustering is a workhorse of unsupervised learning, but it is notoriously brittle to outliers, distribution shifts, and limited sample sizes. Viewing k-means as Lloyd–Max quantization of the empirical distribution, we develop a distributionally robust variant that protects against such pathologies. We posit that the unknown population distribution lies within a Wasserstein-2 ball around the empirical distribution. In this setting, one seeks cluster centers that minimize the worst-case expected squared distance over this ambiguity set, leading to a minimax formulation. A tractable dual yields a soft-clustering scheme that replaces hard assignments with smoothly weighted ones. We propose an efficient block coordinate descent algorithm with provable monotonic decrease and local linear convergence. Experiments on standard benchmarks and large-scale synthetic data demonstrate substantial gains in outlier detection and robustness to noise.

Introduction

In recent years, the widespread availability of large-scale, high-dimensional datasets has driven significant interest in clustering algorithms that are both computationally efficient and robust to distributional shifts and outliers. The classical clustering method,
K
-means, can be seen as an application of the Lloyd-Max quantization algorithm, in which the distribution being quantized is the empirical distribution of the points to be clustered. This empirical distribution generally differs from the
true
underlying distribution, especially when the number of points to be clustered is small. This induces a distributional shift, which can also arise in many real-world settings, such as image segmentation, biological data analysis, and sensor networks, due to noise variations, sensor inaccuracies, or environmental changes. Distributional shifts can severely impact the performance of clustering algorithms, leading to degraded cluster assignments and unreliable downstream analysis.

-means++ (KM++)
[
2
]

Noise-robust clustering has also been explored using alternative frameworks, such as the fuzzy PCA-guided approach proposed by

I-A

Contributions and Organization

We propose a natural variant of the classic KM algorithm that is robust to distributional shifts. Specifically, the algorithm minimizes the worst-case expected squared error over the
\operatorname{\mathsf{W}_{2}}
family of distributions (formally stated in
Section

II-B
). We derive the necessary conditions for the optimal placement of
K
cluster centers that best represent the
\operatorname{\mathsf{W}_{2}}
family of distributions for a given nominal distribution
\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\circ}
and ambiguity radius
r
. We then propose an efficient block coordinate descent algorithm (
Algorithm

1
) with provable monotonic decrease and local linear convergence that alternates between solving a quadratic program for the soft assignments and applying a closed-form centroid update. Finally, we present numerical simulations to study both performance and runtime. The organization of the paper is as follows. In Section
II
, we formally define the optimization problem on which the clustering method is based. Section
III
gives the necessary conditions for optimality. Section
IV
formalizes the iterative clustering algorithm and provides approximation and local convergence guarantees. In Section
V
, we present numerical simulations on synthetic and real-world datasets and show that, in the data-starved and corrupted regimes, our proposed algorithm outperforms standard and robust baselines
[
2
,
6
,
17
]
in terms of outlier detection and classification accuracy.

Our approach is especially suited for low-data regimes in which the empirical distribution does not represent the true distribution well, making it difficult for standard algorithms to learn the true distribution or detect outliers. It is also suited for settings in which the empirical data is corrupted with outliers. This is discussed in detail in Section
IV-D
. The proofs of our theorems and lemmas are included in the Appendix.

II
Problem Setup

Let
X_{\sharp}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{}}}{{\sim}}\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\sharp}
be a random vector that encapsulates the true data population of interest, drawn from an
unknown
probability distribution
\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\sharp}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Our objective is to partition this population into
K\in\mathbb{N}
distinct clusters. Formally, we seek a partition of the domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into
K

i.e.
,
\Phi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{K}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that

\Phi_{k}\cap\Phi_{l}=\emptyset\text{ for }k\neq l,\qquad\bigcup\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}\Phi_{k}=\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Additionally, each cluster is associated with a representative center,
i.e.
,
\mu_{k}\in\Phi_{k}
for
k\in[K]
. For any such pair of cluster centers and partitions
(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi})
, where
\mathbf{M}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{1}&\dots&\mu_{K}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times K}
and
\bm{\Phi}\coloneqq\{\Phi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, we define an associated clustering function (also called a quantizer)
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
that maps a given data point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to the representative center of the cluster containing
x
, namely,

\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi}}(x)\coloneqq\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}\mu_{k}\operatorname{\mathds{1}}_{\Phi_{k}}(x).

(1)

Standard K-Means Clustering as Empirical Risk Minimization

In order to assess the performance of a clustering scheme
(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi})
for an arbitrary random vector
X
following a distribution
\operatorname{\mathds{P}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we consider the expected squared
L_{2}
-distance between the random vector
X
and its corresponding cluster center
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi}}(X)
, namely:

Definition II.1

(Clustering Risk)
.

The
L_{2}
-risk of a clustering scheme
(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi})
for a distribution
\operatorname{\mathds{P}}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined as

\operatorname{\mathsf{Risk}}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi},\operatorname{\mathds{P}})\coloneqq\operatorname{\mathds{E}}_{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}\left[{\|{X-\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi}}(X)}\|_{\text{}}^{2}}\right].

(2)

Ideally, we seek to find a clustering scheme
(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi})
that minimizes the population risk under the true but unknown population distribution
\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\sharp}
, namely,

\inf_{(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi})}\!\operatorname{\mathsf{Risk}}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi},\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\sharp})\!=\!\inf_{(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi})}\!\operatorname{\mathds{E}}_{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\sharp}}\left[{\|{X_{\sharp}\!-\!\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi}}(X_{\sharp})}\|_{\text{}}^{2}}\right].

(3)

In reality, we almost never have access to the true distribution to begin with. Instead, usually a finite dataset
\mathcal{D}_{N}\coloneqq\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of
N\in\mathbb{N}

\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\sharp}
. In this case, the empirical distribution

\widehat{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}_{N}\coloneqq N^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{n}}

(4)

of the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{N}
can be used as a proxy for the true but unknown population distribution
\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\sharp}
, leading to the
empirical clustering risk
minimization:

Problem II.2

(Standard
K
-Means as Empirical Risk Minimization)
.

Given a dataset
\mathcal{D}_{N}
, find a pair of
K
cluster centers and regions
(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi})
that minimizes the empirical clustering risk,
i.e.
,

\inf_{(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi})}\operatorname{\mathsf{Risk}}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi},\widehat{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}_{N})=\inf_{(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi})}\operatorname{\mathds{E}}_{\widehat{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}_{N}}\left[{\|{X\!-\!\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi}}(X)}\|_{\text{}}^{2}}\right]=\inf_{(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi})}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|{x_{n}\!-\!\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi}}(x_{n})}\|_{\text{}}^{2}.

This is the standard objective used in traditional
K
-means clustering. For a fixed partition
\bm{\Phi}
, the objective is convex in the centers
\mathbf{M}
. However, the joint optimization over
\mathbf{M}
and
\bm{\Phi}
is highly non-convex. Nevertheless, the necessary optimality conditions are obtained as follows:

Theorem II.3

(Optimality of
K
-Means
[
19
,
18
]
)
.

For a fixed set of centers
\mathbf{M}
, the optimal partitioning
\bm{\Phi}^{\star}
is the nearest-neighbor partition, i.e.,

\Phi^{\star}_{k}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\|{x-\mu_{k}}\|_{\text{}}\leq\|{x-\mu_{l}}\|_{\text{}}\quad\forall l\neq k\right\},

(5)

for
k\!=\!1,\dots,K
. Furthermore, the optimal centers

for a dataset

satisfy the necessary conditions below:

\mu^{\star}_{k}=\frac{\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}\operatorname{\mathds{1}}_{\Phi^{\star}_{k}}(x_{n})}{\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\operatorname{\mathds{1}}_{\Phi^{\star}_{k}}(x_{n})}=\operatorname{\mathds{E}}_{\widehat{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}_{N}}\left[{X\mid X\in\Phi_{k}^{\star}}\right].

The popular Lloyd-Max algorithm is developed as a fixed-point method for finding locally optimal centers.

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}^{(t)}\!\leftarrow\!\left\{x\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\|{x\!-\!\mu^{(t)}_{k}}\|_{\text{}}\leq\|{x\!-\!\mu^{(t)}_{l}}\|_{\text{}},\;\forall l\neq k\right\},

II-B

Distributionally Robust K-Means Clustering

When the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{N}
is generated by sampling directly from the population
\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\sharp}
, the representativeness of the empirical distribution
\widehat{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}_{N}
as a proxy for the population
\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\sharp}
diminishes severely in high-dimensional, data-scarce settings such that
\log(N)\ll d
, due to the curse of dimensionality. Moreover, standard
K
-means does not provide an a priori performance guarantee for the underlying population when the dataset is subject to distribution shifts, making it highly susceptible to cluster misrepresentation. Examples of distributional shifts include cases in which the empirical distribution is corrupted with noise, certain clusters are under- or over-represented due to sampling bias, or the data is drawn from a skewed or shifted population relative to the true underlying distribution.

To overcome these limitations, we explicitly incorporate our uncertainty about the true population distribution
\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\sharp}
into the design of
K
-means clusters through an ambiguity set of plausible probability distributions consistent with the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{N}
, to which the true distribution
\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\sharp}
belongs. As motivated in the introduction, we will describe this notion of plausibility using the Wasserstein-2 distance.

Definition II.4

(Wasserstein-2 Ambiguity Sets)
.

The
\operatorname{\mathsf{W}_{2}}
-ambiguity set of radius
r\in(0,\infty)
around a nominal distribution
\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\circ}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined as

\mathbb{B}_{r}(\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\circ})\coloneqq\left\{{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\mid\operatorname{\mathsf{W}_{2}}(\operatorname{\mathds{P}},\,\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\circ})\leq r}\right\}.

(7)

In this paper, we take the empirical distribution
\widehat{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}_{N}
as the nominal distribution. Notably, the Wasserstein-2 ambiguity set offers the significant advantage of encompassing continuous, mixed, and discrete distributions. Accordingly, we impose the following assumption for the remainder of our paper:

Assumption II.5
.

Given
\mathcal{D}_{N}
and
r\!>\!0
, the true distribution
\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\sharp}
of the underlying population belongs to the ambiguity set
\mathbb{B}_{r}(\widehat{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}_{N})
.

The radius
r\!>\!0
essentially determines the level of uncertainty about and/or deviation from the unknown population distribution
\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\sharp}
. When the dataset samples are drawn
\mathrm{i.i.d.}
from the population
\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\sharp}
, it is indeed possible to determine an upper bound on
r
that guarantees that
Section

II-B
holds with high probability.

Section

II-B
allows establishing performance bounds on the population risk of any clustering scheme through the worst-case risk attained among all plausible distributions in the ambiguity set, defined as follows:

Definition II.6

(Worst-Case Risk)
.

Given a dataset
{\mathcal{D}}_{N}
and radius
r\!>\!0
, the worst-case risk of a quantization scheme
(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi})
is defined as

\operatorname{\mathsf{WC-Risk}}_{N,r}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi})\coloneqq\sup_{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}\in\mathbb{B}_{r}(\widehat{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}_{N})}\operatorname{\mathsf{Risk}}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi},\operatorname{\mathds{P}})=\sup_{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}\in\mathbb{B}_{r}(\widehat{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}_{N})}\operatorname{\mathds{E}}_{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}\left[{\|{X-\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi}}(X)}\|_{\text{}}^{2}}\right].

(8)

Although the true distribution is not known, the worst-case risk provides an upper bound on the population risk of a clustering scheme
(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi})
,

\inf_{\mathbf{M}^{\prime},\bm{\Phi}^{\prime}}\operatorname{\mathsf{Risk}}(\mathbf{M}^{\prime},\bm{\Phi}^{\prime},\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\sharp})\leq\operatorname{\mathsf{Risk}}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi},\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\sharp})\leq\operatorname{\mathsf{WC-Risk}}_{N,r}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi}).

(9)

Thus, minimizing the worst-case clustering risk among all plausible distributions in the ambiguity set instead of directly minimizing the empirical risk provides a proxy with guaranteed performance in the face of uncertainties and distribution shifts between empirical training data and the true population. This is formalized in the following problem.

Problem II.7

(Distributionally Robust
K
-Means)
.

Given a dataset
\mathcal{D}_{N}
and an ambiguity set radius
r>0
, find a clustering scheme
(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi})
that minimizes the worst-case clustering risk,
i.e.
,

\inf_{(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi})}\sup_{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}\in\mathbb{B}_{r}(\widehat{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}_{N})}\operatorname{\mathsf{Risk}}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi},\operatorname{\mathds{P}})=\inf_{(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi})}\sup_{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}\in\mathbb{B}_{r}(\widehat{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}_{N})}\operatorname{\mathds{E}}_{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}\left[{\|{X-\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{M},\bm{\Phi}}(X)}\|_{\text{}}^{2}}\right].

(10)

The dependence of the radius of the ambiguity set
r
on the number of points and the problem dimension is highlighted through the following theorem.

Theorem II.8

(High-Confidence Ambiguity Sets
[
26
]
)
.

Let
d\!>\!4
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}_{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\sharp}}\left[{\exp(\|{X}\|_{\text{}}^{\alpha})}\right]<+\infty
for an

. When
{\mathcal{D}}_{N}
is drawn

, the true distribution

resides in the ambiguity set

(12)

Here,

The radius

Optimality Conditions for DR-Clusters

Lemma III.1

(Optimality of Nearest-Neighbor Partition)
.

For any fixed centroids
\mathbf{M}=\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{1}\,\dots\,\mu_{K}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times K}

\displaystyle\triangleq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\min_{k\in[K]}\|{x-\mu_{k}}\|_{\text{}}^{2}-\gamma\|{x-x_{n}}\|_{\text{}}^{2}.

(19)

\displaystyle\bm{\pi}_{n}^{\star}

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{\bm{\pi}\in\Delta_{K}}\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}\|{x_{n}^{\star}-\mu_{k}}\|_{\text{}}^{2}\pi_{k}.

(20)

Proof: Appendix
C
.

Note that we have introduced a latent variable, the optimal probability mass function (pmf)
\bm{\pi}_{n}^{\star}\!\in\!\Delta_{K}
, which is a function of the data point
x_{n}
. This pmf can be interpreted as a stochastic cluster assignment of
x_{n}
to the given set of centroids
\mathbf{M}
, namely,
\pi_{kn}^{\star}
represents the conditional probability of assigning the data point
x_{n}
to the
k^{\textrm{th}}
cluster.
Observe that the term
\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}\|{x_{n}-\mu_{k}}\|_{\text{}}^{2}\pi_{kn}
in (
18
) represents the expected squared distance between the data point
x_{n}
and the centroids
\mathbf{M}
under the stochastic assignment rule
\bm{\pi}_{n}
. In contrast,
\mathbf{M}\bm{\pi}_{n}
denotes the weighted average of the cluster centers, so that
\|{x_{n}-\mathbf{M}\bm{\pi}_{n}}\|_{\text{}}^{2}
quantifies the bias associated with the data point
x_{n}
under the stochastic cluster assignment scheme
(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi})
, where
\bm{\Pi}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\pi}_{1}&\dots&\bm{\pi}_{N}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times N}.
Thus, the second term in the optimization problem (
18
) acts as a regularizer. This regularization, governed by
\gamma>1
, mediates the trade-off between the expected squared distance of
x_{n}
to the cluster centroids and the bias introduced by the stochastic cluster assignment.
Furthermore,
x_{n}^{\star}
represents the worst-case data point associated with
x_{n}
and the given centroids
\mathbf{M}
, so that the worst-case distribution becomes
\operatorname{\mathds{P}}^{\star}=N^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{n}^{\star}}
. These worst-case data points are simply shifted away from the original data
x_{n}
in the direction of the averaged cluster center
\mathbf{M}\bm{\pi}_{n}
, introducing a bias scaled by the inverse of
\gamma-1
. As the radius of the ambiguity set diminishes,
i.e.
,
r\to 0
, we have
\gamma\to\infty
. In this limiting case, both the regularization and perturbation terms vanish, thereby recovering the standard
K
-means over the dataset
{\mathcal{D}}_{N}
. We conclude this section with
Theorem

III.4
, which formally states the main result of this section,
i.e.
, the local optimality conditions for the distributionally robust centroids.

Theorem III.4

(Necessary Conditions for Local Optimality)
.

Given a fixed dataset
{\mathcal{D}}_{N}
and a radius
r>0
, any locally optimal centroids
\mathbf{M}^{\star}
for the Wasserstein-2 distributionally robust
K
-means clustering
Section

II-B
must satisfy

\mu^{\star}_{k}=\frac{\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}^{\star}\pi^{\star}_{kn}}{\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\pi^{\star}_{kn}},\quad k=1,\dots,K.

(21)

Here we assume that every cluster is non-empty, i.e.,
\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\pi^{\star}_{kn}>0
for all
k
; otherwise the corresponding centroid
\mu_{k}^{\star}
is undefined and can be removed.
Here,
\mathbf{X}^{\star}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}^{\star}&\dots&x_{N}^{\star}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
and
\Pi^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times N}
are as in (
19
), and if the optimal dual variable is an interior point
\gamma^{\star}>1
(i.e., the optimizer of
\gamma
is not at the boundary
\gamma=1

\gamma^{\star}
satisfies

\gamma^{\star}=1+r^{-1}\sqrt{{N^{-1}}\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\left\|{x_{n}-\mathbf{M}^{\star}\bm{\pi}^{\star}_{n}}\right\|_{\text{}}^{2}}.

(22)

Remark.
Throughout this paper we assume
\gamma>1

IV
Efficient Algorithm for DR-K-Means

In this section, we present a practical algorithm inspired by the theoretical insights developed in
Section

III
to identify distributionally robust centroids. The core of our approach lies in formulating an iterative method that computes locally optimal centroidal regions through a fixed-point iteration à la
K
-means. These fixed points are characterized by the KKT conditions in
Theorem

III.4
. We conclude this section with
Theorem

IV.3
on the convergence of this algorithm and
Section

IV-D
, which discusses outlier detection.

IV-A

Algorithm Description

Given a dataset
{\mathcal{D}}_{N}
and an initial set of centroids
\mathbf{M}^{(0)}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d\times K}
, we aim to find a set of centroids that are locally optimal for the fixed-
\gamma
surrogate
F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M})\coloneqq\min_{\bm{\Pi}\in\Delta_{K}^{\times N}}J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi})
, in an iterative manner. We
first need to compute the stochastic assignment probabilities
\bm{\Pi}^{(t)}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{K\times N}
corresponding to the current centroids
\mathbf{M}^{(t)}
by solving (
18
), which is a convex quadratic program. The centroids are updated, with
\mathbf{M}^{(t+1)}
being the minimizer of the objective
J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi})
for fixed
\bm{\Pi}^{(t)}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\argmin_{\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times K}}J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi}^{(t)}),

(23)

\displaystyle J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi})\!\triangleq\!\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|{x_{n}\!-\!\mu_{k}}\|_{\text{}}^{2}\pi_{kn}\!+\!\frac{1}{\gamma-1}\|{x_{n}\!-\!\mathbf{M}\bm{\pi}_{n}}\|_{\text{}}^{2}\right)

This is an unconstrained convex quadratic objective in
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times K}
and admits a closed-form solution. Note that as
\gamma\rightarrow\infty
, the second term in (
23
) vanishes and we
recover the classical Lloyd-Max algorithm
. In this limit,
the stochastic decoder transitions to the deterministic nearest-neighbor decoder, thereby eliminating ambiguity about the source distribution.
For clarity, we present the algorithm for a fixed parameter
\gamma>1
.

IV-D

Outlier Detection

Our algorithm is particularly useful when the empirical distribution is corrupted with outliers (data points from a distribution other than the true distribution). Suppose we observe
N=n_{1}+n_{2}
points, where

\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{0}
are inliers and

\{Y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\star}
are outliers (independent of the inliers).
Let the corrupted empirical distribution be

\widehat{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}_{N}\!\coloneqq\!\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\delta_{X_{i}}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\delta_{Y_{j}},\qquad c\!\coloneqq\!\frac{n_{2}}{N}.

Theorem IV.6
.

Assume
\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{0}
and
\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\star}
satisfy
\operatorname{\mathsf{W}_{2}}(\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{0},\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\star})=D>0
, and that both satisfy the moment condition
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[\exp(\|X\|^{\alpha})]<\infty
for some
\alpha>2
. With probability at least
1-\varepsilon

\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{0}
belongs to the Wasserstein ambiguity ball centered at the corrupted empirical measure:

(\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{0}^{\otimes n_{1}}\!\otimes\!\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\star}^{\otimes n_{2}})\!\left\{{\operatorname{\mathsf{W}_{2}}(\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{0},\widehat{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}_{N})\leq r_{\mathrm{cont}}}\right\}\;\geq\;1\!-\!\varepsilon,

where

r_{\mathrm{cont}}(N,\varepsilon,c)\coloneqq r(n_{1},\varepsilon/2)+\sqrt{c}\bigl(D+r(n_{1},\varepsilon/2)+r(n_{2},\varepsilon/2)\bigr),

and
r(n,\varepsilon)
is the high-confidence radius from Theorem 2 of

[

]
(with

replaced by

r(n,\varepsilon)\!\coloneqq\!\begin{cases}\left({\frac{\log(C_{0}/\varepsilon)}{c_{0}\,n}}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}},&\textrm{if}\;n\geq\frac{\log(C_{0}/\varepsilon)}{c_{0}},\\[2.84526pt]
\left({\frac{\log(C_{0}/\varepsilon)}{c_{0}\,n}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}},&\textrm{if}\;n<\frac{\log(C_{0}/\varepsilon)}{c_{0}},\end{cases}

for constants
C_{0},c_{0}>0
depending on
\alpha

Remark.
The population-level assumption
\operatorname{\mathsf{W}_{2}}(\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{0},\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\star})=D

\operatorname{\mathsf{W}_{2}}(\hat{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}_{n_{1}},\hat{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}_{n_{2}})\leq\operatorname{\mathsf{W}_{2}}(\hat{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}_{n_{1}},\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{0})+D+\operatorname{\mathsf{W}_{2}}(\operatorname{\mathds{P}}_{\star},\hat{\operatorname{\mathds{P}}}_{n_{2}})

In this setting,
outlier recall
is a popular metric for clustering algorithms
[
6
,
17
]

Once a clustering algorithm has identified cluster centers, we measure the distances of each data point to their respective cluster center. VI
Conclusion

We introduced a distributionally robust variant of the
K
-means algorithm that explicitly accounts for distribution shifts by minimizing the worst-case clustering risk over a
\operatorname{\mathsf{W}_{2}}
ambiguity set centered at the empirical data distribution. This ensures robustness of the cluster placements to distributional uncertainties. We established that the proposed algorithm operates as a descent method, provided approximation and local linear convergence guarantees, and derived the necessary conditions for the optimal
K
cluster centroids. Numerical simulations confirm the efficacy of our approach, demonstrating its performance in data-starved and corrupted regimes where traditional robust algorithms may falter.

By

Here
\Pi=[\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{N}]\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times N}
, with each
\pi_{n}\in\Delta_{K}
.

Let
M^{\star}
be a local minimizer of the robust objective, and let

(\Pi^{\star},\gamma^{\star})
be an inner minimizer associated with
M^{\star}
. Then

(M^{\star},\Pi^{\star},\gamma^{\star})

over

\mathbb{R}^{d\times K}\times\Delta_{K}^{N}\times(1,\infty).

For each

by the saddle-point formula from

III

x_{n}^{\star}=x_{n}+\frac{x_{n}-M^{\star}\pi_{n}^{\star}}{\gamma^{\star}-1}.

We first derive the centroid condition. Fix

. Since

\nabla_{\mu_{k}}\|x_{n}-\mu_{k}\|^{2}=2(\mu_{k}-x_{n}),

and, for fixed
\pi_{n}^{\star}

\nabla_{\mu_{k}}\|x_{n}-M\pi_{n}^{\star}\|^{2}=2\pi_{kn}^{\star}(M\pi_{n}^{\star}-x_{n}),

we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu_{k}}J_{\gamma^{\star}}(M^{\star},\Pi^{\star})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\pi_{kn}^{\star}\left[(\mu_{k}^{\star}-x_{n})+\frac{1}{\gamma^{\star}-1}(M^{\star}\pi_{n}^{\star}-x_{n})\right].

By the definition of

,

x_{n}^{\star}=x_{n}+\frac{x_{n}-M^{\star}\pi_{n}^{\star}}{\gamma^{\star}-1},

hence

(\mu_{k}^{\star}-x_{n})+\frac{1}{\gamma^{\star}-1}(M^{\star}\pi_{n}^{\star}-x_{n})=\mu_{k}^{\star}-x_{n}^{\star}.

\nabla_{\mu_{k}}J_{\gamma^{\star}}(M^{\star},\Pi^{\star})=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\pi_{kn}^{\star}(\mu_{k}^{\star}-x_{n}^{\star}).

Since

For every

\bm{\Pi}^{\star}

J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{\star},\cdot)

is the multiplier for
\sum_{k}\pi_{kn}=1

\lambda_{kn}^{\star}\geq 0

\pi_{kn}\geq 0

\bm{\Pi}^{\star}

Define the tangent space
\mathcal{T}^{\star}

H_{\Pi\mathbf{M}}&R\end{bmatrix}_{(\mathbf{M}^{\star},\bm{\Pi}^{\star})},

Step 2: The SOSC is equivalent to
\nabla^{2}F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})\succ 0

We establish the central equivalence.

Proposition F.1

(Equivalent characterization of the SOSC)

Suppose LICQ and strict complementarity hold at
(\mathbf{M}^{\star},\bm{\Pi}^{\star})

P^{\star}\succ 0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\nabla^{2}F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})\succ 0.

By the Schur complement criterion (
[
31
]

H_{\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}}-H_{\mathbf{M}\Pi}\,R^{-1}\,H_{\Pi\mathbf{M}}\succ 0.

(51)

It remains to identify the Schur complement with the envelope Hessian
\nabla^{2}F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})

G(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi})\coloneqq\nabla_{\bm{\Pi}}J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi})\big|_{\mathcal{T}^{\star}}=0.

At the optimum
(\mathbf{M}^{\star},\bm{\Pi}^{\star})
, we have
G(\mathbf{M}^{\star},\bm{\Pi}^{\star})=0
by first-order optimality. The IFT requires that the Jacobian of
G
with respect to
\bm{\Pi}
is invertible:

\frac{\partial G}{\partial\bm{\Pi}}=\nabla^{2}_{\Pi\Pi}J_{\gamma}\big|_{\mathcal{T}^{\star}}=R.

Since
R\succ 0
(by affine independence),
R
is invertible, and the IFT guarantees that
\bm{\Pi}^{\star}(\mathbf{M})
is an implicit function of
\mathbf{M}
in a neighborhood of
\mathbf{M}^{\star}
. Moreover, since
J_{\gamma}
is polynomial (hence
C^{\infty}
), the IFT gives that
\bm{\Pi}^{\star}(\mathbf{M})
is
C^{\infty}
.

The envelope is therefore
F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M})=J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi}^{\star}(\mathbf{M}))
. Differentiating twice and substituting
G(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi}^{\star}(\mathbf{M}))=0
yields the classical sensitivity formula
[
32
, Theorem 4.24]
:

\nabla^{2}F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})=H_{\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}}-H_{\mathbf{M}\Pi}\,R^{-1}\,H_{\Pi\mathbf{M}}.

(52)

Comparing (
52
) with (
51
), the second condition is precisely
\nabla^{2}F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})\succ 0
. Since
R\succ 0
by assumption, the equivalence follows.
∎

Step 3: Direct contraction of the one-step map

Let
m\coloneqq\operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{dK}
and
m^{\star}\coloneqq\operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})
; throughout, we identify
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times K}
with its vectorization
m
. Let
q\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{N}(r_{n}-1)
and define a reduced coordinate
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
via

\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Pi}(z))\coloneqq\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Pi}^{\star})+Vz,

where
V=\operatorname{diag}(U_{1},\dots,U_{N})
. For
z
near
0
,
\bm{\Pi}(z)
stays on the same simplex faces as
\bm{\Pi}^{\star}
. Define the reduced objective

\widehat{J}_{\gamma}(m,z)\coloneqq J_{\gamma}\bigl(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi}(z)\bigr),

viewed as a function of
(m,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{dK}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}
, and let

H_{mm}\coloneqq\nabla^{2}_{mm}\widehat{J}_{\gamma}(m^{\star},0),\quad H_{mz}\coloneqq\nabla^{2}_{mz}\widehat{J}_{\gamma}(m^{\star},0),\quad H_{zz}\coloneqq\nabla^{2}_{zz}\widehat{J}_{\gamma}(m^{\star},0).

Note that
H_{mm}=H_{\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}}\succ 0
and
H_{zz}=R\succ 0
.

By the IFT (Step 2), there is a unique
C^{\infty}
map
m\mapsto z_{\gamma}(m)
near
m^{\star}
with
z_{\gamma}(m^{\star})=0
, solving
\nabla_{z}\widehat{J}_{\gamma}(m,z_{\gamma}(m))=0
. Differentiating both sides with respect to
m
at
m=m^{\star}
via the chain rule gives

\frac{dz_{\gamma}}{dm}\bigg|_{m=m^{\star}}=-H_{zz}^{-1}H_{zm}.

(53)

Similarly, the centroid-update map
\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}(z)\coloneqq\argmin_{m}\widehat{J}_{\gamma}(m,z)
satisfies

\frac{d\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}}{dz}\bigg|_{z=0}=-H_{mm}^{-1}H_{mz}.

(54)

The one-step alternating-minimization map is
T_{\gamma}(m)\coloneqq\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}(z_{\gamma}(m))
, a composition of the two maps above. By the chain rule,

\frac{dT_{\gamma}}{dm}\bigg|_{m=m^{\star}}=\frac{d\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}}{dz}\bigg|_{z=0}\cdot\frac{dz_{\gamma}}{dm}\bigg|_{m=m^{\star}}=(-H_{mm}^{-1}H_{mz})(-H_{zz}^{-1}H_{zm}),

which gives

\frac{dT_{\gamma}}{dm}\bigg|_{m=m^{\star}}=H_{mm}^{-1}H_{mz}H_{zz}^{-1}H_{zm}=:H_{mm}^{-1}S.

(55)

By the Schur complement identity (cf. (
52
)),
H_{mm}-S=\nabla^{2}F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})\succ 0
, so
0\preceq S\prec H_{mm}
. In the
H_{mm}
-inner product,
\frac{dT_{\gamma}}{dm}\big|_{m^{\star}}=H_{mm}^{-1}S
is self-adjoint with operator norm

q_{0}\coloneqq\bigl\|\tfrac{dT_{\gamma}}{dm}\big|_{m^{\star}}\bigr\|_{H_{mm}}=\lambda_{\max}(H_{mm}^{-1/2}S\,H_{mm}^{-1/2})<1.

Since
T_{\gamma}
is
C^{1}
, the map
m\mapsto\|\frac{dT_{\gamma}}{dm}\|_{H_{mm}}
is continuous and equals
q_{0}<1
at
m=m^{\star}
. Therefore, for any
\rho_{\star}\in(q_{0},1)
, we can shrink the neighborhood
\mathcal{B}
of
m^{\star}
so that
\|\frac{dT_{\gamma}}{dm}\|_{H_{mm}}\leq\rho_{\star}
for all
m\in\mathcal{B}
. Since
T_{\gamma}(m^{\star})=m^{\star}
, the mean-value theorem gives the one-step bound

\|m^{(t+1)}-m^{\star}\|_{H_{mm}}=\|T_{\gamma}(m^{(t)})-T_{\gamma}(m^{\star})\|_{H_{mm}}\leq\rho_{\star}\,\|m^{(t)}-m^{\star}\|_{H_{mm}}.

Iterating this inequality
t
times yields

\|m^{(t)}-m^{\star}\|_{H_{mm}}\leq\rho_{\star}^{t}\|m^{(0)}-m^{\star}\|_{H_{mm}}.

(56)

Step 4: Objective-value rate

Since
F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M})=J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi}^{\star}(\mathbf{M}))
is a composition of
C^{\infty}
maps (Step 2), it is
C^{\infty}
near
\mathbf{M}^{\star}
. With
\nabla F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})=0
and
\nabla^{2}F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})\succ 0
, there exist constants
0<c_{1}\leq c_{2}<\infty
and a (possibly smaller) neighborhood of
\mathbf{M}^{\star}
such that

c_{1}\,\|m-m^{\star}\|_{H_{mm}}^{2}\leq F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M})-F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})\leq c_{2}\,\|m-m^{\star}\|_{H_{mm}}^{2}

(57)

for all
m
in that neighborhood. (The constants
c_{1},c_{2}
arise from the eigenvalues of
H_{mm}^{-1/2}\nabla^{2}F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})\,H_{mm}^{-1/2}
, which is well defined since both
H_{mm}
and
\nabla^{2}F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})
are positive definite.) The sandwich (
57
) is applied only at the two endpoints
m^{(T)}
and
m^{(0)}
—not at every intermediate iterate—so the constant appears only once:

\displaystyle F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{(T)})-F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\,\|m^{(T)}-m^{\star}\|_{H_{mm}}^{2}

(upper bound of (
57
) at step
T
)

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\,\rho_{\star}^{2T}\,\|m^{(0)}-m^{\star}\|_{H_{mm}}^{2}

(iterate contraction (
56
))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}}\,\rho_{\star}^{2T}\,\bigl(F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{(0)})-F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})\bigr).

(lower bound of (
57
) at step
0
)

Setting
\rho_{\star}\coloneqq q_{0}^{2}\in[0,1)
and
C\coloneqq c_{2}/c_{1}\geq 1
yields (
25
). When
\nabla^{2}F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})\not\succ 0
, the KL property still holds for some
\theta\in(1/2,1)
(since
J_{\gamma}
is real analytic), yielding sublinear convergence
O(t^{-(1-\theta)/(2\theta-1)})
.
∎

Remark F.2

(Interpretation of the condition
\nabla^{2}F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})\succ 0
)
.

At a local optimum
\mathbf{M}^{\star}
, the Schur-complement formula (
52
) gives

\nabla^{2}F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})=H_{\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}}-H_{\mathbf{M}\Pi}\,R^{-1}\,H_{\Pi\mathbf{M}}
,
where
H_{\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}}\succ 0
(non-empty clusters) and
R\succ 0
(affine independence).
Every data point contributes positive curvature to
H_{\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}}
, whereas the cross-Hessian

H_{\mathbf{M}\Pi}
is built from the tangent-space isometries
U_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times(r_{n}-1)}
.
When
r_{n}=1
(hard assignment),
U_{n}
is
K\times 0
, so point
n
does not enter

H_{\mathbf{M}\Pi}
at all; only boundary points (
r_{n}\geq 2
) contribute to the correction

H_{\mathbf{M}\Pi}\,R^{-1}\,H_{\Pi\mathbf{M}}
.
Hence
\nabla^{2}F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})\succ 0
holds whenever most assignments are hard.

More precisely, for a fixed active-set pattern, the inner solution map

\mathbf{M}\mapsto\bm{\Pi}^{\star}(\mathbf{M})
is
C^{\infty}
(indeed rational on the region where the relevant Jacobian is invertible).
Hence
\mathbf{M}\mapsto\det\!\bigl(\nabla^{2}F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M})\bigr)
is real-analytic on that region.
If it is not identically zero, its zero set has Lebesgue measure zero.
Therefore
\nabla^{2}F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{\star})\succ 0
is a generic condition within each active-set region.

Appendix G
Proof of
Theorem

IV.3

Proof.

Because
\bm{\Pi}^{(t)}
minimizes
\bm{\Pi}\mapsto J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{(t)},\bm{\Pi})
, we have

F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{(t)})=J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{(t)},\bm{\Pi}^{(t)}).

Because
\mathbf{M}^{(t+1)}
minimizes
\mathbf{M}\mapsto J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi}^{(t)})
,

J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{(t+1)},\bm{\Pi}^{(t)})\leq J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{(t)},\bm{\Pi}^{(t)}).

By the definition of the envelope,

F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{(t+1)})=\min_{\bm{\Pi}}J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{(t+1)},\bm{\Pi})\leq J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{(t+1)},\bm{\Pi}^{(t)}).

Combining the three displayed equations yields
F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{(t+1)})\leq F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{(t)})
. Since
F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M})\geq\gamma r^{2}
for all
\mathbf{M}
, the monotone nonincreasing sequence
\{F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M}^{(t)})\}
converges.
∎

H-A

Proof of
Theorem

IV.4

Proof.

Define
\widetilde{J}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi},\gamma)\coloneqq\gamma r^{2}+N^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{N}\bigl[\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}\|{x_{n}-\mu_{k}}\|_{\text{}}^{2}\pi_{kn}+(\gamma-1)^{-1}\|{x_{n}-\mathbf{M}\bm{\pi}_{n}}\|_{\text{}}^{2}\bigr]
, so that
\operatorname{\mathsf{WC-Risk}}(\mathbf{M})=\min_{\bm{\Pi},\,\gamma>1}\widetilde{J}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi},\gamma)
.

By definition of the exact inner step,

\operatorname{\mathsf{WC-Risk}}(\mathbf{M}^{(t)})=\widetilde{J}(\mathbf{M}^{(t)},\bm{\Pi}^{(t)},\gamma^{(t)}).

By the
\mathbf{M}
-step,

\widetilde{J}(\mathbf{M}^{(t+1)},\bm{\Pi}^{(t)},\gamma^{(t)})\leq\widetilde{J}(\mathbf{M}^{(t)},\bm{\Pi}^{(t)},\gamma^{(t)}).

By the definition of the envelope,

\operatorname{\mathsf{WC-Risk}}(\mathbf{M}^{(t+1)})=\min_{\bm{\Pi},\,\gamma>1}\widetilde{J}(\mathbf{M}^{(t+1)},\bm{\Pi},\gamma)\leq\widetilde{J}(\mathbf{M}^{(t+1)},\bm{\Pi}^{(t)},\gamma^{(t)}).

Combining these three inequalities yields
\operatorname{\mathsf{WC-Risk}}(\mathbf{M}^{(t+1)})\leq\operatorname{\mathsf{WC-Risk}}(\mathbf{M}^{(t)})
. Since
\widetilde{J}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi},\gamma)\geq\gamma r^{2}\geq r^{2}
, the sequence is bounded below and converges.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

Proof.

The proof follows the same direct contraction strategy as the proof of

, with the inner variable enlarged from

(tangent-space assignment coordinates) to

By the same active-set argument used in the proof of

near

Using the same vectorization

(72)

where the final inequality follows from the union bound. This proves the claim with

r_{\mathrm{cont}}(N,\varepsilon,c)\coloneqq r(n_{1},\varepsilon/2)+\sqrt{c}\bigl(D+r(n_{1},\varepsilon/2)+r(n_{2},\varepsilon/2)\bigr)
.

Appendix J
Additional Algorithms

Let
H(\bm{\pi})\triangleq-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}\log\pi_{k}
be the entropy of a pmf
\bm{\pi}\in\Delta_{K}
. For
\lambda>0
, define the entropy-regularized objective

\displaystyle J_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi})\triangleq\gamma r^{2}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left[{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|{x_{n}-\mu_{k}}\|_{\text{}}^{2}\pi_{kn}+\frac{1}{\gamma-1}\|{x_{n}-\mathbf{M}\bm{\pi_{n}}}\|_{\text{}}^{2}-\lambda H(\bm{\pi}_{n})}\right].

(73)

Note that the entropy term
-\lambda H(\bm{\pi}_{n})=\lambda\sum_{k}\pi_{kn}\log\pi_{kn}
makes
J_{\gamma}^{\lambda}
strictly convex in
\bm{\Pi}
(on the simplex), while the
\mathbf{M}
-block remains unchanged from the unregularized case. For better convergence properties, we propose the following alternating minimization variant of
Algorithm

1
.

Algorithm 3

DR-
K
-Means Clustering with Strongly Convex Regularizers

1:

input:

\gamma\!>\!1
, dataset
{\mathcal{D}}_{N}
, initialization
\mathbf{M}^{(0)}
, convergence tolerance
\varepsilon\!>\!0
,

2:

repeat

3:

\begin{aligned} \bm{\Pi}^{(t)}&\leftarrow\argmin_{\bm{\pi}_{1},\dots,\bm{\pi}_{N}\in\Delta_{K}}J_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M}^{(t)},\bm{\Pi}),\end{aligned}

4:

\begin{aligned} \mathbf{M}^{(t+1)}&\leftarrow\argmin_{\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times K}}J_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi}^{(t)})\end{aligned}

5:

Increment
t\leftarrow t+1

6:

until

\begin{aligned} \max_{k}\|{\mu_{k}^{(t+1)}\!-\!\mu_{k}^{(t)}}\|_{\text{}}/\max_{l}\|{\mu_{l}^{(t)}}\|_{\text{}}\leq\varepsilon\end{aligned}

7:

return

\mathbf{M}^{(t)}

Algorithm

3
requires exact block minimization of
J_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi})
. For the
\bm{\Pi}
-step, the entropy regularizer does not admit a simple closed form (due to coupling through
\|x_{n}-\mathbf{M}\bm{\pi}_{n}\|^{2}
), so an iterative inner solver is needed.

Theorem J.1

(Linear convergence with entropy regularization)
.

Fix
\gamma>1
and
\lambda>0
. Let
\mathbf{M}^{\star}_{\lambda}
be a non-degenerate strict local minimizer of
F_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M})\coloneqq\min_{\bm{\Pi}\in\Delta_{K}^{\times N}}J_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi})
, i.e.,
\nabla^{2}F_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M}^{\star}_{\lambda})\succ 0
, with all clusters non-empty. Then there exist
\delta>0
,
\rho_{\lambda}\in[0,1)
, and
C_{\lambda}\geq 1
such that for all
\|{\mathbf{M}^{(0)}-\mathbf{M}^{\star}_{\lambda}}\|_{\text{}}\leq\delta
, the iterates of
Algorithm

3
satisfy

F_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M}^{(T)})-F_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M}^{\star}_{\lambda})\leq C_{\lambda}\,\rho_{\lambda}^{T}\bigl(F_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M}^{(0)})-F_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M}^{\star}_{\lambda})\bigr).

(74)

Furthermore, the approximation bias satisfies
|{F_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M})-F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M})}|\leq\lambda\log K
for all
\mathbf{M}
.

Compared to
Theorem

IV.2
, the entropy regularization provides the following advantages:

1.

No affine independence required
: the entropy forces
\pi^{\star}_{kn}>0
for all
k,n
, making
R\succ 0
regardless of centroid geometry (the entropy contributes
\frac{\lambda}{N}\operatorname{diag}(\pi_{kn}^{-1})
to the
\bm{\Pi}
-Hessian).

2.

No strict complementarity required
: the entropy pushes all assignments to the relative interior of
\Delta_{K}
, so strict complementarity is vacuously satisfied.

3.

SOSC reduces to envelope non-degeneracy
: by
Appendix

F
, the SOSC is equivalent to
\nabla^{2}F_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M}^{\star}_{\lambda})\succ 0
, which holds generically (
Appendix

F
).

Proof.

Step 1: Interior minimizer and constraint qualification

The logarithmic term from
-\lambda H(\bm{\pi}_{n})
forces the minimizer
\bm{\Pi}^{\star}_{\lambda}
to the relative interior:
\pi^{\star}_{kn}>0
for all
k,n
. Consequently, no inequality constraints
\pi_{kn}\geq 0
are active (
A_{n}=[K]
for all
n
), and strict complementarity is vacuously satisfied (there are no binding inequality constraints whose multipliers need checking). LICQ requires the gradients of the active constraints to be linearly independent; the only active constraints are the equalities
\sum_{k}\pi_{kn}=1
, whose gradient is
\mathbf{1}_{K}
—a single nonzero vector, which forms a linearly independent set by itself.

Step 2: SOSC via
Appendix

F

The restricted
\bm{\Pi}
-Hessian on the tangent space
\mathcal{T}=\{\delta\bm{\Pi}:\sum_{k}\delta\pi_{kn}=0\}
is

R_{\lambda}=\frac{2}{N(\gamma-1)}\operatorname{blkdiag}\bigl(U_{n}^{T}\mathbf{M}^{\star T}\mathbf{M}^{\star}U_{n}\bigr)_{n=1}^{N}+\frac{\lambda}{N}\operatorname{blkdiag}\bigl(U_{n}^{T}\operatorname{diag}(\pi_{kn}^{\star\,-1})U_{n}\bigr)_{n=1}^{N}.

The entropy contribution satisfies
U_{n}^{T}\operatorname{diag}(\pi_{kn}^{\star\,-1})U_{n}\succcurlyeq(\max_{k}\pi^{\star}_{kn})^{-1}I_{K-1}\succ 0
since all
\pi^{\star}_{kn}>0
. Hence
R_{\lambda}\succ 0

regardless
of centroid geometry—no affine independence needed.

By
Appendix

F
,
P^{\star}_{\lambda}\succ 0\iff\nabla^{2}F_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M}^{\star}_{\lambda})\succ 0
, which is our hypothesis.

Step 3: Convergence via direct contraction

With
R_{\lambda}\succ 0
(Step 2) and
\nabla^{2}F_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M}^{\star}_{\lambda})\succ 0
(hypothesis), the proof of
Theorem

IV.2
(Steps 3–4) applies verbatim with
J_{\gamma}
replaced by
J_{\gamma}^{\lambda}
. In particular, the entropy term
-\lambda H(\bm{\pi}_{n})
is
C^{\infty}
on the interior of
\Delta_{K}
, and Step 1 ensures the minimizers lie in the interior, so the IFT and all subsequent arguments carry through. This yields the stated iterate and objective-value bounds with
C_{\lambda}=c_{2}/c_{1}
as in the proof of
Theorem

IV.2
.

Step 4: Approximation bias

Since
H(\bm{\pi}_{n})\geq 0
, subtracting the entropy term can only decrease the surrogate:
J_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi})\leq J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi})
. Taking the infimum over
\bm{\Pi}
gives
F_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M})\leq F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M})
. Conversely,
H(\bm{\pi}_{n})\leq\log K
gives
J_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi})\geq J_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M},\bm{\Pi})-\lambda\log K
, so
F_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M})\geq F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M})-\lambda\log K
. Together,

0\;\leq\;F_{\gamma}(\mathbf{M})-F_{\gamma}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{M})\;\leq\;\lambda\log K\qquad\forall\,\mathbf{M}.

∎

Appendix K
Interpretation of the stochastic assignment probabilities

Lemma K.1

(Closed-form saddle point in the scalar setting)
.

Assume
d=1
, fix
\gamma>1
, and let the centroids satisfy

\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<\cdots<\mu_{K}.

Set
\mu_{0}:=-\infty
,
\mu_{K+1}:=+\infty
, and

\mu_{k+\frac{1}{2}}:=\frac{\mu_{k}+\mu_{k+1}}{2},\qquad k=1,\dots,K-1.

Define

\displaystyle\Phi_{\gamma,k}

\displaystyle:=\mu_{k}+\left(1-\gamma^{-1}\right)\left[\frac{\mu_{k-1}-\mu_{k}}{2},\frac{\mu_{k+1}-\mu_{k}}{2}\right],\qquad k=1,\dots,K,

(75)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\gamma,k+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle:=\mu_{k+\frac{1}{2}}+\gamma^{-1}\left[-\frac{\mu_{k+1}-\mu_{k}}{2},\frac{\mu_{k+1}-\mu_{k}}{2}\right],\qquad k=1,\dots,K-1.

(76)

For a sample
x_{n}
, define

q_{k+\frac{1}{2},n}:=\frac{x_{n}-\mu_{k+\frac{1}{2}}}{(\mu_{k+1}-\mu_{k})/2},\qquad k=1,\dots,K-1.

(77)

Then the saddle point
(x_{n}^{\star},\pi_{n}^{\star})
from
Section

III
satisfies

x_{n}^{\star}=\begin{cases}x_{n}+\dfrac{x_{n}-\mu_{k}}{\gamma-1},&x_{n}\in\Phi_{\gamma,k},\\[8.00003pt]
\mu_{k+\frac{1}{2}},&x_{n}\in\Phi_{\gamma,k+\frac{1}{2}},\end{cases}

(78)

and the
k
-th coordinate of
\pi_{n}^{\star}
is

\pi_{kn}^{\star}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1+\gamma q_{k-\frac{1}{2},n}\bigr),&x_{n}\in\Phi_{\gamma,k-\frac{1}{2}},\\[8.00003pt]
1,&x_{n}\in\Phi_{\gamma,k},\\[8.00003pt]
\dfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1-\gamma q_{k+\frac{1}{2},n}\bigr),&x_{n}\in\Phi_{\gamma,k+\frac{1}{2}},\end{cases}\qquad k=1,\dots,K,

(79)

with the convention that
\Phi_{\gamma,\frac{1}{2}}=\Phi_{\gamma,K+\frac{1}{2}}=\varnothing
.

Proof.

Fix a scalar point
x\in\mathbb{R}
. By
Section

III
, a saddle point
(x^{\star},\pi^{\star})
satisfies

\displaystyle x^{\star}

\displaystyle=x+\frac{x-\sum_{j=1}^{K}\pi_{j}^{\star}\mu_{j}}{\gamma-1},

(80)

\displaystyle\pi^{\star}

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{\pi\in\Delta_{K}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}(x^{\star}-\mu_{j})^{2}\pi_{j}.

(81)

Because the objective in (
81
) is linear in
\pi
, every minimizer is supported on the set of centroids that are closest to
x^{\star}
. Since

\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<\cdots<\mu_{K},

a scalar point can have at most two nearest centroids, and if there are two of them, then they must be consecutive.

We consider the two possible cases.

Case 1: a unique nearest centroid

Suppose that
x^{\star}
is strictly closer to
\mu_{k}
than to all other centroids. Then

\pi^{\star}=\delta_{k}.

Substituting this into (
80
) yields

x^{\star}=x+\frac{x-\mu_{k}}{\gamma-1}.

The centroid
\mu_{k}
is the unique nearest one precisely when
x^{\star}
lies in the Voronoi cell of
\mu_{k}
, i.e.,

\mu_{k-\frac{1}{2}}<x^{\star}<\mu_{k+\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\mu_{k\pm\frac{1}{2}}:=\frac{\mu_{k}+\mu_{k\pm 1}}{2}.

Substituting the expression for
x^{\star}
and solving for
x
gives

\mu_{k}+\left(1-\gamma^{-1}\right)\frac{\mu_{k-1}-\mu_{k}}{2}<x<\mu_{k}+\left(1-\gamma^{-1}\right)\frac{\mu_{k+1}-\mu_{k}}{2}.

This is exactly the interior of
\Phi_{\gamma,k}
. Hence, for
x\in\Phi_{\gamma,k}
,

x^{\star}=x+\frac{x-\mu_{k}}{\gamma-1},\qquad\pi^{\star}=\delta_{k}.

Case 2: two nearest centroids

Suppose now that
x^{\star}
has two nearest centroids. Then they must be
\mu_{k}
and
\mu_{k+1}
, and necessarily

x^{\star}=\mu_{k+\frac{1}{2}}:=\frac{\mu_{k}+\mu_{k+1}}{2}.

In this case,

\pi^{\star}=p\,\delta_{k}+(1-p)\,\delta_{k+1}\qquad\text{for some }p\in[0,1].

Substituting this into (
80
) gives

\mu_{k+\frac{1}{2}}=x+\frac{x-(p\mu_{k}+(1-p)\mu_{k+1})}{\gamma-1}.

Rearranging,

\displaystyle\gamma(\mu_{k+\frac{1}{2}}-x)

\displaystyle=\mu_{k+\frac{1}{2}}-(p\mu_{k}+(1-p)\mu_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\left(p-\frac{1}{2}\right)(\mu_{k+1}-\mu_{k}).

Therefore,

p=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\gamma(x-\mu_{k+\frac{1}{2}})}{\mu_{k+1}-\mu_{k}}=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(1-\gamma q_{k+\frac{1}{2}}(x)\Bigr),

where

q_{k+\frac{1}{2}}(x):=\frac{x-\mu_{k+\frac{1}{2}}}{(\mu_{k+1}-\mu_{k})/2}.

The constraint
p\in[0,1]
is equivalent to

|x-\mu_{k+\frac{1}{2}}|\leq\gamma^{-1}\frac{\mu_{k+1}-\mu_{k}}{2},

which is precisely the condition
x\in\Phi_{\gamma,k+\frac{1}{2}}
. Hence, for
x\in\Phi_{\gamma,k+\frac{1}{2}}
,

x^{\star}=\mu_{k+\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\pi^{\star}=p\,\delta_{k}+(1-p)\,\delta_{k+1},\qquad p=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(1-\gamma q_{k+\frac{1}{2}}(x)\Bigr).

Applying the above characterization to
x=x_{n}
yields (
78
). The formula (
79
) follows by reading off the weight assigned to centroid
\mu_{k}
: it equals
1
on
\Phi_{\gamma,k}
, equals

\frac{1}{2}\bigl(1-\gamma q_{k+\frac{1}{2},n}\bigr)

on
\Phi_{\gamma,k+\frac{1}{2}}
, and equals

\frac{1}{2}\bigl(1+\gamma q_{k-\frac{1}{2},n}\bigr)

on
\Phi_{\gamma,k-\frac{1}{2}}
.
∎
