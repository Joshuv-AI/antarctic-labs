Characterization of an alkali- and glucose-tolerant β-glucosidase from Karamay saline-alkali soil and its structural basis for glucose tolerance
Authors: Qian Zhu, Qin Duan, Fei Wang, Zong-Jin Shao, Wei Hu, Yu-Kun Bi, Xiang Wang, Jian-Lan Li, Dan Zhu, Zhi-Hua Lv, Zheng-Feng Yang, Yi-Rui Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndustrial applications of β-glucosidases are often constrained by high salinity, alkaline conditions, and glucose inhibition.ResultsA glycoside hydrolase family 1 β-glucosidase, B0-BG40, was mined from the metagenome of saline-alkali soil in Karamay, Xinjiang, China. When heterologously expressed in Escherichia coli, B0-BG40 exhibited optimal activity at 45 °C and pH 8.6, retaining > 60% of its maximal activity over 20–55 °C and pH 5.6–9.6. The enzyme was highly stable at 25 °C, 40 °C and 45 °C and under alkaline conditions, maintaining > 85% residual activity after prolonged incubation and showing activity enhancement following incubation at pH 8.0–10.0. B0-BG40 also tolerated up to 2.0 M NaCl and 4.0 M glucose, and displayed weak glucose inhibition (Ki = 1033.5 mM). Combined with the results of protein homology modeling and molecular docking, a reasonable mechanistic hypothesis was proposed: the excellent glucose tolerance of the enzyme may be related to its narrow and deeply recessed catalytic channel, and this special channel structure could hinder glucose molecules from entering the active site.ConclusionsB0-BG40 is a salt-, alkali-, and glucose-tolerant β-glucosidase with strong potential for applications in food and feed processing and cellulosic ethanol production.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12896-026-01191-5.Keywords: β-Glucosidase, Alkaliphilic, Salt-tolerant, Glucose-tolerant, Thermostability

Introduction

Cellulose, the Earth’s most abundant biopolymer, comprises glucose units linked by β-1,4-glycosidic bonds. Its enzymatic depolymerization yields glucose and soluble oligosaccharides that are key substrates for bioethanol production and food processing [1]. Efficient cellulose saccharification depends on the coordinated action of endoglucanases (EC 3.2.1.4), exoglucanases (EC 3.2.1.91), and β-glucosidases (EC 3.2.1.21). Among these, β-glucosidases complete the hydrolysis of cellobiose and short cello-oligosaccharides into glucose, relieving product inhibition on upstream cellulases and driving saccharification to completion [2]. Although β-glucosidases are widespread in animals, plants, and microorganisms [3, 4], industrial applications mainly rely on microbial enzymes, as plant- and animal-derived counterparts generally have low yields, limited activity, and high production costs [5, 6].

In practice, however, many β-glucosidases are inhibited or destabilized under process-relevant conditions such as high glucose concentrations, elevated salinity, alkaline pH, chemical reagents, and high temperatures, which substantially constrains their performance in industrial setting [7–9]. Enzymes sourced from extreme environments often display intrinsic tolerance to such stresses. Representative examples include the halotolerant β-glucosidase Bgl3HB from Trichoderma harzianum isolated from Hainan mangroves, which exhibits strong salt and ion tolerance [10]; the hot-spring metagenome-derived BglM, which shows enhanced thermal stability and tolerance to glucose and ethanol [11]; and a β-glucosidase from the marine bacterium Alteromonas sp. L82, which displays cold adaptation and salt tolerance [12]. Other reports describe enzymes with partial glucose tolerance: the β-glucosidase from Thermoanaerobacterium aotearoense P8G3#4 is activated by 50–250 mM glucose and retains more than 40% activity in 1 M glucose but loses stability at elevated temperatures [13], whereas the recombinant EaBgl1A from Antarctic soil bacteria maintains 50% activity in 1 M glucose and retains half of its activity after prolonged incubation at 30 °C [14]. Despite these advances, many reported glucose-tolerant β-glucosidases exhibit low catalytic efficiency or insufficient stability when exposed to multiple simultaneous process stresses, limiting their applicability [15–17]. Moreover, the structural determinants of glucose tolerance—such as features that modulate product access to the active site—remain poorly understood.

Because approximately 99% of microorganisms are not readily cultivable using standard laboratory techniques, accessing novel β-glucosidases from extreme environments remains challenging [18]. Metagenomics overcomes this limitation by directly retrieving functional genes from environmental DNA and has emerged as a powerful strategy for enzyme discovery [11, 19].

Here, we used a metagenomic approach to identify a new glycoside hydrolase family 1 (GH1) β-glucosidase gene (b0-bg40) from saline–alkaline soil in Karamay, Xinjiang, China, and heterologously expressed it in E. coli. Comprehensive biochemical characterization revealed strong alkaline preference, along with notable salt and glucose tolerance and thermal stability. To investigate the mechanistic basis of glucose tolerance, we analyzed the enzyme’s structure and performed molecular docking. These findings highlight B0-BG40 as a robust candidate for applications in feed and food processing and in cellulosic bioethanol production.

Materials and methods

Sample, medium, strains and plasmid

A saline–alkaline sediment sample (total salt content, 29.05%; pH 8.62) was collected from Karamay, Xinjiang, China (45.56104° N, 85.20898° E) and immediately stored on dry ice until analysis. E. coli DH5α (Shenzhen KT Life Technology) was employed for cloning and constitutive expression using the plasmid pSHY211 [20]. Cells were cultured in Luria–Bertani (LB) medium (10 g/L tryptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, 10 g/L NaCl) supplemented with kanamycin (50 µg/mL). Solid medium was prepared by supplementing LB medium with 2% (w/v) agar (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). Gene insertion was performed via restriction digestion (EcoR I, Hind III; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and seamless cloning (pEASY-Uni Seamless Cloning Kit; TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China), followed by gel extraction and purification as necessary. Protein expression was assessed by SDS–PAGE (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), and β-glucosidase activity was quantified using a glucose oxidase–peroxidase assay kit (BioSino Bio-Technology & Science Inc., Beijing, China).

DNA extraction and metagenomic sequencing

Metagenomic DNA was extracted with the PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit (MOBIO, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (GENEWIZ, Suzhou, China). Raw reads were processed and analyzed using the Integrated Microbial Genomes (IMG) system (https://img.jgi.doe.gov/). Predicted open reading frames were functionally annotated against the COG [21], KEGG [22], and Pfam [23] databases.

Sequence analysis

Sequence homology was assessed using BLAST searches against the NCBI database. Signal peptides were predicted using SignalP (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/SignalP-5.0/), and nucleotide sequences were translated into amino acid sequences using the ExPASy Translate tool Theoretical isoelectric point (pI) and molecular weight were calculated using the ExPASy ProtParam server (https://www.expasy.org/). For phylogenetic analysis, β-glucosidase sequences with high similarity from related genera were used as the ingroup, whereas more divergent sequences from other genera served as the outgroup. Maximum-likelihood trees were constructed in MEGA 11 using the Poisson correction model. The three-dimensional structure of B0-BG40 was predicted using the AlphaFold server. Multiple sequence alignment was performed in ClustalX 2.0 and visualized with ESPript 3.0. Structural models were rendered in PyMOL 2.6.0.

Amplification, cloning expression, and screening of the b0-bg40 gene

Using the metagenomic DNA as template, b0-bg40 was amplified with primers b0-bg40-F (CATCATCATCATCATCATGAAATGCGCATGAAGGGCCTTCGG) and b0-bg40-R (GTGCTCGAGTGCGGCCGCAAGTTATTCGAGACCGGTGATCTCGCG). The italicized regions correspond to homology arms for recombination into pSHY211 pre-digested with EcoR I and Hind III [20]. PCR products were inserted into linearized pSHY211 using the pEASY-Uni Seamless Cloning Kit and transformed into E. coli DH5α by Ca2+-mediated chemical transformation. Transformants were selected on LB–kanamycin agar (50 µg/mL) based on the absence of of green fluorescence and were verified by colony PCR and Sanger sequencing. The confirmed construct was designated pSHY211-B0-BG40 and validated by alignment with the metagenomic sequence.

Expression, purification, and identification of B0-BG40

The recombinant strain E. coli DH5α/pSHY211-B0-BG40 was streaked on LB–kanamycin agar (50 µg/mL) for activation. Single colonies were inoculated into LB broth and incubated at 37 °C with shaking (180 rpm) for 8 h, followed by 25 °C for 12 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C and disrupted by ultrasonication on ice. After clarification by centrifugation, the supernatant was applied to Ni–NTA affinity resin as described previously [24]. The eluted protein was analyzed by 12% SDS–PAGE with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 staining. Protein concentration was determined by the Bradford assay (Sangon Biotech) using bovine serum albumin as the standard.

Enzyme activity assay of β-glucosidase

β-Glucosidase activity was measured using cellobiose (Sangon Biotech) as the substrate. Reactions (100 µL) contained 10 µL enzyme and 90 µL buffer with 1% (w/v) cellobiose and were incubated under the specified conditions for 15 min. Reactions were quenched by freezing at − 80 °C for 5 min. Aliquots (10 µL) were transferred to a 96-well plate, mixed with 200 µL glucose oxidase–peroxidase reagent, incubated at 37 °C for 10 min, and absorbance was read at 492 nm. Glucose concentration was determined as described [25]. One unit (U) of activity was defined as the amount of enzyme releasing 2 µmol of glucose per minute from cellobiose under the assay conditions. Each experimental group was independently repeated three times, and the mean value was used for subsequent analyses.

Optimal reaction temperature and thermal stability

The optimal temperature was determined at pH 7.0 across 5–75 °C in 5 °C increments by measuring relative activity. Thermal stability was evaluated by incubating the enzyme at 25, 40, 45, 50, 55 and 60 °C for up to 10 h. Aliquots were collected every hour, and the residual enzyme activity was determined under standard assay conditions. Activity prior to incubation was defined as 100%. Each experimental group was independently repeated three times, and the mean value was used for subsequent analyses.

Optimal pH and pH stability

The optimal pH was determined at the optimal temperature using citrate–phosphate buffer (pH 3.0–8.0) and glycine–NaOH buffer (pH 8.0–11.0). For pH stability, the enzyme was incubated at 4 °C in buffers spanning pH 3.0–10.0 for 12 and 24 h. Residual activity was measured under standard conditions and expressed relative to the untreated control. Each experimental group was independently repeated three times, and the mean value was used for subsequent analyses.

Effects of different metal ions and inhibitors on the activity of the recombinant enzyme

The effects of metal ions (K⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe³⁺, Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺, Co²⁺, Cu²⁺, Ag⁺, Mn²⁺, Pb²⁺, and Ni²⁺) at final concentrations of 1 and 10 mM on β-glucosidase activity were assessed. Chemical reagents were also tested, including EDTA, SDS, PMSF, and DTT (0.1% and 1%, w/v), and methanol (MeOH), ethanol (EtOH), β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME), isopropanol (IPA), and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (1% and 10%, v/v). In addition, the effects of four metal ions (Mg²⁺, Fe³⁺, Ca²⁺, and Cu2+) on recombinant β-glucosidase activity were evaluated at final concentrations of 2, 5, 15, and 20 mM. All reactions were performed under optimal assay conditions, and residual activity was expressed relative to the untreated control. Each experimental group was independently repeated three times, and the mean value was used for subsequent analyses.

Effects of NaCl on the activity of the recombinant enzyme

As determined in Section “Optimal reaction temperature and thermal stability” and “Optimal pH and pH stability”, the optimal reaction temperature and pH for B0-BG40 were 45 °C and 8.6, respectively. To assess the immediate effect of salt, enzyme activity was measured in 1% (w/v) cellobiose solutions containing 0–3500 mM NaCl under optimal conditions (45 °C, pH 8.6, 30 min). Activity without NaCl was defined as 100%. Salt tolerance was further evaluated by pre-incubating B0-BG40 in glycine–NaOH buffer (pH 8.6) with 0–5000 mM NaCl at 4 °C for 12 or 24 h, followed by determination of residual activity under standard conditions. Activity prior to NaCl exposure was used as the control [26]. Each experimental group was independently repeated three times, and the mean value was used for subsequent analyses.

Effects of glucose and ethanol on the activity of the recombinant enzyme

Glucose tolerance was evaluated using p-nitrophenyl-β-D-glucopyranoside (pNPG) as the substrate. Reaction mixtures contained 10 µL purified enzyme, 125 µL glucose solution (0–4000 mM), and 15 µL pNPG (final 1 mM). Reactions were incubated at 45 °C for 10 min and terminated by adding 450 µL of 1 M Na₂CO₃. Absorbance was measured at 405 nm; substrate blanks served as controls. Ethanol tolerance was assessed by measuring activity in 1% (w/v) cellobiose solutions containing 0–40% (v/v) ethanol under optimal conditions (45 °C, pH 8.6, 30 min). Activity in the absence of ethanol was defined as 100%. Each experimental group was independently repeated three times, and the mean value was used for subsequent analyses.

Determination of substrate specificity and kinetic parameters

Substrate specificity of B0-BG40 was assessed using the following substrates: cellobiose, p‑nitrophenyl‑β‑D‑glucopyranoside (pNPG), α‑lactose, D-(+)-maltose monohydrate, D-(+)-trehalose dihydrate, D-(+)-melibiose hydrate, raffinose, sucrose, corncob xylan, Avicel, carboxymethyl cellulose sodium salt (CMC‑Na), bagasse xylan, and beechwood xylan. Unless otherwise stated, reactions were performed under the standard assay conditions (45 °C, pH 8.6), using 1% (w/v) substrate and the same enzyme loading and incubation time. For cellobiose, glucose release was quantified using the glucose oxidase–peroxidase method at 492 nm; for pNPG, p‑nitrophenol release was quantified at 405 nm. Kinetic parameters were determined with cellobiose at 2–20 mg/mL. Initial rates were measured at 5 and 10 min to verify linearity. Apparent Km and Vmax values were obtained by fitting the initial rate data to the Michaelis–Menten equation using nonlinear regression; Lineweaver–Burk plots were generated for visualization and consistency checks. A blank control without enzyme addition was included simultaneously. Each experiment was independently performed in triplicate, and mean values were used for subsequent data analysis.

Structural analysis of glucose tolerance in B0-BG40

Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina with glucose as the ligand. The receptor was kept rigid, while the ligand was treated as flexible. The docking grid was centered on the predicted catalytic pocket, which was identified together with potential access channels using the Hotspot Wizard server (https://loschmidt.chemi.muni.cz/hotspotwizard/); channel- and pocket-related geometric parameters were obtained from the server outputs. Key residues and structural features implicated in substrate access were visualized in PyMOL. Docking analyses were performed using default parameters, and docking scores were used for comparative analysis rather than absolute binding affinity estimation.

Enzymatic hydrolysis of sugarcane bagasse and rice straw by B0-BG40

Pretreatment was performed according to a previous study [2]. Briefly, 10 g of each substrate, including sugarcane bagasse and rice straw, was ground and passed through an 80-mesh sieve. The ground samples were heated in water at 80 °C for 2 h. In addition, rice straw was further subjected to alkaline pretreatment with 2% NaOH at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:20 at 80 °C for 2 h. After pretreatment, all samples were rinsed with deionized water until the wash water became colorless, then drained and dried at 80 °C for subsequent use.

For hydrolysis, 0.2 g of each pretreated substrate was suspended in 4 mL of glycine–NaOH buffer (pH 8.6), mixed thoroughly, and soaked overnight. The residues were collected by centrifugation and incubated with 0.1 mg cellulase from Trichoderma reesei (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) and 0.2 mg recombinant B0-BG40. Reactions were incubated at 35 °C for 10 h, with samples collected at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 h. After centrifugation, the supernatants were collected, and glucose concentrations were measured using a glucose oxidase–peroxidase assay kit. A blank control without enzyme addition was included. Each experiment was independently performed in triplicate, and mean values were used for subsequent data analysis.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise stated, all experiments were independently performed in three biological replicates, and mean values were used for subsequent analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 21.0, and results were reported as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s multiple range test, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05 [27].

Results

Cloning, expression, purification and sequence analysis of B0-BG40

A new β-glucosidase gene, named b0-bg40, was predicted from the metagenomic data of saline–alkaline soil in Karamay. Its open reading frame (ORF) is 1,431 bp, encoding a 476‑amino‑acid protein. It shares 56.79% sequence identity with characterized GH1 β‑glucosidases, indicating that it represents a distinct member of this family. Phylogenetic analysis placed B0‑BG40 within the GH1 clade alongside related bacterial β‑glucosidases (Fig. 1). The b0-bg40-encoding gene was amplified by PCR and verified by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis. The amplicon appeared as a single clear band under UV illumination (Fig. S1). The target PCR product was purified using a gel extraction kit. Colonies lacking green fluorescence under UV illumination indicated successful transformation of the recombinant plasmid pSHY211-B0-BG40 into E. coli DH5α competent cells (Fig. S2). Sanger sequencing confirmed the insert sequence, which matched the metagenomic contig. The B0‑BG40 sequence has been submitted to GenBank (accession number: XHB39177).

Maximum‑likelihood phylogenetic tree based on amino acid sequences showing the position of B0‑BG40 among related β‑glucosidases. Bootstrap values (percentages of 1,000 replications) are shown at the nodes

ProtParam predicted a theoretical pI of 5.88 and a molecular mass of 53.7 kDa for B0‑BG40. The recombinant His‑tagged protein was purified using Ni2+ affinity chromatography, and SDS–PAGE revealed a single band consistent with the expected size (Fig. 2A). Structural modeling indicated a canonical GH1 (β/α)8 TIM‑barrel fold (Fig. 2B). Multiple sequence alignment with representative GH1 enzymes identified two conserved glutamates, Glu179 and Glu381, as putative catalytic residues, and suggested several residues that may contribute to substrate binding (Gln33, His134, Asn178, Tyr326, Trp428, Glu435, Trp436) (Fig. 3).

Enzymatic properties of purified β-glucosidase B0-BG40

Optimal temperature and thermal tolerance

Using 1% cellobiose as substrate, B0‑BG40 exhibited optimal activity at 45 °C and retained more than 80% of maximal activity between 25 and 50 °C. The enzyme showed appreciable low‑temperature activity, retaining 25%, 36%, and 81% activity at 5 °C, 15 °C, and 25 °C, respectively, whereas activity declined rapidly above 50 °C (Fig. 4A). Thermal stability analysis showed that the enzyme exhibited excellent stability at room and moderate temperatures. After incubation at 25 °C, 40 °C and 45 °C for 10 h, the residual activity remained above 85%. Notably, at 50 °C, enzyme stability decreased markedly after 7 h of incubation, with an estimated half-life of approximately 8.17 h, and activity was almost completely lost after 9 h. At 55 °C, the enzyme retained more than 40% residual activity after 4 h of incubation, whereas at 60 °C, it was completely inactivated after 2 h (Fig. 4C).

(C) Thermal stability at 25 °C, 40 °C, 45 °C, 50 °C, 55 °C and 60 °C during incubation for 0–10 h. (D) pH stability after incubation at the indicated pH. Data are mean ± SEM (n = 3). * P < 0.05; ** P < 0.01. Activity prior to treatment was defined as 100% (6.25 ± 0.18 U/mg)

Optimal pH and pH stability

B0‑BG40 showed optimal activity at pH 8.6 and remained active across a broad pH range of 5.6–9.6 (Fig. 4B). pH stability assays demonstrated strong alkaline tolerance; after incubation at 4 °C for 12–24 h, the enzyme retained more than 60% activity from pH 6.0 to 10.0, with higher residual activity observed in the alkaline range (Fig. 4D). By contrast, residual activity decreased markedly under acidic conditions (pH 4.0-5.6), falling below 60%. The combination of mesophilic operating temperatures, pronounced alkaliphily, and high pH stability is advantageous for industrial settings where reduced energy input and alkaline processing conditions are desirable.

Effects of metal ions, chemical reagents, NaCl, glucose, and ethanol on the activity of recombinant enzyme B0-BG40

To evaluate the effects of metal ions and chemical reagents, relative B0-BG40 activity was measured in the presence of different additives. B0-BG40 showed differential sensitivity to these additives: 1 mM Fe³⁺ significantly activated the enzyme, whereas 1% EDTA slightly enhanced its activity. In contrast, Zn2+, Co2+, Ag+, Mn2+, Pb2+, and Ni2+ strongly inhibited activity at concentrations of 1 mM and 10 mM; notably, 10 mM Zn2+ and Ag+ completely inactivated the enzyme. High concentrations of SDS and β-ME also fully inactivated the enzyme, whereas the other chemical reagents caused concentration-dependent reductions in activity (Fig. 5).

To further assess dose-dependent effects, Mg2+, Fe³⁺, Ca2+, and Cu2+ were tested at 0–20 mM. Fe³⁺ exerted a biphasic effect, initially activating and subsequently inhibiting the enzyme. Mg2+ slightly activated the enzyme at low concentrations but reduced activity at higher concentrations. Ca2+ caused a gradual decrease in activity, although considerable activity was still retained at 20 mM. Notably, Fe³⁺ and Cu2+ at at concentrations of 15 mM and 20 mM nearly abolished enzyme activity (Fig. 6).

Consistent with its saline–alkaline origin, B0‑BG40 displayed notable halotolerance. Although activity declined with increasing NaCl, the enzyme still retained more than 20% relative activity in the presence of up to 2,000 mM NaCl (Fig. 7A). In stability assays, pre‑incubation at 500 mM NaCl yielded 67% and 33% residual activity after 12 h and 24 h, respectively (Fig. 7B). Additionally, 1 mM pNPG was used as a substrate to evaluate the glucose tolerance of B0-BG40. B0-BG40 retained more than 50% activity at glucose concentrations up to 3,500 mM, with a Ki value of 1033.5 mM (Fig. 8). The high Ki and sustained activity under molar glucose conditions highlight B0-BG40 as a promising candidate for high-sugar industrial applications.

B0-BG40 also tolerated ethanol, retaining more than 70% activity at 5% (v/v) and more than 25% at 15% (v/v) ethanol (Fig. S4). Collectively, these findings indicate that B0-BG40 tolerates metal ions, high salt, and ethanol at concentrations relevant for lignocellulose bioconversion.

Substrate specificity and kinetic parameters of recombinant enzyme B0-BG40

B0‑BG40 showed highest activity toward the aryl β‑glucoside pNPG (11.03 ± 0.10 U/mg), followed by the natural substrate cellobiose (6.25 ± 0.18 U/mg). No activity was detected toward α‑lactose, sucrose, corncob xylan, Avicel, CMC‑Na, bagasse xylan, or beechwood xylan under the tested conditions, indicating a preference for β‑glucosidic linkages in small, soluble substrates. Kinetic analysis with cellobiose under optimal conditions yielded an apparent Km of 31.50 mM and a Vmax of 15.53 µmol/min/mg. The kinetic parameters determined using the Michaelis–Menten equation were consistent with those derived from the Lineweaver–Burk double-reciprocal plot (Fig. S5).

Predicted structural basis for the high glucose tolerance of B0‑BG40

In silico analysis indicated that B0‑BG40 possesses a narrow, deep substrate-access channel leading to the catalytic pocket. Channel geometry calculated by Hotspot Wizard estimated an approximate radius of 2.2 Å and length of 3.8 Å (Fig. 9). The catalytic glutamates (E179 and E381) reside at the base of this channel, suggesting restricted access for small molecules in B0-BG40.

Predicted catalytic pockets and substrate‑access channels of B0‑BG40. Catalytic pockets are shown in yellow; access channels are shown in purple

Consistent with this architecture, docking suggested weaker binding of glucose than cellobiose in B0‑BG40 (best‑scoring poses: -5.3 vs. -6.9 kcal/mol) (Fig. 10). Although docking scores are not absolute measures of binding affinity, the relative trend implies a potentially reduced product occupancy of the active site in B0‑BG40. Several hydrophobic residues (V182, L186, V237, G238) cluster around E179 at the channel entrance in B0-BG40, forming a putative gate that is hypothesized to limit monosaccharide diffusion (Fig. 11). Collectively, these computationally derived features offer a hypothetical structural basis for the high glucose tolerance of B0-BG40.

Synergistic hydrolysis of lignocellulosic substrates by B0-BG40 and commercial cellulase

Evaluating the hydrolytic activity of B0-BG40 on sugarcane bagasse and rice straw provides an effective assessment of its potential application in lignocellulose degradation. The results indicate that supplementing commercial cellulase with β-glucosidase B0-BG40 significantly enhanced the hydrolysis of sugarcane bagasse and alkali-pretreated rice straw compared with cellulase alone (Fig. 12). In contrast, the combination of B0-BG40 and commercial cellulase did not show a significant improvement in the hydrolysis of hot-water-pretreated rice straw compared with commercial cellulase alone (Fig. S6). Notably, B0-BG40 showed no detectable hydrolytic activity toward sugarcane bagasse or rice straw when used alone. However, when combined with commercial cellulase, B0-BG40 exhibited a pronounced synergistic effect during the hydrolysis of hot-water-pretreated sugarcane bagasse and alkali-pretreated rice straw. This synergistic effect suggests that B0-BG40 can act in concert with commercial cellulase, highlighting its potential for applications in lignocellulose degradation.

Discussion

As the terminal enzyme in the cellulase system, β-glucosidase plays a central role in lignocellulose bioconversion [28]. Metagenomic analysis of saline-alkaline soil from Karamay, Xinjiang identified a novel β-glucosidase gene, b0-bg40, which shared only 56.79% sequence similarity with a GH1 β-glucosidase from Puniceicoccaceae bacterium K14, supporting its novelty. The recombinant enzyme B0-BG40 exhibited optimal activity at 45 °C and pH 8.6, with maximal activity at pH 8.0–9.0 and less than 60% relative activity at pH 4.0–5.6, indicating a mesophilic and alkaliphilic enzyme. This alkaline preference sets B0-BG40 apart from most reported β-glucosidases, which generally exhibit optimal activity at pH 4.0-7.5 and stability across pH 4.0–9.0 [29]. Its catalytic properties resemble those of the β-glucosidase from Wickerhamomyces anomalus BS81, isolated from olive brine, which exhibits an optimal temperature of 35 °C and optimal pH of 8.5 [30]. B0-BG40 also appears more pH-stable than β-glucosidases from xerophilic Aspergillus chevalieri, Alteromonas sp. L82 and Bacillus cellulosilyticus [12, 27, 31]. These properties may facilitate industrial applications by lowering energy requirements and enhancing process flexibility.

B0-BG40 also displayed high thermal stability. In contrast to Bgl3HB from mangrove-derived Trichoderma harzianum, which retained less than 80% activity after 4 h at 50 °C [10], and the metagenome-derived Bgl1269, which retained only 20% of maximal activity after 30 min at 45 °C [32], B0-BG40 retained more than 80% relative activity after 10 h at both 40 °C and 45 °C. Such stability is beneficial for industrial processes, including simultaneous saccharification and fermentation and consolidated bioprocessing, operating under conditions compatible with fermentative microorganisms such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae [33, 34].

During biofuel production from lignocellulosic biomass, high concentrations of glucose are generated. Glucose, the end product of the β-glucosidase-catalyzed reaction, inhibits enzyme activity, thereby limiting the industrial application of β-glucosidases [35]. Given the high glucose concentrations generated during biomass hydrolysis, developing β-glucosidases capable of tolerating such conditions is essential for improving the efficiency of enzymatic hydrolysis systems [35, 36]. A notable feature of B0-BG40 is its remarkable glucose tolerance. Although activity declined as glucose concentration increased from 0 to 4000 mM, more than 30% activity was retained at 4000 mM, with a glucose inhibition constant (Ki) of 1033.5 mM, far exceeding the 0.5–100 mM range reported for most microbial β-glucosidases [31]. According to the classification proposed by Cao et al. [37] and expanded by Salgado et al. [35], B0-BG40 can be classified as a type II enzyme with glucose tolerance. Enzymes in this group exhibit Ki values for glucose exceeding 0.1 M.

Glucose tolerance is critical for β-glucosidase applications, making its structural basis particularly relevant. Certain GH1 enzymes can tolerate glucose concentrations 10–1,000 times higher than those tolerated by GH3 enzymes [38, 39]. In this context, Giuseppe et al. [40] discussed that glucose tolerance is influenced by the molecular characteristics of the active site entrance, including its shape and electrostatic properties. Specifically, the active site entrance in these enzymes is narrower than in GH3 enzymes, limiting glucose access to the catalytic pocket and reducing its role as a substrate in the catalytic cycle. Consistent with this model, B0-BG40 is predicted to have a channel radius of 2.2 Å and a length of 3.8 Å, whereas the glucose-sensitive GH1 enzyme TrBgl2 has a slightly wider but much shorter channel (2.4 Å and 1.5 Å, respectively) [41] (Fig. 13). The catalytic residues E179 and E381 of B0-BG40 are located at the base of this channel, suggesting that limited substrate access potentially contributes to glucose tolerance. Docking analysis further aligns with this hypothesis. B0-BG40 showed binding free energies of -5.3 kcal/mol for glucose and − 6.9 kcal/mol for cellobiose, whereas TrBgl2 showed values of -6.0 and − 6.2 kcal/mol, respectively (Fig. 14). The lower affinity of B0-BG40 for glucose may therefore reduce product inhibition. Additionally, glucose-tolerant GH1 enzymes often contain conserved aromatic and aliphatic residues at the + 2 subsite that restrict glucose entry into the − 1 subsite [40, 42]. In B0-BG40, the hydrophobic residues V182, L186, V237, and G238 near E179 are hypothesized to perform a similar gatekeeping function. These structural interpretations for B0-BG40 remain hypothetical and require further experimental validation.

Although the catalytic efficiency of B0-BG40 toward cellobiose (Km = 31.50 mM) is moderate compared to certain highly active β-glucosidases [14, 44], it offers a valuable trade-off by demonstrating robust endurance in extreme environments. For instance, unlike enzymes optimized solely for mild laboratory conditions, B0-BG40 maintains functional stability under alkaline pH and severe product inhibition (Ki = 1033.5 mM). However, we acknowledge that the current saccharification assays primarily assess its synergistic performance under standard conditions. A parallel quantitative comparison with other reported glucose-tolerant β-glucosidases was not conducted in this study, and the exact degree of synergistic enhancement remains to be fully quantified. Furthermore, actual industrial biomass conversion often involves a complex matrix of simultaneous stresses. To fully establish its translational significance, future studies will need to rigorously benchmark B0-BG40 under multi-stress conditions (i.e., concurrent high glucose, high salinity, and alkaline pH) to define its absolute operational advantages and limitations in real-world applications. In summary, the enzymatic characteristics of B0-BG40 revealed here position it as a highly promising candidate among reported β-glucosidases (Table 1), exhibiting multiple traits favorable for industrial applications [6, 13–15, 32, 37, 43–45].

Conclusion

In this study, metagenomic mining of Karamay saline–alkaline soil yielded a novel GH1 β-glucosidase, B0-BG40, which was successfully heterologously expressed in E. coli. B0-BG40 exhibits an alkaline preference, high thermal stability, and exceptional glucose tolerance (Ki ≈ 1030 mM), maintaining activity under process-relevant stresses. Structural modeling and molecular docking analysis suggest a potential correlation between its high glucose tolerance and the narrow, deeply recessed catalytic channel. These features render B0-BG40 a promising biocatalyst for food processing and cellulosic bioenergy, and highlight saline–alkaline environments as valuable reservoirs of industrial enzymes.
