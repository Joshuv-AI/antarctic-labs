Understanding fibromyalgia beyond pain: emotional imbalance and the case for compassionate care
Authors: Ana M Pinto, Mariana Luís, Leonard Calabrese, Rinie Geenen, José António Pereira da Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIt is postulated that fibromyalgia reflects a neurobiologically rooted imbalance between overactivated threat systems and hyporesponsive soothing systems, shaped by adversity. Adverse childhood experiences leave epigenetic marks that sensitise pain and stress pathways. Emotions have measurable biological correlates-they modulate immune, neuroendocrine and inflammatory processes that sustain chronic pain. The patient–clinician relationship is a biologically active intervention, operating through placebo mechanisms, neural synchrony and vagal coregulation. Compassionate, validating clinical encounters can restore safeness and improve outcomes-not only in fibromyalgia but across a wide range of chronic pain conditions.

Fibromyalgia is one of the leading causes of chronic pain-related disability, its prevalence being estimated at 2%–4% worldwide.1 Despite this, it remains widely misunderstood and often poorly managed, resulting in prolonged suffering, repeated consultations and mutual frustration between patients and clinicians across all specialties.2 Fibromyalgia presents with considerable phenotypic heterogeneity: a systematic review of 39 cluster-analytic studies identified different profiles for symptom manifestation, personality, psychological burden and treatment response.3 Subgroup membership extends beyond clinical presentation, with distinct biomarker signatures mapping onto hypothesised pathogenetic mechanisms expressed across, but not uniformly present within, the patient population.4–6 Mounting evidence from affective neuroscience, stress biology and placebo science points to an underappreciated dimension of this complexity: affect dysregulation and the relational context of care. For a meaningful subset of patients, a threat-soothing imbalance may be not only a feature of the condition but a tractable target for intervention above and beyond interventions targeting symptoms. This reframing has implications for every clinician who encounters chronic pain in daily practice.

While empathy, compassion and validation are core to any therapeutic alliance irrespective of aetiology, they may become especially critical when emotional dysregulation and prior experience of invalidation are present. Here, the therapeutic relationship shifts from a contextual factor to the mechanism through which change becomes possible. Qualitative evidence shows that people with fibromyalgia regard these competencies as key for clinical safety and trust, in part because stigma, dismissal and invalidation remain pervasive features, aggravating disease burden, prolonging diagnostic delay and rendering the encounter iatrogenic.7 Yet empathic care, even when offered, may not be perceived as such: people with fibromyalgia show stronger negative interpretation biases for ambiguous socio-affective cues from clinicians than both chronic pain and pain-free populations. Although directionality remains unclear, this suggests that the way care is perceived is itself part of the clinical picture, and that empathic engagement must be deliberate, consistent and explicit.8

The challenge of fibromyalgia across clinical practice

Fibromyalgia is characterised by chronic widespread pain alongside a broad spectrum of associated symptoms, including fatigue, unrefreshing sleep and cognitive dysfunction. Current taxonomic frameworks classify it as a primary and nociplastic pain condition.9 10

Fibromyalgia is associated with significant suffering, reshaping of one’s sense of self and disruption of individual and social functioning.11 The condition constitutes one of the most common reasons for referral to rheumatology, pain and rehabilitation services.12 Patients with fibromyalgia and other forms of chronic primary pain are encountered daily in primary care and across virtually every medical specialty.13 Fibromyalgia often co-occurs with other chronic diseases, aggravating their impact, confounding their assessment and worsening outcomes.14 Many clinicians feel ill-equipped to address it, and some approach it with scepticism or regard it as beyond their scope due to the lack of an objective diagnostic marker. This stance is both scientifically unwarranted and therapeutically counterproductive, and can inadvertently compound patients’ suffering and undermine the clinician’s capacity for meaningful intervention. This reticence may stem from limited understanding of fibromyalgia’s intricate mechanisms, from patient–clinician communication challenges and from the lack of consistently effective therapies. A deeper understanding of the emotional and neurobiological underpinnings of fibromyalgia may help clinicians across disciplines approach these patients with greater empathy, efficacy and professional satisfaction.

Emotional dysregulation as a relevant mechanism

No single factor is universal or sufficient to fully account for fibromyalgia. Understanding it requires a biopsychosocial framework that recognises its multifactorial nature, with biological (central sensitisation, autonomic dysregulation, non-restorative sleep and physical deconditioning), psychological (cognitive-affective and socioemotional dysregulation such as alexithymia, reduced affect differentiation, altered social cognition and heightened sensitivity to interpersonal stressors) and social factors (trauma, socioeconomic deprivation) interacting and mutually reinforcing one another.14 Thus, besides biological factors, psychosocial factors also play a role in fibromyalgia. A comprehensive review of the literature14 supports the association between fibromyalgia and chronic stress, which is reinforced by recent evidence of the association of fibromyalgia with heightened levels of emotions related to threat (eg, anxiety, anger) and lower intensity of drive and soothing emotions (eg, pride, contentment, relaxation).15 This emotional profile reliably distinguished fibromyalgia from controls (area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve=0.83), a finding consistent with the FITSS (Fibromyalgia: Imbalance of Threat and Soothing Systems) model’s predictions about the emotional signature of threat–soothing imbalance.16

The FITSS model16 provides an integrative framework that emphasises the central role of the Salience Network in the maintenance of this condition. It postulates that this ‘central alarm system’ is continuously activated by an imbalance between an oversensitive threat perception—tuned to vigilance, defence, and pain anticipation—and a hyporesponsive soothing system that supports safeness, social connection and physiological recovery. This dysregulation keeps mind and body in a persistent, exhausting ‘fight-or-flight’ mode. By keeping its many effectors (eg, the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, the autonomic nervous system) in overdrive, the hyperactive salience network may generate and amplify the typically multifaceted symptoms of fibromyalgia: pain, fatigue, cognitive difficulties and sensory hypersensitivity.16

Data-driven risk scores have demonstrated that mood, neuroticism, life stressors and sleep are prospectively associated with pain topography, long-term pain and pain-related disability, with mood being the primary precursor of distally spreading pain.17 Noteworthy, the FITSS model is an integrative working hypothesis with incipient empirical support, not an established causal account of fibromyalgia across all presentations; available evidence corroborates rather than confirms it, and intervention efficacy does not constitute strict logical validation. However, where emotional dysregulation is prominent, it represents a neurobiologically grounded response shaped by genetic predisposition, developmental history and early relational experiences: a finding that should orient clinicians towards validation rather than blame attribution or aetiological reductionism.

From adversity to biology: how life shapes pain

While early adversity, including associated timing, type and recurrence features, may shape pain experience and pain trajectories across the lifespan,18 it constitutes neither a necessary nor sufficient condition for fibromyalgia development, as evidenced by the substantial proportion of patients with no identifiable trauma history. Prospective and cross-sectional designs nonetheless indicate links between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and chronic widespread pain across the lifespan, with dose-dependent effects in adolescents and emotional distress as a possible mediating pathway.18–20 Elevated ACE prevalence, ORs exceeding 3.0 relative to controls in some studies,21 and preclinical and preliminary epigenetic evidence of stress-regulatory reprogramming suggest that fibromyalgia reflects, in at least a meaningful subset of patients, the biological echoes of adversity. Replication across independent samples strengthens confidence in these associations, yet causal inference remains constrained: most studies are predominantly observational, of low quality and do not account for potential genetic liability, retrospective recall bias and the frequent co-occurrence of adversity types.21 22

ACEs have been linked to threat sensitisation, safety learning deficits and altered stress reactivity, disrupting the development of emotion regulation competencies and depriving the child of the tools needed for a balanced emotional experience throughout life.23–25

These psychological processes have measurable biological correlates: emotions do not run in a biological vacuum. They depend on and induce chemical and physiological responses involving the immune system, inflammatory processes and a variety of multipotent neurotransmitters.26 Chronic stress has been shown to alter pain-related and emotion-related neurobiological mechanisms induce hyperalgesia and allodynia and contribute to the development of depressive symptomatology. Recent preclinical work has demonstrated that chronic social stress induces autoantibodies and neutrophils reactive to nervous tissues,27 28 findings that, despite requiring further replication in human designs, offer a plausible biological bridge between psychosocial adversity and the immune and inflammatory disturbances documented in this condition.

The clinical relevance of this pathway is clear. The worsening of fibromyalgia during periods of stress is a commonplace finding in clinical practice. By implication, mitigating stress—whether through psychological intervention, improved social support or a more therapeutic clinical relationship—may have analgesic and regulatory effects. This principle extends beyond fibromyalgia to chronic primary pain conditions broadly, to functional somatic syndromes and to postacute infection syndromes (PAIS), which share overlapping mechanisms of central sensitisation, stress-related immune dysregulation and emotion dysregulation.29–32 Childhood adversity may also disrupt vagally mediated autonomic regulation of pain and inflammation, representing an additional pathway through which early experience shapes later pain biology.33 34

The clinical encounter as a therapeutic intervention

Recognising the emotional and neurobiological underpinnings of fibromyalgia should affect how clinicians approach the consultation. Professional objectives cannot be served by questioning patients’ legitimacy, but by helping them restore safety and agency in their own lives and bodies. The narrow and modest efficacy of pharmacological agents35—many of which modulate mood—underscores the need to complement them with interventions that address the emotional and relational dimensions of pain. Effective management of fibromyalgia should follow a personalised, stratified and targeted approach, combining different modalities as recommended in treatment guidelines,36 including exercise, comorbidity management, psychoeducation and behavioural modification. Within psychological interventions, mindfulness, acceptance-based therapies, compassion-focused approaches and Emotional Awareness and Expression Therapy have shown meaningful benefits across pain, affect and quality of life.37 38

This therapeutic effort can and should start at the clinical encounter itself, through a deliberate shift: from mutual frustration to understanding, from dismissal to validation and from fear, shame and guilt to safeness, connection and coregulation.39

Attachment-informed models propose that the clinician functions as a secure base recruiting evolutionarily conserved bonding systems, supporting affect regulation and enabling the exploratory behaviour necessary for change.40 Social safety theory extends this, proposing that safe, affiliative interpersonal cues regulate psychophysiological processes, including neuroimmune function, gut–brain axis activity and pain modulation, with preliminary empirical support but unestablished causal directionality.41 Converging perspectives from interpersonal neurobiology and predictive processing suggest that relational safety modulates autonomic and limbic threat processing, disinhibits higher-order regulatory functions and establishes conditions under which maladaptive expectations may be disconfirmed and/or modified.42 43 Collectively, these frameworks suggest a plausible mechanistic pathway: clinician warmth and safety engage regulatory systems, while threat or dismissal may amplify pathophysiology that sustains the condition. The patient–clinician interaction, often framed as an ethical or humanistic aspect of care, constitutes a biologically active intervention in itself, with a distinct neurobiological substrate, benefitting both patients and clinicians who engage meaningfully with it.44 Meta-analytic findings link empathic communication to small but consistent improvements in pain, anxiety and satisfaction,45 and highlight a moderate modality-independent association between therapeutic alliance and overall treatment outcome.46 In a recent study of chronic low back pain, a common and challenging condition within the spectrum of nociplastic pain disorders, perceived clinician empathy emerged as a dominant factor associated with sustained improvement in pain.47 Causal inference remains limited by the associative nature of this evidence, and the incremental contribution of the therapeutic relationship beyond specific treatment content has yet to be demonstrated.

Mechanistic insight into these effects comes most compellingly from placebo science. Contextual factors of the therapeutic encounter, such as the clinician’s empathy, warmth and perceived competence, have been shown to reduce pain, enhance well-being and diminish symptom burden through neurobiologically mediated pathways.48 Neuroimaging research demonstrates that empathic engagement with another person’s pain relief recruits neural networks associated with pain modulation.49 During clinical encounters, pain relief operates partly through bio-behavioural attunement, including nonverbal mimicry, physiological synchrony and interbrain concordance in regions supporting mentalising (being able to understand what we and others think and feel and how that shapes our behaviour) and emotional mirroring.50

While the mechanisms through which it operates are gradually coming into focus, their magnitude and durability in fibromyalgia remain to be established, limited by the absence of long-term follow-ups and comparisons with active interventions. A particularly promising step in this direction is the INTEGRO protocol, which explicitly investigates perceived therapeutic alliance and physiological attunement as candidate mediators of psychotherapeutic efficacy in fibromyalgia, offering the first empirical test of the relational–biological interface in this population.51

Implications for clinical practice

What does this mean for the clinician seeing patients with chronic pain? First, it means understanding and explicitly acknowledging that the patient’s pain is real and biologically grounded—not imagined, exaggerated or a manifestation of moral or mental weakness. Second, it means recognising that one’s own behaviour during the consultation—tone of voice, facial expressions, body language, the quality of attention—is not peripheral to treatment but constitutive of it. Third, it means that validation, empathy and compassionate communication are not merely desirable interpersonal qualities but therapeutic tools with neurobiological mechanisms of action.

Practical steps include taking a careful psychosocial history that acknowledges adversity without pathologising it; providing a clear, honest explanation of chronic pain mechanisms that normalises the patient’s experience; avoiding language that triggers shame or delegitimises symptoms; providing a safe haven for sharing and coregulation; and actively supporting the patient’s sense of agency and self-efficacy. Clinicians should be aware that their own emotional responses to these patients—frustration, helplessness or disbelief—are not irrelevant noise but signals that can either disrupt or inform the therapeutic process. Training in compassionate, client-centred communication and reflective practice may help clinicians navigate these encounters more effectively, benefiting both parties.

These principles apply not only to fibromyalgia but to the vast spectrum of chronic primary pain conditions—chronic low back pain, chronic pelvic pain and interstitial cystitis, irritable bowel syndrome, temporomandibular disorders and migraine—as well as to chronic fatigue syndrome and PAIS.9 The therapeutic encounter is among the most ubiquitous and least costly interventions available to medicine; the observations reviewed here suggest that it is also among the most potent, when conducted with awareness and skill. Realising this shift requires confronting individual and systemic barriers, including attitudinal biases, clinicians’ burnout and compassion fatigue, communication deficits, time-pressured consultations and fragmented care, and demands sustained investment in training, reflective practice and structural reform, underpinned by a cultural reorientation in how medicine engages with conditions that resist biomedical categorisation. Ultimately, patients with fibromyalgia embody not mystery but meaning—opportunities intertwined with vulnerability and resilience. For clinicians, these patients represent a demanding yet enriching challenge: an invitation to explore what is not obvious, to embrace the complexity of being human. In this process, empathy and compassion, anchored in science, become therapeutic for the patient and deeply restorative for the clinician.39 44
