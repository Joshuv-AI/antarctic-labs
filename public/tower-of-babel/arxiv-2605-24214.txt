Abstract
. Entropy functions played a key role in the development of mathematical theory for hyperbolic conservation laws. The notion of entropy, which is intimately connected with symmetry, is an extension imposed on nonlinear systems of conservation laws. In this context, Friedrichs raised the question whether the assumed symmetries can also be derived. We introduce a variational formulation that addresses Friedrichs’ question: an entropy function is derived as an extremal object, from which we deduce, rather than impose, the maximum entropy production principle of Dafermos.

1.2.
Symmetry and conservation

The entropy Hessian in (
1.6

A_{0}^{S}({\mathbf{u}}):=D^{2}\eta({\mathbf{u}})>0
, puts the system (
1.3
) into Friedrichs’ symmetric form,
[

A_{0}^{S}({\mathbf{u}}){\mathbf{u}}_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}A_{j}^{S}({\mathbf{u}}){\mathbf{u}}_{x_{j}}=0,\qquad A_{j}^{S}({\mathbf{u}}):=A_{0}^{S}({\mathbf{u}})A_{j}({\mathbf{u}}),\ \ j=1,\ldots,d.

The system is symmetric in the sense that
A_{j}^{S},j=0,1,\ldots,d
are symmetric, and therefore hyperbolic in the sense that

\sum_{j=0}^{d}A_{j}^{S}\omega_{j}
are diagonalizable with real eigenvalues. However, symmetrization comes at the expense of conservation: the symmetric
A_{j}^{S}({\mathbf{u}})
need not be Jacobian matrices which would enable to identify (
1.9
) as a system of conservation laws.

In his seminal 1961 paper entitled “An interesting class of quasilinear systems”
[

]
, Godunov identified a class of quasilinear equations
that can be written in a form that is both symmetric and in conservation form:

(1.10)

\big(\nabla L_{0}({\mathbf{v}})\big)_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\big(\nabla L_{j}({\mathbf{v}})\big)_{x_{j}}=0,

with a quasi-linear symmetric form expressed in terms of the corresponding Hessians

(1.11)

(D^{2}_{\mathbf{v}}L_{0}){\mathbf{v}}_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}(D^{2}_{\mathbf{v}}L_{j}){\mathbf{v}}_{x_{j}}=0.

Godunov showed that the equations of compressible gas dynamics as well
as other hyperbolic systems in mathematical physics admit the conservative form (
1.10
) for a proper choice of
L_{j}=L_{j}({\mathbf{v}})
and variables
{\mathbf{v}}
that were worked out in each case.

A final and decisive step, bridging the work of Godunov with that of Friedrichs and Lax, was taken in 1980 by Mock,
[

]

\eta({\mathbf{u}})

\psi_{0}({\mathbf{v}})

\psi_{j}({\mathbf{v}})

\begin{split}{\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{v}})&=\nabla\psi_{0}({\mathbf{v}}),\qquad\psi_{0}({\mathbf{v}}):=\langle{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{v}})\rangle-\eta({\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{v}}))\\

This yields the quasi-linear form of (
1.12

2.
A variational principle

We established the relation between symmetry and entropy in the context of nonlinear conservation laws: the existence of an entropy function amounts to the assumption of existence of a symmetrizer, (
1.6
), or “symmetrization on the right” in an entropy variables formulation (
1.12
). This assumption of symmetry/existence of an entropy is
imposed
on
N

N\geqslant 3
. We now reformulate the question of Friedrichs as follows:

Is it possible to derive the existence of entropy function(s) rather than merely assuming

their existence?

We aim to answer this question in terms of the following variational principle. It involves state vector functions,
{\mathbf{u}}(t,\cdot)\in{\mathscr{W}}\subset\text{BV}\cap L^{\infty}
, and a set of strictly convex functions acting on these states,
\eta\in{\mathscr{E}}\subset{\mathscr{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R})
, which we refer to as
observables
. The precise nature of the states admitted into
{\mathscr{W}}
, and the observables admitted into
{\mathscr{E}}
, will be explored below. At this stage we postulate that

{\mathscr{E}}
should be an affine space so that each
\eta\in{\mathscr{E}}
generates the
N
-parameter family
\eta_{\mathbf{c}}\in{\mathscr{E}}
,

\text{if}\ \ \eta\in{\mathscr{E}}\ \ \text{then}\ \ \eta_{\mathbf{c}}({\mathbf{u}}):=\eta({\mathbf{u}})+\langle{\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{u}}\rangle\in{\mathscr{E}}\quad\text{for all}\ \ {\mathbf{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Definition 2.1

(
Variational solutions).

We say that

is a

belongs to a non-empty set of observables

, and

\big\langle{\mathbf{b}}({\mathbf{u}}),{\mathbf{f}}_{j}({\mathbf{u}})_{x_{j}}\big\rangle_{\!\boldsymbol{\phi}}=\big[\big({\mathbf{b}}({\mathbf{u}})^{\top}A_{j}({\mathbf{u}})\big){\mathbf{u}}_{x_{j}}\big]_{\!\boldsymbol{\phi}}.

The DLM theory extends Volpert theory
[
Vol1967
]
for non-conservative products which is based on the straight path,
\boldsymbol{\phi}(s,{\mathbf{u}}{\scriptscriptstyle-},{\mathbf{u}}{\scriptscriptstyle+})=(1-s){\mathbf{u}}{\scriptscriptstyle-}+s{\mathbf{u}}{\scriptscriptstyle+}
.
Note that the variational statement takes into account a proper family of locally Lipschitz paths which impact the value of the non-conservative products.

2.1.
Weak solutions

A variational solution
\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}
should minimize
{\mathcal{I}}_{\eta_{{}_{\mathbf{c}}}}({\mathbf{u}})
. In particular, since
\nabla\eta_{\mathbf{c}}=\nabla\eta+{\mathbf{c}}
, we have

{\mathcal{I}}_{\eta}(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}})\leqslant{\mathcal{I}}_{\eta_{{}_{\mathbf{c}}}}(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}})={\mathcal{I}}_{\eta}(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}})+\int\limits_{t=t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int\limits_{\Omega}\Big\langle{\mathbf{c}},\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}{\mathbf{f}}_{j}(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}})_{x_{j}}\Big\rangle_{\!\boldsymbol{\phi}}\,{\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}}{{\textnormal{d}}}t,

hence

\int\limits_{t=t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int\limits_{\Omega}\Big\langle{\mathbf{c}},\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}{\mathbf{f}}_{j}(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}})_{x_{j}}\Big\rangle_{\!\boldsymbol{\phi}}\,{\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}}{{\textnormal{d}}}t\geqslant 0\ \ \text{for all}\ \ {\mathbf{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

In the present case of a Jacobian coefficient matrix, the DLM product coincides with the distributional derivative of the corresponding flux,

{\mathbf{f}}_{j}({\mathbf{u}})_{x_{j}}=\langle A_{j}({\mathbf{u}}),{\mathbf{u}}_{x_{j}}\rangle_{\!\boldsymbol{\phi}}
,
and is therefore independent of the path
\boldsymbol{\phi}
.
Since the integrand is conservative, its integral is path independent, and we conclude

\int\limits_{t=t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int\limits_{\Omega}\Big\{\overline{u}^{k}_{t}+\text{div}\,{\mathbf{f}}^{k}(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}})\Big\}{\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}}{{\textnormal{d}}}t=\int\limits_{\Omega}\overline{u}^{k}(t,\cdot){\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}}\Big|^{t=t_{2}}_{t=t_{1}}+\int\limits_{t=t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}{\mathbf{f}}^{k}(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}})\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\,{\textnormal{d}}S=0,\ \ k=1,\ldots,N.

Corollary 2.1
.

A variational solution is a weak solution of (
1.1
).

The “linearization” argument of
\eta_{\mathbf{c}}
for large
|{\mathbf{c}}|>\|\nabla\eta({\mathbf{u}})\|_{L^{\infty}}
which recovers
\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}
as a weak solution of the “underlying” conservation law, is reminiscent of the recovery of scalar weak solutions,
u
, by linearization of Kružkov’s entropies (
1.7
),
\eta_{c}=|u-c|
for large
c>\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}
.

It is clear that the closure of
{\mathscr{E}}
under linear perturbations,
\big\{\eta_{\mathbf{c}}:{\mathbf{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\big\}
, is in fact equivalent to having the class of variational solutions,
\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}
, include weak solutions of (
1.1
). In what follows, we shall need to consider perturbations of such weak solutions. Accordingly, we let the set of admissible states,
{\mathscr{W}}
, include arbitrarily small
\epsilon
-perturbations of weak solutions,

(2.3)

{\mathscr{W}}(t_{1},t_{2})=\left\{{\mathbf{u}}+\delta{\mathbf{u}}\ :\ {\mathbf{u}}\ \text{is a weak solution of \eqref{eq:conservative}};\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\vskip 2.84544pt\displaystyle\delta{\mathbf{u}}\in C_{0}^{\infty}\big((t_{1},t_{2}),\Omega\big)\\
\displaystyle\|\delta{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\infty}<\epsilon\end{array}\right\}\right\}.

The notion of a variational solution takes the following form.

Variational solutions revisited. ​​​I
.
(\eta,\overline{{\mathbf{u}}})
is a variational solution of (
1.1
) if
\eta
belongs to the non-empty set of observables
{\mathscr{E}}
, and
\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}
is a weak solution which is a minimizer of
{\mathcal{I}}_{\eta}({\mathbf{u}})
observed by

\eta
, such that for all perturbed
{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathscr{W}}
with arbitrarily small
\epsilon
,

(2.4)

{\mathcal{I}}_{\eta}\big(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}};(t_{1},t_{2})\big)\leqslant{\mathcal{I}}_{\eta}\big({\mathbf{u}};(t_{1},t_{2})\big)\ \textnormal{for all}\ \ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mathbf{u}}(t,\cdot)\in{\mathscr{W}}(t_{1},t_{2}),&t\in(t_{1},t_{2})\\
{\mathbf{u}}(t,\cdot)=\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}(t,\cdot),&t\leqslant t_{1}.\end{array}\right\}.

The variational formulation (
2.4
) is based on a “push forward” action functional
{\mathcal{I}}_{\eta}({\mathbf{u}})
. In particular, the admissible states,
{\mathbf{u}}(t,\cdot)
— and therefore the variational solution
\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}(t,\cdot)
— are anchored in the initial data,
{\mathbf{u}}(0,\cdot)={\mathbf{u}}_{0}
, prescribed at
t_{1}=0
. Thereafter, the states
{\mathbf{u}}(t,\cdot),\ t>t_{1}>0
are perturbations of weak solutions which evolve from the variational solution
\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}(t_{1},\cdot)
.

2.2.
Derivation of entropy functions

We compute the formal first variation in
{\mathcal{I}}_{\eta}({\mathbf{u}})
using smooth perturbations,
\|\delta{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\infty}<\epsilon
, compactly supported inside
(t_{1},t_{2})\times\Omega
,

\begin{split}{\mathcal{I}}_{\eta}&({\mathbf{u}}+\delta{\mathbf{u}})-{\mathcal{I}}_{\eta}({\mathbf{u}})\\
\approx&\int\limits_{t=t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int\limits_{\Omega}\left\{\Big\langle D^{2}\eta({\mathbf{u}})\delta{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{u}}_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}A_{j}({\mathbf{u}}){\mathbf{u}}_{x_{j}}\Big\rangle_{\!\boldsymbol{\phi}}+\Big\langle\nabla\eta({\mathbf{u}}),(\delta{\mathbf{u}})_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\big(A_{j}({\mathbf{u}})\delta{\mathbf{u}}\big)_{x_{j}}\Big\rangle_{\!\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\}\,{\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}}{{\textnormal{d}}}t.\end{split}

The approximation on the right takes into account first-order perturbations in
\delta{\mathbf{u}}
, and ignores terms of order
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
, given that that
{\mathbf{u}}
has bounded amplitude in
BV\cap L^{\infty}
.

The two terms on the right that involve time derivatives cancel each other

\begin{split}\int\limits_{t=t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int\limits_{\Omega}&\left\{\big\langle D^{2}\eta({\mathbf{u}})\delta{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{u}}_{t}\big\rangle_{\!\boldsymbol{\phi}}+\big\langle\nabla\eta({\mathbf{u}}),(\delta{\mathbf{u}})_{t}\big\rangle_{\!\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\}\,{\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}}{{\textnormal{d}}}t\\
&=\int\limits_{t=t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int\limits_{\Omega}\big\langle\nabla\eta({\mathbf{u}}),\delta{\mathbf{u}}\big\rangle_{t}\,{\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}}{{\textnormal{d}}}t=\int\limits_{\Omega}\big\langle\nabla\eta({\mathbf{u}}),\delta{\mathbf{u}}\big\rangle{\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}}\Big|_{t=t_{1}}^{t=t_{2}}=0.\end{split}

For the remaining spatial terms we have

(2.5)

\begin{split}&\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int\limits_{t=t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int\limits_{\Omega}\left\{\Big\langle D^{2}\eta({\mathbf{u}})\delta{\mathbf{u}},A_{j}({\mathbf{u}}){\mathbf{u}}_{x_{j}}\Big\rangle_{\!\boldsymbol{\phi}}+\Big\langle\nabla\eta({\mathbf{u}}),(A_{j}({\mathbf{u}})\delta{\mathbf{u}})_{x_{j}}\Big\rangle_{\!\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\}\,{\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}}{{\textnormal{d}}}t\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int\limits_{t=t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int\limits_{\Omega}\left\{\Big[(\delta{\mathbf{u}})^{\top}D^{2}\eta({\mathbf{u}})A_{j}({\mathbf{u}}){\mathbf{u}}_{x_{j}}\Big]_{\!\boldsymbol{\phi}}-\Big[(\delta{\mathbf{u}})^{\top}A^{\top}_{j}({\mathbf{u}})D^{2}\eta({\mathbf{u}}){\mathbf{u}}_{x_{j}}\Big]_{\!\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right\}\,{\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}}{{\textnormal{d}}}t.\end{split}

This follows from the following key feature of the DLM-Volpert theory: whenever a non-conservative product
\big[{\mathbf{b}}({\mathbf{u}})^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}_{x}\big]_{\!\boldsymbol{\phi}}
forms a “perfect derivative”, it coincides with the standard distributional derivative and is independent of the path
\boldsymbol{\phi}
,
[
DLM1995
, Proposition 1.5]
,
[
Vol1967
, §13.2]
; in particular,

\big\langle{\mathbf{b}}({\mathbf{u}})_{x},{\mathbf{u}}\big\rangle_{\!\boldsymbol{\phi}}+\big\langle{\mathbf{b}}({\mathbf{u}}),{\mathbf{u}}_{x}\big\rangle_{\!\boldsymbol{\phi}}=\Big[\Big({\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\frac{\partial{\mathbf{b}}({\mathbf{u}})}{\partial{\mathbf{u}}}+{\mathbf{b}}({\mathbf{u}})^{\top}\Big){\mathbf{u}}_{x}\Big]_{\!\boldsymbol{\phi}}=\big\langle{\mathbf{b}}({\mathbf{u}}),{\mathbf{u}}\big\rangle_{\!x},

is independent of the path
\boldsymbol{\phi}
, which justifies the “integration by parts” on the second integrand in (
2.5
).

Since we may choose arbitrary smooth, compactly supported perturbations
\delta{\mathbf{u}}
, the requirement that
(\delta_{\mathbf{u}}{\mathcal{I}}_{\eta})(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}})=0
implies

(2.6)

\big[D^{2}\eta(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}})A_{j}(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}})\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}_{x_{j}}\big]_{\!\boldsymbol{\phi}}=\big[A^{\top}_{j}(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}})D^{2}\eta(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}})\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}_{x_{j}}\big]_{\!\boldsymbol{\phi}},

and since this is intended to hold independently of the choice of path, we are led to

D^{2}\eta(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}})A_{j}(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}})=A^{\top}_{j}(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}})D^{2}\eta(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}),\quad j=1,\ldots,d.

This is precisely the symmetrizability condition (
1.6
) restricted to the variational solution
\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}
. At the formal level, if the variational principle is required to hold for a sufficiently rich class of weak solutions, then the stationarity condition forces the pointwise symmetrization relation (
1.6
).

In this manner the notion of an entropy function is deduced from the variational principle (
2.1
) or (
2.4
):
\eta\in{\mathscr{E}}
is an admissible observable if its Hessian symmetrizes the Jacobians, (
1.6
), that is, if
\eta
is an entropy function.
This answers Friedrichs’ question in the sense that the notion of entropy is derived as a stationarity condition for the variational functional
{\mathcal{I}}_{\eta}
.

Remark 2.2

(
Entropic vs. non-entropic systems)
.

The notion of variational solution does not treat the set of observable as an “extension” which augments the hyperbolic system of conservation laws, but as an intrinsic part of the solution. Since observables coincide with entropy functions, the set of observables
{\mathscr{E}}
is restricted. Therefore, we make a distinction between two classes of systems of equations:

Entropic systems which admit non-empty set
{\mathscr{E}}
— this is the “interesting” class of conservation laws of Godunov
which admit (at least one) entropy function. This is the class of systems discussed in this paper.
In case there is more than one entropy function, we can fix one preferred
\eta\in{\mathscr{E}}
and we refer to
\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}
as a variational solution observed by that
\eta
. At the same time, we can discuss a variational solution observed by all
\eta\in{\mathscr{E}}
.
Then there is the other class of systems of conservation laws, at least when
N\geqslant 3
, which admit no entropy extension, i.e., where
{\mathscr{E}}
is empty, as in (
1.8
). This class of “non-entropic” systems do not admit variational solutions. The study of variational solutions for such systems requires a different setting.

We close by noting the following duality. The closure of the set of observables with respect affine perturbations,
\eta_{\mathbf{c}}\in{\mathscr{E}}
, enforces variational solutions to be weak solutions,
\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}\in{\mathscr{W}}
, while letting the set of admissible states
{\mathscr{W}}
include local perturbations of weak solutions,
{\mathbf{u}}+\delta{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathscr{W}}
, enforces the observables to be entropy
functions,
\eta\in{\mathscr{E}}
. In this context, the class of perturbations considered in (
2.3
), which consists of all

\{\delta{\mathbf{u}}\}
in the
L^{\infty}

\epsilon
-ball, is a “rich” class.
It should possible to consider a restricted class of perturbations and consequently, to further restrict the set of perturbed weak solutions
{\mathscr{W}}(t_{1},t_{2})
in (
2.3
). A key question of interest is
to identify a minimal set of perturbed weak solutions which will enforce (
2.6
)
.

3.
The entropy inequality

Fix

Variational solutions revisited. ​​​II

Note that (

\int\limits_{\Omega}\eta\big(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})\big){\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}}\Big|^{t=t_{2}}_{t=t_{1}}+\int\limits_{t=t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}{\mathbf{q}}^{\eta}\big(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})\big)\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\,{\textnormal{d}}S\,{{\textnormal{d}}}t\leqslant 0.

4.
Maximum entropy production

The possible lack of uniqueness in the entropy-based selection principle based on a preferred single entropy inequality (
1.5
), motivated Dafermos as early as 1973,
[
Daf1973
]
, to formulate an
entropy rate admissibility criterion
. Given an strictly convex entropy
\eta
, it seeks a solution
\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}
which maximizes the entropy dissipation
rate
among all other weak solutions

(4.1)

\frac{{\textnormal{d}}_{+}}{{{\textnormal{d}}}t}\int\eta(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})){\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}}\leqslant\frac{{\textnormal{d}}_{+}}{{{\textnormal{d}}}t}\int\eta({\mathbf{u}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})){\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}}\ :\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathbf{u}}\ \text{is a weak solution}\\
{\mathbf{u}}(\tau,\cdot)=\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tau,\cdot),\ \ \tau\leqslant t.\end{array}\right.

That is, there is no weak solution
{\mathbf{u}}(\tau,\cdot)
which coincides with
\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tau,\cdot)
for
\tau<t
, and for which the
reverse
of (
4.1
) holds.

This can be interpreted in the general context of maximization
of the entropy production, the so-called MaxEnt principle, that was developed in different contexts during the second half of the 20th century, with contributions (and sometimes controversies) from I. Prigogine (1947), J. M. Ziman (1956),
E. T. Jaynes (1957), H. Ziegler (1963), Dewar [2007] and many others listed in, e.g.,
[
MS2006
]
6
6

6

As noted in
[
GLC2020
]
, Dafermos’ MaxEnt criterion relates to Ziegler’s principle for closed thermodynamic systems rather than Prigogine’s MinEnt principle principle which applies to open thermodynamic systems
.
Dafermos proved that, in the context of hyperbolic conservation laws, his MaxEnt criterion singles out the unique entropy solution in scalar equations and 1D
p
-system
[
Daf1973
]
and selects the admissible solution of 1D Riemann problem, at least with small initial variation
[
Sev1990
,
Daf2009
]
,
and it was utilized in a host of different applications, e.g.,
[
HHW2020
,
Kle2023
]
.

4.1.
A local version of MaxEnt

We appeal to the variational formulation (
3.2
).
Given that the observable
\eta
is an entropy function, (
3.2
) identifies
\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}
as a variational solution which maximizes the local entropy production
rate
, in the sense that
\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}
is a minimizer among all weak solutions,
{\mathbf{u}}
,

(4.2)

\begin{split}\frac{{\textnormal{d}}_{+}}{{{\textnormal{d}}}t}\int\limits_{\Omega}&\eta(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})){\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}}+\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}{\mathbf{q}}^{\eta}(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}(t,{\mathbf{x}}))\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\,{\textnormal{d}}S\\
&\leqslant\frac{{\textnormal{d}}_{+}}{{{\textnormal{d}}}t}\int\limits_{\Omega}\eta({\mathbf{u}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})){\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}}+\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}{\mathbf{q}}^{\eta}({\mathbf{u}}(t,{\mathbf{x}}))\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\,{\textnormal{d}}S\ :\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathbf{u}}\ \text{is a weak solution}\\
{\mathbf{u}}(\tau,\cdot)=\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tau,\cdot),\ \ \tau\leqslant t.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

This is a

version of Dafermos’ MaxEnt criterion (
4.1
), localized to arbitrary
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It is not assumed, but is deduced directly from the variational principle: dividing (
3.2
) by
t_{2}-t_{1}
and letting
t_{2}\rightarrow t_{1}=t
while noting that

vanishes as well.

At this stage we can summarize the three main consequences of our variational principle outlined in Definition
2.1
, namely —
variational solutions are weak solution;
stationary observable must be an entropy function; and
if
\eta
is an entropy function, the variational principle implies local MaxEnt principle.

5.
In search of a uniqueness selection principle

5.1.
The entropy inequality as a selection principle

There is a large body of recent work that addressed the (non-)uniqueness question of entropy solutions. The canonical system addressed in this large body of work is the isentropic Euler equations, which consists of
N=d+1
equations for
(\rho,\rho\hskip 0.56917pt{\mathsf{v}})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(5.1)

{\mathbf{u}}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\
\rho\hskip 0.56917pt{\mathsf{v}}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\mathbf{f}}_{j}({\mathbf{u}})=\begin{bmatrix}\rho\hskip 0.56917pt{\mathsf{v}}_{j}\\
\displaystyle\rho\hskip 0.56917pt{\mathsf{v}}_{j}{\mathsf{v}}+p\delta_{ij}\vskip 2.84544pt\end{bmatrix},\ \ j=1,\ldots,d,

with pressure
p=p(\rho)
. The
\gamma
-law,
p(\rho)=\rho^{\gamma}
, corresponds to Euler system (
1.15
) with constant specific entropy
S
. Here, the energy

\displaystyle\eta(\rho,\rho\hskip 0.56917pt{\mathsf{v}}):=\frac{1}{2}\rho|{\mathsf{v}}|^{2}+\rho\int^{\rho}\frac{p(r)}{r^{2}}{\textnormal{d}}r
is serving as the entropy function.

The relative entropy-based uniqueness within the class of strong
rarefaction-based solutions was proved in
[
CC2007
,
FK2015
]
.
Following the initial breakthrough of De Lellis and Székelyhidi in

[
DS2010
]
which used convex integration to construct non-unique “wild” solutions for bounded data, a series of results of non-uniqueness with improved
regularity followed with regular density
[
Chi2014
]
, compactly supported data
[
AW2021
]
, 2D Lipschitz and smooth initial data
[
CDK2015
,
CKMS2021
]
, and even non-unique entropy conservative Hölder solutions
[
GK2022
]
. General non-uniqueness for shock-based solutions was proved in
[
CK2014
,
CK2018
,
MK2018
]
and was extended to the full Euler equations in
[
KKMM2020
]
.
These works make clear that the selection principle based on a single entropy inequality to single out a unique weak solution from among solutions for the multidimensional isentropic equations (
5.1
) does not suffice for uniqueness.

Lax commented on this situation in his Gibbs lecture
[
Lax2007
]
“
Just because we cannot prove that compressible
flows with prescribed initial values exist doesn’t mean that we cannot compute them.
”
Numerical evidence for non-uniqueness of entropic solutions of Euler equations
was further investigated in
[
FMT2016
]
.

Example 5.1

(

.

Appendix A
Conservation laws with homogeneous fluxes

The compressible Euler equations (
1.15
) have the distinctive property of spatial fluxes which are homogeneous,
{{\mathbf{f}}}^{\scalebox{0.8}{\text{\tiny Euler}}}_{j}(\lambda{\mathbf{u}})=\lambda^{\beta}{{\mathbf{f}}}^{\scalebox{0.8}{\text{\tiny Euler}}}_{j}({\mathbf{u}})
of degree
\beta=1
.
The purpose of this short appendix is to note that
the homogeneous structure of Euler equations can be put in a
symmetric form
,
[
Tad1984
]
. We think that it could be further utilized in the variational formulation of Euler equations.

To this end we use the class of convex entropy functions (corresponding to
\displaystyle h(S)=e^{S/(\alpha+\gamma)}
)

(A.1)

\eta({{\mathbf{u}}}^{\scalebox{0.8}{\text{\tiny Euler}}})=-(p\rho^{\alpha})^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\gamma}},\qquad\alpha\geqslant 0

which are homogeneous of degree
\displaystyle\beta_{\alpha}=\frac{\alpha+1}{\alpha+\gamma}
. Expressed in terms of the entropy variables,
{\mathbf{v}}=\nabla\eta({{\mathbf{u}}}^{\scalebox{0.8}{\text{\tiny Euler}}})
, the Euler equations admit the symmetric form

{{\mathbf{u}}}^{\scalebox{0.8}{\text{\tiny Euler}}}({\mathbf{v}})_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}{{\mathbf{f}}}^{\scalebox{0.8}{\text{\tiny Euler}}}_{j}({\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{v}}))_{x_{j}}=\big(A_{0}^{S}\big)^{-1}{\mathbf{v}}_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}A_{j}^{S}{\mathbf{v}}_{x_{j}}=0,

with symmetric Jacobians, (
1.14
),
\displaystyle A_{j}^{S},\ j=0,1,\ldots,d
.
The temporal and spatial fluxes,
{{\mathbf{u}}}^{\scalebox{0.8}{\text{\tiny Euler}}}({\mathbf{v}})
and
{{\mathbf{f}}}^{\scalebox{0.8}{\text{\tiny Euler}}}_{j}({\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{v}}))
, are homogeneous of degree

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{\alpha}-1}=\frac{\alpha+\gamma}{1-\gamma}<0
.
Since the fluxes are homogeneous of degree
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{\alpha}-1}
, Euler’s identity enables us to rewrite (
A.1
) in the symmetric conservative form

\big(\big(A_{0}^{S}\big)^{-1}{\mathbf{v}}\big)_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\big(A_{j}^{S}{\mathbf{v}}\big)_{x_{j}}=0,

The homogeneity of Euler equations implies that
if
{\mathbf{v}}(t,\cdot)
is an entropy variable solution of Euler equations then any scalar multiple of
{\mathbf{v}}
is also a solution of Euler equations.
What other systems share this property? the system of MHD equations, a natural candidate, does not share this property because of the dependence of its fluxes on dimensional parameter of magnetic permeability
\mu^{*}
,
[
Tad1984
, §5]
. The other system is ultra-relativistic Euler equations which express the conservation of momentum
{\mathbf{m}}=p{\mathsf{v}}
and energy
E=3p+p|{\mathsf{v}}|^{2}
, e.g.,
[
Kun2005
,
KKMR2024
]

\begin{split}(4m_{j}\sqrt{1+|{\mathsf{v}}|^{2}})_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\big(4m_{k}{\mathsf{v}}_{j}+p\delta_{jk}\big)_{x_{j}}&=0\\
E_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\big(4m_{k}\sqrt{1+|{\mathsf{v}}|^{2}}\big)_{x_{j}}&=0\end{split}

and we observe that both, the temporal and spatial fluxes are homogeneous of degree 1 in
(p,{\mathbf{m}})
(since
{\mathsf{v}}
is homogeneous of degree 0).
