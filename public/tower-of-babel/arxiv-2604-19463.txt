On combining estimated and analytic covariance matrices
Alan Heavens, Lorne Whiteway, Elena Sellentin

Abstract
The statistical analysis of cosmological data often assumes a Gaussian sampling distribution and relies on covariance matrices estimated from simulations. In this setting, the likelihood function of the data is not Gaussian but is instead a multivariate Student- t t distribution, arising from marginalisation over an inverse-Wishart distribution for the true covariance matrix. This framework, introduced by 27 and extended by 22 , provides a principled drop-in replacement to the Gaussian likelihood with Hartlap correction [ 8 ] . The latter removes bias in the precision matrix; it is still widely used, despite failing to reproduce the heavy tails of the true distribution (thus yielding inaccurate probabilities, especially in the case of tensions between datasets). In practice, cosmological analyses frequently involve additional Gaussian error contributions, for example from instrumental noise, foregrounds, super-sample covariance, or emulator uncertainties. The resulting likelihood function is a convolution of the Sellentin-Heavens or Percival et al. likelihoods with an extra Gaussian contribution, and does not have a simple expression. In this note, we derive an accurate approximation for the combined likelihood function, another multivariate Student- t t distribution which inherits the heavy tails. The parameters of the Student- t t distribution are determined by matching the covariance and multivariate kurtosis to those of the true distribution. We identify many application areas where this may be useful, and derive here simple-to-apply practical formulae for the effective degrees of freedom and scale matrix of the Student- t t likelihood function when some element of the covariance matrix is estimated from simulations. We also include a slightly more expensive but fast sampling algorithm, based on the mixture representation of the Student- t t distribution, which avoids the approximation altogether, but is not the drop-in replacement for the normal Gaussian or Hartlap likelihood function that the Student- t t approximation in this paper provides.



1
Introduction

The analysis of cosmological data has traditionally involved the construction of informative summary statistics, such as estimates of the clustering correlation function or power spectrum. It is then common to assume a Gaussian sampling distribution (or likelihood function). While there are more sophisticated techniques, such as field-level inference
[
12
,
11
,
23
, e.g.]
or simulation-based inference
[
1
,
19
,
13
, e.g.]
, summary statistics with assumed Gaussian distributions remain the typical choice for the first analysis of new datasets. Often the assumptions are not questioned, despite the highly non-Gaussian nature of present-day cosmological fields, and the Gaussian likelihood approximation may be poor, even in cases where the central limit theorem might suggest otherwise
[
28
, e.g.]
.

Accepting the Gaussian premise also involves further challenges, notably through the difficulty of providing a covariance matrix (a four-point function). Often the only way this can be done is via estimation from a large number of simulations. It is the inverse of the covariance matrix that appears in the Gaussian likelihood function, and it is well-known that the inverse of the estimated covariance matrix is biased. An approach widely used in cosmology has been to correct for this bias using the Hartlap factor
[
8
]
. This method is straightforward. It was adopted in many early survey pipelines, and it continues to be widely used, despite not giving the correct likelihood, in particular being unable to capture the heavy tails induced by covariance uncertainty.
In contrast, marginalising analytically over the inverse-Wishart posterior for the covariance leads exactly to a multivariate Student-
t
likelihood
[
27
]
; this can be adapted to a frequency-matching prior to give good frequentist coverage
[
22
]
.

The aim of this work is to generalise this Student-
t
framework to the following fairly common setting: one element of variability has a covariance that is estimated from simulations, and there is another source of Gaussian variability that has an analytic or otherwise theoretically computable covariance. This setting may arise for example in cosmic shear studies, where an analytic super-sample covariance
[
18
,
26
, e.g.]
is added to a within-sample covariance estimated from simulations. The situation can also arise in Bayesian hierarchical models (BHMs), which are increasingly applied in cosmology to capture the propagation of uncertainties across multiple levels of data modelling. Marginalisation over uncertain covariances within one level of a BHM naturally gives rise to a multivariate Student-
t
contribution to the likelihood.

In addition, extra Gaussian components commonly enter the hierarchy, for example from shape noise
[
5
,
32
, e.g.]
or from emulator uncertainties. The convolution of Student-
t
and Gaussian distributions therefore emerges naturally within BHMs, but it lacks a simple closed-form expression. In super-sample covariance, fluctuations of background modes larger than the survey window modulate small-scale power, producing a coherent covariance component that can be computed analytically or semi-analytically via response functions and the survey window
[
31
,
17
]
. Such a combination is common in weak lensing and galaxy clustering studies
[
4
,
14
, e.g.]
. In another application area, the computational expense of running cosmological simulations has spurred the adoption of emulators using Gaussian processes or neural networks. Uncertainty in the emulator predictions contribute an additional, analytic prediction variance that depends on training design and kernel hyperparameters
[
9
,
25
,
22
, e.g.]
. In the Cosmic Microwave Background at low multipoles the distribution of estimated angular power spectra is non-Gaussian, often modelled by Wishart or Student-
t
forms
[
7
]
. At the same time, instrumental noise is commonly assumed to be Gaussian.

Given the prevalence in cosmology of the combination of Student-
t
and Gaussian distributions, it is useful to have an accurate approximation for the resulting likelihood function, retaining the heavy-tailed behaviour while keeping the likelihood analytically tractable. In classical statistics, the convolution of independent distributions and the matching of moments is a well-studied problem
[
15
]
; such an approach can be applied to this problem, so that we approximate the convolution by another Student-
t
distribution, with modified parameters set by matching the covariance as well as the multivariate kurtosis of
20
. We provide here compact expressions for the resultant likelihood function. The approximation is inevitably imperfect (Appendix
A
).

As an alternative, we can define an efficient but approximation-free sampler using the mixture representation of the Student-
t
distribution in the convolution. We use this both to demonstrate the accuracy of the Student-
t
approximation, and as a standalone algorithm for sampling the exact distribution.

2
Covariance Marginalisation

Before we consider the combination of Student-
t
and Gaussian-distributed variable, we review briefly the derivations of the Student-
t
distributions that arise from uncertainty in the true covariance matrix
\bm{C}
, when only an estimate from simulations is known.

Let us assume that we have a data vector drawn from a Gaussian distribution of length
p
and mean
{\bm{\mu}}
:
\bm{X}\sim{\cal N}({\bm{\mu}},\bm{C})
. An estimate of the covariance matrix
\hat{\bm{C}}
is obtained from
N_{\rm sim}
simulations:

\hat{\bm{C}}\equiv\bm{S}=\frac{1}{N_{\rm sim}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm sim}}(\bm{X}_{i}-\bar{\bm{X}}_{i})(\bm{X}_{i}-\bar{\bm{X}}_{i})^{\top},

(1)

where
\bar{\bm{X}}
is the sample mean.
The sample covariance matrix follows a Wishart distribution
[
2
]
:

(N_{\rm sim}-1){\bm{S}}\sim\mathcal{W}_{p}(\bm{C},\,N_{\rm sim}-1).

(2)

Given
\bm{S}
, the true covariance matrix
\bm{C}
is uncertain, so to construct the likelihood for
\bm{X}
we marginalise over it:

P(\bm{X}|\bm{S})\propto\int\,P(\bm{X}|\bm{C})\,P(\bm{S}|\bm{C})\,P(\bm{C})\,d\bm{C}.

(3)

27
chose to marginalise
\bm{C}
with a Jeffreys prior
[
33
]

P(\bm{C})\propto|\bm{C}|^{-(p+1)/2}
for the true covariance, deriving the exact likelihood for the
p
-dimensional data
\bm{X}
conditioned on
\bm{S}
:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SH}}(\bm{X}|{\bm{\theta}},\bm{S})\propto|\bm{S}|^{-1/2}\left(1+\frac{\chi_{S}^{2}}{N_{\mathrm{sim}}-1}\right)^{-\frac{N_{\mathrm{sim}}}{2}};\quad\chi_{S}^{2}=(\bm{X}-{\bm{\mu}})^{\top}\bm{S}^{-1}(\bm{X}-{\bm{\mu}}),

(4)

where
{\bm{\theta}}
are the model parameters, which are usually to be inferred.

22
modified this by introducing a frequency-matching prior,
P(\bm{C})\propto|\bm{C}|^{-(m-N_{\mathrm{sim}}+p+1)/2}
, which then modifies the likelihood of the data to

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Percival}}(\bm{X}|{\bm{\theta}},\bm{S})\propto|\bm{S}|^{-1/2}\left(1+\frac{\chi_{S}^{2}}{N_{\mathrm{sim}}-1}\right)^{-m/2},

(5)

with

m=2+N_{\bm{\theta}}+\frac{N_{\mathrm{sim}}-1+B(p-N_{\bm{\theta}})}{1+B(p-N_{\bm{\theta}})},\quad B=\frac{N_{\mathrm{sim}}-p-2}{(N_{\mathrm{sim}}-p-1)(N_{\mathrm{sim}}-p-4)},

(6)

and where
N_{\bm{\theta}}
is the number of parameters in the problem.
This distribution allows the posteriors to be interpreted as frequentist confidence intervals. It reduces to the Sellentin-Heavens likelihood in the large-
N_{\mathrm{sim}}
limit.

2.1
Sellentin-Heavens and Percival et al. likelihoods as Student-
t
distributions

The Student-
t
distribution is defined, for
p
-dimensional data
\bm{X}
with
\nu
degrees of freedom, location vector
\bm{\mu}
, and
scale matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
, by

\bm{X}\sim t_{\nu}({\bm{\mu}},\bm{\Sigma})\;\propto\;|\bm{\Sigma}|^{-1/2}\left[1+\frac{1}{\nu}(\bm{X}-\bm{\mu})^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{X}-\bm{\mu})\right]^{-(\nu+p)/2}.

(7)

The likelihood functions (
4
and
5
), although not obviously Student-
t
distributions, can nevertheless be put in this form. The
27
likelihood (
4
) has parameters:

\nu_{\mathrm{S}H}=N_{\rm sim}-p;\quad\bm{\Sigma}_{{\mathrm{S}H}}=\frac{N_{\rm sim}-1}{N_{\rm sim}-p}\,\bm{S},

(8)

while the frequency-matched
22
likelihood (
5
) has parameters:

\nu_{\mathrm{P}}=m-p;\quad\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{P}}=\frac{N_{\rm sim}-1}{m-p}\,\bm{S}.

(9)

The requirement
\nu=m-p>4
(for the kurtosis to be well-defined) requires more (typically
\sim 2p
) simulations than are needed simply for
\bm{S}
to be invertible. If
m<p+4
then the function exists and is everywhere positive, but it is not a Student-
t
distribution, as this would require
\nu>1
. The function is not a proper sampling distribution in this case, as its integral over the data space diverges and so it cannot be normalised. One might be tempted to use it as an improper pseudo-likelihood distribution; however, the approximation in this paper cannot be used, since it relies on matching the covariance and kurtosis, both of which diverge. Whether another approximation is possible, perhaps based on matching curvature at the peak, is not something that we have pursued.

3
Approximate distribution of the sum of Student-
t
and Gaussian distributed variables

Let us consider the situation in which simulations are used to estimate one component of variability, but another non-simulated additive component (such as super-sample covariance, from implicit separation of scales
[
31
]
or measurement error) needs to be included. We assume all elements have underlying Gaussian distributions.

Consider a Student-
t
distributed variable
\mathbf{X}
with degrees of freedom
\nu
and scale matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
:
\mathbf{\bm{X}}\sim t_{\nu}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma})
, and an independent Gaussian-distributed variable
\mathbf{Y}
with covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{g}
:
\mathbf{Y}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{g})
. For simplicity we assume both distributions have zero mean.

The distribution of
\mathbf{Z}\equiv\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{Y}
is a convolution with no simple expression, but with known moments. It inherits the heavy tails of the Student-
t
distribution, motivating its approximation by another Student-
t
distribution with different degrees of freedom
\nu_{\star}
and scale matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}
, i.e. we approximate

\mathbf{Z}\;\dot{\sim}\;t_{\nu_{\star}}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}).

(10)

The goal is to find
\nu_{*}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}
; we do this by matching the covariance and multivariate kurtosis to those of the true distribution.

The covariance of the original Student-
t
distribution is
\nu\bm{\Sigma}/(\nu-2)
for
\nu>2
, and, since
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
are independent, the total covariance is the sum

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{Z}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{g}+\left(\frac{\nu}{\nu-2}\right)\mathbf{\Sigma}.

(11)

In addition to matching the covariance, we equate the
20
multivariate kurtosis of
\bm{Z}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{Z}^{-1}\mathbf{Z})^{2}\right]
, to the Mardia kurtosis of a Student-
t
distribution with degrees of freedom
\nu_{\star}
,

{\rm Kurt(t_{\nu_{\star}})}=\frac{p(p+2)(\nu_{\star}-2)}{\nu_{\star}-4}.

(12)

The full derivation is in appendix
B
, and we simply quote the results here:

\nu_{\star}=4+\frac{2\,p(p+2)}{\kappa};\qquad\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}=\left(\frac{\nu_{*}-2}{\nu_{*}}\right)\mathbf{\Sigma}_{Z},

(13)

where

\kappa=\frac{2\nu^{2}}{(\nu-2)^{2}(\nu-4)}\left[(\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{N})^{2}+2\,\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{N}^{2})\right];\quad\mathbf{N}=\bm{\Sigma}_{Z}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}.

(14)

The key quantities
\nu_{\star}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}
(
13
) that define the approximate distribution can be calculated for the addition of Gaussian noise to the Student-
t
distributions of
27
or
22
by appropriate insertion of
\nu=\nu_{SH}
or
\nu_{P}
and scale matrix
\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{\Sigma}_{SH}
or
\bm{\Sigma}_{P}
from (
8
) or (
9
). For the new Student-
t
distribution to be proper, the minimum number of simulations is increased compared to the case of no additional Gaussian component. This reduces the differences with a Gaussian approximation, but nevertheless is more accurate, especially in the tails; this can be important in the presence of tensions, when tail probabilities can be very different.

6
Conclusions

We have presented approximate but accurate solutions for the convolution of Gaussian and Student-
t
distributions, motivated by problems in cosmological data analysis where the covariance matrix of one element of fluctuations is estimated from simulations, and is then combined with another Gaussian source of variability, such as super-sample covariance, shape noise in weak lensing, or measurement error. In one and many dimensions, we showed how to construct an effective multivariate Student-
t
distribution by matching covariance and kurtosis, following the framework of multivariate kurtosis introduced by
20
and subsequent treatments of multivariate
t
-distributions
[
15
, e.g.]
. This approximation retains heavy tails and analytic tractability, while accommodating additional Gaussian contributions such as instrumental noise, foreground residuals, or emulator errors.

Our results generalise the Student-
t
framework introduced by
27
and extended by
22
, which arise naturally from marginalising over covariance uncertainty. In contrast, the widely used Gaussian likelihood with Hartlap correction
[
8
]
only corrects the bias of the inverse covariance in expectation, while leaving the likelihood Gaussian, and in particular underestimating the sampling distribution in the tails. The approximation developed here provides a simple and principled solution that preserves the heavy-tailed behaviour expected from finite-simulation covariance estimation, and remains valid when further Gaussian noise contributions are present. The approximation can also be used in Bayesian hierarchical models (BHMs), where uncertainties from multiple levels of the hierarchy propagate into the effective likelihood, and the method can combine levels of the hierarchy for efficiency if needed in a statistically principled way.

From a practical perspective, these effective Student-
t
likelihoods are straightforward to implement in cosmological inference pipelines, requiring only adjusted degrees of freedom and scale matrix - it is basically a simple fix to existing Gaussian or Student-
t
codes. Specifically, the (natural) log likelihood is given simply by

\log{P(\bm{X}|\bm{S},\bm{\Sigma}_{g})}={\rm const}-\frac{(\nu_{\star}+p)}{2}\log\left[1+\frac{1}{\nu_{\star}}{(\bm{X}-{\bm{\mu}})^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}^{-1}(\bm{X}-{\bm{\mu}})}\right]

(17)

where
\nu_{\star}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}
are given by eq. (
13
), using eq. (
14
), which is obtained from the frequency-matching prior values of
\nu=m-p
and
\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{\Sigma}_{P}
given in eq. (
9
). A Python implementation is provided in Appendix
D
.

As an alternative, an efficient sampling of the exact convolution can be done using the mixture representation as shown in section
5.1
.

Appendix A
Non-elliptical distribution

We show that a Student-
t
approximation to the convolution of a Student-
t
and a Gaussian is necessarily inexact. Recall that elliptical distributions are precisely those for which the characteristic function has the form

\phi(\mathbf{t})\equiv\mathbb{E}[\exp(i\mathbf{t}\bm{X})]=\exp(i\mathbf{t}^{\top}\bm{\mu})\,\psi\!\left(\mathbf{t}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{t}\right)

(A.1)

for some vector
\bm{\mu}
, nonnegative-definite
\bm{\Sigma}
, and scalar
characteristic generator function
\psi
. The Student-
t
and Gaussian distributions are both elliptic: for the Student-
t
distribution,
\bm{\mu}
and
\bm{\Sigma}
are the mean and the shape matrix, and the characteristic generator is

\psi_{\textrm{ST}}(u)\propto u^{\nu/4}K_{\nu/2}(\sqrt{\nu u})

(A.2)

(where
K
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind), while for the Gaussian distribution,
\bm{\mu}
and
\bm{\Sigma}
are the mean and the covariance matrix, and the characteristic generator is

\psi_{\textrm{G}}(u)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}u\right)\,.

(A.3)

Recall also that the characteristic function of the sum of two independent random variables is the product of the individual characteristic functions.

In what follows we assume that all distributions have zero mean; the extension to non-zero means is straightforward. Let
\bm{X}\sim t_{\nu}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\bm{\Sigma}})
be a Student-
t
distribution and
\bm{Y}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{g})
an independent Gaussian. Assume that
\bm{Z}=\bm{X}+\bm{Y}
is Student-
t
say
\bm{Z}\sim t_{\nu}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{\star})
. Taking the natural log of the characteristic generators and rearranging yields

-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{t}^{\top}\Sigma_{g}\mathbf{t}=\left[\frac{\nu}{4}\log(\mathbf{t}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}\mathbf{t})+\log(K_{\nu/2}(\sqrt{\nu\mathbf{t}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}\mathbf{t}}))\right]-\left[\frac{\nu}{4}\log(\mathbf{t}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{t})+\log(K_{\nu/2}(\sqrt{\nu\mathbf{t}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{t}}))\right]+\textrm{const}\,.

(A.4)

But here the LHS is a polynomial in
\mathbf{t}
while the RHS is not. This contradiction shows that
\bm{Z}
cannot be Student-
t
, and so our approximation must be inexact.

We can say more; we show that if
\bm{\Sigma}_{g}
and
\bm{\Sigma}
are not scalar multiples of each other then
\bm{Z}
cannot be
any
elliptical distribution i.e. it cannot have elliptical contours. If
\bm{Z}
is elliptical, then the natural log of its characteristic generator
\psi_{\star}
will satisfy

\log(\psi_{\star}(\mathbf{t}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}\mathbf{t}))=\frac{\nu}{4}\log(\mathbf{t}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{t})+\log(K_{\nu/2}(\sqrt{\nu\mathbf{t}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{t}}))-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{t}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{g}\mathbf{t}+\textrm{const}

(A.5)

for some
\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}
(which must be positive definite because
\bm{\Sigma}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{g}
are). Let
E
be the set of
\mathbf{t}
for which the quadratic form
\mathbf{t}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}\mathbf{t}
is unity. Fix
\mathbf{t}\in E
, and evaluate eq. (
A.5
) at
\sqrt{u}\mathbf{t}
; we obtain

\log(\psi_{\star}(u))=\frac{\nu}{4}\log(u\,\mathbf{t}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{t})+\log(K_{\nu/2}(\sqrt{\nu\,u\,\mathbf{t}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{t}}))-\frac{1}{2}\,u\,\mathbf{t}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{g}\mathbf{t}+\textrm{const}\,.

(A.6)

This fixes the definition of
\psi_{\star}
. The LHS is independent of
\mathbf{t}
, so the RHS must be as well. However on the RHS there can be no cancellation between the constant, linear, log, and log Bessel terms. Thus the quadratic forms
\mathbf{t}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{t}
and
\mathbf{t}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{g}\mathbf{t}
must both be constant on
E
. This can only happen if
\bm{\Sigma}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{g}
are scalar multiples of
\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}
and hence of each other; in this latter case while eq. (
A.6
) gives the characteristic function of
\bm{Z}
, the corresponding probability density function will not have a simple closed form.

Appendix B
Covariance and kurtosis matching for the Gaussian-Student-
t
convolution

We derive the parameters of that multivariate
Student-
t
distribution that matches both the covariance and
the Mardia’s multivariate kurtosis of the convolution of a Student-
t
with a Gaussian.

Let
\bm{X}\sim t_{\nu}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\bm{\Sigma}})
have a
p
-dimensional
Student-
t
distribution with
\nu>4
degrees of freedom and scale matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
and let
\bm{Y}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{g})

be an independent Gaussian variable. We will consider
\bm{Z}=\bm{X}+\bm{Y}
.

B.1
Mixture representation

The multivariate Student-
t
distribution with a scale matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
and degrees of freedom
\nu
can be written as a scale–mixture representation

[
3
,
15
]
:

\bm{X}\,|\,\lambda\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{0},\frac{\mathbf{\bm{\Sigma}}}{\lambda}\right),\qquad\lambda\sim\mathrm{Gamma}\!\left(\frac{\nu}{2},\,\frac{\nu}{2}\right).

(B.1)

Hence, conditional on
\lambda
,

\bm{Z}\,|\,\lambda\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{0},\,\mathbf{\bm{\Sigma}_{g}}+\frac{\mathbf{\bm{\Sigma}}}{\lambda}\right).

(B.2)

Note that when marginalised over
\lambda
, (
B.2
) does not give an exact Student-
t
distribution for
\bm{Z}
.

The total covariance of
\bm{Z}
is obtained from integrating
\mathbb{E}[\bm{Z}\bm{Z}^{\top}|\lambda]
over
\lambda
. Using
\mathbb{E}[1/\lambda]=\nu/(\nu-2)
for a Gamma distribution, we find

\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Z}}=\mathbb{E}[\bm{Z}\bm{Z}^{\top}]=\mathbf{\bm{\Sigma}_{g}}+\left(\frac{\nu}{\nu-2}\right)\,\mathbf{\bm{\Sigma}}.

(B.3)

B.2
Mardia’s kurtosis

For Gaussian-distributed
\bm{W}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma})
and an arbitrary symmetric matrix
\mathbf{M}
, we see via Wick’s theorem that

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\bm{W}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\,\bm{W}\right)^{2}\right]=\left(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{M}\bm{\Sigma})\right)^{2}+2\,\mathrm{tr}\!\left[(\mathbf{M}\bm{\Sigma})^{2}\right].

(B.4)

Now for an arbitrary mean-zero distribution
\bm{D}
, the kurtosis of
20
is defined to be

\textrm{Kurt}(\bm{D})=\mathbb{E}\left[(\bm{U}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{D}}^{-1}\bm{U})^{2}\right],

(B.5)

where
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{D}}
is the covariance of
\bm{D}
and
\bm{U}\sim\bm{D}
.
Using eq. (
B.4
) with
M=\bm{\Sigma}_{g}^{-1}
we see that the Mardia kurtosis of a
p
-dimensional Gaussian is
p(p+2)
.
This, together with the mixture representation and with
\mathbb{E}[1/\lambda^{2}]=\nu^{2}/[(\nu-2)(\nu-4)]
, then shows that the Mardia kurtosis of a
p
-dimensional Student-
t
distribution with
\nu
degrees of freedom is

{\mathrm{Kurt}}(t_{\nu})=\frac{p(p+2)(\nu-2)}{\nu-4}.

(B.6)

B.3
Expectation over the mixture

Setting
\mathbf{M}=\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Z}}^{-1}
in eq. (
B.4
) and using the mixture representation, we find

{\mathrm{Kurt}}(\bm{Z})=p(p+2)+\frac{2\nu^{2}}{(\nu-2)^{2}(\nu-4)}\left[(\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{N})^{2}+2\,\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{N}^{2})\right]\equiv p(p+2)+\kappa,

(B.7)

where
\mathbf{N}=\bm{\Sigma}_{\bf Z}^{-1}\mathbf{\bm{\Sigma}}
.

We now match the covariance and kurtosis of
\bm{Z}
to that of a Student-
t
distribution with degrees of freedom
\nu_{\star}
and scale matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}
, and hence covariance
\nu_{\star}\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}/(\nu_{\star}-2)
and kurtosis

p(p+2)(\nu_{\star}-2)/(\nu_{\star}-4)
.
This yields the effective degrees of freedom

\nu_{\star}=4+\frac{2\,p(p+2)}{\kappa}

(B.8)

and scale matrix

\bm{\Sigma}_{\star}=\frac{(\nu_{\star}-2)}{\nu_{\star}}\left[\bm{\Sigma}_{g}+\left(\frac{\nu}{\nu-2}\right)\bm{\Sigma}\right].

(B.9)

Together, (
B.8
) and (
B.9
) provide the parameters of the
effective Student-
t
approximation.

Appendix C
Lebesgue measure, eigenvalues, and uniform sampling on determinant fibres

We derive the probability density for the eigenvalues of a covariance
matrix under a Lebesgue-induced prior of the form
P(\bm{C})\propto|\bm{C}|^{q}
.

C.1
Lebesgue measure on symmetric positive-definite matrices

Let
C
be a real, symmetric, positive-definite
p\times p
matrix. The
Lebesgue measure on the space of symmetric matrices is

d\bm{C}=\prod_{i\leq j}dC_{ij}.

(C.1)

Any absolutely continuous prior
P(\bm{C})
is defined with respect to this measure.

We consider priors of the form

P(\bm{C})\,d\bm{C}\;\propto\;|\bm{C}|^{q}\,d\bm{C},

(C.2)

where
q\in\mathbb{R}
(with
q=-(p+1)/2
corresponding to the Jeffreys prior considered by
27
).

C.2
Eigenvalue–eigenvector decomposition

Following
33
, we proceed via eigenvalue–eigenvector factorisation of the covariance matrix:

\bm{C}=\bm{Q}\,\mathbf{\Lambda}\,\bm{Q}^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad\mathbf{\Lambda}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{p}),

(C.3)

with
\bm{Q}
an orthogonal matrix and
\lambda_{i}>0
. With this change of variables, the Lebesgue measure factorises as (see
21
p.105)

d\bm{C}\;=\;\left(\prod_{i<j}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|\right)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{p}d\lambda_{i}\right)d\mu(\bm{Q}),

(C.4)

where
d\mu(\bm{Q})
is Haar measure on the orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}(p)
.
The factor
\prod_{i<j}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|
is the Vandermonde determinant.

Including the determinant weight gives

P(\lambda)\,\prod_{i}d\lambda_{i}\;\propto\;\left(\prod_{i}\lambda_{i}^{q}\right)\left(\prod_{i<j}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|\right)\prod_{i}d\lambda_{i}.

(C.5)

Defining the (natural) log-eigenvalues
\ell_{i}=\log\lambda_{i}
, the density becomes

P(\ell)\,\prod_{i}d\ell_{i}\;\propto\;\exp\!\left[(q+1)\sum_{i}\ell_{i}\right]\prod_{i<j}\left|\exp(\ell_{i})-\exp(\ell_{j})\right|\prod_{i}d\ell_{i}.

(C.6)

Using

|\exp({\ell_{i}})-\exp({\ell_{j}})|=2\exp\left(\frac{\ell_{i}+\ell_{j}}{2}\right)\,\sinh\!\left(\frac{|\ell_{i}-\ell_{j}|}{2}\right),

we obtain

P(\ell)\;\propto\;\exp\!\left(\frac{p+1+2q}{2}\sum_{i}\ell_{i}\right)\prod_{i<j}\sinh\!\left(\frac{|\ell_{i}-\ell_{j}|}{2}\right).

(C.7)

C.3
Separation into scale and shape

Define the mean log-eigenvalue
\bar{\ell}=(1/p)\sum_{i=1}^{p}\ell_{i}
,
and define shape variables
r_{i}=\ell_{i}-\bar{\ell}
whence
\sum_{i}r_{i}=0
.

Then
\bar{\ell}=\frac{1}{p}\log|\bm{C}|
controls the determinant,
while
r=(r_{1},\dots,r_{p})
parametrises each
determinant fibre
(i.e. the set of all matrices with a common determinant).

Because the transformation
\ell\mapsto(\bar{\ell},r)
is linear, the Lebesgue measure
splits as

d^{p}\ell=\sqrt{p}\,d\bar{\ell}\,d^{p-1}r,

(C.8)

where
d^{p-1}r
is Lebesgue measure on the hyperplane
\sum_{i}r_{i}=0
.
We can ignore the constant
\sqrt{p}
, and substituting
\ell_{i}=\bar{\ell}+r_{i}
into (
C.7
) gives

P(\bar{\ell},r)\;\propto\;\exp\!\left[\frac{p(p+1+2q)}{2}\bar{\ell}\right]\prod_{i<j}\sinh\!\left(\frac{|r_{i}-r_{j}|}{2}\right).

(C.9)

C.4
Conditioning on fixed determinant

Fixing the determinant
|\bm{C}|
is equivalent to conditioning on
\bar{\ell}=\mathrm{const}
.
The exponential prefactor then becomes an overall constant and drops out.
The induced density on the fibre is therefore

P_{\text{fibre}}(r)\;\propto\;\prod_{i<j}\sinh\!\left(\frac{|r_{i}-r_{j}|}{2}\right),\qquad\sum_{i}r_{i}=0.

(C.10)

C.5
Regularisation

The density (
C.10
) is improper: it diverges logarithmically for
large spectral spreads. For numerical sampling we therefore introduce a weak,
rotationally invariant confining term

P_{\tau}(r)\;\propto\;\prod_{i<j}\sinh\!\left(\frac{|r_{i}-r_{j}|}{2}\right)\exp\!\left(-\frac{\tau}{2}\|r\|^{2}\right),

(C.11)

with
\tau\ll 1
.
This regularisation ensures normalisability while leaving the posterior dominated
by the likelihood (e.g. a Wishart or Student-
t
factor).

C.6
Implications for sampling

Equation (
C.11
) defines a smooth log-concave target function on the

(p-1)
-dimensional subspace
\sum_{i}r_{i}=0
. A Hamiltonian sampler operating directly on
r

respects the correct Lebesgue-induced measure, with the correct eigenvalue repulsion, and is combined with an independent draw of
\bar{\ell}
.

We separately generate random orthogonal matrices
\bm{Q}
via a standard QR decomposition. A
p\times p
matrix with independent standard normal entries is QR-decomposed, and the signs of the columns of
\bm{Q}
were adjusted using the diagonal of R. The resulting matrix is Haar-distributed on
O(p)

[
30
]
. Combining with the samples of
\lambda_{i}
, this yields samples of
\bm{C}=\bm{Q}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{Q}^{\top}
with prior density (
C.11
) above
|\bm{C}|=1
.

Appendix D
Python implementation

We provide here a Python function for the moment-matched natural log-likelihood.

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import cho_factor, cho_solve
from scipy.special import gammaln

def loglike_student_t_gauss_mm(data, mu, S_hat, C_g, Nsim, method="percival", Ntheta=2):
"""
Moment-matched Student-t (natural) log-likelihood for convolution of
Student-t(S_hat, Nsim) and Gaussian(C_g) distributions

Parameters
----------
data : (p,) array
mu : (p,) array, model expectation value of data
S_hat: (p,p) estimated covariance from simulations
C_g : (p,p) additional known Gaussian covariance
Nsim : int, number of simulations used for the S_hat covariance estimate
method : "Percival" (Percival et al 2022; default) or "SH" (Sellentin-Heavens 2016)
Ntheta : int, number of model parameters

Returns
-------
logL : float, natural log of likelihood (up to an additive constant)
nu_star : float, approximate Student-t degrees of freedom
Sigma_star : (p,p) array, approximate Student-t scale matrix
"""

p = data.size
r = data - mu

# Base Student-t parameters
if method.lower() == "sh":
nu = Nsim - p

elif method.lower() == "percival":
B = (Nsim - p - 2) / ((Nsim - p - 1)*(Nsim - p - 4))
m = 2 + Ntheta + (Nsim - 1 + B*(p - Ntheta)) / (1 + B*(p - Ntheta))
nu = m - p

else:
raise ValueError("Method must be ’Percival’ or ’SH’")

if nu <= 4:
raise ValueError("Need nu > 4 for kurtosis matching.")

# Base Student-t scale matrix (not covariance)
Sigma = (Nsim - 1)/nu * S_hat

# Covariance of the base Student-t
C_t = (nu/(nu - 2.0)) * Sigma

# Moment-matched total covariance
C_star = C_g + C_t

# Kurtosis matching
Nmat = np.linalg.solve(C_star, Sigma)
trN = np.trace(Nmat)
trN2 = np.trace(Nmat @ Nmat)

kappa = (2.0 * nu**2) / ((nu - 2.0)**2 * (nu - 4.0))
kappa *= (trN**2 + 2.0 * trN2)

nu_star = 4.0 + 2.0 * p * (p + 2.0) / kappa

# Effective Student-t scale matrix
Sigma_star = ((nu_star - 2.0)/nu_star) * C_star

# Log-likelihood under the moment-matched Student-t
cho = cho_factor(Sigma_star, lower=True, check_finite=False)
x = cho_solve(cho, r, check_finite=False)
quad = float(r @ x)
logdet = 2.0 * np.sum(np.log(np.diag(cho[0])))

logL = (
gammaln(0.5*(nu_star + p)) - gammaln(0.5*nu_star)
- 0.5*(p*np.log(nu_star*np.pi) + logdet)
- 0.5*(nu_star + p)*np.log1p(quad/nu_star)
)

return float(logL), float(nu_star), Sigma_star
