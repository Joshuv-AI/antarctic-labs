Ultrasound evaluation of urinary tract morbidity in adults living in a Schistosoma haematobium endemic region in Mozambique
Authors: João Tiago Serra, Cláudia Conceição, Carina Silva, Mohsin Sidat, Silvana Belo, Ricardo Thompson, Daniel Kaminstein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUltrasound-based assessment of urinary tract morbidity remains central to the evaluation of Schistosoma haematobium infection, but the WHO Niamey protocol is operationally complex and has been used mainly in children and higher-transmission settings. We aimed to describe urinary tract structural abnormalities in adults living in an endemic area of Mozambique using a preliminary application of a modified Niamey-derived protocol adapted for point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) and to compare these findings with parasitological and molecular evidence of infection.MethodologyWe conducted a descriptive cross-sectional study between April and October 2018 among individuals aged 15 years or older living in the Chókwè Health and Demographic Surveillance System area, Mozambique. Schistosoma haematobium infection was defined by urine filtration and/or DNA detection. Urinary tract ultrasound was performed using a simplified Niamey-derived POCUS protocol. All examinations were performed by a trained investigator, and a subset of images was reviewed remotely for interobserver agreement.Principal findingsOur study included 912 ultrasound exams. The prevalence of ultrasound detected urinary tract abnormalities was 37.9% (95% CI 34.8%-41.2%). Of these, 14.5% were positive for Schistosoma haematobium. We found that 11.8% (95% CI 9.7%-13.9%) had a lower urinary tract abnormality and 29.3% (95% CI 26.7%-32.7%) had an upper urinary tract abnormality. The renal pelvis was the most common location for ultrasound abnormalities (23.1%), followed by the ureters (20.4%) and the bladder wall (11.6%). Interobserver agreement was substantial for bladder findings (κ = 0.65), ureteral findings (κ = 0.78), and the final ultrasound score (κ = 0.63) and moderate for kidney findings (κ = 0.59).ConclusionIn this adult population from a historically endemic area, POCUS identified a substantial burden of urinary tract structural abnormalities despite a relatively low prevalence of current infection. In adults, particularly in low-prevalence or post-control settings, such abnormalities should not be interpreted as specific markers of active schistosomiasis, because they may reflect chronic sequelae, prior infection, or other urinary tract pathology. Background

Schistosomiasis is a waterborne neglected tropical disease caused by trematode parasites of the genus Schistosoma [1]. Urogenital schistosomiasis, mainly caused by S. haematobium, commonly presents with microscopic or macroscopic hematuria, frequently accompanied by dysuria. Although hematuria may decline over time, chronic infection can progress to long-term urinary tract morbidity, including bladder wall fibrosis and calcification, hydroureter, hydronephrosis, and, in severe cases, renal failure [2–4]. In addition, the International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies S. haematobium infection as carcinogenic to humans, reflecting its well-established association with squamous cell carcinoma of the bladder [5].

The burden of urinary schistosomiasis remains particularly high in sub-Saharan Africa. It is estimated that approximately 71 million individuals experience schistosomiasis-related hematuria, 100 million develop bladder pathology, and nearly 20 million suffer from hydronephrosis attributable to the disease [6]. A recent review of complicated urogenital schistosomiasis highlighted substantial heterogeneity in diagnostic and management approaches, although ultrasonography emerged as the most frequently used imaging modality [7]. Ultrasound enables the visualization of lower urinary tract abnormalities, including bladder wall thickening, abnormal bladder configuration, trabeculation, diverticula, intraluminal masses, pseudopolyps, and calcifications, as well as upper urinary tract manifestations such as hydroureter and hydronephrosis [8]. Although cystoscopy remains the gold standard for the evaluation of bladder mucosal lesions and schistosomiasis-associated malignancy, ultrasonography offers a non-invasive, affordable, and field-adapted alternative [9]. Moreover, ultrasonographic findings of upper urinary tract involvement have demonstrated good correlation with those obtained using more invasive imaging techniques, such as intravenous urography [10].

The Niamey protocol remains the cornerstone of ultrasound-based assessment of schistosomiasis-related urinary tract morbidity [11]. However, its implementation is subject to several limitations, particularly in field settings where suboptimal technical conditions may compromise image quality, increase interobserver variability, and reduce the reliability of assessments [12]. In addition, the epidemiology of schistosomiasis has evolved in many endemic regions. The widespread implementation of preventive chemotherapy, coupled with improvements in water supply and sanitation, has contributed to a decline in infection intensity and in the prevalence of severe ultrasound-detectable lesions. Under these circumstances, the Niamey protocol may be less sensitive for detecting subtle or early-stage morbidity while remaining effective for identifying advanced structural disease [13, 14]. To address these challenges, several modifications and simplified adaptations have been proposed [15–17]. Increasingly, the Niamey protocol is viewed not as a rigid diagnostic instrument but as a flexible reference framework that can be adapted for point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS), tailored to different epidemiological settings and age groups, and integrated with parasitological, antigen-based, and molecular diagnostic methods. Such an approach may improve morbidity assessment while preserving the protocol’s conceptual strengths.

We conducted a population-based survey in Mozambique to characterize urinary tract structural abnormalities using a modified Niamey-derived POCUS protocol and to compare ultrasound findings with parasitological and molecular markers of S. haematobium infection. This exploratory analysis aimed to assess the utility of a simplified ultrasound approach for evaluating schistosomiasis-related morbidity in an adult population living in an endemic setting.

Methods

Study area and population

Chókwè District is located in the southern region of Mozambique. The Chókwè Health Research and Training Center (CITSC) established a Health and Demographic Surveillance System (HDSS) in 2010, which currently follows approximately 100,000 of the district’s 183,000 inhabitants and covers a geographical area of about 600 km². Each registered individual is assigned a unique, lifelong, and non-transferable identification code, enabling longitudinal community-based follow-up. Annual demographic surveillance rounds are conducted to update information on births, deaths, migration, household characteristics, and other health-related events, following standardized procedures established by the INDEPTH Network [18]. The present study was conducted within a broader research project investigating the epidemiology, risk factors, morbidity, and diagnostic markers of urinary schistosomiasis among adults living in this endemic rural setting. Detailed epidemiological findings, sampling procedures, and a map of the study area have been reported elsewhere [19].

Sampling and field procedures

We conducted a cross-sectional study among individuals aged 15 years or older living in households registered in the HDSS database in 2018. In that year, the HDSS listed 19,578 households with at least one member aged 15 years or older. Sampling followed a two-stage cluster design. Households were treated as the primary sampling units, and the smallest HDSS administrative units were considered secondary sampling units.

The required number of clusters was estimated using the methodology recommended by the WHO for the Expanded Programme on Immunization cluster surveys [20]. Following this approach, 30 clusters were selected with a target of 30 individuals per cluster (30 × 30 design). The sampling frame for secondary units consisted of the 131 smallest HDSS administrative units. Because the number of households varied across these units, we used probability proportional to population size to ensure an equal probability of selection for each cluster. After systematically selecting 30 HDSS units, the second sampling stage focused on recruiting 30 individuals within each of these 30 clusters.

Using the household as the primary sampling unit, we first estimated how many households were required per cluster. Assuming an average of two residents aged ≥ 15 years per household, 13 households would be expected to yield at least 30 eligible individuals. Based on previous CITSC field experience, we anticipated a refusal rate of approximately 20% and therefore increased the target to 19 households per cluster. These households were randomly selected from the HDSS database, resulting in a total of 570 households selected for participation.

For each cluster, a list of selected households was generated from the HDSS, including the names and dates of birth of all registered members. Given the high mobility of the local population and the possibility of outdated records, a replacement strategy was implemented: if no member of a selected household could be contacted after two visits or if the dwelling could not be located, the household was replaced by the nearest household with residents present at the time of the visit. All individuals who reported currently living in the household at the time of the survey were invited to participate, regardless of whether they appeared on the original HDSS list or the duration of their residence in that household.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied in the field by trained research staff. Participants were eligible if they were aged 15 years or older at the time of recruitment, resided in a selected household and were able to understand the study information and provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: known pregnancy or pregnancy documented by ultrasound at recruitment; history of abdominal trauma requiring medical care in the previous six months; history of kidney or bladder surgery, as indicated by an abdominal or back scar not attributable to any other self-reported cause; inability to be located after at least two home visits; and failure to provide a required biological sample.

Field activities followed the steps summarized in Fig. 1. Ultrasound examinations were performed in community areas close to participants’ households. For each cluster, a suitable site for setting up a tent and the necessary logistical equipment was identified in advance in collaboration with local community leaders. Data and sample collection and analysis

Sociodemographic and clinical data were collected using an individual questionnaire (Additional file 1), administered by trained members of the research team. The principal investigator did not participate in questionnaire administration and was solely responsible for performing the ultrasound examinations to minimise potential bias in interpreting ultrasound findings due to prior knowledge of urinary schistosomiasis symptoms or history. Questionnaires were completed electronically using Open Data Kit software on handheld tablets.

Whenever feasible, urine samples were collected between 10:00 and 14:00 [21]. Samples were stored in portable insulated bags, transported to the CITSC laboratory and processed on the same day. All samples underwent parasitological and molecular analysis for urinary schistosomiasis infection diagnostics. A volume of 10 mL from all urine samples was filtered through a syringe with a 20 μm mesh size and filters were examined by microscopy for the presence of S. haematobium eggs after adding a drop of Lugol’s iodine [22]. Infection intensity was classified according to WHO criteria as light (1–49 eggs/10 mL urine) or heavy (≥ 50 eggs/10 mL urine) for S. haematobium [23]. Urine samples were also examined for the presence of blood and proteins with Mission urinalysis reagent strips 10U (ACON; San Diego, USA). The strip results were read by direct comparison with the color scale provided with the test. Urine test strip results were classified for blood as +-, +, ++, +++, 5–10 Ery/L, and 50 Ery/µL; for proteins as 15 mg/dL, 30 mg/dL, 100 mg/dL, 300 mg/dL, and 2000 mg/dL.

Molecular analysis was conducted at the Institute of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine laboratory in Lisbon. For the molecular diagnosis of both S. haematobium and S. mansoni, specific primer pairs previously described in the literature were used [24, 25]. Details on sample storage, transport, processing and the PCR method used have been published elsewhere [19]. Participants were considered positive for schistosomiasis infection if at least one diagnostic test (parasitological or molecular) yielded a positive result.

Simplified Niamey ultrasound protocol

Following the recommendations of Akpata et al. [15], we implemented a descriptive, preliminary application of a modified Niamey-derived protocol for assessing urinary tract pathology related to S. haematobium infection and incorporated an additional score for the echogenic “snow sign” (Fig. 2) [26]. The adapted protocol was designed to be operationally feasible for POCUS while maintaining core elements of the original Niamey framework.

Ultrasound examination

All study participants were invited to undergo an abdominal ultrasound to detect structural abnormalities associated with S. haematobium infection. Ultrasound examinations were performed using a portable Philips Lumify S4-1 probe (Bothell, WA, USA). Ultrasound was not mandatory; refusal to undergo ultrasound did not exclude participants from other components of the study. Examinations followed predefined steps and a standardised classification protocol (Additional file 2).

Before the exam, participants were asked to drink approximately 1500 mL of water to ensure adequate bladder filling. Optimal bladder volume was considered to be between 200 and 250 mL, estimated using the formula: length × width × height × 0.52. When the participant reported a strong urge to void, he or she was positioned supine with the arms alongside the body. In the transverse view of the bladder, right–left and craniocaudal dimensions were measured. The bladder lumen and wall were systematically assessed for the presence of the echogenic snow sign and for wall thickening, irregularity, and focal lesions such as masses, polyps or tumors. A longitudinal view of the bladder was then obtained and the anteroposterior diameter measured, with particular attention to the distal ureters.

Participants were then asked to empty the bladder completely. The examination was resumed with assessment of the kidneys, with the participant again in the supine position and hands placed behind the neck. A longitudinal view of the right kidney was obtained to evaluate the proximal ureter and renal pelvis, immediately followed by a transverse view to assess the renal pelvis. The same sequence was repeated for the left kidney and ureter. Still images and short video clips of all key ultrasound views were recorded and stored. A schematic of the POCUS protocol, including probe positions and steps, is shown in Fig. 3.

Ultrasound review

A subset of ultrasound examinations was randomly selected for remote expert review by the senior author (DK) to assess interobserver agreement and for quality control purposes. Based on published estimates of interobserver agreement for ultrasound-detected urinary tract abnormalities, we assumed a kappa statistic (κ) of approximately 0.7 [27, 28]. Using alpha 0.05, 1-beta 0.05, 2 tails, p₀=0.7, and p₁=0.8, we selected a random sample of 244 ultrasound images. Six ultrasound images and video clips were lost during data storage, leaving a final sample of 236. The expert reviewer had had access to dynamic clips and static images and was blinded to both laboratory results and the principal investigator’s ultrasound scores.

Ethical considerations

Administrative approval for the study was obtained from the Gaza Provincial Health Directorate. The protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Bioethics Committee of the National Institute of Health and the Mozambican National Bioethics Committee for Health (83/CNBS/2017). Final administrative authorization was granted by the Mozambican Minister of Health. The district government and the municipal council of Chókwè town were informed as part of ongoing institutional collaboration. Informed consent procedures were adapted to age and preferred language (Portuguese or Changana). Adults aged 18 years or older who agreed to participate provided written informed consent; illiterate participants affixed a fingerprint in lieu of a signature in the presence of an independent witness who signed the form. For adolescents aged 15–17 years, written assent (or fingerprint) was obtained from the minor, together with written consent (or fingerprint) from a parent or legal guardian, following the same procedures used for adults. All consent forms were issued in duplicate, with one copy retained by the participant and one by the research team.

At the end of the study, medical treatment was provided to all participants with schistosomiasis or other diagnosed intestinal parasitic infection. In ultrasound abnormalities related or not to the scope of the study, whose urgency or potential severity warranted a more detailed assessment, the participant was referred to the nearest health facility.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participant characteristics and study variables as absolute and relative frequencies. In bivariate analyses, associations between categorical variables were examined using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were small. Percentage estimates were accompanied by Wald confidence intervals. Interobserver agreement for ultrasound findings was assessed using Cohen’s kappa or weighted kappa, as appropriate for the type of variable. Kappa values were interpreted as follows: <0.00, poor agreement; 0.00–0.20, slight; 0.21–0.40, fair; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, substantial; and 0.81–1.00, almost perfect agreement. Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 29.0, and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Of the 1033 participants enrolled in the main study, 88.3% (912/1033) volunteered to complete a urinary tract ultrasound. The vast majority were women, 71.7% (659/912), with a median age of 34 (IQR 32) years old. The median height was 160 cm (IQR 11), the median weight was 60 kg (IQR 16) and the mean BMI was 22.9 kg/m² (IQR 5.49).

Infection, hematuria and proteinuria data

The overall prevalence of S. haematobium infection was 10.7% (98/912), 10.3% (26/253) in males and 10.9% (72/659) in females. The highest prevalence (16.0%) was observed in patients aged 15 to 24 years old and decreased with age (Table 1). The majority of those with a positive parasitological examination for S. haematobium (30/912) had a light intensity infection, with a mean egg intensity of 20 eggs/10 mL.

Of the 346 ultrasound exams with urinary tract abnormalities, 14.5% (50/346) had a positive laboratory result for S. haematobium infection. The proportion was higher in those with a lower urinary tract abnormality (26.9%) than in the ones with an upper urinary tract lesion (11.4%).

Discussion

The aim of this work was to describe structural abnormalities of the urinary tract using a descriptive, preliminary application of a modified Niamey-derived protocol adapted for POCUS and to compare these findings with parasitological and molecular evidence of S. haematobium infection.

Ultrasound structural morbidity

Our results showed an overall prevalence of urinary tract abnormalities of 37.9% (95% CI 34.8%-41.2%) in the adults living in the Chókwè HDSS area. Ultrasound examination plays an essential role in the assessment and follow-up of schistosomiasis infection and its complications, as well as to determine disease burden [8]. In chronic schistosomiasis, the infection may be completely asymptomatic and egg excretion may be very low or absent, and thus ultrasound abnormalities may be the first finding of the disease [29–31]. Of the 346 abnormal ultrasound scans, 14.5% (50/346) were positive for S. haematobium, with 89.5% of these having a light intensity infection. Previous data on S. haematobium infection in the area reported a prevalence of 35% in school-aged children in Chókwè between 2005 and 2007, and two mass chemotherapy campaigns took place in 2014 and 2016, covering more than 90% of children (personal communication). In our study, 33.1% of participants reported having ever been diagnosed with schistosomiasis, and 18.3% reported having received specific treatment for the infection.

Ultrasound abnormalities in adults should not be interpreted as specific indicators of active S. haematobium infection, especially in settings shaped by repeated praziquantel exposure. Instead, the main purpose of ultrasound in this situation is to assess cumulative structural morbidity. The relationship between current parasitology and organ damage gradually dissociates as infection intensity decreases and egg excretion becomes lighter, sporadic, or nonexistent [32, 33]. Therefore, the low percentage of parasitologically positive individuals among those with abnormal ultrasound scans in our cohort should not be interpreted as contradictory, but rather, it is consistent with a population where ultrasound is more likely to detect residual fibrotic remodeling, previous infections, and chronic sequelae. This is especially important for adults, as repeated injuries and lifetime exposure build up over time. Furthermore, not every lesion found on an adult urinary tract ultrasound is specific to schistosomiasis.

Because the ultrasound sample was primarily female (71.7%) and the prevalence of current S. haematobium infection was relatively low (10.7%), interpretation of these prevalence estimates must also be done with caution. Context-dependent sex-related variations in schistosomiasis are influenced by both gendered exposure patterns and biology. Males frequently have higher prevalence overall, according to a systematic review and meta-analysis, but the size and even direction of sex differences vary greatly between settings, with transmission intensity, occupational roles, and domestic water contact influencing observed patterns [34].

Lower urinary tract score

The lower urinary tract score assessed two main aspects of the bladder: the bladder content for the presence of an echogenic snow sign and the bladder wall for irregularities or thickening greater than 5 mm or any polyps, masses or tumors. As inadequate bladder filling prior to ultrasound is a potential confounding factor, we measured bladder volume for quality purposes [15, 35]. In our case, the median pre-ultrasound bladder volume was 173 mL (IQR 161), indicating a moderate bladder filling sufficient for examination [36].

In the first phase, we assessed the presence of an echogenic snow sign within the bladder lumen, which has been suggested as a novel sonographic finding in urinary schistosomiasis in a study conducted in Sudan [26]. The term “echogenic snow sign” describes bright sonographic flares observed in the bladder due to tissue debris and agglutinated blood cells suspended in the urine. Previous studies have reported this ultrasound finding in 21.5 to 61.5% of adults [37, 38]. However, we found that only 0.3% of our study participants had an echogenic snow sign during bladder content assessment. It is also worth noting that many conditions other than urinary schistosomiasis can cause an echogenic snow sign on the bladder, such as bacterial or fungal infections, stones or even non-specific bladder inflammation, and so this is a highly non-specific sign [39].

The most typical bladder ultrasound features of urinary schistosomiasis are parietal thickness, wall irregularities and the formation of endoluminal protrusions such as pseudopolypoid formations and masses [8]. Since the first attempt to standardize the ultrasound assessment of urinary schistosomiasis morbidity with the WHO Cairo protocol in 1990, further developed in Niamey in 1996, several modifications have been proposed to facilitate its use [11, 40]. A bladder wall thickening of 5 mm has long been accepted as the cut-off value for schistosomiasis induced bladder wall morbidity and several grading scores have been proposed over the years [15, 41–44]. Using a simplified bladder wall scoring, we identified bladder wall thickening > 5 mm or any polyps, masses or tumors in 11.6% of the study population. This is a lower prevalence than studies found in adults in the population of countries such as Yemen (82.7%), Nigeria (33.9–57.8%), Ghana (44.1%), Gabon (38.9%), Angola (36.3%), Kenya (16–36%), Madagascar (33.3%), Egypt (20%) and Mali (13.4%) [9, 10, 16, 30, 31, 37, 38, 44–50]. Different sampling methods, age groups and ultrasound assessment protocols make it difficult to compare results, although the high prevalence of S. haematobium infection in almost all previous studies, compared to the moderate prevalence of urinary schistosomiasis (14.5%) in our study, could be an explanation.

Bladder abnormalities should be interpreted as indicators of cumulative damage requiring parasitological, molecular, and clinical correlation rather than as specific proof of ongoing schistosomiasis in adult populations with lifetime exposure, particularly in post-mass drug administration or declining-transmission settings. This distinction is crucial because, even in cases where contemporaneous egg detection is negative or of low intensity, persistent bladder morbidity is still clinically significant.

Upper urinary tract score

For scoring purposes, the upper urinary tract ultrasound evaluation was divided into ureter and kidney pelvis assessment, in a similar approach to the WHO Niamey’s ultrasound guidelines. To avoid potential confounders, participants were asked to urinate until they felt an empty bladder before the upper urinary tract examination [15].

The ureters are known to be involved in as much as up to 65% of cases of S. haematobium infections, mainly due to ureteral dysfunction, fibrosis and stenosis [8]. Fibrotic changes increase with prolonged infection, but vesicoureteral reflux is an early complication of schistosomiasis caused by ureteral dysfunction. Ureter dilation >3 mm in any extension was found in 20.4% of participants. Studies reporting the prevalence of specific schistosomiasis ureter disease in the adult population show a prevalence of 32.5% in Egypt, 31% in Yemen, 23.3% in Nigeria, 23% in Mali, 8.8% in Ghana, 5.9% in Gabon and 3.8% in Angola [9, 10, 16, 38, 47–49]. These data not only corroborate our findings but also highlight the need for specific ureter pathology assessment and scoring in the morbidity assessment of ureter ultrasound.

The renal parenchyma is sporadically affected or otherwise completely normal even in advanced stages of urinary schistosomiasis [8]. Kidney involvement is often the result of chronic obstruction and hydronephrosis is, therefore, the main pathological feature to be assessed. The WHO Niamey protocol measures the parenchymal thickness (distance between the renal pelvis and the capsule) as a result of pelvis dilation in the case of obstruction, the latter being the most severe stage [11]. In our ultrasound protocol, we have measured the renal pelvis fissure itself, considering an anechoic fissure within an echogenic renal pelvis > 2 mm as abnormal [15, 50]. Using this modified kidney scoring, we have identified a renal pelvis > 2 mm in 23.1%, of which 9.6% were ≥ 11 mm. This new scoring improves the sensitivity of ultrasound in detecting kidney pelvis dilatation, which may be important in areas where schistosomiasis prevalence is decreasing and upper urinary tract pathology tends to be less severe. Studies on the prevalence of schistosomiasis associated hydronephrosis in adults report 52.5% in Egypt, 34.5% in Yemen, 9-18.6% in Nigeria, 16% in Gabon, 8.4% in Madagascar, 2-7.5% in Kenya, 7.3% in Ghana, 3.7% in Angola and 1% in Mali. Although there is a wide range of variation, the data support our findings, particularly if we use a renal pelvis ≥ 11 mm as a cutoff value [9, 10, 16, 30, 38, 44–49, 51].

Similar to the evaluation of the lower urinary tract, the refinement of the upper urinary tract ultrasound protocol, as we have done, is particularly important for adult population screening in low prevalence areas with low intensity infections. However, minimal pelvic dilation can be difficult to differentiate from anechoic imaging due to vessels in the kidney’s pelvis and so further research with this simplified upper urinary tract scoring is needed for better refinement.

Strengths and limitations

POCUS protocols are only valuable if they can be taught and utilized by clinicians in the settings where the pathology is common. Highly trained ultrasound experts are in short-supply in resource limited settings [52]. However, the present study did not evaluate field implementation by general clinicians, because all ultrasound examinations were performed by the principal investigator, a trained operator, under research conditions. Our findings, therefore, demonstrate feasibility and preliminary interpretive reproducibility under controlled conditions, but they do not define the training duration, learning curve or operational variability that would determine routine scalability.

Expert review was used to assess interobserver agreement. We found a substantial agreement for almost every anatomical structure of the urinary tract, except for the renal pelvis, where the agreement was only moderate. These results contrast with the ones of Remppis et al., in which the interobserver agreement was good for bladder wall thickening, poor/fair for ureter dilatation and very good for the renal pelvis [16]. A possible explanation is that we used a substantially lower threshold for renal pelvis fissure dilatation (≥ 2 mm versus 1 cm). This lower cut-off is likely to increase sensitivity for detecting subtle or early pelvicalyceal dilatation, which may be particularly relevant in lower-intensity or post-control settings where advanced obstructive disease is less common. However, this gain in sensitivity is likely to come at the expense of specificity, because minimal anechoic separation in the renal sinus may also reflect physiological variation, transient fullness related to hydration status, or even sonographic misclassification of adjacent vascular structures rather than true pathological dilatation.

On the other hand, in our study, the ultrasound scans were remotely reviewed by the expert under different image visualization conditions than those performed in the field. Darker rooms might allow a better definition of ultrasound abnormalities and thus influence the classification of smaller lesions. The classification in the field was based on a dynamic real-time examination, allowing a better assessment than static images and video clips reviewed by the expert. Nevertheless, for quality purposes, the substantial agreement found on much of the classification allows us to be more certain of the ultrasound abnormalities found. These results should therefore be interpreted as proof-of-concept for a simplified protocol rather than as evidence that the approach is already ready for widespread deployment by general clinicians. Future implementation studies should evaluate abbreviated training packages, competency thresholds, supervision requirements and between-operator variability under real field conditions.

The adult-only design also affects both interpretation and comparability. Many Niamey-based morbidity studies and treatment-response studies have been conducted in school-aged or preschool-aged children, in whom current egg excretion and ultrasound-detected lesions may be more tightly coupled and more rapidly reversible after praziquantel treatment [53]. By contrast, adult populations remain important to assess precisely because they may show lower levels of detectable active infection while still carrying clinically relevant cumulative morbidity from prior exposure, persistent fibrosis or residual obstructive change, and they may continue to benefit from diagnosis, follow-up, and treatment [29, 45]. Our results should therefore be generalized primarily to adult populations living in long-standing endemic settings with previous praziquantel exposure.

Conclusion

In this adult population living in a historically Schistosoma haematobium endemic area of Mozambique, urinary tract POCUS identified a substantial burden of structural abnormalities, despite a relatively low prevalence of infection defined by urine filtration and/or DNA detection. These findings support the value of ultrasound for documenting cumulative urinary tract morbidity in adults, particularly where parasitological positivity is infrequent and may underestimate long-term disease burden. However, in adult and post-control settings, ultrasound abnormalities should not be interpreted as specific markers of active schistosomiasis, as many lesions may reflect chronic sequelae. The modified Niamey-derived POCUS protocol showed promising feasibility and acceptable interobserver agreement under controlled research conditions with a trained operator. Nevertheless, these findings should be regarded as proof of concept, rather than evidence of readiness for routine widespread use. Further studies are needed to refine diagnostic thresholds, assess training requirements and operator variability, and validate implementation in real-world endemic settings and across different age groups.
