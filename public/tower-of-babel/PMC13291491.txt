SLC25A3 exports mitochondrial copper to metalate cytochrome c oxidase and prevent cuproptosis
Authors: Mohammad Zulkifli, Ifrah Farid, Laura E. Oldfather, Vinit C. Shanbhag, Scot C. Leary, Michael J. Petris, Paul A. Cobine, Vishal M. Gohil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Copper (Cu) is an essential cofactor for cytochrome c oxidase (CcO), a mitochondrial respiratory chain enzyme that is metalated in the intermembrane space (IMS) primarily using Cu derived from the mitochondrial matrix pool. While Cu import into the matrix depends on the inner membrane carrier SLC25A3, the route by which matrix Cu is exported to the IMS for insertion into CcO has remained a major, unresolved step in intramitochondrial Cu trafficking. Here, we leveraged our recent discovery that the Cu ionophore elesclomol (ES) releases Cu directly into the mitochondrial matrix to show that SLC25A3 is required for exporting Cu to the IMS for CcO metalation. Loss of SLC25A3 decreases mitochondrial Cu content and CcO activity as expected. Strikingly, bypassing the loss of SLC25A3 with ES-mediated Cu delivery to the matrix fails to restore CcO function; rather, it drives toxic Cu retention and triggers cuproptosis, revealing that SLC25A3-facilitated Cu export is the limiting determinant of CcO metalation. Heterologous expression in Lactococcus lactis confirms that SLC25A3 can mediate Cu export. These results suggest that SLC25A3 is the long-sought mitochondrial Cu exporter with a dual role in enabling CcO metalation and gating susceptibility to cuproptosis.

Copper (Cu) is an essential micronutrient that serves as a cofactor for cytochrome c oxidase (CcO), the terminal enzyme of the mitochondrial respiratory chain (1). Cu enters the mitochondrial matrix through the mitochondrial carrier family transporter SLC25A3 (2), generating a matrix Cu pool that is used for metalating CcO (3). However, Cu is inserted into the CcO by the intermembrane space (IMS) localized Cu metallochaperones (1). This requires matrix Cu to be exported back across the inner mitochondrial membrane—a key step in intramitochondrial Cu trafficking that has remained unresolved for decades (1, 4).

We recently showed that the membrane-permeable Cu ionophore elesclomol (ES) can bypass upstream Cu-uptake defects by releasing Cu directly into the mitochondrial matrix (5). In this manner, ES rescues CcO abundance and activity in genetic models of Cu deficiency (6, 7), a finding consistent with the existence of a yet to be identified matrix-to-IMS Cu export mechanism. Here, using ES as a chemical tool, we identify this missing step by revealing that SLC25A3 is required for exporting Cu from the matrix to the IMS for incorporation into CcO, and that its loss sensitizes cells to ES-mediated cuproptosis.

Results and Discussion

Previous studies have implicated SLC25A3 in mitochondrial Cu and phosphate import (2, 8). The Cu transport function of SLC25A3 was shown using mammalian cell lines and reconstitution experiments in liposomes, though directionality of Cu transport was not established (2). To address this issue, we constructed a Slc25a3−/− rat H9c2 cardiomyoblast cell line because SLC25A3 is highly expressed in the heart (proteinatlas.org). The loss of SLC25A3 protein in slowly proliferating Slc25a3−/− cells was confirmed by immunoblotting (Fig. 1A). To verify that loss of SLC25A3 produced a defect in mitochondrial respiration, we measured oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and found that Slc25a3−/− cells exhibited reduced basal and maximal OCR (Fig. 1B). Reduced OCR was due to the decreased abundance of CcO-containing complexes, which require Cu for both their activity and stability (Fig. 1C). To assess how loss of SLC25A3 affects cellular metal homeostasis, we measured whole-cell metal abundance and found significantly lower Cu in Slc25a3−/− than wild-type (WT) cells (Fig. 1D). By contrast, Slc25a3−/− cells accumulated excess Fe (Fig. 1E), consistent with the known requirement of Cu for Fe export in mammalian cells, whereas Zn was not altered (Fig. 1F). To probe cytosolic Cu content, we examined the intracellular distribution of ATP7A, a Cu exporter that is retained in the perinuclear trans-Golgi network under basal conditions and relocalizes to post-Golgi vesicles in response to increased cytosolic Cu (9). As expected, ATP7A was largely confined to the perinuclear region of WT cells; however, it was predominantly localized within post-Golgi vesicles that extended toward the cell periphery in Slc25a3−/− cells (Fig. 1G), consistent with elevated cytosolic Cu.

Next, we tested whether direct, ES-mediated Cu delivery to the mitochondrial matrix could substitute for SLC25A3 in Cu delivery to CcO. In Slc25a3−/− cells, ES failed to restore the abundance of a Cu-containing CcO subunit, COX1, and instead markedly reduced levels of the mitochondrial Fe–S proteins succinate dehydrogenase subunit B (SDHB) and aconitase 2 (ACO2) (Fig. 2A), consistent with mitochondrial Cu accumulation and Cu-mediated turnover of Fe–S cluster-containing proteins (10). By contrast, treatment with Cu-ATSM and Cu-disulfiram, which do not rely on the FDX1-dependent mechanism of Cu release in the mitochondrial matrix, partially rescued COX1 levels and did not reduce SDHB and ACO2 abundance (Fig. 2 B and C). This difference is consistent with the two proposed Cu delivery routes to CcO (4), where ES utilizes route one in which matrix Cu serves as the primary source of Cu for metalation of CcO while Cu-ATSM and disulfiram likely employ a second, less efficient route which involves making Cu directly available to IMS Cu chaperones. To determine whether ES-Cu treatment elevates mitochondrial Cu in Slc25a3−/− cells, we quantified whole-organelle Cu content without distinguishing between the matrix and IMS pools. As expected, basal mitochondrial Cu was lower in Slc25a3−/− compared to WT cells (Fig. 2D). However, ES-Cu increased mitochondrial Cu by ~threefold in the knockout, as compared to only ~1.1 fold in the WT cells (Fig. 2D). This excessive Cu accumulation in Slc25a3−/− mitochondria was accompanied by a stronger induction of cuproptosis markers relative to WT cells, including loss of lipoylated dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT) and dihydrolipoamide S-succinyltransferase (DLST) and increased DLAT oligomerization (Fig. 2 E and F). Together, these results indicate that ES delivers Cu into the matrix in the absence of SLC25A3, but this Cu cannot be exported to the IMS for delivery to CcO and is instead entrapped in the matrix leading to cuproptosis (10). To directly test an export function for SLC25A3, we expressed human SLC25A3 in Cu-loaded Lactococcus lactis, a bacterial system for testing mitochondrial transporters (2) (Fig. 2G). After nisin-induced expression, SLC25A3-expressing cells showed significantly greater Cu export than control (Fig. 2H). These results support the ability of SLC25A3 to mediate Cu export in L. lactis, although it should be noted that this system does not recapitulate mitochondrial compartmentalization.

In conclusion, our findings resolve a longstanding knowledge gap in intramitochondrial Cu trafficking by implicating SLC25A3 in the export of matrix Cu to the IMS-localized metallochaperones for CcO metalation while protecting cells from Cu-driven cuproptosis (Fig. 2I). This pathway mirrors the mechanism used in gram-negative bacteria, where Cu is imported into the cytoplasm and subsequently exported to the periplasm to metalate CcO (11), suggesting an evolutionarily conserved strategy for Cu delivery to CcO.

Material and Methods

Cell culture, mitochondrial respiration, immunoblotting, and heterologous expression of SLC25A3 in L. lactis was performed as reported previously (3, 5). Detailed description of the methods is provided in SI Appendix.
