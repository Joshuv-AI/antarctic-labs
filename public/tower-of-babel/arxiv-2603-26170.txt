An inverse problem for semilinear wave equations on metric tree graphs
Sergei Avdonin, Matti Lassas, Jinpeng Lu, Medet Nursultanov, Lauri Oksanen

Abstract
. We study the inverse problem for a semilinear wave equation on metric tree graphs. From the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map defined at all but one of the boundary vertices, we recover unknown connectivity of the graph, lengths of the edges, the time-independent potential and the time-dependent coefficient of the nonlinear term of the equation.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
35R30, 35L71, 34B45

1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega=\Omega(V,E)
be a finite connected metric graph with the set of vertices
V
and the set of edges
E
.
We consider the following semilinear wave equation on the metric graph
\Omega
up to time
T>0
,

(1.1)

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})u(x,t)+q(x,t)u(x,t)+a(x,t)u^{3}(x,t)=0,&x\in\Omega\setminus V,\ t\in(0,T),\\
u(x,t)|_{t\leq 0}=0,&x\in\Omega,\end{cases}

with given boundary conditions on a subset of vertices
\Gamma\subset V
and matching conditions on the other vertices
V\setminus\Gamma
.
We are interested in the following inverse problem.

Inverse Problem.

Given the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map for the equation (
1.1
) on a subset of vertices
\Gamma\subset V
up to time
T
, is it possible to determine the graph structure (i.e., the connectivity and lengths of edges) and the functions
a,q
?

Clearly one can only hope to solve this problem up to isometry of the graph, and only recover the functions
a,q
in some time domain depending on
T
.
As the first step, we study this problem on metric trees,
i.e. graphs without cycles.
The goal of this paper is to solve the inverse problem for metric trees with the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map defined on the set of all but one of the leaves, in the case that the potential
q
is time-independent.

1.1.
Inverse problems for linear and nonlinear equations on metric graphs.

Inverse theory of network-like structures is an important part of the rapidly developing area of
applied mathematics – analysis on graphs with a variety of important applications to many problems of science and engineering. The known results in this direction concern almost exclusively linear differential equations defined on trees and periodic lattices.

A variety of inverse spectral problems on metric graphs were studied
in many papers, see e.g. books
[
15
]
,
[
29
]
, survey
[
38
]
and references therein. Dynamical inverse problems on metric trees were studied, e.g. in
[
10
,
7
,
9
]
.
In particular, the most significant result of
[
10
]
developed a constructive and robust procedure for recovering tree’s parameters, which became known as the leaf-peeling (LP) method.
This method was extended to inverse boundary problems for various types of partial differential equations (PDEs) on trees
in a series of subsequent papers, see, e.g.
[
7
,
8
,
11
,
13
]
.
The proposed identification procedure is recursive, and it allows
recalculating efficiently the inverse data from the
original tree to the smaller trees, “pruning” leaves step by step up to the rooted edge.
A discrete version of the inverse conductivity problem was studied on tree graphs
[
22
]
and on (multi-dimensional) square lattices
[
19
,
21
]
.
Inverse problems on (perturbed) periodic lattices are studied in e.g.
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
24
,
25
,
33
,
37
]
, and recently in
[
18
]
based on a different approach developed in
[
16
,
17
]
.

Nonlinear equations on graphs naturally appear in several fields of sciences, such as condensed matter physics, hydrodynamics, nonlinear waveguides and optic fibers, see e.g.
[

]
and references therein.
Initial boundary value problems and control problems for nonlinear PDEs on metric graphs were studied in several works, see e.g.
[

,

]
.
To the best of our knowledge, we are not aware of papers concerning inverse problems for nonlinear differential equations on metric graphs. In the present paper we study inverse problems for nonlinear equations on trees, and plan to consider general metric graphs in subsequent papers. We use the ideas for solving inverse problems for nonlinear PDEs on manifolds developed in
[
30
]
and
the ideas of leaf-peeling method or, more exactly, its “leaf-cleaning” version
[

]
.

1.2.
Main result

Let
\Omega=\Omega(V,E)
be a finite, connected graph, where
V,E
are the sets of vertices and edges of
\Omega
respectively. Each edge in
E
is associated with two vertices in
V
called its endpoints. We assume that
V=\{v_{i}:i\in I\}
and
E=\{e_{j}:j\in J\}
are nonempty;
I
and
J
are subsets of
\mathbb{N}.

An edge
e_{j}
between
v_{i}
and
v_{k}
is denoted as
e_{j}(v_{i}v_{k})
. The set of indices of the edges incident to
v_{i}
is denoted by
J(v_{i})
. For trees, the set of all leaves (vertices of degree
1
), denoted by
\Gamma:=\{v_{i}\in V:|J(v_{i})|=1\},
plays the role of the graph boundary.
Here, we use
|\cdot|
to denote the cardinality of a set of features on a graph.
We recall that a graph is called a
metric graph
if every edge
e_{j}\in E
is identified with an interval
(0,l_{j})
of the real line with a positive length
l_{j}
.

To describe our result on inverse problem for trees, we assume that controls and observations are available at all leaves in
\Gamma
except one leaf
\gamma_{0}
where the Dirichlet condition is imposed.
Namely, we set

(1.2)

\Gamma_{0}:=\Gamma\setminus\{\gamma_{0}\},

and consider the equation

(1.3)

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})u(x,t)+q(x)u(x,t)+a(x,t)u^{3}(x,t)=0,&x\in\Omega\setminus V,\ t\in(0,T),\\
u(\cdot,t)|_{\Gamma_{0}}=f(t),\quad u(\gamma_{0},t)=0&t\in(0,T),\\
u(x,t)|_{t\leq 0}=0,&x\in\Omega,\\
u\textrm{ subject to the Kirchhoff-Neumann condition on }V\setminus\Gamma.\end{cases}

The Kirchhoff-Neumann condition states the continuity of the solution
u
at each vertex
and
that the sum of the normal derivatives of
u
in the direction outwards the vertex is zero; a precise formulation is given in (
2.1
).
Note that the Kirchhoff-Neumann condition at the vertices of degree
1
reduces to the usual Neumann condition.

We introduce the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map on
\Gamma_{0}
by the rule

(1.4)

\Lambda_{T}f=\partial u^{f}|_{\Gamma_{0}\times(0,T)},

where
u^{f}
is the solution of the problem (
1.3
).
Note that we will only use the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map for small sources
f
(in certain norms, depending on
T
), in which case the semilinear equation (
1.3
) has a unique solution, see Lemma
6.2
.
Observe that the wave observations cannot distinguish vertices of degree
2
, at which the wave behavior is not altered by the Kirchhoff-Neumann condition at the vertex.

We write
C^{1}(\Omega\times[0,T])
for the space of functions that are continuous on
\Omega\times[0,T]
, and are
C^{1}
on each edge-time rectangle
[0,l_{j}]\times[0,T]
for every
e_{j}\in E
.
In (
1.3
) we assume that

(1.5)

a\in C^{1}(\Omega\times[0,T]).

We prove that the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map (
1.4
) on
\Gamma_{0}
up to time
T
for the semilinear wave equation (
1.3
) determines the tree structure and coefficients
a,q
up to some time, assuming
T
is sufficiently large.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\Omega
be a finite metric tree and
\Gamma
be the set of all leaves of
\Omega
.
Let
\gamma_{0}
be one leaf and denote
\Gamma_{0}=\Gamma\setminus\{\gamma_{0}\}.

Suppose that we are given the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
\Lambda_{T}
on
\Gamma_{0}
for the semilinear wave equation (
1.3
) up to time
T
, with
q\in C^{1}(\Omega)
and
a\in C^{1}(\Omega\times[0,T])
.
Denote

(1.6)

D(\gamma_{0}):=\max_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}}\,d(\gamma,\,\gamma_{0}),

where
d
is the distance function on the metric tree.
Assume
T>2D(\gamma_{0})
. Then
\Lambda_{T}
uniquely determines the metric tree structure of
\Omega
up to vertices of degree
2
, the (time-independent) potential
q
on
\Omega
, and the coefficient
a
on

(1.7)

\Big\{(x,t)\in\Omega\times[0,T]:D(\gamma_{0})+d(x,\gamma_{0})\leq t\leq T-D(\gamma_{0})+d(x,\gamma_{0})\Big\}.

Remark 1.1
.

The domain (
1.7
) in Theorem
1
is not the maximal domain that the given data can recover, especially on the edges incident to the boundary vertices in
\Gamma_{0}
, but it is a simple formulation valid for the whole graph, see Figure
8
.
For the simple case of interval
[0,L]
, the domain of recovery (
1.7
) for the coefficient
a
is illustrated in Figure
1
.

Figure 1.
As a special case of Theorem
1
, the reconstruction of the time-dependent coefficient
a
on the interval
[0,L]
. Suppose control and observation of waves are available at only one endpoint
x=0
, i.e.,
\Gamma_{0}=\{0\}
and
\gamma_{0}=\{1\}
. Then
\Lambda_{T}
uniquely determines
a
on the domain (
1.7
), the one enclosed by the blue lines.

Since the first linearization of
\Lambda_{T}
is the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map for the linear equation
(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})v+qv=0
(see Lemma 2.2 below), the first part of Theorem
1
(recovery of the metric tree structure and time-independent potential) follows from known results, e.g.
[
10
,
13
]
. Thus, we assume the metric tree structure and
q
are known from now on and focus on the second part of the theorem: the recovery of the time-dependent coefficient
a
.
The basic idea is to use the linearization method developed in
[
30
]
for manifolds.
The main difficulty in the discrete case is that waves can interact with their reflections at the internal vertices in a complicated way due to general configurations of the graph, which may cause certain information to be hidden from wave observations.
In the case of metric trees, we are able to construct a general procedure in this paper so that each edge can be isolated from wave observations.
We note that our method also works for nonlinearity
u^{m}
with
m\geq 3
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we introduce metric graphs and discuss an inverse problem for the linear wave equation.
In Section
3
we study the geometric optic solutions for the linear wave equation on metric graphs. Section
4
is devoted to linearization techniques and presents the solution on our inverse problem for the simplest graphs. Section
5
completes the solution to the inverse problem for the semilinear wave equation on metric trees.
Finally, Section
6
contains the results concerning the direct problem for the semilinear wave equation on metric graphs.

2.
Preliminaries and inverse problems for linear wave equation

2.1.
Metric graphs and Hilbert spaces on graphs

The graph
\Omega
determines naturally the Hilbert space of square
integrable functions
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\Omega).
We define the space
\mathcal{H}^{1}

of continuous functions
y
on
\Omega
such that

y_{j}:=y|_{e_{j}}\in H^{1}(e_{j})
for every
j\in J
and denote by
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}
the subspace
\{y\in\mathcal{H}^{1}\,:y|_{\Gamma}=0\}.

Let
\mathcal{H}^{-1}
be the dual space of
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}
.
We further introduce the space
\mathcal{H}^{2}
of functions
y\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
such that

y_{j}\in H^{2}(e_{j})
for every
j\in J,
and
for every
v\in V\setminus\Gamma
, the equalities

(2.1)

\sum_{j\in J(v)}\partial y_{j}(v)=0;\quad y_{j}(v)=y_{k}(v),\ j,k\in J(v),

holds. Here (and everywhere later on)

\partial y_{j}(v)
denotes the
derivative of
y
at the vertex
v
taken along the edge
e_{j}

in the direction outwards the vertex.
Vertex conditions (
2.1
) are called the
standard

or
Kirchhoff-Neumann
(KN) conditions. For the boundary vertices they are just Neumann conditions.

Let
q
be a real valued function (potential) such that
q|_{e_{j}}\in C[0,l_{j}]
. We define the Schrödinger operator on the graph
\Omega
as the operator

(2.2)

\,\mathcal{L}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+q\,

in
\mathcal{H}
with the domain
\mathcal{H}^{2}\cap\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}.

We now associate the following initial boundary value problem (IBVP) for the wave equation to the graph
\Omega
:

(2.3)

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})u(x,t)+q(x)u(x,t)=0,&x\in\Omega\setminus V,\ t>0,\\
\sum_{j\in J(v)}\partial u_{j}(v,t)=0,&v\in V\setminus\Gamma,\ t>0,\\
u_{j}(v,t)-u_{k}(v,t)=0,&j,k\in J(v),\ v\in V\setminus\Gamma,\ t>0,\\
u(\cdot,t)|_{\Gamma}=f(t),&t>0,\\
u(x,0)=0,\ \partial_{t}u(x,0)=0,&x\in\Omega.\\
\end{cases}

This IBVP was studied by many authors.
For different conditions on the potential
q

the proof of the following proposition can be found in
[
3
]
,
[
31
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
12
]
,
and
[
14
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
m:=|\Gamma|,\,{f}\in\mathcal{F}^{T}:=L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{m})
and

q\in L^{1}(\Omega).
There exists a unique solution
u=u^{f}(x,t)
to the IBVP (
2.3
) (understood in a weak sense) and

(2.4)

u\in C([0,T];\mathcal{H})\cap C^{1}([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{-1}),

that is,
u(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{H}
,
\partial_{t}u(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{H}^{-1}
for all
t\in[0,T]
, and both
u(\cdot,t)
and
\partial_{t}u(\cdot,t)
are continuous with respect to
t
in corresponding norms.
If
f\in\mathcal{F}^{T}_{1}:=H^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{m}),\,f(0)=0,
the solution to the IBVP (
2.3
)
u(\cdot,t)
belongs to the energy space for all
t\in[0,T]
:

(2.5)

u\in C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{1})\cap C^{1}([0,T];\mathcal{H}).

If
{f}\in C^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{m})
,
{f}(0)={f}^{\prime}(0)={0}
and
q\in C(\Omega)
, the IBVP (
2.3
) has a unique classical solution.

2.2.
Inverse dynamical and spectral problems for the IBVP (
2.3
)

The
response operator
(dynamical Dirichlet-to-Neumann map)
R^{T}
acting in
\mathcal{F}^{T}
for the IBVP (
2.3
) is defined by the following rule:

(2.6)

(R^{T}f)(t)=\partial u^{f}(\cdot,t)|_{\Gamma},\ \ t\in(0,T),\ \ Dom\;R^{T}=\mathcal{F}^{T}_{1}.

The inverse dynamical problem is to recover the tree (connectivity of the graph
\Omega
and lengths of the edges) and the potential
q
on the edges from the response operator
R^{T}
and
determine
T
that allows to solve this problem.

Along with the dynamical problems, inverse spectral problems have also attracted great attention.
Inverse spectral data is the sequence of pairs
\mathcal{S}:=\{\lambda_{n},\partial\phi_{n}|_{\Gamma}\},\,n\in\mathbb{N},
consisting of the eigenvalues of the operator
\mathcal{L}

and traces of the derivatives of its eigenfunctions on the boundary of the graph. Another kind of spectral data, which is equivalent to
\mathcal{S},
is the Titchmarsh–Weyl (TW) matrix function (spectral Dirichlet-to-Neumann map).

The relations between different kinds of inverse data were discussed in several papers, see e.g.
[
28
]
,
[
10
]
. In particular, it is known that the spectral data determine the response operator
R^{T}
for all
T\geq 0
through the Fourier–Laplace transform and vice versa.
If the dynamical data are given on a finite time interval, we are not able to find the spectral data in such a way and we need to use dynamical methods for solving the inverse problem.

The following lemma allows us to solve the problem of finding
q(x)
independently of the nonlinear problem.
Let
\Gamma_{0}\subset\Gamma
, and let
\Lambda_{T}^{\rm lin}
be the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map on
\Gamma_{0}
for the linear equation, i.e., corresponding to the equation (
1.3
) with
a=0
.

Lemma 2.2
.

The operator
\Lambda_{T}^{\rm lin}
is determined by
\Lambda_{T}
.

Proof.

Let
f
be a boundary source. For
\varepsilon>0
, consider the equation

(2.7)

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})u(x,t)+q(x)u(x,t)+a(x,t)u^{3}(x,t)=0,&x\in\Omega\setminus V,\ t\in(0,T),\\
u(\cdot,t)|_{\Gamma}=\varepsilon f(t),&t\in(0,T),\\
u(x,t)|_{t\leq 0}=0,&x\in\Omega,\\
u\textrm{ subject to the Kirchhoff-Neumann condition on }V\setminus\Gamma.\end{cases}

Let
u_{\varepsilon}
be the solution of the equation.
Using arguments similar to those in
[
32
, Proposition 2]
, one can show that the solution is differentiable with respect to
\varepsilon
, see Proposition
6.3
. Define

(2.8)

\tilde{u}:=\left.\partial_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}.

By differentiating (
2.7
) with respect to
\varepsilon
, we obtain that
\tilde{u}
solves the equation

(2.9)

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})\tilde{u}(x,t)+q(x)\tilde{u}(x,t)=0,&x\in\Omega\setminus V,\ t\in(0,T),\\
\tilde{u}(\cdot,t)|_{\Gamma}=f(t),&t\in(0,T),\\
\tilde{u}(x,t)|_{t\leq 0}=0,&x\in\Omega,\\
\tilde{u}\textrm{ subject to the Kirchhoff-Neumann condition on }V\setminus\Gamma.\end{cases}

Then
\Lambda_{T}^{\rm lin}
can be expressed as follows

\Lambda_{T}^{\rm lin}f=\left.\partial\tilde{u}\right|_{\Gamma_{0}\times(0,T)}=\partial_{\varepsilon}\big(\Lambda_{T}(\varepsilon f)\big)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}.\qed

3.
Geometric optics on metric graphs

This section is for the explicit formula, modulo
O(h)
, for the geometric optics solution of the (linear) wave equation on metric graphs with time-independent potential function
q
. We use this solution in the next section to find the coefficient
a(x,t).

(3.1)

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})v(x,t)+q(x)v(x,t)=0,&x\in\Omega\setminus V,\ t>0,\\
v(\cdot,t)|_{\Gamma}=f(t),&t>0,\\
v(x,t)|_{t\leq 0}=0,&x\in\Omega,\\
v\textrm{ subject to the Kirchhoff-Neumann condition on }V\setminus\Gamma.\end{cases}

The boundary source of our interest is of geometric optics type:

\exp\big(ih^{-1}\theta(t)\big)\chi_{b}(t),

where
h>0
is a small parameter, and
\chi_{b}
is a smooth cutoff function near
b
with an independent parameter
b>0
, say
\chi_{b}=1
on
[b/2,3b/2]
and vanishing outside of
(0,2b)
.
To simplify computations, we choose

(3.2)

\theta(t)=t,

and the boundary source

(3.3)

\displaystyle f_{h}(t):=e^{ih^{-1}t}\chi_{b}(t).

The geometric optics solution for the wave equation on the interval
[0,l]
with the boundary source (
3.3
) at
x=0
has the following form up to time
l
(see, e.g.
[
27
, Thm. 2.64]
or
[
34
,
35
]
for details),

(3.4)

v(x,t)=e^{ih^{-1}(t-x)}\chi_{b}(t-x)+R_{h}(x,t),\quad x,t\in[0,l],

where the remainder
R_{h}
satisfies

(3.5)

|R_{h}(x,t)|\leq C_{b}h,

for some constant
C_{b}>0
depending on
\chi_{b}
and
\|q\|_{C^{1}}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

With the Dirichlet boundary condition imposed at
x=l
, the geometric optics solution
v(x,t)
on the interval
[0,l]
, with the boundary source (
3.3
) at
x=0
, has the following form up to time
2l
,

v(x,t)=e^{ih^{-1}(t-x)}\chi_{b}(t-x)-e^{ih^{-1}(t+x-2l)}\chi_{b}(t+x-2l)+O(h),\quad x\in[0,l],\;t\in[0,2l].

To prove the lemma we check that the main term satisfies the equation and boundary conditions and estimate the rest. The extension of this lemma to tree graphs is discussed in the next subsection.

3.1.
Geometric optics on a star graph

Let us consider a star graph, namely
n
intervals
I_{1},\cdots,I_{n}
with length
l_{1},\cdots,l_{n}
glued at
x=0
, where we parametrize the intervals
I_{j}
as
[0,l_{j}]
.
We a boundary source of the form (
3.3
) at time
t=-l_{1}
at
x=l_{1},
the endpoint
z_{1}
of
I_{1}
, namely
f(z_{1},t)=f_{h}(t)
, and
0
on all other boundary vertices.
The solution
v^{h}
of the equation (
3.1
) with this boundary source has the following form on
I_{1}
,

(3.6)

v^{h}(x,t)=e^{ih^{-1}(t+x)}\chi_{b}(t+x)+O(h),\quad x\in I_{1},\;t\in[-l_{1},0],

Note that the phase function here differs slightly from (
3.4
) due to different parametrization.

The following lemma characterizes the behaviour of geometric optics solutions past the common vertex of a star graph.

Lemma 3.2
.

On the metric graph of gluing
n
intervals at one common point parametrized as above, the geometric optics solution of the equation (
3.1
), with the boundary source chosen as above, has the following form on the time interval
t\in[0,\min_{j}l_{j}]
:

(3.7)

v_{1}^{h}(x,t)=e^{ih^{-1}(t+x)}\chi_{b}(t+x)-\frac{n-2}{n}e^{ih^{-1}(t-x)}\chi_{b}(t-x)+R_{h}(x,t),\quad x\in I_{1},

(3.8)

v_{j}^{h}(x,t)=\frac{2}{n}e^{ih^{-1}(t-x)}\chi_{b}(t-x)+R_{h}(x,t),\quad x\in I_{j},\ j=2,\cdots,n,

where
v_{j}^{h}
is the restriction of the solution
v^{h}
on
I_{j}
.

Proof.

By the Kirchhoff-Neumann condition, we have at
x=0
for any
t
,

\begin{cases}v_{1}^{h}(0,t)=v_{2}^{h}(0,t)=\cdots=v_{n}^{h}(0,t),\\
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{x}v_{j}^{h}(0,t)=0.\end{cases}

Solving these equations and examining
0
-th order terms (with respect to
h
) gives the coefficients appeared in the formulae. Indeed, let
c_{j}
be the coefficients appeared in
v_{j}^{h}
, respectively. Then the conditions above give

\begin{cases}f_{h}(t)-c_{1}f_{h}(t)=c_{2}f_{h}(t)=\cdots=c_{n}f_{h}(t),\\
f_{h}^{\prime}(t)+c_{1}f_{h}^{\prime}(t)-\sum_{j=2}^{n}c_{j}f_{h}^{\prime}(t)=0.\end{cases}

Hence

c_{1}=\frac{n-2}{n},\quad c_{j}=\frac{2}{n},\quad j=2,\cdots,n.\qed

Now we consider the simplest graph, interval
[0,l]
with the boundary control
f=f_{h}(t)
applied at the left endpoint:
v(0,t)=f(t),\ v(l,t)=0.
One can check that
for
t>0
the solution of (
3.1
) is presented as

(3.9)

v(x,t)=\Big(1+R_{h}(x,t)\Big)\,\Big(f(t-x)-f(t+x-2l)+f(t-x-2l)-f(t+x-4l)+\cdots\Big).

If the boundary control
f=f_{h}(t)
is applied at the right endpoint:
v(0,t)=0,\ v(l,t)=f(t),
then the solution of (
3.1
) takes the form

(3.10)

v(x,t)=\Big(1+R_{h}(x,t)\Big)\,\Big(f(t+x-l)-f(t-x-l)+f(t+x-3l)-f(t-x-3l)+\cdots\Big).

Lemma
3.2
and formulas (
3.9
), (
3.10
) allow us to construct the solution to the problem (
3.1
) with
f=f_{h}(t)
for all
t>0.

To derive the equations describing the solution to the problem (
3.1
) on a general graph we can propose another way to solve the problem described in Lemma
3.2
. We denote by
g(t)
the value of the solution
v(0,t)
at the interior vertex and write the matching conditions connecting
g
and
f
.
We obtain a delay integral equation of the second kind Volterra type. On a general graph we obtain a system of such equations.

Let us begin with a star graph described above. We put

F(x,t)=f(t+x-l_{1})-f(t-x-l_{1})+f(t+x-3l_{1})-f(t-x-3l_{1})+\ldots,

G_{j}(x,t)=g(t-x-l_{1})-g(t+x-l_{1}-2l_{j})+g(t-x-l_{1}-2l_{j})-g(t+x-l_{1}-4l_{j})+\ldots,\ \;j=1,\ldots,n.

Up to the zero-th order terms,

v_{1}(x,t)=\Big(1+R_{h}(x,t)\Big)\Big(F(x,t)+G_{1}(x,t)\Big),

v_{j}(x,t)=\Big(1+R_{h}(x,t)\Big)G_{j}(x,t),\ \;j=2,\ldots,n.

Now we present the second KN condition,
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{x}v_{j}(0,t)=0.
It is convenient to introduce a new time variable instead of
t-l_{1}
and denote it again by
t.
Taking into account that

\partial_{x}F(0,t)=2\Big(f^{\prime}(t)+f^{\prime}(t-2l_{1})+f^{\prime}(t-4l_{1})+\ldots\Big),

and examining the zero-th order term in
h
, we obtain:

(3.11)

\partial_{x}F(0,t)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{x}G_{j}(0,t)=0.

This equation can be presented in the form

(3.12)

2\Big(f^{\prime}(t)+f^{\prime}(t-2l_{1})+f^{\prime}(t-4l_{1})+\ldots\Big)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Big(g^{\prime}(t)+2g^{\prime}(t-2l_{j})+2g^{\prime}(t-4l_{j})+\ldots\Big),

and rewritten as

(3.13)

2\Big(f(t)+f(t-2l_{1})+f(t-4l_{1})+\ldots\Big)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Big(g(t)+2g(t-2l_{j})+2g(t-4l_{j})+\ldots\Big).

It allows us to find
g(t)
by
f(t)
and, therefore, to find the main terms of the Gaussian beam on a star graph. For that, we consider all linear combinations
\sum_{j=1}^{n}2\,k_{j}\,l_{j}
with
k_{j}\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
and
organize these numbers
0,t_{1},t_{2},\ldots
in the increasing order on the real axis. Then the equations (
3.12
) can be solved in steps starting with the interval
(0,t_{1}),
then on
(t_{1},t_{2}),
and so on.

Example. Consider a 3-star graph with
l_{1}=2,\,l_{2}=1,\,l_{3}=5.
Then solving equation (
3.13
) in this specific case we obtain:

for
0<t<2,\ g(t)=\frac{2}{3}\,f(t);

for
2<t<4,\ g(t)=\frac{2}{3}\,f(t)-\frac{4}{9}\,f(t-2);

for
4<t<6,\ g(t)=\frac{2}{3}\,f(t)-\frac{4}{9}\,f(t-2)+\frac{2}{27}\,f(t-4);

for
6<t<8,\ g(t)=\frac{2}{3}\,f(t)-\frac{4}{9}\,f(t-2)+\frac{2}{27}\,f(t-4)-\frac{8}{81}\,f(t-6);

and so on.

Let

4.
Linearization method

Using the linearization method, the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
\Lambda_{T}
defined in (

(4.1)

\partial_{\varepsilon_{1}}\partial_{\varepsilon_{2}}\partial_{\varepsilon_{3}}\Big(\Lambda_{T}(\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}f_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}f_{3})\Big)\Big|_{\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon_{3}=0}=\partial w\big|_{\Gamma_{0}\times(0,T)},

where

(4.2)

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})w+qw=-6av_{1}v_{2}v_{3},\\
w|_{\Gamma\times(0,T)}=0,\\
w|_{t\leq 0}=0,\end{cases}

and, for
j=1,2,3
,

(4.3)

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})v_{j}+qv_{j}=0,\\
v_{j}|_{\Gamma_{0}\times(0,T)}=f_{j},\ v_{j}|_{\{\gamma_{0}\}\times(0,T)}=0,\\
v_{j}|_{t\leq 0}=0.\end{cases}

Let
T>0
, and
v_{0}
be the solution of an auxiliary wave sending from
t=T
,

(4.4)

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})v_{0}+qv_{0}=0,\quad\quad\quad t\in(0,T),\\
v_{0}|_{\Gamma_{0}\times(0,T)}=f_{0},\ v_{0}|_{\{\gamma_{0}\}\times(0,T)}=0,\\
v_{0}|_{t=T}=0,\quad\partial_{t}v_{0}|_{t=T}=0.\end{cases}

Using the equations above and integration by parts, one has

(4.5)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}6av_{0}v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma_{0}}f_{0}\partial w,

the right-hand side of which is determined by the given data
\Lambda
.

For starters, we could consider the problem on the interval

where we have control and observation only at

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\Omega=[0,l]
, and suppose that control and observation are available at

(i.e.,
\Gamma_{0}=\{0\}
).
Assume

.
Then

uniquely determines

on
\big\{(x,t)\in[0,l]\times[0,T]:2l\leq x+t\leq T\big\}

Proof.

Let

The waves

are given in (

\displaystyle v_{2}(x,t)=e^{ih^{-1}(t-x-s)}\chi(t-x-s)+o(1),

\displaystyle v_{3}(x,t)=\overline{v_{2}(x,t)},

The wave
v_{1}

v_{1}(x,t)=e^{ih^{-1}(t-x-t_{0})}\chi(t-x-t_{0})-e^{ih^{-1}(t+x-2l-t_{0})}\chi(t+x-2l-t_{0})+o(1).

The wave
v_{0}
is the time-reflection of wave

in (

\displaystyle v_{0}(x,t)=v(x,2l+t_{0}-t)=e^{ih^{-1}(2l+t_{0}-t-x)}\chi(2l+t_{0}-t-x)+o(1),

as

uniformly on

. Therefore, since

is supported in

, it follows that

\int_{0}^{2l+2t_{0}}\int_{0}^{l}av_{0}v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}dxdt=-\int_{0}^{2l+2t_{0}}\int_{0}^{l}a(x,t)\chi^{2}(t-x-s)\chi^{2}(t+x-2l-t_{0})dxdt+o(1).

and recalling (

can be determined by the given

. After changing the variables, we obtain

I=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-s/2}^{s/2}\int_{-s/2}^{s/2}a\left(\frac{1}{2}(z-y+2l+t_{0}-s),\frac{1}{2}(z+y+2l+t_{0}+s)\right)\chi^{2}(y)\chi^{2}(z)dzdy.

, i.e. the red point in Figure
3

\Lambda_{T}
. Hence

on the line
\{x+t=2l+t_{0}\}
can be determined.

Let

. We take

and range

in
[t_{0},2l+t_{0}]
.
The construction above determines
a
on the line
\{x+t=A\}
.
Taking

determines
a
on

by continuity, and taking

determines

on

Thus
\Lambda_{T}
determines
a
on the closed parallelogram domain
\{2l\leq x+t\leq T\}

∎

Proposition 6.1
.

Let

\{t=T\}

(6.1)

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})u(x,t)+q(x)u(x,t)=F(x,t),&\text{in }(\Omega\setminus V)\times(0,T),\\
u(\cdot,t)|_{\Gamma}=f(t),&\text{on }\Gamma\times(0,T),\\
u(x,t)|_{t\leq 0}=0,&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u\textrm{ satisfies the Kirchhoff-Neumann condition on }V\setminus\Gamma,\end{cases}

has a unique solution
u\in E^{1}
satisfying the energy estimate

(6.2)

\|u\|_{E^{1}}\leq C\left(\|f\|_{H^{3/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})}+\|F\|_{L^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))}\right),

where
C
depends only on

,
q
, and
\Omega
.

Finally, if assume additionally that

(6.3)

F\in C^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)),\qquad F\arrowvert_{t=0}=0,\qquad\text{and }f\in H^{3+1/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|}),

then for any edge from
E
,
e\simeq(0,l)
, it follows that
u\arrowvert_{e}(\cdot,t)\in H^{2}([0,l])
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

Step 1: The case
f=0
.
Define a sesquilinear form

\mathbf{a}(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x}u\overline{\partial_{x}v}dx,

with domain
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. This yields the nonnegative self-adjoint operator
A
corresponding to
-\partial_{x}^{2}
with Kirchhoff condition on
V\setminus\Gamma
.

Consider the space
X=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
, equipped with norm

\|(u,v)\|_{X}=\left(\mathbf{a}(u,u)+\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Since
\Gamma\neq\emptyset
,
X
is a Hilbert space. Define operators

\mathcal{A}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-I\\
A&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathcal{B}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
q&0\end{bmatrix}

with the domains
D(\mathcal{A}_{0})=D(A)\times H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and
D(\mathcal{B})=X
. Finally, we set
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{0}+\mathcal{B}
. Since
\mathcal{B}
is bounded,
D(\mathcal{A})=D(\mathcal{A}_{0})
. Since
-\mathcal{A}
is a bounded perturbation of the skew-adjoint operator
-\mathcal{A}_{0}
, it generates strongly continuous semigroup
\{T(t)\}_{t>0}
. Then,

\mathcal{U}^{F}=\begin{bmatrix}u^{F}\\
v^{F}\end{bmatrix}=\int_{0}^{t}T(t-s)\mathcal{F}(s)ds,\qquad\text{where }\mathcal{F}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
F\end{bmatrix},

solves the equation

\begin{cases}\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{U}^{F}=-\mathcal{A}\mathcal{U}^{F}+\mathcal{F},\\
\mathcal{U}^{F}(0)=0.\end{cases}

In particular,
v^{F}=\partial_{t}u^{F}
, and hence,
u^{F}\in E^{1}
. Moreover,

(6.4)

\|\mathcal{U}^{F}\|_{X}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|T(t-s)\mathcal{F}(s)\|_{X}ds\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\|\mathcal{F}(s)\|_{X}ds=C\|F\|_{L^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))}.

Using the Poincare inequality, we complete the proof for the case
f=0
.

Step 2: The case
F=0
.

Fix a leaf
v\in\Gamma
and its incident edge
e\simeq(0,l)
with endpoint
x=l
corresponding to
v
. Then there exists a function
w_{e}\in H^{2}([0,l]_{x}\times[0,T]_{t})
with

w_{e}=0\quad\text{near }\{t=0,T\}\cup\{x=0\},\qquad w_{e}\arrowvert_{x=l}=f\arrowvert_{v},

and satisfying

(6.5)

\|w_{e}\|_{H^{2}([0,l]\times[0,T])}\leq C\|f\arrowvert_{v}\|_{H^{3/2}([0,T])}.

This can be done by using Sobolev extension theorem and multiplication by a cutoff function which is
1
near the support of
f\arrowvert_{v}
and zero near the boundaries corresponding to
t=0,T
and
x=0
.

The above inequality implies

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|w_{e}(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\partial_{t}w_{e}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|f\arrowvert_{v}\|_{H^{3/2}([0,T])}.

Set
w=\sum_{v\in\Gamma}w_{e}
on boundary edges and
w\equiv 0
elsewhere. Then
w
satisfies the trivial initial condition, the Kirchhoff-Neumann condition on
V\setminus\Gamma
, and

(6.6)

\|w\|_{E^{1}}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{3/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})}.

We set
u^{f}=v+w
, where
v
is the solution to the equation

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})v+qv=-(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})w-qw,&\text{in }(\Omega\setminus V)\times(0,T),\\
v=0,&\text{on }\Gamma\times(0,T),\\
v|_{t\leq 0}=0,&\text{in }\Omega,\\
v\text{ satisfies the Kirchhoff-Neumann condition on }V\setminus\Gamma.\end{cases}

Since
w_{e}\in H^{2}(e\times[0,T])
, we know that

-(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})w-qw\in L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))\subset L^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)).

Therefore, from the first step, we obtain

\displaystyle\|v\|_{E^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})v+qv\|_{L^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{e\in E}\left(\|\partial_{t}^{2}w\|_{L^{2}(e\times[0,T])}+\|\partial_{x}^{2}w\|_{L^{2}(e\times[0,T])}+\|qw\|_{L^{2}(e\times[0,T])}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{e\in E}\|w\|_{H^{2}(e\times[0,T])}.

Here, the constant
C
depends only on the graph,
T
, and
q
. By estimate (
6.5
),

(6.7)

\|v\|_{E^{1}}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{3/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})}.

Therefore, due to (
6.6
), we derive that

(6.8)

\|u^{f}\|_{E^{1}}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{3/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})}.

Step 3: General case.

Finally, by linearity,
u=u^{F}+u^{f}
solves (
6.1
) with estimate (
6.2
) following from (
6.4
) and (
6.8
). Uniqueness follows from the group property or an energy identity for homogeneous data.

Step 4: Under assumptions (
6.3
).
Since
\mathcal{A}_{0}
is skew-adjoint and
\mathcal{B}
is bounded, we know that
-\mathcal{A}=-\mathcal{A}_{0}-\mathcal{B}
is closed densely defined and satisfies

\|(\mathcal{A}+\lambda I)^{-k}\|_{X\rightarrow X}\leq(\lambda-\|\mathcal{B}\|_{X\rightarrow X})^{-k},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N},\text{ }\lambda>\|\mathcal{B}\|_{X\rightarrow X}.

Therefore, by
[
26
, Theorem 1.19, IX, Ch. 5]
and
[
26
, (1.49), IX, Ch. 5]
, it follows that
\mathcal{U}^{F}\in D(\mathcal{A})
, and hence,
u^{F}\in D(A)
. In particular,
u^{F}\arrowvert_{e}\in H^{2}([0,l])
for any
e\simeq[0,l]
.

Since
f\in H^{3+1/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})
, we can additionally require
w_{e}
to be in
H^{4}([0,l]\times[0,T])
, where
e\simeq[0,l]
. Then,

-(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})w-qw\in C^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))

and, as we showed above,
v\arrowvert_{e}(\cdot,t)\in H^{2}([0,l])
, for
e\simeq[0,l]
. By construction of
w
, this is true for
u^{f}
, and hence, for
u=u^{F}+u^{f}
.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
q\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
L>0
, and
a\in C^{1}(\Omega\times[0,T])
be such that
\|a\|_{C^{1}(\Omega\times[0,T])}<L
. There exist
\kappa
,
\rho>0
such that if
f\in H^{3/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})
satisfies
\|f\|_{H^{3/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})}<\kappa
and
f=0
near
\{t=0\}\cup\{t=T\}
, then there is a unique solution to

(6.9)

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})u(x,t)+q(x)u(x,t)+a(x,t)u^{3}(x,t)=0,&x\in\Omega\setminus V,\ t\in(0,T),\\
u(\cdot,t)|_{\Gamma}=f(t),&t\in(0,T),\\
u(x,t)|_{t\leq 0}=0,&x\in\Omega,\\
u\textrm{ subject to the Kirchhoff-Neumann condition on }V\setminus\Gamma,\end{cases}

in the ball

B_{\rho}(0)=\{u\in E^{1}:\;\|u\|_{E^{1}}\leq\rho\}.

Moreover, the solution satisfies the estimate

\|u\|_{E^{1}}\leq C_{L,T}\|f\|_{H^{3/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})}.

Proof.

The argument parallels the proof of
[
32
, Lemma 1]
. The only difference is that
[
32
, Lemma 1]
assumes
s\in\mathbb{N}
, whereas here
s=0
. That assumption is used to guarantee that
E^{s+1}
is an algebra, which remains true in our setting. All other steps are identical.

Assume
F\in L^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
and
f\in H^{3/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})
be such that
f=0
near
\{t=0\}\cup\{t=T\}
. By Proposition
6.1
, equation (
6.1
) admits a unique solution
u\in E^{1}
.

For fixed
f
, define the source-to-solution map

S:E^{1}\cap\{F\arrowvert_{t=0}=0\}\longrightarrow E^{1}\cap\{F\arrowvert_{t=0}=0\},

which assigns to
F
the solution
u
of equation (
6.1
). Next, set

\Theta:B_{\rho}(0)\cap\{F\arrowvert_{t=0}=0\}\longrightarrow B_{\rho}(0)\cap\{F\arrowvert_{t=0}=0\},\qquad\Theta u:=S(au^{3}),

with
\rho>0
to be chosen later. Since
E^{1}
is an algebra (e.g.
[
1
]
),
\Theta
is well defined. By (
6.2
),

\|\Theta u\|_{E^{1}}\leq C_{T}\bigl(\|f\|_{H^{3/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})}+\|au^{3}\|_{L^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))}\bigr)\\
\leq C_{T}\bigl(\|f\|_{H^{3/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})}+\|au^{3}\|_{E^{0}}\bigr)\leq C_{T}\bigl(\kappa+\|a\|_{C(\Omega\times[0,T])}\rho^{3}\bigr).

Therefore, for sufficiently small
\kappa
and
\rho
,
\Theta
defines a self-map of
B_{\rho}(0)
.

The remaining part of the proof is a repetition of the steps in
[
32
, Lemma 1]
, with
[
32
, estimate (16)]
replaced by (
6.2
).
∎

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
q\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
L>0
, and
a\in C^{1}(\Omega\times[0,T])
with
\|a\|_{C^{1}(\Omega\times[0,T])}<L
. Then there exist
\kappa,\rho>0
such that for any
f_{j}\in H^{3/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})
and
\varepsilon_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
j=1,2,3
, satisfying

\|\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}f_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}f_{3}\|_{H^{3/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})}\leq\kappa,\qquad f_{j}=0\text{ near }\{t=0\}\cup\{t=T\},

the equation (
6.9
) with boundary input
f=\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}f_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}f_{3}
admits a unique solution
u
in the ball
B_{\rho}(0)
. Moreover,

\|u\|_{E^{1}}\leq C_{L,T}\|f\|_{H^{3/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})}.

The solution admits the expansion

u=\varepsilon_{1}v_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}v_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}v_{3}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}=3\\
k_{j}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\begin{pmatrix}3\\
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\end{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{1}^{k_{1}}\varepsilon_{2}^{k_{2}}\varepsilon_{3}^{k_{3}}w_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}+\mathcal{R},

where, for
j=1,2,3
, each
v_{j}
solves

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})v_{j}+qv_{j}=0,&\text{in }(\Omega\setminus V)\times(0,T),\\
v_{j}=f_{j},&\text{on }\Gamma\times(0,T),\\
v_{j}|_{t\leq 0}=0,&\text{in }\Omega,\\
v_{j}\text{ satisfies the Kirchhoff-Neumann condition on }V\setminus\Gamma,\end{cases}

and for
k_{j}\in\{0,1,2,3\}
, each
w_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}
solves

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})w_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}+qw_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}+av_{1}^{k_{1}}v_{2}^{k_{2}}v_{3}^{k_{3}}=0,&\text{in }(\Omega\setminus V)\times(0,T),\\
w_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}=0,&\text{on }\Gamma\times(0,T),\\
w_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}|_{t\leq 0}=0,&\text{in }\Omega,\\
w_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}\text{ satisfies the Kirchhoff-Neumann condition on }V\setminus\Gamma,\end{cases}

with remainder bounded by

\|\mathcal{R}\|_{E^{1}}\leq C_{T}\|a\|_{E^{1}}^{2}\|f\|_{H^{3/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})}^{5}.

Proof.

The argument follows from
[
32
, Proposition 2]
.
Existence, uniqueness, and the first estimate are given by Lemma
6.2
.
We now prove the remaining claims.

Note that
\mathcal{F}=u-\varepsilon_{1}v_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}v_{2}-\varepsilon_{3}v_{3}
satisfies

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})\mathcal{F}+q\,\mathcal{F}=-au^{3},&\text{in }(\Omega\setminus V)\times(0,T),\\
\mathcal{F}\arrowvert_{\Gamma}=0,&\text{on }\Gamma\times(0,T),\\
\mathcal{F}\arrowvert_{t\leq 0}=0,&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\mathcal{F}\text{ subject to the Kirchhoff-Neumann condition on }V\setminus\Gamma.\end{cases}

By Proposition
6.1
and the algebra property of
E^{1}
, we obtain

(6.10)

\|\mathcal{F}\|_{E^{1}}\leq C_{T}\|au^{3}\|_{L^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|au^{3}(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\\
\leq C_{T}\|au^{3}\|_{E^{1}}\leq C_{T}\|a\|_{E^{1}}\|u\|_{E^{1}}^{3}\leq C_{T}\|a\|_{E^{1}}\|f\|_{H^{3/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})}^{3}.

Next, consider the remainder term

\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{F}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}=3\\
k_{j}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\begin{pmatrix}3\\
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\end{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{1}^{k_{1}}\varepsilon_{2}^{k_{2}}\varepsilon_{3}^{k_{3}}w_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}.

It satisfies

(6.11)

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})\mathcal{R}+q(x)\mathcal{R}=-au^{3}+a\big(\varepsilon_{1}v_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}v_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}v_{3}\big)^{3},&\text{in }(\Omega\setminus V)\times(0,T),\\
\mathcal{R}\arrowvert_{\Gamma}=0,&\textrm{on }\Gamma\times(0,T),\\
\mathcal{R}\arrowvert_{t\leq 0}=0,&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
\mathcal{R}\text{ subject to the Kirchhoff-Neumann condition on }V\setminus\Gamma.\end{cases}

Applying Proposition
6.1
, estimate (
6.10
), and the algebra property of
E^{1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}\|_{E^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{T}\|au^{3}+a(\varepsilon_{1}v_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}v_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}v_{3})^{3}\|_{E^{1}}

(6.12)

\displaystyle\leq C_{T}\|a\|_{E^{1}}\|\mathcal{F}\|_{E^{1}}\|u^{2}+u(\varepsilon_{1}v_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}v_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}v_{3})+(\varepsilon_{1}v_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}v_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}v_{3})^{2}\|_{E^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{T}\|a\|_{E^{1}}^{2}\|f\|_{H^{3/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})}^{5}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 6.4
.

Let the assumptions and notation of Proposition
6.3
hold.
Assume additionally that
f_{j}\in H^{3+1/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})
for
j=1,2,3
.
Let
h\in H^{3+1/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})
be compactly supported in
(0,T)
with sufficiently small
H^{3/2}
norm, and let
v^{h}
be the solution of

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})v(x,t)+q(x)v(x,t)=0,&x\in\Omega\setminus V,t\in(0,T),\\
v(\cdot,t)|_{\Gamma}=h(t),&t\in(0,T),\\
v(x,t)|_{t\geq T}=0,&x\in\Omega,\\
v\textrm{ subject to the Kirchhoff--Neumann condition on }V\setminus\Gamma.\end{cases}

Then

(6.13)

-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}h\partial_{\varepsilon_{1}}\partial_{\varepsilon_{2}}\partial_{\varepsilon_{3}}\Lambda(\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}f_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}f_{3})\big|_{\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon_{3}=0}=6\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}av^{h}v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}.

Proof.

We recall the finite difference operator: for a function
g
depending on
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3})
,

D^{3}_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3}}\big|_{\varepsilon=0}g(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}\varepsilon_{3}}\sum_{\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{3}}(-1)^{3+|\sigma|}g(\sigma_{1}\varepsilon_{1},\sigma_{2}\varepsilon_{2},\sigma_{3}\varepsilon_{3}).

By
[
32
, Lemma 10]
and the expansion in Proposition
6.3
,

D^{3}_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3}}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}+q)u=6(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}+q)w_{1,1,1}+D^{3}_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3}}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}+q)\mathcal{R}.

Integration by parts against
v^{h}
gives

-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}v^{h}D^{3}_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3}}\Lambda(\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}f_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}f_{3})\\
=6\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}v^{h}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}+q)w_{1,1,1}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}v^{h}D^{3}_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3}}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}+q)\mathcal{R}.

By recalling that
(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}+q)w_{1,1,1}=-av_{1}v_{2}v_{3}
and integrating by parts once more, we obtain

-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}hD^{3}_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3}}\Lambda(\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}f_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}f_{3})\\
=6\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}av^{h}v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}v^{h}D^{3}_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3}}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}+q)\mathcal{R}.

It remains to show that the remainder term vanishes in the limit
\varepsilon_{j}\to 0
.
Since

(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}+q)\mathcal{R}=-au^{3}+a(\varepsilon_{1}v_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}v_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}v_{3})^{3},

the estimate (
6
) implies

\big\|D^{3}_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3}}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}+q)\mathcal{R}\big\|_{E^{0}}\leq\frac{C_{T}}{\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}\varepsilon_{3}}\|a\|_{E^{1}}^{2}\|\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}f_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}f_{3}\|_{H^{3/2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma|})}^{5}.

Therefore, taking
\varepsilon_{j}\rightarrow 0
,
j=1,2,3
, we obtain (
6.13
).
∎
