A Burst-Learning Electrocardiogram Curriculum for Internal Medicine Residents
Authors: Theodora C. Esomonye, Alladdin Makawi, Ridwan Azees, Jalal K. Ghali, Kisha Burgess, Ndausung Udongwo, Judith Volcy, Melvin R. Echols
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInternal medicine residents frequently encounter arrhythmias on admission and telemetry, yet studies consistently demonstrate suboptimal electrocardiogram (ECG) interpretation accuracy and inadequate formal training.InterventionWe implemented a 14-week structured “burst-learning” ECG curriculum at a single safety-net academic internal medicine residency, comprising twelve 15- to 30-minute case-based sessions covering 20 high-acuity rhythms aligned with American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines.A 20-item knowledge quiz and scenario-linked 5-point Likert confidence survey were administered before and after the curriculum (unpaired cohorts).ResultsTwenty-six residents completed baseline and 15 completed postcurriculum assessments. Mean confidence rose from 2.81 ± 0.94 to 3.48 ± 0.87 (P = 0.03); knowledge scores did not change meaningfully (from 7.38 ± 2.35 to 7.87 ± 1.64; P = 0.45). Postcurriculum status independently predicted higher confidence (β = 0.61; 95% CI: 0.04-1.19; P = 0.038).ConclusionsA brief, low-cost burst curriculum was associated with increased resident ECG confidence and was durably incorporated into the residency schedule. However, diagnostic accuracy gains will require a longer dose and paired measurement.

Accurate and timely electrocardiographic (ECG) interpretation is a frontline competency for internal medicine (IM) trainees, who are often the first clinicians to evaluate inpatient rhythm abnormalities and activate acute care pathways.1 Nevertheless, published evaluations across the U.S. and international training programs consistently report a median ECG interpretation accuracy of approximately 40% to 58% among IM and emergency medicine residents, with few training programs hosting dedicated ECG education. A recent multicountry survey of IM and emergency medicine residents reported that 36% of trainees had ever attended a formal ECG course, with more than half rating their prior ECG training as inadequate.2 Misinterpretation of acute findings, such as ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), ventricular tachycardia (VT), and high-grade atrioventricular (AV) block, has been associated with delays in reperfusion, escalation, and definitive therapy.3

Within the U.S. Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) framework, ECG competency is embedded in IM milestones but is not operationalized through a mandated curriculum, resulting in substantial variability across programs.4, 5, 6 At our safety-net academic program, informal faculty review and resident surveys identified low baseline confidence in arrhythmia management and no formal longitudinal ECG curriculum beyond Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS) certification. The present quality improvement (QI) initiative aimed to implement a brief, recurring, case-based ECG curriculum and to determine whether it improved resident confidence and interpretive performance for high-acuity rhythms.6, 7, 8, 9, 10

Project Rationale and Aim

A departmental needs assessment revealed 3 gaps: no structured ECG curriculum, low resident confidence in managing acute arrhythmia, and inconsistent rhythm recognition during overnight and telemetry coverage. Based on evidence from simulation-based and case-based interventions, we created a 14-week “burst-learning” ECG curriculum to boost confidence and interpretation skills among IM residents in a safety-net hospital.

We hypothesized that a frequent, brief, case-based approach would boost self-reported confidence faster than knowledge scores. We used spaced repetition, immediate feedback, and decision-making exercises to improve critical thinking and pattern recognition. This QI project focused on trainee outcomes and implementation success, with patient safety considered a long-term effect rather than an immediate result.

Methods

Setting and participants

The project was conducted within the Morehouse School of Medicine Internal Medicine Residency Program at Grady Memorial Hospital, a large, urban safety-net academic teaching hospital in Atlanta, Georgia. From March to June 2025, all 44 categorical IM residents (postgraduate year [PGY]–1 to PGY-3) were invited to take part. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, using self-generated participant codes. Because sessions and assessments were administered during existing conference blocks, residents on night float, off-site rotations, vacation, or urgent clinical duties were not always available to attend or complete surveys. The program received Institutional Review Board review and was granted exempt status as an educational QI initiative.

Intervention: burst-learning ECG curriculum

The intervention consisted of 12 faculty- or senior-fellow–led sessions, each 15 to 30 minutes in duration, delivered across 14 weeks during regularly scheduled morning report and noon conference blocks. Session content covered the 20 high-acuity rhythm and ischemic patterns most likely to require immediate clinical intervention, prioritized from the 2017 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology/Heart Rhythm Society guidelines for management of ventricular arrhythmias,11 the 2019 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/Heart Rhythm Society guideline for supraventricular tachycardia,12 and the 2022 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions chest pain guideline for acute ischemia.13 Targeted topics included the following: anterior, inferior, lateral, and posterior STEMI; non–ST-segment elevation ischemic patterns; bradyarrhythmia (sinus bradycardia, second- and third-degree AV block); supraventricular tachyarrhythmias (atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia); ventricular tachyarrhythmias (monomorphic VT, polymorphic VT, torsades de pointes, ventricular fibrillation); left and right bundle branch block; left and right ventricular hypertrophy; hyperkalemic ECG patterns; and acute pericarditis and ECG patterns that may accompany large pericardial effusion, including low QRS voltage or electrical alternans. Tamponade was reviewed as a hemodynamic diagnosis rather than an ECG diagnosis.

Each session used a standardized 4-part structure: 1) clinical vignette with real 12-lead ECG or telemetry strip; 2) guided rhythm recognition and differential; 3) discussion of first-line management actions; and 4) facilitator-led debrief. The “burst” format, rather than high-fidelity manikin simulation, was selected to align with scheduling constraints and to maximize exposure frequency.10

Measures

The primary outcome was mean self-reported confidence in managing high-acuity ECG scenarios, measured on a 5-point Likert scale across 20 cases. The secondary outcome was total score on a 20-item ECG knowledge quiz (range: 0-20), administered before and after the curriculum. Surveys were anonymous and unpaired. The pre- and postcurriculum responses were analyzed as independent cohorts.

Analysis

Continuous variables are shown as mean ± SD; categorical variables as count (%). Pre-post group comparisons used 2-sample t-tests (α = 0.05). Subgroup analyses assessed confidence based on ECG training and session attendance, pooling Likert responses. Confidence was modeled using multivariable linear regression, including postcurriculum status, prior ECG training, session attendance, and PGY level. Analyses were conducted in R version 4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). The QI pilot lacked power for small knowledge score effects, but confidence intervals are reported. Because participant responses across scenarios were not independent, robust standard errors were clustered at the resident level.

Results

Participants

Of 44 eligible residents, 26 (59%) completed the precurriculum assessment and 15 (34%) completed the postcurriculum assessment. Across responses pooled for subgroup analysis (N = 44 resident-encounter records), 17 (38.6%) were PGY-1, 14 (31.8%) PGY-2, and 13 (29.5%) PGY-3. Seventeen residents (38.6%) reported prior formal ECG training (eg, medical school elective, ACLS-plus course), and 22 (50.0%) attended more than one curriculum session (mean sessions attended: 1.45 ± 1.63, range: 0-8). Baseline and postcurriculum cohorts were similar in PGY distribution and prior ECG training, without statistically significant differences (Table 1). Confidence (primary measure)

Mean scenario-level confidence increased from 2.81 ± 0.94 precurriculum to 3.48 ± 0.87 postcurriculum, a mean difference of +0.67 points (P = 0.03). Confidence gains were concentrated in recognition of wide-complex tachyarrhythmias (VT/ventricular fibrillation), high-grade AV block, left ventricular hypertrophy, and ECG patterns associated with large pericardial effusion (Figure 1). Tamponade was reviewed as a hemodynamic diagnosis rather than an ECG diagnosis. Secondary outcomes

Attending more than one session (β = 0.52; P < .001) and prior ECG training (β = 0.63; P < .001) were also independent positive predictors. PGY-3 status was modestly negatively associated with confidence relative to PGY-1 (β = −0.23; P = 0.045), potentially reflecting better-calibrated self-assessment among senior residents. Given the exploratory nature of the analysis and limited sample size, regression results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Mean knowledge score increased marginally from 7.38 ± 2.35 (37% correct) to 7.87 ± 1.64 (39% correct), a mean difference of +0.48 (95% CI: −0.75 to 1.71; P = 0.45) and did not demonstrate a statistically significant or clinically meaningful change. All 12 sessions were delivered as scheduled. Half of the residents attended multiple sessions despite busy shifts. The curriculum is now part of the 2025 to 2026 IM residency schedule as a recurring protected-time block managed by a faculty–chief resident team. Session materials are stored for future use. No adverse events or negative feedback occurred; all comments praised the format and pacing.

Discussion

This single-center QI initiative introduced a brief, recurring ECG curriculum that resulted in a modest but statistically significant increase in self-reported confidence (mean: +0.67 points on a 5-point scale; P = 0.03). Confidence gains remained after adjusting for PGY level and prior ECG training. Knowledge scores did not change significantly, aligning with the literature that short educational doses boost confidence more than accuracy. Notably, longer curricula (≥12 months) with spaced retrieval and pre-post testing typically achieve improvements in accuracy.

The greatest increases in confidence were in high-acuity, low-frequency domains such as wide-complex tachyarrhythmia, high-grade AV block, and pericardial disease, including ECG findings that may accompany large effusion, where limited exposure makes coaching particularly valuable. Implementation findings included the following: 1) delivery within existing conference blocks without protected faculty funding, aiding transferability to resource-limited settings; 2) durable integration through ownership by faculty and chief residents, with reusable materials included in future schedules; and 3) a modest negative correlation between PGY-3 status and confidence, likely reflecting better calibration rather than skill loss. This approach aligns with a competency management system that addresses variability in cardiovascular training and offers a cost-effective way to improve ECG education, especially in programs serving high cardiovascular disease populations.14

Limitations

This project has limitations. The data are reported from a single-center, single-program QI initiative with a small sample (44 residents; 26 precurriculum and 15 postcurriculum respondents), and incomplete follow-up. Secondly, because the surveys were anonymous and unpaired, individual comparisons and formal retention analysis were not possible. Self-reported confidence measures are susceptible to response bias, and the lower postcurriculum response rate raises the possibility of response bias toward more engaged participants. The identical pre- and postknowledge quizzes might be biased toward improvement via recall, but no significant knowledge gain was observed, so this is unlikely to affect confidence results. We did not measure clinical outcomes such as time to escalate, treatment accuracy, or adverse events, so patient safety benefits are theoretical. Generalizability beyond this setting is uncertain. Future efforts will add paired assessments, longer follow-up with 6- and 12-month retention testing, and linkage to telemetry escalation metrics. Despite these limitations, this formal QI initiative for dedicated ECG education could improve patient safety in subsequent iterations of this curriculum.

Conclusions

The short, guideline-based ECG curriculum was practical and easy to implement, and it led residents to feel more confident in recognizing arrhythmias. However, knowledge scores did not really change during the brief intervention. Moving forward, it would be helpful to use a longer curriculum, pair assessments, and track clinical outcomes to get a clearer picture.
