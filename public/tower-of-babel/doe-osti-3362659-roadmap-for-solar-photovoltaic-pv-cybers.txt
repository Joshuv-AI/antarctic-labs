 vi

 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 As the solar energy sector continues to expand its integration into the broader energy infrastructure
alongside many legacy generation and other energy sources, this growth presents both unprecedented
opportunities and new risks. Solar photovoltaic (PV) energy’s increasing reliance on digital and cloud-
based technologies along with the uniquely interconnected systems across diverse stakeholders increases
complexity and creates an evolving grid attack surface for motivated cyber adversaries. Cyberattacks on
critical power infrastructure have the potential to undermine the ability to deliver safe, reliable, secure,
and cost-effective power to customers. They can cause disruptions in energy production and delivery,
damage to equipment, financial losses, and compromises in national security. Therefore, ensuring that
cybersecurity is an intrinsic design principle in solar energy systems is paramount to not only managing
risk but also guaranteeing the benefits and opportunities solar energy systems provide to support a more
resilient energy future.
 The importance of protecting solar energy systems is growing as solar deployments continue to rise
and as reliance on diverse energy sources grows. Solar energy and other variable resources are called
upon to provide critical energy generation and grid services. Cyberattacks on these systems, particularly
in aggregate, have the potential to cause increasingly larger scale disruptions and cascading effects on
interdependencies. Ensuring the cybersecurity of solar energy infrastructure is crucial to maintaining the
reliability and resilience of the energy supply. Protecting solar energy systems from cybersecurity threats
helps safeguard investments and maintain the economic viability of new energy generation projects. It is
not just the generation assets that must be secured; solar energy systems provide and rely on vast amounts
of data for intelligent operation. Cybersecurity measures are essential to protect these large streams of
data from unauthorized access, to prevent tampering, and to maintain data integrity—all of which are vital
for accurately monitoring, controlling, managing, and optimizing solar energy generation.
 There have been substantial advancements in cybersecurity within the solar energy sector since the
original Roadmap for Solar PV Cybersecurity was developed in 2017. [1] Key progress includes the
release of the updated IEEE 1547-2018 standard for interconnection of distributed energy resource (DER)
and the associated cybersecurity guide, IEEE 1547.3. [2] For device certification, an outline of
investigation was published, Underwriter’s Laboratory (UL) 2941, which provides basic and advanced
cybersecurity requirements for DER, inverter-based resources (IBRs), smart inverters, monitoring
devices, and control devices. [3] Cybersecurity risk evaluation tools customized to the solar industry have
been developed and piloted with industry partners. Offerings of communication equipment for inverters
have expanded to include more protocols that use encryption, authentication, and other state-of-the-art
advances. Regulatory frameworks have increasingly incorporated cybersecurity requirements and include
consideration of locally sourced energy assets. Targeted training programs have been established to
enhance the cybersecurity skills and awareness of professionals. A DER standards library has been
developed that integrates a collection of guidelines, reports, and documentation that are essential for
ensuring secure, optimized, and effective communication, cybersecurity, and safety of solar PV and other
DER systems. [4] These efforts have collectively strengthened the sector’s resilience against
cybersecurity threats and improved overall security.

 vii

 However, there are still key gaps and challenges that need to be addressed in industry and through
research efforts supporting the continuous improvement and maturity necessary across the sector.
Stakeholders must race to mature their cybersecurity capabilities as they combat a growing number of
real-world cybersecurity incidents that affect solar energy systems and mitigate the increasing scale of
vulnerabilities discovered and disclosed in key types of equipment.
 An overview of key challenges identified is provided below. Included are tables summarizing both
research challenges (Table ES-1) and implementation challenges (Table ES-2). The distinction is used to
highlight the difference between challenges, which require further investment to find solutions suitable
for or customized to the solar industry and adaptable to emerging technological advances, and challenges
that have well-accepted technical solutions but are not widely adopted by industry due to various
deployment barriers. Table ES-1 and Table ES-2 (a) summarize challenges identified in this Roadmap,
(b) recommend the timeline on which they should be addressed (e.g., near-term, mid-term, or long-term),
(c) provide insights and ideas for setting risk-based implementation goals that will mitigate risks
associated with any gaps, and (d) include additional details about the nature of the identified challenges.
 Collectively, this information presents a prioritized strategy, or time-phased roadmap, for maturing
the security of the solar industry and increasing engagement among solar energy stakeholders.
• Near-term (“do now”) challenges are intended to be things that can be addressed in the next 2–4
 years. These are things for which mature solutions exist but need to be better deployed in the solar
 industry or are resources that researchers can quickly develop for the solar industry based on the
 current state of the art.
• Mid-term (“do next”) challenges can be addressed in the next 4–7 years. These are technologies that
 may require more time for development and testing before they are mature enough to deploy. These
 challenges require regulatory or standardization support, which takes time to get approved or which
 will take time to socialize with industry to promote adoption.
• Long-term (“do later”) challenges may take over 7 years to effectively address. These include items
 like addressing emerging technologies and their applicability and impacts to solar energy.
 The intent of this Roadmap is not to be prescriptive, but rather to support sector efforts to identify and
prioritize next steps and to serve as a model for the solar industry as it works to strengthen its cyber
resiliency. Organizations, owners, operators, and industry stakeholders may adopt various aspects that
best meet their individualized needs. The United States Department of Energy (U.S. DOE), national
laboratories, university partners, and other researchers and practitioners may choose to address the
challenges that align with their unique organizational risks and overall missions. Both research and
industry challenges for solar PV systems and operations are summarized below.

 Roadmap for Solar Photovoltaics (PV) Cybersecurity
 A Vision for Improving Cyber Maturity of Distributed and Utility-scale
 Solar Photovoltaics Installations
 1. INTRODUCTION
 The solar energy sector is increasingly integrated with digital technologies that facilitate remote
monitoring, control, and automation. The rise of smart grid technologies and digital devices has enhanced
the performance and scalability of solar power generation and has expanded the cyber threat landscape.
Emerging technologies, such as smart inverters that enhance grid stability and operations and digital
integration that enables real-time data collection and management, increase the potential attack surface of
cyberattacks. Additionally, AI and machine learning are used for predictive maintenance and grid
management, while many technologies, such as blockchain, are emerging to secure transactions and data
integrity within solar energy systems. The overall goal is for this industry to enable immutability, ensure
provenance, and provide authentication and access control capabilities for users.
 Common attack vectors and techniques include phishing and social engineering to gain initial
network access, privilege escalation to compromise data confidentiality and system integrity, and
ransomware attacks that target data availability and confidentiality. Web interfaces used for remote
control and monitoring are vulnerable to web exploitation techniques such as structured query language
injection, cross-site scripting, and cross-site request forgery. Malware and viruses can disrupt operations
or steal sensitive information, while distributed denial of service attacks can overload the system with
traffic, causing service disruptions. Known vulnerabilities in industrial information technology (IT)
equipment, including routers and firewalls, have been exploited to disrupt operation technology (OT)
environments. These factors highlight the need for robust cybersecurity measures and continuous
monitoring to safeguard the solar energy sector against evolving cybersecurity threats.
 The 2017 Roadmap for Solar Photovoltaic (PV) Cybersecurity laid the foundation for securing PV
systems by focusing on stakeholder engagement, research and development (R&D), standards and
guideline development, and best practices [9]. The roadmap aimed to ensure that grid operators, system
owners, and aggregators could communicate securely and effectively using resilient networks. However,
the increasing complexity of technology and cybersecurity threats necessitates an updated approach. The
original roadmap provided a comprehensive strategy for 2017–2022, emphasizing the importance of
collaboration among industry stakeholders, developing and implementing cybersecurity standards, and
promoting best practices across the sector. As the landscape has changed, new challenges and
technologies have emerged, requiring a refreshed roadmap to address these developments and ensure
continued protection of solar energy systems.
 The purpose of this report is to provide an updated cybersecurity roadmap for the solar energy sector,
building on the foundation established in the 2017 roadmap. This updated document aims to outline the
current state of cybersecurity in the solar industry, identify new and emerging threats, and propose
actionable strategies and best practices for stakeholders to implement. It will also highlight the
importance of collaboration, continuous education, and adaptable regulatory frameworks in maintaining a
secure and resilient solar energy infrastructure. The updated roadmap is structured as follows:
• Introduce the current cybersecurity environment and the need for an updated approach due to
 evolving threats and advancements in technology
• Review national cybersecurity initiatives, technological advancements in solar energy, and the
 associated cybersecurity challenges
• Analyze current and emerging cybersecurity threats specific to solar energy systems

 1

• Outline state-of-the-art R&D and necessary areas of continued investment for enhancing security
• Review existing cybersecurity standards
• Discuss the need for further coordination to implement useful standards
• Detail the roles and responsibilities of various stakeholders in improving cybersecurity
• Conclude with a summary of key points, emphasizing the importance of proactive measures, and with
 an inclusion of a resources section with additional information and guidelines.
 This document assumes the following definitions:
• Vulnerability: A weakness, flaw, or gap in a system, network, or process that could be exploited by a
 threat to cause harm.
• Threat: Any potential event or actor that could exploit a vulnerability to cause harm to a system, data,
 or organization.
• Risk: The potential for loss or harm that arises when a threat exploits a vulnerability.

 2. NATIONAL ENERGY CYBERSECURITY EFFORTS
 The U.S. critical infrastructure sectors provide essential services that support all aspects of society.
Energy is one of 16 sectors, and it is uniquely positioned as an enabling function that is essential to the
operation of other critical infrastructure. The Energy Information Administration (EIA) identified that the
electricity sector produces 4,183 billion kilowatt-hours (kWh) in 2023. [10] Solar energy generation
accounted for 5.71% of that total (239 billion kWh) in 2023, which is approximately 25 times more than
the share of energy produced by solar just a decade earlier.a Some states with heavy adoption of solar
have seen hours-long periods of time with much higher solar generation, and in California, solar energy
(grid-scale PV, rooftop PV, and concentrated solar) accounted for 28.9% of the state’s electricity demand
in 2023. [11] Nevada, Massachusetts, Hawaii, and Vermont all had over 15% of their state’s total
generation produced by solar energy in 2023, demonstrating heavy adoption in geographically diverse
areas. [12] Without a stable energy supply, the health and welfare of American citizens are threatened,
and the U.S. economy cannot function. Solar is an increasingly important contributor to that energy
supply, and its contribution is only expected to rise as organizations work toward meeting state, federal,
and organizational energy generation targets.
 In the U.S., energy assets and critical infrastructure components are owned by private, federal, state,
and local entities. More than 80% of the country’s energy infrastructure is owned by the private sector.
[13] Because of this wide array of energy sector stakeholders, the development of public/private
partnerships is a valuable means by which the energy sector can realize security and resilience goals.
Additionally, the increased integration of advanced technologies into energy infrastructure has created a
larger cyberattack surface, which has led to more frequent and sophisticated attacks from nation states and
cyber criminals. In response, the government and private sectors continue to increase spending on
cybersecurity operations and maintenance. [14]
 The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) leads the federal government’s effort to ensure cyberattacks
do not catastrophically impact the energy sector. DOE provides support to the U.S. energy sector by
pursuing high-priority activities that are coordinated with the strategies, objectives, and activities of the
broader federal government and energy sector stakeholders, including leveraging people, partnerships,
and resources found in its various offices and national laboratories.
 2.1. National Cybersecurity Strategy
 The National Cybersecurity Strategy released in 2023 highlights the risk of increased digitization
across all areas of life. [15] The view of critical systems as a digital ecosystem aligns well with the key
challenges facing the solar energy industry. The increasing connectivity of solar systems to enable fast
access to data for many types of stakeholders is one example of the real-time global collaboration,
leveraging vast amounts of data and computing power acknowledged by the National Cybersecurity
Strategy as one of the components enabling a vision of a prosperous, connected future. This future, to be
successful, will depend on the cybersecurity and resilience of its underlying technologies and systems.
 The Strategy highlights that building a smart grid, powered by distributed locally sourced electricity
and balanced with intelligent systems, is key to a bright and resilient future of energy abundance.
However, some of the key challenges identified clearly apply to the solar industry. Too often, industry
layers new functionality and technology onto already intricate and brittle systems at the expense of
security and resilience. An attack on one organization, sector, or state can rapidly spill over to other
sectors and regions. The potential cost of attacks like this will only grow as interdependencies increase.
Malicious cyberspace activity has evolved from nuisance defacement to espionage and intellectual
property theft to damaging attacks against critical infrastructure. Once available only to a small number of

 The Strategy highlights this call to action:
 Every day, cyber defenders foil state-backed attacks and prevent criminal plots
 around the world. But the underlying structural dynamics of the digital

 secure and protected, and neither incidents nor errors cascade into catastrophic,
 systemic consequences.
 To accomplish this goal, the Strategy notes that two fundamental shifts are required in allocating
roles, responsibilities, and resources in cyberspace. First, it is necessary to rebalance the responsibilities to
defend cyberspace. Individuals, small businesses, state and local governments, and infrastructure
operators have limited resources and competing priorities, yet these actors’ choices can have a significant
impact on the nation’s cybersecurity. From the solar perspective, consideration must be given to the
original equipment manufacturers (OEMs), vendors, developers, integrators, owners, operators, and
connected utilities, the resources available to each of these stakeholders, and how cyber risk and
responsibilities can be equitably distributed. These all provide opportunities to strengthen public-private
relationships. The second shift required by the Strategy is to realign incentives to favor long-term
investments. This challenge is particularly poignant in the solar industry, in which goals to drive costs
down and encourage high rates of adoption may be at odds with cybersecurity measures that are seen as
cost adders. This Roadmap highlights how including cybersecurity considerations from the design phase
in solar energy systems can keep costs low and make security and resilience accessible for all.
 This Roadmap will highlight how existing efforts are addressing the strategy as well as gaps and
challenges that need to be addressed to develop tools, standards, and best practices for each pillar.
Therefore, the implementation of the national cyber strategy will be a crucial opportunity for those
involved in the solar industry.
2.1.1. DOE Cyber Strategy
 The 2024 DOE Cybersecurity Strategy presents a detailed plan to protect the nation’s energy
infrastructure from escalating cyber threats. [16] Central to the strategy is a robust risk management
framework designed to systematically identify, assess, and mitigate cybersecurity risks across the energy
sector. This framework aims to ensure a proactive stance in handling potential vulnerabilities and threats
to critical energy systems.
 A key focus of the Strategy is enhancing the resilience of energy systems and improving response
capabilities. DOE aims to ensure that energy infrastructure can quickly recover from cybersecurity
incidents, minimizing downtime and disruption. This not only involves advanced technological solutions
but also comprehensive response protocols and recovery plans.
 Collaboration and information sharing are pivotal elements of the Strategy. DOE emphasizes the
importance of increased cooperation between government agencies, private industry, and international
partners. Sharing threat intelligence and best practices will help create a unified defense against cyber
threats. Additionally, the Strategy highlights the need for continuous innovation and research, investing in
cutting-edge cybersecurity technologies to stay ahead of emerging threats.

 4

 Workforce development is another critical area, with DOE prioritizing education, training, and
recruitment to build a skilled cybersecurity workforce. This approach ensures a steady pipeline of
qualified professionals capable of addressing complex cybersecurity challenges. Finally, the Strategy
underscores the importance of strengthening requirements, standards, and governance structures to
support effective cybersecurity practices and compliance throughout the energy sector. The overarching
goal is to create a resilient, secure, and adaptable energy infrastructure capable of withstanding the
evolving landscape of cyber threats.
 The Strategy addresses many challenges that are acutely felt in the solar industry along with the rest
of the energy sector. Collaboration, information sharing, investment in research and innovation,
workforce development, and improved implementation and enforcement structures to support
cybersecurity for solar deployments are all needed and in alignment with the challenges identified in this
Roadmap.
 2.2. Existing and Related Cybersecurity Projects
2.2.1. Critical Energy Cybersecurity Accelerator
 Designed to bolster emerging technologies, the Critical Energy Cybersecurity Accelerator aims to
identify the most urgent security gaps in the modern electrical grid and expedite disruptive solutions to
market. Critical Energy Cybersecurity Accelerator cohorts go through an accelerator period of 3–12
months, sharing ideas and threat intelligence before validating solutions in the lab. These cohorts work
with the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) to use its grid simulation environment to evaluate the
efficacy of different innovative cybersecurity technologies, referred to as solutions. These evaluations are
conducted, each centered around a theme such as authentication and authorization. Future cohort themes
will address a wide range of industrial control system (ICS) cybersecurity challenges facing the future
electric grid based on the most pressing cybersecurity challenges encountered by utilities and DOE
partners. [17]
2.2.2. Grid Modernization Cybersecurity Initiatives
 As the energy grid evolves to accommodate emerging technologies, cybersecurity remains a critical
challenge in ensuring resilience and reliability. In fiscal year 2024, the DOE Grid Modernization
Laboratory Consortium funded two cybersecurity-standards-related projects. Both projects focus on
cybersecurity standardization addressing different but complementary aspects of the modern grid
infrastructure. The first, “Assessment and Coordination of DER Cybersecurity Standard,” aims to unify
cybersecurity standards for DERs, ensuring a cohesive security approach across diverse technologies such
as solar, battery storage, and smart inverters. [18] The other, “Assessment and Coordination of Electric
Vehicle Supply Equipment (EVSE) Cybersecurity Standards,” emphasizes harmonizing cybersecurity
standards for electric vehicle supply equipment to enhance certification processes and establish a
standardized cybersecurity framework for electric vehicle charging infrastructure. [19] Both initiatives
share a common goal: mitigate cybersecurity risks by addressing gaps and inconsistencies in existing
standards, fostering collaboration among industry stakeholders, and promoting secure-by-design
principles. Together, these initiatives contribute to a more secure, resilient, and modernized power grid by
developing harmonized cybersecurity requirements, certification programs, and testing protocols.
2.2.3. Solar Energy Cybersecurity Research
 Research projects sponsored by the DOE Solar Energy Technologies Office (SETO) addressed the
specific needs and considerations for solar PV cybersecurity, making significant progress since the last
roadmap was published in 2017. Other state and national organizations have taken on challenges specific
to solar cybersecurity too. Outcomes, tools, and resources from these projects will be discussed in the
appropriate areas of the R&D Section in this report, but this section highlights efforts at the project level.

 5

 Securing Solar for the Grid [20]: The Securing Solar for the Grid (S2G) project is a national
laboratory-led research effort focused on achieving the highest level of cybersecurity maturity of solar
technologies, including equipment, digital supply chains, and bulk and distribution electric power grid
facilities. S2G is a collaborative effort among national laboratories to address gaps in cybersecurity
standards, best practices, testing, and analysis for solar PV and DERs. The S2G project is working to
ensure the safety and security of the electric grid with high levels of solar PV and other forms of DERs by
developing a national cybersecurity certification standard for DER and merging this standard with other
existing cybersecurity guidelines and recommendations to make it easier and cheaper for solar to securely
integrate with the grid. S2G supports national laboratories leadership in three research areas:
• Standards Development and Best Practices: Stakeholder engagement is conducted to investigate gaps
 in existing standards and develop best practices that can become standards to enable the secure
 integration of inverter-based resources (IBRs) and DERs.
• Cybersecurity Tool Kit and Supply Chain: Teams conduct research and develop tools to understand
 stakeholders’ cybersecurity posture, risk assessments to inform investments, and device design
 security and maturity model for cyber supply chain and supply chain testing.
• Education and Workforce Training: Educational modules and training development is conducted to
 increase cybersecurity awareness and knowledge among solar stakeholders.
 Cybersecurity Advisory Team for State Solar [21]: This is a project implemented by the National
Association of State Energy Officials and NARUC to mitigate cybersecurity risks and consequences in
solar energy developments. With support from DOE SETO, the project leverages state, federal, and
private-sector expertise on cybersecurity, grid, and PV to identify model solar-cybersecurity programs
and actions for states to take in partnership with utilities and the solar industry. National Association of
State Energy Officials, NARUC, and their members seek to proactively address cyber threats in all energy
areas, including solar infrastructure.
 The project uses a state-led advisory group and dialogue with solar and cybersecurity experts to
advance education and access to technical assistance. The project seeks to develop actionable solar-
cybersecurity strategies and roadmaps, as well as create stronger public-private partnerships and intra-
and interstate cooperation for greater consumer and utility solar cybersecurity.
 Essential Grid Operations from Solar [22]: The Essential Grid Operations from Solar (EOS)
project is a national laboratory-led research and industry engagement effort that aims to expedite the
development and adoption of reliability standards for IBR integrating into electric power systems. The
EOS project aims to speed up power systems’ reliability standards development by addressing short-term
standards needs while considering long-term gaps for future standards. The scope of EOS encompasses
standards topics for system stability, planning of bulk power and distribution systems, system protection,
system inertia, and other technical issues. These standards will impact the design, manufacture, testing,
and certification of equipment, as well as their performance, interconnection, and operation in the nation’s
power grid.
 SolarSnitch [23]: SolarSnitch is a technology developed by Sandia National Laboratories, which
aims to detect cyberattacks through inspection of cyber and physical data in PV smart inverters using
supervised and unsupervised machine learning algorithms capable of online learning. [24] This bump-in-
the-wire technology for monitoring smart inverter communications processes information using deep
packet inspection. The technology was selected in the 2024 Technology Commercialization Fund Base
Annual Appropriations Core Laboratory Infrastructure for Market Readiness lab call to mature and
prepare it for commercialization.

2.2.4. Other Related Research
 The DOE Office of Cybersecurity, Energy Security, and Emergency Response (CESER), established
in February 2018, has the task of improving energy infrastructure security and supporting DOE’s national
security mission. Furthermore, CESER leads DOE’s emergency preparedness efforts and coordinates
responses to energy sector disruptions, including physical and cyberattacks, natural disasters, and man-
made events. CESER also invests in R&D by private industry and the national laboratories for the next
generation of advanced technologies. CESER develops and supports numerous programs, including
public-private data sharing and analysis platforms such as the following:
 Cyber-Informed Engineering [25]: Cyber-informed engineering (CIE) is a proactive approach to
cybersecurity that integrates engineering controls from the early design stages of infrastructure systems to
mitigate the highest consequence events resulting from cyber incidents. By considering potential worst-
case scenarios during the design phase, CIE aims to engineer out cyber risk, creating resilient systems
designed to withstand modern and future cyber threats. Key aspects include targeting both threats
(adversary actions and exposure) and vulnerabilities (system weaknesses), recognizing that while these
threats may not be directly controllable, it is possible to manage exposure to reduce risks.
 The Cybersecurity Risk Information Sharing Program [26]: Managed by the Electricity
Information Sharing and Analysis Center (E-ISAC), the Cybersecurity Risk Information Sharing Program
demonstrates a public-private partnership meant to enable the exchange of classified and unclassified
threat information.
 The Cyber Testing for Resilience of Industrial Control Systems program [27]: The Cyber
Testing for Resilience of Industrial Control Systems program inventories and tests energy sector digital
components to correlate with cyber threat and supply chain information. Through partnerships with six
national laboratories, the program partners with technology developers, manufacturers, asset owners and
operators, and interagency partners to perform expert testing of devices and inform improvements in
component design and manufacturing.
 The Cybersecurity Capability Maturity Model [28]: The Cybersecurity Capability Maturity Model
(C2M2) is a free tool available to help organizations evaluate their cybersecurity capabilities and optimize
security investments. It uses a set of industry-vetted cybersecurity practices focused on IT and OT assets
and environments.
 Cybersecurity for the Operational Technology Environment [29]: This initiative is a high-priority
CESER investment to enhance energy sector threat detection of anomalous behavior potentially indicating
malicious cyber activity in OT networks. The research initiative aims to develop tools and capabilities
that can provide energy asset owners and operators with timely alerts and actionable information.
 2.3. Supporting Organizations
 Coordination and collaboration with other stakeholders in the critical infrastructure protection (CIP)
mission space will strengthen solar industry and DOE efforts for cybersecurity. On November 16, 2018,
President Trump signed into law the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency Act of 2018. This
landmark legislation elevated the mission of the former National Protection and Programs Directorate
within the Department of Homeland Security and established the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure
Security Agency (CISA), which includes the National Cybersecurity and Communications Integration
Center. [30]

 7

 The U.S. Department of Homeland Security CISA works with industry and state, local, tribal, and
territorial governments to secure critical infrastructure and information systems. CISA also provides
information on emerging threats and hazards so that appropriate actions can be taken, as well as ICS tools
and training to partners to help those in government and industry manage the risks to their assets, systems,
and networks. By partnering with CISA, DOE can leverage these resources to enhance solar cybersecurity
through joint risk assessments, information sharing, and participation in specialized training programs..
 The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) regulates the interstate transmission of
electricity, among other things. FERC’s Office of Energy Infrastructure Security provides leadership and
expertise and helps the Commission identify, communicate, and mitigate potential cyber and physical
risks. FERC has statutory authority to oversee the reliability of the power grid and does so through its
approval of North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC), which has created and enforced
mandatory reliability standards. Collaboration between DOE and FERC can ensure that solar-
cybersecurity initiatives align with reliability standards and incorporate best practices for mitigating risks
to DERs
 NERC is a not-for-profit international regulatory authority whose mission is to ensure the effective
reduction of risks to the reliability and security of the grid within the continental United States and other
jurisdictions. NERC operates the E-ISAC, which gathers and analyzes security data, shares appropriate
data with stakeholders, coordinates incident management, and communicates mitigation strategies with
stakeholders. E-ISAC, in collaboration with DOE and the Electricity Subsector Coordinating Council,
serves as the primary security communications channel for the electric industry. NERC conducts industry-
wide security exercises such as GridSecCon and GridEx. DOE can work with NERC and E-ISAC to
integrate solar-specific considerations into reliability planning, participate in joint exercises, and
strengthen information-sharing channels for emerging threats.
 Through these partnerships, DOE can help build a more resilient energy ecosystem, ensuring that
cybersecurity measures for solar technologies are aligned with broader efforts to protect critical
infrastructure.
 2.4. Regulation
 Regulation of IBR assets in the energy sector is evolving, particularly with the recent FERC order
directing NERC to identify and register owners and operators of currently unregistered bulk power
system-connected IBRs, which NERC is working to execute by 2026. [31,32] This shift, requiring IBR
generators above 20 MW to register with NERC, is poised to impact a wide spectrum of solar producers
and aggregators, compelling them to adhere to NERC requirements, which may eventually include NERC
CIP standards. Under these regulations, affected entities must register with NERC, and may soon be
required to implement robust cybersecurity measures and prepare for regular audits to demonstrate
compliance. DOE is actively providing resources to assist entities in meeting these new requirements,
highlighting the increasing imperative to secure the integration of DERs into the national grid.
 Government agencies, regulatory bodies (e.g., NERC and FERC), and industry associations play
pivotal roles in key recommendations, standards, and guidelines shaping the cybersecurity landscape of
the energy sector. NERC oversees the development and enforcement of the primary cybersecurity
regulatory framework—CIP standards—ensuring entities comply with a rigorous set of requirements
aligned to industry standards frameworks to safeguard the bulk power system. FERC oversees and
governs NERC development and enforcement of the CIP standards, even directing action in some cases.

 8

 NERC CIP standards dictate essential security management controls, incident response protocols, and
personnel training requirements crucial for protecting critical infrastructure. Key regulations and
guidelines shape the cybersecurity landscape of the energy sector. The National Institute of Standards and
Technology Cybersecurity Framework provides a voluntary structure for managing cybersecurity risk,
complemented by the MITRE ATT&CK framework, which details adversary tactics, techniques, and
procedures. [33,34] International standards, such as ISO 27001, and certification frameworks assess and
certify the maturity of information security management systems within organizations. [35] These
frameworks, along with exercises like GridEx and programs like Liberty Eclipse, underscore the sector’s
commitment to fortifying defenses against cyber threats and maintaining the reliability and security of the
power grid.
 One of the challenges moving forward is conditioning the government and private-sector parties to be
proactive in their adoption of strong cybersecurity controls in all of these areas. Regulation sets a baseline
for cybersecurity, but it does not mitigate all cyber risk. Regulation in combination with an organizational
risk management program that considers the cyber risk tolerance level unique to each entity will help shift
mentalities from a reactive approach to a proactive and engaged approach.

 3. SOLAR ENERGY TECHNOLOGY LANDSCAPE
 This section provides a baseline description of solar PV technology and architectures, with a focus
on the communications and digital equipment required to enable business functions. From a cybersecurity
perspective, while it is important to know how solar generation works to understand the criticality of
various components, the key considerations for vulnerability assessment and mitigation will be related to
the digital control, monitoring, and communication required for both distributed and bulk solar
generation.
 3.1. Solar PV Energy Generation
 Solar PV technologies convert solar irradiance into electric energy by absorbing sunlight through the
PV cells in the panels. The U.S. EIA explains solar PV generation of energy as follows: A PV cell is
made of semiconductor material. When photons strike a PV cell, they will reflect off the cell, pass
through the cell, or be absorbed by the semiconductor material. Only the photons that are absorbed
provide energy to generate electricity. When the semiconductor material absorbs enough solar energy,
electrons are dislodged from the material’s atoms. Special treatment of the PV cell’s surface during
manufacturing makes the front surface of the cell more receptive to the dislodged, or free, electrons so
that the electrons naturally migrate to the surface of the cell.
 The movement of electrons, which all carry a negative charge, toward the front surface of the PV cell
creates an imbalance of electrical charge between the cell’s front and back surfaces. This imbalance, in
turn, creates a voltage potential similar to the negative and positive terminals of a battery. Electrical
conductors on the PV cell absorb the electrons. When the conductors are connected in an electrical circuit
to an external load, such as a battery, electricity flows through the circuit.
 The PV cell is the basic building block of a PV system. Individual cells can vary from 0.5 to 4.0
inches across. However, one PV cell can only produce 1–2 watts, which is only enough electricity for
small uses, such as powering calculators or wristwatches.
 PV cells are electrically connected in a packaged, weather-tight PV panel (sometimes called a
module). PV panels vary in size and in the amount of electricity they produce. The electricity-generating
capacity for PV panels increases with the number of cells in the panel or in the surface area of the panel.
PV panels can be connected in groups to form a PV array. A PV array can be composed of as few as two
PV panels to as many as hundreds of PV panels. The number of PV panels connected in a PV array
determines the amount of electricity the array can generate. [36] The progression from cells to arrays is
shown in Figure 1.

Figure 1. Components of solar array.

 PV cells generate direct current (DC) electricity. DC electricity can be used to charge batteries that
power devices that use DC electricity. Nearly all electricity is supplied as alternating current (AC) in
electricity transmission and distribution systems. Inverters are used on PV panels or in PV arrays to
convert the DC electricity to AC electricity.
 3.2. Overview of System Boundaries
 To harden solar PV systems to different cybersecurity threats, it is important to understand modern
plant design. Solar PV generation is very modular, which allows panels, strings, and arrays of solar PV to
be built with varying numbers of units to create the desired nameplate capacity of the system. Due to the
modularity, there are a variety of configurations and architectures that may be used to monitor and control
solar PV systems. This report focuses on two primary architectures: (1) distributed solar PV systems, such
as those found on rooftop solar systems, and (2) utility-scale, high voltage-connected solar PV sites.
There are notable similarities in these systems, but there are also key differences that affect the cyber
threat surface for these systems.
3.2.1. Distributed Solar PV Architecture
 Distributed solar PV applications are not limited to rooftop solar. Community solar, commercial
solar, or even distribution-utility operated solar PV installations connected at the distribution voltage can
all be considered distributed solar. This section focuses on a simple, behind-the-meter application as
additional features are highlighted in utility-scale architecture descriptions.
 The solar panels are connected to an inverter, utilizing a maximum power point tracking algorithm
integrated either in the inverted or through a separate device. Maximum power point tracking technology
is a DC-to-DC converter that optimizes the production of PV panels. In most behind-the-meter solar PV
systems, the panels are mounted directly to a hard surface, such as a roof, and do not require the angle of
the panels to be adjusted in relation to the movement of the sun. The inverter converts the solar energy to
AC energy, which may be used to provide energy for household appliances or in the case of over-
production, connected to the grid. Meters collect data for the integrator, utility, and homeowners to view
for various purposes, often sending data to a central platform in the cloud. These key components are
shown in Figure 2.

Figure 2. Example of a home solar system architecture.

 11

 States, counties, and different distribution utilities or cooperatives have varying policies and programs
on metering for behind-the-meter generation. In some cases, a single meter may be used, tracking the net
power production or consumption. In other cases, separate tracking of the solar generation and the total
load may be required, meaning that additional metering infrastructure is required. These setups may also
vary based on the type of aggregated system operations that are in place. In some cases, behind-the-meter
solar PV owners may interact directly with their utilities. In other cases, aggregators may be involved in
managing multiple distributed installations in an area, streamlining interactions for utilities. Often, the
integrator may maintain a connection to the system to provide maintenance or other services, or contracts
may even be set up such that the property owner leases the solar PV equipment from the integrator, who
is also the true owner of the system. These varying stakeholder interactions and agreements may affect
the metering and controls infrastructure. Lack of standardization of agreement types, monitoring
requirements, and infrastructure is a challenge. Battery systems are commonly used in combination with
distributed solar applications to use locally produced energy and store it for times during the day (or
night) when solar generation is unavailable, but that stored energy can continue to be used to serve local
loads. More complex metering and management systems and hybrid inverters may be used for combined
solar and battery systems. These systems have similar cybersecurity considerations, but any additional
complexity should be considered in the overall cybersecurity risk assessment.
 Managing battery charging and discharging is one of the primary functions of a control system, but it
can also be used to curtail solar energy production and protect the solar panels against back-feeding of
energy. Cases in which distributed solar PV systems can be used to provide grid services, the controller
will also process signals from the grid and send commands to the inverter about desired real and reactive
power output to provide those grid services. In addition to processing local control loops based on input
signals and control signals from the grid operator (if enabled), the control system typically has a wireless
connection (often using the user’s Wi-Fi system or a cellular modem) that sends production data and other
asset health information to the vendor or installer. The vendor or installer typically makes this information
available to the owner through a cloud service or a mobile application. Sometimes, using the vendor-
provided cloud service is the only way for owners to access production information. Although utilities
will typically have their own meter, they may also get access to the vendor’s data, often with required
consent from the owner, through the cloud service.
3.2.2. Utility-Scale Solar PV Architecture
 In terms of energy production, utility-scale solar PV systems function similarly to distributed solar
PV systems, but they typically have more ancillary equipment such as trackers or environmental
monitors. This ancillary equipment, while not directly associated with power production, is also remotely
accessed and must be considered for cybersecurity purposes. Solar panels, or strings of solar panels, are
connected to an inverter to collect solar energy. Inverters may also be strung together to create single
points of connection to higher voltage feeders. Strings of panels will be controlled by local controllers
measuring solar irradiance to track the progress of the sun and adjust the angle of the solar panels to face
directly into the sun to maximize energy production. See Figure 3.
 Each inverter will receive commands from the PV plant supervisory control and data acquisition
(SCADA) system, which in turn receives signals from a plant operations center, which may be operated
by a utility or by a third-party integrator. If the plant is not directly integrated with a utility control center,
then the remote plant operations center will have connectivity to the utility system operations center to
provide production information and coordinate any grid-support functions. Utilities will also likely use
traditional metering technologies at the point of connection that is integrated with their own distribution
or transmission SCADA systems.

3.2.2.1. Solar Plant Communications
 As solar generation increases to become a greater portion of the nation’s power supply, it is critical to
effectively monitor and control generation. Highly reliable communication infrastructure in solar plants
plays a key role in enabling the real-time operation, monitoring, and control of both solar PV plants and
the electric power grid to ensure grid stability, especially when combined with other resources, like
storage, wind, or vehicle charging. Solar systems are capable of providing a range of additional grid
services to include voltage regulation, frequency support, and ancillary services with effective
communication and control strategies. To enable this functionality, grid operators communicate to plant
controllers, which then communicate commands to each of the solar PV strings and any other co-located
hybrid resources. This section discusses the implementation of a network architecture that follows best
practices recommended for the industry. Network architectures may look very different based on the
business functions supported, vendor communication requirements, state and federal regulation,
equipment limitations, and other design constraints. This reference communications architecture
demonstrates consideration of cybersecurity practices while enabling key business functions.
 The plant controller is connected to grid operators either through dedicated communication lines or
public Internet. In either case, effective demilitarized zones should be created that facilitate secure control
system data access. Virtual Private Network (VPN) access or firewall rules can be established to allow
only authenticated users to send and receive data from the plant controller. These digital perimeter
defenses are essential to block adversary action or reconnaissance of the internal solar local area network
that runs from the plant controller to each of the solar arrays. A key aspect of a digital perimeter strategy
is micro-segmentation so that equipment with different access control permissions is segmented into its
own perimeter. With complex sites with a range of equipment types, such as trackers and inverters, it is
essential to properly configure firewall access independently to each, but this adds complexity and if not
properly configured can introduce vulnerabilities. The grid operators (e.g., aggregators, utilities,
independent system operators, regional transmission organizations) connect directly to the local SCADA
system through a firewall to send dispatch or grid service requests. Those commands are then interpreted

 13

by one or more servers as configured by the plant owner/operator. The plant operator may also send
dispatch commands depending on how agreements are set up with the utility. The local SCADA systems
relay commands to the individual inverters for strings of arrays and any other co-located resources.
Generally, these local communications are assumed to be secure because they are isolated from public
networks.
 Unlike traditional utility generation that does not require persistent remote access from multiple
stakeholders, many solar and other IBR OEMs maintain connectivity to their systems to provide asset
health monitoring or proactive maintenance services or to maintain the ability to push remote code
updates. Other stakeholders, such as integrators, developers, or service contractors may also maintain
persistent remote connectivity to support legitimate business functions and monitor data. These
connections increase the potential attack surface of the solar PV systems. In addition to a larger number of
connections and access points that could be compromised by a motivated adversary, disruption to any of
the third parties also has the potential to affect operations, either through a simple lack of availability of a
service or through compromise of that third party’s legitimate connection. Supply chain attack vectors,
such as SolarWinds or a compromised employee, must be considered when designing systems with
vendor access because the equipment owner has little visibility or control over this risk.
 Layers of communication are needed within solar plants to effectively manage the solar PV assets.
For example, local feedback loops sense the direction of the sun and actuate the solar panels for
maximum energy production. Communications with the utility enable the transfer of production data,
expected production, system health, and monitoring of electrical protection of the plant. This integration
is highlighted in Figure 4.
 Forecasting servers can serve multiple purposes, including communicating with the grid what the
expected output of the plant is on minutes-ahead, hours-ahead, or day-ahead increments, allowing the
utility to account for the variability of the generation and enabling the solar plant to bid into generation
markets. Additionally, forecasting servers may help account for incoming extreme weather, sending
signals to store the panels in optimal positions to protect them from high wind, hail, or other damaging
conditions.

Vendors, manufacturers, and other external collaborators may also need access to data to provide device

 3.3. Interconnected Technologies
 Several technologies outside of simple solar PV energy generation may be required for grid
integration and support features, especially if they are deployed in combination with other energy
technologies such as storage or other IBRs.
 Advanced distribution management system, a software that helps electric utilities manage and
optimize their distribution networks, as well as meet customer expectations of reliability, power quality,
data security, and resilience to cyber and natural threats.
 The “advanced” elements of an advanced distribution management system go beyond traditional
distribution management systems by providing next-generation control capabilities. It can help utilities
manage high penetrations of DERs, reduce outage costs and disruption, improve grid reliability and
operations, perform fault isolation and restoration, peak demand management, etc. [37]
 Advanced monitoring and prediction tools are used to forecast the amount of solar generation that
will be available, which is required to allow solar PV facilities to bid into energy markets and for utilities
to effectively manage the dispatch of other generation resources in their portfolio. These systems, often
cloud-based, use sophisticated weather prediction models updated with live measurements of solar
irradiance at physical generation sites. Tampering with these systems could cause financial impacts or
potentially larger disruptions depending on the system’s backup capacity.
 It is increasingly common to deploy solar in combination with other resources, most frequently
storage. This allows for smoothing of the variable output of solar generation. Even wind-solar plants
achieve a smoothing effect through the complementarity of the resources [38]. It is worth noting that
many considerations are as similar for these hybrid plants as they are for solar-only plants, allowing for
the increased complexity when more systems are introduced. Solar system architectures also have notably
similar architectures to other independent IBR deployments. Many of the challenges discussed in this
Roadmap can be applied to other IBR and hybrid energy systems too. Some specifics of the technology
and the landscape of stakeholders should be taken into account to appropriately prioritize challenges or
cybersecurity controls for different technologies.

 4. SOLAR CYBER THREAT LANDSCAPE
 The solar energy sector is rapidly evolving, incorporating emerging technologies such as Internet-
connected devices, smart inverters, and cloud-based management systems. These advancements enhance
optimization and control but also introduce significant cybersecurity challenges. Smart inverters and
network-connected devices enable real-time monitoring and control, while cloud-based management
systems offer remote access and data analysis for optimization. Dynamic export capabilities improve grid
integration and flexibility. However, these innovations create new attack vectors.
 Attack paths against solar energy systems have been demonstrated through real-world incidents,
researcher exploration, and disclosed vulnerabilities. While there is not significant evidence that threat
actors are specifically targeting solar systems, solar assets and solar companies have been growing targets
due to their association with critical infrastructure. As solar penetration continues to grow, the potential
consequence of an attack grows, and with it, the potential adversary interest in these assets. Denial-of-
service (DoS) and ransomware are common attacks across many sectors, and several incidents of this
nature have been reported to affect solar companies or organizations that manage solar assets. In multiple
cases, known vulnerabilities in IT equipment have been exploited in OT environments, leading to a loss
of view for operators or third parties. While these attacks may not affect power generation, they still
create operational risks for AOOs and may expose critical company data or personal data for employees
or customers.
 Similarities can be seen across many vulnerabilities that have been disclosed affecting solar energy
products, as discussed in the following section. The operating system command injection allows arbitrary
code execution and unauthorized commands. Hard-coded credentials or weak password requirements can
lead to unauthorized access—often default device credentials are never changed and can be found as
easily as looking through a device manual. Inadequate input sanitization in products enables command
injection and other exploits, which is particularly concerning if these interfaces are exposed to the public
Internet. Persistent Wi-Fi networks or other exposed connectivity points leave configurations accessible
to attackers. Until product security is prioritized across the board, it is likely that simple vulnerabilities
will continue to be found and may be increasingly exploited by motivated adversaries.
 Several governments across the world have acknowledged the rising threat to solar energy systems.
Agencies in Denmark, Germany, Australia, and the U.S. have published reports or released alerts
highlighting such potential threats and consequences. [39–44]. The industry is at a critical juncture,
needing to balance technological advancements with robust cybersecurity measures to protect against
these sophisticated threats.
 The following section discusses the three key elements of cyber risk: vulnerabilities, threats, and
consequences. For each risk factor, the current state and key considerations for solar cybersecurity are
presented, using real-world examples where applicable. Following this, a review of known incidents
affecting the solar industry is presented.
 4.1. Device Vulnerabilities
 In the rapidly evolving ecosystem of solar energy systems and their integration with Internet-
connected devices, the identification of several security vulnerabilities across various platforms and
products shows the need for an increase in awareness. In some cases, vulnerability discovery occurs
through standardized or research-driven assessments and allows vendors to create and deploy patches.
Sandia National Laboratories explored and documented a standardized process for discovery of PV
inverter vulnerabilities. [45] Adversaries can also discover issues through their own reconnaissance and
exploit actions.

 17

 Disclosed vulnerabilities do not necessarily indicate poor design or implementation from a particular
vendor, and in fact, self-disclosed vulnerabilities from a company are an indication of robust security-
oriented code review processes and of awareness and commitment to security. A summary of relevant
vulnerabilities disclosed for solar energy systems was published by DER Security, and a more detailed
review of solar vulnerabilities can be found in[X] [46]. Identification and disclosure of vulnerabilities
result in improvements to device and system security. However, not all companies have good
vulnerability reporting or disclosure response programs. Several vulnerabilities in residential solar
systems were reported in 2016 and 2017, related to open Wi-Fi access ports, open Secure Shell (SSH)
ports, hard-coded credentials, command injection vulnerabilities, and weak or misconfigured
authentication methods or password storage [47–49]. Some of these vulnerabilities were found to only
exist in development versions of products accidentally shipped to customers or were disputed by the
vendor. The vulnerabilities below highlight the themes and trends in vulnerabilities disclosed for solar
energy products.
 Research Challenge: Vulnerability vs. Weak Design (Challenge R3). There are
 increasing reports of vulnerabilities from researchers that are disputed by
 developers because these behaviors are part of the system design, not a flaw in
 design or execution. As with the example of the vulnerability assigned for not
 requiring strong passwords, these may be seen as features by designers or simply
 a lack of implementing best practices that does not rise to the level of
 vulnerability. There is still a research challenge to determine what should rise to
 the level of a disclosed vulnerability and to distinguish between vulnerabilities of
 this nature and simple lack of adherence to standards or best practices (which
 may not be mandatory).
 In other more recent examples, researchers have discovered vulnerabilities in residential solar systems
and solar monitoring tools that are available online. [50]
 Industry Challenge: Vulnerability Disclosure and Management (Challenge I1).
 Vulnerability management—the process of identifying, evaluating, treating, and
 reporting security vulnerabilities—is not a priority for many in the solar
 industry. New technologies and rapid growth allow for new and small companies
 to emerge. Many of these companies lack the resources to appropriately develop
 and validate meaningful security measures. Research from Sandia National
 Laboratories has provided best practices for solar manufacturers to identify,
 respond to, and responsibly disclose vulnerabilities. [51]
 CISA provides a well-developed process for disclosing and addressing
 vulnerabilities: (https://www.cisa.gov/coordinated-vulnerability-disclosure-
 process)
 While these standardized processes for vulnerability disclosure are incredibly
 valuable, the solar PV industry is still challenged to keep up with vulnerability
 reports since they are disclosed using standard common vulnerability and
 exposure formats, which are not tagged by industry. Alerts can be set to track
 particular vendors, but vulnerabilities associated with subcomponents or
 embedded services may still be difficult to track. Vendor agreements are one
 mechanism that can be leveraged to require disclosures to customers and
 support tracking embedded systems or products to a reasonable extent.

 18

 With many of these vulnerabilities discovered by researchers, access to the web interface or to the
local network on which the solar system was installed was necessary to exploit the vulnerabilities. This
highlights the importance between tracking vulnerabilities and tracking exploitability. A Vulnerability
Exploitability Exchange score is useful to accompany common vulnerabilities and exposures or other
software bill-of-materials (SBOM) tracking to examine not only the presence of a vulnerability, given the
presence of the device and a certain revision of hardware, software, or firmware, but also to evaluate how
significant that vulnerability is in the context of the environment. This concept works both ways.
Exploitability and vulnerability are both needed to create an attack surface for adversaries. One research
team found that over 130,000 solar energy systems had some sort of presence (usually a web interface) on
the public Internet. [52] While it is generally considered to be a best practice to ensure that these
interfaces are properly protected by passwords and other authentication methods and placed behind
firewalls to limit access, the mere presence of these systems on the Internet does not mean that they are all
vulnerable or could be misused by adversaries.
 Industry Challenge: Remote Access (Challenge I14). Remote access to solar PV
 systems is a functional requirement due to the distributed and sometimes
 geographically remote nature of PV deployments. Wired connections to all
 systems are impractical, and multiple stakeholders have legitimate business
 functions that require access to real-time data or controls. To support these
 requirements, remote access monitoring and control platforms have been
 developed, often with web interfaces. As seen in the description of vulnerabilities
 above, it is not uncommon to find web vulnerabilities in these platforms that can
 reveal sensitive data or grant unauthorized access to systems. This problem of
 vulnerabilities in remote access platforms is compounded by the exposure of
 many systems to the Internet. The large attack surface and apparent lack of
 security on some of these open web systems is an urgent challenge that can be
 addressed through applying best practices for web development and access
 control. OEM remote access to systems for asset maintenance and firmware
 updates also contributes to this risk. Firmware updates, if not properly tested and
 vetted for specific environments, have been known to cause disruptions in
 operational environments.
 Once identified, vulnerabilities within field devices will often use “over-the-air” updates to patch the
software and firmware. This involves a secure update and boot process, requiring additional security
elements to maintain the integrity of the update. For example, PV inverters need to create and maintain
strong Root of Trust, robust cryptographic key management, firmware recovery mechanisms, and
appropriate levels of logging for future auditing. This and other elements associated with the secure
updates and boot process for PV inverters are discussed in a Sandia National Laboratories report. [53]
 In multi-vendor environments, each vendor may use proprietary software, hardware, and
communication protocols, making it difficult to standardize security practices. Vendors may adhere to
different security standards, leaving gaps in the overall security posture. Unfortunately, some products
may lack robust security features, like encryption, and render the entire system vulnerable. Many of these
PV sites require separate management tools, which complicate vulnerability identification and patching.
Furthermore, coordinating updates or patches across the many vendors may be cumbersome and result in
delays or overlooked vulnerabilities. Some research has provided guidance in this area, but further
adoption by industry is needed to address this challenge. [54]

 19

 Industry Challenge: Multi-Vendor Integrations (Challenge I5). At utility-scale
 PV sites, the integration of products from multiple vendors is critical for
 managing PV and battery storage systems. Asset operators may work with
 several organizations, including the following:
 • Inverter company access to monitoring data for warranty and process
 control purposes
 • Tracker company access to implement hail mitigation strategies
 • Utility access for ongoing production and revenue data
 • Cybersecurity vendor access for ongoing system cyber maintenance
 • Equipment vendor access for software upgrades.
 However, the access required by these entities inherently increases the attack
 surface—the total number of potential entry points that a malicious actor could
 exploit. Effective vulnerability management is essential to mitigate the
 operational and security risks introduced by this complexity. This is a near-term
 requirement. Not having a solution results in ad hoc access strategies, including
 unrestricted VPN access, which subject the entire system to cybersecurity
 vulnerabilities.
 To ensure secure and reliable communications, vendors shall adopt protocols that enable secure
interoperability. This entails the implementation of protocols that include encryption, authentication, and
integrity checks to prevent tampering. Standardized methods often define performance metrics such as
latency, reliability, and bandwidth thresholds appropriate for the application. Resilience measures are also
valuable and include specifications of fallback modes and logging/monitoring for detecting anomalies.
 4.2. Established Threat Actors
 Cyber threats to the solar industry come from actors with varying motives and capabilities.
Nation-state groups, backed by significant resources and geopolitical objectives, represent the most
sophisticated adversaries. Criminal organizations, while generally less advanced, remain persistent and
financially motivated—though they may not be as selective about their targets. Understanding the
connection between the intent and the capability of the threat actor is essential for assessing risk and
prioritizing defenses.
 Criminal groups, often motivated by financial gain, possess advanced capabilities and resources,
sometimes purchasing exploits rather than developing them internally. They tend to target systems with
known vulnerabilities, focusing on financial institutions, healthcare providers, and other sectors where
data and system integrity are critical. Unlike individual hackers who might be driven by disruption,
criminal groups operate with clear financial incentives and often coordinate sophisticated and
well-planned attacks. One example of cyber criminals in action involved 16 Danish utilities targeted by a
coordinated attack on firewalls within their OT systems, exploiting a vulnerability that had been disclosed
just a few weeks prior. [55] Some of these utilities operated solar generation. Remediations were quickly
applied, but just a few weeks later, a second wave of attacks started, affecting several utilities over the
following days. These attacks also targeted the same brand of firewalls but used a previously undisclosed
vulnerability. It took a few days for the firewall vendor to address the vulnerability and release a patch.
Some of the utilities chose to shut down as many of their remote operations as possible, which is believed
to have disrupted the visibility into distributed generation assets. The compromised firewalls were used
for their computing power to then attack other targets. A similar objective was achieved through the
compromise of multiple ICS devices, including solar monitoring products, adding the affected devices to
Mirai botnets. As the devices joined the botnet, they were used to execute distributed DoS attacks and
other malicious activities, emphasizing the importance of timely patching and robust network security
practices to protect critical infrastructure from such widespread compromises. [56]

 Industry Challenge: Patch Management (Challenge I2). Even when patches for
 vulnerabilities are released, the Mirai botnet incident and firewall exploits
 highlight that patches are only effective when deployed in the operational
 environments. Given the diverse stakeholder landscape for solar energy, the
 industry needs to develop and apply best practices for applying patches quickly.
 The approach for this may vary based on the type of system (residential,
 aggregated, utility-scale), but solar has the unique opportunity to deploy patches
 compared to much of the energy sector. High availability requirements are
 important, but since solar energy (in most parts of the world) cannot produce for
 at least a few hours each day, there is an opportunity to apply patches during this
 “scheduled downtime.” Patches still need to be vetted and tested before they are
 applied. However, as compared to a thermal generation plant that might run for
 months continuously or even to wind energy that can be very intermittent at all
 times of the day, solar PV systems have regular opportunities to apply patches
 without affecting generation capacity. Dashboards to assist operators with
 tracking the disclosure of new vulnerabilities and affected products and versions,
 associated patches, and criticality of addressing issues can help operators
 prioritize patch management strategies. Additionally, open-source data can be
 collected to track patch release cycles for end-user and prospective customer
 awareness. Additional best practices for patch management for DER are
 highlighted in research done by Sandia National Laboratories. [57]
 Cyber-secure remote patch management and access controls are critical when
 multiple parties are involved, including asset owner, cybersecurity management
 vendor, and equipment vendor.
 These known incidents underscore the importance of understanding the role of IT equipment (e.g.,
firewalls) in OT systems. It is important to know all the devices that are on a system and how the devices
are configured to conduct good cyber hygiene and to apply patches for disclosed vulnerabilities.
Integrating OT and IT networks has exposed OT systems to various cyber threats, necessitating security
guidelines and tools, with dedicated firewalls for OT networks being proposed due to the different
properties of OT networks compared to IT networks. Conventional patching involving a device reboot
does not apply to OT devices controlling critical processes, necessitating in-memory vulnerability
patching. Control binaries in OT devices are often compiled by proprietary compilers, hindering the
patching process and relying on OT vendors for rapid vulnerability discovery and patch development. By
maintaining a comprehensive asset inventory and ensuring all devices are properly configured and
updated, organizations can better protect their OT systems from cyber threats and maintain robust
cybersecurity hygiene.
 Research Challenge: IT/OT Integration (Challenge R8). The blurring line
 between IT and OT networks presents emerging threats, highlighting the growing
 cybersecurity risks facing the OT environment. There is a well-known “IT/OT
 divide” in security management practices, but as the worlds of IT and OT come
 together in modern solar systems, a comprehensive approach that addresses this
 divide and considers holistic security measures is needed—not solely security
 solutions customized for the unique OT solar devices.
 Ransomware groups have become a significant threat within the scope of criminal organizations,
leveraging sophisticated tactics to breach confidentiality and disrupt operations. Critical infrastructure has
been a popular target for ransomware, and the solar sector has not been exempt. For example, a major
manufacturer was targeted by the LockBit 3.0 ransomware group in 2022. [58] As organizations become
more adept at maintaining backups, ransomware groups have adapted by stealing data and threatening to
release it unless the ransom is paid, thereby breaching both the availability and confidentiality of data.

 21

 Script kiddies or individual hackers, often motivated by curiosity or the thrill of exploration rather
than a specific cause, can also pose a threat. For instance, a cyberattack on a Utah-based independent
power producer managing wind and solar assets was targeted in March 2019 by a DoS exploitation of a
known vulnerability in a public-facing firewall. [59] This incident disrupted communications with several
solar and wind installations for short intervals. While the attack did not affect critical control systems or
power generation, it highlighted the potential impact of generic software vulnerabilities on utility
operations and underscored the need for robust cybersecurity measures across the sector.
 Nation-state organizations are considered the most sophisticated and well-resourced threat actors,
operating with the explicit or implicit support of governments. Their campaigns aim to disrupt or gather
intelligence on critical infrastructure, often with long-term objectives. While solar assets have not been
primary targets, attacks on broader grid systems, such as the 2015 and 2016 attacks on the Ukrainian
power grid or the 2022 attack on satellite infrastructure affecting European wind turbines, demonstrate
potential collateral impacts. [60–61] These actors’ capabilities and geopolitical motives make them a
persistent concern for the energy sector.
 Supply chain threats are also worth noting. Whether malicious or unintentional, opportunities exist for
vulnerabilities to be introduced into hardware or software during the design, manufacturing, assembly, or
shipping stages of development. The U.S. has a small but growing domestic manufacturing capacity for
solar PV systems, though supply chain attacks can impact even domestic design and manufacturing when
cyber threats are involved. Developers and integrators are encouraged to screen their suppliers using a
supply chain risk management program, building in protections and mitigations against a potentially
untrusted supply chain.
 Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) for distributed generation energy systems is generally limited and
even more so for solar PV systems. While large security companies may report on ICS or OT CTI trends
broadly, detailed trends specific to solar PV systems are often overlooked.
 Industry Challenge: Sector Specific CTI (Challenge I7). As the deployment and
 potential impact of solar energy systems continue to grow, the necessity for
 specific CTI focused on these systems becomes increasingly critical. Sector-wide
 CTI sharing is critical to combat mutual threats but encounters challenges in
 segregating and controlling intelligence dissemination, which impedes
 cooperation. Moreover, the integration of intelligent PV inverters and DERs
 introduces vulnerabilities that necessitate focused cybersecurity measures to
 ensure grid stability and resilience.
 The rising number of cyberattacks targeting PV systems underscores the urgency of robust
cybersecurity strategies to protect these critical grid components. Frameworks like the Distributed Energy
Resource Cybersecurity Framework (DER-CF) [62] and advanced mitigation techniques, such as voltage
control, data diodes, and blockchain, are essential to strengthening the cybersecurity posture of electric
grids incorporating solar PV systems. As such, the evolving landscape of solar energy calls for enhanced
CTI to safeguard the reliability of digital energy infrastructures.
 Current CTI-sharing platforms, such as E-ISAC and DOE’s Energy Threat Analysis Center, serve the
broader energy sector but lack solar-specific focus. To address this gap, the industry must establish or
expand CTI-sharing mechanisms tailored to solar vulnerabilities. This requires collaboration among
stakeholders to develop standardized protocols, increase participation through education and incentives,
and evolve regulatory frameworks for secure, transparent sharing. These measures will enable the solar
industry to improve its cybersecurity posture and mitigate evolving threats effectively.
 4.3. Consequences for Adversary-Impacted Solar Systems
 Cyberattacks on solar energy systems threaten both energy production and grid stability. These
attacks primarily target digital components, such as inverters, gateways, and communication systems,

 22

rather than the solar panels themselves. Operational consequences can be grouped into events that affect
the view, control, or safety of a system. While the most severe consequences are less likely, they warrant
consideration.
 Loss or denial of view occurs when stakeholders do not have access to view data or access systems.
DoS attacks can prevent devices from communicating or interrupt communications architecture, which
could block access to real-time data. Impacts to view may also be a secondary effect. For example, many
companies, when affected by ransomware, shut down their own connections to remote systems to prevent
the spread of the malware while they investigate the situation. Even if attacks do not directly affect power
production, they can block system visibility, delay response times, and exacerbate disruptions. A final
impact to consider in this category is the manipulation of view. False data injection attacks have been
proposed to manipulate the operator’s view of what the system is doing. [63,64] This could cause the
operator to make poor decisions based on false data or hide potentially dangerous situations from
operators’ view.
 Research Challenge: Variations of Cyber Event Consequences for Different
 Stakeholders (Challenge R1). In addition to the type of consequence an incident
 has, it is important to consider which stakeholder is impacted by that
 consequence and if there are any cascading impacts. A ransomware on a service
 provider may have very different effects than a ransomware attack on an owner
 or operator due to their respective roles in maintaining reliability of connected
 systems. Incident response plans and consequence assessments are essential for
 understanding when and how stakeholders can mitigate potential impacts. All
 stakeholders should be considered, including managed service providers and
 cloud vendors.
 Loss of control covers both loss of the ability to control and adversary injection of malicious
commands. Vulnerabilities in these components can allow adversaries to execute arbitrary commands,
disrupting operations and compromising data integrity. At its extreme, loss of control becomes a loss-of-
safety event.
 Research Challenge: Remote Forensics (Challenge R9). Threat actors continue
 to exploit systems and assets in diverse ways, many of which cause loss of system
 or DoS to those responsible for incident response. Even though these events are
 not the worst attack that can occur, they can, however, prevent remote access to
 the systems/assets affected by them, making remote forensics nearly impossible.
 Because solar energy system security is becoming more relevant, standardized
 protocols and practices for forensics need to be developed.
 Another type of consequence to consider relates to solar trackers and asset health. If these systems
become compromised, attackers could manipulate these systems to orient the panels in undesired
directions. A severe example of this might be an attacker gaining access to a solar tracker system and
writing instructions for the system to point the panels face up in a storm. These panels would typically be
stored facing a direction that would keep them safe from wind and hail damage during a storm. Standards
for security in solar energy systems can help outline baseline security settings for solar tracking systems
to help them eliminate these vulnerabilities.
 Research Challenge: Realistic Cyber Impact Models (Challenge R10). Industry
 has expressed the challenge of understanding what the impacts from cyberattacks
 would be on solar energy systems and connected power systems. Many
 assumptions have to be made for cyber-physical threat models, which contributes
 to the challenge of accurately assessing what the most likely or most
 consequential outcome of a cyber intrusion could be. Research can support this

 23

 challenge by developing high-fidelity cyber-physical models to demonstrate or
 validate the impact of cyberattacks on power systems.
 Non-operational impacts, such as financial losses and market shifts, are also relevant for consequence
analysis. For example, in the solar industry, corporate espionage led to significant industry repercussions.
[65] National energy security is also at risk due to reliance on foreign supply chains and weak
cybersecurity frameworks for DERs like rooftop solar. Timely firmware updates remain critical, despite
compatibility concerns. Mitigating these risks requires securing digital components, strengthening
industry-wide cybersecurity practices, and ensuring prompt patching. Cyberattacks on solar systems pose
enduring threats beyond immediate disruptions, making it essential for informed decision-making to
integrate NERC and RTO-ISO reliability and resilience analysis into risk modeling.
 Research Challenge: Establishing Cyber Metrics (Challenge I6). In a
 multi-stakeholder environment, the ability to classify and quantify both the
 impact of cyber incidents and benefits of implementing cyber controls can
 provide a better understanding of cyber risk and risk mitigation and the
 distribution of risk across stakeholders. Implementing right-sized controls for a
 system requires a cost-benefit justification, which in turn requires the ability to
 measure the cost and benefit of cyber risk. Costs can be evaluated by assessing
 the direct and indirect impacts of incident consequences, factoring in the
 likelihood of those outcomes. Benefits can be evaluated by the relative security
 provided by cybersecurity mitigations and the cost to implement these controls.
 The impacts and risk tolerance will vary from organization to organization, but
 being able to provide a method by which to measure cyber risk will allow each
 organization to justify their investment in cybersecurity.

 5. SOLAR CYBERSECURITY RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT
 5.1. State of the Art
 In the rapidly evolving landscape of solar PV deployments, cybersecurity awareness has escalated
alongside the proliferation of connected devices and the sophistication of cyber threats. Commercial firms
are spearheading advanced solutions tailored for DER environments. Some firms provide protection
solutions, embedding identity-based security mechanisms within protocols to authenticate and validate
data packets, ensuring end-to-end security and access control over public Internet channels. Other firms
complement these efforts by offering robust threat detection and response capabilities, enhancing
resilience against evolving cyber threats.
 Initiatives like the SunSpec Cybersecurity Working Group, Solar Energy Industries Association
(SEIA) Cybersecurity Working Group, and Smart Electric Power Alliance (SEPA) Cybersecurity
Working Group play pivotal roles in advancing DER cybersecurity. These collaborative efforts focus on
best practices in communication security, network architecture, access controls, and vulnerability
assessments. They host vital platforms for industry collaboration and innovation in DER cybersecurity
through fostering dialogue and solutions for emerging challenges.
 Current practices in safeguarding DER networks include the adoption of Zero-Trust Architecture
(ZTA), use of secure hardware modules like trusted platform modules for cryptographic key protection,
and integration with identity management services, such as Active Directory, for robust access
management. Secure communication protocols like named data networking are preferred for their
encryption, authentication, and access control capabilities, offering superior protection compared to
traditional protocols. Regular security audits, adherence to best practices, and participation in
information-sharing programs like the Cybersecurity Risk Information Sharing Program further bolster
cybersecurity readiness.
 Looking forward, gaps remain in understanding emerging threats specific to DER environments,
refining standardized vulnerability assessments, and enhancing interoperability across various DER
components. Research efforts should focus on advancing secure network architectures, improving threat
detection and response mechanisms, and developing resilience strategies that address the unique
challenges posed by DER integration. Collaboration among government agencies, industry stakeholders,
and cybersecurity initiatives will be essential in driving these research agendas and ensuring continued
security and resilience of DER systems amid ongoing technological advancements and evolving cyber
risks.
5.1.1.       Identify
 Identifying the systems and the associated threats, vulnerabilities, and risks to systems is a critical,
initial step of cybersecurity.
 Regarding solar cybersecurity, initiatives such as the Idaho National Laboratory’s (INL) Cyber
Security through Hardware Integration, Education, and Layered Defense (SHIELD) [66], which includes
tailoring tools like the Cyber Security Evaluation Tool and Malcolm to the solar industry, play pivotal
roles in safeguarding utility-scale solar installations against evolving cyber threats. Cyber SHIELD,
funded by DOE Wind Energy Technologies Office, Water Power Technologies Office, and SETO,
focuses on enhancing cybersecurity resilience specifically tailored to solar assets. It offers open-source
tools and expert assessments that help operators understand and mitigate the unique cyber risks associated
with solar generation systems. Existing identification processes are robust but need continuous
improvement to address new and sophisticated threats.

 Industry Challenge: Asset inventory and management (Challenge I8).
 Asset inventory and management are challenging in the solar industry due to the
 high number of components and stakeholders with various roles in operating or
 maintaining equipment. It is not uncommon for solar plants to be bought and
 sold, requiring each new owner to develop or verify asset inventory. Changes
 may be made during routine maintenance. Additionally, solar assets often
 integrate more cloud-based and managed services into their design approach.
 This introduces additional stakeholders with shared responsibility whose assets
 may need to be considered when developing asset inventory and management to
 inform implementation of certain controls (e.g., incident response). Further
 complicating the newer asset model architecture challenges, different service
 providers may install and connect their own equipment to provide the third-party
 service. Finally, traditional asset management prioritizes high-cost items, such
 as solar panels, which may not be the most important components (e.g., inverters
 or controllers) from a security perspective. Robust tools for asset management
 exist, but they may not be commonly used in the solar industry or used for
 cybersecurity purposes. Stakeholders, including OEMs, developers, AOOs, and
 anyone with shared responsibilities, must work together to keep updated, field-
 verified asset databases.
 Integrating the Secure Design and Development Cybersecurity Capability Maturity Model
(SD2-C2M2) into these efforts provides a structured and technical approach to enhance cybersecurity
resilience across solar energy systems. [67] SD2-C2M2 emphasizes comprehensive cybersecurity
practices tailored to the software design and development of equipment and software manufacturers or
development departments of large end-user organizations. It advocates for rigorous software security
assessment processes that include evaluating interfaces between software components, implementing
secure coding practices through regular source code reviews and automated scanning, and using
well-defined data structures with built-in programming language features. Furthermore, SD2-C2M2
advocates for the secure storage of source code with strict access controls, recommends third-party
libraries undergo vulnerability scanning, and emphasizes comprehensive software testing, including
regression testing, input limit testing, runtime verification, and vulnerability testing. These practices are
crucial for ensuring robust cybersecurity measures across all stages of solar software development—from
the initial design through deployment and maintenance—ultimately bolstering the resilience of solar
infrastructures against evolving cybersecurity threats. Security and privacy are key elements of
SD2-C2M2. All data recorded using the tool is only available on the user’s computers. The online
SD2-C2M2 tool does not collect any assessment data—manufacturer-entered information is not
accessible by the website hosts or DOE. Additional research is needed to refine these practices and ensure
they remain effective against emerging threats. Existing identification practices within SD2-C2M2 are
comprehensive but require ongoing updates and enhancements.

 26

 The Solar Resilience Maturity Model (Sol-REMM) is designed to support self-assessments by utility
scale, commercial, and industrial solar operators. [68] The model provides descriptive rather than
prescriptive industry-focused guidance for resilience programs. The model content is presented at a high
level of abstraction so that it can be interpreted by solar operators of various types, structures, and sizes.
An assessment can be completed in a matter of hours by an operator’s designated “Sol-REMM facilitator”
with input from a diverse set of subject matter experts. The assessment can be conducted in one day or
spread out over a period of days or weeks to accommodate staff schedules. The Sol-REMM tool
automatically generates a detailed output product for sharing results with the operator’s subject matter
experts, decision-makers, and other designated stakeholders. As with the SD2-C2M2 tool, security and
privacy are key elements of Sol-REMM. All data recorded using the tool is only available on the user’s
computers. The online Sol-REMM tool does not collect any assessment data—operator-entered
information is not accessible by the website hosts or DOE. Further research is needed to enhance the
adaptability of Sol-REMM to diverse solar operators and to integrate real-time data analysis capabilities.
Existing identification processes within Sol-REMM are effective but need to be expanded to cover a
broader range of cybersecurity scenarios.
 As the modern grid shifts from centralized to decentralized generation sources, it becomes more
reliant on complex, data-driven communications, which increase the number of access points outside a
utility’s control. Additionally, grid devices are often managed by consumers or third parties who may not
fully understand the importance of cybersecurity. To address these challenges, NLR developed DER-CF,
which guides users through a tailored questionnaire, providing a comprehensive view of a site’s
cybersecurity [69,70]. Focusing on three key pillars—governance, technical management, and physical
security—ensures that tasks and questions are directed to the appropriate personnel. Each of these areas
and their subdomains plays a vital role in cybersecurity, and the DER-CF helps users address the roles
effectively. The DER-CF walks users through a customized questionnaire to assess a site’s overall
cybersecurity. It also helps users navigate these critical areas, enabling them to track progress and
generate automated cybersecurity reports at any time. Leveraging DER-CF with other tools like Module-
OT and Advanced Research on Integrated Energy Systems (ARIES) Cyber Range will enable users to
identify and understand a facility’s cybersecurity posture and help protect modern power systems in
which communications and controls are spread across diverse and distributed technologies. [71]
 In summary, while initiatives like Cyber SHIELD, Cyber Security Evaluation Tool, Malcolm,
SD2-C2M2, and Sol-REMM offer robust frameworks for enhancing cybersecurity in solar energy
systems, significant gaps remain. Further research is needed to develop advanced threat detection
mechanisms, improve supply chain risk assessment models, implement comprehensive supply chain
security measures, refine tailored guidance for DER supply chains, and enhance the availability and use
of assessment tools like Sol-REMM. Existing identification processes are effective but require continuous
improvement to address the evolving cybersecurity landscape. By addressing these gaps, the solar energy
sector can significantly enhance its resilience against cybersecurity threats, ensuring the reliability and
security of critical energy infrastructure.
5.1.2. Protection Methodologies
 Concerning solar energy cybersecurity, best practices approaches emphasize the selection of devices
and design of networks to proactively defend systems against potential attacks. Recommended best
practices, such as the use of secure communications, ZTA, and Software-Defined Networking (SDN), as
well as device-level considerations, such as supply chain security, provide necessary measures for
securing in-field solar assets. These innovations have the potential to redefine traditional security
paradigms by ensuring rigorous verification and dynamic control over network resources, crucial for
safeguarding critical energy infrastructures. Some firms provide protection solutions, embedding
identity-based security mechanisms within its protocol to authenticate and validate data packets, ensuring
end-to-end security and access control over public Internet channels.

 27

 Protection can occur in all parts of the system design, from devices to network architecture,
communications, and processes. For network communications, protocols that provide encryption and
authentication, such as named data networking, are preferred, given the need for remote communications
at many sites and the need to secure that remote access.
 ZTA and related paradigms, such as “defined trust,” reexamine traditional network security by
rejecting implicit trust within network perimeters. The critical need is to define just enough access so that
different devices or actors on the network can do their jobs, and access can be restricted to only that
functionality. Instead of assuming trust once inside, ZTA mandates rigorous verification of every user and
device, employing robust methods like multi-factor authentication. Contextual factors, such as user
location, device health, and behavioral patterns, dynamically influence access permissions, with
additional verification steps applied based on risk assessment. Micro-segmentation divides network
resources into isolated zones with specific access controls, ensuring that users and devices only access
necessary resources. Continuous monitoring and real-time analysis swiftly detect anomalies, bolstering
security defenses. Adaptive security approaches enable responsive adjustments to threats and network
conditions, enhancing overall security resilience.
 SDN revolutionizes the management and security of network infrastructures in solar energy systems.
Unlike traditional setups reliant on fixed configurations and hardware-centric management, SDN
separates the control plane from the data plane, centralizing network management for dynamic control
and optimization of traffic flows. In solar energy contexts, SDN enables automated and programmable
network configurations that adapt to changes in energy production and distribution. It enhances security
by providing centralized visibility and control over network traffic, which facilitates effective
implementation of security frameworks such as ZTA. SDN’s capability to segment and isolate critical
energy infrastructure components reduces attack surfaces and fortifies cybersecurity measures.
Additionally, SDN optimizes resource allocation by dynamically allocating bandwidth and prioritizing
traffic according to real-time operational demands. This scalability and flexibility support the seamless
integration of new technologies into solar energy networks, ensuring operational reliability and robust
protection against cybersecurity threats. Note that SDN is one approach for secure network architectures,
but it has its own challenges. SDN is typically implemented on a central server, which is often in a
security vendor cloud. In OT energy systems, connectivity to the cloud cannot be ensured (i.e., the system
should work and be secure if the cloud connection goes down).
 Remote distributed security enforcement is critical for energy OT systems and may require additional
solutions beyond the current state of the art, but there is adaptability of these methods as well. SDN can
also be implemented in a fixed and rigid manner that emulates a more traditional point-to-point
connection methodology in a networked environment. In this method, the controller node is disconnected
from the operational network, and configuration modifications performed in the control plane are
disallowed until the controller is reconnected. In static implementations, such as many OT environments,
this approach can greatly reduce the ability for a rogue device to attach to the network or for a
compromised node to establish unauthorized communications using an unauthorized protocol or to an
unconfigured node. The tradeoff is decreased dynamic flexibility of the environment compared to a fixed
engineered network design. This static-engineered approach further enables and enforces ZTA concepts.

 Supply chain security can refer to the risk of being able to receive components and systems from key

The concept that inverters could be manipulated to create adverse effects on the grid is not new.

5.1.3. Detect/Analyze
 Intrusion detection systems (IDS) play a key role in cybersecurity, providing continuous monitoring
to detect unauthorized access attempts, data breaches, or other suspicious activities. These systems use
signature-based detection that relies on known threat patterns. New work in industry and in research
includes anomaly-based detection, which identifies deviations from normal behavior. This involves the
integration of machine learning and AI to further enhance detection capabilities and detect sophisticated
cybersecurity threats.
 Several commercial companies offer IDS solutions capable of enhancing PV plant networks,
including host-based or network-based solutions. These solutions can detect malicious actions using both
signature- and anomaly-based methods. Some solutions provide next-generation firewalls that combine
traditional firewall functions with advanced security features like deep packet inspection, IDS, application
awareness, and threat intelligence. Some solutions collect and correlate log data from multiple sources to
detect security incidents.
 Research at Sandia National Laboratories includes cyber-physical analysis initiatives focused on
improving cybersecurity. In recent years, a team at Sandia National Laboratories developed a Proactive
Intrusion Detection and Mitigation System that senses when inverters are under attack and deploys
corrective actions. The solution uses unsupervised online learning that actively learns and adapts to its
environment. The Proactive Intrusion Detection and Mitigation System evolved into a commercialization
project, now known as SolarSnitch, and development efforts are ongoing to shape the solution into a
commercial-ready product. [74]
 In a recent paper, Pacific Northwest National Laboratory translated publicly available distribution
models of various sizes and configurations to enable the development, replication, and benchmarking of
cybersecurity test procedures. [75] Their research demonstrates how cybersecurity threats are analyzed
and managed within DER-rich environments. These models can integrate with other cyber-physical
systems and enable advanced real-time simulation to provide a comprehensive understanding of grid
dynamics and vulnerabilities associated with DER integration. The translated models and results
demonstrating cybersecurity scenarios are made publicly available on GitHub for replication and
benchmarking. By integrating insights from the translated distribution models, cybersecurity teams can
better anticipate, detect, and mitigate risks associated with DERs.
 Future research endeavors should prioritize refining Security Orchestration and Automated Response
(SOAR) capabilities tailored specifically to DER management systems, ensuring robust protection against
emerging cybersecurity threats in all types of energy landscapes.

5.1.4.      Respond
 Once a cyber intrusion event has started, organizations must respond to understand what happened,
contain the incident, reduce the impact if possible, and start recovery as needed. This area of
cybersecurity focus does not have as much solar-specific R&D as other areas. This may be due to there
being few known cybersecurity incidents that have directly affected operational solar systems. As
described in the threat assessment section of this report, some incidents have affected utilities who happen
to operate some solar assets as part of their portfolios, and some incidents have affected solar products or
service providers without causing operational impacts in the field.
 While there are some tools that can help with automated response to detected events, many
organizations employ cybersecurity service providers who perform monitoring and incident response for
contracted customers.
 SOAR provides a pivotal framework for automated responses to threats. SOAR integrates advanced
security tools, such as IDS, Security Information and Event Management platforms, and automated
response mechanisms, to bolster the resilience of DER environments. SOAR frameworks specifically
tailored to the complexities of solar PV systems automate the detection of anomalies and potential
cybersecurity threats across DER assets like inverters and smart meters. [77]
 Real-time monitoring and threat intelligence feeds enable swift identification of malicious activities,
leveraging machine learning algorithms to detect known attack patterns early on. Central to SOAR’s
effectiveness is its automated incident response capability. Upon detecting security breaches, predefined
response playbooks are triggered, executing actions, such as isolating compromised devices, blocking
malicious IP addresses, or implementing containment measures, to halt malware propagation. This
automation not only minimizes response times but also reduces the risk of human error, which is crucial
in fast-paced cybersecurity threat landscapes. SOAR solutions seamlessly integrate with existing security
infrastructures within DER networks – often provided as an additional feature for Security Information
and Event Management services – enhancing visibility and streamlining cybersecurity operations.
Moreover, SOAR’s adaptability and scalability accommodate updates in threat intelligence and evolving
attack vectors specific to solar energy systems.
 Additional research might include the development of effective/scalable methods to detect anomalies
in singular devices and across the system of multiple devices. The end goal for these methods would be to
accurately determine what information is needed in a timely manner. The SOAR playbooks discussed
previously include a response as part of their considerations and can be considered a starting point for this
research.
 Research Challenge: Incident Response Resources (Challenge R13). DER
 systems need a refined incident response and endpoint detection solutions.
 Additionally, ICS devices could benefit from a “Health Check” feature to help
 utilities identify compromised devices in the system. This could analyze the health
 of both the compromised device and the entire system that it is a part of.
 During incident response, identifying and containing the source of the issue is an important technical
challenge, but another important consideration is who needs to be notified and at what point in the
investigation and response timeline. In the large expanse of stakeholders in an industry, different
organizations may have an obligation to disclose incidents to other companies they work with (e.g., a
service provider with remote access to systems should disclose cybersecurity incidents to customers that
they are connected to, even if it is precautionary). There may also be different regulatory requirements to

 5.2. Research and Development Needs
 As the solar energy sector becomes increasingly reliant on digital systems and data-driven operations,
the need for robust cybersecurity measures grows urgently. Emerging technologies, such as post-quantum
cryptography (PQC) and AI, along with customized IDS and cloud computing, offer promising avenues
to enhance the cybersecurity resilience of solar installations. This section outlines the critical R&D needs
to integrate these advanced technologies, address existing vulnerabilities, and safeguard solar energy
infrastructure against evolving cybersecurity threats.
 PQC represents a pivotal milestone in fortifying cybersecurity by addressing vulnerabilities posed by
current cryptographic standards and future quantum computing threats. Solar energy infrastructure,
heavily reliant on digital communication and data integrity, faces increasing risks from cyberattacks that
exploit conventional encryption methods. Integrating PQC offers promising avenues to enhance defenses,
providing robust encryption that remains resilient against quantum computing capabilities. This shift
holds significant potential for safeguarding sensitive data transmitted across solar networks and defending
against potential breaches. Although post-quantum attacks are not likely for several years, the lifetime of
OT solar energy systems can be 20–30 years, as post-quantum attack capabilities are likely in their
lifetime. The National Institute of Standards and Technology has begun to identify encryption algorithms
resistant against quantum computing and most DERs include reasonably powerful processors. However,
there are still implementation challenges for these algorithms, including compatibility with legacy devices
and processes and adoption of the new technology by providers.
 Research Challenge: What is the Impact of PQC on Solar Technologies and
 Communications? (Challenge R12). Key areas requiring further exploration
 include the adaptation of PQC algorithms to suit the unique constraints of solar
 energy systems, such as resource limitations and real-time data processing
 requirements. Optimized algorithms are needed that can perform quick
 encryption and decryption processes without compromising system performance.
 Understanding the compatibility of PQC with existing solar-cybersecurity
 frameworks is critical to ensure seamless integration without introducing
 vulnerabilities. Existing systems should be crypto agile in the sense that new
 PQC technologies can be deployed on those systems in the future as technology
 evolves.
 AI holds significant promise for a broad range of applications in the solar energy sector, including
enhancing operations and cybersecurity, yet several critical gaps and challenges remain to be addressed.
AI can be used to predict solar output, maximize use of available energy, make decisions about operations
and market bidding, and support other business critical applications for solar PV energy. Several ISOs
have noted that predicting residential PV production and its impact to the grid is a growing challenge. AI
is one solution that can adequately predict the generation and availability of those resources which can be
considered in day-ahead or near-term planning. As the industry relies more on AI for decision-making
support, it is essential that security controls be applied to ensure the integrity, and availability of these PV
systems for operators to make decisions.

 33

 Many AI-for-security applications primarily focus on data security, addressing biases or errors in data
and mitigating miscorrelations stemming from inadequate training or coding errors. AI can bolster data
security by monitoring and analyzing vast amounts of data to detect anomalies and potential cyber threats
in real-time, thereby enhancing the resilience of solar infrastructure against cyberattacks. However,
challenges persist in ensuring AI systems are free from biases or errors inherent in the training data,
which could lead to inaccurate threat assessments or security measures. The risk of miscorrelations due to
insufficient or biased training data poses a significant concern, potentially resulting in misidentified
threats or vulnerabilities. In addition, the application of AI to energy systems can itself present
cybersecurity vulnerabilities in the transport of system data potentially between multiple AI large
language model agents exposing confidentiality and authentication risks to privacy or cyber monitoring
system integrity.
 Research Challenge: How AI Can be Used to Support Security for Solar
 Energy Systems and the Risks of Growing AI Applications on the Security of
 Solar Systems (Challenge R2). Establishing standardized frameworks and
 protocols for AI deployment in solar cybersecurity is crucial to ensure consistent
 and reliable performance across diverse solar energy systems and environments.
 Further research is needed to develop AI models that can effectively recognize
 and mitigate biases, errors, and miscorrelations in the context of solar
 cybersecurity. AI access controls and multi-party cybersecurity for data and
 analysis will be essential within a multi-agent AI ecosystem with multiple vendor
 large language model agents, training, and context data. Ongoing R&D efforts
 will be essential to address these gaps and optimize the effectiveness of AI in
 safeguarding solar energy infrastructure from emerging cyber threats.
 The implementation of a customized IDS for solar energy infrastructure is imperative, given the
increasing sophistication of cyber threats targeting critical energy sectors. Unlike the wind industry,
where tailored IDS solutions have already demonstrated effectiveness, the solar energy sector lacks a
similarly customized approach. R&D efforts must focus on adapting existing IDS frameworks to the
unique characteristics and vulnerabilities of solar installations. This involves integrating advanced
network and host-based IDS technologies that can detect anomalies and potential breaches in OT and IT
layers of solar systems. At the same time, any AI monitoring system should be designed using a
cyber-secure framework so that it does not introduce vulnerabilities itself.
 Research Challenge: Develop Custom IDS for Solar Systems (Challenge R6).
 Understanding the specific OT environment of solar systems is crucial. Solar PV
 systems and inverters have distinct communication protocols and operational
 behaviors that differ significantly from those in wind energy systems.
 Consequently, the IDS for solar must be adept at monitoring these unique
 patterns and identifying deviations that may indicate cyber intrusions while
 reducing tool complexity and cost to encourage broader industry adoption.
 Incorporating machine learning algorithms to analyze data from sensors and
 control systems can enhance the system’s ability to detect subtle signs of
 malicious activity.
 Cloud computing has emerged as a pivotal force in modernizing infrastructure across various sectors,
including the power industry. The integration of cloud technologies into power systems highlights its
transformative potential. These solutions leverage cloud computing to enhance real-time data analysis,
improve operations, and bolster system reliability. However, the application of cloud computing within
the solar energy field, particularly concerning solar cybersecurity, remains an area of active exploration
and development.

 34

 As solar energy systems increasingly become integral to the power grid, the need for advanced cloud-
based solutions to manage and protect these systems grows. Cloud computing can offer significant
benefits for solar energy by enabling smart data management, real-time monitoring, and predictive
analytics, akin to how it supports utilities in optimizing grid operations and maintenance. While the
advantages of cloud computing in the power sector are becoming clearer, its implementation and
associated risks for solar energy systems require further research and refinement.
 The adoption of cloud computing introduces both opportunities and challenges. On one hand, cloud
platforms can enhance the security of solar energy infrastructure through advanced data encryption, threat
detection, and real-time monitoring. On the other hand, the transition to cloud-based systems must
address potential cybersecurity risks, such as data breaches and unauthorized access, particularly given
the sensitive nature of operational and performance data. In addition, the ability to measure and control
the critical energy asset must be maintained, even when cloud connectivity is lost. Ensuring robust

further R&D as cloud-based operations for DER improve. Compliance certification for these services also
needs to be explored.
 While large-scale solar PV installations (e.g., commercial, industrial, utility) often have management
functions by their owners or operators and the large-scale project owners may be more reluctant to trust
third-party cloud providers, small-scale installations primarily at residences do not have local
management capability. Vendor-supported and cloud-based management can provide the ability to
monitor, manage, and recover from cyberattacks and incidents. Installed equipment should have a “fail-
secure” capability that can allow remote autonomous recovery from attacks and non-malicious incidents.
Recent experience with incidents, such as the CrowdStrike outage in July 2024, have shown the impact of
a bad automatically installed update that required manual intervention for recovery. The fail-secure
approach could autonomously revert the device to a secure and known-good state with secure
communications enabled to allow recovery and update using remote management from the vendor or
other management service provider. This approach can also be implemented by large-scale operators to
minimize manual intervention at devices especially for installations in a geographically diverse portfolio.

 6. STANDARDS DEVELOPMENT
 Cybersecurity has become increasingly important, particularly for critical infrastructure such as the
electric grid. A decade ago, DERs were not viewed as critical since they were generally small and
could be seen as negative loads or small variations to existing loads. However, now and into the
high-penetration DER future, cybersecurity attacks on DER could have serious impacts on the grid,
particularly in aggregate. For this reason, several cybersecurity strategies, regulations, and standards
are beginning to develop cybersecurity requirements and recommendations as applied to DER.
 Standards can aid in reducing the attack surface and minimizing the consequences that DERs may
face otherwise. As mentioned in the CESER/SETO report titled, “Cybersecurity Considerations for
Distributed Energy Resources on the U.S. Electric Grid,” the consequence of manipulating trip thresholds
of PV inverters through compromised firmware settings could result in grid instability, disconnection of
the DER from the bulk power system, loss of power, and a complete blackout. [80] Supporting reliability
and security standards will be required to manage and optimize DER generation.
 Developing voluntary or mandatory cybersecurity standards for devices, networks, or applications
within the solar ecosystem requires an evaluation of emerging challenges, assessment of cyber risks using
risk assessment tools and maturity models, development of sector-specific cybersecurity requirements,
coordinated feedback from industry stakeholders, and validation of proposed cybersecurity controls. Over
the last few years, several standards development efforts have been initiated, among such the following
are worth highlighting:
• UL 2941 technical committee was formed for building a national cyber certification program by
 developing the consensus for Underwriter’s Laboratory (UL) 2941. It seeks to recognize this
 cybersecurity certification standard as an American National Standard.
• IEEE 1547.3 was updated in 2023 to address the broad requirements of cybersecurity, going beyond
 the DER interface.
• IEEE 1547 standards working group is starting to draft mandatory cybersecurity requirements for
 DER equipment and systems for the upcoming IEEE 1547-2025 standard revision.
• SunSpec Alliance has established a voluntary DER certification program by getting feedback from
 the newly established subgroup comprised of alliance members.
• ISA 99 WG14 focuses on the development of Distribution Cybersecurity Requirements.

 6.2. Standards Approval Process
 Standards typically lag behind the state-of-the-art technology and industry adoption of that
technology. There is a good reason for this. If standards are to be adopted and enforced, industry and
regulators have mutual interests in ensuring that these standards are fair, do not place an undue burden on
adopters, and achieve the goals for safety, security, or reliability as intended. The general process for
standards approval follows the four steps of draft, review, comments, and adopt.
 The timeline for reviewing updates to standards depends on the complexity and the number of
comments received during the review stages. New standards can take even longer to reach consensus. For
example, the UL 2941 standard is now preparing for a third round of review after addressing over 400
comments from industry. Different standards development organizations (SDOs) may have different
thresholds for achieving consensus, which may also affect the duration of the review process.
 Standards are updated at different intervals. There are significant burdens associated with updating
standards, including the time and effort to revise the standards and reach consensus, the costs to industry
to update or redesign their products and services to meet the standards, and the cost of certifying
technologies or implementations to the new requirements. However, standards often need to be updated to
reflect changes in technology and deployment. For example, the original IEEE 1547 standard was
developed when IBR penetration was low, and it was simpler not to deal with these devices during
transient stability events and instead require that they disconnect from the grid rather than risk forming
unintentional islands. By the mid-2010s, it was clear that IBR penetration was rising to significant levels
and expected to continue increasing. The impact of all these devices tripping during instability could be
larger than the initiating event. The IEEE 1547-2018 standard required adjustable ride-through and
grid-support capabilities for IBR connections. With the incorporation of digital solutions, the
technological landscape is evolving quickly, and thus a higher rate of updates is expected for standards
that consider these technologies and the digital threat landscape. Also, less mature standards have the
tendency to be updated more frequently due to minor technical issues or editorial mistakes.
 Most SDOs are made up of dedicated industry participants committed to the mission of improving
their fields through standardization. The process of joining working groups or technical committees for
standards can be opaque to outsiders. Some working groups may require a balance of different
stakeholders (e.g., vendors, users) so the committee cannot be unfairly influenced by a particular
stakeholder group. For UL Standards and Engagement, there is the option to apply for a technical
committee membership. For the IEC, the interested party needs to be a member of the National
Committee. Some other organizations are accessible by paying a personal or corporate membership.
 Industry Challenge: SDO Representation (Challenge I17). Given the complex
 and dynamic network of solar stakeholders, it is important to ensure that there is
 adequate representation of different stakeholders in SDOs that draft the
 cybersecurity standards for the industry. A library of relevant standards, their
 associated SDOs and committees or working groups, and points of contact could
 provide a starting point for interested industry members to get involved.

 6.3. Standards Compliance and Adoption
 Standards mean nothing unless they are adopted, which often may be limited or not occur without
enforcement in some matter. This is one of the biggest areas of controversy in industry. Most entities do
not want to be governed by standards or regulation. Voluntary standards are often not followed or may be
implemented inconsistently with little-to-no internal governance or oversight to ensure the execution of
controls. However, mandatory standards introduce a different set of issues, and entities may struggle to
implement them. Factors contributing to the poor adoption or implementation might include the
following:
• Lack of stakeholder investment (e.g., how or why it is beneficial to implement a standard)
• Little-to-no guidance/expertise available to help implementors
• Limited resources due to company size or other factors
• Reactive approaches to standards and security
• Inflexibility in standards and inability to quickly evolve with the dynamic cybersecurity landscape
• The organization’s size and specific risk profile, there is no one size fits all.
 Users, installers, and purchasers struggle with awareness of standards, knowledge of how to
implement standards, and organizational maturity that allows them to adopt standards. Educating
stakeholders on the existence of standards and providing risk-based guidance on how to implement them
significantly impacts adoption. Other ways to increase voluntary adoption may include incentivizing
organizations financially to adopt them. Regulation can also drive the adoption of standards.
Cybersecurity frameworks may be adopted by different state or federal regulatory bodies and integrated
into regulations. However, one big challenge is managing the lack of harmonization. Solar stakeholders
will need to consider how different standards across regulatory agencies (e.g., different states or countries
with different requirements) will affect their cybersecurity strategy, program, and controls design, along
with any impacted business processes.
 Research Challenge: Centralizing Cybersecurity Standards (Challenge R7).
 One of the biggest challenges to achieving centralized standards is the burden
 that it is to vendors in adopting the standards. This effort takes a tremendous
 amount of time and other resources to adhere to. For some, it may not be

 In the U.S., the most significant federal cybersecurity regulation is the NERC CIP requirements. CIP

Bulk Electric System. Requirements vary based on the potential impact of the assets to the bulk power
system. Historically, most solar installations have been below the minimum threshold requiring NERC

identify and register owners and operators of currently unregistered bulk power system-connected IBRs.
Working closely with industry and stakeholders, NERC is executing a FERC-approved work plan to
achieve the identification and registration directive by 2026. This process may include the application of
NERC CIP standards to solar assets.

 7. STAKEHOLDER ROLES
 One of the unique features of solar energy systems compared to most other energy generation assets
is the number of different stakeholders that could have a role in the life cycle of a solar PV system. This
number is further increased by the variability in plant size (from a single rooftop to a multi-megawatt
plant) and associated stakeholders for different types of installations. From OEMs (of which there may be
several for the various components required) to developers and integrators, to aggregators, to utilities and
other operators, to additional service providers, there are many stakeholders that rely on continuous or
intermittent access to the systems to perform normal business functions. From a cybersecurity
perspective, challenges arise from the diverse and heterogeneous ecosystem of stakeholders, ranging from
access management to network security, to data security, and more. This section describes the role of
several key stakeholders, although the groupings provided here are not exhaustive, and other stakeholders
may be present that do not fit neatly into one of these categories.
 Industry Challenge: Roles and Responsibilities (Challenge I12). The “shared
 responsibility” challenge is more prevalent in the distributed asset sector due to
 the more prevalent use of cloud-based, third-party, and managed services models
 for cyber assets and security controls. Industry advisers have expressed that
 there is a need to better identify who is responsible for different security controls
 and how to ensure security at DER installations where utilities have limited or no
 control over that security measure and its implementation. Especially in the
 wake of any crisis or security incident, each of these parties needs to be aware of
 who is responsible for what. To effectively determine responsibility, buy-in from
 all stakeholders is needed. This would mean that industry partners across all
 categories would need to be willing to collaborate in R&D for demonstrations of
 their roles/responsibilities. In addition to this, there needs to be a method or
 mechanism for ensuring accountability across stakeholders. The RTO-ISO
 “shared responsibility” matrixes are used to define who is responsible for which
 NERC controls apply to Transmission Owners and Operators and this model is a
 good example of to develop and document shared responsibilities. [99]
 7.1. Original Equipment Manufacturers
 Industry 4.0 has forever changed the cybersecurity landscape for all manufacturers because of the
increased integration of IT and OT. [81] The real challenge manufacturers face is making sure that
cybersecurity is a focus from the inception of the design and requirements process. Cybersecurity, in the
solar industry and across industrial sectors, has traditionally been more of a bolt-on practice rather than a
built-with-from-the-beginning approach. This is something that emerging methods, such as CIE, take into
consideration. CIE aims to integrate cybersecurity considerations into the conception, design,
development, and operation of any physical system, energy, or otherwise to mitigate or even eliminate
avenues for cyber-enabled attacks. This framework is designed to be used in the early manufacturing
stages to help ensure products are built with the end in mind. OEMs have the responsibility of
manufacturing and producing products with the correct security controls based on their intended use for
the environment that they will be used in. As previously mentioned, solar manufacturers should ensure
their products are designed with cybersecurity in mind, including secure firmware and software updates
and development of products to withstand various cyber threats, contributing to the overall resilience of
solar systems.

 44

 Manufacturers also have the responsibility of familiarizing themselves with the many security issues
that their products aim to resolve as well as the security flaws that exist for their catalog of products.
Some of the main concerns surrounding this involve ensuring proper security in supply chain sourcing
and trusting in the supplier. It is imperative that the many components, such as chips and motherboards,
are sourced from verified and trusted vendors. If components are obtained from non-legitimate sources,
manufacturers can be exposed to issues ranging from compromised systems to costly lawsuits. This can
be especially difficult to achieve when the competition sells components at a more competitive price.
However, it is the responsibility of manufacturers to do their research and verify the integrity of their
suppliers.
 OEMs may be a source of risk depending on the country that they are in or the country in which they
manufacture their parts. Sensitive countries may go through extensive efforts to compromise individual
components with the end goal of monitoring or controlling the systems and data that the components end
up in.
 7.2. Developers and Integrators
 Developers and integrators have the responsibility of properly understanding the needs of the
consumer. This includes understanding the unique environments that they build or install as well as the
general vulnerabilities that these systems are susceptible to. Some of these areas of responsibility include
networks, hardware, and software. Each organization will have very different requirements for their
systems and assets. If developers and integrators have a one-size-fits-all approach in their implementation
of these systems, problems are sure to follow.
 Consumers want convenience, but convenience is often the enemy of security. It is the responsibility
of developers and integrators to understand where convenience is permissible and where it is not so
consumers receive the security protection they are obligated to have. This functionality needs to be
understood, communicated, and built into the systems by developers and integrators. In the ideal scenario,
a well-designed system can be both secure and convenient to use. It should be the goal of cybersecurity
system designs to meet this goal. In addition, cybersecurity should be applied by default so that the
system can be deployed in a consistent cybersecurity manner.
 7.3. Asset Owners
 Asset owners play a crucial role in ensuring robust cybersecurity measures are in place. Asset owners
are responsible for the security and integrity of their systems, which includes safeguarding against
cybersecurity threats that could disrupt operations or compromise data. A primary responsibility is
implementing and maintaining comprehensive security requirements that encompass the entire life cycle
of the solar DER system, from design and deployment to maintenance and decommissioning.
 Asset owners must ensure that all hardware and software components adhere to industry best
practices and standards for cybersecurity. This involves regularly updating software to patch known
vulnerabilities, deploying firewalls, and employing IDS. Additionally, asset owners should conduct
regular risk assessments and vulnerability scans to identify and mitigate potential threats before they can
be exploited. They may also be held responsible through regulation (e.g., registered asset owners are
responsible for NERC CIP compliance).
 Workforce training is also a vital component of an asset owner’s role. Ensuring that all personnel
involved with the solar DER system are trained in cybersecurity best practices helps to minimize human
error, which is often a significant vector for cyberattacks. This training should cover recognizing phishing
attempts, using strong passwords, and understanding the importance of physical security measures.

 45

 Asset owners must foster a culture of collaboration and information sharing with other stakeholders,
including utility companies, cybersecurity experts, and regulatory bodies. Sharing threat intelligence and
best practices can help create a more resilient defense against cybersecurity threats. By staying informed
about the latest cybersecurity developments and potential threats, asset owners can proactively adjust their
strategies to better protect their solar DER systems.
 In summary, the role of asset owners in the cybersecurity of solar DER systems involves
implementing rigorous security measures, conducting regular assessments, ensuring continuous training
for personnel, and actively participating in the broader cybersecurity community to maintain a resilient
and secure energy infrastructure.
 7.4. Asset Operators
 Asset operators in solar DER systems have critical responsibilities in maintaining and enhancing
cybersecurity. Their role primarily involves the day-to-day management and operational security of these
systems, ensuring they function effectively while remaining protected from cybersecurity threats. One of
their key duties is to monitor system performance continuously, identifying and responding to any
anomalies or suspicious activities that could indicate a security breach.
 Operators are tasked with the implementation of security protocols and procedures defined by asset
owners. This includes ensuring that all system components are configured correctly and adhere to the
latest cybersecurity standards. They are responsible for applying software updates and patches promptly
to mitigate vulnerabilities and for managing access controls to ensure that only authorized personnel can
interact with critical system components.
 Regularly conducting system audits and cybersecurity drills are another important aspect of the
operator’s role. These activities help to identify potential weaknesses in the system and test the
effectiveness of the current security measures. By simulating potential attack scenarios, operators can
assess their preparedness and make necessary adjustments to their incident response plans.
 In addition to technical responsibilities, asset operators must maintain clear communication with asset
owners and other stakeholders. This involves reporting any security incidents, potential threats, or system
irregularities in a timely manner. Effective communication ensures that all parties are aware of the current
security status and can collaborate on measures to enhance the system’s defenses.
 Asset operators play a crucial role in helping asset owners by aiding in personnel training and
awareness. They ensure that all team members are knowledgeable about cybersecurity best practices and
understand their roles in maintaining system security. Through continuous education and reinforcement of
security protocols, operators help to cultivate a culture of vigilance and proactive defense against
cybersecurity threats within the organization.
 7.5. Service Providers and Other Third Parties
 Several additional parties may have persistent or temporary physical or digital access to solar PV
systems. This can include solar tracking and environmental monitoring services, which run their
prediction algorithms in a cloud environment and can send commands to tracker controllers. For example,
these services could store panels when there is imminent high winds or hail to protect them from damage,
as well as interact with operators and utilities to provide updated production forecasts. Maintenance
services may be contracted out, which may include granting physical access to technicians for routine
maintenance or repairs and digital access for diagnostics or persistent cyber access for asset health
monitoring. Still other services may be used to provide insight into optimizing resource dispatch,
requiring up-to-date information on power production and state-of-health for the plant.

 46

 While these third parties are not individually responsible for the reliability of the solar power plant,
they play an important role in maintaining the resilience of the assets and have the potential to negatively
impact operations through error, maloperation, or cyberattack. Service providers must be aware of the
potential impact they have and apply appropriate mitigations to limit the likelihood or severity of
cybersecurity incidents. To enforce cybersecurity responsibilities, service and procurement contract
language can outline expectations of the asset owner and requirements for the supplier. This may involve
some prescribed actions (e.g., notify customers within X days of any identified cybersecurity incidents) as
well as requirements to maintain a cyber risk management program, providing evidence of ongoing
conformity to that program.
 As discussed in the threat landscape section, attacks on third parties can affect solar energy operations
as a secondary impact. Understanding the potential impact of third parties is a responsibility for both
suppliers and consumers, and mutually agreed-on responsibilities to share and mitigate risk fairly are
required.
 Industry Challenge: Incorporation of Other Third parties (Challenge I4).
 Involvement from additional third parties, such as weather resource and
 forecasting providers, maintainers, cloud service providers, and several others,
 that may touch parts of digital infrastructure are of interest. Moving forward,
 solar energy systems will likely require coordination and cooperation with these
 third parties who are not currently involved in these areas.
 Industry Challenge: Demonstrate the payoff of Investing in Cyber Resilience
 (Challenge I13). To encourage widespread adoption of cyber controls and
 develop a culture of cybersecurity in the solar industry, organizations need to
 understand the payoffs of investing in cyber resilience. Across all stakeholders,
 organizations must be able to see the benefit to them to invest in cybersecurity
 and cyber resilience measures. This is key to cooperation and appropriate
 distribution of risk and responsibilities across stakeholders.
 7.6. Workforce Development
 Workforce development can be used to improve cybersecurity awareness and confidence across all
types of stakeholders. Workforce development in cybersecurity is gaining momentum for critical
infrastructure. Traditionally, organizations focus on new processes or new technologies to solve their
cybersecurity problems. While both are necessary for streamlining the process, the most neglected piece
to the puzzle is the organization’s people. Most cybersecurity incidents occur because of human error.
[82] Figure 9 illustrates this well. Any organization is only as strong as its weakest link. Because human
error is rarely the focus when seeking how to fix cybersecurity issues, organizations continue to face the
same cybersecurity challenges, rarely moving past them.

 47

Figure 9. People, process, and technology must intersect for security.

 When the cyber education and training of an organization’s workforce increases, its collective
cybersecurity posture improves. However, not everyone in the organization shares the same amount of
responsibility for cybersecurity. It is important to build the foundations of a proper Security, Education,
Training, and Awareness plan tailored to the organization. The Security, Education, Training, and
Awareness plan is meant to ensure that each employee is aware of what their responsibilities are
regarding cyber-safety in the organization. For some employees, the annual training refresher on phishing
could be sufficient. However, for those in more technical roles, the annual awareness training is not
sufficient. More advanced training is required.
 The cybersecurity threat landscape is ever-evolving and becoming more complicated. Issues like
ransomware in ICS environments are on the rise; traditional methods for hardening systems are becoming
outdated. Solar is no exception. This mandates a need for continued improvement in education and
training for the workforce, especially for those that touch and manage critical systems and assets.
Organizations need to shift from the mentality of a reactive security approach to a proactive one.
Organizations that practice and perform tabletop exercises with their workforce are more aware of the
threats that they can encounter and are more prepared when real incident-response situations arise.
 Industry Challenge: Workforce Development (Challenge I15). Solar
 organizations need to invest in cyber workforce development. Depending on the
 type of stakeholder, this may involve developers, designers, engineers, operators,
 technicians, and other specialists, all of whom may need cybersecurity training
 to execute their role in securing the infrastructure appropriately.
 Price and execution competition within the solar industry often make it difficult to implement broad
and expensive cybersecurity solutions. That said, simple solutions that support some of the unique aspects
and distributed nature of the solar industry, such as secure remote access, can help mitigate some of the
nearer term threats the solar industry is not prepared to address.
 One method of successful workforce development is from efforts led by industry working groups
such as the SunSpec Webinars, a series of sessions hosted by the SunSpec Alliance. They focus on
educating stakeholders about cybersecurity challenges and best practices, particularly around DERs.
These webinars cover topics such as these:
• Cybersecurity standards (e.g., IEEE 2030.5, SunSpec Modbus)
• Best practices for securing communication protocols and devices.
• Insights into regulatory requirements like California Rule 21

 48

• Emerging threats and trends in DER cybersecurity
• Vendor and utility use studies on implementing security measures.
 They are valuable resources for anyone in the solar and DER industry, including engineers,
policymakers, and researchers, to stay up to date on the evolving cybersecurity landscape.
 7.7. Coordination
 Because of the ever-evolving nature of cybersecurity and the threat landscape, new tactics,
techniques, and procedures will be required to combat them effectively. One area that may benefit each of
these groups is by working together in future endeavors to coordinate and mitigate risk. As the division of
responsibilities for each group becomes more defined, manufacturers, developers, and asset owners could
work together by initializing agreements between the different parties. These agreements could include
anything from additional support between vendors and asset owners, to trainings hosted by
manufacturers, to developers regarding cybersecurity best practices. Additionally, these groups can work
together to further define the division of responsibilities of each group.
 Effective cybersecurity in the solar energy sector requires collaboration among various stakeholders,
including energy providers, equipment manufacturers, cybersecurity experts, and regulatory bodies.
Sharing information about emerging threats and vulnerabilities helps develop comprehensive defense
strategies and implement best practices. Establishing and enforcing cybersecurity standards and
regulations are crucial for ensuring a consistent, unified approach to protecting solar energy systems.
Regulatory frameworks should be adaptable to address the evolving nature of cybersecurity threats and
technological advancements. Continuous education and training for personnel involved in the operation
and maintenance of solar energy systems are essential to enhance their ability to identify and respond to
cybersecurity threats. Raising awareness about the importance of cybersecurity within industry and
among consumers helps to foster a culture of security and vigilance.
 7.8. Information Sharing
 The adage, “many hands make light work,” is appropriate when it comes to information sharing.
Organizations can benefit greatly from visiting known vulnerability databases for the assets they own.
Similarly, being involved in online forums or working groups with other collaborators, consumers, and
suppliers of the same systems and assets promotes further awareness and support for security
vulnerabilities. The E-ISAC is an important resource and is operated by NERC. The purpose of the E-
ISAC is to help minimize cyber and physical security risk across North America for the electric industry.
It provides an information-sharing platform where threats to the electric grid of any magnitude are
discussed. It is a knowledge repository in which users can discover and disclose cyber-physical incidents
and their experience about how to mitigate and correct them. Additionally, it gives access to most of the
ISACs with relevance to solar. See Appendix B for examples of threat intelligence resources.
 Cybersecurity working groups are another way to stay up to date on industry-relevant topics. SEPA,
SEIA, and the SunSpec Alliance host three well-known working groups focused on solar energy systems.
SEPA aims to improve the future of the electric power industry through research, standards, education,
and collaboration with industry partners. SEIA is an international non-profit organization focused on the
benefits of locally sourced energy in the solar and solar storage industries. SunSpec Alliance is also an
international, non-profit with a mission to accelerate the growth of the distributed energy industry by
standardizing communication protocols, system interfaces, and security practices that enable DER
systems to interoperate transparently.

 8. CONCLUSION
 As the solar energy sector advances, prioritizing robust cybersecurity is crucial for safeguarding
DERs and maintaining the reliability of critical infrastructure. Addressing the complex challenges in solar
cybersecurity necessitates a multifaceted approach, integrating advanced technologies, harmonized
standards, and effective stakeholder collaboration. The development and adoption of cybersecurity
standards are pivotal in defining best practices and ensuring industry-wide consistency. While DOE
SETO research initiatives and standards, such as UL 2941 and IEEE 1547, provide valuable frameworks,
their slow review and updating processes can impact their relevance. Enhancing the standards
development process and increasing industry participation in working groups is essential to keep pace
with technological advancements and emerging threats. The solar industry also faces challenges related to
overlapping standards and the adoption of multiple frameworks. Research efforts, such as the Grid
Modernization Initiative 2.2 Harmonization of DER Cybersecurity Standards project funded by EERE
and CESER, are vital for guiding industry players through these complexities. Aligning standards and
acknowledging their overlap can help reduce the burden on stakeholders and streamline certification
processes.
 Coordinated efforts in information sharing, education, and incident-response exercises are crucial for
enhancing collective resilience. Programs like CyberStrike STORMCLOUD can significantly contribute
to workforce development and preparedness. Recent regulatory changes, such as the FERC order to lower
the registration threshold for IBRs, will increase regulatory requirements for many solar plants.
Understanding and managing these changes will be critical for compliance and operational stability.
Additionally, navigating the challenges of determining applicable standards and avoiding redundant
certifications underscores the need for clear guidance and streamlined processes. To advance
cybersecurity in the solar energy sector, it is necessary to prioritize accelerating standards development,
promoting harmonization, enhancing coordination among stakeholders, and addressing regulatory and
certification challenges. By focusing on these priorities, the solar energy sector can strengthen its
cybersecurity posture, ensuring the protection and reliability of locally sourced energy infrastructure amid
evolving cybersecurity threats.
