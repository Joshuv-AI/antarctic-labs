A randomized controlled study on the effects of the T-REX Twente (Thoracic Surgical Rehabilitation Experts Twente) sternal precautions on quality of life and physical activity levels in cardiac surgery patients, compared to standard care in patients following a median sternotomy: the study protocol
Authors: N Wielens, K Roerdink, W Lynch, L Esau, J van der Palen, F R Halfwerk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn 2023, a majority (86%) of open-heart surgeries was performed at Thorax Centrum Twente (TCT) via a full median sternotomy. Currently, there is no consensus on postoperative sternal precautions following full median sternotomy. Research from the USA and Canada suggests that existing restrictive sternal precautions may not be necessary. More lenient sternal precautions, such as the “Keep Your Move in the Tube” principle, have shown positive outcomes, with no significant complications. Patients following this approach experienced fewer mobility issues and reported improved quality of life and reduced anxiety. This study explores the potential benefits of fewer restrictions, which could reduce patient anxiety and lead to fewer follow-up visits. This study aims to determine whether the Thoracic Surgical Rehabilitation Experts Twente (T-REX Twente) sternal precautions have a small positive effect on the Modified MacNew Quality of Life after Myocardial Infarction questionnaire (QLMI-2), physical activity, and reduction of movement-related anxiety in patients after full median sternotomy, compared to standard restrictive sternal precautions. It also assesses whether the T-REX sternal precautions result in no negative effects on pain, wound healing, or postoperative complications.MethodsThis prospective, randomized, controlled, single-blind study will include adult patients undergoing full median sternotomy at TCT between June 2024 and June 2026, all participating in outpatient cardiac rehabilitation. Exclusion criteria include intensive care unit stays over 72 h, delirium, dementia, severe cognitive impairments, language barriers, or treatment by an external referring cardiologist. The control group will adhere to current restrictive sternal precautions, whereas those in the intervention group will follow the T-REX sternal precautions, which allow lifting, pushing, or pulling as long as arm movement remains within a defined “tube.” The primary endpoint is the change in QLMI-2 from baseline (T0) to start of phase II cardiac rehabilitation (T4).DiscussionThe T-REX Twente sternal precautions may improve quality of life, physical activity, and reduce movement-related anxiety, supporting the idea that less restrictive postoperative sternal precautions can enhance patient outcomes.Trial registrationCCMO Trial Register NL78107.100.23, registered on 29 February 2024. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

In the Netherlands, almost 10,000 open-heart surgeries are performed annually, with the majority (89%) of these procedures being carried out via median sternotomy in 2023 [1]. In 2023, the Thorax Centrum Twente (TCT) conducted 866 open-heart surgeries, of which 749 (86%) were performed via median sternotomy.

There is no global consensus regarding postoperative mobilization precautions following a median sternotomy. Restrictive precautions do not improve sternal outcomes, yet may negatively influence functional recovery [2]. Indeed, less restrictive post-sternotomy protocols were associated with improved functional outcomes and earlier independence in activities of daily living (ADL), without an increase in sternal complications [2]. Moreover, early upper extremity resistance exercise after median sternotomy does not result in clinically relevant sternal micromotion, while strict weightlifting restrictions lack high-quality evidence [3]. These findings suggest that strict movement limitations may unnecessarily reduce weight-bearing activities of the upper limbs, potentially contributing to muscle deconditioning and prolonged ADL dependency following heart surgery. Although early mobilization from post-operative day 1 is recommended as part of the Enhanced Recovery after Cardiac Surgery (ERAS Cardiac) program [4], specifications on how to do so with high levels of evidence are still needed.

We conducted a survey within the TCT network to assess current precautionary practices after median sternotomy [5]. On postoperative day 3, significant variations were found in clinical practice regarding sleeping positions, transitioning from a lying to a sitting position, and post-discharge activities such as carrying groceries or vacuuming.

The current postoperative sternal precautions at TCT include, among other things, advising against lifting, pushing, or pulling more than 2 kg for the first 6 weeks to protect the sternum (Table 1).

However, there is limited evidence supporting standard postoperative sternal precautions [6]. Furthermore, to date, they have been framed negatively toward patients (“thou shall not”). The current sternal precautions may be too restrictive and based on insufficient evidence [7].

Adams et al. developed the concept of “Keep Your Move in the Tube” [7], as seen in Fig. 1. Several follow-up studies have successfully implemented these revised movement sternal precautions [8, 9]. The idea behind these precautions is to keep the upper arms close to the body, as if the patient is inside an imaginary tube with their upper body. This allows patients to safely perform weight-bearing movements while avoiding excessive strain on the sternum. Patients may lift, push, or pull as long as their arms remain within this “tube” (see Fig. 1). Previous studies have found no adverse effects when applying this guideline [7–9]. Specifically, no differences were found in the number of sternum-related complications between the control group and the intervention group, such as sternal refixation, superficial sternal wound infection, and deep sternal wound infection (DSWI) [9], as measured by the Sternal Instability Scale [10]. Additionally, an increase in upper body mobility was observed after 2–3 weeks post cardiac surgery [7].

However, another study found no significant difference in quality of life [9]. This finding may be attributed to the lack of a validated measurement instrument and a lack of post-hospitalization follow-up.

Objectives {7}

This study aims to determine whether the Thoracic Surgical Rehabilitation Experts (T-REX Twente) sternal precautions have a positive effect on the Modified MacNew Quality of Life after Myocardial Infarction questionnaire (QLMI-2) [11], physical activity, and reduction of movement-related anxiety in patients after full median sternotomy, compared to standard restrictive sternal precautions. It also assesses whether the T-REX sternal precautions result in no negative effects on pain, wound healing, or postoperative complications.

We hypothesize that the T-REX Twente sternal precautions will have a small positive effect on quality of life, physical activity levels, and movement-related anxiety in cardiac patients following median sternotomy, compared to current restrictive sternal precautions.

Trial design {8}

The T-REX Twente trial is a prospective, randomized, single-blind study with two parallel groups. Allocation is by block randomization with a block of 4 in a 1:1 ratio. Primary endpoints will be assessed with a superiority design. An effect on at least one primary outcome is required to deem the study successful. Figure 2 is a flowchart for enrollment and analysis.

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting {9}

Thorax Centrum Twente (TCT) is situated in Medisch Spectrum Twente (MST), a non-academic teaching hospital in Enschede, the Netherlands (see Table 2). Patients are treated from referring hospitals throughout the northern and eastern part of the Netherlands. Only patients with a cardiologist from TCT will be screened for inclusion. A single-center design was chosen since cardiac rehabilitation in our center is conducted externally in collaboration with local physiotherapists, unlike other referring centers where it is provided in-hospital. Additionally, this study utilizes single patient rooms as is standard in our hospital, which serves as an additional requirement for choosing the single-blind study design.

Who will take informed consent? {26a}

A physiotherapist informs the patient about the study at the preoperative outpatient ward. After being informed about the study, patients receive the information brochure with informed consent form to consider. When interested, informed consent is subsequently signed on the day of admission for surgery.

In the case of an “urgent” clinical admission, i.e., needing surgery within the same hospital admission [14], the patient is informed about the study a few days prior to surgery.

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {26b}

Additional consent provisions are required for the collection and use of participant data. This pertains to the retrieval of information from the general practitioner/specialist(s) who treats participants regarding new contact details and/or medical history, to store and use for future research on conditions and/or the treatment method being studied, and if participants want to be contacted for follow-up research after the study. No biological specimens will be obtained for this study.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

Mobilization guidelines applied to the control group consist of standard guidelines and recommendations currently provided to participants (usual care). Contrarily, the intervention group receives mobilization recommendations based on KYMITT principles and were altered for our study into the T-REX Twente sternal precautions.

Over a period of approximately 12 weeks, all participants will complete the three questionnaires QLMI-2, Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia (TSK-17), and Numeric Pain Rating Scale (NPRS) at four time points: preoperatively (T0), on the 4th post-operative day (T2), on the first day of cardiac rehabilitation (4–6 weeks, T4), and on the last day of cardiac rehabilitation (10–12 weeks) (T5). Completing these questionnaires will take approximately 15 min per session. All measurements will take place during routine appointments at the hospital and as part of the outpatient cardiac rehabilitation at the location where the participant is training.

Additionally, two AX3 accelerometers will be placed, on the upper arm and upper thigh using Tegaderm patches (details: see Halfwerk et al. [16]) on the first post-operative day. These AX3 accelerometers will measure the level of movement starting from day 1 postoperatively up to a maximum of 7 days (or earlier when discharged at T1, T2, and T3). Participants have previously indicated that they did not find the AX3 accelerometers burdensome, restrictive, or uncomfortable [16].

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

Participants may withdraw from the study at any time. This decision will have no consequences for their medical care. The investigator may also decide to withdraw a subject from the study for urgent medical reasons. Specific criteria for discontinuation of the study for an individual participant include severe sternal complications, such as recurrent DSWI or the need for sternal refixation. Available data will be handled as an intention-to-treat analysis.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

The primary strategy to ensure sustained implementation of the intervention is to actively inform all relevant healthcare professionals when a T-REX participant is admitted to the clinic or enrolled in cardiac rehabilitation.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

Concomitant care and interventions are described in Tables 3 and 4. Sternal pain between hospital discharge (T3) and end of cardiac rehabilitation (T5) may be treated according to the physician’s discretion. We do not prohibit any type of care in any of our trial arms.

Outcomes {12}

Primary outcomes

The first primary outcome is to evaluate whether the T-REX Twente sternal precautions (Fig. 1), in comparison to the standard sternal precautions used in TCT, have an effect on the quality of life (QLMI-2) of participants following a median sternotomy, from preoperative (T0) to the start of cardiac rehabilitation 4–6 weeks postoperatively (T4).

The second primary objective is to assess the level of participant mobilization as the relative proportion of time spent out of bed during the day, measured by an AX3 accelerometer (Axivity, Newcastle upon Tyne, UK) until the 4th post-operative day (T2). The participant pathway with all measurements is described in Fig. 3.

Secondary outcomes

A secondary outcome of the study is to determine whether the T-REX Twente sternal precautions affect pain, as measured by the Numeric Pain Rating Scale during T0, T2, T4, and at the end of the cardiac rehabilitation (T5) (NPRS). Furthermore, the study aims to investigate whether the T-REX Twente sternal precautions have an effect on the degree of movement-related fear, as measured by the Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia, during T0 (preoperatively), T2, T4, and at the end of cardiac rehabilitation (T5).

Another secondary objective is to assess the effect of the T-REX Twente sternal precautions on both the number of minutes per day and the relative proportion of time spent on individual activities (lying in bed, sitting, standing, walking, cycling, stair climbing) during daytime (7:00–23:00) as measured by the AX3 accelerometers [16]. We measured from the 1 st day postoperative (T1) until discharge or up to a maximum of 7 days post-heart surgery (T3) in line with previous work [16].

Additionally, the study aims to investigate whether the T-REX Twente sternal precautions are associated with a higher incidence of sternal complications within 30 days, using a composite endpoint of sternal refixation, superficial sternal wound infection, and deep sternal wound infection (DSWI).

The hypothesis is that participants will resume activities of daily living more quickly and experience less anxiety, as they are able to do more and are allowed to engage in more activities.

Sample size {14}

The primary outcome of this study is QLMI-2, the validated Dutch translation of the MacNew questionnaire [11]. Previous research using the QLMI-2 in a cohort of 677 cardiac rehabilitation patients indicated that the difference in response scores is normally distributed, with a standard deviation of 0.25 [18]. Another study showed an increase in QLMI-2 scores from 5.2 to 6.1 between hospital discharge (comparable to T3 in our study) and the intake for cardiac rehabilitation (comparable to T4 in our study), reflecting a change of 0.9 [19].

Assuming a small effect size of 0.15 (Cohen’s d [20]) and a statistical power of 80%, a total of 138 participants are required (69 per group) to detect a group*time interaction across three measurement points (T0, T2, and T4). The effect size is based on the expectation that the adapted lifestyle sternal precautions will not result in large differences. As this is a minimal, non-costly intervention, even a small effect can be considered clinically relevant. This calculation is based on repeated ANOVA measures with an alpha of 0.05, power of 0.80, and a nonsphericity correction of 1, computed using G*Power 3.1.9.7.

To account for an estimated 10% dropout rate, we plan to include 77 participants per group, resulting in a total sample size of 154 participants. The anticipated 10% dropout is based on participants who may die as a result of surgery, miss assessments due to post-operative delirium, or experience an extended ICU stay exceeding 72 h. The 30-day mortality rate is approximately 1.3%, and about 10% of participants experience prolonged ICU stays. This results in a total sample size of 154 participants, after appropriate rounding.

Recruitment {15}

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Patients will only be asked to participate in the study by a physiotherapist during preoperative hospital stay (urgent surgery) or during preoperative outpatient screening (elective surgery) (see section 26a).

Sequence generation {16a}

Randomization is generated through CS-Research HiX 6.3, a digital randomization system, where data are pre-entered to ensure equivalent distribution between the two groups. Participants are randomly allocated to control and intervention groups in a 1:1 ratio with a block size of 4. The researcher informs the healthcare professionals of the patient’s group allocation, enabling them to apply the appropriate intervention. Additionally, the researcher ensures that accelerometers and questionnaires are provided to the patient.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

Randomization is performed digitally using CS-Research HiX 6.3 after the patient has provided written informed consent. Until surgery, only authorized staff members have access to the patient’s group allocation. The rest of the study personnel remain blinded to this information until postoperative. Blinding is only possible for the participant and the assessor of outcomes, as the staff needs to know the assigned treatment in order to provide appropriate care. This results in a single-blind design where participants and outcome assessors are blinded.

Implementation {16c}

During the preoperative outpatient screening, the physiotherapist discusses the patient’s potential interest in the study, provides the participant information sheet, informed consent form, and questionnaires, and addresses any questions the patient may have. In cases of an “urgent” clinical admission [14], the patient is informed about the study a few days prior to surgery. If interested, the researcher or delegated physiotherapist is notified, after which further information is provided, and consent is requested. Access to source data and any other patient-identifiable information is restricted to the researchers KR and NW, as well as the principal investigator, FH. All study data are coded and accessible only to the researchers. The coding consists of a unique identifier, for example, H24-005-001.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

Due to the study design, physiotherapists, nurses, and other healthcare professionals cannot be blinded. The participant will be blinded; however, it is possible that they may deduce their group assignment based on the instructions or through conversations with other patients. The investigator (FH) is responsible for the analysis and will therefore be blinded for group assignment.

Procedure for unblinding {17b}

The professionals involved in patient care are not blinded, which means that if the intervention arm of the study should be discontinued, this can be done without obstructions. Only if a significant difference is found in sternal complications after 30 days during the interim analysis, to the disadvantage of the T-REX Twente intervention arm, will FH, who conducts the analysis and is unaware of the group assignments (control or intervention), be fully unblinded.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

Over a period of approximately 12 weeks, participants will complete the three questionnaires QLMI-2, TSK, and NPRS at the following time points: preoperatively (T0), post-operative day 4 (T2), on the first day of cardiac rehabilitation (T4, approximately 4–6 weeks post-operative), and on the final day of cardiac rehabilitation (T5, approximately 10–12 weeks post-operative). Completing the questionnaires will take approximately 15 min per time. Questionnaire descriptions with reliability and validity are available elsewhere (www.meetinstrumentenindezorg.nl).

All assessments will take place during routine clinical appointments at MST and during outpatient cardiac rehabilitation sessions at the location where the participant is enrolled. Questionnaires will be securely stored and documented in the electronic health records, which will also be used to manage the timeline and study phases.

Additionally, two AX3 accelerometers will be applied on the upper arm and upper leg (see [16]) by either a nurse or physiotherapist on post-operative day 1. These AX3 accelerometers will monitor physical activity levels continuously from post-operative day 1 for a period of up to 7 days (or until discharge, if earlier) at T1, T2, and T3.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}

Contact with patients is initiated during their designated hospital visiting hours and throughout their participation in the cardiac rehabilitation program. A structured logbook is maintained throughout the study to document all participating participants by their anonymized patient number, in conjunction with the date of surgery and all relevant measurement time points. Upon inclusion in the study, patients’ data are recorded in the logbook, facilitating systematic tracking of the total number of enrolled participants, those who have commenced the study protocol, and those who have completed all scheduled assessments.

In cases where participants discontinue participation, data collected up to the point of withdrawal will still be included in the final analysis.

An additional follow-up strategy involves scheduling appointments to contact participants by telephone or email, specifically to encourage completion of the questionnaires corresponding to measurement of time points T4 and T5. These questionnaires are then provided either digitally or via postal mail, accompanied by a return envelope to facilitate their return.

Data management {19}

All data pertaining to study participants are collected, stored, and managed exclusively by trained study personnel and research assistants. Physical documents, including signed informed consent forms and paper-based questionnaires, are immediately filed in a secure folder stored behind locked doors. Access to these physical records is restricted to research assistants and researchers.

Digital copies of the informed consent forms and completed questionnaires are stored in a secure Microsoft Teams environment, accessible only to authorized research personnel. In addition, questionnaire data are entered into a pre-configured list within the HiX system (HiX Research module).

Subsequently, data from HiX Research are exported by the research team to an SPSS or Excel file on a local, encrypted hard drive. This file is coded and anonymized, with the code key accessible only to the principal investigators and designated research assistants. The hard drive is stored in a locked cabinet, access to which is restricted solely to the research team.

All data processing and analysis are conducted on coded and de-identified datasets within the MST infrastructure, ensuring compliance with institutional and data protection regulations.

Confidentiality {27}

Participants’ individual study information will not be shared without written permission of the participant, except for mandatory study monitoring and safety purposes. See the “Data management {19}” section for details on storage and handling.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

Time points and variable information were previously described in Fig. 4. Statistical analysis will be performed using repeated measures of analysis within a mixed model framework, employing SPSS software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). The primary focus will be on the interaction effect between group allocation (intervention versus control) and time. A p value < 0.05 will be considered indicative of statistical significance.

For the second primary outcome, the relative percentage of time spent out of bed during daytime hours (defined as 06:00–23:00) will be assessed using two AX3 accelerometers [16], applied during the intensive care unit (ICU) stay and continued on the general ward until post-operative day 4. Previous studies have demonstrated that, on the first day following ICU discharge, patients spend approximately 60% of the daytime lying down, with a daily decrease of 6.5% (p < 0.001) [16].

Interim analyses {21b}

An interim analysis for safety will be conducted once 30 participants have been included in each group. If this interim analysis shows that the intervention group has 3 more patients reaching the combined endpoint than the control group, the study will be stopped. Public outcomes from the Netherlands Heart Registration (2020–2024) for the combined endpoint of sternal wound complications were 1.3% in MST and 0.8% in NL (range 0.4–1.8%) [1]. A higher interim analysis threshold has been chosen due to the small number of patients included, which may present a distorted picture.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {20b}

Netherlands Heart Registration (NHR) variables [12] will be analyzed in the same manner as the secondary outcomes. Normally distributed baseline variables will be analyzed using an unpaired Student’s t-test, or Mann-Whitney U test if non-parametric. Categorical variables will be analyzed using Fisher’s exact test.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

Continuous variables over time, such as QLMI-2 and TSK questionnaires, and NPRS changing over the 4 phases, will be analyzed via a mixed model repeated measurement analysis. The advantage of this model is that incidental missing data can be estimated by patterns of other participants. No data will be imputed.

Plans to give access to the full protocol, participant-level data, and statistical code {31c}

We are exploring the possibility of publishing the anonymized data under open access in accordance with FAIR principles in the 4TU.ResearchData repository.

Oversight and monitoring

Composition of the coordinating center and trial steering committee {5d}

The day-to-day conduct of the trial is overseen by the PI in collaboration with the research assistants. Regular team meetings are held on a weekly basis between the PI and research assistants to monitor study progress and coordinate ongoing activities. Each week, the research assistants update the study team regarding participants scheduled for surgery or commencing cardiac rehabilitation. This ensures timely attention to data collection and management procedures at the relevant measurement time points.

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role and reporting structure {21a}

MST adheres to the Dutch Federation of University Medical Centers (NFU) “Monitoring” guideline. Specific information is outlined in the monitoring plan, dated/versioned (27-09−2023/2.3). This study includes an institutional monitoring advisor responsible for overseeing participant safety. After the first five participants are enrolled in both the control and intervention groups, the monitoring advisor will assess participant safety. Monitoring visits will occur multiple times throughout the study (approximately 2 to 4 times) to ensure continued participant safety.

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

No study-related adverse events (AEs) or serious adverse events (SAEs) are anticipated, given the minimal-risk nature of the intervention. The potential risks associated with the intervention have been previously assessed in KYMITT studies [7, 8].

Passive safety monitoring will be conducted by the research staff throughout the study. Any observed or reported AEs or SAEs will be communicated to the PI, who holds responsibility for reporting them in accordance with the applicable regulations to the Central Committee on Research Involving Human Subjects (CCMO). All adverse events will be recorded in HiX and, if necessary, will be discussed with the research team, TCT management, and an epidemiologist from MST.

In the publication of the trial results, the total number of reported AEs and SAEs will be included to ensure full transparency regarding the safety profile of the intervention.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {23}

In case of significant errors or concerns regarding safety, monitoring of the relevant area will be increased as deemed reasonable, in consultation with institutional monitors. For source data verification, at least 1 participant will be monitored for 100% on all data collected. Initiation visits with monitors are conducted before study starts, and 1 to 2 times per year (see Table 5). The frequency may be adjusted during the study if desired.

Discussion

Throughout the course of the study, there has been an increase in the number of open-heart surgeries performed using a minimally invasive approach. Because a mini-sternotomy or transaxillary access route might affect the studied protocol, we decided not to increase our inclusion criteria. As a result, we may experience delays in the inclusion of participants for the study. However, findings from this research could provide valuable insights for the physiotherapeutic approach to this patient population as well.

Limitations

One of our exclusion criteria is patients who remain in the ICU for more than 72 h. These patients typically represent a more vulnerable subgroup with increased morbidity and mortality, and delayed mobilization. Excluding this population may limit the representativeness of our study population to clinical practice. Other centers have incorporated an all-comer approach for less restrictive sternal precautions [8], and may be applied to all cardiac surgery populations regardless of ICU stay or access route like minimally invasive surgery.

Next, a single-center design may restrict the external validity of the findings. Patients were randomized to limit selection bias, yet results may reflect institutional protocols, or clinical expertise of one hospital.
