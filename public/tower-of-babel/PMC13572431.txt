Quantifying HIV transmission in the context of armed conflict in the Tigray Region, Northern Ethiopia: a dynamic risk equation model
Authors: Abdelkadir Muzey Mohammed, Habtu Alemayehu Atsbeha, Yohannes Yirga Kefela, Woldegebriel Assefa Woldegerima, Kiros Tedla Gebrehiwot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The two-year armed conflict in the Tigray Region of Northern Ethiopia (2020–2022) created a humanitarian crisis marked by widespread sexual violence, healthcare disruption, and displacement of health personnel. However, reliable epidemiological evidence on the contribution of conflict-related sexual violence to HIV transmission remains scarce. This study addresses this gap by quantifying the impact of conflict-related rape on HIV incidence and prevalence in Tigray. A dynamic risk equation model was developed to capture HIV transmission during conflict. In this framework, conflict intensity influences key transmission drivers, including rape prevalence, number of perpetrators, trauma severity, reduced post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) use, and healthcare service degradation. Data from the Central Statistics Agency of Ethiopia, UNAIDS regional estimates, World Health Organization, Ethiopian Public Health Institute, and relevant literature were used. Uncertainty analyses were conducted, complemented by global sensitivity analysis and numerical simulations. This model-based study indicates that mass rape could cause a median of 122 (interquartile range, IQR: 13–681) new HIV infections, indicating conflict-related mass rape could cause a median increase of 13.8% (IQR: 1.9%–62.5%), while the median increase in prevalence could be 0.002% (IQR: 0.00027%–0.0089%). These results suggest the increase in incidence due to rape is epidemiologically notable though the effect on population prevalence is small, reflecting that escalating conflict intensity characterized by increased rape frequency, declining health system capacity, and reduced PEP coverage further amplifies HIV transmission. Integrated strategies, including prevention of sexual violence, timely PEP access, and mobile health services, are essential to mitigate HIV transmission. Humanitarian agencies and conflicting parties should act on both public health and human rights grounds.

Introduction

Sexual violence in conflict has been widely documented in Burundi, Sierra Leone, Rwanda, the Democratic Republic of Congo, Liberia, Sudan, and Uganda1, and most recently in Tigray, Northern Ethiopia2. Armed conflict is often accompanied by widespread sexual violence, displacement, food insecurity, and collapse of health services3–5. Sub-Saharan Africa, which bears the highest HIV burden, is also the region most affected by armed conflicts6. Consequently, conflicts are recognized as accelerators of HIV spread, contributing to increased incidence and prevalence7,8. The Tigray war (2020–2022) exemplifies these dynamics. Reports confirmed widespread rape and other forms of sexual violence were perpetrated against children and older persons, with ages ranging from nine to 60 years old9,10. Community surveys indicated that 43% of women experienced sexual and gender-based violence (SGBV) and nearly 8% were raped, while fewer than 10% accessed support services11. Estimates suggest that 100,000–120,000 women were raped12,13, with rates exceeding those documented in Sierra Leone, Uganda, and Ukraine2.

Patterns of conflict-related sexual violence in Tigray included consecutive, individual, and multiple-perpetrator rapes, involving up to fifteen perpetrators from different armed forces and allied militias2,4,9,10. Many women and girls experienced gang rape by three to nine soldiers, with gang rapes accounting for 68.4% of cases2. According to Insight Insecurity, among 106 surveyed survivors, 67% reported a single rape, while 33% experienced mass rape involving multiple perpetrators; of these 33 cases, 76% involved multiple perpetrators and 24% involved a single perpetrator4.

At the same time, the health system collapsed: 70% of hospitals, 83% of health centers, and over 700 health posts became non-functional14. Presence of armed forces, unsafe transport, drug shortages, and traditional barriers restricted access to care3,9,15. The United Nations Population Fund16 reported that only 1% of facilities provided rape management services, leaving over 90% of survivors without timely post-exposure prophylaxis. As a result, many women developed untreated injuries, infections, and elevated HIV risk. Fisseha et al.2 found 2.7% of survivors contracted HIV, while regional prevalence rose from 1.4% pre-war to 3%17.

More generally, conflict event data and qualitative reports from sources including the Armed Conflict Location & Event Data Project18, Human Rights Watch19, the Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs20, and the International Human Rights Commission Experts Ethiopia & United Nations9 highlight that the intensity of the conflict during 2021–2022 can reasonably be represented on a normalized scale from 0 to 1, with peak periods of violence in 2021 corresponding to values approaching 0.8, reflecting maximum disruption of health services21.

Armed conflict increases HIV transmission risk through multiple interlinked pathways: (1) a direct increase in sexual violence, particularly mass rape with multiple perpetrators; (2) the disruption of health systems, leading to the collapse of HIV prevention, testing, and treatment services; (3) reduced access to PEP for survivors; and (4) increased population displacement and mobility, which can accelerate transmission. This study focuses on integrating the first three pathways through a conflict-intensity-dependent modeling framework.

This study’s findings are relevant to national and international strategies, including Ethiopia’s National HIV/AIDS Strategic Plan and UN Security Council resolutions on conflict-related sexual violence (e.g., UNSCR 1820, 1888), which emphasize the need for integrated prevention and response in humanitarian settings.

Despite the scale of violence and its consequences on the Tigray health system, reliable epidemiological evidence on SGBV during conflict remains scarce2,4,9. Specifically, no quantitative estimates exist for the number of HIV infections directly attributable to conflict-related rape in Tigray, representing a critical knowledge gap for humanitarian response and public health planning. This is in line with Tewabe et al.22, who reported that no rigorous empirical studies have documented the extent of SGBV and its impact on HIV epidemics in Northern Ethiopia, necessitating the use of mathematical models23–26. Mathematical modeling provides a vital alternative to estimate the impact of SGBV on the HIV epidemic where direct data are unavailable; for instance, see the works of Mwangi & Luboobi26, Anema et al.23, and Supervie et al.24. Anema et al.23 and Supervie et al.24 developed a static risk equation model to quantify the HIV infections attributable to conflict-related mass rape in sub-Saharan African countries. Building on these foundations, the present study develops a dynamic risk equation model that extends earlier static models. The key innovations include: (1) a cumulative exposure risk from multiple perpetrators, (2) a dynamic transmission probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _r(c)$$\end{document} that is a function of conflict intensity, and (3) the integration of trauma-related risks and the synergistic effects of health system collapse and PEP decline. This approach aims to quantify how mass rape during the Tigray conflict contributed to HIV incidence and prevalence, offering structured insights for humanitarian response and public health planning.

Methods

Conceptual framework of the study

The conceptual framework, illustrated in Fig. 1, maps the causal pathways from conflict intensity to the risk of HIV transmission. This framework is built on the central premise that conflict is the primary exogenous driver, acting as a “force multiplier” that cascades through demographic, behavioral, and systemic vulnerabilities to produce adverse health outcomes. The model architecture reveals a complex, nonlinear system where the whole is greater than the sum of its parts, and its value lies in its ability to disaggregate a seemingly intractable problem into measurable, interacting components.

Conflict intensity as the core driver

At the apex of the framework is Conflict Intensity (c). The model conceptualizes conflict not as a binary state but as a continuous variable. This is a critical advancement, as it allows for the calibration of the model across different conflict phases—from low-intensity instability to full-scale war. The diagram emanating from this central node signifies the pervasive, often chaotic, and nonlinear way conflict disrupts every facet of society. It does not simply increase the number of risk factors but fundamentally alters their nature and interrelationships. The dynamic nature of our model is based on the conflict intensity, which influences several factors to vary.

Model formulation

This study builds upon the seminal works of Anema et al23. and Supervie et al24., which developed static risk equation models to estimate HIV infections arising from mass rape in sub-Saharan African countries. In their framework, the number of new infections is a function of the proportion of women raped, HIV prevalence among assailants, per-act transmission probability, and the at-risk female population aged 15–49.

We extend this earlier work by developing a dynamic model where conflict-related drivers of HIV infection are explicitly linked to conflict intensity (c). Data sources and model analysis

Estimating HIV transmission risk from conflict-related sexual violence presents profound methodological challenges, particularly in active conflict settings such as Tigray. The collection of primary data during ongoing hostilities is constrained by limited humanitarian access, security risks to researchers and respondents, the displacement of populations, the destruction of health information systems, and the severe trauma experienced by survivors, which complicates ethical data collection2,15,20. Furthermore, conflict-related sexual violence is systematically underreported due to stigma, fear of reprisal, and the collapse of formal reporting mechanisms30,33. Consequently, traditional epidemiological surveillance is largely infeasible, necessitating a reliance on secondary data sources and methodological approaches that can rigorously address these limitations.

To address the paucity of data for assessing the consequences of mass rape on HIV incidence and prevalence in Tigray, we employed a multi-pronged strategy. Parameters were estimated based on: (1) available study results and humanitarian reports documenting the pattern of the conflict and conflict-related sexual violence; (2) regional epidemiological data from proximate settings; (3) clinical and biological parameters from established literature; and (4) necessary extrapolation from comparable conflict settings where more robust data exist31–33.

Given the inherent uncertainties in these data sources, we implemented a comprehensive approach to uncertainty management: Triangulation: All parameters were triangulated from multiple credible sources to enhance validity and reduce source-specific bias. Primary data sources included: UNAIDS regional HIV estimates36, World Health Organization clinical guidelines38, Ethiopian Public Health Institute epidemiological reports37, peer-reviewed literature on conflict-related sexual violence1,23,24, and humanitarian situation reports documenting the Tigray conflict4,9,16. The mean in this dataset (444.75) is a value pulled upward by the extremely high estimate of 1,925, highlighting that it may not reflect the typical or expected number of new infections39. The right-skewed distribution of estimates (Table 2) indicates that the potential for a large number of new infections is driven by the co-occurrence of high-risk conditions–specifically a high proportion of the population experiencing rape, involvement of multiple perpetrators, and intense conflict that cripples the health system–rather than being a typical outcome. This is in line with Hoaglin et al.40, who reported that the median estimate is often more appropriate than the mean, especially in public health data characterized by large variability. Our intermediary estimate is, therefore, most likely to be representative of the actual situation of the conflict in Tigray, as it was also used by Anema et al.23 as well as Supervie et al.24.

Consequently, our estimation shows that mass rape could cause a median of 122 (IQR: 13–681) new HIV infections. This highlights that the median of new HIV infections due to mass rape could be 2 per 100,000 individuals of the Tigray population per year. Furthermore, the result shows that widespread rape could cause about 31 new HIV infections per 100,000 individuals of the Tigray population in the most extreme scenarios where: 15% of the female population aged 5–49 years were raped, HIV prevalence among perpetrators was 8 times the weighted prevalence of the assailants’ country population prevalence, the average number of assailants per woman per year was 4, conflict intensity was 0.8, trauma-related exposure was maximum 1.0, PEP decline was maximum 0.8, health system degradation was maximum 0.9, and the HIV transmission rate was highest at 4 times the average high rate. This high-end scenario corresponds to the most severe documented conditions, which in Tigray (2021) was characterized by widespread violence and near-total health system collapse. Recent empirical studies from Tigray have documented wartime HIV prevalence of 1.2% among tested individuals42 and poor HIV outcomes increasing from 28.6% pre-war to 54.5% during active conflict43, providing contextual validation for our modeled estimates.

Increase in incidence and prevalence

Health system degradation operates as a proximate mechanism through which conflict intensity amplifies transmission risk, while rape proportion, number of assailants, assailant HIV prevalence, cumulative exposure, trauma, and PEP coverage all contribute to shaping the magnitude and velocity of infection spread. Overall, Figs. 3 and 4 provide a coherent picture of how structural, behavioral, biological, and health-system factors interact synergistically to drive HIV transmission during armed conflict, with conflict intensity serving as the fundamental force multiplier across all pathways.

Discussion

The discussion below interprets these model-based estimates. Readers should keep in mind the limitations of the input data and the model structure, which are described in the following section.

The impact of mass rape on HIV epidemics in Tigray remains largely unknown due to the difficulty of collecting comprehensive data during the conflict. This study employed a dynamic risk equation model to quantify the contribution of conflict-related rape to HIV transmission, incorporating trauma-related exposure, PEP coverage, and health system accessibility as functions of conflict intensity and cumulative exposure risk. Model parameters were informed by regional epidemiological data from UNAIDS, WHO, EPHI, and relevant literature, and calibrated through 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations to capture uncertainty.

The numerical simulations (Figs. 3 and 4) reveal how conflict-driven factors interact to shape HIV transmission. As conflict intensifies, health system functionality and PEP coverage decline. Small initial losses produce moderate infection increases, but near-total collapse leads to disproportionately large surges, reflecting the disruption documented in Tigray where 70% of hospitals became non-functional. Cumulative exposure risk, driven by assailant HIV prevalence and number of perpetrators, amplifies transmission beyond additive effects. The transition from single to multiple assailants produces the largest risk increase, consistent with Tigray’s 68.4% gang rape prevalence. Trauma-related exposure increases susceptibility through biological mechanisms but exhibits diminishing returns at extreme levels. Conflict intensity itself emerges as a critical driver: infections rise steeply beyond a threshold, underscoring that early intervention may yield disproportionately large reductions in HIV burden.

Comparing our findings with earlier studies, Anema et al.23 estimated that conflict-related rape in sub-Saharan Africa could lead to a 5–28% increase in HIV incidence, while Supervie et al.24 reported a 0.5–2.0% increase in prevalence in specific conflict zones. Our median estimate of a 13.8% increase in incidence is consistent with these ranges, despite the lower baseline HIV prevalence in Tigray (1.4% pre-war). Recent empirical evidence from Tigray supports our estimates: Weledegebriel et al.43 documented that poor HIV outcomes increased from 28.6% pre-war to 54.5% during active conflict, with ART drug availability dropping by 68.8%. Similarly, Shfare et al.42 reported a wartime HIV prevalence of 1.2% among tested individuals, with 36% STI prevalence and evidence of sexual violence in 19.4% of female STI cases.

The modest increase in overall prevalence (0.002%) is explained by the large denominator–the total population (6.25 million) and the existing pool of people living with HIV (approximately 49,141). The additional 122 new infections from mass rape are small relative to this pool. However, the 13.8% relative increase in incidence is epidemiologically notable, representing a sudden surge that can disrupt routine prevention and treatment services during a humanitarian crisis.

Sensitivity analysis revealed that the most influential factors driving HIV incidence were conflict intensity, health system coverage, rape proportion, number of assailants, assailant HIV prevalence, PEP coverage, and trauma-related exposure. These findings provide actionable guidance for prioritizing interventions. The study’s methodological framework offers a mechanistic understanding of HIV transmission risk associated with mass rape.

The analysis underscores that increasing rape incidences, combined with declining health system capacity and reduced PEP coverage during active conflict, contribute to new HIV infections. This highlights the urgent need for humanitarian agencies to implement targeted measures, including rapid PEP provision, mobile health services, and protective programs. Long-term consequences of armed conflict may exacerbate HIV epidemics post-conflict through health infrastructure collapse and displacement of health workers. Investment priorities should include rebuilding health facilities, strengthening survivor-centered care, and restoring comprehensive HIV prevention programs. Conflict-related sexual violence has been recognized more broadly as a driver of infectious disease outbreaks, including HIV, in humanitarian settings worldwide44.

Challenging core model assumptions

Several key assumptions warrant explicit consideration, as they directly shape the model’s outputs and the interpretation of its results. First, the model assumes a linear aggregation of risk factors (Assumption 5), which may underestimate the synergistic effect of co-occurring risks (e.g., trauma and lack of PEP). Consequently, the results may represent a conservative estimate of true transmission risk, particularly in settings where multiple vulnerabilities converge. Second, the parameters are treated as independent (Assumption 4), although in reality, conflict intensity simultaneously influences multiple factors. This simplification, while necessary for tractability, may obscure the compounding effects that characterize complex humanitarian emergencies, suggesting that the true uncertainty surrounding the estimates may be even greater than reported. Third, the baseline HIV prevalence among perpetrators is assumed constant, which may have fluctuated during the conflict. If perpetrators came from regions with higher HIV prevalence than our weighted average, our estimates could be conservative; conversely, if the trauma-related risk factor E is overestimated (Assumption 1), our results could be inflated. These directional biases underscore the importance of interpreting the findings with attention to the underlying assumptions.

The wide interquartile ranges and broad confidence intervals underscore the substantial uncertainty inherent in estimating outcomes under conflict conditions. This uncertainty should be interpreted not as a weakness of the analysis, but as an honest reflection of the variability in conflict experiences–a variability that is amplified by the very assumptions examined above. For decision-makers, this means that planning should be scenario-based, focusing on interventions (such as ensuring PEP availability) that are effective across a range of plausible outcomes, regardless of how the model’s assumptions are specified.

Limitations

Despite these insights, the study has limitations. First, the study relied on secondary data with variable quality, as primary data collection was not feasible. The conflict in Tigray was characterized by the destruction of key health institutions, many districts remaining inaccessible, and survivors facing significant stigma and risk of retaliation. Under these constraints, traditional hospital- or community-based data collection was neither operationally feasible nor ethically justifiable. The model does not capture onward HIV transmission through sexual partnerships or mother-to-child transmission, nor does it account for variations in individual behavior, co-infections, or socio-economic factors. Third, the model’s representation of time via conflict intensity (c) is a proxy; it does not capture the precise timing of rape events relative to PEP availability, which could affect transmission risk. Fourth, the assumption of parameter independence may not hold, potentially leading to either over- or under-estimation of risk. These limitations emphasize the need for comprehensive, context-specific data and advanced modeling approaches, such as dynamic compartmental or agent-based models, to better evaluate the long-term impacts and intervention scenarios of conflict-related sexual violence on HIV transmission.

Conclusion

This model-based study indicates that conflict-related rape during the Tigray war markedly increased HIV incidence by a median of 13.8%, highlighting an urgent public health challenge. While overall prevalence remained minimally affected (median absolute increase of 0.002%), the elevated incidence underscores the critical need for comprehensive strategies combining prevention of sexual violence, timely PEP interventions, and resilient healthcare infrastructure. Strengthening health systems, expanding post-exposure prophylaxis coverage, and providing survivor-centered support are essential to mitigate HIV transmission in conflict-affected populations. For humanitarian agencies and conflicting parties, this means prioritizing: (1) deploying mobile health units to provide PEP and survivor-centered care in hard-to-reach areas; (2) training community health workers in SGBV first-line support and HIV risk assessment; and (3) re-establishing functional health facilities with dedicated services for SGBV survivors as part of the post-conflict recovery plan. Integrating social protection measures with biomedical interventions is crucial for addressing the complex drivers of HIV risk in war-affected settings. This study introduces a novel conflict-intensity-driven dynamic modeling framework that goes beyond static risk equations by capturing the interplay of escalating violence, health system collapse, and cumulative perpetrator exposure. Future research employing more sophisticated modeling techniques, such as dynamic compartmental or agent-based models, is needed to fully capture epidemic dynamics and the potential effects of interventions in such complex humanitarian contexts.
