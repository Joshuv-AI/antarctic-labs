The WDM Time-Frequency Transform in Gravitational-Wave Data Analysis I: Formalism Preprint: APS/123-QED
Aaron D. Johnson, Katerina Chatziioannou, Jake Summers

Abstract
For slowly-varying noise, time-frequency methods offer a natural middle ground between the efficiency of the frequency domain and the generality of the more expensive time domain. Despite growing adoption, such methods remain less well documented and less familiar in the gravitational-wave literature, compared to the ubiquitous frequency domain. Aimed at gravitational-wave analysts, in this paper we present a self-contained account of time-frequency methods, detail derivations for a specific basis, namely the Wilson-Daubechies-Meyer (WDM) basis, and share intuition and lessons learned. We document key concepts: the orthogonality and good time-frequency localization of the basis, the edge effects at the DC and Nyquist frequencies, the forward and inverse transforms and their practical implementation, and the noise covariance matrix and likelihood in the time-frequency domain.



I
Introduction

Time-frequency analyses are rooted in Gabor’s seminal paper that applied the mathematics of quantum mechanics to signal processing
11
.
Gabor constructed a transform that uses a system of windowed complex exponentials to produce a time-frequency representation on a time-frequency grid (which he called the “information plane”) consisting of a set of (not necessarily orthogonal) “quanta of information,” or
logons
.
A Gabor system is a collection of time-frequency shifted “atoms” of the form

g_{mn}(t)=e^{2\pi imbt}g(t-na)\,,

(1)

where
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
a,b
are step sizes for translation in time and frequency respectively such that
g_{mn}
is centered at
(an,bm)
.
The sampling density is related to the product
ab
.
Tiling the atoms so that they exactly cover the time-frequency plane without overlapping yields a critically-sampled system with
ab=1
.
1
1

1

In more familiar notation that we will adopt later, this corresponds to
\Delta T\Delta F=1
for the complex Gabor basis, where
\Delta T
and
\Delta F
are the bin sizes in time and frequency respectively. For WDM,
\Delta T\Delta F=1/2
as its coefficients are real.

If
ab>1
, the system is incomplete and there are signals that cannot be represented by the atoms.
If
ab<1
, the system is overcomplete: the atoms are not orthonormal and a
given signal has no unique representation.
Critical sampling comes at high cost: the Balian-Low theorem
1
;
21
prohibits critically-sampled, orthonormal Gabor bases that are also well-localized, i.e., have a finite variance, in both time and frequency,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^{2}|g(t)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\infty\quad\mathrm{or}\quad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f^{2}|\tilde{g}(f)|^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f=\infty\,.

(2)

This means that either the time or frequency domain (or both) window must have poor localization.
This situation is familiar from the common Fourier transform where a box function (well-localized in time) is transformed to a sinc function (spans all frequencies).
2
2

2

The Balian-Low theorem applies to the entire basis of Gabor atoms, not to its individual elements. Each atom may saturate the Fourier uncertainty principle and be well localized in both time and frequency. The theorem states that a basis constructed from those atoms will not be simultaneously orthogonal and critically sampled.

Three escape routes around the conditions of the Balian-Low theorem achieve simultaneous good time and frequency localization:

•

Gabor frames at overcritical density,
ab<1
,
give up orthonormality and accept a redundant representation. In a data analysis context, this complicates the likelihood calculation which is no longer a simple sum over bins. An example of overcritical sampling is the short-time Fourier transform.

•

Wavelet transforms
replace the uniform time-frequency grid with a non-uniform dyadic one, thus gaining localization at the cost of frequency-dependent resolution.

•

Wilson bases
keep both critical sampling and orthonormality by replacing the complex exponentials of the Gabor system with real trigonometric functions on a half-shifted lattice.

The Wilson-Daubechies-Meyer (WDM) transform takes the third route.

Originally proposed in the context of the coherent WaveBurst pipeline that searches for unmodeled excess power
19
, the WDM transform combines three insights.
First, in 1987
31
proposed real cosine and sine modulations on a half-shifted lattice, rather than the complex exponential of Eq. (
1
), as a way to evade the Balian-Low theorem at critical density.
Second, in 1991
7
proved that if a window that satisfies certain conditions generates a Gabor system at twice the critical sampling density, then the equivalent Wilson system is an orthonormal basis with rapid time-domain decay (exponential for smooth windows, algebraic for compact windows).
The critically-sampled and orthonormal Wilson basis that is constructed from the overcomplete Gabor frame falls outside the conditions of the Balian-Low theorem as it uses real modulations instead of complex exponentials.
Together, the above results constitute the “Wilson-Daubechies” component of the WDM transform.
Third, the Meyer scaling function
23
is a smooth window function that is compact in frequency and satisfies the conditions of Ref.
7
.
The Wilson-Daubechies basis guarantees orthonormality, critical sampling, and good time and frequency localization.
The Meyer window guarantees compact frequency support such that a signal with limited frequencies appears in few time-frequency cells and reduces false-alarms in excess power searches.
Implementation-wise, the WDM transform requires
\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
operations
3
.

More recently,
3
proposed the WDM transform in the context of modeled searches and inference as a way to handle nonstationary noise.
Such analyses rely on noise-weighted inner products of the form

(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{b})\equiv\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\,,

(3)

where
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
are data representations in some (yet unspecified) basis and
\mathbf{C}
is the noise covariance matrix in the same basis.
The inner product can be evaluated in any basis, including the frequency
6
, time
14
, or WDM
3
, as long as the data and noise covariance are calculated self-consistently.
For stationary noise, the Fourier domain is the clear choice as the noise covariance is diagonal
29
;
18
and the Gaussian likelihood reduces to the Whittle likelihood
28
.
However, in theory nothing precludes a frequency-domain non-diagonal covariance and associated inner product.
In practice, therefore, the choice of basis is driven by practical considerations such as orthonormality and computational efficiency
24
or compactness of the signal and noise representation.
The WDM basis is convenient given its orthogonality and
\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
cost, while also guaranteeing that signals whose power is concentrated along a track in time-frequency such as gravitational-wave chirps have sparse representations
3
.
In the context of LISA
2
,
8
showed that it can model the confusion noise’s cyclostationarity caused by the satellite’s motion around the sun, while
9
inferred the properties of stellar-origin binaries assuming a known, but nonstationary, noise.

The motivation for this work is to provide a self-contained description of the WDM transform that can serve as an aid toward implementation, discuss its advantages and disadvantages and relation to the more familiar time- and frequency-domain representations, and impart intuition in light of its rising popularity.
Prior treatments leave the derivation, discretization, edge bands, and covariance normalization implicit; we make them explicit and reproducible.
This paper is the first in a series of two: here we describe the formalism and stationary noise, while in the companion

I.1
Notation and Fourier Conventions

Throughout, we denote a frequency-domain function with an overhead tilde and a continuous argument of
f
or discrete index
k
.
The corresponding time-domain function has a continuous argument of
t
or discrete index
j
.
We use
m
as the frequency index and
n
as the time index in the WDM domain.
The notation
(mn)
denotes a flattened array over both index values so that all pairs fall in a single vector.
Bold fonts denote vectors or matrices in the sense of a collection of numbers such as a timeseries or the noise covariance.
In index notation, elements of the vectors or matrices are denoted with square brackets, e.g.,
x[j]
.
However, we keep subscripts for WDM domain quantities to declutter the notation.

Unless stated otherwise, we adopt the Fourier convention using frequency (instead of angular frequency)

\begin{split}\tilde{x}(f)&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-2\pi ift}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\,,\\
x(t)&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde{x}(f)e^{2\pi ift}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\,.\end{split}

(4)

In discrete form, each integral turns into a Riemann sum as

\begin{split}\tilde{x}[k]&=\Delta t\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}x[j]e^{-2\pi ijk/N}\,,\\
x[j]&=\Delta f\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\tilde{x}[k]e^{2\pi ijk/N}=\frac{1}{N\Delta t}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\tilde{x}[k]e^{2\pi ijk/N}\,.\end{split}

(5)

The
numpy
fast Fourier transform (FFT) convention involves no normalization prefactor on the forward transform and a division by the number of points for the inverse:

\begin{split}\tilde{x}_{\mathrm{np}}[k]&=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}x[j]e^{-2\pi ijk/N}\,,\\
x[j]&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\tilde{x}_{\mathrm{np}}[k]e^{2\pi ijk/N}\,.\end{split}

(6)

In practical computations, the difference between the two conventions comes down to the decision to include the increment
\Delta t
in the Fourier transform definition.
Whichever way is chosen, the final result should be unaltered assuming appropriate normalization quantities are included in different places.
Numerical algorithms such as those in Appendix
D
are presented in the
numpy
convention in order to match what is practically implemented.
Correspondingly, we use
\mathrm{FFT}()
and
\mathrm{IFFT}()
as operators to indicate the FFT and its inverse respectively in the
numpy
convention.
Where possible, we will discuss how formulas need to be adjusted to switch conventions.
Because the convention of Eq. (
4
) is used in
LISA Analysis Tools

17
(and also
LALSuite

20
and
5
), we call it the “LT convention” from here on.

In Sec.
V
, we also invoke the discrete unitary convention

\begin{split}\tilde{x}_{\mathrm{unit}}[k]&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}x[j]e^{-2\pi ijk/N}\,,\\
x[j]&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\tilde{x}_{\mathrm{unit}}[k]e^{2\pi ijk/N}\,.\end{split}

(7)

The purpose of this is conciseness: many normalization factors that are absorbed into definitions or cancel anyways, explicitly disappear.

The continuous noise PSD is denoted by
S
rather than
S_{n}
to avoid confusion with the WDM time cell index
n
.

II
The WDM transform

Throughout, we consider a set of discrete, real time-domain data
x[j]
which are sampled from a function
x(t)
at intervals of
\Delta t
.
With this sampling rate, the time-domain data consist of
N
samples at times
(0,\Delta t,2\Delta t,\ldots,(N-1)\Delta t)
, for a total observation time of
T_{\mathrm{obs}}=N\Delta t
.
In the frequency domain, there are
N/2+1
complex data points
\tilde{x}[k]
at frequencies
(0,\Delta f,2\Delta f,\ldots,(N/2)\Delta f)
with
\Delta f=1/T_{\mathrm{obs}}
.
As in Gabor’s construction, the WDM transform breaks the time-frequency plane up into cells with temporal extent
\Delta T
and bandwidth
\Delta F
as shown in Fig.
1
.
We use indices
n
and
m
for the time and frequency coordinates of the cells respectively.

Figure 1:
Uniform time-frequency grid of the WDM transform. Time is indexed by
n
and cells have temporal width
\Delta T
. Frequency is indexed by
m
and cells have height
\Delta F
. The cell area saturates the uncertainty principle,
\Delta F\Delta T=1/2
. For
N
time-domain data points, there are
N-N_{t}
full-sized and
2N_{t}
half-sized time-frequency cells that correspond to the DC and Nyquist frequencies.
Similar to the frequency domain, half of the edge bins are identically zero. The total number of degrees of freedom on the entire grid is thus
N-N_{t}+(2N_{t})/2=N
, matching the number of time-domain data points.

The total number of degrees of freedom remains the same in the time, frequency, and time-frequency domain representations.
This preservation of information can be traced to the fact that all transforms are complete and orthonormal.
In the time domain, the data are a real one-dimensional vector of length
N
.
In the Fourier domain, we have a one-dimensional complex vector of length
N/2+1
.
This still adds up to a total of
N
degrees of freedom because the
N/2-1
interior bins each consist of two degrees of freedom (real and imaginary part), while the DC and Nyquist bins are purely real.
In the time-frequency domain, the time axis is tiled with
N_{t}
time bins, while the frequency axis is tiled with
N_{f}-1
interior frequency bins and two edge half-bins, corresponding to the DC and Nyquist frequencies.
Each interior cell contributes one degree of freedom, but half the edge cells are empty.
Overall, the total number of degrees of freedom adds up to
(N_{f}-1)N_{t}+(2N_{t})/2=N_{f}N_{t}=N
.
There is no loss of information in performing the WDM transform except when
N
needs to be truncated so that it is divisible by
N_{f}
.

The behavior of the edge frequency bins in the WDM domain is qualitatively similar to the frequency domain.
In the frequency domain, the DC bin is purely real, a simplification that can be traced to the fact that a constant (zero frequency) has an amplitude but not a phase.
Mathematically, the sine basis of the Fourier transform evaluated at zero frequency vanishes identically.
In the WDM domain, both the basis norm and the basis coefficient vanish identically for
m=0
and odd-
n
cells.
The behavior of the Nyquist bin is a consequence of finite sampling and aliasing.
As there are only
2
data points per cycle, a phase shift of the Fourier mode results only in a rescaling of the amplitude.
Mathematically, the sine Fourier basis vanishes on all time-domain discrete data where it reduces to
\sin(n\pi)
.
A similar result is reached for the WDM basis for cells with
m=N_{f}
and odd
N_{f}+n
.

With the time-frequency grid in hand, we can define some useful quantities that enter the formalism.
The total time of observation
T_{\mathrm{obs}}
covers the horizontal span of the time-frequency grid.
Since we have subdivided it into
N_{t}
points,

\Delta T=T_{\mathrm{obs}}/N_{t}=\Delta tN_{f}\,,

(8)

where we have used
T_{\mathrm{obs}}=N\Delta t
and
N=N_{t}N_{f}
.
Because we are saturating the uncertainty principle
\Delta T\Delta F=1/2
,

\Delta F=1/(2N_{f}\Delta t)\,.

(9)

The time and frequency extent of WDM cells is larger than the corresponding time- and frequency-domain bins,
\Delta T>\Delta t
and
\Delta F>\Delta f
.
Each cell encloses
\Delta T/\Delta t=N_{f}
of the time-domain samples and
\Delta F/\Delta f=N_{t}/2
of the frequency-domain samples.

II.1
The generalized Meyer scaling function

Figure 2:
The generalized Meyer filter as used in the WDM basis, varying

with the constraint that
2\alpha+\beta=\Delta F
required for the partition-of-unity (POU) property. The filter vanishes identically beyond the rolloff regions. The parameter
d

d

\alpha

\beta
adjust the width of the flat and rolloff regions.

The Wilson basis uses a pair of window functions at symmetrically spaced positive and negative frequencies, centered at
m\Delta F
and
-m\Delta F
.
The
7
construction requires a real window
\tilde{\phi}
that is sufficiently smooth and satisfies

\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}\tilde{\phi}(f+p\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f+(p+2j)\Delta F)=\frac{\delta_{j0}}{\Delta F}\,,\quad\forall j\in\mathbb{Z}\,.

(10)

The case
j=0
amounts to partition-of-unity depicted in Fig.
3
.
The case
j\neq 0
imposes cross-band cancellations.
Any filter function that satisfies these conditions can be used to construct an orthogonal Wilson basis.

For the WDM basis, the window in the frequency domain is a scaled version of the Meyer scaling function,
\tilde{\phi}(f)=\left(1/\sqrt{\Delta F}\right)\,\tilde{\varphi}(f)
,
with

\tilde{\varphi}(f)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|f|<\alpha\\
\cos\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\nu_{d}\left(\frac{|f|-\alpha}{\beta}\right)\right]&\text{if }\alpha\leq|f|<\alpha+\beta\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\,,

(11)

and

\displaystyle\nu_{d}(x)

\displaystyle=I_{x}(d,d)=\frac{(2d-1)!}{[(d-1)!]^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}(-1)^{k}\binom{d-1}{k}\frac{x^{d+k}}{d+k}\,,

(12)

where
I_{x}(d,d)

is the regularized beta function which is a polynomial of degree
2d-1
for positive integer
d
.

The filter has compact support,
\supp\tilde{\phi}=\supp\tilde{\varphi}=[-(\alpha+\beta),(\alpha+\beta)]
.
The free parameters
\alpha,\beta,d
are subject to the constraint
2\alpha+\beta=\Delta F
, discussed further below.
The filter function for different values of its free parameters is shown in Fig.
2
.
It is characterized by a flat region that is exactly
1
(whose width is determined by
\alpha
), a cosine-type rolloff (whose width is determined by
\beta
and shape by
d
), and vanishes exactly outside its area of support.
Equation (
12
) generically satisfies
\nu_{d}(0)=0
and
\nu_{d}(1)=1
, hence the boundaries of the falloff region vary from
\cos(0)=1
to
\cos\left(\pi/2\right)=0,
which match the adjacent regions and make the function continuous and
C^{d-1}
, i.e., differentiable to order
d-1
.
Each Meyer scaling function covers one frequency cell and up to half of the two adjacent cells.
Hence the maximum support of a filter centered at
f=0
is
[-\Delta F,\Delta F]
, achieved for
\alpha=0,\beta=\Delta F
.
Despite this overlapping support, the resulting WDM basis is orthonormal.
The compact support of the Meyer function means that the window automatically satisfies the
j\neq 0
condition of Eq. (
10
).

The Meyer filter further satisfies the
j=0

10

Partition-of-unity requires that the overlapping filters squared add up to a constant value even in the overlap regions:

\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}\tilde{\varphi}(f-p\Delta F)^{2}=1\,.

(13)

The partition-of-unity condition ensures a smooth handoff between neighboring Meyer bands.
As one filter falls off, the next ramps up so that the total power response remains constant, preventing gaps or double counting in frequency space.
Schematically, consider
f>0
and the
m=0
filter centered at
f=0
and the
m=1
filter centered at
f=\Delta F
.
The
m=0
filter is constant and equal to
1
until
f=\alpha
.
At that point, the
m=0
filter starts turning off and the
m=1
filter starts turning on.
At
f=\alpha+\beta
the
m=0
filter reaches a value of zero, coinciding with the place where the
m=1
filter reaches a value of one, as
\alpha+\beta=\Delta F-\alpha\Leftrightarrow 2\alpha+\beta=\Delta F
.
At that point, the handoff between the two filters is complete, the
m=1
filter remains at a value of one until
f=\Delta F+\alpha
, and so on.
Mathematically, partition-of-unity relies on two conditions: the identity

\nu_{d}(x)+\nu_{d}(1-x)=1\,,

(14)

that follows from Eq. (
12
),
and the parameter constraint
2\alpha+\beta=\Delta F
that ensures that adjacent filters have overlapping rolloff regions.
See Appendix
A
for the derivation.

Subsequent derivations will be presented for generic parameters
\alpha,\beta
and
d
.
Numerical implementations and figures, unless otherwise stated, employ
\alpha=\Delta F/4
,
\beta=\Delta F/2
, and
d=4
.
For this choice, the filter has support in

Moreover, for
d=4

\nu_{4}(x)=35x^{4}-84x^{5}+70x^{6}-20x^{7}\,.

(15)

Before moving on to the full WDM transform, we clarify that use of the Meyer scaling function (which also appears in canonical wavelet transforms) does not qualify WDM to be a ‘‘wavelet transform.’’
The latter formally uses translations and dilations, typically with a dyadic scaling,

For example, the standard square window is used in the short-time Fourier transform and can also be related to the Haar wavelet, but these transforms have very different properties.
Specifically, the Haar wavelet transform does not guarantee an exact frequency associated with each basis function, while the short-time Fourier transform is either overcomplete or has poor frequency localization as both simultaneously are prohibited by the Balian-Low theorem.

II.2
The WDM basis

The WDM basis tiles the time-frequency plane with a scaled Meyer window function that covers positive and negative frequencies centered at
m\Delta F
and
-m\Delta F
respectively.
In the frequency domain,

\tilde{g}_{mn}(f)=A_{m}e^{-2\pi inf\Delta T}\left[C_{mn}^{*}\tilde{\phi}(f+m\Delta F)+C_{mn}\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\right]\,,

(16)

where we have defined

A_{m}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}&m=0,N_{f}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&\mathrm{~otherwise}\end{cases}\,,\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad C_{mn}=\begin{cases}1&m+n\mathrm{~even}\\
i&m+n\mathrm{~odd}\end{cases}\,.

(17)

Orthogonality of this basis is shown in Appendix
B
:

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde{g}_{mn}(f)\tilde{g}^{*}_{pq}(f)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f=\delta_{mp}\delta_{nq}\begin{cases}0&m=0,n\mathrm{~odd}\\
0&m=N_{f},N_{f}+n\mathrm{~odd}\\
1&\mathrm{~otherwise}\end{cases}\,.

(18)

The vanishing cells at the DC and Nyquist frequencies correspond to the half-degrees of freedom of these bands.

The positive/negative frequency coverage is the crucial step in Wilson’s construction that distinguishes Eq. (
16
) from a Gabor system of Eq. (
1
).
The inverse Fourier transform of the frequency-shifted window is

\tilde{\phi}(f\pm m\Delta F)\xrightarrow{\text{IFFT}}e^{\mp 2\pi im\Delta Ft}\phi(t)\,,

(19)

and adding the time shift leads to

e^{-2\pi inf\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}(f\pm m\Delta F)\xrightarrow{\text{IFFT}}e^{\mp 2\pi im\Delta F(t-n\Delta T)}\phi(t-n\Delta T)\,.

(20)

The basis then becomes

\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{mn}(f)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}A_{m}e^{-2\pi inf\Delta T}\left[\tilde{\phi}(f+m\Delta F)+\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\right]&m+n\mathrm{~even}\\
-A_{m}e^{-2\pi inf\Delta T}i\left[\tilde{\phi}(f+m\Delta F)-\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\right]&m+n\mathrm{~odd}\end{cases}\quad\xrightarrow{\text{IFFT}}

\displaystyle{g}_{mn}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}A_{m}\left[e^{-2\pi im\Delta F(t-n\Delta T)}+e^{+2\pi im\Delta F(t-n\Delta T)}\right]\phi(t-n\Delta T)&m+n\mathrm{~even}\\
-A_{m}i\left[e^{-2\pi im\Delta F(t-n\Delta T)}-e^{+2\pi im\Delta F(t-n\Delta T)}\right]\phi(t-n\Delta T)&m+n\mathrm{~odd}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2A_{m}\cos\left[2\pi m\Delta F(t-n\Delta T)\right]\phi(t-n\Delta T)&m+n\mathrm{~even}\\
-2A_{m}\sin\left[2\pi m\Delta F(t-n\Delta T)\right]\phi(t-n\Delta T)&m+n\mathrm{~odd}\end{cases}\,.

(21)

Since
\tilde{\phi}(f)
is both real and even,
\phi(t)
is real as well, which makes the time-domain basis real.

Contrasted with the Gabor system of Eq. (
1
), this basis replaces the complex exponentials with real sine and cosine modulations.
The modulation alternates across the lattice: at a fixed frequency, i.e., fixed
m
, consecutive time cells counted by
n
alternate between sine and cosine.
The alternating time cells are
\pi/2
apart in phase and the lattice is half-shifted by
\Delta T/2
.
Still, for a given
m
the two combined real half-shifted lattices contain the same information as the complex Gabor lattice.

As shown in Fig.
3
, the full WDM basis in the frequency domain is a set of
N_{f}-1
full-width filters for the frequency band interior and
2
half-width filters for the edges.
Basis functions covering the two edges of the frequency band at the DC and Nyquist frequencies have a prefactor smaller by
\sqrt{2}
, necessary for normality, see Appendix
B
.
This is because the positive and negative frequency segments of the basis overlap for the DC and Nyquist bins.
As real time-domain data have Hermitian symmetry under the Fourier transform, we restrict the figure to positive frequencies.
An example of the time, frequency, and WDM-domain data for a simple linear chirp signal is given in Fig.
4
.

\Delta T
.
The vertical axis gives the real, imaginary, and absolute value of the frequency-series with partitions of size
\Delta F
.

III
Forward and inverse transform of the data

The WDM transform is more efficiently calculated from frequency-domain data, because the Meyer filter has compact support in the frequency domain but it is only algebraically decaying (but formally stays nonzero) in the time domain.
Going directly from the time domain to the WDM domain would thus require truncation of the filter function.
If we start from time-domain data, we first Fourier transform them and then compute the WDM transform.
Below, we present this route of the transform as well as its inverse.
The frequency index runs
m\in[0,N_{f}]
, with
m=0
the DC band and
m=N_{f}
the Nyquist band for a total of
N_{f}+1
indices.
The time index runs
n\in[0,N_{t}-1]
for a total of
N_{t}
indices.
The two band edges contribute
N_{t}/2
degrees of freedom each, preserving the count
N_{f}N_{t}=N
.

III.1
Frequency domain to WDM domain

We carry out the derivation for a continuous function,
\tilde{x}(f)
, and discretize the final answer to connect to numerical implementations.
Once in the Fourier domain, the data can be expanded in the WDM basis of Eq. (
16
)

\tilde{x}(f)=\sum_{m=0}^{N_{f}}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}w_{mn}\tilde{g}_{mn}(f)\,,

(22)

where
w_{mn}
are the real WDM coefficients, with
m
the frequency and
n
the time index of a given time-frequency cell.
The coefficients are calculated in the standard way of multiplying by the conjugate of a basis element and invoking orthogonality:

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde{x}(f)\tilde{g}_{pq}^{*}(f)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{m,n}w_{mn}\,\tilde{g}_{mn}(f)\tilde{g}_{pq}^{*}(f)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\Rightarrow\\
w_{mn}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde{x}(f)\tilde{g}^{*}_{mn}(f)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\,.

(23)

Substituting Eq. (
16
) in Eq. (
23
), and using the compact support of the Meyer filter, we find

w_{mn}=A_{m}\left[\int_{(-m-1)\Delta F}^{(-m+1)\Delta F}C_{mn}\tilde{x}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f+m\Delta F)e^{2\pi inf\Delta T}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f+\int_{(m-1)\Delta F}^{(m+1)\Delta F}C_{mn}^{*}\tilde{x}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)e^{2\pi inf\Delta T}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\right]\,.

(24)

The limits of integration have been restricted to the maximum possible support of the Meyer filter.
Substituting
f\rightarrow-f
in the first integral,

w_{mn}=A_{m}\left[\int_{(m-1)\Delta F}^{(m+1)\Delta F}C_{mn}\tilde{x}(-f)\tilde{\phi}\left(-f+m\Delta F\right)e^{-2\pi inf\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f+\int_{(m-1)\Delta F}^{(m+1)\Delta F}C_{mn}^{*}\tilde{x}(f)\tilde{\phi}\left(f-m\Delta F\right)e^{2\pi inf\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\right]\,.

(25)

Since the time series is real, the data have Hermitian symmetry,
\tilde{x}(-f)=\tilde{x}^{*}(f)
.
We use the even symmetry of the filter function,
\tilde{\phi}(f)=\tilde{\phi}(-f)
, to rewrite the first integral as

w_{mn}=A_{m}\left[\int_{(m-1)\Delta F}^{(m+1)\Delta F}C_{mn}\tilde{x}^{*}(f)\tilde{\phi}\left(f-m\Delta F\right)e^{-2\pi inf\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f+\int_{(m-1)\Delta F}^{(m+1)\Delta F}C_{mn}^{*}\tilde{x}(f)\tilde{\phi}\left(f-m\Delta F\right)e^{2\pi inf\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\right]\,.

(26)

Since the first integral is the complex conjugate of the second, the coefficients are manifestly real:

w_{mn}=2A_{m}\int_{(m-1)\Delta F}^{(m+1)\Delta F}\Re\left[C^{*}_{mn}\tilde{x}(f)\tilde{\phi}\left(f-m\Delta F\right)e^{2\pi inf\Delta T}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\,.

(27)

There are two ways of looking at this equation.
As
m
increments, either “slide” the filter
\tilde{\phi}
across the data, or keep the filter fixed and move the data
\tilde{x}
through it.
We choose the latter as it lines up with numerical implementations and fix the filter through the transformation
f^{\prime}=f-m\Delta F
.
Since
\Delta F\Delta T=1/2
, this can be written as

\displaystyle w_{mn}

\displaystyle=2A_{m}\int_{-\Delta F}^{\Delta F}\Re\left[C^{*}_{mn}\tilde{x}(f^{\prime}+m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}\left(f^{\prime}\right)e^{2\pi in(f^{\prime}+m\Delta F)\Delta T}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f^{\prime}

\displaystyle=2A_{m}(-1)^{mn}\int_{-\Delta F}^{\Delta F}\Re\left[C_{mn}^{*}\tilde{x}(f^{\prime}+m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}\left(f^{\prime}\right)e^{2\pi inf^{\prime}\Delta T}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f^{\prime}

\displaystyle=2A_{m}(-1)^{mn}\int_{-\Delta F}^{\Delta F}\Re\left[C_{mn}^{*}\tilde{x}(f+m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}\left(f\right)e^{2\pi inf\Delta T}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\,.

(28)

In the last line, we have relabeled
f^{\prime}\rightarrow f
.
This derivation applies to the interior and edge
m=0,N_{f}
bands alike.
Numerically, the basis prefactor makes the edge coefficients a factor of
\sqrt{2}
lower than the interior ones.

A crucial difference, however, is that Eq. (
28
) vanishes identically for edge cells with
m+n
odd, thus leaving the edge bands with a total of
N_{t}/2
degrees of freedom, compared to
N_{t}
for the interior ones.
For the DC band, this is immediately seen from Eq. (
24
) which for
m=0
reduces to

\displaystyle w_{0n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\int_{-\Delta F}^{\Delta F}C_{0n}\tilde{x}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f)e^{2\pi inf\Delta T}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f+\int_{-\Delta F}^{\Delta F}C_{0n}^{*}\tilde{x}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f)e^{2\pi inf\Delta T}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\Delta F}^{\Delta F}\Re\left[C_{0n}^{*}\right]\tilde{x}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f)e^{2\pi inf\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\,.

(29)

For
n
odd,
\Re\left[C_{0n}^{*}\right]=0
and the WDM coefficients vanish for any data.

For the
m=N_{f}
Nyquist band, the derivation cannot be done purely in the continuous domain and we need to consider the finite sampling condition.
In Appendix
C
we show that the finite sampling of the time-domain data results in a periodicity of twice the Nyquist frequency for the frequency-domain data.
Equivalently,
\tilde{x}(f-2N_{f}\Delta F)=\tilde{x}(f)
and
\tilde{x}(f-N_{f}\Delta F)=\tilde{x}(f+N_{f}\Delta F)
.
Returning to Eq. (
24
) for
m=N_{f}
, we find

w_{N_{f}n}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\int_{(-N_{f}-1)\Delta F}^{(-N_{f}+1)\Delta F}C_{N_{f}n}\tilde{x}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f+N_{f}\Delta F)e^{2\pi inf\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f+\int_{(N_{f}-1)\Delta F}^{(N_{f}+1)\Delta F}C_{N_{f}n}^{*}\tilde{x}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f-N_{f}\Delta F)e^{2\pi inf\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\right]\,.

(30)

Focusing on the integral of the first term,
I_{1}
, we substitute
f^{\prime}=f+2N_{f}\Delta F
and get

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{(N_{f}-1)\Delta F}^{(N_{f}+1)\Delta F}C_{N_{f}n}\tilde{x}(f^{\prime}-2N_{f}\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f^{\prime}-N_{f}\Delta F)e^{2\pi in(f^{\prime}-2N_{f}\Delta F)\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{(N_{f}-1)\Delta F}^{(N_{f}+1)\Delta F}C_{N_{f}n}\tilde{x}(f^{\prime})\tilde{\phi}(f^{\prime}-N_{f}\Delta F)e^{2\pi inf^{\prime}\Delta T}(e^{-2\pi i})^{nN_{f}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{(N_{f}-1)\Delta F}^{(N_{f}+1)\Delta F}C_{N_{f}n}\tilde{x}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f-N_{f}\Delta F)e^{2\pi inf\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\,,

(31)

where in the second line we have used the
2f_{\mathrm{Nyq}}
periodicity of the data and in the last line we have redefined
f^{\prime}\rightarrow f
.
With this, the full WDM coefficient is

\displaystyle w_{N_{f}n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\int_{(N_{f}-1)\Delta F}^{(N_{f}+1)\Delta F}C_{N_{f}n}\tilde{x}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f-N_{f}\Delta F)e^{2\pi inf\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f+\int_{(N_{f}-1)\Delta F}^{(N_{f}+1)\Delta F}C_{N_{f}n}^{*}\tilde{x}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f-N_{f}\Delta F)e^{2\pi inf\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{(N_{f}-1)\Delta F}^{(N_{f}+1)\Delta F}\Re\left[C_{N_{f}n}^{*}\right]\tilde{x}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f-N_{f}\Delta F)e^{2\pi inf\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\,.

(32)

Similarly to the DC case, WDM coefficients for Nyquist cells with
N_{f}+n
odd vanish.

III.1.1
Discretization

Equation (
28
) gives the WDM coefficients for the continuous transform.
We now switch to practical implementation details and discretize it, also switching from the FFT convention of Eq. (
4
) to the
numpy
discrete Fourier transform of Eq. (
6
).
The discretization
f_{k}=k\Delta f
has two effects: (i)
\tilde{x}(f_{k})\rightarrow\Delta t\,\tilde{x}[k]
, and (ii) the WDM integral becomes a sum with increment
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\rightarrow\Delta f
.
Noting that
\Delta F/\Delta f=N_{t}/2
and
\Delta f\Delta T=1/N_{t}
yields

w_{mn}=2A_{m}(-1)^{mn}\Delta f\Delta t\sum_{k=-N_{t}/2}^{N_{t}/2-1}\Re\left(C_{mn}^{*}\tilde{x}\left[k+m\frac{N_{t}}{2}\right]\tilde{\phi}\left[k\right]e^{2\pi ink/N_{t}}\right)\,.

(33)

The limits of
(-\Delta F,\Delta F)\rightarrow\{-N_{t}/2,N_{t}/2-1\}
correspond to the maximum possible bandwidth of the filter function.
4
4

4

For maximal efficiency we could use a
pruned
FFT
22
to avoid the zeros in the filter given the choice of filter parameters.
However, with our choice of
\alpha,\beta
, the filter is nonzero in
[-(3/4),(3/4)]\Delta F
, so this saves at most 25% of computation time and depends heavily on the efficiency of the implementation.

Next, we adjust the sum so that it centers both the filter and the
m
th band.
This keeps the frequencies in order from negative to positive,
k\in\left[0,N_{t}-1\right]
, which is more intuitive and lines up exactly with the implementation in
numpy

12
.
Defining
k^{\prime}=k+N_{t}/2
we get

\displaystyle w_{mn}

\displaystyle=2A_{m}(-1)^{mn}\Delta f\Delta t\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{N_{t}-1}\Re\left(C_{mn}^{*}\tilde{x}\left[k^{\prime}+(m-1)\frac{N_{t}}{2}\right]\tilde{\phi}\left[k^{\prime}-\frac{N_{t}}{2}\right]e^{2\pi in(k^{\prime}-N_{t}/2)/N_{t}}\right)\,.

(34)

The exponential yields another factor of
(-1)^{n}
.
Exchanging the sum with the real operator and relabeling
k^{\prime}\rightarrow k
, we define the windowed slice of the spectrum
\tilde{u}_{m}[k]
of length
N_{t}
as the data scaled by the
m
th filter

\tilde{u}_{m}[k]=\tilde{x}\left[k+(m-1)\frac{N_{t}}{2}\right]\tilde{\phi}\left[k-\frac{N_{t}}{2}\right]\,,

(35)

with
k\in[0,N_{t}-1]
and
y_{m}[n]
as a vector of length
N_{t}

N_{t}y_{m}[n]=N_{t}\frac{1}{N_{t}}\sum_{k=0}^{N_{t}-1}\tilde{u}_{m}[k]e^{2\pi ink/N_{t}}=N_{t}\,\mathrm{IFFT}(\tilde{u}_{m}[k])\,,

(36)

where the factor of
1/N_{t}
accounts for the inverse FFT prefactor in the
numpy
convention as shown in Eq. (

).
The remaining normalization terms of Eqs. (
34
) and (
36
) simplify to

2A_{m}(-1)^{mn}(-1)^{n}\Delta f\Delta tN_{t}=2A_{m}(-1)^{(m+1)n}\frac{N_{t}}{N}=2A_{m}(-1)^{(m+1)n}\frac{1}{N_{f}}\,,

(37)

where in the first equality we have used
\Delta f\Delta t=1/N
. Finally, from Eq. (
34
) we arrive at the final form of the WDM coefficients for the interior bands in the
numpy
convention:

w_{mn}=\frac{2A_{m}}{N_{f}}(-1)^{(m+1)n}\Re\left(C_{mn}^{*}y_{m}[n]\right)\,.

(38)

In the LT convention, the
1/N_{f}
factor is hidden so that the prefactor in Eq. (
38
) is simply
2A_{m}
.

In practice, the prescription is to loop over rows of frequency index
m
and compute all columns of time index
n
.
For each segment of data, the filter function
\tilde{\phi}[k]
covers
N_{t}
points of data
\tilde{x}[k]
and the same number are output upon inverse Fourier transform.

III.2
WDM domain to frequency domain

To recompose the data from the WDM back to the frequency domain we start by substituting Eq. (
16
) for the basis in Eq. (
22
) and restrict to the one-sided positive frequency data

\tilde{x}(f)=\sum_{m=0}^{N_{f}}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}A_{m}w_{mn}e^{-2\pi inf\Delta T}\left[C_{mn}^{*}\tilde{\phi}(f+m\Delta F)+C_{mn}\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\right]\,.

(39)

Negative frequencies can be trivially obtained via Hermitian symmetry.
From here the calculation for the interior and edge bands proceeds differently.

III.2.1
Interior frequency Bands

For the interior bands,
m\neq 0,N_{f}
, the first term of Eq. (
39
) drops out as
\tilde{\phi}(f+m\Delta F)
has no support for
f>0
.
The contribution of a single interior band
m
on the frequency-domain data is

\tilde{x}_{m}(f)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}w_{mn}C_{mn}e^{-2\pi inf\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}\left(f-m\Delta F\right)\,.

(40)

Transforming
f^{\prime}=f-m\Delta F
(and relabeling
f^{\prime}\rightarrow f
) yields

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{m}\left(f+m\Delta F\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\tilde{\phi}\left(f\right)\sum_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}w_{mn}C_{mn}e^{-2\pi in(f+m\Delta F)\Delta T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\tilde{\phi}\left(f\right)\sum_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}(-1)^{mn}w_{mn}C_{mn}e^{-2\pi inf\Delta T}\,.

(41)

III.2.2
Edge frequency bands

The contribution of the edge bands
m=0,N_{f}
requires two modifications: the basis prefactor is
1/2
instead of
1/\sqrt{2}
and the positive/negative components of the basis coincide.
The two terms in the square bracket of Eq. (
39
) collapse to a single filter, which follows immediately for
m=0
and by the arguments of Appendix
C
for the
2f_{\mathrm{Nyq}}
periodicity of the discrete data for
m=N_{f}
.

For both edge bands, their contribution to the frequency-domain data is

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{m}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}w_{mn}\left[C^{*}_{mn}+C_{mn}\right]\tilde{\phi}\left(f-m\Delta F\right)e^{-2\pi inf\Delta T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}w_{mn}\Re\left[C_{mn}\right]\tilde{\phi}\left(f-m\Delta F\right)e^{-2\pi inf\Delta T}\,.

(42)

For
n+m
odd the summand vanishes as
\Re\left[C_{mn}\right]=0
but also
w_{mn}=0
.
Transforming
f^{\prime}=f-m\Delta F
(and relabeling
f^{\prime}\rightarrow f
) yields

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{m}(f+m\Delta F)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\phi}\left(f\right)\sum_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}(-1)^{mn}w_{mn}\Re\left[C_{mn}\right]e^{-2\pi inf\Delta T}\,.

(43)

Compared to Eq. (
41
) for the interior bands, the contribution of the edge bands can be obtained by the same equation setting
m=0,N_{f}
and multiplying by
\sqrt{2}
.
The factor of
\sqrt{2}
is a combination of two effects: (i) division by
\sqrt{2}
to account for the different basis prefactor, but also (ii) multiplication by
2
for the coinciding positive/negative frequency filters.

III.2.3
Discretization

To discretize, we set
f_{k}=k\Delta f
and
\tilde{x}_{m}(f)\rightarrow\Delta t\,\tilde{x}_{m}[k]
.
To cover all bands with a single equation, we define
\tilde{A}_{m}=1/(2A_{m})
instead of the basis prefactor for the interior or exterior bands.
Starting with Eq. (
41
)

\displaystyle\Delta t\,\tilde{x}_{m}\left[k+m\frac{N_{t}}{2}\right]=\tilde{A}_{m}\tilde{\phi}[k]\sum_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}(-1)^{mn}w_{mn}C_{mn}e^{-2\pi ink/N_{t}}\,.

(44)

Following similar steps to the forward transform, we center the filter and reindex from
k\in\left[-N_{t}/2,N_{t}/2-1\right]
to
k\in\left[0,N_{t}-1\right]
, picking up a factor of

We arrive at

\tilde{x}_{m}\left[k+(m-1)\frac{N_{t}}{2}\right]=\frac{\tilde{A}_{m}}{\Delta t}\tilde{\phi}\left[k-\frac{N_{t}}{2}\right]\tilde{Z}_{m}[k]\,,

(45)

where we have defined

z_{m}[n]=w_{mn}C_{mn}(-1)^{(m+1)n}\,,\qquad\tilde{Z}_{m}=\mathrm{FFT}\left(z_{m}\right)[k]\,,

(46)

again based on the conventions of Eq. (

).
This is the contribution of a given WDM band

to the frequency-domain data.

\tilde{x}[\ell]=\sum_{m}\tilde{x}_{m}[\ell]=\sum_{m}\tilde{x}_{m}\left[k+(m-1)\left(\frac{N_{t}}{2}\right)\right]\,,

(47)

where the index
k
of each WDM band

maps to the corresponding

IV
Transform of the noise Covariance

Besides the data, computing the likelihood in the WDM domain also requires the noise covariance.
In the WDM basis, the noise covariance is a matrix
\Lambda_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}=\langle n_{mn}n_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{*}\rangle
where
n_{mn}
is the WDM representation of the noise and angle brackets denote an expectation value.
The matrix flattens the
m
and
n
(and
m^{\prime},n^{\prime}
) indices into a single
(mn)
index of size
(N_{f}+1)N_{t}=N+N_{t}
.
Thus nominally the covariance is a
(N+N_{t})\times(N+N_{t})
matrix.
However, not all matrix elements are independent and half-occupied edge bands (
m=0,N_{f}
with
m+n
odd) render some of the matrix entries identically zero.
The expected
N\times N
effective matrix size matches the time- and frequency-domain covariances.

IV.1
Frequency domain to WDM domain

From Eq. (
27
), the noise in the WDM domain is

n_{mn}=2A_{m}\int_{(m-1)\Delta F}^{(m+1)\Delta F}\Re~\left[C_{mn}^{*}\tilde{\mathbf{n}}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)e^{2\pi inf\Delta T}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\,,

(48)

and the conjugate is

n^{*}_{mn}=2A_{m}\int_{(m-1)\Delta F}^{(m+1)\Delta F}\Re~\left[C_{mn}\tilde{\mathbf{n}}^{*}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)e^{-2\pi inf\Delta T}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\,.

(49)

Though
n_{mn}
is real, expressing its complex conjugate in this form allows us to eventually form the frequency-domain covariance matrix,
\langle\tilde{\mathbf{n}}(f)\tilde{\mathbf{n}}^{*}(f^{\prime})\rangle=\bm{\Sigma}(f,f^{\prime})
.
The argument of the expectation value is then

n_{mn}n^{*}_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}=4A_{m}A_{m^{\prime}}\int_{(m^{\prime}-1)\Delta F}^{(m^{\prime}+1)\Delta F}\int_{(m-1)\Delta F}^{(m+1)\Delta F}\Re\left[X(f)\right]\Re\left[Y(f^{\prime})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f^{\prime}\,,

(50)

where

\displaystyle X(f)

\displaystyle=C_{mn}^{*}\tilde{\mathbf{n}}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)e^{2\pi inf\Delta T}\,,

(51)

\displaystyle Y(f^{\prime})

\displaystyle=C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}\tilde{\mathbf{n}}^{*}(f^{\prime})\tilde{\phi}(f^{\prime}-m^{\prime}\Delta F)e^{-2\pi in^{\prime}f^{\prime}\Delta T}\,.

(52)

Using
\Re(X)\Re(Y)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\Re(XY)+\Re(XY^{*})\right]
and the Hermitian symmetry of the Fourier-transformed noise, the expectation value of the integrand decomposes into two terms:

\displaystyle\langle X(f)Y(f^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=C_{mn}^{*}C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}\langle\tilde{\mathbf{n}}(f)\tilde{\mathbf{n}}^{*}(f^{\prime})\rangle\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f^{\prime}-m^{\prime}\Delta F)e^{2\pi i(nf-n^{\prime}f^{\prime})\Delta T}\,,

(53)

\displaystyle\langle X(f)Y^{*}(f^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=C_{mn}^{*}C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{*}\langle\tilde{\mathbf{n}}(f)\tilde{\mathbf{n}}^{*}(-f^{\prime})\rangle\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f^{\prime}-m^{\prime}\Delta F)e^{2\pi i(nf+n^{\prime}f^{\prime})\Delta T}\,.

(54)

The noise contribution has reduced to the covariance matrix in the frequency domain,
\langle XY\rangle\propto\langle\tilde{\mathbf{n}}(f)\tilde{\mathbf{n}}^{*}(f^{\prime})\rangle=\bm{\Sigma}(f,f^{\prime})
and
\langle XY^{*}\rangle\propto\langle\tilde{\mathbf{n}}(f)\tilde{\mathbf{n}}^{*}(-f^{\prime})\rangle=\bm{\Sigma}(f,-f^{\prime})
.
Overall,

\Lambda_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}=2A_{m}A_{m^{\prime}}\int_{(m^{\prime}-1)\Delta F}^{(m^{\prime}+1)\Delta F}\int_{(m-1)\Delta F}^{(m+1)\Delta F}\left(\Re~\langle X(f)Y(f^{\prime})\rangle+\Re~\langle X(f)Y^{*}(f^{\prime})\rangle\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f^{\prime}\,.

(55)

For generic, non-stationary noise both terms are nonzero.
This fully generic form of the covariance matrix is not illuminating, and cannot be further simplified without making assumptions about the noise properties.
We will return to the covariance structure and provide examples in the companion paper
16
.

The inverse covariance matrix, that directly enters the likelihood, transforms from the frequency to the WDM domain via the same formula, by replacing
\bm{\Sigma}\rightarrow\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}
.

IV.2
WDM domain to Frequency domain

The frequency-domain noise in terms of its WDM decomposition is

\tilde{\mathbf{n}}(f)=\sum_{m=0}^{N_{f}}\tilde{n}_{m}(f)\,,

(56)

where
\tilde{n}_{m}(f)
is the contribution of each band
m
, given by Eq. (
41
) for interior bands and requiring another factor of
\sqrt{2}
for the DC and Nyquist bands.
The frequency-domain covariance is then

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}(f,f^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\langle\tilde{\mathbf{n}}(f)\tilde{\mathbf{n}}^{*}(f^{\prime})\rangle=\sum_{m=0}^{N_{f}}\sum_{m^{\prime}=0}^{N_{f}}\langle\tilde{n}_{m}(f)\tilde{n}^{*}_{m^{\prime}}(f^{\prime})\rangle\,.

(57)

Focusing on the interior bands,
m,m^{\prime}\neq 0,N_{f}
, the expectation value of the per-band noise is

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{n}_{m}(f)\tilde{n}^{*}_{m^{\prime}}(f^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f^{\prime}-m^{\prime}\Delta F)\sum_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{N_{t}-1}C_{mn}C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{*}\langle n_{mn}n_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}\rangle e^{-2\pi inf\Delta T+2\pi in^{\prime}f^{\prime}\Delta T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f^{\prime}-m^{\prime}\Delta F)\sum_{nn^{\prime}}C_{mn}C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{*}\Lambda_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}e^{2\pi i(n^{\prime}f^{\prime}-nf)\Delta T}\,.

(58)

For edge bands, this expression is multiplied by
\sqrt{2}
for each of
m
and
m^{\prime}
in
\{0,N_{f}\}
, before summing over bands to obtain the full covariance in Eq. (
57
).

IV.3
Stationary noise

For stationary noise, the covariance matrix in the time domain depends on time differences only

\langle\mathbf{n}(t)\mathbf{n}^{*}(t^{\prime})\rangle=R(t-t^{\prime})\,,

(59)

where
R(t)
is the autocorrelation function.
The covariance matrix of the Fourier-transformed noise is

\langle\tilde{\mathbf{n}}(f)\tilde{\mathbf{n}}^{*}(f^{\prime})\rangle=\int\int\langle\mathbf{n}(t)\mathbf{n}^{*}(t^{\prime})\rangle e^{-2\pi ift+2\pi if^{\prime}t^{\prime}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}=\int\int R(t-t^{\prime})e^{-2\pi ift+2\pi if^{\prime}t^{\prime}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,,

(60)

and using the transformation
\tau=t-t^{\prime}
we get

\langle\tilde{\mathbf{n}}(f)\tilde{\mathbf{n}}^{*}(f^{\prime})\rangle=\left[\int R(\tau)e^{-2\pi if\tau}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right]\int e^{2\pi i(f^{\prime}-f)t^{\prime}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\equiv S_{2}(f)\delta(f-f^{\prime})\,.

(61)

The second integral is equal to the Dirac delta function,
\delta(f-f^{\prime})
, and from the Wiener-Khinchin theorem, we identify the term in brackets as the two-sided noise power-spectral density (PSD),
S_{2}(f)
, defined as the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation function.
It is related to the one-sided PSD
S(f)
through

S_{2}(f)=\frac{S(f)}{2}\,.

(62)

Overall,

\bm{\Sigma}(f,f^{\prime})=\langle\tilde{\mathbf{n}}(f)\tilde{\mathbf{n}}^{*}(f^{\prime})\rangle=\frac{1}{2}S(f)\delta(f-f^{\prime})\,,

(63)

which is just the standard stationary noise condition of diagonal frequency-domain covariance.
Similarly, we obtain
\bm{\Sigma}(f,-f^{\prime})=(1/2)S(f)\delta(f+f^{\prime})
.

IV.3.1
Forward transform

Returning to Eq. (
55
), we start with the first term,

\displaystyle I^{XY}_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=2A_{m}A_{m^{\prime}}\int_{(m^{\prime}-1)\Delta F}^{(m^{\prime}+1)\Delta F}\int_{(m-1)\Delta F}^{(m+1)\Delta F}\Re~\langle X(f)Y(f^{\prime})\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f^{\prime}

\displaystyle=A_{m}A_{m^{\prime}}\Re\left[\int_{(m^{\prime}-1)\Delta F}^{(m^{\prime}+1)\Delta F}\int_{(m-1)\Delta F}^{(m+1)\Delta F}C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}^{*}S(f)\delta(f-f^{\prime})\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f^{\prime}-m^{\prime}\Delta F)e^{2\pi i(nf-n^{\prime}f^{\prime})\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=A_{m}A_{m^{\prime}}\Re\left[C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}^{*}\int_{(m-1)\Delta F}^{(m+1)\Delta F}S(f)\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f-m^{\prime}\Delta F)e^{2\pi i(n-n^{\prime})f\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\right]\,.

(64)

The ensuing calculation follows the orthogonality proof of Appendix
B
.
Transforming
f^{\prime}=f-m\Delta F
and then relabeling
f^{\prime}\rightarrow f
, we get

\displaystyle I^{XY}_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=A_{m}A_{m^{\prime}}\Re\left[C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}^{*}\int_{-\Delta F}^{\Delta F}S(f+m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f+(m-m^{\prime})\Delta F)e^{2\pi i(n-n^{\prime})(f+m\Delta F)\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\right]

\displaystyle=A_{m}A_{m^{\prime}}\,(-1)^{(n-n^{\prime})m}\Re\left[C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}^{*}\int_{-\Delta F}^{\Delta F}S(f+m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f+(m-m^{\prime})\Delta F)e^{2\pi i(n-n^{\prime})f\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\right]\,.

(65)

The second term of Eq. (
55
) is

\displaystyle I^{XY^{*}}_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=2A_{m}A_{m^{\prime}}\int_{(m^{\prime}-1)\Delta F}^{(m^{\prime}+1)\Delta F}\int_{(m-1)\Delta F}^{(m+1)\Delta F}\Re~\langle X(f)Y^{*}(f^{\prime})\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f^{\prime}

\displaystyle=A_{m}A_{m^{\prime}}\Re\left[\int_{(m^{\prime}-1)\Delta F}^{(m^{\prime}+1)\Delta F}\int_{(m-1)\Delta F}^{(m+1)\Delta F}C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}^{*}S(f)\delta(f+f^{\prime})\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f^{\prime}-m^{\prime}\Delta F)e^{2\pi i(nf+n^{\prime}f^{\prime})\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f^{\prime}\right]\,.

(66)

For interior bands, this term vanishes because
\delta(f+f^{\prime})
has support for
f=-f^{\prime}
, but the integration domain is on
f\in\left[(m-1)\Delta F,(m+1)\Delta F\right]
which has
f,f^{\prime}>0
unless
m=0,N_{f}
.
As always, therefore, edge bands need to be handled separately.
For
m=0
or
m^{\prime}=0
, for example, the integration domain includes negative frequencies, so
\delta(f+f^{\prime})
has nonzero support.
For the DC-DC terms (
m=m^{\prime}=0
), the
I^{XY}_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}
and
I^{XY^{*}}_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}
terms coincide, doubling the DC-DC block relative to the interior band formula.
An analogous statement holds for the Nyquist-Nyquist terms: the negative-
f
filter aliases onto the positive-
f
one via the
2f_{\mathrm{Nyq}}
periodicity from Appendix
C
.
The overall doubling of the covariance from the
I^{XY^{*}}_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}
term for edge bands is exactly canceled by the reduction in
A_{m}^{2}
(from 1/2 to 1/4) at the edges.
Therefore the same formula applies for all bands after substituting the prefactors, reaching the final expression for the covariance matrix of stationary noise:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,(-1)^{(n-n^{\prime})m}\,\Re\left[C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}^{*}\int_{-\Delta F}^{\Delta F}S(f+m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f+(m-m^{\prime})\Delta F)e^{2\pi i(n-n^{\prime})f\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\right]\,.

(67)

The filter functions in the integrand overlap only for
|m-m^{\prime}|\leq 1
and thus all other matrix elements vanish.
However, matrix elements with
m^{\prime}=m\pm 1
are nonzero; the WDM covariance matrix is not diagonal even under perfectly stationary noise.

For a diagonal WDM covariance matrix, the noise needs to additionally be locally flat, at least to within the three WDM bins where the filters overlap.
Centering at bin
m
, the slow-varying noise assumption amounts to expanding the PSD to zeroth order
S(f+m\Delta F)\approx S(m\Delta F)
.
The covariance is then

\Lambda_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}\approx\frac{1}{2}\,(-1)^{(n-n^{\prime})m}\,\Re\left[C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}^{*}S(m\Delta F)\int_{-\Delta F}^{\Delta F}\tilde{\phi}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f+(m-m^{\prime})\Delta F)e^{2\pi i(n-n^{\prime})f\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\right]\,.

(68)

The integral is computed in Appendix
B
as part of the orthogonality proof, yielding a diagonal covariance

\Lambda_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}\approx\frac{1}{2}S(m\Delta F)\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\,.

(69)

The WDM diagonal covariance is proportional to the frequency-domain covariance via the PSD evaluated at the bin midpoint,
m\Delta F
, under the assumption that the noise does not change appreciably across adjacent bins.

3
instead evaluates an integral of the PSD over each filter band, yielding the diagonal elements of the full WDM noise covariance matrix while ignoring off-diagonal terms.
First-order corrections to the slowly-varying noise approximation and the off-diagonal terms were calculated in
4
.
The effect of each of these approximations on inference is examined in the sequel
16
.

IV.3.2
Inverse transform

We now present the inverse transform to return from the WDM domain noise covariance matrix
\Lambda_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}
to the noise PSD in the frequency domain
S(f)
.
We start with Eq. (
57
) and specializing to the stationary noise case,
f=f^{\prime}
,
collapses the sums
m,m^{\prime}
to only the band-tridiagonal
m=m^{\prime}
and
m=m^{\prime}\pm 1
.
Since the covariance matrix is symmetric, we can reduce this further to only the diagonal and one of the off-diagonals counted twice

\bm{\Sigma}(f,f)=\sum_{m=0}^{N_{f}}\left[\langle\tilde{n}_{m}(f)\tilde{n}^{*}_{m}(f)\rangle+2\,\Re\langle\tilde{n}_{m}(f)\tilde{n}^{*}_{m+1}(f)\rangle\right]\,.

(70)

The real operator in the second term is required because
\langle\tilde{n}_{m}(f)\tilde{n}^{*}_{m+1}(f)\rangle
is complex valued and conjugate symmetric, which can be seen by swapping the
\tilde{n}_{m}
and
\tilde{n}^{*}_{m+1}
indices.
Next we specialize Eq. (
58
) to stationary noise, moving from a coefficient of
1/2
for the inner bands to
\tilde{A}_{m}\tilde{A}_{m^{\prime}}
to account for the edge bands

\langle\tilde{n}_{m}(f)\tilde{n}^{*}_{m^{\prime}}(f)\rangle=\tilde{A}_{m}\tilde{A}_{m^{\prime}}\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f-m^{\prime}\Delta F)\sum_{nn^{\prime}}C_{mn}C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{*}\Lambda_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}e^{-2\pi ifr\Delta T}\,,

(71)

where again
r=n-n^{\prime}
.
Though not immediately obvious due to the presence of the
C_{mn}C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{*}
term, the summand depends only on time differences and hence is Toeplitz in structure.
We prove this in Appendix
G
.
Therefore, the sum over
n,n^{\prime}
collapses to a sum over
r
times a multiplicity
\mu[r]
for each lag, schematically

\sum_{nn^{\prime}}f[r]=\sum_{r}\mu[r]f[r]\,,

(72)

with

\mu[r]=\begin{cases}N_{t},&\text{interior},\\
N_{t}/2,&\text{edge bands}\,.\end{cases}

(73)

We define

t_{mm^{\prime}}[r]=\mu[r]C_{mn}C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{*}\Lambda_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}\,.

(74)

and Eq. (
71
) is now

\langle\tilde{n}_{m}(f)\tilde{n}^{*}_{m^{\prime}}(f)\rangle=\tilde{A}_{m}\tilde{A}_{m^{\prime}}\,\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f-m^{\prime}\Delta F)\sum_{r}t_{mm^{\prime}}[r]e^{-2\pi ifr\Delta T}\,,

(75)

When inserted into Eq. (
70
), we find

\bm{\Sigma}(f,f)=\sum_{m=0}^{N_{f}}\left[\tilde{A}_{m}^{2}\tilde{\phi}^{2}(f-m\Delta F)\sum_{r}t_{mm}[r]e^{-2\pi ifr\Delta T}+2\Re\,\tilde{A}_{m}\tilde{A}_{m+1}\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f-(m+1)\Delta F)\sum_{r}t_{mm+1}[r]e^{-2\pi ifr\Delta T}\right]\,.

(76)

and after one final translation to
f\rightarrow f+m\Delta F
we obtain,

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{N_{f}}\left[\tilde{A}_{m}^{2}\tilde{\phi}^{2}(f)\sum_{r}t_{mm}[r]e^{-2\pi i(f+m\Delta F)r\Delta T}+2\Re\tilde{A}_{m}\tilde{A}_{m+1}\tilde{\phi}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f-\Delta F)\sum_{r}t_{mm+1}[r]e^{-2\pi i(f+m\Delta F)r\Delta T}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{N_{f}}\left[\tilde{A}_{m}^{2}\tilde{\phi}^{2}(f)\sum_{r}(-1)^{mr}t_{mm}[r]e^{-2\pi ifr\Delta T}+2\Re\tilde{A}_{m}\tilde{A}_{m+1}\tilde{\phi}(f)\tilde{\phi}(f-\Delta F)\sum_{r}(-1)^{mr}t_{mm+1}[r]e^{-2\pi ifr\Delta T}\right]\,.

(77)

This equation gives the noise PSD in terms of the WDM noise covariance in continuous form.

IV.3.3
Discretization

To discretize the covariance expression for Eq. (
67
), we follow similar steps to Sec.
III.1.1
:
f\rightarrow k\Delta f
,
\tilde{x}(f)\rightarrow\tilde{x}[k]
, and
df\rightarrow\Delta f
.
The stationary PSD is evaluated at each of the bins
m\Delta F
, yielding

\Lambda_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta f(-1)^{(n-n^{\prime})m}\,\Re\left\{C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}^{*}\sum_{k=-N_{t}/2}^{N_{t}/2-1}S\left[k+m\frac{N_{t}}{2}\right]\tilde{\phi}[k]\,\tilde{\phi}\left[k+(m-m^{\prime})\frac{N_{t}}{2}\right]e^{2\pi i(n-n^{\prime})k/N_{t}}\right\}\,.

(78)

Centering the bins with
k^{\prime}=k+\frac{N_{t}}{2}
and relabeling
k^{\prime}
back to
k
gives

\Lambda_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta f(-1)^{(n-n^{\prime})(m+1)}\Re\left\{C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}^{*}\sum_{k=0}^{N_{t}-1}\tilde{K}_{mm^{\prime}}[k]e^{2\pi i(n-n^{\prime})k/N_{t}}\right\}\,,

(79)

where we discretize
\tilde{K}_{mm^{\prime}}
,

\tilde{K}_{mm^{\prime}}[k]=S\left[k+(m-1)\frac{N_{t}}{2}\right]\tilde{\phi}\left[k-\frac{N_{t}}{2}\right]\,\tilde{\phi}\left[k+(m-m^{\prime}-1)\frac{N_{t}}{2}\right]\,.

(80)

The covariance depends only on
n-n^{\prime}
so it has a Toeplitz structure up to the parity factor induced by the term
C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}^{*}
.
Since
n\in[0,N_{t}-1]
, then
r=n-n^{\prime}\in[-(N_{t}-1),(N_{t}-1)]
.
With this definition,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Delta f(-1)^{r(m+1)}\Re\left\{C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}^{*}\sum_{k=0}^{N_{t}-1}\tilde{K}_{mm^{\prime}}[k]e^{2\pi irk/N_{t}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Delta fN_{t}(-1)^{r(m+1)}\Re\left\{C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}^{*}K_{mm^{\prime}}[r]\right\}\,,

(81)

where

N_{t}K_{mm^{\prime}}[r]=\frac{N_{t}}{N_{t}}\sum_{k=0}^{N_{t}-1}\tilde{K}_{mm^{\prime}}[k]e^{2\pi irk/N_{t}}=N_{t}\,\mathrm{IFFT}(\tilde{K}_{mm^{\prime}}[k])\,,

(82)

where the
N_{t}
factor is required for the IFFT as defined by
numpy
.
An algorithm block for this calculation is given in Appendix
D
.

The likelihood calculation, explored in Sec.
V
, requires the inverse rather than the covariance itself.
In general, the inverse of a matrix with some degree of symmetry, see following discussion, will be dense.
However, in this case, the inverse can be trivially obtained by noting that for stationary noise, the frequency domain inverse is
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(f,f^{\prime})\sim 1/S(f)
.
The WDM inverse covariance in the symmetric Fourier convention can therefore be obtained from Eq. (
81
) by setting
S(f)\rightarrow 1/S(f)
.
The WDM inverse covariance has the same symmetry structure as the covariance.

To discretize the backward transformation from the stationary WDM noise covariance to the noise PSD in the frequency domain, we start with Eq. (
63
)

\begin{split}{\bm{\Sigma}}[k,k^{\prime}]&=\frac{1}{2}S[k]\delta\left[(k-k^{\prime})\Delta f\right]=\frac{1}{2\Delta f}S[k]\delta_{kk^{\prime}}=\frac{T}{2}S[k]\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\,.\end{split}

(83)

This equation can be solved directly for
S[k]

S[k]=\frac{2}{T}\bm{\Sigma}[k,k]\,.

(84)

Employing the discretized form of Eq. (
77
) we find

S[k]=\frac{2}{T}\sum_{m=0}^{N_{f}}\left[\tilde{A}_{m}^{2}\tilde{\phi}^{2}[k]\sum_{r}(-1)^{mr}t_{mm}[r]e^{-2\pi irk/N_{t}}+2\Re\tilde{A}_{m}\tilde{A}_{m+1}\,\tilde{\phi}[k]\tilde{\phi}\left[k-\frac{N_{t}}{2}\right]\sum_{r}(-1)^{mr}t_{mm+1}[r]e^{-2\pi irk/N_{t}}\right]

(85)

Finally, shifting
k\rightarrow k-N_{t}/2
and using that
\tilde{\phi}[k-N_{t}]=\tilde{\phi}[k]
,

S[k]=\frac{2}{T}\sum_{m=0}^{N_{f}}\left[\tilde{A}_{m}^{2}\tilde{\phi}^{2}\left[k-\frac{N_{t}}{2}\right]\,\mathrm{FFT}((-1)^{(m+1)r}t_{mm}[r])+2\Re\tilde{A}_{m}\tilde{A}_{m+1}\,\tilde{\phi}\left[k-\frac{N_{t}}{2}\right]\tilde{\phi}\left[k\right]\,\mathrm{FFT}((-1)^{(m+1)r}t_{mm+1}[r])\right]\,.

(86)

An implementation of this equation is given in Appendix
D
.

Transforming from the noise PSD in the frequency domain to the block tri-diagonal WDM covariance matrix costs
\mathcal{O}(N\log N_{t})
to form
K_{mm^{\prime}}[r]
with a fast Fourier transform.
This means that the forward noise covariance to PSD transformation has cost
\mathcal{O}(N\log N_{t})
.
The inverse transformation has two fast Fourier transformations per block, one for the diagonal and one for the off-diagonal.
Once again, the inverse transformation from the WDM noise covariance matrix to the frequency domain noise PSD has cost
\mathcal{O}(N\log N_{t})
.
These costs only cover the special case of stationary noise, and generic cases will require more computation.

IV.3.4
Structure

The noise covariance matrix of Eq. (
81
) has
(N_{f}+1)N_{t}\times(N_{f}+1)N_{t}
entries that encode the
N/2+1
degrees of freedom of stationary noise.
Its structure is simplified by a number of symmetries, but even then the number of nonzero, unique entries is greater than
N/2+1
.
This means that entries are in general not independent even when symmetries are taken into account, which complicates noise modeling.
While in the frequency domain we could directly model each
S[i]
as an independent number, we cannot do so with each WDM cell.

Below we discuss some of the covariance matrix general structure and elucidate it with a schematic for the case
N_{f}=3,m\in[0,3]
and
N_{t}=4,n\in[0,4)
.
This is only illustrative and we focus on properties that apply generically to all values of
N_{f}
and
N_{t}
.
5
5

5

For
N_{t}=4
in particular, all cross-band blocks vanish, but that is not a generic property of other
N_{t}
values and hence we do not show it here.

1.

The covariance matrix is symmetric in
(mn)\leftrightarrow(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})
.

2.

It is block tri-diagonal, where the three diagonals correspond to
|m-m^{\prime}|\leq 1
and the blocks correspond to
n
.
It therefore suffices to compute the
m=m^{\prime}
and
m=m^{\prime}+1
elements and obtain the
m=m^{\prime}-1
entries by symmetry.

In our example, the flattened matrix is block tri-diagonal with shape
(N_{f}+1)\times(N_{f}+1)=4\times 4
blocks

\Lambda\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}D_{0}&B_{01}&0&0\\
B^{T}_{01}&D_{1}&B_{12}&0\\
0&B^{T}_{12}&D_{2}&B_{23}\\
0&0&B^{T}_{23}&D_{3}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(87)

Each block is a
N_{t}\times N_{t}=4\times 4
matrix.

3.

Up to parity each
(m,m^{\prime})
block depends on
n,n^{\prime}
only through
r=n-n^{\prime}
and therefore has a parity-corrected Toeplitz structure.

4.

The interior (
m,m^{\prime}\neq 0,N_{f}
) diagonal (
m=m^{\prime}
) blocks are symmetric, have a constant diagonal, and are Toeplitz for
r
even and alternating-sign Toeplitz for
r
odd.

In our example, this corresponds to blocks
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
, each of which is schematically

D_{m}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}d_{0}&d_{1}&d_{2}&d_{3}\\
d_{1}&d_{0}&-d_{1}&d_{2}\\
d_{2}&-d_{1}&d_{0}&d_{1}\\
d_{3}&d_{2}&d_{1}&d_{0}\\
\end{pmatrix}\,.

(88)

5.

The edge (
m,m^{\prime}=0,N_{f}
) diagonal (
m=m^{\prime}
) blocks have similar properties as the interior ones and additionally rows/columns with
m+n
odd vanish identically.

In our example, this corresponds to blocks
D_{0}
and
D_{3}
, the former of which is schematically

D_{m}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}d_{0}&0&d_{2}&0\\
0&0&0&0\\
d_{2}&0&d_{0}&0\\
0&0&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\,.

(89)

6.

The diagonal elements of the cross-band blocks vanish by parity arguments: for
m^{\prime}=m+1
and
r=n-n^{\prime}=0
,
K_{m(m+1)}
is purely real, while
C_{mn}C_{(m+1)n}^{*}
is purely imaginary.
Overall then,
\Re\left\{C_{mn}C_{(m+1)n}^{*}K_{m(m+1)}\right\}=0
.

7.

The interior (
m,m^{\prime}\neq 0,N_{f}
) cross-band (
m=m^{\prime}+1
) blocks are Toeplitz for
r
odd and alternating-sign Toeplitz for
r
even. The blocks are not fully symmetric, as odd-
r
stripes are symmetric and even-
r
stripes are antisymmetric.

In our example, this corresponds to blocks
B_{12}
and
B_{21}
, each of which is schematically

B_{m,m+1}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&b_{1}&-b_{2}&b_{3}\\
b_{1}&0&b_{1}&b_{2}\\
b_{2}&b_{1}&0&b_{1}\\
b_{3}&-b_{2}&b_{1}&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(90)

8.

The edge (
m,m^{\prime}=0,N_{f}
) cross-band (
m=m^{\prime}+1
) blocks have the same properties as the interior ones and additionally rows or columns with edge
m
and
m+n
odd vanish.

In our example,

B_{0,1}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&b_{1}&-b_{2}&b_{3}\\
0&0&0&0\\
b_{2}&b_{1}&0&b_{1}\\
0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad B_{2,3}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&b_{1}&0&b_{3}\\
0&0&0&b_{2}\\
0&b_{1}&0&b_{1}\\
0&-b_{2}&0&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(91)

V
Likelihood and inner products

With the data and covariance transforms in hand, in this section we derive inner products and the likelihood function in the WDM domain.
Derivations are simpler if we recast the transforms in terms of vectors and matrices, rather than the coefficients and integrals of the preceding sections.
We therefore start by establishing this notation before moving to the Gaussian likelihood, following Ref.
25
.
In the process, we derive the standard frequency-domain Whittle likelihood and re-define the noise PSD,
S(f)
.
In the WDM domain, we obtain the analogous likelihood function but with an
evolutionary
noise PSD,
S(t,f)
, that depends on frequency as well as time.

V.1
WDM transform under linear algebra notation

In matrix notation, the transform equations are formal and hold for any generic data or noise, making no assumption about noise stationarity or other simplifications.
As such, they are not meant for use in production analyses where inverting matrices would be prohibitively expensive, but instead as a tool for derivations.
In the
\mathbf{W}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{W}}
transform matrices introduced below, we pack the DC and Nyquist bins into
N
elements by putting the nonzero DC elements in the
N_{t}/2
even bins and the Nyquist elements into the
N_{t}/2
odd bins.
By doing so, we avoid non-square matrices that are not unitary.
The same trick needs to also be performed on the Fourier domain transform matrix
\mathbf{F}
.
The matrices can be unpacked in the end to include the DC and Nyquist half-bins in their unpacked (singular) representations for user manipulation.

We define a vector
\mathbf{w}
holding the WDM coefficients
w_{(mn)}
in flattened form.
The corresponding time-domain and frequency-domain vectors are
\mathbf{x}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
respectively.
The time-to-WDM matrix operator
\mathbf{W}
and the frequency-to-WDM matrix operator
\tilde{\mathbf{W}}
are representations of the WDM transform from each of these domains to the WDM domain:

\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}=\tilde{\mathbf{W}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\,.

(92)

The
N
-point discrete Fourier transform is represented by operator
\mathbf{F}
with entries
F_{jk}=\left(1/\sqrt{N}\right)e^{-2\pi ijk/N}
and conjugate transpose
\mathbf{F}^{\dagger}
:

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{Fx}\,,\qquad\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{F}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\,.

(93)

In their packed form, both the WDM and the Fourier transform are unitary, so in the unitary normalization of Eq. (
7
) the operators satisfy

\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T}=\mathbf{FF}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{I}\,,

(94)

where
\mathbf{I}
is the identity.
From this, the frequency domain operator
\tilde{\mathbf{W}}
can be written as

\tilde{\mathbf{W}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{Wx}=\mathbf{WF}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\Rightarrow\tilde{\mathbf{W}}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{F}^{\dagger}\,.

(95)

Thus
\tilde{\mathbf{W}}\tilde{\mathbf{W}}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{I}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{W}}
is also unitary.

Both the time-to-WDM
\mathbf{W}
and the frequency-to-WDM
\tilde{\mathbf{W}}
transformation matrix can be written down analytically.
While this exercise is pedagogical, multiplying a square matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}
and a vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
requires
\mathcal{O}(M^{2})
operations.
This is slower than performing the same calculation via (I)FFT as derived in Sec.
III
and is not the preferred method of transformation; however, this formulation provides a general-use prescription for transforming not only vectors, but also matrices such as the noise covariance matrix and products of vectors and matrices such as the likelihood.
This formulation of the WDM transform has found use in investigations of in-painting data gaps
25
and will be used in the companion paper
16
.

V.2
Generic Covariance

In this notation, the covariance matrix for generic zero-mean noise is defined via the expectation value of the noise,

\mathbf{C}=\langle\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{T}\rangle\,.

(96)

Using
\tilde{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbf{F}\,\mathbf{n}
, we can derive a transformation from the time to the frequency domain,

\bm{\Sigma}=\langle\tilde{\mathbf{n}}\tilde{\mathbf{n}}^{\dagger}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{F}\,\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{F}\,\mathbf{n})^{\dagger}\rangle=\mathbf{F}\langle\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{T}\rangle\mathbf{F}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{F}\,\mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{F}^{\dagger}\,.

(97)

A similar expression can be derived between the time, frequency, and WDM noise covariance matrices.
Denoting the noise vector in the WDM domain as
\mathbf{n_{w}}
with elements
n_{(mn)}
, we find

\bm{\Lambda}=\langle\mathbf{n_{w}}\mathbf{n_{w}}^{\dagger}\rangle=\langle\tilde{\mathbf{W}}\tilde{\mathbf{n}}(\tilde{\mathbf{W}}\tilde{\mathbf{n}})^{\dagger}\rangle=\tilde{\mathbf{W}}\langle\tilde{\mathbf{n}}\tilde{\mathbf{n}}^{\dagger}\rangle\tilde{\mathbf{W}}^{\dagger}=\tilde{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\Sigma}\tilde{\mathbf{W}}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{W}^{T}\,.

(98)

Since
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T}=\mathbf{I}
, Eq. (
98
) can also be applied to the inverse covariance matrix, shown by taking the inverse of both sides to find

\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}=\tilde{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{W}}^{\dagger}\,.

(99)

Depending on the noise properties, it might be faster to obtain the covariance inverse in the frequency domain and compute the inverse WDM covariance with Eq. (
99
) directly.
For example, for stationary noise the inverse covariance in the frequency domain is trivial to calculate, while inverting the WDM covariance involves inverting a block tri-diagonal matrix.

V.3
Likelihood

As a scalar, the likelihood is invariant under these transforms, but we can compute it using data and the covariance matrix expressed in any domain.
As long as this is done self-consistently and without making domain-specific assumptions (such as diagonal frequency-domain covariance if the noise is not stationary), the final value will be the same.

We now combine the transformed data and noise covariance to compute the Gaussian likelihood in the WDM domain.
We have real data
\mathbf{d}
in the time domain consisting of noise
\mathbf{n}
and a signal
\mathbf{h}
6
6

6

The subsequent equations can trivially be extended to multiple signals
\mathbf{H}=\sum_{i}\mathbf{h}_{i}
.
with parameters
\bm{\theta}
,

\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{h}(\bm{\theta})\,.

(100)

Further, we assume that the noise follows a zero-mean multivariate Gaussian,

\mathbf{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{C})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{N}\det\mathbf{C}}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{n}\right)\,,

(101)

with covariance matrix
\mathbf{C}
.
Given an estimate of the noise covariance and signal parameters
\bm{\theta}
, the residual
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{d}-\mathbf{h}(\bm{\theta})
is equal to
\mathbf{n}
if
\bm{\theta}
are the true parameters.
The likelihood is then

p(\mathbf{d}\mid\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{N}\det\mathbf{C}}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{r}^{T}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{r}\right)\,,

(102)

or in the more common logarithmic form

\ln p(\mathbf{d}\mid\bm{\theta})=-\frac{N}{2}\ln(2\pi)-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\mathbf{C}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{r}^{T}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{r}=-\frac{N}{2}\ln(2\pi)-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\mathbf{C}-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{r}\mid\mathbf{r})_{t}\,,

(103)

where
(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{b})_{t}\equiv\mathbf{a}^{T}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{b}
is the time-domain noise-weighted inner product.

To obtain the frequency-domain inner product and likelihood function, we
transform the argument of the exponential as

\mathbf{r}^{T}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}^{\dagger}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{r}=\left(\mathbf{F}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{r}}\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{F}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{r}}\right)=\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{\dagger}\left(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{F}^{\dagger}\right)\tilde{\mathbf{r}}=\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{\dagger}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{r}}=(\mathbf{r}\mid\mathbf{r})_{f}\,,

(104)

where
(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{b})_{f}\equiv\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{b}
is the frequency-domain noise-weighted inner product.
The determinant of the covariance is

\det\mathbf{C}=\det\left(\mathbf{F}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{F}\right)=\det\mathbf{F}^{\dagger}\det\mathbf{\Sigma}\det\mathbf{F}=\det\mathbf{\Sigma}\,,

(105)

since
\mathbf{F}
is unitary and thus
\det\mathbf{F}\det\mathbf{F}^{\dagger}=\det\mathbf{F}(\det\mathbf{F})^{*}=|\det\mathbf{F}|^{2}=1
.
Collecting all terms, the log-likelihood is then

\ln p(\mathbf{d}\mid\bm{\theta})=-\frac{N}{2}\ln\left(2\pi\right)-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\bm{\Sigma}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{\dagger}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{r}}=-\frac{N}{2}\ln\left(2\pi\right)-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\bm{\Sigma}-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{r}\mid\mathbf{r})_{f}\,.

(106)

The frequency-domain expression, like the time-domain one above, is exact and applies even under non-stationary noise in which the frequencies are correlated as long as the inner product is calculated appropriately.
In that case, the nonstationary covariance matrix will be dense in general and require a more expensive inversion and multiplication than its stationary counterpart.
But as long as the full nonstationary covariance matrix is employed, the frequency-domain likelihood will be exact and equal to the time-domain one.

We obtain the likelihood in the WDM domain with similar steps, starting with the time-domain representation of Eq. (
103
) and the
\mathbf{W}
transform matrix.
Starting with the exponential, we denote the WDM residual as
\mathbf{r_{w}}
, such that
\mathbf{r_{w}}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{r_{w}}
, and obtain

\mathbf{r}^{T}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{r}=(\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{r_{w}})^{T}\mathbf{C}^{-1}(\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{r_{w}})=\mathbf{r_{w}}^{T}\left(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{T}\right)\mathbf{r_{w}}=\mathbf{r_{w}}^{T}\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{r_{w}}=(\mathbf{w}\mid\mathbf{w})_{tf}\,,

(107)

where
(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{b})_{tf}\equiv\mathbf{a}^{T}\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{b}
is the WDM domain noise-weighted inner product.
Similarly, the determinant of the covariance is

\det\mathbf{C}=\det\left(\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{W}\right)=\det\mathbf{W}^{T}\det\mathbf{\Lambda}\det\mathbf{W}=\det\mathbf{\Lambda}\,,

(108)

since
\mathbf{W}
is orthogonal and thus
\det\mathbf{W}=\det\mathbf{W}^{T}=\pm 1
.
Collecting again all the terms, the log-likelihood is

\ln p(\mathbf{d}\mid\bm{\theta})=-\frac{N}{2}\ln(2\pi)-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\bm{\Lambda}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{w}^{T}\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{w}=-\frac{N}{2}\ln(2\pi)-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\bm{\Lambda}-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{w}\mid\mathbf{w})_{tf}\,.

(109)

Thus the likelihood is the same, regardless of which domain it is computed in.

V.4
Stationary Noise and the Whittle Likelihood

Under stationary noise, where the noise covariance in the frequency domain is diagonal, the frequency-domain likelihood reduces to the standard Whittle likelihood.
The stationary noise condition, Eq. (
63
), can be discretized as

\begin{split}{\Sigma}[k,k^{\prime}]&=\frac{1}{2}S[k]\delta\left[(k-k^{\prime})\Delta f\right]=\frac{1}{2\Delta f}S[k]\delta_{kk^{\prime}}=\frac{T}{2}S[k]\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\,,\\
{\Sigma}[k]&=\frac{T}{2}S[k]=\sigma^{2}[k]\,,\end{split}

(110)

where
\sigma^{2}[k]
is the noise variance per frequency bin, and from here on we return to our standard convention.
The inner product is then

\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{\dagger}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{r}}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{|\tilde{r}[k]|^{2}}{\Sigma[k]}=\frac{2}{T}\left[\frac{|\tilde{r}[0]|^{2}}{S[0]}+2\,\sum_{k=1}^{N/2-1}\frac{|\tilde{r}[k]|^{2}}{S[k]}+\frac{|\tilde{r}[N/2]|^{2}}{S[N/2]}\right]=2\,\sum_{k=0}^{N/2}\epsilon[k]\frac{|\tilde{r}[k]|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}[k]}\,,

(111)

where in the last expression we have used the Hermitian symmetry to sum over only the DC and positive frequencies and

\epsilon[k]=\begin{cases}1,\qquad&k\in[1,\frac{N}{2}-1]\,,\\
\frac{1}{2},\qquad&k\in\{0,\frac{N}{2}\}\,.\end{cases}

(112)

The DC and Nyquist components are real and hence do not get doubled with only one degree of freedom instead of the two associated with the inner bands.
In the above sum, these terms are multiplied by
1/2
relative to the inner bands to account for this.
This is almost always neglected when writing this expression in paper and often overlooked even in software implementations of the inner product.
Taking the continuum limit of Eq. (
111
) from a sum to an integral as
\sum_{k}\rightarrow T\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f=(1/\Delta f)\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f
, leads to the standard frequency domain noise-weighted inner product

\langle\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{b}\rangle=4\,\Re\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{a}^{*}(f)\tilde{b}(f)}{S(f)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\,.

(113)

The overall factor of
4
comes from two effects: combining positive/negative frequencies and the one-sided PSD definition.
Finally, we arrive to the Whittle likelihood in the frequency domain as

\begin{split}\ln p(\mathbf{d}\mid\bm{\theta})&=-\frac{N}{2}\ln\left(2\pi\right)-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\bm{\Sigma}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{\dagger}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{r}}\\
&=-\frac{N}{2}\ln\left(2\pi\right)-\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2\sum_{k=0}^{N/2}\epsilon[k]\ln\sigma^{2}[k]-\frac{1}{2}\left[2\,\sum_{k=0}^{N/2}\epsilon[k]\frac{|\tilde{r}[k]|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}[k]}\right]\\
&=-\frac{N}{2}\ln\left(2\pi\right)-\sum_{k=0}^{N/2}\epsilon[k]\ln\sigma^{2}[k]-\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathbf{r}\mid\mathbf{r}\rangle\,.\end{split}

(114)

Up to normalization, this matches the common Whittle likelihood used in the gravitational-wave data analysis literature (e.g., Eq. 1 of Ref.
17
), though the per-bin variance
\sigma^{2}[k]
is sometimes displayed as the noise PSD (typically
S_{n}(f)
elsewhere, but
S(f)
here).
The noise PSD should be used if the Whittle likelihood is written in the continuous form; otherwise, one should use the per-bin variance as we have defined it here.

In the WDM domain, the stationary noise covariance is not diagonal, see Sec.
IV.3
.
For a diagonal covariance, we need to additionally assume that the noise is white, though corrections can be small for a slowly varying spectrum
4
.

More generically, the evolutionary noise PSD augments the standard stationary PSD
S(f)
with a time dependence.
We defer a formal definition and exploration of the continuous evolutionary noise PSD
S(t,f)
and its discretized form to the companion paper
16
.
For now a working definition will suffice: an extension of the noise covariance at the center of time intervals of width
\Delta T
that is both stationary and slowly varying with
f

\langle\mathbf{\tilde{n}}(f)\mathbf{\tilde{n}}^{*}(f^{\prime})\rangle\approx\frac{1}{2}S(n\Delta T,f)\delta(f-f^{\prime})\,.

(115)

Assuming that the time dependence is slow and negligible on the scale of
\Delta T
, the covariance reduces to

\Lambda_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}\approx\frac{1}{2}S(n\Delta T,m\Delta F)\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2}S_{mn}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\,,

(116)

where the evolutionary PSD
S_{mn}
is evaluated at the middle of the time-frequency bins.
Equation (
116
) lacks a factor of
T
that appears in the frequency-domain covariance of Eq. (
110
) when the delta function
\delta(f-f^{\prime})
is discretized.
This is because the WDM covariance includes a frequency integral of the frequency-domain covariance, e.g., Eq. (
64
), which absorbs the delta function before discretization.
The inner product is then

\mathbf{w}^{T}\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{w}=\sum_{mn}\frac{w^{2}_{mn}}{\Lambda_{(mn)(mn)}}=2\sum_{mn}\frac{w_{mn}^{2}}{S_{mn}}\,.

(117)

Finally, we assemble the full likelihood

\begin{split}\ln p(\mathbf{d}\mid\bm{\theta})&=-\frac{N}{2}\ln\left(2\pi\right)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{mn}\ln\left(\frac{1}{2}S_{mn}\right)-\sum_{mn}\frac{w_{mn}^{2}}{S_{mn}}\\
&=-\frac{N}{2}\ln\left(2\pi\right)+\frac{N}{2}\ln 2-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{mn}\ln S_{mn}-\sum_{mn}\frac{w_{mn}^{2}}{S_{mn}}\\
&=-\frac{N}{2}\ln\left(\pi\right)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{mn}\ln S_{mn}-\sum_{mn}\frac{w_{mn}^{2}}{S_{mn}}\,.\end{split}

(118)

Compared to the frequency-domain Whittle likelihood of Eq. (
114
), the WDM-domain Whittle likelihood lacks the factor of 2 that comes from negative/positive frequencies in the Fourier domain and does not require special treatment of the edge bins.

A more detailed derivation and exploration of the properties of the evolutionary noise PSD will be presented in the companion paper
16
.

VI
Common Questions

To conclude, we address commonly asked questions and potential points of confusion about time-frequency analyses and the WDM transform.

VI.1
Is there any loss of information when using the WDM transform?

No. The WDM basis is complete and orthonormal, Appendix
B
.
The transform maps
N
real time-domain samples to
N=N_{f}N_{t}
real WDM coefficients.
The WDM degrees of freedom thus exactly match the time-domain and frequency-domain ones.
In the WDM domain, the
N_{f}-1
interior frequency bins contribute
(N_{f}-1)N_{t}
real coefficients, one per cell.
For the two edge bins, DC and Nyquist, half the cells are empty and thus contribute
N_{t}/2
coefficients each.
The total is

(N_{f}-1)N_{t}+(2N_{t})/2=N_{f}N_{t}=N\,,

(119)

matching the original time-series degrees of freedom.

The original time series can be recovered exactly via the inverse transform of Sec.
III.2
.
Equivalently, the WDM transform satisfies Parseval’s identity.
Defining the discrete forward transform matrix
\mathbf{W}
from the time to the WDM domain, per Sec.
V
, via

w_{mn}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}W_{(mn)k}\,x_{k}\Rightarrow\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}\,,

(120)

basis orthonormality implies
\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{I}
, where
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix. Therefore

\sum_{m=0}^{N_{f}}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}w_{mn}^{2}=\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{x}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}x_{k}^{2}\,,

(121)

so no signal power is lost.

Practical implementations can, however, introduce information loss.
First, if
N
is a prime number and not divisible by
N_{f}
, the data must be zero-padded or truncated and some boundary samples might be altered.
Second, transforming to the WDM domain from the time-domain directly requires truncation of the filter function.
In the time domain
{\phi}(t)
decays algebraically as
1/t^{d+1}
and thus has formally infinite support, while in the frequency domain
\tilde{\phi}(f)
has compact support.
For this reason, in Sec.
III
we first FFT the data to the frequency domain and then transform to the WDM domain.

VI.2
Where does the phase information go if the WDM coefficients are real?

Figure 7:
Visualization of how changing the phase value
\phi
of a simple sinusoid
h(t)=\cos(2\pi f_{0}t+\phi)
affects the corresponding WDM coefficients.
Each panel shows the time-domain signal (thick black lines, with arbitrary normalization), the WDM-domain signal (colored boxes), and gridlines demarking the boundary of the WDM cells, for sinusoids with varying phase
\phi
(top to bottom).
The relevant frequency index here is
m_{0}=1
and therefore per Eq. (
127
) odd
n
have
w_{m_{0}n}\propto-\cos\phi
while even
n
have
w_{m_{0}n}\propto+\sin\phi
.

Consider a simple monochromatic signal given in the time domain by

h(t)=A\cos(2\pi f_{0}t+\phi)\rightarrow h_{k}=A\cos(2\pi f_{0}t_{k}+\phi)\,,\quad\mathrm{where~}t_{k}=k\Delta t,k\in[0,N)\,.

(122)

If the frequency
f_{0}
lies on the Fourier grid with
f_{0}=\ell_{0}\Delta f
, then a discrete Fourier transform under the symmetric convention yields

\displaystyle\tilde{h}[\ell]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h_{k}e^{-2\pi i\ell k/N}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}A\cos(2\pi f_{0}t_{k}+\phi)e^{-2\pi i\ell k/N}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}A\cos(2\pi\ell_{0}\Delta fk\Delta t+\phi)e^{-2\pi i\ell k/N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}A\cos\left(2\pi\frac{\ell_{0}k}{N}+\phi\right)e^{-2\pi i\ell k/N}=\frac{A}{2\sqrt{N}}e^{i\phi}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{2\pi i\left(\ell_{0}-\ell\right)k/N}+\frac{A}{2\sqrt{N}}e^{-i\phi}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{-2\pi i\left(\ell_{0}+\ell\right)k/N}

\displaystyle=\frac{A}{2\sqrt{N}}e^{i\phi}N\delta_{\ell,\ell_{0}}+\frac{A}{2\sqrt{N}}e^{-i\phi}N\delta_{\ell,-\ell_{0}}=\frac{A\sqrt{N}}{2}\left(e^{i\phi}\delta_{\ell,\ell_{0}}+e^{-i\phi}\delta_{\ell,-\ell_{0}}\right)\,.

(123)

Restricting to positive frequencies,
\ell=\ell_{0}
, the Fourier coefficient for bin
\ell
is

\tilde{h}[\ell_{0}]=\frac{A\sqrt{N}}{2}\left(\cos\phi+i\sin\phi\right)\,.

(124)

Therefore the signal amplitude is encoded in the complex coefficient amplitude and the signal phase in the complex coefficient phase.
A pure cosine signal has a real coefficient and a pure sine signal has an imaginary coefficient.
Two signal degrees of freedom (amplitude and phase) map onto two Fourier degrees of freedom (the real and imaginary part).

In the WDM domain all coefficients are real and the signal phase is instead encoded in adjacent time cells.
The WDM coefficients for the sinusoid signal, assuming interior bands, are obtained by substituting the continuous frequency-domain signal

\tilde{h}(f)=\frac{A}{2}\left[e^{i\phi}\delta\left(f-f_{0}\right)+e^{-i\phi}\delta\left(f+f_{0}\right)\right]\,,

(125)

into Eq. (
28
):

w_{mn}=(-1)^{mn}\frac{A}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{-\Delta F}^{\Delta F}\Re\left[C_{mn}^{*}\left[e^{i\phi}\delta\left(f+m\Delta F-f_{0}\right)+e^{-i\phi}\delta\left(f+m\Delta F+f_{0}\right)\right]\tilde{\phi}(f)e^{2\pi inf\Delta T}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\,.

(126)

The two delta functions set
f=f_{0}-m\Delta F
and
f=-f_{0}-m\Delta F
respectively.
Within the integration limits of
[-\Delta F,\Delta F]
, the two conditions become
(m-1)\Delta F\leq f_{0}\leq(m+1)\Delta F
and
(m-1)\Delta F\leq-f_{0}\leq(m+1)\Delta F
respectively.
The second condition cannot be satisfied for
m>0
and
f_{0}>0
and thus the integral of the second term vanishes.
The first condition is satisfied for
m\in\{m_{0}-1,m_{0},m_{0}+1\}
where
m_{0}=f_{0}/\Delta F
.
Assuming again that
f_{0}
falls at the center of a WDM cell,
f_{0}=m_{0}\Delta F
, the only contribution is from
m=m_{0}
where
\phi(f=f_{0}-m_{0}\Delta F=0)=1/\sqrt{\Delta F}
:

w_{m_{0}n}=(-1)^{m_{0}n}\frac{A}{\sqrt{2\Delta F}}\Re\left[C_{m_{0}n}^{*}e^{i\phi}\right]=(-1)^{m_{0}n}\frac{A}{\sqrt{2\Delta F}}\begin{cases}\cos{\phi}&m_{0}+n\mathrm{~even}\\
\sin{\phi}&m_{0}+n\mathrm{~odd}\end{cases}\,.

(127)

The information that was previously packaged into a single complex Fourier coefficient is now spread over two real coefficients on adjacent time cells of opposite parity.
The signal amplitude is encoded in the sum of the squared coefficients and the signal phase in their ratio.
Likewise, two signal degrees of freedom now map onto two adjacent WDM coefficients of opposite parity at the same band
m_{0}
.

In Fig.
7
, we provide a visualization of this example monochromatic signal for four phase values:
\phi=0
,
\phi=\pi/2
,
\phi=\pi
, and
\phi=3\pi/2
.
Since the sinusoids have constant frequency in the center of a bin, the nonzero cells are confined to one row, while the phase information enters in each pair of adjacent time cells.
In this example,
\cos\phi
and
\sin\phi
are always either zero or one, so only one out of every two time cells has a nonzero coefficient; intermediate phase values would allow for both cells in each pair to be nonzero, with the ratio providing
\tan\phi
.

This calculation also reveals that there is no limit of the WDM series to the frequency series as two real numbers need to be combined into one complex number.
The exact correspondence between the two in the limit
N_{f}\rightarrow N/2
,
N_{t}=2
is derived in Appendix
E
.

VI.3
Is WDM a wavelet or wavelet packet transform?

No. Wavelet transforms lie on a non-uniform (typically dyadic) time-frequency grid.
Wavelet packet transforms are more complicated and allow for flexible (including uniform) tilings, but employ a different construction than the WDM transform.
Wavelet and wavelet packet transforms are associated with the affine group (translations and dilations), while the WDM transform is associated with the Heisenberg group (translations in time and frequency).

In wavelet transforms, the basis is generated through translations and dilations of a “mother wavelet”
\psi(t)
, e.g.,

\psi_{jn}(t)=2^{j/2}\psi\left(2^{j}t-n\right)\,.

(128)

The dyadic dilation produces logarithmically-spaced cells with height
2^{j}
and width
2^{-j}
, effectively favoring time resolution over frequency resolution at high frequencies and vice versa.
The WDM transform, by contrast, tiles the time-frequency plane through translations and frequency modulations

g_{mn}(t)=g_{m}(t-n\Delta T)\,,\qquad g_{m}(t)\propto\cos(2\pi m\Delta Ft)\phi(t)\text{ or }\sin(2\pi m\Delta Ft)\phi(t)\,,

(129)

see Eq. (
21
).
This results in uniformly-spaced cells with the same height
\Delta F
and width
\Delta T
, see Fig.
1
.

The dyadic-wavelet and WDM grids differ in the quality factor
Q
, which is the ratio of the band center to the bandwidth
\Delta f_{\mathrm{bw}}
.
A dyadic wavelet scales both by the same factor such that

Q=\frac{f_{c}}{\Delta f_{\mathrm{bw}}}=\mathrm{const.}

(130)

However, for the WDM transform we have up to factors of
{\cal{O}}(1)
due to the rolloff

Q\propto\frac{m\Delta F}{\Delta F}=m\,,

(131)

which grows linearly with band index.
The band-dependent
Q
shows that the WDM transform is not a dyadic wavelet transform.

The quality factor does not by itself exclude a wavelet packet transform, which can have uniform bands with a linearly-growing
Q
.
The distinction is instead in the construction of the basis.
WDM modulates a single fixed window with sines/cosines (Heisenberg).
Wavelet packets, by contrast, build bands by repeatedly dilating/halving the frequency axis (affine); halving all bands equally yields a uniform grid resembling WDM’s, while other choices yield more customized grids.

The Meyer scaling function appears in both the WDM transform (as the window) and the Meyer wavelet transform (as the mother wavelet).
However, this overlap is nominal as the two transforms employ it differently and the crucial difference is that the WDM transform does not perform dilations.

Appendix A
Partition of unity

In this Appendix, we prove the partition-of-unity property of the Meyer scaling function in Eq. (
11
):

{\mathcal{P}}=\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}\tilde{\varphi}(f-p\Delta F)^{2}=1\,,

(132)

assuming

\nu_{d}(x)+\nu_{d}(1-x)=1\,,

(133)

which follows from Eq. (
12
).
Without loss of generality, we restrict
f\in[0,\Delta F]
such that only the
p=0
and
p=1
filters have support.
This area includes half of each filter’s constant region plus the rolloff region where they overlap:

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}_{0}(f)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }f<\alpha\\
\cos\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\nu_{d}\left(\frac{f-\alpha}{\beta}\right)\right]&\text{if }\alpha\leq f\leq\alpha+\beta\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\,,

(134)

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}_{1}(f)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }f>\Delta F-\alpha\\
\cos\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\nu_{d}\left(\frac{\Delta F-f-\alpha}{\beta}\right)\right]&\text{if }\Delta F-(\alpha+\beta)\leq f\leq\Delta F-\alpha\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }f>\alpha+\beta\\
\cos\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\nu_{d}\left(1-\frac{f-\alpha}{\beta}\right)\right]&\text{if }\alpha\leq f\leq\alpha+\beta\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\,,

(135)

where in the last line we have simplified the limits using the constraint
2\alpha+\beta=\Delta F
.
For
f<\alpha
and
f>\alpha+\beta
, Eq. (
132
) is trivially satisfied as
{\mathcal{P}}=\tilde{\varphi}_{0}^{2}=1
and
{\mathcal{P}}=\tilde{\varphi}_{1}^{2}=1
respectively. For
\alpha\leq f\leq\alpha+\beta
we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\varphi}_{0}^{2}+\tilde{\varphi}_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\nu_{d}\left(\frac{f-\alpha}{\beta}\right)\right]+\cos^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\nu_{d}\left(1-\frac{f-\alpha}{\beta}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\nu_{d}\left(\frac{f-\alpha}{\beta}\right)\right]+\cos^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\left(1-\nu_{d}\left(\frac{f-\alpha}{\beta}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\nu_{d}\left(\frac{f-\alpha}{\beta}\right)\right]+\sin^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\nu_{d}\left(\frac{f-\alpha}{\beta}\right)\right]=1\,,

(136)

where going from the second to third line we have used the property
\nu_{d}(x)+\nu_{d}(1-x)=1
and from the third to the fourth line we have applied trigonometric identities.
Thus Eq. (
132
) is satisfied for all values of
f
.

Appendix B
Orthogonality

In this Appendix, we show orthogonality of the WDM basis. Substituting Eq. (
16
) into Eq. (
18
) we get

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\tilde{g}_{mn}(f)\tilde{g}_{pq}^{*}(f)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f

\displaystyle=A_{m}A_{p}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-2\pi i(n-q)f\Delta T}\left[C_{mn}^{*}\tilde{\phi}(f+m\Delta F)+C_{mn}\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\right]\left[C_{pq}\tilde{\phi}(f+p\Delta F)+C_{pq}^{*}\tilde{\phi}(f-p\Delta F)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\,.

(137)

A filter centered at
m\Delta F
only has support in the bands centered at
(m-1)\Delta F
,
m\Delta F
, and
(m+1)\Delta F
.
Therefore Eq. (
137
) vanishes unless
|m-p|\leq 1
, since otherwise the filters have no overlapping support.

Before proceeding, we simplify the above expression. We define

I^{+}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-2\pi i(n-q)f\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}(f+m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f+p\Delta F)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\,,

(138)

make the substitution
x=f+m\Delta F
and recall that
\Delta F\Delta T=1/2
to get

\displaystyle I^{+}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-2\pi i(n-q)(x-m\Delta F)\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}(x)\tilde{\phi}(x+(p-m)\Delta F)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=e^{i(n-q)m\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-2\pi i(n-q)x\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}(x)\tilde{\phi}(x+(p-m)\Delta F)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=(-1)^{(n-q)m}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-2\pi i(n-q)x\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}(x)\tilde{\phi}(x+(p-m)\Delta F)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\,.

(139)

Similar steps lead to

\displaystyle I^{-}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-2\pi i(n-q)f\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f-p\Delta F)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f

\displaystyle=(-1)^{(n-q)m}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-2\pi i(n-q)x\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}(x)\tilde{\phi}(x-(p-m)\Delta F)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\,.

(140)

Further substituting
x\rightarrow-x
and recalling that
\tilde{\phi}
is an even function leads to

\displaystyle I^{-}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{(n-q)m}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{2\pi i(n-q)x\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}(x)\tilde{\phi}(x+(p-m)\Delta F)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\left(I^{+}\right)^{*}\,.

(141)

Similarly, we obtain

\displaystyle I^{-+}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-2\pi i(n-q)f\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f+p\Delta F)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f

\displaystyle=(-1)^{(n-q)m}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-2\pi i(n-q)x\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}(x)\tilde{\phi}(x+(p+m)\Delta F)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\,,

(142)

\displaystyle I^{+-}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-2\pi i(n-q)f\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}(f+m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f-p\Delta F)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f

\displaystyle=(-1)^{(n-q)m}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{2\pi i(n-q)x\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}(x)\tilde{\phi}(x+(p+m)\Delta F)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\left(I^{-+}\right)^{*}\,.

(143)

Introducing

F^{\pm}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-2\pi i(n-q)x\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}(x)\tilde{\phi}(x+(p\pm m)\Delta F)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\,,

(144)

Eq. (
137
) becomes

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}

\displaystyle=A_{m}A_{p}\left[C_{mn}^{*}C_{pq}I^{+}+C_{mn}^{*}C_{pq}^{*}I^{+-}+C_{mn}C_{pq}I^{-+}+C_{mn}C_{pq}^{*}I^{-}\right]

\displaystyle=A_{m}A_{p}(-1)^{(n-q)m}\left[C_{mn}^{*}C_{pq}F^{-}+C_{mn}^{*}C_{pq}^{*}\left(F^{+}\right)^{*}+C_{mn}C_{pq}F^{+}+C_{mn}C_{pq}^{*}\left(F^{-}\right)^{*}\right]

\displaystyle=2A_{m}A_{p}(-1)^{(n-q)m}\left[\Re\left(C_{mn}^{*}C_{pq}F^{-}\right)+\Re\left(C_{mn}C_{pq}F^{+}\right)\right]\,.

(145)

For orthogonality we need to verify that this expression vanishes if
|p-m|=1
or
p=m
,
n\neq q
.

B.1
Normalization

For
p=m
,
n=q
the inner product needs to normalize to unity. Momentarily restricting to
p=m\neq 0,N_{f}
, we find

\displaystyle F^{+}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\tilde{\phi}(x)\tilde{\phi}(x+2m\Delta F)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=0\,,

(146)

as
\tilde{\phi}(x)
and
\tilde{\phi}(x+2m\Delta F)
have no overlapping support, and

\displaystyle F^{-}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\tilde{\phi}(x)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{\Delta F}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\tilde{\varphi}(x)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\,.

(147)

The integral of the Meyer filter can be split off in the flat and rolloff regions:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\tilde{\varphi}(x)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int^{-\alpha}_{-\alpha-\beta}\cos^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\nu_{d}\left(\frac{|f|-\alpha}{\beta}\right)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f+\int_{-\alpha}^{\alpha}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f+\int^{\alpha+\beta}_{\alpha}\cos^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\nu_{d}\left(\frac{|f|-\alpha}{\beta}\right)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f

\displaystyle=2\alpha+2\int^{\alpha+\beta}_{\alpha}\cos^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\nu_{d}\left(\frac{f-\alpha}{\beta}\right)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\,.

(148)

Substituting
x=(f-\alpha)/\beta
yields

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\tilde{\varphi}(x)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=2\alpha+2\int^{\alpha+\beta}_{\alpha}\cos^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\nu_{d}\left(\frac{f-\alpha}{\beta}\right)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f=2\alpha+2\beta\int^{1}_{0}\cos^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\nu_{d}\left(x\right)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\,.

(149)

Another substitution
x\rightarrow 1-x
in the last integral yields

\displaystyle\int^{1}_{0}\cos^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\nu_{d}\left(x\right)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int^{1}_{0}\cos^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\nu_{d}\left(1-x\right)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int^{1}_{0}\cos^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\left(1-\nu_{d}\left(x\right)\right)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int^{1}_{0}\sin^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\nu_{d}\left(x\right)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\,,

(150)

where we have used the partition-of-unity condition,
\nu_{d}(x)+\nu_{d}(1-x)=1
, and the fact that
\cos^{2}x+\sin^{2}x=1
.
Collecting everything, we find

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\tilde{\varphi}(x)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=2\alpha+\beta=\Delta F\,,

(151)

and therefore
F^{-}=1
.

The inner product then reduces to

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}

\displaystyle=2A_{m}^{2}\left[\Re\left(C_{mn}^{*}C_{mn}F^{-}\right)+\Re\left(C_{mn}C_{mn}F^{+}\right)\right]=2\frac{1}{2}\Re\left(C_{mn}^{*}C_{mn}\right)=1

(152)

for both
m+n
even and odd.

For
m=p=0,N_{f}
, a similar calculation ensues with a few differences.
Firstly,
F^{+}
no longer vanishes, instead
F^{+}=F^{-}=1
.
For
m=0
, this is obvious.
For
m=N_{f}
,
F^{+}
cannot be obtained from the continuous expression of Eq. (
144
).
The result
F^{+}=F^{-}=1
is instead a consequence of aliasing due to finite sampling where the filter centered at
N_{f}\Delta F
aliases onto the one at
-N_{f}\Delta F
, see Appendix
C
.
Second, the prefactor is smaller by a factor of
\sqrt{1/2}
.
Overall, the inner product for the DC (
m=0
) and Nyquist (
m=N_{f}
) bins evaluates to

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}

\displaystyle=2A_{m}^{2}\left[\Re\left(C_{mn}^{*}C_{mn}F^{-}\right)+\Re\left(C_{mn}C_{mn}F^{+}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=2\frac{1}{4}\left[\Re\left(C_{mn}^{*}C_{mn}\right)+\Re\left(C_{mn}C_{mn}\right)\right]\,,

(153)

which vanishes for
m+n
odd and evaluates to
1
for
m+n
even.
Thus for the DC and Nyquist bands, half the cells are empty.

B.2
Orthogonality,
p=m
,
n\neq q

Setting
p=m\neq 0,N_{f}
,
n\neq q
we again get

F^{+}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-2\pi i(n-q)x\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}(x)\tilde{\phi}(x+2m\Delta F)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=0\,,

(154)

as the filters in the integrand share no support, and

F^{-}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-2\pi i(n-q)x\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}(x)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\,.

(155)

This expression amounts to the Fourier transform of
\tilde{\phi}(x)^{2}
and thus
F^{-}
is real, as
\tilde{\phi}(x)^{2}
is real and even.
The inner product reduces to

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}

\displaystyle=2A_{m}^{2}(-1)^{(n-q)m}\Re\left(C_{mn}^{*}C_{mq}\right)F^{-}\,.

(156)

•

Case 1:
n-q
is odd. In this case,
m+n
and
m+q
have opposite parities and
\Re\left(C_{mn}^{*}C_{mq}\right)=0
. The inner product thus vanishes for odd
n-q
.

•

Case 2:
n-q
is even. In this case, defining an integer
n-q=2k
, the integral is

F^{-}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-2\pi i(2k)x\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}(x)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\,.

(157)

We invoke the partition-of-unity equation

\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}\tilde{\phi}(f-p\Delta F)^{2}=\frac{1}{\Delta F}\,,

(158)

which we Fourier transform to obtain

\displaystyle\int e^{-2\pi ift}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}\tilde{\phi}(f-p\Delta F)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f

\displaystyle=\int e^{-2\pi ift}\frac{1}{\Delta F}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\Rightarrow

(159)

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}\int e^{-2\pi ift}\tilde{\phi}(f-p\Delta F)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta F}\delta(t)\xrightarrow{u=f-p\Delta F}

(160)

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}\int e^{-2\pi i(u+p\Delta F)t}\tilde{\phi}(u)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta F}\delta(t)\rightarrow

(161)

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-2\pi ip\Delta Ft}\int e^{-2\pi iut}\tilde{\phi}(u)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta F}\delta(t)\rightarrow

(162)

\displaystyle\int e^{-2\pi iut}\tilde{\phi}(u)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-2\pi ip\Delta Ft}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta F}\delta(t)\,.

(163)

The first term on the left hand side is
F^{-}
evaluated at
t=2k\Delta T=2k/(2\Delta F)
.
The second term for the sum of complex exponentials can be expressed through the Poisson summation formula as

\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-2\pi ip\Delta Ft}=\frac{1}{\Delta F}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta\left(t-\frac{\ell}{\Delta F}\right)\,,

(164)

leading to

\int e^{-2\pi iut}\tilde{\phi}(u)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta\left(t-\frac{\ell}{\Delta F}\right)=\delta(t)

(165)

which means that the integral must vanish for all integer multiples of
t=\ell/\Delta F\neq 0
, including
k/\Delta F
which corresponds to
F^{-}
.
The inner product thus vanishes for even
n-q
.

For
p=m=0,N_{f}
, the calculation is essentially identical, only
F^{+}
no longer vanishes but it is equal to
F^{-}
, similarly to the normality calculation above.
From then, the argument proceeds unaltered and the inner product vanishes by the parity argument for
n-q
odd and partition-of-unity and Poisson summation resulting in
F^{+}=F^{-}=0
for
n-q
even.

B.3
Orthogonality,
|p-m|=1

We restrict to
1<p=m+1<N_{f}
without loss of generality, as the
m=p+1
case follows by symmetry. Now

F^{+}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-2\pi i(n-q)x\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}(x)\tilde{\phi}(x+(2m+1)\Delta F)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=0\,,

(166)

as the filters have no support, and

F^{-}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-2\pi i(n-q)x\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}(x)\tilde{\phi}(x+\Delta F)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\,.

(167)

The inner product reduces to

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}

\displaystyle=2A_{m}A_{m+1}(-1)^{(n-q)m}\Re\left(C_{mn}^{*}C_{(m+1)q}F^{-}\right)\,.

(168)

The surviving term, only has support in the region where the adjacent windows overlap,
[-\Delta F,0]
.
The change of variables
u=x+\Delta F/2
centers the functions about the overlap interval and yields

\displaystyle F^{-}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\Delta F}^{0}e^{-2\pi i(n-q)x\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}(x)\tilde{\phi}(x+\Delta F)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{-\Delta F/2}^{+\Delta F/2}e^{-2\pi i(n-q)\left(u-\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}\left(u-\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(u+\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=e^{2\pi i(n-q)\frac{\Delta F\Delta T}{2}}\int_{-\Delta F/2}^{+\Delta F/2}e^{-2\pi i(n-q)u\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}\left(u-\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(u+\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=e^{i(n-q)\frac{\pi}{2}}\int_{-\Delta F/2}^{+\Delta F/2}e^{-2\pi i(n-q)u\Delta T}\tilde{\phi}\left(u-\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(u+\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u

(169)

Since
\tilde{\phi}(u)
is even, so is the product

\tilde{\phi}\left(u-\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(u+\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)=\tilde{\phi}\left(-u+\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(-u-\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)=\tilde{\phi}\left((-u)-\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left((-u)+\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)

(170)

•

Case 1:
n-q=2k
is even. In this case,
C_{mn}
and
C_{(m+1)q}
have opposite parity and thus their product is imaginary. The inner product is proportional to the imaginary part of
F^{-}
,

\displaystyle\Im\left[F^{-}\right]

\displaystyle=\sin\left[k\pi\right]\int_{-\Delta F/2}^{+\Delta F/2}\cos\left[2\pi(2k)u\Delta T\right]\tilde{\phi}\left(u-\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(u+\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle-\cos\left[k\pi\right]\int_{-\Delta F/2}^{+\Delta F/2}\sin\left[2\pi(2k)u\Delta T\right]\tilde{\phi}\left(u-\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(u+\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\,.

(171)

The first term vanishes due to
\sin\left[k\pi\right]=0
for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, while the second term vanishes as the integral of an odd function (the even filter product and the odd sine) over a symmetric interval.
Overall,
\Im\left[F^{-}\right]=0
and the inner product vanishes for even
n-q
.

•

Case 2:
n-q=2k+1
is odd. In this case,
C_{mn}
and
C_{(m+1)q}
have even parity and thus their product is real. The inner product is proportional to the real part of
F^{-}
,

\displaystyle\Re\left[F^{-}\right]

\displaystyle=\cos\left[k\pi+\frac{\pi}{2}\right]\int_{-\Delta F/2}^{+\Delta F/2}\cos\left[2\pi(2k+1)u\Delta T\right]\tilde{\phi}\left(u-\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(u+\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle+\sin\left[k\pi+\frac{\pi}{2}\right]\int_{-\Delta F/2}^{+\Delta F/2}\sin\left[2\pi(2k+1)u\Delta T\right]\tilde{\phi}\left(u-\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)\tilde{\phi}\left(u+\frac{\Delta F}{2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\,.

(172)

The first term vanishes due to
\cos\left[k\pi+\pi/2\right]=0
for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, while the second term vanishes as the integral of an odd function (the even filter product and the odd sine) over a symmetric interval.
Overall,
\Re\left[F^{-}\right]=0
and the inner product vanishes for odd
n-q
.

For the DC (
p=1
,
m=0
) and Nyquist (
p=N_{f}
,
m=N_{f}-1
) bins again
F^{+}=F^{-}
, after which the argument proceeds similarly.

Appendix C
Nyquist periodicity

In this Appendix, we show that the finite sampling results in the frequency-domain data having a periodicity of twice the Nyquist frequency
f_{\mathrm{Nyq}}=N_{f}\Delta F=1/(2\Delta t)
.
The time-domain data are sampled at intervals of
\Delta t

x_{s}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}x_{k}\delta(t-k\Delta t)\,.

(173)

Substituting the samples, the continuous Fourier transform is

\tilde{x}_{s}(f)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x_{s}(t)e^{-2\pi ift}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{k}x_{k}\delta(t-k\Delta t)e^{-2\pi ift}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{k}x_{k}e^{-2\pi ifk\Delta t}\,,

(174)

which is periodic with frequency twice the Nyquist frequency
f_{\mathrm{Nyq}}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{s}(f+2f_{\mathrm{Nyq}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}x_{k}e^{-2\pi i(f+2f_{\mathrm{Nyq}})k\Delta t}=\sum_{k}x_{k}e^{-2\pi ifk\Delta t}e^{-4\pi if_{\mathrm{Nyq}}k\Delta t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}x_{k}e^{-2\pi ifk\Delta t}\left(e^{2\pi i}\right)^{k}=\sum_{k}x_{k}e^{-2\pi ifk\Delta t}=\tilde{x}_{s}(f)\,.

(175)

This is a direct consequence of finite sampling and of course does not apply for continuous functions.

Appendix E
Correspondence between WDM and the Fourier transform

In this Appendix, we derive the correspondence between the WDM and Fourier transforms in the appropriate limit,
N_{t}=2
and
N_{f}=N/2
.
Starting with Eq. (
41
) for the interior bands, the contribution of WDM band
m
to the frequency domain data is

\tilde{x}_{m}(f+m\Delta F)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\tilde{\phi}(f)\sum_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}w_{mn}C_{mn}(-1)^{mn}e^{-2\pi inf\Delta T}\,,

(176)

where the discrete values of the frequency series fall at
f=k\Delta F
where
k=\{-N_{t}/2,\ldots,N_{t}/2-1\}
.
Setting
N_{t}=2
and
k=\{-1,0\}
, we have

\tilde{x}_{m}(k\Delta F+m\Delta F)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\tilde{\phi}(k\Delta F)\sum_{n=0}^{1}w_{mn}C_{mn}(-1)^{mn}e^{-2\pi ink\Delta F\Delta T}\,.

(177)

Since
\tilde{\phi}(-\Delta F)=0
by the compact support of the filter, only
k=0
survives

\tilde{x}_{m}(m\Delta F)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Delta F}}\left[w_{m0}C_{m0}+(-1)^{m}w_{m1}C_{m1}\right]\,,

(178)

where we have used
\tilde{\phi}(0)=1/\sqrt{\Delta F}
.
Finally, noting that
(-1)^{m}C_{m1}=iC_{m0}
, we can simplify this expression to

\tilde{x}(m\Delta F)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Delta F}}C_{m0}\left(w_{m0}+iw_{m1}\right)\,.

(179)

The calculation for the edge bands
m=0,N_{f}
proceeds similarly and gives

\tilde{x}(0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta F}}w_{00}\,,\qquad\tilde{x}(N_{f}\Delta F)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta F}}\begin{cases}w_{N_{f}0}&N_{f}~\mathrm{even}\,,\\
-w_{N_{f}1}&N_{f}~\mathrm{odd}\,,\end{cases}

(180)

both of which are real as expected.

In the limit of
N_{t}=2
and
N_{f}=N/2
, each complex value of the frequency series is given by a set of two real values in the WDM domain.

Appendix F
The short-time Fourier transform

By the Balian-Low theorem, the short-time Fourier transform (SFT) cannot be simultaneously orthonormal and well-localized in both time and frequency.

The common application of searches for continuous waves from isolated neutron stars prioritizes orthonormality
15
, which is achieved by making the rectangular SFT windows non-overlapping.
While this has good localization in time, the Balian-Low theorem guarantees poor localization in frequency.
For example, for a rectangular window in the time domain

g_{m}(t)=\begin{cases}1&m\,\Delta T\leq t<(m+1)\,\Delta T\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\,,

(181)

the frequency domain is

\tilde{g}_{m}(f)=\Delta T\,\frac{\sin\left(2\pi f\Delta T/2\right)}{2\pi f\Delta T/2}e^{-2\pi if\Delta T\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\,.

(182)

This
\operatorname{sinc}
function is maximized at
f=0
and falls off as
1/f
and therefore has infinite second frequency moment

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f^{2}|\tilde{g}_{m}(f)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f\sim\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f=\infty\,.

(183)

As an outcome, the transform of a signal has support beyond the time-frequency bins the signal crosses, smearing to adjacent frequencies as
1/f
.
Isolated neutron star signals are intrinsically compact in frequency.
Even with the frequency spread due to pulsar spin evolution and Doppler modulation, support is confined within a single frequency bin per SFT for typical SFT durations.
For such monochromatic signals, the frequency smearing reduces to simply the window’s spectral response, i.e., the
\operatorname{sinc}
Dirichlet kernel
30
.
Sub-bin frequency offsets are recovered in demodulation
30
, while the frequency drift across the observation is tracked by demodulating each SFT to the signal’s expected frequency.
Compact binary signals have a faster frequency evolution that cannot be contained within a single bin per SFT.
For a linear frequency evolution, the response generalizes to the Fresnel kernel
26
that combines two contributions: the frequency bins that the signal crosses within an SFT and the
1/f
rectangular window smearing.
In both cases, truncation and a corresponding error tolerance are required in the number of frequency bins used to evaluate the transform.
Alternatively, non-rectangular windows can achieve better frequency localization (steeper than
1/f
kernel fall-off), but at the expense of overlapping windows in time and non-orthogonality
10
.

Simultaneous good time and frequency localization can only be achieved by oversampled Gabor frames,
\Delta T\Delta F<1
, thus abandoning orthonormality.
The practical implication is that the covariance matrix, used to evaluate likelihoods and inner products, has two contributions: the noise correlations and the cross-terms between atoms.
For a Gabor frame with atoms
g_{mn}
centered at time
n\Delta T
and frequency
m\Delta F
, the data coefficients are

d_{mn}=\int d(t)\,g_{mn}^{*}(t)\,dt\,,

(184)

where

\int g_{mn}^{*}(t)g_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\neq\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\,.

(185)

Inner products, such as the data/signal term in the likelihood, take the schematic form

\displaystyle(d\mid h)

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{ij}d^{*}_{i}C^{-1}_{ij}h_{j}\,,

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{mn,m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}d_{mn}^{*}\,G_{mn,m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}\,h_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}\,,

(186)

where the matrix
G_{mn,m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}

encodes both the noise covariance (present in both orthonormal bases and frames) and the frame correlations, i.e., the overlap between distinct atoms.
The precise form of
G
depends on whether the frame is tight, with generic frames involving a dual frame that further complicates the expressions.
In either case
G
is generally dense, entangling the noise covariance and the frame’s structure.
Separating the noise and frame contributions is not possible outside of special cases such as the slowly-varying PSD limit where
\mathbf{C}^{-1}
is diagonal in the frequency domain.

Appendix G
Toeplitz derivation

In this Appendix, we show that Eq. (
74
) has a Toeplitz structure, i.e., it only depends on
r=n-n^{\prime}
.
Per Eq. (
67
) (via a change of variables
f\rightarrow f-m\Delta F
), the WDM domain noise covariance matrix for stationary noise is

\Lambda_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}=\frac{1}{2}\,\Re\!\left[\,C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}^{*}\int_{(m-1)\Delta F}^{(m+1)\Delta F}\!S(f)\,\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\,\tilde{\phi}(f-m^{\prime}\Delta F)\,e^{2\pi irf\Delta T}\,\mathrm{d}f\,\right]\,.

(187)

We introduce the cross-band noise PSD as

\tilde{K}_{mm^{\prime}}(f)=S(f)\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f-m^{\prime}\Delta F)\,,

(188)

and its inverse Fourier transform as

K_{mm^{\prime}}[r]=\int_{(m-1)\Delta F}^{(m+1)\Delta F}S(f)\tilde{\phi}(f-m\Delta F)\tilde{\phi}(f-m^{\prime}\Delta F)e^{2\pi irf\Delta T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}f.

(189)

which depends only on the lag
r
.
Rewriting Eq. (
187
) with these definitions, we find

\Lambda_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}=\frac{1}{2}\Re\left(C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}^{*}K_{mm^{\prime}}[r]\right)\,.

(190)

Equation (
74
) depends on
C_{mn}C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{*}\Lambda_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}
which is then

\displaystyle C_{mn}C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{*}\Lambda_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}C_{mn}C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{*}\Re\left(C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}^{*}K_{mm^{\prime}}[r]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}C_{mn}C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{*}\left\{C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}^{*}K_{mm^{\prime}}[r]+C^{*}_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}K^{*}_{mm^{\prime}}[r]\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left\{K_{mm^{\prime}}[r]+\left(C^{*}_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}\right)^{2}K^{*}_{mm^{\prime}}[r]\right\}\,,

(191)

where we have used
|C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}^{*}|^{2}=1
.
To show that this is Toeplitz, it remains to show that
\left(C^{*}_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn}\right)^{2}
is a function of
r
only.
We find

(C^{*}_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}C_{mn})^{2}=(-1)^{m^{\prime}+n^{\prime}}(-1)^{m+n}=(-1)^{(m+m^{\prime})+(n+n^{\prime})}=(-1)^{(m+m^{\prime})+(n-n^{\prime})}=(-1)^{(m+m^{\prime})+r}\,,

(192)

where from the third to the fourth equality, we use the fact that
n+n^{\prime}
and
n-n^{\prime}
differ by
2n
and have the same parity for all
n,n^{\prime}
.
Therefore
C_{mn}C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{*}\Lambda_{(mn)(m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}
and Eq. (
74
) depend only on
r
, and therefore have Toeplitz structure.

In other words, for stationary noise the WDM covariance matrix has a parity-modulated Toeplitz structure, which becomes exactly Toeplitz when multiplied by
C_{mn}C_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{*}
.
