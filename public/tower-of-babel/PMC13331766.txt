Multiple substance use by HIV status among United States adults, 2021–2024
Authors: Juhan Lee, Simay Ipek, Andrea H Weinberger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubstance use is common among people with HIV (PWH) and is associated with lower antiretroviral therapy adherence, poorer HIV outcomes, and negative health consequences. PWH are more likely to use substances than the general population; however, less is known about multiple substance use, which is important as it might have greater negative effects for PWH (e.g., decreased life expectancy, negative mental health symptoms) than single substance use. This study examined the differences in the number of substances used by HIV status among United States (US) adults.MethodsWe analyzed pooled data from the 2021–2024 National Survey of Drug Use and Health (N = 186,823; 51.3% female, 61.3% non-Hispanic White). Multivariable Poisson regression models examined associations between self-reported lifetime HIV diagnosis (predictor) and number of substances used in the past month (outcome), adjusting for covariates.ResultsPWH reported a greater number of substances used in the past month compared to adults without HIV (adjusted prevalence ratio [APR] = 1.60; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.38–1.85). This association remained significant among individuals reporting any substance use (APR = 1.27, 95% CI = 1.13, 1.43) and among those reporting polysubstance use (2 or more substances) (APR = 1.15, 95% CI = 1.06, 1.24). Among PWH, the most common patterns were alcohol only, alcohol plus tobacco, and alcohol plus tobacco plus cannabis.ConclusionsPWH are more likely to engage in multiple substance use than adults without HIV, supporting the integration of substance use assessment, education, and treatment referrals into HIV care.Keywords: Substance use, Alcohol, Tobacco, cannabis, HIV

Introduction

Substance use is common among people with HIV (PWH). Among PWH in the United States (US), 40.6% reported commercial tobacco use and 37.4% reported cannabis use in 2021–2023 (Lee et al., 2025). In 2015–2019, 55% of PWH reported alcohol use, 1.2% reported heroin use, 5.8% reported cocaine use, 7.4% reported inhalant use, 6.9% reported methamphetamine use, 1.2% reported hallucinogen use, and 2.9% reported pain reliever misuse (Hai et al., 2025). Among PWH, substance use weakens the immune system and damages liver and neurological functions and is related to poorer adherence to HIV treatment and poorer HIV outcomes (Haorah et al., 2025; Lier et al., 2024; National Instiutes of Health, 2025).

The use of multiple substances is also common (Rockhill et al., 2025). Nearly half of US drug overdose deaths in 2022 involved multiple substances (e.g., alcohol plus at least one other substance), and federal agencies have encouraged research related to use of multiple substances such as the co-use of alcohol, tobacco, and cannabis (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), 2022; National Cancer Institute, 2025). Given that PWH are more likely to use each substance than people without HIV (Shiau et al., 2017), PWH may also use a greater number of substances. Multiple substance use is particularly concerning as pharmacological effects of each substance synergistically interact and have greater negative impacts than single substance use, including poorer ART adherence, lower levels of CD4 counts, and mental health symptoms among PWH (Bertholet et al., 2023; Korthuis et al., 2008; Przybyla et al., 2022).

PWH, compared to people without HIV, are more likely to report tobacco and cannabis co-use, and this co-use was associated with other substance use, such as alcohol and other substance misuse (Lee et al., 2025). However, this study only focused on tobacco and cannabis co-use and did not characterize multiple substance use patterns among PWH. Another study analyzed 2017–2018 data from N = 237 PWH in New York and observed that 21.4% of PWH reported polysubstance use (Przybyla et al., 2022). Similarly, Davy-Mendez and colleagues analyzed 2018–2020 data from N = 2865 PWH in Northern California and found that 26.4% reported polysubstance use (Davy-Mendez et al., 2023). However, data from 2020 or before may not reflect aspects of more recent substance use (e.g., electronic cigarette popularity, increasing cannabis legalization), which warrants updated analysis.

A knowledge gap thus remains in understanding patterns of multiple substance use by those with versus without HIV at the US population level in recent year datasets. This study used US national data to examine the number of substances used by HIV status. We hypothesized that adults with HIV would be more likely to report multiple substance use compared to adults without HIV.

Methods

Dataset and analytic sample

We used a pooled dataset of all adult respondents aged ≥ 18 years (N = 186,823) from the 2021–2024 public-use National Survey of Drug Use and Health (NSDUH), an annual nationally-representative survey of the civilian, noninstitutionalized population of the US that uses a multi-staged, stratified sampling design (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2022).

Ethics

The original survey protocol was approved by RTI's Institutional Review Board (IRB), and the secondary data analysis of the publicly available, deidentified NSDUH dataset was exempt from IRB review. To protect the confidentiality of respondents, RTI and SAMHSA conducted a multistep statistical disclosure limitation method, including removing any personal identifying information and geographic identifiers, and handling variables that have a high potential of personal identification (e.g., categorization, top-and-bottom coding, local suppression). Re-identification of the dataset is nearly impossible and strongly prohibited. Data were kept on password protected computers and only the lead author had access to the data.

Measures

The primary predictor was self-reported lifetime HIV diagnosis assessed by the item: “a doctor or other health care professional has ever told you that you had… HIV or AIDS (yes, no).” The primary outcome was the number of substances used in the past month (a count variable ranging from 0 to 12), including alcohol, tobacco/nicotine, cannabis, cocaine/crack, heroin, hallucinogens (LSD, PCP, ecstasy, DMT/AMT/foxy, ketamine, salvia), inhalants, and methamphetamine use as well as prescribed pain reliever, prescribed tranquilizer, prescribed simulant, and prescribed sedative misuse (i.e., use without a prescription of one's own, use in greater amounts, more often, or longer than directed, and/or use in any other way not directed by a doctor). The assessment of inhalant use in the 2024 NSDUH was different from the question asked in years prior to 2024 (i.e., respondents were asked to report the use of inhalants “for fun or to get high” in 2024 versus “for kicks or to get high” in prior years). The NSDUH indicates that “changes in this question did not affect the comparability of estimates between 2024 versus 2021-2023 for the use of any illicit drug use in the past year” (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA), 2025). Further this study focused on a count of substances used across years and did not compare inhalant use from one year to another. The prevalence of past-month inhalant use was 0.3% (weighted), which might have negligibly affected the results.

Covariates, selected based on the previous literature related to multiple substance use (Avery et al., 2025), included survey year (2021, 2022, 2023, 2024), age (18–25 versus 26 years or older), sex (female, male), race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, non-Hispanic other races), income ($0–$74,999, $75,000 or more), and past-month serious psychological distress (assessed by Kessler-6; serious psychological distress was classified as a score of 13 or higher) (Kessler et al., 2003).

Statistical analysis

We conducted descriptive statistics of study variables among the total adult respondents and stratified them by HIV status. Using the Rao–Scott-adjusted chi-squared tests for categorical variables and Wald-adjusted t-test for continuous variables, we examined the bivariate associations between HIV status and study variables. We then conducted a multivariable Poisson regression model on the number of substances used in the past month by HIV status, adjusted for covariates. We also described the top 3 most frequently used substances by HIV status. As supplemental analyses for multiple substance use, we conducted the same multivariable Poisson regression model only among those who used any substance in the past month (N = 120,932) and among those who used 2 or more drugs in the past month (N = 49,323).

The sampling weight was created for a pooled dataset of 4-year NSDUH data based on the recommendation (ANALWT2_C/4 [the number of years of pooled dataset]) (Center for Behavioral Health Statistics and Quality, 2017), and a complex sampling design was applied for variance estimation using svyset. We considered p < 0.05 (2-tailed) the statistical significance cutoff and followed the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. All analyses were conducted using STATA 19.5 (College Station, TX, US).

Results

Among the total adult respondents in the combined 2021–2024 NSDUH sample (see Table 1), 86.7% were 26 years old or older, 51.3% were women, 61.3% were non-Hispanic White, 56.6% made less than $75,000 in the past year, and 7.7% reported past-month serious psychological distress. Of the total adult respondents, 0.4% (weighted) reported a lifetime HIV diagnosis. Among adult respondents who reported any past-month substance use, the weighted mean of the number of substances used in the past month was 1.51 (SD = 0.8).

After adjusting for covariates (see Table 2), PWH reported a greater number of past-month substances used, compared to people without HIV (adjusted prevalence ratio [APR] = 1.60, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.38, 1.85). This finding was consistent among those who used one or more substances in the past month (APR = 1.27, 95% CI = 1.13, 1.43; Supplemental Table 1) and among those who used two or more substances in the past month (APR = 1.15, 95% CI = 1.06, 1.24; Supplemental Table 2). This suggests that PWH were more likely to report multiple substance use compared to people without HIV.

Discussion

This study observed greater multiple substance use and a greater number of substances used by PWH (versus adults without HIV) both in the full sample and just among those who reported multiple substance use. Multiple substance use, compared to single substance use, among PWH might be related to lower levels of CD4 counts, decreased life expectancy and negative mental health outcomes (Bertholet et al., 2023; Korthuis et al., 2008; Przybyla et al., 2022). Our findings highlight the need for additional research to examine patterns and drivers of multiple substance use to aid development of polysubstance prevention and cessation interventions and clinical guidance for incorporating substance use prevention and cessation within the HIV care continuum.

The potential reasons for multiple substance use by PWH remain unclear. PWH might be speculated to more frequently inject substances and engage in chemical sex (Dasgupta et al., 2019), which may be related to the use of other substances. However, these “hard” substances were not the frequently used substances identified by our findings. Another speculation is that PWH might use multiple substances for self-medication and coping with stress or mental health symptoms (White et al., 2014). Future studies should examine details related to the patterns of multiple substance use among PWH (e.g., which substances, when, how, why).

Consistent with previous findings in prior years (Bertholet et al., 2023; Przybyla et al., 2022), we observed that the most common substances used and their combinations among PWH were “alcohol only,” “alcohol + tobacco,” and “alcohol + tobacco + cannabis.” A study by Bertholet et al. found that “alcohol + cannabis” use was common among N = 233 PWH with a substance use disorder history (Bertholet et al., 2023). A study by Przybyla and colleagues also found “alcohol + cannabis” was the most frequently used polysubstance combination among N = 237 PWH from Montefiore Medical Center (Bronx, New York) (Przybyla et al., 2022). Given the negative health consequences associated with use of these substances among people with HIV and the underutilization of substance use treatment among PWH (West et al., 2024), our findings can inform clinical practices related to substance use assessment, patient education and referral for cessation of these substances among people within HIV care settings.

Limitations

First, we relied on surveys, which might have self-report biases. Second, the NSDUH excluded populations that have a higher prevalence of HIV and substance use (e.g., people experiencing homelessness, people experiencing incarceration) (Centers for Disease Prevention and Control, 2023; Stockman & Strathdee, 2010). Third, this study was a secondary analysis of previously collected data from a study not specifically designed to examine our research question. As such, we did not have data on patterns of use of multiple substances, frequency or potency of substances used, or reasons for use of substances alone and in combination. Fourth, unmeasured confounders such as social determinants of health might be related to multiple substance use among PWH (Avery et al., 2025). Fifth, even though we pooled 4-year datasets, the sample size of PWH (N = 454) was still small when considering subgroups (e.g., subgroups using different patterns of substances).

Conclusion

Our findings on the greater number of substances used by adults with HIV compared to adults without HIV can inform future research directions and clinical guidance. Future research should examine multiple substance use patterns, such as which substances are used (e.g., beer/wine versus liquor, cigarettes versus e-cigarettes, potency of each substance), how multiple substances are used (e.g., different occasion use versus concurrent or simultaneous use), and why multiple substances are used (e.g., self-medication, recreation). This in-depth understanding will inform the development of interventions for multiple substance use among people with HIV. Multiple substance use among PWH could inform current clinical guidance (Bositis & St. Louis, 2019; Haldane et al., 2017; Hill et al., 2023), including screening for multiple substance use, education about the harmful effects of using multiple substances, and referrals to substance use treatment programs.
