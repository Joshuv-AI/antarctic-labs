Extraoperative Subdural Grid Electrode Stimulation for Primary Motor Cortex Localization in Medically Intractable Epilepsy
Authors: Panu Boontoterm, Siraruj Sakoolnamarka, Karanarak Urasyanandana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and PurposeExtraoperative subdural grid electrode (SDE) implantation with cortical stimulation mapping (CSM) is a cornerstone of presurgical evaluation in medically intractable epilepsy, enabling localization of epileptogenic and eloquent cortex. Precise identification of the primary motor cortex (PMC) is essential to minimize postoperative motor deficits, particularly in pediatric patients. This study evaluated the clinical utility of extraoperative CSM for PMC localization and examined factors associated with stimulation amplitude thresholds in a large cohort.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 297 patients with medically refractory epilepsy who underwent SDE implantation with extraoperative CSM. Motor mapping was performed using standardized stimulation parameters. Postoperative motor outcomes were assessed and correlated with preservation of mapped PMC. Functional motor areas may deviate from classical anatomical landmarks, underscoring the necessity of direct functional mapping.

Introduction

Epilepsy is a common neurological disorder and approximately 20–30% of patients continue to experience seizures despite optimal pharmacological treatment.1 For these medically refractory cases, epilepsy surgery offers the greatest likelihood of achieving seizure freedom. A critical determinant of successful surgical outcome is the accurate localization of the epileptogenic zone while preserving eloquent cortical regions, particularly the primary motor cortex (PMC), to minimize postoperative neurological deficits.2

Subdural grid electrode (SDE) implantation is an established technique for presurgical evaluation in patients with medically intractable epilepsy. This approach enables high-resolution cortical recording and allows extraoperative cortical stimulation mapping (CSM) to identify functional areas, including motor and sensory cortices.3 Direct electrical stimulation provides functional information that cannot be reliably inferred from anatomical landmarks alone.

Accurate localization of the PMC is especially important in pediatric patients. Ongoing neurodevelopment and cortical plasticity may alter the anatomical-functional relationship of motor areas, particularly in children with early brain injury or cortical malformations.4–7 Consequently, classical anatomical landmarks such as the precentral gyrus may not consistently correspond to functional motor cortex.

Although extraoperative CSM is widely used, data regarding stimulation amplitude thresholds and factors influencing cortical excitability remain limited, particularly in large pediatric cohorts. Furthermore, most published studies originate from Western populations, and data from Southeast Asia are scarce. A better understanding of motor cortex localization and stimulation thresholds in a large pediatric population may help optimize functional mapping protocols and improve surgical safety.8–10

The objectives of this study were to: 1) evaluate the clinical utility of extraoperative SDE stimulation for identifying the PMC in a large cohort of patients with medically intractable epilepsy and 2) investigate clinical, radiological, and pathological factors associated with stimulation amplitude thresholds.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective study was conducted at the Pediatric Comprehensive Epilepsy Centre and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB no. IRBRTA 1731/2559).

Patient population

Medical records of patients with medically refractory epilepsy who underwent extraoperative SDE implantation between October 2006 and December 2024 were reviewed.

Inclusion criteria

Diagnosis of medically refractory epilepsy and underwent SDE implantation with extraoperative CSM (Fig. 1).

Exclusion criteria

Inability to complete functional mapping due to operative complications (e.g., intracranial hemorrhage), inadequate electrode coverage of the central sulcus, precentral gyrus, or postcentral gyrus, and incomplete medical records.

A total of 297 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis.

Preoperative evaluation

All patients underwent a standardized presurgical evaluation, including detailed clinical history, neurological examination, prolonged video-electroencephalography monitoring, structural and functional neuroimaging (computer tomography, magnetic resonance imaging [MRI], single photon emission computed tomography, and/or positron emission tomography as indicated), neuropsychological assessment, and multidisciplinary epilepsy conference review.

CSM

Extraoperative CSM was performed on postoperative 3 days by a pediatric neurologist and neurosurgeon. Biphasic rectangular electrical pulses were delivered at a frequency of 50 Hz with a pulse width of 200 μs and a stimulation duration of up to 5 seconds. Stimulation amplitude was initiated at 2.0 mA and increased in 1–2 mA increments to a maximum of 20 mA. Stimulation was terminated upon elicitation of a reproducible motor response, detection of after-discharges, or stimulus-induced seizure. The minimum stimulation amplitude required to elicit a consistent motor response was recorded (Figs. 2–5).

Antiseizure medication (ASM) status during mapping

All patients were maintained on stable ASMs regimens during the extraoperative mapping period. No acute medication withdrawal or dose escalation was performed immediately prior to stimulation. ASM exposure was categorized according to the number of medications (monotherapy vs. polytherapy) and mechanism of action (sodium channel- blocking agents, gamma-aminobutyric acid agonists agents, and others) for secondary analysis.

Postoperative motor outcome assessment

Postoperative motor function was assessed through standardized neurological examinations at hospital discharge and during outpatient follow-up at approximately 3 months and 6 months. Motor outcomes were classified as: 1) no new motor deficit; 2) transient motor deficit, defined as new weakness resolving within 3 months; 3) persistent motor deficit, defined as weakness lasting longer than 3 months.

Motor outcomes were compared between patients in whom the mapped PMC was fully preserved and those in whom resection occurred adjacent to or partially involving mapped motor sites.

Complications

Grid-related complications (e.g., infection, hemorrhage) and stimulation- related adverse events (e.g., after-discharges, provoked seizures) were systematically recorded.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as means and percentages. Pearson’s correlation test was used to analyze associations between stimulation amplitude thresholds and clinical, radiological, and pathological variables. Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05.

Ethical approval and consent

The study was approved by the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20240429003) and Institutional Review Board (IRBRTA 1731/2559). Because this was a retrospective study using de-identified medical record data, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived, consistent with institutional ethical guidelines. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, CIOMS guidelines (2012), and ICH-GCP standards.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 297 patients with medically intractable epilepsy were included in the final analysis. The mean age at surgery was 11.3 years. Most patients demonstrated normal developmental status (242 patients [81.5%]), while 55 patients (18.5%) had developmental delay. Neuronal migration disorders were the most common pathological diagnosis (74.1%), followed by gliosis (14.8%), tumors (7.4%), and infection or inflammation (3.7%). Preoperative brain MRI revealed structural abnormalities in 242 patients (81.5%), reflecting the high prevalence of lesional epilepsy in this cohort. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Identification and localization of the PMC

Extraoperative CSM successfully identified the PMC in all 297 patients. Motor responses were most frequently elicited from electrode contacts located over the precentral gyrus adjacent to the central sulcus, consistent with classical anatomical expectations. The overall distribution of identified PMC sites is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Despite this predominance, variability in functional localization was observed. Atypical PMC localization was identified in 33 patients (11.1%). Atypical localization was defined as reproducible motor responses occurring more than 10 mm anterior or posterior to the anatomically defined precentral gyrus. These motor responses were observed either anterior to the precentral gyrus or within the postcentral gyrus. Representative examples of typical and atypical motor cortex localization relative to anatomical landmarks are shown in Fig. 2.

Quantitative spatial analysis demonstrated that atypical PMC localization was significantly associated with earlier age at seizure onset and with lesions involving the perirolandic cortex. The mean distance of motor response sites from the central sulcus was significantly greater in patients with atypical localization compared with those with typical localization. Detailed spatial, lesion-related, and clinical characteristics of typical versus atypical PMC localization are summarized in Tables 1–6. No significant associations were observed between atypical localization and sex, developmental status, MRI abnormality, or pathological diagnosis.

Stimulation amplitude thresholds

The mean stimulation amplitude threshold required to elicit a reproducible motor response during extraoperative mapping was 11.5 mA (range, 4–20). Patient age demonstrated a significant inverse correlation with stimulation amplitude threshold (p=0.001), indicating that younger patients required higher stimulation amplitudes to evoke motor responses. This relationship is illustrated in Fig. 3.

When stratified by age, patients younger than 7 years required significantly higher stimulation amplitudes than older children and adolescents. This age-related association remained significant after controlling for ASM burden, including number of medications and medication class. No statistically significant associations were identified between stimulation amplitude threshold and sex, developmental status, MRI abnormalities, or pathological diagnosis. Factors associated with stimulation amplitude thresholds are summarized in Table 2.

Postoperative motor outcomes

Postoperative motor outcomes were available for all patients. Overall, new motor deficits were uncommon. Transient postoperative motor deficits occurred in 6.7% of patients and resolved within 3 months, while persistent motor deficits lasting longer than 3 months occurred in 2.4%.

Patients in whom the functionally mapped PMC was fully preserved demonstrated a significantly lower rate of persistent postoperative motor deficits compared with patients in whom resection occurred adjacent to or partially involving mapped motor sites. These outcome differences are detailed in Table 3, supporting the clinical relevance of functional PMC identification during presurgical planning.

Mapping-related parameters and complications

SDE implantation and extraoperative mapping were well tolerated overall. The mean number of grid contacts per patient was 48 and the mean duration of invasive monitoring was 6.2 days. Representative images of SDE implantation and functional mapping procedures are shown in Figs. 4, 5, respectively.

Stimulation-induced seizures occurred in 4.0% of patients and were self-limited. After-discharges without clinical seizure manifestation were observed in 7.1% of patients. Grid-related complications were infrequent, with infection occurring in 1.0% of patients and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage in 0.7%. Mapping-related parameters and complications are summarized in Tables 4–6.

Discussion

In this large cohort of patients with medically intractable epilepsy, we demonstrate that extraoperative CSM using SDEs reliably identifies the PMC and provides clinically actionable information that influences surgical outcomes. The principal findings of this study are threefold: 1) functional PMC localization was achieved in all patients, with measurable variability relative to anatomical landmarks; 2) younger age was independently associated with higher stimulation amplitude thresholds; and 3) preservation of functionally mapped PMC was associated with a significantly lower rate of persistent postoperative motor deficits.

Functional localization of the PMC

Although the PMC was most commonly localized to the precentral gyrus adjacent to the central sulcus, atypical PMC localization was identified in 11.1% of patients. As shown in Table 6, atypical motor responses were located significantly farther from the central sulcus and were frequently associated with perirolandic lesions and earlier seizure onset. These findings highlight the limitations of relying solely on anatomical landmarks for surgical planning, particularly in pediatric populations and in patients with early-onset epilepsy.

The bidirectional displacement of motor representations-both anterior and posterior to the precentral gyrus-suggests that functional motor organization may be altered by developmental and pathological factors. This observation supports prior reports of cortical reorganization in the setting of early brain injury and reinforces the necessity of direct functional mapping during presurgical evaluation.10–15

Mechanisms underlying atypical motor cortex localization

The association between atypical PMC localization and earlier seizure onset, as well as lesion proximity to the perirolandic cortex (Table 6), supports a developmental reorganization model. In this framework, early epileptogenic insults interfere with typical motor network maturation, leading to redistribution of functional motor representations. The quantitative spatial analysis performed in this study advances prior descriptive observations by providing objective metrics that link lesion geometry, developmental timing, and functional motor mapping.14–17

Age-related differences in cortical excitability

A key finding of this study is the significant inverse relationship between age and stimulation amplitude threshold. Younger patients, particularly those under 7 years of age, required higher stimulation amplitudes to elicit motor responses (Fig. 3, Table 2). Importantly, this association persisted after controlling for ASM burden, indicating that developmental factors rather than pharmacological effects are the primary drivers of age-related differences in cortical excitability.15–17

These findings have direct procedural implications. Anticipating higher stimulation thresholds in younger children may prevent under-stimulation and improve the accuracy of functional localization while maintaining procedural safety.

Clinical impact of functional mapping on surgical outcomes

Beyond localization, this study demonstrates that functional mapping has tangible clinical consequences. Patients in whom the functionally mapped PMC was preserved experienced significantly fewer persistent postoperative motor deficits (Table 3). This finding underscores that extraoperative CSM not only identifies eloquent cortex but also contributes to improved neurological outcomes by informing surgical decision-making.17

Pediatric-specific considerations and safety

Extraoperative motor mapping was well tolerated in this cohort, with low rates of stimulation-related seizures and grid-related complications (Table 5). These findings support the safety of this approach when performed using standardized protocols and multidisciplinary oversight. In pediatric patients, gradual stimulation escalation, close monitoring for after-discharges, and careful interpretation of motor responses remain essential.

Limitations

This study has limitations. Its retrospective, single-center design may limit generalizability. Although ASM exposure was accounted for by medication number and class, serum drug levels were not uniformly available and may contribute to residual confounding. In addition, long-term motor and functional outcomes beyond early postoperative follow-up were not systematically assessed. Nonetheless, the large cohort size, standardized stimulation parameters, and comprehensive spatial analysis provide robust evidence supporting the clinical value of extraoperative CSM in pediatric epilepsy surgery.

Extraoperative CSM using SDEs is a reliable and clinically impactful method for identifying the PMC in patients with medically intractable epilepsy. Functional motor areas may deviate from classical anatomical landmarks, particularly in patients with early seizure onset and perirolandic lesions, emphasizing the necessity of direct functional assessment. Younger children require higher stimulation amplitudes, a factor that should be anticipated during presurgical planning. Importantly, preservation of the functionally mapped PMC is associated with a significantly lower rate of persistent postoperative motor deficits, underscoring the role of functional mapping in optimizing surgical safety and neurological outcomes.
