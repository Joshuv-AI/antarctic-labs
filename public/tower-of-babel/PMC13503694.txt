Cable versus cerclage wire in weber tension band fixation for transverse olecranon fractures: a biomechanical study in a synthetic ulna model
Authors: Recep Selcuk Eyceyurt, Onur Suer, Meliksah Uzakgider, Mesut Tahta, Mahmut Pekedis, Cemil Kayali, Ahmet Adnan Karaarslan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stable fixation of displaced simple transverse olecranon fractures is required to maintain reduction under triceps traction and enable early elbow motion. Although Weber tension band fixation is widely used, the biomechanical influence of tension band material (cable vs cerclage wire) across different pin/anchorage designs remains unclear. This study compared resistance to fracture-line separation among six Weber-based constructs in a standardized synthetic ulna model. Sixty commercially available synthetic ulnae were osteotomized to create a transverse olecranon fracture model (Mayo type IIA) and randomly assigned to six groups (n = 10/group): bicortical pins, eyelet pins, and AA crimp constructs, each combined with either cable or cerclage wire in a figure-of-eight configuration. Specimens underwent monotonic tensile loading simulating triceps traction. Forces corresponding to 2 mm and 3 mm fracture-line separation were recorded. Between-group comparisons were performed within cable and cerclage subgroups, and cable-versus-cerclage comparisons were conducted within matched constructs (α = 0.05). At 2-mm separation, mean forces (N) were 316.1 ± 98.1 (bicortical cable), 277.0 ± 101.7 (eyelet cable), and 249.1 ± 104.2 (AA crimp cable), versus 273.1 ± 34.4 (bicortical cerclage), 283.6 ± 38.4 (eyelet cerclage), and 282.2 ± 36.9 (AA crimp cerclage). At 3-mm separation, mean forces were 340.5 ± 129.6, 336.5 ± 111.0, and 319.4 ± 78.8 for the cable constructs, and 332.9 ± 48.3, 354.7 ± 42.4, and 349.0 ± 37.2 for the cerclage constructs (bicortical, eyelet, and AA crimp, respectively). No significant differences were observed among pin/anchorage designs within the cable or cerclage subgroups at either threshold (all p > 0.05). Similarly, cable versus cerclage comparisons within matched constructs showed no significant differences at 2 mm or 3 mm (all p > 0.05). Under the specific conditions of this synthetic ulna model and monotonic tensile loading protocol, no statistically significant differences were detected between cable- and cerclage wire–based Weber tension-band constructs at either the 2-mm or 3-mm separation threshold. Bicortical, eyelet, and AA-crimp–based configurations also showed no significant differences in early gapping resistance. These findings should be interpreted as controlled monotonic early-separation data and not as evidence of clinical equivalence or fatigue durability.

Introduction

Olecranon fractures disrupt the elbow extensor mechanism and, when displaced, require sufficiently stable fixation to maintain reduction under triceps traction and to permit early elbow motion. Olecranon fractures represent a substantial proportion of adult upper-extremity injuries, and simple displaced transverse patterns—commonly classified as Mayo type IIA—are frequently encountered in clinical practice1.

For displaced and minimally comminuted olecranon fractures, tension band wiring (TBW) remains a widely used technique based on the principle of converting posterior tensile forces into compression at the articular surface2,3. However, under simulated triceps loading, fracture-line separation (gapping) may occur, reducing effective compression and potentially leading to loss of reduction, which can compromise the safety of early mobilization. Accordingly, the force required to reach a predefined separation threshold (e.g., 3 mm) provides a pragmatic biomechanical endpoint that reflects functional construct stability1.

Despite the widespread clinical use of TBW, numerous technical modifications of wire/pin configurations have been proposed to enhance stability, while implant irritation and complications such as wire migration have further driven ongoing efforts to optimize Weber-based constructs3,4. Recent biomechanical studies have also evaluated alternative screw–tension band constructs, pin configuration, pin length, and pin interval, emphasizing that fixation stability depends not only on the implant configuration but also on the loading protocol and selected failure endpoint5,6.

Cable-based tension-band constructs have been introduced as a potential alternative to conventional cerclage wire, partly because crimp-secured systems may offer different handling and locking characteristics. However, whether cable provides a statistically detectable difference in early fracture-line separation resistance compared with cerclage wire remains uncertain, particularly when evaluated across different pin/anchorage configurations in a standardized transverse fracture model. Moreover, because fatigue behavior, cable abrasion, and bone–implant interactions may become apparent primarily under cyclic loading, monotonic testing should be interpreted as an assessment of early gapping resistance rather than long-term construct durability.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to compare the biomechanical resistance to fracture-line separation among six Weber-based fixation configurations applied to a standardized Mayo type IIA transverse olecranon fracture model using synthetic ulnae. Specifically, we evaluated constructs using different pin anchorage methods (bicortical, eyelet, and AA crimp–based configurations) combined with either cable or cerclage wire tension banding, and quantified the forces required to produce 2 mm and 3 mm of fracture-line separation under monotonic tensile loading simulating triceps traction.

Materials and methods

Specimens and fracture model

Sixty commercially available polyurethane foam ulna specimens (Sawbones®, Malmö, Sweden) were used to establish a standardized olecranon fracture model. A transverse osteotomy was created at the metaphyseal region of the olecranon with an oscillating saw to reproduce a simple transverse olecranon fracture (Mayo type IIA). Prior to fixation, all specimens were visually examined to ensure similar fracture-line characteristics and comparable bone geometry. The specimens were then randomly assigned to six experimental groups (n = 10 each) based on the planned fixation construct (Fig. 1).Figure 1Fixation constructs. Synthetic ulnae were selected to minimize interspecimen variability in bone geometry and material properties, thereby allowing a controlled comparison of construct behavior under identical experimental conditions. This approach was intended to isolate the mechanical effect of fixation configuration; however, it does not reproduce biologic bone variability, tendon–bone interactions, or cable-related wear in human bone.

The use of synthetic ulnae therefore represents a methodological trade-off. It improves standardization by reducing variability in bone size, density, and morphology across groups, which is useful when comparing multiple fixation configurations. However, this consistency is achieved at the expense of physiologic realism, particularly with respect to pin purchase, local implant–bone wear, and failure progression. Accordingly, the findings should not be extrapolated directly to cadaveric bone, osteoporotic bone, or clinical durability.

All fixation procedures were carried out by one experienced surgeon to limit technical variation. Pin trajectory and inter-pin spacing, as well as figure-of-eight tension band placement, were standardized and replicated consistently for all specimens within each group.

For all constructs, tensioning was standardized procedurally rather than instrumentally. Cable crimping and cerclage wire twisting were performed by the same experienced surgeon using the same reduction sequence, with visual confirmation of fracture apposition and elimination of visible slack before final fixation. No tensiometer or load-monitoring device was used to quantify the initial tension applied to the cable or cerclage wire. Therefore, the applied initial tension should be considered technique-standardized but not directly measured.

Biomechanical testing

To determine the mechanical characteristics of the constructs, specimens were tested under monotonic tensile loading at room temperature (~ 24 °C) using a universal testing machine (Fig. 2). The distal part of each specimen was embedded in a custom ABS mold and secured in the lower grip (Fig. 2). Tensile loading was applied to the proximal olecranon fragment via a cerclage wire loop passed through a 3.5-mm cannulated screw. Specifically, after fracture fixation with two parallel pins (either standard K-wires or eyelet pins, depending on the group), a 2.7-mm drill hole was created between the pins in the proximal fragment and a 10-mm-long 3.5-mm cannulated screw was inserted. The cerclage wire was threaded through the cannulated screw and looped to connect the proximal fragment to the testing apparatus, thereby simulating triceps traction. This setup distributed the applied load along the cannulated screw wall and throughout its length, reducing the risk of the cerclage wire cutting into the synthetic bone. Two reference points were marked on the fracture line, and separation was monitored using a video camera (David-Cam-3.1-M) (Fig. 3). Force data were recorded using a 5-kN load cell at a sampling rate of 10 Hz. Forces corresponding to 2 mm and 3 mm fracture-line separation were determined9,10. Fracture-line separation was quantified using a custom Python–OpenCV workflow that tracked the relative distance between two user-defined points in real time. The forces corresponding to 2-mm and 3-mm fracture-line separation were selected as predefined endpoints. The 2-mm threshold was chosen because it is commonly used in olecranon fracture literature as a clinically relevant cut-off for articular displacement, fixation loss, or unacceptable postoperative gapping. The 3-mm threshold was included as an additional early-to-moderate separation endpoint to further challenge the constructs while remaining below gross catastrophic failure definitions.

Monotonic tensile loading was chosen as an initial comparative protocol to quantify the force required to reach predefined early fracture-line separation thresholds under controlled conditions. This protocol was not intended to reproduce cyclic elbow motion, fatigue accumulation, cable-related abrasion, or long-term bone–implant interaction. Therefore, the present mechanical endpoint reflects early gapping resistance under a simplified simulated triceps traction model.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test for each group and separation threshold, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. Because some subgroups showed departures from normality and variance homogeneity was not consistently satisfied, Welch-based comparisons and Games–Howell post hoc testing were used where appropriate. Welch’s one-way ANOVA was used to compare differences among the groups when the assumption of equal variances was violated, and the Games–Howell post hoc test was used for pairwise comparisons because it does not assume equal variances across groups. For matched cable-versus-cerclage comparisons within the same anchorage design, independent-samples t-tests were performed, with Welch correction applied when equal variance assumptions were violated. All tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The sample size was set at 10 specimens per group, consistent with commonly used group sizes in experimental biomechanical fixation studies. A formal a priori power analysis was not performed. As a sensitivity estimate, with 10 specimens per group, a two-sided independent-samples comparison at α = 0.05 provides 80% power to detect only large pairwise standardized effects of approximately Cohen’s d = 1.32. Therefore, the study should be interpreted as being powered to detect large mechanical differences, but not to prove equivalence or exclude small-to-moderate differences between constructs.

Additionally, the measurements were visualized using violin plots, which display the distribution of data points for the groups (Fig. 4). In a split violin plot, a wider section indicates a higher density of data points within the group, while a narrower section indicates a lower density. Discussion

The principal finding of the present biomechanical study is that, in a standardized Mayo type IIA synthetic transverse olecranon fracture model subjected to monotonic tensile loading along the simulated triceps vector, no statistically significant difference was detected between cable-based and cerclage wire–based figure-of-eight tension band constructs in the force required to produce 2-mm or 3-mm fracture-line separation. Similarly, within each tension-band material subgroup, bicortical pins, eyelet pins, and AA-crimp–based constructs did not differ significantly in early gapping resistance. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, because the study was not designed as an equivalence or non-inferiority analysis and cannot exclude smaller differences between constructs.

A key explanation for the lack of separation-based differences is that endpoint choice and loading protocol strongly influence which “weak link” is being tested. Cadaveric studies using realistic cyclic triceps loading have shown that different fixation methods may generate similar initial compression yet diverge in implant survival/fatigue behavior. Hahn et al. found no significant differences in fracture compression among intramedullary screw, locking plate, and TBW, but reported a markedly higher risk of implant failure for TBW during cyclic loading, with failure defined as implant breakage or fracture gapping > 5 mm11. Similarly, Midtgaard et al. reported no difference in fracture displacement between TBW and locking plate fixation after cyclic testing and load-to-failure, but hardware failure occurred predominantly in TBW constructs, and the metal cerclage twist was identified as the weakest link12. These observations are important for interpreting our results: because we focused on forces at 2–3 mm separation during monotonic loading, our test preferentially captures early gapping resistance rather than fatigue-related failure modes such as twist-related failure, crimp slippage, cable abrasion, or progressive pin migration.

The 2-mm gapping threshold has both clinical and experimental relevance in olecranon fracture fixation. Clinically, residual or recurrent intra-articular displacement greater than approximately 2 mm is commonly considered undesirable, whereas experimentally several biomechanical studies have used 2 mm of gap formation or displacement increase as a criterion for fixation loss6,10,13–15. In the present study, the 3-mm threshold was added to evaluate whether group differences became more apparent with greater early separation, without shifting the endpoint toward gross catastrophic failure. In contrast, more severe failure definitions, such as > 5 mm gapping under cyclic triceps loading, assess a different mechanical domain and may reveal fatigue-related failure mechanisms not captured by the present protocol11.

The present model should therefore be interpreted as a simplified early-separation model rather than a physiologic failure model. Polymeric ulnae provide experimental consistency but cannot reproduce the pin-retention, wear, and bone–implant interaction properties of cadaveric or osteoporotic human bone. Similarly, monotonic tensile loading captures the force required to reach predefined gapping thresholds but does not reproduce the cyclic environment in which clinical fixation failure, progressive loosening, or implant-related wear may occur.

Recent biomechanical studies have continued to refine olecranon fixation strategies by evaluating alternative screw–tension band constructs, pin configuration, pin length, and pin interval, highlighting that construct performance may be influenced by both implant geometry and loading protocol5,6. Zhao et al. measured fracture gap across flexion angles in a Mayo type IIA synthetic model and reported average gaps within a limited early-displacement range for classic TBW, with no specimen failure during cyclic loading5. Lee and Lee evaluated ring-pin parameters in transverse olecranon fracture fixation and emphasized the importance of pin configuration in tension-band mechanics6. These contemporary data support the need to interpret fixation stability not as a single property, but as a construct-specific response that depends on endpoint selection, loading direction, and whether cyclic or monotonic testing is used.

Our findings also align with studies that directly quantify fracture displacement/gap under simulated triceps loading. Vesterby et al., using paired cadaveric elbows and optical displacement measurement, reported very small fracture displacement after cyclic triceps loading to 300 N for 200 cycles, indicating that well-performed tension band constructs can maintain near-anatomic apposition under physiologic-range cyclic traction16. Taken together, these data suggest that, in controlled transverse models, early gapping tends to remain within a limited range under moderate cyclic loads, while clinically relevant separation thresholds represent a pragmatic benchmark to compare construct reserve against loss of reduction. In our model, the forces associated with reaching 2–3 mm separation were not significantly different across the evaluated constructs, supporting the interpretation that early separation resistance under monotonic loading was broadly similar between cable and wire tension-band materials and among the tested anchorage variants.

From a practical standpoint, many pin/anchorage modifications, such as eyelet-type features or locking/crimp elements, are adopted primarily to reduce clinically important complications such as pin migration, soft-tissue irritation, and hardware removal, rather than to fundamentally change early gapping mechanics. Migration and symptomatic hardware are repeatedly reported as common reasons for reoperation after TBW, and comparative clinical series emphasize that these complications are multifactorial17. Design innovations aimed at preventing migration have been associated with lower migration/discomfort and lower secondary displacement rates in clinical practice4,18. In addition, clinical comparisons have suggested that complication profiles may not be explained solely by whether K-wires are transcortical or intramedullary13. In this context, our biomechanical observation—that eyelet- and AA-crimp–based constructs did not significantly alter early separation resistance—may be mechanically informative within the limited context of early monotonic gapping, but should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical durability. These results indicate only that the tested anchorage modifications did not reduce early monotonic gapping resistance in this simple transverse model.

Importantly, cable-specific concerns may not be fully assessed by the present model. In clinical or cadaveric bone, repeated cyclic loading could theoretically promote cable-related abrasion, local bone wear, or progressive loss of tension, particularly because braided cable has different surface characteristics from smooth cerclage wire. The absence of cyclic loading and biologic bone in the present study means that cable wear-through, fatigue durability, and long-term fixation behavior were not evaluated. Accordingly, the present data should be viewed as an early-separation comparison and not as evidence that cable and cerclage wire would perform similarly under repetitive physiologic loading.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, synthetic ulna models do not reproduce variability in bone quality, soft-tissue constraints, and tendon–bone interactions seen clinically. Although synthetic models provide geometric and material consistency, they have different pin retention, wear, and bone–implant interaction properties compared with cadaveric or osteoporotic human bone. Second, we used monotonic tensile loading in a single vector, which does not capture cyclic fatigue, combined bending/rotation, repetitive elbow motion, or progressive implant–bone wear. This limitation is particularly relevant for cable constructs, because potential cable abrasion or wear-through behavior would be expected to emerge primarily under cyclic loading rather than during a single monotonic test. Third, the combination of synthetic bone and monotonic loading is an important limitation. Because synthetic ulnae do not reproduce the wear characteristics of human bone and because the constructs were not subjected to repetitive loading, the present study cannot evaluate cable-related abrasion, progressive bone cut-out, or wear-through phenomena. This is particularly relevant when comparing cable and cerclage wire constructs, as potential differences may become apparent only under cyclic loading in biologic bone. Fourth, the initial tension applied during cable crimping and cerclage wire twisting was not quantified. Although all constructs were prepared by the same surgeon using a standardized technique, unmeasured variation in initial preload may have influenced construct behavior. Fifth, although loading was continued until catastrophic failure, failure modes were not prospectively categorized or quantitatively analyzed. Therefore, the present study cannot determine whether ultimate failure occurred predominantly through wire twist loosening, crimp slippage, pin migration, synthetic bone cut-out, or other construct-specific mechanisms. Future studies should include predefined failure-mode classification, particularly under cyclic loading conditions. Sixth, the study was not designed as an equivalence or non-inferiority study. The lack of statistically significant differences should therefore not be interpreted as proof that the constructs are mechanically equivalent. With 10 specimens per group, the study was primarily capable of detecting large differences and may not exclude small-to-moderate differences between fixation methods. Finally, our outcomes were forces at 2 mm and 3 mm separation. Although these thresholds have clinical and experimental precedent, translation to the safety of early mobilization remains indirect.

Clinical implications and future directions

Within the tested monotonic synthetic model, cable- and cerclage wire–based constructs showed no statistically significant difference in early fracture-line separation resistance, and the evaluated pin/anchorage variants showed similar early gapping behavior. Therefore, in simple transverse patterns, implant selection should not be based solely on the present monotonic separation data. Practical considerations such as surgeon familiarity, cost, handling characteristics, implant availability, and strategies to mitigate migration or soft-tissue irritation remain relevant.

However, clinically important differences may emerge under cyclic loading and failure-mode analysis, where wire twist failure, crimp slippage, cable abrasion, pin migration, or bone cut-out may become more relevant. Future work should incorporate physiologic cyclic loading with elbow motion, evaluate predefined failure modes, and extend testing to cadaveric and osteoporotic bone models to better characterize fatigue performance and clinical applicability.

A more physiologic next step would be to compare selected cable and cerclage constructs in cadaveric specimens under cyclic loading that incorporates elbow motion or repetitive triceps traction. Such a design would allow evaluation of fatigue durability, cable-related abrasion, progressive bone cut-out, pin migration, crimp slippage, wire twist failure, and ultimate failure mechanisms. Therefore, the present study should be considered an initial controlled early-gapping comparison rather than a definitive assessment of clinical construct durability.

Conclusion

In this standardized synthetic Mayo type IIA transverse olecranon fracture model tested under monotonic tensile loading simulating triceps traction, no statistically significant differences were detected between cable-based and cerclage wire–based figure-of-eight Weber constructs in the force required to reach 2-mm or 3-mm fracture-line separation. Likewise, bicortical, eyelet, and AA-crimp–based configurations showed no significant differences in early gapping resistance under the tested conditions. These results should be interpreted within the limitations of a synthetic bone model and monotonic loading protocol and should not be extrapolated to cyclic fatigue performance or in vivo construct durability. Further studies using cadaveric specimens, physiologic cyclic loading, and predefined failure-mode analysis are warranted.
