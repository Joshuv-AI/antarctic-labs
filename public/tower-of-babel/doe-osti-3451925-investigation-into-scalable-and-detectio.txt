Enhanced Satellite Conjunction
Assessment
Purpose
We look to manipulate conjunction assessments for “viewable conjunctions” and produce them continuously. The term “viewable
conjunctions” is defined as the locations in space and time of a satellite and a Resident Space Object (RSO) where the satellite can
view the RSO under a set of definable constraints. As an example, Low Earh Orbit (LEO) satellites view the Earth from under 2000
km and viewing a RSO in space requires calculation of orbital propagation under constraints (e.g. solar illumination, distance
between observer and target, orbital location for viewable event,…) based on the state vectors of position, velocity, with covariance
for both satellite and RSO. This white paper outlines how we can obtain such solutions and is divided into two aspects. The first
aspect to address is the proposal of investigation into a high-performance computing implementation of satellite viewable
conjunction assessment. Implementation of a solution to the first aspect would allow for continuous solutions to viewable
conjunctions under constraints decreasing latency in satellite maneuver requests. The second aspect to address is the proposal of
detection-enhancement of observations between satellites and RSOs. Expanding beyond the spatial characterization of RSOs into
spectral characterization as well as orchestrated multiple observations provides more informed assessments for potential
conjunctions.

The investigations will use computing resources at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL). With the provision of Laika (Maxar
software) and direction from the sponsor, output will be made accessible through the Laika dashboard. Through a gated schedule of
technical deliverables to the sponsor, ORNL will provide progressively more robust viewable conjunction assessments from both
modelled and actual ephemeris data (see Figure 1).

Background
This section will give a cursory explanation of the 3 most important aspects of the viewable conjunction assessments to be utilized in
this proposal: the orbital propagation algorithm, the available software, and the varied ephemeris input.

Most conjunction assessments performed in the space situational awareness community regard collision observations. The software
and methodologies center on calculating an intersection or near intersection of objects in orbit around the Earth. The 18 Space
Defense Squadron (SDS) and 19 SDS actively work in this area of providing satellite lifecycle information for collision avoidance all
the way to deorbit.

Viewable conjunctions are those opportunities in space and time when a satellite can view, under defined constraints, an RSO. To
the benefit of that activity, the collision community produces input that can be used to calculate viewable conjunctions. By using
the U.S. Space Surveillance Network around the world to detect, track, catalog, and identify RSOs, they produce information on the
locations, velocities, and covariance on locations and velocities that can be used to calculate viewable conjunctions. We refer to
that information in the rest of this document as the state vector.

Algorithm
There are a few options when searching for orbital propagation algorithms. The progenitor of all such algorithms was the Simplified
General Perturbations 4 (SGP4) model developed in 1970 to propagate near-Earth satellite trajectories. Many different updates
have been made since SGP4 was made available but the underlying algorithm has remained basically the same even though
manifested in different software algorithms, refactors, and extended utilities. A notable official implementation of the SGP4
algorithm is the Special Perturbations Ephemeris propagator (SPEPH). As of 2022, it was the best-available trajectory propagation
algorithm used by the United States Space Force (USSF) and is export controlled. Some notable commercial and government
capabilities are shared in Table 1 below. No High-performance computing (HPC) implementations of SGP4-like algorithms are
known.

Ephemeris Inputs
There are many different ephemeris formats but only some are appropriate for use in the viewable conjunction assessment
proposed in this document. For the sake of positional accuracies, the format must include the covariance values for space and
velocity. Accuracies are highly dependent on orbit (LEO, MEO, GEO) and many other factors. Because of these requirements, the
generic on-orbit ephemeris format, the Modified ITC Ephemeris format, and the Orbital Ephemeris message (OEM) format are
appropriate. They include the position/velocity covariance matrix which is an upper triangular 6x6 matrix that outlines the
covariances involving positions x,y,z and velocities ẋ, ẏ, ż. It is useful to explain more about the formats that come as Two Line
Elements and Vector Covariance Matrix described below.

Conjunction Two Line Elements (TLE)

Two lines Element (TLE) were widely used for on-orbit conjunction prediction. The accuracy of TLE data had great impact on
conjunction prediction results and was one of the necessary parameters for collision probability calculation. Only when the
predicted position accuracy of TLE data was calculated exactly, then the value of collision probability was reliable. A conjunction TLE
refers to a close approach or potential collision between two or more objects in orbit, where the positions of those objects are
predicted using TLE sets. TLEs are a widely used format for representing satellite orbits, but their accuracy can impact the reliability
of conjunction predictions.

Vector Covariance Matrix (VCM)

Conjunction VCM can refer to the use of Vector Covariance Matrix (VCM) data in conjunction assessments. VCM data provides
information about the state and covariance (uncertainty in position and velocity) of a single object (like a satellite or piece of space
debris). Conjunction analysis uses this data to assess the risk of a collision between two orbiting objects.

For collision avoidance in space, both TLEs and VCMs provide information about the orbits of space objects. In essence, while TLEs
provide a basic understanding of an object's orbit, VCMs offer a more detailed and accurate picture, including the uncertainty
associated with the orbital state. This uncertainty information is vital for performing robust collision risk assessments and planning
avoidance maneuvers.

Due to the environment of cislunar objects, the state vector requires constant updates, the frequency of which varies. The
collection by 19 SDS of screenings of every RSO is at a minimum of once every 24 hours. Updates to many aspects of the screening
process occur every 8 hours. This frequency dictates the bounds for applicability of TLEs and VCMs, as well as the target for HPC
completion.

Available Software
Many orbital propagation software tools incorporate covariance propagation to analyze and understand the uncertainty associated
with satellite orbits. Covariance represents the errors or uncertainties in the estimated orbital state (position and velocity).
Propagating this covariance along with the orbit allows users to predict how these uncertainties evolve over time and understand
the confidence level of their orbit predictions. A requirement is that the software processes tracking data and generates orbit
ephemeris with realistic covariance and is used for accurate orbit estimates. As seen in Table 1, there are varied prices and
extension capabilities.

Proposed Methodology and Software
Regardless of which software/algorithm we use for the methodology, we will need to conduct verification/validation that the results
we are getting are equivalent to results that the sponsor receives through their system. Reducing differences in the
software/algorithm we use will reduce any complexities in this verification and validation process.

Assuming the most divergent situation, we will prepare to build a completely different software/algorithm situation. We would
prefer to receive Laika and BlueSim so we can have an almost identical system but with that possibly not the case, we will settle on
one of the systems listed in Table 1 above. Based on this decision, a set of verification and validation results will need to be
expressed from the sponsor to ORNL so we can check that the system we stand-up will be sufficient in result and timing. We
anticipate growing the sophistication of the result and decreasing the time through HPC. Below outlines a very general proposed
methodology in 3 stages: Collect, Process, Output.

Collect:

A.1: Model or observation created TLEs and VCMs for a number of satellites and RSOs. Look to automate collection and format
validation)

A.2: Constraints to define the applicability of the viewable conjunction calculations

Process:

B.1: Execute orbital propagation software that is appropriate for ingestion of both ephemeris and covariance information.

B.2: Collect timings and spatial accuracy from software utilizing inputs and constraints

B.3: Determine viewable conjunctions from proximity of satellite to RSOs with constraints

Output:

C.1: Validation of results against sponsor-approved and provided orbital propagation set

C.2: Single, multiple, and spatial/spectral observation predictions for proposed tasking.

C.3: Baseline and evaluation of HPC (CPU/GPU) as a solution for continuous viewable conjunction output.

Proposed Investigation for High Performance Computing
In rough orders of magnitude, one can assess a need for viewable conjunctions with the following numbers:
 (1) 10,000 satellites in Earth orbit
 (2) 1,000,000 pieces of space debris of significance
 (3) 5 constraints regarding viewable conjunctions
The number of combinations is then roughly 50 billion. At approximately 0.002 milliseconds per SGP4 model event, it is well within
the order of need of HPC to make weekly assessments of all combinations.
An investigation and implementation can involve a cluster consisting of multiple individual nodes/servers interconnected by a fast
network at ORNL in parallel and simultaneously as no edge conditions or communication needs would be necessary to produce
single, viewable conjunctions. The size of the cluster and the handling of data distribution and assemblage for dissemination would
be aspects of the investigation and implementation.

We propose baselining with a goal to determine what size HPC is necessary for the continuous viewable conjunction problem. We
also propose evaluation if CPU or GPU or both are more appropriate to the problem. ORNL has existing HPC systems to use for
baselining.

Proposed Investigation for Detection Enhancement
One view of a RSO from a single viewer gives a partial description of object. To produce a full spatial description of a RSO, multiple
views from potentially multiple viewers through time are required. The production of the “next best look” would be a benefit to the
effort of viewable conjunctions. Requesting the calculation of what two views of a RSO from two complementary aspects would give
a more comprehensive spatial understanding of the RSO. We propose that ORNL provides an additional capability to existing
sponsor conjunction assessment that would deliver over a period where multiple, complementary views of a particular RSO can be
assessed and reported so that tasking can be provided forward.
Spatial description of RSOs is valuable and additional material or thermal information about RSOs would add additional information
to more completely characterize the RSO. Many satellites that could be used for viewable conjunctions have spectral acquisition
capabilities outside of the visible range and into other electromagnetic regions. One could involve such metadata outside of the
visible region in tasking to allow for information such as material identification and thermal exchange information. Such information
could inform the viewer on answering questions such as: (1) what is the potential lifecycle of this RSO due to its rigid or
fragmentable material composition? (2) Is this RSO undergoing any thermal or electronic effects that may help to characterize its
use or functionality?
These two proposed investigations into enhanced detection could be investigated for inclusion into sponsor capability and
integrated into the Laika dashboard.

Conclusion
We recommend an investigation into two potential improvements to viewable conjunction assessments: high performance
computing implementation and enhanced detection. Justification for HPC implementation allows continuous discovery of satellite
observations of RSOs under multiple viewing, positional, and tasking priority conditions. Under current assessment computing
constraints, such discovery is limited.
