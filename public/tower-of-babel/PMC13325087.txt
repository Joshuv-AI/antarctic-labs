Quantifying Formation Permeability Effects on High-Temperature Aquifer Thermal Energy Storage
Authors: Zhenjie Sun, Xiaoguang Wang, Di Deng, Yanyong Wang, Yun Huang, Chuanyin Jiang, Yinhui Zuo, Wei Jiang, Lijun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-temperature aquifer thermal energy storage (HT-ATES) has emerged as a promising technology for mitigating fluctuations in renewable energy supply, with system performance being strongly controlled by formation permeability. In this study, a three-dimensional coupled thermo-hydro-mechanical (T-H-M) model was employed to investigate the influence of mean permeability, anisotropy, and heterogeneity on doublet HT-ATES performance in aquifers. The results indicate that higher mean permeability promotes lateral energy dispersion and reduces pumping energy demand but simultaneously decreases thermal recovery. As the permeability anisotropy of the storage aquifer increases, lateral dispersion of heat is enhanced, vertical focusing is suppressed, and thermal recovery efficiency is reduced. Heterogeneity and spatial continuity intensify preferential flow, with heterogeneity having a more pronounced negative impact on recovery efficiency. Regarding conventional sandstone formations, modest temperature changes do not bring about notable modifications to the formation’s porosity and permeability characteristics. Hot wells were found to be more sensitive than cold wells due to lower density and viscosity, as well as the enhanced convection of hot water. Overall, weak anisotropy and low heterogeneity were identified as optimal conditions for balancing recovery efficiency and pumping requirements, providing quantitative insights for formation selection and parameter optimization in HT-ATES system design.

Introduction

With the continuous expansion of renewable energy capacity-348 GW were added globally in 2022, marking the 21st consecutive year of growth and surpassing the combined addition of all other energy sources-power supply has become increasingly affected by meteorological and geographical variability, resulting in significant supply demand imbalances. Underground thermal energy storage (UTES), which enables the storage and release of heat on a seasonal time scale, has been recognized as an effective approach to improve the reliability of renewable-energy-integrated heating and energy-supply systems. − Among the various UTES technologies, aquifer thermal energy storage (ATES) has already been implemented at scale, with more than 3000 projects worldwide. , In particular, high-temperature ATES (HT-ATES; depth >500 m, temperature >50 °C) has gained prominence due to its higher storage temperature, extended retention time, and enhanced operational stability. ,

The primary constraint of HT-ATES operation is formation permeability. Higher permeability reduces viscous pressure drop and pumping demand but simultaneously enhances convective plume spreading and thermal breakthrough. Conversely, lower permeability mitigates thermal losses yet increases pressure differential and energy consumption. − Consequently, system design requires optimization within the trade-off between dispersion losses and pressure-related energy cost. However, the manner in which this optimal window varies with permeability statistics-specifically mean values, anisotropy, heterogeneity index-has not been systematically quantified.

Substantial progress has been made in the modeling and mechanistic understanding of HT-ATES. Prior studies have included three-dimensional thermo-hydraulic evaluations of doublet configurations, − assessments of heterogeneity on recovery and uncertainty, high-resolution site-scale modeling and interference analysis, two-dimensional characterization of temperature field, calibration methods for thermal interference distance, regional optimization frameworks integrating thermo-hydraulic models with machine learning, efficient simulation frameworks coupling grids and geological modeling, feasibility assessments of deep aquifer hot-water cycling, and T-H-M approaches for evaluating energy efficiency, ground deformation, and coupled processes.

Despite these advances, three critical gaps remain in the understanding of the permeability–system performance relationship. First, most investigations have relied on single permeability values or homogeneous assumptions, with limited quantification of the combined influence of anisotropy, horizontal correlation length, and heterogeneity (characterized by the Dykstra–Parsons coefficient V DP or variance of in k). , Second, verifiable criteria and design ready mappings along the causal chain linking permeability to energy dispersion, then to injection/production pressure, and finally to seasonal recovery efficiency were lacking. Third, the dynamic effects of permeability statistics across the complete operational cycle (heat injection-cold production, heat storage, heat recovery-cold injection, and cold storage) remain insufficiently characterized, thereby constraining parameter optimization and risk mitigation.

In this study, a traditional doublet HT-ATES (as illustrated in Figure ) was investigated through the development of a three-dimensional fully coupled T-H-M model, in which local thermal nonequilibrium (LTNE), stress-dependent permeability, poroelasticity, and thermo-poroelasticity are accounted. Under consistent operating conditions, the effects of four key permeability attributes mean, horizontal-to-vertical anisotropy, horizontal correlation length, and heterogeneity were systematically evaluated with respect to energy dispersion, pressure response, and seasonal recovery efficiency. By encompassing the complete operational cycle while ensuring engineering feasibility, quantitative mappings from permeability characteristics to key performance indicators were established, operable design windows were identified, and mitigation strategies involving well spacing, pumping–injection allocation, and phased operation were proposed. These findings provide theoretical and methodological support for formation selection and parameter optimization on the HT-ATES system design.

Model Validation and Setup

Initial and Boundary Conditions

A multiphysics coupled T-H-M model was developed based on the finite element method. Model accuracy in terms of geomechanical deformation, fluid flow, and heat transfer was validated in Appendix A, and mesh-independence verification was conducted in Appendix B.

The model domain was defined as 200 m × 200 m × 50 m (length × width × thickness), with an infinite element applied to a 50 m outer buffer to represent the far field and to minimize boundary-induced distortions in pressure and temperature. The formation was assumed to be buried at a depth of 1400 m, with an initial temperature of 50 °C, a geothermal gradient of 25 °C/km, an initial pore pressure of 13.6 MPa, a hydraulic gradient of 9.8 kPa/m, a vertical stress load of 31.5 MPa, and a horizontal stress of 25 MPa. The aquifer was considered fully saturated with formation water under initial conditions, and the injected hot or cold water was assumed to remain in a liquid state under the prevailing pressures.

A traditional doublet configuration was employed, where the hot well and cold well penetrated vertically through the 50 m-thick aquifer with a spacing of 141.4 m, this well spacing was determined with due regard to the practical operational requirements of thermal storage systems and the optimal selection of currently mature technological solutions. Seasonal cycling over a 10-year period was simulated and consisted of four 90-day phases: heat injection/cold production, heat storage, heat recovery/cold injection, and cold storage. A constant mass flow rate of 2.5 kg/s was imposed at both wells: During summer, 90 °C water was injected into the hot well and cold water was produced from the cold well, whereas during winter, 10 °C water was injected into the cold well and hot water was produced in the hot well. The top and bottom boundaries were set as impermeable and adiabatic.

The computational domain was discretized using triangular-prismatic elements, and the well boundaries were meshed with quadrilateral elements, as shown in Figure . A locally refined mesh was applied in the near-wellbore region to resolve flow and heat-transfer gradients.

Results

Effects on Energy Diffusion

Effect of Average Permeability

For mean-permeability formation cases, the direction and spatial extent of energy spreading were significantly controlled by the average permeability (Figure a). Within the formation, the heat/cold plume center was observed to migrate along a corridor with consistent direction (i.e., it moved within a fixed directional range rather than random deviation); at higher mean permeability, the migration range of this center during injection/production was enlarged (Figure b). In the horizontal directions, a high permeability of 200 mD increased the maximum dispersion range relative to the baseline by 21.3% and 19.4% for the hot well, and by 13.4% and 10.5% for the cold well, respectively (Figure c). This behavior is attributed to reduced flow resistance at higher permeability, which accelerates fluid-driven energy transport along principal horizontal pathways. In the vertical direction, a lower permeability of 50 mD increased the thermal-well dispersion range by 23.1%, as restricted horizontal flow forced energy to diffuse vertically into zones of lower resistance. Higher permeability increased the Darcy velocity, enabling rapid advection and expansion of the horizontal extent but also enhancing thermal losses to the surrounding formation, whereas lower permeability suppressed horizontal advection, promoted near-wellbore accumulation, and facilitated deeper vertical penetration.

Effect of Permeability Anisotropy

For mean-permeability formation cases, the directionality of energy spreading was significantly altered by permeability anisotropy (Figure a). With increasing anisotropy, the migration range of the heat/cold plume center during injection/production was enlarged (Figure b). Under strong anisotropy (e.g., k z/k x,y = 0.6), the horizontal dispersion of the hot well increased by 5.7% in the X direction relative to the baseline (i.e., k z/k x,y = 1), whereas the vertical dispersion decreased by 5.1%. For the cold well, the decreased in vertical dispersion was 5.4% (Figure c). These responses arise because reduced vertical permeability under enhanced anisotropy suppresses vertical flow and drives energy accumulation along the horizontal direction. At the same time, it enhances heat exchange with distant low-temperature formations, which constitutes a detrimental condition for improving energy recovery efficiency.

Effect of Horizontal Autocorrelation Length

The horizontal dimensionless autocorrelation length, which characterizes the spatial continuity of the permeability field, was used to assess its influence on energy spreading (Figure a). Larger λDh resulted in a wider migration envelope of the heat/cold plume center during injection/production (Figure b). Under strong continuity (e.g., λDh = 0.4), the vertical spread around the hot well increased by 56.0% relative to the homogeneous model (i.e., λDh = 0), whereas horizontal spread decreased by 11.4%–13.8% (Figure c). This behavior arises because large λDh renders the permeability distribution more segmented in the vertical direction and creates complex horizontal preferential channels, thereby enhancing the anisotropy of energy distribution.

Effect of Heterogeneity

Energy spreading was modulated by formation permeability-field heterogeneity (V DP) through changes in the complexity of flow pathways (Figure a). A larger migration envelope of the heat/cold plume center during injection/production was observed with increasing heterogeneity (Figure b). Under strong heterogeneity (e.g., V DP = 0.86), a 15.5% increase in the horizontal spread near the hot well (X direction) and a 26.5% increase in the vertical spread near the cold well (Z direction) were obtained (Figure c). These outcomes are attributed to the facilitated formation of pronounced preferential pathways as heterogeneity intensifies, along which the thermal front tends to propagate. When these pathways coincide with the natural front pattern in the homogeneous case, superposition strengthens the trend; when they are misaligned, an antagonistic effect reorients the front. Consequently, substantial variability in the final thermal-front configuration may arise across different heterogeneity realizations (i.e., random fields).

Effects on Injection/Production Pressure

Effect of Average Permeability

The injection/production pressure difference decreased markedly with increasing mean permeability, as shown in Figure . At a mean permeability of 200 mD, the average pressure differences of the thermal and cold wells reduced to 0.17 and 0.30 MPa, corresponding to decreases of 52.8% and 54.5% relative to the baseline value of 0.36 and 0.66 MPa, respectively, and were accompanied by a synchronous decline in energy consumption. In contrast, at a mean permeability of 50 mD, the pressure differences rise to 0.70 and 1.21 MPa, as lower permeability sharply elevated flow resistance. These trends are governed by Darcy’s law: higher permeability reduces viscous loss and thus pumping power requirements, whereas low permeability suppresses dispersion but elevates pressure differentials that may induce formation deformation. Consequently, a design trade-off is required between reduced pumping energy and increased thermal dispersion.

Changes in injection-production pressures of hot wells and cold wells under different average permeability conditions, (a) hot wells, (b) cold wells.

Effect of Permeability Anisotropy

Permeability anisotropy increased vertical flow resistance yet did not alter the injection/production pressure difference, as shown in Figure , and no significant variation in the pressure difference between hot and cold wells was observed under strong anisotropy (e.g., k z/k x,y = 0.6). This behavior occurs because the system’s injection-production flow rate was maintained constant, and the increased vertical flow resistance did not exceed the critical threshold affecting the overall injection/production pressure difference, thus failing to induce significant fluctuations in the pressure difference between hot and cold wells.

Effect of Horizontal Autocorrelation Length

The horizontal dimensionless autocorrelation length modulated injection/production pressure by altering flow-path continuity, as shown in Figure . Under weak continuity (e.g., λDh = 0.4), average pressure differences of the thermal and cold wells increased to 0.45 and 1.13 MPa, corresponding to increases of 25.0% and 71.2% relative to the homogeneous case (0.36 and 0.66 MPa). The difficulty of injection and production was attributed to pronounced vertical interlayer contrasts that impeded fluid motion; the resulting high hydraulic resistance and tortuous flow paths hindered operations.

Effect of Heterogeneity

Permeability heterogeneity modified local flow resistance through the extreme-value distribution of permeability; its pressure response was governed by the dominance of permeability extremes, in contrast to the continuous-path effect associated with the horizontal autocorrelation length, as shown in Figure . Under weak heterogeneity (e.g., V DP = 0.39), pressure differences for the thermal and cold wells decreased slightly relative to homogeneous caseto 0.33 and 0.53 MPa, respectivelybecause moderately dispersed high-permeability zones provided parallel conductance and mitigated local pressure concentrations. Under strong heterogeneity (e.g., V DP = 0.86), pressure differences increased to 0.58 and 0.81 MPa, as low-permeability barriers imposed bottlenecks that necessitated higher driving pressures.

Effects on Energy Recovery Efficiency

Effect of Average Permeability

Energy recovery efficiency decreased with increasing mean permeability, as shown in Figure . At 50 mD, thermal and cooling recovery efficiencies were 68.0% and 72.9%, exceeding the baseline values of 64.9% and 69.8% owing to a smaller energy dispersion envelope and reduced losses. At 200 mD, the thermal and cooling recovery efficiencies declined to 64.1% and 65.6%, respectively, falling below the baseline, because excessive lateral dispersion directed energy into nontarget zones. Low permeability improved efficiency by restricting dispersion and reducing losses, albeit at the expense of higher pumping energy. High permeability reduced energy consumption; however, the attendant dispersion offset the efficiency gains.

Heat and cold recovery efficiencies under different average permeability conditions, (a) heat recovery efficiency, (b) cold recovery efficiency.

Effect of Permeability Anisotropy

Energy recovery efficiency was affected by permeability anisotropy through regulation of dispersion directionality, as shown in Figure . In strongly anisotropic formations (e.g., k z/k x,y = 0.6), thermal and cooling recovery efficiencies were 60.9% and 67.8%, respectively, lower than the baseline values of 64.9% and 69.8%. Under weak anisotropy (e.g., k z/k x,y = 0.8), the attenuation of both efficiencies was mitigated. These phenomena are attributed to the reduction in vertical permeability under enhanced anisotropy, which suppresses excessive vertical dispersion and increases heat-exchange losses with distant formations in the horizontal direction. Additionally, the higher viscosity of the cold fluid increases interwell residence time, which reduces the attenuation of cooling recovery efficiency.

Heat and cold recovery efficiencies under different permeability anisotropy conditions, (a) heat recovery efficiency, (b) cold recovery efficiency.

Effect of Horizontal Autocorrelation Length

System recovery efficiency was modulated by the horizontal dimensionless autocorrelation length through its control on spatial continuity, as shown in Figure . Under strong continuity (e.g., λDh = 0.2), thermal and cooling recovery efficiencies reached 65.6% and 70.8%, slightly exceeding the baseline values of 64.9% and 69.8%. When continuity was weaker (e.g., λDh = 0.4), thermal recovery declined modestly to 63.5%, whereas cooling recovery continued to increase to 71.7%. These trends are attributed to short-range continuity of high-permeability zones at weaker λDh, which mitigates the excessive dispersion observed in the homogeneous case and yields slightly higher overall recovery. By contrast, stronger λDh fragments the permeability field and limit long-range spreading, making fluid dispersion to distant regions more difficult.

Effect of Heterogeneity

Permeability heterogeneity exerted an adverse influence on recovery efficiency by disrupting permeability uniformity, as shown in Figure . In strongly heterogeneous formations (e.g., V DP = 0.86), thermal and cooling recovery efficiencies declined to 57.9% and 64.3%, corresponding to reductions of 7.0% and 5.5% relative to the baseline (64.9% and 69.8%). Under weak heterogeneity (e.g., V DP = 0.39), efficiencies were slightly lower than the homogeneous case, with modest reductions of −1.9% (thermal) and −3.7% (cooling). These outcomes are attributed to highly uneven permeability fields: high-permeability channels expedite transport into nontarget zones, creating unrecoverable energy, whereas low-permeability zones impose strong resistance that traps energy and promotes local accumulation, jointly reducing overall recovery. Although weak heterogeneity can diversify flow paths and mitigate local concentration, the added pathway complexity still induces partial losses, yielding efficiencies marginally below those of homogeneous systems.

Discussion and Conclusions

In this study, geostatistical methods were employed to construct permeability fields, which were then coupled with three-dimensional T-H-M simulations to systematically evaluate the effects of formation permeability parameters, including mean permeability, anisotropy, horizontal autocorrelation length, and heterogeneity, on the performance of a traditional doublet HT-ATES system. The analysis elucidated the interactions among energy dispersion, injection/production pressure, and recovery efficiency under permeability-controlled conditions.

The present results are generally consistent with previous ATES/HT-ATES studies showing that aquifer heterogeneity can redirect thermal plumes and reduce recovery efficiency by generating preferential flow paths. , Compared with previous T-H or T-H-M studies that mainly relied on homogeneous aquifer assumptions − or focused on site-specific case analyses, , this study establishes a systematic mapping from permeability statistics to energy dispersion, pressure demand, and seasonal recovery efficiency under identical operating conditions. This mapping further highlights the nonlinear and directional nature of energy transport and its sensitivity to formation characteristics. Therefore, the permeability-based results presented here should be interpreted as screening-level guidance for formation selection and parameter optimization, complementing rather than replacing site-calibrated HT-ATES simulations. The principal conclusions are as follows:For conventional sandstone formations, the modest temperature contrast between the injected fluid and the formation did not induce appreciable changes in formation porosity, permeability, or flow pathways. Energy dispersion was not dominantly regulated by thermal stress; instead, the system response was primarily governed by the intrinsic thermo-mechanical properties of sandstone (e.g., low thermal expansion and high mechanical stability) together with inherent differences in fluid properties. In addition, hot wells exhibited substantially higher sensitivity to permeability variations than cold wells, which was attributed to stronger gravitational and viscous constraints associated with the higher density and viscosity of cold water.Permeability parameters synergistically modulated energy dispersion and injection/production pressure through distinct mechanisms. Dispersion regulated by permeability was characterized by directional and nonlinear behavior: increases in mean permeability expanded horizontal spreading and elevated the risk of energy loss; strong anisotropy enhanced horizontal spreading but impaired vertical energy accumulation; and weak spatial continuity or strong heterogeneity promoted preferential flow and reduced the uniformity of energy distribution. For conventional sandstone formations, the modest temperature contrast between the injected fluid and the formation did not induce appreciable changes in formation porosity, permeability, or flow pathways. Energy dispersion was not dominantly regulated by thermal stress; instead, the system response was primarily governed by the intrinsic thermo-mechanical properties of sandstone (e.g., low thermal expansion and high mechanical stability) together with inherent differences in fluid properties.
