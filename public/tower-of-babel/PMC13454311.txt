Production of aromatics from high density polyethylene over a hierarchical zeolite synthesised from steam-assisted crystallisation
Authors: Xinrui Zheng, Meng He, Longfei Lin, Tianrui Bi, Ziyu Cen, Danielle Crawshaw, Benjamin Moore, Pascal Manuel, Wanying Han, Jiapeng Jiao, Zhanghui Xia, Xuelei Mei, Yaqin Wang, Yani Liu, Jianling Zhang, Mufan Li, Mingyuan He, Sihai Yang, Haihong Wu, Buxing Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe conversion of high-density polyethylene wastes to aromatic feedstocks in noble-metal-free systems poses a significant challenge. Here, we report a steam-assisted synthesised zeolite (SA-HZSM-5) that can promote the conversion of high-density polyethylene to aromatics, achieving simultaneously high conversion and selectivity (85.4% and 49.2%, respectively) at 260 °C, outperforming noble metal-based catalysts, such as Ru-HZSM-5, under the same conditions. The produced aromatics predominantly consist of valuable monocyclic aromatics (circa 85%). The steam-assisted synthetic method endows a specific distribution of Lewis and Brønsted acid sites in SA-HZSM-5. In situ neutron diffraction and infrared spectroscopy, coupled with modelling, reveal a dual-site adsorption mechanism of mono-olefins intermediates, facilitating their transformation into dienes with suitable spaced double bonds, a feature that is crucial for the sequential cyclisation and dehydroaromatisation into aromatic compounds. This study underscores the potential of hierarchical zeolite materials for efficient upcycling of plastic wastes into chemical feedstocks.Subject terms: Sustainability, Heterogeneous catalysis, Catalyst synthesis

Introduction

Plastic wastes currently present significant environmental challenges due to their resistance to degradation1–4. As of 2015, the global production of plastic waste amounted to approximately 6.3 billion tonnes, with only 9% recycled, 12% incinerated, and 79% accumulated in landfills or dispersed into the marine system, causing catastrophic impacts on the environment and ecosystems5–9. Thus, the development of a new method to eliminate plastic wastes via their conversion to feedstocks is an urgent task10. Waste polyolefins, which constitute over 50% of plastic wastes, are extremely stable materials11,12. Various strategies have been developed for the conversion of polyolefins into valuable chemicals, such as liquid fuels13–18, olefins19–21, surfactants22, aldehydes and alcohols23. Recently, the catalytic conversion of polyolefins to value-added aromatic compounds has garnered considerable interests24–35.

Zeolites were initially employed to facilitate polyolefin degradation at high temperatures (>400 °C)25–27. For example, a composite catalyst of CaO and Ga/ZSM-5 converted low-density polyethylene (LDPE) into aromatic hydrocarbons with a high yield of 48% at 500 °C25. Subsequent strategies introduced noble-metal catalysts to enable polyethylene (PE) conversion under milder conditions (<300 °C)29–31. Notably, Pt/Al2O3 catalysed the transformation of PE into surfactant-like alkylaromatics at 280 °C29,30, while Ru/ZSM-5 facilitated the degradation of PE into aromatic hydrocarbons at the same temperature, achieving a yield of 33% (Fig. 1a)31. More recently, non-noble-metal catalysts have demonstrated promising results in polyolefin aromatization under mild conditions32–35. For instance, commercial ZSM-5 promoted polyethylene degradation to light aromatics at 280 °C, yielding 50.6% aromatics32. However, its high feed ratio limits scalability for industrial applications. Additionally, CO2-assisted aromatization has been reported to enhance aromatic hydrocarbon production from PE34. Despite these advancements, the mechanistic understanding of non-noble metal-catalysed polyethylene conversion to aromatics under mild conditions remains unclear. There is an urgent need to elucidate the microscopic reaction mechanisms and develop more efficient and practical catalysts surpassing the performance of commercially available ZSM-5.

Here, we report the efficient conversion of high-density polyethylene (HDPE) into aromatic compounds with a high selectivity of 49.2% on conversion of 85.4% at 260 °C over a noble-metal-free zeolite (SA-HZSM-5) prepared via a steam-assisted synthetic method (Fig. 1b). The selectivity of aromatics is limited to ca. 50% because the hydrogen species generated during aromatisation are transferred readily to alkenes, resulting in the formation of alkanes23,34,36,37. Significantly, this approach has reached the theoretical limit for production of aromatics over SA-HZSM-5. The steam-assisted synthetic method furnishes SA-HZSM-5 with a specific distribution of Lewis and Brønsted acid sites. Isotope-labelling experiment, neutron powder diffraction (NPD), density functional theory (DFT) modelling and in situ infrared spectroscopy have elucidated the reaction mechanism. Mono-olefins intermediates generated from HDPE cracking adsorb on Brønsted acid sites through C = C bonds and interact with Lewis acid sites via C-H bonds, facilitating their dehydrogenation to form dienes, which possess suitable spaced double bonds owing to the specific spatial arrangement of acid sites on SA-HZSM-5. These dienes can readily undergo cyclisation and dehydroaromatisation to yield aromatics that are comprised of 85% high-value monocyclic compounds. Notably, noble metals such as ruthenium can activate the C-H bonds of PE and intermediate hydrocarbons to abstract hydrogen species. This process generates unsaturated carbon-carbon bonds, which facilitates diene formation and subsequent aromatization into final aromatic products31. Conversely, ruthenium also exhibits hydrogenation activity and favours alkane formation. Our noble-metal-free system leverages Lewis acid sites derived from cost-effective aluminum, which effectively replicate the catalytic role of ruthenium and surpass its performance (aromatic yield of 42.0% at 260 °C on SA-HZSM-5 compared with 32.7% at 280 °C on Ru/HZSM-5; see Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, SA-HZSM-5 demonstrates excellent catalytic stability and maintains high performance across HDPE samples with varying molecular weights, highlighting its industrial potential. Additionally, its distinct distribution of active sites offers valuable insights and methodological guidance for the development of next-generation aromatization catalysts.

Results

Synthesis and characterisation

SA-HZSM-5 with a Si/Al ratio of 30 was synthesised through a facile one-step steam-assisted synthetic method38. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Supplementary Fig. 1) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Fig. 2a) of SA-HZSM-5 revealed clusters of nanocrystals featuring intracrystalline micropores and intergranular mesopores. The hexahedral shape of HZSM-5 aligned with previous reports39. Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Fig. 2b) confirmed the MFI structure and phase purity. The nitrogen adsorption/desorption isotherm of SA-HZSM-5 (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 2) shows a Type I curve at P/P0 < 0.02. This characteristic confirms the presence of micropores. Furthermore, the hysteresis loop in the P/P0 range of 0.8 to 1.0 exhibits Type IV behaviour. This observation demonstrates the existence of mesopores as well40. In contrast, the isotherm of HZSM-5 (this and below all refer to commercial sample) lacked apparent hysteresis owing to the absence of mesopores. SA-HZSM-5 displayed a higher Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area and pore volume compared with HZSM-5 (Supplementary Table 2), which is critical to enhance the mass transfer of intermediates. Solid-state 29Si NMR spectrum of SA-HZSM-5 shows increased quantities of Q3 [Si(OSi)3(OH)] (-100 ppm) and Q2 [Si(OSi)2(OH)2] (-93 ppm) species, indicating the presence of silanol defects41 (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Table 3). The 27Al NMR spectrum (Fig. 2e) identified both tetra-coordinated aluminum at 55 ppm and a distinct six-coordinated aluminum species at 0 ppm, the latter arising from the framework of extra-framework aluminum species formed during steam-assisted crystallization42. The infrared spectra of adsorbed pyridine (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 3) highlighted differences in acidity between these catalysts. Specifically, SA-HZSM-5 displays a distinct absorption band at 1450 cm-1. While this signal attenuates as desorption temperatures rise, it consistently exceeds the equivalent intensity from the HZSM-5. These results demonstrate a higher density and superior strength of Lewis acid sites within the SA-HZSM-5. Despite similar total acid contents (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 4), acid sites in SA-HZSM-5 are more accessible to bulky molecules, as confirmed by 2,6-di-tert-butylpyridine infrared spectroscopy (DTBPy-IR) (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Discussion 1).

Catalytic performance

In a typical reaction, HDPE was mixed with the catalyst at a 4:1 mass ratio. This mixture was placed into an autoclave, purged with N2, and heated to 260 °C for 24 hours. The reaction was halted by cooling the autoclave to ambient temperature, and products were collected for analysis. Leading catalysts, such as HZSM-5, Ru/HZSM-5, Zn/HZSM-5 and Ga/HZSM-5, revealed low activity for aromatisation (yields of 3.7-15.4%, Fig. 3a,c and Supplementary Table 5, entries 1–9) and SA-HZSM-5 demonstrated a superior yield of aromatic compounds (42%; Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table 5, entry 10). The catalytic performance of different metals supported on SA-HZSM-5 was investigated (Supplementary Table 5, entries 11-13), and their aromatization activities were lower than that of SA-HZSM-5. We also prepared a control catalyst (PS-HZSM-5) via post-synthetic steam treatment of HZSM-5. Despite superior conversion and aromatic yields relative to the parent HZSM-5, PS-HZSM-5 proved catalytically inferior to the SA-HZSM-5 due to their acidic properties (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table 6). Notably, the reaction time can be reduced to 1–5 hours without compromising the catalytic performance of SA-HZSM-5 by adjusting the temperature and/or the catalyst-to-polymer ratio (Supplementary Table 5, entries 14-18). Under these conditions, SA-HZSM-5 still outperforms HZSM-5 (Supplementary Table 5, entry 19). Moreover, monoaromatics accounted for 85% of the produced aromatics on SA-HZSM-5, with polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) comprising only 15%. In contrast, HZSM-5 exhibited an aromatic selectivity of just 19.9%, with PAHs representing 41.2%. These results indicate that SA-HZSM-5 favours monoaromatic production over PAHs compared with HZSM-5 (Supplementary Tables 7 and 8, Supplementary Fig. 7). This advantage is attributed to the abundant mesopores in SA-HZSM-5, which facilitate the diffusion of monoaromatics, preventing their overreaction on active sites. Additionally, a scale-up experiment—using a fourfold increase in feedstock and catalyst loading at 260 °C for 24 hours—demonstrated comparable catalytic performance (HDPE conversion: 80.7%, aromatic selectivity: 48.9%, Supplementary Table 5, entry 20).

Time-resolved analysis of the liquid-phase products on SA-HZSM-5 revealed that, during the initial stage (0–5 h), the products were predominantly composed of C>9 monoaromatics along with C6–C12 alkanes and alkenes. As the reaction progressed (5–20 h), the yields of alkanes and alkenes gradually decreased, while the production of C6–C9 aromatics (benzene, toluene, xylene, and trimethylbenzene) increased significantly. The aromatic selectivity reached a plateau after 20 h and attained its maximum yield at 24 h (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 8). In contrast, on HZSM-5, C>9 aromatics were initially formed but increased only slightly over time, with minimal production of C6–C9 aromatics (Supplementary Fig. 9). This distinct distribution of aromatic products indicates a fundamental difference in reaction mechanisms between SA-HZSM-5 and HZSM-5. Moreover, elemental analysis of gas, liquid products and solid residues demonstrated an H/C ratio of approximately 2 (Fig. 3e). Importantly, SA-HZSM-5 shows retained yields of aromatics over 5 cycles, with the catalyst calcined before each run (Fig. 3f). The used catalyst shows little change to the structure and acidic sites, demonstrating the excellent stability of SA-HZSM-5 (Fig. 3g,h and Supplementary Fig. 10). We also assessed the catalyst performance without regeneration. After four cycles, both HDPE conversion and aromatic selectivity declined (Supplementary Fig. 11), likely due to active-site blockage by polymeric residues and coke deposits, which inhibited the interaction between fresh HDPE and the catalytic centers. Calcination of the used catalyst fully restored its activity (Supplementary Fig. 11). Thermogravimetric analysis of the solid residues revealed that coke deposited on SA-HZSM-5 primarily comprises heavy coke, indicated by weight loss above 400 °C, and a minor fraction of light coke exhibiting weight loss below 400 °C43–45 (Supplementary Fig. 12). The lower coke yield observed with SA-HZSM-5, combined with its higher aromatic yield compared with HZSM-5, highlights its better resistance to coking (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). We further examined the effect of polydispersity index (PDI) and weight-average molecular weight (Mw) of PE on catalytic performance (Supplementary Fig. 14, Supplementary Table 9). For HDPE samples with similar PDI values but varying Mw, catalytic tests revealed that both conversion and aromatic selectivity remained consistent below 200 kDa, but declined progressively when Mw exceeded this threshold (Supplementary Fig. 14b, Supplementary Table 9; PE-1 to PE-5). For samples with comparable Mw but differing PDI, catalytic performance remained unaffected (Supplementary Fig. 14c, and Supplementary Table 9; PE-2 and PE-6). These results indicate that SA-HZSM-5 exhibits broad tolerance toward different types of HDPE. Furthermore, catalytic tests using commercially recycled HDPE and polypropylene achieved over 80% conversion and approximately 50% aromatics selectivity (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 15, Supplementary Table 9), highlighting the practical applicability of SA-HZSM-5 for plastic waste valorization.

Reactivity of hydrocarbons on SA-HZSM-5 and HZSM-5

To elucidate the superior catalytic activity of SA-HZSM-5, we assessed the reactivities of a series of hydrocarbons on both SA-HZSM-5 and HZSM-5 catalysts. HDPE undergoes initial C-H bond activation and β-scission upon adsorption onto the solid acid catalyst to generate long-chain alkenes. Therefore, we selected 1-cetene as a model compound for this study (Fig. 4a-d). The reaction of 1-cetene on SA-HZSM-5 yielded aromatics with a selectivity of 20.6%, alongside dienes and cycloolefins at 5 hours (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 16a). As the reaction proceeded, the proportions of dienes and cycloolefins diminished, whereas those of aromatics increased to 50%. In contrast, reactions on HZSM-5 resulted in a significant production of mono-olefins and smaller amounts of dienes, cycloolefins, and aromatics (6.4%) at 5 h (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 16b). Over time, the selectivity for mono-olefins decreased, with a gradual increase in the production of dienes, cycloolefins and aromatics (18.9%). Thus, a reaction pathway is proposed for the conversion of alkenes to aromatics (Fig. 4c). The mono-olefin was protonated on Brønsted acid sites, forming a carbenium, which then underwent β-scission and dehydrogenation to form dienes, which subsequently cyclised into cycloolefins, followed by further dehydroaromatisation to yield aromatics.

We hypothesized that SA-HZSM-5 exhibits enhanced activity for the dehydrogenation of mono-olefins to dienes compared with HZSM-5. This was validated using 1-octene and 1,7-octadiene as model compounds (Fig. 4c), both of which were detected as reaction intermediates during the conversion of HDPE over SA-HZSM-5 (Supplementary Fig. 8). For the reaction of 1-octene, the selectivity of aromatics on HZSM-5 is significantly lower than that of SA-HZSM-5 (19.9%, 50.4%, respectively; Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 17), while for 1,7-octadiene, the selectivity of aromatics on HZSM-5 is improved (37.6%, 47.6%, respectively; Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 18). These results demonstrate the superior activity of SA-HZSM-5. The data also confirm that dienes exhibit higher reactivity than mono-olefins. Therefore, dienes constitute the critical intermediates for the cyclization and aromatization. Moreover, the conversion of 1-octene on HZSM-5 produced C>9 aromatics rather than C6–C9 aromatics (Supplementary Fig. 17b), indicating that HZSM-5 lacks the ability to directly dehydrogenate short-chain mono-olefins into dienes. Instead, short-chain mono-olefins likely undergo dimerization to form long-chain alkenes, which subsequently collapse to generate dienes, facilitating further aromatization. Additionally, we examined the dehydroaromatisation of cycloolefins to aromatics using 4-methylcyclohexene as a model compound, where SA-HZSM-5 demonstrated a higher selectivity of aromatics than HZSM-5 (51.9%, 34.7%, respectively, Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 19). The superior catalytic performance probably arises from the high abundance of Lewis acid sites and the cooperative effect between Brønsted and Lewis acid sites in SA-HZSM-5. These features facilitate dehydrogenation and the subsequent aromatization of hydrocarbon intermediates. Overall, SA-HZSM-5 exhibited enhanced catalytic activity compared with HZSM-5 in both the dehydrogenation and dehydroaromatisation (Fig. 4c), and thus facilitated the formation of aromatics.

Identification of active sites

By adjusting the synthesis temperature, two distinct SA-HZSM-5 catalysts (SA-HZSM-5-160 and SA-HZSM-5-200) were prepared, and their porosity and acidity properties are provided in Supplementary Tables 10,11 and Supplementary Fig. 20. Despite the differences in porosity and acid content, their Lewis-to-Brønsted (L/B) acid ratios and catalytic performance remained essentially unchanged (Supplementary Table 11, Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21), indicating that aromatic selectivity is primarily governed by the nature of the acid sites. To further probe the catalytic activity of SA-HZSM-5, we investigated the structures of active sites by 31P NMR using trimethylphosphine oxide (TMPO) as a probe molecule (Fig. 5a,b). SA-HZSM-5 featured abundant framework tricoordination Al (FTAl) sites, which acted as strong Lewis acid sites and were coordinated with basic TMPO molecules [64/65 ppm, TMPO∙∙∙Al(OSi)3; 61 ppm, TMPO∙∙∙Al(OSi)2(OH); 67 ppm, TMPO∙∙∙Al(OSi)(OH)2]46 (Fig. 5a,b, Supplementary Table 12). Conversely, TMPO adsorbed on HZSM-5 predominantly formed TMPOH+ ions, as indicated by a strong signal at 76 ppm, attributed to protonation by Brønsted acid sites, while signals of coordinated molecules to Lewis acid sites (63–69 ppm) were comparatively weak. This observation is consistent with Py-IR results, which showed a higher L/B acid ratio (derived from the peak area ratio at 1450 and 1540 cm−1) in SA-HZSM-5 than HZSM-5 (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Table 4). Additionally, SA-HZSM-5 possessed extra-framework Al3+ sites (EFAL), also acting as Lewis acids and coordinating with basic TMPO molecules [47 ppm, TMPO∙∙∙Al3+]47, consistent with 27Al NMR results (Fig. 2e). Notably, the signal from the FTAl site in SA-HZSM-5 splits into two peaks at 64 and 65 ppm (Fig. 5a), which was originated from the different location in the mesopores and micropores of the zeolite48. These results demonstrate the diversity and abundance of Lewis acidic sites in SA-HZSM-5, which played a key role in its excellent catalytic performance.

Both dehydrogenation and dehydroaromatization involve the activation of C-H bonds. The hydride transfer reaction between 2-methylpentane (i-C5H12) and deuterated hexane (C6D14) was investigated at 260 °C to compare the activity of SA-HZSM-5 and HZSM-5 for the activation of C-H bonds. On SA-HZSM-5, i-C5H12-xDx (x = 1–8) and C6D14-yHy (y = 1–4) were observed, whereas HZSM-5 exhibited negligible activity (Fig. 5c–e, Supplementary Figs. 22–24 and Supplementary Discussion 2). The pronounced activity of SA-HZSM-5 in C-H bond activation is attributed to its extensive Lewis acid sites, which facilitate both dehydrogenation and dehydroaromatization processes16.

Controlled experiments on SA-HZSM-5 with partial removal of Al sites by ammonia hexafluorosilicate (AHFS) were conducted to further study the role of Lewis acid sites. SA-HZSM-5 was dealuminated to varying extents by adjusting the AHFS-to-Al ratio (AHFS/Al = 1.0, 0.5, 0.375, 0.25) (Supplementary Table 13), and the structural morphology of SA-HZSM-5 was preserved (Supplementary Figs. 25 and 26). The 31P NMR spectrum of TMPO adsorbed on dealuminated SA-HZSM-5 revealed a progressive decrease in FTAl and EFAL sites—corresponding to Lewis acids—with increasing dealumination, accompanied by a minor reduction in Brønsted acid sites (Fig. 5f, Supplementary Fig. 27). These findings are consistent with the results of Py-IR, NH3-TPD, and 27Al NMR spectroscopy, as evidenced by the decrease in the weakened IR peak intensity of Lewis acid sites at 1450 cm−1 (Fig. 5g, Supplementary Figs. 28–30 and Supplementary Table 13). The influence of Lewis acid sites on catalytic performance was studied at a fixed amount of total acid (72 μmol) by adjusting the mass of catalysts based on the Py-IR and NH3-TPD results (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Fig. 31). First, the reaction of 1-cetene over SA-HZSM-5 with varying Lewis acid content was examined, and the diene content in the products was analyzed after a 5-hour reaction. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 31, diene selectivity declines with decreasing Lewis acid content, underscoring the critical role of Lewis acid sites in diene formation. Moreover, we investigated the influence of Lewis acid on the aromatization of HDPE. As the proportion of Lewis acid decreased, the conversion of HDPE remained very high, but the selectivity of aromatics gradually decreased (from 49.2% to 27.4%; Fig. 5h). In contrast, maintaining a fixed amount of Lewis acid sites (4.5 μmol) while reducing the total amount of acid sites resulted in a gradual decrease in HDPE conversion (from 54.3% to 39.1%), but the selectivity of aromatics was unaffected (Fig. 5i). These observations demonstrate that the total amount of acid sites (both Brønsted and Lewis acid sites) influenced HDPE conversion, while the presence of Lewis acid sites is crucial for the formation of aromatics. To distinguish the contribution of Lewis acid sites generated during the steam-assisted crystallization from those potentially introduced by high-temperature calcination (650 °C), we evaluated the catalytic performance of SA-HZSM-5-550, which was prepared with a reduced calcination temperature of 550 °C. Pyridine-FTIR and 27Al NMR analyses confirmed that calcination at 550 °C effectively avoids the formation of additional Lewis acid sites induced by thermal treatment (Supplementary Fig. 32, Supplementary Table 14). Catalytic tests revealed that SA-HZSM-5-550 exhibited activity comparable to that of the SA-HZSM-5 (calcinated at 650 °C) (Supplementary Fig. 32), demonstrating that the active sites responsible for the enhanced aromatization performance originate from the in situ steam-assisted crystallization process rather than from post-synthetic calcination. Finally, we removed Brønsted acid sites from SA-HZSM-5 by ion exchange with a sodium chloride solution, while retaining the Lewis acid sites (Supplementary Fig. 33), and performed catalytic tests using 1-octene as the model reactant. The products consisted predominantly of alkenes and alkanes, with only a minor fraction (<10%) of C10+ aromatic hydrocarbons (Supplementary Fig. 33). These results clearly show that Lewis acid sites alone are insufficient to catalyse the aromatization of alkenes. Rather, the cooperation between Brønsted and Lewis acid sites is essential for the reaction to proceed.

Study of reaction mechanism

NPD was applied to investigate the adsorption of deuteropyridine (d-PY) in SA-HZSM-5, aiming to identify the locations of Brønsted and Lewis acid sites. The datasets confirmed the retention of framework structure and enabled the direct visualisation of two distinct binding sites for d-PY, labelled as d-PYI and d-PYII within the pores (Fig. 6a,b, Supplementary Figs. 34–36, Supplementary Tables 15–17 and Supplementary Discussion 3). d-PYI and d-PYII were observed near the intersection of straight and sinusoidal channels, respectively (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Fig. 36a). The adsorbed d-PYI in SA-HZSM-5 interacted with a bridging O(H) centre through its intra-ring N centre (N···O = 2.999 Å) to form pyridinium ion (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Fig. 36b, Supplementary Discussion 4), suggesting Al-O1(H)-Si configuration as the Brønsted acid site. The intra-ring N centre of d-PYII directly bound to framework T9 site (N···T = 2.674 Å), thereby identifying T9 as the Lewis acid site (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Fig. 36b, Supplementary Discussion 4). It is worth noting that this method does not allow direct observation of the precise location of acid sites. Moreover, acid sites are not necessarily arranged in a periodic manner, and the probability of a specific site appearing within a unit cell is governed by its fractional site occupancy factor (S.O.F.) (Supplementary Table 16).

We further constructed a unit cell containing Lewis and Brønsted acid sites consistent with those identified by NPD experiments and introduced a 1-octene molecule (a1, Supplementary Fig. 37) for DFT calculations to investigate its adsorption dynamics on SA-HZSM-5 (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Fig. 36c). Regardless of the initial orientation, the calculated adsorption configuration shows that 1-octene undergoes protonation at a Brønsted acid site, forming an electrostatic interaction with the bridging oxygen (C···O = 1.76 Å). Simultaneously, the C–H bond of the adsorbed 1-octene is positioned near the T9 site (H···T = 3.55 Å) (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Fig. 36c). To further understand this interaction, we performed differential charge density analysis to examine charge redistribution around the C–H bond of 1-octene adjacent to the T9 site during adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 38). The analysis reveals significant electronic redistribution: electron density accumulates around the hydrogen atom, while the adjacent carbon atom shows notable depletion (Supplementary Fig. 38). These results suggest that the Lewis acid site polarizes the C–H bond, enhancing electron density near the hydrogen atom. This polarization enables van der Waals interactions between the C–H bond and the Lewis acid site, thereby facilitating subsequent dehydrogenation. Moreover, adsorption calculations for various spatial conformers of 1-octene (a2–a6, Supplementary Fig. 37, Supplementary Table 18) consistently revealed that the C–H moiety remains located six carbon atoms away from the protonation center upon adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 39). This spatial constraint favours the formation of dienes with appropriate geometry, thereby promoting subsequent cyclization into six-membered ring structures49. These demonstrate that the Lewis acid sites in conjunction with the suitably spaced Brønsted acid sites endows SA-HZSM-5 with superior aromatisation activity. In addition, we established a comprehensive free energy profile for the aromatization of 1-octene over the SA-HZSM-5 catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 40). The results demonstrate that the dehydrogenation of 1-octene to a diene intermediate represents the rate-limit step in the catalytic cycle. Subsequent cyclization and further dehydrogenation steps exhibit substantially lower energy barriers, which is consistent with our experimental observations. Differential charge density and Bader charge analyses were performed to quantify the electron transfer during the rate-determining step (Supplementary Fig. 41). The results reveal that octene loses 1.58 e during the dehydrogenation process to form olefinic carbenium ions, and these electrons are transferred to the zeolite framework. This significant electron transfer from the adsorbed hydrocarbon to the catalyst provides direct evidence of a strong electronic interaction. Consequently, these findings substantiate the crucial role of SA-HZSM-5 in facilitating the rate-determining step and underscore its high catalytic efficiency in promoting the aromatization pathway.

We further conducted in situ IR analysis to study the cracking of HDPE over SA-HZSM-5 and HZSM-5 (Fig. 6d, Supplementary Fig. 42). Upon reaction, we observed a decrease in the intensities of methylene group bands at 2929 and 2858 cm−1, alongside an increased presence of methyl groups at 2965 cm−1 on both SA-HZSM-5 and HZSM-5. This suggests that both zeolites effectively cleave HDPE chains into lighter hydrocarbons. However, the sp2-hybridized C–H stretching vibrations of mono-olefins or dienes50,51 (around 3080 cm−1, Supplementary Fig. 43a,b) were not observed in Fig. 6d, likely due to their rapid protonation or transformation at the studied conditions (catalyst/HDPE ratio = 4/1). In contrast, the stretching modes of aromatic ring (1605 cm−1) and the sp2-hybridized C–H stretching vibrations of aromatics (3020 cm-1)52,53 were progressively enhanced on SA-HZSM-5 (Fig. 6d, Supplementary Fig. 43c,d), but were negligible on HZSM-5 (Supplementary Fig. 42). This demonstrated the superior aromatisation activity of SA-HZSM-5.

Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) analysis was performed to measure the molecular weight of polymers at different reaction times. For conventional microporous zeolites such as HZSM-5, the molecular weight of HDPE remains largely unchanged over extended reaction times (Supplementary Fig. 44). This result indicates that chain scission occurs primarily at the chain ends, although some scission within the polymer backbone may also occurs through the process such as partial melting and cracking at external acid sites. These HDPE molecules crack into short-chain alkenes until fully consumed, while those that do not interact with acid sites remain intact. The absence of Brønsted–Lewis acid site pairs with appropriate spatial constraints in HZSM-5 restricts the direct conversion of short-chain mono-olefins into dienes, thereby limiting aromatic production. In contrast, for SA-HZSM-5, the polymer molecular weight decreased rapidly (Supplementary Fig. 44), suggesting that HDPE molecules could access both internal and external acid sites. This enhanced accessibility facilitates cleavage at both chain ends and within the polymer backbone, producing a mixture of short- and long-chain alkenes. The mono-olefins readily convert to dienes, which undergo further aromatization, as demonstrated by 1-octene and 1-cetene model reactions (Fig. 4a, e).

The reaction mechanism of HDPE cracking and aromatisation on SA-HZSM-5 was proposed (Fig. 6e). Upon adsorption onto SA-HZSM-5, HDPE is activated either via hydride abstraction on Lewis acid sites16,54 or protonation on Brønsted acid sites55, generating carbenium or carbonium ions as the initiation step. The carbonium ions formed on Brønsted acid sites are unstable and rapidly collapse, yielding carbenium ions along with H2 or alkanes. These carbenium ions undergo deprotonation, releasing unsaturated polymers that further convert into long- and short-chain alkenes via β-scission. Short-chain mono-olefins, adsorbed on Brønsted acid sites, interact with Lewis acid sites to undergo hydride abstraction, leading to the formation of olefinic carbenium ions, as confirmed by NPD experiments and DFT calculations. These olefinic carbenium ions are subsequently converted into C6-C9 aromatics (one to three substituents) through cyclization and dehydroaromatization. Meanwhile, long-chain mono-olefins undergo either hydride abstraction by Lewis acid sites or collapse on Brønsted acid sites to form olefinic carbenium ions, which undergo cyclization and dehydroaromatization, accompanied by C–C bond cleavage, producing C6-C9 and C>9 aromatics (Fig. 6e, Supplementary Fig. 7). It is worth noting that C>9 aromatic hydrocarbons have 2–5 substituents (Supplementary Fig. 45), which is attributed to the isomerization of alkenes (Supplementary Fig. 46).

In contrast, HZSM-5 predominantly facilitates end scission of HDPE, generating short-chain alkenes as the primary products. However, due to the lack of Brønsted–Lewis acid site pairs with suitable spatial constraints, HZSM-5 is less effective at forming olefinic carbenium ions via hydride abstraction32,56–59. As a result, short-chain mono-olefins undergo dimerization, followed by collapse on Brønsted acid sites32,55 to form olefinic carbenium ions, which then transform into C>9 aromatics (Supplementary Fig. 47).

SA-HZSM-5 possesses abundant Lewis acid sites and Brønsted–Lewis acid site pairs with suitable spatial constraints, enabling a distinct reaction mechanism that enhances the formation of olefinic carbenium ions, a key intermediate for aromatic production. This unique catalytic behaviour significantly improves aromatic selectivity compared with HZSM-5. Although SA-HZSM-5 exhibits higher selectivity to aromatic compounds compared with HZSM-5, reactions on both catalysts maintain a balance of hydrogen and carbon (Supplementary Table 7). The hydrogen produced during dehydrogenation was transferred to short-chain alkenes to form alkanes.

Discussion

The conversion of HDPE into value-added chemicals in noble-metal-free systems is a promising strategy for eliminating plastic wastes. This work demonstrates that SA-HZSM-5, prepared from a steam-assisted method, can efficiently catalyse the conversion of HDPE into aromatic compounds under mild conditions and systematically demonstrates the reasons why its catalytic activity surpasses that of noble metal catalysts. The conversion and selectivity reached 85.4% and 49.2%, respectively, at 260 °C, outperforming state-of-the-art catalysts31–35 operating under our reaction conditions (Supplementary Table 5). Notably, the product predominantly comprised valuable monocyclic compounds, accounting for 85% of the total yield. The pivotal step in this system involves the dehydrogenation of mono-olefins into dienes, which must be properly spaced between the double bonds for effective cyclisation into six-membered ring structures. NPD and DFT calculations demonstrate that SA-HZSM-5 features Brønsted and Lewis acid sites with suitable distances, which enables the efficient transformation of mono-olefins to dienes with C=C bonds separated by six carbons, significantly enhancing the aromatisation. This approach synergistically integrates Lewis acid sites with Brønsted acid sites, establishing a noble-metal-free catalytic system for sustainable upcycling of HDPE waste into high-value feedstocks for fine chemicals and advanced materials. The techno-economic analysis, which includes catalyst regeneration, estimates an operating cost of $770 per metric ton (Mt) of aromatic products and a profit margin of $412 per Mt, demonstrating potential for industrial application (Supplementary Discussion 5; Supplementary Tables 19–21). However, it should be noted that this TEA is based on laboratory-scale catalytic data and does not consider mass or heat transfer limitations at industrial scale. In addition, the economic outcome is sensitive to variations in external conditions, operational costs, and feedstock prices.

Methods

Synthesis of SA-HZSM-5

SA-HZSM-5 zeolite was prepared by steam-assisted crystallization. In a typical experiment, 0.336 g aluminum isopropoxide (AIP, AR) was added to 144 g deionized water, stirred at room temperature. Then, 12 g tetrapropyl ammonium hydroxide (TPAOH, 25%) and 10.4 g tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, 98%) were added slowly. The molar ratio of the above raw materials was: 1SiO2: 0.0165 Al2O3: 0.295TPAOH: 0.055Na2O: 160H2O. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 12 hours and dried in an oven at 70 °C for 24 hours to obtain a dry gel, which was then placed into a 100 mL Teflon-lined autoclave equipped with a mesh separator in the middle. Deionized water (5 g) was added to the bottom of the Teflon liner, followed by crystallization at 180 °C for 30 h, maintaining the vapour pressure inside the autoclave at 1.0 MPa, and the obtained sample was washed three times in deionized water, dried at 120 °C for 12 h, calcined at 650 °C for 5 h, and recorded as SA-HZSM-5. The catalysts prepared at 160 °C and 200 °C were denoted as SA-HZSM-5-160 and SA-HZSM-5-200, with corresponding vapour pressures of 0.6 and 1.6 MPa, respectively. The catalyst calcined at 550 °C was denoted as SA-HZSM-5-550.

The HZSM-5 zeolite (SiO2/Al2O3 = 25) was calcined in static air at 550 °C for 6 h. Subsequently, 0.5 g of the calcined HZSM-5 was placed in a tube furnace and heated to 450 °C at a ramp rate of 5 °C/min, followed by steam treatment for 6 h. After cooling to room temperature, the obtained sample was denoted as PS-HZSM-5.

Catalytic conversion of polyolefins

The catalytic reaction was carried out in a 20 mL Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave. In a typical reaction, the polyolefin is mixed with the catalyst by grinding for 5 minutes and then loaded into an autoclave. The autoclave was purged three times with nitrogen, and the pressure was adjusted to the ambient pressure. The reactor was sealed and kept at 260 °C for 24 h. The reactor was then cooled to room temperature. For scale-up experiments, we used a larger autoclave equipped with mechanical stirring to ensure adequate mixing and temperature homogeneity. The gaseous products were collected using a gas sampling bag. Then, 2 mL of dichloromethane (CH2Cl2) was added to the autoclave to extract the liquid products. Subsequently, the autoclave was placed in an oven at 120 °C for 12 h to remove the liquid and isolate the solid residues. Identification of liquid products was performed using a GC-MS (Agilent 5977 A), equipped with a HP-5 MS capillary column (0.25 mm in diameter, 30 m in length). The quantitative analysis of the liquid products was conducted using a gas chromatography (GC Agilent 8890B) equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID) and HP-5 MS capillary columns (0.25 mm in diameter, 30 m in length). The standard curve method was used in quantification. Quantitative analysis of gas products was performed by a gas chromatography (GC Agilent 8890B), equipped with an FID, a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and four capillary columns, including HP-PLOT Al2O3 S column (50 m × 0.53 mm × 15 μm), HP-PLOT Q column (20 m × 0.53 mm × 40 μm), HP-PLOT Q column (30 m × 0.53 mm × 40 μm), HP-PLOT molesieve column (30 m × 0.53 mm × 25 μm). Neutron powder diffraction

Neutron powder diffraction experiments were carried out using the long-wavelength neutron diffractometer WISH of ISIS Facility at Rutherford Appleton Laboratory (UK). The d5-pyridine@SA-HZSM-5 were prepared by charging activated SA-HZSM-5 with slightly excessive amount of deuterated pyridine (C5D5N). The sample was then heated at 50 °C for 1 h to disperse the d5-pyridine uniformly, and then vacuumed at 150 °C for 30 min to remove the physically adsorbed d5-pyridine. The loaded sample was then transferred into cylindrical vanadium sample cans and sealed with indium wire. Time-of-flight neutron diffraction patterns covering 5 banks of varied d-spacing ranges were collected at 100 K.

DFT calculations

Density function theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the CP2K-2023.1 package60–62. Perdew-Burke-Ernzerh (PBE) functional was used to describe the system. Unrestricted Kohn-Sham DFT was used as the electronic structure method in the framework of the Gaussian and plane waves (GPW) way. The Goedecker-Teter-Hutter (GTH) pseudopotentials and Three-ζ molecularly optimized basis sets with two polarization functions (TZV2P-MOLOPT-GTH) have been used for all elements. The Brillouin zone was sampled with gamma points. A plane-wave energy cutoff of 400 Ry (1 Ry = 13.606 eV) was employed. The geometries were optimized using the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS) algorithm. The convergence threshold of density matrix during self-consistent field (SCF) method was 1×10–6 Hartree, and convergence criterion for the forces was set to 4.5×10–4 Bohr/Hartree63–67. The van der Waals (vdW) interaction was amended by the DFT-D3 method of Grimme.
