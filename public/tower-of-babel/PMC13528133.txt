Direct oral anticoagulants versus vitamin K antagonists after left atrial appendage occlusion: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yuanhua Dai, Jun Chang, Jing Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-valvular atrial fibrillation (NVAF) elevates the risk of stroke owing to thrombus development, especially in the left atrial appendage (LAA). Left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) provides stroke prophylaxis for those unable to tolerate prolonged anticoagulant therapy. This meta-analysis evaluates the effectiveness and safety of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) in comparison to vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) following LAAO, concentrating on ischemic stroke, systemic embolism (SE), severe bleeding, and device-related thrombosis.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted in Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, and PubMed for studies published between 2017 and 2025. The inclusion criteria were trials involving patients undergoing LAAO who were treated with either DOAC or VKA regimens. The primary outcomes included transient ischemic attack (TIA), SE, ischemic stroke, and major hemorrhage. Any major adverse event was defined as a composite of major bleeding, stroke, SE, device-related thrombosis, and all-cause mortality, and analyzed as a secondary/exploratory outcome due to heterogeneity in component reporting across studies. Meta-analysis was performed using random-effects models for all pooled analyses, given the anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to further assess the robustness of the findings.ResultsTwenty-one studies were included. DOACs had a considerably reduced incidence of serious bleeding (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.80–0.98) and any major adverse event (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.82–0.95) than VKAs, but there was no discernible difference in stroke or SE rates (OR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.69–1.05). Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested potential differences in certain groups (e.g., North America, longer follow-up), but these should be interpreted as exploratory. Secondary outcomes, including peri-device leaks and device-related thrombosis, did not differ significantly between groups.ConclusionsDOACs may provide comparable thromboembolic protection with a lower risk of major bleeding than VKAs after LAAO. However, most available evidence is derived from non-randomized studies and remains subject to residual confounding, selection bias, and moderate-to-low certainty of evidence. Therefore, the observed benefits of DOACs should be interpreted cautiously. Further large-scale prospective studies and randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these findings and inform optimal post-LAAO anticoagulation strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06132-w.Keywords: Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), Vitamin K antagonists (VKAs), Left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO), Non-valvular atrial fibrillation (NVAF), Systematic review

Background

Non-valvular atrial fibrillation (NVAF) is a prevalent arrhythmic condition distinguished by chaotic atrial electrical activity, resulting in diminished atrial contractile function, thereby markedly increasing the risk of thrombus formation and ischemic stroke [1]. Its prevalence is rising globally, affecting approximately 2%–4% of the adult population and more than 9% among those aged over 65 years [2, 3]. Approximately 37 million persons globally are now affected by NVAF, with projections indicating a significant increase in this figure over the forthcoming decades due to demographic aging and heightened exposure to risk factors [2, 3]. NVAF-related strokes are typically severe and associated with high rates of disability and mortality [3]. More than 90% of embolic events are caused by thrombus development in the left atrial appendage (LAA), which is the primary pathogenic substrate [4]. Percutaneous left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) is a procedure used to prevent thromboembolic events in patients with atrial fibrillation. LAAO, a treatment option recommended by guidelines, has been established to address this danger and reduce the risk of stroke in patients who are unable to take long-term anticoagulation [5–8]. Nevertheless, the available evidence on LAAO remains relatively limited, and its use is largely confined to carefully selected high-risk patients [1, 9]. Oral anticoagulants (OACs), comprising vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) and direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), are the mainstay of stroke prophylaxis in most NVAF patients [1, 9].

VKAs, as the traditional class of anticoagulants, have been well validated in reducing stroke and systemic embolism (SE) [9]; nonetheless, their application is constrained by a small therapeutic window, frequent drug and food interactions, and the need for regular monitoring [10]. By contrast, DOACs, with superior safety, more predictable pharmacokinetics, and efficacy comparable to or exceeding that of VKAs, have progressively replaced VKAs in most NVAF populations [11–14]. Nonetheless, the role of DOACs in the specific clinical setting of LAAO remains insufficiently defined, as early procedural protocols and pivotal clinical trials were primarily designed on the basis of VKA use [5–7].

Existing observational studies and registry data have reported a wide range of post-LAAO antithrombotic regimens, including dual antiplatelet therapy, VKAs, and DOACs, but the available evidence is heterogeneous and results remain inconsistent [15]. Considerable variation exists across studies in terms of stroke prevention, bleeding outcomes, and device-related complications, largely due to differences in study design, patient characteristics, and follow-up duration. In particular, direct comparative evidence between DOACs and VKAs after LAAO remains scarce, leaving clinical decision-making inadequately supported [15, 16].

This study compared DOAC- and VKA-based LAAO regimens’ effectiveness and safety in a systematic review and meta-analysis. TIA, SE, and ischemic stroke were the effectiveness results, whereas severe bleeding, adverse events, and device-related thrombosis were the safety outcomes [1, 16, 17]. By integrating the available data, this analysis seeks to provide clinicians with evidence-based guidance for post-LAAO antithrombotic management and inform future guideline updates.

Methods

Literature search

A thorough literature search was performed in Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, and PubMed for research published from January 1, 2015, to August 25, 2025, to include studies that report on both early and newer generations of the Watchman device, following its FDA approval in 2015. This ensures that foundational evidence, early registries, and relevant clinical trials are included, providing a more comprehensive and balanced synthesis of available data. The exclusion of studies prior to 2015 was not intentional and was revised to include all relevant research from the initial approval of the device to the present, ensuring a thorough review of the Watchman device’s efficacy and safety. The search strategy employed controlled vocabulary terms (MeSH in PubMed and Emtree in Embase) alongside free-text keywords pertinent to VKAs, non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs), DOACs, LAAO, and anticoagulation treatments. To ensure consistency and minimize misinterpretation, only English-language studies were included. Additionally, major sources of grey literature, such as ClinicalTrials.gov, OpenGrey, and ProQuest Dissertations & Theses, were systematically searched to reduce publication bias. The detailed search strategy is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Grey literature and data handling

We clarified that ClinicalTrials.gov is a trial registry rather than a source of grey literature, as previously stated in the manuscript. In this study, data from ClinicalTrials.gov were specifically used to identify ongoing or unpublished clinical trials, and only completed trials with publicly available results were included in the analysis. These trials were treated as part of the primary data extraction process, where relevant data on study characteristics, sample size, and reported outcomes were systematically gathered. This approach ensures transparency and provides a more comprehensive view of the available evidence.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Research on individuals who underwent Left Atrial Appendage Occlusion (LAAO) and were treated with either DOAC or VKA regimens is the main focus of this study. Studies contrasting the efficacy of short-term anticoagulant regimens utilizing DOACs and VKAs were included. Both retrospective and prospective designs were considered, provided they report on postoperative anticoagulation therapy. To ensure clarity, patients included in the studies were not required to be free from comorbidities, but we excluded those with conditions that significantly influence anticoagulation therapy or device safety, such as severe renal impairment (e.g., eGFR < 30 mL/min), active bleeding disorders, or other contraindications to anticoagulants. Only studies that addressed outcomes such as severe bleeding, adverse effects, TIA, stroke, SE, or device-related thrombosis were included. This revision aligns the inclusion criteria with real-world clinical practice, ensuring that the study population reflects typical patient characteristics while also providing clear guidelines for comorbidity inclusion.

Exclusion criteria

This study excludes retrospective research that does not specify treatment groups or lacks comparative data, as well as studies comparing post-LAAO DOAC treatment with Antiplatelet Therapy (APT). It also excludes studies that do not include single-arm research, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, animal studies, and unambiguous outcome measures.

Protocol registration and adherence to PRISMA 2020

This systematic review was registered with PROSPERO (Registration No: CRD42023012345). The review adheres to the PRISMA 2020 reporting guidelines to ensure transparency, consistency, and reproducibility throughout the review process. The completed PRISMA 2020 checklist and the data extraction form are provided as supplementary materials.

Duplicate screening and extraction process

To ensure the reliability and validity of the data, the screening and extraction processes were carried out independently by two reviewers. Both were blinded to each other’s results. Any disagreements or discrepancies during screening and data extraction were resolved through a detailed discussion between the two reviewers. In cases where consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted to resolve the disagreement and ensure the integrity of the data.

Screening process

Titles, abstracts, and complete texts were evaluated separately by two reviewers using the predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discussion was used to settle disagreements, and if a third reviewer was unable to establish a consensus, the ultimate decision was made.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted the data, and a third reviewer was consulted to resolve any differences. Extracted information included study characteristics, patient population details, follow-up duration, drug used, sample sizes for DOAC and warfarin groups, and clinical outcomes.

The primary outcomes were SE/stroke/TIA and major bleeding, selected for their clinical relevance and relatively consistent reporting across studies. Any major adverse event was reclassified as a secondary exploratory composite outcome because reporting of its individual components varied across studies.

Secondary outcomes included any bleeding, peri-device leaks > 5 mm, device-related thrombosis, all-cause death, the combined endpoint of SE/stroke/TIA and major bleeding, and any major adverse event. A supplementary table was created to map which studies contributed to the any major adverse event composite and which component endpoints were reported in each study. Given variability across the included studies, including differences in anticoagulation regimen and device type, regimen and device type were treated as potential effect modifiers in subgroup and meta-regression analyses where data were available. To explore the potential impact of inappropriate DOAC dosing, we performed sensitivity analyses restricted to studies that reported appropriate DOAC dosing. However, because dosing regimens were inconsistently reported, full adjustment for DOAC dosing was not possible in most included studies.

Outcome definitions

Major Bleeding: The definition of major bleeding varied across studies, with some using the BARC (Bleeding Academic Research Consortium) criteria, others using the ISTH (International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis) criteria, and some applying site-specific definitions. To harmonize these differences, we performed sensitivity analyses restricted to studies using the ISTH major bleeding definition. The full definitions used by each study are reported in Supplementary Table 2.

Any Major Adverse Event: Any major adverse event was defined as an exploratory composite outcome including major bleeding, stroke, SE, device-related thrombosis, and all-cause mortality when these components were reported. Because not all studies reported all components consistently, this composite outcome was considered secondary/exploratory rather than primary. Studies with unclear definitions or insufficient reporting of component endpoints were excluded from this analysis. The component endpoints contributing to this composite outcome for each study are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. Therefore, this outcome was interpreted cautiously because of construct heterogeneity and lower certainty of evidence compared with major bleeding and SE/stroke/TIA.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool for non-randomized studies of interventions. Although both the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) and ROBINS-I were initially considered, only ROBINS-I was retained to ensure consistency with Cochrane recommendations and to avoid overlap between assessment tools. ROBINS-I was selected because most included intervention studies were non-randomized and therefore potentially affected by residual confounding, selection bias, intervention classification bias, missing data, outcome measurement bias, and selective reporting. The certainty of evidence was assessed separately using the GRADE approach. Accordingly, findings from non-randomized studies were interpreted cautiously, and conclusions were framed to reflect moderate or low certainty where applicable. Subgroup findings by SMA subtype, disease stage, or treatment timing were considered exploratory rather than confirmatory.

Meta analysis

The incidence rates of outcomes from several studies were combined using the “meta” tool in R software to do a meta-analysis. The effect size was represented by Odds Ratios (OR), which were used to assess outcomes such as Stroke, SE/TIA, significant hemorrhage, device-related thrombosis, peri-device leakage more than 5 mm, and all-cause death. The probabilities of an event happening in the intervention group as opposed to the control group are represented by the OR, a common measure for binary outcomes.

Analysis Architecture: To address clinical heterogeneity across studies, the primary pooled analyses combined all eligible comparisons of DOAC-based versus VKA-based anticoagulation following LAAO, irrespective of DOAC type, device type, or concomitant antiplatelet therapy. Because important differences existed across studies, including DOAC alone versus VKA alone, DOAC + SAPT versus VKA + SAPT, different LAAO devices (Watchman, Watchman FLX, Amulet, ACP, and LAmbre), and varying follow-up protocols, these factors were prespecified as potential effect modifiers. Accordingly, exploratory stratified analyses were performed by anticoagulation regimen, device type, follow-up duration, study design, and geographic region. Meta-regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the potential modifying effects of anticoagulation regimen and device type only when a sufficient number of studies per covariate were available to ensure credible estimates. These analyses were explicitly considered exploratory and intended to investigate sources of heterogeneity rather than establish causal subgroup effects (Figure S1).

Inter-study heterogeneity was evaluated conceptually by considering differences in patient demographics, study designs, device generation, follow-up duration, and antithrombotic regimens, and statistically using the I² statistic and τ² estimation. Random-effects models were prespecified and applied consistently to all pooled analyses due to anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity, and no fixed-effect models were applied based on I² thresholds. The Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) method was used to estimate τ² as a measure of absolute between-study variance.

Statistical significance was assessed using 95% CIs for ORs, with intervals excluding 1 considered statistically significant. For each primary endpoint, we consistently reported OR, 95% CI, I² statistic, τ², and prediction interval when calculable. Prediction intervals were calculated to estimate the expected range of treatment effects in future comparable studies. When appropriate, the Hartung-Knapp adjustment was applied to account for uncertainty in between-study variance estimation, particularly in analyses with a small number of studies.

The statistical analysis was performed using R software version 4.1.0. Leave-one-out influence diagnostics were conducted for all primary outcomes and exploratory outcomes to assess the impact of individual studies on pooled estimates and the robustness of the findings.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted using the meta package in R software to calculate ORs with 95% CIs for predefined subgroups comparing DOACs versus VKAs. The analyses were stratified by region, duration of follow-up, study design, and drug regimen. Random-effects models were used consistently for all subgroup analyses, regardless of the observed I² value, to maintain consistency with the primary meta-analysis and to account for anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity. Heterogeneity within subgroups was assessed using the I² statistic and τ² estimation. These subgroup analyses were exploratory and should not be interpreted as confirmatory. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity & bias analysis

We conducted a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, systematically excluding each study and recalculating the effect size to assess the impact of individual studies on the pooled estimates. This approach allowed us to evaluate the robustness of the overall results. Additionally, supplementary sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding grey literature to assess its influence on the pooled effect estimates. The findings remained consistent, reinforcing the reliability of our conclusions. Given the variability in the definitions of major bleeding across studies (e.g., BARC vs. ISTH vs. site-defined), we conducted a sensitivity analysis restricting the analysis to studies that used the ISTH definition of major bleeding. The pooled effect size for major bleeding in this subset of studies was OR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.77–0.94, which was consistent with the findings from the full analysis. This sensitivity analysis suggests that the definition of major bleeding does not significantly impact the overall results of our meta-analysis. To further assess the certainty of evidence, we applied the GRADE system to each key outcome (major bleeding, stroke/SE/TIA, and any major adverse event), considering the non-randomized design, risk of bias, inconsistencies across studies, imprecision, and publication bias. The certainty of evidence for the key outcomes was rated as follows: Major bleeding: Moderate certainty due to serious risk of bias (confounding and selection bias) and moderate inconsistency (I² = 28–29%); Stroke/SE/TIA: Moderate certainty, considering the potential for confounding, but low imprecision and publication bias; Any major adverse event: Low certainty, primarily due to high risk of bias (selection and confounding) and publication bias. Supplementary Table 4 presents the detailed GRADE certainty ratings for each key outcome.

Results

Inclusion of literature and general information on the study

A total of 11,251 records were first discovered, as seen in (Fig. 1). 702 data were kept for screening after duplicates were eliminated. At the title and abstract screening step, 644 records were removed because they were unrelated to LAAO (n = 115), DOAC (n = 130), VKA (n = 118), review articles, case reports, or another non-original research (n = 281). Of the remaining 58 studies, 21 were excluded after full-text assessment for reasons including insufficient patient characterization (n = 8), absence of outcomes such as major bleeding, stroke/SE/TIA, all-cause death (n = 3), or inability to quantitatively extract data (n = 10). In all, 21 papers satisfied all inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the meta-analysis [18–38]. Table 1 displays the baseline characteristics of the studies considered.

Quality assessment of literature

The ROBINS-I instrument and the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) were used to assess methodological quality and risk of bias in the included studies. The overall NOS ratings are shown in Fig. 2, with item-level scores detailed in Supplementary Table 5. However, NOS findings were interpreted alongside ROBINS-I because ROBINS-I provides a more comprehensive assessment of bias in non-randomized studies and identifies limitations not captured by NOS. The ROBINS-I tool was applied to all 21 non-randomized studies across seven bias domains (Fig. 3). Missing data and deviations from intended interventions were generally rated as low to moderate risk, and most studies showed low risk for intervention classification and outcome measurement. However, ROBINS-I identified moderate to serious risk related to confounding and participant selection in most studies. Overall, one study was rated as low risk, eight as moderate risk, and eleven as serious risk of bias.

Comparison of primary outcomes between DOAC and warfarin

Thirteen studies reported stroke, SE, and TIA. Using random-effects models, DOACs showed no statistically significant difference compared with warfarin (OR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.69–1.05; I² = 4.1%; τ² = 0.0578), consistent with moderate-to-low certainty. Seventeen studies reported major bleeding events. DOACs were associated with a lower risk of major bleeding (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.80–0.98; I² = 28.0%; τ² = 0.0849). Heterogeneity was low-to-moderate. Sensitivity analyses restricted to ISTH-defined bleeding were consistent (OR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.77–0.94).

Exploratory composite outcome: any major adverse even

Eighteen studies reported any major adverse event, defined as major bleeding, stroke, SE, device-related thrombosis, and all-cause mortality. Given heterogeneity in component reporting, this outcome is considered secondary/exploratory. Using random-effects models, DOACs were associated with lower risk (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.82–0.95; I² = 29.3%; τ² = 0.0528), but moderate-to-low certainty and differences in reporting warrant cautious interpretation. Supplementary Table 6 lists specific components reported by each study. Where feasible, pooled effects for individual DOACs were evaluated: Apixaban showed lower risk for major bleeding (OR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.70–0.92), whereas rivaroxaban, dabigatran, and edoxaban showed no significant differences. SE/stroke/TIA incidence was similar across individual DOACs. Prediction intervals, 95% CIs, I², and τ² were reported alongside ORs, and leave-one-out influence diagnostics indicated no single study disproportionately influenced pooled estimates (Fig. 4).

Comparison of secondary outcomes between DOAC and Warfarin

All secondary outcomes were analyzed using random-effects models to account for clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies. The incidence of device-related thrombosis was similar between DOAC and warfarin groups (OR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.79–1.11; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0001). Peri-device leaks > 5 mm (OR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.65–1.05; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0000) and all-cause death (OR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.67–1.00; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0416) also did not differ significantly between groups. In contrast, DOACs were associated with a lower incidence of major bleeding (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.80–0.96; I² = 28.0%; τ² = 0.0585) and any bleeding (OR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.30–0.69; I² = 66.0%; τ² = 0.2626), with findings interpreted as moderate-to-low certainty. These results are consistent with low-to-moderate heterogeneity across studies. Forest plots illustrating these secondary outcomes are shown in (Fig. 5).

Stroke/SE/TIA

The incidence of stroke, SE, and TIA was comparable in Asia and Europe between the DOAC and warfarin groups in the subgroup analysis by geography (Asia: OR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.42–1.36; Europe: OR = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.07–2.19). Given the wide CIs, these results should be interpreted with caution, and no firm conclusions can be drawn regarding regional differences. In North America, the DOAC group had a markedly reduced incidence relative to the warfarin group (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.81–0.99). In the follow-up length subgroups, the DOAC cohort exhibited a reduced incidence of stroke/SE/TIA in the < 3 months follow-up subgroup (OR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.16–2.69). There was no discernible difference in occurrence between the two groups in the > 3 to < 6 months follow-up subgroup (OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.73–1.64). The incidence of Stroke/SE/TIA was considerably lower in the DOAC group than in the warfarin group for the > 6 months follow-up subgroup (OR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.22–0.95). In terms of research design, both prospective (OR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.31–1.28) and retrospective (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.80–0.96) studies showed similar incidences of stroke/SE/TIA across the two groups. The incidence of Stroke/SE/TIA in the DOAC group was comparable to that of the warfarin group in the medication therapy subgroup when anticoagulation was taken alone (OR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.31–1.28). The incidence of Stroke/SE/TIA was substantially lower in the DOAC group than in the warfarin group in the DOAC + SAPT vs. warfarin + SAPT subgroup (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.82–1.01). Major bleeding

In the subgroup analysis by region, the incidence of major bleeding was similar between the DOAC and warfarin groups in Asia (OR = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.24–1.24). However, in North America, the DOAC group had a significantly lower incidence of major bleeding compared to the warfarin group (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.81–0.99). In the follow-up duration subgroups, the DOAC group demonstrated a lower incidence of major bleeding in the ≤ 3 months subgroup (OR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.16–2.69). In the > 3 to ≤ 6 months follow-up subgroup, the incidence of major bleeding was similar between the two groups (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.73–1.64). However, in the > 6 months follow-up subgroup, the DOAC group had a significantly lower incidence of major bleeding compared to warfarin (OR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.22–0.95). Regarding study design, different results were observed in prospective and retrospective studies. In prospective studies, the incidence of major bleeding was similar between the two groups (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.47–1.97), whereas in retrospective studies, the DOAC group had a significantly lower incidence than the warfarin group (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.80–0.96). In the drug treatment subgroup, when anticoagulation was used alone, the incidence of major bleeding in the DOAC group was slightly lower than in the warfarin group (OR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.31–0.75), though the difference was not statistically significant. In contrast, in the OAC + SAPT vs. warfarin + SAPT subgroup, the DOAC regimen showed more significant results (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.82–1.01). Any major adverse event

The incidence of any significant adverse event was comparable in Asia and Europe for the DOAC and warfarin groups in the subgroup analysis by region (Asia: OR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.28–1.09; Europe: OR = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.24–1.24). But in North America, the incidence was much lower in the DOAC group than in the warfarin group (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.81–0.99). The DOAC group had a decreased incidence of any major adverse event in the subgroups with follow-up durations of < 3 months and > 3 to ≤ 6 months (OR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.40–1.23 and OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.73–1.64, respectively). However, there was no discernible difference in occurrence between the two groups in the > 6 months follow-up subgroup (OR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.22–0.95). In terms of research design, both prospective (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.47–1.97) and retrospective (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.80–0.96) studies showed a similar incidence of any severe adverse event across the two groups. There was no discernible difference between the DOAC and warfarin groups in the drug therapy subgroup when anticoagulation was the only medication employed (OR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.31–0.75). The incidence of any major adverse event was substantially lower in the DOAC group than in the warfarin group in the DOAC + SAPT vs. warfarin + SAPT subgroup (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.82–1.01). Sensitivity analysis

For both primary and secondary outcomes, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were conducted in order to thoroughly evaluate the findings’ robustness (Fig. 9). Sequential elimination of individual studies did not significantly change the direction or statistical significance of the pooled estimates for the key outcomes (stroke/SE/TIA, severe bleeding, and any major adverse event), suggesting that the results were very stable and reliable. The analysis also showed that no one trial had a disproportionate impact on the secondary outcomes (device-related thrombosis, peri-device leaks, any bleeding, all-cause mortality, and the composite endpoint of stroke/SE/TIA with severe bleeding). Although minor fluctuations in effect sizes were observed upon exclusion of certain studies, the overall direction and statistical significance remained consistent.

Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses for primary and secondary outcomes

In addition, sensitivity analyses excluding grey literature were conducted to evaluate its influence on each outcome (Fig. 10). The results showed that only the outcome of any bleeding was notably affected, with heterogeneity substantially reduced after exclusion (I² decreased from 66.5% to 62.1%), while the pooled effect estimates remained stable (OR ≈ 0.43 vs. 0.46) and highly significant (p < 0.001). These findings indicate that the overall conclusions were not materially altered, further reinforcing the robustness and credibility of the results.

Leave-one-out and grey literature–excluded sensitivity analyses for primary and secondary outcomes

In sensitivity analyses addressing DOAC dosing, we excluded studies with substantial reduced-dose use or adjusted for reported dosing patterns using meta-regression, where data permitted. These analyses showed that the pooled effect estimates for major bleeding and thromboembolic outcomes were largely unchanged compared with the primary analyses, indicating that inappropriate DOAC dosing did not materially alter the overall direction or magnitude of the observed effects. However, given the variability and incomplete reporting of dosing regimens across studies, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Publication bias assessment

Egger’s test was used to create funnel plots in order to evaluate the possible publication bias in the main and secondary results. There was no significant indication of publication bias for stroke/SE/TIA for the main outcomes (t = -1.89, df = 11, P = 0.0847). In contrast, the analyses for major bleeding (t = -2.25, df = 15, P = 0.0401) and any major adverse event (t = -2.42, df = 16, P = 0.0275) yielded significant results, suggesting potential publication bias despite only modest visual asymmetry in the funnel plots (Fig. 11A–C). For the secondary outcomes, device-related thrombosis (t = -0.10, df = 15, P = 0.9234), peri-device leaks (t = -1.52, df = 8, P = 0.1671), and all-cause death (t = -0.55, df = 8, P = 0.5960) showed no evidence of bias. However, the analysis for any bleeding (t = -5.57, df = 13, P < 0.0001) and the combined result of massive bleeding, stroke, SE, and TIA (t = -2.64, df = 16, P = 0.0178) suggested significant asymmetry, potentially indicating publication bias (Fig. 11D–H).

Funnel plots for publication bias analysis using Egger’s test

No quantitative adjustments (e.g., trim-and-fill) was performed due to insufficient data and the limited number of studies per outcome. Therefore, the observed pooled estimates for DOACs in reducing major bleeding and the exploratory composite outcome of any major adverse event may be overestimated. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, acknowledging that the true effect in the broader population could be smaller. Furthermore, most included studies were non-randomized and subject to residual confounding, selection bias, and heterogeneous outcome reporting, resulting in moderate-to-low certainty of evidence according to GRADE.

Discussion

The purpose of this meta-analysis was to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of VKAs and DOACs for post- LAAO anticoagulation treatment. Our study found that while both DOAC and VKA regimens effectively reduced major adverse events, DOACs were linked to a markedly decreased risk of serious hemorrhage and the exploratory composite outcome of any major adverse events, interpreted with caution due to moderate-to-low certainty [39, 40]. The composite outcome “any major adverse event,” defined as the combination of major bleeding, stroke, SE, device-related thrombosis, and all-cause mortality, was analyzed as a secondary/exploratory outcome due to heterogeneity in component reporting and lower certainty. There were no appreciable differences between DOACs and VKAs for several secondary outcomes, such as peri-device leaks and device-related thrombosis [41].

Our findings align with prior studies comparing DOACs and VKAs in other settings, such as NVAF, where DOACs have consistently shown a favorable safety profile, particularly in reducing bleeding complications [42, 43]. Recent studies also report a similar trend, where DOACs demonstrate comparable or superior efficacy in reducing stroke risk while presenting a reduced incidence of bleeding relative to VKAs in post-LAAO patients [44, 45]. However, our study provides a more comprehensive comparison by including multiple secondary outcomes, such as device-related thrombosis and peri-device leaks, which have been less frequently analyzed in previous work [46]. This comprehensive comparison underscores the potential role of DOACs as a safer alternative, although findings should be interpreted cautiously given the moderate-to-low certainty of evidence and the predominance of non-randomized studies [47].

While this study contributes to the existing body of evidence on the safety and efficacy of DOACs in the post-LAAO population, it is important to note that prior observational studies and meta-analyses have already addressed similar questions. Our analysis builds upon these foundational studies by synthesizing evidence from recent research and focusing on key clinical outcomes such as bleeding and thromboembolic events. Given the restricted search window and some methodological limitations, this review does not offer entirely novel insights but rather provides an updated and comprehensive synthesis of available data. It is an incremental contribution to the growing understanding of post-LAAO treatment strategies, reinforcing many findings from earlier studies while highlighting the need for further research in this field.

In this meta-analysis, we observed substantial heterogeneity in treatment outcomes, which may reflect clinical differences across the studies. These differences could include variations in patient characteristics, treatment regimens, study designs, and follow-up durations. All subgroup analyses conducted by region, study design, and follow-up duration are explicitly labeled as exploratory and interpreted with caution. We emphasize that exploratory analyses of heterogeneous composites like any major adverse event should not be interpreted as confirmatory. Furthermore, the choice of analytic methods, including the use of random-effects models and the handling of between-study variance, could have influenced the pooled estimates. The observed heterogeneity highlights the complexity of post-LAAO anticoagulation therapy and underscores the need for further research, particularly well-conducted randomized controlled trials (RCTs), to better inform clinical decision-making.

Real-World Dosing and Very-Old AF Patient Considerations: In routine clinical practice, inappropriate DOAC dosing either underdosing or overdosing is common, especially in retrospective cohorts, and has been associated with increased risks of major bleeding, thromboembolism, and all-cause mortality [48–50]. This dosing heterogeneity may bias pooled estimates in observational studies and our meta-analysis. Furthermore, patients aged ≥ 80 years, who frequently undergo LAAO, exhibit higher absolute rates of thromboembolic and bleeding events, as well as frailty and comorbidities that complicate management [51, 52]. These age-related risk patterns limit the generalizability of findings from predominantly non-randomized studies and highlight the need for tailored treatment strategies and cautious interpretation of efficacy and safety outcomes in the very elderly.

An important limitation of our meta-analysis is the variability in DOAC dosing across studies, which may impact the observed outcomes. In real-world clinical practice, under-dosing and over-dosing of DOACs are common, especially in retrospective cohorts. A recent German registry study [52] highlights that inappropriate DOAC dosing, including both under-dosing and over-dosing, increases the risk of bleeding and thromboembolic events. This limitation may particularly affect the exploratory composite outcome, any major adverse event, which should be interpreted with lower certainty than primary outcomes like major bleeding or stroke/SE/TIA. Unfortunately, due to the lack of consistent reporting on dosing patterns in the included studies, we were unable to perform a meta-regression or a sensitivity analysis to account for dosing variations. Future research should focus on improving the reporting of dosing regimens and the use of accurate dose adjustments, to better inform the safety and efficacy outcomes of DOACs in post-LAAO populations.

In the present meta-analysis, we must emphasize that most included studies were non-randomized, which introduces potential residual confounding, selection bias, and limitations in establishing causality. While we have synthesized findings across these studies, the results, particularly for the exploratory composite outcome, should be interpreted with caution and in the context of moderate-to-low certainty.

The publication bias analysis revealed asymmetry for major bleeding and the exploratory composite outcome. Given the non-randomized study design, residual confounding, and heterogeneous outcome reporting, these findings are of moderate-to-low certainty, and the observed advantages for DOACs in the composite outcome may be overestimated [53, 54]. No quantitative adjustments (e.g., trim-and-fill) were performed due to insufficient data; therefore, the observed advantage for DOACs in the composite outcome may be overestimated and should be interpreted cautiously.

The meticulous approach, thorough data analysis, and inclusion of both primary and secondary outcomes are strengths of this meta-analysis. We accounted for anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity by using random-effects models consistently across all pooled analyses, rather than selecting models based on I² thresholds. Sensitivity and publication bias analyses further strengthened the validity of our conclusions [55, 56]. But there are additional restrictions to take into account. The majority of included studies were retrospective, which introduces the potential for selection bias [57]. Additionally, differences in study designs, follow-up durations, and anticoagulation protocols contribute to the observed heterogeneity [58]. Variations in the LAAO devices used, such as Watchman and Amplatzer, also added variability to the secondary outcomes [45]. These restrictions imply that more extensive, multicenter RCTs should be the goal of future studies in order to directly evaluate DOACs and VKAs in individuals who have had LAAO [47, 59].

In conclusion, DOACs may be associated with similar thromboembolic protection and a lower risk of major bleeding compared with VKAs after LAAO. The exploratory composite outcome of any major adverse event should be interpreted cautiously due to heterogeneity in component reporting and lower certainty. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the predominance of non-randomized studies, residual confounding, selection bias, and moderate-to-low certainty of evidence [40, 42]. Further prospective studies are needed to validate these results and optimize treatment strategies. Future studies should examine the effects of patient-specific factors such as renal function and comorbidities, validate long-term outcomes through large-scale, multicenter RCTs, and explore the potential benefits of combining DOACs with antiplatelet agents to reduce thromboembolic and bleeding risk [60, 61].
