Beyond crisis: rethinking the evaluation of psychosocial interventions after mass casualty incidents
Authors: Caroline Rometsch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Mass casualty incidents lead to significant psychological distress and mental health complaints. In recent years, psychosocial and psychotherapeutic health care offers have increasingly been integrated into mass casualty protocols, yet it has become clear that emergency systems lack structured evaluation mechanisms, and research on exemplary healthcare models remains limited. This commentary discusses gaps in health service utilisation evaluation and assessment after mass casualty incidents.Methods: This commentary provides a narrative overview of conceptual and methodological evaluation challenges, informed by current evidence and clinical experience.Results: Findings from post-disaster contexts demonstrate the necessity of embedding psychosocial and psychotherapeutic health care offers within mass casualty incident structures to address both immediate and long-term mental health needs. Methodologically, validated symptom instruments per se are established (e.g. IES-R, K6 for post-traumatic stress disorders), however, a key barrier is the absence of validated, context-specific instruments specifically designed to capture healthcare utilisation evaluations (e.g. care pathways, perceived quality, helpfulness of psychosocial interventions, trust in care structures, continuity across acute and long-term post-disaster support systems).Conclusion: For the first time, this commentary outlines time-dependent indicators as a proposed framework that should guide assessment after mass casualty incidents. Future research should take a systematic approach into consideration to evaluate evidence of longitudinal studies, thereby supporting the establishment of scalable, theory-based models of psychosocial and psychotherapeutic care for post-disaster contexts. In addition, centralised post-crisis registries are needed to allow systematic registration, monitoring, and evaluation of psychosocial interventions and pathways to care.KEYWORDS: Mass casualty incidents, psychosocial care, evaluation, registries, terror attack, mass trauma, post-disaster mental healthPALABRAS CLAVE: Incidentes con víctimas múltiples, cuidado psicosocial, evaluación, registros, ataque terrorista, trauma masivo, salud mental posterior a desastres

Introduction

Mass casualty incidents such as attacks, shootings, and terrorist assaults often lead to considerable mental health complaints and bring the urgent need for adequate post-disaster care into light (Mitchell and Everly Jr, 2023) as shown after the Paris attacks with affected individuals suffering from acute stress reactions, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (Berthail et al., 2024), reactive depression, anxiety disorders (Sanz and García-Vera, 2021). Psychosomatic symptoms such as sleep disturbances, palpitations, pain experiences are commonly reported (Van Overmeire, Van Keer, and Bilsen, 2021).

The Berlin Christmas market attack in 2016 demonstrated the crucial role of integrated psychosocial support as well as targeted training for first responses (Henke et al., 2017). Mental health care was rapidly provided on-site, offering low-threshold access for both victims and emergency personnel. Early crisis interventions included psychological first aid, psychoeducation, structured follow-up referrals (Hauer et al., 2018). Psychosocial emergency care, as delineated in national guidelines (Bundesamt für Bevölkerungsschutz and Katastrophenhilfe, 2012) plays an essential role in alleviating acute and long-term psychological complaints and the onset of mental disorders. It comprises preventive, acute, and follow-up interventions aimed at restoring mental health and social functioning after terror attacks (Finkeldei, Kern, and Rinne-Wolf, 2024). Targeted psychological interventions, especially CBT, (Grant, Bowman, and Johnson, 2024) showed high evidence after both single-event and multiple-event (Hoppen et al., 2024). The attack on the Magdeburg Christmas market in December 2024 represented a prime example for the rapidly mplementation of a comprehensive, interprofessional psychosocial emergency care, integrated within the hospital’s mass casualty protocols with transition into a specialised trauma ambulance for the long term treatment of affected individuals (Rometsch et al., 2025).

Psychosocial care refers primarily to early, low-threshold, trauma-informed supportive interventions such as psychoeducation, emotional support, screening, within acute care structures (Afzal et al., 2024). Psychotherapeutic care refers to structured evidence-based psychological treatment targeting trauma-related symptoms (Semerci and Uzun, 2023), and psychosomatic care refers to integrated biopsychosocial treatment approaches addressing persistent interactions between mental complaints and somatic symptoms (Engel, 1977). This commentary reflects on how psychosocial care within health care systems after mass casualty incidents can be evaluated, with a particular focus on methodological challenges and measurement gaps, shifting the focus beyond symptom-specific assessments towards a framework of proposed indicators to assess healthcare utilisation, perceived quality of care, and system responsiveness across the recovery trajectory.

Posttraumatic stress disorder after mass casualty incidents

Previous findings from Madrid showed that injured victims and nearby residents had higher rates of PTSD, depression, and anxiety than police officers (Gabriel et al., 2007) with ranges of PTSD between 33 and 39%, compared to substantially lower rates among indirect victims (e.g. 4% in the general population, around 6% among emergency workers, and 17–29% among relatives and friends) (Paz García-Vera, Sanz, and Gutiérrez, 2016).

Health care utilisation after mass casualty incidents

Attacks in France in 2015 showed that half of affected individuals with mental health complaints did not receive psychosocial support (Vuillermoz et al., 2022). A qualitative study among hospitalised adolescent survivors of the 2011 Utøya terrorist attack in Norway revealed the importance of both trauma-focused conversations and routine exchanges with clinicians to develop psychological processing, a sense of safety, restoration of trust and resilience (Bugge et al., 2019). Therefore, key recommendations after attacks addresses the demand of coordinating services efficiently, proactively addressing access barriers with targeting groups at-risk (Reifels et al., 2013).

National responses to attacks in Norway, France, and Belgium varied considerably in the structure and duration of psychosocial care, with Norway implementing proactive long-term follow-up within primary care, France focusing on acute psychological support through specialised departments, and Belgium offering acute services, however, without systematic long-term care (Stene et al., 2022). Evidence indicates, even months after the man-made attack in the Netherlands, affected individuals continued to face substantial difficulties in accessing appropriate healthcare services (Bosmans et al., 2022). After the attack on the Magdeburg Christmas market and the implementation of first psychosocial support within the mass casualty protocols, individuals received psychotherapeutic support as needed within a specialised trauma ambulance with long-term recovery. This strategy reflects stepped care models, where crisis support serves as a gateway to psychosomatic and psychiatric interventions (Rometsch et al., 2025). A widely endorsed framework for psychosocial support following crises is provided by the Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) Guidelines, which advocate a multi-layered, multi-sectoral approach to promote mental health and psychosocial well-being in emergencies (Committee, 2006). Based on these guidelines, the ‘Five Essential Elements’ of mass trauma intervention were promoted with the core elements: safety, calming, self- and collective efficacy, connectedness, and hope (Hobfoll et al., 2007). These principles support in reducing symptoms of anxiety, depression, posttraumatic stress, and distress, alongside improvements in mood, perceived safety, social connectedness, and a sense of control (Hermosilla et al., 2023). This brings the absence of centralised post-crisis registries in many countries such as Germany into the light (O’Reilly, Cameron, and Joshipura, 2012; Bommakanti et al., 2018). Such centralised registries could systematically capture a core longitudinal dataset including exposure characteristics, pathways to psychosocial, psychotherapeutic, and psychosomatic care, timing and continuity of follow-up, patient-reported outcomes, and symptom trajectories over time (Porgo, Moore, and Tardif, 2016). Beyond epidemiological monitoring, such registries may support identification of unmet needs and underserved groups, enable benchmarking of care structures, facilitate evaluation of long-term service effectiveness, and provide a flexible infrastructure that can adapt to changing post-crisis needs (Stubbs et al., 2024). Unlike Norway’s proactive registration and tracking systems, global psychosocial response mechanisms lack structured longitudinal follow-up, limiting targeted outreach and systematic collecting and tracking psychosocial demands and needs of affected individuals (Mobinizadeh et al., 2022).

Evaluation and methods of psychosocial health care services after mass casualty incidents

A range of studies examined tools to evaluate the operational performance and system response during mass casualty incidents such as the validated Disaster Management Assessment Tool for Health Care Practitioners with strong psychometric properties to assess disaster preparedness across health professions (Elshami et al., 2025) or the three-component tool assessing triage, clinical maneuvers, and radio communication for the assessment of evacuation and treatment performance (Ingrassia et al., 2010). A large full-scale hospital simulation integrating geotracking, triage metrics, workflow analysis, and workload assessments provided reproducible benchmark data for hospital performance (von der Forst et al., 2025). However, when operational performance is being assessed, only limited evidence on the real-world accuracy and applicability was demonstrated, as most studies rely on simulations and remain insufficiently validated for live mass casualty incidents scenarios (Abdul-Nabi and Hitti, 2025). While operational tools exist, none address psychosocial care evaluation and trauma-aftermath care. Post-disaster mental health assessments often lack methodological rigour, are highly heterogeneous in design, and frequently neglect critical domains such as risk factor assessment, longitudinal follow-up, or treatment evaluation. This methodological inconsistency makes the comparability of findings and the development of evidence-based service models difficult (Kessler and Wittchen, 2008).

A complexity lies in the notable absence of standardised metrics to assess quality of psychosocial healthcare services in post-disaster contexts. This raises a fundamental methodological challenge: how can healthcare services be reliably assessed in samples that have been profoundly disrupted by an attack experiencing psychosocial distress, mental and somatic complaints? While the prevalence and the trajectory of posttraumatic symptoms and mental health morbidity is well-documented, there is a paucity of empirical work evaluating the perceived quality of care from the patient perspective. The acute burden, existential anxiety, and disorientation experienced by individuals after a mass casualty incident potentially confounds the assessments of patients’ needs. Widely used assessment tools of mental health complaints rely on established instruments such as the Impact of Event Scale–Revised (IES-R) (Weiss, 2007), the PTSD Checklist (PCL) (Weathers, 1993), the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale (CAPS) (Blake et al., 1995), Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6) (Mewton et al., 2016),, or the well-implemented Patient Health Questionnaire (e.g. PHQ-9 for depressive disorder, PHQ-15 for somatic symptom burden) (Kroenke, Spitzer, and Williams, 2001),all of which have demonstrated robust efficacy in evaluating the long-term prevalence trajectories of PTSD, particularly in disaster settings such as earthquakes and industrial disasters, with substantial evidence supporting their reliability in monitoring mental health outcomes across heterogeneous populations over extended periods (Newnham et al., 2022). These instruments are valuable for comparing the psychological outcomes of affected individuals, providing an important methodological advantage in post-disaster research. The primary difficulty in comparing findings across studies arises from the diversity of health care settings, rather than the absence of specific assessment instruments per se. Without standardised metrics and real-time assessments of the health care structure and an evaluation of the quality of (psychosocial and psychosomatic) health care services, it remains difficult to identify which components of psychosocial care are most valued and effective. By evaluating not only the clinical outcomes but also the process and quality of care, disaster-specific tools help to identify gaps in service provision, particularly in contexts where healthcare systems may be under stress or less equipped to handle the specific needs of trauma survivors. Consequently, there is a risk that even well-intentioned (psychosocial and psychosomatic) interventions may not be sufficient in addressing survivors’ needs.

Addressing this gap requires the development of validated tools for assessing satisfaction with psychosocial and psychotherapeutic care considering the diversity of health care systems in the context of collective mass casualty incidents. Such assessment tools should be sensitive to the disrupted frameworks of trust and safety that characterise the post-attack context. Moreover, longitudinal designs are needed to capture how perceptions of care change over time and to identify critical moments for intervention and support.

Recommendations for future assessment instruments

To strengthen future evaluations, newly developed assessment instruments for the evaluation of health care services should incorporate several core domains for affected individuals of mass casualty incidents. First, a detailed account of healthcare structures with standardised indicators of health care utilisation are needed, including e.g. access pathways, timing of first contact, intensity of support, and continuity of care across acute and follow-up phases. Second, patient-reported outcomes must be integrated, capturing, e.g. perceived helpfulness, trust, safety, and satisfaction with psychosocial and psychotherapeutic interventions, all domains widely lacking in current post-disaster evaluations. Third, instruments for the evaluation of health care needs should embed context-sensitive measures reflecting the unique disruptions after mass casualty incidents, such as feelings of threat, disorientation, and impaired help-seeking capacity. Fourth, instruments should include longitudinal components to track, e.g. changing needs over time and to detect delayed-onset distress or drop-out from care. As a systematic synthesis of the literature is still lacking and the proposed evaluation indicators remain conceptual at this stage, the framework should be understood as an initial basis for future operationalisation, empirical refinement, and validation. Recommended assessment constructs across phases following a mass casualty incident

Assessment instruments should be tailored to the temporal dynamics of post-incident recovery, as specific constructs become relevant at different stages following a mass casualty incident. During the immediate phase of the incident and first aid period (0–hours), instruments for health care evaluation should consider peritraumatic reactions, including exposure severity, perceived threat, acute physiological arousal, dissociation, and disorientation, as those early responses are strong predictors of later symptom trajectories and may act as confounding factors in healthcare evaluations. In the hospital phase (hours–days), assessments should keep in mind early trauma symptoms, somatic distress (e.g. pain, autonomic dysregulation, sleep disturbances), and the initiation of psychosocial support or referral pathways. The acute post-discharge phase (days) includes early PTSD, anxiety and depressive symptoms, persistent somatic complaints, and the timing and adequacy of initial psychosocial contacts. During the subacute phase (up to 30 days), assessment tools of health care services and patients’ needs should further more capture PTSD symptom clusters, somatic amplification or functional symptom development, continuity of care, to evaluate perceived helpfulness of interventions. In the sensitive period between 30 and 90 days, instruments have to adapt trauma-specific symptoms, symptom trajectories, delayed-onset distress, functional impairment, treatment adherence, and early drop-out. The 3–6-month phase should emphasise health care services in the light of long-term trajectories of PTSD, recovery versus chronicity, ongoing somatic distress, and access to long-term psychosocial and psychotherapeutic treatment. Finally, in the reintegration period (6–24 months), assessments of health care quality should respect long-term PTSD and depressive symptoms, chronic somatic symptom burden, quality of life, for the evaluation of overall effectiveness and responsiveness of the healthcare system (see Figure 2).

Conclusion

The integration of specialised psychosocial support into mass casualty response offers a promising foundation for stepped care, yet its effectiveness cannot be determined due to the lack of context-adapted instruments to asses healthcare services. While validated instruments for assessing post-traumatic symptoms (e.g. PTSD) exist, specific adaption and evaluation of the health care structure may enhance the precision to capture key aspects of healthcare service utilisation. Developing context-specific, longitudinally sensitive, and healthcare utilisation-specific assessment instruments with a scientific-systematic approach, supported by centralised post-crisis registries, is essential to enable systematic monitoring, targeted outreach, and evidence-based improvement of psychosocial and psychosomatic care after mass casualty incidents.
