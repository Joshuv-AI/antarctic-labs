Pyloric Gland Adenoma of the Gallbladder Diagnosed by Endoscopic Ultrasound-Guided Biopsy After Partial Cholecystectomy
Authors: Farhan Mohiuddin, Stephanie Eschete, Munraj Singh, Stephen W. Landreneau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pyloric gland adenoma of the gallbladder is a rare benign lesion with malignant potential, often diagnosed postoperatively due to limited imaging specificity. We present a 67-year-old man with previous partial cholecystectomy found to have a suspicious gallbladder remnant lesion on imaging concerning for malignancy. Endoscopic ultrasound identified a 28.4 × 14 mm intraluminal mass, and fine-needle biopsy via a transduodenal approach confirmed pyloric gland adenoma with low-grade dysplasia. This case highlights the diagnostic utility of endoscopic ultrasound-guided biopsy in gallbladder lesions and emphasizes procedural considerations, including bile leak risk, while aiding surgical planning for lesions with neoplastic potential.

INTRODUCTION

Gallbladder polyps are identified in 5% of the population, with most being benign or incidentally discovered on imaging.1 Pyloric gland adenoma (PGA) of the gallbladder is a rare, benign epithelial tumor characterized by packed mucinous glands resembling gastric pyloric glands.2 Although benign, there is potential risk of dysplasia and malignant transformation which is associated with poor prognosis. Diagnosis is typically made following cholecystectomy as imaging modalities such as computed tomography and transabdominal ultrasound lack specificity; however, endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) offers further resolution with the potential to perform biopsy which improves diagnostic accuracy. We present a case of PGA diagnosed via EUS-guided biopsy, highlighting the role of EUS in diagnosis and procedural considerations including bile leak risk.

CASE REPORT

A 67-year-old man with a past medical history of acute cholecystitis with partial cholecystectomy presented to the emergency department for evaluation of nausea. Eight months prior, he underwent a partial cholecystectomy for pain concerning for acute cholecystitis. This was necessitated by extensive adhesions of the gallbladder to the small bowel and colon, which precluded complete resection.

A computed tomography scan of the abdomen in the emergency department revealed a 4.6 cm heterogeneous structure in the gallbladder fossa, representing either residual gallbladder tissue or postoperative changes. Subsequent magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) without contrast demonstrated cholecystitis of the retained gallbladder neck with a solid/semisolid intraluminal lesion, prompting recommendation for follow-up imaging. At this time, the patient was discharged from the emergency department with antiemetics and close outpatient follow-up.

Repeat MRCP 4 weeks later showed a 2.6 cm intraluminal, mildly enhancing lesion within the gallbladder remnant, demonstrating avid diffusion restriction, raising concern for cholangiocarcinoma. Subcentimeter lymph nodes were noted within the porta hepatis. The patient was referred to gastroenterology for further evaluation. EUS was performed for lesion characterization demonstrating a 28.4 × 14 mm mass confined within the gallbladder lumen (Figure 1). No porta hepatitis lymph nodes were noted on EUS and the previous MRCP findings were attributed to being reactive. Fine-needle biopsy (FNB) was performed using a 22-gauge core needle via a transduodenal approach.

(A) Endoscopic ultrasound confirming a 28.4 × 14 mm mass in the gallbladder confined within the lumen. (B) Image indicating fine-needle biopsy performed using a 22-gauge needle using a transduodenal approach.

Histopathology from the EUS-guided biopsy revealed a PGA with low-grade dysplasia (Figure 2). Multidisciplinary discussion with surgical oncology led to the decision for cholecystectomy. Surgical pathology confirmed PGA with no invasive carcinoma and resection margins negative for dysplasia.

DISCUSSION

Gallbladder polyps are unusual findings after partial cholecystectomy, but early detection and diagnosis could improve surgical planning and outcomes, as the survival rates of gallbladder cancer are low. In patients who are surgical candidates, cholecystectomy should be pursued when polypoid lesions measure over 10 mm, or if cholelithiasis is present, as this correlates with higher rates of malignancy; however, size is not an exclusive determinant of dysplastic or malignant transformation, and other factors may influence surgical decision-making like clinical symptoms, characteristics of the lesion, and the patient's overall health.3

Transabdominal ultrasound is the initial method of evaluating gallbladder polyps, though using EUS has shown to improve diagnostic accuracy of gallbladder adenomas and offer a potential for combination with FNB (EUS-FNB) to further improve specificity and guide decision-making on warranted surgical interventions.4 EUS-FNB provides architecturally preserved tissue cores that enable histologic evaluation and immunohistochemistry, which may be valuable for differentiating benign from malignant gallbladder lesions.4

There are 4 types of gallbladder adenomas: pyloric, intestinal, foveolar, and biliary. The most common subtype of gallbladder adenomas, pyloric-type are identified as polypoid, well-demarcated from the surrounding mucosa, noninvasive epithelial neoplasm, and composed of uniform and closely packed pyloric glands in tubular structures lined by a single layer of cuboidal or low columnar cells.5,6 On histology, there is diffuse MUC6 expression and squamoid morules, which differentiate it from all other adenomas. Of all identified gallbladder adenomas, PGAs have an estimated 27%–29% risk of high-grade dysplasia or carcinoma in situ, the second most common, only to intestinal-type adenomas.6,7

While obtaining tissue samples from gallbladder lesions may be difficult, 1 multicenter retrospective study evaluated EUS-guided FNB for diagnosing gallbladder cancer in 170 patients across 3 hospitals. The procedure demonstrated 83.4% specificity, 100% specificity, and 84.1% accuracy. The procedure was also safe with 4 mild bleeding events and no serious adverse events Lesions that were less than 40 mm, had wall-thickening morphology, fundal location, or lacked liver invasion/metastases were more difficult to sample directly from the gallbladder.8

Cholecystectomy remains the mainstay of treatment of high-risk gallbladder adenomas, however, more extensive surgical resection with complete cholecystectomy, lymphadenectomy, and partial hepatectomy may be required in cases with known or suspected malignancy.6
