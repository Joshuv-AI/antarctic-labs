Elizabeth Chen is elected to the U.S. National Academy of Sciences
Authors: Robert S Krauss
Published: 2026

Elizabeth Chen

Photo credit: Henrik Olund

On April 28, 2026, Elizabeth Chen, a member of the Editorial Board of Skeletal Muscle, was elected to the U.S. National Academy of Sciences in recognition of her work on mechanisms of cell-cell fusion. Elizabeth was raised in Jilin, China and came to the United States to attend graduate school at UCLA. After two years there, she transferred to Stanford University, where she performed PhD thesis work with Bruce Baker on patterning of Drosophila imaginal discs. For postdoctoral training, Elizabeth joined Eric Olson’s lab at University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center (UTSW). It was as a postdoctoral fellow that Elizabeth began her study of myoblast fusion, using development of the Drosophila embryonic body wall musculature as a tractable model system. In the Olson lab, Elizabeth set up a forward mutagenesis screen to identify genes involved in embryonic myoblast fusion [1]. She identified about a dozen loci required for this process and thereby set the stage for her rich contributions to the molecular and cell biology of myoblast fusion and cell-cell fusion in general. In 2004, Elizabeth started her own lab in the Department of Molecular Biology and Genetics at Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine, rising to the rank of Professor. In 2016, she returned to UTSW as Professor of Molecular Biology and Cell Biology. Throughout this time, Elizabeth has maintained her focus on the process of cell-cell fusion, applying the highly rigorous and creative approaches for which she has become known. Here, I summarize some of the work leading to her current recognition.

In the Drosophila embryo, two types of myoblasts contribute to muscle formation: muscle founder cells that seed the formation of distinct muscle fibers and fusion-competent myoblasts (FCMs) that fuse with founder cells and with nascent myofibers produced by initial rounds of fusion [2, 3]. Work performed in the 1990s implicated the small GTPase Rac1 in myoblast fusion in fruit flies [4]. Although Rac1 is a major regulator of the actin cytoskeleton, it has additional functions also. It was not until 2007 that several groups, including Elizabeth’s, identified an unambiguous role for actin dynamics in myoblast fusion [5–8]. Multiple factors whose functions are dedicated to regulation of the actin cytoskeleton proved necessary for formation of dense actin foci at sites of myoblast fusion, and for fusion itself. While muscle founder cells and FCMs are distinct cell types, it was not anticipated that subcellular structures driving membrane fusion would be cell-type specific. However, Elizabeth’s group observed that the actin foci found at fusion sites are present in FCMs, but not in founder cells [9]. The demonstration of this asymmetry proved to be fundamental to the further development of the story.

Strikingly, Elizabeth demonstrated that FCMs utilize the branched actin polymerization machinery to generate finger-like invasive protrusions that extend into the body of the founder cell [9]. However, founder cells are not passive partners in this interaction; rather, FCM protrusions induce a myosin II (MyoII)-dependent mechanosensory response in the founder cells, thereby producing cortical tension and structural resistance in the latter cell; i.e., a mechanically-resistant surface for the protrusion to push against [10]. This interaction, in turn, brings the cell membranes together beyond the distance mediated by cell adhesion molecules and promotes fusion pore formation. The region of asymmetric intercellular interaction between FCMs and founder cells was named by Elizabeth the fusogenic synapse, and she went on to show that similar structures are also formed during myoblast fusion in zebrafish and mice [11, 12]. At the fusogenic synapse, membrane merger between two cells is mediated by fusogens, specialized proteins that directly drive this process by further bringing apposing cell membranes within a critical distance of each other [13]. The identity of the Drospohila myoblast fusogen remains unknown, but a C. elegans fusogen, Eff-1, has been identified, as have two key vertebrate myoblast fusogens: myomaker and myomixer/myomerger/minion [14–17]. Using the non-fusing Drosophila cell line S2, Elizabeth showed that it was possible to reconstitute cell-cell fusion in these cells by providing the elements required for: (1) formation of actin-based invasive protrusions; and (2) a fusogen, in this case Eff-1. Both were required, with protrusions being necessary for fusogen engagement across two apposing cell membranes to induce fusion [18]. It seems likely that myomaker and myomixer/myomerger/minion will prove to have a similar relationship to invasive protrusions as Eff-1 in the reconstituted S2 cell system.

In addition to identifying, and placing into a logical order, a network of actin regulatory proteins in myoblast fusion, Elizabeth has illuminated the biochemical functions of many of these proteins in ways that make sense of their action in fusing and non-fusing cells. For example, she showed that the large GTPase dynamin is a multifilament actin-bundling protein that forms helical structures to generate mechanically stiff actin super-bundles in cellular invasive protrusions [19]. She also demonstrated that WIP (WASP-interacting protein, identified as the product of the solitary gene in her original Drosophila genetic screen [6]) is itself an actin-bundling protein [11].

Elizabeth has used a combination of genetics, cell biology, biochemistry, biophysics, and mathematical modeling to illuminate how dynamic regulation of the F-actin cytoskeleton undergirds cell-cell fusion, a process required not only for skeletal muscle development but for fertilization, bone remodeling, immune responses, and formation of the placenta. In addition to her outstanding achievements in research, she has also been an exemplary member of the cell biology community and is currently President-elect of the American Society for Cell Biology. Please join me in congratulating Elizabeth for her well-earned and well-deserved election to the National Academy of Sciences.
