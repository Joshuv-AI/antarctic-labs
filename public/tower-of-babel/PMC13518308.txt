Simulation of long-term spatio-temporal environmental dynamics using a unified benchmark of neighbor augmenting, LSTM and graph attention models
Authors: Kaveh Karimadini, Sharareh Pourebrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rapid environmental change has increased the need for predicting the long-term geospatial reliably. However, accurately modeling spatio-temporal geospatial dynamics remains challenging Because of the nonlinearities, complex spatial dependency, and external driving factors, it is difficult to predict. In this paper, a comprehensive benchmarking framework is proposed for the comparison of neighborhood-based, graph-based and attention-based spatiotemporal deep learning models, with the same preprocessing, training and testing procedure.. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) models with and without auxiliary variables are compared with hybrid Graph Attention Network–LSTM (GAT–LSTM) models and fully attention-based GAT–Temporal Attention models, with and without a feed-forward (MLP/FFN) block. All models are trained using a unified preprocessing and evaluation pipeline on annual satellite data in Network Common Data Form (NetCDF) from 2000 to 2023, with 2024 reserved as a fully unseen test dataset. Global pixel-wise measures such as R2, RMSE, MAE, MAPE, and correlation are used to evaluate model performance based on performance of vectors and alignment of predicted vectors and reference vectors. . Findings indicate that the LSTM–CA with auxiliary inputs (3 × 3 neighborhood) performs the best and most stable performance (R2 ≈ 0.95), highlighting the importance of the integrated Cellular Automata (CA) structure and auxiliary driving factors. The GAT–Temporal Attention model with an MLP block ranks second, while removing the MLP or using hybrid LSTM–GAT configurations lead to unstable or degraded performance. Index-wise analysis shows that vegetation and water-related indices are more predictable. The results indicate that strong temporal modeling of information combined with auxiliary information is more important than complexity of spatial attention. The main novelty of this paper is that it does not introduce a new model for a neural network, instead it proposes a comparative engineering experiment to assess the conditions where neighborhood-based temporal models could be superior to graph-attention models in geospatial long-range prediction applications.Electronic supplementary materialThe online version of this article (10.1038/s41598-026-56762-5) contains supplementary material, which is available to authorized users.

Introduction

Spatio-temporal prediction plays a central role in a wide range of applications, including transportation systems1, air pollution and weather monitoring2,3, land-use planning4,5, agricultural productivity assessment (Anikó6), smart city and urban development ,, environmental management7, and public health 8. In these domains, reliable forecasting supports proactive planning, risk mitigation, and more efficient allocation of resources9. However, learning the joint evolution of data across space and time remains a challenging task due to the complex, nonlinear, and non-stationary nature of real-world processes, as well as the rapidly growing volume and heterogeneity of data generated by sensors and satellite observations. As noted by Kumar et al.9, joint spatio-temporal prediction requires modeling tightly coupled dependencies across both dimensions while handling high-dimensional and heterogeneous data sources.

Traditional probabilistic and shallow machine learning models often struggle to capture such complexities, particularly when faced with strong nonlinearities, non-stationarity, and long-range dependencies. These limitations have motivated the increasing adoption of deep learning approaches, which offer greater representational capacity and flexibility for modeling complex spatio-temporal dynamics.

Advances and limitations in recent spatio-temporal models

Since around 2021, deep learning has become a dominant paradigm in spatio-temporal forecasting. Hybrid architectures that combine Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) with sequential models such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) (Smagulova et al.10) networks have gained popularity, especially in traffic and mobility applications. For example, Simini et al.11 introduced the Deep Gravity model for predicting human mobility flows by leveraging geographic features and deep neural networks, achieving improved accuracy over classical gravity models and better generalization to data-scarce regions.

In parallel, Transformer-based models have had a major impact on time-series modeling. The Informer model12 introduced a sparse self-attention mechanism to address the quadratic complexity of standard Transformers, enabling the modeling of long-range temporal dependencies in large-scale datasets. These ideas have since been extended to spatio-temporal contexts, including air quality forecasting (Liang et al.13) and global weather prediction14,15.

Despite these advances, important limitations remain. Zhou et al.16 reported that fully attention-based spatio-temporal models, such as GMAN17, can produce overly diffuse and uninformative attention maps, suggesting that explicit spatial structure must be incorporated into model architectures. At the same time, large-scale models such as FourCastNet15 and GraphCast14 have demonstrated the remarkable potential of data-driven approaches for high-resolution weather forecasting, but they also highlight the growing complexity and computational demands of modern spatio-temporal models. Pathak et al.15 unveiled FourCastNet, a global forecasting model using adaptive Fourier neural operators. FourCastNet matched the precision of the European Centre’s state-of-the-art numerical weather prediction model over small scales and even surpassed it in more complex phenomena like precipitation, in about under 2 s of real-time data, making the week-long prediction that took five orders of magnitude more time using conventional models. The data-driven emulator demonstrated how AI models can be more efficient. For example, by the year 2023, spatio-temporal prediction technologies had branched out into different scales of applicability, with some models utilizing the hybrid architecture of transformers and GNNs. In air quality prediction, for instance, the study by Liang et al.13 proposed a novel air quality prediction model called AirFormer, characterized by its two-stage transformer architecture. The first stage consists of two self-attention modules for effective spatio-temporal feature learning. The model also incorporates latent variables for addressing uncertainty associated with air pollution data. With data sampled over 4 years and more than 1,000 monitoring stations, this model realizes a 5–8% prediction error reduction compared to the state-of-the-art. Concurrently14, took weather forecasting further with the GraphCast model, a multi-scale graph neural network model used in forecasting the weather. GraphCast is characterized as a model of the global atmosphere as a graph where it learns to predict hundreds of weather variables looking ahead 10 days. Notably, it has beaten the world’s best 10-day forecast system in 90% of 1,380 verification metrics, particularly in forecasting phenomena such as tropical cyclones or heatwaves. The main benefit of Graph Attention Network (GAT) (Veliˇckovi´c et al.18) over Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) is that GAT is anisotropic, meaning it learns edge-specific attention weights and can assign different importance to different neighbors. In contrast, GCN is isotropic, applying the same transformation uniformly across neighbors19, which limits its ability to model asymmetric or context-dependent relationships.

Emerging trends also suggest that spatial–temporal forecasting is increasingly adopting specialized architectural approaches for multi-scale dependency learning, missing data resilience, and non-stationary temporal dynamics. For instance, MGSFformer20,21 implements multi-granularity spatio-temporal fusion in air quality prediction, underscoring the relevance of modeling dependencies across multiple spatial and temporal resolutions. On the other hand, GinAR + 20,21 solves the problem of multivariate time-series forecasting in scenarios involving missing values, which is particularly pertinent in remote sensing, given the frequent presence of clouds that may obstruct satellite images. Finally, MetaGNSDformer22 integrates meta-learning, gated non-stationary dynamics, and frequency-aware attention to solve point-interval prediction, indicating an emerging focus on models capable of handling distribution shift and non-stationary temporal dynamics. While these papers differ in their application areas, they all emphasize the significance of benchmarking tools that can test models on accuracy, robustness, missing data handling, and generalization to novel environmental conditions. Lastly, heterogenous ensemble surrogate-assisted optimization techniques, as seen in lithium-ion battery charging strategy optimization, are part of the wider trend of integrating different model families to enhance robustness against nonlinear system dynamics23.

Hybrid architectures for spatio-temporal modeling: progress and limitations

Recent years have seen increasing interest in hybrid architectures that integrate signal processing, graph-based learning, and recurrent or attention-based temporal modeling. For instance, Wang et al.24 proposed a VMD–GAT–BiLSTM model, combining Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD), Graph Attention Networks (GAT), and Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM), for urban air quality forecasting, achieving substantial reductions in MAE and notable improvements in R2 across multiple cities. In traffic forecasting, Ma et al.25 introduced the spatio-temporal traffic large model (STTLM), demonstrating the benefits of Transformer-based pretraining with contextual information.

Beyond conventional sensor data, novel modalities have also been explored. Xu et al.26 proposed Imagery2Flow, which predicts human mobility flows directly from satellite imagery using a combination of computer vision and graph learning. In the seismic domain, Leema et al.27 introduced SeismoQuakeGNN, a hybrid GNN–Transformer framework that achieved an R2 of 0.85 for earthquake magnitude prediction, illustrating the growing maturity of hybrid spatio-temporal modeling approaches.

In the specific context of land use/cover change (LUCC) modeling, hybrid approaches combining Cellular Automata (CA) and LSTM have shown promising results. Rezaie et al.7 demonstrated that an LSTM–CA model with class weighting can effectively address data imbalance and achieve high classification accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, significantly outperforming traditional Markov-based methods. This highlights the complementary roles of CA in representing local spatial neighborhood interactions and LSTM in capturing temporally informed transition rules. More recently, Li et al.28 explored the integration of Transformers with CA for dynamic LUCC simulation, pointing to the underexplored potential of attention mechanisms in this domain. Fan et al.29,30,31 further proposed ExHAGN, a dynamic heterogeneous graph framework that models human activities and land-use changes jointly, achieving consistent improvements over state-of-the-art statistical and deep learning baselines.

However, Spatio-temporal modeling is inherently affected by both model-form and parametric uncertainties. The nonlinear and non-stationary nature of geospatial processes makes the selection of an optimal architecture difficult, and different modeling assumptions or hyperparameter choices can lead to substantially different predictions. This often results in ensemble divergence, where multiple models produce conflicting outcomes for the same scenario.

Although a wide range of statistical, machine learning, and deep learning techniques have been proposed, most studies focus on introducing new architecture rather than systematically comparing fundamentally different modeling paradigms under a common evaluation framework, particularly for long-term generalization to a fully unseen future time step.

Research gap and motivation

Two main gaps can be identified in current literature. First, there is a lack of engineering-level comparative studies that evaluate neighborhood-based models such as neighborhood augmented LSTM against graph-based attention models under a unified data preprocessing, training, and testing pipeline. Second, most existing studies emphasize short-term accuracy or application-specific performance, while long-term generalization to an entirely unseen future year remains underexplored. This limitation is particularly critical for engineering applications, where forecasts are intended to support forward-looking planning and decision-making.

Addressing these gaps requires evaluation frameworks that (i) explicitly capture both local spatial interactions and long-range spatial dependencies, (ii) model temporal dynamics using recurrence and/or attention mechanisms, and (iii) assess predictive performance, under (i) and (ii), on a truly unseen future period. This research paper describes comparative experiments carried out under controlled conditions, as opposed to research work which is predominantly aimed at the design of new architecture. The novelty of this work is that different spatio-temporal modelling strategies are applied and experimented under similar conditions: NA-LSTM (Neighborhood Augmented Long Short Term Memory), Graph Attention Recurrent Model, Fully Attention-Based Temporal Model.

Objective and role of the proposed methods

Motivated by these considerations, the main objective of this study is to provide a controlled benchmarking framework that systematically compares neighborhood-augmented LSTM forecasting and graph-attention-based architectures for long-term spatio-temporal geospatial forecasting. Materials and methods

The proposed framework comprises (i) harmonized spatio-temporal data preprocessing, (ii) spatial feature construction using either cellular automata (CA) neighborhood encoding or graph attention mechanisms, (iii) temporal modeling via LSTM or multi-head self-attention, (iv) supervised one-step-ahead training with the Huber loss, and (v) post-processing and vectorized multi-band evaluation. This unified pipeline ensures a consistent and fair comparison across all model families. Study area

The study was conducted in the Klang River Basin, located in the western region of Peninsular Malaysia (Fig. 2). The basin covers approximately 127,326 hectares and includes major parts of the Klang Valley, including Kuala Lumpur and large areas of Selangor. As Malaysia’s main administrative, commercial, and financial region, the basin is highly urbanized and undergone rapid land use changes over recent decades. These changes have altered hydrological processes, surface temperature patterns, ecosystem services, and overall environmental quality, making the basin a suitable testbed for long-term spatio-temporal forecasting under strong anthropogenic pressure.Fig. 2Study area: Klang River Basin, Peninsular Malaysia.

Data preprocessing

All geospatial inputs were resampled and aligned to a common longitude–latitude grid with consistent spatial resolution and extent forming a regular grid of size H× W, ensuring one-to-one correspondence across satellite bands, indices, and auxiliary variables. All datasets were organized into annual steps. Temporal consistency was enforced such that each grid cell maintained a continuous yearly record. Years with incomplete coverage were excluded to form a balanced spatio-temporal tensor suitable for sequence learning.

To support neighborhood-based modeling, particularly in the NA-LSTM framework, border cells were explicitly handled to ensure that every cell possessed a complete neighborhood context. The study area was treated as a closed square grid to avoid edge bias and ensures consistent spatial dependency modeling across the domain.

All undefined, missing, or invalid pixel values arising from sensor gaps, cloud contamination, or preprocessing artifacts were replaced with zeros prior to normalization. This strategy helps to avoid propagation of NaN and to keep the numbers stable during convolutional and graph-based message passing. After checking that it was not a significant proportion of the data and that no systematic spatial bias is introduced by the absence, zero-filling was selected as a neutral placeholder.

Each annual snapshot was structured into multi-channel tensors, where channels correspond to spectral bands and auxiliary variables. For NA-LSTM models, spatial neighborhoods were later unfolded into feature vectors, while for graph-based models, the same gridded data were converted into node-feature matrices.

Importantly, all models shared the same prepared input data, differing only in how spatial and temporal relationships were encoded. This common data preparation pipeline ensures that performance differences arise from model architecture rather than data inconsistencies.

Static variables (DEM, slope, and distance-to-road/river rasters) were evaluated to avoid over-parameterization and reduce weak predictors. The association between each factor and the target spatial pattern was quantified using Cramer’s V in IDRISI TerrSet. Following Eastman (2009), predictors with Cramer’s V ≥ 0.40 were considered strong and retained, predictors with V < 0.15 were excluded, and intermediate values were evaluated based on interpretability and spatial relevance. Only retained variables were included in the “with auxiliary” configurations.

Temporal robustness validation

Apart from the last holdout transition for 2023 → 2024, a second method called “rolling origin” was adopted for the temporal validation, in order to find the stability of the rankings. Here the models are evaluated with the data of the next year, after training the models on the data up to year t. The tested transitions include 2018 → 2019, 2019 → 2020, 2020 → 2021, and 2023 → 2024. The last transition, 2023 → 2024, continued being the focus, while the rest were used in measuring stability of conclusions made. Uncertainty quantification through repeated training

The models were trained with five separate random seeds to estimate prediction uncertainty and training stability. The entire training process was repeated for each seed with the same train–test split and hyperparameter settings. This last reported performance was mentioned as the mean and standard deviation of R2 for test case 2023 → 2024 across 5 runs. This way one can get an empirical estimation of the uncertainty of the model related to random initialization and stochastic optimization.

Robustness assessment under noise and multi-step input forecasting

To assess the robustness of the system under degraded inputs, the normalized 2023 input was purposely perturbed before the 2024 prediction was made. The normalized values of the channels of the input were perturbed with the addition of gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.01, 0.03, and 0.05 and then the values were clipped between 0 and 1. Additionally, a 2 step input frame was provided to every year, for the prediction of the next year evaluated for the test hold out of 2023 → 2024. The performance of the model was tested against 2024 (no perturbation) data for each perturbation scenario to assess the model performance with R2.

Final forecast and performance evaluation

Final forecast generation

Applying the model fθ to the 2023 inputs produces the 2024 spatial forecast:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{x}_{2024} = f\theta \left( {\tilde{x}2023} \right)$$\end{document}

Performance was quantified using Mean Absolute Error (MAE) across all spatial locations and depth layers.

Performance evaluation and vectorization strategy

To ensure a consistent and comprehensive evaluation of model performance, all predicted and reference geospatial outputs for the year 2024 were processed in a vectorized form prior to metric computation. Specifically, for each spectral band and derived index, the two-dimensional raster output (H × W) was flattened into a one-dimensional vector. The flattened vectors corresponding to all bands were then concatenated to form a single unified prediction vector. The same procedure was applied to the ground-truth dataset (Landsat2024.nc), resulting in a corresponding reference vector.

Pixels with missing or undefined values (e.g., NaN or masked values) were removed from both the prediction and reference vectors to ensure equal length and one-to-one correspondence between samples. Performance metrics, including R2, RMSE, MAE, MAPE, and correlation, were then computed between these two aligned vectors. This strategy enables a global, pixel-wise evaluation across all bands and spatial locations, providing a robust and unbiased assessment of overall predictive performance.

Clear performance differences were observed across model families. The LSTM with auxiliary inputs (T = 1) achieved the highest overall accuracy, with consistently strong performance across most indices and an average R2 of approximately 0.95. The LSTM with auxiliary inputs consistently outperformed all other models in terms of RMSE, MAE, correlation, and R2. The GAT–Temporal Attention model with MLP showed competitive performance, particularly for vegetation- and water-related indices, but remained inferior to the LSTM with auxiliary inputs. The negative R2 values for some model–index combinations should be considered as a sign of weak predictive generalization, and not as an error in the calculations. When the prediction error is greater than the error from the average value of the observed test values, the value of R2 is negative. For this study, the temporal variation or value range was limited for some of the indicators during the unseen test period. In such a case, moderate prediction errors could result in R2 of a very negative value. Furthermore, various auxiliary indices don’t react in the same way to land surface changes over time. The temporal evolution of some indices may not be correlated to the temporal evolution of the target year, but they may contain useful environmental information for the model. Shared spectral or environmental information may also be reflected in similar metrics across some indices, such as NDVI, SAVI, and GNDVI, are all related to the condition of the vegetation; and other indices may respond similarly to moisture, built-up intensity or surface dryness. Thus, such values were kept as diagnostic information on the instability of the model and the difficulty of predicting the index.

Ablation and architectural comparisons

The ablation effects discussed above are quantitatively summarized in Figs. 4, 5, 6, and 7, which reveal consistent performance differences attributable to the inclusion of auxiliary inputs, the depth of spatial attention, and the presence of the MLP/FFN block.

Effect of auxiliary inputs. Including auxiliary variables led to a substantial improvement in performance. The LSTM with auxiliary inputs achieved an average R2 of approximately 0.95, whereas the same architecture without auxiliary inputs achieved an average R2 of approximately 0.79, highlighting the importance of external driving factors for long-term forecasting.

Effect of spatial attention depth. For the 2023 → 2024 holdout test, S-GAT = 1 outperformed S-GAT = 2; however, the rolling-origin validation showed that S-GAT = 2 achieved better average temporal robustness across multiple test years. This suggests that shallower spatial attention may be more effective for the specific 2024 transition, whereas the deeper GAT configuration may capture more stable spatial dependencies across different temporal splits.

Effect of the MLP/FFN block in temporal attention. Removing the MLP/FFN block from the temporal attention architecture resulted in a pronounced performance drop, with the average R2 decreasing to approximately 0.34, indicating that the feed-forward component is critical for effective temporal representation learning.

Hybrid LSTM–GAT architectures. The LSTM–GAT models exhibited inconsistent results across configurations. One configuration (S-GAT = 2, T = 1) achieved moderate performance (R2 ≈ 0.61), whereas another (S-GAT = 1, T = 2) showed substantially lower performance (R2 ≈ 0.24), indicating sensitivity to architectural design choices and training stability.

Computational complexity and scalability

An operational geospatial forecasting scenario is one where the number of pixels may be quite large when expanding the spatial resolution or the extent of the study area, so computational scalability is important. The LSTM–CA model adopts a fixed 3 × 3 neighborhood representation; thus its input size increases linearly with the number of pixels and channels. By comparison, graph-based models involve building and fitting graph representations of the nodes and edges. The number of nodes is H × W (1825, 1867) and the number of edges is about 4HW (excluding boundary effects) for a regular grid with four-neighbor connectivity. GAT-based models thus add extra memory and computational cost in computing attention weights for graph edges. There are also attention operations across temporal steps which are a requirement for temporal attention models, but which are not so expensive in the current study as the length of the annual sequence is relatively short.

The result of the trainable parameter counts demonstrate that the NA-LSTM models are significantly simpler than the GAT–Temporal Attention models. The NA-LSTM model can be trained with just 16,485 trainable parameters, while the GAT–Temporal Attention configurations require 55,301–59,269 parameters. Even with that lower capacity, it was able to get the highest average rolling-origin R2. Metric behavior and outliers

MAPE values were found to be highly sensitive for indices with values near zero, leading to inflated percentage errors in some cases. In addition, negative R2 values were observed for certain model–index combinations, indicating performance worse than a mean-value baseline. These negative R2 values should be interpreted as evidence of weak predictive generalization rather than calculation errors. R2 becomes negative when the prediction error is larger than the error obtained by using the mean of the observed test values as a baseline. In this study, some indices had limited temporal variation or a narrow value range during the unseen test period. Under such conditions, even moderate prediction errors can lead to large negative R2 values.

Not all auxiliary indices respond equally to land-surface change over time. Some indices provide useful environmental information for the model, but their temporal behavior may not always be strongly linked to the observed changes in the target year. Similar metrics across some indices may also reflect shared spectral or environmental information, as indices such as NDVI, SAVI, and GNDVI are all related to vegetation conditions, while other indices may respond similarly to moisture, built-up intensity, or surface dryness. Therefore, these values were retained as diagnostic evidence of model instability and index-specific prediction difficulty.

These effects highlight the importance of interpreting MAPE and R2 jointly with MAE, RMSE, and correlation. Discussion

Temporal context and auxiliary information

The better performance of the LSTM model with the addition of the auxiliary inputs confirms that the long-term geospatial dynamics are controlled by both multi-year temporal relationships and the exogenous inputs. The performance difference between the LSTM model with and without the use of auxiliary variables suggests that using only the spectral history is not enough to capture all the inter-annual variability. Rather, static and dynamic covariates offer complementary information which increases the robustness of predictions and contributes to better generalization to future conditions that are not seen during the training process. The results provide evidence in favor of the idea that a model incorporating temporal memory and context, not just autoregressive spectral patterns, is essential for reliable long-term forecasting of the environment.

Effectiveness and limitations of spatial attention

One way to understand the performance gain of the NA-LSTM model is the model’s inductive bias. Specifically, the assumption embedded into the architecture of the 3 × 3 neighborhood-augmented input ensures that there is a strong local-spatial assumption incorporated into the model architecture. This assumption is quite similar to the local dynamics of environmental processes, where adjacent pixels have similar land cover, water and vegetation features. Unlike graph attention models, which need to learn spatial weights from a small number of samples per year, the neighborhood-augmented LSTM model gets local spatial information directly. The latter learns annual time-series dynamics, while external environmental effects are learned through auxiliary features. This generally implies that the model will have a lower learning load as it is not required to learn all the assumptions from scratch.

Spatial attention mechanisms can improve the performance of predictive modeling when suitably designed, as the results show that the GAT–Temporal Attention model with an MLP block outperforms the others. The modest improvements over the LSTM with auxiliary inputs, however, imply that, at least for the current data and forecasting duration, temporal dynamics and auxiliary drivers are more important than complex spatial message passing.

Moreover, the performance drop as the spatial GAT depth increased suggest two potential factors. The first issue is that repeated graph-attention aggregation may smooth the representation of neighboring nodes, which can diminish the local contrast between heterogeneous land-cover or environment states. The first problem is that repeated graph-attention aggregation can smooth the representation of neighboring nodes and decrease local contrast between heterogeneous land-cover or environmental states. Second, the temporal sample size is small as the data is annual, and deeper spatial-attention models will be more difficult to reliably optimize.

The significance of the MLP/FFN block in attention-based models

The significant performance gap between temporal attention models with and without the MLP/FFN block demonstrates the importance of nonlinear feature transformation in attention networks. Without this, the model performs less well in projective temporal learning in the target space and suffers from underfitting and poor generalization.

This is aligned with the standard designs of Transformer models, where the feed-forward network is built into the core of representation learning and plays a critical role for attaining the desired model expressiveness.

Hybrid LSTM–GAT architectures and model stability

The instability in LSTM–GAT models may also be caused by the sequential interaction of two complex blocks, with LSTM capturing temporal transitions from these transformed representations, and graph attention capturing spatial information in a graph. The mistakes made in the encoding of spatial information can be injected into the temporal learning block, where they can be susceptible to initializations and depth of yearly satellite data with short time series.

The LSTM–GAT hybrid models performed inconsistently and often sub-optimally, suggesting that using a spatial attention extractor alongside the temporal model of LSTM layers is not sufficient to achieve good forecasting performance. These models are sensitive to the architectural depth and layer ordering, making them more challenging to optimize and harder to be trained in some situations with more instability, especially for long term extrapolation scenarios. In comparison, simpler and better-conditioned architectures, such as the LSTM with auxiliary inputs, exhibit more stable, reliable and interpretable performance in the current forecasting problem.

Index-specific challenges and metric behavior

The reduced predictive performance observed for EVI and BSI across several models suggests that these indices are more sensitive to noise, scaling effects, or complex nonlinear dynamics. This underscores the importance of index-wise evaluation rather than relying exclusively on aggregated performance metrics. Moreover, MAPE was also determined as being less reliable in the case of indices with values near zero because the absolute error is quite significant in comparison with the percentage error. In this context, therefore, it is more meaningful to examine the predictive performance of models as reflected in MAE, RMSE, correlation, and R2.

Lastly, some negative values for R2 in some configurations shed light on the fact that the measures need to be calculated consistently and interpreted carefully, as R2 values can be moderately high and show worse performance than a mean-value baseline.

This study does not conduct complete spatial-zone-wise validation by land-use class, elevation zone or ecological subregion, although it does carry out index-wise evaluation and spatial error diagnostics (prediction differences and error dispersion). Future studies should be carried out in these spatial zones to further determine the strengths and weaknesses of the models in the geographic areas.

Overfitting risk and limited temporal samples

There are relatively few time training transitions possible with consisting, as it is only observed annually from 2000 to 2024. This poses a risk of overfitting, especially for models that have high capacity like graph-attention and temporal-attention models. Although there will be numerous samples within the spatial dimension because of the large number of examples available that describe the interaction at a pixel level, these examples are not completely independent because of the spatial autocorrelation between them. Therefore, with respect to the model architecture and errors in the spatial domain, good performance on the left-out year should also be viewed that way. Several regularization techniques were employed during training and evaluation to avoid overfitting, dropout, early stopping, Huber loss and held-out unseen-year test. Also rolling cross-validation was conducted to test the generalization of the architecture performance.

Conclusion

This study proposed a unified benchmarking framework for one-step-ahead long-term spatio-temporal geospatial forecasting using multi-band satellite data. Using a harmonized preprocessing pipeline, consistent train–test splitting (2000–2023 for training and 2024 as a fully unseen test year), and vectorized pixel-wise evaluation, we compared neighborhood-based NA-LSTM models with and without auxiliary variables against graph-attention-based architectures, including GAT–LSTM hybrids and GAT–Temporal Attention models with ablation of the MLP/FFN block. The vectorized pixel-wise evaluation was retained as the primary benchmark because it provides a consistent basis for comparing all model configurations across the full study area and all output indices under the same testing condition.

Across all indices and metrics, the NA-LSTM model augmented with auxiliary inputs achieved the best and most stable performance (average R2 ≈ 0.95), demonstrating that robust temporal modeling combined with external driving factors is more critical for long-term generalization than increasing spatial attention complexity. The results show that the effect of GAT depth depends on the evaluation setting: S-GAT = 1 performed better on the 2024 holdout test, whereas S-GAT = 2 achieved the second-highest average R2 under rolling-origin validation.. Removing the MLP/FFN from the temporal attention architecture caused a pronounced drop in predictive skill, confirming that the feed-forward component is essential for effective representation learning in attention-based models.

Index-wise analysis further showed that vegetation- and water-related indices (e.g., NDVI, NDWI, MNDWI, SAVI) are consistently more predictable than EVI and BSI, which exhibited higher variability and sensitivity to scaling and near-zero values. In addition, MAPE was found to be unreliable for indices close to zero, highlighting the importance of interpreting percentage-based errors together with MAE, RMSE, correlation, and R2.

Future work should extend this benchmark in five directions. First, multi-step and long-horizon forecasting should be evaluated to quantify error accumulation over multiple future years. Second, uncertainty quantification methods such as Monte Carlo dropout, deep ensembles, or quantile prediction should be incorporated to assess forecast reliability. Third, the framework should be tested across multiple geographic regions and environmental contexts to evaluate spatial transferability The rolling-origin validation provides evidence of temporal robustness, the analysis of this study is still limited to one geographic region. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as region-specific rather than fully generalized across all environmental contexts. Future studies should extend the same benchmarking framework to regions with different climatic, topographic, land-use, and ecological characteristics to further test the spatial transferability of the models. Fourth, self-supervised and foundation-model approaches for Earth observation could be explored to improve representation learning when labeled temporal samples are limited. Finally, explainable AI methods, including attention-map inspection, feature attribution, and spatial error diagnostics, should be used to improve interpretability and support decision-making in environmental monitoring.
