BCKDK protects against obesity-induced cardiac remodelling and dysfunction by alleviating mitochondrial oxidative stress and ROS-driven MAPK signalling
Authors: Yu Liu, Ming Ma, Yan Guo, Huan Wang, Yufan Xiao, Yang Xu, Zhensheng Cai, Qianqian Wang, Wenfeng Zhou, Yujia Xiao, Kai Atlass, Hailong Cao, Yuyu Yao, Zilin Sun, Yi Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsImpaired branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) catabolism has been implicated in obesity cardiomyopathy (OCM), and systemic inhibition of branched-chain ketoacid dehydrogenase kinase (BCKDK), a key negative regulator of BCAA oxidation, improves cardiac function. However, whether cardiomyocyte-specific manipulation of BCAA catabolism is sufficient to confer cardioprotection remains unknown.Methods and resultsCardiomyocyte-specific BCKDK knockout and overexpression mouse models were generated and subjected to high-fat diet feeding, followed by echocardiography, transcriptomic, metabolomic, and molecular analyses. The mechanistic findings were further validated using in vitro experiments. Despite reduced myocardial BCAA levels, cardiomyocyte-specific BCKDK deletion unexpectedly exacerbated cardiac dysfunction and ventricular remodelling in OCM. Consistently, cardiac BCKDK expression was reduced in OCM. In contrast, cardiomyocyte-specific BCKDK overexpression improved cardiac function and remodelling, accompanied by a further reduction in myocardial BCAA levels, attenuation of mitochondrial oxidative stress, and suppression of MAPK-driven inflammatory signalling. Mechanistically, BCKDK reprogrammed mitochondrial metabolism to restrain oxidative stress. Moreover, mitochondrial ROS scavenging with MitoTEMPO alleviated mitochondrial dysfunction, and reversed the MAPK activation induced by BCKDK deficiency in vitro.ConclusionThese findings reveal an unexpected BCAA-independent role of BCKDK in preserving cardiomyocyte mitochondrial function and restraining inflammatory signalling in OCM. Our study identifies cardiomyocyte-intrinsic BCKDK as a potential therapeutic target, while cautioning against overestimating the cardioprotective effects of systemic BCKDK inhibition, which may be driven primarily by extracardiac mechanisms.

Introduction

With the improvement of living conditions, obesity has become a global epidemic, with its prevalence continuing to rise across all age groups and regions [1,2]. Obesity contributes to multiple cardiovascular complications, including obesity cardiomyopathy (OCM). OCM refers to myocardial structural and functional abnormalities caused by obesity, occurring independent of ischemic heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, and other heart diseases. Cardiac remodelling in OCM is usually characterized by ventricular hypertrophy, and myocardial fibrosis, leading to diastolic dysfunction, that initially progresses to obese heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. To-date, multiple mechanisms, including lipid accumulation, oxidative stress, inflammation, autophagic defects, and mitochondrial dysfunction, have been proposed in the pathogenesis of OCM [4]. Nonetheless, precise mechanisms for the aetiology of OCM remain insufficiently elucidated, and myocardial alterations often remain subclinical for years [7], thereby hindering the development of effective therapeutic strategies.

Reduced cardiac branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) catabolism has been reported in isotope-labelled metabolic flux using isolated working hearts from mice with 10-week high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obesity [8]. The BCAAs, including leucine, isoleucine, and valine, are essential amino acids obtained exclusively from the diet. Their oxidative catabolism occurs within the mitochondria, and begins with a reversible transamination catalysed by mitochondrial branched-chain aminotransferase (BCAT2), producing branched-chain α-keto acids (BCKAs). These BCKAs then undergo irreversible oxidative decarboxylation by the branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase (BCKDH) complex. After multiple additional reactions, the BCAA oxidation pathway can terminate at the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, or earlier during the formation of intermediates that have paracrine signalling activity, such as 3-hydroxyisobutyrate (3-HIB), or intermediates that are used for the generation of monomethyl branched-chain fatty acids. BCKDH kinase (BCKDK) phosphorylates the E1α subunit of BCKDH (BCKDHA) to inhibit the activity of BCKDH complex. Conversely, protein phosphatase Mn2+-dependent 1K (PPM1K) removes BCKDHA phosphorylation to promote BCKDH complex activity [9]. In the HFD combined with N[w]-nitro-l-arginine methyl ester (l-NAME)-induced HFpEF model, cardiac BCAA levels were found to be elevated, and both global BCKDK knockout and pharmacological enhancement of systemic BCAA catabolism using BT2 (3,6-dichlorobenzo[b]thiophene-2-carboxylic acid), a highly selective inhibitor of BCKDK, effectively improved cardiac function and attenuated pathological remodelling [10]. These findings led us to hypothesize that restoring BCAA catabolic flux in the heart via selectively deleting BCKDK in cardiomyocytes might similarly alleviate functional impairment and pathological remodelling in OCM.

In the present study, we generated cardiomyocyte-specific BCKDK knockout mice to enhance BCAA catabolism, and found that, contrary to expectations, loss of BCKDK in cardiomyocytes exacerbated obesity-induced cardiac dysfunction and remodelling. Further analysis revealed that BCKDK expression is reduced in cardiomyocytes under OCM condition. Restoring BCKDK levels through cardiomyocyte-specific overexpression markedly attenuated cardiac dysfunction and remodelling. Multi-omics profiling, together with in vivo and in vitro validation, demonstrated that these protective effects are mediated through improved mitochondrial respiratory function and suppression of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)-dependent inflammatory response. These findings highlight cardiomyocyte-specific BCKDK as a promising therapeutic target in OCM, while cautioning that systemic BT2 treatment, which suppresses cardiac BCKDK expression [11], may introduce unanticipated adverse cardiac effects and that its apparent cardioprotective benefits may largely derive from systemic mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Animals

Animal studies were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Southeast University (No.20240930002, Nanjing, China). To avoid the interference of hormonal change, only male mice were used in this study. Wildtype C57BL/6J mice were obtained from GemPharmatech (Jiangsu, China). All mice were maintained with controlled environmental conditions of temperature (22 ± 0.5 °C), humidity (60 ± 5%) and lighting (12 h light/12 h dark cycle) and had free access to either normal diet (ND, 10 kcal% fat, Research Diets, D12450J) or high fat diet (HFD, 60 kcal% fat, Research Diets, D12492) and water. All HFD-fed mice achieved ≥ 20% body weight gain within 8 weeks, and no animals were excluded. There were no exclusions among mice fed the ND.

For each mouse models, mouse body weight (BW) was recorded every two weeks. Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) and intraperitoneal insulin tolerance test (IPITT) was performed one week before euthanasia. For IPGTT, mice were fasted for 16 h and subsequently intraperitoneally injected with d-glucose (1 g/kg body weight; Sigma, G7021, USA). For IPITT, mice were fasted for 5 h and subsequently intraperitoneally injected with insulin (0.75 U/kg body weight; Solarbio, IR14902, China). Blood glucose levels from the tail vein were assessed using a glucometer (Bayer Contour® Plus glucometer, Switzerland) at 0, 15, 30, 60, and 120 min post-injection. At the end of the study, mice were anesthetized with 1-2% isoflurane, blood was collected from the retro-orbital sinus, followed by euthanasia via cervical dislocation under anaesthesia. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The right tibia length (TL) and heart weight (HW) were measured at the same time. Hearts were immediately dissected and rinsed three times in pre-cooled PBS. The hearts were gently blotted dry with paper towels, weighed, then divided, and were either fixed or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for further analysis. Blood sample was centrifugated at 3000 ×g for 15 min to get serum. The serum was stored at −80 °C before analysis. The blood triglyceride (TG), cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) were measured using an automatic biochemical analyser (Chemray 800).

From 8 weeks of age, mice were subjected to a 28-week dietary intervention and randomly divided into ND and HFD groups. In one cohort, mice were euthanized at week 28 of HFD feeding, with seven mice in the ND group and nine mice in the HFD group used for terminal analyses. Cell culture and treatments

Cardiomyocytes were isolated from 1- to 3-day-old C57BL/6J mice. Neonatal mice were rapidly rinsed with 75% ethanol for surface sterilization and euthanized by decapitation using sterile scissors. The chest was then opened along the sternum, and the ventricular myocardium was rapidly harvested and minced. We then used multiple rounds of digestion with 0.6 mg/mL pancreatin (Sigma, P3292, USA) and 0.4 mg/mL collagenase II (Worthington, WLS004176, USA) prepared in ADS buffer. After each digestion, the supernatant was collected and immediately quenched with horse serum. The pooled cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 ×g for 5 min, after which the supernatant was discarded and the cell pellet was resuspended in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). After 45 min of incubation, a portion of fibroblasts was removed from the suspension; Percoll liquid (Cytiva, 17089101, USA) stratification and centrifugation (1600 ×g, slow rise and fall, 40 min) were used to isolate cardiomyocytes from other cells. By removing fibroblasts from the upper layer and blood cells from the bottom, only cardiomyocytes from the middle layer were collected and cultured. After 12 h of culture, cardiomyocytes were washed with PBS to remove non-adherent cells and subsequently transfected. After 8 h of serum starvation, cardiomyocytes and fibroblasts were cultured in standard medium (BSA, 5.5 mM d-glucose, 200 μM BSA) or palmitic acid medium (PA, 5.5 mM d-glucose, 200 μM PA [Sigma, P0500, USA]) for 40 h. Adenoviral transfection was performed at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 50 for 8 h, after which the medium was replaced with standard medium (5.5 mM d-glucose) for 40 h. Adenovirus expressing mouse BCKDK gene (AD-BCKDK) and empty control adenoviruses (AD-CON) were generated by OBiO Technology (Shanghai, China).

Rat H9c2 cardiomyoblasts (Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology, China) were maintained within DMEM that include 10% FBS. Adenoviral transfection was performed at a MOI of 50 for 8 h, after which the medium was replaced with standard medium (BSA, 5.5 mM d-glucose, 200 μM BSA) or palmitic acid medium (PA, 5.5 mM d-glucose, 200 μM PA) for 40 h. Adenovirus expressing rat BCKDK gene (AD-BCKDK), adenovirus expressing rat BCKDK small hairpin RNA (AD-shBCKDK), and their corresponding empty control adenoviruses (AD-CON and AD-shNC) were generated by OBiO Technology (Shanghai, China). The top and bottom strand sequences of AD-shBCKDK are shown below: TCGAGGTCCGCTACTTCCTGGATAAATTCAAGAGATTTATCCAGGAAGTAGCGGACTTTTTTA and AGCTTAAAAAAGTCCGCTACTTCCTGGATAAATCTCTTGAATTTATCCAGGAAGTAGCGGAC.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (v.10.0). All quantification graphs are presented as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). Statistical tests and sample sizes are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Investigators were blinded during histological and immunofluorescence quantification. For continuous variables, two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test was used for comparison between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons for more than two groups. For categorical variables, the Fisher's exact test was performed when the total number was under 40. A value of probability (p) < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

1.Obesity-Induced Cardiac Dysfunction Is Associated with Impaired BCAA Catabolism.

1.Obesity-Induced Cardiac Dysfunction Is Associated with Impaired BCAA Catabolism.

Obesity-Induced Cardiac Dysfunction Is Associated with Impaired BCAA Catabolism.

Wild-type (WT) C57BL/6J mice were continuously fed with high-fat diet (HFD; 60 kcal% fat) for 28 weeks to induce obesity (Fig. 1A). During the feeding period, HFD-fed mice developed marked obesity (Fig. 1B), impaired systemic glucose clearance as assessed by intraperitoneal glucose tolerance tests (IPGTT) (Fig. 1C), and dysregulated lipid metabolism evidenced by elevated serum triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels (Fig. 1D). Echocardiographic assessment revealed diastolic dysfunction in HFD-fed mice, evidenced by a significant elevation of E/E′ ratio, while left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) remained preserved (Fig. 1E and F). Compared with normal diet (ND) controls, HFD-fed mice exhibited a significant increase in heart weight (HW) and heart weight-to-tibia length ratio (HW/TL) (Fig. 1G and H). These results confirm the successful establishment of an obesity cardiomyopathy (OCM) model characterized by systemic metabolic dysfunction and impaired cardiac diastolic function.

Since previous reports have associated elevated BCAA levels with detrimental cardiac effects [10,12,13], we next investigated whether cardiac BCAA catabolism was altered in this model by examining the expression of key enzymes in BCAA catabolic pathway and BCAA levels. Western blot analysis demonstrated a marked increase in cardiac BCKDHA phosphorylation (p-BCKDHA/BCKDHA ratio) in HFD-fed mice relative to controls (Fig. 1I), indicating inhibition of the rate-limiting BCKDH complex. In line with this enzymatic suppression, direct quantification of cardiac BCAA contents revealed a significant increase in BCAA levels in HFD-fed mice (Fig. 1J). Together, these molecular and metabolic findings indicate a pronounced impairment of cardiac BCAA catabolic flux in OCM hearts.2.Activation of BCAA Catabolism via Cardiomyocyte-Specific BCKDK Deletion Fails to Confer Cardiac Protection under HFD.

2.Activation of BCAA Catabolism via Cardiomyocyte-Specific BCKDK Deletion Fails to Confer Cardiac Protection under HFD.

Activation of BCAA Catabolism via Cardiomyocyte-Specific BCKDK Deletion Fails to Confer Cardiac Protection under HFD.

To assess whether activation of cardiac BCAA catabolism benefits OCM hearts, we generated a cardiomyocyte-specific BCKDK knockout model, given that BCKDK deletion has been shown to enhance cardiac BCAA oxidation [14]. We generated inducible cardiomyocyte-specific BCKDK knockout (BCKDKflox/flox/Myh6-CreEsr1+, BCKDKiKO) mice (SFig. 1A–C). Littermates carrying floxed alleles (BCKDKflox/flox/Myh6-CreEsr1-, BCKDKfl/fl) were used as controls (Fig. 2A). HFD feeding evoked a clear divergence in body weight between ND and HFD-fed mice (Fig. 2B). IPGTT and IPITT analyses revealed comparable glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity between ND- and HFD-fed mice (Fig. 2C; SFig. 1D). In addition, plasma lipid measurements demonstrated significantly elevated TG, TC, HDL, and LDL levels in HFD-fed mice compared with ND controls (SFig. 1E). However, within the HFD group, no significant differences in body weight, glucose tolerance, insulin sensitivity, or plasma lipid levels were observed between BCKDKfl/fl and BCKDKiKO mice (Fig. 2C; SFig. 1D and E).

Western blotting further confirmed the efficient loss of BCKDK protein expression in BCKDKiKO hearts. Phosphorylation of BCKDHA, the direct substrate of BCKDK, was significantly reduced in BCKDKiKO mice (Fig. 2D). Consistent with this reduction in BCKDHA phosphorylation, cardiac BCAA levels were significantly decreased in BCKDKiKO hearts, returning to levels comparable to those of ND controls (Fig. 2E).

To evaluate the impact of increased cardiac BCAA catabolism in response to obesity, we performed echocardiographic assessment after 28 weeks of HFD feeding. Following HFD induction, BCKDKiKO mice displayed worsened diastolic function compared with BCKDKfl/fl controls, as indicated by a significant elevation in E/E′ ratio, accompanied by a mild reduction in systolic function (mean LVEF: 57.7% in HFD + BCKDKfl/fl versus 53.6% in HFD + BCKDKiKO) (Fig. 2F and G). Interventricular septal thickness at end-diastole (IVS; d) and left ventricular posterior wall thickness at end-diastole (LVPW; d) were further increased in HFD + BCKDKiKO mice compared to HFD + BCKDKfl/fl controls (SFig. 1F). Morphometric and histological analyses further corroborated these findings. Following HFD induction, BCKDKiKO mice exhibited more pronounced increases in HW and HW/TL compared with BCKDKfl/fl littermates (Fig. 2H and I). Consistently, wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) staining demonstrated an increased cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area (Fig. 2J and K), Sirius Red staining revealed exacerbated interstitial fibrosis (Fig. 2L and M; SFig. 1G), and TUNEL staining indicated an increased proportion of apoptotic cardiomyocytes (SFig. 1H) in BCKDKiKO hearts relative to BCKDKfl/fl controls under HFD feeding.

Since enhancing BCAA catabolism via cardiomyocyte-specific BCKDK deletion failed to improve cardiac dysfunction and remodelling under HFD intervention, and given that BCKDK protein levels were reduced in the hearts of HFD + BCKDKfl/fl mice compared with ND controls (Fig. 2D), we reasoned that BCKDK might contribute to OCM onset or progression through mechanisms unrelated to BCAA catabolism in hearts. In the knockout cohort, 442 metabolites were reliably quantified. Comparison between ND + BCKDKfl/fl and HFD + BCKDKfl/fl hearts identified 147 differentially accumulated metabolites (p < 0.05) (SFig. 6A). Pathway enrichment analysis using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 revealed significant enrichment (p < 0.05) in “Galactose Metabolism”, “Amino Sugar Metabolism”, “α-Linolenic Acid Metabolism”, “Pantothenate and CoA Biosynthesis”, “Phosphatidylinositol Phosphate Metabolism”, and “Citric Acid Cycle” (SFig. 6B), indicating HFD-associated metabolic reprogramming with disturbances in carbohydrate and lipid metabolism, and impaired mitochondrial oxidative metabolism in the heart [32]. Comparison between HFD + BCKDKfl/fl and HFD + BCKDKiKO hearts identified 59 differentially accumulated metabolites (p < 0.05) (SFig. 6C). Enrichment analysis highlighted the top 10 pathway (p < 0.05) involvement of “Citric Acid Cycle”, “Valine, Leucine and Isoleucine Degradation”, and “Glycolysis” (SFig. 6D), prompting a pathway-oriented analysis of metabolites related to glucose utilization (including glycolysis, and polyol and hexosamine branches), TCA cycle metabolism, BCAA oxidation, and redox balance to infer potential flux redistribution under HFD.

HFD feeding increased myocardial l-leucine levels, whereas cardiomyocyte-specific BCKDK deletion reduced all three BCAAs. Notably, the valine-derived downstream metabolite 3-hydroxyisobutyrate (3-HIB) was elevated under HFD and further increased upon BCKDK deletion (Fig. 9C and D). HFD also increased myocardial glucose levels and was accompanied by accumulation of metabolites within the polyol and hexosamine branches. BCKDK deletion did not attenuate these HFD-associated increases in glucose and polyol/hexosamine intermediates; instead, glycolytic intermediates accumulated (Fig. 9C–E; SFig. 6F and G), indicative of a glycolytic intermediate build-up in the context of constrained downstream oxidative metabolism. Supporting this notion, multiple tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates were significantly decreased in HFD + BCKDKiKO hearts (Fig. 9C–F). Exceptionally, α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), which is linked to branched-chain ketoacid transamination, was increased, whereas 2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG) was decreased (SFig. 6H), suggesting compensatory anaplerotic input yet overall impaired TCA cycle throughput. Moreover, HFD + BCKDKfl/fl hearts exhibited a compensatory elevation of glutathione (GSH), whereas BCKDK deletion blunted this adaptive response, as evidenced by reduced GSH levels and a lower glutathione-to-oxidized glutathione (GSH/GSSG) ratio (Fig. 9C–G). Consistent with the increased fibrosis detected by Sirius Red staining, metabolites associated with fibrotic extracellular matrix (ECM) remodelling, including hydroxyprolyl-proline and galactosylhydroxylysine, were elevated in HFD + BCKDKiKO hearts compared to HFD + BCKDKfl/fl hearts (SFig. 6I).

In the overexpression cohort, among the 358 accurately quantified metabolites, 27 were significantly altered (p < 0.05) (SFig. 6J). Pathway enrichment analysis of these differentially accumulated metabolites identified the top 10 pathways (p < 0.05), with notable enrichment in “Valine, Leucine and Isoleucine Degradation”, “Mitochondrial Electron Transport Chain”, and “Citric Acid Cycle” (SFig. 6K). Consistent with the BCAA assay results, valine, leucine and isoleucine were all reduced in the HFD + AAV-BCKDK group, accompanied by decreased 3-HIB, suggesting enhanced BCAA utilization (Fig. 9J and K). In parallel, several metabolites associated with mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) and TCA cycle, including ATP, NAD and succiniate, were markedly increased (Fig. 9J–L). Notably, ATP levels were substantially elevated despite the ATP deficiency typically observed under obesity conditions [6,33], and confirmed in our knockout cohort (Fig. 9C). To further corroborate these findings, we overexpressed BCKDK using adenovirus in primary neonatal cardiomyocytes. Seahorse assays demonstrated that BCKDK overexpression significantly enhanced mitochondrial respiration, as evidenced by increased oxygen consumption rates (OCR) (SFig. 6L). The concomitant reduction in BCAAs and their downstream metabolite, together with increased mitochondrial oxidative intermediates, supports enhanced entry of BCAA-derived carbon into the TCA cycle under augmented mitochondrial oxidative capacity, thereby reducing myocardial BCAA levels under HFD. Moreover, glutathione and pyroglutamate levels were further increased in the HFD + AAV-BCKDK group compared with HFD + AAV-CON (Fig. 9J–M), indicative of improved redox homeostasis following BCKDK overexpression.

To gain initial understanding into how BCKDK regulates mitochondrial function, we first examined its subcellular localization in cardiomyocytes, as previous reports have reported that BCKDK may localize to the cytosol [15] or nucleus [22] in non-cardiac cells. We extracted cytosolic and mitochondrial fractions from heart tissues and detected that BCKDK was present in mitochondria, whereas its abundance decreased under HFD conditions and was absent in cardiomyocyte-specific BCKDK knockout hearts (SFig. 7A). Consistently, high-resolution confocal imaging demonstrated that BCKDK immunostaining substantially overlapped with TOMM20 signals in H9c2 cells, further confirming its mitochondrial localization (SFig. 7B). We next interrogated published BCKDK immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) data obtained from hearts of mice subjected to a 10-week HFD [11]. Cross-referencing the IP-MS identified proteins with the Mouse MitoCarta 3.0 database, which catalogues 1140 mitochondrial proteins [34], yielded 267 mitochondrial candidates. Subsequent filtering based on statistical significance (p < 0.05) and fold change (FC > 1) refined the list to 79 potential BCKDK-interacting proteins (SFig. 7C). Gene Ontology Biological Process enrichment analysis revealed that these proteins were predominantly associated with mitochondrial respiratory pathways (SFig. 7D). Notably, NADH-ubiquinone oxidoreductase 75 kDa subunit (NDUFS1) emerged as one of the top four BCKDK-associated proteins ranked by fold change, highlighting it as a strong candidate for functional interaction. One study has reported that BCKDK interacts with NDUFS1 to enhance the structural integrity and enzymatic activity of mitochondrial Complex I, thereby reducing mitochondrial ROS production in dopaminergic neuronal cells [17]. Consistently, in H9c2 cells, we validated the interaction between BCKDK and NDUFS1 by co-immunoprecipitation (SFig. 7E). To further assess mitochondrial respiratory chain organization, we performed silver-stained Blue Native PAGE analysis using mitochondria isolated from H9c2 cells. BCKDK knockdown significantly decreased the abundance of Complex I and the signal in the high-molecular-weight supercomplex region, which are generally composed of Complex I associated with Complex III and Complex IV (SFig. 7F and G) [[35], [36], [37]]. Discussion

Previous study has established that BCAA catabolism is impaired in the obese heart [8], a finding reproduced in our model. Recent research demonstrated that systemic restoration of BCAA catabolic flux, through global BCKDK knockout or pharmacological BCKDK suppression with BT2 [10], significantly improved insulin signalling in the myocardium, and reversed the diastolic dysfunction and cardiac hypertrophy associated with HFpEF. In contrast to these systemic approaches, our cardiomyocyte-specific genetic models reveal a distinct role of BCKDK in OCM. Despite effectively lowering cardiac BCAA levels, cardiomyocyte-specific BCKDK deletion failed to confer cardiac benefits and instead exacerbated diastolic dysfunction and pathological remodelling. Conversely, restoring BCKDK expression in cardiomyocytes markedly attenuated diastolic dysfunction and pathological remodelling, primarily by enhancing mitochondrial respiratory capacity, reducing ROS production, and suppressing inflammatory response. Collectively, these findings redefine BCKDK as a cardioprotective regulator within the myocardium and distinguish its intrinsic actions from those inferred through systemic inhibition.

BCAAs and their downstream metabolic intermediates have been recognized as bioactive signalling molecules intimately related to the onset and progression of cardiovascular and metabolic disorders; consequently, dietary and pharmacological manipulation of BCAA catabolism offers a prospect for future therapeutics [9]. In HFpEF models induced by HFD combined with l-NAME, both global BCKDK deletion and pharmacological inhibition with BT2 effectively enhanced BCAA catabolism, relieved BCAA accumulation-related toxicity, and improved cardiac outcomes, partly through systemic metabolic benefits such as amelioration of insulin resistance [10]. However, whether these benefits reflect a direct advantage of augmenting cardiac BCAA catabolism within cardiomyocytes remains unclear. To address this, we employed cardiomyocyte-specific BCKDK knockout mice, not because this animal model has been shown to enhance cardiac BCAA oxidation [14], but because BT2 administration is known to downregulate BCKDK expression [11]. Unexpectedly, despite robust activation of BCAA catabolism, cardiomyocyte-specific BCKDK deletion exacerbated cardiac dysfunction and remodelling, indicating that BT2-mediated cardiac benefits cannot be solely attributed to enhanced cardiac BCAA utilization. Moreover, assessments of glucose tolerance, insulin sensitivity and circulating lipid levels in both knockout and overexpression cohorts revealed no corresponding extracardiac metabolic benefits, indicating that the cardiac phenotypes in our cardiomyocyte-specific models are mechanistically distinct from those driven by systemic BCKDK deletion or pharmacological inhibition. These results suggest that systemic BT2 therapy obscure potential adverse effects associated with reduced cardiac BCKDK expression, even when the net global effect appears favorable, underscoring the need to delineate the cardiac-intrinsic functions of BCKDK, and to develop therapeutic strategies that selectively modulate BCAA metabolism without perturbing the cell-autonomous roles of BCKDK in the heart. Moreover, BT2 has been reported to exert off-target effects, including enhanced tryptophan catabolism [38], modulation of mROS [39], and incomplete phenocopying of BCKDK deficiency [14,40], further illustrating the limitations of relying solely on systemic BT2 treatment to infer the physiological and pathological roles of BCKDK. Together with recent evidence that BCKDK overexpression, but not BT2 treatment, enhances CAR-T cell metabolic fitness and antitumor efficacy [41], our findings underscore the importance of cellular and pathological context in developing BCKDK-targeted therapeutic strategies. Moreover, the translational development of BCKDK overexpression-based therapy for OCM will require future studies to determine whether delayed cardiomyocyte-targeted BCKDK overexpression can reverse established cardiac dysfunction, and to define the therapeutic window, optimal timing, durability of benefit, and long-term safety.

Although impaired BCAA catabolism has been observed across several cardiovascular diseases [13,14,42,43], the decrease in BCKDK expression observed in our long-term HFD model appears to be context-specific rather than a generalized feature of cardiac disease. In human dilated cardiomyopathy, cardiac BCKDK levels remain unchanged [43], whereas myocardial infarction induces a sustained upregulation of BCKDK from 1 to 16 weeks after injury [42]. Moreover, cardiomyocyte-specific overexpression of the insulin-independent glucose transporter GLUT1, which enhanced cardiac glucose uptake, did not alter cardiac BCKDK abundance [13]. Notably, cardiomyocyte-specific BCKDK deletion did not exacerbate cardiac dysfunction and remodelling in reported non-metabolic stress models, including myocardial infarction [14], isoproterenol-induced hypertrophy [14], and transverse aortic constriction (TAC)-induced pressure overload [44], suggesting that the pathological relevance of BCKDK loss is context dependent. The distinct responses observed under non-metabolic stress and chronic metabolic stress likely reflect fundamental differences in mitochondrial injury patterns and substrate utilization programs across disease settings [28,45,46]. Glycolytic compensation predominates in myocardial infarction and TAC-induced heart failure, whereas OCM imposes an increased fatty acid oxidation-driven mitochondrial burden that exacerbates mitochondrial stress [28,45,47]. Importantly, our Bulk-RNA seq and qRT-PCR data indicate that BCKDK downregulation occurs at the mRNA level, suggesting that transcriptional regulation contributes to reduced BCKDK expression under long-term HFD stress. Obesity reshapes the cardiac metabolic environment, and epigenetic mechanisms have been proposed to serve as an interface linking environmental cues to transcriptional regulation in OCM [48]. Consistent with this possibility, our metabolomic profiling revealed HFD-induced alterations in metabolites involved in the TCA cycle and redox homeostasis, which may influence epigenetic regulation through mechanisms such as fumarate-associated dioxygenase activity [49,50] and redox-sensitive chromatin remodelling [51]. In addition, the transcription factor carbohydrate-response element binding protein (ChREBP) has been implicated in the transcriptional regulation of BCKDK in the liver during obesity [15], whereas whether a similar mechanism operates in cardiomyocytes under HFD stress remains unknown. These possibilities provide important directions for future studies investigating the upstream mechanisms responsible for BCKDK downregulation in OCM.

The BCAA oxidation pathway proceed through the TCA cycle, or terminate prematurely with the accumulation of cytotoxic intermediates, such as 3-HIB leading to insulin resistance in mice [52], or serve as precursors for monomethyl branched-chain fatty acids during lipogenesis [53]. Moreover, excessive BCAAs can activate the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, thereby driving pathological hypertrophy [54]. In our study, cardiomyocyte-specific BCKDK overexpression not only eliminated cardiac BCAA accumulation but also enhanced downstream TCA cycle and ETC activity, indicating that the increased BCAA clearance reflects improved capacity for terminal mitochondrial oxidation rather than merely accelerated upstream entry into the catabolic pathway. This mitochondrial reinforcement suggests that BCKDK overexpression fundamentally restores the metabolic capacity required for safe and productive BCAA utilization as an energy substrate. Conversely, although BCKDK deletion facilitated BCAA entry into the initial steps of catabolism, mitochondrial dysfunction under HFD conditions likely prevented complete BCAA oxidation, leading instead to the accumulation of potentially harmful intermediates and exacerbation of cardiac dysfunction and remodelling. Thus, the cardiac benefits are attributable not to lowering BCAA levels per se, but to ensuring their complete mitochondrial oxidation. These findings further suggest that BCAA accumulation in OCM represents a compensatory adaptation to impaired mitochondrial function, limiting flux through a catabolic pathway that, when incompletely executed, could generate cytotoxic intermediates.

Our findings reveal that BCKDK exerts cardioprotective effects in OCM beyond its canonical regulation of BCAA catabolism, notably by maintaining mitochondrial ultrastructural integrity and augmenting mitochondrial respiration. Our analysis and validation of previously published IP-MS data [11] revealed that BCKDK primarily interacts with proteins involved in mitochondrial respiratory chain, although the molecular basis and functional consequences of these interactions remain incompletely understood. Beyond the regulation of BCAA catabolism, BCKDK interacts with other substrates to modulate diverse macronutrient pathways and mitochondrial function. For instance, BCKDK preferentially localized in the cytosolic fraction of liver, and the overexpression of BCKDK increased the phosphorylation of ATP-citrate lyase (ACL) and activated de novo lipogenesis [15]. During embryonic development, BCKDK serves as a compensatory mechanism for pyruvate dehydrogenase kinases (PDK) family by inactivating pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC). Depletion of BCKDK and PDK family led to the loss of PDC phosphorylation, an increase in PDC activity and the flux of pyruvate into the TCA cycle, and embryonic lethality [16]. In neurodegenerative disorders, BCKDK binds directly to NDUFS1 and modulates mitochondrial health [17]. In addition, BCKDK participates in multiple biological processes, including cell proliferation, invasion, and migration through non-metabolic pathways. In tumour cells, BCKDK has been depicted to induce constitutive MEK-ERK signalling cascade activation [55,56], competitively interact with Talin1 to disrupt E3 ubiquitin ligase-TRIM21 binding to Talin1 and inhibit the Talin1 ubiquitination degradation [19], promote MYC transcription by phosphorylating the Bcl-2-associated transcription factor 1 (BCLAF1) [21], and, when localized to the nucleus, phosphorylate RING finger protein 8 (RNF8) to regulate DNA repair [22]. Collectively, these observations underscore BCKDK as a pleiotropic regulator to improve mitochondrial function and suppress inflammatory signalling in OCM.

MitoTEMPO only partially restored mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production in BCKDK-deficient H9c2 cells, indicating that mROS is an important but not exclusive mediator of BCKDK-dependent mitochondrial protection. Together with our analysis and validation of previously published IP-MS data [11], prior evidence supporting the pleiotropic roles of BCKDK [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]], and our metabolomic evidence of alterations in mitochondrial metabolic pathways, these findings suggest that BCKDK supports mitochondrial respiratory function in HFD-stressed cardiomyocytes through mitochondrial mechanisms upstream of mROS accumulation, such as the regulation of respiratory chain Complex I assembly. In addition, our transcriptomic data showing restoration of mitochondrial respiratory programs upon BCKDK overexpression suggest that broader transcriptional regulation also contribute to the observed mitochondrial recovery, including potential transcription factor activation or epigenetic remodelling. Therefore, defining the precise molecular mechanisms by which BCKDK preserves mitochondrial respiratory function and coordinates mitochondrial metabolic homeostasis remains an important direction for future investigation.

Our study has several limitations. First, the in vivo experiments were performed only in male mice, although female mice have been reported to exhibit relative protection against cardiac fibrosis and HFpEF development, potentially owing to estrogenic effects and lower testosterone levels [57,58]. To minimize biological variability in this initial phenotypic and mechanistic investigation, we focused on male mice. Future studies are needed to determine whether BCKDK exerts similar or sex-specific effects in the female myocardium during obesity. Second, although our in vitro MitoTEMPO rescue experiments support the involvement of mROS-MAPK signalling in BCKDK-deficient H9c2 cells, the in vivo relevance of this pathway requires further investigation. Given that systemic modulation of mROS or MAPK signalling may affect multiple cardiac and extracardiac cell types, future studies using temporally controlled and cell type-specific approaches are needed to define its in vivo contribution, with careful consideration of treatment timing and toxicity. Additionally, for ethical reasons, the human myocardial tissue used in this study was collected from the atrium but not the ventricle. Human atrial and ventricular myocardium exhibit substantial chamber-specific molecular differences [59]. Moreover, obesity-related cardiac remodelling differs between chambers, with ventricular involvement associated with diastolic dysfunction and atrial remodelling linked to atrial fibrillation [6]. Therefore, atrial findings may not fully reflect the ventricular metabolic response to obesity, and future assessment of BCKDK expression in ventricular samples from patients with obesity would provide important translational evidence.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that BCKDK preserves mitochondrial structural integrity and enhances mitochondrial respiratory function in cardiomyocytes independently of its canonical role in BCAA catabolism, thereby limiting MAPK-driven inflammatory response and ameliorating ventricular remodelling and cardiac dysfunction during obesity. This work expands our understanding of the multifaceted regulatory functions of BCKDK in OCM and reveals that its cardioprotective effects extend far beyond metabolic control of BCAAs. Restoring BCKDK expression in cardiomyocytes may therefore represent a promising and effective therapeutic strategy for the treatment of OCM.
