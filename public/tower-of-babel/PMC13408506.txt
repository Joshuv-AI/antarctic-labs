From Courtroom to Intensive Care Unit: The Unfinished Journey of End-of-life Care in India
Authors: Ripenmeet Salhotra
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

I read with great interest Dr Raj K Mani's recent commentary on the Harish Rana judgment, which is indeed a significant moment for end-of-life care (EOLC) in India, particularly in reaffirming that clinically assisted nutrition and hydration can be withdrawn.1 However, the case also highlights a persisting gap between legal recognition and practical implementation.

Despite the Supreme Court's 2023 simplification of the process through Primary and Secondary Medical Boards (PMB/SMB), the Harish Rana case has been described as the first instance in three years where this framework has actually been put to use, only after the family approached the Supreme Court to set it in motion.2 While some encouraging policy-level initiatives have emerged in states such as Karnataka, Maharashtra, and Haryana, practical implementation remains limited.3 To the best of current knowledge, a routinely accessible and fully functional PMB/SMB pathway is still unavailable in most parts of the country.

Decisions around withholding or withdrawing life support have long been part of Indian intensive care unit (ICU) practice—shaped by clinical judgment, honest conversations with families, and a shared ethical sensibility, even without a formal legal scaffold.4 The introduction of a structured PMB/SMB pathway, while intended to provide legal clarity, has inadvertently created a dilemma for intensivists. Clinicians who previously made such decisions in good faith may now feel legally vulnerable if similar actions are taken without formal board approval. As a result, a framework that is not yet uniformly accessible risks acting as a deterrent rather than a support, with clinicians concerned that ethically appropriate decisions may later be questioned as non-compliant with the prescribed process.

Advance medical directives present a similar tension. The growing emphasis on patient autonomy is welcome and long overdue. But even a valid, carefully prepared directive still requires review through the PMB/SMB process before it can be acted upon—a process that, as noted, remains largely non-functional across most of the country.5

The Rana judgment deserves recognition as a legal milestone. But it also serves as a pointed reminder that reform on paper and reform in practice are two very different things. The promise of dignified end-of-life care can become meaningful only when the legal pathway is both accessible and practically implementable.
