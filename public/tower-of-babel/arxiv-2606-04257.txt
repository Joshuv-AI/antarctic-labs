Hardness as an Information Constraint: A Unifying Meta-Complexity Assumption
Hunter Monroe

Abstract
Monroe [ 21 ] shows that the nonexistence of an optimal proof system can be read as an information constraint regarding canonical hard instances: no sound arithmetic theory simulates the extensions adjoining sufficiently large, unprovable Busy Beaver values. Furthermore, if the best-known route to simulation is also necessary—that is, if simulation requires a relative-consistency explanation over a weak base theory—then the same constraint holds for inaccessible Kolmogorov-randomness facts. Call this Kolmogorov Hardness ( KH ). We argue that major open questions in proof and computational complexity can likewise be reformulated as information constraints involving Kolmogorov-random strings. Finite-scale and hierarchy-level forms of KH yield, as conditional consequences, dense families of small hard tautologies, no-mutual-help phenomena for independent random axioms, 𝐏𝐇 \mathbf{PH} noncollapse with explicit dense separators at each level, SAT ∉ 𝐏 / poly \texttt{SAT}{\notin}\mathbf{P}/\mathrm{poly} , and canonical disjoint 𝐍𝐏 \mathbf{NP} pairs arising from random-axiom constructions. Time-bounded and sparse-support variants extend the same template to one-way functions via Liu–Pass, derandomization, natural-proofs-style limitations, and Feige-style random refutation. This framework gives a unified working model of complexity theorists’ beliefs, organized around canonical hard instances. It seems self-evident that efficient proofs in a theory should not leverage true randomness facts the theory cannot verify. Yet the structural features of KH and its variants suggest they may be formally independent of standard metatheories. They behave like reflection principles; their internal readings fail in nonstandard models even when the corresponding external readings are true; and the same information constraints may apply to the metatheories themselves. We propose a research program: extend the model, clarify the case for self-evidence, resolve questions of formal independence, and, more ambitiously, identify which principles are potential new axioms.

Introduction

A Common Informational Constraint.

This paper proposes that several central hardness conjectures in complexity theory can be organized around a common informational constraint. Monroe
[
21
, Theorem 3.10]
shows that, if no optimal proof system exists
[
17
]
, then every theory fails to simulate extensions obtained by adjoining sufficiently large exact Busy Beaver value statements. That paper further conjectures that the known sufficient conditions for simulation are exhaustive; if so, the same proof-theoretic obstruction extends from Busy Beaver value statements to inaccessible Kolmogorov-randomness facts. We use this obstruction as a template for a broader framework: feasible proof systems, algorithms, and circuits should not obtain leverage from information that the relevant theory cannot already certify or explain over an appropriate weak base. The aim is not to claim unconditional lower bounds, but to isolate a common proof-theoretic strengthening of familiar hardness expectations. Under finite-scale, hierarchy-level, sparse-refutation, and average-case bridge principles, inaccessible randomness facts give a unified source for strengthened conjectures in proof complexity, circuit complexity, random refutation, derandomization, and cryptography.

The unifying payoff.

The main contribution of this paper is to argue that
KH
is not an isolated proof-complexity conjecture but the core of a broader unifying meta-complexity assumption. In this paper, the
Unifying Meta-Complexity Assumption
is the package consisting of
KH
together with its finite-scale, hierarchy-level, average-case, no-mutual-help, and bridge-strengthened variants. The common content of the package is that feasible methods should not obtain substantial proof-theoretic, algorithmic, or circuit-theoretic leverage from true randomness information that remains inaccessible over the relevant weak base theory. The finite-scale forms developed below produce dense families of hard bounded-consistency instances and support no-mutual-help principles for independent random axioms. Hierarchy-level strengthenings yield explicit dense separators throughout
\mathbf{PH}
and imply standard circuit consequences such as
\texttt{SAT}{\notin}\mathbf{P}/\mathrm{poly}
. Further resource-specific variants specialize the same proof-theoretic obstruction to additional regimes, giving conditional routes to derandomization, one-way functions via the Liu–Pass characterization of average-case hardness for time-bounded Kolmogorov complexity, natural-proofs-style limitations, and Feige-style random refutation through sparse-support reflection. The unifying claim is not that these frontier consequences are unconditional, but that each reduces to the same kind of obstruction: feasible computation should not obtain leverage from information that the relevant theory cannot already explain.

The information-constraint template.

The common methodological template is as follows. To rule out a proposed algorithm, proof system, or circuit family, we identify a finite information predicate whose decision would amount to accessing a stronger layer of proof-theoretic or Kolmogorov information. A feasible method at level
i
should have a level-respecting explanation of its success: its efficiency and correctness should be certifiable in the corresponding theory
\mathcal{T}_{i}
, not only in a stronger theory
\mathcal{T}_{i+1}
. When such a certificate would force
\mathcal{T}_{i}
to prove unbounded randomness or consistency information unavailable at level
i
, the relevant reflection principle rules out the method.
Thus the framework is not merely a list of lower-bound conjectures; it is organized by a no-cheating principle for feasible computation, specialized across the paper to proof-theoretic, finite-scale, hierarchy-level, and average-case notions of hardness. Across the extensions, the same template recurs: choose a randomness predicate calibrated to the resource regime, assert that the relevant information is inaccessible to the weak theory at finite scale, and add only the bridge principle needed to translate feasible success into forbidden feasible proofs.

A unified working model, and possibly an axiom schema.

The framework has two readings. On the modest reading,
HRC
,
KH
, and their extensions provide a unified working model for complexity theorists’ informal hardness expectations behind central open problems in complexity theory. The model identifies canonical sources of hardness: random strings
x{\in}R
are mapped to almost-always-hard tautologies, circuit families, and boundary languages, with explicit reflection and calibration assumptions marking where each consequence enters. This reading makes load-bearing assumptions explicit, exposes calibration parameters, and organizes several neighboring programs—including proof-complexity generators, average-case hardness of time-bounded Kolmogorov complexity, and unprovability of circuit lower bounds in bounded arithmetic—as instances or analogs of the information-constraint template. On the more ambitious reading, these principles are candidate axioms for complexity theory, with
KH
as the most direct candidate for self-evidence among the random-information principles: it says that inaccessible Kolmogorov-randomness facts should not provide feasible proof-theoretic leverage unless they are already weak-base accessible. The finite-scale, hierarchy-level, average-case, and cryptographic variants are less primitive; they are calibrated specializations of the same barrier, with additional assumptions needed to obtain the corresponding complexity-theoretic payoffs. The relevant question then becomes whether these barriers are fruitful, self-evident, formally independent, and robust enough to deserve axiomatic status.

The research program.

Several concrete targets emerge. One can try to prove restricted instances of
HRC
or
KH
for weak proof systems, search for nonstandard models witnessing the failure of converse reflection, sharpen the finite-scale thresholds, test the hierarchy-level predicates against known proof-complexity techniques, analyze the random-axiom disjoint
\mathbf{NP}
pairs, calibrate the
R_{t}
interface for average-case hardness, one-way functions, and derandomization, and determine whether the sparse-support reflection principles needed for Feige’s hypothesis hold in any natural proof-theoretic setting.

Main technical contributions.

The paper has three kinds of contributions.

(1)

Bounded-consistency setup.
We recall the standard proof-complexity translation between simulation of arithmetic theories and polynomial-size proofs of bounded consistency statements such as

(2)

Technical payoff.
We prove structural consequences of these principles, including density, no-mutual-help, robustness under changes of universal machine, persistence under finite true extensions, and conditional unprovability of universal self-applicable schemas. (3)

Classical proof barriers.

Because the principles considered here are meant to yield consequences beyond the usual formulation of
\mathbf{P}{\neq}\mathbf{NP}

\mathbf{PH}

The framework is not relativizing in the usual oracle sense: its central claims depend on arithmetized proof predicates, weak-base access to randomness assertions, and bounded-consistency reflection, each of which is sensitive to the way the oracle is represented inside the theory. Thus oracle separations do not refute the framework; rather, they mark the point at which purely relativizing information is insufficient and nonrelativizing proof-theoretic information must enter. Aaronson and Wigderson’s algebrization barrier
[

Why

Every hypothesis in the meta-complexity program above is, on its face, about the complexity of a complexity measure: it asserts that some problem is hard to

\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}}

Later assumptions attempt to extend this maximality principle beyond bounded consistency to the other resource regimes studied below.

This equivalent contrapositive is the form called

\mathcal{S}{\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{23.674pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-23.674pt\raisebox{-1.96112pt}{\makebox[23.674pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}\hskip-23.674pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[23.674pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{n^{O(1)}}$}}}$}}}}\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+\phi}(n)

2.4
The Busy Beaver Information Constraint

For each

2.5
The Kolmogorov Hardness Conjecture

Fix a deterministic universal Turing machine
U
, no time bound, and a constant

. Let

, the set of Kolmogorov-random strings with logarithmic deficiency at most

; for background on Kolmogorov complexity and the random set

see Li and Vitányi
[
19

. We write

for

once

by another standard incompressibility predicate changes only coding constants and the associated formalization of the added axiom

. The exception is density: quantitative density claims use the fact that, with logarithmic deficiency, almost all strings of each length lie in
R
, so a different randomness convention may require corresponding changes to the density estimates. By Chaitin-style incompleteness
[
5
]
, any sound computably axiomatized extension of arithmetic proves

for only finitely many true instances.

Definition 2.7

(

Theorem 2.8
.

Assume

for theory
\mathcal{S}
. Let

.

Proof.

The key elementary fact is that
\mathrm{EA}
proves
\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+(x{\in}R)}{\rightarrow}x{\in}R
. Indeed, if
x{\notin}R
, then there is a finite computation witnessing that some program of length below the randomness threshold outputs

. The weak base

Theorem 2.9

(Robustness under change of universal machine)
.

Let

If

3.1
Finite-Scale Hardness

The Kolmogorov Hardness (
KH
) conjecture is asymptotic. It asserts that, for almost all Kolmogorov-random strings
x{\in}R
, a sound arithmetic theory
\mathcal{S}{\supseteq}S^{1}_{2}
eventually lacks
n^{c}
-size proofs of
\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+(x{\in}R)}(n)
for every constant
c
. The finite-scale version asks for a sharper lower bound at the natural scale set by the length of the random string. This refinement is essential for connecting
KH
to concrete proof-complexity instances: it lets one ask whether
\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+(x{\in}R)}(n)
is already hard for a specific small value of
n
, and hence whether the propositional tautology encoding that finite consistency statement is hard. Finite-scale hardness becomes especially important in later sections, where the relevant complexity-theoretic conjectures concern explicit input lengths rather than only eventual asymptotic failure. In this sense,
R
functions as an immunity device against lookup-table objections: finite tables can absorb finitely many exceptions, but they cannot explain the uniformly recurring hardness generated by almost all incompressible strings.

The combined threshold below is used only to exclude trivial small cases. These include values of
n
below the minimum length of any valid proof string, values of
|x|
below the Chaitin threshold for the ambient theory, and values of
n
so small that a proof of length at most
n
could not even mention the added axiom
x{\in}R

\mathcal{S}
. Let

be the maximum of three quantities: a vacuity threshold, namely the least length of a valid
\mathcal{S}
-proof string in the fixed proof calculus; a semantic threshold, namely a bound beyond which
\mathrm{EA}{+}\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}}
proves no true sentence of the form
x{\in}R
; and a mentionability threshold, namely a bound ensuring that, whenever
n{\geq}N_{R}^{\mathcal{S}}
, a proof of length at most
n
can mention the added axiom
x{\in}R
.

2

\lambda
large enough that the formula
x{\in}R
, for

. The finite-scale statements below should be read with
\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+(x{\in}R)}(\lambda n)
in place of
\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+(x{\in}R)}(n)
whenever raw symbol lengths are relevant. Since replacing
n
by

only changes exponential bounds by a constant factor in the exponent, we suppress

and continue to write
\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+(x{\in}R)}(n)

For every fixed admissible base

3.1.3
Relative Consistency Version

A relative-consistency analog of
SETH-K-Finite
is possible, but it carries a threshold problem. For arbitrary true extensions, finite exceptions are not merely technical: a sentence
\phi
may encode bounded information about particular proof lengths, and
\mathcal{S}
may contain axioms asserting bounded consistency of
\mathcal{S}
itself up to very large values of

Specializing the relative-consistency version to
\phi{\equiv}x{\in}R
recovers the random-axiom finite-scale principle, while the density conclusions come from the abundance of random strings. The relative-consistency version supplies the general explanatory discipline; the random-axiom specialization supplies the canonical scale and the density theory.

3.2
Noncollapse of
PH

This section gives the main complexity-theoretic test case for the program. We formulate a hierarchy-level strengthening of

that converts the Kolmogorov-randomness obstruction into ordinary polynomial-hierarchy separations.
3
3

3

A relative-consistency analog of

would face a threshold problem: for an arbitrary true sentence

gives a canonical scale. More importantly, such an analog would no longer be directly about deciding the finite predicates

that sit at level

Informally, the assumption says that a polynomial-time Turing machine with access to level-

PH
; and if its efficiency and correctness were certified by the associated theory

.

At level

and

\mathbf{NP}{\neq}\mathbf{coNP}

measuring Kolmogorov randomness relative to level-

whose decision complexity naturally lies at level

. Second, the lower-bound assumption must be stated algorithmically: no polynomial-time Turing machine with oracle access to
\Pi_{i}^{p}
should decide the canonical next-level predicate

. Third, the proof-theoretic explanation must use a stronger ambient theory,

\mathcal{T}_{i}

For this reason, the hierarchy-level argument is not phrased as a relative-consistency principle like

4

4

Let

{<}|x|{-}d\log|x|

Definition 3.7

(Finite level-
i
oracle approximation)
.

Fix

The notation

3.2.2
SETH-K-PH

The assumption

is the main bridge from the random-axiom framework to standard polynomial-hierarchy lower bounds. The guiding idea is that level-

To avoid confusing arithmetical truth predicates with polynomial-hierarchy oracles, we separate the coding claim from the hardness assumption. The coding lemma says that the finite predicates used to express level-

says that these predicates remain hard even with access to the level-

Lemma 3.8
.

For each

.

Proof.

The predicate

asserts the nonexistence of a short description which, within time

Assumption 3.9

(

3.2.3
Explicit Dense

Bare noncollapse,

, asserts only the existence of some separating language.

).

Theorem 3.12
.

Assume

. For each

, let

be the time bound supplied by the assumption. Then:

(1)

(Explicitness.)
The predicate

(Density.)

1{-}n^{-d}

(3)

(Sparse-side localization.)

For (1), Lemma

from

For (2), a string

3.2.4
Feasible Reflection at Level
i

The preceding assumption is algorithmic: level-
i
feasible computation should not decide the next layer of finite randomness information. This subsection gives a proof-theoretic explanation for that assumption. The explanation is a higher-level analog of Feasible Reflection. At level
0
, Feasible Reflection, as formulated in Monroe
[
21
]
, says that external efficient simulation should have an internal relative-consistency explanation: for every true sentence
\phi
, if
\mathcal{S}{\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{23.674pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-23.674pt\raisebox{-1.96112pt}{\makebox[23.674pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}\hskip-23.674pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[23.674pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{n^{O(1)}}$}}}$}}}}\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}{+}\phi}(n)
, then
\mathrm{EA}{\vdash}\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}}{\rightarrow}\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}{+}\phi}
. Thus external polynomial-size simulation by
\mathcal{S}
should be reflected by an internal explanation over the weak base
\mathrm{EA}
.

At level
i
, the relevant external datum is a polynomial-time Turing machine with oracle access to
\Pi_{i}^{p}
deciding the canonical finite randomness predicate
Q_{i}^{t}
. The certification principle should be read with a strict level discipline: a purported level-
i
feasible method must have its efficiency and correctness certified at level
i
, by
\mathcal{T}_{i}{:=}\mathcal{S}{+}\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{true}}
, not by the stronger theory
\mathcal{T}_{i+1}
. Allowing
\mathcal{T}_{i+1}
to certify the method would beg the question, since
\mathcal{T}_{i+1}
already has access to the next layer of arithmetical truth that the machine is supposed to decide feasibly. Thus the load-bearing bridge is not the broad claim that efficient algorithms unprovable in any theory do not exist; it is the level-respecting claim that genuine level-
i
feasible success on
Q_{i}^{t}
should be certifiable within the corresponding level-
i
theory. If no such level-
i
certification is possible without forcing
\mathcal{T}_{i}
to prove unbounded randomness facts forbidden by relativized Chaitin-style incompleteness, that is evidence against the existence of the level-
i
algorithm itself.

Assumption 3.13

(

i
)
.

For every
i{\geq}1
, let
\mathcal{T}_{i}{:=}\mathcal{S}{+}\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{true}}
. Let
t
be a polynomial time bound, and let
M
be a polynomial-time Turing machine with oracle access to
\Pi_{i}^{p}
. Suppose that, for a sufficiently long true string

, the theory
\mathcal{T}_{i}
certifies the accepting correctness of
M
on
x
for the finite predicate

; that is,
\mathcal{T}_{i}
proves that
M
accepts

. Then
\mathcal{T}_{i}{\vdash}x{\in}R_{i}
.

Assumption 3.14

(
Level-Respecting Certification Bridge
)
.

For every
i{\geq}1
, let
\mathcal{T}_{i}{:=}\mathcal{S}{+}\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{true}}
. If there is a polynomial time bound

which correctly decides the canonical predicate

has a level-respecting certificate: for every sufficiently long true string

, the theory

, not
\mathcal{T}_{i+1}
, certifies the accepting correctness of
M
on
x
for
Q_{i}^{t}
.

The Level-Respecting Certification Bridge is the substantive step. Certified Feasible Reflection alone rules out only
\mathcal{T}_{i}
-certified feasible success; the bridge asserts that an actual level-
i

cannot require a stronger level-
(i{+}1)
theory to certify its success on the true random inputs relevant to the Chaitin obstruction. In this sense, the bridge is a no-cheating principle: a method advertised as level-

Theorem 3.15
.

Assume Certified Feasible Reflection at Level

and the Level-Respecting Certification Bridge for every

. Then

holds.

Proof.

Fix
i{\geq}1
. Suppose, toward a contradiction, that the level-
i
instance of
SETH-K-PH
fails. Then for every polynomial time bound
t
, the canonical predicate
Q_{i}^{t}(x)
is decidable by a polynomial-time Turing machine with oracle access to
\Pi_{i}^{p}
. Fix such a time bound
t
and such a machine

. By the Level-Respecting Certification Bridge, for every sufficiently long true string

, the theory
\mathcal{T}_{i}
certifies the accepting correctness of

on

for

. By Certified Feasible Reflection at Level

, it follows that
\mathcal{T}_{i}{\vdash}x{\in}R_{i}
for every sufficiently long true string

. This contradicts relativized Chaitin-style incompleteness, since

proves only finitely many true level-

holds. Since

∎

This theorem should be read as a proof-theoretic rationale for

, not as a route to

itself. The reflection-and-certification argument explains why a level-

would be suspicious: if its success were certifiable at level

, it would force
\mathcal{T}_{i}
to prove unbounded randomness facts beyond the finite exceptions allowed by relativized Chaitin-style incompleteness; if it could be certified only at level

4
From
KH
to Computational Randomness

The preceding sections use the unbounded Kolmogorov-randomness predicate
R
as a source of fixed true axioms
x{\in}R
. That is the right object for
KH
: Chaitin-style incompleteness gives a genuine proof-theoretic obstruction, since a sound theory proves only finitely many true assertions
x{\in}R
. But average-case complexity, cryptography, natural proofs, and derandomization are not naturally formulated for fixed unbounded truth assertions. They require decidable predicates over varying inputs. The first task in this section is therefore to develop the necessary shift from
R
to time-bounded randomness predicates.

This shift should not be read as replacing the source of hardness. The unbounded predicate
R
remains the reflection-theoretic source of inaccessibility, while the time-bounded predicates provide the computational interface. The relevant boundary is the gap, inside
R_{t}
, between genuine randomness and merely time-bounded randomness: strings in
R
on one side, and strings in
R_{t}{\setminus}R
on the other. The central question is whether feasible average-case or nonuniform access to this boundary would spill over into usable access to genuine randomness facts
x{\in}R
. The framework predicts that such spillover is unavoidable in the calibrated settings below: an efficient method that reliably detects this boundary without yielding weak-base access to the corresponding
R
-facts would be exactly the kind of unexplained feasible leverage that
KH
forbids.

This viewpoint puts one-way functions squarely inside the framework. Liu–Pass
[
20
]
characterize the existence of one-way functions in terms of mild average-case hardness of a time-bounded Kolmogorov-complexity problem: roughly, deciding whether a string has a short description that outputs it within a prescribed time bound. Thus, once one accepts the necessary computational shift from fixed unbounded randomness facts
x{\in}R
to decidable time-bounded randomness predicates, the remaining missing ingredient is not an ad hoc cryptographic assumption. It is a reflection-and-calibration layer connecting the decidable time-bounded problem back to the proof-theoretic source of hardness. The route is therefore:
R
supplies the proof-theoretic source of inaccessibility; average-case boundary reflection says that a successful heuristic for the decidable time-bounded boundary would expose genuine
R
-facts too often; boundary calibration identifies that proof-theoretic boundary with the Liu–Pass time-bounded Kolmogorov predicate on a suitable ensemble; and Liu–Pass then converts mild average-case hardness of that predicate into one-way functions. Natural proofs and derandomization are treated afterward as further tests of the same random-information boundary.

The assumptions in this section do more than postulate cryptographic or derandomization hardness. They separate the proof-theoretic source of hardness from the computational interface through which that hardness can be tested. The unbounded predicate
R
supplies genuine weak-base inaccessibility; the time-bounded predicates supply decidable, length-indexed problems suitable for average-case and nonuniform complexity; and the bridge assumptions say precisely when feasible access to the decidable boundary would spill back into weak-base access to genuine randomness facts. Thus the analytical content is a boundary principle: the hard computational object is not “randomness” in general, but the interface between true unbounded randomness and merely time-bounded incompressibility.

4.1
From
R
to
R_{t}

The principles in previous sections use the unbounded Kolmogorov-randomness predicate
R
. This choice is essential in the fixed-axiom proof-theoretic formulation. If
R
is replaced naively by a time-bounded predicate
R_{t}
, the resulting statement loses the intended inaccessibility: for each fixed true instance
x{\in}R_{t}
, the assertion
x{\in}R_{t}
is a finite bounded-computation claim, and hence is eventually provable in sufficiently weak arithmetic. Thus
R_{t}
should not replace
R
in
KH
or in the basic fixed-axiom form of
SETH-K-Finite
.

Nevertheless,
R_{t}
is the right interface with average-case complexity, cryptography, natural proofs, and derandomization. These subjects concern decidable predicates over varying inputs, with uniform bounds in the input length. In that setting the relevant issue is not whether a fixed true assertion
x{\in}R_{t}
is ever provable, but whether the boundary between time-bounded and genuine randomness can be detected efficiently often enough to be useful. The time-bounded predicate therefore gives a computational companion to the unbounded
R
-principles:
R
supplies the reflection-theoretic source of inaccessibility, while
R_{t}
supplies the decidable predicate needed for uniform and average-case formulations.

For a time bound
t
, let
Q_{0}^{t}(z)
denote the base-level time-bounded randomness predicate:
Q_{0}^{t}(z)
holds iff no program of length
{<}|z|{-}d\log|z|
outputs
z
within
t(|z|)
steps on the fixed universal machine. Thus
Q_{0}^{t}
is the global predicate for time-bounded incompressibility, distinct from a pointed random axiom
x{\in}R
, which is a true unbounded randomness assertion at a specific string. A global algorithm for
Q_{0}^{t}
does not automatically certify any particular assertion
x{\in}R
. The bridge assumption needed throughout this section is therefore a spillover principle: sufficiently successful feasible access to the decidable time-bounded boundary should expose genuine randomness information often enough to contradict Chaitin-style incompleteness.

4.2
One-way Functions from Average-Case Boundary Reflection

The Liu–Pass characterization makes the preceding spillover question especially sharp. For sufficiently large polynomial time bounds, one-way functions exist exactly when a corresponding time-bounded Kolmogorov-complexity predicate is mildly hard on average. Thus, after the necessary shift from fixed unbounded randomness facts
x{\in}R
to decidable time-bounded predicates, one-way functions become a direct test case: the missing ingredient is an average-case reflection principle saying that a heuristic for the time-bounded boundary would expose genuine randomness information too often.

The direct predicate
\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+(x{\in}R)}(n)
is unsuitable for average-case cryptographic transfer on distributions concentrated on true random strings, because these consistency statements are automatically true and the constant-
1
algorithm succeeds. The better object is the boundary between genuine randomness and feasible time-bounded randomness, captured below by the boundary-exposure predicate
E_{\mathcal{S}}(x,N)
. This is why
R_{t}
appears here rather than in the fixed-axiom formulation: the average-case problem must be decidable and uniform in
x
, while the underlying reflection obstruction is still supplied by the unbounded predicate
R
.

Definition 4.1

(Boundary-exposure predicate)
.

For a theory
\mathcal{S}
and a threshold
N
, let
E_{\mathcal{S}}(x,N){:=}\exists m{\leq}N\,\neg\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+(x{\in}R)}(m)
. Equivalently,
E_{\mathcal{S}}(x,N)
holds if there is a contradiction proof of length at most
N
from the extension
\mathcal{S}+(x{\in}R)
.

For a polynomial time bound
t
, let
K^{t}(z)
denote the
t
-time-bounded Kolmogorov complexity of
z
: the length of the shortest program that outputs
z
within
t(|z|)
steps on the fixed universal machine. The object Liu–Pass characterize is the problem of
computing

on uniformly random inputs, not a fixed-threshold decision predicate. We use the gapped decision form
\mathrm{MK}^{t}\mathrm{P}
—on input
z
, decide whether
z
has a description below the chosen length threshold that outputs
z
within
t(|z|)
steps—only as an interface to the boundary-exposure predicate below; the equivalence with one-way functions is stated for computing
K^{t}
. Any reduction routed through the decision problem
\mathrm{MK}^{t}\mathrm{P}
should cite the corresponding decision/
\mathrm{MINKT}
variant rather than
[
20
]
, since that equivalence is the one Liu–Pass actually establish for the value problem.

Theorem 4.2

(Liu–Pass
[
20
]
)
.

There is a polynomial
t
for which the following holds. One-way functions exist if and only if the time-bounded Kolmogorov complexity function
K^{t}
is
mildly hard on average over the uniform distribution
: there is a polynomial

such that no probabilistic polynomial-time algorithm, given

, outputs

with probability at least
1-1/p(n)
for all sufficiently large

. Here
K^{t}(x)
is the length of the shortest program that outputs
x
within
t(|x|)
steps on the fixed universal machine.

Assumption 4.3

(
Average-Case Boundary Reflection
)
.

There exist a computable polynomial time bound
t
, a polynomial

, and a polynomial-time samplable ensemble
\mathcal{D}{=}\{D_{n}\}
supported on
R_{t}{\cap}\{0,1\}^{n}
such that both
\Pr_{x\sim D_{n}}[x{\in}R]
and
\Pr_{x\sim D_{n}}[x{\in}R_{t}{\setminus}R]
are bounded away from
0

, and such that the following reflection principle holds: if some probabilistic polynomial-time algorithm decides
E_{\mathcal{S}}(x,N(n))
on

with success probability
1{-}o(1)
, then for almost every
x{\sim}D_{n}
with

, one has
\mathrm{EA}{+}\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}}{\vdash}x{\in}R
.

The two-sided support condition is essential. If
\mathcal{D}_{n}
were concentrated almost entirely on genuinely random strings, then
E_{\mathcal{S}}(x,N(n))
would be false with probability
1{-}o(1)
, and the constant algorithm outputting “false” would succeed. Thus the assumption would collapse immediately into an impossible demand that
\mathrm{EA}{+}\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}}
prove almost all sampled true randomness assertions. The condition that both
R
and
R_{t}{\setminus}R
have non-negligible mass is what prevents the boundary problem from being defeated by a constant heuristic.

Proposition 4.4
.

Average-Case Boundary Reflection
implies that no probabilistic polynomial-time algorithm decides
E_{\mathcal{S}}(\cdot,N(n))
on

with success probability
1{-}o(1)
.

Proof.

A success-
1{-}o(1)
decider would satisfy the antecedent of
Average-Case Boundary Reflection
. The conclusion would imply
\mathrm{EA}{+}\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}}{\vdash}x{\in}R
for a positive-probability, and hence infinite, set of true random strings

. This contradicts Chaitin-style incompleteness for the fixed weak base
\mathrm{EA}{+}\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}}
. Therefore no such decider exists.
∎

To connect this native boundary-hardness statement to one-way functions, one needs a calibration between the proof-theoretic boundary predicate and time-bounded Kolmogorov complexity.

Assumption 4.5

(
Boundary Calibration
)
.

The ensemble
\mathcal{D}
, the threshold
N(n)
, and the time bound
t
are chosen so that two conditions hold. First, on
\mathcal{D}
the boundary-exposure predicate
E_{\mathcal{S}}(\cdot,N(n))
tracks
t
-time-bounded Kolmogorov complexity up to a polynomial-time computable reindexing. Second, this correspondence bridges
\mathcal{D}
to the uniform distribution on which Liu–Pass operates: any average-case algorithm computing
K^{t}
over the uniform distribution on
\{0,1\}^{n}
that is strong enough to violate the Liu–Pass hardness condition yields, through the reindexing, a
1{-}o(1)
-success heuristic for
E_{\mathcal{S}}(\cdot,N(n))
on
x{\sim}D_{n}
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Assume
Average-Case Boundary Reflection
and
Boundary Calibration
. Then one-way functions exist.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.4
, no probabilistic polynomial-time algorithm decides
E_{\mathcal{S}}(\cdot,N(n))
on
x{\sim}D_{n}
with success probability
1{-}o(1)
. By
Boundary Calibration
, any algorithm computing
K^{t}
over the uniform distribution strong enough to violate the Liu–Pass hardness condition would transfer to such a forbidden heuristic for
E_{\mathcal{S}}(\cdot,N(n))
. Hence
K^{t}
is mildly hard on average over the uniform distribution. By Theorem
4.2
, one-way functions exist.
∎

The theorem isolates the cryptographic content cleanly. The hardness assumption is not an ad hoc assertion that one-way functions exist. It is the same reflection idea used throughout the paper, applied to an average-case boundary predicate. The remaining mathematical work is calibration: proving that the proof-theoretic boundary between
\mathcal{S}+(x{\in}R)
consistency and contradiction exposure tracks the Liu–Pass time-bounded Kolmogorov boundary on a suitable two-sided ensemble.

4.3
Natural Proofs as a Compatibility Test

The next application is explanatory rather than deductive. The natural-proofs barrier of Razborov–Rudich
[
28
]
is the closest classical analog of the information-constraint viewpoint: it says, in one central setting, that large efficient tests exposing pseudorandom structure are too strong to be available if cryptographic hardness holds. The precise claim here is not that Kolmogorov hardness proves the natural-proofs barrier. The claim is that the framework is compatible with the barrier, and explains why the canonical hard families should lie on the natural-proofs-resistant side.

Recall that a combinatorial property
\mathcal{P}{=}\{\mathcal{P}_{n}\}
, where
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a set of Boolean functions on
n
inputs, is
\mathbf{P}/\mathrm{poly}
-natural with density
\delta
if membership
f{\in}\mathcal{P}_{n}
is decidable by polynomial-size circuits from the
2^{n}
-bit truth table of
f
, and
\Pr_{f}[f{\in}\mathcal{P}_{n}]{\geq}\delta_{n}
, with
\delta_{n}{\geq}2^{-O(n)}
. It is useful against a circuit class
\mathcal{C}
if every
f{\in}\mathcal{P}_{n}
, for infinitely many
n
, lies outside
\mathcal{C}
.

For a polynomial time bound
t
, let
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{tt}}
be the Boolean-function family whose length-
2^{n}
truth tables satisfy
Q_{0}^{t}
. That is,
f{\in}\mathcal{R}_{t,n}^{\mathrm{tt}}
iff the truth table of
f
has no description of length below the chosen randomness threshold that outputs it within
t(2^{n})
steps.

Assumption 4.7

(
Natural-Test Reflection
)
.

Fix a polynomial time bound
t
, and let
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{tt}}
be the Boolean-function family whose truth tables satisfy
Q_{0}^{t}
. No
\mathbf{P}/\mathrm{poly}
-natural property useful against
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{tt}}
gives feasible distinguishing access to the boundary between time-bounded-compressible truth tables and typical truth tables.

Informally, the forbidden object is a large efficient truth-table test that reliably separates low-time-complexity truth tables from typical truth tables. Such a test would expose exactly the kind of usable randomness-boundary information that the framework treats as unavailable.

Proposition 4.8
.

Assume
Natural-Test Reflection
. Then no
\mathbf{P}/\mathrm{poly}
-natural property useful against
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{tt}}
can serve as a lower-bound method for the random-information family covered by the framework.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{P}
is a
\mathbf{P}/\mathrm{poly}
-natural property with density
\delta_{n}{\geq}2^{-O(n)}
and useful against
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{tt}}
. By constructivity, membership in
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is decidable from the
2^{n}
-bit truth table by a
\mathbf{P}/\mathrm{poly}
procedure. By largeness, this procedure accepts a non-negligible fraction of truth tables. By usefulness, it rejects the truth tables in
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{tt}}
. Thus
\mathcal{P}
gives feasible distinguishing access between time-bounded-compressible truth tables and typical truth tables, contrary to
Natural-Test Reflection
. Hence any lower-bound method for the random-information family covered by the framework must be non-natural in the Razborov–Rudich sense.
∎

4.4
Derandomization

The final application concerns hardness-vs-randomness. For every polynomial time bound
t
, the predicate
Q_{0}^{t}
lies in
\mathbf{E}{:=}\mathrm{DTIME}(2^{O(n)})
: on input
z
of length
n
, one checks all programs of length
{<}n{-}d\log n
, simulates each for
t(n)
steps, and accepts iff none outputs
z
. Thus an exponential circuit lower bound for
Q_{0}^{t}
is exactly the kind of hard
\mathbf{E}
predicate that can feed the Impagliazzo–Wigderson
[
10
]
generator.

Assumption 4.9

(
Exponential Circuit Hardness for Random Information
)
.

There are a polynomial time bound
t
and
\epsilon{>}0
such that every Boolean circuit family deciding
Q_{0}^{t}
on inputs of length
n
has size at least
2^{\epsilon n}
for all sufficiently large
n
.

Proposition 4.10
.

Assume
Exponential Circuit Hardness for Random Information
. Then
\mathbf{P}{=}\mathbf{BPP}
.

Proof.

The predicate
Q_{0}^{t}
lies in
\mathbf{E}
by construction. By the Impagliazzo–Wigderson hardness-vs-randomness theorem, a language in
\mathbf{E}
with circuit complexity
2^{\Omega(n)}
yields a pseudorandom generator sufficient to derandomize
\mathbf{BPP}
, giving
\mathbf{BPP}{\subseteq}\mathbf{P}
. The reverse inclusion is immediate.
∎

The proposition identifies the exact derandomization target: exponential circuit hardness for the decidable predicate
Q_{0}^{t}
. The role of
KH
is to explain why such hardness should follow from the inaccessibility of genuine randomness facts. The remaining bridge is nonuniform and pointed: small circuits for a random-information problem must amount to usable access to particular axioms
x{\in}R
, and hence to weak-base certification of those axioms.

To connect this global predicate back to
KH
, one needs a pointed version of the time-bounded randomness problem. Fix, nonuniformly, an infinite sequence of true random strings
x_{n}{\in}R{\cap}\{0,1\}^{\ell(n)}
, with
\ell(n){\leq}n^{O(1)}
, such that
\mathrm{EA}{+}\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}}{\nvdash}x_{n}{\in}R
for all sufficiently large
n
. The sequence is not assumed to be computable; the uniformity lies only in the construction which, given a parameter
x
and a length
n
, produces a pointed predicate
A_{x,n}^{t}
. Intuitively,
A_{x,n}^{t}
is the time-bounded-randomness boundary problem associated with the axiom
x{\in}R
.

Assumption 4.11

(
Pointed Circuit-to-Randomness Reflection
)
.

For every polynomial time bound
t
and every nonuniform inaccessible sequence
(x_{n})_{n}
as above, if
A_{x_{n},n}^{t}
has Boolean circuits of size
2^{o(n)}
for infinitely many
n
, then
\mathrm{EA}{+}\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}}{\vdash}x_{n}{\in}R
for infinitely many such
n
.

Proposition 4.12
.

Assume
Pointed Circuit-to-Randomness Reflection
. Let
(x_{n})_{n}
be any infinite nonuniform sequence of true random strings
x_{n}{\in}R{\cap}\{0,1\}^{\ell(n)}
, where
\ell(n){\leq}n^{O(1)}
, such that
\mathrm{EA}{+}\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}}{\nvdash}x_{n}{\in}R
for all sufficiently large
n
. Then, for all sufficiently large
n
, the associated problems
A_{x_{n},n}^{t}
do not have Boolean circuits of size
2^{o(n)}
.

Proof.

If
A_{x_{n},n}^{t}
had Boolean circuits of size
2^{o(n)}
for infinitely many
n
, then
Pointed Circuit-to-Randomness Reflection
would imply
\mathrm{EA}{+}\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}}{\vdash}x_{n}{\in}R
for infinitely many such
n
. This contradicts the assumed weak-base inaccessibility of
x_{n}{\in}R
for all sufficiently large
n
. Hence such circuits can occur only for finitely many
n
.
∎

Natural-test reflection supplies the analogous compatibility statement for Razborov–Rudich natural proofs. A
\mathbf{P}/\mathrm{poly}
-natural property useful against
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{tt}}
would be a feasible test, operating on truth tables, that accepts a large set of typical truth tables while rejecting the time-bounded-randomness family. By constructivity, membership in the property is decidable from the
2^{n}
-bit truth table by a
\mathbf{P}/\mathrm{poly}
procedure; by largeness, it accepts a non-negligible fraction of truth tables; and by usefulness, it rejects the truth tables in
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{tt}}
. Thus it gives feasible distinguishing access between time-bounded-compressible truth tables and typical truth tables, contrary to
Natural-Test Reflection
. This is a compatibility claim, not a derivation of the Razborov–Rudich barrier from
KH
: the point is that both frameworks treat large efficient tests exposing randomness information as unavailable.

The framework therefore gives a strength-indexed route from
KH
to derandomization. A pointed bridge turns small circuits for
A_{x,n}^{t}
into usable access to the axiom
x{\in}R
; a global-to-pointed reduction then transfers this obstruction to
Q_{0}^{t}
. The quantitative strength of the obstruction determines the derandomization conclusion: superpolynomial pointed hardness yields low-end derandomization,
2^{n^{\delta}}
-hardness yields quasipolynomial-type derandomization through the Nisan–Wigderson regime, and
2^{\Omega(n)}
-hardness yields
\mathbf{P}{=}\mathbf{BPP}
by Impagliazzo–Wigderson.

4.5
Sparse Randomness and Feige-Style Refutation

The preceding derandomization discussion uses time-bounded Kolmogorov randomness to express the idea that efficient algorithms should not exploit randomness information unavailable to the relevant theory. A related issue arises for Feige’s random 3-
SAT
hypothesis. The usual predicate
R{=}\{x{:}K_{U}(x){\geq}|x|{-}d\log|x|\}
is not well matched to Feige-style sparsity, because it measures randomness among all bitstrings of a given length. A string of length
m
and Hamming weight
\rho m
, for fixed
\rho{<}1/2
, has at most
2^{H(\rho)m}
possibilities, and therefore cannot generally have Kolmogorov complexity close to
m
. To model sparse random formulas, the randomness predicate should instead be relative to the appropriate sparse ensemble.

4.5.1
Sparse Kolmogorov randomness

For
m{\in}\mathbb{N}
and
w{\leq}m
, define the sparse randomness predicate

R^{\mathrm{sp}}_{m,w}{=}\{x{\in}\{0,1\}^{m}:|x|_{1}{=}w\ {\land}\ K_{U}(x\mid m,w){\geq}\log\binom{m}{w}{-}d\log m\}
.
Thus
x
is random not among all
m
-bit strings, but among strings with exactly
w
ones. This is the entropy-correct notion of Kolmogorov randomness for sparse objects. Since
\log\binom{m}{w}{=}w\log(m/w){+}O(w)
, the density
w/m
may be far below
1/2
, provided the sparse slice still has enough entropy to support the finite-scale hardness being asserted.

This gives a natural Feige-style specialization. Let
\mathcal{C}_{n}
be the set of possible 3-literal clauses on
n
variables, so
|\mathcal{C}_{n}|{=}N_{n}{=}\Theta(n^{3})
. We use the distinct-clause model for notational simplicity. The usual model sampling
\Delta n
clauses independently with replacement differs from this model by total variation
o(1)
at constant density, since the probability of a repeated clause is
O((\Delta n)^{2}/N_{n}){=}O(1/n)
. Nothing in the argument depends on this distinction. A random 3-
CNF
formula with
\Delta n
clauses is represented by a subset
S{\subseteq}\mathcal{C}_{n}
of size
w_{n}{=}\Delta n
, equivalently by a bitstring of length
N_{n}
and Hamming weight
\Delta n
. Its ambient density is
\Delta n/N_{n}{=}\Theta(1/n^{2})
. Define

R^{\mathrm{Feige}}_{n,\Delta}{=}\{S{\subseteq}\mathcal{C}_{n}:|S|{=}\Delta n\ {\land}\ K_{U}(S\mid n,\Delta){\geq}\log\binom{N_{n}}{\Delta n}{-}d\log n\}
.
A formula in
R^{\mathrm{Feige}}_{n,\Delta}
is therefore Kolmogorov-random within the same sparse-support regime as Feige’s distribution.

This predicate has the right density profile. The support has only
\Delta n
clauses out of
\Theta(n^{3})
possible clauses, so the visible density is
\Theta(1/n^{2})
, not close to
1/2
. The randomness lies in the choice of the sparse support. Moreover, by the standard counting bound, all but an
n^{-d}
fraction of clause supports of size
\Delta n
lie in
R^{\mathrm{Feige}}_{n,\Delta}
. Thus replacing Feige-random formulas by formulas satisfying
R^{\mathrm{Feige}}_{n,\Delta}
does not change the distribution on a
1{-}o(1)
fraction of instances.

This subsubsection should be read as a density statement, not as a proof of Feige’s hypothesis. It shows that the information-constraint framework can be placed in a Feige-style entropy regime: the hard instances may be sparse random supports of clauses, rather than nearly unbiased bitstrings. To obtain Feige’s original random-refutation hypothesis, one still needs a bridge principle connecting successful refutation of the usual random 3-
CNF
distribution to feasible proofs of the corresponding instancewise unsatisfiability claims.

4.5.2
Bridge assumptions implying Feige’s hypothesis

We now isolate the bridge needed to derive Feige’s hypothesis from the finite-scale hardness framework. Write
\mathcal{F}_{n,\Delta}
for the distribution on
3
-
CNF
formulas on
n
variables obtained by drawing
\Delta n
clauses independently and uniformly from the
\Theta(n^{3})
possible
3
-literal clauses. Since repeated clauses occur with probability
o(1)
at constant density, we identify a formula with its clause support on the
1{-}o(1)
event that no repetition occurs. Feige’s hypothesis says that, for suitable constant densities
\Delta
, there is no polynomial-time proper refuter for random 3-
SAT
: no polynomial-time algorithm which never declares a satisfiable formula unsatisfiable and which declares
\Phi
unsatisfiable with probability
1{-}o(1)
over
\Phi{\sim}\mathcal{F}_{n,\Delta}
.

Assumption 4.13

(
Sparse Feige Hardness
)
.

For some sufficiently large constant
\Delta
and every sound admissible theory

\mathcal{S}
in the intended range: for a
1{-}o(1)
fraction of formulas

\Phi{\sim}\mathcal{F}_{n,\Delta}
satisfying
R^{\mathrm{Feige}}_{n,\Delta}
, the
theory
\mathcal{S}
has no polynomial-size proof of the instancewise claim

\texttt{Unsat}(\Phi)
.

Assumption 4.14

(
Refutation Reflection
)
.

Fix a sound admissible theory
\mathcal{S}
and a density
\Delta
. If there is a polynomial-time proper refuter succeeding with probability
1{-}o(1)
on
\Phi{\sim}\mathcal{F}_{n,\Delta}
, then, for a
1{-}o(1)
fraction of such formulas
\Phi
, the theory
\mathcal{S}
has polynomial-size proofs of
\texttt{Unsat}(\Phi)
.

Theorem 4.15
.

Assume
Sparse Feige Hardness
and
Refutation Reflection
. Then Feige’s random 3-
SAT
refutation hypothesis holds at density
\Delta
.

Proof.

Suppose toward a contradiction that there is a polynomial-time proper refuter succeeding with probability
1{-}o(1)
on
\Phi{\sim}\mathcal{F}_{n,\Delta}
. By
Refutation Reflection
, for a
1{-}o(1)
fraction of formulas
\Phi{\sim}\mathcal{F}_{n,\Delta}
, the theory
\mathcal{S}
has polynomial-size proofs of
\texttt{Unsat}(\Phi)
. By the counting bound above, a
1{-}o(1)
fraction of formulas from
\mathcal{F}_{n,\Delta}
also satisfy
R^{\mathrm{Feige}}_{n,\Delta}
. The intersection of these two
1{-}o(1)
events still has probability
1{-}o(1)
, and conditioning on
R^{\mathrm{Feige}}_{n,\Delta}
preserves a
1{-}o(1)
fraction because
R^{\mathrm{Feige}}_{n,\Delta}
itself has probability
1{-}o(1)
. Hence
\mathcal{S}
has polynomial-size proofs of
\texttt{Unsat}(\Phi)
for a
1{-}o(1)
fraction of the sparse Kolmogorov-random formulas covered by
Sparse Feige Hardness
. This contradicts
Sparse Feige Hardness
. Therefore no such polynomial-time proper refuter exists.
∎

What the theorem does and does not contain.

The deductive
step is immediate; the mathematical content sits earlier, in the full-measure
embedding of §
4.5.1
: a
1{-}o(1)
fraction
of
\mathcal{F}_{n,\Delta}
lies in
R^{\mathrm{Feige}}_{n,\Delta}
, so sparse
Kolmogorov-randomness is not a substitute distribution but a full-measure condition
inside Feige’s own distribution.
Sparse Feige Hardness
and

Refutation Reflection
are the load-bearing hypotheses; the theorem only
records that, once random refutation is read as an information constraint on sparse
random clause supports, Feige’s hypothesis is exactly their conjunction.

Disjoint
\mathbf{NP}
Pairs from Feasible Reflection

Disjoint
\mathbf{NP}
pairs are a standard interface between proof complexity and the existence of optimal proof systems. Recall that a
disjoint
\mathbf{NP}
pair
is a pair
(A,B)
of
\mathbf{NP}
languages with
A{\cap}B{=}\emptyset
; a
separator
is a set
C
with
A{\subseteq}C
and
C{\cap}B{=}\emptyset
; and the pair is
\mathbf{P}
-separable
if some such
C
lies in
\mathbf{P}
. Razborov’s canonical pair construction associates disjoint
\mathbf{NP}
pairs with propositional proof systems, and the Grollmann–Selman theory connects complete disjoint
\mathbf{NP}
pairs with optimal proof systems. This section gives the corresponding random-axiom boundary pair and isolates the reflection step needed to make it
\mathbf{P}
-inseparable.

The canonical pair.

Fix an admissible sound base theory
\mathcal{S}
. Define

A_{\mathcal{S}}{:=}\{(x,1^{m}):\text{there is an }\mathcal{S}{+}(x{\in}R)\text{-proof of }\bot\text{ of length }\leq m\},

and

B_{\mathcal{S}}{:=}\{(x,1^{m}):\text{there is an }\mathcal{S}\text{-proof of }\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+(x{\in}R)}(m)\text{ of length }\leq m\}.

Both languages are in
\mathbf{NP}
: membership in
A_{\mathcal{S}}
is witnessed by a contradiction proof, and membership in
B_{\mathcal{S}}
is witnessed by an
\mathcal{S}
-proof of the bounded-consistency statement. The verifier treats
x{\in}R
as the distinguished added axiom; it does not verify the truth of
x{\in}R
.

The pair
(A_{\mathcal{S}},B_{\mathcal{S}})
is disjoint. If
(x,1^{m}){\in}A_{\mathcal{S}}
, then
\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+(x{\in}R)}(m)
is false. Since
\mathcal{S}
is sound,
\mathcal{S}
cannot also have a proof of that false bounded-consistency statement. Hence
(x,1^{m}){\notin}B_{\mathcal{S}}
.

Assumption 5.1

(
Disjoint-Pair Feasible Reflection
)
.

If the canonical pair
(A_{\mathcal{S}},B_{\mathcal{S}})
is
\mathbf{P}
-separable at all sufficiently large random-axiom lengths, then the separator yields weak-base access to the random axiom or feasible bounded-consistency proofs for the random extension: for a density-
1
set of true
x{\in}R
at those lengths, either
\mathrm{EA}{+}\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}}{\vdash}x{\in}R
, or
\mathcal{S}{\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{23.674pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-23.674pt\raisebox{-1.96112pt}{\makebox[23.674pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}\hskip-23.674pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[23.674pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{n^{O(1)}}$}}}$}}}}\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+(x{\in}R)}(n)
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Assume
Disjoint-Pair Feasible Reflection
and
SETH-K-Finite
. Then there is no
\mathbf{P}
separator for the canonical random-axiom pair
(A_{\mathcal{S}},B_{\mathcal{S}})
whose separation is correct on all sufficiently large random-axiom lengths.

Proof.

Suppose such a
\mathbf{P}
separator existed. By
Disjoint-Pair Feasible Reflection
, for a density-
1
set of true
x{\in}R
at those lengths, either
\mathrm{EA}{+}\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}}{\vdash}x{\in}R
, or
\mathcal{S}{\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{23.674pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-23.674pt\raisebox{-1.96112pt}{\makebox[23.674pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}\hskip-23.674pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[23.674pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{n^{O(1)}}$}}}$}}}}\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+(x{\in}R)}(n)
. For sufficiently long true

, the first alternative contradicts the Chaitin threshold for

. The second contradicts

\mathbf{P}
separator exists.
∎

6
Independence: Status and Program

No formal independence result is currently known for any natural instance of
HRC
or
KH
. This is the most important metamathematical gap in the program. The principles are meant as external claims about genuine polynomial-size proof families: if a sound theory
\mathcal{S}
lacks a weak-base relative-consistency explanation for
\mathcal{S}{+}\phi
, then
\mathcal{S}
should not have polynomial-size proofs of
\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+\phi}(n)
. To turn this into an independence theorem, one must first decide which formal reading is being tested and then control the nonstandard proof objects that appear in models of arithmetic or set theory.

Internal and external readings.

For fixed
\mathcal{S}
,
\phi
, and
c
, let
\mathrm{Sim}^{c}_{\mathcal{S},\phi}(n)
be the arithmetical assertion that there is an
\mathcal{S}
-proof of
\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+\phi}(n)
of length at most
n^{c}
. The first-order shadow of an
HRC
instance is the sentence
\forall n\,\neg\mathrm{Sim}^{c}_{\mathcal{S},\phi}(n)
. Since
\mathrm{Sim}^{c}_{\mathcal{S},\phi}(n)
is a bounded existential and hence decidable, this shadow is
\Pi^{0}_{1}
, the same shape as
\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}}
; quantifying over the exponent under the asymptotic reading,
\forall c\,\exists^{\infty}n\,\neg\mathrm{Sim}^{c}_{\mathcal{S},\phi}(n)
, raises it to
\Pi^{0}_{2}
, matching the complexity of
FR
, whose antecedent “
\mathcal{S}
has poly-size proofs” is
\Sigma^{0}_{2}
. The restriction to random axioms in
KH
is what keeps the principle arithmetical at all: “
\phi
true” for arbitrary
\phi
invokes the Tarski truth predicate, whereas “
x\in R
” is
\Pi^{0}_{1}
, so the
\forall x
closure of
KH
stays at
\Pi^{0}_{2}
while the fully universal
\forall\mathcal{S}\,\forall\phi
schema, carrying the side conditions “sound” and “true,” is non-arithmetical.

This internal reading is useful for formal independence questions, but it is not the same as the intended external reading. The external reading says that there is no standard polynomial bound and no standard uniform family of genuine
\mathcal{S}
-proofs of
\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+\phi}(n)
. The distinction matters because nonstandard models may contain apparent proof codes, apparent exponents, and apparent simulations that do not correspond to any standard proof family.

The standardness obstacle.

The main obstacle is familiar from Gödelian reflection. If
\mathcal{S}
is consistent, recursively axiomatized, and strong enough for Gödel’s second incompleteness theorem, then
\mathcal{S}{+}\neg\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}}
is consistent. In any model
M{\models}{\mathcal{S}}{+}\neg\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}}
, the model contains a nonstandard object that it regards as a proof of contradiction. By formalized proof manipulation,
M
then regards every sentence as having an
\mathcal{S}
-proof. Thus raw internal proof existence cannot by itself distinguish genuine external proof families from nonstandard artifacts. The same problem appears for polynomial simulations: a model may believe that it has a polynomial-size proof family because its proof codes, exponents, or bounding functions are nonstandard.

This explains why
HRC
and
KH
are standardness-sensitive principles. They are not merely first-order assertions about an internal proof predicate. They assert that genuine external feasible proof families exist only when weak-base relative consistency explains them. A direct proof or refutation inside an ordinary ambient theory must therefore control the standard cut, not just the internal proof predicate.

Internal unprovability.

There is one simple Gödelian fact worth retaining. Any sufficiently explicit self-applicable
KH
schema for a theory
\mathcal{S}
implies
\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}}
. Indeed, if
\mathcal{S}
were inconsistent, then a fixed
\mathcal{S}
-proof of contradiction would remain a contradiction proof in every extension
\mathcal{S}{+}(x{\in}R)
, giving trivial short refutations of
\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+(x{\in}R)}(n)
for all large
n
. Thus a self-applicable
KH
assertion excluding such short refutations implies
\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}}
. By Gödel’s second incompleteness theorem,
\mathcal{S}
cannot prove that self-applicable assertion, assuming
\mathcal{S}
is consistent and satisfies the usual hypotheses.

The same observation applies to any self-applicable
HRC
schema strong enough to imply the corresponding
KH
instance. This does not prove independence from
\mathrm{ZFC}
or from stronger metatheories. It only explains why the universal all-theories formulation is necessarily external: no sufficiently strong sound theory should be expected to certify, from within itself, a principle that applies to itself and implies its own consistency.

Changing the ambient theory.

One might try to avoid this limitation by proving
KH
for
\mathcal{S}
in a stronger ambient theory
\mathcal{T}
. This is a legitimate strategy, but it does not remove the reflection issue; it only moves the boundary. There is a simple monotonicity principle. If
\mathcal{B}{\subseteq}\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{B}{\vdash}x{\in}R
, then
\mathcal{T}{\vdash}x{\in}R
. Equivalently, if
\mathcal{T}{\nvdash}x{\in}R
, then
\mathcal{B}{\nvdash}x{\in}R
. Thus strengthening the ambient theory can certify more randomness facts and can shrink the class of axioms that count as inaccessible, but it cannot make inaccessibility disappear altogether for any fixed sound computably axiomatized ambient theory.

The reason is the relativized Chaitin phenomenon. For each fixed sound computably axiomatized theory
\mathcal{T}
, the usual Chaitin argument applies with
\mathcal{T}
in place of
\mathcal{S}
: there is a constant
c_{\mathcal{T}}
such that
\mathcal{T}
proves no true assertion
K_{U}(x){>}c_{\mathcal{T}}
. Hence, for the logarithmic-deficiency predicate
R
, there is a threshold
\chi_{\mathcal{T},R}
such that every true
\mathcal{T}
-provable instance
x{\in}R
has
|x|{<}\chi_{\mathcal{T},R}
. In particular, even a strong ambient theory leaves all sufficiently long true randomness facts
x{\in}R
inaccessible relative to itself.

This explains the abstract’s qualification that a stronger ambient theory may itself be subject to
KH
. A proof of
KH
for
\mathcal{S}
inside
\mathcal{T}
would be informative, but if
\mathcal{T}
is also a sound computably axiomatized theory in the intended range of the framework, then the same random-axiom question can be asked again for
\mathcal{T}
. The hierarchy of ambient theories therefore does not terminate the issue by fiat. It yields a relative program: prove as much
KH
as possible for weaker theories inside stronger theories, while recognizing that every fixed sound computably axiomatized ambient theory has its own Chaitin boundary and hence its own potential
KH
instances.

The independence target.

The appropriate formal target is therefore not the external schema directly, but a first-order internal approximation. For fixed
\mathcal{S}
,
\phi
, and
c
, one can ask whether the internal sentence
\forall n\,\neg\mathrm{Sim}^{c}_{\mathcal{S},\phi}(n)
is independent of a background theory such as
\mathrm{ZFC}
. The positive consistency direction would follow from the actual truth of the lower bound in the standard model. The hard direction is consistency with its failure: one would need a model in which there are internally polynomial-size proofs of
\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+\phi}(n)
while the weak-base side condition
\mathrm{EA}{\nvdash}\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}}{\rightarrow}\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+\phi}
remains intact.

Thus the key open problem is a model-construction problem. One needs a nonstandard model, or a suitable cut inside such a model, closed under the relevant polynomial functions but not under stronger growth, carrying apparent feasible bounded-consistency proofs for the chosen extension. The difficulty is to keep the exponent standard and the proof lengths genuinely polynomial over the cut, rather than merely polynomial according to a nonstandard parameter. This is the point at which the independence program becomes a concrete technical problem rather than a slogan.

Program.

The independence program has three concrete tasks. First, fix robust first-order internal readings of the
HRC
and
KH
instances to be tested. Second, construct nonstandard models or cuts in which the internal lower bound fails because the model contains apparent feasible bounded-consistency proofs. Third, prove that the weak-base side condition remains true in the relevant sense, so that the model witnesses failure of the
HRC
direction rather than simply changing the relative-consistency facts.

Progress on any of these tasks would clarify the status of the proposed principles. A positive independence result would support the view that
HRC
and
KH
are genuine reflection principles rather than ordinary theorems awaiting proof. A failure of the program would also be informative: it might reveal that the principles are either too strong, too weakly formalized, or more tightly connected to existing proof-complexity lower bounds than the present framework currently shows.

Precedents from independence and unprovability.

The independence question for
KH
and its variants belongs to an existing line of work on whether central complexity-theoretic separations may outrun standard formal systems. Aaronson’s survey addresses the possible formal independence of
\mathbf{P}
versus
\mathbf{NP}

[
1
]
. Independence of
KH
would not by itself imply independence of
\mathbf{P}{\neq}\mathbf{NP}
. If
KH
or one of its finite-scale or hierarchy-level strengthenings implies standard separations, then an independence result for
KH
would show that the proposed proof-theoretic explanation of those separations outruns the ambient theory. It would not rule out the possibility that the same ambient theory proves
\mathbf{P}{\neq}\mathbf{NP}
by some different method.

Pudlák’s work on proof lengths, finite consistency, and the arithmetic/proof-complexity interface
[
24
,
25
]
provides the proof-theoretic precedent most relevant here: bounded consistency has a feasible proof theory distinct from the full consistency statements governed by Gödel incompleteness. Razborov’s unprovability results for circuit lower bounds in bounded arithmetic
[
27
]
give a complementary complexity-theoretic precedent: under suitable pseudorandomness assumptions, certain lower-bound statements themselves cannot be proved in weak theories. The present program should be read in this tradition. It does not assert formal independence from
\mathrm{ZFC}
or from any specific strong theory; rather, it asks whether the feasible bounded-consistency consequences associated with random-axiom extensions are inaccessible to the theories to which the corresponding
KH
instances apply.

7
Conclusion

This paper has developed a proof-theoretic framework for organizing canonical hardness in complexity theory. The starting point is the bounded-consistency characterization problem: when does a sound arithmetic theory
\mathcal{S}
have polynomial-size proofs of
\mathrm{Con}_{\mathcal{S}+\phi}(n)
? The known positive direction says that an
\mathrm{EA}
-level relative-consistency explanation yields feasible relative consistency and hence simulation.
HRC
proposes the converse: absent such a weak-base explanation, feasible bounded-consistency proofs should not exist. The random-axiom specialization,
KH
, applies this dividing line to true Kolmogorov-randomness facts
x{\in}R
, where Chaitin-style incompleteness supplies a canonical source of weak-base inaccessibility.

The central claim is that these principles isolate a coherent and testable source of hardness. Feasible proof-theoretic leverage should come with an explanation in the appropriate weak base; a theory should not obtain usable bounded-consistency information from a true random axiom
x{\in}R
unless that randomness fact is already weak-base accessible. This no-private-information principle is the common thread behind
HRC
,
KH
, and its strengthenings. The
Unifying Meta-Complexity Assumption
should be understood not as
KH
alone, but as the package generated by
KH
: its finite-scale, hierarchy-level, average-case, no-mutual-help, and bridge-strengthened forms, all governed by the same no-private-information principle.

Several pieces of the framework are unconditional or structural. The positive direction from weak-base relative consistency to feasible relative consistency gives the benchmark. Chaitin-style incompleteness gives only finitely many weak-base-accessible true randomness facts. The random-axiom formulation yields canonical bounded-consistency tautology families. The robustness and persistence results show that the construction is not an artifact of a particular universal machine or of small finite changes in the base. These results do not prove
KH
, but they explain why the random-axiom instances are natural hard candidates rather than arbitrary encodings.

The finite-scale and hierarchy-level principles give the main conditional payoff. Finite-scale
KH
yields dense hard families, rules out intermediate hard-density behavior, and supports no-mutual-help phenomena for independent random axioms. The hierarchy-level strengthening gives explicit dense separators through
\mathbf{PH}
, and in particular yields
\mathbf{PH}
noncollapse and the standard Karp–Lipton consequence
\texttt{SAT}{\notin}\mathbf{P}/\mathrm{poly}
. This is the most developed computational test case for the framework: it turns the proof-theoretic obstruction into explicit level-by-level complexity consequences.

The average-case, sparse-refutation, and random-information material should be read as calibrated extensions rather than as consequences of
KH
alone. Passing from the unbounded predicate
R
to time-bounded predicates such as
R_{t}
,
Q_{0}^{t}
, and the Liu–Pass time-bounded Kolmogorov predicate requires additional bridge assumptions. Passing to Feige’s random 3-
SAT
refutation hypothesis likewise requires a sparse-support predicate and reflection bridge: almost every Feige-random clause support is Kolmogorov-random in its sparse slice, but the implication to Feige depends on
Sparse Feige Hardness
and
Refutation Reflection
. With these bridges, the same boundary viewpoint gives conditional routes to one-way functions, natural-proofs-style limitations, derandomization, and random-refutation hardness; without them, these remain research targets. This separation is important. The paper’s core contribution is not to derive every frontier consequence from one conjecture, but to identify exactly where the proof-theoretic obstruction ends and where new calibration assumptions begin.

The framework is also calibrated against the classical proof barriers. It is not a relativizing proof strategy: the hard instances are defined through a fixed universal machine, arithmetized proof predicates, and weak-base access to concrete randomness facts
x{\in}R
, not through an oracle-invariant simulation argument. Relativizing the ambient computation changes the relevant Kolmogorov predicates and the available descriptions. This does not prove that the framework evades every strengthened barrier, such as algebrization, but it places the proposal on the non-relativizing side of the Baker–Gill–Solovay lesson. Likewise, the natural-proofs discussion is a compatibility result, not a derivation of the Razborov–Rudich barrier: the canonical hard families should require non-natural methods because large efficient tests exposing randomness information would themselves constitute the kind of feasible access the reflection principles forbid.

The resulting research program is concrete. One can try to prove restricted instances of
HRC
or
KH
for weak proof systems, search for nonstandard models witnessing the failure of converse reflection, sharpen the finite-scale thresholds, test the hierarchy-level predicates against known proof-complexity techniques, analyze the random-axiom disjoint
\mathbf{NP}
pairs, calibrate the
R_{t}
interface for average-case hardness, one-way functions, and derandomization, and determine whether the sparse-support reflection principles needed for Feige’s hypothesis hold in any natural proof-theoretic setting.

The contribution is a candidate organizing principle for lower-bound theory: feasible leverage requires weak-base explanatory access. In the bounded-consistency setting, this principle identifies the canonical hard instances and explains why random-axiom extensions should resist simulation. Its success would give a common source for hardness behind
\mathbf{P}{\neq}\mathbf{NP}
,
\mathbf{PH}
noncollapse, and related lower-bound problems; its failure would reveal a new mechanism by which proof systems extract feasible information from true random facts without weak-base certification.
