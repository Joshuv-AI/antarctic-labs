To select or not to select: predictively consistent priors instead of model selection
Anna Elisabeth Riha, Leevi Lindgren, David Kohns, Paul-Christian Bürkner, Aki Vehtari

Abstract
Bayesian modelling workflows often consider multiple candidate models of varying complexity. Model selection is commonly used to navigate potential trade-offs between model complexity and generalisability to new data. We study when model selection is unnecessary or can even be harmful for predictive performance in finite data regimes and find that the need for selecting simpler models can depend on prior choice. We formalise predictively consistent priors, which keep prior predictive implications stable as model complexity increases. Across examples and numerical experiments, including adding covariates in linear and logistic regression, forward variable selection, and nonlinear modelling, flexible models with predictively consistent priors typically match or outperform selected simpler models in out-of-sample predictive performance. When selection helps, it can indicate poor joint prior implications, such as excessive prior mass on implausible predictive values. Based on our findings, we propose replacing the notion of sparsity or parsimony at the level of model components with specifying priors that remain sensible in predictive space as models become more complex.

model selection, model comparison, prior specification, predictively consistent priors, Bayesian workflows

Introduction

Modellers often start with a simple model that is easy to fit, but, ultimately, want to safely iterate towards more complex models that predict well and are expressive yet computationally feasible
(
32
)
.
Alternative modelling choices, model checks, or iterative refinements can generate multiple candidate models, motivating transparent strategies for model expansion and comparison

(see, e.g.,
69
;
28
)
.

15
emphasise the need for both parsimonious and complex models for scientific progress.
Complex models are often more realistic
(
68
)
, but preferences or resource limits can motivate choosing a simpler candidate via model selection
(for reviews of Bayesian model selection and criteria of model quality, see
80
;
8
, , respectively)
.

Statistical folklore says that selecting models with fewer components can avoid overfitting, that is, it helps to balance accuracy on observed (training) data with generalisability on unseen (test) data
(
38
)
.
A typical textbook example of this is a polynomial regression where higher-degree polynomials eventually achieve an arbitrarily good fit to the given data, but predict poorly on out-of-sample data
\citetext
51
, Ch. 7
;
43
.
Model selection is a commonly suggested remedy, but has itself also been criticised to favour models that are prone to overfitting, for example, when considering stepwise selection from many candidate models
(
73
;
18
)
.

To give practical guidance for Bayesian modelling workflows, we examine when model selection is needed and when it may even harm out-of-sample predictive performance.
Further, we investigate how prior choices affect selection outcomes and the level of model complexity we can reach safely without deteriorating test performance.
To this end, we focus on additive models in finite data regimes, which covers a wide range of use cases across the quantitative sciences.
In particular, we

•

Provide general desiderata for good priors and specifically define predictively consistent priors (second-order consistency and predictive parsimony; Sections
3
-
4
).

•

Demonstrate predictively consistent priors in three case studies: adding covariates in linear and logistic models, forward stepwise selection, and increasing nonlinear complexity (Sections
4.2
,
5.1
-
5.3
).

•

Give an example of adjusting a joint prior towards predictive consistency for a randomised-controlled-trial setup with available prior knowledge for the treatment coefficient (Section
5.4
).

•

Summarize our main takeaways and practical recommendations (Section
1.1
).

Our main recommendation is to replace the notion of sparsity or parsimony at the level of model components with
predictively consistent priors
, that is, proper priors that retain consistent prior predictive distributions on the target space when model complexity increases
(
31
;
27
)
.
We operationalise this notion via the implied variance of the predictor distribution (intuition: Section
3.1
; formal definition: Section
3.2
).

When correctly integrating over uncertainties and using predictively consistent priors, we find that selecting a simpler model is often unnecessary.
Throughout our experiments in Section
5
, complex models with predictively consistent priors typically match or exceed the out-of-sample performance of selected simpler models; and when worse, the loss is usually negligible.
In some cases, selection within a set of nested models can even harm performance relative to using the biggest model.
If overfitting is a concern, this should be addressed in the initial (joint) priors rather than by selecting among models with predictively inconsistent priors that may favour overfitting.
Importantly, we are not saying model selection cannot be useful.
Resource constraints can force reductions in model size and selecting a simpler model can also mitigate the shortcomings of a prior.
In fact, when selection “helps”, it can indicate a poor prior with too much mass on nonsensical predictive values, motivating better joint prior specification upfront.

1.1
Takeaways for modellers

(1) Joint prior specification should consider independence assumptions and dependence structures between model components.

Seemingly weakly informative marginal priors like independent normal priors can imply surprisingly informative joint priors
(Figure
4
; see also
17
)
.
Independence is not a safe default as exemplified throughout Section
5.1
to
5.2
.
At the same time, when a component is truly independent as in Section
5.4
, assuming dependence can also lead to problematic prior predictives and worse predictive performance compared to a prior that reflects the dependence structure.

(2) Prior predictive checks and sensitivity analysis can reveal deficiencies.

Prior predictive checks often detect too diffuse priors producing nonsensical prior predictive distributions
(
23
;
32
)
and prior sensitivity analysis highlights influential choices and prior-data conflicts
(
44
)
.
Checking joint quantities and model summaries can reveal unintended joint effects of individually specified priors and predictive inconsistencies
(Figures
4
,
5
and
16
; see also
1
;
2
)
.

(3) With predictively consistent priors, model selection is not always needed.

Predictively consistent priors keep relevant features of the prior predictive distribution stable as model components are added (intuition: Section
3.1
; formal definition: Section
3.2
).
Across our experiments (Section
5
), posterior integration combined with such priors allows increasing model complexity without loss of out-of-sample performance and simpler models rarely outperform more complex ones
(see also
56
;
68
)
.

(4) Priors are important, even if the main goal is to reduce the model size.

When cost or explainability motivate reducing model size, projection predictive inference is recommended
(
36
;
63
;
62
;
59
;
52
)
.
Our experiments in Section
5.2
show that a predictively consistent prior can improve the forward search process of
projpred

(
61
)
, compared to the case when independent diffuse priors are used.

(5) Model selection being helpful might be an indication of a poor prior.

If model selection favours a simpler model over a more complex one, this can be caused by a prior placing too much probability mass on nonsensical predictive values favouring overfitting (see Figures
4
and
7
).
If selecting a simpler model seems to improve results, there is reason to revisit the chosen priors, especially, if wide default priors have been used or marginal priors have been chosen without considering potential dependence structures between model components.

1.2
Related work

1.2.1
In favour of big models

Building the largest, encompassing model when possible and computationally feasible is a repeatedly advocated idea
(
49
;
56
;
68
;
80
;
59
)
.
There are many reasons to build complex models, for example, to include all available information and allow flexibility for future applications of the model.
Established concepts like the bias-variance trade-off underline tensions between model complexity and predictive performance on test data
(
38
)
.
At the same time, complex yet well-generalising models, for example, Gaussian processes or (Bayesian) neural networks, suggest less straightforward relationships between complexity, bias, and variance
(
56
;
67
;
83
)
.

68
argue that parsimony need not imply that a simpler model would perform better, when Ockham’s razor is applied at the level of functional complexity.
In the context of generalisation phenomena of deep neural networks like double descent,

83
reasons that “restriction biases”
(
83
, p.3)
are not necessary to avoid overfitting and shows that similarly unintuitive generalisation also occurs in simpler models like linear regression models.

Even if we do not work with the largest model directly, for example, because it is computationally expensive, it can still be appealing to build a model that is as complex as is feasible.
Expanding a candidate model is often recommended, especially when the initial model does not satisfy criteria of model quality
\citetext
see, e.g.,
30
;

58
.
In real-world applications of Bayesian data analysis,
a typical workflow is to start with a simple, tractable model and iteratively expand toward more realistic models that capture the investigated phenomenon well, provide reliable posterior results and accurate predictions, while also performing model checking and monitoring computation and convergence and revising models if needed
(
32
)
.
As such, Bayesian workflows require strategies for safe model expansion, that is, increasing model complexity without losses in out-of-sample performance.
Moreover,
49
suggests performing model selection by identifying models that perform similarly to the largest model encompassing all uncertainty about the modelling problem.
In continuation and based on ideas by
35
and
16
, projection predictive inference finds simpler models with comparable predictive performance to a reference model
\citetext
36
;
62
.
Importantly, the quality of the results depends on the chosen reference model and we need well-performing complex models for successful projection predictive selection
(see, e.g.,
63
;
52
;
59
)
.

1.2.2
Prior specification and model complexity

Prior specification and model selection are separate tasks in Bayesian workflows
(see, e.g,
5
;
47
;
80
;
53
)
, but the motivation for employing model selection can help to set better priors from the start.
Particularly, if we care about posterior predictive performance on yet unobserved data, we need to consider the implications of our prior assumptions in predictive space
(
31
;
71
)
.
In practice, limited data availability restricts what we can learn and not all effects in our models can be large
(
75
)
, but that does not imply that some or most of the true effects are zero.
Instead of “sparsifying” the model after building it, priors can reflect a notion of sparsity that allows small or close-to-zero effects despite increasing model complexity.

83
recommends that modellers should
“embrace a flexible hypothesis space, with a soft preference for simpler solutions […] consistent with the data”
(
83
, p.1)
as a result of investigating generalisation properties of complex models like deep neural networks.
While targeting different models, this suggestion is conceptually close to our further discussion of desired properties of priors in Section
3.1
and
3.2
.

The more parameters a model has, the more challenging it can be to define meaningful individual priors that also imply sensible joint priors.
It is not enough to elicit priors on a parameter-to-parameter basis, as their joint behaviour affects inferences
(
31
;
54
)
.
Sometimes, a simpler model is used only because it is hard to come up with a sensible prior for a more complex version of the model
(
56
)
.
A range of existing approaches tackle aspects of this challenge, for example, by embedding sparsity assumptions with global-local shrinkage priors
(
65
)
, restricting predictive complexity with reference model priors
(
55
)
, considering a prior tree structure and a hierarchical decomposition of prior contributions for Bayesian hierarchical models
(
39
;
22
)
, or setting a prior on a joint quantity like the proportion of variance in the outcome explained by the model
(Bayesian
R^{2}
,
26
)
, that is propagated back to implied priors for the individual model components
\citetext
91
;
1
;
87
.
Another strategy to ensure sensible joint priors is setting priors on function space.
This is an integral part of approaches using Gaussian processes
(
67
)
and nonparametric Bayes
\citetext
40
;
33
, including tree-based function priors such as Bayesian additive regression trees
(
12
)
.
It has also been studied in the context of prior specification for Bayesian neural networks
(see, e.g.,
21
;
20
;
76
)
.
To enable a more thorough mathematical investigation, we focus primarily on additive models in this paper.

Evaluating predictive performance

We use the log score
\mathrm{u}(\mathrm{M}(y),\tilde{y})=\log p_{\mathrm{M}}(\tilde{y}\mid y)
as a default utility to evaluate a model’s predictive performance for new observations
(
24
;
5
;
34
)
.
The expected log predictive density (
\mathrm{elpd}
)
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{elpd}(\mathrm{M}\mid y)=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{y}}\big[\log p_{\mathrm{M}}(\tilde{y}\mid y)\big]

(4)

where the expectation is with respect to the DGP of
\tilde{y}
and
p_{\mathrm{M}}(\tilde{y}\mid y)=\int p_{\mathrm{M}}(\tilde{y}\mid\theta)p_{\mathrm{M}}(\theta\mid y)\text{d}\theta
is the posterior predictive density of
\mathrm{M}
.
Given independent test data
\{\tilde{y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{\text{test}}}
, we can estimate

\mathrm{elpd}_{\text{test}}(\mathrm{M}\mid y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{test}}}\log p(\tilde{y}_{i}\mid y)
.
The in-sample analogue is the log pointwise predictive density
\mathrm{lppd}(\mathrm{M}\mid y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p(y_{i}\mid y)
.
When no test data are available, leave-one-out cross-validation
(LOO-CV,
24
)
can approximate out-of-sample performance via
\mathrm{elpd}_{\text{loo}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p(y_{i}\mid y_{-i})
, where
p(y_{i}\mid y_{-i})=\int p(y_{i}\mid\theta)p(\theta\mid y_{-i})\text{d}\theta
is the LOO posterior predictive density obtained by fitting the model to the data without the
i

LOO-CV with Pareto-smoothed importance sampling
(PSIS,
78
;
81
)
avoids
n
refits and provides Pareto-
\hat{k}
values to diagnose the reliability of the estimates.
To support comparability, we report
\mathrm{elpd}_{\text{test}}
on the
\mathrm{elpd}_{\text{loo}}
scale, that is, scaled with
(n/n_{\text{test}})
.
On
\mathrm{elpd}_{\text{loo}}
scale, a difference
\leq 4
can indicate indistinguishable predictive performance
(
72
;
53
)
.

Overfitting

Overfitting is a commonly used concept to caution against “fitting to the data” without ensuring good test performance
(
38
)
.
It is difficult to assess for a single dataset and model, but, for two models
\mathrm{M}_{1}\subset\mathrm{M}_{2}
, we say
\mathrm{M}_{2}
is
overfitting relative to

\mathrm{M_{1}}
on the training data
y
if

\displaystyle\mathrm{lppd}(\mathrm{M}_{2}\mid y)\geq\mathrm{lppd}(\mathrm{M}_{1}\mid y)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{elpd}_{\text{test}}(\mathrm{M}_{2}\mid y)<\mathrm{elpd}_{\text{test}}(\mathrm{M}_{1}\mid y),

(5)

that is, if in-sample predictions improve (or remain the same) but out-of-sample performance reduces.
When a test set is unavailable, the second inequality can be replaced by
\mathrm{elpd}_{\text{loo}}(\mathrm{M}_{2}\mid y)<\mathrm{elpd}_{\text{loo}}(\mathrm{M}_{1}\mid y)
, or by corresponding
\mathrm{elpd}
s obtained from any other CV scheme.
If we can define a unique ordering based on model complexity, we can use the same reasoning for non-nested models.
There is no requirement that the models are true-structure models.
However, misspecified models can sometimes simultaneously overfit and underfit for different areas of observed data, for example, when fitting a linear model to data with nonlinear patterns or vice versa.

Best-predicting models

Following
3
, an
oracle model
is a model in
\mathcal{M}
that, after being fitted on training data
y=\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, has the lowest expected loss on unseen data
\tilde{y}
from the DGP.
With the log-score, this is equivalent to maximising
\mathrm{elpd}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{OR}}(y)\,\in\,\argmax_{\mathrm{M}\in\mathcal{M}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{y}}\!\left[\mathrm{u}\!\left(\mathrm{M}(y),\tilde{y}\right)\right]\,=\,\argmax_{\mathrm{M}\in\mathcal{M}}\,\mathrm{elpd}(\mathrm{M}\mid y).

(6)

For simplicity, we will just speak of
the
oracle model in the following, assuming its uniqueness.
The oracle depends on
y
and averaging additionally over
y
gives the
oracle in expectation
:

\argmax_{\mathrm{M}\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}_{y,\tilde{y}}\!\left[\mathrm{u}(\mathrm{M}(y),\tilde{y})\right]
, which is what we report when summarising over repeated simulations in Section
5
.
The oracle maximises expected utility within
\mathcal{M}
.
So, for any other model
\mathrm{M}\in\mathcal{M}\setminus\{\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{OR}}(y)\}
, we have
\mathrm{elpd}(\mathrm{M}\mid y)\leq\mathrm{elpd}(\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{OR}}(y)\mid y)
, with strict inequality whenever the maximiser is unique.
The oracle is defined only with respect to the collection
\mathcal{M}
, which does not need to contain any true-structure models.
Even when a true-structure model is included, the oracle does not need to be a true-structure model itself
(
3
, p. 46)
.
Following (
5
), models more complex than
\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{OR}}(y)
with lower expected predictive performance are overfitting, and models simpler than
\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{OR}}(y)
with lower expected predictive performance are underfitting.

3
Properties of good priors

Given a true-structure model, a “good” prior puts prior mass close to the true
\theta_{0}
and enables fast convergence of the posterior mass towards
\theta_{0}
.
There are no universally good priors, as choosing priors is always context-dependent
(
58
;
31
)
, but we can identify four desired properties
based on existing recommendations and discussions
(
31
;
54
;
71
)
.

3.1
Intuition

We want proper priors to make models (partially) generative before seeing data and enable well-defined prior predictive checks.

Good priors should provide a prior predictive distribution that generates data, which we would expect to observe
(
31
;
27
)
.
We can investigate this using prior predictive checking and prior sensitivity analysis
(
31
;
27
;
23
;
44
)
and by visualising joint quantities like a-priori Bayesian
R^{2}
to reveal unintended joint implications
(
2
)
.

We want to choose priors such that the model-generated data is sensible, that is, the prior predictive distributions reflect key characteristics of our modelling problem such as scale, variability or tail behaviour.

Joint prior behaviour affects inferences
(
31
;
54
)
.
When considering a collection of models, prior predictives should follow prior beliefs even as model complexity increases.
Predictively consistent priors, formally defined in Section
3.2
, satisfy this logical consistency requirement, for example, by keeping the prior predictive variance constant or restricting it such that it will not explode as the model complexity grows.
4
4

4

In case of modelling processes with infinite variance some other variability measure would be used.

When adding model components or otherwise increasing model complexity, the prior predictives should stay sensible and aligned with prior beliefs.

Most often, we can only keep some properties like the first two moments of the predictive distribution similar.
For nested models,
29
recommend using larger models with priors favouring “the region of the parameter space near the smaller model”
(
29
, p.131)
.
This is also the main principle for penalised complexity priors
(
71
)
and motivates a fourth desideratum.

For a more complex version of a simpler model, a good prior should be centred and place most mass on the simpler (base) model, especially when the predictive distribution changes with added model complexity.

Parsimony is complementary to, and weaker than, second-order predictive consistency (see Section
3.2
), but still a useful principle for setting priors on less intuitive parameters such as the degrees of freedom of the Student-
t
-distribution.
While predictive consistency often implies parsimony, exceptions are discussed in Lemma
3.2.3
.

3.2
Formalisation

We now formalise Desiderata
3.1
and
3.1
.
Consider a sequence of nested models
\{\mathrm{M}_{d}\}_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
with increasing complexity.
The complexity index
d
may refer to number of predictors, varying-effect terms, or group levels.
Each model
\mathrm{M}_{d}
has the following hierarchical structure:

1.

Hyperparameters:

(\zeta_{d},\psi)\sim p(\zeta_{d},\psi)
, where
\zeta_{d}
are prior hyperparameters for
\theta_{d}
(e.g., coefficient scales, group-level variances), and
\psi
collects likelihood parameters outside any latent hierarchy on
\theta_{d}
(e.g., residual variance, dispersion, degrees of freedom).

2.

Model parameters:

\theta_{d}\mid\zeta_{d}\sim p(\theta_{d}\mid\zeta_{d})
, using the first
d
model components, including latent group-level effects.

3.

Systematic component:

\eta_{d}:=f_{d}(x_{d};\theta_{d})
, a deterministic function of
\theta_{d}
and inputs
x_{d}
, including group-level design matrices.

4.

Observation model:

y\mid\eta_{d},\psi\sim p(y\mid\eta_{d},\psi)
, where only
\eta_{d}
depends on
\theta_{d}
and
\psi
are shared across all models.

The joint prior is
p_{d}(\theta_{d},\zeta_{d},\psi)=p(\theta_{d}\mid\zeta_{d})\,p(\zeta_{d},\psi)
.
Thus, the
d
-indexed hyperparameters
\zeta_{d}
affect
y
only through the path
\zeta_{d}\to\theta_{d}\to\eta_{d}\to y
. Let
x_{d}\sim p_{d}(x_{d})
be a single predictor observation from a reference distribution such that
x_{d}
is independent of parameters (
x_{d}\perp(\theta_{d},\zeta_{d},\psi)\ \forall d
) with
\mathbb{E}[x_{d}]=0
and unit marginal variance.
The
induced signal distribution

q_{d}(\eta_{d})
is the law of the scalar systematic component
\eta_{d}=f_{d}(x_{d};\theta_{d})
, obtained as the pushforward of the joint prior
p(x_{d},\theta_{d},\zeta_{d})
under
f_{d}
,

q_{d}(\eta_{d})=\int\mathbb{I}(\eta_{d}=f_{d}(x_{d};\theta_{d}))\,p(x_{d})\,p(\theta_{d},\zeta_{d})\,dx_{d}\,d\theta_{d}\,d\zeta_{d},

(10)

where
p_{d}(\theta_{d},\zeta_{d})
is the marginal prior of
(\theta_{d},\zeta_{d})
obtained from
p_{d}(\theta_{d},\zeta_{d},\psi)
by integrating out
\psi
.

Definition 3.2.1

(Second-order predictive consistency)
.

A prior sequence
\{p_{d}\}_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
is
second-order predictively consistent
if

1.

the prior predictive variance of the systematic component is globally bounded:

\sup_{d\in\mathbb{N}}\;\mathrm{Var}_{q_{d}}(\eta_{d})\leq C

(11)

for some constant
C\in\left[0,\infty\right)
, and

2.

the variance converges to a finite asymptotic limit,
L\in\left[0,\infty\right)
:

\lim_{d\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}_{q_{d}}(\eta_{d})=L.

(12)

Definition
3.2.1
uses the distribution over
\eta_{d}
rather than the prior predictive over
y
, since observation models with infinite or undefined marginal variance (e.g., Student-
t_{\nu}
with
\nu\leq 2
) violate the bound on global boundedness regardless of the prior, while for bounded responses (e.g., binary data) the prior predictive variance is trivially bounded and finite.
The supremum condition (
11
) allows for non-monotone variance sequences and ensures global boundedness.
Non-monotonicity can arise when the signal aggregates many components, for example, in Bayesian neural networks
(
48
)
, or when parameters are negatively correlated.
Note that
C
may be potentially zero for point priors.
The limit condition (
12
) ensures that the variance stabilises to some
L
, which, due to potential non-monotony, may differ from
C
.
5
5

5

We acknowledge that the limit condition (
12
) implies a finite global variance.
However, we would like to highlight explicitly that the variance sequence should be well-behaved and that the global and limiting variance may be two different values.

Together, this formalises Desideratum
3.1
: the prior predictive distribution stays sensible as model complexity grows precisely when the signal variance remains bounded and converges.

In Desideratum
3.1
, parsimony is complementary to, and usually weaker than, second-order predictive consistency: it does not require bounded variance, but controls how much the induced signal distribution changes across model sizes.
A natural formalisation is to require that the induced signal distributions do not diverge from one another as
d\rightarrow\infty
and that their pairwise divergences accumulate to a finite sum.

Definition 3.2.2

(Predictive parsimony)
.

Assume
q_{d+1}\ll q_{d}
for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
A prior sequence
\{p_{d}\}_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies
predictive parsimony
if the KL divergence between successive induced signal distributions is summable:

\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q_{d+1}\,\|\,q_{d}\right)<\infty.

(13)

The asymmetric direction
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{d+1}\|q_{d})
encodes a preference for the simpler model by penalising mass placed by
q_{d+1}
where
q_{d}
has zero support.
The absolute-continuity assumption
q_{d+1}\ll q_{d}
ensures each summand is finite.
For Gaussian signal families
q_{d}=\mathrm{normal}(0,V_{d})
, the KL divergence between successive models conditional on
V
is

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{d+1}\|q_{d})=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{V_{d+1}}{V_{d}}-1-\log\frac{V_{d+1}}{V_{d}}\right],

(14)

which depends only on the variance ratio
V_{d+1}/V_{d}
.

Lemma 3.2.3

(Relationship between predictive consistency and parsimony)
.

Let
q_{d}=\mathrm{normal}(0,V_{d})
with
V_{d}>0
for all
d
. Write
\mathrm{S}
for sequences satisfying second-order predictive consistency (Definition
3.2.1
) and
\mathrm{F}
for those satisfying predictive parsimony (Definition
3.2.2
). Then:

1.

\mathrm{S}\not\subseteq\mathrm{F}
: bounded and convergent variance need not imply parsimony. For example, the oscillating but convergent series
|V_{d+1}-V_{d}|=\Omega(d^{-1/2})
does not fulfil parsimony.

2.

\mathrm{F}\not\subseteq\mathrm{S}
: parsimony does not necessarily imply bounded variance. For example, the independent normal prior (
V_{d}=d\overline{\tau}^{2}
) is in
\mathrm{F}
but not
\mathrm{S}
.

3.

\mathrm{S}\cap\mathrm{F}
: a prior sequence is in both
\mathrm{S}
and
\mathrm{F}
if and only if
V_{d}
is bounded, convergent,
\inf_{d}V_{d}>0
, and
\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}(V_{d+1}-V_{d})^{2}<\infty
. Monotone convergence to a strictly positive limit is a sufficient condition.

The proof is given in Appendix
B
.
A key consequence is that predictive parsimony alone is too weak to exclude independent normal priors: each added predictor changes the signal distribution only marginally (
\overline{\tau}^{2}
out of
d\overline{\tau}^{2}
, giving
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{d+1}\|q_{d})\approx 1/(4d^{2})
,
summable), yet the variance increments are multiples of
d
, so
V_{d}=d\overline{\tau}^{2}
diverges for any
\overline{\tau}>0
.

3.2.1
Predictive consistency and parsimony for global-local priors

For the linear model (
19
), the widely used class of global-local scale-mixture priors
(
65
)
takes the conditional form
\theta_{j}\mid\zeta_{d}\sim\mathrm{normal}(0,\tau^{2}\phi_{j})
, where
\zeta_{d}=(\tau^{2},\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d})
contains the global scale
\tau^{2}
and local weights
\phi_{j}\geq 0
.
Typically, heavy-tailed priors are chosen for the local weights and global scale to simultaneously shrink noise heavily to zero and only weakly regularise signals.
For centred priors and a standardised predictor reference distribution, the decomposition under global local priors becomes:

\mathrm{Var}_{q_{d}}(\eta_{d})=\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}}\!\left[\tau^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\phi_{j}\right].

(15)

This yields a necessary and sufficient condition for predictive consistency:

Corollary 3.2.4

(Characterisation of predictive consistency for global-local priors)
.

Consider the linear model (
19
) with standardised predictor reference distribution and a centred global-local prior with
\theta_{j}\mid\zeta_{d}\sim\mathrm{normal}(0,\tau^{2}\phi_{j})
.
The prior sequence is second-order predictively consistent (Definition
3.2.1
) if and only if

\sup_{d\in\mathbb{N}}\;\;\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}}\!\left[\tau^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\phi_{j}\right]<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{d\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}}\!\left[\tau^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\phi_{j}\right]\;\text{exists}.

(16)

Proof.

Both conditions are direct translations of (
11
) and (
12
) via the identity (
15
).
\square

For any global-local prior with local weights satisfying
\sum_{j}\phi_{j}=1
, Corollary
3.2.4
reduces second order predictive consistency to requiring
\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}}[\tau^{2}]<\infty
, independently of model size.
Some global-local priors respect this condition, others can be set up to be predictively consistent, see Appendix
E.1
for more details.

Parsimony for global-local priors.

When the local weights are constrained to the simplex, that is
\sum_{j}\phi_{j}=1
,
and the prior on
\tau^{2}
does not depend on
d
, the conditional variance
\mathrm{Var}(\eta_{d}\mid\tau^{2},\phi)=\tau^{2}
is independent of
d
.
With a Gaussian conditional prior on
\theta_{d}
, the variance sequence
V_{d}
entering Lemma
3.2.3
is constant in
d
, so
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{d+1}\|q_{d})=0
in the variance-only view.
The full mixture distributions
q_{d}
still depend weakly on
d
through the law of
\tau^{2}\sum_{j}\phi_{j}x_{d,j}^{2}
, but this dependence vanishes as
d\to\infty
(e.g., by concentration of
\sum_{j}\phi_{j}x_{d,j}^{2}
around its mean when
\phi_{j}=1/d
).
For unconstrained global-local priors like the horseshoe prior
(
11
)
, parsimony additionally requires square-summable relative increments of
S_{d}:=\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}}[\tau^{2}\sum_{j}\phi_{j}]
, which must be verified case by case.

Implications of Desiderata 3-4 on shrinkage

In the context of global-local priors, shrinkage factors
\kappa_{j}=(1+\sigma^{-2}\tau^{2}\phi_{j})^{-1}
are routinely analysed in order to characterise the properties of the chosen hyper-priors on the implied distribution of effective non-zero coefficients defined as
m_{\text{eff},d}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}(1-\kappa_{j})

(
64
)
6
6

6

Whenever the observation model is not normal, one may use the approximating variance
\tilde{\sigma}^{2}
as defined in
64
.
Because
\frac{x}{\sigma^{2}+x}\leq\frac{x}{\sigma^{2}}
for any
x\geq 0
, we can establish a strict upper bound on the expected effective number of parameters, conditional on
\sigma^{2}
:

\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}}[m_{\text{eff},d}]=\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\tau^{2}\phi_{j}}{\sigma^{2}+\tau^{2}\phi_{j}}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\tau^{2}\phi_{j}}{\sigma^{2}}\right]=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}}\left[\tau^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\phi_{j}\right]\leq C/\sigma^{2}.

(17)

However, predictive parsimony, alone does not guarantee a bound on the effective number of non-zero coefficients, since under the isotropic normal prior with fixed variance
\overline{\tau}^{2}
:

m_{\text{eff},d}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\bar{\tau}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+\bar{\tau}^{2}}\right)=d\left(\frac{\bar{\tau}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+\bar{\tau}^{2}}\right).

(18)

As
d\to\infty
, the effective model size
m_{\text{eff},d}\to\infty
.

Generality beyond additive normal models.

Since Definitions
3.2.1
and
3.2.2
are stated in terms of the induced signal distribution
q_{d}
and its variance
\mathrm{Var}_{q_{d}}(\eta_{d})
, they apply not only to additive normal regression but to any model for which this quantity is well-defined, including models where the role of
\theta_{d}
may be taken by an infinite-dimensional latent stochastic process, such as in a Gaussian process.
In such cases, the definitions still provide a verifiable criterion and can be evaluated via prior predictive simulations.

\displaystyle y\sim\mathrm{normal}\left(f(x_{d};\theta_{d}),\sigma^{2}\right)\;\;\text{with}\;\;\theta_{d},\sigma^{2}\sim p_{d}(\theta_{d},\sigma^{2}),

where

is the linear predictor over the first

\theta_{d}

We assume the moments

Denoting
\Sigma_{x,d}=\mathrm{Cov}_{p_{d}}(x_{d})

\mathrm{Var}_{q_{d}}(\eta_{d})=\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\Sigma_{x,d}\Sigma_{\theta,d}\right)+\mu_{\theta,d}^{T}\Sigma_{x,d}\mu_{\theta,d}.

If, in addition, the prior is centred so that

\mathrm{Var}_{q_{d}}(\eta_{d})=\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\Sigma_{\theta,d}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathrm{Var}_{p_{d}}(\theta_{j}).

(21)

For independent normal priors

4.3
Priors on function space

Function-space priors provide yet another framework for constructing predictively consistent priors.
They allow eliciting prior knowledge directly on properties of functions, like smoothness, periodicity, or stationarity, rather than individual coefficients.
When these are chosen explicitly to bound the function’s marginal variance, they ensure the total signal variance remains controlled as more complexity is added.
In Section
5.3
, we use Gaussian process priors which are among the most commonly used function-space priors
(e.g.,
67
)
.

The Hilbert space approximate Gaussian process
(HSGP,
70
;
74
)
used in Section
5.3
approximates a Gaussian process by a truncated basis expansion

\displaystyle\eta_{k}=f_{k}(x)\approx\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(S_{\theta}\!\left(\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\right)\right)^{1/2}\phi_{j}(x)\,\beta_{j},

(24)

with
\beta_{j}\sim\mathrm{normal}(0,1)
,
eigenvectors
\phi_{j}
and eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}
of the covariance operator of the kernel, spectral density
S_{\theta}
, and length-scale
\ell
and marginal scale of the GP
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{GP}}
as hyperparameters
\theta=(\ell,\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{GP}})
.
For fixed
\theta
,
\mathrm{Var}\big(f_{k}(x)\mid\theta\big)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}S_{\theta}\big(\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\big)\,\phi_{j}^{2}(x)
converges to
\kappa_{\theta}(x,x)=\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{GP}}
as
k\to\infty
.
Integrating over any proper prior on
\theta
and averaging over any reference predictor distribution
p(x)
gives
\mathrm{Var}_{q_{k}}(\eta_{k})\leq\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{GP}}]
for all
k
, so HSGPs satisfy Definition
3.2.1
with
C=\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{GP}}]
.
In contrast, for raw polynomials
f_{k}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\beta_{j}x^{j}
with independent
\beta_{j}\sim\mathcal{\mathrm{normal}}(0,\overline{\tau}^{2})
and fixed
\overline{\tau}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{q_{k}}\big(\eta_{k}\big)=\overline{\tau}^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\mathbb{E}[x^{2j}].

(25)

If
p(x)
places non–negligible mass near or beyond
|x|=1
, the partial sums
\sum_{j=0}^{k}\mathbb{E}[x^{2j}]
grow with
k
.
For instance, they diverge when
\Pr(|x|\geq 1+\delta)>0
, and even for
x\sim\mathrm{Uniform}[-1,1]
.

10
10

10

Note that if
|x|\leq c<1
almost surely, the series converges.

This violates Definition
3.2.1
and helps to explain the decline in test performance observed in our experiments in Section
5.3
.

More generally, for any model
f_{d}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}w_{j}(x)\,\beta_{j}
with
\beta\sim\mathcal{\mathrm{normal}}(0,\Sigma_{d})
,
second-order predictive consistency holds if
\sup_{d}\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[w_{d}(x)^{T}\Sigma_{d}\,w_{d}(x)\big]<\infty
.
When
\Sigma_{d}=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{j}^{2})
, this reduces to
\sup_{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sigma_{j}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\big[w_{j}(x)^{2}\big]<\infty
.
This includes the linear model with
w_{j}(x)=x_{j}
and the bound from
R^{2}
, the HSGP with
w_{j}(x)=S_{\theta}(\sqrt{\lambda_{j}})^{1/2}\phi_{j}(x)
and the bound from
\kappa_{\theta}(x,x)
, and basis-function expansions generally.

5.4
Experiment 4: Randomised control treatment effect studies

Previous sections focused on predictive performance, demonstrating that the best predictive performance is obtained using all relevant data combined with predictively consistent priors.
We now turn to causal inference: estimating the average treatment effect in a randomised controlled trial (RCT), a standard methodology for studying causal effects of interventions from economics to medicine
(
14
)
.
We demonstrate that conditioning on high-dimensional covariate sets, which are predictive of the target and not the treatment probability, can improve inference for the treatment effect.
However, predictive
consistency alone in the sense of Definition
3.2.1
is not sufficient to realise these efficiency gains and may even increase bias compared to not using the covariates in the model at all.
We develop theory to support the simulation findings.

We are interested in estimation of the following model:

y_{i}\sim\mathrm{normal}(\alpha z_{i}+x_{i}^{T}\beta,\sigma^{2}),

(30)

where the treatment assignment
z_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.5)
for
i=1,\dotsc,n
and covariates
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
with columns drawn independently from a standard normal distribution.
Here, since
z\perp X
, the inclusion of
X
into the model are assumed not to have a bias effect and ideally have a variance reduction effect onto the posterior of
\alpha
.
13
13

13

In the parlance of
13

6
Discussion

This work revisits the role of model selection in Bayesian workflows and argues that it is not a substitute for building good models in the first place.
We investigate when selecting a simpler model is necessary, when it can harm out-of-sample predictive performance, and how prior choices affect safe model expansion.
Our main proposal is to use
predictively consistent
priors instead of enforcing parsimony at the level of individual model components through model selection.

We define predictively consistent and parsimonious priors in Section
3.2
and illustrate how such priors can make model selection unnecessary in settings where it is often recommended to avoid overfitting.
Our results lead to concrete recommendations for checking joint prior implications and building complex yet well-generalising models (Section
1.1
).
Across our examples and experiments in Section
4.2
and
5
,
large models with predictively consistent priors usually reach equal or better out-of-sample predictive performance than selected simpler models.
When simpler models perform better, gains are usually small.
In some cases, selection from a nested set of models even harms test performance relative to using the largest model with a suitable joint prior.
This also matters for iterative modelling workflows.
A common concern is that expanding models step by step can lead to overfitting.
Our results suggest that predictively consistent priors can safeguard against deteriorating test performance and thereby support safe model expansion.

The relevant question is not whether a prior is specified jointly or component-wise, but whether its joint implications reflect the structure of the modelling problem.
This is especially important for models with many covariates, where reasonable marginal priors can still imply an unreasonable prior predictive distribution.
Independence is not a safe default, but imposing dependence can also be harmful.
Section
5.4
further illustrates that prior structure can matter for inference, not only for prediction.
In the considered setting, splitting the prior on the treatment effect and the other covariate effects can yield sharper posterior intervals without adding bias, whereas a joint prior over treatment and covariate effects can introduce bias.

Priors based on interpretable joint quantities are one practical way to make prior specification more manageable
(see, e.g.,
1
;
2
)
.
We discuss R2D2 priors and function-space priors as two examples for constructing predictively consistent
priors.
Other priors may also satisfy predictive consistency, provided that they control the induced signal distribution as complexity increases.
Thus, the main message is not tied to a particular prior family, but the question is whether the chosen prior keeps the prior predictive distribution sensible and stable with increasing model complexity.

Our work has several limitations.
First, we focus on additive models.
This allows a clean formalisation, but also limits the scope of our results.
Future work should extend the concept of predictively consistent priors to non-additive modelling scenarios.
Second, our experiments focus on scenarios with
n\geq p
.
We expect the problematic patterns observed for independent normal priors to get much worse when
n<p
.
However, a systematic investigation of other
n
-
p
regimes remains open.
Third, we compare only selected prior configurations.
Results for other priors might fall between the considered comparison points, for example, when scaling normal priors depending on the number of covariates.
Such
p
-scaled normal priors would also restrict prior predictive variance but at the cost of shrinking all coefficients strongly towards zero.
More work is needed to compare a wider range of models and prior choices.

Finally, most of our experiments focus on predictive performance, assuming that the considered models are causally sensible.
In more realistic cases of causal analysis, we should not blindly include all possible potential predictors, but need to consider the causal structure
(e.g., possible confounders, colliders, and backdoor effects, see
51
)
to include only predictors that would minimise the bias in the estimation of the effect of interest.
Predictive consistency alone does not guarantee unbiased estimation of a causal effect.
Our example in Section
5.4
probes aspects of this in one controlled setting.
More work is needed to define desirable prior properties for causal analyses, where the focus is not only on predictive performance.

establishing the equivalence
\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}D_{\mathrm{KL}}<\infty\iff\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\delta_{d+1}^{2}<\infty
.

Part (ii): Sufficiency.

If
|\delta_{d+1}|\leq A\,d^{-g}
for some
A>0
and
g>1/2
, then
\delta_{d+1}^{2}\leq A^{2}d^{-2g}
and
\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}d^{-2g}<\infty
since
2g>1
.

Part (ii): Sharpness.

Fix
0<g\leq 1/2
and choose
V_{1}
large enough that
V_{d}:=V_{1}+\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}(-1)^{k}k^{-g}\geq V_{\min}>0
for all
d
(possible since the alternating series converges by Leibniz’s test, so the partial sums are bounded).
Then
V_{d}\to L:=V_{1}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}k^{-g}<\infty
and
\sup_{d}V_{d}<\infty
, so Definition
3.2.1
is satisfied.
However,
\delta_{d+1}=(-1)^{d}d^{-g}
, giving
\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\delta_{d+1}^{2}=\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}d^{-2g}=\infty
since
2g\leq 1
.
By part (i),
\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}D_{\mathrm{KL}}=\infty
.
∎

Connections between
\mathrm{Var}(\eta)
, Bayesian
R^{2}
, and
\mathrm{elpd}

We assume a data-generating process
y\sim\mathrm{normal}(x^{T}\beta^{*},\sigma^{2*})
and the model

\displaystyle y\mid\beta,\sigma^{2}\sim\mathrm{normal}(x^{T}\beta,\sigma^{2}).

(37)

Our prior assumptions for
\beta
influence
\Sigma_{\beta}=\mathrm{Var}(\beta)
.
This affects the scale of the linear predictor
\eta(x)=x^{T}\beta
, since the marginal variance of the linear predictor is
\mathrm{Var}\left(\eta(x)\right)=x^{T}\mathrm{Var}(\beta)x=x^{T}\Sigma_{\beta}x
.
For data
D=\{x_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and new observation
\{\tilde{x},\tilde{y}\}
, we can approximate the posterior predictive distribution
p(\tilde{y}\mid\tilde{x},D)
with
\mathrm{normal}(\mu(\tilde{x}),v(\tilde{x}))
, where

\displaystyle\mu(\tilde{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\eta(\tilde{x})\mid D\right]=\tilde{x}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\beta\mid D\right]

(38)

\displaystyle v(\tilde{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma^{2}\mid D\right]+\mathrm{Var}(\eta(\tilde{x})\mid D)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma^{2}\mid D\right]+\tilde{x}^{T}\mathrm{Var}(\beta\mid D)\tilde{x}.

(39)

This means that the predictive variance
v(\tilde{x})
at
\tilde{x}
is a sum of the posterior mean of the observation noise variance and the posterior variance of the linear predictor.
For a normal model with normal prior
\beta\sim\mathrm{normal}(0,\Sigma_{\beta})
, the posterior mean is
\mathbb{E}[\beta\mid D]=(X^{T}X+\sigma^{2}\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1})^{-1}X^{T}y
(with design matrix
X
and outcome vector
y
) and it becomes apparent that
\Sigma_{\beta}
influences both predictive mean and variance.

Using
\mu(\tilde{x})
and
v(\tilde{x})
, the pointwise log predictive density for new observation
\{\tilde{x},\tilde{y}\}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathrm{lpd}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})=\log p(\tilde{y}\mid\tilde{x},D)\approx-\frac{1}{2}\left[\log(2\pi v(\tilde{x}))+\frac{(\tilde{y}-\mu(\tilde{x}))^{2}}{v(\tilde{x})}\right].

(40)

Taking the expectation with respect to the true DGP

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{lpd}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left[\log(2\pi v(\tilde{x}))+\frac{(\tilde{y}-\mu(\tilde{x}))^{2}}{v(\tilde{x})}\right]\right]

(41)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi v(\tilde{x}))-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[(\tilde{y}-\mu(\tilde{x}))^{2}\right]}{v(\tilde{x})}

(42)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)-\frac{1}{2}\log(v(\tilde{x}))-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}_{*}+(\tilde{x}^{T}\beta_{*}-\mu(\tilde{x}))^{2}}{v(\tilde{x})},

(43)

where we explicitly separate the true noise variance
\sigma^{2}_{*}
from the model parameter
\sigma^{2}

(see (

We see that
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{lpd}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})]
is affected by
(\tilde{x}^{T}\beta_{*}-\mu(\tilde{x}))^{2}
and
v(\tilde{x})
through
-\frac{1}{2}\log(v(\tilde{x}))
and
-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}_{*}+(\tilde{x}^{T}\beta_{*}-\mu(\tilde{x}))^{2}}{v(\tilde{x})}
.
When
\mathrm{Var}(\eta(x))
increases,
v(\tilde{x})
increases.
Then,
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{lpd}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})]
is penalised more with
-\frac{1}{2}\log(v(\tilde{x}))
.

The approximation with a normal distribution, and, therefore, also (
40
), are only exact for a conjugate model with normal priors and known
\sigma^{2}
.
Otherwise, we can use posterior draws

and
\sigma^{2(s)}

\displaystyle\approx\widehat{\mu}(x)=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\eta^{(s)}(x)

(44)

\mathrm{lpd}

v(x)

\displaystyle\mathrm{lpd}(x,y)

\displaystyle\approx\log\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\left[\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2(s)}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(y-\eta^{(s)}(x)\right)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2(s)}}\right)\right].

(46)

Random

.

F.3.1
Proofs for the Treatment Effect Experiment

We provide formal derivations for the results stated in Section
5.4
. Consider the model in Equation (
30
) in vector form:
y=\alpha z+X\beta+\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon\sim\mathrm{normal}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{n})
, and independent priors
\alpha\sim\mathrm{normal}(0,\tau_{\alpha}^{2})
and
\beta\sim\mathrm{normal}(0,\Sigma_{\beta})
.

The following assumptions are maintained throughout all propositions in this section unless stated otherwise.

Maintained assumptions.

(A1)

Well-specified model.
The data-generating process is
y=\alpha^{*}z+X\beta^{*}+\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon\sim\mathrm{normal}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{n})
, and the model is the normal linear model in Equation (
30
). In particular, the likelihood is correctly specified.

(A2)

Randomised treatment.
Treatment assignments
z_{i}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.5)
independently of
X
and
\varepsilon
.

(A3)

Covariate regularity.
The columns of
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
are drawn independently from a sub-Gaussian distribution with zero mean and unit variance (the standard normal is the leading case). In particular, columns of
X
are independent of
z
.

(A4)

Gaussian conditional priors.
Conditional on all hyperparameters,
\alpha\sim\mathrm{normal}(0,\tau_{\alpha}^{2})
and
\beta\sim\mathrm{normal}(0,\Sigma_{\beta})
independently, with
\Sigma_{\beta}
positive definite. All derivations condition on
\sigma^{2}
.

(A5)

Rank condition.

\mathrm{rank}(X)=\min(n,p)
almost surely, which holds with probability one under Assumption
(A3)

(
82
)
.

Proposition F.3.1

(Marginal posterior variance of
\alpha
)
.

The conditional posterior
(\alpha,\beta)\mid y,X,z,\sigma^{2}
is multivariate normal. The marginal posterior variance of
\alpha
is

\mathrm{Var}(\alpha\mid y,X,z,\sigma^{2})=\left[\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}z^{T}(I_{n}-H_{\beta})z+\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}\right]^{-1},

(55)

where
H_{\beta}=X(X^{T}X+\sigma^{2}\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1})^{-1}X^{T}
, and the posterior mean is

\mathbb{E}[\alpha\mid y,X,z,\sigma^{2}]=\mathrm{Var}(\alpha\mid y,X,z,\sigma^{2})\cdot\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}z^{T}(I_{n}-H_{\beta})y.

(56)

Proof.

Write
y\sim\mathrm{normal}(W\vartheta,\sigma^{2}I_{n})
with
W=[z\;X]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(p+1)}
and
\vartheta=(\alpha,\beta^{T})^{T}
. The prior is
\vartheta\sim\mathrm{normal}(0,\Sigma_{\vartheta})
with
\Sigma_{\vartheta}=\mathrm{diag}(\tau_{\alpha}^{2},\Sigma_{\beta})
. The posterior precision matrix is

\Lambda=\sigma^{-2}W^{T}W+\Sigma_{\vartheta}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma^{-2}z^{T}z+\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}&\sigma^{-2}z^{T}X\\
\sigma^{-2}X^{T}z&\sigma^{-2}X^{T}X+\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

(57)

Denote the blocks as
\Lambda_{11}=\sigma^{-2}z^{T}z+\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}
,
\Lambda_{12}=\sigma^{-2}z^{T}X
,
\Lambda_{22}=\sigma^{-2}X^{T}X+\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1}
. By the Schur complement formula for block matrices, the
(1,1)
entry of
\Lambda^{-1}
is

\mathrm{Var}(\alpha\mid\cdot)=\left(\Lambda_{11}-\Lambda_{12}\Lambda_{22}^{-1}\Lambda_{21}\right)^{-1}.

(58)

Computing the off-diagonal product:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{12}\Lambda_{22}^{-1}\Lambda_{21}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-4}z^{T}X\left(\sigma^{-2}X^{T}X+\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1}\right)^{-1}X^{T}z

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-4}z^{T}X\cdot\sigma^{2}\left(X^{T}X+\sigma^{2}\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1}\right)^{-1}X^{T}z

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-2}z^{T}\underbrace{X\left(X^{T}X+\sigma^{2}\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1}\right)^{-1}X^{T}}_{=H_{\beta}}z.

(59)

Substituting into the Schur complement:

\Lambda_{11}-\Lambda_{12}\Lambda_{22}^{-1}\Lambda_{21}=\sigma^{-2}z^{T}z-\sigma^{-2}z^{T}H_{\beta}z+\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}=\sigma^{-2}z^{T}(I_{n}-H_{\beta})z+\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}.

(60)

For the posterior mean, the standard formula for partitioned normal posteriors gives:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\alpha\mid\cdot]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}(\alpha\mid\cdot)\left[\sigma^{-2}z^{T}y-\Lambda_{12}\Lambda_{22}^{-1}\cdot\sigma^{-2}X^{T}y\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}(\alpha\mid\cdot)\cdot\sigma^{-2}\left[z^{T}y-z^{T}H_{\beta}y\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}(\alpha\mid\cdot)\cdot\sigma^{-2}z^{T}(I_{n}-H_{\beta})y.

\square

Throughout the remainder of this section we use the following notation:
H_{\beta}=X(X^{T}X+\sigma^{2}\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1})^{-1}X^{T}
,
d_{z}=\sigma^{-2}z^{T}(I_{n}-H_{\beta})z
, and
s=d_{z}/(d_{z}+\tau_{\alpha}^{-2})
. We write
\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{\min(n,p)}
for the non-zero eigenvalues of
X^{T}X
with associated left singular vectors
u_{1},\dots,u_{\min(n,p)}
, and
P_{X}
for the projection onto
\mathrm{col}(X)
. The statements that follow consider two regimes:

(i)

Finite-sample

n
fixed,
p
increasing towards
n
— analysed non-asymptotically via exact identities, monotonicity in
p

bounds;

(ii)

Proportional

p=\lfloor\gamma n\rfloor
with
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
n\to\infty
— where the random-matrix
\Theta/o_{p}

Lemma F.3.2

(Basis-free identity for the residualised data precision)
.

Under Assumptions
(A2)
and
(A4)
,

\mathbb{E}[d_{z}\mid X,\Sigma_{\beta}]=\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\bigl[\bigl(n-\mathrm{tr}(H_{\beta})\bigr)+\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}(I_{n}-H_{\beta})\mathbf{1}_{n}\bigr],

(61)

with
\mathrm{tr}(H_{\beta})\in[0,p)
strictly whenever
\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1}\succ 0
.

Proof.
By Assumption
(A2)
,
z_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.5)
independently, so
\mathbb{E}[z]=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{n}
and
\mathrm{Cov}(z)=\frac{1}{4}I_{n}
. Applying
\mathbb{E}[z^{T}Az]=\mathbb{E}[z]^{T}A\mathbb{E}[z]+\mathrm{tr}(A\cdot\mathrm{Cov}(z))
with
A=I_{n}-H_{\beta}
yields (
61
). For any
\Sigma_{\beta}\succ 0
,
H_{\beta}=X(X^{T}X+\sigma^{2}\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1})^{-1}X^{T}
satisfies
0\preceq H_{\beta}\prec P_{X}
(the strict upper bound follows from
\sigma^{2}\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1}\succ 0
), so all non-zero eigenvalues of
H_{\beta}
lie in
[0,1)
and
\mathrm{tr}(H_{\beta})<p
strictly. The argument is basis-free and does not require
\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1}
and
X^{T}X
to commute.
\square

Finite-sample regime:
n
fixed,
p\to n

The propositions in this subsection characterise
\mathbb{E}[d_{z}\mid X,\phi]
, the prior precision
\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}
, the shrinkage factor
s=d_{z}/(d_{z}+\tau_{\alpha}^{-2})
, the posterior precision

, and the posterior variance

in regime (i).

Proposition F.3.3

(Normal prior, regime (i))
.

Suppose
\Sigma_{\beta}=I_{p}
and
\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. The eigendecomposition of
H_{\beta}
gives

\mathbb{E}[d_{z}\mid X]=\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\nu_{j}+\sigma^{2}}\bigl[1+(u_{j}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n})^{2}\bigr]+\frac{n-p}{4\sigma^{2}}+\frac{\|(I_{n}-P_{X})\mathbf{1}_{n}\|^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}.

(62)

As
p\to n
, the second and third terms vanish but the regularisation sum (first term) is strictly positive: the ridge penalty
\sigma^{2}I_{p}
keeps
H_{\beta}\prec I_{n}
even at
p=n
. Hence

(a)

\mathbb{E}[d_{z}\mid X]
is strictly positive and bounded above by
n/(2\sigma^{2})
;

(b)

the posterior precision
d_{z}+\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}
lies in
(\tau_{\alpha}^{-2},\infty)
bounded;

(c)

s=d_{z}/(d_{z}+\tau_{\alpha}^{-2})
lies in
(0,1)
bounded away from both endpoints;

(d)

the posterior variance
(d_{z}+\tau_{\alpha}^{-2})^{-1}
lies in
(0,\tau_{\alpha}^{2})
without attaining
\tau_{\alpha}^{2}
.

Proof.
For
\Sigma_{\beta}=I_{p}
,
\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1}=I_{p}
commutes with
X^{T}X
, so
H_{\beta}
is co-diagonalisable with
X^{T}X
and its non-zero eigenvalues equal
\nu_{j}/(\nu_{j}+\sigma^{2})
. Hence
\mathrm{tr}(H_{\beta})=\sum_{j}\nu_{j}/(\nu_{j}+\sigma^{2})
and
\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}(I_{n}-H_{\beta})\mathbf{1}_{n}=\sum_{j}[\sigma^{2}/(\nu_{j}+\sigma^{2})](u_{j}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n})^{2}+\|(I_{n}-P_{X})\mathbf{1}_{n}\|^{2}
. Substituting into Lemma
F.3.2
gives (
62
). Each summand
\sigma^{2}/(\nu_{j}+\sigma^{2})
lies in
(0,1]
and is strictly positive whenever
\nu_{j}<\infty
, so the regularisation sum is
>0
for every
p\leq n
. As
p\to n
,
(n-p)\to 0
and
\|(I_{n}-P_{X})\mathbf{1}_{n}\|^{2}\to 0
, so
\mathbb{E}[d_{z}\mid X]>0
throughout. The upper bound
\mathbb{E}[d_{z}\mid X]\leq n/(2\sigma^{2})
follows from
\mathrm{tr}(H_{\beta})\geq 0
(so
n-\mathrm{tr}(H_{\beta})\leq n
) and
\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}(I_{n}-H_{\beta})\mathbf{1}_{n}\leq\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n}=n
(as

). The conclusions on posterior precision,

and
(d_{z}+\tau_{\alpha}^{-2})^{-1}
, using
\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}=\mathcal{O}(1)
.
\square

Proposition F.3.4

(Joint R2D2 prior, regime (i))
.

Suppose
(\alpha,\beta)
share an R2D2 prior with weights
\phi=(\phi_{\alpha},\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{p})\sim\mathrm{Dirichlet}(a,\dots,a)
of dimension
p+1
for some
a>0
, so
\tau_{\alpha}^{2}=\tau^{2}\phi_{\alpha}
and
\beta_{j}\sim\mathrm{normal}(0,\tau^{2}\phi_{j})
. By Dirichlet aggregation,
\phi_{\alpha}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(a,pa)
. Then:

(a)

the conditional prior precision
\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}=1/(\tau^{2}\phi_{\alpha})
grows linearly in
p
: for
a>1
, the exact Beta inverse moment gives
\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}]=(a(p+1)-1)/[(a-1)\tau^{2}]=\Theta(p/\tau^{2})
; for any
a>0
, the in-probability statement
\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}=\Theta_{p}(p/\tau^{2})
holds (for
a\leq 1
the moment
\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}]
diverges due to mass near
\phi_{\alpha}=0
, but the in-probability statement is unaffected);

(b)

conditional on
\phi
, the prior on
\beta
provides ridge-type regularisation with effective per-coordinate penalty
\sigma^{2}/(\tau^{2}\phi_{j})
, so the eigenvalues of
H_{\beta}=X(X^{T}X+\sigma^{2}\mathrm{diag}(1/(\tau^{2}\phi_{j})))^{-1}X^{T}
are strictly below
1
, and
\mathbb{E}[d_{z}\mid X,\phi]
is strictly positive and bounded as
p\to n
;

(c)

the shrinkage factor
s=d_{z}/(d_{z}+\tau_{\alpha}^{-2})
is stochastically decreasing in
p
at stochastic scale
s=\Theta_{p}(1/p)
(no limit is taken at fixed
n
): quantitatively,
1-s\geq c\,p/(c\,p+d_{z,\max})
, where
c=\Theta_{p}(1/\tau^{2})
and
d_{z,\max}=\sup_{p\leq n}\mathbb{E}[d_{z}\mid X,\phi]<\infty
, so
s
attains its strictly positive minimum at
p=n
;

(d)

the posterior precision
d_{z}+\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}=\Theta_{p}(p)
is dominated by the prior contribution, so the posterior variance is
(d_{z}+\tau_{\alpha}^{-2})^{-1}=\Theta_{p}(1/p)
. The posterior mean
s\,\hat{\alpha}_{\text{OLS}_{\beta}}
is centred at a fraction
s
of
\hat{\alpha}_{\text{OLS}_{\beta}}
that decreases stochastically in
p
. In the proportional regime this fraction stays bounded away from
1
in probability (Proposition
F.3.8
), leaving a non-vanishing multiplicative bias rather than a collapse to zero.

Proof.
For (a),
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{\alpha}]=1/(p+1)
and, for
a>1
, the standard Beta inverse moment
\mathbb{E}[1/\phi_{\alpha}]=(a+pa-1)/(a-1)
gives
\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}]=(a(p+1)-1)/[(a-1)\tau^{2}]
. For any
a>0
,
\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{\alpha})=p/[(p+1)^{2}(pa+a+1)]
, so the squared coefficient of variation is
\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{\alpha})/\mathbb{E}[\phi_{\alpha}]^{2}=p/(pa+a+1)\to 1/a
, a positive constant. The weight
\phi_{\alpha}
therefore does
not
concentrate at its mean for fixed
a
. Instead
(p+1)\phi_{\alpha}\to\mathrm{Gamma}(a,a)
in distribution, a non-degenerate positive limit, so
(p+1)\phi_{\alpha}=\Theta_{p}(1)
and
\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}=1/(\tau^{2}\phi_{\alpha})=\Theta_{p}(p/\tau^{2})
. The order statement holds for every
a>0
, reflecting that the limit is bounded away from
0
and
\infty
in probability rather than concentration of
\phi_{\alpha}
at
1/(p+1)
. For (b), conditional on any draw of
\phi
with
\phi_{j}>0
,
\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1}=\mathrm{diag}(1/(\tau^{2}\phi_{j}))\succ 0
. Since
\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1}
is heterogeneous, it does not commute with the dense
X^{T}X
, so
H_{\beta}
does not share the eigenbasis of
X^{T}X
at finite
p
and the per-coordinate formula
\nu_{j}/(\nu_{j}+\sigma^{2}/(\tau^{2}\phi_{j}))
is not literally an eigenvalue identity. We use a Weyl-type sandwich (Weyl’s monotonicity inequality for self-adjoint matrices): setting
c_{\min}=\min_{j}1/(\tau^{2}\phi_{j})
and
c_{\max}=\max_{j}1/(\tau^{2}\phi_{j})
, the operator inequality
c_{\min}I_{p}\preceq\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1}\preceq c_{\max}I_{p}
implies

\frac{\nu_{j}}{\nu_{j}+\sigma^{2}c_{\max}}\;\leq\;\lambda_{j}(H_{\beta})\;\leq\;\frac{\nu_{j}}{\nu_{j}+\sigma^{2}c_{\min}}\quad(j=1,\dots,p),

(63)

where
\lambda_{j}(H_{\beta})
are the non-zero eigenvalues of
H_{\beta}
in
\mathrm{col}(X)
(the remaining
n-p
eigenvalues are
0
). Since
\sigma^{2}c_{\min}>0
, every
\lambda_{j}(H_{\beta})<1
strictly, so by Lemma
F.3.2

\mathbb{E}[d_{z}\mid X,\phi]
is strictly positive and bounded as
p\to n
. For (c) and (d), since
p\leq n
is bounded and
\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}
is stochastically increasing in
p
(from (a)) while
d_{z}
is bounded (from (b)),
s=d_{z}/(d_{z}+\tau_{\alpha}^{-2})
is stochastically decreasing in
p
. The lower bound
1-s=\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}/(\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}+d_{z})\geq\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}/(\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}+d_{z,\max})

Proposition F.3.5

(Heavy-shrinkage tightening for the joint R2D2 prior)
.

Under the setting of Proposition
F.3.4
, suppose the heavy-shrinkage condition

\frac{p+1}{n}\;\gg\;\frac{R^{2}}{1-R^{2}}

(64)

\phi

F.3.2
reduces to

\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}=\Theta_{p}(p/\tau^{2})
from Proposition
F.3.4
and evaluation at the prior mean
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{\alpha}]=1/(p+1)
, the shrinkage factor satisfies

(66)

The approximation is valid for any
(n,p)
for which (

in Appendix

Proof.

through the Weyl sandwich (

for every

\square

Proposition F.3.7

(Normal prior, regime (ii))
.

Under the setting of Proposition
F.3.3
and Assumption
(A3)
, the empirical spectral distribution of

converges to the Marchenko–Pastur law on
[(1-\sqrt{\gamma})^{2},(1+\sqrt{\gamma})^{2}]

(
50

4
)
, so
\nu_{j}=\Theta(n)
uniformly in

and the regularisation sum in (
62

. Two terms of (
62

and the treatment-mean term
\|(I_{n}-P_{X})\mathbf{1}_{n}\|^{2}/(4\sigma^{2})

Proposition F.3.8

(Joint R2D2 prior, regime (ii))
.

Under the setting of Proposition
F.3.4
, in regime (ii):

(a)

F.3.2

Frequentist coverage

Proposition F.3.10

(Frequentist coverage of the posterior credible interval)
.

Let
d_{z}=\sigma^{-2}z^{T}(I_{n}-H_{\beta})z
denote the residualised data precision for
\alpha
, let
s=d_{z}/(d_{z}+\tau_{\alpha}^{-2})
denote the shrinkage factor, and let
\alpha^{*}
denote the true treatment effect. The
(1-\omega)
central posterior credible interval has length

L=2\,z_{\omega/2}\big/\sqrt{d_{z}+\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}},

(67)

where
z_{\omega/2}=\Phi^{-1}(1-\omega/2)
is the standard normal quantile, and its frequentist coverage probability is

C=\Phi\!\!\left(\frac{z_{\omega/2}+\delta}{\sqrt{s}}\right)-\Phi\!\!\left(\frac{-z_{\omega/2}+\delta}{\sqrt{s}}\right),

(68)

where
\delta=(1-s)\,|\alpha^{*}|\sqrt{d_{z}+\tau_{\alpha}^{-2}}
is the bias-to-posterior-standard-deviation ratio. For the split and base model priors (
s\approx 1
,
\delta\approx 0
), coverage is near-nominal. For the joint R2D2 prior (
s\ll 1
,
\delta
large), coverage can fall far below the nominal level. (We write the credible level as
\omega
to avoid collision with the aspect ratio
\gamma=p/n
of regime (ii).)

Proof.

From Proposition
F.3.1
, the posterior
\alpha\mid y,X,z,\sigma^{2}
is normal with mean
\hat{\alpha}_{\text{post}}=s\cdot\hat{\alpha}_{\text{OLS}_{\beta}}
and variance
\sigma_{\text{post}}^{2}=(d_{z}+\tau_{\alpha}^{-2})^{-1}
, where
\hat{\alpha}_{\text{OLS}_{\beta}}=[z^{T}(I_{n}-H_{\beta})z]^{-1}z^{T}(I_{n}-H_{\beta})y
is the regularised least-squares estimate from Proposition
F.3.1
. The
(1-\omega)
central credible interval is
\hat{\alpha}_{\text{post}}\pm z_{\omega/2}\,\sigma_{\text{post}}
, so its length is
L=2\,z_{\omega/2}\,\sigma_{\text{post}}
.

Writing
A=I_{n}-H_{\beta}
and
b=z^{T}Az
, under the data-generating process
y=\alpha^{*}z+X\beta^{*}+\varepsilon
the conditional frequentist moments of
\hat{\alpha}_{\text{OLS}_{\beta}}
given
X,z
are

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\alpha}_{\text{OLS}_{\beta}}\mid X,z]=\alpha^{*}+\frac{z^{T}AX\beta^{*}}{b},\qquad\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\alpha}_{\text{OLS}_{\beta}}\mid X,z)=\frac{\sigma^{2}\,z^{T}A^{2}z}{b^{2}}.

(69)

We invoke two simplifications. The variance simplification is exact in the flat-prior limit
H_{\beta}=P_{X}
(where
A^{2}=A
) and asymptotically exact in the heavy-shrinkage limit
H_{\beta}\to 0
(where
A\to I_{n}
, so
A^{2}\to A
); the confounding term, by contrast, is identically zero only in the flat-prior limit (
AX=0
) and is merely
negligible
(not zero) under heavy shrinkage:

•

Confounding term.
Under Assumption
(A2)
(
z\perp X
), taking expectation over
z
alone,
\mathbb{E}_{z}[z^{T}AX\beta^{*}\mid X]=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}AX\beta^{*}
. Because the columns of
X
are mean-zero,
\mathbf{1}_{n}
is essentially uncorrelated with
\mathrm{col}(X)
, so
\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}AX\beta^{*}=O_{p}(\sqrt{np})
; and because
z^{T}AX\beta^{*}
is
linear
in
z
, a sub-Gaussian (Hoeffding/Bernstein) bound controls its fluctuation by
\|AX\beta^{*}\|\leq\|X\beta^{*}\|=\Theta(\sqrt{np})
. With
b=z^{T}Az=\Theta(n)
this gives
z^{T}AX\beta^{*}/b=O_{p}(\sqrt{p/n})
under the experiment’s design (
\|\beta^{*}\|^{2}=\Theta(p)
). The term is exactly zero in the flat-prior limit (
AX=0
); in the heavy-shrinkage limit
A\to I_{n}
it leaves
z^{T}X\beta^{*}/b\neq 0
, but it is negligible
relative to
the shrinkage bias
(1-s)|\alpha^{*}|
, since the same design gives
\alpha^{*}=\Theta(\sqrt{p})
and hence a ratio
\Theta(1/\sqrt{n})\to 0
. We therefore absorb it into the
o_{p}(1)
correction.

•

Variance term.

A
is symmetric with eigenvalues
1-h_{j}\in(0,1]
, so
A^{2}\preceq A
and
z^{T}A^{2}z\leq z^{T}Az
, with equality when every
h_{j}\in\{0,1\}
. Approximating
z^{T}A^{2}z\approx z^{T}Az
gives
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\alpha}_{\text{OLS}_{\beta}}\mid X,z)\approx\sigma^{2}/b=d_{z}^{-1}
.

Under these approximations, the frequentist bias of
\hat{\alpha}_{\text{post}}=s\hat{\alpha}_{\text{OLS}_{\beta}}
is
(s-1)\alpha^{*}
, and its frequentist variance is
s^{2}\cdot d_{z}^{-1}=s\cdot\sigma_{\text{post}}^{2}
(using
d_{z}\sigma_{\text{post}}^{2}=s
). The standardised frequentist error
(\hat{\alpha}_{\text{post}}-\alpha^{*})/\sigma_{\text{post}}
is therefore distributed as
\mathrm{normal}(-\delta,s)
, where
\delta=(1-s)\,|\alpha^{*}|/\sigma_{\text{post}}
. The credible interval
\hat{\alpha}_{\text{post}}\pm z_{\omega/2}\,\sigma_{\text{post}}
covers
\alpha^{*}
if and only if
|\hat{\alpha}_{\text{post}}-\alpha^{*}|\leq z_{\omega/2}\,\sigma_{\text{post}}
. Standardising:

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\Pr\!\left(\left|\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{\text{post}}-\alpha^{*}}{\sigma_{\text{post}}}\right|\leq z_{\omega/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\!\left(\frac{-z_{\omega/2}+\delta}{\sqrt{s}}\leq Z\leq\frac{z_{\omega/2}+\delta}{\sqrt{s}}\right),

(70)

where
Z\sim\mathrm{normal}(0,1)
, yielding Equation (
68
).
For the split prior (
s\approx 1
,
\delta\approx 0
),
C\approx 1-\omega
.
For the joint R2D2 prior,
\delta
grows with
|\alpha^{*}|
and
(1-s)
, while the interval boundaries widen by
1/\sqrt{s}
; the net effect is that the bias shifts the interval away from
\alpha^{*}
, producing undercoverage.
\square
