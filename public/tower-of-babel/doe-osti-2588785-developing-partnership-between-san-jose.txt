 Abstract
One of the key objectives of this project was to develop partnership between San Jose State
University (SJSU) and the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL), a US Department
of Energy (DOE) funded national laboratory. Both institutions are closely located within the San
Francisco Bay Area in California and their researchers share overlapping research interests and
expertise related to Earth system sciences. By leveraging their connections with LLNL, faculty
and students from SJSU gained exposure to state-of-the-art observations and simulations related
to Earth system sciences, including but not limited to the usage of the facility data provided by
the DOE Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) program and data analysis techniques for
interpreting and analyzing the DOE Energy Exascale Earth System Model (E3SM) simulations.
Specifically, four main tasks were accomplished to foster new research training and education
opportunities for SJSU faculty and students in collaboration with LLNL scientists:
1) Enhancing observational data analysis proficiency among SJSU faculty and students
through training from LLNL scientists on utilizing publicly available DOE ARM observations.
2) Building numerical modeling expertise at SJSU and developing new capabilities enabling
SJSU students and researchers to conduct data analysis and simulations based on the DOE
E3SM.
3) Broadening collaboration by submitting new research proposals to various DOE funding
opportunities and preparing manuscripts to peer-reviewed journals.
4) Providing internship opportunities for SJSU students and supporting their career
advancement in Earth system sciences.
Overall, this funded project established a sustainable, long-term partnership between SJSU and
LLNL by leveraging existing connections. It has generated long-term impacts by enhancing and
broadening the knowledge and skill sets of the faculty and students at SJSU.

1. Objectives, Approaches Used, and Findings
1.1 Objectives of the project
The primary objectives of this project were to:
(1) Enhance the data analysis capabilities of SJSU faculty and students for utilizing DOE ARM
observational datasets.
(2) Improve the evaluation of DOE E3SM model simulations by developing fundamental skills in
data analysis and numerical modeling.
(3) Foster sustainable collaboration by submitting new research proposals to various DOE Funding
Opportunity Announcements (FOAs).
(4) Provide career development support to SJSU students through internships and career fairs.
1.2 Approaches used
A multi-faceted approach was developed to strengthen collaboration between SJSU and LLNL
scientists:
 (1) Establishing Regular Engagement: We instituted recurring meetings between the SJSU
 team and LLNL collaborators. For example, PI Diao secured a regular visitor badge for
 LLNL in collaboration with Dr. Shaocheng Xie, facilitating consistent on-site, in-person
 meetings that significantly enhanced the connection between the two institutions.
 (2) Pursuing Follow-on Funding: Building on the RDPP grant, the team actively pursued
 subsequent funding opportunities. This included submissions to the DOE FAIR FOA
       (2023), the DOE ESMDA FOA (2024), and the DOE ASR FOA (2023, 2024, 2025). A
 significant outcome was the successful acquisition of a DOE CRC grant in 2023, awarded
 in collaboration with LLNL scientist Dr. Qi Tang. This CRC proposal was developed
 through direct discussions during Dr. Diao’s site visits, demonstrating a direct benefit of
 the RDPP-funded collaboration.
 (3) Disseminating Research Findings: The team prioritized the publication of research
 results in peer-reviewed journals. To date, this project has partially supported four journal
 articles, the details of which are provided in Section 2.
 (4) Mentoring Early-Career Researchers: The project supported two graduate students and
 one postdoctoral researcher. Student Ching An Yang completed internships at LLNL in
 Summer 2023 and 2024 and subsequently commenced her PhD studies at the University
 of Michigan in Fall 2024, where she was awarded a prestigious Rackham Merit Fellowship
 given to top 5% of the incoming students. Another student, Deren Ngo, attended an LLNL-
 hosted career fair in 2024. The postdoctoral researcher, Dr. Neel Desai, engaged with
 LLNL scientists online and at conferences and has since been hired as an adjunct faculty
 member at SJSU.
1.3 Key findings
This research yielded several critical insights into cloud phase distributions and the performance
of the DOE E3SM model:

(1) Hemispheric Asymmetry in Cloud Phase (Yang, Diao et al., GRL, 2025)
Analysis of DOE ARM campaigns (ISDAC, M-PACE, ACME) and NSF-funded campaigns
revealed a significant hemispheric asymmetry. The Southern Hemisphere exhibits a higher
occurrence frequency and mass fraction of supercooled liquid clouds than the Northern
Hemisphere. The E3SM simulations failed to capture this asymmetry, indicating a potential model
deficiency in representing the unbalanced cloud feedback to a changing climate between
hemispheres.
(2) High-Latitude Cloud Phase Biases (Desai, Diao et al., 2025, JGR-Atmospheres)
A comparison of DOE ARM MARCUS (Southern Hemisphere) and COMBLE (Northern
Hemisphere) campaigns showed that northern high latitudes have a lower ice phase occurrence
frequency than southern high latitudes. For all cloud types (low-, mid-, high-level, and convective),
the E3SM model consistently overestimated the supercooled liquid phase. While the model
accurately simulated cloud top and base height, these persistent phase biases across latitudes and
cloud types suggest a potential link to errors in simulated relative humidity.
(3) Hydrometeor Classification and Vertical Structure (Desai, Diao et al., 2023)
Using DOE ARM MARCUS data, we classified eight hydrometeor categories (ice, liquid, mixed
phase, rain, drizzle, snow, aerosols, and clear sky) from lidar, radar, and radiosonde measurements.
Results confirmed that cloud vertical structure is latitude-dependent and reiterated the E3SM's
consistent overestimation of supercooled liquid water. These phase biases were present even in
single-phase clouds (i.e., a vertical column of clouds with a single phase), indicating they cannot
be explained by the model's inherent misrepresentation of vertical cloud layer heterogeneity.
(4) Synoptic Influences on Cloud and Radiation Biases (Barone, Diao et al., 2024)
Analysis of the ARM MICRE and AWARE campaigns quantified the influence of synoptic
dynamics using a cyclone compositing method. Observations showed a stronger asymmetry in
cloud and radiative properties between western and eastern cyclone sectors at McMurdo compared
to Macquarie Island. The E3SMv1 consistently underestimated the ice water path (IWP) at both
locations, across all sectors and seasons. Biases in cloud fraction, LWP, and IWP were negatively
correlated with shortwave (SW) radiation biases and positively correlated with longwave (LW)
radiation biases. Notably, the model's biases in radiative and cloud properties relative to cyclone
evaluation of CMIP3 models.
