Impact of precision oncology research in pediatric poor prognosis cancer: patient, parent and healthcare provider perspectives
Authors: N. McCloskey, P. Mahon, S. R. Rassekh, K. Hetherington, D. A. Regier, C. E. Wakefield, S. Pollard, R. J. Deyell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComprehensive genomic analyses are increasingly accessible to children, adolescents and young adults (AYAs) with poor prognosis cancers. Challenges and successes of pediatric precision oncology studies from the perspectives of AYA patients, parents and healthcare providers (HCPs) are poorly described.MethodsBetween March 2021 and May 2023, we interviewed AYA patients (12–21 years), parents and HCPs who participated in pediatric precision oncology studies for poor prognosis cancers in British Columbia. Interviews followed an investigator-developed semi-structured topic guide. Data were coded inductively and deductively by one qualitative researcher and one trainee, supported by two additional team members. Analytic themes were established using qualitative thematic analysis.ResultsWe interviewed 9 AYAs, 10 parents, and 17 HCPs. We identified five analytic themes: importance of clear communication of study information between patients, families and multidisciplinary HCPs; a need to support disclosure, understanding and clinical integration of research results; barriers to accessing innovative therapy and mitigation strategies; approaches to managing parent, patient and HCP hopes and expectations; personal challenges and stressors related to participation.ConclusionsWe highlight unmet needs and offer practical considerations for integrating precision oncology into clinical practice. Considerations include educating and supporting oncologists through genomics results disclosure, increasing engagement with multidisciplinary HCPs, streamlining access to study information and results, coordinating efforts to clinically validate results and access therapies, and establishing real-world outcome data to inform clinical decision-making. Implementation of these strategies will optimize care for patients and families who are navigating poor prognosis cancers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16299-2.

Introduction

Despite often intensive, multi-modal approaches to therapy, approximately 20% of children and adolescents diagnosed with cancer in Canada will have refractory or relapsed disease [1] for which survival outcomes remain dismal [2]. Whole genome and transcriptome analysis (WGTA) provides an opportunity to provide diagnostic and prognostic clarification, identify therapeutically targetable variants and discover underlying cancer predisposition [3–7]. As the accessibility of clinically validated WGTA improves, jurisdictions are increasingly adopting this testing as standard of care [8–12]. Precision oncology refers to the incorporation of the genomic characterization of an individual patient’s tumour and germline to inform clinical management broadly, ranging from diagnosis, prognosis and treatment to cancer predisposition. Shifting precision oncology from research to clinical practice requires a clear understanding of benefits and risks, alongside barriers and facilitators to implementation from the perspectives of those who have participated in such initiatives.

In British Columbia (BC), children, adolescents and young adults (AYAs) with relapsed, refractory, or poor prognosis cancers (predicted overall survival < 30%) have access to precision oncology studies offering WGTA of tumor and matched germline [7, 13]. Between 2013–2019, 79 participants in BC completed WGTA of whom 76 (96%) had a potentially actionable variant identified when transcriptome data was included. Similar to other international pediatric precision oncology trials, [4–6] 35% (n = 26) received targeted therapy, of whom 15 (54%) demonstrated radiographic response or stable disease for ≥ 6 months [7].

Pediatric precision oncology programs impact a diverse group of patients, parents and healthcare providers (HCPs) who collectively face multiple challenges which include setting expectations of the possibility of actionable results, both from tumour and germline sequencing, and the subsequent navigation of clinical integration of results. From a parent perspective, hope is a key driver for participation in precision oncology trials, [14, 15] and parents have often exhausted all treatment options for their child, leading to a strong willingness to participate in research [16, 17]. However, HCPs are faced with managing expectations of parents [14, 18, 19], who may have poor understanding of precision medicine trials and may overestimate the likelihood of benefit for their child [14, 20–22]. It is critical that we understand the complex considerations and stressors associated with WGTA on all stakeholders as this testing becomes more accessible and incorporated into routine clinical care. We must also endeavor to learn directly from the experiences of the young people with poor prognosis cancer whose voices have been underrepresented in this field in current literature.

Understanding results, both for patients, parents and providers, and considering treatment options for therapies with uncertain clinical benefit in a poor prognosis setting are additional recognized challenges [14, 15]. Further complicating the pediatric precision oncology programs, communicating WGTA results to families and integrating recommendations into care, without formal training, can be difficult for HCPs [19, 23, 24]. Although targeted therapies have shown potential to deliver clinical benefit [4, 6, 7], few patients ultimately receive molecularly informed therapies [25] and most do so outside the context of traditional clinical trials, limiting research evidence generation [26–28]. Additionally, patient access to targeted therapies is variable, and associated with regulatory and payor hurdles [19, 23, 29]. In the Canadian context, our universal healthcare system is publicly funded and spending is overseen by provincial governments. Innovative therapy access for children with cancer is complicated by a vast geography, a limited number of early phase clinical trials that are unequally distributed nationally, and a general delay in Health Canada approval of novel therapies that typically receive regulatory approval in other, larger jurisdictions first.

Despite a wealth of literature characterizing patient and HCP experiences and challenges with research participation, there is an unmet need to understand how best to support patients, parents and HCPs pursuing WGTA for poor prognosis pediatric cancers [15, 30, 31]. and our research aims to address this gap. Implementation of precision oncology relies in part on participant and HCP expectations of personal utility [32–36]. Alongside ongoing efforts to implement WGTA as part of standard practice across Canada, it is imperative to elicit patient, family and HCP perspectives to align expectations and address informational needs.

We explore the experiences of AYAs, parents, and HCPs who have participated in pediatric precision oncology studies to characterize challenges, successes, and recommendations for process improvement. As the application and integration of pediatric precision oncology advances, these results will improve understanding of the barriers and facilitators for effective implementation of precision oncology and address unmet needs of those involved.

Methods

Participant eligibility criteria

We sought to ascertain experiences from three sampling frames: English speaking AYA patients, parents, and HCPs previously involved in two studies [Precision Oncogenomics (POG) and its national pediatric counterpart, Precision Oncology in Young People (PROFYLE)]. POG/PROFYLE eligibility included children or AYAs (< 29 years) with poor prognosis cancer (predicted overall survival < 30%) [7, 13]. Enrolled participants in these research studies underwent whole genome sequencing of tumour and matched germline DNA, along with whole tumour transcriptome analysis. Actionable variants were categorized using a Canadian pediatric precision oncology classification framework (Supplementary Table 1) and level of evidence was assigned. A descriptive POG/PROFYLE participation workflow is provided in Fig. 1. For our interview study, eligible participants were: 1) AYAs (12–21 years) who previously particapted in POG/PROFYLE and received genomic results, 2) Parents of children who previously participated in POG/PROFYLE, including bereaved parents (≥ 6 months following their child’s death), and 3) HCPs (oncologists, surgeons, medical geneticists and genetic counselors) involved in the care of children enrolled in POG/PROFYLE.

Participant recruitment

Participants were recruited sequentially through BC Children’s Hospital, the only pediatric tertiary care hospital in BC. Eligible AYAs and parents previously provided consent to be re-approached for research. Permission to approach eligible AYAs and parents was provided by their primary oncologist. AYAs and parents were approached and consented at clinic visits by study investigators uninvolved in the patient’s clinical care (R.D., N.M.) or via mailed invitation packages for those no longer in follow-up. Applying purposive sampling, we recruited a breadth of HCP disciplines. HCPs were approached for participation by email invitation (R.D, N.M.).

One study member (N.M.) with no prior interaction with participants conducted semi-structured interviews by phone or Zoom for HealthCare [39], as per participant preference. Parents could accompany AYAs for their interview if preferred by the AYA. Interviews were audio-recorded and professionally transcribed.

Data analysis

Data collection and analysis proceeded concurrently. While we did not adhere to a single theoretical framework, we used interpretative description (ID) to inform the design and analysis of semi-structured interviews [40, 41]. Through the ID approach, our objectives were to describe participant, caregiver and healthcare provider experiences in a precision oncology research initiative, while generating practical insights and recommendations for study improvement, rather than adhering to a specific theoretical lens. ID was selected as a nimble framework for identifying thematic constructs, grounded in the context of applied research to inform process and policy.

Transcripts were analyzed inductively and deductively. Researchers (N.M., P.M., R.D., S.P.) developed initial coding trees for each sampling frame. Analysts independently identified in vivo codes and met to generate separate coding frameworks by setting definitional bounds around discussion topics and discussing underlying constructs. Similar and overlapping codes were grouped to establish major themes and sub-themes. N.M. and P.M. completed intercoder reliability assessments [42] until the analytic research team agreed both analysts were consistent in their coding and interpretation. Remaining data was coded by N.M. using NVivo 12 [43].

While three coding frameworks were developed, investigators cross-referenced frameworks to identify areas of similarity, overlap, and differences. This approach facilitated the development of the overarching thematic model, while capturing heterogeneity across sampling frames. Recruitment stopped once thematic saturation was reached, according to an inductive thematic saturation model [44].

Results

Between March 2021 and May 2023, we approached 20 AYAs, 25 parents and 22 HCPs of whom 10 (50%), 12 (48%) and 17 (77%) enrolled, and 9 (45%), 10 (40%) and 17 (77%) completed an interview. (Table 1). Two adolescents, both aged 14 years, were joined by a parent on their request for their interview. One AYA did not complete the interview due to evidence of distress on the pre-interview Emotional Thermometer Tool. Two consented parents did not schedule an interview. In two cases, a parent and adolescent from the same family participated. Among the 17 unique cancer patients represented, prior genomic study participation had revealed actionable somatic variants with level of evidence ≥ 3 in 16 (94%) patients and these somatic variants guided therapy in 10 (59%) patients. Among the 10 targeted therapies pursued, seven were obtained through compassionate access programs for off-label use, one patient was enrolled in an industry sponsored clinical trial and Health Canada approved Single Patient Studies (SPS) were written for two patients to obtain innovative therapies [27]. Two (12%) patients had germline pathogenic variants identified in cancer predisposition genes. We identified five major themes shared between the three sampling frames as outlined below. Themes with illustrative quotes

Communication and collaboration

Participants highlighted the importance of clear communication between patients, families and HCPs throughout research participation in creating an accessible, inclusive and collaborative program. HCPs identified strong communication with study lead oncologists as key to a successful program. As primary oncologists did not attend POG/PROFYLE consent meetings, they reported uncertainty regarding how study expectations were explained to their patients and families [Table 4, Q1]. Some oncologists wished to be more engaged in this process and relied on support from study leads to integrate WGTA results [Q2]. Genetic counselors and surgeons, who were involved in the study via the consent process or through tissue acquisition, respectively (Fig. 1), expressed interest in being engaged further with results follow-up for their patients and related education opportunities [Q3].

Parents and AYAs described feeling “in control” of their choice to participate in POG/PROFYLE [Q4-5]. However, AYAs and parents collectively reported difficulty recalling the consent process and details of the research studies [Q6-7]. Similarly, they reported some uncertainty regarding how study results influenced their care and would have preferred access to written research reports to improve understanding of results [Q7-9].

Navigating uncertainty and new technologies

AYAs, parents and HCPs expressed enthusiasm for integrating WGTA into care despite uncertainty and complexity of WGTA results [Q10-11]. Parents and AYAs described accessing new targeted therapies as “a shot in the dark” [Q12], and although concerned about unknown efficacy and potential side effects, remained hopeful for success [Q13]. Parents appreciated that WGTA could potentially identify new treatments, provide reassurance in exploring all options and help “[leave] a legacy” [Parent] for their child through their contribution to research. Similarly, AYAs embraced WGTA for “invoking curiosity”, providing “a sense of comfort” [Q14] and reducing uncertainty about whether their “siblings would get [cancer] too” [Q15-16].

Oncologists valued WGTA for its learning opportunities and reassurance they had not “left any stone unturned” [Q17]. Oncologists felt WGTA results were often “over their heads” [Q18] and they lacked “the expertise” and formal training to comfortably disclose and integrate results into clinical care. Many oncologists rated their knowledge of genetics as below average; most felt their knowledge improved through study participation and all HCPs identified a need for ongoing provider education. (Table 3).

While supportive of WGTA research, participants across the three sampling frames also expressed reservations. Surgeons described performing additional biopsies exclusively for research purposes as “a little bit ethically murky” [Surgeon] and “a hard balance to reach” [Q19] when evaluating surgical risks with low likelihoods of clinical benefit. Similarly, some parents were hesitant about participation in WGTA research if additional research biopsies would be required of their children.

Although AYAs and parents were comfortable sharing their genetic data for research purposes within and outside of Canada, AYAs comparatively voiced greater concern about sharing data with for-profit pharmaceutical companies. One parent noted they did “not want people making money off [their] child's illness and suffering” [Q20]. AYAs were more vocal, describing the concept as “sketchy”, not wanting to “be a guinea pig, poked and tested on” or to “make a company a bunch of money” [Q21].

Barriers to clinical utility

Parents and HCPs noted translation of WGTA research results to targeted therapies remains impeded by barriers including: 1) time delays in receiving and acting upon WGTA results [Q22] when treatment is “extremely time sensitive” [Oncologist], 2) a limited evidence base and “unchartered waters” [Oncologist] related to pediatric drug safety and efficacy [Q23-24] and 3) limited access to early phase clinical trials and therapies [Q25-26]. For oncologists, administrative and regulatory hurdles in navigating complex pathways to access novel, molecularly targeted drugs were highlighted [Q27-28]. AYAs did not associate timeliness of results or logistical barriers as having a meaningful impact on their treatment course [Q29-30].

Managing hope and expectations

The degree to which hope impacted decisions to participate in POG/PROFYLE and informed communication between HCPs and families was discussed frequently. While genetic counselors believed families were informed of potential risks and benefits of participation at the time of consent [Q31], some oncologists found navigating this challenging [Q32]. Oncologists reported their job is to “never to take away hope” [Q33], but also shared that setting realistic expectations while understanding the likelihood of potential benefit was necessary to avoid the “disappointment that inevitably comes after” [Oncologist]. Most parents described their hope as high despite feeling informed about uncertain benefit [Q34]. Some parents, including bereaved, expressed “lean[ing] onto hope like a lifeboat” [Q35]. Others described tempering their expectations to avoid disappointment [Q36] and to prepare themselves; to “almost grieve ahead of time” [Bereaved mother]. Notably, both bereaved and non-bereaved parents did not express regret or downsides in participating in the genomic studies.

AYAs expressed comparatively pragmatic expectations and hope [Q37-38]. WGTA was viewed as “another step” in their investigations and “something to occupy [their] mind”. Many AYAs expected WGTA was unlikely to benefit them but may provide answers about their cancer and help future patients [Q39-40]. AYAs frequently described experiencing hope while holding realistic expectations, hence “not the wrong type of hopeful” [Q40].

Personal challenges and stressors

Across sampling frames, participants voiced personal stressors and challenges associated with POG/PROFYLE research participation. Some oncologists experienced stress throughout the process of integrating WGTA results into their patient’s disease management [Q41]. Other HCPs felt “the burden of advocacy” navigating logistical barriers, accessing therapies, while also “racing against the clock” [Q42]. Genetic counselors describe challenges during their consent process with families including limitations of available outcome data [Q43] and lengthy consent forms which include post-mortem consent for tumor sampling. Parents and AYAs described the time surrounding the consent process as stressful [Q44] including consenting while in “survival mode” [Q45] near the time of diagnosis or relapse. Notably, awaiting results was not described as stressful and considered a low priority for both parents and AYAs [Q46-47]. Finally, AYAs worried about their families’ coping strategies, with one stating “having cancer is much easier than watching someone you love have cancer.”

Distinct from somatic findings, the possibility of discovering underlying cancer predisposition was a source of worry for both parents and AYAs. Some parents worried about potential implications of germline results for their other children and being at “fault” for carrying a heritable cancer predisposition [Q48], while AYAs expressed distress regarding cancer predisposition results for themselves or their siblings [Q49]. A parent whose child received a new pathogenic germline variant described feeling that their disclosing oncologist was unfamiliar with the variant and that, although the variant was thought to be unrelated to the current cancer, it carried increased risks for other future cancers [Q50]. Some AYAs interpreted an absence of a germline cancer predisposition to mean “I was the problem” and worried “if it was my fault?” [Q51].

Discussion

This study establishes a thematic framework for AYAs, parents and HCPs who have participated in pediatric precision oncology studies in Canada. We identify unifying themes highlighting the psychosocial impacts, challenges and benefits related to the clinical implementation of pediatric precision oncology. Our Canadian context is also an important consideration as we add to the literature surrounding the unique challenges encountered in a single payer health care system, with vast geography and limited pediatric oncology early phase clinical trials and clinical trial resources. We highlight the challenges, stressors and unsupported work that often follows the return of potentially actionable therapeutic targets for the patient’s primary oncology provider. Consistent with prior literature, oncologists may not feel fully equipped to disclose and integrate WGTA results into practice [19, 24]. Similarly, genetic counselors and surgeons express interest in more active involvement in study result follow-up for its learning opportunities [19]. Also consistent with existing evidence, most parents maintained hope despite understanding the low likelihood of benefit [14, 15, 22, 31], while parents and HCPs recognized hope as necessary in poor prognostic settings [45, 46]. Congruent with existing evidence [14, 15, 31], AYAs and parents were generally satisfied with participation even in the absence of clinical utility, citing non-clinical benefits such as altruism, exploring all options, eliminating uncertainty, and acquiring information.

Our findings highlight differences in perspectives across sampling frames and add to the existing literature, specifically pertaining to the under representation of AYA experiences and expectations. AYAs reported tempered expectations about receiving actionable targeted therapies in comparison to parents. Although parents and AYAs in our study voiced concerns over the potential implications of germline findings for family members, similar to a recent Canadian study31, they described minimal stress associated awaiting research results. Our findings contrast with a longitudinal Australian study, describing the waiting period as particularly difficult for parents [15]. Patients in our study typically pursued other therapies while awaiting results, which may have tempered anxiety around anticipated new somatic actionable findings with potential treatment implications.

There is a growing recognition that up to 10–16% of children with high-risk cancer may carry germline cancer predisposition variants and many of these are not routinely identified in current clinical practice [4, 7]. Similarly, in this interview cohort, two participants (12%) had received actionable germline cancer predisposition variants in their preceding precision oncology studies. The recognition of this possibility had a significant impact on both parents and AYAs, with some evidence of misunderstanding and feelings of guilt [Q48-51]. Although patients with pathogenic germline variants were referred for further genetic counselling and clinical validation studies, our findings suggest that participants, both parents and patients, may benefit from additional support during the “waiting period” for results and if cancer predisposition variants are returned.

Across our five proposed considerations for improved pediatric precision oncology trial and process design, we identify unmet needs related to educational support for oncologists, enhanced integration of the larger healthcare provider team to support patients and families following sequencing, greater access to study information among patients and their families, collection and use of real world data to reduce evidentiary uncertainty, and clinical validation of study findings to support innovative therapy access.

In light of HCPs desire for enhanced education, our results emphasize the need to support oncologists with results disclosure, precision medicine education, and practical recommendations from molecular tumor boards to assist clinical integration of WGTA results. Parents and AYAs indicated a desire for access to written study reports, highlighting the need for simplified consent forms and lay summaries of study processes and results which may improve knowledge translation and further mitigate family and patient concerns [14, 21, 31]. As the likelihood of clinical benefit for current participants remains low [7], ensuring access to study updates and information may strengthen non-clinical benefit, satisfaction and acceptance of WGTA in pediatric oncology.

Finally, oncologists value guidance in interpretation of molecular tumour board reports and their integration into clinical decision making. They reported feeling unsupported in their efforts to prioritize options and access targeted therapies in our single payer healthcare system with limited clinical trial availability. Seven of ten patients who accessed molecularly informed therapies in this cohort did so via compassionate access requests for off-label medications with established pediatric dosing information. In the current Canadian pediatric oncology landscape, limited sites have a Drug Access Navigator whose role often includes assistance with obtaining off-label medications via compassionate access. In some cases, compassionate access requests are needed for drugs that have already received approval in other jurisdictions, such as the Federal Drug Administration in the United States, but are not approved by Health Canada. Only one of ten patients in the current cohort who accessed targeted therapies following their precision oncology results did so in the context of a clinical trial, and another two only accessed therapy after their primary oncologists and treatment site undertook the extensive work of implementing a Health Canada approved SPS for them [27]. Early phase clinical trial options are limited and when available, typically only open at a very small number of trial sites nationally. In order to overcome these barriers, there is a growing recognition for the need to implement decentralized clinical trial models [47–49]. Finally, the extensive work involved in undertaking a SPS for one patient is also constrained by site and oncologist specific resources resulting in an impact on local clinical trial units and suspected geographical inequity. We suggest that Canadian pediatric oncologists may benefit from increased drug access navigation support, access to centralized clinical trial resources (https://u-link.care/) and decentralized models, and support to undertake SPS, or advocacy to streamline the requirements for SPS in certain cases (Table 5).

Results of this study should be interpreted in light of its limitations. Firstly, all parent participants were mothers, which is consistent with the general underrepresentation of fathers in clinical pediatric research [50, 51]. Interviews were conducted in English, limiting our ability to characterize how cultural differences, language barriers and equitable practices influence understanding, satisfaction and informed consent. This study focused on a single institution; thus study design, interdisciplinary approach and policy barriers may not reflect those faced in other jurisdictions. Finally, the median time between precision oncology study consent and study interview was approximately 1.5 years for both AYAs and parents, with the potential for recall bias and also potentially for illness severity as those with more aggressive disease may be underrepresented [52]. Also, only 40% of approached parents and 45% of approached adolescents completed an interview, which could be a reflection of their illness severity, and again may introduce selection bias.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides a comparative thematic framework for the experiences of Canadian AYA, parent and HCP participants in pediatric oncology programs and highlights the success, challenges and impacts of its implementation. We propose five practical considerations to inform the future integration of comprehensive genomic testing in pediatric cancer as it shifts from research into routine clinical practice.
