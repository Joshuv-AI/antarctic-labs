Avian paramyxovirus type 1-associated severe pneumonia in humans: Molecular characterization and zoonotic transmission risk
Authors: Bin Liu, Qingwen Ding, Shunjun Tang, Huajiang Dong, Rong-jiao Li, Min Gan, Jinbo Wei, Nailou Zhang, Chen Wu, Tie-han Zhang, Hong-zhi Yu, Zhenhua Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAvian paramyxovirus serotype 1 (APMV-1, Newcastle disease virus) is a major poultry pathogen. Introduction

The rising burden of emerging zoonotic diseases threatens global public health, driven by interconnected socioeconomic, environmental, and ecological changes [[1], [2]]. Respiratory viruses—exemplified by coronaviruses (e.g., SARS-CoV, MERS-CoV) [3], [4] and influenza viruses [5], [6]—demonstrate profound pandemic potential. Members of the Paramyxoviridae family also pose persistent cross-species risks [7], [8]. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has revolutionized pathogen detection, enabling timely assessment of the clinical and public health significance of novel or underreported pathogens [9].

APMV-1 (Newcastle disease virus) causes Newcastle disease, a highly contagious and economically devastating infection of wild and domestic birds worldwide [10]. Classified in the genus Orthoavulavirus, virulent APMV-1 strains induce severe systemic disease affecting the respiratory, digestive, and central nervous systems, leading to high avian mortality [[11], [12], [13]]. The virus circulates endemically in Africa, Asia, and Central/South America [14], with frequent outbreaks in Chinese poultry populations [15].

Notably, while primarily an avian pathogen, APMV-1 can infect humans, typically causing self-limiting conjunctivitis or mild influenza-like illness in individuals with occupational avian exposure [16]. However, a growing number of case reports document severe outcomes, including fatal pneumonia in immunocompromised patients (caused by classical APMV-1 [17] and pigeon paramyxovirus [[18], [19]]) and encephalitis [20]. Confirmed cases of APMV-1-associated severe pneumonia in immunocompetent individuals remain exceptionally rare [21], and comprehensive analyses linking robust etiological identification to definitive zoonotic transmission chains are lacking.

Here, we report a case of severe pneumonia in a 65-year-old immunocompetent male with close contact with feeder chickens, caused by APMV-1. We present a systematic investigation combining clinical characterization, mNGS diagnostics, and environmental epidemiology to confirm the etiology and establish a direct avian-to-human transmission link. This work emphasizes APMV-1's zoonotic potential and highlights the critical need for integrated surveillance and research into determinants of cross-species pathogenicity.

Methods

Study design and case identification

This study integrated human clinical case investigation with environmental outbreak tracing to establish zoonotic transmission. The case was identified at the Infectious Disease Department of the PAP Medical Center on April 25, 2023—30 days after symptom onset. The patient was diagnosed with respiratory failure, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and severe pneumonia. Clinical information, including symptoms, blood tests, gas analysis, and treatment, was extracted from his electronic medical record.

On April 26, a BALF sample was collected before moxifloxacin administration for metagenomic sequencing, nucleic acid detection, and serological testing. Leftover serum from a procalcitonin assay collected between April 24 and 27 was obtained for further serological analysis. A throat swab sample was collected on April 27 for RT-PCR and ELISA testing. On April 28, the patient received intravenous methylprednisolone (40 mg, twice daily) and gamma globulin (10 g, once daily), and was subsequently transferred to Haihe Hospital's emergency department for continued treatment. The patient was discharged on May 4. (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Epidemiological and environmental investigation

Field investigators and clinicians used a standardized form to gather epidemiological data—including demographic characteristics, recent poultry exposure, medical conditions, and work environment—on day 2 following the patient's hospital admission. The patient resided in Dongli District, Tianjin, China, and had purchased 12 chickens one month prior; one chicken had fallen ill and died. The chickens were sourced from a live poultry market and kept in a backyard enclosure with poor sanitation, and the patient also had three free-roaming dogs. During hospitalization, the remaining chickens died and were buried by the family (including the one that died on April 26). The patient's son and a doorkeeper had close contact with him but remained asymptomatic.

Environmental and animal samples were collected the same evening. Environmental samples from the chicken rearing area (cage swabs, pond wastewater, feed, and chicken droppings) were collected and immediately stored in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) on dry ice. Tissue samples (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, large and small intestines, brain, and trachea) were obtained from deceased chickens and stored identically. Samples were mechanically sheared and homogenized using a DS1000 tissue grinder (NovaStar, China). Following centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, supernatants were collected for RNA extraction.

Laboratory confirmation and pathogen characterization

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS)

A total of 8 mL of BALF was sent to Willingmed Medical Technology Beijing Co., Ltd. for analysis. The PIseq™ DNA + RNA pathogen co-detection kit was used for metagenomic analysis of suspected pathogens. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NextSeq 550 DX platform, and data were filtered using FastQC software to remove low-quality reads. Filtered reads were aligned to the human reference genome using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) algorithm to exclude human-related sequences. Remaining reads were mapped to a pathogenic microbial database optimized by Willingmed Medical Technology, revealing the presence of APMV-1.

Molecular detection and quantification

Viral RNA was extracted from patient (BALF, throat swab) and environmental samples using a QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit. cDNA was synthesized using a Vazyme Reverse Transcription Kit and amplified via PCR. Three primer sets were designed for: RT-PCR targeting APMV-1 HN and L genes, qRT-PCR targeting the APMV-1 F gene, and full-length amplification of the APMV-1 F gene. RT-PCR for APMV-1 detection included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles, and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Positive bands were purified and ligated into the pGEM-T vector for Sanger sequencing. Viral load was quantified via qRT-PCR targeting the F gene using SYBR® Green Master Mix (Bio-Rad, USA). A 10-fold serial dilution of the pGEM-T-NDV-108 plasmid—constructed by cloning a fragment of the viral F gene into the pGEM-T vector—was used as the viral genome copy number standard.

Antigen and antibody detection

APMV-1 antigen levels in patient, chicken tissue, and environmental swab samples were determined using an APMV-1 antigen sandwich ELISA kit (Jiangsu Jingmei Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., China). APMV-1 IgG antibody levels in oropharyngeal swabs, BALF, serum, and chicken tissue samples were measured using an APMV-1 IgG ELISA kit (Jiangsu Jingmei Bio-Technology). APMV-1 IgM antibody levels in deceased chicken samples were assessed using an APMV-1 IgM ELISA kit (Jiangsu Boshen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). OD450 values were obtained with an HBS-1096C enzyme-linked analyzer (Nanjing Dete Experimental Equipment Co., Ltd., China). Data processing and visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism V8.0.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). These assays represent investigative research-use measurements rather than standard clinical diagnostics.

Phylogenetic and evolutionary analysis

To assess phylogenetic relationships, 617 Class I APMV-1 F gene sequences from the updated NDV classification system (https://github.com/NDVconsortium/NDV_Sequence_Datasets) were analyzed. All sequences were aligned using the MAFFT V7.49 multiple alignment algorithm [23], and the resulting alignment was trimmed using TRIMAL v1.4 [24]. A model substitution test was performed using ModelTest-NG V0.1.7. The best-fit model (TPM1uf + I + G4) was selected, and an ultrafast bootstrap analysis with 1000 replicates was conducted using IQ-TREE v2.2 to construct a Maximum Likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree. Results were visualized using FigTree v1.4.4 (tree.bio.ed.ac.uk). A separate Maximum Likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed based on alignment of the partial APMV-1 L gene with the reference APMV-1 genome.

Results

Clinical presentation and diagnostic work-up

The 65-year-old male patient had no underlying conditions and developed dyspnea and chest tightness on April 2, 2023. Initial CT scan showed minor calcifications (Fig. 1 A1–A4). After 25 days of worsening symptoms, follow-up CT revealed bilateral ground-glass opacities and consolidations (predominantly in the lower lung zones) consistent with severe pneumonia and ARDS (Table 1, Fig. 1 B1–B4). Upon admission, the patient received high-flow oxygen therapy (HFOT) up to 50 L/min with FiO2 of 0.50, bronchoscopy, and antibiotic treatment with moxifloxacin (400 mg, once daily). A 10-mL BALF sample was collected for routine bacterial/fungal culture and clinical mNGS. Treatment was adjusted to intravenous methylprednisolone (40 mg, twice daily) and gamma globulin (10 g, once daily). On April 28, the patient was transferred to Haihe Hospital for further clinical management and was subsequently discharged on May 4. At discharge, his routine blood biochemistry and blood gas analysis parameters were all within normal ranges (Table 1), and repeat chest CT scans demonstrated marked improvement in pulmonary lesions (Fig. 1 C1–C4). Peripheral blood immune cell subset profiling via flow cytometry (Supplementary Table 2) and serum cytokine quantification by ELISA (Supplementary Table 3) were completed on April 25, the day of hospital admission. These immunological assays were conducted solely to verify that the patient was immunocompetent and free of underlying primary immune deficiency. While the resulting profiles confirmed intact host immune function, these immune readouts provide no supportive evidence for a causal relationship between APMV-1 and the patient's acute respiratory illness.

Metagenomic sequencing identifies APMV-1 as the causative pathogen

Sputum culture (collected the morning after admission) revealed no pathogenic bacteria or fungi. Serology tests detected no antibodies against hepatitis B/C, Epstein-Barr virus, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae, respiratory syncytial virus, influenza A/B virus, Legionella pneumophila, or rubella virus. Nasopharyngeal swab RT-PCR was negative for COVID-19, reducing the likelihood of environmental contamination with APMV-1 sequences.

mNGS of BALF identified 40 APMV-1-specific reads (50.6% relative microbial abundance) covering 10.78% of the APMV-1 genome (Supplementary Table 1). The closest match was strain NDV/Chicken/CN/JX/30F2/2017 (GenBank: MH289908) (Fig. 2A). High viral load and absence of other significant pathogens strongly implicated APMV-1 as the causative agent.

Confirmation of zoonotic transmission through multi-sample analysis

Molecular detection and viral load

PCR detected NDV HN and L gene fragments in chicken lung, No. 2 cage swab, patient BALF, and throat swab samples, with positive signals in the cage swab (Fig. 2C). Full-length amplification of NDV F gene segments (NDV-999-1, NDV-454-2, NDV-864-3) yielded products in the cage swab and BALF, but not in chicken lung (Fig. 2D, E). Sanger sequencing confirmed APMV-1 sequences in the cage swab matching those in patient BALF.

qRT-PCR detected APMV-1 nucleic acids in patient BALF and high viral loads in environmental samples (Fig. 3A, B). Chicken tissues showed significant APMV-1 RNA levels, particularly in lungs and intestines (Fig. 3C).

Antigen detection

APMV-1 antigen was detected by ELISA in BALF and serial serum samples. BALF antigen levels reached 2400 ng/L—approximately twice the serum antigen level—indicating active viral replication in lung tissue. High antigen levels were also detected in deceased chicken tissues (brain, heart, liver, spleen, lungs) and environmental samples (Fig. 3D–F).

Antibody detection

APMV-1 IgG was markedly higher in the patient's BALF (1800 ng/L) and initial serum sample (500 ng/L) compared to baseline clinical thresholds, confirming active infection and immune response (Fig. 4G). No IgG was detected in subsequent serum samples (No. 2 and No. 3). High IgG and IgM titers were detected in deceased chicken tissues (Fig. 3H–I).

Phylogenetic analysis reveals a common poultry origin

PCR and NGS determined the F gene sequence of the isolated APMV-1 strain (GenBank: OR136507.1), which shared 98.13% nucleotide identity and 98.92% amino acid identity with Chicken/China/LGD525/2019 (GenBank: MZ736949.1)—a strain isolated from chickens in Guangdong, China. Maximum Likelihood phylogenetic analysis based on the NDV F gene showed the patient's strain clustered with LGD525, belonging to Class I genotype 1.1.2 1b (predominantly present in Chinese poultry populations) (Fig. 4A).

A 585 bp fragment of the APMV-1 L gene was cloned from BALF (GenBank: PX664121.1). Phylogenetic analysis of this fragment revealed the patient's strain clustered with NDV/Environment/CN/JX/47×/2016 (isolated in Jiangxi Province in 2016), also belonging to genotype 1.1.2 1b (Fig. 4C).

The APMV-1 F protein requires cleavage by host cell proteases into F1 and F2 (linked by disulfide bonds) to become active. Previous reports indicate highly virulent NDV strains have the cleavage site consensus sequence 112R/K-R-Q-R/K-R-F117, while weakly virulent strains have 112G/E-K/R-Q-G/E-R-L117. The NDV/Chicken/CHN/TJ/environment/2023 strain in this study contains the motif 112E-R-Q-E-R-L117 (Fig. 4B)—characteristic of weakly virulent strains [22]—confirming that avian low-pathogenicity APMV-1 can cause severe human disease.

Discussion

This study conclusively links a severe pneumonia case in an immunocompetent individual to zoonotic transmission of APMV-1 Class I genotype 1.1.2 1b from domestic poultry. Integrating clinical data, mNGS diagnostics, and phylogenetic analysis, we established a clear transmission chain: “sick chicken → contaminated environment → human infection.” This challenges conventional risk assessments, as even low-virulence avian APMV-1 genotypes can pose significant threats to human health.

Historically, human APMV-1 infections are rare and self-limiting, primarily affecting individuals with occupational avian exposure [16]. Severe outcomes were previously confined to immunocompromised hosts [17], [19], [20], but our case and a prior report [18] demonstrate disease severity in immunocompetent individuals. Underdiagnosis likely stems from APMV-1's exclusion from routine differential diagnoses—we advocate for its inclusion in evaluations of severe respiratory infections of unknown origin with poultry contact, confirmable via RT-PCR or ELISA.

Epidemiological and laboratory evidence supports direct avian-to-human transmission: the patient's exposure to sick chickens, high viral loads in environmental and chicken samples, and sequence identity between human and poultry-derived strains. APMV-1 spreads among birds via respiratory droplets and fecal-oral routes [[25], [26]], with human infection occurring through contact with infected birds or contaminated fomites [27]—consistent with our findings.

A critical paradox emerges: the lentogenic avian virulence marker (112E-R-Q-E-R-L117 cleavage site) [[28], [29]] contrasts with severe human ARDS. This suggests potential dissociation between avian virulence (classically governed by the F protein cleavage site) and cross-species transmission capacity. Host range determinants are multifactorial, involving receptor-binding and neuraminidase activities of the HN protein, as well as potential adaptations in other viral proteins (e.g., polymerase). Thus, while the cleavage site motif indicates low virulence in poultry, its presence in a strain with zoonotic potential underscores that this canonical molecular marker does not reliably predict host species tropism [30]. This finding necessitates comprehensive genomic and phenotypic evaluation of such strains to assess their true pandemic risk. Further research is needed to elucidate these mechanisms.

From a One Health perspective, APMV-1 persistence in poultry—combined with backyard rearing and live poultry market practices—creates ongoing human exposure risks [[31], [32]]. We recommend enhancing APMV-1 surveillance by including it in avian-derived respiratory pathogen diagnostic panels (particularly in live poultry market regions) and implementing targeted biosecurity measures for backyard farming (e.g., routine virus detection, immediate sick bird isolation). Integrating animal, human, and environmental surveillance via One Health frameworks is essential for early detection of zoonotic threats [33].

This study has several inherent limitations that should be explicitly acknowledged. First, viable viral isolation was not achieved from either the patient's clinical specimens or collected avian samples, precluding direct experimental proof of APMV-1 infectivity and in vivo active replication. Accordingly, we have moderated all definitive causal and zoonotic transmission conclusions throughout the manuscript to reflect this constraint. Second, while mNGS profiling confirmed APMV-1 as the dominant microbial constituent with high relative abundance, the complex pulmonary microenvironment harbors multiple low-abundance co-detected microbes, whose potential synergistic, exacerbating or opportunistic effects on the patient's severe pneumonia cannot be fully excluded. Third, the commercial ELISA kits deployed for viral antigen and specific antibody measurement are research-use-only exploratory reagents without comprehensive diagnostic validation for human serum and BALF matrices. Uncharacterized sample matrix interference may bias quantitative readouts, so all immunoassay-derived data must be interpreted with appropriate caution. Fourth, our environmental sampling was cross-sectional rather than sustained long-term surveillance, which limits our capacity to systematically evaluate the regional prevalence and circulation dynamics of this APMV-1 genotype.

Looking ahead, follow-up work will prioritize constructing standardized infection models to dissect viral protein structure and functional mechanisms. Large-scale longitudinal epidemiological investigations will also be necessary to accurately quantify genotype-specific spillover and zoonotic transmission risks at the human-animal interface.

In conclusion, APMV-1 genotype 1.1.2 1b is a potential human respiratory pathogen capable of causing severe disease in immunocompetent adults. Our study provides a model for zoonotic transmission investigation, emphasizing the need to elucidate determinants of cross-species pathogenicity to mitigate spillover risk.

Conclusions

This study provides strong associative evidence that APMV-1 Class I genotype 1.1.2 1b can cross the species barrier and cause life-threatening pneumonia in immunocompetent humans. The integrated surveillance and research approach highlights the underrecognized zoonotic potential of this endemic poultry virus. Enhanced surveillance across avian and human populations and further research into viral determinants of cross-species pathogenicity are urgently needed to mitigate future spillover risks.
