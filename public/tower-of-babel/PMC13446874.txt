Insights in Fetal Growth Trajectories of Spontaneous Twin Anemia Polycythemia Sequence Twins
Authors: Margot J.A. van de Sande, Lisanne S.A. Tollenaar, Monique C. Haak, Joanne E.J.T. Verweij, Claar C.M.M. Lap, Floor M.M. Prein, Enrico Lopriore, Femke Slaghekke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTwin anemia polycythemia sequence (TAPS) is a monochorionic twin condition, where imbalanced blood flow causes anemia in the donor twin and polycythemia in the recipient twin. While TAPS donors are frequently small for gestational age (SGA, birth weight <10th centile), fetal growth patterns are yet poorly understood.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study of all spontaneous TAPS pregnancies diagnosed at Leiden University Medical Center between 2002 and 2024. Fetal growth was assessed by estimated fetal weight (EFW), with fetal growth restriction (FGR) defined as an EFW <10th centile. Secondary outcomes included SGA at birth and placental share distribution.ResultsA total of 45 consecutive spontaneous TAPS pregnancies were included. Donors consistently had lower EFW than recipients. At 20 weeks, FGR was present in 42% (5/12) of donors versus 0% (0/11) of recipients (p < 0.001). No recipients developed FGR before 24 weeks. By 30 weeks, FGR affected 55% (6/11) of donors and 9% (1/11) of recipients (p = 0.028). At birth, 58% (22/38) of donors had a larger placental share than their cotwin.ConclusionDespite a slightly larger placental share, TAPS donor twins are at higher risk of FGR. At birth, three out four TAPS donors are SGA compared to one out of five recipients.

Introduction

Twin anemia polycythemia sequence (TAPS) occurs in monochorionic twin pregnancies and is characterized by net inter-twin blood transfusion through miniscule placental anastomoses, resulting in anemia in the donor and polycythemia in the recipient twin [1]. In contrast to twin-twin transfusion syndrome (TTTS), TAPS develops without large intertwin discordance in amniotic fluid. TAPS occurs spontaneously in 3–5% of monochorionic twin pregnancies, and in 2–16% following laser surgery for TTTS, due to the presence of residual miniscule placental anastomoses [2, 3]. Previous studies have shown that TAPS donors often face severe neonatal complications, including hematological problems (such as anemia and lymphocytopenia), renal insufficiency, metabolic disturbances (like hypoglycemia and lactate acidosis), persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn and significant long-term neurodevelopmental impairment [4–8]. While the exact cause of these adverse outcomes is likely to be multifactorial, fetal growth restriction (FGR) is known to be an important predictor of neonatal outcome. Previous studies showed that approximately 60% of TAPS donors are small for gestational age (SGA) at birth [5, 9]. After birth, gross examination of the placenta often shows that the donor twin has a larger placental share compared to the recipient twin, as illustrated in Figure 1 [10]. These placental characteristics prompt the question whether TAPS donors are already growth-restricted during pregnancy due to chronic nutrient loss through placental anastomoses, or if they initially have greater growth potential due to a larger placental share, but experience reduced growth velocity later in pregnancy [11]. However, these fetal growth patterns have not been studied before. To address this, the aim of our study was to assess the fetal growth trajectory and the occurrence of FGR in spontaneous TAPS donors and recipients that were treated expectantly or with intrauterine transfusion (IUT) (with or without partial exchange transfusion [PET]). Additionally, we examined the birth weight, calculated the presence of growth restriction at birth (birth weight <10th centile) and the placental share of both twins.

Methods

For this study, all monochorionic twins diagnosed antenatally with spontaneous TAPS at the Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC) between 2002 and 2024, were considered eligible. Cases were included when they met the antenatal criteria for TAPS, defined as a middle cerebral artery peak systolic velocity (MCA-PSV) discordance of more than 0.5 multiples of the median (MoM) [12]. Only cases treated with IUT (±PET) or those treated expectantly with confirmed TAPS after birth based on the postnatal criteria of Slaghekke et al. [13], were included. These cases were selected to provide a representative fetal growth trajectory. TAPS cases without fetal growth records, as well as those with intrauterine fetal demise, termination of pregnancy, or laser surgery treatment were excluded from this study. Laser-treated cases were excluded because fetoscopic laser surgery dichorionizes the placenta, thereby disrupting the native placental angioarchitecture. This dichorionization fundamentally alters the placental sharing and intertwin vascular connections, resulting in a different impact on fetal growth compared with the natural course of TAPS. In contrast, intrauterine transfusion (IUT) and expectant management preserve the original placental architecture. Additionally, post-laser TAPS cases (TAPS occurring after laser treatment for TTTS) were also excluded, as these placentas were originally TTTS placentas and were not expected to reflect the typical fetal growth pattern of TAPS cases.

The following obstetric and fetal data were retrospectively extracted from our database: gravidity, parity, gestational age (GA) at diagnosis of TAPS, antenatal TAPS stage at diagnosis, GA at birth, birth weight and the presence of postnatal TAPS. The severity of antenatal TAPS was assessed using the staging system, previously published by Tollenaar et al. [12] (online suppl. Table 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553073) Presence of postnatal TAPS was based on an inter-twin hemoglobin difference greater than 8 g/dL, along with at least one of the following criteria: reticulocyte count ratio >1.7 or the presence of miniscule placental anastomoses (diameter <1.0 mm), identified through color-dyed injection of the placenta [13–15]. The different postnatal TAPS stages are displayed in online supplementary Table 2. SGA was defined as a birth weight below the 10th centile, according to Hoftiezer et al. [9].

Ultrasound data were retrospectively retrieved from clinical records, including fetal head circumference (HC), biparietal diameter, abdominal circumference (AC), and femur length (FL). The estimated fetal weight (EFW) was calculated using the Hadlock formula, based on biometric measurements of HC, AC, and FL [16]. The intertwin EFW discordance was calculated by (EFW of the larger fetus – EFW of the smaller fetus)/EFW of the larger fetus. Only the ultrasound measurements taken at the LUMC were included. The EFW was measured every 2 weeks from 16 weeks onward for the remaining pregnancy. FGR was defined as an EFW below the 10th centile following this Hadlock formula.

Data on placental characteristics were prospectively collected in a database. All MC twin placentas evaluated at the LUMC are routinely injected using specific dye colors correlated to specific vessels, allowing detailed observation of types of placental anastomoses [14, 17]. After dye injection, placentas were photographed with scale reference to be digitally saved for computer analysis using ImageJ (version 1.57; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MA, USA). The fetal placental territories were determined by the margins of the twin-specific colored dyes and were expressed by a percentage of the total placental surface. The placental surface discordance was calculated as (larger placental share-smaller placental share)/larger placental share × 100.

The primary outcomes were the fetal growth patterns with median EFW per GA and the proportion of FGR in TAPS donors and recipients throughout the pregnancy. Secondary outcomes were the presence of SGA and placental share of both twins.

SPSS version 25 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for performing statistical analysis. Data are reported as n (%) or median (interquartile ranges [IQRs]). Differences between donors and recipients were calculate using the paired t test for continuous normally distributed outcomes and the generalized estimated equation module for categorical outcomes. A p value <0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance. The Medical Ethics Committee of the LUMC approved this study.

Results

In total, 54 TAPS pregnancies, treated expectantly or with IUT (±PET) between 2002 and 2024 in our center, were found eligible for this study. Nine pregnancies were excluded from further analysis due to antenatal spontaneous recovery of TAPS. Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of the 45 remaining, included pregnancies. In 15 of the 45 pregnancies, IUT was performed, while the remaining 30 pregnancies were managed expectantly. The median GA at diagnosis of TAPS was 27.4 weeks (IQR 22.1–30.9 weeks). TAPS twins from our cohort were born at a median GA of 33.6 weeks (IQR 30.4–35.1 weeks). A total of two neonates died due to severe complications: one due to spontaneous intestinal perforation followed by peritonitis and pneumothorax without ability for surgery due to very low birth weight (450 g) combined with extreme prematurity (26 weeks), the other one died following a decision to withhold active postnatal support, given a birth weight of 430 g at 25 weeks’ gestation. Both neonates were TAPS donor twins.

The median EFW of the TAPS donor and recipient twins throughout gestation is shown in Figure 2 and Table 2. Throughout the entire pregnancy, the median EFW of the TAPS donor twin remained lower compared to the recipient twin. As early as 20 weeks of pregnancy, the EFW of the TAPS donor twin is already significantly lower compared to that of the recipient twin (298 [IQR 218–322] and 335 [IQR 324–354]), respectively (p = 0.01).

The EFW discordance was 8.5% (IQR 5.2–29.0) at 20 weeks of GA and 11.8% (IQR 5.0–32.8) at 30 weeks of GA (p = 0.063) (online suppl. Fig. 1). At 20 weeks of GA, 42% (5/12) of donors showed growth restriction compared to 0% (0/11) of recipients (p < 0.001). Postnatal Characteristics

Postnatal characteristics are shown in Table 2. The median birth weight in TAPS donors was 1,700 g (IQR 1,105–1,988 g), while in recipients, it was 1,860 g (IQR 1,543–2,300 g) (p < 0.001). SGA was detected in 73% (33/45) of TAPS donors and 24% (11/45) of recipients (p < 0.001). The birth weight discordance was 17.7% (IQR 9.9–26.3) (online suppl. Fig. 1).

A total of 38 out of 45 placentas were injected with color dye to determine placental share. The donor and recipient had placental shares of 52.0% (41.9–60.7) and 48.0% (39.3–58.1), respectively (p = 0.134). In 58% (22/38) of twin pairs, TAPS donors had the largest placental share. Of these twin pairs, 73% (16/22) were growth restricted at birth.

Discussion

This is the first study to investigate fetal growth patterns in spontaneous TAPS twins. Our data indicate that at 20 weeks of gestation, 42% of TAPS donor twins are growth restricted. This trend persists as the pregnancy progresses, with 55% of donor twins having FGR by 30 weeks. Furthermore, we showed that the median EFW of donor twins remains consistently lower than that of recipient twins throughout the entire pregnancy, despite donors having a larger placental share in approximately 60% of cases. This observation supports our hypothesis that, although donor twins often have a larger placental share, the placental function seems to be compromised. Possibly, edema and impaired vascular structures within the hydropic placenta may hinder nutrient and oxygen exchange, thus restricting fetal growth in the donor twin. However, further histological analysis of untreated spontaneous TAPS placentas is needed to explore the mechanisms underlying this placental dysfunction more thoroughly.

Furthermore, anemia in donor twins, as a result of feto-fetal transfusion from donor to recipient twin, likely plays a significant role in the development of FGR in donor twins as well. A reduced red blood cell count impairs the blood’s oxygen-carrying capacity, leading to systemic hypoxia. As a result, fetal tissues receive insufficient oxygen, which can hinder normal growth and increase the risk of growth restriction [18].

In contrary to donors, none of the recipients were growth restricted at the beginning of pregnancy. From 30 weeks of gestation onward, a subset of recipient twins also developed FGR. Previous studies have shown that late-onset FGR is most often linked to uteroplacental insufficiency [19]. In recipients, this insufficiency may arise later in pregnancy due to their relatively smaller placental territory, which can restrict nutrient availability. As a result, around 20% of TAPS recipients were found to be growth restricted at birth, which aligns with findings in previous studies [5].

In our cohort, 73% of TAPS donors showed growth restriction at birth, which is higher than the 60% reported in previous studies [5]. This discrepancy could be attributed to differences in treatment approach, as our cohort consisted exclusively of cases managed expectantly or with IUT (PET), and excluded cases treated by laser surgery.

In the group treated with IUT (±PET), there may be a temporary improvement in growth potential due to the provision of blood transfusions. However, this effect is likely short-lived, as the placental anastomoses persist, continuing to allow blood transfusion from the donor twin to the recipient twin. We deemed it important to include this group in our study as well, mainly because the potential for long-term improvement in fetal growth is expected to be limited. Furthermore, due to the small sample size, a subgroup analysis comparing the expectant management and IUT groups could not be performed.

It is important to interpret our results with caution due to the small sample size and retrospective nature of this study. The rarity of TAPS, along with the exclusion of post-laser TAPS cases and TAPS cases treated by laser surgery, limits the number of cases included in our analysis. Nevertheless, our cohort represents the first group of spontaneous TAPS cases examined on their fetal growth pattern. Another factor to consider when interpreting our results is the limited number of fetal growth measurements per gestational age. This limitation arises because only ultrasound measurements taken at the LUMC were included. This decision was made to ensure consistency and reliability in the ultrasound assessments, as all scans were performed by highly experienced ultrasonographers at LUMC, the national referral center for complicated monochorionic twin pregnancies, with specialized expertise in recognizing and evaluating TAPS. However, it is important to acknowledge that this approach may have inadvertently led to the exclusion of some earlier fetal growth measurements taken at other hospitals prior to the referral. Theoretically, this could have led to an overrepresentation of fetal growth measurements from more severe TAPS cases at an earlier stage.

Future research should focus on examining the placental histology of spontaneous TAPS cases to gain a deeper understanding of the pathophysiology of FGR in TAPS donors, despite their larger placental share. It would also be interesting to explore why the recipient twin is growth restricted at birth in about 1 out 5 cases, despite receiving extra nutrients through placental anastomoses from the donor twin. These studies could provide important insights into the long-term impact of different management strategies on fetal and neonatal development, ultimately helping caregivers in optimizing care for TAPS pregnancies.

In conclusion, our findings offer important insights into the fetal growth patterns of spontaneous TAPS twins. Our results indicate that both TAPS donors and recipients are at risk of experiencing FGR, with donors being significantly smaller throughout pregnancy compared to recipients. These findings highlight the importance for caregivers to carefully monitor fetal growth throughout the whole pregnancy, as TAPS donors are at risk for early growth restriction and TAPS recipients may experience growth restriction later on in pregnancy as well.
