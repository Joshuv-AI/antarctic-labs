Machine learning–based prediction of bronchiolitis in children under two years: a multicenter study within a single metropolitan area
Authors: Raoof Nopour, Saeed Barzegari, Mostafa Shanbehzadeh, Zahra Mahmoudvand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBronchiolitis is a leading cause of hospitalization in infants, yet early and accurate risk prediction remains a clinical challenge. Traditional models often lack the complexity to capture nonlinear interactions or the transparency required for clinical trust.ObjectiveThis multicenter study aimed to develop, validate, and interpret machine learning (ML) models for predicting bronchiolitis risk in children under two years of age using clinical and socioeconomic determinants.MethodsIn this retrospective study, data from 1,260 children (529 with bronchiolitis and 731 without) were collected at 5 pediatric centers within a single metropolitan area, from January 2023 through December 2024. Seventeen predictors were analyzed. After rigorous preprocessing and K-NN imputation, eight ML algorithms, including four base learners and four ensemble methods, were developed. Model performance was evaluated using AUC and metrics derived from confusion matrices. Clinical utility was assessed via Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) and calibration plots. Explainable AI (XAI) was implemented using Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to interpret model decisions.ResultsXGBoost achieved an AUC of 0.863 [0.828–0.897] in the test set, demonstrating higher predictive performance than base learners such as NB (AUC: 0.583 [0.529–0.638]). DCA and calibration plots confirmed that XGBoost provides superior net benefit and calibration performance across a range of threshold probabilities. SHAP analysis identified overcrowding, severe acute malnutrition, and maternal smoking as the most influential predictors, highlighting the critical role of socioeconomic and environmental factors in disease susceptibility.ConclusionThe integration of XGBoost with SHAP values provides a robust, transparent, and clinically actionable framework for early prediction of bronchiolitis based on variables available at initial clinical presentation. Introduction

Bronchiolitis is a clinical term describing inflammation of the small airways (bronchioles), often resulting in airway edema, mucus plugging, and varying degrees of obstruction [1, 2]. It is most commonly caused by respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and represents a leading cause of acute lower respiratory tract infection in infants and young children [3]. Bronchiolitis is a major contributor to pediatric morbidity worldwide and remains one of the most frequent causes of hospitalization in children under two years of age [4, 5], accounting for approximately 16% of all hospital admissions in this age group [6].

Although overall mortality and hospital length of stay have remained relatively stable, the clinical burden of bronchiolitis has increased substantially, with greater severity and increased healthcare resource utilization. Recent evidence suggests more than a two-fold increase in intensive care unit (ICU) admissions and mechanical ventilation use, accompanied by an approximately 18% rise in hospitalization costs [7, 8]. In parallel, RSV-associated infections, which are strongly linked to bronchiolitis, have shown an increasing trend over the past few decades [9, 10]. Consequently, bronchiolitis has become a leading cause of pediatric ICU admissions, placing a significant burden on healthcare systems due to the increasing need for advanced supportive care [7]. These trends underscore the importance of improving early identification and preventive strategies to reduce disease burden [4].

In recent years, prediction models have emerged as valuable tools in healthcare, supporting early risk stratification and improving clinical decision-making [11, 12]. In particular, machine learning (ML)-based approaches have demonstrated strong predictive performance across a wide range of clinical applications, enabling improved identification of high-risk populations and more efficient allocation of healthcare resources [13, 14].

In the context of bronchiolitis, previous ML studies have primarily focused on predicting disease severity in hospitalized infants [15], early-onset bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome [16], and treatment requirements [17]. Related ML-based approaches have been explored in conditions such as diffuse panbronchiolitis [18] and other pediatric respiratory diseases [19]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no multicenter study has developed and validated ML models for early clinical risk stratification of bronchiolitis in children under 2 years of age. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and validate multicenter ML models incorporating explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques to predict the risk of bronchiolitis in children under 2 years of age.

Methods

Study setting and data source

The research community in this retrospective study consisted of suspected children with bronchiolitis infection who were admitted to five clinical centers of Mofid Center, Tehran Specialized and Subspecialty Children’s Hospital, Hakim Hospital, Shahid Fahmideh Children’s Hospital, and Bahrami Hospital in Tehran City. Clinical data were retrospectively collected from eligible patients admitted between January 2023 and December 2024. Data preprocessing, machine learning analyses, and manuscript preparation were subsequently performed after completion of the data collection period.

These children received either positive or negative diagnostic results for bronchiolitis infections and subsequently underwent therapies as advised by the physicians. The data of these patients were stored in a single integrated database in an Excel Worksheet (.xlsx). This database included data on 1260 suspected patients with bronchiolitis, some of whom were diagnosed with bronchiolitis and others who were not. The non-bronchiolitis group included children initially suspected of bronchiolitis who were subsequently determined not to meet the clinical diagnostic criteria for bronchiolitis after physician evaluation and diagnostic assessment. These cases mainly consisted of mild viral respiratory illnesses and non-bronchiolitis respiratory presentations. Children with confirmed bronchopneumonia and chronic cardiopulmonary or neurological disorders were excluded.

Among the total cases, 529 and 731 were assigned to bronchiolitis and non-bronchiolitis cases, respectively, based on physicians’ assessments. The positive and negative cases were coded 1 and 0, respectively, in the current database.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All children aged less than 24 months were considered for this study. On the contrary, children aged more than 24 months, with confirmed bronchopneumonia, and with chronic illness such as cardiac, pulmonary, and neurological disorders were excluded from the population consideration. Features and outcome variable

The risk factors, including gender, socioeconomic status, fever, preterm birth, weak immune system, smoking, overcrowding, contact with other children, having siblings exposed to infections, muscle weakness, maternal allergy, maternal asthma, maternal smoking, maternal hypertension, maternal diabetes mellitus, severe acute malnutrition, and family history of asthma, were considered as input in the current database. These features were used to establish prediction models for bronchiolitis in children aged less than 2 years. All predictors were obtained from information available during the initial clinical evaluation, before diagnostic confirmation. Accordingly, the proposed framework should be interpreted as an early presentation-based risk stratification model rather than a pre-symptomatic prediction tool. Notably, these variables were extracted from routinely documented clinical records within a multicenter database.

Outcome definition

Bronchiolitis diagnosis was defined based on physician-documented clinical diagnoses recorded at each participating pediatric center. These diagnoses were extracted from a harmonized multicenter database and reflected routine clinical practice rather than a strictly standardized research protocol. No uniform requirement for confirmatory laboratory testing (e.g., RSV testing) or imaging-based adjudication was applied across all centers, and diagnoses represent clinically assigned bronchiolitis cases during hospital evaluation.

Database preprocessing

Data preprocessing was conducted in three phases. First, the database was screened for duplicates by two independent investigators (R.N. and M.Sh.). Duplicate entries (defined as multiple records for a single patient or redundant IDs) were consolidated. Second, some records contained invalid values outside predefined clinical ranges; these entries were treated as missing values and subsequently imputed. To prevent information leakage, the dataset was first partitioned into a training set (70%) and an independent test set (30%) using stratified random splitting. All subsequent procedures, including imputation and exploratory analysis, were derived solely from the training data. Records with > 10% missing values were excluded. For the remaining data, missing values were imputed using the K-Nearest Neighbors (K-NN) algorithm. Categorical variables were numerically encoded to facilitate the imputation process. The preprocessing parameters established on the training set were then consistently applied to the independent test set to ensure rigorous validation. The proportion and distribution of missing values across variables is presented in Table S1 in Supplementary-A.

Feature selection

As data volume increases, machine learning methods, data pattern discovery, and data processing become less efficient. Higher data dimensionality increases the likelihood of noisy, redundant, and irrelevant data, leading to greater overfitting and a higher risk of errors. One effective technique to reduce the adverse impact of high-dimensional data is feature selection (FS). In this technique, a subset of the original features is selected based on relevance, thereby improving model performance by excluding irrelevant features from model construction [20]. FS is critical for developing more understandable and straightforward models, improving algorithms’ performance, preventing overfitting, and preparing clean, understandable data [21, 22]. In this study, univariate LR analysis was initially used as a screening tool to identify the most clinically relevant features and to ensure the statistical significance of the predictors included in the models (P < 0.05). To avoid information leakage, feature selection was performed exclusively on the training dataset after data splitting. The selected predictors were subsequently applied to the independent test set without further modification. Additionally, this approach was intended to support model interpretability and clinical practicality, and reduce redundancy rather than to impose strict exclusion criteria. The IBM SPSS Statistics V 25 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp., USA) environment was used for data analysis in this step.

Model development

We used eight selected ML algorithms to establish prediction models for bronchiolitis. Four base algorithms were Decision Tree (DT), Naïve Bayes (NB), Multi-layer perception (MLP), and K-NN. On the contrary, we used four ensemble algorithms: eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), and Random Forest (RF). The selection of these algorithms was based on their greater applicability and higher performance in completing ML tasks in biomedical research than other algorithms. In this step, we used the Python programming language (V 3.12) with various libraries, including pandas, seaborn, matplotlib, and NumPy, as well as Weka V 3.9, to build prediction models using the aforementioned algorithms and data analysis methods.

Data splitting strategy

To ensure a rigorous evaluation and prevent information leakage, the dataset was initially partitioned into a training (70%) and an independent test (30%) set using stratified random splitting before any preprocessing or imputation procedures were conducted. We used the hold-out strategy to split the original dataset. In this strategy, the data are randomly divided into two subsets: the training set and the test set. The training set is used to develop ML models, while the test set is used to evaluate the accuracy of the trained model. Typically, two-thirds (70%) of the data are used for training, and one-third (30%) for testing the model’s performance [23, 24]. We used 70% of the data for the training set and 30% for assessing the accuracy of the established prediction models by the hold-out strategy. To optimize the predictive performance of the established models on the training data, we used K-fold cross-validation (K = 10). In this strategy, the primary data is split into 10 equal folds. Nine folds are used for training, and one for testing the developed model. To mitigate the bias in the models’ performance regarding the data used, this splitting process is iterated (K = 10) times, and the performance is considered as the average of the performances in 10 iterations. Moreover, to mitigate the impact of data imbalance between bronchiolitis and non-bronchiolitis cases, we employed stratified 10-fold cross-validation. Notably, stratified 10-fold cross-validation is an extensively used and preferred assessment technique for data mining tasks, rather than conventional data splitting strategies. This technique is efficient at mitigating differences in prediction error, enhancing the use of training and test data while avoiding overfitting and overlapping between training and test sets, and supporting the experimental theory presented by arbitrary data splitting strategies [25].

Hyperparameter tuning

Systematic hyperparameter optimization is essential for maximizing the predictive performance of ML algorithms. In this study, we employed Grid Search Cross-Validation (GridSearchCV) to identify the optimal parameter configurations for each model. This approach involves an automated, exhaustive search over a predefined grid of hyperparameter values to identify the combination that yields the highest performance. By integrating GridSearchCV within the stratified cross-validation framework on the training set, we mitigated the risk of overfitting and prevented optimistic bias. The hyperparameter search ranges were selected based on established literature and computational feasibility [26–28]. Although GridSearchCV was used for systematic optimization, broader parameter spaces may further improve model performance in future studies.

Model assessment

We used comprehensive performance assessment criteria to evaluate the predictive performance of ML models for bronchiolitis in children. The performance criteria of the confusion matrix, including Positive Predictive Value (PPV), Negative Predictive Value (NPV), sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and F-score, were employed to assess the predictive performance ability of the ML models for bronchiolitis. We plotted the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve to calculate the Area Under the Curve (AUC), providing a threshold-independent measure of the models’ discriminative ability. Additionally, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) and calibration plots were utilized to assess the clinical utility and reliability of the predicted probabilities.

Model explainability

While machine learning models excel at capturing complex patterns, their “black-box” nature often limits clinical adoption. To enhance transparency and clinical trust, we employed Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), a framework rooted in cooperative game theory [29, 30]. SHAP provides a mathematically rigorous method for quantifying each clinical feature’s contribution to the model’s final prediction. By calculating SHAP values, we identified the direction and magnitude of each predictor’s influence, ensuring the model’s decisions are interpretable and clinically actionable [28, 31, 32].

Results

Data preprocessing and sample characteristics

Twelve records, including data from four bronchiolitis and eight non-bronchiolitis cases, were identified as duplicates and excluded from further analysis for this study. Twenty-five rows, including 10 and 15 data from bronchiolitis and non-bronchiolitis cases, had invalid values and were considered for removal and filling as missing values. Ten records, including five bronchiolitis and five non-bronchiolitis, contained more than 10% missing data across all features and were therefore omitted from the spreadsheet. Thirty-eight samples, including 17 bronchiolitis and 21 non-bronchiolitis rows, had less than 10% missing data and were imputed by the nearest neighboring values identified by the K-NN algorithm. Finally, 1238 rows, including 520 and 718 cases belonging to bronchiolitis and non-bronchiolitis patients, remained in this study for data analysis. Model explanation

As this study demonstrated, the XGBoost model achieved higher predictive performance for predicting bronchiolitis among children aged less than 2 years. We generated a SHAP value plot using XGBoost, a higher-performing model for predicting bronchiolitis, to demonstrate explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) for interpretability. The SHAP plot in Fig. 6 revealed that overcrowding was the most influential predictor, exhibiting a strong positive association with the outcome. Severe acute malnutrition and maternal smoking were also major contributors, consistently increasing the predicted risk. Socioeconomic and exposure-related factors showed moderate effects, while demographic variables, such as gender, had negligible impacts.

Discussion

Primary findings

This study developed an ML framework for early clinical risk stratification of bronchiolitis in children under 2 years of age. By benchmarking four base classifiers against four ensemble methods, XGBoost emerged as the best-performing model. It demonstrated strong predictive performance in both the training and independent test datasets, although a moderate decline in performance was observed in the test set, suggesting possible overfitting.

The clinical robustness of the model was supported by Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) and calibration plots, indicating favorable net benefit and good agreement between predicted and observed risks across a range of threshold probabilities.

Importantly, SHAP analysis improved interpretability by identifying the most influential features in the model’s predictions. Environmental and socioeconomic factors, particularly overcrowding, severe acute malnutrition, and maternal smoking, were the most influential variables associated with increased predicted risk of bronchiolitis. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating social determinants of health into pediatric risk prediction models. The strong contribution of overcrowding and exposure-related variables may reflect increased viral transmission dynamics in confined environments, particularly for RSV, which is highly contagious among infants. Similarly, the impact of maternal smoking and malnutrition may be explained by their effects on immune system impairment and airway vulnerability, increasing susceptibility to lower respiratory tract infections.

Model performance and algorithmic superiority

Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on predicting disease severity or hospitalization outcomes, our study aimed to predict the early risk of bronchiolitis before clinical deterioration. The performance of XGBoost is consistent with previous studies that report the effectiveness of gradient boosting methods on structured clinical data. Mateo et al. [33] and Raita et al. [34] similarly reported that XGBoost and Gradient Boosted Decision Trees achieved high discrimination (AUC 0.88–0.95) in bronchiolitis-related prediction tasks.

In contrast, base learners showed relatively lower and less stable performance, particularly on the test data. This finding aligns with Chen et al. [35], who reported limited discriminative ability for LR in similar clinical prediction settings. Overall, these results confirm the advantage of ensemble learning methods in capturing nonlinear relationships and improving predictive stability in pediatric datasets.

Beyond the discrimination performance, the model demonstrated good calibration and clinically meaningful net benefit in decision curve analysis, indicating its potential value across a wide range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities. It suggests that the model could support risk stratification workflows, particularly in early triage settings where rapid identification of high-risk infants is critical. For example, patients identified by the model as high-risk may benefit from closer monitoring, early supportive interventions, or priority referral to higher levels of care, potentially reducing disease progression and healthcare burden. To address the inherent lack of transparency in ML models, SHAP-based annotations were used to increase interpretability. This approach allows clinicians not only to understand which features have the greatest overall impact but also to see how specific factors contribute to predictions in individual patients. Such interpretive capability is particularly important in clinical settings, as it supports informed decision-making and may increase physician trust and acceptance of AI-based tools.

Limitations and future implications

Some limitations in this study should be considered. Several important environmental and virological variables, including air pollution exposure and viral genotype information, were unavailable due to the retrospective design. It may influence residual confounding and limit the comprehensiveness of the predictive framework. Integrating these factors may improve model performance and biological interpretability. The study’s retrospective nature and lack of standardized operational diagnostic definitions across centers may introduce heterogeneity and affect the model’s reproducibility. Reliance on routine clinical assessment without centralized or uniform virological confirmation likely led to inter-center diagnostic variability. Also, future prospective studies should focus on integrating real-time clinical data and longitudinal follow-ups to evaluate the long-term impact of the model on patient outcomes and hospital resource optimization. Lacking consistent alternative diagnoses due to the retrospective design, this model serves as a bronchiolitis risk-stratification tool rather than a comprehensive differential diagnostic framework. While the XGBoost model exhibited superior performance, a moderate decline in predictive metrics was observed when transitioning from the training to the independent test set. This reduction likely reflects a combination of model optimism, limited sample size, and the inherent heterogeneity of multicenter clinical data. Performance declines in non-ensemble algorithms confirm generalization challenges in moderate clinical datasets; however, ensemble methods (especially XGBoost) show superior stability and generalization. Despite cross-validation and tuning, some training optimism is expected; results reflect internal validation only and require further external validation for generalizability.

Univariate analysis may miss variable interactions; however, with only 17 predictors, model simplicity was prioritized, making the risk of omitted interactions acceptable. Although this study used data from multiple pediatric centers in a single metropolitan area, all datasets were pooled before model development and testing, necessitating external validation with center-based or geographically distinct cohorts before clinical implementation. Some hyperparameter search intervals were relatively limited, and several optimal values were identified near predefined boundaries. Future studies using broader search spaces and more advanced optimization strategies, such as Bayesian optimization or randomized search methods, may further enhance model tuning and predictive performance.

Implications for future practice

The findings of this study suggest that explainable ML models may support early clinical risk stratification of bronchiolitis in pediatric settings. Following prospective and external validation, integrating the intelligent XGBoost-based system with EHR data could assist clinicians in identifying high-risk infants at initial presentation and support more timely triage and risk-based monitoring decisions in children. In other words, by identifying a high-risk child, the clinician can implement targeted interventions, such as prioritized follow-up for infants from overcrowded households or immediate oxygen saturation monitoring for those with underlying malnutrition. An important strength of the proposed framework is the incorporation of explainable AI through SHAP analysis, which enhances transparency by identifying the most influential predictors contributing to model outputs. Rather than functioning as a black-box system, the model may provide clinically interpretable insights regarding individual risk profiles, potentially improving clinician trust and facilitating practical adoption in real-world settings. The prominent contribution of socioeconomic and environmental factors, including overcrowding and maternal smoking, further emphasizes the importance of considering social determinants of health in pediatric respiratory disease prevention. These findings suggest that future preventive strategies may benefit from combining clinical risk assessment with targeted public health interventions and parental education programs. Overall, explainable ML approaches may contribute to more data-driven and personalized pediatric care while supporting more efficient allocation of healthcare resources.

Conclusion

By incorporating socioeconomic and environmental determinants alongside clinical indicators, our XGBoost model provides a promising framework for predicting bronchiolitis risk in children under 2 years of age. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on disease severity or hospitalization outcomes, this study may contribute to earlier risk stratification and support targeted interventions in pediatric healthcare settings. Nevertheless, additional prospective and external validation studies are required before broad clinical implementation.
