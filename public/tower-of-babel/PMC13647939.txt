Synthesis, SAR
Analysis, and Antifungal Potency against
Plant Pathogenic Fungi of New Azidopyrazoles

Daniel Canseco-González, Rosa E. Sánchez-Fernández, Elizabeth Navarro-Cerón, Stephania Olivares-Sánchez, Alejandro O. Viviano-Posadas, Alejandro Dorazco-González, Ricardo Parra-Unda, Gilmar López-Armenta, Adrián L. Orjuela, Jorge Alí-Torres

Abstract

The development of potent synthetic antifungal agents
for plants
is an ongoing challenge of modern chemistry that impacts biological,
medicinal and environmental sciences. Herein, a concise Structure–Activity
Relationship (SAR) model involving seven synthetic azidopyrazoles
toward three pathogens of agricultural products and ornamental plants: Fusarium oxysporum, Rhizoctonia sp.,
and Alternaria alternata is described.
The set of azidopyrazoles 1–7 includes a central
fragment of 3-azido-1H-pyrazole substitutes in different
positions of the ring (5-substituted, R = H, 1; R = −CH3, 2; R = –Ph, 3; R = –PhOCH3, 5; 4-substituted, R = –Ph, 4; R = –(4-Cl-Ph), 6) and 3-substituted 5-azido-1H-pyrazole (R = thiophene, 7) which are prepared
via a high-yielding (>85%), one-step synthesis from commercial
cheap
aminopyrazoles. Compounds 3 and 7 showed
the greatest inhibition with IC50 values of 26.52 ±
1.14 and 26.75 ± 1.36 μg/mL for F. oxysporum; 18.63 ± 1.08 and 14.39 ± 1.09 μg/mL for Rhizoctonia sp.; and 15.53 ± 1.14 and 21.02 ±
1.37 μg/mL for A. alternata.
SAR analysis revealed that aryl substitution at C5 increased markedly
the antifungal potency. In contrast, unsubstituted and alkyl-substituted
pyrazoles (1, 2) had low activity. Combining 3 with Ag-nanoparticles (NPs) doubled its efficacy only against Rhizoctonia sp. (AgNPs, 10 nm, 100 ng/mL). From IC50 = 18.63 ± 1.08 to 9.65 ± 1.38 μg/mL). Molecular
docking with MEP36 supported these results: compound 3 had the highest binding affinity (−6.8 kcal/mol) due to π–alkyl
interaction with Val274 residue. Furthermore, root mean square deviation
analyses confirmed the tight binding mode.

Introduction

The chemistry of organic azides has been
extensively investigated
since the 19th century. The first documented synthesis of an azido-substituted
organic compound occurred in 1864, when Griess synthesized phenyl
azide (A). Organic azides are versatile due to their
chemical nature. They consist of three nitrogen atoms arranged linearly,
forming a polyatomic anion characterized by its high reactivity (B). For example, reactions of organic azides with alkynes
yield 1,4–1,2,3-triazoles via Huisgen 1,3-dipolar cycloadditions or imines can be formed when reacting with carbonyl
compounds (Aza-Wittig reaction).

Azido-substituted organic compounds have applications
in various
fields, including the explosive-related industry (i.e., sodium azide
NaN3) and medicinal chemistry. Although NaN3 is applied as a pest
control agent, its high toxicity and phytotoxicity limit its desirability
for use.
,
 On the other hand, pyrazoles are five-membered
heterocyclic compounds with two adjacent nitrogen atoms.
,
 These molecules are found in biology, medicine, material chemistry,
and agriculture such as fungicides or
agrochemicals, but their synthesis typically involves multiple steps.

Consequently, atom economy emerges
as a pivotal factor in contemporary chemical synthesis, underscoring
the imperative for enhancing process sustainability. Atom economy,
a term coined in the field of synthetic chemistry, signifies the principle
of leveraging novel synthetic methodologies to optimize the integration
of starting materials into the final products. Recently, functionalized
pyrazole derivatives have been evaluated as pesticides due to properties
such as low toxicity and activity. Recently,
pyrazole derivatives have attracted attention for their applications
as herbicides, fungicides,
−

 and insecticides.
,−

 For instance,
furametpyr and penthiopyrad (C,D), both fungicides employed in various crops for pest and
disease management, have noted advantages and disadvantages.

Furametpyr is an aromatic pyrazole amide which is used to manage
rice sheath rust by inhibiting the biosynthesis of aspartate and glutamate
in the target fungus. Penthiopyrad is a broad-spectrum fungicide,
demonstrates efficacy against diseases resistant to strobilurins and
demethylation inhibitors, yet has documented serious mammalian toxicity,
including effects on liver, thyroid, adrenal, and kidney glands, which
may increase cancer risk.

Despite
the established applications of azido-substituted organic
molecules in several fields, their use as antifungal compounds against
plant pathogenic fungi is practically unexplored. This work presents
a concise yet comprehensive rationalized synthesis of seven novel
aryl and nonaryl azido-pyrazoles, exhibiting high atom economy in
a single-step synthesis. Furthermore, it encompasses a comprehensive
examination of their structure–activity relationship (SAR)
against three fungal plant pathogens. This SAR is supported by in-silico
calculations, such as molecular docking and molecular dynamic simulations.

Experimental Section

Chemicals and Materials

Amino pyrazole precursors were
purchased from Aldrich and used directly without further purification.
NaNO2 and sodium azide (NaN3) salts were also
purchased from commercial distributors. All solvents used for the
syntheses of azido pyrazole derivatives were used as received from
Aldrich. In most cases HPLC quality solvents were used as water or
ethyl acetate used for extraction procedures. Unless specified otherwise,
NMR spectra were recorded at 25 °C on a Bruker Advance spectrometer
operating at 300 (1H NMR) and 75 MHz (13C NMR),
respectively. The chemical shifts (δ) are given in ppm relative
to TMS as internal standard. Assignments are based on homo- and heteronuclear
shift correlation spectroscopy. Low resolution mass spectrometry experiments
were recorded on a Shimadzu GCMSQP2010 Plus in the EI mode, 70 eV,
200 °C via direct inlet probe at positive polarity.

General Procedure to Synthesize Azidopyrazoles 1–7

The new azido pyrazole derivatives 1–7 were successfully synthesized using a modified
established procedure, starting from
primary amine pyrazoles via the formation of the diazonium salt group,
which was subsequently replaced by the azido group using NaN3. shows the
general reaction and chemical structures of azido pyrazoles 1–7.

Chemical Synthesis

One equiv of the corresponding amino
pyrazole was placed in a round-bottomed flask and dissolved in a 10%
water/hydrochloric acid solution. A homogeneous solution was observed,
indicating complete dissolution of the starting material.

The
solution was cooled to 10 °C using a water/ice bath and stirred
for 10 min. Subsequently, 1.25 equiv of NaNO2 were slowly
added to the ammonium salt formed, and the mixture was stirred for
30 min at 10 °C. Next, 1.50 equiv of NaN3 were added
and the reaction continued at 10 °C for an additional 30 min.
The final solution was stirred for another 18 h at room temperature.

Note: For compound 7 the reaction was left stirred
for 6 h only, otherwise the formation of a polymeric compound is obtained,
and the yield of the desirable compound is affected.

Isolation

For all compounds, the crude products were
isolated by liquid–liquid extraction using ethyl acetate/water.

Prior to extraction, the crude product solution was neutralized
with NaHCO3 until the pH reached neutral. The extraction
procedure involved three rounds of extraction with ethyl acetate,
followed by washing with water, brine, and drying with anhydrous Na2SO4. The mixed organic phases containing ethyl
acetate and the crude product were evaporated using a rotary evaporator
until the desired residue was obtained. The yielded products are color
powders (reddish, greenish, orange or yellow).

Purification

The purification process involved reprecipitation
using a CHCl3/pentane system. A solution of the crude product
was formed with minimal CHCl3, which was then added to
cold pentane cooled to −15 °C. This method yielded pure
products that were dried under vacuum until no solvents were detected
by spectroscopic analyses.

Evaluation of Antifungal Activity

The antifungal activity
was assessed by examining the impact of triazole and azido pyrazole
derivatives on the radial growth of three phytopathogenic fungi: Rhizoctonia sp., Fusarium oxysporum, and Alternaria alternata. The agar
dilution method involves the dissolution
of compounds in methanol or acetone, followed by their combination
with Potato Dextrose Agar and water. Each compound, with a concentration
of 100 μg/mL, was meticulously prepared and placed into Petri
dishes. Following this, the compounds were solidified, inoculated
with fungal mycelium, and subsequently incubated at a temperature
of 28 °C for a period of 12 days Captan 50 served as the positive
control, while negative controls included PDA-water, PDA-methanol,
and PDA-acetone. Bioassays were conducted with four replicates in
a randomized design. Mycelial diameter measurements were taken daily
over 7 days, calculating inhibition percentages compared to control
cultures at specific intervals for each fungus.

Synergistic Effect of Compound 3 with Ag Nanoparticles
(Ag-NPs)

Compound 3 was evaluated at a concentration
of 10 μg/mL by agar dilution method Ag-NPs were added culture
media at 1% of compound 3 concentration (100 ng/mL).
Ag-NPs stock suspensions at 0.02 mg/mL with a particle size of 10
nm were used. The percentage of inhibition of each treatment was calculated
using as negative control PDA-water and PDA-Ag-NPs at 100 ng/mL.

Determination of Mean Inhibitory Concentration (IC50) Values

The determination of the IC50 values
of the antifungal compounds 3, 3 + AgNPs, 7, and Captan 50 on the radial growth of the fungal pathogens
was conducted using the agar dilution method at a concentration range
of 5 to 100 μg/mL (27.02 to 540.34 μM) for compound 3 and 3 + AgNPs, (26.15 to 522.99 μM) for
compound 7, and 1 to 50 μg/mL (3.33
to 166.34 μM) for Captan 50 and by calculating the percentage
of inhibition at each concentration.

Statistical Analysis

The data obtained from the evaluation
of the antifungal activity were then subjected to one-way analysis
of variance (ANOVA, α = 0.05), followed by Tukey’s multiple
comparison tests. The statistical analysis of bioactivity data and
the determination of IC50 values were performed using the
GraphPad Prism software, version 5.00 (GraphPad Software Inc., San
Diego, CA, USA). The data are expressed as the mean ± standard
deviation (SD) or standard error (SE). In accordance with the methodologies
employed by Sánchez-Fernández et al., a p value of 0.05 or less was considered
to indicate statistical significance.

Protein Sequence Alignment

To identify conserved residues
potentially involved in substrate interaction or molecular recognition,
a multiple sequence alignment was performed using MEP36-like proteins
from three phytopathogenic fungi. The sequences analyzed included Rhizoctonia solani (KAF8751731.1) and A. alternata (XP_018383305), both retrieved from
the NCBI protein database, as well as F. oxysporum, obtained from a crystallographic structure available in the Protein
Data Bank (PDB ID: 7QFY;) This latter sequence corresponds
to a structurally resolved MEP36 protein, providing a reference for
identifying functional residues.

All sequences were formatted
in FASTA and submitted to the online version of MAFFT v7 using default parameters, which apply the FFT-NS-2
algorithm for efficient and accurate alignment of moderately similar
sequences. MAFFT was selected due to its proven ability to detect
conserved regions across homologous proteins.

The alignment
was manually examined to identify residues potentially
involved in ligand binding, as inferred from the crystallographic
structure of the F. oxysporum MEP36
protein. Special attention was paid to positional correspondence and
chemical conservation of key residues across the three sequences.
This comparative approach enabled the assessment of functional conservation
among MEP36 homologues from different fungal species, providing preliminary
insights that may inform future studies, such as comparative structural
modeling or molecular docking with shared ligands.

Computational Details

Seven azido-pyrazole derivatives 1–7 and their corresponding amino-pyrazole precursors
(1P–7P) were modeled and optimized in their geometries
through DFT (B3LYP/6–31 + G­(d,p)) calculations using the Gaussian
16 suite of programs. Frequency calculations
confirmed that all optimized structures corresponded to local minima
on the potential energy surface by the absence of imaginary vibrational
modes. Solvent effects (water) were incorporated using the SMD solvent
model. NBO charges were computed during
optimization and used for accurate partial atomic charge assignments
in subsequent docking simulations.

The biological target selected for docking was the Zn­(II)-metalloproteinase
MEP36 from F. oxysporum, using the
crystallographic structure deposited under PDB ID: 7QFY. The protein was
prepared using AutoDock Tools, by removing
crystallographic water molecules and cofactors, adding polar hydrogens,
and assigning Gasteiger charges. The
docking grid was centered on the catalytic pocket of the enzyme, including
residues involved in substrate coordination and interaction with the
catalytic Zn­(II) ion.

Molecular docking simulations were performed
using four different
programs: AutoDock Vina, mina, Qvina2, and Qvinaw. Ligands were prepared in PDBQT format with torsional
flexibility assigned according to AutoDock Tools protocols. Each program
generated multiple binding poses per ligand, with the top-ranked pose
selected based on its predicted binding affinity (in kcal/mol).

To improve the reliability and consistency of the docking predictions,
a consensus scoring approach was employed by calculating the average
binding affinity across all four programs for the top-ranked pose
of each ligand.

To assess the stability of the ligand–protein
complex with
the highest binding affinity, a 100 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation
was performed using AmberTools. The complex
was prepared using the ff14SB force field for the protein and GAFF for the ligand, solvated in a TIP3P water box with a 10 Å buffer, and neutralized by adding counterions. After
a two-step energy minimization and gradual heating to 300 K, the system
was equilibrated under NPT conditions. The production run was conducted
at 300 K and 1 atm using a 2 fs time step. Electrostatic interactions
were calculated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method, and bond
constraints involving hydrogen atoms were enforced using the SHAKE
algorithm. Trajectory analysis included root-mean-square deviation
(RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) and hydrogen bond analysis.

To complement the docking scores, binding free energies (ΔG
bind) were estimated using the Linear Interaction
Energy (LIE) method. Following standard single-trajectory protocols,
the interaction energy between the ligand and the surrounding environment
(protein + solvent) was calculated across the simulation frames. The
electrostatic (V
ele) and van der Waals
(V
vdW) interaction energies were extracted,
and the final ΔG
bind was computed
using the empirical equation: ΔG
bind = α⟨ΔV
vdW⟩
+ β⟨ΔV
ele⟩ +
γ, where the scaling parameters were set to α = 0.18,
β = 0.50, and γ = 0.0 kcal/mol.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of Azidopyrazole Compounds (1–7)

The synthesis of compounds 1–7 was successfully accomplished using a modified synthetic
protocol. The process commenced with
commercially available primary amine pyrazoles substituted at the C3 and C5 positions of the five-membered
heterocycle (). During the procedure, an in situ diazonium salt
intermediate was generated by reacting to the primary amine pyrazoles
with NaNO2 in an acidic medium. Subsequent substitution
of the diazonium group by an azido group from NaN3 afforded
the target products.

Progress of the reactions was monitored via
thin layer chromatography
(TLC), and upon completion (i.e., full consumption of the starting
material), the reactions were quenched with NaHCO3, thereby
neutralizing the acidic pH to neutrality (pH 7.0–7.5). Product
isolation was achieved through extraction with ethyl acetate. For
the preparation of compounds 1–7, independent
batches (more than three per compound) were synthesized. Notably,
for compounds 1–6, extending the reaction time
overnight (∼18 h) led to a modest increase in yield, although
the azidation step typically required only three to 4 h for completion.

In contrast, for compound 7 (the thiophene-substituted
azido pyrazole), extending the reaction beyond six to 8 h resulted
in significant formation of an undesirable polymeric byproduct, adversely
affecting overall yield. Single-step yields for compounds 1–7 ranged between 85% and 94% (to see details see ).

Characterization of Azidopyrazole Compounds 1–7

The identity and purity of compounds 1–7 were confirmed by 1H, 13C NMR spectra, HRMS-ESI­(+)
for 2–7, HRMS-IE­(+) for 1 and FT-IR
experiments (Figures S1–S28).

As anticipated, signals corresponding to the pyrazole frameworks
were observed. The presence of pyrazole protons was detected in the
range of 5.76 to 7.71 ppm, thereby indicating their aromatic nature,
as illustrated in . For compounds 1–4 and 7, the N–H
proton of the pyrazoles manifested as broad signals ranging from 10.07
to 12.37 ppm on the 1H NMR spectra, indicative of their
acidity character.

Moreover, the aromatic protons of the phenyl ring
(compounds 3–6) resonate at 7.43 and 7.41 ppm
as multiplets within
the aromatic zone of the spectra. In contrast, neither of the 1H NMR spectra of compounds 5 and 6 showed the signal for the pyrazole proton attached to the N1 position
of the heterocyclic core. The absence of these signals is common for
strongly acidic protons such as those found in pyrazoles and amines,
as well as in other functional groups like alcohols and can also be
explained by tautomeric equilibria.

The pyrazole proton of the
compound manifests at 6.16 ppm as a
singlet. Conversely, the connectivity patterns of pyrazoles 1–7 were ascertained through 13C NMR analysis,
which yielded anticipated signals for the pyrazole carbons in all
compounds.

Moreover, the connectivity patterns were determined
by the presence
of substituents, as evidenced by the 13C NMR spectra. For
instance, the resonance of the quaternary carbon (Cq-N3) with the attached azido substituent (-N3) to the pyrazole
core occurs within the range of 147.63 to 149.35 ppm (). Regarding the individual
pattern of all other carbons for each compound in 13C NMR
see the Supporting Information. Indeed,
the synthesis of azidopyrazole derivatives has received minimal attention
to date. Nevertheless, in 2020, Ewies derivatized azidopyrazole derivatives to yield complex azidopyrazoles
with different functionalities, which have been identified as both
cytotoxic compounds and antimicrobials. In comparison to that previous
azidopyrazoles, the 1H NMR and 13C NMR characterization
of compounds 1–7 is consistent with them.

The Direct Analysis in Real Time approach with positive polarity
(MS-DART+) was used to conduct mass spectrometry experiments.
These analyses indicated that the mass spectrum of compounds 1–7 corresponds to the anticipated molecular mass of
the new azido pyrazole compounds.

High resolution mass spectra
with positive scan of ESI were used
to identify the protonated molecular ions for the corresponding compounds.
AHRMS-EI­(+) spectra of the synthesized compounds are shown in Supporting Information.

Next, FT-IR spectra
were measured for all compounds. The experimental
results obtained from the Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) analysis
have provided compelling evidence that can be used to identify the
characteristic fingerprints of these compounds. These results indicate
the presence of distinct functional groups (for more information see Supporting Information files). Concurrently,
the FT-IR experiments have revealed the connectivity pattern of the
compounds, with a particular emphasis on the direct detection of the

Molecular Alignment

Fungalysins represent a family
of Zn­(II)-metalloproteinases (MEP36) identified in pathogenic fungi
and certain bacteria. Although these proteases exhibit peptide motifs
similar to those found in bacterial thermolysins, they share limited
sequence homology with this extensively studied class of enzymes.
Members of the M36 family function as extracellular metalloproteinases
and play a significant role in the pathogenicity and virulence of F. oxysporum. The active site of MEP36 is defined
by the Glu185 residue, while His184 and His188 are key residues involved
in Zn­(II) coordination and are essential for protease functionality.

Given the critical nature of these residues and the enzyme’s
contribution to fungal virulence, MEP36 is a promising molecular target,
especially considering its role in degrading plant chitinases produced
as a defense response.

MEP36 enzymes
are exclusive to fungi, and multiple sequence alignment
reveals strong conservation within the catalytic core’s active
site regions. These findings provide
insight into the potential mechanism of action for pyrazole compounds
against pathogens such as F. oxysporum, A. alternata, and Rhizoctonia species. Notably, the binding sites in F. oxysporum and A. alternata demonstrate substantial
overlap, which may explain comparable inhibition test outcomes. Conversely,
significant differences in the binding site alignments between Rhizoctonia and the other two fungi correspond to improved
inhibition results, suggesting alternative binding interactions and
potentially stronger compound affinities. Specifically, the sequence alignment identifies residue 275 as a
point of divergence between Rhizoctonia sp. and the
other fungi. Because the adjacent Val274 is the critical π-alkyl
contact stabilizing compound 3, this substitution at
position 275 likely alters the local steric environment and flexibility
of the hydrophobic pocket. This structural perturbation mechanistically
explains the enhanced binding affinity and distinct IC50 trends observed for compound 3 against Rhizoctonia sp. relative to F. oxysporum and A. alternata.

The crystal structure of MEP36
is available in the Protein Data
Bank (PDB) and was utilized in the present computational studies.
Metalloproteases (MEPs) encompass several families of hydrolytic enzymes,
most of which are Zn­(II)-dependent due to the presence of two histidine
residues forming a HEXXH motif, as is the case for the M36 enzyme
analyzed here (PDB ID: 7QFY). This motif consists of H184, E185, Y186, T187, and
H188. Previous research has explored the functions
of various metalloproteinases, including MEP36, MEP35, and MEP43,
across different pathogens. For instance, F. oxysporum produces an MEP36 protein, FoMEP1, that
operates alongside the serine protease FoSep1 to degrade tomato-derived
chitinases. Notably, azido pyrazoles
specifically those substituted with aryl groups at their five-membered
framework positions (3, 5, 7) have demonstrated promising inhibitory activities, exhibiting high
affinity for both Zn­(II)-binding sites (H184 and H188) and the active site E185 .

Molecular Docking

To evaluate how azidation affects
molecular binding affinity, azido-pyrazole compounds 1–7 and their amino precursors (1P–7P) were docked
to Zn­(II)-metalloproteinase MEP36 (PDB: 7QFY) (Tables S5–S6).

summarizes
the consensus docking scores. The azido derivatives consistently showed
more favorable (more negative) binding energies than their amino counterparts,
indicating improved interaction with the catalytic site. Notable examples
include compounds 3 (−6.8 vs −6.1 kcal/mol), 5 (−6.7 vs −6.4 kcal/mol), and 7 (−6.3 vs −5.8 kcal/mol). The greatest binding affinity
improvements occurred in compounds with initially lower affinities,
such as compound 1 (−5.3 vs −4.1 kcal/mol)
and compound 2 (−5.7 vs −4.5 kcal/mol).
Overall, azidation enhances binding affinity, especially in low-affinity
scaffolds, with a consistent trend observed across the series. Moreover,
all azido derivatives outperformed the azide anion itself, which exhibited
a weighted binding affinity of −3.0 kcal/mol.

As depicted in and Figure S30–S32, all
pyrazole-derived compounds demonstrate a comparable binding configuration
within the active site of the MEP36 metalloproteinase. Molecular docking
analyses indicate that each ligand occupies the same binding pocket,
stabilized by two to three hydrogen bonds with catalytic residues.
Glu214 (2.6 Å), His188 (2.0 Å), and Ser191 (2.6 Å)the
latter two involved in Zn­(II) coordinationact as points of
interaction for the ligands. The consistent interaction pattern indicates
that variations in binding affinity among the complexes are mainly
influenced by the type and spatial arrangement of substituents on
the pyrazole group.

For compound 3, its higher binding
affinity is attributed
to favorable π-alkyl interactions between its phenyl group and
the Val274 residue, which contributes to the stabilization of the
hydrophobic cavity. The π-alkyl interaction observed for compound 3 is absent in compounds 1, 2, 4, and 6 due to the arrangement of their substituents,
whereas compound 5 exhibited an interaction pattern and
binding affinity similar to those of compound 3. Notably,
amino acid sequence alignment reveals that the residue corresponding
to Val274 in F. oxysporum is replaced
by Thr274 in Rhizoctonia, while the adjacent residue,
His275, is replaced by Leu275. These sequence substitutions could
contribute to differences in the physicochemical environment of the
binding site, thereby influencing the interaction with the ligand.
However, the observed IC50 values do not show a direct
correlation with the conservation of Val274 and His275, given that A. alternata and F. oxysporum conserve these residues yet exhibit distinct antifungal activities.
Although these residues may contribute to compound binding, they are
not the sole determinants of the observed species-dependent activity.

Other sequence differences within or near the binding region, as
well as species-specific biological factors, could also contribute
to the differences in antifungal activity.

RSMD Molecular Dynamic Simulations

RMSD profiles from
molecular dynamics simulations show that MEP36 maintains structural
stability over 100 ns, with RMSD values between 1.1 and 1.5 Å.

Compound 3, the top ligand, displays moderate RMSD
fluctuations (0.25–1.25 Å) but remains stable in the active
site.

The abrupt drops in RMSD observed during the trajectory
(e.g.,
at ∼30 ns and ∼95 ns) correspond to transient rotameric
adjustments of the phenyl ring, which briefly samples an alternative
hydrophobic contact before returning to its stable π-alkyl interaction
with Val274. These fast structural realignments do not disrupt the
core hydrogen bond network, confirming a stable binding mode.

These findings indicate a strong and persistent interaction between
compound 3 and MEP36, supporting its potential as an
effective inhibitor.

Furthermore, the Linear Interaction Energy
(LIE) estimations calculated
from the trajectory support these findings. The interaction energy
components highlight that the binding of compound 3 is
predominantly driven by van der Waals interactions (⟨V
vdW⟩ = −50.61 kcal/mol), which
is consistent with the critical π-alkyl interactions observed
with Val274, compared to a smaller electrostatic contribution (⟨V
ele⟩= −0.64 kcal/mol). The resulting
free energy of binding (ΔG
bind)
of −8.46 ± 0.45 kcal/mol corroborates the thermodynamic
stability predicted by molecular docking.

As illustrated in –, three principal hydrogen
bond distances were tracked throughout a 100 ns molecular dynamics
simulation. The GLU214 O···H (pyrazole) interaction
(black curve) remained highly stable (∼2.2–2.5 Å),
reflecting a robust and persistent contact that effectively anchors
the ligand within the binding pocket. The SER191 O···N
(azide) interaction (red curve) exhibited moderate stability (∼3.0–4.5
Å), indicating its function as a secondary stabilizing factor.
Conversely, the HIS188 NH···N (azide) interaction (green
curve) displayed greater variability (∼4.0–6.5 Å),
which is consistent with transient bonding behavior.

This variability may stem from the intrinsic flexibility
of the
azide moiety and the propensity of HIS188 to coordinate with the Zn­(II)
center, as inferred from the weaker interactions observed during the
molecular docking analysis. Collectively, these findings support a
dual anchoring mechanism characterized by dynamic modulation at the
azide terminus.

Conclusions

This study presents a short yet conclusive
SAR model consisting
of seven novel azido pyrazoles, which were evaluated for antifungal
activity against three phytopathogenic fungi:
F. oxysporum, Rhizoctonia sp., and A. alternata. Among these compounds, analogs 3 and 7 showed notable growth-inhibitory effects
with mean IC50 values for radial fungal growth as follows: F. oxysporum (26.52 ± 1.14 and 26.75 ±
1.36 μg/mL); Rhizoctonia sp. (18.63 ±
1.08 and 14.39 ± 1.09 μg/mL); and A. alternata (15.53 ± 1.14 and 21.02 ± 1.37 μg/mL), respectively.

A significant synergistic effect was observed when compound 3 (10 μg/mL) was combined with Ag-nanoparticles (10
nm, 100 ng/mL).

The experimental findings from this study were
corroborated by
computational simulations, encompassing molecular docking and dynamic
studies. These findings suggest that compounds 3 and 7 have the potential as candidates against plant pathogenic
fungi and provide molecular insights into its probable mode of action
in F. oxysporum.

Furthermore,
this study showed that atom economy can be used to
design simple small molecules in one step, leading to promising new
fungicides. Using the rationalized SAR approach, novel azido pyrazoles
were developed and tested as antifungals. These efforts advance the
search for effective, environmentally friendly agrochemicals for plant
protection and resistance management.
