A collaboration mechanism for medical insurance settlement based on isolation forest and Fleiss kappa smart contract
Authors: Chuanjia Yao, Zhihui Jiang, Xufeng Su, Xinchun Ma, Li Li, Siya Zhao, Ye Zhu, Hongyu Nie, Yi Deng, Weida Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe management of medical insurance funds is pivotal to the development of medical consortia. These funds serve as the operational lifeline of medical consortia and constitute critical public resources essential for public welfare. Medical expense settlement involves multiple stakeholders, including patients, tiered healthcare institutions, and insurance administrative agencies. However, disputes frequently arise between medical insurance authorities and hospitals regarding expense legitimacy due to information asymmetry and interpretative discrepancies. Such conflicts impede smoothness of payment mechanism, thereby undermining consortium operations and inter-institutional collaboration. To address these challenges, this study proposes a blockchain-based framework integrating medical expense investigation with insurance settlement. The system employs two core components: Anomaly detection via the Isolation Forest (IF) algorithm to identify potentially irregular expenses. Consensus-driven adjudication using Fleiss Kappa-based smart contracts facilitated by an anonymous panel of medical experts. This design enhances coordination between expense oversight and settlement processes, thus streamlining dispute resolution for medical expense anomalies and improving the scientific governance of insurance funds. Experimentally, the statistical validity demonstrated for unsupervised anomaly detection of IF and potential engineering applicability indicated in the analysis of healthcare cost. The healthcare consortium blockchain based on a Delegated Proof-of-Stake (DPoS) consensus mechanism and smart contract batch processing achieved a peak throughput of 229.64 transactions per second (TPS) and reduced transaction costs by up to 3,095 gwei. This demonstrates scalability for real-world medical insurance collaborative settlement systems.Keywords: Medical insurance fund, Medical expenses, Isolation forest, Fleiss kappa, Smart contractSubject terms: Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The Medical insurance system is a vital component of social security systems and an indispensable part of national administrative frameworks worldwide1,2. Its primary goal is to alleviate the economic burden of illness on populations, particularly by addressing prohibitively high medical costs. Countries including Germany, Japan, and the United States have each developed distinct healthcare security systems. Basic medical insurance specifically aims to protect residents against catastrophic disease risks, reduce the financial burden of healthcare for the insured, enhance access to medical services, improve population health, and mitigate illness-induced poverty and impoverishment3. Concurrently, significant progress in medical informatization, driven by the widespread establishment of medical consortia from provincial capitals to grassroots institutions, has laid a foundation for advancing hierarchical diagnosis and treatment policies. These policies facilitate patient access and reduce costs. Medical insurance revenue now constitutes the primary and increasingly dominant source of total income for medical institutions4. Establishing robust medical insurance fund supervision mechanisms and promoting hospital revenue-expenditure balance are thus critical for institutional stability. While insured patients receive high-quality care, after treatment, the prevailing settlement process often requires upfront payment by patients or families. Post-treatment, hospitals reconcile expenses, the portion covered by insurance is then claimed from the Medical Insurance Center (MIC). This process involves sequential stages-claim submission, auditing, and reimbursement. Once each of the stages delays, hospital cash flow is consequently impaired, potentially hindering normal operations5. As enrollment grows, reaching approximately 1.334 billion in China by the end of 2023, the proportion of hospital revenue dependent on insurance settlements rises steadily. Thus, the negative impact of reimbursement lag on hospital finances and operations is consequently intensifying6.

The era of universal coverage underscores the critical role of medical insurance in safeguarding public health needs, reducing cost burdens, and improving population health7. Strengthening medical insurance fund management and enhancing its utilization efficiency are therefore paramount. However, as China’s medical insurance reforms deepen and coverage expands, fund management faces mounting pressure8. A significant challenge is the proliferation of medical insurance chargebacks and administrative disputes. These issues not only damage institutional reputations and erode patient confidence in the settlement system, but also obstruct revenue collection. Disputes often arise between MICs and providers regarding the legitimacy of specific claims, sometimes escalating to judicial intervention. This ultimately disrupts operations within medical consortia and degrades patients’ experience9,10. Establishing an effective collaborative settlement mechanism involving MICs, consortium institutions, and patients is thus urgently needed to ensure rational fund use, reduce claim denials, and improve efficiency11,12.

Emerging technologies like Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Smart Contract (SC) offer promising solutions by enhancing trust and informatization in healthcare, including insurance settlement13,14. To address these challenges, this paper proposes an innovative blockchain-based medical insurance settlement collaboration mechanism integrating the Isolation Forest algorithm and Fleiss Kappa smart contracts mediated by an anonymous medical expert panel. Leveraging secure medical data sharing, the mechanism establishes triple consistency through technical management: blockchain ledger consensus (DPoS), expert panel evaluation consensus (Kappa), and item-specific outlier ratio consensus (r), enabling relatively accurate and fair assessment of disputed medical expenses. Isolation forest algorithm

The Isolation Forest Algorithm is an efficient anomaly detection method implemented based on ensemble learning. In DRG/DIP payment scenarios, cost anomalies (such as inflated settlements and panel biases) and disease data anomalies (such as mismatched diagnosis and treatment costs) are core management pain points, which are highly compatible with the algorithm characteristics of Isolation Forest. In addition, DRG/DIP involves massive medical data, and the linear time complexity (O(n)) and high precision of Isolation Forest enable rapid processing. They can discover data with different patterns from other data in the massive data to meet the real-time monitoring needs of medical insurance, and are SOTA algorithms that meet the requirements of big data processing.

Unlike density or distance based algorithms, the IF algorithm directly characterizes the degree of isolation between data. Therefore, this algorithm is simple, efficient, and widely used in the industrial sector. Fleiss kappa

Fleiss kappa (denoted as K) quantifies inter-rater reliability. Typically, its value ranges from 0 to 1. The closer it is to 1, the higher the degree of consistency. Therefore, in order to ensure the accuracy of the evaluation of whether medical expenses belong to abnormal expenses by the medical expert panel, the measurement of Kappa coefficient is introduced.

When the annotation results are dominated by randomness, it indicates that the annotation results have no consistency at all (the observed consistency is caused by randomness). Of course, it is also possible that annotators tend to give completely inconsistent annotation results. The variations of Kappa revolve around different calculation methods for and. Cohen’s Kappa can only be used to evaluate the consistency between two annotators. Scott’s Pi uses different strategies to calculate randomness compared to Cohen’s Kappa, while Fleiss Kappa generalizes based on Scott’s Pi to evaluate consistency for any fixed number of annotators. Due to the involvement of multiple individuals in the medical expert panel and their focus on binary classification problems involving multiple projects. Therefore, Fleiss Kappa was used to quantify the consistency of multiple doctors’ evaluations of multiple medical expense items, reducing the influence of personal bias and improving the objectivity and fairness of decision-making.

DPoS consensus

DPoS (Delegated Proof of Stake) is an innovative blockchain consensus algorithm designed to enhance network efficiency, scalability, and energy efficiency through a system of elected representatives. It enables all token holders to participate in voting, selecting a panel of trusted witnesses responsible for generating and validating new blocks. This approach addresses the high energy consumption and slow confirmation times of traditional Proof of Work (PoW) while introducing a democratic governance structure to ensure security and transparency. In DPoS, a voter’s weight is proportional to their token holdings, ensuring fair resource allocation. Elected witnesses produce blocks in turns at scheduled intervals, packaging pending transactions and broadcasting them to the network. Other witnesses then verify these blocks to achieve consensus. Once validated by a majority, a block is added to the blockchain. Witnesses are typically rewarded through newly minted tokens or transaction fees, incentivizing active participation. A key feature of DPoS is its adaptability. Token holders may change their votes dynamically, enabling rapid replacement of underperforming or malicious witnesses. Some implementations also allow witnesses to adjust parameters such as block size and block time, helping the network adapt to changing demands and maintain operational efficiency. Among its advantages, DPoS is highly energy-efficient, as it eliminates computationally intensive puzzle-solving. With a limited and known number of witnesses, transactions can be confirmed rapidly, improving throughput. The system also supports decentralized governance, allowing token holders to influence network direction through voting while preserving censorship resistance. However, DPoS faces certain challenges. Although designed to prevent centralization, influence can still concentrate among a small panel of large witnesses, posing centralization risks. The mechanism also relies heavily on active community participation, and low voter turnout may weaken security and reliability. Furthermore, while rare in practice, collusion among a majority of witnesses could theoretically compromise the network, a security concern that must be guarded against. In summary, DPoS is a consensus algorithm aimed at improving blockchain performance and encouraging broad community engagement. It has been adopted by notable projects such as BitShares, Steem, and EOS, yet continues to evolve as developers address its limitations and explore further enhancements.

Within each medical context, differences arise in specific diagnostic and treatment plans, methodologies, and measures, depending on factors such as the unique characteristics of the patient and advancements in medical technology. The core issue in evaluating whether institutional medical conduct poses risks to fund safety lies in assessing the reasonableness of the medical practice9. The healthcare fund supervision department is an administrative body responsible for managing the income and expenditure of healthcare funds. Its oversight encompasses medical practices as well as domains like pricing and charging, which also exhibit highly specialized characteristics15. The two entities (medical practice and fund supervision) are closely interconnected. However, their functions differ significantly, and the areas they are responsible for are largely distinct. Healthcare fraud poses significant financial losses to healthcare systems and insurance providers, making fraud detection technologies critically important16–20. In recent years, researchers have proposed various innovative approaches to address this challenge, demonstrating a clear evolutionary trend in technological sophistication and detection efficiency. Comparion with existing methods about Fraud detection.

Matloob et al. put forward a sequential mining architecture that relied on historical transaction patterns to generate rule engines but required regular updates to adapt to new fraud patterns, limiting flexibility21. Besides, their research did not integrate blockchain, smart contracts, On-Chain & Off-Chain Architecture or human–machine collaboration. Yoo et al. primarily explored graph analysis and machine learning models to enhance medical insurance fraud detection. Through comparative analysis of algorithms-including Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) and traditional models, they found that machine learning models incorporating graph centrality features achieved superior fraud detection performance22. However, their methodology omitted blockchain technology, smart contracts, and On-Chain & Off-Chain collaboration. Regarding human–machine synergy, the AI models embodied a collaborative mechanism where human expertise and automated systems jointly achieved research goals. Overall, this study highlighted the critical role of advanced AI algorithms in medical fraud detection. Hamid Z et al. proposed a method combining association rule mining with unsupervised learning to detect medical insurance fraud, showing moderate effectiveness23. They also valued the utility of data mining in assisting medical insuranse fraud detection. However, it addressed neither blockchain nor smart contracts, and limitations in dataset diversity and temporal analysis constrained its practical utility.

Islayem et al.’s study shared key traits with ours, including AI algorithms, blockchain integration, smart contracts, On-Chain & Off-Chain architecture, and human–machine collaboration. However, the technical designs differed. They proposed an Ethereum-based health insurance fraud detection system using large language models (LLMs), which innovated in AI algorithms and architecture but faced limitations: the “black-box” nature of LLMs caused poor interpretability, making results unsuitable for medical arbitration. Additionally, the system ignored underlying blockchain performance bottlenecks24. While detection speed was rapid, it failed to account for high-concurrency latency in deployment. Human-AI collaboration was limited to unidirectional LLM chatbot queries, lacking a closed-loop dispute mechanism.

In contrast, our study constructed a lightweight fraud detection and dynamic arbitration framework using the Isolation Forest algorithm (unsupervised learning) and Fleiss Kappa smart contracts. The algorithm directly processed high-dimensional data, eliminating LLM dependencies and black-box issues to enhance interpretability. Integrating Delegated Proof-of-Stake (DPoS) consensus with batch-processing contracts achieved high throughput (229.64 TPS) and reduced single-transaction cost by 3059 gwei. An innovative dynamic arbitration layer quantified expert consensus via the Fleiss Kappa model, forming a closed-loop workflow from machine preliminary screening to expert adjudication. On-chain DPoS storage ensured security, while off-chain arbitration handled computation, supporting end-to-end efficiency. Collectively, this study demonstrated advantages over existing research in data transparency, interpretability, cost efficiency, throughput, and multi-party collaboration, advancing medical fraud detection from accuracy-focused approaches toward collaborative problem-solving.

Comprehensive comparative analysis

The comprehensive results collectively validated three-phase anomaly detection pattern of Isolation Forest algorithm.

Exploration Phase: During 100–300 samples, Accuracy-dominated with low Recall (0.2–0.5 F1), identifying core normal patterns.

Oscillation Phase: During 400–900 samples, Recall peaked (nearly 1.0) while Precision dropped sharply, causing systemic decline of F1 (0.57–0.65). This reflected the precision-recall trade-off under fixed thresholds.

Convergence Phase: While reaching 1,000 samples, Full metric recovery with stable F1 (0.83–0.87), achieving dynamic Precision-Recall balance.

Experiments confirmed that Isolation Forest reaches usable performance at 400 samples and stabilizes at 1,000 samples. Early F1 superiority of Hospital 1 suggested more centralized expense structures where anomalies were captured via shorter isolation paths. Metric convergence at 1,000 samples occurred despite distributional differences, aligning with the core mechanism of Isolation Forest algorithm, namely anomaly density quantification through path lengths. The results demonstrated statistical validity for unsupervised anomaly detection and indicated potential engineering applicability in the analysis of healthcare cost.

Blockchain simulation experiment

Consensus mechanism testing

In the cooperative mechanism for medical insurance settlement based on alliance chain in this article, there are multiple nodes including medical insurance centers, banks, medical consortium hospitals at all levels, insured patients, and anonymous medical expert panels. After each node publishes transaction information in the medical insurance center, the transaction undergoes verification, and blocks are generated once management nodes reach consensus. The consensus layer of the medical insurance settlement system designed in the article adopts the Delegated Proof of Stake (DPoS) algorithm. The impact of the consensus mechanism on the time of transaction packaging and block generation is an important aspect of measuring the performance of the medical insurance collaborative settlement system based on the consortium chain. Therefore, the experiment simulated and tested the consensus mechanism of the proposed medical insurance settlement system. Since the consensus algorithm determines how nodes agree on transaction order and state, its efficiency directly affects overall system performance.

To evaluate the performance of the DPoS (Proof of Stake) consensus mechanism, PyCharm was used as the development tool and Ganache was used to simulate the Ethereum distributed network. Through this method, smart contracts could be quickly deployed in the local environment and their functionality could be tested. In this experiment, the core logic of the DPoS consensus mechanism was implemented and validated for MO (Medical Order Form), ER (Examination Report Form), and EI (Expense List). The experimental setup included multiple roles such as medical insurance centers, banks, medical consortium hospitals at all levels, insured patients, and anonymous medical expert panels, with a total of 100 nodes participating.

According to the DPoS rules, 21 witness nodes were elected from these nodes to be responsible for the creation and confirmation of blocks. Every 15 transactions were defined as a batch, and when reaching this threshold, a new block was packaged by the witness node of the current round. According to the experimental results, as shown in Fig. 9, the total time required to generate 60 blocks was 38.10 s, during which a total of 900 transactions were processed. Specifically regarding the generation time of each block, the maximum confirmation time observed was 0.7928 s (block 16), while The minimum confirmation time was 0.5087 s (block 31). This fluctuation in block generation time stems from two aspects: network latency and the witness election process. As the experiment was conducted in a simulated environment, although external factors were minimized as much as possible, slight network latency persisted. In addition, the dynamic witness election in DPoS may introduce brief adjustment periods after new witnesses were selected, occasionally prolonging block generation.

Time cost of block generation under DPoS consensus mechanism.

In summary, the experimental results illustrated that the DPoS mechanism with 100 nodes and 21 witnesses handled large-scale transactions effectively while maintaining a stable block generation rate. However, it also should be noted that observed time variations reflect real-world operational scenarios. Future research could further optimize network configuration or consensus algorithms to reduce such delays.

Conclusion

This study proposed a novel blockchain-based collaborative mechanism integrating the Isolation Forest algorithm and Fleiss Kappa smart contracts mediated by anonymous medical expert panel (AMEP) to address persistent challenges in medical insurance settlement. By screening medical expenses through Isolation Forest, establishing triple consistency constraints (blockchain ledger consensus, expert evaluation consensus, and item-specific outlier ratio consensus), and implementing automated settlement decisions via smart contracts, the mechanism effectively eliminated medical knowledge barriers between insurance administrators and healthcare providers. This integrated approach enhanced the accuracy of abnormal cost identification, accelerated dispute resolution, and improved settlement efficiency, ultimately strengthening fund supervision while optimizing cash flow for medical institutions at all tiers. Through our researching, we found Isolation Forest algorithm is feasible when applying in screening medical expenses. The Dpos consensus proved effective in transaction blocking, generating 60 blocks in 38.10 s, during which a total of 900 transactions were processed. Moreover, smart contract batch processing achieved a peak throughput of 229.64 transactions per second (TPS) and reduced transaction costs by up to 3,095 gwei. The framework demonstrated significant potential for improving the collaboration of stakeholders in the insurance system and supporting sustainable medical consortium operations.

However, there are still several limitations that warrant consideration. Firstly, the experimental validation relied on the datasets only for learning and research from hospitals of the same region, which may constrain the generalizability of findings in real-time deployment environment across diverse healthcare settings and regional payment policies. Secondly, the mechanism was tested in simulated blockchain environments rather than real-world implementations, leaving open questions about its performance under operational constraints such as cross-institutional data interoperability, expert panel coordination challenges, and integration with legacy healthcare systems. Thirdly, while the proposed triple-consistency framework enhanced objectivity, its dependency on predefined thresholds (Kappa > 0.6, r-value) required further validation and adjustment against clinical and financial ground truths. Finally, scalability and deployment challenges were also met with by our research while applying in the real-world. Medical Data Hashing for Chain Storage ensured traceability and tamper resistance, but required promoting standardized data interfaces to achieve compatibility with heterogeneous hospital systems. AMEP’s anonymity mechanism reduced decision-making pressure on experts, while necessitating the establishment of incentive mechanisms (e.g., token rewards) to guarantee review participation. Smart contract rules must incorporate pre-configured threshold elasticity, enabling adaptive configuration to accommodate regional disparities in medical insurance policies. Our future research will focus on multi-center trials with expanded real and updated datasets, develop implementation frameworks for real-world deployment, and explore AI augmentation of expert panels to improve scalability. These efforts aim to enhance the mechanism’s practical applicability in evolving healthcare ecosystems.
