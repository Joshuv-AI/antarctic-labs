Executive Summary
The electrification of transportation demands smarter planning tools that can precisely
guide infrastructure investment and operational strategy. This project developed and
demonstrated a high-performance optimization framework tailored for transit bus fleet
electrification. Built around detailed system modeling and mathematical optimization,
the framework enables agencies to quickly identify the most cost-effective infrastructure
and vehicle configuration solutions. This streamlines the electrification process while
ensuring the most effective use of limited investment resources.

Applied to a real-world transit system in Southern California, the framework revealed
dramatic cost savings: up to 79% reduction in infrastructure investment and 25%
reduction in total electrification costs compared to conventional planning approaches. It
also identified optimal locations and specifications for charging infrastructure, electric
bus deployment, and energy management. These outcomes are unlikely to be achieved
through heuristic or ad hoc planning approaches.

The transformative value of this work lies in its scalability and adaptability. The
framework is designed to be repeatable across regions through the use of standardized
data inputs and is flexible enough to support electrification planning across multiple
transportation modes, including buses and delivery fleets. With further development,
this toolset has the potential to serve as a widely accessible platform for transit
agencies and fleet operators aiming to accelerate electrification while minimizing cost
and operational risk. This project marks a significant step toward data-driven, system-
level electrification planning and lays the foundation for a market-ready product with
broad national relevance.

 I. Background
Electrifying transit bus fleets is a key strategy for improving air quality and long-term
operating costs. Switching from diesel or compressed natural gas (CNG) to battery-
electric buses eliminates tailpipe emissions and improves public health1. Policies such
as California’s Innovative Clean Transit regulation, which mandates zero-emission fleets
by 2040, reflect this growing priority.
However, fleet electrification poses technical and operational challenges. Battery
electric buses require significant capital investment in vehicles, charging infrastructure,
and utility upgrades, while also introducing range limitations and charging downtimes
that complicate scheduling. Planning efforts are often heuristic, lacking comprehensive
tools to optimize costs, emissions, and infrastructure.

Recent research has addressed parts of this challenge. Optimization models minimize
life-cycle costs by jointly planning vehicle deployment, charging infrastructure, and
schedules 2. Others introduce multi-objective frameworks balancing cost and
emissions3. Yet, few studies integrate detailed life-cycle emissions and health impacts
into system-level planning, and none use mixed-integer linear programming to
simultaneously optimize phased transitions, costs, and air quality. Mixed-integer linear
programming enables exact, system-level optimization over complex decision variables
and constraints, making it well-suited for capturing the multi-objective tradeoffs in transit
electrification.
This study addresses these gaps by developing a mixed-integer linear programming
based, multi-objective optimization framework that integrates fleet management,
infrastructure planning, life-cycle costs, and air quality impacts. The model is applied to
OmniTrans in San Bernardino, California, to demonstrate the benefits of
comprehensive, system-level planning over conventional heuristics.

II. Project objectives
The overarching goal of this project is to develop a suite of tools that support cost-
effective electrification strategies for all modes of transport and initially apply them to the
electrification of transit agencies. These tools serve the dual purpose of (a) reducing the
planning costs of electrification by circumventing the need for individual entities to
develop bespoke planning tools and (b) identifying sophisticated solutions to
consolidate electrification infrastructure deployment and reduce operation costs through
system-level optimization.

The technical approach integrates data and modeling to provide a tractable yet
comprehensive system characterization spanning transportation, energy, and human
health dimensions. The tool suite defines and explores a multidimensional solution

 6

space of feasible electrification configurations, guided by constraints and cost
parameters:

 • Vehicle requirements and performance: This includes modeling vehicle
 movement needs (e.g., bus routes and schedules), energy system constraints
 (battery capacity, charging rates), and life cycle costs (monetary and air quality).
 The framework prioritizes electrification opportunities that deliver the greatest
 benefit with the least upfront investment.
 • Infrastructure design and utilization: Charging-related costs account for
 infrastructure installation (including procurement and grid integration costs),
 electricity delivery (capacity and usage-based costs), and life cycle impacts (air
 quality). Optimal solutions maximize infrastructure utilization, improving the cost-
 effectiveness of capital investments.

The tools are designed for multi-objective optimization and can be tailored to
stakeholder priorities, such as minimizing the net present system cost across capital
and operational costs.
In Phase I, the project focused on establishing the optimization framework and
validating its performance through a case study. Four primary objectives were defined,
each including specific milestones (Table 1). The project completed all milestones on
schedule.

III. Project methods
This project developed an integrated modeling and optimization framework to plan cost-
effective transit fleet electrification strategies. The methodology combines operational
data, fleet composition, energy consumption modeling, and cost structures into a unified
simulation and optimization workflow, as illustrated in Figure 1. Starting with transit
operating requirements and fleet configurations (electric and diesel buses), the model
identifies feasible charging opportunities and simulates service-block level energy
consumption. These inputs feed into a detailed charging systems simulation that defines
charger placement, battery sizing, and electric service-block assignments. A mixed-
integer linear programming optimization solver then minimizes total system costs. The
output is an optimized electrification plan that finds the most cost-efficient system that
meets all performance requirements.

 a. Bus energy consumption modeling
To support infrastructure deployment and usage modeling, essential data were collected
and modeled as part of the project’s foundational analysis. Energy consumption was
modeled for electric, compressed natural gas (CNG) (3.48 miles per gallon diesel
equivalent (mpdge)4), and diesel (4.33 mpdge4) buses. For electric buses in particular,
high-resolution energy consumption was simulated on a per-route basis using the
Future Automotive Systems Technology Simulator (FASTSim) framework 5. Road grade

 9

information for each route was derived from the U.S. Geological Survey’s digital
elevation model 6, and additional energy expenditures from battery preconditioning 7–9
and HVAC (heating, ventilation, and air conditioning) systems were estimated using
historical temperature data from San Bernardino, California 10,11.
To project charging needs for both buses and passenger vehicles, vehicle flow and
energy demand were also modeled. For buses, General Transit Feed Specification
(GTFS) data were acquired from OmniTrans 12, the local transit agency, which provides
detailed schedules that formed the foundation of the usage model. The modeled
adoption of electric buses aligns with OmniTrans' Zero Emission Rollout Plan, which
outlines their transition to a fully electric fleet by 2040 13, as shown in Figure 2.

Figure 2. Transition to electric buses by OmniTrans in San Bernardino, California.
 b. Car charging demand
In an effort to consolidate charging infrastructure for electrified transportation, this
project modeled the shared (multi-modal) usage of bus charging infrastructure with
passenger vehicles. Modeling charger utilization for passenger vehicles in 2040
revealed that projected energy demand significantly exceeds the current available
charging infrastructure. Based on travel data derived for San Bernardino 14–16, an 18%
travel growth projection 17, anticipated public charging share (5%) 18, vehicle energy
efficiency 19, and California’s zero-emission vehicle sales mandates 20, daily energy
demand is projected to reach 10.1 gigawatt-hours (GWh). In contrast, existing public
infrastructure, based on current charger deployment reported in the Alternative Fuels
Data Center (AFDC) 21 and additional bus charging capacity from this analysis, would
only support approximately 3.3 GWh/day at full utilization. As such, utilization is highly
dependent on targeted policies and planned charger deployment. Based on California’s
planned daily usage patterns, 150-kW chargers are expected to operate at 10%

 10

utilization (360 kWh/day), while 350-kW chargers are projected at 7% utilization (588
kWh/day) 22. Only 150-kW and 350-kW chargers were considered viable for shared use
between buses and passenger vehicles, since their power cabinets can be shared and
only require the addition of charger pedestals and associated cabling to accommodate
passenger vehicle charging. In contrast, Level 2 and 50-kW DC fast chargers have
integrated cabinets and therefore necessitate full procurement and installation costs for
each individual charger.

 e. Life cycle assessment
The life cycle assessment evaluated the human health impacts from fine Particulate
Matter (PM2.5) and quantified Carbon Dioxide equivalent (CO2e) emissions. CO2e
emissions were calculated using a cradle-to-grave system boundary, while PM2.5
emissions were assessed for upstream emissions from electricity generation and
emissions during vehicle operation.
Human health impact modeling was conducted using the Intervention Model for Air
Pollution (InMAP), which estimates changes in pollutant concentrations and resulting
health impacts from marginal emission reductions, such as replacing ICEVs with electric
buses 55. The model was configured to reflect a Valuation of Statistical Life of $13.2
million, in accordance with guidance from the U.S. Department of Transportation 56.
Population and baseline mortality rates were scaled to projected 2040 levels 57,58, and
the health impact assumed a 6% increase in mortality for every 10 micro-grams per
meter cubed increase in PM2.5 concentrations59.

Emissions modeled in the analysis included volatile organic compounds (VOCs),
nitrogen oxides (NOₓ), ammonia (NH3), sulfur oxides (SOₓ), and primary PM2.5.
Emissions from electricity generation used for charging were derived from power plant
data in the National Emissions Inventory60 and scaled using generation totals from the
Emissions and Generation Resource Integrated Database (eGrid)61. For San
Bernardino, the only particulate matter-emitting sources in grid electricity were natural

 13

gas combined cycle and combustion turbine power plants. The time-of-day share of
electricity supplied by natural gas was based on the 2040 mid-case grid mix projections
from the Cambium 2023 dataset62. The time-of-day impact of charging health damages
was shown to be minimal ranging from $0.0005 to $0.002 per kWh.
Differences in brake and tire wear emissions among electric, CNG, and diesel buses
were assumed to be negligible and thus modeled as equivalent. Tailpipe emissions for
CNG and diesel vehicles were sourced from the literature63–65. The analysis showed
that switching to a fully electric fleet in San Bernardino would yield major reductions in
human health damages. Specifically, replacing CNG buses with electric buses is
estimated to reduce health damages by approximately $685 thousand per year, while
switching from diesel buses results in an estimated annual reduction of $2.03 million.
On a per-route (service-block) basis, the estimated annual health benefit of
electrification ranged from $237 to $9,600 for CNG replacement and from $741 to
$29,423 for diesel replacement. The spatial reduction to human health damages are
illustrated in Figure 4 for CNG and Figure 5 for diesel below.

 f. Optimization framework
A modeling framework was developed in Python to enable multi-objective optimization
of fleet electrification using mixed-integer linear programming. A preprocessing code
was created to import and transform GTFS data into a standardized, discrete-time
format compatible with the optimization framework. This transformation allowed the
model to be broadly applicable across transit agencies, using spatially and temporally
resolved GTFS data for bus driving and stop activity. In the framework, stop events of at
least 15-minutes were treated as potential charging opportunities, while driving
segments represented specific energy consumptions or energy recoveries from
regenerative braking.

The objective of the optimization algorithm was to minimize total system costs including
financial and air quality (PM2.5 and CO2e) costs while satisfying all operational and
physical constraints. Using vehicle activity, grid capacity, and cost parameters, the
model solved for a vector of decision variables, which is summarized in Table 6.

The elements from Table 6 and Table 7 were arranged into a decision variable vector
(x), a constraint matrix (A), a constraint vector (b), and a cost vector (c) to formulate the
optimization problem for mixed-integer linear programming as shown in equation (1):

(1) minimize cᵀx subject to Ax ≤ b, xⱼ ∈ ℤ ∀j ∈ I, xⱼ ∈ ℝ ∀j ∉ I

Where x ∈ ℝⁿ was the vector of decision variables, with a subset I ⊆ {1, ..., n}
constrained to be integers; c ∈ ℝⁿ is the cost coefficient vector; and A ∈ ℝᵐˣⁿ, b ∈ ℝᵐ
define the linear inequality constraints. This formulation captures both discrete and
continuous decisions under linear cost and constraint structures.

The developed optimization framework supports repeatable, rapid system planning and
is scalable to any size fleets (>1000 vehicles), including non-bus vehicle types
(commercial trucks, cars, etc.). The software’s performance was demonstrated through
the case study of San Bernardino, California.

IV. Project results
The optimization framework and supporting data were applied to demonstrate the
optimization model using OmniTrans’ transit system as a case study in San Bernardino,
California. The optimal electrification plan was developed using GTFS data from the day
with the highest projected energy demand, ensuring sufficient charging capacity across
all days of the year. The cost of transitioning an ICEV fleet was modeled separately for
both CNG and diesel scenarios and included a 7% discount rate. The baseline
optimization scenario focused on transitioning from diesel to electric buses and did not
include passenger vehicle charging in the analysis.

 a. Aggregated charging infrastructure comparison
OmniTrans published their Zero Emission Bus Rollout Plan, which we modeled as a
benchmark to demonstrate the value of our optimal plan compared to the existing plan

 18

13. The existing plan for San Bernardino includes installing 150-kW chargers at both the

East and West Valley bus depots. Each charger will have two plugs, with one plug
allocated per bus 13. A comparison of our optimal plan and the existing plan under full
fleet electrification for the number of EVSE, EVSE capital costs, and charging capacity
is shown in Figure 6.

Figure 6. Comparison of existing plan and Optimized Kinetics plan for charging
deployment including a) charger count, b) procurement and installation cost, and
c) total charging capacity. Abbreviations: kilowatt (kW), megawatt (MW).
As shown in Figure 6, the Optimized Kinetics plan right-sizes the system to minimize
total system cost by strategically reducing the number of chargers and selecting a mix
of EVSE power levels that meet the exact operational needs of the bus fleet. In
comparison to the existing plan, this approach reduces EVSE procurement and
installation costs by $8 million and decreases total charging capacity and grid impact by
66%.

Bernardino’s planned replacement schedule for ICEVs with electric buses. The

transition from 2025 to 2040 was allowing the optimization framework to determine

efficiency of electric versus diesel buses on specific routes, the battery capacity
required, the siting of charging infrastructure, and the operational schedule. Based on
the number of diesel and electric buses planned each year, the optimal service blocks

Although the EVSE cost savings are shared equally between buses and passenger

approximately $0.13 per mile, compared to $0.15 per mile without the shared
infrastructure. For context, this cost is lower than the projected 2040 gasoline fuel cost
of $4.85 per gallon for ICEVs 28, which is $0.19 per mile based on a fuel economy of

V. Discussion
Throughout this project, all milestones associated with each objective were successfully
achieved. Moreover, the optimization framework developed through this STTR effort
demonstrates a compelling value proposition for commercialization. It offers significant
cost savings for fleet electrification and introduces a scalable, rapidly repeatable
planning approach with the potential for broad impact.

This study has shown that realizing the air quality and cost benefits from electric buses
require strategic planning that goes beyond simple vehicle replacement. This study
demonstrates how a system-level, multi-objective optimization framework that
incorporates financial costs, air quality impacts, and infrastructure constraints, can
meaningfully enhance the cost-effectiveness of transit fleet electrification.

A central finding of this work is the substantial cost savings achieved through optimized
electrification planning relative to the baseline heuristic plan. The optimized plan
reduced capital investment in charging infrastructure by 79%, cut total costs by 25%,

 28

and delivered $2.24 in air quality benefits per dollar of electrification premium. These
savings were enabled by detailed modeling of service blocks, battery sizing, grid
constraints, and distributed energy resources, such as photovoltaic systems with battery
storage. The result is a configuration that meets operational needs while minimizing
excess infrastructure investment.

The analysis also revealed that en-route charging and distributed energy resource
integration are key strategies for managing energy costs and grid demand. By aligning
high-power charging with photovoltaic generation during daylight hours, the system
reduces reliance on costly utility electricity and flattens peak grid loads. Although depot-
only charging offers simpler implementation, it leads to higher capital and energy costs.
These findings suggest that agencies willing to invest in more complex but strategically
located infrastructure can achieve significant long-term savings.
In addition, a scenario analyzing the shared use of EVSE infrastructure between buses
and passenger vehicles showed modest cost reductions for the transit agency, but
relatively greater benefits for passenger car operators. While these savings may not be
enough to independently justify adoption, they point to opportunities for cost-sharing
and enhanced infrastructure utilization, especially if supported by policy incentives or
public-private partnerships.

Notably, the study's total cost of ownership analysis for new fleet procurement,
independent of existing vehicle assets, reinforced the advantage of electric buses long-
term. When comparing CNG, diesel, and electric buses, the optimized electric bus
configuration yielded the lowest total cost per mile.

These results support growing evidence that system-level planning, rather than
incremental or heuristic decision-making, is essential to maximize the benefits of limited
transit electrification investment dollars. Importantly, this work contributes to the broader
literature by integrating life cycle air quality damages into the optimization framework, a
feature often missing in prior cost-based planning models. This provides a more holistic
view of societal impacts and helps align fleet decisions with public health goals.

VIII. Impact
 a. Impact on the development of the principal discipline of the project
  This project has advanced the field of electrified transportation by introducing a
  practical, data-driven framework for transitioning transit fleets from fossil fuel-powered
  buses to electric. It moves beyond the limitations of traditional electric bus pilot
  programs or rigid bus-to-charger ratios. The research team developed a suite of
  modeling tools that evaluate both financial costs and air quality impacts across a wide
  range of electrification strategies. Unlike conventional approaches that often overspend
  on charging infrastructure or overlook the total cost of ownership, the system-optimized
  method identifies the most cost-effective combination of five key elements: charging
  station locations and capacity, charging schedules, distributed energy resource sizing
  and operation, bus battery capacity, and fuel type selection for each bus route. By
  optimizing these elements together, the model achieves significant reductions in total
  system costs.

  More broadly, this work contributes essential knowledge on how to scale up transit
  electrification in a way that is technically rigorous and financially responsible. It marks a
  shift away from business-as-usual, where diesel use continues by default or electric
  buses are added without a comprehensive plan. Instead, the project offers a fully
  integrated strategy that balances operational needs with cost and air quality
  considerations. The tools also support charger sharing between buses and passenger
  vehicles, extending the value of charging infrastructure investments. Importantly, the

 31

approach is built on standardized, repeatable methods, creating a foundation for
expanding the planning model to other fleet types and transportation modes. This work
establishes system-level optimization as an effective approach for guiding the next
generation of electrification planning.
 b. Impact on society beyond science and technology
The project has the potential to deliver meaningful societal benefits by transforming how
transit agencies plan and implement fleet electrification. By incorporating a robust
modeling and optimization framework into the planning process, the project enables a
shift from ad hoc or arbitrary decision-making toward data-driven strategies that balance
economic efficiency, operational performance, and air quality outcomes. This approach
allows transit agencies to make more effective investments while enhancing public
health and service reliability. Optimized electrification planning also reduces overall
transit system costs, which may enable lower fares for riders or reduce dependence on
government subsidies. Optimized planning also helps ease grid demand, supporting
more stable and affordable electricity prices for the public.

Furthermore, electrification results in significant reductions in fine particulate matter,
directly improving air quality. In addition, electric buses generate less noise than their
diesel counterparts, enhancing the rider experience and reducing noise impacts on
surrounding neighborhoods. Collectively, these improvements contribute to a higher
quality of life, more efficient use of public resources, and improved public health.
