Poisson Gauge Theories in Three Dimensions: Exact Solutions and Conservation Laws


Abstract
We investigate Maxwell–Chern–Simons theory on a three-dimensional noncommutative spacetime endowed with a constant spacelike Poisson structure. By exploiting the residual rotational symmetry, we construct exact classical solutions corresponding to pointlike electric and magnetic charges. We demonstrate that noncommutativity acts as a natural regulator, ensuring a finite total electromagnetic energy and thereby resolving the classical self-energy divergence. Furthermore, some of these solutions exhibit a non-perturbative dependence on the noncommutativity parameter and allow for the generation of an arbitrary magnetic flux. We also present a noncommutative generalization of Gauss’s law, providing a robust framework for the physical interpretation of these exact solutions.

2
Poisson gauge theory

We study a three-dimensional gauge theory defined on the Minkowski spacetime

\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
equipped with Cartesian coordinates
x^{\mu}=(x^{0},x^{1},x^{2})
and
a metric of signature
(-++)
. The spacetime is endowed with a Poisson structure

\{x^{\mu},x^{\nu}\}=\theta^{\mu\nu},

(2.1)

where
\theta^{\mu\nu}
is a constant antisymmetric tensor. In three
dimensions,
\theta^{\mu\nu}
is necessarily degenerate and can be
parametrized as

\theta^{\mu\nu}=\epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda}\xi_{\lambda}

(2.2)

for a given vector
\xi^{\mu}
. Depending on the causal nature of
\xi^{\mu}
,
the resulting noncommutativity is space-like, time-like, or light-like. In this
work, we focus on time-like noncommutativity, assuming

\{x^{0},x^{i}\}=0,\qquad\{x^{i},x^{j}\}=g\,\epsilon^{ij},\qquad i,j=1,2\,,

(2.3)

where the parameter
g
has the dimension of length squared and controls the scale of noncommutative deformation and
\epsilon^{ij}\epsilon_{kj}=\delta^{i}_{k}
.

The gauge field is a one-form
A=A_{\mu}(x)\,dx^{\mu}
on
\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
. Infinitesimal gauge
transformations with parameter
\varepsilon(x)
are postulated in the form

\delta_{\varepsilon}A_{\mu}=D_{\mu}\varepsilon:=\partial_{\mu}\varepsilon+\{A_{\mu},\varepsilon\}\,.

(2.4)

The commutator of two transformations closes according to

[\delta_{\varepsilon_{1}},\delta_{\varepsilon_{2}}]=\delta_{\{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\}},

(2.5)

implying that the underlying gauge algebra is non-Abelian. The field-strength tensor is defined as

F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}+\{A_{\mu},A_{\nu}\}\,,

(2.6)

and satisfies the standard Bianchi identity

D_{\lambda}F_{\mu\nu}+D_{\mu}F_{\nu\lambda}+D_{\nu}F_{\lambda\mu}=0

(2.7)

with respect to the covariant derivative defined in (
2.4
). Under the gauge transformations (
2.4
) the field strength transforms in the adjoint
representation:

\delta_{\varepsilon}F_{\mu\nu}=\{F_{\mu\nu},\varepsilon\}\,.

(2.8)

Following the pattern of Yang–Mills theory, it is straightforward to define a Poisson analogue for Maxwell–Chern–Simons theory.
This is described by the action

{S_{MCS}=\int d^{3}x\left[-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda}\left(A_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}A_{\lambda}+\frac{1}{3}A_{\mu}\{A_{\nu},A_{\lambda}\}\right)\right]\,,}

(2.9)

which is gauge invariant up to a
boundary term. Throughout this work, indices are raised and lowered using the Minkowski metric
\eta_{\mu\nu}
. By definition, the gauge field

A_{\mu}
has the dimension of inverse length; consequently, both the Chern–Simons level
\kappa
and the action
S_{MCS}
share this dimension.
Varying the action yields the field equations

D_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu}+\kappa F^{\nu}=0,\qquad F^{\nu}:=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\nu\mu\lambda}F_{\mu\lambda}.

(2.10)

The Maxwell term provides propagating degrees of freedom, while the
Chern–Simons term induces a topological mass
[
21
,
22
]
.

The translation invariance of the action (
2.9
) implies the conservation of energy and momentum. While the canonical energy-momentum tensor can be derived using Noether’s theorem, we present here a more geometric derivation. First, consider the symmetric tensor

\Theta^{\mu\nu}=F^{\mu}F^{\nu}-\frac{1}{2}\eta^{\mu\nu}F_{\lambda}F^{\lambda}\,.

(2.11)

Computing its covariant divergence, we find

D_{\mu}\Theta^{\mu\nu}=F^{\nu}D_{\mu}F^{\mu}+F^{\mu}D_{\mu}F^{\nu}-F_{\lambda}D^{\nu}F^{\lambda}\,.

(2.12)

The first term on the right-hand side vanishes due to the Bianchi identity (
2.7
). The remaining two terms cancel on-shell, since

F^{\mu}D_{\mu}F^{\nu}-F_{\lambda}D^{\nu}F^{\lambda}=F_{\lambda}(D^{\lambda}F^{\nu}-D^{\nu}F^{\lambda})\approx\kappa F_{\lambda}F_{\mu}\epsilon^{\lambda\mu\nu}=0\,.

(2.13)

Therefore, we can write

D_{\mu}\Theta^{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}\Theta^{\mu\nu}+\{A_{\mu},\Theta^{\mu\nu}\}=\partial_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}\approx 0\,,

(2.14)

where

T^{\mu\nu}=\Theta^{\mu\nu}-\theta^{\mu\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}A_{\lambda}\Theta^{\lambda\nu}

(2.15)

is the desired energy-momentum tensor. Unlike in conventional gauge theory, the tensor
T^{\mu\nu}
is neither symmetric nor gauge invariant. Both properties are recovered in the commutative limit, where it reduces to the standard energy-momentum tensor of the electromagnetic field.
Note that for our choice of Poisson brackets (
2.3
), the energy-momentum density
T^{0\mu}=\Theta^{0\mu}
depends only on the field strength tensor
F_{\mu\nu}
, so that
\delta_{\varepsilon}T^{0\mu}=\{\varepsilon,T^{0\mu}\}
. As a result, the total energy-momentum of the field

\mathcal{P}^{\mu}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int(F^{0}F^{\mu}-\frac{1}{2}\eta^{0\mu}F^{2})d^{2}x

(2.16)

is a gauge-invariant vector quantity, provided that the gauge parameter
\varepsilon
or the fields vanish at infinity. In particular, the total energy is given by the positive-definite integral

\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{P}^{0}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int(F^{2}_{01}+F^{2}_{02}+F^{2}_{12})d^{2}x\,.

(2.17)

The asymmetry of
T^{\mu\nu}
cannot be removed by a Belinfante–Rosenfeld shift. In the presence of the fixed background tensor

\theta^{\mu\nu}
, Lorentz invariance is explicitly broken. A symmetric energy-momentum tensor would generate conserved Lorentz currents,

M^{\mu\nu|\lambda}=x^{\mu}T^{\nu\lambda}-x^{\nu}T^{\mu\lambda}\,,\qquad\partial_{\lambda}M^{\mu\nu|\lambda}=0\,,

(2.18)

contradicting this symmetry breaking. The asymmetry of

T^{\mu\nu}
is therefore structural and inherent to the theory.

Nevertheless, there remains a residual Lorentz symmetry corresponding to the little group of the vector
\xi_{\lambda}=\epsilon_{\lambda\mu\nu}\theta^{\mu\nu}
. It is generated by the Hamiltonian vector field

R=\{H,-\}=x_{\mu}\theta^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu}\,,\qquad H:=\frac{1}{2}\eta_{\mu\nu}x^{\mu}x^{\nu}\,,

(2.19)

and acts on
A_{\mu}
through the Lie derivative:

\mathcal{L}_{R}A_{\mu}=RA_{\mu}+\eta_{\mu\lambda}\theta^{\lambda\nu}A_{\nu}\,.

(2.20)

Since the Lagrangian density
L
of (
2.9
) is constructed in terms of the
R
-invariant tensors
\eta^{\mu\nu}
,
\epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda}
, and
\theta^{\mu\nu}
, we can write

RL=\frac{\delta S}{\delta A_{\mu}}\mathcal{L}_{R}A_{\mu}+\partial_{\nu}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial(\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu})}\mathcal{L}_{R}A_{\mu}\right)\,.

(2.21)

The last equation is equivalent to the conservation law
\partial_{\nu}{M}^{\nu}\approx 0
, where

{M}^{\nu}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial(\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu})}\mathcal{L}_{R}A_{\mu}+\theta^{\nu\mu}x_{\mu}L\,.

(2.22)

The spatial integral for the total angular momentum of the field

\mathcal{M}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int d^{2}x\,{M}^{0}

(2.23)

simplifies considerably for the Poisson brackets (
2.3
). We obtain

{M}^{0}=\Pi^{i}\mathcal{L}_{R}A_{i}\,,\qquad R=x_{i}\epsilon^{ij}\partial_{j}\,,

(2.24)

where

\Pi^{i}=F^{i0}+{\frac{1}{2}}\kappa\epsilon^{ij}A_{i}

(2.25)

are the momenta canonically conjugate to the fields
A_{i}
.

Gauge symmetry also gives rise to a
lower-degree conservation law

[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
. This stems from the global shift symmetry of the gauge
transformations (
2.4
),

\varepsilon\to\varepsilon+c\,,

(2.26)

where
c
is an arbitrary constant. Applying the general procedure of Refs.
[
25
]
,
[
26
]
yields an on-shell closed one-form
J=J_{\mu}dx^{\mu}
constructed from the gauge field
A_{\mu}
and its derivatives. In the case at hand, one can derive this one-form in a more direct way. The starting point is the Noether identity associated with the equations of motion:

D_{\nu}D_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu}-\kappa D_{\nu}F^{\nu}\equiv 0\,.

(2.27)

This is equivalent to
\partial_{\nu}B^{\nu}=0
, where the vector field

B^{\nu}=(D_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu}-\kappa F^{\nu})-\theta^{\nu\lambda}\partial_{\lambda}A_{\rho}(D_{\mu}F^{\mu\rho}-\kappa F^{\rho})

(2.28)

vanishes on shell.
Since the divergence of
B^{\nu}
is identically zero for arbitrary
A_{\mu}
, there exists a bivector field
J^{\mu\nu}=-J^{\nu\mu}
such that
B^{\mu}=\partial_{\nu}J^{\nu\mu}
. A direct verification confirms that the sum

J^{\mu\nu}=J^{\mu\nu}_{M}+\kappa J^{\mu\nu}_{CS}\,,

(2.29)

where

\begin{array}[]{rcl}J_{M}^{\mu\nu}&=&\displaystyle f^{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{4}\theta^{\mu\nu}f^{\alpha\beta}f_{\alpha\beta}-(\theta^{\alpha\mu}f^{\nu\beta}-\theta^{\alpha\nu}f^{\mu\beta})\partial_{\alpha}A_{\beta}\\[8.53581pt]
&+&\theta^{\mu\nu}A^{\beta}\partial^{\alpha}\{A_{\alpha},A_{\beta}\}+A^{\beta}\partial_{\alpha}(\theta^{\alpha\mu}\{A^{\nu},A_{\beta}\}-\theta^{\alpha\nu}\{A^{\mu},A_{\beta}\})+\theta^{\mu\rho}\theta^{\nu\lambda}\partial_{\rho}A^{\alpha}\partial_{\lambda}A^{\beta}f_{\alpha\beta}\\[8.53581pt]
&+&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big(\theta^{\mu\nu}\big(A^{\beta}\{A^{\alpha},\{A_{\alpha},A_{\beta}\}\}+\frac{1}{2}\{A,A\}^{2}\big)+(\theta^{\mu\rho}\theta^{\nu\lambda}-\theta^{\nu\rho}\theta^{\mu\lambda})A^{\beta}\partial_{\rho}A^{\alpha}\partial_{\lambda}\{A_{\alpha},A_{\beta}\}\Big)\end{array}

(2.30)

and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}J_{CS}^{\mu\nu}&=&\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\nu\mu\alpha}A_{\alpha}+(\varepsilon^{\alpha\beta\nu}\theta^{\mu\lambda}-\varepsilon^{\alpha\beta\mu}\theta^{\nu\lambda})A_{\alpha}\partial_{\lambda}A_{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}\theta^{\mu\nu}A^{\alpha}f_{\alpha}\\[8.53581pt]
&+&\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma}\big(\theta^{\mu\nu}A_{\alpha}\{A_{\beta},A_{\gamma}\}+2\theta^{\mu\lambda}\theta^{\nu\rho}A_{\alpha}\partial_{\rho}A_{\beta}\partial_{\lambda}A_{\gamma}\big)\,,\end{array}

(2.31)

possesses the desired property. Here, we have employed the notation

f_{\alpha}=2\varepsilon_{\alpha\mu\nu}\partial^{\mu}A^{\nu}\,,\quad f_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}\,,\quad\{A,A\}^{2}=\{A^{\mu},A^{\nu}\}\{A_{\mu},A_{\nu}\}\,.

(2.32)

Upon dualization, we obtain a one-form
J=\epsilon_{\mu\nu\lambda}J^{\mu\nu}dx^{\lambda}
, which is closed on the equations of motion.
The relation
dJ\approx 0
represents a Poisson generalization of Gauss’s law. Notice that the formula for
J_{M}
is applicable in any dimension. Neither of the bivectors above survives in conventional Maxwell or Chern–Simons theory with a non-Abelian gauge group. A lower-degree conservation law arises, however, in unimodular gravity
[
28
]
.

There is a considerable ambiguity in the definition of conserved bivector (
2.29
): one may add terms that either vanish on-shell or can be expressed as the divergence of a three-vector. However, this ambiguity does not affect the associated conserved charges.
In the case of pure Chern–Simons theory, for example, the on-shell closed one-form

J=\big(A_{\mu}+\frac{1}{2}\theta^{\nu\lambda}A_{\nu}\partial_{\mu}A_{\lambda}\big)dx^{\mu}

(2.33)

is as valid as the one given by (
2.31

dJ=\frac{1}{2}F_{\mu\nu}\omega^{\mu}_{\alpha}\bar{v}^{\nu}_{\beta}dx^{\alpha}\wedge dx^{\beta}\approx 0\,,

(2.34)

where

\omega^{\mu}_{\alpha}=\delta^{\mu}_{\alpha}+\theta^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\alpha}A_{\nu}\,,\qquad v^{\nu}_{\beta}=\delta^{\nu}_{\beta}+\partial_{\mu}A_{\beta}\theta^{\mu\nu}\,.

(2.35)

For
\theta
sufficiently small, the matrices
\omega
and
v
are invertible, and we denote
\bar{v}=v^{-1}
. The geometry of symplectic groupoids provides an alternative interpretation of this conservation law. Following
[
16
]
, the exact two-form
F^{s}=dJ
represents the
gauge covariant
field strength associated with
A
. For a recent discussion regarding the relationship between different field-strength tensors, see

[
29
]
.

3
Poisson–Chern–Simons theory

Let us start with a pure Chern–Simons theory. We seek
SO(2)
-invariant solutions of the flatness condition

F_{\mu\nu}=0\,.

(3.1)

The most general rotation-invariant ansatz for
A
is given by

A_{0}=A_{0}(t,\rho)\,,\qquad A_{i}=x_{i}\widetilde{\Psi}(t,\rho)+\varepsilon_{ij}x^{j}\widetilde{\Phi}(t,\rho)\,,

(3.2)

where
t=x^{0}
and
\rho=\frac{1}{2}(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})
; the functions
\widetilde{\Psi}
and
\widetilde{\Phi}
represent the polar and axial parts of the vector potential
A_{i}
. The gauge transformations (
2.4
) for the
A_{0}
,
\widetilde{\Psi}
, and
\widetilde{\Phi}
take the form

\delta_{\varepsilon}A_{0}=\partial_{t}\varepsilon\,,\qquad\delta_{\varepsilon}\widetilde{\Psi}=(1-g\widetilde{\Phi})\partial_{\rho}\varepsilon\,,\qquad\delta_{\varepsilon}\widetilde{\Phi}=g\widetilde{\Psi}\partial_{\rho}\varepsilon\,,

(3.3)

where the gauge parameter
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\rho,\,t)
.
Upon substituting (
3.2
) into the definition (
2.6
), we obtain the system of equations

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle F_{0i}=\frac{1}{g}\big(x_{i}(\partial_{t}\Psi-g\Phi\partial_{\rho}A_{0})-\varepsilon_{ij}x^{j}(\partial_{t}\Phi+g\Psi\partial_{\rho}A_{0})\big)=0\,,\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle F_{ij}=\frac{1}{g}\varepsilon_{ij}\partial_{\rho}\big(\rho(\Psi^{2}+\Phi^{2}-1)\big)=0\,,\end{array}

(3.4)

where we introduced the notation

\Psi=g\widetilde{\Psi}\,,\qquad\Phi=1-g\widetilde{\Phi}\,.

(3.5)

Integration of the last equation yields

\Psi^{2}+\Phi^{2}=1+\frac{c}{\rho}\,,

(3.6)

where
c=c(t)
is an arbitrary function of time. By parametrizing

\Psi=\displaystyle\sqrt{1+\frac{c}{\rho}}\,\sin{(g\varphi)}\,,\qquad\Phi=\displaystyle\sqrt{1+\frac{c}{\rho}}\,\cos{(g\varphi)}\,,

(3.7)

for arbitrary function
\varphi(t,\rho)
, and substituting these into the remaining two equations

\partial_{t}\Psi=g\Phi\partial_{\rho}A_{0}\,,\qquad\partial_{t}\Phi=-g\Psi\partial_{\rho}A_{0}\,,

(3.8)

we find

\partial_{t}c=0\,,\qquad\partial_{\rho}A_{0}=\partial_{t}\varphi\,.

(3.9)

Applying the gauge transformations (
3.3
) shows that the fields
\varphi
and
A_{0}
are purely gauge:

\delta_{\varepsilon}\varphi=\partial_{\rho}\varepsilon\,,\qquad\delta_{\varepsilon}A_{0}=\partial_{t}\varepsilon\,.

(3.10)

This allows us to impose the gauge fixing condition
A_{0}=\varphi=0
, leading to the solution

A_{0}=0\,,\qquad A_{i}=\frac{1}{g}\varepsilon_{ij}x^{j}\left(1-\sqrt{1+\frac{c}{\rho}}\right)\,.

(3.11)

All rotationally invariant solutions to the zero-curvature equation (
3.1
) are therefore gauge equivalent to stationary configurations labelled by a single constant
c
with dimension length squared. Since the only fundamental parameter with this dimension is the coupling constant
g
, it is natural to set
c=-2qg
for a dimensionless constant
q
.

The solution exhibits an expected singularity at the origin
\rho=0
, which can be interpreted as a localized source.
Indeed, in the commutative limit
g\to 0
, the solution (
3.11
) reduces to a flat
U(1)
connection

A^{\circ}=\lim_{g\to 0}A=q\,\frac{x_{1}dx_{2}-x_{2}dx_{1}}{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}\,,

(3.12)

defined on the punctured plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\{0\}
and possessing the holonomy

q=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint A^{\circ}\,.

(3.13)

Furthermore,
A\to A^{\circ}
as
\rho\to\infty
; hence, the one-forms (
3.11
) and (
3.12
) coincide asymptotically. If
\gamma_{\infty}
is a space-like circle of infinite radius, then the integral

q=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{\gamma_{\infty}}A

(3.14)

represents the total charge of the field configuration.
Note that this is not the standard electric charge, since we are considering Chern–Simons theory rather than Maxwell theory. Instead,
q
is naturally interpreted as a magnetic flux produced by a 2D analogue of an ideal Aharonov–Bohm solenoid localized at a single point. In conventional (2+1)-dimensional gauge theory, such magnetic fluxes are usually attributed to hypothetical point particles called
anyons

[
30
]
. We adopt this terminology and henceforth refer to
q
as the magnetic flux associated with a static anyon.

As discussed in Sec.
2
, the Poisson–Chern–Simons theory admits a lower-degree conservation law (
2.33
). For the specific solution (
3.11
), one readily finds

J=A^{\circ}
and consequently

q=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint J\,,

(3.15)

for any simple loop enclosing the origin. This provides a rigorous interpretation of (
3.11
) as the magnetic flux localized at a single point.

Note that the Poisson structure (
2.3
) explicitly breaks invariance under spatial reflection

x^{i}\to-x^{i}
. However, the model remains invariant under the combined transformation

x^{i}\to-x^{i}\,,\qquad g\to-g\,,

(3.16)

which simultaneously reverses the sign of the coupling constant. If the coupling
constant is restricted to be non-negative,
g\geq 0
, this symmetry effectively corresponds to
the reversal of the magnetic charge,
q\to-q
.

For
c=-2qg>0
, the solution (
3.11
) is well-defined on the entire plane except
at the origin,
\rho=0
, where it is discontinuous. Note that the discontinuity of the gauge potential
A_{i}(x)
is finite and depends on the direction from which the singular point is approached. In the commutative case, this limiting value is always infinite. Consequently, noncommutativity partially regularizes the singularities of anyonic solutions.

For
c=-2qg<0
, additional singularities appear at
\rho=-c
.
In this case, the solution is well-defined only outside a disk of radius

r=\sqrt{-2c}
and becomes complex inside this region. Thus, positive and negative
fluxes (for a fixed sign of
g
) exhibit distinct analytic structures.
This asymmetry, however, is consistent with CPT invariance.

From a physical viewpoint, there is nothing pathological about the breakdown of a classical
solution in a small spatial region. One should keep in mind that Poisson gauge theory
is, by construction, a low-energy effective approximation to a gauge theory on a genuinely
noncommutative spacetime. Accordingly, it is expected to be applicable only at length scales
much larger than the fundamental scale
\ell=\sqrt{g}
.

4
Multianyon solutions

The translation invariance of the equations of motion allows one to shift the solution (
3.11
) into another stationary solution in which a static anyon is located at the point
y
:

A_{0}=0\,,\qquad A_{i}=\frac{1}{g}\varepsilon_{ij}(x^{j}-y^{j})\left(1-\sqrt{1+\frac{2qg}{(x_{1}-y_{1})^{2}+(x_{2}-y_{2})^{2}}}\right)\,.

(4.1)

Starting from this three-parameter family of gauge potentials, we construct a more general solution that depends on
3n
parameters, corresponding to a configuration of
n
anyons. The nonlinearity of the field equations (
3.1
) precludes a simple linear superposition of single-anyon solutions (
4.1
).

In
[
16
]
, it was suggested to interpret the gauge field
A
as a bisection of the symplectic groupoid
over the Poisson manifold
\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
.
The set of bisections forms a group
\mathcal{B}
, which acts on
\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
via diffeomorphisms. The identity element of
\mathcal{B}
is
A=0
, and the group multiplication is defined by

A\circ A^{\prime}=A_{\mu}\big(x^{\nu}+\theta^{\nu\lambda}A^{\prime}_{\lambda}(x)\big)dx^{\mu}+A^{\prime}_{\mu}(x)dx^{\mu}\,.

(4.2)

For
\theta\neq 0
, this multiplication is both nonlinear and nonlocal. The action of a bisection
A
on
\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
is given by

x^{\mu}\mapsto\phi_{A}^{\mu}(x)=x^{\mu}+\theta^{\mu\nu}A_{\nu}(x)\,,

(4.3)

so that
\phi_{A}\circ\phi_{A^{\prime}}=\phi_{A\circ A^{\prime}}
. Bisections with vanishing curvature (
2.6
)
are referred to as
Lagrangian
1
1

1

The image of a Lagrangian bisection is a Lagrangian submanifold of the symplectic groupoid, hence the name.
. They constitute a subgroup
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{B}
.
Thus, the solution space of the field equations
F_{\mu\nu}=0
is identified with the subgroup of Lagrangian bisections. The group multiplication (
4.2
) then provides a natural framework for the nonlinear superposition of particular solutions.

Let
A(q,y)
denote the single-anyon solution (
4.1
). Given
n
such solutions with charges and locations
(q_{k},y_{k})
, we define the
n
-anyon configuration by

A(q_{1},y_{1};\ldots;q_{n},y_{n})=A(q_{1},y_{1})\circ A(q_{2},y_{2})\circ\cdots\circ A(q_{n},y_{n})\,.

(4.4)

Since the group
\mathcal{L}
is non-Abelian, the resulting configuration depends on the ordering of the factors.
Taken together, the various products (
4.4
) constitute a subgroup in
\mathcal{L}
. We denote this subgroup
\mathcal{A}
and refer to as the
anyon group
.

The explicit substitution of (
4.1
) into (
4.4
) leads to a rather cumbersome algebraic expression. Therefore, we present only the two-anyon solution with one anyon located at the origin:

A(q_{1},0;q_{2},y)=a_{1}(z){\varepsilon_{ij}z^{j}dx^{i}}-a_{2}(z){\varepsilon_{ij}(z^{j}-y^{j})}dx^{i}\,,

(4.5)

where

a_{1}(z)=\frac{1}{g}\left(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{2q_{1}g}{z^{2}}}\right)\,,\qquad a_{2}(z)=\frac{1}{g}\left(1-\sqrt{1+\frac{2q_{2}g}{(z-y)^{2}}}\right)\,,

(4.6)

and

z^{i}=\phi^{i}_{A(q_{2},y)}(x)=x^{i}-g(x^{i}-y^{i})a_{2}(x)\,,

(4.7)

c.f. Eq.(
4.3
). This solution simplifies considerably for
y=0
, when the two anyons are placed at the same point. In this case, we find that

A(q_{1},0;q_{2},0)=A(q_{1}+q_{2},0)\,,

(4.8)

which is consistent with expectations: any rotation-invariant solution is defined by a single parameter – the magnetic flux
q
– which is obviously additive in the commutative limit. In particular, superposing anyons with opposite fluxes results in
A(q,0;-q,0)=0
, which may be interpreted as anyon-antianyon annihilation.
In the opposite regime
y^{2}\gg g
, when the two anyons are well-separated, the interaction between them becomes negligible.
Consequently, the system can be approximated as the linear superposition of two independent single-anyon solutions.

To further clarify the structure of the two-anyon solution (
4.5
), we compute the on-shell closed form (
2.33
). After some algebraic manipulations, we obtain

J=\frac{q_{1}\epsilon_{ij}z^{j}dz^{i}}{z^{2}}+\frac{q_{2}\epsilon_{ij}(z^{j}-y^{j})dz^{i}}{(z-y)^{2}}-df\,,

(4.9)

where

f=\frac{g}{2}(y_{1}z_{2}-y_{2}z_{1})a_{1}(z)a_{2}(z)

(4.10)

is a single-valued function that vanishes in the commutative limit. Integrating
J
along infinitesimal loops allows one to locate the magnetic fluxes
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
at the points
z^{i}=0
and
z^{i}=y^{i}
, respectively. We conclude that the interacting anyons preserve their magnetic fluxes, while their positions shift according to Eq. (
4.7
).

The non-Abelian nature of the anyon group
\mathcal{A}
is characterized by its derived group
[\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}]
. This is generated by the commutators

[A_{1},A_{2}]=A_{1}\circ A_{2}\circ A_{1}^{-1}\circ A_{2}^{-1}

(4.11)

of multianyon solutions. By construction, all elements of the derived group
[\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}]
vanish in the commutative limit.
For a pair of single-anyon solutions

A_{1}(q_{1},0)
and
A_{2}(q_{2},y)
, the commutator evaluates to

[A_{1},A_{2}]^{i}(x)=x^{i}+\Gamma^{i}_{1}\Big(y-\Gamma_{2}\big(y-\Gamma_{-1}(y+\Gamma_{-2}(x-y))\big)\Big)\,,

(4.12)

where we have introduced the vector-valued functions

\Gamma^{i}_{\pm a}(x)=x^{i}\sqrt{1\pm\frac{2q_{a}g}{x^{2}}}\,,\qquad a=1,2\,.

(4.13)

To determine the positions of the anyons – defined by the poles of the commutator (
4.11
) – we utilize the general properties of the covariant field strength tensor
[
16
]
:

F^{s}(A_{1}\circ A_{2})=\phi^{\ast}_{A_{2}}F^{s}(A_{1})+F^{s}(A_{2})\,.

(4.14)

Recalling the relation between the covariant strength tensor and the conserved current,
F^{s}=dJ
,
we obtain the composition law for the currents:

J(A_{1}\circ A_{2})=\phi^{\ast}_{A_{2}}J(A_{1})+J(A_{2})+df(A_{1},A_{2})\,,

(4.15)

where
f
is a scalar function. Consequently, if
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
denote the poles of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
, respectively, then the poles of the composition
A_{1}\circ A_{2}
are located at
z_{1}=\phi_{A_{2}}(y_{1})
and
z_{2}=y_{2}
. Applying this result to the commutator (
4.11
), we find four distinct poles at the positions:

z_{1}=\phi^{-1}_{A_{2}\circ A_{1}\circ A_{2}^{-1}}(y_{1})\,,\qquad z_{2}=\phi^{-1}_{A_{2}\circ A_{1}}(y_{2})\,,\qquad z_{3}=\phi^{-1}_{A_{2}}(y_{1})\,,\qquad z_{4}=y_{2}\,,

(4.16)

carrying fluxes
q_{1},q_{2},-q_{1}
, and
-q_{2}
, respectively. So, the total magnetic flux is equal to zero.

5
Coulomb’s potential

We now consider the Poisson deformation of the source-free Maxwell’s equations:

D^{\mu}F_{\mu\nu}=0\,.

(5.1)

Unlike the Poisson–Chern–Simons theory discussed above, these equations describe propagating degrees of freedom.
We are interested in solutions that would correspond to a deformation of the Coulomb potential in conventional 3D electrodynamics.
Therefore, we are looking for
SO(2)
-invariant stationary solutions. They are defined by the electromagnetic potentials (
3.2
), where
\widetilde{\Phi}
and
\widetilde{\Psi}
are now functions of a single variable
\rho

3.4

\partial_{t}\widetilde{\Phi}=\partial_{t}\widetilde{\Psi}=0

\begin{array}[]{rcl}D^{\mu}F_{\mu 0}&=&2\partial_{\rho}\Big(\rho(\Psi^{2}+\Phi^{2})\partial_{\rho}A_{0}\Big)=0\,,\\[14.22636pt]
D^{\mu}F_{\mu i}&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{g}\Big[g^{2}(\partial_{\rho}A_{0})^{2}+2\partial^{2}_{\rho}\big(\rho(\Phi^{2}+\Psi^{2})\big)\Big](x_{i}\Psi-\varepsilon_{ij}x^{j}\Phi)=0\,,\end{array}

\delta_{\varepsilon}A_{0}=0\,,\qquad\delta_{\varepsilon}A_{i}=-(\Phi x_{i}-\Psi\epsilon_{ij}x^{j})\partial_{\rho}\varepsilon

(5.3)

for
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\rho)

\Phi
and
\Psi

As the second equation factorizes, the system (

\Psi=\Phi=0
and
A_{0}(\rho)
is arbitrary. Then

A_{i}=\frac{1}{g}\epsilon_{ij}x^{j}\quad\mbox{and}\quad F_{ij}=\frac{1}{g}\epsilon_{ij}\,.

(5.4)

However, this solution lacks a well-defined commutative limit, and its physical interpretation remains obscure.

Substituting

\Psi=R(\rho)\sin{\varphi(\rho)}\,,\qquad\Phi=R(\rho)\cos{\varphi(\rho)}\,,\qquad h(\rho)=\rho R^{2}(\rho)

reduces the system (

\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{\rho}(h\partial_{\rho}A_{0})=0\,,\\[14.22636pt]

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{\rho}h=-\frac{g^{2}e^{2}}{2h^{2}}\,,\end{array}

(5.6)

where

is an integration constant. By definition,
h(\rho)\geq 0

\varphi(\rho)
drops out of the equations due to gauge invariance (

Integrating this equation for the inverse function

\rho=\frac{h}{a}\sqrt{a+\frac{g^{2}e^{2}}{h}}-\frac{g^{2}e^{2}}{2a^{3/2}}\ln{\left(\frac{\sqrt{a+\frac{g^{2}e^{2}}{h}}+\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{a+\frac{g^{2}e^{2}}{h}}-\sqrt{a}}\right)}-c\,.

(5.8)

is the only parameter with the dimension of length squared, it is natural to parametrize

as

Then, in the commutative limit, the logarithmic term vanishes, and one obtains

h=\sqrt{a}\rho\,.

(5.9)

Combining Eqs.(
5.6

A_{0}=\frac{e}{\sqrt{a}}\ln{\left(\frac{\sqrt{a+\frac{e^{2}g^{2}}{h}}+a}{\sqrt{a+\frac{e^{2}g^{2}}{h}}-a}\right)}=\frac{e}{\sqrt{a}}\ln h+\mathrm{const}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{e^{2}g^{2}}{h}\Big)\,.

(5.10)

6
Poisson–Maxwell–Chern–Simons theory

We finally turn to a Coulomb-like solution in the full Poisson–Maxwell–Chern–Simons theory, governed by the field equations:

D^{\mu}F_{\mu\nu}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\varepsilon_{\nu\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta}=0\,.

(6.1)

Using the same stationary, rotation-invariant ansatz as before, we reduce these equations to the following system:

\partial_{\rho}A_{0}=\frac{2ge+\kappa\rho}{2gh}-\frac{\kappa}{2g}\,,\qquad\left(\frac{2ge+\kappa\rho}{2h}\right)^{2}+2\partial^{2}_{\rho}h=\frac{\kappa^{2}}{4}\,,

(6.2)

where
e
is an integration constant. For
\kappa=0
, these equations recover (
5.6
). Unlike the pure Maxwell case, the second equation admits no obvious first integral, which complicates exact integration. Since the free (commutative) theory is known to describe a massive particle, the scalar potential
A_{0}
produced by a point source is expected to be short-range. This implies the following asymptotic behavior:

A_{0}\to 0\,,\qquad\partial_{\rho}A_{0}\to 0\quad\mbox{as}\quad\rho\to\infty\,.

(6.3)

The system (
6.2
) possesses the following exact solution satisfying the asymptotic conditions:

h=\rho+\frac{2ge}{\kappa}\,,\qquad A_{0}=0\,.

(6.4)

This solution corresponds to a purely anyonic configuration:

A_{i}=\frac{1}{g}\varepsilon_{ij}x^{j}\left(1-\sqrt{1+\frac{2ge}{\kappa\rho}}\right)\,,

(6.5)

with the flux
q=-e/\kappa
, cf. (
3.11
).

Using the constant
\kappa
, it is convenient to introduce the dimensionless variables

\bar{h}=\frac{\kappa^{2}}{4}h\,,\qquad\bar{\rho}=\frac{\kappa^{2}}{4}\rho+\bar{\rho}_{0}\,,\qquad\bar{\rho}_{0}=\frac{\kappa ge}{2}\,.

(6.6)

In terms of these variables, Eq. (
6.2
) takes the form:

2\frac{d^{2}\bar{h}}{d\bar{\rho}^{2}}+\left(\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\bar{h}}\right)^{2}=1\,.

(6.7)

The solutions to the equations are connected by the relation

h(\rho)=\frac{4}{\kappa^{2}}\bar{h}\Big(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{4}\rho+\frac{\kappa ge}{2}\Big)\,.

(6.8)

The form of the left hand side of Eq.(
6.7
) suggests two possible asymptotics for solutions as
\bar{\rho}\to\infty
: linear
\bar{h}=\bar{\rho}
and quadratic
\bar{h}=\bar{\rho}^{2}/2
. The latter leads to a growing scalar potential:

\partial_{\rho}A_{0}=-\frac{\kappa}{2g}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad A_{0}\sim\rho\,.

(6.9)

This does not correspond to a short-range potential. Therefore, we focus on solutions with linear asymptotics and write

\bar{h}=\bar{\rho}+Y(\bar{\rho})

(6.10)

for some function
Y(\bar{\rho})
that vanishes at infinity. Such solutions can be viewed as perturbations of the exact anionic solution (
6.5
). Upon substituting into (
6.7
), we find

\left(\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\bar{\rho}+Y}\right)^{2}+2\frac{d^{2}Y}{d\bar{\rho}^{2}}=1\,.

(6.11)

To extract the asymptotic behavior, we approximate the equation for large
\bar{\rho}
, where nonlinear corrections become subleading:

\frac{d^{2}Y}{\partial\bar{\rho}^{2}}=\frac{Y}{\bar{\rho}}\,.

(6.12)

The decaying solution to this equation is expressed in terms of the modified Bessel function of the second kind:

Y(\bar{\rho})=C\sqrt{\bar{\rho}}\,K_{1}\Big(2\sqrt{\bar{\rho}}\Big)\sim C\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}{\sqrt[4]{\bar{\rho}}}\;e^{-2\sqrt{\bar{\rho}}}\,.

(6.13)

Using the identity
K_{1}=-K_{0}^{\prime}
for the Bessel functions, we can integrate the first equation in (
6.2
) within the same approximation:

\partial_{\rho}A_{0}=-\frac{\kappa}{2\rho}Y\quad\Longrightarrow\quad A_{0}={\frac{1}{2}}C\kappa K_{0}(\kappa\sqrt{\rho})\,.

(6.14)

In the asymptotic region, this gives the two-dimensional Yukawa potential

A_{0}\sim{\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{\rho}}}\;e^{-\kappa\sqrt{\rho}}\,,

(6.15)

where
\kappa
can be interpreted as the mass of the gauge boson. We note that the approximate scalar potential (
6.14
) is in fact an exact
solution of the Helmholtz–Yukawa equation in two dimensions:

(\nabla^{2}-\kappa^{2})A_{0}(x)=-Q\delta^{2}(x)\,,\qquad Q=2\pi\kappa C\,.

(6.16)

Therefore, at large distances, our solution coincides with the scalar potential generated by a point electric charge
Q
in 3D massive electrodynamics.

(6.25)

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we have constructed rotationally invariant solutions to the Maxwell–Chern–Simons equations in a noncommutative spacetime. Our analysis demonstrates that at large distances (
r\gg\sqrt{g}
), these solutions asymptotically recover their commutative counterparts, ensuring the correspondence principle at macroscopic scales. However, in the UV limit (
r\to 0
), the noncommutative geometry acts as a natural regulator, either resolving the central singularities or fundamentally altering the short-range behavior. Most notably, we established that the total electromagnetic energy of the Yukawa-type potential becomes finite, thereby resolving the “electromagnetic mass” problem.

Furthermore, the non-analytic dependence of some solutions on the noncommutativity parameter
g
suggests that standard perturbative expansions may fail to capture essential features of the theory. In the pure Chern–Simons case, we have generalized the multianyon solutions and revealed that their nonlinear superposition is governed by a Lie groupoid. This endows the solution space
\mathcal{A}
with a non-Abelian group structure, potentially impacting the braiding statistics of these objects. Finally, the derived generalized Gauss law provides a robust framework for interpreting the physical parameters of the exact solutions.

It would be of great interest to extend these results to higher dimensions and to investigate other types of noncommutativity within this framework.
