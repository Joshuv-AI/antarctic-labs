Transformer-GNN fusion with gradient surgery for electricity price forecasting
Authors: Bingxiang Wu, Pengcheng Yang, Yiming He, Huayang Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate short-term electricity price forecasting is crucial for efficient market operations and reliable power grid management. Existing methods, however, frequently struggle to model both extended temporal dependencies and the spatial correlations arising from network constraints. To address this, we present a hybrid forecasting framework that integrates a Transformer encoder for long-term temporal representation, a graph neural network (GNN) for topology-aware spatial modeling, and a PCGrad gradient surgery mechanism to mitigate training conflicts across heterogeneous components. Our architecture combines market-level signals from PJM hourly records with auxiliary grid-level physical measurements derived from an IEEE 39-bus PMU simulation through synchronized window-based alignment and an attention-based fusion module. Rather than treating the IEEE 39-bus system as a literal topological surrogate of PJM, the simulated PMU stream is used as a physically informed auxiliary modality for methodological validation of cross-domain spatiotemporal fusion. Evaluated on these two datasets, the proposed model achieves improved performance over classical and hybrid benchmarks in the fused-data setting. Across five independent runs, the proposed model attains the lowest average error among the compared fused-data methods and shows narrower uncertainty intervals than the strongest multimodal baseline. These results support the claim that temporally aligned fusion of market signals and auxiliary physical features can improve forecasting in this proof-of-concept setup, while not implying reconstruction of PJM-specific physical topology.Keywords: Electricity price forecasting, Spatiotemporal modeling, Transformer, Graph neural network (GNN), Multimodal data fusion, Conflict-aware optimizationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Accurate regional electricity price prediction is vital for market participants, enabling improved bidding strategies, procurement decisions, and operational planning, as well as facilitating the efficient integration of variable renewable energy sources. Regional price dynamics are influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including demand fluctuations, weather conditions, grid topology, generation mix, and policy shifts, which collectively generate intricate spatiotemporal patterns that forecasting models must capture1,2.

While significant advancements have been made, several core challenges persist. Firstly, electricity price series exhibit both long-range temporal dependencies and spatial interactions across networked nodes; methods focusing solely on either temporal or spatial dimensions often fail to capture these cross-domain couplings. Secondly, real-world datasets are inherently multimodal and high-dimensional, complicating the effective fusion of heterogeneous features. Thirdly, joint training on diverse data streams frequently introduces optimization difficulties, such as gradient conflict, unstable convergence, and compromised model generalization, which are common in multimodal learning scenarios3,4.

Existing research has addressed the forecasting problem from multiple perspectives. Conventional time-series analysis and machine learning techniques, including support vector machines and decision trees, offer interpretability and ease of implementation. However, their reliance on manual feature engineering limits their capacity to handle strong nonlinearities and long-term dependencies12,13. Deep learning approaches, such as Long Short-Term Memory networks (LSTMs)5–7 and convolutional neural networks (CNNs)8,9, automate feature extraction and enhance temporal modeling, yet they typically disregard the underlying grid topology and spatial relationships. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)10,11 have proven effective in encoding network structure and nodal interactions. Concurrently, Transformer-based architectures excel at capturing long-range temporal dependencies through self-attention mechanisms12,13. Other lines of inquiry employ generative models for data synthesis or imputation and apply reinforcement learning for deriving trading or dispatch policies. Although several hybrid architectures integrating temporal and spatial modules have been proposed, achieving effective feature fusion and ensuring stable joint training remain significant unresolved issues14,15.

From the literature, we identify three primary research gaps: (1) A scarcity of unified frameworks capable of jointly modeling temporal and spatial dependencies at scale; (2) The relative immaturity of multimodal fusion techniques, where naive concatenation or simplistic fusion often degrades performance; and (3) Insufficient attention to the optimization instabilities induced by conflicting training signals from multi-source data in many empirical studies16,17.

To address these gaps, this paper proposes a cohesive framework that integrates temporal and spatial learning while enhancing training stability. Our model utilizes a Transformer encoder to derive temporal embeddings from price and ancillary time-series data, coupled with a Graph Neural Network to capture structurally informed spatial patterns from an auxiliary power-system modality. To tackle multimodal fusion challenges, we introduce a gradient surgery mechanism designed to detect and mitigate conflicting gradients originating from distinct feature streams, thereby stabilizing the optimization process and promoting more effective joint representation learning. It should be noted that the simulated PMU data are incorporated as an auxiliary physically informed feature stream to examine whether topology-related dynamics can improve forecasting when fused with real market observations under synchronized temporal windows. Comparisons against PJM-only baselines therefore reflect both architectural effects and the contribution of the additional auxiliary modality. To reduce over-interpretation, the manuscript consistently distinguishes between “architecture benefit” and “extra-information benefit,” and frames the study as a proof-of-concept demonstration of cross-domain multimodal fusion rather than a literal reconstruction of PJM-specific physical topology. Proposed method

The proposed framework adopts a modular design to jointly model temporal dynamics and spatial dependencies for regional electricity price prediction. This architecture processes heterogeneous input data, extracts distinct temporal and spatial representations, integrates them into a cohesive feature embedding, and employs an optimization mechanism designed to minimize interference between learning objectives. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the system comprises five core components: a data preprocessing module, a temporal encoder, a spatial encoder, a multimodal fusion layer, and a conflict-aware optimization module.

Overall architecture of the proposed framework with PCGrad-based training. The PJM market stream and the auxiliary PMU stream are aligned at the hourly-window level; the PMU branch is used as a physically informed auxiliary modality rather than a literal PJM-topology surrogate.

The data preprocessing module is responsible for standardizing and synchronizing diverse input streams, including historical price sequences, load profiles, meteorological variables, and auxiliary grid-state information. The market stream and the auxiliary physical stream are aligned at the temporal-window level. Following normalization and missing data imputation, time-series data are directed to the temporal encoder, while structural network information is routed to the spatial encoder.

Temporal representation learning is handled by a Transformer-based encoder. Utilizing a multi-head self-attention mechanism, this module captures both local variations and extended temporal dependencies within the price series and associated chronological data.

Spatial relationships are modeled through a graph representation constructed from the auxiliary physical-system modality. In this graph, nodes correspond to buses in the IEEE 39-bus benchmark system and edges denote physical electrical connections. The resulting spatial embeddings are not interpreted as a literal reconstruction of the PJM market topology; instead, they provide a physically informed auxiliary representation that is fused with the market stream at the feature level.

The fusion layer synthesizes the temporal and spatial embeddings into a unified representation for final prediction. We implement an attention-based fusion strategy, which dynamically assigns importance weights to features from each modality, enabling the model to adaptively prioritize the most relevant information at each forecasting step.

To ensure stable training across the heterogeneous learning tasks, the optimization module incorporates a gradient conflict mitigation technique, specifically the PCGrad method. This approach detects and resolves conflicting gradient directions arising from different data modalities, thereby enhancing training convergence and final model generalization.

Collectively, these components constitute an end-to-end pipeline that combines raw market observations with an auxiliary physically informed graph modality to generate short-term price forecasts. The design is intended to evaluate the benefit of cross-domain spatiotemporal fusion, rather than to claim a one-to-one reconstruction of the operational PJM network.

Spatial module

Spatial relationships are modeled through a graph representation constructed from the auxiliary physical-system modality. In this graph, nodes correspond to buses in the IEEE 39-bus benchmark system and edges denote physical electrical connections. The resulting spatial embeddings are not interpreted as a literal reconstruction of the PJM market topology; instead, they provide a physically informed auxiliary representation that is fused with the market stream at the feature level.

More specifically, the auxiliary PMU branch is assumed to encode temporally synchronized indicators of physically meaningful system states, such as voltage-stress variation, phase-angle dispersion, and line-flow fluctuation patterns. These signals are not treated as node-wise surrogates for PJM market zones. Rather, they are used as structured contextual descriptors that may correlate with market regimes associated with congestion sensitivity, volatility amplification, or changes in system operating stress. In this sense, the useful transfer mechanism is not exact topological transfer, but transfer of physically informed temporal context.

The graph used in this work is formalized as a fixed undirected topology during training. Each node corresponds to a bus in the auxiliary benchmark system, associated with its local feature vector, and each edge reflects an electrical connection with an optional weight based on interaction intensity.

The implementation uses a fixed graph during model training and inference. If the underlying topology changes only slightly, such as through limited line-status adjustments or modest parameter perturbations, the adjacency matrix can in principle be updated and the model can be fine-tuned from the pretrained weights. However, substantial topology changes, major market redesign, or deployment on a network with a markedly different scale would typically require retraining or at least domain-specific adaptation. More flexible strategies, such as dynamic adjacency learning, graph attention over latent links, or hierarchical pooling for scale transfer, represent important directions for extending the present framework.

Conflict-aware optimization

Effective multimodal learning, particularly the joint training of temporal and spatial feature extractors, is often challenged by gradient interference. The learning signals arising from heterogeneous data streams—such as market-driven temporal sequences and physically structured graph representations—may point in conflicting optimization directions, thereby slowing convergence and degrading final model performance. To address this, we incorporate a gradient surgery technique, specifically the Projecting Conflicting Gradients (PCGrad) method18,19, into our optimization process.

The optimization process is explicitly defined through three related objectives. First, the temporal branch produces an auxiliary prediction based solely on the Transformer-derived temporal representation, yielding a temporal loss . Second, the spatial branch produces an auxiliary prediction based solely on the graph-based spatial representation, yielding a spatial loss . Third, the fused spatiotemporal representation is passed to the final forecasting head, yielding the main forecasting loss . All three losses are computed against the same target electricity price, but they supervise different representational pathways.

The use of auxiliary losses serves a practical purpose: it yields branch-specific gradients that can be explicitly compared and reconciled. This design is more informative than relying only on the gradient of the main fused loss, because it allows the optimization procedure to distinguish whether a gradient originates from the temporal pathway, the spatial pathway, or the fused prediction pathway. To quantify whether PCGrad is actually needed, we additionally monitor per-batch gradient-conflict statistics during training. Experiments

Data description and preprocessing

To evaluate the proposed framework, we use two temporally aligned but functionally distinct data streams: a real-world hourly electricity market dataset from PJM and an auxiliary physically informed PMU dataset generated from an IEEE 39-bus benchmark simulation. The two streams are not assumed to share a one-to-one node correspondence. Instead, they are synchronized at the hourly time-window level and fused at the representation stage to examine whether physically structured auxiliary information can improve price forecasting.

PJM market data: The PJM dataset20 comprises hourly records from January 2010 to December 2020 and provides the forecasting target, namely locational marginal price (LMP), together with market-related covariates such as system load, generation output, and fuel price indicators. These variables form the real-world market stream used by the temporal encoder.

PJM market data: The PJM dataset20 comprises hourly records from January 2010 to December 2020 and provides the forecasting target, namely locational marginal price (LMP), together with market-related covariates such as system load, generation output, and fuel price indicators. These variables form the real-world market stream used by the temporal encoder.

IEEE 39-bus PMU data: The auxiliary PMU stream is generated from a dynamic simulation of the IEEE 39-bus benchmark system over the same calendar period21. The simulation is initialized from the benchmark network parameters and driven by time-varying operating profiles designed to emulate changing grid conditions, including load variation, dispatch perturbation, and line-flow fluctuation. At each simulated step, phasor-style measurements are recorded, including voltage magnitudes, phase angles, and line-flow quantities for the buses and branches of the benchmark system. This synthetic PMU stream is not interpreted as a literal topological surrogate of PJM, but as an auxiliary physically informed modality used to test the value of cross-domain spatiotemporal fusion.

PMU simulation and hourly aggregation pipeline: The raw PMU outputs are generated at high temporal resolution and then processed to produce hourly features compatible with the PJM price labels. Specifically, the PMU channels are first denoised using a Butterworth low-pass filter. The filtered signals are then aggregated within each hour to obtain summary statistics, including mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, and selected lagged values from the immediately preceding window. These hourly PMU feature summaries are timestamped using the end of each hourly window and aligned with the corresponding PJM hourly market label. Therefore, each forecasting sample contains a historical PJM look-back sequence and an auxiliary PMU feature tensor derived only from information available up to the prediction time.

Preprocessing and leakage prevention: Missing values in the PJM stream are imputed before window generation, and extreme outliers are filtered using interquartile-range analysis. All normalization and scaling operations are fitted using the training split only and then applied to the validation and test splits without refitting. For the PMU stream, filtering, aggregation, and lag construction are performed chronologically so that no future information is introduced into earlier samples. After preprocessing, both streams are segmented into synchronized fixed-length windows to construct aligned multimodal samples for model training and evaluation.

To improve reproducibility, we further clarify the optimization workflow. In each mini-batch, the temporal encoder and spatial encoder first produce modality-specific latent representations. Two auxiliary prediction heads are attached to the temporal and spatial branches, producing auxiliary losses and , respectively. The fused representation then produces the final forecasting loss . Gradients of these three losses are computed separately with respect to the shared trainable parameters, after which PCGrad is applied to remove pairwise conflicting gradient components before the optimizer update. Branch-specific prediction heads are optimized jointly with the backbone in an end-to-end manner.

Because optimization is stochastic for deep learning models, each deep baseline and each multimodal variant was repeated over 5 independent runs with different random seeds. The reported results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation across runs. For the main benchmark and optimization ablation tables, we additionally report 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the primary metric MSE and p-values obtained from paired two-sided t-tests against the strongest relevant baseline (for the main benchmark) or against the PCGrad variant (for the optimization ablation). To characterize convergence behavior, we additionally record the epoch at which the best validation performance is reached and the standard deviation of validation loss over the final training epochs.

All experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, an Intel Core i9 processor, and 64 GB RAM. The implementation was based on Python 3.10 and PyTorch 2.1 with CUDA 12.1 acceleration. Training time was recorded as wall-clock time from the start of optimization to the end of the final epoch. Inference latency was measured on the test set after model warm-up and is reported as the average per-sample prediction time under the same hardware and batch-size setting.

Results

Training dynamics and stability

The training dynamics of different model configurations are illustrated in Fig. 2, which plots the loss curves for three variants: a Transformer-only model, a GNN-only model, and the proposed full model with PCGrad optimization. All models successfully minimize their training loss over epochs. However, significant differences are observed in validation loss behavior. The proposed model demonstrates a markedly smoother and more stable validation curve with reduced oscillation compared to single-modality counterparts. This stability suggests that the PCGrad component effectively mitigates the conflicting gradient directions arising from the heterogeneous temporal and spatial learning objectives, thereby promoting better generalization. In contrast, the Transformer-only and GNN-only models exhibit more erratic validation loss trajectories, indicating a higher susceptibility to overfitting or unstable updates when processing complex, unimodal signals. These observations empirically confirm that the integration of gradient surgery enhances training robustness for multimodal forecasting tasks.

Training and validation loss trajectories for unimodal baselines and the fused model. PCGrad-equipped multimodal model exhibits smoother validation behavior and lower late-epoch oscillation, indicating improved optimization stability.

Ablation study on optimization strategy

To clarify the specific contribution of PCGrad, we compare the proposed optimization strategy with three alternative training schemes under the same architecture and data setting: (1) standard joint optimization using a single fused loss, (2) weighted-loss optimization with fixed coefficients for the temporal, spatial, and fused objectives, and (3) staged training in which the temporal and spatial branches are first pretrained separately and then jointly fine-tuned. The PCGrad variant uses the same architecture and objectives as the weighted loss setting but resolves pairwise gradient conflicts before parameter updates.

Table 2 summarizes the mean ± standard deviation over five runs, together with the 95% confidence interval for MSE and the p-value of each alternative relative to the PCGrad variant. Standard joint optimization provides a strong baseline but exhibits both higher average error and larger across-run dispersion. Weighted-loss optimization partially improves the balance among objectives, while staged training further stabilizes early optimization; however, neither matches the mean performance nor the run-to-run stability of PCGrad. The smaller standard deviation and narrower confidence interval of PCGrad support the claim that its contribution is not limited to improving average metrics, but also to reducing optimization variability across runs.

Robustness under partial modality availability

To assess the practical robustness of the proposed framework, we further evaluate model performance under partial modality availability. Three degraded-input scenarios are considered: (1) partial masking of PMU-derived auxiliary features, (2) complete removal of the PMU stream at inference time, and (3) partial masking of selected PJM covariates while preserving the target price history. For partial masking, a fixed proportion of feature channels is randomly dropped at test time, and results are averaged over multiple masking patterns.

Table 3 reports mean ± standard deviation over five runs. As expected, performance degrades when one modality becomes incomplete, but the magnitude of degradation differs across cases. Removing the PMU branch causes only a moderate decline relative to the full multimodal setting, indicating that the temporal PJM stream remains the dominant source of predictive information. Partial masking of PJM-side inputs generally has a stronger effect, reflecting the importance of market-driven temporal context for short-term price formation.

Relative to the strongest multimodal baseline (LSTM + GCN under the same PJM + PMU setting), the mean MSE decreases from 0.1619 to 0.1439, and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals do not overlap. A paired two-sided t-test over five runs yields p=0.0038 for MSE, indicating that the improvement is statistically robust under the fused-data configuration. By contrast, comparisons against PJM-only baselines should be interpreted more cautiously: those differences reflect both architectural improvement and the availability of an additional auxiliary modality, rather than a pure architecture-only gain.

The results reveal two distinct patterns. First, within the PJM-only setting, deep temporal models (especially the Transformer baseline) outperform classical machine-learning methods, confirming the importance of long-range temporal modeling for market data. Second, within the PJM + auxiliary PMU setting, multimodal models outperform unimodal ones on average, suggesting that synchronized physically informed auxiliary features can provide additional predictive context in this experimental setup. This evidence supports the utility of fused-data learning, but it should not be interpreted as proof that the learned graph branch reconstructs PJM-specific physical topology.

The 95% CI and p-value analyses confirm that the gain over the strongest multimodal baseline is statistically supported, whereas the larger numerical gap to PJM-only models should be interpreted as a combined architecture-and-modality effect.

Discussion

The present results suggest that the proposed framework benefits from combining temporal market information with an auxiliary physically structured stream within a unified learning pipeline. However, this benefit should be interpreted carefully. The observed improvement reflects a fused-data forecasting setup, and therefore it contains two intertwined components: an architecture effect (the way the model integrates temporal and spatial encoders under conflict-aware optimization) and an extra-information effect (the fact that the fused model has access to an additional modality). The comparison with the strongest multimodal baseline shows that the architecture itself contributes meaningfully under matched inputs, whereas the larger gap to PJM-only baselines should not be read as a pure architecture-only superiority claim.

A key conceptual question is why the auxiliary spatial branch can help PJM price forecasting at all. Our interpretation is that the synchronized PMU stream supplies physically structured temporal context rather than literal market-topology information. In particular, dynamic PMU summaries may act as proxies for broad system states associated with electrical stress, fluctuation intensity, and regime-dependent volatility. When aligned with hourly market observations, these descriptors can provide complementary context that helps the temporal encoder discriminate among different price-formation regimes. This is therefore a transfer of physically informed contextual signals, not a transfer of exact node-level topology from IEEE 39-bus to PJM.

The study should still be regarded as a proof-of-concept rather than as evidence of full PJM-specific physical reconstruction. The auxiliary PMU stream is synthetic, and the learned graph should be interpreted as an auxiliary representation rather than an operational model of PJM zonal connectivity. In addition, the model remains primarily data-driven and may be less responsive to abrupt exogenous disruptions, such as extreme weather events, market interventions, or large-scale outages, unless such signals are explicitly incorporated as inputs.

Another practical consideration is partial modality availability. The robustness analysis indicates that the model degrades gracefully when the auxiliary branch is incomplete or absent, which suggests that the fused design can function in a best-effort multimodal mode and revert toward a weaker market-dominant mode under telemetry limitations. This is encouraging for practical deployment, but it does not remove the need for careful monitoring of modality quality in real applications.

From a deployment perspective, the framework is most appropriate for offline training with periodic retraining and hourly or moderate-latency inference. The higher computational burden relative to simpler baselines is likely acceptable in such scenarios, but it may be less suitable for ultra-low-latency edge deployment or rapidly changing graph settings. Future work will therefore focus on three directions: (i) incorporating richer exogenous signals such as weather forecasts, outage notifications, and market announcements; (ii) reducing computational overhead through pruning, quantization, and efficient operator design; and (iii) investigating tighter spatiotemporal coupling and market-specific topology learning when physically matched grid data become available.

Conclusion

This paper introduces a forecasting framework that integrates a Transformer encoder for temporal analysis with a Graph Neural Network for spatial representation, coordinated via a PCGrad gradient surgery mechanism. The architecture is designed to model heterogeneous temporal and structurally informed signals within a unified learning pipeline for electricity price forecasting.

Experimental evaluation on PJM market data together with an auxiliary synthetic IEEE 39-bus PMU stream shows that the proposed framework can improve predictive accuracy relative to the compared baselines in the fused-data setting. Additional optimization and robustness analyses further indicate that conflict-aware training contributes to lower across-run variability and smoother convergence, while the model also degrades gracefully when one modality becomes incomplete.

At the same time, the present work should be understood as a proof-of-concept study of cross-domain multimodal fusion, not as a literal reconstruction of PJM-specific physical topology. The auxiliary graph branch contributes physically informed contextual information, but the resulting representation does not imply one-to-one transfer of market topology from the IEEE 39-bus benchmark. Within that scope, the proposed framework provides a useful methodological step toward integrating market signals with auxiliary physical descriptors for short-term electricity price forecasting. Future work will focus on physically matched grid–market datasets, stronger exogenous conditioning, and lighter deployment-oriented variants.
