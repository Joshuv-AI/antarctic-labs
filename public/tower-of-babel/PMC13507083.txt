IFI208, an interferon-stimulated gene, promotes host antiviral responses through liquid-liquid phase separation of MDA5
Authors: Guangqiang Ye, Jiaxiu Gao, Xiaohong Liu, Qiongqiong Zhou, Li Huang, Haoxuan Cao, Guihong Zhang, Zhaoxia Zhang, Hongyang Liu, Changjiang Weng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PYHIN (IFI200/HIN-200) family members are involved in host antiviral immune responses by detecting viral nucleic acids through HIN domains. Within this family, the function of IFI208, which lacks a HIN domain, remains largely unknown. In this study, we identify IFI208, a member of the PYHIN family, as an interferon stimulated gene (ISG). Upon exposure to viral DNA and RNA, IFI208 inhibits viral replication by increasing type I interferon (IFN) production. Mechanistically, upon infection, IFI208 is translocated to the cytoplasm through the chromosome region maintenance 1 (XPO1) transporter protein, and interacts with melanoma differentiation associated gene 5 (MDA5) to enhance the ability of this protein to recognize double stranded RNA (dsRNA) and promote its oligomerization. Taken together, we identify IFI208 as an interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) that induces dsRNA dependent liquid-liquid phase separation of MDA5. Our results indicate that the XPO1-IFI208-MDA5 axis has an important function in the innate responses to viruses.

Introduction

The innate immune system is the host’s first line of defense against microbial infections1. Key components of innate antiviral responses are type I interferons (IFN) and downstream Interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), which establish an antiviral environment2. In infected cells, pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), such as viral nucleic acids, are detected by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), including cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)3 and retinoic acid inducible gene I (RIG-I) like receptors (RLRs)4. This recognition activates downstream adaptor molecules, such as stimulator of interferon genes (STING) and mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS), and then TANK binding kinase 1 (TBK1) and IκB kinase ε (IKKε)5 are activated, leading to the production of type I IFNs. Type I IFNs subsequently bind to interferon alpha receptor 1 (IFNAR1) and IFNAR26, initiating the JAK-STAT signaling pathway7,8, which in turn induces the expression of numerous ISGs, thereby sustaining the homeostasis of the antiviral responses.

Liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) is a fundamental regulator of innate responses. This biophysical phenomenon enables the dynamic organization of cellular components, thereby enhancing the efficiency of pathogen detection and immune activation9. cGAS LLPS is essential for the production of the second messenger cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) and the subsequent activation of STING signaling pathway10. The formation of STING cGAMP condensates plays a role in inhibiting the activity of TBK1, which helps to mitigate excessive immune activation11. Likewise, MAVS droplets favor the effective recruitment and release of the transcription factor interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), thereby accelerating antiviral response activation12. Thus, LLPS is a critical mechanism for the regulation of immune surveillance and cellular stress. Whether LLPS-mediated regulation extends to other molecules within RIG-I or MDA5 and cGAS signaling pathways is currently unknown, and may reveal further immunoregulatory mechanisms actionable for therapeutic designs.

HIN-200 family members are PRRs involved in viral nucleic acid recognition via the HIN domain13. Notably, IFI16 and AIM2, two members of this family, recognize the genomes of DNA viruses and trigger the production of IFNs and inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β14,15. IFI16 also exhibits different phase separation stages, developing from droplets to solid filaments in response to viral infection16. LLPS dynamic regulation is coordinated by post translational modifications, indicating a complex interaction between innate immune sensing and LLPS. The N terminus of interferon inducible protein 208 (IFI208) contains a PYD domain similar to that of HIN-200 family members, but notably, IFI208’s C terminus does not contain a HIN domain. Thus far, the implication and mechanism of action of IFI208 during immune responses has remained unknown.

In this work, we find that IFI208 promoter, mRNA transcription, and protein production are activated by viral infection and type I IFN agonists, through IFNAR1 and IFNAR2-dependent mechanisms. Further, IFI208 increases type I IFN production, which inhibits viral replication. Subsequently, Ifi208 deficiency significantly impairs the induction of downstream antiviral genes activated by DNA and RNA viruses, leading to increased susceptibility of Ifi208-deficient mice to DNA and RNA viral infections. This demonstrates that IFI208 is an ISG. Upon viral infection, IFI208 is translocated from the cytoplasm by the nuclear protein XPO1, which enables it to interact with MDA5, enhancing its ability to bind double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), and promoting its aggregation. Ultimately, we show that IFI208 induces dsRNA-dependent LLPS of MDA5. Overall, IFI208 serves as an auxiliary adaptor molecule that enhances MDA5’s recognition of dsRNA, a crucial step in innate antiviral responses.

Results

IFI208 is an interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) with antiviral function

To test if IFI208 expression was regulated by IFN signaling, we constructed a reporter gene under the control of Ifi208 promoter and monitored its expression in cells infected with RNA (vesicular stomatitis virus [VSV], Sendai virus [SeV], and encephalomyocarditis virus [EMCV]), or DNA (herpes simplex virus [HSV-1], pseudorabies virus [PRV], and adenovirus [ADV]) viruses. This system demonstrated that both RNA and DNA viruses enhanced IFI208 promoter activity (Fig. 1A). Consistent with these results, synthetic low- and high-molecular weight analogs of viral dsRNA, respectively LMW- and HMW-poly (I:C), the virus like repeated DNA analog poly(dA:dT), interferon stimulatory DNA (ISD), and IFN-α, all upregulated IFI208 promoter activity, while bacterial lipopolysaccharides (LPS), recognized by a different range of receptors, did not (Fig. 1B). Of note, IFN-α upregulated IFI208 promoter activity in a time dependent manner (Fig. S1A). In keeping with the regulation of IFI208 by IFN, Jaspar software predicted multiple binding sites for two proteins of the IFN pathway, STAT1 and STAT2, on IFI208 promoter (Fig. S1B–C). Based on the location of these sites, we constructed three reporters with truncated IFI208 promoters to identify which regions were critical for IFI208 activation (Fig. S1D). This experiment revealed a synergic effect of the three truncated regions, as depletion of either of them resulted in a drastic loss of promoter activation (Fig. S1E). Next, we compared IFN-α responsiveness of IFI208 promoter to those of the promoters of major ISGs, i.e., ISG56, ISG54, IRF3, IRF7, and ISRE. IFI208 promoter responded to IFN-α at a level comparable to that of ISG54, the response of which had the lowest amplitude compared to those of ISG56, IRF3, IRF7, and ISRE (Fig. S1F). The pattern of Ifi208 mRNA upregulation in mouse leukemic monocyte macrophage cell line (RAW264.7) infected with VSV, SeV, EMCV, HSV-1, or PRV (Fig. 1C) or exposed to LMW-poly(I:C), HMW-poly(I:C), or poly(dA:dT) (Fig. 1D) mirrored that of IFI208 promoter activation, confirming a transcriptional regulation of Ifi208 by type I IFN. A kinetics study of Ifi208 mRNA and IFI208 protein induction at 0, 4, 8 or 12 h, with these same stimuli, in RAW264.7 and peritoneal cavity macrophages (PerCMs) isolated from wild type mice further demonstrated mRNA and protein inducibility of IFI208 by viral infections and type I IFN (Fig. S2A–L). To further assess the dependency of IFI208 upregulation on type I IFN inducing pathway and direct IFN-α signaling, we compared mRNA and protein induction of this gene in PerCMs from interferon alpha/beta receptor alpha chain deficient (Ifnar1-/-) and sufficient (Ifnar1+/+) mice (Fig. 1E–H). Upon viral infection or RNA/DNA analogs, only Ifnar1+/+ cells responded by upregulating IFI208 expression depends on IFNAR1, whereas in Ifnar1-/- cells, the expression of IFI208 remained unchanged (Fig. 1E–H). Similarly, IFN-α induced IFI208 mRNA and protein in Ifnar1+/+ macrophages, but not in Ifnar1-/- (Fig. 1I, J). This effect of IFN-α through IFNARI was specific, as it was blocked in Ifnar1+/+ macrophages by anti IFNAR1 and anti IFNAR2 antibodies (Fig. 1K). Thus, IFI208 expression is upregulated in response to IFN-α or different stimuli depending on IFNAR1 and IFNAR2 signaling, suggesting a role for IFI208 in host antiviral responses.

Next, we compared the dose responses of Ifi208 transcriptional activation to those of other known ISGs (Isg56, Isg54, Isg15 and Ifi16) in mice PerCMs stimulated with HMW-poly(I:C) or poly(dA:dT) at 0.01, 0.1, or 1 μg/mL or infected with EMCV or HSV-1 at 0.01, 0.1, or 1 multiplicity of infection (MOI) for 12 h. In terms of fold induction, Ifi208 mRNA upregulation surpassed that of all ISGs assessed, with both synthetic agonists and EMCV. However, Ifi208 mRNA was upregulated to a lesser extent than Isg56 mRNA with HSV-1, while its induction remained similar to that of Isg15 and Ifi16 mRNAs (Fig. S3A–D). Thus, Ifi208 mRNA could serve as a marker of type I IFN pathway activation under agonist stimulation or viral infection.

To explore the influence of IFI208 level on host antiviral responses, we generated a cervical carcinoma cell line (HeLa cells) overexpressing stably Flag-IFI208 (HeLa-IFI208) or an empty control vector (HeLa-E.V), by lentiviral transfection. Monitoring of Ifnb1 mRNA induction upon EMCV or HSV-1 infection in both lines showed that overexpressed IFI208 promoted a significant increase in Ifnb1 mRNA level (Fig. S4A and D), which correlated with decreased viral replication (Fig. S4B and E), and higher levels of IRF3 and TBK1 phosphorylation (Fig. S4C and F). This experimental system also indicated that overexpressed IFI208 successfully blocked the replication of the fluorescent reporter viruses, green fluorescent protein (GFP)-VSV and GFP-HSV-1 (Fig. S4G). Of note, the results of an IFN sensitivity assay also showed that IFI208 promoted IFN-I production induced by SeV infection in the human embryonic kidney cell line (HEK293T) (Fig. S4H). Altogether, these results indicated that IFI208 is an ISG that is positively regulated by type I IFN and promotes type I IFN production itself, thereby inhibiting virus replication.

IFI208 deficiency reduces cellular antiviral responses

To study the role of IFI208 in type I IFN production and antiviral responses in vivo, we generated Ifi208 knockout (Ifi208 -/-) mice by removing the second exon of the gene by homologous recombination. The correct excision of this exon and absence of gene product were verified, respectively, by PCR genotyping and western blot analysis (Fig. S5A–C). Upon treatment with HMW-poly(I:C), LMW-poly(I:C), or poly(dA:dT), or upon infection with EMCV, VSV, or HSV-1, primary bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) from Ifi208-/- showed lower mRNA expression of Ifnb1, Isg56, and Il-6 than their Ifi208+/+ counterparts (Fig. 2A–C). A kinetics study during infection of PerCMs with EMCV or HSV-1 confirmed the inability or lower ability or Ifi208-/- PerCMs to induce Ifnb1, Isg56 and Il-6 mRNA during infection (Fig. 2G–I and 2K–M). The impaired production of cytokine mRNAs correlated with higher viral load in Ifi208-/- than in Ifi208+/+ PerCMs (Fig. 2J and 2N). Thus, IFI208 deficiency resulted in decreased production of type I IFN, ISGs, and inflammatory cytokines, which enabled viral replication.

To further delineate the contribution of IFI208 to type I IFN production and antiviral immunity in vivo, we infected 6-week-old Ifi208+/+ and Ifi208-/- mice with HSV-1 or EMCV by intraperitoneal injection, and monitored their survival. This experiment showed that Ifi208-/- mice had a significantly higher susceptibility to HSV-1 and EMCV than Ifi208+/+ mice (Fig. 3A and G). Consistently, at 48 and 72 h post infection, the target organs of HSV-1 (liver and spleen) or EMCV (brain and heart) from the Ifi208-/- mice contained significantly lower amounts of Ifnb1 mRNA than those of the Ifi208+/+ mice (Fig. 3B, C and 3H–I). These results held true at protein level, when comparing serum IFN-β in infected Ifi208-/- and Ifi208+/+ mice (Fig. 3D and J). In keeping with the default of IFN-β induction in Ifi208-/- mice, three days after infection, the viral titers in the target organs of these mice were higher than those in Ifi208+/+ mice (Fig. 3E and K). Moreover, while infected Ifi208+/+ mice displayed slight to mild histopathological changes in their target organs, Ifi208-/- mice had severe histopathological changes (Fig. 3F and 3L). IFI208 is transferred from the nucleus to the cytoplasm by XPO1

We noticed that IFI208 is a nuclear protein. To determine the cellular localization of IFI208 before and after viral infection, HeLa-IFI208 were infected with VSV or HSV-1 for 0, 4, 8, 12 h, and the nuclear cytoplasmic separation results showed the IFI208 undergoes translocation from the nucleus to the cytoplasm during viral infection (Fig. 4A and 4B). Additionally, we also found that endogenous IFI208 undergoes translocation from the nucleus to the cytoplasm during viral infection in RAW264.7 (Fig. 4C). The results of immunofluorescence staining corroborated that IFI208, which is overexpressed in HeLa cells or endogenously present in RAW264.7, predominantly localizes within the nucleus, with a minor presence in the cytoplasm under resting conditions. The nuclear export protein exportin 1 (XPO1) is currently the only identified export protein that functions by recognizing specific signals. XPO1 can recognize hydrophobic nuclear export signals (NES) that are rich in leucine, and it mediates the transport of proteins between the nucleus and cytoplasm with the involvement of the GTPase Ran (RanGTP)17. We predicted that the C terminus of IFI208 contains multiple NES using the LocNES NES prediction tool by Chook Lab (Fig. 4F)18.

Co-immunoprecipitation (CoIP) assays with tagged proteins showed that XPO1 could interact with IFI208 (Fig. S6A). This result was confirmed with endogenous XPO1 and IFI208 in mouse PerCM infected with either VSV or HSV-1, Immunoglobulin G (IgG) as the negative control, and PTBP1 as the positive control known to bind XPO1 (Fig. S6B)19. The Chromosomal Maintenance 1 (CRM1) repeat domain of XPO1 is responsible for the recognition of and binding to NES sites20. We sought to identify the domain of XPO1 responsible for the binding of IFI208 by constructing three deletion mutants of XPO1 (XPO1-X1, XPO1-X2 and XPO1-X3) (Fig. S6C). This demonstrated that the N terminus domain (XPO1-X1, amino acid [aa] 1–344) and the middle fragment containing the CRM1 repeat domain (XPO1-X2, aa 345–601) of XPO1 could mediate XPO1 and IFI208 interaction, but not its C-terminus domain (NXPO1-X3, aa 602–1070), containing a CRM1 domain (Fig. S6C). Reciprocally, five truncated mutants of IFI208 (IFI208-F1 to IFI208-F5) were constructed to define the domain involved in XPO1 binding (Fig. S6D). This strategy revealed the C-terminal region of IFI208, present only in IFI208-F3 and IFI208-F5 mutants and essential for its co-precipitation with XPO1, as binding regions for XPO1 CRM1 repeat domain. To investigate the potential co-localization of XPO1 and IFI208 during viral infection, we monitored the localization of IFI208 and endogenous XPO1 in HeLa-IFI208 cells at 4 h post viral infection, using immunofluorescence microscopy. IFI208 and XPO1 exhibited extensive co-localization within the nucleus and cytoplasm (Fig. S6E). Notably, endogenous IFI208 and XPO1 were also found to significantly co-localization within the nucleus and cytoplasm at 4 h post viral infection in RAW264.7 cells (Fig. S6F).

To investigate the role of XPO1 in virus-induced nuclear and cytoplasmic translocation of IFI208, we treated HeLa-IFI208 or RAW264.7 with Selinexor, the unique FDA-approved oral XPO1 inhibitor21. prior to infection with VSV or HSV-1. Western blot analysis indicated that Selinexor had markedly impeded the nuclear cytoplasmic translocation of IFI208, compared to vehicle-treated cells (Fig. S7A–D). This result was further confirmed by direct visualization of IFI208 using immunofluorescence microscopy. In HeLa cells infected with GFP-VSV or GFP-HSV-1, IFI208 remained predominantly within the nucleus following Selinexor treatment, whereas it translocated to the cytoplasm in untreated infected cells (Fig. S7E). Thus, chemical inhibition targeting XPO1 disrupted the nuclear and cytoplasmic translocation of IFI208 induced by viruses.

To formally demonstrate that XPO1 facilitated the nuclear cytoplasmic translocation of IFI208, we endeavored to generate stable XPO1-deficient RAW264.7 and HeLa cell lines. However, our attempts to obtain homozygous Xpo1 knockout cells were unsuccessful, likely due to the critical roles played by XPO1 in various cellular processes. Therefore, we recursed to small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) transfection to target XPO1 in HeLa-IFI208 cells (Fig. 4G), which were subsequently infected with VSV or HSV-1 and analyzed 12 h after post infection. In cells treated with siRNAs targeting XPO1, the cytoplasmic translocation of IFI208 during viral infection was markedly inhibited, compared with control siRNA (Fig. 4H). Similar observations were made by immunofluorescence microscopy, demonstrating that siRNA targeting of XPO1 markedly impeded the nuclear and cytoplasmic translocation of IFI208 in HeLa-IFI208 cells upon GFP-VSV or GFP-HSV-1 infection, compared to cells treated with control siRNAs (Fig. 4I). Thus, XPO1 engages with IFI208 and facilitates the nuclear and cytoplasmic translocation of IFI208 during viral infection.

IFI208 promotes MDA5 recognition of dsRNA and activates oligomerization

To delineate the domain of IFI208 associated with type I IFN upregulation, full-length or five truncated IFI208 proteins (IFI208-F1 to IFI208-F5) were ectopically expressed in HEK293T cells. Upon infection with EMCV or HSV-1, only IFI208-F3 and IFI208-F5 retained the same ability as the full length protein to enhanced the activation of an IFN-β promoter reporter gene (Fig. S8A–B). Similarly, IFI208-F3 and IFI208-F5 could increase Ifnb1 mRNA levels to the same extent as full length IFI208 upon stimulation with HMW-poly(I:C) or poly(dA:dT) (Fig. S8C–D). Thus, the C-terminal domain of IFI208, present in IFI208-F3 and IFI208-F5 but not in IFI208-F1, IFI208-F2, or IFI208-F4, is essential to promote type I IFN production.

To clarify which downstream targets of IFI208 could mediate its effect on IFN-β production, we used Co-IP assays to examine the interaction between IFI208 and known effectors of the type I IFN pathways, i.e., cGAS, STING, RIG-I, MDA5, MAVS, TBK1, and IRF3. Among these candidates, IFI208 interacted with MDA5 (Fig. 5A). Immunofluorescence analysis further established that IFI208 colocalized with MDA5 in the cytoplasm of RAW264.7 (Fig. 5D). To investigate which domains on IFI208 and MDA5 were responsible for their interactions, we tested which truncated IFI208 proteins would co-precipitate with MDA5. We found that MDA5 interacted uniquely with protein forms containing IFI208 C-terminal domain (Fig. S9A). MDA5 contains three structural domains: a CARD domain, which initiates downstream signaling via homotypic binding with the CARD of MAVS; a DExD/H helicase domain acting as an intracellular sensor or receptor for viral RNA; a C-terminal RNA binding domain22. We generated five deletion mutants of MDA5 to delineate its binding domain for IFI208. All five MDA5 mutants were capable of co-precipitating with IFI208. However, the two truncated mutants MDA5-D1 and MDA5-D4, encompassing the CARD domain and the DExD/H helicase domain plus the RNA binding domain, respectively, exhibited higher recovery, suggesting enhanced affinity of these protein forms for IFI208 compared to other mutants (Fig. S9B).

As IFI208 does not possess a characteristic viral nucleic acid binding HIN 200 domain like the other members of the IFI200 family14,15, we investigated whether IFI208 could still bind LMW-poly(I:C) or HMW-poly(I:C), and influence the ability of RIG-I or MDA5 to recognize dsRNA. RNA pull-down assays demonstrated that IFI208 exhibited no binding capacity for LMW-poly(I:C) or HMW-poly(I:C) (Fig. 5E–F). However, IFI208 seemed to increase the amount of MDA5 pulled down by HMW-poly(I:C). In keeping with its inability to co-precipitated with RIG-I (Fig. 5A), IFI208 did not influence the amount of RIG-1 pulled down by LMW-poly(I:C) (Fig. 5E–F). Furthermore, we observed that IFI208 potentiated the binding of MDA5 to the EMCV’s genomic RNA (Fig. 5G). In PerCM from Ifi208-/- mice, the recognition of the EMCV genome by endogenous MDA5 was strongly impaired compared to Ifi208+/+ mice (Fig. 5H). These results implied that IFI208 played a role in potentiating dsRNA recognition by MDA5.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the self-association of MDA5 2CARD is concentration dependent and driven by oligomerization, leading to the formation of oligomers larger than tetramers. After MDA5 activation, its 2CARD binds to MAVS CARD domain, resulting in MAVS oligomerization, downstream signaling, and IFN induction22,23. Therefore, we analyzed whether IFI208 affected RIG-I and MDA5 oligomerization. IFI208 promoted the oligomerization of MDA5 induced by HMW-poly(I:C), but had no effect on LMW-poly(I:C)-induced RIG-I oligomerization (Fig. 5I–J). Comparison of the level of MDA5 oligomerization in HMW-poly(I:C) stimulated or EMCV-infected PerCM from Ifi208+/+ and Ifi208-/- mice further demonstrated the role of IFI208 in promoting MDA5 oligomerization, as this MDA5 oligomers were strongly reduced in the absence of IFI208 (Fig. 5K–L). Analysis by immunofluorescence showed that upon infection with EMCV or stimulation with HMW-poly(I:C), MDA5 formed punctate aggregated colocalized with cytoplasmic IFI208 in Ifi208+/+ PerCM, whereas these aggregates were absent in IFI208 in Ifi208-/- PerCM activated in the same conditions (Fig. 5M). Altogether, these results support that IFI208 acts as a coreceptor of MDA5 that promotes its oligomerization MDA5 and facilitates dsRNA recognition.

Next, we examined the ability of IFI208 to promote the formation of membrane less macromolecular aggregates of MDA5 by LLPS using the program PONDR. This strategy predicted a largely unfolded intrinsically disordered region (IDR) within IFI208 and MDA5 proteins (Fig. S10A–B). To test whether IFI208 and MDA5 could undergo LLPS in vitro, we produced recombinant GFP-conjugated IFI208 (GFP-IFI208) and mcherry-conjugated MDA5 (mcherry-MDA5) fusion proteins (Fig. S10C). Increasing concentrations of purified GFP-IFI208 or mcherry-MDA5 proteins resulted in an increasing number of GFP-IFI208 and mcherry-MDA5 droplets. On the contrary, GFP or mCherry proteins produced from the GFP-empty vector (GFP-E.V) and the mCherry-empty vector (mCherry-E.V) failed to aggregate (Fig. S10D). For GFP-IFI208 at 15 μM, extended incubation time in Polyethylene glycol 8000 (PEG 8000) increased the size of the aggregates progressively from nearly undetectable small foci to regular droplets, and then to larger droplet clusters (Fig. 6D–E). To investigate whether IFI208 foci formed via LLPS, we monitored the dynamic of molecular exchanges in these foci by FRAP. This analysis revealed a post photobleaching recovery time of 10 min., suggesting the existence of a liquid state and local rapid exchange rate for IFI208 proteins (Fig. 6F–G). Thus, in vitro, IFI208 can undergo LLPS, which may be enhanced upon EMCV infection.

Mixing mcherry-MDA5 with GFP-IFI208 promoted the formation of regular droplets and larger droplet clusters, whose number and size were further enhanced by the addition of HWM-poly(I:C) (Fig. S10E–F). Moreover, the number and size of mixed mcherry-MDA5 and GFP-IFI208 droplets increased to some extent under increasing NaCl concentrations (Fig. 6H–I). Finally, we also observed that the addition of HMW-poly(I:C) provoked a dynamic fusion of GFP-IFI208 and mCherry-MDA5 droplets to form bigger aggregates (Fig. 6J). This series of experiments led us to propose that IFI208 facilitates LLPS of MDA5 in a manner that is contingent on the presence of HMW-poly(I:C).

IFI208 functions via MDA5 mediated signaling pathway

To confirm that IFI208 acted through MDA5 mediated signaling pathway, we used siRNA to knockdown MDA5 expression in HeLa-IFI208 cells (Fig. S11A). In keeping with the previous results, reduced MDA5 expression suppressed IFN-β mRNA and protein induction by EMCV or HSV-1 (Fig. 7A–C and E–G)24,25. Concomitantly, knocking down MDA5 markedly impaired IFI208’s ability to potentiate Ifnb1 mRNA upregulation and IFN-β secretion during viral infections (Fig. 7A–C and E–G). Importantly, the stable expression of IFI208 in HeLa cells resulted in a reduction of approximately 2 logarithmic units in the titers of EMCV or HSV-1. However, this effect was partly reversed if MDA5 expression was knocked down (Fig. 7D and H). This suggests that the knockdown of MDA5 significantly impaired IFI208’s antiviral activity. Discussion

HIN-200 proteins are primarily nuclear proteins that are involved in the transcriptional regulation of genes important for cell cycle control, differentiation, and apoptosis26,27. In this study, we found that viral infections can induce the upregulation of IFI208 expression via the IFNAR1 and IFNAR2 signaling. As a positive regulator of type I IFN production induced by DNA and RNA virus infections, IFI208 inhibited viral replication. Consistently, the absence of IFI208 significantly impaired the induction of downstream antiviral genes activated by DNA and RNA viruses, leading to increased susceptibility of Ifi208-/- mice to DNA and RNA infections. Therefore, we identified IFI208 as an ISG. During viral infection, IFI208 translocated to the cytoplasm through XPO1, where it interacted with MDA5, thereby enhancing MDA5’s oligomerization and capacity to recognize double-stranded RNA (dsRNA). Ultimately, we confirmed that IFI208 induced LLPS of MDA5 in a poly(I:C) dependent manner. Thus, IFI208 functions as an auxiliary receptor that enhances MDA5’s recognition of dsRNA through LLPS, indicating that the XPO1-IFI208-MDA5 axis is critical during innate responses to DNA and RNA viruses (Fig. 8A).Fig. 8The proposed model of the XPO1-IFI208-MDA5 axis facilitates the innate immune response triggered by viral infection.A The schematic illustrates the mechanism by which IFI208 facilitates MDA5 recognition of dsRNA through LLPS, thereby augmenting innate immune responses triggered by DNA and RNA viruses. Our investigation revealed that RNA and DNA viruses can lead to the upregulation of IFI208 expression, which depends on the IFNAR1 and IFNAR2 signal. Furthermore, we observed that treatment with various type I IFN pathway agonists and model viruses in RAW264.7 cells resulted in a significantly higher level of mRNA upregulation of Ifi208 when compared to previously identified ISGs, such as Isg56, Isg54, Isg15, and Ifi16, particularly under conditions involving HMW-poly(I:C), poly(dA:dT) and EMCV treatment. These findings suggest that IFI208 may serve as a prominent marker for the activation of the IFN signaling pathway. Consistent with these results, Ifi208-/- mice exhibited a marked impairment in type I IFN production in response to infections with HSV-1 and EMCV, resulting in heightened susceptibility to viral infections compared to Ifi208+/+mice. These results further imply that IFI208 may function as an ISG with antiviral activity.

During the nuclear export process, RanGTP binds to XPO1, causing a conformational change that exposes its hydrophobic groove, which contains a NES binding site. This change enables cargo proteins carrying NES domains to bind XPO1 and form a stable ternary complex, which in turn leads to activation and conformational changes. The activated complex then associates with the nuclear pore complex to transport the cargo protein to the cytoplasm17. We predicted and confirmed that the C terminus of IFI208 contained multiple NES using the LocNES NES prediction tool. Additionally, we found that XPO1 chemical inhibition with Selinexor or knockdown by siRNA blocked the nuclear and cytoplasmic translocation of IFI208. Further investigation revealed that the XPO1-IFI208-MDA5 axis played an important role in virus-induced innate responses.

We noticed that IFI208 enhanced MDA5’ ability to recognize its ligand, thereby facilitating MDA5 oligomerization. Previous studies have shown that HSV-1 and EMCV can activate MDA5 signaling24,25,28, this supports the joint action of IFI208 and MDA5 during EMCV and HSV-1 infection. In this study, we found that IFI208 interacted with MDA5, suggesting that IFI208 may augment the activity of MDA5 in type I IFN signaling. VSV and L WM-poly(I:C) are primarily recognized by RIG-I25, which activates downstream signaling pathways, while HSV-1 mainly relies on the cGAS-STING signaling pathway to initiate type I IFN production29,30. We found that IFI208 deficiency resulted in decreased production of type I IFN, ISGs, and inflammatory cytokines, which enabled viral replication (VSV, EMCV, and HSV-1). Nevertheless, the present study focuses exclusively on elucidating the role of IFI208 in MDA5-mediated signaling. The mechanisms through which IFI208 regulates key proteins within other antiviral signaling pathways remain to be elucidated and warrant further investigation.

These studies indicate that key molecules in the innate immune signaling pathways can regulate the host’s innate immune response through LLPS, which is vital for maintaining the balance of the host’s antiviral innate immune response. For example, TRIM25 can bind to RNA through its PRY/SPRY domain, triggering LLPS and subsequently recruiting RIG-I for condensation31. MAVS can undergo LLPS both in vitro and in cells, which is a key step in the signaling of RLRs12. cGAS-DNA can form droplets through LLPS10, and STING condensates can spatially separate STING-TBK1 from IRF3, thereby preventing excessive activation of the innate immune response11. IRF3 undergoes liquid-liquid phase separation in the nucleus, and the formation of IRF3 with ISRE-DNA condensates is crucial for the activation of the innate immune response32. In this study, we found that IFI208 can undergo LLPS both in vivo and in vitro, and IFI208 can promote LLPS of MDA5 in a poly(I:C) dependent manner, indicating that IFI208-MDA5 forms macromolecular aggregates in cells, which is crucial for MDA5’s recognition of dsRNA. However, whether the droplets formed by MDA5 during viral infection require the involvement of other post translational modification proteins still needs further research. Our study demonstrated the ability of IFI208 to promote the formation of LLPS by MDA5 which promotes the antiviral function of the host’s innate immune response.

In summary, we identified IFI208 as an ISG. During viral infection, XPO1 mediates the nuclear and cytoplasmic transport of IFI208, which then binds to MDA5 in the cytoplasm to promote its binding to dsRNA and oligomerization, acting as an auxiliary partner for MDA5 to enhance MDA5 mediated IFN signaling. This study enriched the regulatory network related to the innate immune response involving HIN200 families in hosts. Importantly, we showed that IFI208 drove the LLPS of MDA5, which is crucial for the activation of innate immune signaling pathway.

Methods

Facility and ethics statements

This study was performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the Ministry of Science and Technology of the People’s Republic of China. The protocols were approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of the Harbin Veterinary Research Institute (HVRI) of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (CAAS) (Approval numbers: 241011-03-GR).

Mice

C57BL/6 Ifi208 knockout mice or wild type mice were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (Su Zhou, China) Inc. Mice (male and female, all C57BL/6 background) were used at 6-week-old, unless otherwise specified. Mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle, with a controlled room temperature of 22 ± 2 °C and relative humidity of 55 ± 5 %. CO₂ gas was used for euthanasia. The protocols were approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of the HVRI of the CAAS. Four single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting the third exon of the Ifi208 gene were designed as follows: gRNA-A1(GATTCCATTGTTTACCTTCT-GGG), gRNA-A2 (GCACCATGAAA GGCATAG AT-GGG), gRNA-B1 (TTCCCTTTTGTGAAGTTACT-GGG), and gRNA-B2 (TG TGACTCCAGCACCATGAA-AGG). These sgRNAs were synthesized by GenScript. The Cas protein and sgRNAs were microinjected into fertilized eggs derived from C57BL/6 J mice. Subsequently, the fertilized eggs were transferred into the oviducts of pseudo-pregnant female mice to generate the F0 generation. Positive F0 mice were then crossed with wild-type C57BL/6 J mice to produce F1 heterozygous offspring (Ifi208+/-). Interbreeding of F1 heterozygous mice yielded F2 homozygous knockout mice (Ifi208-/-). The primers used for genotyping were as follows: Forward primer 1: 5’-CTGATAAGTGTATACATCAGGACC-3’, Reverse primer 1: 5’-CAGGCTGTGAAAGGTCATATCC-3’; Forward primer 2: 5’-CATCTAT GCCTTTCATGGTGCTGGAG-3’, Reverse primer 1: 5’-CAGGCTGTGAAAGGTCATATCC-3’.

Cells and viruses

HEK293T, HeLa, and RAW264.7 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; reference C11995500CP, GIBCO, Grand Island, NE, USA). The mouse PerCMs and the primary BMDMs were cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; 10091-148, GIBCO), 100 U penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The well-known IFN-inducer Sendai/Cantell strain (SeV strain, product code VR-907), was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and amplified in specific pathogen free eggs. The VSV and VSV-GFP were kindly provided by Prof. Zhigao Bu (Harbin Veterinary Research Institute [HVRI], China). HSV-1 was kindly provided by Prof. Chunfu Zheng (Fujian Medical University, China), GFP-HSV-1 was kindly provided by Prof. Xiaojun Wang (HVRI, China).

Confocal microscopy

The cells were transfected with the indicated plasmids and then fixed for 10 min with 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) pH 7.4. The fixed cells were permeabilized for 15 min with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS and then blocked in PBS with 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min. The cells were incubated with the appropriate primary antibodies and then stained with Alexa Fluor 488-labeled goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G, Alexa Fluor 633 goat anti-rabbit IgG(H + L), or Alexa Fluor 594-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG. IFI208, MDA5, and XPO1 localization was visualized using a Zeiss LSM-880 laser scanning fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany) under a 63 × oil objective. IFI208, MDA5, and XPO1 co-localization was quantified using Zeiss processing system software.

Co-IP and western blot assay

For Co-IP, cells were lysed in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, and 10% glycerol) containing 1 mM Phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and a protease inhibitor cocktail (4693132001; Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Then, cell supernatants were incubated with anti-Flag (M2) agarose or with protein G Plus-Agarose immunoprecipitation reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) together with 1 μg of the indicated antibodies at 4 °C overnight on a roller. The pellets were washed five times with cell lysis buffer. For western blot analysis, 20% amounts of cell lysates and immunoprecipitants were resolved by 10–12% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and were then transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). After incubation with primary and secondary antibodies, the membranes were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) or an Odyssey two-color infrared fluorescence imaging system (LI-COR). Western blots were quantified with Image Studio.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

HEK293T cells were cotransfected with the indicated plasmids. After 24 h, the cells were harvested and lysed in lysis buffer, and luciferase activities of IFI208-, IFN-β-, ISG54-, ISG56-, IRF3-, IRF7-, ISRE-reporters, and TK-Renilla reporter were measured with a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (E1910; Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The data were normalized to the transfection efficiency by dividing the firefly luciferase activity by the Renilla luciferase activity. Each experiment was conducted independently three times. Representative results are shown.

RNA pulldown

HEK293T cells were cotransfected with Flag-tagged IFI208, MDA5 or RIG-I for 24 h or the mouse peritoneal macrophages were infected with EMCV. Then, the cells were lysed in lysis buffer containing PMSF and a cocktail of protease inhibitors as above. Cell supernatants were incubated with biotinylated LMW-poly(I:C), HMW-poly(I:C) (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) or EMCV genomic dsRNA for 12 h at 4 °C, and then incubated with streptavidin beads (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for another 4 h at 4 °C. The beads were washed three times with lysis buffer and analyzed by immunoblotting with the indicated antibodies.

Isolation of macrophages

PerCMs were isolated from mice four days after injection of thioglycolate (BD Biosciences, Franklin, NJ, USA) and then cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. BMDMs were prepared from bone marrow cells isolated from mice femur cultured continuously for 10 days in medium and conditions as above, with additional macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) (50 ng/mL), to promote mononuclear cells differentiation into macrophages.

Generation of HeLa-IFI208 stable cell line

The Flag-tag and Flag-tagged mouse Ifi208 gene were cloned into the pLVX-ISRE-Puro vector (Clontech). The HeLa cells were infected with the lentiviral particles expressing Flag-tag or Flag-tagged mouse Ifi208 gene in the presence of 8 mg/mL polybrene. Three days post-infection, the cells were treated with 1 μg/mL puromycin for two weeks. The Flag-tag or Flag-tagged IFI208 overexpression efficiency was assessed by western blot, and the cell line was named HeLa-E.V, for empty vector, or HeLa-IFI208.

FRAP assays

FRAP assays were conducted using a Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope. Purified proteins or GFP-IFI208-expressing HeLa cells were bleached with a 488 nm laser at 20% laser power for 1 second. Delayed images were captured approximately 10 minutes after bleaching. The fluorescence intensity of the regions of interest (ROIs) was corrected using unbleached control areas and normalized to the intensity of the ROI before bleaching.

In vitro phase separation assay

Purified proteins were in buffers containing 20 mM Tris HCl (pH 7.0) and specified NaCl concentrations. Protein aliquots (5 μl) were loaded onto glass slides, covered with a coverslip and imaged using an LSM880 confocal microscope (Zeiss). After mixing recombinant GFP-IFI208 and mcherry-MDA5 proteins with poly(I:C), phase separation was performed in the corresponding NaCl concentration buffer.

Purification of recombinant proteins

After optimization of the construction and methods, DH5α E. coli bacteria were transformed with a pET-32a plasmid carrying the open reading frame (ORF) encoding the recombinant target proteins (GFP-IFI208 or mcherry-MDA5). The cells were precultured overnight at 37 °C in LB medium containing ampicillin (Amp+). Twenty milliliters of this overnight cultured was further grown in 200 mL LB medium containing Amp+. When the cultured reached the exponential phase (optical density at 600 nm (OD600) = 0.6–0.7), protein expression was induced by adding 1 mM Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) for 20 hours at 16 °C. Cells were then collected and lysed by ultrasound, and the protein was purified using a nickel column according to the protocol of the AKTA chromatography system manufacturer.

ELISA

The concentrations of IFN-β in serum were measured by ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Mouse IFN-β ELISA kit (EMC016) was purchased from NeoBioscience Technology (Shenzhen, China).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 8.0.2), Adobe Photoshop 2020 was used to create figures and BioRender was used for making the Schematic diagram (https://biorender.com). The results were statistically evaluated using Two-tailed unpaired t-test or One-way ANOVA. The value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
