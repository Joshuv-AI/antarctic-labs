Lipase from Stenotrophomonas maltophilia strain HO5 for efficient biodiesel synthesis using non-edible plant oils

ABSTRACT
The increasing global demand for sustainable and renewable energy has intensified interest in microbial lipases as efficient biocatalysts for biodiesel production. A lipase-producing bacterium isolated from waste-oil-contaminated environments was identified as Stenotrophomonas maltophilia strain HO5 through 16 S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing. The strain exhibited a maximum lipase production of 28 U mL⁻¹. The purified monomeric enzyme (approximately 55 kDa) showed a Michaelis constant (Km) of 1.728 mM and a maximum reaction velocity (Vmax) of 80 U mL⁻¹, indicating high catalytic efficiency. The enzyme retained 112.05% residual activity in the presence of ferric ions and 113.10% activity in methanol, while ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid caused strong inhibition (> 80%), suggesting partial metal ion dependence. The enzyme efficiently catalyzed the conversion of non-edible wild olive oil and taramira oil into biodiesel, confirmed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy through characteristic ester carbonyl stretching peaks. Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry analysis revealed fatty acid methyl esters ranging from C8 to C24. Wild olive oil biodiesel was dominated by methyl oleate (48.53%), followed by methyl linoleate (21.64%) and methyl palmitate (14.85%), whereas taramira oil biodiesel contained higher proportions of methyl linoleate (32.81%), methyl oleate (22.37%), and methyl erucate (18.72%). The predominance of unsaturated methyl esters confirms efficient enzymatic transesterification and highlights the catalytic robustness and industrial potential of this lipase for sustainable biodiesel production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55459-z.

Introduction

The increasing global demand for sustainable and renewable energy sources has intensified interest in biodiesel as an environmentally friendly alternative to fossil fuels1. Biodiesel consists of fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) produced through transesterification of triglycerides present in vegetable oils or animal fats with short-chain alcohols, typically methanol2. In this reaction, the glycerol backbone of triglycerides is replaced by alcohol molecules, yielding biodiesel and glycerol as a by-product3. Although chemical catalysts are widely used for this process, they often require harsh reaction conditions, generate soap by-products and require complex downstream purification4. In contrast, lipase-catalyzed transesterification offers significant advantages, including biodegradability, high substrate specificity, and reduced energy requirements. In addition, lipases can operate under mild conditions while tolerating diverse feedstocks such as waste oils and oils with high free fatty acid content5. These properties make lipases particularly attractive for sustainable biofuel production.

The selection of appropriate feedstocks is equally critical for the economic viability of biodiesel production. Non-edible oils provide cost-effective and sustainable alternatives to edible oils, avoiding competition with food resources6. Wild olive (Olea oleaster) and taramira (Eruca sativa) oils are promising candidates due to their availability and adaptability to marginal lands7. Wild olive is extensively distributed in the hilly and semi-arid regions of Pakistan, including Azad Kashmir, Gilgit-Baltistan, Balochistan, Karak, Swat, Dir, Chitral, and North and South Waziristan8. Taramira, an annual herb cultivated in Pakistan, India, and parts of the Middle East, is recognized for its high oil content and suitability for biodiesel applications9. The utilization of such non-edible feedstocks enhances sustainability while reducing production costs9.

Microbial lipases, particularly those of bacterial origin, offer several advantages for industrial applications, including ease of isolation, rapid growth on inexpensive media, genetic amenability, and high enzyme yields10. Lipases from species such as Bacillus subtilis and Kocuria flava have been successfully applied in biodiesel synthesis from Pongamia and waste cooking oils11. However, despite the recognized catalytic versatility of the genus Stenotrophomonas, there remains limited information regarding the application of S. maltophilia lipases in biodiesel production12. To date, no comprehensive study has demonstrated the use of methanol-tolerant S. maltophilia lipase for efficient biodiesel synthesis from non-edible oils.

Recent studies report that methanol tolerance in lipases can be enhanced through metabolic engineering approaches such as directed evolution and glycosylation modifications13. These engineered enzymes often show improved stability and activity in high methanol conditions, enabling efficient biodiesel production. This suggests such tolerance is often enhanced through artificial modification rather than occurring naturally in many microbial lipases. In contrast, the present study demonstrates a naturally occurring S. maltophilia strain HO5 lipase with intrinsic methanol tolerance without any genetic engineering, highlighting its novelty and potential for biodiesel production from non-edible oils under mild reaction conditions.

In this regard, we report the isolation, characterization, and catalytic evaluation of a methanol-tolerant lipase from S. maltophilia strain HO5 and demonstrate its effectiveness in producing biodiesel from wild olive and taramira oils under mild reaction conditions. This work provides new insights into the biotechnological potential of S. maltophilia lipase as a sustainable alternative to conventional chemical catalysts for biodiesel production.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and bacterial isolation

A total of 15 oil-contaminated samples (11 soil and 4 water) were collected from different regions of Pakistan, including Islamabad (33.6844° N, 73.0479° E), Rawalpindi (33.5651° N, 73.0169° E), Swat (35.2220° N, 72.4258° E), Dir (35.2058° N, 71.8756° E), and Gilgit-Baltistan (35.8026° N, 74.9839° E) (Fig. 1A). The distribution of samples collected from each location is shown in Fig. 1B. Each sample was serially diluted and aseptically spread onto nutrient agar (2.5%) plates for bacterial isolation. Distinct colony morphologies and pigmentation patterns were observed during the isolation process (Figure S1). Pure cultures were obtained using the streak plate method (Figure S2). Detailed information on all samples, including labeling, habitat, location, and type, is provided in Table S1.

Primary and secondary screening of lipase-positive bacterial isolates

Primary screening was performed using tributyrin agar (TBA) medium. Purified bacterial isolates were spot-inoculated onto TBA plates and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Lipase activity was assessed based on the ratio of hydrolysis zones14. Secondary screening of lipase-positive isolates was then conducted using both submerged fermentation (SmF) and solid-state fermentation (SSF)15,16.

Lipase enzyme and protein estimation

Lipase activity was determined using p-nitrophenyl palmitate (pNPP) as the substrate, with minor modifications17, and absorbance was recorded at 410 nm. One unit (U) of lipase activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to release 1 µmol of p-nitrophenol per minute under the assay conditions, and enzyme activity was expressed as U mL⁻¹. Protein concentration in the crude filtrate was determined using the Bradford colorimetric assay18.

Identification of selected lipase-positive bacterial isolates

The selected lipase-positive bacterial isolates were characterized based on colony morphology, including shape, margin, surface, elevation, texture, optical properties, and pigmentation19. Biochemical tests (Gram staining, catalase, oxidase, Voges–Proskauer, methyl red, motility, and carbohydrate fermentation) were performed according to standard protocols20.

Genomic DNA of the optimum lipase-producing bacterial strain HO5 was extracted using the phenol–chloroform method, and molecular identification was performed through amplification of the 16 S rRNA gene using universal primers 27 F and 1492R21. The amplified product was confirmed on a 1% agarose gel, followed by purification using a QIAquick PCR purification kit (QIAGEN, Germany), and sequencing was performed by Macrogen (Seoul, Korea). Sequence assembly and phylogenetic analysis were conducted using BioEdit version 7.1.9 and MEGA-7, respectively. Multiple sequence alignment of reference GenBank sequences was performed using ClustalW and MUSCLE (n = 12). Pseudomonas sp. was used as the outgroup, and a neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree was constructed with 1000 bootstrap replicates22.

Optimization, purification, and characterization of lipase

The optimal growth and fermentation conditions for lipase production were determined using a one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) experimental design. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). To determine the optimal incubation period, lipase activity was assayed at 8-hour intervals from 24 to 96 h. Maximum extracellular lipase production was observed at 64 h, after which enzyme activity gradually declined, possibly due to nutrient depletion and proteolytic degradation23. Therefore, 64 h was selected as the optimal incubation time for subsequent experiments. The effects of different culture media (M1–M524–28; Table S2), incubation time, inoculum size (0.5–1.5%), shaking speed (100–200 rpm), carbon and nitrogen sources, pH (5–10), and temperature (30–55 °C) were systematically evaluated to optimize lipase production by Stenotrophomonas maltophilia strain HO5.

Purification of the optimized crude lipase was carried out using ammonium sulfate precipitation, dialysis, and Sephadex G-100 gel filtration chromatography. The molecular weight of the purified enzyme was estimated by SDS-PAGE followed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 staining29.

Purified lipase from S. maltophilia strain HO5 was characterized for enzyme activity across different pH values (2.0–11.0), temperatures (20–70 °C), and pNPP concentrations (0.5–1.4 mM). Kinetic parameters (Km and Vmax) were determined from a Lineweaver–Burk plot. For stability assays, pre-incubation was performed at 45 °C in Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0), and the effects of metal ions, organic solvents, surfactants, and inhibitory agents were evaluated relative to an untreated control30.

Biodiesel production and quantification of conversion efficiency

Transesterification was used to evaluate biodiesel production using Stenotrophomonas maltophilia lipase as a biocatalyst31. Briefly, 10 mL of refined vegetable oil was mixed with methanol at an oil-to-methanol molar ratio of 1:3, and the reaction was initiated by adding 50 U of immobilized S. maltophilia lipase. The reaction mixture was incubated at 40 °C with gentle shaking for 24 h. After completion, the reaction was terminated, and the biodiesel phase was separated by centrifugation. Fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) were quantified using gas chromatography (GC) equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID) and a capillary column. Lipase-catalyzed transesterification for biodiesel synthesis

Biodiesel production was carried out using non-edible wild olive oil (WWO) and taramira oil (TMO). The free fatty acid (FFA) content of both oils was determined by acid–base titration according to32. Transesterification reactions were performed in a 100 mL two-neck round-bottom flask equipped with a reflux condenser and magnetic stirring. A total of 40 mL of preheated oil was used as the substrate. Methanol was added at an oil-to-methanol molar ratio of 1:3, corresponding to 4.8 mL methanol per 40 mL oil. Reaction parameters, including methanol-to-oil ratio, enzyme loading, temperature, water content, and reaction time, were selected based on previously reported optimized conditions for bacterial lipase-mediated biodiesel production33,34 and further validated through preliminary laboratory trials; therefore, the reactions were conducted under fixed conditions.

Lipase (20 U/mL) was added at a final enzyme loading of 5% (v/v) relative to the oil volume. The reaction mixture was incubated at 40 °C under continuous stirring at 250 rpm for 24 h. To minimize lipase inhibition by excess methanol, the methanol was added in three equal portions at 0, 8, and 16 h. The water content of the reaction mixture was maintained at approximately 5% (w/w), which is considered favorable for maintaining lipase activity and structural stability. After completion of the reaction, the mixture was allowed to settle for phase separation, and the upper biodiesel layer was collected for analysis. Functional group confirmation of fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) was performed using Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Bruker Tensor 27) over the range of 4000–500 cm⁻¹.

Biosafety and containment measures

Given that S. maltophilia is recognized as an opportunistic pathogen, appropriate biosafety measures were implemented for laboratory-scale and potential industrial applications. All experimental procedures in this study were conducted under Biosafety Level 2 (BSL-2) conditions, including the use of certified biological safety cabinets for aerosol-generating steps, appropriate personal protective equipment, and strict aseptic handling35. Culture materials and waste were sterilized by autoclaving prior to disposal to ensure containment.

Statistical analysis

The results of lipase activity from S. maltophilia strain HO5 were expressed as mean ± SD. All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. Statistical significance of optimized parameters was evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Least Significant Difference (LSD) test, performed using Statistix 8.1 software. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Optimization of lipase from S. maltophilia strain HO5

SmF experiments were conducted to optimize lipase production by S. maltophilia strain HO5. Among the tested fermentation media, M2 medium showed the highest lipase activity (6.39 U mL⁻¹; p = 0.00117; Fig. 4A). Time-course analysis revealed maximum lipase production (6.4 U mL⁻¹; p = 0.00898) at 48 h (Fig. 4B). An inoculum size of 1% resulted in the highest enzyme activity (9.3 U mL⁻¹; p = 0.00849; Fig. 4C), while an agitation speed of 150 rpm further enhanced lipase production to 13.5 U mL⁻¹ (p = 0.00265; Fig. 4D).

Among the tested carbon sources, olive oil significantly enhanced lipase production (13.6 U mL⁻¹; p = 0.00104; Fig. 4E), with an optimal concentration of 1% yielding 16.2 U mL⁻¹ (p = 0.00358; Fig. 4F). Ammonium sulfate was identified as the most effective nitrogen source, producing 20.5 U mL⁻¹ lipase activity (p = 0.00113), which further increased to 23.2 U mL⁻¹ at 1% concentration (p = 0.00514; Fig. 4G–H). The maximum lipase production (27.8 U mL⁻¹; p = 0.00308) was observed at pH 8 (Fig. 4I), and the highest enzyme activity (32.2 U mL⁻¹; p = 0.00108) was recorded at 45 °C (Fig. 4J).

Purification of lipase from S. maltophilia strain HO5

The precipitation of crude enzyme with ammonium sulfate ((NH₄)₂SO₄) at 40% saturation resulted in a lipase yield of 78.43%, with 2.67-fold purification and a specific activity of 13.66 U mg⁻¹. Subsequent dialysis yielded 63.26% recovery, with 4.74-fold purification and a specific activity of 24.25 U mg⁻¹. Gel filtration chromatography produced a single peak of enzymatic activity (Fig. 5A), indicating the absence of isoenzymes, and resulted in 39.13% yield, 8.38-fold purification, and a specific activity of 42.85 U mg⁻¹. The active fractions were collected, and the overall purification profile is summarized in Table S6.

SDS-PAGE analysis revealed that the purified lipase from S. maltophilia strain HO5 was a homogeneous monomeric protein with an approximate molecular weight of 55 kDa (Fig. 6).

Among the tested metal ions, Fe³⁺ (1 mM) increased residual lipase activity to 112.05% (p = 0.00301), whereas Ag⁺ acted as a strong inhibitor, reducing residual activity to 15.04% (Fig. 9A). The purified lipase also exhibited significant methanol tolerance (p = 0.00351), retaining 113.10% activity at 5% methanol (Fig. 9B).

Conventional oils (WOO, TMO) screening for biodiesel production

In the FTIR spectrum of wild olive oil (WOO) (Fig. 10), characteristic peaks were observed at 3008.01, 2921.72, 2851.86, 1460.86, 1378.67, 1234.84, 756.11, and 721.18 cm⁻¹, corresponding to aliphatic C–H stretching and bending vibrations. A strong absorption band at 1742.34 cm⁻¹ confirmed the presence of ester carbonyl (C = O stretching). Additional bands at 1158.82, 1095.13, and 869.12 cm⁻¹ were attributed to C–O stretching and = C–H bending vibrations, indicating the presence of esterified lipid structures in WOO.

Similarly, the FTIR spectrum of taramira oil (TMO) (Fig. 11) showed peaks at 3005.96, 2919.66, 2855.91, 1460.86, 1376.62, 1238.95, and 721.18 cm⁻¹, corresponding to aliphatic C–H stretching vibrations. The absorption band at 1744.40 cm⁻¹ was attributed to ester carbonyl (C = O stretching), while the peak at 1000.24 cm⁻¹ was assigned to C–O stretching vibrations.

Biodiesel Production Efficiency and Methanol Tolerance of S. maltophilia Lipase

The efficiency of S. maltophilia HO5 lipase in catalyzing biodiesel production was systematically evaluated by varying enzyme concentration, methanol content, and incubation time (Tables 1 and 2). The results demonstrate that enzyme concentration and incubation time are key factors influencing biodiesel yield. At 30 U enzyme loading, biodiesel yields ranged from 63 ± 2.1% to 69 ± 2.4%, with the lowest conversion observed at 20% methanol after 12 h, and a slight increase upon extending the reaction to 24 h. Increasing methanol concentration to 30% slightly reduced yields at the same enzyme loading, indicating mild methanol inhibition (Table 2). At higher enzyme loading (50 U), biodiesel production significantly improved, with a maximum yield of 89 ± 3.4% achieved at 20% methanol after 24 h. Even at 30% methanol, the enzyme maintained substantial catalytic activity, producing 81–83% biodiesel.

Overall, a clear trend was observed: increasing enzyme concentration and reaction time enhanced biodiesel conversion, whereas higher methanol concentrations moderately inhibited activity. These findings highlight the robustness of S. maltophilia lipase, which tolerates up to 30% methanol, operates under mild reaction conditions (40 °C, 24 h), and achieves high conversion efficiency from non-edible oils. The observed yields are comparable with previously reported bacterial lipases, demonstrating the potential of this enzyme for industrial biodiesel production using sustainable feedstocks.

FTIR analysis of synthesized biodiesel (LOBD)

In FTIR analysis, good transesterification was established. The synthesized LOBD biodiesel (Figure. 12) had a characteristic (C = O ester stretching) with an infrared shift at 1748.43 cm− 1, and LTMBD biodiesel (Figure. 13) had the peak at 1742.34 cm− 1 with an infrared shift. The overall spectrum of each synthesized biodiesel showed a reduction in O-H (3400–3500 cm− 1) and an increase in C-O (1000–1300 cm− 1), suggesting the formation of esters and low free fatty acids, respectively.

GC–MS analysis of fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs)

GC–MS analysis confirmed the successful conversion of WWO and TMO into FAMEs, with eighteen compounds (C8–C24) identified based on retention time and mass spectral matching (similarity index > 90%). WWO-derived biodiesel was dominated by methyl oleate (48.53%), followed by methyl linoleate (21.64%) and methyl palmitate (14.85%), with minor fractions of methyl stearate (6.24%), methyl palmitoleate (3.26%), and methyl linolenate (3.48%). In contrast, TMO biodiesel contained a higher proportion of polyunsaturated and long-chain FAMEs, primarily methyl linoleate (32.81%), methyl oleate (22.37%), and methyl erucate (18.72%), along with notable amounts of methyl behenate (4.86%) and methyl gondoate (3.75%) (Table 3). The predominance of unsaturated methyl esters in both samples confirms efficient lipase-catalyzed transesterification and reflects the native fatty acid profiles of the respective feedstocks. Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive biochemical and catalytic characterization of S. maltophilia strain HO5 lipase and demonstrates its applicability in biodiesel production from non-edible oils, highlighting its potential as a sustainable industrial biocatalyst. The strain was isolated from an oil-contaminated environment, a niche that selectively enriches lipolytic microorganisms capable of utilizing lipids as a carbon source. Among 26 lipase-positive isolates, HO5 exhibited the highest hydrolytic index (5.18 ± 0.46), indicating strong extracellular lipase production and validating the effectiveness of tributyrin agar-based primary screening.

Secondary screening under submerged fermentation revealed a maximum activity of 6.30 U mL⁻¹, suggesting efficient inducible expression in the presence of olive oil. Homogeneous dispersion of lipid substrates under shaking conditions likely enhanced interfacial activation of lipase and transcriptional upregulation of lipase-encoding genes36,37. Subsequent optimization demonstrated that M2 medium supported maximal enzyme production at 48 h, confirming the importance of balanced ionic composition and lipid induction. NaCl, MgSO₄, and CaCl₂ likely contributed to osmotic balance and structural stabilization, while olive oil functioned as both inducer and substrate38,39.

Optimization of process parameters revealed that a 1% inoculum size maximized enzyme production (9.3 U mL⁻¹), indicating that an optimal biomass density prevents nutrient depletion and metabolic stress40. Agitation at 150 rpm improved oxygen transfer and substrate dispersion, resulting in peak lipase activity (13.5 U mL⁻¹), whereas excessive agitation likely imposed shear stress on the cells. Similarly, 1% olive oil supported maximum enzyme induction (13.6 U mL⁻¹), while higher concentrations may have reduced oxygen solubility and caused substrate inhibition39. Nitrogen optimization demonstrated that (NH₄)₂SO₄ increased lipase activity to 20.5 U mL⁻¹, highlighting the importance of coordinated carbon–nitrogen metabolism in enzyme biosynthesis.

Physicochemical optimization indicated an alkaline preference (pH 8) and moderate thermophilic behavior (45 °C), yielding activities of 27.8 and 32.2 U mL⁻¹, respectively. These conditions favor efficient substrate–enzyme interactions while preserving structural integrity, characteristics desirable for industrial biotransformations41.

Purification resulted in 56.52% recovery and 9.08-fold purification, with SDS–PAGE revealing a single 55 kDa band, consistent with reported bacterial lipases (40–70 kDa range). Kinetic analysis showed a Km of 1.728 mM and Vmax of 80 U/ml, indicating high substrate affinity and catalytic efficiency. The enzyme retained > 100% relative activity at pH 8.0 and 45 °C, confirming structural stability under optimized conditions. Enhancement of activity in the presence of Fe³⁺ (112.05%) suggests metal ion-mediated stabilization of the catalytic conformation, whereas strong inhibition by EDTA confirms its metalloenzyme nature. Surfactant-mediated activation (Triton X-100, 117%) likely reflects improved substrate emulsification and increased interfacial accessibility34.

Methanol tolerance is a critical determinant for enzymatic biodiesel synthesis42. Notably, HO5 lipase retained 113.10% residual activity at 5% methanol, demonstrating structural resilience in alcohol-rich environments. This property is particularly significant because methanol commonly disrupts enzyme conformation and reduces catalytic efficiency during transesterification reactions42.

The catalytic competence of purified HO5 lipase was validated in biodiesel production assays using non-edible feedstocks43. Systematic evaluation of enzyme loading, methanol concentration, and reaction time revealed that biodiesel yield is strongly dependent on enzyme concentration and incubation duration. At 30 U enzyme loading, yields ranged from 63 ± 2.1% to 69 ± 2.4%, with moderate inhibition observed at 30% methanol. Increasing enzyme loading to 50 U significantly enhanced conversion efficiency, achieving a maximum yield of 89 ± 3.4% at 20% methanol after 24 h. Importantly, even at 30% methanol, biodiesel yields remained between 81 and 83%, indicating only mild alcohol-induced inhibition. These findings demonstrate that HO5 lipases maintain catalytic efficiency under relatively high methanol concentrations and mild reaction conditions (40 °C, 24 h), comparing favorably with previously reported bacterial lipases and supporting its industrial relevance13,42.

Compared to conventional alkaline catalysis, which typically requires elevated temperatures, strong chemical catalysts, and generates soap by-products in high free fatty acid feedstocks, the HO5 lipase-mediated process operates under milder conditions (40 °C, neutral-to-alkaline pH) with minimal downstream purification requirements44. Furthermore, reported bacterial lipases often exhibit reduced stability beyond 10–20% methanol, whereas HO5 lipase maintained high conversion efficiency even at 30% methanol44. The observed biodiesel yield (89%) is comparable to or higher than several previously reported microbial lipase systems, underscoring its competitive catalytic performance.

Chemical validation of transesterification was confirmed by both FTIR and GC–MS analyses45,46. FTIR spectra revealed characteristic ester carbonyl (C = O) and C–O stretching vibrations, confirming fatty acid methyl ester formation. GC–MS profiling identified eighteen fatty acid methyl esters (C8–C24) with similarity indices above 90%, indicating efficient substrate conversion. Wild olive oil biodiesel was dominated by methyl oleate (48.53%), followed by methyl linoleate (21.64%) and methyl palmitate (14.85%), whereas taramira oil biodiesel contained higher proportions of polyunsaturated and long-chain esters, including methyl linoleate (32.81%), methyl oleate (22.37%), and methyl erucate (18.72%). The predominance of C16–C18 methyl esters in both biodiesel samples is advantageous for fuel quality, contributing to optimal viscosity, improved combustion characteristics, and favorable cold-flow properties47,48. The compositional profiles closely reflected the intrinsic fatty acid composition of the respective feedstocks, supporting selective and efficient lipase-mediated transesterification rather than nonspecific chemical conversion49.

The opportunistic pathogenic nature of S. maltophilia necessitates careful consideration of biosafety implications in both research and potential industrial applications50. Although the present findings highlight the strong catalytic potential of HO5 lipase, appropriate handling and containment of live cultures remain essential to minimize occupational and environmental risks51. The implementation of proper biosafety measures, including controlled laboratory conditions and effective waste decontamination, is critical when working with such organisms52. For future large-scale applications, alternative approaches such as heterologous expression of the lipase gene in non-pathogenic hosts or the use of immobilized enzyme systems may improve operational safety while maintaining catalytic performance53. These considerations are important to ensure that the biotechnological advantages of the enzyme are balanced with safe and sustainable process development.

Lipase immobilization is an effective strategy for improving operational stability, facilitating enzyme recovery, and enabling repeated reuse in biodiesel production54. Techniques such as adsorption, covalent binding, entrapment, and cross-linked enzyme aggregates can enhance methanol tolerance and long-term catalytic performance55. The HO5 lipase exhibited alkaline stability, moderate thermostability, strong methanol tolerance, and high catalytic efficiency, achieving high biodiesel yields under mild reaction conditions. Its industrial applicability could be further improved through immobilization, reusability studies, and pilot-scale process optimization.

Conclusion and future recommendations

The study demonstrates that S. maltophilia strain HO5 is a highly effective source of lipase with high kinetic characteristics, chemical stability, and the ability to produce biodiesel in high yield using non-edible oils, making it a promising eco-friendly biocatalyst. The study provides an understanding of cleaner biodiesel production, which involves the use of plant-based raw materials feedstock and the biocatalyst lipase. Although pilot-scale trials verified the potential of S. maltophilia strain HO5-derived lipase, further research is required to maximize lipase stability, reusability, biodiesel yield, explore efficient non-edible feedstocks, and assess the environmental and economic viability of large-scale biodiesel production. Future studies should focus on enzyme immobilization techniques to enhance operational stability and repeated use, genetic or metabolic engineering approaches to improve lipase productivity, and process optimization in continuous bioreactor systems for industrial application. In addition, comprehensive life-cycle assessment, techno-economic analysis, and engine performance testing of the produced biodiesel are recommended to validate its commercial sustainability and practical applicability.
