Lactate, mean platelet volume, and nSOFA as combined predictors of perforated necrotizing enterocolitis and mortality in preterm infants: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Dabin Huang, Yunnan Xiao, Yuanlong Fang, Fei Li, Jiazhang Chen, Jiahao Lai, Yaoxiang Tang, Liling Yang, Shangjie Xiao, Hu Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerforated necrotizing enterocolitis (PNEC) is a lethal complication in preterm infants with high mortality, but timely identification and prognostic stratification remain clinically challenging.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed 110 preterm infants with surgical NEC admitted between 2023–2025, stratified into non-intestinal perforation (NIP group, n = 55) and PNEC (n = 55) subgroups. Correlation, multivariate logistic regression, mediation, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed.ResultsCompared with the NIP group, the PNEC group had significantly higher lactate (6.0 vs. 3.9 mmol/L), mean platelet volume (MPV, 12.20 vs. 10.79 fL), neonatal Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (nSOFA, 5.0 vs. 2.0), and mortality (27.3% vs. 10.9%) (all p < 0.05). Multivariate regression identified lactate (OR = 1.218) and MPV (OR = 1.725) as independent risk factors for PNEC. Mediation analysis showed lactate exerted 32.53% indirect effect on PNEC via MPV, and nSOFA mediated 23.95% of lactate’s effect on mortality. The combination of lactate + MPV + nSOFA outperformed single markers in predicting PNEC (AUC = 0.800) and mortality (AUC = 0.949).ConclusionsThe combination of lactate, MPV, and nSOFA exhibits synergistic predictive value for PNEC and its associated mortality. Lactate and MPV identify early perforation risk, while nSOFA stratifies mortality risk. This combination provides clinical utility for the early intervention of high-risk neonates with NEC.

Introduction

Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) remains the most lethal gastrointestinal emergency in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), disproportionately affecting very low birth weight (VLBW, <1500 g) and extremely low birth weight (ELBW, <1000 g) preterm infants [1,2]. The global incidence of NEC ranges from 2% to 12% in neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) populations [3], with mortality rates exceeding 30% in severe cases [4]—especially when complicated by intestinal perforation, which necessitates urgent surgical intervention and is associated with long-term sequelae such as short bowel syndrome, neurodevelopmental impairment, and growth failure [5–7]. Clinically, NEC is stratified into medically managed (non-surgical) NEC and surgical NEC based on disease severity and treatment strategy [8]. Notably, not all surgical NEC cases involve intestinal perforation. The mortality rate of surgical NEC exceeds 15% [8], while the risk of death in patients who progress to perforated NEC (PNEC), the most lethal subtype of surgical NEC, increases significantly to 30–76% [9–12].

Despite advances in neonatal care, the timely identification of preterm infants at high risk of progressing to PNEC and fatal outcomes remains a critical unmet clinical need. Current clinical approaches for diagnosing and stratifying NEC severity rely primarily on the modified Bell’s staging system. However, Bell’s staging often lacks sensitivity in the early phases of NEC, and its ability to predict progression to perforation or mortality is limited [13]. Accumulating evidence highlights the multifactorial pathophysiology of PNEC, which involves three core processes: intestinal ischemia–hypoxia and metabolic dysregulation, excessive inflammatory response and platelet activation, and progressive multi-organ dysfunction [14]. Single biomarkers targeting only one of these pathological cascades inevitably have inherent limitations in sensitivity or specificity, and cannot achieve accurate risk stratification across the full course of PNEC.

Serum lactate, as a metabolic marker of persistent hypoxemia and impaired tissue perfusion, reflects the severity of ischemic injury and predicts fatal outcomes in NEC [15]. However, its sole use lacks specificity, as hyperlactatemia may also be associated with asphyxia or sepsis in preterm infants [16]. Recent studies have confirmed that serum lactate, when used in combination with immuno-inflammatory biomarkers (e.g., intestinal fatty acid-binding protein or albumin) [17,18], exhibits favorable predictive value for the severity of NEC. This suggests that lactate is not merely a byproduct of anaerobic metabolism, but may also be involved in regulating the immunomodulatory function in NEC. The Complete Blood Count (CBC), a readily accessible auxiliary indicator in NICUs, has been validated such that dynamic changes in its platelet parameters and immune cell subsets serve as valuable biomarkers for NEC [19]. Among these parameters, mean platelet volume (MPV), a surrogate marker of platelet activation, directly reflects the degree of platelet aggregation and pro-inflammatory state. However, MPV alone is insufficient to predict the mortality of PNEC, as it mainly reflects the inflammatory and thrombotic state rather than the systemic organ dysfunction that directly determines survival outcomes.

During the progression of NEC to severe disease with intestinal perforation, the condition can shift from a regionally restricted intestinal inflammatory disease to progressive multiorgan dysfunction syndrome, which ultimately results in life-threatening outcomes in preterm infants. As a validated clinical assessment tool, the neonatal Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (nSOFA) score is specifically constructed to provide an objective quantification of the extent of organ dysfunction in critically ill neonates, with its core evaluation covering the respiratory, cardiovascular, and hematologic systems [20]. Recent clinical studies have verified the potential predictive value of the nSOFA score for mortality risk in critically ill preterm infants with late-onset sepsis, respiratory distress syndrome, or necrotizing enterocolitis [20–22]. However, there remains a critical lack of clarity regarding the performance of standalone nSOFA scoring in PNEC-specific risk stratification. While existing literature has validated the favorable predictive value of nSOFA for mortality and the need for surgical intervention in unselected NEC cohorts, previous studies—most notably the pivotal work by Lewis et al.—have included all infants requiring surgical management for NEC, without isolating PNEC as a discrete study endpoint. This critical oversight has left an unaddressed gap in our understanding of whether nSOFA can effectively distinguish between surgical NEC with and without intestinal perforation [20]. More importantly, a single nSOFA score lacks sufficient sensitivity for early prediction of intestinal perforation, as multi-organ dysfunction often occurs after intestinal perforation, rather than preceding it.

To address the key clinical dilemmas and unresolved research gaps outlined above, we performed this retrospective cohort study to integrate and analyze metabolic markers, hematologic inflammatory indices, and the nSOFA score within a unified analytical framework. This study is expected to provide a cost-effective, widely accessible tool for early risk stratification of PNEC, and to guide timely clinical intervention to improve the survival outcomes of preterm infants with high-risk NEC.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was performed at The Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, spanning from January 2023 to June 2025. Eligibility screening was systematically conducted for neonates hospitalized with a diagnosis of NEC who had received surgical intervention during their admission. A total of 180 initial cases of surgically managed NEC were identified, among which 110 fulfilled the pre-specified inclusion criteria. These eligible participants were further allocated into two groups: the non-intestinal perforation group (NIP, n = 55) and the intestinal perforation group (PNEC, n = 55) (Figure 1). PNEC was defined as surgical NEC with intestinal perforation confirmed by intraoperative findings, with or without preoperative radiological evidence of pneumoperitoneum; the NIP group was defined as surgical NEC without intraoperative evidence of intestinal perforation, and no preoperative pneumoperitoneum on abdominal radiography. The protocol of this study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of The Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (approval No. 2026ZSLYEC-024) and registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07207915). Regarding informed consent: Due to the study’s retrospective nature, the IRB waived the requirement for written informed consent from participants or their legal guardians. The waiver was justified as: 1) data were extracted from de-identified hospital medical records (no personal identifiers), eliminating privacy risks; 2) the retrospective analysis involved no additional interventions or follow-up for participants, posing no harm to their rights or health. This waiver complies with the ethical guidelines for human research in the Declaration of Helsinki and the regulations of the hospital’s IRB.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Preterm infants with a gestational age of less than 35 weeks, diagnosed with NEC according to the ERNICA 2025 guidelines and requiring surgical intervention [8]. Specifically, Surgical NEC was defined as confirmed NEC (Bell stage ≥ IIa) with surgical indications including suspected or confirmed perforation, fixed bowel loop, clinical deterioration despite maximal medical therapy, or portal venous gas. In the PNEC group, intestinal perforation was confirmed intraoperatively. (2) Infants with complete clinical data and study parameters.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Preterm infants with a gestational age ≥ 35 weeks or term infants; (2) Infants diagnosed with inherited metabolic diseases; (3) Infants diagnosed with spontaneous intestinal perforation, congenital intestinal atresia, Hirschsprung’s disease, or other congenital gastrointestinal malformations; (4) Infants lacking core laboratory parameters required for the study.

Data collection

Structured data abstraction was conducted using a standardized case report form (CRF), with all relevant data retrieved from the electronic medical records of enrolled infants. Variables collected systematically fell into four primary domains: (1) Neonatal baseline and perinatal history, encompassing sex, birth weight, gestational age, 1-minute and 5-minute Apgar scores; (2) Maternal obstetric history, with a focus on delivery mode, presence of chorioamnionitis, and preterm rupture of membranes; (3) Clinical data specific to NEC disease course, including Bell’s staging classification, disease-related complications, treatment modalities received (invasive mechanical ventilation, surgical intervention), and survival outcomes; (4) Core laboratory and scoring parameters, consisting of CBC indices, circulating lactate concentrations, and all requisite variables for nSOFA score computation. All blood samples for these parameters were collected within 24 h after the clinical diagnosis of surgical NEC was established and strictly prior to any surgical intervention for all enrolled infants. The nSOFA score was derived by quantifying the presence of organ dysfunction (Table S1, online calculator: https://peds.ufl.edu/apps/nsofa/default.aspx/).

To mitigate sample selection bias, this study stringently adhered to pre-specified inclusion and exclusion criteria, thereby ensuring the representativeness of the enrolled participants. Data extraction was conducted independently by two investigators utilizing standardized CRF. For missing or ambiguous data records, a third independent party performed review and adjudication to preclude subjective selection bias. Subsequent to comparing baseline characteristics among the groups, variables exhibiting imbalances were incorporated into multivariate models for adjustment, with the aim of reducing confounding-associated bias.

Sample size calculation

The sample size of this study was determined with the primary outcome as the core reference, which was to differentiate whether intestinal perforation developed in neonates with surgically managed NEC. Using published data from Lewis et al. [20] and preliminary NEC cases from the research center, the expected intergroup Cohen’s d value was calculated as 0.8. Under the conditions of two-tailed α = 0.05 and statistical power (1–β) = 0.80, G*Power 3.1 software was used to determine that at least 40 patients per group were required. Considering a potential 10% data loss (due to incomplete electronic medical records or missing laboratory parameters, as noted in the exclusion criteria), the sample size was adjusted to 45 patients per group. A 1:1 group ratio was selected for this calculation as it provides the maximum statistical power for a given total sample size. The final cohort included 55 patients per group (PNEC group and NIP group). Post hoc validation was conducted using the R packages pwr and powerMediation confirmed that the actual statistical power for detecting the observed intergroup difference in nSOFA scores was 92%, and the power for the secondary outcome (mortality) was > 85%—consistent with the useful predictive value of nSOFA for NEC-related mortality reported in the provided document.

Statistical analysis

We conducted all statistical analyses and generated graphical plots using GraphPad Prism 10.0 and SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corporation) software. For data description, normally distributed continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation; non-normally distributed skewed data were presented as median (interquartile range); and categorical data were reported as counts and corresponding percentages. Intergroup comparisons were performed using the chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, while the Student’s unpaired t test or Mann–Whitney U test was adopted for continuous variables based on their distribution characteristics. Variables that reached statistical significance (p < 0.05) in univariate regression analysis were subsequently incorporated into a multivariate logistic regression model for further analysis. A complete-case analysis was performed for all secondary variables with missing data, and no data imputation was conducted in this study.

Smoothed curve fitting and threshold effect analysis were performed using EmpowerStats software (version 2.0) to explore the relationship between lactate levels or mean platelet volume (MPV) and outcomes (intestinal perforation or mortality) in surgical NEC. Mediation analysis was performed using PROCESS macro 4.1 to explore the causal pathway relationships between the variables [23]. The predictive efficacy of single biomarkers and the combined model of nSOFA, lactate, and MPV for PNEC occurrence and mortality was evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using two-tailed tests, and a p value of less than 0.05 was defined as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the preterm infants with surgical NEC

A total of 180 neonates diagnosed with surgical NEC were initially identified for eligibility evaluation during the study timeframe from January 2023 to June 2025. Among them, 110 preterm infants with a gestational age of less than 35 weeks met the inclusion criteria, with 55 assigned to the NIP group and 55 to the PNEC group. A CONSORT flow diagram is illustrated in Figure 1. The study population included 75 males (68.2%) and 35 females (31.8%). Of the enrolled infants with surgical NEC, 89 survived (80.9%) and 21 died (19.1%); notably, the mortality rate in the PNEC group (15 cases, 27.3%) was significantly higher than that in the NIP group (6 cases, 10.9%) (p = 0.029).

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the two groups are summarized in Table 1. No statistically significant between-group differences were detected with respect to gestational age, birth weight, age at onset of NEC, sex distribution, proportion of premature rupture of membranes, mode of delivery, small for gestational age (SGA) infants, intrauterine distress, requirement for mechanical ventilation, maternal chorioamnionitis, feeding pattern, and preterm complications (respiratory distress syndrome [RDS], patent ductus arteriosus [PDA], sepsis, and shock).

Next, we focused on the distribution of immune cells in the CBC and derived inflammatory indices. No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of immune cell counts (white blood cell [WBC], absolute lymphocyte [LYM], absolute neutrophil [NEU], eosinophilic granulocyte [EOS], absolute monocyte [MON], and red blood cell [RBC]) or inflammatory indices (C-reactive protein [CRP], monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio [MLR], neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio [NLR], neutrophil-to-monocyte ratio [NMR], systemic inflammatory response index [SIRI], systemic inflammatory index [SII], and aggregate inflammatory systemic index [AISI]) (all p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Notably, the platelet count (PLT) in PNEC patients was significantly lower than that in the NIP group (104.0 [56.5, 168.5] × 109/L vs. 184.0 [111.0, 294.5] × 109/L, p < 0.001), while the MPV and platelet distribution width (PDW) were significantly higher (MPV: 12.20 ± 1.39 fL vs. 10.79 ± 1.18 fL, p < 0.001; PDW: 17.2% [16.9, 17.5] vs. 16.7% [16.5, 17.0], p < 0.001) (Table 2).

We further evaluated metabolic indices in patients with surgical NEC and found that lactate levels were significantly elevated in the PNEC group (6.00 [4.0, 8.70] mmol/L vs. 3.90 [1.92, 5.40] mmol/L, p < 0.001), whereas blood glucose levels showed no significant difference between the two groups (Table 2).

Finally, we assessed organ dysfunction in patients with surgical NEC and found that the nSOFA score in the PNEC group was significantly higher than that in the NIP group (5.0 [2.0, 8.0] vs. 2.0 [0, 6.0], p = 0.001), suggesting that when NEC progresses to intestinal perforation, the condition worsens, accompanied by multiorgan dysfunction (Table 2).

Subsequently, multivariate logistic regression analysis identified lactate (odds ratio [OR] = 1.218, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.015–1.461; p = 0.034) and MPV (OR = 1.725, 95% CI 1.121–2.653; p = 0.013) as independent risk factors for the progression of severe NEC to intestinal perforation (Figure 2; Table S2).

No significant correlations were found between serum lactate and albumin, routine hematologic or inflammatory indices: albumin levels (r = −0.016, p = 0.867; Figure 3a), CRP levels (r = −0.071, p = 0.458; Figure 3b), WBC (r = 0.101, p = 0.294; Figure 3c), NEU (r = 0.116, p = 0.226; Figure 3d), LYM(r = 0.015, p = 0.874; Figure 3e), MON (r = 0.049, p = 0.611; Figure 3f), EOS (r = −0.062, p = 0.522; Figure 3g).

Notably, serum lactate exhibited significant correlations with platelet-related parameters: it was negatively correlated with PLT (r = −0.309, p = 0.001; Figure 3h) and positively correlated with MPV (r = 0.486, p < 0.001; Figure 3i) and PDW (r = 0.314, p < 0.001; Figure 3j). Correlation analysis was further performed to explore whether inflammation affects platelet-related parameters. We found that CRP was negatively correlated with PLT (r = −0.247, p = 0.009; Figure 3k). Importantly, PLT counts were negatively correlated with MPV (r = −0.381, p < 0.001; Figure 3l) or PDW (r = −0.195, p = 0.041; Figure 3m).

With respect to organ dysfunction, serum lactate levels were positively correlated with nSOFA scores (r = 0.617, p < 0.001; Figure 3n). Additionally, nSOFA scores showed a robust negative correlation with PLT counts (r = −0.609, p < 0.001; Figure 3o) and a strong positive correlation with MPV (r = 0.397, p < 0.001; Figure 3p).

Synergistic predictive value of lactate, MPV and nSOFA in the assessment of PNEC and its associated mortality

Smoothing curve fitting and threshold effect analysis confirmed a nonlinear relationship between lactate levels and mortality in preterm infants with surgical NEC (likelihood ratio test, LRT; p = 0.041), whereas MPV showed a linear association with intestinal perforation (LRT; p = 0.088) (Figure 4a,b; Tables S3 and S4). Specifically, for lactate, when the level was below the threshold of 7.4 mmol/L, each 1 mmol/L increase was associated with an elevated risk of death (OR = 2.91, 95% CI 1.43–5.93; p = 0.003). Regarding MPV, each 1-unit increase was associated with a 2.31-fold higher risk of intestinal perforation (OR = 2.31, 95% CI 1.60–3.32; p < 0.001).

To dissect the direct and indirect effects of lactate metabolism, platelet-related parameters (PLT, MPV), and nSOFA score on intestinal perforation and in-hospital death during the progression of PNEC, causal mediation analyses were performed (Figure 4c–f; Tables S4–S8).

Results showed that lactate exerted both direct and indirect effects on intestinal perforation: the total effect of lactate on intestinal perforation was significant (β = 0.040, 95% CI 0.021–0.054, p < 0.001), with 87.43% attributed to the direct effect (β = 0.034, 95% CI 0.014–0.051, p = 0.004) and 12.57% to the indirect effect mediated by PLT (β = 0.005, 95% CI 0–0.013, p = 0.048; Figure 4c; Table S5). In contrast, lactate also mediated its effect on intestinal perforation via MPV: the total effect was significant (β = 0.040, 95% CI 0.023–0.056, p < 0.001), with 67.47% as the direct effect (β = 0.027, 95% CI 0.005–0.045, p = 0.028) and 32.53% as the indirect effect through MPV (β = 0.014, 95% CI 0.005–0.024, p < 0.001; Figure 4d; Table S6).

Notably, the effect of lactate on death was partially mediated by nSOFA: the total effect was significant (β = 0.005, 95% CI 0.001–0.014, p < 0.001), with 76.05% as the direct effect (β = 0.004, 95% CI 0.001–0.011, p < 0.001) and 23.95% as the indirect effect via nSOFA (β = 0.001, 95% CI 0–0.005, p = 0.048; Figure 4e; Table S7). Further analysis revealed that nSOFA exerted a minor indirect effect on death through MPV: the total effect of nSOFA on death was significant (β = 0.008, 95% CI 0.002–0.019, p < 0.001), with 86.02% as the direct effect (β = 0.007, 95% CI 0.002–0.016, p < 0.001) and 13.98% as the indirect effect mediated by MPV (β = 0.001, 95% CI 0–0.005, p = 0.050; Figure 4f; Table S8).

Additionally, nSOFA, as an indicator reflecting organ dysfunction, may hold potential predictive value for PNEC. The individual nSOFA score exhibited moderate predictive performance for PNEC (AUC = 0.679, 95% CI 0.578–0.779, p = 0.001), with a sensitivity of 85.5% and specificity of 45.5%. In contrast, the combined biomarkers significantly improved predictive performance (AUC = 0.800, 95% CI 0.719–0.880, p < 0.001), with a sensitivity of 61.8% and specificity of 87.3% (Figure 4g; Tables S9).

Notably, the nSOFA score showed strong predictive performance for in-hospital mortality of PNEC (AUC = 0.893, 95% CI 0.831–0.955, p < 0.001), with a sensitivity of 90.5% and specificity of 84.3% (cutoff value = 6.50). Importantly, the combination of lactate, MPV, and nSOFA achieved the strongest predictive performance for PNEC-related mortality (AUC = 0.949, 95% CI 0.905–0.992, p < 0.001), with a sensitivity of 90.5% and specificity of 91.0% (Figure 4h; Tables S10).

Discussion

PNEC remains a lethal complication in preterm infants, with mortality rates ranging from 30% to 76% and long-term sequelae that severely impair prognosis [9–12,25]. Although neonatal care has seen substantial advances in recent decades, early and accurate identification of infants at elevated risk of PNEC and associated life-threatening outcomes continues to pose a significant clinical dilemma [26]. This retrospective cohort study fills key knowledge gaps by demonstrating the synergistic predictive value of lactate, MPV, and nSOFA score for PNEC and its associated mortality, while offering new insights into the putative pathophysiological pathways connecting these biomarkers.

Serum lactate emerged as a pivotal metabolic marker in our study, with significantly elevated levels in PNEC patients and independent predictive value for intestinal perforation (OR = 1.218, 95% CI 1.015–1.461, p = 0.034). This aligns with prior research linking lactate to tissue hypoxia and ischemic injury in NEC [15,27], but our mediation analysis adds depth by revealing potential dual associative pathways linking lactate to PNEC progression: 87.43% of its effect on perforation was direct, while 12.57% was mediated by PLT, and an even larger 32.53% was mediated by MPV. These results suggest that lactate not only reflects anaerobic metabolism from intestinal ischemia but also modulates platelet activation [28]—supported by its positive correlation with MPV (r = 0.486, p < 0.001) and negative correlation with PLT (r = −0.309, p = 0.001). Additionally, lactate’s effect on mortality was partially mediated by nSOFA (23.95% indirect effect), highlighting its role in linking metabolic derangement to multiorgan dysfunction, a precursor to fatal outcomes. This complements Wang et al.’s [15] observation that lactate predicts NEC prognosis but adds granularity by linking metabolic derangement to organ failure progression—a critical insight for understanding why hyperlactatemia precedes clinical deterioration in PNEC.

Among platelet-related parameters, MPV is noteworthy as an independent risk factor for PNEC (OR = 1.725, 95% CI 1.121–2.653, p = 0.013). This contrasts with conventional focus on PLT count (which was reduced in PNEC, p < 0.001) and underscores MPV’s role as a surrogate for platelet activation—an overlooked mechanism in NEC pathogenesis. Platelets are key mediators of intestinal inflammation and coagulation; activated platelets release proinflammatory cytokines and form microthrombi, exacerbating ischemic injury [29–31]. Our correlation analysis supports this: MPV was negatively associated with PLT (r = −0.381, p < 0.001) and positively linked to nSOFA (r = 0.397, p < 0.001), indicating that platelet activation (reflected by elevated MPV) correlates with organ dysfunction and perforation. Furthermore, MPV mediated 13.98% of nSOFA’s effect on mortality, suggesting it may bridge organ failure and fatal outcomes. This extends prior work on platelet parameters in NEC by demonstrating MPV’s superior specificity (94.5%) for PNEC [32]—addressing the limitation of PLT reduction, which is nonspecific to NEC (e.g., also seen in sepsis) [33].

The nSOFA score, a validated tool for quantifying neonatal organ failure [21], showed moderate predictive performance for PNEC (AUC = 0.679) but strong utility for mortality (AUC = 0.893), consistent with Lewis et al.’s [20] finding that nSOFA predicts NEC-related death but not specifically PNEC. However, our study provides critical new insights beyond this prior work: the landmark study by Lewis et al. did not distinguish between perforated and non-perforated surgical NEC. Our study advances this by demonstrating nSOFA’s mortality predictive power stems from its ability to capture multiorgan involvement: it correlated strongly with lactate (r = 0.617, p < 0.001) and PLT (r = −0.609, p < 0.001), reflecting the convergence of metabolic derangement and hematologic dysfunction in fatal cases [34]. Notably, nSOFA’s limited specificity for PNEC (45.5%) highlights a key distinction: intestinal perforation may precede overt multiorgan failure, making metabolic (lactate) and hematologic (MPV) markers more useful for early perforation detection, while nSOFA better identifies subsequent mortality risk.

The combination of lactate, MPV, and nSOFA achieved superior predictive performance for both PNEC (AUC = 0.800) and mortality (AUC = 0.949), outperforming any single biomarker. This synergy arises from their complementary nature: lactate reflects ischemic metabolism, MPV captures platelet activation [35], and nSOFA quantifies organ dysfunction [36]. For PNEC, the combination balanced sensitivity (61.8%) and specificity (87.3%), overcoming the limitations of individual markers: lactate’s high sensitivity (98.2%) but low specificity (41.8%) and MPV’s high specificity (94.5%) but low sensitivity (49.1%). For mortality, the combined AUC was 0.949 (sensitivity 90.5%, specificity 91.0%), demonstrating significantly higher predictive ability compared to other NEC prognostic biomarkers (e.g., lactate/albumin ratio, AUC = 0.725).

A key strength of our findings is the accessibility of the biomarkers: lactate and MPV are routinely measured in complete blood count panels, and nSOFA is calculated from bedside parameters (respiratory support, inotropes, PLT) [21]. This makes the trio feasible in resource-limited settings, with potential clinical applications including triggering urgent surgical evaluation (e.g., lactate > 2.85 mmol/L plus MPV > 12.45 fL) or intensive monitoring (e.g., nSOFA > 6.50 indicating high mortality risk). The combined lactate-MPV-nSOFA model could potentially be integrated into the daily clinical workflow of NICUs to facilitate standardized risk stratification, timely clinical decision-making, and personalized management for preterm infants with surgical NEC. Notably, this combined model is currently hypothesis-generating, and must be validated in prospective, multicenter cohorts before it can be widely implemented into routine clinical practice.

Several limitations exist in the present study. First, the retrospective single-center design of this investigation may lead to the introduction of selection bias (e.g., variable documentation of nSOFA components). Second, the sample size (110 preterm infants) limits subgroup analyses, particularly in extremely preterm infants (< 28 weeks’ gestation). Third, we lacked dynamic multi-time-point monitoring data, and were unable to evaluate whether the changing trends of biomarkers have better predictive performance than single time-point absolute values, including the core clinical question of whether a rising MPV over 12 h has greater predictive value for intestinal perforation than a single elevated value. Future prospective multicenter studies should systematically explore the dynamic trajectories of lactate, MPV, and nSOFA to further optimize the predictive model. Fourth, we did not account for potential confounders such as enteral feeding volume or antibiotic exposure duration (beyond 48 h pre-evaluation). Most importantly, the excellent discriminative performance of the combined lactate-MPV-nSOFA model observed in this single-center retrospective cohort requires further confirmation in well-designed, prospective, multicenter studies before it can be translated into clinical application. Additionally, integrating these markers with point-of-care ultrasound (e.g., for pneumatosis intestinalis) could further enhance PNEC detection. Finally, developing machine learning models to optimize composite biomarker performance would facilitate clinical adoption.

Conclusions

Lactate, MPV, and nSOFA exhibit synergistic predictive value for PNEC and its associated mortality in preterm infants with surgical NEC. Lactate and MPV are associated with risk of impending intestinal perforation in the early phase of surgical NEC progression (e.g., the lactate > 2.85 mmol/L and MPV > 12.45 fL cutoffs identified in this study may indicate a need for urgent surgical evaluation), while nSOFA correlates with subsequent mortality risk (e.g., nSOFA > 6.50 may signal elevated in-hospital mortality risk). These findings define a potentially effective clinical decision framework for the management of preterm infants with surgical NEC. While the combined model achieved excellent discriminative performance for PNEC (AUC = 0.800) and mortality (AUC = 0.949) in this single-center retrospective cohort, further prospective multicenter validation is required to confirm the generalizability of this combined predictive model before its clinical implementation.
