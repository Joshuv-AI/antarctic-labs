1.0 Introduction and Background
Interlune, Lisbon Group (a contractor to Interlune) and the Pacific Northwest National
Laboratory (PNNL) have evaluated the technical and economic feasibility of applying
magnetocaloric liquefaction (MCL) techniques to the isotopic separation of helium-3 (3He) from
the helium extracted from domestic Liquid Natural Gas (LNG) as a means to increase the
nation’s supply of 3He without requiring additional tritium production. The results are
summarized in this report, as well as the related report from PNNL, FWP-85288,
“Magnetocaloric Refrigeration for Helium Liquefaction Applied to Helium 3 Separation.”
(Archipley 2025) This latter report contains more detail on the application of MCL technology to
provide cooling power, whereas this report, RE-00004, focuses on the isotopic separation of 3He
and integration of MCL technology to assess technical and economic feasibility of the overall
system.

1.1 Summary
This final report summarizes the technical and economic feasibility study conducted by
Interlune, Lisbon Group, and Pacific Northwest National Laboratory on separating 3He from 4He
derived from liquid natural gas (LNG) using magnetocaloric liquefaction (MCL), superleak
heat-flush, and fractional distillation techniques. The project aims to address the growing
shortage of 3He, a critical resource for quantum computing and national security, by exploring a
new domestic source, the bulk helium supply.

The study found that separating 3He from LNG-derived helium is technically feasible and
potentially economically viable. Analysis of helium samples from North American sites revealed
potentially higher 3He concentrations than previously recorded, significantly enhancing the
economic prospects of extraction. A lab-scale prototype MCL system design was created to cool
helium gas down to 1.8 Kelvin temperature. This would then be flowed into a 3He enrichment
system, incorporating a two-step approach: pre-enrichment using a superleak and final
enrichment using cryogenic distillation. This design was supported by detailed system analysis,
component designs, and a concept of operations.

An economic feasibility analysis was conducted for a plant-scale system integrated into an
existing helium liquefaction facility. Sensitivity analysis was performed on input assumptions like
capital costs, operating life, discount rate, electrical power, maintenance costs, and helium leak
rate. Even under conservative assumptions, the production cost per liter of 3He was significantly
lower than the current market price, indicating commercial viability.

The report concludes that leveraging MCL technology for 3He separation from LNG-derived
helium is a promising pathway to increase domestic 3He supply. The report outlines a proposed
schedule and budget in a next phase for developing, building and testing a lab-scale prototype
on a three-year timeline and suggests further steps for pilot plant scale implementation.

1.1 Background
Since 2009, the Department of Energy Office of Isotope R&D and Production (DOE IP) has
been rationing the distribution of US-sourced 3He due to demand that would otherwise exceed
supply (Shea and Morgan, 2010). Recently, growth in superconducting quantum computing
research has spurred significant increases in demand for dilution refrigerators that use 3He to
reach the millikelvin temperatures required for superconducting qubit operation. 3He also
continues to be in demand for other applications including neutron detection for national
security, and research in areas such as low temperature physics, medical imaging, and fusion
energy. Millikelvin-level dilution refrigeration systems can only operate using 3He, and as a
result, 3He supply presents a bottleneck to further growth of the quantum computing industry.

In order to secure this important part of the quantum computing supply chain, new domestic
sources of 3He must be found. The most attractive near-term domestic source may be the
separation of 3He from LNG. Approximately 71 billion liters of helium were extracted from LNG
deposits in 2021 (USGS, 2022), containing 3He at concentrations of up to approximately 242
parts per billion (Shea and Morgan, 2010). Separating 3He from LNG-derived helium may
therefore present an opportunity for generating up to 17,000 liters of 3He per year, tripling
current annual domestic production.

Cooling and liquefaction of helium is a necessary step in separating 3He from 4He. Recently,
DOE-sponsored work at PNNL has developed magnetocaloric liquefaction technologies with the
objective of achieving large reductions in the total energy and capital costs required for
liquefaction of hydrogen. This technology has the potential to be applied to helium liquefaction
as well, and thus could make 3He separation from domestic helium much more economical.

Beyond 3He separation, this technology could yield substantial economic and environmental
benefits for helium production and delivery in general, by reducing the amount of energy
required for its liquefaction. Most helium is delivered as a liquid (National Research Council,
2010), and therefore reducing the energy required for liquefaction would have benefits for the
majority of helium produced.

As research into the isotopic separation of 3He to enable greater domestic supply, the project
aligns with the Department of Energy Office of Isotope R&D and Production’s mission to
“conduct research and development (R&D) to develop transformative isotope production,
separation, and enrichment technologies” and to “mitigate U.S. dependence on foreign supplies
of isotopes and promote robust domestic supply chains for U.S. economic resilience” as
described in the solicitation.

1.2 Prior Literature Review and State of the Art
This section details the review of available literature and state of the art on the subject matter
prior to the start of the project in June 2024, in order to provide context on how the project has
advanced prior work.

Interlune Corporation​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ 4

In the United States, the sole source of 3He production is Tritium beta decay. Tritium is a
byproduct of nuclear weapons and power production and an isotope of Hydrogen with one
proton and two neutrons. Tritium has a half life of approximately 12.3 years. Currently, all
domestic 3He is sourced from tritium beta decay and is processed at the National Isotope
Development Center Savannah River (National Isotope Development Center, 2023).

Prior to September 2001, annual 3He production was generally greater than the demand. The
combined effect of reduced nuclear weapons stockpiles after the end of the Cold War and
increased demand after 2001 for national security purposes resulted in a 3He shortage (Shea
and Morgan, 2010).

The presence of 3He in domestic 4He supplies has been well documented. Previously, the
Bureau of Mines (now Bureau of Land Management) performed 3He analysis at points along the
Bureau’s helium pipeline which connected various privately owned plants supplying crude
helium. These plants were located in Kansas, Oklahoma, Colorado, and Texas, and many
commercial Helium plants continue to operate in this area. The Bureau data showed 3He
concentrations of 109-200 ppb in crude Helium, with 3He/4He ratios ranging from 0.176-0.242 x
106 (Davidson and Emerson, 1990). Approximately 71 billion liters of helium were extracted from
LNG deposits in 2021 (USGS, 2022) making LNG-derived Helium an attractive potential source
of 3He, if the 3He can be separated cost effectively at scale.

Previous investigations of separating 3He from LNG using traditional cryogenic liquefaction
technologies showed that the cost of producing 3He using this approach was unlikely to be
economical, with results showing that costs are primarily driven by the energy required for
cryogenic cooling and separation. A study on the separation of 3He from LNG-derived 4He
showed that the energy required for cooling from room temperature to the 4He condensation
temperature of 4.2K represents approximately 70-75% of the total energy required for 3He
separation (Niechial et al, 2020).

Recently, DOE-sponsored work at the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) has
developed magnetocaloric liquefaction technologies with the objective of achieving both a 40%
reduction in the total energy required for liquefaction hydrogen, as well as a 25% reduction in
equipment cost (Archipley and Barclay, 2023). While currently focused on hydrogen, these
approaches can be extended to helium, enabling lower cost separation of both 3He and (as a
side benefit) liquefaction of 4He.

3
 He extraction from LNG has been successfully obtained on a small scale since the 1940’s.
Multiple steps must be undertaken to obtain isotopically pure 3He. This review focuses on three
basic steps: cooling, pre-enrichment, and final enrichment, and the state of the art in these
areas as it pertains to the proposed methods.

1.2.2 Pre-Enrichment
The pre-enrichment phase begins with helium with trace amounts of 3He that has been cooled
to below the superfluid temperature of 4He. There are two primary techniques used for the
pre-enrichment phase that rely on the superfluid temperature of 4He being higher than that of
3
 He. The two methods are heat flush and superleak (Bentoumi et al., 2013). Heat flush uses
thermal counterflow - the phenomenon that superfluids flow towards a heat source while normal
fluids flow away. Superleak uses the low viscosity properties of a superfluid that allow it to flow
through a porous media in order to perform the separation. These techniques can be used
separately or in combination.

Reynolds et. al demonstrated 100% removal of 3He, with the richest concentration of 3He/4He
collected increasing to 4% compared to the original concentration of 0.01% using the heat flush
method (Reynolds et al., 1949). Soller et. al improved upon this model to achieve an enrichment
factor of 30,000 with a separation rate of 3 liters per hour of gas (Soller, Fairbank, and Crowell,
1953).

Yoshiki et. al demonstrated the use of the superleak method to create isotopically pure 4He. The
experiment succeeded in reducing the 3He/4He to less than 3 × 10−9 (Yoshiki et al., 2005).
Eichelberger et. al used a combination of the heat flush and superleak methods to demonstrate
an increase in the superfluid 4He flowing through the superleak as the heater power was
increased (Eichelberger, Grove, and Jones, 1964).

1.2.3 Final Enrichment
After pre-enrichment, the sample of 3He is further purified in a final enrichment step which most
commonly uses cryogenic distillation. Distillation methods cool a pre-enriched concentration of
Helium to cryogenic temperatures. Since 3He has a lower boiling point than other isotopes, the
mixture can be cooled to just above this point, allowing other isotopes to be condensed and
then siphoned off leaving pure 3He. Wilkes demonstrated a lab-scale continuous distillation
apparatus which obtained a product consisting of 99.95% 3He (Wilkes, 1970).

4.0 Requirements for 3He Enrichment
In sizing and designing a lab-scale experiment 3He enrichment system, we imposed two primary
requirements:

 1.​ Produce a measurable amount of 3He at an isotopic purity of 99.8%
 2.​ Process a stream of helium with an isotopic ratio representative of natural gas

Meeting these requirements would demonstrate our ability to enrich 3He to justify scaling up to a
pilot-scale system. Note that the first requirement was driven by consultation with the primary
industry supplier of 3He, Linde and the product specifications that they normally are asked to
meet by customers. However certain markets such as dilution refrigeration could live with lower
isotopic purity so long as chemical purity is high. This is because the product loaded into a
dilution refrigerator is a mixture of 3He at approximately 6.6% concentration and 4He. Reduction
in requirements for isotopic purity could significantly improve the performance of the device and
would be explored in later phases.

As will be discussed in later sections, we find that the lab-scale prototype will be limited by
helium liquefaction rate. Given the low concentrations of 3He, the production rate of 3He could
be exceptionally small when starting from terrestrial concentrations and the incoming bulk
helium process stream feasible for a lab scale device, such that control of the flow would
become unnecessarily complex. Therefore, we intend to take a two-step approach to
enrichment.

5.0 Isotopic Separation Trade Study
Our work included investigation of various separation technologies for enriching 3He. Table 5
details the comparison between the various options.

The concentration of 3He in 4He in typical terrestrial samples is on the order of 100-200 ppb.
This equates to 0.00001 - 0.00002%. Our final target purity for 3He is 99.8%. In order to achieve
this goal, the helium will go through a pre-enrichment phase that will bring the concentration up
to at least 5% and a final enrichment phase to hit the target concentration. Pre-enrichment can
be accomplished with heat flush, superleak, or a combination of the two. The heat flush method
relies on the property of superfluid 4He to move toward a heat source. Normal fluid flows away
from a heat source. A superleak is a porous material that allows superfluid to pass through it but

Interlune Corporation​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ 10

does not allow normal fluid through. The final enrichment stage will be a cryogenic distillation
column that relies on the difference in boiling point between 3He and 4He.

Each step in the enrichment process has been previously demonstrated at a laboratory scale.
Yoshiki et. al. (2005) proved that using the heat flush method in conjunction with a superleak
creates faster mass flow through the superleak. Increasing the mass flow rate through the
superleak allows the pre-enrichment stage to have a higher throughput. A cryogenic distillation
column to separate 3He from 4He was demonstrated by Wilkes (1971). The system was fed with
a 5% concentration 3He and enriched up to 99%.

The primary limitations in scaling of these past experiments has been the cooling power
available from conventional cryocoolers. The proposed MCL system will allow us to scale to far
higher throughput rates.

Thermodiffusion refers to the movement of particles in a fluid under the influence of a
temperature gradient. This effect is governed by the Soret coefficient, which quantifies the
degree of separation of isotopes due to thermal gradients. Thermodiffusion can be conducted at
room temperature; however, it requires large columns and does not scale well to an industrial
facility.

Membrane separation of the helium isotopes has only been theoretically studied and relies on
quantum tunneling differences to generate selective permeability. This process must also be
conducted at cryogenic temperatures meaning that it may not provide a substantial efficiency
gain over cryogenic distillation. Additionally, membrane defects may prove difficult to surmount
in a real world test as opposed to the theoretical simulations.

5.1 Superfluid Separation
The superleak technology relies on the unique superfluid behavior of 4He at cold temperatures
while 3He remains a normal fluid. The transition of 4He from its normal state to a superfluid state
occurs below a temperature of 2.17 K, known as the 𝛌-point. As the temperature decreases, the
percentage of 4He that exists in the superfluid state increases. A superleak is a phenomenon
that preferentially allows the flow of the superfluid fraction of 4He. This process relies on
nano-scale pores to separate the superfluid component. Conventionally, a puck of Vycor glass
(as shown in Figure 2) has been used to achieve this separation due to its porous structure.

 Figure 2. Example of Nano-Scale Porous Vycor Glass Used for Superleak

The superfluid is able to flow through the nanopores given that it has no viscosity and the flow is
therefore inviscid. The 3He would not flow through the nano-scale pores, thus enriching the 3He
concentration upstream. When analyzing the flow rate of superfluid 4He through a superleak, the
limiting factor is the onset of vorticity at high enough velocities. This velocity is known as the
critical velocity, and has been analytically and experimentally demonstrated to be 30 cm/s (Van
Alphen et al., 1969).

Superfluid 4He demonstrates another unique property in that it flows towards sources of heat
while all other normal fluids flow away. This can be leveraged to encourage flow through a
superleak without imposing a static pressure differential. Our enrichment system design takes
advantage of both superfluid characteristics to maximize superfluid separation rates.

5.2 Fractional Distillation
Fractional distillation is a method used to separate 3He from 4He by leveraging the difference in
their vapor pressures, known as relative volatility. This technique is effective for increasing 3He
concentrations by approximately 100x. However, it requires significant energy for cooling and
condensing the helium isotopes. The size of the distillation system is directly related to the feed
rate of the helium mixture.

Below is a diagram of a typical fractional distillation column. The feed fluid enters the column
approximately halfway up the height (this position varies based on operation conditions), and
fluid exits the column at the top at the bottom. The top product in this case has a high
concentration of 3He and the bottom products, or raffinate, has very little 3He. Sufficient cooling
power to liquefy rising vapor is provided by the condenser at the top, and heat is provided by the
reboiler to vaporize the liquid that has fallen to the bottom of the column. Depending on the
desired quality of the product flow, some of the condensed liquid must be returned to the column
as reflux.

 Figure 3. Fractional Distillation Column Concept for 3He Enrichment.

The efficiency of fractional distillation is influenced by the relative volatility between 3He and 4He,
which increases as the temperature decreases. Higher relative volatility translates to lower
energy requirements and increased throughput in the distillation process. Incorporating a
superfluid removal step prior to fractional distillation can dramatically reduce the feed rate,
thereby enhancing the overall efficiency and reducing energy consumption.

6.0 Enrichment System Analysis Model

6.1 Superleak and Heat Flush
The superleak is sized based on the required flow rate and target 3He concentration using the
critical velocity of superfluid helium. For this work, the second part of the demonstration starting
at an intermediate concentration will be the limiting operating condition. We assume the
incoming stream concentration is 0.01% 3He and we require a concentration greater than 1%
3
  He for fractional distillation after removing superfluid 4He. This results in 99.7% of the incoming
stream flowing through the superleak and not into the distillation column. Given a system flow
rate of 10 SLM and a critical velocity of 30 cm/s of liquid 4He, the minimum superleak diameter
is calculated to be ⅛ inch. In order to provide margin and be consistent with the maximum
liquefaction rate of the MCL system, we have chosen a diameter ¼ inch.

In an industrial-scale system installed a helium liquefaction plant, as described in section 4 and
later in section 12 (economic feasibility analysis), a higher overall flow rate and even greater
percentage of that flow will move through the superleak instead of into the distillation column.
Rather than the superleak sized for 10 SLM, it will have a diameter to accommodate 29,497
SLM. Otherwise, as shown in section 4 and 7.1, the flow that does not go through the superleak
and instead enters the distillation column is on the same order of magnitude in the lab-scale and
industrial-scale system.

Since the flow of superfluid through a superleak is based on a chemical potential gradient, we
require an equation of state to estimate the heat flush heater power. This equation of state has
been derived previously by Miller and Brisson (2001) with the assumption that the 3He
concentration is below 10%, which is valid for our purposes. The schematic shown in Figure 5 is
a screenshot from Engineering Equation Software (https://fchartsoftware.com/ees/) with the

6.2 Fractional Distillation
For analysis of helium isotope fractional distillation, Interlune utilizes software originally
developed by Dr. Anthony Busigin at NITEK USA Inc. for helium distillation. This software
employs the MESH equations (Seader 1985) to accurately model the distillation process, taking
into account the complex behavior of liquid and vapor mixtures. It also incorporates data from
various public sources to determine the fluid properties of the helium mixture, ensuring accurate
and reliable simulations.

The following are the inputs to the analysis:

  ●​ Column flow rate = 33 mL/min
  ●​ Feed 3He concentration = 3%
  ●​ Product 3He concentration = 99.8%
  ●​ Feed and condenser temperature = 1.8 K
  ●​ No packing - wetted-wall column

The analysis resulted in a minimum column length of 30 inches and a diameter of 0.25 inches.
This length could be reduced by including a packing material to increase the interphase surface
area, but given the low flow rates, a wetted-wall column which relies on fluid film on the wall, will
be sufficient and allows for easier manufacturing.

6.3 Helium Supply Sizing
3
 He distillation is a complex process, requiring careful management of various parameters for
optimal efficiency. A model simulating a distillation system with a 10 SLM (standard liters per
minute) flow rate of helium mixture containing 0.01% 3He and aiming for a 3% 3He concentration
at the distillation feed reveals some key operational challenges.

This model predicts that a minimum liquid inventory of 2.5 mL of helium must be maintained
within the distillation column to ensure continuous flow and prevent operational disruptions.
However, achieving this necessary liquid volume takes considerable time. Starting with a low
initial concentration of 3He, it takes approximately 6 hours of operation to accumulate enough
3
 He within the purified stages of the column. During this startup period, a significant amount of
helium mixture is processed, consuming roughly 3,600 liters of the enriched gas.

While 6 hours might seem like a reasonable startup time, it can be further reduced by adjusting
certain operational parameters. For instance, increasing the starting concentration of 3He in the
feed mixture would allow the column to reach the required liquid inventory more quickly.
Alternatively, lowering the desired purity of the final 3He product can also expedite the process,
although this comes at the expense of product quality.

Once the distillation column reaches a steady operational state, the production rate and overall
helium consumption become critical factors. Producing 1 mL of 99.8% pure 3He per minute
requires a large amount of helium gas. For every liter of 3He produced, an additional 10,000
liters of helium gas are consumed, highlighting the inherent inefficiency of the process.
Therefore, producing a single liter of high-purity 3He takes a full 24 hours and requires a total
helium input of 14,000 liters. This underscores the importance of optimizing the distillation
process to minimize helium consumption and maximize production efficiency.

7.0 Enrichment System Design
Figure 8 shows the P&ID for the proposed lab-scale 3He enrichment system. The system
consists of upstream gas supply modules, the cold module (consisting of superfluid separation
and distillation components and highlighted by an orange box), and three downstream
recompression and storage modules for each of the helium streams out of the cold module.

The cold module is the only subsystem that fully integrates with the MCL system. This is done
through a series of heat exchangers which progressively reduce the feed stream temperature to
1.8 K where superfluid separation and isotopic distillation can be performed.

Considerations were made for the recompression and storage of the superfluid stream (denoted
“Superleak Storage”), where the vast majority of gas will flow. Given the estimated quantities of
gas, this stream will need to be recompressed to above 1000 psia to be reduced to a
reasonable volume, whereas the other two streams (3He product and 4He raffinate) will be a
small enough volume that a storage pressure of 50 psia is sufficient.

7.2 Concept of Operations
The following high level operational steps to perform an enrichment process reference the
enrichment system P&ID in Figure 8.

 1.​ Leak test by pulling vacuum using the leak detector and spraying the exterior of the
 system with helium.
 2.​ System purge - Fill entire flow circuit with helium from volume CYL-2, pull gas out using
 the vacuum pump, repeat a few times.
 3.​ Turn on the MCL system (drop temperature T1 to 1.8 K).
 4.​ Fill system from volume CYL-1 while keeping downstream needle valves closed (NV-2,
       NV-3, and NV-4).
 5.​ Open needle valve NV-2 to begin superleak flow.
 6.​ Open needle valve NV-1 to begin filling the column.
 7.​ Wait for helium to liquefy in the column (indicated by diminishing oscillations in the signal
 of pressure measurement P5).
 8.​ Extract product (top of column) and raffinate (bottom of column) using needle valves
 NV-3 and NV-4, respectively.
 9.​ Quality of 3He product will be measured by a binary gas analyzer (BGA) and should
 correlate to temperature measurement T2.
 10.​Samples can be extracted through isolation valves ISO-3, ISO-5, and ISO-7.
 11.​Re-compress and store gas for reuse in volumes V3, V4, and V5.

7.3 Instrumentation and Control
The system has pressure transducers, temperature sensors, and a liquid helium level sensor for
control. Product purity will be determined with a binary gas analyzer. The superleak will be
controlled with a heater and needle valve. The distillation column will be controlled by setting
inlet and outlet flow rates as well as the reboiler and condenser temperatures. Distillation
column performance can be estimated from a temperature sensor near the top of the column.

12. Economic Feasibility Analysis
This economic feasibility analysis fulfills Project Objective #3 described in section 2, to estimate
the end-to-end cost of producing 3He from domestic bulk helium sources at various levels using
an industrial plant-scale implementation of the MCL and 3He separation system described in this
report.

12.1 Scope and Major Assumptions
We developed a concept for a plant-scale system to serve as the basis for economic analysis.
As large quantities of bulk helium are currently produced today and stored in large containers in
a liquefaction plant, we believe the most straightforward way to develop a first-of-a-kind
industrial scale implementation of 3He separation system is to leverage existing cooling and
storage infrastructure. In this concept, the new system would be brought in as a skid and
integrated into an existing helium liquefaction plant. It would pull helium already liquefied at 4 K
into an industrial scale MCL to sub-cool it to 1.8 K, then pass this on to an 3He enrichment
system, which would output two process streams. One would be a highly enriched 3He gas, and
the other would be a liquid helium mixture warmed back to 4 K. The latter would be returned to
storage dewars. A high-level block diagram of this approach is shown in the following figure. It
differs from the lab-scale process flow shown in Figure 1 in that the incoming helium starts as a
4 K liquid in this concept rather than a 20 K gas, but otherwise conceptually the process is the
same. In addition to the large scale flows of liquid helium into and out of the skid, a small
quantity of liquid helium process stream is tapped from the plant to cool the MCL system,
placing additional electrical power load on the plant.

Interlune Corporation​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ 28

 Figure 16. Block Diagram of Plant-Scale 3He Production Skid for Economic Analysis

Aside from minimizing the amount of new infrastructure required, the approach also has the
benefit of working in parallel and independently from bulk helium production. If the MCL or
enrichment system is offline for any reason, bulk helium production would not be disrupted.

Although this approach minimizes infrastructure costs, it does not take advantage of the
significant energy benefit of MCL to perform bulk helium liquefaction to replace the conventional
compressive cooling methods at large helium liquefaction plants. The ability of MCL to provide a
greater percentage of the overall cooling power for helium liquefaction can be studied in future
phases.

The scale of the plant concept analyzed is 29,497 SLPM helium-production rate based on the
scale of an existing large US liquefaction facility operated by Linde.

12.2 Economic Modeling Methodology
The goal of economic modeling was to derive a 3He production cost per liter and to compare
that production cost to market prices in order to determine commercial viability. Due to the
uncertainty in inputs to a production cost model, sensitivity analysis was performed on inputs. A
“Base Case” was derived using what was deemed realistic assumptions for all parameters,
along with a “Conservative” case, to understand how sensitive economic feasibility is to input
uncertainty.

Production costs are expected to be heavily dominated by capital costs, therefore the cost of
financing was incorporated using the following methodology:

 1.​ A projection of cash flows was set up to cover the useful life of a 3He operating plant
 a.​ Cash outflows for capital investments were assumed to occur in years 1-2
 b.​ Operations including revenue and operations costs were assumed to start in year
 3 and extend to the end-of-life of useful life the plant, which varied in the Base
 Case compared to the Conservative case
 2.​ The Net Present Value (NPV) of the cash flow projection was then computed using a
 discount rate. The discount rate captures financing costs, risk premiums and opportunity
 costs. The resulting NPV, if positive, indicates the capital investment is earning more
 than the discount rate. If NPV is negative, it indicates the capital investment is earning
 less than the discount rate.
 3.​ The revenue from the cash flow projection is a function of the price of 3He per liter. The
 per liter price was set such that the NPV was $0. This resultant price therefore exactly

Interlune Corporation​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ ​ 30

 offsets both the financing cost as well as the operating cost, and is therefore equal to the
 production cost per liter reported in section 11.4.

Operating Life
The Operating life dictates how long revenue operations continue on the 3He separation

components and advancement of improved processes that may render the equipment obsolete,
it was decided to be much more conservative and use 10-years (for the Base Case) and
15-years for the Conservative Case.

Discount Rate

premium. Based on existing rates in corporate bond markets for different grades of debt, a
discount rate of 10% was used for the Base Case and 15% for the Conservative Case.

Electrical Power
For the electrical power cost of the system, $0.16/kWh was used for commercial power cost.
Continuous energy expenditure is estimated at 510 kW (base) to 765 kW (conservative) to
operate the system. Operation of the MCL device itself is miniscule, as reported in FWP-85288
(Archipley 2025) this is only 1.3 kW. The power draw of the 3He enrichment device is also
miniscule, estimated at less than 0.7 kW. Instead, the power draw relates to the 1.8 kW
additional cooling load generated by the MCL as described in FWP-85288 that must be rejected
to the plant helium liquefier system via a continuous liquid helium cooling stream, increasing the
power consumption of the helium liquefaction plant to compensate. This load is lifted to room
temperature at 0.25 efficiency requiring 508 kW of power, computed using a relationship to
satisfy the second law of thermodynamics:

 Workideal = Lift* (Thot/Tcold – 1)

In this case, Lift is 1.8 KW, Thot is an assumed ambient temperature in the environment of the
plant of 295K, and Tcold is 4.2K. This ideal work is then factored by 4x due to 25% efficiency to
get 508 kW for the additional load on the liquefaction plant.

Future study could look at taking this load via the MCL instead, and to reduce power
consumption with heat exchanger utilizing 2 K exit helium from the superleak.

Maintenance Cost
A percentage of facility replacement cost (i.e., the original capital costs) based on chemical
industry benchmarks was used - 10% (Base) to 15% (Conservative). Note the final magnitude of
these numbers suggests there are no dedicated full-time maintaining the MCL and 3He
separation system facility 24/7, but rather they are on call or a duty of staff otherwise
maintaining and operating the liquefaction plant.

Helium Leak Rate
In the plant concept, all the bulk liquid helium stored at a large-scale production facility is cycled
through the 3He separation system before being returned to storage. This introduces the
potential for leaks to the atmosphere to incur costs, since the total value of the bulk helium
exceeds the total value of 3He. The cost of bulk helium was estimated using recent data on
Grade A helium prices of $41.58/kg (USGS 2022). At a continuous flow rate of 29,497 SLPM,
this equates to 315.91 kg/hr flowing into the system, or about $13,136 worth of helium flowing
through the system per hour. Therefore, even a 1% external leak will result in a loss of $1.15M
of helium per year.

Based on Interlune experience operating helium systems in its own cryogenics lab, 2% was
used in the Base Case analysis and 4% was used in the Conservative Case analysis. This is
the driving production cost per liter and so will need to be carefully controlled through design
and timely helium-leak detection.
