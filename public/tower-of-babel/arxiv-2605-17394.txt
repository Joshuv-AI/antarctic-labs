Stochastic Zeroth-Order Optimization Under Heavy-Tailed Noise

Abstract
We study stochastic zeroth-order (ZO) optimization of smooth nonconvex objectives under heavy-tailed sample-gradient noise. This regime is motivated by empirical evidence that gradient noise in modern machine learning can violate the bounded-variance assumptions used in classical ZO theory. While first-order methods have optimal rates under bounded p p -th moment noise for p ∈ ( 1 , 2 ] p\in(1,2] , analogous high-probability guarantees for nonconvex ZO methods are much less understood. The ZO setting is not a direct corollary of first-order theory. First-order methods observe stochastic gradients, whereas derivative-free methods only query noisy function values and build finite-difference estimates. Thus, weak- L p L_{p} control of ∇ F ​ ( x , ξ ) − ∇ f ​ ( x ) \nabla F(x;\xi)-\nabla f(x) must first be transferred to scalar directional estimates. We propose the Robust Scalar-Clipped Zeroth-Order method (RSC-ZO), a two-point method that clips each scalar directional derivative before aggregation. Under sample-wise smoothness and a weak- L p L_{p} tail condition on the sample-gradient noise, RSC-ZO finds an ε \varepsilon -stationary point with high probability using O ~ ​ ( d p 2 ​ ( p − 1 ) ​ ε − 3 ​ p − 2 p − 1 ) \widetilde{O}\!\left(d^{\frac{p}{2(p-1)}}\varepsilon^{-\frac{3p-2}{p-1}}\right) noisy function evaluations. This matches the optimal first-order ε \varepsilon -dependence. At p = 2 p=2 , the bound becomes O ~ ​ ( d ​ ε − 4 ) \widetilde{O}(d\varepsilon^{-4}) , matching the classical stochastic ZO dimension–accuracy dependence, but with a high-probability guarantee and under a weaker weak- L 2 L_{2} condition that can allow infinite variance. We also analyze a momentum variant and quantify its batch-size/stepsize tradeoff.

Introduction

We consider the stochastic optimization problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathbb{P}}\bigl[F(x;\xi)\bigr],

where
(\Xi,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
is a probability space. For each
\xi
,
x\mapsto F(x,\xi)
is differentiable, and for each
x
, it is measurable with respect to
\xi
. We assume that
f
is finite-valued and bounded below. The finite-sum case is recovered by taking
\Xi=\{1,\dots,n\}
and
\mathbb{P}
to be the uniform distribution.

In this paper, we consider the zeroth-order (ZO) setting where the algorithm can query only noisy function values
F(x;\xi)
, with no
access to gradients. This oracle model arises naturally in black-box adversarial
attacks
(
5
;
14
)
, simulation-based
optimization
(
8
)
, and, more recently, memory-efficient
fine-tuning of large language models, where backpropagation is prohibitive and
ZO methods such as MeZO achieve competitive performance using only forward
passes
(
21
)
.

For smooth nonconvex objectives, stochastic ZO methods

(
11
;
3
)
establish the benchmark

O(d\,\varepsilon^{-4})
query complexity for finding an

\varepsilon
-stationary point in expectation, when each sample function
x\mapsto F(x;\xi)
is smooth and the gradient noise has bounded variance.

The bounded-variance assumption is, however, increasingly at odds with the
regimes in which ZO methods are deployed. Empirical evidence in modern machine
learning indicates that stochastic gradient noise is often
heavy-tailed
.
For example,
28
provide evidence of heavy-tailed
stochastic-gradient noise in BERT pretraining, with empirical variance estimates
that fail to stabilize as the sample size increases. Similar behavior has been
reported in image classification:
25
estimate
stochastic-gradient tail indices below
2
, consistent with infinite-variance
noise. Heavy-tailed gradients have also been documented in reinforcement
learning
(
10
)
. In such regimes, the second moment may be
infinite or poorly controlled. This poses a challenge not only for first-order
gradient-based stochastic methods, but also for ZO methods, whose updates rely
on gradient estimates reconstructed from noisy function evaluations.

The first-order literature has responded with a substantial line of work showing
that
gradient clipping
, possibly combined with momentum or normalization,
can restore convergence guarantees under bounded
p
-th moment noise on the
sample gradients for
p\in(1,2]

(
12
;
28
;
6
;
24
;
23
;
19
;
4
)
. For smooth nonconvex first-order stochastic optimization under
bounded
p
-th moment noise on the sample gradients, the standard benchmark is
the optimal in-expectation
\varepsilon
-dependence

\Theta(\varepsilon^{-(3p-2)/(p-1)})

28
. Matching high-probability guarantees
were later obtained by
6
.

The zeroth-order picture is much less developed.

For zeroth-order optimization, results under heavy-tailed noise are scarce and
almost entirely confined to the convex setting.
15

obtain complexity bounds for nonsmooth convex ZO under symmetric heavy-tailed
noise using mini-batched median estimates of two-point gradient differences followed by vector clipping;

16
treat convex compact domains; and

17
give an accelerated method when the variance is
infinite. To the best of our knowledge, no prior result appears to give a high-probability complexity bound for smooth
nonconvex stochastic ZO that matches the classical finite-variance expectation
benchmark. This work closes this gap under a
weak-
L_{p}

tail condition on the sample gradient noise

\nabla F(x;\xi)-\nabla f(x)

(Assumption
1.3
), which is strictly weaker than the classical bounded
p
-th moment assumption and allows for infinite variance even when
p=2
.

Why the nonconvex ZO case is not a corollary.

A natural temptation is to view ZO under heavy tails as an immediate consequence
of first-order results. In first-order SGD, the noise assumption is imposed
directly on the stochastic gradient noise

\nabla F(x;\xi)-\nabla f(x)
, and the update uses
\nabla F(x;\xi)
itself.
Thus the stochastic error appearing in the update is exactly the quantity
controlled by the assumption. In zeroth-order optimization, however, the
algorithm does not observe
\nabla F(x;\xi)
. It only observes noisy function
values and forms directional finite-difference estimates. Consequently, the
error in the update is not the original sample-gradient noise vector, but a
scalar directional derivative multiplied by a random direction, together with
finite-difference and smoothing effects. Therefore, weak-
L_{p}
control of

\nabla F(x;\xi)-\nabla f(x)
must first be transferred to the scalar
two-point estimator. This directional transfer is the main ZO-specific step in
our proof and is absent from standard first-order analyses. The difficulty is
amplified by the fact that our assumption is a weak-
L_{p}
tail condition,
which is strictly weaker than the classical bounded
p
-th moment assumption.

Our setting sits at the intersection of heavy-tailed first-order optimization and classical stochastic ZO theory.

1.1
Assumptions

We now state the assumptions used throughout the paper. Our analysis relies on two ingredients: sample-wise smoothness of the stochastic objective and a direct probability control on the sample gradient noise.

Assumption 1.1

(Well-defined problem)
.

The function
f
is bounded from below, i.e.:
f_{*}:=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)>-\infty
and we define the initial gap
\Delta_{0}:=f(x_{0})-f_{*}
.

Assumption 1.2

(Sample smoothness)
.

For
\mathbb{P}
-almost every
\xi
, the map
x\mapsto F(x;\xi)
is
L
-smooth, i.e.,

\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\left\|\nabla F(x;\xi)-\nabla F(y;\xi)\right\|_{2}\leq L\left\|x-y\right\|_{2}.

Assumption 1.3

(Weak-
L_{p}
control of the sample gradient noise)
.

There exist
p\in(1,2]
and
\sigma>0
such that, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and every
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}_{\xi}\bigl(\left\|\nabla F(x;\xi)-\nabla f(x)\right\|_{2}>t\bigr)\leq\left(\frac{\sigma}{t}\right)^{p}.

Remark 1.4
.

Assumption
1.3
is weaker than the bounded
p
-th moment condition used in previous works. Indeed,
a uniform bound

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left\|\nabla F(x;\xi)-\nabla f(x)\right\|_{2}^{p}\leq\sigma^{p}

implies Assumption
1.3
by Markov’s inequality, while the converse need not
hold: weak-
L_{p}
control may allow an infinite
p
-th moment
(see Proposition
A.1
).

Remark 1.5
.

Under Assumption
1.2
, in order to justify
\nabla f(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\nabla F(x;\xi)]
and conclude that
f
is
L
-smooth, it is enough to assume that there exists
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left\|\nabla F(x_{0};\xi)\right\|_{2}<\infty
. Indeed, for any compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\sup_{x\in K}\left\|\nabla F(x;\xi)\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\nabla F(x_{0};\xi)\right\|_{2}+L\sup_{x\in K}\left\|x-x_{0}\right\|_{2},

so dominated convergence yields
\nabla f(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\nabla F(x;\xi)]
. Then

\left\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\right\|_{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left\|\nabla F(x;\xi)-\nabla F(y;\xi)\right\|_{2}\leq L\left\|x-y\right\|_{2},

which shows that
f
is
L
-smooth. Throughout the paper, we assume that such a point
x_{0}
exists.

M

\tau

G_{\mu}(x;M,\tau):=\frac{d}{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell}(x))u_{\ell}.

\beta=0

1.3
Spherical smoothing and notation

For
\mu>0
, define the spherical smoothing of
f
by

f_{\mu}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{B}^{d})}[f(x+\mu v)],

where
\mathbb{B}^{d}:=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\|v\right\|_{2}\leq 1\}
is the unit Euclidean ball. It is a classical result
(
7
)
that

\nabla f_{\mu}(x)=\frac{d}{\mu}\,\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}[f(x+\mu u)u].

Using the symmetry of the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, this can be rewritten in the two-point form

\nabla f_{\mu}(x)=d\,\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\!\left[\frac{f(x+\mu u)-f(x-\mu u)}{2\mu}\,u\right].

Accordingly, for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, we define

D_{\mu}f(x,u):=\frac{f(x+\mu u)-f(x-\mu u)}{2\mu},

so that

\nabla f_{\mu}(x)=d\,\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}[D_{\mu}f(x,u)u].

2
High-Probability Analysis of Base RSC-ZO

Notation.

Let

\bar{\Delta}_{0}:=\Delta_{0}+\frac{L\mu^{2}}{2}.

For brevity, define

S_{\mu}:=\frac{\sqrt{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}+\sigma}{\sqrt{d}}+L\mu.

This scale collects the three quantities that control the scalar directional
finite-difference estimates. The term

\sqrt{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}/\sqrt{d}
controls the noiseless directional derivative,
the term
\sigma/\sqrt{d}
controls the stochastic gradient noise after
projection onto the random direction
u
, and the term
L\mu
controls the
finite-difference smoothing error. After multiplication by the zeroth-order
factor
d
, the corresponding estimator scale is
dS_{\mu}
.

Goal.

We first establish a high-probability bound on the estimator error

g(x)-\nabla f_{\mu}(x)
. For each sample, define the two-point directional
derivative estimate

Y_{\ell}:=\frac{F(x+\mu u_{\ell};\xi_{\ell})-F(x-\mu u_{\ell};\xi_{\ell})}{2\mu}.

The scalar-clipped zeroth-order estimator is then

g(x):=\frac{d}{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell})\,u_{\ell},

where the pairs
(u_{\ell},\xi_{\ell})
are i.i.d., with

u_{\ell}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and
\xi_{\ell}\sim\mathcal{P}
.

We decompose the estimator error as

g(x)-\nabla f_{\mu}(x)=\underbrace{g(x)-\mathbb{E}[g(x)]}_{A:\ \text{fluctuation}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[g(x)]-\nabla f_{\mu}(x)}_{B:\ \text{clipping bias}}.

The term
A
captures the empirical fluctuation of the clipped average, while

B
captures the deterministic bias introduced by clipping. We control these
terms separately in Lemma
B.7
and
Lemma
B.5
. Choosing the clipping level to balance
the two terms, up to logarithmic factors, yields the following localized
deviation bound for a single batched estimator at a fixed, non-random point

x
. The extension from this fixed-point estimate to the random trajectory is
handled by localization and a union bound in the proof of
Theorem
2.3
.

Theorem 2.1

(Localized deviation with tuned clipping)
.

Assume that Assumptions
1.1
,
1.2
, and

1.3
hold. Fix
\delta\in(0,1]
and
T\geq 3
, set

\lambda:=\log(T/\delta),

and assume
M\geq\lambda
. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy

f(x)-f_{*}\leq 4\bar{\Delta}_{0},

and choose

\tau:=8\,S_{\mu}\left(\frac{M}{\lambda}\right)^{1/p}.

Then, with probability at least
1-\delta/T
,

\|g(x)-\nabla f_{\mu}(x)\|_{2}\leq C_{p}\,d\,S_{\mu}\left(\frac{\lambda}{M}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\sqrt{1+\log(M/\lambda)},

where

C_{p}:=120+\frac{2^{4-p}}{p-1}.

All intermediate lemmas appear in Appendix
B
. These
include the clipping-bias bound under weak-
L_{p}
control, directional
weak-
L_{p}
bounds for the noiseless signal and the shared-randomness estimator

Y
, the clipped second-moment estimate, and the vector Bernstein inequality.
The corresponding detailed appendix statement is
Corollary
B.9
.

We turn the localized deviation bound of Theorem
2.1
into
a descent estimate for the smoothed objective
f_{\mu}
. Since the update uses
the clipped estimator
g(x_{t})
in place of
\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})
, the key step
is to combine the smoothness inequality for
f_{\mu}
with the high-probability
control of
g(x_{t})-\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})
.

Proposition 2.2

(One-step descent inequality)
.

Assume
f
is
L
-smooth. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let

x^{+}:=x-\alpha g(x)
and
e(x):=g(x)-\nabla f_{\mu}(x).

If
\alpha\leq\frac{1}{4L}
, then

f_{\mu}(x^{+})\leq f_{\mu}(x)-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x)\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{5\alpha}{4}\|e(x)\|_{2}^{2}.

For
M\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mu>0
,
\delta\in(0,1)
, and
T\geq 3
, define

\eta_{0}(M,\mu,\delta,T):=C_{p}\,d\,S_{\mu}\left(\frac{\log(T/\delta)}{M}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\sqrt{1+\log\!\left(\frac{M}{\log(T/\delta)}\right)},

and

\eta(M,\mu,\delta,T):=\eta_{0}(M,\mu,\delta,T)+L\mu.

Iterating Proposition
2.2
along the trajectory of
Algorithm
1
, applying the localized deviation bound of
Theorem
2.1
on the event that the iterates remain in the region

f(x_{t})-f_{*}\leq 4\bar{\Delta}_{0}
, and taking a union bound over
T
steps yields
our main result. The localization argument is given in
Appendix
B
.

Theorem 2.3

(High-probability convergence of base RSC-ZO)
.

Assume that Assumptions
1.1
,
1.2
, and

1.3
hold. Let
\mu>0
,

\Delta_{0}:=f(x_{0})-f_{*},
and
\bar{\Delta}_{0}:=\Delta_{0}+\frac{L\mu^{2}}{2}.

Let
\epsilon>0
,
\delta\in(0,1]
,
M\in\mathbb{N}
, and define

T:=\max\left\{3,\,\left\lceil\frac{32L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right\rceil\right\}.

Run Algorithm
1
with
\beta=0
(no momentum), batch size
M
, stepsize

\alpha:=\frac{1}{4L},

and clipping threshold

\tau:=8\,S_{\mu}\left(\frac{M}{\log(T/\delta)}\right)^{1/p}.

Assume

M\geq\log(T/\delta),\qquad\eta(M,\mu,\delta,T)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{4},\qquad\epsilon^{2}\leq 32L\bar{\Delta}_{0}.

Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}.

Remark 2.4

(Query complexity)
.

The total number of stochastic function queries is
2MT
. Choose

\mu\asymp\epsilon/(Ld)
. Then
dL\mu=O(\epsilon)
,
L\mu=O(\epsilon/d)
, and

\bar{\Delta}_{0}=\Delta_{0}+O(\epsilon^{2}/(Ld^{2}))
. Since

T=\max\left\{3,\left\lceil\frac{32L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right\rceil\right\},

we have
T=O(1+L\Delta_{0}/\epsilon^{2})
. When the second term dominates the
constant
3
, this is
T=O(L\Delta_{0}/\epsilon^{2})
.

Up to logarithmic factors, the condition

\eta(M,\mu,\delta,T)\leq\epsilon/4
reduces to

dS_{\mu}\left(\frac{\log(T/\delta)}{M}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\lesssim\epsilon.

Moreover, under the above choice of
\mu
,

dS_{\mu}=\sqrt{d}\,(\sqrt{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}+\sigma)+dL\mu=\sqrt{d}\,(\sqrt{L\Delta_{0}}+\sigma)+O(\epsilon).

Therefore it is enough to choose

M\gtrsim\log(T/\delta)\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}\,(\sqrt{L\Delta_{0}}+\sigma)}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}},

up to logarithmic factors. Combining this with
T=O(L\Delta_{0}/\epsilon^{2})
gives

MT=\widetilde{O}\!\left(L\Delta_{0}\bigl(\sqrt{L\Delta_{0}}+\sigma\bigr)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\frac{d^{\frac{p}{2(p-1)}}}{\epsilon^{\frac{3p-2}{p-1}}}\right).

Thus the total number of stochastic function queries,
2MT
, has the same order
up to the leading factor
2
. In particular, at
p=2
,

MT=\widetilde{O}\!\left(L\Delta_{0}(\sqrt{L\Delta_{0}}+\sigma)^{2}\,d\,\epsilon^{-4}\right),

so the method has the classical
d\,\epsilon^{-4}
dimension–accuracy
dependence up to logarithmic and problem-dependent factors. This matches the
classical bounded-variance ZO benchmark

(
11
;
3
)
, but under the strictly weaker
weak-
L_{2}
tail condition and as a high-probability, rather than
in-expectation, guarantee. The
\epsilon
-dependence further matches the
optimal first-order rate of
28
.

Remark 2.5

(Trivial regime)
.

The condition
\epsilon^{2}\leq 32L\bar{\Delta}_{0}
isolates the regime where the
iteration bound above is needed. If
\epsilon^{2}>32L\bar{\Delta}_{0}
, then
Lemma
B.14
gives

\|\nabla f(x_{0})\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2L(f(x_{0})-f_{*})=2L\Delta_{0}\leq 2L\bar{\Delta}_{0}<\epsilon^{2}.

Hence the desired stationarity conclusion already holds at the initial point.

3
High-Probability Analysis of Momentum RSC-ZO

We now study the momentum variant of RSC-ZO, corresponding to
\beta>0
in
Algorithm
1
. In this variant, the algorithm averages
clipped directional estimates over time, which reduces the running batch size
required per iteration (see
(
2
, Section 2.2)
for a discussion on how momentum reuses past iterates to mimic a large batch). This reduction comes with a proportional stepsize
restriction of order
(1-\beta)/L
.

The method first computes a warm-start estimator

g_{0}=G_{\mu}(x_{0};M_{0},\tau_{0}),\,m_{0}=g_{0}
and
x_{1}=x_{0}-\alpha m_{0}.

Then, for
t\geq 1
, it forms an independent clipped two-point estimator

g_{t}=G_{\mu}(x_{t};M,\tau)=\frac{d}{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\psi_{\tau}(Y_{t,\ell})u_{t,\ell},

and updates

m_{t}=\beta m_{t-1}+(1-\beta)g_{t},\qquad x_{t+1}=x_{t}-\alpha m_{t},

where
\beta\in(0,1)
is the momentum parameter. The analysis below focuses
on the high-momentum regime
\beta\in[1/2,1)
, where the batch-size reduction
is most relevant.

Theorem 3.1

(High-probability convergence of momentum RSC-ZO)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1.1
,
1.2
, and

1.3
hold. Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
,

\delta\in(0,1]
, and
\beta\in[1/2,1)
. Choose

\mu\leq\frac{\epsilon}{4Ld}.

Let

\Delta_{0}:=f(x_{0})-f_{*}
and
\bar{\Delta}_{0}:=\Delta_{0}+\frac{L\mu^{2}}{2}

and assume
\epsilon^{2}\leq 32L\bar{\Delta}_{0}
. Define

S_{\mu}:=\frac{\sqrt{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}+\sigma}{\sqrt{d}}+L\mu
and
\mathcal{R}_{\mu}:=dS_{\mu}.

Choose

\alpha=\frac{1-\beta}{16\sqrt{3}\,L},\qquad T=\left\lceil 512\sqrt{3}\,\frac{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{(1-\beta)\epsilon^{2}}\right\rceil+2,

and set

\lambda:=\log\frac{2T}{\delta},\qquad\lambda_{0}:=\log\frac{2}{\delta}.

Let

\tau=8S_{\mu}\left(\frac{M}{(1-\beta)\lambda}\right)^{1/p},\qquad\tau_{0}=8S_{\mu}\left(\frac{M_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right)^{1/p}.

Let
M_{0},M\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume
M_{0}\geq\lambda_{0}
,

M\geq(1-\beta)\lambda
, and

M_{0}=\widetilde{\Theta}\left(\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right),\qquad M=\widetilde{\Theta}\left((1-\beta)\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right).

Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\frac{1}{T-2}\sum_{t=1}^{T-2}\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}.

Remark 3.2

(Query complexity and momentum tradeoff)
.

The notation
\widetilde{\Theta}(\cdot)
hides only logarithmic factors and
constants depending on
p
, but not on

d,L,\Delta_{0},\sigma,\epsilon,\delta
, or
\beta
. The total number of
stochastic function evaluations is

Q_{\rm MOM}:=2M_{0}+2M(T-1).

Using the choices in Theorem
3.1
, we obtain

Q_{\rm MOM}=\widetilde{O}\left(\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right)+\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right).

Moreover, under the above choice of
\mu
,

\bar{\Delta}_{0}=\Delta_{0}+O\!\left(\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{Ld^{2}}\right)
and
\mathcal{R}_{\mu}=\sqrt{d}(\sqrt{L\Delta_{0}}+\sigma)+O(\epsilon).

Hence

Q_{\rm MOM}=\widetilde{O}\left(\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}(\sqrt{L\Delta_{0}}+\sigma)}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right)+\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{L\Delta_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}(\sqrt{L\Delta_{0}}+\sigma)}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right).

Thus, compared with base RSC-ZO, momentum reduces the running batch size by a
factor of order
1-\beta
, while the admissible stepsize scales as

\alpha\asymp\frac{1-\beta}{L}.

The per-iteration query cost decreases, but the number of iterations increases
by the reciprocal factor. The total query complexity is therefore preserved up
to logarithmic factors and the additive warm-start cost.

Momentum reduces the batch size requirement of Theorem
2.3
. In particular, it suffices to take
\beta=1-\widetilde{\Theta}\left(\big(\frac{\epsilon}{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}\big)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right)
, and
T=\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right)
to guarantee

\frac{1}{T-2}\sum_{t=1}^{T-2}\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}.
with probability at least
1-\delta
, and most importantly, this is obtained for a batch size
M
constant that can be even
M=1
(note that
M_{0}
will stay unchanged).

5
Controlled experiment: clipping before versus after aggregation

We include a controlled experiment to isolate the estimator-level effect of
scalar clipping. The goal is not to benchmark on a difficult objective, but to
test the mechanism suggested by our theory: in zeroth-order estimation under
heavy-tailed noise, it is beneficial to clip each scalar directional derivative
before aggregation, rather than only clipping the final batched vector.

We consider

f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2}
and
F(x;\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2}+\langle\zeta,x\rangle
,
where the noise is sparse and heavy-tailed,

\zeta=sAe_{J}.

Here
s
is uniform on
\{-1,+1\}
,
J
is uniform on

\{1,\ldots,d\}
, and
A
is Pareto with tail exponent
p
. Thus

\mathbb{E}[\zeta]=0
, the population objective is
f
, and for

p\in(1,2)
the stochastic gradient noise has finite first moment but infinite
variance. For a two-point shared-randomness estimator,

Y_{\ell}=\frac{F(x+\mu u_{\ell};\zeta_{\ell})-F(x-\mu u_{\ell};\zeta_{\ell})}{2\mu}=\langle x,u_{\ell}\rangle+\langle\zeta_{\ell},u_{\ell}\rangle.

We compare three natural estimators:

\displaystyle g_{\rm raw}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}Y_{\ell}u_{\ell},

\displaystyle\text{unclipped ZO estimator},

\displaystyle g_{\rm vec}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{clip}_{r_{\rm vec}}(g_{\rm raw}),

\displaystyle\text{vector clipping after aggregation},

\displaystyle g_{\rm sc}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell})u_{\ell},

\displaystyle\text{scalar clipping before aggregation}.

The final-vector baseline is the direct analogue of standard gradient clipping
in SGD: one first forms a stochastic gradient-like vector and then clips its
norm. In contrast, scalar clipping acts earlier, at the level of each
one-dimensional directional measurement.

6
Discussion and Conclusion

We studied stochastic zeroth-order optimization for smooth nonconvex objectives under heavy-tailed sample-gradient noise. The key challenge is that ZO methods do not observe the stochastic gradients on which the noise assumption is imposed; they only observe function values and construct directional finite-difference estimates. Our analysis shows that weak-
L_{p}
control of the sample-gradient noise can be transferred to scalar two-point directional estimates under sample-wise smoothness and shared randomness.

We proposed RSC-ZO, which clips each scalar directional derivative before aggregation. This prevents extreme directional measurements from dominating the final gradient estimator. Under weak-
L_{p}
noise, RSC-ZO achieves a high-probability complexity bound matching the optimal first-order
\epsilon
-dependence. At
p=2
, it recovers the classical
d\epsilon^{-4}
stochastic ZO dependence, but under the weaker weak-
L_{2}
condition and with high probability. We also analyzed a momentum variant, showing that momentum reduces the required running batch size while preserving the total query complexity up to logarithmic factors.

Our experiments support the proposed mechanism. In controlled heavy-tailed quadratic problems, scalar clipping improves the estimator direction compared with raw ZO estimation and final-vector clipping. In RoBERTa-large fine-tuning, RSC-ZO improves over MeZO under the same forward-pass budget.

Limitations.

Our theory assumes sample-wise smoothness, weak-
L_{p}
control of the sample-gradient noise, and shared randomness in the two-point estimator. These assumptions are natural for isolating the effect of heavy-tailed sample-gradient noise in two-point ZO methods, but they do not cover nonsmooth objectives, pure function-value noise models, or black-box systems where shared randomness is unavailable. The theoretically prescribed clipping threshold also depends on problem-specific quantities; in our experiments, we therefore tune the threshold by grid search. Developing adaptive clipping rules with comparable high-probability guarantees is an important direction for future work.

Our empirical results are intended to support the estimator-level mechanism predicted by the theory. The controlled heavy-tailed quadratic experiment isolates the benefit of clipping scalar directional derivatives before aggregation, while the RoBERTa-large fine-tuning experiments show that the same mechanism can improve MeZO-style training under a fixed forward-evaluation budget. A broader empirical study across model scales, tasks, hyperparameter budgets, and robust ZO alternatives would further clarify the practical scope of the method. Finally, although the dimension dependence matches classical stochastic ZO benchmarks at
p=2
, it remains a bottleneck in very high-dimensional problems. Combining scalar clipping with structured perturbations or low-dimensional subspace methods is a promising direction.

Appendix A
Weak-
L_{2}
Noise Can Have Infinite Variance

A natural question is whether the specialization
p=2
in
Assumption
1.3
simply recovers the classical bounded-variance setting. Indeed, by
Markov’s inequality, any bounded-variance model satisfies Assumption
1.3
with

p=2
. More precisely, if

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\|\nabla F(x;\xi)-\nabla f(x)\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

then

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\|\nabla F(x;\xi)-\nabla f(x)\|_{2}>t\right)\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{t^{2}}\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ t>0.

Thus, the bounded-variance regime is contained in our
p=2
framework. The converse, however, is
false: even at
p=2
, Assumption
1.3
still allows stochastic models with infinite
gradient-noise variance. Therefore, the
p=2
case of our theory is not a reformulation of
bounded-variance results, but a genuine extension beyond them.

Proposition A.1

(The case
p=2
still allows infinite variance)
.

Let
f_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be any
L
-smooth function, and let
Z
be a real-valued random variable
with density

\rho(z)=|z|^{-3}\mathbf{1}_{\{|z|\geq 1\}}.

Define the stochastic objective

F(x;\xi):=f_{0}(x)+Z(\xi)\,x_{1},

where
x_{1}
denotes the first coordinate of
x
, and let

f(x):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[F(x;\xi)].

Then the following hold:

1.

f(x)=f_{0}(x)
, and for
\mathbb{P}
-almost every
\xi
, the map
x\mapsto F(x;\xi)

is
L
-smooth.

2.

The sample gradient noise satisfies

\nabla F(x;\xi)-\nabla f(x)=Z(\xi)e_{1},

where
e_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0)^{\top}
.

3.

Assumption
1.3
holds with
p=2
and
\sigma=1
, since for every
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\|\nabla F(x;\xi)-\nabla f(x)\|_{2}>t\right)=\mathbb{P}(|Z|>t)\leq t^{-2}.

4.

The second moment is infinite:

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\|\nabla F(x;\xi)-\nabla f(x)\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}]=\infty.

Proof of Proposition
A.1
.

Since
\rho
is symmetric,
Z
has zero mean whenever the first moment exists. Moreover,

\mathbb{E}|Z|=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|z|\rho(z)\,dz=2\int_{1}^{\infty}z\cdot z^{-3}\,dz=2\int_{1}^{\infty}z^{-2}\,dz<\infty,

so
\mathbb{E}[Z]=0
. Therefore,

f(x)=\mathbb{E}[F(x;\xi)]=f_{0}(x)+\mathbb{E}[Z]\,x_{1}=f_{0}(x).

Also,

\nabla F(x;\xi)=\nabla f_{0}(x)+Z(\xi)e_{1},

and hence

\nabla F(x;\xi)-\nabla f(x)=Z(\xi)e_{1}.

Because the added term
Z(\xi)x_{1}
is linear in
x
, it does not affect smoothness; therefore

F(\cdot;\xi)
is
L
-smooth whenever
f_{0}
is
L
-smooth.

Since

\|\nabla F(x;\xi)-\nabla f(x)\|_{2}=|Z(\xi)|,

for every
t\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}(|Z|>t)=2\int_{t}^{\infty}z^{-3}\,dz=t^{-2},

while for
0<t<1
one trivially has
\mathbb{P}(|Z|>t)\leq 1\leq t^{-2}
. Thus

\forall t>0,\qquad\mathbb{P}\!\left(\|\nabla F(x;\xi)-\nabla f(x)\|_{2}>t\right)\leq t^{-2},

so Assumption
1.3
holds with
p=2
and
\sigma=1
.

Finally,

\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}z^{2}\rho(z)\,dz=2\int_{1}^{\infty}z^{2}\cdot z^{-3}\,dz=2\int_{1}^{\infty}z^{-1}\,dz=\infty.

Hence the gradient noise has infinite variance.
∎

Takeaway.

The case
p=2
is not simply a restatement of bounded-variance theory. It strictly enlarges the
noise model while preserving, up to logarithmic factors, the benchmark complexity

O\!\left(d\,\sigma^{2}\,\epsilon^{-4}\right)

known for stochastic zeroth-order methods in the
individual smoothness
setting under a
bounded-variance assumption on the sample gradient noise

[
11
,
3
]
. In particular,

Theorem

B.11
achieves the benchmark complexity bound

O(d\,\sigma^{2}\,\epsilon^{-4})
up to logarithmic factors under the weaker polynomial-tail
assumption (see Remark
B.12
).

Appendix B
High-Probability Analysis of Base RSC-ZO

Notation.

Define

f_{*}:=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x),\Delta_{0}:=f(x_{0})-f_{*},

and

\bar{\Delta}_{0}:=\Delta_{0}+\frac{L\mu^{2}}{2}.

For brevity, write

S_{\mu}:=\frac{\sqrt{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}+\sigma}{\sqrt{d}}+L\mu.

Goal of this section.

Our goal is to prove a high-probability bound on the estimator error

\|g(x)-\nabla f_{\mu}(x)\|_{2},

where

Y_{\ell}:=\frac{F(x+\mu u_{\ell};\xi_{\ell})-F(x-\mu u_{\ell};\xi_{\ell})}{2\mu}\quad\text{and}\quad g(x):=\frac{d}{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell})u_{\ell}.

We decompose the estimator error as

g(x)-\nabla f_{\mu}(x)=\underbrace{(g(x)-\mathbb{E}[g(x)])}_{A}+\underbrace{(\mathbb{E}[g(x)]-\nabla f_{\mu}(x))}_{B}.

Here,
A
is the empirical fluctuation of the clipped sample average, while

B
is the bias introduced by clipping. Hence, to obtain a high-probability
upper bound on
\|g(x)-\nabla f_{\mu}(x)\|_{2}
, it suffices to bound

\|A\|_{2}
and
\|B\|_{2}
separately. We first consider
B
.

Let

Y:=\frac{F(x+\mu u;\xi)-F(x-\mu u;\xi)}{2\mu},

where
u\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
is independent of
\xi
. Then

B=\mathbb{E}[g(x)]-\nabla f_{\mu}(x)=d\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\psi_{\tau}(Y)-Y)u\bigr].

Therefore,

\|B\|_{2}=d\,\Bigl\|\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\psi_{\tau}(Y)-Y)u\bigr]\Bigr\|_{2}\leq d\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[|\psi_{\tau}(Y)-Y|\bigr].

To control the quantity
\mathbb{E}[|\psi_{\tau}(Y)-Y|]
, we first establish the following abstract clipping-bias bound.

Lemma B.1

(Clipping bias under weak-
L_{p}
control)
.

Let
Z
be a real-valued random variable such that, for some
S>0
,

\forall t\geq 4S,\qquad\mathbb{P}(|Z|>t)\leq 2^{2p+1}S^{p}t^{-p}.

Then, for every
\tau\geq 4S
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[|Z-\psi_{\tau}(Z)|\bigr]\leq\frac{2^{2p+1}}{p-1}\,S^{p}\tau^{1-p}.

It remains to verify that the directional estimator
Y
satisfies the hypothesis of
Lemma
B.1
. To this end, we use the decomposition

Y=\bigl(Y-D_{\mu}f(x,u)\bigr)+D_{\mu}f(x,u).

Thus, it is enough to control the tails of
Y-D_{\mu}f(x,u)
and

D_{\mu}f(x,u)
. The first term measures the deviation of the stochastic
directional estimator around its conditional mean, while the second is the
directional signal itself. These are controlled in
Lemmas
B.2
and

B.3
, respectively.

Lemma B.2

(Directional weak-
L_{p}
bound)
.

Assume that Assumptions
1.2
and

1.3
hold. Let
u\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, independent
of
\xi
. Then, for every fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and every
s\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}_{u,\xi}\!\left(\left|\frac{F(x+\mu u;\xi)-F(x-\mu u;\xi)}{2\mu}-D_{\mu}f(x,u)\right|>\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{d}}+L\mu\right)s\right)\leq s^{-p}.

Lemma B.3

(Localized weak-
L_{p}
bound for the noiseless directional signal)
.

Assume that Assumptions
1.1
and

1.2
hold. Let
p\in(1,2]
. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy

f(x)-f_{*}\leq 4\bar{\Delta}_{0}
, and let

u\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
. Then, for every
s\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}_{u}\!\left(|D_{\mu}f(x,u)|>\left(\frac{\sqrt{8L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}}{\sqrt{d}}+L\mu\right)s\right)\leq s^{-p}.

Lemma B.4

(Localized weak-
L_{p}
bound for the shared-randomness directional estimator)
.

Assume that Assumptions
1.1
,
1.2
, and

1.3
hold. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy

f(x)-f_{*}\leq 4\bar{\Delta}_{0}
. Let

u\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, independent of
\xi
, and define

Y:=\frac{F(x+\mu u;\xi)-F(x-\mu u;\xi)}{2\mu}.

Then, for every
s\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(|Y|>4S_{\mu}s\right)\leq 2s^{-p}.

Equivalently, for every
t\geq 4S_{\mu}
,

\mathbb{P}(|Y|>t)\leq 2^{2p+1}S_{\mu}^{p}t^{-p}.

We may now combine the preceding ingredients to obtain the following deterministic bound on the clipping bias term
B
.

Lemma B.5

(Bias induced by clipping)
.

Assume that Assumptions
1.1
,
1.2
, and

1.3
hold. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy

f(x)-f_{*}\leq 4\bar{\Delta}_{0}.

Let
u\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, independent of
\xi
, and set

Y:=\frac{F(x+\mu u;\xi)-F(x-\mu u;\xi)}{2\mu}.

Then, for every
\tau\geq 4S_{\mu}
,

\Bigl\|d\,\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\tau}(Y)u]-\nabla f_{\mu}(x)\Bigr\|_{2}\leq\frac{2^{2p+1}}{p-1}\,d\,S_{\mu}^{p}\,\tau^{1-p}.

Equivalently, if

B:=\mathbb{E}[g(x)]-\nabla f_{\mu}(x),

then

\|B\|_{2}\leq\frac{2^{2p+1}}{p-1}\,d\,S_{\mu}^{p}\,\tau^{1-p}.

We now turn to the empirical fluctuation term

A=\frac{d}{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\Bigl(\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell})u_{\ell}-\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell})u_{\ell}]\Bigr).

The summands are i.i.d. centered random vectors. We therefore control
A

using the vector Bernstein inequality of Lemma
B.16
.
To apply that lemma, it remains to verify an almost-sure bound on each summand
and a suitable second-moment bound. For each
\ell
, define

X_{\ell}:=\frac{d}{M}\Bigl(\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell})u_{\ell}-\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell})u_{\ell}]\Bigr).

Since
\|u_{\ell}\|_{2}=1
almost surely and
|\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell})|\leq\tau
, we have

\|X_{\ell}\|_{2}\leq\frac{d}{M}\Bigl(|\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell})|+\|\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell})u_{\ell}]\|_{2}\Bigr)\leq\frac{2d\tau}{M}.

This verifies the first condition in Lemma
B.16
.

To verify the second condition, we use the following clipped second-moment estimate.

Lemma B.6

(Clipped second moment under weak-
L_{p}
control)
.

Let
Z
be a real-valued random variable such that, for some
S>0
,

\forall t\geq 4S,\quad\mathbb{P}(|Z|>t)\leq 2^{2p+1}S^{p}t^{-p}.

Then, for every
1<p\leq 2
and every

,

\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\tau}(Z)^{2}]\leq 64\,S^{p}\tau^{2-p}\bigl(1+\log(\tau/S)\bigr).

We are now ready to combine the vector Bernstein inequality with the clipped
second-moment estimate to control the empirical fluctuation term

A=g(x)-\mathbb{E}[g(x)].

This yields the following high-probability bound.

Lemma B.7

(Empirical fluctuation of the clipped estimator)
.

Assume that Assumptions
1.1
,
1.2
, and

1.3
hold. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy

f(x)-f_{*}\leq 4\bar{\Delta}_{0}.

Let
\rho\in(0,1)
, and let
\tau\geq 4S_{\mu}
. For
\ell=1,\dots,M
, let

Y_{\ell}:=\frac{F(x+\mu u_{\ell};\xi_{\ell})-F(x-\mu u_{\ell};\xi_{\ell})}{2\mu},\qquad\widetilde{Y}_{\ell}:=\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell}),

where the pairs

and

Then, with probability at least

\displaystyle\|A\|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{8d}{\sqrt{M}}S_{\mu}^{p/2}\tau^{1-\frac{p}{2}}\sqrt{1+\log\left(\frac{\tau}{S_{\mu}}\right)}\left(1+\sqrt{2\log(1/\rho)}\right)+\frac{8d\tau}{3M}\log(1/\rho).

Combining the fluctuation and bias bounds yields the following localized high-probability estimate for the full estimator error
g(x)-\nabla f_{\mu}(x)

Corollary B.8

(Localized high-probability deviation of the clipped estimator)
.

Assume that Assumptions
1.1
,
1.2
, and

1.3
hold. Fix
\delta\in(0,1)

, and set

\lambda:=\log\frac{T}{\delta}.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy

f(x)-f_{*}\leq 4\bar{\Delta}_{0}.

Let
\tau\geq 4S_{\mu}
. For
\ell=1,\dots,M
, let

Y_{\ell}:=\frac{F(x+\mu u_{\ell};\xi_{\ell})-F(x-\mu u_{\ell};\xi_{\ell})}{2\mu},\qquad g(x):=\frac{d}{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell})u_{\ell},

where the pairs
(u_{\ell},\xi_{\ell})
are independent across
\ell
, with

u_{\ell}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and
\xi_{\ell}\sim\mathbb{P}
. Then, with
probability at least
1-\delta/T
,

\displaystyle\left\|g(x)-\nabla f_{\mu}(x)\right\|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{8d}{\sqrt{M}}S_{\mu}^{p/2}\tau^{1-\frac{p}{2}}\sqrt{1+\log\left(\frac{\tau}{S_{\mu}}\right)}\left(1+\sqrt{2\lambda}\right)+\frac{8d\tau}{3M}\lambda+\frac{2^{2p+1}}{p-1}\,dS_{\mu}^{p}\tau^{1-p}.

We now instantiate Corollary
B.8
with the clipping
level used in the algorithm. This choice balances the leading fluctuation
and bias terms up to logarithmic factors and gives the following simplified
localized deviation bound.

Corollary B.9

(Appendix form of Theorem
2.1
)
.

Assume that Assumptions
1.1
,
1.2
, and

1.3
hold. Fix
\delta\in(0,1]
and
T\geq 3
, and set

\lambda:=\log\frac{T}{\delta}.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy

f(x)-f_{*}\leq 4\bar{\Delta}_{0}.

Suppose that
M\geq\lambda
, and choose

\tau:=8S_{\mu}\left(\frac{M}{\lambda}\right)^{1/p}.

For
\ell=1,\dots,M
, let

Y_{\ell}:=\frac{F(x+\mu u_{\ell};\xi_{\ell})-F(x-\mu u_{\ell};\xi_{\ell})}{2\mu},\qquad g(x):=\frac{d}{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell})u_{\ell}.

Then, with probability at least
1-\delta/T
,

\left\|g(x)-\nabla f_{\mu}(x)\right\|_{2}\leq\left(120+\frac{2^{4-p}}{p-1}\right)dS_{\mu}\left(\frac{\log(T/\delta)}{M}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\sqrt{1+\log\left(\frac{M}{\log(T/\delta)}\right)}.

We now turn the localized deviation bound of
Corollary
B.9
into a descent estimate for
the smoothed objective
f_{\mu}
. Since the update uses the clipped estimator

g(x_{t})
in place of
\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})
, the key step is to combine the
smoothness inequality for
f_{\mu}
with the high-probability control of

g(x_{t})-\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})
. The next proposition gives a deterministic
one-step descent inequality.

Proposition B.10

(One-step descent inequality)
.

Assume that
f
is
L
-smooth. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let

x^{+}:=x-\alpha g(x)
, and define

e(x):=g(x)-\nabla f_{\mu}(x).

If
\alpha\leq\frac{1}{4L}
, then

f_{\mu}(x^{+})\leq f_{\mu}(x)-\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{5\alpha}{4}\left\|e(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}.

For
M\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mu>0
,
\delta\in(0,1)
, and
T\geq 3
, define

C_{p}:=120+\frac{2^{4-p}}{p-1},

\eta_{0}(M,\mu,\delta,T):=C_{p}\,dS_{\mu}\left(\frac{\log(T/\delta)}{M}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\sqrt{1+\log\!\left(\frac{M}{\log(T/\delta)}\right)},

and

\eta(M,\mu,\delta,T):=\eta_{0}(M,\mu,\delta,T)+L\mu.

Theorem B.11

(High-probability convergence of base RSC-ZO)
.

Assume that Assumptions
1.1
,
1.2
, and

1.3
hold. Let
\mu>0
,

\Delta_{0}:=f(x_{0})-f_{*}

and

\bar{\Delta}_{0}:=\Delta_{0}+\frac{L\mu^{2}}{2}.

Let
\epsilon>0
,
\delta\in(0,1]
, and
M\in\mathbb{N}
, and define

T:=\max\left\{3,\left\lceil\frac{32L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right\rceil\right\}.

Let
\{x_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be generated by Algorithm
1

with
\beta=0
, batch size
M
, stepsize
\alpha=1/(4L)
, and threshold

\tau:=8S_{\mu}\Bigl(\frac{M}{\log(T/\delta)}\Bigr)^{1/p}.

Suppose that

M\geq\log\frac{T}{\delta},\qquad\eta(M,\mu,\delta,T)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{4},\qquad\epsilon^{2}\leq 32L\bar{\Delta}_{0}.

Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\nabla f(x_{t})\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}.

In particular, on the same event,

\min_{0\leq t<T}\left\|\nabla f(x_{t})\right\|_{2}\leq\epsilon.

Remark B.12

(Complexity implied by Theorem
B.11
)
.

The total number of stochastic function queries is
2MT
. Choose

\mu\asymp\epsilon/(Ld)
. Then
dL\mu=O(\epsilon)
,

L\mu=O(\epsilon/d)
, and

\bar{\Delta}_{0}=\Delta_{0}+O\!\left(\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{Ld^{2}}\right).

Since

T=\max\left\{3,\left\lceil\frac{32L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right\rceil\right\},

we have

T=O(1+L\Delta_{0}/\epsilon^{2}).

When
L\Delta_{0}/\epsilon^{2}\gtrsim 1
, this simplifies to

T=O(L\Delta_{0}/\epsilon^{2}).

Up to logarithmic factors, the condition

\eta(M,\mu,\delta,T)\leq\epsilon/4

reduces to

dS_{\mu}\left(\frac{\log(T/\delta)}{M}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\lesssim\epsilon.

Moreover, under the above choice of
\mu
,

dS_{\mu}=\sqrt{d}\,(\sqrt{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}+\sigma)+dL\mu=\sqrt{d}\,(\sqrt{L\Delta_{0}}+\sigma)+O(\epsilon).

Therefore it is enough to choose

M\gtrsim\log(T/\delta)\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}\,(\sqrt{L\Delta_{0}}+\sigma)}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}},

up to logarithmic factors. Combining this with

T=O(L\Delta_{0}/\epsilon^{2})
gives

MT=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(L\Delta_{0}\left(\sqrt{L\Delta_{0}}+\sigma\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\frac{d^{\frac{p}{2(p-1)}}}{\epsilon^{\frac{3p-2}{p-1}}}\right).

Thus the total number of stochastic function queries,
2MT
, has the same
order up to the leading factor
2
. In particular, at
p=2
,

MT=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(L\Delta_{0}(\sqrt{L\Delta_{0}}+\sigma)^{2}d\,\epsilon^{-4}\right),

so the method has the classical
d\,\epsilon^{-4}
dimension–accuracy
dependence up to logarithmic and problem-dependent factors.

Remark B.13

(Trivial regime)
.

The condition
\epsilon^{2}\leq 32L\bar{\Delta}_{0}
isolates the regime where the
iteration bound above is needed. If
\epsilon^{2}>32L\bar{\Delta}_{0}
, then
Lemma
B.14
gives

\|\nabla f(x_{0})\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2L(f(x_{0})-f_{*})=2L\Delta_{0}\leq 2L\bar{\Delta}_{0}<\epsilon^{2}.

Hence the desired stationarity conclusion already holds at the initial point.

Lemma B.14

(Gradient bound from suboptimality)
.

Assume that
f
is
L
-smooth and bounded below by
f_{*}
. Then, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2L\bigl(f(x)-f_{*}\bigr).

Proof.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
L
-smoothness gives

f\Bigl(x-\frac{1}{L}\nabla f(x)\Bigr)\leq f(x)-\frac{1}{2L}\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Since
f_{*}\leq f(y)
for every
y
, we obtain

f_{*}\leq f\Bigl(x-\frac{1}{L}\nabla f(x)\Bigr)\leq f(x)-\frac{1}{2L}\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|_{2}^{2},

which rearranges to the claim.
∎

Theorem B.15

(
[
22
, Theorem 5]
)
.

Suppose that the
X_{i}
are
m
independent mean-zero random variables with values in a Hilbert space
H
, satisfying
\|X_{i}\|\leq c
. Then for
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\Pr\left\{\left\|\sum_{i}X_{i}\right\|>\sqrt{\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}\|X_{i}\|^{2}}\left(1+\sqrt{2\ln(1/\delta)}\right)+\frac{4c}{3}\ln(1/\delta)\right\}<\delta.

The following lemma is the finite-dimensional form used in our analysis.

Lemma B.16

(Vector Bernstein inequality with variance proxy)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{M}
be independent mean-zero random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Assume that

\left\|X_{\ell}\right\|_{2}\leq R\quad\text{almost surely for all }\ell,

and

\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}\left\|X_{\ell}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq V.

Then, for every
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\left\|\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}X_{\ell}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{V}\left(1+\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)}\right)+\frac{4R}{3}\log(1/\delta).

Proof.

We can apply Theorem
B.15
with

H=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
m=M
, and
c=R
. The theorem gives

\left\|\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}X_{\ell}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}\left\|X_{\ell}\right\|_{2}^{2}}\left(1+\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)}\right)+\frac{4R}{3}\log(1/\delta)

with probability at least
1-\delta
. Since

\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}\left\|X_{\ell}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq V,

the result follows.
∎

Lemma B.17

(Smoothing bias)
.

Assume that
f
is
L
-smooth. Then, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and every
\mu>0
,

\left|f_{\mu}(x)-f(x)\right|\leq\frac{L\mu^{2}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\left\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x)-\nabla f(x)\right\|_{2}\leq L\mu.

Proof.

By
L
-smoothness, for every
v\in\mathbb{B}^{d}
,

\left|f(x+\mu v)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),\mu v\rangle\right|\leq\frac{L\mu^{2}}{2}\|v\|^{2}\leq\frac{L\mu^{2}}{2}.

Taking expectation over
v\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{B}^{d})
and using

\mathbb{E}[v]=0
, we obtain

|f_{\mu}(x)-f(x)|\leq\frac{L\mu^{2}}{2}.

Next, since
f
is
L
-smooth,

\nabla f_{\mu}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{B}^{d})}[\nabla f(x+\mu v)].

Therefore,

\left\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x)-\nabla f(x)\right\|_{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{B}^{d})}\left\|\nabla f(x+\mu v)-\nabla f(x)\right\|_{2}\leq L\mu\,\mathbb{E}\left\|v\right\|_{2}\leq L\mu,

since
\left\|v\right\|_{2}\leq 1
almost surely for
v\in\mathbb{B}^{d}
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
B.1
.

We have

\psi_{\tau}(z)=\operatorname{sgn}(z)\min\{|z|,\tau\}.

Therefore

|Z-\psi_{\tau}(Z)|=\bigl(|Z|-\tau\bigr)_{+},

where
a_{+}:=\max\{a,0\}
.

For any nonnegative random variable
X
and any
a>0
,

\mathbb{E}[(X-a)_{+}]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{a}^{\infty}1_{\{X>t\}}\,dt\bigg]=\int_{a}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(X>t)\,dt.

Applying this with
X=|Z|
and
a=\tau
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\bigl[|Z-\psi_{\tau}(Z)|\bigr]=\int_{\tau}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(|Z|>t)\,dt.

Since
\tau\geq 4S
, the assumed tail bound is valid for every
t\geq\tau
, so

\mathbb{E}\bigl[|Z-\psi_{\tau}(Z)|\bigr]\leq 2^{2p+1}S^{p}\int_{\tau}^{\infty}t^{-p}\,dt.

Since
p>1
, we have

\int_{\tau}^{\infty}t^{-p}\,dt=\frac{\tau^{1-p}}{p-1}.

Hence

\mathbb{E}\bigl[|Z-\psi_{\tau}(Z)|\bigr]\leq\frac{2^{2p+1}}{p-1}\,S^{p}\tau^{1-p}.

∎

Proof of Lemma
B.2
.

Fix
x
, and condition on
x
. By the fundamental theorem of calculus,

\frac{F(x+\mu u;\xi)-F(x-\mu u;\xi)}{2\mu}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\bigl\langle\nabla F(x+\mu su;\xi),u\bigr\rangle\,ds.

Similarly,

\frac{f(x+\mu u)-f(x-\mu u)}{2\mu}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\bigl\langle\nabla f(x+\mu su),u\bigr\rangle\,ds.

Hence

\frac{F(x+\mu u;\xi)-F(x-\mu u;\xi)}{2\mu}-D_{\mu}f(x,u)=\bigl\langle\nabla F(x;\xi)-\nabla f(x),u\bigr\rangle+R(x,u,\xi),

where

\displaystyle R(x,u,\xi)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\bigl\langle\nabla F(x+\mu su;\xi)-\nabla F(x;\xi),u\bigr\rangle\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\bigl\langle\nabla f(x+\mu su)-\nabla f(x),u\bigr\rangle\,ds.

By Assumption
1.2
and Remark
1.5
, both

F(\cdot;\xi)
and
f
are
L
-smooth. Therefore

|R(x,u,\xi)|\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}L\mu|s|\,ds+\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}L\mu|s|\,ds=L\mu.

Thus

\left|\frac{F(x+\mu u;\xi)-F(x-\mu u;\xi)}{2\mu}-D_{\mu}f(x,u)\right|\leq|\langle\zeta,u\rangle|+L\mu\quad\text{where }\quad\zeta:=\nabla F(x;\xi)-\nabla f(x).

Define

v:=\begin{cases}\zeta/\|\zeta\|_{2},&\zeta\neq 0,\\[4.0pt]
e_{1},&\zeta=0,\end{cases}

where
e_{1}
is the first canonical basis vector, and let

V:=\sqrt{d}\,|U_{1}|,\quad\text{where}\quad U\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}),

is chosen independently of
\xi
. Since
u\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, by rotational
invariance we have, conditionally on
x
and
\xi
,

\sqrt{d}\,|\langle v,u\rangle|\overset{d}{=}V.

Since
|\langle\zeta,u\rangle|=\frac{\|\zeta\|_{2}}{\sqrt{d}}\,\sqrt{d}\,|\langle v,u\rangle|
, it follows that,
conditionally on
x
and
\xi
,

|\langle\zeta,u\rangle|\overset{d}{=}\frac{\|\zeta\|_{2}}{\sqrt{d}}\,V.

Moreover, since
1<p\leq 2
,

\mathbb{E}[V^{p}]=d^{p/2}\mathbb{E}[|U_{1}|^{p}]\leq d^{p/2}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[U_{1}^{2}]\bigr)^{p/2}=1.

Therefore, for every
r>0
,

\displaystyle\Pr\!\left(|\langle\zeta,u\rangle|>r\;\middle|\;x\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Pr\!\left(|\langle\zeta,u\rangle|>r\;\middle|\;x,\xi\right)\middle|\;x\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Pr\!\left(\frac{\|\zeta\|_{2}}{\sqrt{d}}V>r\;\middle|\;x,\xi\right)\middle|\;x\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Pr\!\left(\|\zeta\|_{2}>\frac{r\sqrt{d}}{V}\;\middle|\;x,V\right)\middle|\;x\right].

Since
V
is independent of
\xi
, Assumption
1.3
yields

\Pr\!\left(\|\zeta\|_{2}>\frac{r\sqrt{d}}{V}\;\middle|\;x,V\right)\leq\left(\frac{\sigma V}{r\sqrt{d}}\right)^{p}.

Hence

\displaystyle\Pr\!\left(|\langle\zeta,u\rangle|>r\;\middle|\;x\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\frac{\sigma V}{r\sqrt{d}}\right)^{p}\middle|\;x\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\sigma}{r\sqrt{d}}\right)^{p}\mathbb{E}[V^{p}]\leq\frac{\sigma^{p}}{r^{p}d^{p/2}}.

Finally, for every
s\geq 1
, since
L\mu\leq L\mu s
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\!\left(\left|\frac{F(x+\mu u;\xi)-F(x-\mu u;\xi)}{2\mu}-D_{\mu}f(x,u)\right|>\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{d}}+L\mu\right)s\;\middle|\;x\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\Pr\!\left(|\langle\zeta,u\rangle|+L\mu>\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{d}}+L\mu\right)s\;\middle|\;x\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\Pr\!\left(|\langle\zeta,u\rangle|>\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{d}}\,s\;\middle|\;x\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left(\frac{\sigma}{(\sigma/\sqrt{d})s\,\sqrt{d}}\right)^{p}=s^{-p}.

∎

Proof of Lemma
B.3
.

Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
f(x)-f_{*}\leq 4\bar{\Delta}_{0}
, and let
u\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
.
By the fundamental theorem of calculus,

D_{\mu}f(x,u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\bigl\langle\nabla f(x+\mu su),u\bigr\rangle\,ds=\langle\nabla f(x),u\rangle+R_{\mu}(x,u),

where

R_{\mu}(x,u):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\bigl\langle\nabla f(x+\mu su)-\nabla f(x),u\bigr\rangle\,ds.

Since
f
is
L
-smooth by Remark
1.5
,

|R_{\mu}(x,u)|\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}L\mu|s|\,ds=\frac{L\mu}{2}.

Set
g:=\nabla f(x)
. By Lemma
B.14
and the localization

f(x)-f_{*}\leq 4\bar{\Delta}_{0}
,

\|g\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2L(f(x)-f_{*})\leq 8L\bar{\Delta}_{0}.

If
g=0
, then

|D_{\mu}f(x,u)|\leq\frac{L\mu}{2}\leq L\mu s
for every
s\geq 1,

so the claim is immediate.

Assume now that
g\neq 0
, and define

v:=\frac{g}{\|g\|_{2}},
where
Z:=\sqrt{d}\,|\langle v,u\rangle|.

By rotational invariance,

Z\overset{d}{=}\sqrt{d}\,|U_{1}|,\quad\text{where}\quad U\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}).

Hence, since
1<p\leq 2
,

\mathbb{E}[Z^{p}]=d^{p/2}\mathbb{E}[|U_{1}|^{p}]\leq d^{p/2}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[U_{1}^{2}]\bigr)^{p/2}=1.

Therefore, for every
r>0
, Markov’s inequality gives

\mathbb{P}_{u}\!\left(|\langle g,u\rangle|>r\right)=\mathbb{P}_{u}\!\left(Z>\frac{r\sqrt{d}}{\|g\|_{2}}\right)\leq\left(\frac{\|g\|_{2}}{r\sqrt{d}}\right)^{p}\mathbb{E}[Z^{p}]\leq\left(\frac{\|g\|_{2}}{r\sqrt{d}}\right)^{p}.

Now let
s\geq 1
. Since
\|g\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{8L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}_{u}\!\left(|\langle g,u\rangle|>\frac{\sqrt{8L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}}{\sqrt{d}}\,s\right)\leq s^{-p}.

Using

|D_{\mu}f(x,u)|\leq|\langle g,u\rangle|+\frac{L\mu}{2}

and the fact that
\frac{L\mu}{2}\leq L\mu s
for
s\geq 1
, we conclude

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{u}\!\left(|D_{\mu}f(x,u)|>\left(\frac{\sqrt{8L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}}{\sqrt{d}}+L\mu\right)s\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{u}\!\left(|\langle g,u\rangle|>\frac{\sqrt{8L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}}{\sqrt{d}}\,s\right)

\displaystyle\leq s^{-p}.

∎

Proof of Lemma
B.5
.

Let

S:=\frac{\sqrt{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}+\sigma}{\sqrt{d}}+L\mu.

Since

\mathbb{E}[Y\mid u]=\frac{f(x+\mu u)-f(x-\mu u)}{2\mu}=D_{\mu}f(x,u),

we have

d\,\mathbb{E}[Yu]=d\,\mathbb{E}[D_{\mu}f(x,u)u]=\nabla f_{\mu}(x).

Therefore,

d\,\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\tau}(Y)u]-\nabla f_{\mu}(x)=d\,\mathbb{E}[(\psi_{\tau}(Y)-Y)u].

It holds that

\Bigl\|d\,\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\tau}(Y)u]-\nabla f_{\mu}(x)\Bigr\|_{2}\leq d\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[|\psi_{\tau}(Y)-Y|\bigr].

By Lemma
B.4
, the random variable
Y
satisfies

\forall t\geq 4S,\quad\mathbb{P}(|Y|>t)\leq 2^{2p+1}S^{p}t^{-p}.

Since the lemma assumes
\tau\geq 4S
, Lemma
B.1
applies and yields

\mathbb{E}[|Y-\psi_{\tau}(Y)|]\leq\frac{2^{2p+1}}{p-1}S^{p}\tau^{1-p}.

Substituting this into the previous bound gives

\Bigl\|d\,\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\tau}(Y)u]-\nabla f_{\mu}(x)\Bigr\|_{2}\leq\frac{2^{2p+1}}{p-1}\,d\,S^{p}\tau^{1-p}.

Recalling the definition of
S
, this is exactly

\Bigl\|d\,\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\tau}(Y)u]-\nabla f_{\mu}(x)\Bigr\|_{2}\leq\frac{2^{2p+1}}{p-1}\,d\left(\frac{\sqrt{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}+\sigma}{\sqrt{d}}+L\mu\right)^{p}\tau^{1-p}.

For the equivalent form, let

g(x):=\frac{d}{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell})u_{\ell}

where
(Y_{\ell},u_{\ell})
are i.i.d. copies of
(Y,u)
. Then

\mathbb{E}[g(x)]=d\,\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\tau}(Y)u],

and therefore

\mathbb{E}[g(x)]-\nabla f_{\mu}(x)=d\,\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\tau}(Y)u]-\nabla f_{\mu}(x).

Thus the same bound holds for

B:=\mathbb{E}[g(x)]-\nabla f_{\mu}(x).

Proof of Lemma
B.6
.

We have

\psi_{\tau}(z)=\operatorname{sgn}(z)\min\{|z|,\tau\},

so

\psi_{\tau}(Z)^{2}=\min\{Z^{2},\tau^{2}\}.

Using the layer-cake formula,

\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\tau}(Z)^{2}]=2\int_{0}^{\infty}t\,\mathbb{P}(|\psi_{\tau}(Z)|>t)\,dt=2\int_{0}^{\tau}t\,\mathbb{P}(|Z|>t)\,dt.

We split the integral at
4S
:

\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\tau}(Z)^{2}]=2\int_{0}^{4S}t\,\mathbb{P}(|Z|>t)\,dt+2\int_{4S}^{\tau}t\,\mathbb{P}(|Z|>t)\,dt.

Since
\mathbb{P}(|Z|>t)\leq 1
, the first term is bounded by

2\int_{0}^{4S}t\,dt=16S^{2}.

For the second term, use the assumed tail bound:

2\int_{4S}^{\tau}t\,\mathbb{P}(|Z|>t)\,dt\leq 2^{2p+2}S^{p}\int_{4S}^{\tau}t^{1-p}\,dt.

Now, for
4S\leq t\leq\tau
and
1<p\leq 2
,

t^{1-p}=t^{-1}t^{2-p}\leq t^{-1}\tau^{2-p},

hence

\int_{4S}^{\tau}t^{1-p}\,dt\leq\tau^{2-p}\int_{4S}^{\tau}\frac{dt}{t}=\tau^{2-p}\log\!\left(\frac{\tau}{4S}\right).

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\tau}(Z)^{2}]\leq 16S^{2}+2^{2p+2}S^{p}\tau^{2-p}\log\!\left(\frac{\tau}{4S}\right).

Since
\tau\geq 4S
and
1<p\leq 2
,

S^{2}\leq S^{p}\tau^{2-p},

so

16S^{2}\leq 16S^{p}\tau^{2-p}.

Also,

\log\!\left(\frac{\tau}{4S}\right)\leq\log\!\left(\frac{\tau}{S}\right).

Thus

\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\tau}(Z)^{2}]\leq\Bigl(16+2^{2p+2}\log(\tau/S)\Bigr)S^{p}\tau^{2-p}.

Finally, since
1<p\leq 2
, we have
2^{2p+2}\leq 64
. Hence

\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\tau}(Z)^{2}]\leq 64\,S^{p}\tau^{2-p}\bigl(1+\log(\tau/S)\bigr).

∎

Proof of Lemma

.

Let

∎

Proof of Proposition
B.10
.

Since
f
is
L
-smooth, its spherical smoothing
f_{\mu}
is also
L
-smooth. Hence

f_{\mu}(x^{+})\leq f_{\mu}(x)+\left\langle\nabla f_{\mu}(x),x^{+}-x\right\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\left\|x^{+}-x\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Using
x^{+}-x=-\alpha g(x)
, we obtain

f_{\mu}(x^{+})\leq f_{\mu}(x)-\alpha\left\langle\nabla f_{\mu}(x),g(x)\right\rangle+\frac{L\alpha^{2}}{2}\left\|g(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Now write

g(x)=\nabla f_{\mu}(x)+e(x),

so that

\left\langle\nabla f_{\mu}(x),g(x)\right\rangle=\left\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\langle\nabla f_{\mu}(x),e(x)\right\rangle.

Moreover,

\left\|g(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}=\left\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x)+e(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\left\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}+2\left\|e(x)\right\|_{2}^{2},

and, by Young’s inequality,

\left|\left\langle\nabla f_{\mu}(x),e(x)\right\rangle\right|\leq\left\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x)\right\|_{2}\,\left\|e(x)\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\left\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|e(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Substituting these bounds into the previous inequality yields

f_{\mu}(x^{+})\leq f_{\mu}(x)-\alpha\Bigl(\frac{3}{4}-L\alpha\Bigr)\left\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha(1+L\alpha)\left\|e(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}.

If
\alpha\leq\frac{1}{4L}
, then

\frac{3}{4}-L\alpha\geq\frac{1}{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad 1+L\alpha\leq\frac{5}{4}.

Therefore,

f_{\mu}(x^{+})\leq f_{\mu}(x)-\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{5\alpha}{4}\left\|e(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
B.11
.

Set

\lambda:=\log\frac{T}{\delta}.

Since
T\geq 3
and
\delta\in(0,1]
, we have

\lambda\geq\log 3>1.

We first define the filtration generated by the algorithm. Let

\mathcal{F}_{-1}:=\sigma(x_{0}),

and for
t\geq 0
, define

\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\sigma\!\left(x_{0},\{u_{s,\ell},\xi_{s,\ell}:0\leq s\leq t,\ 1\leq\ell\leq M\}\right).

Then

is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable, while
g_{t}
is

\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable. Moreover, conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
,
the fresh samples

For each

, define the localization event

\mathcal{L}_{t}:=\Bigl\{f(x_{s})-f_{*}\leq 4\bar{\Delta}_{0}\ \text{for all }s=0,\dots,t\Bigr\}.

Since
x_{0},\dots,x_{t}
are
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable, we have

\mathcal{L}_{t}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1}.

Also define the estimator-good event

\mathcal{E}_{t}:=\Bigl\{\left\|g_{t}-\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})\right\|_{2}\leq\eta_{0}(M,\mu,\delta,T)\Bigr\}.

On the event
\mathcal{L}_{t}
, the point
x_{t}
satisfies

f(x_{t})-f_{*}\leq 4\bar{\Delta}_{0}.

Therefore, conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, we may apply
Corollary
B.9
to the fixed point
x_{t}
,
using the fresh samples at iteration
t
. Hence, almost surely,

\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{c}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\leq\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\frac{\delta}{T}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{c}\cap\mathcal{L}_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}^{c}}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}^{c}}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{c}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\frac{\delta}{T}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{T}.

By the union bound,

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{t=0}^{T-1}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{c}\cap\mathcal{L}_{t})\right)\leq\delta.

Define

\mathcal{G}:=\bigcap_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(\mathcal{L}_{t}^{c}\cup\mathcal{E}_{t}\right).

Since

\mathcal{G}^{c}=\bigcup_{t=0}^{T-1}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{c}\cap\mathcal{L}_{t}),

it holds that

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G})\geq 1-\delta.

We work on the good event
\mathcal{G}

for the rest of the proof.
We first prove by induction that the iterates remain localized. Clearly

f(x_{0})-f_{*}=\Delta_{0}\leq\bar{\Delta}_{0}\leq 4\bar{\Delta}_{0},

so
\mathcal{L}_{0}
holds. Suppose that
\mathcal{L}_{t}
holds for some

t\leq T-2
. Then, on the good event,
\mathcal{E}_{s}
holds for every

s=0,\dots,t
, since
\mathcal{L}_{t}
implies
\mathcal{L}_{s}
for all

s\leq t
. Proposition
B.10
gives, for
every

,

f_{\mu}(x_{s+1})\leq f_{\mu}(x_{s})-\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{s})\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{5\alpha}{4}\eta_{0}(M,\mu,\delta,T)^{2}.

Dropping the negative term and summing from
s=0
to
t
, we obtain

f_{\mu}(x_{t+1})-f_{*}\leq f_{\mu}(x_{0})-f_{*}+\frac{5\alpha}{4}(t+1)\eta_{0}(M,\mu,\delta,T)^{2}.

We have
t+1\leq T
, and using Lemma
B.17
,

f_{\mu}(x_{0})-f_{*}\leq f(x_{0})-f_{*}+\frac{L\mu^{2}}{2}=\bar{\Delta}_{0}
, then

f_{\mu}(x_{t+1})-f_{*}\leq\bar{\Delta}_{0}+\frac{5\alpha T}{4}\eta_{0}(M,\mu,\delta,T)^{2}.

Now
\alpha=\frac{1}{4L}
,
\eta_{0}\leq\eta\leq\epsilon/4
, and

T=\max\left\{3,\left\lceil\frac{32L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right\rceil\right\}\leq\frac{96L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}.

Indeed, if
a:=32L\bar{\Delta}_{0}/\epsilon^{2}
, then the assumption

\epsilon^{2}\leq 32L\bar{\Delta}_{0}
gives
a\geq 1
, and hence

\max\{3,\lceil a\rceil\}\leq 3a=\frac{96L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}.

Therefore,

\frac{5\alpha T}{4}\eta_{0}^{2}\leq\frac{5}{16L}\cdot\frac{96L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{16}=\frac{15}{8}\bar{\Delta}_{0}.

Consequently,

f_{\mu}(x_{t+1})-f_{*}\leq\frac{23}{8}\bar{\Delta}_{0}.

Using Lemma
B.17
,

f(x_{t+1})-f_{*}\leq f_{\mu}(x_{t+1})-f_{*}+\frac{L\mu^{2}}{2}\leq\frac{23}{8}\bar{\Delta}_{0}+\bar{\Delta}_{0}=\frac{31}{8}\bar{\Delta}_{0}<4\bar{\Delta}_{0}.

Thus
\mathcal{L}_{t+1}
holds. By induction,
\mathcal{L}_{t}
holds for all

t=0,\dots,T-1
. Hence, on the good event,
\mathcal{E}_{t}
holds for all

t=0,\dots,T-1
.

over
t=0,\dots,T-1
, and using
\left\|e_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq\eta_{0}(M,\mu,\delta,T)
, gives

\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq f_{\mu}(x_{0})-f_{*}+\frac{5\alpha T}{4}\eta_{0}(M,\mu,\delta,T)^{2}.

f_{\mu}(x_{0})-f_{*}\leq\bar{\Delta}_{0}

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{2\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{\alpha T}+\frac{5}{2}\eta_{0}(M,\mu,\delta,T)^{2}.

Using
\alpha=\frac{1}{4L}
and

T\geq\frac{32L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}},

we get

\frac{2\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{\alpha T}=\frac{8L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{T}\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4}.

Therefore,

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4}+\frac{5}{2}\eta_{0}(M,\mu,\delta,T)^{2}.

Using Lemma
B.17
,

\left\|\nabla f(x_{t})\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})\right\|_{2}+L\mu.

Hence

\left\|\nabla f(x_{t})\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\left\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})\right\|_{2}^{2}+2L^{2}\mu^{2}.

Averaging over
t=0,\dots,T-1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\nabla f(x_{t})\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4}+\frac{5}{2}\eta_{0}^{2}\right)+2L^{2}\mu^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}+5\eta_{0}^{2}+2L^{2}\mu^{2}.

Since

\eta=\eta_{0}+L\mu,

we have

5\eta_{0}^{2}+2L^{2}\mu^{2}\leq 5(\eta_{0}+L\mu)^{2}=5\eta^{2}.

Using the assumption
\eta\leq\epsilon/4
, we conclude that

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\nabla f(x_{t})\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}+5\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{16}=\frac{13}{16}\epsilon^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}.

∎

Appendix C
Proofs for the High-Probability Analysis of Momentum RSC-ZO

This appendix proves the high-probability guarantee for the momentum variant of
RSC-ZO. The proof builds on the localized scalar-clipping estimates used for
the base method and adds a momentum-tracking argument. The main quantity to
control is the error between the momentum vector
m_{t}
and the smoothed
gradient
\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})
, while keeping the iterates in the localized
region where the one-step estimator bounds apply.

Roadmap.

We first decompose each clipped estimator into its conditional mean, centered
fluctuation, and clipping bias. We then control the geometrically weighted
fluctuation accumulated by the momentum recursion. After removing this
fluctuation, the remaining tracking error satisfies a stable deterministic
recurrence. Combining this recurrence with the Lyapunov function defined below
and a first-exit localization step gives the convergence bound.

C.1
Setup and notation

We work under Assumptions
1.1
,
1.2
, and

1.3
. Write

\Delta_{0}:=f(x_{0})-f_{*},\qquad\bar{\Delta}_{0}:=\Delta_{0}+\frac{L\mu^{2}}{2},\qquad S_{\mu}:=\frac{\sqrt{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}+\sigma}{\sqrt{d}}+L\mu.

Thus
dS_{\mu}=\sqrt{d}(\sqrt{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}+\sigma)+dL\mu
.

Let
\beta\in[1/2,1)
,
T\geq 4
,
\delta\in(0,1]
, and

M_{0},M\in\mathbb{N}
. Define

\lambda:=\log(2T/\delta)
,
\lambda_{0}:=\log(2/\delta)
, and assume

M\geq(1-\beta)\lambda
,
M_{0}\geq\lambda_{0}
. The running and warm-start
thresholds are

\tau:=8S_{\mu}\left(\frac{M}{(1-\beta)\lambda}\right)^{1/p},\qquad\tau_{0}:=8S_{\mu}\left(\frac{M_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right)^{1/p}.

We also write
q:=(1-\beta)\lambda/M
and
q_{0}:=\lambda_{0}/M_{0}

For

\mathcal{F}_{-1}:=\sigma(x_{0})

\mathcal{F}_{0}

\mathcal{F}_{t}

. Then
x_{t}
is

\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable for every
t\geq 1
, while
g_{t},m_{t},x_{t+1}

\mathcal{F}_{t}

t\geq 1

Then
\mathcal{L}_{t}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1}

C.2

We use the following localized one-step consequences of the scalar-clipping

Lemma C.1

(Localized clipped-estimator bounds)
.

There exist absolute constants
C_{\rm bias},C_{\rm var}>0
such that the
following holds. Let

\mathcal{H}
-measurable and assume

conditionally independent of
\mathcal{H}

Y_{\ell}:=\frac{F(x+\mu u_{\ell};\xi_{\ell})-F(x-\mu u_{\ell};\xi_{\ell})}{2\mu},\qquad Z_{\ell}:=\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell})u_{\ell},\qquad g:=\frac{d}{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}Z_{\ell}.

\bar{g}:=\mathbb{E}[g\mid\mathcal{H}]

\|\bar{g}-\nabla f_{\mu}(x)\|_{2}\leq C_{\rm bias}dS_{\mu}^{p}\tau^{1-p}.

W_{\ell}:=(d/M)(Z_{\ell}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{\ell}\mid\mathcal{H}])

\mathcal{H}

\|W_{\ell}\|_{2}\leq\frac{2d\tau}{M}\quad\text{a.s.},\qquad\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}[\|W_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{H}]\leq C_{\rm var}\frac{d^{2}}{M}S_{\mu}^{p}\tau^{2-p}\left(1+\log\frac{\tau}{S_{\mu}}\right).

Proof.

Under localization, the shared-randomness directional estimator satisfies

\Pr(|Y_{\ell}|>r\mid\mathcal{H})\lesssim\left(\frac{S_{\mu}}{r}\right)^{p},\qquad r\geq S_{\mu}.

Therefore

\|\bar{g}-\nabla f_{\mu}(x)\|_{2}\leq d\,\mathbb{E}[|Y_{\ell}|\mathbf{1}\{|Y_{\ell}|>\tau\}\mid\mathcal{H}]\lesssim dS_{\mu}^{p}\tau^{1-p}.

Also
\|Z_{\ell}\|_{2}\leq\tau
, and hence

\|W_{\ell}\|_{2}\leq\frac{d}{M}\left(\|Z_{\ell}\|_{2}+\mathbb{E}[\|Z_{\ell}\|_{2}\mid\mathcal{H}]\right)\leq\frac{2d\tau}{M}.

Finally,

\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}[\|W_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{H}]\leq\frac{4d^{2}}{M}\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell})^{2}\mid\mathcal{H}],

and weak-
L_{p}
tail integration gives

\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell})^{2}\mid\mathcal{H}]\lesssim S_{\mu}^{p}\tau^{2-p}\left(1+\log\frac{\tau}{S_{\mu}}\right).

Absorbing numerical constants into
C_{\rm bias},C_{\rm var}
proves the
claim.
∎

Lemma C.2

(Vector Freedman)
.

Let
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be a martingale-difference sequence in a Hilbert space
with respect to a filtration
(\mathcal{H}_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
. Assume

\|X_{i}\|_{2}\leq R
a.s. for all
i
, and

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\|X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{H}_{i-1}]\leq V\quad\text{a.s.}

Then there is an absolute constant
C_{F}>0
such that, for every
\eta>0
,
with probability at least
1-e^{-\eta}
,

\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right\|_{2}\leq C_{F}(\sqrt{V\eta}+R\eta).

C.3
Stopped weighted fluctuation bound

We first control the stochastic fluctuation accumulated by the momentum
recursion. The stopped process
z_{t}
is a geometrically weighted sum of
centered batch errors on the localized event.

Lemma C.3

(Stopped weighted fluctuation)
.

If
C_{U}
is sufficiently large, then for every
t\in\{1,\ldots,T-1\}
,

\Pr(\|z_{t}\|_{2}>U)\leq\frac{\delta}{2T}.

Consequently, the event

\mathcal{E}:=\left\{\max_{1\leq t\leq T-1}\|z_{t}\|_{2}\leq U\right\}

satisfies
\Pr(\mathcal{E})\geq 1-\delta/2
.

Proof.

Fix
t\in\{1,\ldots,T-1\}
. For
s\leq t
, set

Z_{s,\ell}:=\psi_{\tau}(Y_{s,\ell})u_{s,\ell}
,

\bar{Z}_{s}:=\mathbb{E}[Z_{s,1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{s-1}]
, and

W_{s,\ell}:=(d/M)(Z_{s,\ell}-\bar{Z}_{s})
. Then

a_{s}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}W_{s,\ell}
, and therefore

z_{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\beta^{t-s}(1-\beta)\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{L}_{s}}W_{s,\ell}.

Reveal the samples
(s,\ell)
in lexicographic order and define

\mathcal{H}_{s,\ell}:=\sigma\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{s-1},\{(u_{s,j},\xi_{s,j}):1\leq j\leq\ell\}\right),\qquad\mathcal{H}_{s,0}:=\mathcal{F}_{s-1}.

Since
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{L}_{s}}
is
\mathcal{F}_{s-1}
-measurable and

\mathbb{E}[W_{s,\ell}\mid\mathcal{H}_{s,\ell-1}]=0
, the variables

X_{s,\ell}^{(t)}:=\beta^{t-s}(1-\beta)\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{L}_{s}}W_{s,\ell}

form a Hilbert-valued martingale-difference array. On
\mathcal{L}_{s}
,
Lemma
C.1
gives

\|X_{s,\ell}^{(t)}\|_{2}\leq\frac{2d\tau(1-\beta)}{M}=:R,

and

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}[\|X_{s,\ell}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{H}_{s,\ell-1}]

\displaystyle\leq C_{\rm var}\frac{d^{2}(1-\beta)}{M}S_{\mu}^{p}\tau^{2-p}\left(1+\log\frac{\tau}{S_{\mu}}\right)=:V.

Here we used

(1-\beta)^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\beta^{2j}\leq 1-\beta
. Applying
Lemma
C.2
with
\eta=\lambda
, with probability at
least
1-e^{-\lambda}=1-\delta/(2T)
,

\|z_{t}\|_{2}\leq C_{F}(\sqrt{V\lambda}+R\lambda).

It remains to simplify the two terms. Since

\tau/S_{\mu}=8q^{-1/p}
and
q=(1-\beta)\lambda/M\leq 1
,

R\lambda=\frac{2d\tau(1-\beta)\lambda}{M}=16dS_{\mu}q^{\frac{p-1}{p}},

and

\sqrt{V\lambda}\leq CdS_{\mu}q^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\sqrt{1+\log\frac{M}{(1-\beta)\lambda}}.

Choosing
C_{U}
sufficiently large gives

\|z_{t}\|_{2}\leq U
with probability at least
1-\delta/(2T)
. A union bound
over
t=1,\ldots,T-1
gives
\Pr(\mathcal{E})\geq 1-\delta/2
.
∎

C.4
Bias and drift bounds

Lemma C.4

(One-step clipping bias)
.

On
\mathcal{L}_{t}
, for every
t\geq 1
,
\|b_{t}\|_{2}\leq B
. Consequently,
on
\mathcal{L}_{t}
,

\left\|\sum_{s=1}^{t}\beta^{t-s}(1-\beta)b_{s}\right\|_{2}\leq B.

Proof.

On
\mathcal{L}_{t}
, each
x_{s}
,
1\leq s\leq t
, belongs to the localized
region. Lemma
C.1
gives

\|b_{s}\|_{2}\leq C_{\rm bias}dS_{\mu}^{p}\tau^{1-p}\leq CdS_{\mu}q^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\leq B,

where we used
\tau=8S_{\mu}q^{-1/p}
and chose
C_{B}
sufficiently large.
The weighted bound follows from

\sum_{s=1}^{t}\beta^{t-s}(1-\beta)\leq 1
.
∎

Lemma C.5

(Drift recurrence)
.

For every
t\geq 2
,

u_{t}=\beta u_{t-1}+(1-\beta)b_{t}+r_{t},\qquad r_{t}:=\beta(\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t-1})-\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})).

Moreover, if
\beta\in[1/2,1)
, then on
\mathcal{L}_{t}
,

\|u_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1+\beta}{2}\|u_{t-1}\|_{2}^{2}+2(1-\beta)B^{2}+\frac{3\beta^{2}L^{2}\alpha^{2}}{1-\beta}\|m_{t-1}\|_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

Using
m_{t}=\beta m_{t-1}+(1-\beta)g_{t}
and

g_{t}=\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})+a_{t}+b_{t}
, we get

\displaystyle e_{t}

\displaystyle=\beta e_{t-1}+(1-\beta)a_{t}+(1-\beta)b_{t}+\beta(\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t-1})-\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})).

Since
\widehat{z}_{t}=\beta\widehat{z}_{t-1}+(1-\beta)a_{t}
, subtracting gives
the stated recurrence for
u_{t}
.

Now work on
\mathcal{L}_{t}
. Let

q_{t}:=\beta u_{t-1}+(1-\beta)b_{t}
. Since
\|b_{t}\|_{2}\leq B
,

\|q_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\beta\|u_{t-1}\|_{2}^{2}+(1-\beta)B^{2}.

Young’s inequality with
\rho=(1-\beta)/(2\beta)
gives

\|q_{t}+r_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1+\beta}{2}\|u_{t-1}\|_{2}^{2}+2(1-\beta)B^{2}+\frac{3\beta}{1-\beta}\|r_{t}\|_{2}^{2},

where we used
\beta\geq 1/2
. By
L
-smoothness of
f_{\mu}
,

\|r_{t}\|_{2}\leq\beta L\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\|_{2}=\beta L\alpha\|m_{t-1}\|_{2}.

Thus the last term is at most

3\beta^{3}L^{2}\alpha^{2}\|m_{t-1}\|_{2}^{2}/(1-\beta)
, which is bounded by the
displayed term since
\beta<1
. This proves the claim.
∎

C.5
Descent and Lyapunov inequality

Lemma C.6

(Momentum descent)
.

Let
t\geq 1
and assume
\alpha\leq 1/(2L)
. Then

f_{\mu}(x_{t+1})\leq f_{\mu}(x_{t})-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{4}\|m_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\|u_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\|\widehat{z}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}.

In particular, on
\mathcal{L}_{t}\cap\mathcal{E}
,

f_{\mu}(x_{t+1})\leq f_{\mu}(x_{t})-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{4}\|m_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\|u_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha U^{2}.

Proof.

By
L
-smoothness of
f_{\mu}
and
x_{t+1}=x_{t}-\alpha m_{t}
,

f_{\mu}(x_{t+1})\leq f_{\mu}(x_{t})-\alpha\langle\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t}),m_{t}\rangle+\frac{L\alpha^{2}}{2}\|m_{t}\|_{2}^{2}.

Using

-\langle a,b\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\|a-b\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|a\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|b\|_{2}^{2}

with
a=\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})
,
b=m_{t}
, and

m_{t}-\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})=u_{t}+\widehat{z}_{t}
, we obtain

f_{\mu}(x_{t+1})\leq f_{\mu}(x_{t})-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{2}(1-L\alpha)\|m_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|u_{t}+\widehat{z}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}.

Since
\alpha\leq 1/(2L)
,
1-L\alpha\geq 1/2
, and

\|u_{t}+\widehat{z}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\|u_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+2\|\widehat{z}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
. This gives
the first claim. On
\mathcal{L}_{t}
,
\widehat{z}_{t}=z_{t}
, and on

\mathcal{E}
,
\|z_{t}\|_{2}\leq U
, which gives the second claim.
∎

Theorem C.7

(One-step Lyapunov inequality)
.

Assume
\beta\in[1/2,1)
and

\alpha\leq\min\left\{\frac{1}{2L},\frac{1-\beta}{4\sqrt{3}\,\beta L}\right\}.

Then, on
\mathcal{L}_{t+1}\cap\mathcal{E}
, for every
t=1,\ldots,T-2
,

\Phi_{t+1}\leq\Phi_{t}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{8}\|m_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha(U^{2}+4B^{2}).

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{L}_{t+1}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{t}
,
Lemma
C.6
gives

f_{\mu}(x_{t+1})\leq f_{\mu}(x_{t})-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{4}\|m_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\|u_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha U^{2}.

Also, Lemma
C.5
applied at time
t+1
gives

\|u_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1+\beta}{2}\|u_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+2(1-\beta)B^{2}+\frac{3\beta^{2}L^{2}\alpha^{2}}{1-\beta}\|m_{t}\|_{2}^{2}.

Subtracting
\|u_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
, multiplying by
2\alpha/(1-\beta)
, and adding
to the previous display gives

\Phi_{t+1}\leq\Phi_{t}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-\alpha\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{6\beta^{2}L^{2}\alpha^{2}}{(1-\beta)^{2}}\right)\|m_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha U^{2}+4\alpha B^{2}.

The stepsize condition gives

6\beta^{2}L^{2}\alpha^{2}/(1-\beta)^{2}\leq 1/8
. Hence the coefficient of

\|m_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
is at least
1/8
, proving the claim.
∎

C.6
Warm start and initialization

Define

\mathcal{E}_{0}:=\{\|g_{0}-\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{0})\|_{2}\leq G\},\qquad\mathcal{I}:=\{\Phi_{1}-f_{*}\leq 2\bar{\Delta}_{0}\}.

Lemma C.8

(Warm-start deviation)
.

If
C_{G}
is sufficiently large, then

\Pr(\mathcal{E}_{0})\geq 1-\frac{\delta}{2}.

Proof.

The point
x_{0}
satisfies
f(x_{0})-f_{*}\leq\bar{\Delta}_{0}\leq 4\bar{\Delta}_{0}
.
Applying the localized deviation bound from
Corollary
B.8
, with failure probability
\delta/2
,
batch size
M_{0}
, and threshold

\tau_{0}=8S_{\mu}\left(\frac{M_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\right)^{1/p},

gives the claim after increasing
C_{G}
if necessary.
∎

Lemma C.9

(Initialization)
.

Assume
\alpha\leq 1/(4L)
. If

\frac{5\alpha}{4}G^{2}+\frac{2\alpha}{1-\beta}\left(\beta((1+L\alpha)G+L\alpha H_{\mu})+(1-\beta)B\right)^{2}\leq\bar{\Delta}_{0},

then
\mathcal{E}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
. Consequently,

\Pr(\mathcal{I})\geq 1-\delta/2
.

Proof.

Work on
\mathcal{E}_{0}
. Since
x_{1}=x_{0}-\alpha g_{0}
, the one-step descent
inequality for
f_{\mu}
gives

f_{\mu}(x_{1})\leq f_{\mu}(x_{0})-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{0})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{5\alpha}{4}\|g_{0}-\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{0})\|_{2}^{2}.

Thus
f_{\mu}(x_{1})-f_{*}\leq\bar{\Delta}_{0}+(5\alpha/4)G^{2}
. By
(
C.9
), this is at most
2\bar{\Delta}_{0}
. Hence

f(x_{1})-f_{*}\leq f_{\mu}(x_{1})-f_{*}+\frac{L\mu^{2}}{2}\leq 3\bar{\Delta}_{0}<4\bar{\Delta}_{0},

so
\mathcal{L}_{1}
holds on
\mathcal{E}_{0}
.

By smoothness,
\|\nabla f(x_{0})\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2L\Delta_{0}
. Since

\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{0})-\nabla f(x_{0})\|_{2}\leq L\mu
, we have

\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{0})\|_{2}\leq H_{\mu}
, and therefore

\|g_{0}\|_{2}\leq H_{\mu}+G
. Moreover,

\|g_{0}-\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{1})\|_{2}\leq G+L\alpha\|g_{0}\|_{2}\leq(1+L\alpha)G+L\alpha H_{\mu}.

Since
m_{1}=\beta g_{0}+(1-\beta)g_{1}
,

u_{1}=m_{1}-\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{1})-(1-\beta)a_{1}=\beta(g_{0}-\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{1}))+(1-\beta)b_{1}.

Because
\mathcal{L}_{1}
holds, Lemma
C.4
gives

\|b_{1}\|_{2}\leq B
, and hence

\|u_{1}\|_{2}\leq\beta((1+L\alpha)G+L\alpha H_{\mu})+(1-\beta)B.

Combining the last bounds,

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}-f_{*}

\displaystyle\leq\bar{\Delta}_{0}+\frac{5\alpha}{4}G^{2}+\frac{2\alpha}{1-\beta}\left(\beta((1+L\alpha)G+L\alpha H_{\mu})+(1-\beta)B\right)^{2}\leq 2\bar{\Delta}_{0}.

Thus
\mathcal{E}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
. The probability bound follows from
Lemma
C.8
.
∎

C.7
Localized convergence

Theorem C.10

(Localized high-probability convergence for momentum RSC-ZO)
.

Assume the hypotheses of Lemma
C.9
and
Theorem
C.7
. Suppose moreover that

(T-1)\alpha(U^{2}+4B^{2})\leq\bar{\Delta}_{0}.

Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

f(x_{t})-f_{*}\leq 4\bar{\Delta}_{0}\qquad\text{for all }t=1,\ldots,T-1,

and

\frac{1}{T-2}\sum_{t=1}^{T-2}\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{8\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{\alpha(T-2)}+4U^{2}+16B^{2}+2L^{2}\mu^{2}.

Proof.

By Lemmas
C.9
and

C.3
,

\Pr(\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{E})\geq 1-\delta
. Work on

\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{E}
.

We first prove localization. Suppose localization fails before time
T
, and
let

r:=\min\{t\in\{1,\ldots,T-1\}:f(x_{t})-f_{*}>4\bar{\Delta}_{0}\}.

The initialization proof gives
f(x_{1})-f_{*}<4\bar{\Delta}_{0}
, so
r\geq 2
and

\mathcal{L}_{r-1}
holds. For each
t=1,\ldots,r-2
, we have

\mathcal{L}_{t+1}
, so Theorem
C.7
applies.
Dropping the negative terms and summing,

\Phi_{r-1}\leq\Phi_{1}+(r-2)\alpha(U^{2}+4B^{2})\leq f_{*}+2\bar{\Delta}_{0}+(r-2)\alpha(U^{2}+4B^{2}).

By Lemma
C.6
at time
r-1
,

f_{\mu}(x_{r})\leq f_{\mu}(x_{r-1})+\alpha\|u_{r-1}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha U^{2}\leq\Phi_{r-1}+\alpha U^{2},

where we used
2\alpha/(1-\beta)\geq\alpha
. Hence

f_{\mu}(x_{r})-f_{*}\leq 2\bar{\Delta}_{0}+(T-1)\alpha(U^{2}+4B^{2}).

Using
f(x)\leq f_{\mu}(x)+L\mu^{2}/2
and
(
C.10
), we get

f(x_{r})-f_{*}\leq 2\bar{\Delta}_{0}+(T-1)\alpha(U^{2}+4B^{2})+\frac{L\mu^{2}}{2}\leq 4\bar{\Delta}_{0},

contradicting the definition of
r
. Thus
\mathcal{L}_{T-1}
holds.

Now Theorem
C.7
applies for all

t=1,\ldots,T-2
. Summing and using
f_{\mu}(x_{T-1})\geq f_{*}
gives

\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T-2}\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\Phi_{1}-f_{*}+(T-2)\alpha(U^{2}+4B^{2}).

Since
\Phi_{1}-f_{*}\leq 2\bar{\Delta}_{0}
,

\frac{1}{T-2}\sum_{t=1}^{T-2}\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{4\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{\alpha(T-2)}+2U^{2}+8B^{2}.

Finally,
\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|_{2}\leq\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})\|_{2}+L\mu
, and hence

\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\|\nabla f_{\mu}(x_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+2L^{2}\mu^{2}.

Averaging proves the claim.
∎

C.8
Complexity consequence

We first state the complexity in terms of

\bar{\Delta}_{0}=\Delta_{0}+L\mu^{2}/2
. Under the final smoothing choice for

\mu
, this recovers the simpler dependence on
\Delta_{0}
.

Corollary C.11

(Complexity of momentum RSC-ZO)
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, choose
\mu\leq\epsilon/(4Ld)
, and assume

\epsilon^{2}\leq 32L\bar{\Delta}_{0}
. Choose

\alpha=\frac{1-\beta}{16\sqrt{3}\,L},\qquad T=\left\lceil 512\sqrt{3}\,\frac{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{(1-\beta)\epsilon^{2}}\right\rceil+2.

Assume
G\leq c_{0}\epsilon
,
U\leq c_{1}\epsilon
, and

B\leq c_{1}\epsilon
, for sufficiently small absolute constants

c_{0},c_{1}>0
. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\frac{1}{T-2}\sum_{t=1}^{T-2}\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}.

Moreover, up to logarithmic factors and the lower constraints

M_{0}\geq\lambda_{0}
,
M\geq(1-\beta)\lambda
, it is enough to choose

M_{0}=\widetilde{\Theta}\left(\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}(\sqrt{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}+\sigma)+dL\mu}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right),

and

M=\widetilde{\Theta}\left((1-\beta)\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}(\sqrt{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}+\sigma)+dL\mu}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right),

where the hidden constants depend only on
p
. Consequently, the total number
of stochastic function evaluations satisfies

\displaystyle Q_{\rm MOM}

\displaystyle:=2M_{0}+2M(T-1)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{O}\left(\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}(\sqrt{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}+\sigma)+dL\mu}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right)

\displaystyle+\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}(\sqrt{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}+\sigma)+dL\mu}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right).

Under
\mu\leq\epsilon/(4Ld)
, we have

\bar{\Delta}_{0}=\Delta_{0}+O\!\left(\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{Ld^{2}}\right),\qquad dS_{\mu}=\sqrt{d}(\sqrt{L\Delta_{0}}+\sigma)+O(\epsilon).

Thus

Q_{\rm MOM}=\widetilde{O}\left(\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}(\sqrt{L\Delta_{0}}+\sigma)}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right)+\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{L\Delta_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}(\sqrt{L\Delta_{0}}+\sigma)}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right).

Thus momentum reduces the running batch size by a factor of order
1-\beta
,
while preserving the total query complexity up to logarithmic factors and the
additive warm-start cost.

Proof.

We verify the hypotheses of
Theorem
C.10
. The chosen stepsize satisfies

\alpha=\frac{1-\beta}{16\sqrt{3}\,L}\leq\min\left\{\frac{1}{4L},\frac{1-\beta}{4\sqrt{3}\,\beta L}\right\}

for all
\beta\in[1/2,1)
. Also, by smoothness and

\epsilon^{2}\leq 32L\bar{\Delta}_{0}
,

H_{\mu}=\sqrt{2L\Delta_{0}}+L\mu\leq C\sqrt{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}.

Using
G\leq c_{0}\epsilon
,
B\leq c_{1}\epsilon
, and the choice of
\alpha
,
the initialization condition is bounded by

C\left(c_{0}^{2}+c_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{16^{2}\cdot 3}\right)\bar{\Delta}_{0}.

The numerical constant in the stepsize is chosen so that, after taking

c_{0},c_{1}
sufficiently small, the last display is at most

\bar{\Delta}_{0}
. Similarly,
(T-1)\alpha\leq C\bar{\Delta}_{0}/\epsilon^{2}
, and
therefore

(T-1)\alpha(U^{2}+4B^{2})\leq Cc_{1}^{2}\bar{\Delta}_{0}\leq\bar{\Delta}_{0}

after decreasing
c_{1}
. Hence the localization condition holds.

Theorem
C.10
gives

\frac{1}{T-2}\sum_{t=1}^{T-2}\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{8\bar{\Delta}_{0}}{\alpha(T-2)}+4U^{2}+16B^{2}+2L^{2}\mu^{2}.

The chosen
T
, the assumptions on
U,B
, and

\mu\leq\epsilon/(4Ld)
make the right-hand side at most
\epsilon^{2}
after
choosing
c_{0},c_{1}
small enough.

It remains to solve
G\leq c_{0}\epsilon
,
U\leq c_{1}\epsilon
, and

B\leq c_{1}\epsilon
. Since

dS_{\mu}=\sqrt{d}(\sqrt{L\bar{\Delta}_{0}}+\sigma)+dL\mu
, the stated choices of

M_{0}
and
M
satisfy these inequalities up to logarithmic factors. Finally,
each two-point direction uses two stochastic function evaluations, so

Q_{\rm MOM}=2M_{0}+2M(T-1)
. Substituting the estimates for
M_{0},M,T
proves
the complexity bound.
∎

Remark C.12

(Interpretation)
.

The running-phase concentration depends on

\frac{(1-\beta)\lambda}{M}

rather than
\lambda/M
. Equivalently, the momentum buffer has the same
concentration behavior as an estimator with effective running batch size

M_{\rm eff}\asymp\frac{M}{1-\beta}.

This explains why the running batch size can be reduced by a factor of order

1-\beta
. The reduction is coupled to the stability condition

\alpha\lesssim(1-\beta)/L
: choosing
\beta
closer to one reduces the
running batch size, but also forces a proportionally smaller stepsize. The
separate warm start is needed because the first momentum vector does not yet
benefit from time averaging.

Appendix D
Experiments: Clipping Before versus After Aggregation

We now provide a controlled experiment designed to isolate the mechanism behind
scalar clipping in stochastic zeroth-order optimization. The goal is not to
benchmark on a difficult objective, but to test a specific prediction of our
analysis: under heavy-tailed noise, clipping each scalar directional derivative
before aggregation can be substantially more effective than clipping only the
final batched vector estimator.

The latter baseline is important because it is the most direct analogue of
standard gradient clipping in SGD. In first-order stochastic optimization, one
observes a stochastic gradient vector and clips its norm before taking a step.
The corresponding zeroth-order analogue is therefore to first construct the
batched gradient proxy

g_{\rm raw}=\frac{d}{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}Y_{\ell}u_{\ell}

and then apply vector clipping,

g_{\rm vec}=\operatorname{clip}_{r_{\rm vec}}(g_{\rm raw}).

Our method clips earlier, at the level of each scalar directional derivative

Y_{\ell}
, before averaging. We also include the unclipped estimator

g_{\rm raw}
, which corresponds to the standard MeZO-style zeroth-order
update without clipping. Thus, the experiment compares three natural choices:
no clipping, clipping after constructing a gradient-like vector as in standard
SGD gradient clipping, and clipping each zeroth-order directional measurement
before aggregation.

Experimental setup.

We consider the quadratic objective

f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2},

observed through the stochastic zeroth-order oracle

F(x;\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2}+\langle\zeta,x\rangle.

The noise is sparse and heavy-tailed:

\zeta=sAe_{J},

where
s
is uniform on
\{-1,+1\}
,
J
is uniform on

\{1,\ldots,d\}
, and
A
follows a Pareto distribution with tail exponent

p
. Thus

\nabla F(x;\zeta)-\nabla f(x)=\zeta.

For
p\in(1,2)
, this noise has finite first moment but infinite variance.
The random sign ensures that
\mathbb{E}[\zeta]=0
, so the population objective
is exactly
f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2}
. Since the perturbation

\langle\zeta,x\rangle
is linear in
x
, every sample objective remains

1
-smooth.

For a direction
u_{\ell}
, the shared-randomness two-point estimator satisfies

Y_{\ell}=\frac{F(x+\mu u_{\ell};\zeta_{\ell})-F(x-\mu u_{\ell};\zeta_{\ell})}{2\mu}=\langle x,u_{\ell}\rangle+\langle\zeta_{\ell},u_{\ell}\rangle.

We compare three estimators:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle g_{\rm raw}=\frac{d}{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}Y_{\ell}u_{\ell},&\text{raw / MeZO-style estimator},\\[11.99998pt]
\displaystyle g_{\rm vec}=\operatorname{clip}_{r_{\rm vec}}(g_{\rm raw}),&\text{vector clipping after aggregation},\\[11.99998pt]
\displaystyle g_{\rm sc}=\frac{d}{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell})u_{\ell},&\text{scalar clipping before aggregation}.\end{cases}

Why this comparison matters.

The distinction between
g_{\rm vec}
and
g_{\rm sc}
is central.
Final-vector clipping controls only the norm of the already-aggregated vector.
Indeed, when
\|g_{\rm raw}\|_{2}>r_{\rm vec}
,

\operatorname{clip}_{r_{\rm vec}}(g_{\rm raw})=r_{\rm vec}\frac{g_{\rm raw}}{\|g_{\rm raw}\|_{2}},

so the direction of
g_{\rm raw}
is preserved. Therefore, if a few extreme
directional derivatives
Y_{\ell}
dominate the sum, final-vector clipping can
make the step smaller but cannot repair the corrupted direction.

In contrast, scalar clipping acts before aggregation:

Y_{\ell}\mapsto\psi_{\tau}(Y_{\ell}).

Thus each directional contribution is bounded before it enters the average. This
prevents a single heavy-tailed directional derivative from dominating the
estimated direction.

We emphasize that clipping each individual vector
dY_{\ell}u_{\ell}
before
averaging would be essentially equivalent to scalar clipping, since

\|dY_{\ell}u_{\ell}\|_{2}=d|Y_{\ell}|.

The meaningful baseline is therefore clipping the final batched estimator, which
is the natural analogue of standard gradient clipping.

Fair tuning.

For each method, we tune the stepsize using
6
validation seeds. For
final-vector clipping, we additionally tune the vector clipping radius

r_{\rm vec}
, while for scalar clipping we tune the scalar threshold

\tau
. We then fix the selected hyperparameters and evaluate all methods on

20
independent seeds. The reported curves and tables are computed only from
these evaluation seeds, not from the validation seeds used for tuning.

Direction diagnostic.

To verify the mechanism, we measure the cosine similarity between each estimator
and the true gradient:

\frac{\langle g,\nabla f(x)\rangle}{\|g\|_{2}\|\nabla f(x)\|_{2}}.

Figure

shows that the raw and final-vector-clipped
estimators have nearly identical cosine distributions. This is expected because
final-vector clipping only rescales
g_{\rm raw}
and therefore preserves its
direction. By contrast, scalar clipping substantially improves alignment with
the true gradient, increasing the median cosine from approximately
0.317
to
approximately
0.543
.

This confirms that the advantage of scalar clipping is not merely norm control.
The improvement comes from changing the aggregation itself: extreme directional

Summary.

These experiments support the estimator-level mechanism underlying our theory.
Final-vector clipping is useful because it controls the norm of the stochastic
zeroth-order estimate, and indeed it improves over the raw estimator. However,
it preserves the direction of the already-aggregated heavy-tailed estimate.
Scalar clipping acts earlier: it clips each noisy directional derivative before
aggregation. This yields better alignment with the true gradient, lower final
stationarity, and higher success rates across dimensions and tail indices.
