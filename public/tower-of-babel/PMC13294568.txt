Cardiac Diseases in Sudan 1970–2025: Current Situation and Future Prospects
Authors: Eldisugi Hassan Mohammed Humida, Hussain Gadelkarim Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The healthcare system in Sudan has collapsed as a result of the civil conflict, prompting worries over the management of cardiovascular disease (CVD). Using public data, this analysis compiled epidemiological data for heart diseases in Sudan between 1970 and 2025. A search of MEDLINE/PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Scopus, Web of Knowledge and Google Scholar, as well as WHO reports and public databases that satisfied the inclusion criteria yielded a comprehensive collection of literature on CVD in Sudan. CVDs and associated risk factors are prominent due to the country’s diverse geography, poor-quality healthcare system as a result of prolonged conflict, significant population movement, the climate crisis and socio-economic determinants. CVD monitoring in Sudan demands enhanced healthcare systems, data collection methodologies, public awareness, research, supportive legislation, active community participation in health promotion and greater access to technology.

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is one of the leading causes of death worldwide, significantly affecting mortality rates in both high- and low-income countries.1 Cardiovascular disease (CVD) has a global burden, and incidence is expected to rise over the next few decades as the population ages. CVD prevalence, adjusted for age and population, should remain steady, with mortality falling, indicating that primary preventive measures will be mostly unchanged, and improving worldwide treatment outcomes. Hypertension, diet and cholesterol are projected to be the greatest CVD risk factors in the next two decades.2

Africa has one of the highest mortality rates from non-communicable diseases (NCDs) and a disproportionate prevalence of CVD. In 10 years, non-communicable diseases will surpass communicable diseases on the continent. Hypertension, cardiomyopathies and atherosclerosis have escalated due to unmanaged risk factors, resulting in an elevated incidence of stroke and heart failure.3

In Sudan, a prevalence of 2.5% for heart disease has been reported, although this 2006 data pre-dates the formation of South Sudan as an independent country in 2011.4 Hypertensive heart disease, rheumatic heart disease, ischaemic heart disease and cardiomyopathy account for >80% of CVD, and hypertension has a prevalence of about 20%. Local studies indicated inadequate control rates and high levels of target-organ damage, suggesting that many individuals with hypertension are not receiving effective treatment and are at increased risk for serious complications. Congenital heart disease occurs in 0.2% of the paediatric population.4

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is one of the foremost causes of mortality worldwide. Timely identification is linked to enhanced prevention and effective intervention. A single biomarker for detecting the early stages of CAD is currently not available.5 CAD is a significant contributor to heart failure (HF) and is a potential target for treatment. Pharmacological treatment, electrophysiological devices and coronary revascularisation can avoid left ventricular remodelling, sudden mortality and reinfarction as CAD progresses. Clinicians must meticulously evaluate the risk-benefit profile and patient preferences of surgical candidates with CAD and HF to ascertain the optimal revascularisation strategy. Numerous novel pharmacological therapies are being formulated to address CAD and HF.6 CAD and atherosclerosis constitute a significant and widespread concern, and the development of modern diagnostics and treatments for atherosclerotic lesions is moving towards minimally invasive methods.7

Coronary angiography (CA) is the most efficient method to detect atherosclerotic CAD or dynamic stenosis. Expert advice and evidence-based clinical guidelines for CA use are important. Technology has aided the understanding of cardiac pathophysiology and provided new methods for diagnosis and treatment. Understanding potential risks allows clinicians to plan surgery and avoid complications. Interventional cardiology has expanded dramatically since the first CA was performed in 1958, improving its technique and boosting the evidence for its applications.8

Computational tomography CA (CTCA) is increasingly recognised as the preferred diagnostic approach for assessing patients presenting with stable anginal symptoms. CTCA is a reliable method for assessing these patients, showing no significant rise in major adverse cardiovascular events and a decrease in revascularisation rates.9 Despite mounting evidence that CCTA can detect CAD, it remains underused in peri-procedural planning, particularly in the acute setting. CCTA’s sensitivity and specificity for obstructive disease have improved with flow reserve monitoring, and future advances will allow for more accurate cross-sectional plaque characterisation. CCTA could replace ad hoc pre-procedural planning for CAD patients considering percutaneous coronary intervention, improving equipment selection, cath lab safety and efficiency. Implementing routine pre-procedural CCTA before coronary angiography and interventions should be investigated to establish cost and benefits.10

Rheumatic heart disease is the predominant cause of cardiac morbidity and mortality among Sudanese children and young adults, with echocardiographic prevalence rates reaching 19 per 1,000 in children and 61 per 1,000 in individuals aged 10–40 years. Rheumatic heart disease in Sudan presents in a severe form in 85% of cases, with only ~10% of patients having access to surgical intervention.11–17

Peripartum cardiomyopathy and congenital heart disease occur in Sudan at comparable rates to those found in other African nations.4 CAD and emergency coronary revascularisations are essential for the reduction in morbidity and mortality linked to CAD in Sudan, particularly in the context of continuing conflict in the country. Implementing and sustaining this service during armed conflict and on the battlefield poses challenges.18

This review aims to assess the current state and future prospects for cardiac diseases in Sudan by collecting epidemiological metrics using data published from 1970 to 2025.

Methods

Search Strategy

A literature search for articles related to various CVDs in Sudan was conducted in Medline/PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Scopus, Web of Knowledge, Google Scholar, WHO reports and public databases that met the specified inclusion criteria. Terms relevant to cardiology, including angiography, coronary, CVD and CAD, were used in connection with Sudan. No filters were applied.

Inclusion Criteria

Only literature published from 1970 to 2025 regarding cardiac diseases and CAD in Sudan was included. All sources depicting the types of CVDs, together with additional influencing factors, such as hypertension, diabetes and other NCDs, were incorporated into the criterion.

Exclusion Criteria

Publications not in English and those that focused on treatment, pharmacological research and qualitative investigations were excluded. Publications related to laboratory experiments, including animal trials, were also excluded.

Results

There were 408 records identified through database searching. Following initial screening, 280 records had irrelevant titles and abstracts and 76 were duplicates, leaving 52 articles to be assessed for eligibility (Figure 1).

CVDs in Sudan

Formerly, Sudan was the largest nation in Africa and the ninth largest in the world, covering an area of about 2.6 million km2. In the aftermath of South Sudan’s secession in 2011, after decades of war since 1955, Sudan’s territory decreased to roughly 1.88 million km², making it the third largest country in Africa. The estimated population of Sudan in 2026 was 53.2 million.19

Epidemiology

The prevalence of CVD in Sudan in 1970 was largely unrecorded, but CVD was relatively rare in sub-Saharan Africa before the 2000s, with prevalence rates fluctuating between 0.3% and 3% in earlier periods. Nevertheless, this reduced rate may also arise from insufficient diagnostic capabilities and under-reporting, rather than accurately reflecting the incidence of the disease.20,21

The availability of epidemiological data concerning various diseases in many African nations is often restricted and disjointed, but the increasing prevalence of CVD in Africa is becoming a significant public health concern.4 The incidence of CVD in Sudan is increasing compared to global rates, accompanied by worsening outcomes (Figures 2 and 3), mostly due to the years of armed conflict that have led to the collapse of the country’s health system.22–25

CVD is the most prevalent cause of mortality worldwide, causing the deaths of an estimated 19.8 million individuals globally in 2022, which accounted for 32% of all global fatalities.26 MIs and strokes were responsible for 85% of these deaths, and 75% of CVD mortality occurred in low- and middle-income countries.

In 2021, at least 38% of the 18 million premature deaths of people <70 years caused by NCDs were attributable to CVD.27 NCDs, particularly CVDs, are prevalent throughout Africa, where CVDs account for 38.3% of all NCD-related deaths and 22.9 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), primarily affecting people <30 years. In contrast to the pattern observed in most high-income nations, where cardiovascular fatalities have decreased, Africa has seen a 50% increase in CVDs over the past three decades. Inadequate financing and limited research capabilities in sub-Saharan Africa have hampered epidemiological studies and so the real burden of CVD in the region remains unknown.28

Despite inadequate statistics, CVD is the leading cause of death and morbidity in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, including Sudan.29 An analysis of the Global Burden of Disease Study calculated CVD incidence, prevalence, deaths, years of life lost, years lived with disability and DALYs. The authors found that CVD incidence increased from 1990 to 2021 and the age-standardised incidence rate decreased. CVD prevalence increased at stable age-standardised rates, and the age-standardised DALY rate decreased from 11,421.8 to 7,353.8 per 100,000. Men have consistently outnumbered women in incidence, prevalence, death and DALYs. The leading causes of DALYs were ischaemic heart disease, stroke and hypertension. The most significant risk factors in MENA countries were high systolic blood pressure, diet and LDL cholesterol.

Countries where there have been wars or ongoing conflict have higher death, illness burden (measured by DALYs) and disease incidence rates, as conflict often ruins medical infrastructure, decreases access to key health services, and diminishes pharmaceutical and vaccination supplies.30

Risk Factors

The assessment of epidemiological indicators for CVD in Sudan has remained unchanged since 2011 when an analysis of data from the 2006 Sudan Household Study (SHHS) and annual health statistics from the Sudan Federal Ministry of Health indicated a 2.5% prevalence of heart disease.4 Hypertensive heart disease, rheumatic heart disease, ischaemic heart disease and cardiomyopathy represented >80% of CVD in Sudan. The occurrence of hypertension was 20.1% in the SHHS and 20.4% in the WHO STEPwise approach to surveillance data. Regional investigations uncovered insufficient control rates alongside a notable incidence of target-organ damage. The prevalence data for rheumatic heart disease were only available for Khartoum State, revealing a decline from three per 1,000 individuals in the 1980s to 0.3 per 1,000 in 2003. No information was available regarding any other states. In 1989, the incidence of coronary events was 112 per 100,000 individuals, while the mortality rate stood at 36 per 100,000 individuals. The prevalence rates for low physical activity, obesity, hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, diabetes and smoking were recorded at 86.8%, 53.9%%, 23.6%&, 19.8%, 19.2% and 12%, respectively, in a study published in 2011. Peripartum cardiomyopathy manifests in about 1.5% of all deliveries and congenital cardiac disease impacts around 0.2% of the paediatric population.4

Tobacco, unhealthy diets (including excess salt, sugar and fat), obesity, physical inactivity, hazardous alcohol use and air pollution are behavioural and environmental risk factors for most CVDs.27 Diets that were deficient in vegetables were responsible for roughly 682,400 deaths and 16 million DALYs due to CVD globally in 2021, a downward trend in recent years.31 Most of those affected were >75 years old. Predictions suggest that CVD prevalence will increase in lower-middle socio-demographic regions, with African countries potentially facing heightened concerns connected to low levels of vegetable consumption by 2030 and 2050.

CVD Prevention

Daily habits and actions have a major influence on the risk of CVD, particularly coronary heart disease. Research has demonstrated that fitness, nutrition, weight control and quitting smoking all reduce the risk of CVD. These lifestyle practices lowered CVD risk by more than 80% and diabetes risk by more than 90%.32

Despite substantial evidence that lifestyle variables raise CVD, few people adhere to changes. Healthcare providers must aggressively integrate health promotion and advice on healthy lifestyles into their regular practice. This is entirely lacking in Sudan and must be implemented.

Currently, there appears to be a lack of systematic lipid profiling conducted at the population or large-cohort level in Sudan. Without reliable data on the distribution of key lipid biomarkers, it is difficult to accurately evaluate the true burden of dyslipidaemia and its influence on CVD risk. The rising occurrence of NCDs, the impact of socio-economic factors, urbanisation, and disruptions to healthcare and nutrition caused by conflict, underscore the lack of lipid surveillance as a notable gap in public health understanding, which may conceal a substantial, preventable burden of CVD.

Several studies on CVD prevention presented at the 2024 European Society of Cardiology Congress demonstrated significant advances in CVD management. In patients with high LDL cholesterol and acute MI, apolipoprotein A-1 infusions reduced risk. Plozasiran lowered pancreatitis risk by lowering triglycerides and apolipoprotein C-III. A 14-year study connected smoking while young to cardiovascular issues. Baseline high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), LDL cholesterol and Lp(a) levels strongly predicted 30-year outcomes in women. Alternative LDL-lowering therapy lowered diabetes risk and LDL cholesterol, similar to high-intensity statins. Following MI, early combination lipid-lowering therapy improved outcomes. Mediterranean and Nordic diets reduced the risk of atherosclerotic CVD. Researchers presented new lipid-lowering medications, biomarker-based risk prediction and lifestyle interventions for CVD prevention. Early and personalised treatment choices lowered long-term cardiovascular risk, according to these studies.33

Monitoring lifestyle or behavioural risk factors by using quality indicators is critical for primary CVD prevention. In a systematic review of primary care quality indicators conducted to summarise indicators for monitoring lifestyle risk factors, the authors identified 90 distinct indicators.34 The most common were smoking status (21%), weight (18%), smoking cessation (13%), general vaccination against high-risk infectious disease (9%) and physical activity (8%). Alcohol consumption (3%) and healthy eating (2%) were the least reported. Importantly, advising on healthy eating (16%) was more common in the literature than assessing good eating (4%).

Human longevity research has grown in popularity as global life expectancy has increased. Long-lived individuals who have delayed age-related diseases can be used to study healthy ageing and lifespan.35 Longevity is influenced by a range of variables, including genetic, epigenetic, immunological, metabolic and environmental factors. Human lifespan studies demonstrate that genetic polymorphisms, epigenetic changes, diet and physical activity all influence longevity. Understanding these features is vital for devising longevity-promoting measures as life expectancy increases.

Sudan’s armed conflict and inadequate access to healthcare are leading to higher CVD mortality rates among older people (>80 years). Hypertension, ischaemic heart disease, heart failure, AF and cerebrovascular illness are common in this age group and they require ongoing management and emergency care. Conflict-driven interruptions increase risk in prevention, chronic care and emergency response, resulting in more acute events and deaths.

Early Detection

Early identification of CVD enables counselling and medication management. Early diagnosis of CAD in patients with chest pain is difficult and involves invasive methods, which are not widely available in low-income countries. Thus, a non-invasive biomarker for early CAD detection in angina patients is required, as well as a comparison of inflammatory biomarkers of atherosclerosis.36

Progressive CVDs are often not diagnosed early in patients with common comorbidities, where symptoms are not reported or are thought to be related to pre-existing conditions.37 A study that investigated a diagnostic approach using a combination of a symptom and risk factor questionnaire and low-cost, accessible tests for people with type 2 diabetes or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease more than doubled the number of new diagnoses of heart failure, AF and CAD. Such an approach may improve the early detection and treatment of CVD in low-income countries.

Biomedical care, healthcare and disease prediction are using emerging technologies, including artificial intelligence. Machine learning has emerged as an efficient tool for predicting cardiac disease by analysing clinical data, allowing for timely intervention and tailored treatment.38

CVD Intervention

CVDs pose a considerable health challenge in Sudan, reflecting similar trends observed in other African countries. Percutaneous coronary intervention is crucial for the management of CVDs; however, many African countries encounter substantial obstacles in providing these vital services.

In April 2018, Sudan’s government initiated a comprehensive programme to provide cardiac catheterisation, enabling all coronary procedures and selected valve treatments at no cost nationwide. The Sudan National Cardiac Center (NCC) is the primary authority in charge of this programme. In mid-April 2023, armed conflict resumed in Sudan, leading to substantial disruption of healthcare services, particularly in cardiac care. More than 4 million individuals have been displaced, with over 1 million seeking asylum in neighbouring countries.

Armed conflict significantly affects healthcare delivery. Despite the ongoing conflict, Sudan’s government-funded interventional cardiac treatment programme remains operational, albeit with significant limitations. The main challenges are the reallocation of the professional workforce, the availability and management of medical supplies, patient mobility and secure access to operational facilities, as described in the studies showing the history of CVD publications from Sudan (Table 1).39

According to data released by the WHO in 2020, 43,966 deaths in Sudan were attributed to CHD, accounting for 20.16% of all deaths in the country.51 The age-adjusted mortality rate was 231.30 per 100,000 individuals, giving Sudan the 20th highest mortality rate worldwide.

Conclusion

Sudan has a history marked by the prevalence of various endemic and communicable diseases, alongside NCDs in contemporary times. The diverse geography of the country, the decline of the health system as a result of ongoing armed conflict, substantial population displacement, climate variability and socio-economic factors collectively contribute to the high incidence of various health problems, including CVD and its associated risk factors.

Addressing CVD in Sudan requires comprehensive strategies that include enhancing healthcare systems, improving data collection methods, raising public awareness, promoting research initiatives, formulating supportive policies, engaging communities in health promotion and the use of technology. By focusing on these areas, Sudan can improve its capacity to monitor and manage CVD, thereby alleviating their impact on the population.
