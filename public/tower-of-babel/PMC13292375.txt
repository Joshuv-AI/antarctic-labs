Two years into an ongoing war: a six-wave prospective study of PTSD, depression, anxiety, and posttraumatic growth following the October 7, 2023, attack in Israel
Authors: Yossi Levi-Belz, Doron Amsalem, Yoav Groweiss, Carmel Blank, Hili Kohavi, Yuval Neria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe October 7, 2023, terrorist attack in Israel and the ensuing prolonged war represent an unprecedented context of ongoing collective trauma. While early studies documented substantial acute psychiatric burden, the long-term trajectories of distress and posttraumatic growth (PTG) across the general population remain less understood. This study traced these trajectories over 27 months, utilizing a rare pre-war baseline to distinguish war-related outcomes from preexisting distress.MethodsIn this prospective six-wave national cohort study, the analytical sample consisted of 680 Israeli adults (mean age 41.13 years [SD = 13.75]; 343 [50.4%] women; 544 [80.0%] Jewish and 136 [20.0%] Arab) analyzed via a Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) framework. Participants were assessed approximately one month before the attack (T1) and followed up at 1 (T2), 6 (T3), 12 (T4), 18 (T5), and 27 (T6) months post-attack. The analytical sample included 29 (4.3%) participants who reported direct exposure to the attack, 129 (18.9%) who reported the loss of loved one, and 102 (15.0%) reported reserve military service during the war.FindingsDistress followed a parabolic trajectory characterized by a sharp acute rise followed by gradual attenuation toward baseline levels. Probable PTSD rose from 16.1% (T1) to 29.8% at T2 and declined to 19.7% (T6). Probable depression rose from 30.5% at T1 to 43.1% at T2 and significantly declined to 23.3% (T6). Anxiety rose from 22.1% (T1) to 40.1% at T2 and reached 21.0% (T6). Conversely, PTG increased progressively from T1 (M = 2.48, SD = 1.57) to T6 (M = 6.10, SD = 1.91; p < 0.001). Probable PTSD trajectory was significantly predicted by Arab ethnicity (OR = 4.07), and direct exposure (OR = 6.31). Probable depression trajectory was predicted by female sex (OR = 1.82), Arab ethnicity (OR = 3.62), and reserve service (OR = 4.38). Probable anxiety trajectory was predicted by Arab ethnicity (OR = 2.20) and reserve service (OR = 2.93), while higher PTG was uniquely predicted by female sex and the loss of a loved one (p = 0.007).InterpretationLong-term adaptation to ongoing national trauma is characterized by the co-occurrence of symptom stabilization and progressive posttraumatic growth. However, the differential vulnerability identified among Arab citizens, women, and highly exposed groups underscores the necessity for sustained, stratified mental health monitoring while reinforcing the relational and meaning-making resources essential for resilience during prolonged conflict.FundingNot applicable.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Previous research on the mental health consequences of terrorist attacks and prolonged warfare is often limited by cross-sectional designs, post-event data collection, and non-representative sampling methods. Most studies lack true pre-event baseline assessments, which restricts the ability to distinguish war-specific psychopathology from preexisting distress related to prior national instability. Furthermore, longitudinal evidence on the coexistence of psychiatric distress and posttraumatic growth during an ongoing, multi-front military confrontation remains particularly scarce.

Added value of this study

This nationwide prospective cohort study tracks mental health and posttraumatic growth trajectories from a unique pre-war baseline through 27 months of ongoing conflict. Utilizing a Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) framework with an analytical sample of 680 participants, the study found that distress followed a curvilinear trajectory. Findings indicate that distress peaked acutely at 1 month before gradually attenuating toward baseline levels. Specifically, probable PTSD rose from 16.1% at baseline to 29.8% acutely, returning to 19.7% at 27 months (p = 0.149 vs. T1). Depression prevalence significantly declined below baseline levels (23.3% at T6 vs. 30.5% at T1, p = 0.008). Probable anxiety similarly rose from 22.1% at baseline to 40.1% at T2, before reaching 21.0% at T6. Conversely, posttraumatic growth showed a progressive increase from baseline (M = 2.48) to 27 months (M = 6.10, p < 0.001). At 27 months, persistent distress was robustly predicted by Arab ethnicity (PTSD OR = 4.07, 95% CI 2.23–7.41; depression OR = 3.62, 95% CI 1.95–6.72), direct exposure (PTSD OR = 6.31, 95% CI 2.04–19.48), and reserve military service (depression OR = 4.38, 95% CI 1.73–11.08; anxiety OR = 2.93, 95% CI 1.25–6.89).

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings demonstrate that long-term adaptation to mass national trauma involves a dynamic process where distress stabilization and progressive psychological growth occur simultaneously. These results emphasize the necessity for prolonged, stratified mental health monitoring that accounts for the differential vulnerability identified across specific demographic and exposure-related groups. Interventions should shift from general crisis relief toward targeted strategies that promote community belongingness, social support, and meaning-making processes to foster resilience during a protracted national crisis.

Introduction

On October 7, 2023, Israel experienced one of the most devastating terrorist attacks in its history.1 In a single day, more than 1200 civilians and soldiers were murdered, thousands were injured, and many individuals were subsequently displaced, bereaved, or mobilized for reserve duty. In the aftermath, Israel entered a prolonged war with Hamas that later expanded to additional fronts and has continued almost uninterrupted over the past two and a half years, with no clear end in sight. This convergence of extreme initial violence, traumatic loss, prolonged warfare, and sustained societal disruption created a rare national trauma context in which acute and chronic stressors were layered onto one another.2

Early findings indicated a substantial acute psychological burden. Approximately 1 month after the attack, nearly one-third of participants in a nationally representative sample met criteria for probable PTSD, and more than 40% met criteria for probable depression and anxiety, underscoring the scale of the immediate psychiatric impact.3,4 Emerging longitudinal findings suggest that psychiatric symptoms may decline over subsequent months at the Israeli population level.5,6 These findings are in line with previous trauma research, consistently showing that resilience and gradual recovery are common following exposure to trauma7,8 and war.9,10 Bearing in mind the exceptionally severe and continuously unfolding crisis such as October 7 and the prolonged war that followed, it remains unclear how the Israeli population would respond to cope with an ongoing, sustained wartime emergency.

Importantly, emerging evidence suggests that the mental health burden following the October 7 attack and the subsequent war was not distributed evenly across the population but instead concentrated in subgroups characterized by heightened vulnerability. Women and Arab citizens, for example, showed a greater increase from the month before to the month after the attack in probable PTSD, depression, and anxiety than men and Jewish citizens, respectively.11,12

Beyond gender and ethnicity, age and prior trauma history were significantly found to shape individual responses to national trauma. Recent evidence from the current conflict indicates that younger adults exhibit markedly higher levels of acute stress and PTSD symptoms compared to older adults, whose lower distress levels may reflect unique coping mechanisms and accumulated resilience.13 Furthermore, a history of trauma can sensitize individuals to current stressors; for instance, Holocaust survivors with preexisting PTSD have shown heightened anxiety, depression, and war-related PTSD symptoms following the October 7 attack compared to those with low prior PTSD levels.14 These factors likely contribute to the differential psychiatric outcomes observed throughout the prolonged war.

Exposure-related vulnerability was similarly evident, with directly exposed and bereaved individuals showing higher levels of persistent PTSD and depression over time,13,14 as well as reserve-duty combatants who exhibited elevated distress and substantial suicidal risk.5,6 Such findings converge with a broader literature indicating that exposure intensity, traumatic loss, minority status, and ongoing threat shape differential psychiatric outcomes following terrorism and war.15, 16, 17, 18

Importantly, however, most studies of terrorism, disaster, and war lack true pre-event baseline assessments, thereby limiting the ability to distinguish newly emergent psychopathology from preexisting distress; this limitation, emphasized in the 9/11 literature, remains highly consequential for interpreting long-term outcomes.18,19 In the present context, this limitation is particularly important, as the central issue is no longer merely whether symptoms increased in the immediate aftermath of October 7, but whether vulnerability in these higher-risk groups subsided, persisted, or became more differentiated over the prolonged course of an ongoing war.

Alongside the importance of tracing trajectories of distress-related symptoms over time, it is equally important to consider the possibility of a positive psychological change under conditions of ongoing exposure to traumatic events. Posttraumatic growth refers to positive psychological change that emerges from the struggle with highly challenging life circumstances and may be reflected in domains such as appreciation of life, closer relationships, personal strength, new possibilities, and spiritual–existential change,20 which may coexist with ongoing distress. Findings from war-affected populations suggest that positive psychological reorganization may unfold under chronic adversity rather than only after danger has passed.21, 22, 23 In this sense, a comprehensive account of adaptation to October 7 and the ensuing war requires attention not only to the persistence or decline of psychopathology, but also to the possible emergence of growth-related processes in the general population and in potentially high-risk subgroups over time.

The present study

The present study examined prospective longitudinal changes in probable PTSD, depression, anxiety, and posttraumatic growth in a prospective national cohort of Israeli adults following the October 7, 2023, attack and the ensuing war. Baseline data were collected approximately 1 month before the attack as part of an ongoing nationwide study. Because such data are rarely available in studies of terrorism, war, and other mass trauma events, this design provided a rare and methodologically important pre-event assessment (T1). Participants were then reassessed 1 month (T2), 6 months (T3), 1 year (T4), 1.5 years (T5), and 2 years and 3 months (T6) after the attack.

This design enabled us to trace within-person change across the first 27 months of this unfolding crisis and to examine whether final-wave outcomes (T6) were associated with demographic characteristics, baseline symptom levels, and key exposure-related experiences. The inclusion of a true pre-war time point also allowed a more rigorous interpretation of longitudinal comparisons, as changes across waves could be evaluated against mental health status measured before the onset of war rather than only against post-event observations. By extending follow-up beyond both the acute and first-year phases3,6 and by examining growth alongside psychopathology, the present study seeks to clarify the nature of long-term adaptation to October 7 and the ensuing war in the Israeli population.

Based on the theoretical framework and existing literature on mass trauma, we formulated three primary hypotheses:a.Distress Trajectories: Distress symptoms (probable PTSD, depression, and anxiety) would follow a quadratic trajectory, characterized by a sharp acute rise one month after the attack followed by a gradual attenuation toward pre-war baseline levels.b.Growth Trajectory: PTG would exhibit a distinct and progressive linear increase across the 27-month follow-up period, reflecting its nature as a slower cognitive process.c.Predictors of Long-term Distress and Growth: long-term distress and growth at the final assessment (T6) would be significantly and independently predicted by demographic factors (e.g., Arab ethnicity, female sex) and trauma-related experiences (e.g., direct exposure, loss of a loved one, and reserve military service), even after accounting for pre-war symptom levels.

Methods

Participants

The present analyses were based on a longitudinal cohort of Israeli adults assessed across six measurement waves following the October 7, 2023, terrorist attack, conducted from August 2023 to January 2026. The cohort originated from a diverse national sample of 908 participants at the pre-attack baseline (T1). Retention rates across the follow-up waves were as follows: 710 (78.2%) at T2, 614 (67.6%) at T3, 600 (66.1%) at T4, 580 (63.9%) at T5, and 560 (61.7%) at the final T6 assessment.

To maximize the utilization of available data and reduce potential bias associated with complete-case analysis, the analytical sample for the current study was defined using a Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) framework. Inclusion was restricted to participants who provided data for at least three of the six assessment waves, resulting in a final analytical sample of 680 individuals. This approach allows for the inclusion of participants with incomplete trajectories while ensuring sufficient data points for robust estimation of both linear and quadratic trends (see flow chart in Supplementary Fig. S1).

To assess potential attrition bias, we compared the analytical sample (n = 680) with participants who were excluded due to providing fewer than three waves of data (n = 228). Chi-square tests and independent-samples t-tests revealed no significant differences between the groups on key sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex, ethnicity) and baseline (T1) mental health variables (PTSD, depression, and anxiety symptoms). These findings suggest that attrition was largely random with respect to the study’s core variables. Detailed comparisons are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Measures

Posttraumatic stress symptoms

Posttraumatic stress symptoms were assessed using the International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ), a validated self-report measure of ICD-11 PTSD and complex PTSD.22,23 Following our earlier reports, we used the 9 PTSD-specific items, including 6 symptom items assessing re-experiencing, avoidance, and sense of threat (2 items per cluster), rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 4, and 3 items assessing functional impairment in relationships, work, and other important life domains. At T1, participants were instructed to rate their symptoms relative to the most significant stressor at that time, which for the vast majority of the sample was the peak of the national judicial reform crisis. From T2 through T6, the index event was explicitly anchored to the October 7 attack and the subsequent war. For continuous analyses, PTSD symptom items were summed to index symptom severity. For dichotomous analyses, probable PTSD was defined according to the ITQ scoring rules, requiring endorsement of at least 1 symptom from each PTSD cluster and at least 1 functional impairment item.24,25 Internal consistency was high across the reported waves, with Cronbach’s α ranging from 0.86 to 0.87.

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2), a brief validated depression screener derived from the PHQ-9.26 The scale includes 2 items reflecting the core DSM depressive symptoms of depressed mood and anhedonia during the previous 2 weeks, rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 to 3. For continuous analyses, item scores were summed to yield a total depression severity score ranging from 0 to 6. For dichotomous analyses, a probable depression variable was defined using the established cutoff score of ≥ 3.26,27 Internal consistency was good across waves, with Cronbach’s α ranging from 0.82 to 0.86.

Anxiety symptoms

Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-2 (GAD-2), a validated 2-item anxiety screener derived from the GAD-7.28,29 The items assess symptom frequency over the previous 2 weeks on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 3. For continuous analyses, item scores were summed to create an anxiety severity score ranging from 0 to 6. For dichotomous analyses, a probable anxiety variable was defined using the standard cutoff score of ≥3, which has shown acceptable diagnostic sensitivity and specificity in prior research.30 Internal consistency was good across waves, with Cronbach’s α ranging from 0.84 to 0.88.

Posttraumatic growth

Posttraumatic growth was assessed using the Posttraumatic Growth Inventory–Short Form (PTGI-SF), a 10-item self-report measure of positive psychological change following highly challenging life events.31 The short form includes two items from each of the five domains assessed in the original Posttraumatic Growth Inventory - relating to others, new possibilities, personal strength, spiritual change, and appreciation of life. At T1, PTG was assessed in relation to significant life events, primarily the ongoing socio-political upheaval surrounding the judicial reform; from T2 onward, the reference event was strictly the October 7 attack and the ensuing war. Items were rated on a 7-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 to 7, with higher scores indicating greater perceived posttraumatic growth. Internal consistency across the study waves was high, with Cronbach’s α ranging from 0.88 to 0.94.

Sociodemographic and trauma-related characteristics

We collected demographic variables including age, sex, and ethnicity. We also assessed three exposure-related characteristics: direct exposure to the October 7 attack (i.e., being present in the Gaza-envelope communities during the attack), loss of a family member or close friend during the attack or the subsequent war, and active reserve military service during the war. Military reserve service and loss of family members or friends due to the war were assessed at each of the six measurement waves. To capture the full extent of impact, participants were categorized as 'reservists' or as having ‘experienced loss' if they reported these statuses at any point during the 27-month follow-up period. As the sample consisted of a national civilian panel of adults, military service refers exclusively to reserve duty and not to mandatory or permanent service.

Procedure

Participants were recruited online through a professional survey company (Panel4All), which maintains a large panel of Israeli adults and enables demographically balanced sampling. As in our earlier reports6 from this cohort, quota sampling was used to approximate national distributions of age, sex, ethnicity, religiosity, education, and socioeconomic status, based on Israeli Central Bureau of Statistics data. While this approach provides a diverse national sample, it is recognized as a non-probability, quota-based framework rather than an unrestricted probability sample. The survey was administered by Qualtrics, and participants received monetary compensation through the survey company.

The study is based on an ongoing prospective national cohort first assessed approximately 1 month before the October 7, 2023, terrorist attack. Assessments were conducted at preattack baseline (T1), approximately 1-month postattack (T2), approximately 6 months postattack (T3), approximately 1-year postattack (T4), approximately 1.5 years postattack (T5), and approximately 2 years and 3 months postattack (T6), yielding a total of six measurement waves included in the present analyses.

Participants received a study information letter describing the study aims and the investigators’ contact details. Participation was voluntary, and electronic informed consent was obtained prior to participation. Participants were assured of anonymity, confidentiality, and their right to withdraw at any stage. Those who completed the baseline survey were recontacted by email and invited to complete each subsequent wave. Because the study included sensitive assessments of trauma-related distress, participants were provided at the end of each survey with contact information for national mental health helplines and support services.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (Ethics Committee) of the Ruppin Academic Center (Protocol No. 175/2023). All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in the study.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all study variables at each wave. Probable PTSD, probable depression, and probable anxiety were coded dichotomously (0 = absent, 1 = present), and are therefore reported as the proportion of participants meeting criteria for the respective probable diagnosis at each time point. Posttraumatic growth was analyzed as a continuous variable.

To evaluate longitudinal trajectories and identify predictors of change, Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) were employed. For binary outcomes (PTSD, depression, and anxiety), a logit link function was used. PTG, as a continuous measure, was modeled using a linear GEE framework. The models included both linear and quadratic time components to test for curvilinear trajectories. To maximize data retention while ensuring robust estimation of these trends, the analysis included all participants who completed at least three measurement waves (N = 680). This approach accounts for within-subject dependency and utilizes all available data points, providing a more precise estimation of population-level changes compared to complete-case analysis.

Hierarchical regression models (logistic for binary distress; linear for PTG) were used to identify predictors of T6 outcomes. Predictors were entered in three blocks: demographics (age, sex, ethnicity), corresponding T1 baseline levels, and war-related exposure (direct exposure, loss, and reserve service). The full hierarchical steps and statistical coefficients are detailed in Supplement 2.

To account for multiple pairwise comparisons across the six measurement waves, alpha levels were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. All analyses were conducted in SPSS version 26.

Longitudinal trajectories and multi-level predictors of psychiatric distress and posttraumatic growth

Probable PTSD trajectories and predictors

The prevalence of probable PTSD exhibited significant fluctuations across the six assessment waves. At the pre-war baseline (T1; August 2023), the prevalence rate was 16.1%. As it can be seen on Fig. 2, following the October 7 attack, a sharp elevation was observed at T2 with prevalence nearly doubling to 29.8%. This peak was followed by a gradual and steady attenuation: 26.6% at T3, 23.0% at T4, 20.7% at T5, and reaching 19.7% at the final assessment (T6). To statistically evaluate this pattern, a Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) with a logit link function and binomial distribution was employed. The model confirmed a robust curvilinear (quadratic) trajectory for PTSD over time. A significant positive linear time effect was identified (B = 0.642, SE = 0.118, Wald χ2(1) = 29.38, p < 0.001), reflecting the rapid escalation in PTSD prevalence immediately following the traumatic exposure. The GEE model further examined the impact of demographic and exposure-related factors as main effects across the entire study period. At it can be seen on Table 2, the analysis identified several robust predictors of probable PTSD risk. Direct exposure to the October 7 attack was the most substantial risk factor (p < 0.001), with participants directly exposed to the attack demonstrating 3.30 times higher odds of probable PTSD compared to those who were not (OR = 3.30, 95% CI [1.63, 6.67]). Significant ethnic disparities were observed throughout the study period (p < 0.001), as Arab participants demonstrated 4.03 times higher odds for probable PTSD compared to Jewish participants (OR = 4.03, 95% CI [2.85, 5.71]). Serving in the military reserves during the war was also identified as a major risk factor (p < 0.001); participants called to active duty had 2.57 times higher odds of PTSD compared to those who did not serve in the reserves (OR = 2.57, 95% CI [1.68, 3.92]). Additionally, experiencing loss of a relative significantly increased the risk (p = 0.013), with such individuals showing 1.60 times higher odds of probable PTSD (OR = 1.60, 95% CI [1.10, 2.32]). A small but significant protective effect for age was identified (B = −0.012, p = 0.032), suggesting that for every year of increased age, the risk of PTSD decreased slightly (OR = 0.988, 95% CI [0.97, 0.99]). Probable depression trajectories and predictors

Longitudinal analysis revealed that the prevalence of probable depression underwent substantial fluctuations following the October 7 attack. As it can be seen on Fig. 2, from an initial baseline of 30.5% at T1, prevalence rose to a peak of 43.1% at T2, subsequently declining to 32.6% at T3, 28.3% at T4, 23.7% at T5, and 23.3% at T6. Statistical modeling via Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) identified a significant quadratic (curvilinear) time effect (B = −0.041, SE = 0.015, Wald χ2(1) = 7.89, p = 0.005), validating the non-linear attenuation of depressive symptoms over the 27-month study period. Although the linear time component was not statistically significant independently (p = 0.212), the significant quadratic term confirms that the acute surge in depression was followed by a measurable decrease.

At it can be seen on Table 2, multivariable modeling identified several demographic and exposure characteristics as significant determinants of probable depression risk throughout the conflict. Notably, biological sex was a strong predictor (p < 0.001), with women exhibiting 2.20 times higher odds of probable depression compared to men (OR = 2.20, 95% CI [1.66, 2.92]). Ethnic disparities were also significant (p < 0.001), with Arab participants demonstrating 2.37 times higher odds of depression than Jewish participants (OR = 2.37, 95% CI [1.65, 3.40]). Direct exposure to the initial attack remained a critical risk factor (p = 0.003), as exposed individuals demonstrated 2.63 times higher odds of depression relative to those without direct exposure (OR = 2.63, 95% CI [1.39, 4.98]). Active military reserve service during the war was also associated with increased risk (p < 0.001), as individuals called to reserve duty demonstrated 2.31 times higher odds of depression (OR = 2.31, 95% CI [1.47, 3.65]). Age demonstrated a small but significant protective effect (OR = 0.989, 95% CI [0.98, 0.99], p = 0.036).

Probable anxiety trajectories and predictors

As it can be seen on Fig. 2, longitudinal monitoring of probable anxiety indicated a substantial increase in prevalence following the October 7 attack, rising from a pre-war baseline of 22.1% at T1 to a peak of 40.1% at T2. This acute elevation was followed by a non-linear decline, with prevalence rates reaching 26.3% at T3, 23.3% at T4, 21.9% at T5, and 21.0% at the final T6 assessment. Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) analysis confirmed a robust curvilinear trajectory, evidenced by both a significant positive linear time effect (B = 0.229, SE = 0.10, Wald χ2(1) = 5.06, p = 0.024) and a significant negative quadratic time effect (B = −0.049, SE = 0.01, Wald χ2(1) = 11.28, p < 0.001). These findings further support the observation that the initial surge in anxiety symptoms significantly attenuated over the study period, following an inverted U-shaped pattern.

The multivariable GEE model identified several key demographic and service-related predictors that consistently influenced the risk of probable anxiety throughout the war period. At it can be seen on Table 2, biological sex emerged as a primary predictor (p < 0.001), with women exhibiting 2.44 times higher odds of meeting criteria for probable anxiety compared to men (OR = 2.44, 95% CI [1.81, 3.32]). Ethnic factor also played a significant role (p < 0.001), as Arab participants demonstrated 2.04 times higher odds of probable anxiety than Jewish participants (OR = 2.04, 95% CI [1.43, 2.91]). Active military reserve service was identified as a significant risk factor (p < 0.001), with those called to active duty showing 2.35 times higher odds of anxiety relative to those not serving in the reserves (OR = 2.35, 95% CI [1.52, 3.65]). Age provided a small but statistically significant protective effect (OR = 0.984, 95% CI [0.97, 0.99], p = 0.002), indicating that older age was associated with slightly lower risk. In this longitudinal model, direct exposure to the attack and experience of losing a loved one showed non-significant trends toward increased risk.

Prediction of mental health outcomes at 27 Months (T6)

Hierarchical regression analyses were conducted to identify key predictors for psychological outcomes at the final follow-up (T6). Detailed statistical models, including all hierarchical steps and coefficients, are provided in Supplement T2.

Probable PTSD at T6 (27 months after the attack) was significantly and independently predicted by Arab ethnicity (OR = 4.07, p < 0.001), higher baseline symptom severity at T1, and direct exposure to the October 7 attack, which increased the odds of a probable diagnosis more than sixfold (OR = 6.31, p = 0.001). For probable depression at T6, female sex (OR = 1.82, p = 0.022), Arab ethnicity (OR = 3.62, p < 0.001), and baseline depressive symptoms emerged as significant risk factors, while reserve military service was identified as a robust exposure-related predictor (OR = 4.38, p = 0.002). Similarly, probable anxiety at 27 months (T6) was predicted by younger age (p = 0.001), Arab ethnicity (OR = 2.20, p = 0.013), and higher baseline anxiety levels, with reserve military service again emerging as a significant predictor of long-term distress (OR = 2.93, p = 0.014).

The level of PTG at T6 was uniquely shaped by both demographic and experiential factors. Female sex and higher baseline PTG levels significantly predicted greater growth at the final assessment. Furthermore, the loss of a loved one served as a primary catalyst for growth, significantly predicting higher PTG scores at 27 months (p = 0.007). While these regression models accounted for 21%–35% of the variance in distress symptoms, the PTG model explained a smaller portion of the variance (R2 = 0.05).

Discussion

The present study traced long-term psychological responses to the October 7, 2023, attack and the prolonged war that followed in a prospective six-wave national cohort, including pre-attack baseline data. At the population level, the findings point to a sharp increase in distress one month after the attack, followed by differential trajectories of recovery: a rapid return to pre-war baseline levels for anxiety, a significant decline below baseline for depression, and a more gradual and protracted decline in PTSD symptoms toward the already elevated pre-war baseline. PTG increased progressively over time before reaching a point of stabilization in the later waves, indicating a distinct and slower process of positive psychological change. At the same time, several high-risk groups continued to show elevated levels of distress over the long term. Crucially, it is important to take into account that the T1 baseline does not represent a ‘neutral’ state, as it was captured during intense internal socio-political unrest. Consequently, observed trajectories reflect shifts superimposed upon an existing landscape of national volatility rather than a return to absolute psychological tranquility. Together, these findings highlight the need to understand October 7 and the ensuing war through multiple parallel processes, including acute psychiatric burden, gradual recovery, growth-related change, and persistent vulnerability.

The overall pattern of sharp increase followed by a gradual decline in mental pain is broadly consistent with previous studies, showing that, even after severe collective trauma, many individuals in the general population may show gradual stabilization through repeated mobilization of coping resources, restoration of routine, and the buffering effects of social connectedness and shared meaning.7,8,16 In the Israeli context, it can be plausible to suggest that this process may have been further shaped by strong forms of belongingness and collective solidarity, which may have buffered distress following October 7 and facilitated hope, support-seeking, and psychological containment under threat.32 Importantly, while the current study did not empirically measure perceived solidarity, it remains a plausible contextual factor that may have buffered the long-term psychological impact of the war. Future research should formally assess whether such collective prosocial behaviors serve as mediators of national resilience.

While not directly assessed as mediators in the current model, the observed attenuation in symptoms coincides with specific developments during the war—such as the release of some hostages, shifts in military dynamics, and periods in which the crisis became somewhat more interpretable or containable— which may have contributed to a gradual reduction in acute distress. Still, this decline should be interpreted as a contextual hypothesis rather than a direct causal inference: because the pre-attack baseline was itself collected during a period of substantial national strain surrounding the judicial reform crisis,33,34 the later return to baseline may reflects relative stabilization toward a pre-existing state of tension rather than a return to ordinary psychological conditions.

A distinct pattern emerged for posttraumatic growth. Unlike the parabolic trajectories of probable PTSD, depression, and anxiety, PTG followed a steady upward progression that reached stabilization in later waves. This pattern reflects a cumulative process of cognitive integration and saturation that unfolds as the conflict persists. The findings align with the conceptualization of growth as a slow-unfolding process of rebuilding psychological assumptions following profound disruption. This trajectory is consistent with the view that posttraumatic growth is not an immediate response to trauma, but a slower process that develops through sustained struggle with highly challenging circumstances and may coexist with ongoing distress.20 In war settings, such growth has been observed even while threat remains ongoing: among Ukrainian adults assessed during the first months of the war, appreciation of life and spiritual–existential change were especially prominent,21 and related work suggests that positive psychological change can unfold under ongoing conflict and displacement rather than only after danger has passed.22,23 In the present context, the gradual increase in posttraumatic growth may reflect cumulative processes of meaning-making35 and renewed hope over time.36

More specifically, the substantial increase in PTG at T4 aligns with the meaning-making model and the theory of shattered assumptions. These frameworks posit that growth is a ‘slow-unfolding’ process requiring the cognitive integration of trauma into a revised global meaning system. According to Janoff-Bulman,37 the initial trauma shatters fundamental assumptions about the world’s benevolence and predictability. The one-year anniversary (T4) likely provided the necessary temporal window for ‘deliberate rumination’ and the progressive rebuilding of these psychological assumptions, allowing growth-related insights to consolidate into measurable change.38 Although the direct impact of memorialization was not evaluated, the temporal alignment between these national rites and the observed increase in PTG offers a compelling hypothesis regarding the role of collective ritual in facilitating posttraumatic growth.

The regression analyses further refined this picture by showing that long-term outcomes were shaped not only by wartime exposure, but also by demographic position and pre-war vulnerability.

In the present study, demographic factors were informative: female sex was a significant predictor, associated with a twofold increased risk for probable depression and a nearly two-and-a-half-fold increased risk for probable anxiety compared to men. Arab citizens exhibited a markedly higher risk for all distress outcomes, including a fourfold increased risk for probable PTSD, and older age predicting lower odds across all outcomes - findings that align with earlier post–October 7 evidence pointing to greater vulnerability among women and Arab citizens in the acute aftermath of the attack.11,12 Baseline symptom severity then made a substantial additional contribution across outcomes, underscoring that pre-war distress meaningfully shaped later adjustment.

Exposure-related variables continued to predict long-term distress even after adjustment for demographic factors and pre-war symptom levels. Direct exposure to the October 7 attack remained a consistent predictor of chronic distress, associated with a threefold increase in the odds of probable PTSD and a more than twofold increase in the odds of depression. Direct exposure was the strongest exposure-related predictor of probable PTSD at T6, increasing the odds more than sixfold. Reserve military service emerged as a critical risk factor, consistently predicting a more than twofold risk across all distress domains, indicating that the burden of prolonged service extended well into the later stages of the war. These findings suggest that the psychological toll of the conflict was driven by both the acute intensity of the initial traumatic encounter and the sustained operational demands and repeated exposures of a prolonged, multi-front war.5,18,19,39,40 Taken together, these results suggest that while the general population exhibits signs of stabilization toward baseline levels, the psychiatric burden remains concentrated within these high-risk demographic and exposure clusters, underscoring the need for targeted, long-term public health interventions.

The analyses also identified female sex, Arab ethnicity, and the loss of a loved one as the primary catalysts for PTG throughout the 27-month follow-up. While these factors, along with baseline PTG levels, significantly predicted long-term growth at T6, the final model explained substantially less variance (5.3%) compared to the distress models. This discrepancy suggests that while psychiatric symptoms are closely anchored to objective exposure and demographic risk, growth may be more heavily influenced by internal, intra-psychic processes—such as cognitive processing and meaning-making—that operate independently of the direct impact of trauma. These findings align with the theoretical conceptualization of growth as a distinct and idiosyncratic psychological process that operates independently of the direct impact of trauma.20,38

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, all outcomes were assessed with self-report measures, and the PTSD, depression, and anxiety indicators represent probable diagnoses rather than clinical interviews or full diagnostic evaluations, which may have introduced reporting and recall biases and may either overestimate or underestimate true prevalence. Second, although the present design benefited from a rare pre-attack baseline, it did not include explicit measures of prior trauma history, limiting the ability to distinguish the effects of October 7 and the war from the contribution of earlier traumatic exposures. Additionally, it should be noted that the traumatic index event for the ITQ and PTGI-SF shifted from socio-political unrest at the T1 baseline to the October 7 attack and the subsequent war from T2 onward. While this approach provided a vital psychological baseline of the population’s capacity for distress and growth during a national crisis, the direct comparison of these event-specific measures across the 27-month period should be interpreted with caution, as the underlying nature of the stressors evolved.

Third, the exposure indicators used here—direct exposure, loss of a loved one, and reserve military service—necessarily simplify what were likely complex, cumulative, and overlapping wartime experiences; as prior work on this cohort noted, such classifications may obscure multidimensional or additive exposure effects. Fourth, while quota sampling was employed to match national demographic distributions, the use of a non-probability professional survey panel may limit generalizability. To mitigate potential selection bias, we adjusted for key demographic covariates in all statistical models. Moreover, modest fixed compensation was non-contingent on symptom severity, minimizing the risk of response bias.

Moreover, some potentially high-risk populations may have been underrepresented, and the findings remain embedded in a highly specific Israeli sociopolitical context of prolonged war, which may also limit broader generalizability. While Bonferroni corrections were implemented for multiple longitudinal comparisons, the results should be viewed as descriptive of the population’s course over 27 months rather than definitive individual-level clinical transitions.

It is also critical to note that individual trajectories of trauma and growth are significantly shaped by genetic architecture, which contributes to both PTSD risk and resilience.41,42 While biological and genetic markers were not measured in the current study, the interplay between chronic, high-intensity war exposure and inherited predisposition likely accounts for a portion of the observed heterogeneity in mental health outcomes. Future research integrating such biopsychosocial markers is essential to further elucidate the complex mechanisms driving these longitudinal patterns.

Taken together, the present findings suggest that the long-term psychological course of October 7 and the prolonged war was characterized by a dual process: sharp initial rise in probable PTSD, depression, and anxiety followed by differential patterns of attenuation—characterized by a rapid return to baseline for depression and anxiety and a more protracted decline in PTSD symptoms, alongside a progressive increase in posttraumatic growth over time. This pattern indicates that the psychological aftermath of an unfolding national trauma cannot be understood solely in terms of persistent psychopathology or recovery, but rather as a dynamic process in which distress, stabilization, and growth may coexist over extended periods.7,20 At the same time, women, Arab citizens, directly exposed individuals, bereaved participants, and reserve-duty service members remained at elevated risk, two years following October 7, 2023. In this sense, the study results highlight not only the broader population trajectory following October 7, but also the uneven distribution of long-term burden within it.

These results suggest several practical implications. The first concerns the need for long-term and stratified mental health monitoring, rather than reliance on short-term post-disaster screening alone.2 Because several late outcomes remained associated with demographic and exposure-related risk even after controlling for baseline pre-war symptoms, the findings support sustained surveillance of groups with heightened vulnerability—notably women, Arab citizens, directly exposed individuals, bereaved persons, and reserve-duty service members.18,19

A second implication is that intervention efforts should not focus solely on symptom reduction, but also on strengthening interpersonal and community-level protective processes, particularly among groups that may remain psychologically burdened and socially isolated over time. Prior findings from this cohort suggest that belongingness can buffer the contribution of depression to suicide ideation following October 7, underscoring the clinical and public-health value of interventions that reinforce connection, social support, community participation, and a sense of shared meaning.23 At the same time, the gradual increase in posttraumatic growth observed in the present study suggests that long-term care should also support meaning-making, hope, and opportunities for constructive psychological reorganization under prolonged threat. Accordingly, the practical challenge is not only to identify distress early, but to reduce the loneliness that may intensify risk during prolonged war43 and to build the personal and relational resources that allow vulnerable individuals and communities not only to endure, but to recover more safely and adapt more constructively over time.44
