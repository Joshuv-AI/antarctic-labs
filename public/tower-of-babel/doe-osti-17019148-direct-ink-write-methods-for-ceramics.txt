Abstract
Direct ink write (DIW) printing was optimized for ceramic multi-material and embedded
printing for producing compressible dies and printing complex parts respectively.
Traditional ceramics production utilizes subtractive manufacturing to produce intricate
yet dense parts, requiring significant amounts of time and energy while generating
substantial material waste. Printing parts with DIW reduces the machining process and
allows for the production of compressible dies: a complex ceramic part encased in a
ceramic scaffold to ensure homogenous densification when sintering. Multi-material
DIW printing successfully produced the compressible die configuration, while the
feasibility of producing free-standing complex ceramic parts was demonstrated through
embedded printing.
Introduction
 Ceramics, known for their excellent insulating properties, heat resistance, and
superior hardness, are often used in aerospace and energy applications. Ceramics
production has two main steps: the compaction of the ceramic particles to form a green
body and sintering for densification. In traditional manufacturing, the ceramic billet is
densified to fit a cylindrical die, then post-machined as a hard and dense material; this
process is costly and wasteful of material. Consequently, sintering complex shapes
faces the travel distance problem, in which a rigid punch cannot deform to uniformly
press parts with varying thicknesses. The part then exhibits densification gradients,
leading to poor mechanical properties or even failure.
 To address both processing limitations, compressible dies that conform to the
geometry of the part and the cylindrical nature of traditional hot-pressing dies can be
used. A method to produce these dies is through direct ink write (DIW), an additive

1

manufacturing technique that involves the extrusion of a ceramic ink layer-by-layer to
print complex parts1.
 Multi-material DIW can be utilized to print the ceramic part and an outer
compressible die that supports the part during sintering. The outer die will compress
along with the part during the hot-pressing process, transferring the force more
uniformly to the complex shape within. Embedded DIW printing of ceramics was also
tested for the printing of complex geometry without the need for custom substrates or
additional supports while broadening the viscosity ranges of inks which can be used.
Both methods have the capability to streamline the advanced ceramics manufacturing
process, allowing for greater throughput of high-fidelity parts.

 Fig 1: Schematic of process flow from DIW to pressing for compressible die

 With this project, the feasibility of printing the compressible dies was explored.
With the success in multi-material and embedded printing, there is potential to scale the
technique and apply it to printing dies used in commercial manufacturing.
Materials and Methods
Formulations and Printing Configuration
 Boron nitride (BN) and graphite inks were used in this project, with a solids
loading of 38 vol% and 45 vol% respectively. The ceramic powder (Sigma Aldrich) was
incorporated into a polyethyleneimine (PEI) (Sigma Aldrich) and water mixture and spin-
mixed until homogenous. A chemical crosslinker was added to the ceramic ink for
embedded printing.
 All printing was on the Aerotech A3200 mechanical gantry system and a
pneumatic pressure extrusion system. The ceramic inks were loaded into 10 mL
syringes and printed with 400 um and 600 um diameter syringes (Nordson). A gypsum
substrate and dual-nozzle configuration (Fig 2) was used for the multi-material DIW.

2

 Fig 2: Printing with dual nozzle setup, with a close up of the multi-material print

 A hydrogel suspension medium consisting of carbomer, water, and a chemical
crosslinker was used for embedded printing. All parts were air-dried for 48 hours, with
embedded prints setting in the gel for 24 hours prior to the drying process to ensure the
ceramic parts were fully crosslinked.
Rheology
 Rheology was performed on the TA Instruments Discovery Hybrid Rheometer 1
with a 20mm Peltier plate geometry and an 800 um gap. A boron nitride (BN), graphite,
and hydrogel were tested.
Results and Discussion
Multi-material Printing
 The established workflow used software to generate the G-Code and was
constrained to 2D traces and basic shapes. To allow for greater flexibility and faster
iteration, a Python script was adapted for printing more complex geometry such as
cones. This reduced the G-Code generation time by half.
 The low viscosity inks required significantly less pressure (~18 psi) for extrusion.
When the inks had over 50 vol% of water, it would “melt” on the gypsum substrate,
leading to structural collapse. When the inks were high viscosity and paste-like, more
pressure (~32 psi) was required for even extrusion and produced more rigid and self-
supporting parts.
 Nozzles of varying diameters were tested. A smaller 400 µm nozzle extruded a
finer filament, which translated into a flatter cone. The 60 µm nozzle extruded thicker
lines, leading to a cone about twice as tall (Fig 3).

3

 Fig 3: Left to right, cone printed with 600 µm and 400 µm diameter nozzles

 This demonstrates the trade-offs between print speed and resolution in DIW: A
smaller nozzle lead to more seamless layers, but the thinner layers require double the
time to print the same geometry. The larger nozzles inherently print larger parts and
decrease print time, but have visible layer lines.
 The final print was of an inner BN cone and the graphite compressible die
encapsulating it; Fig 4 captures the printing process.

 Fig 4: DIW printing of multi-material compressible die at the (a) base (b) mid-section (c) top (d)
side view of the printed die

 Improvements include scaling the compressible die beyond the current ~14 cm
diameter to demonstrate compatibility with industry-standard dies. To accommodate for
the compression the samples are under during the sintering step, a gap between the
inner part and outer die could also be added.
 BN and graphite were selected for their performance as ceramic inks. Moving
forward, printing could be tested with silicon carbide and boron carbide to evaluate
printing with advanced ceramics and the impact of powder morphology on print quality.
 Pressure-based printing is largely dependent on ink viscosity, which varies
between ink batches and can be an inconsistent metric. Thus, printing based on flow
rate or extrusion volume could be explored to establish more consistent printing metrics.
Embedded Printing

4

 The use of embedded printing was demonstrated by printing a cone and a series
of rings printed in suspension above each other (Fig 5). After 48 hours, the prints did not
sink or warp in the hydrogel.

Fig 5: Embedded ceramic prints (a) Graphite rings printed in suspension above each other (b) BN cone
printed upside down

 While inks both high and low in viscosity extruded and set successfully in the gel,
the parts with less warping and compaction were the parts printed from the high
viscosity inks. Although largely composed of water, the gel still has enough mass to
condense and warp the parts if the ink is low viscosity and not capable of immediately
setting.
Rheological Testing
 Previous literature for embedded printing cited an order of magnitude greater
than the hydrogel matrix itself for smoother extrusions2. This was demonstrated in this
embedded printing system (Fig 6); the gel exhibited a linear trend and had viscosities
around a magnitude lower or equivalent to the inks used. The BN ink was seen to
expand during printing, which could be attributed to the rapid drop in viscosity seen
beyond 10 1/s, leading to overextrusion3.

5

 Fig 6: Rheology for inks and hydrogel medium

 Another phenomenon seen in embedded printing is the lower pressure
requirements. When extruding at 32 psi as used for traditional DIW, the extruded
filament would over extrude and squiggle, whereas lowering it to 22 psi produced
smooth lines. A potential cause could be water from the hydrogel moving into the gel
itself, forming a low-viscosity region.
Hydrogel Formulation
 The hydrogel was formulated to possess the following properties: optically clear,
shear-thinning, self-recovering, and viscous enough to support ceramic parts.
 The addition of water transformed the hydrogel from a stiff, cloudy gel that
retained bubbles into an optically clear medium fit for printing. But, excessive
concentrations of water would lead to “blooming”, where excess water was being pulled
into the inks and causing it to dissipate from the extrusion path.
 Shear-thinning behavior allowed the hydrogel to flow under pressure, ensuring
even extrusion of the ceramic filament. Rheological testing of the hydrogel medium
identified a crossover point from predominantly solid, storage modulus (G’) behavior to
liquid-like loss modulus (G”) behavior at around a 6000% oscillation strain (Fig 7). As
the ratio of G” to G’ behavior, a tan delta value beyond 1 could suggest that the
hydrogel network transformed into a liquid beyond 10,000% oscillation strain.

6

 Fig 7: Moduli and tan dela for hydrogel medium

 The formulation was also developed to be self-recovering and would infill after
being passed through with the nozzle. This ensured that parts would be level and could
be printed directly in suspension of each other, increasing the surface area for printing
and maximizing efficiency.
 The shear-rate for the hydrogel and ink system could be quantified to determine
the ideal ink viscosities for printing and potentially explain the lower pressure
requirements for embedded printing. More complex structures, such as lattices and
multi-material parts, could also be printed to explore the capabilities of embedded
ceramic printing.
Conclusion
Ceramics printing was realized through two avenues of DIW printing: traditional and
embedded printing. The compressible die configuration was successfully printed
through multi-material printing. Preliminary testing and printing of free-standing
structures suggested that complex ceramic parts could be produced using embedded
printing. With applications ranging from turbines in spacecraft and semiconductor
packaging, customizable, high-throughput and high-resolution production pathways for
ceramics is crucial to innovations in a variety of technological sectors. Future work could
include printing a wider variety of parts and sintering them to identify the next steps to
translate ceramics DIW into industrial ceramics manufacturing.
