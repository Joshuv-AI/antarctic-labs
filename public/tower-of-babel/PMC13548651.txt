Outer nuclear layer thinning as an in vivo biomarker for discriminating probable FTLD-tau from probable FTLD-TDP with PET-supported subtyping
Authors: Zhengxi Zhang, Qinqin Zhang, Yang Chen, Ruijie Zeng, Meizhen Min, Haolun Sun, Yujie Lai, Yingying Shen, Yuting Yang, Ming Feng, Zi Jin, Ying Zhang, Hebei Gao, Fan Lu, Yan-Jiang Wang, Meixiao Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesOuter nuclear layer (ONL) thinning has been identified in frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD); however, its utility for distinguishing the subtypes of FTLD-tauopathy (FTLD-tau) and TDP-43 proteinopathy (FTLD-TDP) remains unknown. We investigated whether ONL thickness provides a subtype-informative retinal signal for differentiating PET-supported probable FTLD-tau (pFTLD-tau) from probable FTLD-TDP (pFTLD-TDP) in vivo.MethodsPatients clinically diagnosed with FTLD were subtyped into pFTLD-tau and pFTLD-TDP groups based on multimodal PET and clinical criteria. Normal controls (NCs) were cognitively unimpaired on standardized testing and clinical evaluation. Macular images were acquired using swept-source OCT. A custom deep learning algorithm segmented the retina into eight sublayers. The thickness of each retinal sublayer was assessed across the eight sectors of the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) grid. Retinal thickness differences were analyzed using generalized estimating equations, and exploratory discrimination models were evaluated using age- and sex-adjusted stepwise logistic regression with apparent and bootstrap optimism-corrected AUCs reported. Exploratory partial correlation analysis was conducted to examine the associations between ONL thickness and cognitive scores.ResultsA total of 86 participants were included (21 pFTLD-tau, 27 pFTLD-TDP and 38 NCs). Widespread ONL thinning was observed in pFTLD-tau (Cohen’s d= -0.753 to -1.268 vs. controls; -0.666 to -1.069 vs. pFTLD-TDP; all FDR-adjusted P < 0.05), while ONL in pFTLD-TDP remained preserved. A model combining retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), ONL, and myoid–ellipsoid zone (MEZ) thickness showed exploratory discrimination for differentiating pFTLD-tau from pFTLD-TDP (apparent AUC, 0.922; optimism-corrected AUC, 0.866). The outer thickness model yielded higher AUC estimates than the inner thickness model (0.884/0.835 vs. 0.713/0.630), and the individual ONL model showed moderate exploratory discrimination (0.808/0.765). ONL thickness was correlated with cognitive scores in pFTLD-tau (partial r = 0.433–0.483; all P < 0.05), whereas corresponding associations in pFTLD-TDP did not reach statistical significance.DiscussionONL thinning was preferentially observed in pFTLD-tau and contributed to exploratory discrimination between PET-supported probable FTLD subtypes. These findings suggest that ONL thickness may provide complementary, noninvasive information for probable FTLD subtype stratification, with potential to facilitate therapeutic trial enrollment and personalized management. Future studies incorporating neuropathological confirmation and fluid biomarkers are warranted to validate these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13195-026-02111-7.

Introduction

Frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD) is a leading cause of early-onset dementia, pathologically characterized by two major subtypes: FTLD-tau, marked by tau aggregation, and FTLD-TDP, defined by TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43) inclusions. [1] Although certain clinical phenotypes or genetic backgrounds may suggest a specific pathology, the two subtypes often exhibit substantial clinical and anatomical overlap, making differential diagnosis challenging. [2] With the advent of tau-targeting monoclonal antibodies, [3] gene-silencing therapies, [4] and candidate drugs targeting TDP-43 neurotoxicity, [5] accurate in vivo subtyping of FTLD has become critical for precision medicine and clinical trial enrollment. [6] While PET imaging can improve diagnostic accuracy and help rule out co-pathology, its cost and radiation exposure limit widespread use. [7–9] Therefore, identifying rapid, noninvasive, and cost-effective surrogate biomarkers remains an urgent priority.

The retina, as a pathological extension of the central nervous system, offers a unique window into neurodegenerative processes within the brain. [10] Optical coherence tomography (OCT) provides high-resolution, noninvasive, and reproducible visualization of retinal structure, serving as a potential biomarker for neurodegeneration. [10–13].

A growing body of evidence specifically implicates outer nuclear layer (ONL) thinning, which contains photoreceptor nuclei, as a characteristic feature of FTLD-tau. One cross-sectional OCT study reported a significant reduction in ONL thickness in FTLD-tau compared with both controls [14] and patients with Alzheimer’s disease (AD). [15] Longitudinal data further confirmed progressive outer retinal atrophy, predominantly within the ONL in FTLD-tau, [16] and postmortem analyses provided histopathologic validation of ONL thinning observed on OCT. [17] In contrast, retinal alterations in FTLD-TDP remain poorly characterized. While some studies have described possible thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), [10, 18, 19] emerging evidence also suggests possible involvement of the outer retina. [10, 20–22] However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has directly compared retinal layer thicknesses between FTLD-tau and FTLD-TDP within the same cohort using PET-supported subtyping. Consequently, it remains unknown whether ONL thinning is a specific marker for FTLD-tau pathology or a general feature of FTLD, thereby limiting its diagnostic utility.

The primary objectives of this study were to systematically investigate retinal features in a clinically diagnosed FTLD cohort with PET-supported subtyping into probable FTLD-tau (pFTLD-tau) and probable FTLD-TDP (pFTLD-TDP). Specifically, we aimed to determine whether ONL thinning constitutes a subtype-informative structural biomarker that distinguishes pFTLD-tau from pFTLD-TDP using OCT with an automated segmentation algorithm.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited from the Chongqing Ageing & Dementia Study (CADS), registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR1900027622; ChiCTR2000040786), encompassing enrollments from January 2021 to July 2025. All participants underwent standardized cognitive assessment and ocular OCT, with slit-lamp examination and color fundus photography to exclude anterior or posterior segment disease. Demographic data (age, sex, education, hypertension, and diabetes), ocular parameters (best-corrected visual acuity [BCVA], intraocular pressure [IOP], spherical equivalent [SE]), and cognitive scores were collected. Cognition was evaluated with the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), both adjusted for years of education. [23] These instruments were collected a priori as harmonized global screening measures of overall cognitive severity for cross-syndrome comparison in this PET-supported probable FTLD cohort, [24–26] although they may be less sensitive to syndrome-specific, domain-level deficits. All examinations were completed within one month of each other, and family history was obtained from all participants. PET-supported subtype assignment followed a syndrome-led, differential diagnosis-guided, pathway-specific PET/MRI workflow with final multidisciplinary consensus. Clinical syndromic diagnoses were assigned according to prespecified consensus criteria, including the revised 2011 Rascovsky criteria for behavioural variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD), the 2011 international consensus criteria for primary progressive aphasia (PPA) variants, and the 2017 MDS-PSP criteria for progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP). [27–29] Multimodal PET/MRI was used as supportive evidence within this workflow. [8, 30–33] Detailed operational criteria for each included clinical syndrome are provided in the Supplementary Methods. Fluid biomarkers (e.g., NfL) were not systematically collected, and neuropathological confirmation (the diagnostic gold standard for FTLD [34]) was unavailable; therefore, in vivo molecular subtype assignments were classified as PET-supported probable rather than definitive. To support PET-informed subtype assignment, FTLD participants underwent one of two PET/MRI protocols selected according to the primary pathological differential diagnosis after clinical assessment and review of available structural and metabolic imaging. Participants were routed to the 4R-tau-supportive PET/MRI protocol if they showed clinical or available MRI/FDG-PET features suggestive of 4R tauopathy, including PSP- or corticobasal syndrome (CBS)- spectrum signs, nonfluent/agrammatic variant of PPA (nfvPPA). [29, 35, 36] Participants lacking these features suggestive of 4R tauopathy underwent the AD-exclusion PET/MRI protocol when AD remained a relevant alternative diagnosis.

In the AD exclusion protocol, Participants underwent Aβ-PET ([¹¹C]PiB or [¹⁸F]florbetapir, AV45), [¹⁸F]MK-6240 tau-PET, FDG-PET and co-acquired structural MRI. Aβ-PET negativity, absence of AD-typical cortical [¹⁸F]MK-6240 tau deposition, FTLD-compatible rather than AD-compatible FDG-PET hypometabolism, and absence of alternative structural lesions on MRI were used to reduce the likelihood of AD pathology. [37].

In the 4R tau support protocol, Participants underwent [¹⁸F]PI-2620 tau-PET, FDG-PET, structural MRI and DaTSCAN. [¹⁸F]PI-2620 tau-PET was used as the primary tau-supportive imaging component to assess regional uptake patterns compatible with 4R tauopathy. [38] FDG-PET and structural MRI were used to evaluate syndrome-concordant neurodegenerative patterns and to assess alternative etiologies. [29, 30, 39] An [¹⁸F]PI-2620 uptake pattern compatible with 4R tauopathy, rather than an AD-typical tau distribution, together with concordant FDG-PET/MRI findings was interpreted as supportive evidence for PET-supported probable FTLD-tau and as reducing the likelihood of clinically relevant AD co-pathology. [40, 41] DaTSCAN was reviewed as ancillary evidence of nigrostriatal dopaminergic involvement. [42].

PET images were interpreted using standardized, tracer-specific visual criteria by two trained readers blinded to retinal OCT measurements; discrepant interpretations were adjudicated by a third reader. Final PET-supported subtype assignment was determined by multidisciplinary consensus involving neurology and nuclear medicine/neuroimaging specialists, integrating the clinical syndrome, PET/MRI findings, family history, and available genetic information. pFTLD-tau required concordant clinical and multimodal imaging support for tauopathy. [28, 29, 38] pFTLD-TDP required a compatible bvFTD or svPPA phenotype, no PET/MRI evidence supporting AD pathology or 4R tauopathy according to the applied imaging pathway, FTLD-compatible FDG-PET patterns, available genetic information not favoring MAPT-related FTLD-tau, and no available evidence favoring an alternative diagnosis. [2, 28, 34, 37, 43] The pFTLD-TDP designation was interpreted as an indirect, PET-supported probable classification rather than biomarker-confirmed TDP-43 pathology. [44].

Normal controls (NCs) were cognitively unimpaired on standardized testing and clinical evaluation, with no history of neurologic disease. When available, prior clinical imaging and/or PET showed no major structural abnormalities or evidence of neurodegeneration.

Exclusion criteria: Ocular conditions affecting the optic nerve or retina (e.g., glaucoma, macular degeneration, vitreoretinal disease, active intraocular inflammation, clinically significant prior intraocular inflammatory disease) or intraocular surgery within 6 months; neuroimaging-confirmed AD, Parkinson’s disease, or other neurodegenerative disorders; severe neurologic or systemic comorbidities, including stroke, active systemic inflammatory or autoimmune disorders, and vascular/metabolic diseases with potential ocular-cerebral complications, such as uncontrolled hypertension or diabetes; OCT signal strength < 6/10; Cancer, alcoholism, or substance abuse. Pre-specified exclusion reasons are shown in Fig. 1.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committees of Wenzhou Medical University and the Army Medical Center of PLA (Approval Nos. 2020-012-K-10 and 2020-14) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

OCT acquisition

Macular structural imaging was performed on a VG200 swept-source OCT system (Intalight, Henan, China) with a 1050 nm central wavelength and 200 kHz scan rate. Eye-tracking minimized motion artifacts. Eighteen radial B-scans through the fovea were acquired. Image quality was independently assessed; scans with signal strength < 6 or motion artifacts were excluded. Both eyes were analyzed when available; otherwise, the eye meeting image-quality criteria was included. Retinal parameters were measured using a macula-centered Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) grid (Fig. 2A).

B-scans were processed using a fully automated deep-learning algorithm [45] to segment eight retinal sublayers (Fig. 2B): RNFL, ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer (GCIPL), inner nuclear layer (INL), outer plexiform layer (OPL), ONL, myoid–ellipsoid zone (MEZ), outer segment of photoreceptor (OS), and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE).

Statistical analysis

Group differences were tested with generalized estimating equations (GEE), adjusting for age, sex, years of education, BCVA, and inter-eye correlation. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d. Multiple comparisons were controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, and FDR-adjusted P values are reported where applicable. All P values are two-sided, with a significance threshold of 0.05. Categorical variables were compared with chi-square tests. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test; normally distributed data were analyzed with ANOVA, and non-normal data with the Kruskal–Wallis test. Exploratory discrimination analyses were performed to distinguish each pFTLD subtype from NCs and to differentiate the two pFTLD subtypes from each other. Predefined model blocks included inner thicknesses (RNFL, GCIPL, INL), outer thicknesses (OPL, ONL, MEZ, OS, RPE), combined thicknesses, individual ONL thicknesses model, and cognitive scores (MMSE, MoCA). Variables showing significant group differences in the corresponding comparison were entered as candidate predictors in stepwise logistic regression. Age and sex were forced into all primary models a priori; years of education was additionally forced into cognition-score models. BCVA-adjusted sensitivity analyses were also performed. Predictors retained by Wald-test-based stepwise selection, together with the forced covariates, constituted the final selected discrimination models. Apparent AUCs were calculated from ROC curves in the original dataset, and optimism-corrected AUCs were estimated using 1,000 stratified participant-level bootstrap resamples [46]. For logistic regression and partial correlation analyses, participants with bilateral data were represented by the mean of both eyes. Partial correlation analyses between ONL thickness and cognition were performed controlling for age, sex, education, and BCVA, with results reported as partial r. To evaluate potential confounding by visual acuity, we examined partial correlations between BCVA and MMSE/MoCA within each FTLD subtype, in the combined pFTLD cohort, and in the entire analytic cohort including NCs, adjusting for age, sex, education, and subtype group where applicable. Unless otherwise stated, values are mean ± SD.

Data availability

OCT images cannot be shared as per data protection policies in place in our respective institutes. The data that support the findings of this study are available upon reasonable request from qualified investigators.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

This study enrolled 48 patients with FTLD and 38 NCs (Fig. 1). The pFTLD-tau group (n = 21, 31 eyes) comprised 20 participants with clinically diagnosed PSP (PSP; 30 eyes) and one with nfvPPA (1 eye). The pFTLD-TDP group (n = 27, 43 eyes) comprised 25 participants with clinically diagnosed bvFTD (40 eyes) and two with svPPA (3 eyes). The NCs (n = 38, 59 eyes) included 10 participants (13 eyes) who underwent PET evaluation and 28 cognitively normal family members (46 eyes).

Compared with the NCs, both pFTLD-tau and pFTLD-TDP showed significantly lower MMSE and MoCA scores and worse BCVA (all FDR-adjusted P < 0.01). No significant differences were observed between the two subtypes in cognitive scores or BCVA. Other demographic variables did not differ significantly among groups (Table 1).

Differences in retinal thicknesses

Both FTLD subtypes showed evidence of inner retinal thinning relative to NCs, with more widespread changes in the pFTLD-tau group (FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 in affected regions; Fig. 3; Table S1). ONL exhibited widespread, significant thinning in pFTLD-tau (Cohen’s d= -0.753 to -1.268; all FDR-adjusted P < 0.05), whereas ONL thickness did not differ significantly in pFTLD-TDP (all FDR-adjusted P > 0.05). Several sectors of other outer retinal layers (OPL, MEZ, RPE) were also significantly thinner in pFTLD-tau (Cohen’s d= -0.697 to -0.951; all FDR-adjusted P < 0.05).

Exploratory discrimination using retinal thicknesses and cognitive scores

Exploratory discrimination was assessed using stepwise logistic regression. Figure 5 shows the apparent ROC curves, and Table S2 reports the corresponding apparent and bootstrap optimism-corrected AUCs. For distinguishing pFTLD-tau from NCs, the combined thickness model retained GCIPL and ONL and showed the numerically highest AUC estimates (apparent AUC, 0.945 [95% CI, 0.894–0.996]; optimism-corrected AUC, 0.918 [95% CI, 0.850–0.981]; Fig. 5A), followed by the cognitive scores model (apparent/optimism-corrected AUCs, 0.936/0.906). The individual ONL model showed apparent and optimism-corrected AUC estimates of 0.847 and 0.814, respectively. For distinguishing pFTLD-TDP from NCs, the cognitive scores model showed the highest AUC estimates (0.946/0.929; Fig. 5B). BCVA-adjusted sensitivity analyses showed directionally consistent patterns: the combined thickness and cognitive scores models had comparably high AUCs for pFTLD-tau vs. NCs (0.982/0.939 and 0.980/0.937), whereas the cognitive scores model showed the highest AUC estimates for pFTLD-TDP vs. NCs (0.954/0.931; Table S3).

For distinguishing pFTLD-tau from pFTLD-TDP, no cognitive scores model was reported because no cognitive predictor was retained beyond the forced covariates. Among retinal models, the combined thickness model retained RNFL, ONL, and MEZ and showed the numerically highest AUC estimates (apparent AUC, 0.922 [95% CI, 0.852–0.993]; optimism-corrected AUC, 0.866 [95% CI, 0.769–0.951]; Fig. 5C; Table S2). The outer thickness model showed higher apparent and optimism-corrected AUC estimates than the inner thickness model (0.884/0.835 vs. 0.713/0.630). Within both the combined and outer thickness models, ONL had the lowest apparent and bootstrap-refitting ORs among retained standardized predictors (Table S2). The individual ONL model also showed moderate exploratory discrimination, with apparent and optimism-corrected AUCs of 0.808 (95% CI, 0.688–0.928) and 0.765 (95% CI, 0.654–0.891), respectively. BCVA-adjusted sensitivity analyses showed directionally consistent patterns. In this sensitivity analysis, the combined thickness model showed the highest AUC estimates (0.952/0.884; Table S3), followed by the outer thickness model (0.910/0.850). In both models, the retained ONL predictor showed the lowest OR among standardized predictors.

Correlations between ONL thickness and cognition

In exploratory partial correlation analyses, ONL thickness was positively correlated with MMSE and MoCA scores in the combined pFTLD-tau + NCs cohort (partial r = 0.289–0.439, P < 0.05; Fig. 6, Table S4). In the combined pFTLD-TDP + NCs cohort, corresponding associations did not reach statistical significance. In subtype-specific exploratory analyses, ONL thickness was correlated with MMSE and MoCA in the pFTLD-tau group (partial r = 0.433–0.483, P < 0.05; Table S5), whereas corresponding associations in pFTLD-TDP did not reach statistical significance. BCVA was not significantly correlated with MMSE or MoCA within either FTLD subtype, in the combined pFTLD cohort, or in the full analytic cohort including NCs after the prespecified covariate adjustments (Table S6).

Discussion

In this clinically diagnosed FTLD cohort with PET-supported probable subtyping, we evaluated whether OCT-derived retinal structural parameters provide exploratory subtype-informative signals for distinguishing pFTLD-tau from pFTLD-TDP. Our findings are consistent with prior reports of outer-retinal involvement in FTLD-tau, particularly thinning of the photoreceptor ONL. ONL-containing retinal models showed good exploratory discrimination estimates between PET-supported probable subtypes, and outer thickness models yielded higher AUC estimates than inner thickness models. In exploratory analyses, ONL thickness was correlated with global cognitive scores in pFTLD-tau, whereas corresponding associations in pFTLD-TDP did not reach statistical significance. These findings suggest that ONL thinning may be preferentially associated with pFTLD-tau and may provide complementary, noninvasive information for PET-supported probable subtype stratification, rather than replacing cognitive screening.

Widespread ONL thinning was identified in the pFTLD-tau group, while ONL thickness in pFTLD-TDP remained comparable to that of NCs; representative OCT B-scans with segmentation overlays are shown in Fig. 7 for visual illustration. These findings align with prior reports identifying ONL thinning in FTLD-tau, [14, 15] including histopathologic validation [17] and longitudinal observations of progressive ONL loss, [16] and support further evaluation of ONL thickness as a candidate retinal measure.

A key contribution of this work is the direct comparison of ONL-centered retinal models between PET-supported probable FTLD subtypes within the same cohort. In the pFTLD-tau versus pFTLD-TDP comparison, the age- and sex-adjusted combined retinal-thickness model retained RNFL, ONL, and MEZ and showed good exploratory discrimination (apparent/optimism-corrected AUC, 0.922/0.866), whereas no cognitive predictor was retained beyond the forced covariates. ONL showed the lowest apparent and bootstrap-refitting ORs among retained standardized predictors in the combined and outer thickness models. The outer thickness model yielded higher AUC estimates than the inner thickness model (0.884/0.835 vs. 0.713/0.630), and the individual ONL model showed moderate exploratory discrimination (0.808/0.765). These findings suggest that ONL thinning may provide a preferential candidate outer-retinal signal associated with pFTLD-tau, while inner-retinal thinning may be less subtype-informative in this cohort.

Pathophysiologically, ONL selectivity in FTLD-tau may reflect its cellular composition and molecular pathology. While tau deposition has been demonstrated in both inner and outer retinal layers from FTLD-tau patients, [47] we noted a relative predominance of thinning in the ONL. Given that tau is a key microtubule-associated protein maintaining photoreceptor ciliary function, [48] this may contribute to a selective vulnerability of photoreceptors to tau pathology, [49, 50] and ONL is rich in photoreceptor nuclei. This hypothesis may help explain the preferential ONL thinning observed in pFTLD-tau, whereas retinal alterations in pFTLD-TDP remain less well characterized and may be more heterogeneous. [10, 18–22].

In line with the observed ONL thinning pattern, ONL thickness was correlated with global cognitive scores in pFTLD-tau, whereas corresponding associations in pFTLD-TDP did not reach statistical significance. Taken together, these findings suggest that ONL thickness may provide subtype-informative and severity-related information in pFTLD-tau; however, the absence of significant correlations in pFTLD-TDP should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sensitivity and range restrictions of MMSE/MoCA. In FTLD-tau, trans-neuronal tau may spread in a prion-like fashion [51] or tau-related photoreceptor toxicity may provide a putative mechanistic link between retinal and cortical degeneration; however, this interpretation remains speculative without longitudinal and neuropathological confirmation. In pFTLD-TDP, the lack of significant ONL-cognition associations may reflect clinical heterogeneity, limited sensitivity of global cognitive measures, or retinal changes that are less strongly expressed in the ONL. Prior retinal studies in TDP-43—related contexts suggest possible retinal involvement, but the layer-specific pattern remains incompletely characterized. [10, 18, 19, 21] BCVA was reduced in FTLD but did not differ between pFTLD-tau and pFTLD-TDP. Given potential effects on visually mediated MMSE/MoCA items, we adjusted the group-comparison and OCT–cognition analyses for BCVA. BCVA was not significantly correlated with MMSE or MoCA within either subtype, in the combined pFTLD cohort, or in the full analytic cohort, suggesting that reduced visual acuity, as measured by BCVA, is unlikely to fully account for the observed ONL–cognition associations.

Compared with prior studies (Table S7), this work offers several notable strengths: (1) the integration of multimodal PET imaging with clinical criteria for PET-supported probable in vivo subtyping, which, to our knowledge, enabled the first direct comparison of retinal features between pFTLD-tau and pFTLD-TDP; (2) the use of a standardized SS-OCT platform coupled with a validated deep-learning segmentation algorithm for retinal sublayers, improving the objectivity and reproducibility of measurements.

Our study has several limitations. First, although PET/MRI was used to support in vivo subtyping, neuropathological confirmation was unavailable [34]; therefore, subtype assignments should be interpreted as PET-supported probable rather than definitive. Fluid biomarkers (e.g., NfL) were not systematically available, which could have further supported diagnostic confidence. Second, the diagnosis of pFTLD-TDP assignment relied on indirect evidence, including the absence of clinical and available imaging evidence supporting tau pathology, reduced likelihood of AD pathology, and supportive clinical and FDG-PET/MRI findings. Although bvFTD and svPPA provide clinical priors toward FTLD-TDP, bvFTD is pathologically heterogeneous and no validated in vivo TDP-43 biomarker was available; therefore, mixed or alternative FTLD pathologies, including FTLD-tau or FTLD-FUS, cannot be fully excluded. [2, 34, 43] Third, the pFTLD-tau cohort predominantly consisted of PSP-spectrum cases, limiting generalizability to other FTLD-tau subtypes, such as Pick disease. Fourth, amyloid/tau PET was not routinely performed in all cognitively unimpaired controls; therefore, subclinical neurodegenerative pathology cannot be fully excluded and may have introduced residual confounding and attenuated group differences. Future studies should prioritize more feasible biomarker strategies for control-group exclusion. Fifth, PET interpretation relied primarily on standardized visual reads, which remain the main approach in clinical practice. [8, 52] Due to multiple tracers and heterogeneous acquisition/reconstruction protocols, a cohort-wide SUVR pipeline was not feasible, which may limit objectivity and reproducibility. Sixth, detailed cataract grading and contrast-sensitivity testing were unavailable; therefore, residual effects of cataract on visually mediated cognitive testing cannot be fully excluded, despite OCT quality control and BCVA adjustment. Finally, the cross-sectional, single-center design and the limited sample size, especially in pFTLD-tau, limit the stability and generalizability of the exploratory discrimination models. Although bootstrap internal validation was performed, residual optimism and overestimation of model performance cannot be fully excluded. Larger, multicenter, longitudinal studies with external validation and FTD-relevant, domain-specific cognitive measures are warranted.

To our knowledge, this PET-supported in vivo comparison provides the first evaluation of retinal sublayer structural differences between pFTLD-tau and pFTLD-TDP. ONL thickness showed a preferential reduction in pFTLD-tau and contributed to exploratory discrimination between PET-supported probable subtypes, supporting its potential as a complementary noninvasive marker that warrants validation in larger, externally validated cohorts.
