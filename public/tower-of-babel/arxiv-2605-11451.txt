Convex order and heat flow for projection profiles of ℓ p n \ell_{p}^{n} balls
Soufiane Fafe

Abstract
. Let B p n B_{p}^{n} be the unit ball of ℓ p n \ell_{p}^{n} , with 1 ≤ p < 2 1\leq p<2 . We study central densities of one-dimensional marginals of the uniform measure on B p n B_{p}^{n} and of its Gaussian heat-flow regularizations. The profile is standardized by multiplying the central density by the standard deviation of the marginal. The key comparison is distributional: if the squared coordinates of one direction majorize those of another, then the corresponding squared projection is larger in convex order. A heat-flow identity turns this distributional comparison into strict Schur convexity of the smoothed central profile at every positive time. Together with the classical central-section theorem at t = 0 t=0 , this gives coordinate maximizers and diagonal minimizers for every t ≥ 0 t\geq 0 . We also evaluate the endpoint constants along the standard coordinate-to-diagonal chain and give a fourth-cumulant criterion for monotonicity of the coordinate profile.

1.
Introduction

Central profiles under heat flow

Let
\mu
be a centrally symmetric log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
h_{\mu,\theta}
denote the density of the one-dimensional marginal
\langle X,\theta\rangle
,
X\sim\mu
, in a direction
\theta\in S^{n-1}
. The value
h_{\mu,\theta}(0)
is the boundary density of the central halfspace orthogonal to
\theta
. We consider the scale-invariant central profile

\widetilde{A}_{\mu}(\theta)=\sqrt{\Var\langle X,\theta\rangle}\,h_{\mu,\theta}(0),\qquad\theta\in S^{n-1}.

This normalization makes the profile invariant under rescaling of the one-dimensional marginal.

In this paper we specialize to the unconditional model

B_{p}^{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x_{1}|^{p}+\cdots+|x_{n}|^{p}\leq 1\},\qquad 1\leq p<2,

and to its Gaussian heat-flow regularization

\mu_{p,n,t}=\nu_{p,n}*\gamma_{t},\qquad t\geq 0,

where
\nu_{p,n}
is normalized Lebesgue measure on
B_{p}^{n}
and
\gamma_{t}
is the centered Gaussian measure with covariance
tI_{n}
. If
Y\sim\mu_{p,n,t}
and
h_{p,n,t,\theta}
is the density of
\langle Y,\theta\rangle
, set

A_{p,n,t}(\theta)=h_{p,n,t,\theta}(0),\qquad\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(\theta)=\sqrt{\Var\langle Y,\theta\rangle}\,h_{p,n,t,\theta}(0).

At
t=0
, this is the normalized central hyperplane-section profile of
B_{p}^{n}
; for
t>0
, it is the corresponding profile after Gaussian smoothing. We prove that the coordinate and diagonal extremizers persist under Gaussian smoothing, with the positive-time comparison following from convex order of squared projections.

Central sections and convex order

At
t=0
, the coordinate and diagonal extremizers are classical. For
0<p<2
, central hyperplane sections of
B_{p}^{n}
are Schur-convex in the squared direction vector
(\theta_{1}^{2},\ldots,\theta_{n}^{2})
: the coordinate direction maximizes the central section volume and the main diagonal minimizes it. This theory originates in work of Meyer–Pajor and Koldobsky
[
8
,
5
,
6
]
and is available in the Schur-convex form of Eskenazis
[
3
]
; equality and stability refinements are due to Chasapis–Nayar–Tkocz
[
2
]
. We recall the precise form needed below in Section
4
.

For
1\leq p<2
, we show that the same directional order holds at the level of the laws of squared projections. We prove that squared one-dimensional projections of
\nu_{p,n}
are ordered in convex order according to majorization of the squared direction vector. Equivalently, for every convex test function
\Phi
:

\mathbb{E}\Phi(\langle\eta,X\rangle^{2})\leq\mathbb{E}\Phi(\langle\theta,X\rangle^{2})\qquad\text{whenever }s(\theta)\succ s(\eta).

Gaussian smoothing converts this distributional comparison into a comparison of central densities. The heat-flow identity of Proposition
2.2
expresses
\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(\theta)
for
t>0
as a Laplace transform of
\langle X,\theta\rangle^{2}
. Since
z\mapsto e^{-z/(2t)}
is strictly convex, the convex-order theorem implies strict Schur convexity of
\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}
for every
t>0
. Combining this positive-time result with the classical central-section theorem at
t=0
yields coordinate and diagonal extremizers for all smoothing times.

Main results

For
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
write

s(\theta)=(\theta_{1}^{2},\ldots,\theta_{n}^{2}).

The order on such vectors is the usual majorization order. Thus, on the sphere,
s(\theta)\succ s(\eta)
means that the squared mass of
\theta
is at least as concentrated as that of
\eta
.

Theorem A

(Convex order for squared projections)
.

Let
1\leq p<2
, let
X\sim\nu_{p,n}
, and let
\theta,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfy
\lVert\theta\rVert_{2}=\lVert\eta\rVert_{2}
. If

s(\theta)\succ s(\eta),

then

\langle\eta,X\rangle^{2}\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\langle\theta,X\rangle^{2}.

Theorem
3.1
proves Theorem A. For
1<p<2
the proof combines the sign–Dirichlet representation, a two-dimensional lower stop-loss comparison, and an append lemma for adding common coordinates.
The case
p=1
follows by weak convergence.

The heat-flow identity gives, for
t>0
,

\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{1+\frac{v_{p,n}}{t}}\,\mathbb{E}\exp\left(-\frac{\langle X,\theta\rangle^{2}}{2t}\right),\qquad X\sim\nu_{p,n},

where
v_{p,n}=\mathbb{E}X_{1}^{2}
. Since
z\mapsto\exp(-z/(2t))
is strictly convex, Theorem A yields the following positive-time comparison.

Theorem B

(Positive-time heat-flow Schur convexity)
.

Let
1\leq p<2
and
t>0
. The map

\theta\mapsto\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(\theta)

is strictly Schur-convex as a function of
s(\theta)
. In particular, with

u_{\mathrm{diag}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(1,\ldots,1),

one has

\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(u_{\mathrm{diag}})\leq\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(\theta)\leq\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(e_{1}),\qquad\theta\in S^{n-1},

with equality only for the corresponding signed-permutation orbits.

Adding the known central-section result at
t=0
gives the extremizer theorem for all smoothing times.

Theorem C

(Coordinate and diagonal extremizers for all smoothing times)
.

Let
1\leq p<2
,
n\geq 2
, and
t\geq 0
. Then for every
\theta\in S^{n-1}
,

\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(u_{\mathrm{diag}})\leq\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(\theta)\leq\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(e_{1}).

The right equality occurs exactly for coordinate directions
\{\pm e_{i}\}
, and the left equality occurs exactly for diagonal directions

\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(\pm 1,\ldots,\pm 1)\right\}.

The same statement holds for the unstandardized profile
A_{p,n,t}
.

On the canonical chain from
e_{1}
to
u_{\mathrm{diag}}
, the endpoint constants admit the following explicit form. For

u^{(k)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}(\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{k},0,\ldots,0),\qquad 1\leq k\leq n,

these constants factor through densities of normalized sums of independent generalized Gaussian variables. This yields a strict endpoint chain and, together with Theorem B, a strict canonical-chain ordering for all
t\geq 0
; see Section
5
.

For the coordinate profile, monotonicity in
t
is determined by the sign of the fourth cumulant. Put

\Phi_{p,n}(t)=\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(e_{1}),\qquad\Delta_{p,n}=m_{4,p,n}-3v_{p,n}^{2},

where
v_{p,n}=\mathbb{E}X_{1}^{2}
and
m_{4,p,n}=\mathbb{E}X_{1}^{4}
for
X\sim\nu_{p,n}
.

Theorem D

(Coordinate heat-flow monotonicity)
.

For
1\leq p<2
, the coordinate profile
\Phi_{p,n}
is nonmonotone if
\Delta_{p,n}<0
and is strictly decreasing on
(0,\infty)
if
\Delta_{p,n}\geq 0
. Equivalently, writing

R_{p,n}=\frac{m_{4,p,n}}{v_{p,n}^{2}},

the monotonicity threshold is
R_{p,n}=3
.

2.
Projection profiles and the heat-flow identity

The volume of
B_{p}^{n}
is

|B_{p}^{n}|=\frac{(2\Gamma(1+1/p))^{n}}{\Gamma(1+n/p)}.

(2.1)

The following beta-integral computation gives the coordinate moments and the variance normalization used in the profile.

Lemma 2.1

(Coordinate moments)
.

Let
X\sim\nu_{p,n}
and let
r>-1
. Then

\mathbb{E}|X_{1}|^{r}=\frac{\Gamma(1+n/p)\Gamma((r+1)/p)}{\Gamma(1/p)\Gamma(1+(n+r)/p)}.

(2.2)

In particular,

v_{p,n}:=\mathbb{E}X_{1}^{2}=\frac{\Gamma(3/p)\Gamma(1+n/p)}{\Gamma(1/p)\Gamma(1+(n+2)/p)}.

(2.3)

Therefore

\Var\langle X,\theta\rangle=v_{p,n}\lVert\theta\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

The density of
X_{1}
is proportional to
(1-|u|^{p})_{+}^{(n-1)/p}
. Normalizing by (
2.1
) gives

f_{p,n}(u)=\frac{|B_{p}^{n-1}|}{|B_{p}^{n}|}(1-|u|^{p})_{+}^{(n-1)/p}.

The substitution
z=u^{p}
gives (
2.2
). The covariance is diagonal by sign symmetry and has equal diagonal entries by permutation symmetry.
∎

For
t>0
define

M_{p,n,t}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\exp\left(-\frac{\langle X,\theta\rangle^{2}}{2t}\right),\qquad X\sim\nu_{p,n}.

Equivalently, for
\lambda>0
,

L_{p,n,\lambda}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}e^{-\lambda\langle X,\theta\rangle^{2}},

so that
M_{p,n,t}(\theta)=L_{p,n,1/(2t)}(\theta)
.

Proposition 2.2

(Smoothing identity)
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
,
n\geq 2
,
t>0
, and
\theta\in S^{n-1}
. Then

\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{1+\frac{v_{p,n}}{t}}\,\mathbb{E}\exp\left(-\frac{\langle X,\theta\rangle^{2}}{2t}\right),\qquad X\sim\nu_{p,n}.

(2.4)

Consequently, for fixed
p,n,t
, optimizing
\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}
over directions is equivalent to optimizing
M_{p,n,t}
.

Proof.

Let
G
be a standard Gaussian vector independent of
X\sim\nu_{p,n}
. Then the projection of
X+\sqrt{t}G
in direction
\theta
is

\langle X,\theta\rangle+\sqrt{t}Z,

where
Z\sim N(0,1)
is independent. Therefore its density at zero is

h_{p,n,t,\theta}(0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\mathbb{E}\exp\left(-\frac{\langle X,\theta\rangle^{2}}{2t}\right).

Multiplying by the standard deviation
\sqrt{v_{p,n}+t}
gives (
2.4
).
∎

We use the standard majorization order on nonnegative vectors with the same total mass. Thus
s\succ r
if

\sum_{i=1}^{m}s_{i}^{\downarrow}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}^{\downarrow}\qquad(1\leq m<n),\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i},

where
s^{\downarrow}
and
r^{\downarrow}
denote decreasing rearrangements. On unit directions,
s(\theta)
belongs to the simplex

\Delta_{n-1}=\{s\in[0,\infty)^{n}:s_{1}+\cdots+s_{n}=1\}.

3.
Convex order and positive-time Schur convexity

The positive-time comparison rests on the following convex-order theorem for squared projections. For nonnegative integrable random variables
U,V
, write
U\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}V
if

\mathbb{E}\Phi(U)\leq\mathbb{E}\Phi(V)

for every convex
\Phi
for which the expectations exist.

Theorem 3.1

(Squared-projection convex order)
.

Let
1\leq p<2
and let
X\sim\nu_{p,n}
. If
\theta,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfy

\lVert\theta\rVert_{2}=\lVert\eta\rVert_{2},\qquad s(\theta)\succ s(\eta),

then

\langle\eta,X\rangle^{2}\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\langle\theta,X\rangle^{2}.

(3.1)

Remark 3.2

(Sharpness of the range)
.

The convex-order conclusion of Theorem
3.1
is false for
p>2
with the same majorization hypothesis. Let
X\sim\nu_{p,n}
, let
q=p/(p-1)<2
, and take

\theta=e_{1},\qquad\eta=\frac{e_{1}+e_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}.

Then
s(\theta)\succ s(\eta)
. If the conclusion of
Theorem
3.1
held for this
p
, then

\langle\eta,X\rangle^{2}\preceq_{\rm cx}\langle e_{1},X\rangle^{2}.

However,

\sup_{x\in B_{p}^{n}}|\langle e_{1},x\rangle|=1,\qquad\sup_{x\in B_{p}^{n}}|\langle\eta,x\rangle|=\|\eta\|_{q}=2^{1/q-1/2}>1.

Thus
\mathbb{P}(\langle\eta,X\rangle^{2}>1)>0
, while

\langle e_{1},X\rangle^{2}\leq 1
almost surely. Testing convex order with

\Phi(z)=(z-1)_{+}
gives a contradiction.

Reduction for
1<p<2
.

The appendix proves the case
1<p<2
by the following reduction.

(i)

Sign–Dirichlet representation.
The uniform measure on
B_{p}^{n}
is represented by independent Rademacher signs and Dirichlet-distributed weights (Lemma
A.1
). This reduces squared projections of
X\sim\nu_{p,n}
to squared weighted Rademacher sums.

(ii)

Two-dimensional base case.
In dimension
2
, majorization amounts to balancing the two coefficients. A lower stop-loss comparison on planar
\ell_{p}
balls yields the squared convex-order statement (Proposition
A.17
).

(iii)

Append lemma.
Theorem
A.14
shows that squared convex order is preserved when a common coordinate, with its Beta weight and independent Rademacher sign, is appended. Its proof uses a distributional
second-derivative formula and a weighted layer inequality.

(iv)

Lifting to arbitrary dimension.
Majorization is generated by finitely many Robin–Hood transfers
[
7
, Ch. 2]
. Starting from the two-dimensional comparison and repeatedly appending common coordinates yields the general
n
-dimensional statement (Theorem
A.18
).

Proof.

For
1<p<2
, this is Theorem
A.18
. It remains to pass to the endpoint
p=1
. Let
p_{j}\downarrow 1
. The bodies
B_{p_{j}}^{n}
converge to
B_{1}^{n}
in measure, and their volumes converge. Hence the uniform measures
\nu_{p_{j},n}
converge weakly to
\nu_{1,n}
. Since all variables under consideration are supported in a common compact interval, the inequality

\mathbb{E}\Phi(\langle\eta,X_{p_{j}}\rangle^{2})\leq\mathbb{E}\Phi(\langle\theta,X_{p_{j}}\rangle^{2})

passes to the limit for every continuous convex
\Phi
on the relevant compact interval. This is exactly (
3.1
) for
p=1
.
∎

For the endpoint
p=1
in the strictness argument, we need the following fourth-moment computation.

Lemma 3.3

(Fourth moments for the cross-polytope)
.

Let
X\sim\nu_{1,n}
be uniform on
B_{1}^{n}
and let
i\neq j
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}X_{i}^{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{24}{(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)(n+4)},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}X_{i}^{2}X_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)(n+4)}.

(3.2)

Consequently, for every
\theta\in S^{n-1}
,

\mathbb{E}\langle\theta,X\rangle^{4}=3\,\mathbb{E}X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{2}+\bigl(\mathbb{E}X_{1}^{4}-3\,\mathbb{E}X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{2}\bigr)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}^{4},

(3.3)

and the coefficient
\mathbb{E}X_{1}^{4}-3\,\mathbb{E}X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{2}
is strictly positive.

Proof.

In the positive orthant of
B_{1}^{n}
, set
T_{i}=x_{i}
and let
T_{0}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}
. The Jacobian is one, and the simplex coordinates are uniformly distributed. Restoring the independent Rademacher signs gives

X_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}T_{i},\qquad(T_{0},T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})\sim\operatorname{Dirichlet}(1,1,\ldots,1),

with the signs independent of
T
. In particular,
X_{i}^{2}=T_{i}^{2}
and
X_{i}^{2}X_{j}^{2}=T_{i}^{2}T_{j}^{2}
.
For a Dirichlet
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n})
vector, the mixed moments satisfy

\mathbb{E}\prod_{k=0}^{n}T_{k}^{m_{k}}=\frac{\Gamma(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}a_{\ell})}{\Gamma(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}a_{\ell}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}m_{\ell})}\prod_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\Gamma(a_{k}+m_{k})}{\Gamma(a_{k})}.

Here all
a_{k}=1
and
\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}a_{\ell}=n+1
. Taking first
m_{i}=4
and all other
m_{k}=0
, and then taking
m_{i}=m_{j}=2
, gives the two identities in (
3.3
).

Finally, expanding
\langle\theta,X\rangle^{4}
and using sign symmetry to eliminate odd mixed moments yields

\mathbb{E}\langle\theta,X\rangle^{4}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}^{4}\mathbb{E}X_{i}^{4}+6\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\theta_{i}^{2}\theta_{j}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}X_{i}^{2}X_{j}^{2},

which simplifies to (
3.3
) since
\sum_{i}\theta_{i}^{2}=1
. The coefficient equals
12/((n+1)(n+2)(n+3)(n+4))>0
.
∎

We now record the strict positive-time form needed for uniqueness of the extremizers.

Proposition 3.4

(Strict positive-time Schur-convexity)
.

Let
1\leq p<2
,
t>0
, and
\theta,\eta\in S^{n-1}
. If

s(\theta)\succ s(\eta)

and
s(\theta)
is not a permutation of
s(\eta)
, then

M_{p,n,t}(\theta)>M_{p,n,t}(\eta).

(3.4)

Consequently,

\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(\theta)>\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(\eta).

Proof.

Theorem
3.1
gives

\langle\eta,X\rangle^{2}\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\langle\theta,X\rangle^{2}.

Since
z\mapsto e^{-z/(2t)}
is strictly convex, the inequality

M_{p,n,t}(\theta)\geq M_{p,n,t}(\eta)

is strict unless the two squared-projection laws coincide. This is the usual equality case in Strassen’s martingale characterization of convex order: under a martingale coupling, equality for a strictly convex test function forces the conditional law to be degenerate almost surely; see, for example, the standard convex-order treatment in
[
10
, Ch. 3.A]
.

It remains only to rule out equality of laws. If
1<p<2
, let
q=p/(p-1)>2
. The support of
\langle\theta,X\rangle
on
B_{p}^{n}
is

[-\|\theta\|_{q},\|\theta\|_{q}].

Since
s(\theta)\succ s(\eta)
and the vectors are not permutations,

\sum_{i}s_{i}(\theta)^{q/2}>\sum_{i}s_{i}(\eta)^{q/2},

because
x\mapsto x^{q/2}
is strictly convex. Hence the supports of the two projection laws differ.

For
p=1
, the support argument with
q=\infty
is not always strict, so we use fourth moments.
By Lemma
3.3
,
\mathbb{E}\langle\theta,X\rangle^{4}
is an affine function of
\sum_{i}\theta_{i}^{4}
with strictly positive slope.
Strict majorization implies
\sum_{i}\theta_{i}^{4}>\sum_{i}\eta_{i}^{4}
, hence the fourth moments (and therefore the laws) are different. Proposition
2.2
transfers (
3.4
) to the standardized profile.
∎

4.
Central sections and extremizers for all smoothing times

At
t=0
, the density
A_{p,n,0}(\theta)
is the central hyperplane section volume divided by
|B_{p}^{n}|
:

A_{p,n,0}(\theta)=\frac{\vol_{n-1}(B_{p}^{n}\cap\theta^{\perp})}{|B_{p}^{n}|}.

(4.1)

Indeed, this is the coarea formula applied to the projection
x\mapsto\langle x,\theta\rangle
.

We use the following form of the classical central-section theorem. The extremal inequalities go back to Meyer–Pajor and Koldobsky, the Schur-convex formulation is due to Eskenazis, and the equality cases used here follow from the stability theorem of Chasapis–Nayar–Tkocz. The following extremizer and equality-case form is the only part needed below.

Theorem 4.1

(Classical central-section extremizers; see
[
8
,
5
,
3
,
2
]
)
.

Let
0<p<2
and
n\geq 2
. For every
\theta\in S^{n-1}
,

A_{p,n,0}(u_{\mathrm{diag}})\leq A_{p,n,0}(\theta)\leq A_{p,n,0}(e_{1}).

(4.2)

The right equality occurs exactly for coordinate directions
\{\pm e_{i}\}
, and the left equality occurs exactly for diagonal directions

\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(\pm 1,\ldots,\pm 1)\right\}.

Eskenazis proved the stronger Schur-convexity statement for

\theta\mapsto\vol_{n-1}(B_{p}^{n}\cap\theta^{\perp})

as a function of
s(\theta)
for every
p\in(0,2)

[
3
]
; see also
[
4
]
. The equality cases used here follow from the stability estimates of Chasapis–Nayar–Tkocz.

By unconditionality and permutation symmetry, we may replace
\theta
by the nonnegative decreasing rearrangement of
(|\theta_{i}|)
.
In the coordinate-maximal case their inequality
[
2
, (2)]
, after arranging
|\theta_{1}|\geq\cdots\geq|\theta_{n}|
, gives

\frac{\vol_{n-1}(B_{p}^{n}\cap\theta^{\perp})}{\vol_{n-1}(B_{p}^{n}\cap e_{1}^{\perp})}\leq\bigl(|\theta_{1}|^{p}+(1-\theta_{1}^{2})^{p/2}\bigr)^{-1/p}\leq 1.

Since
\theta
is a unit vector arranged in nonnegative decreasing order,
|\theta_{1}|>0
.
For
0<|\theta_{1}|\leq 1
and
0<p<2
, equality in the last inequality forces
|\theta_{1}|=1
, hence
\theta
is a coordinate direction.
In the diagonal-minimal case their stability inequality
[
2
, (3)]
gives

\frac{\vol_{n-1}(B_{p}^{n}\cap\theta^{\perp})}{\vol_{n-1}(B_{p}^{n}\cap u_{\mathrm{diag}}^{\perp})}\geq 1+c_{p}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\theta_{j}^{2}-1/n)^{2},

so equality forces
\theta_{j}^{2}=1/n
for all
j
.
These two implications give the signed-permutation equality cases in Theorem
4.1
.
The independent canonical-chain proof in Section
5
below also gives strict endpoint ordering along the chain without invoking strict Schur-convexity.

Theorem 4.2

(Extremizers for all smoothing times)
.

Let
1\leq p<2
,
n\geq 2
, and
t\geq 0
. Then for every
\theta\in S^{n-1}
,

\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(u_{\mathrm{diag}})\leq\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(\theta)\leq\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(e_{1}).

(4.3)

The right equality occurs exactly for coordinate directions
\{\pm e_{i}\}
, and the left equality occurs exactly for diagonal directions

\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(\pm 1,\ldots,\pm 1)\right\}.

The same statement holds for the unstandardized profile
A_{p,n,t}
.

Proof.

For
t>0
, Proposition
3.4
applies. The vector
(1,0,\ldots,0)
majorizes every point of
\Delta_{n-1}
, and every point of
\Delta_{n-1}
majorizes
(1/n,\ldots,1/n)
. The strict equality cases are exactly the corresponding permutation classes of squared coordinates, and unconditionality gives the signed orbits.

At
t=0
, the profile is the central-section density, so the claim follows from Theorem
4.1
. Since the variance factor is independent of
\theta
at every
t\geq 0
, standardization does not change the order or equality cases.
∎

5.
Canonical directions and endpoint constants

We next evaluate the endpoint constants at

u^{(k)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}(1,\ldots,1,0,\ldots,0),\qquad 1\leq k\leq n.

They factor through the densities of normalized sums of independent generalized Gaussian variables, which gives an independent proof of strictness along the canonical chain.

For
1\leq k\leq n
set

H_{k}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}=0\}

and define

S_{p,k}:=\vol_{k-1}(B_{p}^{k}\cap H_{k}),\qquad S_{p,1}:=1.

The convention
S_{p,1}=1
is the usual zero-dimensional volume convention.

Proposition 5.1

(Endpoint factorization)
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
,
n\geq 2
, and
1\leq k\leq n
. Then

A_{p,n,0}(u^{(k)})=\frac{\Gamma(1+n/p)}{\Gamma(1+(n-1)/p)}\frac{\Gamma(1+(k-1)/p)}{(2\Gamma(1+1/p))^{k}}S_{p,k}.

(5.1)

Equivalently,

A_{p,n,0}(u^{(k)})=\frac{\Gamma(1+n/p)}{\Gamma(1+(n-1)/p)}\,b_{p,k},

(5.2)

where

b_{p,k}:=\frac{\Gamma(1+(k-1)/p)}{(2\Gamma(1+1/p))^{k}}S_{p,k}.

(5.3)

Proof.

At
t=0
,
A_{p,n,0}(u^{(k)})
is the central section volume divided by
|B_{p}^{n}|
. Write a point of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
(y,z)
with
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n-k}
. The hyperplane perpendicular to
u^{(k)}
is
H_{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k}
. Hence

\vol_{n-1}(B_{p}^{n}\cap(u^{(k)})^{\perp})=\int_{B_{p}^{k}\cap H_{k}}|B_{p}^{n-k}|\,(1-\|y\|_{p}^{p})^{(n-k)/p}\,\,\mathrm{d}y.

Because
B_{p}^{k}\cap H_{k}
is a
(k-1)
-dimensional star body with gauge
\|y\|_{p}
, homogeneity gives

\int_{B_{p}^{k}\cap H_{k}}(1-\|y\|_{p}^{p})^{(n-k)/p}\,\,\mathrm{d}y=S_{p,k}\,\frac{\Gamma(1+(k-1)/p)\Gamma(1+(n-k)/p)}{\Gamma(1+(n-1)/p)}.

Using

|B_{p}^{m}|=\frac{(2\Gamma(1+1/p))^{m}}{\Gamma(1+m/p)}

for
m=n
and
m=n-k
, and then dividing by
|B_{p}^{n}|
, gives (
5.1
).
∎

The constants
b_{p,k}
can be represented as densities of normalized sums. Let

g_{p}(x)=\frac{e^{-|x|^{p}}}{2\Gamma(1+1/p)},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},

(5.4)

and let

\varphi_{p}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\xi x}g_{p}(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x

be its characteristic function.

Proposition 5.2

(Fourier representation of the endpoint constants)
.

For every
1\leq p<\infty
and every
k\geq 1
,

b_{p,k}=\frac{\sqrt{k}}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{p}(\xi)^{k}\,\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

(5.5)

Equivalently,
b_{p,k}
is the density at zero of

\frac{Z_{1}+\cdots+Z_{k}}{\sqrt{k}},

where
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{k}
are independent with density
g_{p}
.

Proof.

Let
Z=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{k})
have product density
\prod_{i}g_{p}(z_{i})
. By the coarea formula, the density at zero of
Z_{1}+\cdots+Z_{k}
is

\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\frac{1}{(2\Gamma(1+1/p))^{k}}\int_{H_{k}}e^{-\|z\|_{p}^{p}}\,\,\mathrm{d}z.

Again using homogeneity in the
(k-1)
-dimensional body
B_{p}^{k}\cap H_{k}
,

\int_{H_{k}}e^{-\|z\|_{p}^{p}}\,\,\mathrm{d}z=S_{p,k}\Gamma(1+(k-1)/p).

Multiplication by
\sqrt{k}
gives the density at zero of
(Z_{1}+\cdots+Z_{k})/\sqrt{k}
, which is exactly (
5.3
). The Fourier inversion formula gives (
5.5
).
∎

Lemma 5.3

(Normalized Gaussian scale mixture)
.

Let
1\leq p<2
, and let
Z
have density
g_{p}(x)=e^{-|x|^{p}}/(2\Gamma(1+1/p))
. Then there is a positive non-degenerate random variable
V
such that

Z\mid V\sim N(0,V).

Equivalently,

g_{p}(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi V}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2V}\right)\right].

(5.6)

Proof.

Since
p/2\in[1/2,1)
, the function
r\mapsto e^{-r^{p/2}}
is completely monotone. Bernstein’s theorem for completely monotone functions
[
9
, Ch. 1]
gives a probability measure
\rho
on
(0,\infty)
such that

e^{-|x|^{p}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-sx^{2}}\,\rho(\,\mathrm{d}s).

Here
\rho
has no atom at
0
, because an atom at
0
would give a nonzero constant term and hence a nonintegrable component in
e^{-|x|^{p}}
. The kernels in this display are unnormalized. Since

e^{-sx^{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{s}}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(1/(2s))}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2(1/(2s))}\right),

we reweight
\rho
by the positive factor
(2\Gamma(1+1/p))^{-1}\sqrt{\pi/s}
. The resulting finite measure has total mass one because
g_{p}
integrates to one. If
S
has this reweighted law and
V=1/(2S)
, then (
5.6
) follows. The mixing law cannot be a point mass for
p<2
, since a degenerate Gaussian mixture would have density proportional to
e^{-cx^{2}}
, which corresponds only to the case
p=2
.
∎

Theorem 5.4

(Strict endpoint chain for
1\leq p<2
)
.

For every
1\leq p<2
and every
k\geq 1
,

b_{p,k+1}<b_{p,k}.

(5.7)

Consequently, for every
n\geq 2
,

A_{p,n,0}(u^{(1)})>A_{p,n,0}(u^{(2)})>\cdots>A_{p,n,0}(u^{(n)}).

(5.8)

Proof.

Let
Z
have density
g_{p}
. By Lemma
5.3
, write
Z\mid V\sim N(0,V)
with
V>0
non-degenerate.

Conditional on independent copies
V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}
, the normalized sum

\frac{Z_{1}+\cdots+Z_{k}}{\sqrt{k}}

is Gaussian with variance

\overline{V}_{k}:=\frac{V_{1}+\cdots+V_{k}}{k}.

Therefore

b_{p,k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\mathbb{E}\,\overline{V}_{k}^{-1/2}.

(5.9)

The sample means decrease in convex order:
\overline{V}_{k+1}\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\overline{V}_{k}
. Indeed, choose uniformly a
k
-element subset
I
of
\{1,\ldots,k+1\}
, independently of the variables. The average
k^{-1}\sum_{i\in I}V_{i}
has the same law as
\overline{V}_{k}
, and its conditional expectation given
V_{1},\ldots,V_{k+1}
is
\overline{V}_{k+1}
. Jensen’s inequality gives the convex-order relation.

Since
x\mapsto x^{-1/2}
is strictly convex on
(0,\infty)
, the convex-order relation gives
b_{p,k+1}\leq b_{p,k}
. The inequality is strict: if equality held, the martingale coupling above would be degenerate for this strictly convex test function, which would force the random
k
-subsample average to equal the full
(k+1)
-average almost surely. That would force
V_{1}=\cdots=V_{k+1}
almost surely, contradicting non-degeneracy of the mixing law. Hence

b_{p,k+1}<b_{p,k}.

The endpoint chain follows from the common positive factor in (
5.2
).
∎

Corollary 5.5

(Cross-polytope endpoint constants)
.

For
p=1
and every
1\leq k\leq n
,

A_{1,n,0}(u^{(k)})=\frac{n\sqrt{k}}{2^{2k-1}}\binom{2k-2}{k-1}.

(5.10)

Proof.

In section-volume normalization, (
5.10
) is the canonical-direction form of the Meyer–Pajor formula
[
8
]
. It also follows directly from Proposition
5.2
. For
p=1
,

g_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{2}e^{-|x|},\qquad\varphi_{1}(\xi)=\frac{1}{1+\xi^{2}}.

Thus Proposition
5.2
gives

b_{1,k}=\frac{\sqrt{k}}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\xi}{(1+\xi^{2})^{k}}=\frac{\sqrt{k}}{2\pi}\sqrt{\pi}\frac{\Gamma(k-1/2)}{\Gamma(k)}=\frac{\sqrt{k}}{2^{2k-1}}\binom{2k-2}{k-1}.

Since
\Gamma(1+n)/\Gamma(n)=n
, (
5.10
) follows from (
5.2
).
∎

Theorem 5.6

(Canonical-chain ordering for all smoothing times)
.

Let
1\leq p<2
,
n\geq 2
, and
t\geq 0
. For

u^{(k)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}(\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{k},0,\ldots,0),\qquad 1\leq k\leq n,

one has

A_{p,n,t}(u^{(1)})>A_{p,n,t}(u^{(2)})>\cdots>A_{p,n,t}(u^{(n)}),

(5.11)

and equivalently

\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(u^{(1)})>\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(u^{(2)})>\cdots>\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(u^{(n)}).

Proof.

For
t>0
, let

s^{(k)}=s(u^{(k)})=(\underbrace{1/k,\ldots,1/k}_{k},0,\ldots,0).

Then
s^{(k)}
strictly majorizes
s^{(k+1)}
for every
k<n
. Proposition
3.4
gives the strict positive-time chain. For
t=0
, the chain is Theorem
5.4
. Standardization multiplies every term by the same positive factor.
∎

6.
Coordinate heat flow and the fourth cumulant

We now study the time dependence of the coordinate profile. For
X\sim\nu_{p,n}
let

\Phi_{p,n}(t):=\widetilde{A}_{p,n,t}(e_{1}),\qquad t>0.

The coordinate density is

f_{p,n}(u)=C_{p,n}(1-|u|^{p})_{+}^{(n-1)/p},\qquad C_{p,n}=\frac{\Gamma(1+n/p)}{2\Gamma(1+1/p)\Gamma(1+(n-1)/p)}.

(6.1)

Therefore

\Phi_{p,n}(t)=\sqrt{v_{p,n}+t}\,\frac{2C_{p,n}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-u^{p})^{(n-1)/p}e^{-u^{2}/(2t)}\,\,\mathrm{d}u.

(6.2)

Let

m_{4,p,n}:=\mathbb{E}X_{1}^{4}=\frac{\Gamma(5/p)\Gamma(1+n/p)}{\Gamma(1/p)\Gamma(1+(n+4)/p)}

(6.3)

and define

\Delta_{p,n}:=m_{4,p,n}-3v_{p,n}^{2}.

(6.4)

Lemma 6.1

(Small-time expansion of the coordinate profile)
.

Let
1\leq p<2
and
n\geq 2
, and let
Z\sim N(0,1)
. As
t\downarrow 0
,

h_{p,n,t,e_{1}}(0)=C_{p,n}\left(1-\frac{n-1}{p}\,\mathbb{E}|Z|^{p}\,t^{p/2}+O_{p,n}(t)\right),

(6.5)

and therefore

\Phi_{p,n}(t)=C_{p,n}\sqrt{v_{p,n}}\left(1-\frac{n-1}{p}\,\mathbb{E}|Z|^{p}\,t^{p/2}+O_{p,n}(t)\right).

(6.6)

Moreover,

\Phi_{p,n}^{\prime}(t)=-C_{p,n}\sqrt{v_{p,n}}\,\frac{n-1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}|Z|^{p}\,t^{p/2-1}+O_{p,n}(1).

(6.7)

In particular,
\Phi_{p,n}^{\prime}(t)<0
for all sufficiently small
t>0
.

Proof.

By definition,

h_{p,n,t,e_{1}}(0)=\frac{2C_{p,n}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-u^{p})^{(n-1)/p}e^{-u^{2}/(2t)}\,\,\mathrm{d}u.

After the change of variables
u=\sqrt{t}\,y
, this becomes

h_{p,n,t,e_{1}}(0)=C_{p,n}\,\mathbb{E}\left(1-t^{p/2}|Z|^{p}\right)_{+}^{(n-1)/p}.

The expansion

(1-r)^{(n-1)/p}=1-\frac{n-1}{p}r+O_{p,n}(r^{2})\qquad(0\leq r\leq 1)

then gives (
6.5
); the Gaussian tail outside
|Z|\leq t^{-1/2}
is exponentially small and is absorbed in the remainder. Multiplying by

\sqrt{v_{p,n}+t}=\sqrt{v_{p,n}}\,(1+O_{p,n}(t))

gives (
6.6
).

It remains to justify the derivative sign. Differentiating the preceding expectation for
t>0
gives

\frac{d}{dt}h_{p,n,t,e_{1}}(0)=-C_{p,n}\,\frac{n-1}{2}\,t^{p/2-1}\mathbb{E}\left[|Z|^{p}\left(1-t^{p/2}|Z|^{p}\right)_{+}^{(n-1)/p-1}\right].

The expectation tends to
\mathbb{E}|Z|^{p}
as
t\downarrow 0
; this follows by truncating to
|Z|\leq R
and using the Gaussian tail to control the complement uniformly for small
t
. Hence

\frac{d}{dt}h_{p,n,t,e_{1}}(0)=-C_{p,n}\,\frac{n-1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}|Z|^{p}\,t^{p/2-1}+O_{p,n}(1).

Combining this with the derivative of
\sqrt{v_{p,n}+t}
gives (
6.7
), since
p/2-1<0
and the derivative of the variance factor contributes only
O_{p,n}(1)
.
∎

Lemma 6.2

(A signed-measure criterion)
.

Let
\mu
be a nonzero finite signed measure on
[0,1]
whose density has sign pattern
+,-,+
: it is positive on
(0,a)
, negative on
(a,b)
, and positive on
(b,1)
, allowing
a=0
or
b=1
by the corresponding one-sided interpretation. Assume

\int_{0}^{1}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0,\qquad\int_{0}^{1}x\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq 0.

Then

\int_{0}^{1}\phi(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)>0

for every strictly decreasing strictly convex
C^{1}
function
\phi
on
[0,1]
.

Proof.

Let

K(x)=\mu([0,x]).

Because the density of
\mu
has sign pattern
+,-,+
and
K(1)=0
, the function
K
is initially positive and has at most one sign change, from positive to negative. Also

\int_{0}^{1}x\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=-\int_{0}^{1}K(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x,

so the first-moment assumption gives
\int_{0}^{1}K(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x\geq 0
.

Integration by parts gives

\int_{0}^{1}\phi\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{0}^{1}(-\phi^{\prime}(x))K(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x.

The function
g=-\phi^{\prime}
is positive and strictly decreasing. If
K\geq 0
, the result is immediate. Otherwise let
c
be the sign-change point of
K
. Then
g(x)\geq g(c)
on the positive part of
K
and
g(x)\leq g(c)
on the negative part of
K
, so

\int_{0}^{1}gK\,\,\mathrm{d}x\geq g(c)\int_{0}^{1}K\,\,\mathrm{d}x\geq 0.

Strictness follows because
K
is not identically zero and
g
is strictly decreasing; if
\int K=0
, the positive part of
K
lies to the left of the negative part, so replacing the constant weight
g(c)
by the strictly decreasing weight
g
gives a strict increase.
∎

Theorem 6.3

(Coordinate heat-flow classification)
.

Let
1\leq p<2
and
n\geq 2
. If

\Delta_{p,n}<0,

then
t\mapsto\Phi_{p,n}(t)
is not monotone on
(0,\infty)
. If

\Delta_{p,n}\geq 0,

then
t\mapsto\Phi_{p,n}(t)
is strictly decreasing on
(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

Set

W=X_{1}^{2},\qquad L(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}e^{-\lambda W},\qquad\lambda=\frac{1}{2t},

and write
v=v_{p,n}
. By the smoothing identity,

\Phi_{p,n}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{1+2v\lambda}\,L(\lambda).

Thus
\Phi_{p,n}
is decreasing in
t
exactly when

F(\lambda):=\sqrt{1+2v\lambda}\,L(\lambda)

is increasing in
\lambda
.

As
t\downarrow 0
, Lemma
6.1
gives

\Phi_{p,n}^{\prime}(t)<0

(6.8)

for all sufficiently small
t>0
.

As
t\to\infty
,

\Phi_{p,n}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{1+\frac{v}{t}}\,\mathbb{E}e^{-W/(2t)}.

Since
W
is bounded, the following expansion may also be differentiated termwise. Expanding the two factors and using
\mathbb{E}W=v
and
\mathbb{E}W^{2}=m_{4,p,n}
gives

\Phi_{p,n}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(1+\frac{\Delta_{p,n}}{8t^{2}}+O_{p,n}(t^{-3})\right),

(6.9)

and hence

\Phi_{p,n}^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{\Delta_{p,n}}{4\sqrt{2\pi}\,t^{3}}+O_{p,n}(t^{-4}).

(6.10)

If
\Delta_{p,n}<0
, then (
6.8
) and (
6.10
) show that
\Phi_{p,n}^{\prime}
is negative near
0
and positive for large
t
. Thus
\Phi_{p,n}
is not monotone.

Assume now that
\Delta_{p,n}\geq 0
. We prove
F^{\prime}(\lambda)>0
for every
\lambda>0
. The density of
W
is

q(w)=C_{p,n}w^{-1/2}(1-w^{p/2})^{(n-1)/p},\qquad 0<w<1.

Differentiating
\log q
gives

\frac{q^{\prime}(w)}{q(w)}=-\frac{1}{2w}-\frac{n-1}{2}\frac{w^{p/2-1}}{1-w^{p/2}}.

Since

F^{\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{S(\lambda)}{\sqrt{1+2v\lambda}},\qquad S(\lambda)=vL(\lambda)+(1+2v\lambda)L^{\prime}(\lambda),

integration by parts yields

S(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\lambda w}\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w),

(6.11)

where

\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w)=q(w)w\left[v(n-1)\frac{w^{p/2-1}}{1-w^{p/2}}-1\right]\,\mathrm{d}w.

(6.12)

The boundary terms vanish because
wq(w)\to 0
at both endpoints.

It remains to identify the sign pattern of
\mu
. Put

r(w)=\frac{w^{p/2-1}}{1-w^{p/2}}.

Then
r(w)\to\infty
as
w\downarrow 0
and as
w\uparrow 1
. Moreover,

\frac{d}{dw}\log r(w)=\frac{p/2-1}{w}+\frac{(p/2)w^{p/2-1}}{1-w^{p/2}},

which has the unique zero
w^{p/2}=1-p/2
. Hence
r
decreases and then increases, with a unique minimum. The density factor in brackets in (
6.12
) is therefore positive near both endpoints and can have at most two zeros. Since

S(0)=\int_{0}^{1}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=v-\mathbb{E}W=0,

it cannot remain nonnegative on the whole interval. Thus it crosses twice, and the density of
\mu
has sign pattern
+,-,+
.
Moreover,

\int_{0}^{1}w\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w)=-S^{\prime}(0)=-(m_{4,p,n}-3v^{2})=-\Delta_{p,n}\leq 0.

Applying Lemma
6.2
to the strictly decreasing strictly convex function
w\mapsto e^{-\lambda w}
gives
S(\lambda)>0
for every
\lambda>0
. Hence
F
is strictly increasing in
\lambda
, and therefore
\Phi_{p,n}
is strictly decreasing in
t
.
∎

Corollary 6.4

(Threshold form)
.

Define

R_{p,n}:=\frac{m_{4,p,n}}{v_{p,n}^{2}}=\frac{\Gamma(5/p)\Gamma(1/p)}{\Gamma(3/p)^{2}}\frac{\Gamma(1+(n+2)/p)^{2}}{\Gamma(1+n/p)\Gamma(1+(n+4)/p)}.

(6.13)

Then
\Phi_{p,n}
is nonmonotone if
R_{p,n}<3
and strictly decreasing if
R_{p,n}\geq 3
.
For every fixed
1\leq p<2
, the sequence
n\mapsto R_{p,n}
is strictly increasing and has limit

R_{p,\infty}=\frac{\Gamma(5/p)\Gamma(1/p)}{\Gamma(3/p)^{2}}>3.

(6.14)

Consequently there is a finite threshold

N(p):=\min\{n\geq 2:R_{p,n}\geq 3\}

such that
\Phi_{p,n}
is nonmonotone for
n<N(p)
and strictly decreasing for
n\geq N(p)
.

Proof.

The first assertion is Theorem
6.3
, since
\Delta_{p,n}=v_{p,n}^{2}(R_{p,n}-3)
. Put
\alpha=1/p
and
x=1+n\alpha
. The
n
-dependent factor in
R_{p,n}
is

\frac{\Gamma(x+2\alpha)^{2}}{\Gamma(x)\Gamma(x+4\alpha)}.

Its logarithmic derivative in
x
is

2\psi(x+2\alpha)-\psi(x)-\psi(x+4\alpha)>0,

because the trigamma function
\psi^{\prime}
is positive and decreasing. Hence
R_{p,n}
is strictly increasing in
n
. The gamma-ratio asymptotic gives the limit (
6.14
).

Finally,
R_{p,\infty}>3
for
p<2
because it is the kurtosis of the generalized Gaussian density
g_{p}
. By the Gaussian-scale-mixture representation used in Theorem
5.4
, this kurtosis equals

3\frac{\mathbb{E}V^{2}}{(\mathbb{E}V)^{2}},

which is strictly larger than
3
when the mixing law is non-degenerate. This proves finiteness of
N(p)
.
∎

Corollary 6.5

(Cross-polytope coordinate profile)
.

For
p=1
,
\Phi_{1,n}
is nonmonotone for
n=2,3
and strictly decreasing for every

.

Proof.

For
p=1
,

Thus
\Delta_{1,n}
has the same sign as
n^{2}-n-8
. This is negative for

and positive for every

. The claim follows from Theorem
6.3
.
∎

7.
Conclusion

The main result is that majorization of
s(\theta)
implies convex-order comparison of
\langle X,\theta\rangle^{2}
for
1\leq p<2
. The classical central-section theorem supplies the endpoint comparison at
t=0
. For
1\leq p<2
, this gives a distributional strengthening of the endpoint central-section comparison:

s(\theta)\succ s(\eta)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\langle\eta,X\rangle^{2}\preceq_{\rm cx}\langle\theta,X\rangle^{2}.

Since the smoothed central profile is a Laplace transform of this squared projection, the convex-order comparison gives strict positive-time Schur convexity and hence the extremizer theorem for all smoothing times.

The proof separates the endpoint input from the positive-time argument. The central-section theorem is used only at
t=0
; for
t>0
, the comparison follows from convex order of squared projections. The canonical-chain calculation gives explicit endpoint constants, while the fourth-cumulant criterion determines the monotonicity of the coordinate profile in
t
.

We leave two questions open. First, within the range
1\leq p<2
, does an analogous convex-order comparison hold for other
\ell_{p}
-radial unconditional measures on
B_{p}^{n}
, for example for densities proportional to

F(\|x\|_{p})\mathbf{1}_{B_{p}^{n}}(x)?

Second, is there an analogous monotonicity criterion for arbitrary directions?

The restriction
p<2
is sharp for the convex-order comparison itself. For
p>2
, the convex-order conclusion in the same majorization direction already fails for
\theta=e_{1}
and
\eta=(e_{1}+e_{2})/\sqrt{2}
: although
s(\theta)\succ s(\eta)
, the support of
\langle\eta,X\rangle^{2}
extends beyond the support of
\langle e_{1},X\rangle^{2}
. Thus any theory beyond
1\leq p<2
cannot be a direct extension of the convex-order principle proved here.

Appendix A
Proof of the convex-order theorem for
1<p<2

This appendix proves Theorem
3.1
for
1<p<2
. Throughout the appendix set

\alpha=\frac{1}{p}\in\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right).

A.1.
Reduction of the proof

The argument reduces the
n
-dimensional statement to a two-dimensional
comparison. The sign–Dirichlet representation rewrites projections of

\nu_{p,n}
as weighted Rademacher sums. A lower stop-loss comparison proves
the two-dimensional case, and a beta–Rademacher append lemma preserves squared
convex order when common coordinates are added. Since majorization is generated
by Robin–Hood transfers, these ingredients imply the theorem.
The append lemma is proved by showing convexity of the lower stop-loss
transform after one coordinate is appended. The weighted centered and dual
layer inequalities supply the required distributional second-derivative
estimate.

We use the following sign–Dirichlet form of the standard probabilistic
representation of the uniform measure on

[

Lemma A.1

(Sign–Dirichlet representation)
.

Let
X^{(n)}
be uniform on
B_{p}^{n}
. Then

X_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}T_{i}^{\alpha},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,

where
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
are independent Rademacher signs and

(T_{0},T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})\sim\operatorname{Dirichlet}(1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha),

independently of the signs. Here
T_{0}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|^{p}
.

Proof.

In the positive orthant set
t_{i}=x_{i}^{p}
. Then

\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\,\mathrm{d}x_{n}=\alpha^{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}^{\alpha-1}\,\,\mathrm{d}t_{1}\cdots\,\mathrm{d}t_{n},

with
t_{i}\geq 0
and
t_{1}+\cdots+t_{n}\leq 1
. Adding the slack coordinate
t_{0}=1-\sum_{i}t_{i}
gives the stated Dirichlet law. The signs are independent and uniform by unconditionality.
∎

We shall use the following standard stop-loss characterization of the convex order; see, for example,
[
10
, Chapter 3]
.

Lemma A.2

(Lower stop-loss criterion)
.

Let
U,V
be nonnegative integrable random variables. Then
U\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}V
if and only if
\mathbb{E}U=\mathbb{E}V
and

\mathbb{E}(a-U)_{+}\leq\mathbb{E}(a-V)_{+}\qquad(a\geq 0).

Proof.

The usual stop-loss characterization for integrable random variables says that
U\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}V
is equivalent to equality of means and

\mathbb{E}(U-a)_{+}\leq\mathbb{E}(V-a)_{+}\qquad(a\geq 0).

All expectations are finite because
U,V
are nonnegative and integrable. Since
(a-U)_{+}=a-U+(U-a)_{+}
, equality of means makes the upper and lower stop-loss forms equivalent.
∎

Lemma A.3

(Dirichlet append step)
.

Let

be a projection generated by a

-dimensional
p
-ball through the sign-Dirichlet representation. Appending one coordinate with coefficient

gives

(A.1)

where

Proof.

In the

Lemma A.4

(Weighted beta-trapezoid)
.

Let
1/2<\alpha<1
and let
\delta\geq 3\alpha
. Then for
0\leq a<b\leq 1
,

\alpha\int_{a}^{b}t^{2\alpha-1}(1-t)^{\delta}\,\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{b^{2\alpha}-a^{2\alpha}}{4}\left((1-a)^{\delta}+(1-b)^{\delta}\right).

(A.2)

Proof.

Put
y=t^{2\alpha}
and
\mu=1/(2\alpha)\in(1/2,1)
. The integral on the left of (
A.2
) is

\frac{1}{2}\int_{a^{2\alpha}}^{b^{2\alpha}}(1-y^{\mu})^{\delta}\,\,\mathrm{d}y.

For
F(y)=(1-y^{\mu})^{\delta}
,

F^{\prime\prime}(y)=\delta\mu y^{\mu-2}(1-y^{\mu})^{\delta-2}\bigl(1-\mu+(\mu\delta-1)y^{\mu}\bigr).

Since
\delta\geq 3\alpha
,
\mu\delta\geq 3/2>1
, so

is convex. The trapezoid rule for convex functions gives the claim.
∎

For
B>0
set

u(s)=(1-s^{p})^{\alpha},\qquad H(s)=Bu(s)+s,\qquad G(s)=Bu(s)-s,\qquad 0\leq s\leq 1.

Lemma A.5

(Root configurations)
.

The function
G
is strictly decreasing on
[0,1]
. The function
H
has at most one critical point; it is increasing before this point and decreasing after it. Consequently, for a fixed
L>0
, every connected component of

\mathcal{A}_{L}=\{s\in[0,1]:|G(s)|<L<H(s)\}

is central, mixed, or cap, in the following sense.

(i)

If
0<L<1
, then either the component is central, opened by
G=L
and closed by
G=-L
, or it is mixed, opened by
H=L
and closed by
G=-L
.

(ii)

If
L>1
, then either it is opened by an increasing-branch root of
H=L
and closed by a decreasing-branch root of
H=L
, or it is opened by
G=L
and closed by a decreasing-branch root of
H=L
.

(iii)

The cases
L=1
, endpoint roots, and tangencies are limits of the preceding cases.

Proof.

Differentiating gives

u^{\prime}(s)=-s^{p-1}(1-s^{p})^{\alpha-1}.

Thus
G^{\prime}(s)=Bu^{\prime}(s)-1<0
. Also

H^{\prime}(s)=1-Bs^{p-1}(1-s^{p})^{\alpha-1}.

The positive function
s\mapsto s^{p-1}(1-s^{p})^{\alpha-1}
is strictly increasing from
0
to

, so

changes sign at most once. The configurations follow by intersecting
-L<G<L
and
H>L
with these monotonicity facts.
∎

Theorem A.6

(Weighted centered layer theorem)
.

Let
1/2<\alpha<1
and
\delta\geq 3\alpha
. For every
B>0
and

LB\sum_{|G|=L}\frac{(1-s^{p})^{\delta+\alpha}}{|G^{\prime}(s)|}+LB\sum_{H=L}\frac{(1-s^{p})^{\delta+\alpha}}{|H^{\prime}(s)|}\geq\int_{\{|G|<L<H\}}s(1-s^{p})^{\delta}\,\,\mathrm{d}s.

(A.3)

Endpoint roots and tangencies are interpreted by limits.

We prove the theorem component by component. In all component calculations below we use the variable

t=s^{p},\qquad s=t^{\alpha},\qquad u(s)=(1-t)^{\alpha}.

Thus letters
a,b
denote
t
-coordinates of the opening and closing roots. At a root of
H=L
, respectively of
G=\pm L
, the coarea denominators in the original

-variable become

\displaystyle\frac{(1-s^{p})^{\delta+\alpha}}{|H^{\prime}(s)|}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-t)^{\delta+\alpha+1}}{|1-Lt^{1-\alpha}|},

\displaystyle\frac{(1-s^{p})^{\delta+\alpha}}{|G^{\prime}(s)|}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-t)^{\delta+\alpha+1}}{|1\pm Lt^{1-\alpha}|},

(A.4)

where the sign is

at a root of

and

at a root of

, then

The two formulas for

are identical, using

Lemma A.7

(Universal polynomial inequality)
.

Let
0<s<1
and
s^{2}\leq r\leq s
. Then

(1+s^{2})(1+r)(1+r^{3})\leq 2(1+r^{2}s)^{2}.

(A.5)

Proof.

Let

F_{s}(r)=2(1+r^{2}s)^{2}-(1+s^{2})(1+r)(1+r^{3}).

Then

F_{s}(s)=(1-s)^{2}(1+s)^{2}(s^{2}-s+1)\geq 0.

Write
r=s^{2}+z(s-s^{2})
,
0\leq z\leq 1
. Expanding in powers of
z
gives

F_{s}(r)-F_{s}(s)=(r-s)H_{s}(r).

Since

, it is enough to prove

. In Bernstein form as a polynomial in

, the polynomial

-H_{s}(s^{2}+z(s-s^{2}))

has coefficients

(1-s)P_{0}(s),\quad\frac{(1-s)(1+s)}{3}P_{1}(s),\quad\frac{1-s}{3}P_{2}(s),\quad(1-s)(1+s)P_{3}(s),

where

\displaystyle P_{0}(s)

\displaystyle=s^{7}+2s^{6}+s^{5}-3s^{3}-s^{2}+s+1,

\displaystyle P_{1}(s)

\displaystyle=4s^{5}+3s^{4}-6s^{2}+3,

\displaystyle P_{2}(s)

\displaystyle=6s^{5}+9s^{4}-3s^{3}-8s^{2}+3s+3,

\displaystyle P_{3}(s)

\displaystyle=4s^{3}-3s^{2}+1.

These four polynomials have nonnegative Bernstein coefficients on
[0,1]

\displaystyle P_{0}

\displaystyle:1,\frac{8}{7},\frac{26}{21},\frac{6}{5},\frac{33}{35},\frac{3}{7},0,2,

\displaystyle P_{1}

\displaystyle:3,3,\frac{12}{5},\frac{6}{5},0,4,

\displaystyle P_{2}

\displaystyle:3,\frac{18}{5},\frac{17}{5},\frac{21}{10},\frac{6}{5},10,

\displaystyle P_{3}

\displaystyle:1,1,0,2.

Hence

, so
F_{s}(r)\geq F_{s}(s)\geq 0
.
∎

Lemma A.8

(Mixed component at the base exponent)
.

The estimate (
A.3
) holds on every mixed component when
\delta=3\alpha
.

Proof.

The mixed roots are described in

B(1-a)^{\alpha}+a^{\alpha}=L,\qquad b^{\alpha}-B(1-b)^{\alpha}=L,\qquad 0<a<b<1.

Set

X=a^{\alpha},\quad Y=b^{\alpha},\quad U=1-a,\quad V=1-b=wU,\quad r=w^{\alpha}.

Solving the two root equations gives

B=\frac{Y-X}{U^{\alpha}+V^{\alpha}},\qquad L=\frac{YU^{\alpha}+XV^{\alpha}}{U^{\alpha}+V^{\alpha}}=\frac{Y+rX}{1+r}.

(A.6)

The endpoint denominators from (

For

\alpha\int_{a}^{b}t^{2\alpha-1}(1-t)^{3\alpha}\,\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{Y^{2}-X^{2}}{4}U^{3\alpha}(1+w^{3\alpha}).

After cancelling the common positive factor

Cauchy’s inequality gives

\frac{1}{d_{a}^{+}}+\frac{w^{4\alpha+1}}{d_{b}^{+}}\geq\frac{(1+w^{2\alpha+1/2})^{2}}{d_{a}^{+}+d_{b}^{+}}.

We claim that

To verify it, put

This follows from the three inequalities

r-u_{0}\leq r(1-u_{0}),\qquad 1-u_{0}^{k+1}\geq(1-u_{0})(1+u_{0}^{k}),\qquad r(1-w)\leq(1+w)(1-r).

The last inequality is equivalent to

∎

Lemma A.10

(Cap components)
.

The estimate (
A.3
) holds on every cap component for every
\delta\geq 3\alpha
.

Proof.

Assume
L>1
. If the active component is empty, there is nothing to prove. Suppose first that the component begins at a root of

on the increasing branch, written in
t
-coordinates as

B(1-a)^{\alpha}+a^{\alpha}=L.

The opening endpoint contribution is

L(L-a^{\alpha})\frac{(1-a)^{\delta+1}}{1-La^{1-\alpha}}.

The entire bulk is bounded by

The left side is at least

Lemma A.13

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\{|L-D|<x<L+D\}}W(R)D(R)\,\,\mathrm{d}R.

\phi_{L,a}^{\prime}=-2(x-a)\mathbf{1}_{|x-a|<L}
,

x\phi_{L,a}^{\prime\prime}-\phi_{L,a}^{\prime}=-2a\mathbf{1}_{|x-a|<L}+2xL(\delta_{a-L}+\delta_{a+L}).

Apply this with
a=D(R)
and
a=-D(R)
and average. Integrating in
R
against
W(R)\,\mathrm{d}R
and using the one-dimensional coarea identity gives

xF^{\prime\prime}(x)-F^{\prime}(x)=\text{the right side of }\eqref{eq:app-dist-lemma}.

Finally, since
q=x^{2}
,

G^{\prime\prime}(q)=\frac{xF^{\prime\prime}(x)-F^{\prime}(x)}{4x^{3}}.

For endpoint and nonsimple roots, fix compact intervals
I_{N}\Subset I
whose endpoints are not level roots and whose union exhausts
I
. Perturb the level
x
and the
C^{1}
functions
L,D
by arbitrarily small
C^{1}
perturbations so that all roots in
I_{N}
are simple. The identity just proved applies to the perturbed data. Let the perturbation tend to zero: the bulk terms converge by dominated convergence, and the endpoint terms converge as one-dimensional coarea measures. Finally let
N\to\infty
. The tail hypothesis in the statement justifies this passage on noncompact intervals. In the append application the hypothesis is satisfied because the tails are dominated by the integrable weight
(1+R^{p})^{-\beta-3\alpha}
, so no mass is lost at infinity. This proves the distributional identity under the stated hypotheses.
∎

Theorem A.14

(Beta-Rademacher append lemma)
.

Let
1/2<\alpha<1
,
\beta\geq 1+2\alpha
, and
c\geq 0
. Let
Y_{1},Y_{2}
be square-integrable real random variables. Let

T\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)

and let
\varepsilon
be a Rademacher sign. Assume that
(T,\varepsilon)
is independent of
(Y_{1},Y_{2})
. If
Y_{1}^{2}\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}Y_{2}^{2}
, then

\left((1-T)^{\alpha}Y_{1}+cT^{\alpha}\varepsilon\right)^{2}\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\left((1-T)^{\alpha}Y_{2}+cT^{\alpha}\varepsilon\right)^{2}.

Proof.

It suffices to check lower stop-loss functions
\Phi_{\rho}(q)=(\rho^{2}-q)_{+}
and affine functions. Affine functions are preserved because the transformation adds the same second-moment contribution to both sides. The case
\rho=0
is trivial, since
\Phi_{0}(q)\equiv 0
on
[0,\infty)
; hence assume
\rho>0
.

For
q\geq 0
set

G(q)=\mathbb{E}_{T,\varepsilon}\left(\rho^{2}-\left((1-T)^{\alpha}\sqrt{q}+cT^{\alpha}\varepsilon\right)^{2}\right)_{+}.

The Rademacher average makes this depend only on
q
. We prove that
G
is convex. Put

R=\left(\frac{T}{1-T}\right)^{\alpha}.

Then

(1-T)^{\alpha}=(1+R^{p})^{-\alpha},\qquad T^{\alpha}=R(1+R^{p})^{-\alpha},

and the density of
R
is a positive multiple of
(1+R^{p})^{-\alpha-\beta}\,\mathrm{d}R
. Hence, up to a positive constant,

G(q)=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1+R^{p})^{-\beta-3\alpha}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\pm}\left(\rho^{2}(1+R^{p})^{2\alpha}-(\sqrt{q}\pm cR)^{2}\right)_{+}\,\,\mathrm{d}R.

Set

L(R)=\rho(1+R^{p})^{\alpha},\qquad D(R)=cR,\qquad W(R)=(1+R^{p})^{-\beta-3\alpha}.

Lemma
A.13
gives a second derivative formula. Now pass to

s=\frac{R}{(1+R^{p})^{1/p}},\qquad R=\frac{s}{(1-s^{p})^{\alpha}},\qquad\,\mathrm{d}R=(1-s^{p})^{-\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Then

1+R^{p}=(1-s^{p})^{-1},\qquad L(R)\pm D(R)=\rho\frac{1\pm(c/\rho)s}{(1-s^{p})^{\alpha}}.

Writing
A=c/\rho
and
y=x/\rho
, the dual layer functions are

h(s)=\frac{1+As}{(1-s^{p})^{\alpha}},\qquad g(s)=\frac{1-As}{(1-s^{p})^{\alpha}}.

The weights transform as follows. Since

W(R)L(R)=\rho(1+R^{p})^{-\beta-2\alpha}=\rho(1-s^{p})^{\beta+2\alpha}

and

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}R}{\,\mathrm{d}s}=(1-s^{p})^{-\alpha-1},

we have, at endpoint roots,

\frac{W(R)L(R)}{|(L\pm D)^{\prime}(R)|}=\frac{(1-s^{p})^{\beta+\alpha-1}}{|(\rho^{-1}(L\pm D))^{\prime}(s)|}.

For the bulk term,

W(R)D(R)\,\,\mathrm{d}R=\rho As(1-s^{p})^{\beta+\alpha-1}\,\,\mathrm{d}s.

Thus the bracket in the second derivative formula is
\rho
times the dual-layer expression in Theorem
A.12
, with parameter
A=c/\rho
and threshold
y=x/\rho
. Hence
G^{\prime\prime}\geq 0
distributionally on
(0,\infty)
. Continuity at
0
extends convexity to
[0,\infty)
.

Since
Y_{1}^{2}\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}Y_{2}^{2}
and
G
is convex,

\mathbb{E}G(Y_{1}^{2})\leq\mathbb{E}G(Y_{2}^{2}),

which is the desired lower stop-loss inequality after appending the coordinate.
∎

The two-dimensional base case

Lemma A.15

(Two-interval comparison)
.

Fix
\ell\geq 0
,
0\leq B\leq 1
, and
0\leq x\leq y
. Define

\Phi_{\ell}(t)=(\ell^{2}-t^{2})_{+},\qquad Q_{\ell}(x,y)=\Phi_{\ell}(By-x)-\Phi_{\ell}(By+x).

Then

Q_{\ell}(x,y)\geq Q_{\ell}(y,x).

Proof.

Since
\Phi_{\ell}
is even,

Q_{\ell}(x,y)=\Phi_{\ell}(|By-x|)-\Phi_{\ell}(By+x).

For
A\leq C
,

\Phi_{\ell}(A)-\Phi_{\ell}(C)=\int_{A}^{C}2r\mathbf{1}_{\{r<\ell\}}\,\,\mathrm{d}r.

Thus
Q_{\ell}(x,y)
is the weighted mass of the interval

I_{1}=[|By-x|,By+x],

while
Q_{\ell}(y,x)
is the corresponding mass of

I_{2}=[y-Bx,y+Bx].

Write

a_{1}=|By-x|,\quad b_{1}=By+x,\qquad a_{2}=y-Bx,\quad b_{2}=y+Bx.

Then
a_{1}\leq a_{2}
,
b_{1}\leq b_{2}
, and

b_{1}^{2}-a_{1}^{2}=b_{2}^{2}-a_{2}^{2}=4Bxy.

For any interval
[a,b]
with
a\leq b
,

\int_{[a,b]\cap[0,\ell]}2r\,\,\mathrm{d}r=\begin{cases}0,&\ell\leq a,\\
\ell^{2}-a^{2},&a<\ell<b,\\
b^{2}-a^{2},&b\leq\ell.\end{cases}

We compare the two intervals by the possible positions of
\ell
. If
\ell\leq a_{1}
, both masses are zero. If
a_{1}<\ell\leq a_{2}
, only the first mass is positive. If
a_{2}<\ell\leq b_{1}
, the difference is

(\ell^{2}-a_{1}^{2})-(\ell^{2}-a_{2}^{2})=a_{2}^{2}-a_{1}^{2}\geq 0.

If
b_{1}<\ell\leq b_{2}
, the first mass is
b_{1}^{2}-a_{1}^{2}=b_{2}^{2}-a_{2}^{2}
, while the second is at most this value. If
b_{2}<\ell
, the two masses are equal. Thus the mass of
I_{1}
is always at least the mass of
I_{2}
, proving the claim.
∎

Lemma A.16

(Boundary identity in dimension two)
.

Let
\rho\geq 0
and
u(s)=(1-s^{p})^{\alpha}
. Let

\theta(\varphi)=ae_{1}+be_{2},\qquad a=\sqrt{\sigma}\cos\varphi,\qquad b=\sqrt{\sigma}\sin\varphi,

with
a>0
and
0\leq b\leq a
. Put
B=b/a
and
\ell=\rho/a
. If

F_{\rho}(\varphi)=\int_{B_{p}^{2}}(\rho^{2}-\langle\theta(\varphi),x\rangle^{2})_{+}\,\,\mathrm{d}x,

then

\displaystyle-F_{\rho}^{\prime}(\varphi)

\displaystyle=2a^{2}\alpha\int_{0}^{1/2}\big((1-t)^{2\alpha-1}-t^{2\alpha-1}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\big[Q_{\ell}(t^{\alpha},(1-t)^{\alpha})-Q_{\ell}((1-t)^{\alpha},t^{\alpha})\big]\,\mathrm{d}t.

(A.16)

Proof.

Let
V(x_{1},x_{2})=(x_{2},-x_{1})
. Then
\operatorname{div}V=0
and

V\cdot\nabla\langle\theta(\varphi),x\rangle=\langle\theta^{\prime}(\varphi),x\rangle.

A standard Lipschitz approximation justifies applying the divergence theorem to
(\rho^{2}-\langle\theta,x\rangle^{2})_{+}
. On the positive boundary arc
x=(s,u(s))
, the outward normal measure is
(-u^{\prime}(s),1)\,\mathrm{d}s
, so

V\cdot n\,\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=(-u(s)u^{\prime}(s)-s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Summing over the four signed arcs and using evenness gives

-F_{\rho}^{\prime}(\varphi)=2a^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(-uu^{\prime}-s)\left[\Phi_{\ell}(Bu-s)-\Phi_{\ell}(Bu+s)\right]\,\mathrm{d}s.

Now set
t=s^{p}
. Then
s=t^{\alpha}
,
u=(1-t)^{\alpha}
, and

(-uu^{\prime}-s)\,\mathrm{d}s=\alpha\big((1-t)^{2\alpha-1}-t^{2\alpha-1}\big)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Pairing
t
with
1-t
gives (
A.16
).
∎

Proposition A.17

(Two-dimensional lower stop-loss order)
.

Let
X^{(2)}
be uniform on
B_{p}^{2}
,
1<p<2
. If
\theta,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
have the same Euclidean norm and

(\theta_{1}^{2},\theta_{2}^{2})\succ(\eta_{1}^{2},\eta_{2}^{2}),

then

\langle\eta,X^{(2)}\rangle^{2}\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\langle\theta,X^{(2)}\rangle^{2}.

Proof.

By unconditionality and permutation symmetry, it is enough to consider

\theta(\varphi)=ae_{1}+be_{2},\qquad a=\sqrt{\sigma}\cos\varphi,\qquad b=\sqrt{\sigma}\sin\varphi,\qquad 0\leq\varphi\leq\pi/4.

Increasing
\varphi
balances the squared coordinates. For
\rho>0
define

F_{\rho}(\varphi)=\int_{B_{p}^{2}}(\rho^{2}-\langle\theta(\varphi),x\rangle^{2})_{+}\,\,\mathrm{d}x.

We show that
F_{\rho}
is nonincreasing in
\varphi
. If
a=b
or
b=0
, this follows by limiting. Otherwise Lemma
A.16
applies. For
0\leq t\leq 1/2
, set
x=t^{\alpha}
and
y=(1-t)^{\alpha}
. Then
0\leq x\leq y
, so Lemma
A.15
gives

Q_{\ell}(x,y)\geq Q_{\ell}(y,x).

Also
(1-t)^{2\alpha-1}-t^{2\alpha-1}\geq 0
because
2\alpha-1>0
. Hence
-F_{\rho}^{\prime}(\varphi)\geq 0
.

The means are equal along the path, since

\mathbb{E}\langle\theta(\varphi),X^{(2)}\rangle^{2}=v_{p,2}(a^{2}+b^{2}).

The lower stop-loss criterion gives the desired convex order.
∎

Theorem A.18

(Squared-projection convex order for
1<p<2
)
.

Let
1<p<2
. Let
\theta,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfy

\lVert\theta\rVert_{2}=\lVert\eta\rVert_{2},\qquad s(\theta)\succ s(\eta).

If
X^{(n)}
is uniform on
B_{p}^{n}
, then

\langle\eta,X^{(n)}\rangle^{2}\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\langle\theta,X^{(n)}\rangle^{2}.

Proof.

By unconditionality of
B_{p}^{n}
, signs of the coefficients can be absorbed into the Rademacher variables in the sign–Dirichlet representation, so we may work with nonnegative coefficients. It is enough to prove the claim for one Robin-Hood transfer, since finite compositions of such transfers generate majorization
[
7
, Ch. 2]
and convex order is transitive. Permute coordinates so the transfer acts on coordinates
1,2
, while the remaining coordinates are common. Proposition
A.17
gives the squared convex order for the two active coordinates alone. Now append the common remaining coordinates one at a time. If the current dimension is
d\geq 2
, Lemma
A.3
writes the append operation as

Y\mapsto(1-T)^{\alpha}Y+cT^{\alpha}\varepsilon,\qquad T\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,1+d\alpha).

Since
1+d\alpha\geq 1+2\alpha
, Theorem
A.14
applies and preserves squared convex order. After all common coordinates have been appended, the elementary transfer is proved in the original dimension. Iterating over the transfers proves the theorem.
∎
