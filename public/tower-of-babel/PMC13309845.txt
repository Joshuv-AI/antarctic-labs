Long non-coding RNA triplex-dependent regulation of melanoma gene networks
Authors: Kushani Shah, Eleni Anastasakou, Leinal Sejour, Serafima Guseva, Christopher Rauchet, Leon Wert-Lamas, Riccardo Calandrelli, Robert Distel, Sheng Zhong, Ioannis Vlachos, Carl D Novina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract SLNCR is a long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) that promotes melanoma formation. Previously, we have shown that SLNCR regulates gene expression via its interactions with different transcription factors. Here we show that SLNCR is associated with several genomic sites that contain RNA•DNA triplex target sites (TTSs). The primary sequence of SLNCR contains four triplex-forming regions (TFRs), which are homologous to the TFRs of MEG3, HOTAIR, and PARTICLE lncRNAs. Full-length SLNCR overexpression promotes proliferation, migration, and invasion of melanoma cells by inducing characteristic gene expression changes of genes that are enriched in pathways involved in cell cycle regulation and cell migration. The transcriptomic signature of full-length SLNCR is reversed by deletions of TFR1, 2, 3, or 4. Full-length SLNCR is structurally flexible and permissive of different interactions with the DNA and/or proteins. Deletion of any individual TFR causes SLNCR to gain structural definition, potentially preventing some of its native interactions. Using RNA sequencing and the Triplex Domain Finder tool, we demonstrate that SLNCR TFRs form RNA•DNA:DNA triplexes with the TTSs in promoters of key genes (nodes) predicted to regulate melanoma migration and invasion networks. We further validate the effect on invasion using A375 cells in functional assays. Finally, inhibitory oligos that block SLNCR•DNA:DNA triplex formation reversed the effect of SLNCR-mediated migration ex vivo, demonstrating that triplex formation plays a role in driving melanoma progression.

Introduction

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) play key roles in gene regulation [1]. LncRNAs bind epigenetic [2], transcription [3], splicing [4], RNA transport and stability [5, 6], and translation factors [7] to regulate these processes. In some cases, lncRNAs can hybridize with RNAs to promote RNA decay [8] or serve as RNA sponges [9].

A more recently discovered mechanism of lncRNA activity is the formation of RNA•DNA:DNA triple helices (triplexes) [10]. Such triplexes form when double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) elements, called triplex target sites (TTSs), accommodate stretches of single-stranded RNA (ssRNA)-containing triplex-forming regions (TFRs). When triplexes form, the DNA remains double stranded, and the RNA interacts with the major groove of DNA. This mechanism was initially studied in the context of X-chromosome dosage compensation in female mammals. LncRNAs such as XIST and XACT coat inactive and active X chromosomes, respectively, through chromatin contacts [11, 12] and recruit proteins to activate heterochromatin formation [13, 14]. Other examples of lncRNAs that form RNA•DNA:DNA triplexes include MEG3 [15], HOTAIR [16, 17], TERC [18], DHFR [19], and PARTICLE [20]. MEG3 contains a single TFR at its 5′ end and forms triple helices with GA-rich DNA elements at the distal promoter regions of transforming growth factor (TGF)-β pathway genes [15]. The core of MEG3 also contains a protein-binding site for EZH2 [15]. Triplex formation and recruitment of the Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 via EZH2 at TGF-β pathway genes induced the formation of heterochromatin at those loci. Conversely, MEG3 binds to p53 to activate downstream targets; however, it is not clear whether MEG3 forms an RNA•DNA:DNA triplex at the promoters of p53 target genes [21, 22]. Thus, the outcome of triplex formation is often dictated by the nature of the recruited factor rather than triplex formation itself. Indeed, MEG3•DNA:DNA triplex formation can be important for breast cancer cell invasion [15] and brain tumor vascularization in mice [23], depending on context.

For these reasons, it is critical to perform global and unbiased analyses of triplex-dependent changes in target gene expression. SLNCR is a tissue-restricted lncRNA. Among differentiated tissues, SLNCR is expressed [2–8 reads per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (RPKM)] in skin, brain, small and large intestine, esophagus, stomach, and bladder [24]. Here, we describe four TFRs identified in SLNCR that affect gene expression, and annotate these TFRs to network-level regulation of the cell cycle (checkpoints) and cell migration. Thus SLNCR•DNA:DNA triplex formation might control cell biological processes and, when dysregulated, could activate oncogenes and inhibit tumor suppressor genes. These observations suggest that lncRNA•DNA:DNA triplex formation and network-level triplex-dependent gene regulation may be more common than previously thought.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

A375 cells were cultured as adherent cells in DMEM (Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium, Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). A375 and HeLa cells were purchased from the ATCC.

Stable A375 cells expressing various SLNCR TFR deletion constructs were generated by transfecting cells with pcDNA3.1 plasmid consisting of a puromycin-resistant gene and a CAG promoter expressing various TFR deleted constructs. After 48 h of growth in complete DMEM consisting of 2 μg ml−1 puromycin, single cells were sorted into a 96-well plate. Individual clones were picked from each construct that had similar SLNCR RNA levels and were then used for all downstream experiments.

For proliferation assays, stable cells (or transfected cells) were seeded at 0.01 million per well in a 24-well plate 48 h before the first count or cells were transfected with the indicated triplex blocking oligos (BLKs) 48 h post-seeding, and proliferation was measured every 48 h by manual cell counting using a hemocytometer and trypan blue dye.

For migration and invasion assays using A375 cells, 2.5 × 104 cells in serum-free medium were plated in either BD BioCoat™ matrigel inserts or uncoated control inserts (Corning), placed into DMEM with 30% FBS, and incubated for 16 h. Cells that did not migrate or invade were removed using a cotton-tipped swab, and chambers were rinsed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stained using the Fisher HealthCare™ PROTOCOL™ Hema 3™ Fixative and Solutions. Cells were imaged at ×20 magnification in three fields of view for three technical replicates for each of the three independent replicate experiments.

Plasmid construction

SLNCR1 was synthesized by Biomatik Corporation and cloned into pCDNA3.1 (−) or between the NheI and HindIII sites in pcDNA3.1/Puro-CAG-VSFP-CR (Addgene plasmid 40257). The TFR deletion constructs were synthesized via site-directed mutagenesis by Genewiz. The same constructs were lifted from pcDNA3.1/Puro-CAG-VSFP-CR by PCR using SLNCR-specific primers and introduced into pBluescript-II-SK(+) (Agilent #212205) by Gibson assembly (NEB E2611), directly after the T7 promoter and immediately followed by a HindIII site.

RNA extraction and cDNA library preparation

RNA was isolated using the Qiagen RNeasy® plus Mini Kit (Qiagen 74134) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted RNA was either submitted to Genewiz (Azenta) for RNA sequencing or cDNA was generated using SuperScript III (Invitrogen 18080093) reverse transcriptase according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The indicated transcripts were quantified using SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR® Green SuperMix (BioRad 1725270) on a CFX384 Touch™ Real-Time PCR Detection System.

RNA-sequencing analysis

Raw sequencing reads were quality-checked using FastQC (v0.11.5) (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) and data were pre-processed with Cutadapt (v2.5) [25] for adapter removal following best practices [26]. Gene expression quantification was performed by aligning against the GRCm38 genome using STAR (v2.7.3a) [27] and quantifying reads against Ensembl v98 [28] annotated transcript loci with featureCounts (Subread 1.6.2) [29]. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.24.0) [30], while ClusterProfiler (v3.12.0) [31] was utilized for downstream functional investigations. Differentially expressed genes were harnessed by the ClusterProfiler interface to perform the enrichment analysis and visualize results, while providing functional annotation from a variety of online resources including the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Gene Ontology (GO), Reactome, and Molecular Signature databases. Plots were generated in R using ggplot2 (v3.3.3) [32] and ComplexHeatmap (v2.6.2) [33].

Triplex Domain Finder analysis

The “genomic region test” of the Triplex Domain Finder software package [34] was used to identify the potential binding sites for TFRs in the 2 kb transcription start-proximal regions of all annotated protein-coding genes. Human genome (hg38) reference transcript annotations were obtained from the University of California Santa Cruz Genomics Institute (UCSC) [35]. For instance, the command used for TFR1 was “rgt-TDF regiontest -r TFR1.fasta -bed all_1000_t.bed -organism hg38 -rn TFR1 -o TFR1 -l 10”. The input bed file consisted of genomic regions flanking annotated transcription start sites ± 1000 bp. Default parameters for yje “genomic regions test” were used for all TFR tests.

The “promoter region test” of the Triplex Domain Finder package [34] was used to identify the potential binding sites for TFRs in the promoter regions of the differentially expressed genes between SLNCR-FL and SLNCRΔTFR2. The hg38 genome assembly was used in this analysis, and minimal triplex length was set to 10 bp. Other parameters were used with their default values.

Modified electrophoretic mobility shift assays

Electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) were performed as previously described by Cetin et al. [36] with the following modifications. The RNA (TFRs) and DNA (TTS) oligos were ordered from IDT. To label the annealed dsDNA, a 40 μl reaction was set up using 2.5 µM dsDNA, T4 PNK/10× PNK buffer (NEB, M0201S), [γ-32P]ATP (Perkin Elmer, BLU002A250UC), and nuclease-free water. The reaction was incubated at 37°C for 1 h followed by addition of 2 µl of 10× Apyrase Buffer and 1 µl of Apyrase (NEB, M0398S), and incubated for 1 h at 37°C to stop the labeling reaction. RNA oligos were heated to 95°C for 5 min then incubated on ice for 5 min before adding to the reaction. The 20 μl reactions were assembled at room temperature with 600 pmol of synthetic TFR1–TFR4 and NEAT1 TFR1 (positive control from [36]) incubated with 0.75 pmol of γ-32P-labeled dsDNA oligonucleotides which harbor sequences of TFR target genes. The reaction was incubated for 1 h at 37°C. At 30 min into the incubation, 1 U of RNase H (Invitrogen 18021014) was added where indicated. Triplex formation was monitored on a Novex TBE Gel, 20%, 12-well (Invitrogen EC63152BOX). The gel was dried for 45 min at 80°C. The dried gel was exposed to a film overnight and imaged the next day using the Amersham Typhoon Imager. The DNA oligos were ordered from IDT. The BLKs were ordered from AUM BioTech, LLC (Philadelphia, PA, USA). Some of the bases in the sequences of the BLKs listed in Table 1 were chemically modified and may have substitutions.

RNA sample preparation for in vitro assays

SLNCR-FL or ∆TFR mutant RNA was prepared based on the method described in [37]. Briefly, samples were in vitro transcribed using the TranscriptAid T7 High Yield Transcription Kit (ThermoFischer Scientific K0441) for 2 h at 37°C. Samples were then serially digested with TURBO DNase (Invitrogen AM2238) and with Protease K (Invitrogen 25 530 049) for 1 h each to ensure complete removal of DNA and transcription enzymes. Excess ions were chelated by addition of molar equivalents of EDTA pH 8.0 (Invitrogen AM9260G). RNA was then purified by passing through an Amicon column with 100 kDa cut-off (UFC510008) in a total of 5 ml of chromatography buffer (8 mM MOPS pH 6.5, 100 mM KCl, 0.1 mM EDTA pH 8.5) and concentrating the sample to a final volume of 500 μl. RNA was quantified by nanodrop, and 1 μg samples were folded by addition of a monovalent ion mixture to 1× (250 mM KCl, 50 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 0.1 mM Na-EDTA pH 8.5) and MgCl2 to 15 mM, incubating for a minimum of 30 min at 37°C. After folding, samples were kept at 37°C until final use and otherwise discarded. The temperature of folded RNA was never allowed to fluctuate.

MgCl2-containing gel electrophoresis

MgCl2-containing gel electrophoresis was performed as in [38]. Briefly, 1% agarose gels were run in 1× TBE buffer (Invitrogen LC6675) supplemented with the indicated concentrations of Mg2+. Gels in TBE with no Mg2+ and with 2 mM Mg2+ were run for 45 min at 110 V, and gels containing 5 and 10 mM Mg2+ were run for 120 min at 80 V. All gels were stained with 1× SYBR Safe gel stain (Invitrogen S33102) upon casting.

SHAPE probing

SHAPE probing was done as described in [39] following the amplicon library preparation workflow. The pure, DNA-free RNA was quantified by nanodrop, and 1 μg of RNA was reverse transcribed with Induro reverse transcriptase (NEB, M0681) with random 6-mer primers following the manufacturer’s protocol and with extension time for 1 h. Induro RT is the commercially available equivalent of the marathon RT recommended by previously published protocols. Samples were cleaned up by Zymogen DNA Clean & Concentrator-5 columns (Zymo Research D4013) following the alkaline hydrolysis method for RNA removal and eluting in nuclease-free water. Samples were then amplified by PCR covering the majority of SLNCR in overlapping fragments as done in [40]. The PCR amplicons were purified from a 1% TBE–agarose gel and extracted by the crush–soak method. The purified DNA fragments were quality controlled by Tapestation D1000 electrophoresis (Agilent, 5067-5582 and 5067-5583). Library preparation and next-generation sequencing were performed at the Molecular Biology Core Facilities (MBCF) at the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute. Raw fastq files were analyzed and SHAPE reactivities were calculated by ShapeMapper 2.62 [41]. Correlation analyses of the per nucleotide SHAPE reactivities across replicates were calculated by Spearman’s correlation ranking test using GraphPad Prism 10 (version 10.5.0).

One-dimensional 1H nuclear magnetic resonance

DNA duplexes were annealed in water by melting at 95°C for 5 min followed by gradual cooling at a rate of 1°C min−1 to 4°C, at an initial strand concentration of 500 µM. The annealed duplexes were then diluted 10-fold in Triplex buffer (10 mM Tris, 50 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2). For triplex formation, an RNA strand was added in a 10-fold molar excess, yielding final concentrations of ~500 µM RNA and 50 µM DNA duplex. Samples were incubated at 37°C for 1 h, followed by buffer exchange using 3 kDa cut-off centrifugal filters, with four cycles of 4-fold dilution and concentration. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) data were collected on a 900 MHz Bruker Avance spectrometer equipped with a 5 mm triple-resonance cryogenic probe (TCI 900 H-C/N-D Z-GRD) at 10°C. Data were collected and analyzed using Topspin 3.2 (Bruker).

Results

SLNCR harbors RNA–DNA triplex-forming regions and contacts genomic sites

The lncRNA SLNCR is expressed in cells derived from ectoderm (brain) and mesoderm (skin) lineages [24], and in cancers its increased expression has been functionally implicated in melanoma invasion [42, 43]. Therefore, SLNCR expression is thought to be important during embryogenesis for migration of neuro-ectoderm-derived cells into mesoderm [44, 45].

To identify targets for SLNCR in early, pre-committed cells, we examined endogenous SLNCR association with genomic loci in human embryonic stem cells (H1) and foreskin fibroblasts (HFFs) by mining in situ Mapping of RNA-Genome Interaction (iMARGI) datasets [46]. In this study, the authors applied an unsupervised method to identify significant patterns in RNA–DNA interactions across the genome using the iMARGI pipeline (sysbiocomp.ucsd.edu/public/frankyan/imargi_pipeline/further_analysis.html) that was run on the contact matrix deposited on the 4D Nucleome Data Portal (https://data.4dnucleome.org) with the following IDs: H1 cells, 4DNESNOJ7HY7; and HFFs, 4DNES9Y1GHK4. Figure 1A (for H1 cells) and Supplementary Fig. S1A (for HFFs) show the contact matrix downloaded from the HiGlass viewer tool of the 4D Nucleome Data Portal focusing around chromosome 17, where SLNCR is located. RNA is on the rows and the DNA contacts are on the columns. Supplementary Table S1 presents the annotated iMARGI read pairs where RNA ends are overlapping SLNCR, and DNA ends are a summary of the target genes sorted by the number of read pairs in decreasing order. The first tab for each cell line has the reads obtained from running the iMARGI pipeline and the second tab has annoted gene name and biotype from H1 cells and HFFs. iMARGI identified 2482 SLNCR-associated loci in H1 cells and 888 SLNCR-associated loci in HFFs (Supplementary Table S1).

SLNCR can form complexes with several transcription factors including the androgen receptor (AR), the brain-specific homeobox protein 3a (Brn3a), the early growth response protein 1 (EGR1), and the early region 2 binding factor (“early 2 factor”; E2F1) [42, 43, 47, 48]. To identify SLNCR targets that could mediate migration of primary cells, we searched iMARGI datasets for high-confidence SLNCR targets (i.e. having at least 10 reads of SLNCR at that locus) with transcription factor-binding sites for known SLNCR-interacting proteins. We used chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) datasets as a basis of genomic sites bound by these transcription factors [43]. However, direct comparison between the iMARGI and ChIP-seq data did not identify significant common targets between genes bound by AR and SLNCR (Fisher’s exact test P-value = 0.213) or EGR1 and SLNCR (Fisher exact test P-value = 0.208) or E2F1 and SLNCR (Fisher’s exact test P-value = 0.253). The non-significant P-values indicate that the overlap seen is likely to be due to chance. Therefore, we looked for other mechanisms for RNA binding to DNA.

Prior studies have described a direct interaction of RNA with DNA via triplex formation [36, 49, 50]. We searched the literature for previously reported RNA sequences that form triplexes [51] and compared them with the sequence of SLNCR using Triplex Domain Finder [34]. Triplex Domain Finder characterizes the potential for RNA to form RNA•DNA:DNA triplexes within specific target DNA regions. It begins by identifying DNA-binding domains (DBDs) using overlapping RNA regions from TFR–TTS pairs. The algorithm then compares the number of TTSs formed by a DBD in target regions (promoters for this study) with that in background regions (random genomic areas). DBDs with significantly more TTSs in promoter regions than background are considered potential RNA TFRs. This exploration identified four TFRs in SLNCR by homology with previously reported TFRs (TFRs1, 2, 3, and 4).

We used the “genomic region test” function within Triplex Domain Finder to evaluate whether a TFR is likely to form triplexes in gene promoters and to specify TTSs for these TFRs. The TTS motifs of SLNCR are similar to those of MEG3 [15], HOTAIR [16], and PARTICLE [20], and like those, consist of AG-rich DNA sequences (Fig. 1B). Similarly, the corresponding TFRs of SLNCR are homologous (P = 0.001) to previously validated TFRs identified in other lncRNAs including MEG3, PARTICLE, and HOTAIR (Fig. 1B). TFR1 (nucleotides 1272–1284), TFR2 (nucleotides 1434–1446), TFR3 (nucleotides 867–880), and TFR4 (nucleotides 1090–1101) are 12–14 nt long purine-rich sequences (79–100%) (Supplementary Fig. S1B) and are predicted to bind both purine- and pyrimidine-rich DNA; however, polypurinic stretches are commonly required for triplex formation [52].

Triplex Domain Finder predicted that the SLNCR TFRs bind their respective TTSs in either parallel or anti-parallel orientation depending on the TFR motif and allowed for mismatches (Supplementary Table S2). Triplex Domain Finder analysis showed that there are 30 genes (among the top 10% of the genes that have a TTS for any of the TFRs) that could form triplexes with more than one of the TFRs in SLNCR. However, the actual TTS sequence varied within the same gene promoter depending on the TFR pair (Supplementary Table S3). The predicted TFRs in SLNCR have similar purine content but are not homologous to each other. All SLNCR TFRs are predicted to have similar triplex-forming potential based on the total DNA targets identified by the “genomics region test” function (Supplementary Table S2).

We then used the Triplex Domain Finder tool [34] to identify the iMARGI DNA locations bound by SLNCR that could potentially form RNA•DNA:DNA triplexes. To do this, we compared the list of biologically relevant SLNCR-associated loci from the H1 dataset with the top 20% of the DNA sites from Triplex Domain Finder analysis, and found that there were common targets identified between the two methods (Fig. 1C). A common target between iMARGI and the TFR1 target is SLC39A11 and between iMARGI and TFR2 targets is SDK2. Figure 1D (H1 cells) and Supplementary S1C (HFFs) show the zoomed in contact matrix focusing on SLNCR (RNA/rows) and SLC39A11 (DNA/columns) on the left and SDK2 on the right. This list of common genes implied that the loci that were bound by SLNCR most probably contained TTSs (Supplementary Table S4).

To test if the likelihood of overlap between SLNCR genomic sites obtained from iMARGI and the top 10% of the TTSs predicted by Triplex Domain Finder is not random, we performed a Fisher’s exact test, resulting in a P-value < 0.0001. We hypothesized that SLNCR could bind directly to sequences in the promoters by forming SLNCR•DNA:DNA triplexes. We therefore tested a few of these SLNCR–target pairs in subsequent biophysical and biochemical assays to validate the interactions in vitro.

SLNCR triplex-forming regions regulate melanoma gene expression

SLNCR expression is thought to be important in embryogenesis for migration of cells into neuro-ectoderm [44, 45]. Since we cannot perform experiments with human embryonic cells, we used the melanoma cell line A375 which we have previously shown to be suitable for studying the effect of ectopic expression of SLNCR on invasion [42] and cell cycle progression [40].

To identify genes affected by SLNCR•DNA:DNA triplex formation, we overexpressed full-length SLNCR (SLNCR-FL) or mutant SLNCR constructs with deletion of TFR1–TFR4 (SLNCRΔTFR1–ΔTFR4) in A375 cells and then performed RNA-seq (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Fig. S2A–C). Triplex Domain Finder analysis indicates that TFR–TTS pairs need not be exact complementary matches, therefore point mutants for each TFR most likely will not result in disruption of the RNA–DNA triplex. Thus, each of the mutant TFR constructs expresses SLNCR with 12–14 nt deletions corresponding to the sequence of the respective TFR. We observed TFR-dependent changes in gene expression patterns (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Table S5). Compared with empty control, SLNCR up-regulated numerous genes (red) and down-regulated others (blue). This pattern was generally reversed upon the deletion of either of the TFRs. This suggested that RNA•DNA:DNA triplex formation may play a major role in SLNCR-induced gene regulatory networks and phenotypes. RNA-seq experiments for TFR1 and TFR2 deletions were performed together, and experiments with TFR3 and TRF4 were performed separately and therefore analyzed separately. To avoid batch effects, we included FL and empty control constructs with each batch. Our expectation is for the magnitude of gene expression changes mediated by RNA•DNA triplexes to be rather modest, but to lead to larger effects at the pathway level. This is akin to microRNA (miRNA)-mediated gene regulation, where relatively modest changes are observed at any one target gene individually, but with cumulative effects at the pathway level that often lead to very pronounced (even embryonically lethal) phenotypes. Despite all these factors, both TFRΔ1 and TFRΔ2 are highly successful experiments, leading to a clear identification and statistically significant enrichment in the investigated SLNCR pathways (migration and invasion).

SLNCR triplex-forming regions regulate gene networks involved in cell proliferation and migration

We performed pathway analysis of the top 500 down-regulated genes for each SLNCRΔTFR1–ΔTFR4 construct compared with SLNCR-FL. Cell cycle checkpoints, DNA damage checkpoints, and transcriptional regulation by p53 pathways were enriched upon TFR1 deletion (∆TFR1). Translation and EGFR (epidermal growth factor receptor) signaling were among the top pathways affected upon ∆TFR2. Like ∆TFR1, ∆TFR3 and ∆TFR4 led to enrichment of cell division, DNA repair, and cell cycle regulation pathways in down-regulated genes. (Fig. 2B–E). Genes commonly affected by all ∆TFRs compared with FL include CSK, DDIAS, HIST1H2AH, LRP1, and OGFRL1 which play roles in cell cycle, apoptosis, and growth signal regulation according to DAVID GOTERM analysis (Supplementary Fig. S2D). It is important to note that while each of the TFRs had some genes in common with other TFRs, they each had >100 unique targets (Supplementary Fig. S2D).

SLNCR-FL versus SLNCR∆TFR2 differentially expressed genes contain predicted triplex-forming region 2 target sites

We used Triplex Domain Finder to predict if any of the genes differentially expressed upon TFR deletion contained potential TTSs. While we did not see predicted TTSs among genes differentially expressed upon deletion of TFR1, TFR3, or TFR4, 25.5% of SLNCR-FL versus SLNCR∆TFR2 differentially expressed genes had TFR2 target sites in their promoters (Supplementary Fig. S2E; Supplementary Table S6). We then employed Shiny Go [53] to identify the pathways enriched among the differentially expressed genes with TFR2 TTSs and found that these genes regulated cell cycle, metabolism, and oncogenic signaling (Fig. 2F; Supplementary Fig. S2F). For instance, genes coding for PTEN and YWHAZ are predicted to have TFR2 TTSs and have independently been implicated in melanoma. PTEN is often mutated or lost in melanoma and is an important signaling molecule [54, 55]. YWHAZ is classified as a hub protein important for tyrosinase-triggered melanoma formation [56]. Together, these data implicate that SLNCR•DNA triplexes at genes such as PTEN and YWHAZ may regulate cell migration and metastasis.

Structural basis for SLNCR triplex formation

To assess whether the TFRs of SLNCR could physically form a triplex with DNA elements in cells, we performed a meta-analysis of our previously published dimethyl sulfate (DMS)-probing data of SLNCR-FL [40]. DMS is a cell-permeable chemical that reacts with ribonucleotides if they are not involved in Watson–Crick pairs (e.g. single stranded). We observed that the residues of TFR1 and TFR3 were mostly highly reactive (Supplementary Fig. S3A). The residues of TFR2 were moderately reactive. Our available data were not sufficient to explore TFR4 because DMS is biased towards A and C nucleotides and was therefore unable to probe the G-rich TFR4. Together, these data suggest that the nucleotides of TFRs 1, 2, and 3 are in single strand conformation within cells and therefore could interact with DNA to form triplexes.

Structural consequences of deleting triplex-forming regions within SLNCR

To assess the effects of each TFR deletion on SLNCR secondary structure, we produced pure SLNCR-FL and SLNCR ∆TFR1–∆TFR4 RNA in vitro and performed native agarose electrophoresis with increasing concentrations of MgCl2 (Fig. 3A). Divalent cations (e.g. Mg2+) are critical for RNA tertiary folding, and increasing the MgCl2 concentration is expected to promote the RNA to gain structural definition [57]. SLNCR-FL ran as two distinct and sharp bands in the absence of Mg2+ ions, and titration of MgCl2 in the electrophoresis buffer resulted in upward smearing of the sample (Fig. 3A). Because the smear appeared toward a higher apparent molecular weight than the no MgCl2 condition, it is unlikely that the RNA was degraded. The observed pattern of migration in the agarose matrix suggests that SLNCR-FL exists in multiple alternative tertiary structural conformations that are held together by inter- and intra-molecular interactions facilitated by the presence of divalent ions in the buffer. We hypothesize that intracellular SLNCR-FL behaves similarly and that as it samples different structures, it gains structural definition through its interaction with proteins or other biomolecules. The ∆TFR mutants migrated similarly to SLNCR-FL in the absence of Mg2+ ions, as observed by two distinct bands with dominant (darker) and recessive (lighter) intensities. Unlike SLNCR-FL, the mutant bands gained definition with increasing cation concentrations. These observations suggest that deleting any of the four TFRs biases SLNCR towards a defined structural conformation and it loses the ability to sample different alternative structures.

To explore this hypothesis further, we performed in vitro SHAPE probing of purified SLNCR-FL and SLNCR∆TFR1−∆TFR4 constructs with the SHAPE reagent 1M7 in triplicate—except SLNCR ∆TFR2 that was only sequenced in duplicate [39, 58]. We chose this strategy for two reasons: (i) A375 cells naturally express SLNCR-FL which would confound the results of an in-cell approach and (ii) the SHAPE reagent 1M7 has no base-bias as compared with the commonly used chemical DMS which is biased towards single-stranded adenines and cytosines. Thus, an in vitro approach using 1M7 was deemed most suitable. We performed correlation analysis of the individual nucleotide SHAPE reactivities across independent replicates. SLNCR-FL constructs showed high variability, with median r = 0.126, whereas the SLNCR ∆TFR constructs showed high correlation, with median r = 0.805 across all replicates of all mutants (Supplementary Fig. S3B−F; Supplementary Table S7). Plotting the SHAPE reactivities of the ∆TFR mutants after aligning them showed that the per nucleotide reactivities overlapped across the different mutants but not with the in vitro SHAPE trace of full-length SLNCR (Fig. 3B). Notably, in-cell DMS probing of SLNCR-FL had produced highly correlating data (median r = 0.882) [40]. Together, our past and present data support that SLNCR interacts with proteins and/or other nucleic acids in the cell to stabilize its conformation and to present the single-stranded TFR regions for triplex formation. Deleting the TFRs disrupts the ability of SLNCR to sample different structures in vitro and locks it into a thermodynamically stable but functionally inactive conformation.

Comparing the per nucleotide SHAPE reactivities of the deletion mutants, we observed that deleting any one of the TFRs maintained all long-range intramolecular interactions while causing minimal local rearrangements, limited only to the deleted region (Fig. 3B). Together our in-cell and in vitro structural characterization of SLNCR-FL and the ∆TFR mutants support that wild-type SLNCR is flexible and able to sample various conformations which are stabilized in cells via inter- and intra-molecular interactions. Deleting any of the TFRs prevents SLNCR from sampling different conformations and locks it in the most thermodynamically stable conformation.

SLNCR forms triplexes at promoters containing RNA•DNA:DNA triplex target sites

To test whether predicted SLNCR TFR•TTS pairs can form RNA•DNA:DNA triplexes, we performed EMSA using radiolabeled dsDNA oligonucleotides corresponding to predicted TTS sequences. We selected these targets from common loci between iMARGI and Triplex Domain Finder results (Fig. 1B). After incubating SLNCR-TFRs with predicted TTSs, the mobility of RNA•DNA:DNA triplexes was resolved from dsDNA duplexes by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Consistent with RNA•DNA:DNA triplex formation, RNase H treatment did not affect the mobility of the complex, excluding the possibility that the mobility shifted complex was an R-loop RNA–DNA duplex (Fig. 4A). A previously described RNA, NEAT1, was used as a positive control for triplex formation with its DNA target FLI1 [36]. We incubated a TTS sequence predicted to bind TFR1 (SDK2) with TFR2 and did not see the mobility of the TTS, supporting that triplex formation between SLNCR TFRs and their target TTSs is sequence specific (Fig. 4A, three lanes from right). Thus, EMSA experimentally validated each SLNCR TFR and target TTS prediction for selected SLNCR TFR–TTS pairs and provided a proof of principle for our predicted observations (Fig. 4A).

SLNCR forms a triplex at promoters containing RNA•DNA:DNA TTSs. (A) EMSA using 600 pmol of synthetic TFR1–TFR4 and NEAT1 TFR1 (positive control from [36]) incubated with 0.75 pmol of double-stranded 32P-labeled oligonucleotides which harbor sequences of TFR target genes predicted using Triplex Domain Finder. The last lane for every TFR + TTS reaction indicates treatment with 0.5 U of RNase H and shows that the shifted band is a triplex and not an R-loop. The three lanes on the right show TFR2 and one of the TTSs of TFR1 to demonstrate sequence specificity. (B–E) 1D 1H NMR spectra of dsDNA TTS (black) or SLNCR TFR–TTS pairs (∆TFR1 in green; ∆TFR2 in blue; ∆TFR3 in yellow; and ∆TFR4 in purple). Respective Hoogsteen pairs are shown to the left of each graph.

RNA•DNA:DNA triplexes form via Hoogsteen hydrogen bonding between the third base of the RNA and the Watson–Crick base pair in the DNA. Hoogsteen base pairing can occur between the RNA strand and purine-rich DNA sequences [10]. Triplexes are classified as either “parallel” or “antiparallel” based on whether the RNA strand runs 5′ to 3′ (parallel) or 3′ to 5′ (antiparallel) with respect to the purine-rich DNA strand. Triplets involved in parallel triplex formation include TA•T, CG•C, and CG•G, and those involved in antiparallel orientation include TA•A, TA•T, and CG•G [59].

We assessed triplex formation by 1D 1H NMR spectroscopy. Therefore, we produced samples of ssRNA and dsDNA oligos bearing the sequences of the SLNCR TFRs and their respective DNA TTSs. NMR spectra of dsDNA alone were recorded first, followed by the titration of the corresponding RNA. Triplex assembly was monitored by the appearance of diagnostic imino resonances characteristic of Hoogsteen base pairing (U•A and C+–G), and of mismatch pairing (G•AT, G•GC, G•TA), relative to spectra of the isolated RNA and DNA components [60–62, 63].

Comparison of the DNA:DNA and RNA•DNA:DNA spectra revealed new imino resonances (Fig. 4B–E), indicating triplex formation. Importantly, the imino peaks observed for the DNA duplex remain unchanged, indicating that the DNA base pairing was not disturbed and that all DNA:DNA contacts remained in the Watson–Crick conformation. The emergence of new imino peaks in the region between 9 and 13 ppm is consistent with the formation of Hoogsteen/reverse Hoogsteen hydrogen bonds, and strongly supports triplex assembly.

At pH 7.4, complexes with TFR1, TFR3, and TFR4 showed imino resonances in the putative Hoogsteen region (10–12 ppm). For TFR1, a new signal near 13.0 ppm is compatible with a U•AT pairing, and the additional peak at 10.2 ppm may reflect a mismatch G•AT or G•GC [62]. TFR3 shows peaks at 13.2 ppm, consistent with U•AT pairing, at 11.6 and 10.5 ppm, which could arise from G•AT/GC contacts. Based on the predicted contacts for parallel and antiparallel RNA orientation, the presence of an imino peak for U•AT in both complexes suggests a parallel orientation for the triplex. For TFR4, new peaks were observed at 13 ppm, probably corresponding to U•AT, and at 10.6 ppm, consistent with G•GC. In that case, the presence of a U•AT pairing suggests an antiparallel RNA orientation, but any inference about strand orientation is tentative.

For TFR2, only minor spectral changes were detected at physiological pH 7.4 upon RNA addition (data not shown). TFR2 is C rich and hence needs to be protonated to form a triplex. Without the added proton, the geometry and available hydrogen bond donors of the cytosine molecule do not allow it to properly pair with the guanine–cytosine Watson–Crick base pair. At pH 5.2, we observed duplex distortion, which led to a change in the intensity and position of peaks between duplex and triplex. New imino peaks were observed at ∼14.5–15 ppm consistent with C+ imino protons, suggesting a C+–G Hoogsteen base pair. A cluster at ∼11.0 ppm may reflect U–G contacts. Given that U–G involves imino contributions from both bases, these signals could arise from multiple states. Based on 1D data alone, we cannot determine whether the cytosine involved in C+–G originates from RNA:DNA or DNA:DNA, and the U–G features are likely to represent an equilibrium between alternative pairing arrangements. In cells, where the physiological pH is 7.4, the protonation can be unfavorable, but this interaction may still be possible with workarounds such as localized pH changes, use of modified nucleotides, and aid from chromatin-modifying enzymes.

Overall, the emergence of new imino resonances across TFR1/3/4 (pH 7.0) and TFR2 (pH 5.2) is consistent with triplex formation, but definitive pairing assignments and strand orientation would require additional experiments.

Taken together, we have used different biochemical methods to show that the TFRs of SLNCR can form stable complexes with their predicted TTS–DNA targets. These complexes are genuine triplexes that depend on Hoogsteen base pairing and cannot be undone by treatment with RNase H. Thus, we have provided a proof of principle for SLNCR triplex-mediated gene regulation and for the predictive power of our computational approach.

SLNCR triplex formation promotes melanoma invasion

To test whether triplex formation is responsible for melanoma invasion, we overexpressed the SLNCR∆TFR constructs in A375 cells and performed matrigel invasion assays (Supplementary Fig. S4A–C). Cells overexpressing full-length SLNCR (SLNCR-FL) showed 2-fold greater invasion compared with cells expressing control vector. Cells overexpressing SLNCRΔTFR1, 3, and 4 invaded 2-fold less than SLNCR-FL and at similar levels compared with empty vector, whereas cells overexpressing SLNCRΔTFR2 invaded 3-fold less than SLNCR-FL (Fig. 5A, B). Notably, deleting either of the TFRs reversed the effect of SLNCR-FL. This result suggests that (i) individual TFRs regulate pathways involved in invasion and/or that (ii) the deletion of each TFR affects the overall folding and protein interactions of SLNCR in a way that prevents the other TFRs from interacting with DNA.

Next, we assessed the effect of TFR deletion on A375 cell proliferation. SLNCR-FL increased the rate of melanoma cell proliferation by up to 2-fold (Fig. 5C). To maintain the integrity of full-length SLNCR and test the effect of each individual TFR, we obtained blockmer antisense oligos (ASOs) directed against each TFR which block triplex formation via the respective TFR without affecting the effective levels of SLNCR (Supplementary Fig. S5A). We then incubated A375 cells with each of the ASOs and performed matrigel invasion assays (Fig. 6B, C). Cells transduced with ASOs directed against TFR2 and TRF4, alone or combined, showed a 2-fold reduction when compared with cells incubated with scramble ASO. In contrast to expectations, ASOs against TFR1 and TFR3 did not block SLNCR-mediated invasion. This result was unexpected because SLNCR∆TFR1 and SLNCR∆TFR3 did not promote melanoma cell invasion as efficiently as SLNCR-FL (Fig. 5A, B). One possible explanation for this discrepancy is that TFR1 and TFR3 deletions did not affect overall SLNCR structure in vitro but may have affected SLNCR folding in cellulo to alter protein interactions in cells required for invasion. Consistent with deletion constructs, ASOs targeting the different SLNCR TFRs did not significantly affect proliferation of A375 cells (Fig. 6D).

Discussion

Here we show evidence that SLNCR•DNA:DNA triplexes can regulate gene expression and promote cell migration or cancer phenotypes. In situ hybridization identified SLNCR associated with several genes in primary cells, suggesting biological roles for this lncRNA in untransformed cell gene regulation. Deleting TFRs altered cell cycle and cancer progression gene networks and reduced these cancer phenotypes, indicating that SLNCR•DNA:DNA triplexes can regulate these networks. Finally, biochemical (Fig. 4A) and biophysical experiments (Fig. 4B–E) demonstrate that SLNCR sequences can form triplexes with promoters of genes in these networks.

Overexpressing SLNCR-FL leads to characteristic changes in gene expression in A375 cells ([42] and Fig. 2). However, overexpressing SLNCRΔTFR1–∆TFR4 deletion mutants only affects subsets of these genes, suggesting SLNCR triplex-dependent and -independent gene regulation. Of these, only SLNCR TFR2 and TFR4 led to cancer-relevant phenotypes. Therefore, not all TFRs that alter gene expression are disease-relevant and not all cell biological changes can be annotated to each TFR. Indeed, two different TFRs from the same lncRNA or from different lncRNAs might form triplexes at a common gene. It has been shown that some promoters are accessible in certain cell types or tissues, making them “hotspots” for triplex formation with multiple TFRs from one or more lncRNAs [36]. Our studies using Triplex Domain Finder also predicted that SLNCR TFRs have common gene promoters that each of the TFRs bind. These common gene promoters are enriched in “transcription factor”, “transcription”, and “DNA binding” David GO [64] functional pathways. Additionally, our structural studies (Fig. 3), deletion experiments (Fig. 5), and steric blocking ASO experiments (Fig. 6) indicate that the TFRs might not work completely independently.

Triplex formation may demonstrate the property of cooperativity. That is, one or more TFRs on SLNCR may be required for stronger triplex formation at another site, which could explain why SLNCRΔTFR1–∆TFR4 constructs affect SLNCR functionality differently, and why a single TFR deletion can inhibit SLNCR function in invasion or proliferation. TFR2 may have a protective role in SLNCR biology as deleting it reduces the invasion compared with empty control, but its effect could be masked by other TFRs. Alternatively, changes in the SLNCRΔTFR2 construct may disrupt DNA binding or localization, or modify interaction with proteins involved in cell invasion. For these reasons, it is difficult to directly link specific triplexes to disease processes and then to target specific RNA•DNA:DNA triplexes for therapy.

Gene expression can be regulated like an “on–off” switch. Examples of this type of regulation include the accessibility or inaccessibility of a locus, and transcription factor or splicing factor binding to a particular locus, gene, or RNA [65]. In other cases, gene expression can be regulated like a “dimmer” switch, subtly changing the output of several genes in a given pathway [66]. Examples of this type of regulation include different small RNAs such as miRNAs [67] and RNAs that work as a miRNA sponges [68]. In these latter cases, the binding of a specific miRNA might have a very modest effect on the stability or translation of its target RNA, but the effect of the miRNA may be very robust in regulating multiple genes in a common pathway. LncRNAs that form lncRNA•DNA:DNA triplexes might function more like miRNAs in that the exact effect at a target might be more subtle but may regulate key nodes in a pathway or multiple genes in a common pathway.

An increasing number of lncRNAs have been shown to form lncRNA•DNA:DNA triplexes. A logistical regression analysis identified 442 “super-lncRNAs” that bind to super-enhancers in 27 different human cell lines [69], suggesting that RNA•DNA:DNA triplex formation may be more common than previously thought. These super-lncRNAs may work at proximal (cis-acting) and distal (trans-acting) loci. However, very few of these have proven disease-relevant roles.

Here we have comprehensively characterized multiple TFRs on SLNCR by using a prediction algorithm, validated TFR binding in mobility shift assays and NMR, and functionally annotated lncRNA•DNA:DNA triplex formation in response to invasion. Experiments systematically introducing individual SLNCR TFR sequences in an SLNCR null background cell line would not have rescued invasion as just the 12–14 nt fragment cannot bind other proteins required to activate or suppress genes involved in invasion and migration pathways. We thus used the blocking oligos to retain as much structural integrity of the rest of the SLNCR RNA molecule when trying to inhibit RNA•DNA:DNA triplex formation. Network-level effects seen in pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 2B–F; Supplementary Fig. S2D–F) may reflect both direct triplex-mediated regulation and secondary downstream effects.

SLNCR can form complexes with multiple different transcription factors including AR, Brn3a, EGR1, and E2F1 [42, 43, 47, 48] in different contexts. Thus, SLNCR might confer transcription factor-dependent regulation by transcription factor binding to its cognate binding site or, in the absence of transcription factor, binding to DNA by SLNCR•DNA:DNA triplex formation. Indeed, it may be worthwhile to assess if any of these transcription factors are functionally recruited to genes with TTSs specific for SLNCR. In fact, a similar mechanism of action was shown for SLNCR–AR complex recruitment to EGR1 bound to genomic loci [43]. Further studies in different disease models and in primary cells are needed to translate our findings with SLNCR into general mechanisms of lncRNA function.

Combined in-cell and in vitro structural analysis showed that SLNCR is generally a flexible lncRNA. We hypothesize that SLNCR is structurally stabilized within cells via intra- and inter-molecular interactions with proteins and DNA. Deleting the TFRs in vitro “locks” SLNCR to the most thermodynamically stable co-transcriptionally acquired conformation. Thus, the structure–function relationship in SLNCR is akin to the proper function of a “slinky” toy. A slinky works properly if it is perfectly intact. If it is bent such that its smooth and regular coils are not in line, the slinky can no longer properly “walk” down steps. Similarly, a single TFR deletion in SLNCR can disrupt its proper activity in gene network regulation. To test this hypothesis, the ∆TFR mutants would have to be studied by RNA-probing techniques in cells. This approach combined with transcription factor recruitment analysis would further our understanding of the SLNCR regulatory network and mechanism. As new methodologies are becoming available, such as long-read sequencing or CRISPR editing, we envisage that in the future this could be done. Furthermore, we would characterize in detail the effects of TFR deletion and blockade on the structure of full-length SLNCR in cells. Our current work has laid the foundation for such an approach.

Another interesting observation is that genomic triplex target sites are enriched in ALU and SINE elements relative to the rest of the genome [36]. Hoogsteen base pairing can occur between RNA and purine-rich DNA [10], and LINEs and SINEs have low sequence complexity that is often purine rich [70]. LINEs and SINEs in the DNA are thought to have evolved to tether regulatory RNAs to specific DNA loci [36]. It is tempting to speculate that lncRNA triplex formation may be a means to identify and silence mobile genetic elements through direct binding and blocking of transposon activities or through recruitment of factors that repress transposition. By analogy to the situation in cancers, lncRNAs are aberrantly expressed in many cancer types, and dysregulation of the epigenome leads to inappropriate activation of numerous loci, making many more loci accessible to lncRNAs harboring TFRs. Theoretically, any lncRNA that contains TFRs could participate in lncRNA•DNA:DNA triplexes to promote or inhibit cancer pathways.

Limitations of the study

This study does not explore how ssRNA can access duplex DNA to form triplexes within densely packed chromatin. Further experimentation using techniques such as ATAC-seq and ChIP using antibodies for open chromatin marks such as H3K4me3 and histone acetylation can help answer if the chromatin at these sites is already open to allow for triplex formation.

Previous SLNCR studies show that it binds to AR and Brn3a [42], EGR1 [43], and E2F1 [40]. Attributing regulatory effects directly to SLNCR is limited by its reliance on these transcription factors. The idea that SLNCR could mediate transcription factor-dependent regulation without the factors binding their cognate elements remains to be rigorously tested. It is therefore important to determine whether any of these factors are functionally recruited to genes bearing SLNCR-specific TTSs.

Target validation in this study is limited to data obtained from iMARGI studies [46] and triplex domain finder predictions from melanoma cells. Experiments such as ChIRP and ChART could potentially provide additional mechanistic insight into the roles of SLNCR-mediated RNA–DNA triplex formation in normal and disease cell states.
