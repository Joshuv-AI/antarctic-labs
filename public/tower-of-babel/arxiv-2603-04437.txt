ASFL: An Adaptive Model Splitting and Resource Allocation Framework for Split Federated Learning E-mail: {chuiyangmeng, vincentw}@ece.ubc.ca, tangm3@sustech.edu.cn
Chuiyang Meng 1 , Graduate Student Member, IEEE , Ming Tang 2 , Member, IEEE , and Vincent W.S. Wong 1 , Fellow, IEEE

Abstract
Federated learning (FL) enables multiple clients to collaboratively train a machine learning model without sharing their raw data. However, the limited computation resources of the clients may result in a high delay and energy consumption on training. In this paper, we propose an adaptive split federated learning (ASFL) framework over wireless networks. ASFL exploits the computation resources of the central server to train part of the model and enables adaptive model splitting as well as resource allocation during training. To optimize the learning performance (i.e., convergence rate) and efficiency (i.e., delay and energy consumption) of ASFL, we theoretically analyze the convergence rate and formulate a joint learning performance and resource allocation optimization problem. Solving this problem is challenging due to the long-term delay and energy consumption constraints as well as the coupling of the model splitting and resource allocation decisions. We propose an online optimization enhanced block coordinate descent (OOE-BCD) algorithm to solve the problem iteratively. Experimental results show that when compared with five baseline schemes, our proposed ASFL framework converges faster and reduces the total delay and energy consumption by up to 75% and 80%, respectively.

Introduction

The emergence of federated learning (FL)
[
26
]
enables distributed model training across clients (e.g., smartphones, sensors, and embedded systems) while preserving data privacy.
However, in practical systems, conventional FL suffers from several limitations.
First, FL requires clients to train the entire model locally, which imposes substantial computation and energy burden on resource-constrained clients.
As the model and datasets grow in size and complexity, training full models on these clients becomes prohibitively expensive in terms of the delay and energy consumption on training.
This degrades the learning efficiency significantly.
Second, the central server remains under-utilized in FL, as it only performs simple parameter aggregation with a small computation load.
This can lead to system-wide inefficiency, especially in scenarios where the central server is equipped with powerful computation resources (e.g., graphics processing units (GPUs)) and an uninterrupted power supply.

Recently, split learning (SL)
[
8
]
has been proposed to address the aforementioned issues.
During SL training, the model is split into two parts.
Each client maintains one part of the model (called the client-side model), while the central server stores the remaining part (called the server-side model).
The client performs forward propagation (FP) and determines the intermediate output using its client-side model and local data.
The intermediate output is sent to the central server.
The central server continues the training with backpropagation (BP) using the server-side model and determines the gradient of the model.
It sends the gradient back to the client to complete the BP.
When a client has completed its BP with its client-side model, it then transfers the model to the next client for further training.

In

Moreover, these works assumed that the models are pre-split before training and require periodic client-side model aggregation by the central server, resulting in significant communication overhead.
They do not consider adaptive model splitting, where the models can be split at different layers in each training round and only several middle layers are transmitted between the clients and the central server, which can potentially reduce the communication overhead.

In this paper, we address the following question:

How to jointly improve the learning performance and reduce the delay and energy consumption of SFL training over wireless networks?

We aim to develop an SFL framework which enables adaptive model splitting and resource allocation over wireless networks.
Achieving this goal is challenging due to the following reasons.
First, the dynamic wireless channel conditions may introduce packet errors and thus degrade the learning performance.
Second, it is challenging to characterize the effect of model splitting and resource allocation decisions (i.e., resource block (RB) and transmit power allocation decisions) on the convergence rate.
Third, the coupling of those decisions further complicates the solution.
To overcome these challenges, our work makes the following contributions:

•

We propose an adaptive SFL (ASFL) framework to determine the model splitting and resource allocation decisions in each training round.
The proposed ASFL framework can improve the convergence rate and adapt to dynamic channel conditions in wireless networks.
In addition, ASFL takes into account packet errors during the intermediate output transmission and their impact on the learning performance.

•

We analyze the convergence rate of ASFL.
In particular, we quantify the impact of those decisions (i.e., model splitting, RB allocation, and transmit power allocation decisions) on the convergence rate.
Based on the analytical results, we formulate a joint problem which optimizes the learning performance subject to the long-term delay and energy consumption constraints of ASFL.

•

To solve the formulated problem, we propose an online optimization enhanced block coordinate descent (OOE-BCD) algorithm by decoupling the problem into a model splitting subproblem, an RB allocation subproblem, and a transmit power allocation subproblem.
To solve the model splitting subproblem, we propose an online optimization algorithm to determine the model splitting decision by considering the long-term delay and energy consumption constraints.
The stability of our proposed online optimization algorithm is guaranteed by showing that the decisions satisfy the long-term delay and energy consumption constraints.
Then, we determine the RB allocation decision by solving an integer programming problem.
Finally, we propose an iterative algorithm to solve the transmit power allocation subproblem.
We solve these subproblems alternately in each training round.

•

We conduct experiments on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 datasets using VGG-19 and ResNet-50.
We compare our proposed ASFL framework with federated averaging (FedAvg)
[
26
]
, SL
[
35
]
, SFL
[
33
]
, ACC-SFL
[
41
]
, and EPSL
[
24
]
.
Results show that in a wireless network setting with 10 clients, our proposed ASFL framework converges faster than the baseline schemes and can reduce the total delay and energy consumption on training by up to 75% and 80%, respectively.

II
System Model

We consider SFL over a wireless network.
Let
\mathcal{R}=\{1,2,\ldots,R\}
and
\mathcal{N}=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
denote the set of
R
training rounds and the set of
N
clients, respectively.
Each client
n\in\mathcal{N}
has a local dataset
\mathcal{D}_{n}
with
D_{n}
training samples.
Let
\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r}
denote client
n
’s model in the
r
-th training round.
We consider the model has
M
layers.
The set of layers is denoted by
\mathcal{M}=\{1,\ldots,M\}
.
In the
r
-th training round,
\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r}
is split into a client-side model
\mathbf{w}_{n}^{\mathrm{c},r}
and a server-side model
\mathbf{w}_{n}^{\mathrm{s},r}
, which are trained by client
n
and the central server, respectively.
We have
\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r}=[\mathbf{w}_{n}^{\mathrm{c},r};\mathbf{w}_{n}^{\mathrm{s},r}]
.

For client
n\in\mathcal{N}
, let
\bm{\xi}_{n}=(\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathbf{y}_{n})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}
denote a mini-batch of training samples of
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, where
\mathbf{x}_{n}
and
\mathbf{y}_{n}
are the training data and the corresponding labels, respectively.
Let
F_{n}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r};\bm{\xi}_{n})
denote the local loss function of client
n
on
\bm{\xi}_{n}
with model
\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r}

We denote the expected loss of client
n

F_{n}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}_{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}F_{n}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r};\bm{\xi}_{n})

Let

\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}D_{n}\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r}/\sum_{j=1}^{N}D_{j}

We aim to minimize

The optimal model is denoted as

.

Stage 1

r

If

Each client has at least one layer in its client-side model for privacy protection, i.e.,

Note that

If

(r-1)

Hence, the clients transmit the model parameters of middle layers (i.e., from the

-th to the
\|\bm{\lambda}^{r-1}\|_{0}
-th layer) to the central server.
Otherwise, the central server transmits the model parameters of middle layers to the clients.
In addition, we require that all layers up to and including the

-th layer are for the client-side model, while the subsequent layers are for the server-side model.
Therefore, we have
\lambda_{m}^{r}\leq\lambda_{m-1}^{r}

In stage 1, the delay is incurred due to adaptive model splitting.

c_{n}^{\mathrm{UP},r}
are as follows:

\displaystyle c_{n}^{\mathrm{DN},r}=B^{\mathrm{DN}}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{p^{\mathrm{s}}\lvert h_{n}^{r}\rvert^{2}}{B^{\mathrm{DN}}N_{0}}\right),

(4)

\displaystyle c_{n}^{\mathrm{UP},r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}u_{n,k}^{r}B\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{p_{n}^{r}\lvert h_{n}^{r}\rvert^{2}}{BN_{0}}\right),

(5)

where
B^{\mathrm{DN}}
and
p^{\mathrm{s}}
denote the downlink bandwidth and the transmit power of the central server, respectively.
Let
T_{n}^{\mathrm{S1},r}
denote the delay of stage 1 of client
n
in the
r
-th training round.
Let
\bm{\psi}=(\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\ldots,\psi_{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
denote the model size vector, with
\psi_{m}
representing the size of the
m
-th layer (in bits).
If
\|\bm{\lambda}^{r}\|_{0}\geq\|\bm{\lambda}^{r-1}\|_{0}
, then the size of the transmitted model parameters of the middle layers is equal to
(\bm{\lambda}^{r}-\bm{\lambda}^{r-1})^{\intercal}\bm{\psi}
.
Otherwise, the size of the transmitted model parameters of the middle layers is
(\bm{\lambda}^{r-1}-\bm{\lambda}^{r})^{\intercal}\bm{\psi}
.
Hence,
T_{n}^{\mathrm{S1},r}
satisfies

\displaystyle T_{n}^{\mathrm{S1},r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})=\begin{cases}\frac{(\bm{\lambda}^{r}-\bm{\lambda}^{r-1})^{\intercal}\bm{\psi}}{c_{n}^{\mathrm{DN},r}},&\text{if $\|\bm{\lambda}^{r}\|_{0}\geq\|\bm{\lambda}^{r-1}\|_{0}$},\\
\frac{(\bm{\lambda}^{r-1}-\bm{\lambda}^{r})^{\intercal}\bm{\psi}}{c_{n}^{\mathrm{UP},r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r})},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(6)

The delay of stage 1 in the
r
-th training round is given by

\displaystyle T^{\mathrm{S1},r}(\mathbf{U}^{r},\mathbf{p}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})=\max_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\{T_{n}^{\mathrm{S1},r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})\}.

(7)

Stage 2 has two processes: the client-side model FP and the intermediate output transmission.
Let
\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{FP}}=(v_{1}^{\mathrm{FP}},\ldots,v_{M}^{\mathrm{FP}})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
denote the vector of computation workload for FP, with
v_{m}^{\mathrm{FP}}
denoting the computation workload for FP of the
m
-th layer (in floating point operations (FLOPs)).
The delay of the client-side model FP of client
n
in the
r
-th training round is as follows:

\displaystyle T_{n}^{\mathrm{CFP},r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})=\frac{\kappa^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{FP}}D_{n}}{f_{n}},

(8)

where
f_{n}
and
\kappa^{\mathrm{c}}
denote client
n
’s central processing unit (CPU) frequency and the number of CPU cycles required for a client to complete one FLOP, respectively.
Let
q_{m}
denote the output size of the
m
-th layer (in bits).
Since
\bm{\lambda}^{r}
is a binary vector and
\lambda_{m}^{r}\leq\lambda_{m-1}^{r},m\in\mathcal{M}\backslash\{1\}
, the size of the intermediate output at the
\|\bm{\lambda}^{r}\|
-th layer can be represented by
\sum_{m=1}^{M-1}(\lambda_{m}^{r}-\lambda_{m+1}^{r})q_{m}
.
The delay of the intermediate output transmission of client
n
in the
r
-th training round is

\displaystyle T_{n}^{\mathrm{UP},r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})=\frac{D_{n}\sum_{m=1}^{M-1}(\lambda_{m}^{r}-\lambda_{m+1}^{r})q_{m}}{c_{n}^{\mathrm{UP},r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r})}.

(9)

The total delay of stage 2 in the
r
-th training round is

\displaystyle T^{\mathrm{S2},r}(\mathbf{U}^{r},\mathbf{p}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\>\max_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\{T_{n}^{\mathrm{CFP},r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})+T_{n}^{\mathrm{UP},r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})\}.

(10)

Stage 3 has four processes: the server-side model FP, the server-side model BP, the gradient transmission, and the client-side model BP.
Since
\beta_{n}^{r}
is a random variable, we define the expected delay of the server-side model FP in the
r
-th training round as the time required for the central server to complete the FLOPs of all server-side model FP, which is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\beta}^{r}}[T^{\mathrm{SFP},r}(\mathbf{U}^{r},\mathbf{p}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\>\frac{\kappa^{\mathrm{s}}(\mathbf{1}_{M}-\bm{\lambda}^{r})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{FP}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big(1-s_{n}^{r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r})\big)D_{n}}{f^{\mathrm{s}}},

(11)

where
f^{\mathrm{s}}
and
\kappa^{\mathrm{s}}
denote the CPU frequency of the central server and the number of CPU cycles required for the central server to complete one FLOP, respectively.
The expected delay of the server-side model BP in the
r
-th training round is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\beta}^{r}}[T^{\mathrm{SBP},r}(\mathbf{U}^{r},\mathbf{p}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\>\frac{\kappa^{\mathrm{s}}(\mathbf{1}_{M}-\bm{\lambda}^{r})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{BP}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big(1-s_{n}^{r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r})\big)D_{n}}{f^{\mathrm{s}}},

(12)

where
\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{BP}}=(v_{1}^{\mathrm{BP}},\ldots,v_{M}^{\mathrm{BP}})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
denotes the vector of computation workload for BP, with
v_{m}^{\mathrm{BP}}
denoting the computation workload for BP of the
m
-th layer (in FLOPs).
The size of the gradient of the
(\|\bm{\lambda}^{r}\|_{0}+1)
-th layer can be represented by
\sum_{m=1}^{M-1}(\lambda_{m}^{r}-\lambda_{m+1}^{r})\psi_{m+1}
.
The expected delay for the gradient transmission to client
n\in\mathcal{N}
in the
r
-th training round is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\beta_{n}^{r}}[T_{n}^{\mathrm{DN},r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\>\frac{\big(1\!-\!s_{n}^{r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r})\big)D_{n}\sum_{m=1}^{M-1}(\lambda_{m}^{r}-\lambda_{m+1}^{r})\psi_{m+1}}{c_{n}^{\mathrm{DN},r}}.

(13)

The expected delay of the client-side model BP of client
n\in\mathcal{N}
in the
r
-th training round is obtained as

\displaystyle\!\mathbb{E}_{\beta_{n}^{r}}[T_{n}^{\mathrm{CBP},r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})]\!=\!\frac{\kappa^{\mathrm{c}}\big(1\!-\!s_{n}^{r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r})\big)(\bm{\lambda}^{r})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{BP}}D_{n}}{f_{n}}.

(14)

The expected total delay of all clients in stage 3 of the
r
-th training round is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\beta}^{r}}[T^{\mathrm{S3},r}(\mathbf{U}^{r},\mathbf{p}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\>\max_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\beta}^{r}}[T^{\mathrm{SFP},r}(\mathbf{U}^{r},\mathbf{p}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})+T^{\mathrm{SBP},r}(\mathbf{U}^{r},\mathbf{p}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})

\displaystyle+T_{n}^{\mathrm{DN},r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})+T_{n}^{\mathrm{CBP},r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})]\}.

(15)

Thus, the expected total delay in the
r
-th training round is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\beta}^{r}}[T^{r}(\mathbf{U}^{r},\mathbf{p}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})]=T^{\mathrm{S1},r}(\mathbf{U}^{r},\mathbf{p}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})

\displaystyle+T^{\mathrm{S2},r}(\mathbf{U}^{r},\mathbf{p}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\beta}^{r}}[T^{\mathrm{S3},r}(\mathbf{U}^{r},\mathbf{p}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})].

(16)

II-B
2
Energy Consumption

Each client incurs energy consumption through four processes: model splitting, client-side model FP, intermediate output transmission, and client-side model BP.
We do not consider the energy consumption at the central server due to its continuous power supply.

The energy consumption of client
n\in\mathcal{N}
for model splitting in the
r
-th training round is given by

\displaystyle E_{n}^{\mathrm{MS},r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})=\frac{(\bm{\lambda}^{r-1}-\bm{\lambda}^{r})^{\intercal}\bm{\psi}p_{n}^{r}}{c_{n}^{\mathrm{UP},r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r})}.

(17)

The energy consumption of client
n\in\mathcal{N}
for client-side FP in the
r
-th training round is the energy required for client
n
to process all training samples, which is given by

\displaystyle E_{n}^{\mathrm{FP},r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})=D_{n}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{FP}}\phi\kappa^{\mathrm{c}}(f_{n})^{2},

(18)

where
\phi
is the energy consumption coefficient.
The energy consumption of client
n\in\mathcal{N}
for the intermediate output transmission in the
r
-th training round can be expressed as

\displaystyle E_{n}^{\mathrm{UP},r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})=p_{n}^{r}T_{n}^{\mathrm{UP},r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r}).

(19)

The expected energy consumption of client
n\in\mathcal{N}
for client-side model BP in the
r
-th training round is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\beta_{n}^{r}}[E_{n}^{\mathrm{CBP},r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\>\big(1-s_{n}^{r}(p_{n}^{r},\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r})\big)D_{n}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{BP}}\phi\kappa^{\mathrm{c}}(f_{n})^{2}.

(20)

The expected total energy consumption of client
n
in the
r
-th training round is obtained as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\beta_{n}^{r}}[E_{n}^{r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})]=E_{n}^{\mathrm{MS},r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})+E_{n}^{\mathrm{FP},r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})

\displaystyle+E_{n}^{\mathrm{UP},r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})+\mathbb{E}_{\beta_{n}^{r}}[E_{n}^{\mathrm{CBP},r}(\mathbf{u}_{n}^{r},p_{n}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r})].

(21)

III
Theoretical Analysis and Problem Formulation

In this section, we first characterize the impact of decision variables on the convergence rate of our proposed ASFL framework.
Then, we present the problem formulation.

III-A

Theoretical Analysis

Without loss of generality, we conduct analysis under non-convex loss functions.
We first present the following assumptions which are widely used in the literature (e.g.,
[
18
,
36
,
6
]
).

Assumption 1.

The loss function of each client
n\in\mathcal{N}
is continuously differentiable and
L
-smooth.
That is, for arbitrary two vectors
\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n}^{r}
, we have

F_{n}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r})\leq F_{n}(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n}^{r})+\langle\nabla F_{n}(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n}^{r}),\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r}-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n}^{r}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r}-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n}^{r}\|^{2}.

Assumption 2.

The variance of the local stochastic gradient of each client
n\in\mathcal{N}
is upper-bounded, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}_{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}\big[\|\nabla F_{n}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r};\bm{\xi}_{n})-\nabla F_{n}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r})\|^{2}\big]\leq\sigma_{F}^{2}
.

Assumption 3.

The expected square norm of the gradient of each client
n\in\mathcal{N}
is upper-bounded, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}_{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}[\|\nabla F_{n}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r};\bm{\xi}_{n})\|^{2}]\leq\psi_{F}^{2}
.

We introduce two lemmas which are widely used in the literature (e.g.,
[
18
,
36
]
) to facilitate our proof.

Lemma 1.

For arbitrary vector
\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r}
,
n\in\mathcal{N}
, we have
\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r}\|^{2}\leq N\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r}\|^{2}
.

Lemma 2.

For arbitrary two vectors
\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n}^{r}
,
n\in\mathcal{N}
, we have
\langle\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r},\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n}^{r}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}(\|\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r}\|^{2}+\|\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n}^{r}\|^{2}-\|\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r}-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n}^{r}\|^{2})
.

We denote the average client-side model and server-side model after model splitting at the beginning of the
r
-th training round as
\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r}
and
\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}
, respectively.
Now, we present the convergence rate of our proposed ASFL in Theorem 1.

Theorem 1.

Under Assumptions 1
-
3 and Lemmas 1
-
2,
the convergence rate of our proposed ASFL is bounded by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\|\nabla F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r})\|^{2}\leq\frac{2}{R}\big(F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{1})-F(\mathbf{w}^{\star})\big)+3(\sigma_{F}^{2}+\psi_{F}^{2})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\>\underbrace{\frac{2(L+2)}{NR}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\|\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r}-\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r}\|^{2}+(1-\beta_{n}^{r})^{2}\|\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}\|^{2})}_{\text{Average long-term model discrepancies}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\>2(L+2)\eta^{2}\psi_{F}^{2}.

(22)

Proof.

The model of client
n
at the beginning of the
(r+1)
-th training round is given by
\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r+1}=\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r}-\beta_{n}^{r}\eta\nabla F_{n}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r};\bm{\xi}_{n}^{r})
.
We can derive
\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r+1}
as follows:

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r+1}=\>

\displaystyle\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big(\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r}-\beta_{n}^{r}\eta\nabla F_{n}^{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r};\bm{\xi}_{n}^{r})\big);

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\big(\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}-\eta\nabla F_{n}^{\mathrm{s}}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r};\bm{\xi}_{n}^{r})\big)\Big]

\displaystyle=\>

\displaystyle\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r};\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}\Big]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\eta\nabla F_{n}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r};\bm{\xi}_{n}^{r}).

(23)

Based on eqn. (
III-A
), we expand
\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r+1})]
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r+1})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\>\mathbb{E}\Big[F\Big(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r}-\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r}+\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r};\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}\Big]

\displaystyle-\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\nabla F_{n}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r};\bm{\xi}_{n}^{r})\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{a})}{\leq}

\displaystyle\>\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r})]-\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\langle\nabla F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r}),\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r}-\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r};

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}\Big]+\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\nabla F_{n}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r};\bm{\xi}_{n}^{r})\Big\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle+\frac{L}{2}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r};\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}\Big]-\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r}

\displaystyle-\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\nabla F_{n}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r};\bm{\xi}_{n}^{r})\Big\|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{b})}{=}

\displaystyle\>\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r})]-\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\nabla F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r})\|^{2}+\Big\|\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r}-\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r};

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}\Big]+\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\nabla F_{n}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r};\bm{\xi}_{n}^{r})\Big\|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\nabla F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r})-\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r}+\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r};\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}\Big]

\displaystyle-\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\nabla F_{n}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r};\bm{\xi}_{n}^{r})\Big\|^{2}\Big]\Big)+\frac{L}{2}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r};

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}\Big]-\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r}-\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\nabla F_{n}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r};\bm{\xi}_{n}^{r})\Big\|^{2}\Big],

(24)

where inequality (a) results from Assumption 1.
Equality (b) is obtained by using Lemma 2.
For illustration simplicity, we define
A_{1}=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\nabla F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r})-\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r}+\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r};\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}\Big]-\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\nabla F_{n}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r};\bm{\xi}_{n}^{r})\Big\|^{2}\Big]

.

A_{1}
satisfies

\displaystyle A_{1}=

\displaystyle\>\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\nabla F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nabla F_{n}(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r};\xi_{n}^{r})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nabla F_{n}(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r};\xi_{n}^{r})-\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r}+\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r};

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}\Big]-\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\nabla F_{n}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r};\bm{\xi}_{n}^{r})\Big\|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{a})}{\leq}

\displaystyle\>3\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nabla F_{n}(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nabla F_{n}(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r};\xi_{n}^{r})\Big\|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle+3\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nabla F_{n}(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r};\xi_{n}^{r})\Big\|^{2}\Big]+3A_{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{b})}{\leq}

\displaystyle\>\frac{3}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\nabla F_{n}(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r})-\nabla F_{n}(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r};\xi_{n}^{r})\Big\|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle+\frac{3}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\nabla F_{n}(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r};\xi_{n}^{r})\Big\|^{2}\Big]+3A_{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{c})}{\leq}

\displaystyle\>3(\sigma_{F}^{2}+\psi_{F}^{2})+3A_{2},

(25)

where inequalities (a) and (b) result from Lemma 1.
Inequality (c) results from Assumptions 2 and 3.
Therefore,
\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r+1})]
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r+1})]\leq

\displaystyle\>\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r})]-\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r})\|^{2}+\frac{3}{2}(\sigma_{F}^{2}+\psi_{F}^{2})

\displaystyle+\frac{L+2}{2}A_{2}.

(26)

Then, we analyze
A_{2}
.
In particular, it satisfies

\displaystyle A_{2}=

\displaystyle\>\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\nabla F_{n}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r};\bm{\xi}_{n}^{r})+\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r};

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}\Big]-\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r};\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}\Big]\Big\|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{a})}{\leq}

\displaystyle\>2\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\nabla F_{n}(\mathbf{w}_{n}^{r};\bm{\xi}_{n}^{r})\Big\|^{2}\Big]+2\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r};

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}\Big]-\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r};\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}^{r}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}\Big]\Big\|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{b})}{\leq}

\displaystyle\>\frac{2\eta^{2}\psi_{F}^{2}}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\beta_{n}^{r})^{2}+\frac{2}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Big\|\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r}-\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r}\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Big\|\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}-\beta_{n}^{r}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}\Big\|^{2},

(27)

where inequality (a) is derived using Lemma 1.
Inequality (b) results from Lemma 1 and Assumption 3.
Therefore,
\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r+1})]
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r+1})]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\>\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r})]-\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r})\|^{2}+\frac{3}{2}(\sigma_{F}^{2}+\psi_{F}^{2})

\displaystyle+\frac{(L+2)\eta^{2}\psi_{F}^{2}}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\beta_{n}^{r})^{2}+\frac{L+2}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Big\|\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r}-\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r}\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{L+2}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Big\|\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}-\beta_{n}^{r}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}\Big\|^{2}.

(28)

To determine the convergence rate, we sum up both sides of inequality (
III-A
) for all
R
training rounds and multiply both sides by
\frac{2}{R}
.
Then, we rearrange the inequality above and obtain the convergence rate of our proposed ASFL as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\|\nabla F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{r})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{a})}{\leq}

\displaystyle\>\frac{2}{R}\big(F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{1})-F(\mathbf{w}^{\star})\big)+\frac{2(L+2)\eta^{2}\psi_{F}^{2}}{NR}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big(\beta_{n}^{r}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{2(L+2)}{NR}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Big\|\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r}-\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r}\Big\|^{2}+3(\sigma_{F}^{2}+\psi_{F}^{2})

\displaystyle+\frac{2(L+2)}{NR}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Big\|\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}-\beta_{n}^{r}\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{b})}{\leq}

\displaystyle\>\frac{2}{R}\big(F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{1})-F(\mathbf{w}^{\star})\big)+2(L+2)\eta^{2}\psi_{F}^{2}+3(\sigma_{F}^{2}+\psi_{F}^{2})

\displaystyle+\frac{2(L+2)}{NR}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Big\|\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r}-\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{c},r}\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{2(L+2)}{NR}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(1-\beta_{n}^{r})^{2}\Big\|\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}\Big\|^{2},

(29)

where inequality (a) is obtained by using
F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{R+1})\geq F(\mathbf{w}^{\star})

Inequality (b) results from the fact that

\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}=\mathbf{w}_{n,\bm{\lambda}^{r}}^{\mathrm{s},r}

This completes the proof of Theorem 1.
∎

Remark 1.

Theorem 1 suggests that the first two terms and the fourth term on the right-hand side of inequality (

\{\mathbf{U}^{r},\mathbf{p}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r}\}_{r\in\mathcal{R}}
.
Thus, we should optimize the decision variables
\{\mathbf{U}^{r},\mathbf{p}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r}\}_{r\in\mathcal{R}}
in order to minimize the average long-term model discrepancies (i.e., the third term on the right-hand side of inequality (

Recall from Section

that

is a binary random variable, which is equal to 1 with probability

Hence, we aim to minimize the expected average long-term model discrepancies by considering the randomness of
\beta_{n}^{r}
.
On the other hand, the decision variables
\{\mathbf{U}^{r},\mathbf{p}^{r},\bm{\lambda}^{r}\}_{r\in\mathcal{R}}
can affect the delay and energy consumption significantly.
Thus, we need to determine them properly to jointly improve the learning performance and efficiency of our proposed ASFL.

III-B

Problem Formulation

We aim to minimize the expected average long-term model discrepancies while guaranteeing the delay and energy consumption constraints.
We formulate the problem as follows:

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\minimize_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\mathbf{U}^{r},\mathbf{p}^{r},\\

(30b)

(30d)

\displaystyle\quad 0\leq p_{n}^{r}\leq P^{\mathrm{max}},\quad n\in\mathcal{N},r\in\mathcal{R},

(30e)

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{1}^{r}=1,\quad r\in\mathcal{R},

(30f)

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{m}^{r}\leq\lambda_{m-1}^{r},\quad m\in\mathcal{M}\backslash\{1\},r\in\mathcal{R},

(30g)

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{m}^{r}\in\{0,1\},\quad m\in\mathcal{M}\backslash\{1\},r\in\mathcal{R},

(30h)

\displaystyle\quad u_{n,k}^{r}\in\{0,1\},\quad n\in\mathcal{N},k\in\mathcal{K},r\in\mathcal{R}.

(30i)

Constraints (

The challenges of solving problem (

IV-A

Model Splitting Subproblem

We first fix the decision variables

IV-A
1
Online Optimization Algorithm

By fixing the decision variables
\{\mathbf{U}^{r},\mathbf{p}^{r}\}_{r\in\mathcal{R}}
and rearranging constraints (
30b
) and (
30c
), we have
g_{0}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})=\frac{1}{R}\sum\nolimits_{r=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\beta}^{r}}[T^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})]-\gamma\leq 0

,

Given
\{\mathbf{U}^{r},\mathbf{p}^{r}\}_{r\in\mathcal{R}}

30

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\minimize_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\bm{\lambda}^{r}\}_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\end{subarray}}}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{R}\sum\nolimits_{r=1}^{R}g_{\mathrm{obj}}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})

(31a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{subject\>to}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{R}\sum\nolimits_{r=1}^{R}g_{0}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})\leq 0,

(31b)

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{R}\sum\nolimits_{r=1}^{R}g_{n}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})\leq 0,\>\>\>n\leq\mathcal{N},

(31c)

\displaystyle\quad\mathrm{constraints}\>\>\textrm{(\ref{c8})},\>\textrm{(\ref{c7})},\>\mathrm{and}\>\textrm{(\ref{c2})}.

By using Lyapunov optimization

]

Let

We initialize

as
\mathbf{Q}^{0}=\mathbf{0}_{N+1}

In the

\displaystyle Q_{n}^{r+1}=\max\{\mu Q_{n}^{r}+(1-\mu)g_{n}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r}),0\},

\displaystyle\Delta^{r}=L(\mathbf{Q}^{r+1})-L(\mathbf{Q}^{r})=\frac{1}{2}(\|\mathbf{Q}^{r+1}\|^{2}-\|\mathbf{Q}^{r}\|^{2}),\quad r\in\mathcal{R}.

To jointly guarantee the stability of the virtual queue vector and optimize our objective, instead of solving problem (

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\minimize_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\lambda}^{r}\end{subarray}}}

\displaystyle\quad\Delta^{r}+Vg_{\mathrm{obj}}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})

(34a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{subject\>to}

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{1}^{r}=1,

(34b)

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{m}^{r}\leq\lambda_{m-1}^{r},\>\>m\in\mathcal{M}\backslash\{1\},

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{m}^{r}\in\{0,1\},\>\>m\in\mathcal{M}\backslash\{1\}.

Due to constraints (

Stability Analysis

We provide stability analysis of our proposed online optimization algorithm and characterize the impact of the tunable parameter
\mu
.
We introduce an assumption which is widely used in the literature (e.g.,
[
42
,
12

Assumption 4.

There exist two positive constants

and

such that for arbitrary decision vector
\bm{\lambda}^{r}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\beta}^{r}}[T^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})]\leq T^{\max}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\beta_{n}^{r}}[E_{n}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})]\leq E^{\max}
,
n\in\mathcal{N}
,

.

We present three lemmas which bound the constraints, value of the virtual queue, and drift-plus-penalty term, respectively.

Lemma 3.

The values of the square of constraints satisfy
(g_{0}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r}))^{2}=\max\{\gamma^{2},(T^{\max}-\gamma)^{2}\}=G_{1}
and
(g_{n}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r}))^{2}=\max\{\delta^{2},(E^{\max}-\delta)^{2}\}=G_{2}
,
n\in\mathcal{N}
,
r\in\mathcal{R}
.

Proof.

Based on Assumption 4, we have

\displaystyle\lvert g_{0}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})\rvert=\max\{\gamma,T^{\max}-\gamma\}.

(35)

Thus, the following equality always hold:

\displaystyle\big(g_{0}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})\big)^{2}=\max\{\gamma^{2},(T^{\max}-\gamma)^{2}\}.

(36)

We use similar steps to obtain
\big(g_{n}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})\big)^{2}=\max\{\delta^{2},(E^{\max}-\delta)^{2}\}
,
n\in\mathcal{N}
,
r\in\mathcal{R}
.
∎

Lemma 4.

The values of virtual queues are bounded by

Q_{0}^{r}\leq\sqrt{G_{1}}
and
Q_{n}^{r}\leq\sqrt{G_{2}}
,
n\in\mathcal{N}
,
r\in\mathcal{R}
.

Proof.

We use induction for our proof.
First, we have
Q_{1}^{1}=0<\sqrt{G_{1}}
.
Then, we suppose
Q_{0}^{r}\leq\sqrt{G_{1}}
.

Q_{1}^{r+1}
satisfies

\displaystyle Q_{1}^{r+1}\overset{(\text{a})}{\leq}

\displaystyle\>\lvert\mu Q_{0}^{r}+(1-\mu)g_{0}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})\rvert

\displaystyle\overset{(\text{b})}{\leq}

\displaystyle\>\mu Q_{0}^{r}+(1-\mu)\lvert g_{0}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})\rvert

\displaystyle\overset{(\text{c})}{\leq}

\displaystyle\>\mu\sqrt{G_{1}}+(1-\mu)\sqrt{G_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\>\sqrt{G_{1}},

(37)

where inequalities (
a
) and (
b
) hold due to eqn. (
32
) and the triangle inequality, respectively.
Inequality (

) results from Assumption 4 and Lemma 3.
We use similar steps to derive
Q_{n}^{r}\leq\sqrt{G_{2}}
,
n\in\mathcal{N}
,
r\in\mathcal{R}
.
∎

Lemma 5.

The drift-plus-penalty term is bounded by

\displaystyle\Delta^{r}+Vg_{\mathrm{obj}}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\>\frac{1}{2}(1-\mu)^{2}(G_{1}+NG_{2})+\mu(1-\mu)(Q_{0}^{r}g_{0}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})

\displaystyle\>+\sum_{n=1}^{N}Q_{n}^{r}g_{n}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r}))+Vg_{\mathrm{obj}}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r}),\quad r\in\mathcal{R}.

(38)

Proof.

The Lyapunov drift satisfies

\displaystyle\Delta^{r}=\frac{1}{2}\big((Q_{1}^{r+1})^{2}-(Q_{0}^{r})^{2}\big)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big((Q_{n}^{r+1})^{2}-(Q_{n}^{r})^{2}\big).

(39)

In particular,

\displaystyle(Q_{1}^{r+1})^{2}-(Q_{0}^{r})^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(\text{a})}{=}

\displaystyle\>\frac{1}{2}(\mu^{2}-1)(Q_{0}^{r})^{2}+\mu(1-\mu)Q_{0}^{r}g_{0}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(1-\mu)^{2}(g_{0}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r}))^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(\text{b})}{\leq}

\displaystyle\>\mu(1-\mu)Q_{0}^{r}g_{0}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})+\frac{1}{2}(1-\mu)^{2}G_{1},

(40)

where equality (a) results from eqn. (
32
).
Inequality (b) results from Lemma 3 and the fact that
\frac{1}{2}(\mu^{2}-1)(Q_{0}^{r})^{2}\leq 0
.
Then, we use similar steps to bound
(Q_{n}^{r+1})^{2}-(Q_{n}^{r})^{2}
,
n\in\mathcal{N}
.
Finally, we substitute these two terms into the drift-plus-penalty term and obtain the result.
∎

Based on Lemma 5, we present an additional assumption which is widely used in the literature (e.g.

Assumption 5.

For any

Theorem 2.

Based on Lemma 4, the constraint violation is bounded by

\displaystyle\!\!\!\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}g_{n}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})\leq\begin{cases}\big(1+\frac{1}{(1-\mu)R}\big)\sqrt{G_{1}},&\text{if $n=0$},\\

\displaystyle(1-\mu)g_{0}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})\leq Q_{1}^{r+1}-Q_{0}^{r}+(1-\mu)Q_{0}^{r}.

Theorem 3.

Based on Assumption 5 and Lemmas 3

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R}\sum\nolimits_{r=1}^{R}\big(g_{\mathrm{obj}}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})-g_{\mathrm{obj}}^{\star}\big)\leq\frac{W+C}{V},

Based on Assumption 5, we have

\displaystyle L(\mathbf{Q}^{r+1})-L(\mathbf{Q}^{r})+Vg_{\mathrm{obj}}^{r}(\bm{\lambda}^{r})

\displaystyle\>\frac{1}{2}(1-\mu)^{2}(G_{1}+NG_{2})+\mu(1-\mu)(Q_{0}^{r}g_{1}^{\star}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}Q_{n}^{r}g_{n}^{\star})

Remark 2.

Theorems 2 and 3 suggest that as
R
approaches infinity, the constraint violation is bounded by constant values, which indicates the stability of our proposed algorithm.
The performance gap is also bounded by a constant value.
In addition, there is a trade-off between the performance gap and constraint violation.
That is, as
\mu
increases, the performance gap decreases (i.e., the convergence rate is improved) while the constraint violation increases (i.e., the total delay and energy consumption on training increases) and vice versa.
We will show in Section

properly can balance the learning performance and efficiency of our proposed ASFL.

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\minimize_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{p}^{r},\gamma_{1}^{r},\gamma_{2}^{r},\gamma_{3}^{r},\\

\displaystyle\quad g_{\mathrm{obj}}^{r}(\mathbf{p}^{r})

(50a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{subject\>to}

\displaystyle\quad T_{n}^{\mathrm{S1},r}(p_{n}^{r})\leq\gamma_{1}^{r},\quad n\in\mathcal{N},

IV-D

Computation Complexity Analysis

To determine the computation complexity of our proposed OOE-BCD algorithm in each training round, we analyze each of the three subproblems.
For the model splitting subproblem, since there are at most
M
feasible solutions, the computation complexity is linear in the number of model splitting decisions, which is
\mathcal{O}(M)
.
For the RB allocation subproblem, the computation complexity is
\mathcal{O}(N^{K})
.
For the transmit power allocation subproblem, since we need to determine the transmit power for each client, the computation complexity is
\mathcal{O}(N\log_{2}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{p}}}))
.
Therefore, the overall computation complexity of our proposed OOE-BCD algorithm in one training round is
\mathcal{O}(\log_{2}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{o}}})(M+N^{K}+N\log_{2}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{p}}}))
.

\chi_{n}^{r}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1)

The CPU frequency of each client
n\in\mathcal{N}

24

.

A list of key simulation parameters is presented in Table

We use CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100
[

We adopt VGG-19
[
32

and ResNet-50
[
11
]

The model is allowed to be split only before the convolutional layer or the fully-connected layer.
The number of training rounds
R
, the learning rate
\eta

To study the effect of non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) data partitioning, similar to other works in FL
[
1
,
17
]
, we use the Dirichlet distribution
\mathrm{Dir}(\rho)
with parameter
\rho
to distribute the data with different labels across clients.
Let
N_{\mathrm{c}}
denote the number of classes of a dataset.
The Dirichlet distribution
\text{Dir}(\rho)
generates the probability vector
\mathbf{q}_{n}=(q^{1}_{n},q^{2}_{n},\ldots,q^{N_{c}}_{n})
, where
q^{i}_{n}
represents the fraction of data samples from class
i
assigned to client
n
, with
\sum_{n=1}^{N_{c}}q^{i}_{n}=1
.
The probability density function of the Dirichlet distribution is given by

f(\mathbf{q}_{n};\rho)=\frac{\Gamma(N_{c}\rho)}{\left(\Gamma(\rho)\right)^{N_{c}}}\prod_{n=1}^{N_{c}}(q^{i}_{n})^{\rho-1}
,
where
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the gamma function.
When
\rho
decreases, the data heterogeneity across clients increases and vice versa.
Unless stated otherwise, we set
N=10
and
\rho=10
.
To ensure fairness, we allocate the same number of training samples to each client’s local dataset.
We compare the performance of our proposed ASFL with the following five baseline schemes:

•

[
26
]

We use the same RB and transmit power allocation decisions as ASFL.

•

SL
[
35
]

•

SFL
[
33

•

[
41

•

EPSL
[
24
]

We introduce evaluation metrics for our experiments as follows:

•

Average testing accuracy:
This is the ratio of the total number of correct predictions to the number of testing samples.
It quantifies how well the trained model performs on downstream tasks.

•

Total delay on training:
This is the end-to-end model training latency.

•

Total energy consumption on training:
This metric captures the total communication and computation energy consumption of all clients.
It corresponds to the total battery usage of end devices.

•

Average packet error rate:
This is the ratio of the number of packets with errors to the total number of transmitted packets.
It captures the communication reliability over wireless links, which strongly affects the success rate of model update.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed an ASFL framework over wireless networks.
It enables adaptive model splitting and provides efficient resource allocation during training.
We theoretically analyzed the convergence rate of our proposed ASFL framework.
We designed an OOE-BCD algorithm to adaptively determine the model splitting and resource allocation decisions.
Experimental results showed that when compared with five baseline schemes, our proposed ASFL framework converged faster and reduced the total delay and energy consumption on training by up to 75% and 80%, respectively.
We also showed the robustness of our proposed algorithm to different degrees of data heterogeneity across clients’ local datasets.
For future work, we plan to design ASFL with heterogeneous model splitting to further improve the learning efficiency under heterogeneous hardware configurations across clients.
