Nematicidal potential of Carissa carandas seed extract against Meloidogyne incognita and identification of its bioactive compounds
Authors: V Basavaraj, M S Sharada, M R Sampathkumar, H M Mahesh, K N Amruthesh, A Shanthi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlant-parasitic nematodes, especially root-knot nematodes (RKNs) of the genus Meloidogyne, are major agricultural pests, causing crop yield losses of up to 80% in vegetables. Rapid reproduction intensifies soil infestation, and reliance on synthetic nematicides is declining due to environmental and health risks, necessitating sustainable alternatives. This study investigates the nematicidal potential of Carissa carandas L. methanolic seed extract, known for bioactive compounds, against Meloidogyne incognita juveniles (J2) and egg masses. In vitro assays at concentrations of 250, 500, and 1000 ppm demonstrated that the extract significantly increased J2 mortality and inhibited egg hatching in a concentration- and time-dependent manner. At 1000 ppm, the extract caused pronounced nematode anatomical disruptions, with Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) revealing severe structural changes in egg masses. An in-vivo pot experiment assessed C. carandas seed powder against M. incognita on muskmelon cv. ‘Arkasiri’ in a glasshouse setting, comparing its efficacy with Fluopyram, a commercial nematicide. Both treatments, applied 15- and 30-days post-inoculation, markedly improved plant growth parameters and reduced nematode indicators such as root galls and soil nematode populations. At 30 days, C. carandas treatment achieved a 94.26% reduction in root galls, while Fluopyram achieved 96.78%, with the latter showing a 99.10% decrease in nematode populations. GC–MS analysis of the extract identified bioactive compounds, including fatty acids, likely responsible for nematicidal effects. These results indicate that C. carandas seed extract offers a promising, eco-friendly approach for managing M. incognita, supporting plant-based nematicidal development for integrated pest management.Keywords: Nematicidal activity, Seed extract, Juvenile mortality, Egg-hatching, Ultrastructural changes, Bioactive compoundsSubject terms: Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Ecology, Ecology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Plant-parasitic nematodes (PPNs) are widely recognized as hidden adversaries in agriculture, capable of reducing crop yields by up to 80% in vegetable production1. Due to the similarities between symptoms induced by PPNs and those caused by fungal infections, water stress, or physiological disorders, PPNs have earned the moniker “clandestine foe” of farmers2. Among PPNs, root-knot nematodes (RKNs), particularly of the Meloidogyne genus, are the most prevalent and destructive, affecting over 3000 plant species worldwide and resulting in estimated crop losses of nearly USD 100 billion annually3–5. The damage caused by Meloidogyne spp. arises from their unique feeding strategy, which involves the formation of specialized feeding sites (giant cells) in plant roots, leading to impaired nutrient uptake and reduced plant growth6. These structural changes hinder water and nutrient uptake, leading to stunted growth, reduced vigor, and, ultimately, decreased crop productivity7. Additionally, Meloidogyne species reproduce rapidly, completing multiple generations in one growing season, which exacerbates the infestation level in agricultural soils over time.

Muskmelon (Cucumis melo L.) is an economically important cucurbitaceous crop widely cultivated for its nutritional value, taste, and commercial significance8. However, muskmelon production is severely threatened by RKNs, particularly M. incognita, M. javanica, and M. arenaria, which induce root galling and significantly reduce yields9. To combat RKNs, various management strategies have been developed, including chemical nematicides, crop rotation, biological control, and integrated pest management10,11. However, the detrimental effects of synthetic nematicides on the environment, human health, and non-target organisms have led to the restriction or banning of many of these chemicals12, spurring interest in sustainable alternatives.

Plant-based nematicides, derived from the natural defense mechanisms of plants, have shown considerable promise as an environmentally friendly alternative. These nematicides, sourced from plant leaves, roots, seeds, and fruits, exhibit various actions against nematodes, including reduced reproduction, disrupted feeding behavior, and increased mortality rates13. With their advantages of biodegradability, minimal environmental impact, and compatibility with organic farming, plant-based nematicides are gaining traction as an integral component of integrated pest management strategies14,15. Nevertheless, while substantial attention has been given to plant extracts in nematicidal research, seeds have been relatively underexplored12.

Carissa carandas L., a member of the Apocynaceae family known as Karonda, is a minor fruit-bearing plant commonly found in tropical and subtropical regions of India16. This plant is reputed for its broad spectrum of bioactive compounds, including terpenoids, steroids, saponins, and flavonoids, which contribute to its diverse pharmacological properties, such as antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and anthelmintic activities17–21. However, research on the nematicidal efficacy of C. carandas seed extract, particularly against RKN, remains limited.

To address this gap, the current study investigates the potential of C. carandas seed extract as a nematicidal agent. This research encompasses an in-vitro study of the seed extract’s effects on the hatching and mortality rates of M. incognita juveniles (J2) and egg masses, alongside ultrastructural analysis of treated egg masses via Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM). Additionally, an in-vivo pot culture experiment is conducted to assess the efficacy of C. carandas seed powder in managing M. incognita in muskmelon (cv. ‘Arkasiri’) under glasshouse conditions. The present study focuses on evaluating the nematicidal activity of Carissa carandas seed extract under in vitro conditions as a preliminary step toward the development of eco-friendly nematode management strategies.

Results

This study evaluated the effects of various concentrations of methanolic seed extract from C. carandas on the hatching and mortality of M. incognita J2 juveniles over different incubation periods, comparing treatments of 250, 500, and 1000 ppm to a distilled water control. The extract exhibited a concentration-dependent impact on juvenile mortality, with significantly higher death rates across all concentrations over a 72-h exposure, as indicated by ANOVA and Tukey’s post hoc tests (p < 0.05). Additionally, the efficacy of C. carandas seed powder and the nematicide Fluopyram on muskmelon growth and nematode suppression was assessed at 15- and 30-day post-inoculation (DPI). Both treatments significantly improved plant growth (measured by shoot and root lengths and weights) and reduced nematode reproduction metrics, including root gall counts, female nematodes, egg masses, and soil nematode populations, as shown in Figs. 3 and 4.

Juvenile mortality

In this experiment, J2 mortality of Meloidogyne incognita induced by the methanolic seed extract of Carissa carandas was evaluated using the direct-contact method (Table 1). The results revealed significant differences among the various concentrations tested, indicating that both extract concentration and exposure time had a pronounced effect on juvenile mortality. No mortality was observed in the distilled water control at 12, 24, and 48 h, while only negligible mortality (0.66 ± 0.33) was recorded at 72 h. All tested concentrations of the extract exhibited nematicidal activity, with mortality increasing progressively with both concentration and duration of exposure. At 12 h, mortality ranged from 7.33% at 250 ppm to 64.66% at 1000 ppm. This trend continued at 24 and 48 h, where mortality increased significantly across treatments. The 1000-ppm concentration was particularly effective, causing complete (100%) mortality within 48 h, which remained unchanged at 72 h. Similarly, the 500-ppm treatment showed a steady increase in mortality from 22.44% at 12 h to 84.44% at 72 h, while the 250-ppm treatment exhibited comparatively lower but consistent toxicity, reaching 40.22% at 72 h.

Egg hatching inhibition

The effect of the methanolic seed extract of Carissa carandas on egg hatching of Meloidogyne incognita was evaluated using a direct-contact bioassay (Table 2). The results revealed significant differences among treatments, indicating that both extract concentration and exposure time markedly influenced egg hatching. In the control (distilled water), egg hatching increased progressively with time, reaching 211.00 ± 3.60 (93.77%) at 72 h. All tested concentrations of the extract significantly inhibited egg hatching compared to the control. The degree of inhibition increased with increasing concentration of the extract. At 12 h, the number of hatched juveniles was considerably reduced from 22.00 ± 1.52 at 250 ppm to 3.33 ± 0.33 at 1000 ppm, corresponding to inhibition levels of 49.55% and 92.28%, respectively. This trend continued at 24, 48, and 72 h, with higher concentrations consistently showing greater inhibition of egg hatching. The 1000-ppm concentration was the most effective treatment, showing maximum inhibition of 95.24% at 72 h, followed by 48 and 24 h at the same concentration. The 500-ppm treatment also exhibited substantial inhibition, ranging from 78.35% at 12 h to 67.24% at 72 h. In contrast, the 250-ppm treatment showed comparatively lower inhibition, indicating a dose-dependent response. The chemical nematicide Fluopyram exhibited the highest efficacy, resulting in 96.08% inhibition at 12 h and complete (100%) inhibition of egg hatching from 24 h onwards. The critical difference (C.D.) values further confirm significant variation among treatments at all time intervals.

Microscopic observations of unhatched eggs revealed internal disintegration of embryos in treated samples (Fig. 1, 04), indicating the destructive effect of the extract on embryonic development. In-vivo pot culture experiment

At 15 DPI, plants treated with C. carandas seed powder showed enhanced shoot length (24.33 cm) and root length (16.27 cm), representing increases of 44.63% and 43.02%, respectively, over the inoculated control. Fluopyram had a comparable effect, resulting in shoot and root lengths of 24.53 cm and 18.07 cm, corresponding to 45.08% and 48.83% increases. Both treatments significantly boosted shoot weights (4.40 g with C. carandas and 4.49 g with Fluopyram) while markedly reducing root galls by 79.4% and 84.85% for C. carandas and Fluopyram, respectively. Additionally, Fluopyram eliminated egg masses and achieved the lowest nematode soil population (14.67 J2 per 250 g of soil), with a reproduction factor (Rf) of 0.058, compared to an Rf of 0.108 for C. carandas (Table 5; Figs. 2 and 3). After 30 DPI, both C. carandas seed powder and Fluopyram continued to promote plant growth and limit nematode reproduction. The shoot lengths increased to 85.57 cm for C. carandas and 85.97 cm for Fluopyram, reflecting growth improvements of 35.87% and 36.17%, respectively, compared to the control. Root lengths also improved to 24.47 cm for C. carandas and 24.7 cm for Fluopyram. Both treatments further reduced root galls, achieving reductions of 94.26% and 96.78% for C. carandas and Fluopyram, respectively. The severity of infection was assessed using the Root Knot Index (RKI), which is a scale used to quantify gall formation caused by nematodes. The lowest in the treated was found at 1.33 after 30 DPI. Fluopyram was most effective in lowering the final nematode population, achieving a 99.10% reduction over the inoculated control, with an Rf of 0.050 (Table 6; Figs. 2 and 4).

Ultrastructural study of M. incognita egg mass

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) analysis revealed significant ultrastructural changes in the egg masses of M. incognita treated with 1000 ppm of C. carandas methanolic seed extract. The untreated control egg masses displayed typical, intact surface morphology with smooth, regular contours and well-defined eggs encapsulated within the gelatinous matrix (Fig. 5. 01 and 02). In contrast, egg masses treated with the methanolic seed extract exhibited several notable alterations.

SEM micrographs of M. incognita egg mass (Control and C. carandas treated). (01-Gelatinous matrix of controlled egg mass showing smooth and intact surface, 02-A single egg of controlled egg mass showing typical structure, 03-Egg mass treated in C. carandas showing disruption gelatinous matrix and surface area, 04-Eggs showing collapsed structure, 05-Egg mass treated in C. carandas showing cracks and fissures, 06-Egg mass treated in C. carandas showing the shrinkage of eggshell, 07-Leakage of protoplasmic contents from the egg).

The surface of treated egg masses showed substantial disruption compared to the smooth surface of the control (Fig. 5. 03). The integrity of the gelatinous matrix appeared compromised, with noticeable cracks and fissures (Fig. 5. 05). Individual eggs within the treated egg mass exhibited deformation, with some eggs showing collapsed structures and irregular shapes (Fig. 5. 04). This deformation suggests potential eggshell damage, likely caused by interaction with the methanolic seed extract. The penetration of the seed extract into the egg mass resulted in structural changes in the eggshell, leading to its shrinkage (Fig. 5. 06), leakage of protoplasmic contents (Fig. 5. 07), and ultimately the death of the eggs. The gelatinous matrix surrounding the eggs in treated samples showed signs of disintegration (Fig. 5. 03). This disintegration may facilitate increased exposure of the eggs to the methanolic seed extract, enhancing the efficacy of the treatment.

Characterization of bioactive compounds through GC–MS

The methanolic seed extract with high nematicidal activity against M. incognita, upon column fractionation over silica gel, yielded 30 fractions. Elution with ethyl acetate and methanol produced the active fraction (23rd fraction), which exhibited high juvenile mortality of M. incognita. GC–MS analysis of the bioactive fraction confirmed the presence of eight bioactive compounds with different retention times (RT). The compounds identified in the bioactive fraction of the seed extract by GC–MS analysis are listed in order of their retention time (RT), molecular weight (MW), molecular formula (MF), percent probability (P), and structure (Table 6). Discussion

The genus Meloidogyne, known as root-knot nematodes, are polyphagous plant parasites that cause root deformations called galls or root knots, leading to reduced agricultural yields and significant economic losses22. Plants produce various bioactive secondary metabolites, such as alkaloids, saponins, tannins, flavonoids, glycosides, and fatty acids, which play a crucial role in defense against pathogens. When botanicals decompose, they release these metabolites and other organic acids into the soil23. These compounds can be toxic to nematodes, disrupting their normal physiological processes and leading to their death. Typically, these chemicals inhibit egg hatching and increase juvenile mortality.

In this study, the nematicidal effect of the methanolic seed extract of C. carandas on M. incognita was evaluated through in-vitro trials at three concentrations (250, 500, and 1000 ppm). Among the tested concentrations, 1000 ppm was the most effective in reducing egg hatching and increasing juvenile mortality. The chemical nematicide Fluopyram showed higher efficacy, achieving 100% mortality within 24 h, and served as a standard for comparison with the plant extract treatments. These findings are consistent with those of Asif et al.24,25 and Mukhtar et al.26. Similar results were observed by Danahap et al.27, who found 100% mortality after 24 h of exposure to an aqueous extract and 100% mortality after 8 h of exposure to a methanolic extract of Azadirachta indica seeds at the highest concentration of 300 mg/ml.

The in-vitro trials indicate that the C. carandas seed extract has a high nemato-toxic activity against M. incognita second-stage juveniles and eggs after 48 h of exposure, with efficacy directly related to the concentration of the extract. Higher doses resulted in better efficacy, with LC50 values for 48 and 72-h exposure periods being lower than other treatments. Similar results were reported by Agbenin et al.28 and Khan et al.29. The nematicidal potential of the methanolic seed extract is likely due to the presence of certain nemato-toxins or phytochemicals soluble in the solvent. Previous studies have shown that many plants and their derived phytochemicals possess nematicidal potential against a broad range of phytoparasitic nematodes, including root-knot nematode M. incognita30,31. Similar results have been reported using seed extracts for managing root-knot nematodes by Bello et al.32, Gravanis et al.33, Kumar34, Oluwatayo et al.35, Nengroo et al.36, and Rocha et al.12.

M. incognita juveniles exhibited significant anatomical changes, such as large vacuole formation, after 48 h of exposure to the methanolic seed extract of C. carandas. Similar observations were recorded in the eggs, where embryo disintegration was noted. This disruption likely involves interference with embryogenesis, leading to the failure of the eggs to hatch. This alteration is potentially due to the presence of toxic compounds capable of crossing the nematode cuticle and interfering with vital processes, including enzymatic, nervous, and respiratory systems. The characteristic extended body shape of the dead nematodes indicates severe internal disruption, likely affecting essential biological processes. This disruption may involve interference with cellular respiration, enzyme function, or other critical metabolic pathways, leading to nematode mortality. Alternatively, juvenile death might be linked to dehydration caused by the osmotic potential from the high concentration of the seed extract, as reported by Rocha et al.12.

The in-vivo pot culture experiment assessed the efficacy of C. carandas seed powder and Fluopyram in enhancing muskmelon growth and reducing M. incognita populations at 15- and 30-day post-inoculation (DPI), as presented in Tables 4 and 5. The chemical nematicide Fluopyram showed higher efficacy, achieving 100% mortality within 24 h, and served as a standard for comparison with the plant extract treatments. Both treatments significantly affected plant growth parameters, including shoot and root lengths, shoot and root weights, and key nematode reproduction indicators such as root gall count, female nematode numbers, egg masses, and soil nematode populations. Similar results were observed in previous field studies, where natural plant extracts demonstrated efficacy against nematodes. For example, finely chopped leaves of Brassica oleracea effectively reduced nematode populations when incorporated into the soil at high application rates37. Other studies have demonstrated the benefits of various plant extracts, such as Sodom apple fruit, neem, and sesame oil, in suppressing nematode populations and promoting plant growth38,39 (Table 6).

Specifically, at 15 DPI, C. carandas treatment increased shoot and root lengths by 44.63% and 43.02%, respectively, with Fluopyram achieving similar gains of 45.08% in shoot length and 48.83% in root length. Both treatments increased shoot weights significantly. This supports the findings by Udo et al.40, who noted that combining botanical extracts with microbial agents effectively suppressed nematode populations and enhanced plant growth. After 30 DPI, both treatments continued to positively influence growth and nematode suppression. Similar outcomes were observed with Tithonia diversifolia residues and extracts, which suppressed M. incognita egg and juvenile numbers in yams41. Moreover, natural compounds such as those in Lantana camara40 and Cucumis spp.42 have demonstrated nematicidal potential comparable to chemical nematicides, which aligns with Fluopyram’s high efficacy noted in this experiment.

The antagonistic effects of C. carandas methanolic seed extract on M. incognita egg masses were examined using Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) analysis. The results revealed significant ultrastructural changes in treated egg masses compared to untreated controls, highlighting the extract’s efficacy in disrupting the developmental integrity of root-knot nematode eggs. Untreated control egg masses of M. incognita displayed intact surface morphology with smooth, regular contours and well-defined eggs encapsulated within the gelatinous matrix. This typical morphology indicated a healthy and protected state, essential for the survival and hatching of nematode eggs43–45.

In contrast, the treated egg masses exhibited substantial surface disruption, with cracks and fissures compromising the integrity of the gelatinous matrix. This damage indicates the seed extract’s ability to penetrate and alter the structural components of M. incognita egg masses. Individual eggs’ deformation and irregular shapes within the treated egg masses further suggest a direct impact on the eggshells. The observed shrinkage of the eggshells and the leakage of protoplasmic contents imply that the methanolic seed extract of C. carandas disrupts the eggshell structure, leading to the collapse and eventual death of the eggs. Similar results were observed in eggs treated with biocontrol agents45,46. The compromised eggshell integrity likely results from the interaction between the bioactive compounds in the C. carandas seed extract and the eggshell components, causing physical and possibly chemical damage.

The disintegration of the gelatinous matrix surrounding the eggs in treated samples may play a crucial role in enhancing the efficacy of the treatment. By breaking down this protective layer, the extract likely gains increased access to the eggs, facilitating a more profound impact on their viability. This enhanced exposure could be crucial to the observed ultrastructural damage and egg mortality. These findings align with previous research on the nematicidal properties of various biocontrol agents and plant extracts45–47, which have demonstrated various mechanisms of action, including disruption of egg and juvenile structures, inhibition of egg hatching, and increased mortality rates. These results suggest that C. carandas methanolic seed extract holds promise as a nematicidal agent against M. incognita, offering a potential alternative to chemical nematicides.

Literature has documented the antioxidant48, anti-cancer49, antidiabetic19, antimalarial50, anthelmintic20, and antimicrobial51 activities of C. carandas. This study, however, is the first to report its nematicidal activity. The biological activity is supported by studies on the plant’s chemical profile through GC–MS analysis.

The nematicidal properties of the C. carandas seed extract can be attributed to its high content of fatty acids and fatty acid methyl esters, such as undecanoic acid, n-hexadecanoic acid, hexadecanoic acid methyl ester, oleic acid, and 9-octadecenoic acid (Z)-methyl ester. These compounds have been previously reported to possess antimicrobial, antioxidant, pesticide, antifungal, and nematicidal activities30,46,52–56. Similar bioactive compounds with antibacterial activity were reported in Garcinia kola seeds by Seanego and Ndip57. The presence of fatty acids and fatty acid esters was previously reported in C. carandas by Naim et al.58. Fatty acids and their methyl esters are used as surfactants and have activity against insect larvae and nematodes59. Various fatty acids and their derivatives have been employed to manage different nematodes in in-vitro and in-vivo conditions, as previously reported by Davis et al.60, Fabiyi et al.61, Feitelson and Dullum62, and Morgunov et al.63. Vrain64 also reported that twelve fatty acids (C3–C18) and their derivatives had significant mortality activity toward second-stage juveniles of M. hapla in in-vitro and greenhouse trials.

Certain microorganisms, like fungi, also produce fatty acids and fatty acid methyl esters with nematicidal activity30,65,66. Additionally, Pineda-Alegria et al.67 confirmed the effectiveness of fatty acid esters on Haemonchus contortus, a gastrointestinal nematode of ruminant animals, reporting that pentadecanoic acid, palmitic acid, stearic acid, and linoleic acid significantly inhibited egg hatching and increased larval mortality at concentrations of 1.25–20 mg/ml.

Conclusion

The present study highlights the potent nematicidal activity of C. carandas methanolic seed extract against M. incognita, demonstrating its efficacy in inhibiting egg hatching and increasing juvenile mortality in a concentration-dependent manner. The in-vitro and in-vivo experiments confirmed the extract’s effectiveness in suppressing nematode populations while promoting plant growth in muskmelon. SEM analysis further revealed ultrastructural damage in nematode eggs, indicating the extract’s ability to compromise egg viability. The nematicidal activity is attributed to bioactive compounds, particularly fatty acids, and their methyl esters, known for their pesticidal properties. These findings suggest that C. carandas seed extract has the potential to be developed as a natural alternative to chemical nematicides, contributing to sustainable nematode management in agriculture. Further validation through greenhouse and field trials is necessary to confirm the efficacy of the extract under natural conditions.

Materials and methods

Collection and identification of C. carandas

Matured and ripened fruits of C. carandas were collected from the Gopinatham forest region (12.0524° N, 77.6987° E), Chamarajanagara district, Karnataka state, India. The plant specimen was identified by Dr. K. N. Amruthesh, Professor, Department of Studies in Botany, University of Mysore, and authenticated with a voucher specimen number of ‘UOMBOT24CC256’ at the Herbarium of the Department of Studies in Botany, Manasagangotri, University of Mysore, Mysuru.

Preparation and extraction of C. carandas

The collected fruits of C. carandas were cut, and the seeds were separated and washed thoroughly with sterile distilled water, air-dried under shade for two weeks, and pulverized into fine powder using a blender. The powder was then sieved using a sieve mesh to obtain a fine powder and remove any unwanted debris. The powdered seed material was then kept in a clean and dry bag for further use. Fifty grams (50 g) of powdered seeds of C. carandas were extracted using the Soxhlet apparatus in methanol for 12 h at a temperature of 40 °C to obtain the methanol extract. Methanol was used because it has the ability to extract both polar and non-polar constituents in the seeds. The solvent was regularly changed until no coloration was observed, and the extract was concentrated using a rotary evaporator. The resulting extract was stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C until use68. Solutions of different concentrations of seed extracts were considered as treatments. Various concentrations (250, 500, and 1000 ppm) of seed extracts were prepared by diluting the stock solution with the required amount of double-distilled water.

Nematode inoculum

Egg masses and second-stage juveniles (J2) in this experiment were randomly collected from a previously characterized pure culture of M. incognita, maintained and raised on tomato (cv. AMBER) plants at the glasshouse of the Department of Studies in Botany, Manasagangotri, University of Mysore, Mysuru. The experiment was conducted under glasshouse conditions with a temperature of 25 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 60–70%, and a natural photoperiod (approximately 12 h light/12 h dark). Plants were watered regularly to maintain optimal soil moisture, and standard agronomic practices were followed throughout the experiment69.

Mortality study

The present study was conducted at the Department of Studies in Botany, University of Mysore, Manasagangotri, Mysuru, Karnataka, 570006 India. The tomato plants inoculated with M. incognita were uprooted from the soil, and the root system was washed gently with running tap water to remove adhering soil. The egg mass of M. incognita was gently picked using forceps under a stereo zoom-microscope. Egg masses were incubated for 48 h by the modified Baermann funnel method to obtain infective second-stage juveniles. The population density of J2 was calculated from 5 replications of one ml aliquots of inoculum suspension. Freshly hatched 150 s-stage juveniles (48 h old) were taken in 2.5 cm diameter petri plates containing 5 ml of each treatment solution. J2s kept in distilled water were treated as a control. Plates were covered with a lid and incubated at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) during the experiment period. Fluopyram was used as a positive control. It was applied at a concentration of 250 µg/ml, in accordance with the recommended application guidelines. The Experiment was conducted in triplicate, and each treatment was replicated three times. Data on mortality were recorded after 12, 24, 48, and 72 h after incubation70.

Mortality of the J2 was assessed by observing the mobility of the J2 under a stereo zoom-microscope at 60 X magnification and expressed as the percentage of the total population. Egg hatching inhibition

A single egg mass of M. incognita was kept in 2.5 cm diameter petri plates containing 5 ml solution of each treatment. The egg mass kept in distilled water was treated as a control. Each treatment was replicated three times. Petri plates were kept at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C). For egg hatchability inhibition studies, eggs were exposed to respective treatments, and observations were recorded at regular intervals. The treatment solution was periodically replaced after each observation to maintain extract activity and minimize contamination. During replacement, care was taken to avoid loss of hatched juveniles (J2). Fluopyram was used as a positive control. It was applied at a concentration of 250 µg/ml, in accordance with the recommended application guidelines. The Experiment was conducted in triplicate, and each treatment was replicated three times. Percent egg hatching inhibition over control was calculated using the formula70,71.

In-vivo pot culture experiment

A pot culture experiment was conducted to investigate the management of the root-knot nematode (M. incognita) on muskmelon cv. ‘Arkasiri’ by assessing the nematicidal efficacy of C. carandas seed powder compared to the standard chemical nematicide Fluopyram, along with an uninoculated control. This study was set up under controlled glasshouse conditions at the Department of Studies in Biotechnology, University of Mysore, Mysuru (12.3105719° N, 76.6224571° E). Muskmelon seeds (Cucumis melo L., cv. ‘Arkasiri’) were surface-sterilized with 0.1% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) for one minute, then rinsed thoroughly three times with sterile distilled water. The sterilized seeds were then sown in plastic seedling trays filled with a steam-sterilized soil mixture. For the pot culture, 2 kg earthen pots were used, each filled with a sterilized potting mixture of red soil, sand, and farmyard manure (FYM) in a 2:1:1 ratio72. As recommended by prior studies, the C. carandas seed powder was added to the soil mixture as a soil amendment at 10 g per kg of soil73,74. A chemical nematicide, Fluopyram, was used as a positive control. It was applied at a concentration of 250 µg/ml as a soil amendment, in accordance with the recommended application guidelines. Healthy muskmelon seedlings, approximately 14 days old, were transplanted into pots arranged in a completely randomized design. Seven days post-transplantation, M. incognita was inoculated at a density of 1 J2 per gram of soil. It was placed around the root zone by creating three shallow holes near the plant and subsequently covering them with sterilized soil. The experiment was terminated on the 15th and 30th days post-inoculation, and plant growth parameters, including shoot and root length as well as fresh weights of shoots and roots, were recorded. Plants were carefully uprooted, and root observations on gall number, female nematodes, and egg masses were assessed using the acid-fuchsin staining method75. Soil nematode populations were estimated by processing the soil samples following Cobb’s76 technique and the modified Baermann funnel method77. The nematode reproduction factor was calculated based on soil population data. Root gall indices were rated on a 0–5 scale78–80. The Experiment was conducted in triplicate, and each treatment was replicated three times.

Ultrastructural study of M. incognita egg mass

The antagonistic activity of C. carandas against M. incognita egg masses was confirmed through SEM analysis. This study examined egg masses of M. incognita treated for 72 h with the methanolic seed extract of C. carandas. Egg masses placed in distilled water served as controls. The gelatinous matrix of the egg mass was slightly teased out, and the eggs were scattered before being placed on a carbon-coated stub and introduced into the chamber of a scanning electron microscope (SEM) for better examination of the eggs and gelatinous matrix. Observations were conducted using a EVO MA 15 (Carl Zeiss, Germany) SEM available at the Institution of Excellence (IOE), Vijnana Bhavana, Manasagangotri, Mysuru. The SEM allows samples to be observed in a low-pressure environment and high relative humidity, eliminating the need for prior processing of biological wet samples such as nematode eggs69.

Purification and characterization of bioactive compounds

Column chromatography

The chromatographic separation was carried out in a silica gel 60–120 mesh size column. The silica slurry prepared in the mobile phase was poured into a column packed with hexane and eluted with hexane and chloroform (9:1–1:9 and 100% chloroform), followed by ethyl acetate and methanol (9:1–1:9 and 100% methanol), and improved column packing by tapping it from outside. After column packing, the test extract prepared in dry silica was loaded on the top of the column bed to get an even layer81. Five grams of methanol extract was subjected to a silica gel column because the methanol portion of the extract possesses the highest nematicidal activity against M. incognita in in-vitro assays. After loading the sample, the mobile phase was poured through the side wall of the column, and fractions were collected in 3 ml quantities. During elution, the constant flow rate of eluent was maintained for all fractions. A total of 30 fractions were collected. Individual fractions, when tested, revealed that the 23rd fraction that was eluted using ethyl acetate and methanol had maximum juvenile mortality activity.

GC–MS analysis

The fraction that was eluted in column chromatography using ethyl acetate and methanol exhibiting nematicidal activity was subjected to GC–MS. The Clarus 680 GC was used in the analysis employed a fused silica column, packed with HP-5MS (5% biphenyl 95% dimethylpolysiloxane, 30 m × 0.25 mm ID × 250 µm df), and the components were separated using Helium as carrier gas at a constant flow of 2 ml/min. The injector temperature was set at 250 °C during the chromatographic run. The 1.0 µL of sample was injected into the instrument. The oven temperature was as follows: 100 °C (2 min); followed by 200 °C at the rate of 10 °C min−1; and 200 °C, where it was held for 1 min and then followed by 300 °C at the rate of 25 °C min−1; it was held for 8.00 min. The mass detector conditions were: Inlet line temperature of 250 °C; ion source temperature of 230 °C; and ionization mode electron impact at 70 eV, a scan time of 0.2 s, and a scan of 0.1 s. The fragments from 40 to 600 Da. The spectra of the components were compared with the database of the spectra of known components stored in the GC–MS NIST (2017) library.

Statistical analysis

Values are the means (± SE) of three consistent readings from each experiment. All experiments were conducted in a completely randomized design (CRD). The data were analyzed using one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). Differences among treatment means were compared using Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05.

The degrees of freedom (df), F-values, and significance levels were calculated and included where appropriate. The data were processed to present the results using OPSTAT, which is available online at CCSHAU, Hisar website (www.hau.ac.in).
