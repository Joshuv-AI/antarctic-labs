Executive Summary I Abstract

This final report summarizes multi-year research efforts conducted through the U.S. Department of Energy's
Research Development and Partnership Pilot (RDPP) program at Texas Southern University and partner
institutions. These efforts have advanced the understanding of atmospheric chemistry, aerosol-cloud
interactions, and air quality variability across Southeast Texas.

Undergraduate and graduate student researchers led projects that aligned with key RDPP goals, utilizing data
from the DOE ARM TRACER campaign and tools such as MATLAB, Python, and optical measurement
instruments.

Key Research Themes:

 • Aerosol-Cloud Interactions: Multiple students examined how particulate matter (PM2.5) correlates with
 Cloud Condensation Nuclei (CCN) in convective cloud formation. Using measurements from TRACER
 and instruments like the SMPS and OPC, they showed how meteorological conditions and wind
 direction influence these interactions.

 • Air Quality and Meteorology: Students used a decade of air quality and meteorological data to
 investigate how synoptic weather regimes affect ozone and PM2.5 exceedances. A Self-Organizing Map
 (SOM) approach revealed that post-trough and anticyclonic conditions are most associated with high
 ozone events.

 • Vertical Mixing and Boundary Layer Profiling: A Tethered Balloon System (TBS) was used to study
 how vertical temperature and humidity profiles influence aerosol concentrations. Results demonstrated
 the importance of atmospheric stability in trapping or dispersing pollutants.

 • Emerging Topics: New student-led projects explored microplastics in atmospheric chemistry and
 emissions from natural sources like limonene, highlighting the intersection of environmental chemistry
 and air quality.

Broader Impacts:
The projects engaged a diverse group of students in high-level research, fostering skills in atmospheric
modeling, coding, scientific communication, and teamwork. Research products included posters,
presentations, and technical manuscripts. Collaborations with BNL provided mentorship and access to cutting-
edge facilities, laying the foundation for ongoing contributions to climate and environmental science.
Project Activities and Accomplishments
Overview of Research Activities

The research conducted under the RDPP awards focused on investigating aerosol-cloud interactions, regional
air quality variability, and boundary layer dynamics in Southeast Texas. The work leveraged atmospheric data
collected through the DOE's Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) facility during the TRACER
campaign, as well as regional air quality datasets from the Texas Commission on Environmental Quality
(TCEQ).

Activities were grouped into the following research thrusts:

1. Aerosol-Cloud Interaction Studies (Durgan, Diggs, Williams, Newton)

Students evaluated the relationship between PM2.5 concentrations and cloud condensation nuclei (CCN), using
data collected from the ARM site in La Porte, TX.

Key Accomplishments:

 • Integrated measurements from the Scanning Mobility Particle Sizer (SMPS), Optical Particle Counter
 (OPC), and Aerosol Chemical Speciation Monitor (ACSM).

 • Developed MATLAB scripts to calculate PM2.5 and CCN values across different supersaturation levels.
 • Found strong correlations between PM2.5 and CCN during specific wind directions and seasons.

 • Concluded that wind patterns significantly modulate aerosol-cloud interactions and may influence
 storm intensification.

2. Large-Scale Meteorological Patterns and Air Quality (Jones, Seymore)

Students used a decade of summer meteorological data and TCEQ air quality measurements to assess how
synoptic weather regimes affect pollutant concentrations.
Key Accomplishments:

 • Classified weather patterns into four regimes: pre-trough, post-trough, anticyclonic, and transitional
 using the Self-Organizing Map (SOM) method.

 • Demonstrated that ozone exceedances were most common under post-trough and anticyclonic
 conditions.

 • Found a weak but notable correlation between PM2.5 and ozone concentrations during late summer
 months.

 • Created Python-based visualizations, histograms, and correlation matrices to support the analysis.

3. Vertical Mixing and Boundary Layer Structure (Fontenette, Seymore)

Students analyzed Tethered Balloon System (TBS) data from the TRACER campaign to study atmospheric
stability and vertical aerosol profiles.
Key Accomplishments:

 • Profiled temperature, relative humidity, and aerosol concentration across altitudes up to 1,500 meters.
 • Identified stability-induced stratification and pollutant trapping in the lower boundary layer.

 • Quantified diurnal shifts in vertical mixing and confirmed that unstable afternoon conditions help
 disperse aerosols.

4. Emerging Research Topics in Atmospheric Chemistry (Diggs, Jones)

Additional student-led research introduced new chemical and environmental questions to the group's scope.

Key Accomplishments:

 • Microplastics in Atmospheric Chemistry: Investigated how airborne microplastics may interact with
 atmospheric gases to form secondary pollutants.

 • Limonene Emissions: Explored the role of limonene— a biogenic volatile organic compound— in urban
 photochemical smog formation using a small-scale experimental setup.

Publications, Presentations, and Research Products
1. Peer-Reviewed Publication

Title:
Computational Investigation of Methoxy Radical-Driven Oxidation of Dimethyl Sulfide: A Pathway Linked to
Methane Oxidation
Authors:
Bruce M. Prince, Daniel Vrinceanu, Mark C. Harvey, Michael Jensen, Maria Zawadowicz, Chongai Kuang

Status:
Published in Gases (MDPI), 2026.

Prince, B. M.; Vrinceanu, D.; Harvey, M. C.; Jensen, M.; Zawadowicz, M.; Kuang, C. Computational Investigation
of Methoxy Radical-Driven Oxidation of Dimethyl Sulfide: A Pathway Linked to Methane Oxidation. Gases 2026, 6,
Article 2. https://www.mdpi.eom/2673-5628/6/l/2

Summary:
This study presents a comprehensive computational investigation of dimethyl sulfide (DMS) oxidation by
methoxy radicals (CHsO*), a pathway linked to methane oxidation chemistry. The results demonstrate that
CHO* may play a localized yet meaningful role in sulfate precursor formation under methane-influenced
atmospheric conditions, complementing traditional hydroxyl radical oxidation pathways.

Conclusion and Final Remarks

The research conducted under the Department of Energy's Research Development and Partnership Pilot (RDPP)
program at Texas Southern University (TSU) has significantly advanced both scientific understanding and
student development in atmospheric science and environmental chemistry. Through faculty -led and student-
driven projects, this work has contributed to new insights into aerosol-cloud interactions, air quality variability,
boundary layer dynamics, and emerging environmental contaminants.
Key accomplishments include:

 • Identification of synoptic-scale weather patterns that influence ozone and PM2.5 exceedances in
 Southeast Texas.

 • Characterization of cloud condensation nuclei (CCN) under varying aerosol loads using ARM¬
 TRACER data.

 • Experimental and computational exploration of VOCs and radical-driven oxidation pathways relevant
 to climate modeling.

 • Publication of a peer-reviewed article in Gases (MDPI, 2026) documenting computational investigation
 of methoxy radical-driven oxidation of dimethyl sulfide.

In addition to the scientific achievements, the project has made a meaningful impact on workforce
development. Students gained hands-on experience with data analysis, experimental design, and
computational modeling— skills essential for careers in climate and atmospheric research. Several student
researchers participated in internships at Brookhaven National Laboratory and presented their work at
regional and institutional symposia.
The collaborative structure between TSU, Brookhaven National Laboratory, and St. Edward's University
provided an effective model for mentoring and research productivity at a minority-serving institution. These

efforts have helped build a sustainable pipeline of diverse talent in the geosciences and support DOE's mission
of broadening participation in climate-related disciplines.

As the project concludes, plans are already in place to incorporate this work into future proposals and ongoing
research programs at TSU. These initiatives will further integrate undergraduates and early-stage graduate
students into high-impact research that addresses pressing questions in climate science, air quality, and
environmental health.
