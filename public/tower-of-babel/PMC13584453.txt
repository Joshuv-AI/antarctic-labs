Association between microinflammatory state and cardiac valvular calcification in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis: a single-centre observational study
Authors: Meiyu Liu, Peishan Peng, Jie Chen, Wei Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the association between microinflammatory state and cardiac valvular calcification (CVC) in maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients, and to compare the predictive value of the C-reactive protein-albumin-lymphocyte (CALLY) index with conventional inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers for CVC severity.MethodsThis single-center cross-sectional study enrolled 203 MHD patients. Patients were categorized by echocardiography into no CVC (n = 67), single valve calcification (n = 69), and double valve calcification (n = 67) groups. Serum levels of hs-CRP, IL-6, TNF-α, and routine biochemical parameters were measured. The CALLY index, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR), prognostic nutritional index (PNI), and geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI) were calculated. Multinomial logistic regression, model fit indices, net reclassification improvement (NRI), integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and bootstrap internal validation were utilized to evaluate the comparative and incremental performance of the biomarkers.ResultsThe prevalence of CVC in the study population was 67.0%. Multinomial logistic regression analysis demonstrated that after adjusting for the six clinical covariates (age, gender, dialysis vintage, serum phosphorus, active vitamin D dosage, and phosphate binders), the CALLY index was independently associated with both single valve calcification (OR = 0.680, P = 0.005) and double valve calcification (OR = 0.527, P < 0.001). The CALLY index also exhibited the highest C-statistic for CVC (0.869). In NRI/IDI analyses, the CALLY index significantly improved both continuous NRI (0.435, P = 0.001) and IDI (0.034, P = 0.004) for double valve calcification and CVC, whereas NLR, IL-6, and hs-CRP did not.ConclusionThe CALLY index is independently associated with CVC severity in MHD patients. Incorporating the CALLY index into standard clinical parameters provides significant incremental predictive value for identifying multi-valvular involvement.Keywords: Cardiac valvular calcification, CALLY index, Inflammation, Maintenance hemodialysis

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) has emerged as a major global health challenge, with an estimated 788 million adults aged 20 years and older affected worldwide in 2023, representing a global age-standardised prevalence of 14.2% [1]. The disease burden is particularly severe as it progresses to the end stage; according to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2023, the number of patients requiring kidney failure with replacement therapy, including maintenance hemodialysis, has risen to 4.59 million worldwide [2]. Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of mortality among patients with end stage kidney disease [3]. Cardiac valvular calcification, particularly involving the mitral and aortic valves, is a highly prevalent complication in these patients and serves as a strong marker for generalised atherosclerosis and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [4, 5]. CVC is an active and strictly regulated pathological process driven by metabolic derangements, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation [6].

Microinflammation is defined as a persistent, low-grade systemic inflammatory state occurring independently of active infection, typically without overt clinical symptoms and evidenced only by mild elevations of circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines. First proposed by Schömig et al. in 2000, microinflammation is closely associated with protein-energy wasting in MHD patients [7]. Although its exact mechanisms remain incompletely understood, extensive research implicates metabolic disorders, oxidative stress, gut dysbiosis, and dialysate composition [8–10]. Mounting evidence suggests that microinflammation plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis and progression of CVC [6, 11]. Specifically, inflammatory biomarkers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) have been strongly correlated with the development of vascular and valvular calcification [12–15]. The NLR, a simple marker reflecting systemic inflammatory state, has been demonstrated to be closely associated with cardiovascular calcification in MHD patients. Tang et al. reported that NLR was an independent risk factor for cardiovascular calcification and predicted CVC in a cohort of 362 MHD patients [16]. The CAR, which integrates inflammatory and nutritional state, has shown promising prognostic value in valvular heart disease. In a nationwide prospective cohort study of 5,519 patients with moderate-to-severe valvular heart disease, Zhang et al. demonstrated that a high CAR was independently associated with 2-year all-cause mortality and improved risk stratification when added to conventional risk models [17].

In the hemodialysis population, the interplay between inflammation, nutritional state, and cardiovascular outcomes has been increasingly recognized. Yaprak et al. demonstrated that the PNI exhibited superior predictive value for mortality compared with the NLR in a cohort of hemodialysis patients, underscoring the importance of composite inflammatory indices in this population [18]. Similarly, Wang et al., in a longitudinal study of hemodialysis patients, demonstrated that dynamic changes in the PNI and GNRI were significantly associated with reduced risks of both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Among the indices evaluated, the baseline GNRI and its post-dialysis trajectory exhibited the strongest predictive performance [19]. Furthermore, recent studies have emphasized the role of cardiometabolic risk profiling in dialysis patients, wherein multidimensional markers integrating inflammation and nutrition have shown incremental prognostic value beyond traditional risk factors [20]. These findings further illustrate the prognostic value of multidimensional indices that integrate nutritional and inflammatory parameters in the dialysis population.

The CALLY index is a novel integrative biomarker that reflects both nutritional and inflammatory state. Originally developed by Iida et al. based on the PNI to predict prognosis after hepatocellular carcinoma resection [21] its clinical utility has since expanded to tumor prognosis [22–24], the incidence of osteoarthritis [25], cardiovascular outcomes [26–28], and metabolic disorders [29, 30]. However, its prognostic value in MHD patients remains incompletely characterized. While Huang et al. reported that the CALLY index independently predicted all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in MHD patients [31, 32], Cakmak et al. found that it did not demonstrate independent prognostic value in a smaller cohort [33]. These discrepant findings underscore the need for further evaluation of the CALLY index in specific clinical contexts—including its potential association with cardiovascular structural abnormalities such as CVC, which has yet to be elucidated.

Despite these advances, studies specifically addressing the predictive value of NLR and CAR for CVC severity—particularly in distinguishing single versus double valve calcification—remain limited, and direct comparisons with novel composite markers such as the CALLY index are lacking.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between microinflammation and CVC in MHD patients, evaluate the predictive value of the CALLY index for CVC, and compare its performance with conventional inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers (hs-CRP, IL-6, NLR, PNI, GNRI, and CAR).

Materials and methods

Study population

A total of 203 patients undergoing MHD at the Blood Purification Centre of Xinhua Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, were enrolled between December 2024 and December 2025. Among them, 120 (59.1%) were male and 83 (40.9%) were female.

Inclusion Criteria: (1) Aged ≥ 18 years; (2) On regular hemodialysis for ≥ 3 months, with a frequency of three sessions per week (4–5 h per session); (3) Provision of written informed consent.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) A history of acute infection within the last 6 months; (2) Severe cardiac, pulmonary, or hepatic dysfunction; (3) Active malignancy or currently undergoing oncology treatment; (4) Active phase of rheumatic or autoimmune diseases.

Research methods

Collection of clinical data

Demographic data, primary renal disease aetiology, medical history, and personal history were collected, including age, sex, dialysis vintage, height, weight, smoking history, and comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and coronary heart disease.

Medication histories of all enrolled patients were reviewed. All patients were on long-term treatment with phosphate binders and active vitamin D. The type of phosphate binder (calcium-containing: calcium acetate, calcium carbonate D3; or non-calcium-containing: sevelamer carbonate, lanthanum carbonate chewable tablets) and the weekly dosage of active vitamin D were documented. Active vitamin D preparations included calcitriol and alfacalcidol, with dosages uniformly converted to equivalent calcitriol dose (µg / week) using the conversion formula: 1 µg calcitriol = 0.5 µg alfacalcidol. The use of calcimimetics was also recorded.

Biochemical and laboratory measurements

Pre-dialysis blood samples were collected to measure biochemical parameters, including alkaline phosphatase (ALP), serum albumin (Alb), serum prealbumin (PAB), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine (SCr), uric acid (UA), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), intact parathyroid hormone (iPTH), serum calcium, phosphorus, and magnesium. Complete blood count parameters, comprising white blood cell count (WBC), platelet count (PLT), neutrophil count (N), lymphocyte count (L), and hemoglobin (Hb) were analysed along with inflammatory markers such as hs-CRP, IL-6, CRP, and tumour necrosis factor (TNF-α) by the Department of Clinical Laboratory at our hospital.

Body weight before and after blood purification, the duration of the blood purification session, and post-dialysis BUN were collected.

The following indicators were calculated:

CALLY index = [Albumin (g/L) × Lymphocyte count (10⁹/L)]⁄[CRP (mg/L) × 10]

BMI = Body weight (kg) / Height (m)²

NLR = Neutrophil count (10⁹/L) / Lymphocyte count (10⁹/L)

CAR = hs-CRP (mg/L) / Albumin (g/L)

PNI = Albumin (g/L) + 5 × Lymphocyte count (×10⁹/L)

GNRI = 1.489 × serum albumin (g/L) + 41.7 × (current weight / ideal weight). Ideal body weight was calculated using the Lorenz formulae: for men, height (cm) − 100 − (height − 150)/4; for women, height (cm) − 100 − (height − 150) / 2.5.

Corrected calcium (Cac) = Serum calcium (mmol/L) + 0.02 × (40 – Albumin (g/L))

Calcium-phosphorus product (Ca×P) = Corrected calcium (mmol/L) × Serum phosphorus (mmol/L) × 12.4

Kt/V = −ln (Cpost / Cpre − 0.008×t) + (4-3.5 × Cpost / Cpre) × ΔBW / BWpost, where Cpost and Cpre represent pre- and post-dialysis blood urea nitrogen concentrations, t denotes the dialysis session duration (hours), ΔBW indicates the intradialytic weight loss (kg), and BWpost is the post-dialysis body weight (kg)

Echocardiographic assessment

Colour Doppler echocardiography was performed by two designated experienced sonographers to evaluate and record calcification of the aortic and mitral valves. CVC was defined as the presence of at least one area of intense echogenicity (high-echo nodules) on the valve leaflets or annulus [34].

Definition and grouping

Coronary artery disease (CAD) was diagnosed according to the 2023 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology (AHA/ACC) guidelines [35].

Diabetes mellitus (DM) was diagnosed according to the 2024 American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria [36].

In the absence of a universally accepted threshold for defining microinflammation in maintenance hemodialysis patients, we avoided arbitrary dichotomization. Instead, circulating inflammatory biomarkers—hs-CRP, IL-6, and the derived indices (NLR, CAR, and CALLY index)—were analyzed as continuous variables in all regression models. For descriptive purposes, hs-CRP levels > 3 mg/L were considered indicative of elevated cardiovascular risk based on AHA/CDC guidelines, but this cutoff was not used to define groups for primary analyses [37].

Residual renal function (RRF) was evaluated based on a 24-hour urine output ≥ 100 mL, consistent with previous studies [38].

Patients were categorized based on echocardiographic findings:

No CVC group: no calcification of either the aortic or mitral valve;

No CVC group: no calcification of either the aortic or mitral valve;

Single valve calcification group (Single VC): calcification of either the aortic valve or the mitral valve only;

Single valve calcification group (Single VC): calcification of either the aortic valve or the mitral valve only;

Double valve calcification group (Double VC): calcification of both the aortic and mitral valves.

Double valve calcification group (Double VC): calcification of both the aortic and mitral valves.

Blood purification modality and adequacy

All enrolled patients underwent blood purification three times weekly, consisting of two hemodialysis sessions and one hemodiafiltration session, with one session of hemodialysis combined with hemoperfusion performed every two weeks. Blood purification adequacy was assessed by the urea clearance index (Kt/V).

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in SPSS 27.0 and R 4.5.3. Continuous variables were first evaluated for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (±s) and analyzed using Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA. Non-normally distributed data are expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR), and compared among the three CVC groups using the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis H test. Categorical variables were summarized as counts (percentages) and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Multivariable multinomial logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of single and double valve calcification (reference: no CVC). Model discrimination was assessed using the C-statistic, and fit was evaluated by Nagelkerke’s R² and the Akaike information criterion (AIC). Beyond comparing the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), the relative parameters of continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were calculated to quantify the absolute reclassification and discrimination gains when adding each biomarker to the baseline clinical model. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 203 MHD patients were enrolled, including 67 (33.0%) in the no CVC group, 69 (34.0%) in the single valve calcification group, and 67 (33.0%) in the double valve calcification group. As shown in Table 1, significant differences were observed among the three groups in age, dialysis vintage, non-calcium-containing phosphate binder use, and active vitamin D dosage (all P < 0.05). Patients in the double valve calcification group were older, had longer dialysis vintage, lower rates of non-calcium-containing phosphate binder use, and lower active vitamin D dosage. No significant differences were found in sex, BMI, smoking history, or comorbidities (all P > 0.05).

Independent risk factors for CVC

Univariate analysis revealed that age, dialysis vintage, albumin, serum phosphorus, calcium-phosphorus product, iPTH, hs-CRP, IL-6, NLR, CAR, and LAD were significantly associated with CVC (all P < 0.05). The CALLY index was significantly protective against both single valve (OR = 0.645, P < 0.001) and double valve calcification (OR = 0.456, P < 0.001) (Table 3).

To evaluate the independent statistical associations between these continuous biomarkers and CVC severity, multinomial logistic regression models were executed using the “no CVC” group as the reference. To control for potential clinical confounding without introducing a risk of model overfitting, a fully adjusted 7-variable regression model was strictly applied for each biomarker. The baseline adjustment block comprised six pre-specified covariates: age, sex, dialysis vintage, serum phosphorus, active vitamin D dosage, and non-calcium-based phosphate binders.

As presented in Table 5, after controlling for the six baseline clinical covariates, the continuous CALLY index remained significantly and independently associated with both single valve calcification (OR = 0.680, 95% CI: 0.518–0.892, P = 0.005) and double valve calcification (OR = 0.527, 95% CI: 0.370–0.749, P < 0.001). Stratified bootstrap validation (2000 resamples) confirmed the robustness of the main findings. The CALLY index, NLR, and CAR maintained significant associations with double valve calcification, with minimal bias and BCa confidence intervals excluding zero (CALLY: bias − 0.071, BCa 95% CI:-1.087–-0.397, P < 0.001; NLR: bias 0.022, 0.047–0.746, P = 0.010; CAR: bias 0.100, 0.253–2.030, P = 0.002). In contrast, the originally significant association of PNI became non-significant after bootstrap correction (bias − 0.027, P = 0.095), while IL-6, hs-CRP, and GNRI did not consistently show significance.

Bayesian multinomial logistic regression yielded results that were materially consistent with both the maximum likelihood and bootstrap estimates. For the CALLY index, the posterior mean coefficient for double valve calcification was − 0.70 (95%CI: -1.11–-0.35), corresponding to a posterior odds ratio of approximately 0.50. The posterior estimates for NLR (0.38, 0.11–0.68) and CAR (1.01, 0.35–1.71) also remained significant. PNI showed only a marginal posterior effect (-0.09, -0.18–-0.01), while IL-6, hs-CRP, and GNRI exhibited credible intervals that crossed or approached zero. All models showed excellent convergence (Rhat = 1.00, bulk-ESS > 2000).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of 203 MHD patients, we systematically evaluated the association between microinflammatory state and CVC, and for the first time compared the predictive value of the CALLY index with other inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers for CVC severity. The main findings are as follows. The CALLY index showed a stepwise decrease with increasing CVC severity and remained an independent protective factor after adjusting for multiple confounders. The CALLY showed the best model fit and discrimination among the markers assessed. Non-calcium-containing phosphate binders and active vitamin D were independently associated with reduced calcification odds. The CALLY index independently predicted both aortic and mitral valve calcification, whereas hs-CRP, NLR, and CAR only predicted aortic valve calcification.

The present study demonstrated that phosphorus and calcium-phosphorus product were independent risk factors for CVC, with increasing severity of CVC associated with higher levels. This finding aligns with the established role of calcium-phosphorus homeostasis in cardiovascular calcification. Vieceli Dalla Sega et al. emphasized that hyperphosphatemia-induced osteogenic differentiation of vascular smooth muscle cells represents a core mechanism of cardiovascular calcification, with elevated calcium-phosphorus product directly promoting ectopic calcium deposition in valvular tissue [6]. Juhász et al., in a prospective study of 154 cardiovascular patients, first demonstrated that the plasma phosphate/pyrophosphate ratio was significantly associated with aortic valve calcification burden, remaining an independent determinant even after adjusting for traditional risk factors, highlighting the central role of phosphate homeostasis dysregulation in valvular calcification [39]. Vervloet systematically summarized the impact of CKD-mineral and bone disorder on arterial stiffness, noting that hypercalcemia and hyperphosphatemia promote vascular calcification and increase arterial stiffness, thereby exacerbating cardiovascular risk [40].

Non-calcium-containing phosphate binder use and higher active vitamin D dosage were associated with lower odds of double valve calcification. These findings are consistent with accumulating evidence on the cardiovascular benefits of optimizing phosphate binder selection. Mason et al., in a systematic evaluation, demonstrated that calcium-containing phosphate binders promote vascular calcification progression more significantly than sevelamer, supporting the preferential use of non-calcium-containing agents [41]. Zhang et al., in a meta-analysis of 3,271 patients, confirmed that sevelamer was associated with reduced coronary artery calcification progression compared with calcium-based binders [42]. Regarding vitamin D analogs, Gravellone et al. summarized that active vitamin D receptor activators exert cardiovascular protective effects through suppression of PTH secretion, upregulation of Klotho expression, and enhancement of matrix Gla protein activity [43]. More recently, Meng et al. demonstrated in animal models that the phosphate binder sevelamer significantly attenuates bioprosthetic valve calcification through mechanisms independent of serum calcium and phosphorus regulation [44].

A key aspect of this work is the direct comparison of the CALLY index with PNI and GNRI, which are established nutritional-inflammatory indices. Yaprak et al. recently demonstrated that GNRI, MIS, and PNI predict mortality in hemodialysis patients, with GNRI performing best [18]. In our data, however, PNI and GNRI did not exhibit robust independent associations with CVC severity, and PNI lost significance after bootstrap validation. This discrepancy may be due to the absence of a direct inflammatory component (CRP) in PNI and GNRI, whereas the CALLY index intentionally incorporates CRP, thereby better capturing the low-grade inflammation that drives valvular calcification [6, 12–15]. Similarly, Wang et al. showed that dynamic changes in PNI and GNRI were associated with reduced mortality [19]; our findings extend this concept to structural cardiovascular endpoints, suggesting that for CVC, inflammatory load is a crucial dimension.

The CALLY index also demonstrated favorable incremental predictive value, significantly improving both continuous NRI and IDI for double valve calcification. Although CAR and NLR showed significant associations in some models, only the CALLY index consistently improved both metrics. These results echo the concept advanced by Arslan that a multidimensional assessment of cardiometabolic risk—integrating nutrition, inflammation, and body composition—may outperform simpler markers [20]. The CALLY index, derived from widely available laboratory parameters, offers a pragmatic summary of several interrelated pathways.

The differential predictive pattern for aortic versus mitral valve calcification merits consideration. The CALLY index predicted both, whereas single-dimension inflammatory markers predicted only aortic calcification. This aligns with reports that mitral valve calcification is more strongly influenced by metabolic and nutritional factors [45, 46] and underscores the value of a composite marker for comprehensive risk assessment.

Although serum TNF-α levels differed across the three CVC groups in unadjusted comparisons, this association was no longer significant after multivariable adjustment. This pattern may reflect confounding by age and other inflammatory markers; in a recent study of 58 maintenance hemodialysis patients, IL-6 demonstrated stronger and more consistent associations with echocardiographic remodeling parameters than TNF-α, which was more strongly influenced by age and albumin levels [47]. An earlier report found that TNF-α was an independent risk factor for CVC; however, that analysis did not simultaneously adjust for IL-6, CRP, and other inflammatory indices [48]. These observations suggest that when multiple inflammatory markers are concurrently measured and adjusted, as in the present study, TNF-α offers limited additional predictive value beyond IL-6 and composite markers such as the CALLY index.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the temporal relationship between inflammation and valve calcification remains uncertain. Second, the relatively modest sample size may limit generalizability. These analyses were limited by substantially reduced sample sizes, which may result in low statistical power and imprecise confidence intervals. Third, echocardiographic assessment of CVC was qualitative; although two experienced sonographers independently acquired images, no formal inter-observer agreement statistics were calculated, and computed tomography‑based calcium scoring was unavailable for quantification. Fourth, inflammatory markers were measured at a single time point, which may not fully capture the dynamic nature of microinflammation. Additionally, potential residual confounding from dialysis adequacy (Kt/V was comparable across groups), medication adherence (documented from prescription records rather than objectively assessed), and other unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. Future multicenter prospective studies with longitudinal follow-up are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusion

In summary, microinflammatory state, as reflected by circulating inflammatory biomarkers, was independently associated with the severity of CVC. The CALLY index was independently associated with both aortic and mitral valve calcification and showed favorable predictive performance for assessing CVC severity in this population.
