Strigolactone‐mediated architecture regulation and stress resilience: Insights and innovations for crop breeding
Authors: Qingliang Hu, Jiayang Li, Bing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStrigolactones (SLs) were initially identified as rhizosphere signals that trigger germination of parasitic weeds and promote branching in arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi. More recently, SLs have been characterized as a class of carotenoid‐derived plant hormones that regulate plant architecture and stress responses. This review systematically summarizes their diverse functions in shaping shoot architecture and root development, as well as their ability to mediate acclimation to various abiotic and biotic stresses. It also discusses the canonical signaling module composed of D14, MAX2/D3, and D53/SMXLs and its extensive interactions with other hormonal pathways. Finally, this review suggests that future research should focus on elucidating the dynamic responses to environmental stress mediated by the SL pathway, decoding the functional diversification of SLs in different plant species, and leveraging SLs as rhizosphere signals to control parasitic weeds. Precise spatiotemporal modulation of SL activity is crucial for balancing its functional complexity and will contribute to designing crops with optimized plant architectures and enhanced stress resilience.

INTRODUCTION

Strigolactones (SLs) are sesquiterpene lactone hormones that play key roles in regulating plant development and orchestrating responses to environmental stress. They were named after their initial identification as germination stimulants for the seeds of the parasitic weed Striga (Cook et al., 1966). In 2005, SLs were recognized as signaling molecules that promote hyphal branching in arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi, thus revealing their fundamental role in plant–fungal symbiosis (Akiyama et al., 2005; Figure 1). Since the 1990s, genetic studies on increased shoot branching or tillering mutants in model plants, such as the more axillary growth (max1–max4) in Arabidopsis thaliana, ramosus (rms1–rms6) in pea (Pisum sativum), dwarf/high tillering dwarf (e.g., d27, d17, d10, d14, d3, d53, and htd) in rice (Oryza sativa), and decreased apical dominance (dad1–dad3) in petunia (Petunia hybrida), have revealed that the highly branched phenotypes of these mutants result from defects in the biosynthesis or signaling of a mobile molecule (Beveridge et al., 1994; 1996; Napoli, 1996; Beveridge et al., 2000; Stirnberg et al., 2002; 2007; Turnbull et al., 2002; Sorefan et al., 2003; Booker et al., 2004, 2005; Foo et al., 2005; Ishikawa et al., 2005; Snowden et al., 2005; Johnson et al., 2006; Lazar and Goodman, 2006; Zou et al., 2006; Arite et al., 2007, 2009; Simons et al., 2007; Gao et al., 2009; Lin et al., 2009; Liu et al., 2009). In 2008, two independent research groups identified SLs as the key small molecules underlying these highly branched phenotypes, thereby establishing SLs as a new class of plant hormones (Gomez‐Roldan et al., 2008; Umehara et al., 2008; Figure 1).

A historical perspective on SL research and applications

This timeline highlights key advances in SL research, spanning their functional redefinition, mechanistic elucidation, and translational development. The major phases include (1) initial identification of SLs as rhizosphere chemical signals for parasitic plants and mycorrhizal fungi, followed by their recognition as endogenous hormones that inhibit shoot branching; (2) clarification of core SL biosynthesis and signal transduction pathways; and (3) deepened insights into molecular mechanisms of the SL pathway and agricultural applications to optimize plant architecture and resilience. SLs, strigolactones.

Subsequent research rapidly elucidated the core pathways of SL biosynthesis, transport, and signaling (Figure 1). SL biosynthesis begins with all‐trans‐β‐carotene, which is isomerized by D27 to form 9‐cis‐β‐carotene (Lin et al., 2009; Alder et al., 2012). This intermediate is sequentially cleaved by CCD7 (encoded by MAX3/D17/HTD1/RMS5) and CCD8 (encoded by MAX4/D10/RMS1/DAD1) to produce carlactone (CL), which is further metabolized into various canonical and noncanonical SLs, such as CLA, MeCLA, MeCLAC + 16 Da, 4‐deoxyorobanchol (4DO), and orobanchol, in multiple plant species (Abe et al., 2014; Seto et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2014; Brewer et al., 2016; Yoneyama et al., 2018; Cardoso et al., 2019; Wakabayashi et al., 2019). The ATP‐binding cassette transporter PDR1 is crucial for SL transport, as pdr1 mutants exhibit reduced SL exudation and impaired AM symbiosis (Kretzschmar et al., 2012).

The SL receptors belong to the α/β‐hydrolase protein family (Hamiaux et al., 2012; Waters et al., 2012; Kagiyama et al., 2013; Nakamura et al., 2013; Zhao et al., 2013; Chevalier et al., 2014; de Saint Germain et al., 2016; Yao et al., 2016; Hu et al., 2017; Shabek et al., 2018). D14 in angiosperms and KARRIKIN INSENSITIVE 2 divergent (KAI2d) subclades in parasitic plants are SL receptors that arose from a duplication of the karrikin receptor KAI2 (Conn et al., 2015; Toh et al., 2015; Tsuchiya et al., 2015). Interestingly, in rice, the KAI2 ortholog D14‐Like (D14L) is also required for AM symbiosis (Gutjahr et al., 2015). SL perception involves the F‐box protein D3/MAX2/RMS4, which acts as the substrate‐recognition subunit within an SCF ubiquitin ligase complex (Stirnberg et al., 2002, 2007; Waters et al., 2017). The essential role of the F‐box protein suggests that SL signaling involves ubiquitination‐dependent degradation of target proteins. A key breakthrough was the identification of D53 as a repressor of SL signaling (Jiang et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2013). D53 and its homologs, the Suppressor of MAX2‐Like 6, 7, and 8 (SMXL6, 7, and 8) proteins, contain ethylene‐responsive element binding factor‐associated amphiphilic repression (EAR) motifs that mediate interactions with the transcriptional co‐repressors Topless (TPL) and TPL‐related proteins (TPRs) (Jiang et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2013; Soundappan et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2015; Liang et al., 2016; Ma et al., 2017). Upon SL perception, D14 interacts with D3/MAX2 and D53/SMXL6, 7, 8 in an SL‐dependent manner, leading to the ubiquitination and degradation of the repressors D53/SMXL6, 7, 8 and the activation of downstream transcriptional responses (Jiang et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2013; Soundappan et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2015; Liang et al., 2016). The systematic identification of SL‐responsive downstream genes has provided crucial tools and insights, effectively overcoming a major bottleneck in SL signaling research (Wang et al., 2020a).

Recent research on SLs has advanced rapidly, deepening our understanding of their mechanisms and exploring potential agricultural applications (Figure 1). Several novel enzymes involved in canonical or noncanonical SL biosynthesis have been identified, including CYP711A5 and OsCYP706C2 in rice, ZmCYP706C37 in maize, and CLA methyltransferase and CYP722C in Arabidopsis (Mashiguchi et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023a, 2024; Wang et al., 2023a; Zhou et al., 2025). Notably, SL profiles in maize are closely linked to Striga resistance, and the evolutionary trajectory of interorganismal SL biosynthesis has been clarified (Li et al., 2023a; Zhou et al., 2025). Under low phosphate conditions, SL biosynthesis and secretion are enhanced in the liverwort Marchantia paleacea, further stimulating the secretion of chitin oligomers from mycorrhizal fungi. This process promotes the expression of symbiotic genes while suppressing strong immune responses in Marchantia paleacea (Tan et al., 2025). Our understanding of SL signaling has also deepened to encompass D3 conformational switching, D14 and SMXLs phosphorylation, and SMXLs phase separation (Tal et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2024a; Li et al., 2025a, 2025b; Su and Xue, 2025). Among these processes, D14 phosphorylation plays a key role in low‐nitrogen‐repressed tillering (Hu et al., 2024a). Intriguingly, root microbiota can modulate the SL pathway of crops through their metabolites. For example, cyclo(Leu‐Pro), a cyclic dipeptide produced by a root‐associated bacterium, binds directly to the SL receptor D14 in rice, thereby activating SL signaling and inhibiting rice tillering (Zhang et al., 2025a). A recent study revealed that the P3 protein of the rice grassy stunt virus (RGSV) blocks SL perception by binding directly to the receptor D14, thereby preventing its interaction with D3 in rice (Yang et al., 2026). From an applied perspective, engineering SL transporters has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance crop resistance against parasitic weeds such as Striga and Orobanche (Ban et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2025). For comprehensive updates on these advances, readers are directed to recent reviews (Wang et al., 2024; Chang et al., 2025; Niu et al., 2025; Ou et al., 2025).

Since their identification as hormones, SLs have been shown to regulate various developmental and physiological processes, including plant architecture (e.g., shoot branching, plant height, and leaf and root development) and resilience to phosphate deficiency, drought, and other abiotic stresses. This review summarizes the roles of SLs in shaping plant architecture and stress responses and discusses their potential applications in the breeding of crops with optimized growth and improved stress tolerance.

STRIGOLACTONES AND BRANCH DEVELOPMENT

Strigolactones inhibit branch number

Shoot branching is a key feature of phenotypic plasticity in plants and significantly contributes to architectural diversity (Wang et al., 2018a). In cereal crops such as rice and wheat, these branches, known as “tillers”, are essential for panicle number and grain yield (Wang and Li, 2008, 2011). Branch development occurs through two main phases: the initiation of axillary meristems and the formation of buds, followed by either bud outgrowth or dormancy. The first stage is largely controlled by developmental genes, while the second stage is coordinated by hormonal and environmental factors (Shimizu‐Sato and Mori, 2001).

SLs act as key regulators of bud outgrowth during branching/tillering. Beyond the well‐studied model plants such as Arabidopsis thaliana, pea, petunia, and rice, this role is highly conserved across diverse crops and trees, including wheat, barley, millet, maize, sorghum, tomato, poplar, grapevine, switchgrass, peach, sugarcane, and silver birch, as evidenced by the enhanced branching phenotype in SL biosynthesis and signaling mutants (Muhr et al., 2016; Pan et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2017, 2021b; Chen et al., 2018, 2023; Cochetel et al., 2018; Ren et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022; Guan et al., 2023; Su et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023c; Awan et al., 2024; Kuijer et al., 2024; Inoue et al., 2025; Kelly et al., 2025; Korek et al., 2025; Pei et al., 2025).

Significant progress has been made in elucidating the molecular mechanisms through which SLs inhibit shoot branching. The D14–D3/MAX2–D53/SMXL6, 7, 8 module plays a central role in SL signaling (Jiang et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2013; Soundappan et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2015, 2020a). Downstream transcription factors of D53/SMXL6, 7, 8 are key regulators of branch development. The Teosinte Branched1 (TB1) gene encodes a transcription factor that belongs to the TEOSINTE BRANCHED1, CYCLOIDEA, PCF (TCP) family and acts as a central hub that inhibits bud outgrowth in SL signaling (Wang et al., 2018a). This gene, identified in maize, is also known as OsTB1 or FINECULM1 in rice and as BRC1 in Arabidopsis and pea (Hubbard et al., 2002; Aguilar‐Martínez et al., 2007; Minakuchi et al., 2010; Studer et al., 2011; Braun et al., 2012). In Arabidopsis, SLs trigger the ubiquitination and degradation of SMXL6, 7, and 8 proteins, thereby activating BRC1 expression via distinct transcription factors (Wang et al., 2020a). The transcription factors SQUAMOSA PROMOTER BINDING PROTEIN‐LIKE 9 (SPL9), SPL15, BES1 (bri1‐EMS‐SUPPRESSOR 1), and TCP4 interact with SMXL6, 7, and 8 and directly bind the BRC1 promoter to regulate its transcriptional activation (Hu et al., 2019a; Xie et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2026). BRC1 directly activates the expression of Homeobox Protein 21 (HB21), 40, and 53, thereby stimulating abscisic acid (ABA) biosynthesis and repressing outgrowth in axillary buds (Aguilar‐Martínez et al., 2007; González‐Grandío et al., 2017; Seale et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2020a). A recent mathematical model focusing on the bud–bud competition indicates that BRC1 inhibits the basal rate of auxin efflux in buds, thereby repressing branch development (Nahas et al., 2025). Interestingly, SL signaling also regulates branching through the BRC1‐independent pathway that alters the biosynthesis and signaling of trehalose 6‐phosphate (Fichtner et al., 2024; Figure 2A).

Molecular mechanisms of SL‐regulated shoot branching in Arabidopsis and rice

In the absence of SLs, SMXL6, 7, and 8 proteins form complexes with the transcriptional co‐repressor TPL, repressing the expression of BRC1 and their own genes. (B) SLs suppress tillering in rice. Without SLs, the repressor D53 inhibits tillering by recruiting TPL/TPR co‐repressors. SL perception induces D14 to interact with D3, promoting the ubiquitination and degradation of D53, which relieves the multilayered repression of tillering. (C) SLs increase the branch angle. In SL‐deficient mutants, elevated auxin levels enhance polar auxin transport (PAT), resulting in an enhanced gravitropic response and erect growth. In rice, Ideal Plant Architecture1 (IPA1) acts as a direct downstream transcription factor of D53 in the regulation of tiller number (Song et al., 2017; Figure 2B). IPA1, also known as OsSPL14, belongs to the Squamosa Promoter Binding Protein‐Like (SPL) transcription factor family (Jiao et al., 2010; Miura et al., 2010). IPA1 loss‐of‐function mutants display high tillering and dwarf phenotypes and are insensitive to rac‐GR24. D53 directly interacts with IPA1 and represses its transcriptional activity (Song et al., 2017). IPA1 directly binds to the promoter of OsTB1 to suppress tillering in rice (Lu et al., 2013; Figure 2B). Whether SLs modulate ABA content via IPA1‐mediated regulation of OsTB1 remains unclear and requires further investigation. The transcription factor OsMADS57 interacts with OsTB1 and directly binds to the D14 promoter, inhibiting its transcription and positively regulating tillering (Guo et al., 2013). In pea, PsBRC1 expression levels are significantly lower in SL biosynthesis (rms1 and rms2) and signaling (rms4) mutants than in wild‐type plants and are upregulated in rms1 and rms2 following rac‐GR24 application (Braun et al., 2012). Furthermore, the exogenous supply of both sucrose and cytokinins (CKs) inhibits PsBRC1 expression (Braun et al., 2012; Dun et al., 2013). In maize, the key SL signaling repressor ZmD53 directly interacts with the SPL transcription factors UB2, UB3, and TSH4 to inhibit TB1 expression, thereby promoting branch formation (Liu et al., 2021b). Homologs of the SPL and TB1 transcription factors also play important roles in wheat, tomato, tobacco, peach, Tulipa gesneriana, and soybean (Gao et al., 2016; Moreno‐Pachon et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2023c; Ji et al., 2025; Pei et al., 2025).

SLs orchestrate branch/tiller development by interacting with multiple factors within complex regulatory networks. For instance, in Arabidopsis, SLs inhibit polar auxin transport in the stem by rapidly reducing the polar localization of PIN1 and PIN3 (Bennett et al., 2006, 2016; Shinohara et al., 2013). This process depends on SMXL6, 7, and 8, although the underlying mechanism remains unclear (Soundappan et al., 2015; Liang et al., 2016). In rice, the primary SL‐responsive gene OsCKX9 encodes a cytokinin oxidase that catalyzes CK degradation and regulates tiller number, revealing a key node of crosstalk between SLs and CKs (Duan et al., 2019; Figure 2B). Sucrose promotes tiller development by inhibiting SL‐triggered D53 degradation (Patil et al., 2022; Figure 2B). In tomato, the transcription factor SPL13 mediates SL‐suppressed branching by repressing the CK biosynthesis gene IPT1 (Chen et al., 2023). Brassinosteroids (BRs) and SLs coordinately regulate branching and tillering in Arabidopsis and rice through interactions among SMXL6, 7, 8/D53 with BSE1/OsBZR1, DLT, and RLA1 (Wang et al., 2013; Fang et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2019a). Gibberellins (GAs) and SLs act synergistically to inhibit branching through the SLR1–OsMADS23‐D14 module (Li et al., 2025c).

In summary, SLs regulate branch number through a combination of conserved signaling pathways and species‐specific molecular modules (Figure 2B). However, the species‐specific mechanisms of SL‐regulated branching, particularly in non‐cereal crops, remain unclear. Furthermore, the molecular mechanisms underlying interactions between the SL pathway and other hormonal and environmental signaling pathways require further investigation.

Strigolactones promote branch angle

The branch angle is a key agronomic trait that influences light interception, lodging resistance, and planting density, thereby affecting crop yield (Wang and Li, 2008; Wang et al., 2022). Sang et al. (2014) first identified SLs as regulators of branch angle through suppressor screening of the extremely spreading rice lazy1 (la1) mutant (Li et al., 2007; Sang et al., 2014). Mutations in SL signaling genes (D3 and D14) or biosynthesis genes (D10, D17, and D27) largely restored the la1 phenotype, resulting in erect growth. This study shows that LA1 regulates asymmetric auxin distribution in the shoot base but is not involved in auxin biosynthesis. Instead, SLs promote tiller angle by inhibiting local auxin biosynthesis, which reduces asymmetric auxin distribution upon gravistimulation, and this SL‐mediated regulatory mechanism is independent of LA1 (Sang et al., 2014; Figure 2C).

This branch angle mechanism is evolutionarily conserved, as the SL‐deficient mutants in Arabidopsis (max4 and max2) also exhibit increased gravitropic responses in their hypocotyls and form branches that are more erect (Sang et al., 2014; Soundappan et al., 2015; Liang et al., 2016; Figure 2C). Consistent with this, the smxl678 triple mutant with enhanced SL signaling exhibits a slightly growth‐spreading phenotype (Soundappan et al., 2015; Liang et al., 2016), supporting the idea that SLs modulate branch angle via SMXL proteins. Interestingly, an SMXL7 variant with a disrupted EAR motif can still partially rescue branch angle defects (Liang et al., 2016), suggesting the presence of both EAR‐dependent and EAR‐independent regulatory mechanisms.

Key unanswered questions regarding branch angle regulation include the mechanistic basis of SL action via SMXL proteins, particularly the downstream targets of SMXL7 in EAR‐independent pathways. The interface between SL signaling and the core auxin‐dependent gravitropic framework remains poorly understood. It is unclear whether a mechanism similar to that observed in apical hook development, whereby auxin gradients direct differential growth within cells, also plays a role in SL‐mediated branch angle control (Cao et al., 2019). Furthermore, the manner in which environmental signals such as light and nutrients are integrated into SL‐mediated control of branch angle remains unclear.

STRIGOLACTONES AND STEM DEVELOPMENT

Strigolactones promote internode elongation

Plant height is a pivotal agronomic trait that influences crop yield by affecting lodging resistance, canopy structure, and photosynthetic efficiency (Peng et al., 1999). The success of the Green Revolution largely relied on the manipulation of GA pathways, as evidenced by the introduction of semi‐dwarf varieties of wheat and rice (Peng et al., 1999; Sasaki et al., 2002). In addition to GAs, other hormones, including SLs, contribute to height regulation. SL‐deficient mutants in Arabidopsis, rice, pea, and petunia exhibit a consistent dwarfism phenotype, which is reversible upon the application of rac‐GR24 (Beveridge et al., 1994, 1996, 2000; Napoli, 1996; Stirnberg et al., 2002, 2007; Turnbull et al., 2002; Sorefan et al., 2003; Booker et al., 2004, 2005; Foo et al., 2005; Ishikawa et al., 2005; Snowden et al., 2005; Johnson et al., 2006; Zou et al., 2006; Arite et al., 2007, 2009; Simons et al., 2007; Gao et al., 2009; Lin et al., 2009; Liu et al., 2009; Jiang et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2013; de Saint Germain et al., 2016). These studies support the conserved role of SLs in promoting internode elongation.

The mechanisms underlying SL‐mediated height control differ significantly across species. In rice, SL deficiency impairs GA biosynthesis and enhances GA inactivation, resulting in decreased levels of active GAs (Figure 3A). Mutants such as d14 and d17 exhibit reduced expression of GA biosynthetic genes and increased expression of GA catabolic genes (Zou et al., 2019). These mutants also exhibit reduced sensitivity to exogenous GAs, suggesting that SLs influence both GA homeostasis and signaling responsiveness, potentially by regulating the DELLA protein (Nakamura et al., 2013; Sun et al., 2023). In cotton, the SL signaling component Gossypium hirsutum D53 (GhD53) interacts with the transcription factor Gossypium hirsutum gibberellin‐regulating transcription factor (GhGARF) to suppress the expression of GA catabolizing genes (Figure 3B). Loss‐of‐function mutants of GhD53 increase active GA levels and promote cell elongation (Tian et al., 2024), revealing a GA‐dependent mechanism similar to that in rice. In contrast, pea employs a GA‐independent pathway. The additive dwarfism observed in rms1 le double mutants, the unchanged GA content in rms1, and the unaltered stability of the DELLA protein under rac‐GR24 treatment collectively suggest that SLs regulate internode elongation in pea by promoting cell division rather than through GA‐mediated cell elongation (de Saint Germain et al., 2013; Figure 3C).

SLs play a conserved role in promoting internode elongation

(A) In rice, SLs primarily depend on GA signaling to regulate shoot elongation. (B) In cotton, the SL signaling repressor GhD53 interacts with the transcription factor GhGARF to promote GA biosynthesis and increase plant height. (C) In pea, SLs regulate internode elongation independently of GA. GA, gibberellin; GhD53, Gossypium hirsutum D53; GhGARF, Gossypium hirsutum gibberellin‐regulating transcription factor; SLs, strigolactones.

The mechanistic differences between GA‐dependent pathways in rice and cotton and GA‐autonomous pathways in pea may reflect divergent evolutionary adaptations in monocots and dicots. Future comparative genomic studies may help identify conserved and species‐specific nodes in the SL‐regulated height network, offering novel strategies for optimizing plant height in crop breeding.

Strigolactones promote the secondary growth of stem

Secondary growth, driven by vascular cambium activity after primary growth, results in thickening of plant roots and stems (Caño‐Delgado et al., 2010; Miyashima et al., 2013; Collins et al., 2015). This process is not only crucial for the mechanical strength and stress resistance in crops but also determines wood production, which is the world's most vital renewable resource and carbon storage reservoir (Spicer and Groover, 2010; Miyashima et al., 2013; Bar‐On et al., 2018). SLs positively regulate secondary growth in Arabidopsis. Both SL biosynthesis mutants (max1, max3, and max4) and SL signaling mutant (max2) display significantly reduced vascular cambium activity and stem diameter. The application of SL analog rac‐GR24 strongly promotes cambium proliferation in both wild‐type and max1 plants, resembling cellular dynamics during secondary growth initiation (Agusti et al., 2011). Tissue‐specific complementation experiments demonstrated that the max2 phenotype can be rescued by MAX2 expression under the WOX4 promoter, but not under other cambium‐related promoters (Agusti et al., 2011), indicating that SL signaling specifically promotes cambium activity within vascular tissues. Cambial cell activity is bidirectionally regulated by BES1 and WOX4 in an antagonistic manner (Hu et al., 2022). WOX4 promotes cambium proliferation, and BES1 represses WOX4 transcription to facilitate xylem and phloem differentiation. SL signaling triggers BES1 degradation, thereby enhancing WOX4 expression and promoting cambial proliferation (Hu et al., 2022). Recent single‐nucleus RNA sequencing and histological analyses revealed increased vessel density and size in d14, max2, and max1, but not in brc1, suggesting that SLs inhibit the vessel differentiation within vascular tissues independently of the BRC1‐inhibited shoot branching pathway (Zhao et al., 2025). Furthermore, the expression of a dominant SL‐resistant SMXL7 variant promoted vessel formation from the cambium, and HD‐ZIP III transcription factors were identified as downstream targets of SMXL6, 7, and 8 that negatively regulate vessel differentiation (Zhao et al., 2025).

STRIGOLACTONES AND LEAF DEVELOPMENT AND SEEDLING MORPHOLOGY

Strigolactones regulate leaf shape and leaf senescence

Leaf morphological characteristics directly influence plant physiological processes and reflect adaptive strategies for resource acquisition (Tsukaya, 2006). In Arabidopsis, mutants defective in SL biosynthesis and signaling exhibit rounded leaves and shortened petioles. In contrast, the SL signaling‐enhanced mutant smxl678 exhibits narrower leaves and completely suppresses the rounded‐leaf phenotype of max3, indicating that the SMXL6, 7, and 8 proteins play crucial roles in leaf elongation (Booker et al., 2004, 2005; Scaffidi et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2015). Further studies have identified TCP1 as an early SL‐responsive gene that functions downstream of SMXL6, 7, and 8, providing a direct molecular link between SL signaling and leaf shape (Wang et al., 2020a; Figure 4A). Additionally, in Medicago truncatula, SL biosynthesis mutants (Mtccd7 and Mtccd8) and the signaling mutant Mtd14 all exhibit reduced leaf margin serration. Exogenous rac‐GR24 restored serration in SL‐deficient mutants in a dose‐dependent manner but had no effect on the signaling mutants (Lauressergues et al., 2015). Taken together, these studies indicate that SLs regulate the leaf shape and margin development.

SL regulation of leaf shape and senescence

(A) SLs promote leaf elongation in Arabidopsis by facilitating the formation of the D14–SCFMAX2–SMXL6 complex, which leads to ubiquitin‐mediated degradation of SMXL6 and subsequent release of TCP1 expression. (B, C) SLs regulate senescence through two pathways. In darkness (B), ethylene biosynthesis increases, promoting senescence signaling and upregulating the SL biosynthetic genes MAX3 and MAX4. SL signaling further enhances ethylene‐induced leaf senescence in the dark. In age‐dependent senescence (C), the age‐related miR156‐SPL module activates SPL9/15, which directly upregulates MAX3 and MAX4 to drive SL accumulation. SL perception by the AtD14–MAX2 complex then mediates ubiquitination and degradation of the SPX1/2 repressors, relieving the inhibition of WRKY75 and activating SA accumulation and senescence.

Leaf senescence is a programmed developmental process influenced by internal factors, such as age and flowering status, and external stimuli, including darkness, nutrient deficiency, and biotic and abiotic stresses (Lim et al., 2007). In Arabidopsis, SLs positively regulate senescence in the dark. This is evidenced by the extended leaf longevity observed in SL pathway mutants (max1, max2/ore9, max3, max4, and Atd14), as well as by the results of grafting experiments, which confirm a promotion of shoot senescence by root‐derived SLs (Woo et al., 2001; Ueda and Kusaba, 2015). Darkness stimulates ethylene (ET) biosynthesis, which upregulates the SL biosynthetic genes MAX3 and MAX4. Genetic evidence indicates that ET promotes senescence through both SL‐dependent and SL‐independent pathways (Ueda and Kusaba, 2015; Figure 4B). Similar delayed‐senescence phenotypes in rice SL mutants and responses to rac‐GR24 demonstrate the conservation of this mechanism (Yamada et al., 2014).

Recent molecular studies have elucidated the integration of SL signaling with age‐dependent senescence pathways. The miR156–SPL module establishes an age‐related transcriptional cascade in which SPL9/15 directly activates SL biosynthetic genes (MAX3 and MAX4), promoting age‐dependent SL accumulation (Wu and Poethig, 2006; Jing et al., 2024). Downstream SL perception involves the AtD14–MAX2 complex, which mediates the ubiquitination and degradation of SPX1/2 repressors (Figure 4C). This degradation relieves the inhibition of the transcription factor WRKY75 by SPX1 and SPX2, thereby enabling the activation of the salicylic acid (SA) biosynthesis gene SID2 and subsequent SA accumulation (Jing et al., 2024). Thus, the SPX1/2–WRKY75–SID2 module establishes a direct molecular link between age‐dependent SL signaling and SA‐mediated senescence initiation (Figure 4C).

Strigolactones regulate hypocotyl/mesocotyl development

Elongation of the hypocotyl (in Arabidopsis) and mesocotyl (in rice) is essential for seedling emergence and is tightly regulated by darkness and hormone signals (de Wit et al., 2016; Zhan et al., 2019; Krahmer and Fankhauser, 2024; Guo et al., 2025). SLs act as central integrators in this process, engaging in complex interactions with the karrikin (KAR), CK, and BR signaling pathways (Hu et al., 2014; Jia et al., 2014; Sun et al., 2018b; Wang et al., 2020b; Zheng et al., 2020).

In Arabidopsis, both SLs and the related smoke‐derived compound KARs regulate hypocotyl elongation through shared signaling components. The perception of SLs by D14 promotes the association between D14 and the E3 ligase complex SCFMAX2, which promotes the degradation of SMXL2 and SMAX1, thereby repressing hypocotyl elongation in a MAX2‐dependent manner (Wang et al., 2020b; Li et al., 2022). Similarly, the perception of KAR by KARRIKIN INSENSITIVE 2 (KAI2) recruits the SCFMAX2 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex to target SMAX1 and SMXL2 for degradation, thereby suppressing hypocotyl elongation (Nelson et al., 2012; Chang et al., 2025; Figure 5A).

SLs regulate hypocotyl and mesocotyl elongation by integrating with KAR, CK, and BR signaling in Arabidopsis and rice

KARs promote the association of KAI2 with SMXL2 and MAX2, while SLs induce the AtD14–MAX2–SMXL2 complex, both leading to SMXL2 degradation. SMXL2 is a shared proteolytic target in both pathways. (B) In rice, SLs and KARs inhibit mesocotyl elongation via the D14–D3–D53 and D14L–D3–OsSMAX1 pathways, respectively. In rice, both SL biosynthesis mutants (d10, d17, and d27) and signaling mutants (d3 and d14) exhibit significantly longer mesocotyls in darkness, mainly due to a three‐ to sixfold increase in cell number (Hu et al., 2010, 2014). The phenotype is rescued by rac‐GR24 in biosynthesis but not in signaling mutants, confirming that SLs inhibit mesocotyl elongation by suppressing cell division through a D14‐dependent pathway (Hu et al., 2010). Genetic dissection revealed that mesocotyl elongation is primarily regulated by the KAR‐signaling module (D14L–D3–OsSMAX1), whereas tillering is controlled by the canonical SL‐signaling module (D14–D3–D53) (Zheng et al., 2020). The specific stereoisomers GR245DS (SL‐active) and GR24ent‐5DS (KAR‐active) inhibit elongation through their respective pathways, with additive effects when combined (Zheng et al., 2020; Figure 5B).

The function of SLs also overlaps with that of other hormones. For example, CKs promote mesocotyl elongation, but SLs antagonize this effect partly through the class II TCP transcription factor OsTCP5 (Hu et al., 2014). SLs increase OsTCP5 expression, whereas CKs decrease it. The overexpression of OsTCP5 attenuates CK‐induced mesocotyl elongation (Figure 5B). BR signaling also interacts with SLs via the OsGSK2‐CYC U2–D3 module. Natural variations in the BR signaling repressor OsGSK2 affect mesocotyl length, with high‐activity OsGSK2 phosphorylating the cell division protein CYC U2, enabling its recognition and degradation by F‐box D3 in an SL‐dependent manner (Sun et al., 2018b; Figure 5B).

Collectively, these findings establish SLs as key regulators of hypocotyl/mesocotyl development, integrating multiple hormonal and environmental signals through both conserved and species‐specific mechanisms (Figure 5).

STRIGOLACTONES AND PLANT FLOWERING

Flowering time is a crucial adaptive trait shaped by multiple environmental and endogenous cues, including photoperiod, vernalization, temperature, and hormonal signals (Michaels, 2009; Quiroz et al., 2021). SLs are important regulators of flowering and play species‐specific roles.

In Arabidopsis, for instance, mutants impaired in SL biosynthesis (max4) or signaling (Atd14) exhibit significantly earlier flowering than wild‐type plants, indicating a repressive role for SLs in this context (Zhang et al., 2019; Bai et al., 2024). Interestingly, SLs appear to regulate flowering through distinct, concentration‐dependent mechanisms. One study revealed that, although exogenous application of high concentrations of SLs primarily accelerates flowering by reducing melatonin (MT) content, endogenous low concentrations of SLs largely repress flowering by downregulating the expression of key flowering promoters such as SPL3, 4, and 9 (Zhang et al., 2019). Recent evidence has revealed that Target of Eat1 (TOE1), a transcription factor belonging to the AP2 family, is a pivotal component of endogenous SL‐mediated flowering repression. In the SL deficiency mutants, the function of TOE1 was repressed in two manners: protein degradation of TOE1 and sequestration of TOE1 by the SMXL proteins. In wild‐type plants, the SL receptor AtD14 binds and stabilizes TOE1 in an SL‐dependent manner, thereby preventing its proteasomal degradation and enhancing its inhibitory function on flowering (Bai et al., 2024). Meanwhile, SLs triggered the degradation of SMXL6, 7, and 8, relieving their sequestration of TOE1 and enabling TOE1 to directly inhibit the transcription of Flower Locus T (FT) (Corbesier et al., 2007; Bai et al., 2024; Figure 6A).

The role of SLs in flowering time regulation is species‐dependent

(A) In Arabidopsis, SLs act as floral repressors, largely by inhibiting the expression of florigen genes such as FT. (B) In tomato, SLs promote flowering through pathways that involve upregulating the florigen gene SINGLE FLOWER TRUSS (SFT). Black arrows and red blunt‐ended arrows indicate positive and negative regulation, respectively.

In tomato, SLs modulate flowering time through divergent mechanisms. Silencing of CCD7 and CCD8 delays flowering. This effect can be rescued either by grafting with wild‐type rootstocks or by treatment with the synthetic SL analog GR245DS, both of which promote flowering and are accompanied by increased florigen gene expression (Kohlen et al., 2012; Visentin et al., 2024). SLs also promote flowering in tomato by inducing the accumulation of miR319, which accelerates the degradation of LANCEOLATE (LA) transcripts to induce flowering (Visentin et al., 2024). LA is a flowering repressor that primarily acts by suppressing the florigen gene SINGLE FLOWER TRUSS (SFT) and modulating the GA pathway (Silva et al., 2019; Visentin et al., 2024; Figure 6B).

The complexity of SL‐mediated flowering regulation is further illustrated in petunia, where different SL‐related dad mutants exhibit different flowering phenotypes. The dad1 mutant shows late flowering, whereas dad2 and dad3 resemble the wild type (Snowden et al., 2005). Under short‐day conditions, SL‐deficient petunia exhibits enhanced photoperiod sensitivity, suggesting that SLs may coordinate flowering and architecture in response to day length (Snowden et al., 2005).

Notably, the roles of SLs in plant architecture can be uncoupled from those in flowering time in certain agronomic contexts. For example, in Brassica napus, CRISPR‐edited BnD14 mutants exhibit ideal plant architecture, with increased branching and reduced plant height, while retaining wild‐type flowering time and crop yield (Stanic et al., 2021), highlighting both conserved and species‐specific roles for SLs in flowering regulation. Future work aimed at elucidating these diverse regulatory networks will be essential for establishing molecular frameworks to guide precision breeding in crops.

STRIGOLACTONES AND ROOT DEVELOPMENT

Plant root systems play a critical role in the uptake of water and nutrients, anchorage, and hormone biosynthesis (Coudert et al., 2010; Petricka et al., 2012; Rellán‐Álvarez et al., 2016; Ötvös and Benková, 2017). Dicots (e.g., Arabidopsis) develop taproot systems comprising persistent primary roots and lateral branches, whereas monocots (e.g., rice) form fibrous systems dominated by adventitious roots following seminal root senescence. Adventitious roots emerge from non‐root tissues, such as the Arabidopsis root‐hypocotyl junction and rice crown roots (Coudert et al., 2010; Petricka et al., 2012). Recent studies have demonstrated the multiple roles of SLs in root development.

In Arabidopsis, SL biosynthesis or signaling mutants (max1, max2, and max4) exhibit shortened primary roots, reduced cortex cell numbers, and increased lateral root density (Agusti et al., 2011). The inhibitory effect of SLs on lateral roots depends on the signaling components Arabidopsis Histidine Kinase 3 (AHK3) and Arabidopsis Response Regulator 1 (ARR1)/ARR12, which are involved in the regulation of auxin transport (Jiang et al., 2016; Figure 7A). Interestingly, the loss of Short Hypocotyl 2 (SHY2), a key regulator of auxin and CKs, abolished the ability of SLs to regulate lateral roots (Tian et al., 2002; Knox et al., 2003; Koren et al., 2013; Li et al., 2020a), indicating that SHY2 plays a pivotal role in the interaction SL, CK and auxin signaling pathways (Figure 7A). Moreover, the transcription factors Monopteros 5 (TMO5) and TMO5‐Like1 negatively regulate the SL‐mediated inhibition of lateral root density, whereas HY5 and MYB12 mediate SL‐induced flavanol accumulation to promote root hair elongation (Struk et al., 2022; Figure 7A).

Molecular mechanisms of SL‐regulated root development in Arabidopsis and rice

(A) In Arabidopsis, SLs regulate root development through a network involving MYB12, HY5, TMO5, and SHY2. (B) In rice, SLs regulate lateral root density, root elongation, and crown root number via CRL1, OsPIN1, and OsSHR1. Black arrows and red blunt‐ended arrows indicate the positive and negative regulation, respectively.

Studies on rice have revealed that the crown root length of d10 and d14 mutants is significantly shorter than that of the wild type. The shortened crown root phenotype in d10 can be restored by the application of rac‐GR24 (Arite et al., 2012; Sun et al., 2015). A recent study revealed that OsSHR1 directly binds to the promoters of OsSWEET2a/4/16 (sugar transporter genes), thereby promoting their expression and regulating sugar allocation (Feng et al., 2025). This process promotes root elongation and decreases crown root number (Figure 7B). SLs promote nitric oxide (NO)‐induced elongation of the seminal root by triggering the degradation of D53, thereby activating the IPA1/OsSPL14‐mediated expression of OsPIN1b and modulating auxin transport (Sun et al., 2016, 2021; Figure 7B). Notably, Crown Rootless1 (CRL1) has recently been identified as an early SL‐responsive gene in roots that restrains lateral root density (Inukai et al., 2005; Yuan et al., 2023; Figure 7B).

Furthermore, under dark conditions, pea ccd7 and ccd8 mutants produced fewer adventitious roots than the wild type (Urquhart et al., 2015). In apple, SLs inhibit the formation of adventitious roots via two pathways. One of these involves the degradation of MdORE1 by Md‐miR164b. The other involves MdSMXL7 interacting with MdWRKY6 to activate MdBRC1 transcription, thereby inducing the expression of MdGH3.1 and inhibiting auxin signaling and adventitious root formation (Fan et al., 2022, 2023). In grapevine, VvMAX2 interacts directly with the lateral organ boundary proteins VvLOB and VvLBD19, thus positively regulating lateral root density (Xu et al., 2024).

In summary, SLs play a pivotal role in regulating various aspects of root system architecture, including primary root growth, lateral root formation, root hair elongation, and adventitious root development (Figure 7). The underlying mechanisms are both conserved and species‐specific, providing a vital theoretical basis for improving root system architecture in crops.

STRIGOLACTONES AND NUTRIENT UTILIZATION

Strigolactones regulate plant acclimation to phosphorus deficiency

Phosphorus is an essential macronutrient for plants, playing a crucial role in key physiological processes such as energy metabolism and nucleic acid synthesis. However, most of the phosphorus in soil exists in insoluble forms, and the scarcity of available phosphate (Pi) is one of the main environmental factors limiting crop growth (Paz‐Ares et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2026). In response to low Pi stress, plants have evolved various mechanisms, including transcriptional reprogramming, root architecture remodeling, and enhancement of phosphorus absorption, transport, and utilization (Paz‐Ares et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2026).

Low Pi strongly promotes SL biosynthesis and secretion in various plant species, such as rice, maize, wheat, tomatoes, and sorghum (Yoneyama et al., 2007, 2012; López‐Ráez et al., 2008; Umehara et al., 2010). The mechanism underlying low Pi‐enhanced SL biosynthesis has been revealed in rice (Yuan et al., 2023). Low Pi activates the Oryza sativa phosphorus‐starvation response (OsPHR2) transcription factor, which directly promotes the expression of Nodulation Signaling Pathway 1 (NSP1) and NSP2 (Yuan et al., 2023). NSP1 and NSP2 then form a heterodimer that recognizes specific cis‐elements in the promoter regions of SL biosynthetic genes (D27, D17, and D10), significantly enhancing the transcriptional activity of these genes and ultimately promoting SL biosynthesis (Liu et al., 2011; Yuan et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2026; Figure 8A).

SLs facilitate plant adaptation to phosphorus and nitrogen starvation

(A) Low phosphorus stress induces the assembly of the D14–SDEL1–OsSPX4 complex, promoting OsSPX4 degradation and subsequent nuclear translocation of OsPHR2, which activates the transcription of NSP1 and NSP2. The NSP1–NSP2 complex binds to the promoters of SL biosynthesis genes and enhances their expression, leading to elevated SL levels that suppress tillering, optimize root architecture, and coordinate nitrogen and phosphorus uptake in rice. (B) Low nitrogen stress increases phosphorylation of the SL receptor D14 at its N‐terminal domain, thereby stabilizing the protein and prolonging signal perception. Concurrently, low nitrogen promotes SL biosynthesis and enhances D53 degradation, which enables GRF4 and IPA1 to activate downstream genes. These processes enhance nitrogen acquisition, root growth, and tiller regulation during nitrogen deficiency. Black arrows and red blunt‐ended arrows indicate positive and negative regulation, respectively.

Enhanced SL biosynthesis under low Pi conditions plays a pivotal role in promoting AM fungal symbiosis (Akiyama et al., 2005; Kretzschmar et al., 2012; Cope et al., 2022; Ge et al., 2022; Paz‐Ares et al., 2022; Meng et al., 2025). SLs were first identified as branching factors for AM fungi under low Pi conditions (Akiyama et al., 2005). Low Pi‐induced SL biosynthesis promotes the release of short‐chain chitin oligomers (CO4 and CO5) from AM fungi, thereby enhancing the expression of symbiotic genes and suppressing immune responses (Tan et al., 2025). SLs induced by low Pi also regulate root hair density, root length, and lateral root development to optimize phosphorus absorption. For instance, SLs promote root hair development by increasing the expression of the auxin receptor TIR1 in Arabidopsis (Mayzlish‐Gati et al., 2012). Tomato SL‐deficient mutants exhibit reduced total root length and decreased phosphorus absorption efficiency under low Pi conditions (Santoro et al., 2020). Overexpression of the SL transporter gene PDR1 in petunia enhances lateral root formation and root hair elongation (Kretzschmar et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2018). In rice, low Pi‐induced SL biosynthesis promotes seminal root elongation and reduces lateral root density, depending on genes such as CRL1 and OsPIN1b (Sun et al., 2018a; Yuan et al., 2023). Low Pi also inhibits shoot branching in multiple species, thereby reducing nutrient consumption (Xi et al., 2015; Wen et al., 2016; de Souza Campos et al., 2019; Sigalas et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2023). In rice, this process is mediated by the NSP1/NSP2–SL–D14 module, which upregulates OsTB1 expression to inhibit tiller bud outgrowth (Yuan et al., 2023; Figure 8A).

SL signaling integrates directly with phosphorus signaling networks. Under Pi sufficiency, the OsSPX4 protein interacts with OsPHR2, thereby preventing its nuclear import and inhibiting the expression of phosphorus starvation‐induced genes in rice (Paz‐Ares et al., 2022; Meng et al., 2025). Under low‐Pi conditions, inositol pyrophosphate (IP) levels decrease, resulting in complex dissociation, the nuclear import of OsPHR2, and the activation of phosphorus starvation responses (Paz‐Ares et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2023; Meng et al., 2025). Low‐Pi‐induced SL accumulation enhances the interaction between the SL receptor D14 and the ubiquitin E3 ligase SDEL1, resulting in the formation of a D14–SDEL1–OsSPX4 complex that promotes OsSPX4 degradation (Ruan et al., 2019; Gu et al., 2023). This process facilitates the nuclear import and activation of additional OsPHR2 proteins, thereby promoting the expression of phosphorus starvation‐induced genes (Figure 8A).

Strigolactones regulate plant acclimation to low nitrogen

Nitrogen is a fundamental component of essential biological macromolecules, such as proteins and nucleic acids (Wang et al., 2018b; Liu et al., 2022b). A shortage of nitrogen markedly constrains plant physiological performance and agricultural productivity (Liu et al., 2022b). The promotion of SL biosynthesis by low nitrogen levels exhibits species‐specific variation. In Chinese milk vetch, marigold, lettuce, rice, wheat, and sorghum, both nitrogen deficiency and phosphorus deficiency enhance SL production (Yoneyama et al., 2007, 2012, 2013; Sigalas et al., 2023). However, legumes such as alfalfa and red clover only respond to low phosphorus, with low nitrogen having no significant effect (Yoneyama et al., 2007, 2012, 2013). This difference may be related to the prioritization of nitrogen versus phosphorus demand and the metabolic characteristics of plants.

SLs increase the efficiency of nitrogen acquisition and optimize resource allocation by coordinating nutrient uptake, root system morphology, and shoot architecture (Hu et al., 2026). Under low nitrogen conditions, SL accumulation in rice promotes the degradation of D53 to release the transcription factor growth‐regulating factor 4 (GRF4), which promotes and integrates nitrogen assimilation, carbon fixation, and growth (Li et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2023). GRF4 activates the expression of genes involved in nitrogen metabolism and transport (e.g., OsAMT1.1, OsGS1.2, and OsNRT1.1B). Concurrently, low nitrogen induces the protein degradation of DELLA through a D14‐ and D53‐dependent mechanism, further relieving the inhibition of GRF4 activity and enhancing nitrogen assimilation (Nakamura et al., 2013; Sun et al., 2023). This synergistic increase in nitrogen uptake and assimilation significantly improves plant acclimation to low nitrogen (Figure 8B).

SLs also mediate the remodeling of root system architecture in response to low nitrogen conditions. SL signaling integrates with nitric oxide (NO) signaling to promote seminal root elongation and lateral root development (Sun et al., 2016). Furthermore, the SL‐triggered degradation of D53 releases SPL14/17 transcription factors, which regulate PIN1b transcription and consequently control auxin transport and root growth in response to nitrate supply (Sun et al., 2021).

Branch suppression under low nitrogen levels is largely governed by SL accumulation and intensified signaling, highlighting the intricate crosstalk between SLs and other phytohormonal pathways. SLs interact with the auxin and CK pathways to inhibit axillary bud outgrowth, which plays an essential role in response to low nitrogen (Domagalska and Leyser, 2011; de Jong et al., 2014; Hou et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021). Low nitrogen inhibits the expression of Nitrogen‐mediated Tiller Growth Response 5 (NGR5), thereby elevating the expression of SL signaling genes such as D14 and IPA1/OsSPL14, which inhibit the elongation of tiller buds (Wu et al., 2020). Low nitrogen also promotes the expression of the OsTCP19 transcription factor, which suppresses the BR signal response and coordinates tiller regulation with SL signaling (Fang et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021a). Further in‐depth study demonstrates that low nitrogen enhances SL perception by phosphorylating the N‐terminal domain of the SL receptor D14 in rice (Hu et al., 2024a). This post‐translational modification stabilizes the D14 protein, prolongs the duration of SL signaling, and ultimately restrains tiller development (Figure 8B). The reversibility of this phosphorylation and the phosphatases and kinases responsible warrant further investigation.

REGULATORY ROLES AND MECHANISMS OF STRIGOLACTONES IN PLANT ABIOTIC STRESS RESPONSES

Strigolactones regulate plant acclimation to drought

Drought is a severe abiotic stressor that limits global agricultural production and ecological stability (Zhang et al., 2025c). SLs have emerged as key phytohormones involved in the regulation of plant drought tolerance. In Arabidopsis, genetic evidence strongly supports a positive role for SLs in drought responses. Mutants defective in SL biosynthesis (max3 and max4) or signaling (max2 and Atd14) exhibit increased drought sensitivity, characterized by wider stomatal apertures and a higher rate of leaf water loss (Bu et al., 2014; Ha et al., 2014). The enhanced SL signaling mutant smxl678 exhibits significantly increased drought tolerance, characterized by a decreased leaf stomatal index, increased cuticle permeability, increased anthocyanin biosynthesis, and heightened sensitivity to ABA (Li et al., 2020b; Wang et al., 2020a; Yang et al., 2020; Figure 9A). Interestingly, the smax1smxl2 double mutant exhibits superior growth under osmotic stress compared to the wild type, indicating that SLs and karrikins coordinately regulate drought acclimation in Arabidopsis (Li et al., 2022).

Effects of SLs on different abiotic stresses in Arabidopsis and tomato

(A–D) Strigolactones regulate plant acclimation to drought (A), salinity (B), cold (C), and heat (D) stresses. Black arrows and red blunt‐ended arrows indicate the positive and negative regulation, respectively.

The molecular mechanisms underlying SL‐mediated drought tolerance involve multiple regulatory layers (Figure 9A). SMXL6, 7, and 8 function as transcription factors that directly bind to the promoter of the ABA‐response gene SnRK2.3, thereby negatively regulating ABA signaling (Lian et al., 2023). SLs induce stomatal closure by integrating ABA‐dependent and ABA‐independent pathways (Bu et al., 2014; Lv et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2019). Furthermore, SLs can establish drought memory, enabling faster defense responses upon recurrent stress (Korwin Krukowski et al., 2023). Interestingly, a recent study has revealed that SL signaling also limits xylem vessel formation, representing a strategy that reduces water loss through transpiration under drought conditions (Zhao et al., 2025).

In crops, exogenous application of rac‐GR24 significantly improves drought tolerance in wheat, grapevine, soybean and maize (Sedaghat et al., 2017, 2021; Min et al., 2019; Guo et al., 2023; Song et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023; Cao et al., 2024; Qi et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025). Notably, the function of SLs in drought tolerance displays species specificity. For instance, SLs act as negative regulators of drought tolerance in rice, and mutants such as d10, d17, d3, and d14 exhibit greater drought resilience than the wild type (Haider et al., 2018), underscoring the context‐dependent nature of SL‐mediated responses.

Strigolactones regulate plant acclimation to salt stress

Salt stress inhibits plant growth and reduces yield by disrupting ion balance, osmotic regulation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and photosynthesis. SLs play crucial roles in plant salt tolerance. Compared to the wild type, mutants with defects in SL biosynthesis (max3 and max4) or signaling (max2) exhibit significantly lower survival rates under salt stress (Ha et al., 2014), suggesting that SLs positively regulate salt tolerance in Arabidopsis (Figure 9B). In crops such as rice, wheat, maize, tomato, chili pepper, and Malus hupehensis, rac‐GR24 application enhances salt tolerance through multiple interconnected mechanisms (Ling et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022a; Ma et al., 2022; Jariani et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025b; Yuce et al., 2025). These include: (1) maintaining intracellular K+/Na+ homeostasis; (2) promoting proline accumulation for osmotic adjustment; (3) upregulating the expression of genes encoding antioxidant enzymes (e.g., SOD, POD, CAT, and APX) to strengthen oxidative defense; and (4) increasing the chlorophyll content and the net photosynthetic rate to sustain photosynthesis under stress (Figure 9B).

SLs also integrate multiple stress‐responsive pathways to enhance salt tolerance. In tomato and cucumber, SLs interact synergistically with nitric oxide (NO) to upregulate the expression of genes encoding photosynthetic pigments and antioxidants, thereby reducing oxidative damage (Liu et al., 2022a). Furthermore, SLs increase salt stress acclimation by modulating phospholipid signaling to increase levels of second messengers, such as IP3, PIP2, DAG, and PA, thereby strengthening the responses to salt stress in tomato (Lu et al., 2025).

Strigolactones regulate plant acclimation to heavy metal stress

Soil contamination by heavy metals such as cadmium (Cd), arsenic (As), and aluminum (Al) threatens agricultural productivity and ecosystem stability. SLs mitigate heavy metal toxicity through multi‐layered, species‐specific mechanisms. In cadmium‐stressed plants, rac‐GR24 alleviates toxicity by modulating nutrient uptake and oxidative defense in barley, switchgrass, Artemisia annua and Brassica napus (Tai et al., 2017; Qiu et al., 2021; Wani et al., 2023; Ali et al., 2025). Arsenic enters rice as arsenate via phosphate transporters; SLs reduce its uptake and enhance its vacuolar compartmentalization in roots, thereby alleviating oxidative damage in shoots (Mostofa et al., 2021). In apple (Malus domestica Borkh.), rac‐GR24 alleviates aluminum chloride stress by upregulating MdWRKY53, which increases the expression of MdPMEI45 and MdALS3. This limits the entry of aluminum ions into the cell wall and promotes the sequestration of chloride ions in vacuoles (Zhang et al., 2024c).

Strigolactones regulate plant acclimation to high temperature

High‐temperature stress severely hinders plant growth and development, thereby reducing crop productivity. A 1°C increase in global temperature could reduce the yield of major food crops by 3%–8% (Hu et al., 2024b). SLs regulate physiological responses to high temperatures, alleviating negative effects such as photosynthetic damage, water loss, and cellular injury by modulating phenotype, metabolism and gene expression.

In Arabidopsis, mutants defective in SL biosynthesis (max1, max3, and max4) or signaling (max2 and Atd14) exhibit increased heat sensitivity, with reduced germination and seedling survival (Toh et al., 2012; Li et al., 2023b). The thermosensitive phenotypes of max1 were rescued by rac‐GR24 or GR244DO, but not those of the max2 or Atd14 mutants (Toh et al., 2012; Li et al., 2023b), which confirms the requirement for SL signal transduction. Under heat stress, AtMYBS1 expression is suppressed, releasing the direct inhibition of AtMYBS1 on MAX1 transcription and thereby promoting SL biosynthesis to enhance thermotolerance (Li et al., 2023b; Figure 9C). In tomato, high temperature upregulates the expression of SL biosynthesis genes (CCD7, CCD8, and MAX1) and signaling gene MAX2, and further activates the ABA pathway to enhance the expression of heat shock protein HSP70 (Chi et al., 2021; Figure 9C).

At the physiological level, high‐temperature acclimation in plants is enhanced by SLs through the regulation of stomatal movement and the antioxidant system. The SL‐deficient mutants in tomato exhibit increased stomatal conductance and accelerated water loss at high temperature, whereas SL treatment alleviates these responses to heat stress (Chi et al., 2021). SL treatment also enhances the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, APX, and GR in tomato and tall fescue, thus reducing ROS accumulation and maintaining cell membrane integrity to alleviate oxidative damage (Hu et al., 2019b; Chi et al., 2021; Figure 9C).

Strigolactones regulate plant acclimation to low temperature

Low‐temperature stress is a key environmental factor that limits plant growth, development, and distribution (Ding and Yang, 2022). SLs positively regulate cold tolerance by activating cold response signaling pathways, promoting cell water retention, and enhancing antioxidant defense and photosystem stability.

In Arabidopsis, mutants defective in SL biosynthesis (max1, max3, and max4) or signaling (max2 and Atd14) exhibit reduced freezing tolerance and reduced survival rates, as well as increased ion leakage (Cooper et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2023b). Exogenously applying the SL analog GR245DS restores freezing tolerance of SL biosynthesis mutants but not signaling mutants, indicating that SLs positively regulate cold tolerance (Wang et al., 2023b). WRKY41 negatively regulates plant freezing tolerance by directly suppressing the expression of C‐Repeat Binding Factor/Dre Binding Factor 1 (CBF/DREB1), a core component of cold acclimation. SLs trigger the ubiquitination and degradation of WRKY41, thereby activating the CBF‐dependent cold tolerance pathway (Wang et al., 2023b; Figure 9D). Furthermore, the SL‐triggered ubiquitination and degradation of SMXL6, 7, and 8 can enhance anthocyanin accumulation and contribute to cold tolerance in Arabidopsis (Li et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2020a, 2023b; Figure 9D). In tomato, cold stress induces SL biosynthesis, and the silencing of CCD7, CCD8, MAX1, or MAX2 increases cold sensitivity (Chi et al., 2021). GR245DS alleviates cold‐induced damage in wild‐type and ccd mutant plants, primarily by promoting ABA accumulation (Chi et al., 2021; Figure 9D). SLs also elevate cold tolerance in tomato by raising the HY5 protein level, which promotes the expression of the Autophagy‐Related Gene 18a (ATG18a) to break down cold‐induced denatured and unfolded proteins (Chi et al., 2025; Figure 9D). In apple (Malus × domestica), the interaction between Golden2‐like 1 (MdGLK1) and Brassinazole‐resistant 1 (MdBZR1) enhances cold tolerance by promoting the expression of MdCBF1/2. SLs positively regulate the cold response by degrading MdSMXL8, thereby alleviating its inhibitory effect on the MdGLK1–MdBZR1 complex (Zhang et al., 2025b).

These molecular mechanisms enable SLs to effectively alleviate oxidative damage and photosynthetic inhibition under low temperatures. SLs increase the accumulation of proline and soluble sugars, thereby improving cell water retention and mitigating dehydration injury (Zhang et al., 2020; Omoarelojie et al., 2021). SLs also enhance the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, APX, POD and CAT, reduce the accumulation of H2O2 and O2 −, and lower membrane lipid peroxidation and relative electrolyte leakage (Zhang et al., 2020). These effects maintain cell membrane integrity and enhance plant survival capacity in low‐temperature environments (Figure 9D). Furthermore, SL treatment alleviates the decline in photosystem II efficiency caused by low temperatures in tomato, pepper, mung bean and rapeseed, as evidenced by an increased net photosynthetic rate, an enhanced xanthophyll cycle, and the dissipation of excess excitation energy (Omoarelojie et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024a).

Strigolactones regulate plant acclimation to high light and low light

Both low‐ and high‐light stress can impair physiological function and inhibit plant growth (Shi et al., 2022). Low light is one of the major environmental factors influencing the cultivation of vegetables, such as tomatoes and cucumbers, in greenhouse during the winter and spring. Exogenous application of rac‐GR24 alleviates growth inhibition, improves photosynthetic performance, and reduces oxidative stress in tomato, cucumber, and tall fescue under low light conditions (Lu et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2022; Gong et al., 2024).

High light, which exceeds a plant's optimal photosynthetic capacity, leads to the accumulation of ROS, including hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), superoxide anion radicals (O2 −), and singlet oxygen (1O2). This results in damage to both the photosystem and overall plant growth. Plants adapt to this through non‐photochemical quenching (NPQ) and by adjusting the composition of their photosynthetic apparatus (Shi et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024b). In Arabidopsis, SLs mitigate photodamage under high light conditions by upregulating core photosystem proteins and regulating the balance of chlorophyll and carotenoids (Thula et al., 2023).

REGULATORY ROLES AND MECHANISMS OF STRIGOLACTONES IN PLANT BIOTIC STRESS RESISTANCE

The roles and mechanisms of SLs in plant responses to different pathogens are emerging as hot research topics. In fungal disease models, silencing the SL biosynthesis genes GbCCD7 and GbCCD8b in island cotton (Gossypium barbadense) increased the susceptibility to Verticillium wilt, a disease caused by Verticillium dahliae; consistent with this, rac‐GR24 application enhances resistance (Yi et al., 2023). SLs positively regulate resistance to Verticillium wilt by interacting with the jasmonic acid (JA) and ABA signaling pathways and inducing ROS accumulation (Yi et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2025). With respect to the necrotrophic fungus Botrytis cinerea, rac‐GR24 application significantly enhances resistance in Arabidopsis, tobacco, and grapevine (Fujita et al., 2024; Vogel et al., 2023). The role of SLs in rice blast resistance (caused by Magnaporthe oryzae) appears to be context‐dependent, with reports of both positive and negative regulation. One study revealed that SL‐deficient or signaling mutants (d17 and d14) were more susceptible to Magnaporthe oryzae, whereas another study found that the d10 and d14 mutants displayed increased resistance (Nasir et al., 2019; Lahari et al., 2024). SLs also positively affect rice blast resistance. The SL signaling repressor D53 protein reduces blast resistance by decreasing the expression of the phenylalanine ammonia‐lyase gene and lignin production (Ye et al., 2024).

SLs also play an important role in bacterial diseases. Mutants with deficiency in SL biosynthesis (max3 and max4) or signaling (max2 and Atd14) are markedly more susceptible to Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000 (Kalliola et al., 2020; Kusajima et al., 2022). rac‐GR24 enhances bacterial resistance in both wild‐type and SL biosynthesis mutant (Kalliola et al., 2020; Kusajima et al., 2022), indicating that SLs positively regulate resistance to Pseudomonas syringae. Further research has revealed that the callose formation, SA content, and stomatal conductance are vital components of SL‐mediated bacterial resistance in Arabidopsis (Zhao et al., 2024).

In plant–virus interactions, SLs exhibit context‐dependent regulatory roles. During the tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) infection in Nicotiana benthamiana, the application of exogenous SLs compromised resistance, whereas the SL biosynthesis inhibitors and the silencing of SL biosynthesis genes (NtCCD7 and NtCCD8) enhanced resistance. Transcriptome analyses suggest that SLs probably suppress antiviral defense by interfering with hormone signaling homeostasis (Huang et al., 2024). A recent study in rice revealed that SL signaling is essential for broad‐spectrum antiviral immunity against rice grassy stunt virus (RGSV) and multiple reoviruses (Yang et al., 2026). SL signaling promotes the expression of the transcription factor ONAC131, thereby facilitating the activation of RNAi‐mediated antiviral defenses. The RGSV protein P3 competitively binds to D14, disrupting the D14–D3 complex and blocking signal transduction to suppress the host immune response. Notably, engineered D14 variants that are resistant to P3 interference confer robust RGSV resistance without affecting rice growth or yield, demonstrating the potential of targeting SL signaling to improve resistance to viruses in crops (Yang et al., 2026).

The functions of SLs in nematode stress responses exhibit species‐specific patterns. In tomato, silencing SL biosynthetic genes compromises defense against the southern root‐knot nematode (Meloidogyne incognita), and rac‐GR24 enhances plant resistance (Xu et al., 2019). In contrast, SL biosynthesis (d10 and d27) and signaling (d14) mutants in rice exhibit increased resistance to the root‐knot nematode (Meloidogyne graminicola), with elevated levels of JA and JA‐Ile (Lahari et al., 2019).

CONCLUSIONS AND PROSPECTS

Expansion and deepening of strigolactone signaling from plant development to stress tolerance

Since their recognition as a new class of plant hormones, the molecular mechanisms by which SLs regulate plant architecture have gradually been mapped. The SL biosynthesis pathway involves conserved enzymes (D27, CCD7 and CCD8) that catalyze the formation of carlactone, as well as CYP450 enzymes that produce various SL molecules in different plant species (Alder et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2014; Al‐Babili and Bouwmeester, 2015; Li et al., 2023a; Ou et al., 2025). The SL signaling pathway primarily involves the assembly of the D14–MAX2/D3–D53/SMXL6, 7, 8 receptor complex, which triggers the degradation of the repressor proteins D53/SMXL6, 7, 8. This module precisely regulates the number of plant branches/tillers by relieving the repression on BRC1/OsTB1, a molecular hub that inhibits axillary bud outgrowth (Jiang et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2015, 2020a; Waters et al., 2017; Ou et al., 2025). Furthermore, SLs also regulate other architectural traits, including plant height, root morphology, and leaf senescence (Table 1).

Although recent studies have confirmed the indispensable role of SLs in plant responses to drought, salinity, extreme temperatures, and nutrient stress, our understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms remains limited to phenomenological observations and preliminary associations. Many questions remain unanswered. For instance, how are environmental stress signals perceived and transmitted to the SL signaling system? How does the SL pathway selectively recruit downstream effectors under different stresses to initiate distinct physiological responses? Addressing these questions will require multi‐omics technologies and high‐resolution spatiotemporal expression analysis to dynamically map SL signaling networks under different stress conditions. It will also be crucial to decipher how MAX2/D3 recruit different substrate proteins under varying stresses and to understand the roles of SMXL family members in stress resistance.

Deciphering new functions of strigolactones in species‐specific traits

Classical studies have established the conserved function of SLs in regulating plant architecture, particularly branch development, in model plants (Table 1). However, as research extends to specific traits in a wider range of crops, new roles are emerging. For instance, in apple, the SL repressor D53 homolog interacts with the Agamous‐Like MADS‐Box 9 (AGL9) module to regulate SL‐mediated anthocyanin biosynthesis in fruits (An et al., 2024). In cotton, GhD53 interacts with transcription factors such as GhGARF and GhSLRF to regulate fiber development (Tian et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a). In potato, SLs regulate stolon and tuber development (Pasare et al., 2013). In soybean, SLs act as rhizosphere signaling molecules that regulate nodule formation and nitrogen fixation efficiency (Haq et al., 2017; Rehman et al., 2022). These studies suggest that the SL signaling pathway has been co‐opted into species‐specific developmental processes over the course of evolution, acquiring functions beyond its core role (Table 1).

A key area for future research is functional genomics studies of SLs in various plants, especially non‐model species. This involves creating mutants with altered SL biosynthesis and signaling, and then thoroughly characterizing the resulting phenotypes in relation to key agronomic traits. Molecular and biochemical approaches are essential for deciphering how SL signaling integrates with species‐specific regulatory networks. Ultimately, comparative analyses of SL pathway components, such as the ligand‐binding properties of D14 and the structural variations among SMXL proteins, across different species will ultimately elucidate the role of plant hormone pathways in driving the diversification and adaptive evolution of agronomic traits.

The function and application of strigolactones as rhizosphere communication molecules

SLs play a fundamental role in promoting AM fungal symbiosis as rhizosphere signaling molecules (Akiyama et al., 2005). However, parasitic weeds such as Striga and Orobanche species have evolved to hijack this ancient symbiotic signal. These parasitic weeds infest nearly 70 million hectares of farmland, endangering food security of approximately 300 million people and causing annual economic losses estimated at US$10 billion (Parker, 2009; Yoshida and Shirasu, 2009; Yoshida et al., 2016).

Two principal approaches counteracting these threats are the development of SL analogs and the breeding of parasitic‐resistant crops. The SL analogue approach mimics the survival strategy of parasitic plants, which perceive pre‐applied SL analogs and initiate germination but cannot survive without a host plant. Discovered or synthesized SL analogs include T‐010, AB01, Nijmegen‐1 and SQUAMOSA PROMOTER BINDING PROTEIN‐LIKE (SPL)‐7, among others (Samejima et al., 2016; Zwanenburg et al., 2016; Uraguchi et al., 2018; Chesterfield et al., 2020; Jamil et al., 2022, 2025). Breeding resistant varieties relies on reducing SL biosynthesis or altering SL profiles to significantly reduce parasitic infestation while minimizing the impact on crop architecture. For example, genetically modified tomato plants with altered SL biosynthesis exhibit normal morphology yet are significantly less susceptible to Striga and Orobanche (Wakabayashi et al., 2019). In sorghum, a mutation in the sulfotransferase gene Low Germination Stimulant 1 (LGS1) alters root‐secreted SLs from 5‐deoxystrigol to orobanchol, conferring Striga resistance (Gobena et al., 2017). Furthermore, two recent studies revealed that reducing SL secretion into the soil is an effective strategy for enhancing parasite resistance. Sorghum plants with edited Sorghum bicolor Strigolactone Transporter 1 (SbSLT1) and SbSLT2 displayed normal plant architecture but significantly reduced SL secretion in roots. This led to lower parasite germination and reduced Striga parasitism in the field, thus mitigating yield losses (Shi et al., 2025; Table 1). Tomato lines with edited SL transporter genes SlABCG44 and SlABCG45 exhibited broad‐spectrum resistance to Orobanche races. Notably, SlABCG45 knockout achieved a balance between parasite resistance and fruit yield, significantly reducing yield losses in infested fields (Ban et al., 2025; Table 1).

Future research will focus on elucidating the similarities and differences in SL perception mechanisms between AM fungi and parasitic weeds, including their preferred SL isomers. This will enable the precise regulation of crop SL biosynthesis pathways using gene‐editing technologies, thereby enhancing the utilization of AM fungi while reducing their exploitation by parasitic weeds. These comparative insights are essential for designing highly specific, environmentally safe suicide germination inducers. However, the long‐term impact of parasite‐resistant crops on soil microbial communities, including beneficial AM fungi, must also be considered to ensure this approach can be successfully implemented within a sustainable agricultural framework.

Crosstalk between SLs and other phytohormones in plant development and environmental responses

SLs are closely integrated into the hormonal network that governs plant growth and stress responses. Evidence shows that SLs interact with auxin, CKs, GAs, ABA, ET, BRs, SAs, and JAs through shared regulatory nodes. SL biosynthesis is transcriptionally regulated by multiple hormones. For example, auxin increases the expression of CCD7 and CCD8 to regulate branch development, while ethylene controls these genes through upstream transcription factors, ensuring proper SL production for leaf senescence (Domagalska and Leyser, 2011; Ueda and Kusaba, 2015). Other hormones share core SL signaling components, including the D14 receptor, MAX2/D3, and the D53/SMXL family, which transmit SL signals and integrate inputs from distinct hormonal pathways. D14 promotes an SL‐dependent degradation of the GA repressor SLR1, while D53 stabilizes SLR1 by competing with D14, thereby linking SL and GA signaling during nitrogen responses (Sun et al., 2023). MAX2/D3 is recruited by D14 to degrade D53/SMXLs and by the karrikin receptor KAI2 to target SMAX1 and SMXL2 (Wang et al., 2020b; Li et al., 2022). MAX2/D3 also mediates BR signaling by facilitating OsGSK2‐dependent degradation of CYC U2, thus coupling BR and SL pathways during mesocotyl elongation (Sun et al., 2018b). D53 and SMXL6, 7, and 8 interact with BR signaling effectors, including BES1, OsBZR1, RLA1, and DLT, to co‐regulate tillering and branching (Wang et al., 2013; Hu et al., 2019a; Fang et al., 2020). The SMXL8‐AGL9 module integrates SL and GA signaling to synergistically control anthocyanin biosynthesis in apple (An et al., 2024). SLs also integrate with ABA, SAs, and JAs to modulate plant responses to abiotic and biotic stress.

SLs act as key integrators within the hormonal network, interacting with other hormones through shared biosynthetic regulators, signaling hubs, and transcriptional co‐regulators. This multi‐level systemic integration allows SLs to convert external environmental signals into internal instructions that reset hormonal balance and coordinate the plant life cycle. Systematic analysis of the dynamic hormone homeostasis, protein–protein interactions, and tissue‐specific transcriptional cascades centered on SLs will strongly support crop improvement, promoting balanced growth, stress resilience and higher yields.

Integration and challenges of strigolactones in crop breeding

A primary goal of this research area is to apply fundamental findings to crop breeding (Table 1). Current knowledge of SL‐related traits, including tillering/branching, root development, stress resilience, and microbial interactions, highlights their potential for breeding improvement. For instance, the superior allele of the rice SL biosynthesis gene HTD1/D17 (HTD1 HZ ) was pivotal in the Green Revolution (Wang et al., 2020c). Moderate expression of SL biosynthesis regulators NSP1 and NSP2 increases tiller number, main panicle length, biomass, and yield per plant under low‐ and medium‐phosphorus conditions (Yuan et al., 2023). Base editing of D14D102N improves viral resistance without compromising plant growth or grain yield (Yang et al., 2026).

However, given the functional complexity of SLs, achieving an optimal balance among their roles in crop architecture, stress resilience, and AM symbiosis remains a challenge in agriculture. Breeding strategies should move beyond simple gene knockout or overexpression. Instead, precise spatiotemporal regulation and dosage control are likely to be more beneficial. Approaches include using tissue‐specific or stress‐inducible promoters to confine SL activity to targeted organs or developmental stages, applying protein engineering to fine‐tune the activity or specificity of key SL components, and exploring natural allelic variation within the SL pathway to identify favorable haplotypes that support optimal functions under specific environmental conditions.

Modelling the interactions between SL signaling networks and other trait‐regulating networks enables us to predict the combined phenotypic effects of genetic modifications. These insights facilitate the integration of optimized SL genes with other beneficial genes to achieve synergistic improvements in yield, quality, and disease resistance. With rapid advances in artificial intelligence and synthetic biology, designing intelligent SL signaling systems that respond dynamically to environmental changes is increasingly feasible. Integrating the SL pathway into “smart design breeding” for climate change therefore offers significant potential for sustainable agriculture.
