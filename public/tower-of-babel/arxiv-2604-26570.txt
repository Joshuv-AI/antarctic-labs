Ramsey Property and Pathological Sets: Almost Disjointness, Independence and Other Maximal Objects Thanks: ∗ Corresponding author
Jialiang He, Jintao Luo ∗, Shuguo Zhang

Abstract
. We show that under 𝖹𝖥 + 𝖢𝖢 ℝ \mathsf{ZF}+\mathsf{CC}_{\mathbb{R}} , if the Ramsey property holds for all sets in a good pointclass Γ \Gamma , then there is no MAD family in Γ \Gamma , proving a long-standing conjecture made by A.R.D. Mathias in 1977. This also holds for ℐ \mathcal{I} -MAD families with respect to analytic ideals ℐ \mathcal{I} including ℰ ​ 𝒟 \mathcal{ED} , ℰ ​ 𝒟 fin \mathcal{ED}_{\mathrm{fin}} , and Fin α \mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha} for all countable ordinals α \alpha . Under the same assumption, we show that if any one of the Baire property, Lebesgue measurability or Ramsey property holds for all sets in Γ \Gamma , then there is no maximal independent family in Γ \Gamma . Under the stronger assumption 𝖹𝖥 + 𝖣𝖢 ℝ \mathsf{ZF}+\mathsf{DC}_{\mathbb{R}} , we further prove that if the Ramsey property holds for all sets in Γ \Gamma , then Γ \Gamma contains no Vitali sets and thus no Hamel bases.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

The study of regularity properties constitutes a central and enduring theme in the foundations of mathematics. Since the emergence of modern real analysis, mathematicians have sought to delineate the boundary between well-behaved objects, i.e. those that can be approximated by simple geometric or combinatorial configurations, and pathological objects, whose existence challenges our intuitive understanding of the mathematical universe. This dichotomy between regularity and pathology remains a cornerstone of descriptive set theory and continues to drive the field’s development.

The classical regularity properties are well-established. A set of real numbers is said to be Lebesgue measurable if it can be assigned a meaningful length or volume. A set has the Baire property if it differs from an open set by a meager set, a topological notion of regularity. A subset of
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
, the collection of infinite subsets of
{\mathbb{N}}
, is said to have the Ramsey property if it admits the existence of infinite homogeneous subsets under partitions. These properties constitute the primary framework for evaluating structural regularity, each capturing a different facet of what it means for a set to be tame.

However, the
Axiom of Choice

{\sf AC}
, a foundational postulate of modern set theory, guarantees the existence of sets that fail to satisfy certain regularity properties. The Vitali set is the paradigmatic example of a non-measurable set whose existence relies crucially on the Axiom of Choice. Hamel bases, i.e. bases of
\mathbb{R}
viewed as a vector space over
\mathbb{Q}
, provide another example of pathological phenomena, as they are also non-measurable and their existence implies discontinuous additive functions. In the combinatorial realm, maximal almost disjoint (MAD) families are a central example of such pathology: a MAD family is a maximal collection of infinite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
with the property that any two distinct sets in the family intersect only finitely. Their existence, typically proved using Zorn’s Lemma, yields highly complex combinatorial objects that resist simple description. In addition to MAD families, the literature has also focused on other classical combinatorial pathologies, such as
m
aximal
i
n
d
ependent (MID) families
[
35
]
. More recently,
\mathcal{I}
-MAD families, as generalizations of MAD families with respect to different ideals, have been introduced to probe ever deeper into the structure of pathological combinatorial objects
[
1
]
.

The tension between combinatorial pathologies and structural regularity is particularly evident in the study of Ramsey property. Let
{[A]^{\omega}}
be the set of all infinite subsets of
A
where
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
. A set
X\subseteq{[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}}
is called Ramsey if
\exists B\in{[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}}
, such that either
{[B]^{\omega}}\subseteq X
or
X\cap{[B]^{\omega}}=\emptyset
. One can view a set
X\subseteq{[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}}
as a
2
-coloring of the complete hypergraph
{[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}}
, and the Ramsey property asserts that this coloring has an infinite monochromatic hyperclique. In this notation, all subsets of
{[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}}
being Ramsey is equivalent to the generalized Ramsey theorem
\omega\rightarrow(\omega)^{\omega}_{2}
.

It turns out that the existence of MAD families is closely related to Ramsey property. The systematic investigation of this topic was initiated by A. R. D. Mathias. In his seminal paper
[
32
]
, Mathias introduced the concept of a happy family (now often referred to as a selective coideal) and developed the notion of Mathias forcing. Within this framework, he proved that no analytic MAD family exists
[
32
, Corollary 4.7]
, and established the consistency of the non-existence of MAD families from a Mahlo cardinal. As he had already shown that all sets are Ramsey in Solovay’s model
[
30
]
, a model collapsed from an inaccessible cardinal
[
45
]
, these results suggested the following questions:

Conjecture 1.1
.

a

(1)

In Solovay’s model where all sets are Lebesgue measurable, have Baire property and Ramsey property, are there infinite MAD families?

(2)

[
32
, Page 87]
Is it a theorem of
{\sf ZF}+{\sf DC}_{\mathbb{R}}
that there are no (infinite) MAD families assuming all sets are Ramsey?

Towards Conjecture
1.1
(1), Asger Dag Törnquist proved that there are indeed no infinite MAD families in Solovay’s model
[
48
]
. Subsequently, Itay Neeman and Zach Norwood gave another proof of this result and additionally showed that under
\mathsf{AD}^{+}
or within
L(\mathbb{R})
in the presence of large cardinals, MAD families are impossible
[
37
]
. Karen Bakke Haga, David Schrittesser and Törnquist generalized these results and proved that under
\mathsf{AD}
and
V=L(\mathbb{R})
, or under
\mathsf{AD}^{+}
and dependent choice, there are no
\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha}
-MAD families for all countable ordinals
\alpha

[
1
]
. Towards Conjecture
1.1
(2), in their celebrated paper
[
41
]
, Schrittesser–Törnquist showed that, under the hypothesis that all sets have the Ramsey property augmented by the additional principle
\unif
, there are indeed no MAD families. Furthermore, Schrittesser-Törnquist proved that, assuming
{\sf R\text{-}Unif}
, all sets being Ramsey implies no MAD families with respect to all transfinitely iterated Fréchet ideals
[
42
]
. Recently, the results on determinacy and MAD families have been generalized to uncountable cardinals by William Chan, Stephen Jackson and Nam Trang
[
10
]
.

The problem of the consistency strength of MAD families was eventually resolved by Horowitz–Shelah, who showed that the non-existence of MAD families is equiconsistent with
\mathsf{ZFC}

[
18
]
. However, whether the extra assumption
\unif
in the theorem of Schrittesser–Törnquist is necessary remained open.

Among these combinatorial pathologies, the structure and existence of maximal independent families have also been extensively investigated. It is a classical result that there are no analytic infinite MID families, proved by Arnold W. Miller
[
35
]
via forcing methods. Projective level MID families are investigated in
[
5
]
, and it is shown under
{\sf ZFC}
that if all sets in
\Gamma
have the Baire property then there are no infinite MID families in
\Gamma
, where
\Gamma
is a pointclass closed under existential quantification over reals. Recent investigation has also focused on the spectrum of MID families and its large cardinal version
[
14
,
12
]
.

Besides these maximal combinatorial objects, continuous pathologies have also been widely studied. It is well known that Vitali sets are not Lebesgue measurable and have no Baire property, so there are no analytic Vitali sets. While a Hamel basis can be Lebesgue measure zero
[
44
]
, it is also a classical result that there are no analytic Hamel bases, from a measure-theoretic argument
[
22
]
. This was also proved in
[
35
]
via Cohen forcing. And globally, classical regularities indeed destroy Hamel bases classwise. It is folklore that the existence of a Hamel basis implies the existence of a Vitali set
[
3
]
, thus if all sets have Baire property or Lebesgue measurability, there are no Hamel bases.

1.2.
Main Results

Our first main result establishes that Ramsey regularity suffices to eliminate a fundamental class of combinatorial pathology. Recall that a boldface pointclass is a collection of subsets of Polish spaces closed under continuous preimages and containing all clopen sets
[
25
, p. 118]
. For our purposes, we call a pointclass
good
if it contains all clopen sets and is closed under countable intersections, finite products, countable unions, continuous images, and continuous preimages. With this terminology in place, we have the following theorem under the
Axiom of Countable Choice for Reals

\mathsf{CC}_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Theorem 1.2

(
\mathsf{ZF}+\mathsf{CC}_{\mathbb{R}}
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a good pointclass such that every set in
\Gamma
has the Ramsey property. Then there is no infinite MAD family in
\Gamma
.

This theorem provides a definitive answer to Mathias’ conjecture.

Our second main result extends this investigation from combinatorial pathology to the classical non-combinatorial examples. Under the
Axiom of Dependent Choice for Reals

\mathsf{DC}_{\mathbb{R}}
, we prove:

Theorem 1.3

(
\mathsf{ZF}+\mathsf{DC}_{\mathbb{R}}
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a good pointclass such that every set in
\Gamma
has the Ramsey property. Then
\Gamma
contains no Vitali sets, and hence no Hamel bases.

This result is somewhat unexpected, as Vitali sets and Hamel bases are typically connected to the failure of regularities in the sense of measure and category on the one-dimensional real line, while the Ramsey property serves as a form of regularity in the hypergraph
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
, which is a zero-dimensional space. It demonstrates that the Ramsey property, despite its combinatorial nature, can also have implications for the structure of the real line itself, beyond the zero-dimensional combinatorial objects.

Our third main result concerns MID families. An independent family is a collection of subsets of
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
such that any finite Boolean combination of distinct sets from the family is infinite
[
35
]
. A MID family is a maximal such family. We prove that:

Theorem 1.4

(
\mathsf{ZF}+\mathsf{CC}_{\mathbb{R}}
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a good pointclass. If every set in
\Gamma
satisfies any of the Lebesgue measurability, Baire property, or the Ramsey property, then
\Gamma
contains no MID family.

This result is notable for its uniformity, in contrast to the situation for MAD families. In Solovay’s model, every set is Lebesgue measurable and there are no MAD families, while it has also been proved in
[
17
]
and
[
29
, Example 14.3.6]
that there is a model of
\mathsf{ZF}+\mathsf{DC}
where a MAD family exists and all sets of reals are Lebesgue measurable. This means that MID families are no less destructible than MAD families against classical regularity.

Our fourth main result extends this investigation to generalized MAD notions that have recently attracted significant attention in the literature. We prove that:

Theorem 1.5

(
\mathsf{ZF}+\mathsf{CC}_{\mathbb{R}}
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a good pointclass such that every set in
\Gamma
has the Ramsey property. Then
\Gamma
contains no
\mathcal{ED}
-MAD families, no
\mathcal{ED}_{\mathrm{fin}}
-MAD families, and no
\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha}
-MAD families for any
\alpha<\omega_{1}
.

These generalizations are not straightforward, as each class represents a distinct combinatorial structure with its own properties.
\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha}
-MAD families generalize the notion of almost disjointness using ideals of the form
\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha}

[
23
]
, while
\mathcal{ED}_{\mathrm{fin}}
-MAD and
\mathcal{ED}
-MAD families arise from the study of the eventually different ideal and its variants
[
2
]
.

The case of
\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha}
-MAD families was previously proved in
[
42
]
under the additional hypothesis
\unif
, where the definition of
\mathcal{I}
-MAD families for arbitrary ideals
\mathcal{I}
on
\mathbb{N}
is also given. Our result improves upon
[
42
]
in two respects: we eliminate the need for the extra assumption
\unif
, and our proof is considerably simpler.

2.2.
Definitions

Definition 2.2
.

A relation is total if its domain is full. Now we define some well-known choice principles as follows.

(1)

Let
\mathsf{CC}_{\mathbb{R}}
be the assertion that every countable family of nonempty subsets of
\mathbb{R}
has a choice function.

(2)

Let
\mathsf{DC}_{\mathbb{R}}

and every total relation

\langle x_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{N}}\rangle

.

It is well known that
\mathsf{DC}_{\mathbb{R}}

. Also, recall that a set

is not bijective to any natural number, and is

\mathbb{N}
to

\mathsf{CC}_{\mathbb{R}}
implies that infinite subsets of
\mathbb{R}

.

A set

Note that for any

{[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}}
. Let
\hat{A}
be the unique strictly increasing function from

. The bijection
[\hat{A}]:{[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}}\rightarrow{[A]^{\omega}}
which sends

Definition 2.4
.

For any
a\in{[{\mathbb{N}}]^{<\omega}},A\in{[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}}

, we define

Definition 2.5
.

A

\Gamma

.

An

4.
Ramsey property and Vitali set

We now turn to Vitali sets, another classical object of study. We will use the following facts about the Ramsey property.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a good pointclass.

(1)

All sets in
\Gamma
are Ramsey if and only if all sets in
\Gamma
are completely Ramsey.

(2)

(
{\sf ZF}+\mathsf{DC}_{\mathbb{R}}
)
If all sets in
\Gamma
are Ramsey, then for any
\Gamma
-measurable
f:{[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}}\rightarrow Y

is Polish, there is an
H\in{[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}}
such that
f\upharpoonright{[H]^{\omega}}
is continuous.

Proof.

(1)

One direction is trivial since completely Ramsey sets are Ramsey. Conversely, for any
X\in\Gamma
and
[a,A]
, define
f:{[A]^{\omega}}\rightarrow{[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}}
such that
f(T)=a\sqcup T
. Then
f^{-1}[X]
is in
\Gamma\upharpoonright{[A]^{\omega}}
by the closure property of
\Gamma
. So
f^{-1}[X]
is Ramsey in

and there is

is homogeneous for

(2)

This proposition is similar to
[
24
, Exercise 19.19]
. Let

be a basis of

. Since

is
\Gamma
-measurable, we have

, so by (1) they are completely Ramsey. Now, we follow an argument similar to the fusion argument in

, i.e.

i.

Let

ii.

For any

(let

for convenience) such that for any

We can now prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.2

(
{\sf ZF}+\mathsf{DC}_{\mathbb{R}}
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a good pointclass. If every subset of

\Gamma

\Gamma
.

Proof.

Suppose otherwise, and let

be a Vitali set.
For any

to be the unique element in

such that

. Note that

is
\Gamma
-measurable.

We define the following encoding function

to record combinatorics on

{\mathrm{e}}(x):=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{x(i)+1}},

Let

be the sequence of

is irrational if and only if its binary expansion

0.0_{x(0)}10_{x(1)-x(0)-1}1\cdots 0_{x(n+1)-x(n)-1}1\cdots

is not periodic, where a binary expansion
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
of a real is called periodic if
there are natural numbers
k,l
such that
x_{i+l}=x_{i}
for every
i>k
.

Consider
f=v\circ{\mathrm{e}}:[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, which is also
\Gamma
-measurable. By Proposition
4.1
(2), there exists
H\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
such that

is continuous.

Claim 4.3
.

The function

is constant on

.

Proof of Claim
4.3
.

If not, there exist

with

. Fix disjoint open sets

such that

for

. By continuity, there exist basic open subsets

for

By the property of the encoding function, we have

. Also, we have

Let

Write

. Then
1\notin V
. We show that

is a Vitali set in
\Gamma

For any

, write

with

. If
z_{1}
does not appear as some

, then

. Otherwise, let
p
be the coefficient of

by a rational.

with

, contradicting the uniqueness of the basis representation of

. Thus

, which means

. Hence

is a Vitali set.

Now we show

. Denote

5.
Regularity properties and MID family

In this section, we prove that within a given good pointclass
\Gamma
, under any of the three classical regularity properties i.e. Lebesgue measurability, Baire property, or Ramsey property, there is no maximal independent family. The proof proceeds by first showing that the existence of an infinite MID family forces a decomposition of
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
into countably many filters and ideals from
\Gamma
, and then using this decomposition to derive a contradiction from each regularity assumption.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
be a MID family from
\Gamma
.
Let
\mathcal{U}
consist of all finite disjoint unions of basic open sets of
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
. Note that
{\mathcal{U}}
is countable.

For
x\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
, define

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}(x)=\{(F,G)\in K([\mathbb{N}]^{\omega})\times K([\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}):

\displaystyle F,G\in[\mathcal{A}]^{<\omega},\ F\cap G=\emptyset,

\displaystyle\sigma(F,G)\setminus x\text{ is finite }\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}(x)=\{(F,G)\in K([\mathbb{N}]^{\omega})\times K([\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}):

\displaystyle F,G\in[\mathcal{A}]^{<\omega},\ F\cap G=\emptyset,

\displaystyle\sigma(F,G)\cap x\text{ is finite }\},

where

\sigma(F,G)=\bigcap_{a\in F}a\cap\left(\mathbb{N}\setminus\bigcup_{b\in G}b\right).

For disjoint
U,V\in\mathcal{U}
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(U,V)

\displaystyle=\{x\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}:\exists(F,G)\in\langle U\rangle\times\langle V\rangle,(F,G)\in{\mathcal{C}}(x)\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(U,V)

\displaystyle=\{x\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}:\exists(F,G)\in\langle U\rangle\times\langle V\rangle,(F,G)\in{\mathcal{D}}(x)\}\cup[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega},

where
\langle U\rangle\times\langle V\rangle
denotes the basic open set

\{(F,G)\in K([\mathbb{N}]^{\omega})\times K([\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}):F\subseteq U,G\subseteq V\}.

We begin with the following key decomposition.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a good pointclass. If
\Gamma
contains a MID family, then
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
is a countable union of filters and ideals from
\Gamma
.

Proof.

The proof proceeds in three steps. First, for any two disjoint open sets
U,V
, we show that
\mathcal{F}(U,V)
and
\mathcal{I}(U,V)
are a filter and an ideal, respectively. Second, we prove that every real
x\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
belongs to some
\mathcal{F}(U,V)
or
\mathcal{I}(U,V)
. Finally, we verify that these filters and ideals belong to
\Gamma
.

Claim 5.3
.

If
{\mathcal{F}}(U,V)
and
{\mathcal{I}}(U,V)
are non-empty, then
{\mathcal{F}}(U,V)
is a proper filter and
{\mathcal{I}}(U,V)
is a proper ideal.

Proof of Claim
5.3
.

We verify the ideal properties for
\mathcal{I}(U,V)
; the filter properties for
\mathcal{F}(U,V)
are analogous. In fact,
{\mathcal{F}}(U,V)
is the dual filter of
{\mathcal{I}}(U,V)
.

(1)

If
x\in\mathcal{I}(U,V)
and
y\subseteq x
, then
y\in\mathcal{I}(U,V)
.

This is clear when
x
is finite. If
x
is infinite, pick
(F,G)\in\langle U\rangle\times\langle V\rangle
with
(F,G)\in{\mathcal{D}}(x)
. Then
\sigma(F,G)\cap x
is finite, and since
y\subseteq x
, the same
(F,G)
witnesses
y\in\mathcal{I}(U,V)
.

(2)

For any
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{I}(U,V)
, we show that
x_{1}\cup x_{2}\in{\mathcal{I}}(U,V)
.

Pick
(F_{i},G_{i})\in\langle U\rangle\times\langle V\rangle
with
\sigma(F_{i},G_{i})\cap x_{i}
finite. Let
F=F_{1}\cup F_{2}
,
G=G_{1}\cup G_{2}
. Then
(F,G)\in\langle U\rangle\times\langle V\rangle
and
F\cap G=\emptyset
. We have that

\displaystyle\phantom{\big(}\sigma\left(\mathmakebox[\widthof{$F_{1}$}][c]{F},\mathmakebox[\widthof{$G_{1}$}][c]{G}\right)\cap\left(x_{1}\cup x_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\sigma\left(F_{1},G_{1}\right)\cap\sigma\left(F_{2},G_{2}\right)\big)\cap\left(x_{1}\cup x_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big[

\displaystyle\big(\sigma\left(F_{1},G_{1}\right)\cap\sigma\left(F_{2},G_{2}\right)\big)\cap x_{1}\big]

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\!\cup

\displaystyle\big[

\displaystyle\big(\sigma\left(F_{1},G_{1}\right)\cap\sigma\left(F_{2},G_{2}\right)\big)\cap x_{2}\big]

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\big(\sigma\left(F_{1},G_{1}\right)\cap x_{1}\big)\cup\big(\sigma\left(F_{2},G_{2}\right)\cap x_{2}\big),

which is finite. Hence
x_{1}\cup x_{2}\in\mathcal{I}(U,V)
.

(3)

We show that
\mathbb{N}\notin\mathcal{I}(U,V)
. If there is any witness
(F,G)
, then
\sigma(F,G)\cap\mathbb{N}=\sigma(F,G)
is finite. But by independence
\sigma(F,G)
is infinite.

∎

Now, we show that every
x\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
lies in some
\mathcal{F}(U,V)
or
\mathcal{I}(U,V)
with
U,V\in{\mathcal{U}}
. If
x\in[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega}
, then
x\in\mathcal{I}(U,V)
for any
U,V\in\mathcal{U}
by definition.
Assume
x\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
. By the maximality of the MID family
\mathcal{A}
, there exist finite disjoint
F,G\subseteq\mathcal{A}
such that either
\sigma(F,G)\setminus x
or
\sigma(F,G)\cap x
is finite. We can find disjoint
U,V\in\mathcal{U}
with
F\subseteq U
,
G\subseteq V
. Then
(F,G)\in\langle U\rangle\times\langle V\rangle
, and

\left\{\begin{aligned} \text{if }\sigma(F,G)\setminus x\text{ is finite, }&\text{then }x\in\mathcal{F}(U,V);\\
\text{if }\sigma(F,G)\cap x\text{ is finite, }&\text{then }x\in\mathcal{I}(U,V).\end{aligned}\right.

Since
\mathcal{U}
is countable, we have that

\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}U,V\in\mathcal{U}\\
U\cap V=\emptyset\end{subarray}}\big(\mathcal{F}(U,V)\cup\mathcal{I}(U,V)\big)

is a countable union of filters and ideals.

Finally, we show that
\mathcal{F}(U,V)
and
\mathcal{I}(U,V)
belong to
\Gamma
. We prove that
\mathcal{F}(U,V)\in\Gamma
. Since the pointwise complement
x\mapsto{\mathbb{N}}\setminus x
is a homeomorphism of
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
, we also have that
\mathcal{I}(U,V)\in\Gamma
.

Define

\displaystyle Z=\big\{\left(\left(F,G\right),x\right)

\displaystyle\in K\left({[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}}\right)\times K\left({[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}}\right)\times{\mathcal{P}}\left({\mathbb{N}}\right):

\displaystyle\left(F,G\right)

\displaystyle\in\langle U\rangle\times\langle V\rangle\cap{[{\mathcal{A}}]^{<\omega}}\times{[{\mathcal{A}}]^{<\omega}}\ \wedge

\displaystyle F\cap G

\displaystyle=\emptyset\ \wedge\ \sigma\left(F,G\right)\cap x\text{ is finite}\big\}.

Note that the function
f_{n}:\langle x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\rangle\mapsto\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}
is a continuous function from
\left({[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}}\right)^{n}
to
K({[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}})
, so we have that

\langle U\rangle\times\langle V\rangle\cap{[{\mathcal{A}}]^{<\omega}}\times{[{\mathcal{A}}]^{<\omega}}\in\Gamma.

As the rest of conditions for
Z
are Borel, we have
Z\in\Gamma
, and its projection
{\mathcal{I}}(U,V)
is in
\Gamma
.

This completes the proof that
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
is a countable union of filters and ideals from
\Gamma
. ∎

We recall the following well-known results characterizing the “smallness” of filters.

Lemma 5.4

(Sierpiński)
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}
be a proper filter. Then it is Lebesgue measurable if and only if it has measure zero, and it has Baire property if and only if it is meager.

Proof.

See
[
2
, Theorem 4.1.1]
. Note that it is also true for proper ideals.
∎

It is well known that all sets being Ramsey implies all filters are meager: it is a theorem of Mathias that
\omega\rightarrow(\omega)^{\omega}
implies that all filters on
\omega
are feeble
[
31
]
; also, by M. Talagrand, a filter is feeble if and only if it is meager
[
46
]
. Below we restate the relativized version of the theorem stated in
[
37
, Theorem 7.2]
.

Lemma 5.5
.

If all subsets of
{[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}}
in
\Gamma
are Ramsey, then all proper filters in
\Gamma
are meager.

Proof.

We argue with ideals. If not, let
{\mathcal{I}}
be a non-meager proper ideal in
\Gamma
. So by Lemma
5.4
it does not have the Baire property. By
[
2
, Theorem 4.1.2]
we have that

\forall x\in{[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}}\ \Big[\exists A\in{\mathcal{I}}\ \big[\text{$\{i\in{\mathbb{N}}:[x(i),x(i+1))\subseteq A\}$ is infinite}\big]\Big].

Define

E({\mathcal{I}})=\left\{x\in{\mathbb{N}}:\bigcup_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}[x(2i),x(2i+1))\in{\mathcal{I}}\right\},

which is in
\Gamma
. We show that
E({\mathcal{I}})
is not Ramsey.

For any
x\in{[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}}
, we obtain some
A\in{\mathcal{I}}
as above, and let

I=\{i\in{\mathbb{N}}:[x(i),x(i+1))\subseteq A\}.

Define
x_{1}\in{[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}}
such that

\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}\ \big[x_{1}(2n)=x(I(2n)),x_{1}(2n+1)=x(I(2n)+1)\big].

So
x_{1}\in E({\mathcal{I}})
. Let
x_{2}=x_{1}\backslash\{x_{1}(0)\}
. Since

{\mathbb{N}}\setminus\left(\bigcup_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\big[x_{1}(2i),x_{1}(2i+1)\big)\sqcup\bigcup_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\big[x_{2}(2i),x_{2}(2i+1)\big)\right)\text{ is finite},

by properness we have
x_{2}\notin E({\mathcal{I}})
. This means that for any real
x\in{[{\mathbb{N}}]^{\omega}}
we have a subreal in
E({\mathcal{I}})
and a subreal not in
E({\mathcal{I}})
. So
E({\mathcal{I}})
is not Ramsey. Thus,
{\mathcal{I}}
must be meager.
∎

We now combine the decomposition theorem with known results on filters and ideals to obtain the main result of this section.

Theorem 5.6

(
{\sf ZF}+\mathsf{CC}_{\mathbb{R}}
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a good pointclass. If every set in
\Gamma
is Lebesgue measurable, has Baire property or Ramsey property, then
\Gamma
contains no MID family.

Proof.

Assume
\Gamma
contains a MID family
\mathcal{A}
. By Lemma
5.2
, the Cantor space
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
is a countable union of proper filters and proper ideals in
\Gamma
.

(1)

If all sets in
\Gamma
are Lebesgue measurable, then by Lemma
5.4
and the countable additivity of measure,
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
is null, which is impossible.

(2)

Similarly, if all sets in
\Gamma
have the Baire property, then
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
is meager, contradicting the Baire category theorem.

(3)

Also, if all sets in
\Gamma
have the Ramsey property, then by Lemma
5.5
the space
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
is also meager, which is impossible.

Hence
\Gamma
cannot contain a MID family. Note that
{\sf CC}_{\mathbb{R}}
is sufficient for all the arguments above.
∎

As an immediate application of Theorem
5.6
, we obtain the following result under the
Axiom of Determinacy

{\sf AD}
.

Corollary 5.7

(
{\sf ZF}+{\sf AD}
)
.

There are no MID families.

Proof.

Under
{\sf AD}
, every set of reals has the Baire property and
{\sf CC}_{\mathbb{R}}
holds. Let
\Gamma
be the pointclass of all subsets of Polish spaces. Theorem
5.6
implies that
\Gamma
contains no MID family. Hence no MID family exists.
∎

6.
Ramsey property and
\mathcal{I}
-MAD family

Having established that the Ramsey property eliminates ordinary MAD families, we now extend this result to
{\mathcal{I}}
-MAD families for eventually different ideals and iterated Fubini products of Fréchet ideals. We introduce the notion of
dichotomously coded
ideals and show that for any such ideal
\mathcal{I}
, the Ramsey property precludes the existence of
\mathcal{I}
-MAD families, provided a sufficiently definable coding operator exists. We then verify that several classes of ideals satisfy this property, including
\mathcal{ED}
,
\mathcal{ED}_{\mathrm{fin}}
, and
\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha}
for all countable ordinals
\alpha
.

Motivated by the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we define the following class of ideals:

Definition 6.1
.

We say an ideal
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
is
dichotomously coded
if for every Dedekind-infinite
\mathcal{I}
-MAD family
\mathcal{A}
, there exists a
coding operator

\Phi:[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}\to\mathcal{I}^{+}
satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

For every
H\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
, there exist
y\in[H]^{\omega}
and
A\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\Phi(y)\setminus A\in\mathcal{I}
.

(2)

For every
H\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
, there exist
y\in[H]^{\omega}
and
A\in\mathcal{A}
such that both
A\cap\Phi(y)\in\mathcal{I}^{+}
and
\Phi(y)\setminus A\in\mathcal{I}^{+}
.

The following proposition is a straightforward adaptation of Theorem
3.1
.

Proposition 6.2

(
{\sf ZF}
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a good pointclass and
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
be a dichotomously coded ideal. If every set in
\Gamma
has the Ramsey property and there exists a
\Gamma
-measurable coding operator for
\mathcal{I}
, then
\Gamma
contains no Dedekind-infinite
\mathcal{I}
MAD family.

Proof.

The proof follows exactly the same argument as in Theorem
3.1
, with the following substitutions:

(1)

The ordinary MAD family
\mathcal{A}
in Theorem
3.1
is replaced by the
\mathcal{I}
-MAD family
\mathcal{A}
.

(2)

The operator
\Phi(x)
defined by
\{A_{x(2n)}(x(2n+1)):n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is replaced by the
\Gamma
-measurable coding operator
\Phi
from Definition
6.1
.

(3)

The set
P=\{z\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}:\exists A\in\mathcal{A},\ \Phi(z)\subseteq A\}
becomes

P=\{z\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}:\exists A\in\mathcal{A},\ \Phi(z)\setminus A\in\mathcal{I}\}.

The rest of the proof is identical, showing that
P
is not Ramsey, contradicting the hypothesis that all
\Gamma
-sets are Ramsey.
∎

6.1.
\mathcal{ED}
and
\mathcal{ED}_{\textnormal{fin}}

a

We now show that the eventually different ideals
\mathcal{ED}

\mathcal{ED}_{\text{fin}}

6.1
. This will allow us to apply Proposition
6.2
and conclude that the Ramsey property precludes the existence of
\mathcal{ED}
-MAD and
\mathcal{ED}_{\text{fin}}
-MAD families. We first introduce some notation and a special subclass of
\mathcal{ED}^{+}
.

Definition 6.3
.

Define
\mathcal{ED}^{++}
to be the collection of all

satisfying the following condition: if

is the
n
-th element of
\mathrm{dom}(X)

|X(m)|=n+1
. We can also define
\mathcal{ED}_{\text{fin}}^{++}\subseteq\mathcal{ED}_{\text{fin}}^{+}
in the same way.

For each
X\in\mathcal{ED}^{++}
and integers

, we denote

X[n,k]=(m_{n},m_{k}),

where
m_{n}
is the
n
-th element of
\mathrm{dom}(X)
and
m_{k}
is the
k
-th element of
X(m_{n})
. We denote
m_{n}
by
X[n]
.

We need the following fact.

Fact 6.4
.

For any
X\in\mathcal{ED}^{+}
, there is a subset
Y\subseteq X
such that
Y\in\mathcal{ED}^{++}
. This also holds for
\mathcal{ED}_{\textnormal{fin}}
.

Proposition 6.5
.

\mathcal{ED}
and
\mathcal{ED}_{\textnormal{fin}}
are dichotomously coded.

Proof.

We give the proof for
\mathcal{ED}
; the case of
\mathcal{ED}_{\text{fin}}
is analogous, using
\mathcal{ED}_{\text{fin}}^{++}
in place of
\mathcal{ED}^{++}
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a Dedekind-infinite
\mathcal{ED}
-MAD family. Fix a sequence
\{A_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
of distinct elements in
\mathcal{A}
. We may assume that the domains
\{\mathrm{dom}(A_{n}):n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
are pairwise disjoint and that each
A_{n}
belongs to
\mathcal{ED}^{++}
:

(1)

We shrink every
A_{n}
so that
A_{n}\in\mathcal{ED}^{++}
. Note that
{\rm dom}(A_{n})
is infinite.

(2)

Define pairwise disjoint infinite subsets
Q_{n}=\{q_{n,j}:j\in{\mathbb{N}}\}\subseteq{\rm dom}(A_{n})
in the following order:

q_{0,0}<q_{1,0}<q_{1,1}<q_{0,1}<q_{2,0}<\cdots.

This is done by picking
q_{n,j}\in{\rm dom}(A_{n})
such that it is larger than any previously picked
q
’s.

(3)

Replace
A_{n}
by
\{(j,k)\in A_{n}:j\in Q_{n}\}
and further shrink to
\mathcal{ED}^{++}
sets.

Note that for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have
A_{n}[0]<A_{n+1}[0]
.

Define the coding operator
\Phi:[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}\to\mathcal{ED}^{+}
as

\Phi(x)=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcup_{i=0}^{n}A_{x\left(\frac{n(n+5)}{2}\right)}\Big[x\left(\tfrac{(n+1)(n+6)}{2}-1\right),x\left(\tfrac{n(n+5)}{2}+1+i\right)\Big].

.

. Now we verify that

satisfies the two conditions in Definition

.

Claim 6.6
.

For every
H\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
, there exist
y\in[H]^{\omega}
and
A\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\Phi(y)\subseteq A
.

Proof of Claim
6.6
.

Since
\mathcal{A}
is an
\mathcal{ED}
-MAD family and
\Phi(H)\in\mathcal{ED}^{+}
, by maximality there exists
A\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\Phi(H)\cap A\in\mathcal{ED}^{+}
.

We construct
y\subseteq H
recursively. For convenience, let
y(-1)=-1
. Assume we have already defined
y(j)
for
j<\frac{k(k+5)}{2}
. We now define the next block of elements corresponding to index
k
, i.e. from
y\left(\tfrac{k(k+5)}{2}\right)
to
y\left(\tfrac{(k+1)(k+6)}{2}-1\right)
.

By the definition of
\Phi(H)
, for the fixed
H\left(\frac{(n+1)(n+6)}{2}-1\right)
-th column of
A_{H\left(\frac{n(n+5)}{2}\right)}
, the vertical indices appearing in
\Phi(H)
are exactly the values
H(m)
for
m
in the interval
\left[\frac{n(n+5)}{2}+1,\frac{(n+1)(n+6)}{2}-1\right)
. Since
\Phi(H)\cap A\in\mathcal{ED}^{+}
, there are infinitely many such columns for which this intersection contains at least
k+1
elements. Thus for each
k
we can find minimal
n_{k}
such that

H\left(\tfrac{n_{k}(n_{k}+5)}{2}\right)>y\left(\tfrac{k(k+5)}{2}-1\right)

and the interval

\left[\frac{n_{k}(n_{k}+5)}{2}+1,\ \frac{(n_{k}+1)(n_{k}+6)}{2}-1\right)

contains

Define

by setting

\displaystyle y\left(\tfrac{k(k+5)}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=H\left(\tfrac{n_{k}(n_{k}+5)}{2}\right),

\displaystyle y\left(\tfrac{k(k+5)}{2}+1+i\right)

\displaystyle=H(j^{k}_{i}),\ 0\leq i\leq k,

\displaystyle y\left(\tfrac{(k+1)(k+6)}{2}-1\right)

\displaystyle=H\left(\tfrac{(n_{k}+1)(n_{k}+6)}{2}-1\right).

\Phi(y)

.

Claim 6.7
.

For every

, there exist

and

such that both

and

belong to

.

.

H\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
. By Claim

, pick
A\in\mathcal{A}
and

such that
\Phi(x)\subseteq A
.

Note that
A\neq A_{n}
for all

, so we have
A\cap A_{n}\in\mathcal{ED}
for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
This induces an increasing sequence
\{l_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
such that for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\forall m>l_{n}[|(A_{n}\cap A)(m)|<l_{n}].

We construct
y\in[x]^{\omega}
recursively. Let
y(-1)=-1
and for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, assume we have already defined
y(j)
for
j<\frac{k(k+5)}{2}
. We now define
y\left(\tfrac{k(k+5)}{2}\right),\cdots,y\left(\tfrac{(k+1)(k+6)}{2}-1\right)
.

(1)

When
k
is even, we do exactly as in Claim
6.6
.

(2)

When
k
is odd,

i.

Let

n_{k}=\min\left\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:x\left(\tfrac{n(n+5)}{2}\right)>y\left(\tfrac{k(k+5)}{2}-1\right)\right\}

and define
y\left(\tfrac{k(k+5)}{2}\right)=x\left(\tfrac{n_{k}(n_{k}+5)}{2}\right)
.

Let

\displaystyle y\left(\tfrac{(k+1)(k+6)}{2}-1\right)=\min\big\{m\in x:

\displaystyle m>y_{k}\ \wedge\ A_{y_{k}}[m]>l_{y_{k}}

\displaystyle\wedge

\displaystyle\left|\left(y_{k},m\right)\cap x\right|>k+1+l_{y_{k}}\big\},

where
y_{k}=y\left(\tfrac{k(k+5)}{2}\right)
and
\left(y_{k},m\right)
denotes an open interval.

iii.

Denote

. By the definition above, we can find

y_{k}<q_{0}^{k}<\cdots<q_{k}^{k}<y_{k+1}

and

Such
q_{i}^{k}
’s exist, because at this

-th column of
A_{y_{k}}
, there are less than

-many indices in
(y_{k},y_{k+1})\cap x
which substitute into

, and there are more than

-many points in total. Define

By construction, for each
k
, the
k+1
terms in

coming from index

are all inside
A
when

is even, and all outside
A
when

is odd. Hence both
A\cap\Phi(y)
and

are in
\mathcal{ED}^{+}
.

∎

Since
\Phi
satisfies both conditions of Definition

, we conclude that
\mathcal{ED}
is a dichotomously coded ideal. ∎

.

6.2.
\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha}
-MAD Family

a

We now turn to the ideals
\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha}
for countable ordinals
\alpha
. The proof that these ideals are dichotomously coded follows the same pattern as for
\mathcal{ED}
, but requires a careful handling of the tree structure
S_{\alpha}
. To this end, we first introduce the analog of
\mathcal{ED}^{++}
for the
\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha}
hierarchy. We recall Section
2
for the notations used below.

Definition 6.10

(
[
42
]
)
.

For each countable ordinal
\alpha
with
1\leq\alpha<\omega_{1}
, we recursively define the collection
\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha++}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(S_{\alpha})
as follows:

(1)

If
\alpha=1
, then
\mathrm{Fin}^{1++}=\mathrm{Fin}^{1+}
.

(2)

If
\alpha\geq 2
, then
X\subseteq S_{\alpha}
belongs to
\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha++}
if and only if
\mathrm{dom}(X)
is infinite and for every
n\in\mathrm{dom}(X)
, we have
X(n)\in\mathrm{Fin}^{\gamma^{\alpha}_{n}++}
.

We list some useful facts here.

Fact 6.11
.

Let
\alpha\geq 1
.

(1)

Every
X\in\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha+}
contains a subset
Y\subseteq X
with
Y\in\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha++}
.

(2)

For
X\in\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha++}
, we have
\rho_{X}(\emptyset)=\alpha
, and for any
t\in T(X)
with
\rho_{X}(t)>0
, we have
X(t)\in\mathrm{Fin}^{\rho_{X}(t)++}
.

(3)

A set
X
is in
\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha++}
if and only if it is non-empty and for any
s\in T(X)
with
\rho_{X}(s)\geq 1
, we have
\mathrm{dom}(X(s))
is infinite.

Proof.

For brevity, we outline the proofs as follows.

(1)

This is proved via induction on
\alpha
and the proof is omitted.

(2)

i.

Note that, via a simple induction, for any set
X\subseteq S_{\alpha}
, integer
n
, and sequence
m\in T(X(n))
, we have
\rho_{X}(n^{\frown}m)=\rho_{X(n)}(m)
. From this we can prove the first part.

ii.

For the second part, note that
X(t)\in\mathrm{Fin}^{\rho_{X(t)}(\emptyset)++}
and we have
\rho_{X(t)}(\emptyset)=\rho_{X}(t)
by iteratively using
\rho_{X}(n^{\frown}m)=\rho_{X(n)}(m)
.

(3)

Assume
X\in\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha++}
, then it is non-empty, and by (2) the condition on the right-hand side holds. Conversely, we do induction on
\alpha
.

i.

For
\alpha=1
, we have
T(X)=\{\emptyset\}\cup X
so
\rho_{X}(\emptyset)=1
. By the assumption,
X=X(\emptyset)
is infinite.

ii.

For
\alpha>1
, as
X\not=\emptyset
, we have
\rho_{X}(\emptyset)>1
. So by the assumption
{\rm dom}(X)={\rm dom}(X(\emptyset))
is infinite. Also, for any
n\in{\rm dom}(X)
, by definition
X(n)\not=\emptyset
. Since for any
s\in T(X(n))
we have
\rho_{X(n)}(s)=\rho_{X}(n^{\frown}s)
and
{\rm dom}(X(n)(s))={\rm dom}(X(n^{\frown}s))
,
X(n)
has satisfied the assumption. By the induction hypothesis we have
X(n)\in\mathrm{Fin}^{\gamma_{n}^{\alpha}++}
and thus

.

∎

Recall that for any

Definition 6.12

For

as the set of indices of

, and we denote

. Also, for any

.

(2)

For

and

, we say

of

and

. Let

, and for any

. For convenience, we say the empty sequence is the index of itself. By our definition, no proper initial segment of a terminal sequence is itself a terminal sequence; each terminal sequence corresponds to a maximal finite path in
T(X)
.

The following proposition is also useful.

Proposition 6.13
.

Let
\alpha\geq 1
. For any

, we have that
A\in\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha++}
if and only if for any
t\in{{\mathbb{N}}^{\omega}}
, there is unique

such that

. In this case we denote

by

.

Proof.

a

(1)

Suppose

. We show that for any
t\in{{\mathbb{N}}^{\omega}}
, there is
i\geq 1
such that
t\upharpoonright i\in\operatorname{TS}(A)
. Such

is unique if it exists. We proceed by induction on
\alpha
.

i.

For
\alpha=1
, the statement is trivial: for any
t
, take
i=1
.

ii.

For
\alpha>1
, suppose this direction holds for any
\beta<\alpha
. Fix
t\in{{\mathbb{N}}^{\omega}}
. As
A\in\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha++}
, we have
{\rm dom}(A)
is infinite, so the
t(0)
-th element
A[t(0)]
exists. Also,
A\llbracket t(0)\rrbracket\in\mathrm{Fin}^{\gamma(\alpha,A[t(0)])++}
, thus by the induction hypothesis applied to
t\upharpoonright[1,+\infty)
we obtain

such that
(t\upharpoonright[1,+\infty))\upharpoonright i\in\operatorname{TS}(A\llbracket t(0)\rrbracket)
. This means
t\upharpoonright(i+1)\in\operatorname{TS}(A)
.

(2)

Conversely, let

satisfy the condition on the right-hand side. We also proceed by induction on
\alpha
.

i.

For
\alpha=1
, by the assumption, for any
t\in{{\mathbb{N}}^{\omega}}
, there exists
i\geq 1
such that
A[t\upharpoonright i]\in A
. By the definition of the terminal sequence, we have
A[t\upharpoonright i](0)
is the
t(0)
-th element of

, which holds for every

. Thus

.

ii.

For
\alpha>1

. Similarly we have that
A[t\upharpoonright i](0)

t(0)
-th element of

, which holds for every

is infinite. We show that for any

and for any

there is

with

. For any

, let

. By assumption on

. As

\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha++}

∎

Theorem 6.18

(
{\sf ZF}
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a good pointclass and let
1\leq\alpha<\omega_{1}
. If every set in
\Gamma
has the Ramsey property, then
\Gamma
contains no Dedekind-infinite
\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha}
-MAD family.

Proof.

For any countable
\alpha
, the coding operator constructed in Proposition
6.14
is continuous. For
\alpha=1
, it is proved in Theorem
3.1
. For
\alpha>1
, th continuity follows from the fact that for any
p\in S_{\alpha}
,

\displaystyle p\in\Phi(x)\text{ if and only if }

\displaystyle\exists n\in{\mathbb{N}}\ \exists t\in{\mathbb{N}}^{|p|-1}\ p=A_{x(3n)}[x(3n+1),m(x,n,t)],

\displaystyle p\notin\Phi(x)\text{ if and only if }

\displaystyle\bigg[\forall n{\ \mathrm{s.t.}\ }A_{3n}[0]\leq p(0),\ p(0)\neq k_{x,n}\bigg]\ \vee

\displaystyle\bigg[\exists n\in{\mathbb{N}}\ \big[\ p(0)=k_{x,n}\ \wedge\ \forall t\in B(n,p)

\displaystyle p\neq A_{x(3n)}[x(3n+1),m(x,n,t)]\ \big]\bigg],

where

B(n,p)=\left\{s\in{\mathbb{N}}^{|p|-1}:\forall i\in[1,|p|)\ 3\pi(n,s\upharpoonright i)+2\leq p(i)\right\},

which is a finite set. Note that the second equivalence is true because

A_{3n}[0]\leq A_{x(3n)}[0]<k_{x,n}

and every evaluation of

is no less than
3\pi(n,t\upharpoonright i)+2
, for any
i\geq 1
.

Applying Proposition
6.2
with
\mathcal{I}=\mathrm{Fin}^{\alpha}
together with the assumption that every set in
\Gamma
has the Ramsey property yields the desired conclusion.
∎

7.3.
MID Families

For MID families, corresponding results have also been obtained. By adapting a combinatorial machinery from Eisworth and Shelah to the classical recursion framework, Brendle–Fischer-Khomskii proved that there is a
{\bm{\Sigma}}^{1}_{2}
Sacks indestructible MID family which is preserved in countable supported iterations of Sacks forcing, and they also showed that if there is a
{\bm{\Sigma}}^{1}_{2}
MID family, then there is a
{\bm{\Pi}}^{1}_{1}
MID family
[
5

. It seems that in a classical setting, regularities cannot preserve MID families while destroying MAD families.

Globally, our results show that classical regularities either only destroy MID families (the Baire property and the Lebesgue measurability), or destroy MAD and MID families at the same time (the Ramsey property). This phenomenon is also reflected in local generic extensions. Among the standard forcing notions in the literature, Sacks forcing is the only one for which the iterated models satisfy
\mathfrak{i}=\omega_{1}

\mathfrak{i}
is the
independence number

[
4

7.4.

Although we have had various examples of regularities that destroy pathologies, there are also some exceptions. Maximal eventually different (MED) families, as an analog to MAD families in the Baire space, have Borel and even compact instances

38

We would like to pose a general question in a less formal way. Before that some definitions need to be introduced.

Definition 7.11
