<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>With the first atomic test approaching, doctors and scientists at Los Alamos began looking closely at precautions and radiation safety measures. Dr. Louis Hempelmann—the director of medical and health affairs at Los Alamos—prepared the first part of a memorandum that described the weapons effects that could pose dangers, such as blast, radiation, and radioactive materials. Discussing what later became known as fallout, Hempelmann wrote that the “worst possible hazard from radioactive dust would seem to be the one in which the explosion is of sufficient energy to only get the material in the form of a cloud of fine dust.” If it landed on the shelter it would be "dangerous … to breathe for more than 6 minutes.”</p>

<p>To make it possible for real-time measurements of beta and gamma radiation and airborne fission products and to protect staff from radiation, the second part of the memorandum, prepared by electronics specialist Richard Watts, recommended deploying measuring instruments at shelters and mobile units and the organizational structure needed for this work.</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>In this memorandum, Robert Oppenheimer discussed the consequences of an air burst of an atomic weapon and the precautions that the bomber crew needed to take. The weapon was composed of toxic material and the explosion would emit radiation that would be “injurious within a mile” and “lethal within a radius of about six-tenths of a mile.” Moreover, “most of the [radioactive] activity will rise to a considerable height above the target and will remain as a fairly compact cloud for a period of hours after the detonation.” Because of that danger, he recommended safety measures for aircrews to take after the detonation. Oppenheimer did not mention any possibility that the bomb could produce radiation sickness, but he was aware of the problem.<a href="#_edn14" name="_ednref14">[17]</a></p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>With their knowledge of the radiation effects of a nuclear detonation, Joseph Hirschfelder (a physicist working in Los Alamos’ Ordnance and Engineering Division), the physical chemist John Magee, and others recognized the safety hazards associated with a test. Reinforcing Louis Hempelmann’s message, in a memorandum to Kenneth Bainbridge, the physicist in charge of Trinity, they cited the “definite danger of dust containing active material and fission products falling on towns near Trinity and necessitating their evacuation.”-<a href="#_edn15" name="_ednref15">[18]</a> Looking at what could happen to people in the area, they suggested that an individual could receive “approximately 22 R” in the hours after the explosion. <div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>A few days after the Hiroshima bombing, <a href="http://armedforcesjournal.com/august-11-1945-u-s-unleashes-bombs-on-2-japanese-cities/">Harold Jacobson</a>, a New York city-based scientist who had briefly worked for the MED, wrote a widely distributed report, first published in the<em> San Francisco Examiner</em>, asserting erroneously and sensationally that the radiation would render Hiroshima uninhabitable for seventy years. <div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>In this memorandum to Oppenheimer, <a href="https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/profile/george-kistiakowsky/">George B. Kistiakowsky</a>&nbsp;— who led explosives research at Los Alamos--reviewed pending issues, including the expansion of Los Alamos’s Site-S (production of special lenses for plutonium bombs) and the future of the “Project,” but began by briefly recounting the internal discussion over how to respond to Japanese reports about radiation sickness.<a href="#_edn01" name="_ednref01">[33]</a> Kistiakowsky described how, in response to queries from General Groves about the reports, he had teletyped recommendations that the Manhattan Project director had rejected. Kistiakowsky then asked Louis Hempelmann and others to prepare a memorandum for Groves on the effects of the bombings but seems to have hesitated in sending it after Groves said, during a press conference at Oak Ridge, Tennessee, that the accounts from Japan were “propaganda” and that radiation had not caused any deaths.<a href="#_edn02" name="_ednref02">[34]</a> In light of the Hempelmann team’s findings, Kistiakowsky observed that Groves had “stuck his neck out by a mile.” A FOIA request and subsequent appeal to the National Nuclear Security Administration led to the unearthing of the Hempelmann memo [See Document 21, below] but not Kistiakowsky’s teletype.</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>In several conversations on September 7 Groves continued to dismiss reports of Japanese deaths from radiation effects. Speaking with Harvard University President James B. Conant, who was also a top Manhattan Project official, Groves said there was “no basis” to reports of deaths from radiation, but said that Col. Stafford Warren, the chief medical officer, was going to Japan to look into the problem. Groves also had a conversation with Dr. Charles Rea, head of the Oak Ridge base hospital, who explained to him the difference between leukemia, an increase in white blood cells, and leukopenia, a decrease in white cells that was experienced by people exposed to radiation effects.</p>

<p>Groves mentioned a document discussing “three different causes of casualties,” possibly a reference to the recent Los Alamos memorandum (which may have eventually reached Groves, despite Kistiakowsky’s hesitations). When Groves asked whether “diet” could be a cause of leukopenia, Rea said it wasn’t, but suggested that “it could be caused by some medicines like benzidrine [sic].” As in an earlier conversation [See Document 19], Rea believed that post-explosion deaths in Japan were caused by “heat” but he added that deaths that occurred later “could be caused from the blast,” noting, for example, the cases of “people who are in accidents and who walk away apparently unharmed and die the next day.”</p>added that deaths that occurred later “could be caused from the blast,” noting, for example, the cases of “people who are in accidents and who walk away apparently unharmed and die the next day.”</p>

<p>Speaking with Oppenheimer later that morning, it was evident that Groves bought into Rea’s story not the findings of the Los Alamos staff. When Oppenheimer mentioned “delayed effects from radiation,” Groves said that Rea had accounted for the deaths as “delayed heat and blast cases.” On that, Oppenheimer made no comment. Groves would eventually accept that delayed radiation effects caused deaths [See Document 27].</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>With Groves seeking information that would refute the accounts of radiation sickness, General Farrell sent back reports that deemphasized the radiation problem. While recognizing that the “Japanese reports …. are essentially correct, as to clinical effects from single gamma radiation dose,” Farrell qualified that statement by reporting that Stafford Warren found that only a “small percentage of injured survivors” had radiation sickness. He did not mention that when meeting with the U.S. and international press in Tokyo, he had derided accounts of radiation sickness as “Japanese propaganda.” Farrell also reported that “Warren found no measurable radioactivity on preliminary measurements under detonation site or elsewhere on ground, streets, ashes or other materials.” In fact, however, Warren and James Nolan were finding a “very slight amount of radioactivity on the ground.”<a href="#_edn30" name="_ednref30">[37]</a></p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>One of the witnesses to the attack on Hiroshima that members of the U.S. survey team met was Johannes Siemes, a German Jesuit priest, a Ph.D. historian who taught philosophy at Sophia University.<a href="#_edn31" name="_ednref31">[38]</a> Escaping firebombed Tokyo, Siemes traveled to Hiroshima where he stayed in a Jesuit novitiate located in a suburb, Nagatsuke. According to his eye-witness account, he felt the immediate blast effects of the bomb, witnessed the mass fires, and the devastation and the human toll, which he tried to alleviate with the small resources that were available. Noting that Japanese newspapers were reporting on an “atomic bomb,” Siemes discussed the connections between radiation sickness and the “gamma rays that had been sent out” by the explosion: there “seems to be some truth in the statement that the radiation had some effect on the blood” (an article published by Siemes in 1946 was more definitive on the relationship).<a href="#_edn32" name="_ednref32">[39]</a></p>truth in the statement that the radiation had some effect on the blood” (an article published by Siemes in 1946 was more definitive on the relationship).<a href="#_edn32" name="_ednref32">[39]</a></p>

<p>During the weeks after the attack Siemes wrote a moving account of his experience that he provided to Stafford Warren (under unclear circumstances), who took it to Dr. Averill Liebow, one of the members of the U.S. survey team. A U.S. immigrant, Liebow spoke German and on 27 September 1945 translated Siemes’ account, dictating it to a U.S. Army Sergeant who typed it up. Liebow later modestly wrote that his translation “did less than full justice …to the style, excitement, and literary merit of the original German.”<a href="#_edn33" name="_ednref33">[40]</a> When Stafford Warren sent a secret report to General Groves on the effects of the atomic bombings he included a copy of the translation “for its value as background material” [See document 23].<a href="#_edn34" name="_ednref34">[41]</a></p>

<p>Siemes’ account led the U.S. doctors to another priest, Wilhelm Kleinsorge, who had been in Hiroshima and was in a Tokyo hospital suffering from radiation sickness. He may have been the first such patient that the U.S. doctors met. Later, when the journalist John Hersey visited Tokyo to research the atomic bombings, he acquired a copy of Siemes’ narrative, which had a significant impact on the article that he published in the <em>New Yorker.</em><a href="#_edn35" name="_ednref35">[42]</a><em> .</em></p>bombings, he acquired a copy of Siemes’ narrative, which had a significant impact on the article that he published in the <em>New Yorker.</em><a href="#_edn35" name="_ednref35">[42]</a><em> .</em></p>

<p>For its historical interest and importance, this is very likely an original, or certainly a very early copy, of Liebow’s translation as dictated in September 1945, the typescript of which made its way into the hands of John Hersey. This rendition is not wholly legible, however, and tidier versions of Siemes’ account are available, such as this online version, in the nicely typed format that went to General Groves and the Chief of Naval Operations (see Documents 23 and 25], but also in the 1946 MED public<a href="https://www.atomicarchive.com/resources/documents/med/index.html"> report</a> on the atomic bombings. Why Siemes’ report attracted such interest among U.S. officials is worth considering; such aspects as his straightforward narration, his raising of philosophical questions, and his European background may have been elements.</p>

<p>The report focused on medical findings, radioactivity measurements, and estimates of damage to structures. On casualties, Warren provided detailed information on the kinds of injuries suffered, including thermal burns and those caused by blast effects, and the delayed symptoms of radiation poisoning, which showed up after three to thirty days. He reported that of the “approximately 4000 patients admitted to hospitals [in both cities], 1300 or 33% showed effects of radiation and of this number approximately one-half died.”</p>

<p>Warren found persisting low levels of radiation at ground zero and several kilometers away. While finding that the “intensity of radiation is quite low [it] is measurable with the very sensitive instruments used.” In some cases, the acceptable tolerance dose “is exceeded slightly.” Referring to the spread of fallout, he cited Japanese data “that patients in Nagasaki as far away as 4 kilometers [showed] effects of radiation.”</p>

<p>The MED’s <a href="https://www.atomicarchive.com/resources/documents/med/index.html">1946</a> public report made similar points, denying any health injuries from induced or residual radiation. “Black rain” was not yet on Warren’s mental map, although the phenomenon is mentioned in the Navy report.</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>To develop new legislation governing the use of atomic energy in the United States, the U.S. Senate created a Special Committee that held a series of public hearings in late 1945 and early 1946. In his testimony on 28 November 1945, General Leslie Groves discussed the scope of the Manhattan Project, the problem of international control, and the destructive effects of atomic explosions. He did not provide a factual account of radiation effects, however, although the information provided by Stafford Warren would have made reasonably informative answers entirely possible. Perhaps wanting to preserve secrecy or possibly unwilling to admit uncertainty, Groves made bizarre and misleading statements to the Senators.</p>

<p>When Senator Warren Austin (R-VT) asked Groves if there was any radioactive “residue” at Hiroshima or Nagasaki, Groves ignored Warren’s report with a definitive answer: “there is none. Richard Russell (D-GA) observed that there was “tremendous fear” in the United States of atomic energy and its use, which made him want to know more. On radioactivity and the bombings generally, Groves said that he saw no choice between inflicting radioactivity on a “few Japanese” and saving “10 times as many American lives.” He claimed that no one suffered radiation injury “excepting at the time that the bomb actually went off, and that is an instantaneous damage.”</p>panese” and saving “10 times as many American lives.” He claimed that no one suffered radiation injury “excepting at the time that the bomb actually went off, and that is an instantaneous damage.”</p>

<p>Groves continued to go out on a limb by declaring that it “really would take an accident for … the average person, within the range of the bomb to be killed by radioactive effects.” Going further out on a limb, Groves stated that the victims of radiation whose exposure was not enough to kill them instantly would die “without undue suffering. In fact, they say it is a very pleasant way to die.” Groves’ testimony has received scathing criticism as an “outrageous untruth” (Peter Wyden) and “patently untrue” (Eileen Welsome).<a href="#_edn36" name="_ednref36">[43]</a></p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>In striking contrast to Groves’ testimony was the statement of Los Alamos physicist Philip Morrison, who had been with the U.S. group surveying weapons damage in Hiroshima and Nagasaki during September-October 1945. He gave the Senate Special Committee a thorough and affecting presentation of the damage inflicted by the initial blast, thermal effects, radiation and fire. In contrast to Groves, who said radiation sickness was “a very pleasant way to die,” Morrison described how those who survived the blast and heat “died anyway … from a further effect, the effects of radiumlike rays emitted in great number from the bomb at the instant of the explosion.” Highly impressed by the testimony, one of the Committee members, Senator Edwin Johnson (D-Co) said that “I should like to say to the witness, to Mr. Morrison, that he is the most eloquent witness that I have ever heard since I have been around Congress.” That would be far from the last public praise for Morrison who became a well-known and respected advocate for the cause of nuclear disarmament.</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>The U.S. Navy sent an intelligence group -- a “technical mission”-- to do a wide-ranging investigation of a variety of topics, mainly involving naval technology but also the impact of the atomic bombings. Coordinated by Captain Clifton G. The medical team led by Naval Reserve Captain Shields Warren, a Harvard pathologist, produced two reports on the atomic bombings, including this report, which contains the striking finding: “The atomic bomb is the most terrible agent of destruction known to man.” <a href="#_edn38" name="_ednref38">[45]</a></p>e atomic bombings, including this report, which contains the striking finding: “The atomic bomb is the most terrible agent of destruction known to man.” <a href="#_edn38" name="_ednref38">[45]</a></p>

<p>Illustrated with culturally insensitive graphs, the Navy report provided broad coverage of radiation exposure, including “secondary” or “residual radiation” and their effects. The report provided detailed information on the fallout problem, with a finding of “physiologically significant” levels of “residual radiation” in outlying areas after the bombings, including potentially dangerous levels in Nagasaki’s Nishiyama district. One of the data points was a reference [see page 45] to what became known in Japan as “black rain” – radioactive “droplets of yellow-brown liquid” that fell after the explosion.<a href="#_edn39" name="_ednref39">[46]</a></p>

<p>Like Stafford Warren, Shields Warren found the Siemes narrative compelling and presented it in its entirety. The report included first-hand accounts by various Japanese who had been in Hiroshima and Nagasaki at the time of the bombings. One of them, Professor Masao Tsuzuki with Tokyo Imperial University (now University of Tokyo), had used rabbits in radiation effects research in the 1920s. When Philip Morrison, with Los Alamos’ Weapons Physics Division, met Tsuzuki in Tokyo, the latter slapped him on the knee, saying, to the effect: “the Americans—they are wonderful. <div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>A leading member of the British mission to the Manhattan Project, physicist and mathematician <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Penney,_Baron_Penney">William Penney</a> conducted research that helped establish the optimum <a href="https://blog.nuclearsecrecy.com/2012/08/08/the-height-of-the-bomb/">height of burst</a> for the detonations above Hiroshima and Nagasaki to maximize their destructiveness. As a member of the team of scientists and medical doctors who traveled to the two cities during September-October 1945, Penney collected evidence on the physical destruction cause by the bombs, which he analyzed in this report.</p>

<p>Through visual inspection of flagpoles, shrines, cans, glass panels, wooden floors, and other things damaged in the blasts, and noting the distance of each from ground zero, Penney estimated the peak pressure, in pounds per square inch (p.s.i.), experienced by various objects and structures. Summarizing his results on pages 59-60, Penney estimated a peak pressure of 100 p.s.i. in Nagasaki based on damage to an earthenware drainpipe found 1200 feet from ground zero. For Hiroshima, Penney estimated a peak pressure of 35 p.s.i. at ground zero based on damage to the door of a safe. Penney’s report was probably the first systematic look at the problem of peak pressure, which would be a subject of continuing interest for war planners and civil defense specialists.</p>r of a safe. Penney’s report was probably the first systematic look at the problem of peak pressure, which would be a subject of continuing interest for war planners and civil defense specialists.</p>

<p>Penney’s summary of his major conclusions tacitly credited the calculations of Los Alamos scientists: Not only did “the bombs [perform] exactly according to design in their height of burst,” they were “placed in such positions that they could not have done more damage by any alternative bursting point.” Thus, the “extent of the blast damage was exactly that predicted.” In other words, the point of aerial detonation was to maximize damage, not to reduce radioactive fallout, as some later claimed.<a href="#_edn05" name="_ednref05">[49]</a></p>

<p>Penney explained that his report could guide any future use of atomic weapons: “the information collected would enable reasonably accurate predictions to be made of the blast damage likely to be caused in any city where an atomic explosion was conceivable.”</p>

<p>On destruction by fire, Penney observed that it was “tremendous” in both cities, but he did not attach any “special significance” to it, because he believed that it was a “property more of the cities themselves than of the bomb.” Writing that the explosions did not start any fires “instantly,” Penney said that “straw and many grass mats which were hanging outside to air, were ignited immediately and no doubt some fires were started in this way.” A “more likely cause” of fires, in his view, were “charcoal braziers, one of which was to be found in nearly every house.” He further added that the Japanese observed “many peculiar effects due to ‘fire winds’” that were not accurately recorded.</p>

<p>Commenting on radioactivity in a brief section about the “morale” effect of the bombings, Penney observed that the “danger of radioactive contamination on the ground terrified the whole population and had resulted in only spasmodic elements at clearing debris.”</p>effect of the bombings, Penney observed that the “danger of radioactive contamination on the ground terrified the whole population and had resulted in only spasmodic elements at clearing debris.”</p>

<p>Among Penney’s “Main Results” were his estimates of the explosive yields for the Hiroshima and Nagasaki detonations. Penney estimated 5 kilotons (kt) of T.N.T. for Hiroshima and 20 kt for Nagasaki. The latter estimate would turn out to be roughly accurate, but 5 kt for Hiroshima was quite low. Years later, Penney and British colleagues produced an <a href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/73796">estimate</a> of 12 kt for Hiroshima. In 1946, when Penney prepared this report, other Los Alamos scientists and officials were trying to establish yield estimates, but this was difficult for lack of precise data, such as the <em>Enola Gay</em>’s “true altitude” when it dropped the weapon.<a href="#_edn06" name="_ednref06">[50]</a> During the 1980s, Los Alamos published <a href="https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1489669">estimates</a> of 15 kt for Hiroshima and 21 kt for Nagasaki, acknowledging that “the outside limits of uncertainties in these values are believed to be 20 percent for Hiroshima and 10 percent for Nagasaki.”</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>Prepared by Shields Warren, this is a study in the histology, or microscopic anatomy, of atomic bombing victims based on data from autopsies conducted by Japanese and U.S. medical specialists. Written for the use of specialists, the report provides detailed coverage of the impact of ionizing radiation on the body’s organs. Slightly more accessible are the conclusions, which include a chronological presentation of the “pattern of injuries” from the moment of detonation through the weeks that followed, as more and more sickened or died from radiation exposure.</p>s, which include a chronological presentation of the “pattern of injuries” from the moment of detonation through the weeks that followed, as more and more sickened or died from radiation exposure.</p>

<p>Part II of the report discussed residual radioactivity at Nagasaki on the basis of the research of Japanese scientists with Kyushu Imperial University (now Kyushu University) and the Institute of Physical and Chemical Research in Tokyo. Their data, evaluated by Warren as “essentially sound,” was the basis of diagrams at the close of the report, including the “detailed location of the explosion center” and the “pattern of deposition of fission products.”</p>

<p><strong>“The Atom Strikes.” (Source: </strong><a href="https://archive.org/details/25042TheAtomStrikes">Internet Archive)</a></p><p>This 1945 Army Signal Corps film reported on the atomic bombings and their impact on Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Produced during the 1945 visit of the MED survey team, the film emphasized the blast effects of the weapons, with virtually no discussion of radiation sickness or harmful radiation. According to the narration, the Hiroshima bomb was “intentionally exploded well above its target in order to dissipate its radioactive material,” a claim that is not supported by primary sources, which emphasized air burst as a way to maximize blast effects. The film included an interview with Johannes Siemes, where he described his personal experience and then received a few questions. One question was about the “stories of the ruins emitting deadly rays.” That, according to Siemes, was “just rumor,” although his narrative [See document 22] indicated more knowledge of the problem.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><article class="align-center media media--type-image media--view-mode-original-no-link">
