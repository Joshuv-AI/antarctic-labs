 Mechanical Characterization of the NIF Ignition Target TMPSA Bonding Flexure

Abstract
 The Thermo-Mechanical Package Sub-Assembly (TMPSA) provides a critical mechanical,
thermal, and electrical interface between a silicon arm and a TMP aluminum can in a NIF ignition
target. During assembly, sixteen silicon pads are bonded to the aluminum using a ﬁxture that
positions the components and applies a repeatable prescribed displacement through a compliant
ﬂexure. The ﬂexure converts ﬁxture interference into displacement and reaction force. Because
bondline thickness variation must be maintained within +/-4 µm, consistent ﬂexure behavior is
important to the assembly process.
 With a recent string of TMPSAs exhibiting low bond strength, the ﬂexures were inspected.
Despite being manufactured to the same speciﬁcations, ﬂexures were found to exhibit variation in
measured sti ness. Additionally, the measured sti ness values did not always follow the presumed
beam mechanics model.
 This work addresses two questions: whether the bonding ﬁxture is working as intended, and
whether the previously made sti ness measurements are accurate representations of the use case.
The ﬂexures are analyzed using Euler-Bernoulli beam theory, measured beam dimensions, ﬁnite
element analysis, and tolerance stack-up calculations. The analysis shows that the ﬁxed-guided
beam mechanics model appropriately represents the ﬂexure during TMPSA bonding, but the chisel-
tip sti ness measurement method introduces a deformation to the inner ring of the ﬂexure that is not
represented during use. The measured sti ness values should therefore be interpreted as test-
condition sti ness values rather than direct measurements of operational ﬂexure sti ness.
 The discrepancy is therefore attributed primarily to the measurement boundary condition
rather than to failure of the ﬁxed-guided beam model. Recommendations are provided for GD&T,
dimensional inspection, and a representative sti ness testing method to better control bondline
variation.

Introduction
 The TMPSA bond is a critical interface in the NIF ignition target assembly. The bond joins the
Silicon Arm to the Aluminum Can and provides mechanical stability, electrical connectivity, and
thermal control in a NIF ignition target. Because the TMPSA conducts heat away from the center of
the target during cryogenic cool-down, the bond must be su iciently thin and uniform. Excessive
pad-to-pad bond thickness variation can increase impedance and reduce the azimuthal thermal
uniformity required for fuel ice layering [1].

 Figure 2: Exploded view of a NIF Ignition Target, highlighting the TMP aluminum can and silicon arm components

 During assembly, the TMPSA bond is formed by placing silver-ﬁlled epoxy between the silicon
pads and aluminum can surfaces, positioning the components in a bonding ﬁxture, and applying a
controlled load while the adhesive is allowed to ﬂow. The epoxy is subsequently cured according to
the manufacturer’s recommendation while the assembly remains in the bonding ﬁxture. The bonding
load is applied by a compliant ﬂexure component. The ﬂexure converts a designed interference into
repeatable displacement and reaction force, creating a controlled bonding condition intended to
minimize bondline thickness variation across all of the silicon pads.
 A recent pattern of reduced bond-strength yield prompted a review of the bonding ﬁxtures
and ﬂexures. Production ﬂexures are manufactured to standard designs, but upon review were found
to have di erent measured sti ness values from expected values. These di erences raised three

questions: whether the ﬂexures were manufactured within the intended sti ness range, whether the
ﬁxed-guided beam model was appropriate for this geometry, and whether the measured sti ness
values represented the ﬂexure’s operational sti ness during bonding.
 This report evaluates the TMPSA bonding ﬂexure from the system requirements level down to
the component tolerance level. First the bond requirements and ﬁxture function are reviewed. Next,
the ﬂexure is modeled using ﬁxed-guided beam mechanics and compared with ﬁnite element
analysis. Measured sti ness data are then compared with analytical and FEA results. Finally,
tolerance stack-ups are used to estimate variation in prescribed ﬂexure displacement and reaction
force.

TMPSA Bond Requirements and Flexure Role
 The TMPSA bond must satisfy thermal and geometric requirements derived from target
ﬁelding requirements. The bond conducts heat from the TMP aluminum can into the silicon arm
during cryogenic operations. To maintain the required azimuthal thermal symmetry the bondline
thickness must be controlled [1]. An overly thick or nonuniform bondline increases thermal
impedances and can reduce control of fuel-ice uniformity during target ﬁelding.
 Table 1 summarizes how the target-level thermal requirement translates into bondline and
ﬁxture requirements. The target level thermal requirement limits allowable temperature variation
across the bond. This thermal requirement restrains the variation of, and sets an upper limit to, the
thickness of the bond. Finally, the ﬁxture and ﬂexure must create a consistent displacement and
reaction force during bonding so that adhesive ﬂow and cure remain consistent enough to maintain
a required bondline variation.

TMPSA Bonding Fixture Overview
 The TMPSA bonding ﬁxture is used to align and bond the silicon arm to the TMP aluminum
can. The ﬁxture consists of a brass base, a brass application block, and a titanium alloy ﬂexure.
During bonding the silicon arm and TMP can are positioned so that the silicon pads contact the ﬂange

face of the aluminum can with an adhesive layer between them. The ﬂexure is then placed over the
silicon arm, and the application block is placed on top – pressing the silicon arm and TMP can
together to compress and ﬂow the adhesive between them.
 The ﬂexure is the compliant element of the ﬁxture that controls the bonding load. Rather than
relying on a directly applied load, which can induce pad-to-pad bond thickness variability, the ﬁxture
is designed with prescribed interference. When the ﬁxture is closed, and su icient load is applied to
the application block, this interference deﬂects the ﬂexure by a known amount. The ﬂexure sti ness
then determines the force applied to the silicon arm, and ultimately, the TMPSA adhesive bondline.

Figure 7: TMPSA bonding ﬁxture (left) comprised of a brass base (green), and a brass application block (pink). Image on
right shows the ﬁxtures prescribed interference where the ﬂexure presses the silicon arm (black) into the TMP can (silver).

 The application block and ﬂexure also contribute a small gravitational load during bonding.
The 105.0 g application block applied approximately 1.03 N, and the 6.5 g ﬂexure applies
approximately 0.065 N, for a combined gravitational load of approximately 1.09 N. By comparison, a
19.6 N/mm ﬂexure deﬂected by the nominal 250 µm displacement produces approximately 4.9 N of
reaction force. Therefore, the bonding ﬁxture should be interpreted primarily as a prescribed-
displacement loading system rather than a dead-weight loading system. Variation in prescribed
displacement and ﬂexure sti ness is expected to dominate variation in applied bonding force.

Current Flexure Designs and Nominal Geometry
 There are currently multiple ﬂexure designs in use or available for use. Each design consists
of an inner ring and an outer ring and a set of radial beam elements that connect the two rings. The
number of beams, beam width, beam height, and e ective beam length determine the ﬂexure
sti ness. Because the beams act approximately in parallel, increasing the number of beams
increases the sti ness linearly. Beam height and beam length have an especially large impact
because the sti ness scales with ℎ and 1/𝐿 , respectively [4], [5]. Because the beam height is
especially thin, even minor variations in this value can have strong impacts on the ﬂexure sti ness.

Fixed-Guided Beam Mechanics Model
 The ﬂexures are modeled as a series of parallel ﬁxed-guided beams under their operational
loading conditions. In the bonding ﬁxture the inner and outer ﬂexure rings constrain the ends of each
beam. When the ﬂexure is compressed, the dominant deﬂection of the beam occurs in the vertical
direction, while the slope on each end remain constrained. This behavior is consistent with a ﬁxed-
guided beam model with prescribed displacement on one end.

Sti ness Measurement Method
 Flexure sti ness was measured using an Instron compression test [2]. The purpose of the
tests was to determine whether production ﬂexures had consistent sti ness between similar designs,
and whether those values matched the designed sti ness.

The measurement procedure was as follows [2]:
 1. Characterize the Instron system and load cell sti ness by performing a compression test
 against a nominally rigid surface.
 2. Place a ﬂexure in the test setup in an inverted orientation.
 3. Compress the center ring using a chisel-tip to 150 µm of deﬂection.
 4. Record the force required to perform the displacement.
 5. Rotate the ﬂexure by 45° and perform the compression test again.
 6. Correct the data by subtracting the system compliance.

 Upon further review, the testing method used to collect this data was not representative of
at-use loading conditions. In the bonding ﬁxture, the ﬂexure is fully supported through distributed
contact with the silicon arm and compressed by the ﬁxture structure. During the Instron sti ness

signiﬁcantly under the applied load and would distribute load equally to each beam. If the inner ring
geometry is not su iciently sti , then it will partially deﬂect under chisel-tip loading which appears
as a lower measured sti ness. This di erence in contact condition changes the deformation of the
ﬂexure and must be considered before equating these values to the beam models.

vertical displacement of the outer ring by the prescribed interference and the ﬁxed inner ring pressed
against the silicon arm.

Discussion of Sti ness Discrepancy
 The apparent disagreement between the measured sti ness data and the ﬁxed-guided beam
model is the central discrepancy in this study. If the Instron measurements represented operational
sti ness, then the beam model, ﬂexure designs, or measured geometry would have to be wrong.
However, the measured values do not consistently follow the trends predicted by measured beam
geometry. This indicates that the test method itself contributes to the measured displacement.
 For example, ﬂexure E has a measured beam height that is signiﬁcantly larger than nominal,
which predicts a higher ﬂexure sti ness. However, the Instron test measured ﬂexure E as one of the
softer ﬂexures. This inconsistency indicates that the measured sti ness values are strongly
inﬂuenced by the testing conditions. The FEA results support this interpretation. Under use case
loading, the inner ring is fully supported by distributed contact with the silicon arm, and the outer
ring is displaced by the ﬁxture interference. This loading condition produces beam deformation that
is reasonably approximated by the ﬁxed-guided beam model. Under the Instron sti ness test
conditions, the chisel tip applies a local load at two opposite points on the face of the inner ring. This
introduces local deformation to the inner ring that contributes to the measured displacement that is
not represented during TMPSA bonding.

 This e ect is most signiﬁcant for sti er ﬂexure designs because the same prescribed
displacement requires higher force, which increases the local deformation of the inner ring under
point loading. Looking at ﬂexure G, the ﬂexure with the highest predicted sti ness, FEA indicated that
the portion of the inner ring contacted by the chisel-tip deﬂected by 57.29 µm, whereas ﬂexure A has
less than one hundredth of a micron of deﬂection. This discrepancy indicates that the testing method
used to measure the ﬂexure sti ness is not appropriate for higher sti ness ﬂexures, and that even
lower sti ness ﬂexures are not loaded in conditions that mirror their use case. While the FEA and
beam models do not match the measured ﬂexure sti ness, the discrepancy in loading conditions
indicates that the measured values are indicative of the testing setup at the time, and do not discredit
the ﬁxed-guided beam mechanics model.

Requirements Assessment
 The current ﬂexure designs appear capable of producing forces greater than the estimated
minimum force required to thin the adhesive bondline [3]. Therefore, the TMPSA bonding process is
unlikely to be limited by insu icient nominal force. However, satisfying the minimum force estimate
is not equivalent to satisfying the +/-4 µm bondline variation requirement. The primary issue is

repeatability of the at use bonding conditions across all ﬂexures, ﬁxtures, and e ective component
geometry.
 The ﬁxed-guided beam model, supported by operational FEA, is appropriate for estimating
the ﬂexure sti ness during use. The historical Instron measurements should not be used as direct
operational sti ness values because the chisel-tip test condition introduces inner-ring deformation
that is not present during bonding [2]. The remaining concern is the allowable range of applied force
caused by ﬂexure geometry variation and prescribed-deﬂection tolerance stack-up.
 Based on the current analysis, the ﬂexures can likely provide su icient bonding force, but the
current ﬁxture/component tolerances do not provide tight control of the resulting force window.
Additional control of the beam height, e ective beam length, and prescribed displacement is
recommended before the current ﬂexure designs can be considered robust with respect to bondline
variation.

Recommended Design and Tolerance Improvements
 Based on the analytical model, measured dimensions, FEA results, and component and
ﬁxture tolerance stack-up, the primary design recommendation is to control the functional sti ness
and prescribed displacement of the ﬂexure during operation.
 The beam height should be treated as a critical dimension. Its current tolerance speciﬁcation
is approximately +/-10% of nominal, and because sti ness scales with ℎ even small deviations can
have a large impact [4], [5]. While the length is scaled inversely, the tolerance speciﬁcation for the
length is less than +/-1% of the nominal, so changes in length will have a lower impact on the sti ness.
Another recommendation is that beam width and beam length should be speciﬁed directly. The
current dimensional controls specify surface tolerances on the negative space in between each
beam which does not represent the functional requirements of a ﬂexure.
 Additionally, a support for the Instron sti ness testing should be designed to simulate
operational conditions of ﬂexures to allow for accurate sti ness measurements. Manufactured
ﬂexures can then be veriﬁed for production use by using sti ness as a metric and conﬁrming
adherence using a standardized test ﬁxture. Finally, the ﬁxture and component tolerances should be
reduced where practical. The current prescribed displacement can range from 205.5 µm to 294.5 µm,
or 250 µm +/-44.5 µm. Because applied force is directly proportional to prescribed displacement,
this tolerance stack-up can create a signiﬁcant force variation even when ﬂexure sti ness is held

constant. Reducing the tolerance range of the dimensions that control prescribed displacement
would reduce the spread in bonding force and improve process repeatability.
 Together, these changes would improve control of the TMPSA bonding repeatability without
requiring a fundamental change to the ﬂexure design. The ﬁxed-guided beam model and operational
FEA indicate that the current modeling approach is appropriate for estimating ﬂexure behavior during
use. The primary opportunity is therefore to reduce variation in the manufactured ﬂexures and in the
prescribed displacement applied by the bonding ﬁxture. This would narrow the force tolerance
window and provide better conﬁdence that the bonding process can consistently produce bonds
that meet the bondline variation requirement.

Conclusion
 This work evaluated the TMPSA bonding ﬂexure using analytical beam mechanics, measured
ﬂexure dimensions, ﬁnite element analysis, historical sti ness measurements, and
ﬁxture/component tolerance stack-up. The results show that the ﬁxed-guided beam model is an
appropriate ﬁrst-order representation of the ﬂexure under operational bonding conditions [4], [5].
Operational FEA produced sti ness values that were in reasonable agreement with the analytical
beam models, supporting the use of the model for design and tolerance evaluation.
 The historical Instron sti ness measurements should not be interpreted as direct
measurements of operational ﬂexure sti ness. The chisel-tip loading condition used during testing
locally deforms the inner ring of the ﬂexure, adding displacement that is not present when the ﬂexure
is supported in the bonding ﬁxture [2]. This explains why the measured values did not always follow
the expected trends predicted from measured beam geometry.
 Analysis indicates that the ﬂexures are likely capable of producing enough force to thin the
adhesive bondline. However, su icient force alone does not demonstrate compliance with the +/-
4µm bondline variation requirement. The dominant concern is repeatability. Beam height, e ective
beam length, and ﬁxture/component tolerance stack-up can signiﬁcantly a ect the applied bonding
force. To improve process control, future work should tighten functional ﬂexure dimensions, directly
control beam width and e ective length, and repeat sti ness testing using a ﬁxture that better
represents the operational loading condition of the ﬂexure during use.
 Future work should also improve the adhesive ﬂow model by accounting more accurately for
the thixotropic behavior of the silver-ﬁlled epoxy and should correlate predicted force/displacement
windows with measured bondline thickness variation.
