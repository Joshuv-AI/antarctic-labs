Mental health and psychosocial support during ongoing armed conflict: position paper of the Global Collaboration on Traumatic Stress
Authors: Jana D Javakhishvili, Josef I Ruzek, Stéfanie Fréel, Janne L Punski-Hoogervorst, Chris M Hoeboer, Miranda Olff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn this position paper, we highlight the urgent need for mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) systems adapted to the realities of populations affected by ongoing armed conflicts and, therefore, continuous traumatic stress. Existing frameworks largely address post-disaster contexts, offering limited guidance for settings of sustained threat. Drawing on an extensive literature search, a scoping review on MHPSS during ongoing armed conflicts, and clinical experience, we outline key mental health challenges during ongoing armed conflict and identify a critical gap in scalable, culturally responsive interventions. Based on this analysis, we propose recommendations across policy, training, staff care, and research domains. We advocate for trauma-informed, rights-based, participatory, and adaptive care systems that strengthen local capacity, integrate measurement-based approaches, and utilize digital and transdiagnostic stepped-care models. Through this paper, we aim to catalyse research, inspire innovation, and encourage global collaboration to more effectively support the mental health of communities living under the enduring strain of armed conflict.KEYWORDS: Armed conflict, building back better, contunuous traumatic stress, mental health, MHPSS, ongoing threat, resilience, stepped-care, transdiagnostic, war

Introduction

The number of armed conflicts has significantly increased over the past decade, with profound and growing impacts on civilian populations worldwide (Davies et al., 2025; Le et al., 2022). Many of these conflicts persist for extended periods, fluctuating in intensity without clear beginnings or ends, and are often punctuated by episodes of mass violence.

Although it is well established that resilience is the most common response to life-threatening events, violence, and loss (Bonanno, 2004; Bonanno et al., 2023), and recent studies have also underscored the remarkable resilience of war-affected populations (Kaim et al., 2024; Kimhi et al., 2023; Saeedi et al., 2025), the demand for mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) in contexts of armed conflict remains substantial. Numerous studies have documented the impact of armed conflict on mental ill-health, particularly in post-conflict settings (Carpiniello, 2023; Charlson et al., 2019; Cheung et al., 2019; Comellas et al., 2015; Morina et al., 2010, 2018; Murthy & Lakshminarayana, 2006; Roberts et al., 2019; Shukri et al., 2023; Wagner et al., 2020). Far fewer studies have explored mental health during the active phase of armed conflict. However, recent evidence highlights elevated distress among civilians, including youth and forcibly displaced people [both refugees and internally displaced people (IDPs)] living under threat. Documented issues include sleep disturbances, symptoms of anxiety, depression, acute stress, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), loneliness, somatic complaints, and substance use (Goto et al., 2024; Hyland et al., 2023; Korda et al., 2025; Kurapov et al., 2023; Pavlova & Rogowska, 2023; Veronese et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2023). A number of studies reveal that the association between exposure to different war-related stressors (e.g. changes in daily routine, proximity to war zone, number of air raid alarms and explosions, traumatic loss, forced displacement) and increased psychological symptoms is influenced by factors such as subjective perception of threat, pre-existing mental health conditions, level of well-being, income, ethnic minority/majority status, gender and age (Amsalem et al., 2025; Eshel et al., 2023; Kurapov et al., 2024; McElroy et al., 2024).

During the initial stages of armed conflict, immediate survival and adjustment shape mental health and psychosocial needs (Shalev, 2022). As conflicts become protracted, accumulating losses, chronic uncertainty, concerns about safety, economic hardship, separation from loved ones, inability to rest and related fatigue, loss of meaning, shattered trust, and moral/emotional exhaustion increasingly create additional sources of distress, requiring a corresponding shift in MHPSS priorities (HIAS Ukraine, 2023; Taubman-Ben-Ari et al., 2025).

Although well-established guidelines exist for MHPSS in post-disaster contexts – such as The European Network for Traumatic Stress (TENTS) Guidelines (Bisson & Tavakoly, 2008), the Essential Elements of Mass Trauma Intervention (Hobfoll et al., 2007), the Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) Guidelines (Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC), 2007), and psychological first aid (PFA) protocols (National Child Traumatic Stress Network (NCTSN), 2006; Ruzek et al., 2007) – there is limited evidence and guidance on how to provide effective MHPSS during active, episodic, or prolonged armed conflict.

To address this gap, this position paper explores MHPSS needs and intervention strategies during ongoing armed conflict. This paper is a product of the joint effort of the Armed Conflict Thematic Group of the Global Collaboration on Traumatic Stress (GCTS). GCTS is a network of traumatic stress professionals, both researchers and clinicians, who collaborate on issues of global importance, to develop and coordinate activities that benefit trauma survivors around the world.

The sections that follow first outline the paper’s methodology, then review existing evidence on MHPSS interventions for civilians – residents of war zones and IDPs, both children and adults – amid ongoing armed conflict and highlight key considerations for implementation. The paper concludes with recommendations for MHPSS policy, training, staff care, and research. While not exhaustive, it serves as a mapping exercise and offers a preliminary framework to guide context-sensitive MHPSS implementation in war-affected settings.

Methodology

We defined armed conflict using the definition recommended by the European Parliament, covering international (between states) and non-international (between governments and organized armed groups, or between such groups within a state) confrontations (International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), 2015). For ongoing armed conflict, we drew on the Uppsala Conflict Data Program, which sets a threshold of at least 25 battle-related deaths per calendar year (Pettersson & Wallensteen, 2015).

We acknowledge that MHPSS encompasses needs beyond mental health care (including shelter, food, water, and other essential resources), and that it is shaped by policies and cultural contexts influencing psychosocial distress and cycles of violence (Ager, 2021; Miller et al., 2021). However, for pragmatic reasons, the focus of this paper is on the mental health care dimension of MHPSS.

Our position and recommendations were developed through the following six-step process (Figure 1):

Step 1. Multidisciplinary team. A group of six professionals – psychiatrists, psychologists, public health policy specialists, and humanitarian workers – with extensive experience in conflict settings (in Armenia, Azerbaijan, Bosnia Herzegovina, Democratic Republic of Congo, Georgia, Israel, Myanmar, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Sudan, South Sudan, and Ukraine) was convened. Members brought expertise in clinical practice, research, training, policy, project management, and staff care. Two members also have direct experience of living and working in war zones during both active and protracted phases of armed conflicts.

Step 2. Reviewing existing knowledge.

2.1. We examined disaster mental health guidance, namely TENTS Guidelines (Bisson & Tavakoly, 2008), the Essential Elements of Mass Trauma Intervention (Hobfoll et al., 2007), IASC Guidelines (Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC), 2007), and psychological first aid (PFA) protocols (National Child Traumatic Stress Network (NCTSN), 2006; Ruzek et al., 2007), adapting principles relevant to ongoing conflict, as well as the European Society for Traumatic Stress Studies (ESTSS) Recommendations on MHPSS During COVID-19 Pandemic (Javakhishvili, Ardino, Bragesjö, Gorniak, et al., 2020). This later document was included as it addresses the population’s mental health needs during an ongoing threat.

2.2. We also reviewed military approaches to mental health management during ongoing threat in different countries (Australia, Canada, Ireland, Germany, Israel, Lebanon, New Zealand, UK, and USA) based on corresponding publications including (but not limited to) available systematic and scoping reviews (Anderson et al., 2020; Maglione et al., 2022; McCreary, 2019; Russel & Figley, 2017) to explore methods that could be translated to support civilians during armed conflicts.

2.3. We examined the only existing systematic review of interventions during ongoing threat (Yim et al., 2024).

2.4. Finally, we conducted a scoping review of evidence on MHPSS interventions during active armed conflict (detailed in the next section).

Step 3. Reflection. Based on the work described in Steps 2.3 and 2.4, we came to the conclusion that at present moment there is extremely scarce evidence on what works in situations of ongoing armed conflict and threat, and that there is a need to agree on principles of policy, research, and staff training in order to collect more robust evidence in the near future. For this purpose, from June 2023 to June 2025, we held quarterly reflection meetings to synthesize findings from the conducted reviews and members’ field observations. We also remained continuously in contact with sharing updates from the field (e.g. novel publications, podcasts, and presentations). Based on the reflection, and through thematic analysis, we identified seven thematic domains for recommendations: systematic approach, foci of interventions, target groups, interventions for stress-related conditions, scaling-up (including training and staff-care), research, and building back better.

Step 4. Key considerations. We formulated key considerations for each of the identified domains, based on the information obtained via Step 2 but also thematic group members’ experiences of working in the different conflict settings affected by continuous traumatic stress.

Step 5. Recommendations. Based on these considerations, we elaborated a package of recommendations on MHPSS during ongoing armed conflict, with a special focus on policies, research, and training of human resources which would allow more systematic effort to collect evidence on MHPSS during ongoing armed conflict and threat, for addressing continuous traumatic stress-related mental health challenges in an evidence-based way.

Step 6. External review. Draft recommendations were circulated to colleagues with conflict-zone experience in diverse cultural and political contexts (e.g. Armenia, Azerbaijan, Georgia, Israel, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Lebanon, Rwanda, Sri Lanka, Syria, Ukraine). Their feedback informed the final version.

Methodology steps.

Available evidence on MHPSS interventions during ongoing armed conflict

In this section, we offer a brief summary of the scoping review; full report of the scoping review is forthcoming (Fréel et al., 2026). The search included English-language quantitative studies published from January 1991 to May 2025 in EMBASE, PsycINFO, PubMed, and SCOPUS. Of 5535 records screened, 47 met inclusion criteria, representing interventions investigated in the Middle East (n = 22), Africa (n = 14), South America (n = 6), Europe (n = 4), and Asia (n = 2). Of these studies, 33 investigated MHPSS interventions for children and/or adolescents (19 exclusively and 14 also including adult populations) and 14 focused exclusively on adults. Seven studies used cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT); five employed narrative exposure therapy (NET). Additional approaches included interpersonal/group psychotherapy (n = 5), psychoeducational or psychosocial interventions (n = 2), family therapy (n = 1), reconsolidation of traumatic memories (n = 1), and stress interventions (n = 1). Six studies implemented broader wellness or social cohesion programmes. Out of 33 studies focused on children and adolescents, only two assessed evidence-based treatment (EBT), while out of 14 studies on adults nearly half were focused on assessing EBT (in particular trauma-focused CBT). Only four interventions used technology (internet tools or phone consultations). Most were conducted in person in urban, rural, or IDP settings. Cultural adaptation (beyond language translation) and qualitative descriptions of study settings, populations, and conflict status were rarely reported.

The scoping review identified substantial methodological limitations, including heterogeneous study quality, limited use of standardized outcome measures, absence of active control conditions, and insufficient follow-up assessments. These constraints restrict the ability to draw firm conclusions regarding the effectiveness and sustainability of MHPSS interventions implemented during ongoing armed conflict. The identified evidence gaps informed the development of recommendations for policy, research design, and workforce training presented in this paper, aimed at strengthening methodological rigour and generating more robust and context-sensitive evidence in future studies.

Key considerations on mental health responses to ongoing threat

The present section focuses on the mental health care components of psychosocial support and outlines key considerations for individual-, community-, and population-level MHPSS strategies under ongoing threat.

Systemic approach

Coordination

The complex system of stressors affecting populations during ongoing armed conflicts, along with their corresponding mental health and psychosocial needs, calls for a systemic, comprehensive, and coordinated response. The challenge is to design and implement multiple and multilevel interventions simultaneously, while also integrating the contributions of many – both local and international – actors, whose roles will need to be carefully assessed, selected, managed, and incorporated into the larger response, to achieve synergy and maximize use of diminishing financing but also, and no less importantly, to reduce potential unintentional harm.

Meaningful participation, local ownership, and leadership

It is important to keep power dynamics in mind and to ensure that priorities, expertise, and decision making are locally informed and led. To be efficient, planning and implementation must consider not only the needs but also the resources of targeted communities, families, and individuals, and should follow decolonization frameworks. Locally driven processes enhance contextual relevance, legitimacy, and sustainability. In their absence, interventions risk remaining externally imposed and insufficiently responsive to lived realities; in the long run, once the immediate threat subsides, affected communities may continue to experience hardship and inequities, undermining peace and stability, for which continuous support from local leadership is crucial.

Diversified delivery settings

A range of delivery settings will probably be required, including emergency gathering places, medical settings, remote treatment facilities, and tele-mental health. Non-medical settings (e.g. schools, and places of worship or community gathering) may also be effectively used for delivering interventions, if they are safe. Active outreach will be especially important, as help-seeking rates are low even in relatively safe environments – particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) (Chikovani et al., 2015; Roberts et al., 2019) – and can be expected to be even lower in high-threat warzones.

Stepped-care approach

With minor adjustments – specifically, shifting the focus from post-disaster contexts to situations of continuous traumatization – the IASC intervention pyramid (International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), 2015) may serve as a practical framework for a comprehensive stepped-care approach amid ongoing threat. This framework spans a continuum from universal psychosocial support at its base to more specialized mental health interventions at higher levels. The mental health care component could be informed by the staging model presented by Patel et al. (2018), which allows for diversified interventions targeting different levels of need. Services need to address mental health problems across their full spectrum, from general distress and subthreshold symptoms to diagnosable conditions. Policies may promote prevention, positive coping, and community-based self- and mutual-help initiatives at individual, family, and community levels.

Foci of interventions

Promoting resilience

Resilience-building at individual, family, and community levels could serve as a key principle for large-scale preventive interventions in armed conflicts. Defining flexibility as the main mechanism behind resilience, Bonanno identifies four core capacities (Bonanno, 2021a, 2021b; Bonanno et al., 2015, 2023): (1) context sensitivity (e.g. gathering reliable information), (2) coping flexibility (adapting strategies through trial and error), (3) emotional regulation flexibility (managing emotions when strategies fail), and (4) monitoring outcomes (assessing and adjusting problem-solving). Interventions can target these capacities, together with the five essential elements of immediate and mid-term mass trauma intervention identified by Hobfoll et al. (2007): safety, calming, connectedness, self- and community efficacy, and hope. In addition, Shalev (2022) proposes resilience tips for surviving warfare adversities, highlighting such strategies as staying informed, maintaining routines, ensuring safety when/where possible, building resources, anticipating developments, sustaining attachments, and maintaining genuine connection with others. As conflict persists, interventions must also support long-term adaptation.

Balancing expectations of resilience with timely care

Expectation of resilience should not delay early detection or timely intervention. Based on identified needs, intervention targets may extend beyond symptoms and psychological distress to include relational and social challenges, such as anger and interpersonal conflict, as well as functional impairments, such as parenting and occupational functioning at both individual and family levels. At the community level, interventions should also draw on culturally and contextually relevant practices that strengthen resilience (e.g. faith-based activities, traditional crafts, or communal arts). Such practices can contribute not only to individual coping but also to the restoration of social cohesion and cultural resources disrupted by displacement and conflict.

Justice, moral injury, and meaning making

Justice and moral injury are central in contexts of human-made violence (Herman, 2005; Rothenberg, 2022; Stevens et al., 2013). Multisectoral collaboration with human rights and justice-oriented local and international organizations is essential: there is no mental health without justice, and cycles of violence cannot be broken in its absence. Justice may also encourage thinking about and orientation towards the future (how to assure justice after the conflict ends), which may contribute to meaning making of traumatic experiences. Experiences of moral injury and concerns about justice should be understood not only as individual but also as collective phenomena, requiring interventions that engage communities and groups alongside individuals.

Maladaptive cognitions

A recent mixed-method survey across Ukraine (HIAS Ukraine, 2023) revealed that up to 38% of respondents explained their distress (mood swings, difficulties in containing their children’s fears and in supporting them) as their personal weakness, underscoring the need to address maladaptive cognitions amid ongoing threat. Well-designed targeted psychoeducation and other CBT-based interventions may help in pathologizing stress-related symptoms and overcoming maladaptive cognitions.

Negative social emotions

These should be understood not only as individual responses but also within their broader social and cultural contexts. Remote emotional support sessions with frontline psychosocial workers in 2022–2023 in Ukraine revealed intense experiences of guilt, anger, existential distress, and exhaustion. Importantly, negative social emotions (e.g. guilt) were often seen as having adaptive functions: those who fled abroad felt guilty towards those who remained; western Ukrainians towards easterners; easterners towards those at the frontline; and those at the frontline towards the fallen. This layered, collective guilt was described as a temporary, yet powerful, source of cohesion and solidarity within the nation (Javakhishvili, 2023).

Tolerance towards uncertainty

Although evidence is limited, available research and clinicians’ reports from Israel, Gaza, and Ukraine since 2022 indicate that uncertainty about the future is a major source of distress for civilians (HIAS Ukraine, 2023; Saeedi et al., 2025; Taubman-Ben-Ari et al., 2025). Strengthening tolerance of uncertainty may therefore be considered as one of the key intervention targets, integrated into resilience- and coping-focused approaches. Corresponding interventions still need to be developed and evaluated.

Target groups

Mental health for all

MHPSS strategies should be grounded in human rights principles, based on impartiality and non-discrimination, and be responsive to the diverse needs of affected populations, especially vulnerable groups. Regardless of birthplace, nationality, age, sex, socioeconomic status, race, religion, ethnicity, or sexual orientation, equitable and affordable access to quality and timely mental health care must be ensured for all, in line with principles of a global mental health movement (Frankish et al., 2018) and universal health coverage (World Health Organization (WHO), 2021).

Family- and community-based approaches are important. While the moral imperative of ‘mental health for all’ is clear, constrained resources during armed conflict demand careful selection of the unit of intervention: individuals, families, organizations, or communities. Emotional distress often extends beyond trauma survivors to those around them. Community-based systems should therefore engage family members to harness familial resources for recovery and prevent secondary distress among relatives. The potential of community-based approaches should be fully leveraged, especially in collectivistic cultural contexts, and grounded in cultural practices. Interventions that promote mutual aid, social support, and cohesion hold promise, and more research is urgently needed on multilevel social ecology interventions that go beyond the individual level, encompassing couples, families, schools, neighbourhoods, communities, and policy systems (Kohrt, 2013; Slone & Peer, 2021).

Population-level interventions

These may include strengthening community problem-solving, managing risk communication, media messaging, and scalable interventions to enhance individual, family, and community coping. Depending on context, this may also include work to support reconciliation and thus lay the groundwork for transitional justice, repair of ruptured social fabrics, and the rebuilding of cultural resources.

Interventions addressing stress-related mental health problems

Trauma-informed care

Although research is needed to demonstrate the effectiveness of trauma-informed care and its constituent elements (Bendall et al., 2021; Hanson & Lang, 2016), the omnipresence of exposure to traumatic experiences in warzones argues for the centrality of a trauma-informed perspective. All MHPSS policies, programmes, and services operating amid ongoing armed conflict should be trauma informed. Trauma-informed systems promote trauma awareness among stakeholders (including schools, local authorities, service providers and policymakers), prevent retraumatization, and support recovery and resilience (Javakhishvili, Ardino, Bragesjö, Kazlauskas, et al., 2020; Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2014).

Transdiagnostic and trans-problem interventions

Transdiagnostic and trans-problem interventions may be particularly valuable for both prevention and treatment, because they increase access to mental health care. Namely, by targeting core psychological processes shared across multiple mental health conditions and providing skills for addressing a variety of different problems, these approaches may offer advantages in terms of cost-effectiveness (Makhashvili et al., 2022). When such interventions are insufficient, diagnosis-specific, advanced treatments – including trauma-focused therapies – should be considered, within the staging model of a mental health framework (Patel et al., 2018).

Adaptation of existing interventions for ongoing threat

It seems likely that many clinical interventions that were developed and tested in conventional circumstances (i.e. after trauma exposure has ceased) could be adapted/modified for use during threatening conditions of ongoing military attack. For example, disaster-related intermediate-intensity CBT interventions have been developed as early post-trauma interventions to prevent the development of PTSD (O’Donnell et al., 2020), and research into their effectiveness is beginning with populations relevant to conditions of war, including gun injury (Williams, Hambrick, et al., 2024) and sudden loss (Williams, Hardt, et al., 2024). Similarly, interventions used to treat acute stress disorder (Briant, 2017) could be adapted for situations of ongoing threat.

Continuous traumatic stress

In contexts of ongoing threat, the construct of continuous traumatic stress (Straker, 1987) has gained increasing conceptual and empirical attention (Stevens et al., 2013), particularly in war-affected settings where exposure is prolonged and lacks a clear endpoint (Frankova et al., 2025; Goral et al., 2021; Kira et al., 2013; Zasiekina et al., 2024). However, the data remain preliminary, and further investigation of the specific characteristics of continuous stress, including adequate measures to evaluate accompanying symptoms, is needed to have more robust evidence on this condition.

Sociocultural adaptation

Interventions developed in other sociocultural contexts require adaptation to align with local socioeconomic­–political conditions and culture. Striking a balance between elements that are generalizable across settings and those that are context specific remains a significant challenge. Insufficient cultural/contextual adaptation carries the risk of wasting valuable resources on an intervention that could have been further optimized for effectivity. A thorough understanding of the local context, including evolving conflict dynamics, is essential to improve intervention effectiveness and implementation.

Time sensitivity

Mental health and psychosocial needs evolve across different phases of a conflict. MHPSS intervention strategies should be adapted to the shifting realities of acute emergencies, prolonged insecurity and uncertainty, and post-conflict transitions. Early interventions may prioritize survival, distress reduction, and practical support. Over time, priorities shift towards addressing war-related fatigue, fostering tolerance of uncertainty, building social cohesion, and meaning making. Stabilization, emotional regulation, and strengthening of coping strategies must be prioritized across all phases, alongside the provision of treatment for trauma-related and stress-induced conditions.

Scaling up interventions

Task-shifting

Many countries and regions affected by war or armed conflict are likely to have few mental health professionals, and ongoing hostilities will greatly degrade helping capacity. Consequently, helping efforts cannot rely solely on professional assistance delivered to individual clients. Alternative approaches, including written self-help materials, media-based education, digital technologies (e.g. internet, phone), care provided by those who are not mental health professionals (e.g. healthcare workers, paraprofessionals, social network members), and task-shifting strategies, will need to be developed. All of these elements require an extensive supportive infrastructure in terms of pre- and in-service training, supervision and staff care, evaluation, and administration.

Evidence-based training

Scalable models (e.g. train-the-trainer) are needed to rapidly build local capacity, using evidence-based methods (Frank et al., 2020) and evaluating training efforts wherever possible. Where feasible, digital formats, including artificial intelligence, can expand access and safeguard participants and providers. For clinicians, training should prioritize best practices and evidence-based prevention and treatment protocols. In conflict settings, where traditional long-term models are not feasible, smart, targeted modules in brief trauma-informed, transdiagnostic care are essential. Booster sessions can adapt the skills of those already trained in trauma-focused therapies. Online training, with structured supervision, can help to sustain quality and mitigate burnout. Training should also introduce practical measurement tools, fostering a culture of measurement-based care.

Technology-facilitated interventions

Scaling up mental health support is likely to depend in part on technology-facilitated care, which can overcome barriers to physical access caused by safety risks and shortages of local providers. Remote professionals can deliver support and coping skills training via the internet and phone, where feasible. For instance, a single session of phone-delivered anxiety management reduced anxiety more effectively than standard hotline counselling among Israeli citizens under missile threat (Somer et al., 2005). Emerging evidence from conflict zones (Javakhishvili et al., 2023; Lahutina et al., 2024) supports the promise of digital interventions. Research shows internet-based treatments can effectively address PTSD (Bröcker, Olff, et al., 2024; Bröcker, Scheffler, et al., 2024; Ruzek, 2021) and other mental health conditions (Philippe et al., 2022), including in LMICs (Fu et al., 2020). In addition, a growing number of self-help apps targeting stress, sleep, and coping (Kuhn et al., 2018; Ruzek et al., 2016) offer scalable tools that can be adapted across languages and contexts, often more efficiently than expanding professional workforces in each setting.

Helping the helpers

Helping the helpers to balance duty of care with self-care, and team management in emergency/humanitarian settings, with a particular focus on moral injury, vicarious trauma, and burnout, is crucial, along with ensuring their physical safety. Staff care, peer-support practices, guaranteed ‘time-outs’ and retreat should be systematically integrated into response efforts and not considered as luxury. The phenomenon of shared traumatic reality (Hutto et al., 2024; Taubman-Ben-Ari et al., 2025) between providers and recipients must also be acknowledged, making available access to supervisors and staff care professionals from outside the conflict setting, potentially via digital platforms, where feasible. At the same time, health professionals around the globe may also have a duty to engage in advocacy, education, and policy reform internationally in order to avoid the normalization of violence associated with armed conflict (Kerbage et al., 2025).

Research

Prioritizing research

In contexts of ongoing war and conflict, the urgency to deliver aid often takes precedence. Local groups mobilize quickly, developing interventions tailored to immediate needs, while external agencies implement programmes that are often based on evidence from different populations. Yet to enhance the effectiveness of support for populations at risk, it is crucial to integrate formal evaluations, assessing whether interventions achieve their intended outcomes and to what extent they are beneficial. Research and data collection, conducted through participatory mechanisms, must be recognized by stakeholders as integral components of MHPSS, not a luxury, and prioritized in humanitarian funding strategies.

Measurement-based care

Owing to resource constraints and unstable implementation environments, gold-standard designs such as randomized controlled trials are often not feasible. Nonetheless, alternative research approaches, such as conducting a cross-sectional study before large-scale implementation to assess whether an intervention addresses the most important issues, using quasi-experimental designs, or applying simple pre–post-follow up evaluations, can provide valuable insights into the effectiveness of both local and externally developed interventions, supporting more informed resource allocation. Transitioning to a measurement-based care model is increasingly recognized as a global mental health priority (Jensen-Doss et al., 2020; Lewis et al., 2019) and is equally relevant in contexts of ongoing armed conflict.

Strengthening local research capacity

Research capacity in conflict-affected settings is highly variable, ranging from well-established academic infrastructures to contexts where research systems are fragile or absent. In settings with limited capacity, efforts should prioritize investment in local expertise rather than the ‘export’ of data. Supporting local professionals with the skills to independently design, implement, analyse, and interpret MHPSS research is essential. This will help to empower local communities, including academic community and universities, and avoid post-colonial approaches to research. Participatory approaches, such as community-based participatory research grounded in open communication, cultural safety, humility, shared learning, and respect, help to ensure that research is led by and responsive to affected communities. International partnerships can support this through training, mentorship, and collaborative projects. Implementing FAIR (findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable) data principles (Kassam-Adams & Olff, 2020) can further enhance the utility and impact of existing datasets for both local and global actors.

Building trust and research literacy

In some settings, particularly in post-totalitarian or politically repressive contexts, research initiatives may be met with public suspicion, especially when conducted by, or in partnership with, international actors. Such concerns often reflect historical experiences in which data collection was associated with surveillance, control, or misuse.

It is therefore essential to engage communities through transparent communication about study aims, data protection, and intended use of findings. Addressing concerns openly is not only an ethical obligation but also an opportunity to strengthen public understanding of the societal and practical value of research. Fostering research literacy and dialogue can enhance participation, counter misinformation, and contribute to more equitable and collaborative knowledge production.

Agreed-upon research standards

To facilitate cross-study comparisons and evidence synthesis, the field would benefit from a core set of agreed-upon outcome measures and reporting conventions. While individual studies may retain flexibility in broader design, aligning on common indicators would substantially enhance the coherence and utility of MHPSS research in conflict zones.

Building back better

While armed conflicts are profoundly destructive, they may also generate openings for systemic transformation. Increased international attention, influxes of donor funding (although increasingly fragile within the shifting dynamics of global politics), and the presence of global actors can create conditions in which long-standing deficiencies in mental health systems are brought to attention, and opportunities for reform emerge.

Within this context, the building back better (BBB) principle (United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (UNDRR), 2025) offers a potentially valuable framework for guiding the development of multisectoral MHPSS systems. Originally introduced in post-disaster recovery, BBB emphasizes not merely restoring previous systems but seizing the opportunity to create stronger, more resilient structures. While its application in protracted crises and armed conflict is still relatively underexplored, and the available evidence remains limited and context dependent (Fernandez & Ahmed, 2019), the concept has important implications for MHPSS in conflict-affected settings.

We propose a tailored definition of BBB in this field: the intentional reshaping (or developing) of MHPSS policies, funding, and service structures during armed conflict to catalyse sustainable and resilient mental health systems for long-term improvements in mental health and well-being supported by multisectoral action and global collaboration. Beyond immediate service delivery, this vision drives systemic reform (World Health Organization (WHO), 2021) through evidence-based practices, strengthened leadership, trauma-informed governance, rights-based legislation, and affordable access to integrated care. It further prioritizes prevention, sustainable financing and strong research infrastructure. It is crucial to seize this window of opportunity, applying BBB principles while balancing urgent survival needs with long-term system strengthening.

Conclusions and recommendations

Ongoing armed conflicts and wars present profound and evolving challenges to the mental health and psychosocial well-being of affected populations, yet knowledge as to how to provide evidence-based and context-sensitive support remains limited. This position paper highlights the urgent need to develop evidence-based professional guidance that helps to enable the delivery of effective MHPSS during periods of ongoing threat. It advocates for scalable, participatory, rights-based, stepped-care, transdiagnostic, and trauma-informed approaches that address both immediate and long-term mental health and psychosocial consequences, based on the BBB principle. By strengthening local capacity, putting in place best clinical and psychosocial practices, advancing targeted research through the use of agreed-upon outcome measures and reporting conventions, and promoting cross-sectoral and global collaboration, the field can move towards more resilient and responsive mental health and psychosocial support systems, even amid the persistent instability of war and armed conflict.
