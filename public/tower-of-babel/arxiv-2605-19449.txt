On the number of finite additive 2-bases
Stefan Weltge, Konrad Zyhalko

Abstract
The number of finite additive 2-bases is known to grow exponentially. While this fact has been established by Marzuola and Miller (2010) using complex analytic techniques embedded in the study of numerical sets, we provide a direct, short proof using elementary probabilistic arguments.



1
Introduction

For a non-negative integer
n
, let
[n]_{0}:=\{0,1,\dots,n\}
.
An
additive 2-base
for
n
is a subset
X\subseteq[n]_{0}
such that
[n]_{0}\subseteq X+X
, where
X+X=\{x+y:x,y\in X\}
.
The study of additive 2-bases is a classical topic in additive number theory, initiated by Rohrbach
[
7
]
whose work focused on the smallest cardinality of an additive 2-base for
n
.
The minimal size of an additive 2-base is known to grow asymptotically as
\sqrt{n}
, and more precise bounds have been established over the years by various authors (see
[
2
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
1
]
,
[
8
]
).

In this short note, we are interested in the number of additive 2-bases for
n
.
To this end, let
\Gamma(n)
be the family of all additive 2-bases for
n
.
The values
|\Gamma(n)|
for
n=0,\dots,65
were calculated by Martin Fuller and are indexed in OEIS
[
6
, A066062]
.
The principal theoretical work yielding a strong lower bound on
|\Gamma(n)|
is due to Marzuola and Miller
[
3
]
, who primarily study “numerical sets with no small atoms” and show that this notion is intimately related to additive
2
-bases.
As a consequence, they find that
|\Gamma(n)|
grows exponentially in
n
and obtain a strong estimate for the asymptotic constant.
However, due to their focus on numerical sets, their approach involves significant complexity.

In this short note, we provide a short and simple proof for the fact that the number of additive 2-bases for
n
grows exponentially in
n
.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists some
\alpha>0
such that
|\Gamma(n)|\geq\alpha\cdot 2^{n+1}
holds for all integers
n\geq 0
.

In what follows, we focus on simplicity rather than optimizing constants, and hence most bounds we obtain are not tight.

2
Exponential lower bound

In all subsequent statements,
n
is assumed to be a non-negative integer.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
X
is a uniformly random subset of
[n]_{0}
and
k\in[n+1]_{0}
, then

\mathbb{P}[k\notin X+X]\leq\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}-1}.

Proof.

For
k=0,1
, the claimed bound is trivial. Letting
E_{i}
denote the event that
\{i,k-i\}\not\subseteq X
, we have for
k\geq 2
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[k\notin X+X]=\mathbb{P}[E_{0}\wedge E_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge E_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[E_{1}\wedge E_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge E_{\lceil k/2\rceil-1}]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{\lceil k/2\rceil-1}\mathbb{P}[E_{i}],

where the second equality follows from the mutual independence of the events.
The claim follows from the observation that
\mathbb{P}[E_{i}]=\frac{3}{4}
for each of the above events, whenever
1\leq i\leq\lceil k/2\rceil-1
and
k\leq n+1
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

If
X
is a uniformly random subset of
[n]_{0}
, then the expected number of integers in
[n]_{0}\setminus(X+X)
is at most
10
.

Proof.

For
k\in[n]_{0}
, let
Y_{k}
be the indicator random variable, that is
1
if
k\notin X+X
, and
0
otherwise.
Note that
|[n]_{0}\setminus(X+X)|=\sum_{k=0}^{n}Y_{k}
and hence using Lemma
2.1
we obtain

\mathbb{E}[|[n]_{0}\setminus(X+X)|]=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}]=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbb{P}[k\notin X+X]\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}-1}\\
=\frac{4}{3}\cdot\frac{1-\left(\sqrt{3/4}\right)^{n+1}}{1-\sqrt{3/4}}\leq\frac{4}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\sqrt{3/4}}\leq 10.

∎

Lemma 2.3
.

We have
|\Gamma(n)|\geq 2^{n}/(n+1)^{20}
.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma^{\prime}(n)
be the family of subsets
X\subseteq[n]_{0}
such that
X
fails to generate at most
20
integers in
[n]_{0}
, i.e.,
|[n]_{0}\setminus(X+X)|\leq 20
.
To estimate the size of
\Gamma^{\prime}(n)
, let
X
be a uniformly random subset of
[n]_{0}
and
Y=|[n]_{0}\setminus(X+X)|
.
By Lemma
2.2
and Markov’s inequality, we have

\mathbb{P}[Y>20]\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y]}{20}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Therefore,

|\Gamma^{\prime}(n)|=(1-\mathbb{P}[Y>20])\cdot 2^{n+1}\geq 2^{n}.

(1)

Now, to every set
X\in\Gamma^{\prime}(n)
add the (at most
20
) elements from
[n]_{0}
that are not in
X+X
.
The resulting set is in
\Gamma(n)
.
Moreover, notice that a single set in
\Gamma(n)
can be obtained in this way from at most
(n+1)^{20}
sets in
\Gamma^{\prime}(n)
, and hence we have

|\Gamma^{\prime}(n)|\leq(n+1)^{20}\cdot|\Gamma(n)|.

(2)

The claim follows by combining inequalities (
1
) and (
2
).
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

For every integer
n\geq 0
, we have

|\Gamma(n+1)|=|\Gamma(n)|+|\{X\in\Gamma(n):n+1\in X+X\}|.

Proof.

The claim follows from the observations that

\{X\in\Gamma(n+1):n+1\in X\}=\{X\cup\{n+1\}:X\in\Gamma(n)\}

and

\{X\in\Gamma(n+1):n+1\notin X\}=\{X\in\Gamma(n):n+1\in X+X\}.\qed

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Consider the ratio

\delta(n):=\frac{|\{X\in\Gamma(n):n+1\in X+X\}|}{|\Gamma(n)|}.

By Lemma
2.4
, we have

|\Gamma(n+1)|=(1+\delta(n))\cdot|\Gamma(n)|.

(3)

By Lemma
2.1
and Lemma
2.3
, we have

\displaystyle 1-\delta(n)=\frac{|\{X\in\Gamma(n):n+1\notin X+X\}|}{|\Gamma(n)|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|\{X\subseteq[n]_{0}:n+1\notin X+X\}|}{|\Gamma(n)|}

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\cdot(n+1)^{20},

and hence

1+\delta(n)\geq 2-\underbrace{\tfrac{4}{\sqrt{3}}\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^{n}\cdot(n+1)^{20}}_{=:t(n)}.

(4)

Note that

\frac{t(n+1)}{t(n)}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\cdot\left(1+\frac{1}{n+1}\right)^{20},

and hence there exists some integer
n_{0}
such that

t(n)\leq\frac{1}{10}

(5)

and

t(n+1)\leq\frac{9}{10}t(n)

(6)

holds for all
n\geq n_{0}
.
We conclude

\displaystyle\frac{|\Gamma(n)|}{2^{n}}=\frac{|\Gamma(n_{0})|}{2^{n}}\cdot\prod_{k=n_{0}}^{n-1}(1+\delta(k))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|\Gamma(n_{0})|}{2^{n_{0}}}\cdot\prod_{k=n_{0}}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{t(k)}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|\Gamma(n_{0})|}{2^{n_{0}}}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=n_{0}}^{n-1}t(k)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|\Gamma(n_{0})|}{2^{n_{0}}}\cdot\left(1-\frac{t(n_{0})}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-n_{0}-1}\left(\frac{9}{10}\right)^{k}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|\Gamma(n_{0})|}{2^{n_{0}}}\cdot\left(1-5\cdot t(n_{0})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|\Gamma(n_{0})|}{2^{n_{0}+1}},

where the first equality follows from (
3
), the first inequality from (
4
) and (
5
), the third inequality from (
6
), and the last inequality from (
5
).
∎
