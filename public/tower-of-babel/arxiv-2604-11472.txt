Resonances extracted in truncated partial-wave analysis are effective mixtures of angular momenta Journal: Physics Letters B
Alfred Švarc

Abstract
In truncated partial-wave analysis, one fits observables that are bilinear in the amplitudes rather than the amplitudes themselves. Truncation is therefore not merely a restriction of the amplitude basis, but of the bilinear interference terms admitted in the fit. As a result, the coefficients extracted in a truncated analysis are generally not projections of the coefficients of the full amplitude. Instead, they are determined by a coupled nonlinear fit in bilinear space and depend on combinations of the full coefficient set that contribute to the retained moments. We demonstrate this in a minimal scalar toy model, where a Hermitian bilinear generated by a Legendre expansion truncated at order 2 is approximated by one truncated at order 1. Even in this simplest case, the fitted low-order coefficients depend on bilinear combinations involving higher-order contributions of the original amplitude, providing a concrete mechanism for truncation-induced angular-momentum mixing. The implication is that quantities extracted in truncated partial-wave analyses that carry resonance information should not, in general, be identified with direct projections of the corresponding quantities of the exact infinite problem. They are truncation-dependent effective mixtures generated by the restricted bilinear fit. Although shown here for scalar scattering, the same algebraic mechanism applies to Legendre-moment analyses and to photoproduction observables. The statement is established at the level of fitted bilinear quantities and reconstructed truncated coefficients; the corresponding interpretation in terms of analytically continued resonance poles is not developed here.

Introduction

Partial-wave methods are among the standard tools for extracting baryon-resonance information from meson photoproduction data. In single-channel pseudoscalar-meson photoproduction, the reaction is described by four complex amplitudes and 16 polarization observables, which has motivated a substantial literature on complete experiments, amplitude ambiguities, and multipole extraction

This is the domain of truncated partial-wave analysis (TPWA), in which the multipole expansion is cut off at some maximal orbital angular momentum
\ell_{\max}
and the remaining parameters are fitted directly to angular distributions and polarization observables

Modern TPWA work has clarified several important aspects of this program. One line of research addresses the relation between complete-experiment ideas and truncated multipole fits, including the question of which observables constrain a finite multipole set most efficiently

[

[

. Closely related is the use of Legendre moments as a compact diagnostic of partial-wave content: one first fits angular distributions by finite Legendre expansions and then studies the energy dependence of the extracted coefficients
[

[

The present work focuses on a point that is physically important, but in our view still not formulated sharply enough. In TPWA, one does not fit the amplitudes themselves. One fits observables, and observables are bilinear functionals of the amplitudes. Therefore truncation is not merely a restriction of the amplitude basis. It is a restriction on the set of bilinear interference terms that are allowed to contribute to the fit. Once this is recognized, a crucial consequence follows: the resonant content extracted in a truncated analysis should not, in general, be identified with the true resonant content of the full, non-truncated problem. Rather, a TPWA at fixed truncation order determines effective coefficients whose fitted bilinear structure is generated by nonlinear combinations drawn from the full coefficient set, not merely by higher-order corrections.

This distinction is stronger than the familiar statement that higher partial waves can influence lower Legendre moments. The issue is not only that omitted waves generate corrections. The point is that, because the fitted quantities are bilinear, lowering the truncation order changes the algebraic structure of the fit itself. As a result, the extracted low-order coefficients are not simple projections of the corresponding coefficients of the full amplitude. They are truncation-dependent effective parameters built from those combinations of the exact amplitudes that survive in the restricted bilinear space. Consequently, the resonances inferred from a truncated fit are, in general, not the true resonances of the exact infinite problem, but effective mixtures of them.

This observation has direct implications for the interpretation of TPWA results. Resonances extracted at two different truncation orders need not represent the same underlying physical objects written with different precision. They may instead correspond to different effective mixtures of the exact non-truncated resonant and non-resonant contributions. In that sense, changing
\ell_{\max}
does not simply refine the same solution; it changes which combinations of the true amplitude are being inferred from the data.

The purpose of this paper is to make this point explicit in the simplest possible setting. We formulate the truncation problem directly at the level of bilinear observables and show that the optimal truncated description is a coupled nonlinear fit, not a coefficient-by-coefficient projection. We then demonstrate the mechanism in a minimal scalar toy model, where a Hermitian bilinear constructed from a Legendre expansion truncated at order
2
is approximated by a Hermitian bilinear truncated at order
1
. In that example, the fitted low-order coefficients depend on bilinear combinations of all coefficients contributing to the retained moments, thereby making the induced angular-momentum mixing completely explicit.

Although the derivation is given for scalar scattering, the algebraic point is general. The same structure underlies Legendre-moment analyses and TPWA fits of photoproduction observables, because these observables are likewise bilinear in the underlying amplitudes. The conclusion is therefore not merely formal: whenever resonances are inferred from truncated bilinear fits, one must distinguish carefully between the true resonances of the full problem and the truncation-dependent effective resonances extracted by the fit.

In this paper, the statement is established directly for the fitted bilinear observables and for the coefficients reconstructed from the retained moments. Since the original coefficients
a_{\ell}(W)
may contain poles, the truncation-induced mixing shown here is already a mixing of pole-bearing quantities. What is not analyzed separately here is the detailed analytic continuation of the reconstructed truncated amplitudes. The further interpretation in terms of analytically continued resonance poles of reconstructed amplitudes is physically well motivated, but lies beyond the explicit scope of the present toy-model derivation.

2
Origin of angular momentum mixing

To keep the discussion transparent, we focus on
2\to 2
scattering of scalar particles.

2.1
The true problem

A general
2\to 2
scalar scattering process is described by the reaction amplitude

f(W,x)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}a_{l}(W)P_{l}(x),

(1)

where the coefficients
a_{l}(W)
contain poles associated with definite angular momentum
l
, and
P_{l}(x)
are the usual Legendre polynomials. This expansion formally extends to infinite order. The partial wave decomposition is unique, and there is no mixing between poles of defined angular momenta.

2.2
Practical truncation

In practice, it is impossible to work with an infinite set of partial waves, so one truncates the series at some finite order
N
:

g(W,x)=\sum_{m=0}^{N}b_{m}(W)P_{m}(x).

(2)

The central point is that the truncated coefficients
b_{m}(W)
need not depend only on one coefficient
a_{l}
with
l=m
once a bilinear of the form
f(W,x)f(W,x)^{*}
is approximated by bilinears constructed from
g(W,x)g(W,x)^{*}
.

2.3
Formulation of the bilinear problem

Since working with an infinite number of terms is impractical, we consider a simplified setting: a Hermitian bilinear of Legendre rank
N
is approximated by Hermitian bilinears of rank
M
with
M<N
:

f(W,x)=\sum_{l=0}^{N}a_{l}(W)P_{l}(x),\qquad g(W,x)=\sum_{m=0}^{M}b_{m}(W)P_{m}(x),\qquad M<N,

(3)

with
a_{l}(W),b_{m}(W)\in\mathbb{C}
. The corresponding Hermitian bilinears are given with:

B_{f}(W,x)=f(W,x)\,\overline{f(W,x)},\qquad B_{g}(W,x)=g(W,x)\,\overline{g(W,x)}.

(4)

To fit
B_{f}(W,x)
by
B_{g}(W,x)
, we expand both quantities in Legendre polynomials:

B_{f}(W,x)=\sum_{l=0}^{2N}F_{l}(W)P_{l}(x),\qquad B_{g}(W,x)=\sum_{l=0}^{2M}G_{l}(W)P_{l}(x),

(5)

where

F_{l}(W)=\sum_{i,j=0}^{N}a_{i}(W)\,\overline{a_{j}(W)}\,C_{ijl},\qquad G_{l}(W)=\sum_{p,q=0}^{M}b_{p}(W)\,\overline{b_{q}(W)}\,C_{pql},

(6)

and the Legendre product coefficients
C_{ijl}
are defined through

P_{i}(x)P_{j}(x)=\sum_{l}C_{ijl}P_{l}(x).

(7)

We minimize the standard Legendre least-squares error

\mathcal{N}=\|B_{f}(W,x)-B_{g}(W,x)\|^{2}=\int_{-1}^{1}|B_{f}(W,x)-B_{g}(W,x)|^{2}\,dx.

(8)

Using orthogonality, this becomes

\mathcal{N}=\sum_{l=0}^{2M}\frac{2}{2l+1}|F_{l}(W)-G_{l}(W)|^{2}+\sum_{l=2M+1}^{2N}\frac{2}{2l+1}|F_{l}(W)|^{2},

(9)

since
G_{l}(W)=0
for
l>2M
. The second sum is independent of
g(W,x)
, so minimizing
\mathcal{N}
is equivalent to minimizing the reduced functional

\mathcal{N}_{\rm red}=\sum_{l=0}^{2M}\frac{2}{2l+1}|F_{l}(W)-G_{l}(W)|^{2}.

(10)

Thus the optimal truncated bilinear fit depends on
f(W,x)
only through
(F_{0},F_{1},\dots,F_{2M})
.

The parameters
b_{0},\dots,b_{M}
do not arise from independent projections of the coefficients
a_{n}
. Instead, they are obtained from a coupled nonlinear optimization problem because

G_{l}(W)=\sum_{p,q=0}^{M}b_{p}(W)\,\overline{b_{q}(W)}\,C_{pql},

(11)

which is quadratic in the unknowns
b_{m}(W)
. Thus each
b_{m}(W)
depends on the whole low-order vector
(F_{0},F_{1},\dots,F_{2M})
.

Therefore, the coefficients
b_{m}(W)
depend on
f(W,x)
only through the bilinear combinations
a_{i}(W)\overline{a_{j}(W)}
that contribute to
(F_{0},F_{1},\dots,F_{2M})
. They do not, in general, depend separately or linearly on the individual
a_{n}(W)
. Truncation in the bilinear problem, therefore, induces angular-momentum mixing in the extracted coefficients: the fitted coefficients of
g(W,x)
generally combine information from many coefficients of
f(W,x)
, but only through the modes of
f(W,x)f(W,x)^{*}
up to order
2M
.

Although derived here for scalar scattering, the same algebraic point applies to Legendre moments of photoproduction observables, which are likewise bilinear in the underlying amplitudes.

The following toy model makes this mechanism explicit.

3
Toy model: connection between the fitted and original amplitudes

Consider the order-2 amplitude

f(x)=a_{0}P_{0}(x)+a_{1}P_{1}(x)+a_{2}P_{2}(x),\qquad a_{0},a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{C},

(12)

and approximate its Hermitian bilinear

B_{f}(x)=f(x)\overline{f(x)}

(13)

by the order-1 Hermitian bilinear

B_{u}(x)=u(x)\overline{u(x)},\qquad u(x)=\alpha_{0}P_{0}(x)+\alpha_{1}P_{1}(x),\qquad\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}\in\mathbb{C}.

(14)

The exact bilinear expansion is

B_{f}(x)=F_{0}P_{0}(x)+F_{1}P_{1}(x)+F_{2}P_{2}(x)+F_{3}P_{3}(x)+F_{4}P_{4}(x),

(15)

with its coefficients given by

\displaystyle F_{0}

\displaystyle=|a_{0}|^{2}+\frac{1}{3}|a_{1}|^{2}+\frac{1}{5}|a_{2}|^{2},

\displaystyle F_{1}

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{Re}(a_{0}\overline{a_{1}})+\frac{4}{5}\,\mathrm{Re}(a_{1}\overline{a_{2}}),

\displaystyle F_{2}

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{Re}(a_{0}\overline{a_{2}})+\frac{2}{3}|a_{1}|^{2}+\frac{2}{7}|a_{2}|^{2}.

(16)

On the other hand,

B_{u}(x)=|u(x)|^{2}=G_{0}P_{0}(x)+G_{1}P_{1}(x)+G_{2}P_{2}(x),

(17)

where

\displaystyle G_{0}

\displaystyle=|\alpha_{0}|^{2}+\frac{1}{3}|\alpha_{1}|^{2},

\displaystyle G_{1}

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{Re}(\alpha_{0}\overline{\alpha_{1}}),

\displaystyle G_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3}|\alpha_{1}|^{2}.

(18)

If the order-1 bilinear ansatz reproduces the first three Legendre coefficients exactly, one has

G_{0}=F_{0},\qquad G_{1}=F_{1},\qquad G_{2}=F_{2}.

(19)

This immediately yields the direct relations

\displaystyle|\alpha_{1}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}F_{2},

\displaystyle|\alpha_{0}|^{2}

\displaystyle=F_{0}-\frac{1}{2}F_{2},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}(\alpha_{0}\overline{\alpha_{1}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}F_{1}.

(20)

Substituting the expressions for
F_{0},F_{1},F_{2}
gives the explicit connection to the original coefficients:

\displaystyle|\alpha_{1}|^{2}

\displaystyle=3\,\mathrm{Re}(a_{0}\overline{a_{2}})+|a_{1}|^{2}+\frac{3}{7}|a_{2}|^{2},

\displaystyle|\alpha_{0}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|a_{0}|^{2}-\mathrm{Re}(a_{0}\overline{a_{2}})+\frac{2}{35}|a_{2}|^{2},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}(\alpha_{0}\overline{\alpha_{1}})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}(a_{0}\overline{a_{1}})+\frac{2}{5}\,\mathrm{Re}(a_{1}\overline{a_{2}}).

(21)

These three equations constrain the fitted order-1 bilinear amplitude up to the usual phase ambiguities.

The important point is that the fitted lower-order quantities depend on combinations of all three original coefficients
a_{0}
,
a_{1}
, and
a_{2}
. Therefore, if the original coefficients contain pole contributions in
W
, then the fitted low-order quantities inherit mixed dependence on those pole-bearing terms. In that precise sense, truncation produces mixing of resonance-carrying contributions already at the level of the fitted bilinear problem. The present toy model does not attempt a separate analysis of the analytically continued poles of the reconstructed truncated amplitudes; its purpose is to establish the mixing mechanism itself.

The above relations hold if the first three Legendre coefficients can be matched exactly by the order-1 Hermitian bilinear ansatz. In general, this need not be the case. If no exact solution exists, the fitted coefficients
\alpha_{0}
and
\alpha_{1}
are defined instead by the minimization of the reduced least-squares functional

\mathcal{N}_{\rm red}=2(F_{0}-G_{0})^{2}+\frac{2}{3}(F_{1}-G_{1})^{2}+\frac{2}{5}(F_{2}-G_{2})^{2}.

(22)

The explicit relations above should therefore be understood as the special case in which the low-order coefficients are reproduced exactly.

It is nevertheless clear that
\alpha_{0}
and
\alpha_{1}
each depend on all three order-2 amplitude coefficients. The analytic structure of the initial coefficients is therefore redistributed throughout the lower-order solution. Consequently, even if one starts with distinct resonant contributions in the
S
,
P
, and
D
partial waves, characterized by
L=0,1,2
, the lower-order bilinear fit represents a superposition of these contributions.

4
Results and conclusions

We have shown that truncation in a bilinear fitting problem is qualitatively different from truncation performed directly at the amplitude level. Since the observables are bilinear in the amplitudes, reducing the truncation order does not merely discard higher partial waves. It changes the set of interference terms admitted in the fit, and therefore changes the nonlinear map between the exact amplitudes and the fitted truncated coefficients.

In this formulation, the coefficients of the truncated amplitude are not obtained by simple projection of the corresponding coefficients of the full amplitude. They are determined through a coupled nonlinear optimization problem in bilinear space. Consequently, the fitted low-order coefficients depend on those bilinear combinations of the full coefficient set that contribute to the retained moments. The toy model makes this dependence explicit: even when the fitted ansatz contains only lower partial waves, its coefficients are functions of combinations involving higher-wave contributions of the original amplitude.

This provides a concrete mechanism for angular-momentum mixing in truncated analyses. More importantly, it clarifies the status of resonances extracted from TPWA. In general, the quantities extracted from a truncated analysis that carry resonance information should not be identified with direct projections of the corresponding quantities of the exact non-truncated problem. They are truncation-dependent effective structures generated by the restricted bilinear fit. Equivalently, the fitted low-order quantities inherit mixed contributions from pole-bearing coefficients associated with different angular momenta in the full theory. This is the explicit result established here. Interpreting the corresponding analytically continued poles of reconstructed truncated amplitudes requires an additional step, which is not pursued in the present short note.

This is the main conceptual point of the paper. The issue is not simply that truncation introduces a correction or an uncertainty. Rather, changing the truncation order changes the meaning of the fitted quantities themselves. Resonance information extracted in a TPWA at one value
\ell_{\max}
need not correspond one-to-one to that extracted at another, because the underlying fitted quantities themselves change under truncation. The two may instead correspond to different effective mixtures of the exact underlying resonant and non-resonant content. Therefore resonances extracted at different truncation orders cannot, in general, be compared one-to-one as though they were approximations to the same exact poles.

The same conclusion applies to Legendre-moment analyses of photoproduction observables. Since these observables are also bilinear in the amplitudes, the fitted moments inherit the same truncation-induced mixing mechanism. The distinction between true and effective resonance content is therefore not an artifact of the scalar toy model, but a generic consequence of fitting bilinear observables in a truncated basis.

The practical message is clear. TPWA remains an extremely valuable and often indispensable tool, but the resonances it extracts must be interpreted with appropriate caution. A truncated analysis does not, in general, reveal the true resonance content of the full problem in a direct way. It yields instead a truncation-dependent effective representation of that content. Recognizing this distinction is essential if resonances extracted in TPWA are to be interpreted as physically meaningful objects rather than as truncation-dependent fitting artifacts.
