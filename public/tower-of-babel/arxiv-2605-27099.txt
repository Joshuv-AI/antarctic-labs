Antisymmetric spontaneous resistivity anisotropy due to hard-axis collapse in polycrystalline Co thin films
Y. Fernandes, J. Geshev, A. M. H. de Andrade, A. D. C. Viegas

Abstract
We investigate magnetoresistance phenomena associated with the magnetization hard-axis collapse in polycrystalline Co thin films. Transport measurements reveal that, for specific orientations of the applied magnetic field, the system exhibits distinct remanent resistance levels in both the in-plane longitudinal and transverse voltage responses. In particular, the planar Hall resistance shows multiple stable and reproducible levels at room temperature, enabling the identification of at least three remanent states that can be distinguished and used for information storage. These resistance levels originate from non-uniform magnetic configurations stabilized after the application and removal of the external magnetic field in the hard-axis region. Since this phenomenon remains largely unexplored, we present an incipient study addressing its potential implications from an applied-physics perspective. The observation of such behavior in polycrystalline Co thin films grown on Si substrates suggests a simple and low-cost platform for spintronic memory and sensing devices based on the remanent planar Hall effect.



I
Introduction

The increasing demand for higher-density and multifunctional data storage technologies has stimulated the search for alternative mechanisms for information encoding beyond conventional schemes. In this context, multi-level magnetic memory devices have attracted considerable attention, as they enable the storage of more than one bit per cell and therefore enhance information density without scaling down device dimensions. Magnetotransport phenomena in thin films are particularly well suited for implementing such functionalities due to their compatibility with planar device architectures and electrical readout schemes, which are directly compatible with complementary metal–oxide–semiconductor (CMOS) technology platforms that underpin modern integrated circuits.

Within this framework, the planar Hall effect (PHE), which arises from the anisotropic magnetoresistance (AMR)
McGuire-1975
in ferromagnetic (FM) materials, is particularly sensitive to the orientation of the magnetization (
\mathbf{M}
) with respect to the electric current. This sensitivity enables the electrical detection of distinct magnetic configurations, making it suitable for memory applications.

A remarkable phenomenon observed in thin films with in-plane uniaxial magnetic anisotropy is the so-called hard-axis collapse (HAC)
Idigoras-2011
;
Idigoras-2014
, in which the remanent magnetic state (acheived after the application of a saturating external magnetic field
H_{\mathrm{sat}}
and its subsequent reduction to zero) in the vicinity of the film magnetization hard axis (HA), deviates significantly from that predicted by the coherent rotation model
Becker-1930
;
SW
. This phenomenon is characterized by a behavior observed as
\alpha
, the angle between
\mathbf{H}
and the nominal easy axis (EA), approaches
90^{\circ}
, where prominent peaks appear in the remanent magnetization,
M_{\mathrm{rem}}
. As a result, the system stabilizes in multiple remanent magnetic configurations rather than in a single state, often associated with non-uniform magnetization distributions that give rise to measurable changes in the electrical resistance
Fernandes-2026
;
Fernandes-2026-2
.

Building on this scenario, we demonstrate that HAC in polycrystalline Co thin films can induce at least three stable and distinguishable resistance levels. These states originate from underlying non-uniform magnetic configurations and can be reproducibly accessed through magnetic-field protocols. Notably, polycrystalline Co was chosen due to its low cost and ease of deposition, in contrast to more complex material systems typically employed in integrated circuits. These results may open avenues for potential applications in magnetic memory and sensing devices based on simple FM thin films.

In polycrystalline FM conductors, AMR is commonly described within a phenomenological framework in terms of the resistivity tensor
\boldsymbol{\rho}
, which depends on the orientation of
\mathbf{M}
in relation to
\mathbf{j}

McGuire-1975
;
Hurd-1974
;
Coren-1962

\boldsymbol{\rho}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{\mathrm{xx}}&\rho_{\mathrm{xy}}\\
\rho_{\mathrm{yx}}&\rho_{\mathrm{yy}}\end{pmatrix},

(1)

where
\rho_{\mathrm{xx}}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{yy}}
are the longitudinal resistivities, and
\rho_{\mathrm{xy}}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{yx}}
are the transverse ones. Assuming a uniform magnetization, the components of
\boldsymbol{\rho}
are given by

\rho_{\mathrm{xx}}=\rho_{\perp}+(\rho_{\parallel}-\rho_{\perp})\cos^{2}\theta_{M},

(2)

\rho_{\mathrm{xy}}=(\rho_{\parallel}-\rho_{\perp})\sin\theta_{M}\cos\theta_{M}.

(3)

Here
\rho_{\parallel}
and
\rho_{\perp}
denote the resistivities for
\mathbf{M}
parallel and perpendicular to
\mathbf{j}
, respectively, while the remaining components,
\rho_{\mathrm{yy}}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{yx}}
, follow from symmetry.

The component
\rho_{\mathrm{xx}}
describes the longitudinal AMR, while the transverse AMR, also referred to as PHE, is measured through
\rho_{\mathrm{xy}}
. Both originate from the spontaneous resistivity anisotropy induced by spin-orbit coupling (SOC)

Figs.

\mathrm{LAMR}=\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{xx}}(\alpha)-\rho_{\mathrm{xx}}^{\mathrm{ref}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{xx}}^{\mathrm{ref}}}\times 100

and

\mathrm{TAMR}=\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{xy}}(\alpha)-\rho_{\mathrm{xy}}^{\mathrm{ref}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{xy}}^{\mathrm{ref}}}\times 100,

(5)

where

at

From these plots, it is evident that the behavior at
H_{\mathrm{sat}}
is well described by Eqs.
2
and
3
, which predict a
\cos^{2}(\alpha-\phi_{j})
dependence for LAMR and a
\sin(2\alpha-2\phi_{j})
dependence for the TAMR, whereas the behavior at remanence is more complex. Peaks near the HA in the LAMR measurements, with an amplitude of about 0.4%, are observed. In contrast, the TAMR changes sign across the HA, leading to an approximately 12% variation in the angular vicinity of the HA. In addition, the TAMR exhibits a behavior approximately proportional to
d\rho_{\mathrm{xx}}/d\alpha
as
\alpha
approaches
90^{\circ}
and
270^{\circ}
. This proportionality indicates that the TAMR indeed originates from the anisotropic scattering mechanism associated with SOC. For other field angles, however, the TAMR is proportional to
-d\rho_{\mathrm{xx}}/d\alpha
. We attribute this primarily to the Hall cross not being perfectly aligned with the EA-HA reference frame, i.e., current density
\mathbf{j}
is not exactly parallel to the HA. Consequently, it is reasonable to expect that the sign of TAMR alternates depending on the remanent magnetic configuration. In addition, possible contributions from domain-wall nucleation and the anomalous Hall effect cannot be ruled out
Nagaosa-2010
.

Although a similar result could, in principle, arise from a longitudinal contribution caused by a misalignment of the Hall cross, we do not believe this to be the dominant mechanism, since such a contribution was not observed in the measurements performed above technical saturation. Furthermore, additional measurements performed with the contact probes intentionally misaligned by approximately
45^{\circ}
did not provide a conclusive evidence that the antisymmetric behavior originates from a superposition of LAMR and TAMR.

As already mentioned, it is well established
Idigoras-2011
;
Idigoras-2014
that the HAC is associated with remanent states exhibiting a certain dispersion in the orientations of the magnetization easy axes. In particular, when
\alpha
is in the HA region, the minimum-energy configuration corresponds to an average magnetization oriented along an intermediate direction between the HA and the EA, distinguishing it from other remanent states. Micromagnetic calculations are able to reproduce similar behavior, indicating that the HAC arises from remanent states characterized by microdomains in which the magnetization vectors adopt V-type configurations with respect to the HA
Fernandes-2026-2
.

We believe that the MR data shown in Fig.

90^{\circ}-\epsilon<\alpha<90^{\circ}

One can identify two immediate potential applications of these remanent states. The first is in position sensing, given that an angular deviation of 3–4 degrees in the vicinity of the HA can modify the TAMR by at least 10%. This performance is more efficient than that of sensors based on LAMR in similar systems, which typically do not exceed 1–2% resistance variations.

The second application is in magnetic memory devices. Indeed, we observe that the TAMR can distinguish at least three distinct remanent states. In this context, it is possible to encode information in these states, induced by applying
\mathbf{H}
with controlled intensity and orientation using current lines, for example, and to read it out through TAMR. In this sense, it seems possible to build a three-state non-volatile magnetic memory device or an event recorder in implementations that require three distinct logical levels in simple applications that do not demand aggressive scaling of device dimensions, which can help reduce implementation costs.

For these types of applications using polycrystalline systems, it is desirable that devices operate based on films with a certain degree of structural tolerance, i.e., they do not require precise control of crystalline disorder or an identical distribution of magnetic anisotropy axes among the grains.

IV
Summary and conclusions

We perform magnetization and magnetoresistance measurements in both saturated and remanent states of polycrystalline Co thin films exhibiting magnetization hard-axis collapse. We demonstrate that distinct remanent states can be clearly differentiated, and that their differences can be readily and accurately detected through longitudinal anisotropic magnetoresistance.

We conclude that the hard-axis collapse may be exploited for potential applications. A key advantage of the systems studied here lies in their simplicity and low fabrication cost, as well as in the fact that they rely on remanent states that naturally emerge after the application of an external magnetic field within specific angular ranges. Therefore, no external stimulus is required to maintain a given magnetic state.

In addition, the use of Si-based substrates makes these systems directly compatible with standard CMOS processing, which is a key requirement for large-scale integration into conventional semiconductor technology platforms. Nevertheless, further advances are required to assess the scalability of this effect and to evaluate the feasibility of reducing the physical dimensions of devices operating based on this mechanism.
