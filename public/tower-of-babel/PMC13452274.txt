Impact of FY*X homozygosity of the DARC receptor on Plasmodium knowlesi line A1-H.1 invasion
Authors: Dalia Díaz-Delgado, Katlijn De Meulenaere, Anna Rosanas-Urgell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Duffy antigen receptor for chemokines (DARC) on the human red blood cell (RBC) surface is a key receptor for Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium knowlesi invasion. Non-synonymous polymorphisms in the DARC transcriptional unit generate two codominant alleles, FY*A (FY*01) and FY*B (FY*02), which confer Duffy-positive phenotypes, and the FY*ES (FY*02N.01), which in homozygosity results in a Duffy-negative phenotype that impairs RBC invasion by both species. A less frequent variant, the FY*X (FY*02W.01) allele, reduces DARC abundance on the RBC membrane and is predominantly found in Caucasian populations. Its impact on P. knowlesi RBC invasion remains poorly investigated. Here, we measured the impact of FY*X homozygosity on the human-adapted P. knowlesi line A1-H.1 invasion. Duffy phenotypes of seven blood donors were determined using agglutination-based assays, and targeted sequencing confirmed FY*X homozygosity in one donor. Plasmodium knowlesi invasion was quantified in RBCs from donors with different Duffy types in a single experiment. After one invasion cycle, fold multiplication was markedly reduced in FY*X homozygous RBCs (1.5) compared to Duffy-positive donors (5.1–6.5). These findings demonstrate that, despite its low frequency, the FY*X allele substantially limits RBC invasion of P. knowlesi in vitro, underscoring the need for accurate Duffy typing in culture-based studies.Keywords: Plasmodium knowlesi, Duffy, Duffy X, DARC, Red blood cell invasion

Introduction

Plasmodium knowlesi zoonotic malaria infections in humans were detected in the endemic regions of Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand, Brunei and Cambodia in 2025. Interest in P. knowlesi has increased over the past decades as reported human infections have risen (WHO, 2025). Malaria control and elimination interventions do not target the natural non-human primate hosts of P. knowlesi, which act as infected reservoirs and serve as a continuous source of human infection (Cox-Singh and Singh, 2008; Moon and Bushell, 2025). In parallel, researchers have increasingly used P. knowlesi as an in vitro model for Plasmodium vivax since 2013, when a macaque isolate was successfully adapted for continuous culture in human red blood cells (RBCs), generating the P. knowlesi A1-H.1 laboratory strain (Moon et al., 2013). Plasmodium knowlesi is phylogenetically closer to P. vivax than to P. falciparum, with more than 80% of its genes having orthologues in P. vivax (Carlton et al., 2008). This close evolutionary relationship provides a powerful experimental system to investigate P. vivax gene function and conserved biological features across the parasite life cycle (Verzier et al., 2019; Ndegwa et al., 2021).

Both P. knowlesi and P. vivax, but not P. falciparum, invade RBCs through the interaction between the parasite Duffy binding protein (DBP) and the Duffy antigen receptor for chemokines (DARC) transmembrane receptor (also known as Atypical Chemokine Receptor 1, ACKR1) (Miller et al., 1975; Wertheimer and Barnwell, 1989; Chitnis et al., 1996; Singh et al., 2005; Demogines et al., 2012). Genetic polymorphisms in the DARC gene lead to different alleles that determine the Duffy blood group, as indicated by the International Society of Blood Transfusion (ISBT) (https://www.isbtweb.org/).

The DARC alleles most functionally relevant for human infections with P. vivax and P. knowlesi are the codominant FY*A (FY*01) and FY*B (FY*02) alleles (Höher et al., 2017; https://www.isbtweb.org/). These alleles encode the Fya and Fyb antigens, respectively (Table 1), generating three Duffy-positive phenotypes: Fy(a+b+), Fy(a+b-) and Fy(a-b+). The two alleles differ by a non-synonymous nucleotide substitution on position 125 of the coding sequence (Iwamoto et al., 1995; Tournamille et al., 1995a). Although both alleles occur at comparable frequencies globally (Meyer et al., 2014; Höher et al., 2017), their distributions are region-specific (Howes et al., 2011). The FY*B allele can carry a SNP in the GATA box in the promoter region (−67T→C, previously called −33C→T and −46C→T), generating a third allele, the FY*ES (FY*02N.01), which silences DARC expression in erythrocytes (Tournamille et al., 1995b). In homozygosis (FY*ES/FY*ES), this allele produces a Duffy-negative phenotype (Fy(a-b-)) that strongly impairs RBC invasion by P. vivax and P. knowlesi (Miller et al., 1975; Singh et al., 2005). This homozygous allele combination is geographically restricted to the sub-Saharan region, where it reaches allele frequencies above 0.9 (Howes et al., 2011; Höher et al., 2017).

A much less frequent variant is the FY*X allele, which most commonly occurs on an FY*B background (FY*02W.01) (Lopez et al., 2015; Höher et al., 2017) and harbours a combination of two non-synonymous mutations (265C→T, and 298G→A) within the first cytoplasmic loop of the DARC receptor (Table 1) (Chown et al., 1965; Olsson et al., 1998; Tournamille et al., 1998). This allele occurs primarily in the Caucasian population at very low frequencies (0.015–0.030), depending on the study (Gassner et al., 2000; Schmid et al., 2011; Manfroi et al., 2015; Armstrong-Fisher et al., 2023). These mutations are associated with a marked reduction in DARC protein levels on the RBC membrane in FY*X homozygotes, reaching approximately 10% of the levels detected in Duffy-positive individuals (Tournamille et al., 1998; Yazdanbakhsh et al., 2000). Functional studies have shown that the reduced DARC levels associated with FY*X are primarily attributable to the 265C→T substitution, whereas the 298G→A mutation alone does not affect DARC abundance on the RBC surface and can occur at relatively high frequencies in Caucasian populations (Olsson et al., 1998; Gassner et al., 2000; Yazdanbakhsh et al., 2000; Armstrong-Fisher et al., 2023). Nonetheless, the 265C→T substitution is most commonly found in combination with 298G→A mutation as part of the FY*X allele (https://www.isbtweb.org/). The resulting DARC protein carrying the two amino acid substitutions is thought to compromise protein stability and impair correct membrane integration (Yazdanbakhsh et al., 2000), ultimately limiting the amount of DARC available on the RBC surface of FY*X carriers.

At the population level, the impact of the human FY*X allele on P. knowlesi invasion has not yet been investigated, likely because FY*A is the predominant allele in Southeast Asia, where P. knowlesi is endemic, with approximately 90% of individuals carrying FY*A (Howes et al., 2011; Höher et al., 2017; De Silva et al., 2019; Hongfongfa and Kuesap, 2021).

In the late 1970s, an in vitro study using P. knowlesi-infected blood from rhesus monkeys cultured in human FY*X homozygous RBCs for a single reinvasion (3 hours) reported a 50% reduction in invasion compared to FY*A and/or FY*B RBCs (Mason et al., 1977). However, the effect of the FY*X allele has not been investigated in the P. knowlesi line A1-H.1, which is adapted to grow in human erythrocytes.

Here, we report the impact of the homozygous FY*X genotype on RBC invasion by P. knowlesi line A1-H.1, compared to homozygous FY*X RBCs with Duffy-positive and Duffy-negative RBC donors.

Materials and methods

Collection and processing of blood samples

Whole blood samples (5–10 ml) were obtained by venipuncture from three African and four European voluntary donors at the ITM clinic. Upon collection, blood samples were processed to isolate RBCs by removing serum and white blood cells through aspiration. Isolated RBCs were brought to 50% haematocrit (HTC) in RPMI and stored at 4 °C to be used for parasite cultures within one week of collection.

Duffy phenotyping and genotyping

Duffy phenotyping

Packed RBCs per donor (25 μl) were used to determine the Duffy phenotype (Fy(a+b+), Fy(a+b-), Fy(a-b+) or Fy(a-b-)) using an agglutination-based card assay (ID-Card Fya/Fyb, cat. no. 51270; BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Duffy X genotyping

DNA was extracted from 200 μl of whole blood from an FY*X homozygous donor identified by phenotyping (hereafter “Duffy X donor”) and from the Duffy Fy(a-b+) donor, using the QIAmp DNA Blood Mini kit (cat. no. 51104; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Duffy genotype was determined by Sanger sequencing of two regions of the DARC transcriptional unit (NCBI accession: NG_011626.3). PCR amplification of the promoter region (primers FyN1 and FyN2) was performed to assess homozygosity or heterozygosity at position −67 (FY*ES or wildtype), while the coding region from 31T to 422T (primers FyAB1 and FyAB2) was amplified to detect non-synonymous SNPs that determine the Duffy alleles FY*A, FY*B and FY*X (Table 2). PCR reactions were performed using the GoTaq G2 flexiDNA kit (cat. no. M7806; Promega, Madison, WI, USA) under the following program: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min, 35 cycles (94 °C for 20 s, 62 °C for 15 s, 72 °C for 20 s), and a final extension step at 72 °C for 2 min. Sequencing chromatograms were analysed using SnapGene viewer to determine homozygosity or heterozygosity, and sequences were aligned to the DARC reference sequence to identify SNPs in the amplified coding region.

Plasmodium knowlesi invasion assays and flow cytometry

Plasmodium knowlesi line A1-H.1 was maintained at parasitaemias of 0.5–8.0% in culture medium consisting of RPMI-1640 (cat. no. RPMI-XA; Capricorn Scientific, Ebsdorfergrund, Germany) supplemented with 0.32 g/l sodium bicarbonate, 5.0 g/l Albumax II (cat. no. 11021-029; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), 2.0 g/l D-glucose, 25 mM HEPES, 0.05 g/l hypoxanthine, 0.005 g/l gentamicin, and 2 mM L-glutamine with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated horse serum (cat. no. 16050130; Invitrogen), at 3% HTC. Parasite cultures were incubated at 37 °C under atmospheric conditions of 90% N2, 5% O2, and 5% CO2.

For invasion assays, late-stage parasites were first purified. Cultures with predominantly late-stage infected RBCs (19–24 h post-invasion) were adjusted to 10% HTC, after which 5 ml of this suspension was loaded on a 55% isotonic Nycodenz gradient and centrifuged at 2600 rpm for 10 min with brake 1 (Centrifuge 5810 R; Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). Schizont-infected RBCs were recovered from the interface, washed in RPMI-1604, and diluted to 1.2–1.5% parasitaemia in uninfected RBCs from donors with different Duffy genotypes. After adjusting to 2% HTC, resulting cultures were dispensed into a 96-well plate at 100 μl per well, with 4 wells serving as technical replicates for each RBC donor. Three replicate background wells, consisting of uninfected donor RBCs, were added (100 μl, 2% HTC). Cultures were incubated at 37 °C under the same atmospheric conditions for 24 h to allow one reinvasion cycle.

To determine pre-invasion parasitaemia by flow cytometry, three 50-μl aliquots from the diluted 1.2–1.5% parasitaemia cultures in RBCs from different Duffy genotypes were transferred to a separate 96-well plate and immediately mixed with an equal volume of fixative solution (4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), 0.1% glutaraldehyde (GAH) and 10% 10× PBS). Aliquots were incubated for 30 min at 4 °C, washed with 1× PBS (760× g, 4 min) to remove the fixative, resuspended in 1× PBS, and stored at 4 °C until analysis. To determine post-invasion parasitaemia, 50-μl aliquots were collected from the 4 technical replicates following the 24-h incubation period and fixed using the same procedure.

To measure pre- and post-invasion parasitaemias, fixed cultures were stained with 1:10.000 SYBR green (cat. no. S7563; ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA) in 1× PBS at room temperature for 30 min, washed with 1× PBS (760× g, 4 min), and resuspended in 250 μl of 1× PBS for flow cytometry measurement (CytoFlex, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). Single RBCs were selected based on their Forward and Side Scatter profile (FSC and SSC, respectively). At least 100,000 single RBCs were recorded per well, and parasitaemia was calculated as the proportion of SYBR-green positive events over the total number of single RBCs recorded. Fold multiplication over one invasion cycle was calculated for each donor by dividing the post-invasion parasitaemia of each replicate by the average pre-invasion parasitaemia of that donor, after subtracting the background signal calculated from the background wells.

Results

Duffy X phenotype and genotype

Duffy phenotypes of all donors were assessed using agglutination tests. Results showed slightly lower agglutination for the Duffy X donor (Fy(a-bweak)) compared with a Duffy-positive Fy(a-b+) donor (Fig. 1). Genotypes of the Duffy-positive donor Fy (a-b+) and the Duffy X donor were determined by Sanger sequencing of the amplified regions of the DARC gene. Sanger sequencing results of the Duffy X donor revealed homozygosity for the FY*X allele, including the expected additional mutations in the coding sequence region (265C→T, and 298G→A, Fig. S1). Sequencing of the Duffy-positive donor Fy(a-b+) showed homozygosity at position 125 (G) and 265 (C), and heterozygosity at position 298, with one allele containing a G, and the other an A (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Duffy blood typing agglutination test results for the Duffy Fy(a-b+) (left) and Duffy (a-bweak) (right) donors.

Plasmodium knowlesi invasion assay

We quantified the effect of the homozygous Duffy X donor (Fy(a-bweak)) on P. knowlesi A1-H.1 RBC invasion in a single invasion experiment and compared it to invasion levels in Duffy-positive and Duffy-negative RBC donors.

Pre- and post-invasion parasitaemias were used to calculate fold multiplication over one invasion cycle in every donor (Fig. 2A). Our results show a clear reduction in reinvasion for the Duffy X donor with fold multiplication = 1.5 compared with Duffy-positive donors with fold multiplication ranging from 5.1 to 6.5 (Fig. 2A). Considering the RBC reinvasion of Duffy-positive donors as 100%, reinvasion of P. knowlesi A1-H.1 was inhibited in Duffy-negative donors and reduced to 25.8% in the homozygous Duffy X donor (Fig. 2B).

Fold multiplications calculated in Duffy-negative donors were below 1 (Fig. 2A), indicating that reinvasion did not occur during the 24-h incubation period. Among Duffy-positive donors, we observed a slight preference for RBCs from the Fy(a+b-) donor with average fold multiplication = 6.5, compared with Fy(a-b+) with average fold multiplication = 5.1, and Fy(a+b+) donors with average fold multiplication = 5.9.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that homozygosity of the FY*X allele substantially reduces RBC invasion of P. knowlesi A1-H.1 compared with Duffy-positive donors. The Duffy X donor (Fy(a-bweak)) showed a markedly lower RBC invasion, approximately 4-fold lower (25.8%) than in Duffy-positive donors, while Duffy-negative RBCs did not support detectable invasion. Notably, the positive agglutination for the Fyb antigen observed for the Duffy X donor was comparable to that of a donor carrying two FY*B alleles, despite the reduced DARC expression associated with the FY*X homozygosity. This highlights the limitation of serological typing alone and underscores the potential for misclassification of FY*X carriers when genotypic or quantitative expression data are not considered.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the critical role of DARC as a receptor for P. knowlesi RBC invasion (Miller et al., 1975; Muh et al., 2018; De Silva et al., 2019). Reduced invasion on FY*X homozygous RBCs aligns with earlier observations using a macaque-derived P. knowlesi isolate in a Fy(a-bweak) donor (Mason et al., 1977). The minor fold multiplication differences observed among Duffy-positive donors (Fy(a+b-), Fy(a-b+), Fy(a+b+)) suggest no clear preference for invading FY*A versus FY*B alleles, consistent with in vitro studies on the same human-adapted strain, and with epidemiological data showing similar infection rates between FY*A and FY*B carriers in Southeast Asia (Moon et al., 2013; De Silva et al., 2014, 2019).

The substantially lower invasion observed in FY*X homozygotes likely reflects reduced DARC availability on the RBC surface. The partial reinvasion that still occurs may indicate that even low levels of DARC are sufficient for some parasite entry, consistent with the intermediate Fy(a-bweak) phenotype reported in many agglutination-based studies (Buchanan et al., 1976; Habibi et al., 1980; Olsson et al., 1998; Tournamille et al., 1998; Yazdanbakhsh et al., 2000). These results confirm that the FY*X allele has a functional impact on Plasmodium host cell invasion.

Limitations of this study should be considered. Only one experiment was performed to compare fold-change differences among Duffy-positive donors, limiting statistical power. A second independent experiment using FY*X homozygous RBCs was not feasible due to the extreme rarity of this genotype and the lack of further access to the identified FY*X homozygous donor for repeat experimentation. The number of donors was small, and in vitro culture conditions may not fully replicate the in vivo dynamics of P. knowlesi infection.

Future studies could help validate these findings by increasing the number of FY*X carriers (both hetero- and homozygous) where available. Alternatively, given the rarity of the FY*X allele and the resulting difficulty in obtaining additional donors, the functional impact of this and other DARC rare alleles could be assessed using CRISPR/Cas9-edited human erythroid systems. These systems have been shown to allow investigation of the functional role of human erythrocyte receptors during the invasion process of P. falciparum (Kanjee et al., 2017) and P. vivax (Gruszczyk et al., 2018). The generation of DARC knockout cell lines has already demonstrated the feasibility of this approach (Scully et al., 2019), and highlights the potential to extend it to the study of rare DARC allele variants such as the FY*X.

Evaluating the relationship between the DARC expression levels in FY*X carriers and susceptibility to natural P. knowlesi infections would be of considerable interest to determine whether the effect of the FY*X allele observed in vitro is also reflected in natural infections. However, such analyses are limited by several factors. These include the low allelic frequency (Lopez et al., 2015; Höher et al., 2017) and restricted geographical distribution of the FY*X allele (Gassner et al., 2000; Schmid et al., 2011; Manfroi et al., 2015; Armstrong-Fisher et al., 2023), the absence of this allele in epidemiological studies from P. knowlesi-endemic regions (Howes et al., 2011; Höher et al., 2017; De Silva et al., 2019; Hongfongfa and Kuesap, 2021), and the limited sensitivity of routinely used agglutination-based Duffy phenotyping methods to reliably identify FY*X carriers (Murphy et al., 1997; Olsson et al., 1998; Lopez et al., 2015).

Despite being rare and largely restricted to populations of Caucasian origin (Howes et al., 2011; Manfroi et al., 2015; Höher et al., 2017; Armstrong-Fisher et al., 2023), the pronounced effect of the FY*X allele on P. knowlesi in vitro invasion underscores the importance of accurate Duffy typing. Misclassification of FY*X carriers as FY*B or FY*ES carriers when relying on an agglutination-based test has been previously reported (Murphy et al., 1997; Olsson et al., 1998; Lopez et al., 2015), and our result demonstrates that such misclassification could lead to incorrect conclusions in studies of P. knowlesi susceptibility or invasion. Accurate phenotyping can be achieved by combining agglutination tests with molecular approaches, such as Sanger sequencing, as performed here, or by using pre-designed PCR-SSP (Lopez et al., 2015; Gassner et al., 2000) or PCR-RFLP assays (Castilho et al., 2004; Cavasini et al., 2007) that specifically detect different DARC alleles for unambiguous classification.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, we report the first assessment of P. knowlesi A1-H.1 invasion in RBCs from a homozygous FY*X donor. Although based on a single experiment, our findings indicate that FY*X homozygosity markedly reduces parasite invasion and highlight the importance of accurate Duffy genotyping to avoid misclassification of FY*X carriers in studies of P. knowlesi invasion.
