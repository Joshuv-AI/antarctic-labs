Prevalence, predictors, and academic-related indicators associated with psychoactive drug use among students in tertiary institutions in Rivers State, Nigeria
Authors: Holy Brown, Ibioku Elekima, Moore Ikechi Mike-Ogburia, Promise Enyi Anele, Sotonye Jaja, Olagbegi Olawale Olakanmi, Sotonye Imafidon, Adaeze Chidinma Oreh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPsychoactive drug use among tertiary students is a public health concern with implications for well-being and academic functioning. This study investigated the prevalence, predictors, and academic-related indicators associated with psychoactive drug use among students in tertiary institutions in Rivers State, Nigeria.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted between August 2024 and February 2025 among 1,707 students recruited through multistage sampling from tertiary institutions in Rivers State. Data were collected using a validated, self-administered questionnaire. Descriptive statistics summarised respondent characteristics, drug-use patterns and academic-related indicators. Chi-square tests and independent t-tests assessed group differences, while multivariable logistic regression identified predictors of psychoactive drug use. Multiple linear regression examined the association between psychoactive drug use and self-reported CGPA, with statistical significance set at p ≤ 0.05.ResultsThe prevalence of psychoactive drug use was 7.7% (95% CI: 6.6%–9.1%). Alcohol (68.9%) and marijuana (30.3%) were the most commonly used substances, while stress relief (42.4%) and recreational use (29.5%) were the leading reasons for use. Male students (aOR = 2.28; 95% CI: 1.54–3.37), undergraduates (aOR = 2.65; 95% CI: 1.22–6.57), students earning ₦25,000 – ₦125,000 (aOR = 2.42; 95% CI: 1.46–4.08), and those earning ≥₦125,001 (aOR = 4.52; 95% CI: 2.19–9.17) had higher odds of drug use. Compared with non-users, drug users more frequently reported difficulty concentrating, sleeping during lectures, missing lectures, missing continuous assessments, failing at least one course, and dropping an academic semester or year. However, no significant difference was observed in mean CGPA or satisfaction with CGPA between the two groups and psychoactive drug use was not significantly associated with self-reported CGPA in multiple linear regression (aβ = -0.09; 95% CI: -0.21–0.04).ConclusionsPsychoactive drug use was observed among students in tertiary institutions in Rivers State, especially males, undergraduates, and students with higher personal income. Although psychoactive drug use was not significantly associated with self-reported CGPA, it was linked to certain adverse academic-related indicators, including poorer concentration, absenteeism, missed assessments, course failure, and dropping an academic semester or year. These findings support the need for targeted prevention strategies, campus-based counselling, and accessible rehabilitation referral services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28091-9.Keywords: Psychoactive substance use, Tertiary institution students, Predictors, Academic-related indicators, Substance use, Nigeria

Introduction

Psychoactive drugs are substances that act on the central nervous system and can alter mood, perception, cognition, consciousness, or behaviour [1]. They include both legal and illegal substances such as alcohol, tobacco, cannabis, opioids, cocaine, amphetamine-type stimulants, sedatives, and hallucinogens [2, 3]. The use of these substances among university students has emerged as a critical global public health and social issue [4], with severe consequences for both individual well-being and institutional effectiveness [5, 6]. In many low- and middle-income countries, including Nigeria, the problem appears to be escalating [7]. Recent findings illustrate varying prevalence rates across different regions, partly reflecting the heterogeneity of cultural, economic, and social factors. For instance, Olanrewaju et al. [8] reported a prevalence of 45.7% among undergraduates in southwestern Nigeria, while Johnson et al. [9] found a lower rate of 27.5% in southeastern Nigeria. In Katsina State (northwestern Nigeria), Wada et al. [10] documented a 37% rate of drug and substance use, highlighting the persistent threat to students’ academic, physical, and psychological well-being nationwide.

One of the most concerning aspects of drug use in tertiary institutions is its detrimental impact on academic performance [11, 12]. Multiple studies have shown that students who engage in frequent or severe drug use often experience reduced class attendance, lower grade point averages, and a heightened risk of course failure or withdrawal [13–15]. Furthermore, psychosocial stressors such as anxiety and depressive symptoms have been associated with substance use, exacerbating the negative effects on academic outcomes [16]. Research conducted in other parts of sub-Saharan Africa has highlighted how drug use compounds financial strain, cultural stigma, and limited access to counselling services among university students [17, 18]. These findings echo broader global patterns, indicating that drug use in higher education settings is rarely an isolated issue but is intertwined with mental health, economic challenges, and sociocultural norms [19, 20].

Another compelling dimension involves examining demographic variables linked to drug use. Evidence suggests that gender differences persist, with males typically exhibiting higher prevalence rates [9, 21], though some studies find elevated rates among females depending on substance type and other contextual factors [20]. Age, family background, peer pressure, and personal motivations such as coping with stress or enhancing social acceptance further contribute to a student’s vulnerability to substance use [7, 19]. Although several studies have addressed these risk factors, there is a need for ongoing, context-specific research, given the diversity of cultural practices, societal expectations, and institutional policies across Nigeria’s states and higher education systems [10, 17].

In Rivers State, Nigeria, the prevalence and consequences of drug use among students in tertiary institutions have not been exhaustively documented. There is a relative lack of integrated analyses that simultaneously measure prevalence, determinants, and academic-related indicators. Evidence from Rivers State shows student substance problems, but it remains fragmented. Single-institution studies in the state describe patterns and prevalence among undergraduates, often without integrated multivariable analyses or academic outcome linkages (e.g., course failure/attendance) and with substance-specific focus (e.g., analgesic misuse) [22–24]. Beyond campuses, local work among adolescents and treatment attendees highlights broader community burden [25, 26] but is not generalisable to tertiary students or lacks educational endpoints, highlighting a pipeline of risk into universities without state-wide, multi-institution data. Addressing this gap is crucial for designing targeted interventions, informing policy decisions, and ultimately promoting healthier, more productive educational environments. Thus, this study aimed to investigate the prevalence, predictors, and academic-related indicators associated with psychoactive drug use among students in tertiary institutions in Rivers State, Nigeria.

Methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional study conducted between August 2024 and February 2025 across multiple tertiary institutions in Rivers State, Nigeria, to assess the prevalence and predictors of psychoactive drug use. The design incorporated a nested comparison (drug-users vs. non-drug users) within the same survey to examine associations between psychoactive drug use and some academic-related indicators.

Study area

The study was conducted in Rivers State, South-South Nigeria, with a dense concentration of higher-education institutions and diverse student populations. Participating institutions represented public and private universities as well as polytechnics and colleges across key disciplines.

Study population and sample size

The study population comprised students enrolled in accredited programmes across tertiary institutions in Rivers State. Eligible participants were current undergraduates or postgraduates aged ≥ 16 years. Students with less than one semester of enrolment and those on suspension or deferred admission during the study period were excluded because the study focused on actively enrolled students available within the class-based sampling frame and for whom comparable academic-related indicators could be assessed. Using a sample size calculator [27] and a 47.7% prevalence of drug and substance use from a previous study [8], the minimum sample size was estimated at 382 students. Because the study employed a multistage sampling technique, a design effect of 2.0 was applied to account for clustering, increasing the sample size to 764. This was further adjusted for an anticipated 20% non-response rate, yielding a minimum required sample size of 955 students.

Sampling technique

The study used a multistage sampling technique to recruit students across Rivers State tertiary institutions between August 2024 and February 2025. Eligible tertiary institutions were stratified by type (universities, polytechnics, colleges of health/nursing) and selected in the first stage. The Rivers State University, University of Port Harcourt, Ignatius Ajuru University, PAMO University of Medical Sciences, Madonna University, and National Open University of Nigeria were selected from the list of Universities in the state. Polytechnics were represented by Kenule Beeson Saro-Wiwa Polytechnic and Captain Elechi-Amadi Polytechnic, while the Rivers State College of Health Science and Technology, as well as the Rivers State College of Nursing and Midwifery, were selected to represent colleges of health/nursing. The full list of participating institutions and the number of students recruited from each institution are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

In the second stage, at least two departments or programmes were randomly selected within each institution to reflect, where applicable, agriculture, environmental and applied sciences; arts, humanities and social sciences; business, economics and management; education; engineering, technology and computer science; medical, health and life sciences; and natural sciences and mathematics. In the third stage, academic levels (Years 1–6) and class sessions were randomly selected from official timetables, including postgraduate classes where applicable. In the fourth stage, students were selected systematically from seating arrangements using a pre-specified sampling interval of 3 (k = 3), while refusals or absentees were replaced with the next eligible student.

Although the minimum required sample size was 955 students, no strict allocation target was set for individual institutions. Rather, recruitment was undertaken within the selected departments, academic levels, and class sessions during the study period. This process yielded 1,707 completed questionnaires, and all eligible consenting students sampled during the study period were retained in the analysis. Participation was voluntary, non-incentivised, and based on informed consent.

Data collection tool

A structured, self-administered questionnaire served as the primary data collection tool for this study (Supplementary File 1). It was developed for this study based on existing literature [8, 10, 12] and adapted to the local context. The questionnaire was divided into sections covering demographic details, patterns of drug use, and academic performance indicators. The tool underwent face and content validation through expert review, followed by pretesting among students who were not part of the main study population to assess clarity, relevance, and comprehensiveness. In addition, test–retest reliability was assessed for the behavioural and academic-related items, using Cohen’s kappa for binary items, weighted kappa for ordinal items, and the intraclass correlation coefficient for continuous variables, with satisfactory results. Feedback from the pretest informed minor revisions before the main survey.

Variables

Dependent variables

The primary dependent variable in this study was psychoactive drug use. Psychoactive drug use was operationalised as current self-reported use (yes/no) of any psychoactive drug, while frequency of use and type of drug used were described separately. In addition, the study assessed selected self-reported academic-related indicators in relation to psychoactive drug use. These indicators included feeling uncomfortable in class, ability to concentrate in class, sleeping during lectures, regular lecture attendance, missed continuous assessment tests, missed examinations, course failure, and dropping an academic semester or year (all yes/no), as well as self-reported current CGPA and satisfaction with CGPA (yes/no). These measures were intended to reflect different dimensions of academic experience, including academic functioning, participation, and achievement.

Independent variables

Independent variables included sociodemographic/academic characteristics (age, gender, marital status, family background, programme discipline, degree category, mode of study, academic level); living situation and economic indicators such as occupation, monthly personal income and family income bracket.

Bias mitigation strategies

Several measures were adopted to minimise bias in this study. Selection bias was reduced through the multistage sampling procedure, which involved random selection of institutions, departments/programmes, academic levels, and class sessions, followed by systematic sampling of students within selected classes using a sampling interval of 3 (k = 3). Replacement of absentees or refusals with the next eligible student was standardised in advance to maintain consistency during fieldwork. Social desirability bias was minimised by assuring participants of anonymity and confidentiality and by omitting personal identifiers. Recall and measurement bias were reduced by clearly defining psychoactive drug use as current use, asking specific closed-ended questions, and administering a pre-tested structured questionnaire by a uniformly trained research team using standardised instructions. Information and analytic bias were further minimised through real-time logic checks, predefined coding rules, screening for duplicate or implausible entries, and adherence to a pre-specified analysis plan. Confounding was addressed by adjusting for a priori covariates in the multivariable analysis.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 9, while the sensitivity analysis accounting for clustering was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26. Chi-square tests were used to examine associations between categorical variables, while independent t-tests were used to compare mean self-reported CGPA between psychoactive drug users and non-users. Bivariate logistic regression was initially performed to assess the association between each independent variable and psychoactive drug use. Candidate variables for the multivariable logistic regression model were selected based on prior literature and epidemiological relevance. Multicollinearity among variables considered for inclusion was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), and all variables retained in the multivariable model had VIF values below 2, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity. Some variables, including marital status and occupation, were excluded from the final multivariable model because sparse data in some categories resulted in unstable parameter estimation. Adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported. In addition, multiple linear regression was used to examine the association between psychoactive drug use and self-reported CGPA among respondents with available CGPA data. As a sensitivity analysis, the multivariable logistic regression model was re-estimated using generalised estimating equations (GEE), with institution specified as the clustering variable, to assess the potential impact of intra-cluster correlation arising from the multistage sampling design. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

A total of 1,707 students participated in the study, with a mean age of 21.85 years. The majority were aged 21–30 years (51.6%), followed by those aged 18–20 years (38.6%). Most respondents were female (66.3%) and unmarried (96.2%). By institution type, most were recruited from universities (66.4%), followed by polytechnics (27.3%) and colleges of health/nursing (6.3%). The majority were undergraduates (90.3%), enrolled in full-time programmes (97.5%), and in the upper academic years (55.0%). Most students were in programmes not related to healthcare (66.1%), while 33.9% were in healthcare-related programmes. Nearly half lived in off-campus lodges (48.4%), while 27.8% lived in hostels and 23.8% in family houses. Most respondents were employed in addition to their studies (62.4%). Among those who reported monthly personal income, ₦25,000 – ₦125,000 was the most common range (38.7%), while 18.8% preferred not to disclose their income. Most respondents came from monogamous families (64.8%), and the most frequently reported family income bracket was lower class (39.4%), although 45.9% preferred not to disclose family income (Table 1).

The overall prevalence of psychoactive drug use was 7.7% (95% CI: 6.6%–9.1%) (Fig. 1A). Stratified by institution type, prevalence was lowest in colleges of health/nursing (3.7%) and similar in polytechnics (7.9%) and universities (8.0%) (Fig. 1B). The most commonly cited reason for drug use was stress relief (42.4%), followed by recreational use (29.5%). Other notable reasons included coping with personal problems (21.2%) and medical reasons (21.2%), while fewer respondents cited curiosity (15.2%), peer pressure (6.8%), or social interaction (6.1%) (Fig. 2). Alcohol (68.9%) was the most commonly used substance, followed by marijuana (30.3%) and prescription painkillers (14.4%). Other notable substances included tobacco (10.6%) and opioids (3.8%), while prescription sedatives (3.0%) and cocaine (2.3%) were also reported (Fig. 3). Among the respondents who reported using psychoactive drugs, the majority used them rarely (68.9%), while dealers (53.0%) were the most common source, followed by friends (23.5%). Peer influence was notable, as 71.2% had friends who used psychoactive drugs (Table 2). More than half had attempted to discontinue use (51.5%), with a success rate of 79.4% among them. However, awareness of rehabilitation centres was low, with 79.5% unaware of any facility in Rivers State (Table 3).

Psychoactive drug use was significantly associated with age (p = 0.0134), with the highest prevalence among respondents aged ≥ 31 years (11.4%). Males (12.5%) had a significantly higher prevalence than females (5.3%) (p < 0.0001). Part-time students (19.0%) reported a higher prevalence than full-time students (7.4%) (p = 0.0054). Employment status was also significant (p = 0.0003), with a higher prevalence among employed students (9.6%). Monthly personal income was significantly associated with drug use (p < 0.0001), with the highest prevalence among those earning ≥₦125,001 (22.4%), followed by those earning ₦25,000 to ₦125,000 (11.1%) (Table 4). In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, males had significantly higher odds of psychoactive drug use than females (aOR = 2.28; 95% CI: 1.54–3.37). Undergraduate students were also more likely to use psychoactive drugs than postgraduate students (aOR = 2.65; 95% CI: 1.22–6.57). With respect to personal income, respondents earning ₦25,000–₦125,000 had significantly higher odds of psychoactive drug use (aOR = 2.42; 95% CI: 1.46–4.08), while those earning ≥₦125,001 had the highest odds (aOR = 4.52; 95% CI: 2.19–9.17) (Table 5). The observed differences between crude and adjusted odds ratios for some variables likely reflect confounding after simultaneous adjustment for multiple covariates. It is also important to note that in sensitivity analysis using generalised estimating equations with clustering at the institution level, male gender and undergraduate status remained significantly associated with psychoactive drug use, although some covariate estimates, particularly for monthly income, differed from those in the primary model.

Psychoactive drug use was significantly associated with certain adverse academic-related indicators. Compared with non-users, students who used psychoactive drugs more frequently reported difficulty concentrating in class (13.6% vs. 7.7%; p = 0.0178), sleeping during lectures (24.2% vs. 13.7%; p = 0.0010), missing lectures (31.1% vs. 13.8%; p < 0.0001), missing continuous assessment tests (36.4% vs. 17.2%; p < 0.0001), failing at least one course (50.8% vs. 37.2%; p = 0.0021), and dropping an academic semester or year (5.3% vs. 2.4%; p = 0.0397). However, no significant difference was observed in mean cumulative grade point average (CGPA) between drug users and non-users (3.23 ± 0.712 vs. 3.25 ± 0.711; p = 0.8564), and satisfaction with CGPA was also similar between the two groups (p = 0.1176) (Table 6). Similarly, in crude and adjusted linear regression analyses among respondents with available CGPA data, psychoactive drug use was not significantly associated with self-reported CGPA (cβ = -0.01, 95% CI: -0.15–0.12; aβ = -0.09, 95% CI: -0.21–0.04) (Table 7).

Discussion

The current study found a 7.7% prevalence of psychoactive drug use among tertiary institution students, which is noticeably lower than the figures reported in several Nigerian studies [8, 19]. This relatively low prevalence is also closer to the lower range reported in some African university populations, including 7.4% last-30-day illicit drug use among Ethiopian university students [28] and 6.5% lifetime substance use among Egyptian university students [29], while remaining below the 15% lifetime exposure to illegal substances reported among tertiary students in Rivers State, Nigeria [26]. This finding implies that, although fewer students are engaging in psychoactive drug use compared with many other reported settings, there remains a vulnerable subset of the student population that requires targeted intervention to prevent the escalation of substance-related harms. In contrast, higher prevalence rates have been reported in southwestern and northwestern Nigeria, ranging from 37% in Katsina State [10] to 45.7% in southwestern universities [8]. Even in other sub-Saharan contexts, such as Mekelle University, Ethiopia, prevalence values are substantially higher [17]. These discrepancies may reflect regional variations linked to cultural norms, enforcement strategies, availability of substances, and socio-economic conditions [7, 20]. Meanwhile, certain studies conducted outside Nigeria, such as in the West Bank of Palestine [5], similarly indicate that institutional and cultural factors play significant roles in shaping drug use patterns. These comparisons suggest that the prevalence observed in this study falls within the lower range of reported estimates, but should not be interpreted as the absence of risk, particularly because a clearly identifiable subgroup of students reported current use.

In this study, the dominant motivations for psychoactive drug use were stress relief and recreational use, with a notable subset citing coping with personal problems. These findings align with previous reports that emphasise psychological distress, academic pressure, and emotional challenges as common drivers of substance use among university students [8, 16]. The relatively high proportion of students seeking stress relief suggests that campus environments may be perceived as demanding, both academically and socially, thereby prompting a reliance on substances to manage anxiety or enhance mood. The presence of medical reasons as a cited rationale echoes the findings of Osalusi et al. [11], where students used prescription drugs to self-medicate for various health complaints. This pattern suggests that psychoactive drug use in this cohort may be driven more by coping-related and self-management motives than by experimentation alone.

Interestingly, far fewer respondents in the current study pointed to peer pressure, curiosity, or social interaction compared to studies in southwestern Nigeria, which have identified peer influence and experimentation as major factors [7, 19]. This discrepancy suggests that the relative importance of peer-driven initiation versus coping-driven use may vary across student populations and institutional contexts. For instance, while peer group influence has been highlighted elsewhere as a potent risk factor for initiating substance use [9, 20], the current findings suggest that internal stressors, rather than external pressures, are more prominent among these respondents. The prominence of stress and problem-focused motives highlights the need for enhanced mental health support, structured relaxation outlets, and counselling programs in tertiary institutions, potentially mitigating the likelihood that students resort to drug use as a coping mechanism.

In this study, alcohol emerged as the most commonly used psychoactive substance among the respondents, followed by marijuana and prescription painkillers. This mirrors observations from previous studies, especially in Nigeria, where alcohol is consistently reported as the predominant substance of use among students [11, 20]. The relatively high use of marijuana aligns with findings from Adesida et al. [7] and Johnson et al. [9], which indicate a growing acceptance of cannabis in some student circles. Meanwhile, the inclusion of prescription painkillers and opioids resonates with emerging concerns about the misuse of medically indicated drugs, such as codeine and tramadol, a trend also observed by Olanrewaju et al. [8]. Thus, while alcohol and cannabis remain the dominant substances, the reported use of prescription medicines suggests that student drug-use patterns may be broadening beyond traditionally recognised recreational substances.

Notably, tobacco use was lower than reported in some prior Nigerian studies, suggesting that the substance-use profile in this cohort differs from settings where smoking is more prominent. The relatively modest reports of cocaine and prescription sedatives corroborate the notion that more traditional substances like alcohol and cannabis remain more accessible and culturally accepted, compared to harder drugs such as cocaine [14, 15]. These findings emphasise the need for multifaceted intervention strategies encompassing not only alcohol and cannabis but also the evolving patterns of misusing prescription medications, which pose additional risks through self-medication and potential dependency [16, 19].

The finding that a majority of the respondents who engage in psychoactive drug use do so rarely suggests that many students may be experimenting with substances intermittently rather than engaging in sustained or heavy use. This pattern echoes observations in some Nigerian cohorts, where non-frequent use was reportedly high [8, 9]. This distinction is important because low frequency of use does not necessarily imply low risk when access routes and peer networks remain active. However, the fact that dealers constituted the most common source of drugs is concerning, particularly since easy access to illicit suppliers can facilitate more regular use or escalation to riskier substances. Meanwhile, friends served as another key source, aligning with findings from studies emphasising peer networks in the procurement and normalisation of substance use among students [20, 21]. Peer influence remains salient, with a majority of respondents indicating they had friends who used psychoactive drugs. This corroborates evidence from Adesida et al. [7] and Ferreira et al. [16], where being part of a social circle that condones or encourages substance use significantly raises the likelihood of experimentation. The clustering of users within peer groups can also heighten the risk of polysubstance use, as has been noted in settings where students experiment with multiple substances concurrently [6, 14].

The high proportion of students (51.5%) who attempted to discontinue psychoactive drug use, coupled with a substantial 79.4% success rate among these individuals, signals a promising level of self-motivation and willingness to recover. This observation aligns with findings in some Nigerian studies suggesting that many students view substance use as a transient or situational coping strategy, rather than a long-term habit [7, 9]. However, the low awareness of rehabilitation centres in Rivers State highlights a systemic gap in accessible professional support, echoing concerns elsewhere in sub-Saharan Africa, where limited infrastructure and poor awareness often impede effective treatment and harm-reduction interventions [16, 17]. In particular, researchers have emphasised that high motivation to quit does not necessarily translate into sustained abstinence without proper counselling, peer support, or clinical services [10, 19]. Thus, the findings reveal an important mismatch between individual willingness to discontinue use and limited awareness of formal support pathways, a gap that may increase relapse risk under renewed stress or continued exposure to enabling peer networks.

The present study’s multivariable logistic regression analysis reveals gender, degree category, and personal income as significant predictors of psychoactive drug use, echoing patterns identified in multiple contexts. In this study, males had over twice the odds of psychoactive drug use compared to females, reinforcing a well-documented gender gap in substance use behaviours [10, 21]. This discrepancy may be influenced by social norms and expectations, which often encourage risk-taking behaviours and social experimentation among men, while simultaneously stigmatising drug use among women. Additionally, earlier research has demonstrated that male students may be more exposed to peer influence or use substances to cope with academic or financial pressures, thus compounding the risk [19, 20]. In contrast, some studies report narrower gender differences, attributing variations to campus culture, familial attitudes, and the level of enforcement against illicit drug use [8, 16]. The present finding supports the broader literature showing that male students remain a consistently higher-risk group, although the size of the gender gap may vary across institutional and cultural contexts.

Undergraduates had significantly higher odds of psychoactive drug use than postgraduates after adjustment, suggesting that earlier stages of tertiary education may represent a particularly vulnerable period. This finding likely reflects developmental and contextual factors such as younger age, transitional stressors (such as entry into university, new peer networks), greater exposure to peer influence and campus social events, as well as less structured supervision and coping skills obtainable in some institutions in developing countries like Nigeria. By contrast, postgraduate students often have greater academic maturity and clearer career goals in addition to more constrained schedules, all of which can reduce risk. This finding is consistent with Nigerian and international evidence showing elevated substance use in earlier university stages. Nigerian studies of predominantly undergraduate cohorts report substantial use and peer-linked motives [7, 8], while large student samples elsewhere similarly document high use and polysubstance patterns among younger students [6]. Longitudinal data also show riskier alcohol use early in undergraduate years, aligning with lower use as students mature [30], consistent with the comparatively lower odds among postgraduates in this study. These findings, when taken together, indicate that prevention strategies may be most effective when introduced early in students’ tertiary education, before risky patterns become normalised.

Another notable finding was the income gradient in psychoactive drug use. Compared with respondents with no income, those earning ₦25,000–₦125,000 had higher odds of use, while those earning ≥₦125,001 had an even stronger association, with the highest adjusted odds observed in this group. This income gradient resonates with studies suggesting that increased disposable income may ease the purchase of illicit substances [14, 19]. In contexts where drug costs and socio-economic disparities interact, financially better-off students may have more opportunities to experiment and sustain a drug habit [17, 20]. Thus, while poverty may increase vulnerability through stress-related pathways [16], the present findings suggest that, among students, disposable personal income may act more directly as an access-enabling factor.

The present study showed that psychoactive drug use was associated with certain adverse academic-related indicators, including poorer concentration in class, increased likelihood of sleeping during lectures, and reduced class attendance, findings that are broadly consistent with prior research in Nigerian and international settings [8, 15, 20]. Frequent or problematic substance use is often linked to missed assessments, higher failure rates, and other academic disruptions, as seen here with the notably higher proportion of drug users missing lectures and continuous assessment tests. This pattern aligns with similar observations by El Omari et al. [13], who noted that students with a history of substance use demonstrated poorer academic engagement and performance indicators. This suggests that psychoactive drug use may first manifest through proximal academic-functioning problems, such as concentration and attendance, before any measurable effect on cumulative academic outcomes becomes apparent.

The lack of a significant difference in the mean cumulative grade point average (CGPA) between drug users and non-drug users echoes the findings of Riaz et al. [15] and may partly reflect the predominance of less frequent psychoactive drug use among respondents in this cohort, suggesting that occasional or intermittent use may not always translate into measurable GPA deficits, especially when academic evaluation is broad or includes non-exam assessments that may be less sensitive to sporadic attendance or short-term lapses in concentration. Moreover, some students might engage in compensatory efforts that help maintain their GPAs despite missing classes and assessments. The current study, however, contrasts with reports by Mazur et al. [14], El Omari et al. [13], and Sujan et al. [12], which linked substance use with poorer academic achievement or CGPA-related outcomes among adolescents and university students. In the Bangladeshi university study by Sujan et al. [12], drug abusers had poorer CGPA distribution and lower satisfaction with CGPA than non-abusers. These differences may reflect variation in study populations, sampling approaches, substance-use profiles, and academic outcome measures. Additionally, the fact that 50.8% of drug users in the study failed at least one course and 5.3% dropped an academic semester or year suggests that a subset of users experienced important academic difficulties, consistent with Osalusi et al. [11] and with the broader pattern reported by Sujan et al. [12].

It is also notable that drug users did not differ significantly from non-users in terms of CGPA satisfaction. This contrasts with the findings of Sujan et al. [12] among Bangladeshi university students, who reported significantly lower CGPA satisfaction among drug abusers than non-abusers. This difference may reflect variation in study setting, sampling approach, substance-use profile, or the subjective nature of CGPA satisfaction itself, which may be shaped by personal expectations and self-appraisal rather than objective academic performance alone. Thus, unlike the pattern reported by Sujan et al. [12], the subjective appraisal of academic performance in this cohort appeared relatively similar between users and non-users despite differences in specific academic-related indicators.

This study has a few limitations that may affect interpretation; its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and reliance on self-reported behaviours, CGPA, and other academic-related indicators may have introduced recall and social desirability bias. In addition, the academic-related indicators were not assessed using a uniform recall period, which may have introduced measurement variability. Residual confounding and measurement error are also possible. Furthermore, although multistage sampling was employed, no strict proportional allocation target was set across institutions, so the final sample may not have been fully proportionate to institutional size, which could affect representativeness and limit generalisability. Cluster-level nonresponse may also have arisen if sampled classes were atypical. Although sensitivity analysis accounting for clustering at the institution level was performed, residual intra-cluster correlation at lower sampling levels, such as class sessions, may not have been fully addressed. Future longitudinal studies with verified academic records and biomarker/drug-testing triangulation would strengthen temporal and validity inferences. Despite the noted limitations, this study has several strengths; it is, to our knowledge, the first state-wide study to investigate the prevalence and predictors of psychoactive drug use among students, as well as its association with selected academic-related indicators, across multiple tertiary institutions in Rivers State. Additional strengths include a large, multi-institution sample obtained via multistage sampling with systematic within-class selection, broad disciplinary and degree-level coverage, a pre-tested, standardised questionnaire, and rigorous statistical analyses involving multivariable analysis, which improved the robustness and policy relevance of the study’s findings.

Conclusion

This study found a relatively low prevalence of psychoactive drug use (7.7%) among students in tertiary institutions in Rivers State. Male gender, undergraduate status, and higher personal income were significant predictors of psychoactive drug use. Although psychoactive drug use was not associated with all academic measures, it was associated with some adverse academic-related indicators, particularly poorer concentration in class, absenteeism, missed assessments, and course failure. These findings highlight the need for targeted, context-specific interventions for at-risk groups, alongside strengthened campus-based support services and rehabilitation awareness programmes to mitigate the adverse academic and personal consequences of psychoactive drug use.
