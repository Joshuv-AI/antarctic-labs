Cilostazol-Induced Digoxin Toxicity Presenting as Accelerated Idioventricular Rhythm and Recurrent Syncope
Authors: Sameh Madanieh, Drashti Desai, Gianne Macedo Goedert, Joshua Shultz, Hafiz Hussain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Digoxin remains a clinically relevant therapy for ventricular rate control in atrial fibrillation, particularly in patients with concomitant heart failure.1 However, its narrow therapeutic index predisposes patients to toxicity even when measured serum levels appear therapeutic. Electrolyte disturbances and drug-drug interactions that alter digoxin pharmacokinetics or pharmacodynamics frequently exacerbate toxicity.1 While many interactions are well described, the potential interaction between digoxin and phosphodiesterase-3 (PDE-3) inhibitors such as cilostazol is under-recognized.1,2 To our knowledge, this is the first reported case after a thorough review of drug-drug interactions of both drugs. We report a case of accelerated idioventricular rhythm (AIVR) likely precipitated by this interaction, demonstrating that clinical toxicity may occur despite a “normal” serum digoxin concentration obtained outside the peak distribution window.1

Take-Home Message

•Concomitant digoxin and cilostazol therapy create a pharmacological synergy that potentiates toxicity through both P-glycoprotein–mediated renal inhibition and increased intracellular calcium, lowering the threshold for life-threatening arrhythmias.

History of Presentation

A 77-year-old man with coronary artery disease, chronic heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, and paroxysmal atrial fibrillation presented with 2 days of dizziness, generalized weakness, nausea, vomiting, and blurred vision. His home medications included digoxin 0.125 mg twice daily, and cilostazol had recently been initiated for peripheral arterial disease.

On presentation, he reported palpitations with transient hypotension. Initial electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated normal sinus rhythm (Figure 1). A subsequent symptomatic tracing revealed AIVR with inferior and lateral ST-segment depressions (Figure 2).

Differential Diagnosis

In the context of digitalis intoxication, AIVR is considered a rare manifestation in both the historical and contemporary literature.3,4 While AIVR most commonly arises in the setting of myocardial ischemia and reperfusion injury, other recognized causes include electrolyte disturbances and drug toxicity.5 In this case, alternative etiologies were excluded: Serial troponins and potassium were normal, and no acute structural abnormality was identified on echocardiography. The complete resolution of AIVR following digoxin discontinuation, without any other medication changes, supports drug toxicity as the underlying etiology.

Investigations

Serial troponins and potassium levels were normal. The serum digoxin level was within the therapeutic range; however, the sample was obtained more than 30 hours after the last dose.

Transthoracic echocardiography showed a low-normal left ventricular ejection fraction (50%-55%) with diffuse hypokinesia and no significant valvular disease.

Management

Digoxin was discontinued, while other home medications were continued.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Following cessation of digoxin, the AIVR resolved completely, and the patient remained in stable sinus rhythm without recurrent syncope or palpitations.

Discussion

This case demonstrates how AIVR can emerge as a sign of digoxin toxicity even on a standard dose when cilostazol therapy is introduced, creating a dangerous pharmacological synergy.

Digoxin toxicity results from inhibition of the Na+/K+-ATPase pump, leading to intracellular sodium accumulation, reduced Na+/Ca2+ exchange, and intracellular calcium overload. This promotes delayed depolarizations that can generate ventricular automaticity and AIVR.1,6

Cilostazol and its metabolites inhibit the principal transporter responsible for renal digoxin clearance, thereby increasing systemic exposure and peak concentrations.7 In addition, PDE-3 inhibition elevates intracellular cyclic AMP and enhances calcium influx, creating pharmacodynamic synergy that lowers the threshold for triggered ventricular activity.7,8

The apparently therapeutic digoxin level in this patient was misleading and highlights the importance of appropriate sampling timing. Following oral administration, digoxin undergoes a 6- to 8-hour tissue distribution phase, during which serum concentrations are transiently elevated and do not reflect concentrations at the site of action.5 Only after the distribution phase do steady-state postdistribution serum concentrations equilibrate with tissue concentrations and correlate with pharmacologic and toxic effects. There are different guidelines that recommend different times, for example, the Food and Drug Administration recommends serum digoxin levels be obtained just before the next scheduled dose or at least 6 hours after last dose, and the American Association for Thoracic Surgery recommends sampling at least 12 hours and preferably 24 hours after the last dose, noting that samples drawn earlier maybe falsely elevated because of incomplete tissue distribution.2,9 With a half-life of approximately 36 hours in normal renal function, a level drawn 30 hours after the last dose reflects late elimination rather than peak toxicity during symptom onset.2,6,9 This reveals that digoxin toxicity remains a clinical diagnosis and cannot be excluded by a single serum concentration obtained outside of the appropriate sampling window.

To formally assess the likelihood that the observed AIVR was attributable to the digoxin-cilostazol interaction, we applied the Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale.10 The total score was 4, corresponding to a “possible” adverse drug reaction. Points were assigned for the temporal relationship between cilostazol initiation and symptom onset, resolution of AIVR following digoxin discontinuation (positive dechallenge), and the absence of an alternative explanation for the arrhythmia. The score was limited by the absence of prior reports of this specific interaction (no previous conclusive reports), the lack of rechallenge, and the unavailability of a drug level obtained during the distribution phase. It should be noted that the Naranjo algorithm was originally designed to assess single-drug adverse events rather than drug-drug interactions, which may underestimate causality in interaction cases.11 Nevertheless, the Naranjo scale remains the most widely used and accepted causality assessment tool in published case reports.

In the context of digitalis intoxication, AIVR is considered a rare manifestation in both the historical and contemporary literature.3,4 While AIVR most commonly arises in the setting of myocardial ischemia and reperfusion injury, other recognized causes include electrolyte disturbances and drug toxicity.5 In this case, alternative etiologies were excluded: Serial troponins and potassium were normal, and no acute structural abnormality was identified on echocardiography. The complete resolution of AIVR following digoxin discontinuation, without any other medication changes, supports drug toxicity as the underlying etiology.

We believe this case is of particular importance given the specific interaction with cilostazol. While most clinicians are aware of common digoxin interactions (eg, amiodarone or verapamil), the synergy with PDE-3 inhibitors is frequently overlooked.

Conclusions

Digoxin toxicity remains a clinical diagnosis. The presence of AIVR in a patient receiving digoxin should raise strong suspicion for toxicity regardless of serum concentration, particularly when levels are obtained outside the appropriate sampling window. Concomitant cilostazol therapy may potentiate digoxin toxicity through combined pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic mechanisms, emphasizing the need for proactive dose adjustment and close surveillance when these agents are coprescribed.
