Death anxiety and death-related depression in opioid use disorder: relationships with suicidal ideation, impulsivity, and psychological resilience
Authors: Mustafa Kurt, Ali Taşdemir, Merve Gök
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOpioid use disorder (OUD) is associated with elevated suicide and impulsivity risks, yet the underlying existential vulnerabilities remain underexplored. This study aimed to examine death anxiety and death-related depression as potential mechanisms associated with suicidal ideation and impulsivity in OUD, and to evaluate the protective role of psychological resilience.MethodsParticipants included 86 individuals with OUD in remission (under buprenorphine/naloxone treatment) and 86 healthy controls. Self-report measures assessed death anxiety, death-related depression, suicidal ideation, impulsivity, and psychological resilience. Data were analyzed using group comparisons and hierarchical regression models.ResultsWhile death anxiety did not significantly differ between groups (p = 0.153), the OUD group showed significantly higher death-related depression, suicidal ideation, and impulsivity, alongside lower resilience (p < 0.003). Hierarchical regression analyses revealed that death-related depression positively predicted suicidal ideation (β = 0.278, p = 0.007), whereas psychological resilience demonstrated a significant negative association (β = -0.296, p = 0.002). In the impulsivity model, psychological resilience emerged as the only significant predictor (β = -0.482, p < 0.001).ConclusionsFindings suggest that death-related depression, reflecting existential hopelessness rather than threat-based anxiety, represents an overlooked psychological vulnerability closely linked to suicidal ideation in OUD. Psychological resilience acts as a critical protective factor against both suicidal ideation and impulsivity. Interventions targeting meaning-making and resilience may be vital for mitigating suicide risk in this population. Keywords: Opioid use disorder, Death anxiety, Death depression, Suicidal ideation, Psychological resilience, Impulsivity

Introduction

Addiction is a chronic mental disorder characterized by biological, psychological, and social dysfunction, involving both impulsive and compulsive behavioral processes [1]. Among substance use disorders, opioid use disorder (OUD) warrants particular attention because of its high rates of overdose, medical complications, and premature mortality [2, 3]. Individuals with OUD are often confronted with mortality-related experiences, both directly through overdose risk and indirectly through witnessing substance-related illness, injury, or death within their social networks [4]. OUD has consistently been associated with elevated rates of suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, and suicide mortality compared with the general population [5, 6]. Emerging evidence further suggests that opioid use itself may function as a proximal risk factor for suicidal behavior, particularly among younger individuals [7]. Understanding the psychological mechanisms that contribute to suicidality in OUD therefore remains a major clinical and public health priority.

One potentially important but understudied mechanism involves individuals’ emotional responses to mortality. Contemporary theoretical perspectives suggest that death-related affect is not a unitary construct but rather encompasses multiple emotional dimensions that may have distinct psychological consequences [8, 9]. Two major components have been described: death anxiety, which reflects fear, threat perception, and concerns regarding mortality, and death-related depression, which reflects feelings of meaninglessness, loneliness, hopelessness, and existential resignation in the face of death [9, 10]. Although both dimensions arise from awareness of mortality, they may operate through different psychological pathways. Death anxiety has primarily been conceptualized as a threat-based emotional response that activates defensive coping processes, whereas death-related depression reflects a more enduring existential state characterized by loss of meaning and diminished future orientation [11]. Distinguishing between these dimensions may be particularly relevant in OUD, where chronic exposure to adversity, repeated encounters with mortality-related events, and persistent psychosocial stressors may shape emotional responses to death in complex ways.

Within Terror Management Theory (TMT), death anxiety is conceptualized as a threat-based emotional response arising from awareness of one’s own mortality [12, 13]. According to this framework, mortality salience activates existential anxiety, which is normally regulated through psychological “anxiety-buffer” systems, including self-esteem, close interpersonal relationships, and culturally derived sources of meaning and purpose [11, 14]. When these protective systems function effectively, awareness of death can be integrated without substantial psychological distress. Conversely, disruptions in meaning-making processes or diminished psychological resources may intensify vulnerability to emotional dysregulation and psychopathology [14, 15]. In the context of OUD, chronic substance use is frequently accompanied by social isolation, loss of meaningful roles, stigma, and repeated exposure to mortality-related events, all of which may weaken these anxiety-buffering mechanisms [6]. Consequently, although death anxiety may represent an important psychological process contributing to vulnerability in individuals with OUD, mortality awareness may also manifest through broader existential responses that extend beyond fear-based reactions alone.

Unlike death anxiety, death-related depression reflects existential hopelessness, resignation, loneliness, and loss of meaning rather than fear-based reactions to mortality [9, 10]. From this perspective, awareness of death may no longer be experienced primarily as a threat but as a source of emotional exhaustion and diminished future orientation. This conceptualization closely aligns with the Hopelessness Theory of Depression, which proposes that negative expectations about the future and perceptions of helplessness play a central role in the development of depressive symptoms and suicidal thinking [16, 17]. Contemporary suicide theories similarly emphasize hopelessness and loss of meaning as key psychological drivers of suicidal ideation [18]. Individuals with OUD frequently experience chronic adversity, interpersonal losses, social marginalization, and repeated treatment failures, all of which may contribute to existential despair and reinforce death-related depressive cognitions [19]. Accordingly, death-related depression may represent a more proximal psychological pathway to suicidal ideation than death anxiety itself, particularly in populations exposed to persistent psychosocial and existential stressors.

Beyond suicidal ideation, impulsivity represents another clinically important outcome in OUD. Impulsivity is a core feature of addictive disorders and has been consistently associated with substance use severity, treatment non-adherence, relapse, and self-destructive behaviors [20, 21]. Contemporary models conceptualize impulsivity as a multidimensional construct, with emotion-driven components—particularly negative urgency—showing the strongest associations with suicidal thoughts and behaviors [22, 23]. Recent theoretical work further suggests that impulsive behaviors in OUD may be closely linked to chronic negative affect, emotional dysregulation, and attempts to escape aversive internal states [24]. From this perspective, existential distress and death-related depressive cognitions may contribute to broader patterns of psychological vulnerability encompassing both suicidal ideation and difficulties in behavioral regulation. In contrast, psychological resilience has increasingly been conceptualized as a protective psychological resource that facilitates emotional regulation, adaptive coping, and recovery following adversity. Previous studies have shown that resilience is associated with lower levels of psychological distress, substance misuse, and suicide risk [25–28]. Accordingly, resilience may represent an important factor associated with lower vulnerability to suicidal ideation and impulsive behaviors among individuals with OUD.

Although previous studies have examined death anxiety, resilience, suicidality, and impulsivity separately within substance-using populations, little is known about how distinct dimensions of death-related affect relate to these outcomes in OUD. Existing research has focused predominantly on death anxiety as a unitary construct, while largely neglecting the potentially unique role of death-related depression. Few studies have integrated existentially oriented constructs with behavioral risk factors and protective psychological resources within a common conceptual framework. Drawing on Terror Management Theory, hopelessness-based perspectives of suicidality, and contemporary models of impulsivity, the present study conceptualizes death anxiety and death-related depression as distinct but interrelated dimensions of death-related affect that may differentially contribute to psychological vulnerability in OUD, with psychological resilience examined as a potential protective resource. Therefore, the present study aimed to (1) compare death-related affect between individuals with OUD and healthy controls and (2) examine the contributions of death anxiety, death-related depression, and psychological resilience to suicidal ideation and impulsivity among individuals with OUD.

Materials and methods

Study design and sample

This cross-sectional comparative study was conducted among individuals with OUD attending the Addiction Treatment Center of Adana Dr. Ekrem Tok Mental Health and Diseases Hospital and a healthy control group recruited from the hospital community environment.

Opioid use disorder group selection criteria

The patient group included sequentially admitted individuals aged 18–65 diagnosed with OUD (DSM-5) who were currently in remission under buprenorphine/naloxone maintenance treatment. To objectively confirm remission and treatment compliance, only patients with buprenorphine metabolites detected in routine urine toxicology analyses were included.

Exclusion criteria included signs of acute opioid withdrawal or intoxication, detection of non-prescribed psychoactive substances on urine toxicology screening, severe psychiatric disorders (e.g., schizophrenia spectrum disorders, bipolar disorder, dementia, or significant cognitive impairment), and serious systemic or neurological diseases. Excluding participants with concurrent non-opioid substance use was intended to reduce clinical heterogeneity and facilitate a more specific examination of psychological processes associated with OUD.

Healthy control group selection criteria

The control group was recruited from non-medical support personnel (e.g., security and cleaning staff) within the hospital environment. This recruitment strategy was used to obtain a comparison group with demographic characteristics broadly comparable to those of the patient group, particularly regarding age and sex distributions, while avoiding the inclusion of healthcare professionals. Formal individual matching procedures were not applied; instead, controls were recruited using a frequency-matching approach aimed at achieving similar age and sex distributions at the group level. Physicians, nurses, and other healthcare workers were excluded from recruitment to minimize potential health-literacy bias. Following a semi-structured clinical interview conducted by an experienced psychiatrist, individuals with a current or past psychiatric disorder, history of substance use disorder, psychotropic medication use, or serious medical illness were excluded.

Sample size and power analysis

Using G*Power (Version 3.1.9.2), a minimum of 77 participants per group was required for hierarchical regression analyses (3 predictors, medium effect size f2 = 0.15, α = 0.05, 80% power) and intergroup comparisons. Accounting for potential data loss, the study exceeded this target, enrolling a total of 172 participants (86 OUD patients and 86 healthy controls). Accordingly, the final sample size exceeded the minimum required sample and provided adequate statistical power for the planned analyses.

Ethical approval and process

The study protocol was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Adana City Training and Research Hospital (Date: June 4, 2025; Meeting No: 14; Decision No: 565). Data were collected between August and November 2025. All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection tools

Sociodemographic and clinical data form

Assessed participants’ age, gender, marital status, education, living arrangements, employment, history of mental/physical illness, past suicide attempts, substance use patterns, detoxification admissions, and criminal records.

Templer Death Anxiety Scale (TDAS)

A 15-item true–false self-report scale measuring anxiety related to death and dying [29]. Total scores range from 0 to 15, with higher scores indicating greater death anxiety. A sample item is “I am very much afraid to die”. The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Akça and Köse [30]. Cronbach’s α in the present study was 0.75. Recent systematic reviews have confirmed the robust construct validity and reliability of the TDAS across diverse cultural contexts [31].

Death-Related Depression Scale (DRDS)

A 17-item true–false scale assessing feelings of sadness, meaninglessness, and existential resignation related to death [9, 32]. Total scores range from 0 to 17, with higher scores reflecting greater death-related depressive feelings. A sample item is “When I think of death, I feel tired and lifeless”. Cronbach’s α was 0.78.

Suicide Ideation Scale (SIS)

A 17-item self-report true/false scale assessing suicidal thoughts, attitudes, and related cognitions [33, 34]. The instrument does not employ a single fixed assessment timeframe; most items evaluate current or generally endorsed suicidal ideation and attitudes, whereas some items refer to recent history. Total scores range from 0 to 17, with higher scores indicating more severe suicidal ideation. A sample item is " The idea of ending my life keeps coming into mind”. Cronbach’s α in the present study was 0.89.

Barratt Impulsiveness Scale Short Form (BIS-SF)

A 15-item scale rated on a 4-point Likert scale assessing cognitive and behavioral impulsivity [35, 36]. Total scores range from 15 to 60. A sample item is “I act on impulse”. Higher scores indicate greater impulsivity. Cronbach’s α in the present study was 0.83.

Psychological Resilience Scale for Adults (PRSA)

A 33-item scale rated on a 5-point Likert scale assessing resilience across six domains: structural style, future perception, family cohesion, self-perception, social competence, and social resources [37, 38]. Total scores range from 33 to 165. A sample item is “I easily establish contact with new people”. Higher scores indicate stronger psychological resilience. Cronbach’s α in the present study was 0.92.

Participant flow and response rate

During the recruitment period, 136 individuals with OUD who met the initial eligibility criteria were approached for participation. Of these, 86 completed all study procedures and were included in the final analysis. Fifty individuals either declined participation or did not complete the assessment battery, corresponding to an overall non-response rate of 36.7%. Data collection was conducted through face-to-face interviews and paper-based self-report questionnaires administered in a clinical setting. Informal observations during recruitment suggested that questionnaire burden, literacy-related difficulties, and reluctance to engage with death-related content may have contributed to non-participation. However, because reasons for refusal were not systematically recorded, these observations should be interpreted cautiously. Among participants who agreed to participate and completed the assessment session, no missing item-level data were observed because questionnaires were reviewed immediately after completion and any omitted responses were clarified at the time of assessment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27.0. Normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test together with skewness and kurtosis values. Descriptive statistics were presented as means ± standard deviations, medians [IQR], and frequencies (%), as appropriate. Group comparisons were performed using Independent Samples t-tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, and Chi-square tests. Given the number of between-group comparisons, Bonferroni-adjusted significance levels were additionally examined for the primary group comparisons to reduce the risk of family-wise error. Effect sizes were reported using Cohen’s d and rank-biserial correlation (r). Associations among continuous variables were examined using Spearman correlation coefficients.

Hierarchical linear regression analyses were performed exclusively within the OUD group to identify predictors of suicidal ideation and impulsivity. Based on the study’s conceptual framework, death anxiety (TDAS), death-related depression (DRDS), and psychological resilience (PRSA) were entered sequentially into the regression models. Lifetime suicide attempt history was included as a clinical covariate in the suicidal ideation model because of its well-established association with suicide risk. To evaluate the robustness of the findings, additional sensitivity analyses were conducted adjusting for age, sex, marital status, educational level, and employment status. Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values were below 1.25, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity. Although psychological resilience was theoretically conceptualized as a potential protective resource, formal mediation and moderation analyses were not predefined as primary study objectives and were therefore not included in the main analytic strategy. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Comparison of scale scores between groups

To account for multiple comparisons across the five primary clinical scales (TDAS, DRDS, SIS, BIS, and PRSA), a Bonferroni correction was applied, resulting in an adjusted significance threshold of p < 0.01. When evaluating the relationships between the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the patient group and their scale scores, significant correlations were found between the number of prior inpatient admissions and suicidal ideation (r = 0.349, p = 0.001) and between the number of prior inpatient admissions and psychological resilience (r = -0.248, p = 0.021). Additional exploratory analyses compared death-related affect scores according to lifetime suicide-attempt history. Among the OUD group, 13 patients (15.1%) had a history of at least one suicide attempt. No statistically significant differences were observed between patients with and without a history of suicide attempt regarding their levels of death anxiety (t = -0.527, p = 0.600) or death-related depression (t = -0.655, p = 0.514).

Prediction of suicidal ideation

In the hierarchical regression predicting suicidal ideation (SIS), history of suicide attempt and death anxiety were included in the first step. This initial model significantly predicted suicidal ideation (R² = 0.194, p < 0.001), primarily driven by the strong effect of past suicide attempts (p < 0.001). In the second step, the addition of death-related depression resulted in a significant increase in explained variance (ΔR² = 0.075, p = 0.005). In the third step, psychological resilience further increased the explained variance by 8.3% (ΔR² = 0.083, p = 0.002), and the final model accounted for 35.2% of the total variance in suicidal ideation. In the final model, death-related depression emerged as a significant positive predictor of suicidal ideation (β = 0.278, p = 0.007), whereas psychological resilience demonstrated a significant negative association (β = -0.296, p = 0.002) (Table 4).

Sensitivity analyses

Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted adjusting for age, sex, marital status, educational level, and employment status. The overall pattern of results remained unchanged. In the suicidal ideation model, death-related depression remained a significant positive predictor (p = 0.005), whereas psychological resilience remained a significant negative predictor (p = 0.003). In the impulsivity model, psychological resilience continued to demonstrate a robust inverse association with impulsivity (p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study examined suicidal ideation and impulsivity in individuals with opioid use disorder (OUD) from the perspective of two theoretically distinct dimensions of death-related affect: death anxiety and death-related depression. Several important findings emerged. First, death anxiety levels did not significantly differ between individuals with OUD and healthy controls. Second, death-related depression was significantly elevated in the OUD group. Third, death-related depression—but not death anxiety—emerged as an independent predictor of suicidal ideation, whereas psychological resilience demonstrated a protective association with both suicidal ideation and impulsivity. Taken together, these findings suggest that emotional responses to mortality in OUD may be better understood as multidimensional phenomena rather than as a unitary fear-based construct. In particular, existential themes such as hopelessness, meaninglessness, resignation, and emotional exhaustion appear to be more closely related to suicide vulnerability than fear of death itself.

From a theoretical perspective, these findings align with distinctions proposed by Terror Management Theory and hopelessness-based models of suicidality. Within Terror Management Theory, death anxiety reflects a threat-based response to mortality awareness that typically activates psychological defense mechanisms aimed at preserving meaning, self-worth, and symbolic continuity [11, 12]. In contrast, death-related depression reflects a qualitatively different response characterized by diminished meaning, hopelessness, loneliness, and existential resignation [9, 10]. Contemporary theories of suicide similarly emphasize hopelessness, perceived lack of future possibilities, and loss of meaning as central psychological processes underlying suicidal ideation [17, 18]. Previous research in addiction populations has also highlighted the interplay between existential distress and suicidality [39], as well as the protective role of purpose in life and future orientation against substance-related harms [40]. The present findings extend this literature by demonstrating that death-related depression, rather than death anxiety, accounted for unique variance in suicidal ideation even after controlling for other relevant factors. Importantly, the findings also highlight a potential limitation of exclusively anxiety-focused interpretations of mortality awareness. While Terror Management Theory provides a valuable framework for understanding fear-based responses to death, it may be less well suited to explaining mortality-related depressive states characterized by hopelessness, meaninglessness, and existential resignation. In this respect, our findings suggest that mortality-related distress in OUD may be more comprehensively understood through existential and hopelessness-based perspectives than through anxiety-focused models alone, as awareness of mortality appears to be expressed primarily through existential despair, hopelessness, and a diminished sense of future orientation rather than fear-based reactions.

Although previous studies without healthy control groups have reported elevated death anxiety among chronic substance users [41], the comparable levels observed between our outpatient OUD group and healthy controls may reflect more complex psychological adaptations to repeated exposure to mortality-related events. Individuals with OUD are frequently confronted with overdose risk, substance-related losses, and premature deaths within their social environments [6, 42]. Repeated exposure to threatening experiences may contribute to habituation processes characterized by attenuated fear responses over time [43, 44]. Consistent with this interpretation, earlier age of substance use initiation was associated with lower death anxiety in the present study, suggesting that prolonged exposure to high-risk environments may alter perceptions of mortality. Furthermore, participants were recruited during remission while receiving maintenance treatment, a context that may also reduce acute threat perceptions associated with active substance use. From a Terror Management Theory perspective, mortality awareness does not invariably manifest as overt death anxiety; rather, it may become expressed through disruptions in meaning-making processes, emotional numbing, hopelessness, or existential distress when anxiety-buffering systems are compromised [14, 45]. Accordingly, mortality awareness in OUD may be reflected less through fear-based reactions and more through depressive and existential processes [46]. This pattern may be further reinforced by persistent psychosocial adversities common among individuals with OUD, including financial hardship, social marginalization, and repeated treatment failures. Previous studies have shown that opioid-dependent individuals frequently experience profound demoralization, meaninglessness, and helplessness, which may foster death-related depressive cognitions [47]. This interpretation is consistent with our finding that death-related depression, but not death anxiety, was associated with suicidal ideation. Interestingly, death-related affect did not significantly differ between patients with and without a lifetime history of suicide attempt. This finding is consistent with ideation-to-action models suggesting that hopelessness-related cognitions may be more strongly associated with suicidal ideation than with the transition from ideation to suicidal behavior [48]. However, given the small number of participants with a prior suicide attempt (n = 13), this finding should be interpreted cautiously and requires replication in larger prospective studies.

Psychological resilience demonstrated significant inverse associations with both suicidal ideation and impulsivity in the present study. Within contemporary resilience frameworks, resilience is conceptualized as a protective psychological resource that facilitates adaptive coping, emotional regulation, and behavioral control. Emotion-related components of impulsivity have been consistently linked to addiction severity, relapse risk, and self-destructive behaviors [20, 21], suggesting that resilience may be particularly relevant in buffering emotion-driven impulsive responding in OUD. Consistent with previous literature, resilience may function as a protective factor against existential distress and reduce vulnerability to maladaptive behavioral responses [27, 49]. Importantly, individuals with a greater number of previous inpatient admissions demonstrated higher levels of suicidal ideation and lower levels of psychological resilience. Although the cross-sectional design precludes causal interpretation, this finding suggests that patients requiring repeated hospitalization may represent a particularly vulnerable subgroup characterized by greater psychological burden and diminished coping resources. From an existential perspective, repeated treatment episodes may reflect not only illness severity but also ongoing struggles with meaning, loss, and psychological adaptation [50, 51]. Taken together, these findings reinforce the potential importance of resilience-enhancing interventions as part of comprehensive suicide prevention and recovery-oriented treatment strategies in OUD.

From a clinical perspective, these findings highlight the necessity of moving beyond conventional anxiety-focused approaches when evaluating suicide risk in individuals with OUD. Accordingly, incorporating the assessment of death-related depressive cognitions into routine psychiatric evaluations may help identify individuals who remain vulnerable to suicidal ideation despite relatively low levels of reported death anxiety [18, 52]. Furthermore, the protective associations observed between psychological resilience, suicidal ideation, and impulsivity underscore the potential value of strength-based therapeutic approaches. Interventions targeting meaning-making processes, existential reframing, adaptive coping, and resilience enhancement may represent valuable adjuncts to standard buprenorphine/naloxone maintenance treatment. Integrating these psychosocial and existential dimensions into addiction care may contribute to improved behavioral regulation, reduced suicide vulnerability, and better long-term recovery outcomes in this high-risk population [27, 53–55].

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the direction of relationships between variables. Because death-related affect, impulsivity, and suicidal ideation are dynamic constructs that may fluctuate over time, future research using longitudinal designs is warranted [56]. Furthermore, the reliance on self-report measures and the single-center nature of the study may limit the generalizability of the findings. Although healthcare professionals were excluded from the control group to minimize health-literacy bias, the control sample was recruited from the hospital community environment, and significant differences remained between groups in educational attainment and employment status. Therefore, residual socioeconomic confounding and selection bias cannot be completely excluded. Additionally, because the sample consisted exclusively of individuals in remission under buprenorphine/naloxone maintenance treatment and participants with concurrent non-opioid substance use were excluded, the findings may not fully generalize to individuals with active opioid use or polysubstance use, which is common in real-world clinical populations. Although the introduction discusses social losses and mortality-related experiences within interpersonal networks as potential contributors to death-related affect, these variables were not directly assessed in the present study. Future studies may benefit from examining whether exposure to overdose deaths, bereavement, or social network disruption influences the relationship between death-related affect and suicidal ideation in OUD. Additionally, the relatively small number of participants with a lifetime history of suicide attempt limited the statistical power of subgroup analyses examining death-related affect according to suicide-attempt history. Finally, although psychological resilience demonstrated significant associations with suicidal ideation and impulsivity, the present study did not examine potential mediating or moderating mechanisms.

In conclusion, the present study highlights the multidimensional nature of death-related affect in individuals with OUD. While death anxiety was not independently associated with suicidal ideation or impulsivity, death-related depression emerged as a significant correlate and predictor of suicidal ideation, even after accounting for relevant sociodemographic factors. Psychological resilience demonstrated a robust protective association with both suicidal ideation and impulsivity. These findings suggest that existential hopelessness and loss of meaning may be more clinically relevant than fear of death alone in understanding suicide risk among individuals with OUD. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify causal pathways and to examine whether resilience-based and meaning-centered interventions may help reduce suicide vulnerability in this high-risk population.
