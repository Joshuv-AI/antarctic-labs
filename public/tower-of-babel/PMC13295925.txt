Determinants of polydoctoring among patients with multimorbidity: a Nationwide Longitudinal Fixed-Effects Study in Japan
Authors: Takayuki Ando, Bhautesh D Jani, Junji Haruta, Rei Goto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify which chronic conditions and patient characteristics contribute to increases in the number of regularly visited facilities (RVF), a measure of polydoctoring, among adults with multimorbidity in Japan.DesignRetrospective cohort study using individual fixed-effects Poisson regression models to estimate within-person associations between the onset of each chronic condition and changes in RVF. This approach controlled for all time-invariant personal characteristics. Effect modification by age, geographic region and baseline multimorbidity was also assessed.SettingA nationwide health claims database covering multiple insurance systems in Japan, including municipal National Health Insurance, Employees’ Health Insurance and the Long-Life Medical Care System.Participants4 696 790 adults with two or more chronic conditions and two or more consecutive years of follow-up.ResultsThe onset of examined chronic conditions was associated with changes in RVF, with substantial variation in effect size across conditions. The strongest association was observed for malignancy (RR=1.102; 95% CI 1.101 to 1.103), followed by osteoporosis (RR=1.065; 95% CI 1.064 to 1.065), arthritis (RR=1.061; 95% CI 1.060 to 1.061), obesity (RR=1.049; 95% CI 1.044 to 1.055), stroke (RR=1.044; 95% CI 1.041 to 1.048) and cardiovascular disease (RR=1.043; 95% CI 1.042 to 1.044). Attenuated effects among older adults and individuals with higher multimorbidity suggest that patient capacity and treatment burden may influence patterns of healthcare utilisation. WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Polydoctoring—receiving care from multiple providers or facilities—is a major contributor to care fragmentation among patients with multimorbidity. However, little is known about which specific chronic conditions or patient characteristics are most strongly associated with polydoctoring.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Using a large-scale nationwide claims database and individual fixed-effects models, we found that the onset of examined chronic conditions was associated with changes in regularly visited facilities, with substantial variation in effect size across conditions. Increases were particularly pronounced for malignancy, osteoporosis, arthritis, obesity, stroke and cardiovascular disease. These disease-specific associations were attenuated among adults aged ≥75 years and those with higher baseline multimorbidity.

Our findings identify specific conditions and patient groups at higher risk of polydoctoring, offering actionable targets for care-coordination strategies. These insights may inform the design of more integrated and efficient healthcare delivery systems for people living with multimorbidity.

Introduction

The prevalence of multimorbidity, defined as the coexistence of two or more chronic conditions, is increasing globally due to population ageing and the rise in non-communicable diseases.1 Multimorbidity is associated with increased healthcare utilisation, polypharmacy, reduced quality of life and higher mortality.2,5 Therefore, identifying an appropriate healthcare delivery approach for the management of multimorbidity is an important challenge worldwide.1 One of the major problems of managing multimorbidity is care fragmentation.6 7 Managing patients with multimorbidity often requires care from many different healthcare providers, including specialists, nurses, dietitians and other comedical workers. However, when these providers do not communicate or coordinate effectively, patients can experience fragmented care.8,10 Previous studies have demonstrated that care fragmentation is associated with adverse outcomes, including increased emergency department visits, hospitalisations, inappropriate medication use and mortality.11,13

Polydoctoring, a term defined as a situation where multiple physicians or facilities are regularly involved in the care of chronic conditions for a single patient, has been recognised as a crucial contributor to higher care fragmentation.8 In Japan’s healthcare system, which is characterised by universal health coverage and unrestricted access to specialists without a gatekeeping system, polydoctoring is particularly prevalent and may contribute to inefficient healthcare delivery and increased treatment burden. To measure polydoctoring, we used the regularly visited facilities (RVF) indicator, a previously validated measure that captures facilities involved in the regular management of chronic conditions.8 Previous research using RVF has shown associations with increased fragmentation of care, polypharmacy and healthcare costs.8 14

To investigate the impact of polydoctoring on patients with multimorbidity, it is first necessary to identify which individuals are more likely to experience polydoctoring. A previous cross-sectional study conducted in an urban area of Japan identified eye diseases, osteoporosis, prostate diseases and osteoarthritis as conditions associated with polydoctoring.15 However, this study was limited to a single urban region and used a cross-sectional design that precluded a temporal inference. To address these gaps, large-scale longitudinal studies that can clarify within-person dynamics of polydoctoring are warranted. Therefore, we conducted this study to identify chronic conditions that are independently associated with increases in RVF and to examine whether these associations differ by age groups (older vs younger people), geographic location (urban vs rural) and the baseline degree of multimorbidity.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study utilised the DeSC database provided by DeSC Healthcare, a large-scale claims database compiled from multiple health insurance systems across Japan. The database period spans from April 2014 to June 2024. This database includes three main types of health insurance systems in Japan: (1) Employee-based health insurance (Kempo), which primarily covers employees of large corporations and their dependents; (2) National Health Insurance (Kokuho), operated by municipalities and covering self-employed individuals, unemployed persons and retirees under the age of 75; and (3) the Long-Life Medical Care System (LLMCS), which covers all citizens aged 75 years and older. The DeSC database has been used in previous studies examining healthcare utilisation patterns and outcomes in Japan.14 16 The coverage and fee schedule of the three insurance systems are the same because these are operated under a universal social insurance system.

Study population

Each patient was enrolled into the cohort at the time of registration in their respective insurance system (index date) and was followed until death, disenrollment from the baseline insurance system (including relocation, job change or transition to another insurance scheme), or the end of the database period, whichever happened first. We applied the following inclusion criteria: (1) presence of two or more chronic conditions as defined below and (2) 2 years of continuous follow-up data available. These criteria ensure that we analyse patients with established multimorbidity who have sufficient longitudinal data for within-person analysis.

Variable definitions

The outcome variable was the number of RVF. RVF was defined as the number of medical facilities meeting two criteria in the claims data: (1) having claims in 3 or more months within a given observation window and (2) having an interval of at least 6 months between the first and last claims within that window.8 14 15 17 In this study, RVF was calculated on a monthly basis using a rolling 12-month window, anchored at each calendar month. An RVF value of 0 does not indicate absence of healthcare utilisation, but rather absence of facilities meeting the predefined criteria for regular care within the observation window. While RVF may not capture provider fragmentation within a single facility, prior studies have shown that RVF values correlate with fragmentation of care index, polypharmacy, supporting RVF as a valid indicator in claims-based research.8 14 17 The exposure variable was the onset of each chronic condition. We classified chronic conditions using the Fortin’s list, which includes 20 condition categories commonly observed in multimorbidity research.18 To ensure the identification of sustained chronic conditions, a condition was considered present if it was continuously recorded in the claims data for at least 6 months. For each condition, the first occurrence date was defined as the first recorded month of the condition, provided that the condition continued to be recorded for at least 6 subsequent months, to reduce misclassification from transient or rule-out diagnoses. Incident diagnoses were then defined as conditions whose first occurrence date fell within the 3 months preceding the outcome month. Based on this definition, we constructed monthly time-varying indicators of incident conditions, coded as 1 if a new diagnosis occurred within the specified preceding window and 0 otherwise. We collected baseline patient characteristics including age at index date, sex, insurance type and the insurer’s regional location. Insurance type was defined at cohort entry and categorised as LLMCS (age ≥75 years) versus other insurance (age <75 years). Previous studies have found differences in specialist consultations between urban and rural areas.19 20 Therefore, regional location was classified as urban or rural based on the administrative region of the insurance provider.

Statistical analysis

We first conducted descriptive statistical analyses to summarise the baseline characteristics of the study population. To evaluate the association between newly diagnosed chronic conditions and changes in the number of RVF, we constructed person–month longitudinal data and applied fixed-effects Poisson regression models with a log link. All models included individual fixed effects, thereby estimating associations based exclusively on within-person variation over time and controlling for all time-invariant individual characteristics, whether observed or unobserved (eg, sex, baseline age, insurance characteristics, geographic location and stable socioeconomic factors).21 In addition, calendar-year fixed effects were incorporated to flexibly account for secular trends in healthcare utilisation over the study period. SEs were clustered at the individual level to account for serial correlation within individuals. Regression coefficients were exponentiated to obtain RRs. For each condition, the reference category was months without a new onset (0). Therefore, an RR greater than 1 indicates that the number of RVFs increased in months when that condition newly appeared, compared with months when it did not. To explore whether these associations differed by subgroups, we conducted interaction analyses, testing whether the relationship between disease onset and RVF varied by insurance type (aged 75+ vs <75 years), by geographic region (urban vs rural) and by the number of chronic conditions at baseline (2–4 conditions vs 5+ conditions). Because including interaction terms for all conditions in a single model would substantially increase model complexity, interaction analyses were performed using disease-specific models, in which each chronic condition was evaluated separately with the corresponding interaction term. Since individual fixed effects absorb all time-invariant characteristics, the main effects of subgroup indicators (age/insurance type, urban–rural location and baseline multimorbidity) are not separately estimated; effect modification was, therefore, assessed through interaction terms between these time-invariant subgroup indicators and the time-varying incident diagnosis indicators, which remain identifiable in a fixed-effects framework. Interaction analyses with age group, geographic region and baseline multimorbidity were prespecified based on prior literature and clinical considerations.15 19 Given the large number of interaction tests performed across conditions, multiplicity was addressed in exploratory analyses by controlling the false discovery rate using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. As a robustness check, we also repeated the analysis separately within each subgroup using the same modelling approach. For the analyses involving rural versus urban classification, we conducted a complete case analysis due to missing values in this variable. No imputation was performed for the missing geographic data. To strengthen temporal ordering and address potential reverse causation, we conducted sensitivity analyses using an alternative exposure definition in which new diagnoses were identified using a 2-month window, excluding the current month (ie, diagnoses occurring one or 2 months prior only). The same modelling strategies were applied, and results were compared with those of the main analysis. We assessed multicollinearity among exposure variables using variance inflation factors (VIF). We assessed overdispersion by comparing the residual variance to the mean of the fitted values. Statistical significance was assessed using two-sided tests at α=0.05. All analyses were performed using Python (V.3.13.2) for data manipulation and R (V.4.5.1) for regression analyses.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this study.

Results

A total of 4 696 790 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis (figure 1). Table 1 presents the characteristics of study participants. At the index date, the median age was 75 years (IQR: 68–81), 59.2% were female, and 65.3% were enrolled in LLMCS. At the first month of observation, the most prevalent chronic conditions were hypertension (71.8%), dyslipidaemia (59.5%) and musculoskeletal disorders (55.7%). The median number of comorbid chronic conditions was 4 (IQR: 3–6), and the median RVF was 1 (IQR: 1–2). Median observation period was 53 months (IQR: 36–60).

Figure 2 presents the results of the fixed-effects Poisson regression model as a forest plot, showing how the onset of each chronic condition was associated with changes in the number of RVFs. The strongest association was observed for malignancy (RR=1.102, 95% CI 1.101 to 1.103), indicating that the development of malignancy was associated with a 10.2% increase in the number of RVFs. This was followed by osteoporosis (RR=1.065, 95% CI 1.064 to 1.065), arthritis (RR=1.061, 95% CI 1.060 to 1.061), obesity (RR=1.049, 95% CI 1.044 to 1.055) and stroke (RR=1.044, 95% CI 1.041 to 1.048). The estimated dispersion parameters were below unity (Pearson φ=0.62, deviance φ=0.63), indicating no evidence of overdispersion in the fixed-effects Poisson models. Multicollinearity assessment using VIFs calculated from the within-transformed data showed that all VIF values ranged from 1.0 to 1.1, indicating no problematic multicollinearity (online supplemental table S1).

Figure 3 presents the interaction analyses examining whether the association between incident chronic conditions and the number of RVF differed by geographic setting (urban vs rural) and age group (≥75 vs <75 years). Differences between urban and rural areas were small in magnitude for most conditions, including malignancy (interaction RR 0.991, 95% CI 0.989 to 0.993) and osteoporosis (interaction RR 0.999, 95% CI 0.997 to 1.000), with only modest attenuation observed for selected conditions such as obesity and dementia. In contrast, age-related effect modification was more pronounced. Interaction estimates indicated that the increase in RVF following incident diagnoses was generally attenuated among individuals aged 75 years or older compared with those younger than 75 years. This attenuation was evident for several conditions, including malignancy (interaction RR 0.956, 95% CI 0.953 to 0.958), stroke (interaction RR 0.973, 95% CI 0.965 to 0.980), cardiovascular disease (interaction RR 0.983, 95% CI 0.982 to 0.985) and dementia (interaction RR 0.967, 95% CI 0.961 to 0.974), whereas little or no age-related modification was observed for conditions such as diabetes and hepatitis. Overall, although the associations between incident chronic conditions and RVF were robust across subgroups, effect modification by geographic setting and age group was generally modest. To account for multiplicity arising from multiple interaction tests, we controlled the false discovery rate using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. The adjusted p values are reported in online supplemental tables S2–S4.

Discussion

Using a large-scale longitudinal claims database and fixed-effects regression models that control for time-invariant individual characteristics, we identified chronic conditions that are independently and strongly associated with increases in RVF: malignancy, osteoporosis, arthritis, obesity and stroke. These findings support the hypothesis that the development of certain conditions—particularly those requiring multi-specialty care—can drive increases in healthcare fragmentation. Although these effect sizes were modest at the individual level, their cumulative impact may be substantial in a large ageing population such as Japan. This study also revealed that these associations varied by patient characteristics. Interaction and subgroup analyses revealed that the impact of newly diagnosed conditions on RVF was attenuated among patients aged over 75 and those with five or more baseline chronic conditions.

Our findings are partially consistent with a previous cross-sectional study conducted in an urban area of Japan, which identified eye diseases, osteoporosis, prostate diseases and osteoarthritis as conditions associated with polydoctoring.15 The current study confirms osteoporosis and arthritis as important contributors but reveals malignancy as the strongest predictor—a finding that was not prominent in the previous study. This discrepancy likely stems from differences in data sources and measurement methods. The previous study relied on self-reported disease information from patient questionnaires, which may underestimate the prevalence of malignancy due to underreporting or lack of awareness of early-stage cancers. In contrast, the current study uses claims data that capture all billed diagnoses, including asymptomatic or early-stage malignancies detected through screening programmes. Furthermore, the Fortin list used in this study does not include specific categories for eye diseases as distinct entities, which may explain why these conditions were not highlighted in our results.

Malignancy requires specialised treatment, and suspected cases are typically referred to higher-level medical institutions. In Japan, specialist access is not restricted by gatekeeping, which means that patients can reach oncology specialists without delays. As a result, individuals with newly suspected cancers—including those identified through municipal cancer screening programmes—are often promptly referred to secondary or tertiary care facilities. This system not only facilitates early specialist involvement but also increases the number of facilities involved in a patient’s care, thereby contributing to higher RVF. Osteoporosis and arthritis are common conditions frequently encountered in primary care settings. In many countries, these conditions are typically managed by general practitioners or family physicians as part of their scope of practice.22 23 However, in Japan—where general practice is a relatively new specialty and a robust primary care infrastructure remains underdeveloped—patients often seek care directly from organ-specific specialists. In addition, both conditions are characterised by episodic exacerbations and complications—such as fragility fractures or acute joint symptoms—that may necessitate consultations across multiple specialties, including orthopaedics, endocrinology and rehabilitation services. As a result, conditions that would ordinarily be consolidated within primary care in other health systems tend to generate additional visits to separate facilities in Japan, thereby contributing to polydoctoring and potential care fragmentation.15 Obesity also showed a positive association with RVF, although obesity is infrequently coded in Japanese claims data due to the relatively low population prevalence of BMI-defined obesity and the tendency for diagnosis to be recorded mainly when patients attend specialised metabolic or obesity clinics.24 In addition, stroke and cardiovascular disease showed moderate but consistent associations with increased RVF. These conditions often involve acute hospital admission, followed by continued care in hospital-based specialty outpatient clinics. Postacute management typically spans multiple care settings, including rehabilitation and outpatient follow-up, and transitions across these settings may increase the number of facilities involved even when care remains centred on a single hospital.

Our interaction analyses revealed important heterogeneity in these associations. LLMCS insurance beneficiaries (age ≥75 years) showed systematically smaller effects of chronic conditions on RVF increases compared with younger patients with multimorbidity. Several mechanisms may explain this pattern, particularly treatment burden and patient capacity limitations—older adults face mobility, cognitive, sensory and transportation challenges that constrain their ability to add new providers. Although economic barriers are less likely to explain this attenuation—because out-of-pocket payments are often lower under the LLMCS scheme—the reduced RVF response among older adults suggests that non-financial factors play a more prominent role. In addition, disease prioritisation may occur, whereby patients and physicians focus on managing the most important conditions within the existing care network.25 26 Our study design cannot definitively distinguish among these mechanisms, but the consistent finding suggests that care coordination interventions should be tailored differently for older versus younger multimorbidity patients.

Importantly, the magnitude of effect modification varied across conditions. Attenuation of RVF increases among older adults and those with higher baseline multimorbidity was particularly pronounced for malignancy, dementia, and kidney disease. One possible explanation is that, in patients with substantial comorbidity burden, highly invasive or intensive specialist treatments may be deliberately limited in consideration of overall prognosis, competing risks, and treatment burden.7 Alternatively, these patterns may also reflect under-referral or unmet specialty care needs in vulnerable populations.27 28 Although our study design cannot distinguish between these possibilities, the findings underscore the need for careful assessment of the risks and benefits of specialist referral in older and more complex patients as well as for stronger evidence to guide referral decisions and care coordination strategies in this population.

Similarly, individuals with a higher number of chronic conditions at baseline showed attenuated associations between new disease onset and RVF increases. This may reflect a threshold effect, where patients with already complex care needs and multiple providers are less likely to add new providers in response to additional diagnoses due to cumulative treatment burden.17 25 These findings underscore the need for tailored coordination strategies that acknowledge the limited capacity of highly multimorbid patients to engage with fragmented care systems.

Finally, contrary to previous studies, minimal geographic differences between urban and rural areas likely reflect Japan’s universal health coverage: unrestricted access without referral requirements, cost barriers removed by national insurance and relatively wide distribution of healthcare facilities.19 20 However, our classification based on insurer administrative regions may underestimate true geographic disparities.

This study has several limitations. Although the fixed-effect models control for all time-invariant individual characteristics and calendar-year fixed effects account for system-wide changes over time, unobserved individual-level factors that vary over time—such as changes in family circumstances, caregiving responsibilities or social support—may still confound the observed associations. Death may also act as a competing process. Patients with severe conditions such as malignancy, stroke or congestive heart failure may have less opportunity to accumulate additional RVFs because of shorter survival, potentially leading to underestimation of the observed associations. Despite the use of person–month data and lagged exposure definitions, some residual temporal ambiguity cannot be fully excluded because diagnoses and healthcare utilisation may occur in close temporal proximity. Furthermore, detailed within-condition analyses were not performed; therefore, the precise mechanisms underlying these associations cannot be fully determined. Claims data do not capture disease severity, cannot distinguish physician referral from patient-initiated care and measure healthcare utilisation at the facility level rather than the provider level, precluding direct assessment of care coordination quality. Finally, as this study was conducted within Japan’s healthcare system—characterised by universal coverage, unrestricted access and fee-for-service reimbursement—the findings may not be directly generalisable to settings with gatekeeping or managed care arrangements.

Despite these limitations, this study has several notable strengths. By leveraging a large-scale nationwide claims database comprising more than 4.6 million individuals, we were able to apply individual fixed-effects models that rigorously control for all time-invariant confounders. The person–month longitudinal design, combined with calendar-year fixed effects and sensitivity analyses using lagged exposure definitions, strengthened temporal ordering and enhanced the robustness of the findings. Moreover, the use of a standardised classification of chronic conditions based on Fortin’s list facilitates comparison with prior multimorbidity research across different healthcare systems, increasing the relevance and interpretability of our results.

Conclusion

This study identified specific chronic conditions—malignancy, arthritis, osteoporosis, obesity and stroke—as drivers of polydoctoring and potential care fragmentation among multimorbid patients in Japan. These findings highlight the importance of rethinking how primary care systems manage chronic conditions strongly linked to polydoctoring. Expanding the scope and capacity of primary care, especially for musculoskeletal conditions, may mitigate care fragmentation while maintaining care quality and accessibility. The attenuated associations observed in older adults suggest that patient capacity and treatment burden shape healthcare-seeking behaviour. Together, these results inform condition-specific coordination strategies and support the development of more resilient, equitable primary care systems.
