 Abstract
 With the discovery of the Higgs boson at the CERN LHC, the world’s highest-energy particle acceler-
  ator complex, scientists have acquired an important tool to study the fundamental building blocks of the
  universe. Precision measurements of Higgs bosons produced with large momentum allow for unique in-
  sights into the structure of the interactions of the Higgs boson with other particles that may shed light on
  physics beyond the standard model. While experimentally challenging, exploring such interactions with
  novel artificial intelligence (AI) methods can advance our understanding of the Higgs sector, including
  the Higgs boson’s self-interaction. Moreover, the LHC is undergoing a major upgrade to further increase
  its particle collision rate and thereby operate for an additional decade. The experimental detectors at
  the upgraded facility must process at least a factor of ten more data at rates of hundreds of terabytes
  per second all under challenging conditions. New AI techniques are required to reconstruct and select, or
  trigger on, the most physics-sensitive events in real-time to handle the resulting avalanche of data. The
  proposed research will achieve the goals of the LHC program at the CMS experiment by developing a sub-
  microsecond event reconstruction system using real-time AI algorithms that employ field-programmable
  gate array technologies. By harnessing sophisticated AI techniques, this research focuses on measuring
  the production of Higgs bosons at large momentum while enhancing particle reconstruction methods in
  the trigger and beyond. Overall, the proposed research has broader implications for the use of AI in
  resource-constrained, low-latency embedded applications across all fields of science.

  1 Major Goals
  The primary major goals of the project are threefold. These goals focus on developing tools and techniques
  related to artificial intelligence (AI) in the CMS experiment at the CERN LHC but have an impact beyond
  experimental particle physics.
 The first major goal (MG1) is to improve both real-time and offline event reconstruction and selection
  for the CMS experiment. This includes developing fast AI algorithms on field-programmable gate arrays
  (FPGAs) for the level-1 (L1) trigger high-luminosity LHC (HL-LHC) upgrade by, trained to reconstruct,
  identify, and preserve complex collision signatures. Within the scope of MG1, objectives include develop-
  ing the open-source library hls4ml to translate AI algorithms into FPGA firmware, creating AI algorithm
  codesign techniques to compress AI algorithms for better deployment in FPGAs, and deploying particle-
  based AI algorithms like missing transverse momentum (⃗ pTmiss ), jet tagging, anomaly detection, charged
  particle tracking, and particle-flow reconstruction in the L1 trigger and beyond.
 The second major goal (MG2) is to search for new physics and perform standard model (SM) mea-
  surements with high transverse momentum (pT ) Higgs bosons in CMS. As part of MG2, there are several
  objectives, including developing new AI taggers for unexplored high-pT Higgs boson final states like the
  fully hadronic decay with two W bosons decaying to four quarks (H → WW → qqqq) and the merged
  semileptonic decay with two W bosons H → WW → qqℓν, and developing CMS searches for boosted
  single, double, and triple Higgs boson production. In addition, we performed searches for long-lived
  particles (LLPs) produced through the “Higgs portal” or in the decay of b hadrons.
 Finally, a third major goal (MG3) is the application of AI techniques to generate simulated data at
  the LHC.

 2 Accomplishments
 2.1 Real-time and offline AI for event reconstruction and selection
 Under MG1, we developed real-time AI algorithms on FPGAs or ASICs for charged particle tracking [1–
 3], anomaly detection [4], data compression [5, 6], calorimeter energy regression [7], and jet tagging [8].
 We also continuously maintained hls4ml [9, 10], developed new tools for pruning and quantization [11, 12],
 representing quantized neural networks [13], low-power applications [14], altering skip connections [15],
 improving fault tolerance [16], fast resource estimation [17], and neural architecture codesign [18–20].
 We developed neural networks for p  ⃗Tmiss regression and long-lived particle (LLP) jet tagging for the
 CMS Phase-2 L1 trigger upgrade. Software emulation of preliminary versions of both models [21] was
 included in CMS software framework (CMSSW) in December 2023 thanks to work from postdoctoral
 researchers Dr. Daniel Diaz, graduate students Russell Marroquin Solares, Daniel Primosch, and Anthony
 Aportela, and undergraduate student Simon Poon. Both models are planned to be implemented on CMS
 L1 trigger boards. Postdoctoral researcher Dr. Melissa Quinnan also continues to lead efforts for the
 Run 3 CMS L1 global trigger anomaly detection algorithm AXOL1TL, which has taken data in 2024 and
     2025 [22–24].
 PI Duarte and collaborators developed the first machine-learned particle-flow (MLPF) algorithm [25,
 26]. Graduate student Farouk Mokhtar led efforts to improve it for both CMS [27, 28] and future
 colliders like CLIC [29] and FCC-ee [30]. He also investigated the interpretability of MLPF [31] and
 jet tagger algorithms like ParticleNet [32]. We also studied the interpretability of transformer-based jet
     taggers [33, 34].
 We studied graph neural networks [35] and efficient transformers [36, 37] for charged particle tracking
 and jet tagging [38]. Graduate students Zihan Zhao and Billy Li investigated self-supervised learning
 methods for building “foundation models” for jet tagging and other tasks [39, 40]. Duarte, Kansal, and
 collaborators studied a highly granular multiclassifier as a foundation model [41]. This work was extended
 to the CMS experiment as the global particle transformer (GloParT) algorithm for tagging large-radius
 jets [42]. Duarte and undergraduate student Steven Tsan developed a graph-based autoencoder for
 anomaly detection algorithms [43, 44], undergraduate student Zichun Hao led the development of a
 Lorentz-equivariant autoencoder [45], and with other collaborators we developed normalizing-flow-based
 anomaly detection algorithms [46, 47]
 Our group and collaborators have developed the Services for Optimized Network Inference on Copro-
 cessors (SONIC) framework for inference as a service in scientific experiments. Accelerated inference has
 been demonstrated on FPGAs [48], on GPUs [49], within CMSSW [50], and for tracking [51]. We also
 helped develop the Kubernetes server infrastructure, known as SuperSONIC [52].

 2.2 High-momentum Higgs boson and other exotic physics searches
 Under MG2, the Run 2 boosted HH → bbWW → bb4q analysis led by graduate student Raghav Kansal
 was been approved and released as a physical analysis summary in July 2024 [53]. This work was included
 in the Run 2 “legacy” HH combination [54], submitted to Journal of Physics G: Nuclear and Particle
 Physics. A physics analysis summary has been released for the Run 2 boosted H → WW → qqℓν analysis
 led by graduate student Farouk Mokhtar [55]. One innovative element was the fine-tuning of the GloParT
 tagger to improve the final analysis significance, leading to nearly 2σ combined sensitivity.
 A related project led by graduate student Haoyang (Billy) Li and undergraduate students Darius Chao
 and Kaitlyn White is studying the use of symmetry-preserving attention networks (SPANets) to improve
 the reconstruction of multi-Higgs-boson events [56]. Similar methods were used in the CMS HHH → 6b
 search, released as a physics analysis summary [57]. Finally, graduate students Raghav Kansal, Daniel
 Primosch, and Billy Li contributed to the Run 3 search for boosted HH → bbbb, included in a physics
 analysis summary [58].
 Postdoctoral researcher Dr. Daniel Diaz led a search for LLPs originating from b hadron decays,
 which create electromagnetic or hadronic showers in the CMS endcap muon system, using the B parking
 dataset collected in 2018, accepted by Phys. Rev. D [59]. This follows up previous searches for LLPs
 by our group and collaborators [60–64] Anthony Aportela and undergraduate students Aditya Sriram,
 Trevin Lee, and Emily Pan also developed a graph neural network for identifying LLP showers in the
 CMS muon system, which can be used to analyze the Run 3 data.

 2.3 Generative AI for particle physics simulation
 Under MG3, Raghav Kansal led the creation of a graph-based generative adversarial network for gener-
 ating particle jet data [65, 66]. This work was accepted as a conference paper to the Neural Information
 Processing Conference in 2021. We also proposed metrics for evaluating generative models [67], and
 developed an improved algorithm based on a transformer [68]. Kansal, together with undergraduate
 students Carlos Pareja and Zichun Hao published an article in the Journal of Open Source Software on
 the JetNet library [69]. With collaborators, we also developed generative models based on variational
 autoencoders and normalizing flows [70–72].

 3 Training and Professional Development
 Postdoctoral researchers, graduate students, and undergraduate students have led or participated in this
 work as part of their training and professional development. They have also had the opportunity to
 present their work at various conferences. Postdoctoral researchers and graduate students have also
 participated in the mentorship of undergraduate and summer students, providing valuable experience.
 Finally, Duarte co-authored several reviews of machine learning [73–77], including for the Particle Data
 Group with new sections on self-supervised learning and foundation models [78].
