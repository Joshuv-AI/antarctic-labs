When the Cure Injures
Authors: Mustafa Karaçelik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: complication, left-sided catheterization, stroke, ventricular septal defect

Transcatheter closure of ventricular septal defects is now well established as a minimally invasive alternative to surgery in selected patients, with generally favorable outcomes and the advantage of avoiding cardiopulmonary bypass. The case reported by Yasim et al1 in this issue of JACC: Case Reports is therefore an important corrective. Their description of a massive right hemispheric infarction with subdural hemorrhage in an 18-month-old girl—occurring within 2 hours of a transcatheter vs closure attempt that was ultimately abandoned—raises questions about procedural risk that practitioners in this field cannot afford to ignore.

Periprocedural stroke in pediatric catheterization is rare, with reported incidences of 0.055% to 0.38%, yet outcomes are frequently devastating.2 Infants occupy a particularly vulnerable position: cerebral vessels are small, coagulation responses to heparin are less predictable than in older children, and the immature brain has limited tolerance for ischemia. An embolic burden that might be inconsequential in an adult can prove fatal in a child younger than 2 years.

The Anticoagulation Gap

The most probable precipitant, as the authors argue, was subtherapeutic anticoagulation. The institutional protocol at the time called for an initial heparin bolus of 50 IU/kg and a single activated clotting time (ACT) check at 1 hour. Here, only 1 ACT was obtained (189 seconds), below the 200- to 250-second threshold recommended by pediatric guidelines.3,4 The discrepancy between protocol intent and bedside practice is not unique to this case. Weight-based heparin dosing at the outset of a procedure does not reliably maintain therapeutic anticoagulation when the procedure proves more complex or prolonged than anticipated.

Infants have a shorter heparin half-life than adults, and pharmacokinetics vary considerably. When a procedure is extended by difficult anatomy, repeated device attempts, or unexpected complications, subtherapeutic ACT values can develop well before the scheduled monitoring interval. The authors’ institutional response—increasing the initial heparin dose to 75 to 100 IU/kg and requiring ACT checks every 30 minutes—is appropriate and consistent with available evidence.4 Centers that have not reviewed their anticoagulation protocols in light of this literature would do well to do so.

Contrast-Induced Neurotoxicity: An Underrecognized Contributor

The second mechanism the authors discuss—contrast-related neurotoxicity—warrants serious consideration. Administration of iopromide (Ultravist 370; osmolality 769 mOsm/kg) in a child younger than 2 years produced computed tomography findings, within 3 hours, that did not fit the pattern of isolated thromboembolic infarction. The mixed-density appearance suggested concurrent ischemia and contrast extravasation, and the day-8 magnetic resonance imaging showing vasogenic edema alongside cortical restricted diffusion is consistent with blood–brain barrier disruption playing a role. Contrast-induced encephalopathy after cardiac catheterization has been described in adults but has attracted less attention in pediatric practice.5

High-osmolality contrast agents may be poorly tolerated by the immature blood–brain barrier, and infants have limited physiological reserve to buffer the effects. The institution's adoption of lower-osmolality iohexol (Omnipaque 300; 684 mOsm/kg) for children younger than 2 years is a reasonable response to this concern. More broadly, using the lowest-osmolality agent suitable for the procedure and limiting total contrast volume in small children are practical steps that carry little downside and a defensible physiological basis.

Patient Selection and Procedural Timing

The case also raises questions about patient selection. This child weighed 7.8 kg, had a perimembranous ventricular septal defect with aortic cusp prolapse and mild regurgitation, a concurrent secundum atrial septal defect, and had developed an upper respiratory infection 2 days before admission. Any 1 of these features adds complexity or risk; in combination they describe a patient for whom the risk-benefit calculus of transcatheter intervention deserves particularly careful preprocedural discussion. The authors’ observation that surgical repair may provide a more predictable outcome in infants weighing less than 10 kg with aortic valve pathology should inform how such cases are reviewed at multidisciplinary team meetings going forward.

The recent viral illness is also relevant. Cytokine-mediated endothelial activation following respiratory infection has been linked to an increased risk of childhood arterial ischemic stroke in epidemiological studies.6 No formal guideline specifies how long to wait after viral illness before proceeding with elective catheterization, but the pathophysiology supports a period of deferral in higher-risk cases. The institution now waits 2 to 4 weeks after recent infection before scheduling elective procedures in such patients, which seems a reasonable position in the current absence of prospective data.

Postprocedural Neurological Surveillance

One aspect of this case that merits separate emphasis is the speed with which neurological deterioration was recognized and acted on. Signs of raised intracranial pressure appeared 2 hours post procedure; the team moved promptly to imaging and then to decompressive craniectomy. That response almost certainly influenced the near-complete recovery the child ultimately achieved. Early recognition depended on close observation rather than on any specific monitoring protocol. After complex catheterizations in young children—particularly those with prolonged procedure times, high contrast loads, or anticoagulation concerns—structured neurological checks in the immediate postprocedural period should be part of routine care.

Conclusions

Yasim et al1 describe a case in which several modifiable risk factors overlapped to produce a near-fatal outcome. That the child recovered to near-normal neurological function reflects both the quality of the multidisciplinary response and a degree of good luck that cannot be relied on. The practice changes the authors have implemented—higher initial heparin dosing, more frequent ACT monitoring, preference for lower-osmolality contrast in infants, and deferred scheduling after recent viral illness—are practical, inexpensive, and well grounded in available evidence. They deserve wider adoption.
