ABSTRACT
Predictions from physics-based computational models provide critical information to inform high-
consequence decisions, e.g., engineering design decisions. The ability to assess the reliability
of such predictions is therefore critical. However, to date, reliability assessment rely heavily on
expert judgment and qualitative arguments. This report details the efforts of LDRD 233072 to
develop quantitative methods to assess reliability of model predictions, especially in the context of
simplifying assumptions that can impact their reliability.

1. GOALS

In the absence of real-world data, model predictions provide critical information to inform high-
consequence decisions. State-of-the-art to assess model trustworthiness for extrapolative model
predictions defines a validation hierarchy (or pyramid) [1, 2]. In the validation hierarchy/pyramid,
model and experimental complexity increase toward the top, which constitutes the model used for
the prediction. The issue with state-of-the-art is that it requires expert judgment to define the model
hierarchy and relevant validation experiments, relying on qualitative arguments that experiments
are “close enough” to the prediction scenario to trust the model [1, 3, 4]. Additionally, it relies on
expert judgment to identify which parts of the model are most important to target for validation.
Previous methods addressing these challenges have limitations. [5] and [6] measure the relevance
of validation experiments based on the similarity of models’ sensitivity in validation and prediction
scenarios, but they assume models for validation and prediction share all parameters, which isn’t the
case for simpler models lower in the validation hierarchy. [7] developed a meta-model that linearly
relates sensitivities in validation outputs to the target prediction quantity of interest (QoI). However,
it performed poorly when validation observables did not coincide with the prediction QoI. These
methods’ inability to consider simpler models with fewer parameters and different outputs than the
prediction scenario deemphasizes low-level tests that are most abundant and usually have the least
uncertainties, wasting valuable information.
The lack of quantitative approaches reduces trust in computational models, causing increased (ex-
pensive) testing and prolonged project timelines. The main goal of this project was to develop
a novel approach to quantitatively assess and enhance model prediction reliability. We target a
quantitative and goal-oriented approach to establishing confidence in predictions by (i) identifying
the most important modeling assumptions to predictions, (ii) reducing uncertainties in these as-
sumptions by constraining them with data, and (iii) measuring whether assumptions are deployed
in within their “region of reliability” (to be described in our description of the approach).
If successful, the targeted methodology has the potential to significantly change how we assess
model trustworthiness and guide how programs prioritize investing resources: it allows decision
makers to determine whether assumptions can be relied upon for a model prediction and to determine
whether a test will increase prediction reliability. This work has the potential to impact applications
across the mission space that use physics-based models to inform decisions, including weapon
system modeling and nuclear reactor design.

2. APPROACH

We now discuss the our approach for achieving the aforementioned goals. Consider first that
physics-based models are constructed from reliable physical theories (e.g., conservation of mass)
with embedded, less-reliable modeling assumptions representing unknown terms in the governing
equations (closure or constitutive models, neglected couplings or state variables, assumptions
of isotropy, etc.). These assumptions are the sources of potential error in model predictions.
Thus, we hypothesize a model prediction’s trustworthiness can be determined based on whether its
embedded assumptions are deemed reliable. As such, our novel approach focuses on on assumption
reliability, which represents a paradigm shift in how model trustworthiness is assessed, which has
to-date focused on the reliability of the model overall.
The approach is depicted in Figure 2-1. First, the most important assumptions to the model
prediction are identified. Uncertainties in the model are reduced by calibration with data. To assess
the confidence in the assumptions important to prediction, we then quantitatively confirm (a) the
assumptions are important to validation tests (i.e., they affected the model’s ability to reproduce
data, and thus were tested) and (b) the assumptions have been tested in scenarios relevant to the
prediction. If (a) and (b) are satisfied, the assumptions (and thus the prediction) can be considered
trustworthy.

This project has focused on three key capabilities to enable this approach: (i) identify the most
important assumptions to predictions by quantifying and measuring the sensitivity of predictions
to model-form uncertainty arising from model assumptions; (ii) reduce uncertainties by informing
them with data; and (iii) identify a feature space that defines the use case of an assumption, which
may differ from the overall model’s use case (for example, a system-level model for hypersonic flight

 13

might be characterized by inflow velocity, altitude and angle of attack, while its embedded gas-
surface chemistry model and associated assumptions might be characterized by local temperature,
pressure, and gas fluxes to the surface).
To develop (i), we expanded the practical applicability of the approach presented in [8], which
measures assumption importance by combining sensitivity analysis with the emerging field of
model-form uncertainty (MFU) representations, which propagate uncertainty in assumptions’ math-
ematical forms to model predictions through parameterized modifications to the structure of the
assumptions. For (ii) we sought extensions to existing Bayesian methods, which account for model
and data uncertainties [9, 10], but which do not necessarily provide meaningful uncertainty quan-
tification in the presence of MFU. For (iii) we aimed to develop approaches to identify a feature
space which determines the use case of an assumption. As described previously, this could differ
from the physics-based model as a whole. If such a feature space can be identified, validation and
prediction use cases can be mapped into the feature space and distances can be measured there.
In this feature space one could define a “region of reliability” around validation cases, where the
assumption has been stress-tested against data for accuracy of the overall model prediction. So long
as the prediction use case fell within this region, the assumption could be considered trustworthy.

3. RESULTS

This section details our project’s efforts to realize the three novel capabilities needed to realize our
approach.

3.1. Identify important assumptions

Towards (i), in this project we expanded our analyses and performed more rigorous assessments of
the approach first described in [8], which are documented in [11]. In [11] we derive and numerically
demonstrate that two different MFU parameterizations producing similar effects on output statistics
result in similar sensitivity indices. That is, our approach is robust to the specific way MFU is
parameterized. Additionally, we showed how sensitivity indices can be meaningfully interpreted
after calibration with data, which can induce statistical dependence between inputs that violate the
usual assumptions underlying Sobol’ indices.

Agile MFU representation development

MFU representations to date have been time-intensive and required significant subject matter
expertise to develop. To address this challenge, as part of this project, in [12] we investigated a
hybrid physics-based, data-driven approach to accelerate the development process. The approach
incorporates physics-based understanding of the nature of the errors arising from simplifying
assumptions to pose an embedded MFU representation (also called an enrichment) in the governing
equations. For example, for the reduced gas-surface chemistry model considered in the paper, the
impacts of neglecting species led to reduced competition for surface sites and reduce production of
the main product of interest, carbon monoxide. We thus introduced a simple “placeholder species”
MFU representation that incorporated these effects back into the model but with far fewer equations
than tracking all neglected species.
Once the physics-based enrichment has been defined, any remaining unknown phenomenology
is informed through data-driven modeling. For our application problem, we used data-driven
modeling to represent a pseudo-reaction rate mapping placeholder species bonded to the surface
to the product, carbon monoxide. Novel in our approach is a staged approach wherein a pointwise
enrichment value is informed from data, which is then used to develop the data-driven model.
The staged approach allows inspection of the pointwise enrichment and its dependencies to better
inform the data-driven model development. Using this approach we were able to identify a subset
of gas-surface chemistry model inputs that were important to the MFU representation, which we
used as our inputs to the data-driven model.

 15

 [12] represents a positive step towards more agile development of MFU representations that is less
reliant on subject-matter expertise and instead uses data to inform MFU formulations. Over-reliance
on data-driven approaches may negatively impact the extrapolative power of MFU representations
(also called generalizability in the machine learning literature). However, even if the ultimate
goal is a fully physics-based representation, rapid prototyping using data-driven methods can help
inform such representations by revealing the phenomenology of the MFU across use cases.

Scalable sensitivity analysis

Once an MFU representation has been developed, it is still necessary to measure its importance
in validation and prediction use cases. To do so, we employ Sobol’ indices, which measure the
fraction of output variance that can be attributed to a single input or a group of inputs. However,
Sobol’ indices can computationally infeasible with traditional methods, whose cost scales linearly
with the number of input factors and can often require a minimum of 104 model evaluations [13].
Given-data SA methods have been developed to reduce this computational intractability [14,15], but
they were presented largely in the context of continuous random variables. For practical deployment
of our approach, we envision as a preliminary step sensitivity analysis with respect to alternative
assumptions, i.e., categorical random variables (choosing between closure or constitutive model
A vs model B). Therefore, as part of this project, in [16] we extended the given-data approach to
be amenable to categorical random variables, as well as developed other extensions that enable
greater scalability and accuracy. Specifically, we developed an algorithm for streaming or parallel
computation of the indices, and we introduced a more flexible Sobol’ index estimator with an
analysis of how it could be adapted to minimize error for a given sample size.
Further work would be needed to enable practical computation of grouped Sobol’ indices using
the given-data method, but our extensions open the door for error adaptivity in the computation of
such estimators, which we believe could make this possible.

3.2. Inform model-form uncertainties with data

Towards (ii), in this work we developed a novel approach to inform meaningful uncertainties from
calibration data. While MFU representations can be developed without access to calibration data,
ideally it would be possible to better inform MFU if data are available. A common approach to
inform MFU representations with data uses hierarchical Bayesian inference to inform the hyperpa-
rameters specifying the distribution on MFU parameters [9,17]. For example, if an MFU parameter
is assumed to be normally distributed, the inference problem would be posed in terms of its mean
and standard deviation. The hope with such an approach is that the calibrated hyperparameters will
lead to MFU parameter distributions that in some sense agree with the data.
From the perspective of aiming to adequately quantify uncertainties in model predictions, a desirable
property for an approach that informs MFU from data would be that the parameter distributions
pushed through the model yield output distributions under which the data are deemed probable.
However, in the course of this work we discovered that the previously described hierarchical

 16

Bayesian approach to inform MFU exhibits significant methodological and practical challenges
that make it infeasible for practical applications, which we have detailed in [18]. A key challenge
that limits the feasibility of this approach is that scale hyperparameters such as standard deviation are
often not informed by the data, leading to prediction uncertainties that depend heavily on the prior
specification for the scale hyperparameters. This is especially problematic because we typically
lack information with which to derive principled prior distributions for such hyperparameters.

Therefore, in this project we pivoted to identify an alternative approach that will be practically
applicable. Approaches such as tempering the likelihood [19] or leveraging more flexible notions
of deviation through approximate Bayesian computation (ABC) [20, 21] were explored and found
to help reduce posterior collapse in the presence of model-form uncertainties. However, these
inference approaches did not guarantee that the resulting posterior covered, in some statistical
sense, the calibration data while also being efficient, i.e. concentrated enough to meaningfully
inform decision making.

Beyond attaining pushforward distributions that are consistent with data when calibrating MFU

3.3. Identify features that define scenario for model assumptions

Because (iii) depended significantly on success of (ii), we were limited in our ability to address this
capability. However, as noted previously, the set of features that matters to a model assumption
may not agree with the set of features that matters to the model overall. We indeed found that to be
true for the hypersonic ablation problem considered in [12]. At the system model level the problem
can be parameterized in terms of altitude, inflow and vehicle velocity, angle of attack, etc., but the
inputs to the gas-surface chemistry model were local state variables such as molecular fluxes to the
surface, total pressure, and surface temperature. We were able to identify the subset of these local
state variables that the MFU depended on using sparse symbolic regression, specifically Lasso
regression [24]. This shows that some variant of sparse regression, potentially combined with
dimensional analysis or other similar techniques, could be used to identify a small set of features
that define the scenario in which a model assumption is exercised.
Further development of rigorous techniques to identify such features remains an outstanding re-
search question. We discuss promising paths towards addressing this aspect of the approach
in Chapter 4.

4. FUTURE NEEDS

While we made considerable progress towards developing the capabilities needed to realize the
approach proposed in Chapter 2, more work is needed. Key aspects that need further development
are in the joint calibration of MFU and model parameters and in the quantitative assessment of
validation test relevance to prediction use cases.

Joint calibration of model and MFU parameters

Assuming the novel approach targeting predictive accuracy in the calibration of MFU with data can
be matured, the outstanding challenge of joint calibration with model parameters remains in order to
realize (ii) in Figure 2-1. MFU representations modify and potentially improve model assumptions’
accuracy, reducing bias in model outputs and correspondingly the bias in model parameter estimates.
However, this interplay between MFU and model parameters can lead to confounding effects which
can lead to nonidentifiability issues. Additionally, it may be possible to overfit to data through
nonphysical combinations of MFU and model parameters, leading to overconfident prediction
distributions. These effects can be mitigated by encoding prior information about dependency
structure into the problem [25, 26], but we do not expect to know the dependence between model
and MFU parameters in general.
Based on the intuition that it is easier to calibrate in lower-dimensional spaces, we hypothesize
that by modifying the calibration problem to iterate between updating MFU parameters and model
parameters we can achieve improved accuracy and performance relative to the standard practice of
jointly estimating all parameters. A naïve implementation of this approach would suffer from the
fundamental limitation that fixing one set of parameters to a poor estimate will mislead estimation
of the other. We believe this effect could be tempered using the Bayesian approximation error
(BAE) method [27]. The method is typically applied prior to calibration to account for uncertainty
in model outputs from nuisance parameters that are fixed at a single value for the calibration itself,
thus reducing the misleading effects of fixing the parameters at incorrect values. In this context
BAE would be used in an online fashion to account for uncertainty in model outputs from fixing
either MFU or model parameters at nominal values for a given iteration.

Measuring validation test relevance to prediction use cases

To achieve (iii) in Figure 2-1, a general methodology to define the region of reliability for a
model assumption is needed. Reliable model predictions that extrapolate beyond available data are
possible as long as model assumptions are used within their region of reliability, i.e., where they
have been calibrated and tested. It is reasonable to require assumptions’ use within their regions

 23

of reliability because the features defining the use case for a model differ from those defining the
use case for an embedded model assumption. For example, in the previously discussed hypersonic
ablation modeling problem, while it may not be possible to reproduce system-level flight conditions
in ground tests, it may be possible to reproduce the local state variable values experienced by the
gas-surface chemistry model in flight on the ground.
We hypothesize that if a low-dimensional set of features that characterizes the use case of an
assumption (or, correspondingly, its MFU representation), the scenarios under which the assumption
is calibrated and tested can be mapped into this feature space to measure the distance between use
cases. For the hypersonic ablation modeling problem we used sparse symbolic regression to identify
the low-dimensional set of features that characterized the use case for the MFU representation,
namely the set of local state variables it depended on. However, other methods may be needed for
difference types of application problems, especially those which don’t exhibit local dependence.
Identifying features which are interpretable is a priority since they will be used to assess reliability
of assumptions for prediction.
There are several potential approaches that could be developed for this feature identification prob-
lem. Beyond sparse regression, it may be possible to use sensitivity analysis methods to identify
a subset of model use-case parameters that are most important to the assumption. Dimensional
analysis techniques may be used to identify a set of dimensionless parameters that characterize
the assumptions use case. While in some cases engineering judgment could be used to define
the dimensionless parameters, in other cases data-driven techniques could be leveraged to do
so [28, 29]. Finally, there has been an increased focus on methods for interpretable feature ex-
traction/engineering in the machine learning literature in recent years, which could be leveraged
towards this purpose [30–33].
Once a feature space has been identified, it will be necessary to quantitatively characterize the
region of reliability for an assumption in terms of the distance from calibration or validation points
that it can be used with confidence. We hypothesize this distance can be determined based on the
variability in an assumption’s uncertainty representation when calibrated at two different points
in the feature space. The variability will be measured as a rate of change of the assumption
with respect to changes in the feature space – where intuitively, high rates of change indicate an
assumption is not reliable far beyond its calibration scenario. This distance could be determined
by setting a tolerance on the allowable rate of change the assumption’s uncertainty representation
can have when calibrated using data at two different points in the feature space. Intuitively, a large
rate of change indicates the uncertainty representation of the assumption is not reliable far from the
scenarios for which it was calibrated or tested. The Hellinger distributional distance metric would
be a natural approach to measure the difference between the uncertainty representations [34].

5. CONCLUSIONS

This report summarized the efforts made in this LDRD to develop a quantitative approach to assess
model prediction reliability based on whether modeling assumptions (which are the source of
potential error in the predictions) were adequately tested in validation cases that are relevant to the
prediction use case. The approach relies on the construction of model-form uncertainty (MFU)
representations, which are parameterized modifications of model assumptions, embedded within
the governing equations, and sensitivity analysis, which is used to measure the importance of MFU
to model outputs across use cases.
While we made significant progress in improving the practical feasibility of developing MFU
representations and computing sensitivity to MFU, we encountered unforeseen challenges related
to calibrating the MFU representations using data. Namely, we found that a common approach to
calibrate MFU in the literature had significant practical challenges. In response, we developed an
alternative calibration strategy that takes a more goal-oriented approach to the problem, emphasizing
agreement of model output distribution agreement with data.
There is still significant work to be done to develop generic methods to address all aspects of our
proposed approach, however, the concepts discussed can be brought to new application problems
to inform treatment of MFU. Additionally, the methods developed herein have implications beyond
quantifying MFU and model prediction reliability. For example, the scalability extensions to
Sobol’ index computation are relevant for any sensitivity analysis or parameter screening problem.
Additionally, the novel calibration method is broadly applicable in cases where the computational
model is not a perfect representation of the data-generating process (this is almost always the case
for practical applications). Finally, the hybrid physics-based, data-driven approach to developing
an MFU representation for reduced gas-surface chemistry models can serve as a general approach
to develop reduced models that can reproduce high-fidelity model data at significantly cheaper
computational cost.
