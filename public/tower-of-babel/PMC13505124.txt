Retrospectively reported childhood ADHD symptoms and addiction severity in emerging adults with substance use disorders: cross-sectional indirect associations through time perspective
Authors: Samet Can Demirci, Utkan Boran Aşik, Burak Erman Menkü, Çağatay Haşim Yurtseven, Naz Aksoy, Aleyna Karabulut, Cansu Tayfur, Zehra Arikan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSymptoms of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) are commonly observed in individuals with substance use disorders (SUD) and may be associated with increased addiction severity. Time perspective is a potential psychological mechanism that could explain the association between retrospectively reported childhood ADHD symptoms and addiction severity. In this study, we examined whether the five dimensions of the Zimbardo Time Perspective Inventory (ZTPI) statistically accounted for cross-sectional indirect associations between retrospectively reported childhood ADHD symptoms and addiction severity.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 226 inpatients aged 18–25 years with SUD. retrospectively reported childhood ADHD symptoms was assessed using the Wender Utah Rating Scale (WURS), adult ADHD symptoms using the ASRS, time perspective using the ZTPI, general psychological distress using the DASS-21, and addiction severity using the Addiction Profile Index (API). Group comparisons were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test with Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons, while interrelationships between variables were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation. A parallel indirect-effect model was estimated to examine whether ZTPI dimensions statistically accounted for the association between WURS and API, controlling for age, gender, and DASS-21 total scores.ResultsThe stimulant-predominant group showed higher WURS, ASRS, and Present Hedonistic scores than both the cannabis and pregabalin/gabapentin groups. It also showed higher API scores and lower Future scores than the cannabis group. Higher WURS scores were significantly associated with an earlier onset of substance use, as well as higher ASRS, Past-Negative, Present-Hedonistic, Present-Fatalistic, and API scores; and lower Future scores. In the cross-sectional indirect-effect model, WURS was significantly associated with all five ZTPI subscales. In the final model, the Past-Negative, Present-Hedonistic, Present-Fatalistic, and Future subscales were significantly associated with API scores, whereas the Past-Positive subscale was not significant. The total and direct associations of WURS with API remained significant, and the total indirect effect was also significant. The model explained 58.8% of the variance in addiction severity.ConclusionsIn this cross-sectional inpatient sample, retrospectively reported childhood ADHD symptoms were associated with addiction severity, and this association was statistically accounted for in part by specific time perspective dimensions, particularly higher Present Hedonistic and lower Future orientation. These findings should be interpreted as cross-sectional indirect associations rather than evidence of temporal or causal mediation. If supported by longitudinal studies, time-perspective-informed interventions may represent clinically relevant targets in ADHD–SUD comorbidity.Trial registrationNot applicable.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Substance use disorders (SUD) are a public health problem that clinically begin and accelerate during the period extending from late adolescence to adulthood, placing a significant burden on educational/occupational functioning, social adjustment, and long-term health outcomes [1, 2]. This age range (particularly 18–25) is conceptualized not merely as a chronological “young adulthood” label, but as a specific developmental window where identity and role transitions accelerate, peer contexts become more pronounced, and self-regulation/planning processes are still maturing [3, 4]. Therefore, identifying the psychopathological and cognitive-motivational mechanisms that explain the intensification of SUD during this period is critical for both early intervention and designing more targeted treatment components [1].

Addressing SUD solely at the “present/absent” level may be limited in terms of clinical decision-making, especially during emerging adulthood. The DSM-5 approach emphasizes the assessment of clinical burden within a dimensional continuum based on a severity rating system using a number of criteria, while grouping SUD under a single diagnostic umbrella [5]. A severity-focused perspective provides a more explanatory framework for risk classification and treatment intensity planning by addressing areas such as loss of control, loss of function, craving, and consequences of use [5].

One of the key factors that increase the severity of addiction and complicate the course of treatment is the symptom burden of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and the co-occurrence of ADHD and SUD. A meta-analytic synthesis of prospective studies showing that individuals diagnosed with ADHD in childhood carry a higher risk of substance use and misuse/addiction in follow-ups extending into adolescence and adulthood supports the robustness of this relationship [6]. A recent meta-analysis based on clinical samples also indicates that the prevalence of ADHD in the general population may be around 21%, highlighting the clinical significance of comorbidity [7]. Furthermore, the ADHD–SUD comorbidity is associated with characteristics such as earlier onset, greater poly-substance use, and a more severe clinical course [8]. Previous developmental models also suggest that conduct problems and broader externalizing pathways during adolescence may partly explain the progression from ADHD-related behavioral dysregulation to later substance-related problems; however, these pathways do not fully account for the cognitive-motivational mechanisms that may operate in emerging adulthood [9–11]. Since internalization symptoms such as depression/anxiety, which frequently accompany this picture, can complicate both ADHD and SUD course, it is methodologically important to consider general psychological distress in mechanism studies [12].

The relationship between ADHD and substance use disorder should be interpreted not only in terms of comorbidity but also through clinically critical intermediate outcomes. The literature suggests that ADHD may be associated with earlier onset of substance use and that early/atypical substance use may be a key step in the progression to higher levels of use and substance abuse in later years [13, 14]. Additionally, there is evidence that the severity of ADHD symptoms may influence substance use patterns. The intersection with stimulant substances is particularly noteworthy; a systematic review and meta-analysis combining adult ADHD samples reports that cocaine use and cocaine use disorder are observed at a significant level in individuals diagnosed with ADHD, supporting the risk profile associated with stimulant use [15]. This accumulation suggests that in individuals with high ADHD symptom burden during emerging adulthood, there may be a clinical pattern extending from early onset and stimulant use to more severe addiction.

However, the question of which psychological mechanisms link ADHD-related symptoms with addiction severity remains unclear. At this point, “time-related” processes, particularly time perspective, may offer an explanatory intermediate mechanism. In Zimbardo and Boyd’s model, time perspective is a multidimensional orientation pattern consisting of past negative, past positive, present hedonistic, present fatalistic, and future dimensions. Past Negative reflects a generally aversive and regret-oriented view of previous experiences; Past Positive reflects a warm and nostalgic orientation toward the past; Present Hedonistic reflects a tendency to prioritize immediate pleasure and excitement; Present Fatalistic reflects a sense of helplessness and low perceived control over the present; and Future reflects goal-directed planning, delayed gratification, and orientation toward long-term consequences [16]. In the field of substance use, it is reported that present hedonistic orientation and past negative orientation may be associated with negative consequences of use, while future orientation may play a more protective role through goal-oriented self-control and tolerance for delayed reward [17, 18]. The ADHD literature also highlights the overlap between the “now-focused” orientation and ADHD symptoms, and the possibility that adult ADHD symptoms may be related to time perspective profiles [19, 20]. Therefore, the effect of ADHD symptom burden on addiction severity, mediated through the individual’s orientation on the “present–future” axis and other dimensions of time perspective, is a theoretically and empirically consistent hypothesis.

Present study

The primary aim of this study was to examine whether time perspective dimensions statistically accounted for the association between retrospectively reported childhood ADHD symptoms and addiction severity in emerging adults receiving inpatient treatment for SUD. Childhood ADHD symptoms were assessed using the Wender Utah Rating Scale (WURS), addiction severity using the Addiction Profile Index (API), and time perspective using the five ZTPI dimensions. Given the cross-sectional and retrospective design, the model was conceptualized as an indirect-effect model rather than as evidence of temporal or causal mediation. Secondary analyses explored whether childhood and current adult ADHD symptoms were associated with age of substance use onset, predominant substance type, and addiction severity. The indirect-effect model was adjusted for age, gender, and general psychological distress measured by the DASS-21 total score. Methods

Participants

This study was a cross-sectional analytical investigation examining the relationships between ADHD symptom burden, time perspective, and addiction severity in adults aged 18–25 years with substance use disorder receiving inpatient treatment. Data were collected through consecutive sampling from cases admitted to the Özgür Köy Treatment and Rehabilitation Center between February 15, 2025, and February 15, 2026; outpatients were not included in the study. The three target substance classes were selected a priori because stimulant-, cannabis-, and pregabalin/gabapentin-predominant patterns represented the most clinically frequent and clearly distinguishable non-alcohol target profiles observed in the inpatient unit during the study planning and screening period. Alcohol and tobacco use were recorded descriptively; however, they were not used to define the primary grouping variable because alcohol co-use was highly prevalent in the sample and did not provide sufficient variability for meaningful group-based comparison.

Inclusion criteria: (i) being between 18 and 25 years of age, (ii) having a diagnosis of SUD according to DSM-5, (iii) providing informed consent, (iv) being clinically stable enough to be evaluated. Exclusion criteria: (i) reporting a predominant pattern of use outside the three target classes, (ii) failing to meet the predominant single-substance criterion (as defined below) in the past 30 days of use or demonstrating a distinct poly-substance pattern, (iii) acute severe medical/psychiatric condition or agitation/delirium that prevents assessment, (iv) refusal to provide informed consent, (v) active ADHD pharmacotherapy (stimulants/atomoxetine/guanfacine), daily/maintenance benzodiazepine or Z-drug use, or maintenance antipsychotic use that could increase psychotropic confounding during the assessment window. Antidepressants were only included in the study if the agent and dose had been stable for at least 4 weeks. These exclusions were applied to reduce heterogeneity during the psychometric assessment window rather than to define the full clinical spectrum of ADHD–SUD comorbidity. Active ADHD pharmacotherapy may modify ADHD symptom reporting, attention-related functioning, and time-related self-regulation, whereas complex polysubstance patterns may complicate the interpretation of substance-group comparisons. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that this strategy limits ecological validity and may exclude clinically important subgroups, particularly individuals with treated ADHD or complex polysubstance use.

Sample flow and grouping:

During the study period, n = 548 cases admitted to the center were screened. Cases that did not provide consent during pre-screening (n = 19), were under 18 years of age (n = 7), and could not be evaluated due to acute clinical conditions (n = 12) were excluded, and n = 510 candidates were included in the evaluation. Candidates were pre-classified into three target groups based on the “primary drug of concern” information in the application record and then verified using TLFB-30 data: stimulants, cannabis, and gabapentinoids. Recent usage patterns were documented using the Timeline Follow-Back (TLFB-30) method; controlled environment days (hospital/prison, etc.) were excluded, and the use of a single target substance class in ≥60% of the last 30 days was accepted as the “predominant target substance” criterion. Cases that did not meet this threshold or whose usage pattern spanned multiple substance classes were excluded. Additionally, cases reporting a dominant substance class outside the three targeted classes were excluded. After all stages, the final sample size was n = 226, with the group distribution being Stimulants n = 78, Cannabis n = 73, and Gabapentinoids n = 75.

Predominant target substance definition: In this study, the grouping variable did not indicate exclusive lifetime use of a single substance. Rather, participants were classified according to the predominant target substance class used during the recent assessment window. Predominant target substance was operationalized as the use of one target substance class on ≥60% of use days during the previous 30 days according to TLFB-30 data, with no other substance class showing a comparable predominant pattern. This approach was used to reduce misclassification by focusing on recent clinically dominant use patterns rather than relying on a single admission-day biological screen or lifetime substance-use history.

Minor note (clinical application): Although UDS was administered at admission as part of routine clinical care, the study’s group classification was based primarily on TLFB-30 because UDS reflects substance exposure within substance-specific detection windows and does not directly indicate the predominant pattern across the previous 30 days. As a robustness check, concordance between TLFB-30-based predominant classification and admission UDS findings was examined descriptively.

Measures

Sociodemographic & clinical form

Age, gender, education, marital status, employment status, subjective economic status, age of onset and duration of substance use, alcohol/tobacco use (yes/no), family history of substance dependence (yes/no), and Form data integrity were assessed using range and consistency checks.

Timeline follow-back (TLFB-30)

TLFB is a calendar-assisted structured interview technique that enables participants to retrospectively report their substance use over the past 30 days on a day-by-day basis, using anchoring events and memory cues [21]. Its validity and reliability have been supported in substance-using populations [22, 23]. In this study, the TLFB-30 was used to document recent patterns of use and to validate the predominant single-substance classification.

Wender Utah Rating scale (WURS)

WURS is a self-report scale developed for the retrospective assessment of childhood ADHD symptoms [24]. The short form consists of 25 items and is scored on a 0–4 Likert scale; a higher score indicates a greater symptom burden. In the Turkish validity–reliability study, internal consistency α = 0.93 and test–retest reliability r = 0.81 were reported [25]. In the present sample, internal consistency was good (Cronbach’s α = 0.86).

Adult ADHD self-report scale v1.1 (ASRS)

The ASRS is an 18-item self-report scale that assesses adult ADHD symptoms over the past 6 months [26]. It is scored on a 0–4 scale (Never–Very often); a higher score indicates more symptoms. In the Turkish validity–reliability study, the total internal consistency α = 0.88 and test–retest reliability r = 0.85 were reported [27]. In the present sample, Cronbach’s α was 0.82.

Zimbardo Time Perspective Inventory (ZTPI):

ZTPI assesses individuals’ time orientation across 56 items and five subscales: Past Negative, Past Positive, Present Hedonistic, Present Fatalistic, and Future [16]. Each item is scored on a 1–5 Likert scale; in the present study, each ZTPI dimension was scored separately by summing its relevant items, and no total ZTPI score was calculated.In this study, the five subdimensions of time perspective were used as parallel mediators in the analyses. Turkish validity–reliability studies have reported that the internal consistency of the subscales is within an acceptable range [28]. In the present sample, Cronbach’s α values for the ZTPI subscales were 0.78 for Past Negative, 0.75 for Past Positive, 0.74 for Present Hedonistic, 0.81 for Present Fatalistic, and 0.82 for Future.

Addiction Profile Index (API)

The API is a 37-item, 0–4 Likert-scale measure that assesses the severity of dependence and related domains; it covers the subdomains of substance use characteristics, diagnostic criteria, impact on life domains, intense craving, and motivation to quit [29]. In this study, the primary outcome variable was defined as the API total score. In the present sample, Cronbach’s α for the API total score was 0.83.

Depression anxiety stress scales-21 (DASS-21)

The DASS-21 is a 21-item self-report scale that assesses symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress over the past week [30]. Each item is scored from 0 to 3; subscale scores are summed and traditionally reported after being multiplied by 2. In the Turkish validity–reliability study, sufficient internal consistency was reported for the subscales [31]. In the present sample, Cronbach’s α for the DASS-21 total score was 0.89.

Procedure

Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Assessments were scheduled during the same half of the day (morning/afternoon blocks) to minimize potential circadian effects. Clinical stability was operationalized as no moderate–severe withdrawal symptoms in the last ≥ 48 hours, no need for emergency sedation, oriented-cooperative consciousness, and stable vital signs. Cases with active ADHD pharmacotherapy, daily/maintenance benzodiazepine/Z-drug use, and maintenance antipsychotic use were excluded to minimize psychotropic confounding; antidepressants/mood stabilizers were included only if the agent and dose had been stable for ≥4 weeks. Standard withdrawal protocols administered for clinical care were not considered an exclusion criterion; psychometric tests were administered ≥ 48 hours after the last symptomatic dose and after the sedative effects had clinically resolved.

During the fieldwork, admission records were collected and pre-classification was performed based on the “primary drug of concern” information. Subsequently, trained evaluators documented recent usage patterns using the TLFB-30. Controlled environment days were excluded, and target substance use was confirmed as the dominant single-substance criterion in ≥60% of the last 30 days. Data (application record, TLFB, and clinical notes) were cross-checked for consistency; conflicting cases were resolved through independent review by two assessors and clinical committee consensus. As part of quality assurance, 10% of files were independently reviewed by a second coder.

Statistical analysis

Data were summarized using descriptive statistics (categorical variables: n and %; continuous variables: mean ± SD when approximately normally distributed and median [IQR] for non-normally distributed variables). Participants were classified into stimulant, cannabis, and pregabalin/gabapentin groups based on predominant single-substance use (≥60% of days) according to the 30-day TLFB. Study variables, including age, age of substance use onset, WURS, ASRS, ZTPI subscale scores, DASS-21 total score, and API total score, were compared across substance groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Effect sizes were reported as epsilon-squared (ε2), and Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons were conducted when the omnibus test was significant. Associations among study variables were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients. Given the number of bivariate correlations, Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to the correlation matrix to reduce the risk of false-positive findings.To estimate whether the five ZTPI subscales statistically accounted for the cross-sectional association between retrospectively reported childhood ADHD symptoms (WURS) and addiction severity (API total score), a parallel indirect-effect model was conducted using Hayes’ PROCESS macro (Model 4) with 5,000 bootstrap samples. All five ZTPI dimensions were entered simultaneously in a single parallel model rather than tested in separate models. Age, gender, and general psychological distress (DASS-21 total score) were included as covariates in the mediation model. Indirect effects were considered statistically significant when the 95% bootstrap confidence interval did not include zero. Because indirect effects often have non-normal sampling distributions, bootstrap confidence intervals were used to provide inference less dependent on normality assumptions. Unstandardized coefficients (B) and standard errors (SE) are reported. To facilitate interpretation of effect magnitude, completely standardized direct and indirect effects were additionally reported for the indirect-effect model. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, with Bonferroni adjustment applied to pairwise group comparisons and Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction applied to the correlation matrix.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the participants

The sample consisted of 226 participants, including 164 males (72.6%) and 62 females (27.4%). The mean age was 21.03 years (SD = 2.09, range = 18–25), and the mean age of substance use onset was 15.41 years (SD = 1.49, range = 11–18). Regarding predominant substance type, 78 participants (34.5%) were in the stimulant group, 73 (32.3%) in the cannabis group, and 75 (33.2%) in the pregabalin/gabapentin group. Alcohol use was highly prevalent in the final sample (n = 221, 97.8%), and all participants reported tobacco use; therefore, alcohol and tobacco were treated as descriptive co-use characteristics rather than grouping variables. Most participants were single and had low to middle socioeconomic status. Discussion

This study examined the association between retrospectively reported childhood ADHD symptoms (WURS) and addiction severity (API total) in 18–25-year-old inpatients with SUD and estimated whether this association was statistically accounted for by the five dimensions of the ZTPI in a cross-sectional parallel indirect-effect model. The total association between WURS and API was significant, and the association remained significant after inclusion of the ZTPI dimensions, indicating partial rather than complete indirect associations. The total indirect effect was also significant. At the level of specific indirect effects, Present Hedonistic, Future, Past Negative, and Present Fatalistic were significant, whereas Past Positive was not. Importantly, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of temporal or causal mediation. Rather, they identify time perspective dimensions that may help characterize the cross-sectional link between retrospectively reported childhood ADHD symptoms and addiction severity in a clinically severe inpatient SUD sample.

The clinical significance of the co-occurrence of ADHD and SUD is strongly emphasized in the literature: Meta-analytic findings have demonstrated that ADHD prevalence is at significant levels in substance use disorder populations; it has been noted that comorbidity may be associated with earlier onset, more severe clinical course, poly-substance use, and difficulties with treatment adherence [7, 8, 32, 33]. In our sample, it was also observed that as the retrospectively reported childhood ADHD symptoms increased, the age of onset of substance use was earlier; this finding is consistent with the view that ADHD-related self-regulation difficulties may create a foundation that can increase the severity of addiction through early risk exposure and a longer window of use [8, 13, 34]. Additionally, the higher WURS and ASRS scores in the stimulant group support the possibility that stimulant use may intersect more frequently with the ADHD phenotype at a clinical level and that this group may require more intensive assessment [8, 35]. In this context, a consistent clinical message emerges with recommendations to routinely screen for ADHD in SUD treatment settings and systematically integrate potential comorbidities into clinical decision-making processes [32, 36].

The most striking finding of the model is that the association between WURS and API showed a significant statistical indirect association through the Present Hedonistic time perspective. Present Hedonistic is associated with decision-making patterns that increase the value of “immediate” rewards and devalue delayed gains. Impulsivity, reward sensitivity, and executive function difficulties commonly seen in ADHD may reinforce this pattern; thus, short-term reinforcers (the rapid effect of the substance) may take precedence over long-term goals, exacerbating the consequences of use [37–39]. In the addiction literature, delay discounting is considered a transdiagnostic process consistently associated with SUD; it is debated whether it could be a plausible mechanism that complicates treatment outcomes [40, 41]. In this context, the increase in addiction severity due to the ADHD symptom load through the “now gratification” orientation conceptually aligns with the Reinforcer Pathology framework, which centers on the interaction of “strong immediate reinforcement + narrowed time horizon” [39, 42]. This situation suggests that, in addition to approaches focused solely on substance use, interventions targeting the patient’s response to immediate reward/stimulus contexts and impulsive choices may also be necessary. Components such as structuring immediate reinforcers, strengthening delay tolerance, and working with the trigger–impulse chain may be critical, especially in patients with a high ADHD symptom load [32, 36].

The second main finding is that the association between WURS and API was also carried indirectly through the Future time perspective; furthermore, in the final model, the “Future” dimension was found to predict the API negatively. This pattern may reflect a vulnerability pattern in which higher ADHD symptom levels are associated with weaker future-focused planning and goal maintenance, which in turn are linked to higher addiction severity. Future orientation has been addressed within a broader psychological construct linked to self-regulation and goal-directed behavior, and multi-domain meta-analytic studies have shown that the future time perspective is associated with behavioral outcomes [43]. One possible neurocognitive explanation for this association is working memory capacity. Working memory is central to self-regulation, planning, goal maintenance, and resistance to immediately rewarding alternatives; deficits in working memory and executive control are also well documented in ADHD [44, 45]. Therefore, reduced Future orientation in individuals with higher ADHD symptom levels may partly reflect difficulty maintaining long-term goals when immediate substance-related cues are salient.

Cognitive approaches aimed at extending the time horizon become important at this point: Episodic future thinking (EFT) is increasingly being studied as a method that can reduce delayed reward discounting and have positive effects on substance use outcomes [46, 47]. Pilot findings suggesting that EFT may reduce impulsive decision-making in clinical samples, along with experimental findings on alcohol demand/discounting dynamics, strengthen the idea that it may broaden the decision-making window by making the “future self” more vivid in individuals with low future orientation [48–50]. Therefore, in our study, the relationship between the weakening of the “Future” orientation and the severity of addiction is more than just a descriptive finding; it has the potential to generate intervention targets: structuring goal/planning skills, clarifying future-oriented values, and adapting EFT-like micro-interventions to clinical programs may be particularly rational in SUD patients with high ADHD symptom burden [42, 47, 50].

Furthermore, alongside these primary findings, the fact that the “Past-Negative” and “Present-Fatalistic” dimensions also serve as significant mediators—albeit to a lesser extent—demonstrates that the clinical picture extends beyond impulsivity and self-regulation deficits. This suggests that negative perceptions of past academic, social, or interpersonal difficulties (Past Negative), together with a sense of helplessness and low perceived control in the present (Present Fatalistic), may represent additional cognitive-emotional correlates of addiction severity among individuals with higher ADHD symptom levels [51–53]. This interpretation is also consistent with the literature indicating that individuals with ADHD may develop a more negative self-concept through repeated experiences of academic failure, interpersonal criticism, impulsive mistakes, and perceived underachievement [54]. From this perspective, Past Negative orientation may partly capture the internalized emotional residue of these experiences. In addiction models, such negative self-evaluations may increase the salience of substance use as an escape- or relief-oriented coping strategy, linking ADHD-related developmental difficulties with negative reinforcement processes in SUD [55].

Previous studies have noted that retrospective and fatalistic time dimensions are closely associated with general psychological distress and rumination [56–58]. However, it is particularly noteworthy that these two dimensions remained significant mediators in our model even after adjusting for age, gender, and DASS-21 total score. This finding indicates that these temporal orientations are not merely secondary reflections of a general depressive or anxious state; rather, they may represent specific cognitive correlates in the association between ADHD-related symptoms and addiction severity. Finally, the fact that, among the five dimensions examined, only the “Past-Positive” orientation failed to show a significant mediating effect suggests that the escalation of addiction severity is driven more by the presence of active negative and fatalistic schemas regarding the past and present, rather than by a mere lack of positive past representations [57, 58].

From a clinical practice perspective, first, the combined assessment of ADHD symptoms and time perspective in SUD treatment can produce a more structured answer to the question, “Who is at greater risk/who is worsening through which plausible mechanism?” Given the significant prevalence of ADHD in the general population [7] and its association with more severe comorbidities [8, 59], a screening-diagnosis-integrative treatment approach is rational [32, 36]. Secondly, in terms of the content objectives of interventions; components targeting impulsive choices and immediate reinforcement schedules may be prominent in patients with a dominant “now” orientation, while micro-interventions focused on EFT and future self/planning may be prominent in patients with a low “future” orientation [46, 47, 50]. Additionally, for patients burdened by high “past-negative” and “present-fatalistic” orientations, purely behavioral or future-oriented adjustments may not suffice. Cognitive interventions aimed at restructuring negative perceptions of the past and breaking the entrenched sense of helplessness to enhance self-efficacy should also be integrated into the treatment plan [56, 60, 61].

The original contribution of our study is that it examines the relationship between retrospectively reported childhood ADHD symptoms and addiction severity not only as a general association, but also in relation to specific time perspective dimensions that may represent clinically meaningful cognitive-motivational correlates. The combined assessment of WURS and ASRS provided a broader developmental characterization of ADHD symptom burden and offered convergent support across retrospective and current symptom levels. In addition, the comparison across three different predominant substance types provided a practical framework suggesting that ADHD burden may be related to clinical profile. Furthermore, testing the five subscales of the ZTPI within the same parallel mediation model, controlling for covariates such as age, gender and general distress level, has shown which time dimensions (primarily present-hedonistic and future, alongside past-negative and present-fatalistic) are more specifically discriminative. This integrated approach highlights that both deficits in the “temporal self-regulation window” and the presence of “negative/fatalistic cognitive schemas” may constitute clinically relevant domains for understanding heterogeneity in ADHD–SUD comorbidity.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design prevents conclusions about temporal ordering or causal mediation; therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as cross-sectional statistical indirect associations rather than evidence that time perspective prospectively mediates the relationship between childhood ADHD symptoms and later addiction severity. Second, WURS is a retrospective self-report measure and may be affected by recall bias, current affective state, and adult reinterpretation of childhood experiences. Third, the sample consisted of 18–25-year-old inpatients from a specialized addiction center, which limits generalizability to outpatient, community, or milder SUD populations. Fourth, the grouping strategy was based on recent predominant target substance use rather than exclusive lifetime single-substance use. Because alcohol use was highly prevalent and many patients with SUD have polysubstance histories, the findings should not be generalized to alcohol-predominant, opioid-predominant, sedative/hypnotic-predominant, or complex polysubstance-use populations. Fifth, excluding individuals with active ADHD pharmacotherapy or complex polysubstance patterns improved internal comparability but reduced ecological validity. Finally, the use of self-report questionnaires may have increased common method variance, and analyzing the API as a total score may have obscured domain-specific associations. Future longitudinal and broader-sample studies should test whether time-perspective profiles are associated with different substance classes, addiction severity domains, and treatment needs.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional inpatient sample of emerging adults with SUD, retrospectively reported childhood ADHD symptoms were associated with addiction severity. This association was statistically accounted for in part by specific time perspective dimensions, particularly higher Present Hedonistic orientation and lower Future orientation, with smaller contributions from Past Negative and Present Fatalistic orientations. These findings should be interpreted as cross-sectional indirect associations rather than evidence of prospective or causal mediation. Nevertheless, they suggest that time perspective may be a clinically meaningful cognitive-motivational domain in ADHD–SUD comorbidity. Longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to determine whether targeting immediate reward orientation, future-directed planning, and negative/fatalistic temporal schemas can improve treatment outcomes.
