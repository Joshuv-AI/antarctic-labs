Posterior Sampling Reinforcement Learning with Gaussian Processes for Continuous Control: Sublinear Regret Bounds for Unbounded State Spaces
Hamish Flynn, Joe Watson, Ingmar Posner, Jan Peters

Abstract
We analyze the Bayesian regret of the Gaussian process posterior sampling reinforcement learning (GP-PSRL) algorithm. Posterior sampling is an effective heuristic for decision-making under uncertainty that has been used to develop successful algorithms for a variety of continuous control problems. However, theoretical work on GP-PSRL is limited. All known regret bounds either fail to achieve a tight dependence on a kernel-dependent quantity called the maximum information gain or fail to properly account for the fact that the set of possible system states is unbounded. Through a recursive application of the Borell-Tsirelson-Ibragimov-Sudakov inequality, we show that, with high probability, the states actually visited by the algorithm are contained within a ball of near-constant radius. To obtain tight dependence on the maximum information gain, we use the chaining method to control the regret suffered by GP-PSRL. Our main result is a Bayesian regret bound of the order 𝒪 ~ ​ ( H 3 / 2 ​ γ T / H ​ T ) \widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(H^{3/2}\sqrt{\gamma_{T/H}T}) , where H H is the horizon, T T is the number of time steps and γ T / H \gamma_{T/H} is the maximum information gain. With this result, we resolve the limitations with prior theoretical work on PSRL, and provide the theoretical foundation and tools for analyzing PSRL in complex settings.

Introduction

The study of decision-making under uncertainty deals with the exploration-exploitation trade-off using statistical techniques.
Posterior sampling, also known as Thompson sampling or probability matching
(
35
)
, is an approach based on random sampling from a belief over optimal decisions. Despite its heuristic origins, posterior sampling is now understood to be theoretically sound
(
30
)
.
Compared to other principled methods, such as “optimistic” approaches, posterior sampling usually performs better and is often simpler to implement, especially when conjugate priors are available
(
26
)
.

In the posterior sampling reinforcement learning (PSRL) algorithm, a belief is maintained over Markov decision processes (MDPs) and an optimal control oracle is used to compute an optimal policy for a sampled MDP in an episodic fashion, resulting in random exploration over the space of possible optimal policies.
For MDPs with continuous states and actions, Gaussian processes can be used as a tractable yet versatile prior belief over MDPs.
However, while posterior sampling has been shown to enjoy strong theoretical performance guarantees across a range of decision-making problems, theoretical work on PSRL with Gaussian processes is limited. We identify several limitations with previous results, which we address in the rest of the paper.

Problem one: unbounded state spaces.
In the setting that we consider, the system states are corrupted by Gaussian noise, which means that the set of possible states is unbounded. If this is not properly accounted for, then a problem-dependent quantity known as the maximum information gain (cf.  Section
3.3
) can grow linearly with the number of time steps. In addition, arguments used to control suprema of Gaussian processes fail when the Gaussian process is defined on an unbounded domain. To obtain rigorous theoretical guarantees, it is necessary to show that the states actually encountered by PSRL lie within a bounded subset of the state space with high probability.

Problem two: sub-optimal rates.
Most regret bounds for GP-PSRL rely on an argument due to
24
, in which one constructs confidence sets that contain the true MDP and all the sampled MDPs. One can then show that the Bayesian regret of PSRL is as good as the worst-case regret of any optimistic algorithm. When tight confidence sets are available, this argument can yield near-optimal regret bounds. However, due to the difficulty of constructing confidence sets for functions in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs)
(
23
)
, when applied to GP-PSRL, this approach typically results in sub-optimal dependence on the maximum information gain.

Problem three: limited priors.
Previous regret bounds for the setting that we consider allow for limited choices of the prior. Approaches based on the “PSRL is as good as optimism” argument mentioned previously only allow for priors with support contained within a ball of the RKHS associated with the kernel function. In particular, this does not include Gaussian process priors. Other works have used bounds for suprema of Gaussian processes to upper bound the regret of GP-PSRL (cf. Theorem 4 in
12
). While these approaches do allow one to use a Gaussian process prior, they typically impose strong smoothness assumptions on the covariance kernel. For instance, Theorem 4 of
12
requires that the kernel function is four times differentiable.

Contributions.
Our contribution is as follows:

•

We show that, with high-probability, the states visited by GP-PSRL (or indeed, any algorithm) are contained within a Euclidean ball whose radius grows only logarithmically with the total number of time steps
T
. We prove this using a recursive application of a tail bound for suprema of Gaussian processes, which is referred to as the Borell-Tsirelson-Ibragimov-Sudakov inequality.

•

We prove a Bayesian regret bound with the best known dependence on the maximum information gain (cf. Table
1
). Our regret analysis accommodates Gaussian process priors, and only requires that the kernel function is bounded and Hölder continuous.

Related Work

Posterior sampling originated as an effective heuristic for decision-making under uncertainty
(
35
)
.
However, it has since been shown to satisfy (near-)optimal regret bounds for a variety of multi-armed bandit problems
(

2

and Gaussian process bandits, which is also known as kernelized bandits
(
30
;
11

Posterior sampling was extended to reinforcement learning by
32

(
24
;
26
)
and in MDPs with bounded eluder dimension
(
25
)
. Several works have applied PSRL to linear quadratic control problems
(
3
;
4
;
27

16

, as well as other parametric control control problems
(
1

, often focusing on the infinite-horizon setting.
12

15
proved Bayesian regret bounds for PSRL in MDPs with linear dynamics with respect to a fixed feature space.

Recent work has applied posterior sampling to MDPs in which the reward function and the dynamics are drawn from a multi-output GP. In this setting, correlations between the state-dimensions and the reward function can be exploited to reduce the regret suffered.
7
propose an optimistic posterior sampling algorithm, but do not provide a regret bound.

8

establish a regret bound for PSRL with multi-output GPs, which captures correlations between the reward function and the dynamics. However, the regret bound only applies to MDPs with deterministic dynamics, so it is not comparable to ours.
Moreover, all value functions are assumed to have bounded first and second derivatives.

Another line of work has focused on optimistic algorithms for the setting described in Section
3
.

20
developed confidence sets for the dynamics using a regularized least squares estimate and designed an algorithm that plays optimistically with respect to this confidence set.
This algorithm satisfies a worst-case regret bound of the order
\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(H^{3/2}\gamma_{T}\sqrt{T})
, but it is intractable to implement.

13
proposed a more practical optimistic algorithm, which introduces “hallucinated controls” into the predictions of the dynamics model and then performs a greedy policy search in this augmented system. The resulting algorithm is shown to satisfy a worst-case regret bound of the order
\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(H^{2}\gamma_{T}^{(H+1)/2}\sqrt{T})
for MDPs with dynamics governed by a function with bounded RKHS norm.

3.2
Regret Minimization in MDPs

We consider the following episodic interaction protocol. At the start of the interaction, the true MDP
{{\mathcal{M}}_{\star}=({\mathcal{S}},{\mathcal{A}},r,P^{\star},\rho,H)}
is drawn randomly from a known prior distribution (which will be specified in Section
3.3
). The interaction then proceeds for a total of
T
time steps, which is divided into
N
episodes, each of length
H
(so
T=NH
). At the start of each episode
n=1,\dots,N
, the agent chooses a policy
\pi_{n}
, and an initial state
{\bm{s}}_{n,1}
is drawn from the initial state distribution
\rho
. Then for each step
h=1,\dots,H
: (a) the agent observes the state
{\bm{s}}_{n,h}
; (b) the agent draws an action
{\bm{a}}_{n,h}\sim\pi_{n}({\bm{s}}_{n,h},h)
from a policy
\pi_{n}
; (c) the next state
{\bm{s}}_{n,h+1}\sim P^{\star}({\bm{s}}_{n,h},{\bm{a}}_{n,h})
is drawn from the transition kernel
P^{\star}
(unless
h=H
). The agent is therefore defined by the sequence of policies
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}
, which we will also refer to as “the algorithm”. The interaction between the agent and the MDP produces a random sequence of outcomes
{\bm{s}}_{1,1},{\bm{a}}_{1,1},\dots,{\bm{s}}_{N,H},{\bm{a}}_{N,H}
.

The goal of the agent is to minimize the regret, which is the loss in reward caused by playing the polices
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}
instead of the optimal policy. We define the regret after
T
steps as

{\mathcal{R}}_{T}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{N}V_{\pi^{\star},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})-V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})\right]\,.

The expectation is taken with respect to all sources of randomness, including the random draw of
{\mathcal{M}}_{\star}
at the start of the interaction and any randomness involved in the agent’s selection of the policies
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}
. This notition of regret is usually referred to as the Bayesian regret.

Posterior Sampling Reinforcement Learning

from the Bayesian posterior distribution of

{\mathcal{F}}_{n-1}

to be the MDP sampled by PSRL at the beginning of episode

. PSRL then plays any policy
\pi_{n}
which is an optimal policy for the sampled MDP

. For the model we consider with a conjugate Gaussian process prior, we refer to PSRL as GP-PSRL.

As discussed in Section

f^{\star}_{i}
of

4
Regret Analysis

We state and sketch the proof of a Bayesian regret bound for GP-PSRL. The method of proof can be split into two parts. In the first part, we show that with high probability, all the states observed by the algorithm are contained within a Euclidean ball with a radius that grows only logarithmically with
T
. In the second part, under the event that all the observed states are bounded, we prove an upper bound for the Bayesian regret of GP-PSRL.

The general idea behind the first part of the proof is illustrated in Figure
1
. We exploit the fact that as long as the norm of the current state is bounded, then the norm of the next state has sub-Gaussian tail behavior, which means it can be bounded with high probability. In particular, since
{\bm{s}}_{n,h+1}
can be written as

{\bm{s}}_{n,h+1}=f^{\star}({\bm{s}}_{n,h},{\bm{a}}_{n,h})+\bm{\epsilon}_{n,h+1}\,,

the norm of
{\bm{s}}_{n,h+1}
is sub-Gaussian as long as the norm of
f^{\star}({\bm{s}}_{n,h},{\bm{a}}_{n,h})
is sub-Gaussian. If the norm of
{\bm{s}}_{n,h}
is bounded, then for some finite radius
R
, the norm of
f^{\star}({\bm{s}}_{n,h},{\bm{a}}_{n,h})
can be upper bounded by the supremum
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{s}}(R)}\|f^{\star}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}
. To show that this supremum is sub-Gaussian, we use a version of the Borell-Tsirelson-Ibragimov-Sudakov (BTIS) inequality
(
9
;
36
)
, which states that the supremum of a Gaussian process is sub-Gaussian (cf.  Lemma
4.1
). Within each episode, the bounds on the norms of the states will increase as the step
h
increases. It remains to show that this increase is not too fast (cf.  Section
4.3
).

The first state in each episode is drawn from an isotropic Gaussian, and so its norm is sub-Gaussian. As long as the norm of the current state is bounded, the next state is sub-Gaussian, and so its norm is also sub-Gaussian. The bound on the norm of the state at step
h
will grow with
h
. The challenge is to show that this bound does not grow too quickly.

The second part of the proof can itself be split into two parts. We show that the regret of PSRL can be upper bounded by a sum of model estimation errors (cf.  Section
4.5
) and then we upper bound the model estimation errors (cf.  Section
4.6
). The main technical novelty in this part of the proof is in how we upper bound the estimation errors under weak smoothness assumptions on the kernel.

4.1
Suprema of Gaussian Processes

We describe the tools that we use to control suprema of Gaussian processes. First, we focus on the supremum
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}f({\bm{x}})
, where
{\mathcal{Z}}
is some subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
and
f\sim{\mathcal{G}}{\mathcal{P}}(0,c({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}))
. For any (sufficiently large) threshold
u
, we would like to have an upper bound for the tail probability
{\mathbb{P}}(\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}f({\bm{x}})>u)
.
We use the BTIS inequality to obtain such a tail bound (Lemma
4.1
).

Lemma 4.1

(Borell-Tsirelson-Ibragimov-Sudakov)
.

Let
{\mathcal{Z}}
be a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
, let
f\sim{\mathcal{G}}{\mathcal{P}}(0,c({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}))
and suppose that
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}f({\bm{x}})]<\infty
. Let
\sigma^{2}:=\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\mathbb{E}[f({\bm{x}})^{2}]
. For every
u>0
,

{\mathbb{P}}\bigg(\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}f({\bm{x}})-\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}f({\bm{x}})\bigg]\geq u\bigg)\leq e^{-\frac{u^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\,.

The BTIS inequality states that the supremum of a Gaussian process is a sub-Gaussian random variable, and that the sub-Gaussian parameter is at most the maximum variance of the GP. If the kernel satisfies Assumption
3.3
, then
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\mathbb{E}[f({\bm{x}})^{2}]\leq C
. For
u
much larger than
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}f({\bm{x}})]
, we therefore have the estimate

{\mathbb{P}}\bigg(\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}f({\bm{x}})\geq u\bigg)\sim e^{-\frac{u^{2}}{2C}}\,.

(4)

To determine how large
u
needs to be for this to be a reasonable estimate of the tail probability, we need to upper bound the expected supremum of
f
. To do so, we can use the chaining method
(
14
)
.

Lemma 4.2

(Dudley)
.

Let
{\mathcal{Z}}
be a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
. If
f\sim{\mathcal{G}}{\mathcal{P}}(0,c({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}))
, then

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}f({\bm{x}})\bigg]\leq 12\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log\mathsf{N}({\mathcal{Z}},d_{c},\epsilon)}\mathrm{d}\epsilon\,.

We recall that
\mathsf{N}({\mathcal{Z}},d_{c},\epsilon)
is the
\epsilon
-covering number of
{\mathcal{Z}}
with respect to the natural distance
d_{c}
. The quantity
\log\mathsf{N}({\mathcal{Z}},d_{c},\epsilon)
is known as the metric entropy of
{\mathcal{Z}}
(w.r.t.
d_{c}
), and consequently, the integral in Lemma
4.2
is sometimes called the entropy integral. Loosely speaking, the chaining argument tells us that as long as
f
is “sufficiently continuous” then the expected supremum of
f
is controlled by the size of
{\mathcal{Z}}
, as measured by the natural distance
d_{c}
.
For any metric
d
, let us define the diameter of
{\mathcal{Z}}
as

\mathrm{diam}_{d}({\mathcal{Z}}):=\sup_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}d({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\,.

Notice that for any
\epsilon\geq\mathrm{diam}_{d_{c}}({\mathcal{Z}})
, any point in
{\mathcal{Z}}
forms an
\epsilon
-covering of
{\mathcal{Z}}
w.r.t.
d_{c}
. This means that the upper limit of the entropy integral can be replaced by
\mathrm{diam}_{d_{c}}({\mathcal{Z}})
. Therefore, explicit upper bounds for the expected supremum can be obtained by plugging in upper bounds for the covering number and the diameter of
{\mathcal{Z}}
with respect to
d_{c}
.

4.2
Suprema of Vector-Valued Gaussian Processes

We turn our attention to suprema of vector-valued Gaussian processes. In particular, let
f_{1},\dots,f_{d_{s}}\sim{\mathcal{G}}{\mathcal{P}}(0,c({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}))
be independent GPs and let
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{d_{s}})
. For any radius
R>0
, we would like to have an upper bound for the tail probability
{\mathbb{P}}(\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}>u)
. Thanks to the following Gaussian concentration inequality (Lemma
4.3
), the supremum of
\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}
can be dealt with using the tools described in Section
4.1
.

Lemma 4.3

(Theorem 2.26 in
39
)
.

Let
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
be a vector of i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables, and let
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be
L
-Lipschitz with respect to the Euclidean metric. Then the random variable
g(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
is
L
-sub-Gaussian.

Since the Euclidean norm is 1-Lipschitz w.r.t. the Euclidean metric, Lemma
4.3
tells us that for any
{\bm{x}}
, the random variable
\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}
exhibits concentration similar to each of the components
f_{i}({\bm{x}})
. It therefore does not seem unreasonable to expect that a version of the BTIS inequality should hold for
\sup_{{\bm{x}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}
.
Lemma
4.4
shows that this is true.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose that Assumption
3.3
is satisfied. Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{d_{s}}\sim{\mathcal{G}}{\mathcal{P}}(0,c({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}))
be independent GPs and let
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{d_{s}})
. For any
R>0
, let
{\mathcal{Z}}={\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)
. For any
u>0
,

{\mathbb{P}}\bigg(\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\bigg]\geq u\bigg)\leq e^{-\frac{u^{2}}{2C}}\,.

The proof of Lemma
4.4
can be found in Appendix
B.2
, and is inspired by the proof of the standard BTIS inequality from
38
(cf. Lemma 6.12). The idea is to establish that for any finite subset
{\mathcal{Z}}\subset{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)
, the supremum
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}
is equal in distribution to a
\sqrt{C}
-Lipschitz function of a finite-dimensional standard Gaussian random vector. By Lemma
4.3
, this means that the supremum is
\sqrt{C}
-sub-Gaussian. One can then use separability (cf. Definition
A.1
) to show that this holds even when
{\mathcal{Z}}={\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)
. As one might expect, the chaining argument can be applied to
\sup_{{\bm{x}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}
as well.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{d_{s}}\sim{\mathcal{G}}{\mathcal{P}}(0,c({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}))
be independent Gaussian processes and let
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{d_{s}})
. For any
R>0
, let
{\mathcal{Z}}={\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)
. If Assumption
3.3
is satisfied, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 12\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log\mathsf{N}({\mathcal{Z}},d_{c},\epsilon)}\mathrm{d}\epsilon

\displaystyle\quad+(12+\sqrt{d_{s}})\sqrt{C}\,.

The proof of Lemma
4.5
can be found in Appendix
B.1
, and is inspired by the proof of the chaining argument from
38
(cf. Theorem 5.24). Compared to the bound in Lemma
4.2
, the bound in Lemma
4.5
has an extra term. This is due to the fact that
\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}
is not zero-mean. In Appendix
B.1
, we derive bounds on the covering number and diameter of
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)
w.r.t.
d_{c}
, where
c
is any kernel that satisfies our smoothness and boundedness assumptions. As described in Section
4.1
, these bounds can be combined with Lemma
4.5
to obtain an explicit bound on the supremum (cf. Lemma
B.5
).
Lemma
4.6
shows that when
u
is at least twice as big as this upper bound, the tail probability
{\mathbb{P}}(\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}>u)
satisfies a bound similar to the one in (
4
).

Lemma 4.6
.

Suppose that Assumption
3.3
and Assumption
3.4
are satisfied. Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{d_{s}}\sim{\mathcal{G}}{\mathcal{P}}(0,c({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}))
be independent GPs and let
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{d_{s}})
. For any
R>0
and any
u
such that

u\geq 84\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{C(d_{s}+d_{a})\log(5+5R^{\alpha}L/C)}\,,

we have

{\mathbb{P}}\bigg(\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\geq u\bigg)\leq e^{-\frac{u^{2}}{8C}}\,.

The proof of Lemma
4.6
can be found in Appendix
B.3
.

4.3
Tail Bound for the Norm of the Largest State

We can now show that the states observed by running the algorithm remain close to the origin. We begin by formalizing the argument described at the beginning of this section. Let
R_{1},\dots,R_{H}
be a sequence of radii and let us define
A_{n,h}
to be the event that the state observed at step
h
of episode
n
has norm less than
R_{h}
, i.e.,

A_{n,h}:=\{\|{\bm{s}}_{n,h}\|_{2}\leq R_{h}\}\,.

In Appendix
C.1
(see also the proof of Lemma
C.4
), we prove that

{\mathbb{I}}\{\cup_{n=1}^{N}\cup_{h=1}^{H}A_{n,h}^{\mathsf{c}}\}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H}{\mathbb{I}}\{A_{n,h}^{\mathsf{c}}\}{\mathbb{I}}\{A_{n,h-1}\}\,.

From the definition of
A_{n,h}
, this means that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\cup_{n=1}^{N}\cup_{h=1}^{H}\{\|{\bm{s}}_{n,h}\|_{2}>R_{h}\})

(5)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}[{\mathbb{I}}\{\|{\bm{s}}_{n,h}\|_{2}>R_{h}\}{\mathbb{I}}\{\|{\bm{s}}_{n,h-1}\|_{2}\leq R_{h-1}\}]\,.

This inequality tells us that if for all
n\in[N]
and
h\in[H]
we can control the probability that
\|{\bm{s}}_{n,h}\|_{2}
exceeds
R_{h}
, given that the
\|{\bm{s}}_{n,h-1}\|_{2}\leq R_{h-1}
, then this automatically controls the probability that the norm of any state, say
{\bm{s}}_{n,h}
, exceeds the value
R_{h}
. It remains to determine how large
R_{1},\dots,R_{H}
need to be to ensure that the right-hand side of (
5
) is of the order
1/T
. In Appendix
C.2
, we use the tail bound in Lemma
4.6
to upper bound each term on the right-hand side of (
5
). We then determine specific values of
R_{1},\dots,R_{H}
that ensure that this bound is at most
2/T
. Finally, we prove by induction that if
T
is sufficiently large, then these values of
R_{1},\dots,R_{H}
satisfy
R_{h}\leq R
for all
h\in[H]
, where
R
is given in (
6
). Since

{\mathbb{P}}\big({\textstyle\sup_{n\in[N],h\in[H]}}\|{\bm{s}}_{n,h}\|_{2}>R\big)\leq{\mathbb{P}}\big(\cup_{n=1}^{N}\cup_{h=1}^{H}A_{n,h}^{\mathsf{c}}\big)\,,

this leads to the following result.

Lemma 4.7
.

Suppose that Assumption
3.1
, Assumption
3.3
and Assumption
3.4
are satisfied. Let us define

D:=168\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{\max(C,\sigma^{2})(d_{s}+d_{a})}\,,

and

R:=D\sqrt{\log(10(T+R_{a})\max(1,L/C))}\,.

(6)

If the total number of rounds
T
satisfies

T\geq D\sqrt{2\log(10D\max(1,L/C)(R_{a}+1))}\,,

(7)

then

{\mathbb{P}}\big({\textstyle\sup_{n\in[N],h\in[H]}}\|{\bm{s}}_{n,h}\|_{2}>R\big)\leq\frac{2}{T}\,.

The proof can be found in Appendix
C.3
. Roughly speaking, whenever
T
is larger than
\sqrt{d_{s}+d_{a}}
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}\big({\textstyle\sup_{n\in[N],h\in[H]}}\|{\bm{s}}_{n,h}\|_{2}\geq\sqrt{(d_{s}+d_{a})\log(T)}\big)\sim\frac{1}{T}\,.

Since
\|{\bm{x}}_{n,h}\|_{2}^{2}=\|{\bm{s}}_{n,h}\|_{2}^{2}+\|{\bm{a}}_{n,h}\|_{2}^{2}
, Lemma
4.7
also gives us a bound on the norm of the largest state-action pair. In particular, let us define

\widetilde{R}:=\sqrt{R^{2}+R_{a}^{2}}\,,

(8)

where
R
is given in (
6
). Then

{\mathbb{P}}\big({\textstyle\sup_{n\in[N],h\in[H]}}\|{\bm{x}}_{n,h}\|_{2}>\widetilde{R}\big)\leq\frac{2}{T}\,.

4.4
Bounding Regret by Value Estimation Error

We turn to the second part of the proof of our regret bound. The first step is to re-write the Bayesian regret as a sum of value estimation errors
V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})-V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}^{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})
. The
n
th
term in the sum is the difference between the value of the policy
\pi_{n}
in the sampled MDP
{\mathcal{M}}_{n}
and the true MDP
{\mathcal{M}}_{\star}
. The following lemma is a re-statement of Lemma 1 in
24
.

Lemma 4.8
.

If
\pi_{n}
is an optimal policy for
{\mathcal{M}}_{n}
, then

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{T}=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{N}V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})-V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}^{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})\right]\,.

The reason that the regret can be re-written in this way is that given any history
{\mathcal{F}}_{n-1}
,
f^{\star}
and
f^{(n)}
have the same conditional distribution.

4.5
Bounding Regret by Model Estimation Error

Let us now define the good event
A
as

A:=\{{\textstyle\sup_{n\in[N],h\in[H]}}\|{\bm{s}}_{n,h}\|_{2}\leq R\}\,.

Having established that the bad event
A^{\mathsf{c}}
occurs with low probability, we conduct the rest of the regret analysis under the good event, which ensures that
\sup_{n\in[N],h\in[H]}\|{\bm{x}}_{n,h}\|_{2}\leq\widetilde{R}
(cf. (
8
)). The next step is a well-established idea in the analysis of model-based reinforcement learning algorithms. We show that the sum of the value estimation errors can be controlled by the sum of the model estimation errors. Results of this type are sometimes referred to as simulation lemmas
(
22
)
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Suppose that Assumption
3.2
is satisfied. For any event
A
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big(V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})-V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})\big)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{HR_{\max}}{\sigma}\mathbb{E}\bigg[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\|f^{(n)}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})-f^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})\|_{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad+2R_{\max}H\sqrt{2\pi T}\,.

Lemma
4.9
is more or less a combination of two known results. The first is a result from Section 5.1 of
24
, which expresses the sum of the value estimation errors as a sum of Bellman errors. In Appendix
D
, we prove a slight extension of a result (cf.  Lemma
D.1
). The main difference between is that both the value estimation errors and the Bellman errors are multiplied by an indicator variable. The second half of Lemma
4.9
is essentially the same as Lemma 1 in
15
, and allows us to upper bound the sum of the Bellman errors by the sum of the model estimation errors. In Appendix
D
we give a simpler (but less general) proof of this result using Pinsker’s inequality (cf.  Lemma
D.2
).

4.6
Bounding the Model Estimation Error

The final step is to upper bound the sum of the estimation errors. One way to do this is to construct confidence sets that contain both
f^{\star}
and
f^{(n)}
with high probability. The sum of the estimation errors can then be upper bounded by the sum of the widths of these confidence sets. We believe that this idea was first used in the analysis of PSRL by
24
(see also
25
;
26
). The problem with this approach, at least if one is interested in GP-PSRL, is that it tends to give bounds on the model estimation error that have linear dependence on
\gamma_{T}
(or related quantities such as the Eluder dimension
29
), which would lead to a final regret bound with a sub-optimal dependence on
\gamma_{T}
.

A second way to upper bound the sum of the estimation errors is to exploit the fact that, conditioned on any history
{\mathcal{F}}_{n-1}
,
f^{(n)}
and
f^{\star}
are both Gaussian processes, and use bounds for suprema of Gaussian processes. This approach is commonly used in proofs of Bayesian regret bounds for Gaussian process bandits
(
31
)
. However, even in recent work (e.g. ,
34
;
33
), it is typically assumed that the kernel satisfies much stronger smoothness conditions than the Hölder continuity condition in Assumption
3.4
. Namely,
31
and
34
;
33
assume that samples from the GP are differentiable, and that the partial derivatives are uniformly bounded with high probability. This is satisfied for stationary kernels which are four times differentiable
(
17
;
31
)
, which rules out Matérn kernels with
\nu\leq 2
. We adopt this approach, and show that under much weaker smoothness conditions, it can be used to derive bounds on the estimation error that have square root dependence on the maximum information gain.

Figure 3
:
A log-log plot of cumulative regret against steps.
The dotted line shows the best fit of a 1/2 slope to verify our proposed
\sqrt{T}
rate for the squared exponential kernel, and slopes of 9/10, 11/14 and 13/18 for the Matérn 1/2, 3/2 and 5/2 kernels respectively, following the specialized rates in Section
4.8
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Suppose that assumptions
3.1
,
3.3
and
3.4
are satisfied. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\|f^{(n)}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})-f^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})\|_{2}\right]=

\displaystyle\hskip 60.00009pt{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{(d_{s}+d_{a})H\gamma_{N}(\sigma^{2},\widetilde{R})T\log(T)}\right)\,.

The proof of Lemma
4.10
is in Appendix
E.3
. The general idea is to separate the estimation error into a discretized estimation error and two discretization error terms via a single-step discretization. For some
\epsilon\in(0,\widetilde{R}]
, let
B_{\epsilon}
be a minimal
\epsilon
-cover of
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})
w.r.t.
d_{2}
and let
\omega:{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})\to B_{\epsilon}
be any function that maps points in
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})
to the closest point in
B_{\epsilon}
. The discretized estimation error can be upper bounded using an exponential moment inequality for
\chi^{2}
random variables (cf.  Lemma
E.1
) and an elliptical potential lemma (cf.  Lemma
E.3
). Each of the discretization error terms is at most

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\|f({\bm{x}})-f(\omega({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}\right]\,,

(9)

where
f_{1},\dots,f_{d_{s}}\sim{\mathcal{G}}{\mathcal{P}}(0,c({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}))
are independent GPs and
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{d_{s}})
. The challenge is to show that if
\epsilon
is sufficiently small, then (under weak smoothness conditions) the discretization error is negligible. We notice that each component of
\widetilde{f}({\bm{x}}):=f({\bm{x}})-f(\omega({\bm{x}}))
is another Gaussian process with the kernel

\displaystyle\widetilde{c}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})

\displaystyle:=c(\omega({\bm{x}}),\omega({\bm{y}}))-c(\omega({\bm{x}}),{\bm{y}})

\displaystyle\quad\;\;-c({\bm{x}},\omega({\bm{y}}))+c({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\,.

While this kernel function is not continuous, it is still piecewise Hölder continuous. This allows us to suitably upper bound the covering number of
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})
w.r.t. the distance
d_{\widetilde{c}}
(cf.  Lemma
E.7
). It can also be shown that the diameter of
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})
w.r.t.
d_{\widetilde{c}}
can be made arbitrarily small by decreasing
\epsilon
(cf.  Lemma
E.8
). Therefore, when we use the chaining argument to upper bound the supremum in (
9
), the resulting entropy integral can be made arbitrarily small as well (cf.  Lemma
E.12
). The proof of Lemma
4.10
then boils down to choosing a suitable value of
\epsilon
.

4.7
Main Result

We can now state our main result.

Theorem 4.11
.

Suppose that assumptions
3.1
-
3.4
are satisfied. Define
\widetilde{R}
as in
(
8
)
. For all
T
that satisfy
(
7
)
,

{\mathcal{R}}_{T}={\mathcal{O}}\left(H^{3/2}\sqrt{(d_{s}+d_{a})\gamma_{N}(\sigma^{2},\widetilde{R})T\log(T)}\right)\,.

The proof is in Appendix

, though it is mainly just a matter of combining all the steps described previously.

4.8
Specialization to Matérn Kernels

Using a bound on the maximum information gain from
37
, we can specialize our main result to Matérn kernels.

Lemma 4.12

(Lemma 2 in
37
)
.

For the Matérn kernel with parameter
\nu>0
,

\gamma_{T}(\sigma^{2},\widetilde{R})={\mathcal{O}}\left(T^{\frac{d_{s}+d_{a}}{2\nu+d_{s}+d_{a}}}\log^{\frac{2\nu}{2\nu+d_{s}+d_{a}}}(T)\widetilde{R}^{\frac{2\nu(d_{s}+d_{a})}{2\nu+d_{s}+d_{a}}}\right)\,.

Since in Theorem
4.11
,
\widetilde{R}={\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{(d_{s}+d_{a})\log(T)})
, we obtain the following regret bound for Matérn kernels.

Corollary 4.13
.

Suppose that
c
is the Matérn kernel with smoothness parameter
\nu
. Suppose that assumptions
3.1
-
3.2
are satisfied. Let
d:=d_{s}+d_{a}
. For all
T
that satisfy
(
7
)
,

{\mathcal{R}}_{T}={\mathcal{O}}\big(H^{\frac{3\nu+d}{2\nu+d}}d^{\frac{\nu+1}{2}}T^{\frac{\nu+d}{2\nu+d}}\log^{\max(\frac{\nu+1}{2},\frac{3\nu+1}{2\nu+2})}(T)\big)\,.

Up to logarithmic factors, this recovers the best known rate in
T
–even in the special case of Gaussian process bandits.

Experiments

We perform an empirical study of GP-PSRL to verify our regret analysis.
We consider a 2D navigation task with a 2 dimensional state space and 2 dimensional action space and known reward.
A stationary GP models the dynamics under Euler integration, i.e.
{{\bm{s}}_{h+1}={\bm{s}}_{h}+\Delta\cdot f({\bm{s}}_{h},{\bm{a}}_{h})}
for integration timestep
\Delta
.
For tractable posterior sampling, we implemented a sparse Gaussian process approximation with random features
(
28
)
.
To compute approximate global policies and value functions to compare returns, we discretized the state-action space and performed value iteration as the optimal control oracle.

shows Bayesian cumulative regret over 100 episodes with planning horizon of 20.
Within this budget, smoother GP priors exhibit greater sample efficiency due to their lower maximum information gain.

validates the rates of our regret bounds with a log-log plot.

contains empirical rates for
{\mathcal{R}}_{T}
vs
H
and
\gamma_{T}
.

6
Discussion

We have established the first regret bound for GP-based reinforcement learning that simultaneously achieves a near-optimal rate in
T
and accommodates both unbounded state spaces and weak smoothness assumptions. We provide some further discussion about our work below.

Implications for GP bandits.
Our upper bound on the sum of the model estimation errors is inspired by techniques that are commonly used to analyze GP bandit algorithms. However, as we mentioned in Section
4.6
, we’re able to remove the usual assumption that, with high probability, sample paths from the prior have uniformly bounded partial derivatives. We believe that our proof of Lemma
4.10
is applicable to GP bandits, and that it enables one to prove Bayesian regret bounds for GP bandits under weaker smoothness conditions than was previously thought possible (see e.g. , the discussion following Theorem 2 in
31
).

Locally bounded kernel functions.
The assumption that the kernel function is uniformly bounded rules out some commonly used kernels, such as the linear kernel, which are only locally bounded. By locally bounded, we mean that there exists a function
C:(0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that for all
R>0
, the kernel function is bounded by
C(R)
on a ball of radius
R
. While our analysis does not break completely when the uniform boundedness assumption is relaxed to local boundedness, our tail bound for the norm of the largest state in Lemma
4.7
would become much worse. In particular, the value of
R
in (
6
) may pick up exponential dependence on
H
. To obtain a result comparable to Lemma
4.7
with locally bounded kernels, we expect that it is necessary to restrict the algorithm to play stabilizing policies.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Machine
Learning. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, none
which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.

Appendix A
Additional Definitions

A.1
Separable Gaussian Processes

For the chaining arguments to go through, we need to make a technical assumption, which is that the Gaussian processes we work with are separable. Fortunately, all the Gaussian processes that we consider are separable, so this is not a problem. Any random process is separable if it satisfied the property in the definition below.

Definition A.1
.

A random process
(f({\bm{x}}))_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}
is called separable if there is a countable set
{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}\subseteq{\mathcal{Z}}
such that

f({\bm{x}})\in\lim_{{\bm{z}}\to{\bm{x}},{\bm{z}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}f({\bm{z}})~~\text{for all}~{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}~\text{a.s.}\,.

A consequence of
f
being separable is that there exists a countable subset
{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}\subseteq{\mathcal{Z}}
such that
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}f({\bm{x}})=\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}f({\bm{x}})
almost surely.

A.2
Covering Numbers and Proper Covering Numbers

In several places, we will make use of
\epsilon
-covers of subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
. In general, it is preferable that we do not restrict the elements of a cover of some set to themselves be elements of the set being covered. On one occation however, it will be important that the elements of the cover of some set are also elements of the set being covered. We therefore distinguish between two types of covers.

Definition A.2

(
\epsilon
-covering and covering number)
.

Consider the metric space
({\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}},d)
. A set
M\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
is an
\epsilon
-cover for
{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
(w.r.t. the metric
d
) if for all
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
, there exists
{\bm{y}}\in M
such that
d({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\leq\epsilon
. The
\epsilon
-covering number
\mathsf{N}({\mathcal{Z}},d,\epsilon)
of
{\mathcal{Z}}
w.r.t. the metric
d
is the cardinality of the smallest
\epsilon
-cover for
{\mathcal{Z}}
.

Note that an
\epsilon
-cover of
{\mathcal{Z}}
is not required to be a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
. This has the advantage that the
\epsilon
-covering number satisfies a certain monotonicity property. In particular, for two sets
{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
, and any metric
d
, any
\epsilon
-cover for
{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
is also an
\epsilon
-cover for
{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}
, which means

\mathsf{N}({\mathcal{Z}}_{1},d,\epsilon)\leq\mathsf{N}({\mathcal{Z}}_{2},d,\epsilon)\,.

Next, we define proper
\epsilon
-covers and proper covering numbers.

Definition A.3

(Proper
\epsilon
-covering and proper covering number)
.

Consider the metric space
({\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}},d)
and a subset
{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
. A set
M\subseteq{\mathcal{Z}}
is a proper
\epsilon
-cover for
{\mathcal{Z}}
if for all
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
, there exists
{\bm{y}}\in M
such that
d({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\leq\epsilon
. The proper
\epsilon
-covering number
\mathsf{N}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathcal{Z}},d,\epsilon)
of
{\mathcal{Z}}
w.r.t. the metric
d
is the cardinality of the smallest proper
\epsilon
-cover for
{\mathcal{Z}}
.

The proper
\epsilon
-covering number does not satisfy the same monotonicity property. For example,
\mathsf{N}_{\mathrm{pr}}([0,1],d_{2},1/2)=1
, whereas
\mathsf{N}_{\mathrm{pr}}([0,1/2)\cup(1/2,1],d_{2},1/2)=2
.

Appendix B
Tail Bounds for Suprema of Gaussian Processes

We prove the results given in Section
4.2
. In Appendix
B.1
, we prove Lemma
4.5
and we use bounds on the covering number and diameter of
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)
w.r.t
d_{c}
to obtain an explicit upper bound for the expected supremum. In Appendix
B.2
, we prove Lemma
4.4
. Finally, in Appendix
B.3
, we prove Lemma
4.6
.

B.1
Expected Suprema of Gaussian Processes

To apply the chaining method to GPs with kernels that satisfy our smoothness and boundedness assumptions, we need upper bounds on both the covering numbers and the diameter (w.r.t
d_{c}
) of a Euclidean ball with a given radius. We will use the following bound on the covering number of a Euclidean ball (not necessarily centered at the origin) with respect to the Euclidean metric.

Lemma B.1

(Lemma 5.7 in
39
)
.

For any
R>0
,
\epsilon>0
and
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
,

\mathsf{N}({\mathbb{B}}_{{\bm{x}}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R),d_{2},\epsilon)\leq\bigg(1+\frac{2R}{\epsilon}\bigg)^{d_{s}+d_{a}}\,.

If,
\epsilon\leq R
, then this upper bound can be simplified slightly to

\mathsf{N}({\mathbb{B}}_{{\bm{x}}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R),d_{2},\epsilon)\leq\bigg(\frac{3R}{\epsilon}\bigg)^{d_{s}+d_{a}}\,.

For any kernel that satisfies Assumption
3.4
, we can turn this into an upper bound for the covering number of a Euclidean ball with respect to the natural distance associated with the kernel.

Lemma B.2
.

Suppose that a kernel
c
satisfies Assumption
3.4
, and let
d_{c}
denote the natural distance associated with
c
. Then for every
R\geq 0
,

\mathsf{N}({\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R),d_{c},\epsilon)\leq\bigg(1+\frac{2R(2L)^{1/\alpha}}{\epsilon^{2/\alpha}}\bigg)^{d_{s}+d_{a}}\,.

Proof.

Lemma
B.1
tells us that

\mathsf{N}({\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R),d_{2},\epsilon)\leq\bigg(1+\frac{2R}{\epsilon}\bigg)^{d_{s}+d_{a}}\,.

Thus for any
\epsilon_{0}>0
, we can construct an
\epsilon_{0}
-cover
M
of
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)
in the metric
d_{2}
, such that
|M|\leq(1+\frac{2R}{\epsilon_{0}})^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
. By Assumption
3.4
, we have

\displaystyle d_{c}^{2}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})

\displaystyle=c({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}})-2c({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})+c({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}})

\displaystyle\leq|c({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}})-c({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})|+|c({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}})-c({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}})|

\displaystyle\leq 2L\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|_{2}^{\alpha}\,.

In particular,
d_{c}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\leq\sqrt{2Ld_{2}^{\alpha}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}
. Therefore, if we choose
\epsilon_{0}=(\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2L})^{1/\alpha}
, then
M
is an
\epsilon
-covering of
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)
in the metric
d_{c}
.
∎

Recall that for any subset
{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
and any metric
d
, the diameter of
{\mathcal{Z}}
is
\mathrm{diam}_{d}({\mathcal{Z}}):=\sup_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}d({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
. For any kernel that satisfies Assumption
3.3
we can upper bound the diameter of
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)
w.r.t. the natural distance
d_{c}
.

Lemma B.3
.

Suppose that a kernel
c:{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies Assumption
3.3
, and let
d_{c}
denote the natural distance associated with
c
. Then for every
R\geq 0
,

\mathrm{diam}_{d_{c}}({\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R))\leq 2\sqrt{C}\,.

Proof.

By Assumption
3.3
, we obtain

d_{c}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\sqrt{c({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}})-2c({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})+c({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}})}\leq\sqrt{4C}\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

The last tool we need before we can apply the chaining method is a concentration inequality for
\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}
. Since the Euclidean norm is 1-Lipschitz w.r.t. the Euclidean metric, we can use Lemma
4.3
to show that
\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}
is sub-Gaussian.

Corollary B.4
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{d_{s}}\sim{\mathcal{G}}{\mathcal{P}}(0,c({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}))
be independent, centered Gaussian processes and let
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{d_{s}})
. For any
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
,
\|f({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{y}})\|_{2}
is
d_{c}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
-sub-Gaussian. In particular, for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\big(\lambda\big(\|f({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{y}})\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}[\|f({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{y}})\|_{2}]\big)\big)\right]\leq\exp\bigg(\frac{\lambda^{2}d_{c}^{2}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{2}\bigg)\,.

(10)

Proof.

Let
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be the function
g({\bm{x}}):=\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}
. The reverse triangle inequality states that, for all
{\bm{v}},{\bm{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}}

g({\bm{v}})-g({\bm{u}})=\|{\bm{v}}\|_{2}-\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2}\leq\|{\bm{v}}-{\bm{u}}\|_{2}\,.

It follows that
g
is 1-Lipschitz w.r.t. the Euclidean metric. If
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
are such that
\|f({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{y}})\|_{2}
is identically equal to 0, then (
10
) is trivially satisfied. Suppose that
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
are such that
\|f({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{y}})\|_{2}
is not identically 0. In this case, one can verify that

f({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{y}})\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,d_{c}^{2}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}){\bm{I}})\,.

Thus
f({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{y}}))/d_{c}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
is a vector of i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables. By Lemma
4.3
, for all
\lambda_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\bigg(\frac{\lambda_{0}}{d_{c}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}\big(\|f({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{y}})\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}[\|f({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{y}})\|_{2}]\big)\bigg)\right]\leq\exp\bigg(\frac{\lambda_{0}^{2}}{2}\bigg)\,.

The claim follows by substituting
\lambda=\lambda_{0}/d_{c}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
.
∎

We can now prove the chaining argument in Lemma
4.5
.

Proof of Lemma
4.5
.

We first prove the result in the finite case, where
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)
is replaced by any finite non-empty subset
{\mathcal{Z}}\subset{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)
. We then use separability (cf.  Definition
A.1
) to remove this restriction.

By Lemma
B.3
, we know that
{\mathcal{Z}}
has finite diameter. Let
k_{0}
be the largest integer such that
2^{-k_{0}}\geq\mathrm{diam}_{d_{c}}({\mathcal{Z}})
. For
k\geq k_{0}
, let
M_{k}
be a
2^{-k}
-cover of
{\mathcal{Z}}
w.r.t.
d_{c}
such that
|M_{k}|=\mathsf{N}({\mathcal{Z}},d_{c},2^{-k})
. Also, for each
k\geq k_{0}
, define
\omega_{k}:{\mathcal{Z}}\to M_{k}
such that
d_{c}({\bm{x}},\omega_{k}({\bm{x}}))\leq 2^{-k}
for all
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
. Since
2^{-k_{0}}\geq\mathrm{diam}_{d_{c}}({\mathcal{Z}})
, we can take
M_{k_{0}}:=\{{\bm{x}}_{0}\}
, where
{\bm{x}}_{0}
is any point in
{\mathcal{Z}}
. Fix any
m\geq k_{0}
. By introducing a telescoping sum and then using the triangle inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}-\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f(\omega_{m}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\|f({\bm{x}}_{0})\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=k+0+1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f(\omega_{k}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}-\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f(\omega_{k-1}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\big\{\|f({\bm{x}})-f(\omega_{m}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}\big\}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\|f({\bm{x}}_{0})\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=k_{0}+1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\big\{\|f(\omega_{k}({\bm{x}}))-f(\omega_{k-1}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}\big\}\right]\,.

Since
{\mathcal{Z}}
is finite, we can choose
m
to be large enough such that
d_{c}({\bm{x}},\omega_{m}({\bm{x}}))=0
for all
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
, and so the first term disappears. Due to Assumption
3.3
, the second term satisfies the upper bound

\mathbb{E}\left[\|f({\bm{x}}_{0})\|_{2}\right]\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d_{s}}\mathbb{E}[(f_{i}({\bm{x}}_{0}))^{2}]}\leq\sqrt{d_{s}c({\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\leq\sqrt{Cd_{s}}\,.

All that remains is to control the sum. For all
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
,

d_{c}(\omega_{k}({\bm{x}}),\omega_{k-1}({\bm{x}}))\leq d_{c}({\bm{x}},\omega_{k}({\bm{x}}))+d_{c}({\bm{x}},\omega_{k-1}({\bm{x}}))\leq 3\cdot 2^{-k}\,.

By Corollary
B.4
, it follows that
\|f(\omega_{k}({\bm{x}}))-f(\omega_{k-1}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}
is
3\cdot 2^{-k}
-sub-Gaussian. We upper bound the
k
th
term by adding and subtracting
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\mathbb{E}[\|f(\omega_{k}({\bm{x}}))-f(\omega_{k-1}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}]
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\big\{\|f(\omega_{k}({\bm{x}}))-f(\omega_{k-1}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}\big\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\mathbb{E}[\|f(\omega_{k}({\bm{x}}))-f(\omega_{k-1}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\big\{\|f(\omega_{k}({\bm{x}}))-f(\omega_{k-1}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}[\|f(\omega_{k}({\bm{x}}))-f(\omega_{k-1}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}]\big\}\right]\,.

Since
\|f(\omega_{k}({\bm{x}}))-f(\omega_{k-1}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}
is
3\cdot 2^{-k}
-sub-Gaussian, the first term satisfies

\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\mathbb{E}[\|f(\omega_{k}({\bm{x}}))-f(\omega_{k-1}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}]\leq\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\big(\mathbb{E}[\|f(\omega_{k}({\bm{x}}))-f(\omega_{k-1}({\bm{x}}))\|_{2}^{2}]\big)^{1/2}\leq 3\cdot 2^{-k}\,.

The second term can be upper bounded by using Jensen’s inequality and the sub-Gaussian property. To save space, let us write

g({\bm{y}},{\bm{z}}):=\|f({\bm{y}})-f({\bm{z}})\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}[\|f({\bm{y}})-f({\bm{z}})\|_{2}]\big\}\,.

Also, let us define the set

M:=\{(\omega_{k}({\bm{x}}),\omega_{k-1}({\bm{x}})):{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}\}\subseteq M_{k}\times M_{k-1}\,.

The cardinality of
M
at most
|M_{k}||M_{k-1}|\leq|M_{k}|^{2}
. From our above discussion, for each
({\bm{y}},{\bm{z}})\in M
,
g({\bm{y}},{\bm{z}})
is centered and
3\cdot 2^{-k}
-sub-Gaussian. By Jensen’s inequality, for any
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}g(\omega_{k}({\bm{x}}),\omega_{k-1}({\bm{x}}))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\exp\bigg(\lambda\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}g(\omega_{k}({\bm{x}}),\omega_{k-1}({\bm{x}}))\bigg)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\exp\big(\lambda g(\omega_{k}({\bm{x}}),\omega_{k-1}({\bm{x}}))\big)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\sum_{({\bm{y}},{\bm{z}})\in M}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\big(\lambda g({\bm{y}},{\bm{z}})\big)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\lambda}\log|M_{k}|+\frac{\lambda(3\cdot 2^{-k})^{2}}{2}\,.

If we choose
\lambda=\frac{2\sqrt{\log|M_{k}|}}{3\cdot 2^{-k}}
, we get the inequality

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}g(\omega_{k}({\bm{x}}),\omega_{k-1}({\bm{x}}))\right]\leq 6\cdot 2^{-k}\sqrt{\log|M_{k}|}\,.

Combining everything so far, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\right]\leq\sqrt{Cd_{s}}+\sum_{k=k_{0}+1}^{m}\{6\cdot 2^{-k}\sqrt{\log\mathsf{N}({\mathcal{Z}},d_{c},2^{-k})}+3\cdot 2^{-k}\}\,.

We separate the sum, and upper bound each part separately. For any
D\in{\mathbb{R}}
and any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

2^{-k}D=2(2^{-k}-2^{-k-1})D=\int_{2^{-k-1}}^{2^{-k}}D\mathrm{d}\epsilon\,.

Therefore, the first part of the sum satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{k=k_{0}+1}^{m}6\cdot 2^{-k}\sqrt{\log\mathsf{N}({\mathcal{Z}},d_{c},2^{-k})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}6\cdot 2^{-k}\sqrt{\log\mathsf{N}({\mathcal{Z}},d_{c},2^{-k})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}12\int_{2^{-k-1}}^{2^{-k}}\sqrt{\log\mathsf{N}({\mathcal{Z}},d_{c},2^{-k})}\mathrm{d}\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}12\int_{2^{-k-1}}^{2^{-k}}\sqrt{\log\mathsf{N}({\mathcal{Z}},d_{c},\epsilon)}\mathrm{d}\epsilon

\displaystyle=12\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log\mathsf{N}({\mathcal{Z}},d_{c},\epsilon)}\mathrm{d}\epsilon\,.

The second part of the sum satisfies

\sum_{k=k_{0}+1}^{m}3\cdot 2^{-k}\leq\sum_{k=k_{0}+1}^{\infty}3\cdot 2^{-k}=3\cdot 2^{-k_{0}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-k}=3\cdot 2^{-k_{0}}\,.

By Lemma
B.3
and the fact that
k_{0}
is the largest integer such that
2^{-k_{0}}\geq\mathrm{diam}_{d_{c}}({\mathcal{Z}})
,

3\cdot 2^{-k_{0}}=6\cdot 2^{-k_{0}-1}\leq 6\cdot\mathrm{diam}_{d_{c}}({\mathcal{Z}})\leq 12\sqrt{C}\,.

Therefore, since the covering number is monotone w.r.t.
{\mathcal{Z}}
, for any finite subset
{\mathcal{Z}}\subset{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\right]\leq 12\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log\mathsf{N}({\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R),d_{c},\epsilon)}\mathrm{d}\epsilon+12\sqrt{C}+\sqrt{Cd_{s}}\,.

Now suppose that
{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)
does not necessarily have finite cardinality. By separability, there exists a countable subset
{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}\subseteq{\mathcal{Z}}
such that
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}=\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}
almost surely. Let
{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}^{(n)}
be the first
n
elements
{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}
. By the monotone convergence theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}^{(n)}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}^{(n)}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 12\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log\mathsf{N}({\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R),d_{c},\epsilon)}\mathrm{d}\epsilon+12\sqrt{C}+\sqrt{Cd_{s}}\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

We specialize this result to obtain the following bound on the expected supremum.

Lemma B.5
.

Suppose that Assumption
3.3
and Assumption
3.4
are satisfied. Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{d_{s}}\sim{\mathcal{G}}{\mathcal{P}}(0,c({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}))
be independent Gaussian processes and let
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{d_{s}})
. For any
R>0
,

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\bigg]\leq 42\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{C(d_{s}+d_{a})\log(5+5R^{\alpha}L/C)}\,.

Proof.

Due to Lemma
4.5
, we just need to bound the entropy integral
\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log\mathsf{N}({\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R),d_{c},\epsilon)}\mathrm{d}\epsilon
. Using the bounds on the covering numbers (cf.  Lemma
B.2
) and diameter (cf.  Lemma
B.3
) of
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)
w.r.t.
d_{c}
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log\mathsf{N}({\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R),d_{c},\epsilon)}\mathrm{d}\epsilon\leq\int_{0}^{2\sqrt{C}}\sqrt{(d_{s}+d_{a})\log(1+2R(2L)^{1/\alpha}/\epsilon^{2/\alpha})}\mathrm{d}\epsilon\,.

Using the substitution
\delta=\epsilon/(2\sqrt{C})
, we can re-write this integral as

\int_{0}^{2\sqrt{C}}\sqrt{(d_{s}+d_{a})\log(1+2R(2L)^{1/\alpha}/\epsilon^{2/\alpha})}\mathrm{d}\epsilon=2\sqrt{C}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{(d_{s}+d_{a})\log(1+2R(2L)^{1/\alpha}/(2\sqrt{C}\delta)^{2/\alpha})}\mathrm{d}\delta\,.

For all
\delta\in(0,1]
,
1\leq 1/\delta^{2/\alpha}
, which means

\sqrt{\log(1+2R(2L)^{1/\alpha}/(2\sqrt{C}\delta)^{2/\alpha})}\leq\sqrt{\log(1+2R(2L)^{1/\alpha}/(4C)^{1/\alpha})}+\sqrt{(2/\alpha)\log(1/\delta)}\,.

Using this inequality and the identity
\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\log(1/x)}\mathrm{d}x=\sqrt{\pi}/2
(see e.g. , 4.215 in
18
), we obtain

\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\log(1+2R(2L)^{1/\alpha}/(2\sqrt{C}\delta)^{2/\alpha})}\mathrm{d}\delta\leq\sqrt{\log(1+2R(2L)^{1/\alpha}/(4C)^{1/\alpha})}+\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{\pi/2}\,.

Combining everything so far, we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log\mathsf{N}({\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R),d_{c},\epsilon)}\mathrm{d}\epsilon\leq 2\sqrt{C(d_{s}+d_{a})}\Big(\sqrt{\log(1+2R(2L)^{1/\alpha}/(4C)^{1/\alpha})}+\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{\pi/2}\Big)\,.

(11)

This upper bound on the entropy integral can be replaced by a simpler but looser bound. Since
x^{1/\alpha}+y^{1/\alpha}\leq(x+y)^{1/\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
x,y\geq 0
, we have

1+\frac{2R(2L)^{1/\alpha}}{(4C)^{1/\alpha}}=1^{1/\alpha}+\bigg(\frac{(2R)^{\alpha}2L}{4C}\bigg)^{1/\alpha}\leq(1+R^{\alpha}L/C)^{1/\alpha}\,.

Using this inequality, and
\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}\leq\sqrt{2(a+b)}
for all
a,b\geq 0
, we can replace the bound in (
11
) by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log\mathsf{N}({\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R),d_{c},\epsilon)}\mathrm{d}\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq 2\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{C(d_{s}+d_{a})}\Big(\sqrt{\log(1+R^{\alpha}L/C)}+\sqrt{\pi/2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2}\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{C(d_{s}+d_{a})\log(\exp(\pi/2)(1+R^{\alpha}L/C))}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2}\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{C(d_{s}+d_{a})\log(5+5R^{\alpha}L/C)}\,,

where in the last step we used the inequality
\exp(\pi/2)\leq 5
. Plugging this bound on the entropy integral into Lemma
4.5
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\right]\leq 24\sqrt{2}\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{C(d_{s}+d_{a})\log(5+5R^{\alpha}L/C)}+12\sqrt{C}+\sqrt{Cd_{s}}\,.

The sum of the last two terms satisfies

\displaystyle 12\sqrt{C}+\sqrt{Cd_{s}}\leq(6\sqrt{2}+1)\sqrt{C(d_{s}+d_{a})}\leq\frac{6\sqrt{2}+1}{\sqrt{\log(5)}}\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{C(d_{s}+d_{a})\log(5+5R^{\alpha}L/C)}\,.

The result now follows from the inequality
24\sqrt{2}+(6\sqrt{2}+1)\log^{-1/2}(5)\leq 42
.
∎

B.2
Concentration of Suprema of Gaussian Processes

We prove Lemma
4.4
, which states that
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}
is a sub-Gaussian random variable. First, we state and prove a technical lemma.

Lemma B.6
.

For each
i\in[n]
, let
g_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a function which is
L
-Lipschitz w.r.t. the Euclidean norm. Then the function
\max(g_{1},\dots,g_{n}):{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
given by
\max(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})({\bm{x}}):=\max_{i\in[n]}g_{i}({\bm{x}})
is also
L
-Lipschitz w.r.t. the Euclidean norm.

Proof.

Fix
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
and let
i^{\star}:=\argmax_{i\in[n]}g_{i}({\bm{x}})
. Since
g_{i^{\star}}
is
L
-Lipschitz, we have

\max_{i\in[n]}g_{i}({\bm{x}})-\max_{i\in[n]}g_{i}({\bm{y}})\leq g_{i^{\star}}({\bm{x}})-g_{i^{\star}}({\bm{y}})\leq L\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|_{2}\,.

A similar argument shows that
-(\max_{i\in[n]}g_{i}({\bm{x}})-\max_{i\in[n]}g_{i}({\bm{y}}))\leq L\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|_{2}
.
∎

We can now prove Lemma
4.4
.

Proof of Lemma
4.4
.

We first prove the result in the finite case, where
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)
is replaced by any finite subset
{\mathcal{Z}}\subset{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)
. We then use separability to remove this assumption (cf. Definition
A.1
).

Let
{\mathcal{Z}}=\{{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n}\}\subset{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
and for all
i\in[d_{s}]
and
j\in[n]
, let
Z_{i,j}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
be a standard Gaussian random variable. We would like to show that
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}
is equal in distribution to a Lipschitz function of
Z_{1,1},\dots,Z_{d_{s},n}
. By Lemma
4.3
, we would then have that
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}
is sub-Gaussian. Since each component
f_{i}
of
f
is a zero-mean GP with covariance kernel
c
, we have
[f_{i}({\bm{x}}_{1}),\dots,f_{i}({\bm{x}}_{n})]^{\top}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,{\bm{C}})
, where the
(i,j)
th
entry of
{\bm{C}}
is
C_{i,j}=c({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{j})
. Since the components
f_{1},\dots,f_{d_{s}}
are independent, we therefore have

\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}({\bm{x}}_{1})\\
\vdots\\
f_{1}({\bm{x}}_{n})\\
\vdots\\
f_{d_{s}}({\bm{x}}_{1})\\
\vdots\\
f_{d_{s}}({\bm{x}}_{n})\end{bmatrix}\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\vdots\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{bmatrix}\,,\begin{bmatrix}C_{1,1}&\cdots&C_{1,n}&\cdots&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\-\ &\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
C_{n,1}&\cdots&C_{n,n}&\cdots&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\-\ &\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\-\ &\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&\cdots&C_{1,1}&\cdots&C_{1,n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\-\ &\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&\cdots&C_{n,1}&\cdots&C_{n,n}\end{bmatrix}\right)\,.

(12)

For each
i\in[d_{s}]
and
j\in[n]
, let us define the function
g_{i,j}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

g_{i,j}(z_{1,1},\dots,z_{d_{s},n}):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}C_{j,k}^{1/2}z_{i,k}\,,

where
C_{j,k}^{1/2}
is the
(j,k)
th
entry of
{\bm{C}}^{1/2}
, and
{\bm{C}}^{1/2}
is a symmetric square root of
{\bm{C}}
(so
{\bm{C}}^{1/2}{\bm{C}}^{1/2}={\bm{C}}
). In light of (
12
), we have
f_{i}({\bm{x}}_{j})\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny d}}}{=}}g_{i,j}(Z_{1,1},\dots,Z_{d_{s},n})
, where
\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny d}}}{=}}
means equality in distribution. For each
j\in[n]
, let us define
g_{j}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by
g_{j}(z_{1,1},\dots,z_{d_{s},n}):=(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{s}}g_{i,j}(z_{1,1},\dots,z_{d_{s},n}))^{1/2}
. We will show that
g_{j}
is
\sqrt{C}
-Lipschitz. We introduce the shorthand
{\bm{z}}:=[z_{1,1},\dots,z_{d_{s},n}]^{\top}
. Using the chain rule, we see that for any
{\bm{z}}\neq 0
,
l\in[d_{s}]
and
m\in[n]
, the partial derivative of
g_{j}
w.r.t.
z_{m,l}
at
{\bm{z}}
is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{g_{j}}}{\partial{z_{l,m}}}({\bm{z}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial{}}{\partial{z_{l,m}}}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{s}}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}C_{j,k}^{1/2}z_{i,k}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{\partial{}}{\partial{z_{l,m}}}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{s}}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}C_{j,k}^{1/2}z_{i,k}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{s}}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}C_{j,k}^{1/2}z_{i,k}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=C_{j,m}^{1/2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}C_{j,k}^{1/2}z_{l,k}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{s}}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}C_{j,k}^{1/2}z_{i,k}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)^{-1/2}\,.

Therefore, for any
{\bm{z}}\neq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla g_{j}({\bm{z}})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{l=1}^{d_{s}}\sum_{m=1}^{n}C_{j,m}^{1/2}C_{j,m}^{1/2}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}C_{j,k}^{1/2}z_{l,k}\bigg)^{2}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{s}}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}C_{j,k}^{1/2}z_{i,k}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)^{-1}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{m=1}^{n}C_{j,m}^{1/2}C_{j,m}^{1/2}\sum_{l=1}^{d_{s}}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}C_{j,k}^{1/2}z_{l,k}\bigg)^{2}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{s}}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}C_{j,k}^{1/2}z_{i,k}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)^{-1}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{m=1}^{n}C_{j,m}^{1/2}C_{j,m}^{1/2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{C_{j,j}}\,,

where the identity
\sum_{m=1}^{n}C_{j,m}^{1/2}C_{j,m}^{1/2}=C_{j,j}
follows from
{\bm{C}}^{1/2}{\bm{C}}^{1/2}={\bm{C}}
. Since (by Assumption
3.3
)
C_{j,j}\leq C
, we have
\|\nabla g_{j}({\bm{z}})\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{C}
for all
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}n}\setminus\{0\}
. Thus we can conclude that
g_{j}
is
\sqrt{C}
-Lipschitz on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}n}
. Let us now define the function
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by
g({\bm{z}})=\max_{j\in[n]}g_{j}({\bm{z}})
. By Lemma
B.6
,
g
is also
\sqrt{C}
-Lipschitz on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}n}
. Together with Lemma
4.3
, this tells us that the random variable
g(Z_{1,1},\dots,Z_{d_{s},n})
is
\sqrt{C}
-sub-Gaussian. Since

\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny d}}}{=}}\sup_{j\in[n]}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d_{s}}g_{i,j}^{2}(Z_{1,1},\dots,Z_{d_{s},n})}=g(Z_{1,1},\dots,Z_{d_{s},n})\,.

the random variable
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}
is also
\sqrt{C}
-sub-Gaussian. In particular, for any
u>0
.

{\mathbb{P}}\bigg(\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\bigg]\geq u\bigg)\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{u^{2}}{2C}\bigg)\,.

(13)

Now suppose that
{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)
does not necessarily have finite cardinality. By separability, there exists a countable subset
{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}\subseteq{\mathcal{Z}}
such that
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}=\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}
almost surely. Let us define
{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}^{(n)}
to be the set that contains only the first
n
elements of
{\mathcal{Z}}
, and let us define the random variable
E_{n}
as

E_{n}:={\mathbb{I}}\Bigg\{\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}^{(n)}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\bigg]\geq u\Bigg\}\,.

Since
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}]\geq\mathbb{E}[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}^{(n)}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}]
for all
n\geq 1
, (
13
) tells us that
\mathbb{E}[E_{n}]\leq\exp(-u^{2}/(2C))
for all
n\geq 1
. Whenever
n\leq n^{\prime}
,
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}^{(n)}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\leq\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}^{(n^{\prime})}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}
almost surely. This means that
E_{n}\leq E_{n^{\prime}}
almost surely. By the monotone convergence theorem,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\bigg(\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\bigg]\geq u\bigg)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\bigg(\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{0}}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\bigg]\geq u\bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{n}\Big]

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[E_{n}]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{u^{2}}{2C}\bigg)\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

B.3
Proof of Lemma
4.6

Using the results from Appendix
B.1
and Appendix
B.2
, we can now prove Lemma
4.6
.

Proof of Lemma
4.6
.

Let us define
H:=\mathbb{E}[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}]
. By Lemma
B.5
, we have

H\leq 42\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{C(d_{s}+d_{a})\log(5+5R^{\alpha}L/C)}<\infty\,.

By Lemma
4.4
, for any
v\geq 0
,

{\mathbb{P}}\bigg(\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\geq v+H\bigg)\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{v^{2}}{2C}\bigg)\,.

Letting
u=v+H
, we have that for any
u\geq H
,

{\mathbb{P}}\bigg(\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R)}\|f({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\geq u\bigg)\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{(u-H)^{2}}{2C}\bigg)\,.

Whenever
u\geq 2H
, we have

, and so

\exp\bigg(-\frac{(u-H)^{2}}{2C}\bigg)\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{u^{2}}{8C}\bigg)\,.

The inequality

is satisfied.
∎

Appendix C
Tail Bounds for the Norm of the Largest State

In Appendix
C.1
, we state and prove some lemmas involving indicator functions that allow us to formalize the argument that the tails of the norm of each state decay rapidly as long as the previous state is bounded. In Appendix
C.2
, we upper bound the probability that at least one state has norm greater than some pre-specified threshold, and we then derive explicit values for the thresholds that ensure that this probability is of the order
1/T
. In Appendix
C.3
, we prove Lemma
4.7
.

C.1
A Trick With Indicator Functions

For
h=1
, we use the convention
{\mathbb{I}}\{\cap_{j=1}^{h-1}A_{j}\}:=1
.

Lemma C.1
.

For any finite collection of events
(A_{h})_{h=1}^{H}
,

{\mathbb{I}}\{\cup_{h=1}^{H}A_{h}^{\mathsf{c}}\}=\sum_{h=1}^{H}{\mathbb{I}}\{A_{h}^{\mathsf{c}}\}{\mathbb{I}}\{\cap_{j=1}^{h-1}A_{j}\}\,.

Proof.

We use induction on
H
. For the base case
H=1
, we have

{\mathbb{I}}\{\cup_{h=1}^{H}A_{h}^{\mathsf{c}}\}={\mathbb{I}}\{A_{1}^{\mathsf{c}}\}=\sum_{h=1}^{H}{\mathbb{I}}\{A_{h}^{\mathsf{c}}\}{\mathbb{I}}\{\cap_{j=1}^{h-1}A_{j}\}\,.

Suppose inductively that for some
H\geq 1
,

{\mathbb{I}}\{\cup_{h=1}^{H}A_{h}^{\mathsf{c}}\}=\sum_{h=1}^{H}{\mathbb{I}}\{A_{h}^{\mathsf{c}}\}{\mathbb{I}}\{\cap_{j=1}^{h-1}A_{j}\}\,.

From this, it follows that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{I}}\{\cup_{h=1}^{H+1}A_{h}^{\mathsf{c}}\}

\displaystyle=1-{\mathbb{I}}\{\cap_{h=1}^{H+1}A_{h}\}

\displaystyle=1-{\mathbb{I}}\{A_{H+1}\}{\mathbb{I}}\{\cap_{h=1}^{H}A_{h}\}

\displaystyle=1-(1-{\mathbb{I}}\{A_{H+1}^{\mathsf{c}}\})(1-{\mathbb{I}}\{\cup_{h=1}^{H}A_{h}^{\mathsf{c}}\})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{I}}\{A_{H+1}^{\mathsf{c}}\}(1-{\mathbb{I}}\{\cup_{h=1}^{H}A_{h}^{\mathsf{c}}\})+{\mathbb{I}}\{\cup_{h=1}^{H}A_{h}^{\mathsf{c}}\}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{I}}\{A_{H+1}^{\mathsf{c}}\}{\mathbb{I}}\{\cap_{h=1}^{H}A_{h}\}+\sum_{h=1}^{H}{\mathbb{I}}\{A_{h}^{\mathsf{c}}\}{\mathbb{I}}\{\cap_{j=1}^{h-1}A_{j}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=1}^{H+1}{\mathbb{I}}\{A_{h}^{\mathsf{c}}\}{\mathbb{I}}\{\cap_{j=1}^{h-1}A_{j}\}\,.

This closes the induction.
∎

The next lemma is basically the same as the union bound.

Lemma C.2
.

For any
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
R\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

{\mathbb{I}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}>R\right\}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{I}}\{x_{i}>R/n\}\,.

Proof.

We upper bound the sum by the max. In particular,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{I}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}>R\right\}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{I}}\{n\cdot\max_{i}(x_{i})>R\}={\mathbb{I}}\{\cup_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}>R/n)\}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{I}}\{x_{i}>R/n\}\,.

This is the bound that we wanted.
∎

C.2
Tail Bound for the Bad Event

We use the following bound on the tail probability of the norm of a Gaussian vector.

Lemma C.3
.

For any
n\in[N]
,
h\in[H]
and
u\geq 2\sigma\sqrt{d_{s}}
,

{\mathbb{P}}\big(\|\bm{\epsilon}_{n,h}\|_{2}>u\big)\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{u^{2}}{8\sigma^{2}}\bigg)\,.

Proof.

Since the Euclidean norm is 1-Lipschitz, Lemma
4.3
tells us that
\|\bm{\epsilon}_{n,h}\|_{2}
is
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian. By Jensen’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\epsilon}_{n,h}\|_{2}]\leq\big(\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\epsilon}_{n,h}\|_{2}^{2}]\big)^{1/2}=\sigma\sqrt{d_{s}}\,.

Since
u\geq 2\sigma\sqrt{d_{s}}
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}\big(\|\bm{\epsilon}_{n,h}\|_{2}>u\big)\leq{\mathbb{P}}\big(\|\bm{\epsilon}_{n,h}\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\epsilon}_{n,h}\|_{2}]>u/2\big)\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{u^{2}}{8\sigma^{2}}\bigg)\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Let
R_{1},R_{2},\dots,R_{H}
be a sequence of radii, whose values we will choose later. For each
n\in[N]
and
h\in[H]
, let us define the event

A_{n,h}:=\{\|{\bm{s}}_{n,h}\|_{2}\leq R_{h}\}\,.

In this section, we will be interested in the event

A:=\cap_{n=1}^{N}\cap_{h=1}^{H}A_{n,h}\,.

This is the event that the Euclidean norm of each state
{\bm{s}}_{n,h}
is at most
R_{h}
. We want to show that if the radii
R_{1},\dots,R_{H}
are large enough, then the complement
A^{\mathsf{c}}
can be made to occur with arbitrarily small probability. For each
h\in[H]
, let us define
\widetilde{R}_{h}:=\sqrt{R_{h}^{2}+R_{a}^{2}}
. For certain choices of
R_{1},\dots,R_{H}
, the following lemma provides an upper bound on the probability that
A^{\mathsf{c}}
occurs.

Lemma C.4
.

For any sequence of radii
R_{1},\dots,R_{H}
such that
R_{1}>2\sigma\sqrt{d_{s}}
and for all
h\geq 1
,

R_{h}\geq 168\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{\max(C,\sigma^{2})(d_{s}+d_{a})\log(5+5\widetilde{R}_{h-1}^{\alpha}L/C)}\,,

(14)

we have

{\mathbb{P}}(A^{\mathsf{c}})\leq N\exp\bigg(-\frac{R_{1}^{2}}{8\sigma^{2}}\bigg)+N\sum_{h=2}^{H}\bigg[\exp\bigg(-\frac{R_{h}^{2}}{32C}\bigg)+\exp\bigg(-\frac{R_{h}^{2}}{32\sigma^{2}}\bigg)\bigg]\,.

Proof.

Using Lemma
C.1
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(A^{\mathsf{c}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{\cup_{n=1}^{N}\cup_{h=1}^{H}A_{n,h}^{\mathsf{c}}\}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A_{n,h}^{\mathsf{c}}\}{\mathbb{I}}\{(\cap_{i=1}^{n-1}\cap_{j=1}^{H}A_{i,j})\cap(\cap_{j=1}^{h-1}A_{n,j})\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A_{n,h}^{\mathsf{c}}\}{\mathbb{I}}\{A_{n,h-1}\}\right]\,.

Here, we use the convention that
{\mathbb{I}}\{A_{n,0}\}:=1
. For any
n\in[N]
and
h=1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A_{n,1}^{\mathsf{c}}\}{\mathbb{I}}\{A_{n,0}\}\right]={\mathbb{P}}\left(\|{\bm{s}}_{n,1}\|_{2}>R_{1}\right)={\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\bm{\epsilon}_{n,1}\|_{2}>R_{1}\right)\,.

By Lemma
C.3
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\bm{\epsilon}_{n,1}\|_{2}>R_{1}\right)\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{R_{1}^{2}}{8\sigma^{2}}\bigg)\,.

We turn our attention to the case where
h>1
. Since
\|{\bm{x}}_{n,h}\|_{2}^{2}=\|{\bm{s}}_{n,h}\|_{2}^{2}+\|{\bm{a}}_{n,h}\|_{2}^{2}
, we have
\|{\bm{x}}_{n,h}\|_{2}\leq\widetilde{R}_{h}
whenever
\|{\bm{s}}_{n,h}\|_{2}\leq R_{h}
. For any
n\in[N]
and
h>1
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{I}}\{A_{n,h}^{\mathsf{c}}\}{\mathbb{I}}\{A_{n,h-1}\}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{I}}\{\|f^{\star}({\bm{s}}_{n,h-1},{\bm{a}}_{n,h-1})+\bm{\epsilon}_{n,h}\|_{2}>R_{h}\}{\mathbb{I}}\{\|{\bm{s}}_{n,h-1}\|_{2}\leq R_{h-1}\}

(15)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{I}}\left\{\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{B}}_{2}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R}_{h-1})}\|f^{\star}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}+\|\bm{\epsilon}_{n,h}\|_{2}>R_{h}\right\}\,.

Using Lemma
C.2
, we obtain

{\mathbb{I}}\bigg\{\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{B}}_{2}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R}_{h-1})}\|f^{\star}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}+\|\bm{\epsilon}_{n,h}\|_{2}>R_{h}\bigg\}\leq{\mathbb{I}}\bigg\{\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{B}}_{2}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R}_{h-1})}\|f^{\star}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}>\frac{R_{h}}{2}\bigg\}+{\mathbb{I}}\bigg\{\|\bm{\epsilon}_{n,h}\|_{2}>\frac{R_{h}}{2}\bigg\}\,.

Thus for any
n\in[N]
and
h>1
,

\mathbb{E}[{\mathbb{I}}\{A_{n,h}^{\mathsf{c}}\}{\mathbb{I}}\{A_{n,h-1}\}]\leq{\mathbb{P}}\bigg(\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{B}}_{2}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R}_{h-1})}\|f^{\star}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}>\frac{R_{h}}{2}\bigg)+{\mathbb{P}}\bigg(\|\bm{\epsilon}_{n,h}\|_{2}>\frac{R_{h}}{2}\bigg)\,.

Following the same reasoning as in the

satisfies the inequality in (

This means that

Combining everything so far results in the statement that we wanted.

Next, we determine how large

Lemma C.5
.

If we set

, and for each

\displaystyle=\sqrt{64\max(C,\sigma^{2})\log(T)}\,,

ensure that each term that appears in the upper bound in Lemma

. By rearranging the following inequality, we can ensure that

if

Suppose now that the value of

has already been fixed. We require that

Next, we require that

To derive explicit bounds on how large

Lemma C.6
.

If, for some constants

,

Since

\frac{1}{2}T^{2}\geq D^{2}\log(DL(R+1))\,.

We add
T^{2}/2
to both sides to obtain

\displaystyle T^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}T^{2}+D^{2}\log(DL(R+1))

\displaystyle=\frac{D^{2}}{2}\frac{T^{2}}{D^{2}}+D^{2}\log(DL(R+1))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{D^{2}}{2}\log(T^{2}/D^{2})+D^{2}\log(DL(R+1))

\displaystyle=D^{2}\log(TL(R+1))

\displaystyle\geq D^{2}\log((T+R)L)\,.

Since

Appendix D
Bounding the Regret by the Estimation Error

We prove a slight extension of a result from a Section 5.1 of
24
which expresses the value estimation error as a sum of Bellman errors. We then give a shorter (but less general) proof of an inequality that is essentially the same as Lemma 1 in
15
, and which allows us upper bound the Bellman errors by the model estimation errors. In Section
D.1
, we use these results to prove Lemma
4.9
.

For brevity, we define
P_{n,h}^{\star}:=P^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})
and
P_{n,h}^{(n)}:=P^{(n)}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})
. The following lemma allows us to upper bound the cumulative value estimation error by a sum of Bellman errors. The main difference between Lemma
D.1
and the result from Section 5.1 of
24
is that both the value estimation errors and the Bellman errors are multiplied by an indicator variable. Without this, we would have an identity instead of an inequality. Repeated application of the Bellman operator introduces a martingale difference sequence, which has expectation 0. However, the product of the indicator variable and this martingale difference sequence may not have expectation 0. Fortunately, one can still upper bound this expected value by a quantity that turns out to be negligible compared to the dominant term of the final regret bound.

Lemma D.1
.

For any event
A
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big(V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})-V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})\big)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\langle P_{n,h}^{(n)}-P_{n,h}^{\star},V_{\pi_{n},h+1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}\rangle\right]+2R_{\max}H\sqrt{2\pi T}\,.

Proof.

First, we use the Bellman equation (cf.  (
1
)) to write

\displaystyle V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})-V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{T}_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}V_{\pi_{n},2}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})-\mathsf{T}_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{\star}}V_{\pi_{n},2}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{A}}}\big(\langle P^{(n)}({\bm{s}}_{n,1},{\bm{a}}),V_{\pi_{n},2}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}\rangle-\langle P^{\star}({\bm{s}}_{n,1},{\bm{a}}),V_{\pi_{n},2}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{\star}}\rangle\big)\pi_{n}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{s}}_{n,1},1)\mathrm{d}{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{A}}}\langle P^{(n)}({\bm{s}}_{n,1},{\bm{a}})-P^{\star}({\bm{s}}_{n,1},{\bm{a}}),V_{\pi_{n},2}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}\rangle\pi_{n}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{s}}_{n,1},1)\mathrm{d}{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle+\int_{{\mathcal{A}}}\langle P^{\star}({\bm{s}}_{n,1},{\bm{a}}),V_{\pi_{n},2}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}-V_{\pi_{n},2}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{\star}}\rangle\pi_{n}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{s}}_{n,1},1)\mathrm{d}{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{A}}}\langle P^{(n)}({\bm{s}}_{n,1},{\bm{a}})-P^{\star}({\bm{s}}_{n,1},{\bm{a}}),V_{\pi_{n},2}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}\rangle\pi_{n}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{s}}_{n,1},1)\mathrm{d}{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle+V_{\pi_{n},2}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,2})-V_{\pi_{n},2}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,2})+D_{n,1}\,,

where
D_{n,h}:=\int_{{\mathcal{A}}}\langle P^{\star}({\bm{s}}_{n,h},{\bm{a}}),V_{\pi_{n},h+1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}-V_{\pi_{n},h+1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{\star}}\rangle\pi_{n}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{s}}_{n,h},h)\mathrm{d}{\bm{a}}-V_{\pi_{n},h+1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,h+1})+V_{\pi_{n},h+1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,h+1})
. From this identity, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big(V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})-V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})\big)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}D_{n,h}\right]

(17)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\int_{{\mathcal{A}}}\langle P^{(n)}({\bm{s}}_{n,h},{\bm{a}})-P^{\star}({\bm{s}}_{n,h},{\bm{a}}),V_{\pi_{n},h+1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}\rangle\pi_{n}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{s}}_{n,h},h)\mathrm{d}{\bm{a}}\right]\,.

To upper bound the first term, we notice that
\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}D_{n,h}
is the sum of a martingale difference sequence with bounded increments. Let us define the random variable

Z_{n,h}:=V_{\pi_{n},h+1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,h+1})-V_{\pi_{n},h+1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,h+1})\,,

and the
\sigma
-algebra

{\mathcal{G}}_{n,h-1}:=\sigma({\bm{s}}_{1,1},{\bm{a}}_{1,1},\dots,{\bm{s}}_{n,h-1},{\bm{a}}_{n,h-1},{\bm{s}}_{n,h},f^{\star},f^{(1)},\dots,f^{(n)})\,.

We notice that

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}D_{n,h}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,h}|{\mathcal{G}}_{n,h-1}]-Z_{n,h}\right]\,.

Moreover, given any
{\mathcal{G}}_{n,h-1}
,
Z_{n,h}
is bounded between
-2R_{\max}H
and
2R_{\max}H
, and is therefore conditionally
2R_{\max}H
-sub-Gaussian. This means that, for any
x>0
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,h}|{\mathcal{G}}_{n,h-1}]-Z_{n,h}\geq x\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\left(\exp\bigg(\lambda\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,h}|{\mathcal{G}}_{n,h-1}]-Z_{n,h}\bigg)\geq\exp(\lambda x)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\bigg(\lambda\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,h}|{\mathcal{G}}_{n,h-1}]-Z_{n,h}\bigg)\right]\exp(-\lambda x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{h=1}^{H-1}\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda(\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,h}|{\mathcal{G}}_{n,h-1}]-Z_{n,h}))|{\mathcal{G}}_{n,h-1}]\right]\exp(-\lambda x)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(2TR_{\max}^{2}H^{2}\lambda^{2}-\lambda x)\,.

Choosing
\lambda=\frac{x}{4TR_{\max}^{2}H^{2}}
, this becomes

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}D_{n,h}\geq x\right)\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{x^{2}}{8TR_{\max}^{2}H^{2}}\bigg)\,.

To save space, let us write
Y:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}D_{n,h}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}Y]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}Y{\mathbb{I}}\{Y\geq 0\}]+\mathbb{E}[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}Y{\mathbb{I}}\{Y<0\}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}Y{\mathbb{I}}\{Y\geq 0\}]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(Y{\mathbb{I}}\{Y\geq 0\}\geq x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(Y\geq x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\bigg(-\frac{x^{2}}{8TR_{\max}H^{2}}\bigg)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=R_{\max}H\sqrt{2\pi T}\,.

In the second term on the right-hand side of (
17
), we would like to take the conditional expectations w.r.t.
\pi_{n}(\cdot|{\bm{s}}_{n,h},h)
outside the sum. It turns out that this can be done by introducing another martingale difference sequence. This time we define the random variable
Z_{n,h}
by

Z_{n,h}=\langle P^{(n)}_{n,h}-P^{\star}_{n,h},V_{\pi_{n},h+1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}\rangle\,.

With the same
\sigma
-algebra
{\mathcal{G}}_{n,h-1}
, we notice that

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\int_{{\mathcal{A}}}\langle P^{(n)}({\bm{s}}_{n,h},{\bm{a}})-P^{\star}({\bm{s}}_{n,h},{\bm{a}}),V_{\pi_{n},h+1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}\rangle\pi_{n}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{s}}_{n,h},h)\mathrm{d}{\bm{a}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,h}|{\mathcal{G}}_{n,h-1}]\right]\,.

Note also that given any
{\mathcal{G}}_{n,h-1}
,
Z_{n,h}
is bounded between
-2R_{\max}H
and
2R_{\max}H
. Therefore, using the same argument as before, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,h}|{\mathcal{G}}_{n,h-1}]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}Z_{n,h}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,h}|{\mathcal{G}}_{n,h-1}]-Z_{n,h}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}Z_{n,h}\right]+R_{\max}H\sqrt{2\pi T}\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

The following lemma is essentially the same as Lemma 1 in

, which is referred to as “Lemma on the property derived from noises with a symmetric probability distribution”. Instead, we prefer to use Pinsker’s inequality to prove this result.

Lemma D.2
.

For any

and

\displaystyle\langle P_{n,h}^{(n)}-P_{n,h}^{\star},V_{\pi_{n},h+1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}}V_{\pi_{n},h+1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}})(P_{n,h}^{(n)}({\bm{s}})-P_{n,h}^{\star}({\bm{s}}))\mathrm{d}{\bm{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\|V_{\pi_{n},h+1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}\|_{\infty}\int|P_{n,h}^{(n)}({\bm{s}})-P_{n,h}^{\star}({\bm{s}})|\mathrm{d}{\bm{s}}

\displaystyle=2\|V_{\pi_{n},h+1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}\|_{\infty}D_{\mathrm{TV}}(P_{n,h}^{(n)}||P_{n,h}^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq 2HR_{\max}\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{n,h}^{(n)}||P_{n,h}^{\star})}

\displaystyle=\frac{HR_{\max}}{\sigma}\|f^{(n)}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})-f^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})\|_{2}\,.

This concludes the proof.

Appendix E
Upper Bounds for the Cumulative Estimation Error

We prove upper bounds on the cumulative estimation error, which is

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\|f^{(n)}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})-f^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})\|_{2}\right]\,,

where
A:=\{\sup_{n\in[N],h\in[H]}\|{\bm{s}}_{n,h}\|_{2}\leq R\}
for some
R>0
. The challenge is to exploit the fact
f^{(n)}
concentrates around
f^{\star}
, without having to work with the posterior covariance kernel. The general idea is to separate the estimation error into a discretized estimation error and two discretization error terms via a single-step discretization. Let
\widetilde{R}=\sqrt{R^{2}+R_{a}^{2}}
. For some
\epsilon\in(0,(7/(e\sqrt{8}))^{\alpha/2}\widetilde{R}]
, let
B_{\epsilon}
be a minimal
\epsilon
-cover of
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})
w.r.t. the Euclidean metric
d_{2}
and let
\omega:{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})\to B_{\epsilon}
defined by
\omega({\bm{x}}):=\argmin_{{\bm{y}}\in B_{\epsilon}}d_{2}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
be a function that maps each
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})
to the closest point in
B
(with ties broken arbitrarily). Using the triangle inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\|f^{(n)}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})-f^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\|f^{(n)}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))-f^{\star}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))\|_{2}\right]

(18)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\|f^{(n)}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})-f^{(n)}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\|f^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})-f^{\star}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))\|_{2}\right]\,.

We call the first term on the right-hand side the discretized estimation error. The remaining two terms are the discretization errors. In Appendix
E.1
, we use an elliptical potential lemma and an exponential moment inequality for chi-squared random variables to upper bound the discretized estimation error. In Appendix
E.2
, we the chaining argument in Lemma
4.5
to upper bound the discretization errors. Finally, in Appendix
E.3
, we prove Lemma
4.10
.

E.1
Bounding the Discretized Estimation Error

We will use the following exponential moment inequality.

Lemma E.1
.

For any finite subset
{\mathcal{Z}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
and any history
{\mathcal{F}}_{n-1}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\frac{\|f^{(n)}({\bm{x}})-f^{\star}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}^{2}}{8\sigma_{n-1}^{2}({\bm{x}})}\bigg|{\mathcal{F}}_{n-1}\right]\leq\log(|{\mathcal{Z}}|)+d_{s}\log(\sqrt{2})\,.

Proof.

For any
i\in[d_{s}]
, any
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
, and any history
{\mathcal{F}}_{n-1}
, the conditional distribution of
f_{i}^{(n)}({\bm{x}})-f_{i}^{\star}({\bm{x}})
is Gaussian with mean 0 and variance
2\sigma_{n-1}^{2}({\bm{x}})
. Thus (when conditioned on
{\mathcal{F}}_{n-1}
),
(f_{i}^{(n)}({\bm{x}})-f_{i}^{\star}({\bm{x}}))/(2^{1/2}\sigma_{n-1}({\bm{x}}))
follows a standard Gaussian distribution. Since the noise in each dimension of the state transitions is independent, these random variables are conditionally independent. Therefore, with
\chi^{2}(k)
denoting a chi-squared distribution with
k
degrees of freedom, conditioned on
{\mathcal{F}}_{n-1}
we have

\frac{\|f^{(n)}({\bm{x}})-f^{\star}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma_{n-1}^{2}({\bm{x}})}\sim\chi^{2}(d_{s})\,.

From the expression for the moment generating function of the
\chi^{2}(d_{s})
distribution, we obtain

\displaystyle\exp\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\frac{\|f^{(n)}({\bm{x}})-f^{\star}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}^{2}}{8\sigma_{n-1}^{2}({\bm{x}})}\bigg|{\mathcal{F}}_{n-1}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\exp\left(\frac{\|f^{(n)}({\bm{x}})-f^{\star}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}^{2}}{8\sigma_{n-1}^{2}({\bm{x}})}\right)\bigg|{\mathcal{F}}_{n-1}\right]\,,

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\exp\left(\frac{\|f^{(n)}({\bm{x}})-f^{\star}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}^{2}}{8\sigma_{n-1}^{2}({\bm{x}})}\right)\bigg|{\mathcal{F}}_{n-1}\right]\,,

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{4}\frac{\|f^{(n)}({\bm{x}})-f^{\star}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma_{n-1}^{2}({\bm{x}})}\right)\bigg|{\mathcal{D}}_{n-1}\right]\,,

\displaystyle\leq|{\mathcal{Z}}|(\sqrt{2})^{d_{s}}\,.

Taking logarithms of both sides gives the inequality that we wanted to prove.
∎

We will also use a version of the elliptical potential lemma. First, we recall the standard elliptical potential lemma (cf. e.g. , Lemma 5.3 and Lemma 5.4 in
31
).

Lemma E.2
.

For any sequence
{\bm{z}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{z}}_{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
and any
{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
, let us define

\sigma_{t}^{2}({\bm{z}})=c({\bm{z}},{\bm{z}})-{\bm{c}}_{t}^{\top}({\bm{z}})({\bm{C}}_{t}+\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}})^{-1}{\bm{c}}_{t}({\bm{z}})\,,

where
{\bm{c}}_{n}({\bm{z}})=[c({\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}_{1}),\dots,c({\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}_{t})]^{\top}
and
{\bm{C}}_{t}
is the
t\times t
kernel matrix with
i,j
th
element
c({\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{z}}_{j})
. Then

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t-1}^{2}({\bm{z}}_{t})\leq\frac{C}{\log(1+C/\sigma^{2})}\log\det\bigg(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}{\bm{C}}_{T}+{\bm{I}}\bigg)\,.

Using Lemma
E.2
, one can prove a version of the elliptical potential lemma that accounts for the fact that
f^{(n)}
is only re-sampled at the end of each episode. The result below is proved in the proof of Theorem 1 in
15
.

Lemma E.3
.

With probability 1,

{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\sigma_{n-1}^{2}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})\leq\frac{2CH}{\log(1+C/\sigma^{2})}\gamma_{N}(\sigma^{2},\widetilde{R})\,.

Proof.

Recall that
A:=\{\sup_{n\in[N],h\in[H]}\|{\bm{s}}_{n,h}\|_{2}\leq R\}
, and that under the event
A
,
\sup_{n\in[N],h\in[H]}\|{\bm{x}}_{n,h}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{R^{2}+R_{a}^{2}}=\widetilde{R}
. Let
{\bm{x}}_{n,\max}:=\argmax_{h\in[H-1]}\sigma_{n-1}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})
be the state-action pair in (the first
H-1
steps of) epsiode
n
for which the posterior variance is maximised. For any
{\bm{x}}
and any
n
, we define the posterior variance at
{\bm{x}}
conditioned on only
{\bm{x}}_{1,\max},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n,\max}
as

\overline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}({\bm{x}}):=c({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}})-\overline{{\bm{c}}}_{n}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\left(\overline{{\bm{C}}}_{n}+\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}}\right)^{-1}\overline{{\bm{c}}}_{n}({\bm{x}})\,,

where
\overline{{\bm{c}}}_{n}({\bm{x}}):=[c({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}_{1,\max}),\dots,c({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}_{n,\max})]^{\top}
and
\overline{{\bm{C}}}_{n}
is the
n\times n
kernel matrix with
i,j
th
element
\{\overline{{\bm{C}}}_{n}\}_{i,j}=c({\bm{x}}_{i,\max},{\bm{x}}_{j,\max})
. Since conditioning on more data can never increase the posterior variance, we have
\sigma_{n}^{2}({\bm{x}})\leq\overline{\sigma}_{n}^{2}({\bm{x}})
. Using Lemma
E.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\sigma_{n-1}^{2}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}(H-1)\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sigma_{n-1}^{2}({\bm{x}}_{n,\max})

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}(H-1)\sum_{n=1}^{N}\overline{\sigma}_{n-1}^{2}({\bm{x}}_{n,\max})

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\frac{C(H-1)}{\log(1+C/\sigma^{2})}\log\det\bigg(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\overline{{\bm{C}}}_{N}+{\bm{I}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2CH}{\log(1+C/\sigma^{2})}\gamma_{N}(\sigma^{2},\widetilde{R})\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

We also use the fact that whenever the kernel satisfies Assumption
3.3
and Assumption
3.4
, the posterior variance is Hölder contininuous.

Lemma E.4
.

Suppose that Assumption
3.3
and Assumption
3.4
are satisfied. Then for any
n\geq 0
,

|\sigma_{n}^{2}({\bm{x}})-\sigma_{n}^{2}({\bm{y}})|\leq 2\sqrt{2CL}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|_{2}^{\alpha/2}\,.

Proof.

We begin by re-writing
\sigma_{n}^{2}({\bm{x}})
. Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be the reproducing kernel Hilbert space associated with the kernel
c
. For any
{\bm{x}}
, let us define
c_{{\bm{x}}}:=c(\cdot,{\bm{x}})\in{\mathcal{H}}
. We define the linear operator
{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n(H-1)}
by

{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}h:=[\langle c_{{\bm{x}}_{1,1}},h\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}},\dots,\langle c_{{\bm{x}}_{n,H-1}},h\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}]^{\top}=[h({\bm{x}}_{1,1}),\dots,h({\bm{x}}_{n,H-1})]^{\top}\,.

We can re-write
\sigma_{n}^{2}({\bm{x}})
as the quadratic form

\sigma_{n}^{2}({\bm{x}})=c({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}})-{\bm{c}}_{n}^{\top}({\bm{C}}_{n}+\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}})^{-1}{\bm{c}}_{n}({\bm{x}})=\langle c_{{\bm{x}}},({\bm{I}}_{{\mathcal{H}}}-{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}({\bm{\Phi}}_{n}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}+\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}})^{-1}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n})c_{{\bm{x}}}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\,,

where
{\bm{I}}_{{\mathcal{H}}}
is the identity function on
{\mathcal{H}}
. By the matrix inversion lemma, we have

{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}({\bm{\Phi}}_{n}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}+\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}})^{-1}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}={\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}({\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}+\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}}_{{\mathcal{H}}})^{-1}\,.

Since
{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}
is positive semi-definite, we have
\|{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}({\bm{\Phi}}_{n}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}+\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}})^{-1}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
. Since
{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}({\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}+\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}}_{{\mathcal{H}}})^{-1}
is also positive semi-definite, we also have
\|{\bm{I}}-{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}({\bm{\Phi}}_{n}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}+\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}})^{-1}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
. For any
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
,

\displaystyle|\sigma_{n}^{2}({\bm{x}})-\sigma_{n}^{2}({\bm{y}})|

\displaystyle=|\langle c_{{\bm{x}}},({\bm{I}}_{{\mathcal{H}}}-{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}({\bm{\Phi}}_{n}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}+\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}})^{-1}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n})c_{{\bm{x}}}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}-\langle c_{{\bm{y}}},({\bm{I}}_{{\mathcal{H}}}-{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}({\bm{\Phi}}_{n}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}+\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}})^{-1}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n})c_{{\bm{y}}}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}|

\displaystyle=|\langle c_{{\bm{x}}}+c_{{\bm{y}}},({\bm{I}}_{{\mathcal{H}}}-{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}({\bm{\Phi}}_{n}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}+\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}})^{-1}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n})(c_{{\bm{x}}}-c_{{\bm{y}}})\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}|

\displaystyle\leq\|c_{{\bm{x}}}+c_{{\bm{y}}}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}\|({\bm{I}}_{{\mathcal{H}}}-{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}({\bm{\Phi}}_{n}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n}^{*}+\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}})^{-1}{\bm{\Phi}}_{n})(c_{{\bm{x}}}-c_{{\bm{y}}})\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|c_{{\bm{x}}}+c_{{\bm{y}}}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}\|c_{{\bm{x}}}-c_{{\bm{y}}}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2CL\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|_{2}^{\alpha}}\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Using Lemma
E.1
and
E.3
we can prove the following upper bound on the discretized estimation error.

Lemma E.5
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\|f^{(n)}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))-f^{\star}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{16CHT}{\log(1+C/\sigma^{2})}\gamma_{N}(\sigma^{2},\widetilde{R})(\log|B_{\epsilon}|+d_{s}\log(\sqrt{2}))}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{16T\sqrt{2CL}\epsilon^{\alpha/2}}\sqrt{T(\log|B_{\epsilon}|+d_{s}\log(\sqrt{2}))}\,.

Proof.

We use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (and Lemma
E.4
) to separate the discretized estimation error into two terms.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\|f^{(n)}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))-f^{\star}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\frac{\|f^{(n)}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))-f^{\star}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))\|_{2}}{2\sqrt{2}\sigma_{n-1}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))}2\sqrt{2}\sigma_{n-1}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\frac{\|f^{(n)}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))-f^{\star}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))\|_{2}^{2}}{8\sigma_{n-1}^{2}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))}}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}8\sigma_{n-1}^{2}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\frac{\|f^{(n)}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))-f^{\star}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))\|_{2}^{2}}{8\sigma_{n-1}^{2}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))}}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}8\sigma_{n-1}^{2}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\frac{\|f^{(n)}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))-f^{\star}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))\|_{2}^{2}}{8\sigma_{n-1}^{2}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))}}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}16\sqrt{2CL}\epsilon^{\alpha/2}}\right]\,.

For the first term, we use Lemma
E.1
, Lemma
E.3
and Jensen’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\frac{\|f^{(n)}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))-f^{\star}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))\|_{2}^{2}}{8\sigma_{n-1}^{2}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))}}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}8\sigma_{n-1}^{2}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{16CH}{\log(1+C/\sigma^{2})}\gamma_{N}(\sigma^{2},\widetilde{R})}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\frac{\|f^{(n)}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))-f^{\star}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))\|_{2}^{2}}{8\sigma_{n-1}^{2}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{16CH}{\log(1+C/\sigma^{2})}\gamma_{N}(\sigma^{2},\widetilde{R})}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in B_{\epsilon}}\frac{\|f^{(n)}({\bm{x}})-f^{\star}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}^{2}}{8\sigma_{n-1}^{2}({\bm{x}})}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{16CHT}{\log(1+C/\sigma^{2})}\gamma_{N}(\sigma^{2},\widetilde{R})(\log|B_{\epsilon}|+d_{s}\log(\sqrt{2}))}\,.

For the second term, we use Lemma
E.1
and Jensen’s inequality again to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\frac{\|f^{(n)}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))-f^{\star}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))\|_{2}^{2}}{8\sigma_{n-1}^{2}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))}}\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}16\sqrt{2CL}\epsilon^{\alpha/2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{16T\sqrt{2CL}\epsilon^{\alpha/2}}\sqrt{T(\log|B_{\epsilon}|+d_{s}\log(\sqrt{2}))}\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

E.2
Bounding the Discretization Errors

Let
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{d_{s}})
, where
f_{1},\dots,f_{d_{s}}\sim{\mathcal{G}}{\mathcal{P}}(0,c({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}))
, and consider the random process
\widetilde{f}
given by
\widetilde{f}({\bm{x}}):=f({\bm{x}})-f(\omega({\bm{x}}))
. It can be seen that each component
\widetilde{f}_{i}
of
\widetilde{f}
is a centered Gaussian princess with the covariance kernel
\widetilde{c}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
given by

\widetilde{c}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}):=c(\omega({\bm{x}}),\omega({\bm{y}}))-c(\omega({\bm{x}}),{\bm{y}})-c({\bm{x}},\omega({\bm{y}}))+c({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\,.

Since
f^{\star}
and
f^{(n)}
(and
f
) have the same marginal distribution, both of the discretisation error terms are equal to

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\|\widetilde{f}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})\|_{2}\right]\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})}\|\widetilde{f}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\right]\,.

We upper bound the expected supremum on the right-hand side using the chaining method. We will first need to establish some properties of
\widetilde{c}
and the corresponding natural distance
d_{\widetilde{c}}
. We would like to have an analogue of Lemma
B.2
, which gives an upper bound on the covering number of
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})
with respect to the distance
d_{\widetilde{c}}
. However, since
\omega
is in general not continuous, neither is
d_{\widetilde{c}}
. This means we that cannot use exactly the same argument as we did in the proof of Lemma
B.2
. Fortunately,
d_{\widetilde{c}}
is still piecewise Hölder continuous, and this fact can be exploited in a similar manner to before. To do so, we will use the following lemma, which establishes a relationship between covering numbers and proper covering numbers (see Appendix
A.2
for the distinction between covering numbers and proper covering numbers).

Lemma E.6
.

For any set
{\mathcal{Z}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
and any
\epsilon>0
such that
\mathsf{N}({\mathcal{Z}},d_{2},\epsilon/2)<\infty
,

\mathsf{N}_{\mathrm{pr}}({\mathcal{Z}},d_{2},\epsilon)\leq\mathsf{N}({\mathcal{Z}},d_{2},\epsilon/2)\,.

Proof.

Let
\{{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{M}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}
be an
\epsilon/2
-cover of
{\mathcal{Z}}
. We partition
{\mathcal{Z}}
into at most
M
sets as follows. We define

B_{1}:=\{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}:d_{2}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}_{1})\leq\epsilon/2\}\,.

Then, for
i=2,3,\dots,M
, we define

B_{i}:=\{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}:d_{2}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}_{i})\leq\epsilon/2\}\setminus\big(\cup_{j=1}^{i-1}B_{j}\big)\,.

If for any
i\in[M]
,
B_{i}=\emptyset
, we can remove it from the partition. In any case, the resulting partition will have at most
M
elements. Assuming, as we may, that each
B_{i}\neq\emptyset
for all
i\in[M]
, we can choose a set of points
\{{\bm{y}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{y}}_{M}\}\subset{\mathcal{Z}}
such that for all
i\in[M]
,
{\bm{y}}_{i}\in B_{i}
. Fix a point
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
. Since
(B_{i})_{i=1}^{M}
is a partition of
{\mathcal{Z}}
, there exists a unique
j\in[M]
such that
{\bm{x}}\in B_{j}
. Since
{\bm{y}}_{j}\in B_{j}
and
\mathrm{diam}_{d_{2}}(B_{j})\leq\epsilon
,
d_{2}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}_{j})\leq\epsilon
. Therefore,
\{{\bm{y}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{y}}_{M}\}
is a proper
\epsilon
-cover of
{\mathcal{Z}}
, and the claim follows.
∎

We can now prove an analogue of Lemma
B.2
.

Lemma E.7
.

For any
\epsilon\in(0,\widetilde{R}]
and
\delta\in(0,\sqrt{8L\epsilon^{\alpha}}]
,

\mathsf{N}({\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R}),d_{\widetilde{c}},\delta)\leq\bigg(\frac{7L^{1/2}\widetilde{R}^{\alpha/2}}{\delta}\bigg)^{2(d_{s}+d_{a})/\alpha}\,.

Proof.

Recall that
B_{\epsilon}
is a minimal
\epsilon
-cover of
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})
w.r.t. the Euclidean metric
d_{2}
, and let
\{{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{M}\}
be the points in
B_{\epsilon}
. We partition
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})
into
M
sets
(B_{i})_{i=1}^{M}
as follows. For each
i\in[M]
, we define
B_{i}:=\{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R}):\omega({\bm{x}})=i\}
. We may assume that each set
B_{i}
is non-empty, since if this was not the case, then
B_{\epsilon}
would not be a minimal
\epsilon
-cover. Since
B_{\epsilon}
is an
\epsilon
-cover of
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})
w.r.t.
d_{2}
, for each
i
we have
B_{i}\subseteq{\mathbb{B}}_{{\bm{x}}_{i}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\epsilon)
. For each
i
, we construct a proper
\delta_{0}
-cover
C_{i}\subseteq B_{i}
of
B_{i}
(w.r.t. the metric
d_{2}
). Fix a point
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})
. Since
(B_{i})_{i=1}^{M}
is a partition of
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})
, there exists
j\in[M]
such that
{\bm{x}}\in B_{j}
, which means
\omega({\bm{x}})={\bm{x}}_{j}
. Since
C_{j}
is a proper
\delta_{0}
-cover of
B_{j}
, there exists
{\bm{y}}\in C_{j}\subseteq B_{j}
such that
\omega({\bm{y}})={\bm{x}}_{j}=\omega({\bm{x}})
and
\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|_{2}\leq\delta_{0}
. By Assumption
3.4
, we have

\displaystyle|\widetilde{c}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}})-\widetilde{c}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})|

\displaystyle\leq|c(\omega({\bm{x}}),\omega({\bm{x}}))-c(\omega({\bm{x}}),\omega({\bm{y}}))|+|c(\omega({\bm{x}}),{\bm{x}})-c(\omega({\bm{x}}),{\bm{y}})|

\displaystyle+|c({\bm{x}},\omega({\bm{x}}))-c({\bm{x}},\omega({\bm{y}}))|+|c({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}})-c({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})|

\displaystyle\leq 2L\|\omega({\bm{x}})-\omega({\bm{y}})\|_{2}^{\alpha}+2L\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|_{2}^{\alpha}\leq 2L\delta_{0}^{\alpha}\,.

Therefore, if we choose
\delta_{0}=(\delta^{2}/(4L))^{1/\alpha}
, then

d_{\widetilde{c}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\leq\sqrt{|\widetilde{c}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}})-\widetilde{c}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})|+|\widetilde{c}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}})-\widetilde{c}({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}})|}\leq\sqrt{4L\delta_{0}^{\alpha}}=\delta\,.

Therefore,
\cup_{i=1}^{M}C_{i}
is a
\delta
-cover of
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})
w.r.t. the metric
d_{\widetilde{c}}
. By combining Lemma
E.6
and Lemma
B.1
, we see that the cardinality of each
C_{i}
satisfies

\bigg(1+\frac{4\epsilon}{\delta_{0}}\bigg)^{d_{s}+d_{a}}\,.

Therefore, using Lemma
B.1
again,

|\cup_{i=1}^{M}C_{i}|\leq M\bigg(1+\frac{2\epsilon}{\delta_{0}}\bigg)^{d_{s}+d_{a}}\leq\bigg(1+\frac{2\widetilde{R}}{\epsilon}\bigg)^{d_{s}+d_{a}}\bigg(1+\frac{4(4L)^{1/\alpha}\epsilon}{\delta^{2/\alpha}}\bigg)^{d_{s}+d_{a}}\,.

Since
\epsilon\leq\widetilde{R}
, we have
1\leq\widetilde{R}/\epsilon
. Similarly, since
\delta\leq\sqrt{8L\epsilon^{\alpha}}
, we have
1\leq 8^{1/\alpha}L^{1/\alpha}\epsilon/\delta^{2/\alpha}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle|\cup_{i=1}^{M}C_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\frac{3\widetilde{R}}{\epsilon}\bigg)^{d_{s}+d_{a}}\bigg(\frac{(4\cdot 4^{1/\alpha}+8^{1/\alpha})L^{1/\alpha}\epsilon}{\delta^{2/\alpha}}\bigg)^{d_{s}+d_{a}}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{3(4\cdot 4^{1/\alpha}+8^{1/\alpha})L^{1/\alpha}\widetilde{R}}{\delta^{2/\alpha}}\bigg)^{d_{s}+d_{a}}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{3^{\alpha/2}(4\cdot 4^{1/\alpha}+8^{1/\alpha})^{\alpha/2}L^{1/2}\widetilde{R}^{\alpha/2}}{\delta}\bigg)^{2(d_{s}+d_{a})/\alpha}\,.

The factor of
3^{\alpha/2}(4\cdot 4^{1/\alpha}+8^{1/\alpha})^{\alpha/2}
can be simplified. Since
\alpha\leq 1
,

\displaystyle 3^{\alpha/2}(4\cdot 4^{1/\alpha}+8^{1/\alpha})^{\alpha/2}\leq\sqrt{3}(2\cdot 2^{1/\alpha}+8^{1/(2\alpha)})^{\alpha}\leq\sqrt{3}(4+\sqrt{8})\leq 7\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

We would also like to have an analogue of Lemma
B.3
, which controls the diameter
\mathrm{diam}_{d_{\widetilde{c}}}({\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R}))
.

Lemma E.8
.

For any
\epsilon>0
,

\mathrm{diam}_{d_{\widetilde{c}}}({\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R}))\leq\sqrt{8L\epsilon^{\alpha}}\,.

Proof.

Let
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}
be any pair of points in
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})
. Due to Assumption
3.4
and the definition of
\omega
, it follows that

|\widetilde{c}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})|\leq|c(\omega({\bm{x}}),\omega({\bm{y}}))-c(\omega({\bm{x}}),{\bm{y}})|+|c({\bm{x}},\omega({\bm{y}}))-c({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})|\leq 2L\epsilon^{\alpha}\,.

(19)

Therefore,

d_{\widetilde{c}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\sqrt{\widetilde{c}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}})-2\widetilde{c}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})+\widetilde{c}({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}})}\leq\sqrt{8L\epsilon^{\alpha}}\,.

Since
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
were arbitrary points in
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})
, we conclude that
\mathrm{diam}_{d_{\widetilde{c}}}({\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R))\leq\sqrt{8L\epsilon^{\alpha}}
.
∎

To apply the chaining method to the discretization error, we also need an upper bound for the entropy integral. For this purpose, we will use the following technical lemma.

Lemma E.9
.

For any
y\in(0,1/e]
,

\int_{0}^{y}\sqrt{\log(1/x)}\mathrm{d}x\leq 2y\sqrt{\log(1/y)}\,.

Proof.

Using the substitution
u=\log(y/x)
, we have

\int_{0}^{y}\sqrt{\log(1/x)}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log(1/y)+u}y\exp(-u)\mathrm{d}u=y\sqrt{\log(1/y)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{1+u/\log(1/y)}\exp(-u)\mathrm{d}u\,.

Since
u/\log(1/y)\geq 0
, we have
1+u/\log(1/y)\leq\exp(u/\log(1/y))
. Also, since
y\leq 1/e
, we have
\log(1/y)\geq 1
. Using these inequalities, we obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{1+u/\log(1/y)}\exp(-u)\mathrm{d}u\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\bigg(\frac{u}{2\log(1/y)}-u\bigg)\mathrm{d}u\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-u/2)\mathrm{d}x=2\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

This lemma has the following consequence.

Corollary E.10
.

For any
z>0
and
y\in(0,z/e]
,

\int_{0}^{y}\sqrt{\log(z/x)}\mathrm{d}x\leq 2y\sqrt{\log(z/y)}\,.

Proof.

Using the substitution
u=x/z
, we have

\int_{0}^{y}\sqrt{\log(z/x)}\mathrm{d}x=z\int_{0}^{y/z}\sqrt{\log(1/u)}\mathrm{d}u\,.

Since
y/z\leq 1/e
, the claim now follows from Lemma
E.9

∎

We can at last state and prove an upper bound for the entropy integral.

Lemma E.11
.

For any
\epsilon\in(0,(7/(e\sqrt{8}))^{\alpha/2}\widetilde{R}]
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log\mathsf{N}({\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R}),d_{\widetilde{c}},\delta)}\mathrm{d}\delta\leq 8\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{(d_{s}+d_{a})L\epsilon^{\alpha}\log(3\widetilde{R}^{\alpha/2}/\epsilon^{\alpha/2})}\,.

Proof.

By Lemma
E.8
,
\mathrm{diam}_{d_{\widetilde{c}}}({\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R}))\leq\sqrt{8L\epsilon^{\alpha}}
, so we can replace the upper limit of the integral by
\sqrt{8L\epsilon^{\alpha}}
. Since
7/(e\sqrt{8})\leq 1
,
\epsilon\leq\widetilde{R}
. Thus by Lemma
E.7
,

\int_{0}^{\sqrt{8L\epsilon^{\alpha}}}\sqrt{\log\mathsf{N}({\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R}),d_{\widetilde{c}},\delta)}\mathrm{d}\delta\leq\sqrt{2(d_{s}+d_{a})/\alpha}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{8L\epsilon^{\alpha}}}\sqrt{\log(7L^{1/2}\widetilde{R}^{\alpha/2}/\delta)}\mathrm{d}\delta\,.

Since
\epsilon\leq(7/(e\sqrt{8}))^{\alpha/2}\widetilde{R}
,
\sqrt{8L\epsilon^{\alpha}}\leq 7L^{1/2}\widetilde{R}^{\alpha/2}/e
. Thus by Corollary
E.10
,

\int_{0}^{\sqrt{8L\epsilon^{\alpha}}}\sqrt{\log(7L^{1/2}\widetilde{R}^{\alpha/2}/\delta)}\mathrm{d}\delta\leq 2\sqrt{8L\epsilon^{\alpha}\log\bigg(\frac{7\widetilde{R}^{\alpha/2}}{8^{1/2}\epsilon^{\alpha/2}}\bigg)}\leq 2\sqrt{8L\epsilon^{\alpha}\log(3\widetilde{R}^{\alpha/2}/\epsilon^{\alpha/2})}\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Finally, we are ready to upper bound each of the discretization errors.

Lemma E.12
.

Suppose that Assumption
3.3
and Assumption
3.4
are satisfied. Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{d_{s}}\sim{\mathcal{G}}{\mathcal{P}}(0,c({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}))
be independent, centered Gaussian processes and let
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{d_{s}})
. Let
\widetilde{f}
be the random process defined by
\widetilde{f}({\bm{x}}):=f({\bm{x}})-f(\omega({\bm{x}}))
. For any
\epsilon\in(0,(7/(e\sqrt{8}))^{\alpha/2}\widetilde{R}]
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})}\|\widetilde{f}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\right]\leq 96\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{(d_{s}+d_{a})L\epsilon^{\alpha}\log(3\widetilde{R}^{\alpha/2}/\epsilon^{\alpha/2})}+6\sqrt{8L\epsilon^{\alpha}}+\sqrt{2d_{s}L\epsilon^{\alpha}}\,.

Proof.

First, we apply the chaining argument from Lemma
4.5
, except with the kernel
\widetilde{c}
. The proof of Lemma
4.5
(with the kernel
c
) uses the fact that
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}}c({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}})\leq C
and
\mathrm{diam}_{d_{c}}({\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(R))\leq 2\sqrt{C}
. Lemma
E.8
gives us an upper bound for
\mathrm{diam}_{d_{\widetilde{c}}}({\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R}))
. From (
19
), we have
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}}\widetilde{c}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}})\leq 2L\epsilon^{\alpha}
. Therefore, Lemma
4.5
tells us that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})}\|\widetilde{f}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\right]\leq 12\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log\mathsf{N}({\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R}),d_{\widetilde{c}},\delta)}\mathrm{d}\delta+6\sqrt{8L\epsilon^{\alpha}}+\sqrt{2d_{s}L\epsilon^{\alpha}}\,.

Using Lemma
E.11
to upper bound the entropy integral, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})}\|\widetilde{f}({\bm{x}})\|_{2}\right]\leq 96\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{(d_{s}+d_{a})L\epsilon^{\alpha}\log(3\widetilde{R}^{\alpha/2}/\epsilon^{\alpha/2})}+6\sqrt{8L\epsilon^{\alpha}}+\sqrt{2d_{s}L\epsilon^{\alpha}}\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

E.3
Proof of Lemma
4.10

We prove Lemma
4.10
by combining the inequalities in Lemma
E.5
and Lemma
E.12
, and then finding a suitable value for
\epsilon
.

Proof of Lemma
4.10
.

Recall that, for some
\epsilon\in(0,(7/(e\sqrt{8}))^{\alpha/2}\widetilde{R}]
,
B_{\epsilon}
is a minimal
\epsilon
-cover of
{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})
w.r.t. the Euclidean metric
d_{2}
, and
\omega:{\mathbb{B}}^{d_{s}+d_{a}}(\widetilde{R})\to B_{\epsilon}
is given by
\omega({\bm{x}}):=\argmin_{{\bm{y}}\in B_{\epsilon}}d_{2}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
. Using the triangle inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\|f^{(n)}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})-f^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\|f^{(n)}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))-f^{\star}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\|f^{(n)}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})-f^{(n)}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\|f^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})-f^{\star}(\omega({\bm{x}}_{n,h}))\|_{2}\right]\,.

Using Lemma
E.5
and
E.12
to upper bound each term on the right-hand side, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\|f^{(n)}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})-f^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{16CHT}{\log(1+C/\sigma^{2})}\gamma_{N}(\sigma^{2},\widetilde{R})(\log|B_{\epsilon}|+d_{s}\log(\sqrt{2}))}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{16T\sqrt{2CL}\epsilon^{\alpha/2}}\sqrt{T(\log|B_{\epsilon}|+d_{s}\log(\sqrt{2}))}

\displaystyle+192T\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{(d_{s}+d_{a})L\epsilon^{\alpha}\log(3\widetilde{R}^{\alpha/2}/\epsilon^{\alpha/2})}

\displaystyle+12T\sqrt{8L\epsilon^{\alpha}}+2T\sqrt{2d_{s}L\epsilon^{\alpha}}\,.

We choose
\epsilon=1/(L^{1/\alpha}T^{2/\alpha})
. By Lemma
B.1
, we have

|B_{\epsilon}|\leq\bigg(\frac{3\widetilde{R}}{\epsilon}\bigg)^{d_{s}+d_{a}}=(3\widetilde{R}(LT^{2})^{1/\alpha})^{d_{s}+d_{a}}=((3\widetilde{R})^{\alpha}LT^{2})^{(d_{s}+d_{a})/\alpha}\,.

Therefore, we have

\log|B_{\epsilon}|+d_{s}\log(\sqrt{2})\leq\frac{d_{s}+d_{a}}{\alpha}\log((3\widetilde{R})^{\alpha}LT^{2})+\frac{d_{s}+d_{a}}{\alpha}\log(2^{\alpha/2})\leq\frac{d_{s}+d_{a}}{\alpha}\log(5\widetilde{R}^{\alpha}LT^{2})\,.

The upper bound on the estimation error becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\|f^{(n)}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})-f^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{\frac{16C(d_{s}+d_{a})HT}{\log(1+C/\sigma^{2})}\gamma_{N}(\sigma^{2},\widetilde{R})\log(5\widetilde{R}^{\alpha}LT^{2})}

\displaystyle+\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{16\sqrt{2C}(d_{s}+d_{a})T\log(5\widetilde{R}^{\alpha}LT^{2})}

\displaystyle+192\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{(d_{s}+d_{a})\log(3\widetilde{R}^{\alpha/2}L^{1/2}T)}+12\sqrt{8}+2\sqrt{2d_{s}}\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Appendix F
Proof of Theorem
4.11

Proof of Theorem
4.11
.

By Lemma
4.8
, we can re-write the Bayesian regret as a sum of value estimation errors. In particular,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{T}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{N}V_{\pi^{\star},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}^{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})-V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}^{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{N}V_{\pi^{\star},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}^{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})-V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})+V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})-V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}^{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[V_{\pi^{\star},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}^{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})-V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})+V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})-V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}^{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})\big|{\mathcal{F}}_{n-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{N}V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})-V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}^{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})\right]\,.

Let us define

R:=168\alpha^{-1/2}\sqrt{\max(C,\sigma^{2})(d_{s}+d_{a})\log(10(T+R_{a})\max(1,L/C))}\,.

Also, let
\widetilde{R}=\sqrt{R^{2}+R_{a}^{2}}
. We define the event
A
as

A:=\bigg\{\sup_{n\in[N],h\in[H]}\|{\bm{s}}_{n,h}\|_{2}\leq R\bigg\}\,.

Since the reward function is bounded between
-R_{\max}
and
R_{\max}
, we have
|V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})|\leq R_{\max}H
and
|V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}^{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})|\leq R_{\max}H
almost surely. Thus by Lemma
4.7

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{N}V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})-V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}^{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[({\mathbb{I}}\{A\}+{\mathbb{I}}\{A^{\mathsf{c}}\})\sum_{n=1}^{N}V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})-V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}^{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})-V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}^{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})\right]+2R_{\max}T{\mathbb{P}}(A^{\mathsf{c}})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})-V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}^{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})\right]+4R_{\max}\,.

Next, by Lemma
4.9
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})-V_{\pi_{n},1}^{{\mathcal{M}}^{\star}}({\bm{s}}_{n,1})\right]\leq\frac{R_{\max}H}{\sigma}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\|f^{(n)}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})-f^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})\|_{2}\right]+2R_{\max}H\sqrt{2\pi T}\,.

Finally, by Lemma
4.10
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{R_{\max}H}{\sigma}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{I}}\{A\}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\|f^{(n)}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})-f^{\star}({\bm{x}}_{n,h})\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{R_{\max}H^{3/2}}{\sigma\alpha^{1/2}}\sqrt{\frac{16C(d_{s}+d_{a})T}{\log(1+C/\sigma^{2})}\gamma_{N}(\sigma^{2},\widetilde{R})\log(5\widetilde{R}^{\alpha}LT^{2})}

\displaystyle+\frac{R_{\max}H}{\sigma\alpha^{1/2}}\sqrt{16\sqrt{2C}(d_{s}+d_{a})T\log(5\widetilde{R}^{\alpha}LT^{2})}

\displaystyle+\frac{192R_{\max}H}{\sigma\alpha^{1/2}}\sqrt{(d_{s}+d_{a})\log(3\widetilde{R}^{\alpha/2}L^{1/2}T)}

\displaystyle+\frac{12\sqrt{8}R_{\max}H}{\sigma}+\frac{2\sqrt{2d_{s}}R_{\max}H}{\sigma}\,.

Therefore, the Bayesian regret satisfies

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{T}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{R_{\max}H^{3/2}}{\sigma\alpha^{1/2}}\sqrt{\frac{16C(d_{s}+d_{a})T}{\log(1+C/\sigma^{2})}\gamma_{N}(\sigma^{2},\widetilde{R})\log(5\widetilde{R}^{\alpha}LT^{2})}

\displaystyle+\frac{R_{\max}H}{\sigma\alpha^{1/2}}\sqrt{16\sqrt{2C}(d_{s}+d_{a})T\log(5\widetilde{R}^{\alpha}LT^{2})}

\displaystyle+\frac{192R_{\max}H}{\sigma\alpha^{1/2}}\sqrt{(d_{s}+d_{a})\log(3\widetilde{R}^{\alpha/2}L^{1/2}T)}

\displaystyle+\frac{12\sqrt{8}R_{\max}H}{\sigma}+\frac{2\sqrt{2d_{s}}R_{\max}H}{\sigma}+4R_{\max}+2R_{\max}H\sqrt{2\pi T}\,.

The dominant term on the right-hand side is the first one, and its growth-rate matches the one stated in Theorem
4.11
.

∎

Appendix G
Experimental Results

For the experimental study, we considered a 2D navigation task where
d_{s}=2
and
d_{a}=2
.
The reward function contained potential functions for a goal state, a central circle ‘obstacle’ and barrier functions inside a state boundary (Fig
6
).
For the optimal control oracle and regret analysis, we discretized the system within this state boundary and performed value iteration to compute approximate optimal policies and compute the exact regret for the approximated MDP.
For the function prior, we used stationary GPs of the form
{\bm{s}}_{h+1}={\bm{s}}_{h}+\Delta\cdot f({\bm{s}}_{h},{\bm{a}}_{h})
, essentially stationary velocity prior under Euler integration.
To estimate the Bayesian regret, we sampled a GP from this prior to be the ‘ground truth’, and then performed GP-PSRL, computing regret by evaluating the policy on the ‘ground truth’ discretized function sample.
To approximate the stationary GP with a parametric model and to benefit from explicit function samples, we constructed random Fourier features from the spectral density of the covariance functions.
The GP prior had fixed hyperparameters, with a prior variance of
1
, a lengthscale of
0.5
and aleatoric noise variance of
1\times 10^{-6}
, and used 1000 random features.
This prior was designed such that samples from the discretized action space sufficiently explored the state space across several function samples from the prior, and also such that the optimal policy solved the navigation task across function samples.
For the MDP, we used a uniform initial state distribution and a nominal task horizon of 20.
These were chosen to ensure adaquate exploration and optimal solutions, as shown in Figures
6
and
6
.
