Broadly reactive human monoclonal antibodies targeting influenza a nucleoprotein lack protective activity in vivo
Authors: Walter N Harrington, David C Brice, Aaron J Schmitz, Jackson S Turner, Rafael Brent, Philip A Mudd, Ali H Ellebedy, Richard J Webby
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe antibody response to influenza virus infection targets numerous viral proteins, with those specific for the hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) surface glycoproteins being the most extensively studied. Antibodies to HA and NA function as independent immune correlates of protection and form the basis of most vaccine approaches. In contrast, the contribution of antibodies to other viral proteins such as the nucleoprotein (NP) remains unclear, and existing studies have reached conflicting conclusions. In this work, we examined three human monoclonal NP antibodies isolated from plasmablasts of two individuals naturally infected with influenza A virus (IAV) during the 2018–2019 influenza season. These antibodies showed strong binding to NP across a broad range of IAV strains, yet none were able to limit weight loss or mortality when delivered prophylactically to mice. Further characterization demonstrated that the antibodies did not neutralize infection, and although they bound to the surface of infected MDCK cells, they did not induce cellular antiviral responses. While the broad cross-reactivity of these monoclonal antibodies is attractive for next-generation vaccine design, strategies that aim to elicit similar responses must either enhance their antiviral potency or clarify their role in modulating viral replication and disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58084-y.Keywords: Influenza A, Monoclonal antibodies, Nucleoprotein, Non-neutralizing antibodies, Broadly reactive, Broad bindingSubject terms: Immunology, Microbiology

Introduction

The WHO estimates that between 290,000 and 650,000 people die from influenza globally each year1. In the US alone, it is estimated that influenza causes 140,000–810,000 hospitalizations and 12,000–61,000 deaths annually2. Beyond seasonal influenza, the threat of an influenza pandemic is ever present, a threat that has been realized four times in the past 100 years3. Vaccination is currently the best strategy for mitigating the effects of both seasonal and pandemic influenza. Influenza vaccines traditionally target the hemagglutinin (HA) protein present on the membrane of the virion which is responsible for receptor engagement and membrane fusion during viral entry into the cell4. The HA protein is also the primary target for neutralizing antibodies generated by the immune system5. Both natural immunity and vaccination place a high selection pressure on the HA protein, causing it to mutate frequently sometimes impacting vaccine effectiveness6.

Currently, influenza vaccine antigens are assessed twice annually and updated as needed to account for antigenic drift of the HA. Beyond antigenic drift, influenza viruses can also undergo antigenic shifts, which are major antigenic changes when separate influenza strains infect the same host concurrently7,8. Antigenic shifts have been responsible for prior influenza pandemics9. Both antigenic drift and antigenic shift pose significant barriers to improving influenza vaccine approaches, particularly those that hope to provide more broadly reactive immunity8,10,11. Because of this, there has been interest in making vaccines that target other influenza proteins that are less variable than HA. The viral nucleoprotein (NP) is one such target12–16.

Though it has been known for some time that influenza-infected cells can express influenza nucleoprotein on their membrane17 and that the human immune system makes antibodies against influenza NP after an infection18, the role that these antibodies play in our immune response requires further exploration. The relatively conserved nature of NP across different influenza viruses suggests broad cross-protection and therapeutic application should these antibodies play a protective role during infection13. One group has shown broad cross-reactivity of a human monoclonal NP antibody across a variety of H1 and H3 subtype viruses in vitro19. Yet, neutralization assays indicate that the NP antibodies are infection-permissive, and these in vitro studies have not been able to determine their immunological significance.

In vivo studies using transgenic mice constitutively expressing a human A(H5N1) monoclonal NP antibody showed that these antibodies had a protective effect against lethal A(H5N1) and A(H1N1) virus infection14. While providing some hope for NP vaccine approaches, it is unclear how important the artificial intracellular expression of the NP antibodies in this study was as intracellular antibody against NP has been shown to block nuclear import of ribonucleoproteins20, but antibodies that could potentially inhibit this process are not naturally imported into cells at the levels displayed in that animal model.

Several studies have shown that NP vaccination in mice can be effective in protecting against lethal infection12–16. However, passive transfer studies using sera from infected mice or rabbits have shown that NP antibodies are not sufficient to offer protection from infection in naive mice21–23. Several studies have indicated that T cell-mediated responses are the dominate driver of NP vaccination protection13,15,24–26. More recently, Carragher et. al. have argued that although T-cell dependent immunity certainly plays a large role in NP vaccination, NP antibodies themselves have a role to play in immunity and protection, as demonstrated by serum from mice immunized with recombinant NP conferring passive protection in naive mice challenged with a sub-lethal dose of A/Puerto Rico/8/34 (H1N1) (PR8)27. LaMere et. al. have similarly shown that passive transfer of NP-immune serum can reduce lung titers on day 8 post infection if given daily, beginning three days before challenge through the first day post challenge to achieve donor level concentration of NP antibodies by the time of challenge28.

Both passive immunity studies used serum from mice immunized with recombinant NP protein. Further study is warranted to determine the protective role of human NP antibodies in the context of natural infection. Here, we characterize three monoclonal NP antibodies derived from patients with acute IAV infection, labeled A9, E4, and C6, to determine their ability to bind, neutralize, and protect against IAVs in vitro and in vivo.

Methods

Patients

Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were collected from two individuals enrolled in a prospective observational cohort study at the Barnes Jewish Hospital Emergency Department (EDFLU study) during the 2018–2019 influenza season29. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Washington University in Saint Louis (Missouri, USA) approved the EDFLU study (approval #201710220). Informed consent was obtained for all patients and all methods were performed in accordance with the guidelines set by the IRB under this study. Subject 1819004 was 65 years old, female, and had a history of chronic lung disease. She was infected with PCR-confirmed H1N1 IAV and presented on the 13th day of symptomatic illness when the PBMC sample was obtained. She had received the 2018–2019 seasonal influenza vaccine prior to disease onset. Subject 1819020 was 45 years old, male, and had no known health problems prior to influenza infection. He was infected with PCR-confirmed IAV and presented on the 7th day of symptomatic illness when the PBMC sample was obtained. He had not received the 2018–2019 seasonal influenza vaccine prior to disease onset.

PBMC isolation

Blood was collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-anticoagulated sample tubes using standard phlebotomy techniques. PBMC were prepared within 8 h of collection by layering over Ficoll and centrifuging at 400g for 30 min. The PBMC layer at the Ficoll interface was collected, washed with 1 × PBS and re-suspended in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 media. Cell counts were obtained, and cells were cryogenically preserved in RPMI-1640 media supplemented with 10% dimethyl sulfoxide and 40% fetal bovine serum (FBS).

Monoclonal antibodies from plasmablasts were expressed without respect to antigen specificity. The antibodies were then screened against recombinant NP from A/Brisbane/10/2007 and B/Brisbane/60/2008 by ELISA. Antibodies A9, E4, and C6 all bound NP from A/Brisbane/10/2007 but not B/Brisbane/60/2008 (Supplemental Fig. 3).

Cell sorting

Staining for sorting was performed using cryo-preserved PBMCs resuspended in PBS supplemented with 2% FBS and 1mM EDTA. Cells were stained for 30 min at 4 °C with CD71-FITC (clone CY1G4), IgD-PerCP-Cy5.5 (clone IA6-2), CD19-PE (clone HIB19), CD4-Alexa 700 (clone SK3), CD20-Pacific Blue (clone 2H7), CD38-BV605 (clone HIT2), and Zombie NIR; all BioLegend. Cells were then washed twice and single plasmablasts (live singlet CD19+ CD4– IgDlo CD20low CD38+ CD71+) were sorted using a FACSAria II (Becton–Dickinson) into 96-well plates containing 2 µL 10 × Single cell lysis buffer (Clontech) supplemented with 1 U/µL RNase inhibitor (Promega) and immediately frozen on dry ice.

Monoclonal antibody generation

Antibodies were cloned as previously described30. In brief, VH, Vλ, and Vκ genes were amplified by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and nested PCR reactions from single-sorted plasmablasts using cocktails of primers specific for IgG, Igλ, and Igκ detailed in31 and then sequenced. To generate recombinant antibodies, PCR was performed with variable and junction gene primers containing short extensions to match the cloning site of the antibody expression vectors (overlap extension PCR) and the amplified fragments were cloned by Gibson assembly, as previously described31–33. Heavy and light chain plasmids were co-transfected into Expi293F cells (Gibco) for expression and antibody was purified with protein A agarose (Invitrogen).

ELISA binding assay

Whole virus binding ELISAs were used to determine the relative binding strength of each NP mAb for a broad spectrum of influenza virus subtypes. The following viruses were used to determine NP binding: A/Brisbane/59/2007 (H1N1), A/Michigan/45/2015 (H1N1), A/Puerto Rico/8/34 (H1N1), A/Ruddy Turnstone/DE/300/2009 (H1N1), A/Swine/Italy/768/88 (H1N1), A/Hong Kong/1/68 (H3N2), A/Hong Kong/4801/2014 (H3N2), A/Swine/TX/4199-2/98 (H3N2), A/Equine/CA/8560/2002 (H3N8), A/Egypt/MOH-NRC-7305/2014 (H5N1), A/Hong Kong/156/97 (H5N1), A/Grey-Headed Gull/Uganda/200144/2017 (H5N8), A/Franklin’s Gull/DE/42/2006 (H7N3), A/Guangzhou/17SF003RG/2017 (H7N9), A/Bangladesh/0994/2011 (H9N2). These viruses had previously been grown in embryonated chicken eggs in large volumes and then inactivated, concentrated and purified to make inactivated whole virus stocks.

To standardize the amount of virus used, a Bradford assay was performed to determine the concentration of the purified whole virus stocks. ELISA plates were coated with 100 uL of 5 ug/mL whole virus in PBS and allowed to incubate at 4 °C overnight. The following day, the plates were washed with PBS and blocked with PBS containing 1% BSA and 0.05% Tween-20. After blocking, plates were washed with PBS and dilutions of each of the NP mAbs were added to corresponding columns of the plate, starting at 1 ug/mL. Plates were again incubated at 4 °C overnight. Plates were washed with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 and a secondary antibody conjugated to HRP targeted to human IgG was then applied. Plates were incubated at room temperature for 1 h and washed with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20. 100 uL of TMB was added to each well and allowed to develop for 2.5–5 min before adding H2SO4 to stop the reaction. Absorbance was measured at 450nm and data were analyzed and graphed in Excel and Prism. Half maximal effective concentration (EC50) values were calculated in Prism via non-linear regression curve fitting as a measure of binding strength.

Competitive ELISA

Competitive ELISA assays were performed to assess epitope overlap between human anti-NP monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and a panel of previously characterized mouse anti-NP mAbs34. High-binding 96-well plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with purified influenza A virus nucleoprotein (NP) at 2 µg/mL in coating buffer. Plates were washed with PBS and blocked with blocking buffer (1% BSA, 0.01% Tween-20) for one hour at room temperature.

Mouse mAbs (M1–M5; clones 3/1, 5/1, 7/3, 150/4, and 469/7, respectively) were diluted in blocking buffer to approximately EC70 using starting dilutions of 1:31,250 (M1), 1:156,250 (M2), and 1:250 (M3–M5), as determined by prior titration (Supplementary Fig. 4). Human anti-NP mAbs A9 and C6 were diluted starting at 1:5,000 from stock concentrations of 13.1 mg/mL and 10.5 mg/mL, respectively, whereas E4 was used at a substantially lower initial dilution of 1:10 from a stock concentration of 18.1 mg/mL. All human mAbs were then serially diluted 1:5. Each mouse mAb was mixed with serial dilutions of human mAbs prior to addition to NP-coated wells and incubated for 1.5h at room temperature to allow competitive binding. Wells containing mouse mAb without human antibody served as controls. Human mAb only wells were also used as controls for cross-reactivity.

Following incubation, plates were washed thoroughly, and bound mouse IgG was detected using a species-specific HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. Signal development was performed using a chromogenic substrate (e.g., TMB), and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Reductions in signal relative to control were interpreted as evidence of competition, suggesting overlapping or spatially proximal epitopes.

Mouse infection

All mouse experiments were approved by the St. Jude animal care and use committee (IACUC, protocol 428) and were performed in accordance with the guidelines set by this committee. Mice that reached predefined humane endpoints (30% weight loss relative to day of challenge) or survived to the study endpoint were euthanized by CO2 inhalation in accordance with institutional guidelines, followed by cervical dislocation as a secondary physical method to ensure death. To determine the in vivo protective potential of each of the three NP mAbs, passive immunity studies were conducted in 6–8-week-old female BALB/c mice (Charles River). Five mice per group were used for all groups. Mice were given 200uL intraperitoneal injections of A6, E4, C6 NP mAbs or control (anti-H1 and anti-H3 produced in goats, and polyclonal anti-NP produced in mice) antibodies at various concentration 24h prior to challenge. Mice were challenged with 1LD50 A/California/04/09pdm (mouse-adapted)35. Mice were followed for 14 days with daily weight and survival measurements. Surviving mice were sacrificed on day 14 or 21. Polyclonal NP antibodies were generated by vaccinating mice intramuscularly with 10ug recombinant NP protein (A/California/04/09 (H1N1)pdm09) combined with Freund’s complete adjuvant, boosting on day 14 with Freund’s incomplete adjuvant and followed by a terminal blood draw on day 35 and serum separation. The serum was then purified and concentrated using a protein G chromatography kit (Pierce, cat. No. 89926) before use in passive immunity studies.

Microneutralization assay

To test the neutralizing capacity of each human NP mAb, four viruses were used: A/California/7/2009 (H1N1), A/Victoria/210/2009 (H3N2), A/Netherlands/10260/2018 (H3N2), and B/Brisbane/60/2008. Ferret anti-sera specific for each virus was used as a positive control. Two separate dilutions of human mAbs were tested (5 mg/mL and 50 mg/mL). A dilution series of each mAb was made (with two different starting points, 50 mg/mL and 5 mg/mL). A positive control dilution series using ferret anti-sera was also made. Each dilution series was then mixed with 100 TCID50 of each virus and allowed to incubate for 1 h. 96-well plates were seeded the night before with MDCK cells (ATCC) in growth media made up of Minimum Essential Medium (MEM, Gibco) supplemented with antibiotics, vitamins, glutamine, and fetal calf sera (FCS). The cells were checked for confluency the next day, and then then infected with each mixture in infection media (identical to growth media, except FCS was replaced with Bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma-Aldrich), washed with PBS then replaced with new infection media plus TPCK-trypsin (1ug/mL) after 1 h of infection, and allowed to incubate for 3 days. After incubation, hemagglutination assays (HAs) were run for each sample to determine infection status.

In vitro cell surface binding

MDCK cells (ATCC) were seeded in 24-well plates containing glass coverslips at a density to reach ~ 80–90% confluency overnight. The cells were then infected with PR8 at an MOI of ~ 0.1 and allowed to incubate overnight. Negative control uninfected wells were included. A hemagglutination assay was performed to validate the infection. Infected and non-infected live cells were labeled with each of the NP mAbs, A9, E4, C6. Briefly, the cultured MDCK cells were washed with PBS and blocked with 3% BSA in PBS for 5min and cooled to 4 °C. After cooling, the NP mAbs were added to the respective wells at a concentration of 1 ug/mL and incubated at 4 °C for 30min. The cells were then washed three times with PBS and incubated with goat α-human IgG conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen A11013) at 4 °C for 60 min. The cells were again washed three times with PBS and fixed in 3.7% formaldehyde at RT for 15 min. After fixation, the coverslips containing the fixed cells were washed in PBS and mounted on slides using Diamond Antifade with DAPI (ProLong) for fluorescent imaging. Imaging was performed on a Nikon widefield microscope and image processing was completed using NIS and ImageJ.

ADCC assay

Antibody-dependent cellular toxicity (ADCC) assays were completed using an ADCC reporter bioassay kit (Promega) as described by the manufacturer, with slight modifications to incorporate influenza NP on the target cells. Briefly, 96-well white opaque plates were seeded with 5,000 MDCK cells, leaving empty rows open for positive control cells supplied in the ADCC kit, and incubated overnight. On the following day, the control antibody was diluted in a 2.5-fold serial dilution and the sample antibodies (A9, E4, C6 NP mAbs) were diluted in a threefold serial dilution, per the kit manufacturer’s specifications. Control target cells were plated in the control wells, and each antibody dilution was added to the corresponding wells in the plate. Effector cells were then added to each well and the plates were incubated at 37°C for 6 h. Luciferase reagent was added after incubation and the plates were measured for bioluminescence. Data analysis was completed using Excel and Prism.

Results

Breadth of NP antibody binding

ELISAs were performed to determine the breadth of binding of each of the monoclonal antibodies across a wide range of IAV (Supplementary Fig. 1) selected to represent phylogenetically distinct NPs: A/Michigan/45/2015 (H1N1), A/Ruddy Turnstone/Delaware/300/2009 (H1N1), A/Brisbane/59/2007 (H1N1), A/Swine/Italy/768/88 (H1N1), A/PuertoRico/8/34 (H1N1), A/California/04/09 (H1N1)pdm09 (mouse adapted), A/Hong Kong/4801/2014 (H3N2), A/Swine/Texas/4199-2/98 (H3N2), A/Hong Kong/1/68 (H3N2), A/Equine/California/8560/2002 (H3N8), A/Egypt/MOH-NRC-7305/2014 (H5N1), A/Hong Kong/156/97 (H5N1), A/Grey-Headed Gull/Uganda/200144/2017 (H5N8), A/Franklin’s Gull/Delaware/42/2006 (H7N3), A/Guangzhou/17SF003RG/2017 (H7N9), and A/Bangladesh/0994/2011 (H9N2). Abbreviations for these viruses can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

EC50 values derived from the normalized ELISA data (Table 1) indicate that all three monoclonal antibodies bind broadly across multiple IAV subtypes, though with notable differences in binding strength. C6 exhibits the widest binding breadth, showing moderate to strong affinity for most strains tested, with reduced binding to Bang/11, TR/DE/09 (H1N1), Mich/15 (H1N1), and PR8 (H1N1), and no detectable binding to Eq/CA/02 (H3N8). A9 demonstrates generally moderate binding across many viruses, with similarly weak binding to TR/DE/09 (H1N1), Mich/15 (H1N1), and PR8 (H1N1), as well as no measurable binding to the equine H3N8 strain. A9 also shows reduced binding to the 2009 pandemic virus (Cal/09pdm). E4 displays the most variable profile, binding strongly to some strains such as HK/68 (H3N2) and HK/97 (H5N1), but showing limited binding to Cal/09pdm, Mich/15 (H1N1), Sw/TX/98 (H3N2), and again no detectable binding to the equine H3N8 virus. Overall, the data show that all three antibodies recognize a broad range of IAV NP proteins, though each differs in sensitivity and in the specific viral lineages where binding is reduced.

Competitive ELISA

Several well-characterized mouse monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), originally designated 3/1, 5/1, 7/3, 150/4, and 469/7 and referred to here as M1–M5, respectively, have previously been described with partially mapped epitopes34. A competitive ELISA was performed using these mAbs to assess whether the human anti-NP mAbs exhibited binding interference, which would suggest overlapping or proximal epitope recognition.

As shown in Fig. 1, binding profiles varied across the mouse mAbs. M1 and M5 displayed minimal competition with all three human mAbs, indicating distinct epitope recognition. In contrast, M3 showed modest inhibition by all three human mAbs, suggesting partial overlap at this epitope. M2 exhibited the strongest inhibition, with C6 producing the most pronounced reduction in signal, consistent with substantial competition for binding to NP. M4 showed moderate inhibition, particularly in the presence of A9 and C6, indicating partial epitope similarity.

Mouse antibodies were used at approximately EC70 and incubated with 1:5 serial dilutions of human monoclonal antibodies. Binding to purified NP protein was measured by detecting mouse IgG with a secondary antibody. The dotted line indicates mouse-only controls. A reduction in signal relative to control indicates competition and suggests overlapping or closely positioned epitopes.

NP mAb passive immunity in vivo

Each of the NP monoclonal antibodies were transferred in vivo via passive immunity experiments in BALB/c mice to determine if any of the antibodies could confer some level of protection against challenge with a mouse adapted strain of Cal/09pdm (H1N1). Mice were passively immunized once with either the human mAbs (A9, E4, C6, ~ 20mg/kg), mouse NP mAbs (3/1, 5/1, 7/3, 150/4, 469/7, ~ 5mg/kg), sera from mice immunized with recombinant NP, monoclonal α-H3 antibodies (negative control), or monoclonal α-H1 antibodies (positive control, ~ 5mg/kg). Immunized mice were then challenged the next day with mouse adapted A/California/04/09 (H1N1)pdm09 at a dose of 1LD50. The mice were followed for two weeks. Mice in all experimental groups had substantial weight loss and mortality that was indistinguishable from the negative control group, indicating that the mAbs could not protect mice in vivo (Fig. 2A). Positive control α-H1 mAbs were sufficient to protect mice both from weight loss and death.

Since each mAb alone was not sufficient to confer protection in vivo, we hypothesized that a cocktail of all three antibodies might work synergistically to confer a level of protection against a lethal dose of influenza. Mice were immunized with a cocktail of A9, E4, and C6 mAbs (45mg/kg solution, 15mg/kg for each antibody). α-H1 and α-H3 mAbs were again used as controls. There was no significant difference in weight loss or survival data between the cocktail group and the negative control (Fig. 2B). Positive control α-H1 mAbs were sufficient to protect mice both from infection and death. Together, these data suggest that NP-targeted antibody responses alone, even in a prophylactic setting, are not sufficient for protection against influenza infection.

NP mAb neutralization in vitro

To determine if these antibodies have any effect on neutralization, four viruses, A/California/7/2009 (H1N1), A/Victoria/210/2009 (H3N2), A/Netherlands/10260/2018 (H3N2), and B/Brisbane/60/2008 were used in a microneutralization assay. No inhibition was observed with any of the human NP mAbs on any of the four viruses, whereas the positive control (ferret anti-sera) showed high levels of neutralization. There was no evidence that any of the sample NP mAbs were able to neutralize influenza infection in vitro, suggesting that these antibodies have some other function besides neutralization.

Live cell NP mAb binding

Previous studies have shown evidence that antibodies against influenza NP could bind to the membrane of infected cells19. To determine if the human NP mAbs characterized here could recognize infected cells, live cell antibody labeling was used. PR8 infected and non-infected MDCK cells were incubated with the primary NP mAb (A9, E4, or C6) followed by incubation with a secondary antibody against human IgG conjugated to a fluorescent probe. Labeling was done at 4 °C and prior to fixation to minimize endocytosis and internal labeling of the antibody. Fluorescent imaging showed what appears to be membrane labeling of infected cells by each of the mAbs, compared to no membrane labeling of non-infected cells (Fig. 3). These data suggest that, while these antibodies do not neutralize influenza virus infection, they do bind to the surface of infected cells, potentially priming cellularly mediated antiviral responses.

NP mAb ADCC in vitro

To determine if the NP mAbs might confer protection through a non-neutralizing manner via their observed binding to the surface of infected cells, an antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) assay was performed on cells infected with PR8. None of the NP mAbs showed ADCC activity (Supplementary Fig. 2). This lack of ADCC elicited by the NP mAbs suggests that NP-specific antibodies binding to the surface of infected cells may play only a small role in the overarching immune response to influenza.

Discussion

One of the most challenging aspects for both vaccine and therapeutic strategies against Influenza A (IAV) infection is the rapid evolution of the virus10. The National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID) has laid out a strategic roadmap towards developing a universal influenza vaccine11. Universal vaccine efforts have focused on conserved epitopes of the HA or NA to induce broad protection across influenza subtypes11,36,37. Other IAV proteins have also been targeted for broad immunity, including NP13,16,26. These vaccine studies also inform antibody therapeutics against IAV infection and severe disease, as antibodies that induce a broad level of protection after vaccination would likely have a similar range of protection when used as a therapeutic.

The IAV NP has been considered for both vaccination and passive immunity studies due to its conserved nature13,14,16,25–27. The three monoclonal antibodies characterized in this study indeed showed a broad cross-reactivity across several IAVs from human, avian, and swine host species. This high degree of cross-reactivity suggests that there is no strong selective pressure on the NP to drive antigenic drift, a notion underscored by the lack of neutralizing or cellular cytotoxicity elicited by the NP mAbs generated in this project. This could be either due to lack of natural selection pressure on NP or the structurally conserved nature of the NP protein limiting its ability to change shape. However, the 2009 H1N1 pandemic strain lineage did show strong disruption of binding from E4, which could indicate that there is a non-zero level of selection pressure. Although these monoclonal antibodies were isolated from individuals infected with seasonal H1N1 viruses descended from the 2009 pandemic lineage, the specific NP mutations that have accumulated in the pH1N1 lineage since its emergence, including those present in Mich/15 (H1N1), appear to disrupt key residues within the epitopes recognized by these mAbs. Thus, even though the donors were exposed to viruses of pH1N1 origin, ongoing antigenic drift in NP, while limited, may have been sufficient to alter these epitopes and reduce binding to both the original pandemic virus and its later descendants. Further, CD8 T-cells have been shown to be drivers of antigenic drift38, which may play a role in the differences in binding across strains shown here.

Competitive ELISAs using a panel of previously characterized mouse anti-NP monoclonal antibodies targeting distinct, partially mapped epitopes demonstrated that IAV NP presents multiple accessible antigenic regions capable of eliciting antibody responses, supporting its potential utility in multi-antibody vaccination or therapeutic strategies. Consistent with this, human sera competitive ELISAs also suggested diverse epitope recognition. Limited overlap was observed, with the human mAb C6 showing the most notable competition, particularly with the mouse mAb M2 and to a lesser extent with M3 and M4, indicating that some NP regions may be more commonly targeted. A live cell binding assay showed that the antibodies were able to bind to the membrane of cells infected with A/PR/8/34, suggesting a potential reason for NP antibodies to be produced during IAV infection.

The specific role that NP antibodies play during infection, especially on the question of if they can confer some level of protection analogous to HA or NA antibodies or if they are byproducts of the immune response, remains unclear. The antibodies characterized here demonstrated a strong binding profile but were not able to neutralize infection, nor did they demonstrate ADCC activity, suggesting a more limited role during infection. There are reports in the literature that mAbs against NP can in certain circumstances confer some level of protection against low levels infection, suggesting that these antibodies might play some role in viral clearance27,28. However, these studies used sublethal challenges and mouse NP immunized donor serum, which may be less relevant to the overall human immune response to influenza and clinical infection, especially in the human upper respiratory tract.

Our study instead used human NP mAbs to passively immunize mice that were then challenged with a lethal dose of Cal/09pdm (H1N1) to determine if any of the three mAbs could confer protection. We saw no differences in weight loss or mortality for each individual mAb given at a high concentration (four times higher than that necessary for the positive control) relative to our negative control. Our mAb cocktail similarly did not differ from the negative control. These results indicate that these NP mAb failed to confer in vivo protection against a lethal dose of IAV. While the role that NP antibodies play in infection remains unclear, our results suggest that the immune response to IAV infection may generate NP antibodies largely as a consequence of NP exposure on the membranes of infected cells, rather than as part of a targeted response against intact virions or infected cells. However, other mechanisms could also contribute to the induction of NP-specific B cell responses, including the release of NP-containing vRNPs from dying or lysing influenza virus–infected airway epithelial cells. More studies are needed to tease out the nuances of the role NP antibodies play in infection.
