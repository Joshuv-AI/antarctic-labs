Inverse Problems for Costs and Controls in LQG MFGs via Mean Field Trajectories
Grégoire Lambrecht, Mathieu Laurière

Abstract
This paper investigates inverse problems for Linear-Quadratic-Gaussian (LQG) Mean Field Games (MFGs) based entirely on the observation of mean-covariance trajectories. We address three sequential challenges: identifying the optimal control for observed initializations, determining the control for arbitrary initializations, and recovering consistent cost parameters. After establishing the existence and uniqueness of the forward Nash equilibrium under mild hypotheses, we analyze the injectivity of the parameter-to-trajectory mapping, demonstrating that it is inherently non-injective and providing sufficient conditions for parameter equivalence. We prove that while the optimal control is locally identifiable for observed initializations under minimal assumptions, global identifiability requires a deeper structural recovery of the game’s costs. To bridge this gap, we propose a constructive semidefinite programming method to infer cost parameters that are strictly consistent with the observed population dynamics. Numerical experiments illustrate this method.



1
Introduction

Mean Field Games (MFGs) provide a mathematical framework for analyzing strategic interactions in large populations of agents. Since their introduction by Lasry and Lions
[
16
]
and Huang, Malhamé, and Caines
[
13
]
, they have been extensively studied from both partial differential equation and probabilistic perspectives, with a primary focus on the existence, uniqueness, and numerical computation of Nash equilibria
[
5
,
7
,
3
,
2
,
8
]
.

In many applications, the parameters governing agent behavior are not directly observable. Instead, one typically has access to data in the form of state trajectories or aggregate statistics of the population. This naturally leads to
inverse problems in MFGs
, where the objective is to recover underlying model parameters or optimal strategies from observed equilibria. Such problems arise in areas such as economics, engineering, and data-driven control, yet remain relatively underexplored.

Existing works on inverse MFG problems often rely on strong structural assumptions, such as direct access to the value function
[
18
,
19
,
17
]
. These assumptions may be restrictive in practical settings where only partial information, such as the evolution of the population distribution, is available. This motivates the study of corresponding inverse problems relying primarily on observable quantities.

Closely related studies exist regarding the inverse problem in classical control theory
[
15
]
, as well as its discrete-time stochastic equivalent in the context of maximum entropy inverse reinforcement learning
[
9
]
. Despite these developments in single-agent or finite-player settings, the literature addressing the inverse problem for LQG games remains rather scarce
[
11
,
10
,
14
]
.

In this paper, we consider inverse problems in the class of
Linear-Quadratic-Gaussian (LQG) Mean Field Games
, where the population distribution is fully characterized by its mean and covariance. This class of games has been extensively studied and has found various applications, see e.g.
[
13
,
4
,
6
,
12
]
. The LQG structure allows us to formulate the inverse problem in terms of
mean–covariance trajectories
, which are natural and observable (or approximately observable) quantities in many applications.

We address three fundamental questions:
(i) whether the optimal control is locally identifiable from observed initial conditions,
(ii) whether the optimal control is globally identifiable for arbitrary initializations, and
(iii) whether one can infer cost parameters that are strictly consistent with the observed equilibria.

Our contributions are as follows. First, we establish existence and uniqueness of Nash equilibria under mild conditions in our formulation. Second, we analyze the
injectivity of the mapping
from model parameters to equilibrium trajectories, showing that it is, in general, inherently non-injective. Third, we provide conditions under which the optimal control is locally identifiable from observed trajectories, and we characterize the information required for global identifiability. Finally, we propose a constructive method to recover
consistent cost parameters
by combining identifiable quantities with a semidefinite programming formulation.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the model and formalizes the inverse problems. Section
3
studies existence, uniqueness, and injectivity properties. Section
4
presents the main identifiability results and reconstruction methods. Section
5
provides numerical experiments, and Section
6
concludes.

Dynamics and equilibrium

Let

Definition 1

The space of

\Gamma_{1}=\{(A,B,\sigma)\in({\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n})^{3}:\det(B)\neq 0\}

\Gamma_{2}=\{(R,Q,Q_{T},\bar{Q},\bar{Q}_{T})\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{++}\times({\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{+})^{4}\}

Let

Let
\bar{x}_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

dX_{t}=(AX_{t}+B\alpha_{t})dt+\sigma dW_{t},\quad X_{0}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\bar{x}_{0},\Sigma_{0}).

(1)

The agent seeks to minimize the cost functional:

\begin{split}{\mathcal{J}}(\alpha)={\mathbb{E}}\bigg[&\int_{0}^{T}(X_{t}^{\dagger}QX_{t}+\alpha^{\dagger}_{t}R\alpha_{t}+X_{t}^{\dagger}\bar{Q}\bar{x}_{t})dt\\
&+X^{\dagger}_{T}Q_{T}X_{T}+X_{T}^{\dagger}\bar{Q}_{T}\bar{x}_{T}\bigg].\end{split}

(2)

where

denotes the mean state of a population of agents following the same control, evolving according to (

It is established in the literature that the population distribution satisfies a system of coupled partial differential equations, and under our setting, this distribution remains Gaussian. Consequently, the mean and covariance trajectory satisfies a deterministic system of differential equations. For brevity, we define a Nash equilibrium operationally through the forward-backward equation system governing the mean-covariance trajectory. We refer to
[
7

5
]

Definition 2
.

Let

\displaystyle\dot{P}

\displaystyle+PA+A^{\dagger}P-PBR^{-1}B^{\dagger}P+Q=0,

(3)

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=(A-BR^{-1}B^{\dagger}P)\Sigma+\Sigma(A-BR^{-1}B^{\dagger}P)^{\dagger}+2\nu,

(4)

\displaystyle\dot{\bar{x}}

\displaystyle=(A-BR^{-1}B^{\dagger}P)\bar{x}-BR^{-1}B^{\dagger}r,

(5)

\displaystyle-\dot{r}

\displaystyle=(A^{\dagger}-PBR^{-1}B^{\dagger})r+\frac{1}{2}\bar{Q}\bar{x},

(6)

subject to the boundary conditions
P_{T}=Q_{T}
,
\bar{x}_{0}=\bar{x}_{0}
,
\Sigma_{0}=\Sigma_{0}
, and
r_{T}=\frac{1}{2}\bar{Q}_{T}\bar{x}_{T}
.

In Theorem
2
we prove that for any
\gamma\in\Gamma
, there exists a unique tuple
(P,\Sigma,\bar{x},r)
satisfying (
3
)-(

).
The optimal control is given by the feedback policy
\alpha_{t}=\varphi(t,X_{t})
, where
\varphi(t,x)=-R^{-1}B^{\dagger}(P_{t}x_{t}+r_{t})
for each
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, which justifies the next definition.

Definition 3
.

For any admissible tuple of parameters
\gamma\in\Gamma
let us define the
trajectory mapping

\displaystyle\psi_{\gamma}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{++}

\displaystyle\to C^{1}([0,T],{\mathbb{R}}^{n})\times C^{1}([0,T],{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{++})

\displaystyle(\bar{x}_{0},\Sigma_{0})

\displaystyle\mapsto(\bar{x},\Sigma),

where
(\bar{x},\Sigma)
is the Nash equilibrium generated by
\gamma
using initial condition
(\bar{x}_{0},\Sigma_{0})
. Let us define the
control mapping

\displaystyle\varphi_{\gamma}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{+}

\displaystyle\to C^{1}([0,T],{\mathbb{R}}^{n})\times C^{1}([0,T],{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n})

\displaystyle(\bar{x}_{0},\Sigma_{0})

\displaystyle\mapsto(R^{-1}B^{\dagger}r,R^{-1}B^{\dagger}P).

2.2
Objectives

In this work, we focus on inverse problems concerning the observation of Nash equilibrium mean-covariance trajectories. Assuming an observer has access to several such trajectories starting with different initializations, we investigate the a priori information and the specific number of trajectory observations required to address the following queries:

(Q1)

Is it possible to recover the optimal control for the observed initializations?

(Q2)

Is it possible to recover the optimal control for any arbitrary initialization?

(Q3)

Is it possible to recover an admissible tuple
\gamma
that reproduces the observed mean-covariance trajectories?

These three questions are distinct yet interrelated.
(Q1)
is conceptually the most straightforward; here, the observer reconstructs the optimal control evaluated exactly along the observed evolution of the population’s mean and covariance. In contrast,
(Q2)
requires the observer to reconstruct the optimal control mapping comprehensively, allowing evaluation at new initializations where the macroscopic trajectory is not explicitly given. For initializations distant from the observed data, the observer risks lacking sufficient local information to infer the underlying structure. While
(Q3)
pursues a deeper structural recovery than
(Q2)
, both overarching questions pose significant analytical challenges regarding parameter identifiability.

We formalize the inverse problems with the following definitions.

Definition 4
.

Let
\gamma\in\Gamma
and a set of
k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
initial conditions
{\mathcal{I}}=\{(\bar{x}_{0}^{i},\Sigma_{0}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{k}
be given. The
observation operator

\psi_{\gamma}({\mathcal{I}})
is the mapping that assigns to
\gamma
the corresponding mean-covariance trajectories:

\psi_{\gamma}({\mathcal{I}}):=\left(\psi_{\gamma}(\bar{x}^{i}_{0},\Sigma^{i}_{0})\right)_{i=1,\dots,k}

(7)

Definition 5
.

Let
\hat{\Gamma}\subset\Gamma
. We say that the optimal control is
locally identifiable
over
\hat{\Gamma}
from the observations
\psi({\mathcal{I}})
if there exists a mapping
F
such that for every
\gamma\in\hat{\Gamma}
and
(\bar{x}_{0},\Sigma_{0})\in{\mathcal{I}}
:

\varphi_{\gamma}(\bar{x}_{0},\Sigma_{0})=F\big(\gamma^{1},\psi_{\gamma}({\mathcal{I}})\big).

(8)

We say that the optimal control is
globally identifiable
over
\hat{\Gamma}
from the observations
\psi({\mathcal{I}})
if there exists a mapping
F
such that for every
\gamma\in\hat{\Gamma}
and
(\bar{x}_{0},\Sigma_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{+}
:

\varphi_{\gamma}(\bar{x}_{0},\Sigma_{0})=F\big(\gamma^{1},\psi_{\gamma}({\mathcal{I}})\big).

(9)

Definition 6
.

Let
\eta:\Gamma\to{\mathbb{R}}^{M}
be a quantity of interest for some
M\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
and
\hat{\Gamma}\subset\Gamma
. We say that
\eta
is
identifiable
with respect to the observations
\psi({\mathcal{I}})
over
\hat{\Gamma}
if there exists a mapping
F
such that for every
\gamma\in\hat{\Gamma}
:

\eta(\gamma)=F\big(\gamma^{1},\psi_{\gamma}({\mathcal{I}})\big)

(10)

Definition 7
.

Let
\gamma^{1}\in\Gamma^{1}
and
\gamma^{2},\gamma^{2^{\prime}}\in\Gamma^{2}
. We say that
\gamma^{2}
and
\gamma^{2^{\prime}}
are
consistent with respect to

\gamma^{1}
, if and only if
\psi_{(\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2})}=\psi_{(\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2^{\prime}})}
.

Before addressing
(Q1)
-
(Q3)
, we establish the mathematical well-posedness of the forward Nash equilibrium and subsequently analyze the injectivity of the trajectory mapping
\psi
.

3
Existence, uniqueness and injectivity

3.1
Existence and uniqueness of Nash equilibrium

Because our primary focus is the inverse problem, we do not aim for maximal generality in establishing conditions for the system of equations. The system decomposes into two coupled Riccati-type boundary value problems ((
3
), (
5
)–(
6
)) and one Sylvester equation (
4
). We refer to
[
1
]
for a comprehensive treatment of such equations, and we recall two crucial theorems from this text in Appendix

We now state the existence and uniqueness of the Nash equilibrium.

Theorem 1
.

There exists a unique solution
P\in C^{1}([0,T],{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{n})
to (
3
) satisfying the terminal condition
P_{T}=Q_{T}
.

Proof.

We refer to Theorem
7

BR^{-1}B^{\dagger}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{+}
,
Q\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{+}
, and
Q_{T}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{+}

by the definition of

Since (

The coupled system (

Theorem 2

For any

Proof.

We rely on Theorem
8
in the appendix, which states that if the Riccati equation

\begin{split}\dot{Z}_{t}=&-BR^{-1}B^{\dagger}+(A-BR^{-1}B^{\dagger}P_{t})Z_{t}\\
&+Z_{t}(A^{\dagger}-P_{t}BR^{-1}B^{\dagger})+\frac{1}{2}Z_{t}\bar{Q}Z_{t}\end{split}

(11)

with

\dot{V}_{t}=BR^{-1}B^{\dagger}-U_{t}V_{t}-V_{t}U_{t}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}V_{t}\bar{Q}V_{t},

(12)

with
V_{T}=0

U_{t}=(A-BR^{-1}B^{\dagger}P_{T-t})

to the dynamics of

BR^{-1}B^{\dagger}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{+}
and
\bar{Q}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{+}
.

The theorem further implies
-V_{t}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{+}

V_{t}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{-}
. Defining
Z_{t}=V_{T-t}
for

,
Z
satisfies Equation (
11

\det(\frac{1}{2}\bar{Q}_{T}Z_{T}-I_{n})\neq 0
. This holds because
\bar{Q}_{T}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{+}
and
Z_{T}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{-}
, implying
\bar{Q}_{T}Z_{T}
has non-positive eigenvalues, which ensures that
(\frac{1}{2}\bar{Q}_{T}Z_{T}-I_{n})
has eigenvalues less than or equal to

∎

We conclude with a lemma bridging the initial and terminal states.
Intuitively, the terminal position
\bar{x}_{T}
encodes information regarding the terminal cost matrix
Q_{T}
. The following lemma confirms that observing trajectories spanning a basis of initial states prevents degenerate terminal states.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\gamma\in\Gamma
.

1.

The mapping
\bar{x}_{0}\mapsto\bar{x}_{T}
is linear, invertible, and independent of the initialization
\Sigma_{0}
.

2.

In particular, for any basis
(\bar{x}_{0}^{1},\dots,\bar{x}_{0}^{n})
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and any
\Sigma_{0}^{1},\dots,\Sigma^{n}_{0}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{++}
, the terminal mean values
(\bar{x}^{1}_{T},\dots,\bar{x}_{T}^{n})
form a basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Proof.

The solution
P
of Equation (
3
) is independent of the initialization
(\bar{x}_{0},\Sigma_{0})
. Building on the proof of Theorem
2
, one can show that:

\bar{x}_{t}=Z_{t}r_{t}+\Phi_{t}\bar{x}_{0}

where
\Phi_{t}
is the unique fundamental matrix satisfying
\dot{\Phi}_{t}=(\frac{1}{2}Z_{t}\bar{Q}+A-BR^{-1}B^{\dagger}P_{t})\Phi_{t}
with
\Phi_{0}=I_{n}
.
At the terminal time
T
, and using
r_{T}=\frac{1}{2}\bar{Q}_{T}\bar{x}_{T}
, this yields:

(I_{n}-\frac{1}{2}Z_{T}\bar{Q}_{T})\bar{x}_{T}=\Phi_{T}\bar{x}_{0}.

Since
\bar{Q}_{T}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{+}
and
Z_{T}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{-}
, the matrix
(I_{n}-\frac{1}{2}Z_{T}\bar{Q}_{T})
has positive eigenvalues and is invertible. Thus:

\bar{x}_{T}=(I_{n}-\frac{1}{2}Z_{T}\bar{Q}_{T})^{-1}\Phi_{T}\bar{x}_{0}.

The transition matrix

is clearly invertible, making the mapping
\bar{x}_{0}\mapsto\bar{x}_{T}
a composition of invertible linear operators.
∎

3.2
Injectivity of the trajectory mapping

From the viewpoint of inverse problems, a fundamental question is the following:
Is it possible to find two distinct tuples of parameters
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma
, with
\gamma\neq\gamma^{\prime}
, such that the generated Nash equilibria are identical?

We answer this question positively by showing that for any tuple of parameters
\gamma=(A,B,R,Q,Q_{T},\bar{Q},\bar{Q}_{T})
, there exists
R^{\prime}
such that
\gamma^{\prime}=(A,I_{n},R^{\prime},Q,Q_{T},\bar{Q},\bar{Q}_{T})
yields the same Nash equilibrium trajectories as
\gamma.

In the remainder of this section, we assume
the dynamics parameters
\gamma_{1}\in\Gamma_{1}
are fixed and known
.

3.2.1
Control parameter

The matrix
B
dictates the control’s authority over the state space. When
B
is invertible, the agent possesses full actuation capability. We establish an exact equivalence between the original game and a normalized problem where
(B,R)
is replaced by
B^{\prime}=I_{n}
and
R^{\prime}=(B^{\dagger})^{-1}RB^{-1}
, preserving the resulting mean-covariance trajectory.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\gamma^{1}=(A,B,\sigma,R,Q,Q_{T},\bar{Q},\bar{Q}_{T})\in\Gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}=(A,I_{n},\sigma,R^{\prime},Q,Q_{T},\bar{Q},\bar{Q}_{T})\in\Gamma^{2}
with
R^{\prime}=(B^{\dagger})^{-1}RB^{-1}
. We have
\psi_{\gamma}=\psi_{\gamma^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Since
BR^{-1}B^{\dagger}=I_{n}R^{\prime-1}I_{n}
, the Riccati system (
3
)–(
6
) induced by
\gamma^{\prime}
is exactly the same as the one induced by
\gamma
.
∎

3.2.2
Cost parameters

Assuming

Conclusion

In this paper, we established the existence and uniqueness of the Nash equilibrium for the Linear-Quadratic-Gaussian (LQG) Mean Field Game (MFG) framework. We demonstrated that the mapping from admissible parameters to equilibrium mean-covariance trajectories is not injective, implying that distinct parameter sets can lead to identical equilibria. Our analysis addressed three key objectives:

First, we showed that by observing mean-covariance trajectories from sufficiently diverse initializations, it is possible to identify the optimal control for those specific initializations. Second, we demonstrated that generalizing this control to unobserved initializations is not straightforward. We showed that this generalization is achievable by addressing our third objective: the recovery of consistent cost parameters. We proved that these parameters can be identified by first extracting crucial identifiable quantities and subsequently solving a Semi-Definite Programming (SDP) problem.

Our numerical experiments support the theoretical findings. In particular, they show that the proposed method accurately reconstructs mean–covariance trajectories and associated controls, even though the recovered cost parameters may differ significantly from the ground truth. This confirms that consistency with observed equilibria, rather than exact parameter recovery, is the appropriate objective in this setting. Simultaneously, the experiments reveal important numerical challenges, notably the sensitivity of certain identification steps to derivative estimation and matrix inversion.

These observations point to a clear direction for future work. A key challenge is to extend the proposed framework to noisy and partially observed data, where trajectories are corrupted by estimation errors or sampling effects. It is crucial to address this setting, as it reflects practical real-world scenarios.
