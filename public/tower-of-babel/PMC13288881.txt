Perspectives on the injured child’s hospital discharge process and opportunities for improvement: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Emsea Akers, Shanthi Ameratunga, Laura Wilkinson-Meyers, Malavika Adams, Braden Te Ao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionUnintentional injury is a leading cause of hospitalisation and disability in children globally. Families may experience a second crisis of injury after the child’s discharge from the hospital, with the traumatic nature of injuries affecting physical and psychosocial recovery. This scoping review aims to summarise the literature on the perspectives of injured children’s caregivers and service providers on the hospital discharge process, the challenges and opportunities to improve discharge planning evident from qualitative and interventional studies, and the investigation of inequities in these studies using the Conceptual Framework of Access to healthcare proposed by Levesque et al in 2013.Methods and analysisA scoping review will be conducted involving a search across four databases: CINAHL Plus, Cochrane Library, Embase and Ovid MEDLINE. Empirical literature published in English between 2000 and 2025 will be eligible for inclusion. Included studies will report caregiver or service provider perspectives on current hospital discharge practice for injured children under 18 years old, investigated a priori as a study objective or a posteriori as a finding, or will report the design, implementation or evaluation of interventions intended to improve hospital discharge for injured children. Levesque et al’s framework will be used to guide data extraction from the selected studies into predefined data charting forms, informing the review questions. Findings will be presented narratively and in tables to highlight the key characteristics of the studies, the perspectives of caregivers and service providers, the challenges and opportunities to improve hospital discharge, the proposed discharge interventions, the investigation of inequities and the gaps in the literature.Ethics and disseminationThe scoping review only uses data in the public domain and does not require ethics approval.Keywords: Caregivers, Health Services Accessibility, Hospital to Home Transition, PAEDIATRICS, Trauma

Introduction

Unintentional injury is a leading cause of death, disability and hospitalisation in children and adolescents globally.1 Road traffic accidents are the primary cause of death in adolescents and are among the top 15 contributors to disability-adjusted life years in children under 15 years old, along with falls.1 In lower-middle-income countries, around 50% of children under 12 years old who present to the emergency department with an unintentional injury acquire a disability, whether permanent or temporary.1 Globally, the most common types of injuries in children resulting in hospital admission are traumatic brain injury, followed by wounds, poisoning, arm fractures and burns.1 In Australia, injury results in 14% of all hospital admissions for adolescents, costing hospitals $A212 million each year.2 In Aotearoa New Zealand between 2010 and 2015, injury was the fifth highest cause of hospitalisation in children under 5 years old, the leading cause of hospitalisation in children between 5 and 14 years old, and the second highest cause of hospitalisation in adolescents over 15 years old.3 Around 90% of injuries in children are unintentional,1 4 and while self-inflicted injuries and those caused by abuse or assault are a serious public health concern, they differ considerably from unintentional injuries in terms of what is needed for adequate and appropriate hospital discharge planning and how they impact recovery.

Due to the significant access barriers to primary care services evident in many countries, reflected by increased costs, longer wait times and fragmented care, the period of hospital presentation or admission for the injured child presents a critical, yet transient, opportunity to set the child on an optimal trajectory for recovery and well-being post-discharge and prevent avoidable rehospitalisation, in the absence of guaranteed transitions of care of the injured child back to their primary care physician.5 6 The transition from hospital to home is sometimes referred to as the second crisis of injury, where the child’s family is still experiencing shock and may be emotionally distressed, suddenly reassuming the role of primary caregiver of the child after discharge, now with new health needs and routines, and perhaps adjusting to the impacts of functional impairment and psychological symptoms of trauma.7 8

Discharge planning is the process of preparing the patient and their caregivers for a transition back to the community or between services, and is commonly initiated on hospital presentation or once the patient’s acute condition has been stabilised, with interdisciplinary processes ongoing until discharge and beyond.9 10 Discharge planning for injured children may comprise a myriad of activities including caregiver education on managing the child’s condition at home, identifying red flag symptoms and seeking medical attention when necessary.9 11 12 Other discharge planning processes may include hospital providers making referrals to other services, including community-based services, organising follow-up appointments, updating prescriptions, coordinating the provision of medical technologies such as devices or equipment, and facilitating families’ access to financial support or psychosocial services.13 14

The Conceptual Framework of Access to Health Care developed by Levesque et al in 201315 will be used in this scoping review to conceptualise caregiver and service provider perspectives, the challenges and opportunities to improve hospital discharge, and the inequities in these processes, providing a frame to compare the perspectives of caregivers, service providers and researchers on hospital discharge planning for paediatric unintentional injury. The five dimensions of approachability, acceptability, availability and accommodation, affordability and appropriateness, and the corresponding five abilities of persons to access services, demonstrate how the design and constraints of the health system and services may support and impede the promotion of healthy populations, considering patients’ broader cultural and psychosocial contexts.15 Levesque et al’s framework is particularly useful for considering interactions between systems, organisations, providers and patients in determining access to and through services, as well as the achievement of good health outcomes.15 The views of key stakeholders, such as caregivers and service providers, are pertinent to improving hospital discharge planning, and dimensions of access capture most experiences of service utilisation and provision; where the views of researchers, evident in particular through their interventions, also present an opportunity to understand the problems and priorities highlighted by different groups.16 17

Health inequities refer to unfair and unjust conditions within and across groups or populations in which groups have variable access to the social determinants of health, opportunities for good health, and, therefore, also disparate health outcomes.18 This scoping review will document the causes, types, descriptions and consideration of inequities evident in the literature, also using Levesque et al’s framework to support the conceptualisation of health inequities, as reflected in patterns of differential access, utilisation and experiences of healthcare services.15 Globally, unintentional injuries in children pose a significant burden on health systems and families; however, low- and middle-income countries are disproportionately affected, experiencing higher rates and severity of injuries and accounting for 95% of all child deaths from injury.1 The burden of unintentional injury in children is also disproportionately distributed within countries, with injury rates, severity, service utilisation, mortality and broader impacts varying by ethnicity, geographic location and socioeconomic status.19,22 Māori children in New Zealand have the highest rate of emergency department presentations with an unintentional injury and are 1.5 times more likely to be admitted to hospital than non-Māori and non-Pasifika children; they also have the lowest rate of claims for New Zealand’s public no-fault accident insurance scheme, Accident Compensation Corporation.23 This scoping review will map the inequities reported by caregivers or service providers or considered by researchers, including in the development and evaluation of interventions, given that the benefits of services and interventions may not be equally experienced.19 24

There is a considerable amount of literature quantifying the problem of paediatric unintentional injury,1 20 23 exploring prevention,125,28 clinical management practices29,31 and the impacts of injury on families,32,34 particularly around traumatic brain injury.35,39 The gaps this scoping review aims to address include understanding stakeholder perspectives on discharge planning for paediatric unintentional injury; the challenges and opportunities to improve discharge planning and the corresponding interventions proposed in the literature; and the inequities in outcomes. A preliminary search of Google Scholar, Ovid MEDLINE and PROSPERO found no existing protocols or reviews published on these topics. A scoping review has been proposed as this methodology enables the authors to develop an understanding of the wider context surrounding an understudied topic (particularly as discharge planning is a broad process and not a single intervention), map the key methods and findings from a broad range of study designs, summarise evidence following rigorous data charting in relation to review questions and identify gaps in the literature.40,42

Aims and objectives

The aims of this scoping review are to develop an understanding of how the current literature approaches critiques of hospital discharge planning for paediatric unintentional injury, and to compare the perspectives of caregivers, service providers, and researchers on the main problems and priorities surrounding this topic. The objectives of this scoping review are to map the variable methods and designs of studies on this topic and their conceptualisation and framing of discharge planning, to summarise caregiver and service provider perspectives on hospital discharge for the injured child and the challenges and opportunities to improve discharge planning using Levesque et al’s framework,15 to map the discharge planning interventions proposed by authors or study participants, and to identify gaps in the literature and any observed inequities or equity considerations described in the studies.

Review questions

This scoping review has four review questions. The search strategy has been developed around the first two review questions, for which qualitative and interventional studies will be eligible for inclusion if they provide evidence to inform either or both of these questions. The third and fourth review questions will be informed by the literature identified for the first two review questions.

The four review questions are as follows:

What are the perspectives of caregivers and service providers on hospital discharge planning for paediatric unintentional injury in current practice?

Methods and analysis

This scoping review will be conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist,43 and the scoping review guidelines presented by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) in the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis.41 42 The scoping review framework described by Arksey and O’Malley40 will also reinforce the methodological approach behind the development of the search strategy and data charting forms in the context of the review questions, as well as the rigorous process for extracting data and presenting the results on an emerging topic with no prior information synthesis publications.44 The Population, Concept and Context (PCC) framework presented in the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis is recommended to define the inclusion criteria for scoping reviews.41 42

Eligibility criteria

Population/participants

This scoping review is focused on unintentional injury in children under 18 years old at the time of the injury, using the United Nations’ definition of a child.45 Participants may be parents, other family members such as grandparents, formal or informal caregivers, or service providers. Service providers include all those involved with the family during the child’s hospitalisation, as well as hospital managers and community providers who offer their perspectives on discharge planning from hospital to community. Studies that do not report outcomes separately for children and adults will be excluded from this review. Unintentional injury will mainly comprise traumatic injuries, including burns, along with injuries such as non-fatal drowning, foreign body ingestion or impaction and poisoning.46 Studies which explore unintentional injury as well as illness or injuries such as birth injury, organ injury secondary to an illness, or iatrogenic injury will still be eligible for inclusion in this review if we can identify data and findings specific to unintentional injury. For example, studies will be eligible where unintentional injury findings are reported separately from other conditions. Studies will be eligible for inclusion if at least 90% of reported injuries are unintentional or the causes of children’s injuries are unspecified. The likelihood of studies recruiting children with self-inflicted injuries or injuries resulting from abuse or assault is relatively low, and given that these causes are documented in around 10% of all injuries in children, it is reasonable to anticipate that this proportion would not exceed 10% of all injuries in the literature.1 4

Concept

This review will include studies in which hospital discharge planning is defined a priori as a study objective, or in which stakeholder perspectives on discharge planning, or challenges and opportunities to improve discharge planning, emerge as study findings. There is a lack of consensus in the literature on what constitutes best practice in discharge planning for paediatric unintentional injury, as it is sometimes an implicit process that involves a range of providers and formal and informal activities throughout admission.9 In this review, discharge planning may be explicitly defined or implied, and will include all activities to provide the injured child’s caregivers with the knowledge, resources and skills to manage the child’s condition at home, adapt activities and routines, be responsive to red flag symptoms, seek further medical attention when necessary, promote psychosocial well-being and facilitate access to all appropriate services to meet the physical and psychosocial needs of the child and their family.9,14

Context

This review will include studies published in any country, acknowledging the disproportionately high burden of childhood unintentional injury in low- and middle-income countries.1 Studies must address the transition of care from the hospital inpatient ward or unit, or emergency department, to the community, but may be based in an outpatient setting if findings relate to the transition from the hospital to the community.

Search strategy

The search strategy has been developed around the first two review questions and is intended to locate published, peer-reviewed empirical literature. Studies will be eligible for inclusion only if they have a qualitative or interventional design, as these studies are needed to inform the review questions. Given the broad nature of the second review question, the perspectives of stakeholders, as evident in qualitative studies, and the findings from interventional studies were deemed most pertinent to understanding the challenges and opportunities for improving hospital discharge planning for paediatric unintentional injury. While studies describing or evaluating current practice discharge planning for paediatric unintentional injury might also infer challenges and opportunities for improvement, these findings are unlikely to reflect the perspectives of caregivers or service providers; therefore, these studies will be excluded from this review.

Original research published in reports will be considered alongside journal articles of qualitative, quantitative or mixed-methods study design. Qualitative studies are likely to be particularly relevant to the first review question, whereas interventional studies are likely to be more suitable for informing the second review question. Interventional studies include those which describe the processes or findings from designing, implementing or evaluating interventions. Sources such as case studies, conference abstracts, editorials, guidelines, literature reviews, opinion articles and protocols will be excluded from this review, as the original perspectives of stakeholders or intervention findings may be obscured in secondary analyses. Snowballing will be used to identify eligible articles from the reference lists of literature reviews retrieved in this search, replacing them as the primary sources of information.47 Likewise, snowballing will be used to identify any relevant publications corresponding to published study protocols. Doctoral theses may be included in this review if there are no publications corresponding to the original research described in the thesis, as identified through snowballing.

The literature search will be conducted on CINAHL Plus, Cochrane Library, Embase and Ovid MEDLINE, as these databases provide ample scope to address the review questions, with Cochrane Library included to locate interventional studies. To guide the development of the search terms, the titles and abstracts of articles on the topics of paediatric unintentional injury and discharge planning were searched on Google Scholar and Ovid MEDLINE to identify related terms.43 The relevant Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and Emtree terms have been sought, tested and integrated into the Ovid MEDLINE and Embase searches. The search strategy was developed for Ovid MEDLINE first, as shown in table 1, and then adapted, tested and tailored for the Cochrane Library, CINAHL Plus and Embase databases.40 In the initial stages of developing the search strategy in Ovid MEDLINE, relevant studies retrieved from literature review reference lists informed additions and modifications to the search terms, acknowledging inevitable trade-offs between sensitivity and specificity.40 We will also examine the reference lists of eligible studies and reviews as a snowballing approach to increase the sensitivity of the search process.40 47 The search strategy will be restricted to literature published between 1 January 2000 and 31 December 2025, and available in English. The timeframe of the past 25 years was selected to provide ample scope and breadth of evidence, while also acknowledging that trauma care processes have evolved, and to limit the search to a period when modern, advanced trauma care pathways have been in existence.

Source selection:

All citations will be imported from the four databases into Microsoft Excel, where duplicates will be identified and removed. The process of source selection will be presented in a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram43 in the scoping review, with the reasons for exclusion of sources also explained narratively. First, titles and abstracts will be screened by the primary reviewer (EA) in line with the inclusion and exclusion criteria defined using the PCC framework.41 42 A second reviewer will replicate the title and abstract screening process for a random sample of 20% of the identified records.41 42 Where disagreements occur, other members of the research team will be involved to reach a consensus, and the eligibility criteria will be redefined where necessary.43 In this initial screening stage, ineligible sources of information will be excluded, such as case studies, conference abstracts, editorials, guidelines, opinion articles and review protocols. Snowballing will be conducted to identify relevant articles corresponding to study protocols and doctoral theses, or contained in the reference list of literature reviews.47 Where snowballing identifies relevant peer-reviewed publications arising from doctoral thesis research, these will be retrieved and will replace the doctoral thesis. Doctoral theses which lack corresponding peer-reviewed publications may still be included in this review, and will be identified in the findings and discussed in the context of other sources.

In the second selection stage, the full texts of articles will be retrieved and screened a second time against the PCC framework41 to finalise eligibility. A second reviewer will replicate the full text screening process for a random sample of 20% of the records to finalise eligibility.41 42 Additionally, the primary reviewer (EA) may flag studies of unclear eligibility to the wider research team to assist in final source selection. The reviewer completing a peer review of the data charting process for the interventional studies (MA) may also be consulted in discussions about source eligibility. The referencing software EndNote V.20 will be used to store and manage the finalised selection of studies for inclusion in this review.

Charting the data

Five data charting forms have been developed in Microsoft Excel to map the key characteristics and methods used in each study, and to extract data relating to the four review questions. The qualitative data analysis software NVivo V.14 will be used in conjunction with the data charting forms to annotate and code the studies and extract data, employing an iterative, rigorous process.40 The evidence informing each review question will be coded in NVivo and Microsoft Excel: perspectives will be coded as positive, negative, neutral or mixed to inform the first review question, challenges and opportunities to improve discharge planning will be distinguished to inform the second review question, the third review question will require extraction of the interventions proposed or reported in the studies, and for the fourth review question, the reviewers will describe the extent of documentation or consideration for inequities in the literature. Evidence informing the first review question must relate to perspectives of current practice discharge planning; thus, the perspectives of participants in the control groups of interventional studies may be relevant. For the second review question, the challenges or opportunities to improve hospital discharge for injured children may be explicitly identified or implied by caregivers, service providers or the authors, or evident from the implementation of interventions to improve hospital discharge. The third review question will map the interventions proposed in the literature. For the fourth review question, all details relating to inequities will be extracted from the literature, including descriptions of inequities and discussions around why inequities have occurred or the impacts of an intervention on inequities.

The data charting forms for review questions 1, 2 and 4 will include a column for the reviewers to use the access framework developed by Levesque et al.15 The reviewers will denote which of the five dimensions of access or the five abilities of persons to access services15 correspond to each piece of evidence informing the review questions. As needed, the reviewers will describe concepts that the framework cannot adequately encompass. The concepts of ‘barriers and facilitators’ described in the paper presenting the access framework15 are largely interchangeable with the terms ‘challenges and opportunities’ used in this scoping review to present a quality improvement and service evaluation framing; however, some challenges or opportunities may relate to practical, clinical considerations specific to unintentional injuries in children, which will be highlighted in the findings of the scoping review.

In line with the framework provided by Arksey and O’Malley40 and the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis,41 42 the first data charting form will denote the study authors, year and country of publication, age of children and types of injuries, the number of participants and their relationship to the child or their role as providers, the study setting, design, main methods, aims, relevance to discharge planning and gaps in the research. Data charting will be completed for all articles initially by EA, followed by a second independent peer review of the interventional studies by MA, who will replicate the same process, also using Levesque et al’s framework15 to chart the data into the predefined forms. The review team will then discuss the findings of both reviewers, including addressing any discrepancies surrounding the completion of the data charting forms, interpretations of evidence or implementation of the access framework,15 with the wider research team brought in as needed for clarification or to help reach a consensus.43

Summarising and reporting findings

The findings relating to the four review questions will be summarised narratively in the scoping review, describing the perspectives of caregivers and service providers on hospital discharge planning for paediatric unintentional injury, the challenges and opportunities to improve hospital discharge, the proposed discharge interventions and any observed inequities or considerations for inequities in the literature. Levesque et al’s framework15 will be used to structure the results and discussion, providing continuity and clear linking of evidence, given its relevance to three of the four review questions. The authors will use the access framework15 to compare and critique differences in perspectives, access-related challenges and opportunities for improvement, and inequities reported in the studies, whether reflecting the views of caregivers, service providers or researchers, and what these might suggest for future efforts to enhance discharge planning. A table will accompany a descriptive summary of the key characteristics of the studies.41 Themes arising in relation to the four review questions may be presented in a graph, table or other figure where visual representation of findings enhances the narrative discussion.41

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval was not required for this scoping review as it only includes publicly available material sourced from peer-reviewed publications. We expect to publish the findings of the scoping review in a peer-reviewed journal. Findings will also be presented in a doctoral thesis (EA) and may be disseminated in other ways, for example, conferences. The findings will also inform an ongoing project focusing on discharge planning processes for injured children, funded by a Research Development Award from the Health Research Council of New Zealand (HRC 23/985).
