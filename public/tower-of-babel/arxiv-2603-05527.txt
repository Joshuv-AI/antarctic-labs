Drifting to Boltzmann: Million-Fold Acceleration in Boltzmann Sampling with Force-Guided Drifting
Pipi Hu

Abstract
Sampling molecular conformations from the Boltzmann distribution is essential for computational chemistry, but iterative diffusion methods are prohibitively slow. Drifting Models offer one-step generation, yet their equilibrium matches the training distribution, which may deviate from the true Boltzmann distribution due to sampling bias. We introduce Drifting Models to molecular conformation generation for the first time, establishing a theoretical bridge via the Drifting Score Identity : for Gaussian kernels, the drifting field’s attraction equals a kernel-weighted average of any distribution’s score function. Substituting molecular force labels—which directly encode the Boltzmann score—yields the Drifting Force Identity and decomposes the field into standard drift plus a Boltzmann correction. We further discover a striking phenomenon unique to molecular systems: force incorporation’s effectiveness reverses across representations . In coordinate space, Force-Interpolated Drifting (FI) dominates by blending physical force directions with data displacements. In distance feature space, Force-Aligned Kernel (FK) achieves superior accuracy by modifying only kernel weights, thereby preserving the manifold of geometrically valid molecules. On MD17 Ethanol, both approaches achieve one-step generation with over 1000x speedup relative to recent score-matching diffusion with Boltzmann guiding, providing more than million-fold acceleration over traditional molecular dynamics, while ensuring perfect structural validity and distributional accuracy rivaling multi-step methods.



Keywords:
Generative models, Molecular conformation sampling, Boltzmann distribution, Drifting models, Force-guided learning, One-step generation

1
Introduction

Sampling molecular conformations from the Boltzmann distribution
p_{\text{Boltz}}(\mathbf{x})\propto\exp(-\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})/k_{B}T)
is fundamental to computational chemistry and materials science.
Accurate Boltzmann sampling enables the prediction of thermodynamic observables, free energies, and structural properties that govern molecular behavior.
Traditional approaches based on Molecular Dynamics (MD) and Monte Carlo (MC) methods can in principle generate Boltzmann-distributed samples, but they suffer from long correlation times and difficulty escaping metastable states, making them computationally prohibitive for complex molecular systems.

Generative Models for Molecular Sampling.

Deep generative models have emerged as a promising alternative for molecular conformation generation.
Flow-based models
[
10
]
, diffusion models
[
12
,
6
]
, and score-based generative models
[
11
]
have achieved remarkable success in generating diverse molecular conformations.
However, models trained purely on data via maximum likelihood or denoising score matching
[
2
]
(DSM) converge to the
data distribution

p_{\text{data}}
, which may differ from the true Boltzmann distribution due to finite sampling, enhanced sampling bias, or non-equilibrium data collection.
The Boltzmann distribution, fundamental to statistical mechanics, encodes equilibrium molecular configurations at finite temperature via an energy-based model
[
3
]
.

Debiasing with Force Labels.

A key property of the Boltzmann distribution is that the score function equals the normalized force:
\nabla\log p_{\text{Boltz}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})/k_{B}T
, where
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})=-\nabla\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})
is the molecular force.
This connection between forces and the score function provides a natural avenue for incorporating energy-based information into generative models.
Existing force-guided approaches
[
4
]
achieve Boltzmann debiasing within diffusion frameworks but require multi-step iterative inference, which can be
10
–
1000\times
slower than single-step generation.

One-Step Generation via Drifting Models.

Drifting Models
[
7
]
provide an elegant framework for one-step generation: a generator
f_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})
maps noise directly to samples, guided by a kernel-based drifting field
[
1
]

\mathbf{V}_{p,q}(\mathbf{x})
toward a target distribution.
The anti-symmetry property (
\mathbf{V}_{p,q}=-\mathbf{V}_{q,p}
) ensures equilibrium when generator and data distributions match.
However, this equilibrium is at
q=p_{\text{data}}
, not
q=p_{\text{Boltz}}
—when training data is biased, generated samples inherit the same bias.

Prior to this work, Drifting Models have only been applied to image generation; their utility for molecular systems remains unexplored.

Our Contribution.

We introduce Drifting Models to molecular conformation generation and bridge drifting with force-guided Boltzmann debiasing through rigorous theory and unique molecular insights:
Our key contributions are:

We prove that for Gaussian kernels, the drifting field’s attraction term equals a kernel-weighted average of the

Theoretical analysis shows that force substitution prevents equilibrium at
q=p_{\text{data}}
(correction term survives), while exact equilibrium at

p_{\text{Boltz}}

\omega
or
\gamma

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Boltzmann Distribution and Force Labels

Consider a molecular system with atomic coordinates
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 3}
, where
n
is the number of atoms.
The system’s potential energy is given by
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})
, and at temperature
T
the equilibrium distribution is the Boltzmann distribution:

p_{\text{Boltz}}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})}{k_{B}T}\right),

(1)

where
Z=\int\exp(-\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})/k_{B}T)\,d\mathbf{x}
is the partition function and
k_{B}
is Boltzmann’s constant.

The molecular force is the negative gradient of the potential energy:

\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})=-\nabla\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}).

(2)

A crucial property connecting forces to the Boltzmann distribution is that the score function equals the rescaled force:

\nabla\log p_{\text{Boltz}}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})}{k_{B}T}.

(3)

This identity is the foundation for force-guided generative modeling: force labels, readily available from molecular simulations or neural network potentials, carry direct information about the target Boltzmann distribution.

2.2
Drifting Models

Drifting Models
[
7
]
train a one-step generator
f_{\theta}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
that maps noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim p_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
, e.g., standard Gaussian, to samples
\mathbf{x}=f_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})
, with the pushforward distribution denoted
q_{\theta}
.
Training is guided by a
drifting field

\mathbf{V}_{p,q}(\mathbf{x})
that points from the current generator distribution
q
toward the target distribution
p
.

Given positive samples
\mathbf{y}^{+}\sim p
(training data) and negative samples
\mathbf{y}^{-}\sim q
(generated data), the drifting field is defined as:

\mathbf{V}_{p,q}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{Z_{p}Z_{q}}\mathbb{E}_{p,q}\!\left[k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{+})k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{-})(\mathbf{y}^{+}-\mathbf{y}^{-})\right],

(4)

where
k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is a kernel function and
Z_{p},Z_{q}
are normalization constants.
Since
\mathbf{y}^{+}
and
\mathbf{y}^{-}
are sampled independently, the expectation factors, and the field decomposes into an attraction term toward positive samples and a repulsion term away from negative samples:

\mathbf{V}_{p,q}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{V}_{p}^{+}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{V}_{q}^{-}(\mathbf{x}),

(5)

where the attraction and repulsion terms are:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{p}^{+}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\bar{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+}-\mathbf{x}),

(6)

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{q}^{-}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\bar{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j}^{-})(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{-}-\mathbf{x}),

(7)

with normalized kernel weights
\bar{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})=k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})/\sum_{i}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{i})
.

The original Drifting Model uses a Laplacian kernel:

k_{\text{Lap}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|}{\tau}\right),

(8)

where
\tau>0
is the bandwidth parameter.
The generator is trained with a self-play loss:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\!\left[\left\|f_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})-\operatorname{sg}\!\left(f_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})+\mathbf{V}_{p,q}(f_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}))\right)\right\|^{2}\right],

(9)

where
\operatorname{sg}(\cdot)
denotes the stop-gradient operator.

Anti-Symmetry and Equilibrium.

The drifting field (
4
) satisfies anti-symmetry: swapping
p\leftrightarrow q
flips the sign of
(\mathbf{y}^{+}-\mathbf{y}^{-})
, yielding
\mathbf{V}_{p,q}=-\mathbf{V}_{q,p}
.
As a direct consequence, when
q=p
, the field vanishes:
\mathbf{V}_{p,p}(\mathbf{x})=0
for all
\mathbf{x}
.
This guarantees that the data distribution
p
is an equilibrium of the drifting dynamics.
However, this equilibrium is at
q=p_{\text{data}}
, not necessarily at
q=p_{\text{Boltz}}
.

3
Method

We now present our main contribution: a principled connection between the drifting field and molecular forces that enables Boltzmann-constrained one-step generation.

3.1
Drifting Score Identity

Our key insight is that for Gaussian kernels, the drifting field’s attraction term equals a kernel-weighted average of the
score function
of the sampling distribution—for
any
sufficiently regular distribution, not just the Boltzmann distribution. This fundamental relationship is established through an integration-by-parts argument.
When combined with the score–force identity specific to
p_{\text{Boltz}}
, this reveals exactly how force labels can shift the equilibrium from
p_{\text{data}}
toward
p_{\text{Boltz}}
.

Theorem 3.1

(Drifting Score Identity)
.

Let
k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\exp(-\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}/(2\tau^{2}))
be a Gaussian kernel.
For any sufficiently regular distribution
p
(i.e.,
p(\mathbf{y})k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\to 0
as
\|\mathbf{y}\|\to\infty
), the attraction term of the drifting field satisfies, in the population limit:

\mathbf{V}_{p}^{+}(\mathbf{x})=\tau^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{p}\!\left[\bar{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\log p(\mathbf{y})\right],

(10)

where
\bar{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})/\mathbb{E}_{p}[k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})]
is the normalized kernel weight.
That is, the attraction term equals
\tau^{2}
times a kernel-weighted average of the score function
\nabla\log p
of the sampling distribution.

Proof.

The attraction term in the population limit is

\mathbf{V}_{p}^{+}(\mathbf{x})={\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{y})}[k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})]}/{\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{y})}[k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]}
.
We transform the numerator.

Step 1: Kernel gradient identity.

For the Gaussian kernel,
\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=-k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})/\tau^{2}
, so:

k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})=-\tau^{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

(11)

Step 2: Integration by parts.

Substituting (
11
) into the numerator and applying integration by parts (boundary terms vanish by the regularity assumption):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p}[k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})]

\displaystyle=-\tau^{2}\int p(\mathbf{y})\,\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\tau^{2}\int\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}p(\mathbf{y})\cdot k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,d\mathbf{y}=\tau^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{p}\!\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\log p(\mathbf{y})\cdot k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right].

(12)

Dividing by
\mathbb{E}_{p}[k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]
:

\mathbf{V}_{p}^{+}(\mathbf{x})=\tau^{2}\cdot\frac{\mathbb{E}_{p}[\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\log p(\mathbf{y})\cdot k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]}{\mathbb{E}_{p}[k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]}=\tau^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{p}\!\left[\bar{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\log p(\mathbf{y})\right].\qed

(13)

Remark 3.1
.

The identity (
10
) holds cleanly for Gaussian kernels because
\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}k=-(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})k/\tau^{2}
produces the exact
(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})
factor appearing in the attraction term.
For the Laplacian kernel,
\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}k=-(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})k/(\tau\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|)
introduces an extra
1/\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|
normalization, and the relationship to scores is no longer clean.
This motivates our use of Gaussian kernels for force-guided drifting.

The general identity (
10
) involves the score
\nabla\log p
, which is typically unknown.
However, for the Boltzmann distribution, the score–force identity (
3
) provides direct access:
\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\log p_{\text{Boltz}}(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{y})/k_{B}T
.
Substituting this into Theorem
3.1
immediately gives:

Corollary 3.2

(Drifting Force Identity)
.

When
p=p_{\text{Boltz}}
, the attraction term of the drifting field satisfies:

\mathbf{V}_{p_{\text{Boltz}}}^{+}(\mathbf{x})=\tau^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{Boltz}}}\!\left[\bar{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\frac{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{y})}{k_{B}T}\right].

(14)

That is, the Boltzmann-weighted attraction can be computed entirely from force labels, without knowledge of the score function.

In practice, training data comes from a distribution
p_{\text{data}}
that may differ from
p_{\text{Boltz}}
.
Replacing the unknown
\nabla\log p_{\text{data}}
with the available force labels
\mathbf{F}/k_{B}T
introduces a systematic mismatch; we analyze this effect and its control through Force-Interpolated Drifting in Section
3.2
.

3.2
Force-Interpolated Drifting (FI)

Since the displacement form and the force form are two expressions for the same quantity (Theorem
3.1
), a natural approach is to
interpolate
between them at the per-term level.
Note that in high-dimensional molecular systems, the per-term magnitudes can differ substantially (
\|\tau^{2}\mathbf{F}/k_{B}T\|\sim 150\times\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|
), so the interpolation weight also serves as a variance control mechanism.

Definition 3.1

(Force-Interpolated Attraction)
.

Given positive samples
\{\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+}\}
with force labels
\{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})\}
and an interpolation weight
\omega\in[0,1]
, the force-interpolated attraction is:

\mathbf{V}_{\omega}^{+}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j}\bar{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})\Big[(1-\omega)(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+}-\mathbf{x})+\omega\,\tau^{2}\frac{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})}{k_{B}T}\Big].

(15)

Equivalently:

\mathbf{V}_{\omega}^{+}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{V}_{p_{\text{data}}}^{+}(\mathbf{x})+\omega\sum_{j}\bar{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})\underbrace{\left[\tau^{2}\frac{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})}{k_{B}T}-(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+}-\mathbf{x})\right]}_{\text{force--displacement residual}}.

(16)

Proposition 3.3

(
\omega
Controls Boltzmann Correction)
.

In the population limit with training data from
p_{\text{data}}
, the force-interpolated attraction decomposes as:

\mathbf{V}_{\omega}^{+}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{V}_{p_{\text{data}}}^{+}(\mathbf{x})+\omega\cdot\tau^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\!\left[\bar{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\log\frac{p_{\text{Boltz}}(\mathbf{y})}{p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{y})}\right].

(17)

That is,
\omega

linearly scales
the Boltzmann correction—the discrepancy between force labels (encoding
\nabla\log p_{\text{Boltz}}
) and the true data score (
\nabla\log p_{\text{data}}
).

Proof.

Taking the expectation of (
16
) over
p_{\text{data}}
and applying Theorem
3.1
to
p_{\text{data}}
:
the residual expectation is
\tau^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[\bar{k}\,\nabla\log p_{\text{Boltz}}]-\tau^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[\bar{k}\,\nabla\log p_{\text{data}}]=\tau^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[\bar{k}\,\nabla\log(p_{\text{Boltz}}/p_{\text{data}})]
.
∎

Setting
\omega=1
(full force substitution) and including the repulsion term, the complete force-substituted drifting field decomposes as:

\hat{\mathbf{V}}(\mathbf{x})=\underbrace{\mathbf{V}_{p_{\text{data}}}^{+}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{V}_{q}^{-}(\mathbf{x})}_{\text{standard anti-symmetric drift}}+\underbrace{\tau^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\!\left[\bar{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\log\frac{p_{\text{Boltz}}(\mathbf{y})}{p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{y})}\right]}_{\text{Boltzmann correction}}.

(18)

Interpretation.

The hyperparameter
\omega
interpolates between:

•

\omega=0
: standard drifting, equilibrium at
p_{\text{data}}
.

•

\omega=1
: full force substitution, equilibrium shifted toward
p_{\text{Boltz}}
.

•

0<\omega\ll 1
: partial Boltzmann correction with controlled variance.

The interpolation weight
\omega
acts as a control mechanism, allowing the model to balance between the displacement-based component (which matches the data distribution) and the force-based component (which encodes Boltzmann constraints).

Variance.

The per-term magnitude of the interpolated vector is:

\|(1-\omega)(\mathbf{y}_{j}-\mathbf{x})+\omega\,\tau^{2}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{y}_{j})/k_{B}T\|\leq(1-\omega)\|\mathbf{y}_{j}-\mathbf{x}\|+\omega\|\tau^{2}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{y}_{j})/k_{B}T\|,

(19)

which for
\omega=0.01
and a
150\times
magnitude ratio gives
\approx 2.5\times
the displacement scale—well within the range where the standard kernel ESS suffices.
Unlike full force substitution (
\omega=1
), which amplifies per-term magnitudes by
{\sim}150\times
, Force-Interpolated Drifting with small
\omega
adds only
\mathcal{O}(\omega)
additional variance while providing the full Boltzmann correction direction.

3.3
Force-Aligned Kernel (FK)

An alternative approach to incorporating force information is to modify the kernel
weights
rather than the displacement vectors.
The key idea is
importance reweighting
: if the training data comes from
p_{\text{data}}\neq p_{\text{Boltz}}
, we can correct for the distributional mismatch by upweighting data points that are more likely under
p_{\text{Boltz}}
.

Derivation.

The standard drifting attraction weights each positive sample
\mathbf{y}_{j}
by the normalized Gaussian kernel
\bar{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})\propto k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})
.
To bias the attraction toward Boltzmann-relevant samples, we would like to use a
corrected
kernel that incorporates the Boltzmann probability of each data point:

\bar{k}_{\text{corrected}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})=\frac{k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})\cdot p_{\text{Boltz}}(\mathbf{y}_{j})^{\gamma}}{\sum_{i}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{i})\cdot p_{\text{Boltz}}(\mathbf{y}_{i})^{\gamma}},

(20)

where
\gamma>0
controls the reweighting strength.
Since
p_{\text{Boltz}}(\mathbf{y}_{j})\propto e^{-\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{y}_{j})/k_{B}T}
, the softmax logit for the corrected kernel is:

\ell_{j}=\log k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})+\gamma\log p_{\text{Boltz}}(\mathbf{y}_{j})=-\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\|^{2}}{2\tau^{2}}-\frac{\gamma\,\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{y}_{j})}{k_{B}T}+\text{const},

(21)

where the constant (including
\log Z
) cancels in the softmax normalization.
When energy labels
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{y}_{j})
are available in the training data, this gives an
exact
energy-reweighted kernel with no approximation.

In settings where only force labels are available (or to obtain a position-dependent form), we can express the energy term using a first-order Taylor expansion of
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})
around
\mathbf{y}_{j}
:

\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{y}_{j})\approx\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{y}_{j})\cdot(\mathbf{y}_{j}-\mathbf{x}).

(22)

Substituting into Eq. (
21
) and noting that
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})
is constant across all
j
(hence cancels in softmax):

\ell_{j}\approx\underbrace{-\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\|^{2}}{2\tau^{2}}}_{\text{standard distance}}+\underbrace{\gamma\cdot\frac{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{y}_{j})\cdot(\mathbf{y}_{j}-\mathbf{x})}{k_{B}T}}_{\text{force-alignment term}}.

(23)

This force-based form has an intuitive geometric meaning: data points where the force
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{y}_{j})
points toward the query
\mathbf{x}
receive higher weight, preferentially attracting the generator toward regions that the Boltzmann distribution favors.

Definition 3.2

(Force-Aligned Kernel)
.

Given positive samples
\{\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+}\}
with force labels
\{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})\}
(and optionally energy labels
\{\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})\}
), the Force-Aligned Kernel uses the logits from Eq. (
23
) (or Eq. (
21
) when energies are available) with
\gamma>0
controlling the reweighting strength.
The force-aligned attraction is:

\mathbf{V}_{\text{FA}}^{+}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j}\bar{k}_{\text{FA}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+}-\mathbf{x}),\quad\bar{k}_{\text{FA}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})=\frac{\exp(\ell_{j})}{\sum_{i}\exp(\ell_{i})}.

(24)

Proposition 3.4

(Boltzmann Reweighting)
.

The energy-reweighted kernel (Eq.
21
) satisfies exactly, and the Force-Aligned Kernel (Eq.
23
) satisfies approximately:

\bar{k}_{\text{FA}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})\approx\frac{k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})\cdot p_{\text{Boltz}}(\mathbf{y}_{j})^{\gamma}}{\sum_{i}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{i})\cdot p_{\text{Boltz}}(\mathbf{y}_{i})^{\gamma}}.

(25)

For the force-based form, the approximation error is controlled by
\epsilon=\tau\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{E}\|/\|\nabla\mathcal{E}\|
: the Taylor remainder
R_{j}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}_{j})^{\top}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{E}(\xi_{j})(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}_{j})=\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2}\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{E}\|)
is negligible relative to the linear term
\mathcal{O}(\tau\|\nabla\mathcal{E}\|)
when
\epsilon\ll 1
.

Interpretation.

The hyperparameter
\gamma
controls the
reweighting strength
, interpolating between the standard kernel (
\gamma=0
, no Boltzmann correction) and full Boltzmann reweighting (
\gamma=1
, where the kernel weight is proportional to
k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})\cdot p_{\text{Boltz}}(\mathbf{y}_{j})
).

Stability.

Since the displacement
(\mathbf{y}_{j}-\mathbf{x})
in Eq. (
24
) is unchanged from the standard method, the magnitude of
\mathbf{V}_{\text{FA}}^{+}
is bounded by the same
\mathcal{O}(1)
scale as the standard attraction.
This completely avoids the variance disaster of direct force substitution while still incorporating force information via the softmax weights.

3.4
Feature-Space Drifting

The coordinate-space drifting field (Sections
2.2
–
3.3
) computes kernel weights and displacements directly in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times 3}
.
While effective for MLP generators, this formulation is sensitive to rigid-body motions and can fail for architectures that produce less coordinated outputs, such as equivariant Transformers
[
9
]
.
An alternative is to use energy-guided debiasing with potential score matching
[
8
]
to correct for biased training data.
A natural remedy is to operate in a
feature space
that factors out rotational and translational symmetry.
In this section we define a pairwise-distance feature map and derive the
exact
force-interpolation formula in this space—without the per-pair projection approximation commonly used in internal-coordinate methods.

Feature map.

We define the feature map
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times 3}\to\mathbb{R}^{P}
as the vector of all pairwise interatomic distances:

\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x})=\|x_{i_{k}}-x_{j_{k}}\|,\quad k=1,\ldots,P=\tbinom{n}{2},

(26)

where
(i_{k},j_{k})
enumerates all atom pairs.
This map is translation- and rotation-invariant by construction.
Its Jacobian
J(\mathbf{x})=\partial\phi/\partial\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times D}
(with
D=3n
) has a sparse analytical form: for pair
k=(i_{k},j_{k})
,

\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial x_{a}^{\alpha}}=\begin{cases}+\hat{r}_{i_{k}j_{k}}^{\alpha}&\text{if }a=i_{k},\\[2.0pt]
-\hat{r}_{i_{k}j_{k}}^{\alpha}&\text{if }a=j_{k},\\[2.0pt]
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(27)

where
\hat{r}_{ij}=(x_{i}-x_{j})/\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|
is the unit bond direction.
Each row of
J
has exactly 6 nonzero entries (3 per atom in the pair), giving
6P
nonzeros in total.

Feature-space drifting.

Replacing Cartesian distances with feature-space distances in the kernel, the feature-space attraction and repulsion are:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\phi}^{+}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\bar{k}_{\phi}\!\big(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+}\big)\big(\phi(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})-\phi(\mathbf{x})\big),

(28)

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\phi}^{-}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\bar{k}_{\phi}\!\big(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j}^{-}\big)\big(\phi(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{-})-\phi(\mathbf{x})\big),

(29)

where
\bar{k}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})\propto\exp\!\big(-\|\phi(\mathbf{x})-\phi(\mathbf{y}_{j})\|^{2}/(2\tau_{\phi}^{2})\big)
and
\tau_{\phi}
is the feature-space bandwidth.
The generator is trained with the feature-space loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\phi}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\!\left[\left\|\phi(f_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}))-\operatorname{sg}\!\left(\phi(f_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}))+\mathbf{V}_{\phi}^{+}-\mathbf{V}_{\phi}^{-}\right)\right\|^{2}\right],

(30)

where gradients flow back to the generator through the differentiable map
\phi\circ f_{\theta}
.

Exact Feature-Space Force Interpolation.

To extend Force-Interpolated Drifting (Definition
3.1
) to feature space, we need the
feature-space force

\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
—the analog of the Cartesian force
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
in the space of pairwise distances.

When the potential energy depends only on pairwise distances—
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})=\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\phi(\mathbf{x}))
—the chain rule gives
\mathbf{F}=J^{\top}\mathbf{G}
, where
\mathbf{G}=-\nabla_{\phi}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
.
Since
J\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times D}
is non-square and rank-deficient (rank
D-6
due to 3 translational and 3 rotational zero modes), the exact feature-space force is obtained as the minimum-norm solution of
J^{\top}\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{F}
:

\boxed{\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y})=J(\mathbf{y})\,\big(J(\mathbf{y})^{\top}J(\mathbf{y})\big)^{+}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{y}),}

(31)

where
(J^{\top}J)^{+}
denotes the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of the
D\times D
Gram matrix.

The feature-space Force-Interpolated Drifting is then:

\mathbf{V}_{\phi,\omega}^{+}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j}\bar{k}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})\bigg[(1-\omega)\big(\phi(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})-\phi(\mathbf{x})\big)+\omega\,\tau_{\phi}^{2}\,\frac{\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})}{k_{B}T}\bigg].

(32)

This is the direct analog of Definition
3.1
in feature space, with
\mathbf{F}\to\mathbf{G}
.
However, the two terms can have vastly different scales: defining the
scale ratio

\rho\;=\;\frac{\tau_{\phi}\,\overline{\|\mathbf{G}\|}}{k_{B}T},

(33)

we observe
\rho\approx 300
in our molecular experiments (Section
5
), because the bandwidth
\tau_{\phi}
(set by data density) is decoupled from the force magnitude
\|\mathbf{G}\|/k_{B}T
.
When
\rho\gg 1
, even
\omega=0.01
leads to force-term dominance, degrading performance below standard feature-space drifting.

We therefore rescale the force term so that both components have
\mathcal{O}(\tau_{\phi})
magnitude:

\boxed{\mathbf{V}_{\phi,\omega}^{+}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j}\bar{k}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})\bigg[(1-\omega)\big(\phi(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})-\phi(\mathbf{x})\big)+\omega\,\frac{\tau_{\phi}}{\overline{\|\mathbf{G}\|}}\,\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})\bigg],}

(34)

where
\overline{\|\mathbf{G}\|}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y}_{j})\|
is precomputed alongside
\mathbf{G}
at zero additional training cost.
With this normalization,
\omega\in[0,1]
directly controls the interpolation ratio without requiring extreme values.

Computational cost.

Since
\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y}_{j})
depends only on data points and not on generated samples, it can be
precomputed once
before training, adding zero overhead to the training loop.

summarizes the per-sample cost for ethanol (
n=9

D=27

Feature-space Force-Aligned Kernel.

The Force-Aligned Kernel (Definition
3.2
) extends to feature space by replacing Cartesian forces with the exact feature-space force
\mathbf{G}
:

\ell_{j}^{\phi}=-\frac{\|\phi(\mathbf{x})-\phi(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})\|^{2}}{2\tau_{\phi}^{2}}+\gamma\cdot\frac{\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})\cdot\big(\phi(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})-\phi(\mathbf{x})\big)}{k_{B}T}.

(35)

The alignment term can have magnitude
{\sim}\rho
(Eq.
33
) relative to the standard logits, so we standardize within each minibatch:

\ell_{j}^{\phi}=-\frac{\|\phi(\mathbf{x})-\phi(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})\|^{2}}{2\tau_{\phi}^{2}}+\gamma\cdot\frac{A_{j}}{\mathrm{std}(A)},\quad A_{j}=\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})\cdot\big(\phi(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{+})-\phi(\mathbf{x})\big),

(36)

where
\mathrm{std}(A)
is computed over the positive batch.
This makes
\gamma
scale-invariant:
\gamma\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
regardless of the absolute force and distance scales.
Force-Interpolated Drifting (
34
) and the Force-Aligned Kernel can be combined by using
\bar{k}_{\phi}^{\text{FA}}
in place of
\bar{k}_{\phi}
.

Remark 3.2

(Metric correction)
.

The feature-space score of the Boltzmann distribution includes an additional
metric correction
:

\nabla_{\phi}\log q_{\text{Boltz}}(\phi)=\frac{\mathbf{G}}{k_{B}T}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\phi}\log\det(J^{\top}J),

(37)

where the second term accounts for the non-flat volume element in internal coordinates (analogous to the Fixman potential in constrained molecular dynamics).
In the current work we omit this correction, which is justified when the metric varies slowly relative to the kernel bandwidth
\tau_{\phi}
.
Incorporating the full metric correction is an interesting direction for future work.

4.1
Equilibrium Analysis

The decomposition (
18
) enables a transparent equilibrium analysis of the force-substituted drifting field.

Proposition 4.1

(Equilibrium Shift)
.

Consider the force-substituted drifting field (
18
) with positive samples from
p_{\text{data}}
.

(a)

q=p_{\text{data}}
is
not
an equilibrium when
p_{\text{data}}\neq p_{\text{Boltz}}
: the standard drift vanishes by anti-symmetry, but the Boltzmann correction
\tau^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[\bar{k}\,\nabla\log(p_{\text{Boltz}}/p_{\text{data}})]\neq 0
pushes the system past
p_{\text{data}}
toward
p_{\text{Boltz}}
.

(b)

q=p_{\text{Boltz}}
is an exact equilibrium
if and only if
positive samples also come from
p_{\text{Boltz}}
(i.e.,
p_{\text{data}}=p_{\text{Boltz}}
).

Proof.

Part (a).

At
q=p_{\text{data}}
, the repulsion uses the same distribution as the attraction’s data-driven component:
\mathbf{V}_{q}^{-}=\mathbf{V}_{p_{\text{data}}}^{-}
.
By anti-symmetry,
\mathbf{V}_{p_{\text{data}}}^{+}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{V}_{p_{\text{data}}}^{-}(\mathbf{x})=0
.
The Boltzmann correction term, however, depends only on the positive samples and survives:

\hat{\mathbf{V}}(\mathbf{x})\big|_{q=p_{\text{data}}}=\tau^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\!\left[\bar{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\log\frac{p_{\text{Boltz}}(\mathbf{y})}{p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{y})}\right]\neq 0,

(38)

driving the generator distribution beyond
p_{\text{data}}
toward
p_{\text{Boltz}}
.

Part (b).

When
p_{\text{data}}=p_{\text{Boltz}}
and
q=p_{\text{Boltz}}
, both positive and negative samples come from
p_{\text{Boltz}}
.
The repulsion term is
\mathbf{V}_{q}^{-}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{Boltz}}}[\bar{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})]
.
By Theorem
3.1
applied to
p=p_{\text{Boltz}}
, together with the score–force identity (
3
):

\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{Boltz}}}[\bar{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})]=\tau^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{Boltz}}}\!\left[\bar{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\frac{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{y})}{k_{B}T}\right]=\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{\text{force}}^{+}(\mathbf{x}).

(39)

Therefore
\hat{\mathbf{V}}(\mathbf{x})=\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{\text{force}}^{+}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{V}_{q}^{-}(\mathbf{x})=0
.

Conversely, when
p_{\text{data}}\neq p_{\text{Boltz}}
, the attraction uses kernel weights normalized over
p_{\text{data}}
while the repulsion at
q=p_{\text{Boltz}}
uses kernel weights normalized over
p_{\text{Boltz}}
.
These normalizations differ, so exact cancellation fails and
q=p_{\text{Boltz}}
is not an exact equilibrium; the true equilibrium lies between
p_{\text{data}}
and
p_{\text{Boltz}}
.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

Proposition
4.1
characterizes the
idealized
force-substituted field.
For the practical Force-Aligned Kernel (Definition
3.2
), the equilibrium is further modulated: by Proposition
3.4
, the effective weights target the tilted distribution
p_{\text{data}}\cdot p_{\text{Boltz}}^{\gamma}
rather than
p_{\text{Boltz}}
exactly, with
\gamma\ll 1
providing a partial but stable correction.

Remark 4.2

(Anti-Symmetry)
.

Force alignment breaks strict anti-symmetry since attraction and repulsion use different kernels.
However, unlike scaling relative magnitudes (which causes collapse
[
7
]
), our modification only changes weighting within attraction, keeping displacements unchanged.
Proposition
4.1
(a) confirms this shift moves the equilibrium rather than breaking balance.

4.2
Approximation Quality

Approximation quality.

The Force-Aligned Kernel involves two levels of approximation.
First, the Boltzmann reweighting (Proposition
3.4
) relies on a first-order Taylor expansion controlled by the parameter
\epsilon=\tau\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{E}\|/\|\nabla\mathcal{E}\|
, which measures the kernel bandwidth relative to the length scale of force variation; the approximation is exact for locally affine potentials and improves as
\tau\to 0
.
Second, using
p_{\text{data}}
samples in place of
p_{\text{Boltz}}
yields an effective sampling distribution
p_{\text{data}}\cdot p_{\text{Boltz}}^{\gamma}/Z
, which interpolates between
p_{\text{data}}
(
\gamma=0
) and a distribution tilted toward
p_{\text{Boltz}}
(
\gamma>0
).
While no single
\gamma
achieves full correction in general, any
\gamma>0
shifts the effective distribution in the correct direction.

Biased training protocol.

We deliberately use the

A held-out set of 5,000 equilibrated frames (frames 10k–15k) serves as the Boltzmann reference.

Evaluation metrics.

Beyond the standard
h(r)

per-molecule structural validity

•

h(r)
MAE / TVD /
\mathcal{W}_{2}
: interatomic distance histogram error, total variation distance, and 2-Wasserstein distance against the Boltzmann reference.

•

Stability
: fraction of generated molecules where all pairwise distances deviate
<0.5
Å from the reference mean.

•

Bond MAE
: mean absolute deviation of covalent bond lengths from the Boltzmann reference (only bonded pairs with
\bar{d}<1.6
Å).

•

Bond Stability
: fraction of samples with
all covalent bonds
within 0.5 Å of reference—a stricter structural validity check that excludes noisy torsional degrees of freedom.

•

Per-type TVD
: separate distance distribution TVD for each atom-pair type (C–C, C–H, C–O, H–H, H–O), avoiding the masking effect of mixed-pair histograms.

5.3
Feature-Space Results: Distance-Coordinate Representation

We extend the force-guided methods to an internal-coordinate representation using pairwise distances (a rotationally invariant feature space).
We evaluate the feature-space methods derived in Section
3.4
—exact feature-space force (Eq.
31
), scale-normalized Force-Interpolated Drifting (Eq.
34
), and the feature-space Force-Aligned Kernel (Eq.
36
)—using the MLP distance-space generator that naturally produces structurally valid molecules (Bond MAE
=0.006
Å, Bond Stability
=100\%
) when trained without force labels.

Motivation.

When using a naive per-pair projection
\mathbf{G}^{\text{proj}}=J\mathbf{F}
instead of the exact feature-space force, Force-Interpolated Drifting degrades
h(r)
TVD from
0.194
to
0.317
—a catastrophic failure despite identical training data and setup.
This reveals three key issues:
(i) the naive per-pair projection ignores metric coupling between pairs sharing atoms;
(ii) the force scale
\tau_{\phi}^{2}\|\mathbf{G}\|/k_{B}T\approx 300\times
the data displacement;
(iii) the force vector
\mathbf{G}
does not lie on the data manifold in distance space.
Here, we employ the exact feature-space force (
31
) with scale normalization (
34
) for all results, with the Force-Aligned Kernel (
36
) as an alternative approach.

Setup.

All variants share the same architecture as Drifting-MLP-Dist: an MLP generator (
6\times 512
, SiLU, 1.36M parameters), trained for 20,000 steps with the same hyperparameters (batch size 256, 512 positive samples,
\text{lr}=10^{-3}
, cosine schedule).
The feature-space bandwidth
\tau_{\phi}=1.61
is set by the median heuristic on pairwise distances in feature space.
The exact feature-space force
\mathbf{G}
(Eq.
31
) is precomputed once for all
N=2{,}700
training samples (
<0.1
s on GPU, Table
2
).
For the scale-normalized FI,
\overline{\|\mathbf{G}\|}=59.5
(exact) and
196.2
(naive), giving scale factors
\tau_{\phi}/\overline{\|\mathbf{G}\|}\approx 0.027
and
0.008
respectively.
We sweep
\omega\in\{0.1,0.3,0.5\}
for FI and
\gamma\in\{0.1,0.5,1.0\}
for FK.

Results.

Table

presents the results.
Several striking findings emerge:

1.

The feature-space Force-Aligned Kernel achieves the best overall performance.

FK in feature space with
\gamma=0.1
attains
h(r)
TVD
=0.089
—a
54% improvement
over Drifting in feature space (
0.194
) and
36% improvement
over FI in coordinate space (
0.139

0.006
Å) and Bond Stability (
100\%
).
This makes it the only method to simultaneously optimize both distributional accuracy and structural validity across all experiments.

2.

Force-Interpolated Drifting destroys molecular structure in distance space.

All FI-Dist variants exhibit catastrophically low Bond Stability: even the mildest setting (
\omega=0.1
) reduces Bond Stability from
100\%
to
{\sim}49\%
.
This failure is fundamental to the FI mechanism in distance space: directly adding force-derived displacements pushes the target away from the manifold of valid molecular geometries, regardless of whether the force is computed exactly or via naive projection.
Directly adding a force-derived displacement
\mathbf{V}_{\text{force}}=(\tau_{\phi}/\overline{\|\mathbf{G}\|})\sum_{j}\bar{k}_{j}\mathbf{G}_{j}
pushes the target away from the data manifold in distance space, producing 36-dimensional distance vectors that do not correspond to valid molecular geometries.

3.

The force kernel preserves structure because it modifies only the weights, not the displacement.

The FK-Dist method modifies softmax weights via
\ell_{j}=-\|\phi_{x}-\phi_{j}\|^{2}/(2\tau_{\phi}^{2})+\gamma\cdot A_{j}/\text{std}(A)
while the displacement remains
\mathbf{V}^{+}=\sum_{j}w_{j}(\phi_{j}-\phi_{x})
—a convex combination of real data displacements.
This guarantees that the target lies in the convex hull of observed distance vectors, preserving geometric validity by construction.
Force information enters as a
soft attention mechanism
that upweights Boltzmann-relevant neighbors without distorting the distance-space geometry.

4.

Smaller
\gamma
is better: the force kernel should be a subtle correction.

\gamma=0.1
yields TVD
=0.089
;
\gamma=0.5
gives
0.111
;
\gamma=1.0
gives
0.134
.
Larger
\gamma
shifts too much probability mass toward force-aligned neighbors, causing the generator to under-represent geometrically proximate but force-misaligned configurations.
All three settings outperform the drifting baseline, confirming the robustness of the approach.

5.

Force information reverses its effectiveness between coordinate and distance space.

In coordinate space, FI dominates (TVD
0.139
vs FK’s
0.228
); in feature space, FK dominates (TVD
0.089
vs FI’s best
0.170
at
\omega=0.1
).
This reversal arises because in coordinate space, the Cartesian force
\mathbf{F}
is a physically meaningful displacement direction (bond stretching, angle bending) that blends naturally with data displacements, while the scalar force-kernel reweighting is less effective in the high-dimensional, sparsely populated coordinate space.
In distance space, the feature-space force
\mathbf{G}
is an abstract vector in
\mathbb{R}^{36}
that does not correspond to a realizable molecular motion, making direct displacement interpolation harmful—but the alignment scalar
A_{j}=\mathbf{G}_{j}\cdot(\phi_{j}-\phi_{x})
remains a physically meaningful measure of thermodynamic favorability for kernel reweighting.

The feature-space Force-Aligned Kernel resolves the failure of naive feature-space Force-Interpolated Drifting and establishes a new state of the art on biased MD17 Ethanol:

The key insight is that force information in the distance feature space should enter through

This contrasts with coordinate space, where Force-Interpolated Drifting is preferred—a reversal explained by the different roles of the force vector in each representation.

6
Conclusion

This work presents a principled framework for incorporating molecular forces into one-step generative models, enabling fast and accurate sampling from the Boltzmann distribution.
Our key contributions are:

Force-guided generation via Drifting Identity.

We derive the Drifting Score and Force Identities (Theorem
3.1
, Corollary
3.2
) that connect the drifting field to any target distribution, establishing a principled mathematical foundation for force guidance.
This leads to two complementary mechanisms:
Force-Interpolated Drifting (FI)
blends force information into sample displacements, while
Force-Aligned Kernel (FK)
reweights neighbors toward Boltzmann-relevant samples.
In coordinate space on MD17 Ethanol, FI achieves state-of-the-art distributional accuracy (
h(r)
TVD
=0.139
,
\mathcal{W}_{2}=0.031
) with near-perfect structural validity (Bond Stability
=97.5\%
) in a single generation step—
{\sim}2{,}000\times
faster than iterative methods.

To contextualize this speedup: recent work on Potential Score Matching (PSM)
[
8
]
, conducted on an NVIDIA A100 GPU, requires 8 hours of training and 10 minutes to generate 1,000 batches (1,000,000 generation steps). In comparison, traditional MD simulation takes approximately 31 hours and 18 minutes to generate 210,000 steps on the same hardware—a trajectory length commonly used in MD17 datasets. Our method, implemented on an NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU, achieves speedups of over 2,000× relative to PSM. Since PSM itself offers approximately 890× speedup over conventional MD, our method thus provides a cumulative speedup of
more than 1 million times
faster than traditional molecular dynamics simulation, completing in milliseconds what would require over 31 hours with conventional MD approaches.

Representation-aware force guidance.

We demonstrate a striking representation-dependence in force incorporation: FI excels in coordinate space because forces naturally encode bond-level directional information, while FK dominates in feature space (pairwise distances) where direct force interpolation risks producing geometrically infeasible samples.
Our feature-space extension introduces the
exact
force
\mathbf{G}=J(J^{\top}J)^{+}\mathbf{F}
(Eq.
31
), which properly accounts for metric coupling in the distance feature space.
This enables further improvements: FK in feature space achieves
h(r)
TVD
=0.089
with perfect bond stability (Bond Stability
=100\%
), demonstrating the complementary strengths of the two paradigms.

Implications and limitations.

The coordinate/feature-space dichotomy reveals a fundamental principle: optimal force guidance depends on representation.
This finding extends beyond Drifting Models—it suggests that any one-step generator should carefully match the force incorporation method to its coordinate system and the implicit geometry of the feature space.
Precomputing the exact feature-space force adds negligible overhead (
<0.01\%
of training time), making it practical for real applications.

Future work should explore: (i) dimension-adaptive kernels for robustness at larger molecular scales, (ii) extension to protein and materials systems where force guidance becomes increasingly important, (iii) learned or task-specific force weighting schemes, (iv) integration with modern neural network potentials (MACE, NequIP, EquiformerV2), and (v) incorporating metric correction terms (Remark
3.2
) for fully exact Boltzmann sampling in internal coordinates.
The per-bond structural metrics introduced here may also benefit other generative models by providing finer-grained evaluation criteria beyond aggregate distribution matching.
