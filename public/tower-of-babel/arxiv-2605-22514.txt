1.
Introduction

Solving systems of polynomial equations is a central problem in computer algebra and symbolic computation, with applications ranging from algebraic geometry and invariant theory to optimization and real algebraic geometry.
Given a system

\bm{f}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})\subset k[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}],

where
k
is a field,
solving
\bm{f}=0
can be interpreted in different ways. From a numerical
point of view, it means approximating the isolated points of the variety
defined by the ideal generated by
\bm{f}
while from a symbolic computation
perspective, it means providing an exact description, for instance through zero-dimensional parametrizations such as rational univariate representations or geometric resolutions,
of the solution set that allows one to extract further algebraic or
geometric information efficiently.
In general, polynomial system solving, which is known to be NP-hard
(
8
;
9
)
, is a difficult problem. In this
paper, we focus on the computer algebra setting and consider
systems with additional algebraic structure, which allows for more
efficient algorithms.

Our problem.

In many applications, polynomial systems exhibit additional algebraic structure that can be exploited to design faster algorithms. In this work we focus on
composable polynomial systems
, where each equation can be written as

f_{i}(\bm{X})=h_{i}\big(g_{1}(\bm{X}),\dots,g_{n}(\bm{X})\big),\qquad i=1,\dots,n,

for polynomial maps
\bm{g}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
and
\bm{h}=(h_{1},\dots,h_{n})
.

By exploiting the compositional structure, instead of solving
\bm{f}(\bm{X})=0
directly, whose complexity depends on the degrees of the composed polynomials
f_{i}
, one may first solve the
outer system

\bm{h}(\bm{Y})=0

in new variables
\bm{Y}
, and then lift its solutions through the
inner map

\bm{g}
.
Since the degrees of
\bm{h}
and
\bm{g}
are often much smaller than those of
\bm{f}
, this approach can significantly reduce the computational cost.

Applications.

An important special case occurs when
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
belong to
the subring
k[g_{1},\dots,g_{n}]
, where
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
are algebraically
independent. In this situation, each
f_{i}
admits a unique representation

f_{i}=h_{i}(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
for some polynomial

h_{i}\in k[Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}]
, and the composable framework applies directly.

Another natural class of examples arises in invariant theory. Let

G\subset\mathrm{GL}_{n}(k)
be a finite reflection group. By the
Chevalley–Shephard–Todd theorem
(
4
;
34
)
, the invariant ring

k[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]^{G}
is a polynomial algebra of the form

k[g_{1},\dots,g_{n}]
. Consequently, every
G
-invariant polynomial
f_{i}

can be written as a composition
f_{i}=h_{i}(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
.
Typical examples include the symmetric groups
S_{n}
, the hyperoctahedral
groups
B_{n}
, the dihedral groups
I_{2}(m)
, and the exceptional reflection
groups
E_{6},E_{7},E_{8},F_{4},H_{3},
and
H_{4}
. Exploiting this structure can reduce the number of points to compute and the overall arithmetic cost of solving
\bm{f}=0
.

This approach has been studied in various contexts. Classical invariant-theoretic algorithms are discussed in
(
33
;
5
)
, while applications to reflection groups appear in
(
22
)
. It has also been used to develop fast algorithms for solving invariant polynomial systems, including the connectivity of semi-algebraic sets
(
23
;
24
)
, emptiness of algebraic sets
(
21
)
, computing critical points of polynomial maps
(
38
;
7
)
, polynomial optimization
(
25
)
, and the Euler-Poincaré characteristic
(
2
)
.

In
(
39
)
, we developed a randomized algorithm that,
given polynomial maps
\bm{g}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
and
\bm{f}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
,
computes polynomials
\bm{h}=(h_{1},\dots,h_{n})\in k[Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}]
such that

\bm{f}=\bm{h}\circ\bm{g}
, that is, each
f_{i}
is expressed as a polynomial in

g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
. The arithmetic complexity is softly polynomial in the
sizes of the straight-line programs computing
\bm{f}
and
\bm{g}
, as well as in
the degree bound of the representation. In the important case where
\bm{f}

is invariant under a finite reflection group whose invariant ring is
generated by
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
, the degrees of the
h_{i}
are bounded by

\max_{i}\deg(f_{i})
, leading to particularly efficient computations.

Therefore, for these structured classes of systems, one can first apply
the representation algorithm of
(
39
)
to compute the outer
map
\bm{h}
from
\bm{f}
and
\bm{g}
, and then apply the compositional solving
algorithm developed in this paper to the pair
(\bm{g},\bm{h})
. This yields an
efficient procedure for computing the solutions of the original system

\bm{f}=0
.

Contribution

We present a probabilistic symbolic algorithm for computing all isolated regular solutions of composable systems
\bm{f}=\bm{h}\circ\bm{g}
.
Our method combines geometric resolutions with a global Newton-Hensel (homotopy) lifting procedure.
First, we compute a zero-dimensional parametrization of the regular solutions of the outer system
\bm{h}=0
using symbolic homotopy techniques.
Then, we lift these solutions through the inner map
\bm{g}
by a parametric Newton-Hensel scheme, and finally discard points where the Jacobian of
\bm{g}
is singular.
All computations are performed using straight-line programs, ensuring complexity bounds polynomial in the input size.

The resulting algorithm computes a geometric resolution of the isolated regular solutions of
\bm{f}=0
with arithmetic complexity
softly polynomial in the straight-line program sizes of
\bm{h}
and
\bm{g}
and in the quantities

C=\prod_{i}\deg(h_{i}),\qquad D=\prod_{i}\deg(g_{i}),

which bound respectively the number of solutions of the outer and inner systems.
In contrast, solving the composed system directly would typically depend on the much larger Bézout bound
\prod_{i}\deg(f_{i})
.
Thus, our complexity depends separately on the inner and outer maps rather than on their composition, yielding substantial savings for structured systems.

While homotopy lifting and geometric resolutions are classical tools, their combination with compositional structure has not been previously exploited to obtain complexity bounds depending separately on the inner and outer maps. The main novelty of this work lies in this structured decomposition and the resulting reduction from Bézout-type bounds to
CD
.

Theorem 1.1.

Let
k
be a field of characteristic zero or sufficiently large positive characteristic. Let

\bm{f}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
be polynomials in
k[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
, and assume that each
f_{i}
is composable as
f_{i}=h_{i}(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
for
i=1,\dots,n
.

Then there exists a randomized algorithm

{\sf Solve\_h\_circ\_g}
that takes
\bm{h}=(h_{1},\dots,h_{n})
and
\bm{g}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
as input and outputs
a zero-dimensional parametrization of the isolated regular points of
\bm{f}=0
.
The complexity of this algorithm is

{O}{\,\tilde{}\,}\!\big(n(L_{\bm{h}}+L_{\bm{g}}+n^{2}(\gamma+\sigma))(CE+DJ+DC)\big)

operations in
k
, where

-
L_{\bm{h}}
and
L_{\bm{g}}
are respectively the lengths of the straight-line programs computing
\bm{h}
and
\bm{g}
,

-
C=\deg(h_{1})\cdots\deg(h_{n})
,
E=(\deg(h_{1})+1)\cdots(\deg(h_{n})+1)
,

-
D=\deg(g_{1})\cdots\deg(g_{n})
,

,

-
\gamma=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\deg(h_{i})
, and
\sigma=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\deg(g_{i})
.

In this paper, by a randomized (or probabilistic) algorithm, we mean an algorithm that performs correct computations by making random choices of points lying in a certain non-empty Zariski open subset of appropriate affine spaces.
In this sense, the algorithm is of Monte Carlo type, i.e., it returns the correct output with high probability, at least some fixed constant greater than
1/2
. By repeating the algorithm multiple times, the error probability can be made arbitrarily small; this can be quantified using the Schwartz-Zippel lemma (see e.g.
(
32
)
or
(
36
, Lemma 6.44)
).

Structure of the paper

The paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
introduces the algebraic and algorithmic preliminaries used throughout the paper, including complexity notations, straight-line program representations, and zero-dimensional parametrizations such as rational univariate representations and geometric resolutions.
Section
3
reviews the global Newton-Hensel lifting technique, which serves as the main symbolic continuation tool for refining parametrizations along a homotopy.
Section
4
presents the core contributions of the paper: we first develop a symbolic homotopy algorithm for computing the regular solutions of a square polynomial system in the outer variables, and then exploit the compositional structure
\bm{f}=\bm{h}\circ\bm{g}
to lift these solutions through the inner map.
These ingredients are combined into the complete solving procedure together with a detailed complexity analysis, yielding bounds that depend separately on the degrees and straight-line program sizes of
\bm{h}
and
\bm{g}
.
Examples illustrate how exploiting this structure leads to significant improvements compared to solving the system
\bm{f}
directly.

2.1.
Complexity Notations

The following notation will be used to measure the complexities of our algorithms. The notation

means that there exists a constant

such that

is in
O(g\log(g)^{a})
.

We denote by

\omega<2.373
. In complexity estimates, terms of the form
O(n^{\omega})
account for the cost of linear algebra operations such as matrix inversion or solving linear systems.

For positive integers
d
, let
{\sf M}(d)
denote the cost of multiplying univariate polynomials of degree at most
d
over the base ring
\mathbb{K}
. Using the algorithms of Schönhage and Strassen
(
29
)
, Schönhage
(
30
)
, and Cantor and Kaltofen
(
3
)
, this can be done in
{O}{\,\tilde{}\,}(d)=O(d\log d\log\log d)
operations. Over a finite field with

elements, assuming the existence of a Linnik constant, one can take
{\sf M}(d)=O(d\log q\log(d\log q))
using the result in
(

)
.

2.2.
Data Representations

Let
h\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial of degree
d
. The usual dense representation encodes all
\binom{n+d}{n}
coefficients, while a sparse representation stores only the nonzero terms. In this work, we use a
straight-line program (SLP)
, also known as an
algebraic circuit
, as an alternative representation.

An SLP computing polynomials
h_{1},\dots,h_{m}\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is a sequence

\gamma=(\gamma_{-n+1},\dots,\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{L}),

where
\gamma_{-n+1}:=x_{1},\dots,\gamma_{0}:=x_{n}
, all
h_{i}
are among the
\gamma_{k}
, and for
k>0
, each
\gamma_{k}
is of the form

\gamma_{k}=a*\gamma_{i}\quad\text{or}\quad\gamma_{k}=\gamma_{i}*\gamma_{j},

with
i,j<k
,
a\in\mathbb{K}
, and
*\in\{+,-,\times\}
. The integer
L
is the SLP’s length. Notably, polynomials with many monomials can admit very short SLPs; for example,
(x+1)^{k}
has
k+1
terms but can be computed with a program of length
O(\log k)
.

SLPs were first studied in probabilistic polynomial identity testing and later used in computer algebra for univariate elimination and factorization problems
(
16
;
18
;
19
)
, and polynomial system-solving algorithms based on Newton-Hensel lifting (see e.g.,
(
10
;
11
;
12
;
13
;
31
)
and references therein).

This representation is general: any polynomial of degree
d
in
n
variables can be computed by an SLP of length at most
3\binom{n+d}{n}
, and a sparse polynomial with
N
nonzero terms can be computed with
L=O(Nd)
. Moreover, if two
n
-variate power series are given at precision
d
by SLPs of length
L
, their product truncated at degree
d
can be computed by an SLP of length
O(d^{2}L)
. In fact, all homogeneous components up to degree
d
can be computed using an SLP of length
(d+1)^{2}(L+1)

(
20
)
.

Let
L_{\bm{h}}
and
L_{\bm{g}}
denote the lengths of SLPs for polynomials
\bm{h}=(h_{1},\dots,h_{m})
in
k[Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}]
and for polynomials
\bm{g}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
in
k[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
, respectively.
By appending the SLP of
\bm{h}
after computing
\bm{g}
and substituting
Y_{i}\mapsto g_{i}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
, we obtain a SLP for
\bm{f}=\bm{h}(\bm{g})
.
In particular, the length of a SLP for
\bm{f}=\bm{h}(\bm{g})
is at most
L_{\bm{h}}+L_{\bm{g}}
.

2.3.
Zero-dimensional Parametrizations

Let
V\subset{\overline{k}}{}^{n}
be a finite set defined by polynomials in

k[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
. A
rational univariate representation
(RUR)

\mathscr{R}=((q,v_{1},\dots,v_{n}),\lambda)
of
V
consists of:

•

a square-free polynomial
q\in k[T]
, where
T
is a new indeterminate
and
\deg(q)=|V|
;

•

n
univariate polynomials
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in k[T]
, each of degree at most
\deg(q)
,
such that

q(T)=0,\ X_{i}=\frac{v_{i}(T)}{q^{\prime}(T)}\ (i=1,\dots,n),\text{\ where \ }q^{\prime}=\frac{\partial q}{\partial T};

•

a linear form
\lambda=\lambda_{1}X_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}X_{n}
with coefficients in
k
such that

\lambda(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\equiv Tq^{\prime}\pmod{q},

i.e., the roots of
q
are precisely the values taken by
\lambda
on
V
.

Then we write
V=Z(\mathscr{R})
. This definition implies that
\lambda
takes pairwise distinct values on
V
; it is
therefore called a
separating linear form
. One advantage of RUR is that the rational parametrization, with
q^{\prime}
in the
denominator, provides effective control of coefficient growth when
k=\mathbb{Q}
,
or of the degree in a parameter when working over a rational function field

k(y)
(cf.
(
1
;
13
;
27
)
).

An alternative representation is the
geometric resolution
. A geometric
resolution
\mathscr{S}=((q,w_{1},\dots,w_{n}),\lambda)
of
V
consists of a separating linear form

\lambda
and polynomials
q,w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
in
k[T]
such that
q
is monic and
square-free,
\deg(w_{i})<\deg(q)
for all
i
, and

q(T)=0,\ X_{i}=w_{i}(T)\ (i=1,\dots,n),\ \lambda(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})=T.

If
\mathscr{R}=((q,v_{1},\dots,v_{n}),\lambda)
is a rational univariate representation
of
V
, then, since
q
is square-free,
q^{\prime}
is invertible modulo
q
.
Computing the inverse of
q^{\prime}
modulo
q
yields a geometric resolution

((q,w_{1},\dots,w_{n}),u)
with

w_{i}\equiv(q^{\prime})^{-1}v_{i}\pmod{q}.

We denote by
RUR_to_GR
the procedure that performs this conversion. Let
d=\deg(q)=\deg(V)
.
Using fast polynomial multiplication, the inverse can be computed in

O({\sf M}(d)\log d)
operations in
k
, and the
n
modular multiplications cost

O(n{\sf M}(d))
. Hence, the total cost of the conversion is

{O}{\,\tilde{}\,}(nd)
arithmetic operations in
k
. In particular, this step is
quasi-linear in the output size and negligible compared to the cost of
computing the parametrization itself.
In what follows, we freely switch between these two equivalent representations depending on which form is more convenient for complexity analysis.

3.
Global Newton-Hensel Lifting

Let
\bm{f}=(f_{1}(\bm{X},T),\dots,f_{n}(\bm{X},T))
, with
\bm{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
.
Starting from a parametrization
X_{i}=v_{i}(U)
and a minimal polynomial
q(U)

that describe the solutions of
\bm{f}(\bm{X},T)=0
modulo
T^{m}
, the goal is to compute refined polynomials

V_{i}(U,T)
and

such that

\bm{f}(\bm{V})\equiv 0\pmod{\langle T^{\delta},Q\rangle},\qquad\deg_{T}(V_{i})<\deg_{T}(Q)\leq\delta.

This procedure is called the
GLS_Lifting
algorithm and corresponds to a particular case of
(
13

Under the assumptions that
\bm{f}(\bm{X},T)=0
modulo
T^{m}
and that the Jacobian

at

is invertible, the procedure

in Algorithm

is a Newton-Hensel lifting scheme. Each iteration doubles
the

\lambda(v_{1}(U),\dots,v_{n}(U))=U

Consequently, after
O(\log(\delta))
iterations the algorithm returns a parametrization

(Q(T,U),\bm{V}(T,U))
satisfying
\bm{f}(\bm{V})\equiv 0\pmod{\langle T^{\delta},Q\rangle}
.

4.
Solving Systems of Composable Polynomials

Let
\bm{g}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n}):k^{n}\to k^{n}
and

\bm{h}=(h_{1},\dots,h_{n}):k^{n}\to k^{n}
be polynomial maps.
We consider systems of the form

\bm{f}(\bm{X})=\bm{h}(\bm{g}(\bm{X}))=0,

where
\bm{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
. Then solving
\bm{f}(\bm{X})=0
is equivalent to solving the coupled system

(1)

\begin{cases}h_{1}(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})&=0,\\
\hskip 14.22636pt\vdots&\\
h_{n}(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})&=0,\end{cases}\ \text{and}\ \begin{cases}g_{1}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})-Y_{1}&=0,\\
\hskip 14.22636pt\vdots&\\
g_{n}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})-Y_{n}&=0.\end{cases}

In other words, we first solve the outer system
\bm{h}(\bm{Y})=0
for
\bm{Y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
and then lift each solution through the inner map
\bm{g}
to obtain solutions of
\bm{f}
. This compositional structure will allow us to reduce the computational cost compared to solving
\bm{f}
directly, as the complexity depends on the degrees of
\bm{h}
and
\bm{g}
individually rather than the (potentially much larger) degree of
\bm{f}
.

4.1.
Solving square polynomial systems

We first consider the problem of computing the regular points of a square system

h_{1}(\bm{Y})=\cdots=h_{n}(\bm{Y})=0,\quad\text{where }\bm{Y}=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}),

consisting of
n
equations in
n
variables, using symbolic homotopy techniques.
Solving such square systems is a classical problem in symbolic computation, and efficient algorithms are known.
However, to our knowledge, there is no single reference that provides a complete, detailed algorithm. For this reason, this subsection is devoted to presenting a full procedure.

We also refer the reader to
(
28
;
37
;
15
)
, and references therein, for related discussions and problems.
Algorithm
2
below outputs the
regular points
of
\bm{h}=0
, i.e., the points where the Jacobian of
\bm{h}
has full rank.
Note that isolated points can also be obtained by a minor modification of the last step of this algorithm by using
(
15
, Proposition 12)
.

The
Homotopy_Nonsingular
algorithm solves a square polynomial system by first constructing a simple “start system” whose solutions are easily computed as intersections of random linear forms. It then continuously deforms this system into the target system using a homotopy and lifts the known solutions along this deformation using a Newton–Hensel style lifting procedure (
GLS_Lifting
). Rational function reconstruction finally recovers exact solutions over a rational function field, from which we recover a rational univariate representation over the base field. At the final step, we remove the singular points.

Lemma 4.1.

Let
\bm{h}=(h_{1},\dots,h_{n})
be a polynomial system in
k[\bm{Y}]
with
\bm{Y}=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
.
Let
(q,(v_{1},\dots,v_{n}),\lambda)
be a RUR of a finite set
W\subset V(\bm{h})\cap{\overline{k}}{}^{n}
.

Then there exists a procedure
Remove
that removes all the singular points from
W
using

{O}{\,\tilde{}\,}(C^{2}(n^{3}+n\,L_{\bm{h}}))
operations in
k
, where
L_{\bm{h}}
is the length of a straight-line program computing
\bm{h}
and
C=\deg(q)
.

Proof.

We remove all the points at which the Jacobian matrix
J_{\bm{h}}
of
\bm{h}
vanishes.
To do this, we first construct a straight-line program of length
O(nL_{\bm{h}})
computing
J_{\bm{h}}
.
Next, we evaluate this matrix modulo
q
and use Gaussian elimination modulo
q
to identify the divisors of
q
that need to be removed.
As explained in
(
15
, Section 5)
, this can be done using

{O}{\,\tilde{}\,}(C^{2}(n^{3}+nL_{\bm{h}}))
operations in

Theorem 4.2.

Let
\bm{h}=(h_{1},\dots,h_{n})\in k[\bm{Y}]^{n}
be a square system and let
L
be the length of a straight-line program computing
\bm{h}
.

There exists a randomized algorithm
Homotopy_Nonsingular
that computes a rational univariate representation of all regular solutions of
\bm{h}=0
using

{O}{\,\tilde{}\,}\!\big(CEn(L_{\bm{h}}+n^{2}\gamma)\big)\ \text{operations in }k,

where
C=\deg(h_{1})\cdots\deg(h_{n})
,
\gamma=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}(\deg(h_{i}))
, and

E=(\deg(h_{1})+1)\cdots(\deg(h_{n})+1)
.

Proof.

For
i=1,\dots,n
, the polynomial
p_{i}
is a product of
\deg(h_{i})
linear forms
\ell_{i,j}
.
Each linear form can be computed in
O(n)
operations in
k
, which implies that

\bm{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
can be computed by a straight-line program of length

O\big(n(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{n})\big)

operations in
k
, where
c_{i}=\deg(h_{i})
.

Moreover, the solutions of
\bm{p}=0
are obtained by setting one factor of each

to zero.
Since each
p_{i}
is a product of random linear forms and
k
is a field of characteristic zero, all linear forms

(\ell_{i,j})_{i=1,\dots,n,\,j=1,\dots,c_{i}}

are pairwise distinct. Therefore, the system
\bm{p}=0
has

C=c_{1}\cdots c_{n}

solutions. Each corresponding linear system can be solved in
O(n^{3})
operations, e.g., by Gaussian elimination.
Hence, computing all solutions of
\bm{p}=0
requires

O(Cn^{3})

operations in
k
.

Knowing all the points of
\bm{p}=0
, we can construct a rational univariate representation
\mathscr{R}_{0}

such that
Z(\mathscr{R}_{0})=V(\bm{p})
in time
{O}{\,\tilde{}\,}(Cn)
using fast interpolation
(
36
, Chapter 10)
.
Finally, we remark that, locally around any solution of
\bm{p}=0
, the system
\bm{p}=0
is equivalent to a linear system.
Consequently, the Jacobian matrix of
\bm{p}
is invertible at all its roots.

The system
\r{\in}k[\bm{Y},T]
is defined as

\r{(}\bm{Y},T)=(1-T)\cdot\bm{p}(\bm{Y})+T\cdot\bm{h}(\bm{Y}),

so that in particular
\r{(}\bm{Y},0)=\bm{p}
and
\r{(}\bm{Y},1)=\bm{h}
.
The lengths of the straight-line programs to compute
\bm{h}
and
\bm{p}
are
L
and

O\big(n(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{n})\big),

respectively (as discussed above). Therefore, the system can be computed in

O(L^{\prime}),\text{ with }L^{\prime}=L_{\bm{h}}+n(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{n})=L_{\bm{h}}+n^{2}\gamma,

operations in
k
, with
\gamma=\max(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})
.

Moreover, from the previous discussion, the Jacobian matrix of
\bm{p}=\r{(}\bm{Y},0)
with respect to
\bm{Y}
has full rank at all its solutions.
Hence, all the conditions required to perform the algorithm

are satisfied.

Let us write

Since all the conditions to perform the algorithm

are satisfied and the required precision in

\mathscr{R}

O\big((nL^{\prime}+n^{\omega})\,C\,E\big)

k

(
15

(

(

(

\bm{g}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})

\v{=}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in k[T]^{n}

5.
Conclusions and Future Research

We presented a probabilistic symbolic homotopy algorithm for computing the isolated regular solutions of composable polynomial systems
\bm{f}=\bm{h}(\bm{g})
.
By separating the outer system
\bm{h}
from the inner map
\bm{g}
and combining geometric resolutions with Newton-Hensel lifting, the proposed method achieves arithmetic complexity that scales with the individual degrees and straight-line program sizes of the components, rather than with the typically much larger degrees of the composed system
\bm{f}
.
This demonstrates that exploiting algebraic structure can substantially reduce the cost of symbolic polynomial system solving, particularly for systems arising from subring representations and invariant theory.

Furthermore, as discussed in Section
4.1
, a slight modification of the
Remove
procedure allows us to compute all isolated solutions of the system
\bm{f}=0
, and not only the regular ones. The resulting complexity remains polynomial in the size of the input and in the number of solutions, although with a higher polynomial degree due to the additional algebraic tests required to handle singular points.

Several directions remain for future work.
A first objective is to extend the approach beyond regular solutions and isolated solutions to handle positive-dimensional components, for instance via deflation techniques or equidimensional decompositions.
Another direction is the development of sparse or multihomogeneous variants that better exploit monomial structure and supports.
It would also be valuable to investigate deterministic alternatives to some randomized steps.
Moreover, applications to invariant and symmetric polynomial systems, polynomial optimization, and real solving suggest promising opportunities to integrate the compositional framework into broader symbolic-numeric pipelines.

For our algorithms to become practically applicable, efficient implementations are essential. Although we have already developed a first implementation of our algorithms to validate their correctness on several examples, substantial work remains to achieve high practical efficiency. The implementation of Straight-Line Programs is particularly subtle and has been the subject of significant recent work, notably
(
35
)
, as well as developments within the Mathemagix project. Developing efficient implementations and providing an open-source library constitute important directions for future work.

Finally, the proposed algorithm for solving composable polynomial systems opens several directions for applications in areas where structured nonlinear models arise naturally, such as chemical reaction networks, robotics and geometric control via nonlinear feature liftings of configuration space, and systems biology, where hierarchical interaction structures induce layered polynomial dynamics. In addition, multivariate public-key cryptosystems rely on the computational hardness of solving systems of composable multivariate polynomial equations over finite fields.
