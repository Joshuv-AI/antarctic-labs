Campylobacter spp Bloodstream Infections in Immunocompetent vs Immunodeficient Patients: A Multinational Cohort Study (2009–2024)

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Campylobacter spp bloodstream infections (BSIs) are an emerging clinical challenge, yet information on their clinical features remains limited. We aimed to evaluate their differential clinical characteristics and outcomes in immunocompetent (IC) and immunodeficient (ID) patients.MethodsA multicenter, multinational, retrospective cohort study of consecutive Campylobacter spp BSIs between 2009 and 2024 was carried out. Demographics, Charlson comorbidity index, types of immunodeficiencies, clinical characteristics, and antimicrobial susceptibility were analyzed. The primary outcomes were relapse or reinfection and death (all-cause mortality outcome).ResultsA total of 261 episodes were included, 89 (34.1%) in IC and 172 (65.9%) in ID patients. Among the 172 ID patients, 104 (60.5%) and 68 (39.5%) had humoral or nonhumoral immunodeficiency (primary or secondary), and 42 (24.4%) had primary immunodeficiencies (PIDs). Patients with humoral immunodeficiency (humoral-ID patients) were younger (median age, 40 years [interquartile range, 23–66 years]), had lower Charlson comorbidity indexes (2 [1–5]), and had a higher frequency of previous Campylobacter spp infection. Resistance rates to fluoroquinolones (75%) and macrolides (14.5%) were high. BSI relapse or reinfection occurred in 21 (8.0%) patients, mostly humoral-ID patients (n = 19 [18.3%]), 11 (57.9%) of whom had PID. Death occurred with 30 days (all-cause mortality outcome) in 26 patients (9.9%) across the entire cohort; the 30-day mortality rate was lower in humoral-ID (n = 5 [4.8%]) than in IC (n = 12 [13.5%]; P = .05) patients.ConclusionsHumoral-ID patients and those with Campylobacter spp BSI were younger and had fewer comorbid conditions, and Campylobacter coli was the most frequent species. Relapse or reinfection was common in humoral-ID patients, particularly those with PID. The antimicrobial resistance in C coli and Campylobacter jejuni represents a clinical concern.

Campylobacter spp are the most frequent causative agents of bacterial enteritis in developed countries [1], although secondary bloodstream infection (BSI) occurs in only 1:100–1:1000 episodes of enteric infection [2]; however, studies published in recent years found a 5- and 10-fold increase in the incidence of Campylobacter spp BSIs among hospital-admitted adults in Australia from 2004 to 2013 and in Israel from 2009 to 2021, respectively, without a corresponding increase in detection in stool cultures [3, 4]. The distribution of species involved in Campylobacter spp BSI has changed over time. In a French series of 183 cases of Campylobacter spp BSI prior to 2004 [5], Campylobacter fetus was the most commonly identified species, found in 53% of cases, while in a more recent study from 2015 to 2019 [6], Campylobacter jejuni and C fetus were the most frequent species, at 42.9% and 42.6%, respectively.

Although campylobacteriosis is largely a self-limiting gastrointestinal illness in immunocompetent (IC) patients, in immunodeficient (ID) hosts, particularly those with humoral immunodeficiencies, it can cause persistent or recurrent infections that occasionally progress to bacteremia and extraintestinal disease [7]. Approximately half of all Campylobacter spp BSI episodes occur in ID patients [4]. Among the different types of immunocompromise, humoral immunodeficiencies [6] stand out. In a 5-year retrospective French study including 592 Campylobacter spp BSI episodes, 81 (13.7%) occurred in patients with hypogammaglobulinemia [6]. In addition, Campylobacter coli BSI predominated among patients with hypogammaglobulinemia, compared with C jejuni or C fetus BSI [6].

The frequency of relapse is variable and difficult to measure, as many studies do not include these data, but reported rates have ranged from 0% to 14% [4, 8–10]. In additional, relapse is dependent on patient characteristics, with a higher risk of infection in patients with primary immunodeficiency (PID) [11]. Campylobacter spp BSI is associated with highly variable mortality rates, ranging from 2% to 25% [4, 12, 13], discrepancies likely attributable to differing inclusion criteria across studies. In contrast, mortality rates for patients with nonbacteremic enteritis range from only 1% to 2% [2, 4].

A primary challenge in treating this infection is increasing antimicrobial resistance, particularly to quinolones [2, 6]. Furthermore, a 2019 report from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention identified antibiotic-resistant Campylobacter spp as a “significant public health threat” [14], with about one-third of isolates in the United States being resistant to macrolides or ciprofloxacin. Despite the significance of the problem, there is no consensus on the most appropriate treatment for Campylobacter BSIs or on the prevention of relapses.

In summary, Campylobacter spp BSI episodes, especially those caused by C coli, represent an emerging challenge that disproportionately affects patients with humoral immunodeficiency (humoral-ID patients). To date, no specific studies have described the clinical characteristics, predisposing factors, and differential clinical outcomes of Campylobacter spp BSI in ID patients nor compared them with those in IC individuals. The aim of the present study is to evaluate the differential clinical characteristics and outcomes of Campylobacter spp BSI, in both IC and ID patients and specifically in humoral-ID patients

METHODS

Study Designs and Population

A multicenter, multinational and retrospective cohort study was conducted, including consecutive cases of Campylobacter spp BSI episodes, without age restriction, from 2009 to 2024. The 27 participating hospitals were from 5 countries: Spain, Italy, France, the United Kingdom, and Brazil. We excluded episodes of Campylobacter spp BSI in the same patient with an interval ≤15 days, when the species were the same. No intervention beyond standard clinical practice was performed to manage the patient's condition, with all clinical decisions made by the physician responsible for the patient.

Data Collection

A specific case report form was designed for this study to record the demographics, chronic underlying diseases, Charlson comorbidity index, and presence and type of immunodeficiency for all patients. For patients with PID, the date of diagnosis, genetic mutation, and immunologic phenotype were also recorded. The clinical characteristics of the BSI episodes were collected, including general symptoms and signs, organ-specific and apparatus-related symptoms, and immune-mediated signs and symptoms. Histories of Campylobacter spp infections occurring before the study episode were also considered, as well as prophylaxis with macrolides.

Clinical Definitions

BSI was defined as the isolation of Campylobacter spp from ≥1 blood culture bottle. Relapse was defined as the isolation of Campylobacter spp from blood cultures occurring ≥15 days after a documented negative culture and after resolution of clinical symptoms and signs; patients were evaluated during all available follow-ups. Appropriate empiric therapy was considered if the regimen included ≥1 antimicrobial with activity against the Campylobacter spp isolate and when microbiological information was available. The 7-day and 30-day all-cause mortality outcomes were defined as including deaths from any cause since the first positive blood culture. We defined PID according to the criteria established by the European Society for Immunodeficiencies [15].

The ID patients were those with PID or secondary immunodeficiencies, including solid organ and hematological stem cell transplant recipients; patients with hematological cancers, active solid organ neoplasia, or human immunodeficiency virus infection with a CD4+ lymphocyte count <200/μL; and those receiving immunosuppressive therapy for miscellaneous diseases. Humoral immunodeficiency was defined as PID with hypogammaglobulinemia, previous use of monoclonal antibodies targeting CD20, allogeneic stem cell transplantation, or hematological cancers with a predominant humoral effect (multiple myeloma or chronic lymphocytic leukemia). Sepsis was defined according to Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 guidelines [16].

Microbiological Studies

The identification of Campylobacter spp clinical isolates and their susceptibility to antimicrobials were performed at the clinical microbiology laboratories of the participating centers, according to standard protocols. Minimum inhibitory concentrations were determined according to the International Organization for Standardization 20776-1. The European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) cutoff values [17] were used to determine antimicrobial susceptibility for macrolides (erythromycin and azithromycin), fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin), and tetracycline and the Comité de l’antibiogramme de la Société Française de Microbiologie (CA-SFM) cutoff values [18] were used for gentamicin, amoxicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanate and carbapenems (imipenem, meropenem, and ertapenem). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was not performed on all isolates for all antimicrobials.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Virgen Macarena and Virgen del Rocío University Hospitals at its meeting held on 22 December 2022 (CEI 11/2022), and it was conducted in compliance with current legislation, the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and standards of good clinical laboratory practice. Given the retrospective study design, the absence of any intervention, and the use of anonymized data, the Research Ethics Committee granted a waiver of written informed consent.

Statistical Analysis

A descriptive analysis was conducted, with categorical variables expressed as percentages and continuous variables as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on data distribution. Demographics, comorbid conditions, the presence and type of immunocompromise, previous episodes of Campylobacter spp infection, and frequency of Campylobacter spp causing the current BSI episodes were analyzed for the entire cohort and compared between ID and IC groups. Univariate analyses were performed using χ2 or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables and Student t or nonparametric tests for continuous variables.

The primary clinical outcomes were relapse or reinfection and 30-day all-cause mortality outcome. Secondary outcomes included hospital admission, length of hospital stay (in days), 30-day microbiological cure (defined as negative blood cultures, when available), and 7-day all-cause mortality outcome. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 22.0, and P values were adjusted with the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate method for multiple comparisons (differences were considered significant at q < 0.05). This analysis was reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) recommendations [19] (Supplementary Table 1).

RESULTS

In total, 261 episodes of Campylobacter spp BSI were included, 89 (34.1%) in IC and 172 (65.9%) in ID patients (Table 1). The types of immune deficit in ID patients are specified in Table 2. ID patients had more previous Campylobacter spp infections (17.4%; P = .03), including BSI episodes (10.5%; P = .05). Regarding the current episodes, there were no differences between IC and ID patients in symptoms and signs, including fever, diarrhea, and sepsis (Supplementary Table 2). Campylobacter spp isolates from blood included C jejuni 147 (56.3%), C coli 61 (23.4%), and C fetus 40 (15.3%), with other species in 13 episodes (5.0%). From 2008–2012 to 2020–2024, the episodes caused by C coli increased from 12% to 26.9%, although without significant differences (P = .09) for the other Campylobacter spp, The species distribution differed between the IC and ID patients (P = .02) (Table 1), with higher C coli prevalence in ID patients (P = .007). Susceptibility to antimicrobials is detailed in Table 3, with high rates of resistance to macrolides (14.5%) and ciprofloxacin (73.8%), particularly in C coli and C jejuni.

Across the entire cohort, relapses/reinfections occurred in 21 episodes (8%), with a lower rate in IC patients (2.2%) than in ID patients (11.0%). Seventeen patients had 1 relapse episode, and 4 had 2; those with 2 episodes were humoral-ID patients. Relapses occurred a median of 80 days (interquartile range, 17–529 days) after the first Campylobacter spp BSI episode.

The 30-day all-cause mortality rate in the entire cohort was 9.9%, without differences between IC and ID patients (Table 1). However, the mortality rate with C coli BSI was higher in IC patients (25%) than in ID patients (4.1%) (P = .04) (Supplementary Table 3).

In the IC patients, deaths were associated with sepsis, lung infiltrates, and extraintestinal disease (Supplementary Table 4). The distribution of Campylobacter spp revealed no differences between nonsurvivors and survivors, nor was the use of inappropriate therapy neither associated with mortality risk (Table 4). Among the ID patients, 30-day all-cause mortality outcomes were associated with sepsis (P = .01) and Campylobacter spp distribution (P = .04), mostly due to the high frequency of C fetus (P = .007). Inappropriate therapy was most frequent in ID patients who died (85.7% vs 47.5%) (Table 4 and Supplementary Table 4).

Analysis of IC Patients and Those with Nonhumoral or Humoral Immunodeficiency

When comparing IC patients with patients who had nonhumoral immunodeficiency (nonhumoral-ID patients), no differences were observed in the Campylobacter spp distribution or in the clinical outcomes, including 30-day mortality outcomes. In contrast, IC patients had lower C coli and higher C fetus frequencies than humoral-ID patients. The 30-day mortality rate was higher in IC than in humoral-ID patients (P = .05); however, humoral-ID patients had higher rates of relapses/reinfections than IC patients (P = .007) (Table 5).

Humoral-ID patients had a higher frequency of previous Campylobacter spp BSI than nonhumoral-ID patients (P = .003), and they also had higher frequencies of C coli (P = .008) and lower C fetus (P = .01) BSI. The appropriate therapy and 30-day mortality rates were similar in nonhumoral-ID and humoral-ID patients; however, relapses/reinfections were more frequent in the humoral-ID group (P = .007) (Table 5). Notably, 19 (90.5%) of all 21 relapses/reinfections occurred in humoral-ID patients versus only 2 in IC and none in nonhumoral-ID patients (P = .007). Eleven (57.9%) of these 19 patients had PID and 8 (42.1%) had hematological cancers and were receiving B-cell–depleting treatment. Extraintestinal disease and lung infiltrates occurred in 4 (21.1%) of the 19 cases of relapses in humoral-ID patients, although these associations were not significant (P = .37 and P = .14, respectively).

DISCUSSION

Our findings demonstrated that Campylobacter spp BSIs mostly affect patients with immunocompromise, emphasizing the impact of humoral immunodeficiency. C jejuni was the most frequent, both in IC and ID groups, but C coli was more prevalent in ID patients, particularly in humoral-ID patients. Relapses/reinfections are a significant challenge in the ID subgroup, especially in those with agammaglobulinemia, and humoral-ID patients had more previous Campylobacter spp infections, including BSIs, supporting the importance of humoral immunity in the response to Campylobacter spp infection [20].

The frequency of C jejuni in our cohort is consistent with findings of previous studies [2, 6, 9, 21]. We also observed an increasing number of C coli infections. Although earlier studies [5, 22] reported a lower number of cases, more recent ones document a relatively increased prevalence [4, 6]. The species affecting patients with PID and humoral-ID patients are in agreement with the findings of Tinévez et al [6], showing that C coli was more common in patients with hypogammaglobulinemia; however, their study did not specify the types of immunodeficiency or whether primary or secondary.

Our study revealed that antimicrobial resistance is a particular concern, especially in C coli, with resistance rates exceeding 90% for ciprofloxacin and 50% for azithromycin, which are considered first- and second-line treatments for Campylobacter spp infections [23]. Appropriate empirical antimicrobial therapy was administered more frequently in ID patients, particularly humoral-ID patients, who also experienced higher rates of hospitalization, perhaps because of the perceived frailty in this population. The 7-day mortality rate was low, with a 30-day mortality rate of 9.9% for the entire cohort. Other studies have reported mortality rates ranging from 0% to 18% [6, 9]. Moreover, while the presence of humoral immunodeficiency seems to increase the risk of Campylobacter spp BSI, it does not increase mortality risk.

The fact that humoral-ID patients were more likely to have previous Campylobacter infections and relapses/reinfections aligns with data from previous studies. Fernández-Cruz et al [9] reported 7 cases of relapse, 6 of them in patients with PID. In a pediatric study [11], all but a single relapse occurred in patients with common variable immunodeficiency or agammaglobulinemia. The high risk of relapse/reinfection in agammaglobulinemia, even months or years after the initial episode, has been reported elsewhere [9, 24, 25]. In the present study, almost all patients with relapse had agammaglobulinemia or had previously received B-cell–depleting therapies. The reason patients with agammaglobulinemia are at increased risk of recurrence and infection remains unknown; however, a deficiency of other immunoglobulin (Ig) classes, such as IgA, may contribute to this [20]. Although IgA deficiency is common in other forms of humoral PID that are more prevalent than X-linked agammaglobulinemia, such as common variable immunodeficiency, only 7 episodes of Campylobacter spp BSIs occurred in patients with this form of the condition, and none of them experienced relapses.

Regarding clinical findings, fever and diarrhea were the most common symptoms, without differences regarding immune status. Humoral-ID patients were younger, with fewer comorbid conditions, and had higher rate of skin signs. In our study, half of the patients had diarrhea, with no differences depending on immune status. Humoral immunodeficiency, including low IgG levels and the use of rituximab, has been associated with a prolonged duration of diarrhea [26], and the Campylobacter spp may have contributed to this pattern. Humoral-ID patients were more frequently infected with C coli, a species associated with higher rates of relapse and prior infections [27]. Other potential explanations include the levels of specific IgG against C coli in Ig replacement preparations, as observed with C fetus in a published case [28], or the persistence of an intestinal reservoir of Campylobacter spp [29] after treatment.

The current study has several limitations that must be considered. First, the small sizes of the IC and ID groups, along with the diverse causes of immunodeficiency, prevented us from performing a broad multivariate analysis to determine various risk factors for relapse and, although the univariate analysis was adjusted for multiple comparisons, the results should be considered as hypothesis generating in the variables with low number of events. Second, due to the retrospective nature of this study, certain variables were unavailable in some cases, such as serum Ig levels and antimicrobial susceptibilities, despite the effort made to review and validate the database to recover as much data as possible across all centers. Moreover, the long period of patient inclusion, from 2009 to 2024, make it difficult to recover confident data about the possible suspected food consumption. Finally, secondary immunodeficiencies are followed up in the outpatient clinics for long periods, in contrast with shorter follow-up for IC individuals, which may lead to fewer identifications of Campylobacter spp BSI among IC patients. However, to minimize this potential bias, we searched routinely for Campylobacter spp BSI in each patient, in the microbiology department record of each participating hospital.

Despite these limitations, our study also has several strengths. It is currently one of the largest cohorts of Campylobacter spp BSI, as well as the first to include a significant number of patients with PID. Its multicenter design, including centers of varying complexity and reference centers for the treatment of PID in children and adults, has enabled the evaluation of the impact of PID, a rare disease, on the prognosis and characteristics of Campylobacter spp BSI. In addition, including cases until the end of 2024 has provided data on a change in the Campylobacter spp causing BSI, specifically the moderate increase in C coli, a particularly resistant species, thereby supporting the validity, timeliness, and representativeness of the conclusions.

To summarize, BSIs caused by Campylobacter spp, particularly C coli, frequently affect humoral-ID patients and especially those with PID. The high risk of relapses significantly hinders treatment in these patients. In this context, future studies are needed to evaluate the pathogenesis of relapses, and intervention studies should be conducted to establish optimal antibiotic treatment and its duration.
