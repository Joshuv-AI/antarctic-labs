A poly(I:C)/QS-21-based in situ vaccine synergizes with anti-PD-1 therapy to overcome tumor immunoresistance
Authors: Wenbo Zhang, Xinrui Ni, Luoyi Zhuo, Jiangrui Liu, Guang Yang, Jianming Li, Lingai Tang, Xiuli Du, Chun Shan, Guoqiang Feng, Wenguang Wang, Qi Gao, Qiang Sui, Min Xia, Chunwang Yuan, Dexiang Chen, Yong Yang, Duowei Wang, Xianjing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDespite remarkable advances in cancer immunotherapy, immunoresistance remains a critical barrier to its broader success. To address this challenge, we develop an adjuvant system that combines polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid (poly(I:C)) with QS-21 and functions as an in situ cancer vaccine, effectively boosting antitumor immunity across diverse tumor types. This synergistic formulation leverages QS-21-mediated cytosolic delivery of poly(I:C) to potentiate the TBK1-IRF3-type I interferon axis. This activation licenses robust IRF1 nuclear translocation as a downstream transcriptional integrator, thereby inducing immunogenic cell death in tumor cells. Intratumoral administration of this system enhances dendritic cell maturation and neoantigen presentation, which elicits a robust neoantigen-specific T cell response and generates protective immune memory that prevents tumor recurrence. Moreover, the adjuvant sensitizes tumors to anti-PD-1 therapy and, when combined with microwave ablation, significantly improves antitumor efficacy. Collectively, this approach offers a promising strategy to overcome immunoresistance and advance combination immunotherapies.Keywords: in situ vaccine, poly(I:C), QS-21, immunogenic cell death, immunological memory

Introduction

In recent years, tumor vaccines have emerged as a promising strategy in cancer immunotherapy, aiming to harness the patient’s immune system to target tumor antigens. These vaccines are composed of tumor antigens and adjuvants that stimulate a specific immune response.1,2,3 However, the high heterogeneity and low immunogenicity of tumors pose significant challenges for traditional vaccine development.4,5,6 This often requires laborious ex vivo identification and personalized formulation, escalating both the time and financial investment in treatment.7,8 Conversely, in situ tumor vaccines offer an approach by converting the tumor microenvironment (TME) into an antigen production site.3 By bypassing the need for ex vivo antigen isolation, in situ vaccines present several advantages: reduced systemic toxicity, enhanced bioavailability, personalized treatment potential, and decreased costs in terms of time and resources.9 This strategy is clinically feasible and has yielded promising results, particularly with oncolytic viruses, unmethylated cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG) oligonucleotides,10,11 viral RNA mimics based on polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid (poly(I:C)),12,13 mRNA, and cDNA plasmids.14,15 These attributes render in situ tumor vaccines a compelling candidate for clinical translation and therapeutic application.

Poly(I:C) has been utilized in various formulations such as Hiltonol and BO-112 to enhance antitumor immunity.12,16 It is currently under investigation with immune checkpoint therapies in clinical trials (NCT04525859, NCT04420975, and NCT04508140). However, in some clinical trials, the administration of a single 1- to 2-mg dose of poly(I:C) has been associated with adverse reactions such as fever, local erythema, and coagulopathy (NCT02166905, NCT01984892, and NCT01079741). When used as an adjuvant in combination with bevacizumab, 32% of patients in the high-dose group (1.6 mg) experienced severe adverse reactions, including some brain disorders such as seizure, encephalopathy, cerebrovascular accident, and mental status changes. At the same time, 21.43% of patients suffered from kidney and urinary system diseases like proteinuria, hematuria, and urinary incontinence. Additionally, 7.14% of patients experienced lymphopenia (NCT02078648). These issues indicate that the toxicity of poly(I:C) increases with the dose when it exerts therapeutic effects in clinical applications.17,18,19 Therefore, reducing the clinical dosage and toxicity of poly(I:C) while expanding its efficacy has become an urgent problem that we must address.

QS-21, a key component of the AS01 adjuvant system, is renowned for its Th1-type immune response stimulation, leading to the production of significant antibody titers, including IgG2a, IgG2b, and IgG1, as well as antigen-specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes.20,21,22 Drawing on the combination model of AS01, we co-administered QS-21 with poly(I:C) to investigate their in vivo antitumor effects and immune activation capabilities. To address the short half-life and potential systemic toxicity of high-dose poly(I:C), we co-encapsulated it with QS-21 in cationic and neutral liposomes to form an adjuvant system, MA105. This system, developed for a herpes zoster vaccine, is currently under review for drug marketing authorization.

Herein, we demonstrate the effectiveness of the poly(I:C) + QS-21 adjuvant system as an in situ vaccine that can be used as a monotherapy or synergistically with checkpoint blockade and microwave ablation (MWA). We observed that QS-21 synergized with poly(I:C), inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD) in tumor cells by promoting IRF1 nuclear translocation. Furthermore, we demonstrate that by employing this combination as an in situ vaccine directly targeting local tumors, the efficacy of immunotherapeutic interventions can be amplified.

Results

Development, characterization, and antitumor effect of poly(I:C) + QS-21-based adjuvant system

To enable the efficient in vivo co-delivery of poly(I:C) and QS-21, we engineered both neutral and cationic liposomal formulations and developed an adjuvant system, MA105, based on the optimized composition shown in Figures 1A and 1B. To ensure formulation stability, uniformity, and batch-to-batch reproducibility, QS-21 content was fixed at 50 μg per dose, consistent with the design principles of AS01B.23 Liposomal formulations were subsequently prepared across a range of poly(I:C): QS-21 mass ratios and comprehensively characterized for physicochemical properties. Increasing the poly(I:C): QS-21 ratio to 16:1 and 32:1 resulted in polydispersity index (PDI) values exceeding 0.3 and markedly compromised particle size uniformity (Figure 1C; Figure S1A), whereas the zeta potential remained comparable to that of the 8:1 formulation (Figure 1D). To further evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of formulations with different ratios, we examined their antitumor activities in the B16F10 subcutaneous melanoma model. Intratumoral administration of the 8:1, 16:1, and 32:1 formulations exhibited markedly superior tumor growth inhibition compared with the 2:1 ratio (Figures S1B and S1C); however, the 16:1 and 32:1 ratios required substantially higher poly(I:C) doses without providing additional therapeutic benefit. Based on these integrated physicochemical and in vivo data, the 8:1 ratio was selected as optimal, balancing robust antitumor activity with favorable liposomal stability. Under the optimized 8:1 ratio, the poly(I:C) + QS-21 liposomal formulation exhibited an average particle size of 210 nm, a zeta potential of −7 to −8 mV, and a PDI of approximately 0.2 (Figures 1C and 1D). Additionally, the encapsulation efficiency of poly(I:C) reached 99.64%, with a drug loading capacity of 37.15%; free QS-21 was not detected. Transmission electron microscopy further confirmed the uniform morphology and monodispersity of the liposomes (Figure 1E). To further evaluate the dose-dependent antitumor efficacy of the poly(I:C) + QS-21 combination, we performed in vivo pharmacodynamic studies in the B16F10 melanoma model, systematically testing intratumoral doses of 12.5, 25, 50, and 100 μg (total poly(I:C) + QS-21 mass). The results demonstrated significant tumor growth inhibition across all tested doses, with the 100-μg dose exhibiting the most pronounced and consistent inhibitory effect (Figures 1F and 1G).

Having identified the optimal dose, we next benchmarked the poly(I:C) + QS-21 combination against alternative TLR agonist-based formulations, including monophosphoryl lipid A (MPLA) and single-stranded RNA (ssRNA), each prepared as liposomes and administered intratumorally in the B16F10 melanoma model. Poly(I:C) + QS-21 achieved a tumor inhibition rate of 96.2%, substantially outperforming MPLA + QS-21 (44.4%) and MPLA + ssRNA (51.2%) (Figure 1H), establishing the poly(I:C) + QS-21 liposomal formulation as a particularly promising platform for in situ tumor vaccination. Furthermore, using the optimized dose of 100 μg, we examined visceral toxicity in the B16F10 melanoma model and found no signs of pathological damage or inflammatory responses in key organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney (Figure 1I), indicating that intratumoral poly(I:C) + QS-21 administration is well tolerated without inducing systemic toxicity.

To evaluate the breadth of antitumor activity, we extended these studies to additional syngeneic tumor models, including MC38 and CT26 colorectal cancers, Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) lung cancer, 4T1 triple-negative breast cancer, and H22 hepatocellular carcinoma. Intratumoral poly(I:C) + QS-21 treatment produced robust tumor growth inhibition across all models, with complete tumor regression observed in a subset of animals (Figures S1D–S1H). Notably, in the MMTV-PyMT spontaneous breast cancer model, local poly(I:C) + QS-21 injection significantly suppressed both primary tumor growth and distal pulmonary metastasis (Figures S1I–S1K), demonstrating that locoregional treatment can elicit systemic antitumor immunity.

Poly(I:C) + QS-21 triggers immunogenic cell death and reprograms the tumor microenvironment

Given the potent antitumor efficacy of the poly(I:C) + QS-21 adjuvant system, we sought to delineate its capacity to augment immune cell infiltration within the TME after intratumoral injection. To this end, we scrutinized the status of T cell penetration, the presence of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and the polarization of macrophages. Upon intratumoral delivery, the combination of poly(I:C) and QS-21 induced a pronounced enrichment of CD3+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and M1-polarized macrophages in the TME of B16F10 subcutaneous tumors, relative to monotherapy with either agent alone. The infiltration of CD4+ T cells exhibited an upward trend, whereas the alterations in MDSCs and M2 macrophages were not statistically significant, with their proportions remaining comparatively low (Figure 2A; Figure S2A). To ascertain the specific immune cells responsible for the observed antitumor activity, we selectively depleted CD8+ T cells and macrophages in mice using anti-CD8 and anti-CSF1R monoclonal antibodies, respectively. Our findings demonstrated that depletion of CD8+T cells or macrophages did not diminish the antitumor efficacy of poly(I:C) + QS-21 in immunocompetent mice (Figures 2B and 2C; Figures S2B and S2C). Consistent results were observed in immunodeficient nude mice, where the combination treatment achieved a relative tumor inhibition rate of 62.0% compared with vehicle controls (Figure S2D). This suggests that the antitumor effect of poly(I:C) + QS-21 may be attributed to its intrinsic cytotoxic properties toward tumor cells. Subsequently, we elucidated the individual and synergistic antitumor effects of poly(I:C) and QS-21 in B16F10 subcutaneous tumor, and our results demonstrated that the combinatorial treatment of poly(I:C) + QS-21 yielded a markedly superior antitumor effect (Figure 2D). In parallel with the in vivo experiments, complementary in vitro studies showed that exposure of tumor cells to poly(I:C) + QS-21 liposomes for 72 h resulted in a substantial reduction in cell viability, which was only ∼30% at 32 μg/mL (Figures 2E and 2F).

How does tumor cell death shape antitumor immunity and drive the remodeling of the TME? In light of this, we explored the ICD markers.24 Prompted by the synergistic action of poly(I:C) and QS-21, our data indicate that the combination of these agents facilitates the translocation of intracellular calreticulin to the cell membrane, effectively dispatching an “eat me” signal (Figure 2G). Simultaneously, we observed a significant upregulation in the mRNA expression levels of cytokines pivotal for T cell chemotaxis, including Ifn-β, Cxcl9, Cxcl10, and Ccl525 (Figure 2H). Furthermore, the concentration of ATP, recognized as a critical “find me” signal, saw a substantial elevation in response to the combined treatment, surpassing the levels observed with individual treatments of poly(I:C) and QS-2126 (Figure 2I). Reactive oxygen species (ROS), a hallmark of immunogenic death, exhibited a notable increase in relative expression levels following the dual application of poly(I:C) and QS-21, underscoring the agents’ potential to induce ICD in tumor cells (Figure 2J). Collectively, these findings suggest that the poly(I:C) and QS-21 combination, unlike the vehicle, poly(I:C) alone, and QS-21 alone, can trigger ICD. This process is associated with increased infiltration and activation of immune cells in the TME, which correlates with enhanced antitumor efficacy in multiple models.

IRF1 mediates poly(I:C) + QS-21-induced immunogenic cell death through type I interferon signaling

To investigate the molecular pathways induced by poly(I:C) + QS-21, we performed RNA sequencing on B16F10 cells treated with poly(I:C) + QS-21. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that, compared with the vehicle, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the poly(I:C) + QS-21 combination group were predominantly enriched for type I interferon response (Figure S3A). This pathway is associated with features of ICD in tumor cells. To understand the contribution of poly(I:C) and QS-21 to gene expression, we compared the transcriptional response between poly(I:C), QS-21, and poly(I:C) + QS-21. To go beyond the simple pairwise comparison between immunostimulants, we used a statistical approach initially designed for the analysis of combination drugs.27 This approach categorizes the potential contributive relationships that poly(I:C) and QS-21 provide for each gene expressed (Figure 3A). As indicated by the high proportion of genes categorized under the irrelevance of poly(I:C) or QS-21, in addition to the simple additive effects, we observed that 27 genes were affected by synergistic interactions (Figure 3A). Intriguingly, a distinct subset of genes emerged that remained unresponsive to the individual treatments but were significantly induced upon their co-administration (Figure 3B). We designated these as “synergy” genes, highlighting a dimension of poly(I:C) and QS-21 interaction. To elucidate the most influential relationships that drive gene expression alterations post-combination treatment, we conducted an intersectional analysis using the previously identified DEGs. This strategic approach yielded 22 genes with synergistic effects (Figure S3B), underscoring their pivotal role in the combined treatment’s impact. Additionally, only 1 gene was categorized as “emergent differentially expressed gene” (Figure S3C). GO analysis of the 22 identified genes uncovered a significant enrichment in interferon-related signaling pathways (Figure S3D), and most of them belong to interferon-stimulating genes (ISGs).28 Notably, IRF1, an interferon transcription factor, emerged as a potential keystone in this regulatory network.29 Our in vitro experiments demonstrated that total-cell IRF1 protein levels rose markedly and a pronounced accumulation of IRF1 was detected in the nucleus following a 24-h treatment with poly(I:C) + QS-21, with no significant alteration observed in the cytoplasm (Figures 3C and 3D). This nuclear translocation and upregulation of IRF1 suggests a pivotal role in the activation of interferon pathways.

To further investigate the specific contribution of IRF1 to the antitumor effects of the combination treatment, we performed IRF1 knockdown in the B16F10 cell line using two independent small hairpin RNA constructs (Figure S3E). In vitro cell viability assays revealed that IRF1 knockdown modestly increased the proliferative capacity of B16F10 cells (Figure S4A). These cells were then implanted subcutaneously to assess the impact of IRF1 depletion on the therapeutic efficacy of poly(I:C) + QS-21. The findings revealed that IRF1 knockdown effectively abrogated the antitumor effects induced by the combination treatment (Figures 3E and 3F). Moreover, to ascertain the role of IRF1 in promoting ICD, we assessed the impact of IRF1 knockdown on ROS levels, ATP release, and calreticulin exposure in B16F10 cells post-treatment with poly(I:C) + QS-21 (Figures S4B–S4D). The results indicated that IRF1 knockdown significantly dampened the enhancement of these ICD markers, suggesting a crucial role for IRF1 in mediating the synergistic effects of poly(I:C) + QS-21.

We next sought to determine how the combination of poly(I:C) and QS-21 achieves enhanced IRF1 nuclear accumulation. We hypothesized that QS-21 facilitates cytosolic poly(I:C) delivery by exploiting its high affinity for cholesterol: upon endosomal integration, QS-21 forms transient membrane pores (Lacaille-Dubois, 201930; Lorent et al., 201431), disrupting endosomal integrity and enabling poly(I:C) escape into the cytosol to engage RIG-I-like receptor signaling. To test this model, we first confirmed endosomal internalization as the dominant entry route using pharmacological inhibitors. Pretreatment with dynasore or genistein markedly suppressed IRF1 induction (Figure S3F), while chlorpromazine, a clathrin assembly inhibitor, had no appreciable effect on IRF1 expression, indicating that the poly(I:C) + QS-21 formulation is internalized predominantly via dynamin-dependent and caveolae/lipid-raft-mediated endocytic pathways, rather than through clathrin-mediated endocytosis or direct plasma membrane translocation. Besides, lactate dehydrogenase release assay detected no significant plasma membrane disruption at therapeutic doses within 3 h (Figure S3G), excluding non-specific membrane lysis as a confounding mechanism. We then evaluated the membrane-permeabilizing activity of the poly(I:C) + QS-21 formulation under endosomal pH conditions (pH 6.0) using a hemolysis assay. The results showed that QS-21 incorporation into liposomes significantly enhanced membrane disruption relative to controls (Figure 3G), supporting its capacity to promote endosomal escape.

Consistent with enhanced cytosolic poly(I:C) delivery, the combination treatment induced significantly elevated levels of phosphorylated TBK1 (p-TBK1) within 3 h compared with poly(I:C) monotherapy (Figure 3H) and markedly increased nuclear IRF3 accumulation at the same time point (Figures 3I and 3J). To further investigate the upstream regulatory mechanism of IRF1 induction, we generated IRF3-knockdown B16F10 cells (Figure S3H). IRF3 knockdown markedly attenuated the increase in IRF1 expression induced by the poly(I:C) + QS-21 adjuvant system (Figure 3K). Activated IRF3 is known to drive transcription of type I interferons, particularly IFN-β (Figure 2H), which subsequently engage autocrine and paracrine amplification loops.32 To test this mechanism, we generated B16F10 cells with knockdown of IFNAR1 or IFNGR1 (Figure S3I). IFNAR1 knockdown significantly suppressed poly(I:C) + QS-21-induced IRF1 upregulation, whereas IFNGR1 knockdown had minimal effect (Figure 3L), establishing the type I interferon signaling axis as the primary driver of IRF1 induction. Taken together, these data support a mechanistic model in which QS-21 enhances endosomal membrane permeability to facilitate cytosolic poly(I:C) release, thereby activating the TBK1-IRF3-type I interferon axis, culminating in sustained IRF1 nuclear accumulation and amplification of ICD.

IRF7, an interferon-inducible transcription factor and another downstream mediator of poly(I:C) signaling, was also examined. Although IRF7 expression was upregulated following poly(I:C) + QS-21 stimulation, its expression level and nuclear accumulation were not significantly different from those induced by poly(I:C) alone (Figures S4E and S4F). Consistently, knockdown of IRF7 in B16F10 cells (Figure S3J) did not affect poly(I:C) + QS-21-induced ICD (Figures S4G–S4J). These results suggest that IRF7 is not a major contributor to the ICD process induced by the poly(I:C) + QS-21 adjuvant system.

Poly(I:C) + QS-21 adjuvant system enhances dendritic cell maturation and provokes tumor neoantigen-specific T cell response, eliciting durable immunological memory

The combination of poly(I:C) and QS-21 elicited ICD within tumor cells, leading us to wonder whether this treatment could initiate the cancer-immunity cycle, thereby activating systemic antitumor immune responses and inducing tumor-specific immune memory. Dendritic cells (DCs) are pivotal in orchestrating local inflammation, T cell recruitment, and activation. However, tumor cells and local inflammation have been shown to impair the abundance and costimulatory capacity of intratumoral DCs through various mechanisms, thus suppressing the generation of effector T cell responses.33,34 In light of this, we conducted a comprehensive investigation into DC infiltration and activation within melanoma tissues. Utilizing flow cytometry, we discovered that the combination of poly(I:C) and QS-21 significantly bolstered DC infiltration into tumors (Figure 4A). Furthermore, this treatment led to a marked upregulation of activated DCs expressing the costimulatory molecules CD40 and CD86, along with the antigen-presenting molecule MHC-II, indicating a pronounced enhancement in the maturation of tumor-infiltrating DCs (Figure 4A). Also, the levels of tumor-infiltrating CD103+ DCs were significantly elevated following treatment with poly(I:C) + QS-21. Consistent with the enhanced DC function, the cytokine IL-12, critical for DC-mediated T cell activation,35 and the chemokine CXCL9, which influences T cell trafficking and tumor infiltration,36 were both significantly upregulated in CD103+ DCs within the poly(I:C) + QS-21 treatment group compared with the control (Figures 4A and 4B). Immunofluorescence analysis of B16F10 tumor tissues excised from subcutaneously implanted mice demonstrated a significant recruitment of CD3+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes, along with CD11c+ DCs, subsequent to treatment with a combination of poly(I:C) and QS-21 (Figure 4C). The histological examination disclosed intimate apposition and aggregation phenomena between these immune cells, indicative of enhanced DC-mediated T cell activation, potentially culminating in an intensified immune reaction against the tumor mass.

We next proceeded to establish a bilateral B16F10 melanoma tumor model (Figure S5A). The tumor volume for the right-side tumors disclosed that the combinatorial treatment with poly(I:C) and QS-21 exerted a significantly greater inhibitory effect on tumor growth compared with the vehicle group (Figure S5B), even surpassing the antitumor efficacy of dacarbazine. Accounting for the potential paracrine effects of the right-side tumors, a surgical extirpation was performed on day 15 post-inoculation. Post-surgical monitoring of the left-side tumors revealed that the poly(I:C) + QS-21 treatment cohort demonstrated a significantly diminished rate of tumor progression and an enhanced overall health status (Figure S5C). The collective observations suggest that the systemic immune response induced by the poly(I:C) and QS-21 combination is capable of eliciting an abscopal effect, indicating the systemic immunomodulatory potential of this treatment modality.

To assess the induction of antitumor-specific immune memory by the combination therapy of poly(I:C) and QS-21, we established a tumor re-challenge model (Figure 4D). Thirty days post the initial subcutaneous treatment with poly(I:C) plus QS-21 for MC38 colorectal tumor-bearing mice, a complete tumor regression was observed in 53.8% (14/26) of the treated cohort (Figure 4E). These mice, demonstrating tumor remission, were subjected to a re-challenge with an equivalent dose of MC38 cells in the contralateral axillary region without additional treatment. After a further 20-day observation period, none of the 14 mice exhibited tumor formation, signifying a 100% (14/14) remission rate post-re-challenge (Figure 4E), which underscores the therapeutic efficacy of the treatment in conferring long-term protective immunity against tumor recurrence. To delve deeper into the tumor-specific immune memory and ascertain that the systemic immune response is triggered by poly(I:C) + QS-21, potentially reactivating the cancer-immunity cycle in the aftermath of MC38 treatment, we introduced a re-challenge paradigm by inoculating an equivalent cohort of B16F10 melanoma cells into the contralateral axilla of the mice. Twenty days post-inoculation, tumors emerged in the poly(I:C) + QS-21 group, albeit with slower growth and reduced burden compared with the vehicle group (Figure S5D). This attenuation is hypothesized to stem from an augmented activation of the endogenous immune machinery catalyzed by the poly(I:C) + QS-21 regimen.

Upon completion of 110 days following the initial therapeutic intervention, we conducted flow cytometry analysis of memory immune cells harvested from the spleen and tumor-draining lymph nodes (TdLNs). The group treated with poly(I:C) + QS-21 exhibited a significant upsurge in the frequencies of central memory CD8+ T cells compared with the vehicle group (Figures 4F and 4G). To rigorously assess the capacity of the poly(I:C) and QS-21 combination to induce tumor antigen-specific cytotoxic responses, we conducted an ex vivo tumor-killing assay utilizing lymphocytes derived from B16F10 tumor-bearing mice. The co-culture assay with splenic lymphocytes and both MC38 colorectal adenocarcinoma and B16F10 melanoma cells revealed a significant lysis of B16F10 cells by splenic lymphocytes from the poly(I:C) + QS-21-treated group (Figure S5E, left). In contrast, when MC38 cells were employed as targets, the cytotoxic efficacy of the splenic lymphocytes, even at a high effector-to-target ratio of 20:1, was markedly less efficient, reaching only 10% tumor killing (Figure S5E, right). This disparity in cytotoxic activity further substantiates the tumor specificity of the lymphocytes activated by the poly(I:C) + QS-21 treatment regimen.

Building on preceding experiments, we conducted a refined investigation to ascertain whether intratumoral administration of poly(I:C) combined with QS-21 could elicit tumor neoantigen-specific T cell responses. Post the final intratumoral administration, we executed an in vitro neoepitope stimulation followed by an IFN-γ enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISPOT) assay utilizing lymphocytes harvested from MC38 tumor-bearing mice (Figure 4H). Upon exposure to the 11 characterized neoepitope peptides, which were identified through whole-exome and transcriptome sequencing,37 splenic T cells from the treatment group demonstrated a pronounced increase in IFN-γ secretion relative to the vehicle control group (Figures 4I and 4J). This enhancement in IFN-γ production signifies that the intratumoral injection of the poly(I:C) + QS-21 combination is efficacious in triggering the formation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) that are specific to tumor antigens. In conclusion, the poly(I:C) + QS-21 adjuvant system exerts a profound modulatory effect in the TME, augmenting the peritumoral and intratumoral infiltration of effector immune cells. This approach transforms previously low-immunogenic “cold tumors” into highly immunogenic “hot tumors”. Furthermore, the localized treatment strategy reinstates the cancer-immunity cycle, facilitating systemic immune responses that extend beyond the site of the primary tumor. This reactivation is evidenced by the generation of a robust antitumor immune memory and the induction of systemic CTLs that target tumor-specific antigens.

Poly(I:C) + QS-21 enhances the sensitivity of anti-PD-1 antibody therapy

Despite the marked success of PD-1 immunotherapy, its efficacy is limited, as approximately 70% of patients, especially those with “immune-desert” tumors, are non-responders. Therefore, co-administering adjuvants with anti-PD-1 therapy represents a promising strategy to overcome this limitation and bolster treatment response rates.38 To delve deeper into this synergy, we established a murine model of subcutaneous pancreatic cancer using the FC1242 cell line, a model renowned for its refractoriness to anti-PD-1 monotherapy.39 Subsequently, we assessed the potential of the poly(I:C) + QS-21 in situ vaccine in concert with anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibodies to reverse this therapeutic insensitivity (Figure 5A). Consistent with expectations, the anti-PD-1 monotherapy group exhibited no discernible improvement in tumor suppression relative to the control group, thereby corroborating the innate resistance of FC1242 tumors to anti-PD-1 monotherapy. In stark contrast, the combinatorial regimen of poly(I:C) + QS-21 with anti-PD-1 yielded profound tumor growth inhibition, surpassing the efficacy of poly(I:C) + QS-21 as a stand-alone treatment. These compelling outcomes underscore the capacity of poly(I:C) + QS-21 to avert the insensitivity of pancreatic cancer to anti-PD-1 therapy, unveiling a promising synergistic potential for combined therapeutic strategies (Figure 5B; Figure S6A). To ascertain the generality of this cooperative interaction, we extended the same therapeutic schema to two additional anti-PD-1-refractory murine cell lines, namely, the Myc-CaP prostate cancer line and the mHCC MYC/sgp53 cell line, which was derived from HepMYC/sgTp53 livers. Across both indications, poly(I:C) + QS-21 plus anti-PD-1 produced a marked attenuation of tumor growth unattainable with either agent alone, corroborating the broad-spectrum applicability of the adjuvant system and immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) paradigm (Figures 5C and 5D; Figures S6B and S6C).

Seven days after the third poly(I:C) + QS-21 injection, we evaluated the infiltration and activation of effector cells within the TME across all three models. Combination treatment with poly(I:C) + QS-21 and anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody resulted in a robust enrichment of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells within the TME, together with significantly increased DC infiltration, far exceeding the immune cell infiltration observed in the vehicle-treated control group or the anti-PD-1 monotherapy group (Figures S6D–S6G). Intratumoral injection of poly(I:C) + QS-21 after microwave ablation enhances the efficacy of tumor growth suppression

To augment the clinical efficacy of the poly(I:C) + QS-21 adjuvant system, we delved into its synergistic antitumor potential when combined with MWA, a minimally invasive procedure adept at inducing tumor cell death via hyperthermia.41 Known for its capacity to engender ICD and the subsequent release of a plethora of tumor antigens, MWA was surmised to prime the adaptive immune response when followed by the poly(I:C) + QS-21 adjuvant system. In the Hep53.4 subcutaneous mouse liver cancer model, MWA was executed on the 50th day post-tumor implantation, succeeded by a regimented schedule of intratumoral injections of the poly(I:C) + QS-21 adjuvant combination administered every third day (Figure 6A). A pronounced reduction in tumor volume and weight was observed in the combination group compared with the group receiving poly(I:C) + QS-21 adjuvant alone (Figure 6B; Figure S7A). Recognizing that MWA is clinically applied to pulmonary and mammary malignancies, we extended these studies to LLC and 4T1 syngeneic models. Partial MWA followed by poly(I:C) + QS-21 recapitulated the hepatocellular-carcinoma findings, yielding significant growth inhibition in both extra-hepatic tumors (Figures 6C and 6D; Figures S7B and S7C).

Deep phenotyping of the TME revealed a pronounced increment in absolute numbers and relative frequencies of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells within the ablated-plus-adjuvant lesions (Figure 6E; Figures S7D–S7F), accompanied by significantly increased DC infiltration (Figure S7I). In 4T1-bearing mice, the TdLNs exhibited a selective expansion of CD8+ central-memory (Tcm) and effector-memory (Tem) subsets (Figure 6F); an analogous shift toward memory differentiation was observed inside the primary tumor (Figure S7G). In contrast, LLC tumors displayed a more restricted pattern, with significant augmentation of Tem cells exclusively within both the TdLN and TME, whereas Tcm cells remained virtually undetectable (Figure S7H), implying context-dependent imprinting of memory programs. Finally, multiplex flow cytometry demonstrated that the combinatorial intervention not only increased the pool of activated T cells (Figure 6G) but also up-regulated PD-1 expression on intratumoral CD8+ T lymphocytes (Figures 6H and 6I). This compensatory induction of immune-checkpoint molecules suggests that sequential PD-1/PD-L1 blockade could be rationally integrated to the MWA-plus-adjuvant backbone to further deepen and prolong therapeutic responses.

Discussion

Adjuvants are essential for potentiating antigen-specific immunity and shaping inflammatory responses, underpinning the success of preventive vaccines and emerging therapeutic applications in cancer. Yet their clinical use in oncology has been constrained by challenges in achieving durable systemic responses while maintaining an acceptable safety profile. The in situ vaccination paradigm addresses these limitations by repurposing the patient’s own tumor as an antigen reservoir, offering a personalized, low-toxicity strategy that circumvents ex vivo antigen preparation and enables straightforward integration with existing modalities.9 Here, we demonstrate that the liposomal poly(I:C) + QS-21 combination functions as a highly potent in situ cancer vaccine. Intratumoral delivery elicited broad-spectrum tumor regression across multiple syngeneic models, frequently achieving complete responses, and simultaneously generated systemic antitumor immunity evidenced by abscopal effects, protection against rechallenge, and robust tumor-specific memory T cell responses. These effects were driven by reactivation of the cancer-immunity cycle, with marked remodeling of the TME—including increased infiltration and activation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, M1 macrophage polarization, and enhanced DC recruitment.

A central mechanistic insight of this study is that QS-21 does not merely co-deliver poly(I:C) but fundamentally reprograms its intracellular routing, thereby unlocking a qualitatively distinct signaling outcome. Poly(I:C) administered alone is predominantly sensed by TLR3 within the endosomal compartment, activating the TRIF adaptor and downstream IRF3 via a pathway that is spatially constrained and subject to endosomal degradation. In contrast, our data demonstrate that QS-21 integration into endosomal membranes, driven by its high affinity for cholesterol, generates transient membrane pores under acidic endosomal conditions (pH 6.0), as confirmed by the hemolysis assay. This disruption enables poly(I:C) to escape into the cytosol, where it engages RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs) that converge on TBK1. The significantly elevated p-TBK1 levels and accelerated IRF3 nuclear accumulation observed within 3 h of combination treatment, compared with poly(I:C) alone, are consistent with dual-compartment sensing: endosomal TLR3 activation supplemented by the more potent cytosolic RLR pathway. This route-switching mechanism explains why QS-21 amplifies poly(I:C) signaling far beyond what would be expected from simple co-formulation and provides a mechanistic rationale for the synergistic gene expression patterns identified by our combinatorial transcriptomic analysis. Our data identify a two-stage transcriptional amplification circuit that links innate sensing to ICD. In the first stage, cytosolic poly(I:C) activates TBK1, leading to IRF3 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation. Activated IRF3 drives transcription of IFN-β—the dominant type I interferon produced in this context, as evidenced by the upregulation of Ifn-β mRNA. This is mechanistically significant because IFN-β, unlike IFN-γ, signals through IFNAR1 in an autocrine and paracrine manner, engaging the JAK-STAT pathway to induce a second wave of ISG expression. And the first stage is non-redundant, as loss of IRF3 markedly attenuates IRF1 induction, confirming that IRF3-driven IFN-β production is the proximal upstream signal for IRF1 activation rather than a parallel pathway. In the second stage, secreted IFN-β engages IFNAR1, but not IFNGR1, to amplify IRF1 expression. The receptor specificity demonstrated by our knockdown experiments is biologically important: it places IRF1 induction firmly within the type I interferon axis rather than the IFN-γ/type II axis, which has distinct downstream transcriptional targets and is more typically associated with adaptive immune effector responses. IRF1, as a downstream integrator, then drives the transcriptional programs underlying ICD, including oxidative stress responses, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and damage-associated molecular pattern release, as evidenced by the suppression of ROS generation, ATP secretion, and calreticulin exposure following IRF1 knockdown. This two-stage relay, “TBK1→IRF3→IFN-β→IFNAR1→IRF1→ICD,” constitutes a coherent and experimentally supported mechanistic framework that explains how a localized innate stimulus is amplified into a robust ICD program.

A notable finding of this study is the functional divergence between IRF1 and IRF7, two transcription factors that are both induced by type I interferon signaling and have overlapping roles in antiviral immunity. Although IRF7 expression was upregulated following poly(I:C) + QS-21 stimulation, its nuclear accumulation was not significantly enhanced compared with poly(I:C) alone, and IRF7 knockdown did not impair ICD induction. This divergence is mechanistically informative. IRF7 is primarily a transcriptional amplifier of type I IFN production itself, and its principal role is in the positive feedback loop that sustains IFN-α/β secretion during viral infection. IRF1, by contrast, is a broader transcriptional regulator with established roles in apoptosis, oxidative stress, antigen presentation, and immune effector programs. These functions are directly relevant to ICD. Our finding that IRF1, but not IRF7, is the non-redundant downstream effector of the poly(I:C) + QS-21 combination is consistent with the literature distinguishing the context-dependent roles of these two factors and highlights IRF1 as a more tractable biomarker and therapeutic target in the context of in situ vaccination strategies.

Compared with recent poly(I:C)-based in situ or adjuvant strategies, our liposomal poly(I:C) + QS-21 formulation offers several distinct advantages. For example, nanoplexed poly(I:C) formulations such as BO-112 have demonstrated clinical activity in anti-PD-1 resistant advanced melanoma when combined with pembrolizumab,42 yet these approaches still rely on systemic checkpoint inhibition. Similarly, preclinical studies using poly(I:C) in liposomes or other nanoparticle platforms have shown induction of ICD and macrophage reprogramming in sarcoma-like models and solid tumors,43,44 but frequently require high poly(I:C) doses, lack robust memory induction, and exhibit limited synergy with locoregional therapies such as ablation. Earlier carrier-free drug-delivering-drug strategies combining poly(I:C) with chemotherapeutics45 further introduce systemic exposure risks and dependence on cytotoxic payloads. In contrast, our poly(I:C) + QS-21 system achieves superior and broad-spectrum antitumor efficacy at substantially lower poly(I:C) doses, induces robust ICD and durable immune memory without additional cytotoxic agents, and demonstrates seamless synergy with both anti-PD-1 therapy and MWA. Taken together, these results highlight the unique capacity of the poly(I:C) + QS-21 liposomal formulation to overcome key limitations of existing poly(I:C)-based approaches, providing a mechanistically integrated and clinically promising platform for durable cancer immunotherapy.

From a translational perspective, the systematic optimization of the poly(I:C) + QS-21 liposomal adjuvant system establishes a rational framework to guide clinical development. An 8:1 poly(I:C): QS-21 ratio was selected based on two complementary considerations: formulation stability and antitumor efficacy. Higher ratios (16:1 and 32:1) produced PDI values exceeding 0.3, compromising particle uniformity without conferring additional therapeutic benefit. In contrast, the 8:1 ratio demonstrated consistent and robust antitumor activity across a 12.5- to 100-μg dose range, thereby defining a clear pharmacodynamic dose-response relationship. The combination also markedly improved outcomes when combined with clinically relevant modalities. It overcame primary resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy in refractory models of pancreatic, prostate, and liver cancer, driving robust T cell activation and infiltration. When sequenced after MWA, a widely used minimally invasive procedure, it amplified adaptive immunity, increased effector and memory T cell populations, and further suppressed tumor growth in liver, lung, and breast cancer models. These synergistic effects highlight a clear path for combination strategies in patients who are refractory to standard immunotherapy or who undergo locoregional ablation.

Despite these encouraging results, important limitations remain. Efficacy has been demonstrated in murine syngeneic models. However, validation in humanized systems and patient-derived tumors is needed to confirm translatability. Although the mechanism centers on IRF1-dependent IFN signaling, the precise in vivo contributions of individual pathways require further dissection. Long-term toxicity, detailed pharmacokinetics, and clinical optimal dosing schedules have not been fully characterized. Finally, the therapeutic index in human tumors, particularly those with immunosuppressive microenvironments, remains to be established in early-phase trials.

In summary, the poly(I:C) + QS-21 liposomal adjuvant system represents a safe, potent, and mechanistically well-defined in situ cancer vaccine that reactivates the cancer-immunity cycle, induces durable immune memory, overcomes resistance to immune checkpoint blockade, and synergizes with ablation therapies. The alignment of its effective and safe dose range with existing clinical experience, combined with its compatibility with standard-of-care interventions, strongly supports its advancement into clinical evaluation as a platform for enhancing immunotherapy in solid tumors.

Limitations of the study

Some limitations must be addressed. All efficacy data were generated in murine syngeneic tumor models; their translatability to human tumors, which exhibit greater genetic heterogeneity and immunosuppressive microenvironments, requires validation in humanized models or early-phase clinical trials. Mechanistic insights, while supported by in vitro assays and IRF1 knockdown experiments, were primarily derived from B16F10 cells; the precise in vivo contributions of individual signaling pathways, as well as the detailed process of QS-21-mediated poly(I:C) endosomal escape and cytosolic delivery, await further confirmation using advanced imaging techniques. Finally, optimal dosing schedules, sequencing with anti-PD-1 blockade or ablation therapies, and patient selection criteria remain to be defined in humans. These constraints highlight critical areas for future investigation prior to clinical translation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

All animal protocols and experiments were approved by the Center for New Drug Evaluation and Research, China Pharmaceutical University (Nanjing, China) (Approval No. B20220101-1) and by the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. C57BL/6J mice (RRID: IMSR_JAX:000664, male and female, 6–8 weeks old) and BALB/c nude mice (female, 6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. MMTV-PyMT transgenic mice were obtained from Jiangsu Jicui Pharmaceutical & Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility under a 12 h light/dark cycle, with controlled temperature (22 ± 2°C), humidity (50 ± 10%), and free access to standard chow and water. Mice were group-housed (4–5 per cage) unless specified. Both male and female C57BL/6J mice were used where possible, with sex balanced in all major experiments unless otherwise noted (e.g., BALB/c nude mice were female-only). No significant gender difference was observed in anti-tumor drug efficacy.

Cell culture

Murine melanoma cell line B16F10 (RRID: CVCL_0159) was obtained from the Shanghai Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and cultured in RPMI-1640 (01-101-1A, Biological Industries) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; C04001-500, Vivacell) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (SV30010, HyClone). Murine triple-negative breast cancer cell line 4T1, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cell line FC1242, colorectal cancer cell line CT26, liver cancer cell lines H22 and Hep53.4, prostate cancer cell line Myc-CaP, and lung cancer cell line LLC were also obtained from the same source and cultured in high-glucose DMEM (01-052-1A, Biological Industries) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Murine colorectal cancer cell line MC38 (RRID: CVCL_B288) was purchased from Mcellbank and maintained in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine (G7513, Sigma), 0.1 mM non-essential amino acids (NEAA; 11140050, Gibco), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (35050-061, Gibco), and 10 mM HEPES (H1090, Gibco). The mHCC MYC/sgp53 cell line is a primary hepatocellular carcinoma derived from male C57BL/6 mice in the HepMYC/sgTP53 model and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling, and routinely tested to confirm the absence of mycoplasma contamination. In addition, mHCC MYC/sgp53 cells were further validated by chromatin karyotyping and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) morphological analysis. All cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Method details

Main reagents

In this experiment, the reagents including MPLA + QS-21 (1 mg/mL), MPLA + ssRNA (1 mg/mL), Poly(I:C) + QS-21 (1 mg/mL), Poly(I:C) (8.9 mg/mL), and QS-21 (1.1 mg/mL), were provided by Shanghai Anyikang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All reagents were stored protected from light at 2°C–8°C.

Generation of stably expressing cell lines

To construct a stable IRF1-knockdown B16F10 cell line, we inserted the shIRF1 sequence (Oligo sequence: shIRF1-1: CCGAGACACTAAGAGCAAA, shIRF1-2: GATGGACATTATACCAGAT) into the pLKO.1 vector and then transfected it into HEK293T cells for lentivirus generation. After collecting the supernatant and purifying the virus, it was used to infect the B16F10 cell line, resulting in a stable IRF1-knockdown B16F10 line, as confirmed by qPCR and Western blot.

Tumor models

Tumor-bearing mouse models were established by subcutaneous injection of different mouse-derived tumor cell lines into the corresponding mouse strains.

For dose-optimization and combination studies involving varying ratios or doses of Poly(I:C) + QS-21, as well as single-agent versus combination treatments, B16F10 cells were subcutaneously implanted at 5 × 10^5 cells per mouse. Therapy was initiated 7 days post-inoculation and administered every 3 days for three total doses per mouse. For TLR agonist screening in B16F10 tumors, the inoculum was adjusted to 5 × 10^4 cells per mouse.

For immune cell depletion studies, mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 5 × 10^5 B16-OVA cells in the right flank. Upon reaching approximately 50 mm3 tumor volume (monitored between days 3–5 post-inoculation), mice received intratumoral injections of Poly(I:C) plus QS-21 (100 μg per mouse) every 3 days for three total doses. Antibody-mediated depletion was initiated 1 day prior to the first treatment and maintained until the experimental endpoint, as follows: anti-CSF1R (300 μg per mouse, intraperitoneally) every 2 days; anti-CD8 (400 μg per mouse) or isotype-matched IgG control (200 μg per mouse) twice weekly; and anti-CD4 (200 μg per mouse) once weekly. For the immunodeficient nude mouse model, 1 × 10^6 B16-OVA cells were subcutaneously injected into the right flank. Treatment with Poly(I:C) + QS-21 (100 μg per mouse, intratumoral) was started on day 6 after inoculation and administered every 3 days for a total of three doses. The experiment was concluded on day 17.

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of Poly(I:C) + QS-21 across different tumor models, mice were subcutaneously challenged with the following cell numbers: MC38 (1 × 10^5), CT26 (1 × 10^5), LLC (6 × 10^5), 4T1 (1.25 × 10^5), or H22 (2 × 10^6) cells. All treatment followed the regimen established for the B16F10 subcutaneous tumor model. In the MMTV-PyMT transgenic mouse model, treatment was initiated when tumor volumes reached 100 mm3, with intratumoral injections administered every 3 days for three total doses. Mice were sacrificed one week following the final treatment.

For bilateral subcutaneous melanoma mouse model, B16-OVA were counted and adjusted to viable cell concentration of 1 × 10^6 cells/mL and 1 × 10^7 cells/mL, and 100 μL of the cell suspension was inoculated subcutaneously into right and left axillary regions of the mice (left side with a cell count of 1 × 10^5 per mouse, right side with a cell count of 1 × 10^6 per mouse). The tumor growth on both sides was observed daily starting from the third day after inoculation. Treatment with Poly(I:C) + QS-21 (100 μg/mouse, intratumoral) and dacarbazine (40 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection) began on day 5. Poly(I:C) + QS-21 was dosed every 3 days for 3 doses (days 5, 8, 11), and dacarbazine was administered daily until day 14. The right tumor was excised on day 15, followed by continued monitoring of the left tumor until the experimental endpoint on day 25.

For re-challenged tumor-bearing mouse model, MC38 cells (1 × 10^5 per mouse) were inoculated subcutaneously into the right axillary region of the mice. Tumor growth was observed daily starting from the third day after inoculation. The Poly(I:C) + QS-21 group of mice was administered the treatment every three days for a total of three times. On the 40th day, tumor cells were counted and adjusted to a viable cell concentration, while mice that had completely regressed tumors after Poly(I:C) + QS-21 treatment were selected for re-inoculation with tumor cell suspension into the left axillary region. Tumor growth was observed daily, starting from the third day after re-challenge. On the 100th day, mice that remained tumor-free after MC38 re-inoculation were selected again and re-inoculated with B16F10 (1 × 10^5 per mouse) in the left axillary region. Starting on the third day after inoculation, the growth of the tumor was observed daily until the end of the experiment on the 120th day.

For the anti-PD-1-resistant tumor model, the inoculation numbers per mouse are as follows: FC1242 (1 × 10^6), Myc-CaP (2 × 10^6), and mHCC MYC/sgp53 cells (1 × 10^6). Treatment was initiated when subcutaneous tumors reached 100 mm3. Mice received intraperitoneal anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody (5 mg/kg) and intratumoral Vehicle or Poly(I:C) + QS-21 (25 μg per mouse) every 3 days (×3 doses). Animals were euthanized 7 days following the last injection to reach the experimental endpoint.

For microwave ablation, the inoculation numbers per mouse are as follows: LLC cells (6 × 10^5), 4T1 cells (1.25 × 10^5), Hep53.4 cells (1 × 10^6). After tumor cells were subcutaneously injected on the right flank of mice, once the tumor diameter reached ∼1 cm, partial microwave ablation was performed (10 W, 10 s). One day after ablation, Vehicle or Poly(I:C) + QS-21 were injected intratumorally into the right tumor (25 μg per mouse). The intratumoral injection was performed once every three days (×3 doses). Animals were euthanized 7 days following the last injection to reach the experimental endpoint.

Encapsulation efficiency and drug loading determination

Free (unencapsulated) Poly(I:C) and QS-21 were separated from cationic liposomes using 100-kDa MWCO ultrafiltration centrifugal tubes (WNSwift, Jiangdao Biotechnology). Liposomal formulations were placed in the upper chamber and centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 15–20 min at 25°C. The filtrate collected in the collection tube contained the free (unencapsulated) drug, whereas the liposome-encapsulated fraction was retained in the upper chamber. Poly(I:C) concentration was determined using the Invitrogen Quant-iT RiboGreen RNA Quantitation Kit (R11490, Invitrogen). QS-21 was detected using an Agilent 1260 Infinity III liquid chromatography system.

Flow cytometry

For tumor digestion, the tumor tissues were mechanically disrupted by dissection scissors and then enzymatically digested with gentleMACS Dissociator (Miltenyi) in D-Hank’s buffer containing 0.25 mg/mL collagenase D (11088866001, Roche) and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (DN25, Sigma-Aldrich). The homogenates were filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer to acquire single-cell suspensions. For spleen digestion, the spleen was gently ground in PBS and passed through a 70 μm cell strainer. The single-cell suspension was lysed with red blood cell lysis buffer (420302, Biolegend). Cells were incubated with FcR Blocking Reagent (130-092-575, Miltenyi Biotec) before staining.

For tumor and TdLN immune cell analysis, cells were stained with fluorescence-labeled antibodies, which were listed in the key resources table.

Samples were acquired on an Attune NxT (Thermo Fisher) and analyzed using FlowJo v10.8.1 (TreeStar, USA).

Real-time qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit (RC101-01, Vazyme Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. And RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA with Prime Script RT Master Mix Perfect Real Time kit (R047A, TaKaRa). Real-time qPCR was performed using SYBR Green SYBR Premix Ex Taq kit (RR820A, TaKaRa), and data were analyzed on the QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Gene expression levels were normalized to β-actin and were quantified using the ΔΔCt method.

Calreticulin immunofluorescence staining

B16F10 cells were plated at a density of 2 × 10^5 cells per well in a 6-well plate that had been pre-plated with cell culture slides. Once the cells had fully adhered, the supernatant was aspirated, and the cells were stimulated with Poly(I:C) + QS-21 at a concentration of 32 μg/mL, as well as corresponding concentrations of Vehicle, Poly(I:C) (28.48 μg/mL), and QS-21 (3.52 μg/mL) for 24 h. Each group consisted of three replicate wells.

After 24 h, tumor cells were washed with PBS, incubated with DiO (membrane stain) for 15 min, and subsequently fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. After being washed with PBS three times, the cells were exposed to a blocking buffer (10% goat serum) and then further incubated with anti-calreticulin mouse monoclonal antibody (1:500 dilution) for 12 h. After being thoroughly washed with PBS, the cells were stained with Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-rabbit IgG (dilution ratio 1:500) for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, tissue slices were counterstained with DAPI (C1005, Beyotime Biotechnology) for 5 min. Sections were imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Leica STELLARIS 5) and processed using the LAS X 3.7.2.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated in gradient ethanol. Antigen retrieval was performed using sodium citrate buffer. Nonspecific binding was blocked by incubating the tissue sections with 10% goat serum in PBST. Then, the sections were incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4°C. After being washed, the tissues were incubated with secondary antibodies and stained with DAB. The sections were then counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted.

Multiplex immunohistochemical (mIHC)

Prepare paraffin sections of tumor tissue according to the method described above, and then perform Multiplex immunohistochemical (mIHC) staining following the instructions of the Multiplex Fluorescence Immunohistochemistry Kit (Panovue, 10001100020#). Briefly, after baking, dewaxing, rehydrating, antigen repair and sealing, 50–100 μL of primary antibody working solution was added (diluted recommended ratio using 1× PBST containing 10% goat serum) into the wax circle to fully cover the tissue area, incubate at 37°C for 1 h, and wash with 1× TBST three times, then pipette 50 to 100 μL of HRP-labeled secondary antibody solution into the wax circle to fully cover the tissue area, incubate 30 min, and wash with 1× TBST three times. For fluorescence staining and signal amplification, pipette 50 to 100 μL of 1× dye working solution (single-color TSA fluorescence dye diluted with signal amplification liquid at a ratio of 1:100) into the wax circle to fully cover the tissue area, incubate it for 15 min, and wash with 1× TBST three times. Then, perform microwave antigen retrieval, cool down to room temperature naturally, wash the slides with sterile water once, and soak the slides in 1× TBST for 2 min. The single staining ends, and after DAPI staining, seal the slides for observation or repeat the subsequent staining starting from the blocking step.

Western blot

The cells were lysed in RIPA/IP buffer on ice. Then, the protein concentration of the lysate was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Similar amounts of protein were then separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate‒polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‒PAGE). The proteins were then electroblotted onto PVDF membranes and blocked with 5% TBST milk for 1–2 h. Then, the membranes were incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4°C. The next day, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

Following the methods previously mentioned, mouse B16F10 melanoma subcutaneous tumors were inoculated, grouped, and administered intratumoral injections of Vehicle and Poly(I:C) + QS-21. On the 6th day after the final intratumoral injection, mouse splenic lymphocytes were harvested, resuspended, and counted in RPMI-1640 conditioned medium (containing 10% inactivated FBS, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 0.1 mM NEAA) and adjusted to the appropriate cell concentration to be used as effector cells.

B16F10 or MC38 cells in logarithmic growth were plated at a density of 2 × 10^5 cells per well in a 6-well plate and stimulated with 32 μg/mL Poly(I:C) + QS-21 and 32 μg/mL Vehicle for 24 h. The tumor cells were then stained with CFSE dye and adjusted to a viable cell concentration of 2 × 10^5 cells/mL to be used as target cells.

Sterile 96-well U-bottom plates were prepared with a fixed number of 2 × 10^4 B16F10 or MC38 tumor cells (target cells) per well. Effector cells were added at effector-target ratios of 5:1, 10:1, and 20:1, with corresponding groups of lymphocytes (effector cells) being added to each well. This involves co-culturing splenic cells from the Poly(I:C) + QS-21 group with Poly(I:C) + QS-21 treated B16F10 or MC38 tumor cells. After thorough mixing of effector and target cells, the plate was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 1 min and incubated for 12 h at 5% CO2 and 37°C in the dark. Triplicate wells were set up for each effector-target ratio. Additionally, wells containing only B16F10 or MC38 tumor cells were used to quantify the natural death of target cells. Flow cytometry was used to detect cytotoxic activity. The software FlowJo v10.8.1 was utilized for gating cell populations and exporting data. Data statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0.

IFN-γ ELISPOT

MC38 subcutaneous tumor-bearing mice were established and then randomized into groups for intratumoral injection of poly(I:C) + QS-21 or the corresponding vehicle as described above. 10 days after the last administration, mice were euthanized, and sterile splenocytes were harvested. Add 2 mL of the prepared 1× red blood cell lysis solution to each tube of splenocytes, mix well, and incubate at room temperature for 5 min. Resuspend and count the cells using RPMI-1640 complete medium (containing 10% inactivated FBS), adjust the cell concentration to 2 × 10^6 cells/mL, and keep on ice until ready.

Remove the pre-coated PVDF strip plates from the Mouse IFN-γ precoated ELISPOT kit (Dakewe Bio, 2210005#), add 200 μL/well of RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% inactivated FBS, and stand at room temperature for 5 to 10 min. Add cell suspension to each experimental well (100 μL/well). Bring the 11 peptide stock solutions to room temperature and mix well, adjust the peptide concentration to 1 mg/mL (10× final concentration) with RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% inactivated FBS, and add 10 μL/well to the corresponding experimental wells. After all samples and stimulants have been added, close the plate lid, and incubate the plate at 5% CO2 and 37°C for 24 h. Carry out the ELISPOT assay following the operational instructions provided with the Mouse IFN-γ precoated ELISPOT kit (Dakewe Bio, 2210005) after the stimulation culture.

SRB colorimetric assay

Poly(I:C) + QS-21 solutions at concentrations of 1 μg/mL, 2 μg/mL, 4 μg/mL, 8 μg/mL, 16 μg/mL, and 32 μg/mL were prepared with complete medium corresponding to different cell lines (stock solution 1 mg/mL). B16F10 and MC38 tumor cells were plated at a density of approximately 5000 viable cells per well in four 96-well plates. After 72h, 10% trichloroacetic acid solution was added to each well and fixed overnight at 4°C. The plate was washed five times with 200 μL of pure water and air-dried in a 37°C oven for 2 h. Then, 100 μL of 0.4% SRB dye was added to each well and stained at room temperature for 2 h. After adding 100 μL of Tris-Base to each well, the plate was shaken at 75 rpm for 10 min on a horizontal shaker, and the absorbance of each well was read at 570 nm using a microplate reader.

ATP and ROS detection assay

Tumor cells were plated at a density of 5 × 10^5 cells per well in a 6-well plate and cultured overnight under 5% CO2 at 37°C. The next day, the cells were stimulated with Poly(I:C) + QS-21 (32 μg/mL), Vehicle, Poly(I:C) (28.48 μg/mL), and QS-21 (3.52 μg/mL) for 24 h, with three replicate wells set for each group. After 24 h, the cell supernatant was collected and stored at 4°C for subsequent testing. The ATP content in the supernatant was measured using an ATP detection assay kit (Beyotime, S0026#).

For ROS detection assay, Cells were trypsinized, reseeded into 96-well plates, and incubated with DCFH-DA (2,7-dichlorofuorescin diacetate) fluorescent probe (incubated for 1 h), with detection at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm (Elabscience, E-BC-K138-F#).

RNA-seq and analysis

One microgram of total RNA per sample was used as input material for the RNA sample preparations. Sequencing libraries were generated using NEB Next UltraTMRNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) following manufacturer’s recommendations and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. Briefly, mRNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. Fragmentation was carried out using divalent cations under elevated temperature in NEBNext First Strand Synthesis Reaction Buffer (5×). First, strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primer and M-MuLV Reverse Transcriptase (RNase H). Second strand cDNA synthesis was subsequently performed using DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. Remaining overhangs were converted into blunt ends via exonuclease/polymerase activities. After the adenylation of 3′ ends of DNA fragments, NEB Next Adaptor with hairpin loop structure was ligated to prepare for hybridization. To select cDNA fragments of preferentially 250–300 bp in length, the library fragments were purified with the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA). Then 3 μL USER Enzyme (NEB, USA) was used with size-selected, adaptor-ligated cDNA at 37°C for 15 min followed by 5 min at 95°C before PCR. Then, PCR was performed with Phusion High-Fidelity DNA polymerase, Universal PCR primers, and Index (X) Primer. At last, PCR products were purified (AMPure XP system) and library quality was assessed on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Clustering and sequencing: The clustering of the index-coded samples was performed on a cBot Cluster Generation System using TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After cluster generation, the library preparations were sequenced on an Illumina platform, and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated.

Hemolysis assay

The membrane-disruptive activity of the Poly(I:C) + QS-21 liposomal formulation was evaluated using a hemolysis assay under endosomal-like conditions. Fresh mouse blood was collected and immediately transferred into anticoagulant tubes. Red blood cells (RBCs) were isolated by centrifugation and washed repeatedly with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) at 1600 rpm for 5 min until the supernatant became colorless. The purified RBC pellet was then resuspended in PBS (pH 7.4/6) to obtain a 2% (v/v) erythrocyte suspension. For the hemolysis test, 20 μL of liposome system was mixed with 180 μL of the erythrocyte suspension and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. After incubation, samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 1 min, and 100 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate. The absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader.

siRNA transfection

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection was performed using Hieff Trans RNAiBoost Transfection Reagent (Cat# 40807ES03, Yeasen Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded in culture plates and allowed to reach approximately 60–70% confluence before transfection. siRNA and transfection reagent were diluted separately in serum-free medium, mixed gently, and incubated at room temperature to allow complex formation. The siRNA–lipid complexes were then added to the cells and incubated under standard culture conditions.

The sequences of the siRNAs used in this study were as follows

siIRF3: GGUUGUUCCUACAUGUCUUAA

siIRF7: UCGUAGGGAUCUGGAUGAAGA

siIFNAR1: GCCAGAGACUACUUACUGUUU

siIFNGR1: GCCAGAGUUAAAGCUAAGGUU.

Cells were harvested 48 h after transfection for subsequent analyses. A scrambled siRNA sequence was used as a negative control.

LDH cytotoxicity assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates to 80%–90% confluence, switched to low-serum or serum-free medium, and assigned to blank, spontaneous, maximum release, and experimental treatment groups; one hour before harvest, the maximum release wells were lysed with LDH Release Reagent (10% v/v), after which the plate was centrifuged (400 × g, 5 min) and 120 μL of supernatant per well was transferred and incubated with 60 μL of LDH Reaction Working Solution for 30 min at room temperature in the dark before measuring absorbance at 490 nm (with a reference wavelength ≥600 nm) to calculate cytotoxicity.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: volume = (length × width2)/2. Relative tumor inhibition rate (TIR) was determined as [1 – (ΔT/ΔC)] × 100%, where ΔT and ΔC represent the change in tumor volume from baseline in treatment and control groups, respectively. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.8.1 (RRID:SCR_008520).

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (RRID:SCR_002798). Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise stated. Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was used for two-group comparisons, and one-way or two-way ANOVA were applied for multiple comparisons. Significance was defined as p < 0.05. Sample sizes (n = 6–12 mice per group) were determined based on prior experiments to achieve sufficient statistical power. Animals were randomized by tumor volume at the initiation of treatment. Investigators were blinded to group allocation during tumor measurements and flow cytometry analysis where feasible.
