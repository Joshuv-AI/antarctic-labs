Abstract
. In this paper, we develop an intrinsic consensus-based optimization framework on Riemannian manifolds with bounded sectional curvature. In contrast to extrinsic approaches based on an ambient Euclidean embedding, our model is formulated directly in terms of the Riemannian structure, using logarithmic and exponential maps induced by the intrinsic geodesic distance. We prove the global well-posedness of the proposed particle system and its associated McKean–Vlasov dynamics. We also establish the global convergence of the mean-field equation toward a global minimizer of the objective function under suitable conditions. Numerical experiments on the sphere, hyperbolic space, and the special orthogonal group demonstrate the effectiveness of the intrinsic CBO dynamics for nonconvex optimization problems on manifolds.

Introduction

Global optimization in high dimensions is a central task in scientific computing, data science, and machine learning. Beyond classical gradient-based methods,
consensus-based optimization
(CBO)
[
43
]
has emerged as a robust, derivative-free paradigm inspired by interacting particle systems and collective behavior. In its basic form, CBO evolves a cloud of agents (or particles) through a combination of (i) a drift toward a
consensus point
, computed as a weighted average biased toward low-energy states, and (ii) a state-dependent diffusion that promotes exploration.
The method has been studied extensively from algorithmic, probabilistic, and mean-field perspectives. In particular, mean-field limits yield nonlinear McKean–Vlasov type equations that enable an SDE-based analysis of stability, concentration, and convergence; see, e.g.,
[
5
,
11
,
19
,
26
,
34
]
and references therein.

CBO in Euclidean space.

Let
\mathcal{E}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be an objective function. A prototypical task is the global minimization problem:

\text{Find }x^{*}\in\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{E}(x),

where
\mathcal{E}
may be nonconvex and possibly nonsmooth. For a probability measure
\rho
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a fixed
\alpha>0
, we define the weight function

w_{\alpha}(x):=\exp(-\alpha\mathcal{E}(x)),

and the associated
consensus point

(1.1)

\displaystyle x_{\alpha}[\rho]:=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}w_{\alpha}(y)y\,\rho(\mathrm{d}y)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}w_{\alpha}(y)\,\rho(\mathrm{d}y)}.

This choice of weight function is motivated by the well-known Laplace principle
[
14
,
41
]
; see also
[
36
, Appendix A.2]
.

At the particle level, one considers
N
particles
\{x_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{N}
in Euclidean space driven by a drift toward
x_{\alpha}[\rho_{t}^{N}]
with the empirical measure
\rho_{t}^{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}(t)}
and a multiplicative noise proportional to the distance from consensus. They satisfy the following system of stochastic differential equations (SDEs):

(1.2)

\mathrm{d}x_{i}(t)=-\lambda(x_{i}(t)-x_{\alpha}[\rho_{t}^{N}])\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\|x_{i}(t)-x_{\alpha}[\rho_{t}^{N}]\|\mathrm{d}B_{t}^{i},\quad i=1,\dots,N\,,

where
\lambda>0
controls consensus formation,
\sigma>0
controls exploration, and
\{B^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are independent Brownian motions.

In the mean-field regime, the empirical measure
\rho_{t}^{N}
converges (formally, and in many settings rigorously) to a law
\rho_{t}
solving a nonlinear Fokker–Planck equation of the form:

(1.3)

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\lambda\,\nabla\cdot\!\big((x-x_{\alpha}[\rho_{t}])\rho_{t}\big)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta\!\big(\|x-x_{\alpha}[\rho_{t}]\|^{2}\rho_{t}\big)\,.

Moreover, the corresponding McKean–Vlasov SDE is given by:

(1.4)

\mathrm{d}\bar{x}(t)=-\lambda(\bar{x}(t)-x_{\alpha}[\rho_{t}])\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\|\bar{x}(t)-x_{\alpha}[\rho_{t}]\|\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,,\quad\rho_{t}=\mbox{Law}(\bar{x}(t))\,.

Rigorous derivations of the mean-field limit for the particle system (
1.2
), leading to the PDE (
1.3
) and the McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.4
), are provided in
[
3
,
25
,
33
,
35
]
. The large-
\alpha

w_{\alpha}
near minimizers of
\mathcal{E}
, and the coupling between drift and diffusion promotes the collapse of
\rho_{t}
around (near-)global minimizers under suitable assumptions
[
8
,

,
23
]

. Consequently, the aforementioned extrinsic models rely on ambient projections rather than fully using the intrinsic geometric information of the space where the optimization problem is constrained.

Why manifolds?

In many applications, the decision variable is naturally constrained to a curved space: orientations (
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,
SO(d)
), positive definite matrices (SPD manifolds), shape spaces, low-rank models, and statistical manifolds. In such cases, enforcing constraints via ambient projections can distort the underlying geometry and degrade algorithmic performance. This motivates the development of intrinsic CBO dynamics posed directly on a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
, utilizing only geometric primitives such as geodesic distance, exponential/logarithmic maps, and the Laplace–Beltrami operator.

In several previous works, CBO dynamics on manifolds have been formulated through an extrinsic viewpoint

[
9
,
20
,
23
,
30
]
. In these approaches, the manifold is embedded into an ambient Euclidean space, and the CBO dynamics are constructed using the metric structure induced from the ambient space. However, such an extrinsic formulation has the limitation that it treats the given manifold not as an abstract Riemannian manifold, but rather as an embedded submanifold of a particular Euclidean space. Consequently, the resulting dynamics are not only determined by the intrinsic geometry of the manifold, but are also affected by the chosen embedding and the ambient metric. In particular, the ambient Euclidean distance is generally different from the geodesic distance on the manifold, and the consensus direction or diffusion term defined in the ambient space need not coincide with those naturally induced by the Riemannian metric.

This issue becomes more significant when one considers general Riemannian manifolds. For manifolds such as spheres or Stiefel manifolds, which admit natural Euclidean embeddings, an extrinsic approach can be relatively convenient. However, a general manifold does not come with a canonical ambient space. Even if an embedding exists, for instance by the Nash embedding theorem, the choice of such an embedding is not unique, and the extrinsic dynamics induced by it may also be embedding-dependent. Therefore, to study CBO on general Riemannian manifolds, it is necessary to develop an intrinsic formulation that relies only on the Riemannian structure of the manifold itself, without referring to an auxiliary ambient space.

Another advantage of an intrinsic formulation is its computational aspect. When an
n
-dimensional manifold is represented as an embedded submanifold of a Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the extrinsic dynamics are often implemented in the ambient dimension
d
, which may be larger than the intrinsic dimension
n
. In some cases, this increase in dimension is small; for example, the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n}
is naturally embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. In other cases, the ambient dimension can be much larger than the intrinsic dimension. For example,
SO(n)
has intrinsic dimension
n(n-1)/2
, whereas its standard matrix embedding lies in
\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
. Therefore, depending on the manifold, the dimension of the Euclidean space required for an embedding can become quite large. An intrinsic formulation avoids this artificial enlargement of the state space and can therefore offer computational advantages in the implementation of CBO dynamics on manifolds.

For these reasons, in this paper we focus on developing a CBO framework based on the intrinsic geometry of the manifold. The intrinsic formulation of dynamical systems on manifolds has already been studied in the context of collective behavior and aggregation models. For instance, Fetecau and Zhang introduced self-organization models on Riemannian manifolds in which the interactions are defined intrinsically through the Riemannian distance and the associated geometric structure
[
18
]
. Subsequently, the well-posedness and long-time behavior of intrinsic dynamics on
SO(3)
and the sphere were studied in
[
15
,
17
]
, respectively. More recently, intrinsic dynamics were investigated on Riemannian manifolds with bounded curvature, rather than on a specific model manifold
[
16
]
. See also
[
47
]
for a Riemannian gradient method on the Cartan–Hadamard manifold for
L_{2}
Wasserstein least-squares problems on the space of Gaussian measures equipped with the affine-invariant geometry. We refer the reader to
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
for further developments on intrinsic collective dynamics on Riemannian manifolds. However, these works concern aggregation and self-organization dynamics, rather than consensus-based optimization. Motivated by these developments, we formulate consensus-based optimization dynamics intrinsically on a general Riemannian manifold with bounded curvature in this paper.

Geometric difficulties.

Extending (
1.3
) to a general manifold is not a mere notational change. Two structural obstacles are immediate:

(1)

No subtraction.
The Euclidean drift
(x-x_{\alpha}[\rho])
and the average
\int(y-x)\rho(\mathrm{d}y)
are not well-defined intrinsically. A natural replacement is to use logarithmic vectors
\log_{x}(y)\in T_{x}M
, such that
\|\log_{x}(y)\|=d(x,y)
, and to transport vectors between tangent spaces using parallel transport.

(2)

Cut-locus and curvature effects.
Even on complete manifolds,
\log_{x}(y)
may fail to be globally defined due to the cut-locus. Furthermore, curvature affects both the Lipschitz properties of the logarithmic map and the strength of the diffusion. On negatively curved manifolds in particular, diffusion can inflate moments much more strongly than in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, complicating the
a priori
estimates typically relied upon in Euclidean theory.

These issues suggest that a viable intrinsic CBO model must (a) ensure the logarithmic map is evaluated only in a domain where it is single-valued and regular, and (b) control the diffusion in regions where curvature-driven dispersion would prevent moment closure.

Our approach and main model.

In this work, we propose and analyze CBO dynamics on a class of manifolds with bounded sectional curvature. We place particular emphasis on Cartan–Hadamard manifolds (complete, simply connected and non-positively curved spaces) where geodesics are unique and global convexity properties are available. Throughout this paper, we make the following assumptions:

(M)

M
is a complete, connected, smooth Riemannian manifold without boundary of dimension
d
, with positive injectivity radius
\mathrm{inj}(M)>0
. We denote by
d(\cdot,\cdot)
the intrinsic distance on
M
. Moreover, every sectional curvature
\mathcal{K}(x;\sigma)
of
M
, computed at a point
x\in M
and a
2
-dimensional subspace
\sigma\subset T_{x}M
, satisfies
-\kappa_{-}\leq\mathcal{K}(x;\sigma)\leq\kappa_{+}
for positive constants
\kappa_{\pm}
.

(R)

Given a manifold
M
satisfying
(M)
, we choose positive constants
R
and
\delta\in(0,R)
such that
0<R<R+\delta<\min\left\{\frac{\mathrm{inj}(M)}{2},\,\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}}\right\}
.

(E)

\mathcal{E}
is globally Lipschitz on
M
and
\inf_{x\in M}\mathcal{E}(x)=:\underline{\mathcal{E}}
.

This assumption on
\mathcal{E}
only guarantees the well-posedness of the dynamics. To obtain the long-time behavior, we impose the additional assumptions (A1)–(A3) in Section
4
.

Consequently, if we consider a geodesic ball
B_{R}(o):=\{y\in M:d(o,y)<R\}
centered at a pole
o
with radius
R<\min\left(\frac{\mathrm{inj}(M)}{2},\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}}\right)
, then for any two points
x,y\in B_{R}(o)
, the minimizing geodesic joining them is unique (see Section
2.1
).

To address the lack of vector subtraction, we construct an intrinsic analog of the consensus difference
x_{\alpha}[\rho]-x
. In Euclidean space, this difference is the weighted average of
(y-x)
, that is,

x_{\alpha}[\rho]-x=\frac{1}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}w_{\alpha}(y)\,d\rho(y)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}w_{\alpha}(y)(y-x)\,d\rho(y).

On a manifold, we replace this with the weighted average of
\log_{x}y
, i.e.

\frac{1}{\int_{M}w_{\alpha}(y)\,d\rho(y)}\int_{M}w_{\alpha}(y)\log_{x}y\,d\rho(y).

To avoid cut-locus singularities and ensure
\log_{x}y
is globally well-defined, we restrict the integration domain to a geodesic ball
B_{R+\delta}(o)
with
0<R<R+\delta<\min\left(\frac{\mathrm{inj}(M)}{2},\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}}\right)
. By the triangle inequality, any two points
x,y\in B_{R+\delta}(o)
satisfy
d(x,y)<2(R+\delta)<\min\left(\mathrm{inj}(M),\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}}\right)
, ensuring the required regularity. Hence,
\log_{x}y
is well-defined for all
x,y\in B_{R+\delta}(o)
.

To maintain continuous dependence on the measure
\rho
, we introduce a smooth cutoff function
h_{R,R+\delta}
and define the intrinsic consensus direction
u_{\alpha}(\rho;x)\in T_{x}M
at base point
x
:

u_{\alpha}(\rho;x):=\frac{h_{R,R+\delta}(d(x,o))}{\int_{M}w_{\alpha}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(y)}\int_{B_{R+\delta}(o)}h_{R,R+\delta}(d(y,o))\,w_{\alpha}(y)\log_{x}y\,\mathrm{d}\rho(y)\,.

Namely, the vector
u_{\alpha}(\rho;x)\in T_{x}M
serves as the intrinsic counterpart of
x_{\alpha}[\rho]-x
.
Unlike the Euclidean case as in (
1.1
), the corresponding consensus point
x_{\alpha}(\rho;x):=\exp_{x}\bigl(u_{\alpha}(\rho;x)\bigr)
depends explicitly on the base point
x
. Here the smooth non-increasing function
h:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
satisfies

h(\theta)=1\quad\text{for all }\theta\leq 0,\qquad h(\theta)=0\quad\text{for all }\theta\geq 1.

For any two real numbers
a<b
, we define the rescaled cutoff function
h_{a,b}
by

h_{a,b}(\theta):=h\!\left(\frac{\theta-a}{b-a}\right).

Then
h_{a,b}
is also smooth and non-increasing, and satisfies

h_{a,b}(\theta)=1\quad\text{for all }\theta\leq a,\qquad h_{a,b}(\theta)=0\quad\text{for all }\theta\geq b.

As an example, we can construct
h
as follows:

h(\theta)=\frac{s(1-\theta)}{s(\theta)+s(1-\theta)},\qquad s(\theta):=\begin{cases}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{\theta}\right),&\forall\,\theta>0,\\
0,&\forall\,\theta\leq 0.\end{cases}

The diffusion term also requires a careful modification. The required modification is not merely a formal replacement of the Euclidean Laplacian by the Laplace–Beltrami operator
\Delta_{M}
on
M
. Because the volume of geodesic balls on Cartan–Hadamard manifolds grows much faster, standard diffusion
\Delta_{M}\bigl(\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}\rho_{t}(x)\bigr)
is substantially stronger than in Euclidean space, preventing the derivation of Grönwall-type inequalities for higher-order moments. To counteract this dispersive effect, we apply a smooth cutoff
h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x,o))
to the diffusion term outside the compact active region. To avoid introducing an additional parameter, we use the same
\delta
as in the cutoff for the drift term. Accordingly, we further assume that
\delta\in(0,R)
.

Combining these localized geometric adaptations, we are now ready to formulate the
N
-particle CBO system on
M
to solve the problem

\text{Find }x^{*}\in\arg\min_{x\in M}\mathcal{E}(x)\,.

Let
x_{i}(t)
be the position of the
i
-th particle at time
t>0
. Then, our fully intrinsic CBO system is driven by the following system of SDEs:

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\lambda u_{\alpha}(\rho^{N,X(t)};x_{i}(t))\mathrm{d}t+\sigma h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x_{i}(t),o))\|u_{\alpha}(\rho^{N,X(t)};x_{i}(t))\|\mathrm{d}B_{i}^{M}(t)

\displaystyle=\lambda u_{\alpha}(\rho^{N,X(t)};x_{i}(t))\mathrm{d}t+\sigma h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x_{i}(t),o))\|u_{\alpha}(\rho^{N,X(t)};x_{i}(t))\|\sum_{k=1}^{d}E_{k}(x_{i}(t))\mathrm{d}B_{i}^{k}(t),

where
\rho^{N,X(t)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}(t)}
is the empirical measure,
\{E_{k}(x)\}_{k=1}^{d}
is a local orthonormal frame on
T_{x}M
, and
\{B_{i}^{k}(t)\}_{k=1}^{d}
are i.i.d. one-dimensional Brownian motions.

As
N\to\infty
, (
1.5
) formally satisfies the corresponding McKean–Vlasov process on
M
for the law
\rho_{t}
of the limiting process
\bar{x}(t)
:

(1.6)

\mathrm{d}\bar{x}(t)=\lambda u_{\alpha}(\rho_{t};\bar{x}(t))\mathrm{d}t+\sigma h_{R-\delta,R}(d(\bar{x}(t),o))\|u_{\alpha}(\rho_{t};\bar{x}(t))\|\mathrm{d}B^{M}(t).

Here,
B^{M}(t)
denotes a Riemannian Brownian motion on
M
. In addition, the backward generator of the McKean–Vlasov process acting on
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
is

(1.7)

\mathcal{L}_{t}f(x):=\langle\lambda u_{\alpha}(\rho_{t};x),\nabla_{M}f(x)\rangle+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x,o))^{2}\|u_{\alpha}(\rho_{t};x)\|^{2}\Delta_{M}f(x)

and the law
\rho_{t}
governs the collective behavior via the intrinsic nonlinear Fokker–Planck equation on
M
:

(1.8)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}(x)=-\lambda\mathrm{div}_{M}\left(u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\rho_{t}(x)\right)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{M}\left(h_{R-\delta,R}\left(d(x,o)\right)^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}\rho_{t}(x)\right),\\
&\displaystyle u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)=\frac{h_{R,R+\delta}(d(x,o))}{\int_{M}w_{\alpha}(y)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(y)}\int_{B_{R+\delta}(o)}h_{R,R+\delta}(d(y,o))w_{\alpha}(y)\log_{x}y~\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(y),\end{cases}

where
\mathrm{div}_{M}
and
\Delta_{M}
are the divergence and Laplace–Beltrami operators on
M
, respectively, and we simply write
u_{\alpha}^{t}(x):=u_{\alpha}(\rho_{t};x)
.

Compared to the Euclidean model, an essential difference is that the
consensus point
depends on the base point through
\log_{x}(\cdot)
: the map
x\mapsto u_{\alpha}(\rho;x)
is a vector field rather than a constant vector. Moreover, we
localize
both the definition of
u_{\alpha}
and the diffusion term via the cutoff function
h
in order to (i) avoid cut-locus singularities and (ii) recover tractable moment estimates in negatively curved settings.

Contributions.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

•

We propose an intrinsic CBO particle system (
1.5
) and mean-field model (
1.6
) or Fokker–Planck equation (
1.8
) on manifolds with bounded sectional curvature, based on logarithmic averaging in tangent spaces and a geometric cutoff mechanism.

•

We obtain well-posedness for (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) under natural regularity assumptions on the objective function
\mathcal{E}
and explicit curvature-dependent estimates for the logarithmic map (see Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.2
).

•

We rigorously establish the global convergence of the proposed CBO dynamics (
1.8
) toward a global minimizer of the objective function in the mean-field limit (see Theorem
4.1
).

These results provide a rigorous PDE or SDE framework for analyzing consensus-based optimization beyond Euclidean space while preserving the intrinsic geometry.

We also complement our theoretical analysis with numerical experiments that demonstrate the practical behavior of the proposed intrinsic CBO dynamics (
1.5
). The particle system is discretized by a Riemannian Euler–Maruyama scheme where the intrinsic drift is computed through the logarithmic maps and the particles are updated by the exponential map. We test the algorithm on several representative manifolds, including the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{2}
and the special orthogonal group
SO(3)
using highly nonconvex Ackley-type objective functions adapted to each geometric setting. These numerical experiments illustrate that the intrinsic CBO dynamics can successfully guide initially distributed particles toward the global minimizer even in the presence of multiple local minima. Moreover, the observed decay rate of the empirical variance is consistent with the exponential convergence predicted by our analysis.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we present the geometric concepts and preparatory lemmas that will be used throughout the paper. First, as assumed in Section 1, we begin by assuming that the given manifold
M
satisfies condition
(M)
.

2.1.
Injectivity radius and parallel transport

The exponential map is defined as

\exp_{x}:T_{x}M\to M,

and
\exp_{x}v
is defined as
\gamma(1)
, where
\gamma
is the geodesic satisfying
\gamma(0)=x
and
\dot{\gamma}(0)=v
.

Although
\exp_{x}
is well defined, its inverse map
\log_{x}
may not be well-defined on all of
M
, since
\exp_{x}
is not necessarily injective. To describe the region where
\exp_{x}
is injective, we define the injectivity radius
\mathrm{inj}(M)
as follows: for each
x\in M
, let
\mathrm{inj}_{x}(M)
be the supremum of all
r>0
such that
\exp_{x}
is a diffeomorphism from the open ball
B_{r}(\mathbf{0})\subset T_{x}M
onto its image where
\mathbf{0}
is the (
d
-dimensional) zero vector. Then, we define

\mathrm{inj}(M):=\inf_{x\in M}\operatorname{inj}_{x}(M).

In particular, if
r<\operatorname{inj}(M)
, then for every
x\in M
, the map
\exp_{x}
is injective on
B_{r}(\mathbf{0})\subset T_{x}M
, and hence
\log_{x}
is well defined on the corresponding geodesic ball
B_{r}(x)
.

If we assume

\displaystyle R<\min\left(\frac{\mathrm{inj}(M)}{2},\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}}\right),

then any two points
x,y\in B_{R}(o)
can be connected by a unique minimizing geodesic lying entirely in
B_{R}(o)
(see
[
42
, Theorem 6.4.8]
).

In Euclidean space, vectors at different base points can be compared after translating one of them. On a curved manifold, this should be replaced by parallel transport along a curve connecting two points. More precisely, for
x,y\in M
, we denote

P_{xy}:T_{y}M\to T_{x}M

by the parallel transport along a minimizing geodesic from
y
to
x
. Whenever the minimizing geodesic between
x
and
y
is unique,
P_{xy}
is well-defined.

2.2.
Comparison theorems

In this subsection, we present several comparison results on Riemannian manifolds that are related to curvature bounds. From the Laplacian comparison theorem
[
42
, Lemma 7.1.9]
and assumption
(M)
, we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
M
satisfy
(M)
. Then, for each fixed
y\in M
,

\Delta_{x}d(x,y)\leq(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}\coth\bigl(\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,y)\bigr)

for all
x\in M\setminus\bigl(\{y\}\cup\mathrm{Cut}(y)\bigr)
.

Indeed,
[
42
, Lemma 7.1.9]
is stated under a lower bound on the Ricci curvature. However, under assumption
(M)
, the sectional curvature bound
-\kappa_{-}\leq\mathcal{K}(x;\sigma)
implies the Ricci curvature bound
\mathrm{Ric}(M)\geq-(d-1)\kappa_{-}
. Hence the assumptions of
[
42
, Lemma 7.1.9]
are satisfied.

Now, we introduce the Rauch comparison theorem.

Lemma 2.2

(Rauch comparison theorem
[
12
]
)
.

Let
M
and
\tilde{M}
be Riemannian manifolds with
\mathrm{dim}(\tilde{M})\geq\mathrm{dim}(M)
, and suppose that for all
p\in M
,
\tilde{p}\in\tilde{M}
, and

In the analysis of the diffusion term, we need to compare tangent vectors belonging to different tangent spaces. Besides the Lipschitz continuity of the scalar diffusion coefficient, this requires a quantitative control of the variation of the chosen local orthonormal frame under parallel transport. The following lemma shows that after identifying tangent spaces by parallel transport, the frame changes at most linearly with respect to the geodesic distance between the base points.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\{E_{k}\}
be a smooth local orthonormal frame on
M
. Then, there exists a constant
C_{E}>0
such that for all
x,y\in B_{R+\delta}(o)
, we have

\|E_{k}(x)-P_{xy}E_{k}(y)\|\leq C_{E}d(x,y).

Proof.

Since
x,y\in B_{R+\delta}(o)
, there exists the unique geodesic connecting
x
and
y
. For
\ell:=d(x,y)
, choose a unit speed geodesic
\gamma:[0,\ell]\to M
such that

\gamma(0)=x,\quad\gamma(\ell)=y.

Define
V(s):=P_{x\gamma(s)}E_{k}(\gamma(s))\in T_{x}M
. Then,

V(0)=E_{k}(x),\quad V(\ell)=P_{xy}E_{k}(y)

and in addition, there exists a constant
C_{E,k}
such that

V^{\prime}(s)=P_{x^{1}\gamma(s)}(\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}(s)}E_{k}),\quad\|V^{\prime}(s)\|=\|\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}(s)}E_{k}\|\leq C_{E,k}\leq\max_{1\leq k\leq d}C_{E,k}=:C_{E}

which yields the desired estimate

\displaystyle\|E_{k}(x)-P_{xy}E_{k}(y)\|

\displaystyle=\|V(0)-V(\ell)\|=\left\|-\int_{0}^{\ell}V^{\prime}(s)\mathrm{d}s\right\|\leq C_{E}d(x,y).

Below, we state and provide the proof of the theorem for global well-posedness of particle system (
1.5
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that initial data
x_{i}(0)\in M
satisfy

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}d(x_{i}(0),o)^{2}\right]<\infty.

Then for any
T>0
, there exists a unique solution
x_{i}\in C([0,T];M)
for all
i\in[N]
to the particle system (
1.5
). Moreover, there exists a constant
C>0
such that the averaged second moment satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}d(x_{i}(t),o)^{2}\right]\leq C(1+t^{2}),\quad t\in[0,T].

Proof.

Step 1: Well-posedness.

We establish the existence and uniqueness of a strong solution to the particle system up to time
T>0
. Consider the joint configuration space
M^{N}
equipped with the product metric
D(X,Y)
. To guarantee a unique strong solution without finite-time explosion, it follows from
[
32
, Theorem 1.2.9]
that we must verify that both the drift vector field and the diffusion tensor are locally Lipschitz continuous and satisfy a global growth bound.

\bullet
(Drift vector field): Let
b_{i}(X)
be the drift vector field for the
i
-th particle:

b_{i}(X):=\lambda u_{\alpha}(\rho^{N,X};x_{i})\in T_{x_{i}}M.

For any two configurations
X,Y\in M^{N}
that are sufficiently close (i.e.,
D(X,Y)\leq 2(R+\delta)<\mathrm{inj}(M)
), the unique shortest geodesic is well-defined. Using Lemma
3.4
, the distance between drift vectors under parallel transport
P_{x_{i}y_{i}}
from
y_{i}
to
x_{i}
satisfies:

\|b_{i}(X)-P_{x_{i}y_{i}}b_{i}(Y)\|\leq\lambda C_{4}D(X,Y).

This ensures the combined drift vector field on
M^{N}
is locally Lipschitz continuous.

\bullet
(Scalar diffusion coefficient): Let
a_{i}(X)
be the scalar diffusion coefficient of the
i
-th particle:

a_{i}(X):=\sigma h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x_{i},o))\|u_{\alpha}(\rho^{N,X};x_{i})\|.

The full diffusion term for the
i
-th particle can be viewed as a linear map
\mathcal{S}_{i}(X):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to T_{x_{i}}M
acting on the standard basis
\{e_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\mathcal{S}_{i}(X)e_{k}=a_{i}(X)E_{k}(x_{i})
.

To establish Lipschitz continuity, we must bound the Hilbert-Schmidt norm, denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{\textup{HS}}
, of the difference between the operators at
X
and
Y
under parallel transport:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}_{i}(X)-P_{x_{i}y_{i}}\mathcal{S}_{i}(Y)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d}\|\mathcal{S}_{i}(X)e_{k}-P_{x_{i}y_{i}}\mathcal{S}_{i}(Y)e_{k}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{d}\|a_{i}(X)E_{k}(x_{i})-a_{i}(Y)P_{x_{i}y_{i}}E_{k}(y_{i})\|.

Adding and subtracting the cross term
a_{i}(Y)E_{k}(x_{i})
inside the norm and applying the triangle inequality yields:

\|\mathcal{S}_{i}(X)-P_{x_{i}y_{i}}\mathcal{S}_{i}(Y)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{d}\Big(|a_{i}(X)-a_{i}(Y)|\|E_{k}(x_{i})\|+|a_{i}(Y)|\|E_{k}(x_{i})-P_{x_{i}y_{i}}E_{k}(y_{i})\|\Big).

We now bound each component:

•

Since
\{E_{k}\}
is an orthonormal frame,
\|E_{k}(x_{i})\|=1
.

•

By the smoothness of the chosen local orthonormal frame on
M
, for any two configurations
X,Y\in M^{N}
that
D(X,Y)\leq 2(R+\delta)<\min\left(\mathrm{inj}(M),\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}}\right)
we recall from Lemma
3.5
that

\|E_{k}(x_{i})-P_{x_{i}y_{i}}E_{k}(y_{i})\|\leq C_{E}d(x_{i},y_{i})\leq C_{E}D(X,Y).

•

Because the cutoff function
h_{R-\delta,R}
is smooth, compactly supported, and bounded by
1
,
a_{i}(X)
is globally bounded by some constant
A_{\max}=2\sigma(R+\delta)
by using Lemma
3.1
.

•

Furthermore,
a_{i}(X)
is locally Lipschitz continuous. Specifically, combining the Lipschitz constant
\textup{Lip}(h_{R-\delta,R})
of the cutoff function
h_{R-\delta,R}
and the locally Lipschitz constant
C_{4}
of
u_{\alpha}
from Lemma
3.4
, there exists a constant
L_{a}
such that

|a_{i}(X)-a_{i}(Y)|\leq L_{a}D(X,Y).

By substituting these bounds into the inequality, we obtain:

\|\mathcal{S}_{i}(X)-P_{x_{i}y_{i}}\mathcal{S}_{i}(Y)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{d}\Big(L_{a}D(X,Y)+A_{\max}C_{E}D(X,Y)\Big)=d(L_{a}+A_{\max}C_{E})D(X,Y).

This proves that the diffusion tensor is locally Lipschitz continuous on
M^{N}
.

Because the cutoff function
h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x_{i},o))
explicitly vanishes for
d(x_{i},o)\geq R
, the diffusion coefficient is globally bounded. Moreover, the drift coefficient is bounded by the cutoff
h_{R,R+\delta}
together with Lemma
3.1
.
Hence both the drift and diffusion coefficients are globally bounded on
M^{N}
. In addition, since the coefficients are locally Lipschitz continuous and globally bounded (which trivially satisfies the linear growth condition), standard existence and uniqueness theorems for SDEs on manifolds in
[
32
, Theorem 1.2.9]
guarantee that the system does not explode in finite time, yielding a unique strong global solution
X(t)\in C([0,T];M^{N})
.

Step 2: Moment Bound.
First, we recall from Lemma
3.1
that

\|u_{\alpha}(\rho^{N,X};x_{i})\|\leq 2(R+\delta).

Now, we use
\tilde{\Psi}_{o}(x)=\frac{1}{2}d(x,o)^{2}
to get

\nabla_{M}\tilde{\Psi}_{o}(x)=-\log_{x}o.

It follows from the chain rule that for a sufficiently smooth function
g
,

(3.1)

\Delta_{M}g(d(x,x^{*}))=g^{\prime\prime}(d(x,x^{*}))+g^{\prime}(d(x,x^{*}))\Delta_{M}d(x,x^{*}).

From the Laplacian comparison theorem with

in (
3.1
), we also get

\displaystyle\Delta_{M}\tilde{\Psi}_{o}(x)

\displaystyle\leq 1+(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,o)\coth{\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,o)}

\displaystyle\leq d+(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,o),

where we used
s\coth{s}\leq 1+s
for
s\geq 0
. Recall the generator
\mathcal{L}
of the SDE, and apply it to
\tilde{\Psi}_{o}(x_{i})
:

\mathcal{L}_{t}\tilde{\Psi}_{o}(x_{i})=\langle\lambda u_{\alpha}(\rho_{t};x_{i}),\nabla_{M}\tilde{\Psi}_{o}(x_{i})\rangle+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x_{i},o))^{2}\|u_{\alpha}(\rho_{t};x_{i})\|^{2}\Delta_{M}\tilde{\Psi}_{o}(x_{i}).

The first term can be estimated as

\langle\lambda u_{\alpha}(\rho_{t};x_{i}),\nabla_{M}\tilde{\Psi}_{o}(x_{i})\rangle\leq 2\lambda(R+\delta)d(x_{i},o),

and the second term can be simplified into

\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x_{i},o))^{2}\|u_{\alpha}(\rho_{t};x_{i})\|^{2}\Delta_{M}\tilde{\Psi}_{o}(x_{i})\leq 2\sigma^{2}(R+\delta)^{2}\left(d+(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x_{i},o)\right).

Thus, the generator satisfies

\mathcal{L}_{t}\tilde{\Psi}_{o}(x_{i})\leq 2\sigma^{2}(R+\delta)^{2}d+\left(2\lambda(R+\delta)+2\sigma^{2}(R+\delta)^{2}(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}\right)d(x_{i},o).

Let us denote

m_{2}^{N}(t):=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}d(x_{i}(t),o)^{2}\right].

We take the expectations to find

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}m_{2}^{N}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}2\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{\Psi}_{o}(x_{i}(t))}{\mathrm{d}t}\right]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}2\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}_{t}\tilde{\Psi}_{o}(X_{t}^{i})\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\sigma^{2}(R+\delta)^{2}d+\frac{1}{N}\left(4\lambda(R+\delta)+4\sigma^{2}(R+\delta)^{2}(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[d(x_{i},o)]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\sigma^{2}(R+\delta)^{2}d+\left(4\lambda(R+\delta)+4\sigma^{2}(R+\delta)^{2}(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}\right)\sqrt{m_{2}^{N}(t)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C+C\sqrt{m_{2}^{N}(t)}.

Therefore, the growth rate of
m_{2}
is less than quadratic and we get the desired result.

For a solution
\bar{x}(t)
to McKean–Vlasov process, we use Itô’s formula to see that the law
\rho_{t}=\textup{Law}(\bar{x}(t))
satisfies the following PDE:

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}(x)=-\lambda\nabla_{M}\cdot(u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t};x)\rho_{t}(x))+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{M}\left(h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x,o))^{2}\|u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t};x)\|^{2}\rho_{t}(x)\right)

in the distributional sense, since the generator is given as (
1.7
). From the definition of the weak solution, we know

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{M}\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{M}\nabla_{M}\phi(x)\cdot u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t};x)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\int_{M}h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x,o))^{2}\|u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t};x)\|^{2}\Delta_{M}\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x).

Now, we assume
\phi_{\epsilon}:M\to[0,1]
is a smooth radially symmetric function with respect to
o
satisfying

\phi_{\epsilon}(x):=\phi_{\epsilon}(d(x,o)):=\begin{cases}1&\quad d(x,o)\in[0,R+\delta],\\
0&\quad d(x,o)\in[R+\delta+\epsilon,\infty).\end{cases}

Furthermore, we can choose the set of test functions to satisfy
\{\phi_{\epsilon}(d(\cdot,o))\}\searrow\chi_{B_{R+\delta}(o)}
as
\epsilon\searrow 0
. Since
R+\delta<\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}}
, we choose small
\epsilon
to satisfy

R+\delta+\epsilon<\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}}.

On the one hand, we have

\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\int_{M}h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x,o))^{2}\|u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t};x)\|^{2}\Delta_{M}\phi_{\epsilon}(x)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)=0

since
\Delta_{M}\phi_{\epsilon}(x)\equiv 0
for
x\in B_{R+\delta}(o)
and
|u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t};x)|\equiv 0
on
B_{R+\delta}(o)^{c}
. On the other hand,
\nabla_{M}\phi_{\epsilon}(d(x,o))\cdot u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\geq 0
(see Appendix
A.8
) implies

\lambda\int_{M}\nabla_{M}\phi(x)\cdot u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t};x)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)\geq 0.

Combining the above results gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{M}\phi_{\epsilon}(d(x,o))\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)\geq 0,\quad\forall~\epsilon>0,~t>0.

Now, we assume that the initial distribution
\rho_{0}
is supported in
B_{R+\delta}(o)
, i.e.,
\int_{B_{R+\delta}(o)}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(x)=1
. We also fix
t>0
and
\epsilon>0
. Then, we know the following inequality holds:

\int_{M}\phi_{\epsilon}(x)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)\geq\int_{M}\phi_{\epsilon}(x)\mathrm{d}\rho_{\tau}(x),\quad\forall~\tau\in(0,t).

Since
\phi_{\epsilon}\in C_{b}(M)
, we use the initial condition and the dominated convergence theorem to get

\lim_{\tau\to 0+}\int_{M}\phi_{\epsilon}(x)\mathrm{d}\rho_{\tau}(x)=\int_{M}\phi_{\epsilon}(x)\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(x)\geq\int_{B_{R+\delta}(o)}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(x)=1.

Thus, we know the following inequality holds for any
t>0
and
\epsilon>0
:

\int_{M}\phi_{\epsilon}(x)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)\geq 1.

Again, it follows from the dominated convergence theorem that

1\geq\int_{B_{R+\delta}(o)}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0+}\int_{M}\phi_{\epsilon}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)\geq 1,\quad\forall~t>0.

Finally, we can conclude that
B_{R+\delta}(o)
is the invariant set of the given system. For this reason, we know
x(t)
lies in
B_{R+\delta}(o)
almost surely.

Step 3: Contraction.
Now, we show that
\Phi
is actually a contraction. To this end, for
\mu^{1},\mu^{2}\in C([0,T],\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{M}))
, we consider solutions
x^{i}(i=1,2)
to the linear SDEs driven by the same Brownian motion:

\mathrm{d}x^{i}(t)=\lambda u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{i};x^{i})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x^{i},o))\|u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{i};x^{i})\|\sum_{k=1}^{d}E_{k}(x^{i})\mathrm{d}B^{k}(t).

Now, we set

b^{i}(x;t):=\lambda u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{i};x),\quad a^{i}(x;t):=\sigma h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x,o))\|u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{i};x)\|.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x^{i}(t)

\displaystyle=b^{i}(x^{i};t)\mathrm{d}t+a^{i}(x^{i};t)\sum_{k=1}^{d}E_{k}(x^{i})\mathrm{d}B^{k}(t),\quad i=1,2.

The process
(x^{1},x^{2})
satisfies an SDE on
M\times M
:

\mathrm{d}(x^{1},x^{2})=(b^{1}(x^{1};t),b^{2}(x^{2};t))\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{k=1}^{d}(a^{1}(x^{1};t)E_{k}(x^{1}),a^{2}(x^{2};t)E_{k}(x^{2}))\mathrm{d}B^{k}(t).

For the handy notation, we use

V^{i}_{k}:=a^{i}(x^{i};t)E_{k}(x^{i}).

Then, the generator corresponding to this process is

\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{t}\phi(x^{1},x^{2}):=\langle\nabla\phi,(b^{1},b^{2})\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathrm{Hess}\phi(x^{1},x^{2})[(V_{k}^{1},V_{k}^{2}),(V_{k}^{1},V_{k}^{2})],\quad\forall~\phi\in C^{\infty}(M\times M).

Now, apply Itô’s formula to the process
(x^{1},x^{2})
with
\Psi(x^{1},x^{2})=\frac{1}{2}d(x^{1},x^{2})^{2}
to get

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[\Psi(x^{1}(t),x^{2}(t))]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{t}\Psi(x^{1}(t),x^{2}(t))]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\langle\nabla_{x^{1}}\Psi(x^{1}(t),x^{2}(t)),b^{1}\rangle+\langle\nabla_{x^{2}}\Psi(x^{1}(t),x^{2}(t)),b^{2}\rangle]}_{=:\mathcal{I}_{1}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Hess}\Psi(x^{1},x^{2})[(V_{k}^{1},V_{k}^{2}),(V_{k}^{1},V_{k}^{2})]\right]}_{=:\mathcal{I}_{2}}.

Below, we estimate
\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}
, respectively. Since the solution remains in
B_{R+\delta}(o)
almost surely, we may consider two sample paths of the SDEs:

x^{1}(t),x^{2}(t)\in B_{R+\delta}(o)\quad\forall~t\geq 0.

\bullet
(Estimate of
\mathcal{I}_{1}
): We observe

\nabla_{x^{1}}\Psi(x^{1},x^{2})=-\log_{x^{1}}x^{2},\quad\nabla_{x^{2}}\Psi(x^{1},x^{2})=-\log_{x^{2}}x^{1}.

Then,
\mathcal{I}_{1}
can be simplified as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\langle\nabla_{x^{1}}\Psi(x^{1}(t),x^{2}(t)),b^{1}\rangle+\langle\nabla_{x^{2}}\Psi(x^{1}(t),x^{2}(t)),b^{2}\rangle]=\mathbb{E}[-\langle\log_{x^{1}}x^{2},b^{1}-P_{x^{1}x^{2}}b^{2}\rangle]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[d(x^{1},x^{2})\|b^{1}-P_{x^{1}x^{2}}b^{2}\|]=\lambda\mathbb{E}[d(x^{1},x^{2})\|u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{1};x^{1})-P_{x^{1}x^{2}}u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{2};x^{2})\|]

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\mathbb{E}[d(x^{1},x^{2})\|u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{1};x^{1})-P_{x^{1}x^{2}}u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{1};x^{2})\|]

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda\mathbb{E}[d(x^{1},x^{2})\|P_{x^{1}x^{2}}u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{1};x^{2})-P_{x^{1}x^{2}}u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{2};x^{2})\|]

\displaystyle=:\mathcal{I}_{11}+\mathcal{I}_{12}

\diamond
(Estimate of
\mathcal{I}_{11}
): We use Lemma
3.2
to find

\mathcal{I}_{11}\leq\lambda C_{2}\mathbb{E}[d(x^{1},x^{2})^{2}].

\diamond
(Estimate of
\mathcal{I}_{12}
): We observe

\displaystyle\|P_{x^{1}x^{2}}u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{1};x^{2})-P_{x^{1}x^{2}}u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{2};x^{2})\|

\displaystyle=\|u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{1};x^{2})-u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{2};x^{2})\|]

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}W_{2}(\mu_{t}^{1},\mu_{t}^{2}),

where the isometric property of parallel transport is used for the equality and Lemma
3.3
for the inequality. Thus, by Hölder’s inequality, we have

\mathcal{I}_{12}\leq\frac{\lambda C_{3}}{2}\mathbb{E}[d(x^{1},x^{2})^{2}]+\frac{\lambda C_{3}}{2}W_{2}(\mu_{t}^{1},\mu_{t}^{2})^{2}.

Thus, there exist two positive constants
C_{10}
and
C_{11}
such that

\mathcal{I}_{1}\leq C_{10}\mathbb{E}[d(x^{1},x^{2})^{2}]+C_{11}W_{2}(\mu_{t}^{1},\mu_{t}^{2})^{2}.

\bullet
(Estimate of
\mathcal{I}_{2}
): It follows from estimate (
2.1
) that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Hess}\Psi(x^{1},x^{2})[(V_{k}^{1},V_{k}^{2}),(V_{k}^{1},V_{k}^{2})]

\displaystyle\leq\|V_{k}^{1}-P_{x^{1}x^{2}}V_{k}^{2}\|^{2}+\frac{\kappa_{-}d(x^{1},x^{2})^{2}}{2}(\|V_{k}^{1}\|^{2}+\|V_{k}^{2}\|^{2})

\displaystyle=:\mathcal{I}_{21}+\mathcal{I}_{22}.

\diamond
(Estimate of
\mathcal{I}_{21}
): We decompose
\|V_{k}^{1}-P_{x^{1}x^{2}}V_{k}^{2}\|
into three terms:

\displaystyle\|V_{k}^{1}-P_{x^{1}x^{2}}V_{k}^{2}\|

\displaystyle=\|a^{1}(x^{1};t)E_{k}(x^{1})-a^{2}(x^{2};t)P_{x^{1}x^{2}}E_{k}(x^{2})\|

\displaystyle=\|(a^{1}(x^{1};t)-a^{2}(x^{1};t))E_{k}(x^{1})\|+\|(a^{2}(x^{1};t)-a^{2}(x^{2};t))E_{k}(x^{1})\|

\displaystyle\quad+\|a^{2}(x^{2};t)(E_{k}(x^{1})-P_{x^{1}x^{2}}E_{k}(x^{2}))\|

\displaystyle\leq|a^{1}(x^{1};t)-a^{2}(x^{1};t)|+|a^{2}(x^{1};t)-a^{2}(x^{2};t)|+|a^{2}(x^{2};t)|\cdot\|E_{k}(x^{1})-P_{x^{1}x^{2}}E_{k}(x^{2})\|

\displaystyle=:\mathcal{I}_{211}+\mathcal{I}_{212}+\mathcal{I}_{213}.

\circ
(Estimate of
\mathcal{I}_{211}
): We use Lemma
3.3
to find

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{211}

\displaystyle=|a^{1}(x^{1};t)-a^{2}(x^{1};t)|=\sigma\eta(d(x^{1},o))\left|\|u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{1};x^{1})\|-\|u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{2};x^{1})\|\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sigma h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x^{1},o))\|u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{1};x^{1})-u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{2};x^{1})\|

\displaystyle\leq\sigma C_{3}W_{2}(\mu_{t}^{1},\mu_{t}^{2}).

\circ
(Estimate of
\mathcal{I}_{212}
): We observe

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{212}

\displaystyle=|a^{2}(x^{1};t)-a^{2}(x^{2};t)|

\displaystyle=\sigma|h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x^{1},o))\|u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{2};x^{1})\|-h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x^{2},o))\|u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{2};x^{2})\||

\displaystyle\leq\sigma h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x^{1},o))\left|\|u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{2};x^{1})\|-\|u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{2};x^{2})\|\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\sigma|h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x^{1},o))-h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x^{2},o))|\|u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{2};x^{2})\|

\displaystyle\leq\sigma\|u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{2};x^{1})-u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{2};x^{2})\|+\sigma|h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x^{1},o))-h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x^{2},o))|\|u_{\alpha}(\mu_{t}^{2};x^{2})\|

\displaystyle\leq C_{12}d(x^{1},x^{2})

where we used Lemma
3.2
to estimate the first term and used the Lipschitz property of
h_{R-\delta,R}
and Lemma
3.1
to estimate the second term.

\circ
(Estimate of
\mathcal{I}_{213}
): We recall from Lemma
3.5
that

\displaystyle\|E_{k}(x^{1})-P_{x^{1}x^{2}}E_{k}(x^{2})\|

\displaystyle=\|V(0)-V(\ell)\|=\left\|-\int_{0}^{\ell}V^{\prime}(s)\mathrm{d}s\right\|\leq C_{E}d(x^{1},x^{2}).

Thus, we see

\mathcal{I}_{213}\leq C_{E}|a^{2}(x^{2};t)|d(x^{1},x^{2})\leq C_{13}d(x^{1},x^{2}).

We collect the estimates for
\mathcal{I}_{211}
,
\mathcal{I}_{212}
and
\mathcal{I}_{213}
to find

\mathcal{I}_{21}=\|V_{k}^{1}-P_{x^{1}x^{2}}V_{k}^{2}\|^{2}\leq C_{14}W_{2}(\mu_{t}^{1},\mu_{t}^{2})^{2}+C_{15}d(x^{1},x^{2})^{2}.

\bullet
(Estimate of
\mathcal{I}_{22}
): Recall from Lemma
3.1
that
\|V_{k}^{1}\|^{2}
and
\|V_{k}^{2}\|^{2}
are bounded above by
4\sigma^{2}(R+\delta)^{2}
. Thus, we have

\mathcal{I}_{22}=\frac{\kappa_{-}d(x^{1},x^{2})^{2}}{2}(\|V_{k}^{1}\|^{2}+\|V_{k}^{2}\|^{2})\leq 4\kappa_{-}\sigma^{2}(R+\delta)^{2}d(x^{1},x^{2})^{2}

and consequently, we see

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Hess}\Psi(x^{1},x^{2})[(V_{k}^{1},V_{k}^{2}),(V_{k}^{1},V_{k}^{2})]\right]\leq C_{14}W_{2}(\mu_{t}^{1},\mu_{t}^{2})^{2}+C_{16}\mathbb{E}[d(x^{1},x^{2})^{2}].

Finally, we derive a differential inequality for
D(t):=\mathbb{E}[d(x^{1}(t),x^{2}(t))^{2}]
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}D(t)\leq C_{17}W_{2}(\mu_{t}^{1},\mu_{t}^{2})^{2}+C_{18}D(t).

Recall that we have
D(0)=0
since we consider the common initial condition of the two SDEs.
By Grönwall’s inequality, we integrate this to find:

D(t)\leq C_{17}\int_{0}^{t}e^{C_{18}(t-s)}W_{2}(\mu_{s}^{1},\mu_{s}^{2})^{2}\mathrm{d}s\leq C_{17}te^{C_{18}t}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}W_{2}(\mu_{s}^{1},\mu_{s}^{2})^{2}.

Taking the supremum over
t\in[0,T]
, we eventually obtain

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}W_{2}(\rho^{1}(t),\rho^{2}(t))^{2}\leq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}D(t)\leq C_{17}Te^{C_{18}T}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}W_{2}(\mu_{t}^{1},\mu_{t}^{2})^{2}.

Therefore, if we choose a sufficiently small
T^{*}>0
, there exists
0<C_{19}<1
such that

\sup_{t\in[0,T^{*}]}W_{2}(\rho^{1}(t),\rho^{2}(t))\leq C_{19}\sup_{t\in[0,T^{*}]}W_{2}(\mu^{1}_{t},\mu_{t}^{2}).

Then,
\Phi
is a contraction and there exists a solution by the Banach fixed point theorem.

Because the constants
C_{17}
and
C_{18}
depend only on the geometry of
M
and the cutoff parameters, and are completely independent of the initial measure
\rho_{0}
, we can iterate this contraction argument on sequential intervals
[T^{*},2T^{*}],[2T^{*},3T^{*}],\dots
to extend the unique solution to any time horizon
T>0
.
∎

In addition, it is easy to check that the associated law
\rho=\textup{law}(\bar{x})\in C([0,T],\mathcal{P}_{2}(M))
satisfies the corresponding Fokker–Planck equation (
1.8
) in the weak sense by using Itô’s formula.
Furthermore, if
x_{0}\in B_{R}(o)
and
\mu\in C([0,T],\mathcal{P}(B_{R}(o)))
then
\rho\in C([0,T],\mathcal{P}(B_{R}(o)))
. Therefore,
B_{R}(o)
is an invariant set of the PDE in a similar way. If
B_{R}(o)
is an invariant set, we can reduce the system as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}(x)=-\lambda\mathrm{div}_{M}\bigg(u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\rho_{t}(x)\bigg)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{M}\left(h_{R-\delta,R}\left(d(x,o)\right)^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}\rho_{t}(x)\right),\\
&\displaystyle u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)=\frac{1}{\int_{B_{R}(o)}w_{\alpha}(y)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(y)}\int_{B_{R}(o)}w_{\alpha}(y)\log_{x}y\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(y).\end{cases}

4.
Global Convergence

In this section, we present our main result about the global convergence of the CBO dynamics in a mean-field law for the objective function
\mathcal{E}
. In the previous section, the well-posedness of the mean-field dynamics was established. We now turn to the long-time behavior of the solution. The goal of this section is to show that for a sufficiently large
\alpha
, the law
\rho_{t}
concentrates around a global minimizer
x^{*}
of the objective function
\mathcal{E}
. The proof consists of four steps.

\bullet
Step A: First, we derive a dissipative estimate for the variance functional
\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]
. This estimate shows that
\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]
decays if the velocity vector field
u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)
is sufficiently close to the target field
\log_{x}x^{*}
(see Lemma
4.1
).

\bullet
Step B: Second, we use a quantitative Laplace principle to estimate drift error
\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|
(see Proposition
4.1
).

\bullet
Step C: Third, we show that any initial mass located near the global minimizer persists for any finite time (see Proposition
4.2
).

\bullet
Step D: Finally, we combine these estimates through a stopping time argument to obtain exponential decay of
\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]
up to the prescribed accuracy (see Theorem
4.1
).

4.1.
Dissipative estimate of the energy functional

In the Euclidean space, the following functional was used:

\mathcal{V}_{\textup{Euclidean}}[\rho]:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-x^{*}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x).

Here,
x^{*}
is the global minimizer of
\mathcal{E}
. Since
\|x^{*}-x\|
is replaced by
d(x^{*},x)
, we can naturally generalize the above functional as follows:

\mathcal{V}[\rho]:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{R}(o)}d(x,x^{*})^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho(x),

where
x^{*}\in M
is the global minimizer of
\mathcal{E}
. Here, we integrated only over
B_{R}(o)
since we assume that
\rho_{0}
is supported on
B_{R}(o)
, which is positively invariant along PDE (
1.8
). The functional
\mathcal{V}[\rho]
measures the concentration of the law
\rho
around the target minimizer
x^{*}
. In particular, smallness of
\mathcal{V}[\rho]
implies that most of the probability mass is contained in a small geodesic neighborhood of
x^{*}
. In this regard, we derive the dissipative estimate of
\mathcal{V}[\rho]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
T>0
and let
\rho\in C\left([0,T],\mathcal{P}_{2}(M)\right)
be a weak solution to (
1.8
). Then, the functional
\mathcal{V}[\rho]
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]

\displaystyle\leq-\left(2\lambda-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}(d+2(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}R)\right)\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]

\displaystyle\quad+\Big(\lambda+\sigma^{2}(d+2(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}R)\Big)\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]^{1/2}\sup_{x\in B_{R}(o)}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}(d+2(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}R)\sup_{x\in B_{R}(o)}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|^{2}.

Proof.

We recall from Definition
2.1
the definition of a weak solution to see

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{B_{R}(o)}d(x,x^{*})^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{B_{R}(o)}\left(\nabla_{M}d(x,x^{*})^{2}\right)\cdot u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\int_{B_{R}(o)}\Delta_{M}(d(x,x^{*})^{2})h_{R-\delta,R}\left(d(x,o)\right)^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=:\mathcal{I}_{3}+\mathcal{I}_{4}.

We estimate
\mathcal{I}_{3}
and
\mathcal{I}_{4}
one by one.

\bullet
(Estimate of
\mathcal{I}_{3}
): We use Hölder’s inequality to find

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{3}

\displaystyle=-\lambda\int_{B_{R}(o)}\log_{x}x^{*}\cdot u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=-\lambda\int_{B_{R}(o)}\log_{x}x^{*}\cdot\log_{x}x^{*}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)-\lambda\int_{B_{R}(o)}\log_{x}x^{*}\cdot(u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*})\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\leq-2\lambda\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]+\lambda\int_{B_{R}(o)}d(x,x^{*})\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\leq-2\lambda\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]+\lambda\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]^{1/2}\sup_{x\in B_{R}(o)}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|.

\bullet
(Estimate of
\mathcal{I}_{4}
): The second term can be estimated as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\int_{B_{R}(o)}\Delta_{M}\left(d(x,x^{*})^{2}\right)h_{R-\delta,R}\left(d(x,o)\right)^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\int_{B_{R}(o)}\bigg(1+(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*})\coth\left(\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*})\right)\bigg)\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\int_{B_{R}(o)}\left(d+(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*})\right)\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x).

Here, we used
x\coth x\leq 1+x
at the last inequality. Furthermore, since
x,x^{*}\in{B_{R}(o)}
, we have
d(x,x^{*})\leq 2R
. Thus, we can further estimate
\mathcal{I}_{4}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{4}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}(d+2(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}R)\int_{B_{R}(o)}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}(d+2(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}R)\int_{B_{R}(o)}\|(u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*})+\log_{x}x^{*}\|^{2}\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}(d+2(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}R)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\sup_{x\in B_{R}(o)}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|^{2}+2\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]^{1/2}\sup_{x\in B_{R}(o)}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|+\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]\right).

Hence, we obtain the desired result:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]

\displaystyle\leq-\left(2\lambda-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}(d+2(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}R)\right)\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]

\displaystyle\quad+\Big(\lambda+\sigma^{2}(d+2(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}R)\Big)\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]^{1/2}\sup_{x\in B_{R}(o)}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}(d+2(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}R)\sup_{x\in B_{R}(o)}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|^{2}.

∎

Lemma
4.1
reduces the convergence problem to the control of the drift error

\sup_{x\in B_{R}(o)}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|.

In fact, if this quantity can be bounded by a sufficiently small multiple of
\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]^{\frac{1}{2}}
, then exponential decay of
\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]
is derived.
To obtain a quantitative estimate of the drift error, we impose the following structural assumptions on the objective function which are quite standard in the analysis of CBO-type methods, for instance
[
10
,
23
]
.

Besides
(E)
in the Introduction, we impose the following additional assumptions on
\mathcal{E}
.

(A1)
: There exists a unique
x^{*}\in M
such that
\mathcal{E}(x^{*})=\inf_{x\in M}\mathcal{E}(x)=\underline{\mathcal{E}}
.

(A2)
: there exist
\mathcal{E}_{\infty},R_{0},\eta>0
and
\nu\in(0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\textup{(i)}~~d(x,x^{*})\leq\frac{(\mathcal{E}(x)-\underline{\mathcal{E}})^{\nu}}{\eta},\quad\forall~x\in B_{R_{0}}(x^{*}).

\displaystyle\textup{(ii)}~~\mathcal{E}(x)-\underline{\mathcal{E}}>\mathcal{E}_{\infty},\quad\forall~x\in(B_{R_{0}}(x^{*}))^{c}.

(A3)
: choose
R_{0}>0
and
\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\infty}^{\nu}}{\eta}
sufficiently small in the sense that

\textup{(i)}~~B_{R_{0}}(x^{*})\subseteq B_{R}(o).\quad\textup{(ii)}~~B_{\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\infty}^{\nu}}{\eta}}(x^{*})\subseteq B_{R}(o).

Remark 4.1
.

We provide some comments on (A3) for
\mathcal{E}
. First, regarding (A3)(i), we will later need to integrate over
B_{r}(x^{*})
, for instance in Proposition
4.1
. For this purpose, it is necessary that
B_{R_{0}}(x^{*})\subseteq B_{R}(o)
for every
r\in(0,R_{0}]
. Thus, we need (A3)(i). Second, for (A3)(ii), the domain of integration is
B_{\tilde{r}}(x^{*})
, for instance in the proof of Proposition
4.1
. Again, since we will later assume that
\tilde{r}\leq\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\infty}^{\nu}}{\eta}
, we need (A3)(ii) in order to ensure that the relevant integration domain is contained in
B_{R}(o)
.

4.2.
Quantitative Laplace principle

In this subsection, we quantify how closely the drift
u_{\alpha}^{t}
approximates the ideal drift toward the global minimizer
x^{*}
. The following proposition establishes a quantitative Laplace estimate for the drift. It shows that the drift
u_{\alpha}^{t}
is uniformly close to the ideal field
x\mapsto\log_{x}x^{*}
with a suitable error.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\alpha>0
be fixed. For any
r>0
, define

\mathcal{E}_{r}:=\sup_{y\in B_{r}(x^{*})}\mathcal{E}(y).

Then, for any
r\in(0,R_{0}]
and
q>0
such that

q+\mathcal{E}_{r}\leq\mathcal{E}_{\infty},

we have

\sup_{x\in B_{R}(o)}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|\leq\frac{(q+\mathcal{E}_{r})^{\nu}}{\eta}+\frac{e^{-\alpha q}}{\int_{x\in B_{r}(x^{*})}\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x}\int_{B_{R}(o)}d(y,x^{*})\rho(y)\mathrm{d}y.

Proof.

We fix
r>0
first. Now, let us estimate
\sup_{x\in B_{R}(o)}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|
. To do so, we consider arbitrary
x\in B_{R}(o)
first.

\displaystyle u_{\alpha}(\rho;x)-\log_{x}x^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|w_{\alpha}\rho\|_{1}}\int_{B_{R}(o)}w_{\alpha}(y)\log_{x}y~\mathrm{d}\rho(y)-\log_{x}x^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|w_{\alpha}\rho\|_{1}}\int_{B_{R}(o)}w_{\alpha}(y)(\log_{x}y-\log_{x}x^{*})\mathrm{d}\rho(y).

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\|u_{\alpha}(\rho;x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\|w_{\alpha}\rho\|_{1}}\int_{B_{R}(o)}w_{\alpha}(y)d(y,x^{*})\mathrm{d}\rho(y)=:\mathcal{I}_{5}.

Here, we used
\|\log_{x}y-\log_{x}x^{*}\|\leq d(y,x^{*})
. Let
\tilde{r}\geq r>0
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{5}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|w_{\alpha}\rho\|_{1}}\int_{B_{R}(o)\cap B_{\tilde{r}}(x^{*})}w_{\alpha}(y)d(y,x^{*})\mathrm{d}\rho(y)+\frac{1}{\|w_{\alpha}\rho\|_{1}}\int_{B_{R}(o)\cap B_{\tilde{r}}(x^{*})^{c}}w_{\alpha}(y)d(y,x^{*})\mathrm{d}\rho(y)

\displaystyle=:\mathcal{I}_{51}+\mathcal{I}_{52}.

Below, we estimate
\mathcal{I}_{51}
and
\mathcal{I}_{52}
, respectively.

\bullet
(Estimate of
\mathcal{I}_{51}
): If
y\in B_{R}(o)\cap B_{\tilde{r}}(x^{*})
, then
d(y,x^{*})\leq\tilde{r}
. For this reason, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{51}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|w_{\alpha}\rho\|_{1}}\int_{B_{R}(o)\cap B_{\tilde{r}}(x^{*})}w_{\alpha}(y)d(y,x^{*})\mathrm{d}\rho(y)\leq\frac{\tilde{r}}{\|w_{\alpha}\rho\|_{1}}\int_{B_{R}(o)\cap B_{\tilde{r}}(x^{*})}w_{\alpha}(y)\mathrm{d}\rho(y)\leq\tilde{r}.

\bullet
(Estimate of
\mathcal{I}_{52}
): For any
a>0
, we observe

\displaystyle\|w_{\alpha}\rho\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}(o)}w_{\alpha}(x)\mathrm{d}\rho(x)=\int_{B_{R}(o)}e^{-\alpha\mathcal{E}(x)}\mathrm{d}\rho(x)\geq a\int_{\{x\in B_{R}(o):e^{-\alpha\mathcal{E}(x)}\geq a\}}\mathrm{d}\rho(x).

Recall that

\mathcal{E}_{r}=\sup_{y\in B_{R}(o)\cap B_{r}(x^{*})}\mathcal{E}(y).

If we choose
a=e^{-\alpha\mathcal{E}_{r}}
, then

\displaystyle\int_{\{x\in B_{R}(o):e^{-\alpha\mathcal{E}(x)}\geq e^{-\alpha\mathcal{E}_{r}}\}}\mathrm{d}\rho(x)=\int_{\{x\in B_{R}(o):\mathcal{E}(x)\leq\mathcal{E}_{r}\}}\mathrm{d}\rho(x)\geq\int_{B_{R}(o)\cap B_{r}(x^{*})}\mathrm{d}\rho(x).

Finally, this gives the lower estimate of
\|w_{\alpha}\rho\|_{1}
:

\|w_{\alpha}\rho\|_{1}\geq e^{-\alpha\mathcal{E}_{r}}\int_{B_{R}(o)\cap B_{r}(x^{*})}\mathrm{d}\rho(x).

Now, we can estimate
\mathcal{I}_{52}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{52}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|w_{\alpha}\rho\|_{1}}\int_{B_{R}(o)\cap B_{\tilde{r}}(x^{*})^{c}}w_{\alpha}(y)d(y,x^{*})\mathrm{d}\rho(y)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{\alpha\mathcal{E}_{r}}}{\int_{B_{R}(o)\cap B_{r}(x^{*})}\mathrm{d}\rho(x)}\int_{B_{R}(o)\cap B_{\tilde{r}}(x^{*})^{c}}w_{\alpha}(y)d(y,x^{*})\mathrm{d}\rho(y)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\exp\left(-\alpha(\inf_{y\in B_{R}(o)\cap B_{\tilde{r}}(x^{*})^{c}}\mathcal{E}(y)-\mathcal{E}_{r})\right)}{\int_{B_{R}(o)\cap B_{r}(x^{*})}\rho(x)\mathrm{d}x}\int_{B_{R}(o)\cap B_{\tilde{r}}(x^{*})^{c}}d(y,x^{*})\mathrm{d}\rho(y).

Thus, for any
\tilde{r}\geq r>0
,

\displaystyle\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|\leq\tilde{r}+\frac{\exp\left(-\alpha(\inf_{y\in B_{R}(o)\cap B_{\tilde{r}}(x^{*})^{c}}\mathcal{E}(y)-\mathcal{E}_{r})\right)}{\int_{B_{R}(o)\cap B_{r}(x^{*})}\rho(x)\mathrm{d}x}\int_{B_{R}(o)\cap B_{\tilde{r}}(x^{*})^{c}}d(y,x^{*})\mathrm{d}\rho(y).

Now we choose
\tilde{r}
as

\tilde{r}=\frac{(q+\mathcal{E}_{r})^{\nu}}{\eta}

where
\eta
and
\nu
are introduced in Assumption (A2) (i),
\mathcal{E}_{r}
is defined in the proposition, and
q>0
is an arbitrary number given in the proposition. Since
q+\mathcal{E}_{r}\leq\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
,

\tilde{r}\leq\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\infty}^{\nu}}{\eta}.

On the other hand, we have to check that
\tilde{r}\geq r
indeed holds. Since we consider
r\leq R_{0}
and assume
\underline{\mathcal{E}}=0
without loss of generality, we use Assumption (A2) (i) to find

\tilde{r}=\frac{(q+\mathcal{E}_{r})^{\nu}}{\eta}\geq\frac{\mathcal{E}_{r}^{\nu}}{\eta}=\frac{(\sup_{y\in B_{r}(x^{*})}\mathcal{E}(y))^{\nu}}{\eta}\geq\sup_{x\in B_{r}(x^{*})}d(x,x^{*})=r.

Lastly, we use assumption (A2)(i) and (A2)(ii) to find

\displaystyle\inf_{y\in(B_{\tilde{r}}(x^{*}))^{c})}\mathcal{E}(y)\geq\min\{\mathcal{E}_{\infty},(\eta\tilde{r})^{\frac{1}{\nu}}\}=(\eta\tilde{r})^{\frac{1}{\nu}}

depending on the sign of
\tilde{r}-R_{0}
. Thus, we have

e^{-\alpha(\inf_{y\in(B_{\tilde{r}}(x^{*}))^{c})}\mathcal{E}(y)-\mathcal{E}_{r})}\leq e^{-\alpha q}.

This gives the desired result:

\displaystyle\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(q+\mathcal{E}_{r})^{\nu}}{\eta}+\frac{e^{-\alpha(\inf_{y\in(B_{\tilde{r}}(x^{*}))^{c})}\mathcal{E}(y)-\mathcal{E}_{r})}}{\int_{x\in B_{r}(x^{*})}\rho(x)\mathrm{d}x}\int_{(B_{\tilde{r}}(x^{*}))^{c}}d(y,x^{*})\rho(y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(q+\mathcal{E}_{r})^{\nu}}{\eta}+\frac{e^{-\alpha(\inf_{y\in(B_{\tilde{r}}(x^{*}))^{c})}\mathcal{E}(y)-\mathcal{E}_{r})}}{\int_{x\in B_{r}(x^{*})}\rho(x)\mathrm{d}x}\int_{B_{R}(o)}d(y,x^{*})\rho(y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(q+\mathcal{E}_{r})^{\nu}}{\eta}+\frac{e^{-\alpha q}}{\int_{x\in B_{r}(x^{*})}\rho(x)\mathrm{d}x}\int_{B_{R}(o)}d(y,x^{*})\rho(y)\mathrm{d}y.

∎

Proposition
4.1
shows that the drift error can be made small in the large-
\alpha
regime. However, it also has a possible obstruction: if the mass of
\rho_{t}
near
x^{*}
is small, then the smallness of the drift error is not guaranteed. Thus, we need to show that the dynamics does not instantaneously lose the initial mass around
x^{*}
. This is the purpose of the next subsection.

4.3.
Persistence of local mass near the global minimizer

Next, we show that the probability mass
\int_{x\in B_{r}(x^{*})}\rho(x)\mathrm{d}x
has a positive lower bound for an arbitrarily small
r>0
. For this, we introduce a smooth mollifier
\phi_{r}:M\to[0,1]
whose support is exactly
\overline{B_{r}(x^{*})}
:

(4.1)

\phi_{r}(x):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle e^{f(d(x,x^{*}))},\quad d(x,x^{*})<r,\\
0,\quad\textup{otherwise}\end{cases},\quad f(s):=1-\frac{r^{2}}{r^{2}-s^{2}}.

Then, we have

\int_{x\in B_{r}(x^{*})}\rho(x)\mathrm{d}x\geq\int_{M}\phi_{r}(x)\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

In order to obtain the desired positive lower bound, we estimate the right-hand side term. Before we move on, we need a lower estimate on the Laplacian of
\phi_{r}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\phi_{r}
and
f
be defined in (
4.1
). Then, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{M}\phi_{r}(x)\geq\phi_{r}(x)\left(\frac{2r^{2}(3d(x,x^{*})^{4}-r^{4})}{(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{4}}-\frac{2(d-1)r^{2}\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*})}{(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}}\coth\left(\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*})\right)\right).

Proof.

By a straightforward calculation, we see

f^{\prime}(s)=-\frac{2r^{2}s}{(r^{2}-s^{2})^{2}},\quad f^{\prime\prime}(s)=-\frac{2r^{2}(r^{2}+3s^{2})}{(r^{2}-s^{2})^{3}}.

For later use, we calculate
\nabla_{x}\phi_{r}(x)
:

\nabla_{x}\phi_{r}(x)=\frac{2r^{2}\log_{x}x^{*}}{(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}}\phi_{r}(x).

Let
g
be a function of the distance
d(x,x^{*})
. We recall the chain rule in (
3.1
)

\Delta_{M}g(d(x,x^{*}))=g^{\prime\prime}(d(x,x^{*}))+g^{\prime}(d(x,x^{*}))\Delta_{M}d(x,x^{*})

and the Laplace comparison theorem for
\Delta_{M}d(x,x^{*})
in Lemma
2.1
:

\Delta_{M}d(x,x^{*})\leq(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}\coth(\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*})).

If
g^{\prime}\leq 0
, then we obtain the lower estimate of the Laplacian for
g(d(x,x^{*}))
:

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime}(d(x,x^{*}))+(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}g^{\prime}(d(x,x^{*}))\coth\left(\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*})\right)\leq\Delta_{M}g(d(x,x^{*}))

If we substitute
g(d(x,x^{*}))=\phi_{r}(x)=e^{f(d(x,x^{*}))}
, we have

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(d(x,x^{*}))=f^{\prime}(d(x,x^{*}))e^{f(d(x,x^{*}))},

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime}(d(x,x^{*}))=\left(f^{\prime}(d(x,x^{*}))^{2}+f^{\prime\prime}(d(x,x^{*}))\right)e^{f(d(x,x^{*}))}.

Finally, we use

f^{\prime}(s)^{2}+f^{\prime\prime}(s)=\frac{2r^{2}(3s^{4}-r^{4})}{(r^{2}-s^{2})^{4}}

to obtain the desired result:

\displaystyle\Delta_{M}\phi_{r}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\phi_{r}(x)\left(f^{\prime}(d(x,x^{*}))^{2}+f^{\prime\prime}(d(x,x^{*}))+(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}f^{\prime}(d(x,x^{*}))\coth\left(\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\phi_{r}(x)\left(\frac{2r^{2}(3d(x,x^{*})^{4}-r^{4})}{(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{4}}-\frac{2(d-1)r^{2}\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*})}{(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}}\coth\left(\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*})\right)\right).

∎

Proposition 4.2
.

For all
t\in[0,T]
and
r\in(0,R_{0})
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{x\in B_{r}(x^{*})}\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x\geq\left(\int_{x_{\in}B_{r}(x^{*})}\rho_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x\right)e^{-pt}

for some
p>0
given by

p:=\max\left\{p_{1},p_{2},\frac{2\lambda\beta}{m_{r}}\right\}.

Here,
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are defined in (
4.4
),
\beta
is introduced in (
4.3
) and satisfies (
4.7
), and
m_{r}
is defined in (
4.8
) for any
B>0
with

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{x\in B_{R}(o)}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|\leq B

and for any
c\in(0,1)
satisfying

1+\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}r\leq\frac{3c^{4}-1}{2(d-1)(1-c)^{2}}.

Proof.

We recall

\int_{x\in B_{r}(x^{*})}\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x\geq\int_{M}\phi_{r}(x)\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

We use the formulation of a weak solution in Definition
2.1
to find

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{M}\phi_{r}(x)\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{M}\phi_{r}(x)\partial_{t}\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{M}\phi_{r}(x)\left(-\lambda\textup{div}_{M}(u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\rho_{t}(x))+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{M}(h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x,o))^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}\rho_{t}(x))\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{M}\underbrace{\lambda\langle\nabla\phi_{r}(x),u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\rangle}_{=:T_{1}(x)}\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{M}\underbrace{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{M}\phi_{r}(x)h_{R-\delta,R}\left(d(x,o)\right)^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}}_{=:T_{2}(x)}\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{M}(T_{1}(x)+T_{2}(x))\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

Our goal is to show that there exists
p>0
such that

(4.2)

T_{1}(x)+T_{2}(x)\geq-p\phi_{r}(x),\quad x\in M.

Since the mollifier
\phi_{r}(x)
vanishes outside the ball
\Omega_{r}:=\{x\in M:d(x,x^{*})<r\}
, we restrict ourselves to
\Omega_{r}
. From (A3)(i), we know that
B_{R_{0}}(x^{*})\subseteq B_{R}(o)
. If
r\in(0,R_{0})
, then

d(x,x^{*})<r<R_{0}~~\Longrightarrow~~x\in B_{R}(o)~~\Longrightarrow~~d(x,o)<R.

We introduce two subsets of
\Omega_{r}
:

\displaystyle K_{1}:=\{x\in M:d(x,x^{*})>\sqrt{c}r\}

and

(4.3)

K_{2}:=\{x\in M:-\lambda\langle\log_{x}x^{*},u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\rangle(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}>\frac{r^{2}}{\beta}h_{R-\delta,R}^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}d(x,x^{*})^{2}\}

where
\beta
will be determined later in (
4.7
). Then, we decompose
\Omega_{r}
into three subsets

\Omega_{r}=(K_{1}^{c}\cap\Omega_{r})\cup(K_{1}\cap K_{2}^{c}\cap\Omega_{r})\cup(K_{1}\cap K_{2}\cap\Omega_{r}).

Below, we provide the desired estimate (
4.2
) on each subset.

\bullet
Case (i) (Subset
K_{1}^{c}\cap\Omega_{r})
: In this subset, we have

d(x,x^{*})<\sqrt{c}r,\quad x\in K_{1}^{c}.

For
T_{1}(x)
, we have

\displaystyle T_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda\langle\nabla\phi_{r}(x),u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\rangle=\lambda\left\langle\frac{2r^{2}\log_{x}x^{*}}{(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}}\phi_{r}(x),u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq-2r^{2}\lambda\frac{d(x,x^{*})\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|}{(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}}\phi_{r}(x)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{2\lambda\sqrt{c}(B+\sqrt{c}r)}{(1-c)^{2}r}\phi_{r}(x)=:-p_{1}\phi_{r}(x)

where we used

\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|\leq\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|+d(x,x^{*})\leq B+\sqrt{c}r

and

\frac{1}{r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2}}<\frac{1}{(1-c)r^{2}}.

On the other hand for
T_{2}(x)
, we use the lower estimate of
\Delta_{M}\phi_{r}(x)
in Lemma
4.2
and the upper estimate
h\leq 1
to find

\displaystyle T_{2}(x)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{M}\phi_{r}(x)h_{R-\delta,R}\left(d(x,o)\right)^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{2r^{2}(3d(x,x*)^{4}-r^{4})}{(r^{2}-d(x,x*)^{2})^{4}}-\frac{2(d-1)r^{2}\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x*)}{(r^{2}-d(x,x*)^{2})^{2}}\coth(\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x*))\right)\phi_{r}(x)\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{2r^{6}}{(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{4}}+\frac{2r^{2}(d-1)}{(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}}(1+\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*})\right)\phi_{r}(x)\cdot\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{2r^{6}}{(1-c)^{4}r^{4}}+\frac{2r^{2}(d-1)}{(1-c)^{2}r^{2}}(1+\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}\sqrt{c}r)\right)(B+\sqrt{c}r)^{2}\phi_{r}(x)

\displaystyle=:-p_{2}\phi_{r}(x).

Hence in
K_{1}^{c}\cap\Omega_{r}
, we have

T_{1}(x)+T_{2}(x)\geq-\max\{p_{1},p_{2}\}\phi_{r}(x)

where
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are defined as

(4.4)

p_{1}=\frac{2\lambda\sqrt{c}(B+\sqrt{c}r)}{(1-c)^{2}r},\quad p_{2}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{2r^{6}}{(1-c)^{4}r^{4}}+\frac{2r^{2}(d-1)}{(1-c)^{2}r^{2}}(1+\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}\sqrt{c}r)\right)(B+\sqrt{c}r)^{2}.

\bullet
Case (ii) (Subset
K_{1}\cap K_{2}^{c}\cap\Omega_{r}
): In this subset, we have

d(x,x^{*})>\sqrt{c}r

and

\lambda\langle\log_{x}x^{*},u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\rangle(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}<\frac{r^{2}}{\beta}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}d(x,x^{*})^{2}.

Our goal is now to show that

T_{1}(x)+T_{2}(x)\geq 0,\quad x\in K_{1}\cap K_{2}^{c}\cap\Omega_{r}.

We observe

\displaystyle\frac{T_{1}(x)+T_{2}(x)}{2r^{2}\phi_{r}(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\langle\log_{x}x^{*},u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\rangle}{(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{M}\phi_{r}(x)h_{R-\delta,R}\left(d(x,o)\right)^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2r^{2}\phi_{r}(x)}.

It follows from the lower estimate of
\Delta_{M}\phi_{r}(x)
that

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{M}\phi_{r}(x)}{2r^{2}\phi_{r}(x)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3d(x,x^{*})^{4}-r^{4}}{(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{4}}-\frac{d-1}{(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}}(1+\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*}))

\displaystyle=\frac{(3d(x,x^{*})^{4}-r^{4})-(d-1)(1+\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*}))(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}}{((r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{4}}.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\frac{T_{1}(x)+T_{2}(x)}{2r^{2}\phi_{r}(x)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\lambda\langle\log_{x}x^{*},u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\rangle(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}}{(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{4}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}h_{R-\delta,R}\left(d(x,o)\right)^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\frac{(3d(x,x^{*})^{4}-r^{4})-(d-1)(1+\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*})(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}}{((r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{4}}.

In order to verify
T_{1}(x)+T_{2}(x)\geq 0
, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\left(\lambda\langle\log_{x}x^{*},u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\rangle-\frac{\sigma^{2}(d-1)}{2}h_{R-\delta,R}^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}(1+\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*}))\right)(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}h_{R-\delta,R}\left(d(x,o)\right)^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}(3d(x,x^{*})^{4}-r^{4})\geq 0

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\left(-\lambda\langle\log_{x}x^{*},u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\rangle+\frac{\sigma^{2}(d-1)}{2}h_{R-\delta,R}^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}(1+\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*}))\right)(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}h_{R-\delta,R}\left(d(x,o)\right)^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}(3d(x,x^{*})^{4}-r^{4}).

Our goal is to verify that

(4.5)

-\lambda\langle\log_{x}x^{*},u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\rangle(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}h_{R-\delta,R}^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}(3d(x,x^{*})^{4}-r^{4})

and

(4.6)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{\sigma^{2}(d-1)}{2}h_{R-\delta,R}^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}(1+\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*}))(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}\\
&\quad\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}h_{R-\delta,R}^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}(3d(x,x^{*})^{4}-r^{4}).\end{aligned}

By adding these two inequalities (
4.5
) and (
4.6
), we conclude that
T_{1}(x)+T_{2}(x)\geq 0
. For the first inequality (
4.5
), we assume that
\beta
introduced in subset
K_{2}
satisfies

(4.7)

\frac{1}{c\beta}<\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\left(3-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\right).

Then, we have

\displaystyle-\lambda\langle\log_{x}x^{*},u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\rangle(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle<\frac{r^{2}}{\beta}h_{R-\delta,R}^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}d(x,x^{*})^{2}

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{c\beta}h_{R-\delta,R}^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}d(x,x^{*})^{4}

\displaystyle<\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\left(3-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\right)h_{R-\delta,R}^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}d(x,x^{*})^{4}

\displaystyle<\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}h_{R-\delta,R}^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}(3d(x,x^{*})^{4}-r^{4}).

Next, for the second inequality, it is equivalent to

(d-1)(1+\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*}))(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}(3d(x,x^{*})^{4}-r^{4}).

Define an auxiliary polynomial

g(s):=3s^{4}-r^{4}-2(d-1)(1+\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}s)(r^{2}-s^{2})^{2}.

Then, our goal is to show that
g(s)\geq 0
. Since
\sqrt{c}r\leq d(x,x^{*})=s\leq r
, we have

\displaystyle g(s)

\displaystyle\geq 3c^{4}r^{4}-r^{4}-2(d-1)(1+\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}r)(1-c)^{2}r^{4}

\displaystyle=(3c^{4}-1-2(d-1)(1+\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}r))r^{4}\geq 0

whenever

1+\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}r\leq\frac{3c^{4}-1}{2(d-1)(1-c)^{2}}.

Hence, for any
r>0
, we choose
c
sufficiently close to 1 so that the above relation is satisfied.

\bullet
Case (iii) (Subset
K_{1}\cap K_{2}\cap\Omega_{r}
): In this subset, we have

d(x,x^{*})>\sqrt{c}r

and

-\lambda\langle\log_{x}x^{*},u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\rangle(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}>\frac{r^{2}}{\beta}h_{R-\delta,R}^{2}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|^{2}d(x,x^{*})^{2}.

For
T_{1}(x)
, we recall the definition of
K_{2}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\log_{x}x^{*},u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\rangle}{(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{\|\log_{x}x^{*}\|\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|}{(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}}\geq\frac{\lambda\beta}{r^{2}}\frac{\langle\log_{x}x^{*},u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\rangle}{\|\log_{x}x^{*}\|\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\|h_{R-\delta,R}^{2}}\geq-\frac{\lambda\beta}{r^{2}h_{R-\delta,R}^{2}}

Define

D_{r}:=\sup_{x\in B_{r}(x^{*})}d(x,o).

Then, we claim that

D_{r}<R.

Since
r<R_{0}
, we have
\overline{B_{r}(x^{*})}\subseteq B_{R_{0}}(x^{*})\subseteq B_{R}(o)
. Since
x\mapsto d(x,o)
is continuous, it attains the maximum on
\overline{B_{r}(x^{*})}
. Thus, there exists
x_{r_{0}}\in\overline{B_{r}(x^{*})}
such that

d(x_{r_{0}},o)=\max_{x\in\overline{B_{r}(x^{*})}}d(x,o).

In addition, since
d(x_{r_{0}},o)<R
, we have

D_{r}=\sup_{x\in\overline{B_{r}(x^{*})}}d(x,o)\leq\max_{x\in\overline{B_{r}(x^{*})}}d(x,o)<R.

This shows that since
d(x,o)\leq D_{r}<R
, there exists
m_{r}>0
such that

(4.8)

h_{R-\delta,R}(d(x,o))^{2}\geq m_{r},\quad d(x,o)\leq D_{r}<R.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle T_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\langle\log_{x}x^{*},u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\rangle}{(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}}2r^{2}\phi_{r}(x)\geq-2\frac{\lambda\beta}{m_{r}}\phi_{r}(x).

Next, for
T_{2}(x)
, to see that
T_{2}(x)\geq 0
, it suffices to show that

(3d(x,x^{*})^{4}-r^{4})-(d-1)(1+\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*}))(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}\geq 0.

In fact, in Case (ii), we show that

\displaystyle(3d(x,x^{*})^{4}-r^{4})

\displaystyle\geq 2(d-1)(1+\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*}))(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq(d-1)(1+\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}d(x,x^{*}))(r^{2}-d(x,x^{*})^{2})^{2}.

Thus, we get

T_{2}(x)\geq 0.

Hence, we have

T_{1}(x)+T_{2}(x)\geq-\max\left\{p_{1},p_{2},\frac{2\lambda\beta}{m_{r}}\right\}\phi_{r}(x)=-p\phi_{r}(x)

and this gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{M}\phi_{r}(x)\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{M}(T_{1}(x)+T_{2}(x))\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x\geq-p\int_{M}\phi_{r}(x)\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

Finally, Grönwall’s inequality gives the desired result.
∎

By combining Propositions
4.1
and
4.2
, we obtain the key estimates behind global convergence. Precisely, Proposition
4.2
gives a positive lower bound on
\rho_{t}(B_{r}(x^{*}))
on any finite time interval, whereas Proposition
4.1
shows that once this lower bound is available, the drift error can be arbitrarily small by choosing
\alpha
sufficiently large.

4.4.
Proof of the global convergence toward a minimizer

We now state the global convergence result in the mean-field regime. The theorem states that if the attraction strength is sufficiently large compared to the diffusion-curvature contribution and
\alpha
is chosen large enough, then the variance functional
\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]
decays exponentially until the law reaches an arbitrarily small neighborhood of the global minimizer.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\rho\in C\bigl([0,T],\mathcal{P}_{2}(M)\bigr)
be a weak solution to equation (
1.8
) on any time interval
[0,T]
with
T>0
. In addition, the initial datum satisfies

\int_{x\in B_{r}(x^{*})}\rho_{0}(x)dx>0,\quad\forall~r\in(0,R_{0}).

Then, for every
\varepsilon\in(0,\mathcal{V}[\rho_{0}])
, there is
\alpha_{0}>0
such that, for each
\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}
, one can find a time

T_{*}\leq T_{\varepsilon}:=-\frac{2}{C_{20}}\log\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathcal{V}[\rho_{0}]}\right),\quad\text{where}\quad C_{20}:=2\lambda-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\bigl(d+2(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}\,R\bigr)>0,

satisfying

\mathcal{V}[\rho_{T_{*}}]\leq\varepsilon,

and such that for all
t\in[0,T_{*}]
,

\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]\leq e^{-\frac{C_{20}}{2}t}\,\mathcal{V}[\rho_{0}].

Proof.

We begin by recalling the differential inequality from Lemma
4.1
:

(4.9)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]\leq-C_{20}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]+C_{21}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]^{1/2}\sup_{x\in M}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|+C_{22}\sup_{x\in M}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|^{2},

where the constants are defined as

\displaystyle C_{21}:=\lambda+\sigma^{2}\bigl(d+2(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}\,R\bigr),\quad C_{22}:=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\bigl(d+2(d-1)\sqrt{\kappa_{-}}\,R\bigr).

For any
\varepsilon>0
, define the stopping times

T^{\alpha}_{1}:=\inf\bigl\{t>0\mid\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]\leq\varepsilon\bigr\}\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\},

T^{\alpha}_{2}:=\inf\Bigl\{t>0\,\Big|\,\sup_{x\in M}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|\geq\mathcal{V}[\rho_{0}]^{1/2}+3\Bigr\}\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\},

and set
T_{\alpha}:=\min\{T^{\alpha}_{1},T^{\alpha}_{2},T_{\varepsilon}\}
.

We will show that for sufficiently large
\alpha
, the following two estimates hold for all
t\in[0,T_{\alpha})
:

(4.10)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in B_{R}(o)}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|

\displaystyle\leq\min\!\left\{\frac{C_{20}}{4C_{21}},\,\sqrt{\frac{C_{20}}{4C_{22}}}\right\}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]^{1/2}=:C_{*}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]^{1/2},

(4.11)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in B_{R}(o)}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]^{1/2}+1.

We first assume a priori that (
4.10
) and (
4.11
) hold. Assuming (
4.10
), we substitute it into (
4.9
) to obtain, for all
t\in[0,T_{\alpha}]
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]

\displaystyle\leq-C_{20}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]+C_{21}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]^{1/2}\cdot C_{*}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]^{1/2}+C_{22}\bigl(C_{*}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]^{1/2}\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=-C_{20}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]+C_{21}C_{*}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]+C_{22}C_{*}^{2}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]

\displaystyle\leq-C_{20}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]+\frac{C_{20}}{4}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]+\frac{C_{20}}{4}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]=-\frac{C_{20}}{2}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}],

where the last inequality follows from the definition of
C_{*}
. Grönwall’s inequality then yields

\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]\leq e^{-\frac{C_{20}}{2}t}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{0}].

In particular,
\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]
is strictly decreasing on
[0,T_{\alpha}]
. Consequently, by (
4.11
) and the continuity of
\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]
,

\sup_{x\in B_{R}(o)}\|u_{\alpha}^{T_{\alpha}}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|\leq\mathcal{V}[\rho_{T_{\alpha}}]^{1/2}+1\leq\mathcal{V}[\rho_{0}]^{1/2}+1.

By continuity of
\sup_{x\in M}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|
, this implies
T^{\alpha}_{2}>T_{\alpha}
. Hence,

T_{\alpha}=\min\{T^{\alpha}_{1},T_{\varepsilon}\},

and therefore
\mathcal{V}[\rho_{T_{\alpha}}]\leq\varepsilon
, which completes the main part of the proof.

It remains to establish the claims (
4.10
) and (
4.11
). By the definition of
T^{\alpha}_{2}
, the quantity
\sup_{x\in M}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|
is uniformly bounded for
t\in[0,T_{\alpha})
. Thus, we may apply Proposition
4.2
with some constant
p>0
independent of
\alpha
to obtain, for all
t\in[0,T_{\alpha})
,

\rho_{t}\bigl(B_{r}(x^{*})\bigr)\geq\rho_{0}\bigl(B_{r}(x^{*})\bigr)e^{-pt}\geq\rho_{0}\bigl(B_{r}(x^{*})\bigr)e^{-pT_{\varepsilon}}=:C_{0}>0.

We now choose parameters
q,r,\alpha>0
such that

\underbrace{\frac{(q+\mathcal{E}_{r})^{\nu}}{\eta}}_{\text{for }q,r\text{ small}}\leq\frac{C_{*}}{2}\varepsilon^{1/2},\quad\underbrace{\frac{e^{-\alpha q}}{C_{0}}\sqrt{2}}_{\text{for }\alpha\text{ large}}\leq\frac{C_{*}}{2}.

With these choices and by Proposition
4.1
, we have for all
t\in[0,T_{\alpha})
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in B_{R}(o)}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{(q+\mathcal{E}_{r})^{\nu}}{\eta}+\frac{e^{-\alpha q}}{\int_{B_{r}(x^{*})}\rho_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}\sqrt{2}\,\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{C_{*}}{2}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]^{1/2}+\frac{e^{-\alpha q}}{C_{0}}\sqrt{2}\,\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]^{1/2}\leq C_{*}\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]^{1/2},

which shows (
4.10
).

To verify (
4.11
), we repeat the same argument with slightly different parameter constraints. Specifically, choose
q,r,\alpha>0
such that

\frac{(q+\mathcal{E}_{r})^{\nu}}{\eta}\leq 1,\quad\frac{e^{-\alpha q}}{C_{0}}\sqrt{2}\leq 1.

Then, we find

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in B_{R}(o)}\|u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)-\log_{x}x^{*}\|\leq 1+1\cdot\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]^{1/2}\leq\mathcal{V}[\rho_{t}]^{1/2}+1,

as required.
∎

5.
Numerical Experiments

In this section, we present a series of numerical experiments to illustrate the practical performance and theoretical properties of the proposed intrinsic CBO algorithm. We aim to demonstrate the global convergence of the particle system (
1.5
) on various Riemannian manifolds, including those with non-positive sectional curvature (where our global geometric guarantees apply) and those with positive curvature (where the geometric cutoffs enforce local regularity).

To simulate the continuous-time interacting stochastic particle system (

where
\rho^{N,k}

, and

is a random tangent vector whose components are drawn independently from a standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
with respect to a local orthonormal frame. The complete procedure for our intrinsic CBO method, incorporating the stable Log-Sum-Exp trick and localized geometric cutoffs, is summarized in Algorithm

. In the following, the CBO hyperparameters are set to
\alpha=10^{9}
,

,

5.1.
Global Optimization on the Sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}

For our first numerical experiment, we consider the unit sphere
M=\mathbb{S}^{2}
embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. This manifold has a constant positive sectional curvature
\kappa_{+}=1
and an injectivity radius of
\mathrm{inj}(\mathbb{S}^{2})=\pi
.

Theoretical vs. Numerical Domain.

As established in our theoretical analysis, the well-posedness and global convergence guarantees require the geometric cutoffs to satisfy
R+\delta<\min\left(\frac{\mathrm{inj}(M)}{2},\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}}\right)
which ensures the Lipschitz continuity of the logarithmic map. However, in practical numerical optimization, restricting the particle swarm to a local hemisphere is often unnecessary and limits exploration. Therefore, to demonstrate the global robustness of the algorithm, we relax the theoretical cutoffs for this experiment and allow the swarm to explore the entire manifold (effectively setting
h\equiv 1
). To safely handle the cut-locus singularity that occurs when two particles are perfectly antipodal (
d(x,y)=\pi
), we introduce a standard numerical tolerance in the logarithmic map, regularizing the consensus drift to zero if particles cross this exact threshold.

Geometric Primitives.

The intrinsic distance between two points
x,y\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
is given by
d(x,y)=\arccos(\langle x,y\rangle)
. The Riemannian logarithmic map and exponential map, which are used to compute the consensus point and update particle positions, are given respectively by:

\displaystyle\log_{x}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{d(x,y)}{\sqrt{1-\langle x,y\rangle^{2}}}\big(y-\langle x,y\rangle x\big)\in T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2},

\displaystyle\exp_{x}(v)

\displaystyle=x\cos(\|v\|)+\frac{v}{\|v\|}\sin(\|v\|)\in\mathbb{S}^{2},\quad\text{for }v\in T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2}.

The Objective Function.

We adapt the classic Ackley function—a highly nonconvex benchmark characterized by a nearly flat outer region and numerous local minima—to the sphere. We define the spherical Ackley function
\mathcal{E}:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
using the ambient coordinates
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{\top}
:

\mathcal{E}(x)=-20\exp\left(-0.2\sqrt{\frac{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}{2}}\right)-\exp\left(\frac{\cos(c\pi x_{1})+\cos(c\pi x_{2})}{2}\right)+20+\exp(1),

where
c=10
controls the frequency of the local minima and
\exp(1)
is added to normalize so that its minimum value becomes zero. Note that the spherical Ackley function defined above is perfectly symmetric across the origin. Consequently, it possesses two identical global minima located at the antipodal points
x^{o}=(0,0,\pm 1)^{\top}
, with a minimum value of
\mathcal{E}(x^{o})=0
. To verify that the intrinsic CBO algorithm relies purely on coordinate-free geometric primitives, we construct an offset spherical Ackley function
\mathcal{E}_{x^{*}}(x)=\mathcal{E}(R_{x^{*}}^{\top}x)
. Here,
R_{x^{*}}
is a rotation matrix that maps
x^{o}
to an arbitrary point
x^{*}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
. This transformation effectively shifts the global minimizer to
x^{*}
while perfectly preserving the highly nonconvex, corrugated topography of the energy landscape.

Experimental Setup and Results.

We define the arbitrary global minimizers at
x^{*}=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1,1,1)^{\top}
and initialize
N=80
particles uniformly at random across the entire sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, representing a state of maximum entropy without geometric confinement.

Despite the presence of numerous local traps and the relaxation of the geometric confinement constraints, the state-dependent diffusion allows the particle swarm to effectively explore the manifold. As the iterations progress, the swarm smoothly collapses toward the target. The algorithm ultimately reaches strict consensus, identifying the global minimizer
x^{*}
and empirically validating the robustness of the intrinsic dynamics on unbounded or positively curved manifolds.

5.2.
Global Optimization on Hyperbolic Space
\mathbb{H}^{2}

To demonstrate the performance of the intrinsic CBO algorithm on manifolds with negative sectional curvature, we consider the two-dimensional hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. This provides a crucial theoretical contrast to the sphere: because
\mathbb{H}^{2}
possesses constant negative curvature (
\kappa\equiv-1
) and an infinite injectivity radius, the manifold has no cut locus. Consequently, the logarithmic map is globally Lipschitz continuous. This guarantees that our theoretical mean-field limit and global convergence bounds hold across the
entire

h
.

Geometric Primitives.

We employ the standard hyperboloid (or Lorentz) model, embedding
\mathbb{H}^{2}
into the Minkowski space
\mathbb{R}^{2+1}
. Points
x\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
are defined as
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\mid\langle x,x\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}=-1,x_{3}>0\}
, where the Lorentz inner product is given by
\langle x,y\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}:=x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}-x_{3}y_{3}
.

The intrinsic geodesic distance between any two points is computed as
d(x,y)=\operatorname{arccosh}(-\langle x,y\rangle_{\mathcal{L}})
. The Riemannian logarithmic and exponential maps are globally smooth and given by:

\displaystyle\log_{x}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{d(x,y)}{\sinh(d(x,y))}\big(y+\langle x,y\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}x\big)\in T_{x}\mathbb{H}^{2},

\displaystyle\exp_{x}(v)

\displaystyle=x\cosh(\|v\|_{\mathcal{L}})+\frac{v}{\|v\|_{\mathcal{L}}}\sinh(\|v\|_{\mathcal{L}})\in\mathbb{H}^{2},\quad\text{for }v\in T_{x}\mathbb{H}^{2},

where the norm on the tangent space is induced by the Lorentz metric,
\|v\|_{\mathcal{L}}=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}}
.

The Objective Function.

To test the algorithm’s ability to escape local minima in an exponentially expanding volume, we define a highly nonconvex, coordinate-free Radial Ackley function on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
:

\mathcal{E}_{x^{*}}(x)=-20\exp\left(-0.2\,d(x,x^{*})\right)-\exp\left(\cos(c\pi\,d(x,x^{*}))\right)+20+\exp(1),

where
c=10
controls the frequency of the local minima. This creates an unbounded funnel containing infinitely many concentric rings of local minima surrounding an arbitrary global minimizer
x^{*}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
.

We define the global minimizer at
x^{*}=(2,2,3)^{\top}

5.3.
Global Optimization on the special orthogonal group
SO(3)

To demonstrate the flexibility of the intrinsic CBO algorithm on compact Lie groups, we evaluate its performance on the special orthogonal group
SO(3)
, the manifold of
3\times 3
rotation matrices. Optimization on
SO(3)
is central to numerous applications in robotics, computer vision, and aerospace engineering. Like the sphere,
SO(3)
is a compact manifold with positive curvature and possesses a cut locus (occurring at rotations of exactly
\pi
radians). Consequently, our theoretical bounds requiring geometric cutoffs formally apply. However, consistent with our findings on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, we relax these cutoffs in practice and allow the swarm to explore the entire group, regularizing the logarithmic map at the exact cut locus to ensure numerical stability.

Geometric Primitives.

The manifold is defined as
SO(3)=\{R\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\mid R^{\top}R=I,\det(R)=1\}
. The tangent space at a rotation
R
is given by
T_{R}SO(3)=\{R\Omega\mid\Omega\in\mathfrak{so}(3)\}
, where
\mathfrak{so}(3)
is the Lie algebra of
3\times 3
skew-symmetric matrices. We equip
SO(3)
with the standard bi-invariant Riemannian metric, defined via the Frobenius inner product
\langle X,Y\rangle_{R}=\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}(X^{\top}Y)
.

The intrinsic geodesic distance between two rotation matrices
R_{1},R_{2}\in SO(3)
is the absolute angle of rotation required to align them:
d(R_{1},R_{2})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|\log(R_{1}^{\top}R_{2})\|_{F}
, where
\log
denotes the principal matrix logarithm. The Riemannian logarithmic and exponential maps are elegantly defined via their matrix counterparts:

\displaystyle\log_{R_{1}}(R_{2})

\displaystyle=R_{1}\log(R_{1}^{\top}R_{2})\in T_{R_{1}}SO(3),

\displaystyle\exp_{R}(V)

\displaystyle=R\exp(R^{\top}V)\in SO(3),\quad\text{for }V\in T_{R}SO(3).

The Objective Function.

To maintain a consistent benchmark across all manifold topologies, we define a highly nonconvex, coordinate-free Ackley function on
SO(3)
. Given an arbitrary target rotation matrix
R^{*}\in SO(3)
, the energy of a candidate rotation
R
is formulated entirely in terms of the geodesic distance
\theta=d(R,R^{*})\in[0,\pi]
:

\mathcal{E}_{R^{*}}(R)=-20\exp(-0.2\theta)-\exp(\cos(c\pi\theta))+20+\exp(1),

where
c=10
. This generates a multimodal energy landscape defined over the group of rotations, featuring deep concentric traps in the rotation angle space.

Experimental Setup and Results.

We define an arbitrary global minimizer
R^{*}
and initialize
N=150
particles drawn uniformly at random from the Haar measure on
SO(3)
. This unbiased initialization ensures a state of maximum orientational entropy, completely covering the manifold.

\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(M)
is the set of 1-Lipschitz functions on
M
, i.e.
\phi\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(M)
if and only if

|\phi(x_{1})-\phi(x_{2})|\leq d(x_{1},x_{2}),\quad\forall~x_{1},x_{2}\in M.

Hence, we find

\left|\int_{M}w_{\alpha}(y)(\rho_{2}(y)-\rho_{1}(y))\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq\mathrm{Lip}(w_{\alpha})W_{1}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2}).

Here,
w_{\alpha}
is Lipschitz since the objective function
\mathcal{E}
is Lipschitz. Finally, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{I}_{72}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(R+\delta)}{\int_{M}w_{\alpha}(y)\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}(y)}\mathrm{Lip}(w_{\alpha})W_{1}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\leq\left(\frac{2(R+\delta)\mathrm{Lip}(w_{\alpha})}{\inf w_{\alpha}}\right)W_{1}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2}).

\bullet
(Estimate of
\mathcal{I}_{71}
): To estimate
\mathcal{I}_{71}
, we consider a transport plan
\pi
whose marginals are
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R+\delta}(o)}h_{R,R+\delta}(d(y,o))w_{\alpha}(y)\log_{x}y(\rho_{1}(y)-\rho_{2}(y))\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R+\delta}(o)\times B_{R+\delta}(o)}\left(h_{R,R+\delta}(d(y_{1},o))w_{\alpha}(y_{1})\log_{x}y_{1}-h_{R,R+\delta}(d(y_{2},o))w_{\alpha}(y_{2})\log_{x}y_{2}\right)\mathrm{d}\pi(y_{1},y_{2})

The integrand can be decomposed into

\displaystyle h_{R,R+\delta}(d(y_{1},o))w_{\alpha}(y_{1})\log_{x}y_{1}-h_{R,R+\delta}(d(y_{2},o))w_{\alpha}(y_{2})\log_{x}y_{2}

\displaystyle\quad=\bigg(h_{R,R+\delta}(d(y_{1},o))-h_{R,R+\delta}(d(y_{2},o))\bigg)w_{\alpha}(y_{1})\log_{x}y_{1}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+h_{R,R+\delta}(d(y_{2},o))\bigg(w_{\alpha}(y_{1})-w_{\alpha}(y_{2})\bigg)\log_{x}y_{1}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+h_{R,R+\delta}(d(y_{2},o))w_{\alpha}(y_{2})\bigg(\log_{x}y_{1}-\log_{x}y_{2}\bigg).

Then, we estimate the whole integrand as

\displaystyle\|h_{R,R+\delta}(d(y_{1},o))w_{\alpha}(y_{1})\log_{x}y_{1}-h_{R,R+\delta}(d(y_{2},o))w_{\alpha}(y_{2})\log_{x}y_{2}\|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathrm{Lip}(h_{R,R+\delta})d(y_{1},y_{2})(\sup w_{\alpha})d(x,y_{1})+\mathrm{Lip}(w_{\alpha})d(y_{1},y_{2})d(x,y_{1})+(\sup w_{\alpha})d(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle\quad=\bigg(\mathrm{Lip}(h_{R,R+\delta})(\sup w_{\alpha})d(x,y_{1})+\mathrm{Lip}(w_{\alpha})d(x,y_{1})+(\sup w_{\alpha})\bigg)d(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle\quad\leq\bigg(\sup w_{\alpha}\big(2(R+\delta)\mathrm{Lip}(h_{R,R+\delta})+1\big)+2(R+\delta)\mathrm{Lip}(w_{\alpha})\bigg)d(y_{1},y_{2}).

Hence, we can estimate
\mathcal{I}_{71}
as follows:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{I}_{71}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\int_{M}w_{\alpha}(y)\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}(y)}\left\|\int_{B_{R+\delta}(o)}h_{R,R+\delta}(d(y,o))w_{\alpha}(y)\log_{x}y(\rho_{1}(y)-\rho_{2}(y))\mathrm{d}y\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\inf w_{\alpha}}\bigg(\sup w_{\alpha}\big(2(R+\delta)\mathrm{Lip}(h_{R,R+\delta})+1\big)+2(R+\delta)\mathrm{Lip}(w_{\alpha})\bigg)

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pt\times\int_{B_{R+\delta}(o)\times B_{R+\delta}(o)}d(y_{1},y_{2})\mathrm{d}\pi(y_{1},y_{2}).

Since the above inequality holds for an arbitrary transport plan
\pi
, we get

\|\mathcal{I}_{71}\|\leq\frac{1}{\inf w_{\alpha}}\bigg(\sup w_{\alpha}\big(2(R+\delta)\mathrm{Lip}(h_{R,R+\delta})+1\big)+2(R+\delta)\mathrm{Lip}(w_{\alpha})\bigg)W_{1}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2}).

Finally, we obtain the desired result:

\displaystyle\|\tilde{u}_{\alpha}(\rho_{1};x)-\tilde{u}_{\alpha}(\rho_{2};x)\|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|\mathcal{I}_{71}\|+\|\mathcal{I}_{72}\|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{1}{\inf w_{\alpha}}\bigg(\sup w_{\alpha}\big(2(R+\delta)\mathrm{Lip}(h_{R,R+\delta})+1\big)+4(R+\delta)\mathrm{Lip}(w_{\alpha})\bigg)W_{1}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2}).

A.7.
Proof of Lemma

\displaystyle\|u_{\alpha}(\rho^{N,X};x_{i})-P_{x_{i}y_{i}}u_{\alpha}(\rho^{N,Y};y_{i})\|

A.8.
Proof of
\nabla_{M}\phi_{\epsilon}(d(x,o))\cdot u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\geq 0

By our choice of
\phi_{\epsilon}
, we have

\nabla_{M}\phi_{\epsilon}(d(x,o))=0

for all
x
satisfying either
d(x,o)<R+\delta
or
d(x,o)>R+\delta+\epsilon
. Thus, we assume

R+\delta\leq d(x,o)\leq R+\delta+\epsilon<\mathrm{inj}(M).

Then, we have

\nabla_{M}\phi_{\epsilon}(d(x,o))=-\left(\frac{\phi_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(d(x,o))}{d(x,o)}\right)\log_{x}o

with
\phi_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(d(x,o))\leq 0
. Since the inner product between
\nabla_{M}\phi_{\epsilon}(d(x,o))
and
u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)
can be calculated as

(A.2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\nabla_{M}\phi_{\epsilon}(d(x,o))\cdot u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\\
&=-\left(\frac{\phi_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(d(x,o))}{d(x,o)}\right)\log_{x}o\cdot u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\\
&=-\left(\frac{\phi_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(d(x,o))}{d(x,o)}\right)\frac{h_{R,R+\delta}(d(x,o))}{\int_{M}w_{\alpha}(y)\mathrm{d}\rho(y)}\int_{B_{R+\delta}(o)}h_{R,R+\delta}(d(y,o))\,w_{\alpha}(y)\big(\log_{x}o\cdot\log_{x}y\big)\mathrm{d}\rho(y).\end{aligned}

The Rauch comparison theorem in Lemma
2.2
and the spherical cosine law give

\displaystyle\cos\left(\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}d(y,o)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\cos\left(\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}d(x,o)\right)\cos\left(\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}d(x,y)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sin\left(\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}d(x,o)\right)\sin\left(\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}d(x,y)\right)\cos(\angle yxo).

By using

(A.3)

\displaystyle d(y,o)\leq R+\delta\leq d(x,o)<\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}},

we have

\displaystyle\cos\left(\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}(R+\delta)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\cos\left(\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}(R+\delta)\right)\cos\left(\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}d(x,y)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sin\left(\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}d(x,o)\right)\sin\left(\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}d(x,y)\right)\cos(\angle yxo).

Since
\cos(\sqrt{\kappa_{+}}d(x,y))\leq 1
, we get
\cos(\angle(yxo))\geq 0
. Therefore, we get
\log_{x}o\cdot\log_{x}y\geq 0
for any
x
and
y
satisfying (
A.3
). Finally, we substitute it into (
A.2
) to obtain the desired result:

\nabla_{M}\phi_{\epsilon}(d(x,o))\cdot u_{\alpha}^{t}(x)\geq 0.
