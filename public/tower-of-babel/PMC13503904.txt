Integrated slope stability assessment of earthen canals under seismic loading using pseudostatic analysis and vetiver grass reinforcement
Authors: Wahidullah Hazim, Asadullah Ziar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarthen canals in seismically active regions face combined hydraulic and earthquake loading that can compromise slope stability. This study evaluates the effectiveness of vetiver grass as a bioengineering reinforcement through Pseudostatic slope stability analysis of a critical section (IT3) of the Khush Tepa Canal using the Morgenstern–Price limit equilibrium method. Steady-state, rapid drawdown, and seismic scenarios were systematically assessed to quantify their influence on slope performance and reinforcement benefits. Results show that the canal is stable under steady-state conditions (FoS = 2.11), while seismic loading reduces stability (FoS = 1.25, a 40.8% reduction), and rapid drawdown represents the most critical scenario (FoS = 1.10). Vetiver grass reinforcement substantially improves levee stability, increasing the FoS from 1.93 to 2.37 (a 22.8% gain) under static conditions. This improvement is attributed to increased apparent cohesion from the dense root network, which constrains failure surfaces to shallower depths and prevents deep rotational sliding. These quantitative improvements demonstrate that vetiver grass provides measurable structural reinforcement. The findings support integrating nature-based solutions into earthen canal design to enhance both hydraulic and seismic resilience in earthquake-prone regions.Keywords: Pseudostatic analysis, Slope stability, Rapid drawdown, Steady state, Vetiver grassSubject terms: Engineering, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Earthen canals constitute a vital component of irrigation and water conveyance systems, particularly in developing and seismically active regions where cost effective construction methods are preferred. Despite their widespread use, earthen canals are highly susceptible to slope instability due to unfavorable soil conditions, seepage forces, fluctuating groundwater levels, and external dynamic loading. Failures of canal slopes can result in significant water losses, service interruptions, and damage to surrounding infrastructure, underscoring the importance of rigorous slope stability assessments under both static and seismic conditions1,2.

Seismic loading imposes additional inertial forces on soil masses, substantially modifying stress distributions and potentially triggering slope failures even when static stability criteria are satisfied. In practical engineering applications, pseudostatic analysis remains one of the most widely adopted methods for evaluating slope stability under earthquake loading. This approach represents seismic effects through equivalent horizontal and vertical acceleration coefficients, allowing the reduction in factor of safety to be quantified under seismic conditions3,4. Recent studies continue to support the applicability of pseudostatic methods for preliminary and comparative seismic stability evaluations of earth structures, including embankments, levees, and canal slopes, particularly in regions where advanced dynamic analyses are constrained by limited data availability.

To assess the influence of seismic loading on earth structures such as slopes and walls, researchers frequently employ pseudostatic analysis, in which inertial forces are represented by equivalent static coefficients to approximate earthquake effects in limit equilibrium frameworks5. systematically investigated slope stability under both static and pseudostatic conditions, demonstrating that dynamic forces associated with seismicity can substantially reduce safety factors compared to purely static analyses, particularly for steep slopes, and highlighting the need for reinforcement in susceptible zones during design and planning processes. Similarly, studies on seismic earth pressures show that conventional pseudostatic approaches may oversimplify soil structure interactions and underestimate dynamic loading effects; advanced formulations that account for construction-induced stress fields and seismic response characteristics reveal that inertial forces can alter pressure distributions and reduce safety margins if not properly incorporated into design6. Furthermore, analytical developments in pseudostatic thrust formulations for cantilever retaining walls illustrate how seismic coefficients modify active thrust predictions and influence failure surface geometry, offering enhanced insight into dynamic behavior and reinforcing the value of pseudostatic methods for seismic design applications in geotechnical engineering7.

In addition to seismic forces, the stability of earthen canal slopes is governed by the balance between driving and resisting forces associated with soil self-weight, shear strength parameters, and pore water pressures. Slope stability and weight equilibrium (Slope/W 2025) analyses provide a robust framework for assessing potential failure mechanisms along critical slip surfaces. Such analyses are especially critical for canals constructed in soft, silty, or low-cohesion soils and in areas with shallow groundwater tables, where the likelihood of instability is considerably higher2,8.

In recent years, nature-based and bioengineering solutions have emerged as sustainable alternatives to conventional slope stabilization techniques. Among these, vetiver grass (Chrysopogon zizanioides) has received increasing attention due to its extensive, dense, and vertically oriented root system, which can penetrate several meters into the soil. Contemporary experimental and numerical studies have demonstrated that vetiver roots significantly enhance soil shear strength, primarily through an increase in apparent cohesion and improved soil root interaction. Laboratory investigations using slope stability models have reported notable increases in factor of safety for vetiver-reinforced slopes compared with bare soil conditions9,10. Recent analytical studies have reported very large increases in soil cohesion due to vetiver root reinforcement, with observed gains of over 350% at shallow depths in experimental slope models, highlighting the strong mechanical contribution of vetiver root systems to slope stability and supporting the higher cohesion improvements considered in this study11.

Advanced numerical modeling and recent research have also examined the temporal behavior of vetiver reinforcement, including the effects of root growth and decomposition on long-term slope stability. Findings published in Computers and Geotechnics indicate that while root decay may reduce reinforcement over time, vetiver-based systems still provide substantial improvements in near-surface stability when appropriately maintained12. Moreover, comparative studies evaluating bio-engineered slopes against conventional reinforcement techniques under combined rainfall and pseudostatic seismic loading have shown that vetiver grass offers effective shallow slope reinforcement, particularly when integrated with proper hydraulic and geotechnical design considerations13.

Despite recent advances in slope stability and bioengineering applications, several limitations remain in the existing literature. Most previous studies have primarily focused on either static slope stability analysis or simplified seismic evaluations, often neglecting the combined effects of seismic loading and vegetation-based reinforcement. In addition, bioengineering approaches such as vetiver grass have typically been assessed under non-seismic conditions, with limited consideration of their mechanical contribution within rigorous limit equilibrium frameworks. Furthermore, existing studies rarely integrate pseudostatic seismic analysis with advanced numerical tools such as Slope/W 2025, and they often lack site-specific calibration, particularly for regions with complex geotechnical and seismic characteristics like Afghanistan.

To address these limitations, the present study provides a comprehensive and integrated assessment of earthen canal slope stability under both static and seismic conditions. Specifically, this research combines pseudostatic seismic loading with Slope/W 2025 limit equilibrium methods, while explicitly incorporating the reinforcing effects of vetiver grass within the stability analysis. Unlike previous approaches, this study evaluates the combined influence of seismic forces, hydraulic conditions, and bioengineering measures within a unified framework.

Moreover, this work advances the current state of knowledge by offering a site-specific investigation based on the geotechnical and seismic conditions of Afghanistan, a region where such studies are scarce. The results provide quantitative evidence of the effectiveness of vetiver grass as a sustainable and cost-effective solution for enhancing slope stability under seismic loading. Therefore, this study not only fills a critical research gap but also contributes practical insights for improving the seismic resilience and long-term performance of earthen canal infrastructure using nature-based solutions.

Materials and methods

Study area

The Khush Tepa Canal Project is a major infrastructure initiative in northern Afghanistan, designed to irrigate over 550,000 hectares of arid and semi-arid land14. The canal traverses diverse geological formations, necessitating detailed geotechnical investigations to ensure the structural integrity and stability of the canal slopes. Key indicators for the planning and sizing of the project include water availability, soil conditions, and environmental constraints. According to14,15, the Khush Tepa Canal is being executed in three phases, commencing from Kaldar (67.778832 E, 37.089639 N) and passing through the districts of Khulm, Nahri Shahi, and Dawlatabad in Balkh province. It then continues through Aqcha, Mardyan, and Khwaja Du Koh in Jawzjan province, ultimately reaching the Andkhoy district of Faryab province.

In the present study, boreholes IT3∼B10 were evaluated along the first phase of the main canal, which extends from the intake point at Kaldar (km 2.1) to Dawlatabad (km 108.0) (Fig. 1). The slope stability analysis focused on the section with the highest risk (Sec. IT3 at km 2.1), characterized by a high groundwater level (2.5 m from ground level), less cohesive soil layers, and a tendency for sand dune formation (Fig. 2). This analysis provides insight into the most critical areas for slope failure, guiding appropriate design and stabilization measures16.

Slope stability analysis framework

Slope stability analysis is a critical component in the design and long-term performance assessment of earthen canals, particularly in regions characterized by high groundwater levels, variable hydraulic loading, and seismic activity. In this study, two-dimensional slope stability analyses were conducted for the Khush Tepa Canal using Slope/W 2025 in limit equilibrium mode with the Morgenstern–Price method, which satisfies both force and moment equilibrium and is widely accepted for complex slope stability problems. The Factor of Safety (FoS) is defined as the ratio of the total resisting forces (or moments) to the total driving forces (or moments) and was computed internally by Slope/W 2025 using the Morgenstern–Price formulation18–20.

The stability analyses focused on canal section IT3 (KM 2.1), selected based on site investigation results indicating shallow groundwater conditions and the presence of silt, poorly graded sand (SP), and silty sand with gravel (SM). These predominantly cohesionless soils exhibit relatively high permeability and are sensitive to seepage forces and pore water pressure variations, making them more vulnerable under transient hydraulic conditions such as rapid drawdown. Additionally, periodic flooding and sand dune activity may influence surface erosion and long-term slope performance. Therefore, IT3 was considered hydraulically sensitive and representative of potentially critical stability conditions.

Multiple hydraulic (steady-state and rapid drawdown), geotechnical, seismic, and bioengineering scenarios were evaluated to assess slope performance under both long-term and transient loading conditions. In SLOPE/W 2025, boundary conditions were defined to realistically simulate field behavior: the canal water level was applied as a total head boundary, the downstream side was modeled as a seepage/atmospheric boundary to allow pore pressure dissipation, and the model base was assumed relatively impervious according to subsurface data. These assumptions were adopted to ensure a realistic pore water pressure distribution and a reliable estimation of safety factors.

Hydraulic loading conditions

Steady-state seepage conditions

Steady-state slope stability represents long-term operational conditions in which groundwater flow and pore water pressures are fully developed and remain constant with time. Under such conditions, seepage forces and pore pressures significantly influence effective stress and soil shear strength. The configuration of the phreatic surface plays a dominant role in determining slope stability, particularly for cohesionless soils that are highly sensitive to pore pressure variations21.

In steady-state analyses, drained shear strength parameters are appropriate, as pore pressures are assumed to have equilibrated with prevailing groundwater conditions22. Previous numerical investigations have demonstrated that changes in water level and seepage line location can significantly alter safety factors of embankment slopes23. Accordingly, the steady-state stability analysis, shown in Fig. 3, was performed to evaluate the long-term stability of the canal embankment under fully saturated conditions, with particular emphasis on Section IT3, where the groundwater table is approximately 2.5 m below the ground surface.

Model geometry, material properties, boundary conditions, and assigned water table entry and exit surfaces for canal stability analysis at Full Supply Level (FSL).

Rapid drawdown conditions

Rapid drawdown was modeled to represent the sudden reduction of the canal water level following emergency discharge or operational changes. Initially, steady-state seepage conditions were established under full supply level to generate a realistic pore water pressure distribution within the embankment. Subsequently, the external water level was instantaneously reduced while maintaining the internal pore pressure distribution unchanged to simulate undrained or partially drained soil response. This approach reflects the physical mechanism of rapid drawdown, where external hydrostatic support is removed faster than pore water pressures can dissipate. As a result, effective stress and shear strength are temporarily reduced, producing one of the most critical loading conditions for earthen slopes. Modeling this condition is essential to evaluate the short-term stability risk and ensure adequate safety under transient hydraulic scenarios1,8.

In this study, rapid drawdown conditions, shown in Fig. 4, were simulated to evaluate the short-term stability of the canal slopes immediately following a sudden reduction in the canal water level.

Levee and freeboard stability modeling

The IT3 section of the Khush Tepa Canal is located in an area characterized by sand dunes, high flood potential, and wind-driven sediment transport. To protect the canal from flood overtopping and sand ingress, a levee with adequate freeboard has been proposed. The stability of the levee and freeboard slopes is critical, as failure could lead to flooding, sediment intrusion, and disruption of canal operation. Therefore, a dedicated levee and freeboard slope stability analysis was conducted, as shown in Fig. 5, to assess the safety of the canal embankment under prevailing geotechnical and hydraulic conditions. Vetiver grass reinforcement modeling

Mechanism of soil reinforcement by vetiver grass

Vetiver grass has been widely recognized as an effective bioengineering solution for slope stabilization. Experimental, physical modeling, and numerical studies have shown that vetiver roots significantly enhance soil shear strength, primarily through increased apparent cohesion and, to a lesser extent, improvements in the internal friction angle11,24,25.

Box-plot synthesis of reported data indicates that soil cohesion commonly increases by more than 100%, with potential increases of several 100% depending on soil type, root density, and plant age. In contrast, improvements in internal friction angle are generally moderate, typically limited to a few degrees26,27. The reinforcing effect is strongly age-dependent, with significantly greater cohesion enhancement observed after approximately 180 days of root development.

Adopted reinforcement parameters

For the present analysis, conservative reinforcement parameters were adopted to avoid overestimating the effects of vegetation. A 150% increase in soil cohesion and a 3% increase in internal friction angle were applied to vegetated zones, consistent with the lower bound of reported improvements. A vetiver root depth of 2 m was assumed to ensure penetration beyond shallow failure surfaces, and a horizontal planting spacing of 30 cm was selected to provide adequate root overlap and effective reinforcement. These assumptions are in accordance with established vetiver bioengineering practices and reported field studies28,29.

It should be noted that the uniform application of cohesion and friction angle increases, as well as the assumed 2 m root depth, represent a simplification of complex root–soil interactions in the field. In reality, root reinforcement is spatially variable and depends on root density, distribution, and soil conditions. As a result, this assumption may influence the predicted stability results, potentially leading to localized overestimation or underestimation of the Factor of Safety (FoS). However, the selected parameters are conservative and based on reported lower-bound improvements28,29, ensuring that the overall reinforcing effect of vetiver is not overestimated. Future studies could incorporate advanced root–soil interaction models to better capture spatial variability and temporal changes in root strength.

Results and discussion

Slope stability analyses were performed using Slope/W 2025 to simulate the behavior of the Khush Tepa Canal embankments under a range of hydraulic and geotechnical scenarios. The study considered steady-state conditions, rapid drawdown, pseudostatic (seismic) loading, and the stabilizing influence of vetiver grass on both the levee slopes and the freeboard sections of the canal. These analyses provide a comprehensive assessment of slope performance under both long-term and transient loading conditions.

Steady-state slope stability of the canal

The steady-state slope stability of Section IT3 at KM 2.1 was evaluated using the Morgenstern–Price method. The analysis considered 405 potential slip surfaces (Fig. 10), accounting for fully developed groundwater conditions with a groundwater level of approximately 2.5 m. The resulting Factor of Safety (FoS) of 2.11 (Fig. 11) indicates that the canal slope is stable under long-term saturated conditions. This result is consistent with the findings of16, who reported a similar FoS of approximately 2.10 for the same canal section, confirming the reliability of the analysis.

Results of the Morgenstern–Price stability analysis at full supply level, based on 405 potential slip surfaces, highlighting the critical slip surface with the minimum factor of safety.

Critical slope surface of section IT3 at full supply level of the canal, analyzed using the Morgenstern–Price limit equilibrium method.

Under steady-state conditions, the critical slip surface exhibits a deep-seated rotational geometry extending to a maximum depth of approximately 6.5 m below the crest (Fig. 11). The failure surface daylights near the toe at elevation 3 m and follows a circular arc through the foundation materials. This deep-seated configuration indicates that the failure mechanism is foundation-controlled, where pore pressures at depth govern the driving forces. The circular shape suggests homogeneous soil behavior with minimal structural control, typical of cohesive materials under long-term saturated conditions. The FoS of 2.11 confirms that the slope maintains an adequate margin against rotational failure, with resisting moments significantly exceeding driving moments along the entire slip surface (Fig. 12).

Column representation of the critical slip surface for Section IT3 under full supply level, illustrating the Morgenstern–Price method application.

Slope stability under rapid drawdown conditions

The slope stability of the canal under rapid drawdown conditions was analyzed for the sandy soil embankment. During a sudden drop in canal water level, the lateral hydrostatic support is removed faster than the dissipation of internal pore pressures within the soil. This reduction in effective stress and shear strength increases driving forces relative to resisting forces. Under these conditions, the Factor of Safety (FoS) decreases to 1.10 (Figs. 13 and 14), indicating that the slope is marginally stable and more susceptible to instability compared to steady-state or non-seismic conditions.

Critical slope surface of section IT3 at drawdown of the canal, analyzed using the Morgenstern–Price limit equilibrium method.

Results of the Morgenstern–Price stability analysis under drawdown conditions, based on 405 potential slip surfaces, highlighting the critical slip surface with the minimum factor of safety.

Rapid drawdown induces a fundamental transformation in the failure mechanism from deep-seated to shallow circular sliding. The critical slip surface becomes significantly shallower (approximately 3–4 m deep) compared to the 6.5 m depth observed under steady-state conditions (Fig. 10). The horizontal extent of the failure mass reduces from 18 to 20 m to approximately 12–14 m, and the exit point migrates upward, daylighting higher on the slope face above the original toe. This geometric evolution indicates that the loss of confining pressure from the external water level becomes the dominant destabilizing factor. The sandy soil experiences a sudden reduction in effective stress as positive pore pressures generated during drawdown cannot dissipate rapidly. The failure mechanism transitions from foundation-controlled deep rotation to slope-parallel sliding within the embankment materials. The marginal FoS of 1.10 confirms the slope is near limit equilibrium, where any additional perturbation could trigger failure along this shallower surface.

Stability of the levee and freeboard (non-vegetated)

The IT3 canal reach is located in an area dominated by sandy soils, with high susceptibility to flooding and wind-blown sand deposition. To protect the canal infrastructure, a levee with adequate freeboard has been designed to prevent overtopping and limit sand intrusion. The stability analysis of the levee and its freeboard, performed without seismic effects or vetiver reinforcement, yielded a Factor of Safety (FoS) of 1.93 (Fig. 15).

Critical slope surface of the canal levee and freeboard, analyzed using the Morgenstern–Price limit equilibrium method.

The critical slip surface exhibits a deep-seated rotational geometry extending to approximately 6.5 m depth, daylighting near the toe at 3 m elevation with a horizontal extent of 18–20 m. This deep circular configuration indicates a foundation-controlled failure mechanism governed by the shear strength of underlying materials rather than near-surface soil properties.

Effect of vetiver grass reinforcement

The inclusion of vetiver grass increased the FoS from 1.93 to 2.37 (Fig. 16), representing a 22.8% improvement due to root reinforcement enhancing soil cohesion and resisting slip surface development. Vetiver reinforcement fundamentally alters the failure mechanism. The critical slip surface becomes significantly shallower (3–4 m depth), a 40% reduction, and is constrained and flattened, no longer following a deep circular path. The failure mass horizontal extent reduces to 12–14 m, and the exit point migrates higher on the slope face.

Critical slope surface of the canal levee and freeboard with vetiver grass reinforcement, analyzed using the Morgenstern–Price limit equilibrium method.

The root network (3–4 m depth) creates a mechanically stabilized zone that forces the failure mechanism to transition from foundation-controlled deep rotational sliding to reinforcement-controlled constrained failure. Rather than propagating through the root zone, the slip surface is either shifted downward or constrained to shallower geometry that mobilizes root tensile strength. This explains why the 22.8% FoS improvement exceeds what a simple cohesion increase would predict; the reinforcement fundamentally changes how the slope fails.

Pseudostatic slope stability analysis

Canal slopes under steady-state and seismic loading

A pseudostatic slope stability analysis was performed to assess the effects of seismic loading on the Khush Tepa Canal embankments. Under steady-state conditions without seismic influence, the Factor of Safety (FoS) was 2.11, indicating stable slopes. When seismic forces were incorporated using the pseudostatic approach, the FoS decreased to 1.25 (Figs. 17, 18 and 19), demonstrating a significant reduction in stability and highlighting the increased vulnerability of the canal slopes under earthquake loading.

Critical slope surface of the main canal section, analyzed under pseudostatic seismic loading using the Morgenstern–Price limit equilibrium method.

Column view of the critical slip surface for section IT3 under full supply level, analyzed using the Morgenstern–Price method under pseudostatic seismic loading.

Morgenstern–Price pseudostatic seismic stability analysis of 405 potential slip surfaces, showing the critical slip surface.

The introduction of pseudostatic seismic forces modifies both the geometry of the critical slip surface and the underlying failure mechanism. While the slip surface maintains its deep-seated character (approximately 6.5 m depth), the circular arc is displaced outward and expands horizontally to encompass a larger failure mass (~ 22 m) compared to the static case (Fig. 10). This expansion occurs because horizontal inertial forces activate additional soil volumes along the potential failure surface. The failure mechanism remains rotational but with fundamentally different kinematics: seismic loading introduces cyclic shear stresses that can lead to progressive degradation of soil strength, particularly in the sandy materials near the toe. The exit angle becomes flatter, indicating that the driving forces now include significant horizontal components. The 40.8% reduction in FoS (from 2.11 to 1.25) reflects not only increased driving forces but also the potential for strain-softening behavior under cyclic loading conditions.

To further quantify the influence of seismic loading, a comparison of stability parameters under steady-state and pseudostatic conditions was conducted. Under non-seismic conditions, resisting moments (3,718.4 kN·m) and resisting forces (143.42 kN) significantly exceeded activating moments (1,760.9 kN·m) and activating forces (67.933 kN), confirming a stable configuration. However, the introduction of pseudostatic horizontal and vertical seismic loads resulted in a substantial increase in activating moments (2,566.8 kN·m) and activating forces (99.816 kN), accompanied by reductions in resisting moments (3,199.6 kN·m) and resisting forces (124.33 kN) (Tables 5 and 6). These combined effects clearly demonstrate the destabilizing impact of seismic forces on the canal embankment system (Fig. 20).

Therefore, seismic effects must be explicitly considered in canal design, and appropriate mitigation measures such as slope flattening, structural reinforcement, or bio-engineering solutions may be required to ensure stability during earthquake events2,8.

Canal slopes under rapid drawdown and seismic loading

A pseudostatic slope stability analysis under rapid drawdown conditions was conducted to assess the combined effects of sudden water-level reduction and seismic loading on the Khush Tepa Canal embankments. Under steady-state conditions, the Factor of Safety (FoS) was 2.11. However, under the combined scenario of rapid drawdown and pseudostatic seismic loading, the FoS decreased sharply to 0.79 (Fig. 21). This substantial reduction in FoS indicates that the canal slopes become highly unstable when these two destabilizing mechanisms act together, highlighting a critical risk scenario for slope failure.

This scenario represents the most critical loading condition, in which the combination of rapid drawdown and seismic loading produces a complete transformation in the failure geometry. The critical slip surface becomes extremely shallow (approximately 2–3 m depth), representing the shallowest failure surface among all analyzed cases (Fig. 21). The geometry approaches an almost planar configuration, running nearly parallel to the slope face with limited horizontal extent (8–10 m from the crest). This represents a transition from rotational sliding to thin veneer failure along the slope surface. The failure mechanism integrates two processes:

Levee and freeboard without vetiver grass

The critical slip surface depicted in Fig. 22 exhibits a deep-seated rotational geometry, extending to a depth of approximately 6.5 m and daylighting near the 3 m elevation contour. This configuration indicates that the failure mechanism is governed by the shear strength of underlying foundation materials rather than near-surface soil properties. Under pseudostatic seismic loading applied to the levee and freeboard, horizontal inertial forces simulate earthquake conditions, reducing the Factor of Safety from 1.93 under steady-state conditions to 1.53, a substantial decline of approximately 20.70%. This significant reduction reflects the increased vulnerability of the unreinforced levee to deep-seated failure during seismic events. The deep rotational configuration engages a large soil mass extending below the toe, suggesting that the foundation materials possess lower cyclic shear strength and may be susceptible to strain-softening under dynamic loading. In the absence of vetiver grass, the slope lacks the root reinforcement that would otherwise provide apparent cohesion in the upper soil profile, leaving the levee fully dependent on the intrinsic shear strength of its foundation materials. Consequently, the pseudostatic analysis underscores the inherent seismic vulnerability of the unreinforced levee and freeboard, highlighting the need for either deep soil improvement or engineered buttressing to mitigate seismically induced sliding and preserve freeboard integrity during earthquake loading.

Levee and freeboard with vetiver grass

The pseudostatic stability analysis (Fig. 23) demonstrates that vetiver grass reinforcement maintains levee integrity under seismic loading, with the Factor of Safety (FoS) decreasing from 2.37 under static loading to 1.80 under earthquake forces. Although seismic loading reduces stability by approximately 24%, the post-seismic FoS of 1.80 exceeds typical design thresholds (e.g., 1.25), confirming adequate performance.

Critical slope surface of the levee and freeboard under pseudostatic seismic loading with vetiver grass, analyzed using the Morgenstern–Price limit equilibrium method.

This resilience stems from the mechanical reinforcement provided by vetiver roots. Seismic loading in levees typically triggers shallow failures within the upper freeboard zone; however, as shown in Fig. 23, the critical slip surface extends to a depth of approximately 6.5 m and daylights near the 3 m elevation, indicating that vetiver reinforcement contributes to a deeper, more stable failure geometry compared to unreinforced conditions. In pseudostatic analysis using the Morgenstern–Price method, the horizontal seismic coefficient (kh) increases driving moments by simulating inertial forces. Vetiver roots counteract this by adding apparent cohesion to the soil, which increases resisting moments along potential failure planes. This root-generated shear strength directly offsets seismically induced stresses, explaining why vegetated slopes substantially outperform non-vegetated ones. The engineering implication is clear: vetiver grass provides cost-effective seismic stabilization of earthen embankments, promoting deeper, more stable failure surfaces without requiring structural measures.

In this study, vetiver grass was modeled as a bioengineering reinforcement to improve slope stability. Due to the absence of site-specific field measurements, the parameters for cohesion contribution, root depth, and spacing were adopted from published literature as conservative estimates. Vetiver is known to establish successfully in arid sand dune environments, where its dense fibrous root system can enhance shallow soil shear strength and improve near-surface stability through soil reinforcement and increased apparent cohesion. However, it is important to note that the present analysis is based on a static slope stability framework, and the potential effects of seismic-induced phenomena such as liquefaction, excess pore water pressure generation, and cyclic degradation of soil strength were not explicitly evaluated. Therefore, the reinforcing effect of vetiver grass in this study is limited to static conditions and does not account for possible strength loss mechanisms under strong earthquake loading. While regular maintenance may be required during the establishment phase, its long-term performance provides a sustainable means of improving slope stability and mitigating erosion, making it a practical nature-based solution for earthen canal embankments under static and quasi-static loading conditions in similar environments.

Conclusion

This study quantitatively assessed the stability of slopes along the Khush Tepa Canal under various critical conditions using a pseudostatic limit equilibrium framework. The analysis of section IT3, identified as the most critical due to its shallow groundwater and cohesionless soils, yielded specific numerical insights into the factors governing slope performance.

The analysis quantified the destabilizing impact of seismic and hydraulic forces. While the slope is stable under long-term steady-state conditions (FoS = 2.11), the application of pseudostatic seismic loading reduces the Factor of Safety (FoS) by 40.8% to exactly 1.25, the lower bound of the USACE minimum acceptable threshold shown in Fig. 24. This demonstrates that seismic effects alone can bring a nominally stable slope to the edge of the acceptable range.
