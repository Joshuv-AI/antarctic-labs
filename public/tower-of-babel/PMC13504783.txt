Biclonal gammopathy with coexistent proximal tubulopathy and cast nephropathy in multiple myeloma: a rare case report
Authors: Shanshen Yu, Jia Sun, Xufeng Mei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Light chain cast nephropathy is the most common cause of acute kidney injury in myeloma patients, typically presenting with elevated serum creatinine. Light-chain proximal tubulopathy is a manifestation of monoclonal light chain-associated renal injury, characterized by the accumulation of free light chains within proximal tubular cells. However, the co-occurrence of κ light-chain MM with an additional IgG-λ monoclonal band, cast nephropathy with normal serum creatinine, and light-chain proximal tubulopathy is extremely rare, posing significant challenges to diagnosis and treatment. Background

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a hematological malignancy originating from neoplastic plasma cells, typically characterized by the presence of a monoclonal immunoglobulin [1]. The diagnosis of MM requires evidence of either ≥ 10% clonal plasma cells on bone marrow examination or a biopsy-confirmed plasmacytoma, in addition to one or more myeloma-defining events [2]. MM accounts for 10%-15% of all blood cancers, making it the second most common haematological malignancy [1, 2].

Among MM, there is a special subtype characterized by the coexistence of two monoclonal immunoglobulins on serum immunofixation electrophoresis (IFE). One of these monoclonal immunoglobulins is predominantly elevated and fulfills the diagnostic criteria for MM, whereas the other is only slightly increased. This phenomenon can be interpreted as the concurrent presence of an active MM clone and a benign monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance clone [3].

Light chain cast nephropathy (LCCN) represents the most prevalent cause of acute kidney injury in these patients with MM [4]. A key pathological feature of cast nephropathy is the formation of cast precipitates in the loop of Henle, which result from the interaction between monoclonal free light chains (FLCs) overproduced by myeloma cells and Tamm-Horsfall protein; thereby obstructing the distal renal tubules [5]. Light-chain proximal tubulopathy (LCPT) is a manifestation of monoclonal light chain-associated renal injury, characterized by the accumulation of FLCs within proximal tubular cells [6].

Herein, we report a rare case of κ light-chain MM with an additional IgG-λ monoclonal band, complicated by LCCN (normal serum creatinine) and LCPT, aiming to enrich clinical understanding.

Case presentation

A 54-year-old female patient was admitted to our department on December 20, 2024, due to “detection of urinary protein for 2 months”. Outpatient urinalysis revealed a urinary total protein-to-creatinine ratio of 8548 mg/g·Cr and a urinary microalbumin-to-creatinine ratio of 415.2 mg/g·Cr. Additional relevant examinations were performed after admission. Laboratory findings showed urinary protein of 1+, with a 24-hour urinary protein excretion of 9860 mg; urinary potassium was 46 mmol/L (random urine), urinary creatinine was 5674 µmol/L; PH was 7.45; fractional excretion of potassium was 12.56%; hemoglobin was 93.0 g/L; serum creatinine was 53 µmol/L, serum potassium was 3.42 mmol/L, and β2-microglobulin is 4.9 mg/L. The concentrations of serum free κ chain and λ chain were 5170.00 mg/L and 20.40 mg/L, respectively, yielding a κ/λ ratio of 253.43. M-protein was detected by serum protein electrophoresis (Table 1). Chest CT showed decreased density of the left clavicle and fractures of the left 9th and 10th ribs. Physical examination revealed a mild anemic appearance.

Laboratory testing

The aforementioned findings were suggestive of monoclonal gammopathy-associated kidney injury, prompting us to perform more detailed evaluations. Urine IFE detected positive free κ chains, whereas serum IFE confirmed positive results for monoclonal immunoglobulin IgG-λ and free κ light chains (Fig. 1). Renal biopsy pathology indicated κ-light chain cast nephropathy, morphologically heterogeneous and variably sized lysosomes were observed in the cytoplasm of some renal tubular epithelial cells with dominant expression of κ light chains (Fig. 2). Routine bone marrow examination, bone marrow biopsy and bone marrow flow cytometry revealed a plasma cell myeloma bone marrow picture, with plasma cells accounting for approximately 70% (Fig. 1), showed κ light chains positivity. Whole-body PET/CT indicated nodular foci with increased glucose metabolism in the spleen, which was suggestive of plasmacytoma.

Two months prior, she had a history of a right rib fracture but did not undergo surgical treatment. Before admission, she had taken allisartan isoproxil tablets for one month, but there was no significant reduction in proteinuria. Family history was unremarkable, with no identified familial genetic disorders. She is a housewife, with harmonious family relationships and a good financial status. She has no history of smoking or alcohol consumption, and no history of occupational exposure.

The medical history is briefly summarized as follows: a 54-year-old female who was found to have proteinuria two months ago. Subsequent examinations revealed significantly elevated urinary total protein, abnormalities in FLCs and IFE. Bone marrow aspiration and renal biopsy were also performed.

Based on the aforementioned medical history, laboratory findings, and pathological results, the final diagnosis was confirmed as κ light-chain MM with an additional IgG-λ monoclonal band, classified as Durie-Salmon stage IIA and International Staging System stage II, complicated with κ-light chain cast nephropathy and LCPT. Due to limited conditions, the quantification of M protein, measurement of IgG-λ level, and fluorescence in situ hybridization testing were not performed. Thus, the accurate prognosis of MM is difficult to determine; however, currently the renal function is normal, indicating a good renal prognosis. The patient was subsequently administered chemotherapy with the VDT-PACE regimen, with the specific dosing schedule as follows: bortezomib 2 mg on days 1, 4, 8, 11; dexamethasone 40 mg on days 1–4; thalidomide 100 mg orally every night; cisplatin 15 mg on days 1–4; epirubicin 20 mg on days 1–4; cyclophosphamide 600 mg on days 1–4; and etoposide 60 mg on days 1–4. Following two cycles of chemotherapy, urinary protein levels and serum FLC levels decreased significantly. However, hemoglobin levels gradually declined to severe anemia, with a reticulocyte percentage of 0.06% (Table 1). Consequently, a repeat bone marrow aspiration was performed in March 2025, which revealed 30% myeloma cells and markedly reduced erythroid hyperplasia. Thus, the regimen was changed to T-PCD (T: thalidomide 100 mg once nightly orally; P: bortezomib 2 mg on days 1, 4, 8, 11; C: cyclophosphamide 0.6 g on days 1–4; D: dexamethasone 7.5 mg once daily) plus testosterone undecanoate (80 mg twice daily). After six weeks, the patient’s hemoglobin increased to 81 g/L. After the improvement of anemia, the patient experienced no other serious adverse reactions. Moreover, the patient had good compliance and came to the hospital regularly for chemotherapy. She received hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in January 2026, and overall prognosis remains good. The patient never expected such a complex disease, and thanks to family and medical staff for their support, she persevered to bone marrow transplant.

Discussion and conclusions

This case presents several key clinical questions: (1) Based on the renal pathology findings, is it sufficient to diagnose LCPT? (2) Why does LCCN exist but blood creatinine remains consistently normal? (3) Why are two M proteins detected in serum IFE but only one in urine?

LCCN represents a pattern of distal renal tubule involvement in MM. FLCs bind to Tamm-Horsfall protein, then forming casts, subsequently inducing tubular atrophy, progressive interstitial inflammation and fibrosis [4].LCPT predominantly involves injury to the proximal renal tubules. When FLCs flow through the proximal renal tubules, they can bind to the cubilin-megalin complex receptor, taken up by epithelial cells via excessive endocytosis, and subsequently accumulate in lysosomes with resistance to proteolysis [7].

The concurrent occurrence of LCCN and LCPT in MM is indeed rare, a review of the literature reveals that there are currently 10 relevant case reports (Table 2) [8–17]. Recently, Renal Pathology Society/International Kidney and Monoclonal Gammopathy Research Group proposed diagnostic criteria for non-crystalline LCPT, including “monotypic immunoglobulin light chain staining in proximal tubular cell cytoplasm and non-crystalline inclusions within proximal tubular cell cytoplasm by light microscopy and electron microscopy” [18]. Therefore, we considered the presence of LCPT in this described case.

LCCN is typically associated with acute kidney injury [5]. A retrospective analysis of 178 biopsy-confirmed LCCN patients demonstrated that the severity of medullary inflammation, giant cell reactions, and cast formation correlates with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) at the time of diagnosis, whereas the extent of cortical casts and interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy is associated with eGFR during follow-up [19]. The small number of casts and mild giant cell reaction, tubular atrophy, and interstitial fibrosis likely explain the persistently normal serum creatinine in this case.

In the present case, bone marrow biopsy demonstrated that plasma cells accounted for approximately 70% of the nucleated cell population, accompanied by a markedly elevated serum free κ-light chain at 5170.00 mg/L with a κ/λ ratio elevated to 253.43—findings consistent with the diagnosis of MM [2]. Furthermore, serum IFE confirmed the coexistence of monoclonal immunoglobulin IgG-λ and free κ light chain, which is just a rare condition [20]. Some cases of biclonal condition originate from class switching of a single intact clone, with the most common combination being IgG and IgA. Other cases arise from two independent plasma cell clones, each producing its own monoclonal immunoglobulin, characterized by unrelated dual light chain expression [3]. In our case, the two M proteins were IgG-λ and free κ light chains, with completely distinct light chains. Therefore, we hypothesize that they originate from two independent plasma cell clones producing unrelated monoclonal antibodies. Given that no IgG-λ positivity was detected by bone marrow immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry, or renal pathology, combined with the approximately one-year follow-up results (Table S1), none of the currently available data provide evidence that the IgG-λ clone is pathogenic. It appears to be a minor, benign clone whose burden is insufficient to be clearly detected by bone marrow immunohistochemistry but is sufficient to be identified by serum immunofixation electrophoresis. In contrast, the κ light chain is the dominant clone responsible for the disease manifestations. This pattern is consistent with the scenario described in the literature, namely “the coexistence of an active multiple myeloma clone and a benign monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance-like clone”. The treatment response also supports the dominant pathogenicity of the κ clone. After chemotherapy, serum free κ light chain levels decreased from 5170 mg/L to 1980 mg/L, urinary protein rapidly turned negative, and serum IgG levels decreased from 17.07 g/L to 10.51 g/L (Table 1); however, renal response occurred mainly in parallel with the decline in κ FLC. In summary, we consider the IgG-λ clone to be a “bystander” clone, likely at the stage of monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance, without causing independent organ damage. Certainly, limitations exist. Imaging suggested splenic infiltration, but no splenic histological examination was performed; therefore, direct evidence identifying the dominant clone in the spleen is lacking. Furthermore, this conclusion is based on current clinical follow-up data. Theoretically, with disease evolution or chemotherapy selection pressure, the IgG-λ clone might acquire pathogenicity through clonal evolution in the future.

Under physiological conditions, the glomerular filtration barrier permits the passage of molecules with a molecular weight of 60–70 kDa into the renal tubular lumen. In the setting of glomerular filtration barrier injury and increased permeability, high-molecular-weight molecules can traverse the barrier and be detected in the urine [21]. In the present case, renal impairment was predominantly induced by monoclonal κ light chains. Given the small molecular weight of light chains, which facilitates their excretion in the urine, κ light chain positivity was confirmed by urine immunofixation electrophoresis. In contrast, IgG-λ was not deposited in the kidneys and did not contribute to renal pathogenesis. Given that the glomerular lesions are not severe, factors such as low serum IgG-λ concentration and insufficient detection sensitivity may have led to the absence of IgG-λ in the urine. Pradhan et al. reported a case with serum IFE showing two bands (IgG and κ), urine IFE only κ, and renal biopsy confirming cast nephropathy [22].

After two cycles of chemotherapy with the VDT-PACE regimen, this patient developed progressively worsening severe anemia, with hemoglobin levels declining to 50 g/L. First, anemia caused by MM typically presents as normocytic normochromic anemia. Bone marrow smears generally show a relative reduction in erythroid percentage rather than a “marked reduction,” and the reticulocyte count is usually normal or mildly decreased. A near-absence of reticulocytes (0.06%) is extremely rare. Therefore, MM itself cannot fully explain the development of extremely severe anemia with erythroid aplasia following chemotherapy. Second, the VDT-PACE regimen (including thalidomide and cyclophosphamide) often causes pancytopenia, but here only the erythroid lineage was severely affected (at Hb 50 g/L: WBC 4 × 10⁹/L, PLT 185 × 10⁹/L), which is inconsistent with typical chemotherapy-induced pancytopenia. Third, the association between pure red cell aplasia and monoclonal gammopathy has been supported by the literature [23]. However, the rapid exacerbation of anemia in this patient occurred precisely after VDT-PACE chemotherapy, suggesting that chemotherapeutic agents may have also contributed to this process, leading to severe anemia with erythroid aplasia despite residual myeloma burden. The exact mechanism remains unclear, and the above are only clinical observations; further studies are needed to clarify the exact cause.

In summary, we report a case of κ light-chain MM with an additional IgG-λ monoclonal band complicated by both LCCN (normal serum creatinine) and LCPT. Its strengths lie in the elucidation of a rare phenomenon and a reasoned resolution of apparent contradictions. The limitations are primarily the absence of key confirmatory studies, including FISH, splenic pathology, and immunological evidence of pure red cell aplasia. The rarity of multiple comorbidities in this case not only highlights the complexity of clinical diagnosis and treatment but also provides valuable clinical experience for the management of rare myeloma subtypes and their complications.
