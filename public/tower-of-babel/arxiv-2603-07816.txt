Infinite Words with very Low Factor Complexity: an introduction to Combinatorics on Words
Mélodie Andrieu

Abstract
These lecture notes provide an introduction to combinatorics on words and its interactions with dynamics, algebra, and arithmetic. The central theme is the notion of low factor complexity for infinite words. We investigate the following guiding questions: What is the minimal complexity of a non-trivial infinite word over a binary, ternary, or more generally finite alphabet? How should “non-triviality” be formalized? Which words achieve this minimal complexity? Are there many? Are they interesting? In exploring these questions, we introduce classical objects and tools from combinatorics on words such as Sturmian words and Rauzy graphs , as well as little-known and new results. In particular, the third chapter is devoted to a theorem by R. Tijdeman from 1999, which generalizes a seminal result of M. Morse and G. Hedlund from 1938, and of which we provide a conceptually new, algebraic proof by J. Cassaigne and the author from 2022. The first chapter requires no prior knowledge in combinatorics on words and symbolic dynamics. It is intended for graduate students. The second and third chapters are intended both for graduate students and researchers familiar with Sturmian words and their generalizations. This text is based on a lecture delivered during the 7th edition of the international conference Dyadisc, held in Valparaíso, Chile, in December 2024. The new results it contains have also been presented in various seminars since 2023.

Overview

Let
d\geq 1
be an integer. An
infinite
d
-ary word
is a sequence
w\in\{1,\ldots,d\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. The
complexity
of
w
is the function that counts, for every integer
n
, the number of distinct subwords of length
n
that appear in
w
.
The aim of this course is to answer the following two questions:

1) What is the
minimal
complexity of
interesting

d
-ary words?

2) Which words achieve this minimal complexity?

Of course, answering these questions will require a rigorous and legitimate definition for
minimal
and
interesting
.

For binary words, the situation has been perfectly understood since the 1940s:
Sturmian words
are exactly the
interesting
(which in this case means
non-eventually periodic
) words with minimal complexity. Chapter
1
is a self-contained introduction to Sturmian words. We introduce them from a purely combinatorial perspective, and show how they relate to continued fractions, and to some dynamical systems. In particular, we emphasize why the rational independence of their letter frequencies is a central property of Sturmian words. In Chapter
1.4
, we explore how both the seminal theorem of M. Morse and G. Hedlund from 1938 and Sturmian words can be generalized to
d
-ary alphabets, when
d\geq 3
. We will see that, in this context, “interesting” can no longer mean “non-eventually periodic”, but should instead become “with rationally independent letter frequencies”. We conclude the chapter by discussing a theorem by R. Tijdeman from 1999 (independently rediscovered by J. Cassaigne and the author in 2022), which gives the minimal complexity of
d
-ary words with rationally independent letter frequencies. Finally, in Chapter
1.11
, we provide a conceptually new, algebraic proof of Tijdeman’s theorem, and develop its consequences on the combinatorial structure of
d
-ary words with rationally independent letter frequencies and minimal complexity.

The three chapters can be read independently.

Chapter 1
A seminal theorem by Morse and Hedlund (1938)

1.1
Preliminaries: infinite words, complexity

Let
u
be a finite word and
w
be an
infinite word
(by definition: a sequence in
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
where
\mathcal{A}
is a finite set called an
alphabet
). We say that
u
is a factor of length
n
of
w
, which we denote by
u\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(w)
, if
u
occurs in
w
as a block of
n
consecutive letters. For example,
art
is a factor of length 3 of the French word
montmartre
(
\texttt{art}\in\mathcal{L}_{3}(\texttt{montmartre})
).

Definition 1.1
.

The
complexity
of an infinite word
w
is the function that counts, for each integer
n
, the number of factors of length
n
in
w
:

\begin{array}[]{rccl}p_{w}:&\mathbb{N}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{N}\\
&n&\longmapsto&\mathrm{card}\big(\mathcal{L}_{n}(w)\big).\end{array}

(Note that, by convention, we always have
p_{w}(0)=1
: every word admits exactly one factor of length
0
, the “empty word”, denoted by
\epsilon
.)

We let the reader check the following examples.

Example 1.2
.

(i) If
w_{1}=1^{\omega}=111111...
is the constant unary word, then
p_{w_{1}}(n)=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Indeed, the unique factor of length
n
of
w_{1}
is
1...1
(that is, the letter
1
repeated
n
times). Note that
w_{1}
has the absolute minimal complexity.

(ii) If
w_{2}=(12)^{\omega}=12121212...
is the periodic word with period
12
, then
p_{w_{2}}(n)=2
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
. Indeed,
w_{2}
admits exactly two factors of length
n
: one that starts with the letter
1
, and one that starts with the letter
2
.

(iii) If
w_{3}=(1233)^{\omega}=1233\cdot 1233\cdot 1233...
is the periodic word with period
1233
, then
p_{w_{3}}(1)=3
and
p_{w_{3}}(n)=4
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
. Indeed,
w_{3}
is written with exactly
3
letters, and every factor of length
n\geq 2
is solely determined by its first two letters:
12
,
23
,
33
, or
31
.

(iv) The decimal
Champernowne word
, which is obtained by concatenating the base
10
expansions of all integers:

w_{C}=0123456789101112131415161718192021...

has complexity
p_{w_{C}}(n)=10^{n}
,
\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Indeed, every finite
10
-ary word appears in
w_{C}
. (Exercise: Where is the first occurrence of the factor
02
?) This complexity is maximal for infinite
10
-ary words.

(v) It is believed, but far from being proved, that the decimal expansion of the golden ratio also has maximal complexity. This claim is a particular case of a much larger conjecture, attributed to E. Borel in 1950, which asserts that every irrational algebraic number is “normal in every base”
[
12
]
.

As the name suggests, the complexity quantifies how complicated an infinite word is. This combinatorial function is to be understood as a refinement of the dynamical notion of entropy, which measures how much the knowledge of a small portion of a trajectory gives information about its possible long-term behavior. The next section establishes that, on this scale, periodic words are the simplest words.

1.2
The seminal theorem by Morse and Hedlund

The next theorem appeared in the first article of M. Morse and G. Hedlund
[
31
]
; it is one of the foundational results of Combinatorics on Words.

Theorem 1.3

(M. Morse and G. Hedlund, 1938)
.

The following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

The infinite word
w
is eventually periodic;

(ii)

Its complexity
p_{w}
is bounded;

(iii)

There exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
p_{w}(n)\leq n
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) An eventually periodic word
w=u\cdot p^{\omega}
admits at most one new factor of length
n
(whatever
n
is) starting at each position of its preperiod
u
or its period
p
. Therefore, its complexity
p_{w}
is bounded above by the sum of the lengths of its preperiod and period:
|u|+|p|
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii) This is trivial: it suffices to take
n=M
, where
M\in\mathbb{N}
is an upper bound for the complexity function.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i) Since
p_{w}(0)=1
(it was our convention),
p_{w}(n)\leq n
, and since
n\mapsto p_{w}(n)
is non-decreasing (indeed, every factor of length
n
of
w
can always be extended into a factor of length
n+1
), there must exist
n_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
such that
p_{w}(n_{0})=p_{w}(n_{0}+1)
. This equality implies that, if
u
is a factor of length
n_{0}
, then all its occurrences in
w
are always followed by the same letter. (Otherwise,
u
would give rise to two factors of length
n_{0}+1
, implying that
p_{w}(n_{0}+1)>p_{w}(n_{0})
.) Furthermore, among the
p_{w}(n_{0})
factors of length
n_{0}
occurring in
w
, at least one, let us call it again
u
, must occur infinitely often. Thus, there exists
v\in\mathcal{L}(w)
such that
uvu\in\mathcal{L}(w)
(by
\mathcal{L}(w)
we denote the set of all factors of
w
, regardless of their length). Since every factor of length
n_{0}
of
w
is always followed by the same letter, we deduce that the factor
u
is always followed by the factor
vu
. This shows that there exists
p\in\mathcal{L}(w)
such that
w=pu(vu)^{\omega}
,
i.e.
,
w
is eventually periodic.
∎

Remark 1.4
.

It follows from this proof that a word
w
is eventually periodic if and only if its complexity function is
locally constant
: there exists a length
m
for which
p_{w}(m+1)=p_{w}(m)
, if and only if, its complexity is eventually constant: for all
n\geq m
,
p_{w}(n)=p_{w}(m)
.

The contraposition of (iii)
\Rightarrow
(i) states that if
w
is not eventually periodic, then its complexity satisfies
p_{w}(n)\geq n+1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This leads to the following natural question.

Question 1.5
.

Does there exist an infinite word
w
whose complexity is exactly
p_{w}(n)=n+1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
?

As we shall see, the answer to this question is ‘yes’. This positive answer will raise three new questions:

1. How many infinite words satisfy this complexity condition?

2. What do they look like? Can we construct them effectively/efficiently?

3. Are they interesting?

The remainder of this chapter is devoted to answering these questions. As a spoiler, the answers will be: ‘uncountably many, essentially one for each irrational number’, ‘yes’, ‘yes’, and ‘YES!’.

Definition 1.6
.

The infinite words
w
with complexity
p_{w}(n)=n+1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
are called
Sturmian words
.

Example 1.7
.

Although we have not yet proven that Sturmian words exist (this will be done later), here is a first example, called
the Fibonacci word
.

w_{fibo}=1211212112112121121211211212112112121121211211212112121...

In the next sections, we will see how this word is constructed, and why it is called “Fibonacci”.

Exercise 1.8
.

Prove that, if an infinite word satisfies
p_{w}(n)=n+1
for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then it is Sturmian.

1.3
The class of Sturmian words

The study of Sturmian words was initiated in 1940 by M. Morse and G. Hedlund in their second article
[
32
]
. They have been, and continue to be, extensively studied. This section is intended to be a self-contained introduction to Sturmian words. However, it is far from being exhaustive (for example, abelian properties are deliberately missing). We refer the interested reader to
[
30
, Chapter 2]
for complements.

Outline of the section.
In the next two subsections, we assume that Sturmian words do exist and study their properties. We will see that, if they exist, they must be intimately connected to continued fractions. In the third subsection, we use continued fractions to design an effective and efficient way to construct them—thus finally proving that they
indeed
exist. In the fourth and last subsection, we explain how Sturmian words can be dynamically and geometrically visualized.

1.3.1
Basic properties

The next proposition gathers elementary properties of Sturmian words that will be needed in the sequel.

Proposition 1.9
.

Let
w
be a Sturmian word. Then:

(i)

w
is a binary word (hereafter, we set the alphabet to
\mathcal{A}=\{1,2\}
).

(ii)

w
is “recurrent” (by definition: every factor of
w
occurs infinitely many times).

(iii)

If
S
denotes the “shift map” (by definition: the map that erases the first letter of an infinite word; for instance,
S(314159...)=14159...
), then
S(w)
is also a Sturmian word.

(iv)

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a unique factor
u\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(w)
such that both extensions
u1
and
u2
are factors of
w
(definition: we say that
u
is “right-special”).

Proof.

(i) Trivial: since
p_{w}(1)=2
,
w
uses exactly two letters.

(ii) By contradiction, assume that
u\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(w)
does not occur infinitely many times; suppose, for example, that it does not occur after position
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, if we denote by
w^{\prime}:=S^{N}(w)
the copy of
w
after position
N
, we have
u\notin\mathcal{L}_{n}(w^{\prime})
, hence
p_{w^{\prime}}(n)<p_{w}(n)=n+1
. This contradicts Theorem
1.3
, since
w^{\prime}
remains non-eventually periodic.

(iii) Trivially, every factor of
S(w)
is a factor of
w
. Since
w
is recurrent, the converse is also true: every factor of
w
is a factor of
S(w)
. Therefore, the complexity functions of
S(w)
and
w
are equal, and
S(w)
is a Sturmian word.

(iv) Since
w
is a binary word, the quantity
p_{w}(n+1)-p_{w}(n)
is exactly the number of right-special factors of length
n
of
w
. For a Sturmian word
w
, this difference equals
1
, so for each length
n
,
w
admits exactly one right-special factor of length
n
.
∎

Exercise 1.10
.

Let
w
be a Sturmian word. Prove that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a unique
v\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(w)
such that both left-extensions
1v
and
2v
are factors of
w
(definition: we say that
v
is “left-special”).

1.3.2
Interlude: Crash course on the continued fraction

Before going further, let us recall what a continued fraction is, and why it is an extremely interesting object. (In the next subsection, we will explain how it connects to Sturmian words.)

The continued fraction is a
numeration system
, that is, a way to represent real numbers by a finite or infinite sequence of integers. The continued fraction expansion
(a_{n})_{n}
of a positive real number
x
is classically denoted by

x=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\cdots].

For example, the continued fraction expansion of
17/6
is
[2;1,5]
, the continued fraction expansion of the golden ratio
\varphi:=(1+\sqrt{5})/2
is
[1;1,1,1,1,1,1...]
(the infinite sequence constant, equal to
1
), while the continued fraction expansion of
\pi
is an infinite sequence that starts with
[3;7,15,1,292,1,1,1,2,...]
.
Contrary to the base expansion, the integers
a_{n}
that appear in the continued fraction, which we call “partial quotients”, are not necessarily bounded.

Question: How do we calculate the partial quotients?

The partial quotients
(a_{n})
of
x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
are exactly the successive
integer
quotients that appear in the “Euclidean algorithm” applied to the pair of
real numbers

(x,1)
. For example, the first partial quotient
a_{0}
of
x
is the unique integer
q
such that

x=q\times 1+r,

with
0\leq r<1
. (In other words,
a_{0}=\lfloor x\rfloor
and
r=\{x\}
are the integer and fractional parts of
x
.) Then, the second partial quotient
a_{1}
is the integer quotient in the “Euclidean division” of
1
by
r
(which is also, equivalently, the integer quotient in the Euclidean division of
1/r
by
1
), and so on. Algorithmically, we would write:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\hskip 8.5359pt\mathtt{def\;CF(}x\mathtt{):}&\\
\hskip 25.6073pt\mathtt{print}\lfloor x\rfloor&\\
\hskip 25.6073pt\mathtt{if}\{x\}\neq 0,\;\mathtt{return\;CF}\big(\frac{1}{\{x\}}\big)&\end{array}

Note that this algorithm does not always terminate.

The next proposition illustrates why the continued fraction is an extremely interesting algebraic and analytic tool: it unambiguously represents real numbers, provides high-quality rational approximations, and characterizes both rational and quadratic
1
1

1

Definition: a real number is
quadratic
(resp.
cubic
or
algebraic
) if it is a root of a polynomial of degree
2
(resp. degree
3
or any degree) with integer coefficients.
numbers.

Proposition 1.11
.

Let
x=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\cdots]
be a positive real number.

(1)

The continued fraction expansion of
x
is finite if and only if
x\in\mathbb{Q}
.

(2)

[Dirichlet’s theorem]
The sequence of rational numbers

\left(\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}\right)_{n}:=\big([a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n}]\big)_{n}=\left(a_{0}+\frac{1}{a_{1}+\frac{1}{\ddots+\frac{1}{a_{n}}}}\right)_{n}

converges efficiently to
x
; moreover, these rational numbers are “best approximations” of
x
in the following sense: one cannot find a rational number that is closer to
x
than
p_{n}/q_{n}
, without paying the price of a larger denominator.

(3)

The following map is a bijection:

\begin{array}[]{ccl}\mathbb{R}_{>0}\setminus\mathbb{Q}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{N}\times(\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1})^{\mathbb{N}}\\
x&\longmapsto&(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\end{array}

(4)

[Lagrange’s theorem]
An irrational number
x
is a quadratic if and only if its continued fraction expansion is eventually periodic.

It is a long-standing open problem (the question was initially asked by C. Hermite in 1850) to generalize Lagrange’s theorem to cubic, or more generally, algebraic numbers. This question is one of the seminal motivations of C. G. J. Jacobi for introducing a “multidimensional continued fraction” (on which we shall come back later).

1.3.3
Connection between Sturmian words and continued fractions

Sturmian words and continued fractions are connected through a renormalization process that we introduce now.

Let
w
be a Sturmian word. Since
w
is not eventually periodic, it must contain both factors
12
and
21
. Since
p_{w}(2)=3
,
w
admits a third factor of length
2
, which can be either
11

xor
(exclusive or)
22
. We say that
w
is “of type
1
” if it contains
11
, and “of type
2
” otherwise.

The renormalization process.
Let
i\in\{1,2\}
, and denote the other letter by
j
. Let
w
be a Sturmian word of type
i
. Then, every occurrence of the letter
j
in
w
(except the first one when
w
starts with
j
) is preceded by at least one occurrence of
i
. Let
R_{i}(w)
denote the infinite word obtained from
w
by erasing the
i
that immediately precedes every
j
. Our renormalization process is then defined by

R(w)=\begin{cases}R_{1}(w)\text{ if $w$ is of type 1,}\\
R_{2}(w)\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Example 1.12
.

It is useful to illustrate the renormalization process with an example. To this end, consider again the
Fibonacci word
, which we temporarily assume is Sturmian.

w_{fibo}=121121211211212112121121121211211212112121121121211212...

The Fibonacci word is of type
1
, hence

R(w_{fibo})=R_{1}(w_{fibo})=212212122122121221212212212122122...

We claim that this new infinite word is also Sturmian.

Theorem 1.13
.

If
w
is a Sturmian word, then
R(w)
is also a Sturmian word.

Therefore, we can iterate
R
infinitely many times. We define the “run-length” sequence
(b_{n})_{n}
of a Sturmian word
w
as the sequence recording the number of times the operation
R_{1}
(resp.
R_{2}
) is repeated before the next application of
R_{2}
(resp.
R_{1}
). Specifically, if
(R_{i_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
denotes the successive values taken by the operator
R
when iterated on
w
, we have

i_{0}i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}i_{4}i_{5}i_{6}...=1^{b_{0}}2^{b_{1}}1^{b_{2}}2^{b_{3}}...

(Note that, by convention,
b_{0}
denotes the number of consecutive iterations of
R_{1}
before the first application of
R_{2}
.)

Since
(R_{i_{n}})_{n}
contains both operators
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
infinitely many times (see Exercise
1.17
), its run-length
(b_{n})
is an
infinite
sequence of
integers
. More precisely, we must have
b_{k}>0
for every
k\geq 1
, and
b_{0}=0
if and only if the Sturmian word
w
under consideration is of type
2
.

Example 1.14
.

The first iterations of the operator
R
on the Fibonacci word are:

\begin{array}[]{lllll}w&=w_{fibo}&&=121121211211212112121121121211211212112...&\text{type 1}\\
w^{\prime}&=R(w)&=R_{1}(w)&=212212122122121221212212...&\text{type 2}\\
w^{\prime\prime}&=R^{2}(w)&=R_{2}(w^{\prime})&=121121211211212...&\text{type 1}\\
w^{\prime\prime\prime}&=R^{3}(w)&=R_{1}(w^{\prime\prime})&=212212122...&\text{type 2}\\
\text{etc.}&&&&\end{array}

In fact, we could prove that
w^{\prime\prime}=w_{fibo}
(this is a particularity of the Fibonacci word), and deduce that the run-length of
w_{fibo}
is the constant sequence equal to
1
.

Remark 1.15
.

By definition, a “substitution” is a function from the alphabet to the set of finite words. It extends to finite and infinite words as a morphism with respect to concatenation. (For example, the image of the finite word
aaba
by the substitution
s:a\mapsto aab
,
b\mapsto ba
is

s(aaba)=s(a)s(a)s(b)s(a)=aabaabbaaab
.) Now, if
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
denote the particular substitutions

\begin{array}[]{lllll}\sigma_{1}:&1\mapsto 1&&\sigma_{2}:&1\mapsto 21\\
&2\mapsto 12&&&2\mapsto 2,\end{array}

then observe, in Example
1.14
, that
w
can be reconstructed from its image
w^{\prime}
by applying
\sigma_{1}
. Similarly, the infinite words
w^{\prime}
and
w^{\prime\prime}
can be reconstructed from their respective images
w^{\prime\prime}
and
w^{\prime\prime\prime}
by
w^{\prime}=\sigma_{2}(w^{\prime\prime})
and
w^{\prime\prime}=\sigma_{1}(w^{\prime\prime\prime})
.
These two substitutions
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
will play a central role in the sequel.

Exercise 1.16
.

Let
w
be a Sturmian word of type

.

1) Prove that the equality
w=\sigma_{i}(R_{i}(w))
does not always hold. (Hint: consider

Proof of Theorem
1.13
.

Let
w
be a Sturmian word of type
i
, the other letter being again denoted by
j
. We want to prove that
w^{\prime}:=R(w)=R_{i}(w)
is also a Sturmian word. First, observe that
w^{\prime}
is eventually periodic if and only if
w
is eventually periodic—which it is not. Therefore, by the Morse–Hedlund theorem
1.3
, we must have
p_{w^{\prime}}(n)\geq n+1
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

For the converse inequality, we will prove that, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
w^{\prime}
admits at most one right-special factor of length
n
. (Therefore, using again the argument in the proof of Proposition
1.9
, Assertion (iv), we have
p(n+1)-p(n)\leq 1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, whence
p(n)\leq n+1
.) We argue by contradiction.
Assume that
w^{\prime}
contains two distinct right-special factors of equal length,
u
and
v
. Then the four extensions
ui
,
uj
,
vi
and
vj
are factors of
w^{\prime}
. By Exercise
1.16
, their images under the substitution
\sigma_{i}
are factors of the original Sturmian word
w
:
\sigma_{i}(ui)=\sigma_{i}(u)i
,
\sigma_{i}(uj)=\sigma_{i}(u)ij
,
\sigma_{i}(vi)=\sigma_{i}(v)i
, and
\sigma_{i}(vj)=\sigma_{i}(v)ij
. We can be slightly more precise by claiming that the two words
\sigma_{i}(u)ii
and
\sigma_{i}(v)ii
are factors of
w
. Indeed, since
ui
is a factor of
w^{\prime}
, either
uii
or
uij
(or both) is also a factor of
w^{\prime}
, from which it follows that either
\sigma_{1}(u)ii
or
\sigma_{1}(u)iij
is a factor of
w
; in either case,
\sigma_{1}(u)ii
is a factor of
w
—the argument being analogous for
\sigma_{1}(v)ii
.

At this point, we have established that the two finite words
\sigma_{i}(u)i
and
\sigma_{i}(v)i
, which are distinct since
u\neq v
, form two right-special factors of the Sturmian word

The next theorem establishes the connection between Sturmian words, our renormalization process, and continued fractions.

Theorem 1.18
.

Let

Remark 1.20
.

Classically, the existence of letter frequencies in Sturmian words is derived from an abelian property called “balancedness”, which we choose not to develop in this lecture. Boshernitzan’s theorem enables us to establish the same result, directly from the complexity.

Proof of Theorem
1.18
, assertions (ii) and (iii).

Let
w
be a Sturmian word. Denote by

and

its letter frequencies.

(ii) By definition of the run-length sequence

. Step 1: initial iterations of

(which already resembles the continued fraction…)

Step 2: iterating

(iii) Since

1.3.4
An efficient construction of Sturmian words

In this subsection, we prove that Sturmian words exist, and that they are uncountably many: essentially one per positive irrational number. These results rely on an efficient construction of Sturmian words with the continued fraction.

The next theorem presents the construction. It uses the substitutions
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
, which appeared in Section
1.3.3
as reverses of the renormalization process
R
. They were defined by

\begin{array}[]{lllll}\sigma_{1}:&1\mapsto 1&&\sigma_{2}:&1\mapsto 21\\
&2\mapsto 12&&&2\mapsto 2.\end{array}

Theorem 1.22
.

Let
(s_{n})_{n}\in\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\}^{\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in which both substitutions
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
occur infinitely often. Then

(i)
the two sequences of finite words
(s_{1}\circ s_{2}\circ\cdots\circ s_{n}(1))_{n}
and
(s_{1}\circ s_{2}\circ\cdots\circ s_{n}(2))_{n}
converge, as
n\to\infty
, to the same infinite word
w_{0}
, which is Sturmian;

(ii)
the run-length of the substitution sequence
(s_{n})_{n}
(defined, again, as the number of times
\sigma_{1}
is repeated before the first apparition of
\sigma_{2}
, then the number of times
\sigma_{2}
is repeated before the next apparition of
\sigma_{1}
, and so on) is exactly the continued fraction expansion of the ratio
\frac{f_{1}}{f_{2}}
, where
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
denote the letter frequencies in the Sturmian word
w_{0}
.

(The topology we use is the Tychonoff topology for the product set
\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
: a sequence of finite or infinite words
(u_{n})_{n}
converges to an infinite words
w
if the words
u_{n}
share larger and larger prefixes with
w
.)

Example 1.23
.

Let
(s_{n})_{n}=\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots
be the periodic sequence with period
(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
. Then, the sequence of finite words
u_{n}:=(s_{1}\circ s_{2}\circ\cdots\circ s_{n}(1))_{n}
, which starts with

\begin{array}[]{l}u_{0}=u_{1}=1\\
u_{2}=u_{3}=121\\
u_{4}=u_{5}=12112\\
u_{5}=u_{6}=1211212112112\\
u_{7}=u_{8}=1211212112112121121211211212112112\\
...\end{array}

converges to a Sturmian word
w_{0}
. This Sturmian word
w_{0}
is called “Fibonacci word”— it is the example of a Sturmian word that we mentioned in advance in this lecture. As we announced in Examples
1.14
and
1.19
, the ratio of its letter frequencies is the golden ratio. Indeed, the run length of the substitution sequence
(s_{n})_{n}
is the constant sequence, equal to
1
; therefore,
f_{1}/f_{2}=[1;1,1,1,1,1,1,1,...]=\varphi
. The following exercise justifies its name “Fibonacci”.

Definition 1.25
.

The Sturmian words obtained in Theorem

are called

. The sequence of substitutions

A natural question is: is every Sturmian word standard, that is, is every Sturmian word the limit of a sequence of iterated images by the substitutions
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}

or equivalently, such that

admit the same set of factors.

The next result is a corollary of Theorem
1.22
. It states that there exist uncountably many Sturmian words, essentially one per positive irrational number.

Corollary 1.27
.

For every positive irrational number

, there exists a unique standard Sturmian word
w_{0}
whose letter frequencies satisfy
\frac{f_{1}}{f_{2}}=x
.

Proof.

By Proposition
1.11
, the continued fraction is a bijection between the set of positive irrational numbers and the set of infinite sequences of integers
\mathbb{N}\times(\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1})^{\mathbb{N}}
, which is itself in bijection, through the notion of “run length”, with the set of sequences of substitutions
\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\}^{\mathbb{N}}
in which
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
appear infinitely many times.
Therefore, by Theorem
1.22
, for every positive irrational number

, there exists a unique standard Sturmian word whose letter frequencies satisfy
f_{1}/f_{2}=x
.
∎

1.3.5
Equivalent dynamical and geometrical descriptions of Sturmian words

In this last subsection, we present (with no proof), alternative dynamical and geometrical constructions of Sturmian words. Specifically, we will see that
Sturmian words encode the
minimal
trajectories of

- a billiard ball on a square table,

- a point subjected to the linear flow on the two-dimensional torus,

- a rotation on the circle,

and that they “digitize” the half-lines of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with irrational slopes.

(We recall that a topological dynamical system is
minimal
when every point has a dense trajectory. In the next chapter, we will discuss the importance of the minimal and irrational assumptions.)

We first define the dynamical and geometrical systems mentioned above, and explain how to encode them with infinite words.

Billiard words.

Let
x\in[0,1)^{2}
and
\theta\in(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{2}
. The billiard word
w=w_{\mathrm{billiard}}(x,\theta)
is the infinite binary word encoding the trajectory of a billiard ball, initially located at position
x
, and launched with a momentum
\theta
, on the square table
[0,1]^{2}
. Its
n
-th letter is defined by:

w[n]=\begin{cases}1\text{ if the $n$-th edge hit by the ball is vertical,}\\
\text{2 if the $n$-th edge hit by the ball is horizontal.}\end{cases}

(Since it will not be useful in this lecture, we choose not to encode trajectories that hit corners.)
Example: In Figure
1.1
, the billiard word starts with
w_{\mathrm{billiard}}(x,\theta)=12112
…

Digitization of straight lines.

Again, let
x\in[0,1)^{2}
and
\theta\in(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{2}
. Denote by
\mathcal{G}=\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}\cup\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}
the integer grid of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The
cutting sequence

w=w_{line}(x,\theta)
is the infinite binary word that digitizes the half-line
\mathcal{D}
parameterized by
x+t\theta
,
t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Its
n
-th letter is defined by

w[n]=\begin{cases}1\text{ if the $n$-th intersection of the line $\mathcal{D}$ with the grid $\mathcal{G}$ is on a vertical branch,}\\
\text{2 otherwise.}\end{cases}

(We do not digitize the lines that pass through a grid intersection.)
Example: The half-line of Figure 2
1.1
is digitized by an infinite word which starts with
w_{line}(x,\theta)=12112...
. Interpretation: if we interpret a letter
1
as a unit step to the right, and the letter
2
as a unit step upwards, the word
w
is the “staircase” that best represents the line
\mathcal{D}
.

Coding a linear flow on the torus.

Let

The next theorem establishes the connection between these dynamical and geometrical systems, and Sturmian words.

Theorem 1.29
.

Let
w
be a standard Sturmian word with letter frequencies
(f_{1},f_{2})

\begin{array}[]{rcl}w&=&w_{\mathrm{billiard}}(0,(f_{1},f_{2}))\\
&=&w_{\mathrm{line}}(0,(f_{1},f_{2}))\\
&=&w_{\mathrm{flow}}(0,(f_{1},f_{2}))\\
&=&w_{\mathrm{rotation}}(f_{2},f_{2}).\end{array}

We conclude this chapter with two remarks and the proof of Theorem

Remark 1.31
.

We recall from Theorem
1.18
that the letter frequencies of a Sturmian word satisfy
f_{1}/f_{2}\notin\mathbb{Q}
. This condition is necessary and sufficient to ensure the minimality of the square billiard, and the minimality of linear flow on the torus. It is also equivalent to asking
f_{2}\notin\mathbb{Q}
(due to the equalities
f_{1}/f_{2}=(1-f_{2})/f_{2})=1/f_{2}-1
), and thus, equivalent to asking the minimality of the circle rotation.

Remark 1.32
.

We almost have the set equality

\{\text{Sturmian words}\}=\{w_{\mathrm{rotation}}(y,\alpha)\;|\;y\in[0,1),\alpha\in[0,1)\backslash\mathbb{Q}\}.

Indeed, the reverse inclusion is true, while the direct inclusion is almost true: for every possible pair of letter frequencies
(f_{1},f_{2})
, only countably many Sturmian words are missing (see Exercise
1.34
at the end of the chapter).

1.5
Motivations

The first chapter of this course was devoted to the study of Sturmian words, which form a remarkable class of binary infinite words. As a brief reminder:

•

The
complexity
of an infinite word
w\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
is the function
p_{w}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
that counts, for each integer
n
, the number of factors (i.e., subwords) of length
n
in
w
.

•

A celebrated theorem by M. Morse and G. Hedlund from 1938 asserts that an infinite word is eventually periodic if and only if there exists an integer
n
for which
p_{w}(n)\leq n
. Therefore, every non eventually-periodic word must satisfy
p_{w}(n)\geq n+1
for all
n
.

•

There exist infinite words whose complexity is exactly
p_{w}(n)=n+1
for all
n
; they are called
Sturmian words
.

•

Sturmian words are written over the binary alphabet (indeed, they satisfy
p_{w}(1)=2
). They exhibit remarkable interactions with arithmetic, dynamics, and geometry. More precisely, they are intimately connected to the
continued fraction
, and can equivalently be defined as the symbolic codings of irrational rotations, irrational trajectories on a square billiard table, minimal exchanges of two intervals, and digitizations of straight lines with an irrational slope.

Therefore, it is natural to wonder to what extent these satisfying results can be extended to larger alphabets.

Question 1.35
.

Let
d\geq 3
. Does there exist a
d
-ary analogue of the Morse–Hedlund theorem, that is, a theorem guaranteeing that, below a given complexity bound, infinite
d
-ary words are “trivial”?

Question 1.36
.

If so, can we dynamically and arithmetically characterize, when
d\geq 3
, the set of non-trivial
d
-ary infinite words with minimal complexity?

The aim of this second chapter is to convince the reader that the Theorem of Tijdeman provides a very satisfying answer to Question
1.35
, and that Question
1.36
, which is essentially open at the time of writing, deserves further investigation.

1.6
Examples of
d
-ary generalizations of Sturmian words

Sturmian words can be generalized to
d
-ary alphabets in multiple ways:

1)

From a combinatorial point of view, by imposing conditions on the set of special factors, or as limits of compositions by specific substitutions over the
d
-letter alphabet;

2)

From an arithmetic point of view, as words generated by
d-1
-dimensional continued fraction algorithms (note that there exist several equally legitimate choices for the continued fraction, which give rise to several classes of
d
-ary words);

3)

From a geometric or dynamical point of view, as the coding of billiard trajectories on a polygonal or hypercubic table, or as the coding of a rotation of the torus
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}/\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}
, or as the coding of an exchange of
d
intervals,
etc
.

These constructions give rise to various families of well-studied
d
-ary infinite words, such as
Arnoux–Rauzy words

[
6
]
,
episturmian words

[
21
,
27
,
26
]
,
words associated with the Cassaigne–Selmer continued fraction algorithm

[
15
]
,
dendric words

[
10
]
,
S-adic words

[
33
, Chapter 12]
,
[
11
]
,
natural codings of interval exchange transformations

[
29
,
37
,
25
,
20
]
,
hypercubic billiard words
[
5
,
7
,
9
]
, etc.
They are all considered as generalizations of Sturmian words.

Interestingly, these
d
-ary generalizations of Sturmian words do not all have the same complexity function. For example, on the ternary alphabet, the complexity of Arnoux–Rauzy and Cassaigne–Selmer words is
n\mapsto 2n+1
, the complexity of non Arnoux–Rauzy episturmian words is strictly lower than
n\mapsto 2n+1
, and the complexity of almost all cubic billiard words is
n\mapsto n^{2}+n+1
.

1.7
What is the minimal complexity for a
d
-ary word? (Discussion on what “minimal” should mean.)

From now on, and throughout Chapters 2 and 3, when we say that an infinite word
w
is a “
d
-ary infinite word”, we mean that
d
distinct letters actually appear in
w
. Specifically, we require that
p_{w}(1)=d
.

The next theorem asserts that the constant function
p:n\mapsto d
is the absolute minimal complexity for a
d
-ary infinite word.

Theorem 1.37
.

Let
d\geq 1
. Let
w\in\{1,\ldots,d\}^{\mathbb{N}}
be an infinite word with
p_{w}(1)=d
. Then
p_{w}(n)\geq d
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
. Moreover, there exists a
d
-ary infinite word
w
such that
p_{w}(n)=d
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
.

Proof.

The first assertion follows from the equality
p_{w}(1)=d
and the fact that complexity
p_{w}
is a non-decreasing function. For the second assertion, it suffices to check that the periodic word
w=(12\ldots d)^{\omega}
is a
d
-ary infinite word with the desired complexity.
∎

However, Theorem
1.37
is of limited interest: by taking
d=2
, we do not retrieve the Morse–Hedlund theorem that we wish to extend: “the minimal complexity function for a non eventually-periodic binary word is
n\mapsto n+1
”.
In our research for a generalization of this theorem, we need at least to keep restricting ourselves to non eventually-periodic
d
-ary words. As we will see in the following paragraphs, this restriction is not even sufficient.

The theorem below asserts that the linear function
n\mapsto n+d-1
is the minimal complexity for a non eventually-periodic
d
-ary word.

Theorem 1.38
.

Let
d\geq 2
. Let
w\in\{1,\ldots,d\}^{\mathbb{N}}
be a non eventually-periodic word with
p_{w}(1)=d
. Then
p_{w}(n)\geq n+d-1
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
. Moreover, there exists a non eventually-periodic
d
-ary word
w
such that
p_{w}(n)=n-d-1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
.

Proof.

Since
w
is non eventually-periodic, it must admit at least one right-special factor of each length; therefore, its complexity function is strictly increasing. Since
p_{w}(1)=d
, an immediate induction yields
p_{w}(n)\geq n+d-1
for every
n\geq 1
.

For the second assertion, let
w_{0}
be a Sturmian word over the binary alphabet
\{d-1,d\}
, and consider the
d
-ary infinite word obtained as the concatenation
w:=12\ldots(d-2)w_{0}
. Since
w_{0}
is Sturmian,
w
is non-eventually periodic and has the desired complexity function
n\mapsto n+d-1
. Indeed, for every
n\geq 1
, the word
w
admits
d-2
factors that start with the letters
1,2,\ldots,d-2
, respectively, and
n+1
factors that stem from the Sturmian word
w_{0}
, and are written with the sole letters
d-1
and
d
.
∎

The reader may be disappointed by the construction made in the proof above: we simply picked a Sturmian word, and prefixed it by some additional letters in order to make it fit the definition of “
d
-ary word”. These additional letters do not even reappear in
w
. We are far away from a satisfactory generalization of Sturmian words.

To exclude these cumbersome examples, we may require
that every letter occurs infinitely many times in
w
. We can even take an extra precaution and require that
every factor
occurs infinitely many times in
w
, with bounded gaps. We recall that this property is called
uniform recurrence
, and that Sturmian words are uniformly recurrent (establishing this property was the aim of Exercise
1.17
in Chapter 1).

Question 1.39
.

Does there exist a non-eventually periodic, uniformly recurrent
d
-ary word
w
, with complexity
n\mapsto n+d-1
?

The answer is ‘yes’, as evidenced by the following exercise.

Exercise 1.40
.

Let

the image of

by the substitution

1) Prove that

. (Hint: prove that

by studying right-special factors of

d
-letter alphabet, for

.

Again, the construction made in Exercise

is frustrating: the word

Question 1.41
.

Does there exist a non-eventually periodic, uniformly recurrent word, with complexity
n\mapsto n+d-1
, which is not an artificial distortion of Sturmian words?

The answer is ‘no’. The next theorem asserts that all non eventually-periodic, uniformly recurrent
d
-ary words with complexity
n\mapsto n+d-1
can be constructed as images of Sturmian words by substitutions similar to that of Exercise
1.40
.

Theorem 1.42

(S. Ferenczi and C. Mauduit, 1997; see
[
23
]
, Lemma 4)
.

Let
d\geq 3
, and
w
be an infinite word. The word
w
is recurrent, with complexity
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}\mapsto n+d-1
if, and only if, there exist:

-

an integer
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

-

a Sturmian word
w_{0}\in\{a,b\}^{\mathbb{N}}
,

-

a partition of the alphabet
\{1,\ldots,d\}=\mathcal{A}\sqcup\mathcal{B}\sqcup\mathcal{C}
where
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{N_{\mathcal{A}}}\}
,
\mathcal{B}=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{N_{\mathcal{B}}}\}
and
\mathcal{C}=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{N_{\mathcal{C}}}\}
, with both
\mathcal{C}\neq\emptyset

\mathcal{A}\sqcup\mathcal{B}\neq\emptyset

such that

, where
S
denotes the shift operator and
\sigma
is the substitution defined by

a\mapsto c_{1}\ldots c_{N_{\mathcal{C}}}a_{1}\ldots a_{N_{\mathcal{A}}},\hskip 14.22636ptb\mapsto c_{1}\ldots c_{N_{\mathcal{C}}}b_{1}\ldots b_{N_{\mathcal{B}}}.

Furthermore, in this case, the word

is uniformly recurrent.

(It seems that this theorem has been previously established for ternary alphabets by P. Alessandri in
[

]
, see also
[

Therefore, in the literature, the infinite words with complexity

.

1.8
The condition of rational independence of letter frequencies.

In the previous section, we saw that the non
eventual-periodicity condition, which is very efficient to exclude trivial words on the binary alphabet, is too weak to eliminate them on larger alphabets.
The aim of this section is to convince the reader that, on the
d
-letter alphabets, for arbitrary
d\geq 1
, a good condition to ask is the
rational independence
of the letter frequencies.

Let us recall that the real numbers
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
are “rationally independent” when none of them can be written as a linear combination of the others with rational coefficients or, equivalently, when the dimension of the linear space they spanned over the field
\mathbb{Q}
is
d
.
The conviction that, when dealing with arbitrary size alphabets, the condition of rational independence of letter frequencies should replace the non eventually-periodicity is motivated by the following four facts.

Fact 1. The interesting part of the Morse–Hedlund theorem (namely: an infinite word with a low complexity is eventually periodic) can be formulated in terms of rational dependence of the letter frequencies.

Specifically, we have:

Proposition 1.43
.

Let
w\in\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. If there exists an integer
n\geq 1
such that
p_{w}(n)\leq n
, then
w
has rationally dependent letter frequencies.

Proof.

Under this assumption, the Morse–Hedlund theorem (Chapter 1, Theorem
1.3
) guarantees that the word
w
is eventually periodic; let us write it
w=u\cdot p^{\omega}
. One readily checks that the frequencies of the letters
1
and
2
in
w
are equal to those in its periodic suffix
p^{\omega}
, that is
f_{1}=|p|_{1}/|p|
and
f_{2}=|p|_{2}/|p|
. These (rational) numbers are obviously rationally dependent.
∎

Fact 2. Sturmian words have rationally independent letter frequencies, and it is one of their central properties.

We recall from Chapter
1
that this property first emerges through their connection with the continued fraction. Specifically, Theorem
1.18
asserts that the ratio of the letter frequencies
f_{1}/f_{2}
of any Sturmian word is an irrational number, which is equivalent to asserting that
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are rationally independent. This property further manifests itself in all geometrical and dynamical characterizations of Sturmian words, as highlighted by Remark
1.31
: Sturmian words encode
minimal
rotations of the circle,
minimal
square billiard trajectories,
minimal
linear flows on the two-dimensional torus, and half-lines of the plane with an
irrational
slope.

Finally, it is worth noticing that, by consequence of their definition and of Proposition
1.43
, Sturmian words are, among the set of binary words with rationally independent letter frequencies, exactly those with minimal complexity.

Fact 3. Classes of words which are commonly considered as good combinatorial or dynamical generalizations of Sturmian words also have rationally independent letter frequencies.

It is the case, for example, of the words encoding minimal trajectories in an hypercubic billiard table (see, for example,
[
2
]
), but also of Arnoux–Rauzy words
[
3
,
22
]
, strict episturmian words
[
4
, Chapter 4]
, and Cassaigne–Selmer words
[
14
,
15
]
.

Fact 4. No
d
-ary quasi-sturmian word, for
d\geq 3
, has rationally independent letter frequencies.

It will be a consequence of the theorem of Tijdeman presented in the next section.
In the particular case of recurrent
d
-ary words with complexity
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}\mapsto n+d-1
, this fact can alternatively be derived from Theorem
1.42
, as in the exercise below.

Exercise 1.44
.

Let
d\geq 3
. Let
w
be a recurrent
d
-ary word with complexity
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}\mapsto n+d-1
. Prove that
w
admits letter frequencies, and that these letter frequencies span a rational linear space of dimension
2
.

Now that the
rational independence of the letter frequencies
appears as a reasonable replacement for the
non eventually-periodic
condition when the alphabet is of arbitrary size, a new question arises.

Question 1.45
.

Let
d\geq 3
. What is the minimal complexity for a
d
-ary word with rationally independent letter frequencies?

This question was fully answered by R. Tijdeman in 1999.

1.9
A theorem by Tijdeman

Unfortunately, Tijdeman’s answer has remained mostly unnoticed (it is for example missing in
[
8
]
) until 2022, when it was rediscovered by J. Cassaigne and the author of these lecture notes.

Tijdeman’s theorem states that the minimal complexity for an infinite
d
-ary word with rationally independent letter frequencies is the linear function
n\mapsto(d-1)n+1

Theorem 1.46

(Tijdeman, 1999,
[
35
]
)
.

Let
d\geq 1
. Let
w\in\{1,\ldots,d\}^{\mathbb{N}}
be an infinite word with
p_{w}(1)=d
and rationally independent letter frequencies. Then
p_{w}(n)\geq(d-1)n+1
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
. Moreover, there exists a
d
-ary infinite word
w
, with rationally independent letter frequencies, and such that
p_{w}(n)=(d-1)n+1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
.

Of course, when
d=2
, we retrieve the Proposition
1.43
that motivated our investigation.

1.10
Towards a characterization of
d
-ary infinite words with minimal complexity in Tijdeman’s sense.

The aim of this subsection is to initiate the study of infinite words that achieve Tijdeman’s minimal complexity. First, we claim that they are all recurrent.

Proposition 1.47
.

Let
d\geq 1
, and let
w
be an infinite word with complexity
p_{w}(n)=(d-1)n+1
and rationally independent letter frequencies. Then
w
is recurrent.

Proof.

Let
w
be an infinite word with complexity
p_{w}(n)=(d-1)n+1
and rationally independent letter frequencies
f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}
. We proceed by contradiction. Assume that
w
is not recurrent, and denote by
u
a factor that appears finitely many times in
w
. Let
w^{\prime}
be a suffix of
w
that does not contain the factor
u
. Observe that
w^{\prime}
is still an infinite
d
-ary word (indeed, if a letter is missing in
w^{\prime}
, then its frequency in
w
was
0
, which is impossible), and that the letter frequencies of
w^{\prime}
are exactly those of
w
(indeed, the frequencies of the letters do not depend on a finite prefix). Furthermore, for
n=|u|
, we have
p_{w^{\prime}}(n)\leq p_{w}(n)-1<(d-1)n+1
. Therefore,
w^{\prime}
is an infinite
d
-ary word with rationally independent letter frequencies and a complexity smaller than the minimal bound given by Tijdeman’s Theorem
1.46
. We have reached a contradiction.
∎

It is natural to ask which classical classes of infinite words are optimal for Tijdeman’s theorem

Question 1.48
.

Let
d\geq 3
. Which
d
-ary infinite words have rationally independent letter frequencies, and complexity
p_{w}:n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}\mapsto(d-1)n+1
?

We recall that for
d=1
and
d=2
, these classes of words are completely understood.

•

For
d=1
, it is the single word
\{w=111111111111...\}
. Indeed, it is the unique
1
-ary word, and it trivially has rationally independent letter frequencies and the desired complexity
n\mapsto 1
.

•

For
d=2
, these are exactly Sturmian words. Indeed, Sturmian words are, by definition, the infinite words with complexity
n\mapsto n+1
; moreover, their letter frequencies are rationally independent by Theorem
1.18
.

By contrast, to this day, only partial answers are known when
d\geq 3
.

•

For
d=3
, we know that the class of words with Tijdeman’s minimal complexity contains at least all Arnoux–Rauzy words, all Cassaigne–Selmer words, and almost all codings of interval exchange transformations on three intervals: indeed, these are ternary words with complexity
n\mapsto 2n+1
, and rationally independent letter frequencies.

•

More generally, for arbitrary alphabet size
d
, the class of words with Tijdeman’s minimal complexity contains at least all strict episturmian words, and almost all codings of interval exchange transformations on
d
intervals.

Interestingly, all the aforementioned classes of words (namely: Sturmian, Arnoux–Rauzy, Cassaigne–Selmer and strict episturmian words, but also the codings of regular interval exchange transformations) belong to a meta-class of infinite words known as
dendric words
(which will be defined in the next chapter).
In fact, it will be a by-product of our alternative proof of Tijdeman’s theorem that all infinite words with minimal complexity in Tijdeman’s sense are dendric.

Theorem 1.49

(Andrieu, Cassaigne, 2022,
unpublished
)
.

Let
d\geq 1
. Every infinite
d
-ary word with rationally independent letter frequencies and complexity
n\mapsto(d-1)n+1
is dendric.

Since every
d
-ary dendric word has complexity
n\mapsto(d-1)n+1
(see
[
10
]
or Proposition
1.74
in Chapter 3), we deduce the following characterization.

Corollary 1.50
.

Let
d\geq 1
. The set of infinite
d
-ary words with rationally independent letter frequencies and complexity
n\mapsto(d-1)n+1
, and the set of
d
-ary dendric words with rationally independent letter frequencies, coincide.

Unfortunately, not all dendric words have rationally independent letter frequencies. Notably, some codings of regular
d
-interval exchange transformations do not: see, for example,
[
36
, Example 3.3]
. Therefore, the question of characterizing infinite
d
-ary words with Tijdeman’s minimal complexity, in the spirit of the elegant combinatorial, geometrical and dynamical characterizations of Sturmian words remains an open problem for
d\geq 3
at the time of writing.

1.11
The original and the strengthened versions of Tijdeman’s theorem

The original theorem by Tijdeman is, in fact, stronger than the Theorem
1.46
that we stated. It formalizes and
quantifies
the idea according to which, if the complexity of an infinite word
w
does not grow fast enough, then
w
is
algebraically poor
. In this last section, we present the complete, original version of Tijdeman’s theorem, and we introduce a new, strengthened version of it.

To state the original form of Tijdeman’s theorem, in which the existence of the letter frequencies is not even required, we first need to introduce some notation. Given a
d
-ary infinite word
w
, we consider the sequence

\Big(\frac{1}{n}(|\mathrm{pref}_{n}(w)|_{1},\ldots,|\mathrm{pref}_{n}(w)|_{d})\Big)_{n}

which lives in the compact set
[0,1]^{d}
. If this sequence converges, the limit is, by definition, the frequencies of the letters in
w
. If not, we denote by
F_{w}
the set of all its subsequential limits:

F_{w}:=\Big\{(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}\;\Big|\;\begin{array}[]{c}\text{there exists an increasing sequence $(n_{l})_{l}\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ s.t.}\\
\forall\,i\in\{1,\ldots,d\},\;|\mathrm{pref}_{n_{l}}(w)|_{i}/n_{l}\underset{l\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}f_{i}\end{array}\Big\}.

(1.3)

We now define the integer
\delta_{w}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
as the
minimal degree of irrationality
of the letters in
w
:

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\delta_{w}:=\min\big\{\dim\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})\,|\,(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})\in F_{w}\big\}.\end{array}

Theorem 1.51

(Tijdeman 1999, original version)
.

Let
w
be an infinite
d
-ary infinite word. Denote by
\delta_{w}
the minimal degree of irrationality of the letters in
w
. Then, for every
n\geq 1
,

p_{w}(n)\geq(\delta_{w}-1)(n-1)+d.

In fact, Tijdeman’s original theorem can be strengthened by replacing the minimal degree
\delta_{w}
of irrationality of the letters by the
maximal degree
of irrationality of the letters:

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\Delta_{w}:=\max\big\{\dim\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})\,|\,(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})\in F_{w}\big\}.\end{array}

(1.4)

In the Exercise
1.55
below, we exhibit an infinite word
w
for which the minimal and maximal degrees of irrationality of the letters differ.

Theorem 1.52

(Andrieu, Cassaigne, 2022,
unpublished
)
.

Let
w
be an infinite
d
-ary word. Denote by
\Delta_{w}
the maximal degree of irrationality of the letters in
w
. Then, for every
n\geq 1
,

p_{w}(n)\geq(\Delta_{w}-1)(n-1)+d.

Theorem
1.52
will be established in Chapter 3.

We conclude this chapter by two remarks and an exercise.

Remark 1.53
.

If
w
admits frequencies of letters, and if furthermore these frequencies are rationally independent, both Theorems
1.51
and
1.52
boil down to Theorem
1.46
. Indeed, in this case, we have
\Delta_{w}=\delta_{w}=d
, from which we immediately derive

(\Delta_{w}-1)(n-1)+d=(d-1)n+1.

Remark 1.54
.

It will follow from its proof that Theorem
1.52
can be equivalently stated as follows: “if there exists an integer
k\geq 1
and a length
m\geq 0
such that
p_{w}(m+1)-p_{w}(m)\leq k-1
, then
\Delta_{w}\leq k
”, or equivalently, “
\Delta_{w}\leq\min_{m\geq 0}\,\{p_{w}(m+1)-p_{w}(m)+1\}
”.
This formulation is to be understood as the
d
-ary counterpart of “a word is eventually periodic if and only if its complexity function is locally constant” which is, as we discussed in Remark
1.4
of Chapter 1, equivalent to the Morse–Hedlund theorem.

1.12
Introduction

In this third and last chapter, we present an alternative proof of Tijdeman’s original theorem from 1999. This proof was obtained, but unpublished, by J. Cassaigne and the author in 2022. It actually establishes a slightly stronger result than Tijdeman’s original theorem, namely:

Theorem 1.56

(Andrieu, Cassaigne; also numbered Theorem
1.52
in Chapter 2)
.

Let
w
be an infinite
d
-ary word. Denote by
\Delta_{w}
the maximal degree of irrationality of the letters in
w
. Then, for every
n\geq 1
,

p_{w}(n)\geq(\Delta_{w}-1)(n-1)+d.

We recall that in the theorem above, the frequencies of the letters in
w
are not required to exist; instead, we work with the set
F_{w}
of their “pseudo-frequencies”, that is, the set of all subsequential limits of the sequence

(\frac{1}{n}(|\mathrm{pref}_{n}(w)|_{1},\ldots,|\mathrm{pref}_{n}(w)|_{d}))_{n}.

The “maximal degree of irrationality of the letters” is then, by definition,

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\Delta_{w}:=\max\big\{\dim\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})\,|\,(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})\in F_{w}\big\}.\end{array}

Our proof relies on linear algebra (notably, the rank–nullity theorem and the study of the dimension of some eigenspaces) applied to a particular rectangular matrix, which we call the “flow matrix”. Such matrices are closely related to two classical and interconnected objects in combinatorics on words:
Rauzy graphs
and
extension graphs
. By contrast, Tijdeman’s original proof is mostly combinatorial.

Furthermore, the technique developed in this alternative proof enables us to establish the following new result, representing a step forward in the characterization of
d
-ary words with minimal complexity in Tijdeman’s sense.

Theorem 1.57
.

(Andrieu, Cassaigne; also numbered Theorem
1.49
in Chapter 2)
.
Let
d\geq 1
. Every infinite
d
-ary word with rationally independent letter frequencies and complexity
n\mapsto(d-1)n+1
is dendric.

The chapter is divided into four sections. In Section
1.13
, we introduce the classical notion of Rauzy graph, and the new notion of flow matrix. In Section
1.14
, we prove Theorem
1.56
. In Section
1.15
, we compare our proof with that of Tijdeman. Finally, in Section
1.16
, we recall the classical notions of extension graphs and dendricity, and use our proof of Theorem
1.56
to establish Theorem
1.57
.

1.13
Preliminaries: definitions of Rauzy graphs and flow matrices

1.13.1
Rauzy graphs

Rauzy graphs were introduced, among other excellent ideas, by Rauzy in
[
34
]
. They are a well-studied and useful tool in combinatorics on words.
By definition, the “Rauzy graph” of an infinite word
w
for a given length
n\in\mathbb{N}
is the directed graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
whose:

•

vertices are the factors of length
n
of
w
(
V:=\mathcal{L}_{n}(w)
);

•

edges are the factors of length
n+1
of
w
(
E:=\mathcal{L}_{n+1}(w)
). Specifically, the edge labeled by
v\in\mathcal{L}_{n+1}
joins the word formed by the first
n
letters of
v
to the word formed by its last
n
letters.

Example 1.58
.

The Rauzy graph of the eventually periodic word

w=2(010)^{\omega}=2010010010010010...

for the length
n=1
is:
0
1
2
00
01
10
20

Indeed, one readily checks that
\mathcal{L}_{1}(w)=\{0,1,2\}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}(w)=\{00,01,10,20\}
.

Remark 1.59
.

By definition, a directed graph is “semi-connected” if, for every pair of vertices
u
and
u^{\prime}
, there exists a path from
u
to
u^{\prime}
or a path from
u^{\prime}
to
u
. The Rauzy graph of every infinite word is semi-connected. Indeed, when sliding a window of size
n
from left to right along
w
, we follow a path along its length-
n
Rauzy graph that visits every vertex.

We encourage the reader who is unfamiliar with Rauzy graphs to consider the following two exercises.

.

By definition, a directed graph is “strongly connected” if for every pair of vertices
u
and
u^{\prime}
, there exists a path going from
u
to

.

are strongly connected if and only if
w
is recurrent.

uniform
recurrence of an infinite word
w
in terms of its Rauzy graphs?

.

.

4
. (We recall that the Fibonacci word was defined in Chapter 1, Exercise
1.23
.)

4
on this Rauzy graph?

n\in\mathbb{N}
and
w
be an arbitrary Sturmian word. Describe the shape of the Rauzy graph of
w
for length

.

1.13.2
Flow matrices

Let
w
be an infinite word, and
n
a nonnegative integer.
By definition, the “flow matrix” of
w
for the length
n
is the rectangular matrix
M
, indexed by
u\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(w)
and
v\in\mathcal{L}_{n+1}(w)
, and defined by:

M_{u,v}:=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $v$ starts but does not end with $u$,}\\
-1&\text{ if $v$ ends but does not start with $u$,}\\
0&\text{ if $v$ neither starts nor ends with $u$, or starts and ends with $u$.}\end{cases}

Its size is
p_{w}(n)\times p_{w}(n+1)
, which is also equal, with the notation of the Rauzy graphs, to
|V|\times|E|
. To represent it explicitly, we fix an arbitrary order on the sets
\mathcal{L}_{n}(w)
and
\mathcal{L}_{n+1}(w)
. In this course, we always choose the lexicographic order.

Example 1.62
.

Pursuing Example
1.58
, the flow matrix, for the length
n=1
, of the eventually periodic word
w=2(010)^{\omega}=2010010010010010...
is:

M=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&-1&-1\\
0&-1&1&0\\
0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix}

It is easy to derive the flow matrix from the knowledge of the same-parameter Rauzy graph: on the row indexed by a vertex
u\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(w)
, each incoming edge contributes
-1
, and each outgoing edge contributes
+1
.

The name
flow matrix
is inspired by Kirchhoff’s junction rule for hydraulic and electric circuits. The flow matrix is different from the classical
adjacency matrix
in graph theory, which is always a square matrix.

Remark 1.63
.

The flow matrix is to be understood as the difference between what could be called the “right- and left-extension matrices” of
w
for the length
n
:
M=R-L
, where

R_{u,v}:=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $v$ starts with $u$,}\\
0&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

is the matrix representing the right-extensions of the length-
n
factors of
w
(indeed, the right-extensions of
u\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(w)
are precisely the factors
v\in\mathcal{L}_{n+1}(w)
such that
R_{u,v}=1
), and where

L_{u,v}:=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $v$ ends with $u$,}\\
0&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

is, similarly, the matrix representing the left-extensions of the length-
n
factors of
w
.

In particular, it follows from their definitions that:

- Every column of
R
and of
L
contains exactly one entry equal to
1
.

- Every row of
R
contains at least one entry equal to
1
. If
w
is recurrent, every row of
L
also contains at least one entry equal to
1
. By contrast, if
w
is not recurrent, for
n
sufficiently large,
L
contains exactly one row full of zeros (as in Example
1.62
).

- A factor
u\in\mathcal{L}(w)
is right-special (resp. left-special) if and only if the row indexed by
u
in the matrix
R
(resp.
L
) contains several entries equal to
1
.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.56
.

1.14
Proof of Theorem
1.56

1.14.1
General structure of the proof

Let
d\geq 1
. Let
w
be an infinite
d
-ary word (we recall that this implies
p_{w}(1)=d
). We proceed by contradiction and minimality.
By contradiction, assume that there exists a length
n\geq 1
such that

p_{w}(n)<(\Delta_{w}-1)(n-1)+d,

(1.5)

where
\Delta_{w}
denotes the maximal degree of irrationality of the letters in
w
.
(Note that for the inequality to hold, we must have
n\geq 2
.)
Let
m+1
be the minimal length
n
for which the inequality (
1.5
) holds. By minimality of
m+1
, we have the twofold estimates

\begin{cases}p_{w}(m)\geq(\Delta_{w}-1)(m-1)+d,\\
p_{w}(m+1)<(\Delta_{w}-1)m+d\end{cases}

(1.6)

from which we deduce that

p_{w}(m+1)-p_{w}(m)\leq\Delta_{w}-2.

(1.7)

By definition of
\Delta_{w}
, there exists a tuple of real numbers
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}
(called
pseudo-frequencies of letters
) such that
\dim\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})=\Delta_{w}
, and

\Big(\frac{1}{n_{l}}(|\mathrm{pref}_{n_{l}}(w)|_{1},\ldots,|\mathrm{pref}_{n_{l}}(w)|_{d})\Big)_{l}\longrightarrow_{l\rightarrow\infty}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}),

(1.8)

for a certain increasing sequence of integers
(n_{l})_{l}
. Up to extracting a subsequence from the sequence
(n_{l})_{l}
, we can also assume that every factor
v
of length
m+1
(for the parameter
m
in the estimates (
1.6
)) admits a pseudo-frequency
f_{v}
, that is

\Big(\frac{|\mathrm{pref}_{n_{l}}(w)|_{v}}{n_{l}}\Big)_{l}\longrightarrow_{l\rightarrow\infty}f_{v}.

(1.9)

The goal of the proof is to show that the pseudo-frequencies of length-
m+1
factors span a rational linear space of dimension at most
\Delta_{w}-1
, and deduce that, in turn, the pseudo-frequencies of the letters
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})
also span a rational linear space of dimension at most
\Delta_{w}-1
. This will contradict the choice made in (
1.8
) for the family of pseudo-frequencies
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})
.

Denote by
M
the flow matrix of the infinite word
w
for the specific length
m
. The core of the proof consists in studying the kernel of
M
, viewed first as a matrix with rational coefficients and then as a matrix with real coefficients. It is divided into the following three lemmas, which are proved in the next three subsections.

Lemma 1.64
.

Let
M
be the flow matrix of the infinite word
w
for the length
m
. Then the vector of pseudo-frequencies of the length-
m+1
factors satisfies

(f_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)}\in\ker_{\mathbb{R}}(M).

Lemma 1.65
.

Let
M
be the flow matrix of the infinite word
w
for the length
m
.
Then the dimension of the kernel of
M
satisfies

\dim\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M)\leq\Delta_{w}-1.

We recall that
\ker_{\mathbb{R}}(M)
and
\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M)
denote the sets of all vectors
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)}
and
x\in\mathbb{Q}^{\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)}
, respectively, such that
Mx=0
. They form linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)}
and
\mathbb{Q}^{\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)}
, respectively.

Lemma 1.66
.

The rational linear space spanned by the pseudo-frequencies of letters satisfies

\dim\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})\leq\Delta_{w}-1.

If Lemma
1.66
holds, then Theorem
1.56
follows. Indeed, we have simultaneously
\dim\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})=\Delta_{w}
(by construction of the pseudo-frequencies of letters
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})
), and
\dim\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})<\Delta_{w}
. A contradiction.

1.14.2
Proof of Lemma
1.64

Let
u\in\mathcal{L}_{m}(w)
and
l\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since every occurrence of
u
in the infinite word
w\in\{1,\dots,d\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, except possibly the first one when
w
begins with
u
, is preceded by a letter, thus forming a factor
v
of length
m+1
, we have the general estimate:

|\mathrm{pref}_{n_{l}}(w)|_{u}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)\\
\text{ s.t. $v$ ends with $u$}\end{subarray}}|\mathrm{pref}_{n_{l}}(w)|_{v}\quad+\quad\begin{cases}1&\text{if $w$ starts with $u$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(1.10)

With a similar argument, we obtain the second estimate:

|\mathrm{pref}_{n_{l}}(w)|_{u}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)\\
\text{ s.t. $v$ starts with $u$}\end{subarray}}|\mathrm{pref}_{n_{l}}(w)|_{v}\quad+\quad\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\mathrm{pref}_{n_{l}}(w)$ ends with $u$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(1.11)

From these estimates and from the definition of the pseudo-frequencies of the length-
m+1
factors of
w
(
1.9
), we deduce that every factor
u
of length
m
also admits a pseudo-frequency
f_{u}
:

\Big(\frac{|\mathrm{pref}_{n_{l}}(w)|_{u}}{n_{l}}\Big)_{l}\longrightarrow_{l\rightarrow\infty}f_{u},

which satisfies the following conservation law:

f_{u}=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)\text{ s.t. $v$ starts with $u$}}f_{v}=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)\text{ s.t. $v$ ends with $u$}}f_{v}.

(1.12)

Remark 1.67
.

In an electric circuit shaped as the Rauzy graph of
w
for the length
m
, and in which the intensity of the electric current on the branch indexed by

is

, this conservation law is exactly the Kirchhoff’s junction rule.

The second equality in (
1.12

\sum_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)\text{ s.t. $v$ starts with $u$}}f_{v}-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)\text{ s.t. $v$ ends with $u$}}f_{v}=0

and is valid for every

.
Therefore, by definition of the flow matrix

M\cdot(f_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)}=0.

Since the vector
(f_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)}
has real entries a priori, we conclude that

(f_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)}\in\ker_{\mathbb{R}}(M).

Lemma
1.64
is proven.

Proof of Lemma
1.65

Let
M
be the flow matrix of the infinite word
w
for the length
m
. We recall that the flow matrix has entries in
\{-1,0,1\}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
, and has size
p_{w}(m)\times p_{w}(m+1)
.

By the rank–nullity theorem applied to
M
and its transpose, we have

\begin{cases}\dim\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M)=p_{m+1}(w)-\mathrm{rk}(M),\\
\mathrm{rk}(M)=\mathrm{rk}(M^{t})=p_{m}(w)-\dim\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M^{t});\end{cases}

from which we derive, using the estimate (
1.7
)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\dim\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M)&=&p_{w}(m+1)-p_{w}(m)+\dim\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M^{t})\\
&\leq&\Delta_{w}-2+\dim\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M^{t}).\end{array}

(1.13)

We are now going to prove that
\dim\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M^{t})=1
, or more precisely, that
\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M^{t})=\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}((1,\ldots,1)^{t})
. Once this is proven, the inequality above becomes

\dim\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M)\leq\Delta_{w}-1,

and establishes Lemma
1.65
.

First, by construction, the sum of the entries in any column of
M
equals
0
(it can also be seen as an immediate consequence of Remark
1.63
); therefore,
(1,\ldots,1)^{t}\in\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M^{t})
. Conversely, we want to prove that if
x\in\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M^{t})
, then all the coordinates of
x=(x_{u})_{u\in\mathcal{L}_{m}(w)}
are equal. (In other words, we want to prove that
\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M^{t})\subseteq\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}\{(1,\ldots,1)^{t}\}
.) Let
u\neq u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{m}(w)
. Since the Rauzy graph
\mathcal{G}
of
w
for the length
m
is semi-connected (as is the case of all Rauzy graphs of infinite words, see Remark
1.59
), there exists in
\mathcal{G}
a path from
u
to
u^{\prime}
, or from
u^{\prime}
to
u
. Say the path is from
u
to
u^{\prime}
. Denote by
v_{1},\ldots,v_{l}
the list of the consecutive edges that lead from
u
to
u^{\prime}
, and by
u_{0},\ldots,u_{l}
the list of vertices successively visited, including the endpoints. The path can be represented as follows:

u=u_{0}\underset{v_{1}}{\to}u_{1}\underset{v_{2}}{\to}u_{2}\underset{v_{3}}{\to}\cdots\to u_{l-1}\underset{v_{l}}{\to}u_{l}=u^{\prime}.

(1.14)

Since
u^{\prime}\neq u
, we may assume, after possibly shortening the path, that the vertices
u_{0},\ldots,u_{l}
are pairwise distinct.

Now, by definition of a Rauzy graph, for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,l\}
, the factor
v_{i}
starts with
u_{i-1}
and ends with
u_{i}
. Therefore, one finds exactly two nonzero entries in the row of

indexed by the factor

: an entry “
+1
” at position
u_{i}
, and an entry “
-1
” at position
u_{i-1}
, which are distinct. Finally, since

, we have
x_{u_{i}}-x_{u_{i-1}}=0
.
The equality

being true for every

, it propagates along the path (

), and we obtain the desired equality

. We thus proved that all the coordinates of

are equal, from which we deduce that
\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M^{t})
is contained in, and in fact equal to,
\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}((1,\ldots,1)^{t})
. Therefore,
\dim\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M^{t})=1
, and the equality (

) becomes

\dim\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M)\leq\Delta_{w}-1.

Lemma
1.65
is proven.

1.14.4
Proof of Lemma
1.66

For this proof, we advise the reader to keep track of the class (scalar, vector with rational or real entries, etc.) of each object.

Denote
\delta=\dim\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M)
(by Lemma
1.65
we already know that
\delta\leq\Delta_{w}-1
). Let
b_{1},\ldots,b_{\delta}\in\mathbb{Q}^{\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)}
be a basis of
\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M)
, that is, a basis of the kernel of
M
viewed as a matrix with
rational entries
.
The key point of the proof is the following general result from linear algebra: since
\mathbb{Q}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, the family of vectors
b_{1},\ldots,b_{\delta}
also forms a basis of
\ker_{\mathbb{R}}(M)
, that is, of
\ker(M)
viewed as a matrix with
real entries.
(For completeness, we recall this general result in the next subsection.) Therefore, since, by Lemma
1.64
, the pseudo-frequency vector
f:=(f_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)}
belongs to
\ker_{\mathbb{R}}(M)
, there exist real numbers
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

f=\sum_{i=1}^{\delta}\lambda_{i}b_{i}.

This vector equality is equivalent to the following
p_{m+1}(w)
scalar equalities: for every
v\in\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)
, we have

f_{v}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\delta}\lambda_{i}b_{i,v}

where
b_{i,v}
denotes the coordinate of the vector
b_{i}
indexed by
v
.
In this equality, we have
f_{v}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
b_{i,v}\in\mathbb{Q}
. Therefore, the real number

f_{v}
belongs to the rational linear space
\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\delta})
; and since the
\lambda_{i}
do not depend on the choice of

, it follows that

\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}((f_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)})\subseteq\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\delta}).

We now come back to the pseudo-frequencies

of the
letters
in

. With a counting argument similar to (

), it is easy to establish that

f_{j}=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)\text{ s.t. $v$ starts with $j$}}f_{v}\;,

for every
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
; from which we deduce that

f_{j}\in\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}((f_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)})

(still for every

), hence

\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})\subseteq\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}((f_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{m+1}(w)})\subseteq\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\delta}).

The above inclusions of linear spaces imply the inequality

\dim\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})\leq\dim\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\delta})\leq\delta.

Since
\delta=\dim\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M)\leq\Delta_{w}-1
by Lemma
1.65
, Lemma
1.66
is proven. The proof of Theorem
1.56
is complete.

1.14.5
Annex: A brief reminder of linear algebra

For the reader’s convenience, we prove the following general lemma, which played a crucial role in the proof of Lemma
1.66
.

Lemma 1.68
.

(i)
If the vectors
e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
are
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent, then they are also
\mathbb{R}
-linearly independent.

(ii)
Let
M\in\mathcal{M}_{m,n}(\mathbb{Q})
. If the vectors
e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
form a basis of
\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M)
, then they also form a basis of
\ker_{\mathbb{R}}(M)
.

Proof.

(i) First, suppose
d=n
. In this case, the
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent vectors
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
form a basis of
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
and therefore generate the canonical basis of
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
through rational linear combinations. Since the canonical basis of
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
coincides with the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it follows that every vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be expressed as a linear combination with real coefficients of the vectors

e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
. Hence,
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
form a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Now, in the general case
d\leq n
, we complete the set
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}
of
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent vectors into a basis
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
of
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
. By the argument above, this extended set forms a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, from which it follows that the vectors
e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}
are
\mathbb{R}
-linearly independent.

(ii) By the previous point, the family
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{d})
is
\mathbb{R}
-linearly independent. To show that it forms a basis of
\ker_{\mathbb{R}}(M)
, it suffices to verify that
\dim\ker_{\mathbb{R}}(M)=d
. Applying the rank–nullity theorem gives

\dim\ker_{\mathbb{R}}(M)=n-\mathrm{rk}(M);

and the rank of
M
is the same whether
M
is considered over
\mathbb{Q}
or
\mathbb{R}
. (This can be verified by observing that Gaussian elimination is unaffected by the ambient field.) Hence, applying rank–nullity over
\mathbb{Q}
yields

\dim\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M)=n-\mathrm{rk}(M),

and therefore

\dim\ker_{\mathbb{R}}(M)=\dim\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M)=d
.
This proves that
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{d})
forms a basis of
\ker_{\mathbb{R}}(M)
, completing the proof of Lemma
1.68
.∎

1.77
.

The proof of the first assertion is an immediate adaptation of that of Lemma
1.65
. We recall that it relies on the connectedness of
\mathcal{G^{\prime}}
(proven in Lemma
1.76
), from which we establish that
\ker_{\mathbb{Q}}(M^{\prime t})=\mathrm{Span}((1,\ldots,1)^{t})
. Then, a double application of the rank-nullity theorem yields the desired equality. We prove the second assertion, which can still be understood as a Kirchhoff’s junction rule on
\mathcal{G^{\prime}}
. Denote by
M^{\prime}[r]
the row of
M^{\prime}
indexed by
r\in V^{\prime}
. Our aim is to prove that for every
r\in V^{\prime}
,

M^{\prime}[r]\cdot(f_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{n+1}(w)}=0.

(1.16)

This equation is trivially satisfied for every
r\neq u_{1},u_{2}
since
M^{\prime}[r]
is also a row of
M
, the flow matrix of the original Rauzy graph
\mathcal{G}
for length
n
. It thus suffices to reuse Lemma
1.64
.
For
r=u_{1}
and
r=u_{2}
, the equation (
1.16
) comes from the following equalities:
for every
a\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, we have

f_{au}=\sum_{b\in\{1,\ldots,d\}}f_{aub};

and symmetrically, for every
b\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, we have

f_{ub}=\sum_{a\in\{1,\ldots,d\}}f_{aub}.

Since there exists no edge that connects
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, we have that
f_{aub}=0
if
(a,b)\in L_{1}\times R_{2}\cup L_{2}\times R_{1}
. Hence

\sum_{a\in L_{1}}f_{au}=\sum_{a\in L_{1}}\sum_{b\in R_{1}}f_{aub}=\sum_{b\in R_{1}}f_{ub},

(1.17)

and

\sum_{a\in L_{2}}f_{au}=\sum_{a\in L_{2}}\sum_{b\in R_{2}}f_{aub}=\sum_{b\in R_{2}}f_{ub}.

(1.18)

The equations (
1.17
) and (
1.18
) are respectively equivalent to
M^{\prime}[u_{1}]\cdot(f_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{n+1}(w)}=0
and
M^{\prime}[u_{2}]\cdot(f_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{n+1}(w)}=0
. Lemma
1.77
is proven.
∎

From Lemma
1.77
, with the exact same calculation as in Lemma
1.66
, we prove that the letter frequencies of
w
span a rational linear space of dimension at most
d-1
. We reach the desired contradiction. The proof of Theorem
1.57
is complete.
∎
