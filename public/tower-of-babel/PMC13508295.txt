“We are blind to quite a lot of things”: perspectives of a communications curriculum enlisting persons with disability (PWD) as patient educators and its impact on medical professional identity formation
Authors: Vivien Lee, V Vien Lee, Anna Szücs, Jun Cong Goh, Jeffrey Jiang, José M Valderas, Victor W K Loh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAttitudinal barriers toward persons with disability (PWD) persist in healthcare systems, with lack of awareness and experience impeding optimal treatment of PWD. Medical professional identity formation, which can be described as the transformative integration of both clinical acumen and humanistic attitudes, needs to include exposure to and interactions with PWD. A communications workshop was devised for third-year medical students with PWD enlisted as patient educators. We aimed to understand students’ and tutors’ perspectives of the workshop, the meaning they gained from interacting with PWD, and how the workshop contributed to their professional identity formation.MethodsSemi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted with 13 students and 7 tutors, and analysed with thematic analysis following an inductive approach until reaching thematic saturation. Coding occurred independently, and researchers met to discuss their findings and integrate different perspectives and codes into a final set of themes and subthemes.ResultsThemes derived included how medical students and tutors started their medical education journey with an aspiration to care; how values that contribute to professional identity formation are acquired in a learning community (direct experience, modelling behaviour, reflection and through authentic conversation); the educational value of PWD as patient educators in medical training (the historical absence of disability in curriculum, tutors co-learning with students from PWD educators, the uncertainty of long-term impact); and the impact of the PWD educator patient voice in professional identity formation.ConclusionsThe PWD educator voice may develop sensitivity and empathy for PWD. First person narratives of their perspectives, challenges, and triumphs has the potential to challenge learners’ preconceived notions, strengthen confidence of learners in interacting with PWD, and promote social inclusiveness of PWD as a facet of ongoing student and clinician professional identity formation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09540-w.Keywords: Communications curriculum, Family Medicine, Patient Educator, Patient voice, Persons with disabilities, Person centredness, Professional identity formation, Social inclusion

Introduction

The United Nations convention of rights requires healthcare professionals to provide care of the same quality for people with disability (PWD) as to others [1]. Yet, healthcare systems are failing the more than a billion PWD worldwide [2–4]. Despite efforts to stem healthcare inequity, structural and attitudinal barriers for access to health and health care for this large segment of the population remain stubbornly ubiquitous [4, 5].

Healthcare practitioners encounter PWD in the course of work, yet historically, medical students have had minimal formal exposure to disability during training [6]. The call to strengthen disability training [2] has resonated with medical students, and has been heeded in medical training [7, 8]. Yet consideration of the needs and preferences of PWD in curricula are the exception rather than the norm for medical schools [9].

As PWD comprise 16% of the world’s population [2], holistic care for PWD should be integral to medical professional identity. Professional identity formation is the transformative integration of both clinical acumen and humanistic attitudes of a competent, compassionate physician with one’s core identity and values [10–15]. This occurs through the internalization of the characteristics, values, and norms of the profession through socialization with the “real world” of medicine [16–18]. Curricular design supporting professional identity formation include formal curricular elements such as ethics, communications, professionalism, medical humanities; and also informal or hidden curricular elements enabled by mentorship, role modelling and social symbolisation [19–21].

Patient educators, as experts of their experience [22, 23] have been found to add a rich, additional perspective to medical training [22, 24, 25]. The narrative of lived experience provides learners with deeper understanding of diseases and disabilities that are beyond the ability of textbooks and lecturers to teach. Open, respectful engagement with persons living with disabling conditions may engender sensitivity, empathy, and critical thinking on issues of equity and social justice in students [26]. Time for reflection, personal and shared, provides a setting where unexamined assumptions may be reconsidered, and professional values are built upon authentic experience [11, 13, 17, 27, 28].

In Singapore, public attitudes toward PWD are generally positive. While most of the general public vouch for greater inclusiveness, few are comfortable with direct interaction with PWD [29]. Misconceptions impair quality of care for PWD [30, 31]. Singapore’s Enabling Masterplan 2030 launched in 2022 outlines the city-state’s strategy for greater PWD enablement [32]. An early visible effect of the initiative include “persons with intellectual disability” courses organized by the College of Family Physicians Singapore since 2022 that teaches general practitioners how to care for adults with intellectual disabilities [33]. Yet much more is called for [3], with reforms needing to promote favourable physician mindsets towards PWD already during medical training, to achieve a broader effect.

With the above in mind, PWD were enlisted as patient educators for a “Communications with Persons with Disabilities” workshop for third-year undergraduate medical students attending their family medicine posting. The workshop significantly improved student attitudes [34]. However, the experiences of participants, both tutors and students, have not been examined.

We aimed to understand learners’ and tutors’ perspectives of the workshop, the meaning they gave to interacting with PWD, and how the curriculum affected professional identity formation.

Methods

Setting

Third-year medical students from Yong Loo Lin School of Medicine, National University of Singapore attended the compulsory communications with PWD workshop [35] during their Family Medicine posting. There were between 70 and 80 students per batch.

Pre-workshop videos and reading materials were given to students prior to the workshop. The four-hour in-person workshop begun with a plenary session. The students were then broken into smaller groups of 12 or 13 and rotated through three different stations with PWD educators who had different disability (vision, hearing, and mobility). Each station would comprise of a ten-minute simulated consultation between student volunteer and PWD educator who enacted a scripted role consistent with his/her actual disability. Peers would observe in a fishbowl format [36]. The subsequent 20-minute-long facilitated discussion consisted of feedback from peers, tutor, and the PWD, and any questions the students had for the PWD. Questions typically asked include how they became PWD, how they experienced the healthcare system, how they manage their social encounters, and how they acquired and coped in daily life. Students were subsequently given time at the end of each station for students to pen their reflections. Reflections and learning points would be subsequently shared at the combined group to end off the workshop.

Study design

We conducted a series of semi-structured interviews with workshop participants.

All tutors who facilitated the workshop were invited to participate in the study by email and text message. All students who attended the workshop between June and October 2023 were invited to participate in the study through multiple means: announcements after each workshop, email invitations, and snowball sampling. While a formal count was not made of those in attendance on the day, a majority of the two batches were present. The workshop was not graded, and students were assured anonymity with the knowledge that non-participation will not affect any grades.

Procedure

All participants were required to provide written informed consent as detailed in the participant information sheet and requirements of the study. Participants were each given a study ID for de-identification. A short survey asked for their details including prior training in PWD care, and any significant personal experience with PWD. For tutors, additional questions included postgraduate qualifications, years of clinical and teaching experience, type of clinical practice, and average number of PWD seen per week.

Semi-structured interview guides were developed by the study team based on a framework proposed by Kallio et al. [37] that consist of formulating the preliminary semi-structured interview guide; pilot testing the guide; and presenting the complete semi-structured interview guide. The student interview guide was piloted by field testing on eight third-year medical students via online Zoom platform to confirm the coverage and relevance of the content of the formulated, preliminary guide and to test its implementation. The interview guides were further adapted throughout data collection to match each conversation’s flow (see Supplementary Material, p. 4 and 5 for the initial version). The tutor interview guide included three main sections: how the curriculum affected students’ medical professional identity, how the curriculum affected their own medical professional identity, and strengths and weaknesses of the curriculum. Similarly, the student interview guide explored how the curriculum affected students’ medical professional identity, and their perspectives towards the curriculum.

Audio-recorded, hour-long semi-structured individual interviews were conducted, in-person or via Zoom, by a trained interviewer (AS for tutors, VVL for students) who had no prior contact with the student participants, though they knew some of the tutors. Both interviewers are full-time researchers with qualitative research experiences and training for interview skills. Reflexive notes were taken by the interviewers at the end of each interview. Meanwhile, they actively reflected on the impact of their thoughts and emotions on the interviews. For instance, when faced with questions that the participants seemed more hesitant to share, the interviewers would reflect on whether their questioning methods were appropriate and promptly adjusted the interview strategies to ensure safety and openness.

While tutors were not given any vouchers for study participation, students were given a SGD ten-dollar (approximately six British pounds) beverage voucher for interview participation. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National University of Singapore (NUS-IRB-2022-608).

While all tutors who taught in the curriculum participated, student recruitment continued until thematic saturation was reached. As the interviews progressed, analysed the transcripts of each interview and compared the themes in the new interview data with those in the already-collected data. For example, in the interviews with the students, after interviewing 10 students, we found that the information obtained from the subsequent 3 consecutive interviews only enriched and refined the existing themes, and no new themes emerged. After discussion with the research team, we believed that data saturation had been reached at this point, and we stopped data-collection. No repeat interviews were required.

Data analysis

All recordings were de-identified and transcribed verbatim. Microsoft Excel was utilized for coding and categorization of themes.

Analysis followed a inductive and thematic approach outlined by Braun and Clarke [38] to gradually distil themes and patterns related to professional medical identity. The analysis did not employ a pre-determined conceptual framework when deriving the initial set of codes. Team members (VL, VVL, GJC) independently coded all the transcripts, and met regularly to discuss findings and differences in opinion, to develop the coding framework and initial themes. The final set of themes were refined with a focus on how the interaction with patient educators with disabilities helped to develop professional medical identity amongst students, and consensus was reached in discussion with the main study team.

In the following section, quotations are reproduced verbatim with hesitation sounds and repetitions deleted. Deleted elements are indicated as “…” and writings within [ ] have been added for clarity. Readers accustomed to British English may find Singlish expressions curious, nonetheless meanings should remain largely clear.

Themes

The study reveals a cohesive process in which medical students develop their professional identity when caring for PWD. Their initial aspiration to provide equitable care is nurtured within a learning community that emphasizes experiential learning, role modelling, reflection, and authentic conversations. Direct exposure to PWD, particularly as patient educators, serves as a transformative experience, addressing historical gaps in disability education and challenging stereotypes. Ultimately, the PWD voice emerges as a unifying element, amplifying the impact of these experiences and highlighting the need for disability education to be intentionally integrated into medical training.

Theme 1: we all started here: the aspiration to care

For students and tutors, the workshop was a moment to ponder their professional values, and where they were in relation to the humanistic ideals that they all started out with as aspiring physicians. Observation of their seniors at work coloured student idealism. Unease was expressed about the prospect of becoming jaded or less person-oriented during care provision.

On the other hand, tutors saw their ideals mirrored by students. They also reflected how these were tempered by experience and clinical realities. Nonetheless, relationship-based care remained distinctly valued by tutors.

Theme 2: values are acquired in a learning community

Each experience representing a building block

In their initial interactions, students were often awkward and often focused mainly on biomedical details of the case scenario while foregoing explorations of PWD’s illness experience. Confidence developed with further interaction, including the confidence to ask sensitive questions around the PWD’s personal disability, and what term to use to best describe the person and his/her disability. Theme 2 illustrative quotes

Role-modelling shapes behaviour

Peers and tutors role-modelled how to interact with PWD, while learners’ careful observations of such interactions provided examples for observers to consider how they would eventually interact with PWD with different disabilities.

The value of reflection

Learners penned their thoughts on reflection sheets written toward the end of each station. Time and space set aside for reflection was precious, especially because there were multiple thoughts to process. It allowed thoughts to percolate, insights to form, and for these to be documented.

The importance of authentic conversation

Listening to each other in a facilitated safe space allowed for deeper learning. Conversation about one’s authentic experience added nuance to what was learnt. Authentic conversation would often bring to light facets of sensitivity and person-centeredness toward PWD not previously considered, with the awareness that these could apply even to persons without obvious disability both in the clinical setting and in society-at-large.

Theme 3: the educational value of PWD exposure in medical training

Historical absence of disability in the medical curriculum

Even as tutors noted how teaching in the curriculum surfaced gaps in holistic communications and how these may be bridged, they wistfully noted the dearth of exposure to PWD during their medical training. For most tutors, learning to communicate with PWD occurred informally and on-the-job, often through trial and error garnered through experience. Theme 3 illustrative quotes

Tutors as co-learners with students

Tutors described how they appreciated being “co-learners” with students, and like students, garnered new insights in the workshop.

PWD educators in medical training: “An eye-opening experience”

Both tutors and students described the workshop as “eye opening”, and were unanimous that the opportunity to learn from PWD was important and beneficial for future practice.

The uncertainty of long-term impact

While some students articulated new-found motivation to take practical steps in the short to medium term, the effects of the single workshop on participants and their future medical practice remains to be seen. Albeit promising, it was impossible to gauge the extent that the workshop impacted today’s learners to improve their social inclusiveness and attitudes towards PWD in their clinical practice after graduation.

Theme 4: the impact of the PWD patient educator voice

According to students and tutors, bringing PWD into the curriculum to share the authentic PWD voice allowed students to put themselves better in another’s shoes [39]. This engendered their sensitivity, empathy and compassion towards PWD. Hearing from PWD brought home a disquieting awareness of the inconsistently growing, yet awkward, social space that PWD find themselves in with Singapore’s healthcare system and civil society. Whether student or tutor, it was a common experience that assumptions harboured about PWD were dismantled by engaging with the PWD and each other in the workshop.

The workshop provided learners more respectful options in communication strategies, invited learners to consider gaps in social inclusivity, and how healthcare practitioners could be more mindful toward PWD presenting at our clinics, hospitals and in society-at-large.

The workshop was also a reminder that PWD were not just topics for classroom discussion, nor were they mere clinical problems to be solved or issues to be debated. They are first and foremost “persons presenting”, beyond labels or ideas read about in the media and textbooks.

The workshop was also considered a reminder us at clinics and in our wards, it was a call for doctors by both tutors and students to see the person behind the disability and the presenting complaint, whether one was “disabled” or not. It invited participants, students and tutors, to consider what more was needed for greater social inclusivity toward PWD among us, both in healthcare and in society.

Discussion

We started with medical students’ and tutors’ aspiration to care. Then uncovered how values are acquired in a learning community - through direct experience, modelling behaviour, reflection and through authentic conversation. Next, we delved into the educational value of PWD in medical training – the historical absence of disability in curriculum, tutors co-learning with students from PWD educators, the uncertainty of long-term impact. Lastly, we discussed the impact of the PWD educator patient voice in professional identity formation.

While medical training can more confidently make claims on clinical competency outcomes [40, 41], its ability to shape future doctors’ humanistic values is less certain [42, 43]. The literature on the erosion of empathy during medical school [44, 45] is emblematic of this shortcoming.

The lens of professional identity formation [11, 46] informs us that professional values developed throughout medical school is a dynamic that results from the interactions between learners’ prior and developing identity, and the complex confluence of people and situations of the “medical world” encountered during and beyond the curriculum.

Our study demonstrates provides insights to our earlier findings of how of how the enlistment of PWD patient educators in a disability-inclusive curriculum positively affects student attitudes toward PWD [32]. We have observed that bringing the PWD patient voice into the curriculum is, with careful planning, an effective catalyst in an overall strategy for strengthening professional values [20] and professional identity formation. This is particularly useful for improving attitudes toward PWD [34], strengthening communication skills, empathic understanding, and developing a person-centred approach to patients, whether or not they are identified as a PWD [17, 47–51].

Our findings cohere with earlier work on how humanistic ideals, values, and professional identity reside within learners [52], and how these may be encouraged by experiential learning [53–55], and role modelling by peers and tutors [12, 13, 17, 49, 56]. It also shows how reflective learning [11, 17, 57, 58] in a safe collaborative environment [59–61] positively strengthens person-centredness as a value.

While we were cognizant of the dearth of disability curricula historically in most medical schools, and that most practitioners learnt to work with PWD patients through clinical experience [60–62], tutors describing themselves as co-learners and as being “enlightened” through the curriculum was an unexpected result. This finding humbly supports the notion that medical professional identity formation is life-long and we are never quite “done” in refinement of values as both scientists and humanists.

Limitations

There are several limitations in our study design. Participant selection bias is inherent in the recruitment and qualitative sampling. As we had stopped recruitment of student participants upon thematic saturation, there may be parts of the student population who were not sampled. It was possible that students who were less interested in the workshop will not take part in the study, and their perspectives would not be captured in the results. Nonetheless we believed our findings cohere with published work in the areas of PWD curricula, patient voice, and the work on professional identity formation. As a single workshop that allowed for the lived personal narratives of PWD to be heard within a packed curriculum, this meant that learners were limited in the types of disability they were exposed to, and the time allocated to the workshop was not sufficient for students and tutors to acquire all aspects of working with PWD.

We also could not conclude on the long-term impact of this one-time session on this study. Future research should evaluate long-term effects of different workshop modalities with the same theme, whether a high-day workshop as in this study has similar outcomes to a multi-session longitudinal curricula.

Conclusions

Bringing the PWD voice in the medical classroom may have positive effects on the professional identities of learners. With attention to experiential learning and safe reflective spaces, potential effects on learners could include self-reported improvement of empathy toward PWD; and greater awareness towards the needs of patients.

Further curricular intervention could comprise a broader range of disabilities integrated across the curriculum. As curriculum planners we mulled about how additional curriculum time would be nice to amplify these effects without sacrificing other important curricular objectives. Nonetheless, our study provided a promising possibility that PWD in a communications workshop may contribute positively to the developing professional identities of both tutors and students.
