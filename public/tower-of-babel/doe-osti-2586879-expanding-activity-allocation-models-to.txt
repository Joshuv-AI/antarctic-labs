Abstract
 Exercise facilities have been proven to have numerous physical, mental, and
psychological benefits, yet exercise facilities are still inaccessible to a large portion of
the population. This study serves to explore the accessibility of fitness centres through
geographical, demographic, and temporal lenses through an expansion of the
UrbanPop framework that seeks to allocate simulated agents to fitness centres in the
Clarksville metro to explore the effects of travel distances on different demographics
throughout the week. Findings indicate that senior and retired demographics
consistently travel longer distances to exercise in the larger Clarksville area, likely due
to tendencies to live further from the center of the metropolitan area. Furthermore, all
demographics tend to travel further distances to exercise on the weekends rather than
the weekdays, indicating that travel distance can affect likelihood of agents to travel,
especially on weekdays when many agents are in the workforce or participating in
schooling.

Introduction
 Simulated population models are essential for exploring human dynamics and
understanding human security and social equity issues within human systems.
Synthetic populations (virtual representation of individuals plausibly residing within an
area) use simulated behavior of individual’s movements and interactions to create a
greater, more complex understanding of the behavior of an overall community. These
models have applications ranging from urban planning, accessibility to key resources,
public health, and disaster preparedness.
 The UrbanPop spatial microsimulation framework, developed by Oak Ridge
National Laboratory, generates high-fidelity synthetic populations, referred to as agents,

 This paper builds from the foundational framework of UrbanPop to allocate
agents to optimal activity spaces for non-anchor activities, particularly fitness centres in
Clarksville, TN. Four different time points across the seven days of the week were used
to allocate agents to exercise locations and route the transportation networks used. This
process differs from anchor activities as there is less information dictating the allocation
process, allowing for a focus on resource accessibility through demographic and
temporal lenses. Using this research, the UrbanPop framework can expand its network
into other non-anchor activity spaces as well as other metropolitan areas, leading to a
more comprehensive understanding of behavior and movement of communities across
time.

 Going to the gym has a number of physical benefits, including significant
differences in blood pressure, Physical Fitness Index (measures overall physical fitness
and recovery time), energy expenditure, and mid-upper arm circumference (used to
identify nutritional status) between gym-goers and non-gym-goersError! Reference source not

 There are also several social and mental health benefits. One study that explores
mental health and wellbeing benefits for female gym-goers notes that gym-based
resistance training improves self-acceptance, personal growth, social affiliation,
autonomy, and increases ability to move into a ‘flow state’, an ordered state of mind that
derives from being cognitively and emotionally absorbed in an activityV. These benefits
in particular can have a significant impact on community members in high-stress jobs or
situations. For example, an article about visiting paramedics and Ambulance
Community Offices receiving free access to a nearby gym states that having a gym
leaves them more physically and mentally fit, giving them “more of an opportunity to
deal with [unexpected and stressful scenarios] appropriately and manage it all”. These
benefits can make a huge difference in community members that frequently encounter
high-stress scenarios or are struggling with maintaining their mental and physical health
and wellbeing. However, this also highlights the importance of ensuring fitness facilities
are accessible. This initiative was crucial as previous fitness centre locations and

membership options didn’t suit on-call paramedics and first responders who might “get
paged partway through a workout”VI
 In a 2002 National Health Interview Survey, only 61% of adults report perceived
access to a fitness facilityVII. As this survey is self-reported, this statistic is based on
sentiment rather than any accessibility measurement. Therefore, the remaining 39% of
adults surveyed could have a perceived lack of access due to any combination of
factors, from cost of membership, lack of disability accommodation, or distance from
fitness facility locations.
 According to a study conducted in England, the availability of physical activity
facilities declines with a level of deprivation measuring four fewer facilities per 100,000
square feet between the least- and most-deprived areasVIII. Another similar study in
Wuhan, China finds that urban gyms are unevenly distributed and walkability to gyms
varies greatly between urban and rural neighborhoods of the cityIX. This inequality in a
variety of metropolitan areas worldwide indicates a likelihood of the inaccessibility of
fitness centres in areas further from the main metropolitan area and particularly in areas
where the community has a greater need for aid to assist people to live more physically
active lifestyles, even in the United States. This paper seeks to isolate the accessibility
factor of distance by measuring the distance of probable routes that simulated agents in
Clarksville, TN take to exercise locations throughout the week, exploring demographic
and temporal disparities to expose gaps in accessibility.

 From the ActLike package, the allocate_student_simulations.ipynb was used as the
basis for agent allocation. ActLike works by allocating agents from the synthetic
population to destinations based on their capacity (the amount of people reasonably
expected to visit each location)XIV XV. The activity schedules were used to create
Clarksville agentsII. The agents were determined by extrapolating agents whose activity
location was designated exercise for the designated hour (08-09 for morning, 11-12 for
day, 19-20 for evening, 22-23 for night) from the larger dataset then downsizing further
by extrapolating only agents whose activity location was designated home for the
preceding hour. From there the location data for the corresponding day is read in,
particularly the maximum customer capacity for each location for the specific time of day
and the geographic data.
 Allocation and routing were limited to trips made from agent residences to exercise
locations. Therefore, all allocations were assigned without a significant portion of the
population that are traveling to exercise from other locations besides their residences.
This exclusion was made because the agents have assigned home locations associated
with their agent number, but if the agents are traveling from another location, this
location would have to be derived using similar techniques as these locations are not
designated in the activity schedules. This adds a level of uncertainty in both the starting
location and the destination of the route.
 With this data, the cij and schoolsim functions were run to determine the optimal
allocation of all agents, considering max capacity of each location and travel distance.
These results were then read into the 04_waypoint_routing.ipynb notebook from the
MoveLike package, where origin and destination locations create a Linestring that is
then mapped onto the existing road networks of the area to simulate the realistic routes
each agent would take to travel from their residence to the fitness centre. The MoveLike
packages prepares road networks from OpenStreetMap and then overlays the routes
onto these road networks, creating realistic routes from location to locationXIV XV. This
allows for an accurate measurement of the distance travelled by the agent. Using this
dataset, different demographics can be extrapolated. The dataset was frequently sorted
by destination, age group, gender, or NHTS role.

Results
 To explore different demographics, agents were sorted by previously designated
roles, age groups, and gender. Each agent with an activity schedule had already been
assigned a label from each role, and their schedule was extrapolated from this
demographic information. Age groups were assigned based on the age of the agent
(child = 0-18, young adult = 18-34, adult = 34-49, older adult = 50-64, senior = 65+).
NHTS roles, based on their primary responsibility in the household, were also assigned
to agents. Adults were largely sorted into the categories homemaker, other,
unemployed and seeking work (unemp__seeking_work), and worker (wrk_only).
Children were entirely sorted between the student categories, including students who
are labor force eligible (student__lf_elig or student 16+), and the youth categories for
children that do not attend school.

Saturday. It also has similar reduced hours as Saturday for the chosen fitness centres,

Discussion
 This research demonstrates the benefits of modeling human dynamics with vivid
synthetic populations. Using routing and allocation technology, we are able to produce a
reasonable estimate of the neighborhoods in which people reside and interact and the
locations in which they travel to, as well as support a more nuanced characterization of
human activities at specific temporal and activity spaces.
 The routes provided in the research above show how the refined understanding
of human dynamics can aid in understanding fitness accessibility. This framework can
be used to examine fitness centres for which travelling to exercise might pose a
challenge towards people in the community. Additionally, the expansion of activity
spaces in the context of the UrbanPop network furthers assessments of the times and
routes used by people to commute to exercise. This furthers the goal of understanding
the times and places commutes are most sensitive to nearby disruption and estimates
occupancy to anticipate the best time of day to reach people.
 This research has several limitations that will, hopefully, be overcome in future
work. The UrbanPop framework operates on several key assumptions. First, it assumes
that all travel within the study occurs along road networks. While Clarksville, TN is a
primarily road-based transportation center, it can be assumed that not all transportation
is road-based. Secondly, as agents are simulated, these results can be assumed to not
be completely accurate to real-life routes taken by people in the Clarksville metro area,
though the hope is that routes are comparable to the overall movement of the
population.
 Specifically, for the scope of the research on fitness centre routing, there are
additional limitations to consider for the specific activity spaces being explored. First, the
research assumes that all agents are traveling to exercise at fitness centres, and
specifically the 38 locations for which data and locations were found. There are other
locations in Clarksville, TN that could be considered exercise activity spaces (including
outdoor parks, specialized exercise facilities, etc.) and other locations that could be
classified as gyms or fitness centres for which data was not available in the datasets
and/or collection methods used. As a further note, for this limitation, all outdoor
recreation spaces were excluded from data collection as an estimated capacity cannot
be calculated accurately using floor area and PDT estimations.
 Furthermore, the optimization method for agent allocation only considers location
capacity and travel distance. However, with non-simulated agents, there are several
outside factors that determine the decision of exercise location. Some that are not
considered in this allocation include the cost of membership, location-specific

 18

constraints, personal preference of the agent, and location consistency. All these factors
significantly influence the decision-making process of people traveling to exercise.
 Cost, in particular, can be a huge factor in determining the accessibility of gyms
and the likelihood of community members buying membership to a fitness facility. In a
2025 Health & Fitness Association survey, nearly one-third of Americans that intended
to focus on health and fitness in the coming year cited cost as top factor that might
prevent them from achieving their fitness goals in 2025XVI.
 Additionally, there are concerns from community members that feel that gyms do
not provide them with the accommodations or welcoming space that they need to be
willing to front the cost of gym membership. Gender stereotypes in exercise, particular
the idea that “women do cardio, men lift weights”, can cause some women to be
reluctant to engage in exercise using specific pieces of equipment or areas that are
farther from traditional feminine spaces. If a fitness centre puts greater emphasis in
traditionally masculine or feminine activity types, the opposite gender can become more
reluctant to commit to the cost of membership or even seek other options that are more
inconvenient to themXVII. Membership costs can also cause concern from community
members that have disabilities that are not properly accommodated by gym equipment.
They often feel that they are paying for more than they can reasonably access and are
often pushed by gyms to pay more to access accommodations or specialized
equipment that is more accessible to them, such as specialized trainers, other services,
or an extended exercise periodXVIII.
 Some factors, such as cost of membership, can later be included by altering the
cost-optimization matrix to account for income levels of agents and estimating their
likelihood of purchasing a membership to a specific location. Other factors, such as
location consistency, can be addressed by building on previous allocation models to
create consistent routes for agents. Other factors, however, such as personal and
exercise type preference, could be estimated by demographic data of specific location
or activity type, but likely could not be fully accounted for. For example, if a gym
specializes in gymnastics, then preference for allocated agents would likely be younger
demographics, however, there is no way to account for whether agents take gymnastics
classes and are interested in the specific activity type. Additionally, if the membership of
a gym is limited to the military and their families, there is no way to determine which
agents have access to membership. For issues in accommodations and welcoming
spaces, further research can be done into each individual location, especially for
accessibility of equipment, but the best estimate for these often come from demographic
and survey data collected by private companies or from the location themselves, which
is not easily accessible at the moment.
