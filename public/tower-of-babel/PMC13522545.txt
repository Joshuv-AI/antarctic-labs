Prior acute ozone injury modulates inflammatory responses to subsequent repetitive ozone exposures in mice
Authors: Kshitiz Paudel, Sonika Patial, Yogesh Saini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ground-level ozone (O3), a criteria air pollutant, can cause significant adverse effects on lung health, including airway inflammation, compromised lung function, and increased susceptibility to lung infections. This study was designed to determine whether a history of acute lung injury from high-concentration O3 modulates respiratory tract inflammatory responses to repetitive low-concentration O3 exposures. Accordingly, we hypothesized that mice acutely exposed to a high concentration of O3 would exhibit a modulated type-2 (Th2)-like inflammatory response to subsequent, repetitive O3 exposures, even after full recovery from non-Th2 acute inflammation caused by the initial exposure. Ten-week-old C57BL6/J mice were exposed to either filtered air (FA) or 2 ppm O3 for 3 h. After a three-week recovery period, the mice received daily exposures to FA or O3 for 4 h per day over nine days. We evaluated immune cells and inflammatory mediators in cell-free bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), and examined mucous cell metaplasia (MCM) and epithelial cell injury in lung tissue sections. As expected, mice in the FA-FA group, which were not exposed to O3, showed no overt signs of injury or inflammation. Mice in the O3-FA group, which received acute exposure to 2 ppm O3 and allowed to recover for 3 weeks, exhibited homeostatic immune cell composition within lung airspaces and no evidence of MCM, suggesting almost complete recovery from non-Th2 acute inflammation caused by the initial exposure. In contrast, mice in the FA-O3 group, which received only repetitive exposures to O3, exhibited elevated eosinophil counts and prominent MCM, suggesting Th2 inflammatory responses. These mice also displayed increased numbers of FIZZ1+ epithelial cells in the lungs. Compared with the FA-O3 group, mice in the O3-O3 group, which received acute exposure to 2 ppm O3, were allowed to recover for 3 weeks, and then repetitively exposed to 1 ppm O3, exhibited attenuated Th2 inflammatory responses, as evidenced by diminished eosinophil and lymphocyte counts and attenuated MCM. Compared with the FA-O3 group, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) from the O3-O3 group showed lower levels of eotaxin, IL-1α, IL-1β, and IL-4, but higher levels of G-CSF, KC, IL-6, and IL-12, with a trend toward higher IL-10. Furthermore, lung sections in the O3-O3 group also displayed reduced numbers of mast cells and a trend toward fewer FIZZ1+ epithelial cells. These findings suggest that prior exposure to acute, high-concentration O3 modulates inflammatory and remodeling responses induced by subsequent repeated exposures to low-concentration O3. This study highlights the health impacts of pollution, particularly for populations experiencing intermittent high-level exposures to O3.

Introduction

Ground-level ozone (O3), a secondary air pollutant, is produced as a result of a photochemical reaction between oxides of nitrogen (NOx) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs), which are emitted from automobiles and various industrial establishments. The ground-level O3 concentrations peak during hot, sunny days, especially in urban areas with high traffic1. Ground-level O3 is a major public-health problem, with ~ 490,000 deaths attributed to O3 exposure worldwide in 20212. Ground-level O3 irritates the respiratory system, triggering airway injury and inflammation, which can result in structural airway remodeling and reduced lung function3, 4. Exposure to O3 also exacerbates preexisting lung conditions, such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), increasing the severity of symptoms and reducing responsiveness to conventional therapies5, 6, 7, 8. Despite these health concerns, our understanding of the respiratory disturbances caused by O3 is limited.

Acute versus repetitive O3 exposures produce different pathological outcomes in both humans and mice9, 10, 11. Acute exposure of mice to 2–3 ppm O3 triggers a rapid neutrophilic inflammatory response, airway epithelial cell injury, and the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that induce oxidative stress and disrupt the respiratory epithelial barrier12, 13, 14, 15. In contrast, 1–3 weeks of repetitive exposure of mice to 0.5–1 ppm O3 triggers type-2 (Th2)-like inflammation, which is characterized by eosinophilic recruitment and mucous cell metaplasia (MCM)16, 17, 18. Eosinophil recruitment is mediated, in part, by increased production of eotaxins19, 20, 21, while MCM is driven by IL-4/IL-13 signaling through IL-4 receptor alpha (IL-4Rα), which promotes goblet cell differentiation and mucus production22, 23, 24. IL-4 further promotes the accumulation and activation of mast cells, which in turn release IL-4 and eotaxin, further amplifying the Th2 response25, 26. Repetitive O3 exposure is also associated with alternatively activated (M2) macrophages in the lung, a phenotype classically induced by IL-4/IL-13 signaling27, 28. This is accompanied by increased expression of resistin-like molecule alpha (RELMα/FIZZ1)27, which is expressed by M2 macrophages29 and airway epithelial cells30 and is implicated in airway remodeling and fibrosis31, 32.

While the inflammatory responses to acute versus repetitive O3 exposures have been characterized9, 10, the impact of one type of exposure on the subsequent inflammatory response to the other remains poorly understood. Due to the unpredictable fluctuations in ground-level O3 concentration and exposure duration, prior O3 exposure can influence the respiratory response to subsequent encounters. Prior acute lung injury can also alter responses to later insults33, 34. For example, lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced acute lung injury has been shown to dampen a subsequent house dust mite (HDM)-induced mucoinflammatory phenotype in mice34. Similarly, repetitive O3 exposure (four doses) attenuates the severity of lung injury caused by a subsequent acute O3 exposure in mice35. However, whether an initial acute O3-induced lung injury affects the manifestation of subsequent, repetitive O3-induced mucoinflammatory outcomes has not yet been investigated.

To compare the lung’s response to repetitive O3 exposure in mice with or without prior acute O3 exposure, we hypothesized that mice acutely exposed to a high concentration of O3 would exhibit a modulated Th2 inflammatory response to subsequent, repetitive O3 exposures, even after full recovery from non-Th2 inflammation caused by the initial exposure. Accordingly, ten-week-old C57BL6/J mice were exposed to either 2 ppm O3 or filtered air (FA) for 3 h. After a three-week recovery period, mice were repetitively exposed to 1 ppm O3 or FA for nine days (4 h/day). We evaluated the recruitment of immune cells into the airspaces, concentrations of inflammatory mediators in cell-free bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), markers of lung epithelial injury, and MCM responses. The findings from this study offer new insights into how an initial high-concentration O3-induced acute lung injury and inflammation alters airway responses to subsequent low-concentration, repetitive O3 exposures, with important implications for understanding susceptibility to O3-induced lung diseases.

Methods

Animal husbandry, experimental design, and O3 exposure

Eight-week-old C57BL/6J female mice were procured from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME) and were acclimatized for 2 weeks in the Division of Laboratory Animal Medicine vivarium at Louisiana State University (LSU). Based on our prior study demonstrating greater inflammatory cell infiltration and enhanced transcriptional responses to repetitive O3 exposure in female mice compared with males, the present study was conducted exclusively in female mice to focus on the more responsive sex16. Mice were maintained in individually ventilated, hot-washed polycarbonate cages on a 12 h light-dark cycle and provided with a regular diet and water ad libitum. All animal use protocols were approved by the LSU Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and conducted in accordance with the guidelines in the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. This study follows the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines.

Mice were randomly assigned to four experimental groups: (1) Group 1, FA-FA: Mice were exposed to FA for 3 h, followed by a three-week recovery period, and were then exposed repetitively to FA (9 days; 4 h/day) (Fig. 1A); (2) Group 2, O3-FA: Mice were exposed to O3 (2 ppm) for 3 h, followed by a three-week recovery period, and were then exposed repetitively to FA (9 days; 4 h/day) (Fig. 1A); (3) Group 3, FA-O3: Mice were exposed to FA for 3 h, followed by a three-week recovery period, and were then exposed repetitively to O3 (9 days; 1 ppm at 4 h/day) (Fig. 1A); (4) Group 4, O3-O3: Mice were exposed to O3 (2 ppm) for 3 h, followed by a three-week recovery period, and were then exposed repetitively to O3 (9 days; 1 ppm at 4 h/day) (Fig. 1A).

O3 generation and data acquisition were done as previously described16. Briefly, O3 was generated using an O3 generator (TSE Systems, Chesterfield, MO), and its concentration was continuously monitored in real-time with a UV photometric O3 analyzer (Envia Altech Environment, Geneva, IL). Data were acquired through DACO monitoring and control software (TSE Systems, Chesterfield, MO). Mice that were designated for FA exposures were exposed to FA in identical chambers. Since mice are nocturnal and exhibit higher activity during the night36, O3 and FA exposures were conducted at night to simulate real-life scenarios of increased human activity phases.

Bronchoalveolar lavage and sample harvesting

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and sample harvesting were conducted as previously described16. Within 12–16 h after the last exposure, mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of 2,2,2-tribromoethanol (250 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). Following midline laparotomy, mice were euthanized via exsanguination through transection of the caudal vena cava. A 20G stub adapter was inserted into the trachea and secured with a suture. The left main stem bronchus was ligated with a suture to prevent lavaging of the left lung lobe. The right lung was lavaged with a calculated volume [(body weight in grams/2 × 0.035 × 1000 = volume in µL] of ice-cold Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) without calcium and magnesium. BAL fluid (BALF) was centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Cell-free supernatants were stored at −80 °C for protein and cytokine analysis. Cell pellets were resuspended in 500 µL DPBS for total cell counts and differential staining. The unlavaged left lung lobe was stored in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF) for histopathological analyses and immunohistochemistry, while the right lung lobes were snap-frozen and stored at −80 °C.

Histopathological analysis

Formalin-fixed, unlavaged left lung lobes were paraffin-embedded, sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm, and processed for histopathological analyses. Alcian blue-periodic acid Schiff (AB-PAS) staining was performed to examine mucopolysaccharide contents in epithelial cells and airway lumens. For identification of mast cells, lung sections were stained with 1% Toluidine Blue (pH 2.3; T3260, Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA). The slides were observed with an ECLIPSE Ci-L microscope, and photomicrographs were captured via a DS-Fi2 camera attachment (Nikon, Melville, NY).

Immunohistochemistry

The immunohistochemical localization of major basic protein (MBP), mucin 5B (MUC5B), found in inflammatory zone 1 (FIZZ1), and IL-4Rα was performed using previously published methods27, 37. Briefly, lung sections were deparaffinized with Citrisolv (Decon Labs Inc., King of Prussia, PA), rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 70%, 30%), and then processed for antigen retrieval. For MBP, antigen retrieval was performed by incubating the sections with Proteinase K at 37 °C for 20 min. For MUC5B, FIZZ1, and IL-4Rα, a citrate buffer-based heat-induced antigen retrieval method was used, where slides were heated in 10 mM tri-sodium citrate dihydrate solution with 0.05% Tween 20 (pH 6.0) at 95–100 °C for 30 min. Endogenous peroxidases were quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min at room temperature. Sections were then blocked with a blocking buffer for 20 min and incubated with primary antibodies: rat monoclonal MBP (MT-14.7.3; Mayo Clinic, Scottsdale, AZ), rabbit monoclonal FIZZ1 (ab39626; Abcam, Cambridge, MA), rabbit polyclonal MUC5B (UNC223, University of North Carolina, Chapel Hill, NC), and goat polyclonal IL-4Rα (AF530; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) for 1 h at room temperature. This was followed by two washes with wash buffer. The sections were then rinsed in PBS and processed using VECTASTAIN Elite ABC HRP Kits (PK-6101 (Rabbit), PK-6104 (Rat), and PK-6105 (goat); Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA) and chromogenic substrate conversion with ImmPACT NovaRED HRP Substrate Kit (SK-4800; Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA). Sections were rinsed in tap water and counterstained with Gill’s Hematoxylin-I (EMD Millipore Corporation, Burlington, MA), dehydrated through graded ethanol series, and mounted with VectaMount mounting medium (H-5000; Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA). Imaging was performed using an ECLIPSE Ci-L microscope, and photomicrographs were captured with a DS-Fi2 camera attachment (Nikon, Melville, NY).

Cytokine analyses

Cell-free BALF was assayed for mouse eotaxin, G-CSF, KC/CXCL1, IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-12p40, IL-6, IL-10, and IL-4 using a Luminex XMAP-based assay, following the manufacturer’s protocol (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). Cytokine concentrations were calculated using standard curves generated for each analyte, with assay sensitivities in the picogram per milliliter (pg/ml) range. Measurements were reported with a precision of 0.01 pg/ml. Cytokine concentrations falling below the lower limit of quantification were assigned a value of 0.01 pg/ml below the analyte’s lowest detectable concentration to allow inclusion in the statistical analyses.

Statistical analyses

One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons was used to determine significant differences among multiple groups. Grubbs’ test was used to identify and remove outliers. The data were expressed as mean ± SEM. Significant differences between the two groups were determined with Student’s t test. A p-value less than or equal to 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.2.3 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA).

Results

Prior acute O3 exposure attenuates body weight changes induced by subsequent repetitive O3 exposure

To investigate the effects of prior acute O3 exposure on the lung’s response to repetitive O3 exposure, ten-week-old female mice were assigned to four experimental groups (Fig. 1A), i.e., FA-FA, O3-FA, FA-O3, and O3-O3, as described in the methods section. First, we monitored body weight change during the three-week recovery phase following acute exposure to FA or 2 ppm O3. As expected, the FA-exposed mice showed consistent weight gain (Fig. 1B). In contrast, mice exposed to 2 ppm O3 exhibited a significant reduction in body weight gain within 2 days post-exposure, with partial recovery by day 5. However, body weight remained significantly lower compared to FA-exposed mice at both day 5 and day 9 post-exposure (Fig. 1B). By day 21 post-exposure, mice acutely exposed to O3 had recovered from their initial body weight loss (Fig. 1B).

To determine if the history of acute O3 exposure had any impact on the body weight change caused by subsequent repetitive O3 exposure, we next monitored body weight gain during the course of repetitive O3 exposures. The FA-FA group showed consistent body weight gain during the repetitive FA exposures, as expected (Fig. 1C). The O3-FA group, i.e., mice that had prior acute O3 exposure, showed slightly reduced body weight gain during the repetitive FA exposures, but the differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 1C). Both the FA-O3 and O3-O3 groups showed reduced body weight gains, following 2 repeated doses of O3. Interestingly, however, the reduction in body weight gain was significantly less in the O3-O3 group compared to the FA-O3 group. Furthermore, while the FA-O3 mice showed substantial recovery from body weight loss, O3-O3 mice exhibited only a modest increase in body weights by day 9 of the repetitive O3 exposures (Fig. 1C). These data indicate that prior acute O3 exposure influences body weight responses during subsequent repetitive O3 exposures.

Prior acute O3 exposure modulates immune cell recruitment in the lung airspaces during subsequent repetitive O3 exposure

To determine the effects of prior acute O3 exposure on pulmonary immune responses to repetitive O3 exposure, BALF was analyzed to assess immune cell composition. Immune cell composition and counts were comparable between FA-FA and O3-FA groups. In both groups, macrophages were the predominant cell type, with minimal presence of granulocytes, i.e., neutrophils and eosinophils (Fig. 2A–E). These data suggest that a three-week recovery period following acute O3 exposure was sufficient to allow near-complete restoration of normal immune cell composition in mice. As previously reported16, mice in the FA-O3 group exposed to repetitive O3 alone exhibited increased immune cell counts (Fig. 2A–F), which were attributable to the increased counts of macrophages, neutrophils, eosinophils, and lymphocytes. Compared with the FA-O3 group, the O3-O3 group exhibited a trend toward reduced total immune cell counts, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2B). However, compared with the FA-O3 group, mice in the O3-O3 group exhibited significantly lower numbers of eosinophils (Fig. 2E,G) and lymphocytes (Fig. 2F), while neutrophil counts showed an upward trend (Fig. 2D). These data suggest that prior acute O3 exposure modulates the classical eosinophilic response to repetitive O3 exposure.

Previously, we have shown that FIZZ1 protein expression is elevated in the airway epithelium in repetitively O3-exposed mice27, 37, supporting its role as a marker of Th2-associated inflammation and epithelial remodeling in response to repetitive O3 exposure. Consistent with prior reports, we observed robust FIZZ1 immunostaining in the airway epithelium of FA-O3 mice (Fig. 3A–C). In contrast, mice in the O3-O3 group exhibited a trend toward reduced FIZZ1 expression; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 3A–C). These data suggest a potential attenuation of the epithelial remodeling responses in the O3-O3 group.

Prior acute O3 exposure alters changes in the levels of inflammatory mediators in the airspaces during subsequent repetitive O3 exposure

To assess the effect of prior acute O3 exposure on the release of inflammatory mediators into the airspaces of mice subjected to repetitive O3 exposure, we measured the levels of inflammatory mediators in the BALF harvested from FA-O3 and O3-O3 mice. As compared with the FA-O3 mice, the O3-O3 mice exhibited altered levels of inflammatory mediators. Eotaxin, IL-1α, and IL-1β levels were significantly reduced in the BALF from O3-O3 mice compared with FA-O3 mice (Fig. 4A–C). IL-12 p40 showed a downward trend in the BALF from O3-O3 mice compared with FA-O3 mice. (Fig. 4F). In contrast, G-CSF, KC, and IL-6 levels were significantly elevated in the BALF from O3-O3 mice compared with FA-O3 mice (Fig. 4D,E,G). Additionally, IL-10 exhibited an upward trend in O3-O3 mice compared with FA-O3 mice (Fig. 4H). These data indicate that prior acute O3 exposure alters the release of inflammatory mediators into the airspaces of mice subjected to repetitive O3 exposure.

Prior acute O3 exposure attenuates mucous cell metaplasia induced by subsequent repetitive O3 exposure

Previous reports indicate that repetitive O3 exposure induces type 2 (Th2) -like inflammatory responses characterized by MCM4, 16, 27, 38, 39. Therefore, we assessed the effect of prior acute O3 exposure on MCM, a hallmark feature of repetitive O3 exposure16, 27. We performed alcian blue-periodic acid Schiff (AB-PAS) staining and immunohistochemical localization of mucin protein, MUC5B in lung sections of the four experimental groups of mice. As expected, the airway epithelial staining for AB-PAS and MUC5B in the FA-FA group confirmed the absence of MCM (Fig. 5A–C). Furthermore, the extent of AB-PAS and MUC5B staining was comparable between the FA-FA and the O3-FA groups (Fig. 5A–C), indicating that a three-week recovery period following acute O3 exposure was sufficient and did not result in MCM. As previously reported38, FA-O3 mice exhibited increased MCM, as indicated by significant increase in AB-PAS and MUC5B staining. However, the O3-O3 mice exhibited significantly reduced MCM, as indicated by diminished AB-PAS and MUC5B staining (Fig. 5A–C), suggesting the suppression of MCM responses in mice that were previously subjected to acute O3-induced lung injury.

Prior acute O3 exposure reduces mast cell counts, IL-4 production, and Il-4Rα-expressing immune cells in mice subjected to subsequent repetitive O3 exposure

Based on our data highlighting altered Th2 response, i.e., reduced eosinophilia and diminished MCM in O3-O3 mice, we speculated that Th2 cytokine production would be diminished in the O3-O3 versus FA-O3 mice. To test this, first, we assessed the BALF levels of IL-4, a Th2 cytokine and a ligand for IL-4Rα40, in O3-O3 versus FA-O3 mice. As anticipated, the levels of IL-4 were significantly reduced in the BALF from O3-O3 versus FA-O3 mice (Fig. 6A). Because IL-4 is expressed at a high level in the mast cells25, 41, we next performed special staining for mast cells on the lung sections from FA-O3 and O3-O3 mice. Consistent with the lower IL-4 levels, the mast cell counts were significantly lower in O3-O3 versus FA-O3 mice (Fig. 6B,C). Additionally, we performed immunohistochemical localization of IL-4Rα on lung sections from FA-O3 and O3-O3 mice. We observed significantly lower numbers of IL-4Rα-expressing immune cells in O3-O3 versus FA-O3 mice (Fig. 7A,B). These data suggest that the diminished Th2 response in O3-O3 mice is likely associated with diminished mast cell recruitment, and, in turn, reduced production of IL-4, and reduced numbers of IL-4Rα-expressing immune cells.

Discussion

Both acute high-concentration and repetitive low-concentration O3 exposures disrupt lung homeostasis and elicit inflammatory responses that are distinct in nature9, 15, 16, 27. While acute high-concentration O3 exposure is characterized by neutrophilic inflammation associated with epithelial cell injury and desquamation42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48, repetitive low-concentration O3 exposure induces Th2 inflammation marked by eosinophilic recruitment and MCM16. The pulmonary consequences of both acute and repetitive O3 exposures have been studied extensively in humans and animal models9, 15, 16, 27, 42, 49. While the inflammatory responses to acute versus repetitive O3 exposures have been characterized9, 10, 11, the impact of one type of exposure on the subsequent inflammatory response to the other remains poorly understood. Prior exposure to repetitive O3 has been shown to dampen acute O3-induced lung inflammation35. However, the impact of prior O3-induced acute lung injury on the subsequent development of repetitive O3-induced mucoinflammatory phenotype has not been investigated. To address this knowledge gap, we subjected C57BL/6J female mice to a single acute O3 exposure (2 ppm for 3 h), followed by a three-week recovery interval, and subsequent repetitive O3 exposures (nine days; 1 ppm for 4 h per day). To our knowledge, this study is the first to evaluate the effects of prior O3-induced acute lung injury on the nature of mucoinflammatory responses to subsequent repetitive O3 exposure.

This study revealed several interesting findings: (1) mice with prior acute O3 exposure showed significantly reduced eosinophil counts, but increased neutrophil counts in the BALF following repetitive O3 exposure, indicating modulated recruitment of these immune cell types in the lungs, (2) mice with prior acute O3 exposure had significantly lower numbers of AB-PAS-positive and MUC5B-positive airway epithelial cells following repetitive O3 exposure, indicating diminished MCM and MUC5B expression, (3) prior acute O3 exposure led to altered levels of multiple inflammatory mediators following subsequent repetitive O3 exposure, reflecting a reprogramming of the airway inflammatory cytokine milieu, (4) prior acute O3 exposure exhibited a trend towards inhibiting the repetitive O3-induced increase in epithelial expression of FIZZ1, a molecule linked to airway eosinophilia50 and airway remodeling51, and (5) prior acute O3 exposure mitigates the expression of IL-4 and IL-4Rα suggesting suppressed activation of Th2 signaling pathway following repetitive O3 exposure. Collectively, comparison of repetitive O3-exposed mice with and without prior acute O3 exposure indicates that prior acute O3 exposure can elicit long-term, possibly irreversible, changes that modify the lung’s subsequent response to repetitive O3 exposure.

Airway eosinophilia is a characteristic pulmonary response to repetitive O3 exposure4, 16, 17. Consistent with previous reports16, 17, 39, 52, 53, FA-O3 mice in this study exhibited increased eosinophil counts. In contrast, the O3-O3 mice exhibited marked attenuation of eosinophilic inflammation. Eosinophil recruitment to the lung is largely driven by eotaxin54, a robust eosinophil chemoattractant, which was decreased in the O3-O3 versus FA-O3 mice. Both alveolar macrophages and airway epithelial cells have been identified as important sources of eotaxin55, 56, 57, suggesting that the differences observed between FA-O3 and O3-O3 mice may have been a result of the differential responses of these cells to O3 exposure. How the prior acute O3 exposures suppress eotaxin expression programs in these cell types remains to be further investigated.

Acute pulmonary insults have been documented to significantly impact alveolar macrophage populations. Specifically, acute O3 exposure has been shown to induce apoptotic cell death in alveolar macrophages58, 59. Furthermore, LPS challenge33 and Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection60 can cause an initial decline in resident alveolar macrophage numbers. The decline of resident alveolar macrophage population is compensated by recruited bone marrow-derived monocytes that differentiate into alveolar macrophages34, 61. This shift in alveolar macrophage ontogeny from original fetal liver-derived to bone marrow-derived populations can alter macrophage functional programs and has been shown to modulate type-2 inflammatory responses, including attenuation of HDM-induced eosinophil recruitment34. In the current study, total macrophage counts were comparable between FA-O3 and O3-O3 mice. However, O3-O3 mice had significantly fewer numbers of IL-4Rα-expressing macrophages compared to FA-O3 mice. Myeloid IL-4Rα signaling is a critical regulator of eotaxin production and eosinophilic lung inflammation in mouse models62, 63, providing a plausible mechanistic link between reduced IL-4Rα-expressing macrophages and diminished eosinophilia. Together, these findings suggest that prior acute O3 exposure may have switched the alveolar macrophage ontogeny and phenotype in O3-O3 mice, favoring bone marrow-derived macrophages with reduced IL-4Rα expression, and thereby abrogating repetitive O3 exposure-induced eosinophilia.

Airway epithelial cells are also a prominent source of eotaxin in the lung; therefore, a reduction in epithelial-derived eotaxin can also result in reduced eosinophil recruitment64. Various inflammatory mediators are known to induce the release of eotaxin from epithelial cells. For example, IL-1β stimulates eotaxin production in airway epithelial cells56. Another potent inducer of epithelial eotaxin is IL-4, which is also an important player in allergen- and OVA-induced eosinophilia65, 66, 67. Compared with FA-O3 mice, O3-O3 mice exhibited lower levels of IL-1β and IL-4, which may have contributed to decreased epithelial eotaxin production. Alternatively, prior acute O3 exposure might have induced long-term changes in epithelial signaling, thereby reducing responsiveness to type-2 stimuli and limiting eotaxin production. Further studies investigating the role of alveolar macrophages, airway epithelial cells, and cytokines implicated in eotaxin production, including IL-1β and IL-4, are warranted to further substantiate these mechanisms.

Repetitive O3 exposures induce MCM in the airway epithelium16, 17, 39, 68, 69, 70, 71, 72. IL-4 induces mucin gene expression and goblet cell differentiation in airway epithelial cells22. Moreover, IL-4Rα signaling in airway epithelial cells is essential for MCM and mucin secretion23. While IL-4 promotes the recruitment and differentiation of Th2 cells73, Th2 cells and mast cells are known sources of IL-425, 41, 74. Consistent with previous reports16, 17, 39, 68, 69, 70, 71, 72, we observed increased numbers of AB-PAS+ and MUC5B+ airway epithelial cells in FA-O3 mice. However, this response was attenuated in O3-O3 mice, which was associated with reduced lymphocyte counts, fewer mast cells in the lungs, and lower IL-4 levels in the BALF. Collectively, prior acute O3 exposure appears to suppress repetitive O3-induced expansion of mast cells and/or Th2 cells, thereby reducing IL-4 levels and epithelial IL-4Rα signaling, resulting in attenuated MCM. In the current study, prior acute O3 exposure resulted in a relative increase in neutrophilic lung inflammation following repetitive O3 exposure. Several mechanisms likely contributed to the higher trend of neutrophilia. Proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and G-CSF are known to mediate neutrophil recruitment in the lung75, 76. Consistent with these reports, compared with FA-O3 mice, the O3-O3 mice exhibited increased BALF levels of G-CSF and IL-6. In addition, IL-6 and IL-17 play key roles in O3-induced neutrophil infiltration77, 78. IL-6 is critical for the differentiation and maintenance of Th17 cells79, while IL-17, a hallmark Th17 cytokine, can further amplify IL-6 production and, together with G-CSF, promote neutrophil production80. On the other hand, IL-4-induced IL-4Rα signaling has been shown to limit neutrophilic infiltration, whereas impairment of IL-4Rα signaling exacerbates rhinovirus-induced neutrophil recruitment in the lung81. Therefore, increased IL-6 and G-CSF levels together with reduced IL-4 levels raise the possibility that prior acute O3 exposure results in switching from Th2-predominated to a Th17-predominated neutrophilic response, which may contribute to the trend toward increased neutrophilic recruitment in O3-O3 mice. A critical element of this study is the inclusion of a three-week recovery period between acute high-concentration O3 exposure and the subsequent repetitive low concentration O3 exposures. Our data from the O3-FA group confirm that, by the end of this recovery period, immune cell populations had returned to baseline levels, indicating near-complete resolution of the initial injury. Of note, the three-week recovery period was selected based on our recently published report that O3-induced acute lung injury and inflammatory responses are resolved within ~ 9 days post-exposure82. The modulated responses we observed in O3-O3 mice, following near-complete resolution of initial injury, suggests more durable form of immunological modulation, potentially involving long-lasting changes in the lung microenvironment. In contrast to the current paradigm, had repetitive O3 exposures been conducted immediately after acute O3 exposure, different outcomes might have been observed, but the exact nature of such modulation remains unexplored.

Although our study demonstrates that O3 exposure modulates airway inflammation by attenuating Th2 inflammation and shifting the inflammatory profile in mice, it has a few limitations. First, only female mice were included, which may limit the generalizability of the findings because sex-related differences in immune and inflammatory responses have been reported. Second, the effect of prior acute high-concentration O3 exposure on immediately subsequent low-concentration repetitive O3 exposure could not be explored. Third, we could not identify cellular and molecular players driving the attenuation of Th2 responses in O3-O3 mice.

In summary, this study revealed several interesting findings. First, prior acute O3 exposure abolished the characteristic eosinophilic recruitment normally observed following repetitive O3 exposure, and this effect was accompanied by reduced levels of eotaxin in the airspaces. Second, prior acute O3 exposure exaggerated repetitive O3-induced neutrophilic inflammation. Third, prior acute O3 exposure diminished repetitive O3-induced MCM, which was accompanied by lower IL-4 levels, and decreased expansion of mast cells and Th2 cells. Collectively, these novel findings indicate that O3-induced acute lung injury reprograms airway innate immune and epithelial responses to subsequent repetitive O3 exposures.
