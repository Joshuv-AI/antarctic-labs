Average Steps Until Absorption on Random Walks on Sea Dragon Trees
John Estes*, Tyler Jackson, Zachary Chapman, Lillian Ates

Abstract
For a graph G G and vertices u , v u,v , we define the ASUA of v v , t ⁡ ( G , v , u ) t(G,v,u) , to be the average steps until absorption along a random walk terminating at u u . We define a sea dragon to be a tree with a unique path P P such that if d ⁡ ( u ) ≥ 3 d(u)\geq 3 for some vertex u u , then u ∈ V ⁡ ( P ) u\in V(P) . We use Markov chains to determine t ⁡ ( G , v , u ) t(G,v,u) for all vertices of several classes of sea dragons, a broad subclass of trees. Additionally, we give several results on equations related to ASUAs on general graphs.

1.2
Our Proof Method

For a given graph, calculating
N=(I-Q)^{-1}
is a standard operation, but calculating
N
for a class of graphs is not a direct exercise. However,
N^{-1}=I-Q
is rather straightforward, and since
N
is an invertible matrix, the equation
N\cdot[1]=[t]
has a unique solution as does
N^{-1}\cdot[t]=[1]
.

For Theorems
4.4
and
5.3
, we will provide a potential solution
[t]
and show that this potential solution satisfies
N^{-1}\cdot[t]=[1]
in all cases. Since there is a unique solution to this equation, our proposed solution is the true solution.

In Section
2
, we demonstrate how
N^{-1}\cdot[t]=[1]
leads to a set of constraint equations with variables
t(v_{1}),t(v_{2}),\ldots,t(v_{n-1})
that a potential solution must satisfy. We refer to these equations as
ASUA equations
.

Preliminary Results of ASUA Equations

2.1
ASUAs of Neighbors

Before presenting our main results, we identify several important results that we will rely on in subsequent sections. We define, for vertex set
S\subseteq V
,
t_{\mu}(S)
to be the average of ASUAs of vertices of
S
. That is,

t_{\mu}(S)=\dfrac{1}{|S|}\left(\sum_{v}t(G,v,u)\right),

where
u
is an absorbing vertex for a random walk on
G
. Approaching ASUAs through use of
t_{\mu}(N(v))
allows us to utilize graph structures to calculate
[t]
more directly.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
G
be a graph and
v,u\in V(G)
, and let
t(v)=T(G,v,u)
. Then

t(v)=t_{\mu}(N(v))+1.

Proof.

Let
d(v)=k
and
N(v)=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
. Then the row corresponding to
v
,
r_{v}
, of
N^{-1}=I-Q
has entry 1 at the index corresponding to
v
,
-\frac{1}{k}
for entries corresponding to
v_{i}
for all
i
, and 0 elsewhere.

Thus
r_{v}\cdot t^{T}=t(v)-\frac{1}{k}(t(v_{1})+t(v_{2})+\ldots+t(v_{k}))=1.
Hence
t(v)=t_{\mu}(N(v))+1
.
∎

Theorem
2.1
, relates
t(v)
to ASUAs of other vertices. We refer to this characterization as the ASUA equation of
t(v)
. If
d(v)=1
, we can quickly determine the ASUA equation
t(v)
.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
N(u)=\{v\}
and
t(v)=T(G,v,x)
. Then
t(u)=t(v)+1
.

Similarly,

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
v\in V
such that
N(v)=\{x,y,u\}
where
d(u)=1
. Then
t(v)=\frac{1}{2}(t(x)+t(y))+2
.

Proof.

From Theorem
2.1
and Corollary
2.2
,

t(v)=\frac{1}{3}(t(x)+t(y)+t(u))+1,\text{\hskip 36.135pt}t(u)=t(v)+1.

Thus,
3t(v)=t(x)+t(y)+t(u)+3=t(x)+t(y)+(t(v)+1)+3
. Solving for
t(v)
, we obtain
t(v)=\frac{1}{2}(t(x)+t(y))+2
.

∎

Suppose

.

Let

Proof.

By Theorem
2.1
,
t(u_{1})=t(u_{2})+1
and

for

.

We will prove, by induction, that
t(u_{1})=t(u_{j})+(j-1)^{2}
with
t(u_{1})=t(u_{1})+(1-1)^{2}
and
t(u_{1})=t(u_{2})+(2-1)^{2}

Suppose that for some

. Then,

\displaystyle t(u_{1})

\displaystyle=t(u_{a})+(a-1)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(t(u_{a-1})+t(u_{a+1}))+1+(a-1)^{2}

\displaystyle 2t(u_{1})

\displaystyle=t(u_{a-1})+t(u_{a+1})+2+2a^{2}-4a+2

\displaystyle=(t(u_{1})-(a-2)^{2})+t(u_{a+1})+2a^{2}-4a+4

\displaystyle t(u_{1})

\displaystyle=t(u_{a+1})+a^{2}.

Thus

. To complete the proof, note that
t(v)=t(u_{l+1})=t(u_{1})-l^{2}

t(u_{j})=t(u_{1})-(j-1)^{2}=(t(v)+l^{2})-(j-1)^{2}

∎

Corollary 2.5
.

Let

\displaystyle(r+2)t(v)=

\displaystyle t(x)+t(y)+\sum_{i=1}^{r}t(u_{i})+r+2

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2t(v)=

\displaystyle t(x)+t(y)+2d+2

\displaystyle t(v)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(t(x)+t(y))+(d+1).

Let

(2)

\displaystyle=n^{2}-i^{2}-2(a-1)n-1-2si+2i+i-1-2\displaystyle\sum_{j=s+1}^{a}k_{j}-k_{s}+2

\displaystyle=g(s,i)-2+k_{s}-k_{s}+2=g(s,i).

Lastly, Type IV structure is characterized by each of
v_{i-1},v_{i}
, and
v_{i+1}
all belonging to different sections. In this case,
i=k_{s}=k_{s+1}-1
, and
t(v_{i})=\dfrac{1}{2}(g(s-1,i-1)+g(s+1,i+1))+2
. Thus,

\displaystyle t(v_{i})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}(g(s-1,i-1)+g(s+1,i+1))+2

\displaystyle=n^{2}+2(a-1)n-i^{2}-1\,+

\displaystyle\hskip 36.135pt+\dfrac{1}{2}(-2(s-2)(i-1)-2s(i+1)-2\displaystyle\sum_{j=s}^{a}k_{j}-2\displaystyle\sum_{j=s+s}^{a}k_{j})+2

\displaystyle=n^{2}-i^{2}-2(a-1)n-1-2si+2i+2-2\displaystyle\sum_{j=s+1}^{a}k_{j}-k_{s}-(k_{s}+1)+2

\displaystyle=g(s,i)-1+k_{s}-k_{s}-1+2=g(s,i).

Thus
t(v_{i})=g(s,i)
for all values of
i
proving the theorem.

∎

5
ASUAs of SD2 and SD3

Now we present similar theorems for SD2 and SD3 graphs.

Theorem 5.1

(ASUAs of SD2 Trees)
.

Let
t(v_{i})=t(T(n,(k,b)),v_{i},v_{n})
. Then

t(v_{i})=\begin{cases}n^{2}-k^{2}+2(b-1)(n-k)+(k+1)^{2}-(i-1)^{2},&\text{for }1\leq i\leq k-1\\
n^{2}-i^{2}+2(b-1)(n-i),&\text{for }k\leq i\leq n-1\end{cases}

Theorem 5.2

(AUSAs of SD3 Trees)
.

Let
t(v_{i})=t(T(n,k^{(c)}),v_{i},v_{n})
. Then

t(v_{i})=\begin{cases}n^{2}-k^{2}+2(c-1)(n-k)+(k+1)^{2}-(i-1)^{2},&\text{for }1\leq i\leq k-1\\
n^{2}-i^{2}+2(c-1)(n-i),&\text{for }k\leq i\leq n-1\end{cases}

Surprisingly, ASUA equations for SD2 and SD3 graphs are the same for the same values of
b
and
c
. To prove Theorems
5.1
and
5.2
, we introduce a more general class of sea dragons, SD4.

We define the category SD4,
T(n,k,(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{r}))
, of sea dragons as follows. For
1\leq i\leq r
, let
S_{i}
be a stem of length
c_{i}
attached to
v_{k}
via
u_{i}
. If
G
is a SD4 sea dragon and
r=d
, then
G
is also SD2, and if
r=1
and
c_{1}=d
, then
G
is class SD3. We now find ASUAs of SD4 sea dragons.

Theorem 5.3

(ASUAs of SD4 Trees)
.

Let
t(v_{i})=t(T(n,k,(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{r})),v_{i},v_{n})
, and
c_{1}+c_{2}+\ldots+c_{r}=d
. Then

t(v_{i})=\begin{cases}n^{2}-k^{2}+2(d-1)(n-k)+(k-1)^{2}-(i-1)^{2},&\text{for }1\leq i\leq k-1\\
n^{2}-i^{2}+2(d-1)(n-i),&\text{for }k\leq i\leq n-1\end{cases}

Proof.

Let

f(i)=\begin{cases}n^{2}-k^{2}+2(d-1)(n-k)+(k-1)^{2}-(i-1)^{2},&\text{for }1\leq i\leq k-1\\
n^{2}-i^{2}+2(d-1)(n-i),&\text{for }k\leq i\leq n-1.\end{cases}

As before, we will show that
t(v_{i})=f(i)
for all
i
,
1\leq i\leq n-1
through use of ASUA equations providing the unique solution to
N^{-1}\cdot[t]=[1]
, and we have several cases:
i=1
,
i=n-1
,
1\leq i\leq k-2
,
k+1\leq i\leq n-1
,
i=k-1
, and
i=k
. In particular, from Theorem
2.1
and Corollary
2.5
, we will rely on the following ASUA equations:

\displaystyle t(v_{1})

\displaystyle=t(v_{2})+1

\displaystyle t(v_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}t(v_{n-1})

\displaystyle t(v_{i})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}(t(v_{i-1})+t(v_{i+1}))+1\text{ for }2\leq i\leq n-2\text{ and }n\neq k

\displaystyle t(v_{k})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}(t(v_{k-1})+t(v_{k+1}))+(d+1).

Let
a=n^{2}-k^{2}+2(d-1)(n-k)+(k-1)^{2}
, and suppose
i=1
. Then
t(v_{1})=t(v_{2})+1.
If
k\neq 2
, then
t(v_{1})=f(2)+1=a-(2-1)^{2}+1=a=f(1)
. If
k=2
, then
t(v_{1})=n^{2}-2^{2}+2(d-1)(n-2)+1=f(1)
.

Let
i=n-1
. Then
t(v_{n-1})=\dfrac{1}{2}f(n-2)+1
. If
k\neq n-1
, then

\displaystyle t(v_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}f(n-2)+1

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}(n^{2}-(n-2)^{2}+2(d-1)(n-(n-2))+1

\displaystyle=2n-1+2(d-1)

\displaystyle=n^{2}-(n-1)^{2}+2(d-1)(n-(n-1))=f(n-1).

Likewise if
i=n-1
and
k=n-1
, then

\displaystyle t(v_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}f(n-2)+1

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}(n^{2}-(n-2)^{2}+2(d-1)(n-(n-2))+(n-2)^{2}-(n-2)^{2})+1

\displaystyle=2n-1+2(d-1)

\displaystyle=n^{2}-(n-1)^{2}+2(d-1)(n-(n-1))=f(n-1).

Let
i\in\{2,\ldots,k-2\}
. Then
t(v_{i})=\dfrac{1}{2}(f(i-1)+f(i+1))+1
. Thus

\displaystyle t(v_{i})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}(f(i-1)+f(i+1))+1

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}(a-(i-2)^{2}+a-i^{2})+1

\displaystyle=a-(i-1)^{2}=f(i).

Let
i\in\{k+1,\ldots,n-2\}
. Then
t(v_{i})=\dfrac{1}{2}(f(i-1)+f(i+1))+1
. Thus

\displaystyle t(v_{i})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}(f(i-1)+f(i+1))+1

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}(n^{2}-(i-1)^{2}+2(d-1)(n-(i-1))+n^{2}-(i+1)^{2}+2(d-1)(n-(i+1)))+1

\displaystyle=n^{2}-i^{2}-1+2(d-1)n-\dfrac{1}{2}(2(d-1)(i-1)+2(d-1)(i+1))+1

\displaystyle=n^{2}-i^{2}+2(d-1)(n-i)=f(i).

Let
i=k-1
. Then
t(v_{k-1})=\dfrac{1}{2}(f(k-2)+f(k))+1
. Note that this ASUA equation requires the left and right pieces of
f(i)
. Thus,

\displaystyle t(v_{k-1})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}(f(k-2)+f(k))+1

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}(n^{2}-k^{2}+2(d-1)(n-k)+(k-1)^{2}-(k-3)^{2}+n^{2}-k^{2}+2(d-1)(n-k))+1

\displaystyle=n^{2}-k^{2}+2(d-1)(n-k)+2k-4+1

\displaystyle=n^{2}-k^{2}+2(d-1)(n-k)+k^{2}-2k+1-k^{2}+4k-4

\displaystyle=n^{2}-k^{2}+2(d-1)(n-k)+(k-1)^{2}-(k-2)^{2}=f(k-1).

Lastly, let
i=k
. Then
t(v_{k})=\dfrac{1}{2}(f(k-1)+f(k+1))+(d+1)
. Thus

\displaystyle t(v_{k})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}(f(k-1)+f(k+1))+(d+1)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}(n^{2}-k^{2}+2(d-1)(n-k)+(k-1)^{2}-(k-2)^{2}+n^{2}-k^{2}+2(d-1)(n-(k+1)))+(d+1)

\displaystyle=n^{2}-k^{2}+2(d-1)(n-k)-(d-1)-2+(d+1)

\displaystyle=n^{2}-k^{2}+2(d-1)(n-k)=f(k).

Thus
t(v_{i})=f(i)
for all
i
,
1\leq i\leq n-1
.

∎

6
Other Questions to Consider

ASUA equations provide valuable insights in calculating average steps until absorption along a random walk. In particular, they focus on local structure and avoid the need to calculate inverses of arbitrarily large matrices. There are several variations to our setup that may be considered. We allowed steps in the walk to be equally weighted, though, by adding parallel edges, one could easily adjust probabilities to fit uneven weights on the edges. If one desired multiple absorption points on a graph, say
x
and
y
, we note ASUAs for this graph are the same as a new graph in which an edge,
xy
, is added to the graph and then contracted.

For graph
G
, let
t_{\sigma}(G,u)=\sum_{V}t(v)
be the sum total of ASUAs of vertices of
G
with absorbing vertex
u
, and let
t^{\prime}(G,v,u)
be the average steps needed to traverse from
v
to
u
and back to
v
. Besides classifying ASUAs on other classes of graphs, we are interested in looking for sharp bounds for
t_{\sigma}
and
t^{\prime}
for classes of graphs. For trees, we hypothesize that
t_{\sigma}(S_{n})\leq T\leq t_{\sigma}(P_{n})
and
t^{\prime}(S_{n})\leq T\leq t^{\prime}(P_{n})
for any tree
T
of order
n
, where
S_{n}
is the star on
n
vertices.
