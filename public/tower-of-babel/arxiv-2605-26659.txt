FINOM: Fast Sinkhorn on Non-uniform Meshes
Qihao Cheng, Qichen Liao, Hao Wu, and Shuai Yang

Abstract
A linear-complexity algorithm for computing the Wasserstein-1 distance on non-uniform meshes is proposed. This work extends the fast Sinkhorn algorithms from [Q. Liao et al., Commun. Math. Sci., 20(2022)] and [Q. Liao et al., J. Sci. Comput., 98 (2024)] to non-uniform meshes. In those prior works, a distinctive collinear structure of the kernel matrix on uniform meshes was identified, enabling O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) acceleration via dynamic programming. While non-uniform meshes are prevalent in practical applications like computational fluid dynamics and finance, their lack of collinearity has hindered direct acceleration. In this paper, we introduce the concept of a “dividing index”, which partitions the kernel matrix into two blocks. We demonstrate that each block exhibits a quasi-collinear property, a generalization of the structure found in uniform meshes. Leveraging this insight, we develop F ast S I nkhorn algorithm on NO n-uniform M eshes ( FINOM ), a dynamic programming approach that reduces the per-iteration complexity of the Sinkhorn algorithm from O ⁡ ( N 2 ) O(N^{2}) to O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) . Extensive numerical experiments on 1D and 2D problems confirm these improvements, achieving speed-ups of several orders of magnitude while maintaining accuracy.

One-dimensional FINOM

In this section, we discuss the fast algorithm for the Wasserstein-1 metric on 1D non-uniform meshes. Without loss of generality, we assume that the locations
\boldsymbol{x}=\left(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{N}\right)
and
\boldsymbol{y}=\left(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{N}\right)
are sorted in ascending order, i.e.,
x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{N}
and
y_{1}<y_{2}<\cdots<y_{N}
.

2.1
Sinkhorn algorithm and the kernel matrix

The Sinkhorn algorithm
[
7
,
29
]
addresses an entropy-regularized version of Problem (
1.1
):

\min_{\Gamma\in\Pi(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})}\sum_{i,j}C_{ij}\gamma_{ij}+\varepsilon\sum_{i,j}\gamma_{ij}\left(\log\gamma_{ij}-1\right),

(2.2)

yielding an efficient and approximate optimal transport to the primal problem. The solution of Problem (
2.2
) is unique and has the form

\gamma_{ij}=\phi_{i}k_{ij}\psi_{j},\quad\text{where}\quad k_{ij}=e^{-\frac{c_{ij}}{\varepsilon}},

or equivalently,
\Gamma=\operatorname{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)K\operatorname{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{\psi}\right)
. Here
K=\left[k_{ij}\right]=e^{-C/\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

is the kernel matrix, and the scaling vectors
\boldsymbol{\phi}=\left[\phi_{i}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}=\left[\psi_{j}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

can be obtained by the iterative method:

\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(l+1)}=\boldsymbol{v}\oslash\left(K^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(l)}\right),\quad\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(l+1)}=\boldsymbol{u}\oslash\left(K\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(l+1)}\right).

(2.3)

Here
\oslash
represents point-wise division and
l
denotes the iterative counter.

Matrix-vector multiplication (e.g.,
K\boldsymbol{\psi}
) typically requires
O(N^{2})
computations and dominates the complexity of a Sinkhorn iteration in (
2.3
). In previous works
[
20
,
21
]
, by leveraging the special structure of the kernel matrix on a uniform mesh, the complexity of such matrix-vector multiplication has been effectively reduced to
O(N)
, thus accelerating the Sinkhorn algorithm. To extend this linear-complexity result to non-uniform meshes, we introduce a block partitioning framework, which is enabled by a key tool called the dividing index.

2.2
Block partitioning via the dividing index

In
[
20
,
21
]
, due to the uniformity of the mesh, the lower and upper triangular parts of the kernel matrix exhibit a collinear structure, which are defined as the Lower-ColLinear Triangular Matrix and Upper-ColLinear Triangular Matrix in
[
21
, Section 3.1]
. However, this structure does not hold for the kernel matrix under non-uniform mesh grids. Instead, an analogous structure is sought by partitioning the kernel matrix. This partitioning is formalized using the dividing index:

Definition 2.1

(Dividing index)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}=\left(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{N}\right)
and
\boldsymbol{y}=\left(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{N}\right)
be sorted meshes with
x_{1}<x_{2}<\dots<x_{N}
and
y_{1}<y_{2}<\dots<y_{N}
. Define
\zeta:\{1,2,\cdots,N\}\to\{0,1,2,\cdots,N\}
as a mapping, where for each
x_{i}
(the coordinate corresponding to row
i
of the kernel matrix
K
):

•

If
y_{1}\leq x_{i}<y_{N}
, then
\zeta(i)
is the unique index satisfying
y_{\zeta(i)}\leq x_{i}<y_{\zeta(i)+1}
.

•

If
x_{i}<y_{1}
, then
\zeta(i)=0
.

•

If
x_{i}\geq y_{N}
, then
\zeta(i)=N
.

With this dividing index, we can now impose a block partition on the kernel matrix. The dividing index decomposes
K
into two complementary sub-blocks: the lower block
K_{L}=[\check{k}_{ij}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, where

\check{k}_{ij}=\begin{cases}k_{ij}=e^{-(x_{i}-y_{j})/\varepsilon},\ &1\leq i\leq N,\;1\leq j\leq\zeta(i),\\
0,\ &1\leq i\leq N,\;\zeta(i)<j\leq N,\end{cases}\;

and the upper block
K_{U}=[\hat{k}_{ij}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, where

\hat{k}_{ij}=\begin{cases}0,\ &1\leq i\leq N,\;1\leq j\leq\zeta(i),\\
k_{ij}=e^{-(y_{j}-x_{i})/\varepsilon},\ &1\leq i\leq N,\;\zeta(i)<j\leq N.\end{cases}\;

Through this decomposition, we observe the following relation: For
1\leq i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq N,

\displaystyle\check{k}_{i_{1}j}\;/\;\check{k}_{i_{2}j}\;=\;e^{-(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{2}})/\varepsilon},\quad 1\leq j\leq\zeta(i_{1}),

(2.4)

\displaystyle\hat{k}_{i_{1}j}\;/\;\hat{k}_{i_{2}j}\;=\;e^{-(x_{i_{2}}-x_{i_{1}})/\varepsilon},\quad\zeta(i_{2})<j\leq N.

This relation shows that both
K_{L}
and
K_{U}
exhibit a row-wise constant-ratio property wherever the corresponding entries are nonzero.

Crucially, the dividing index also induces a staircase nonzero pattern. Consider
K_{L}
as an example, the nonzero entries in row
i
form a contiguous block from column 1 to column
\zeta(i)
, and these blocks are non-decreasing in length (i.e.,
\zeta(i)\leq\zeta(i+1)
), forming a lower staircase. Such specific property, namely staircase nonzero support, together with constant ratios between consecutive rows in overlapping nonzero parts, enables a compact
O(N)
-space representation of
K_{L}
, as established by the following theorem:

Theorem 2.2

(Vector representation of
K_{L}
)
.

The sub-block
K_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
induced by a dividing index
\zeta
, admits the following representation with a ratio vector
\boldsymbol{r}=[r_{i}]_{1\leq i\leq N-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
, and a set of
N
block-edge vectors
\mathcal{H}=\left\{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\eta}_{N}\right\}
with a total of
\zeta(N)
entries:

K_{L}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}\\
r_{1}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}&\;\;\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}\\
r_{1}r_{2}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}&\;\;r_{2}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}&\;\;\boldsymbol{\eta}_{3}\\
\vdots&\;\;\vdots&\;\;\vdots&\quad\ddots\\
\displaystyle\prod_{t=1}^{N-1}r_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}&\;\;\;\displaystyle\prod_{t=2}^{N-1}r_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}&\;\;\cdots&\quad\cdots&\quad\boldsymbol{\eta}_{N}&\quad&\end{pmatrix},

(2.5)

where

\displaystyle r_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\check{k}_{i+1,1}}{\check{k}_{i1}},\quad i=1,\dots,N-1,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}

\displaystyle=[\check{k}_{i,\zeta(i-1)+1}\;,\dots,\;\check{k}_{i,\zeta(i)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{\zeta(i)-\zeta(i-1)},\quad i=1,\dots,N.

(2.7)

Proof 2.3
.

Consider any entry
\check{k}_{ij}
of
K_{L}
. If there is some
s<i
such that
\zeta(s-1)<j\leq\zeta(s)
, then we have

\check{k}_{ij}=\check{k}_{sj}\;\prod\limits_{t=s}^{i-1}\frac{\check{k}_{t+1,j}}{\check{k}_{tj}}=\check{k}_{sj}\;\prod\limits_{t=s}^{i-1}\frac{\check{k}_{t+1,1}}{\check{k}_{t1}}=\check{k}_{sj}\;\prod\limits_{t=s}^{i-1}r_{t},

where
\check{k}_{sj}\in\boldsymbol{\eta}_{s}
. Otherwise

, then
\check{k}_{ij}\in\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}
or
\check{k}_{ij}=0
.

An analogous conclusion holds for
K_{U}
as well:

Theorem 2.4

(Vector representation of
K_{U}
)
.

The sub-block
K_{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
induced by a dividing index
\zeta
, admits the following representation with a ratio vector
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}=[r^{\prime}_{i}]_{1\leq i\leq N-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
, and a set of
N

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\left\{\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime}_{1},\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime}_{N}\right\}
with a total of
\left(N-\zeta(1)\right)

K_{U}=\begin{pmatrix}\quad&\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime}_{1}\;\;\;&\cdots\;\;&\cdots&\displaystyle\prod_{t=1}^{N-2}r^{\prime}_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime}_{N-1}&\;\;\displaystyle\prod_{t=1}^{N-1}r^{\prime}_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime}_{N}\\
&&\ddots&\vdots&\;\;\vdots&\vdots\\
&&&\;\;\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime}_{N-2}&\;\;r^{\prime}_{N-2}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime}_{N-1}&\;\;r^{\prime}_{N-2}r^{\prime}_{N-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime}_{N}\\
&&&&\;\;\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime}_{N-1}&\;\;r^{\prime}_{N-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime}_{N}\\
&&&&&\;\;\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime}_{N}\end{pmatrix},

(2.8)

where

\displaystyle r^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{k}_{i,N}}{\hat{k}_{i+1,N}},\quad i=1,\dots,N-1,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle=[\hat{k}_{i,\zeta(i)+1}\;,\dots,\;\hat{k}_{i,\zeta(i+1)}],\quad i=1,\dots,N.

(2.10)

.

Consider two meshes

2.3
Fast matrix-vector multiplication

The key step of the Sinkhorn algorithm is to iteratively update
\boldsymbol{\phi}
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}
through (
2.3
), where the
O(N^{2})
matrix-vector multiplications dominate the complexity. In this subsection, we present an efficient method that computes
K\boldsymbol{\psi}
and
K^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}
with linear complexity. The following discussion will focus on the computation of
K\boldsymbol{\psi}
, as the approaches for the remaining cases are similar.

First, we decompose
K\boldsymbol{\psi}=K_{L}\boldsymbol{\psi}+K_{U}\boldsymbol{\psi}\triangleq\boldsymbol{p}+\boldsymbol{q}
, where

p_{i}\;=\;\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\zeta(i)}\check{k}_{ij}\psi_{j},\quad q_{i}=\sum\limits_{j=\zeta(i)+1}^{N}\hat{k}_{ij}\psi_{j},\quad i=1,2,\cdots,N.

(2.11)

By Theorem
2.2
,
K_{L}
is fully specified by a ratio vector
\boldsymbol{r}
and block-edge vector set
\mathcal{H}=\{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. We similarly partition
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1:\zeta(N)}
into
N
segments:
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1:\zeta(N)}=[\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}^{\top},\dots,\boldsymbol{\psi}_{N}^{\top}]^{\top}
, where

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}=[\psi_{\zeta(i-1)+1},\dots,\psi_{\zeta(i)}]^{\top},\quad i=1,\dots,N.

Note that for each
i
,
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
has the same dimension as
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}
.
Instead of computing
p_{i}
directly, we start with the initial result
p_{1}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\zeta(1)}\check{k}_{1j}\psi_{j}=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}
, and use the recursive computation given by

\displaystyle p_{i+1}=\sum_{s=1}^{i}\;\;\sum_{j=\zeta(s-1)+1}^{\zeta(s)}

\displaystyle\check{k}_{i+1,j}\psi_{j}+\sum_{j=\zeta(i)+1}^{\zeta(i+1)}\check{k}_{i+1,j}\psi_{j}

(2.12)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{i}\Bigl(\prod_{t=s}^{i}r_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{s}\Bigr)\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{s}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i+1}

\displaystyle=r_{i}\Bigl(\sum_{s=1}^{i-1}\Bigl(\prod_{t=s}^{i-1}r_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{s}\Bigr)\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{s}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\Bigr)+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i+1}

\displaystyle=r_{i}p_{i}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i+1},\quad i=1,\dots,N-1.

Similarly,
\boldsymbol{q}
can be computed via a backward recursion

q_{N}=\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime}_{N}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime}_{N},\quad q_{i}=r^{\prime}_{i}q_{i+1}+\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime}_{i},\quad i=N-1,\dots,1.

(2.13)

Here
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
is the ratio vector of
K_{U}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is the set of block-edge vectors.
[\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime}_{N}]
is the corresponding partition of
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\zeta(1)+1:N}
, where each has the same dimension as
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime}_{i}
.

\zeta(1)+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N-1}\left(\zeta(i+1)-\zeta(i)+1\right)=N+\zeta(N)-1,

and the number of additions is at most

\zeta(1)-1+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N-1}\left(\zeta(i+1)-\zeta(i)\right)=\zeta(N)-1.

Analogously, computing
\boldsymbol{q}
by (
2.13
) requires at most
\left(2N-\zeta(1)-1\right)
multiplications and
\left(N-\zeta(1)-1\right)
additions. Consequently, the matrix-vector multiplication
K\boldsymbol{\psi}
requires no more than

\left(N+\zeta(N)-1\right)+\left(2N-\zeta(1)-1\right)=3N+\zeta(N)-\zeta(1)-2<4N,

(2.14)

multiplications, and

\left(\zeta(N)-1\right)+\left(N-\zeta(1)-1\right)+N=2N+\zeta(N)-\zeta(1)-2<3N,

(2.15)

additions. The additional term
+N
in (
2.15
) accounts for the extra additions incurred when computing the sum
\boldsymbol{p}+\boldsymbol{q}
. Therefore, the complexity of FINOM is
O(N)
.

Remark 2.6
.

In practice, the log-domain stabilization
[
6
]
technique is widely used to ensure the numerical stability of the Sinkhorn algorithm, which can also be incorporated into our fast matrix-vector multiplication. The log-domain stabilization uses vectors
\boldsymbol{a}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
to “absorb” the excessive part of iterates
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(t)}
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(t)}
:

\boldsymbol{a}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{a}+\varepsilon\ln\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(t)}\right),\quad\boldsymbol{b}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{b}+\varepsilon\ln\left(\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(t)}\right),\quad\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(t)}\leftarrow\mathbf{1}_{N},\quad\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(t)}\leftarrow\mathbf{1}_{N},

and the kernel is replaced with
\operatorname{diag}\left(e^{\boldsymbol{a}/\varepsilon}\right)K\operatorname{diag}\left(e^{\boldsymbol{b}/\varepsilon}\right)
. Correspondingly, we have to replace
\boldsymbol{r}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}
in (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) with

r_{i}\leftarrow e^{\left(a_{i+1}-a_{i}\right)/\varepsilon}\cdot r_{i},\quad\check{k}_{i,j}\leftarrow e^{\left(a_{i}+b_{j}\right)/\varepsilon}\check{k}_{i,j}.

The updates for
K_{U}
and for the transposed case are analogous. This leads to the stabilized fast algorithm with linear complexity.

3
Extension to high dimensions

In this section, we discuss the extension of FINOM to the 2D case, which already contains the essential idea of the higher-dimensional extension. More precisely, the 2D and higher-dimensional algorithms are obtained by applying the 1D fast matrix-vector multiplication in Section
2.3
successively along each coordinate direction.

To avoid confusion, we record the following notation.

•

K\in\mathbb{R}^{NM\times NM}
: the 2D kernel matrix, which will be shown to satisfy
K=K_{y}\otimes K_{x}
;

•

K_{[i,j]}
: the
(i,j)
-th block of
K
;

•

K_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
: the kernel matrix generated by the 1D point sets
\{x_{k}^{1}\}_{k=1}^{N}
and
\{x_{l}^{2}\}_{l=1}^{N}
along the
x
-direction;

•

K_{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
: the kernel matrix generated by the 1D point sets
\{y_{i}^{1}\}_{i=1}^{M}
and
\{y_{j}^{2}\}_{j=1}^{M}
along the
y
-direction;

•

For any matrix, its
(i,j)
-th entry is denoted by
(\cdot)_{ij}
(e.g.,
(K_{x})_{ij}
,
(K_{y})_{ij}
, or
(K_{[i,j]})_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}
);

•

\operatorname{vec}(\cdot)
: the column-major vectorization of a matrix.

3.1
The 2D kernel matrix

Consider two probability distributions

\boldsymbol{u}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{11}&u_{12}&\cdots&u_{1M}\\
u_{21}&u_{22}&\cdots&u_{2M}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
u_{N1}&u_{N2}&\cdots&u_{NM}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\boldsymbol{v}=\begin{pmatrix}v_{11}&v_{12}&\cdots&v_{1M}\\
v_{21}&v_{22}&\cdots&v_{2M}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
v_{N1}&v_{N2}&\cdots&v_{NM}\end{pmatrix},

on two 2D non-uniform meshes

\boldsymbol{x}^{1}\times\boldsymbol{y}^{1},\qquad\boldsymbol{x}^{2}\times\boldsymbol{y}^{2},

where
\boldsymbol{x}^{1},\boldsymbol{x}^{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\boldsymbol{y}^{1},\boldsymbol{y}^{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, and
\times
represents the Cartesian product of the 1D positions. We order the grid points in column-major order. The corresponding kernel matrix
K\in\mathbb{R}^{NM\times NM}
can then be written as an
M\times M
block matrix with blocks of size
N\times N
:

K=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c}K_{[1,1]}&K_{[1,2]}&K_{[1,3]}&\cdots&K_{[1,M]}\\
\hline\cr K_{[2,1]}&K_{[2,2]}&K_{[2,3]}&\cdots&K_{[2,M]}\\
\hline\cr K_{[3,1]}&K_{[3,2]}&K_{[3,3]}&\cdots&K_{[3,M]}\\
\hline\cr\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\hline\cr K_{[M,1]}&K_{[M,2]}&K_{[M,3]}&\cdots&K_{[M,M]}\\
\end{array}\right).

For each fixed
1\leq i,j\leq M
, the block
K_{[i,j]}
is the
N\times N
kernel matrix generated by the two sets of points

\{(x^{1}_{k},y^{1}_{i})\}_{k=1}^{N},\qquad\{(x^{2}_{l},y^{2}_{j})\}_{l=1}^{N}.

In other words, within the block
K_{[i,j]}
, the second coordinates
y^{1}_{i}
and
y^{2}_{j}
are fixed, while the first coordinates
x^{1}_{k}
and
x^{2}_{l}
vary. Therefore,

\left(K_{[i,j]}\right)_{kl}=e^{-\left(\left|x^{1}_{k}-x^{2}_{l}\right|+\left|y^{1}_{i}-y^{2}_{j}\right|\right)/\varepsilon}=e^{-\left|x^{1}_{k}-x^{2}_{l}\right|/\varepsilon}\,e^{-\left|y^{1}_{i}-y^{2}_{j}\right|/\varepsilon}.

(3.16)

This shows that every block shares the same matrix and differs only by a scalar factor
e^{-\left|y^{1}_{i}-y^{2}_{j}\right|/\varepsilon}
. More precisely, let

K_{x}=\left[e^{-\left|x^{1}_{k}-x^{2}_{l}\right|/\varepsilon}\right]_{k,l=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},\qquad K_{y}=\left[e^{-\left|y^{1}_{i}-y^{2}_{j}\right|/\varepsilon}\right]_{i,j=1}^{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M},

(3.17)

be the 1D kernel matrices in the two coordinate directions, then

K_{[i,j]}=(K_{y})_{ij}K_{x},\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq M.

Consequently,

K=K_{y}\otimes K_{x},

(3.18)

where
\otimes
is the Kronecker product.

3.2
Fast matrix-vector multiplication

The structure (
3.18
), together with the 1D fast matrix-vector multiplication developed in Section
2.3
, enables the efficient computation of
K\boldsymbol{\psi}
and
K^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}
for
\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathbb{R}^{NM}
.

Reshape
\boldsymbol{\psi}
into an
N\times M
matrix
\Psi
in column-major order, so that
\boldsymbol{\psi}=\operatorname{vec}(\Psi)
. Using (
3.18
) and the identity

\operatorname{vec}(AXB)=\left(B^{\top}\otimes A\right)\operatorname{vec}(X),

where
A,X,
and
B
are matrices such that the product
AXB
is well-defined, we have

K\boldsymbol{\psi}=(K_{y}\otimes K_{x})\boldsymbol{\psi}=\operatorname{vec}\left((K_{x}\Psi)K_{y}^{\top}\right).

(3.19)

In the identity above, by taking
A=K_{x},\;X=\Psi,
and
B=K_{y}^{\top}
, we directly obtain the expression on the right-hand side.
Similarly, reshaping
\boldsymbol{\phi}
into an
N\times M
matrix
\Phi
in column-major order, so that
\boldsymbol{\phi}=\operatorname{vec}(\Phi)
, we obtain

K^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}=(K_{y}^{\top}\otimes K_{x}^{\top})\boldsymbol{\phi}=\operatorname{vec}\left((K_{x}^{\top}\Phi)K_{y}\right).

(3.20)

Incorporating (
3.19
) and (
3.20
) into the Sinkhorn iteration produces the 2D FINOM, as summarized in Algorithm

.

Remark 3.1
.

The 2D FINOM can also be combined with log-domain stabilization. Although the absorbed kernel

K^{\prime}=\operatorname{diag}(e^{\boldsymbol{a}/\varepsilon})K\operatorname{diag}(e^{\boldsymbol{b}/\varepsilon})

generally no longer admits a direct Kronecker product representation, the matrix-vector multiplications

K^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\psi}
and
(K^{\prime})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}
can still be carried out efficiently by applying the 1D stabilized fast algorithm
successively along each coordinate direction. In this way, the stabilized 2D algorithm preserves the same
linear complexity
O(NM)
with respect to the total number of grid points. For completeness, the detailed
derivation and the corresponding algorithm are provided in Appendix
A
.

Remark 3.2
.

The same idea extends directly to higher dimensions. If the mesh has sizes
n_{1},\dots,n_{d}
and the kernel factorizes as
K=K_{d}\otimes\cdots\otimes K_{1}
, then each application of
K
or
K^{\top}
can be carried out successively along each coordinate direction by the same 1D fast algorithm. Therefore, the per-iteration complexity remains linear in the total number of grid points
\prod_{\alpha=1}^{d}n_{\alpha}
.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed FINOM, a fast Sinkhorn algorithm for computing the entropy-regularized Wasserstein-1 distance on non-uniform meshes. In one dimension, we introduced the dividing index and showed that the resulting partitioned kernel matrices possess a quasi-collinear structure, which leads to a linear-complexity dynamic programming method for matrix-vector multiplication. In two and higher dimensions, the fast algorithm is obtained by applying the one-dimensional fast multiplication successively along each coordinate direction, so that the per-iteration complexity remains linear in the total number of grid points.
Numerical experiments on 1D and 2D random distributions show that FINOM significantly outperforms the standard Sinkhorn algorithm in computational time while producing essentially identical transport plans. The observed timings are consistent with the theoretical
O(N)
behavior in 1D and
O(NM)
behavior in 2D.

Appendix A
2D FINOM with log-domain stabilization

In this appendix, we provide the detailed derivation mentioned in Remark
3.1
. While the Kronecker product representation enables efficient computation, it makes log-domain stabilization
[
6
]
harder to exploit. Under log-domain stabilization, the kernel becomes
K^{\prime}=\operatorname{diag}(e^{\boldsymbol{a}/\varepsilon})K\operatorname{diag}(e^{\boldsymbol{b}/\varepsilon})
, with arbitrary absorption vectors
\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{NM}
. However,
K^{\prime}
generally lacks a Kronecker factorization compatible with
K=K_{y}\otimes K_{x}
, because the absorption vectors
\boldsymbol{a}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
are typically not separable across dimensions. This prevents direct Kronecker-based fast computation under log-domain stabilization.

To address this issue, we do not work directly with the matrix
K^{\prime}
. Instead, we evaluate
K^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\psi}
and
(K^{\prime})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}
through decoupled 1D steps, so that the Kronecker structure of
K
can still be exploited after incorporating the absorption vectors.
This decoupled procedure extends the one-dimensional stabilization in Remark
2.6
to higher dimensions without increasing complexity, thereby preserving numerical stability and computational efficiency.

We illustrate the idea using
K^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\psi}
; the case of
(K^{\prime})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}
is handled in the same way.
Specifically, reshape
\boldsymbol{b}
into
B\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
in column-major order. Then,

\displaystyle K^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\psi}=\operatorname{diag}(e^{\boldsymbol{a}/\varepsilon})

\displaystyle K\operatorname{diag}(e^{\boldsymbol{b}/\varepsilon})\boldsymbol{\psi}

(1.21)

\displaystyle=e^{\boldsymbol{a}/\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\odot\left(K\left(e^{\boldsymbol{b}/\varepsilon}\odot\boldsymbol{\psi}\right)\right)=e^{\boldsymbol{a}/\varepsilon}\odot\left(\left(K_{y}\otimes K_{x}\right)\left(e^{\boldsymbol{b}/\varepsilon}\odot\boldsymbol{\psi}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=e^{\boldsymbol{a}/\varepsilon}\odot\mathrm{vec}\left(\left(K_{x}\left(e^{B/\varepsilon}\odot\Psi\right)\right)K_{y}^{\top}\right),

where
\boldsymbol{\psi}=\operatorname{vec}(\Psi)
and
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication.

Let
Q=K_{x}\left(e^{B/\varepsilon}\odot\Psi\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
. For each
j=1,\dots,M
, the
j
-th column

Q(:,j)=K_{x}\left(e^{B(:,j)/\varepsilon}\odot\Psi(:,j)\right)=K_{x}\operatorname{diag}\left(e^{B(:,j)/\varepsilon}\right)\Psi(:,j)\;,

can be computed using the 1D stabilized fast matrix-vector multiplication
K_{x}\Psi(:,j)
, with absorption vectors
\boldsymbol{a}_{x}=\mathbf{0}_{N}
,
\boldsymbol{b}_{x}=B(:,j)
in Remark
2.6
. Together, these
M
columns form the full matrix
Q
. Each computation takes
O(N)
, resulting in a total complexity of
O(NM)
.

Reshaping
\boldsymbol{a}
into
A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
in column-major order, (
1.21
) can be rewritten as

K^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\psi}=e^{\boldsymbol{a}/\varepsilon}\odot\mathrm{vec}\left(QK_{y}^{\top}\right)=\mathrm{vec}\left(e^{A/\varepsilon}\odot QK_{y}^{\top}\right).

(1.22)

Let
P=e^{A/\varepsilon}\odot QK_{y}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
. Similar to the computation of
Q
, the matrix
P
can be computed row by row. For each
k=1,\dots,N
, the
k
-th row satisfies

P(k,:)=e^{A(k,:)/\varepsilon}\odot\left(Q(k,:)K_{y}^{\top}\right)=\left(\operatorname{diag}\left(e^{A(k,:)^{\top}/\varepsilon}\right)K_{y}Q(k,:)^{\top}\right)^{\top},

Therefore,
P(k,:)
can be obtained by applying the 1D stabilized fast algorithm to
K_{y}Q(k,:)^{\top}
with left absorption
\boldsymbol{a}_{y}=A(k,:)^{\top}
and right absorption
\boldsymbol{b}_{y}=\mathbf{0}_{M}
. Each of the
N
row computations costs
O(M)
, leading to a total complexity of
O(NM)
.

Finally,
K^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\psi}=\operatorname{vec}(P)
, so the overall complexity remains
O(NM)
. The computation of
(K^{\prime})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}
