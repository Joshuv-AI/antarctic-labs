Advances in oncolytic viruses immunotherapy of hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Shilin You, Min Wang, Tengyue Zhang, Qingwen Wang, Mingjun Zhang, Haiting Wang, Xuesi Hua, Yanping Su, Yongqing Li, Peng Qu, Ye Qiu, Yinqing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis review aims to address the therapeutic potential of new generation of oncolytic viruses (OVs) for liver cancer with the focus on hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).MethodsWe evaluate the therapeutic status of oncolytic virus therapy (OVT) for liver cancer, addresses the research question of how different OVs perform in treating the malignancy, and and analyze the challenges remain in their clinical treatment based on the synthesis of both preclinical and clinical studies investigating various OVs, including Herpes simplex virus (HSV), Adenovirus (AdV), Vaccinia virus (VV), Coxsackievirus (COX), and Newcastle disease virus (NDV).ResultsOVs selectively infect and lyse tumor cells, stimulating anti-tumor immunity. HSV and VV have demonstrated high efficacy and safety in studies. Genetically engineered AdV and NDV platforms, especially those expressing immune checkpoint inhibitors or cytokines, show promising anti-tumor activity. Advances in viral engineering and delivery systems have improved tumor selectivity and immune activation. Key challenges identified include host antiviral immunity, delivery efficiency, and optimal patient selection.ConclusionOVT represents a promising immunotherapeutic strategy for liver cancer. While significant progress has been made in both efficacy and safety through genetic modification, ongoing innovation in viral engineering, combination therapies.

Introduction

Liver cancer is global health burden due to increasing rates of morbidity and mortality. It is evaluated that there were 905,700 new liver cancer cases and 830,200 new liver cancer deaths worldwide in 2020 [84]. Therefore, these results lead to the urgent need for more effective treatments.

About 90% of all primary liver cancers is HCC, which is caused by Chronic viral hepatitis, particularly hepatitis B and C [94], and other risk factors such as alcohol abuse, obesity, fatty liver, and diabetes [79]. Even though the progress in both prevention and screening of HCC is made, many patients are still diagnosed at late stages, when curative treatment is no longer possible [56]. The Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging system is built to guide clinical treatment of HCC [40]. In the early stages (BCLC 0–A), surgery and ablation is offered as potential cures. For intermediate disease (BCLC B), survival rates were improved via transarterial chemoembolization. the patients with Late-stage HCC (BCLC C) is often treated with tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as sorafenib. In end-stage disease (BCLC D), therapeutic approaches are extremely limited, and liver transplantation may be considered as one of them for a few patients [30]. Current therapies rarely produce durable responses. Many HCC patients’ survival rates are low due to tumor heterogeneity, immune evasion [57], and drug resistance [66, 67]. These challenges have driven growing interest in novel therapeutic methods, including immunotherapy and virotherapy. Among them, OVT has been regarded as one particularly promising approach gradually with the development of genetic engineering.

Anti-tumor mechanisms of Ovs

OVs are designed to selectively infect and destroy cancer cells while normal tissues are rarely damaged. Their anti-tumor activity relies on two main mechanisms: direct oncolysis and anti-tumor immune activation [6, 47, 50]. After infection, OVs replicate inside tumor cells, leading to cell lysis and the release of both viral particles and tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), further activating pathogen- and damage-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs and DAMPs), which stimulate dendritic cell (DC) maturation, type I interferon production, and T-cell priming.Woo et al. [100] The lysis process also damages tumor vasculature and breaks down the surrounding stromal barrier, helping the virus spread deeper into the tumor mass. As a result, cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), natural killer (NK) cells, and macrophages are recruited to attack the tumor (Fig. 1).

(2) Innate immune activation: OV infection induces robust secretion of type I interferons, which activate NK cells to mediate non-specific cytotoxicity against tumor cells. (3) Adaptive immune activation: OV-mediated tumor cell lysis releases TAAs (including neoantigens), which are internalized, processed, and presented by activated APCs, such as dendritic cells via MHC class I and II molecules. APCs engage TCRs on naive CD8 + and CD4 + T cells, respectively, triggering their clonal expansion and activation. Activated CD8 + CTLs execute antigen-specific killing of tumor cells, while CD4 + helper T cells secrete IL-2 to amplify CD8 + T cell effector function and support long-term im-munological memory. These coordinated interactions between OVs, tumor cells, endothelial cells, and innate/adaptive immune effectors result in the systemic antitumor immune response that eliminates local tumors and suppresses distant metastatic disease. Genetic engineering enhances the anti-tumor response of OVs. Many OVs are modified to express immune-stimulatory molecules such as Granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), IL-12, or checkpoint inhibitors targeting PD-1 or PD-L1 [1, 49, 78]. These modifications strengthen T-cell activation, reduce immune suppression in the tumor microenvironment (TME), and establish long-lasting systemic immunity—especially valuable in metastatic or multi-focal liver cancer. However, challenges still remain. The host immune system limit viral spread through neutralizing antibodies and cytokine responses [95]. Both safety and pathogenicity concerns may be carefully managed. Continued advances in viral engineering, delivery strategies, and immune modulation are essential to unlock the full therapeutic potential of OVs in cancer therapy.

Categories of Ovs

Both naturally occurring and genetically engineered viruses were revealed to exhibit the strong anti-tumor activity and induce long-term immune response. Each of them has unique biological features, replication strategies, and immune interactions that influence its therapeutic potential. In the review, we display the several viruses, including AdV, HSV, NDV, MV(Measles Virus), COX, VV. These viruses have been investigated in both pre-clinical studies (Table 1) and clinical trials (Table 2) for liver cancer treatment, indicating the potential in OVs as a targeted therapeutic approach [52]. The researchers identified the suitable candidates for different tumor types due to the diversity of OVs [50]. These comparative approaches have accelerated progress in understanding how OVs destroy cancer cells while minimizing toxicity.

The milestone in this field reached in 2015, when Talimogene laherparepvec (T-VEC), an engineered HSV-1 strain for melanoma treatment was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [23]. T-VEC has been considered as a significant stage in OVT, since it contribute to durable tumor control in humans with broader applications in solid tumors, including HCC. Another notable OV is Pexastimogene devacirepvec (Pexa-Vec), vaccinia virus with the deletion of the thymidine kinase gene which restricts viral replication within tumor cells [86, 92]. Pexa-Vec expressing GM-CSF recruits DCs through interferon cytokine expression, enhancing tumor infiltration [77]. Even although phase III trials for HCC showed mixed results, its safety and immunogenicity were confirmed in earlier studies, indicating the potential of OVs in liver cancer therapy [85].

Research progress of oncolytic virotherapy in liver cancer

OV in liver cancer treatment

Liver cancer remains one of the most difficult cancers to treat, particularly in advanced stages. Traditional therapies such as surgery, chemotherapy, transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), and immunotherapy have improved therapeutic effect, but remain limit due to tumor recurrence and immune tolerance [64]. Many patients are ineligible for curative treatments, underscoring the need for new therapeutic approaches. OVT offers one unique approach by combining direct tumor lysis with the activation of immune system. Both pre-clinical study and clinical trials have demonstrated the potential of OVs in HCC [59]. These studies have provided encouraging evidence of tumor regression and immune remodeling. The effectiveness of OVT in HCC depends on several factors, including receptor expression on tumor cells, the host immune response, and viral genetics [71]. Commonly used viral platforms in HCC research include HSV, AdV, VV, IV and COX [33]. Each of these viruses has distinct biological advantages and has been applied due to tumor selectivity, replication efficiency, and immune modulation. We have shown both advantages and limitations of the different types of virus (Table 3).

ADV

AdVs are divided into the genera Mastadenovirus and Aviadenovirus on the basis of their origin [12]. They are further classified into seven types according to the isolated genotypes [42]. AdVs are non-enveloped DNA viruses which possess a capsid with the characteristics of a multi-vertex icosahedron [11]. While AdVs cause respiratory, gastrointestinal, and ocular infections, their well-characterized genome and safety profile have made them attractive platforms for OVT [70]. Human Adenovirus serotype 5 (Ad5) is the most commonly used type of virus in current research [103], due to its small genome, easy operation, high titer after purification, and the ability to effectively infect and lyse tumor cells through multiple receptors such as adenovirus receptor, CD46 and integrin.Hensen et al. [38]. Ad5 has a strong safety record with high gene delivery capacity and a low risk of genomic integration which minimizes mutagenic potential [107]. These characteristics make it an ideal vector for HCC therapy [44]. AdVs enter the human body as an anti-tumor agent in vivo, triggering the body ‘s immune response [13]. At the same time, they are not integrated with the host gene during replication and expression, and have good bio-safety [82].

Recent studies have focused on genetically engineered AdVs to enhance tumor selectivity and immune activation. One novel engineered AdVs, Ad-GD55–α-Tim-3, uses the GP73 promoter to drive viral replication and encodes an antibody against TIM-3, an immune checkpoint molecule highly expressed on HCC cells [60]. In HCC models, the virus was shown to reduce the expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6), increase anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10), decrease immuno-suppressive factors such as TGF-β and IDO [80], and inhibit tumor growth without damaging normal tissues. Another innovative AdV5, Ad5-ApoA1, expresses apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1) to remodel cholesterol metabolism within TME, promoting cholesterol efflux in CD8⁺ T cells, further reducing the expression of both PD-1 and LAG-3 on CD8⁺ T cells, reversing T-cell exhaustion, and inducing long-term immune memory. The approach combines metabolic regulation with oncolytic activity, producing strong anti-tumor effects in pre-clinical HCC models [102].

Clinical progress has been made with OBP-301, an attenuated Ad5 driven by the human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT) promoter, by which viral replication was restricted within tumor cells. In a phase I trial of patients with late-stage HCC, OBP-301was revealed to have an excellent safety, increased CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, and disease stabilization in most participants, even although overall efficacy remained modest [39]. Recent clinical study tested the combined treatment of H101 (Oncorine), an oncolytic AdV with the PD-1 inhibitor Nivolumab in patients with refractory late-stage HCC. The regimen got an objective response rate of 11% and a median overall survival of 15 months, indicating that the combination therapy may be benefit for clinical treatment of HCC [105]. Mechanistically, H101 infection of HCC cells upregulates PD-L1 expression as a feedback response to antiviral immunity, while simultaneously increasing intratumoral CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration. Nivolumab then blocks the PD-1/PD-L1 interaction, restoring T-cell effector functions including IFN-γ and granzyme B release. This dual mechanism of “virus-induced immunogenic cell death (ICD) plus checkpoint blockade” converts the immunosuppressive “cold” TME into an immunogenic “hot” TME. Optimal sequencing (AdV administered prior to anti-PD-1) is recommended to establish a T-cell-infiltrated baseline. These findings indicate that AdV-based OVT can serve as a versatile platform for combination immunotherapy in HCC. In summary, AdV-based OVT is disposed as a versatile and well-studied approach for liver cancer treatment. By integrating tumor-selective promoters, immunomodulatory genes, and combination approaches, AdVs were continually constructed to move toward more potent and durable therapeutic approach against HCC.

HSV

HSVs are primarily categorized into two types: HSV-1 and HSV-2 [98]. Both are DNA-based viruses [99] that facilitate the binding of viral particles to host cells through the glycoproteins B and C on their surfaces [41]. HSV-1 is associated with oral and skin infections, while HSV-2 is typically linked to genital infections [51]. HSV-1 has become one of the most widely used viral backbones for oncolytic virotherapy (oHSV). It provides several advantages, including rapid host cell entry, broad receptor distribution, efficient replication, and the ability to stimulate both cellular and humoral immune responses [90]. These properties promote HSV-1 to become one strong candidate for treating HCC [49]. Up now, most clinical investigations about HSV1 were involved in the treatment of solid tumors such as melanoma, glioma, and breast cancer, and the encouraging results have been reported. However, it faces numerous challenges in clinical application, such as vector complexity, limited stability, and potential off-target effects. Even though numerous pre-clinical studies have been shown potent anti-tumor activity of oHSV against liver cancer, few clinical trials have been completed successfully, indicating that the anti-tumor roles of oHSV for HCC need be investigated further .

Recent advances in genetic engineering have led to highly selective HSV variants which were armed with immune-modulating genes. HSV1 expressing a single-chain antibody fragment (scFv) against human PD-1 was investigated to show strong anti-tumor efficacy in both mouse and nude mouse models bearing liver tumor [49]. YST-oHSV, one modified HSV1, significantly inhibited HCC growth, enhanced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, and established long-term immune memory without harming normal tissues [49]. Mechanistically, YST-oHSV achieves local and sustained PD-1 blockade within the TME by expressing an anti-PD-1 scFv, which avoids systemic toxicity associated with conventional ICIs, andsimultaneously reverses T-cell exhaustion. The virus-induced tumor lysis releases TAAs and DAMPs, promoting DC maturation and CD8⁺ T-cell cross-priming. The strategy synergizes direct oncolysis with local immune checkpoint inhibition, establishing long-term systemic immune memory against HCC.

Another promising strategy is localized delivery. A study were completed to compare oHSVs delivered via transarterial viroembolization (TAVE) with those via intratumoral injections (conventional methods) in VX2 liver cancer models. The results demonstrated that oHSVs delivered via TAVE contributed to greater viral distribution within the liver tumors, higher tumor necrosis rates, prolonged survival rates of the animals compared to those delivered via intratumoral injections, further generating anti-tumor effects and immune memory, particularly for immune-excluded HCC [17]. The synergistic mechanisms of TAVE include: (1) Physical synergy – embolic agents (such as lipiodol) not only induce ischemic necrosis but also delay viral clearance by reducing blood flow, thereby prolonging viral residence and replication within the tumor [93];

(2) Immune synergy – TACE-induced necrosis releases abundant DAMPs and TAAs, which synergize with oHSV-induced PAMPs to activate TLR pathways, promote DC cross-priming, and elicit systemic anti-tumor immunity. Additionally, the post-embolization inflammatory microenvironment upregulates adhesion molecules (such as ICAM-1), facilitating viral entry into tumor cells. This sequential application (embolization first, followed by oHSV) maximizes spatial tumor killing and temporal immune amplification, particularly for immune-excluded HCC [17].

To improve systemic delivery, some of researchers have developed surface-engineered oHSV coated with glycosylated polyethylene glycol (glyco-PEG) [61]. The modification protects the virus from neutralizing antibodies, and directs it toward HCC cells that express the asialoglycoprotein receptor. In mouse models, the glyco-PEG–modified oHSV was found to exert increased tumor specificity, deepen tissue penetration, reduce off-target effects, and enhance immune activation [61].

Early clinical evidence supports the safety of oHSV-based therapy for liver tumors. In a phase I/II study, After the treatment of the oncolytic HSV NV1020, 50% of patients with colorectal cancer liver metastases was improved, following an overall survival of 11.8 months with minimal toxicity [36]. However, recent Ib/II clinical trial of intrahepatic T-VEC plus Pembrolizumab was revealed to exhibit limited efficacy—ORRs of 8.3% (non-HCC liver mets) and 13.6% (HCC) with no clinical activity across tumor types (NCT02509507). These results contrast with promising preclinical data, highlighting the translation challenges in oHSV therapy and underscoring the need for viral constructs, patient selection, and delivery strategies.

HSV-based OVT has been strengthened rapidly through improvements in precision targeting, immunomodulation, and delivery methods, even though the clinical treatment in liver cancer remains a challenge, both engineered HSV vectors and combination therapy holds significant potential for future therapeutic breakthrough.

NDV

NDV is a highly contagious RNA virus in birds [4, 87]. Its genome replication involves the nucleoprotein (NP), phosphoprotein (P), matrix (M), fusion (F), hemagglutinin-neuraminidase (HN), RNA polymerase (L), as well as V and W proteins [19]. NDV exhibits oncolytic properties such as tumor-selective replication, lysis, and immune stimulation, was investigated as one promising candidate for OVT [88]. The anti-tumor potential of NDV was first reported in 1964, when one patient with myeloid leukemia exhibited significant reduction in leukemic blasts after NDV treatment [97]. Since then, NDV has gained increasing attention as one OV. Over ten clinical trials about anti-tumor of NDV were studied, involving several types of cancer, such as melanoma [18], glioblastoma [68], and ovarian cancer [3].

In recent years, systematic progress has been made in NDV therapy against HCC due to natural strain screening, genetic engineering, delivery system improvement, and immune mechanism studies. Early research focused on enhancing the oncolytic activity of NDV via genetic modification. Altomonte et al., constructed the recombinant virus rNDV/F3aa(L289A) with an L289A mutation to promote HCC cell fusion and apoptosis in vitro [5]. In immunocompetent rat models, hepatic artery infusion of virus induced tumor-specific syncytia formation, extensive necrosis, and significantly prolonged survival without notable toxicity [5]. The preclinical study demonstrated that engineering the viral fusion protein can enhance the oncolytic potency of NDV [5].

Zhong et al., developed a recombinant NDV carrying a porcine α1,3-galactosyltransferase gene (NDV-GT) [110]. NDV-GT OVT was revealed to induce the expression of αGal antigens on tumor cells, triggering complement-mediated hyperacute rejection and subsequent cellular immune responses [110]. In one CRISPR/Cas9-generated primary liver cancer model in monkeys, After intravenous NDV-GT injection, significant anti-tumor effects were observed with the increased infiltration of NK cells, CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells within TME [110]. In addition, they completed an interventional clinical trial (ChiCTR2000031980) involving patients with advanced solid tumors, including HCC, to reveal the disease control rate of 90% after NDV-GT treatment with no severe adverse events or high levels of neutralizing antibodies [110]. These findings indicate that NDV-GT lyses tumor cells directly and elicits systemic anti-tumor immunity, promising clinical translational potential [110].

Beyond engineered strains, naturally occurring NDV with potent oncolytic activity have also been explored. Chen et al., screened multiple wild-type NDV strains to identify HK84, which inhibited growth by over 80% in several HCC cell lines, including SK-HEP-1, at low multiplicities of infection [21]. In nude mouse xenograft models, intratumoral injection of NDV/HK84 led to complete regression in some tumors. RNA sequencing results revealed that its oncolytic activity was associated with strong activation of the type I interferon signaling pathway [21], suggesting that certain natural NDV strains possess efficient and relatively safe feature.

NDV-based OVT also activates innate immune cells, particularly natural killer (NK) cells within TME. Huang et al., revealed that NK cells stimulated with NDV-infected Huh7 cells led to the differential expression of 1568 genes in NK cells, including IFNG, TRAIL, and FAS, compared to those in control group [45]. Pathway enrichment analysis indicated these genes were significantly associated with RIG-I-like receptor signaling, NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, and Jak-STAT signaling pathways [45]. The study provides molecular insight into how NDV may enhance NK cell recognition and tumor-killing capacity by reshaping their gene expression profiles [45].

To address challenges such as neutralization and poor tumor targeting during systemic delivery, novel delivery methods have been developed. Li et al. constructed a gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) porous microneedle patch loaded with NDV [58]. The formulation enabled the sustained release of infectious virus for over 24 h in vitro. Compared to free virus, microneedle-delivered NDV suppressed proliferation, migration, and invasion of HepG2 live cancer cells more effectively and induced higher apoptosis levels [58]. In HepG2 xenograft models, the microneedle system was showed to contribute to stronger inhibition ability of tumor growth without significant organ damage or weight loss in mice [58], offering a new technical approach for both sustained release and enhanced efficacy of NDV. Mechanistically, the microneedle breaches the stromal barrier, delivering NDV directly into the tumor bed, while the GelMA matrix provides three-dimensional support, protects the virus from neutralizing antibodies, and achieves a concentration-time synergy. The physical delivery system also enhances the local release of NDV-induced PAMPs and TAAs, prolonging DC activation and T-cell priming compared to bolus injection. The “material-plus-virus” strategy offers one novel approach for local treatment of HCC, particularly for superficial or accessible tumors.

In summary, NDV-based OVT for HCC have diversified, encompassing strain selection, genetic engineering, novel delivery systems, and immune mechanism exploration. These advances have improved the direct oncolytic efficacy of NDV, and deepened the understanding of its immunomodulatory effects within TME, laying a solid foundation for NDV as the monotherapy or in combination with other immunotherapies for HCC.

MV

MV is a single-stranded RNA virus belonging to paramyxovirus, which is spherical or linear in shape. Its genome is about 16KB in length. Six genes in the genome encode six structural and functional proteins respectively: nucleoprotein, phosphorylated protein, M protein, fusion protein, Hemagglutinin protein and RNA dependent RNA polymerase [83]. Attenuated vaccines for MV have been shown to be safe in clinical trials over the past few decades, which gives MV an advantage in OVT. The oncolytic efficacy of MV can be improved by genetic engineering, such as inserting immunoregulatory transgenes to stimulate anti-tumor immune response in the laboratory [8]. Through genetic modification, MV are targeted to infect specific cancer cells, reducing the adverse reactions caused by excessive doses of drugs. MV echistatin vector (MV-ERV) is a viral vector targeting integrin alpha (v)beta3, which is based on MV Edmonston vaccine strain. MV-ERV can target tumor cells expressing CD46 receptor [109]. The oncolytic activity of MV by inserting immunomodulatory factors was improved to stimulate the anti-tumor immune response. For example, tumor cells that express moderate or high levels of interleukin-13 receptor α2 (IL-13Rα2) receptor are targeted by MV-GFP-HAA-IL-13, which are unable to infect the non-transformed cells, such as normal human astrocytes and fibroblaststhat, do not express the IL-13Rα2 receptor [32]. MV-NAP virus stimulates the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines by expressing helicobacter pylori neutrophil activating protein (NAP), inducing an effective anti-tumor immune response in lung and breast cancer xenotransplantation models [16]. Oncolytic MV has been demonstrated in multiple solid tumor such as lung cancer, multiple myeloma, non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, and ovarian cancer. Most phase I clinical trials of oncolytic MV have been progressed successfully, and MV therapy has recently emerged as safe and promise approach for human cancer treatment. But clinical trials are still in their early stages, and the benefits can be limited by the present immunity against MV [14].

COX-A

COX belongs to the Picornaviridae family and is a small, non-enveloped virus with a single-stranded positive-sense RNA genome [15]. It is divided into two main groups: Coxsackievirus A (COX-A) and Coxsackievirus B (COX-B). COX-A is typically associated with hand, foot, and mouth disease, while COX-B is linked to myocarditis and other systemic infections [81]. Despite its pathogenic potential, COX-A has recently gained attention as the safe and effective OV. COX-A lies in its strong tumor selectivity and natural ability to induce cell death. The virus enters cells through specific receptors— decay-accelerating factor (DAF) and intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1)—which are frequently over-expressed on the surface of tumor cells, including HCC [15]. After infection, COX-A replicates rapidly and triggers apoptosis or immune-mediated tumor destruction.

COX-A21 (V937) is regarded as the most extensively studied strain [25]. its efficient entry is completed in the presence of both DAF and ICAM-1, both of which provide high specificity for tumor cells. In pre-clinical tumor models, after COX-A21 treatment, robust anti-tumor immunity was induced by the activation of T cells and cytokine release without damaging normal tissues [91]. Clinical studies have confirmed its favorable safety with no grade 3 or 4 adverse events reported [7]. A recent phase II open-label trial tested V937 in patients with metastatic melanoma involving the liver cancer [69]. Both Intratumoral and intravenous administrations of OVs were revealed to produce measurable tumor shrinkage in both injected and non-injected lesions, indicating that V937 treatment induces systemic immune activation. Even although only partial responses were observed, the median progression-free survival of 3.7 months demonstrated biological activity and safety. While clinical efficacy in liver cancer were partially found, its ability to activate systemic anti-tumor immunity promotes it one valuable candidate for future trials in HCC, particularly in combination with immunotherapy treatment.

Oncolytic VV

VV, a double-stranded DNA poxvirus, is best known for its historical use as the smallpox vaccine [34]. It was revealed to has a large, brick-shaped structure and replicate entirely within the cytoplasm of infected cells via its own DNA-dependent enzymes [24]. Since it is not necessary for VV to enter nuclear, it avoids interference with host DNA to enhance its safety as OV. VV possesses several advantages for cancer therapy. It exhibits a large genetic capacity which allows the insertion of therapeutic transgenes, and the stability for both intravenous and intratumoral delivery [27]. Its rapid infection causes tumor cell lysis within 24 h, followed by strong activation of innate and adaptive immune responses.

Recent studies have explored multiple approaches to enhance the antitumor efficacy of VV in HCC. One example is VV-AVL, a recombinant VV carrying Aphrocallistes vastus lectin (AVL) which enhances tumor cytotoxicity as the marine lectin. VV-AVL was displayed to reduce HCC viability significantly, induce both HCC apoptosis and increased interferon (IFN-α/β) production by activating IRF3 signaling. In tumor mouse models, the anti-tumor efficacy of VV-AVL was observed to suppress tumor growth and induce strong immune responses without harming normal tissues.

One recombinant oncolytic VV based on the Guang9 strain (VG9) and armed with the IL-24 transgene (VG9-IL-24) has been investigated to exert promising antitumor efficacy in preclinical models of HCC [28]. IL-24 is one tumor suppressor cytokine known to selectively induce apoptosis in cancer cells. After VG9-IL-24 treatment, both efficient replication and specific cytotoxicity were observed in various HCC cell lines (SMMC-7721, HepG2, Hep3B) [28], while minimal replication and cytotoxicity were present in normal human liver cells (HL-7702). The scientist display high levels of IL-24 expression, increased apoptosis of tumor cell apoptosis and G2/M cell cycle arrest in treated HCC cells. In vivo, after a single intratumoral injection of VG9-IL-24 into SMMC-7721 xenograft models in nude mice, tumor blockage and prolonged survival were found compared to control treatment groups. The preclinical study suggests that VG9-IL-24 is worthy of further investigation for HCC therapy as one promising candidate.

Another promising construct, TG4023 (MVA-FCU1), is a modified vaccinia Ankara (MVA) strain encoding the FCU1 gene, which converts the non-toxic prodrug flucytosine into 5-fluorouracil within tumors. In a phase I clinical trial for liver cancer treatment, intratumoral TG4023 injections combined with oral flucytosine were well tolerated. About half of the patients achieved disease stabilization, with only mild fever and fatigue as side effects. The clinical trial demonstrated the feasibility and safety of VV-based gene therapy in liver tumors.

Despite three oncolytic VVs above, oncolytic VV were armed with other different transgenes for HCC treatment. One of the most popular VV-based OVs is Pexastimogene devacirepvec (Pexa-Vec, JX-594 [76], which lacks the thymidine kinase gene, restricting replication in tumor cells with active DNA synthesis, and expresses GM-CSF to enhance immune cell recruitment [74]. in early-phase clinical trials, tumor necrosis and T-cell activation were observed in HCC patients [76]. However, in a large phase III trial comparing Pexa-Vec plus sorafenib with sorafenib alone, the survival rates of patients were not improved, likely due to suboptimal dosing schedules and immunosuppressive effects of sorafenib [1]. According to the researchers’ analysis, In early studies, the replication of DNA viruses is closely dependent on thymidine kinase (TK) [37]. Pexa-Vec had its TK1-related genes deactivated during modification, rendering it reliant on continuously dividing active cells for its own replication. Sorafenib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) [48], was found to block the constant division of liver cancer cells. Thus, Sorafenib inhibits the replication of Pexa-Vec, preventing the combined benefit from emerging. Mechanistic insight into antagonism: Sorafenib, as a TKI, suppresses cell cycle-dependent kinase activity, thereby blocking the replication of Pexa-Vec, which relies on active DNA synthesis in dividing cells due to its TK gene deletion [37]. This antagonism highlights a critical principle for combination therapy: TK-deleted VVs should not be combined with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) that arrest cell cycle progression.

Potential alternative combinations: In contrast, combining VV with TKIs with anti-angiogenic properties, such as sunitinib, may offer greater synergy. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that oncolytic vaccinia virus combined with sunitinib significantly enhances tumor vascular disruption and apoptosis in mouse models [73]. Mechanistically, sunitinib reduces tumor vascularity and, when administered after VV, amplifies viral-induced anti-vascular effects [73]. Additionally, oncolytic vaccinia itself has been shown to suppress VEGF levels within the TME during active infection, creating a window of opportunity for sequential anti-angiogenic therapy Sequencing strategies (VV administered first to establish intratumoral replication, followed by targeted agents that do not directly inhibit viral polymerases) represent a promising direction warranting future preclinical investigation [43].

Discussion and future directions

Liver cancer is one of the most challenging malignancies, due to late-stage diagnosis, intrinsic resistance mechanisms, and an immunosuppressive TME. Even though current treatment such as surgery, targeted agents, and immunotherapy, have been confirmed to improve patient healthy, durable responses remain limit to a small subset. In this review, OVT has been considered as a compelling therapeutic approach, by which direct tumor lysis is completed, followed with potent anti-tumor immune activation.

Genetically engineered OVs such as T-VEC, G47Δ, JX-594, and H101 have been shown significant progress with clinical or pre-clinical efficacy for various solid tumors. In liver cancer, these agents were revealed to exhibit distinct advantages, including selective tumor replication, immune microenvironment modulation, and potential synergy with existing therapies. Notably, oncolytic HSV and VV have shown to exert anti-tumor ability in preclinical HCC models, and early clinical trials results have also been shown safety and efficacy. The combination of OVs with checkpoint inhibitors, transarterial therapies, or systemic agents may further enhance therapeutic results. Nevertheless, substantial challenges remain. These critical hurdles include improving the delivery methods to overcome anatomical and immunological barriers, mitigating antiviral immunity, and ensuring tumor specificity without compromising safety.

The identification and validation of predictive biomarkers are critical for OVT in HCC. Given the interindividual variability in therapeutic response, there is necessary for biomarkers to enble rational patient stratification. Several categories of candidate biomarkers have been proposed.

First, TME characteristics represent key determinants of OVT efficacy. Immunologically “hot” TME characterized by pre-existing CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and low levels of immunosuppressive cells (such as Tregs, MDSCs) may be more responsive to OV-induced immune activation [104]. In contrast, desmoplastic or highly fibrotic tumors may constitute physical barriers that restrict viral dissemination.

Second, the expression levels of viral entry receptor on tumor cells directly governs OV entry and replication efficiency. For instance, CD46 (one receptor for measles virus) and nectin-1 (one canonical receptor for HSV) have been shown to correlate with oncolytic efficacy in preclinical HCC models.

Third, host immune parameters, including pre-existing neutralizing antibody titers against the OV backbone and baseline IFN-γ levels, may regulate both safety and response. Additionally, emerging evidence highlights novel protein biomarkers such as Zymogen granule protein 16B (ZG16B), which is upregulated across multiple malignancies including pancreatic, colorectal, gastric and oral cancers. ZG16B has been implicated in tumorigenesis and immune regulation within the TME, and its elevated expression correlates with poor clinical prognosis. Mechanistically, ZG16B appears to excert immunomodulatory effects by enhancing MDSC and M2 macrophage activity while promoting DC maturation. These properties suggest its potiteial utility as one biomarker candidate for OVT, although direct its role in HCC remains to be fully elucidated [22].

Notably, a recent phase I trial of VG161 (an engineered oHSV expressing IL-12, IL-15, and a PD-1-PD-L1 blocker) in advanced HCC developed an efficacy-prediction model based on five differentially expressed genes (SEZ6, AKR1C1, LILRA5, NCKAP5, SETD9). The gene signature effectively identified patients likely to derive clinical benefit and was associated with prolonged overall survival [89]. Furthermore, patients with prior sensitivity to checkpoint inhibitor therapy were showed to correlate with enhanced responses to VG161, suggesting that prior CPI response status may serve as a clinical predictor [89]. Collectively, these finding underscore the translational potential of biomarker-driven precision OVT.

Beyond biomarker discovery, several innovative directions are poised to advance the OVT field. Single-cell sequencing technologies, in particular have enabled high-resolution dissection of OV-TME interactions. For instancesingle-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis of HCC patients treated with the synthetic oncolytic adenovirus (SynOV) demonstrated the increased proportion of CD8⁺ T cells, enhanced CXCL9-CXCR3-mediated cell-cell communication, and normalization of Kupffer cells and macrophages within the TME [65]. Similarly, single-cell analysis of VG161-treated cancer patients demonstrated dynamic remodeling of immune cell programs, including the expansion of effector T cells and NK cells, and identified specific tumor epithelial cell subsets (such as Epi-C2) associated with treatment response [89]. Spatial transcriptomics further complements scRNA-seq by preserving tissue architecture, enabling precise mapping of OV distribution and immune cell infiltration patterns within the tumor niches. Together, these technologies provide critical mechanistic insights into both resistance and response, facilitating the rational design of combination strategies.

Another promising direction is the development of personalized OV platforms based on HCC molecular subtypes. HCC is one highly heterogeneous malignancy with distinct molecular classes (such as proliferation subclass, Wnt/CTNNB1-mutant subclass), which exhibit different immune landscapes and clinical outcomes [75]. Proliferation-class tumors, characterized by active cell cycle signaling and an immunosuppressive TME, may be more susceptible to OVs that rely on host DNA synthesis for replication (such as TK deficient vaccinia virus), as these virues preferentially replicate in rapidly dividing cells [29, 72]. Conversely, Wnt/CTNNB1-mutant HCCs, typically characterized by immune exclusion and low T-cell infiltration [54, 62], may benefit from OVs engineered to express T-cell recruiting chemokines (such as CXCL9, CXCL10) or other immunomodulatory transgenes to overcome immune exclusion [53].

The implementation of molecular subtype-guided OV selection has the potential to significantly enhance therapeutic efficacy. For instance, patients with high expression of specific viral entry receptors or those with “hot” TME signatures may be prioritized for OV monotherapy, whereas those with immunosuppressive phenotypes may require combination with ICIs or TME-normalizing agents. The Integration of multi-omics profiling, including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, into clinical trial design will be essential to advance precision OVT.

Looking forward, the development of the next generation (Third generation) OV will depende on addressing several challenges (Fig. 2). These include the establishment of cell-based/nanoparticle delivery systems to improve tumor targeting and system stability, the application of advanced genetic engineering strategies to enhance tumor selectivity and therapeutic potency, and the continued identification of predictive biomarkers to enable precise patient selection. In summary, these innovation are expected to define the future landscape of OVT.

Conclusion

In summary, OVT is a novel and adaptable immunotherapeutic approach for liver cancer. The innovation in viral vector design, combination therapy and clinical translation were continued to get the therapeutic landscape for patients with late-stage hepatic malignancies.
