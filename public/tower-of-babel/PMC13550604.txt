Reticulocalbin 3 as a novel biomarker for assessing liver disease severity and identifying decompensated status in cirrhosis
Authors: Feng-Wei Shi, Zhi-Peng Wu, Jie Sun, Fang-Ping Ding, Wen-Hui Cao, Ying-Fei Wang, Hong-Bo Shi, Ying-Min Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDecompensation in liver cirrhosis reduces survival and is driven by systemic inflammation and vascular dysfunction. Accurately diagnosing decompensated cirrhosis remains challenging due to the inherent subjectivity of traditional tools like the Child-Pugh score. In this prospectivity study conducted at a university-affiliated liver specialty hospital, we aimed to identify circulating biomarkers to refine diagnostic precision and establish an objective clinical framework. Through a rigorous selection pipeline-incorporating univariate analysis, correlation checks, and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO)-multivariate regression- Reticulocalbin 3 (RCN3), platelet-derived growth factor BB (PDGF-BB), and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) were identified as key diagnostic indicators. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) status was included as a critical covariate in all models to adjust for baseline imbalances as identified during the variable selection process. An integrated model combining these biomarkers with the Child-Pugh score achieved superior discriminative performance (AUC = 0.867), significantly outperforming the clinical baseline. Furthermore, to eliminate the inter-observer variability of clinical assessments, we developed an objective configuration by substituting subjective signs of ascites and encephalopathy with RCN3 alongside laboratory parameters (Total bilirubin, Serum Albumin, and Prothrombin Time) (AUC = 0.852). Our findings suggest that RCN3, PDGF-BB, and VCAM-1 provide critical insights into the pathophysiological state of cirrhosis. The proposed objective framework offers a standardized, reproducible alternative that optimizes cirrhosis staging while maintaining high diagnostic accuracy without clinical subjectivity.Keywords: Cirrhosis, Markers, Inflammation, FibrosisSubject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Gastroenterology, Medical research

Introduction

Cirrhosis is a chronic, progressive liver disease caused by various factors. Histologically, it is characterized by widespread hepatocyte necrosis, abnormal regeneration, angiogenesis, extensive fibrosis, and pseudolobule formation. Cirrhosis causes liver dysfunction and frequently leads to complications in advanced stages. The compensated phase is often asymptomatic for a prolonged period but may progress to decompensation. Decompensation involves rapid clinical deterioration with signs of portal hypertension and impaired synthetic function, including ascites, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, and jaundice1–3. The onset of ascites, variceal hemorrhage (VH), or hepatic encephalopathy signals the transition from compensated advanced chronic liver disease (cACLD) or compensated cirrhosis to a systemic disorder and is associated with markedly reduced survival4. Therefore, accurate identification and prompt detection of cirrhosis decompensation are essential.

The Child-Pugh classification and the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) are widely used to assess cirrhosis severity5,6. MELD is a validated prognostic tool for short-term outcomes in patients with decompensated cirrhosis7,8. The Child-Pugh score is commonly applied to evaluate hepatic dysfunction and to provide prognostic information in cirrhosis9. Compensated cirrhosis typically corresponds to Child–Pugh class A, while decompensated disease is usually class B or C. However, two Child-Pugh components—hepatic encephalopathy and ascites—are subjective, since their assessment can be influenced by clinician judgment and by treatments such as diuretics or lactulose.

Systemic inflammation is a major driver of cirrhosis progression, critically influencing the transition from compensated to decompensated stages and contributing to episodes of acute decompensation. It is a central pathological mechanism that underlies organ dysfunction and failure, with acute-on-chronic liver failure its most clinical outcome10,11. Patients with acute decompensated cirrhosis (ADC) exhibit systemic inflammatory features, including increased white blood cell and neutrophil counts and elevated inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), cytokines, and chemokines, compared with patients with compensated cirrhosis or healthy controls12. In addition, vascular impairment, angiogenesis, and the extent of hepatic fibrosis are important for assessing liver disease severity.

Current laboratory parameters for assessing cirrhosis severity predominantly reflect hepatic function, and established clinical scoring systems, including the Child-Pugh classification and the MELD score, do not account for inflammatory status or vascular dysfunction. Therefore, this study aimed to identify inflammation-and-vascular function-related factors associated with disease severity and to integrate them with the Child-Pugh score to develop a novel diagnostic model for identifying decompensated cirrhosis.

Materials and methods

Participants

This is a prospective observational, non-interventional study. A total of 465 cirrhotic patients with cirrhosis admitted to Youan Hospital, Capital Medical University, Beijing, China, from April 2023 to May 2024 were initially enrolled. Notably, as this study focused exclusively on hospitalized patients, the recruitment reflects the specific clinical indications for admission in our center. Patients with decompensated cirrhosis were typically admitted for the management of acute complications (e.g., ascites or variceal bleeding). In contrast, since asymptomatic patients with compensated cirrhosis are generally managed in outpatient settings, those admitted to the hospital while in the compensated stage were predominantly there for elective hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) surveillance or specialized interventions, such as surgical resection or localized ablation. This recruitment pattern accounts for the observed distribution of HCC prevalence within the cohort. Demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were retrieved from electronic medical records. Blood samples were prospectively collected from eligible participants for biomarker analysis; no therapeutic or diagnostic interventions beyond standard clinical care were assigned for the purpose of the study. Ethics approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Youan Hospital, Capital Medical University (No. LL-2022-141-K) and the study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05932927). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and data were anonymized. All methods were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable institutional and national guidelines and regulations. Ultimately, 145 patients were excluded, mainly because qualifying blood samples for biomarker testing were not available.

Data collection

The following data were collected for all participants: demographic characteristics including sex, age, height and weight; cirrhosis etiology classified as alcoholic, hepatitis B or hepatitis C; comorbidities including hypertension and diabetes; disease severity scores Child-Pugh class and MELD score; arterial blood gas parameters; and laboratory tests including complete blood count, liver and renal function tests, coagulation profile and infection markers.

Detection of biomarkers

10mL of whole blood sample were collected from each consented participant into tubes containing EDTA. Plasma was isolated by centrifugation at 2000 ×g for 10 min and stored at − 80 °C until batch analysis. Levels of Reticulocalbin 3(RCN3), sphingosine-1-phosphate(S1P), angiopoietin-2(Ang-2), and von Willebrand factor(vWF) were measured by commercial ELISA kits (Human Reticulocalbin 3, Biorbyt orb1213944; S1P, EKF58355; Angiopoietin-2, KHC1641; vWF, KHC1641). sE-selectin, intercellular adhesion molecule-1(ICAM-1), platelet-derived growth factor BB(PDGF-BB), tumor necrosis factor α(TNF-α), vascular cell adhesion molecule-1(VCAM-1) and vascular endothelial growth factor A(VEGF-A) were measured with a custom ProcartaPlex 6-Plex Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a Luminex 200 system. All assays were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 and R 4.5.0. Visualizations were produced in GraphPad Prism 9 and R. Continuous variables are reported as median (interquartile range) or mean (standard deviation) and categorical variables as number (percentage). Group comparisons were conducted using χ² or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical data, t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for two-group continuous data, and ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test for multi-group comparisons. Univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted using binary logistic regression; results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To control the False Discovery Rate (FDR) arising from multiple comparisons across the clinical and biochemical variables, P values from the univariates analysis were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

Subsequently, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was employed to refine variable selection and minimize overfitting. Multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). For all multivariable models (including the clinical-biomarker and scoring models), the VIF values were consistently low, ranging from 1.01 to 1.50 (all < 5), indicating that no significant multicollinearity influenced the stability of the models. Discriminative performance was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC), with Delong’s test employed to compare the AUC values between different models. TO further assess the predictive refinement and model improvement, Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) were calculated; the categorical NRI was determined using the optimal probability threshold based on the Youden index. Correlation heatmaps were used to display associations between biomarkers and Child-Pugh score and components; the absolute value of r indicates strength and the sign indicates direction. Nomograms were developed and assessed by Hosmer-Lemeshow (H-L) tests and calibration plots. Clinical utility was evaluated by decision curve analysis (DCA).

Results

Analysis of patients’ clinical characteristics

After screening, a total of 320 patients were included in the analysis, comprising 82 individuals with compensated liver cirrhosis and 238 with decompensated liver cirrhosis (Fig. 1). A statistically significant difference was observed in the demographic variable of age between patients with compensated and decompensated liver cirrhosis. In terms of etiology, significant differences were also found among the groups, including alcoholic liver disease, hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, autoimmune hepatitis, and other causes. Statistically significant differences were observed in the prevalence of complications, including HCC, esophagogastric varices, history of gastrointestinal bleeding, ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, and renal insufficiency. Statistically significant differences were also observed in the severity of cirrhosis, as assessed by Child-Pugh score, Child-Pugh classification, and MELD score, as well as in laboratory parameters including hematologic profiles, liver function tests, and coagulation profiles. Additionally, a significant difference was noted in the partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO₂) on arterial blood gas analysis (Table 1). Significant differences were also detected in the serum levels of RCN3, S1P, Ang-2, vWF, ICAM-1, PDGF-BB, VCAM-1, and VEGF-A across different stages of cirrhosis, including compensated and decompensated states, as well as among Child-Pugh classes (Fig. 2).

Identification of predictors via step-wise selection and correlation analyses

To minimize the risk of Type Ⅰ errors arising from multiple comparisons, we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction to all univariate analyses. The results demonstrated that Child-Pugh score, MELD score, HCC status, and several biomarkers (including RCN3, S1P, Ang-2, ICAM-1, PDGF-BB, and VCAM-1) were significantly associated with cirrhosis decompensation (all adjusted P < 0.05) (Table 2). The identification of HCC status as a significant factor (P < 0.001) underscored its importance as a critical clinical confounder to be addressed in subsequent modeling.

In Model 1, both Child-Pugh score and HCC status were highly significant factors (P < 0.001). In the integrated Model 2, the addition of RCN3, PDGF-BB, and VCAM-1 improved the model fit, as evidenced by a reduction in AIC from 263.5 (Model 1) to 251.78 (Model 2). Clinical implementation and validation of the integrated model (model 2)

To facilitate clinical application, a nomogram was developed based on the integrated Model 2 (Fig. 6). The model achieved a high original C-index of 0.867, demonstrating strong discriminative ability. The model’s internal stability was rigorously assessed using 10-fold cross-validation, which yielded a consistent mean AUC of 0.856(± 0.087). Furthermore, bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) was performed to evaluate the potential for optimism, resulting in a corrected mean AUC of 0.879(95% CI: 0.817–0.915). Delong’s test confirmed that the AUC of Model 2 was significantly higher than that of the baseline clinical Model 1 (P = 0.035). These validation procedures collectively indicate that the integration model is robust and possesses high discriminative reliability.

Subgroup consistency and interaction analysis

To evaluate whether the association between the investigated biomarkers and decompensation status was influenced by the presence of HCC, an interaction analysis and a subgroup evaluation were performed (Fig. 7). The forest plot demonstrated that the interaction term between RCN3 and HCC status was not statistically significant (OR = 0.78, 95%CI: 0.48–1.27, P = 0.323). This lack of interaction indicates that the discriminative utility of RCN3 for identifying cirrhosis decompensation remains consistent across both HCC and non-HCC subgroups, reinforcing its potential as a broad-spectrum biomarkers.

In the non-HCC subgroup context, RCN3 maintained a consistent association trend. The analysis showed that RCN3 remained a relevant factor related to decompensation status (OR = 1.48, 95%CI: 0.94–2.34, P = 0.088). Although the P-value exceeded the 0.05 threshold in this complex model, the direction and magnitude of the effect size remained stable, supporting RCN3 as a robust marker of decompensation presence that transcends the potential confounding effect of malignancy.

Development and evaluation of an objective integrated model (model 4)

To enhance the objectivity and minimize the influence of clinical subjectivity (such as the assessment of ascites and hepatic encephalopathy in the Child-Pugh score), we developed Mode 4 as a purely objective tool. This model incorporates RCN3, HCC status, and laboratory parameters (TBIL, ALB, and PT). Model 4 demonstrated a favorable fit with an AIC of 262.86. While RCN3 exhibited a marginal association in this objective configuration (OR = 1.12, P = 0.110), it was retained to maintain the model’s integrated discriminative (Table 3).

The discriminative performance of Model 4 was robust, achieving an original C-index of 0.852. Internal validation via 10-fold cross-validation yielded a consistent mean AUC of 0.843(± 0.078), and bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) showed an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.856(95% CI: 0.801–0.903). Notably, Delong’s test indicated that the AUC of Model 4 was comparable to that of the baseline clinical Model 1 (P = 0.735). To further evaluate the reclassification efficiency, we calculated the NRI and IDI using the optimal clinical threshold (0.704). Compared to the clinical-sign-based model, Model 4 yielded a Categorical NRI of 0.0485 (P = 0.24) and an IDI of 0.0115 (P = 0.55). While not statistically superior, these results indicate that substituting subjective signs with RCN3 and laboratory markers preserves the model’s discriminative utility without loss of accuracy.

The calibration of Model 4 was excellent, as evidenced by a H-L test result of P = 0.713, indicating no significant lack of fit. A nomogram based on Model 4 was also constructed (Fig. 8). Furthermore, DCA confirmed that Model 4 provides a substantial net clinical benefit, reinforcing its potential as an objective alternative for identifying decompensation risk in clinical practice without relying on subjective scoring.

Discussion

In this study, the association between inflammatory and vascular-function biomarkers and liver cirrhosis severity was examined. A diagnostic assessment model for decompensated cirrhosis was developed by integrating selected biomarkers with the Child-Pugh score. Multiple biomarkers were measured in the cohort. Serum levels of RCN3, Ang-2, vWF, ICAM-1, VCAM-1, and VEGF-A increased with advancing Child-Pugh class, while S1P and PDGF-BB decreased. These biomarkers differed significantly between patients with Child-Pugh class B or C and those with class A. The biomarker levels were associated with both compensated and decompensated cirrhosis. RCN3, PDGF-BB, and VCAM-1 were identified as independent risk factors for decompensated cirrhosis by multivariable logistic regression and correlation analyses.

PDGF overactivation is linked to lung and liver fibrosis. In the liver, PDGF actives hepatic stellate cells and promotes fibrosis. Inhibition of PDGF signaling suppress stellate cell proliferation and reduces fibrosis13. Diang et al. reported that serum PDGF-BB correlates with fibrosis severity in chronic hepatitis B14. Eurys et al. found elevated serum PDGF-BB in alcohol-related cirrhosis15. Zhang et al. proposed it as a diagnostic biomarker for hepatic fibrosis16. In the present study, serum PDGF-BB was significantly associated with cirrhosis severity, supporting the link between cirrhosis severity and liver fibrosis.

However, our multivariable analysis revealed that lower serum PDGF-BB levels were associated with higher risk of decompensation, suggesting a seemingly ‘protective’ association. This apparent paradox likely stems from the hematological alterations inherent in advanced cirrhosis. Platelets serve as the primary reservoir for circulating PDGF-BB. As liver disease progresses to the decompensated stage, patients frequently develop severe thrombocytopenia-a consequence of hypersplenism-included sequestration and impaired hepatic thrombopoietin (TPO) production. Therefore, the decline in systemic PDGF-BB levels in decompensated patients may be a secondary manifestation of the reduction in platelet count rather than a direct reflection of attenuated fibrogenesis. This indicates that while PDGF-BB remains a critical driver of local hepatic fibrosis, its circulating concentration in the context of advanced cirrhosis functions as a surrogate marker for the severity of portal hypertension and hematopoietic exhaustion, explaining tis inverse correlation with the risk of decompensation observed in our cohort.

VCAM-1 is an immunoglobulin-superfamily transmembrane protein that mediates leukocyte adhesion by binding integrins α4β1 and α4β7. It anchors recruited leukocytes and is implicated in many inflammatory diseases. Elevated VCAM-1 levels reflect endothelial activation or chronic inflammation rather than direct structural endothelial damage and are pro-inflammatory state17. Furuta et al. showed that, in NASH, toxic lipids induce dysregulated VCAM-1 expression on liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), promoting recruitment of pro-inflammatory monocytes and subsequent inflammation and fibrosis18. Guo et al. proposed that VCAM-1 on LSECs directly modulates fibrogenesis19. Lefere et al. found that serum VCAM-1 independently predicts significant fibrosis (≥ F2) in patients with NAFLD, suggesting its potential as a non-invasively maker of clinically relevant fibrosis20. Our results show that serum VCAM-1 is associated with cirrhosis severity, further supporting that disease severity relates to hepatic fibrosis and inflammation.

RCN3 is a CREC-family Ca²⁺-binding proteins localized to the secretory pathway. It contains six EF-hand motifs that regulate calcium homeostasis and support protein trafficking. RCN3 is encoded at chromosome 19q13 and functions in the endoplasmic reticulum to facilitate protein biosynthesis21,22. Beyond its function in the secretory pathway, RCN3 regulates multiple biological processes; previous studies have indicated that RCN3 in inflammation and fibrosis, including myocardial, ligament, and pulmonary fibrosis23,24. Research on RCN3 in liver disease remain scarce; only our team has reported an association between RCN3 and hepatopulmonary syndrome (HPS), and circulating RCN3 as a potential early biomarker for latent or early-stage HPS in cirrhosis, with improved prediction when combined with ALB25. In the present study, serum RCN3 was positively correlate with cirrhosis severity, suggesting a possible role in hepatic fibrogenesis.

Interesting, despite the established role of systemic inflammation in disease progression, classical inflammatory markers such as CRP and PCT were not significantly associated with decompensation in our cohort. This discrepancy underscores the unique biological positioning of RCN3. Unlike CRP or PCT, which are acute-phase reactants primarily driven by external cytokines or systemic infections, RCN3 likely reflects a distinct ‘ER stress-fibrosis’ axis. Mechanistically, as a regulator of the secretory pathway, the elevation of RCN3 may capture the intensity of hepatic stellate cell activation and the subsequent pro-collagen trafficking-processes that are intrinsic to the transition from compensation to decompensation. Therefore, RCN3 serves as a mechanistic biomarker of organ-specific architectural remodeling and cellular stress, rather than a general marker of systemic immune activation. This intrinsic link to the underlying pathophysiology of liver failure explains why RCN3 offers superior predictive value even when generalized inflammatory markers remain silent.

The strong correction between these factors and cirrhosis severity underscores the links among systemic inflammation, hepatic fibrosis, and disease progression. Traditional scoring systems, such as Child-Pugh and MELD scores, are widely utilized but do not directly incorporate molecular markers of inflammatory activity or the ER stress-fibrosis axis. While the Child-Pugh score remains a cornerstone for assessing severity in hospitalized patients, its reliance on the clinical assessment of ascites and hepatic encephalopathy introduces structural subjectivity and inter-observer variability, particularly when these signs are masked by therapeutic interventions.

To address this, we developed Model 4 as a purely objective diagnostic tool by substituting subjective clinical components with RCN3. Our results demonstrate that this RCN3-based configuration achieves a discriminative performance (AUC = 0.852) comparable to the original Child-Pugh score. Furthermore, the non-inferior NRI and IDI results indicate that replacing clinical signs with RCN3 does not compromise the model’s reclassification efficiency or accuracy. This suggests that RCN3 may serve as a robust biochemical surrogate, capturing the underlying physiological deterioration that traditional physical examinations aim to identify. By integrating RCN3 with objective laboratory parameters (TBIL, ALB, and PT), Model 4 offers a standardized and reproducible evaluation, providing substantial net clinical benefit as evidenced by DCA. Such an objective alternative is particularly valuable in clinical trials and resource-limited settings where a consistent and quantifiable assessment of decompensation risk is paramount.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design of this study represents a primary limitation, as it only allows for the identification of associations between biomarkers and the current clinical status of cirrhosis, the model proposed in this study should be interpreted as a diagnostic or severity assessment tool rather than a true prognostic instrument. Second, our single-center, inpatient-based recruitment introduced selection bias (notably the specific HCC distribution) and lacks external validation. To mitigate this, we employed rigorous 10-fold cross-validation and bootstrap resampling to ensure model stability, though subsequent multi-center longitudinal studies are required to confirm tis generalizability. The diagnostic model was built using multivariable logistic regression, and ROC analysis indicated that it outperformed the Child-Pugh score in identifying decompensation. Machine learning and deep learning approaches can identify key predictors, reduce manual intervention, and automatically optimize model weights26,27. In future work, machine learning and deep learning methods will be applied to high-dimensional data to develop diagnostic and prognostic models.

Conclusion

Clinical characteristics and serum biomarkers were systematically compared between patients with compensated and decompensated cirrhosis. Patients with decompensated cirrhosis had significantly higher cytokine levels that correlated with liver function parameters, suggesting a role for these cytokines in disease progression. A diagnostic model was developed and demonstrated good discriminatory performance for identifying decompensation. External validation and mechanistic studies are needed to confirm the model’s generalizability and to clarify the contribution of inflammatory factors to liver disease.
