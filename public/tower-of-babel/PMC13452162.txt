Brent A. Reynolds, pioneer of adult neural stem cell biology
Authors: Loic P Deleyrolle
Published: 2026

Brent A. Reynolds, whose work profoundly reshaped our understanding of the adult brain’s capacity for regeneration, passed away on April 11, 2026, at the age of 63. In 1992, working with Samuel Weiss, Reynolds reported the existence of neural stem cells in the adult mammalian brain, providing a compelling demonstration that the adult central nervous system retains the capacity to generate new neurons. This work challenged long-standing assumptions and opened new directions in the study of brain plasticity and repair.

At a time when neurogenesis in the adult brain was widely considered unlikely, the work by Reynolds and Weiss helped establish a new conceptual framework for studying neural development beyond early life. It also introduced experimental approaches, most notably the Neurosphere assay, that have since become foundational tools used across thousands of laboratories worldwide. These contributions not only transformed neuroscience but also extended into other fields, including cancer biology.

Reynolds’ later work helped bridge these domains through the study of cancer stem cells in brain malignancies. By applying principles derived from neural stem cell biology, his research contributed to a deeper understanding of tumor hierarchy, disease progression, and therapeutic resistance. Today, research programs investigating neural regeneration, cancer stem cell biology, and targeted therapies continue to build on concepts and platforms he helped develop.

Beyond his scientific contributions, Reynolds was an unconventional and deeply original thinker. He approached scientific problems with a perspective that often diverged from prevailing views yet consistently revealed new ways forward. His intellectual independence was matched by a calm, grounded presence that made him a uniquely influential mentor.

I had the privilege of working with Brent for two decades, beginning in 2006 when I joined his newly established laboratory at the University of Queensland after completing my doctoral training in France. At that time, he was returning to academic research following a hiatus, an unconventional path that reflected the same independence of thought he brought to science. That decision marked the beginning of a journey that would take us across continents, from Australia to the United States, and shape the course of my scientific career.

Working alongside him, we contributed to the development of experimental systems to study neural stem cells and their functional potential, work that would later extend into cancer stem cell biology. More importantly, he fundamentally shaped how I think about science. He had a rare ability to bring clarity to complex problems, often reframing them in ways that revealed entirely new directions. His influence extended far beyond experimental design; it defined an approach to inquiry grounded in curiosity, rigor, and openness to unconventional ideas.

That influence continues to guide my work today. In many ways, everything I have built, my research program, my approach to mentorship, and my scientific perspective, traces back to his guidance. It is an influence that has persisted through every stage of my career, from trainee to independent investigator, and one that I now strive to pass on to my own trainees.

Reynolds’ legacy is therefore not only scientific but generational. The trainees who worked with him have gone on to establish their own laboratories, carrying forward the intellectual framework he helped cultivate. Through them, and through the continued use of the tools and concepts he developed, his impact continues to expand.

His work demonstrated that the adult brain is far more dynamic than once believed and helped lay the foundation for fields that are still evolving today. As neuroscience and cancer biology continue to advance, they do so in part through questions he helped bring into focus and approaches he helped define. Brent Reynolds’ influence endures in the science he transformed, in the tools he helped create, and in the people he shaped.
