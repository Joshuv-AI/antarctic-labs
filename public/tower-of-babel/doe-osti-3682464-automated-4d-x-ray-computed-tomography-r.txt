 ABSTRACT

Qualification of advanced structural alloys for energy applications remains gated by long-duration
mechanical testing and by a limited ability to observe, nondestructively and in three dimensions, the
subsurface flaw populations that ultimately govern failure. For additively manufactured components, the
gap is wider still because the flaw population is itself a function of the process parameters used to build
the part. X-ray computed tomography (XCT) is the only nondestructive modality that maps subsurface
flaws volumetrically at the relevant length scales, but its scientific value in a creep campaign depends on
being able to follow the same physical flaw from one interruption to the next. That in turn requires every
scan of the test to be expressed in a single coordinate frame, which, for a creep specimen, is an unusually
difficult registration problem: the region of interest is precisely the region that is changing, and after
rupture, the specimen is no longer one object.

This report describes an automated pipeline that registers XCT data across the full creep history of a
specimen and tracks individual flaws throughout it. Creep specimens were instrumented with purpose-
designed geometric fiducials in their grip sections: a chamfered corner at one end and an added corner
piece at the other. These features are asymmetric by construction and therefore break the near-prismatic
symmetry that defeats conventional surface- and intensity-based registration. Short-scan XCT data (580
projections versus the typical 1,066–2,000 for a full scan) acquired at each interruption were
reconstructed using model-based iterative reconstruction to provide high-fidelity reference volumes,
segmented with a single deep-learning model applied uniformly to every state, and brought into the pre-
creep reference frame by an automated registration pipeline. The pipeline detects the fiducials without
operator input, solves a two-stage rigid transform combining iterative closest-point matching with a
fiducial fit, and verifies the result through a cycle-consistency check between the two independent grip
fiducials that catches wrong-orientation solutions bulk-overlap metrics accept. Post-rupture fragments are
registered back to the same pre-creep frame using the intact grip retained by each fragment, and thus the
entire test, including the fracture event, resolves in one coordinate system. Downstream, per-flaw instance
labeling and drift-corrected correspondence matching yield Lagrangian histories of flaw volume,
morphology, displacement, and strain for every tracked flaw, referenced spatially to the eventual fracture
surface.

On a 316H stainless steel specimen crept at 100 MPa and 725°C to rupture at 678 h, the pipeline tracked
151 individual flaws with consistent identity across four scans (pre-creep, two interruptions, and post-
rupture) and produced per-flaw histories of volume, morphology, and strain. The XCT data resolves a
clear tertiary-creep signature—a 3.8× acceleration in void-volume growth rate together with accelerating
per-flaw strain and falling sphericity—and localize damage to within approximately 1 mm of the eventual
fracture face, in an approximately 2 mm zone that is not where the largest flaws occur. Measured
directional strains (lateral strain exceeding axial strain) are consistent with diffusion-controlled void
growth expected for 316H at this stress and temperature.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Qualification of advanced structural alloys is one of the most significant barriers in deploying new
materials for energy applications. Current practice relies on mechanical testing campaigns that run for
years, and in the case of creep, for a decade or more, with limited ability to assess nondestructively the
subsurface flaws at the microstructural length scales that actually govern failure. The consequence is
visible across the fleet: components that have accumulated tens or hundreds of thousands of service hours
are routinely retired conservatively despite retaining substantial remaining useful life because there is no
practical way to establish what damage they carry internally. Closing that gap requires measurements that

resolve individual flaws, follow them over time rather than sampling them once, and can be produced at a
throughput compatible with a real test campaign.

Additive manufacturing compounds the problem. In a laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) build, the flaw
population is not an incidental defect state but a direct function of the process parameters. Therefore, the
link from process to structure to property to performance can only be established if the as-built flaw
population and its evolution under load are both measured on the same material. This makes the ability to
track flaw evolution through a creep test a prerequisite for data-driven qualification of additively
manufactured components rather than a refinement of it.

X-ray computed tomography (XCT) is the only nondestructive evaluation modality that provides
volumetric subsurface flaw mapping at these length scales. Its limitation is throughput: achieving micron-
scale resolution over a component-scale volume conventionally demands slow, expensive scans that are
impractical to repeat at each interruption of a long-running test. Recent AI-based reconstruction and
super-resolution (SR) methods substantially relax that constraint, recovering near-high-resolution fidelity
from fast, sparsely sampled acquisitions and making it feasible to scan a specimen repeatedly over the
course of a creep campaign without dominating the test schedule.

Acquiring the scans, however, is only half of the problem. A single tomogram is a snapshot. The
scientific content of an interrupted creep campaign lies in the comparison between states, and that
comparison is only meaningful once every scan has been placed in a common coordinate frame so that a
given flaw can be identified as the same physical feature from one state to the next.

1.1 PREVIOUS WORK AND ITS LIMITATIONS

In the preceding year, the project team developed and demonstrated a process for registering pre-creep
and post-creep XCT data of the same specimen. That work succeeded on its own terms. By placing the
before-and-after volumes in a shared frame, the process enabled individual flaws to be tracked as they
changed over the course of the test and enabled identification of the flaw, or flaw-populated region,
plausibly responsible for the rupture that terminated it. The work established that flaw-resolved
comparison across a creep test is scientifically informative and that the fracture location correlates with
identifiable preexisting damage.

The process was, however, manual and interactive. The transformation relating one scan to the next had
to be identified manually: an operator inspected the volumes, recognized corresponding features,
proposed a transformation, applied it, inspected the result, and iterated until the alignment appeared
acceptable. Only then was the registration applied and the downstream analysis performed. This approach
imposes four concrete limitations:

• It does not scale. Every additional scan multiplies operator time, which limits how many
 interruptions—and therefore how much temporal resolution of damage evolution—a test can
 practically accommodate.
• It is not reproducible. A transformation found by visual matching is not a documented, re-derivable
 quantity, and two operators may not converge on the same result.
• It has no built-in verification. A visually plausible alignment can occupy the wrong orientation basin.
 A creep gauge is nearly prismatic and nearly symmetric about its loading axis; therefore a 180° error
 can score almost as well as the correct answer according to any bulk-overlap measure.
• It restricts the study to two states. Before-and-after captures the net change but not the trajectory and
 therefore cannot distinguish a flaw that grew steadily from one that remained dormant until the
 tertiary stage. That distinction is exactly what matters for predicting remaining life.

The difficulty is structural rather than incidental. The gauge section, which carries the flaws of interest, is
deforming, growing voids, and eventually tearing apart, and thus it cannot serve as its own registration
reference: the features available for matching are the features under measurement. The geometry that does
remain stable throughout the test—the grip sections—is nearly featureless and nearly symmetric and
therefore offers surface- and intensity-based methods no information with which to resolve orientation.
After rupture, the problem becomes harder still because the specimen is now two separate fragments, each
bounded by a jagged fracture surface that did not exist in any earlier scan and that actively misleads any
algorithm attempting to match surfaces.

The approach taken here is to engineer the registration reference into the specimen itself and then to build
automated detection and, critically, automated verification around it.

1.2 OBJECTIVE AND SCOPE

The objective of this work is to refine and demonstrate an AI-enhanced XCT pipeline that uses high-
throughput imaging and automated image registration to track flaw evolution across creep stages, from
the pre-creep state through interrupted testing to the post-rupture state, in steel creep specimens.

The scope comprises the following:

• Extraction of creep test coupons from LPBF-printed blocks spanning distinct process-parameter
 conditions
• XCT scanning of the specimens at successive stages of interrupted creep testing; reconstruction using
 model-based iterative reconstruction (MBIR) to provide high-fidelity reference volumes from short-
 scan acquisitions
• AI-based segmentation applied consistently across all states
• Automated 3D registration of the resulting volumes, followed by automated flaw tracking,
 morphological characterization, and strain analysis over the creep history

The pipeline described here replaces the manual, visually driven registration of the previous effort with an
automated process based on designed geometric fiducials, such that adding a scan requires no operator
intervention and produces a documented, independently verified transformation

 2. MATERIALS AND SPECIMENS

2.1 TEST COUPON EXTRACTION FROM LPBF-PRINTED BLOCKS

Creep test coupons were extracted from blocks printed on the M2 LPBF printing system. An initial
characterization of the as-built blocks was performed to establish their porosity levels, and on the basis of
that analysis, coupons were extracted from blocks produced under three separate process-parameter
conditions. This yields a specimen set that spans a deliberate range of as-built flaw populations, allowing
flaw evolution under creep to be examined as a function of the starting defect state rather than at a single
condition. Accordingly, test coupons were extracted from the 52, 71, and 135 J/mm3 blocks.

Before coupon extraction, the blocks while still on the build plate were heat-treated at 1,100°C in an Ar
atmosphere followed by water quenching. Post-quenching creep coupons with the SS-3 geometry were
extracted from each of the three blocks using wire electrical discharge machining. All specimens were
extracted in the build direction of the respective block. The SS-3 geometry was chosen as a demonstration
case for the automated registration and flaw-tracking pipeline because this geometry allowed the entire
gauge length to be imaged in a single volume at a relatively good resolution of approximately 8 µm.

2.2 CREEP SPECIMEN DESIGN AND GEOMETRIC FIDUCIALS

The creep specimen geometry was designed with registration as an explicit requirement. The CAD model
and an example cross section are shown in Figure 1.

 Figure 1. Creep specimen (a) CAD model and (b) geometric fiducials.

 Two geometric fiducials were added in the grip sections, which remain undeformed throughout the test:

• Top grip: A slanted region created by a chamfer of approximately 0.5 mm across one corner of the
 cross section.
• Bottom grip: An additional corner piece measuring 1 mm, consisting of an additive protrusion
 attached at one corner.

Both features are deliberately asymmetric. Each occupies one identifiable corner of the part cross section
and no other, and it is this property that makes automated orientation recovery possible. A prismatic
gauge rotated 180° about its loading axis is nearly indistinguishable from itself by volume overlap; a
chamfer or a corner piece, however, moves to the opposite corner under the same rotation and therefore
resolves the ambiguity unambiguously. The two fiducials are also of different kinds, one subtractive and
one additive, which yields two mechanistically independent orientation estimates. The verification step
described in Section 4.4 exploits that independence.

Placing the fiducials in the grips rather than in the gauge is equally deliberate: the grips are the only part
of the specimen whose geometry is guaranteed not to change during the test, and in the post-rupture state,
each of the two fragments retains one complete grip and therefore one complete fiducial.

 3. XCT ACQUISITION, RECONSTRUCTION, AND SEGMENTATION

3.1 SCAN SCHEDULE

XCT scans were acquired at successive states of the creep history. Scan 1 was the pre-creep baseline at
zero accumulated creep time; Scans 2 and 3 corresponded to the first and second test interruptions; and
the final scan set was acquired after rupture, when the specimen existed as two separated fragments. Each
state was scanned in three regions—top grip, mid-gauge, and bottom grip—so that both the fiducial-
bearing grips and the flaw-bearing gauge were captured at usable resolution.

The schedule is not limited to the states listed. Because every transformation is referenced directly to the
pre-creep baseline rather than chained from one scan to the next, additional interruptions can be
incorporated into the same pipeline without modification or accumulating registration error along the
chain.

All XCT scans in this report were conducted as short scans (180° rotation + fan angle) with a voxel size
of 8.06 µm. The total number of projections was 580, with each projection having a 4 s
integration/exposure time. A source voltage of 200 kV and a source filter of 0.25 mm Cu were selected.

3.2 ADVANCED RECONSTRUCTION AND DEEP-LEARNING SEGMENTATION

MBIR [1] was used to reconstruct these data with sufficient quality to provide high-fidelity reference
scans for subsequent training of SIMURGH [2-4] models for high-throughput characterization of similar
components with reduced scan times. The current scans use 580 projections under a short-scan protocol
[5], compared with the typical 1,066–2,000 projections in a full scan, making the acquisitions faster while
still providing the high-fidelity reference data needed for model training. The longer-term objective is to
enable batch characterization and flaw-evolution tracking across more than 100 coupons. These initial
scans both provide the training reference data and support the development of proof-of-concept
algorithms.

Segmentation of material and flaws was performed using a deep-learning model applied uniformly to
every scan at every state. Consistency here is not a convenience but a requirement of the measurement.
Any state-dependent bias in the segmentation, such as that introduced by per-scan threshold tuning, would
propagate directly into apparent flaw growth and would be indistinguishable in the results from real creep
damage. Applying one model across the campaign makes the segmentation a fixed operator, allowing
differences between states to be attributed to the material rather than to the analysis.

The same deep-learning segmentation models developed during the prior-year AMMT effort [6] and built
on original work described in [7] were used. Although the model was trained primarily on data with a
resolution of approximately 17 µm, the voxel size of the current data was 8.06 µm. To mitigate this
difference, the X-CT scans were interpolated to a voxel size of 17 µm using bilinear interpolation,
segmented using the deep-learning model, and then interpolated back to the original voxel size. Previous
work has shown that this approach, when used with U-Net-based segmentation models, slightly affects
accuracy [8] while saving the time required to retrain models. Nevertheless, a future objective is to train a
new segmentation model that works routinely with multiscale data without retraining and can be
incorporated into future workflows.

which serves as the canonical coordinate system for the entire test. The overall workflow is shown in

4.1 CAD-REFERENCED ORIENTATION

Each mid-gauge volume is first oriented against the specimen CAD model by principal-axis analysis,
optionally refined by iterative closest point (ICP) matching, which places the loading axis along a
canonical array axis. This removes gross differences in how the specimen was mounted in the scanner
between states and establishes a consistent axis convention for everything that follows. The rotation

recovered for a given state is also applied to that state’s grip volumes; therefore, all three regions of a
state enter the next stage in a shared orientation.

4.2 AUTOMATED GRIP FIDUCIAL DETECTION

Fiducials are detected automatically from the segmented grip volumes, with no operator input. The
detector projects each volume onto two orthogonal longitudinal cross-sections, extracts the part boundary,
and estimates the part’s own in-plane frame from the length-weighted dominant edge orientation.
Working in that part-relative frame rather than in array coordinates makes detection independent of
residual tilt and scan resolution.

Within that frame, three complementary detectors are available:

• Slant detection identifies a straight, off-axis boundary segment cutting across a bounding-box corner
 and returns its angle, length, straightness residual, and corner identity. The corner test is scale-free
 and rejects the concave fillets of the specimen geometry, which lie mid-edge rather than at a corner.
• Cut-corner detection generalizes this to rounded as well as straight chamfers by measuring the
 background fraction within an inward window at each of the four corners and identifying the corner
 with an anomalously high void fraction.
• Corner-piece detection identifies an additive protrusion as the largest residual region remaining after
 morphological opening, again constrained to hug a bounding-box corner.

The critical output is not merely a fiducial position but the corner identity, indicating which of the four
cross-section corners the feature occupies. This discrete label is what breaks the 180° symmetry:
comparing corner identities between the reference scan and a moving scan directly determines the
required axis flips, independently of any overlap metric and therefore avoiding the degeneracy to which
overlap metrics are susceptible.

4.3 TWO-STAGE RIGID REGISTRATION

Registration of a grip against its pre-creep counterpart proceeds in the following two stages, which are
then composed into a single rigid transformation:

• Stage 1 (surface ICP): A KD-tree-based ICP is performed on subsampled surface points. Initialized at
 the identity transformation with centroid alignment and using a progressively tightening
 correspondence threshold, it accurately locks the surfaces and edges. Feature-end weighting
 emphasizes surface points near the fiducial end of the part so that the solution aligns the fiducial
 rather than leaving a lever-arm residual there. This stage is precise but blind to the symmetric
 orientation ambiguity.
• Stage 2 (fiducial fit): The fiducial detector is applied to the Stage 1 result and supplies the orientation
 flip that ICP alone cannot determine.

The two stages are composed algebraically into one transformation rather than applied sequentially. This
matters for two reasons: (1) the volume is resampled only once, which avoids the compounded
interpolation blur of a two-pass application; and (2) a single documented transformation is stored for each
scan, making the registration replayable and auditable, which is exactly what the manual process could
not provide.

For the intact interrupted states, the two grips of a given interruption are solved jointly as a shared
rotation with independent translations. The grips are rigidly attached to the same specimen and therefore
share a single rotation, but they are cropped independently from separate scans and thus require separate

translations. Each ICP iteration centers the correspondences within each region, pools them, and solves a
single Kabsch problem for the shared rotation before recovering the two translations from the region
centroids.

4.4 CYCLE-CONSISTENCY VERIFICATION

Because the top and bottom fiducials are physically attached to the same rigid part, the orientation
estimates derived from the top and the bottom must agree. The pipeline computes the two registrations
independently, using the chamfer for the top and the corner piece for the bottom, and then cross-checks
them against two criteria: the axis flips recovered from each must match, and the angular disagreement
between the two independent rotation estimates must fall within tolerance.

This check is frame-independent. It does not require the top and bottom crops to share a coordinate grid,
nor does it depend on any overlap score. It therefore catches precisely the failure mode that both visual
inspection and overlap-based validation miss: a registration that has converged into the wrong orientation
basin. When the two estimates agree, a fused rotation is formed as their geodesic mean so that both
fiducials contribute to the final answer. A further interchange test provides independent confirmation: the
rotation recovered from one region is reapplied to the other with the translation refit, and the resulting
drop in agreement must remain small.

The verified rotation is then transferred to the mid-gauge volume, which carries no fiducials of its own.
This transfer is the step that makes the featureless, deforming gauge registrable at all and is trustworthy
only because the transferred rotation has been independently verified first.

4.5 GAUGE-PLATEAU TRANSLATION FINE-TUNING

With orientation resolved, a final translation-only refinement anchors the gauge itself. The cross-sectional
area profile along the loading axis is used to detect the gauge plateau, the long, flat region of near-
constant cross-sectional area between the two grip transitions. Aligning the plateau centers axially and the
plateau cross-section centroids laterally yields a translation that is robust to arbitrary axial placement of
the specimen within the reconstruction volume and to differences in the amount of grip material captured
across states.

4.6 POST-RUPTURE FRAGMENT REGISTRATION

After rupture, the specimen exists as two fragments, each retaining one intact grip and a portion of the
gauge terminated by the fracture surface. Each fragment is registered back into the pre-creep frame using
the same fiducial logic, with three additions specific to the ruptured state:

• Rupture-tail cropping: The jagged fracture surface is excluded before both fiducial detection and
 surface matching, preventing a surface that did not exist at the baseline from corrupting either
 operation.
• Single-edge fine-tuning: A fragment has only one grip transition; its other end is the fracture face.
 Plateau alignment is therefore anchored at that single edge, with the fragment’s gauge body extending
 inward from it over its physical length. Together, the two fragments span essentially the full original
 gauge height.
• Cross-section tilt correction: A residual rotation about the loading axis is detected by comparing the
 principal angle of the fragment’s gauge cross section with that of the reference gauge and is corrected,
 with safeguards that skip the correction when the cross section is too isotropic for the angle to be well
 determined.

The two registered fragments are then fused into a single post-rupture volume in the pre-creep frame,
completing a continuous, common-frame representation of the specimen from the as-built baseline
through rupture.

 5. AUTOMATED FLAW TRACKING AND EVOLUTION ANALYSIS

Registration into a common frame makes physical flaw identity recoverable, and the downstream analysis
exploits this capability. The post-registration stages are fully automated and run in the following
dependency order:

• Instance labeling: Flaw voxels are extracted from each registered segmentation and labeled as 3D
 connected components, with each individual flaw assigned a unique integer identifier within its scan.
• Morphological characterization: Per-flaw properties are computed for every scan, including volume,
 equivalent diameter, centroid, principal axis lengths, elongation, surface area, and sphericity.
• Cross-scan correspondence: Flaws are matched between successive states using a drift-corrected
 nearest-neighbor scheme. Global drift is first estimated from mutually nearest tight-radius pairs.
 Matching then proceeds with a search radius that scales with the source flaw’s diameter, subject to a
 hard minimum, together with a volume-ratio gate that rejects implausible correspondences. Larger
 flaws are matched first, allowing them to claim their targets before smaller flaws, and targets are
 consumed to enforce one-to-one correspondence. Across the rupture event, flaws are routed to the
 appropriate fragment according to their axial positions.
• Consistent relabeling: Correspondence chains that span the full history are assigned a single
 identifier, and relabeled volumes are written in which each physical flaw carries the same integer in
 every scan. This enables direct visual and quantitative comparison of individual flaws across the full
 test.
• Per-flaw strain and displacement: For each tracked flaw, volumetric strain, directional axial and
 lateral strains, principal semi-axis strains, an effective von Mises-type strain, and centroid
 displacement are computed relative to the pre-creep baseline. These are Lagrangian quantities
 representing the history of a specific flaw rather than population statistics. This capability was not
 available in the earlier two-state manual workflow.
• Spatial mapping: Results are mapped spatially and referenced to the fracture plane so that growth and
 strain can be examined as functions of distance from the eventual rupture surface.

The pipeline produces registered volumes, a per-flaw property and strain table, a consistent label
manifest, and a set of figures and a summary report, all from a single configuration and without operator
intervention at any stage.

 6. RESULTS

6.1 TEST COUPONS AND AS-BUILT FLAW POPULATIONS

Test coupons were extracted from monolithic and hybrid blocks printed on the M2 LPBF system (Figure
3 and Figure 4). The build plate hosted blocks produced at three nominal volumetric energy densities
(VEDs)—52, 71, and 135 J/mm³—which, under nominally optimized parameters, would span a
controlled range of as-built porosities. In practice, a powder-contamination issue documented in parallel
work introduced an additional, unintended flaw population in these builds, and the as-built defect state
therefore reflects both the intended VED variation and the additional contamination.

 energy density. The table shows process parameter details.

lowest-porosity monolithic and hybrid candidates were identified. Because the hybrid blocks were most affected by
 powder contamination, the creep coupons reported here were extracted from the best monolithic block (designated

 then scanned and registered, as described in the following section.

The as-built porosity trends across the monolithic blocks were assessed qualitatively from the preliminary
reconstructions. The 135 J/mm³ condition showed the lowest apparent porosity, and the 71 J/mm³
condition showed the highest, with the 52 J/mm³ condition intermediate. Within each block, the region
nearer the neighboring printed blocks (Part B) showed higher porosity than the interior region (Part A),
most noticeably in the 71 and 52 J/mm³ cases. Defect character also differed by condition: the 71 J/mm³
material showed lack-of-fusion-type defects distributed throughout the full build height, whereas the 52
J/mm³ material showed porosity concentrated toward the top of the build. These observations are
qualitative and are based on preliminary reconstructions.

6.3 FLAW POPULATION EVOLUTION AND STRAIN ANALYSIS

The tracked dataset corresponds to a 316H specimen crept at 100 MPa and 725°C (≈ 0.55 Tm), which
failed after 678 h of cumulative creep time under load (≈ 28 days). The original gauge measured
25.39 × 1.99 × 1.93 mm (97.52 mm³). The creep timeline places the scans at 0 h (pre-creep, 0% of life),
125 h (first interruption, 18%), 361 h (second interruption, 53%), and 678 h (rupture, 100%), yielding
phase durations of 125 h in primary creep, 236 h in secondary creep, and 317 h in tertiary creep. The
clock-time arithmetic from the test record spans 791.8 h, but this includes scan-hold periods at
temperature during which the specimen was held under no load. The 678 h figure is the cumulative creep
time under the 100 MPa load and is therefore the quantity that relates to the creep mechanics.

The XCT data resolve a clear tertiary-creep signature. The void-volume growth rate accelerates by
approximately 3.8× in the tertiary phase, increasing from 32 × 10⁻⁶ mm³/h in the primary phase and
55 × 10⁻⁶ mm³/h in the secondary phase to 211 × 10⁻⁶ mm³/h in the tertiary phase. This self-accelerating
behavior—each new void concentrating stress on the remaining ligaments—is the canonical signature
creep rupture. Per-flaw tracking separates growth from nucleation: although the flaw count rises from 453
to 669 (+48%), the median per-flaw effective strain of the tracked flaws climbs +11.5% by the first
interruption, holds flat at +11.4% through the second interruption (secondary creep), and then roughly
doubles to +25.1% by rupture. Existing flaws therefore doubled their strain during the final phase while
new flaws continued to nucleate. Thus, both mechanisms accelerated together rather than one dominating.

Flaw shape degrades in step with the tertiary phase: the median sphericity of the tracked flaws falls from
0.74 to 0.65 over the test, with approximately three-quarters of that decrease occurring after the second
interruption. Voids near failure do not merely enlarge; they grow more irregular, the signature of diffusive
coalescence along grain boundaries into elongated cavities.

Damage is strongly localized (See Figure 7). For the 151 chains tracked through rupture, strain magnitude
decreases monotonically with distance from the fracture face: within 1 mm of the rupture, the median
volumetric strain magnitude is roughly 65% (median effective strain ≈ 60%), falling to approximately
25% at 1–4 mm and approximately 15% beyond 4 mm. Grip-end flaws changed little, whereas flaws in
the rupture region changed dramatically—the post-hoc fingerprint of where necking and final failure
concentrated stress. The two fragments are not equivalent: the top-gauge fragment is the higher-strain
side, with per-cluster median |ΔV/V| at the rupture edge about twice that of the bottom fragment (74%
versus 34% for the medium-flaw cluster and 56% versus 24% for the large/elongated cluster). It is also
denser per unit length—about 110 flaws/mm over its approximately 2 mm span compared with
approximately 54 flaws/mm on the bottom side—identifying this approximately 2 mm region as the
locally deformed, damage-concentrated zone that became the top fragment.

The directional-strain measurement is the most mechanistically interesting stand-alone result. Under
uniaxial tension along z-axis, one would naively expect flaws to elongate axially (εzz > εyy, εxx). Across the
151 tracked flaws, however, the measured medians are the opposite: εzz ≈ 0%, εyy ≈ +9%, εxx ≈ +7%.
Lateral strain exceeds axial strain, consistent with vacancy-diffusion-driven void growth (Hull–Rimmer,
isotropic with a slight grain-boundary bias) rather than slip-dominated growth, which would elongate

voids along the tensile axis. At 100 MPa and approximately 0.55 Tm the diffusive regime is expected for
316H. Thus, this directional result serves as both a consistency check on the framework and an
independent experimental finding.

One caveat qualifies the spatial trends. Only about 30% of the pre-creep flaws (151/453) survived through
rupture in a trackable form; many near-rupture flaws coalesced into the fracture surface and were lost.
The near-rupture flaws reported here are therefore those that survived rupture, and the highest-strain
flaws—those that joined the fracture—are absent from the dataset, biasing the surviving set toward the
more stable flaws. A complete spatial creep-rate map would require a population balance that also tracks
flaw appearances and disappearances.

The principal-axis and displacement fields (Figure 8) reinforce the same picture across the four states.
Strain is spatially concentrated and increases monotonically near the rupture face: the arrows near
z ≈ 2 mm progressively darken and lengthen across all three creep phases, whereas far-field arrows
remain short and pale. This pattern indicates that strain localization near the eventual necking location
was established early in the test rather than developing only near the end. The phase transition is also
visible directly in the growth rate. Near-rupture arrows lengthen modestly throughout the primary and
secondary phases and then increase substantially in length and intensity during the transition to tertiary
creep, providing a visual signature of tertiary acceleration in addition to the quantitative measurements. A
subset of near-rupture flaws (z ≈ 2–4 mm) also reorients its long axis during the tertiary phase rather than
merely lengthening, consistent with flaw elongation along local grain-boundary networks under
accumulated stress; far-field flaws retain their original orientations.

 7. SUMMARY AND PATH FORWARD

This work replaces the manual, two-state, operator-dependent registration used previously with an
automated, verifiable pipeline that registers XCT data across the full creep history—from pre-creep
through interruptions to post-rupture—into a single pre-creep coordinate frame. The design engineers the
registration reference into the specimen by using asymmetric grip fiducials (a chamfer and a corner piece)
whose corner identities break the 180° symmetry that defeats surface- and intensity-based alignment of a
near-prismatic creep gauge. A two-stage rigid registration composes ICP with a fiducial fit into a single,
auditable transformation, and an independent top–bottom cycle-consistency check catches wrong-
orientation solutions that bulk-overlap metrics accept. Post-rupture fragments, each retaining one intact
grip, are registered back to the same frame; thus, the entire test resolves in one coordinate system without
operator intervention or per-scan tuning.

For a 316H specimen crept at 100 MPa and 725°C to rupture at 678 h, the pipeline tracked 151 flaws with
consistent identities across all four scans and produced per-flaw Lagrangian histories of volume,
morphology, displacement, and strain. Those histories resolve a clear tertiary-creep signature from several
independent measures—a 3.8× acceleration in void-volume growth rate, accelerating per-flaw effective
strain, and a falling sphericity—all of which change slope together after the second interruption. Damage
is spatially concentrated within approximately 1 mm of the eventual fracture face and is asymmetric
between fragments, identifying an approximately 2 mm zone (the eventual top fragment) as the locally

deformed region, although the largest and densest flaws are located elsewhere in the gauge. The measured
directional strains (lateral exceeding axial) are consistent with diffusion-controlled Hull–Rimmer void
growth expected for 316H at this stress and temperature and serve as a consistency check on the
framework. The headline capability is the combination of registration accurate enough to follow
individual flaws through fracture and an analysis that converts that correspondence into flaw-resolved
creep mechanics rather than population averages.

Several directions for future work follow. A population balance that tracks flaw appearance and
disappearance—not only surviving flaws—would remove survivor-selection bias from the near-rupture
strain statistics and enable construction of a complete spatial creep-rate map. Extending the demonstration
across the three as-built process-parameter conditions would allow flaw evolution to be studied as a
function of the initial defect state, and training the SIMURGH and segmentation models toward high-
throughput, multiscale operation would move the workflow from a single-specimen proof-of-concept
toward batch characterization of multiple coupons.

Beyond these, several longer-range directions are planned. A key one is to bring SR reconstruction into
the acquisition itself. Full-size creep coupons are larger than the specimen studied here and, at matched
resolution, currently require several stitched scans per state; a learned SR model would let each state be
captured in a single fast, one-shot scan of a few seconds while recovering the same effective resolution,
enabling substantially higher-throughput characterization across many coupons.

The current creep condition of 725°C and 100 MPa favors a creep deformation mechanism in 316H in
which new voids and cracks develop along grain boundaries because of elemental segregation and
formation of brittle precipitates. A second direction is to conduct interrupted creep-and-XCT testing at a
lower temperature (600°C) so that the influence of preexisting flaws from the printing process on creep
behavior and the underlying materials mechanisms can be studied systematically. This would also allow
the nondestructive flaw histories to be connected to phenomena that can be revealed only through
destructive testing, such as the grain-boundary decohesion that is currently under investigation.

Finally, the pipeline will be generalized to accommodate an arbitrary number of interruptions. The present
demonstration uses a fixed schedule comprising pre-creep, two interruptions, and rupture; making the
number of interruptions a configurable workflow parameter would allow the temporal resolution of
damage evolution to be selected for each test rather than fixed in the code.
