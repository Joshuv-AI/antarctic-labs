Association between age and postoperative delirium after hip or knee replacement: a secondary analysis of a publicly available dataset
Authors: Shunzhong Jing, Liping Jiang, Anyuan Liu, Jiamei Liang, Xiaoqiang Sun, Yunan Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAge is a well-established risk factor for postoperative delirium (POD), but its independent association with POD in older patients undergoing joint replacement warrants further clarification. We examined the association between age and POD in a publicly available arthroplasty dataset.MethodsThis retrospective secondary analysis included 269 patients aged 65 years or older who underwent hip or knee replacement. POD was diagnosed within 5 days after surgery according to Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria and daily bedside assessment using the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM). Univariable and multivariable logistic regression models were used to evaluate the association between age and POD. Adjusted analyses included parsimonious and extended models, and Firth penalized logistic regression was performed as a robustness analysis. As the dataset contained no missing values for the outcome or retained covariates, no imputation was required. A generalized additive model (GAM) was used as an exploratory tool to visualize the shape of the association.ResultsAmong the 269 patients, 62 (23.0%) developed POD. In univariable analysis, increasing age was associated with higher odds of POD (odds ratio [OR] 1.139 per 1-year increase, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.089–1.197; P < 0.001). This association remained stable in the parsimonious adjusted model (OR 1.133, 95% CI 1.073–1.202; P < 0.001), the extended adjusted model (OR 1.134, 95% CI 1.074–1.204; P < 0.001), and Firth penalized logistic regression (OR 1.126, 95% CI 1.068–1.193; P < 0.001). Exploratory smoothing suggested that the adjusted predicted probability of POD generally increased with age across the observed range. The main logistic regression models showed acceptable calibration according to the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.ConclusionsIn older patients undergoing hip or knee replacement, increasing age was independently and robustly associated with POD across multiple analytic approaches. These findings support the use of age as an important factor in perioperative POD risk stratification, while highlighting the need for future studies with richer geriatric phenotyping to address residual confounding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03986-7.

Introduction

Postoperative delirium (POD) is a common acute neurocognitive disorder in older adults after major surgery and is characterized by disturbances in attention, awareness, and cognition. POD is associated with prolonged hospitalization, functional decline, increased healthcare utilization, and higher mortality, making it a major challenge in perioperative care for older patients [1–3]. In orthopedic surgery, hip and knee replacement are widely performed procedures for degenerative joint disease in the aging population. Because these procedures are commonly undertaken in older adults and involve substantial surgical stress, POD remains an important postoperative complication in this setting [2, 3].

The reported incidence of POD after joint replacement varies across studies, and a recent systematic review estimated a pooled incidence of 13.6% in orthopedic patients [2, 3]. Among the many reported predictors, advanced age is one of the most consistently identified risk factors for POD. Previous studies have shown that increasing age is associated with a higher likelihood of POD, including in arthroplasty populations [4, 5]. In addition to age, other perioperative characteristics such as ASA classification, pain, blood loss, and laboratory markers have also been associated with POD risk [5–10]. However, although the overall direction of the age effect is well recognized, the extent to which age remains independently associated with POD within relatively homogeneous elderly arthroplasty cohorts deserves further clarification.

Recently, Yu et al. reported that preoperative indirect bilirubin was independently associated with POD in elderly patients undergoing joint replacement surgery in a single-center cohort [11]. That study provided useful biomarker-oriented evidence, but it did not specifically focus on age as the primary exposure. Given that age is a clinically central but non-modifiable risk factor, re-examining its association with POD within the same dataset may help refine perioperative risk assessment and provide context for biomarker-based findings rather than compete with them [11].

Therefore, the present study performed a retrospective secondary analysis of this publicly available dataset to re-examine age as the primary exposure. The primary objective was to assess whether age remained independently associated with POD after adjustment for relevant perioperative covariates, including ASA classification, bilirubin-related indicators, surgical factors, body weight, and postoperative pain. We also explored the shape of the age-POD association in a descriptive manner, while recognizing the limited ability of this dataset to support reliable threshold inference near the lower age boundary.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was a retrospective secondary analysis of a publicly available de-identified dataset. The original data were obtained from the published study by Yu R, Yang M, Chen J, et al. (2025) [11]. We directly used the complete dataset shared through the Harvard Dataverse platform (10.7910/DVN/AS2K8H). The original study had been approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Third Hospital of Central South University (approval number: K24662), was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and was granted a waiver of informed consent because of its retrospective design [11]. Because the present analysis used only a fully de-identified public dataset and involved no new patient contact or identifiable information, no additional ethical approval was required.

The eligibility criteria, screening process, and source population were identical to those of the original study [11]. In brief, elderly patients aged 65 years or older who underwent hip or knee replacement surgery at Xiangya Third Hospital of Central South University between April 2022 and January 2024 were included. All patients were classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status II-III and received epidural anesthesia. Exclusion criteria included a history of mental or neurological disorders, baseline infection or sepsis, hemolytic anemia, chronic liver disease, markedly elevated liver enzymes (ALT/AST > 2 times the upper limit of normal), receipt of general anesthesia, and incomplete surgical information in the source study [11]. A total of 269 patients were included in the present analysis.

Variable definitions

Exposure

The exposure of interest was age, analyzed as a continuous variable in years.

Outcome

The outcome was postoperative delirium (POD), defined as delirium occurring within 5 days after surgery. According to the original study [11], the diagnosis was confirmed by at least one trained anesthesiologist or geriatrician using the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5), together with daily bedside assessment using the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM). POD was treated as a binary variable (no/yes).

Covariates

Based on clinical knowledge and previous literature [5–10], the following perioperative variables were considered as potential confounders: ASA classification, preoperative liver-related biomarkers measured within 1 week before surgery (ALT, AST, total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, and indirect bilirubin), sex, weight (kg), surgery type (hip or knee replacement), intraoperative bleeding volume (mL), and postoperative Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) pain score. The postoperative NRS score was defined as the worst pain score recorded within 24 h after surgery; in the source dataset, the observed NRS values ranged from 0 to 3. Height was additionally summarized in the baseline and univariable analyses but was not included in the adjusted models because it was not prespecified as a main confounder.

Statistical analysis

As the dataset contained no missing values for the outcome or retained covariates, no imputation was required.

For descriptive analyses, continuous variables were summarized as median (interquartile range) or mean ± standard deviation, as appropriate according to distribution, and categorical variables were summarized as number (percentage). Baseline characteristics were compared between patients with and without POD. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the independent-samples t test, Mann–Whitney U test, or chi-square test, as appropriate.

We first fitted univariable logistic regression models to evaluate the association between each candidate variable and POD. We then examined the independent association between age and POD using a series of multivariable logistic regression models.

A crude model included age only.

A parsimonious adjusted model included age, ASA classification, sex, weight, surgery type, intraoperative bleeding volume, and indirect bilirubin.

An extended adjusted model additionally included ALT, AST, and postoperative NRS score.

Indirect bilirubin was selected as the primary bilirubin indicator in the main adjusted models for two reasons. First, total, direct, and indirect bilirubin are mathematically related and should not be entered simultaneously in the same model because of collinearity. Second, indirect bilirubin was the principal biomarker examined in the source study and therefore provided the most direct basis for consistency with the original dataset context [11]. To improve transparency, full coefficients of the parsimonious and extended models were reported in the Supplementary Material.

Because the number of POD events was relatively limited, we additionally performed Firth penalized logistic regression using the same covariates as in the parsimonious adjusted model as a robustness analysis. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model calibration for the main logistic regression models was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

To explore the functional form of the association between age and POD, a generalized additive model (GAM) was fitted with age as a smooth term. This analysis was considered exploratory and descriptive. Because the cohort was restricted to patients aged 65 years or older and observations were relatively sparse near the lower boundary of the age distribution, threshold-based piecewise modeling was not considered suitable for primary inference.

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the association between age and POD. These included: (1) Firth penalized logistic regression; (2) winsorization of skewed biochemical variables; (3) substitution of indirect bilirubin in the parsimonious model with total bilirubin or direct bilirubin; and (4) modeling age per 5-year increase instead of per 1-year increase. Winsorization was not used in the primary analysis. Instead, 1%−99% winsorization was applied only in sensitivity analyses to ALT, AST, and indirect bilirubin because these biochemical variables showed greater skewness and were more susceptible to the influence of extreme values.

Exploratory subgroup analyses were also conducted according to sex, ASA classification, surgery type, and postoperative NRS group, and formal interaction tests were performed by including multiplicative interaction terms. These subgroup analyses were considered exploratory rather than confirmatory.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2), primarily with the mgcv package for GAM analyses and standard logistic regression procedures for regression modeling.

Sensitivity analyses and exploratory interaction analyses

Sensitivity analyses showed that the association between age and POD was robust across multiple modeling strategies (Supplementary Table S3). The age effect remained statistically significant in the parsimonious model, extended model, Firth logistic model, analyses using winsorized biochemical variables, and models in which indirect bilirubin was replaced by total or direct bilirubin. When age was modeled per 5-year increase, the association remained significant (OR 1.866, 95% CI 1.422–2.511; P < 0.001).

Exploratory subgroup analyses were performed for sex, ASA classification, surgery type, and postoperative NRS group (Supplementary Table S4). Although the positive association between age and POD was generally consistent across subgroup strata, no statistically significant interactions were detected for sex (P for interaction = 0.206), ASA classification (P for interaction = 0.549), surgery type (P for interaction = 0.959), or postoperative NRS group (P for interaction = 0.209).

Discussion

In this secondary analysis of a publicly available single-center dataset, increasing age was consistently associated with higher odds of postoperative delirium in older patients undergoing hip or knee replacement. The association remained materially unchanged across crude, multivariable-adjusted, and Firth penalized models, supporting the robustness of the main finding despite the limited number of POD events. Although exploratory smoothing suggested that the association may not be perfectly linear across the observed age range, the present data do not support reliable inference regarding a specific age threshold.

These findings are broadly consistent with previous studies showing that age is an important predictor of POD in orthopedic and non-cardiac surgical populations. Zhang et al. and Tomite et al. reported that advanced age was independently associated with POD, and the latter observed a markedly increased risk among older arthroplasty patients [4, 5]. Studies by Chung et al. and Wang et al. in total knee arthroplasty populations similarly identified age as a significant predictor of POD [12, 13]. Our results add to this literature by showing that the age-POD association remains robust after adjustment for several perioperative covariates within an elderly joint replacement cohort.

From a clinical perspective, the present findings support the use of age as an important and readily available component of perioperative POD risk stratification. However, age should not be interpreted as a stand-alone determinant of delirium risk. Rather, it is best understood as one marker of vulnerability that should be considered together with comorbidity burden, perioperative factors, and geriatric domains such as frailty and baseline cognitive function. In practice, these findings reinforce the rationale for heightened vigilance and multimodal delirium prevention strategies in older arthroplasty patients, including medication review, optimization of analgesia and anesthesia, early mobilization, orientation support, and broader non-pharmacological preventive pathways [1, 14–21]. Our findings may therefore help inform future age-informed prevention studies, including research on emerging interventions such as intranasal insulin, transcranial direct current stimulation, or auricular vagus nerve stimulation in high-risk older adults [22–24].

An additional noteworthy finding is that the association between age and POD remained highly stable even after adjustment for bilirubin and other perioperative covariates [11]. In the multivariable models, higher indirect bilirubin levels were associated with lower odds of POD, whereas age remained positively associated with POD across all modeling strategies. Taken together, these findings suggest that age and bilirubin may reflect different, potentially complementary aspects of perioperative vulnerability rather than mutually exclusive explanations [11]. A cautious interpretation is that age may capture a broad accumulation of biological susceptibility, whereas bilirubin may be related to antioxidant or anti-inflammatory pathways [11, 16]. Accordingly, the bilirubin-related findings should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive.

This study has several strengths. It focused on a relatively homogeneous population of older patients undergoing hip or knee replacement, thereby reducing some clinical heterogeneity. The analysis also used multiple complementary modeling strategies, including conventional multivariable logistic regression, Firth penalized logistic regression, and several sensitivity analyses. The consistency of the age effect across these approaches increases confidence that the main finding is robust and not driven by a single modeling choice.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, this was a secondary analysis of a single-center dataset, and residual confounding cannot be excluded despite multivariable adjustment. In particular, frailty, baseline cognitive status, preoperative functional dependence, nutritional vulnerability, sleep disturbance, sensory impairment, alcohol use, and detailed perioperative medication exposure were not fully captured. These factors are clinically important determinants of delirium risk and may partly mediate or confound the observed association between age and POD. In addition, patients aged 65–70 years who undergo joint replacement may represent a selected subgroup with a different comorbidity profile than older surgical candidates, which could distort the apparent shape of the age-POD association near the lower boundary of the cohort. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted as evidence of a robust positive association between age and POD, rather than as proof of a specific age threshold. Second, all included patients were ASA physical status II-III and received epidural anesthesia, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other institutions, broader surgical populations, or different anesthetic techniques. Third, although POD assessment in the source study was based on prospective clinical screening and DSM-5 diagnostic standards, some under-recognition or misclassification may still have occurred in routine practice. Nevertheless, the observed incidence of POD (23.0%) falls within the range reported in previous orthopedic studies [2, 3]. Fourth, although the sample size was adequate for the primary analysis, it limited statistical power for subgroup and interaction analyses; accordingly, the absence of statistically significant interaction by sex, ASA classification, surgery type, or postoperative pain group should be interpreted cautiously rather than as definitive evidence of no heterogeneity. Finally, because the cohort included only patients aged 65 years or older, the data were not well suited to support reliable threshold inference near the lower age boundary. Larger multicenter prospective studies with richer geriatric phenotyping, repeated biomarker assessment, and standardized POD ascertainment are needed to further refine the functional form of the age-POD association and reduce residual confounding [10, 25].

Conclusion

In conclusion, among older patients undergoing hip or knee replacement, increasing age was independently associated with postoperative delirium across multiple analytic approaches. These findings support age as an important factor in perioperative POD risk stratification. Future multicenter studies with detailed measures of frailty, cognitive status, and other geriatric vulnerabilities are needed to refine the functional form of this association and reduce residual confounding.
