 ABSTRACT
Accurate population forecasts are important in planning critical infrastructure and services, from housing
and education to healthcare and transport. However, traditional population prediction studies have
only employed traditional machine learning models, which are of limited in capturing complex spatial
interdependencies and patterns. Recently, a computer vision–based framework was introduced that has
promising accuracy, but it has critical limitations for real-world planning applications—it functions only at
fixed spatial resolutions, restricting its use with diverse boundaries such as census tracts, neighborhoods, or
administrative zones. Therefore, in this study we suggest a graph neural network (GNN)–based population
prediction framework called PopGNN. This model performs remarkably compared with state-of-the-art
models and traditional baseline models in the grid and administrative boundaries. Furthermore, our
framework achieves predictive accuracy comparable to that of a computer vision–based model in case
studies in South Korea and Tennessee. Thus, the proposed framework is valuable in that, as a single
model, it can provide accurate population forecasts that address diverse planning demands, ranging from
granular grid-level estimates for precise service allocation and facility location planning to aggregate
administrative-level forecasts for macroscale regional policy decisions and resource distribution.

 1. INTRODUCTION
Governments and businesses rely on accurate population forecasts to plan critical infrastructure and
services, from housing and education to healthcare and transport. Thus, demographic prediction is a
cornerstone of effective policy and investment decisions (Grossman et al. 2022; Grossman, Wilson, and
Temple 2023; Jeong and Lee 2025; Lee et al. 2022; Lee and Jeong 2026; Lutz and Kc 2010; Wilson
et al. 2022). However, current demographic shifts pose unprecedented challenges. Although many
countries, particularly those in the Global North, face overall population decline driven by declining birth
rates and aging populations, populations are concentrating in specific urban centers, creating polarization
in demographic distribution (Jeong and Lee 2025; Lee et al. 2022; Lee and Jeong 2026). These contrasting
trends mean that planners need to address service consolidation and infrastructure downsizing because
of depopulation at regional and municipal levels while managing rapid growth in metropolitan areas that
demands neighborhood-scale infrastructure expansion and suburban growth that demands tract-level
resource allocation.
To address these challenges, recent studies have employed diverse frameworks combining traditional
machine learning models—for example, random forest and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)—with
spatial variables, including socioeconomic indicators, land use patterns, and accessibility measures to
forecast population change across administrative boundaries in countries such as Australia, New Zealand,
and China (Grossman et al. 2022; Grossman, Wilson, and Temple 2023; Wang et al. 2025). Although
these studies have demonstrated reasonable predictive accuracy, traditional machine learning approaches
have inherent limitations in capturing complex spatial patterns and relationships. These models treat each
administrative unit independently, failing to account for the spatial dependencies and interactions between
neighboring regions that fundamentally shape population dynamics. For instance, administrative areas
adjacent to urban centers typically experience higher population growth because of spillover effects, the
accessibility of employment, and the availability of amenities; traditional models struggle to effectively
incorporate such spatial contexts.
More recently, Jeong and Lee (2025) suggested a computer vision–based framework called Popnet,
which combines UNet, convolutional long short-term memory (ConvLSTM), a spatial autocorrelation
module, and deep ensemble methods to forecast future 1 km-gridded populations with promising accuracy.

 1

However, computer vision–based models have critical limitations for real-world planning applications:
they function only at fixed spatial resolutions, a limitation that restricts their use in diverse contexts that
require predictions at scale-specific boundaries such as census tracts, neighborhoods, or administrative
zones.
In this study, we aim to overcome these limitations by developing a graph neural network (GNN)-based
population prediction model called PopGNN that is inherently scale-free and adaptable to any spatial
unit. Unlike traditional regression or pixel-based models, a GNN framework identifies non-Euclidean
spatial relationships and transfers learned patterns across varying geographic contexts. By constructing a
graph structure in which spatial units are connected through k-nearest neighbor relationships, the model
captures flexible spatial dependencies that adapt to diverse administrative boundaries and geographic
scales, enabling analysis at both localized and regional levels.
We validate our approach using the US as the primary study area, employing two distinct spatial boundaries:
(1) regular 30 arc second grids (resolution of about 1 km) that are comparable to existing computer
vision frameworks, and (2) irregular census block group boundaries that reflect actual administrative
units used in policy and planning. This dual validation demonstrates the fundamental advantages of this
model—the ability to handle both regular and irregular spatial data. Additionally, to benchmark our
model’s performance against state-of-the-art computer vision approaches, we compare the benchmark
directly with Popnet (Jeong and Lee 2025) based on the South Korean case. Consequently, these experiments
demonstrate the flexibility of the proposed approach in handling diverse spatial units and the competitive
performance of the approach compared with specialized grid-based models.

 2. DATA AND METHODS
2.1 STUDY AREA AND DATA
The proposed model was tested using the US as the study area; the US not only has diverse urban and rural
landscapes but also provides abundant publicly available datasets suitable for population forecasting. To
demonstrate the flexibility and generalizability of the suggested graph-based framework, we constructed
two distinct datasets representing different spatial resolutions: (1) regular gridded data at a resolution of 30
arc seconds (approximately 1 km), and (2) irregular administrative boundaries at the census block group
level. This dual approach allows us to validate the proposed model’s capability to handle regular grids and
irregular administrative units (which computer vision–based models cannot process).
Following the feature engineering results for population prediction performed by Jeong and Lee (2025),
we constructed input features representing demographic, environmental, and accessibility characteristics.
The input data cover five temporal snapshots—2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2020. Specifically, the
datasets from 2000 to 2015 were used as input features to predict 2020 population; this approach enabled
the model to learn spatiotemporal dynamics over a 20 year period.
For administrative boundaries, we addressed the issue of different census block geometries by matching all
historical block group data (2000–2015) to the 2020 census block group to ensure temporal consistency.
This process was performed by linking historical block group id onto the 2020 block group id based on
IPUMS National Historical Geographic Information System (NHGIS) geographic crosswalk tables.
Table 1 summarizes the variables, data sources, processing methods, and temporal coverage used for both
spatial boundary types (grids and block groups)
For demographic variables, population counts were obtained directly from LandScan Global (Lebakula
et al. 2025; Rose and Bright 2014) for all 5 years (2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, 2020) at the grid level. For the
block groups, we obtained the decennial census data (2000, 2010 and 2020) and American Community
Survey (ACS) 5-year estimates (2005 and 2015) from IPUMS NHGIS (Schroeder et al. 2025). Historic
crude birth rates (CDC WONDER) and crude mortality rates (CDC WONDER) were obtained from
state-level estimates from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Wide-Ranging Online
Data for Epidemiologic Research (WONDER) database. Because crude mortality rates were provided per
100,000 population, we converted them to rates per 1,000 population to maintain consistency with crude
birth rates. Thereafter, we assigned the rates uniformly to all spatial units within each respective state.
Elevation and slope were derived from Shuttle Radar Topographic Mission Void Filled (USGS 2018) and
Global Terrain Slope and Aspect Data (IIASA/FAO 2010), respectively. Because topological variables
rarely change, we employed these data for all years. Then, we computed the average of all raster pixels
falling within the unit’s boundary, ensuring that environmental features are consistently represented
regardless of the boundary’s shape or size.
We constructed accessibility variables using the ohsome API (GIScience Research Group and HeiGIT
2024), which enabled us to collect historical OpenStreetMap data for points of interest (state capitols,
county seats, city halls) and major road networks. Because the locations of administrative centers rarely
change (Jeong and Lee 2025), we used current (2024) point of interest locations for all years. For highway
networks, we obtained data from 2010 and 2015 to capture infrastructure development. However, because
of limited historical OpenStreetMap coverage before 2010, we used the 2010 network as a proxy for 2000
and 2005. For each spatial unit, we estimated the centroid coordinates, calculated Euclidean distances
from the centroid to each point of interest and the nearest major highway, and applied an inverse distance
transformation to represent accessibility (higher values indicate greater accessibility).

 3

Whereas grids inherently possess uniform areas, administrative boundaries such as block groups show
significant variations in size. This heterogeneity in spatial extent is likely to introduce bias when aggregate
metrics are compared across different units. Therefore, when we trained the model on administrative
boundary data, we added the area of each administrative unit (measured in km2 ) estimated based on 2020
boundary data as an input feature to mitigate this issue. This approach allows the model to effectively learn
density-related patterns.
Last, we standardized the input features using the RobustScaler technique (Pedregosa et al. 2011), which
employs the median and interquartile range (Q3 − Q1) to handle outliers effectively.

2.2 METHOD
Figure 1 presents the suggested PopGNN framework for predicting future population regardless of
spatial units across diverse spatial boundaries. Based on recent advances in GNN-based UNet structures
(Deshpande, Bordas, and Lengiewicz 2024; Juarez et al. 2019), our model introduces key innovations
specifically designed for population forecasting: (1) the ability to handle arbitrary spatial boundary types
beyond fixed grids, and (2) a UNet-inspired architecture that enables the consideration of multiscale
spatiotemporal patterns and interdependencies.

2.2.1 Graph Construction
PopGNN begins by constructing a graph representation G = (V, E), where nodes (V) represent spatial
units (either grids or administrative boundaries), and edges (E) encode spatial relationships. For each
spatial unit i, we compute its centroid coordinates and identify its k-nearest neighbors based on Euclidean
distances between centroids. An edge ei j is created from node i to node j if j is among the k-nearest
neighbors of i. This procedure is applied to all nodes; as a result, each node is connected to its spatial
k-nearest neighbors. This construction enables the model to capture spatial proximity relationships
regardless of whether the spatial units are regular grids or irregular administrative boundaries, which is a
critical advantage over convolutional neural network–based approaches that require fixed grid structures.

2.2.2 Graph Attention Networks V2
Graph convolutional networks (GCNs), first suggested by Kipf and Welling (2016), extend the concept of
convolution to graph-structured data through a two-step process at each layer. First, each node aggregates
information from its connected neighboring nodes and itself based on the given graph structure. This
self-loop ensures that a node’s own features are preserved during aggregation. Second, the aggregated
information undergoes a linear transformation and then passes through a nonlinear activation function to
update the node’s feature representation.
However, traditional GCNs aggregate neighboring nodes with equal weights, failing to differentiate the
importance of individual neighbors. To address this limitation, graph attention networks (GATs) were
proposed (Veličković et al. 2017), introducing attention mechanisms to measure the significance of each
neighbor node. GATs also employ multihead attention to capture diverse importance patterns across
different representation subspaces.
Despite these improvements, Brody, Alon, and Yahav (2021) revealed that a GAT’s attention mechanism
is static, which means that the ranking of a neighbor’s importance remains identical regardless of the query
node’s characteristics. This limitation causes GATs to struggle even with simple graph learning tasks.
GATv2, developed by Brody, Alon, and Yahav (2021), addresses the problem by simply reordering the
linear transformation and nonlinearity operations within the attention function. This modification enables
GATv2 to achieve dynamic attention, in which neighbor importance varies according to the query node’s
features, substantially improving the model’s representational capacity.

2.2.3 GATv2-UNet Architecture
Building on the GATv2 layers described in the previous section, our PopGNN model employs a UNet-inspired
encoder–decoder architecture for graph-based spatiotemporal forecasting. A critical difference between
our approach and conventional graph UNet architectures (Deshpande, Bordas, and Lengiewicz 2024;
Juarez et al. 2019) is that our model avoids graph pooling operations that aggregate neighboring nodes
into super-nodes by clustering or merging nodes to obtain high-dimensional representations. Instead, our
architecture keeps all original nodes and their spatial topology throughout all network layers, maintaining
both the nodes and the exact interregional adjacency relationships encoded in the k-nearest neighbor graph.
Furthermore, by employing multiple stacks of GATv2 layers, our model expands the spatial range that
each node effectively considers, enabling the model to obtain broad spatial context from local one-hop
neighborhoods to increasingly larger multi-hop regions. This pooling-free design allows the model to
capture regional interaction patterns without sacrificing node-level granularity. Consequently, each node
integrates information from neighborhoods of diverse scales, capturing population dynamics based on
patterns in surrounding and even distant regions.
However, PopGNN retains the key semantic advantage of UNet–style encoders. The feature channels
are progressively expanded across encoder levels (e.g., from 64 to 128, 256, and 512 channels), allowing
the network to learn increasingly rich and abstract representations of multiscale spatial patterns. In
combination, this dual expansion—from spatial breadth via deeper multi-hop GATv2 stacking to semantic
depth via channel growth—enables each node to encode both fine-grained local attributes and high-level
regional context without any loss of spatial topology.
The spatially enriched node representations from all time steps are then fed into a GATv2–long short-term
memory (LSTM) module between the encoder and decoder, allowing our framework to consider the
spatiotemporal context. Based on the graph convolutional recurrent network framework (Seo et al. 2018),
our GAT-LSTM integrates graph convolutions into all LSTM gates (input, forget, cell, output).
The GATv2-LSTM output is passed to a decoder that progressively refines predictions. Skip connections
from corresponding encoder levels enable semantic upsampling: low-level, fine-grained local attributes
(one-hop locality, 64 channels) from shallow encoder layers are concatenated with high-level, broad
regional context (four-hop receptive fields, 512 channels) from the encoder, enabling the model to consider
multiscale spatiotemporal dynamics for accurate future population prediction at the node level.
Finally, predictions pass through a ReLU activation function to enforce nonnegativity, because population
values cannot be negative.

2.2.4 Training model
We also employed a deep ensemble to improve the prediction accuracy of PopGNN. Five PopGNN models
were trained using the same training dataset.
To optimize model performance while preventing overfitting, we implemented a combination of Bayesian
optimization and fivefold cross-validation during the hyperparameter tuning phase. Bayesian optimization
employs probabilistic modeling to intelligently navigate the search space (Shahriari et al. 2016; Frazier
2018), enabling efficient identification of optimal configurations with fewer iterations. The k-fold
cross-validation strategy further enhanced model robustness by systematically partitioning the training
data, ensuring that each subset served as both training and validation data across different folds. This
approach was particularly suitable given the computational constraints and the need to train multiple
models within our ensemble framework. We explored the hyperparameter space, covering learning rate
values between 0.00001 and 0.001 and the number of epochs from 5 to 100. Training was conducted
using the Adam optimization algorithm, with mean squared error (MSE) serving as the loss function.

 7

Overall processes were performed using an NVIDIA A100 GPU. Identifying the optimal hyperparameter
combination and completing the final training required approximately 16 h per model. Because this
study employed a deep ensemble approach with five individual models, the total computational time was
approximately 80 hours.

2.2.5 Prediction
For ensemble aggregation, we adopted a weighted averaging approach instead of computing simple
averages. Each model’s contribution to the final output was determined by its validation accuracy, which
was assessed by comparing the 2020 predicted population against 2020 actual population data. We used
MSE as the metric to evaluate the validation accuracy of individual PopGNN models. An NVIDIA A100
GPU was also employed for the prediction process.

 3. RESULTS
3.1 OVERALL PERFORMANCE COMPARISON
This study evaluated the performance of the PopGNN framework using standard metrics: coefficient of
determination (R2 ), root mean square error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE). The evaluations
were performed at two spatial resolutions—30 arc second grid and block group data for Tennessee.
For each spatial unit, we selected valid boundaries within which the 2020 actual population (ground
truth) exceeded 0, and then compared the 2020 population predicted using 2000, 2005, 2010, and 2015
data against the actual 2020 population at the spatial unit level. The framework’s performance was
benchmarked against traditional machine learning models—regression tree, random forest, support
vector machine (SVM), and XGBoost—as well as state-of-the-art deep learning architectures, including
PopGNN without residual connections, a single-model PopGNN, and a PopGNN ensemble. Because
traditional machine learning models cannot directly process graph structures and temporal information,
we transformed the data by concatenating features of each node and its neighbors in temporal order into a
flattened one-dimensional feature vector.
Table 2 shows the performance benchmarks for the Tennessee 30 arc second grid boundary. The PopGNN
ensemble outperformed the other approaches, obtaining an R2 of 0.96, RMSE of 54.71, and MAE of 15.68.
Compared to the traditional machine learning models, the PopGNN ensemble had an RMSE 1.5–2.1 times
lower than the tree-based methods (random forest: 116.54, decision tree: 112.69, XGBoost: 84.46) and
about 4.4 times lower than SVM (241.64). Similarly, the proposed model had an MAE 1.5–1.9 times lower
than the tree-based models and approximately 3.2 times lower than SVM. The residual connections proved
to be beneficial, resulting in an RMSE about 7.5% lower and an MAE about 33.0% lower in the PopGNN
ensemble than in the PopGNN ensemble with no residuals (RMSE: 59.14 and MAE: 23.41). The ensemble
architecture also showed advantages over the single-model architecture, reducing RMSE by approximately
3.4% and MAE by 12.4% compared to the single-model PopGNN (RMSE: 56.61 and MAE: 17.90). Based
on the RMSE and MAE values, the PopGNN ensemble framework demonstrated an outstanding capability
to capture spatial population dynamics at the grid level.

To evaluate our model against a specialized state-of-the-art benchmark, we compared the PopGNN
ensemble with the PopNet ensemble (Jeong and Lee 2025), a computer vision–based model for grid data.
For this comparison, we employed the same Tennessee 30 arc second grid dataset as well as the 1 km grid
dataset for South Korea used in our previous PopNet study (Jeong and Lee 2025). Although both models
used the same source data (2000, 2005, 2010, 2015 population and environment datasets), preprocessing
differed according to architectural requirements. PopNet (Jeong and Lee 2025) processed the data as
image vectors for its computer vision backbone, whereas the PopGNN ensemble constructed graph
structures, with nodes representing spatial units and edges capturing geographic relationships. Despite
these preprocessing differences, both approaches predicted the 2020 gridded population in Tennessee

To evaluate the spatial reliability of the model, we analyzed the residuals between the predicted and actual
population for 2020. At the grid level (Figure 2A), the model demonstrates high stability in rural and
nonmetropolitan areas, with residuals mostly confined within a narrow range of ±25 persons. In contrast,
in major metropolitan areas the model shows a mixed pattern of over- and underestimation. At the block
group level (Figure 2B), the distinct urban-rural dichotomy is hard to discern, but a broad regional trend is
evident: the western region of Tennessee tends to be slightly underestimated, whereas the eastern region
tends to be overestimated.

3.3 TENNESSEE POPULATION IN 2040 AT GRID AND BLOCK GROUP LEVELS
Using the trained PopGNN ensemble, we predicted the 2040 Tennessee population distribution at the
grid and block group levels. Because of a lack of future crude birth and death rates at the state level, US
national rates were employed. Both rates were calculated using the US Census Bureau’s 2023 National
Population Projections (US Census Bureau 2023). Specifically, from projected total population and births
and deaths in 2025, 2030 and 2035, crude birth and death rates were estimated for the corresponding years.
The input dataset for future-year predictions used environmental and accessibility variables at their 2020
values, assuming no substantial changes over the projection periods; only demographic variables were
updated with projected births, deaths, and population. For the 2025 scenario, the model used demographic
data from 2005 to 2020. For years after 2025, we used projected population and crude birth and death rates
and kept nondemographic variables unchanged.
Last, to obtain model prediction results consistent with the state-level population projections, we
normalized the predicted results using the state-level population projection results from the University
of Virginia Weldon Cooper Center for Public Service (University of Virginia Weldon Cooper Center for
Public Service 2024). Because these data include projected population only for 2030, 2040, and 2050, the
2025 and 2035 projected populations were calculated using a linear interpolation approach. Under these
principles, we predicted 2025, 2030, 2035, and 2040 populations at the grid and block group levels.
Figures 3A and C show the predicted population for 2040 at the grid and block group levels, respectively,
whereas B and D show changes from 2020 to 2040. Overall, a trend of exurbanization is evident at both
the grid and block group levels: urban-area populations in Memphis and Knoxville are expected to decline
significantly, whereas populations in regional centers or micropolitan areas are expected to increase.
Population increases are also evident across rural areas in western Tennessee. These trends indicate that
populations are shifting toward less dense and more affordable peri-urban or rural environments.

 4. DISCUSSION
The most innovative contribution of this study is the development of a graph-based deep learning
framework that effectively captures spatial dependencies regardless of geometric irregularities. Unlike
conventional computer vision–based approaches (e.g., PopNet) that are strictly coupled to regular grid
units, our PopGNN framework abstracts spatial units and their neighbors into a graph topology. This
topological abstraction models spatial interactions and contexts from the physical shape of the units,
allowing the model to uniformly define spatial adjacency and neighborhood influences. Therefore, the
framework ensures consistent and robust performance across both regular grids and irregular administrative
boundaries.
In addition, the framework inherently supports multirange spatial context learning. By stacking multiple
GATv2 layers, the model progressively expands its effective spatial range, enabling the aggregation of
information from nearest neighbors to broader regional structures. This hierarchical architecture allows
the model to simultaneously capture multiscale demographic interactions as well as regional trends.
These methodological advantages were empirically validated via comprehensive experiments in which
PopGNN demonstrated better performance than other baseline models. In particular, compared to PopNet,
a specialized computer vision model optimized for grid datasets, our framework achieved comparable
predictive accuracy in case studies with South Korean and Tennessee datasets. This finding is significant
because PopGNN achieves grid-level accuracy comparable to that of computer vision architectures but
also consistently demonstrates strong performance on irregular administrative units.
Thus, the proposed framework is valuable in that, as a single model, it can provide accurate population
forecasts that address diverse planning demands, ranging from granular grid-level estimates for precise
service allocation and facility location planning to aggregate administrative-level forecasts for macroscale
regional policy and resource distribution.
A limitation of this study is the application of uniform crude birth and mortality rates at the state level
across all spatial units because of a lack of detailed local data. This may introduce inaccuracies in
population forecasts for fine-grained grids or administrative boundaries. Furthermore, the proposed
framework does not account for future housing supply–related factors, such as large-scale new developments,
greenbelt policy changes, or major infrastructure projects. These structural factors are likely to affect
the accuracy of population distribution predictions. Nonetheless, this study provides significant value
by pioneering a flexible population forecasting framework applicable to regular and irregular spatial
boundaries.

 5. CONCLUSION
This study introduced PopGNN, a GNN-based framework designed for accurate future population
predictions across diverse boundary types. A GNN-based deep learning architecture enables PopGNN to
obviate the need for the manual spatial feature engineering that characterizes traditional machine learning
approaches. Instead, it automatically learns multiscale spatial interdependencies and contexts through
graph message passing, achieving remarkable predictive accuracy compared with state-of-the-art computer
vision and traditional baseline models without explicit spatial preprocessing.
In future research, we could explore the incorporation of future development plan data to enhance
prediction accuracy. We could also perform systematic evaluations across diverse global contexts to
validate PopGNN’s generalizability across varying population distribution patterns and administrative
structures. Furthermore, to address the evident spatial error patterns, future work should integrate a spatial
autocorrelation module into the PopGNN framework. Jeong and Lee (2025) suggest that considering
spatial dependencies significantly improves predictive accuracy by capturing neighborhood effects and
regional clusters. Employing this architectural enhancement would enable the model to self-correct local
biases and generate more spatially consistent forecasts. These efforts would not only expand PopGNN’s
applicability but also improve its predictive performance.

 6. OPERATIONAL IMPLEMENTATION
The current PopGNN with ensemble framework employed five individual PopGNN models. Each model’s
optimized hyperparameters were identified using Bayesian optimization and fivefold cross-validation. The
hyperparameter tuning process took around 14.5 h for each model (for the Tennessee dataset), and final
model training using the resulting optimal hyperparameter combinations took 30 min. In total, tuning and
training the five individual models took 75 h using an NVIDIA A100 GPU. The data and codes used are
available in GitLab (https://code-int.ornl.gov/gbj/popgnn).
Please use the following instructions to reproduce PopGNN:
 1. Install packages using requirements.txt.
 2. Run preprocessing_grid.py to download and preprocess environmental and population data.
 3. Create preprocessed population and environmental datasets using generate_grid_data.py for
 grids and generate_bg_data.py for block groups.
 4. Run gnn_prediction_git.ipynb, which contains the overall procedures from creating training
 and prediction datasets to validating the model based on the given data.
Using an NVIDIA H200 GPU could reduce running time by approximately half. Moreover, 50 GB of
storage would be required to store input data for the whole US.
