NeuroProlog: Multi-Task Fine-Tuning for Neurosymbolic Mathematical Reasoning via the Cocktail Effect
Pratibha Zunjare, Michael S. Hsiao

Abstract
. Large Language Models (LLMs) achieve strong performance on natural language tasks but remain unreliable in mathematical reasoning, frequently generating fluent yet logically inconsistent solutions. We present NeuroProlog , a neurosymbolic framework that ensures verifiable reasoning by compiling math word problems into executable Prolog programs with formal verification guarantees. We propose a multi-task Cocktail training strategy that jointly optimizes three synergistic objectives in a unified symbolic representation space: (i) mathematical formula-to-rule translation (KB), (ii) natural language-to-program synthesis (SOLVE), and (iii) program-answer alignment. This joint supervision enables positive transfer, where symbolic grounding in formula translation directly improves compositional reasoning capabilities. At inference, we introduce an execution-guided decoding pipeline with fine-grained error taxonomy that enables iterative program repair and quantifies model self-debugging capacity. Comprehensive evaluation on GSM8K across four model scales (3B–32B parameters) demonstrates consistent improvements: cocktail training achieves significant accuracy gains of +5.23% (Qwen-32B, p < 0.01 p<0.01 ), +3.43% (GPT-OSS-20B, p < 0.01 p<0.01 ), and +5.54% (Llama-3B, p < 0.05 p<0.05 ) over single-task baselines. Our best configuration (GPT-OSS-20B, 88.3%) outperforms larger program synthesis systems including ToRA-Code-34B (80.7%) and approaches OpenMath-70B (84.6%) with 3.5 × \times fewer parameters, demonstrating superior parameter efficiency. Systematic error analysis reveals scale-dependent learning dynamics: at 32B scale, cocktail training transforms unfixable type errors (12% repair rate) into correctable domain errors (96% repair rate), achieving 92.7% overall correction; at 8B scale, the same training eliminates syntactic errors but introduces semantic failures, revealing a critical capacity threshold for type-safe symbolic reasoning.



1.
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved strong performance on mathematical reasoning benchmarks such as GSM8K
(
Cobbe et al. 2021
)
, aided by techniques like Chain-of-Thought prompting
(
Wei et al. 2022b
)
and Program-of-Thoughts
(
Chen et al. 2022
)
. However, recent studies reveal that LLM reasoning often relies on probabilistic pattern matching rather than formal logical inference
(
Dziri et al. 2023
;
Valmeekam et al. 2022
)
. This leads to brittle behavior: models produce plausible but incorrect solutions, fail under perturbations, and cannot verify intermediate reasoning steps
(
Creswell and Shanahan 2022
)
.

Neurosymbolic approaches attempt to address these limitations by integrating neural models with symbolic representations or external solvers, combining neural flexibility with symbolic rigor
(
Creswell and Shanahan 2022
;
Singh et al. 2026
;
Chen 2025
)
. However, existing methods typically apply symbolic reasoning as a
post-hoc correction mechanism
at inference time—validating or refining LLM outputs using external theorem provers or logic engines
(
Pan et al. 2023a
;
Olausson et al. 2023
)
. This decoupled design prevents models from internalizing symbolic structure during training: the symbolic components serve as verification tools rather than learned reasoning capabilities. Consequently, LLMs remain weak at systematic generalization to novel problem compositions and struggle to produce verifiable, executable reasoning traces without external intervention.

Grounded mathematical reasoning, we posit, requires models to jointly learn three core capabilities: (i) mapping natural language to formal logic, (ii) generating executable programs that encode structured reasoning, and (iii) aligning symbolic outputs with numeric verification. To address this gap, we present
NeuroProlog

, a unified neurosymbolic framework that enforces formal reasoning through multi-task
Cocktail
training
(
Brief et al. 2024
)
as shown in Figure.
1
. Our approach supervises LLMs simultaneously on formula translation, program synthesis, and execution verification within a shared symbolic representation space, inducing cross-task transfer and enabling models to internalize systematic reasoning patterns rather than relying on superficial heuristics.

The main contribution of this work as follows:

•

•

Overall, our work demonstrates that multi-task neurosymbolic training is a promising approach for robust, interpretable, and verifiable mathematical reasoning in LLMs.

2.
Related Work

Mathematical Reasoning in LLMs.

Recent LLMs achieve strong performance on mathematical benchmarks through intermediate reasoning techniques. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting
(
Wei et al. 2022b
)
generates natural language rationales with extensions including zero-shot CoT
(
Kojima et al. 2022
)
and self-consistency decoding
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
. Program-Aided Language Models (PAL)
(
Gao et al. 2023
)
and Program-of-Thoughts (PoT)
(
Chen et al. 2022
)
delegate computation to Python interpreters, generating executable code as reasoning traces. However, these approaches operate at inference time through prompting rather than internalizing symbolic reasoning during training. Studies show LLMs perform probabilistic pattern matching rather than systematic reasoning
(
Dziri et al. 2023
;
Valmeekam et al. 2022
)
, failing under distribution shifts
(
Razeghi et al. 2022
)
. We address this through symbolic supervision during fine-tuning, teaching models to generate verifiable logical programs.

Neurosymbolic AI.

Neurosymbolic approaches combine neural learning with symbolic logic
(
Garcez et al. 2019
;
Kautz 2022
)
. Logic-LM
(
Pan et al. 2023b
)
uses external Prolog solvers, Faithful CoT
(
Lyu et al. 2023
)
translates reasoning to formal logic for verification, and LINC
(
Olausson et al. 2023
)
learns neural-symbolic translations through self-supervised refinement. These methods apply symbolic reasoning post-hoc at inference time. Prolog’s declarative semantics suit mathematical reasoning, and GSM8K-Prolog provides Prolog annotations for GSM8K with single-task supervision. We extend this through multi-task cocktail training with semantic alignment, internalizing symbolic structure during training and enabling self-debugging through execution feedback.

Multi-Task Learning.

Multi-task learning improves generalization through shared representations
(
Caruana 1997
;
Ruder 2017
)
. The
cocktail
effect
(
Li et al. 2025
)
produces synergistic gains from complementary tasks. FLAN
(
Wei et al. 2022a
)
and T0
(
Sanh et al. 2021
)
mix instruction formats, while CodeT5+
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
jointly trains on code understanding and generation. Our cocktail dataset uniquely combines three supervision signals: (i) formula-to-Prolog translation for symbolic grounding, (ii) natural language comments for semantic alignment, and (iii) program execution for numeric verification, mixing reasoning modalities rather than task formats.

Program Synthesis and Error Correction.

Neural program synthesis methods include Codex
(
Chen et al. 2021
)
, AlphaCode
(
Li et al. 2022
)
, and CodeLlama
(
Rozière et al. 2023
)
. Self-Repair
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
trains on error-correction pairs, while Reflexion
(
Shinn et al. 2023
)
uses verbal feedback for iterative improvement. These methods target general-purpose programming rather than formal reasoning. We extend execution-guided decoding by leveraging Prolog’s error taxonomy to guide systematic self-correction, complementing prior work on LLM-based symbolic reasoning and program repair.

3.
Methodology

We present
NeuroProlog
, a neurosymbolic framework that enforces executable mathematical reasoning through multi-task fine-tuning with Prolog-based symbolic supervision. Our approach consists of three core components: (1) a unified training corpus combining declarative mathematical knowledge with procedural problem-solving demonstrations (Section
3.1
), (2) a multi-task cocktail training objective that induces cross-task transfer through shared symbolic representations (Section
3.2
), and (3) an execution-guided decoding pipeline with iterative error feedback (Section
3.3
).

3.1.
Dataset Construction

We construct a unified training corpus designed to enable neurosymbolic mathematical reasoning through Prolog-based representations. Our dataset comprises two complementary components that together instantiate the cocktail training paradigm: a
Mathematical Knowledge Base (KB)
providing declarative symbolic grounding, and a
Problem-Solving Dataset
demonstrating procedural application of mathematical concepts in the form of math word problems.

Mathematical Knowledge Base

The mathematical knowledge base comprises 200 carefully constructed entries that formalize fundamental mathematical concepts as executable Prolog predicates. These entries complement the KB by providing procedural demonstrations that apply declarative symbolic knowledge to specific problem instances. Each entry follows a standardized instruction-input-output format:

Knowledge Base Entry

Instruction:
[KNOWLEDGE] Generate Prolog code that encodes the given mathematical concept

Input:
Natural language description of a mathematical formula, concept, or inference rule with semantic explanation and worked examples

Output:
Executable Prolog program implementing the concept with:

•

Module imports (
library(clpq)
for constraint logic programming)

•

Natural language comments explaining semantic meaning

•

Type-safe predicate definitions with guards

•

Helper predicates for compositional reasoning

•

Standardized
solve/1
interface for testing

Domain Coverage.
The KB entries span 15+ mathematical domains with the following distribution: Basic Statistics (22 entries, 11.0%), Ratio & Proportion (17, 8.5%), Percentage Calculations (17, 8.5%), Number Theory (11, 5.5%), Propositional Logic (9, 4.5%), Boolean Operations (7, 3.5%), Geometry (12, 6.0%), Linear Equations (6, 3.0%), Sequences & Series (3, 1.5%), and mixed topics (96, 48.0%). This distribution ensures broad coverage of foundational concepts while maintaining sufficient density in frequently-applied domains.

Semantic Alignment Through Comments.
A critical design choice distinguishes our KB from conventional code datasets: every Prolog predicate includes natural language comments that explain its mathematical semantics. These semantic annotations, exemplified by the combinations formula, are provided in full in Appendix
A
.

Problem-Solving Dataset.
The second component consists of 310 problem-solving examples based on the knowledge base, together with 7476 entries from GSM8K-Prolog
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
with executable Prolog programs. Each entry follows the format:

Problem-Solving Entry Format

Instruction:
[SOLVE] Generate correct Prolog code that solves the given math problem

Input:
Mathematical word problem with numeric constraints and a question

Output:
Complete Prolog program that:

•

Encodes problem constraints as predicates

•

Implements the reasoning chain through predicate composition

•

Produces the numeric answer via
solve(Result)

Procedural Demonstrations.
Unlike the KB’s declarative knowledge, these entries demonstrate how to
apply
mathematical concepts to concrete problem instances. Crucially, the SOLVE entries reuse predicate patterns from the KB (e.g., factorial/2, combination_count/3) while adapting them to specific problem contexts. This design creates a natural compositional structure where problem-solving builds upon declarative knowledge, facilitating transfer learning during multi-task training.

Problem Type Distribution.
The SOLVE subset encompasses diverse problem types aligned with standard mathematics curricula: Find/Calculate (134 problems), Word Problems (115), Verification/Proof (5), Equation Solving (3), Simplification (2), and Expression Evaluation (1). Word problems include classic categories such as basic multi step arithmetic, counting, age problems, distance-rate-time problems, work-rate problems, and mixture problems.

Quality Assurance

Each entry underwent multi-stage validation to ensure correctness: (1)
Syntactic Verification
via SWI-Prolog parsing to ensure syntactic correctness; (2)
Execution Testing
where each
solve/1
predicate was executed to verify termination and correct output format; (3)
Semantic Review
by domain expertsand researchers to validate mathematical accuracy of both natural language descriptions and Prolog implementations.

3.2.
Multi-Task Cocktail Training

We formulate neurosymbolic mathematical reasoning as a multi-task learning problem where models jointly acquire complementary capabilities through shared symbolic representations. Our training regime induces positive cross-task transfer by supervising declarative knowledge encoding and procedural problem-solving within a unified Prolog generation framework.

Task Formulation.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{\text{KB}}=\{(x_{i}^{\text{kb}},y_{i}^{\text{kb}})\}_{i=1}^{200}
denote the Knowledge Base dataset, where
x_{i}^{\text{kb}}
is a natural language description of a mathematical concept and
y_{i}^{\text{kb}}
is its Prolog implementation with semantic comments.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{\text{SOLVE}}=\{(x_{j}^{\text{solve}},y_{j}^{\text{solve}})\}_{j=1}^{7786}
denote the Problem-Solving dataset, where
x_{j}^{\text{solve}}
is a word problem and
y_{j}^{\text{solve}}
is its executable Prolog program (whose execution yields the verified answer
a_{j}
).

Both datasets are split 90/10 for training and validation.

We train a language model
f_{\theta}
with parameters
\theta
to minimize a weighted multi-task objective:

(1)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{cocktail}}(\theta)=\lambda_{\text{kb}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{KB}}(\theta)+\lambda_{\text{solve}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{SOLVE}}(\theta).

Each task-specific loss is the standard causal language modeling objective over the generated Prolog code:

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{KB}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{KB}}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})\right],

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{SOLVE}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{SOLVE}}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})\right].

The target sequence for both tasks is the Prolog program code; verified answers
a_{j}
are used only at evaluation time for correctness verification through execution (
\sigma(P_{j})=a_{j}
), not during training loss computation. Task weights
\lambda_{\text{kb}},\lambda_{\text{solve}}\geq 0
satisfy
\lambda_{\text{kb}}+\lambda_{\text{solve}}=1
.

Training Protocol.

We fine-tune models using LoRA (without quantization), injecting low-rank adapters into attention layers while freezing pretrained weights. Both KB and SOLVE entries are formatted with task-type prefixes (
[KNOWLEDGE]
vs.
[SOLVE]
) to enable task-conditional generation. The weighting coefficients were selected according to a curriculum scheduling strategy.

Hyperparameters.

All models share a common optimization setup (3 epochs, cosine learning rate schedule with 8% warmup, gradient clipping at 1.0, and bfloat16 precision). LoRA rank, learning rate, and regularization are tuned per model scale, with gradient accumulation yielding effective batch sizes of 32–64. Experiments run on NVIDIA A100, H200 GPUs; full settings appear in Appendix
B.1
.

Training Dynamics.

Figure
7
(Appendix
B
) show smooth convergence with no overfitting; loss plateaus after 400–550 steps across models. Cocktail training consistently achieves lower final validation loss than single-task Prolog training (e.g., Qwen-32B: 0.155 vs. 0.184), indicating positive transfer.

Cocktail Effect Hypothesis

The cocktail effect
(
Luo et al. 2023
;
Dai et al. 2024
)
suggests that complementary multi-task training yields synergistic gains. We hypothesize three transfer mechanisms:

(1)

Compositional Reuse (KB
\rightarrow
SOLVE):
KB predicates (e.g.,
factorial/2
) become reusable building blocks for SOLVE programs, enabling compositional generalization.

(2)

Contextual Grounding (SOLVE
\rightarrow
KB):
Problem-solving provides usage patterns that reinforce abstract formula semantics.

(3)

Unified Type System:
Joint training on Prolog syntax and CLP(Q) constraints induces shared type-safe representations.

Empirical Validation.
We validate this through controlled comparison:
Base
(zero-shot),
Prolog FT
, and
Cocktail FT
. For each model, Prolog FT and Cocktail FT use identical hyperparameters, ensuring performance differences arise from training objective rather than optimization. Cocktail FT outperforms Prolog FT for 3 of 4 models: +2.22% (GPT-OSS-20B), +0.38% (Qwen-32B), +5.24% (Llama-3B). Crucially, cocktail training shifts Qwen-32B errors from unfixable TYPE_ERRORs (87.2%, 12% fixable) to correctable DOMAIN_ERRORs (55.6%, 96% fixable), demonstrating that models learn unified type-safe representations enabling semantic debugging.

The exception is Qwen3-8B (
-2.28\%
), where fine-tuning improves surface syntax but introduces semantic errors, revealing a generation–correction trade-off and suggesting that type understanding requires capacity beyond 8B parameters.

Our cocktail approach differs from prior multi-task paradigms: Instruction tuning (FLAN
(
Wei et al. 2022a
)
, T0
(
Sanh et al. 2021
)
) mixes
task formats
with similar knowledge; we mix
reasoning modalities
(declarative and procedural) within unified symbolic space. Code multi-task methods (CodeT5+
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
) train on bidirectional translation (code
\leftrightarrow
docstring); we train on
concept definition
(KB) and
concept application
(SOLVE), inducing compositional transfer. Self-supervised repair
(
Cropper and Morel 2021
)
learns syntactic error correction; we provide declarative knowledge enabling principled semantic debugging. This neurosymbolic training paradigm bridges informal reasoning and formal logic execution.

Design Choices.

We employ greedy decoding (temperature=0) for reproducibility, use identical weights (
f_{\theta}
) for generation and repair to demonstrate zero-shot debugging, terminate early on first success to minimize cost, and provide error-type-specific repair prompts rather than generic retry messages.

Error Taxonomy and Repair Strategies

We distinguish five error classes based on SWI-Prolog’s diagnostic system:

Syntax Errors

(parser failures):
SYNTAX_OPERATOR_EXPECTED
(missing operators:
X Y
\rightarrow
X+Y

SYNTAX_UNEXPECTED_EOF

SYNTAX_UNEXPECTED_TOKEN

Type Errors

(semantic mismatches):
TYPE_NOT_EVALUABLE
(arithmetic on atoms:
X is foo+3

TYPE_NUMBER_EXPECTED
(non-numeric in numeric context),

(runtime violations):

3.3.3.
Feedback Loop Dynamics and Correction Capability

Our execution-guided pipeline differs from prior self-correction approaches
(
Ning et al. 2024
;
Shinn et al. 2023
)
in three key aspects:

(1)
Definitive feedback
: binary execution results eliminate false positives from heuristic validators;

(2)
Structured diagnostics
: precise error types enable targeted fixes rather than blind regeneration;

(3)
Zero-shot debugging
: identical weights for generation and repair demonstrate internalized semantics without correction-specific training.

Empirically correction capability emerges only at sufficient scale. Qwen-32B Cocktail achieves 92.7% correction rate (
k=3
), with cocktail training shifting errors from unfixable
TYPE_ERROR
s (87.2%, 12% fixable) to correctable
DOMAIN_ERROR
s (55.6%, 96% fixable), demonstrating semantic debugging capability.

3.4.
Evaluation Metrics

We measure model performance through five complementary metrics that capture both final accuracy and intermediate pipeline dynamics:

Accuracy:
Percentage of problems where the final answer
a
matches the ground truth after up to
k=3
iterations. This is our primary metric.

Executability:
Percentage of problems where the model generates at least one valid Prolog program (i.e.,
\sigma(P_{i})\neq\text{PARSE\_ERROR}
for some
i\leq k
). This measures syntactic competence.

First-Try Success:
Percentage of problems solved on the first attempt (
P_{0}
is correct). This measures generation quality without correction.

Correction Rate:
For problems that fail on the first attempt (
P_{0}
is incorrect) but are later solved correctly (
P_{i}
is correct for some
i>0
), the correction rate quantifies the fraction of such cases that are successfully repaired:

(4)

\text{Correction Rate}=\frac{\text{\# initial failures that eventually succeed}}{\text{\# initial failures}}\times 100\%.

This metric captures the model’s self-debugging ability.

Average Iterations:
Mean number of execution attempts per problem. This measures computational efficiency.

Together, these metrics provide a comprehensive view of model behavior beyond endpoint accuracy, revealing how models balance generation quality and correction capability.

4.
Experiments

We conduct a comprehensive evaluation of NeuroProlog across four LLMs spanning a 10
\times
parameter range (3B–32B) to investigate:
(1) whether cocktail multi-task training improves accuracy over base models and single-task fine-tuning,
(2) how model scale interacts with the effectiveness of neurosymbolic training,
(3) how the execution-guided pipeline contributes to error recovery, and
(4) whether cocktail training qualitatively shifts errors toward more correctable failure modes.

Experimental Setup

Models.

We evaluate four models spanning capacity and pretraining regimes:
(i)

(ii)

(general-purpose),
(iii)

(iv)

Training Configurations.

For each model we compare three settings:

4.6.
Pipeline Efficiency

We define pipeline efficiency capturing accuracy, executability, and computational cost:

(5)

\text{Efficiency}=\frac{\text{Accuracy}\times\text{Execution Rate}}{\text{Average Iterations}}

This rewards high correct-answer throughput with minimal iterative overhead. With average iterations ranging 1.02–2.43, the metric is primarily driven by
\mathrm{Accuracy}\times\mathrm{ExecutionRate}
, with iterations penalizing repair-heavy configurations.

Efficiency and accuracy can diverge.
Qwen3-8B shows the largest efficiency gain (+61.5%) despite accuracy loss, as reduced iterations (
2.43\rightarrow 1.54
) and improved executability outweigh accuracy decline for latency-sensitive applications prioritizing throughput. Figure
6
identifies two Pareto-optimal points: GPT-OSS-20B Cocktail for accuracy (88.34%) and Qwen-32B Prolog FT for efficiency (82.8), with Qwen3-8B Cocktail offering a resource-constrained alternative.

5.
Conclusion and Future Work

We presented NeuroProlog, a neurosymbolic framework that improves mathematical reasoning in LLMs through multi-task cocktail training and execution-guided decoding. Joint KB+SOLVE supervision yields statistically significant gains (up to +5.54%), and our best model (GPT-OSS-20B Cocktail, 88.34%) outperforms larger program-synthesis baselines, establishing Prolog-based neurosymbolic reasoning as a scalable symbolic AI foundation for mathematical reasoning and program synthesis.

Model capacity fundamentally alters training impact: at 32B parameters, KB training transforms unfixable type errors into correctable domain errors (92.7% correction rate), while at 8B parameters it eliminates syntactic errors but introduces semantic ones. This scale-dependent behavior demonstrates that models internalize symbolic structure rather than memorize patterns, with type-safe reasoning requiring >8B parameters.
Our iterative refinement pipeline with taxonomy-based error feedback demonstrates that models can self-debug Prolog programs without correction-specific training.

Our 200-entry KB covers 15+ domains but lacks advanced mathematics; scaling to 1000+ entries would improve coverage. We evaluate Prolog exclusively; comparative studies across SMT solvers, Lean, and DSLs would reveal optimal symbolic backends. Four models (3B–32B) provide limited architectural diversity; controlled scaling experiments (1B–70B) would isolate capacity from pretraining effects. GSM8K focuses on grade-school arithmetic; evaluation on MATH, TheoremQA, and domain-specific benchmarks would test generalization. Prolog’s verifiability introduces brittleness for fuzzy reasoning; hybrid neural-symbolic approaches are essential for real-world deployment. Execution-guided error feedback supports mathematics education through structured debugging guidance. Verifiable program generation enables deployment in high-stakes domains (finance, healthcare) requiring formal correctness, though human oversight remains critical for problem formulation.

Cocktail FT introduces a slight shift toward 2-iteration problems (8.0% of total) corresponding to its reduced first-try rate (90.7% vs. 96.4% for base), but dramatically improved correction capability (92.7% vs. 17.0%) means these initial failures are efficiently repaired. The final failure count drops from 39 (base) to 9 (Cocktail FT), demonstrating that the model’s exploration of a broader program space (more initial errors) is more than compensated by enhanced semantic debugging ability.

GPT-OSS-20B: Progressive Monotonic Improvement.

GPT-OSS-20B exhibits a clear monotonic shift from multi-iteration to single-iteration resolution as training intensity increases. The base model distributes problems across all three iteration counts (45.0% at 1-iter, 36.8% at 2-iter, 18.2% at 3-iter). Prolog FT shifts this toward earlier resolution (55.0% at 1-iter), and Cocktail FT further improves to 60.0% first-try success while maintaining strong 2-iteration correction (28.0%). This pattern indicates that training progressively internalizes correct Prolog generation patterns, reducing the need for iterative repair.

Qwen3-8B: Dramatic Inversion with Collapsed Correction.

Qwen3-8B Base presents the most extreme iteration profile of any configuration: only 7.7% first-try success with 50.9% of problems requiring all three attempts (and many still failing). Fine-tuning completely inverts this distribution to 69.8% first-try success, seemingly indicating substantial improvement. However, this comes at the cost of catastrophic correction collapse—problems that do fail initially are rarely recovered (24.1% correction rate vs. 70.7% for base). The net effect is a modest accuracy
decrease
(
-2.28\%
, Table 1 in main paper), revealing that at 8B scale, fine-tuning teaches surface syntax at the expense of semantic reasoning depth required for effective self-debugging.

Llama-3B: Correction-Limited Across All Configurations.

Llama-3B-Instruct maintains poor iteration profiles regardless of configuration. The Prolog FT variant exhibits the worst distribution of any setting: 59.5% of problems exhaust all three iterations with low overall success. Cocktail FT partially recovers first-try success (53.8%) but correction remains extremely weak (8.9%). This capacity limitation suggests that 3B parameters are insufficient for reliable compositional Prolog generation and execution-guided self-correction, regardless of training regime.

E.2.
Mechanistic Interpretation: Qwen-32B (32B Scale)

The transformation from TYPE_ERROR to DOMAIN_ERROR at 32B scale reflects genuine improvement in Prolog type system understanding. TYPE_ERRORs arise when the model attempts semantically invalid operations such as
X is foo + 3
(applying arithmetic to an atom),
Y is "text" * 5
(string in multiplication), or
Z is [1,2,3] + 4
(list in addition). These errors represent fundamental misunderstanding of Prolog’s data type system and the distinction between symbolic terms versus numeric values.

After KB training with 200 type-safe formula implementations, the model internalizes correct type usage patterns. The remaining errors shift to DOMAIN_ERROR class—arithmetic boundary violations such as division by zero (
{X = 10 / 0}
), negative square roots (
sqrt(-5)
), and factorial of negative integers (
factorial(-3, F)
). Critically, DOMAIN_ERRORs are
correctable
: the model can add guard conditions (
Denom =\= 0
), input validation (
N >= 0
), or special case handling. The 96% repair rate for DOMAIN_ERRORs versus 12% for TYPE_ERRORs demonstrates that cocktail training enables principled semantic debugging rather than superficial syntactic pattern matching.

E.3.
Mechanistic Interpretation: Qwen3-8B (8B Scale)

The inverse transformation at 8B scale reveals the limitations of smaller models in learning semantic type constraints. Qwen3-8B Base failures are dominated by SYNTAX_OPERATOR_EXPECTED errors (89.4%)—the model cannot reliably produce well-formed Prolog syntax. After cocktail training, syntactic errors plummet to 18.2%, indicating successful acquisition of surface-level parsing patterns: correct predicate structure, operator placement, clause formatting, and punctuation.

However, this syntactic competence comes at the cost of introducing the same TYPE_ERROR failures that Qwen-32B successfully resolves. The 8B model produces programs that
parse correctly
but apply operations to wrong types—it has learned the
structure
of valid Prolog (syntax) but not the
constraints
governing correct usage (semantics). For example, it might generate
{X = atom_value + 5}
with proper CLP(Q) constraint syntax but semantically invalid atom-in-arithmetic.

The 5% correction rate for TYPE_ERRORs indicates that these failures fundamentally differ from syntactic errors: the model cannot repair them via iteration because it lacks semantic type understanding (e.g., arithmetic requires numeric operands). This gap suggests that semantic type reasoning requires representational capacity beyond 8B parameters.

E.4.
Implications for Model Capacity Requirements

These results establish an empirical capacity threshold for type-safe neurosymbolic reasoning. Models below
\sim
10B parameters can learn surface-level code syntax through supervision but struggle to internalize semantic type constraints that govern correct program behavior. Models above
\sim
30B parameters successfully acquire both syntactic competence and semantic debugging capability, enabling self-correction through execution feedback.

This finding has practical implications for neurosymbolic system design: small models (
<
10B) may benefit more from external symbolic solvers (hybrid architecture) than from end-to-end fine-tuning, while large models (
>
30B) can effectively internalize symbolic reasoning patterns through multi-task supervision. Mid-scale models (10B–30B) occupy an intermediate regime where architectural choices (e.g., retrieval augmentation, tool use, constrained decoding) may be critical for robust performance.
