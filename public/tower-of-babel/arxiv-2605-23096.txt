Encrypted Neural Networks without Overflows
Philipp Kern, Lorenzo Rovida, Samuel Teuber, Edoardo Manino, [1.5mm] Carsten Sinz, Alberto Leporati, [3.5mm] Karlsruhe Institute of Technology, Germany

Abstract
Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) enables private inference by evaluating neural networks on encrypted data. In this way, we can delegate the computation to a third party server without ever revealing the user’s data. Currently, the CKKS scheme is the backbone of most efficient FHE implementations, but it only supports addition, multiplication, and array rotation operations, thus requiring all activation functions of the neural network to be approximated by polynomials within a certain interval, imposing strict design tolerances. In this paper, we demonstrate for the first time that this scheme is vulnerable to overflow attacks , i.e., seemingly benign inputs that can exceed such tolerances of the FHE circuit, thereby causing corrupt and unusable outputs. To avoid them, we propose a formal verification technique that computes certified bounds on the ranges of all neurons in the network. By construction, our method eliminates overflows and, in our experiments, removed observed overflows on all benchmarks, reducing failure rates from up to 47% to 0%. Moreover, our overflow-free solution is compatible with most CKKS-based frameworks, as it allows to simply substitute standard polynomials by polynomials with rigorously designed ranges.

Introduction

Private inference schemes based on fully-homomorphic encryption (FHE) are designed to delegate the bulk of the computation to a third-party, without revealing the data itself

Contributions.

To address this challenge, we introduce a framework for the certified design of FHE NNs.
Our framework guarantees the absence of overflow attacks, while keeping the size of the FHE circuit as small as possible.
In detail, we propose the following contributions:

•

We present an efficient algorithm to find inputs that exceed the expected ranges of NNs with polynomial activations. With it, we demonstrate that the modern design of CKKS NNs is vulnerable to overflows. Our empirical result shows that even a small perturbation can cause up to
47\%
of the tested inputs for a CIFAR10 NN to fail.

•

To mitigate this, we introduce a differential verification framework that can programmatically compute certified bounds on the value range of all neurons in the NN and on the approximation error of the output. The framework can handle both analytic (e.g. GELU) and non-analytic activation functions (e.g. ReLU), fully-connected and convolutional layers.

•

We give theoretical proofs that our bounds are sound for any possible input, i.e. not just small adversarial perturbations, and thus can be used at design time to eliminate the possibility of overflows. We also show that NNs with analytical activation functions (e.g. GELUs) yield an asymptotically smaller approximation error than NNs with non-analytical ones (e.g. ReLUs). This gives theoretical grounding to a recent empirical observation reported in
[
28
]
.

•

We further improve the design of CKKS NNs by allowing for the polynomial activations of different neurons in the same layer to be different, as opposed to uniform approximations in existing work.
We thus generalize the Paterson-Stockmeyer algorithm

16

from evaluating (ciphertext

We also provide an open-source implementation

2.1
Encrypted Computation with CKKS

We will now provide a high-level description of the CKKS scheme and defer a larger overview on FHE schemes to Appendix
A
.

Definition 2.1

(Arithmetic Circuit)
.

Given a ring
R
,
an
arithmetic circuit
(or simply
circuit
)
is a directed acyclic graph
whose nodes compute additions and multiplications
from inputs,
constants, and other nodes. With
n
input nodes and
m
output nodes, the circuit computes a function

C:R^{n}\rightarrow R^{m}
.

The CKKS scheme.

In the CKKS scheme, let
N=2^{k}
for some integer
k>0
and let
Q>1
be a large modulus. A plaintext message
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{N/2}
is first encoded as a polynomial (using
a scaled inverse Fourier transform), then a small random error is introduced to ensure security.
This results in a slightly perturbed message
\mathbf{x}+{\bm{e}}_{\text{CKKS}}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{C}^{N/2}
, which is finally encrypted modulo
Q
.

Like all standard FHE schemes, CKKS is based on the hardness of the Ring-Learning With Errors (RLWE) problem
[
50
]
that requires an injection of random noise during encryption to ensure security.
With noisy inputs, each homomorphic operation introduces additional
noise into the ciphertext, with multiplications contributing most significantly. To manage this, CKKS uses:
(i) a
scaling factor

\Delta
, which controls the numerical precision by encrypting
\Delta\mathbf{x}+{\bm{e}}
, and
(ii) a
modulus chain

Q=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}q_{i}
, with
q_{i}\approx\Delta
, which limits the number of sequential multiplications to reduce the scale back to
\Delta
after a multiplication (whose result has scale
\Delta^{2})
.
This technique implies that the depth of circuits that can be evaluated without additional techniques (such as bootstrapping
[
20
]
) is bounded.

CKKS decryption requires intermediate values to remain in a bounded range.
If the magnitude of the underlying plaintext exceeds half the available modulus, the ciphertext may become undecodable, leading to corrupted outputs.
This makes
range management
a central challenge in CKKS circuit design.
In CKKS, as explained above, standard non-linear activation functions such as ReLU or GELU must be replaced by polynomial approximations.
The accuracy of such approximations depends critically on the input range over which they are evaluated.
If intermediate values exceed the expected range, approximation errors grow rapidly (see Figure
3
) and may ultimately yield arithmetic overflows.
Ensuring that all intermediate values remain within certified bounds is therefore essential for reliable encrypted inference, and forms the basis of our approach.

Formally, the CKKS scheme evaluates an arithmetic circuit
C
as follows (details on how encrypted operations are performed can be found in Appendix
B
):

Definition 2.2

(CKKS Circuit
[
21
]
)
.

Under the CKKS encryption scheme
(\mathsf{KeyGen},\mathsf{Enc},\mathsf{Dec},\mathsf{Eval})
,
a
CKKS circuit
is an arithmetic circuit
C
with operations

\mathsf{ADD}
,
\mathsf{MULT}
and
\mathsf{ROT}
(for rotate),
evaluated on encrypted inputs via

f_{\text{CKKS}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}):=\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{Eval}(C,\mathsf{Enc}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})).

The output satisfies
f_{\text{CKKS}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})=C(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})+e_{\text{CKKS}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})
, where

e_{\text{CKKS}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})
captures the accumulated error in the output of the circuit.

2.2
Polynomial Networks

We can interpret NNs as arithmetic circuits.
As CKKS only supports additions, multiplications and array rotations, we also define polynomial NNs that approximate standard NNs:

Definition 2.3

(Feed-Forward Neural Network)
.

A

I

\mathbf{z}^{(L)}\in\mathbb{R}^{O}

at layer

Definition 2.4

(Polynomial Neural Network)
.

A

computes a function

\mathbf{y}^{(l)}=\pi^{(l)}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{(l)}\right)=\pi^{(l)}\left(W^{(l)}\mathbf{y}^{(l-1)}+\mathbf{b}^{(l)}\right)

\mathbf{y}^{(l)}\approx\mathbf{z}^{(l)}

With these approximations we can differentiate two different sources of error that
influence the CKKS circuit’s output

(1)

The polynomial approximation error

2.3
Range Estimation

To construct accurate polynomial approximations for activation functions,
we must bound the range of the activation’s input (i.e. its pre-activation values).
Therefore, we need to identify intervals
[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{l}}^{(l)},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(l)}]
for each layer
l
such that
f_{\pi}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x})\in[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{l}}^{(l)},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{(l)}]
is satisfied
for all inputs
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{I}
.
If there exist inputs
\mathbf{x}
where
f_{\pi}^{(l)}(\mathbf{x})
is not in the computed approximation interval,
the approximation may result in large errors and uncontrollable growth, eventually leading to arithmetic failures.
This phenomenon
is visible in the
\mathrm{GELU}
approximation in Figure
3
which is precise for the range
\left[-3,3\right]
, but diverges outside.

Figure 3
:
Polynomial approximations diverge to
\pm\infty
outside of the approximation interval.

Sampling-based design.

The most common approach to compute ranges is based on observations from a dataset
\mathcal{D}
sampled from the training data distribution. For intermediate layers
l
, bounds
\mathbf{l}^{(l)},\mathbf{u}^{(l)}
are computed, such that
\mathbf{l}^{(l)}\leq f^{(l)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\leq\mathbf{u}^{(l)}

holds for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{D}
.
Sampling-based estimates provide no guarantees outside the dataset
\mathcal{D}
and may significantly underestimate the true ranges.
As a result, inputs that are out-of-distribution – or perturbed – can easily violate these ranges.

Existing countermeasures
include extending sampled
ranges by a factor of two
[
28
]
or
choosing a constant range of
[-c,c]
for a suitable constant
c>0

[
46
]
.
Others propose to reduce the impact of outliers on ranges by pre-multiplying the weights by orthogonal matrices
[
55
]
.
We show that range violations due to sampling-based designs can lead to catastrophic failures in CKKS NNs.

3
Overflow Attacks on CKKS Networks

Computing
\mathsf{Eval}(f_{\pi},\mathsf{Enc}(\mathbf{x}))
over encrypted data is a
total function

which always returns
some
value.
Whether such value approximately matches the expected result is discovered only at decryption time.
We say that a decryption
fails
if the norm of the underlying plaintext is larger than half the modulus; this induces a wrap around the modulus yielding almost randomly looking results.
To emphasize the latter, we propose an extended definition of CKKS NNs which makes decryption failures explicit:

Definition 3.1

(CKKS Network with Failure Semantics)
.

We modify the definition of CKKS networks such that
the output function’s range includes an additional
value,
\bot
, which indicates failure of the computation. We call the modified function

f^{\bot}_{\text{CKKS}}:\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\R^{O}\cup\,\{\bot\}
.

While the output of
f^{\bot}_{\text{CKKS}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})
is stochastic, due to the cryptographic noise
{\bm{e}}_{\text{CKKS}}
,
we aim to check whether there exist inputs
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{I}
that almost always cause a corrupt output
f^{\bot}_{\text{CKKS}}(\mathbf{x})=\bot
.
We posit the existence of
overflow attacks
which induce failures (i.e.
f^{\bot}_{\text{CKKS}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\right)=\bot
) with high probability:

Definition 3.2

(Attacks and Robustness)
.

Let
f_{\pi}:\mathcal{I}\to\mathbb{R}^{O}

be a polynomial network with expected ranges

[\mathbf{l}^{(l)},\mathbf{u}^{(l)}]
for all
layers
l
.
An input
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{I}
is an
overflow attack on
f_{\pi}
iff there is a layer
l
where

f^{(l)}_{\pi}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\right)\not\in[\mathbf{l}^{(l)},\mathbf{u}^{(l)}]
.
The network
f_{\pi}
is
overflow robust
(on
\mathcal{I}
) if no overflow attacks on
f_{\pi}
exist.

As the polynomial functions used for activation function approximation are typically only well-behaved on the interval
\left[\mathbf{l}^{(l)},\mathbf{u}^{(l)}\right]
(see also Figure
3
and Section
2.3
), inputs outside this range typically induce large-scale deviations between
f
and
f_{\pi}
which yield arithmetical errors.
Indeed, as we show below, such inputs can easily be constructed.

Finding overflows.

To uncover vulnerabilities in CKKS NNs that are not overflow robust, we leverage classic techniques from adversarial example generation:
For a given input
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{I}
we generate random noise
\boldsymbol{\delta}
which we use to produce a perturbed version of the input (denoted
\gamma\left(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\delta}\right)
).
We then use projected gradient descent
[
51
]
to optimize
\boldsymbol{\delta}
in order to find inputs which violate the internal bound assumptions of
f_{\pi}
.
As direct optimization on
f_{\pi}
is numerically instable, we optimize w.r.t.
f
by pushing the internal activation values (i.e.
f^{(l)}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\right)
for some inner layer
l
of the NN) outside the interval used for approximation.
To this end, note that the internal activation values of
f
and
f_{\pi}
are tightly related (until the latter violates its bound) and thus act as a natural surrogate.
Formally, we maximize the distance of internal activation values to the interval centre point
(\mathbf{u}^{(l)}+\mathbf{l}^{(l)})/2
:

\sum_{j}\left\lVert\left(f^{(l)}\left(\gamma\left(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\delta}\right)\right)\right)_{j}-\left(\mathbf{u}^{(l)}_{j}+\mathbf{l}^{(l)}_{j}\right)/2\right\rVert_{\infty}.

Empirical evidence.

As we will show in Section
5.2
, CKKS circuits constructed via sampled bounds are vulnerable to overflow attacks for
realistic
inputs (see examples for CIFAR in Figure
1

6
Conclusions and Directions for Future Work

In this paper, we demonstrate for the first time the vulnerability of current CKKS-based neural networks to overflow attacks. To solve this, we provide a framework to design certified neural networks. By construction, our approach yields networks that are provably overflow robust on the full input space, without sacrificing predictive performance.

Limitations and future work.

We see this work as a starting point for more robust FHE inference.
Precise modelling of the CKKS noise like in
[
24
,
12
]
could strengthen our bounds to include
{\bm{e}}_{\text{CKKS}}
.
Extending our certified design to arbitrary depth networks is possible in theory but requires the inclusion of bootstrapping and co-design of convolution layers
[
37
]
to reduce the CKKS noise.
Finally, our method may occasionally generate polynomials with ill-scaled coefficients, which cause a reduction on the CKKS precision.

Appendix A
A general overview of FHE schemes

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) is an advanced cryptographic primitive that, extending standard encryption, allows operations to be performed on encrypted data, without having access to the secret key. It was firstly theorized by Rivest, Adleman and Dertouzos in
[
65
]
, and made possible by Gentry in its seminal work
[
35
]
.

Some modern FHE schemes.

A few years after Gentry’s work, it is possible to find different approaches to build a FHE scheme from the (ring)-Learning with errors
[
62
,
50
]
hard problem, and we can group them in two categories. The first performs very fast operations but on a single integer value at a time, has an easy programming model and lighter parameters. Originally introduced by Ducas and Micciancio in FHEW
[
27
,
34
]
, the reference scheme for this category is nowadays TFHE, introduced by Chillotti, Gama, Georgieva and Izabachène
[
22
]
. While TFHE allows one to compute arbitrary activation functions via (relatively small) lookup tables, it requires strong quantisation and lacks parallelism, which hampers its overall performance
[
4
]
.

On the other hand, the second group includes schemes that allow operating on many values at the same time, following a
single instruction, multiple data
(SIMD) approach. The main reference schemes for this category are BGV/BFV
[
13
,
14
,
29
]
and CKKS
[
21
]
. The former operate on a plaintext space of the shape
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for some (modestly large) plaintext modulus
p\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, and allow encoding many values in the same ciphertext
[
70
]
— thus enabling SIMD operations. We may refer to this category as
exact-FHE
. To see how the accumulated error can be removed during decryption, let us consider the case of BGV. There, a message
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is encoded as
m+p\cdot e
for some small error term
e
. Hence, by simply evaluating a modular reduction by
p
, one can remove the final error. Unfortunately, this introduces a limit over the amount of values that can be encoded, namely values smaller than
p
. Moreover, BGV and BFV are generally considered less suitable for deep FHE neural networks as they lack an efficient way to evaluate activation functions — only additions and multiplications in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
are available.

In contrast, CKKS
[
21
]
, which we may call
approximate-FHE
, has as its plaintext space the complex space
\mathbb{C}
, and natively encodes vectors of complex numbers. It allows for approximate computations by considering the noise, naturally present for security reasons, as part of the message. Furthermore, it offers efficient primitives to compute vector operations such as element-wise additions, multiplications and rotations. For this reason, it is one of the preferred schemes for executing deep FHE neural networks
[
23
]
.Currently, all the available FHE schemes are based on the hardness of the Ring-Learning With Errors (RLWE) problem
[
50
]
, and, as every other lattice-based cryptography primitive, require injecting random noise during encryption process to ensure security.

Some fresh approaches.

Nowadays, we can find more exotic approaches to FHE, based on the classic schemes introduced in the previous paragraph.
In particular, more recent schemes focus on improving the error growth in deep circuits and on the ability to represent large plaintext rings
[
17
,
18
,
32
,
9
,
41
,
68
]
.
In particular we refer the interested reader to, e.g., GBFV
[
32
,
15
]
, dBFV
[
59
]
, discrete-CKKS
[
9
,
41
,
68
]
, tuple-CKKS
[
18
]
.

Appendix B
The CKKS scheme

The Cheon-Kim-Kim-Song (CKKS) scheme, originally introduced in
[
21
]
, currently represents the only framework to efficiently handle complex (and real) arithmetic in FHE
5
5

5

An exception is given in
[
6
]
, where the authors present an approach to handle float numbers in TFHE. That method, however, poorly fits neural network operations as it allows one to handle a single value at the time, while CKKS has a significant parallelization
. As other FHE schemes, it is lattice-based and it has a security reduction to the hardness of the ring-Learning with Errors (RLWE) problem
[
50
]
.
This is a structured version of the original Learning with errors (LWE) introduced for efficiency reasons.

Definition B.1

(Ring learning with errors (RLWE)
[
50
]
)
.

Let
\chi_{s},\chi_{e}
be distributions over
\mathcal{R}:=\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X^{N}+1)
. The goal of the RLWE problem is to distinguish
({\bm{a}},{\bm{x}})
from
({\bm{a}},{\bm{a}}{\bm{s}}+{\bm{e}})
for uniformly random
{\bm{a}},{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{R}_{Q}
,
{\bm{e}}\leftarrow\chi_{\bm{e}}
, and
{\bm{s}}\leftarrow\chi_{\bm{s}}
. The RLWE
{}_{N,Q,\chi_{e},\chi_{e}}
assumption is that solving the RLWE
{}_{N,Q,\chi_{e},\chi_{e}}
problem is unfeasible.

In this work, we employ an optimized version of the CKKS scheme which uses the double Chinese Remainder Theorem (DCRT) representation of polynomials
[
40
]
, implemented in the OpenFHE library
[
1
]
.

Basics of CKKS.

Let
N=2^{k}
for some integer
k>0
and
Q>0
being some large modulus.
Let
\text{DFT}:\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^{N}+1)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{N/2}
be a discrete Fourier transform (DFT) defined as
{\bm{m}}(X)\mapsto({\bm{m}}(\zeta^{5^{i}}))_{0\leq i<N/2}
, and let
\text{iDFT}:\mathbb{C}^{N/2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^{N}+1)
be its inverse, with
\zeta
being a
2N
-th primitive root of unity.
The cleartext space of CKKS is
\mathbb{C}^{N/2}
, the plaintext space (i.e., after the encoding) is
\mathcal{R}:=\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X^{N}+1)
, and the ciphertext space is
\mathcal{R}_{Q}^{2}
. In order to make the cleartext space compatible with RLWE encryption (which takes a message in
\mathcal{R}
, and returns a ciphertext in
\mathcal{R}_{q}^{2}
), the CKKS scheme uses (the inverse of) a discrete Fourier transform encoding that moves a plaintext from
\mathbb{C}^{N/2}
to
\mathcal{R}
as follows:

\underbrace{\mathbb{C}^{N/2}}_{\text{Cleartext}}\xrightarrow{\;\;\;\text{iDFT}\;\;\;}\underbrace{\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^{N}+1)}_{\text{Plaintext}}\xrightarrow{\;\;\;\text{Rounding}\;\;\;}\underbrace{\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X^{N}+1)}_{\text{Plaintext}}\xrightarrow{\;\;\;\text{Encryption}\;\;\;}\underbrace{\mathcal{R}_{Q}^{2}}_{\text{Ciphertext}},

where the rounding follows ideas from
[
50
, Section 2]
. As it will be shown later, this rounding operation implies that we are required to scale the message by some large factor
\Delta>0
to preserve the most significant digits.
In practice, numbers behave similarly to a fixed-point system, where precision is uniform across all values regardless of their size. The scaling factor
\Delta
is the key parameter governing computational precision: increasing it yields greater accuracy but comes at the cost of (i) making computations more expensive and (ii) requiring a larger modulus
Q
— which impacts the security.

Additionally, given the homomorphisms induced by the (i)DFT:

\begin{split}a+b=\mathrm{DFT}(\mathrm{iDFT}(a)+\mathrm{iDFT}(b))\quad\text{and}\quad a\odot b=\mathrm{DFT}(\mathrm{iDFT}(a)\cdot\mathrm{iDFT}(b)),\end{split}

where
\odot
is the slot-wise multiplication, this naturally induces a SIMD computational paradigm on the plaintext space by simply multiplying ciphertexts in the iDFT domain.

We can summarize the main operations of the scheme as follows.

•

•

CKKS.Keygen
({\bm{t}})
. Given the public parameter tuple

\mathbf{t}

{\bm{s}}\sim\chi_{s}
from
\mathcal{R}
,

\mathbf{a}\leftarrow\mathcal{R}_{Q_{L}}
uniformly, and

\mathbf{e}\sim\chi_{e}

Note that

({\bm{p}})
. Given a plaintext

{\bm{p}}\in\mathcal{R}_{Q_{L}}
and the public key
pk
, sample an
ephemeral mask
{\bm{u}}\sim\chi_{s}
and noise terms

{\bm{e}}_{0},{\bm{e}}_{1}\sim\chi_{e}
. Output the ciphertext

\bigl([pk_{0}\,{\bm{u}}+{\bm{e}}_{0}+\mathbf{p}]_{Q_{L}},\;[pk_{1}\,{\bm{u}}+{\bm{e}}_{1}]_{Q_{L}}\bigr)\;\in\;\mathcal{R}_{Q_{L}}^{2}.

•

CKKS.Decrypt
({\bm{c}})
. Given a ciphertext

{\bm{c}}=(c_{0},c_{1})\in\mathcal{R}_{Q_{L}}^{2}
and the secret key

{\bm{s}}
, compute the inner product
c_{0}+c_{1}\,\mathbf{s}
. Output
the approximate plaintext

\begin{split}c_{0}+c_{1}\,\mathbf{s}&=\bigl(pk_{0}\,{\bm{u}}+{\bm{e}}_{0}+{\bm{p}}\bigr)+\bigl(pk_{1}\,{\bm{u}}+{\bm{e}}_{1}\bigr){\bm{s}}\\
&=\bigl(-{\bm{a}}{\bm{s}}+{\bm{e}}\bigr){\bm{u}}+{\bm{e}}_{0}+{\bm{p}}+{\bm{a}}\,{\bm{u}}\,{\bm{s}}+{\bm{e}}_{1}\,{\bm{s}}\\
&=\underbrace{{\bm{e}}\cdot{\bm{u}}+{\bm{e}}_{0}+{\bm{e}}_{1}\,{\bm{s}}}_{\text{small noise}}+{\bm{p}}\;\approx\;{\bm{p}}\;\in\;\mathcal{R}_{Q_{L}}.\end{split}

Encryption and decryption follow from standard RLWE encryption
[
50
]
. Notice that the CKKS noise is given by two factors: the rounding performed in the encoding and the error inject during encryption.

Homomorphic operations.

The CKKS scheme enables different homomorphic operations, we report in the following some of them:

•

CKKS.Add
({\bm{c}},{\bm{c}}^{\prime})
. Given two ciphertexts

{\bm{c}},{\bm{c}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{Q_{\ell}}^{2}
, output the
component-wise sum

{\bm{c}}_{\text{add}}\;:=\;{\bm{c}}+{\bm{c}}^{\prime}\;=\;(c_{0}+c_{0}^{\prime},\;c_{1}+c_{1}^{\prime})\;\in\;\mathcal{R}_{Q_{\ell}}^{2},

which satisfies

\text{Decrypt}({\bm{c}}_{\text{add}})\approx\text{Decrypt}({\bm{c}})+\text{Decrypt}({\bm{c}}^{\prime})
.

•

CKKS.Mul
({\bm{c}},{\bm{c}}^{\prime})
. Given two ciphertexts

{\bm{c}},{\bm{c}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{Q_{\ell}}^{2}
, compute the
degree-2 tensor product

(c_{0}c_{0}^{\prime},\;c_{0}c_{1}^{\prime}+c_{1}c_{0}^{\prime},\;c_{1}c_{1}^{\prime})\;\in\;\mathcal{R}_{Q_{\ell}}^{3},

and apply
\text{KeySwitch}(\,\cdot\,,sk^{2}\to sk)
to reduce back
to two components. Output

{\bm{c}}_{\text{mul}}\;\in\;\mathcal{R}_{Q_{\ell}}^{2},

which satisfies

\text{Decrypt}({\bm{c}}_{\text{mul}})\approx\text{Decrypt}({\bm{c}})\cdot\text{Decrypt}({\bm{c}}^{\prime})
.
Note that the resulting scaling factor is
\Delta^{2}
; a subsequent call
to
Rescale
is typically needed to restore it to
\Delta
.

•

CKKS.KeySwitch
({\bm{c}},evk)
. Given a ciphertext

{\bm{c}}=(c_{0},c_{1},c_{2})\in\mathcal{R}_{Q_{\ell}}^{3}
encrypted
under
sk^{2}
and an evaluation key

— a public encryption of

sk^{2}
under
sk
— decompose

into a base-

c_{2}=\sum_{j}c_{2}^{(j)}P^{j}
and absorb it into the first two

\bigl(c_{0}+\textstyle\sum_{j}c_{2}^{(j)}\,evk_{0}^{(j)},\;c_{1}+\textstyle\sum_{j}c_{2}^{(j)}\,evk_{1}^{(j)}\bigr)\;\in\;\mathcal{R}_{Q_{\ell}}^{2},

which encrypts the same plaintext under
sk
with a controlled increase
in noise.

•

CKKS.Rescale
({\bm{c}})
. Given a ciphertext

{\bm{c}}\in\mathcal{R}_{Q_{\ell}}^{2}
with scaling factor
\Delta^{2}
,
drop the last modulus

from the chain and divide. Output

\bigl\lfloor\Delta^{-1}\cdot{\bm{c}}\bigr\rceil\;\in\;\mathcal{R}_{Q_{\ell-1}}^{2},

({\bm{c}},i)
. Given a ciphertext

which satisfies

Appendix C
About the security of CKKS–based machine learning.

Remark that, in the (approximate) CKKS scheme, the encrypted message
{\bm{m}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N/2}
is added with some noise
{\bm{e}}_{\text{ckks}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N/2}
for security reasons. One peculiar point of CKKS is that the encrypted message is
{\bm{m}}+{\bm{e}}_{\text{ckks}}
, and the error can not be separated from the message
6
6

6

For the sake of completeness, there are cases, e.g., discrete-CKKS
[
26
]
, where the error can be removed. This implies an assumption over the plaintext space of CKKS, from
\mathbb{C}
to some discrete set of it, e.g.,
\{0,1\}
. Note that discrete-CKKS is typically not employed for privacy–preserving machine learning
. Li and Micciancio showed
[
48
]
that an adversary with access to the decryption error of a CKKS ciphertext through a decryption oracle can mount passive key-recovery attack. Such attack is captured by
the so-called IND-CPA
{}^{\text{D}}
model
[
48
, Def. 2]
, and all countermeasures known to date require the user to compute a bound on the decryption error.

The threat model in this setting as follows: an attacker can have access to decryptions of CKKS ciphertexts — which in real world scenarios can be a very common setup — and she wants to recover information about the secret key. The attacker then asks for a decryption of zero, thus obtaining
{\bm{e}}_{\text{ckks}}
. Remark that decryption is performed as
[\langle sk,c\rangle]_{q}
, so the noise
directly
depends on
sk
, and this can be exploited to mount a key–recovery attack. We refer to
[
48
,
19
]
for further info.

We believe that this work can serve as an improvement in terms of security with respect to the IND-CPA
{}^{\text{D}}
model.
In fact, the strongest countermeasures require the final CKKS error to be bounded to apply some procedure before decryption (e.g., converting the results to discrete-CKKS
[
26
]
, noise flooding, and so on).
In particular, our work allows one to
precisely
identify the CKKS error as follows.
Typical neural networks based on CKKS output the result in the form

{\bm{m}}+{\bm{e}}_{\text{ckks}}+{\bm{e}}_{\text{approx}},

where
{\bm{e}}_{\text{ckks}}
is the CKKS error and
{\bm{e}}_{\text{approx}}
is the error naturally introduced by the fact that polynomial approximations of the activation functions are used. Note that
{\bm{e}}_{\text{approx}}
is defined with respect to the result of the plain version of the network. On the other hand, our output will be in the form

{\bm{m}}+{\bm{e}}_{\text{ckks}},

as we implement exactly the same network in the plain and in the encrypted worlds (i.e., we do not perform approximations in the encrypted domain). Therefore, our result will not be corrupted by
{\bm{e}}_{\text{approx}}
. For instance, one could combine our work with
[
12
]
, to have a strong theoretical foundation on error growth during the evaluation of the circuit; namely it is possible to define somewhat tight bounds on
{\bm{e}}_{\text{ckks}}
and to take countermeasures.

On the other hand, bounding
{\bm{e}}_{\text{approx}}
is much harder task and implies very large bounds, especially in deep networks.
We are not aware of any previous work that allows one to identify with a good precision the CKKS error in a neural network output.

On the role of error in standard CKKS neural networks vs in our work

It is very common to observe performance degradation (e.g., accuracy) in CKKS–based neural networks
[
46
,
25
,
66
,
54
]
. In practice, this means that the performance of the plain and the encrypted networks do not coincide. This is usually tackled by using larger degrees for the polynomials approximating the activation functions, although it can quickly become unpractical — especially for deep circuits.

On the other hand, since our networks are
constructed natively
using polynomials, the output logits contain no
approximation error
in the classical sense, but will only contain the CKKS error.
Notice that we can
immediately
identify the CKKS error as
L_{\infty}({\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}_{\text{CKKS}})
. As discussed above, it is possible to reduce it simply by increasing the values of
q_{i}
and
\Delta
. This is not possible in other works as it gets mixed with the polynomial approximation error, meaning that, by using our solution, improving the precision of the result is as natural as increasing the magnitude of the two parameters that control the fixed-point accuracy of CKKS.

Automatic evaluation of Chebyshev layers.

The evaluation of Chebyshev polynomials follows the footprint firsly introduced in
[
16
]
— which in turn implements a version of the Paterson-Stockmeyer algorithm
[
56
]
adapted for Chebyshev bases — however our approach is slightly more general as in
[
16
]
they assume to evaluate a single polynomial in all the slots of a ciphertext. On the other hand, in our construction we require to evaluate a different polynomial for each slot. Luckily, we can use the strategy described in
[
16
, Section 3.1]
and replace the usage of constants coefficents
c_{i}
to packed coefficients in a plaintext as
{\bm{c}}_{i}
. The following corollary easily follows.

Corollary D.1

(
generalizes
[
16
, Theorem 1]
)
.

There exists an algorithm in CKKS to evaluate
d

n

\sqrt{2n}+O(\log(n))
non-scalar multiplications and
O(n)
scalar multiplications, with
d
being the number of available slots in a CKKS ciphertext.

We remark that the cost of evaluating a single polynomial in all slots or a different polynomial, one for each slot, is the same as constants are typically encoded in plaintexts in order to be compatible with the CKKS multiplication
8
8

8

Minor differences might occur in practice depending on how the multiplication algorithms are implemented for constants vs. plaintexts, but we ignore them
.
We therefore used such algorithm to allow for the evaluation of different polynomials in different slots.
Since the original OpenFHE implementation of CKKS does not provide such an algorithm, we used a custom fork that enables the usage of the
EvalChebyshevSeriesPSBatch

\displaystyle V_{[l,u]}(f)=\sup_{\tau}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}|f(t_{i+1})-f(t_{i})|

(7)

is defined as the supremum of the above sum over all possible partitions.
If
V_{[l,u]}(f)<\infty
, then
f
is of bounded total variation over
[l,u]
.

In some cases, there are easier formulas to compute total variation.
If
f
is monotonic over
[l,u]
, then
f
is of
bounded total variation
over
[l,u]
and

\displaystyle V_{[l,u]}(f)=|f(u)-f(l)|\;.

(8)

If
f
is continuously differentiable over
[l,u]
, then

\displaystyle V_{[l,u]}(f)=\int_{l}^{u}|f^{\prime}(x)|\;dx\;.

(9)

E.2
Bernstein Ellipse

The further a function
f
can be continued into the complex plane, the faster the rate of convergence for the polynomial approximation error.
The bound given in Equation (
5
) uses the largest parameter
\rho
, s.t.
f
can be analytically continued to the interior of the Bernstein ellipse

\displaystyle E_{\rho}=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}~\middle|~z=\frac{u+u^{-1}}{2},~~u=\rho e^{i\theta},~~\rho\geq 1,~~0\leq\theta<2\pi\right\}

(10)

as a measurement for how far into the complex plane
f
can be extended (definition of
E_{\rho}
taken from Wang
et al.

[
75
]
).
Bernstein ellipses for varius choices of
\rho
are illustrated in Figure
7(b)
.

If
f
is
entire
(i.e. it can be analytically continued to all
\mathbb{C}
), then, one can choose any parameter
\rho
for the Bernstein ellipse that leads to a favourable value of
M=\max_{x\in E_{\rho}}|f(x)|
in Equation (
5
).

At a high level, we provide linear over- and underapproximations

and

Appendix G
Additional Information on Differential Verification for Neural Networks

In this section, we provide information for how to instantiate the differential relaxation presented in Theorem
4.2
for the
\gelu
function and report an optimization used to tighten the verified differential bounds.

But first, we provide helpful derivations for expressing differences at the current layer by differences in the preceding layer.

G.1
Additional Derivations

As stated in Section
4.4
, approaches for differential verification of neural networks
[
57
,
58
,
3
,
71
]
use that neuron-wise differences between networks
\net_{1}
and
\net_{2}
can be expressed in terms of the neuron-wise differences of previous layers.
In this section, we give a brief derivation of these expressions.

The difference
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}}^{(l)}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1}^{(l)}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{2}^{(l)}
between two pre-activation values can be partially expressed in terms of the difference
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}^{(l-1)}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{1}^{(l-1)}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{2}^{(l-1)}
between the post activation values of the previous layer as

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}}^{(l)}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{2}=\left(W_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{1}^{(l-1)}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{1}\right)-\left(W_{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{2}^{(l-1)}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{2}\right)

(12)

\displaystyle=\left(W_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{1}^{(l-1)}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{2}^{(l-1)}\right)+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{2}^{(l-1)}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{1}\right)-\left(W_{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{2}^{(l-1)}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{2}\right)

(13)

\displaystyle=\left(W_{1}-W_{2}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{2}^{(l-1)}+W_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{1}^{(l-1)}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{2}^{(l-1)}\right)+\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{2}\right)

(14)

\displaystyle=\left(W_{1}-W_{2}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{2}^{(l-1)}+W_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}^{(l-1)}+\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}_{2}\right)\;.

(15)

Rewriting the difference to partially depend on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{1}^{(l-1)}
is also possible.

A similar relation can be derived for the difference between activation values
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}^{(l)}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{1}^{(l)}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{2}^{(l)}
using

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}^{(l)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{1}^{(l)}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{2}^{(l)}=f\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1}^{(l)}\right)-g\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{2}^{(l)}\right)

(16)

\displaystyle=f\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1}^{(l)}\right)-g\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1}^{(l)}-\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1}^{(l)}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{2}^{(l)}\right)\right)=f\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1}^{(l)}\right)-g\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{1}^{(l)}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Delta}}}^{(l)}\right)\;.

(17)

G.2

\gelu

Instantiating the differential relaxation for
p(x)-\sigma(x-\Delta)
as described in Theorem
4.2
for
\sigma=\gelu

\gelu^{\prime}(x)
of the
\gelu

x\in[l,u]

Since
\gelu(x)
is concave for
D_{1}=(-\infty,-\sqrt{2}]
, convex for
D_{2}=[-\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2}]
and concave for
D_{3}=[\sqrt{2},\infty)
, its derivative is monotonically decreasing, monotonically increasing and monotonically decreasing over the respective domains.
Therefore, we can just set

\displaystyle\partial_{l}

\displaystyle=\min~\left\{\gelu^{\prime}(x)\mid x\in[l_{i},u_{i}],~[l_{i},u_{i}]=D_{i}\cap[l,u],~i\in\{1,2,3\}\right\}

(18)

\displaystyle\partial_{u}

\displaystyle=\max~\left\{\gelu^{\prime}(x)\mid x\in[l_{i},u_{i}],~[l_{i},u_{i}]=D_{i}\cap[l,u],~i\in\{1,2,3\}\right\}\;.

(19)

G.3
Increased Precision using Construction-time Verified Ranges

Note that the formulas to compute the neuron-wise differences (Equations (
15
),(
17
)) still partially depend on the values
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{i}^{(l-1)}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{i}^{(l)}
of neurons in the individual networks
\net_{1}
and
\net_{2}
.
Our differential verification approach extends
VeryDiff

[
71
]
, which computes neuron ranges based on zonotope propagation and is thus limited to linear relaxations with parallel slopes.
State-of-the-art NN verifiers like
\alpha
-CROWN
[
76
]
are known to compute tighter bounds for ranges in single neural networks.
If such a NN verifier was used to compute the verified pre-activation bounds of the polynomial network, we can use the tightened lower and upper bounds

\displaystyle l=\max\{l_{zono},l_{p}\},~\quad u=\min\{u_{zono},u_{p}\}\;,

(20)

where
[l_{zono},u_{zono}]
are the ranges computed by
VeryDiff
and
[l_{p},u_{p}]
are the verified ranges for the polynomial network.
