Abstract
Convex hulls are useful as tight bounding proxies for a variety of tasks including collision detection, ray intersection, and distance computation. Unfortunately, the complexity of polyhedral convex hulls grows linearly with their input. We consider the problem of conservatively simplifying a convex hull to a specified number of half-spaces while minimizing added volume or surface area. By working in the dual representation, we propose an efficient O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n\log n) greedy optimization. In comparisons, we show that existing methods either exhibit poor efficiency, tightness or safety. We demonstrate the success of our method on a variety of input shapes and downstream application domains.

Introduction

Convex hulls are — by definition — the tightest convex bounding volume of a shape.
This makes them useful as primitives in bounding volume hierarchies or directly as broadphase proxies for a variety of shape query tasks including ray intersection, collision detection & handling, distance computation, and sampling.
Unfortunately, the complexity of a polyhedral convex hull grows linearly with that of the shape it encloses: a surface mesh with
n
triangles
will generally induce a convex hull with
O(n)
faces. For example, the convex hull of the modest triangle mesh in Figure
has over 2,000 faces.
Compared to the fixed-complexity of simpler bounding boxes or
k
-DOPs, the complexity of convex hulls slows down their promise of acceleration as a bounding volume.
Convex hull simplification methods exist, but their simplified hulls are either no longer guaranteed to contain the input shape or
sacrifice the tightness of the hull by adding too much volume or surface area.
These issues are compounded for convex hulls of subparts of a shape during approximate convex decomposition (see Figure
1
).

Figure 1:
Our simplification is a natural drop-in to approximate convex decomposition methods, e.g., CoACD
[
30
]
.
Their software optionally allows decimation (vertex dropping) as a post-process, but results in gaps between hulls, which permit false negatives during collision handling. Our simplification is tight and safe.

In this paper, we build
on the theoretical results promising the effectiveness of successive halfspace removal (e.g.,
[
22
,
16
,
17
]
) and
present a practical conservative convex hull simplification heuristic
based on greedy halfspace removal using a priority queue in the style of
triangle mesh simplification

[
12
,
8
]
.
By utilizing the duality of convex polyhedra, the computation of each
candidate halfspace removal is not only efficient (requiring only local information) but also simple to
implement robustly.
The simplified hull remains conservative — strictly containing the input hull — at every step.
Our simplified convex hulls are significantly tighter than alternative heuristics.
Our optimization-based approach lends itself to customization, e.g., favoring minimum surface-area instead of minimum-volume, constraining selected faces on the output, inner approximations instead of outer.
We demonstrate the effectiveness of our simplification on a variety of input shapes and as part of a large bounding volume hierarchy and approximate convex decomposition workflows.
Our source code is released open source at https://github.com/[redacted]/progressive-convex-hull-simplification (see supplemental for now).

Background

We are interested in polyhedral convex hulls bounding a finite set of points or a triangulated mesh. These convex hulls are polyhedra with finite vertices connected by (possibly non-triangular) faces.
Methods for hull simplification differ primarily based on whether the hull is represented by its supporting vertices (V-representation) or as a set of intersecting halfspaces corresponding to each face-plane (H-representation).
Before discussing related work, we lay out some foundations.

Common convex hull algorithms (e.g.,
[
2
,
6
]
) and implementations (e.g.,
CGAL
,
QHull
) work with V-representations for input and output: given as input a set of 3D points, return as output a set of faces indexing those vertices.
However,
downstream applications will often make use of the output’s H-representation, converting each face
i
to a plane equation defined by

\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{i}+b_{i}\leq 0
, where
\mathbf{n}_{i}
and
b_{i}
are the normal to the plane and its distance (as a multiple of
\mathbf{n}_{i}
) from the origin.
For example, to test if two convex shapes overlap we can gather the halfspaces of their H-representations and determine if the total intersection is empty by solving a pure-feasibility linear program.

\displaystyle\text{find }\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\quad\text{subject to }\mathbf{n}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x}\leq-b_{i}\quad\forall i

(1)

where
i
runs over total
m
halfspaces gathered from both convex shapes. This fixed dimension problem can be solved very efficiently in
O(m)
time
[
25
]
.

As we’ve seen, any V-representation
output
hull can be easily converted to a H-representation.
It’s also straightforward to bootstrap any
convex hull algorithm to work with halfspaces as

input
by leveraging convex duals (see, e.g.,

[
9
]

for a complete reference).
Suppose we’re given a set of
n
halfspaces (see 2D inset).
We can compute the convex set forming the intersection of these halfspaces by first choosing a center point
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
known to lie inside the resulting intersection.

For each
primal halfspace
, we construct its
dual vertex
about this center point via:

\mathbf{\phi}_{i}=\frac{-\mathbf{n}_{i}}{\mathbf{n}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{c}+b_{i}}.

(2)

Now, we run any V-representation convex hull algorithm on these

\phi_{i}
vertices,
producing a list of face indices referencing them (see 2D inset).
Dual vertices incident on these faces correspond

to primal halfspaces contributing to the intersection’s surface; unreferenced (interior) dual vertices correspond to halfspaces too far outside the intersection to matter (grey in insets).
Each dual face’s plane defines a
dual halfspace
, corresponding to a
primal vertex
, via:

\mathbf{v}_{j}=\mathbf{c}-\frac{\mathbf{\eta}_{j}}{\beta_{j}},

(3)

where
\mathbf{\eta}_{j}
and
\beta_{j}
are the normal and distance (as a multiple of
\eta_{j}
) from the origin of the
j
th dual halfspace.

The connectivities of the primal and dual hulls are dual in the typical fashion
[
9
,
11
,
19
,
14
]
: i.e., edges between vertices of the primal mesh correspond to face-adjacency in the dual mesh, and
vice versa
.

Exploiting the dual-representation allows construction of the polyhedral intersection of primal halfspaces without any delicate intersection constructions or mesh arrangements (e.g.,
[
31
]
).
Common convex hull implementations (e.g.,
CGAL
,
QHull
) offer halfspace intersection routines implemented in this dual fashion.

The choice of center point
\mathbf{c}
doesn’t matter so long as its interior.
In practice, Eqs.
2
and
3
become numerically sensitive as
\mathbf{c}
gets too close to any halfspace boundary.
For a safe and efficiently identified choice
(or to determine that the halfspace intersection is empty or unbounded), we can solve a linear program to construct the center of the largest inscribed sphere, the Chebyshev center:

\displaystyle\mathop{\text{maximize}}_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},r\geq 0}

\displaystyle\quad r

(4)

subject to

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{n}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{c}+\|\mathbf{n}_{i}\|r\leq-b_{i}\quad\forall i.

(5)

Once again, the dimension of this linear program is small, so we may use very efficient
O(n)
solvers (e.g.,
[
25
]
).

In general position, the convex hull of a set of points will be a polyhedron with triangular faces.
For the intersection of a set of halfspaces — in a similar sense of general position — we get triangle faces on the dual hull. In turn, this corresponds to primal vertices with valence 3: three planes meet at a point.
Primal faces will be polygons with any number of sides.
In our algorithm later, we won’t need a strong general position assumption, but we will show that we can arbitrarily triangulate non-triangle dual faces without affecting behavior.

3
Related Work

Convex hull simplification has been considered in theory and practice.
A basic and key observation of many previous work is that the intersection of any subset of halfspaces in an H-representation will contain the original primal hull (outer approximation).
Similarly (and useful when thinking about the dual hull), any convex hull of a subset of vertices will be contained inside the convex hull of all of the vertices (inner approximation).

3.1
Theory and Bounds

Lopez & Reisner
[
16
]
began in 2D proving bounds on inner (and outer) approximations produced by greedily removing vertices (or halfspaces) according to area change.
In 2D, this area of change is always convex. In 3D and higher, dropping a vertex can produce a non-convex “cap” region (though its dual
is
convex). Overcoming this challenge, Reisner et al.
[
22
]
prove analogous bounds for dimensions greater than two, and Lopez & Reisner
[
17
]
attach the local bounds theoretically to a greedy algorithm.
Cast as an instance of the broader coreset selection problem, theoretical inner approximations have also been considered by forward selection approaches. For example, for sets of points in any dimension, Klimenko & Raichel
[
15
]
consider selecting one vertex at a time and prove bounds on Hausdorff distance of the selection’s convex hull to the full hull.

3.2
Mesh Decimation

Convex hulls as a V-representation are triangulatable meshes, so one approach is to just run standard edge-collapsing mesh simplification
[
8
,
12
]
.
Constraining edge-collapse placement to either endpoint
effectively drops vertices of the V-representation,
shrinking the hull inward.
Vertex dropping is useful if the goal is an
inner approximation, but for applications like collision detection this can lead to false negatives
[
30
]
(see
1
).
To construct an outer approximation, Bloom
[
3
]

proposes to run appearance-preserving mesh simplification
[
8
]
on convex hulls and then offsetting all resulting face plane equations to contain the input. That is, simplify as a V-representation and then ensure containment via conversion to H-representation.
Alternatively, Tan et al.
[
28
]
collapse edges according to a locally volume non-decreasing optimization
[
23
]
. While their application prefers a pure V-representation solution, it comes at the cost of containment tightness.
We show that V-representation simplification is suitable for inner approximations, but H-representation simplification is superior for outer approximation (see Fig.
5
). Conveniently, primal H-representation simplification can be mapped to dual V-representation simplification.

As a warmup we consider simply running appearance-preserving mesh simplification
[
8
]
on the dual mesh.
This already often outperforms previous works and enjoys the simplicity of just stringing together two common subroutines without changing them at all (convex hull, mesh simplification). But quadric error metrics in the dual space do not map
directly to volume or area minimization in the primal space.

3.3
Forward Selection

Our proposed greedy algorithm can be treated as a backward elimination solution to the NP-hard
[
15
]
subset selection problem.
An alternative approach is forward selection, inserting points into a V-representation of halfspaces into an H-representation, while trying to restore the original hull’s volume as efficiently as possible.
In theory, Blum et al.
[
4
]
consider iteratively adding farthest points to a running convex hull and prove bounds.
In practice, Müller et al.
[
20
]
implement a similar
algorithm, picking vertices in order of maximal recovered volume.
Like Bloom
[
3
]
, they convert the resulting V-representation to H-representation for strict containment.
Müller et al. do not mention acceleration; a naive implementation of their pseudocode would be
O(nk^{2})
, for
O(n)
input points and
O(k)
targeted output faces. So, for small output acceleration may be negligible.
Unfortunately, the resulting outputs are not particularly tight (see Fig.
6
).
Schott et al.
[
24
]
cluster halfspaces by normals vectors using
k
-means, refitting the H-representation for strict containment (as
[
3
]
). Unfortunately, this choice of clustering has the opposite of the desired effect, leading to poor fit (see Fig.
7
).

3.4
Other Bounding Primitives

Exact convex hulls are at the far tight-but-expensive end of the bounding volume spectrum.
On the other end, axis-aligned bounding boxes and more generally
k
-DOPs are loose fitting but make up for it with very efficient fitting and evaluation speed.
These methods are effectively H-representation approximations of the convex hull where
m=2k
and plane orientations (
\pm\mathbf{n}_{i}
) are fixed (typically canonical) storing just
b^{+}_{i},b^{-}_{i}
fit to be as tight as possible.
Finding an optimal orientation makes these representations rotation equivariant (similar to convex hulls) while introducing three degrees of freedom to further exploit for tightness.
This orientation is itself a non-linear optimization problem;
CGAL
uses a hybrid-genetic algorithm
[
5
]
, but is out-performed both in terms of tightness and speed by brute force search over well-sampled rotations
[
1
]
.
k
-DOPs are a natural baseline to measure the success of convex hull simplification (see Fig.
2
).
Other primitives such as prisms or zonoids, but have specialized or limited practical use for the tasks considered here.

4
Method

We take as input a set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which define a convex hull.
The halfspaces defined by each face of the convex hull equivalently describe its volume as an intersection.
Our goal is to select a given target number
n
of these halfspaces so that the resulting volume has grown as little as possible.

We immediately convert every non-degenerate face of the convex hull of the input points to its corresponding dual vertex about the hull’s Chebyshev center (see Eq. (
5
)).
A face is considered degenerate if its area-normal vector is numerically unstable.
Unless otherwise noted,
all subsequent steps in our algorithm are closed under rationals (e.g.,
CGAL::Epeck
or
GMP
’s
mpq_t
). In this case, using rationals means degeneracy corresponds to exactly zero area.
However, rational types are slow, so when using floating-point values, we filter out faces with tiny areas (below
10^{-8}
) and nearly identical normals (below
10^{-14}
distance in dual space).

We initialize a triangulated convex hull of the dual vertices corresponding to each primal halfspace. We will refer to this as the “dual hull”.
For each dual vertex, we will consider the
cost
of its removal and the
topological change
to
the dual hull to maintain convexity.
Let’s consider the topology change first.

4.1
Topology Change

Before a dual vertex
i
is considered for removal, we record its one-ring of incident triangles (see inset).
The boundary of this one-ring defines a topological (but geometrically not necessarily planar) polygon with vertices
\mathcal{N}_{i}

Our goal is to find a triangulation of this polygon which remains outwardly convex.
We only need to consider this polygon by the convexity of the dual hull: all faces outside the one-ring are not visible to the vertex
i
and thus their connectivity is not affected by its removal.

One option is to compute the convex hull of the one-ring neighbors
\mathcal{N}_{i}
and only keep the outer envelope. That is, only
keep faces
a,b,c
such that vertex
i
is on the positive side (according to

\text{orient3}(\phi_{a},\phi_{b},\phi_{c},\phi_{i})
, see,
[
27
]
).
This is a good option if
k=|\mathcal{N}_{i}|
is large, as its worst case runtime is
O(k\log k)
.
Most of the time
k\approx 6
. And for those small neighborhoods, it’s much faster to trivially triangulate

\mathcal{N}_{i}
with a fan then flip non-boundary edges until all are convex (worst case
O(k^{2})
).
Let’s call this new local triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{i}
, where each
\{a,b,c\}\in\mathcal{T}_{i}
has
a,b,c\in\mathcal{N}_{i}
.
Importantly, the boundary of
\mathcal{T}_{i}
matches (in order) the one-ring
\mathcal{N}_{i}
.

4.2
Geometry Change

The cost of removing vertex
i
is the
primal
volume added by removing the corresponding primal halfspace (red face in inset) and allowing a new “cap” to extend as previously neighboring halfspaces intersect.
We can construct the primal geometry of this cap without explicit intersections by leveraging the dual representation and the new triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{i}
.
Starting with a reversed version of
\mathcal{T}_{i}
we append all original triangles incident on vertex
i
.
Since the boundary of
\mathcal{T}_{i}
matches
\mathcal{N}_{i}
, this forms a topologically closed (and genus 0) “local dual mesh”.

In the dual space, the corresponding mesh is not necessarily convex (see insets in Sec.
4.1
).
Converting each face
j
of this local dual mesh to a primal vertex (see, e.g., Eq. (
2
)) and
constructing the dual-dual (primal) connectivity, we have a local primal mesh of the “cap” (see inset).
This is by definition convex as it corresponds to the intersection of all primal halfspaces in
\mathcal{N}_{i}
and the negated halfspace
i
.
The volume of this local primal mesh can be computed using Gauss formula (e.g., by summing the area-normal dotted with the centroid of each face).

The surface area change can similarly be computed (albeit not using rationals) by taking the primal area of faces corresponding the
\mathcal{N}_{i}
minus the primal area corresponding to
i
.

4.3
Infinite Volume

Sometimes removing a dual vertex
i
corresponds to an infinite primal volume change (consider removing the green halfspace in the 2D inset).
This happens precisely when the origin in dual space falls outside the new triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{i}
(next 2D inset).
This can be robustly determined by testing

\text{orient3}(\phi_{a},\phi_{b},\phi_{c},\mathbf{0})
for each triangle
\{a,b,c\}\in\mathcal{T}_{i}
.
In practice, dual-to-primal conversion becomes numerically sensitive even when the origin is
nearly
on the outside. Fortunately, this situation corresponds to either extremely large positive volume change (and will be effectively ignored during processing) or extremely negative (e.g.,
-10^{20}
) volume change (which is easily identified).

4.4
Greedy Elimination

For each original dual vertex, we consider its removal, storing a record of its topological change
\mathcal{T}_{i}
and pushing its geometric cost onto a priority queue.
When a dual vertex
i
is popped from the queue, it is removed from the dual hull and its
one-ring
\mathcal{N}_{i}
is triangulated according to
\mathcal{T}_{i}
.
Maintaining the dual hull with a half-edge datastructure makes this topological change efficiently output sensitive.

After dual vertex
i
is removed, the local neighborhood of each former neighbor
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}
has changed.
Updated costs and records are computed for these dual vertices.
They’re updated in the priority queue.

Dual vertices are popped from the queue until the target number is reached.
Most applications merely require halfspace representations: each surviving dual vertex is converted to dual plane by reversing Eq. (
2
).
For visualization, or applications requiring a full primal mesh, the primal vertices are computed using Eq. (
3
) connected with (generally polygonal) dual connectivity.

5
Implementation Details

Our initial
Matlab
prototype used a simple dynamic adjacency matrix to track topology changes: this is completely sufficient, as strict manifoldness by construction is not needed.
In our inevitable C++ implementation, we use
CGAL
’s
Polyhedron
class to track the topology of the dual hull.
Besides being efficient, this strictly manifold half-edge data structure makes it convenient for extracting the topological dual of the dual hull (the primal hull) for final output or intermediary visualization.
Although the dual hull could have polygonal faces with more than three sides, we always triangulate.
If there’s a polygon face with consecutive vertices
ijk
then removing
j
only requires that
i
and
k
are collected. if the triangulation includes other vertices, these will be
harmlessly retriangulated during local triangulation and any performance impact is amortized away.
While all geometric computation is compatible with rationals (e.g.,
CGAL::Epeck
), we default to
CGAL::Epick
which provides exact predicate tests, specifically
orientation
(implementing orient3).
We call
CGAL
’s
convex_hull_3
for the initial hull computations and for one-ring topology changes for high valence vertices (
\geq 100
).
We implement the greedy priority queue using a lazy min heap to handle cost updates.
We use
libigl

[
13
]
for mesh I/O,
Polyscope

[
26
]
for visualization,
SDLP

[
29
]
for linear programming, and

Eigen

[
10
]
for linear algebra.

7
Limitations & Conclusions

Finding the
optimal
subset selection problem is NP-hard.
Like all previous works, our proposal is a heuristic.
It enjoys well-studied theoretical bounds
[
16
,
17
,
22
]

and performs well empirically.
Nevertheless, the hardness can sometimes exhibit itself in suboptimal solutions, especially noticeable for very symmetric shapes simplified to extremely coarse output.
In Fig.
14
, we consider simplifying an icosahedron to four faces.
The optimal output would be a regular tetrahedron.
Due to symmetry in the priority queue costs, our algorithm must make early indistinguishable choices that preclude this output. Of 1000 trials of randomly rotated icosahedrons, the regular tetrahedron only appeared 0.6% of the time.
The most frequent tetrahedron output (95.4%) is irregular and larger.
Sometimes our greedy selection fails to reach four faces because it finds the globally optimal six-sided rhombohedron (1.2%). The regular tetrahedron is unreachable from the rhombohedron because dropping any face makes it infinite volume (like a cube).
