Generalized Tetanus in an Unvaccinated 52-Year-Old Male Gardener: Diagnostic and Therapeutic Challenges in a Low-Resource Setting
Authors: Imman Qadri, Raneem E AlKhawaja, Rahul S Mathew, Israr A Khan, Yasir Y Muhammad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tetanus remains a life-threatening but preventable disease, which continues to manifest in adults lacking sufficient tetanus vaccination and accessibility to tetanus immunoglobulin (TIG). The case discussed is of an unvaccinated 52-year-old Pakistani male patient who developed progressive trismus, difficulty in swallowing, and general spasms following a minor foot wound. The early symptomatology was attributed to psychological stress or heat exhaustion; thus, there was an initial delay in diagnosis. His clinical course was further complicated by the unavailability of TIG, severe autonomic dysfunction, and respiratory compromise requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation and intensive care support. This case is consistent with global patterns, in which unvaccinated adult males with neglected wounds are disproportionately affected, while delayed diagnosis and limited access to immunoglobulin contribute to poorer outcomes. Although intensive care management may help control complications, prolonged recovery with persistent functional impairment is common in the absence of definitive toxin neutralization. This case underscores the need for strengthened tetanus booster vaccination programs, increased clinical awareness of atypical presentations, and resource-sensitive healthcare policies to ensure timely availability of TIG in emergency settings.

Introduction

Tetanus is a potentially fatal yet vaccine-preventable disease, caused by exotoxins released by the anaerobic, spore-forming bacteria, Clostridium tetani. C. tetani is naturally occurring in the environment, found in soil as well as the gastrointestinal tracts and feces of both animals and humans. In the majority of cases, the infection is acquired when spores contaminate any breaches in the skin, commonly due to trauma, and subsequently germinate into active C. tetani bacteria. The bacteria then proceed to produce the tetanus toxin, also called tetanospasmin, which spreads hematogenously and binds to motor neurons [1]. Hence, patients would present with neurological manifestations such as painful muscle spasms, trismus, difficulty swallowing, and autonomic dysfunction [2,3].

Tetanus can be categorized into four distinct clinical entities: generalized, localized, cephalic, and neonatal tetanus, the most common being generalized tetanus, which accounts for approximately 80% of cases [2]. Generalized tetanus is a clinical diagnosis made based on history and physical examination findings [3]. Accordingly, physicians should have a high index of suspicion, as early recognition and intervention improve patient outcomes.

Since the incorporation of the tetanus toxoid vaccine into the routine childhood immunization schedule in the United States during the 1940s, the number of reported cases has steadily declined [3]. In 1974, the World Health Organization (WHO) launched the Expanded Programme on Immunization, a global initiative that established it as an essential part of childhood vaccines worldwide [4]. These public health immunization and booster vaccine guidelines led to a reduction of tetanus-associated incidence and mortality rates by approximately 88% between 1990 and 2019 [5]. Despite a global decline in cases, tetanus continues to pose a critical public health challenge in low socioeconomic regions with suboptimal immunization coverage and ill-equipped healthcare infrastructures [5]. Adult tetanus cases still persist, particularly among elderly and unvaccinated individuals, while neonatal tetanus has been effectively contained through widespread immunization of mothers [5]. The case fatality in resource-limited settings is high, attributable to delayed diagnosis, unavailability of tetanus toxoid vaccines and tetanus immunoglobulin to neutralize circulating toxins, and lack of mechanical ventilatory support [1,2,5].

We present a case of an unvaccinated male gardener who had developed generalized tetanus after an overlooked wound on the foot. Early diagnosis was delayed because of misleading initial symptoms and psychosocial factors that complicated the clinical assessment. We shed light on the complexity of management due to the unavailability of the tetanus immunoglobulin in the emergency department, which contributed to his clinical deterioration requiring intubation, sedation, and prolonged ventilatory support in the intensive care unit (ICU).

Case presentation

A 52-year-old Pakistani male gardener, with no known past medical history and no documented prior immunization, presented to the emergency department with difficulty swallowing, fatigue, dizziness, and reduced level of consciousness for three days. He was accompanied by his friend, who reported that the symptoms began after the patient had been working outdoors in hot weather conditions. Shortly thereafter, the patient experienced generalized fatigue and lightheadedness, prompting him to go indoors to hydrate and cool down. Over the next two days, he developed progressive dysphagia to solids and then liquids. This was associated with generalized weakness. According to his friend, the patient also became increasingly drowsy and less responsive than usual, prompting their presentation to the emergency department for assessment.

His family members were contacted and asked to attend to the emergency department. The relatives indicated that the patient had been experiencing significant psychological stress, leading to the initial attribution of his symptoms to a stress-related etiology. Combined with his nonspecific presentation and lack of definitive clinical findings, this contributed to a delay in considering a more specific diagnosis.

On presentation, he appeared drowsy, diaphoretic, and dehydrated. His vital signs were within normal limits, with a heart rate of 95 beats/minute, blood pressure of 115/90 mmHg, oxygen saturation (SPO2) of 97% on room air, and temperature of 37.5 oC. Neurological examination revealed equal and reactive pupils, mildly reduced power rated 4/5, absence of focal neurological deficits, and negative meningeal signs. Cardiovascular and respiratory examinations were unremarkable.

Given the patient’s reduced level of consciousness and nonspecific neurological manifestations, the emergency physicians requested a non-contrast computed tomography (CT) scan of the head to exclude acute intracranial pathologies and space-occupying lesions. The CT scan (Figure 1) inadvertently showed opacification of the frontal sinus and a mixed fluid density mass with thin walls arising from the left maxillary sinus and occluding the left nasal passage. These findings were suggestive of a left maxillary or nasal passage mucocele. Otherwise, no evidence of acute intracerebral infarct or intracranial hemorrhage was identified.

Based on the radiological finding of a maxillary sinus mass, the patient was referred to an otolaryngologist for further assessment. While he was taken to get examined via flexible fiberoptic nasopharyngoscopy, the patient reportedly became increasingly agitated and unresponsive. Shortly after, he began having severe involuntary muscle spasms in all four limbs and generalized stiffness. The patient developed gasping and appeared to be in respiratory distress. His vitals showed heart rate 112 beats/minute, blood pressure 100/75 mmHg, SPO2 85% on room air. The otolaryngologist kept the patient on oxygen (10 liters via a simple face mask) and informed the emergency physicians of his deteriorating condition.

The patient was transferred directly to the resuscitation bay in the emergency department for further assessment. On examination, he was seen to have a “sardonic smile” facial expression, a characteristic sign of generalized tetanus referred to as risus sardonicus, caused by the sustained spasm of facial muscles. This was accompanied by trismus triggered by minimal stimuli, generalized rigidity, and diffuse muscle spasms involving the entire body. Neurological examination revealed generalized hypertonia and hyperreflexia. These findings raised strong clinical suspicion for tetanus.

The patient received a single 0.5 mL dose of the tetanus toxoid vaccine in view of his incomplete immunization history. In addition, a single dose of intramuscular tetanus immunoglobulin (TIG) 6000 IU should have been administered to neutralize circulating C. tetani toxins. However, TIG was unavailable at the treating institution, posing a significant limitation to optimal management. The hospital pharmacy team attempted to source TIG from nearby healthcare facilities and external pharmaceutical suppliers, but they were unsuccessful.

In view of the patient’s clinical deterioration and impending risk of respiratory depression, the decision was made to secure his airway through endotracheal intubation and sedation with IV propofol (1 mg/kg) and IV fentanyl (1 mcg/kg). He was kept on mechanical ventilatory support (settings: assist control volume control mode, fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) 100%, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) 5 cm H₂O). Furthermore, he was started on midazolam infusion (0-15 mg/hour) and fentanyl infusion (0-300 mcg/hour) for his muscle spasms. Once the patient was stabilized, the family was informed of his clinical status and the need for admission to the ICU. Upon further questioning by the emergency team regarding any recent events that may have precipitated his symptoms, the relatives stated the patient had recently complained of a wound on his foot. According to his friend, the patient had sustained a small puncture wound on his left foot after accidentally stepping on a nail at work a few days prior to presentation.

Examination of the left foot revealed a small puncture wound on the plantar surface, covered by a bandage. The wound was exposed; it was noted to be approximately 0.5 x 0.5 cm in size, with surrounding induration, but no purulent discharge, erythema, or other overt signs of inflammation. The patient had not sought medical attention following the injury. Consequently, the clinical diagnosis of generalized tetanus was confirmed, and the wound was identified as the likely source of infection.

Plain radiographs of the left foot in anteroposterior (AP) view (Figure 4) and lateral view (Figure 5) were obtained that demonstrated no retained foreign body or other abnormal findings. Plastic surgery was consulted for assessment of possible wound debridement; however, since the wound was small and superficial, no surgical intervention was recommended. Orthopedic surgery was also consulted and advised conservative management with local wound care and daily Betadine dressings only.

A cardiology consultation was obtained, and a bedside transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) was performed. He was noted to have good left ventricular function, no regional wall motion abnormalities, an ejection fraction of more than 60%, mild mitral regurgitation, normal right-sided chambers, normal inferior vena cava caliber, and no pericardial effusion. Ultimately, the findings were consistent with myocardial ischemia secondary to sepsis or hypoxia. Serial ECGs and troponin-I levels were recommended for ongoing monitoring.

Given the severity of his condition, the patient was admitted to the ICU. He remained on continuous midazolam and fentanyl infusions that were started in the emergency department for sedation and symptom control. Given the unavailability of the specific TIG, the patient was commenced on intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG 400mg/kg/day) for five days as second-line treatment. For bacterial eradication, antimicrobial therapy was initiated with intravenous metronidazole 500 mg every eight hours and intravenous penicillin G 0.5 million units every six hours for a total duration of 14 days.

Supportive management was initiated, which included intravenous omeprazole 20 mg twice daily as stress ulcer prophylaxis and subcutaneous enoxaparin (Clexane) 4000 IU once daily for venous thromboembolism prophylaxis. A nasogastric tube was also inserted for enteral nutrition, which was initiated and subsequently adjusted according to the dietician's recommendations to ensure adequate caloric intake. Routine laboratory investigations and inflammatory markers were monitored daily to assess the patient’s clinical progression and evaluate for evidence of end-organ dysfunction. Given the anticipated prolonged duration of mechanical ventilation and the ongoing requirement for airway protection, the option of a tracheostomy was discussed with his relatives. With their consent, the patient had a tracheostomy created on the fourth week of his ICU admission to facilitate optimal airway protection, improve secretion management, and support gradual weaning from sedation.

The patient remained in the ICU for a total of 10 weeks, which was complicated by multiple hospital-acquired infections. Accordingly, comprehensive septic workups were performed, including chest radiography, inflammatory markers, and urine, blood, and endotracheal secretions cultures. Various broad-spectrum antibiotics were administered throughout the ICU stay, including intravenous vancomycin and meropenem, which were tailored based on culture sensitivity. In addition, he experienced three episodes of generalized muscle spasms in the ICU, which were managed by increasing sedation and administering 10 mg of intravenous diazepam as required. The ICU team regularly optimized the mechanical ventilation parameters based on ongoing clinical assessment and the patient's respiratory status.

By week 11, the patient’s condition had stabilized, sedation was gradually weaned, and he was successfully taken off ventilatory support. He was able to maintain his oxygen saturation at 95% and above on room air and did not experience any additional episodes of generalized muscle spasms. He was shifted out of the ICU to the general ward to continue antibiotics and supportive care. Intensive physiotherapy and speech therapy were also initiated to facilitate mobilization and functional recovery following his prolonged ICU stay. At 13 weeks, the tracheostomy was closed surgically.

Ultimately, the patient was discharged in stable condition after a prolonged hospital stay of 98 days (14 weeks). Arrangements were made for him to travel back to his home country to continue on an intensive physiotherapy and rehabilitation program.

Discussion

Tetanus remains a significant yet preventable cause of morbidity and mortality on a global scale. While universal childhood immunization programs have substantially reduced tetanus-related disease burden, adherence to routine adult tetanus booster immunization at 10-year intervals continues to be inconsistent in many regions [1]. Protective antibody titers are known to decline over time, thereby supporting current recommendations for booster every 10 years to maintain immunity in adulthood. As a result, adults with absent, incomplete, or undocumented vaccination histories constitute a particularly vulnerable population [6]. The patient described in this report was a 52-year-old Pakistani male gardener without documented prior immunization, a factor that predisposed him to an increased risk of developing generalized tetanus.

Given that the patient presented with an altered level of consciousness, physicians had to rely exclusively on collateral history from his relatives and friends. In view of his non-specific symptoms, including fatigue, generalized weakness, dizziness, and dysphagia, a broad list of differential diagnoses was considered. Initially, his clinical presentation was attributed to heat exhaustion or an acute stress-related condition, as suggested by the collateral history. This represents a diagnostic pitfall, as tetanus was only recognized following clinical deterioration and the emergence of its cardinal, pathognomonic features of generalized tetanus, including involuntary muscle spasms, trismus, and risus sardonicus [2,3,7]. This underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of clinical suspicion for tetanus, particularly in migrant or foreign laborers with uncertain immunization status. Notably, the history of a minor foot wound sustained several days prior was not initially disclosed to the treating physicians, further delaying diagnostic confirmation.

In the Kingdom of Bahrain, comprehensive national vaccination programs and strict public health measures have rendered tetanus a rarely encountered infectious disease [8,9]. Current protocols emphasize prompt wound management and administration of tetanus prophylaxis, particularly in patients with incomplete immunization status [9]. In view of the robust public health measures that have effectively eliminated tetanus in Bahrain, only two cases of severe generalized tetanus have previously been documented in the literature [8,9]. Both cases involved foreign laborers with undocumented vaccination histories who delayed seeking medical care after sustaining minor occupational injuries [8,9]. Given the rarity of generalized tetanus in Bahrain, TIG is not routinely stocked in most healthcare facilities. However, in certain circumstances, it may be procured from external suppliers if available.

The first case, reported in Bahrain in 2013, involved a 27-year-old Indian man who presented with generalized body pain, muscle spasms, difficulty swallowing, and palpitations for seven days [8]. Symptoms developed following a penetrating occupational injury to his foot caused by a tailoring needle. Although the patient initially attended a primary care facility, he only received basic wound care, and no tetanus toxoid vaccine was given as prophylaxis. On subsequent presentation to the emergency department, he exhibited features of generalized tetanus and required elective intubation and ICU admission. Similar to the case we presented, TIG was unavailable; therefore, IVIG was prescribed (400 mg/kg/hr) for a total of five days. Following a prolonged 34-day ICU stay complicated by nosocomial infections, he was successfully extubated and transferred to the infectious disease ward. He was discharged 10 days later in good general condition with continued outpatient physiotherapy.

The second case, reported in Bahrain in 2018, described a 38-year-old Pakistani man who attended the emergency room with a history of neck stiffness and trismus for one day [9]. He had sustained a nail puncture injury 12 days prior while working outdoors but did not seek medical attention, as the wound was minor and appeared uninfected. He was treated with intramuscular tetanus toxoid vaccination and TIG (3000 units) that was sourced from an external pharmaceutical supplier. Furthermore, surgical wound exploration and debridement were performed under general anesthesia. Due to the risk of airway compromise, the patient was electively intubated and admitted to the ICU postoperatively for close monitoring. Similar to our case, intravenous antimicrobial therapy with penicillin G and metronidazole was given for 10 days. After a 45-day ICU admission, he was successfully taken off the mechanical ventilator, weaned off sedation, and shifted to the medical ward. Following an additional 10 days of supportive care and rehabilitation, the tracheostomy was closed, and the patient was discharged home having made a full recovery.

In other affluent, developed countries, several studies have reported on the clinical outcomes of patients with generalized tetanus. A retrospective case series from Hong Kong reported outcomes of 18 adults with generalized tetanus, of whom 16 were intravenous drug users, and two had sustained minor traumatic injuries [10]. The authors identified early airway protection and ventilatory support in an intensive care setting as the cornerstone of management. Prompt administration of tetanus toxoid vaccine and TIG was associated with faster recovery. The study highlighted a potential, albeit limited, benefit of intrathecal TIG in severe disease. Notably, all nine patients who received intrathecal TIG survived and demonstrated shorter mean ICU stays (29.2 vs. 42 days) compared with those receiving intramuscular TIG alone.

A critical therapeutic limitation in the present case was the complete unavailability of human TIG, the preferred agent for neutralization of circulating tetanus neurotoxins [1]. Consequently, his clinical recovery relied primarily on antimicrobial eradication with penicillin G and metronidazole, adjunct IVIG, and intensive supportive care (sedation, muscle relaxants, etc.) [7]. Current guidelines from the United Kingdom Health Security Agency, WHO, and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommend the use of IVIG (at doses of 200-400 mg/kg) as a second-line alternative when TIG is unavailable [11-13].

Tetanus antitoxin preparations are typically derived either from pooled human plasma or equine (horse) sources. Due to the reported risk of adverse reactions associated with equine immunoglobulin, including serum sickness and anaphylaxis, human TIG is preferred when available. However, in many developing countries and resource-limited settings, the high cost and limited availability of human TIG have necessitated the ongoing reliance on equine immunoglobulin [14]. Consistent with this, a case report of a 16-year-old boy from Turkey described initial treatment with equine TIG due to local unavailability of human TIG [15]. However, the patient failed to improve clinically as sufficient doses of equine antitoxin could not be obtained, necessitating the procurement of human TIG from a nearby hospital. On the other hand, a randomized controlled trial from Vietnam demonstrated no significant difference between human versus equine TIG in terms of mechanical ventilation requirement, ICU stay length, or in-hospital mortality [16]. Collectively, these findings underscore that regional resource limitations may necessitate alternative treatment strategies to neutralize circulating tetanus toxin and improve patient prognosis.

In summary, this case highlights the convergence of multiple adverse factors, including delayed diagnosis, lack of prior vaccination, and unavailability of TIG, resulting in severe tetanus requiring prolonged ICU management. Comparison with published literature from various countries reveals consistent themes, including delayed recognition due to non-specific early manifestations, frequent association with minor wounds, high mortality in the absence of TIG, and significant intensive care-related complications. Although intensive care is indispensable in the management of severe disease and acute survival, both this case and the wider literature emphasize that generalized tetanus is fully preventable through appropriate vaccination.

Conclusions

This case illustrates the persistent risk of generalized tetanus among unimmunized adults, particularly in resource-limited settings characterized by inconsistent booster vaccination coverage and limited access to human TIG. The rapid progression from a seemingly minor contaminated wound to a severe systemic disease, complicated by acute respiratory failure and autonomic instability, emphasizes the virulent nature of the tetanus neurotoxin. Furthermore, the non-specific early clinical presentation contributed to diagnostic uncertainty and delayed targeted management. Ultimately, despite being entirely vaccine-preventable, tetanus continues to pose a potentially fatal medical emergency.

Immediate recognition of classical features of tetanus, specifically trismus, dysphagia, generalized muscle rigidity, and dysautonomia, is paramount for expediting therapeutic intervention and optimizing patient survival. This case accentuates the dual necessity of sustaining robust adult booster immunization campaigns and elevating clinician vigilance regarding non-specific clinical presentations. Concurrently, systemic improvements in the supply chain and global access to essential therapeutics, such as human TIG, are critical to mitigating treatment delays. Overall, reinforcement of public health prevention strategies is essential to reducing the global burden of tetanus-related morbidity and mortality.
