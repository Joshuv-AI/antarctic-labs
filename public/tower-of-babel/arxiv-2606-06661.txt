Abstract
. We study convergence of SU(1,1) and SU(2) NLFT with sparse ℓ 2 ​ ( ℤ ) \ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}) data. The asymptotics of the associated polynomials orthogonal on the unit circle is obtained as a corollary.



1.
The
SU(1,1)
and
SU(2)
NLFT and main results.

This note is a companion to
[
4
]
where the
SU(1,1)
nonlinear Fourier transform (NLFT) with general square-summable coefficients was studied and we will use some results and notation from that work. Denote
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2\ldots\},\mathbb{Z}^{+}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
and
\mathbb{Z}
will stand for the set of all integers.
Let
F=\{F_{n}\}|_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a double-infinite sequence of complex numbers and
|F_{n}|<1,\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Taking
N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, we define a compactly supported
F^{\langle N\rangle}
by truncation:
F^{\langle N\rangle}_{n}=F_{n}\cdot\chi_{|n|\leqslant N},n\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
\chi_{E}
denotes the characteristic function of a set
E
. Consider
2\times 2
matrices
\widetilde{X}_{n}(z,F^{\langle N\rangle}),n\in\mathbb{Z}
defined by the recursion

(1.1)

\widetilde{X}_{n}=\frac{1}{(1-|F_{n}^{\langle N\rangle}|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\overline{F_{n}^{\langle N\rangle}}z^{-n}\\
F_{n}^{\langle N\rangle}z^{n}&1\end{array}\right)\widetilde{X}_{n-1},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}

with
\widetilde{X}_{n}=I:=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
for
n<-N
. One has
\widetilde{X}_{n}\in SU(1,1)
for
z\in\mathbb{T}
and a more careful study of this recursion gives (see p.5 in
[
13
]
)

\widetilde{X}_{n}(z,F^{\langle N\rangle})=:\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathfrak{a}_{n}(z,F^{\langle N\rangle})&\mathfrak{b}^{(*)}_{n}(z,F^{\langle N\rangle})\\
\mathfrak{b}_{n}(z,F^{\langle N\rangle})&\mathfrak{a}^{(*)}_{n}(z,F^{\langle N\rangle})\end{array}\right)\,,

where

(1.2)

f^{(*)}(z):=\overline{f(\bar{z}^{-1})}.

Clearly,
f^{(*)}(z)=\overline{f(z)}
if
z\in\mathbb{T}
. We also have

(1.3)

|\mathfrak{a}_{n}|^{2}=|\mathfrak{b}_{n}|^{2}+1,\,z\in\mathbb{T}\,.

We put forward a definition:

Definition.

For
F^{\langle N\rangle}
, define the
SU(1,1)
nonlinear Fourier transform (NLFT) on
\mathbb{Z}
as the map

F^{\langle N\rangle}\mapsto\overbrace{F_{su(1,1)}^{\langle N\rangle}}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{a}(z,F^{\langle N\rangle})\\
\mathfrak{b}(z,F^{\langle N\rangle})\end{array}\right)\,,

where
\mathfrak{a}(z,F^{\langle N\rangle}):=\mathfrak{a}_{\infty}(z,F^{\langle N\rangle})
and

\mathfrak{b}(z,F^{\langle N\rangle})=\mathfrak{b}_{\infty}(z,F^{\langle N\rangle})
.

In fact, since
F_{n}^{\langle N\rangle}=0
for
n>N
, we get

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{a}(z,F^{\langle N\rangle})\\
\mathfrak{b}(z,F^{\langle N\rangle})\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{a}_{n}(z,F^{\langle N\rangle})\\
\mathfrak{b}_{n}(z,F^{\langle N\rangle})\end{array}\right),\quad\forall n\geqslant N\,.

Our first result is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose
\{n_{j}\}|_{j=0}^{\infty}
is a subsequence in
\mathbb{N}
that satisfies

(1.4)

\frac{n_{j+1}}{n_{j}}\geqslant q,\quad\forall j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},\quad n_{0}=1\,,

where
q\geqslant 2
. Take
\{\delta_{j}\}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})
such that
\|\left\{\delta_{j}\right\}\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})}<1
. Let
F_{n_{j}}=\delta_{j},\,\forall j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and set
F_{n}=0
for all other
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. For any
p\in[2,\infty)
, the limit

\lim_{N\to\infty}\overbrace{F_{su(1,1)}^{\langle N\rangle}}

exists in
L^{p}(\mathbb{T},m)
.

Remark.
Clearly, in our theorem, the sparse sequence
F
can be taken infinite in both directions, e.g.,
\{n_{j}\}=\{\pm 2^{s}\},s\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
vs. the standard
n_{j}=2^{j},j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.
The pointwise convergence of
SU(1,1)
NLFT with sparse
\ell^{2}
data has been studied in
[
11
]
. In particular, the a.e. convergence was established there. Later, we will comment on how that can be obtained from our argument.

Remark.
Taking
\delta_{j}>0
,
\{\delta_{j}\}\notin\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})
and
z=1
in Theorem
1.1
shows that the
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T},m)
norm of NLFT can grow infinitely.

We make a few observations before starting with the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\|\{\delta_{j}\}\|_{\ell^{2}}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
. Consider
Y_{n}(z,F)
that solves the recursion

(1.5)

Y_{n}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\overline{F_{n}}z^{-n}\\
F_{n}z^{n}&1\end{array}\right)Y_{n-1},

with
Y_{0}=I
. Clearly,

\widetilde{X}_{n}=\prod_{j=0}^{n}(1-|F_{j}|^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}Y_{n}
for
n\leqslant N
and, therefore, it is enough to show that
\lim_{n\to\infty}Y_{n}(z,F)
converges for a.e.
z\in\mathbb{T}
. We denote

Y_{n}(z,F)=:\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\widetilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{n}(z,F)&\widetilde{\mathfrak{b}}^{(*)}_{n}(z,F)\\
\widetilde{\mathfrak{b}}_{n}(z,F)&\widetilde{\mathfrak{a}}^{(*)}_{n}(z,F)\end{array}\right)\,.

Notice that
Y_{n}=Y_{n-1}
if
F_{n}=0
. We will show that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\widetilde{\mathfrak{b}}_{n}
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\widetilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{n}^{(*)}
converge for a.e.
z\in\mathbb{T}
but we need some notation first.
In what follows,
m
will denote the normalized Lebesgue measure on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
, i.e.,
dm=(2\pi)^{-1}d\theta,\theta\in[0,2\pi)
. Also, for the function
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T},m)
, we write
\widehat{f}_{n}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}fe^{-in\theta}dm,n\in\mathbb{Z}
for the Fourier transform. Similarly, given a sequence
\{G_{n}\}
, we will write
\widecheck{G}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{n}e^{in\theta}
for the inverse Fourier transform whenever the sum makes sense.

Lemma 1.2
.

Assume that
\|\{\delta_{j}\}\|_{\ell^{2}}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
, then

\sup_{n}\|\widetilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{n}^{(*)}(z,F)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)}^{2}=1+O(\|\{\delta_{j}\}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}),\quad\sup_{n}\|\widetilde{\mathfrak{b}}_{n}(z,F)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)}^{2}=O(\|\{\delta_{j}\}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2})\,.

Proof.

Denote for shorthand
\alpha_{j}:=\widetilde{\mathfrak{a}}^{(*)}_{n_{j}},\beta_{j}:=\widetilde{\mathfrak{b}}_{n_{j}}
for
j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
\alpha_{-1}:=1,\beta_{-1}:=0
. Then, for
z\in\mathbb{T}
, we have

(1.6)

\alpha_{j}=\alpha_{j-1}+{\delta}_{j}z^{n_{j}}\overline{\beta}_{j-1},\quad\beta_{j}=\beta_{j-1}+\delta_{j}z^{n_{j}}\overline{\alpha}_{j-1}\,.

Due to
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits\widehat{\alpha}_{j-1}\subset[0,n_{j-1}-1]
and
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits\widehat{\beta}_{j-1}\subset[1,n_{j-1}]
(see
[
13
]
, p.6, Lemma 2), we get the
L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)
-orthogonality of the terms due to (
1.4
). Then, for
j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, we get

(1.7)

\displaystyle\|\alpha_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)}^{2}=\|\alpha_{j-1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)}^{2}+|\delta_{j}|^{2}\|\beta_{j-1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)}^{2},\quad\|\alpha_{-1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)}=1,

(1.8)

\displaystyle\|\beta_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)}^{2}=\|\beta_{j-1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)}^{2}+|\delta_{j}|^{2}\|\alpha_{j-1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)}^{2},\quad\|\beta_{-1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)}=0\,.

Since
\{\delta_{j}\}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})
, we can add these two identities and iterate to get

\|\alpha_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)}^{2}+\|\beta_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)}^{2}=\prod_{s\leqslant j}(1+|\delta_{s}|^{2})\,.

Hence, for
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(1.9)

\displaystyle\|\alpha_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)}^{2}=\|\alpha_{j-1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)}^{2}+O\left(|\delta_{j}|^{2}\prod_{s\leqslant j-1}(1+|\delta_{s}|^{2})\right),\quad\|\alpha_{-1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)}=1,

(1.10)

\displaystyle\|\beta_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)}^{2}=\|\beta_{j-1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)}^{2}+O\left(|\delta_{j}|^{2}\prod_{s\leqslant j-1}(1+|\delta_{s}|^{2})\right),\quad\|\beta_{-1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)}=0\,.

Iterating these formulas, we get our result.
∎

It will help to work with
\alpha_{j}
and
\beta_{j}
on the Fourier side.
For
j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, we let
\Lambda_{j}=\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits\widehat{\alpha}_{j}
and
\Sigma_{j}=\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits\widehat{\beta}_{j}
. As we already noted before,

\Lambda_{j}\subset[0,n_{j}-1],\quad\Sigma_{j}\subset[1,n_{j}]\,.

We will need more notation: given
N\in\mathbb{Z}
and a set
S\subset\mathbb{Z}
, we write
N-S
as a shorthand for a set
\{N-s,s\in S\}
. From (
1.6
), we get for
j\in\mathbb{N}
:

(1.11)

\Lambda_{j}\subset\Lambda_{j-1}\cup(n_{j}-\Sigma_{j-1}),\quad\Sigma_{j}\subset\Sigma_{j-1}\cup(n_{j}-\Lambda_{j-1})\,

and
\Lambda_{0}=\{0\}
and
\Sigma_{0}\subset\{1\}
. Clearly, the sets in the above unions are disjoint due to the sparseness of
\{n_{j}\}
.

Lemma 1.3
.

If
\alpha_{j}=:\sum_{0\leqslant s}x_{s}^{(j)}z^{s},\beta_{j}=:\sum_{0\leqslant s}y_{s}^{(j)}z^{s}
, then for fixed
s
we get
x_{s}^{(j)}=x_{s}
and
y_{s}^{(j)}=y_{s}
for large enough
j
. Moreover,
x:=\{x_{s}\}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})
and
y:=\{y_{s}\}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})
.

Proof.

The first claim follows from (
1.6
) and (
1.11
) which show that
\{x_{s}\}
and
\{y_{s}\}
are defined recursively. The second claim follows from the previous lemma.
∎

The following result was proved in
[
11
]
. It also comes as a corollary from our arguments above.

Corollary 1.4
.

Given assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, the limit

(1.12)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\overbrace{F_{su(1,1)}^{\langle N\rangle}}

exists for a.e.
z\in\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

By the Carleson theorem on convergence of Fourier series
[
3
]
, the series

\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}x_{s}z^{s},\quad\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}y_{s}z^{s}

converge for a.e.
z\in\mathbb{T}
. Since
\{\widetilde{\mathfrak{a}}^{(*)}_{n}\}
and
\{\widetilde{\mathfrak{b}}_{n}\}
are partial sums of these series, we get (
1.12
).
∎

Remark.
In
[
11
]
, it is proved that, provided that
q\geqslant 3
, the convergence of NLFT on a set of positive Lebesgue measure implies that the sparse NLFT data must be in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Let
\sigma
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{T}
with the infinite support (in the sense of cardinality). Denote the monic orthogonal polynomials by
\{\Phi_{n}(z,\sigma)\}
and orthonormal polynomials by
\{\varphi_{n}(z,\sigma)\}
, we use OPUC for both as shorthand. That is,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Phi_{n}(z,\sigma)z^{-j}d\sigma=0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{T}}\varphi_{n}(z,\sigma)z^{-j}d\sigma=0,\quad\forall j\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\},\quad\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\varphi_{n}(z,\sigma)|^{2}d\sigma=1,

\displaystyle\deg(\Phi_{n})=\deg(\varphi_{n})=n,\quad\text{coeff}_{n}(\Phi_{n})=1,\quad\text{coeff}_{n}(\varphi_{n})>0\,,

where
\text{coeff}_{n}(Q)
denotes the
n
-th coefficient of the polynomial
Q
. For any polynomial
Q
, we let
Q^{*}:=z^{n}\overline{Q(\bar{z}^{-1})}
. Notice that such
\ast
-operation is different from the
(\ast)
-operation in (
1.2
) and it depends on
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. In fact,
Q^{*}=z^{n}Q^{(*)}
. Let
\mathbb{D}
denote the open unit disc in
\mathbb{C}
.
The polynomials
\{\Phi_{n}(z,\sigma)\}
satisfy a recursion (
[
12
]
, see (1.5.10) on p. 56)

\Phi_{n+1}=z\Phi_{n}-\overline{\gamma_{n}}\Phi_{n}^{*},\,\Phi_{0}=1,\,n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\,,

where the coefficients
\{\gamma_{n}\}|_{n=0}^{\infty}\in\mathbb{D}^{\infty}
are called recursion (aka Schur or Verblunsky) parameters. It is known that the set of measures
\sigma
and the set of sequences
\{\gamma_{n}\}|_{n=0}^{\infty}\in\mathbb{D}^{\infty}
are bijectively equivalent.
We get the following two results.

Corollary 1.5
.

Suppose
\{n_{j}\}
and
\{\delta_{j}\}
are taken as in the previous theorem. If the recurrence coefficients
\{\gamma_{n}(\sigma)\}
of measure
\sigma
satisfy
\gamma_{n_{j}}=\delta_{j},\forall j\in\mathbb{N}
and
\gamma_{n}=0
for all other
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, then the limits
\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi_{n}^{*}(z,\sigma)
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Phi_{n}^{*}(z,\sigma)
exist for a.e.
z\in\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

The proof is immediate if one uses the connection between
SU(1,1)
NLFT and the OPUC (
[
4
]
, Section 3 or
[
13
]
, Lecture 5).∎

Z_{n}(z,G^{\langle N\rangle})=:\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathfrak{a}_{n}(z,G^{\langle N\rangle})&-\mathfrak{b}^{(*)}_{n}(z,G^{\langle N\rangle})\\
\mathfrak{b}_{n}(z,G^{\langle N\rangle})&\mathfrak{a}^{(*)}_{n}(z,G^{\langle N\rangle})\end{array}\right)\,.

For

, define
SU(2)
nonlinear Fourier transform (NLFT) on

as the map

G^{\langle N\rangle}\mapsto\overbrace{G_{su(2)}^{\langle N\rangle}}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{a}(z,G^{\langle N\rangle})\\

where
\mathfrak{a}(z,G^{\langle N\rangle}):=\mathfrak{a}_{\infty}(z,G^{\langle N\rangle})
and

The proof of the following result repeats the proof of Corollary
1.4
word for word.

Corollary 1.6
.

Suppose
\{n_{j}\}|_{j=0}^{\infty}
is a subsequence of natural numbers that satisfies

(1.14)

\frac{n_{j+1}}{n_{j}}\geqslant q,\quad\forall j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},\,n_{0}=1\,,

where

. Take
\{\delta_{n}\}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+})
and let
G_{n_{j}}=\delta_{j},\,\forall j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and set
G_{n}=0
for all other

. Then,

\,\,\overbrace{G_{su(2)}^{\langle N\rangle}}\text{converges for a.e. $z\in\mathbb{T}$ when $N\to\infty$.}

Now, we are ready to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

It will be sufficient to establish the asymptotics of matrices
Y_{n}
. By interpolation, it is enough to consider
p=2\ell,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
where the case
\ell=1
follows from our arguments above. To explain the idea better, we start by taking
\ell=2
which gives
p=4
.

Recall our definitions of
\alpha_{j}
and
\beta_{j}
, and the coefficients
\{x_{s}\}
and
\{y_{s}\}
from Lemma
1.3
. We can partition the sequences
x
and
y
into blocks using the rapid growth of
n_{j}
and write

x:=\{x_{s}\}=\sum_{j\geqslant 0}P_{j},\quad y:=\{y_{s}\}=\sum_{j\geqslant 0}Q_{j}\,,

where, see (
1.6
),
\widecheck{P}_{0}=1,\widecheck{Q}_{0}=0
and, for
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(1.15)

\widecheck{P}_{j}:=\delta_{j}z^{n_{j}}\overline{\beta}_{j-1},\quad\widecheck{Q}_{j}:=\delta_{j}z^{n_{j}}\overline{\alpha}_{j-1}.

Moreover,
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits P_{j_{1}}\cap\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits P_{j_{2}}=\emptyset
and
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits Q_{j_{1}}\cap\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits Q_{j_{2}}=\emptyset
if
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
.
Hence,
\alpha_{j}=\widetilde{\mathfrak{a}}^{(*)}_{n_{j}}=\sum_{s\leqslant j}\widecheck{P}_{s}
and
\beta_{j}=\widetilde{\mathfrak{b}}_{n_{j}}=\sum_{s\leqslant j}\widecheck{Q}_{s}
. We have

\|\widecheck{P}_{j}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{T},m)}=|\delta_{j}|\|\beta_{j-1}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{T},m)},\,\|\widecheck{Q}_{j}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{T},m)}=|\delta_{j}|\|\alpha_{j-1}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{T},m)}

for every
q\geqslant 1
. Take
\mu\in\mathbb{N}
as a large parameter to be specified later. Define

x^{(d)}:=\sum_{j\geqslant 0}P^{(d)}_{j},\quad d\in\{0,\ldots,\mu-1\},\quad P^{(d)}_{j}:=P_{j\mu+d},

y^{(d)}:=\sum_{j\geqslant 0}Q^{(d)}_{j},\quad d\in\{0,\ldots,\mu-1\},\quad Q^{(d)}_{j}:=Q_{j\mu+d},

and

\Delta_{j}:=\left(\sum_{|s-j\mu|\leqslant 3\mu}|\delta_{s}|^{2}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}.

Notice that
\sum_{j}\Delta_{j}^{2}\lesssim\mu\sum_{j}|\delta_{j}|^{2}
.
We write the sums

(1.16)

f_{j}^{(d)}:=\sum_{s\leqslant j}\widecheck{P}^{(d)}_{s}=f_{j-1}^{(d)}+\widecheck{P}^{(d)}_{j},\quad g_{j}^{(d)}:=\sum_{s\leqslant j}\widecheck{Q}^{(d)}_{s}=g_{j-1}^{(d)}+\widecheck{Q}^{(d)}_{j}

so, for
d\in\{1,\ldots,\mu-1\}
,

(1.17)

\sum_{s\leqslant\mu j+d-1}\widecheck{Q}_{j}=g_{j}^{(0)}+\ldots+g_{j}^{(d-1)}+g_{j-1}^{(d)}+\ldots+g_{j-1}^{(\mu-1)}

and, for
d=0
,

(1.18)

\sum_{s\leqslant\mu j-1}\widecheck{Q}_{s}=g_{j-1}^{(0)}+\ldots+g_{j-1}^{(\mu-1)}\,.

Analogous formulas hold for the sums of
\widecheck{P}_{j}
. Notice that

(1.19)

\sup_{j}\|\alpha_{j}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{T},m)}\leqslant\sum_{d=0}^{\mu-1}\sup_{j}\|f_{j}^{(d)}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{T},m)},\quad\sup_{j}\|\beta_{j}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{T},m)}\leqslant\sum_{d=0}^{\mu-1}\sup_{j}\|g_{j}^{(d)}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{T},m)}

by the triangle inequality. Our goal is to estimate
\|f_{j}^{(d)}\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T},m)}
and
\|f_{j}^{(d)}\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T},m)}
.
To this end, we take the square of both sides in (
1.16
):

(1.20)

\displaystyle(f^{(d)}_{j})^{2}=(f_{j-1}^{(d)})^{2}+(\widecheck{P}^{(d)}_{j})^{2}+2f_{j-1}^{(d)}\widecheck{P}^{(d)}_{j}=:I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}\,,

(1.21)

\displaystyle(g^{(d)}_{j})^{2}=(g_{j-1}^{(d)})^{2}+(\widecheck{Q}^{(d)}_{j})^{2}+2g_{j-1}^{(d)}\widecheck{Q}^{(d)}_{j}=:J_{1}+J_{2}+J_{3}\,.

Notice that
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits\widehat{I}_{1}\subset[0,2n_{(j-1)\mu+d}]
and
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits\widehat{I}_{2}\subset[2(n_{j\mu+d}-n_{j\mu+d-1}),2n_{j\mu+d}]
and
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits\widehat{I}_{3}\subset[(n_{j\mu+d}-n_{j\mu+d-1},2n_{j\mu+d}]
.
Now, we choose
\mu
large enough to make sure that
I_{1}\perp I_{2}
and
I_{1}\perp I_{3}
. This is possible due to (
1.4
). Similarly, we can guarantee that
J_{1}\perp J_{2}
and
J_{1}\perp J_{3}
by taking
\mu
large. Therefore,

(1.22)

\int|f^{(d)}_{j}|^{4}dm=\int|f_{j-1}^{(d)}|^{4}dm+\int|\widecheck{P}^{(d)}_{j}|^{4}dm+O\left(\int|f^{(d)}_{j-1}\widecheck{P}^{(d)}_{j}|^{2}dm\right)+O\left(\int|f^{(d)}_{j-1}|\cdot|\widecheck{P}^{(d)}_{j}|^{3}dm\right)\,.

Now, we can use recursion (
1.15
) to estimate the second, third, and the fourth terms as follows. If
d\in\{1,\ldots,\mu-1\}
, we use Young’s inequality:
ab\leqslant a^{p}/p+b^{q}/q,a,b\geqslant 0,p^{-1}+q^{-1}=1,p\in(1,\infty)
, along with (
1.17
) to get

(1.23)

\displaystyle\int|\widecheck{P}^{(d)}_{j}|^{4}dm\lesssim|\Delta_{j}|^{4}\int|g_{j}^{(0)}+\ldots+g^{(d-1)}_{j}+g_{j-1}^{(d)}+\ldots+g_{j-1}^{(\mu-1)}|^{4}dm,

(1.24)

\displaystyle\int|f^{(d)}_{j-1}\widecheck{P}^{(d)}_{j}|^{2}dm\lesssim\hskip 199.16928pt

(1.25)
