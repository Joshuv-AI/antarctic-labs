A battery-free wireless intelligent aligner for spatially resolved, closed-loop theranostics of chronic oral diseases
Authors: Wei Zhang, Wenbo Hu, Chunyan Li, Kun Wang, Fangyu Shi, Fengyuan Liu, Lin Wang, Lin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intelligent wearable theranostic systems are advancing personalized health care by enabling continuous monitoring and proactive therapy. However, managing chronic oral diseases such as caries and periodontitis is challenging due to the oral cavity’s complex, spatially heterogeneous microenvironments and limited space for powered intraoral electronics. To address this, we developed a wireless, battery-free, and wearable intelligent therapeutic aligner (WiB-ITA) that integrates a butterfly-inspired dual-sided flexible integrated theranostic electrode array (iTEA) to simultaneously monitor compartment-matched pH and nitrite and electrochemically gate on-demand antimicrobial release. The system is powered and commanded via a relay-assisted near-field communication (NFC)–Bluetooth low energy (BLE) dual-hop link, enabling stable battery-free operation across tooth positions and full-arch usability. Validated from in vitro to preliminary human studies, WiB-ITA establishes a scalable, compartment-matched intraoral closed-loop theranostic framework. This integrated theranostic platform represents a breakthrough in personalized oral health care and provides a paradigm for managing other chronic conditions.

INTRODUCTION

Personalized and anticipatory health care paradigms are accelerating the advancement of intelligent wearable theranostic systems that seamlessly integrate continuous physiological or biochemical monitoring with timely, data-driven therapeutic intervention (1–5). Through real-time closed-loop feedback, these platforms can detect early deviations from physiological homeostasis, quantify disease-relevant microenvironmental dynamics, and initiate corrective therapy before irreversible tissue damage occurs (4, 5). Such capabilities substantially enhance therapeutic precision, reduce reliance on delayed clinical assessments, and mitigate risks associated with both under- and overtreatment (4, 5). Consequently, intelligent wearables have demonstrated transformative potential in managing chronic, slow-progressing conditions, including wound infections (6–8), metabolic disorders (9–11), and cardiovascular dysfunction (12–14), where early detection and responsive therapy are critical for favorable outcomes.

Despite this progress, translating closed-loop wearables into the oral cavity, a highly dynamic, spatially heterogeneous, and biochemically complex environment, poses challenges that far exceed those encountered in conventional on-skin or implantable settings (15–17). Clinically, the frequent co-occurrence of dental caries and periodontitis underscores the urgent need for integrated comanagement (18, 19). However, the oral milieu contains distinct biochemical and ecological gradients, where the aerobic supragingival region often supports acidogenic biofilms implicated in caries, while the closely adjacent subgingival zone tends to harbor anaerobic, proteolytic communities that promote periodontal inflammation (20, 21). These contrasting microenvironments exist within micrometer-scale proximity, which makes single-target diagnostic or therapeutic strategies inherently insufficient for comprehensive disease management (22–24). Compounding these clinical demands, the confined, humid, and continuously mobile intraoral space further constrains system design, limiting the feasibility of bulky components, rigid electronics, or wired power modules (25, 26). These factors collectively impose stringent requirements on miniaturization, mechanical compliance, biochemical stability, and energy management for any intraoral closed-loop platform. Meanwhile, the largely asymptomatic early progression of many chronic oral diseases, combined with the temporal mismatch between intermittent clinical assessments and rapid, transient disease kinetics, underscores the urgent need for advanced intraoral sensing and closed-loop intervention systems (4, 27).

Advancing intraoral closed-loop systems relies on a holistic, system-level strategy that integrates spatially resolved sensing and therapy with reliable wireless operation, enabling full-oral-cavity accessibility. Consequently, developing a topology-enabled, fully electrically controlled wireless system with closed-loop diagnostic and therapeutic functionality represents an effective and scalable solution. Within this framework, the structural topology, especially a dual-sided configuration, provides a rational means of accommodating the heterogeneous oral microenvironment. It enables independent layers to interface with distinct biological compartments while preserving spatial resolution and integration density, thereby supporting differentiated detection and treatment of supragingival and subgingival lesions (28, 29). Functionally, purely electrically controlled coupling of biochemical sensing and electroresponsive actuation enables precise, feedback-driven interventions and efficient integration with external wireless control (30, 31). Moreover, electrical signal readout is readily compatible with existing wireless technologies, supporting closed-loop operation and device miniaturization. Leveraging near-field communication (NFC)–based signal readout and power supply enables fully wireless operation of intraoral diagnostic and therapeutic platforms, eliminating the need for bulky connectors or batteries, facilitating continuous monitoring, real-time feedback, and precise actuation within the confined oral cavity (32–34). Its short-range, low-power communication characteristics are ideally suited to the semienclosed oral environment. Integrating sensing, therapy, and wireless control within the chemically active and spatially confined oral cavity remains highly challenging, yet it is essential for bridging the longstanding gap between isolated components and fully autonomous, clinically deployable closed-loop systems, underscoring the critical necessity of pursuing research in this area.

Here, a wireless, battery-free, and wearable intelligent therapeutic aligner (WiB-ITA) was presented for site-matched monitoring, electronically gated on-demand therapy, and robust wireless operation for the closed-loop management of dental caries and periodontitis with a highly dynamic and spatially heterogeneous oral microecosystem. The WiB-ITA integrates three reproducible innovations within a unified, transparent, and conformal aligner. First, addressing oral compartmentalization, a butterfly-inspired dual-sided flexible electrode architecture was constructed. The enamel-facing side tracks acid-burst pH fluctuations, while the gingiva-facing side monitors inflammatory markers (e.g., nitrite, NO2−) associated with periodontal inflammation, enabling compartment-specific readout across micrometer-scale proximity. Second, an electrically addressable drug delivery interface allows electronically triggered, on-demand release of clinically relevant antimicrobials in a confined square millimeter–scale active area, to bridge intermittent diagnosis and responsive treatment. Third, to reconcile the competing requirements of milliwatt-level actuation, intraoral safety, and full-cavity accessibility, a hybrid two-hop wireless architecture pairs NFC with a Bluetooth low energy (BLE) relay reader. This two-hop link enhances bidirectional power and data transfer, reducing reliance on strict phone-to-mouth alignment and ensuring reliable operation across varying tooth positions in the confined oral cavity. Representative in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo validations confirm the platform’s stable multimarker tracking and electrotriggered antibacterial release, advancing intraoral wearables from single-condition applications to a versatile platform for chronic disease management.

RESULTS

System architecture and design of WiB-ITA

The multilayer stacked architecture of WiB-ITA system is presented in Fig. 1A. It consists of a butterfly-inspired dual-sided flexible integrated theranostic electrode array (iTEA), which integrates electrochemical sensors and electrically addressable drug delivery modules. In addition, the system includes a custom-designed miniaturized NFC-enabled control circuit on a flexible printed circuit board (NFC-FPCB), a transparent conformal oral aligner, a hybrid NFC-BLE two-hop relay reader, and a companion smartphone application (app) for data visualization and control, as detailed in fig. S1. This integrated architecture supports battery-free sensing and electronically triggered therapy, positioning WiB-ITA as a promising platform for wearable oral theranostics with site-matched monitoring and intervention (table S1). This configuration supports the closed-loop management strategy illustrated in Fig. 1B. In the WiB-ITA system, by continuously monitoring key oral biomarkers at distinct tooth sites, such as pH in cariogenic enamel niches and NO2− from subgingival periodontal inflammation, in a wireless, battery-free, and in situ manner, the system enables timely identification of pathological changes. When predefined intervention criteria are met, the onboard actuation interface triggers antimicrobial release with programmable timing and dose, with pH < 5.5 for caries and baseline-referenced NO2− elevation for periodontitis.

This in situ operation is supported by the NFC infrastructure, which provides energy supply, real-time sensor readout, and therapeutic control, as illustrated in Fig. 1C. Inductive coupling with a 13.56-MHz electromagnetic field powers the device, while the NFC-FPCB harvests and conditions the harvested energy into a stable voltage for battery-free operation and on-demand drug release. The microcontroller unit (MCU) coordinates electrochemical measurements, executes closed-loop decision logic, and drives the voltage-controlled drug-release routine to conduct localized biomarker monitoring and wirelessly transmit data to the mobile terminal. The companion smartphone app displays oral parameters in real time and communicates via a hybrid two-hop wireless link: An external BLE relay reader maintains reliable NFC coupling to the intraoral device and forwards data to the smartphone via BLE, while control commands are returned through the same relay path to gate therapy, completing the closed-loop feedback. In addition, the spatially resolved electrode array and the NFC control circuit are fabricated using flexible processing techniques (figs. S2 to S5), resulting in a device with a compact, dual-sided design, high mechanical compliance (Fig. 1D), and fully wireless and battery-free operation (Fig. 1E). The device also exhibits reliable wearability (Fig. 1, F and G, and fig. S6). As a result, fully closed-loop, intelligent comanagement of dental caries and periodontitis can be realized using only the WiB-ITA system and a smartphone.

Design and in vitro sensing performance of iTEA

To meet the need for spatially resolved sensing and therapy across adjacent oral disease sites, a butterfly-inspired dual-sided iTEA with distinct dorsal-ventral functional differentiation was developed to enable differential interaction with two juxtaposed microenvironments (Fig. 2A). It integrates caries- and periodontitis-targeted diagnostic-therapeutic units on opposite surfaces of a 25-μm-thick polyimide substrate, with a structure and dimensions precisely matched to dental anatomy. Figure 2B (a) illustrates the spatial integration of these diagnostic-therapeutic units across the two opposing surfaces through a layer-by-layer functionalization strategy. The iTEA was interface-engineered on the multielectrode array substrate to synergistically integrate high-fidelity biosensing with electroresponsive, drug-loaded therapeutic functionality. On the enamel-facing side, potentiometric pH sensing and targeted drug release address caries, whereas the periodontal-facing side incorporates amperometric NO2− detection and targeted drug release for periodontitis. This bioinspired design paradigm provides an effective solution for achieving spatially resolved theranostics in the complex oral environment.

To validate the oral microenvironment monitoring capabilities of the iTEA, we quantitatively evaluated the sensing performance to pH and NO2−. For pH monitoring, the working electrode 1 (WE1) of the enamel-facing sensing unit was selectively modified with iridium oxide nanotubes (IrOx NTs) [Fig. 2B (b)], while the Ag/AgCl reference electrode 1 (RE1) was coated with a polyvinyl butyral (PVB) composite solid-state electrolyte layer to ensure a stable potential reference by mitigating electrolyte fluctuation–induced potential drift at the RE interface. IrOx NTs were chosen as the pH-sensing material owing to their high proton exchange capacity and excellent biocompatibility (Fig. 2C) (35, 36). Fundamentally, the open-circuit potential arises from a proton-coupled electron-transfer equilibrium at the hydrated IrOx surface, primarily governed by the Ir3+/Ir4+ redox couple, resulting in a near-Nernstian dependence on hydrogen-ion activity. In addition, the three-dimensionally (3D) interconnected NT network fabricated via electrospinning provides structural robustness, enabling stable operation under the dynamic conditions of in vivo monitoring. Structurally, the IrOx NTs exhibit a hollow tubular morphology with an average diameter of 120.8 nm [Fig. 2B (b) and fig. S7], which synergistically increase the electrochemically active surface area and facilitate rapid proton transport across the electrode/electrolyte interface. Here, artificial saliva solutions with pH values ranging from 3.0 to 8.0 were prepared to simulate the acidification process from a healthy state (pH ~7.0) to enamel demineralization (pH < 5.5). The open-circuit potential response (Fig. 2D) exhibited a stepwise decrease during acidification and a fully reversible, stable shift during alkalization. The calibration curve (Fig. 2E) demonstrated good linearity (R2 = 0.9992) and a sensitivity of 57.5 mV pH−1, closely matching the theoretical Nernstian slope at 35°C (37). Selectivity tests (Fig. 2F) confirmed minimal interference from other electrolytes or metabolites, reflecting the specific hydrogen ion-selective effect of IrOx.

For the detection of NO2− in the periodontal microenvironment, gold nanoparticles (Au NPs) were uniformly modified on the periodontal-side WE3 via an electrochemical reduction method, providing a sensitive and catalytic interface for NO2− detection (fig. S8). The Au NPs exhibit a densely packed and quasispherical morphology with a typical particle size of 53.2 nm [Fig. 2B (c) and fig. S7], substantially enhancing the electroactive surface area and catalytic efficiency. To further assess the sensing performance, the flexible NO2− sensor was evaluated in artificial saliva using chronoamperometry (Fig. 2G). In this configuration, a constant anodic potential was applied to facilitate the electrochemical oxidation of NO2− to nitrate (NO3−) at the Au-modified WE, following the reaction: NO2− + H2O → NO3− + 2H+ + 2e−. The resulting faradaic current, generated by the two-electron transfer process, served as a quantitative indicator of NO2− concentration. Its measured current increased proportionally with rising NO2− concentrations, producing representative amperometric signals (Fig. 2H) and showing a detectable response at concentrations as low as 1 μM, indicative of high sensitivity. The amperometric response exhibited two distinct linear regimes (Fig. 2I). Specifically, the first spanned concentrations from 1 μM to 3 mM, yielding a high sensitivity of 118.2 μA mM−1 cm−2 with excellent linearity (R2 = 0.9881). The second regime extended from 3 to 20 mM with a sensitivity of 58.5 μA mM−1 cm−2. This dual-range calibration encompasses both physiological and pathological concentrations, rendering it suitable for in situ monitoring of inflammation progression. Selectivity tests confirmed excellent anti-interference capability, as 10-fold higher concentrations of common interfering species, including Na+, K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, urea, sucrose (Suc), glucose (Glu), and bovine serum albumin (BSA), produced negligible effects (Fig. 2J). Further characterization demonstrated good repeatability and reproducibility, sustained response stability over a 10-day evaluation period, and minimal readout variation under representative bending deformation for both the pH and NO2− detection (figs. S9 and S10).

Dental caries is a multifactorial infectious disease characterized by localized demineralization of dental hard tissues, primarily driven by the metabolic activity of cariogenic biofilms. Among the principal pathogens, Streptococcus mutans metabolizes fermentable carbohydrates (e.g., sucrose) to produce extracellular polysaccharides and organic acids. As illustrated in Fig. 2K, glucosyltransferases secreted by S. mutans catalyze the conversion of sucrose into adhesive glucans, which constitute the structural scaffold of the extracellular polysaccharides matrix, thereby facilitating biofilm accumulation and strengthening bacterial adhesion. Concurrently, lactic acid generated during glycolysis induces a marked local decrease in pH, eventually falling below the critical threshold for enamel demineralization (pH < 5.5). To capture these pH dynamics and validate the clinical applicability of the developed sensor, the enamel-side IrOx NT electrode was used to monitor the dynamic acidification of a simulated cariogenic microenvironment by coculturing S. mutans with sucrose in artificial saliva. The sensor provided continuous real-time readout over 24 hours, revealing a gradual pH decrease from ~7.4 as the acidogenic activity of S. mutans intensified (Fig. 2L). Notably, the pH dropped below the critical enamel demineralization threshold of 5.5 within 7 hours, closely reflecting the expected pathogenesis of dental caries. These results demonstrate that the sensor enables in situ, real-time monitoring of cariogenic pH changes, offering valuable potential for early detection and clinical management of dental caries.

Periodontitis arises from microbial dysbiosis and host immune responses, with reactive nitrogen species (RNS) driving oxidative/nitrosative stress and tissue damage. NO2−, a relatively stable RNS-related marker, reflects changes in nitric oxide (NO) metabolism and inflammation, formed when inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS)–catalyzed NO reacts with superoxide to produce peroxynitrite and ultimately contributes to NO2− accumulation (Fig. 2M), making it a widely used indicator of periodontal oxidative stress. Among immune cells, macrophages play a central role in periodontitis and strongly express iNOS under proinflammatory stimulation. Therefore, RAW 264.7 macrophages were selected as an in vitro inflammation model to mimic the periodontal microenvironment and evaluate the NO2− sensor performance. Cells were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide and l-arginine (l-Arg) to induce inflammatory activation and NO2− production. The amperometric curve showed a rapid current increase following l-Arg addition (Fig. 2N, green line), while coincubation with Nω-nitro-l-arginine methyl ester (l-NAME) (an NOS inhibitor) and l-Arg fully suppressed the response (Fig. 2N, blue line), confirming that the signal originated from NOS-dependent, macrophage-released NO2−. Control experiments without cells produced no signal, ruling out interference from l-Arg itself (Fig. 2N, black line). These results demonstrate that the iTEA can monitor NO2− dynamics in a simulated periodontal microenvironment, highlighting its potential for clinical monitoring of periodontal oxidative/nitrosative stress and evaluation of personalized therapies.

In vitro therapeutic evaluation of the iTEA

In addition to real-time monitoring of the oral microenvironment, the iTEA enables on-demand release of charged therapeutics via an intrinsically conducting polymer (ICP), thereby allowing precise electrochemically controlled treatment of dental caries and periodontitis. This system exploits the redox-mediated ion exchange properties of polypyrrole (PPy) doped with polystyrene sulfonate (PSS). Cationic model drugs relevant to caries and periodontitis therapy, chlorhexidine (CHX) and minocycline hydrochloride (MCH), were electrochemically loaded onto the surface of the therapeutic electrode [Fig. 3A (a)]. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of the modified electrode revealed a characteristic cauliflower-like morphology, confirming the successful deposition of PPy coating [Fig. 3A (b) and (c) and fig. S11]. Energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy elemental analysis further verified that the cauliflower-like features contained C, N, O, and Cl (fig. S11). In addition, Raman spectroscopy corroborated the successful incorporation of CHX and MCH within the PPy matrix (fig. S12).

The PPy-based system enables regulated drug loading and precise electroresponsive release under external electrical stimulation via the bipolar electrochemical delivery mechanism intrinsic to ICP (Fig. 3, B and C). Upon application of a negative potential to the WE, fixed anionic dopants within the PPy matrix create electrostatic binding sites that facilitate the incorporation of the cationic therapeutics (CHX+ and MCH+), achieving maximum loading capacities of 4.96 and 2.70 mg, respectively (Fig. 3, D and E). Conversely, when a positive potential is applied, PPy undergoes oxidation-associated protonation, triggering the expulsion of bound cations to maintain charge balance, thereby enabling electrically controlled drug release. To further evaluate the electroresponsive release performance, pulsed electrical stimulation was applied to the electrode. Both PPy/CHX and PPy/MCH systems exhibited pronounced on-demand release behavior, with electrically stimulated release rates exceeding those of passive diffusion (fig. S13). Moreover, the release profiles displayed a characteristic “on-off” switching pattern (Fig. 3, F and G), demonstrating the programmable and precisely controllable drug delivery capabilities of the iTEA.

To validate the therapeutic efficacy of the electroresponsive drug delivery module against dental caries and periodontitis, a multimodal antibacterial assessment was performed targeting the representative pathogens S. mutans and Porphyromonas gingivalis. As anticipated, electrically stimulated drug-loaded electrode (PPy/CHX w/ ES or PPy/MCH w/ ES) demonstrated potent antibiofilm activity against S. mutans, achieving >2-log reductions in colony-forming units (CFU) relative to the untreated control group (Fig. 3, H and I). Control experiments further revealed that the PPy electrode alone (PPy) and electrical stimulation alone (PPy w/ ES) produced no significant antibacterial effects, thereby excluding confounding contributions from the carrier material and electrochemical byproducts. Notably, the marked disparity in antibacterial efficacy between the electrically stimulated PPy/CHX group (PPy/CHX w/ ES) and the nonstimulated drug-loaded group (PPy/CHX w/o ES) confirms that therapeutic activity is governed by the electroresponsive release mechanism. Consistently, SEM microstructural analysis revealed pronounced morphological disruption in bacteria treated with PPy/CHX w/ ES group, including cellular deformation, surface corrugation, and membrane rupture (fig. S14). Parallel evaluation against P. gingivalis biofilm demonstrated comparable therapeutic performance, with CFU reductions exceeding 3 logs (Fig. 3, J and K).

Furthermore, crystal violet staining and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) were used to visualize alterations in biofilm biomass, bacterial viability, and structural thickness after different treatments (Fig. 3L). Quantitative crystal violet analysis and its corresponding statistical comparison across groups revealed that electrically stimulated drug-loaded group (PPy/Drug w/ ES) achieved biofilm disruption rates of ~75.85 and 79.68% against S. mutans and P. gingivalis, respectively (Fig. 3M). 3D CLSM reconstruction further demonstrated that the PPy/Drug w/ ES group exhibited the highest dead-to-live cell ratio within both biofilms, reaching 87.9 and 89.7% respectively, based on SYTO 9/propidium iodide (PI) fluorescence quantification. Correspondingly, biofilm thickness was markedly reduced, decreasing to 38.6 and 42.5% of the untreated control group for S. mutans and P. gingivalis, respectively (fig. S15). These results are consistent with the CFU enumeration and SEM morphological observations, collectively confirming that the iTEA enables on-demand drug release capable of effectively inhibiting and disrupting pathogenic biofilms. This electroresponsive therapeutic strategy, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 3N, therefore holds considerable promise for the clinical management of dental caries and periodontitis.

Integrated theranostic performance evaluation

To systematically evaluate the practical theranostic capabilities of the iTEA and validate its functionality under both controlled and physiological conditions, a complementary dual-model strategy was used. For detail, an ex vivo human cariogenic model (fig. S16) tested the device’s response to biofilm-induced pH drops of dental caries, while an in vivo rat periodontitis model (Fig. 4A) validated its therapeutic effect amid host immune-inflammatory activity. As expected, the iTEA demonstrated robust real-time monitoring capabilities. In the ex vivo cariogenic model, the pH sensor captured a gradual acidification as bacterial proliferation and metabolism progressed, with the control group dropping below the cariogenic threshold of 5.5 after 8 hours (Fig. 4B). In contrast, controlled CHX release in the PPy/Drug w/ ES group markedly slowed acidification, with the therapeutic effect correlating with drug concentration, which depends on the volume of gingival crevicular fluid (fig. S17). In the in vivo periodontitis model, NO2− levels initially rose from day 0 to day 10, reflecting active inflammation, and subsequently declined to baseline following electrically stimulated MCH drug release, demonstrating its dual antibacterial and anti-inflammatory efficacy (Fig. 4C). Across both models, Bland-Altman analysis confirmed the accuracy of the iTEA, showing strong agreement with conventional methods. The mean bias compared with a commercial pH meter was −0.0045 (95% limits: −0.0317 to 0.0227) (Fig. 4D), and with a commercial Griess reagent kit was 0.03514 μM (95% limits: −2.044 to 2.114) (Fig. 4E). The time-resolved pH and NO2− readouts were further rendered as 3D scatter maps to compactly capture the full temporal trajectories and highlight treatment-dependent separation of response patterns (Fig. 4, F and G). These results verify the device’s reliable, high-fidelity performance across both controlled and physiological conditions.

Furthermore, the early-warning capability of the iTEA was evaluated by monitoring pH changes in the ex vivo cariogenic model and NO2− levels in the rat periodontitis model before and after infection (Fig. 4, H and I). Despite the absence of visible changes in enamel slices or intraoral gingiva, the sensors detected clear signal variations, indicating early-stage infection and demonstrating the iTEA’s potential for preemptive disease detection. On-demand treatment was then applied (Fig. 4J); electrically stimulated drug delivery achieved over a 2-log CFU reduction compared with the untreated disease control group and restored healthy gingival morphology, likely by inhibiting biofilm formation and mitigating periodontal inflammation. S. mutans CFU assays showed a consistent trend. These therapeutic outcomes closely correlated with the posttreatment pH and NO2− readings (Fig. 4, B and C), confirming the iTEA’s integrated theranostic functionality and supporting its potential as a cost-effective solution for intelligent management of chronic oral diseases.

To further validate the therapeutic efficacy of the iTEA in an experimental periodontitis model, microcomputed tomography (micro-CT) was used to obtain 3D reconstructed images and 2D sectional views of the maxillary molars (Fig. 5A), followed by quantitative alveolar bone assessments (Fig. 5B). Compared to the healthy control group, the untreated periodontitis group exhibited pronounced periodontal bone loss, confirming successful establishment of the disease model. In contrast, the PPy/MCH w/ ES group exhibited the most significant preservation of alveolar bone architecture. The PPy/MCH w/o ES showed only modest therapeutic benefit, likely attributable to limited passive diffusion of MCH. Quantitative morphometric analyses, including bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb. Th), and trabecular separation (Tb. Sp), further substantiated these observations (Fig. 5, C to E).

To comprehensively elucidate therapeutic outcomes at both microstructural and molecular levels, a systematic histomorphological assessment was performed, including hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, Masson’s trichrome staining, and immunofluorescence analysis of proinflammatory cytokines. Pathogenic bacterial colonization in periodontitis is well recognized to activate host immune defense, thereby initiating an inflammatory cascade characterized by extensive immune cell recruitment. As illustrated in Fig. 5 (F and G), gingival tissue from the untreated periodontitis group exhibited significant inflammatory infiltration, predominantly composed of lymphocytes and neutrophils, indicative of an active host response to bacterial challenge. In contrast, tissues treated with the electroresponsive drug delivery group displayed markedly reduced inflammatory cell infiltration (P < 0.0001 versus periodontitis control), demonstrating effective local immunomodulatory capability.

Masson’s trichrome staining provided complementary histological evidence (Fig. 5, H and I), revealing extensive degradation of collagen fibers in the gingival tissue of the untreated periodontitis group, whereas the PPy/MCH w/ ES group preserved a well-organized collagen architecture comparable to that of healthy tissue. This protective effect indicates that the theranostic system effectively suppresses pathogenic activity, thereby attenuating the secretion of bacterial collagenases responsible for periodontal collagen degradation. In addition, dual-label immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated precise modulation of inflammatory mediators (Fig. 5, J to M). Treatment with PPy/MCH w/ ES group resulted in a significant down-regulation of the proinflammatory factor tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α), concomitant with up-regulation of the anti-inflammatory marker Arginase-1 (Arg-1), confirming the iTEA’s dynamic immunoregulatory capacity. In addition, as the iTEA is designed for wearable intraoral applications, its biosafety is of critical importance. Comprehensive biosafety evaluations were conducted spanning from in vitro to in vivo and from cellular to tissue levels. The results collectively confirm the favorable biosafety profile of the system, supported by multiday cytocompatibility assays (CCK-8, live/dead staining, and cytoskeletal morphology), thermal analysis, hematological evaluation (hemolysis and routine blood analysis), blood biochemical assays (liver and kidney function), and histopathological examination of major organs (figs. S18 to S24).

Wearable operation of preliminary human monitoring

Stable closed-loop operation within the constrained, saliva-rich, and highly dynamic oral environment necessitates the integration of a miniaturized, custom-designed wireless control module, which is essential for ensuring reliable and long-term functionality. While NFC technology is inherently suitable for such applications due to its low power consumption, secure data transfer, and compatibility with biocompatible materials, its practical intraoral deployment faces two major constraints. First, its short communication range restricts operation to close-proximity device pairing, limiting practical use in daily settings. Second, conventional NFC modules are generally too bulky or rigid for seamless integration into the confined spaces of dental aligners or oral appliances. To overcome these constraints, a miniaturized, adaptive NFC-FPCB module was designed and embedded as a low-profile component within a transparent WiB-ITA aligner. The module integrates the receiver coil and control circuitry into an ultrathin (0.5 mm), ultralight (88.5 mg) multilayer-stacked architecture, enabling conformal, low-profile integration while preserving mechanical robustness (Fig. 6, A and B). A dedicated relay reader with dual-mode wireless connectivity was engineered to enable robust intraoral operation. An enlarged, impedance-matched coil establishes a stable 13.56-MHz NFC link across oral soft tissues up to 18-mm separations. This design supports full-arch near-field inductive powering and bidirectional data exchange, achieved by sliding the relay reader along the buccal or palatal surface to align with a target tooth site (Fig. 6C). Feasibility tests at five representative tooth positions spanning the dental arch, including right molar, right premolar, anterior, left premolar, and left molar regions further showed stable real-time wireless readout of both pH and NO2− signals at all tested sites (fig. S25). The acquired signals are then relayed via BLE to a smartphone for long-range connectivity, real-time visualization, and external control. Key design advantages include copackaging of the coil and circuitry for an ultracompact form factor, stable power transfer under dynamic intraoral conditions, and decoupling near-field operation from smartphone proximity.

Electromagnetic performance of the WiB-ITA system was first evaluated by characterizing coupling between the relay reader and the NFC-FPCB receiving coil. Resonance-matching measurements confirmed tuning of the NFC link to 13.56 MHz, enabling efficient inductive power transfer at the intended operating frequency (Fig. 6D). Near-field coupling with the dedicated relay reader produced higher and more stable rectified voltages than direct smartphone powering. This extended the effective power and communication window to maintain the rectified output voltage above the minimum operating voltage of ~2.2 V from 4 to 18 mm, representing an approximately fourfold increase, thereby ensuring reliable sensing readout and command execution (Fig. 6E and movie S1). In addition, long-range usability was assessed via BLE relaying, which enabled stable wireless transmission up to 16.2 m, decoupling intraoral near-field powering from smartphone proximity and supporting flexible operation across multiple tooth positions (Fig. 6F and movie S2). Mechanical robustness was confirmed by resonance-frequency stability under bending, with the NFC-FPCB maintaining a nearly constant resonant frequency across aligner-induced curvatures (Fig. 6G and fig. S26)

To evaluate the long-term usability of WiB-ITA for continuous, in situ monitoring under daily life conditions, a three-day on-body study was conducted in a healthy volunteer. The study spanned typical activities, including breakfast, exercise, lunch, napping, beetroot juice intake, and office work (Fig. 6H). Across measurement days, local sulcular pH exhibited reproducible, event-associated transients around a stable baseline. These were characterized by postprandial acidification followed by gradual recovery during resting periods, with a more pronounced drop after a high-carbohydrate lunch. In contrast to the fast pH dynamics, NO2− remained relatively low and stable during most activities, yet displayed a characteristic delayed elevation following beetroot juice intake. Then, NO2− reached a higher afternoon plateau before gradually declining, consistent with enterosalivary processing of dietary NO3− and time-dependent enrichment/conversion to NO2− in the oral environment. A brief pH challenge using acidic cola (pH 2.5) and alkaline soda water (pH 8.0) also demonstrated rapid responsiveness to acute perturbations (fig. S27 and movie S3). Benchmarking against reference methods showed good agreement between WiB-ITA and a commercial pH meter as well as a standard Griess assay (R2 = 0.8464 and 0.6761, respectively; Fig. 6I), with concordant trends at matched sampling points (fig. S28). Systematic offsets in absolute values are anticipated, as reference assays measure whole-saliva averages, whereas WiB-ITA targets a localized sulcular microenvironment. This site-resolved readout underscores the necessity of position-matched monitoring. While whole-saliva averaging can mask transient, lesion-adjacent dynamics, WiB-ITA enables wearable, real-time parsing of both rapid (pH) and slower (NO2−) event-driven kinetics, thereby supporting long-term physiological tracking and on-demand intervention.

Furthermore, the wearable system was evaluated for real-time readout and initial cross-participant feasibility assessment in healthy, caries, and periodontitis participants by simultaneously and continuously monitoring oral pH and NO2− in situ under resting conditions (Fig. 6, J and K). Relative to the healthy participant, the caries participant exhibited a lower pH band, whereas the periodontitis participant showed elevated NO2− levels (Fig. 6L). Representative real-time traces further indicated stable plateaus with low drift for both analytes within individuals (Fig. 6, M to O), supporting event-driven monitoring and follow-up assessment using individualized baselines as references. These trends are consistent with prior reports that caries-associated oral microenvironments are more prone to acidification, whereas periodontitis is often accompanied by alterations in reactive nitrogen metabolism–related markers (e.g., NO2−) that correlate with inflammatory status (15, 16, 38, 39). Collectively, longitudinal daily-life monitoring and initial cross-participant feasibility assessment demonstrate that the as-designed WiB-ITA enables site-matched, multiplexed continuous readout and resolves distinct kinetics of fast (pH) versus slower (NO2−) variables under wearable operation. Building on these capabilities, Fig. 6P outlines a wearable-enabled, in situ theranostic workflow in which continuous monitoring with wireless connectivity and mobile visualization supports individualized baseline management and provides a translational route toward electronically gated, on-demand intervention and follow-up care.

DISCUSSION

Our results demonstrate that the wireless, battery-free, and wearable WiB-ITA enables spatially resolved, closed-loop comanagement of dental caries and periodontitis in the highly dynamic, space-constrained oral cavity, where synchronized multianalyte readout and on-demand intervention are difficult to achieve with fragmented, single-point sensors. WiB-ITA integrates a compact, butterfly-inspired dual-sided configuration (iTEA) that physically separates supragingival and subgingival interfaces, enabling concurrent, low–cross-talk monitoring of disease-relevant biomarkers within a single conformal device. This dual-sided architecture is motivated by the spatial heterogeneity of the oral microenvironment and the confined intraoral space. As the supragingival and subgingival compartments present distinct pathological cues, a conventional single-sided layout may limit site-matched sensing accuracy and increase the risk of spatial mismatch or signal confounding. Seamlessly coupled to this sensing architecture, an electronically gated drug-delivery module enables efficient, on-demand antimicrobial release under low-current electrical triggering, bridging the functional gap between diagnosis and therapy. These functions are supported by a relay-assisted wireless architecture with a miniaturized NFC-FPCB integrated into a transparent aligner and a dedicated relay reader, which maintains deterministic intraoral near-field powering while improving alignment tolerance and enabling longer-range interaction. Full-arch near-field accessibility is achieved by simply translating the relay reader along the buccal or palatal surface to align with a target tooth site. Consequently, the system provides system-level real-time telemetry and command delivery over ~16.2 m without strict close-contact coupling, reducing antenna-alignment constraints and obviating device removal for data retrieval. Together, these elements establish a practical route to compartment-specific, multiplexed intraoral theranostics with robust wireless usability in daily life.

The functionality and therapeutic capability of the WiB-ITA were validated through a multitiered assessment spanning in vitro, ex vivo, in vivo, and preliminary human evaluation. In vitro, the iTEA demonstrated highly selective and real-time monitoring of pH and NO2−, successfully tracking dynamic pH variations in a simulated cariogenic model and detecting NO2− from activated macrophages. The electrically triggered drug delivery module of the iTEA showed potent antibacterial activity against S. mutans and P. gingivalis, confirming the synergistic potential of real-time sensing and responsive therapy toward oral caries and periodontitis. The integrated theranostic capability was further validated using a complementary dual-model strategy. Specifically, an ex vivo human caries model verified the system’s ability to detect biofilm-induced acidification, while an in vivo rat periodontitis model validated its therapeutic effectiveness. In the animal model, micro-CT analysis revealed significant alveolar bone regeneration, while histological and molecular analyses confirmed substantial attenuation of local inflammation. Crucially, a preliminary human study confirmed that the wearable WiB-ITA could continuously and site-resolvedly track intraoral pH and NO2− dynamics during daily life, providing an initial feasibility demonstration in healthy, caries, and periodontitis participants. Benchmarking against reference methods showed overall agreement, supporting individualized baseline-guided, site-matched monitoring under practical wearable operation.

The significance of the WiB-ITA extends beyond a singular device. The selection of pH and NO2− is based on their relevance to the dominant pathological processes of the two target conditions. pH provides a direct and temporally responsive readout of local acidogenic activity in cariogenic biofilms, whereas NO2− serves as a relatively stable electrochemical surrogate of inflammatory RNS-related activity and is more practical for continuous monitoring than highly transient species such as NO itself. It represents a conceptual shift to a proactive and personalized, and data-driven oral health care. By facilitating continuous monitoring and timely, on-demand intervention, this platform addresses key limitations of conventional dental care, which relies on infrequent clinical visits and often fails to capture early pathological changes. Clinically, the WiB-ITA system could enable individualized risk stratification, early disease interception, and precise therapeutic modulation, particularly valuable for high-risk or difficult-to-monitor populations. In addition, as a research tool, it provides opportunities to study the in situ dynamics of the oral microbiome, inflammatory responses, and host-pathogen interactions with effectively enhanced temporal and spatial resolution. More broadly, the design principles demonstrated in this work, which spatially resolved sensing, closed-loop controlled therapy, and wireless battery-free operation, provide a versatile framework for developing next-generation theranostic systems applicable to other chronic diseases in complex biological environments.

Despite these advances, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, long-term stability and resistance to biofouling in the dynamic and biochemically complex salivary milieu remain to be fully characterized and will be essential for sustained clinical deployment. Second, although the aligner is custom-molded, its geometry may not consistently maintain optimal contact with soft tissues that undergo shape changes during disease progression, such as gingival swelling or recession in periodontitis. Third, the current fabrication process requires manual assembly of microfabricated electronics into the aligner, presenting challenges for large-scale manufacturing, cost control, and quality assurance. Last, the platform still lacks comprehensive clinical validation. In particular, the current biomarker thresholds have not yet been established within a unified clinical interpretive framework and will require validation in conjunction with standard clinical indices. Establishing clinically meaningful correlations between the monitored biomarkers and actual disease states will require large-scale clinical trials involving diverse patient populations. Such trials are essential to validate the system’s efficacy, reliability, and predictive accuracy, ultimately translating this technology into a data-driven tool that can genuinely improve patient care and treatment outcomes while alleviating the burden on health care providers.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials and reagents

Iridium(III) chloride hydrate (IrCl3·xH2O), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), PVB, lipopolysaccharide, l-Arg, and MCH were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA). Carbon conductive inks were commercially acquired from Jujo Chemical Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). Ag/AgCl REs were obtained from Shanghai Julong Electronic Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All materials were used as received without additional purification. Pyrrole monomer, chloroauric acid solution (HAuCl4), l-NAME, and chlorhexidine acetate were procured from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Sodium nitrite (NaNO2), poly(vinyl pyrrolidone) (PVP), PSS, Glu, BSA, urea, Suc, and other routine chemical reagents were purchased from Aladdin Industrial Corporation (Shanghai, China). All aqueous solutions were prepared using ultrapure water (18.3 MΩ·cm) obtained from a Milli-Q Advantage A10 water purification system (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) unless otherwise specified.

Design and fabrication of double-sided flexible electrode array

The double-sided flexible electrode array was designed using 3ds Max 2020 software (Autodesk, USA) and fabricated by FPCB technology. The WE, counter electrode (CE), and RE were screen-printed using conductive carbon and Ag/AgCl inks. Serpentine copper interconnects and contact pads were patterned on the PI substrate to enhance mechanical flexibility and to route electrical connections to the outer edge for interfacing with the NFC-FPCB.

Fabrication of the wireless, battery-free NFC-FPCB module

The NFC circuit was fabricated on a copper-clad PI substrate using standard FPCB processing and electrically insulated. Discrete components were assembled using low-temperature solder paste. The module integrated an NFC tag integrated circuit (IC) (ST25DV04KC; 4-Kbit EEPROM) for wireless communication and data storage. A low-power MCU (STM32L021F4U6TR) performed electrochemical signal acquisition using its 12-bit ADC (open-circuit potential for pH and chronoamperometry for NO2−) and generated programmable electrical pulses to drive the PPy-based drug-delivery module.

Preparation and in vitro characterization of biosensor module

pH electrode

IrOx NTs were drop-cast onto WE1 as the proton-sensitive layer. IrOx NTs were synthesized by electrospinning a precursor containing 0.25 g of IrCl3·xH2O, 0.4 g of PVP, and 2 ml of DMF, followed by annealing at 500°C for 3 hours to form NTs (40). The NTs were dispersed in deionized water (5 mg ml−1), and 5 μl was drop-cast onto the pretreated WE and dried under an infrared lamp.

Reference electrode

A solid-state electrolyte layer was formed by dissolving PVB (78 mg) in methanol (1 ml) and dispersing NaCl (50 mg) by sonication; 3 μl of the mixture was drop-cast onto the RE and dried overnight at room temperature.

NO2− electrode

Au NPs were electrodeposited onto the WE from 2 mM HAuCl4 in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at −0.2 V (versus Ag/AgCl) for 120 s, and the resulting electrode was used for chronoamperometric NO2− detection.

Sensing performance evaluation

Electrochemical performance (sensitivity, selectivity, reproducibility, and stability) was evaluated using a workstation (CHI 650E, Chenhua, China). For pH sensing, standard solutions (pH 3 to 8) were adjusted with 0.1 M HCl/NaOH, verified by a pH meter (Sartorius), and recorded by open-circuit potential. Selectivity was tested in artificial saliva (K+ 40 mM, Na+ 20 mM, Ca2+ 3 mM, Mg2+ 0.5 mM, and BSA 3 mg ml−1). For NO2− sensing, amperometry in PBS was performed with stepwise NO2− additions at 0.85 V (versus. Ag/AgCl); selectivity was evaluated against common salivary interferents (urea, Suc, Glu, and BSA).

In vitro real-time monitoring in bacterial cultures

S. mutans UA159 [American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) 700610; 1 × 107 CFU ml−1] was cultured in brain heart infusion (BHI) with 2% (w/v) Suc under anaerobic conditions (10% H2, 5% CO2, and 85% N2) at 37°C. pH was recorded every 30 min for 24 hours after two-point calibration (pH 4.0 and 7.0).

Electrochemical detection of macrophage-derived NO2−

RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (37°C, 5% CO2), stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (10 μg ml−1) and l-Arg (1 mM), and inhibited with l-NAME (0.5 mM). The NO2− sensor array was preconditioned in 0.1 M PBS for 30 min, and amperometric signals were recorded at 0.85 V (versus Ag/AgCl).

Fabrication and characterization of the drug delivery module

Fabrication and characterization of drug-loaded electrodes

Drug-loaded electrodes were prepared by galvanostatic electropolymerization of PPy/PSS on the electrode surface (0.2 M pyrrole and 0.05 M PSS; 2 mA cm−2), followed by electrochemical drug loading at −0.6 V (versus Ag/AgCl) for 30 min in CHX or MCH solution. Electrode morphology and composition were characterized by electron microscopy, energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy, and Raman spectroscopy. Drug loading and electrically triggered release were quantified by ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectroscopy (Shimadzu UV-3600PC) using calibration curves for CHX (254 nm) and MCH (350 nm); loading capacity was calculated from the drug concentration change in the loading bath. For release tests, drug-loaded electrodes were immersed in deionized water and stimulated with a programmed on/off electrical pattern, and aliquots were analyzed by UV-vis at predefined intervals.

CFU experiment

S. mutans (107 CFU ml−1) was treated as blank, PPy, PPy w/ ES, PPy/CHX w/o ES, and PPy/CHX w/ ES. Cultures were incubated in BHI at 37°C under anaerobic conditions (10% H2, 5% CO2, and 85% N2) for 48 hours. After incubation, suspensions were serially diluted (10−1 to 10−8), and 10 μl was plated on Columbia blood agar with 5% defibrinated sheep blood for anaerobic CFU enumeration. P. gingivalis ATCC 33277 was tested similarly in tryptic soy broth (TSB) supplemented with hemin and vitamin K, using PPy/MCH in place of PPy/CHX. Experiments were performed in triplicate.

SEM analysis of bacterial biofilms

Biofilms grown on coverslips were rinsed with PBS, fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (4°C, 2 hours), dehydrated through graded ethanol (30 to 100%), air-dried, sputter-coated with Au, and imaged by SEM.

Live/dead fluorescence staining and CLSM

Biofilms were stained with SYTO 9 (2.5 μM) and PI (2.5 μM) and imaged by CLSM (Olympus FV1000) with identical acquisition settings. Z-stacks were reconstructed and analyzed using ImageJ and FV10-ASW.

Animal studies

Male Wistar rats (~180 g) were used under protocols approved by the Animal Research Committee of Jilin University (2025 no. 423) and in accordance with national regulations. Experimental periodontitis was induced by molar ligation and bacterial inoculation. After model establishment, rats were randomized into treatment groups and received the corresponding treatments. For groups requiring electrical stimulation, a voltage of 0.65 V was applied for 5 min every 3 days, while electrochemical signal recording was performed according to the experimental design. After 15 days, antibacterial efficacy was assessed by plate counting, followed by euthanasia and collection of maxillary alveolar bone and gingival tissues. Hard-tissue outcomes were analyzed by micro-CT. Soft-tissue inflammation and remodeling were evaluated by H&E, Masson’s trichrome, and immunofluorescence staining.

Biosafety evaluation

Cytotoxicity

Electrode arrays were sterilized by 254-nm UV and incubated in culture medium (37°C, 5% CO2, 72 hours) to obtain extracts. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (3 × 103 cells per well, 24 hours) and then cultured with extracts (fresh medium as control). At 24, 72, and 120 hours, 10 μl CCK-8 was added (37°C, 1 to 2 hours), and optical density at 450 nm (OD450) was measured to determine viability.

Hemolysis assay

Fresh blood was collected into EDTA-K2 tubes and centrifuged (1500 rpm, 15 min), and red blood cells (RBCs) were washed with PBS and resuspended to 4% (v/v) in 0.9% saline. Electrode samples were extracted in 0.9% saline (37°C, overnight); 0.5% Triton X-100 and PBS served as positive and negative controls. RBC suspensions were mixed 1:1 with extracts and incubated (37°C, 2 hours), centrifuged (1500 rpm, 10 min), and OD540 was measured. Hemolysis (%) was calculated using the following formulaHemolysis rate(%)=ODsample−ODnegative controlODpositive control−ODnegative control×100%

In vivo biocompatibility evaluation

After the treatment period, animals were euthanized and major organs (heart, liver, lung, spleen, and kidney) were harvested, fixed in neutral buffered formalin, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with H&E for histopathology.

Wireless monitoring of oral microenvironment by WiB-ITA

The preliminary human wearable monitoring study was conducted under protocols approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Hospital of Stomatology, Jilin University [SJDKQ20(26036)] and in accordance with institutional guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the study. To assess the real-world usability of WiB-ITA for continuous monitoring, a healthy volunteer wore a transparent aligner–integrated device for 3 consecutive free-living days (9:00 a.m. to 6:00 p.m.). The device was fixed at a target tooth site adjacent to the gingival sulcus. Local pH and NO2− were recorded at 33 time points per day (60 s each) during routine activities (meals, exercise, nap, beetroot intake, and office work). In parallel, unstimulated whole saliva was collected at 19 predefined checkpoints and analyzed using a commercial pH meter and a Griess assay for NO2− to enable paired comparison.

In addition, to validate the clinically relevant monitoring performance of WiB-ITA, one participant each presenting healthy status, active caries, and periodontitis completed a 100-min seated session at the same gingival-sulcus–proximal site, with measurements every 10 min (11 time points, 60 s each) to obtain local pH and NO2− time-series data for exploratory analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in SPSS (v27.0.1), unless otherwise noted. Data are presented as means ± SD (n ≥ 3), unless otherwise specified. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t test, and multiple-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post hoc tests. For the human free-living monitoring study and the short-term seated monitoring sessions, data are presented as representative longitudinal time-series or exploratory proof-of-concept observations and were interpreted descriptively rather than by inferential between-group statistical testing. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001).
