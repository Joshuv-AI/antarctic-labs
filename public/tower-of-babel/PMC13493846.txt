Understanding attenuated positive facial expression in depressed women: central role of dorsal raphe network alterations and associated serotonin transporter methylation
Authors: Changjin Jung, Sunyoung Choi, Jieun Kim, In Chul Jung, Young Kyung Seo, Youngeun Choi, Sung Min Choi, Ojin Kwon, Ki-Sun Park, Yousang Jo, Bumwoo Park, Youngkyu Song, Jooyeon Kim, Gyunggoo Cho, Chaejoon Cheong, Hyungjun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnhedonia and loss of pleasure are core features of major depressive disorder, yet their neurobiological basis remains unclear. We examined dynamic facial responses to naturalistic emotional video stimuli in women with major depressive disorder and healthy controls using automated facial expression analysis. Functional MRI combined with tensor-independent component analysis identified brain networks associated with facial expressivity, and targeted DNA methylation analysis was also conducted. Women with depression showed significantly reduced positive facial expressions in response to joyful stimuli, whereas negative facial responses to sad stimuli did not differ between groups. Positive facial expression was associated with the dynamic brain response to emotional video clips measured by fMRI in the dorsal raphe network. We also found the higher temporal coupling between dorsal raphe and epithalamus/periaqueductal gray, and the higher methylation level of serotonin transporter gene in women with depression. Increased methylation levels of SLC6A4 were associated with the enhanced functional connectivity. These findings indicate that altered coordination within a dorsal raphe–centered network underlies attenuated positive facial expression in depressed women, and that this network connectivity is associated with serotonin transporter methylation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56615-1.Keywords: Major depression, Facial expression, fMRI, Naturalistic, Dorsal raphe, MethylationSubject terms: Diseases, Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Loss of pleasure, anhedonia, is the core symptoms of major depressive disorder1. These symptoms can be assessed through clinical interviews, self-report questionnaires, and behavioral observations. Ethological studies have demonstrated that patients with depression exhibit impaired nonverbal behavior (eye contact, hand gestures, and facial movements)2,3. It is assumed that mood and loss of pleasure can be inferred from behavioral observations such as facial expression assessment4. Although facial expression can be served as an objective marker for mood disorders, the common features of facial expression in major depressive disorder (MDD) and their neurobiological correlates have not been fully elucidated.

The serotonergic system, particularly the dorsal raphe nucleus (dRaphe), plays a critical role in modulating affective behavior and emotional expression5. The dRaphe projects to multiple regions implicated in emotion regulation, including the periaqueductal gray (PAG), a midbrain structure involved in vocal and facial expression of emotions, and the epithalamus, where the lateral habenula exerts inhibitory control over serotonergic and dopaminergic activity6,7. Dysfunction within this dRaphe–PAG/habenula network has been observed in both animal models and human studies of depression, contributing to disrupted affective signaling and anhedonia. Notably, tryptophan depletion—an experimental manipulation of central serotonin—has been shown to alter activity in both the dRaphe and habenula, suggesting this circuitry is sensitive to serotonergic modulation and may be disrupted in MDD8,9.

Recent research has also demonstrated that environmental stressors, including early-life adversity, can induce long-lasting epigenetic changes in genes regulating emotional and serotonergic function10. One such gene, the serotonin transporter gene (SLC6A4), is particularly sensitive to stress-related methylation changes that reduce transporter expression and impair serotonin reuptake. Elevated SLC6A4 promoter methylation has been linked to both depressive symptoms and altered brain responses to emotional stimuli11,12. Thus, epigenetic regulation of serotonergic function may contribute to abnormalities in brain circuits underlying emotional expression in MDD.

Conventional functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and behavioral experiments have adopted simple repetitive tasks with static stimuli to explore neural correlates and brain networks related to deficits in psychiatric illness. Although these studies with highly controlled tasks contribute to extending our understanding of the brain and behavior, there is also a growing interest in studies employing naturalistic paradigms with dynamic video clips and sounds13,14. As naturalistic stimuli resemble multimodal dynamics in real life, dynamic video stimuli can evoke higher brain responses than static pictures and localize the related brain network more robustly14,15. In the field of psychiatric neuroimaging, fMRI and behavioral experiments with dynamic stimuli and sound may provide a potential improvement in identifying the behavioral feature and neurobiological underpinnings.

In this study, we hypothesized that facial expressions in patients with depression would have different features from those in healthy controls (HCs), and we measured facial expression scores using emotional video clips. Subsequently, we employed fMRI and targeted DNA methylation profiling to investigate the neural and epigenetic correlates of facial expression in women with MDD and HCs. Our study may provide new insights into the neurobiological and epigenetic mechanisms underlying the altered facial expression in MDD, which may have important implications for understanding the pathophysiology of the depressive disorder.

Results

Positive facial expressions for joyful stimuli were reduced in patients with MDD

We used a webcam to record the facial expression responses of women with MDD and HCs to emotional stimuli. To boost facial expression changes, the naturalistic audio-visual stimuli were presented via a laptop computer screen and headphone. The stimuli consisted of 30-s blocks of emotional stimuli, followed by a 10-s fixation cross. Each 30-s block contained two or three clips obtained from comedy shows, newscasts, and YouTube. Two blocks of stimuli were included for each emotional valence category (joyful and sad; Supplementary Fig. 1). Participants rated how much they feel positive (i.e. joyful), or negative affect (i.e. sad, anxious, and disgusted) after the emotional stimuli. Participants reported they felt joyful for joyful stimuli, and sad for sad stimuli more than other affects (n = 112, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, all P < 0.0001; Supplementary Fig. 2). These results indicate that our task successfully induced appropriate affects for joyful and sad stimuli.

We investigated the characteristics of facial expressions in 66 patients with MDD and 46 HCs. To assess the positive or negative valence of facial expressions, we used iMotions 7.0 software (iMotions Inc., Boston, MA, USA), which provides inferences about emotional valence based on the Facial Action Coding System (FACS)16. A previous study demonstrated that specific emotional states, such as anger, disgust, or sadness, cannot be completely inferred from facial expressions and that humans sometimes feel a mixed emotional state17. Therefore, we classified facial expressions into two valences: positive and negative.

We compared positive facial expressions for joyful stimuli and negative expressions for sad stimuli between the groups. Women with MDD showed significantly lower levels of positive facial expressions for joyful stimuli than HCs (Mean ± SD, 0.59 ± 0.71 for MDD, 1.31 ± 0.56 for HC, Mann–Whitney U-test, U = 639.5, P < 0.0001, survived after Bonferroni correction, Fig. 1a). There was no significant difference in negative facial expressions for sad stimuli between patients with MDD and HCs (Mean ± SD, 0.58 ± 0.51 for MDD, 0.60 ± 0.51 for HC, Mann–Whitney U-test, U = 1 484, P = 0.84, Supplementary Fig. 3).

To address the potential confounding effect of age, which differed significantly between groups, we additionally performed an ANCOVA with age as a covariate for the group comparison of positive facial expression. The group factor remained highly significant (F = 12.29, P < 0.01), while age did not show a significant effect (F = 0.08, P = 0.78). Within-group Spearman correlations between age and positive facial expression were non-significant in both groups (MDD: ρ = −0.21, P = 0.24; HC: ρ = 0.11, P = 0.56), confirming that the group difference in positive facial expression was not attributable to age.

To test reliability of our task (parallel forms reliability), we recorded the facial response in subsample of participants under the two different naturalistic stimuli sets, which yielded the similar level of facial expressions (ICC (2,1) = 0.90 and 0.84 for positive facial expression for joyful stimuli, and negative facial expression for sad stimuli, respectively), indicating the participants showed the consistent level of positive/negative facial expressions across the different stimuli sets.

Subsequently, we compared the subjective rating for joyful and sad stimuli between groups. Women with MDD showed significantly lower rating than HCs, in both joyful feeling for joyful stimuli, and sad feeling for sad stimuli (joyful feeling [Mean ± SD], 4.16 ± 2.64 for MDD, 6.97 ± 1.66 for HC, Mann–Whitney U-test, U = 590.5, P < 0.0001, for joyful stimuli; sad feeling, 4.56 ± 2.61 for MDD, 5.77 ± 1.96 for HC, Mann–Whitney U-test, U = 1,076.0, P = 0.009, for sad stimuli, respectively).

To assess the extent to which facial expressivity tracked subjective emotional experience, we conducted Spearman correlation analyses between post-stimulus affect ratings and facial expression within each group. In controls, self-reported joy after joyful stimuli was marginally associated with positive facial expression (ρ = 0.28, P = 0.06), whereas no association was found for sad stimuli (ρ = 0.06, P = 0.69). In MDD, self-reported joy after joyful stimuli was strongly correlated with positive facial expression (ρ = 0.62, P < 0.0001), and a marginal association was observed for sad stimuli (ρ = 0.22, P = 0.07). Fisher’s r-to-z comparisons indicated that the joyful-condition correlation differed between groups (z = − 2.21, P = 0.027), but the sad-condition correlation did not (z = − 0.83, P = 0.41).

To further examine whether facial expression differences were related to clinical severity, we tested correlations between Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS) and Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) scores and both facial expression and subjective affect ratings. Neither HDRS nor BDI showed significant associations with facial expression measures or subjective ratings in either group (all P’s > 0.21), except for a marginal association between HDRS and joyful subjective rating in the MDD group (P = 0.10). These results indicate that attenuated positive facial expression was not simply driven by overall depression severity.

Coordinated processes in the diencephalon and mesencephalon showed different relationships with positive facial expressions in patients with MDD and HCs

We then investigated the neural correlates of facial expressions and related emotional experience. We performed fMRI scans with naturalistic audiovisual stimuli in patients with MDD (n = 32) and HCs (n = 32). It is difficult to use conventional fMRI methods related to repetitive static stimuli to decode the neural correlates of dynamic audiovisual stimuli. Thus, we adopted a data-driven approach and conducted multivariate analyses using tensor-independent component analysis (tICA)18 to extract functional brain networks driven by naturalistic dynamic stimuli19. The independent component (IC)-wise correlation with facial expressions allowed us to elucidate the specific neural circuitry related to altered facial expressions and emotional experience in MDD.

Given the differential responses in positive facial expressions to joyful stimuli between patients with MDD and HCs, we identified brain regions that demonstrated a significantly different association with positive facial expression between these two groups. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) showed a significant interaction effect on positive facial expressions for joyful stimuli between the estimated score of the brain region (53rd IC of the tICA) and the group (MDD or HC, Fig. 1b, P < 0.05, after Bonferroni correction for testing 55 ICs). Age was additionally included as a covariate in the ANCOVA, and the interaction effect remained significant at the same 53rd IC at 55-dimensionality (P = 0.05 after Bonferroni correction). As a post-hoc analysis, we conducted correlational analyses between the scores of the 53rd IC and positive facial expressions in each group. The estimated score of the 53rd IC was positively correlated with positive facial expressions for the joyful context in HCs (Spearman correlation, n = 31, ρ = 0.42, P = 0.02), whereas, there was a trend of inverse correlation between the 53rd IC score and positive facial expressions for the joyful context in MDD (Spearman correlation, n = 32, ρ = -0.34, P = 0.05). The spatial map of the 53rd IC demonstrated coordinated processes in the diencephalon and mesencephalon, including activity in the dRaphe, hypothalamus and thalamus, and anti-correlated activity in the PAG and epithalamus covering the habenula20 (Fig. 1c-g).

Functional connectivity between dRaphe and PAG/epithalamus in response to joyful stimuli was higher in patients with MDD compared to HCs

Using the data-driven tICA approach, our study identified the brain networks involved in joyful facial expression and related emotional experience. To elucidate the underlying mechanism contributing to the differential associations of the 53rd IC with positive facial expressions between groups, we examined functional connectivity among brain regions within its spatial map during emotional stimuli, along with group differences therein. For timeseries analysis, regions of interest were defined by assessing cluster significance in the spatial map, yielding three clusters: dRaphe (center voxel Montreal Neurological Institute [MNI] coordinates: 2, -32, -10 mm; size: 18 voxels), hypothalamus/thalamus (center voxel MNI coordinates: 2, -8, -4 mm; size: 702 voxels), and the PAG/epithalamus (center voxel MNI coordinates: 2, -24, -2 mm; size: 78 voxels).

We found that the functional connectivity between the clusters corresponding to the dRaphe and the PAG/epithalamus was significantly higher in MDD compared to HC during joyful stimuli (Mean ± SD [beta values in the regression model], 0.21 ± 0.23 for MDD [n = 32], 0.06 ± 0.22 for HC [n = 31], Mann–Whitney U-test, U = 689, P = 0.008, survived after Bonferroni correction, Fig. 2a). The functional connectivity of hypothalamus/thalamus with other two regions did not show significant difference between groups (P > 0.05). Moreover, only MDD group exhibited the significant positive functional connectivity between dRaphe and PAG/epithalamus (MDD: Wilcoxon signed-rank test, V = 483, P < 0.0001), which was an altered relationship relative to the contrasted contributions of these two clusters to the map of 53rd IC. HC group did not showed positive functional connectivity (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, V = 329, P = 0.12).

Differences in functional connectivity (FC) between dRaphe and PAG/epithalamus between groups, and associations of these FC alterations with SLC6A4 DNA methylation and positive facial expressions in response to joyful stimuli. (a) FC between dRaphe and PAG/epithalamus was significantly different between MDD and HC only in response to joyful stimuli (P < 0.001). The dRaphe-to-PAG/epithalamus FC was significantly higher in MDD compared to HC for joyful stimuli. There were no significant differences of FC between these regions for stimuli in response to sad stimuli (P = 0.06). (b) DNA methylation of the SLC6A4 promoter. The SLC6A4 DNA methylation in patients with MDD was higher than that in HCs (P = 0.037). (c) The SLC6A4 methylation exhibited positive correlations with FC between dRaphe and PAG/epithalamus for joyful stimuli in both MDD (ρ = 0.41, P = 0.04, marginally significant after Bonferroni correction) and HC (ρ = 0.68, P < 0.001, significant after Bonferroni correction).

The dRaphe-to-PAG/epithalamus functional connectivity did not differ significantly between MDD and HC for sad stimuli (Mean ± SD, 0.17 ± 0.34 for MDD, 0.02 ± 0.24 for HC, Mann–Whitney U-test, U = 633, P = 0.06, did not survived after Bonferroni correction) (Fig. 2a). In addition, there was no significant difference in functional connectivity of the hypothalamus/thalamus cluster with others between groups under sad stimuli (Mann–Whitney U-test, all P > 0.2).

As a sensitivity analysis to verify the robustness of the tICA findings, we conducted a whole-brain psychophysiological interaction (PPI) analysis using the dRaphe cluster as a seed region (physiological regressor) and joyful stimuli as the psychological regressor (FSL_FEAT first-level PPI). In HC, the positive facial expressions during joyful stimuli positively correlated with PPI connectivity to the hypothalamus and anti-correlated with PPI connectivity to the epithalamus (Mixed-effect, thresholded at z < 1.96), regions overlapping with the spatial map of the 53rd IC. In contrast, in MDD, they showed only anti-correlations with PPI connectivity to the PAG (Mixed-effect, thresholded at z < 1.96), consistent with the altered dRaphe network connectivity pattern identified by tICA. These converging findings from an independent analytic approach support the robustness of the tICA-based result.

DNA methylation of the SLC6A4 promoter and its association with functional connectivity between dRaphe and PAG/epithalamus during joyful stimuli

Environmental stress can lead to epigenetic changes, which induce gene expression and contribute to the pathophysiology of depression. We performed targeted DNA methylation analysis of the SLC6A4 promoter (Supplementary Fig. 4), a known epigenetic regulator of serotonergic signaling. Based on prior reports of blood-brain concordance in DNA methylation patterns21,22, we examined the methylation status in a surrogate tissue and localized the coherent brain regions that interact with the dorsal Raphe nucleus during emotional experiences.

Firstly, we compared the methylation status between groups, and found a significantly higher SLC6A4 methylation in patients with MDD than in HCs (Mean ± SD, 3.40 ± 0.90% for MDD, n = 72, 3.06 ± 0.85% for HC, n = 44, Mann–Whitney U-test, U = 1,951, P = 0.037, Fig. 2b). Moreover, we found a significant positive correlation between SLC6A4 methylation and dRaphe-to-PAG/epithalamus functional connectivity during joyful stimuli in only HC (n = 25, ρ = 0.68, P < 0.001, survived after Bonferroni correction, Spearman’s correlation analysis; Fig. 2c), indicating that higher SLC6A4 methylation was associated with enhanced positive temporal coupling between dRaphe and PAG/epithalamus in response to joyful stimuli. In MDD, the correlation between SLC6A4 methylation and dRaphe-to-PAG/epithalamus functional connectivity was marginally significant (n = 25, ρ = 0.41, P = 0.04, did not survive after Bonferroni correction).

No significant correlation was found between DNA methylation of SLC6A4 and the depressive ratings such as BDI (ρ = 0.09, P = 0.51 in MDD; ρ = 0.04, P = 0.80 in HC), or HDRS (ρ = 0.08, P = 0.57 in MDD; ρ = -0.11, P = 0.50 in HC).

As the ages were significantly different between the groups (patients with MDD, n = 66, 29.8 ± 10.9 years [mean ± SD]; HCs n = 46, 24.1 ± 5.3 years, Mann–Whitney U test, P = 0.04), we re-compared the facial expression scores and SLC6A4 methylation using the age-matched groups, and tested the interaction effect on the positive facial expression for joyful stimuli between 53rd IC of tICA and the group factor for confirmation purposes, which showed the similar results (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Discussions

We found attenuated positive facial expressions under joyful stimuli in women with depression, which is consistent with previous results2,23. Previous ethological studies have typically recorded facial behavior in clinical interviews or hospital wards2,23, whereby the alteration of facial expression was assessed during clinical observation. However, the clinical interview setting was difficult to standardize, and the assessment process was labor-intensive. Our study evoked participants’ facial expressions using emotional video clips and extracted positive expression scores using automated software. This approach allowed us to analyze dynamic facial responses with high time resolution for a moderate sample size of participants (n = 112).

Facial expression is known to reflect one’s emotional state and mood and can transmit one’s intentions in social communication16,24. In the current study, we used naturalistic video clips to evoke facial responses, and the participants were left alone on face recording or brain scanning. As live human interactions were excluded from the face recordings, social intention might have contributed only a small part to shaping facial behavior. In the current study, the facial response to joyful stimuli provided information on emotional state and mood and served as a proxy for core symptoms, such as loss of pleasure, anhedonia.

The pattern of facial expression–emotion coupling across groups warrants further discussion. In MDD, positive facial expression was more strongly coupled to self-reported joyful experience than in controls, despite overall reductions in both facial expression and subjective joy. This may indicate that when positive affect does occur in depressed individuals, it is expressed facially in a relatively intact manner. Accordingly, the attenuation of positive facial expression in MDD may primarily reflect reduced frequency or intensity of positive affective experience—consistent with anhedonia—rather than a blunting of expressive capacity per se. In support of this interpretation, neither facial expression nor subjective ratings showed significant associations with overall symptom severity (HDRS or BDI), suggesting that attenuated positive facial expression reflects a specific aspect of positive affective processing that is partially dissociable from global clinical severity.

In general, depressed patients are expected to experience sadness frequently. If the depressed mood in MDD potentiated their sad affect, these patients would bear greater levels of sad affect compared to healthy participants. Conversely, if patients with MDD exhibited reduced interest in their environment, they would bear lower levels of sad affect than HC25. The latter is the case in the current study, where patients reported experiencing less sadness in response to sad stimuli than HCs. This finding suggests that depression may diminish motivational activity and promote disengagement from the environment.

Similarly, patients with MDD reported experiencing less joyfulness in response to joyful stimuli. We also found the attenuated positive facial expressions in MDD comparing HC under joyful stimuli, which suggests that neural circuitry associated with MDD may affect the emotional experience and facial expression in positive emotional states. The brain network related to positive facial expressions demonstrated activity in the dRaphe and hypothalamus and anticorrelated activity in the epithalamus covering the habenula20 and PAG. This network in women with depression demonstrated a different relationship with positive facial expressions than the network in HCs. Only HCs were positively correlated with positive facial expressions, indicating MDD patients had a different functional connectivity in the circuitry related to emotional experience, including the dRaphe, hypothalamus, epithalamus, and PAG, compared to HC.

The diencephalon and mesencephalon and their roles in depression have been extensively studied. Regarding neural correlates of facial expression, the PAG is known as a “laughter-coordinating center,” cooperating with the frontotemporal lobe, hypothalamus, and raphe nucleus7,26. In rodents, ultrasonic vocalizations express an appetitive or aversive state and can be used for communicative purposes27. Ultrasonic vocalization can be controlled by neural activation in the PAG28. Serotonergic activation in the dorsal PAG is influenced by the dRaphe and the hypothalamus, which modulate appetitive or defensive behavior7,29,30. In previous case studies, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors show effectiveness in treating of pathological laughter and crying31. Taken together, previous studies suggest that serotonergic regulation of emotional experiences and networking among the dRaphe, PAG, hypothalamus, and epithalamus may contribute to shaping positive facial expressions, which was in line with our findings.

Lateral habenula has extensive connections with the raphe nucleus, and has a central role in the pathophysiology of depression. Dysfunction in lateral habenula may influence the motivational processing and emotional experience32. In animal models of depression, the lateral habenula shows hyperactivity, and the suppression of habenular activity induces antidepressant effects33. In human studies, tryptophan depletion in depressed patients induced depressive mood and the brain regional activation in dRaphe and lateral habenula8,9. Our finding that the enhanced functional connectivity between dRaphe and the PAG/epithalamus in MDD was in line with the previous studies. This highly-connected functional network between the dRaphe and habenula in MDD may contribute to the abnormal encoding of emotional valence and finally modulate the facial expression response to joyful video clips.

Environmental factors, such as early-life stress and adverse experiences, may lead to increased methylation of the SLC6A4 promoter region, resulting in decreased expression of the serotonin transporter34. Thus, we attempted to elucidate the underlying mechanism of the altered dRaphe network connectivity in MDD using DNA methylation in the surrogate tissue (i.e., blood sample) based on blood–brain epigenetic concordances21,22. In both MDD and HCs, we found that the dRaphe connectivity to epithalamus and PAG under joyful states were correlated with SLC6A4 methylation. Previous epigenetic studies have demonstrated that changes in SLC6A4 methylation can influence in vivo serotonin transporter expression and may have some relevance to behavioral phenotype35,36. Our findings suggest that dynamic communication between the dRaphe, epithalamus, and PAG under joyful stimuli may be affected by the serotonergic system and linked to positive facial expressions. Moreover, we observed higher SLC6A4 methylation and dynamic coupling between the dRaphe, and PAG for joyful stimuli in patients with MDD, suggesting that functional disruption of the dRaphe network in MDD might be associated with dysregulation of the serotonergic pathway.

Our study has some limitations. Firstly, we focused on an all-female cohort, which removed potential confounds of sex differences in emotional expressivity. Previous studies indicate that women generally exhibit greater facial expressivity for positive emotions compared to men37,38, and sex differences have also been reported in serotonergic function and fronto-limbic circuitry. Therefore, restricting the sample to women allowed us to investigate dorsal raphe network alterations within a more biologically homogeneous group. However, this design limits the generalizability of our findings to men. It is possible that sex-dependent differences in serotonergic modulation and emotional expression may alter the observed associations between dorsal raphe connectivity, facial expressivity, and SLC6A4 methylation. Future studies directly comparing men and women are needed to determine whether the identified network–behavior–epigenetic relationships are sex-specific. Although exogenous hormone use was rare in the present sample, ovarian hormone fluctuations may influence serotonergic circuitry and affective regulation. Future studies incorporating menstrual cycle phase, menopausal status, and circulating hormone levels are needed. Secondly, our investigation on the epigenetic linkage to brain response was conducted in a subdivision of our sample, which limits the statistical power. In the epigenetic analysis, we conducted targeted DNA methylation of the SLC6A4 promoter in blood samples. Since DNA methylation and epigenetic modifications are tissue-specific, brain tissue is the ideal sample for investigating the epigenetic landscape. However, directly measuring methylation in the brain is invasive and carries significant risks. To avoid such risks, surrogate tissues (blood, saliva, and buccal tissue) are widely used for methylation analyses in brain studies, and recent studies have statistically demonstrated the blood–brain epigenetic concordance. They found that 19% of the CpG sites in the genome-wide methylation array were correlated between brain and blood. An intriguing point is that the SLC6A4 methylation site in this study (CpG4) is proximal to the known brain–blood co-methylation site (> 50 bp from cg10901968). This suggests that SLC6A4 methylation in the brain may be similar to that in the blood, and a similar epigenetic mechanism can be expected. Human positron emission tomography studies support blood–brain concordance by showing that central in vivo serotonin synthesis is associated with greater SLC6A4 methylation in the blood39. Thirdly, there was a significant age difference between the patient and control groups. However, the group analyses using age-matched participants produced consistent results with the full groups (See Supplementary Fig. 5), indicating that age did not substantially influence our findings. Fourthly, given the small size and deep brainstem location of the dorsal raphe nucleus, activation detected using 3T fMRI may be influenced by spatial resolution limitations and partial volume effects. Although 3T fMRI studies have reported meaningful results using dRaphe ROIs40–42, it should be interpreted with caution.

In conclusion, we demonstrated attenuated positive facial expressions under joyful stimuli in patients with MDD compared with HCs. The data-driven tICA approach showed that positive facial expressions were associated with the dynamic fMRI response of the dRaphe, hypothalamus, epithalamus, and PAG to emotional video clips. Epigenetic changes in the serotonin transporter were linked to the altered functional connectivity between the dorsal raphe and the PAG/epithalamus, which was hyperconnected in patients with MDD. These findings indicate that serotonergic dysfunction, shaped by epigenetic regulation, may contribute to altered temporal coordination within dorsal raphe–associated circuits and could be related to attenuated positive facial expressions in depressed women.

Materials and methods

Participants

We enrolled 81 women who satisfied the DSM-IV criteria for unipolar MDD and 52 healthy women via local bulletin boards and online advertisements (See Supplementary Table 1 for demographic and clinical characteristics of participants and Supplementary Fig. 6 for participants’ flow diagram). All participants were between 19 and 65 years old and had not used any psychotropic medications within the previous 4 weeks. Of all participants, only one patient had a lifetime history of psychotropic medication. The inclusion criteria for the MDD group were a current diagnosis of MDD and a minimum total score of 14 on the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS). In the HC group, inclusion required a maximum total score of seven on the 17-item HDRS. The exclusion criteria were attempted suicide in the past year or current suicide risk; lifetime diagnoses of bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, schizoaffective or other psychotic disorders; history of alcohol or other substance abuse; pregnancy or breastfeeding; clinically significant liver disease; evidence of untreated or unstable thyroid disorder; brains with structural abnormalities; and clinically significant abnormal findings on physical examinations, routine laboratory tests, and electrocardiography. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Korea Institute of Oriental Medicine (I–1711/001–001–01), Daejeon Korean Medicine Hospital of Daejeon (DJDSKH–16–BM–17), and the Korea National Institute for Bioethics Policy (P01–201609–11–002), and conducted from October 2017 to May 2019. All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committees and with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was registered in the Clinical Research Information Service, established by the Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and embodied as a part of the Primary Registries in the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (KCT0002532 and KCT0002747, https://cris.nih.go.kr).

Assessment of facial expressions induced by emotional valence

The faces of all participants were recorded using a webcam at 30 Hz during naturalistic audiovisual stimuli with emotional valence. Naturalistic audiovisual stimuli were presented via computer screens and headphones. The screen size was 34.0 · 20.3 cm2 with visual angles of 37.6° horizontally and 22.6° vertically. The audiovisual stimuli consisted of 30-s blocks of emotional stimuli with positive (joyful and appetite-inducing), neutral, or negative valence (sad and anxiety-inducing), followed by a 10-s fixation cross. Each 30-s block contained 2–3 clips obtained from comedy shows, newscasts, and YouTube. Two blocks of stimuli were included for each emotional valence category (joyful, sad, appetite-inducing, anxiety-inducing, and neutral; Supplementary Fig. 1). Thus, 10 stimulus blocks were presented to each participant in a pseudo-randomized order. During the recording, the participants were left alone and asked to feel and express their emotions freely. After recording, participants rated how much they felt joyful, sad, disgusted, and fearful/anxious on a 10-point scale from 0 to 9 using E-Prime 3.0 software (Psychology Software Tools, PA, USA). Participants rated joyful feelings higher than other feelings for joyful stimuli (paired t-test, P < 0.001 with Bonferroni correction; Supplementary Fig. 2). For negative emotional valences, sad stimuli evoked higher scores for sad feelings than others (paired t-tests, P < 0.001 with Bonferroni correction; Supplementary Fig. 2). As the cardinal symptom of MDD is the loss of pleasure and depressed mood, we focused on joyful and sad expressions in the following analyses.

Facial expressions were analyzed using iMotions 7.0 software (iMotions Inc., MA, USA), where facial action units were extracted based on the Facial Action Coding System43 and mapped onto seven emotional expressions (joy, fear, disgust, sadness, anger, surprise, and contempt). As a specific emotional state, such as anger, disgust, or sadness, cannot be completely inferred from facial movements, and human sometimes feel a mixed emotional state17, we classified facial expressions into two valences: positive and negative. The positive facial expression value in each frame was defined as the maximum value of iMotions’ joy and smile values. The negative value was defined as the maximum value of iMotions’ sadness, fear, anger, contempt, and disgust value. For each participant, positive facial expression levels were measured using the mean of positive facial expression values under joyful stimuli (30 s × 2 times × 30 frames). Negative facial expression levels were measured under sad stimuli. In order to remove skewness, we performed a logarithmic transformation on the positive/negative facial expression level. To test the inter-rater reliability, we used 80 pictures of faces with positive or negative facial expression values obtained from iMotions 7.0 software. An experienced rater (S.C.) assessed the positive (joyful) or negative (sad, fearful/anxious, and disgusted) facial expression scores (0 to 4) for over 80 pictures based on the facial action coding system43. The negative facial expression score of the human rater was defined as the maximum value for sadness, fear/anxiety, and disgust. The inter-rater reliability between iMotions and the human rater showed good reliability (ICC (2,1) = 0.96 and 0.85 for positive and negative facial expressions, respectively).

In the assessment of facial expressions, 21 participants were excluded because of issues in measurement of the facial expressions (low accuracy of facial landmark detection, low accuracy of facial action unit detection, covering the face, and frequent bowing of their head or being drowsy). Thus, we analyzed facial expressions in 112 participants, with 66 patients with MDD (29.8 ± 10.9 years [mean ± SD]) and 46 HCs (24.1 ± 5.3 years [mean ± SD]). Of the 112 participants, 32 patients with MDD (31.1 ± 10.3 years [mean ± SD]) and 32 HCs (23.7 ± 3.9 years [mean ± SD]) agreed to complete the task-fMRI for the naturalistic audio-visual stimuli.

MRI acquisition and preprocessing

We completed MRI scans using a 3.0-T Philips Achieva scanner (Philips Medical Systems, Best, The Netherlands) with a 32-channel head coil at the Korea Basic Science Institute. T1-weighted anatomical images were acquired using a three-dimensional TFE pulse sequence (TR/TE = 6.7/3.1 ms, flip angle = 9°, voxel size = 1 · 1 · 1 ). Whole-brain blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) images were acquired using a T2*-weighted echo-planar sequence (TR/TE = 2000/35 ms, voxel size = 3 · 3 · 3.5 with a 0.5- mm slice gap). BOLD scans were conducted with the naturalistic audiovisual stimuli (task-fMRI) for 430 s. We used different audiovisual stimuli in the fMRI scans from the stimuli used in the face recordings. Pre-processing for MRI was completed using the analysis packages FSL (FMRIB Software Library)44, FreeSurfer45, and CONN toolbox46,47. The cortical surface in the T1-weighted images was reconstructed for anatomical registration using FreeSurfer recon-all. For BOLD images, head motions along the time points were corrected using FSL-MCFLIRT, and regions of the skull were removed using FSL-BET. Co-registration between the anatomical and BOLD images was completed using bbregister of FreeSurfer, and BOLD images were spatially normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space using FSL-FNIRT (voxel size = 2 × 2 × 2 mm3). Artifacts for head motion and physiological noise were removed by regressing the six translation/rotation motion parameters (from FSL-MCFLIRT), motion-outlier confounding matrices (from FSL_motion-outliers), and four principal components and average of white matter and CSF (CONN toolbox). Spatial smoothing with a 5-mm full-width-at-half-maximum Gaussian kernel and temporal high-pass filtering with a 0.001-Hz cut-off frequency were completed.

Tensor-independent component analysis (tICA) and IC-wise assessments

For decoding the neural correlates of dynamic audio-visual stimuli, it is difficult to use the conventional fMRI analysis method related to repetitive static stimuli. Thus, the BOLD responses to naturalistic stimuli were assessed using a data-driven approach with tICA18. Whole-brain BOLD images from the task-fMRI of 32 patients with MDD and 32 HCs were merged into a three-dimensional matrix (space · time · subject), and tICA was performed using FSL_MELODIC. The merged matrix was separated into multiple ICs corresponding to coordinated processes for emotional stimuli. tICA produces three matrices for each IC: spatial maps (i.e., spatial domain), the time course of each participant (i.e., time course domain), and accuracy estimations of each participant (i.e., subject domain, Supplementary Fig. 7). The spatial map of each IC represents the coordination of multiple brain regions in task-specific or task-nonspecific processes. The estimated score in subject domain of an IC represented how much the individual participant contributed to the spatial map of the IC (i.e., accuracy estimations of each participant) and was used to assess the effects of ICs on each participant. Since we hypothesized that reduced positive facial expression in MDD would be relevant to the dysfunction of a specific neural circuitry, an altered relationship between brain regions and positive facial expression in MDD was investigated. Thus, we assessed the interaction effect between group factors (patients with MDD or HC) and positive facial expressions in an ANCOVA using a dependent variable for the estimated scores of each IC. The interaction effect represented a group difference in IC correlations with facial expressions. Subsequently, we identified the optimal IC dimensionality of tICA (i.e., the parameter for the number of ICs in tICA) that produced an IC representing the most relevant coordinated process (i.e., a spatial map) for the interaction effect. We performed independent tICAs by increasing the IC dimensionality from 2 to 100. The maximum statistical power for the interaction effects was calculated for separate ICs according to different IC dimensionalities (Supplementary Fig. 8). We found optimized IC separation at 55-dimensionality, which showed a significant interaction effect with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons of the number of ICs (all participants for fMRI [patients with MDD, n = 32; HCs, n = 31]; P < 0.05; age-matched participants for fMRI [patients with MDD, n = 26; HCs, n = 26]; P < 0.05). The estimated score in the participant domain of the ICs showed one outlier (Grubb’s test with P < 0.05), and the outlier in HCs was excluded from ANCOVA and further analyses.

The spatial map of each IC was thresholded using a mixture model and an alternative hypothesis testing approach with a threshold level of 0.5 (FSL_MELODIC). The significance of clusters in the threshold map was assessed using Gaussian random field theory with P < 0.05 (FSL_Cluster). We also calculated the mean time-series responses of the IC to naturalistic stimuli using the time course matrix from the tICA. The time course of each participant was scaled to a mean of 0 and a unit standard deviation for the entire period of naturalistic stimuli. The averaged time-series response for patients with MDD and HCs represented fluctuations in the IC under naturalistic dynamic stimuli with positive and negative emotional valence.

DNA methylation analyses based on quantitative bisulfite pyrosequencing

Whole-blood samples were collected from 116 participants for DNA methylation analysis (patients with MDD, n = 72; HCs, n = 44). Among the 116 participants, an fMRI scan was conducted for 50 participants (patients with MDD: n = 25, HCs: n = 25). To investigate DNA methylation levels in the target region, we analyzed the SLC6A4 promoter between − 471 and − 421 base pairs of the transcriptional start site (+ 1), which contains five CpG sites (Supplementary Fig. 4) and is adjacent to exon 1a. This region (chr17 30,236,120 to 30,236,168 in the GRCh38 assembly) is known for its abundant methylation and is associated with various psychiatric disorders48 and SLC6A4 expression49. SLC6A4 CpG4 (CpG 9 in Devlin et al.’s study48) was reported to be associated with methylation status and depressive mood scores in a previous study. Therefore, we measured the percentage of methylation at CpG4 as an indicator of the methylation status of each sample in subsequent analyses.

Functional connectivity analysis between clusters within the spatial map of IC during emotional stimuli

The time-series of averaged BOLD signals for each cluster within the spatial map of IC were extracted and normalized subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation. A generalized linear model was employed to calculate the dependency between the paired time-series from different clusters for the periods of each emotional stimulation. To account for the hemodynamic response, the BOLD time-points corresponding to each emotional stimulation were delayed by 6 s. The beta values derived from the regression model were used for the subsequent statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses, except for IC-wise ANCOVA, were performed using the R Statistical Package (R Core Team, 2021). All data were first tested for normal distribution using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Differences in facial expressions between patients with MDD and HCs were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Differences in the affective ratings for emotional stimuli were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Associations between variables were assessed using Spearman’s correlation analysis. In addition, Spearman’s partial correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the associations among three variables, generating a correlation coefficient between paired of variables while controlling the remaining variable. IC-wise ANCOVA was performed using in-house scripts in MATLAB (MathWorks Inc., MA, USA). The significance level was set at α = 0.05. Age was included as an additional covariate in all ANCOVAs to address the potential confounding effect of the significant age difference between groups.
