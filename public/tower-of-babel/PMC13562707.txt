Tuning intermetallic growth and flexural performance in gallium-based amalgams via Sn/In alloying and solidifying at controlled temperature
Authors: Paul Elliott, Hadi Noori, Hanchen Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGallium-based amalgams and preforms offer promising pathways for room-temperature bonding and low-temperature structural fabrication, yet their mechanical performance is constrained by brittle failure after interdiffusion-based solidification. This study investigates the mechanical and microstructural evolution of pastes formed by mixing copper particles with gallium (Ga), eutectic gallium-indium (GaIn), eutectic gallium-tin (GaSn), and eutectic gallium-indium-tin alloys (GaInSn), subjected to solidification at either room temperature or 100 °C. Four-point flexural testing showed that Ga and GaSn both strengthened after 100 °C solidification, reaching similar strength levels (~ 7 MPa), whereas GaIn and GaInSn weakened. Fracture surfaces showed plate-like intermetallic cleavage in Ga and GaSn, consistent with a rigid CuGa₂ framework. GaIn and GaInSn displayed more ductile features from dispersed In or InSn regions that disrupt CuGa₂ continuity. Overall, composition and solidification temperature control the balance between rigid intermetallic reinforcement and softer secondary phases. GaSn provides the most practical combination of strength, consistency, and cost for these low-temperature metal systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-53663-5.Subject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Gallium (Ga)-based liquid metals are valued for low-temperature metal joining1, soft electronics2, and thermally conductive pastes3 due to their high fluidity, metallic conductivity, and ability to react with diverse substrates4. When mixed with metal particles such as copper, liquid gallium readily reacts at the interface to form solid intermetallic compounds, causing the mixture to gradually solidify into a rigid metallic matrix5. While other metals, such as gold, silver, and nickel, can be used, the low cost and reactions that occur on a practical timescale with copper (Cu) make it an ideal option for room-temperature solidification. Recent advances now extend these alloys into load-bearing applications, such as creating replacement parts like gears, without the need for melting furnaces or post-machining. Liquid metals offer significant advantages in designs where weight and energy consumption are major considerations6, such as in-space or in-field repairs and retrofits. Gallium’s interaction with Cu, therefore, allows not only low-temperature bonding and structural adhesives where reflow soldering is impractical5,7 but also the emerging strategy of “cold-casting” metal for on-demand structural components.

The same interfacial reactivity that underlies Ga’s adhesive capability, however, often limits its mechanical utility. In systems where Ga is combined with copper particles or foils, rapid interfacial diffusion leads to the formation of the brittle intermetallic phase CuGa₂ even at ambient temperatures8,9. As a result, while Ga-Cu amalgams can form strong metallurgical joints, they often exhibit poor ductility and fracture toughness10.

Additions of tin (Sn) and indium (In) to Ga for alloying further complicate but potentially improve the system. Indium lowers the binary eutectic GaIn melting temperature to 15.7 °C, and tin lowers the GaSn melting temperature to 20.5 °C11, as compared to 29.8 °C for pure gallium12, thus keeping pastes fluid at room temperature (RT). Indium, together with tin, yields eutectic GaInSn, which remains liquid down to 10.8 °C13. Both elements can introduce new intermetallics or oxide species that may reinforce or embrittle the alloy, depending on the products14–16. The net mechanical effect of substituting Ga with Sn or In in amalgam systems, particularly under mild thermal treatments, remains largely unexplored.

Processing temperature is a critical constraint not only because polymeric and paper-based substrates in printed electronics and soft robotics seldom survive beyond 150 °C17, but also because semiconductor packaging must limit the cumulative thermal budget. Lower processing temperatures help avoid warpage and reliability loss in underfills and bump interconnects18. They also accommodate the limited thermal stability of flexible or biodegradable substrates (~ 120 °C)19 and preserve the operational lifetime of silicon devices, which decreases sharply with elevated temperature20. As a result, manufacturers strive for even lower peak temperatures to safeguard adhesives and encapsulants. As such devices become more common, the need will grow for amalgams that can serve as ultra-low-melting solders or high-conductivity thermal interface materials, while remaining within the device’s allowable thermal budget.

Within this broader context, preform-based joining methods, such as those described in Stagon et al.21, utilize shaped, thin layers of gallium-based amalgams to bond at low temperatures without the need for conventional soldering or brazing. These preforms enable precise, repeatable application in scenarios where reflow soldering is impractical. While typically conceived as joining media, preforms can also function as part of the structural load path, serving as bonded metallic doublers to stiffen thin panels22 or cast-in inserts to anchor fasteners23. In such cases, the preform is directly subjected to bending in service, making flexural performance a critical design consideration. Yet the bending behavior of Ga-Cu and alloyed amalgam preforms under different curing conditions has not been systematically quantified, limiting their optimization for high-reliability or load-bearing applications.

In this study, we investigate how alloying Ga with Sn and In affects the mechanical performance and microstructural evolution of Ga-Cu amalgams. We formulated a series of paste compositions using liquid Ga, GaIn, GaSn, and GaInSn alloys filled with copper microparticles, and evaluated the influence of solidification temperature (RT and 100 °C) on flexural strength and fracture morphology. Four-point bending tests, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), and X-ray diffractometry (XRD) were used to establish structure-property relationships across testing conditions. The results highlight a tunable trade-off between intermetallic growth and flexural strength, providing design principles for metallic adhesives and RT joining strategies based on Ga amalgams.

Results

For the studied gallium amalgams, raising the temperature of solidification affected flexural strength, but the magnitude and direction of the response depended on alloy chemistry (Fig. 1). For pure Ga, the average flexural strength increased significantly from 4.4 MPa at room temperature to 6.9 MPa at 100 °C (p < 0.001), representing an improvement of roughly 60%, with the standard deviation decreasing from 1.6 to 1.0 MPa. A similar, though less pronounced, strengthening trend was observed in the GaSn alloy, where the flexural strength increased from 6.3 MPa at RT to 7.1 MPa at 100 °C (p = 0.10), corresponding to about an 11% improvement that was not statistically significant, with variability decreasing slightly from 1.0 to 0.8 MPa.

Flexural strength of Cu-particle amalgams. Bar chart showing the mean four-point flexural strength for Ga, GaIn, GaSn, and GaInSn specimens solidified at RT or 100 °C. Error bars represent one standard deviation (n = 10).

In contrast, the GaIn system exhibited a decrease in strength from 5.7 MPa at RT to 4.8 MPa at 100 °C (p = 0.02), indicating a significant weakening trend with reduced variability (1.0 to 0.4 MPa). The GaInSn ternary alloy showed the lowest overall strength among the compositions, declining from 4.1 MPa to 3.7 MPa (p = 0.05), showing a near-significant trend toward lower strength but not meeting the significance threshold, with unchanged variability (0.4 MPa). These results indicate that Sn additions tend to reinforce and stabilize the Ga matrix, particularly at RT. Conversely, indium additions weaken it, and further heating tends to amplify this deterioration in load-bearing performance. The relatively high variability in Ga RT samples may result from inconsistent re-solidification, as RT is below the melting point of Ga. Under these conditions, Ga likely froze at different rates among samples, before diffusion-controlled solidification could proceed very far. This variability in freezing would delay and alter intermetallic formation.

Examination of the fracture surfaces by SEM (Fig. 2) reveals microstructural differences that correspond to the observed hierarchy of flexural strengths. Both the Ga and GaSn materials exhibited abundant, faceted plate-like features consistent with cleavage of intermetallic phases. These plates created flat crack paths and sharp edges indicative of a brittle fracture mechanism. Although the Ga RT and 100 °C fracture surfaces appeared similar, the RT samples experienced non-uniform solidification, resulting in a weaker intermetallic framework. In GaSn, the added Sn does not appear as a distinct ductile phase in the fracture surfaces, consistent with Sn forming a comparatively strong β-Sn phase that does not interrupt the CuGa₂ framework.

All compositions exhibit plate-like intermetallic features, with GaIn and GaInSn displaying more ductile ligaments. Insets highlight these plate-like regions, and arrows mark ductile ligaments. The GaIn samples, in contrast, displayed a mixed morphology where faceted intermetallic regions similar to those in Ga and GaSn, but with small ductile features such as dimples and ligaments, suggesting that softer In regions exist between the intermetallic plates, even though a continuous brittle framework still contributes to load bearing. The GaInSn samples resemble GaIn but exhibit larger ligaments and dimples, consistent with greater disruption of intermetallic continuity. Thus, while all compositions ultimately fracture in a brittle manner, the degree to which added elements interrupt the CuGa₂ network explains the strength hierarchy. Sn leaves the intermetallic framework largely intact and GaSn remains strong, whereas In or InSn introduce soft domains that weaken GaIn and GaInSn.

The microstructure of the Ga RT specimen (Fig. 3a) shows a continuous matrix, visible as lighter grey regions in the BSE image, with embedded darker particles. EDS maps show a strong Cu signal localized in the darker features, indicating locations of Cu particles. The Ga signal is concentrated in the surrounding lighter matrix with a significant amount of Cu signal at a lower intensity compared to the Cu particle areas. Dark areas are also apparent and have enhanced contrast due to the 1 μm diamond polishing suspension that tends to remain in the cavities.

When In is introduced (Fig. 3b), the microstructure evolves to include bright phases distributed within the Ga-rich matrix that tend to contain dark areas. The In EDS map confirms that these areas are composed mainly of In, while the Cu and Ga distributions remain similar to those in the binary Ga-Cu system. The substitution of In for Sn (Fig. 3c) shows similar bright phases distributed within the Ga-rich matrix but without the dark regions. The Sn EDS map confirms that these areas are composed primarily of Sn. For the GaInSn RT case (Fig. 3d), results very similar to GaIn are observed. EDS mapping confirms the presence of Sn and In signals in the light domains, consistent with the presence of an InSn intermetallic phase. The BSE images show more dark regions in the In areas of GaIn and the InSn regions of GaInSn. This is likely because these soft phases are preferentially abraded during polishing, forming pits or trapping debris.

A very similar structure is seen in the 100 °C case as compared to the RT case for Ga and GaSn. Dark voids are seen in all images. In the GaIn (Fig. 4b) and GaSn cases (Fig. 4c), additional bright regions are seen consisting of In or Sn, respectively, with the In regions showing more dark areas.

The GaIn 100 °C (Fig. 4b) and GaInSn 100 °C microstructures (Fig. 4d) show differences as compared to the RT case. In GaIn, the discrete, well-bounded In domains seen at RT become less sharply defined and spread more conformally around adjacent CuGa₂ regions after heating. This smoothing and encroachment of the In phase likely weakens the rigidity of the intermetallic framework, consistent with the significant decrease in strength. A similar pattern emerges in GaInSn. The bright InSn regions visible in the RT condition persist, but after heating, they frequently form thin shells around Cu particles. By collecting as a soft interfacial layer, the InSn phase isolates the Cu particles from the surrounding matrix, which reduces adhesion and may interrupt their contribution to the rigid load-bearing intermetallic framework. Together, these behaviors indicate that in both GaIn and GaInSn, heating promotes migration of soft In-rich phases toward intermetallic surfaces or Cu particles, disrupting network continuity. This disruption likely increases local stress concentrations and contributes to the observed reduction in flexural strength.

The XRD patterns of each composition are shown in Fig. 5. Data were collected from 10° to 90°, with data from 10° to 55° only being shown for clarity. In the Ga samples, the Ga is almost entirely converted to CuGa₂, with the 100 °C case showing larger peaks indicating a more complete conversion. Only small peaks remain for Cu in both cases. In GaIn RT, the microstructure contains a smaller CuGa₂ fraction, and now an In signal is seen, along with a minor fraction of unreacted Cu. After heating to 100 °C, the signal remains very similar.

X-ray diffraction patterns of Cu-particle amalgams formulated with Ga, GaIn, GaSn, and GaInSn after solidification at room temperature or 100 °C. Symbols represent the identified principal crystalline phases of CuGa₂, Cu, In, β-Sn, In1.5Sn0.5, CuInO2, SnO, and the sample holder. Intensities are vertically offset for clarity.

In GaSn RT, the microstructure contains a large CuGa₂ fraction, but now a smaller β-Sn-rich solid solution is also present, along with a minor fraction of unreacted Cu. Heating to 100 °C increases the strength of the β-Sn phase signal. At RT, the β-Sn phase is consistent with a Ga-doped solid solution (Ga₀.₀₇₁Sn₀.₉₂₉), whereas for high-temperature samples, the signal corresponds more closely to pure β-Sn. This transition is likely to occur because some Ga is initially dissolved in the β-Sn-rich solid solution and exists in poorly ordered states that do not contribute to as strong diffraction peaks. Heating promotes segregation and crystallization, resulting in a more distinct and ordered Sn phase. The GaInSn system exhibits the most complex phase evolution. At RT, CuGa₂ coexists with an In₁.₅Sn₀.₅ compound along with a small amount of free Cu. After heating to 100 °C, the CuGa₂ fraction decreases while the In₁.₅Sn₀.₅ phase remains about the same. Additional oxide phases, including CuInO₂ and SnO, also appear together with trace residual Cu still present. The reduction in CuGa₂ likely reflects competition from In, which ties up both Cu and Sn into alternative InSn and CuIn phases and leaves less Ga available to sustain CuGa₂. Although Ga remains in the system, it does not produce distinct XRD peaks because it is either incorporated into a solid solution or present in a liquid-like state that does not diffract coherently. As in GaSn, heating may liberate Sn from Ga solid solution regions, and some of this Sn oxidizes to form SnO.

Pure Ga forms a nearly single-phase CuGa₂. Adding indium introduces discrete In regions, while adding tin produces a Sn-rich secondary phase. The ternary GaInSn system develops a multiphase mixture dominated by In₁.₅Sn₀.₅, together with additional InSn compounds and oxides, consistent with weaker interfacial bonding.

To quantify the microstructural observations from the BSE cross-sections (Figs. 3 and 4), image analysis was performed using ImageJ greyscale thresholding to determine the area fractions of voids, residual Cu, CuGa₂, and secondary phases (Sn, In, or In₁.₅Sn₀.₅) across 5 fields of view per condition. The results are shown in Fig. 6, with numerical values and standard deviations reported in Supplementary Table S1. At room temperature, the CuGa₂ area fraction showed an overall decreasing trend with alloying additions, from 60.6 ± 3.0% in Ga to 49.5 ± 7.4% in GaInSn, though the variability between fields of view means that adjacent compositions in this series are not clearly distinguishable. This general trend is consistent with the progressive displacement of the intermetallic matrix by soft secondary phases. The secondary phase area fractions for RT conditions were 5.8% In in GaIn, 11.1% Sn in GaSn, and 9.6% In₁.₅Sn₀.₅ in GaInSn. The residual Cu fraction was comparable across all RT conditions (31.7–38.3%), suggesting similar degrees of Cu consumption regardless of the liquid metal composition. Void fractions were low across all conditions (1.1–6.4%) and did not correlate with the flexural strength trends, indicating that porosity is not a primary factor governing the observed mechanical behavior. At 100 °C, the CuGa₂ and Cu fractions remained broadly similar to the RT values within the measurement variability. The Sn area fraction in GaSn appeared to decrease from 11.1% to 3.9%, and the In area fraction in GaIn appeared to increase from 5.8% to 9.8%, though these differences should be interpreted in light of the variability observed in each group.

Error bars represent ± one standard deviation from 5 fields of view per condition. Numerical values are given in Supplementary Table S1.

Discussion

Heating the Cu-particle amalgams to 100 °C, as compared to the RT condition, produced different outcomes depending on the added elements. For the Ga and GaSn systems, raising the temperature of solidification increased flexural strength by approximately 60% and 11%, respectively. For GaIn and GaInSn, however, the effect was reversed, and the strength decreased by about 16% and 10%, respectively, after heating. Among these, only the increase in Ga and the decrease in GaIn were statistically significant, while the changes in GaSn and GaInSn were not significant. The key variable governing these opposing trends is how the additions of In, Sn, and InSn regions affect the overall morphology.

Pure Ga converts largely to CuGa₂ at room temperature and does so more completely at 100 °C, showing a clear strength boost upon heating. This trend is likely exacerbated by variations in the re-solidification of Ga due to RT being below its freezing point. Adding Sn introduces discrete β-Sn-rich regions within the Ga matrix while still maintaining a CuGa₂ framework. The similar flexural strength of the Ga and GaSn composites at 100 °C suggests both form comparably rigid CuGa₂ frameworks. GaSn, however, achieves this without the solidification variability seen in pure Ga and further provides improved performance at room temperature due to the lower melting temperature of the eutectic.

In contrast, the GaIn system introduces In domains that reduce the strength of the intermetallic framework relative to Ga and GaSn. Heating to 100 °C results in a statistically significant reduction in strength, yet the XRD results reveal little change in phase composition. This observation suggests that the formation of a new phase does not drive the weakening of the material. The GaInSn alloy extends this behavior, where heating further reduces strength through growth or redistribution of soft InSn regions and a reduction in the CuGa₂ fraction. However, the associated strength decrease appears only as a near-significant trend.

The absence of Cu-Sn and Cu-In intermetallic compounds (e.g., Cu₆Sn₅, Cu₃Sn, CuIn) in the XRD patterns (Fig. 5) reflects both the kinetic and compositional hierarchy of reactions in these systems. The relevant binary phase diagrams for these systems (Supplementary Fig. S1) confirm that CuGa₂ is thermodynamically stable at both processing temperatures, while the Ga-Sn and Ga-In systems are simple eutectics with no binary intermetallic compounds. CuGa₂ forms spontaneously at the Cu interface even at room temperature8,9, and first-principles calculations confirm its thermodynamic stability24. In conventional Cu-Sn solder joints, Cu₆Sn₅ nucleation and growth require either molten Sn at reflow temperatures (> 230 °C) or prolonged solid-state aging at 70–150 °C over hundreds of hours25. Because Ga constitutes the majority of the liquid phase in all four formulations (68.5–100 wt%), it reacts preferentially with Cu, rapidly consuming the available particle surfaces. The relatively small fractions of Sn (up to 13.5 wt%) and In (up to 24.5 wt%) in the liquid are thereby excluded from the Cu interface and instead solidify as beta-Sn, metallic In, or In₁.₅Sn₀.₅, as confirmed by the XRD and EDS results. This competitive exclusion of Sn and In from Cu is consistent with previous observations that Ga-based liquid metals on Cu substrates produce CuGa₂ as the primary intermetallic even when alloyed with Sn15,16.

The activation energy for CuGa₂ is notably lower than the 72 kJ/mol reported for Cu₆Sn₅ growth in the Cu/Sn-0.7Cu system25, with values for other Sn-based solders ranging from 59 to 90 kJ/mol for the individual Cu-Sn intermetallic phases. This disparity in activation energies provides a kinetic basis for the preferential formation of CuGa₂ over Cu-Sn or Cu-In intermetallics in the present amalgam system. Ga reacts with available Cu surfaces substantially faster than Sn or In can at both room temperature and 100 °C, particularly given that Ga constitutes the majority of the liquid phase (68.5–100 wt%). While the geometry of the present system differs from a planar diffusion couple, with Cu particles embedded in a liquid metal matrix rather than flat substrates, the Arrhenius temperature dependence and the relative magnitudes of the activation energies remain applicable as a first-order estimate of the competitive reaction kinetics.

Fractography and polished cross-sections provide a microstructural rationale for these trends. Ga and GaSn fracture surfaces are dominated by flat, faceted intermetallic plates characteristic of brittle cleavage, consistent with their rigid CuGa₂ frameworks, while GaIn and GaInSn display more ductile features such as dimples and torn ligaments that may disrupt the continuity of the CuGa₂ structure. These observations align with the X-ray diffraction data. Ga produces an almost single-phase CuGa₂ and GaIn forms a CuGa₂/In composite in which dispersed In regions interrupt the continuity of the load-bearing intermetallic framework. GaSn contains a mixed CuGa₂/β-Sn network that strengthens upon heating, whereas GaInSn transitions to a CuGa₂/In₁.₅Sn₀.₅/oxide combination that may reduce adhesion between the intermetallic and the particles.

Future work may investigate the long-term mechanical stability of Ga-based amalgams under cyclic loading, thermal aging, and environmental exposure to assess their viability as structural materials in real-world applications. Further studies could include higher curing temperatures and longer dwell times to determine whether the compositions can be further strengthened or if ductility can be improved, and to explore how varying In and Sn content affects the competition between intermetallic formation and weakening due to dispersed phases.

In summary, solidification at 100 °C substantially strengthens the Ga amalgam and yields a non-significant strength gain for GaSn. In contrast, GaIn shows a significant decrease and GaInSn a near-significant reduction in flexural strength. Ga and GaSn benefit from the development of rigid, continuous CuGa₂ frameworks, which are more completely formed in Ga and supplemented by a strong β-Sn phase in GaSn. By contrast, GaIn and GaInSn are weakened by a reduction in the effective CuGa₂ backbone, the growth or redistribution of soft In or InSn regions that migrate toward intermetallic plates or Cu particles, and increased oxide formation in GaInSn. These changes fragment the load-bearing network, potentially promoting local stress concentrations. Among all conditions tested, Ga and GaSn solidified at 100 °C exhibit the highest flexural strengths (~ 7 MPa), with GaSn additionally offering better reproducibility, improved room-temperature strength, and a lower material cost, making it the most practical formulation. Overall, the results demonstrate that alloy chemistry and solidification temperature are effective levers for tuning mechanical performance. Formulations should prioritize continuous CuGa₂ networks, limit the volume and connectivity of soft residual phases, and minimize In segregation that undermines particle anchoring and network continuity.

Methods

The liquid metal components of the amalgams were composed of gallium, indium, and tin, all of which had a purity of 99.99 wt%. Samples described as Ga were pure gallium, GaIn was a eutectic alloy of gallium (75.5 wt%) and indium (24.5 wt%), GaSn was a eutectic alloy of gallium (86.5 wt%) and tin (13.5 wt%), and GaInSn was an eutectic alloy of gallium (68.5 wt%), indium (21.5 wt%), and tin (10.0 wt%). The liquid eutectic compositions were mixed with Cu powder (US Research Nanomaterials, < 8 μm, 99.5 wt% purity, near-spherical morphology; see Supplementary Fig. S2 for particle size distribution) at RT, whereas Ga was heated to just above its melting temperature (~ 40 °C) before mixing with copper powders, all at ambient atmospheric pressure. The particles and liquid metal were mixed in a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube at a Cu to liquid metal weight ratio of 0.7:1. The mixture was vibrated in a dental amalgamator for 20 s, then molded into rectangular specimens (3 × 4 × 50 mm) using a mold with a lightly oiled steel base and cellulose acetate-coated soda-lime glass sidewalls. The RT samples were solidified in 5 days under ambient conditions (21–24 °C), while the 100 °C samples were placed in an electric oven for 1 day, then removed and held at RT for an additional 4 days before flexural testing. Flexural force was measured using the four-point bending test, as specified in ASTM Standard C1161-18, Configuration B. The flexural strength S was calculated as:

where P is the force at fracture, L is the outer support span (40 mm), Li is the inner loading span (20 mm), b is the specimen width (4 mm), and d is the specimen thickness (3 mm). This test method was chosen over three-point bending because it applies a uniform maximum stress across a broader region and more accurately assesses the brittle failure due to structural defects, such as non-uniformly distributed voids. A 500 N load cell was used to measure the force with a reading accuracy of ± 0.5%, down to 0.1% of the load cell capacity. Following the standard recommendation, a minimum of ten specimens of each type were tested. Electron microscopy was performed using a Quanta 600 field emission SEM (FEI) equipped with a Bruker EDS system. Quantitative phase area fractions were determined from polished BSE cross-section images using ImageJ (v1.54p). Five non-overlapping fields of view (100 × 100 μm each) were acquired per condition at 2500x. Images were converted to 8-bit greyscale and segmented by intensity thresholding to distinguish four constituent phases: voids (darkest), residual Cu particles (dark grey), CuGa₂ matrix (light grey), and secondary phases including Sn, In, or In₁.₅Sn₀.₅ (brightest). Threshold ranges were determined from the greyscale histogram of each image and applied consistently across all conditions. The area fraction of each phase was calculated as the ratio of segmented pixels to total image area. The Cu powder particle size distribution was characterized by SEM imaging of the as-received powder dispersed on a carbon adhesive stub. The diameters of 273 individual particles were measured using ImageJ, from which D₁₀, D₅₀, and D₉₀ values were calculated (Supplementary Fig. S2). The X-ray diffraction patterns were collected using a Bruker D8 Advance from 2θ = 10° to 90°, where specimens were disks 10 mm in diameter and 1 mm thick, made under the same conditions as the strength test bars. The Kα2 peaks were stripped, the background was removed, and the peaks were clipped at 4,000 arbitrary units for clarity. Statistical comparisons between room-temperature and 100 °C conditions were performed using two-tailed t-tests assuming unequal variances, with n = 10 per group and α = 0.05. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test, which indicated no significant departures for any group.
