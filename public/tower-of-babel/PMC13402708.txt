The disulfonic acid ANDS disrupts ANP32B-p53 interaction to suppress chronic myeloid leukemia
Authors: Yu-Sheng Wei, Fei-Yu Jin, Hong-Hui Zeng, Hao-Ran Liu, Ying-Yi Chen, Ying-Li Wu, Ai-Wu Zhou, Jian Zhang, Yun Yu, Guo-Qiang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Eradicating leukemic stem cells (LSCs), a major driver of primary resistance and relapse in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) following tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) treatment, is critical for achieving a cure. Previously, we identified that ANP32B promotes CML LSCs’ survival and leukemogenesis by directly binding and inhibiting p53 activity, suggesting a therapeutic opportunity. Here, we show that 1-amino-8-naphthol-2,4-disulfonic acid (ANDS) binds unacetylated p53 and restores p53 activity by disrupting ANP32B-p53 interaction. Consequently, ANDS inhibits CML cell proliferation, impairs LSC function and prolongs survival in the CML mouse model while sparing normal progenitor cells. Based on notion that ANDS, as a previously-identified CaMKP inhibitor, potentially activates p-CaMKIIγ to accelerate CML progression, we find that ANDS combination with KN93, a p-CaMKIIγ inhibitor, synergistically suppresses both TKI-sensitive and -resistant CML. Thus, we highlight that ANDS alone or in combination with CaMKP inhibitor could become a therapeutic strategy for eradicating LSCs and overcoming TKI-resistance in CML.

Introduction

Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) arises from the malignant transformation of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) into leukemic stem cells (LSCs), a process driven by the BCR-ABL1 fusion protein1. While tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have dramatically improved CML management, they fail to eradicate LSCs, leading to therapy resistance, relapse and persistent disease2. Besides BCR-ABL1 activity3–5, LSC survival and self-renewal are also associated with BCR-ABL1-independent mechanisms such as transcription factors and epigenetic modulators6–9. Therefore, targeting these unique LSC regulatory pathways represents a crucial strategy for achieving long-term CML remission.

The acidic nuclear phosphoprotein 32 (ANP32) family, consisting of ANP32A, ANP32B, and ANP32E, has emerged as a compelling therapeutic target in various cancers, particularly hematopoietic malignancies9–14. ANP32A, for instance, promotes acute myeloid leukemia (AML) progression by modulating acetyl-histone 3 (H3) levels in lipid metabolism13. Furthermore, disrupting ANP32A-H3 interactions with a small peptide has efficacy against leukemia in vivo15. Our previous work identified ANP32B to be highly expressed in CML patients and it was essential for LSC survival by directly binding to and inhibiting p53 activity. Remarkably, Anp32b deletion in mice exhibits a synergistic therapeutic effect against CML when combined with TKI9. Therefore, targeting the ANP32B-p53 interaction and elucidating its mechanism is a crucial strategy for eradicating CML LSCs. Herein, we report ANDS (1-amino-8-naphthol-2,4-disulfonic acid), a known Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase phosphatase (CAMKP) inhibitor, as an inhibitor of the ANP32B-p53 interaction, and it can effectively inhibit leukemia progression in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, the combination of ANDS with KN93, a p-CaMKIIγ inhibitor, exhibits synergistic inhibitory effects on both TKI-sensitive and -resistant CML progression in mouse models and human primary cells.

Results

Identification of small-molecule inhibitors of ANP32B-p53 interaction

To identify potent inhibitors of the ANP32B-p53 interaction, we performed a high-throughput drug screen (HTS) using a rigorously validated hit-identification workflow (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Initially, we established an in vitro ANP32B-p53 interaction assay using homogeneous time-resolved fluorescence (HTRF) technology (Supplementary Fig. 1b). This assay employed two antibodies coupled with HTRF reagents, anti-GST-Tb and anti-His-XL665, respectively serving as the fluorescence emission donor and the acceptor molecule. Given that the C-terminal low complexity acidic region (LCAR) of ANP32B mediates its interaction with the C-terminal domain (CTD) of p539, and fragment aa364-393 of p53 was necessary and sufficient for its interaction with ANP32B in vitro (Supplementary Fig. 1c), these antibodies bind to GST-p53-CTD and His-ANP32B-LCAR through their respective GST and His tags. Upon p53-CTD and ANP32B-LCAR interaction, the fluorophores are brought into close proximity, resulting in a high fluorescence energy transfer signal. Conversely, the presence of the inhibitor of ANP32B-p53 binding would lead to a decrease in the HTRF signal. To validate the assay, we in vitro purified GST-p53-CTD, His-ANP32B-LCAR and a control protein, GST-His (Supplementary Fig. 1d). The addition of the dual-labeled GST-His protein resulted in a significant increase in the HTRF ratio (Supplementary Fig. 1e). We observed maximal HTRF signal upon incubation with 30 nM GST-p53-CTD and 30 nM His-ANP32B-LCAR (Supplementary Fig. 1f).

Using the HTRF assay, we screened 7,316 compounds from the Clinical Drug Library of SJTU, and got nine compounds which selectively inhibited the ANP32B-p53 HTRF signal by >25%, without significant effect on the GST-His control HTRF signal (Supplementary Fig. 2a). ANDS directly targets p53

Considering labeling with chromophores to follow biomolecular interactions by fluorescence may introduce some artifact16, we performed cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)17–20 on KU812 cells. The results showed that ANDS at 5 µM, which presented significant inhibition for ANP32B-p53 interaction (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 2b), significantly enhanced the thermal stability of p53 but not ANP32B (Fig. 1c–e). Furthermore, ANDS induced a concentration-dependent stabilization of p53 protein levels at 48 °C (Fig. 1f). In line with these observations, lysate-based CETSA experiments also confirmed p53 as a direct cellular target of ANDS (Supplementary Fig. 2c–f). Furthermore, in vitro streptavidin pull-down assays also demonstrated that biotin-conjugated ANDS, which retained its ability to disrupt the ANP32B-p53 interaction in vitro (Fig. 1g), specifically precipitated GST-tagged p53-CTD, but not ANP32B (Fig. 1h), which could also be seen in KU812 cells (Fig. 1i). In addition, the surface plasmon resonance (SPR)21 analysis showed that the ANP32B-LCAR interacted with the p53-CTD with an equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) of 0.399 μM (Fig. 1j). The pre-incubation of ANDS with p53-CTD significantly weakened the ANP32B-LCAR/p53-CTD interaction, increasing the KD to 18.59 μM (Fig. 1k, l). Additionally, ANDS directly bound to immobilized recombinant p53-CTD with a KD of 1.496 μM (Fig. 1m). Furthermore, competitive binding assays revealed that ANDS inhibited the ANP32B-LCAR/p53-CTD interaction in a dose-dependent manner, with an IC50 of 4.5682 μM (Fig. 1n, o). To explore how ANDS is capable to block ANP32B-LCAR/p53-CTD interaction at the molecular level, [1H, 15N] HSQC spectra were collected under defined experimental conditions. Upon on the binding of ANP32B, the nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) resonances for a number of residues in p53-CTD including G356, L369, S371, K373, G374, K381, K382, L383, M384, F385, K386 and T387 attenuated exceeding 75%. Meanwhile, the NMR resonances for residues including K370, K381, K382, L383 and T387 in p53-CTD underwent significant chemical shift perturbations (CSPs) in response to the presence of ANP32B (Supplementary Fig. 2g, left and Supplementary Fig. 2h). These observations indicate that the affected residues are located near or within the interaction interface between p53-CTD and ANP32B, and that exchange broadening may arise from intermediate dynamics accompanying complex formation. Upon titration of ANDS into p53-CTD, the signals of residues G325, G356, E358, G360, L369, K370, S371, K373, G374, K381, L383, M384, F385, K386, and T387 were attenuated by more than 80%, encompassing those previously affected by ANP32B binding. Meanwhile, the NMR resonances for residues including K320, K321, D324, E388, D391 and D393 in p53-CTD underwent significant CSPs in response to the presence of ANDS (Supplementary Fig. 2g, middle and Supplementary Fig. 2i), suggesting that ANDS’s binding induces extensive conformational rearrangements of p53-CTD. Comparison of the spectra for the p53-CTD: ANDS mixture sample and the p53-CTD: ANP32B: ANDS mixture sample revealed complete spectral overlap and similar signal intensities (Supplementary Fig. 2g, right). This result demonstrates that once ANDS is bound, p53-CTD can no longer associate with ANP32B. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ANDS disrupts ANP32B-p53 interaction by binding to p53.

ANDS restores p53 acetylation and activity by binding to unacetylated p53

While ANP32B was known to bind to and inhibit p53 activity, the precise mechanism of this inhibition remains unclear. Given that the acidic-domain-containing factors like ANP32B and SET/TAF1β, along with other negatively charged disordered proteins, were suggested as readers of positively charged unacetylated p53-CTD22–24, we hypothesized that electrostatic interactions mediate ANP32B-LCAR/p53-CTD binding. To test this, we measured their association across increasing KCl concentrations (50-1000 mM) using GST pull-down and HTRF assay. The results showed that ANP32B-LCAR/p53-CTD binding initially strengthened under 0-50 mM, peaked at 100 mM, and then declined under 100-1000 mM of KCl, while HTRF ratio of GST-His control remained stable (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). These results support an electrostatic interaction mechanism between ANP32B and p53. However, despite the similarities between the LCARs of ANP32B and ANP32A, which also, our data demonstrated that unlike ANP32B, ANP32A did not interact with p53 (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Considering that tyrosine Y179/Y214 in ANP32A-LCAR are not conserved in ANP32B-LCAR (Supplementary Fig. 3d), we mutated the Y179/Y214 residues in ANP32A to glutamic acid (E) in KU812 cells, matching the corresponding residue in ANP32B, and found that the mutant acquired the ability to interact with p53 (Supplementary Fig. 3e), indicating that these two tyrosines blocked interaction of the ANP32A-LCAR with p53, although ANP32A also contains negatively charged residues.

Treatment of KU812 and K562-p53 (p53-deficient K562 cells stably expressing p53) cells with ANDS resulted in a dose-dependent viability reduction, with IC50 values of 4.637 μM and 5.654 μM, respectively (Fig. 2a). Considering that p53 acetylation is established as a regulator of p53 transcriptional activity25,26, we hypothesized that ANDS promotes p53 acetylation and restores its activity by disrupting the acetylation-dependent ANP32B-p53 interaction. Indeed, deletion of ANP32B, but not ANP32A, led to a marked increase in p53 acetylation levels in KU812 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3f), suggesting a non-redundant, ANP32B-specific role in modulating p53 acetylation. Endogenous co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays revealed that 5 μM ANDS nearly abolished the ANP32B-p53 interaction and enhanced p53 acetylation (Fig. 2b, c). In contrast, a ten-fold higher concentration of ANDS (50 μM) was required to significantly disrupt the SET/TAF1β-p53 interaction (Supplementary Fig. 3g), suggesting that ANDS more selectively disrupts the ANP32B-p53 interaction. To confirm ANP32B specifically binds unacetylated p53, we generated constructs of wild-type p53 (p53WT), acetylation-deficient p53 (p53KR) or acetylation-mimicking p53 (p53KQ) as previously reported22. Given that multiple Lys-to-Gln substitutions have previously been associated with enhanced aggregation propensity27,28, we performed light scattering and thioflavin T (ThT) binding assays, which revealed that the p53KQ mutant did not exhibit increased aggregation compared to p53WT and p53KR mutants (Supplementary Fig. 3k, l). Subsequently, co-IP analysis demonstrated that ANP32B formed complexes with p53WT and p53KR, but not with p53KQ, confirming that ANP32B selectively interacts with unacetylated p53 (Fig. 2g). Furthermore, overexpression of CREB-binding protein (CBP), an enzyme responsible for p53-CTD acetylation, abolished the ANP32B-p53 interaction (Fig. 2h). Streptavidin pull-down assay revealed that biotin-ANDS specifically pulled down p53WT and p53KR, but not p53KQ in K562 cells, directly demonstrating ANDS binding to unacetylated p53 (Fig. 2i). Consequently, at 5 μM, ANDS competitively prevented the interaction between ANP32B and unacetylated p53, resulting in increased p53 acetylation levels, while showing no significant effect on the SET/TAF1β-p53 complex at this concentration (Fig. 2g, h). Consistent with this, ANDS enhanced p53 transcriptional activity in K562-p53WT and K562-p53KR cells, but not in K562-p53KQ cells (Fig. 2j, k). Collectively, these results suggest that ANDS competitively binds to unacetylated p53, disrupting its interaction with ANP32B, and thereby reversing its inhibition of p53 activity.

By the way, a previous report showed that phosphorylation of ANP32B within the LCAR leads to a dramatic shift in its cellular localization29. Indeed, we showed that wild-type ANP32B and its phospho-deficient mutant (T244A) interacted with p53 in the nuclei, whereas the phospho-mimetic mutants (T244D and T244E) were predominantly localized to the cytoplasm without the ability binding to p53 (Supplementary Fig. 3m). Furthermore, ANDS treatment did not alter ANP32B phosphorylation status or its nuclear localization but specifically disrupted the ANP32B-p53 interaction in the nuclei (Supplementary Fig. 3n).

ANDS-mediated disruption of ANP32B-p53 interaction inhibits CML progression and impairs LSC function

To determine if ANDS inhibits CML cell growth through disruption of the ANP32B-p53 interaction, we treated KU812 and K562-p53 cells with and without ANP32B knockdown by sgRNAs specifically against ANP32B (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 3h) with 5 μM ANDS. The results showed that ANDS inhibited proliferation and promoted apoptosis in ANP32B-wildtype cells, while ANP32B-knockdown also did it. Notably, ANDS promoted cell proliferation and inhibited apoptosis to a degree in ANP32B-knockdown cells (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Furthermore, 5 μM ANDS suppressed proliferation and promoted apoptosis in K562-p53WT and K562-p53KR cells. However, ANDS promoted growth and inhibited apoptosis in K562-p53KQ cells (Fig. 3c, d). Importantly, although ANDS disrupted the ANP32B-p53 interaction in a dose-dependent manner in mouse normal progenitor 32D cells (Supplementary Fig. 4c), no significant effects on 32D cell growth and apoptosis were observed (Supplementary Fig. 4d, e). To examine the impact of ANDS on Bcr-Abl1WT-induced CML progression in vivo, we first assessed its potential toxicity in normal mice. Continuous administration of ANDS for 30 days resulted in a slight decrease in the frequencies of Lin–Sca-1+c-Kit+ (LSK) cells, including LT-HSCs, ST-HSCs, and MPPs (Supplementary Fig. 4f-g). However, total bone marrow (BM) cell numbers, progenitor and mature cell populations, remained unaffected (Supplementary Fig. 4h–k). Furthermore, no significant alterations in body weight or the appearance of the livers, spleens and lungs were observed following ANDS treatment (Supplementary Fig. 4l–n). These data indicate that ANDS does not significantly impact normal hematopoiesis in mice.

Next, we established CML models using Anp32b+/+ and Anp32b–/– mice, as previously described9 (Supplementary Fig. 4o). At 18 hours post-transplantation, homing in BM or spleen of Anp32b knockout GFP+ leukemic-initiating cells (LICs) showed no significant difference compared to wild-type cells (Supplementary Fig. 4p). To evaluate the efficacy of ANDS on the CML models, on day 15, BM GFP+ leukemic cells from both groups were harvested and treated with ANDS in vitro. The results showed that ANDS dose-dependently inhibited growth and colony formation with apoptosis induction in Anp32b+/+ GFP+ CML cells. Conversely, ANDS promoted growth and inhibited apoptosis in Anp32b–/– GFP+ CML cells (Supplementary Fig. 5a–d). As we reported previously9, Anp32b knockout significantly inhibited CML progression (Fig. 3e-k and Supplementary Fig. 5e-i). To further investigate the role of p-CaMKIIγ in CML progression, we showed that KN93, a known inhibitor of p-CaMKIIγ39–41, significantly downregulated p-CaMKIIγ levels in a dose- and time-dependent manner in CML cell lines (Fig. 5c, d and Supplementary Fig. 8c, d). Notably, the combination of ANDS and KN93 synergistically inhibited CML cell growth and promoted apoptosis compared to either agent alone in control cells. Similar results were obtained in ANP32B-deficient and control K562-p53 cells (Supplementary Fig. 8e–g). To validate these findings in vivo, we treated primary CML mice established by Anp32b+/+ and Anp32b–/– mice with ANDS and/or KN93. Consistently, the combination of ANDS and KN93 synergistically suppressed Anp32b+/+ CML progression compared to either ANDS or KN93 alone in mice, but exhibited no synergistic effect in Anp32b–/– CML progression (Fig. 5h–l). These results indicate that ANDS promotes CML progression by increasing p-CaMKIIγ in the absence of ANP32B. Conversely, when ANP32B is present, inhibiting p-CaMKIIγ enhances the suppressive effects of ANDS on CML.

Discussion

Protein-protein interactions form complex cellular networks fundamental to many key biological processes such as signal transduction, cell proliferation, and cell death. Thus, their perturbation is often associated with many human diseases, especially cancers. Targeting protein-protein interactions has become a promising approach in drug discovery, and ongoing advancements in the field hold the potential to provide highly specific therapies for a wide range of complex diseases42. Extensive evidence implicates ANP32B as a critical mediator in physiological and pathological processes9,12,43–47. Initially, it was reported that acidic domain-containing factors act as a class of acetylation-dependent regulators by targeting p53 and potentially other proteins22,23. Then, we also showed that the deletion of ANP32B, an acidic-domain-containing protein, enhances p53 transcriptional activity to impair LSC function in a murine CML model and exhibits synergistic therapeutic effects with TKIs in inhibiting CML propagation9. Herein, we identified that the small molecule ANDS could effectively impair LSCs of CML and inhibit CML progression in vitro and in mouse models regardless of their sensitivity to TKIs by binding unacetylated p53 and restoring p53 activity through disrupting ANP32B-p53 interaction. Due to ANDS is also a CaMKP inhibitor, which potentially activates p-CaMKIIγ to accelerate CML progression in the absence of ANP32B expression, we found that ANDS combination with p-CaMKIIγ inhibitor KN93 synergistically inhibited both TKI-sensitive and -resistant CML progression in mouse models and human primary cells. Collectively, our findings highlight that ANDS, especially its combination with KN93, could become an effective therapeutic strategy for CML, which warrants performing a clinical trial.

The ANP32B-p53 interaction is specific to unacetylated p53, and ANP32B binding prevents p53 acetylation, thus reducing its activity. Our SPR experiments quantitatively demonstrated that ANP32B-LCAR bound p53-CTD with high affinity, while ANDS disrupted this interaction by directly targeting p53-CTD. Furthermore, considering that p53-CTD is intrinsically disordered, [1H, 15N] HSQC spectra imply that ANDS binding potentially promotes a conformational transition in p53-CTD that precludes its further interaction with ANP32B. Notably, salt-dependent binding assays revealed that electrostatic forces mediated the ANP32B-p53 interaction, consistent with the proposed charge-based recognition mechanism for unacetylated p5322,23. The bell-shaped distribution in salt-dependent binding assays may be explained by the fact that ANP32B-NLS (KRKR) might transiently come into contact with any negatively-charged stretch from ANP32B-LCAR at low ionic strength (50 mM KCl), making it difficult for p53-CTD to bind LCAR. At moderate ionic strength (100 mM KCl), the NLS/LCAR interaction of ANP32B may be disrupted, leading to higher affinity for p53-CTD48. While ANDS effectively disrupted the ANP32B-p53 interaction at low concentrations, it requires much higher doses to impair SET/TAF1β-p53 binding. We speculate that the more acidic polyD/E domain (80.78% D/E) of SET/TAF1β confers greater resistance to ANDS-mediated p53 dissociation compared to ANP32B (66.74% D/E), which remains to be further investigated.

While ANDS demonstrates therapeutic potential in CML by targeting the ANP32B-p53 axis, our study reveals important complexities in its mechanism of action. Notably, Anp32b knockout mice treated with ANDS exhibited worse disease progression compared to untreated controls, suggesting additional ANP32B-independent effects. This paradoxical response may be attributed to ANDS’s known function as a CaMKP inhibitor35,36, which can activate the p-CaMKIIγ pathway—a potent driver of CML blast crisis37,38. Consistent with this, the p-CaMKIIγ inhibitor KN9339–41 enhanced suppressive effects of ANDS on CML, indicating that limiting the CaMKP-CaMKIIγ axis can improve efficacy. ANDS and its derivatives exhibit varied CaMKP activity40,49, therefore, modifying specific active groups within ANDS is crucial. Such modifications could optimize ANDS to better disrupt the ANP32B-p53 interaction while simultaneously minimizing activation of the CaMKP-CaMKIIγ axis.

In summary, our study identifies ANDS as a specific small-molecule inhibitor of ANP32B-p53 interaction that selectively eliminates CML LSCs while sparing normal progenitors. We also demonstrate ANDS as part of a synergistic combination with KN93 that overcomes CaMKIIγ-mediated resistance. These in vitro and in vivo findings establish ANDS as a promising treatment strategy for both TKI-sensitive and -resistant CML.

Ethics declarations

All research described in this study complies with all relevant ethical regulations approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at SJTU-SM as the reference number (JUMC2023-121-A) of the ethical approval(s). For human research participants, we have obtained informed consent from all participants. The sex and/or gender was not considered in the study design and determined based on self-report. The patients’ information is listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Cell culture

HEK293T and HCT116 cells were kept in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium, while 32D, KU812, and K562 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 Medium; KBM5-T315I cells were grown in Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium. All cell lines were supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS, Sigma-Aldric) and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Additionally, 32D cells were cultured with 10 ng/mL mouse interleukin-3 (IL-3). All cell lines, except KBM5-T315I which was kindly provided by YingLi Wu in Shanghai Jiao Tong University, were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC).

Mice

To specifically delete Anp32b in HSCs, Anp32bfl/fl C57BL/6 mice were generated at the Shanghai Model Organisms Center and crossed with stem cell enhancer (Scl)-Cre C57BL/6 mice. Tamoxifen (10 mg/mL in oil) was administered via intraperitoneal injection at 50 μg/g daily for 21 days to activate Cre recombinase. Sex was not considered in the study design and analysis. All animal experiments were approved by the IACUC at SJTU-SM (ethical approval reference number: JUMC2023-121-A).

HTRF and HTS assay

Recombinant GST-His and GST-p53-CTD (aa301-393) were expressed using the pGEX-4T1 vector system and purified using GST beads. His-ANP32B-LCAR (aa163-251) was purified using the pET-32a vector system and followed by Ni-NTA-µSphere. GST-p53-CTD, His-ANP32B-LCAR, or GST-His quantified by Bicinchoninic Acid Assay (BCA, KIT-BCA01, Sino Biological) was incubated with Tb-anti-GST (UA BIOSCIENCE) and XL665-anti-His (UA BIOSCIENCE) in HTRF buffer (0.3% BSA, 0.1% Tween-20 in PBS) using OptiPlate-384 plates at 20 μL per well. After a 2-hour incubation at room temperature without light, signals at 620 and 665 nm were detected by a microplate reader (Spark). The interaction of ANP32B and p53 was indicated by the HTRF ratio (665 nm/620 nm). A series of 10 μM compounds from the Clinical Drug Library of SJTU in DMSO were co-incubated with the mixture followed by the HTRF-based HTS assay to assess the inhibition of compounds in ANP32B-p53 binding. Small molecule screening data are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

GST and streptavidin pull-down assay

Recombinant ANP32B was purified using the pET-32a vector system and His-tag was removed using sentrin-specific peptidase 2. For the GST pull-down assay, GST beads binding with GST or GST-p53-CTD were separately mixed with recombinant ANP32B and DMSO/compounds in binding buffer (0.3% BSA, 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 4 hours at 4 °C and separated complex.

For the streptavidin pull-down assay, streptavidin magnetic beads with biotin or biotin-ANDS were separately mixed with recombinant ANP32B and/or GST-p53-CTD under the same conditions. Endogenous streptavidin pull-down assays were performed using lysates from KU812 or K562 cells.

Immunoprecipitation

Cells were treated with a dose gradient ANDS (T2159, TargetMol, USA) for 48 hours. Flag-IP or endogenous IP utilized to assess the ANP32B-p53 binding, following separately mixed antibodies with cell lysate for 24 hours at 4 °C and separated complex. Antibodies for immunoprecipitation and western blot are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

CRISPR-CAS9 and luciferase assay

SgRNAs targeting ANP32A or ANP32B were cloned into the pLenti-U6-gRNA vector. ANP32A- or ANP32B-knockout cell clones were isolated after virus infection. The specific target sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Relative luciferase activity for p53 Luciferase Reporter Plasmid (Yeasen) was identified using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System Kit (Promega) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

CETSA

Cell lysate detection: KU812 cells in the logarithmic growth phase (approximately 1 × 106 cells/mL) were collected. 1 × 107 cells were re-suspended in PBS and repeated freeze-thaw cycles five times in a liquid nitrogen-37 °C water bath. The lysate was diluted to a final protein concentration of 2 mg/ml. Equal volumes (50 μL) of diluted lysate were incubated with 200 μM ANDS or DMSO for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by heating to a gradient from 39 to 58 °C. Additionally, the lysate was divided equally into tubes containing a dose gradient ANDS at 48 °C for 1 hour.

Intracellular determination: The intracellular experiment was consistent with the protocol published by Jafar et al.20. In particular, KU812 cells in the logarithmic growth phase (approximately 1 × 106 cells/ml) were treated with 5 μM ANDS (IC50 value of 4.637 μM) in the CO2 incubator for 1 hour at 37 °C. Equal volumes of cell suspension were striped with a designated temperature from 39 to 58 °C for 3 min and immediately snap-frozen. Cell suspension was repeated freeze-thaw cycles two times in a liquid nitrogen-37 °C water bath to acquired soluble protein fraction. Additionally, for isothermal dose-response fingerprint, cell suspension was divided equally into tubes after incubated with a dose gradient ANDS in the CO2 incubator for 1 hour at 37 °C and heated for 3 min at 48 °C.

For quantitative analysis, target protein bands were first normalized to the loading control (vinculin, Abclone) using ImageJ-based grayscale scanning. The thermal stability profile was then determined by calculating the soluble fraction at each temperature as a percentage relative to the 25 °C control (defined as 100%). The reported values (mean ± SD) were derived from the three independent biological replicates.

SPR

SPR were performed on a Biacore T200 instrument (Cytiva). Recombinant p53-CTD was immobilized on the CM5 chip (Cytiva) by amine coupling. Briefly, the cell was activated with 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) at 10 μL/min for 800 s. For immobilization, p53-CTD was diluted to 100 μg/mL in sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.0) and injected at 10 μL/min for 4700 s followed by blocking with ethanolamine at 10 μL/min for 800 s at 25 °C. A reference cell in parallel was using the same activation and blocking procedure but without protein. The running buffer was 1× PBS-P (pH 7.4), and immobilization was carried out using 1× EP buffer (0.01 M HEPES, 0.15 M NaCl, 0.003 M EDTA, 0.05% Surfactant P20, pH 7.4).

ANP32B-LCAR or ANDS was injected over immobilized p53-CTD at increasing concentrations at 30 μL/min, with an association time of 120 s and a dissociation time of 600 s with regenerated in 10 mM glycine-HCl (pH 1.5) for 30 s between each injection. To assess the effect of ANDS, the immobilized p53-CTD surface was first incubated with ANDS (5 μM) and then injected with increasing concentrations of ANP32B-LCAR under the same conditions. For competition SPR experiments, p53-CTD pre-incubated with a dose gradient ANDS in solution. p53-CTD binding to the surface immobilized ANP32B-LCAR will decrease and the signal of SPR will reduce because of the binding site occupied by ANDS in the solution.

Sensorgrams were collected using Biacore T200 Control Software v2.0 and analyzed using Biacore T200 Evaluation Software v2.0. Responses from the reference cell were subtracted from the ligand-coupled cell. Kinetic parameters were obtained by global fitting to a 1:1 Langmuir binding model where KD were derived.

Protein aggregation and ThT-binding assay

To assay the aggregation of p53 mutation, 200 nM recombinant p53-CTD mutation (p53-CTDWT, p53-CTD KR, p53-CTD KQ) purified as mentioned before was incubated at 37 °C with constant shaking in HEPES buffer (50 mM HEPES-KOH, pH7.4, 50 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2 and 2 mM DTT) and monitored by measuring the absorption at 600 nm in a microplate reader (Spark).

Quantitative aggregation kinetics were determined by ThT binding assay. 5 μM p53-CTD mutation was incubated at 37 °C with intermittent shaking (1-min shake–1-min rest cycle) in Tris-HCl buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl) with 25 μM ThT. ThT fluorescence was recorded every 15 min by a microplate reader (Spark).

Cell proliferation and apoptosis

Cells were treated with or without compounds, and proliferation was evaluated using cell counts at continuous times. At the last time point, cells were collected and stained with Annexin-V-APC and PI to perform apoptosis.

Bcr-Abl1WT or Bcr/Abl1T315I-induced murine CML model

10-12-week-old donor mice were injected with 5-Fluorouracil at 120 mg/kg via tail vein. 6 days post-injection, Lin- BM cells were sorted and infected with Bcr/Abl1WT or Bcr/Abl1T315I-internal ribosome entry site (IRES)-GFP virus. After secondary infection, 5 × 104 cells were collected and transplanted into lethally irradiated (3 + 3 Gry) C57BL/6 recipient mice. Secondary transplantation was performed using GFP+ leukemic cells isolated from primary Anp32b+/+ and Anp32b−/− CML mice. For therapy, ANDS and Imatinib (T1621, TargetMol, USA) were injected at 10 mg/kg or 100 mg/kg using intragastric administration, respectively, while KN93 (T2697, TargetMol, USA) was injected at 10 mg/kg using intramuscular. All drugs were injected twice daily. The IACUC at SJTU-SM declares that the maximal tumor size/burden was permitted 20 mm (or 4000 mg). We confirm that this limit was not exceeded. A predefined endpoint was set as wildtype GFP+ cells in PB > 50%. Mice were euthanized when the disease progressed to this level. Additionally, we confirm that other humane endpoint parameters, such as a body weight loss exceeding 20%, severe morbidity were strictly monitored and considered. Mice were euthanized if any of these humane endpoints were reached.

Homing assay

Homing assays were performed as described previously52. Briefly, a total of 5 × 106 Lin- BM cells were transduced twice with BCR/ABL1-IRES-GFP virus (with a 24-hour interval) and 2 hours later, they were transplanted into lethally irradiated C57BL/6 recipient mice. GFP+ cells were measured in the BM and spleens 18 hours post-transplantation by flow cytometric analysis (Beckman).

LDA

The GFP+ leukemic cells at five transplantation cell gradients were transplanted into recipient mice aged 6–8 weeks after X-ray irradiation. The survival time of the mice was recorded. The cell gradients and survival data were input into the L-Calc software developed by STEMCELL Technologies to calculate the frequency of LSCs.

Flow cytometry

BM cells were freshly isolated and filtered through a 40 µm strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension. PB cells were treated with ammonium chloride potassium lysis buffer to remove red PB cells. The cells were surface-stained using fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies. Cell apoptosis was analyzed using Annexin-V-PE and DAPI according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Antibodies and dyes are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Colony forming unit assays

BM or CD34+ cells from CML patients were cultured in StemSpan™ SFEM medium, supplemented with human stem cell factors (SCF: 50 ng/ml) and Thrombopoietin (TPO: 10 ng/ml). Cells were seeded into 12-well plates containing MethoCult H4436 (StemCell Technologies) at 200 cells/well. Bcr/Abl1WT-induced murine CML GFP+ BM cells were plated into 12-well plates containing complete MethoCult M3434 medium (StemCell Technologies) at 1000 cells/well. For some experiments, 5 µM Imatinib, ANDS, or KN93 was added to the culture medium. Colonies were counted at 7 or 10 days after plating. (All growth factors were from PeproTech.).

Western blot

Protein extracts were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred onto 0.45 μm nitrocellulose membrane (Millipore), blocked with 5% nonfat milk in PBS and sequentially incubated with the indicated primary antibodies followed by reacted with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-linked secondary antibody (Cell Signaling, Beverly, MA). The blots were visualized with ECL Detection Regent (SB-WB004, Share-bio, Shang Hai); For quantitative analysis, target protein bands were first normalized to the loading control (β-actin or vinculin) using ImageJ-based grayscale scanning. The relative protein quantity was then determined by calculating as a relative to the control (defined as 1.0). Relative densitometric values are provided below the blot images.

Quantitative RT-PCR

Primers for quantitative RT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Table S3. Gene expression (RQ = 2−∆∆Ct) was standardized to the level of reference genes RNA transcripts.

Statistical Analysis

A two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was used to compare specific pairs within multiple groups or between two experimental groups. Error bars indicate the mean ± SD. Two-way ANOVA was employed to compare the proliferation curves. Log-rank test was used to compare survival. Prism 8 and SPSS 20.0 were applied to conduct all statistical analyses. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.
