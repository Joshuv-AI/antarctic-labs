Acid-phase protection of carbon steel by lauramidopropyl amine oxide: evidenced by ICP-AES, electrochemical methods, severe-condition testing, and atomistic modeling
Authors: A. Modrek, Mostafa E. Salem, Mostafa A. A. Mahmoud, Ahmed Z. Ibrahim, Sherin A. M. Ali, Mohamed Abdel-Megid, Mokhtar Al-Abyadh, Ghada G. El-Bana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The corrosion-inhibition performance of commercial lauramidopropyl amine oxide (LAO) toward carbon steel in 0.5 M HCl was evaluated through a combined experimental and computational workflow. Dissolved metal release was quantified by ICP-AES, while potentiodynamic polarization (PDP), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and linear polarization resistance (LPR) were used to probe the interfacial electrochemical response at 293 K. LAO suppressed steel dissolution in a concentration-dependent manner. Based on ICP-AES, the inhibition efficiency increased from 76.5% at 50 ppm to 85.5% at 600 ppm. Electrochemical methods showed the same trend, and at 1200 ppm the inhibition efficiency reached 95.0%, 97.6%, and 97.5% according to PDP, EIS, and LPR, respectively. The small displacement in corrosion potential indicates mixed-type inhibition, whereas the marked increase in charge-transfer resistance and the decrease in double-layer capacitance support the formation of an adsorbed protective layer. Adsorption analysis was consistent with Langmuir-type behavior after converting the inhibitor concentration to molar active-LAO units and afforded ΔG°ads ≈ − 37.9 kJ mol− 1, indicating spontaneous and strong interfacial affinity, while the non-unity Langmuir slope indicates non-ideal adsorption. Temperature-dependent ICP-AES measurements between 293 and 333 K showed increasing corrosion rate with increasing temperature, but retained measurable inhibition at 150 ppm. In contrast, under severe gas-pressurized rotating-vessel conditions at 150 °C, 150 ppm LAO delivered only 27.9% inhibition, demonstrating that room-temperature efficacy does not translate directly to harsh service. To rationalize the adsorption tendency, density functional theory (B3LYP/6–31 + G(d, p)) and Monte Carlo simulations on Fe (110) were performed. The calculated descriptors (EHOMO = − 8.12 eV, ELUMO = − 2.72 eV, ΔE = 5.40 eV, dipole moment = 5.1 D) support favorable interfacial reactivity, while the Monte Carlo adsorption energy (− 175.27 kcal mol − 1) indicates strong adsorption on an idealized iron surface. Taken together, the data identify LAO as an efficient ambient-condition acid inhibitor for carbon steel, while also defining its limitations under high-temperature rotating-vessel conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-026-01861-7.

Introduction

Corrosion remains a persistent threat to steel assets exposed to acidic media in pickling, descaling, acid cleaning, and several oilfield operations. Carbon steel is still widely used in such environments because of its low cost, availability, and acceptable mechanical properties; however, in hydrochloric acid it dissolves rapidly and can suffer severe material loss if the interface is left unprotected. Accordingly, corrosion inhibitors remain one of the most practical mitigation tools for preserving asset integrity and controlling maintenance cost [1–3].

Organic inhibitors and surfactant-based formulations continue to attract considerable interest because their architectures can combine surface-active adsorption centers with hydrophobic fragments that restrict electrolyte access to the metal surface. In recent years, the field has moved beyond reporting inhibition efficiency alone and has increasingly emphasized mechanistic interpretation, interfacial robustness, and the agreement between electrochemical, morphological, and atomistic evidence. This shift is especially important in acid corrosion studies, where high room-temperature efficiency does not necessarily imply stability under severe service conditions [4–6].

Most contemporary corrosion-inhibitor papers focus on newly synthesized molecules. By contrast, commercially available surfactants are often used in practice but remain less thoroughly documented in the open literature, particularly when evaluated with a genuinely multi-technique workflow. Lauramidopropyl amine oxide (LAO) is attractive in this regard because it combines a polar amine-oxide functionality with a hydrophobic alkyl chain, suggesting that it may adsorb strongly and create a barrier-type interfacial film in acidic media [7, 8].

The present work therefore evaluates LAO not as a new-molecule discovery case, but as a practical commercial inhibitor whose operating window must be defined rigorously. Carbon steel was examined in 0.5 M HCl using ICP-AES, potentiodynamic polarization, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, linear polarization resistance, adsorption analysis, temperature-dependent corrosion testing, a severe gas-pressurized rotating-vessel protocol, and SEM. Density functional theory (DFT) and Monte Carlo simulations were further used to probe the adsorption tendency of LAO on Fe (110) [9–13].

The main contribution of the study lies in the way these datasets are interpreted together. The ambient-condition measurements show strong inhibition, whereas the severe rotating-vessel test reveals a pronounced decline in protection at 150 °C. The theoretical calculations rationalize the intrinsic adsorption capability of LAO on an idealized iron surface, while the harsh-condition test defines the practical boundary of that performance. This multiscale perspective is more informative than a room-temperature efficiency claim considered in isolation [14–17]. Compared with many corrosion-inhibitor studies that emphasize only room-temperature inhibition efficiency, the present work was designed to define both the performance and the limitation of a commercially available amine-oxide surfactant. The novelty is therefore not the synthesis of a new inhibitor molecule, but the operationally realistic evaluation of LAO through solution-phase metal release, electrochemical response, adsorption analysis, severe-condition testing, surface morphology, and atomistic modeling. This combined approach allows the high ambient-temperature efficiency of LAO to be interpreted together with its reduced performance under high-temperature rotating-vessel conditions, thereby avoiding overgeneralization from electrochemical data alone. In this sense, the study provides a practical performance boundary for a commercially relevant surfactant inhibitor rather than a simple maximum-efficiency claim.

Experimental

Materials and inhibitor solution

Low-carbon steel was used as the substrate for both immersion and electrochemical experiments. Working electrodes were prepared with an exposed area of 1 cm2, whereas the immersion coupons used for ICP-AES and rotating-vessel measurements had dimensions of 2.5 cm × 2.5 cm × 0.3 cm. Before each experiment, the steel surface was successively polished with silicon-carbide papers up to 1200 grit, degreased with ethanol, rinsed with deionized water, and dried.

The corrosive medium was 0.5 M HCl prepared from analytical-grade hydrochloric acid and deionized water. The inhibitor was a commercial lauramidopropyl amine oxide formulation containing 30 ± 2 wt% active LAO and was used as received. Unless otherwise stated, the inhibitor concentrations reported in ppm refer to the commercial formulation added to the acid solution. For adsorption calculations, these formulation-based concentrations were converted to molar active-LAO concentrations using the active matter fraction of 0.30 and the molecular weight of LAO, 300.48 g mol− 1. A concentrated LAO stock solution was prepared by accurately weighing the required amount of the commercial 30 ± 2 wt% LAO formulation and dissolving it directly in 0.5 M HCl under gentle stirring until a homogeneous solution was obtained. Working inhibitor solutions were then prepared by serial dilution of this stock solution with 0.5 M HCl to obtain the required formulation-based concentrations. Unless otherwise stated, the reported ppm values refer to the commercial LAO formulation added to the acid solution.

Because a preserved mill certificate, OES analysis, or XRF compositional report was not available in the archived dataset, the substrate is not assigned a detailed elemental composition in the present revision. This is a limitation of the reconstructed dataset. Therefore, the absolute corrosion-rate values should be compared cautiously with studies using composition-certified steels, whereas the within-study inhibition trends remain meaningful because all blank and inhibited experiments were conducted using the same steel source and identical preparation procedure.

ICP-AES measurements

Dissolved iron released from carbon steel into the electrolyte was quantified using a HORIBA Jobin Yvon ULTIMA2 radial-view ICP-AES instrument equipped with an autosampler. Carbon-steel coupons were immersed in 100 mL of uninhibited or inhibited electrolyte, and aliquots were withdrawn hourly over a 5 h immersion period.

The dissolved Fe concentration was determined using the analytical lines at 238 and 259.94 nm. The 259.94 nm line was used for adsorption and temperature analyses. Plots of dissolved iron per unit area versus immersion time were linearized, and the resulting slopes were converted to corrosion current density through Faraday’s law.

Electrochemical measurements

Electrochemical measurements were carried out using a Gamry Reference 600 potentiostat in a conventional three-electrode cell under static naturally aerated conditions at 293 ± 2 K. A platinum electrode served as the counter electrode and a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) as the reference electrode. All potentials are reported versus SCE [18].

Each experiment began with 30 min immersion at open-circuit potential, followed sequentially by LPR, EIS, and potentiodynamic polarization on the same electrode surface. LPR scans were recorded from − 20 to + 20 mV around Ecorr at 0.1 mV s− 1. EIS spectra were recorded from 100 kHz to 10 mHz using a sinusoidal perturbation of ± 10 mV. Potentiodynamic polarization curves were collected from − 500 to + 1500 mV at 1 mV s− 1. Inhibition efficiency was obtained from the standard current- and resistance-based expressions [19].

Adsorption and temperature analysis

Surface-coverage values (θ) were derived from ICP-AES inhibition efficiencies and fitted against common adsorption models. For adsorption analysis, formulation-based ppm values were converted to molar active-LAO concentrations using 30 wt% active matter and MW = 300.48 g mol− 1. The highest correlation was obtained for the Langmuir representation, C/θ = 1/Kads + C, and the standard free energy of adsorption was estimated from ΔG°ads = − RT ln(55.5Kads).

The temperature dependence of the corrosion process was examined by ICP-AES in blank 0.5 M HCl and in the presence of 150 ppm LAO over the range 293–333 K. Activation parameters were estimated from Arrhenius and transition-state relationships using the corrosion-rate data.

Severe rotating-vessel test and SEM

To assess inhibitor stability under considerably harsher conditions, blank 0.5 M HCl and 0.5 M HCl containing 150 ppm LAO were tested in a gas-pressurized rotating stainless-steel vessel at 150 °C. Fresh carbon-steel coupons were exposed for 2–5 h and the corrosion response was evaluated by weight loss. The available archived severe-condition dataset contains the 150 ppm test only; therefore, higher LAO concentrations such as 600 or 1200 ppm could not be reconstructed for this revision. The 150 ppm concentration was retained because it represents an intermediate practical dose within the investigated concentration range and allows direct comparison with the temperature-dependent ICP-AES experiment performed at the same concentration. This limitation is explicitly acknowledged, and the severe-condition result is interpreted as defining the limited robustness of 150 ppm LAO rather than the maximum possible performance of LAO under high-temperature conditions.

The archived severe-condition record did not preserve the gas type, pressure, or rotation speed; therefore, these parameters could not be reconstructed reliably. This limitation is acknowledged, and the severe-condition test is interpreted as a preserved comparative dataset rather than a fully parameterized operating protocol.

After immersion, the carbon-steel specimens were removed from the test solution, gently rinsed with deionized water to remove loosely attached corrosion products, degreased with ethanol, and dried at room temperature. The dried surfaces were then examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Representative micrographs were collected from the blank and inhibited specimens to compare the extent of acid-induced surface damage and the morphological effect of LAO addition.

Computational methods

Quantum-chemical calculations were performed for the neutral LAO molecule using density functional theory at the B3LYP/6–31 + G(d, p) level after full geometry optimization and frequency analysis. The absence of imaginary frequencies was used to confirm that the optimized geometry corresponded to a local minimum. Frontier orbital energies, dipole moment, ionization potential, electron affinity, hardness, softness, chemical potential, electronegativity, and electrophilicity index were calculated from the optimized structure using the standard Koopmans-type relationships. The calculations were used as qualitative molecular-reactivity descriptors and not as direct quantitative predictors of inhibition efficiency.

Monte Carlo simulations were conducted using simulated annealing to examine the adsorption tendency of LAO on the Fe (110) surface. The Fe (110) slab was selected as a representative low-index iron surface commonly used in corrosion-inhibitor modeling. The COMPASS force field was employed, electrostatic interactions were treated by Ewald summation, and van der Waals interactions were treated using an atom-based summation method. Adsorption energy, rigid adsorption energy, deformation energy, and total energy were extracted to describe the stability of the adsorbed configuration. Because the simulation represents adsorption on an idealized iron surface, the results are interpreted as qualitative support for interfacial affinity rather than as a complete representation of the real acid-exposed carbon-steel interface [20–23].

Reporting transparency and archival limitations

The archived source package preserves processed ICP-AES curves, electrochemical responses, fitted parameter tables, SEM images, and atomistic-modeling outputs used in this manuscript. However, replicate-level raw files and instrument-export uncertainty summaries were not retained in the archived record used for the present reconstruction. Consequently, the numerical values reported in the main tables are presented as representative determinations from the archived dataset rather than as full mean ± SD datasets.

For this reason, the revised manuscript avoids statistical ranking among closely spaced concentrations and instead emphasizes internally consistent trends observed across independent techniques. The selected data were not chosen to maximize inhibition efficiency; rather, they correspond to the preserved experimental series available for ICP-AES, PDP, EIS, LPR, temperature testing, severe-condition testing, SEM, DFT, and Monte Carlo analysis. This limitation is disclosed explicitly to prevent overstatement of numerical precision and to distinguish concentration-dependent trends from statistically resolved differences.

All figures were regenerated or reformatted to improve readability and accessibility. Axis titles, physical units, panel labels, and legends were revised throughout the manuscript and Supporting Information. Line plots were exported at high resolution, with larger fonts and clearer curve identification. Where applicable, captions were expanded to state the material, corrosive medium, inhibitor concentration, temperature, and the principal conclusion supported by the figure.

ICP-AES evaluation of dissolved iron

ICP-AES directly tracked the accumulation of dissolved iron in the corrosive medium and therefore provided an independent solution-phase measure of steel dissolution. Representative calibration plots for the Fe 259.94 nm line are provided in the Supporting Information (Figures S1 and S2).

Increasing LAO concentration suppressed the dissolved-iron concentration and reduced the calculated corrosion current density relative to the blank [24]. The corresponding dissolved-iron profiles are presented in Fig. 1, and the derived ICP-AES corrosion parameters are summarized in Table 2.

The inhibition efficiency increased from 76.5% at 50 ppm to 85.5% at 600 ppm. A shallow plateau was observed between 150 and 250 ppm, but the overall trend remained strongly inhibitory. This behavior is consistent with progressive surface adsorption of the surfactant and partial blocking of the most active dissolution sites [25].

Potentiodynamic polarization behavior

Potentiodynamic polarization confirmed that LAO decreases both anodic metal dissolution and cathodic hydrogen-evolution currents. The corrosion current density decreased from 125.0 µA cm− 2 in blank acid to 6.2 µA cm− 2 at 1200 ppm LAO, whereas Ecorr shifted only slightly from − 474 to − 469 mV versus SCE. The polarization curves are shown in Fig. 2, and the extracted electrochemical parameters are listed in Table 3.

Because the displacement in Ecorr remained well below the common 85 mV criterion, LAO is best classified as a mixed-type inhibitor under the present conditions. The dominant effect is therefore adsorption-driven suppression of both partial reactions rather than selective poisoning of only one pathway. The polarization efficiency reached 95.0% at 1200 ppm [26, 27].

The Tafel slopes also provide useful mechanistic information. In the blank acid, βa and βc were 94 and 107 mV dec− 1, respectively, indicating active anodic iron dissolution coupled with cathodic hydrogen evolution. After LAO addition, both anodic and cathodic branches shifted toward lower current densities, confirming that LAO retards both partial reactions. The cathodic Tafel slope increased more markedly than the anodic slope at low and intermediate concentrations, suggesting that LAO adsorption strongly affects the hydrogen-evolution process, probably by reducing the number of active cathodic sites available for proton discharge. The anodic slopes also decreased from 94 mV dec− 1 in blank acid to 66–85 mV dec− 1 in inhibited solutions, indicating suppression of iron dissolution through surface blocking. Because the Ecorr displacement remained below 85 mV, these changes support mixed-type inhibition rather than a purely anodic or purely cathodic mechanism.

The simultaneous suppression of anodic and cathodic current densities, together with the small displacement in Ecorr, supports mixed-type inhibition by LAO.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy

The impedance data closely support the polarization results. The Nyquist and Bode responses are presented in Fig. 3, while the fitted impedance parameters are compiled in Table 4. The spectra were fitted using the equivalent circuit Rs–(CPEdl∥Rct), where Rs is the solution resistance, Rct is the charge-transfer resistance, and CPEdl is a constant phase element used to describe the non-ideal capacitive response of the heterogeneous steel/electrolyte interface. The use of a CPE rather than an ideal capacitor is justified by the depressed capacitive loops and by the CPE exponent values below unity. The equivalent circuit is provided in the Supporting Information as Figure S5 to improve the reproducibility of the fitting procedure [28].

The charge-transfer resistance increased from 87 Ω cm² in blank acid to 3666 Ω cm² at 1200 ppm LAO, while the CPE exponent remained below unity, confirming non-ideal interfacial behavior (Table 4). The CPE parameter Y0 is reported in Ω−1 sn cm− 2, not in F cm− 2. The double-layer capacitance, Cdl, was calculated from the CPE parameters using the relationship Cdl = (Y0Rct1−n)1/n. The progressive increase in Rct with inhibitor concentration indicates that LAO adsorption reduces the active surface area available for charge transfer and forms a barrier layer that impedes corrosion reactions [28, 29].

Although a single time-constant circuit was sufficient to describe the preserved spectra, the inhibited interface should not be interpreted as containing only bare charge-transfer resistance. Rather, the fitted Rct is an apparent interfacial resistance that includes the blocking effect of the adsorbed LAO film within the selected equivalent-circuit model. This interpretation is more cautious than assigning the entire resistance solely to charge transfer and is consistent with the decrease in Cdl caused by replacement of interfacial water molecules by adsorbed organic species [29]. The increasing arc diameter and the rise in low-frequency impedance indicate progressively stronger interfacial blocking with increasing inhibitor concentration.

Linear polarization resistance

LPR measurements exhibited the same concentration-dependent behavior, with Rp increasing from 580 Ω cm2 at 50 ppm to 3381 Ω cm2 at 1200 ppm LAO and the inhibition efficiency rising from 85.4% to 97.5% [30]. Representative LPR curves are shown in Fig. 4, and the derived parameters are summarized in Table 5.

The agreement between LPR, EIS, polarization, and ICP-AES is important because each technique interrogates the corrosion process through a different response function. The combined dataset therefore provides strong internal coherence for the conclusion that LAO affords substantial protection to carbon steel in 0.5 M HCl at ambient temperature [31].

Adsorption thermodynamics

Surface-coverage values derived from ICP-AES inhibition efficiencies were used to examine the adsorption behavior of LAO on carbon steel in 0.5 M HCl. Because the inhibitor was supplied as a 30 wt% commercial formulation, the formulation-based ppm concentrations were converted to molar active-LAO concentrations before calculating the adsorption constant. The Langmuir representation, C/θ = 1/Kads + C, gave a high regression coefficient, indicating Langmuir-type concentration dependence of surface coverage. The Langmuir adsorption relationship is shown in Fig. 5, and the thermodynamic parameters are listed in Table 6 [32].

Additional adsorption-isotherm trials, including Temkin, Freundlich, and Frumkin representations, are summarized in the Supporting Information (Table S1). Among the tested models, the Langmuir representation gave the highest regression coefficient (R² = 0.9998), supporting its use as the main empirical adsorption model, although the non-unity slope confirms deviation from ideal Langmuir behavior.

However, the Langmuir slope was not exactly unity, demonstrating that the adsorption process should not be described as an ideal Langmuir monolayer. The non-unity slope may arise from surface heterogeneity, lateral interaction between adsorbed LAO species, changes in adsorption orientation, or the use of a commercial surfactant formulation rather than a single purified molecule. Therefore, the Langmuir fit is used here as an empirical adsorption representation rather than proof of ideal monolayer adsorption. After concentration conversion, Kads was recalculated as approximately 1.02 × 10⁵ L mol− 1 and ΔG°ads as approximately − 37.9 kJ mol− 1 at 293 K. This value indicates spontaneous and strong adsorption with mixed physical/chemical character, but it should be interpreted cautiously because of the non-ideal Langmuir slope [33].

Effect of temperature

Temperature-dependent ICP-AES measurements for the blank and for 150 ppm LAO showed that corrosion accelerated as the solution temperature increased from 293 to 333 K. This is expected for acid dissolution of carbon steel and reflects faster interfacial kinetics and mass transport [34]. The Arrhenius and transition-state plots are presented in Fig. 6, and the derived activation parameters are summarized in Table 7.

The activation parameters reveal an important mechanistic nuance. The inhibited solution showed a lower apparent activation energy than the blank (75.09 versus 81.63 kJ mol− 1), together with a negative entropy of activation. In practical terms, these values are consistent with the formation of an organized activated state and with the persistence of adsorption-mediated protection over the studied range. At the same time, the decrease in Ea does not imply complete coverage of all active sites and therefore does not contradict the continued occurrence of corrosion in the inhibited system [35].

Severe gas-pressurized rotating-vessel performance

The most important practical result of the study is that the strong ambient-condition performance of LAO did not carry over to the severe rotating-vessel test. At 150 °C, the inhibited system exhibited only 27.9% inhibition relative to the blank over the 2–5 h interval. The severe-condition weight-loss response is shown in Fig. 7, with the corresponding values compiled in Table 8.

Schematic illustrations of the high-temperature, high-pressure rotating-vessel arrangement and the stainless-steel pressurized vessel are provided in the Supporting Information (Figures S3 and S4).

It should be emphasized that the severe-condition test was performed only at 150 ppm in the preserved dataset. Therefore, the result does not exclude the possibility that higher LAO concentrations or formulated blends could perform better under the same high-temperature rotating conditions. The conclusion drawn here is narrower: 150 ppm LAO, despite being effective under ambient conditions, is insufficient as a stand-alone inhibitor under the tested severe condition.

This result materially changes how the inhibitor should be interpreted. LAO is not adequately described as a universally high-performance inhibitor simply because it exhibits high inhibition efficiencies at 293 K. Instead, the data define an operating envelope: the inhibitor is effective in ambient acidic exposure, but it loses a large fraction of its protective power in the much harsher high-temperature rotating-vessel environment. From a publication standpoint, this negative result is not a weakness when discussed honestly; it is one of the most informative aspects of the study because it prevents overgeneralization from room-temperature electrochemistry alone [36, 37].

Surface morphology by SEM

SEM observations qualitatively corroborated the electrochemical conclusions obtained at ambient temperature. In the blank acid, the steel surface exhibited severe deterioration consistent with active acid attack, whereas the inhibited surface retained a substantially less damaged morphology in the presence of 150 ppm LAO [38]. Representative SEM micrographs are presented in Fig. 8.

The SEM evidence is used here as morphological support rather than direct chemical proof of LAO-film composition. Because no preserved SEM-EDX, FTIR, Raman, or XPS dataset was available for the reconstructed manuscript, the presence of an inhibitor-derived layer is inferred from the combined decrease in dissolved Fe release, decrease in corrosion current, increase in interfacial resistance, decrease in Cdl, and improved surface morphology. This limitation has been stated explicitly to avoid overinterpreting SEM images alone [39]. The blank surface shows severe acid-induced deterioration, whereas the inhibited surface is comparatively less damaged, supporting adsorption-mediated protection under ambient conditions.

DFT-based electronic descriptors of LAO

The optimized LAO structure exhibits a polar amine-oxide-containing headgroup attached to a hydrophobic alkyl chain, a combination that is intrinsically compatible with adsorption-driven corrosion inhibition. The frontier orbital and reactivity descriptors derived from DFT support this interpretation. The HOMO energy of − 8.12 eV indicates an electron-donating tendency toward vacant iron orbitals, whereas the LUMO energy of − 2.72 eV indicates that the molecule can also participate in back-donation interactions. The HOMO–LUMO energy gap of 5.40 eV is consistent with a molecule that is sufficiently reactive to interact with the surface without being so unstable as to undermine film formation [40]. The calculated quantum-chemical descriptors are summarized in Table 9.

The dipole moment of 5.10 D indicates pronounced molecular polarity and supports favorable electrostatic interaction with the charged steel/solution interface. The recalculated global descriptors are internally consistent with the frontier orbital energies: I = 8.12 eV, A = 2.72 eV, η = 2.70 eV, σ = 0.37 eV− 1, χ = 5.42 eV, µ = −5.42 eV, and ω = 5.44 eV. These descriptors support the ability of LAO to interact with the metal surface through the polar amine-oxide headgroup, although they should be interpreted as qualitative reactivity indicators rather than direct predictors of inhibition efficiency [41].

Monte Carlo adsorption on Fe(110)

Monte Carlo simulations on Fe (110) showed a strongly favorable adsorption configuration for LAO. The calculated adsorption energy was − 175.27 kcal mol− 1, while the rigid adsorption energy was − 180.23 kcal mol− 1 with a modest deformation penalty of 4.96 kcal mol− 1. Such values indicate a strong thermodynamic driving force for spontaneous adsorption on the idealized iron surface [42]. The optimized adsorption configurations are illustrated in Fig. 9, and the Monte Carlo adsorption descriptors are listed in Table 10.

The optimized configuration places the inhibitor nearly parallel to the Fe (110) plane, allowing the polar headgroup to interact strongly with the metal while the hydrocarbon chain spreads across the surface and contributes to site coverage. This geometry is fully consistent with the large increase in charge-transfer resistance and the strong room-temperature inhibition observed experimentally [43].

Integrative mechanistic interpretation

When the experimental and theoretical datasets are considered together, a coherent mechanistic picture emerges. LAO contains a polar amine-oxide functionality capable of strong interaction with the iron surface and a hydrocarbon chain that promotes lateral coverage. At ambient temperature, this combination produces substantial suppression of acid corrosion, as independently confirmed by ICP-AES, polarization, EIS, LPR, adsorption thermodynamics, and SEM [44].

The computational results reinforce this interpretation by showing that LAO is both electronically reactive and strongly adsorbing on Fe (110). However, the rotating-vessel result also demonstrates why atomistic adsorption calculations must be interpreted with discipline [45]. The DFT and Monte Carlo models describe adsorption on an idealized iron surface and rationalize intrinsic affinity, but they do not capture dynamic desorption, acid-assisted film degradation, hydrodynamic shear, competitive adsorption, or thermally accelerated interfacial restructuring at 150 °C. The most defensible conclusion is therefore not that theory and experiment disagree, but that they illuminate different parts of the same problem.

This multiscale interpretation increases the practical value of the study. The work identifies LAO as a viable ambient-condition acid inhibitor and, at the same time, defines the boundary beyond which the present stand-alone formulation is no longer sufficiently robust. That distinction is scientifically and industrially more informative than a simple high-efficiency claim.

Comparison with related surfactant-type corrosion inhibitors

The performance of LAO was compared with selected surfactant-type and amphiphilic organic inhibitors previously reported for carbon steel or mild steel in acidic chloride media. The comparison is provided in the Supporting Information (Table S2). This comparison is not intended as an absolute ranking because electrolyte concentration, temperature, steel composition, exposure time, and measurement method differ among studies. Nevertheless, it places LAO within the range of high-efficiency surfactant-based inhibitors under ambient acid conditions. In the present work, LAO reached 95.0–97.6% inhibition at 1200 ppm under electrochemical testing in 0.5 M HCl, which is comparable to several reported surfactant-based inhibitors [3, 5, 12, 14]. However, the severe-condition result at 150 °C showed only 27.9% inhibition at 150 ppm, highlighting that ambient-condition performance should not be extrapolated directly to harsher service conditions without additional high-temperature validation (Table S2) [36, 37].

Conclusions

Commercial lauramidopropyl amine oxide markedly decreased the corrosion of carbon steel in 0.5 M HCl under ambient conditions. At 293 K, inhibition efficiency increased with inhibitor concentration, reaching 85.5% by ICP-AES at 600 ppm and 95.0–97.6% by PDP, EIS, and LPR at 1200 ppm.

Potentiodynamic polarization identified LAO as a mixed-type inhibitor because both anodic and cathodic current densities decreased and the Ecorr displacement remained below 85 mV. EIS and LPR confirmed strong growth in interfacial resistance with increasing inhibitor concentration, with Rct increasing from 87 Ω cm² in blank acid to 3666 Ω cm² at 1200 ppm LAO.

Adsorption analysis, after conversion of formulation ppm to molar active-LAO concentration, was consistent with Langmuir-type behavior and gave Kads ≈ 1.02 × 10⁵ L mol− 1 and ΔG°ads ≈ − 37.9 kJ mol− 1. The non-unity Langmuir slope indicates that adsorption is not ideally Langmuirian and may involve surface heterogeneity, lateral interactions, or concentration-dependent molecular orientation.

Recalculated DFT descriptors and Monte Carlo simulations support the experimentally inferred adsorption tendency of LAO. The polar amine-oxide headgroup and hydrophobic alkyl chain favor interfacial anchoring and surface coverage, although the computational results should be interpreted as qualitative support for adsorption affinity on an idealized Fe (110) surface.

The practical limitation of LAO became clear under severe gas-pressurized rotating-vessel conditions at 150 °C, where 150 ppm LAO achieved only 27.9% inhibition. Therefore, LAO should be regarded as an effective ambient-condition inhibitor for hydrochloric-acid exposure, but not as a stand-alone high-temperature formulation without further optimization or higher-dose/formulation testing.

Overall, the main contribution of this work is an operationally realistic assessment of LAO in which room-temperature electrochemistry, ICP-AES metal-release monitoring, adsorption analysis, severe-condition testing, SEM morphology, and atomistic modeling are interpreted together with explicit recognition of the dataset limitations.
