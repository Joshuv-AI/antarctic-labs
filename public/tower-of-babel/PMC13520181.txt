Comparison of wear behavior of 3D printed permanent resin, milled zirconia, and primary tooth enamel on antagonist primary canines: in vitro study
Authors: Hacer Nida Akdogan, Nazli Aydin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundData comparing the wear of 3D printed permanent resin (PR) and milled zirconia (Z) to that of primary tooth enamel are limited. This study aimed to investigate the wear behavior of two materials and their effect on antagonist primary canine, in comparison with primary tooth enamel.MethodsMilled monolithic Z (IPS e.max ZirCAD Prime Esthetic, Ivoclar), and PR (Permanent Crown Resin, Formlabs), and primary central incisor enamel (PCIE) (per group n = 15) as the control group were included in the study. Z and PR were polished by intraoral polishing set (Optragloss, Ivoclar Vivadent). Two-body wear tests were performed in a chewing simulator for all specimens, with primary canines used as antagonists (50 N, 1.2 × 10⁵ cycles, 1.56 Hz, 5/55°C). Wear volume and wear depth were assessed by overlapping the data before and after the chewing test with a software program (Geomagic Control X). Vickers hardness tests were performed (n = 10) for both materials. Data were analyzed statistically with one-way ANOVA (p < 0.05).ResultsFor each group, n = 9 specimens were able to complete 120,000 cycles. The results showed that the PR exhibited the highest volume loss, while Z had the lowest wear depth (p < 0.05). When we examined the antagonist teeth, the highest volume loss was observed in the Z group (p < 0.05), but there was no significant difference in wear depth (p = 0.079). The Vickers hardness of Z was significantly higher than that of the PR (p < 0.05).ConclusionBoth highly polished monolithic Z and PR demonstrated comparable antagonist wear to that of PCIE; however, while PR showed a similar wear resistance to PCIE, Z exhibited the highest resistance overall.Clinical significancePR demonstrated wear resistance similar to primary tooth enamel under the conditions of this study, suggesting it may be a suitable option for pediatric restorations where antagonist wear is a primary concern. However, additional properties require further evaluation.Keywords: 3D printed permanent resin, Milled monolithic zirconia, Primary tooth, Wear

Background

Pediatric crowns are indicated to provide esthetics and chewing function in the restoration of primary teeth with extensive crown damage, pulp therapy, traumatic dental injuries, and developmental defects [1, 2].

Although stainless steel crowns have been the first choice for crown treatment of primary teeth for many years, they cannot meet aesthetic expectations [2–5]. The increasing demand for aesthetic restoration of primary teeth by both parents and children has led to the development of various aesthetic crown treatments [2, 5, 6]. With the developing technology and materials, aesthetic alternatives such as composite strip crowns, prefabricated zirconia crowns (PZC), zirconia (Z), and resin crowns produced with computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD-CAM), and three-dimensional (3D) printed permanent resin (PR) have begun to be used [5, 7–9].

Using CAD-CAM methods, single crowns, inlays, and onlays for permanent teeth can be fabricated [10]. CAD-CAM crowns for primary teeth, fabricated directly from solid blocks of Z, have been proposed as an alternative to PZCs [8, 11]. It has been shown that by custom manufacturing with CAD-CAM Z crowns for primary teeth, the challenges associated with PZCs, particularly in terms of aggressive tooth preparation, placement, and fracture resistance, can be overcome [8, 11, 12]. However, while the advantages of CAD-CAM systems are obvious, they also have disadvantages such as a significant amount of wasted material during the milling process and the inability to effectively reuse or repurpose this [13, 14]. Recently introduced Z materials containing 4–5 mol% yttria (4Y/5Y-PSZ: partially stabilized zirconia) have become popular as an aesthetic option, offering greater translucency with a higher content of cubic crystalline phases [15].

An additive manufacturing technique that involves depositing material in successive layers to create a 3D object can be used as an alternative to CAD-CAM manufacturing. This process involves adding materials to build the desired product layer by layer [16, 17]. With additive manufacturing, there is ease of production, time saving, and reduces material waste [18]. This manufacturing method is widely used in dentistry for the production of metal frameworks, models, and temporary restorations [19]. Recently, aesthetic pediatric dental crowns have been produced using this technique [9, 17, 20, 21].

Occlusion and continuous biting forces cause wear on both teeth and restorative material [3]. Wear on teeth depends on enamel strength, enamel and dentin thickness, and biting force [22]. In a study, it was found that the Vickers hardness number (VHN) of primary tooth enamel was 106 VHN and the VHN of permanent tooth enamel was 126 VHN [22]. It has also been reported that primary tooth enamel is less mineralized and has less enamel thickness than permanent tooth [23]. All these factors indicate that primary tooth enamel is more susceptible to wear than permanent tooth. Because primary tooth enamel is less resistant to wear than permanent tooth enamel, the selection of dental materials for primary tooth restorations becomes quite challenging [3, 24]. Therefore, the selection of materials that mimic the wear of natural teeth is important for the clinical success of primary tooth restorations and antagonist primary tooth [3, 25]. Considering all these, when restorations are made with materials that mimic primary tooth wear, the occlusal vertical dimension is preserved throughout growth and development [24].

Advances in digital dental systems and restorative materials have profoundly impacted pediatric dentistry. However, there are limited studies evaluating the wear relationship between 3D printed resins and primary tooth. These studies lack a comprehensive understanding of thermal cycling and polishing procedures and lack a control group that mimics natural tooth wear [7, 9]. Furthermore, to our knowledge, there is no study in the literature evaluating the wear relationship between 4 and 5 mol% Y-TZP based monolithic Z and primary tooth enamel. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the wear behavior of Z, PR, and primary tooth enamel as well as the antagonist primary canines. The null hypothesis of this study was that there was no difference in the wear amounts between Z, PR, and primary tooth enamel as well as in the antagonist primary canines.

Methods

This study was intended as in vitro research and was conducted in the laboratory of the Faculty of Dentistry at Cukurova University. The Noninvasive Clinical Research Ethical Committee of Cukurova University authorized this study with the number 23/146, 26.07.2024 in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration. Before the tooth extraction, the parents were informed about the use of their children’s teeth for research purposes, and their consent was obtained. According to our power analysis (G*Power; Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf), setting the power at 0.95, the significance level (α) at 0.05, and the effect size at 0.97, a total of 21 samples, 7 specimens per group, were determined to be necessary [26]. A total of 45 specimens were used (n = 15). This decision was made to increase the robustness and statistical power of the findings and to account for potential specimen loss. Randomization was performed using a web-based, free tool (http://www.randomizer.org) to eliminate bias across groups in specimen selection. Preparation of teeth

This study was executed with 45 recently extracted, caries free human primary canines and 15 primary upper central incisors that fell because of physiological resorption, orthodontic treatment planning, over-retention. The teeth were preserved in a 0.5% chloramine-T solution prior to the test. Before the experimental procedures, the teeth were examined, and those with worn and cracks were excluded. The pulp chambers of the teeth were filled with flowable composite. As antagonists, the canine teeth with the cusps exposed were embedded in screw hole with cyanoacrylate. Each primary canine was used as an antagonist only once to avoid confounding from cumulative wear. And upper centrals were embedded in acrylic resin (Imicryl Dental, Konya, Turkey) (Ø25 × 30-mm) with their labial surfaces exposed as control group (Fig. 2). The heat that was generated during the polymerization process was dissipated by submerging the acrylic blocks in water.

One sample of the antagonist tooth and each of the test specimens. (from left, antagonist primary canine tooth, 3D printed permanent resin: PR, Zirconia: Z, primary central incisor enamel: PCIE)

Preparation of tested materials

Using CAD software (Tinkercad, Autodesk, San Rafael, CA, USA), a cylinder (Ø11 × 13-mm) for wear test and another cylinder (Ø10 × 3-mm) for hardness tests was designed and exported as a standard tessellation language (STL) file. According to both STL files, 15 wear test cylinders and 10 hardness test cylinders were printed (Form 3B, Formlabs, Somerville, MA, USA) using PR (Permanent Crown, Formlabs, Somerville, MA, USA), and layer thickness was 50 μm. After the printing was complete, the cylinders were immersed in isopropyl alcohol (IPA ≥ 99%) for three minutes in Form Wash (Formlabs, Somerville, MA, USA). Then, they were cured in Form Cure (Formlabs, Somerville, MA, USA) at 60 °C for 20 min. After the supports had been eliminated, a second cycle of ultraviolet (UV) curing lasting twenty minutes was carried out.

According to both STL file, 15 wear test cylinders and 10 hardness tests cylinders were milled from a Z block (IPS e.max ZirCAD Prime, Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein) with a CAM unit (inLab MCX5, Dentsply Sirona, York, PA, USA) and then sintered (inFire HTC Speed, Dentsply Sirona, York, PA, USA) using the standard sintering protocol, involving a 9-hour 50 min duration across 4 stages as per the manufacturer’s recommendations. Both printed and milled cylinders were polished with silicon carbide papers of 800 to 1000 grit, then dark, light blue, and spiral rubber (Optragloss, Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein). The polishing time was measured from the initial contact of each wheel with the specimen and lasted 60 s by a single operator to minimize variability. Wear test

To mimic the physiological conditions of human mastication as closely as possible, an in-vitro two-body wear test was conducted in a chewing simulator (MOD Chewing Simulator; MOD Dental, Ankara, Turkey). Antagonist canine teeth were placed on a top support, while the material specimens were fixed at the bottom with a self-curing acrylic resin (Imicryl Dental, Konya, Turkey) (Fig. 2). Tests were performed with a load of 50 N, repetitions of 1.56 Hz, lateral motion of 0.7 mm, vertical motion of 5 mm, lateral speed of 30 mm/s and vertical speed of 30 mm/s, and 120,000 chewing cycles with a thermal cycling between 5 and 55 °C (dwell time 60 s).

Before the wear test, materials and antagonist canine teeth’s 3D data were obtained using a 3D scanner (Solutionix c500; Medit Corp., Korea) and recorded. After the wear test, again, materials and antagonist canine teeth’s 3D data were obtained by the same device and recorded. Pre-wear and post-wear scan data were overlapped to determine the wear volume loss (mm3) and wear depth (mm) with a software program (Geomagic Control X; 3D Systems Inc., USA) as seen in Fig. 3. Wear depth was measured as the maximum vertical distance between the pre-wear and post-wear surfaces at the point of antagonist contact. Wear volume was calculated as the total volume difference between the superimposed scans.

Vickers hardness (VH) test

A VH test was performed on the cylinder shaped (Ø10 × 3-mm) specimens by using a hardness tester (Buehler MMT 3 digital micro-hardness tester, Lake Bluff) based on the literature [27, 28]. A 300 gf was applied at the polished surface for a dwell time of 15 s for hardness measurements. A microprocessor calculated the hardness values and presented the findings using the measurements made with the device. Each specimen had a total of 3 indentations done at certain areas, and the average of these 3 measurements provided the hardness of each specimen. The mean of each group was then calculated.

Statistical analysis

Statistical evaluation was performed with a software program (IBM SPSS Statistics, v20.0; IBM Corp). All data was descriptively analyzed. Of the initial 45 samples (15 per group), 9 per group completed 120,000 cycles without failure. Of the 18 excluded specimens: 5 A-Z specimens and 2 A-PR specimens were fractured; 1 A-Z specimen, 2 A-PR specimens, and 3 A-PCIE specimens were dislodged from their mounting location; and 3 PCIE specimens showed wear marks on the acrylic instead of the PCIE due to positional deviation. These criteria (specimen fracture, mounting dislodgement, positional deviation causing contact with the wrong surface) were established in the study protocol prior to data collection. The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to evaluate the normality, and the Levene test was used for measuring the homogeneity of the variables. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was used for the wear volume of material, followed by the post hoc Tukey test, and for the wear depth of material, followed by the post-hoc Dunnett T3. A one-way ANOVA test was used for the effect of materials on wear volume and depth of antagonist canine teeth, followed by the post-hoc Tukey test, and the t-test was used for the Vickers hardness (α = 0.05).

Results

The descriptive statistics (mean and standard deviation) for the volume loss and wear depth values are presented in Tables 2 and 3. For the material volume and depth values, normal distribution of the data was verified (Shapiro-Wilk test). Homogeneity of variance was assumed for the volume (p = 0.212) but was violated for the depth (p < 0.001). Statistically significant differences in the wear volume loss of material (F(2,24) = 9.704, p < 0.05) and wear depth of material values (F(2,24) = 14.272, p < 0.001) were obtained. The highest mean wear volume loss of material was observed in the PR (0.312 ± 0.14 mm3), and the lowest value was recorded for the Z group (0.075 ± 0.067 mm3). The highest mean wear depth of material value was observed in the PR (0.199 ± 0.116 mm), and the lowest value was recorded for the Z group (0.008 ± 0.003 mm). Post-hoc analysis revealed that PR and PCIE formed a homogeneous subset for wear depth (p > 0.05), while Z showed significantly lower wear depth than both (p < 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

Tooth wear is a multifactorial process that can cause dentin hypersensitivity, functional and aesthetic issues, and loss of vertical dimension, potentially leading to temporomandibular disorders, overeruption, and traumatic occlusion. Moreover, dental materials with different wear properties can affect the wear rates of antagonist natural teeth and it is important for the clinical success of primary tooth restoration [29–31]. In this study, the wear volume and depth of PCIE, Z, and PR, as well as the antagonist primary canines, were evaluated after the wear test. The null hypothesis of the study was that there was no difference between the wear amounts of Z, PR, and primary tooth enamel as well as in the antagonist primary canines. According to the wear volume results of the materials, there was no difference between the wear on the Z and PCIE, while both were significantly less worn than the PR. When the wear depth results of the materials were examined, there was no difference between the wear depths between the PCIE and the PR, but they were significantly greater than the Z. According to the wear volume results on the antagonist teeth, although A-Z experienced significantly more volume loss than A-PR, A-Z, and A-PR showed similar wear to A-PCIE. There was no statistically significant difference in antagonist wear depth among groups (p = 0.079), although the value approached significance. Therefore, the null hypothesis was rejected, except for the wear depth observed between the antagonist primary canine teeth.

The sample size of the study was calculated as 21, with n = 7. In previous studies, the number of samples per group varied between 3 and 10 [3, 7, 9, 24, 26, 32–34]. Since primary canine teeth were used as antagonists in this study, and considering the possibility of loss of teeth placed in the screw hole during the wear test, n = 15 specimens were prepared for each group. At the end of the study, n = 9 specimens were evaluated for each group, and the number of specimens evaluated was found to be consistent with the literature. Similarly, in the literature, it is seen that the number of specimens in each group varied between 5 and 10 in studies evaluating surface hardness [27, 28]. In this study, 10 samples were prepared from each group for surface hardness, and it was seen that it was compatible with the literature.

Although clinical studies are the best way to determine the wear of dental materials and antagonist teeth, they have the disadvantage of being costly and time consuming, parafunctional habits, eating habits, and the inability to standardize chewing force [35, 36]. Despite its disadvantages, such as not being able to fully reflect the oral environment and the difficulty of interpreting the results in clinical practice, wear in dental materials is evaluated through in vitro studies due to the advantages of providing standard conditions and a reproducible experimental environment before clinical application [36, 37]. In this study, a two-axes chewing simulator that makes a sliding movement after occlusal contact was used and a two-body wear test was conducted.

The chewing force in humans has been shown to vary between 20 and 120 N [38]. The literature has shown that children’s bite force is lower than that of adults [39]. It has also been stated that chewing simulators should mimic the forces in the oral environment as much as possible [35]. The average value of physiological bite force in patients without bruxism is accepted as 50 N [36]. Studies evaluating the wear of primary teeth have shown that the loading force in the chewing simulator varies between 30 and 75 N [3, 7, 9, 23, 24, 32, 40, 41]. In this study, the loading force was determined to be 50 N, consistent with the literature. The number of cycles used in chewing simulators in the literature ranges from 20,000 to 1,200,000 [3, 7, 9, 23, 32, 34, 40, 41]. Daily occlusal contacts with moderate chewing forces have been reported to range from 300 to 700 cycles [42]. In this study, 120,000 chewing cycles were produced for each specimen, varying between 6 months and 1 year. Thermocycling shows a material-dependent effect compared to a constant temperature [35]. At the same time, constant water exchange removes worn particles from the environment, reducing the abrasive effect. In this study, a thermocycling system (5–55 degrees Celsius) similar to the real oral environment was used.

It is known that tooth wear is very common in young children. A previous study reported that tooth wear was highest on incisors and canines when comparing incisors, canines, and molars [43]. Therefore, canines were used as antagonists in this study. The standardization of the enamel surfaces of natural teeth used as antagonists in wear tests is controversial [36]. They reported that the unstandardized enamel cusp of the natural tooth was the most suitable antagonist [44]. Aprismatic enamel layer is thought to be more resistant to wear than prismatic enamel. Both the removal of aprismatic enamel and the thinning of enamel on standardized antagonist cusps account for the greater wear observed [44]. They also noted that standardized and unstandardized enamel exhibit different wear characteristics [45]. Therefore, the enamel surfaces of both the antagonist primary canines and the upper central incisors used as the control group were not standardized in this study. Furthermore, potential differences in enamel hardness and wear behavior between primary central incisors and canines represent a methodological limitation that should be considered when interpreting the results.

After the wear test, both wear volume and wear depth were evaluated on the materials and antagonist teeth. According to the wear volume results of the materials, there was no difference between the wear on the Z and PCIE, while both were significantly less worn than the PR. When the wear depth results of the materials were examined, there was no difference between the wear depths between the PCIE and the PR, but they were significantly greater than the Z. Considering the surface hardness of the Z and primary tooth enamel, the results are controversial [22]. The very low volume losses suggest that methodological errors related to the sensitivity of the scanner and software may have influenced the results. Especially in cases where the difference between the two scans (pre-wear and post-wear) is minimal, the best-fit alignment process and scanning noise can lead to small positional deviations, creating additional volume changes independent of the actual wear. Therefore, some of the volume losses obtained may not reflect the actual wear amount but rather the limitations of the measurement system; this suggests that volume analyses should be interpreted with caution in studies evaluating very low wear [46, 47]. However, zirconia wear values approaching the scanner’s lower detection limit are consistent with visually negligible surface loss, confirming this material’s exceptional wear resistance. In contrast, wear values for the other groups (0.199 ± 0.116 mm and 0.089 ± 0.063 mm) substantially exceeded this threshold, ensuring reliable quantification for those comparisons.

According to the wear volume results on the antagonist teeth, although A-Z experienced significantly more volume loss than A-PR, A-Z, and A-PR showed similar wear to the A-PCIE. There was no difference in wear depth between the antagonist primary teeth. A previous study indicated that volumetric analysis is theoretically comprehensive but less reliable than wear depth [47]. Differences in material deformation around the antagonist contact point may be the reason why the findings of volumetric and vertical wear assessments show that these two metrics do not always correlate proportionately. As a result, assessing both volumetric and vertical loss offers a more thorough comprehension of wear dynamics. Wear in various areas of an antagonist tooth may not change its height, but it can have a substantial impact on its overall volume, clinical integrity, and function because volumetric loss records overall surface reduction, whereas vertical loss represents linear changes. Therefore, a comprehensive approach that takes into account shape deformation, changes in contact area, vertical loss, and volumetric loss collectively is crucial for evaluating the viability of restoration materials [46].

The main concern with Z crowns is their high rigidity, which can cause significant wear to antagonist teeth [7]. However, Z crowns are highly resistant to wear and provide long-term restorations for primary teeth [5, 48]. As a result of this study, the wear depth was reported to be significantly lower due to the high surface hardness of Z, 1085.78 ± 143.5 VHN. When the antagonist teeth were evaluated, the wear depth was greater in A-Z than in the A-PCIE and A-PR, but not significantly. However, A-Z volume loss was similar to that of A-PCIE, although A-PR was significantly higher. Although Z has a high hardness, wear on the antagonist teeth depend largely on the microstructure and surface finish of the restorative material [49]. Studies were conducted on permanent teeth to evaluate the antagonist tooth wear of polished, glazed, and unpolished Z [50, 51]. Polished Z has been found to cause significantly less wear on the antagonist tooth than glazed Z [51]. Similarly, it has been observed that polished Z causes significantly less wear on the antagonist enamel compared to unpolished Z [50]. According to a previous study, at a constant temperature, in a chewing test performed under a 30 N force with 30,000 chewing cycles, the volume loss of the primary tooth compared to unpolished Z is 1.4 mm³ [7]. Another study found that in a chewing test involving 100,000 chewing cycles under a 50 N force with a thermal cycle applied, the volume loss of the primary tooth compared to the glazed Z was 2.66 mm³ [23]. In this study, under thermal cycling and a 50 N force, A-Z wear of polished Z after 120,000 chewing cycles was 2.1 mm³. Considering the number of cycles, force, and volume loss in the study, the lower volume loss in the A-Z compared to previous studies can be explained by the polishing of Z.

Three-dimensional printed restorations are economical in terms of material consumption and time-efficient [52]. These resins provide a natural tooth-like appearance by offering superior esthetics compared to stainless steel crowns used in primary teeth [7, 52]. In a previous study evaluating the wear of PR, Z, and lithium disilicate on primary and permanent teeth, it was shown that PR had the least wear resistance and caused the least wear on the antagonist tooth [7]. In another study, the volumetric and vertical wear of primary teeth was evaluated against PR and PZC. Antagonist primary tooth wear in both dimensions was found to be similar in PR and PZC [9]. A study evaluating the wear characteristics of Z, nano-hybrid composite, and stainless-steel crowns on antagonist primary teeth found that nano-hybrid composite crowns suffered the most wear [53]. According to the results of this study, the wear depth on the A-PR was less than A-Z, and the A-PCIE, but this was not significant. Differently, volume loss of A-PR was significantly lower than A-Z, while it was similar to A-PCIE. Based on volume loss in the material, PR was significantly more worn than Z and PCIE. Although the wear depth of PR was similar to PCIE, it was significantly higher than Z. The results of this study are similar to previous studies. The wear resistance of the materials is also supported by the VH test results (PR: 25.07 ± 1.4 VHN, Z: 1085.78 ± 143.5). This situation can be explained by the different material compositions of Z and PR.

Throughout the approximately ten years that primary teeth are present, they are crucial for the development of mastication, occlusion, and oral functionality. As a result, it’s critical to use the right material to limit wear on the antagonist teeth and to ensure the longevity of restorations [54]. A recent clinical study examined wear of different crown materials, but no control group was specified [29]. Consequently, a comprehensive evaluation that takes into account the unique mechanical properties and developmental characteristics of primary teeth is essential [54]. The authors were unable to find any in vivo studies in the literature on one-year wear of primary teeth. An in vitro study assessed volume loss and maximum depth in primary teeth after a wear test using 20 N force and 100,000 chewing cycles. Volume loss in primary teeth was 0.027 mm3, and depth was 28.69 μm [55]. The findings of the prior research agree with those of the control group in the current study. A strength of this research is that it involves a comparison of the materials utilized in the study with primary teeth. At one-year follow-up, the in vivo study reported wear depth of 0.768 ± 0.057 mm against the CAD-CAM zirconia crown. However, the study did not specify the polishing procedure [29]. In the study, A-Z wear was found to be 0.698 ± 0.256 mm, similar to the clinical study. In this study, the Z and PR specimens were polished with an intraoral polishing set. Following occlusion modifications, it is recommended to polish crowns restored with Z in the clinic. Wear is a complicated process that involves removing material and antagonists from direct stylus contact. It is primarily controlled by surface quality, and hardness [36]. However, although the same set was used for polishing in this study, surface roughness was not measured. This is one of the limitations of the study.

This study has several limitations. The small number of materials that were assessed. In order to precisely evaluate wear and preserve standard test geometry, flat specimens were also chosen. This made it possible to concentrate on material qualities by removing complicating variables like impact and shear as well as the intricacy of crown design. However, it does not replicate the three-dimensional contact dynamics of anatomical crowns. Wear patterns may differ with cusp angles, fossa anatomy, and multi-axial mandibular movements. The oral cavity’s dynamic circumstances, such as pH variations and microbiota, were not examined in this in vitro investigation. Surface roughness (Ra, Rz) was not quantitatively measured in this study. Although a standardized polishing protocol was applied to all specimens, variations in surface topography may have influenced antagonist wear outcomes. Therefore, the antagonist wear findings should be interpreted with caution, and future studies incorporating profilometric analysis are warranted. Furthermore, further clinical research is required to assess primary tooth enamel wear over the long term because the degree of wear throughout short follow-up periods may differ from long-term patterns.

Conclusion

Based on the findings of this in vitro study, the following conclusions were drawn:

PR showed similar wear resistance to primary tooth enamel, but highly polished monolithic Z showed the highest resistance.

Both highly polished monolithic Z and PR demonstrated comparable primary tooth enamel wear, suggesting potentially acceptable antagonist wear behavior.
