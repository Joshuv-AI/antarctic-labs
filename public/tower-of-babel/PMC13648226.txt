Reinforcement-Learning-Enabled
Supervisory Control
of a Full-Scale Anaerobic Digestion Facility Using a Calibrated Digital
Twin

Ahmed I Yunus, Srinivas Jalla, Joe F Bozeman, Yongsheng Chen

Abstract

Anaerobic digestion
(AD) is a cornerstone of energy recovery and
decarbonization at wastewater resource recovery facilities (WRRFs),
yet full-scale digesters are often operated conservatively due to
delayed process feedback, nonlinear stability constraints, and limited
observability. This study presents a novel reinforcement learning
(RL)-enabled supervisory control framework for full-scale AD that
integrates a calibrated mechanistic digital twin model (DTM) with
offline policy learning and counterfactual uncertainty evaluation.
Specifically, the DTM was calibrated to reproduce control-relevant
digester behavior. Using 2.7 years of simulated operation, a batch-constrained Q (BCQ)-reinforcement learning policy was trained to maximize
biogas production while penalizing instability associated with pH
excursions and elevated volatile fatty acids (VFAs)/alkalinity (ALK)
ratios. Counterfactual evaluation against historical, greedy, and
heuristic controls used 1 year unseen DTM simulation and 1 year of
full-scale operational data. The RL policy achieved comparable mean
daily biogas production (+0.35%) in simulated testing. Under the 1
year operational data, it achieved plant-equivalent mean production
of 9802 m3/day, corresponding to a modest potential cumulative
improvement (+2.7%, 93 405 m3/year) subject to substantial
uncertainty P(Δ > 0) = 0.524. These results
indicate that offline-trained RL, combined with a mechanistic digital
twin and counterfactual evaluation, provides a feasible and interpretable
framework for developing and assessing supervisory AD control without
live-plant experimentation while showing that the resulting benefit
must be interpreted under explicit model-plant uncertainty.

Introduction

Wastewater
resource recovery facilities (WRRFs) are increasingly
positioned as critical infrastructure for achieving energy (i.e.,
electricity and fuels) and nutrient recovery in the form of fertilizers
and soil amendment byproducts. Within
this paradigm, anaerobic digestion (AD) plays a central role by converting
organic residuals into methane-rich biogas while simultaneously reducing
sludge volume, and promoting nutrient recovery.
−

 When operated
effectively, AD enables WRRFs to offset energy demand, stabilize biosolids,
and contribute to circular resource management.
−

 However, despite
decades of implementation and incremental optimization, full-scale
AD systems continue to operate well below their theoretical performance
envelope.
,,
 The challenge
is not rooted in the fundamental biochemistry of AD, which is well-understood,
but in the operational complexity of real-world systems.
−

 Full-scale digesters are subject to continuously varying feedstock
compositions, seasonal temperature fluctuations, co-digestion perturbations,
and nonlinear inhibitory dynamics associated with volatile fatty acids
(VFAs), alkalinity depletion, and ammonia accumulation.
,,
 These interacting factors produce
delayed and compounding effects that are difficult to anticipate using
conventional control strategies. As a result, operators often prioritize
stability over performance, sacrificing biogas yield to avoid process
upset.
,

Building on the findings in the current
literature, we posit that
contemporary AD performance is constrained by five persistent operational
bottlenecks: (1) reliance on labor-intensive manual sampling with
sparse temporal resolution, (2) high analytical costs limiting monitoring
frequency of key stability indicators, (3) prohibitive sensor instrumentation
expenses that restrict process observability, (4) supervisory decision-making
under incomplete or noisy data, and (5) dependence on expert operator
intuition that introduces variability and scalability limitations
(more on this in section 1 of the Supporting
Information).
,
 Collectively, these factors indicate
that system performance is governed less by biological limits than
by informational and decision constraints.

Recent advances in
data-driven process optimization offer a promising
pathway forward.
,,
 For example, supervised machine learning (ML) models have demonstrated
strong predictive capability for biogas production, feedstock blending,
and inhibition detection.
,,,−

 However, these
approaches remain fundamentally descriptive: they predict what will
happen under a given condition, but they do not determine what action
should be taken next. Digital twin models (DTMs) provide a critical
bridge across this gap.
,
 By coupling mechanistic
representations of AD processes with real operational constraints,
DTMs can generate dense, high-fidelity trajectories that are otherwise
infeasible to measure in practice. Despite
DTMs’ potential to bridge the WRRF-ML gap, on their own, DTMs
do not close the control loop; they simulate system behavior but do
not prescribe optimal decisions. This limitation motivates the integration
of reinforcement learning (RL) as a supervisory decision-making layer.
,,,,−

 RL is uniquely suited
to environments characterized by delayed feedback, nonlinear dynamics,
and competing objectives. By learning policies that map observed system
states to operational actions, RL enables proactive control strategies
that explicitly balance biogas maximization against stability constraints.
−

 Early applications of RL to AD have demonstrated conceptual feasibility
but remain limited in scope. Existing studies primarily focus on laboratory-scale
digesters or simplified process representations, where RL agents interact
with idealized environments under controlled conditions. These previous studies advance the methodological
foundation of RL-based control but continue to rely on simulated environments
without systematic validation against real plant trajectories.

Critically, no existing study has demonstrated
RL-based supervisory
control of a full-scale AD using a calibrated mechanistic DTM, nor
has any work evaluated learned policies through counterfactual uncertainty
simulation grounded in real WRRF operational data.
,,−
,−

 The absence of such validation represents a major barrier to practical
adoption, as utilities are understandably reluctant to deploy autonomous
control strategies without rigorous evidence of safety. To help address
this gap in the present study, we develop and rigorously evaluate
a RL-enabled supervisory control framework for AD that directly addresses
the informational, temporal, and decision-making constraints limiting
current WRRF AD operation. Instead of focusing on prediction or parameter
estimation, our work targets the control problem itself which is to
address how to select coordinated organic loading actions over time
that increase biogas yield while preserving biochemical stability
under real-world operating uncertainty.

To achieve this objective,
a RL framework is constructed around
a calibrated mechanistic DTM of a full-scale WRRF anaerobic digester.
The DTM is used to generate 3.7 years long horizon, operationally
realistic trajectories that capture nonlinear process dynamics and
stability constraints, enabling offline training of a supervisory
RL policy without risk to the physical plant. The study further establishes
a practical benchmarking strategy, comparing RL policy against three
baselines: historical operator control, a myopic greedy optimization
policy, and a process-based heuristic controller that represents typical
operator decision-making logic.

This design addresses two distinct
scientific questions. First,
how can a generalizable offline supervisory control policy be constructed
when exploration on the physical plant is infeasible and historical
operation spans only a narrow region of the state-action space? Second,
how can the resulting control benefits be reliably estimated when
the training environment differs from the physical plant, such that
model-plant discrepancy may be comparable to or exceed the magnitude
of the controller effect? The first question motivates the DTM-enabled
offline training architecture and the batch-constrained policy formulation;
the second motivates the counterfactual evaluation framework and the
explicit propagation of model-plant discrepancy. Together, these questions
frame the hypothesis tested in this study: that autonomous, data-driven
supervisory control of AD is technically feasible and operationally
credible when enabled by a calibrated DTM and offline batch-constrained Q-learning (BCQ) policy, and that its benefits can be transparently
bounded under realistic model-plant mismatch.

Materials and Methods

Full-Scale
WRRF Facility Description

Data utilized in this study were
obtained from a full-scale WRRF
in the southeastern United States, representative of large municipal
facilities with integrated biological nutrient removal (BNR) and AD-based
resource recovery. The facility operates under highly dynamic conditions,
receiving mixed solids streams including thickened waste activated
sludge (TWAS), imported high-strength organic wastes (HSW), and fats,
oils & grease (FOG), resulting in variable loading and complex
process interactions. Specifically at this full-scale WRRF, the TWAS
constitutes activated sludge from the BNR reactors, primary sludge
and tertiary treatment sludge (). AD is performed in five heated, mesophilic egg-shaped
digesters (3785 m3 each; 18 927 m3 total)
maintained at 35–38 °C with a facility-average reactor
solid retention time (SRT) of ∼14–16 days, placing the
AD system near its nominal loading rate rather than in an underloaded
regime. The AD process is integrated with downstream energy and nutrient
recovery systems, creating a tightly coupled water–energy–nutrient
nexus. These characteristics define a challenging yet realistic control
environment for evaluating advanced supervisory strategies. Detailed
facility configuration, process schematics, and operational parameters
are provided in section 2.1 of the Supporting
Information.

WRRF
Digital Twin Model Development and Data
Engineering

A mechanistic DTM of the full-scale WRRF was
developed using SUMO Dynamita V.22 to provide a safe environment for
RL training and counterfactual evaluation. The DTM incorporated ADM1-based
AD kinetics, key inhibition pathways (i.e., VFA accumulation, ammonia
toxicity, and pH-dependent suppression), and gas-phase mass transfer
dynamics responsive to substrate loading and temperature. The model
calibration targeted system-level consistency across hydraulic retention
time, effluent quality, biosolids production, TWAS handling, FOG and
HSW co-digestion rates, and biogas yield rather than individual kinetic
parameter identification, aligning with the supervisory control objectives
of the present study. Importantly, the calibrated DTM demonstrated
sufficient sensitivity to operational perturbations and produced realistic
system-level outputs for environmental and economic impact assessment,
as validated in our prior work.

Our data engineering followed a three-stage workflow: (1) 3.7 years
of WRRF influent data (Table S1) were cleaned
via interpolation and rolling averages to generate continuous forcing
functions for DTM simulation; (2) the calibrated DTM produced a complete
3.7 year AD operating dataset (Table S2) free of missing values; and (3) 1 year of full-scale AD operational
data (Table S6) was cleaned using quality
checks and multivariate Iterative Extra Tree algorithm imputation
to address approximately 30–40% missingness while preserving
realistic process correlations (refer to Section 2.7 of the Supporting Information). The cleaned data are presented
in Table S6 and Figure S3. Complete DTM development, calibration diagnostics, governing
equations, and BCQ-RL preprocessing protocols are provided in sections 2.2 to 2.7 of the Supporting Information.

Reinforcement
Learning Framework

Markov Decision Process
(MDP) Formulation

Supervisory control is a hierarchical control
strategy in which
a high-level controller coordinates and optimizes overall system operation.
Specifically, it sets operational targets, enforces constraints, and
selects operating modes for lower level controllers such as proportional–integral–derivative
(PID) control or model predictive control (MPC).
,,
 Accordingly, future deployment of an advance
RL supervisory AD control would operate in conjunction with existing
PID control or MPC layers, augmenting established control architectures.
−

 In the present study, RL supervisory control of full-scale AD was
formalized as a discrete-time MDP, defined by the tuple (S,A,R,P), where
(S) denotes the system state, (A) the control action, (R) denotes the scalar reward,
and (P) denotes the state transition dynamics.
,,
 This formulation enabled the
learning and evaluation of control policies that map observed digester
conditions to supervisory feeding decisions under uncertainty. We
explain each definition in detail next.

Offline BCQ-RL Policy Training

Offline BCQ-RL was selected to learn the supervisory control policy
without direct interaction with the physical system (see ). It explicitly constrains policy actions to remain within
the support of historically observed operational behavior, a critical
requirement for process stability in WRRF applications. Alternative
offline algorithms such as conservative Q-learning
and implicit Q-learning instead achieve conservatism
through value-function pessimism and in-sample policy extraction,
respectively, rather than by directly restricting the action space. The offline training dataset was constructed
from approximately 2.7 years of dynamic trajectories generated by
the calibrated SUMO Dynamita DTM, converted into one-step MDP transition
tuples with min-max global scaled state and action variables, with
the remaining 1 year reserved for policy evaluation under unseen DTM
operating conditions. The BCQ-RL policy was trained using 50 000
gradient update steps with a batch size of 256, two critic networks
to reduce overestimation bias. The BCQ-RL utilized an actor and critic
learning rates of 1 × 10–4 and 3 × 10–4 respectively with a behavior-constraining parameter
lambda (λ) of 0.75. This balances the exploitation of high-reward
actions against adherence to historically observed control patterns.
Full algorithm architecture, hyperparameter specifications, and training
diagnostics are provided in Table S8 of section 3.5 of the Supporting Information.

Baseline Controllers (Three Comparison Policies)

To ensure that performance gains attributed to RL are not artifacts
of evaluation design, the BCQ-RL supervisory controller was benchmarked
against three baseline policies: (1) historical operator practice,
representing the actual supervisory decisions implemented at the WRRF
during the 1 year evaluation period; (2) a myopic greedy controller,
which selects the action maximizing a one-step surrogate estimate
of biogas production at each decision step with a biochemical stability
penalty (refer to eq S1; see ); and (3) a process-based
heuristic controller, which adjusts FOG/HSW dosing around the historical
operator trajectory using a three-zone VFA/ALK and pH stability classification
while holding TWAS constant at historical values (see ). All three baselines were
evaluated on the same held-out test period using an identical state
representation, data pipeline, and counterfactual simulator, enforcing
a controlled and directly comparable assessment across policies. Full
algorithm descriptions, pseudocode, and extended evaluation results
are provided in sections 3.5 and 3.6 of the Supporting Information.

Counterfactual Evaluation Framework

The counterfactual
evaluation framework was designed to eliminate
deployment risk while preserving operational realism. The central
premise is to hold the digester operating context fixed, represented
by a time-indexed state trajectory derived from either mechanistic
simulation or observed plant operation, while varying only the supervisory
control actions. Under this design, differences in performance across
controllers are attributed directly to policy decisions rather than
confounding variations in influent conditions, seasonality, or unobserved
operational interventions. All controllers were evaluated using an
identical state-action interface, data pipeline, and normalized representation
defined in , with biogas outcomes estimated using a kNN surrogate trained
on the DTM state-action space for computational efficiency and reproducibility.
Counterfactual evaluation was conducted across two complementary stages:
a held-out mechanistic test window of 1 year to assess generalization
under unseen simulated conditions, and a 1 year full-scale operational
window to evaluate how alternative supervisory policies would have
performed under actual plant states, with all controllers conditioned
on identical daily observed digester conditions. Consistent with recent
supervisory control literature, the present study’s primary
performance outcomes are mean daily biogas production (m3/day) and cumulative biogas production (Mm3), supplemented
by process stability indicators including the fraction of days exceeding
VFA/ALK thresholds and pH excursion frequency, as these metrics reflect
decision quality and operational performance rather than pointwise
predictive accuracy such as mean absolute error (MAE), coefficient
of determination (R
2), and root mean square
error (RMSE).

Results and Discussion

Results are presented in three
stages that establish the validity,
behavior, and deployment feasibility of the BCQ-RL supervisory control
framework. We first validated the DTM environment for offline training
(), then
characterized the learned BCQ-RL policy (), and finally evaluated all control strategies
under counterfactual simulation using 1 year of full-scale data (). Performance
is assessed in terms of biogas production and robustness to model–plant
mismatch via Monte Carlo uncertainty propagation. With this structure
established, we now present the results in detail.

SUMO
Digital Twin Calibration and Validation

The objective of
the SUMO Dynamita DTM calibration was to reproduce
control-relevant system behavior, in contrast to achieving pointwise
agreement or unique identification of kinetic parameters. A comprehensive
calibration verification was performed to ensure that the mechanistic
DTM reproduces the statistical behavior, feature sensitivities, and
system-level interactions observed at the full-scale WRRF. Influent
and effluent performance metrics were evaluated to confirm consistency
with regulatory-relevant water quality distributions under dynamic
operation (refer to Tables S1 and S7). Feature attribution analysis using SHapley
Additive exPlanations (SHAP) was conducted on both the 3.7 year DTM
and 1 year operational AD datasets to verify preservation of dominant
drivers of biogas production (see ), while pairwise correlation and principal
component analysis assessed consistency in interaction structure among
organic loading, stability indicators, and biogas production (see Figure S7 and section 3.4 of the Supporting Information).

As shown in , organic loading variables (FOG/HSW and TWAS) along with SRT consistently
emerge as the dominant drivers of biogas production across both datasets,
corresponding directly to supervisory decision levers and confirming
that the DTM preserves the action-outcome structure governing full-scale
performance. Stability-related variables (VFA/ALK, alkalinity, and
pH) consistently ranked as lower importance drivers, consistent with
AD operational practice of maintaining these parameters within stable
buffering ranges. Together, these verification steps confirm that
the DTM captures the control-relevant input-output relationships and
nonlinear dynamics necessary for supervisory policy development and
evaluation. Detailed statistical analyses and supporting figures are
provided in sections 3–3.4 of the Supporting Information.

BCQ-RL Policy Behavior

In alignment
with customary ML practice, the BCQ-RL policy was tested after training
to evaluate generalization and control performance. When evaluated
counterfactually against historical operation, the RL controller generated
feasible, stable feed schedules within a plant-informed admissible
operating space. As shown in a, the policy maintained smooth, near-historical rates
rather than pursuing aggressive instantaneous loading, holding FOG/HSW
near its lower operating bound while keeping total loading within
the admissible range. This behavior reflects the batch-constraint
RL framework, which restricts actions to the DTM-demonstrated low-loading
regime, and with the reward formulation that penalizes operational
instability. Over the 1 year held-out DTM test period, the RL policy
achieved a mean simulated biogas production of 10 140 m3/day against 10 105 m3/day under recorded
DTM operation, a 0.35% change, without driving the system toward unstable
operating regimes (a).

Evaluation using 1 year of full-scale
WRRF operational data further
illustrates the behavior of the RL framework under real plant conditions
(see b). Under
DTM-simulated conditions, the RL policy achieved a mean biogas production
of 13 307 m3/day, compared to 13 372 m3/day under historical DTM operation, indicating near-parity
performance within the digital environment. The simulated output exceeds
the recorded plant mean of 9547 m3/day by 39%, reflecting
the model-plant discrepancy rather than control-induced gain. After
applying matched-regime discrepancy scaling described in , the RL policy
yielded a mean plant-equivalent daily production of 9802 m3/day (3.59 Mm3 annually), corresponding to a cumulative
difference of Δ = 93 405 m3 with P(Δ > 0) = 0.524. This modest improvement is not statistically
distinguishable from zero when realistic model-plant mismatch is considered.
Collectively, these findings show that the RL policy operates as a
feasible, stability-compatible supervisory controller that sustains
biogas production at near-historical levels rather than acting as
an aggressive optimizer. The findings further suggest that BCQ-RL
performance is primarily bounded by DTM fidelity rather than the learning
algorithm itself.

Counterfactual Evaluation:
Comparative Assessment
of Supervisory Controllers

The counterfactual evaluation
framework provides the core validation of the proposed RL supervisory
controller by benchmarking its performance against three alternative
control baseline strategies under identical realized state trajectories.
By conditioning all controllers on the same system evolution, differences
in outcomes can be attributed solely to action selection and timing,
not to variations in underlying process disturbance, influent composition,
or external operating conditions. As a result, the comparative evaluation
discussed next emphasizes operational performance and process safety.

Biogas Production Performance Across Baseline
Control Strategies

Figures S8a and b and S9a and b of the Supporting Information
summarize the counterfactual biogas production outcomes obtained under
the greedy optimization and process-based heuristic controllers, respectively,
relative to historical operator practice. These results are compared
to the BCQ-RL policy behavior discussed in , enabling a structured assessment of how
different control philosophies trade off short-term energy maximization,
stability awareness, and operational realism.

Greedy
Optimization Baseline (Theoretical
Performance Upper Bound)

The greedy controller represents
a purely myopic optimization strategy, selecting daily FOG/HSW and
TWAS feed rates that maximize immediate predicted biogas production
based on the same surrogate mapping used for RL evaluation. As shown
in Figure S8a, this policy exhibits highly
aggressive and non-smooth feeding behavior, characterized by large,
rapid fluctuations in organic loading that deviate substantially from
historical operation. These actions reflect the absence of temporal
foresight and explicit stability considerations in the greedy objective.

Due to this volatility, the greedy policy increases cumulative
biogas production relative to historical operation over the 1 year
evaluation horizon (see Figure S8b), confirming
that short-term maximization of organic loading can yield higher instantaneous
methane generation. However, the pronounced oscillations in feed rates
underscore a critical limitation: the greedy controller disregards
longer term process dynamics and operator-relevant constraints, producing
control trajectories that would be impractical, and potentially unsafe,
for real-world deployment. For example, any controller that follows
the greedy logic does not take into consideration equipment such as
valves, actuators and pumps. The aggressive switching, “on”
and “off”, of these equipment will lead to their aggressive
wear and tear in a short time. Furthermore, this behavior highlights
the well-known failure mode of myopic optimization in nonlinear biological
systems, where local gains are achieved at the expense of robustness
and interpretability.

Process-Based Heuristic
Baseline (Rule-Based
Comparator)

In contrast, the process-based heuristic controller
is explicitly designed to emulate how experienced operators manage
ADs using biochemical intuition and rule-based logic. This is similar
to PID control in that both approaches rely on feedback from measured
process variables to regulate system behavior and maintain operation
within safe and desired bounds. Albeit they operate at different hierarchical
levels and time scales. This baseline controller modulates FOG/HSW
loading according to traffic-light stability thresholds based on pH
and VFA/ALK ratios, while maintaining TWAS withdrawal to preserve
target SRT. As illustrated in Figure S9a, the resulting action trajectories closely track historical operation,
exhibiting smooth and conservative adjustments that prioritize process
stability.

Correspondingly, the heuristic controller mirrors
historical operator performance (see Figure S9b). As a result, counterfactual simulations showed no statistically
distinguishable gain in cumulative biogas relative to baseline operation.
The overall performance remained comparable to historical operator
and the BCQ-RL controller, while remaining below the greedy upper
bound scenario. This outcome reflects the inherent conservatism of
rule-based operation: by design, the heuristic controller avoids pushing
the system toward high-loading regimes that could jeopardize stability
as seen in the greedy controller, even when additional energy recovery
may be achievable.

Action
Feasibility and Operational Implementability

Deployability
requires that every prescribed action remain physically
and operationally admissible, which the raw BCQ-RL policy does not
guarantee. Each predicted action is therefore projected onto a plant-informed
admissible set before biogas is estimated, comprising per stream operating
ranges for FOG/HSW and TWAS (Table S6),
an SRT-compatible hydraulic-loading band (473 to 1893 m3/day; V = 18 927 m3; SRT 10 to
40 days), a daily ramp-rate limit, and a minimum co-substrate utilization
on stable states (section 3.7 of the Supporting
Information). These constraints define admissible actions only and
are never used as reward-learning signals, so the 1 year plant record
remains unseen during training. Their effect is quantified by comparing
the policy with and without the projection.

The admissibility
layer is necessary rather than cosmetic. Without it, the DTM-trained
policy suppresses FOG/HSW and yields plant-equivalent production below
historical operation (P(Δ > 0) = 0.508; Figure S10), whereas with it, feasible loading
is restored and production recovers to near-historical levels (P(Δ > 0) = 0.524; Figure S11). The layer adjusts 87.7% of daily commands, predominantly by enforcing
the co-substrate floor, while the hydraulic constraint remains non-binding
across the evaluation year. The deployable behavior is thus set by
the feasibility projection rather than by policy hyperparameters,
and the same projection governs whether the prescribed feed is physically
realizable at full scale.

Feed rates are bounded to historically
achieved values, enforcing
mass-balance realism. The greedy comparator is capped at the observed
maximum and TWAS is held within the facility’s operating range,
so no policy prescribes loading beyond the sludge available from the
treatment line. Constraining the frozen policy in this manner lowers
deployment risk relative to the unconstrained greedy and non-learning
heuristic strategies, and situates BCQ-RL as an operationally credible
supervisory controller. The residual biogas differences among these
feasible policies instead originate in the system’s dependence
on organic loading.

Organic-Loading Dependence
of Biogas Production

Biogas production in this system is
governed primarily by organic
loading, a dependence the DTM reproduces. SHAP attribution identifies
solids retention time and organic (co-digestion) loading as the dominant
drivers of yield, with pH, VFA, and the VFA/ALK ratio acting indirectly
by bounding the feasible operating space rather than shaping yield
monotonically ( and section 3.3 of the Supporting Information),
and because the facility operates near its nominal loading at an SRT
of roughly 15 days, biogas responds chiefly to the volatile solids
delivered. This concentrates the residual uncertainty on percent solids,
a secondary biogas contributor (∼11% of SHAP importance; ) that is simultaneously
the poorest-recovered imputed input (R
2 = 0.35; Table S4). The DTM operated within
a narrow, low band (2.62–3.10%; Table S2), whereas the plant ran higher and wider (2.38–4.51%; Table S6). This regime gap is part of a broader
distributional divergence across the stability variables (Figure S12 and Table S12). As a result, the percent-solids component of the plant state fell
outside the DTM training range on 97.3% of evaluation days (Table S17), introducing localized uncertainty
for this yield-relevant input while the remaining stability variables
were largely within the DTM support.

This mismatch bounds achievable
biogas independent of plant history. Because BCQ-RL is batch-constrained
to the SUMO Dynamita DTM’s demonstrated actions, it cannot
exploit operating regimes absent from training, and its output is
capped by DTM fidelity rather than by the learning algorithm. DTM
construction, and specifically the loading regime it demonstrates
and its state support in yield-relevant variables, therefore emerges
as the binding determinant of offline RL performance at full scale.
Deployment ultimately rests on whether these loading-bounded actions
also preserve process stability.

Process
Stability and Forward-State Evaluation

Process stability
is a prerequisite for any supervisory controller
in AD, and it is assessed here through two complementary frameworks.
Under the matched-regime evaluation, all policies act along the same
realized plant trajectory, so the stability indicators, the fraction
of days with VFA/ALK above 0.30 and pH outside 6.8 to 8.0, are identical
across controllers by construction, isolating the effect of action
selection on biogas while holding disturbances and influent conditions
constant. Along that trajectory the plant is stable, exceeding VFA/ALK
0.30 on 2.46% of days and leaving the pH range on 1.64%, consistent
with a facility in steady full-scale operation for over a decade.
Because this framework fixes state evolution, distinguishing controllers
by their stability risk requires forward simulation.

The forward
evaluation rolls each policy’s actions through the kNN surrogate
over a 7 day horizon, allowing state to evolve ( and Table S13). BCQ-RL and the process-based heuristic hold VFA/ALK below the
0.30 instability threshold and pH within 6.8 to 8.0 throughout the
horizon, whereas the greedy controller produces larger VFA, alkalinity,
and VFA/ALK excursions, consistent with its role as a myopic upper
bound rather than a deployable policy. These divergent trajectories,
invisible to the fixed-trajectory indicators, separate the controllers
by the stability risk their actions would generate under closed-loop
operation.

Interpreted together, the two evaluation frameworks in this section
position BCQ-RL as feasible and stability-compatible, but without
a demonstrated performance premium. The greedy controller attains
the largest biogas but drifts toward instability in forward simulation,
while the heuristic preserves stability by construction at conservative
output. BCQ-RL operates within the observed stability envelope at
near-historical production, neither increasing the frequency of high
VFA/ALK, low SRT, or out-of-range pH events nor pushing the system
toward unsafe regimes. Its value therefore lies in delivering deployable,
stability-aware supervisory control under full-scale uncertainty rather
than in maximizing energy recovery.

Summary
of Implications

Operation Implications
at WRRFs

The
results of the present study demonstrate that RL can be formulated
as a supervisory decision-support layer for AD control while remaining
compatible with operational stability constraints. The BCQ-RL policy
does not replace operator judgment or existing control infrastructure;
rather, it augments decision-making by anticipating process responses
that are difficult to infer from short-term observations alone. Across
both mechanistic test simulations and counterfactual evaluation using
full-scale operational trajectories, the RL policy exhibited behavior
consistent with experienced operator intuition, moderating organic
loading in response to stability indicators. This finding directly
addresses a long-standing operational constraint in WRRFs: operators
often prioritize conservatism to avoid upset, even when latent process
capacity exists, and the RL framework demonstrates that this conservatism
can be relaxed selectively and safely through state-aware coordination
of FOG/HSW and TWAS dosing. From an operational perspective, the BCQ-RL
controller is best interpreted as an early-warning supervisory controller
that is particularly valuable in facilities with intermittent or constant
FOG/HSW deliveries, staffing constraints, or delayed laboratory feedback,
where proactive decision support can materially reduce operational
risk while remaining compatible with real-world operational practice.

Societal Implications

Beyond performance,
autonomous or supervisory controllers such as BCQ-RL can introduce
measurable societal benefits by addressing structural barriers that
limit the deployment of advanced treatment systems. Globally, approximately
3.6 billion people lack safely managed sanitation, while more than
2.2 million people in the United States lack access to functional
sanitation.
,
 Meanwhile, AD has been identified
as a core technology capable of improving sanitation access by simultaneously
treating waste, inactivating pathogens, and recovering energy and
nutrients.
,
 However, implementation is frequently
constrained by shortages of trained operators, limited technical capacity,
and inconsistent monitoring infrastructure, factors repeatedly cited
as primary causes of system abandonment in decentralized installations. By embedding supervisory intelligence directly
into process control, BCQ-RL-enabled digesters can partially substitute
for specialized expertise, enabling stable operation in regions lacking
skilled personnel, reducing operator burden in aging utility workforces,
and supporting deployment in resource-constrained communities where
workforce and monitoring limitations currently restrict technology
adoption. Consequently, intelligent control
does not merely optimize process performance but functions as a socio-technical
enabler that broadens participation in sustainable sanitation and
energy recovery systems globally.

Digital
Twin and RL Enables Safe Deployment

A central contribution
of this work is the demonstration that DTM-enabled
offline BCQ-RL provides a credible pathway for safe adoption of autonomous
supervisory control in WRRFs. Rather than requiring live experimentation
on full-scale digesters, the framework leverages a calibrated mechanistic
model to generate realistic training data and evaluate policies through
counterfactual simulation (see ). Importantly, calibration focused on preserving input-output
relationships, nonlinear response patterns, and stability envelopes
relevant to supervisory control, rather than achieving exact trajectory
replication, as supervisory RL requires a faithful representation
of how alternative decisions alter system behavior relative to historical
practice rather than pointwise predictive accuracy. From an implementation
standpoint, this architecture enables shadow-mode deployment, in which
BCQ-RL policy recommendations are generated alongside operator decisions
without direct actuation, allowing utilities to build confidence in
policy behavior and safety before tighter integration with SCADA or
advanced process control systems. In this sense, the DTM does not
merely support algorithm development but actively reduces institutional
and operational barriers to adoption.

Limitations

Four limitations of the
present study warrant explicit acknowledgment. First, controller evaluations
were conducted offline using counterfactual simulation, which ensures
safety and reproducibility but does not capture closed-loop feedback
effects under live deployment, including operator intervention, sensor
delays, or actuator constraints. In addition, the counterfactual framework
cannot fully evaluate how alternative control policies would have
changed the subsequent evolution of plant states, because each policy
is evaluated against a shared historical or simulated trajectory rather
than through real-time plant interaction. Because the plant-equivalent
win probability is near 0.5 (P = 0.524), the reported
performance differences represent plant-translated potential rather
than a robust gain, and standalone deployment should be conditioned
on a higher confidence threshold or on online validation.

Second,
although the SUMO Dynamita DTM preserves dominant system-level dynamics,
relevant to supervisory AD control, it is not an exact replica of
the full-scale WRRF. Residual distribution shift between the DTM and
the real facility affects the quantitative translation of simulated
policy performance to plant-scale outcomes, particularly under operating
conditions that are rare, transient, or outside the calibrated data
envelope. Extrapolation beyond the historical and simulated operating
regimes under this study’s BCQ-RL framework should therefore
be approached cautiously.

Third, the full-scale operational
dataset contained substantial
missingness, requiring imputation before counterfactual evaluation
(section 2.7 of the Supporting Information).
Although imputation quality was evaluated using masked-observation
validation, method comparison, parameter-specific recovery metrics,
and distributional diagnostics, the imputed dataset may still smooth
short-duration disturbances or underrepresent rare process excursions,
particularly for intermittently measured variables such as VFA, alkalinity,
pH, and percent solids, the least recovered variable (R
2 = 0.35). This limitation is important because these
variables directly influence AD stability assessment.

Finally,
sensor dynamics were assumed to be instantaneous and noise-free
in the state representation. In practice, laboratory measurements
of VFAs, alkalinity, and solids are subject to delays and sampling
uncertainty, and explicit modeling of sensor latency and reliability
could further strengthen deployment feasibility.

Future Work

Future research should
extend this framework in four key directions. First, further DTM refinement
should be supported by a dedicated site-specific sampling campaign
guided by IWA’s Good Modelling Practice (GMP) principles. For a complex plant-wide WRRF model such as
the one used in this study, additional sampling and characterization
would be needed to strengthen calibration of influent fractionation,
internal recycle streams, AD feed composition, alkalinity, VFA, ammonia,
solids flows, biogas composition, and overall plant-wide mass balances.
Improving the calibration and validation of these processes would
enhance the physiological realism and predictive fidelity of the DTM,
thereby providing a more reliable foundation for training future BCQ-RL
policies. Such refinement will be particularly important before transitioning
toward closed-loop or online RL deployment in full-scale facilities.

Second, hardware-in-the-loop validation would enable closed-loop
testing of RL policies using real-time plant signals without direct
actuation, bridging the gap between offline evaluation and live deployment
as shown in step 3 of . Second, integration with SCADA data streams would allow
continuous state updating, automated feature engineering, and adaptive
retraining as operating conditions evolve. Third, extending the RL
formulation to multi-objective optimization, explicitly balancing
energy recovery, greenhouse gas mitigation, process stability, and
operating cost, would better reflect the full decision space faced
by WRRF operators. Finally, policy transferability across facilities
with different configurations, co-digestion portfolios, and regulatory
constraints remains an open and important question. DTM-based pretraining
followed by site-specific fine-tuning represents a promising pathway
toward scalable deployment.
