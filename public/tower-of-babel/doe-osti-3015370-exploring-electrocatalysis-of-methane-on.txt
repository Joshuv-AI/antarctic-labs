Abstract
 The original primary objective of this project focused on detailed and systematic molecular-level
mechanistic studies of low-temperature (~100 °C) electrochemical (EC) direct methane-to-methanol
(DMTM) conversion on selected transition metal surfaces. The latter include PtRu, PdAu, and NiCu
electrocatalysts. Specifically, the proposed studies aimed to test a bifunctional hypothetical DMTM
reaction mechanism on these electrocatalysts. The said bifunctional hypothetical DMTM reaction
mechanism involves the following bi-functional elemental reaction steps:
 CH4 + 2TM* (* for a free site) ® TM1–CH3 + TM2–H ® TM1–CH3 + TM2* + H+ + e–                 (1)
 H2O + 2TM* ® TM’1–OH + TM’2–H ® TM’1–OH + TM’2* + H+ + e–                                   (2)
 TM1–CH3 + TM’1–OH ® CH3OH + TM1* + TM’1*                                                    (3)
where TM and TM’ can be the same or different metal elements. However, an alloyed bi-metallic
surface would have more catalytic tunability for basic mechanistic studies than a single-metal one
would have. This is because it would be much more difficult if not impossible to optimize the
Reaction (1) and (2) (R-1 and R-2) simultaneously by which to optimize the Reaction (3) (R-3) on a
single-metal than on a bi-metallic surface. The proposed research would leverage fully the advanced
in situ/operando EC spectroscopic expertise and capability well-developed in the PI’s lab, which
include IR, Raman, NMR, and inline gas chromatograph-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), to delineate
the reaction mechanism(s) of electrocatalytic DMTM conversion and test the validity of the hypotheses
embodied in R-1 through R-3 to the extent possible by varying bimetallic pair as well as their bulk
and surface composition. These experimental investigations would be further augmented by density
functional theory (DFT) calculations as needed.
Progress has been made in the following three directions. First, we have developed an in-line
operando electrochemical mass spectrometry (OECMS) that enables us to observe the reaction
products as the reaction takes place. Second, we have observed unambiguously, by both
electrochemistry and mass spectrometry, that sustained methane, ethane, and propane activation can
be achieved on commercial carbon-supported Pt in a commercial single-stack proton exchanged
membrane fuel cell setup under truly mild conditions: E (electrode potential vs reversible hydrogen
electrode- RHE) <0.6V, T (temperature) < 100 °C, P (pressure) < 4 atmospheric pressure, and RH
(relative humility) < 90%. The kinetics of these electrochemical (EC) direct steaming reforming
reactions under mild conditions are sufficiently fast that they can potentially find sustainable
practical application in hydrogen generation and direct low-carbon alkane fuel cells. In fact, Using
the US National Renewable Energy Laboratory’s (US-NREL’s) H2A-lite: Hydrogen Analysis Lite

general where there exists “a plenty of room” for new discoveries and developments.

I. In-line Operando Electrochemical Mass Spectrometry (OECMS)
Figure 1 shows the OECMS that the PI’s lab has developed. It enables the operando identification of
reaction products coming from the membrane electrode assembly (MEA) reactor by the mass
spectrometer. The MEA reactor (left) is a commercial (Scribner) single-stack proton-exchange-
membrane (PEM) fuel cell setup. The MEA consists of a cathode-anode separating Nafion PEM and
electrocatalyst-loaded anodic and cathodic gas diffusion layers that are hot-pressed together. The
illustrating reaction is the EC-DSMR reaction. Sustained PEM-DSMR takes place on the anodic gas
diffusion layer (GDL) interface:
CH4 + 2H2O ® CO2 + 8H+ + 8e–
The generated protons are transferred through the Nafion PEM and reduced to H2 gas on the cathodic
GDL interface:
8H+ + 8e– ® 4H2
which also serves as the reversible hydrogen electrodes (RHE) under the same conditions as the anode
(temperature, pressure, and relative humidity) and a constant flow of humidified H2 gas.

As alluded to above, literature data indicate that achieving sustained EC CH4 oxidation remains
challenging at the liquid/solid interface of Pt electrodes, where CH4 activation can only be observed in
the two-step method: a very slow dissociative adsorption of CH4 at low potential followed by a high-
potential oxidation of the adsorbates generated from methane-adsorption. However, in our PEM-
DSMR, where reactions most likely occurred at the gas/solid interface that may also contain water
microdroplets, no long-waiting adsorption step is needed for observing sustained methane activation,
as evidenced by the current/MS CVs in Figure 3b. Indeed, the 1st CV (red) produced a (reproducible)
noticeably higher current and amount of CO2 on the left ramp of the peak than the 2nd and 3rd CV
(blue and green), where the latter two overlapped completely (so green was masked by blue therefore
not seen). The higher current from the 1st CV is caused by the oxidation of the pre-adsorbed species,

 4

such as COads, generated during the methane purge of the cell (approx. 30 minutes) before taking the
PEM-DSMR CVs and the corresponding OECMS CVs. This is similar to the slow methane
dissociative adsorption and ensuing adsorbate stripping observed previously at the liquid/solid
interface. Clearly, the sustained PEM-DSMR under mild conditions as reported here, embodied by
the 2nd (blue) and 3rd (green) CVs in Figure 3b, follows a reaction pathway that is different from the one
represented by the two-step methane activation reported previously. In other words, they are different
reactions with very different reaction kinetics. Specifically, the reactions reported in the literature are
the oxidation of methane-adsorption-generated intermediates (the left ramp of our 1st CV, Figure 3b).
However, in addition to the literature-reported oxidation currents, we also observed sustained and direct
CH4 oxidative currents (2nd and 3rd CV scans) that do not originate from the oxidation of the pre-
adsorbed intermediates reported in the literature. Our observations of the PEM-DSMR current were
likely associated with the gas/(microdroplet)/solid interface at the GDL, where a greater mass
transport ability was achieved.
Sustained CO2 formation via PEM-DSMR is also evident in Figure 3d, where the CO2 OECMS signal
at 0.6 V (the upper black curve) was observed continuously and simultaneously with the EC current
(the upper red curve) for 600 s, while no CO2 was produced under Ar flow (lower black curve).
Noticeably, the sustained reaction current was observed without an extensive waiting period for
methane adsorption but with instantaneous response to the applied cell potential, indicating that the
PEM-DSMR reaction kinetics (or reaction turnover frequency, TOF) is fast enough at the
gas/(microdroplet)/solid interface to generate sustained electron transfer that can sustain the current.
Similar PEM-DSMR behaviors, but with an overall much lower activity, were also observed on the
commercial carbon-supported PtRu and Pd black (vide infra) electrocatalysts, with the generation of
13
 CO2 from isotopic EC-13CH4 oxidation on the former. Interestingly, little activity, if any, was
observed on pure carbon supported Ru.

energy for the PEM-DSMR on both Pt/C (Figures 3e) and PtRu/C (not shown here). To convert the
reaction current to the CO2 production rate used in the y-axis in the figures, an external CO2 calibration
was obtained using a flow meter that converts MS counts per second to standard flow (standard cubic
centimeters per minute, SCCM) of CO2 mixed in the CH4 stream under each temperature and pressure.
