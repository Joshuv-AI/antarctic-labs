Provider engagement in rehabilitation services in Pakistan: a qualitative study using the theory of planned behavior
Authors: Farhat Ul Ain, Nukhba Zia, Rozina Salman Thobani, Farzana Aziz, Maheen Fazal, Asad Latif, Muhammad Asim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRehabilitation is very crucial for the treatment of chronic illnesses; it is still not widely used in primary healthcare in low-resource countries like Pakistan. Although there is ample evidence of gaps in the health system, behavioural factors that influence provider engagement with rehabilitation services remain poorly understood, especially regarding attitudes, social influences, and a sense of control over service provision. This study investigated how providers’ attitudes, societal expectations, and perceived control affected their engagement in a rehabilitative health system-strengthening initiative in Pakistan using the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB).MethodsThis qualitative exploratory study was a sub study of ReLAB-HS initiative, which sought to incorporate assistive technology and rehabilitation services into primary care. Data was collected between July and August 2024 from two Networks of Care (Thatta and Swat) from the two provinces Sindh and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Participants (n = 61) were among the five cadres and purposefully chosen, including Lady Health Workers, Lady Health Supervisors, Primary Healthcare Providers, Rehabilitation Professionals, and members of Technical Working Group Data were analyzed using a combined inductive–deductive approach guided by the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), with coding and theme development conducted in Atlas.ti.24.ResultsInterviews revealed that providers’ attitudes shifted from initial skepticism to recognizing rehabilitation as a crucial part of primary care, particularly after observing improvements in patient functioning. Subjective norms, reinforced through peer support and digital platforms such as WhatsApp, played a key role in sustaining engagement, although broader uptake was constrained by cultural norms and gender-related stigma. Perceived behavioral control improved with training and job aids, increasing providers’ confidence to integrate rehabilitation into practice. However, persistent structural and environmental barriers including time constraints, heavy workloads, and limited access to assistive technology reduced providers perceived behavioral control and restricted sustained implementation. Despite these challenges, many providers adopted adaptive strategies to continue delivering rehabilitation services.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control collectively shaped provider engagement with rehabilitation services. Peer networks, supportive supervision, and capacity-building initiatives facilitated positive behavioral change; however, these alone were insufficient for long-term integration at the primary care level. Addressing structural barriers including weak referral systems, limited assistive technology, workforce shortages, and sociocultural stigma is essential for sustainable rehabilitation integration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14915-2.Keywords: Theory of planned behavior, Rehabilitation services, Healthcare providers, Behavioral change, ReLAB-HS, Pakistan

Introduction

Rehabilitation is “a set of interventions designed to optimize functioning and reduce disability in individuals with health conditions in interaction with their environment,” according to the World Health Organization (WHO).” [1] It includes physical, occupational, speech, and psychosocial therapies aimed at improving functioning and quality of life [2]. Within the ReLAB-HS (Learning, Acting, and Building for Rehabilitation in Health Systems) initiative, rehabilitation was viewed as an integrated continuum of care that extended beyond specialized services to include provider-led functional assessment, counseling, exercise guidance, and referral pathways for assistive technologies and chronic disease management. This broader understanding informed the study’s exploration of providers’ experiences and implementation practices. In 2021, an estimated 2.6 billion people globally required rehabilitation, with demand rising due to aging, trauma, non-communicable diseases (NCDs), and post-COVID complications [3]. Despite recognition as a core component of Universal Health Coverage and Sustainable Development Goal 3, rehabilitation remains under-prioritized in many low- and middle-income countries, leading to fragmented delivery and limited access and missed opportunities for early intervention for rehabilitation [4, 5]. While infrastructure and workforce gaps are well-documented, evidence from Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia highlights additional barriers, including weak referral practices, limited inter-professional collaboration, and insufficient incentives for follow-up [6–9]. Studies from India, Nepal, and Bangladesh further show that frontline providers often deprioritize rehabilitation due to low confidence, competing demands, and weak referral linkages between primary and tertiary care [10, 11].

Pakistan faces similar challenges, with rehabilitation largely confined to tertiary care and concentrated in urban centers, contributing to rural–urban disparities [12]. System-level barriers such as workforce shortages, limited training, and weak integration of rehabilitation into primary care persist [13]. However, beyond these structural constraints, provider-level behavioral factors including limited awareness, low confidence, and perceptions of rehabilitation as an additional burden play a critical role in shaping service uptake and referral practices [14]. Rehabilitation professionals also face coordination challenges and inadequate infrastructure, further contributing to fragmented service delivery [15].

While scaling infrastructure and workforce is essential, utilization of rehabilitation services is strongly influenced by healthcare providers’ behaviors, including their intentions, confidence, and motivation, which are shaped by systemic constraints, workplace norms, professional expectations, and individual beliefs [16]. To explore the challenges related to rehabilitation uptake in Pakistan, the study applies the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) [17], drawing on insights from the local intervention ReLAB-HS (Learning, Acting, and Building for Rehabilitation in Health Systems) [18], to examine provider-level behavioral determinants underlying these challenges, particularly referral practices, interdisciplinary engagement, and prioritization of rehabilitation care. ReLAB-HS [18] is a multi-country health systems strengthening program aimed at improving access to rehabilitation and assistive technology through integration within primary healthcare systems. In Pakistan, the initiative focuses on strengthening service delivery in selected districts by building provider capacity, improving referral pathways, and enhancing coordination across levels of care [19]. This study represents a qualitative sub-study examining provider experiences and behavioral determinants within these intervention settings. The purpose of this study is to improve provider support, interprofessional coordination, and referral channels regarding the uptake of rehabilitation services. TPB explains how referral decisions and engagement are influenced by the perspectives of providers, social norms, and perceived skill Unlike alternative theories like the Health Belief Model or Capability, Opportunity, Motivation – Behaviour (COM-B) model, TPB offers a more targeted framework approach for complex healthcare settings where both individual beliefs and workplace environment guide professional decision making. Referral practices are intentional professional behaviors that require providers to evaluate their attitudes, perceived expectations from others, and their ability to perform the behavior before taking action. Therefore, TPB’s emphasis on behavioral intention as the immediate predictor of behavior makes it particularly relevant for understanding rehabilitation referral practices [20]. Providers with a constructive approach, supportive norms, and conviction are more likely to refer patients, whereas those viewing rehabilitation as a burden tend to avoid it [21, 22].

The objective of this study was to explore healthcare provider engagement with rehabilitation services within primary healthcare settings in Pakistan, using the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) to examine how attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control shape referral practices, interprofessional collaboration, and prioritization of rehabilitation care.

Methods

Study design and setting

This qualitative exploratory study was part of ReLAB-HS [19], a health systems strengthening effort that integrates rehabilitation and assistive technology into basic healthcare in Pakistan, implemented in Two Networks of Care (NoCs), Thatta (Sindh) and Swat (Khyber Pakhtunkhwa). The information was collected between July 8 and August 23, 2024.

The district of Thatta, which is primarily rural and vulnerable to flooding, has a high rate of poverty, poor female literacy, and no access to specialized rehabilitative treatment. With very little rehabilitation services, health services are mostly provided by Basic Health Units (BHUs), Rural Health Centres (RHCs), and Lady Health Workers (LHWs) Despite having more resources, Swat, a district in the northern province of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK), has issues with outreach, workforce shortages, and inadequate rehabilitation integration at Primary Health Care (PHC). These locations were chosen to represent the difficulties the rural health system face. Table 1 provides an overview of important district attributes.

Recruitment and study participants

Purposive selection was used to ensure diversity in roles, types of facilities, and engagement levels. Participants included Lady Health Workers (LHWs), Lady Health Supervisors (LHSs), Rehabilitation Professionals (RPs), and members of Technical Working Groups (TWGs) participated in the study. The Technical Working Group (TWG) comprised nine members from academic institutions, public health departments, and rehabilitation organizations. The group provided oversight for the ReLAB-HS initiative [19], monitored implementation progress, supported system-level planning, and facilitated coordination between primary healthcare providers and specialized rehabilitation services. There were 61 participants in total across both sites: 21 LHWs, 10 LHSs, 12 PHC providers, 9 RPs, and 9 TWG members (Table 2). They actively participated in referrals, service delivery, implementation monitoring, and screening for rehabilitation requirements.

All interviews were conducted by trained members of the research team who did not hold supervisory or managerial roles over the participants, minimizing potential influence on responses. Interviewers were introduced to participants as independent researchers focused on understanding provider experiences within the ReLAB-HS initiative [19].

Data saturation was assessed iteratively during data collection by reviewing emerging themes and confirming that no new substantive themes were arising in subsequent interviews. Recruitment continued until saturation was reached across all participant groups.

Within the ReLAB-HS initiative [19], commonly available assistive devices at primary healthcare facilities included walkers, crutches, canes, and bathroom support aids. More advanced devices, such as prosthetics and customized orthoses, were accessed through referral to specialized centres in metropolitan cities. Referral pathways were supported by LHWs, PHC providers, and RPs, with coordination facilitated through the ReLAB-HS network. This operational description provides context for understanding provider experiences, patient access, and reported challenges in device uptake and utilization.

This study did not employ formal disability classification categories as defined under national administrative frameworks such as the Disabled Persons (Employment and Rehabilitation) Ordinance (1981) [31]. Instead, the study focused on rehabilitation needs as encountered in routine service delivery, where providers identified individuals based on functional limitations related to musculoskeletal conditions, injuries, and chronic non-communicable diseases. No standardized disability classification tool was applied across sites. This approach reflects routine clinical practice and aligns with functional models of rehabilitation rather than categorical classification systems.

Operational definition of rehabilitation

For the purpose of this study, rehabilitation services were operationalized as a continuum of provider activities encompassing functional assessment, patient counseling, exercise demonstration, and referral pathways for specialized care, including prosthetic fitting, assistive device provision, and chronic disease management.

This definition reflects routine provider practice within the ReLAB-HS initiative [19] and guided both data collection and analysis, ensuring a shared understanding across participant groups.

Interview guides

For every cadre (LHWs, LHSs, PHC providers, RPs, and TWG members), five semi-structured interview guides were developed (Supplementary File 1). Open-ended questions focused on providers’ perspectives of rehabilitation, willingness and competence to provide services, influence of professional expectations, and difficulties faced in everyday practice. They also captured personal experiences, motivations, and barriers. Prior to data collection, guides were piloted to ensure clarity, cultural appropriateness, and alignment with the defined scope of rehabilitation activities. A subset of themes relevant to provider engagement in rehabilitation services was analyzed for this study.

Data analysis

In order to maintain accuracy and cultural significance, all interviews were verbatim transcribed in the language used for data collection and then translated into English by bilingual researchers.

A portion of the transcripts were cross-checked against audio recordings to ensure consistency and dependability, and team members collectively reviewed the translations.

Thematic analysis followed a combined inductive–deductive approach. Initially, transcripts were read multiple times and open-coded inductively to capture descriptive and interpretive features. Subsequently, codes were mapped deductively to the three constructs of the Theory of Planned Behavior (attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control), allowing integration of emergent insights with a structured theoretical framework.

Codes were modified and categorized into sub-themes within each construct by means of continuous comparison. Atlas.ti.24 was used for coding and theme management. A subset of transcripts was separately coded by three analysts (FUA, FA, MF) to increase credibility. Discrepancies were then reviewed and settled by consensus. The research team employed peer debriefing sessions to critically minimize individual bias and reflect on presumptions.

To enhance interpretive validity, efforts were made during interviews through probing, clarification, and summarizing participant responses to ensure accurate understanding. In addition, regular peer debriefing within the research team supported consistency in coding and interpretation. To further improve the analytical process’s dependability and transparency, an audit trail was kept recording coding choices, analytical notes, and theme development. The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist was used to guarantee the study’s rigor, transparency, and trustworthiness [32].

A total of 59.0% of study participants were female. The largest group was made up of LHWs (34.4%), followed by PHC providers (19.7%), LHSs (16.4%), RPs (14.7%), and TWG members (14.8%). The majority (70.5%) were employed by primary or community-level healthcare facilities, followed by tertiary hospitals (14.7%) and administrative positions (14.8%). Attitudes toward rehabilitation

This section presents findings related to providers’ attitudes toward rehabilitation, reflecting how perceptions of its value, emotional reinforcement, and role integration influenced their engagement with rehabilitation services over time, from early skepticism and unfamiliarity to an understanding of rehabilitation as a crucial component of comprehensive treatment. Emotional rewards from witnessing patient improvements further motivated providers and strengthened their involvement. At the same time, many physicians found it difficult to incorporate rehabilitation into their already demanding clinical obligations due to workload strain.

Shift in perception of rehabilitation’s value

At first, rehabilitation was seen as an incidental aspect of the job of many community and primary care professionals, such as Lady Health Workers (LHWs) and PHC employees. But as a result of training, recommendations, and face-to-face patient encounters, healthcare professionals began to see rehabilitation as essential to managing chronic illnesses, promoting mobility, and promoting general health. A member of the Technical Working Group considered this change, pointing out how rehabilitation became more widely acknowledged in the healthcare system:

They (rehabilitation initiatives) are accepted, especially because they address chronic issues like cardiovascular disease and diabetes, where rehabilitation plays a crucial role. At least something has started, and now doctors are also aware of it. For example, if someone is using a stick, and we teach them exercises so that they no longer need the stick, it’s very beneficial. There is definitely a need for this here. (Sw_TWG_01)

Rehabilitation providers stressed the importance of cross-disciplinary interaction and the chance to learn from one another; this acknowledgment also assisted in bridging professional silos.

It became clear that while we are experts in our own fields, there is still a vast ocean of work ahead. For others, it was also a surprise to see such a highly skilled physical rehabilitation team in place, as they weren’t aware of it. This interaction helped bridge that gap. (Th_RP_01)

At the local level, attitudes changed from doubt to support as new knowledge resulted in real-world improvements for families and made it possible for frontline staff to perceive themselves as active community rehabilitation facilitators rather than merely referrers:

After the training, I went back to my area. I noticed a 4.3-month-old child in my area who couldn’t stand. I referred her to Dr. Sahib, who recommended exercises and medication. With his efforts and the parents’ determination, the child has improved a lot … Now, when I visit the family, I see that one leg is completely straight, and the child can stand on it. The other leg is also showing improvement … For me, it’s a huge success that I was able to help the child by referring her. (Sw_LHW_10)

When taken as a whole, these observations show a distinct change: rehabilitation, which had previously been unknown or marginalized, began to be recognized as an essential part of treatment at the system, provider, and community levels.

Emotional reward and motivation

Deep emotional satisfaction was fostered among providers by observable improvements in patients’ lives, such as improved mobility, decreased pain, increased independence, and confidence in day-to-day activities. This strengthened their commitment to rehabilitation and moved it from a peripheral to a central aspect of their work. Providers at the PHC level talked about the life-changing effects of being able to assist people who were frequently disregarded and viewed as social liabilities:

We are dealing with people who have become isolated from society. In the end, only family members give them a bit of time, others don’t value them. Because these people are considered a burden, and they say, dependency… so these dependent people, if we are helpful to them, I think what greater work is there than this? And another thing is the biggest thing I saw was pain management without medicine. This was very significant to me… (Th_PHC_05)

Seeing noticeable improvements in patients gave LHWs a sense of accomplishment and inspired them to advocate for rehabilitation more broadly, bolstering their position as reliable community health resources:

Earlier, people didn’t know that rehabilitation was even possible. Many patients would just lay in bed for years because they didn’t know they could get better. After getting training, we realized that with some effort, these patients can recover… When we see a patient in our community with such conditions, we ask them if they know that treatment is possible. We talk to them about it and guide those who can benefit from rehabilitation. (Sw_LHW_08)

Community-level supervisors also reflected back on transformative experiences that strengthened their conviction about the importance of rehabilitation. One LHS explained how a prosthetic fitting helped a young teacher who had been amputated regain independence:

I had a patient who was a 17-year-old teacher teaching at a private school. Her legs were congenitally impaired. From childhood, she dragged her foot because she had a seizure due to fever, which damaged one of her legs. She underwent many treatments, but nothing was successful, and that leg was amputated. Then when rehabilitation program started, we provided her with awareness and sent her to a place where prosthetic limbs are fitted. Near the mosque, she was fitted with a prosthetic leg and received treatment. Programs like this make life easier for patients and greatly benefit the community. (Sw_LHS_03)

These encounters strengthened the providers’ commitment to helping patients access these services and reaffirmed their conviction in the benefits of rehabilitation.

Attitudinal response to role expansion

According to our interviews, providers increasingly recognized the value of rehabilitation; however, they expressed mixed attitudes regarding its integration into their existing roles. While the importance of rehabilitation was acknowledged, many participants perceived its inclusion in routine primary care duties as an added responsibility that extended beyond their expected scope of work, particularly in already high-pressure settings. This resulted in feelings of role strain and concerns regarding task allocation rather than rejection of rehabilitation itself.

PHC employees particularly described rehabilitation-related activities as an additional layer of responsibility on top of their existing workload.

One provider emphasized that:

Whenever I’ve discussed these matters before, I’ve mentioned that it’s (rehabilitation) overburdening. All the work is piled onto us. Being the M.O. Incharge feels like having a heavy burden placed on our shoulders. Honestly, I don’t even have time to drink water. (Th_PHC_05)

Similarly, LHWs highlighted concerns regarding role expansion and the need for clearer delineation of responsibilities within community health practice.As one LHW put it:

We say that our workload has increased with these changes… With rehabilitation, the workload has increased, and in our community, we want everything to be done properly… Because besides our regular visits, we are also doing this additional work, and the responsibilities have increased… (Sw_LHW_06)

Importantly, participants did not reject rehabilitation; instead, they suggested restructuring service delivery through task-sharing models. Many proposed that dedicated rehabilitation professionals (RPs) should handle core rehabilitation components, while primary providers focus on screening and referral pathways. This reflects an attitudinal preference for role optimization and delegation rather than reluctance toward rehabilitation services.According to a PHC provider:

For initial treatment, we manage things like controlling their sugar levels and caring for their wounds… but can’t address disability or provide artificial limbs. I can’t even provide a wheelchair here; we refer them for that. (Sw_PHC_05)

Overall, these findings indicate an ambivalent attitude shaped by recognition of rehabilitation’s value alongside concerns about role expansion and workload distribution within existing health system structures.

Subjective norms

Our data analysis revealed that provider behavior was influenced by subjective norms, including both professional expectations and socio-cultural pressures. Two major themes surfaced. First, regular discussion of rehabilitation cases was encouraged, and practices were reinforced by peer influence via WhatsApp, mentoring, and supervision. Second, patient uptake was restricted, particularly among women, by gender norms and the shame associated with disabilities and assistive technology.

Peer influence via whatsapp and supervision

Strong peer expectations fostered by social and professional networks, including supervisory visits and WhatsApp groups, promoted regular discussion of rehabilitation cases. WhatsApp became a useful platform for support and case sharing. For instance, a specialist in rehabilitation stated that:

We have a group, a WhatsApp group, where we discuss patient-related issues regularly. If there’s any problem with a patient, someone can bring it up in the group. (Sw_RP_02)

Even technical stakeholders recognized the platform’s contribution to facilitating rapid and easy collaboration from a systems perspective, particularly in situations where formal channels are less practical.

Because WhatsApp is used globally, right? Now, the LHW can’t send an email, but she can use WhatsApp. We even allowed them that if they have trouble typing, they can send a picture or a voice note. (Th_TWG_04)

In addition to digital networking, face-to-face monitoring and follow-up visits supported new professional standards related to rehabilitation, like reviewing cases frequently, making sure referrals are made on time, and validating the efforts of providers.

Visits have also been made to the primary healthcare facility units where doctors are present, and during these visits, further guidance or, you could say, a sort of followup training has also been conducted. (Th_RP_04)

In the same way, mentorship and peer interactions impacted skill adoption in addition to fostering a sense of shared responsibility. One RP observed that frequent interaction with PHC physicians increased their self-assurance in managing certain rehabilitation requirements on their own:

The thing is PHC doctors now understand rehabilitation and when to initiate it. Patients who used to be referred to me for minor issues are no longer being sent. These cases are handled by them. It’s only after two or three attempts, when they see that it’s not working, that they call me in. (Th_RP_06)

Digital communication, supervision, and mentoring all functioned together to mainstream rehabilitation as a standard component of care and promote shared accountability.

Gender norms and stigma around disability and AT use

According to providers, patients particularly young girls and elderly people were frequently deterred from accepting rehabilitative services or using assistive technology (AT) devices by cultural norms and gender limitations, such as Purdah. One of the biggest obstacles, according to providers, is the stigma associated with disabilities and AT use:

Older patients feel embarrassed or resist using assistive devices… They refuse to use sticks, glasses so these kinds of scenarios come up in the community. It’s still a challenge to overcome certain cultural barriers. There’s a reluctance due to the stigma regarding the use of assistive products. (Th_TWG_01)

Young girls were especially stigmatized because they frequently hid disabilities to avoid criticism. A TWG member shared a personal reflection:

I have polio. I initially hesitated to present myself in front of people or to tell anyone about it. Now, as a doctor, I know everything so I don’t feel hesitation anymore, But in villages and rural areas, expressing these things is very difficult in rural areas, especially for girls, there can be many more issues. They would rather endure the pain than using assistive products. Because they’re worried about what people will say… The pressure from society. They think, “What will people say?” So, because of that, they hide it… (Th_TWG_03).

The need for gender-sensitive service delivery methods was highlighted by providers who noted the logistical difficulties posed by female patients’ reluctance to seek rehabilitation services from male staff, which was another way that gender norms influenced clinical interactions.

Often female patients come and we cannot check or treat them… It becomes an issue for male doctors to manage female patients… In the rehabilitation department, if a female patient goes there, she is attended to by female staff and the majority of our patients are female. (Sw_PHC_04)

The opportunity cost of seeking rehabilitative care, where households must evaluate possible income loss against health benefits, was highlighted as additional hurdle related to mobility constraints imposed by male family members and the financial realities of travel.

They (patients) face difficulties because, often, the patient’s location is far away, and it’s hard for them to get there. They have issues with money and transportation costs. Sometimes, the men don’t let the women go alone, and the men themselves have little time. They might miss work if they go to the hospital. These are poor people. (Sw_LHW_01)

These data collectively demonstrate how favorable provider attitudes can be overridden by financial hurdles, stigma, and gender norms, hence restricting persistent engagement with rehabilitation demonstrating how broader social norms can override supportive professional norms.

Perceived behavioural control

Perceived behavioral control reflects both providers’ confidence and the structural constraints influencing their ability to deliver rehabilitation services. The data revealed three sub-themes. First, their abilities, self-efficacy, and readiness to incorporate rehabilitation were improved by training and resources. Second, despite motivation, action was hampered by operational difficulties and resource gaps, such as personnel shortages, high patient numbers, and a lack of assistive products. Third, in order to maintain rehabilitation services, clinicians used adaptive tactics, such as assigning rehab days, expediting referrals, and utilizing visual aids. Overall, the results indicate that while support and training might boost confidence, systemic investment is needed to remove structural impediments for sustained integration.

Training and tools as enablers

Building provider confidence required training and useful resources including checklists, referral slips, guidelines, and demonstration aids. Structured rehabilitation training sessions were frequently described as eye-opening, empowering staff to recognize rehabilitation requirements, provide referrals, and show fundamental activities. Despite systemic constraints, providers felt more capable as a result of this knowledge, which translated into increased self-efficacy. After receiving training, one LHW considered how her understanding had changed:

We received training about rehabilitation and learned what it is and learned that treatment is possible for disabled children. (Sw_LHW_05)

Technical stakeholders also noted how training helped cadres develop a wider range of capabilities.

The training was beneficial. It was a great learning experience for the doctors as well. They understood what rehabilitation is, and even LHWs benefited. For example, certain skills became second nature. Something they hadn’t thought of before suddenly became clear through the training. They learned exercises and how to perform them in the right way. So, if these trainings continue, it will be very good. (Th_TWG_02)

LHWs talked about applying their training to use in the real world by assisting adults and children with simple rehabilitation methods and making sure that referrals for more complicated needs were made:

The training we received helped us in our routine fieldwork. When we see children who seem weak, we help them with exercises. We do everything we learned in the training, exercises, massages, etc. We were taught this, and that’s what we do in the field… It (training) was mostly about referring clients who are helpless and can’t get around. (Th_LHW_11)

These skills were further strengthened by continued follow-up assistance, which also promoted continued application of rehabilitation practices:

The training sessions and follow-ups strengthened our understanding and implementation of rehabilitation at the community level. (Th_TWG_01)

These results demonstrate that clinicians feel more capable and are more inclined to include rehabilitation into their everyday practice in spite of systemic limitations when they have the necessary information, abilities, and resources indicating enhanced perceived behavioral control.

Operational burdens and resource gaps

Systemic limitations persisted in limiting providers’ capacity to deliver rehabilitation despite having confidence in providing these services. Low patient follow-through, staffing shortages, and assistive technology shortages were ongoing issues that weakened patient confidence and service delivery:

If they only get empty promises, they ask why they should go. (Sw_PHC_02)

This illustrates how a lack of material support can affect both patient compliance and physician motivation. This frustration was reflected by LHWs, who observed that patients were deterred by the lack of resources:

So, whenever I try to send other patients to the hospital, they ask me why I’m sending them if nothing (assistive devices) will be provided. (Sw_LHW_07)

Although they admitted that the lack of human resources made it difficult for them to continue providing these services in addition to their other duties, supervisors were nonetheless dedicated to advancing rehabilitation. One workable way to close this gap is as follows:

Additional workers should be employed for uncovered fields (remote areas). Or, as we already have LHWs working, we have some staff who work in the covered catchment areas, they should also be included. (Th_LHS_03)

Others cited the excessive patient volume and the dearth of physiotherapists and other specialist personnel as obstacles to delivering quality rehabilitation in primary care:

The challenge is that we have a large crowd. The challenge is that we can’t give them proper time. Ahh… ideally, each center should have a physiotherapist who looks after these things as well. If we weren’t so overwhelmed, if the number of patients coming in was limited, then there wouldn’t be an issue. (Th_PHC_04)

From the standpoint of perceived behavioral control, these narratives demonstrate that even while providers might be highly qualified and driven, structural deficiencies, a lack of resources, patient numbers, and staff shortages nevertheless limit action and undermine patient engagement and provider persistence highlighting constraints on perceived behavioral control despite high motivation.

Adaptive coping strategies

Providers created informal coping strategies that incorporated services into regular workflows in order to manage rehabilitation while handling high caseloads. These strategies included setting aside specific days for rehabilitation, expediting referrals, and depending on short visual aids. To balance workloads, PHC employees and LHWs planned rehabilitation for particular days:

For that (rehabilitation), I have specifically designated one day so that it can be attended to carefully and doesn’t affect my other work because there is an overburden, a huge OPD. (Th_PHC_01)

The utilization of patient education materials, referral charts, and visual aids were mentioned by providers as crucial facilitators. During brief consultation windows, these resources expedited rehabilitation conversations and made patient communication easier.

Suppose you show them something practical… Show them pictures. Then they won’t forget. Or if there’s a mobile or even if someone has a mobile, they take a picture of it. (Sw_PHC_04)

These tactics demonstrate how providers-maintained services in spite of workload and resource constraints by striking a balance between conflicting demands and a rising dedication to rehabilitation demonstrating adaptive strategies to sustain perceived behavioral control in resource-limited settings.

Overall, the findings demonstrate how provider attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control interact dynamically to shape engagement with rehabilitation services.

Discussion

This study used the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) [16] to examine find out how Pakistani healthcare providers responded to a systems-level rehabilitation intervention at primary-level facilities. The results are grouped according to the TPB constructs of attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control, show that provider behavior changed over time as a result of experience, training, and contextual obstacles and facilitators. Themes were interpreted in light of TPB constructs to explain not only what behaviors occurred, but also why they occurred, highlighting how experiential learning, peer influence, and resource constraints collectively affected provider engagement. This theoretically grounded approach enhances the explanatory and policy relevance of the findings.

Rehabilitation is now seen as essential to holistic care, particularly for chronic illness and disability, rather than as a burden or a secondary concern. These changes were supported by emotional satisfaction from observable patient improvements, which is in line with TPB data showing that favorable outcomes influence assessments and encourage behavior change [33]. Subjective norms strongly shaped practice. Peer networks, particularly WhatsApp groups, normalized case discussion and fostered collaborative environments. These social dynamics are supported by data from two studies that showed peer mentorship enhanced the quality of care for people with HIV [34] and diabetes [35]. However, peer-led models failed to address cultural hurdles, such as gender norms and the shame associated with assistive technology, especially for women. While the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) provided a useful framework for understanding provider behaviour, the findings of this study also point to broader social and structural determinants shaping access to rehabilitation. Barriers such as gendered mobility restrictions, stigma surrounding assistive technology, and financial constraints reflect systemic inequities rather than solely individual-level behavioral limitations. This highlights an important limitation of using a provider-centered behavioural framework such as TPB, which primarily captures provider cognition and decision-making but does not fully account for patient agency or rights-based dimensions of disability. In this study, people with disabilities are largely represented through provider perspectives, which may underrepresent their role as active participants in care. To address this, our interpretation incorporates a broader biopsychosocial perspective of disability, as articulated in the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) [36], which emphasizes the interaction between health conditions, environmental barriers, and social structures. This perspective situates provider behaviour within wider systems of social exclusion rather than viewing disability solely as a clinical problem.

To ensure analytical rigor, we conducted negative case analysis to identify instances where provider behaviors and experiences diverged from dominant patterns. While the majority of providers reported constructive attitudes, supportive norms, and confidence in delivering rehabilitation, some participants highlighted ongoing challenges. These included limited resources, competing workload priorities, difficulty coordinating referrals, and low patient adherence to recommended interventions. Incorporating these disconfirming cases strengthens the credibility of the analysis and underscores the role of structural constraints alongside provider-level factors.

As clinicians acquired knowledge, self-assurance, and referral resources, perceived behavioral control increased. However, structural barriers including workforce shortages, limited assistive products, and time constraints restricted the translation of intention into practice. This is consistent with the application of scientific literature, which emphasizes how, even in cases when human willingness exists, external obstacles frequently impede the successful conversion of desire and intentions into sustained practice [20, 22]. Nevertheless, providers demonstrated dynamic intention–behavior feedback by allocating therapy days, organizing referrals, and informally monitoring follow-up. TPB extensions, in which behavior reinforces attitudes and perceived control, are supported by this kind of experience adaptation [21]. These results are consistent with international studies showing how attitudes, peer norms, and self-efficacy support service integration in Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) [37].

Based on our qualitative data, we constructed a novel TPB-based model (Fig. 2). During rehabilitation integration within primary healthcare, the model demonstrates how TPB constructs interact to impact provider behavior. The model captures both enabling processes and persistent constraints. By successfully changing behavioral determinants, the program increased primary care rehabilitation uptake. However, social stigma and a lack of resources pose a danger to equal delivery and sustainability.

TPB model applied to provider behaviour in rehabilitation service delivery

The variation in populations described across the findings reflects the absence of a standardized disability classification framework within routine service delivery. Rather than applying formal national categories, providers engaged with patients based on functional limitations encountered in practice. While this approach aligns with functional models of rehabilitation, it also highlights a disconnect between administrative disability classifications and real-world clinical needs, which may contribute to inconsistencies in service planning and delivery. Despite strengthened peer norms, gender and cultural barriers continued to limit access, particularly for women, indicating the need for community sensitization and gender-responsive strategies.

This study demonstrates how even small inputs, like peer support and basic training, can spur behavioral change and alter service objectives and professional identities. Similar enabling processes have been documented in integrated care for Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD), where implementation was sustained by feedback loops and provider empowerment [38]. According to our providers’ experiences, individualized performance feedback has also improved the adoption of evidence-based procedures in primary care [39]. These findings reinforce the interdependence of psychological readiness and structural support, consistent with WHO’s Rehabilitation 2030 priorities [40].

Our results confirm the applicability of TPB in implementation research, while also suggesting that behavior functions both as an outcome and a driver of change. In line with dual-process models like the Integrated Behavioural Model (IBM) and TPB extensions, repeated successes altered attitudes and norms and strengthened motivation [41]. This reflects embedded feedback loops within routine practice.

This study provides novel insights into rehabilitation integration in Pakistan, where services remain concentrated in urban tertiary settings. Findings highlight how provider attitudes, peer norms, and perceived behavioral control interact within system constraints such as workforce shortages, limited assistive technology, and gendered mobility barriers. Unlike prior studies, this research demonstrates the role of informal peer networks, such as WhatsApp groups, in supporting provider engagement and facilitating referral practices. By situating these findings within the TPB framework, the study not only confirms international evidence but also shows how local social and structural realities shape rehabilitation uptake.

Overall, the study makes a theoretical contribution by demonstrating how behavior in low-resource settings is both shaped by and reinforces TPB constructs. Practically, the findings highlight the need to align behavioral interventions with system-level support, community engagement, and equitable resource distribution resource distribution.

Practical and policy implications

In addition to system- and policy-level initiatives, sustained integration of rehabilitation into primary care necessitates consideration of provider attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control.

Rehabilitation duties should be explicitly included in job descriptions and performance reviews in order to improve attitudes. Frequent, skill-based training helps maintain attitudinal change, boost confidence, and underline the need for rehabilitation, especially in the areas of communication and motivational interviewing.

Strong motivators included supervisory recognition and peer influence. Health systems ought to facilitate structured meetings and monitor online forums for problem-solving and peer education. Culturally sensitive initiatives are required at the community level to combat the gender-based stigma associated with assistive technologies and disabilities, particularly in areas where women’s access is restricted by purdah and restrictive customs.

Improved confidence from training and job aids was often undermined by systemic gaps. To strengthen behavioural control, consistent availability of assistive products, functional referral pathways, and workload relief through workforce expansion are essential.

Systemic gaps frequently weakened the gains in confidence brought forth by training and job assistance. Workload reduction through workforce expansion, functional referral pathways, and constant availability of assistive items are crucial for enhancing behavioral control.

Rehabilitation needs to be specifically incorporated into Pakistan’s primary healthcare frameworks, funding, and monitoring systems in order to connect these behavioral insights to system-level action. It is essential to allocate funds specifically for outreach, workforce expansion, and the supply of assistive technologies. Accountability and fair access will be guaranteed by integrating rehabilitation indicators into health information systems and adhering to WHO’s Rehabilitation 2030 [40] goals. Lastly, in order to address clinical requirements alongside the broader social determinants of disability, intersectoral collaboration across health, social welfare, and education is essential.

Strengths and limitations

This study, which offers theory-informed insights on provider attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control, is the first that we are aware of that applies the Theory of Planned Behavior to the delivery of rehabilitation services in Pakistan. The study gathers a multifaceted and complex understanding of the ways in which systemic facilitators and limitations interact with behavioral determinants through qualitative interviews with a range of stakeholders from various cadres. Purposive sampling in two different districts, perspective triangulation, iterative coding, and reflective conversations among researchers all contributed to the methodological rigor, which increased the findings’ legitimacy and generalizability.

A number of limitations must also be noted. The results of this qualitative study are context-specific and not generalizable, and there may be some selection bias in the deliberate selection of study participants. Despite efforts to reduce recollection and social desirability bias through field observation and peer debriefing, relying on self-reported narratives increases the risk of these biases. Crucially, knowledge of demand-side obstacles and attitudes toward rehabilitation services is constrained by the lack of firsthand accounts from patients and caregivers. The use of a provider-centered behavioural framework may have limited the exploration of patient perspectives and agency, which should be considered in future research. In order to evaluate the sustainability of provider behavior change and its effect on service adoption, future research should incorporate these viewpoints and use mixed-method approaches. Participants’ responses may have been influenced by their affiliation with the ReLAB-HS program, including prior training, exposure to program objectives, and familiarity with research team members. While efforts were made to minimize social desirability bias, such program-related influence cannot be fully ruled out.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that peer support, targeted training, and the emotional reward of witnessing patient improvement can enhance provider engagement with rehabilitation services, even within resource-constrained environments at the primary healthcare level. However, changing behavior at the individual level is not enough to promote fair and long-lasting access to rehabilitation. The simultaneous removal of structural and cultural obstacles, such as stigma related to gender, a lack of referral systems, and uneven access to assistive technology, is necessary for long-lasting change. Healthcare systems must take a comprehensive strategy that incorporates institutional investment, provider capacity-building, and cultural responsiveness if behaviorally informed interventions are to reach their full potential. Considering how gendered social norms and stigma greatly influence provider behavior and patient access, future interventions must proactively address these issues. In addition to being in line with the fundamental tenets of Universal Health Coverage (UHC), these initiatives are crucial to achieving the objectives of the WHO Rehabilitation 2030 agenda [40], which aims to provide universal access to integrated, person-centered rehabilitation treatments.
