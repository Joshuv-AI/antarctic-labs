<div class="node__content clearfix"><div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-body field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p><strong>Washington, D.C., June 25, 2025</strong> – During August 1976 talks with the Shah of Iran over the sale of U.S.

nuclear reactors, U.S.

Secretary of State Henry Kissinger “made it clear that reprocessing [spent nuclear fuel] in Iran on a purely national basis would not be an acceptable solution” and proposed a U.S.

“buy back” of used fuel rods from Iran, according to records featured in a new collection of primary source documents on the history of U.S.

nuclear nonproliferation policy under Presidents Nixon and Ford.

(Document 11)</p><p>Other records in the new Digital National Security Archive (DNSA) publication detail how the U.S.

intelligence community evaluated information indicating that South Korea—a close U.S.

ally ruled by longtime dictator Park Chung Hee—had begun a covert nuclear weapons program.

In one recently declassified memo from January 1975 titled “South Korean Capabilities for Nuclear Weapons Development,” U.S.

intelligence analysts doubted that South Korea “could produce a nuclear device in less than ten years even if its program proceeded relatively smoothly.” Key parts of the CIA report were released only recently after a declassification appeal by the National Security Archive.

(Document 7)</p><p>While the U.S.

and its Canadian and French allies were ultimately able to persuade South Korea to set aside its attempt to acquire reprocessing technology, if not its nuclear weapons aspirations altogether, the new DNSA collection,&nbsp;<em>U.S.

Nuclear Nonproliferation 2, Part II: The Nixon-Ford Years, 1969-1976</em>, also documents a number of cases where U.S.and its Canadian and French allies were ultimately able to persuade South Korea to set aside its attempt to acquire reprocessing technology, if not its nuclear weapons aspirations altogether, the new DNSA collection,&nbsp;<em>U.S.

Nuclear Nonproliferation 2, Part II: The Nixon-Ford Years, 1969-1976</em>, also documents a number of cases where U.S.

efforts to thwart the spread of nuclear technology and material, even to allied and friendly governments, were less successful, such as India, Pakistan and Israel, and features key declassified records on a range of nonproliferation issues, including:</p><ul style="margin-left:20px;"><li>The discovery, during late 1960, of Israel’s secret nuclear reactor at Dimona and its possible weapons objective.

(Document 1)</li><li>U.S.

policy to restrict the spread of gas centrifuge technology and the Nixon administration’s approach to the British-Dutch-West German program to establish a consortium to produce nuclear reactor fuel using the technology.

(Document 2)</li><li>The creation of a nuclear safeguards system by the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), which was designed to verify compliance with the NPT.

(Document 3)</li><li>The role of IAEA officials in providing informal intelligence on Taiwan’s secret efforts to acquire nuclear reprocessing technology for a weapons program.

(Document 4)</li><li>India’s May 1974 nuclear test, which jarred the U.S.

government and shocked the Canadians, who had supplied the reactor that provided spent fuel for plutonium reprocessing.

(Document 5)</li><li>The policy review ordered by Kissinger following the Indian test that gave higher priority to the proliferation issue in an effort to try to slow a process that he saw as “inevitable.” (Document 6)</li><li>Efforts by the Ford administration to encourage governments that had not ratified the NPT to do so—especially Japan, where the Liberal Democratic Party claimed uncertainty about U.S.

interest in the NPT.

(Document 8)</li><li>The U.S.interest in the NPT.

(Document 8)</li><li>The U.S.

discovery, in 1974, that Pakistan had embarked on a secret program to develop nuclear weapons, which became a matter of high-level concern in the Ford administration, especially as it became evident that Pakistan had a contract with France to purchase a reprocessing plant.

(Document 9)</li><li>The U.S.

pressure campaign on South Korea, fortified by cooperation with Canada and France, to forego the acquisition of a nuclear reprocessing plant that could be used for producing plutonium.

(Document 10)</li></ul><p>The new collection from ProQuest (part of Clarivate) is the latest installment in the DNSA series—which now consists of 65 distinct document collections—and includes over 1,400 documents on an array of other nonproliferation flashpoints, such as:</p><ul style="margin-left:20px;"><li>Debates within the Nixon administration during 1969 over whether to pressure Israel to sign the NPT, and abandon its weapons program, or to accommodate its secret nuclear status.</li><li>U.S.

ratification of the NPT in 1970 and the Treaty’s entry into force.</li><li>India’s acquisition of plutonium through Canadian-supplied technology, its persistent interest in “peaceful nuclear explosions,” and the unsuccessful U.S.

effort to persuade India to change course.</li><li>Indications, during 1972, that India might stage a nuclear test.</li><li>U.S.

participation in the Zangger Committee, organized by Swiss political scientist Claude Zangger to implement NPT guidelines on nuclear technology exports.</li><li>The creation of the Nuclear Suppliers’ Group following India’s acquisition of plutonium from an unsafeguarded Canadian-supplied reactor to establish stricter guidelines on the export of sensitive technology to non-nuclear states.</li><li>Renewed attention to Taiwan’s nuclear weapons quest during 1976 as U.S.effort to persuade India to change course.</li><li>Indications, during 1972, that India might stage a nuclear test.</li><li>U.S.

participation in the Zangger Committee, organized by Swiss political scientist Claude Zangger to implement NPT guidelines on nuclear technology exports.</li><li>The creation of the Nuclear Suppliers’ Group following India’s acquisition of plutonium from an unsafeguarded Canadian-supplied reactor to establish stricter guidelines on the export of sensitive technology to non-nuclear states.</li><li>Renewed attention to Taiwan’s nuclear weapons quest during 1976 as U.S.

intelligence learned about secret reprocessing on the island and Taiwanese talks with European suppliers of reprocessing technology.</li><li>U.S.

negotiations with Iran during 1975-1976 over the sale of U.S.

nuclear reactors, which involved difficult-to-resolve disputes over Tehran’s freedom of action for using nuclear technology, especially the reprocessing of spent fuel.</li><li>The Ford administration’s review of nonproliferation policy during the summer and fall of 1976, substantially prompted by presidential rival Jimmy Carter’s emphasis on the danger of nuclear proliferation.</li></ul><p>The DNSA set also includes over 100 documents from the 1950s and 1960s that were declassified too late for inclusion in the Archive’s previous nonproliferation collection, covering 1954-1968.

All documents are easily searchable by subject matter, names, and dates, among other indexing categories.</p><p>The sources of the documents for this collection are varied.

Many are from the National Archives, some of them declassified for this project.

NARA sources include Record Group 59 (State Department records) and Record Group 84 (Foreign Service Posts).

Valuable collections in RG 84 include the records of the U.S.

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>Henry J. Gomberg, a professor of nuclear engineering at the University of Michigan, provided information to the State Department and other agencies that helped them connect the dots about the purposes of Israel’s secret Dimona nuclear reactor.<a href="#_edn1" name="_ednref1">[1]</a> With the support of the International Cooperation Administration (a forerunner of USAID), he had been in Israel to work at the <u>Technion</u> on a training program to familiarize students of the peaceful uses of nuclear energy. Before Gomberg left Israel, he briefed U.S. Ambassador Ogden Reid and Embassy staffers on his concerns about the Israeli atomic project. He had developed suspicions when he discovered that the Israelis were working on projects that they would not discuss with him. Gomberg learned that Israeli scientists were seeking to produce gram amounts of plutonium, which was “significant” because “amounts of this size could conceivably find use in weapons.” Responding to a question from Reid, Gomberg “thought it was conceivable that Israel could have weapon capability in this field in less than 10 years.” As it turned out, seven years later the Israelis had a workable bomb.</p></div>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>Since the early 1960s, the U.S. government had closely monitored Western European efforts to develop gas centrifuge technology for producing highly enriched uranium as reactor fuel. Because of the gas centrifuge’s relevance to weapons proliferation, Washington had persuaded the Dutch, West Germans, and the British to apply secrecy controls to the technology to prevent its further dissemination. By the close of the decade, the three countries were working to combine their resources to use the gas centrifuge for producing enriched uranium on an industrial scale. The U.S. government was moderately supportive of the tripartite project, seeing it as compatible with the NPT and as a worthy example of West European cooperation, although it would produce a new competitor in world enriched uranium markets.</p><p>One complication that the AEC shared with the Joint Committee on Atomic Energy was that the British plan to share their centrifuge model with the Dutch and the West Germans would involve the transfer of technology derived from secret U.S. “<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restricted_Data">restricted data.</a>” Yet after meeting with the British and inspecting their centrifuge model, AEC experts decided that it was in the U.S. interest not to interfere. In part, this was because the British model was “unadvanced” compared to U.S. gas centrifuge designs: “it does not appear to be desirable to force them to a more advanced model earlier than their present schedules would seem to call for.” According to the AEC, “From the standpoint of proliferation and potential competition with the U.S. in the foreign uranium market, there is some advantage to leaving things as they are.” The Tripartite project would eventually build a centrifuge plant in the Netherlands that hired Pakistani metallurgist A.Q. Khan who stole design information for Pakistani’s nuclear weapons program.</p></div>partite project would eventually build a centrifuge plant in the Netherlands that hired Pakistani metallurgist A.Q. <div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>The NPT included requirements for the International Atomic Energy Agency to establish and implement verification arrangements to ensure that countries receiving nuclear technology did not divert those resources into the production of fissile material for weapons. According to the CIA, the “verification program required by the NPT is the first significant attempt to enforce an arms control agreement by on-site inspection.”</p><p>Once the Treaty entered into force in 1970, IEAE members began negotiations on inspection requirements, including their “frequency and intensity.” The negotiations were prolonged, but the U.S. attained its “basic objective when it secured for the IAEA the right to perform inspections on 24-hour notice and on a continuous basis wherever necessary to ensure knowledge of how fissionable material was being used.” Some key countries, including Japan and West Germany, favored a “longer notification period,” but the other negotiators appeased them with “explicit” statements that the IAEA “would have no role in the design of nuclear installations and that inspectors were to “make every effort to minimize any practical difficulties for facility operators and the state.”<a href="#_edn2" name="_ednref2">[2]</a></p></div>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>With the explosion of oil prices and increased accumulation of revenues, the Shah of Iran sought to develop a nuclear energy sector as a domestic power alternative to oil and gas. The Shah’s objective raised proliferation concerns in part because he proposed developing a domestic capacity to reprocess spent fuel that could be used as reactor fuel. That raised the risk of plutonium production that could be used for bombs.</p><p>The U.S.-Iran nuclear negotiations coincided with ongoing discussions in the Ford White House on the need to develop credible international alternatives to domestic reprocessing to manage proliferation risk. During talks with the Shah, the U.S. proposed buying back the spent fuel rods and reprocessing them in the United States as a less expensive alternative to reprocessing in Iran. At that point, uranium enrichment options and technology were not part of the talks with Iran.</p><p>When Kissinger met with the Shah in early August 1976, he spelled out the options, including the buy-back option. Kissinger made it clear that “reprocessing in Iran on a purely national basis would not be an acceptable solution.” That seemed acceptable to the Shah and his advisers, but they worried about the reliability of the United States as a supplier of reactor fuel, among other concerns. The talks were friendly, but the Iran nuclear deal would remain unsettled until 1978, not long before the fall of the Shah.</p></div>
