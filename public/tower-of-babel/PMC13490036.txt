The transcription factor ZNF217 drives hepatocellular carcinoma progression by activating de novo lipid synthesis via FASN upregulation
Authors: Yihui Fang, Ting Zhang, Tianxing Ye, Qizhen He, Qinong Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic reprogramming, particularly enhanced lipid synthesis, is a hallmark of cancer that supports tumor growth and progression. However, the transcriptional regulators controlling this process in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remain incompletely understood.MethodsA genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 knockout screen identified ZNF217 as a key regulator of lipid metabolism in HCC. The regulatory effects of ZNF217 were analyzed using RNA sequencing, RT-qPCR, western blotting, and dual luciferase reporter assay. Intracellular lipid content was assessed using flow cytometry, oil red O staining, and triglyceride/cholesterol detection kits. Cell proliferation, migration, and invasion were assessed in vitro. In vivo tumor growth and metastasis were evaluated using nude mouse models. Clinical relevance was analyzed using immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis.ResultsWe identify the transcription factor ZNF217 as a key activator of de novo lipogenesis in HCC. ZNF217 binds to and transactivates the promoters of critical lipogenic enzymes, including fatty acid synthase (FASN) and acetyl-CoA carboxylase 1 (ACC1). ZNF217-driven lipid synthesis, cell proliferation, migration, and invasion were dependent on FASN expression, as demonstrated by pharmacological inhibition and genetic knockdown. ZNF217 overexpression promoted HCC tumor growth and lung metastasis in a FASN-dependent manner in vivo. Clinically, ZNF217 was significantly upregulated in HCC tissues, positively correlated with FASN expression, and associated with poor patient survival.ConclusionsOur findings establish ZNF217 as a novel transcriptional regulator of lipid metabolism in HCC, linking its oncogenic function to the activation of the lipogenic pathway, and propose the ZNF217-FASN axis as a candidate therapeutic target for HCC treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00432-026-06535-4.

Introduction

Metabolic reprogramming is a defining hallmark of cancer, enabling malignant cells to sustain proliferation and survival in nutrient-fluctuating and often scarce microenvironments (Jiang et al. 2023; Lin et al. 2024a, 2024b; Perez et al. 2024). Lipid metabolism, particularly the enhancement of lipid synthesis, plays a critical role in this process (Röhrig and Schulze 2016; Cheng et al. 2018; Li et al. 2021, 2024). It provides not only essential membrane components and energy sources for tumor cells but also serves as signaling molecules that drive tumor progression.

To meet heightened metabolic demands, cancer cells acquire lipids primarily through two pathways: de novo synthesis, involving the endogenous generation of fatty acids from acetyl-CoA, and exogenous uptake, where extracellular lipids are internalized via specific transporters and receptors (Butler et al. 2020). De novo lipid synthesis enables cancer cells to generate fatty acids autonomously, independent of external sources (Menendez and Lupu 2007). This process is tightly regulated by key enzymes, including acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), ATP citrate lyase (ACLY), fatty acid synthase (FASN), and stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (SCD1), which are frequently overexpressed in malignancies (Yamauchi et al. 2011; Zaytseva et al. 2012; Dibble et al. 2022; Wei et al. 2023). Their upregulation is often driven by oncogenic signals such as phosphatidylinositol 3-Kinase/AK strain transforming (PI3K/AKT) and the transcription factor sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (SREBP-1), enhancing lipogenesis to support membrane biogenesis and oncogenic signaling. However, the regulatory factors governing lipid synthesis remain incompletely characterized.

In this study, we employed genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 screening and identified the transcription factor ZNF217, previously reported to promote tumor progression (Nonet et al. 2001; Rooney et al. 2004; Quinlan et al. 2007; Krig et al. 2010; Littlepage et al. 2012; Vendrell et al. 2012) but with an unclear role in lipid synthesis, as a potent regulator that enhances lipid synthesis in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells. We show that ZNF217 upregulates key lipogenic enzymes, including FASN, ACLY, and ACC, thereby promoting HCC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and metastasis. Clinically, ZNF217 expression is upregulated in HCC tissues, correlates positively with FASN levels, and is associated with reduced patient survival. These findings establish ZNF217 as a critical activator of lipid synthesis in HCC.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cell culture

Human hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines HepG2 and human embryonic kidney cell line HEK-293 T were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line MHCC-97H was purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, EXcell) and 1% Penicillin–Streptomycin-Amphotericin B Solution 100 × (TargetMol). Cells were cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO2 in a humidified atmosphere. All cell lines were routinely screened and confirmed mycoplasma-negative.

Genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 knockout library screening

A CRISPR/Cas9 lentiviral library (Addgene #1,000,000,048 and #1,000,000,049) comprising 65,386 single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting 19,052 human proteincoding genes was utilized. HepG2 cells were transduced with the lentiviral library at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of ~ 0.3. Puromycin selection (1 μg/mL) was applied for 14 days for establishment of a stable HepG2-Cas9 cell library. For lipid content-based screening, library cells were stained with LipidTOX™ green neutral lipid stain (Invitrogen) and subjected to fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) for isolation of lipid-enriched populations. Following expansion of the sorted cells, genomic DNA was extracted from 3 × 10⁷ cells. Integrated sgRNA sequences were amplified from genomic DNA using PrimerSTAR HS DNA Polymerase (Takara) and subsequently analyzed by Illumina high-throughput sequencing. The sequencing data were analyzed to identify candidate genes regulating lipid metabolism. Candidate genes were ranked based on statistical criteria, with a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of 0.05 and |log2 fold change|≥ 1.

Plasmids, small interfering RNAs (siRNA), short Hairpin RNAs (shRNA), lentiviruses, and reagents

The targeted sequences of siRNAs and shRNAs used in knockdown experiments are listed in Supplementary Table S1. A ZNF217 expression vector was constructed by cloning the PCR-amplified coding region into pcDNA3 (Invitrogen). ACC1 and FASN promoter luciferase reporters were generated through ligation of PCR-amplified promoter fragments (amplified from genomic DNA) into pGL4-basic vector (Promega). shRNA constructs targeting ZNF217 and FASN were cloned into pSIH-H1-Puro (System Biosciences). Lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfection of HEK293T cells with recombinant lentiviral vectors and pPACK packaging plasmid mix using Megatran (Origene), followed by viral supernatant collection at 48–72 h post-transfection. HCC cells were transduced with lentivirus in the presence of polybrene (10 μg/mL), with stable pools selected using puromycin (1 μg/mL). For rescue experiments, ZNF217-knockdown cells were transduced with shRNA-resistant ZNF217 lentivirus, and stable reconstituted cells were isolated for subsequent analyses. Plasmid transfections were performed using polyethylenimine (PEI, Yeasen), while siRNA transfections were conducted with Lipofectamine RNAiMax (Invitrogen), according to manufacturers' protocols. Antibodies used included: Anti-ZNF217 (67730-1-Ig), anti-FASN (10624-2-AP), anti-ACLY (15421-1-AP), and anti-ACC1 (21923-1-AP) from Proteintech; anti-β-actin-HRP (sc-47778) and HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (sc-2748) from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. BODIPY 493/503 was obtained from Invitrogen.

Flow cytometry

Cells were washed with ice-cold PBS containing 3% FBS, followed by incubation with BODIPY 493/503 (1 μg/mL) for 20 min at 4 °C in the dark. After two washes with PBS/3% FBS, cells were resuspended in 300 μL PBS and filtered through a 35-μm nylon mesh. Cell suspensions were analyzed on a BD FACS Canto™ II flow cytometer, with 1 × 104 events recorded per sample. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo v10.8.1 (BD Biosciences) with live-cell gating based on forward/side scatter.

Oil red O staining

Cells grown on coverslips were washed twice with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After fixation, cells were incubated with freshly prepared 60% Oil Red O working solution (Solarbio) for 15 min. Following two washes with PBS, counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin for 30 s. Coverslips were mounted with aqueous glycerol mounting medium and imaged immediately using an optical microscope. Lipid droplet quantification was performed by threshold-based analysis of red channel intensity using ImageJ software.

BODIPY 493/503 staining

BODIPY 493/503 staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Invitrogen). Briefly, cells were seeded onto coverslips in 24-well plates and incubated overnight. After PBS washing, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Following two PBS washes, cells were stained with BODIPY 493/503 (1 μg/mL in PBS) for 25 min at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (5 μg/mL) for 5 min. Images were obtained using a fluorescence microscope.

Triglyceride and cholesterol assay

Cells were harvested and lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) supplemented with protease inhibitors. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Triglyceride and total cholesterol concentrations in supernatants were determined using commercial assay kits (Applygen Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with absorbance measured at 550 nm on a microplate reader. For tissue analyses, 10-mg portions were homogenized in 300 μL lysis buffer. Homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Resulting supernatants were assayed for both triglycerides and cholesterol using the aforementioned kits. All values were normalized to total protein concentration determined by BCA assay (Pierce).

Long-chain fatty acid (LCFA) assay

Following the indicated treatments, 1 × 10⁷ cells were harvested and sent for analysis to Beijing Qinglian Bio-Company (QLBio). LCFA levels were determined by ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS). Briefly, separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-CLASS system with a reverse-phase column, and detection was carried out on a Waters XEVO TQ-S Micro mass spectrometer operating in negative ESI-MRM mode. Quantification was based on standard curves using TargetLynx software (Waters) for data processing.

Transcriptome sequencing (RNA-Seq)

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Vazyme) and purified with RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). RNA quality was verified via NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher) and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. rRNA depletion was performed using Ribo-Zero Gold Kit (Illumina), followed by cDNA library construction with VAHTS mRNA Capture Beads (Vazyme). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (paired-end 150 bp, ≥ 10 million reads/sample). RNA-seq analysis was carried out by Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd. Differential expression analysis was conducted with DESeq2 (P-value < 0.05, |log₂FC|≥ 1). RNA-Seq data are available in the Gene Expression Omnibus database under accession number GSE313412 (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/).

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail for 30 min on ice. Protein concentrations in supernatants were determined using a BCA assay kit (Vazyme). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed in TBST (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20, pH 7.6) for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following three 10-min TBST washes, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary an-tibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

RNA extraction and reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and then 1 μg of total RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA by using a Reverse Transcription Kit (Vazyme). Quantitative real-time PCR reactions were performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq (Takara) on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR System. Relative changes of gene expression were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

Cells were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured overnight to 70–80% confluency. Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) with the following plasmid ratios per well: 400 ng firefly luciferase reporter vector, 40 ng Renilla luciferase control vector, and 400 ng expression plasmid. After 24-h incubation, cells were lysed with 100 μL 1 × Passive Lysis Buffer (Vazyme) for 15 min at room temperature with gentle agitation. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Clarified supernatants (20 μL) were transferred to opaque 96-well plates. Luciferase activities were quantified using a GloMax Discover System (Promega). Firefly luciferase activity was measured immediately after injection of 50 μL Luciferase Assay Reagent II. Subsequently, 50 μL Stop & Glo Reagent was injected to quench firefly activity and activate Renilla luciferase. The firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity as an internal control.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

ChIP experiments were performed using the Magna ChIP G Assay Kit (Millipore) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were harvested after fixation with 1% formaldehyde and resuspended in lysis buffer. The supernatants were collected after sonication and incubated with ZNF217 antibodies or normal IgG and Protein G magnetic beads. The beads were washed, and the bound chromatin complexes were eluted, purified, and reverse-crosslinked at 62 °C for 2 h, followed by incubation at 95 °C for 10 min. ChIP-enriched DNA was analyzed by real-time PCR using primer sequences listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Cell proliferation

Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 Kit (TargetMol) and colony formation assay. For CCK-8 assay, cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells/well. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, cell viability was measured at 24-h intervals over five time points. Absorbance was then read at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Each time point was assayed in triplicate. For colony formation assay, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells/well. After 14 days, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and stained with 0.5% crystal violet solution for 30 min. Colonies were scanned, and the number was quantified using ImageJ software.

Migration assay

Cells were seeded at 90% confluency in 6-well plates. A 200 μL pipette tip was used to create a straight scratch in the center of each well, and the detached cells were then washed with PBS. Cells were continually cultured for 16 h, and the rate of wound closure was measured. Images were taken at 0 and 16 h, and the relative wound width was quantified using ImageJ software.

Invasion assay

Cells were resuspended in serum-free media and added to the upper chamber of a 24-well transwell insert (Corning Costar), which was covered with 200 μL Matrigel (1:8 dilution in serum-free media). The lower chamber was added with 700 μL media containing 20% FBS. After incubation for 16 h, cells on the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 30 min. Non-invading cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed with a cotton swab. Invading cells were photographed using a microscope and quantified using ImageJ software.

Analysis of tumor growth and metastasis in nude mice

All animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care Committee of Academy of Military Medical Sciences. Briefly, 6-week-old male BALB/c nude mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 6 per group). A total of 1 × 10⁷ HCC cells were subcutaneously injected into the flank of each mouse. Seven days after tumor colonization, diluted DMSO (control, final concentration of 1%) and cerulenin (20 mg/kg) was intraperitoneally injected three times a week for two consecutive weeks. Tumor size was monitored using calipers every 7 days for 42 days. The tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: volume = (length × width2)/2. After euthanasia, the tumors were excised for further study. For lung metastasis study, 1 × 106 HCC cells stably transduced with luciferase-expressing lentivirus containing the indicated expression vectors were injected into the lateral tail vein of male BALB/c nude mice (6 weeks old; n = 6 per group). On the second day, diluted DMSO (control, final concentration of 1%) and cerulenin (20 mg/kg) were injected intraperitoneally. The injection was carried out three times a week for two consecutive weeks. Lung metastasis was monitored in vivo using an IVIS200 imaging system (Xenogen Corporation) 30 days after injection. Following imaging, mice were euthanized, and lungs were excised for metastatic foci analysis.

Human clinical samples

Ninety paired HCC tumor and adjacent normal tissues were obtained from Chinese PLA General Hospital, with the informed consent of patients and approval of the Institutional Review Committees of the Chinese PLA General Hospital. The follow-up data of 72 patients were available.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining

The paraffin sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed using primary antibodies against ZNF217, and FASN, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. The ZNF217 signals were amplified using the TSA plus fluorescein system (K1050, APExBIO) and the FASN signals were amplified using the TSA plus cyanine 3 system (K1051, APExBIO). Protein expression of ZNF217 and FASN was evaluated by an H-score, which was determined by multiplying the percentage of stained cells (0–100%) by the staining intensity (low, 1 + ; medium, 2 + ; strong, 3 +). The median number was used as the cutoff value. We defined scores ≤ 1.305 and > 1.305 as low and high ZNF217 and scores ≤ 1.36 and > 1.36 as low and high FASN. H&E staining was performed using a Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining Kit (Beyotime) according the manufacturer’s protocol. Tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin, respectively. The hematoxylin solution was used to stain the cell nuclei, making them appear blue-purple, while eosin solution was used to stain the cytoplasm and other cellular structures, making them appear pink or red.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 and GraphPad Prism 8. Two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for two comparisons. One-way ANOVA or two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni correction was used for multiple comparisons. The correlation between ZNF217 and FASN expression was analyzed using Spearman’s correlation test. The chi-square test and Fisher's exact probability method were used to analyze the correlation between ZNF217 or FASN expression and clinicopathological parameters in HCC patients. Overall survival and progression-free interval curves were generated by the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between survival curves were compared by the log-rank test. Cox regression model was used for multivariate analysis. All experiments were conducted at least three times and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Identification of ZNF217 as a key activator of de novo lipid synthesis in HCC cells

Through a genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 knockout screen followed by ranking of candidate genes based on statistical criteria (FDR = 0.05, |log2 fold change|≥ 1) (Table S4), we identified three transcription factors—ZNF217, ZNF689, and ZNF746—that were among the top-ranked candidates, and their knockout reduced lipid content in human hepatoma HepG2 cells. (Li et al. 2021). Using SREBP-1c as a positive control, we further demonstrated that knockdown of ZNF217 exerted the most pronounced inhibitory effect on lipid accumulation among these candidates (Fig. 1A). Based on this finding, ZNF217 was selected for subsequent investigation. The specificity of the ZNF217 antibody was confirmed in cells expressing ZNF217 shRNA (Fig. S1A). To validate ZNF217 function and rule out off-target effects, rescue experiments were performed using a shRNA-resistant ZNF217 overexpression plasmid. The results confirmed that the plasmid restored ZNF217 protein expression without affecting the shRNA-resistant ZNF217 mRNA, while the shRNA specifically reduced endogenous ZNF217 mRNA, demonstrating the specificity of the rescue effect (Fig. S1B). Both flow cytometry and Oil Red O staining consistently showed that ZNF217 knockdown significantly reduced lipid droplet levels, an effect that was fully reversed upon ZNF217 re-expression (Fig. 1B, C and Fig. S1C, D). Moreover, ZNF217 knockdown decreased intracellular triglyceride and cholesterol levels, as well as long-chain fatty acid (LCFA) levels, and reintroduction of ZNF217 in knockdown cells reversed these effects (Fig. 1D, E and Fig. S1E, F). ZNF217 promotes the expression of lipid synthesis-related genes

Given that ZNF217 is a known transcription factor, we utilized data from RNA-seq to investigate its role in gene regulation. Volcano plot and heatmap analyses demonstrated that ZNF217 regulates the expression of multiple genes involved in lipid synthesis, including key enzymes in fatty acid biosynthesis such as ACLY, ACC1, and FASN (Fig. 2A, B). Both RT-qPCR and immunoblotting confirmed that ZNF217 knockdown downregulated the expression of these genes, while re-expression of ZNF217 in knockdown cells restored their expression levels (Fig. 2C, D). The ZNF217 binding sites within the promoter regions of FASN and ACC1 were identified based on previously published ChIP-chip data (Krig et al. 2007), which defined the core consensus motif for ZNF217. To determine how ZNF217 modulates the transcription of these genes, we examined promoter regions (up to approximately 1.5 kb) of FASN and ACC1. Luciferase reporter assays showed that ZNF217 overexpression enhanced the activity of FASN and ACC1 promoters containing wild-type but not mutated ZNF217 binding sites (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, ChIP analysis revealed specific recruitment of ZNF217 to the promoter regions of FASN − 171 to − 393 upstream of the transcription start site (TSS) and ACC1 − 903 to − 1101 upstream of the TSS, but not to upstream control regions. (Fig. 2F). Flow cytometry and Oil Red O staining revealed that ZNF217 overexpression increased lipid accumulation, an effect that was reversed by Cerulenin treatment or FASN knockdown (Fig. 3A, B and Fig. S2A, B). Consistent with these findings, measurements of triglyceride and cholesterol levels showed similar trends (Fig. 3C and Fig. S2C). ZNF217 promotes proliferation, migration, and invasion through upregulation of FASN expression

To examine whether FASN mediates the effects of ZNF217 on HCC cell behaviors, we assessed proliferation, migration, and invasion. CCK-8 and colony formation assays revealed that ZNF217 overexpression increased cell proliferation, an effect that was suppressed by Cerulenin treatment or FASN knockdown (Fig. 4A, B and Fig. S3A, B). Similarly, wound healing and transwell assays showed that ZNF217 overexpression enhanced the migration and invasion of HCC cells, both of which were attenuated by Cerulenin or FASN knockdown (Fig. 4C, D and Fig. S3C, D). Conversely, ZNF217 knockdown impaired cell proliferation, and this effect was largely rescued by re-expressing FASN (Fig. 4E). We further assessed ZNF217 and FASN expression by immunohistochemistry (IHC) in the same 90 HCC samples. Statistical analysis revealed a positive correlation between ZNF217 and FASN expression, which was also observed in the TCGA LIHC dataset (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Figure S4B). Moreover, analysis of the TCGA LIHC data showed positive correlations between ZNF217 expression and that of ACLY or ACC1 (Supplementary Figure S4B). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that high ZNF217 expression was associated with shorter overall survival (OS), and high FASN expression similarly correlated with reduced OS in 72 HCC patients (Fig. 6C). Additionally, analysis of the TCGA LIHC cohort indicated that lower ZNF217 mRNA expression was significantly associated with improved overall survival and progression-free interval (Supplementary Figure S4C). Based on immunohistochemistry (IHC) scores, samples were stratified into ZNF217-low and ZNF217-high groups, as well as FASN-low and FASN-high groups. Correlation analyses with clinicopathological characteristics revealed that ZNF217 expression was significantly associated with follow-up survival status, tumor node metastasis (TNM) stage, microvascular invasion, and tumor size (P = 0.009, P = 0.002, P = 0.018, and P = 0.018, respectively), while FASN expression showed significant correlations with follow-up survival status, Alpha-Fetoprotein (AFP) level, TNM stage, microvascular invasion, and tumor size (P = 0.034, P < 0.001, P < 0.00, P = 0.005, and P < 0.001, respectively) (Table S5). In contrast, neither ZNF217 nor FASN expression was significantly associated with age, gender, Child–Pugh grade, HBsAg status, HCV status, or alcohol history. Furthermore, multivariate Cox proportional hazard model analysis identified FASN, but not ZNF217, as an independent prognostic factor for overall survival in our cohort (Table S6). In summary, these findings confirm that ZNF217 is overexpressed in HCC, correlates with key lipid synthesis enzymes including FASN and ACC1, and serves as a marker of poor prognosis in HCC patients.

Discussion

Metabolic reprogramming, particularly the dysregulation of lipid metabolism, is a cornerstone of cancer progression, providing tumor cells with essential building blocks, energy, and signaling molecules (Wang et al. 2022; Li et al. 2023; Parupathi et al. 2025). While the upregulation of key lipogenic enzymes like FASN, ACLY, and ACC is a well-established feature of many cancers, including HCC, the upstream transcriptional regulators orchestrating this program remain incompletely defined. Our study identifies the transcription factor ZNF217 as a critical regulator of de novo lipid synthesis in HCC, linking its oncogenic activity directly to the lipogenic pathway and poor patient prognosis.

ZNF217 is a member of the Krüppel-like transcription factor family, which widely participates in and drives core malignant processes including cell cycle progression, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and chemotherapy resistance by regulating a series of downstream target genes (Bai et al. 2014; Nguyen et al. 2014; Northey et al. 2020b; Fahmé et al. 2022; Qin et al. 2025; Wardrup et al. 2025). Studies have shown that ZNF217 confers resistance to various chemotherapeutic drugs, including paclitaxel, primarily through the regulation of key pathways such as Aurora-A kinase (Thollet et al. 2010). In acute myeloid leukemia, it has been identified as a key member of the core transcriptional regulatory circuit, directly activating oncogenes such as MYB (Zhou et al. 2025). The expression and activity of ZNF217 are tightly controlled by a multi-layer upstream regulatory network, as reported in previous studies. This network encompasses transcriptional control mediated by factors such as GATA3, post-transcriptional repression through microRNAs (e.g., miR‑503, miR‑135, and the matrix stiffness‑responsive miR‑203), and competitive endogenous RNA mechanisms involving lncRNAs like MALAT1 (Jiang et al. 2016; Liang et al. 2020; Northey et al. 2020a; Xu et al. 2022). Furthermore, epitranscriptomic modulation via the m⁶A demethylase FTO and direct pharmacological inhibition have also been documented (Azhati et al. 2023). Previous studies have established that zinc finger protein 217 (Zfp217, human homolog ZNF217) plays a central regulatory role in adipogenesis through a dual mechanism: directly activating the transcription of the demethylase FTO and relieving YTHDF2-mediated inhibition of FTO, thereby reducing mRNA m⁶A methylation levels to promote adipocyte differentiation and lipid accumulation (Song et al. 2019). In animal models, it has been further demonstrated that reducing Zfp217 activity confers resistance to high-fat diet-induced obesity and improves metabolic health (Zeng et al. 2021). Our study extends the functional role of ZNF217 for the first time from adipogenesis to the pathogenesis of HCC. We show that ZNF217 acts as a central transducer of metabolic signals, directly binding to the promoters of key lipogenic genes FASN and ACC1 to drive their transcriptional activation. Further profiling of long-chain fatty acids clarified that ZNF217 primarily promotes de novo lipogenesis. Importantly, ZNF217-mediated lipid accumulation, cell proliferation, migration, and invasion are dependent on FASN. This ZNF217–FASN axis was validated in both in vitro and in vivo models, revealing a novel mechanism by which ZNF217 directly orchestrates the lipogenic program and elevating it from a traditional metabolic regulator to a critical molecular node linking metabolic dysregulation to cancer progression. These findings thus provide a new theoretical foundation and a candidate target for treating obesity-related malignancies through the targeting of lipid metabolism. Building on these established regulatory layers, the previously reported upstream regulators (such as the GATA3-ZNF217 axis) may exert their biological effects at least in part by regulating this adipogenesis program mediated by ZNF217.

The clinical relevance of ZNF217-FASN axis is pronounced. We observed significant co-upregulation of ZNF217 and FASN in HCC tissues compared to adjacent normal liver. The strong positive correlation between ZNF217 and FASN protein levels, corroborated by TCGA data extending the correlation to mRNA levels of FASN, ACLY, and ACC1, suggests this regulatory relationship is operational in human tumors. Most importantly, high expression of either ZNF217 or FASN predicted shorter overall survival for HCC patients. This aligns with the known association of elevated lipogenesis with tumor aggressiveness and poor outcomes (Menendez and Lupu 2007; Fhu and Ali 2020; Martin-Perez et al. 2022; Tiong et al. 2022), and it nominates ZNF217 as a novel prognostic biomarker. Its upregulation in other malignancies (e.g., LUAD, LUSC, COAD) hints atbroader roles but requires validation across cancer types.

This study bridges a significant knowledge gap. Although ZNF217 has been previously implicated in tumor progression, often associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and therapy resistance, its specific mechanism in cancer cell metabolism was elusive. Our study demonstrates that a fundamental aspect of ZNF217’s oncogenicity is its ability to reprogram lipid metabolism. This establishes ZNF217 as an independent transcription factor operating within the HCC lipid metabolic network. The established regulators, such as SREBP-1 (activated by sterol depletion) (Cheng et al. 2022; Jiang et al. 2024; Mi et al. 2025), STAT3 (activated by cytokines like IL-6) (Li et al. 2023; Fan et al. 2024), LXR, and ChREBP (Yang et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2021; Xiong et al. 2025), are characterized by their response to specific metabolic or inflammatory ligands, which directly modulate their transcriptional activity or nuclear translocation. In contrast, current evidence indicates that the expression of ZNF217 is predominantly regulated at the post-transcriptional level by a specific network of non-coding RNAs, such as miR-203 (Ferrarese et al. 2023). More importantly, and as a cornerstone of our study, ZNF217 executes its function by binding to a unique GC-rich motif in the promoters of FASN, ACC1, and ACLY, which is distinct from the canonical response elements (SRE, GAS, LXRE, ChoRE) used by the factors above (Ferrarese et al. 2023). This fundamental difference in the primary regulatory input and, most critically, the DNA recognition mechanism, positions ZNF217 as a non-redundant, parallel regulatory axis that expands the complexity of transcriptional control in HCC lipogenesis. This distinct mechanism positions ZNF217 as a direct executor within the network of transcription factors that respond to oncogenic signals (e.g., PI3K/AKT) to fuel lipogenesis (Guo et al. 2014). Whether ZNF217 operates downstream of these pathways or integrates additional inputs to fine-tune lipid synthesis in response to specific tumor microenvironmental cues is a compelling question for future research. Furthermore, the FASN dependency of ZNF217-driven phenotypes provides a rationale to explore its mechanistic basis in future studies.

Despite the achievements of our study, there are still some limitations. First, it is still unclear what upstream regulatory factors control the expression or activity of ZNF217 in tumor lipid metabolism. Addressing these knowledge gaps is crucial for a comprehensive understanding of ZNF217’s role in tumor lipid metabolism. Future studies should investigate how the reported upstream regulators (e.g., GATA3, specific miRNAs) mechanistically converge on the ZNF217-FASN axis to drive lipogenic reprogramming. Second, this study did not fully explore the broader oncogenic effects of the ZNF217-FASN axis beyond HCC. Although bioinformatics data indicate that ZNF217 is upregulated in other malignancies, whether this axis functions as a liver-specific mechanism or a general metabolic driver requires further investigation to evaluate its biological universality. Finally, and most importantly, our conclusion that the ZNF217–FASN axis represents a candidate therapeutic target remains primarily hypothesis-generating, particularly given the relatively limited sample size of our clinical cohort. To bridge this gap, future work must focus on direct therapeutic validation in clinically relevant models (e.g., using FASN inhibitors like TVB-2640 in PDX or orthotopic HCC models) and in studies with larger patient cohorts, coupled with efforts to explore synergistic effects with existing therapies and to identify predictive biomarkers (such as high ZNF217/FASN expression) for patient stratification. Addressing these aspects is crucial for fully realizing the therapeutic potential of this axis.

Conclusion

In summary, we identify ZNF217 as a critical transcriptional activator of de novo lipid synthesis in HCC. By upregulating key lipogenic enzymes, notably FASN, ZNF217 fuels lipid production that supports aggressive tumor phenotypes in vitro and in vivo. Its clinical overexpression, correlation with the lipogenic pathway, and association with poor survival establish the ZNF217-FASN axis as a contributor to HCC progression and a candidate therapeutic target. These findings expand our understanding of the transcriptional control in cancer metabolism and define ZNF217’s role in metabolic reprogramming, while highlighting the need to dissect its upstream regulators and broader functional relevance.
