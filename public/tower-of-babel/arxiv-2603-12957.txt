A priori adaptive numerical methods for estimating blow-up times of autonomous ODEs Thanks: ⋆ Corresponding author: Johannes Vincent Meo (johannvm@math.uio.no)
Håkon Hoel, Johannes Vincent Meo ⋆

Abstract
. We present effective a priori adaptive numerical methods for estimating the blow-up time for solutions of autonomous ODEs. The novelty of our approach is to base our adaptive steps on the sensitivity of an auxiliary hitting time. We provide results on the theoretical error rates, and show that there is a benefit in terms of computational effort in choosing our adaptive algorithm over alternative approaches. Numerical experiments support our theoretical results and show how the methods perform in practice.

1.
Introduction

For
n
-dimensional autonomous ODE
x^{\prime}(t)=b(x(t))
where solutions blow up in finite time,
this work is concerned with developing effective numerical methods for approximation of the
blow-up time

\tau(x_{0})\coloneqq\inf\Big\{t\geq 0\mid\lim_{s\uparrow t}\left\lvert x(s)\right\rvert=\infty,\;x(0)=x_{0}\Big\}.

Blow-up times are encountered in a wide variety of physical, engineering and biology models, and indicate phenomenons such as thermal runaway in combustion
[
3
]
, collapse in nonlinear optics
[
18
]
and extreme concentration of cells in chemotaxis
[
24
]
. Moreover, in the one-dimensional setting, the blow-up time can be represented as the improper integral

\ \tau=\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{b(x)}\,dx,

which for instance can represent the tail probability of a random variable with probability density function
1/b
.

Our approach has three main pillars that can be summarized as follows: 1. For a given tolerance
\epsilon>0
,
introduce the auxiliary hitting time

\tau_{\epsilon}=\inf\Big\{t\geq 0\mid\lim_{s\uparrow t}\left\lvert x(s)\right\rvert=r(\epsilon),\;x(0)=x_{0}\Big\},

where
r(\epsilon)>0
is a threshold parameter chosen sufficiently large so that
|\tau-\tau_{\epsilon}|=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
.
2. Solve the ODE numerically and compute the hitting time
\bar{\tau}
when the norm of your numerical solution attains the value
r(\epsilon)
.
3. Apply adaptive time stepping with sufficiently small steps to ensure that
|\bar{\tau}-\tau_{\epsilon}|=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, but still large enough to preserve efficiency.

To simplify theoretical studies, we apply the first-order forward Euler scheme as numerical integrator (see Remark
4
for extensions to higher-order integrators).
As an extension of classic works on adaptive methods on bounded domains
[
2
,
7
,
12
,
19
]
, our adaptive time stepping is based on equilibrating error indicators that are the local error of the integrator multiplied with the sensitivity of the threshold hitting time
\tau_{\epsilon}
w.r.t. perturbations in the initial condition.
To our knowledge, this is the first sensitivity based numerical method with the focus on
\tau_{\epsilon}
rather than the solution
x
.
Under relatively mild conditions on
b
, Theorems
1
and
2
present rigorous asymptotic bounds on the approximation error and computational cost, showing that
|\tau-\bar{\tau}_{\epsilon}|=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
is achieved at the computational cost
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
(where the cost measure is proportional to the algorithm’s FLOPS).
Rigorous asymptotic bounds of this kind are rare in the literature on numerical methods for estimating blow-up times, and we show that our method
yields better efficiency than first-order numerical methods using uniform time steps and adaptive methods that do not account for the sensitivity, cf. Sections
2.1
and
4
.

The literature contains several numerical strategies for estimating blow-up times, with a comprehensive survey in
[
1
]
.
Stuart and Floater
[
21
]
propose a transformation of the underlying time-variable, dependent on the size of the current solution, in order to get the correct asymptotic behaviour when using a first-order implicit Euler scheme. Hirota and Ozawa
[
14
]
utilizes an arc-length transformation to extend the computationally very useful bijection between time and solution-state for one-dimensional problems to higher dimensions and applies a higher-order Runge Kutta methods combined with local-error based adaptivity to achieve an experimentally very efficient method, with some need for parameter tweaking. A posteriori adaptive finite-element methods for inhomogeneous ODEs on abstract Hilbert spaces are considered in
[
16
,
6
]
. Our adaptive approach complements these contributions by using a sensitivity-based a priori numerical method for which rigorous convergence rates hold for both the approximation error and the computational cost.

For semi-linear parabolic PDEs, the seminal work by
[
20
]
shows that an appropriately
scaled finite difference method converges to the true blow-up time asymptotically.
Berger and Kohn
[
4
]
introduced an algorithm that rescales the numerical solution when it reaches a predefined threshold value
M
. Rescaling produces a new problem that is solved at finer resolution near the blow-up time and the blow-up point in space. Iterating this procedure leads to an adaptive and localized numerical method.
Convergence analysis of the rescaling method was studied by Cho and Sun
[
8
]
and the rescaling method was extended to ODEs by Cho and Wu in
[
9
]
, where a first order convergence rate is proven for one-dimensional ODEs with
b(x)=x^{p}
for
p>1
.

Lastly, the works
[
17
,
22
]
study the related problem of identifying with numerical methods when true solutions of dynamical systems blow up. Although not relevant for this work, as we impose sufficient growth assumptions on the vector field to ensure blow ups, this is a highly relevant challenge for applications.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the
mathematical problem of estimating blow-up times in 1D, describes our adaptive method in Algorithm
1
and theoretical results on convergence and computational cost
in Theorem
1
. Section
3
extends our adaptive method to
higher-dimensional ODEs. The resulting method is described in Algorithm
2

and Theorem
2
.
Finally, Section
4
presents several numerical examples backing up our theoretical results.

2.
An adaptive numerical method for estimation of blow-up times for 1D ODEs

In this section we introduce an a priori adaptive method for one dimensional ODEs and provide asymptotic rates for the approximation error and computational cost in Theorem
1
. Subsequent remarks shortly discuss possible extensions to separable ODEs and use of higher-order integrators. Lastly, we compare our approach to using uniform steps in Section
2.1
.

We consider the autonomous one-dimensional ODE

\begin{split}x^{\prime}(t)&=b(x(t)),\qquad t\in(0,\tau)\\
x(0)&=x_{0},\end{split}

(1)

for right-hand sides that satisfy the following assumption.

Assumption A
.

The function
b:D=[x_{0},\infty)\to(0,\infty)
with
x_{0}>0
satisfies

1)

b\in C^{1}(D)
with
b^{\prime}
positive and increasing on
D
.

2)

There exist positive monotone decreasing functions
F,G\in C^{1}((0,\infty))
such that
-\frac{d}{dx}G(b(x))\leq\frac{1}{b(x)}\leq-\frac{d}{dx}F(b(x))
for all
x\in D
with

\underset{x\to\infty}{\lim}F(x)=0
and
-\frac{1}{F^{-1}(\epsilon)G^{\prime}(F^{-1}(\epsilon))}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{\alpha})
for some
\alpha>-2
for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
.

3)

There exist some
k>1
such that
\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{\sqrt{b^{\prime}(kx)}}{b(x)}\,dx<\infty
.

4)

The integral
\tau=\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{b(x)}dx
is finite.

Remark 1

(Alternative to Assumption
A.2
)
.

An alternative to Assumption
A.2
is to assume that
b\in C^{2}(D)
with
b^{\prime\prime}(x)>0
and
x\leq b^{\prime}(x)^{C}
for
x\in D
and some constant
C>0
. We will later on, in Remark
2
, show that our main result for this section, Theorem
1
, also holds under this alternative assumption.

As
b
is locally Lipschitz on
D
we recall from
[
23
, Chapter 2]

that (
1
) has a unique solution
x\in C^{1}([0,\tau))
, and
Assumption
A.4
ensures a finite blow-up time.

To account for how the dynamics depends on the initial condition,
we let
x(t;\tilde{x}_{0},t_{0})
denote the solution of

\begin{split}x^{\prime}(t;\tilde{x}_{0},t_{0})&=b(x(t;\tilde{x}_{0},t_{0})),\qquad t\in(t_{0},\tau)\\
x(t_{0})&=\tilde{x}_{0},\end{split}

where
\tilde{x}_{0}\in[x_{0},\infty)
and
t_{0}\in[0,\infty)
. And for
r\in[x_{0},\infty)
, we
set

\tau_{r}(\tilde{x}_{0},t_{0})=t_{0}+\int_{\min\{\tilde{x}_{0},r\}}^{r}\frac{1}{b(x)}\,dx,

(2)

which then denotes the first time
t\geq t_{0}
that
x(t;\tilde{x}_{0},t_{0})
exceeds the value
r
(equivalently, the hitting time of
r
for the process
x(t;\tilde{x}_{0},t_{0})
, when assuming that
x_{0}\leq r
).
We also introduce the short-hand notation
\tau_{r}=\tau_{r}(x_{0},0)
, denoting the hitting time of
r
for
x(t)
.

Let
\bar{x}
denote the forward Euler numerical solution of
x(t)
, given by
the scheme

\bar{x}_{n+1}=\bar{x}_{n}+b(\bar{x}_{n})h_{n}\text{ for }n=0,1,\ldots,N-1,

where
h_{n}>0
is an adaptive time step,
\bar{x}_{0}=x_{0}
, and
N\in\mathbb{N}
is the number of steps.
In our case,
N
will be the smallest integer such that
\bar{x}_{N}>r
where
r
is chosen to control the error
\left\lvert\tau-\tau_{r}\right\rvert
for a given tolerance
\epsilon
.
The iteration
t_{n+1}=t_{n}+h_{n}
for
n=0,1,\ldots,N-1
then defines the grid for our numerical solution, with
\bar{\tau}\coloneqq t_{N}
being our approximation of
\tau_{r}
.
As a consequence of Assumption
A
, we note that
\bar{x}_{n+1}\leq x(t_{n+1})
: If we assume that
\bar{x}_{n}=x(t_{n})
, then

\bar{x}_{n+1}=\bar{x}_{n}+h_{n}b(\bar{x}_{n})=x(t_{n})+h_{n}b(x(t_{n}))\leq x(t_{n})+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}b(x(t))\,dt=x(t_{n+1}),

(3)

as
b
is monotone increasing.

Our a priori adaptive method is presented in Algorithm
1
.
It estimates
\tau
by numerically approximating the auxiliary hitting-time
\tau_{r}
.
We chose
r
such that
b(r)=F^{-1}(\epsilon)
holds, as we from (
5
) have that this is sufficient to ensure
\left\lvert\tau-\tau_{r}\right\rvert\leq\epsilon
.
The ODE is then solved using forward Euler with the adaptive step length
h_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{b^{\prime}(\min\{k\bar{x},r\})}}
until it first passes the value
r
.
The time when
r
is reached,
t_{N}
, is then our estimate
\bar{\tau}
of
\tau_{r}
.
The adaptive time steps
h_{n}
are chosen as they ensures
\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau_{r}\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, see proof of Theorem
1
.

Algorithm 1

Estimating blow-up time of 1D ODE

1:

b,\,x_{0},\,k,\,F^{-1},\,\epsilon>0
.

2:

Set
t=0,\,N=0,\,\bar{x}=x_{0}
and let
r
be such that
b(r)=F^{-1}(\epsilon)
.

3:

while

\bar{x}<r

do

4:

h=\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{b^{\prime}(\min\{k\bar{x},\,r\})}}
.

5:

\bar{x}\leftarrow\bar{x}+b(\bar{x})h
.

6:

t\leftarrow t+h
.

7:

N\leftarrow N+1
.

8:

end

while

9:

\bar{\tau}=t
.

10:

\bar{\tau},\,N
.

As the number of floating point operations per iteration in Algorithm
1
is bounded by a small fixed integer
C
, we have that the total number of operations are
\mathcal{O}(N)
. We will therefore define the computational cost to be the number of iterations
N
.
The main result on the performance of our 1D numerical method is stated below.

Theorem 1

(Properties of Algorithm
1
)
.

Let Assumption
A
hold and let
\bar{\tau}
be calculated using Algorithm
1
.
Then, for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, it holds that
\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
and the computational cost of the method is
\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{-1}\right)
.

Proof.

For any
r\geq x_{0}
we have

\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau_{r}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\tau_{r}-\tau\right\rvert.

(4)

To prove our claim, it suffices to show both terms on the right-hand side are
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
.
From Algorithm
1
we have that
r(\epsilon)
is chosen such that
b(r(\epsilon))=F^{-1}(\epsilon)
, where we write
r
as a function of
\epsilon
to emphasize its dependence of the tolerance.
For the second term, we then get

\left\lvert\tau_{r(\epsilon)}-\tau\right\rvert=\int_{r(\epsilon)}^{\infty}\frac{1}{b(x)}\,dx\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{asmp:b.FandG}}}{{\leq}}\int_{r(\epsilon)}^{\infty}-\frac{d}{dx}F(b(x))\,dx=F(b(r(\epsilon)))=F(F^{-1}(\epsilon))=\epsilon.

(5)

For later reference, from the lower bound of
\frac{1}{b(x)}
in Assumption
A.2
we have that
-\frac{d}{dx}G(b(x))=-G^{\prime}(b(x))b^{\prime}(x)\leq\frac{1}{b(x)}
, thus by setting
x=r(\epsilon)
we get

b^{\prime}(r(\epsilon))\leq-\frac{1}{b(r(\epsilon))G^{\prime}(b(r(\epsilon)))}=-\frac{1}{F^{-1}(\epsilon)G^{\prime}(F^{-1}(\epsilon))}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{asmp:b.FandG}}}{{=}}\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{\alpha}),

(6)

for some
\alpha>-2
.

For the first term of (
4
), we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau_{r(\epsilon)}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\tau_{r(\epsilon)}(\bar{x}_{N},t_{N})-\tau_{r(\epsilon)}(\bar{x}_{0},t_{0})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\left\lvert\tau_{r(\epsilon)}(\bar{x}_{n},t_{n})-\tau_{r(\epsilon)}(\bar{x}_{n-1},t_{n-1})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49428pt+\left\lvert\tau_{r(\epsilon)}(\bar{x}_{N},t_{N})-\tau_{r(\epsilon)}(\bar{x}_{N-1},t_{N-1})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:tau_1D}}}{{\leq}}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\left\lvert h_{n-1}-\int_{\bar{x}_{n-1}}^{\bar{x}_{n}}\frac{1}{b(x)}\,dx\right\rvert+\left\lvert h_{N-1}-\int_{\bar{x}_{N-1}}^{r(\epsilon)}\frac{1}{b(x)}\,dx\right\rvert

\displaystyle\hskip-2.84526pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{asmp:b.C1_bDerPos}}}{{\leq}}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\left\lvert\left(\frac{1}{b(\bar{x}_{n-1})}-\frac{1}{b(\bar{x}_{n})}\right)b(\bar{x}_{n-1})h_{n-1}\right\rvert+h_{N-1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}b^{\prime}(\bar{x}_{n})h_{n-1}^{2}+h_{N-1}.

(7)

In the last inequality we take a first-order Taylor expansion of
b(\bar{x}_{n})
around
\bar{x}_{n-1}
and use the monotonicity of
b^{\prime}
to
\max_{\theta\in[\bar{x}_{n-1},\bar{x}_{n}]}|b^{\prime}(\theta)|\leq|b^{\prime}(\bar{x}_{n})|
.
Moreover, one can show the the last line above is proportional to the sensitivity
\partial_{x_{0}}\tau(r,x_{0})
times the local error in
x
.

The adaptive step length used in Algorithm
1
gives
h_{n-1}=\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{b^{\prime}(\min\{k\bar{x}_{n-1},\,r(\epsilon)\})}}
, but for technical reasons, let us now introduce the following alternative step length:

\displaystyle g_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\min\Bigg\{\underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{b^{\prime}(\min\{k\bar{x}_{n-1},\,r(\epsilon)\})}}}_{h_{n-1}},\underbrace{\frac{(k-1)\bar{x}_{n-1}}{b(\bar{x}_{n-1})}}_{\tilde{g}_{n-1}}\Bigg\},

(8)

where
k>1
is some value such that Assumption
A.3
is satisfied.
Since
g_{n-1}\leq\tilde{g}_{n-1}
, we have that

\bar{x}_{n}=\bar{x}_{n-1}+g_{n-1}b(\bar{x}_{n-1})\leq k\bar{x}_{n-1}.

Combining this with (
2
)
and using that
\bar{x}_{n}<r(\epsilon)
for
n<N
yields

\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau_{r(\epsilon)}\right\rvert\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}b^{\prime}(\min\{k\bar{x}_{n-1},\,r(\epsilon)\})g_{n-1}^{2}+g_{N-1}

Next, the other constraint on
g_{n-1}
,
h_{n-1}
, yields

\displaystyle\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau_{r(\epsilon)}\right\rvert\leq N\epsilon^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon),

(9)

and it remains to bound
N
. We first need to determine which of the constraints on
g_{n-1}
in (
8
) that is the most restrictive. For the quotient
\frac{h_{n-1}}{\tilde{g}_{n-1}}
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{b^{\prime}(\min\{k\bar{x}_{n-1},\,r(\epsilon)\})}}}{\frac{(k-1)\bar{x}_{n-1}}{b(\bar{x}_{n-1})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon b(\bar{x}_{n-1})}{(k-1)\bar{x}_{n-1}\sqrt{b^{\prime}(\min\{k\bar{x}_{n-1},\,r(\epsilon)\})}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon c\bar{x}_{n-1}b^{\prime}(\bar{x}_{n-1})}{(k-1)\bar{x}_{n-1}\sqrt{b^{\prime}(\min\{k\bar{x}_{n-1},\,r(\epsilon)\})}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon c\sqrt{b^{\prime}(r(\epsilon))}}{k-1}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:boundB_der_r}}{=}\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}),

where the transition to the second line uses that there exists a
c>0
such that
b(x)=b(x_{0})+b^{\prime}(x)x\leq cxb^{\prime}(x)
for all
x\in D
.
Thus for sufficiently small
\epsilon
we have that
g_{n-1}=h_{n-1}
.

Introducing the continuous extensions
\bar{x}(t)=\bar{x}_{n-1}
and
h(t)=h_{n-1}
for
t\in[t_{n-1},t_{n})
, we obtain for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
that

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{h_{n-1}}{h_{n-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\bar{\tau}}\frac{1}{h(t)}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{\tau_{r(\epsilon)}}\sqrt{b^{\prime}(k\bar{x}(t))}\,dt+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{\tau_{r(\epsilon)}}^{\max\{\tau_{r(\epsilon)},\bar{\tau}\}}\sqrt{b^{\prime}(r(\epsilon))}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{\sqrt{b^{\prime}(kx)}}{b(x)}\,dx+\frac{\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau_{r(\epsilon)}\right\rvert}{\epsilon}\sqrt{b^{\prime}(r(\epsilon))}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{asmp:b.integrabilityCond}}}{{\leq}}\frac{C}{\epsilon}+\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau_{r(\epsilon)}\right\rvert\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-1}),

(10)

where the transition from the second to the third line uses (
3
), as this shows that
\bar{x}(t)\leq x(t)
, allowing a change of variable in the left integral using
dt=\frac{dx}{b(x)}
.

Inserting into (
9
) gives

\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau_{r(\epsilon)}\right\rvert\leq C\epsilon+\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau_{r(\epsilon)}\right\rvert\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}})\implies\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau_{r(\epsilon)}\right\rvert\leq\frac{C\epsilon}{(1-\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}))}=C^{\prime}\epsilon,

as
1+\frac{\alpha}{2}>0
, and thus

N\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:costEst1D}}}{{=}}\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
.

We then deduce from the above that

\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau_{r(\epsilon)}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\tau_{r(\epsilon)}-\tau\right\rvert\leq C^{\prime}\epsilon+\epsilon=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon).

Furthermore, the computational cost is given by
C^{\prime\prime}N\leq\frac{C^{\prime}C^{\prime\prime}}{\epsilon}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
, for some constant
C^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
.
∎

Remark 2

(Alternative to Assumption
A.2
revisited)
.

We will here show that Theorem
1
holds under the alternative to Assumption
A.2
given in Remark
1
.
Note that
\frac{1}{b(x)}=\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\log(b(x))}{b^{\prime}(x)}\right)+\frac{\log(b(x))}{b^{\prime}(x)^{2}}b^{\prime\prime}(x)
and that integrating the first term of the right-hand side from
r(\epsilon)
to
\infty
yields the negative contribution
-\frac{\log(b(r(\epsilon)))}{b^{\prime}(r(\epsilon))}
.
Then

\left\lvert\tau_{r(\epsilon)}-\tau\right\rvert\leq\int_{r(\epsilon)}^{\infty}\frac{\log(b(x))}{b^{\prime}(x)^{2}}b^{\prime\prime}(x)\,dx\leq\int_{r(\epsilon)}^{\infty}\frac{(C+1)\log(b^{\prime}(x))}{b^{\prime}(x)^{2}}b^{\prime\prime}(x)\,dx,

where the second inequality follows as
b(x)\leq xb^{\prime}(x)\leq b^{\prime}(x)^{C+1}
, using the alternative assumptions in Remark
1
.
Integrating this expression gives
\left\lvert\tau_{r(\epsilon)}-\tau\right\rvert\leq\frac{(C+1)\log(b^{\prime}(r(\epsilon)))}{b^{\prime}(r(\epsilon))}
. And
choosing the threshold function
r(\epsilon)
such that

b^{\prime}(r(\epsilon))=\epsilon^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1})
suffices to ensure that
\left\lvert\tau_{r(\epsilon)}-\tau\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
.
This yields
b^{\prime}(r(\epsilon))=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{\alpha})
for some
\alpha>-2
.
In the proof of Theorem
1
, the above argument replaces the start of the proof, up to and including (
6
). The remaining part of the proof still applies under the alternative assumption.

Remark 3

(Extension to separable ODEs)
.

The method can in principle be extended to stopping times of separable non-autonomous ODEs on the form

\begin{split}x^{\prime}(t)&=f(t,x(t))=g(t)b(x(t)),\qquad t\in(0,\tau)\\
x(0)&=x_{0},\end{split}

where
g:C((0,\infty))
is a positive and locally integrable function.
The explosion time
\tau
then is redefined through the implicit equation

\int_{0}^{\tau}g(t)\,dt=\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{b(x)}\,dx
, or, equivalently,

\tau=G^{-1}\left(\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{b(x)}\,dx+G(0)\right),

where
G
denotes an antiderivative of
g
.
We can then calculate the first term in the argument of
G^{-1}
using Algorithm
1
.

Remark 4

(Higher-order methods)
.

Theorem
1
applies to the forward Euler numerical integrator, which is a first-order method. However, under the more restrictive constraints on
b
given below, it is possible to improve the efficiency of the method further by using a higher-order Taylor scheme.
This higher-order method is for some integer
2\leq\bar{m}\leq m
given by
\bar{x}_{n+1}=\bar{x}_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{\bar{m}}\frac{x^{(i)}(\hat{t})}{i!}h^{i}_{n}
, where
\hat{t}
is such that
x(\hat{t})=\bar{x}_{n}
.
The time stepping needs to be redefined as
h_{n}=\frac{\epsilon^{\frac{1}{\bar{m}}}}{b^{\prime}(\min\{k\bar{x}_{n},r(\epsilon)\})^{\frac{\bar{m}}{1+\bar{m}}}}
.

The extension of Assumption
A
for the higher-order method are as follows:
Let
b\in C^{m}(D)
for
m\geq 3
, let there exist some
C>0
such that
\frac{d^{m}}{dx^{m}}b(x(t))\leq Cb(x(t))b^{\prime}(x(t))^{m}
, let
A.2
hold
for
\alpha>-\frac{m+1}{m}
and let there exist
k>1
such that
\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{b^{\prime}(kx)^{\frac{\bar{m}}{\bar{m}+1}}}{b(x)}\,dx<\infty
. Then one can show
that
\left\lvert\tau-\bar{\tau}\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
is reached
at
\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{-\frac{1}{\bar{m}}}\right)
computational cost.
For brevity, the proof of this result is omitted.

2.1.
Comparing adaptive and uniform time steps

Theorem
1
shows that our adaptive method reaches an error of order
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
with a cost of order
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
.
We will here find how much we gain, measured in computational cost, by using our adaptive algorithm instead of a naive approach using uniform steps.
For brevity, we will assume that
b
satisfies Assumption
A
with
A.2
replaced by the alternative condition in Remark
1
, as this provides a convenient bound on how
b^{\prime}(r)
grows in
r
, see Remark
2
.
From (
2
) in the proof of Theorem
1
we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau_{r(\epsilon)}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}b^{\prime}(\bar{x}_{n})h^{2}+h

\displaystyle\leq h\int_{0}^{\tau_{r(\epsilon)}}b^{\prime}(x(t))\,dt+h\int_{\tau_{r(\epsilon)}}^{\bar{\tau}}b^{\prime}(r(\epsilon))\,dt+h

\displaystyle=h\log\left(\frac{b(r(\epsilon))}{b(x_{0})}\right)+h\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau_{r(\epsilon)}\right\rvert b^{\prime}(r(\epsilon))+h,

as
\frac{d}{dt}\log(b(x(t)))=b^{\prime}(x(t))
. Rearranging, we see that

\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau_{r}\right\rvert\leq\frac{h\log\left(\frac{b(r)}{b(x_{0})}\right)+h}{1-hb^{\prime}(r)}

We want to pick
h
such that
\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau_{r}\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, this implies

h=\min\left\{\bar{h},\tilde{h}\right\}=\min\left\{\frac{\epsilon}{\log\left(\frac{b(r)}{b(x_{0})}\right)},\frac{1}{2b^{\prime}(r)}\right\}.

The cost associated with
\bar{h}
is

N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\bar{h}}{\bar{h}}\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{\bar{\tau}}\log\left(\frac{b(r(\epsilon))}{b(x_{0})}\right)\,dt=\frac{\bar{\tau}}{\epsilon}\log\left(\frac{b(r(\epsilon))}{b(x_{0})}\right),

while we for
\tilde{h}
have

N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\tilde{h}}{\tilde{h}}\leq\int_{0}^{\bar{\tau}}2b^{\prime}(r(\epsilon))\,dt=\frac{2\bar{\tau}}{\epsilon}\log(\epsilon^{-1}),

when using that
b^{\prime}(r(\epsilon))=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1}))
,
cf. Remark
2
.
This shows that using uniform steps increase the asymptotic cost with at least a factor

\log(\epsilon^{-1})
relative to the adaptive method.
We will explore this numerically for different examples in Section
4
.

3.
Extension to higher dimensions

This section introduces the higher-dimensional analogue of the adaptive method in Section
2
.
The asymptotic rates for the approximation error and computational cost is provided in Theorem
2
.
Thereafter we explore stronger, but often verifiable, regularity conditions on the vector field
b
, cf. Lemmas
3
and
4
.
Finally, in Section
3.1
we extend the method to slower growing vector fields, such as
\left\lvert b(x)\right\rvert=\left\lvert x\right\rvert\log(\left\lvert x\right\rvert)
.

We now consider the higher-dimensional ODE

\begin{split}\dot{x}&=b(x)\\
x(0)&=x_{0}\in D,\end{split}

(11)

where
D\coloneq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid|x|>\delta\}
for
a given
\delta>0
and where
\left\lvert\cdot\right\rvert
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Conditions on
b:D\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
that ensure existence of a unique
C^{1}
-solution
that blows up in finite time are detailed in Assumption
B
below. Our objective is once again to compute the blow up time

\tau(x_{0})=\inf\left\{t\geq 0\,\middle|\,\lim_{s\uparrow t}|x(s)|=\infty,x(0)=x_{0}\right\}.

Before presenting assumptions on
b
, we introduce some notation.
Let
\left\|\cdot\right\|
denote the induced matrix 2-norm,

B(\xi)\coloneqq\{x\mid|x|<\xi\}
and
b^{\prime}:D\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
will be used to denote the Jacobian of
b
. Furthermore, let
r:(0,\infty)\to(\delta,\infty)
denote a problem-dependent, monotone-decreasing and continuous function
satisfying
\underset{\epsilon\to 0}{\lim}r(\epsilon)=\infty
.
For
\epsilon>0
, consider the subset
D(\epsilon)\coloneqq D\cap B(r(\epsilon))\subset D

and let

\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0})\coloneqq\underset{t\geq 0}{\min}\{t\geq 0\mid|x(t)|\geq r(\epsilon),\;x(0)=x_{0}\}

denote the solution’s exit-time of the set
D(\epsilon)
.

Assumption B
.

For the ODE (
11
), the function
b\in C^{1}(D,\mathbb{R}^{n})

satisfies the following conditions:

1)

There exist
\check{C},\alpha>0
such that
\check{C}\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2+\alpha}\leq b(x)\cdot x
for all
x\in D
.

2)

There exist
C_{1}>0
such that for any
\epsilon>0
and all
x_{0}\in D(\epsilon)
, we have that
\left\lvert\nabla\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0})\right\rvert\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\left\lvert b(x_{0})\right\rvert}
.

3)

At least one of the following holds:

a)

There is a constant
C_{2}\in(0,1]
such that
C_{2}\leq\frac{b(x)\cdot x}{\left\lvert b(x)\right\rvert\left\lvert x\right\rvert}
holds for all
x\in D
.

b)

For all
x\in D
, we have
x_{i}b_{i}(x)\geq 0
for
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.

4)

We can find constants
C_{3},C_{4}>0,\,\nu>1,\,\upsilon\geq\max\{1-\alpha,0\}
such that for
h>0
sufficiently small, we have
\frac{\sqrt{\left\|b^{\prime}(x)\right\|}}{\left\lvert b(x+b(x)h)\right\rvert}\leq\frac{C_{3}}{\left\lvert x+b(x)h\right\rvert^{\nu}}
and
\frac{\left\|b^{\prime}(x)\right\|}{\left\lvert b(x)\right\rvert}\leq\frac{C_{4}}{\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{\upsilon}}
for all
x\in D
.

5)

Given
h>0
sufficiently small, we have that
\left\lvert b(x)\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert b(x+b(x)h)\right\rvert
for any
x\in D
.

6)

There is a constant
k>1
such that for any
\epsilon>0,\theta\in(0,1)
and
x\in D(\epsilon)
, it holds that

\left\|b^{\prime}\left(x+\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\left\|b^{\prime}(x)\right\|}}\theta b(x)\right)\right\|\leq k\left\|b^{\prime}(x)\right\|.

We note that
(B.1)
implies finite-time blow up of any solution of the ODE as
d|x|^{2}/dt>2\check{C}|x|^{2+\alpha}
, and
b\in C^{1}(D)
ensures there exists a unique solution
x\in C^{1}([0,\tau),\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Remark 5

(Monotonicity of
\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n}\right\rvert
)
.

Note that

\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n}\right\rvert^{2}=\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n-1}+b(\bar{x}_{n-1})h_{n-1}\right\rvert^{2}=\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n-1}\right\rvert^{2}+2\bar{x}_{n-1}\cdot b(\bar{x}_{n-1})+\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\rvert^{2}h_{n-1}^{2},

then a consequence of Assumption
(B.3)
is that

\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n-1}\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n}\right\rvert
.

The multidimensional version of our method is given in Algorithm
2
.
The method estimates
\tau
by approximating
\tau_{\epsilon}
, where
r(\epsilon)
is set such that
\left\lvert\tau-\tau_{\epsilon}\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, see Lemma
1
.
This is done by solving the system of ODEs using forward Euler with the adaptive step length
h_{n}=\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\left\|b^{\prime}(\bar{x})\right\|}}
until the norm of the solution first passes
r(\epsilon)
.
The current time of the solver,
t_{N}
, is then returned as our estimate
\bar{\tau}
. The adaptive step lengths are chosen to ensure
\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau_{\epsilon}\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, see proof of Theorem
2
.

Algorithm 2

Estimating blow-up time of ODE in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

1:

b,\,x_{0},\,\alpha,\,\check{C},\,\epsilon>0
.

2:

Set
t=0,\,N=0,\,\bar{x}=x_{0}
and
r(\epsilon)=\left(\frac{1}{\check{C}\alpha\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}
.

3:

while

\left\lvert\bar{x}\right\rvert\leq r(\epsilon)

do

4:

h=\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\max\{\left\|b^{\prime}(\bar{x})\right\|,1\}}}

5:

\bar{x}\leftarrow\bar{x}+b(\bar{x})h
.

6:

t\leftarrow t+h
.

7:

N\leftarrow N+1
.

8:

end

while

9:

\bar{\tau}=t
.

10:

\bar{\tau},\,N
.

Note that Algorithm
2
is very similar to Algorithm
1
, with the main differences being how
r(\epsilon)
is calculated and that the
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-version does not need a factor
k
in the argument of
b^{\prime}
. However, the assumptions on
b
are stricter in the
\mathbb{R}^{n}
case compared to the 1D case.

From our definition of
D
and
D(\epsilon)
we have a disjoint partition
\partial D(\epsilon)=\partial D_{1}(\epsilon)\sqcup\partial D_{2}(\epsilon)
, where
\partial D_{1}(\epsilon)
is the surface of the ball
B(r(\epsilon))
, while
\partial D_{2}(\epsilon)
is the surface of
B(r(\delta))
.
Note from Assumption
B
that if
x_{0}\in D(\epsilon)
, the process
x(t)
will never hit the inner boundary
\partial D_{2}(\epsilon)
as
x\cdot b(x)>0
for all
x\in D
.
In addition, we have that
\tau_{\epsilon}
satisfies the Hamilton-Jacobi PDE

\begin{split}\nabla\tau_{\epsilon}(x)\cdot b(x)&=-1,\quad x\in D(\epsilon)\\
\tau_{\epsilon}(x)&=0,\hskip 17.78296ptx\in\partial D_{1}(\epsilon),\end{split}

(12)

giving a characterization for
\partial D_{1}(\epsilon)
. Note that the characteristics of
\tau_{\epsilon}
are simply the flows
x(t)
solving (
11
). Due to our assumptions on
b
, we have that these flows are well-behaved in such a way that the unique solution of (
12
) is
\tau_{\epsilon}\in C^{1}(\overline{D(\epsilon)})
, cf.
[
13
, Chapter 3]
.

Before proceeding to the main result of this section, we need the following lemma providing an expression for
r(\epsilon)
that guarantees that we can control the error
\left\lvert\tau(x_{0})-\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0})\right\rvert
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
r(\epsilon)=\left(\frac{1}{\check{C}\alpha\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}
for
\epsilon>0
.
Under Assumption
(B.1)
it then holds for all
x_{0}\in D
that

\left\lvert\tau(x_{0})-\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0})\right\rvert\leq\epsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\tau(x_{0})\leq\frac{1}{\check{C}\alpha\left\lvert x_{0}\right\rvert^{\alpha}}.

Proof.

Assumption
(B.1)
yields that

\frac{d\left\lvert x(t)\right\rvert^{2}}{dt}\geq 2\check{C}\left\lvert x(t)\right\rvert^{2+\alpha}
.
Introducing
y=\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}
, we obtain that

\tau(x_{0})-\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0})=\int_{\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0})}^{\tau(x_{0})}\,dt\leq\frac{1}{2\check{C}}\int_{r(\epsilon)^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}}\,dy=\frac{1}{\check{C}\alpha r(\epsilon)^{\alpha}}=\epsilon,

and

\tau(x_{0})=\int^{\tau(x_{0})}_{0}\,dt=\frac{1}{\check{C}\alpha\left\lvert x_{0}\right\rvert^{\alpha}}\,.

∎

We can then proceed to our main finding.

Theorem 2

(Properties of Algorithm
2
)
.

Let Assumption
B
hold and
\bar{\tau}
be calculated using Algorithm
2
.
Then, for any
x_{0}\in D
and any sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, it holds that

\left\lvert\bar{\tau}(x_{0})-\tau(x_{0})\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Cost}(\bar{\tau})=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1}).

Proof.

We begin by considering the difference
\left\lvert\bar{\tau}(x_{0})-\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0})\right\rvert
. Introduce the notation

\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0},t_{0})=t_{0}+\underset{t\geq 0}{\min}\{x(t)\geq D(\epsilon)\mid x(0)=x_{0}\}.

We are then able to write

\displaystyle\left\lvert\bar{\tau}(x_{0})-\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0})\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\tau_{\epsilon}(\bar{x}_{N},t_{N})-\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0},t_{0})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left\lvert\tau_{\epsilon}(\bar{x}_{n},t_{n})-\tau_{\epsilon}(\bar{x}_{n-1},t_{n-1})\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left\lvert\tau_{\epsilon}(\bar{x}_{n},t_{n})-\tau_{\epsilon}(\bar{x}_{n-1},t_{n})+h_{n-1}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bigg|\nabla\tau_{\epsilon}(\hat{x}_{n-1})\cdot(\bar{x}_{n}-\bar{x}_{n-1})+h_{n-1}\bigg|

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bigg|\nabla\tau_{\epsilon}(\hat{x}_{n-1})\cdot b(\bar{x}_{n-1})h_{n-1}+h_{n-1}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\nabla\tau_{\epsilon}(\hat{x}_{n-1})\cdot b(\hat{x}_{n-1})h_{n-1}-\nabla\tau_{\epsilon}(\hat{x}_{n-1})\cdot b(\hat{x}_{n-1})h_{n-1}\bigg|

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:PDE_exit_time}}{=}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left\lvert\nabla\tau_{\epsilon}(\hat{x}_{n-1})\cdot\left(b(\bar{x}_{n-1})-b(\hat{x}_{n-1})\right)h_{n-1}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left\lvert\nabla\tau_{\epsilon}(\hat{x}_{n-1})\cdot\left(b^{\prime}\left(\hat{\hat{x}}_{n-1}\right)b(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right)h_{n-1}^{2}\right\rvert

\displaystyle{\leq}k\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left\lvert\nabla\tau_{\epsilon}(\hat{x}_{n-1})\right\rvert\left\|b^{\prime}\left(\bar{x}_{n-1}\right)\right\|\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\rvert h_{n-1}^{2}

\displaystyle{\leq}C_{1}k\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\left\|b^{\prime}\left(\bar{x}_{n-1}\right)\right\|\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\rvert}{\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\rvert}h_{n-1}^{2}

(13)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}k\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left\|b^{\prime}(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\|h_{n-1}^{2},

with
\hat{x}_{n-1}=\bar{x}_{n-1}+\theta b(\bar{x}_{n-1})h_{n-1}
and
\hat{\hat{x}}_{n-1}=\bar{x}_{n-1}+\theta^{\prime}b(\bar{x}_{n-1})h_{n-1}
for some
\theta\in(0,1)
and
\theta^{\prime}\in(0,\theta)
. From Algorithm
2
, we have that

h_{n-1}=\epsilon/\sqrt{\left\|b^{\prime}(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\|}
, and inserting this
into the above inequality gives

\left\lvert\bar{\tau}(x_{0})-\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0})\right\rvert\leq kC\sum_{n=1}^{N}\epsilon^{2}=kCN\epsilon^{2}.

(14)

Lemma
2
below implies that
\Delta\left\lvert\bar{x}\right\rvert_{n-1}\coloneqq\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n}\right\rvert_{1}-\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n-1}\right\rvert_{1}\geq C\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\rvert_{1}h_{n-1}
for some constant
C\in(0,1]
and Assumption
(B.1)

implies that
\left\lvert b(x)\right\rvert_{1}\geq C^{\prime}\left\lvert x\right\rvert_{1}^{1+\alpha}
for some
C^{\prime}>0
, where
\left\lvert\cdot\right\rvert_{1}
denotes the 1-norm.
Thanks to these properties, we obtain that

\displaystyle N-1

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n})\right\rvert_{1}}{\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n})\right\rvert_{1}h_{n-1}}h_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n})\right\rvert_{1}+\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\rvert_{1}-\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\rvert_{1}}{\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n})\right\rvert_{1}h_{n-1}}h_{n-1}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\left(\frac{1}{\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n})\right\rvert_{1}h_{n-1}}\Delta\left\lvert\bar{x}\right\rvert_{n-1}+\frac{\left\|b^{\prime}(\bar{x}_{n})\right\|_{1}}{\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n})\right\rvert_{1}}\Delta\left\lvert\bar{x}\right\rvert_{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\left\|b^{\prime}(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\|}_{1}}{\epsilon\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n})\right\rvert_{1}}\Delta\left\lvert\bar{x}\right\rvert_{n-1}+\frac{\left\|b^{\prime}(\bar{x}_{n})\right\|_{1}}{\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n})\right\rvert_{1}}\Delta\left\lvert\bar{x}\right\rvert_{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle{\leq}C\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\left(\epsilon^{-1}\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n}\right\rvert_{1}^{-\nu}\Delta\left\lvert\bar{x}\right\rvert_{n-1}+\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n}\right\rvert_{1}^{-\upsilon}\Delta\left\lvert\bar{x}\right\rvert_{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\epsilon^{-1}\left[-\bar{x}^{1-\nu}\right]_{\left\lvert x_{0}\right\rvert}^{r(\epsilon)}+\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{-1}\right),

where
C
is a positive constant that absorb other constants.
Inserting this bound for
N
in (
14
) yields

\left\lvert\bar{\tau}(x_{0})-\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0})\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon\right)

and

\displaystyle\left\lvert\bar{\tau}(x_{0})-\tau(x_{0})\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\bar{\tau}(x_{0})-\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0})\right\rvert+\left\lvert\tau(x_{0})-\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0})\right\rvert\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Lemma \ref{lemma:boundTauDiff}}}}{{=}}\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon\right).

∎

Remark 6

(Alternative choice for
h
)
.

In some situations, being able to set
h_{n-1}=\epsilon\sqrt{\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\rvert}/\sqrt{\left\lvert b^{\prime}(\bar{x}_{n-1})b(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\rvert}
could be beneficial if either the value of
\sqrt{\left\|b^{\prime}(x)\right\|}
is significantly larger than
\sqrt{\left\lvert b^{\prime}(x)b(x)\right\rvert}
or if the former is more expensive to compute than the latter. From the calculations leading to (
3
), we see that this change of adaptive step length is possible provided
\left\lvert b^{\prime}(x+b(x)h)b(x)\right\rvert\leq C\left\lvert b^{\prime}(x)b(x)\right\rvert
holds for some
C>0
. Additionally, one needs to adjust the first part of Assumption
(B.4)
accordingly for the result of Theorem
2
to still hold.

The next lemma is used in the proof of Theorem
2
above.

Lemma 2

(Lower bound on
\Delta\left\lvert\bar{x}\right\rvert_{n-1}
)
.

If Assumption
(B.3)
holds, then

\Delta\left\lvert\bar{x}\right\rvert_{n-1}\coloneqq\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n}\right\rvert_{1}-\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n-1}\right\rvert_{1}\geq C\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\rvert_{1}h_{n-1}
for a constant
C\in(0,1]
.

Proof.

Let us first consider the case where Assumption
(B.3)(B.3a)
holds. We then have that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n-1}+b(\bar{x}_{n-1})h_{n-1}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\geq\left\lvert(\bar{x}_{n-1}+b(\bar{x}_{n-1})h_{n-1})\cdot\frac{\bar{x}_{n-1}}{\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n-1}\right\rvert^{2}}\bar{x}_{n-1}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\geq\left\lvert\left(1+\frac{b(\bar{x}_{n-1})\cdot\bar{x}_{n-1}}{\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n-1}\right\rvert^{2}}h_{n-1}\right)\bar{x}_{n-1}\right\rvert\geq\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n-1}\right\rvert+C_{2}\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\rvert h_{n-1},

and by equivalence of norms this implies our result. Assume now that Assumption
(B.3)(B.3b)
holds. Then the claim holds as

\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n}\right\rvert_{1}-\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n-1}\right\rvert_{1}=\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n-1}+b(\bar{x}_{n-1})h_{n-1}\right\rvert_{1}-\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n-1}\right\rvert_{1}=\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\rvert_{1}h_{n-1}

∎

Some points in Assumption
B
can be difficult to verify for a given vector
field.
For example, verifying Assumption
(B.2)
analytically requires knowledge of the form of the gradient
\nabla\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0})
which often will not be available. Lemma
3
introduces a sufficient condition for Assumption
(B.2)
that depends on bounding the first variation of
x
. We let
J
denote the first variation of
x
, that is

J(t,x_{0})\coloneq D_{x_{0}}x(t,x_{0})

with
D_{x_{0}}
denoting the Jacobian with respect to
x_{0}

Lemma 3
.

Let Assumption
B
, with the exception of Assumption
(B.2)
, hold and assume there exist
C>0
such that for any
\epsilon>0
and all
x_{0}\in D(\epsilon)
,

\left\|J(\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0}),x_{0})\right\|\leq C\frac{\left\lvert b(x(\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0}),x_{0}))\right\rvert}{\left\lvert b(x_{0})\right\rvert}.

Then Assumption
(B.2)
holds as well.

Proof.

The domain
D(\epsilon)\subset B(r(\epsilon))
, and since
|x(t)|
is strictly increasing, we recall that the solution will hit the sphere
\partial B(r(\epsilon))
at

t=\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0})
. The function
\phi(x)=\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}-r(\epsilon)^{2}

is equal to zero on
\partial B(r(\epsilon))
. Writing,

f(x_{0})=\phi(x(\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0}),x_{0}))
, we thus obtain

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}^{i}}f(x)=0\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,

for any
x_{0}=(x_{0}^{1},\ldots,x_{0}^{n})\in D(\epsilon)
. Expanding the left side of the above equation yields

\nabla f(x_{0})^{T}\left(b(x(\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0}),x_{0}))\partial_{x_{0}^{i}}\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0})+x_{x_{0}^{i}}(\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0}),x_{0})\right)=0,

where
x_{x_{0}^{i}}(\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0}),x_{0})
is the
i
-th column of
J(\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0}),x_{0})

and thus

\nabla\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0})=-\frac{x(\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0}),x_{0})^{T}J(\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0}),x_{0})}{x(\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0}),x_{0})^{T}b(x(\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0}),x_{0}))}.

(15)

Hence

\left\lvert\nabla\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0})\right\rvert\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{asmp:Rn.directionalityOfB.ineq}}}{{\leq}}\frac{\left\|J(\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0}),x_{0})\right\|}{C_{2}\left\lvert b(x(\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0}),x_{0}))\right\rvert}\leq\frac{C\left\lvert b(x(\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0}),x_{0}))\right\rvert}{C_{2}\left\lvert b(x(\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0}),x_{0}))\right\rvert\left\lvert b(x_{0})\right\rvert}=\frac{C_{1}}{\left\lvert b(x_{0})\right\rvert},

where
C_{1}=\frac{C}{C_{2}}
is some positive constant.
∎

Often an expression for
J
will not be readily available, possibly making the bound needed in Lemma
3
hard to find.
An alternative is then to use the sufficient condition given in Remark
7
related to quadratic forms of
b^{\prime}(x)
.

Remark 7
.

Let
v_{k}(t)
denote the
k
-th column of the first variation
J(t,x_{0})
.
Then
\dot{v_{k}}=b^{\prime}(x)v_{k}
with
v_{k}(0)=e_{k}
as
J(0,x_{0})=I
.
A sufficient condition is that there exist some constant
C>0
such that

\underset{v\in\bar{B}\left(\left\lvert b(x)\right\rvert\right)}{\sup}v^{T}b^{\prime}(x)v=\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\max}\left(b^{\prime}(x)+b^{\prime}(x)^{T}\right)\left\lvert b(x)\right\rvert^{2}\leq C^{2}b(x)^{T}b^{\prime}(x)b(x).

(16)

In addition to
C\left\lvert b(x_{0})\right\rvert\geq 1
and
b(x_{0})b^{\prime}(x_{0})b(x_{0})-\frac{\lambda_{\max}(b^{\prime}(x_{0})+b^{\prime}(x_{0})^{T})}{2C^{2}}\geq 0
.
This is the case as then

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\left\lvert b(x(t))\right\rvert^{2}}{2}-\frac{\left\lvert v_{k}(t)\right\rvert^{2}}{2C^{2}}\right)=b(x(t))b^{\prime}(x(t))b(x(t))-\frac{v_{k}^{T}(t)b^{\prime}(x(t))v_{k}^{T}(t)}{C^{2}}\geq 0,

implying that
\left\lvert v_{k}(t)\right\rvert\leq C\frac{\left\lvert b(x(t))\right\rvert}{\left\lvert b(x_{0})\right\rvert}
, as needed.

Lastly, we also provide a convenient, easy-to-check, sufficient condition for Assumption
(B.4)
.

Lemma 4
.

Assume that Assumption
(B.1)
holds and that there exist constants
C>0
and
0<\gamma<\min\{2\alpha,1+\alpha\}
such that
\left\|b^{\prime}(x)\right\|\leq C_{3}\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{\gamma}
for all
x\in D
. Then Assumption
(B.4)
holds with
\nu=-\frac{\gamma-2(1+\alpha)}{2}
and
\upsilon=-(\gamma-\alpha-1)
.

Proof.

We have that

\frac{\sqrt{\left\|b^{\prime}(x)\right\|}}{\left\lvert b(x+b(x)h)\right\rvert}\leq\frac{\sqrt{\left\lvert x+b(x)h\right\rvert^{\gamma}}}{\left\lvert b(x+b(x)h)\right\rvert}\leq\left\lvert x+b(x)h\right\rvert^{\frac{\gamma-2(1+\alpha)}{2}},

and as
\gamma<2\alpha
we get
\nu=-\frac{\gamma-2(1+\alpha)}{2}>1
. Furthermore,
\frac{\left\|b^{\prime}(x)\right\|}{\left\lvert b(x)\right\rvert}\leq\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{\gamma-\alpha-1}
, and from the assumed restriction on
\gamma
, we have
\upsilon=-(\gamma-\alpha-1)\geq\max\{1-\alpha,0\}
.
∎

Assumption B

Consider the ODE (

Finally, we can prove a result analogue to Theorem

for this setting.

Corollary 2
.

Let Assumption
B

′

hold with parameters such that
\gamma\geq 1+\alpha
. Moreover, let

be calculated using Algorithm
2
with

and

. Then, for sufficiently small

with a computational cost of

\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1-\frac{\gamma}{\alpha}}+\epsilon^{-1})&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

The error indicators are in this setting the same as what we have in (
3

h_{n-1}=\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{N\max\{1,b^{\prime}(\bar{x}_{n-1})\}}}

\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle N-1

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n})\right\rvert h_{n-1}}\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n})\right\rvert h_{n-1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\left\|b^{\prime}(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\|}}{\epsilon\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n})\right\rvert}+C^{\prime}\frac{\left\|b^{\prime}(\bar{x}_{n})\right\|}{\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n})\right\rvert}\right)\Delta\left\lvert\bar{x}\right\rvert_{n-1}

∎

4.1.
Note on methods used for comparison

We will in all the bellow examples compare our adaptive method to using uniform steps and the method introduced by Hirota and Ozawa in
[
14
]
.
This method is based on rewriting the underlying ODE into a system for the arc length of the process and then solving this system using a higher-order ODE solver.
There results assume that the blow-up component satisfies
\left\|x(t)\right\|=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{(\tau-t)^{p}}\right)
for some
p>0
.

In the following figures we plot the observed error for this method against a tolerance
\epsilon
that is used as an input for the other numerical methods.
As the method presented by Hirota and Ozawa does not take a tolerance input we use this value to decide how far we are calculating the arc length.
The observed error and cost for this method must therefore be read together in order to see how quickly the error is decreasing relative to the increase in cost.

Due to the use of a fifth-order ODE-solver this method performs impressively, achieving quick convergence for many of the problems we study. However, our adaptive method has some theoretical advantages, as we under less restrictive regularity assumptions on the vector field
b
and without any a priori assumptions on the form of the blow-up components have, in contrast to
[
14
]
, provided rigorous error and complexity bounds for the numerical method.
Following Remark
4
, one may also in some situations use our method with higher-order integrators in order to match the performance of the method by Hirota and Ozawa.
Additionally, our method is not dependent on any parameters that need to be tweaked.

\hat{C},\beta>0
such that
b(x)\cdot x\leq\hat{C}\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2+\beta}
.
Then, starting from (
3
) we get

\displaystyle\left\lvert\bar{\tau}(x_{0})-\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq Ch\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n-1}\right\rvert^{\gamma-\alpha-1}\Delta\left\lvert\bar{x}\right\rvert_{n-1}

\displaystyle=Ch\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n}\right\rvert^{\gamma-\alpha-1}+\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n-1}\right\rvert^{\gamma-\alpha-2}\Delta\left\lvert\bar{x}\right\rvert_{n-1}\right)\Delta\left\lvert\bar{x}\right\rvert_{n-1}

\displaystyle\leq Ch\int_{\left\lvert x_{0}\right\rvert}^{r(\epsilon)}\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n}\right\rvert^{\gamma-\alpha-1}\,d\left\lvert x\right\rvert+Ch^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n-1}\right\rvert^{\gamma+\beta-\alpha-1}\Delta\left\lvert\bar{x}\right\rvert_{n-1}

\displaystyle\leq Ch\log(r(\epsilon))+Ch^{2}r(\epsilon)^{\beta},

where the jump to the second line adds and subtracts
\left\lvert\bar{x}_{n}\right\rvert^{\gamma-\alpha-1}
before taking a Taylor expansion of the subtracted term.
Setting each term equal to
\epsilon
and solving for
h
gives that setting
h=\min\left\{\frac{\epsilon}{\log\left(\epsilon^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right)},\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+1)}\right\}=\frac{\epsilon}{\log\left(\epsilon^{-1}\right)}
ensures
\left\lvert\bar{\tau}(x_{0})-\tau_{\epsilon}(x_{0})\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
.
The cost is then
Cost=\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{-1}\log\left(\epsilon^{-1}\right)\right)
.
From Theorem
2
we expect the same error rate for the adaptive method, but at a lower cost of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
.

From the left plot of Figure
6
we see that the costs are as expected, with again the higher-order method outperforming the first-order methods for smaller
\epsilon
. The same is observed for the error rates in the right plot of the figure. Here the error is calculated against a pseudo solution generated using the adaptive method with
\epsilon=2^{-27}
.

4.7.
Estimating blow-up of slow processes in multiple dimensions

Similarly to Section
4.4
, we here want to considerer functions
b:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
that explodes relatively slow. Let

b(x)=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\log(x_{1}^{2}+2x_{2}^{2})^{1+c}\\
x_{2}\log(2x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})^{1+c}\end{bmatrix},

and set
x(0)=(4,3)
. We let
D=\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B(5)
.
This function satisfies Assumption
B

′

with
\alpha=c,\gamma=1+c
and some finite values for the constants
\check{C}
and
C_{3}
.
Assumption
(B.5)
also trivially holds.
We let
c\in\left\{\frac{1}{2},1\right\}
.
For Assumption
(B.3)(B.3a)
it suffices to note that

\frac{b(x)\cdot x}{\left\lvert b(x)\right\rvert\left\lvert x\right\rvert}\geq\frac{\log(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})^{1+c}}{\log(2(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}))^{1+c}}>0.

Let
R\coloneqq x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}
. Then, for Assumption
(B.2)
we have that (
16
) holds as

\lambda_{\max}(b^{\prime}(x))\left\lvert b(x)\right\rvert_{2}^{2}\leq CR^{2}\log(2R^{2})^{3+3c}\leq C^{\prime 2}b(x)^{T}b^{\prime}(x)b(x),

for some constants
C,C^{\prime}>0
.
Therefore Assumption
(B.2)
is satisfied as the above together with Assumption
(B.3)(B.3a)
is sufficient for Lemma
3
to hold.
For Assumption
(B.6)
we get
\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\left\|b^{\prime}(x)\right\|_{2}}}b(x)\leq\frac{c\epsilon b(x)}{\log(R^{2})^{\frac{1+c}{2}}}.

One may then show that

\frac{\left\|b^{\prime}\left(x+\frac{c\theta\epsilon b(x)}{\log(R^{2})^{\frac{1+c}{2}}}\right)\right\|_{2}}{\left\|b^{\prime}(x)\right\|_{2}}\leq k,

for some constant
k
, which is what we need.

For the uniform steps, we consider the error indicator we found in the proof of Theorem
2
, giving

\left\lvert\bar{\tau}-\tau_{\epsilon}\right\rvert\leq kC\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\left\|b^{\prime}(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\|\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\rvert}{\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\rvert}h^{2}\leq\frac{kCh}{C_{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\left\|b^{\prime}(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\|}{\left\lvert b(\bar{x}_{n-1})\right\rvert}\Delta\left\lvert\bar{x}\right\rvert\leq\frac{kCh}{C_{2}}\log(r)

as
\gamma-\alpha-1=0
. Then, in order to bound the error by
\epsilon
, we must use the uniform step
h=C\frac{\epsilon}{\log(r(\epsilon))}
. From Corollary
1
we have that
r(\epsilon)=e^{\left(\frac{1}{\check{CC_{2}c\epsilon}}\right)^{\frac{1}{c}}}
, thus the cost of using uniform steps is
N=\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{-3}\right)
for
c=\frac{1}{2}
and
N=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
when
c=1
.

From Corollary
2
we have that the same error is achieved using the adaptive method, but at the lower cost of
N=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
when
c=\frac{1}{2}
and
N=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1})^{2})
for
c=1
.

The results from these experiments are shown in Figure
7
. The pseudo solution used to calculate the error is generated using the adaptive method with
\epsilon=2^{-17}
. From the figure we see that both methods have the expected error of order
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
for both values of
c
, and from the plots on the left we see that the cost is as expected. Similarly to what we observed in Section
4.4
, the higher-order method does not perform well here, again most likely due to the blow-up component not having the required form.

4.8.
Semi-discretized reaction-diffusion equation

In the last example, we estimate the blow-up time of a semi-discretized version of a reaction-diffusion equation with a quadratic nonlinearity. We are not able to verify that this problem satisfies Assumption
B
, so this is a purely experimental numerical study.
The PDE is given by
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,x)=\Delta u(t,x)+f(u(t,x))
for
(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times(0,1)
with
f(x)=x^{2}
, Dirichlet-0 boundary conditions
u(t,0)=u(t,1)=0
and the initial condition
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)=100\sin(\pi x)
.

The semi-discretized version of this PDE, with finite-difference approximation in space, is the following system of ODEs in
\mathbb{R}^{m-1}

\begin{cases}\frac{dx_{1}}{dt}=m^{2}(-2x_{1}+x_{2})+x_{1}^{2},\\
\hskip 7.11317pt\vdots\\
\frac{dx_{k}}{dt}=m^{2}(x_{k-1}-2x_{k}+x_{k+1})+x_{k}^{2},\\
\hskip 7.11317pt\vdots\\
\frac{dx_{m-1}}{dt}=m^{2}(x_{m-2}-2x_{m-1})+x_{m-1}^{2}.\end{cases}

The dimension parameter
m\in\mathbb{N}
is related to the spatial grid of the finite-difference approximation
(0,1/m,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
, and the boundary condition is
x_{0}(t)=x_{m}(t)=0
for all
t\geq 0
.
As we here need to discretize in both time and space we let
x_{k}^{n}
denote the numerical estimate of
x_{k}(t_{n})
which again is an approximation of
u(t_{n},\frac{k}{m})
.
We fix
x^{0}=\left[0,100\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{m}\right),100\sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{m}\right),\ldots,100\sin\left(\frac{\pi(m-1)}{m}\right),0\right]
.

We want to numerically estimate the convergence rates for both refinements and for both adaptive and uniform time stepping.
We therefore estimate
\bar{\tau}
for fixed
m=32
and let
\epsilon
vary between
2^{-18}
and
2^{-25}
in order to estimate the convergence rate when refining
\epsilon
.
In order to do the same for
m
we fix
\epsilon=2^{-23}
and let
m\in\{4,8,16,32,64,128,216,512\}
.
Furthermore, experimentation shows that following the alternative choice for
h
given in Remark
6
and setting
h_{n}=\min\left\{\frac{\epsilon\sqrt{\left\lvert b(x)\right\rvert}}{\sqrt{\left\lvert b^{\prime}(x)b(x)\right\rvert}},\frac{1}{2m^{2}}\right\}
gives a significantly lower cost for
m\geq 16
.

The results of this study are given in Table
1
and Table
2
.
From the first table we see that when refining
\epsilon
the error of both methods appear to be of order
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
.
For the cost it looks like there is a log-term involved in the cost for the uniform version, while it is linear for the adaptive method.
These results coincide with what we observed in earlier examples.
On the other hand, when refining
n
we note that the order of the cost and error looks to be quite similar for the two methods. The error has a rate of about
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1.5})
, while the cost is constant for the adaptive method, and actually slightly decreasing in the uniform case.

5.
Conclusion and future work

We have here presented two a priori adaptive numerical methods for estimating the blow-up time of ODEs.
For both methods, we prove that under sufficient regularity,
an
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
approximation error
is achieved at
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
computational cost.
These theoretical findings are validated in several numerical experiments.
In addition, we show how the method can be applied to some problems not satisfying Assumption
A.3
, and discuss how to extend adaptive time stepping from first-order
to higher-order numerical integrators.

There are several possible directions for future research.
One such direction is to try to relax the conditions on
b
in both the 1-dimensional and multidimensional case.
For example, one might look into the possibility of remove the monotonicity assumption, allowing for more interesting dynamics, such as ODEs generating oscillating paths that explode in finite time.
Additionally, adapting our numerical method to a stochastic setting trying to estimate the blow-up time of stochastic differential equation, such as in
[
11
]
, could also prove fruitful. A potential way forward is to combine our method with existing adaptive method for approximating hitting times of SDEs
[
15
]
, relating to the threshold hitting time
\tau_{\epsilon}
applied in the current work.
