Observable Neural ODEs for Identifiable Causal Forecasting in Continuous Time
Jennifer Wendland, Nicolas Freitag, Maik Kschischo

Abstract
Causal inference in continuous-time sequential decision problems is challenged by hidden confounders. We show that, in latent state-space models with time-varying interventions, observability of the latent dynamics from observed data is necessary for identifying dynamic treatment effects, linking control-theoretic observability to causal identifiability, even when hidden confounders affect both treatments and outcomes. We derive a continuous-time adjustment formula expressing potential outcome distributions under treatment trajectories via the measurement model, latent dynamics, and the filtering distribution over latent states given observed histories. We propose Observable Neural ODEs (ObsNODEs), Neural ODE models in observable normal form for causal forecasting. ObsNODEs learn continuous-time dynamics with states reconstructible from observations, enabling outcome prediction under alternative treatment paths. Experiments on synthetic cancer data, semi-synthetic data based on MIMIC-IV, and real-world sepsis data show strong performance over recent sequence models.



1
Introduction

Sequential decision-making requires predicting how actions affect future outcomes under evolving observations. In medicine, this arises in dynamic treatment regimes (DTRs), where treatments are repeatedly adapted to a patient’s longitudinal history
(
23
;
26
)
.

A central challenge is the identifiability of causal treatment effects in continuous time. In observational data, time-dependent confounding and treatment-confounder feedback arise when covariates affect both treatment assignment and outcomes while being themselves affected by earlier treatments
(
12
)
. Hidden confounders further complicate this problem, since the relevant disease state is only partially observed.

We address this problem from a state-space perspective. We introduce
ObsNODEs
, a class of Neural ODE models in observable normal form, and show that observability of the latent state is a necessary condition for identifying dynamic treatment effects in latent continuous-time systems with hidden confounding. This establishes a link between the control-theoretic notion of observability and causal identifiability in continuous-time treatment models. Based on this insight, ObsNODEs support causal forecasting under discrete and continuous treatment trajectories.

Contributions.

(i) We show that observability of the latent state is necessary for identifiability of dynamic treatment effects in latent continuous-time state-space models with hidden confounding affecting both treatment and outcomes, and derive a corresponding continuous-time causal adjustment formula.
(ii) We propose
ObsNODEs
, Neural ODE models in observable normal form that enforce observability by construction for causal forecasting under dynamic treatment regimes.
(iii) We evaluate ObsNODEs on synthetic cancer data, semi-synthetic MIMIC-IV data, and a real-world sepsis example, demonstrating strong causal forecasting performance compared to recent sequence models.

2
Problem statement

We assume to observe a continuous-time multivariate outcome process
\bm{Y}_{t}
and a time-varying treatment process
\bm{A}_{t}
for times
t\in[0,t_{c})
. Given the history
\{\bm{a}_{[0,t_{c})},\bm{y}_{[0,t_{c}]}\}
with
\bm{a}_{[0,t_{c})}=\{\bm{A}_{t}=\bm{a}_{t}|0\leq t<t_{c}\}
and
\bm{y}_{[0,t_{c}]}=\{\bm{Y}_{t}=\bm{y}_{t}|0\leq t\leq t_{c}\}
, the goal is to predict the probability density of the conditional potential outcome
\bm{Y}_{t}(\bm{a}_{[t_{c},t_{c}+s)})
on
[t_{c},t_{c}+s)
under a hypothetical treatment path
\bm{a}_{[t_{c},t_{c}+s)}=\{\bm{A}_{t}=\bm{a}_{t}|t_{c}\leq t<t_{c}+s\}
.

In clinical applications,
\bm{Y}_{t}
may contain irregularly sampled laboratory values, vital signs, or imaging embeddings, while
\bm{A}_{t}
represents binary, categorical, or continuous treatments. Outside randomized trials, treatment assignment depends on the evolving patient history, which induces time-dependent confounding and treatment-confounder feedback.

We treat all measured variables as components of
\bm{Y}_{t}
and define outcomes, when needed, as functions of
\bm{Y}_{t}
. This entails no loss of generality for the identification results below. We use outcome and observed process interchangeably for
\bm{Y}_{t}
.

3
Preliminaries

3.1
Nonlinear state-space model

A continuous-time deterministic state-space model is given by

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{z}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{f}(\bm{z}_{t},\bm{a}_{t}),

(1a)

\displaystyle\bm{y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{h}(\bm{z}_{t}),

(1b)

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{0}

\displaystyle=\bm{\zeta},

(1c)

where
\bm{z}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}}
is a latent state,
\bm{a}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}
a treatment or control, and
\bm{y}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
the observed process. The measurement function
\bm{h}
is not necessarily invertible, in particular when
d_{z}>d_{y}
. We assume
\bm{f}
is Lipschitz in
\bm{z}
, so the system admits a unique solution for any admissible control path and initial condition.

For a given control path, observability asks whether the latent state or, equivalently, its initial value, is uniquely determined by the observed output over a finite horizon
(
3
)
. For a given control
\bm{a}
and initial condition
\bm{z}_{0}=\bm{\zeta}
, let
\bm{z}_{t}(\bm{a},\bm{\zeta})
denote the resulting state trajectory, and define the corresponding output trajectory by
\bm{y}_{t}(\bm{a},\bm{\zeta}):=\bm{h}\!\left(\bm{z}_{t}(\bm{a},\bm{\zeta})\right)
.

Definition 3.1
.

The system (
1
) is
uniformly observable
on an open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}}
if, for any control path
\bm{a}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}
and any
\bm{\zeta},\bm{\eta}\in U
,

\bm{y}_{t}(\bm{a},\bm{\zeta})=\bm{y}_{t}(\bm{a},\bm{\eta})\;\;\forall t\in[0,T]\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\bm{\zeta}=\bm{\eta},

where
[0,T]
is the maximal interval on which both solutions exist.

3.2
Potential outcomes and graphical causal models

We use the potential-outcomes framework and graphical causal models to formalize interventions
(
27
;
24
;
25
;
12
)
. For a treatment path
\bm{a}_{[0,T)}
, let
\bm{Y}_{t}(\bm{a}_{[0,t)})
denote the potential outcome process. We assume consistency and the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA), which excludes that the potential outcome of one unit is affected by treatments of other units and that there are no different forms or versions of a treatment
(
29
;
12
)
. In longitudinal observational settings, identifiability is complicated by time-dependent confounding and treatment-confounder feedback.

3.2.1
The conditional front-door criterion

To connect causal identification with latent state-space models, we use the conditional front-door criterion
(
38
)
, an extension of the front-door criterion
(
24
)
for estimating the effect of an intervention in the presence of an unobserved common cause of both treatment and outcome:

Theorem 3.2
.

(
38
)

A set of variables
M
fulfills the
conditional front-door criterion (CFD)
relative to the ordered pair or variables
(A,Y)
in a directed acyclic graph (DAG)
\mathcal{G}
, if (i)
M
intercepts all directed paths from
A
to
Y
, (ii) there exists a set of conditioning variables
W
that blocks all backdoor paths from
A
to
M
and (iii) all backdoor path between
M
and
Y
are blocked by
\{A\}\cup W
. If
M
fulfills the CFD relative to
(A,Y)
with conditioning variables
W
the causal effect estimate is given by

P(Y(a))=\sum_{m,w,a^{\prime}}P(y|a^{\prime},m,w)P(a^{\prime}|w)P(m|a,w)P(w),

(2)

where the sum over
a^{\prime}
runs over alternative treatments.

Observable Neural ODEs for dynamic treatment regimes

, outcome

and

. The process
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}

We model the observed process by a latent Neural ODE
(
7
;
28
;
19
)
with parameters
\theta

\displaystyle\bm{Z}_{0}\sim p_{\theta}(\bm{Z}_{0}),\qquad\dot{\bm{Z}}_{t}=\bm{f}_{\theta}(\bm{Z}_{t},\bm{A}_{t}),\qquad\bm{Y}_{t}\sim q_{\theta}(\cdot|\bm{Z}_{t},\bm{\epsilon}_{t}),

(3)

where
p_{\theta}
parameterizes the initial state distribution,
\bm{f}_{\theta}
the vector field, and
q_{\theta}
the observation model. We interpret the treatment process as an intervention on the latent dynamics, as illustrated in Fig.
1
(a). Typically, the treatment is adjusted according to the observations and we have a dynamic treatment regime
(
23
;
26
;
12
)
:
\bm{A}_{t}=\bm{\pi}(\bm{y}_{t},\bm{y}_{[0,t)},\bm{a}_{[0,t)})
(see Fig.
1
(b)). An unobserved process
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}
that influences both the outcome and treatment acts as a hidden time-dependent confounder, and the treatment policy
\bm{\pi}
may therefore also depend on
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}
.

4.1
Causal identifiability for the state-space model with hidden confounding

Given observed history
\bm{o}_{[0,t)}=\{\bm{a}_{[0,t)},\bm{y}_{[0,t)}\}
and current observation
\bm{Y}_{t}=\bm{y}_{t}
, we seek the conditional density of the potential outcome at time
t+s
under a hypothetical future treatment path
\bm{a}_{[t,t+s)}
. Under consistency, SUTVA, and an observable latent state-space model of the form (
3
), this density is identified by

\displaystyle P_{\bm{Y}_{t+s}(\bm{a}_{[t,t+s)})|\bm{Y}_{t},\bm{O}_{[0,t)}}(\bm{y}_{t+s}|\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{[0,t)})

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\bm{z}_{t+s}}\int_{\bm{z}_{t}}p(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{[0,t)})\,p(\bm{z}_{t+s}|\bm{a}_{[t,t+s)},\bm{z}_{t})\,p(\bm{y}_{t+s}|\bm{z}_{t+s}),

(4)

even if there is time dependent confounding by an unobserved dynamic process
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}
affecting both treatment
\bm{A}_{t}
and outcome
\bm{Y}_{t}
(see Fig.
1
).

The key requirement is that the latent state is observable from the observed history; otherwise the filtering density
p(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{[0,t)})
remains spread over states that explain the treatment-observation history equally well, preventing identification of causal effects. For the deterministic ODE in (
3
) the dynamics is given by the point mass:
p(\bm{z}_{t+s}|\bm{a}_{[t,t+s)},\bm{z}_{t})=\delta(\bm{z}_{t+s}-\bm{z}_{t}-\int_{t}^{t+s}\bm{f}(\bm{z}_{\tau},\bm{a}_{\tau})\,d\tau)
.
The adjustment formula (
4.1
) also applies to dynamic treatment regimes (Figure
1
(b)), where the current observation and observed history
\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{[0,t)}
is used to guide the future treatment path
\bm{a}_{[t,t+s)}
.

4.1.1
Derivation of a discrete time version of equation (
4.1
) from the CFD

We derive a time discretized version of (
4.1
) and relegate details for the continuous time version to appendix
A.1
. Consider three consecutive time points
0,t
and
t+s
. A time-discretized approximation of the ODE in (
3
) is given by

\bm{Z}_{t+s}\approx\bm{Z}_{t}+\bm{f}(\bm{z}_{t},\bm{a}_{t})\,s
.
Assume we have observed
\bm{o}_{0}=\{\bm{a}_{0},\bm{y}_{0}\}
and
\bm{y}_{t}
. To apply the CFD in theorem
3.2
we make the following identifications

\displaystyle A\leftarrowtail\bm{A}_{t},

\displaystyle\quad Y\leftarrowtail\bm{Y}_{t+s},\quad M\leftarrowtail\bm{Z}_{t+s},\quad W\leftarrowtail\{\bm{Z}_{t},\bm{Y}_{t},\bm{A}_{0},\bm{Y}_{0}\}=\{\bm{Z}_{t},\bm{Y}_{t},\bm{O}_{0}\}\,.

Then, we can check graphically in Fig.
1
(a,b) that
M=\bm{Z}_{t+s}
fulfills the CFD-criterion relative to the ordered pair
(A,Y)=(\bm{A}_{t},\bm{Y}_{t+s})
, if we use
W=\{\bm{Z}_{t},\bm{Y}_{t},\bm{O}_{[0,t)}\}
as conditioning set. Now we compute the probability density for the potential outcome
Y_{t+s}(\bm{a}_{t})
when applying treatment
\bm{A}_{t}=\bm{a}_{t}
at time
t
using (
2
) and replacing the sums by integrals

\displaystyle P_{Y_{t+s}(\bm{a}_{t})}\left(\bm{y}_{t+s}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{z}_{t+s}}\int_{\bm{z}_{t},\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{0}}\int_{\bm{a}^{\prime}_{t}}\left\{p\left(\bm{y}_{t+s}|\bm{a}^{\prime}_{t},\bm{z}_{t+s},\bm{z}_{t},\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{0}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\left.p\left(\bm{a}^{\prime}_{t}|\bm{z}_{t},\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{0}\right)\,p\left(\bm{z}_{t+s}|\bm{a}_{t},\bm{z}_{t},\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{0}\right)\,p\left(\bm{z}_{t},\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{0}\right)\right\}.

For the model (
3
) we have the following independence relations

\displaystyle p\left(\bm{y}_{t+s}|\bm{a}^{\prime}_{t},\bm{z}_{t+s},\bm{z}_{t},\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=p\left(\bm{y}_{t+s}|\bm{z}_{t+s}\right)

\displaystyle p\left(\bm{z}_{t+s}|\bm{a}_{t},\bm{z}_{t},\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=p\left(\bm{z}_{t+s}|\bm{a}_{t},\bm{z}_{t}\right)\,.

Integrating over
\bm{a}^{\prime}_{t}
and using
p\left(\bm{z}_{t},\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{0}\right)=p\left(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{0}\right)\,p\left(\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{0}\right)
we find

\displaystyle P_{Y_{t+s}(\bm{a}_{t})}\left(\bm{y}_{t+s}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{z}_{t+s}}\int_{\bm{z}_{t},\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{0}}\left\{p\left(\bm{y}_{t+s}|\bm{z}_{t+s}\right)p\left(\bm{z}_{t+s}|\bm{a}_{t},\bm{z}_{t}\right)\,p\left(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{0}\right)p\left(\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{0}\right)\right\},

from which we read the conditional density for the potential outcome
Y_{t+s}(\bm{a}_{t})
when applying treatment
\bm{A}_{t}=\bm{a}_{t}
at time
t
, given we have observed outcome
\bm{y}_{t}
at time
t
and the treatment/outcome history
\bm{o}_{0}
at time zero

\displaystyle P_{Y_{t+s}(\bm{a}_{t})|\bm{Y}_{t},\bm{O}_{0}}\left(\bm{y}_{t+s}\left|\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{0}\right.\right)=\int_{\bm{z}_{t+s}}\int_{\bm{z}_{t}}\left\{p\left(\bm{y}_{t+s}|\bm{z}_{t+s}\right)p\left(\bm{z}_{t+s}|\bm{a}_{t},\bm{z}_{t}\right)\,p\left(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{0}\right)\right\}.

4.2
Neural ODEs in observable normal form (ObsNODEs)

The identification formula (
4.1
) requires that the latent state can be inferred from observed outcomes and treatment history. Standard latent Neural ODEs do not guarantee this property. We therefore parameterize the latent dynamics in continuous triangular observable normal form
(
3
;
1
)
:

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{Z}}_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\dot{Z}}_{t}^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\dot{Z}}_{t}^{(i)}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\dot{Z}}_{t}^{(m)}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{Z}_{t}^{(2)}+\bm{\phi}^{(1)}_{\theta_{1}}(\bm{Z}_{t}^{(1)},\bm{a}_{t})\\
\vdots\\
\bm{Z}_{t}^{(i+1)}+\bm{\phi}^{(i)}_{\theta_{i}}(\bm{Z}_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{Z}_{t}^{(i)},\bm{a}_{t})\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\phi}^{(m)}_{\theta_{m}}(\bm{Z}_{t},\bm{a}_{t})\end{pmatrix},\qquad\bm{Y}_{t}=\bm{Z}_{t}^{(1)}.

(5)

Here the state
\bm{Z}_{t}=\left(\bm{Z}_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{Z}_{t}^{(m)}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}}
is partitioned into
m
blocks
\bm{Z}_{t}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
, so that
d_{z}=md_{y}
, and each
\bm{\phi}^{(i)}_{\theta_{i}}
is implemented by a neural network. Under fairly general conditions
(
3
)
, an observable system in the form (
1
) can be transformed into the observable normal form (
5
). In practice we allow additive observation noise,

\bm{Y}_{t}=\bm{Z}_{t}^{(1)}+\bm{\epsilon}_{t},

with centered noise process
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}
, because it can otherwise be included into the dynamics of
\bm{Z}_{t}^{(1)}
.

4.3
Prediction and training of ObsNODEs

We approximate the filtering distribution
p(\bm{z}_{t}\mid\bm{y}_{t},\bm{o}_{[0,t)})
with an RNN encoder, following
7
. Given the inferred state
\bm{z}_{t}
, forecasts under a hypothetical treatment path
\bm{a}_{[t,t+s)}
are obtained by integrating (
5
) forward in time and projecting to the observed space.

Training is self-supervised. For each unit, we choose a decision time
t_{c}
and provide observations up to
t_{c}
. The model then predicts future observations on
(t_{c},t_{f}]
.

Observations are available only at irregular time points
\mathcal{T}_{ij}
.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{ij}(t_{c},t_{f})=\mathcal{T}_{ij}\cap(t_{c},t_{f}]
denote the observation times of component
j
for unit
i
in that forecast window
(t_{c},t_{f}]
. The predicted outcome vector for unit
i
and outcome component
j
at time
t
is to be compared against the corresponding observed value
y_{tij}^{dat}
in the training data.
We minimize the masked squared error

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{(t_{c},t_{f}]}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{y}}\frac{1}{n\,\sigma_{j}^{2}\,|\mathcal{T}_{ij}(t_{c},t_{f})|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{ij}(t_{c},t_{f})}\left(y_{tij}-y_{tij}^{\mathrm{dat}}\right)^{2},

where
|\mathcal{T}_{ij}(t_{c},t_{f})|
denotes the number of observations of component
j
for unit
i
in the forecasting interval
(t_{c},t_{f}]
. This normalization compensates for varying observation frequencies across units and outcome components. The scaling factor
\sigma_{j}^{2}
is the empirical variance of the

j-
th outcome component across all units and observed time points in the training data.

During training, a decision time
t_{c}\in[0,t_{f})
is selected for all units in a batch of size
n
and the corresponding losses
\mathcal{L}_{(t_{c},t_{f}]}
are averaged over different batches. We found that both a fixed set of possible values for
t_{c}
evenly distributed over
[0,t_{f})
or a uniform random choice led to very similar results. Chosing different decision times
t_{c}
in each batch exposes the model to varying assimilation times and forecast horizons.

5.1
Experiments with fully-synthetic data

Related Work

Classical statistical approaches for longitudinal causal inference focus on estimating population-level average potential outcomes and do not condition on individual treatment and outcome histories
(
27
;
2
;
21
;
33
;
30
)
. Consequently, they cannot be used for individualized outcome forecasting. These approaches are typically grounded in structural causal models and identification results such as the longitudinal g-formula. Extensions to continuous time include structural dynamic causal models
(
5
)
, which adopt a different formulation of interventions than considered here. Recent work has developed discrete-time sequence models for estimating conditional average potential outcomes (CAPO) (i.e. the expectation value of (
4.1
)), including recurrent and attention-based architectures

(
4
;
20
;
22
;
14
)
.
A limitation of these approaches is their reliance on fixed time discretizations, whereas clinical measurements are typically observed at irregular time points.

Continuous-time approaches based on neural differential equations address this limitation. Treatment Effect Controlled Differential Equations (TE-CDEs)
(
32
)
combine controlled differential equations
(
19
)
with representation balancing to mitigate time-dependent confounding. The original formulation considered binary treatments and was later extended to continuous treatments
(
36
)
. However, balancing-based approaches may not fully address time-dependent confounding in sequential settings
(
22
)
.

The SCIP-Net
(
13
)
is a recent continuous time method for estimating the CAPO based on a stabilized inverse propensity score weighting, which is, however, restricted to discrete treatment levels.

Our ObsNODE framework is most closely related to CF-ODE
(
6
)
, which parameterizes a continuous-time state-space model of the form (
3
) using a Neural ODE. CF-ODE assumes that observations at treatment time uniquely determine the latent state, corresponding to the observability condition (Definition
3.1
). However, this assumption is not structurally enforced and is difficult to verify in practice. In contrast, ObsNODE employs the observable normal form (
5
), which guarantees observability by construction and makes explicit the link between control-theoretic observability and the identifiability condition required for the causal adjustment formula (
4.1
). Moreover, CF-ODE conditions only on prior observations and does not explicitly incorporate the treatment history.

Together, these limitations motivate a continuous-time state-space formulation that enforces observability and enables causal outcome prediction through an explicit adjustment formula, which we develop in the ObsNODE framework.

7
Conclusion and Limitations

We introduced Observable Neural ODEs (ObsNODE), a continuous-time state-space framework for causal outcome forecasting under sequential treatments. By combining Neural ODE dynamics with an observable latent-state representation and an adjustment formula, ObsNODE enables identification of dynamic treatment effects under time-dependent confounding while allowing parts of the latent state to represent interpretable disease dynamics. Experiments on synthetic, semi-synthetic, and real-world clinical data demonstrate strong causal forecasting performance compared to recent sequence models. As with all causal models, the approach relies on assumptions about the data-generating process whose validity in real-world multimodal settings requires further study. The adjustment formula also provides a natural basis for uncertainty quantification of causal forecasts, an important direction for future methodological work.

A.2
Details on training the ObsNODE model

The datasets were split into training, validation, and test sets and feature-wise Z-score normalization was applied using statistics computed on the training set only. The normalized dataset is subsequently used for model training.

Each multivariate time series was segmented at a decision time point
t_{c}
into two disjoint subsequences. The prefix segment consists of all observations prior to
t_{c}
and is used to condition the recognition model. The model infers an initial state
\bm{z}_{0}
for each individual trajectory using an an LSTM-based recognition network
28
.

To handle missing observations, the recognition LSTM is augmented with an explicit imputation mechanism. The indicator function

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{tij}=\begin{cases}1:&y_{tij}\;\text{is observed}\\
0:&y_{tij}\;\text{is missing}.\end{cases}

indicates whether the data for the
j-
th component of the
i-
th unit at time
t
is observed or not,
The imputation layer is defined as the transformation

\displaystyle{y}_{tij}=y_{tij}\cdot\mathcal{I}_{tij}+b_{j}\cdot\left(1-\mathcal{I}_{tij}\right),

where
b_{j}
are learnable parameters
8
;
35
. This formulation replaces missing entries with trainable values that are optimized jointly with the LSTM parameters in an end-to-end fashion.

The latent initial states
\bm{z}_{0}
inferred by the LSTM, together with the control function
\bm{a}_{t}
are subsequently passed as inputs to the Neural Differential Equation (NODE) model. The observations after the decision time point
t_{c}
are used for loss computation and subsequent optimization. Model parameters are optimized using the Adam optimizer.

The ObsNODE model supports both direct long-horizon forecasting and a recursive scheme, similar in spirit to ODE-RNN
28
. In the long-horizon setting, the state estimated by the LSTM recognition model is used to generate a forecast over the entire prediction window. In the recursive setting, forecasts are produced iteratively: at each step, the ODE model generates a short-term prediction, which is appended to the observation and treatment history sequence observed up to
t_{c}
. Conditioned on this augmented sequence, the LSTM updates the latent state, which then serves as the initial condition for the next ODE rollout. This process repeats until the full horizon is covered, without using any ground-truth observations beyond
t_{c}
.
We find empirically that augmenting the sequence with intermediate predictions can improve stability for longer forecasting horizons in some settings.

Each column corresponds to a subscore (0–4) reflecting the severity of dysfunction in the central nervous, cardiovascular, respiratory, coagulation, hepatic, and renal systems. Subscores of 0 indicate normal organ function as defined by the respective criteria.

We use the MIMIC-IV electronic health record dataset collected at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, which contains data from approximately 450,000 hospital admissions
(
17
)
. The dataset includes demographic information, laboratory values, vital signs, medications, and diagnoses.

For our analysis we focus on sepsis patients identified using the OpenSep pipeline according to the Sepsis-3 criteria
(
15
)
. Sepsis cases were defined by an increase in the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score of at least two points within 48 hours before or 24 hours after a suspected infection.

The SOFA score is used as the clinical outcome variable. It summarizes dysfunction in six organ systems (respiratory, cardiovascular, central nervous system, coagulation, liver, and kidney), each scored from 0 to 4 according to the severity of dysfunction
(
34
)
. An overview of the subscores is given in Table
6
.

We consider patients treated with the antibiotics vancomycin, ceftriaxone, and piperacillin-tazobactam. To assess potential treatment side effects we additionally predict laboratory indicators of organ toxicity, including creatinine (kidney injury) as well as total bilirubin and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (liver injury).

Data preprocessing follows the OptAB pipeline
(
37
)
. The cohort is restricted to ICU patients, and antibiotic treatments are encoded as a vector of three binary variables indicating administration of vancomycin, ceftriaxone, and piperacillin-tazobactam.

Time-dependent covariates were selected from all available dynamic variables by excluding variables recorded in fewer than 50% of patients and retaining those with an absolute Spearman correlation of at least 0.3 with the SOFA score during the first 12 hours. The resulting covariates are listed in Table
7
.

To capture information about measurement patterns, cumulative missingness indicators were added as additional time-dependent covariates. Static covariates include sex, age, height, and weight at admission.

The final dataset contains 3,556 patients. We use 80% of the data for training and 20% for testing, with the training set further split into 80% training and 20% validation data.

A.5
Computational efficiency

For training and hyperparameter optimization of ObsNODE we utilized two Nvidia A100 GPUs, each with 40 GB of memory. The same setting was used for the baseline models DoseAI, OptAB and SCIP-Net. For ICG-Net, we used a NVIDIA H100 GPU with 96 GB of memory.

Training of ObsNODE for the optimal hyperparameter values on a single Nvidia A100 GPU took 140 min for the synthetic cancer data, 49 min for the semi-synthetic data and 65 min for the real world sepsis data.

This is similar to the training times required for the baseline models ICG-Net and SCIP-Net. Training OptAB and DoseAI required between 40 and 74 hours, depending on the data set.
