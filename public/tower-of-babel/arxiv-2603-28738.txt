1.
Introduction

For a simple undirected graph
G
on
n
vertices, let

\lambda_{1}(G)\geq\lambda_{2}(G)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}(G)

denote the eigenvalues of its adjacency matrix. Following Nikiforov
[
19
]
, define

c_{k}:=\sup\left\{\frac{\lambda_{k}(G)}{|V(G)|}:|V(G)|\geq k\right\}.

The problem of bounding
\lambda_{k}(G)
in terms of the order of the graph goes back at least to work of Hong and Powers
[
9
,
10
,
20
]
. Nikiforov
[
19
]
proved the universal estimate

c_{k}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{k-1}}\qquad(k\geq 2),

On the lower-bound side, the balanced complete
k
-partite graph gives
c_{k}\geq 1/k
, and Nikiforov showed that in fact
c_{k}>1/k
for every
k\geq 5

[
19
]
. He therefore asked whether
c_{3}=1/3
and
c_{4}=1/4

[
19
, Question 2.11]
. Powers had earlier proposed a conjecture that
\lambda_{k}(G)\leq\lfloor\frac{n}{k}\rfloor
for connected graphs, but Nikiforov later observed that the original proof is flawed; moreover, Nikiforov’s constructions show that this bound fails for every
k\geq 5
, while Linz later ruled out the case
k=4

[
20
,
19
,
15
]
. The remaining case
k=3
is also recorded in the survey of Liu and Ning
[
16
, Problem 10]
.

Recent work has clarified the first unresolved cases. Leonida and Li verified the sharp third-eigenvalue bound for several important graph classes and formulated a weighted matrix conjecture that would imply it in full generality
[
13
]
. See also Li
[
14
]
for a related asymptotic improvement on Nikiforov’s bound. Very recently, Tang proved the sharp inequality

\lambda_{3}(G)\leq\frac{n}{3}-1
for every graph
G
by confirming the conjecture of Leonida and Li
[
24
]
.

On the lower-bound side, it is easy to see that
c_{3}\geq 1/3
by taking the disjoint union of three equal sized cliques.
[
19
]
proved that
c_{k}>\frac{1}{2\sqrt{k-1}+1}
when
q
is an odd prime power and
k=q^{2}-q+1
.
[
19
]
also showed that
c_{k}>\frac{1}{2\sqrt{k-1}+k^{1/3}}
for sufficiently large
k
. Further, Linz provided several improved constructions for
4\leq k\leq 24

[
15
]
.

Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k\geq 2
be an integer. For every graph
G
on
n
vertices,

\lambda_{k}(G)\leq\alpha_{k}n-1,\qquad\alpha_{k}:=\frac{(k-2)\sqrt{k+1}+2}{2k(k-1)}

Consequently,
c_{k}\leq\alpha_{k}

For
k=2
, this simply follows from the classical sharp bound
\lambda_{2}(G)\leq n/2-1

[
9
,
19
]
. Our result holds for
k\geq 3
. For
k=3
, it recovers Tang’s sharp bound
\lambda_{3}(G)\leq n/3-1

[
24
]
. For
k\in\{4,8,24\}
,
\alpha_{k}
matches Linz’s lower bound
[
15
]
. Therefore, our upper bound is tight for
k\in\{2,3,4,8,24\}
. It is very interesting to note that our upper bound for
\lambda_{k}(G)
is tight whenever an extremal equiangular lines configuration exists in dimension
r=k-1
. We discuss this in further detail in
Section

5.1
.

For every
k\geq 2
one has

\alpha_{k}<\frac{1}{2\sqrt{k-1}}
, so
Theorem

1.1
improves Nikiforov’s universal bound for
\lambda_{k}(G)
. We remark that Nikiforov claimed that there exists a positive
\epsilon_{k}
such that
c_{k}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{k-1}}-\epsilon_{k}
. However, the proof was omitted because it relied on the Removal Lemma of Alon,
Fischer, Krivelevich, and Szegedy
[
1
]
with other tools of analytic graph theory. This would make the proof complicated and give a small
\epsilon_{k}
.

Our proof follows the overall strategy of Tang
[
24
]
. As in their work, the graph-theoretic theorem is deduced from a weighted matrix inequality using the identity

A(G)+A(\overline{G})=J-I

and Weyl’s inequality. The key new modification is the use of Gegenbauer polynomials to generalize the trigonometric upper bound from the rank-
2
case. The required positive-kernel input goes back to Schoenberg
[
21
]
; for related perspectives in spherical coding and semidefinite methods, see also Delsarte–Goethals–Seidel
[
6
]
and Bachoc–Vallentin
[
2
]
.

2.
Positive Kernels

Fix an integer
r\geq 2
. Let
S^{r-1}=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{r}:\|v\|_{2}=1\}
be the unit sphere. For real vectors
u,v
, let
\langle u,v\rangle
denote the standard inner product. For real valued square matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, write
\langle A,B\rangle_{F}=\tr(A^{T}B)
for the Frobenius inner product. This is equivalent to just taking taking the inner product by viewing
A
and
B
as
n^{2}
-dimensional vectors. For a matrix,
\|A\|_{F}=\sqrt{\langle A,A\rangle_{\!F}}
and
\|A\|_{1}=\sum_{i,j}|A_{ij}|
. Let
I_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
denote the identity matrix. A matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is positive semidefinite if
x^{\top}Mx\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is positive semidefinite on
S^{r-1}
if for all unit vectors
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\in S^{r-1}
, the matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
given by
M_{ij}=f(\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle)
is positive semidefinite.

Define

f^{r}_{2}(t)=t^{2}-\frac{1}{r}

f^{r}_{4}(t)=t^{4}-\frac{3}{r(r+2)}

We will prove that
f_{2}^{r}
and
f_{4}^{r}
as positive semidefinite on
S^{r-1}
. In
Section

3
, this will play a crucial role in bounding
\|Q\|_{1}
for a rank
r
orthogonal projection matrix
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
r\geq 2
, define

\phi(u):=uu^{\top}-\frac{1}{r}I_{r},\qquad u\in S^{r-1}.

Then for all
u,v\in S^{r-1}
,

\langle\phi(u),\phi(v)\rangle_{\!F}=f_{2}^{r}(\langle u,v\rangle).

Further,
f_{2}^{r}
is positive semidefinite on
S^{r-1}
.

Proof.

A direct computation gives

\displaystyle\langle\phi(u),\phi(v)\rangle_{\!F}

\displaystyle=\tr\!\left(\left(uu^{\top}-\frac{1}{r}I_{r}\right)\left(vv^{\top}-\frac{1}{r}I_{r}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=(u^{\top}v)^{2}-\frac{1}{r}=\langle u,v\rangle^{2}-\frac{1}{r}=f_{2}^{r}(\langle u,v\rangle).

Now observe that for any
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\in S^{r-1}
, the
n\times n
matrix
(f_{2}^{r}(\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle))_{i,j=1}^{n}
is just the Gram matrix of the matrices
\phi(u_{1}),\ldots,\phi(u_{n})
with the standard Frobenius inner product. Therefore, it must be positive semidefinite.
∎

To prove that
(f_{2}^{r}(\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle))_{i,j=1}^{n}
is PSD, we expressed it as a Gram matrix of certain matrices with the Frobenius inner product. We will do the same thing for
f_{4}^{r}
, but since
f_{4}^{r}
is a degree
4
polynomial, we will need to work with
4
-tensors. The proof of
Section

2
is elementary but requires some computation. We defer this proof to the appendix.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
r\geq 2
,

f_{4}^{r}(t)=t^{4}-\frac{3}{r(r+2)}

is positive semidefinite on
S^{r-1}
.

Remark 2.4
.

Section

2
and
Section

2
are just special cases of a theorem by Schoenberg
[
21
]
. Let
G_{\ell}^{\lambda}
denote the Gegenbauer polynomial of degree
\ell
. These polynomials can be defined in terms of their generating function
[
23
]
:

\frac{1}{(1-2tx+x^{2})^{\lambda}}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}G_{\ell}^{\lambda}(t)x^{\ell}

Schoenberg proved the following strong characterization:
f(t)
is a real continuous function such that the matrix
f(\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle)_{i,j=1}^{n}
is positive semidefinite for all subsets
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}\subseteq S^{r-1}
if and only if
f
is of the form
\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}a_{\ell}G_{\ell}^{r/2-1}
with
a_{\ell}\geq 0
.

In particular,
G_{2}^{r/2-1}
and
G_{4}^{r/2-1}
are positive semidefinite on
S^{r-1}
, and we are simply using
f_{2}^{r}=\frac{r-1}{r}G_{2}^{r/2-1}
and
f_{4}^{r}=\frac{(r-1)(r+1)}{(r+2)(r+4)}G_{4}^{r/2-1}+\frac{6}{r+4}f_{2}^{r}
. Since
f_{r}^{4}
and
f_{r}^{4}
are just positive linear combinations of Gegenbauer polynomials with
\lambda=r/2-1
, they must be positive semidefinite on
S^{r-1}
by
[
21
]
. However, we do not require this strong characterization.

3.
An
\ell_{1}
bound for rank-
r
orthogonal projections

Let
Q=(q_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a rank-
r
orthogonal projection, where
r\geq 2
. So
Q
satisfies
Q=Q^{2}=Q^{T}
. The goal of this section is to determine an upper bound on
\|Q\|_{1}=\sum_{i,j}|q_{ij}|
. It is easy to see that for a rank
r
orthogonal projection
Q
,
\|Q\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{n^{2}}\|Q\|_{F}=n\sqrt{r}
. In
[
17
]
, they prove that
\|Q\|_{1}\leq r+\sqrt{(n-1)r(n-r)}
. Our bound in
Theorem

3.1
is better precisely when
n\geq\binom{r+1}{2}
with equality at
n=\binom{r+1}{2}
. We require this improved bound because
r
will be small relative to
n
in our applications. We prove the following:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a rank-
r
orthogonal projection, where
r\geq 2
.

\|Q\|_{1}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\lvert q_{ij}\rvert\leq\beta_{r}n\qquad\text{ where }\quad\beta_{r}:=\frac{r+\sqrt{r+2}}{1+\sqrt{r+2}}

Proof.

Since
Q
is a rank
r
orthogonal projection, we may write
Q=BB^{\top}
such that
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
where the columns of
B
form an orthogonal set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Further, let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
be the rows of
B
. Write
x_{i}=c_{i}u_{i}
, where
c_{i}=\lVert x_{i}\rVert_{2}\geq 0
and
u_{i}\in S^{r-1}
whenever
c_{i}\neq 0
; if
c_{i}=0
, choose
u_{i}
arbitrarily in
S^{r-1}
. Since the columns of
B
are orthogonal,
B^{\top}B=I_{r}
. Writing out
B^{\top}B
in terms of the rows of
B
, we obtain

(1)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}^{2}u_{i}u_{i}^{\top}=I_{r}\qquad\text{and}\qquad q_{ij}=c_{i}c_{j}\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle

Set

C:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i},\qquad x:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\phi(u_{i}),\qquad X:=\lVert x\rVert_{F}.

To prove
Theorem

3.1
, we establish
Section

3
,
Section

3
and
Section

3
.

Lemma 3.2
.

With the notation above,

C^{2}+rX^{2}\leq rn.

Proof.

\displaystyle C^{2}+rX^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i}c_{j}+r\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i}c_{j}\langle\phi(u_{i}),\phi(u_{j})\rangle_{\!F}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i}c_{j}+r\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i}c_{j}\left(\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle^{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)\text{\qquad by \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{lem:psd2}}

\displaystyle=r\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i}c_{j}\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(c_{i}^{2}+c_{j}^{2})\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle^{2}\text{\qquad by Cauchy-Schwarz}

\displaystyle=r\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}^{2}\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle^{2}\text{\qquad by symmetry}

\displaystyle r\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}^{2}\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=r\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tr(u_{i}u_{i}^{\top}c_{j}^{2}u_{j}u_{j}^{\top})

\displaystyle=r\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tr\left(u_{i}u_{i}^{\top}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}^{2}u_{j}u_{j}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=r\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tr\left(u_{i}u_{i}^{\top}\right)\text{\qquad by \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:projection-model}}

\displaystyle=rn

∎

We will now bound
\|Q\|_{1}
by finding a polynomial
h(t)
that satisfies
|t|\leq h(t)
for all
t\in[-1,1]
and applying it entry wise to
|q_{ij}|=c_{i}c_{j}|\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle|
with
t=|\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle|
. We will consider an expression of the form

a+b\,f_{2}^{r}(t)-\gamma\,f_{4}^{r}(t).

This is because after substituting
t=\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle
and summing over
i,j
, the constant term produces
C^{2}
, the
f_{2}^{r}
-term produces
X^{2}
, while the
f_{4}^{r}
-term is nonnegative since
f_{4}^{r}
is positive semidefinite by
Section

2
. The following lemma formalizes this reduction.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
a,b,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy

\lvert t\rvert\leq a+b\,f_{2}^{r}(t)-\gamma\,f_{4}^{r}(t)\qquad\text{for all }t\in[-1,1],

with
\gamma\geq 0
and
b\leq ra
. Then every rank-
r
orthogonal projection
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
satisfies

\|Q\|_{1}\leq arn.

Proof.

Recall by (
1
) that we may write
Q=BB^{T}
where the rows of
B
are given by
c_{i}u_{i}
for
i\in[n]
and
\|Q\|_{1}=\sum_{i,j}|q_{ij}|=\sum_{i,j}c_{i}c_{j}|\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle|
. Applying the assumed scalar inequality with
t=\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle
, using the fact that
c_{i}\geq 0
and summing over all
i,j
, we obtain

\|Q\|_{1}=\sum_{i,j}|q_{j}|\leq a\sum_{i,j}c_{i}c_{j}+b\sum_{i,j}c_{i}c_{j}f_{2}^{r}(\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle)-\gamma\sum_{i,j}c_{i}c_{j}f_{4}^{r}(\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle).

By definition,

\sum_{i,j}c_{i}c_{j}=C^{2},\qquad\sum_{i,j}c_{i}c_{j}f_{2}^{r}(\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle)=\sum_{i,j}c_{i}c_{j}\langle\phi(u_{i}),\phi(u_{j})\rangle_{\!F}=X^{2}

Also, the positive semidefiniteness of
\bigl(f_{4}^{r}(\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle)\bigr)_{i,j=1}^{n}
implies that

\sum_{i,j}c_{i}c_{j}f_{4}^{r}(\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle)\geq 0.

Therefore

\|Q\|_{1}\leq aC^{2}+bX^{2}.

Since
b\leq ra
, we have

aC^{2}+bX^{2}\leq a\bigl(C^{2}+rX^{2}\bigr).

Now apply
Section

3
to get

\|Q\|_{1}\leq arn.

∎

We now exhibit a concrete choice of coefficients for which the hypotheses of

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
r\geq 2
, and set

s:=\sqrt{r+2}.

Define

a_{r}:=\frac{s^{2}+s-2}{r(s+1)},\qquad b_{r}:=\frac{s(s^{2}+2s+3)}{2(s+1)^{2}},\qquad\gamma_{r}:=\frac{s^{3}}{2(s+1)^{2}}.

Then for every
t\in[-1,1]
,

|t|\leq a_{r}+b_{r}f_{2}^{r}(t)-\gamma_{r}f_{4}^{r}(t).

Moreover,

b_{r}\leq ra_{r}.

Proof.

Recall that

f_{2}^{r}(t)=t^{2}-\frac{1}{r},\qquad f_{4}^{r}(t)=t^{4}-\frac{3}{r(r+2)}.

Since the left-hand side is even in
t
, it suffices to consider
t\in[0,1]
.
A direct expansion gives

a_{r}+b_{r}f_{2}^{r}(t)-\gamma_{r}f_{4}^{r}(t)-t=\frac{(1-t)(st-1)^{2}(st+s+2)}{2(s+1)^{2}}.

The right-hand side is nonnegative for
t\in[0,1]
, since

1-t\geq 0,\qquad(st-1)^{2}\geq 0,\qquad st+s+2>0.

Hence

|t|\leq a_{r}+b_{r}f_{2}^{r}(t)-\gamma_{r}f_{4}^{r}(t)\qquad\text{for all }t\in[-1,1].

Finally,

ra_{r}-b_{r}=\frac{s^{3}+2s^{2}-5s-4}{2(s+1)^{2}}=\frac{(s-2)(s+1)(s+3)+2}{2(s+1)^{2}}\geq 0,

and therefore
b_{r}\leq ra_{r}
.
∎

Completing the proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

Apply
Section

3
with

a=a_{r},\qquad b=b_{r},\qquad\gamma=\gamma_{r}.

By
Section

3
, the hypotheses of
Section

3
are satisfied. Therefore

\|Q\|_{1}\leq a_{r}rn=\frac{s^{2}+s-2}{s+1}n=\frac{r+\sqrt{r+2}}{1+\sqrt{r+2}}n=\beta_{r}n.

∎

4.
From projections to eigenvalues

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
A=(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be symmetric, with eigenvalues

\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\mu_{n}.

Assume that

0\leq a_{ij}\leq 1\quad(i\neq j),\qquad a_{ii}\geq 0\quad(1\leq i\leq n).

Let
r\geq 2
be an integer. Then

\mu_{n-r+1}+\cdots+\mu_{n}\geq-\frac{\beta_{r}}{2}\,n.

In particular,

\mu_{n-r+1}\geq-\frac{\beta_{r}}{2r}\,n.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{r}
denote the set of rank-
r
orthogonal projections in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. By the variation characterization of eigenvalues (Ky Fan’s principle), it is easy to see that

\lambda_{1}(M)+\cdots+\lambda_{r}(M)=\max_{B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}:B^{T}B=I_{r}}\tr(B^{T}MB)=\max_{B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}:B^{T}B=I_{r}}\tr(MBB^{T})=\max_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{r}}\tr(MQ)

We simply apply this to
M=-A
to obtain:

\mu_{n-r+1}+\cdots+\mu_{n}=\min_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{r}}\tr(AQ).

Fix
Q=(q_{ij})\in\mathcal{P}_{r}
. Since
Q
is positive semidefinite, we have
\mathbf{1}^{\top}Q\mathbf{1}\geq 0
. Further,
\tr(Q)=\tr(BB^{T})=\tr(B^{T}B)=r
. This is also evident because
Q
is a rank
r
orthogonal projection and so has
r
eigenvectors with eigenvalue
1
.

\sum_{i<j}q_{ij}=\frac{\mathbf{1}^{\top}Q\mathbf{1}-r}{2}\geq-\frac{r}{2}.

Also,

\sum_{i<j}\lvert q_{ij}\rvert=\frac{\|Q\|_{1}-r}{2}.

Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{i<j}\min(q_{ij},0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i<j}q_{ij}-\sum_{i<j}\lvert q_{ij}\rvert\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(-\frac{r}{2}-\frac{\|Q\|_{1}-r}{2}\right)=-\frac{\|Q\|_{1}}{4}\geq-\frac{\beta_{r}}{4}n,

where the last step uses
Theorem

3.1
.

Now
a_{ij}q_{ij}\geq\min(q_{ij},0)
for every
i<j
, because
0\leq a_{ij}\leq 1
, and
a_{ii}q_{ii}\geq 0
because both factors are nonnegative. Therefore

\displaystyle\tr(AQ)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}a_{ii}q_{ii}+2\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}q_{ij}

\displaystyle\geq 2\sum_{i<j}\min(q_{ij},0)\geq-\frac{\beta_{r}}{2}n.

Taking the minimum over
Q\in\mathcal{P}_{r}
proves the first claim.

Since
\mu_{n-r+1}\geq\mu_{n-r+2}\geq\cdots\geq\mu_{n}
, we have

r\,\mu_{n-r+1}\geq\mu_{n-r+1}+\cdots+\mu_{n},

so

\mu_{n-r+1}\geq-\frac{\beta_{r}}{2r}n.

∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

The case
k=2
is the classical sharp bound
\lambda_{2}(G)\leq n/2-1

[
9
,
19
]
. So assume
k\geq 3
, and set

r:=k-1.

Let
\overline{G}
be the complement of
G
. Then

A(G)+A(\overline{G})=J-I.

The eigenvalues of
J-I
are
n-1,-1,\dots,-1
, so in particular

\lambda_{2}(J-I)=-1.

We use Weyl’s inequality in the form

\lambda_{i}(X)+\lambda_{j}(Y)\leq\lambda_{i+j-n}(X+Y)\qquad\text{whenever }i+j\geq n+1.

Apply this with

X=A(G),\qquad Y=A(\overline{G}),\qquad i=k,\qquad j=n-k+2.

Since
i+j=n+2
, we obtain

\lambda_{k}(G)+\lambda_{n-k+2}(\overline{G})\leq\lambda_{2}(J-I)=-1.

Now apply
Theorem

4.1
with
r=k-1
to
A(\overline{G})
to get

\lambda_{n-k+2}(\overline{G})\geq-\frac{\beta_{k-1}}{2(k-1)}n.

Since

\frac{\beta_{k-1}}{2(k-1)}=\frac{k-1+\sqrt{k+1}}{2(k-1)(\sqrt{k+1}+1)}=\frac{(k-2)\sqrt{k+1}+2}{2k(k-1)}=\alpha_{k},

it follows that

\lambda_{n-k+2}(\overline{G})\geq-\alpha_{k}n

Therefore

\lambda_{k}(G)\leq\alpha_{k}n-1

which is the desired bound.
∎

5.
Concluding Remarks

5.1.
Tight Lower Bounds from Equiangular Lines

A set of lines in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
passing through the origin is said to be equiangular if every pair of distinct lines determine the same angle. Let
N
be the maximum number of equiangular lines in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
. Gerzon proved that
N\leq\binom{r+1}{2}
(see
[
12
]
). We call a set of equiangular lines extremal if
N=\binom{r+1}{2}
. We remark that the argument below is not new, we simply apply it to our problem.

Fix
r\geq 2
. Suppose there exists a system of
N=\binom{r+1}{2}
equiangular lines in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
. Let
k=r+1
. Then there exist a graph on
n=2N
vertices such that
\alpha_{k}(G)=\alpha_{k}n-1
.

Let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{N}
be the representative unit vectors of the equiangular lines. Then
\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle\in\{-\alpha,\alpha\}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
[
12
]
proved that if
N=\binom{r+1}{2}
, then
\alpha=\frac{1}{\sqrt{r+2}}
. Using this, we can construct a graph
G
that satisfies
\lambda_{k}(G)=\alpha_{k}n-1
. This construction goes back to Seidel’s theory of regular two-graphs
[
22
]
.

Let
V(G)=\{(i,+),(i,-):i\in[N]\}
. So
n=|V(G)|=2n
. For
i\neq j
, if
\langle x_{i},x_{j}\rangle=\alpha
, put edges between
(i,+)
and
(j,+)
and between
(i,-)
and
(j,-)
. For
i\neq j
, if
\langle x_{i},x_{j}\rangle=-\alpha
, then put edges between
(i,+)
and
(j,-)
and between
(i,-)
and
(j,+)
. The spectrum of such a graph is well understood (see
[
8
]
) and in particular, the
r+1=k\text{th}
eigenvalue is
\frac{N-r}{\alpha r}
. Substituting
n=2N
,
\alpha=\frac{1}{\sqrt{r+2}}
and
r=k-1
yields
\alpha_{k}n-1
.

So our upper bound is tight for every
k
when an extremal equiangular lines construction exists for
r=k-1
. However, such a construction is only known to exist for
r\in\{1,2,3,7,23\}
. Therefore, we only have tight results for
k\in\{1,2,3,4,8,24\}
. This is also reminiscent of the sphere packing problem where tight results are known for
d\in\{1,2,3,8,24\}
. This is not a coincidence: it is known that the
E_{8}
lattice is optimal for
d=8
[
25
]
and the Leech lattice is optimal for
d=24
[
5
]
. The extremal equiangular lines constructions for
r=7
and
23
are also derived from these lattices
[
7
]
.

However, there may be graphs that match our upper bound for
\lambda_{k}(G)
that do not arise from extremal equiangular constructions. It is also possible that our bound can be improved when an extremal equiangular construction does not exist.

5.2.
Multiplicity of Second Eigenvalue

For a graph
G
, let
m
be the multiplicity of
\lambda_{2}(G)
. Therefore,
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{m+1}\leq\alpha_{m+1}n-1
.
Equivalently,

m\leq\max\bigl\{i\geq 1:\ \lambda_{2}(G)+1\leq\alpha_{i+1}n\bigr\}.

So our result gives an implicit upper bound on the second-eigenvalue multiplicity in terms of
\lambda_{2}(G)
and
n
. By the same graph construction as in
Section

5.1
, this is sharp whenever
m+1\in\{2,3,4,8,24\}
. Since
\frac{1}{\alpha_{m+1}}=\Theta(\sqrt{m})
, we obtain
m\leq O((\frac{n}{\lambda_{2}+1})^{2})
. The asymptotic result already follows from
[
19
]
but we actually get sharper bounds.

[
3
]
show that
m
is bounded by roughly
\frac{n}{\lambda_{2}+1}
for graphs with maximum degree
\Delta
where
\Delta^{O(1)}\leq\log n
in order to obtain an exact bound on the number of equiangular lines in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
with a fixed angle
\arccos{1/(2k-1)}
for
r
at least exponential in a polynomial of
k
, improving on the breakthrough result in
[
11
]
. The bound in
[
3
]
focuses on the sparse graph regime and gets progressively weaker as
\Delta
becomes polynomial while our bound remains meaningful even for much denser graphs.

It would be interesting to obtain bounds on
\lambda_{k}(G)
for graphs that are sparse or have bounded degree. Here is a simple bound. If
G
has average degree
d
and number of edges
e=nd/2
, then
\tr(A(G)^{2})=2e=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}
. So
k\lambda_{k}^{2}\leq 2e=nd
, so
\lambda_{k}\leq\sqrt{nd/k}
. Obtaining better bounds would be interesting for various regimes of
d
.

5.3.
Improving the Upper Bound

Extremal constructions for the equiangular lines problem with
N=\binom{r+1}{2}
can only exist when
r=2,3
or
r+2
is an odd perfect square
[
12
]
. Even when
r+2
is an odd perfect square, the question of existence remains wide open. (We do know that such constructions do not exist for infinitely many
r
with
r+2
an odd perfect square
[
18
]
[
4
]
). Therefore, obtaining a general improvement on our upper bound even by an additive constant for general
k=r+1
is a difficult problem because it would immediately disprove the existence of extremal equiangular lines constructions in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
. Improvements for
r
where
r+2
is not an odd perfect square are likely more feasible. It would be interesting to study the very concrete problem of finding a tight upper bound for
\lambda_{5}(G)
to understand the landscape better.

5.4.
Motivation for Choice of Polynomial

In
Section

3
, we we find coefficients
a_{r},b_{r},\gamma_{r}
such that
H(t)=a_{r}+b_{r}f_{2}^{r}(t)-\gamma_{r}f_{4}^{r}(t)-t\geq 0
for
t\in[-1,1]
with
ra_{r}
as small as possible. It is reasonable to try and find
H
such that
H(1)=0
because if our bound is not tight at the endpoint, we could potentially improve the bound further. Next, for the extremal equiangular constructions with
N=\binom{r+1}{2}
, the common angle is
\alpha=\frac{1}{\sqrt{r+2}}=\frac{1}{s}
. Since we apply the polynomial to bound terms of the form
|t|=|\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle|
which is the common angle between two unit vectors, a reasonable heuristic is to find
H
such that
H(1/s)=0
. Since
1/s
is in the interior of
[0,1]
, we also set
H^{\prime}(1/s)=0
. This turns out to uniquely define
H
.
