Mental health in the Sámi population and the importance of cultural affiliation: cross-sectional analyses at two time points in mid and Northern Norway
Authors: Lars Helander, Anne Silviken, Trond Heir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMental health disparities have been reported among Indigenous populations worldwide. It remains unclear how different dimensions of Sámi affiliation, such as heritage, identity, and language are related to mental health outcomes. We aimed to examine mental health in the Sámi population according to different forms of Sámi affiliation (heritage, identity, and language affiliation). Furthermore, we examined changes in mental health from 2004 to 2012. Data were derived from two cross-sectional population-based studies conducted in 2004 and 2012. Both surveys included information on Sámi heritage, Sámi identity, linguistic background, and mental health assessed by the Hopkins Symptom Checklist-10 (HSCL-10). Slightly higher levels of psychological distress were observed in most Sámi groups compared to the non-Sámi population, with the exception of Sámi-speaking women. Differences over time were generally small, although men with Sámi heritage showed a modest increase in distress. In adjusted analyses, Sámi affiliation remained associated with psychological distress after controlling for age, education, and household income. Psychological distress was slightly higher in the Sámi population compared to the non-Sámi population, although differences were modest. Sámi affiliation appears to be associated with psychological distress beyond socioeconomic factors, potentially reflecting a complex interplay of social, cultural, and historical influences.Keywords: Mental health, assimilation, Indigenous peoples, Sámi

Introduction

High rates of mental health problems have been reported among Indigenous peoples worldwide, including Native Americans in the United States, Aboriginal Australians, and Indigenous populations in Greenland, Canada, and Alaska [1–4]. Concern has been raised regarding young men in Arctic regions and their vulnerability to mental health problems and suicide [5]. A report from the United Nations Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues has highlighted serious concerns regarding Indigenous peoples' mental health and emphasised the need for further research into both the causes and trajectories of mental health problems in these populations [6].

The Sámi are an Indigenous people with their own languages and culture who have inhabited the mid and northern regions of Fennoscandia and the Kola Peninsula for at least 2,000 years [7]. Estimates suggest that approximately 35,000–40,000 Sámi live in Norway, 17,000–20,000 in Sweden, 5,000–7,500 in Finland, and 1,500–2,000 in Russia [8].

Like many Indigenous peoples, the Sámi in Norway have experienced extensive assimilation policies, including language suppression and institutional practices such as boarding schools, which marginalised Sámi language, culture and identity. Similar processes have been documented internationally and have been linked to long-term adverse mental health outcomes through mechanisms such as loss of cultural continuity and intergenerational stress [9,10]. These processes suggest the importance of examining gradations of cultural affiliation, such as language use and identity, rather than treating Indigenous populations as homogeneous.

In parallel, during the past three decades, there has been a gradual revitalisation of Sámi language and culture, marked by the establishment of the Sámi Parliament, Sámi University of Applied Sciences, and the Sámi division of the Norwegian National Broadcasting Corporation [11].

Nevertheless, Sámi affiliation remains heterogeneous, ranging from individuals with Sámi heritage who do not identify as Sámi, to those who identify as Sámi but lack language affiliation, and to those with strong connections to Sámi language and culture [12]. These forms of affiliation may reflect different constellations of cultural continuity, social belonging, minority stress, and integration into the majority population, with both protective and adverse implications for mental health. Sámi language use may indicate stronger cultural continuity and access to culturally embedded forms of communication and social support but may also reflect greater exposure to minority positioning. Sámi identity, regardless of language use, may be associated with a sense of belonging and cultural pride, but also with experiences of stigmatisation or discrimination. In contrast, individuals with Sámi heritage who do not report Sámi identity or language use may represent weaker or less expressed forms of Sámi affiliation, potentially reflecting assimilation processes, but also greater integration into the majority population, which may reduce exposure to minority stress and provide access to socioeconomic resources. Minority stress theory suggests that individuals belonging to marginalised groups are exposed to chronic stressors such as discrimination and stigma, which may negatively affect mental health [13,14]. In a Sámi context, both historical assimilation and ongoing marginalisation may contribute to such stress. At the same time, perspectives on cultural continuity emphasise that cultural identity, language, and community belonging may promote resilience and well-being [4,15]. The balance between these mechanisms is likely context-dependent, with Sámi affiliation potentially functioning as both a protective and a risk factor.

Using data from two large population-based surveys, we examined mental health among Sámi subpopulations defined by Sámi heritage, identity, and language affiliation, compared with the non-Sámi population, representing the majority population residing in the same geographical areas. We also examined changes in mental health between 2004 and 2012.

Methods

Study design and participants

Data were obtained from two population-based health and living condition surveys conducted among the adult Sámi and non-Sámi general population in multicultural areas of mid and northern Norway. The first survey, SAMINOR 1, was conducted in 2003–2004 [16] and the second, SAMINOR 2, in 2012–2014 [17]. Both surveys employed cross-sectional designs. SAMINOR 1 invited 27,987 individuals, of whom 16,865 participated (response rate 60.3%). The final sample included 8,110 men and 8,755 women aged 30–79 years. SAMINOR 2 invited 44,669 individuals, with 11,612 participants (response rate 26.9%), comprising 5,149 men and 6,451 women aged 20–69 years. Measures

In both surveys, a self-administered questionnaire was sent by post to participants' home addresses. The questionnaires included items on demographic characteristics, indicators of ethnicity, and measures of mental health. The questionnaires are publicly available at: https://uit.no/forskning/forskningsgrupper/gruppe?p_document_id=425187.

Mental health was assessed as psychological distress using the 10-item Hopkins Symptom Checklist (HSCL-10), a validated instrument [18]. Participants were asked to report how much they had been bothered by each symptom during the past week in SAMINOR 1 and during the past four weeks in SAMINOR 2. Responses were given on a four-point Likert scale ranging from not at all (1) to very much (4). A mean HSCL-10 score was calculated by summing the item scores and dividing by the number of completed items, with higher scores indicating higher levels of psychological distress. The SAMINOR 1 and SAMINOR 2 surveys were conducted as independent cross-sectional studies. Due to anonymization, it was not possible to identify individuals who may have participated in both surveys. Sámi affiliation was defined independently within each survey based on responses collected at that time point.

Affiliation to Sámi ethnicity and identity was categorised based on three criteria assessed through questions on linguistic background and self-perceived identity: (1) language spoken at home (currently or previously), (2) language spoken at home by parents and grandparents, and (3) self-identification. Response options included Norwegian, Sámi, Kven (a national minority group in Norway), and other; multiple responses were permitted. A hierarchical classification procedure was applied. Classification was based on available information from the three criteria, and participants were assigned to one of four mutually exclusive categories if sufficient information was present, even if one or more items were missing. In cases where individuals met criteria for multiple categories, classification was determined according to the following priority order: Sámi-speaking > Sámi identity > Sámi heritage > non-Sámi. The Sámi-speaking category required both self-identification as Sámi and reported Sámi language use. Participants fulfilling both criteria were classified as Sámi-speaking regardless of other responses. Participants reporting Sámi identity but not language use were classified as Sámi identity. The Sámi heritage category was restricted to participants reporting Sámi family background only, without self-identification as Sámi or Sámi language use. Participants not reporting Sámi self-identification, Sámi language use, or Sámi family background were classified as non-Sámi. This approach allowed classification based on partial information while minimising unnecessary exclusions. Participants who did not respond to any of the ethnicity-related questions, or for whom available information was insufficient to allow classification into one of the predefined categories, were excluded from the analyses (n = 752). In addition, respondents who reported speaking Sámi but did not consider themselves Sámi were excluded from the analyses (n = 566), as their classification within the predefined categories was ambiguous and could introduce misclassification. These individuals did not fit any of the mutually exclusive categories defined by the classification framework and were therefore excluded. This approach prioritised internal consistency but may have limited the ability to capture more complex identity configurations.

The final distribution was as follows: non-Sámi (n = 18,125), Sámi heritage (n = 2,676), Sámi identity (n = 2,886), and Sámi-speaking (n = 4,288).

Information on gender and year of birth was obtained from the Norwegian National Population Register and linked to the SAMINOR datasets by Statistics Norway. Age was defined as the age attained by the end of the year of participation and categorised into three groups: 18–29 years, 30–59 years, and 60–79 years.

Educational level was categorised according to years of completed education within the Norwegian educational system: primary education (0–9 years), secondary education (10–12 years), and higher education (13 years or more).

Household income was categorised as low income (<150,000 NOK), medium–low income (150,000–450,000 NOK), medium–high income (451,000–600,000 NOK), and high income (>600,000 NOK).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means with 95% confidence intervals. Independent t-tests were used to compare mean levels of psychological distress between groups. Linear regression analyses were conducted with HSCL-10 mean score as the outcome variable, including Sámi affiliation and study year (2004 vs 2012) as independent variables, with adjustment for age, education, and household income. All regression analyses were conducted separately for men and women. All tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Interaction between Sámi ethnicity and socioeconomic variables (education and income) was tested by including product terms in the regression models. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA).

Socioeconomic factors were also strongly associated with psychological distress in both sexes, with higher levels observed among individuals with lower income and education (all p < 0.001). The models explained a modest proportion of the variance in HSCL-10 scores (R² ≈ 0.04).

To assess whether group differences changed over time, interaction terms between Sámi affiliation and study year were included (Table 6). No statistically significant interaction between Sámi affiliation and study year was observed in the total sample (F(3, 19602) = 2.16, p = 0.091), although the result approached statistical significance. Discussion

Using repeated cross-sectional data from two time points, the present study shows slightly higher levels of psychological distress in the Sámi population compared with the non-Sámi population residing in the same geographical areas of Norway. Adjustment for age, education, and income attenuated but did not eliminate the observed differences between Sámi and non-Sámi groups. These differences were generally modest for both men and women. This is also reflected in small absolute differences in mean HSCL-10 scores between groups. When Sámi affiliation was categorised by identity and language affiliation, psychological distress was generally higher than in the majority population across Sámi affiliation groups, with the exception of Sámi-speaking women, whose levels were comparable to or slightly lower than those observed in the non-Sámi population. Changes in psychological distress from 2004 to 2012 were generally modest in both the Sámi and non-Sámi populations, and formal interaction analyses indicated that differences between groups were largely stable over time. However, as the analyses are based on repeated cross-sectional data, these findings reflect group-level patterns rather than within-individual changes. The most pronounced increase was observed among men with Sámi heritage, which may reflect challenges related to weaker cultural affiliation or identity ambiguity. No significant interactions were observed between Sámi affiliation and socioeconomic variables, suggesting that factors other than socioeconomic position, such as cultural, historical, or contextual influences, may also be important in understanding the observed differences. The findings should be interpreted in light of potential compositional differences, including variations in age and socioeconomic position. Such factors are well known to influence mental health outcomes and are likely to contribute to the patterns observed in this study. However, as Sámi affiliation remained associated with psychological distress after adjustment, and no significant interaction with socioeconomic variables was observed, these factors do not appear to fully account for the differences. These results are consistent with research on Indigenous and minority populations showing that mental health disparities are shaped not only by socioeconomic conditions but also by historical and ongoing structural factors [1–3]. In the Sámi context, processes of assimilation, loss of language and cultural practices, and experiences of marginalisation and discrimination may have long-term consequences for mental health [4,5]. This perspective aligns with minority stress theory, which posits that exposure to chronic social stressors related to minority status can adversely affect mental health [6,7]. At the same time, a growing body of research highlights that cultural identity, language, and community belonging may also function as protective factors that promote resilience and well-being [8–10]. Taken together, the observed differences likely reflect a complex interplay between socioeconomic conditions and minority stress processes, alongside potential protective aspects of Sámi identity and cultural affiliation. At the same time, the results point to a more complex pattern in which Sámi affiliation, particularly language and identity, may reflect access to protective cultural resources. In line with perspectives on cultural continuity, connections to Sámi language and identity may support resilience, belonging, and meaning-making, and could potentially relate to lower levels of psychological distress.

These findings are consistent with the possibility that Sámi affiliation operates through both risk and protective mechanisms. This dual perspective may contribute to understanding why Sámi identity and language are not uniformly associated with poorer mental health but instead show nuanced and context-dependent associations.

The finding of elevated psychological distress among Sámi participants is consistent with previous studies of Sámi youth and adults in Norway [20] and among Sámi residing on the Swedish side of Sápmi [21,22]. Previous research has documented a higher prevalence of experienced ethnic discrimination [23] and exposure to violence [24] among Sámi individuals compared with the majority population.

The relatively small differences in psychological distress between Sámi affiliation groups defined by identity and linguistic affiliation warrant further reflection. On the one hand, cultural affiliation and a strong indigenous identity may represent a source of resilience at the individual, family, and community levels, particularly when such identity is socially recognised and valued [25,26]. On the other hand, visible cultural and linguistic differences may also increase exposure to discrimination, stigma, and bullying, especially in contexts dominated by the majority culture [27]. In large population-based studies, these opposing forces may partly offset each other, resulting in modest overall differences between Sámi affiliation groups. Against this background, it is notable that the lowest levels of psychological distress were observed among Sámi-speaking women. Within this group, gender differences in psychological distress were smaller than those observed in other Sámi affiliation groups and in the non-Sámi population. One possible explanation relates to the relatively strong social and cultural position of Sámi women. Sámi women have, on average, higher educational attainment than Sámi men and are strongly represented politically, with women constituting more than half of the representatives in the Sámi Parliament [28]. This position is also rooted in traditional Sámi social structures [29]. For example, among Skolt Sámi communities, women traditionally retained ownership of goods they produced, including clothing and food. In several Sámi reindeer-herding communities, women have traditionally played a central role in managing household finances, and this responsibility remains significant in many contemporary Sámi households. In contexts where recognition and social status are closely tied to cultural values that are allowed to persist, strong cultural affiliation may contribute to the maintenance of good mental health [3,26]. This pattern may reflect mechanisms related to cultural continuity, where cultural identity and social roles support resilience and well-being.

Although overall differences in psychological distress between Sámi and non-Sámi populations were small, particular attention should be given to men with Sámi heritage, among whom an increase in psychological distress was observed. The observed increase in psychological distress among men with Sámi heritage may reflect challenges associated with navigating social and cultural change. Individuals lacking a stable cultural or social frame of reference may be particularly vulnerable during periods of rapid societal transformation [3]. A coherent cultural and social identity may function as a protective factor in such contexts, supporting self-esteem and psychological stability [25]. Conversely, suppression or loss of original cultural affiliation may contribute to experiences of shame, rootlessness, and stigmatisation [8]. Follow-up data from the ongoing SAMINOR 3 study may provide valuable insights into these processes.

Strengths and limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. Differences in age composition between the two survey waves may have influenced comparisons over time. Although the age ranges differed between surveys, age distributions shifted in a similar manner across Sámi and non-Sámi groups, supporting the validity of comparisons between ethnic groups. A key limitation of the study is that the two surveys could not be linked at the individual level. Although some individuals may have participated in both SAMINOR 1 and SAMINOR 2, this could not be identified. This reflects the independent cross-sectional design, which does not allow examination of within-individual changes over time. Ethnic self-identification and language affiliation may vary across the life course and contexts, and this potential dynamism could not be captured in the present design. In addition, the hierarchical classification of Sámi affiliation into mutually exclusive categories may have simplified complex and potentially overlapping forms of ethnic affiliation and may not fully capture the fluid and context-dependent nature of identity. The exclusion of participants who reported Sámi language use without Sámi self-identification may have introduced bias, as this group may include individuals with complex, situational, or transitional identity configurations. This group represents a potentially important but methodologically challenging category that was not captured within the current classification framework. Participation rates differed substantially between the two surveys, with a high response rate in the first wave and a markedly lower rate in the second. This reflects a general decline in survey participation in the Norwegian population, particularly among younger men [30]. Importantly, the proportion of participants with a Sámi background was approximately similar in SAMINOR 1 (37%) and SAMINOR 2 (34%), suggesting that both surveys captured comparable population groups. Previous analyses have also indicated that respondents were broadly representative of the population in northern Norway regarding education and household income [31]. Although we adjusted for age, education, and household income in regression analyses, residual confounding by unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. Nevertheless, selection bias cannot be ruled out, and residual confounding by unmeasured social or health-related factors may have influenced the observed associations. Key strengths of the study include the use of large probability samples drawn from areas recognised as Sámi core regions and the inclusion of direct measures of Sámi affiliation based on family lineage, language, and self-identified ethnicity. This approach contrasts with earlier studies relying solely on geographic criteria for Sámi belonging [12]. In addition, the SAMINOR instruments have been evaluated for cultural validity in mental health research, with favourable results [32,33]. To our knowledge, the SAMINOR studies remain the only large-scale epidemiological surveys of mental health among the adult Sámi population in Norway.

Implications

The differences in psychological distress observed in this study are generally small and are unlikely, in themselves, to warrant specific clinical or preventive interventions targeted at Sámi subgroups. Overall, the mental health status of the Sámi population appears broadly comparable to that of the majority population, although some differences were observed.

The relatively favourable mental health observed among Sámi-speaking women may suggest that cultural affiliation and language continuity are important resources for well-being. However, given the independent cross-sectional design, these associations should not be interpreted as causal. The results should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that language use or ethnic identification alone are protective factors; rather, the observed pattern likely reflects broader social and cultural contexts in which cultural continuity, social roles, and community recognition have been suggested to support psychological well-being, and the associations observed here should therefore be interpreted as modest and non-causal. Despite generally small differences between Sámi and non-Sámi populations, the results indicate meaningful variation within the Sámi population, where cultural affiliation and language continuity may represent potential social resources relevant for mental health. Continued monitoring of mental health trajectories, particularly among men with Sámi heritage who do not identify as Sámi, is therefore warranted. Future data from SAMINOR 3 may provide important opportunities to deepen the understanding of how gender, cultural affiliation, and social change interact to influence mental health in the Sámi population. These findings provide a useful baseline for understanding variation in mental health within the Sámi population and for interpreting future developments over time.
