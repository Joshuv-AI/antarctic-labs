Determination of worst-case cephalosporin residues in laboratory glassware using a validated low-level RP-HPLC method
Authors: Saja A. Althobaiti, Fahad M. Alminderej, Hossam F. Nassar, Sayed M. Saleh, Mahmoud A. Mohamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cephalosporin antibiotics are highly allergenic and present serious cross contamination problems in analytical and manufacturing environments. Regulatory agencies require quality control laboratories to establish strict zero-tolerance or health-based carryover exposure limits, making verification of equipment and glassware cleanliness extremely important. The aim of this study was to assess the efficiency of cleaning laboratory glassware procedures by identifying the level of residual contamination of a worst-case cephalosporin antibiotic, cefixime. A validated reverse-phase liquid chromatographic technique was used to measure chemical residues of cefixime, with the remaining detergent contamination measured by conductivity of final rinse water. Cefixime and its related impurities were separated on a C18 reverse-phase column using an environmentally friendly isocratic mobile phase of acetate buffer with a pH of 6.5 and ethanol in an 89:11 (v/v) ratio, and UV was used to detect cefixime at 254 nm. The technique was sensitive, selective, and linear at low levels appropriate in the analysis of residues on glass surfaces. Recovery experiments verified the capability of the approach to find cefixime residues that were left behind after normal cleaning activities. Conductivity measurements were used to demonstrate the effective removal of detergent residue, that could not be adequately detected at 254 nm. The integrated analytical method provides a scientifically grounded and regulatory-applicable approach to holistic cleaning validation of laboratory glassware, which facilitates the control of contamination in pharmaceutical analytical facilities without violating the postulates of green analytical chemistry.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-61191-5.

Introduction

Cleaning validation is an essential part of pharmaceutical quality systems, and it is aimed to guarantee that active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs), excipients, and cleaning agents (and other contaminants) are completely removed from equipment and contact surfaces prior to further use. Cleaning methods should be validated as per International Council for Harmonization (ICH) Q2(R2) guidelines to ensure the systematic reliability of cleaning methods. Cefixime, a member of the cephalosporin class, was intentionally chosen as the target molecule for this study, representing a ‘worst-case scenario.’ The choice is justified not only by the clinical potency and allergenic profile but essentially by the chemical surface-adherence properties. Cefixime has a number of ionizable functional groups, including carboxylic acid and primary amine groups that exhibit a strong affinity for the reactive silanol (Si-OH) groups of unmodified glass surfaces. Dried cefixime residues form a tight, cohesive film under neutral or slightly acidic conditions driven by strong hydrogen-bonding networks, highly resistant to physical rinsing alone, and an optimal challenge matrix for cleaning validation1.

Laboratory glassware (such as volumetric flasks, pipettes, and dissolution vessels) is routinely reused in a pharmaceutical manufacturing facility for testing raw materials, in-process controls, and finished commercial batches. The use of a contaminated flask with residual trace -levels of an allergen (cefixime) to prepare or analyze an entirely different product like pediatric suspension (cefuroxime) can lead to false analytical results or transfer trace contaminants to standard analytical reference checks.

More importantly, cross-contamination in pilot-scale preparation areas or during in-process sampling may lead to unintended carryover into the final product, compromising product quality and patient safety, while inappropriate antibiotic handling and misuse contribute to the broader challenge of antimicrobial resistance2,3. Although cleaning validation has been widely covered in relation to manufacturing equipment, the glassware in laboratories in terms of quality control and analytical laboratories has been rather poorly covered, although it is often reused and has a direct influence on the data integrity4–7. Cephalosporin antibiotics are β-lactams and are regarded as a high-risk substance because they have been well-documented to cause hypersensitivity and allergic reactions even at a low level of exposure8,9. Regulatory bodies across the globe are aware of the potential for cross-contamination of β-lactams and the impact this will have on testing results, and as such, there should be stringent cleaning and residue monitoring plans. Therefore, cephalosporins are often not included in any common manufacturing plant or have very restrictive acceptance limits depending on health-based exposure limits (HBELs) such as permitted daily exposure (PDE) values10–13. Pharmaceutical laboratories routinely test for cefixime (Fig. 1), a third-generation cephalosporin, in raw materials and finished products. Its moderate aqueous solubility, chemical stability issues, and allergenicity predispose it to be a scientifically reasonable worst-case choice in cleaning validation studies. Any cefixime left on laboratory glassware can be problematic in two ways: (1) cross contamination can lead to analytically spurious results, and (2) it may lead to the downstream contamination of products14. Nevertheless, systematic investigations on low-level detection of cephalosporin-based residues on laboratory glass surfaces are still limited in scientific literature despite these risks. The current regulatory directions of the European Medicines Agency (EMA), World Health Organization (WHO), and Pharmaceutical Inspection Co-operation Scheme (PIC/S) focus on the application of toxicologically reasonable acceptance limits and sensitive analytical tools of cleaning validation. These recommendations make it clear that worst-case products should be identified and validated analytical procedures should be applied that can measure residues up to or below HBEL-based limits15,16. Nevertheless, certain analytical models that can be applied to laboratory glassware, particularly that of allergenic antibiotics, are not clearly outlined, thus leaving a gap in the quality control laboratory. The analytical method most used in the determination of pharmaceutical residues at the low-level is liquid chromatography because of its selectivity, sensitivity, and acceptance by regulation bodies. The recent analytical studies have given rise to an increased use of environmentally benign mobile phases to limit the solvent toxicity and waste production without affecting analytical quality. Mobile phases based on ethanol with volatile or mild buffers have been explored as being a sustainable alternative to traditional acetonitrile-based mobile phase in line with the tenets of green analytical chemistry17–22.

The literature was thoroughly reviewed, and it was found that most of the existing chromatographic methods that have been used in cleaning validation of beta-lactams used toxic organic solvents, particularly acetonitrile, to achieve the desired separation23–26. For instance, Akl et al.23 developed and validated a successful surface residue protocol for ceftriaxone, albeit with a complex quaternary mobile phase and high column temperature (50 °C). Contamination monitoring to the ultra- low-level in manufacturing environments has been accomplished by using very sophisticated hyphenated techniques to achieve a picogram-level sensitivity for a few specific contaminants, including cefmetazole and cefpodoxime proxetil24. Mass spectrometry, however, is not as well suited for high throughput, routine equipment clearance because of its high operating costs, maintenance downtime, and technical complexity. Previous successful attempts were able to separate several cephalosporin residues (including cephalexin, cefaclor, and cefixime) from manufacturing equipment and detergents using ion-pair reverse phase - high performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) systems25. However, these historical methods were heavily dependent on mobile phases with dangerous organic modifiers such as acetonitrile in combination with specialized ion-pairing agents. Likewise, multi-analyte separation sweeps often require long gradient run times up to 50 min and expensive ion-pairing reagents26.

In addition to API residues, cleaning chemicals and lab detergents cause non-visible contamination. Detergent residues can interfere with analysis, allow analyte adsorption, and reduce glassware wettability. Ionic detergent residues can be detected by measuring final rinse water conductivity, a simple, sensitive, and cost-effective method utilized as an auxiliary indicator of cleaning performance in controlled laboratories. Thus, the research created and implemented a thorough approach for analyzing laboratory glassware cleaning efficiency to determine cefixime levels as a worst-case cephalosporin model. The chemical residues of cefixime were assessed using a proven reversed-phase liquid chromatographic method and an environmentally friendly mobile phase of acetate buffer (pH 6.5) and ethanol. Conductivity was used to assess detergent contamination. A scientific, regulatory-compliant, and sustainable technique for cleaning validation of laboratory glassware in pharmaceutical quality control is provided.

Experimental

Chemicals and reagents

A Cefixime working standard of suitable purity of 98.4% was used for the preparation of standard and spiking solutions. Acetic acid (analytical grade) and sodium acetate (analytical grade) were used for preparation of the acetate buffer. The organic modifier used in the mobile phase was Ethanol of HPLC grade. The aqueous buffer and all rinse solutions were prepared using purified water conforming to USP compendial standards. The reagents and solvents were of analytical grade or chromatography grade so that they could be used to analyze traces of residues at the low-level. This cleaning was done with the help of the laboratory detergent (1.5% ADD-Pharma 72), which is actively used in the laboratory when cleaning glassware to provide realistic and representative conditions in the quality control laboratory.

Instruments

The liquid chromatographic analysis was done using an Agilent HPLC system where a quaternary pump, autosampler, column oven, and PDA detector were configured. Empower software was used to gather data, control instruments, and make chromatographic outcome assessments.

Conductivity measurements on a Mettler Toledo calibrated digital conductivity meter using automatic temperature compensation were used to assess the detergent residue. The routine laboratory apparatuses like an analytical balance, ultrasonic bath, pH meter, and filtration assemblies were employed during the preparation of the solutions and manipulation of the samples. All the instruments were also qualified and calibrated according to the requirements of the internal quality system.

Mobile phase preparation

The aqueous component of the mobile phase was prepared by dissolving 1.64 mg/mL of sodium acetate in purified water to yield a 20 mM buffer solution. The pH of the buffer solution was set to 6.5 ± 0.05 with either dilute acetic acid solution or sodium hydroxide solution. The mobile phase was prepared by adding a mixture of acetate buffer (pH 6.5) and ethanol in the proportion of 89:11 (v/v). The mixture was then filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE membrane to remove particulate matter and then degassed.

Chromatographic conditions

Chromatographic separation of cefixime was achieved on a C18 stationary phase column of regular size that could be utilized with standard pharmaceutical analyses. The flow rate was maintained at 1.5 mL/min, and the temperature of the column was set in such a way that provided a reproducible retention and shape of the peak. Column temperature was set at 25 °C, and UV detection was done at 254 nm. To ensure that cefixime could be detected at sufficiently low levels, the injection volume was set at 100 µL, thereby increasing the mass of analyte introduced onto the column without compromising peak shape. An optimal run time was set in such a way that it would allow a complete elution of cefixime without affecting the analytical throughput.

Sampling and recovery methodology

Rinse sampling procedure of cefixime and detergent residues

The quantitative collection of the rinse solution was mixed and filtered using a 0.22 μm PTFE membrane filter. Cefixime residues were collected by rinsing the vessel with 10.0 mL purified water and swirling for 2 min to ensure complete wetting of the internal surfaces. The rinse solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE membrane; an aliquot of this filtered solution was used directly for liquid chromatographic determination of cefixime residues (HPLC).

Standard stock and working solutions

An accurately weighed sample of cefixime (33.4 mg) was transferred to a 50 mL volumetric flask, mixed with diluent (35 mL) of the mobile phase, then sonicated until complete dissolution. Next, the volume was corrected to the mark using the same diluent and swirled thoroughly to get the standard stock solution. A 3.0 mL aliquot of the standard stock solution was transferred into a 100 mL volumetric flask, and the diluent was added to bring the nominal concentration of a working standard solution to 20 µg/mL. The mixture was completely mixed and then filtered using a 0.22 μm membrane filter and then subjected to chromatographic analysis (Fig. 2).

Sample preparation for chemical and detergent residues

Detergent residue screening was performed on the same final rinse described in Sect. 2.5.1. The last rinse (10.0 mL purified water) was transferred into a clean bottle and its conductivity measured using a calibrated conductivity meter. The conductivity values were compared with the corresponding values for pure water to determine the presence of residual detergent.

Contamination solution preparation

Suprax POS (~ 2.38 g; 2 mL) was accurately weighed and transferred to a 200 mL volumetric flask, which was filled to the mark with purified water. The mixture was thoroughly mixed to afford a homogeneous stock solution for the controlled contamination of glassware.

Glassware contamination

The prepared contamination solution was used to wet various types of laboratory glassware, such as volumetric flasks (5–1000 mL), conical flasks (250 mL), beakers (50–1000 mL), test tubes, volumetric pipettes (1–25 mL), and graduated pipettes (1 mL).

The following standardized baseline contact contamination times were utilized to replicate routine operational holding cycles: volumetric flasks (60 min), beakers (30 min), and volumetric pipettes (1 min). After the soaking period, the contamination solution was disposed of following our laboratory waste management procedures. Then, the dirty glassware was dried at ambient conditions over a period of 24 h before cleaning. The detection of detergent residue by measuring conductivity was conducted on volumetric pipettes having a capacity of 5 mL and more.

Cleaning procedure

Cleaning was started by treating the soiled vessels with 50 mL of a 2.0% (v/v) solution of the commercial alkaline laboratory detergent ADD-Pharma 72 at an optimized temperature of 40° C ± 2° C for a contact time of 5.0 min under manual agitation.

Instrument calibration

The HPLC photodiode array detector, operating at 254 nm, was calibrated between 0.5 and 20.04 µg/mL using five solutions volumetrically diluted from a working standard solution at 20 micrograms per mL (µg/mL). To ensure that the limit of quantification and the expected maximum residue concentration are bracketed, typical calibration levels were chosen. All the calibration solutions were prepared freshly, combined thoroughly, and filtered using a 0.22 μm membrane filter before injection. All the levels of calibration were loaded into the chromatographic system under the optimized chromatographic conditions. Each concentration level had its peak area of cefixime. The correlation between the peak area and the respective concentration of cefixime was plotted to obtain a calibration curve.

Results and discussion

Method development and optimization

The use of the rinse solution to measure both the cefixime content and undertake conductivity-based analysis of detergent residues was to ensure an easy, reproducible method that could be implemented in the routine procedures in quality control labs. The technique enables a thorough examination of the active pharmaceutical ingredient residues as well as the cleaning agent residues, giving a comprehensive analysis of the glassware cleanliness in accordance with the regulatory demands. The liquid chromatographic method was developed with a goal of satisfying the desired sensitivity, selectivity, and to determine cefixime residues on laboratory glassware at low-levels and meet regulatory and sustainability requirements. Special consideration was given to the mobile phase composition, pH regulation, choice of organic modifier, and chromatographic parameters to make sure that the method would be appropriate in cleaning validation. A C18 stationary phase was chosen because it has a broad regulatory acceptance, reproducibility, and has been shown to be effective in separating β-lactam antibiotics29. The short retention times of cefixime on C8 alkyl chain columns did not provide confidence that the main component was adequately separated from impurities of related structure. Therefore, a standard C18 column was the best choice with the best retention and resolution at relatively mild chromatographic conditions. The aqueous acetate buffer component of the mobile phase was chosen because of its buffering capacity in the moderately acidic to near-neutral pH range and its compatibility with both UV detection and environmentally friendly methods of analysis30. The acetate buffers also reduce salt precipitation and column fouling in contrast to the high ionic strength phosphate buffers, especially with alcoholic organic modifiers. The pH of the acetate buffer was brought to 6.5 because cefixime has optimum chromatographic performance and chemical stability at this pH. At lower pH, partial suppression of ionization increased retention but also caused peak tailing and reduced chromatographic repeatability. At higher pH, excessive ionization of the carboxylate group reduced retention and detector response, leading to poorer sensitivity. These observations reflect chromatographic effects of pH on cefixime ionization rather than degradation of the compound31. A buffer pH of 6.5 allowed a compromise between the stability of analytes, symmetry of the peaks, and adequate retention, which is essential to low-level quantification in cleaning validation experiments. The selection of ethanol as the primary organic modifier was initially guided by green chemistry frameworks to replace conventional toxic solvents like acetonitrile. However, its operational integration required systematic, empirical laboratory optimization rather than theoretical assumption alone. Due to ethanol’s higher viscosity compared to acetonitrile, standard literature baselines were insufficient to predict practical column backpressures and peak broadening for cefixime under low-level residual conditions. Consequently, our experimental phase involved hands-on optimization of the mobile phase composition, adjusting the acetate buffer (pH 6.5) to ethanol ratio to an optimized 89:11 (v/v) to achieve optimal peak symmetry (1.21), excellent column efficiency (theoretical plate count N > 4,500), and highly reproducible retention times (RSD < 0.7%) under safe operational system pressures. All chromatographic values, peak resolutions, and baseline profiles reported herein represent original, primary data generated during our practical validation runs. To facilitate greater simplicity of method, reproducibility, and the ability to use the method in cleaning validation procedures, where retention times and system operation need to be consistent, an isocratic elution mode was chosen. Gradient elution was deemed unnecessary because of the single analyte nature of the assay and the absence of significant interferences caused by the glassware matrix. The flow rate of 1.5 mL/min was identified as the best balance between the analysis time, resolution, and backpressure of the system. Photodiode array detection was considered the technique of choice based on strong anticipated absorbance by cefixime’s two chromophores and it being a widely accepted technique in regulated settings. Cefixime’s UV spectrum (Fig. S1) confirmed λmax at 288 nm and a relative trough around 254 nm. While the stronger absorbance at 288 nm was expected to result in higher sensitivity for the detection of cefixime, the detector baseline noise was significantly increased at this wavelength due to lamp profile characteristics. Furthermore, the detector response at 254 nm was shown to be more stable and reproducible with a lower relative standard deviation in peak area between replicate injections and, importantly, a higher S/N ratio at the limit of quantification. As the molar absorbance at 254 nm was sufficient to quantify cefixime at the target concentration levels, and the overall precision was better at 254 nm than at 288 nm under our HPLC conditions (Table S1), this wavelength was chosen for the processing to optimize the robustness, reproducibility, and quantitative performance of the method.

The rationale behind the sampling method

A residue sampling of laboratory glassware is an important procedure in cleaning validation since it has a direct effect on the validity and representativeness of the determination of the residues. The rinse sampling method was chosen in this research because of the non-porous surface of glass and the need to assess the whole contact surface. Furthermore, this method is readily applicable in quality control laboratories on a routine basis. Rinse sampling is commonly used on smooth surfaces, including borosilicate glass, where it is a very effective method of recovering soluble residues without the variability of swab material composition or operator technique.

Assessment of detergent residue by conductivity

Conductivity was used as a non-selective but sensitive measure of ionic detergent residues that could remain on the glassware surfaces after cleaning. Conductivity of the final rinse water was measured with a calibrated conductivity meter and compared with the conductivity of the purified water used for rinsing. Any dissolved salt contributes to the measured conductivity; therefore, acceptance was based on observing no statistically significant increase in conductivity relative to purified water, indicating negligible Na⁺ and K⁺ ion content and, consequently, the effective absence of detergent residues.

Appraisal of sustainability metrics

Complementary green analytical metrics were employed to characterize the environmental profile of the developed HPLC method without compromising its main aim in cleaning validation. The Analytical Method Greenness Score (AMGS) indicated an overall favorable footprint, with the major contributions coming from instrument energy consumption and solvent EHS (Environmental, Health, and Safety) properties32. In the current experiment, the AMGS analysis has provided the total score of greenness of 499.60 as the summative green footprint of the developed HPLC method (Table S2). The Environmental Assessment Tool (EAT) score related to the developed HPLC method provides a clear understanding of the environmental profile of the chromatographic conditions with special attention to the solvent selection. As expected, water (Mobile Phase A) has a minor environmental impact (Fig. 3), due to its non-toxic, non-flammable, and environmentally harmless nature33. Conversely, the most important contribution to the total HPLC-EAT score is the contribution of ethanol (Mobile Phase B) with a total contribution of about 36.3 EAT units (Table S3). The analytical eco scale (AES) aided analysis of the HPLC method reveals a distinctively positive environmental profile34. The cumulative penalty points were 13, giving an Eco-Scale score of 87 (Table 1), which is an excellent green analytical method based on the established Eco-Scale interpretation criteria (scores greater than 75). The low penalty points are mainly explained by the wise choice of reagents and operating conditions. The high consumption of purified water and sodium acetate that have zero penalty points indicates that these two are non-toxic and harmless to the environment. In spite of the fact that ethanol and acetic acid added a small amount of penalty points (4 and 2, respectively) because of flammability and mild corrosiveness, the values are still much lower than the values added by the traditional HPLC organic solvents like acetonitrile. The number of penalties associated with the instruments was also minimal; the energy consumption of the HPLC system was limited to ≤ 1.5 kWh per sample, which was only one penalty point, and the fact that there were no significant occupational hazards only contributed to the green profile of the method. Other minor penalties were associated with the use of ultrasonics and generation of waste, all of which were inescapable but highly regulated (Fig. 3).

Method validation

The method of liquid chromatography that had been developed to determine cefixime residues on laboratory glassware was validated using the principles of ICH Q2(R2) guidelines in order to prove that the method was suitable to use in cleaning validation35.

Linearity and range

Considering the anticipated levels of cefixime residue on the glassware, stock solutions of cefixime were prepared in the range 0.5–20.04 µg/mL. Processing at 254 nm, a calibration curve for the photodiode array detector was shown to be linear (R2 = 0.9998). Regression analysis (Table 2) confirmed the method to be sufficiently linear and sensitive in the chosen concentration range for accurate quantitative analysis of cefixime residues on glassware used in the laboratory.

LOD and LOQ

Regression analysis of the calibration curve established the cefixime limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ) using the HPLC-UV method to be 0.037 µg/mL and 0.11 µg/mL, respectively (Table 2). This confirmed the method to be sufficiently sensitive for the cleaning validation in line with regulatory standards.

Repeatability

Method repeatability was assessed in line with the ICH Q2(R2) standard. The intra-day repeatability was determined by comparing replicate injections of cefixime at a representative concentration under the same conditions on the same day (Table 2). Inter-day repeatability was assessed by reanalysis of the same sample on different days by different analysts using the same instrumentation and chromatographic conditions. The retention time and peak area repeatability were reported as a percentage relative standard deviation (%RSD). The %RSDs were within the acceptable limits, confirming stable detector response and chromatographic performance (Table S4). These findings confirm that the method is sufficiently reproducible for use in routine quantitative determination of cefixime residues when performing cleaning validation studies.

Accuracy and recovery

Pure water was found to be perfectly suited as an analytical extraction solvent to dissolve pure dried cefixime standards from a glass surface in a recovery study. However, this does not work for real-world glassware contaminated with commercial formulations (Suprax POS suspension), containing polymers, thickeners, and sugars that form a hydrophobic sticky crust when dried. The detergent is needed to break down these excipients, and then pure water is used to extract and rinse any remaining traces for HPLC quantification.

In line with ICH Q2(R2) guidelines to establish method reliability, a surface recovery study was used to evaluate the extraction efficiency of cefixime from a glass surface in the absence of commercial formulation excipients i.e. the situation after the cleaning procedure has been undertaken. Various glassware was exposed to cefixime in solution at three concentrations, 5 µg/mL (low), 10 µg/mL (medium), and 15 µg/mL (high) and allowed to dry. Each concentration level was subsequently analyzed in triplicate (Fig. 4), with recoveries at 97.0–98.6% at the low level, 99.0–100.1% (medium level), and the high level was 99.6–100.2% (high level). These findings confirm a high extraction efficiency at the concentration range studied, further confirming the HPLC method is fit for purpose to measure cefixime during cleaning validation studies.

Robustness

The robustness of the HPLC method developed to determine cefixime was tested as per the ICH Q2(R2) guidelines by analyzing the impact of minimal and intentional changes in the chromatographic conditions on the method performance. The flow rate, mobile phase composition, and detection wavelength were altered under controlled conditions to investigate their effect on method performance, as determined through variations in retention time, peak area, and peak symmetry. As demonstrated, these small differences did not cause any significant change in the chromatographic behavior of cefixime, and the results obtained were within acceptable limits (Table S3). The low variability observed under the altered conditions confirms the method to be robust, reliable and appropriate for use in routine cleaning validation and quality control laboratories.

System suitability tests were also conducted to confirm that the HPLC system performed satisfactorily before and during cefixime analysis, ensuring the reliability and reproducibility of the chromatographic data. The parameters of the suitability were considered through replicate injections of the cefixime standard solution under the optimized chromatographic conditions. Cefixime had a retention time of 5.117 min, which exhibited consistent elution. The relative standard deviation of retention time and peak area were ≤ 0.7% and 0.3%, respectively, which is very good precision in the system. Moreover, the tailing factor (1.1) was within the acceptable range of not exceeding 2.0, which indicated good symmetry of the peak. The findings validate the suitability of the HPLC system in the analysis and fulfill all the system suitability criteria that would enable the determination of cefixime to be reliable in method validation and routine cleaning validation studies.

Specificity

Specificity was considered by comparison of the chromatograms of blank diluent, rinse solutions of clean glassware, cefixime standard solutions, and sample solutions after cleaning. The HPLC method was deemed fit for purpose based on no interfering peaks being detected at the retention time of cefixime in blank or placebo rinse samples (Fig. 5).

Stability of standard solutions

The stability of the working standard solutions was checked by storing the samples at refrigerated temperature (5 °C ± 3 °C) and at room temperature (25 °C) for 48 h. Concentrated solutions were prepared at the actual working concentration and stored under controlled laboratory conditions and examined at various time intervals against freshly prepared solutions. The areas and the retention times of the stored standard solutions were compared with the freshly prepared standards. There was no notable alteration of chromatographic response, peak shape, or retention time within the study period, and the difference in peak area was within a reasonable range. These results show that under the conditions of storage, the cefixime standard solutions remain stable throughout the period used in the assessment, thus validating the choice of the cefixime standard solutions as suitable to use in calibration, validation, and routine analysis in cleaning validation studies (Table S3).

Efficacy of cleaning laboratory glassware

The efficiency of the cleaning method was tested on different types and volumes of laboratory glassware by measuring both the cefixime and detergent residue. As described in the results, no cefixime peaks could be detected in the rinse samples collected in any of the tested glassware, even the volumetric flasks, conical flasks, beakers, test tubes, volumetric pipettes, and graduated pipettes. This confirms the total elimination of cefixime, our worst-case scenario cephalosporin residue, after the cleaning procedure has been performed.

Parallel to this, the detergent residue was assessed by measuring the conductivity of the final rinse water and comparing it to the conductivity of pure water, which should be between 0.73 and 1.19 µS/cm; these values are excellent indicators of detergent residue removal. With small-volume volumetric pipettes (1-4mL), conductivity testing could not be done because of low rinse volume; nonetheless, HPLC results still indicated the lack of residues of cefixime. The experimental conductivity values across all washed glassware items ranged from 0.730 to 1.192 µS/cm. To rigorously evaluate cleaning verification compliance, a baseline control group of input purified water was monitored (n = 10), yielding a mean conductivity of 0.812 µS/cm (SD = 0.045 µS/cm). The rinse conductivity data was subjected to a one-way ANOVA and an independent Student’s t-test against the control water baseline. The statistical analysis revealed no significant difference between the final rinse fluids and the baseline control groups (p = 0.384), as detailed in the supplementary material (Table S5).

All in all, the chemical and conductivity data taken together show that the cleaning procedure adopted is effective and can be reproduced in other geometries and volumes of glassware. These findings indicate that such cleaning is appropriate to be used routinely in pharmaceutical quality control laboratories and offers sufficient control of both active pharmaceutical ingredients and detergent residues, as per cleaning validation and regulatory requirements.

Conclusion

An efficient, sensitive, and eco-friendly analytical plan was created and verified successfully to assess the effectiveness of cleaning laboratory glassware with cefixime as a worst-case cephalosporin antibiotic. The validated RP-HPLC system exhibited system suitability, linearity, accuracy, precision, robustness, and sensitivity in line with ICH Q2(R2) requirements, allowing detection and quantification of cefixime residues at low-levels that are pertinent in cleaning validation requirements. The low values of LOD and LOQ ensured the aptness of the method to ensure stringent control of cross-contamination, and the recovery and precision results ensured the accuracy and reproducibility of the analytical procedure over the concentration range of study.

The method was applied to different types of glassware and different volumes in the laboratory, where no traces of cefixime were observed, which proved the success of the cleaning process implemented. Concomitantly, conductivity readings of the final rinse water indicated an effective removal of detergent residues, which offered a complementary guarantee of cleanliness of glassware. The synergistic analysis of chemical and detergent residues provides an integrated and practical approach to laboratory cleaning validation, based on data integrity and controlled contamination in the pharmaceutical quality control setting. Additionally, to evaluate the adverse effects of the analytical procedure on the environment, several green metrics of chemistry, such as the AES, HPLC-EAT, and AMGS, were critically examined. The advantage of an ethanol-based mobile phase and energy-efficient chromatographic conditions, as demonstrated by the high Eco-Scale score, as well as favorable results in HPLC-EAT and AMGS, was an indication of the ability of the method to comply with the principles of green analytical chemistry without affecting the analytical performance. Overall, the suggested methodology offers a scientifically valid, regulatory-compliant, and environmentally friendly system of laboratory glassware cleaning validation. The method is easily applicable to laboratories of pharmaceutical quality control and can be applied to additional high-risk or allergic substances, helping to enhance better standardization, risk-based decision-making, and sustainable analytical practice.
