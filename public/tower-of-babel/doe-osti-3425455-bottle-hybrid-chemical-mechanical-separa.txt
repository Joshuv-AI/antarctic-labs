1. Executive Summary
The main objective of this project is to develop a hybrid mechanical-chemical recycling
technology for multilayered and laminated plastics. We aimed to separate and upcycle up to
more than 80% of the two main constituents of such structures, polyolefins and polyesters, for a
significantly lower cost and at higher energetic efficiency and much larger throughputs than
chemical recycling.
At the end of the project, the team was able to:
 a) Develop an extrusion-based separation technology that resorts to polyester depolymerizaton
 and extraction and allows for more than 90% of the polyester to be separated in the melt
 from the main polyolefin stream.
 b) Depolymerize the separated PET to more than 90%, which facilitates its posterior
 repolymerization and guarantees its retention in the polymer value-chain.
 c) Develop a zeolite-induced extrusion-based technology able to conduct continuous catalytic
 cracking of polyolefins, including highly contaminated PCR streams, at temperatures as low
 as 350 OC.
 d) Show, using LCA/TEA analysis that the two technologies are much more advantageous
 techno-economically and over the material’s life cycle than existing recycling technologies.

2. Introduction
Less than 10% of the more than 300 million tons of plastic waste produced annually is recycled,
which poses tremendous environmental challenges and therefore, there is strong societal and
regulatory pressure to increase recycling, whether mechanical or chemical. Both types of
recycling have major drawbacks (see Background section) that prevent either of them from being
the answer to the problem on their own. A further complicating factor is that more than 40
million tons of plastics produced annually are composed of blended, multilayered or laminated
polymers (in e.g., films, bottles, etc.), the three main components being polyolefins (90+%,
mainly polyethylene, PE), polyesters (mainly PET) and nylons. This poses its own tremendous
environmental challenges, since recycling of these multi-material structures is not currently
technically feasible, despite some recent attempts for polyolefin laminates. It is clear that new
approaches that combine the best of the existing methods and expand them are urgently
necessary if laminate and multilayered plastic structures are to be recycled at scale. That is the
main goal of this project and if successful, it will represent a paradigm shift in the recycling of
multi-material packaging waste, by not only making it possible, but also by performing at the
million ton scale required to solve the current end-of-life challenges.

3. Background
Mechanical recycling has lower costs, high volume capacity (106+ tons/year) and offers lifetime
savings in excess of 2 tons of CO2 per metric ton per cycle of plastic recycled; it is, however,
very susceptible to contamination and feedstock variability, so it yields products with poor
mechanical properties, i.e., it is a downcycling technique. Chemical recycling largely eliminates
downcycling, due to increased tolerance to contamination and still offers lifetime savings of

approximately 1.5 t of CO2 per metric ton of plastic recycled, e.g. producing low-sulfur diesel
fuel via pyrolysis of waste plastics is up to 14% less greenhouse gas intensive than making the
same fuel from crude oil. However, it is very expensive, with plants costing $50-300M each, and
offers low capacity (104-105 tons/year per plant), so there is a clear scale problem. A further
complicating factor is that more than 40 million tons of plastics produced annually are composed
of blended, multilayered or laminated polymers (in e.g., films, bottles, etc.), the three main
components being polyolefins (90+%), polyesters (mainly PET) and nylons. This poses its own
tremendous environmental challenges, since recycling of these multi-material structures is not
currently technically feasible.
This project proposed to develop a novel hybrid chemical-mechanical technology for recycling
and upcycling of laminated and multilayered plastics. This was based on continuous separation
and post-separation recycling of the mixed melt stream. The first step was to separate in the
molten state, continuously inside a twin-screw extruder the main polyolefin melt stream
contaminated with up to 10% of polyester and other residual polymers. This was achieved by
depolymerizing the polyester phase (polyethylene terephthalate, PET, was used as a test case,
since it is the most commonly used polyester in mixed polyolefin/polyester films) using
glycolysis and then separating it at the transition from the extruder to the die via a melt trap. The
second step was to utilize novel low-temperature (~350 OC) zeolite catalysts to perform
continuous catalytic cracking of the purified (but still residually contaminated) polyolefin melt
stream. As test cases for the polyolefins, we used high-density polyethylene, HDPE, and linear
low-density polyethylene, LLDPE, again because these are the main polyolefins used in mixed
polyolefin/polyester films. In all cases, the methodology was developed using virgin polymers
and eventually tested and validated for post-industrial recyclate, PIR, and post-consumer
recyclate, PCR.
By combining the large scale inherent to mechanical recycling with the upcycling enabled by
chemical recycling, and by optimizing the resulting continuous process, our hybrid technology
will be a true game changer in the field of recyclability of laminate and multilayered plastics.
The result will be significant energetic, environmental and economic impacts. For example, our
hybrid chemical-mechanical recycling technology should yield even bigger savings in
greenhouse gases emissions relatively to crude oil; this is difficult to quantify in advance and
will be assessed and optimized by the Life Cycle Analysis to be performed throughout the
project. Also, by doing polyolefin pyrolysis at low temperatures (~350 OC) during extrusion,
significant saving (more than 50%) relatively to current pyrolysis (performed at 400 OC and
above) should be possible to be achieved. In terms of cost and scale, a large-scale hybrid
extrusion-based process could potentially treat more than 106 tons per year, with a single
machine costing less than $20M. More than ten such lines, with an installed capacity of the order
of 107 tons/year, could be installed for less than the cost of a 105 ton per year chemical recycling
plant. Finally, small and medium-scale decentralized recycling facilities that avoid transportation
costs and incentivize local collection could also be enabled and made economically viable by this
technology, since smaller machines, still capable of 103-104 tons per year, cost less than $1M and
could be decentralized, i.e., located in even small and medium-sized municipalities.

4. Results and Discussion
Task 1 - Verification: The objective of this Task was to benchmark the state-of-the-art of the
technologies and demonstrate baseline target achievement for the NREL team, as well as
defining inputs for the Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) and Techno-Economic Analysis (TEA).
This was successfully achieved, with the NREL Team having certified that the project team has
successfully demonstrated the baseline target achievement and set the inputs for the LCA/TEA.

Task 2 - Polyester Separation and Recycling/Upcycling: The objective of this Task was to
develop and implement the twin-screw extrusion-based technology for separation and
recycling/upcycling of PET. We explored two pathways for PET separation from PET/PE
blends. In Route 1 we performed partial chain extension of the PET, followed by thermo-electro-
rheological manipulation of the blend and filtration of the PET phase. In Route 2, we performed
partial depolymerization of PET and removal via a melt trap. This Task was successfully
completed. The project targets of 20% chain extension in Route 1 (see below) and 80%
depolymerization in Route 2 (see below) have been exceeded, with values in excess of 80%
chain extension and 90% depolymerization having been achieved.
In Route 1 (Separation via Chain Extension), the main hypothesis is that by increasing the
viscosity ratio between the PET and the polyolefin matrix, PET droplet coalescence can be
enhanced and the ensuing larger droplets can be mechanically extracted through a screen at the
end of the extruder and before the die. The main project objective was to extract at least 80% of
the PET from the melt stream, and was successfully achieved, with the separated percentage of
PET in virgin material blends at CWRU being estimated to be higher than 88%. Initial
experiments were performed in a batch mixer that showed that by adding up to 1.5% w/w of
pyromellitic dianhydride (PMDA) to PET significant degrees of chain extension can be achieved,
but branching became a problem above 0.5% w/w of PMDA, as can be seen in Figure 1.
Based on the rheological data, namely the value of ’ in the Newtonian plateau, it is estimated
that a chain extension larger than 80% occurs at 0.5% w/w PMDA. Above this value,
branching and/or crosslinking starts occurring, as can be seen from the disappearance of the
Newtonian plateau at low frequencies and by the fact that the melt becomes more brittle, as
highlighted from the decrease in stress at rupture.

Figure 1. Left: Dynamic viscosity of chain-extended PET. Right: Melt stress at rupture upon extension of
 chain-extended PET.

In addition to rheological data showing the chain-branched PET at higher loading contents, an
initial series of cumulative area ratio (CAR) analyses were conducted on 50/50 wt% PE/PET
blends, at 0%, 0.5%, and 0.75% PMDA content levels. In these tests, samples were cryofractured
for Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) analysis, and the data supports that high loading
contents will hinder coalescence, most likely due to an excessive increase in droplet viscosity.
The samples were analyzed to a standard area of 80m2, roughly the equivalent of a particle size
of 10m in diameter. The highest degree of coalescence was obtained for a PMDA content 0.5%;
at this PMDA level, approximately 60% of the PET has coalesced into droplets larger than 10
m in diameter. These results represent a significant improvement on the 40% obtained when no
PMDA is used and 15% when crosslinking occurs but still fell short of the 80% necessary to
achieve mechanical separation in the screen (the smallest screens available commercially have
10-12 m orifices, thus that was our CAR target). Additional experiments at different PMDA
loading levels between 0.1 and 0.4%, and varying processing conditions to achieve a final degree
of coalescence of 80% proved unsuccessful.
Further testing at PMDA contents slightly above 0.5% revealed that maximal phase separation
occurred at 0.55% PMDA, with approximately 71% of the PET being present in droplet larger
than 100 m in diameter. Even though this is below the target 80%, it is expected that as the
screens retain PET, the effective orifice size will decrease and, therefore, more PET is captured,
so that was the PMDA content used in the scale-up trials at Braskem USA.
PE/PET blends (90/10 w/w%) were extruded and the amount of PET present in the extrudate was
evaluated by FTIR. At the time of the experiments, the twin-screw extruder at Braskem USA did
not have screens, so the samples melt was extruded, pelletized and then ran again through a
single-screw extruder equipped with a 12 m mesh size screen. This screen ruptured within
seconds, which turned out to be a harbinger of problems to come, but it was possible to collect
enough extruded sample to perform the ensuing characterization. FTIR baseline curves were
initially generated for different PE/PET blends containing known amounts up to 10% of PET to
determine unique signatures in which one could identify different PET percentages. The ratios
between the two peaks correlate to the content of the blend. HDPE has a unique peak at 2916 cm-

1
 , and PET a unique peak at 1718 cm-1. Our scale-up results were then plotted on this curve (see
Figure 3) indicate that only 1.2% or PET remained in the main melt stream, which translates
to an 88% percent separation, which clearly exceeds the target of 80%.

In everyday extrusion operations at industrial scale, separation is performed via filtration screens
that are automatically swapped by fresh ones at regular intervals (every 5-10 s at its fastest),
before the pressure build-up becomes too high and screens rupture. For our systems, we
identified two adequate future automatic screen changing technical solutions, which is shown in
Figure 4. Figure 4a depicts a solution involving continuous screen changers (CSC), which
consists in pocket dual piston design that holds multiple screens (other types such as belt or
rotary exist). The chambers each fully support extruder flow individually, with commercial
designs having between 2 and 4 filtration chambers, which can be pulled individually without
disruption. This solution is relatively cheap and can handle higher contamination levels. The

second solution, shown in Figure 4b, is that of self-cleaning screen changers (backflush), in
which the pressure-induced backflow prevents breakage during cleaning or rotation. These are
typically used in high volume applications where small polymer contaminants are an issue (i.e.,
polyolefin recyclates) and offer better pressure control than CSCs but are more expensive and
cannot handle as high contamination levels.

We have completed industrial scale-up trials at Braskem USA with the model blends, where
different mesh size filters were used to achieve the right balance between optimal PET
incorporation content, separation ability and screen life. Four PET concentrations were used (4,
5, 10, 30 and 50 w/w %). Mesh size screens varied from 20 m to 100 m. For each screen time
to failure and PET retention capability were assessed, to guide screen selection, since screen life
will depend on mesh size and PET content. Unfortunately, as can be seen from Table 1, the
screen all failed almost immediately for PET concentrations above 10% w/w. Even for lower
PET concentrations, which are more representative of real-world PIR and PCR, the only screens
that have a long enough lifespan to be able to be changed with any of the above systems (Figure
4) are too coarse, with minimum particle retention sizes in excess of 30 m, which is much
larger than the desired 8-10 m. For example, for a 5% PET blend, a 24 m mesh size screen
still fails after only 6 s. Therefore, they would still be inadequate, since they wouldn’t be able to
remove significant amounts of PET from the melt stream. As such, even though coalescence was
achieved under both lab and very specific industrial conditions, the challenges of scaling-up the
technology are significant and we believe it has limited development/implementation potential.
Therefore, this route was abandoned for the final scaling up with PIR and PCR.

In Route 2 (Separation via Depolymerization), the main hypothesis is that the same levels of PET
separation could be achieved as in Route 1, but this time by decreasing the viscosity ratio enough
the very low-viscosity PET phase could be extracted with the help of melt trap placed at the end
of the extruder. Decreasing the PET viscosity was to be achieved via glycolysis of the polymer
into oligomers and extraction could be aided/enhanced with the injection of Supercritical CO2.
The main objective was to depolymerize PET by up to 90% and achieved 80% separation from
the polyolefin matrix. This was achieved successfully, with levels of depolymerization estimated
to be as high as 97% and separation levels of PET up to 95% were achieved in virgin blends
processed at CWRU.
Initially, up to 10% w/w of ethylene glycol (EG) was batch mixed with a PET with a Mw of
11,000 g/mol; levels of Pet depolymerization estimated to be as high as 99% (from rheological
data) were achieved. Given the fact that EG presents non-zero levels of toxicity, a possible
alternative chemical has been identified to achieve PET depolymerization in Route 2, Bis(2-
hydroxyethyl) terephthalate (BHET). Therefore, BHET-aided depolymerization performed via
the use of both a batch mixer and a twin-screw extruder (TSE), with the results shown in Figure
5 and Table 2. Both EG and BHET are effective as depolymerizing agents of PET, with EG
being the most efficient of the two. Table 1 shows the estimated percentages of depolymerization
obtained from Rheology measurements and the results show that while EG is slightly more
efficient than BHET, the overall depolymerization levels fall short of the stated 95%
depolymerization goal. These estimates were confirmed from Intrinsic Viscosity (IV)
measurements, and these agree with the rheological estimates to a deviation of less than 5%.
In order to increase the overall level of depolymerization, experiments were performed with
higher EG contents, but the results show (see Table 3) that there is a saturation after
approximately 8%, i.e., higher levels of EG incorporation do not lead to decreased Mw.

Given the low boiling point of monoethylene glycol (EG; 197.3 OC boiling point), it is possible
that EG is being lost during processing, which is carried out between 255 OC and 270 OC,
therefore extrusion experiments were performed with 10% dyethylene glycol (DEG; ~245 OC
boiling point) and triethylene glycol (TEG; 285 OC boiling point). As shown in Table 4, GPC
analysis shows that EG added to pure PET induced a degree of depolymerization of 88%, while
for DEG and TEG depolymerization degrees of 98% and 97%, respectively, were found.
Given the difficulties in successfully reproducing Route 1 industrially (see above) and the
success of Route 2, it was decided to abandon the former and focus further experiments on post-
Consumer and Post-Industrial Recyclates (PCR and PIR, respectively). Braskem USA has a
client that produces HDPE/PET films and thus, these were used as the PIR of choice. However,
we came to find out that the main component of these films was PET (93% w/w), with HDPE
being a very minor phase (~7% w/w). At such low HDPE contents, there is not enough
polyethylene to build up pressure at the die and extract the depolymerized PET. In addition, at
93% PET, there wasn’t enough DEG in the system to fully depolymerize the polymer and,
therefore, these experiments were unsuccessful. Therefore, these experiments did not yield

acceptable results, and we are now looking for other PIR suppliers, in conjunction with Braskem
USA, to redo the scale-up trials as soon as possible.

As a final note, we have not been able to find a supplier of PCR that includes a polyolefin
contaminated with a polyester, because (according to various of the suppliers we contacted) thus
far these films have not been recyclable and therefore, there has been no incentive to collect
them at their end-of-life and there are no collection streams in place. Therefore, if this
technology is to be applied industrially in the future, new collection streams for mixed
polyolefin/polyester films will also have to be implemented.

FUTURE WORK
At the time of the Final Technical Report we have another set of runs scheduled at CWRU where
we will blend the current PET-rich PIR with HDPE PCR and various ratios and for various DEG
contents, and assess what is the minimum HDPE content that will allow enough pressure buildup

extrudates that meet the project goals and conduct catalytic cracking extrusion experiments (see
Task 3 below) to demonstrate the upcycling process end-to-end.

Task 3 - Polyolefin Recycling/Upcycling: The main aims of this Task were to implement a
continuous, high-throughput recycling and upcycling via twin-screw extrusion of PE post-PET
separation. We addressed the following fundamental aspects of the process: (1) Evaluation of the
applicable types of the superacidic cracking catalysts, with an emphasis on resilience and

feedstock tolerance; and (2) Development of a low temperature (below 400 OC) recycling
methodology for efficient separation of the heterogeneous catalyst from the extruder output. Our
specific target is to obtain lower hydrocarbons (n < 12) from the original post-consumer and
post-industrial waste at pre-industrial scale was successfully concluded. We synthesized a library
of 200+ zeolite structures at microscale and performed the rapid screening of catalytic activity by
direct-injection mass spectrometry of the pyrolysis products. In agreement with our original
hypothesis, the pore templating agents appear to be the most important factor in improving
catalytic performance, with the washing/calcination protocols being less impactful. Four of the
most reactive zeolite formulations have been selected for sale-up syntheses. The activity of these
catalysts in static depolymerization experiments is significantly better than that of the
commercial ZSM-5 zeolite. All zeolites were tested in our static depolymerization experiments,
which consisted in compounding the polymer and the catalyst, and heated them inside the
thermal separation probe (TSP) directly connected to the EI mass spectrometer. At temperatures
up to 400 OC (the limit for our TSP) we found that samples with ZSM-5 behave largely the same
as baseline samples with no catalyst, as indicated by similar total ion count integration. The
abundance distribution of the fragment peaks was also similar, with multiple small fragments
below 100 m/z, which is consistent with desorption of impurities from the surface. We did not
observe any specific markers of depolymerization, such as evenly spaced peak groups indicating
chain scission. Thus, while ZSM-5 effects depolymerization at higher temperatures, it is
ineffective at 400 C or below and no CG-MS characterization was performed..
Scale-up protocols for production of these catalysts at 10-100 g scale have been developed and
optimized, and at least 10g of each material is now available for further experiments. Our
activity was focused on two key follow-up directions: (1) Establishment of a scalable and
transferrable protocol for compounding and co-extrusion of the catalysts with polymer substrates
of interest, and (2) Development of a rapid GC/MS screening method for separating and
characterizing the hydrocarbon products of depolymerization.
Having studied the reaction under static conditions, we proceeded to the next milestone, reactive
extrusion. In the initial experiments some of the synthesized zeolites have been compounded
with LDPE in a 11m twin-screw co-rotating extruder, and the products were extracted from the
solidified polymer and analyzed by GC-MS. Following that, we proceeded to scale up the
experiments to 10-100 g of polymer (LDPE) under conditions similar to static testing. The
volatile products are collected on a dry ice-cooled condenser and analyzed by GC-MS. Figure 6
shows the example of a gas chromatogram of the volatiles obtained from an extrusion run of
LDPE/CCM-31 at 350 OC. In this case, the components were separated chromatographically on
an HP-5MS column and detected with the same EI MSD as for the previous figure. The peaks
were assigned based on their mass spectra and retention time. The intensity of the peaks in this
chromatogram is indicative of the composition of the mixture. We found that the CCM-31
zeolite, for example, leads to complex product compositions and most of the detected products
are C14 and shorter and the ratio between 7<n<12 and n>=12 hydrocarbons is higher than 90%.
The first extrusion runs were aimed at assessing the cracking efficiency of the four selected
zeolites (labeled Z364, Z365, Z366 and Z506) on a linear low-density polyethylene (LLDPE).
Once the most efficient zeolite is determined, the process repeated an additional two times to
determine the relationship of mass loss vs run time, i.e., to assess the kinetics of the process,
namely how long the cracking process takes to reach completion. The extrudates were collected
and processed into rheology disks for rheology testing. The viscosity of the extrudate from the

four zeolites were compared to determine which yielded the lowest Mw PE (Figure 7). From the
results, Figure Z365 and Z506 had the least change in viscosity, indicating these two zeolites
performed the worst. Z366 had a significant decrease in viscosity leading to significant decrease
in Mw. However, Z364 showed to be the most promising due to the high increase of viscosity at
low frequency indicating that the polymer is highly degraded and the leftover zeolites are
dominating the viscosity behavior. This behavior shows that Z364 performed the best at
decreasing the Mw of the LLDPE. When the material from Z364 was run through the extruder an
additional two times the viscosity decreased with each run indicating a Mw decrease.

  Figure 6. GC-MS chromatogram of the volatile products of LDPE extruded with CCM-31 @350 °C.
 Most of the major fragments are C14 or smaller in agreement with the static pyrolysis screening.

To determine the mass loss/gas yield of the process samples were collected at the die after a set
amount of time to determine the overall mass loss. When an extruder reaches a steady state, the
mass put in is the same as the mass out. So, this means that any mass lost during the extrusion
run is due to degradation into gas that escapes the system. By calculating the mass through in and
out the mass loss can be calculated by subtracting the two. When the four zeolites were
compared in terms of mass loss it was clear the results agreed with the results from rheology.
With the overall performance from least to best being Z506 < Z365 < Z366 < Z364. When the
material collected from the low temperature cracking from Z364 was run an additional two times
a linear relationship could be seen. This relationship shows that three extrusion runs are the
optimized cycles to yield a high degree of gas products. When all three cycles are compared to
the original mass an overall gas yield of 50% was obtained (Figure 8). This is an extremely good
result as the residence time of the extruder was only 3 mins and with 3 cycles an overall time
of 9 mins. Compared to literature, where gas yields of 50-75% are achieved but only after 1-2
hours in a batch reactor, this result is very promising. This means that this process can not only
be done in a continuous manner with similar yields, but also in a tenth of the time.
To get an exact measurement of Mw of the extrudate the samples were sent out for GPC testing
at SGS solutions. From the Rheology and GPC results presented in Figures 9 and 10, the same
trend is seen with rheology and the mass loss calculations. Looking at the molecular weight

distribution (Figure 10 and Table 5), after 3 passes in the extruder the molecular weight of the
extrudate decreased by more than 50% and the polydispersity index increased approximately
fourfold to more than 14.

2. Alternative Reaction Rate Function
The previous results assume a fixed probability of cracking when any polymer chain is near the
catalyst wall. In practice, we should also take into account the orientation of the chain with
respect to the wall, as the catalyst effect may depend on “preferred” orientations. However, this
proved infeasible to model within LAMPPS, as this software only allows fixed bond breaking
probabilities in its configurations. The work in the earlier section, which involves the
probabilities changing over time, involved stopping and restarting the simulation every few
frames. As the probability becomes truly dynamic once orientation of chains is brought in, this
becomes impossible within LAMPPS. Therefore, we wrote our own DPD code to include such
bond breaking functions. This code is parallel and able to run on GPUs.
Simulating dense polymer melts with complex boundary conditions requires significant
computational overhead. To ensure this specialized codebase matches or exceeds the
performance standards of established, general-purpose molecular dynamics frameworks such as
LAMMPS, several optimization strategies were implemented.
First, to achieve the computational performance required for these complex systems and to tailor
the framework to our specific modeling needs, the simulation engine was entirely reimplemented
in C++. This custom build integrates robust multi-core CPU parallelization, efficiently
distributing the computationally intensive pairwise force calculations across available hardware
architectures. As demonstrated in our benchmarking in Figure 14, the system exhibits excellent
scaling with increased thread counts, successfully mitigating processing bottlenecks during
large-scale simulation runs.
Furthermore, to maximize efficiency without sacrificing thermodynamic accuracy, an adaptive
time-stepping algorithm was introduced. Rather than utilizing a static integration step, the
timestep is dynamically modulated based on the instantaneous, evolving properties of the system.
Specifically, the algorithm evaluates the average local particle velocities and the maximum
pairwise force exerted on any single particle during a given frame.
This adaptive methodology guarantees numerical stability during high-energy collision events or
rapid bond scission (by shrinking the timestep) while significantly minimizing unnecessary
computational cycles in regions or timeframes characterized by low dynamical activity (by
expanding the timestep).

 Figure 14. Simulation Benchmarks with adaptive time stepping: Simulation’s performance under
 adaptive and non-adaptive parallel time-stepping approach.

A core advancement in this computational effort is the implementation of an orientation
dependent reaction kinetic model. Based on theoretical observations, it was hypothesized that
bond-cleavage rates are not uniform; rather, they are dependent on the orientation of the polymer
bond relative to the catalyst surface. To computationally capture this dependency, a novel
localized model was developed to continuously evaluate the orientation of polymer bonds as they
approach the catalytic boundary.

of bond scission. The probability of a reaction occurring is modeled to peak at a structurally

3. Simulation Coarse-Graining
Derivation of coarse-grained microstructure evolution models such as phase-field equations from
atomistic details has been a long-standing challenge. We used a data-driven approach to learn
surrogate microstructural simulators directly from molecular dynamics trajectory. The proposed
data pipeline starts with voxelization of the MD supercell into discrete subdomains and
extraction of coarse-grained order parameters such as average composition1. Our interpretable
machine-learning approach consists of a coarse-level free energy function and a mobility
function, without assuming a partial differential equation expression as employed by phase field.
Assuming a Ginzburg-Landau-like Equations of Motion, we trained our ML model end-to-end
with the coarsened MD trajectory data.
This approach was trained on a model system of immiscible binary Lennard-Jones particles
undergoing spinodal decomposition reactions. The ML model performance was assessed with
two perspectives. On the kinetics side, the model was able to reproduce the mesoscale dynamics
and generalized to larger cells than the training set. Physical interpretability of the ML model
was examined with thermodynamic properties such as the temperature and curvature dependence
of interfacial free energies, which emerge directly from the physics and data-driven approach but
are otherwise difficult to obtain quantitatively with existing methods.
4. Machine Learning Pipeline
We developed a machine learning pipeline to predict the average probability of a polymer chain
cracking given a system configuration at an arbitrary moment of the reaction2. The reaction
parameters we model are the size of the pores in the catalyst structure and the body forces due to
the pressure gradient. The system snapshot at a time point is represented through the molecular
weight distribution at that point and the average velocity profile.

Each snapshot is associated with a normalized reaction rate, which is the product of an access
probability with which a chain can enter a pore of a certain diameter, and the probability of chain
scission once it is in the pore. The latter probability is computed from the simulation system
running a trajectory for an arbitrary pore size and pressure gradient and for the former, we
developed an analytical model to estimate the probability of polymer access to the catalyst wall.

based access probability P2. These reveal how physical and fluid dynamic factors independently
influence a particle’s ability to enter and traverse a channel. P1 is determined by the available

Subtask 5.2 - Multiscale Integration (Mesoscale to Macroscale): The main objective of this
subtask was to support the work in Task 2, in particular to develop the ability to predict
morphology, i.e., PET droplet size, under flow, thus allowing the extrusion process in Route 1 to
be designed to achieve maximum coalescence of the PET droplets.
Work in this Task attained almost all the objectives of the revised SOPO. The biggest challenge
in bridging the mesoscale to the macroscale is transferring/mapping results from DPD
simulations to the field theory level, which itself can describe the structure of polymer blends at
arbitrary length scales (e.g., those described in Goma). We did this by applying ML-based
mapping from the discrete mesoscale (DPD) to the continuum (PFT). ML mapping from
mesoscale simulations to PFT calculations has been successful, and results in the ability to
perform field theory calculations in 2D or 3D. Success in this context was defined as being able
to produce pressure and exchange fields (see Figure 18) from the polymer density throughout the
system. We accomplished this using a neural network with two inputs (minority polymer density,
Flory-Huggins parameter), three hidden layers with 10 neurons each, and two outputs (the
pressure and exchange field values). The rightmost column of Figure 18 shows that the
predictions match the ground truth field values with high accuracy. However, careful inspection
shows that there are discrete points of high error throughout the system. Although they appear to
be negligible artifacts of the prediction, the points can lead to significant numerical errors when
calculating polymer densities, as will be discussed in more detail below.

Figure 18. Comparison between “ground truth” pressure and exchange fields from a high-resolution PFT
 calculation, and predicted fields generated by our neural network. The rightmost column computes the
 percentage error between the two approaches.

Previously, our PFT calculations were only possible under equilibrium, static conditions. We
were able to implement the approach of Suo and Whitmore (J. Chem. Phys. 2014, 140, 114901)
to consider the effects of shear at steady state. A caveat to this approach is that currently we are
unable to quantify our shear rates in terms of values that can be directly tied to experiments or
simulations. For this reason, we refer to the shear rates qualitatively as “no shear”, “medium
shear”, and “high shear” to demonstrate the effect of increased shear rate on the deformation of
the droplets. Representative results are shown in Figure 19. A major objective in performing
these calculations is not only to map the mesoscale (with DPD) to the macroscale with PFT and

Goma, but to also quantify the shear rates in our calculations based on droplet shapes so that we
can connect calculations to experimentally relevant conditions.

 Figure 19. Polymer density fields obtained from PFT calculations demonstrating droplet structures (ϕ =
20%). From left to right, the systems correspond to no, medium, and high levels of shear and droplets are
 observed to deform as the shear rate is increased.

To perform this mapping, we carried out 3D DPD simulations of a polymer blend under
pressure-driven flow. A representative snapshot is shown in the top left corner of Figure 20 (only
the minority component is visible). From the particle coordinates in the snapshot, a polymer
density field was calculated (top right, Figure 20) that could be used as an input to our neural
network to predict the exchange and pressure fields that would give rise to that polymer density.
One can see (bottom right, Figure 20) that the fields essentially track with the polymer density
calculated from DPD. However, upon using the predicted fields to calculate the density in PFT,
we observe a mismatch between the density fields and the real, “ground truth” density fields that
were computed from DPD simulations. As such, this result prevents us from acquiring values
from PFT that can be passed to larger length scales (such as within Goma). We worked to
resolve this issue in two ways. First, it was necessary to add more neurons and/or hidden layers
to our neural network model to predict the exchange/pressure fields. There may be noise (e.g.,
similar to the discrete points with high errors shown in Figure 18, right column) which give rise
to large errors in the polymer densities that are calculated due to the underlying nature of the
field theory. It was hypothesized that a better tuned network structure may be able to eliminate
these points. Second, we looked to add random noise to our training data, which has been
suggested as a way to obtain a better tuned neural network. Finally, we explored alternatives to
the neural network used to predict the pressure/exchange fields and are particularly interested in
evaluating whether a random forest may provide a better prediction of those fields, from which
will calculate polymer density fields. We have some indications (Figure 21) that better field
predictions from our ML models will resolve the issue. Upon solving this issue, viscosity and
velocity fields determined from PFT can then be directly incorporated into Goma calculations to
predict realistic droplet morphologies across multiple length scales.
The inability of our SCFT code to produce quantitatively similar polymer densities using fields
predicted from a NN has prevented us from scaling up our polymer field theory calculations to
larger length scales that are commensurate with the length scales studied using continuum
methods like Goma. We identified two main causes of this problem: (1) the fields that are
significantly diverge by several (e.g., 2–3) orders of magnitude, and (2) the fields predicted from
NNs using densities obtained from DPD contain some degree of noise (e.g., in Fig. 20). The
discontinuities in the fields are particularly troubling since solving the polymer field equations is
done in reciprocal space, meaning that the fields undergo repeated Fourier transformations, for
which discontinuities in the fields can lead to numerical inaccuracies. Suggested solutions to

these problems were to either consider training the neural network with SCFT fields containing a
small degree of artificial noise, and to consider using a random forest (RF) approach rather than
a neural network. As we view the prediction of fields from DPD polymer densities to be a
numerical calculation, rather than a decision-making process, we opted to not consider the RF
model initially and instead evaluate it as a last effort to obtain numerical agreement.

 Figure 20. Clockwise from the top left, this figure shows schematically the process of calculating a
 polymer density field from a 3D DPD simulation, predicting the necessary pressure and exchange fields
 using our ML approach, and then calculating the density from PFT.

Considering both the discontinuities in the fields, as well as the inherent noise present when
converting particle locations from DPD into density fields, we hypothesized that applying noise
reduction techniques to the fields could smooth any noise while also smoothing out the small
number of discontinuities that were observed. To this end, we applied a Gaussian blur kernel, to
each site in the field, applying periodic boundary conditions along the edges of the field. The
result of this blurring on the computed density fields, as compared to the spurious results of Fig.
21, is shown in Fig. 22. We observe that initially, the predicted fields give rise to non-uniform
polymer densities within the phase separated domains (center panel, Fig. 22); however, after 200
iterations of the self-consistent algorithm, which takes approximately 2 minutes, we observe
excellent quantitative agreement between the ground truth results (left panel) and the prediction
(right panel). Seemingly, adding a Gaussian blur to the neural network predictions smooths the
fields sufficiently to make them more compatible with the polymer field theory framework.
We next sought to apply this approach to DPD predictions (Fig. 20), which previously showed a
noisy field prediction. Notably, the field theory was unable to produce polymer densities from
these fields (Fig. 20, bottom right panel) and predicted a single, concentrated region of polymer
with an unphysical volume fraction of over 100%. The comparisons between the original
approach and the new approach are highlighted in Fig. 23. The original prediction of the pressure
field is shown in the left panel, with its characteristic noise and discontinuities. The center panel

shows the field predictions with the Gaussian blur applied to the NN predictions, and the

density profile resembles the DPD simulation results shown in Fig. 20. However, there still exist
significant density fluctuations within the domains of the phase separated regions. These density
fluctuations decrease as an increasing number of field theory iterations are performed on the
predicted fields – producing better estimates of the polymer density.

the proposed tasks to advance sustainable plastic recycling, substantial progress has been made
in developing and completing a comprehensive life cycle assessment (LCA) of our recycling
process. This is an important positive outcome for the project, as it provides a rigorous
environmental evaluation of the process and confirms that our recycling approach can be
assessed in a clear and structured way, guiding the optimization and scaling of future plastic
recycling methods in industry and contributing more significantly to environmental protection.

A detailed block flow diagram of the overall recycling process was developed with clearly
defined system boundaries (Figure 33), enabling transparent accounting of major material and
energy flows as well as upstream inputs. The LCA framework was organized into four key
stages: (1) plastic collection and preprocessing, (2) feedstock homogenization and extrusion, (3)
polyester separation and recycling, and (4) polyolefin separation and recycling. Material input
flows were defined based on mixed plastic waste streams together with catalyst and auxiliary
process materials. In parallel, a benchmark LCA model for the conventional pathway
(incineration and conventional PET production) was constructed to support comparative
analysis. The recycling-process LCA model was implemented in GaBi using a functional unit of
1 kg of mixed plastic waste processed. Background data were compiled from Ecoinvent v3.8 and
GaBi 10 databases and supplemented with industrial measurements and literature sources to
improve representativeness.
Experimental data collection at CWRU also supported model development and parameterization.

present that recycling method is promising in improving environmental sustainability.
Building on the total-impact results, the recycling-method LCA was further strengthened through
contribution (hotspot) analyses at two complementary levels (Figure 36): (i) inventory groups
(energy, process materials, facilities, and catalysts) and (ii) process stages (feedstock
preparation/homogenization and separation, PET recycling, LLDPE recycling, BHET-to-PET
conversion, and end-of-life). The results reveal a clear and actionable hotspot structure. Across
many impact categories, impacts are primarily driven by energy use, while a smaller subset of
categories is more strongly influenced by catalyst-related upstream burdens. At the process-stage

level, impacts are concentrated mainly in the core recycling operations, especially PET and
LLDPE recycling, while other stages contribute smaller or more category-specific shares. This is
a constructive outcome because it identifies practical and targeted levers for further improvement
rather than indicating diffuse or unpredictable burdens.

5. Key Achievements
The main achievements of the project were:
  1. The development of an extrusion-based separation technology that resorts to polyester
 depolymerization and extraction and allows for more than 90% of the polyester to be
 separated in the melt from the main polyolefin stream.
  2. The demonstration that an alternative separation route based on enhanced Polyester phase
 separation and subsequent mechanical filtration, although feasible in principle, has serious
 technological constraints that prevent its upscaling and commercialization at present.
  3. The depolymerization of the separated PET to more than 90%, which facilitates its
 posterior repolymerization and guarantees its retention in the polymer value-chain.
  4. The ability to predict blend morphology under shear flow.
  5. The development of a zeolite-induced extrusion-based technology able to conduct
 continuous catalytic cracking of polyolefins, including highly contaminated PCR streams,
 at temperatures as low as 350 OC.
  6. The development of the capability to computationally design ML-assisted optimal zeolites
 for polyolefin cracking.
  7. The development of an on-line rotational rheometer, which we are in the process of
 licensing to ThermoScientific.
  8. The demonstration, using LCA/TEA analysis, that the two technologies are much more
 advantageous techno-economically and over the material’s life cycle than existing
 recycling technologies.
  9. The training of four Post-Doctoral Fellows (two at CWRU and one at each of the National
 Laboratories), five PhD students (all at CWRU) and two MSc students (both at CWRU).
  10. The publication of nine scientific papers in international refereed journals, the filing of
 two provisional patents and presentations at multiple international conferences.

6. Benefits Assessment
As mentioned in Section 4 above, the LCA/TEA analysis strongly supports our recycling
process as a high-value and highly promising approach for mixed plastic waste management.
Compared with the conventional baseline, our process generally shows a more favorable
environmental impact profile across many categories, while also delivering a much clearer
understanding of where impacts arise within the system. Our process is not only environmentally
promising, but also highly optimizable in practical engineering terms.

7. Lessons Learned
The main lesson learned is a positive one and it is that it is indeed possible to use a science and
technology-based approach to recycle/upcycle multi-material plastics parts and structures
without prior separation and regardless of purity of the collected waste stream. This opens the
door to massive increases in plastic products upcycling.
On the less positive side, progress on the computational side was hindered by the difficulty in
developing physically realistic models for highly complex materials and flows, which delayed
the feedback of the computational efforts to the experimental tasks.

8. Conclusions
In our view this project was highly successful in that it showed, for the first time, that it is possible
to upcycle major classes of multi-material plastic products and structures. Furthermore, it showed
that this can be done at the required scales to eliminate the end-of-life problem associated with
these materials. The solution developed within the project is scalable, and economically and
environmentally advantageous compared with current mechanical and chemical recycling. Given
that this was the first time such plastic products were shown to be recyclable ad upcyclable, the
required waste-supply streams are not yet in place and this is, in our view, the main obstacle to a
speedy and wide dissemination of the technology.

9. Future Work/Recommendations
In the short term, the main work to be performed is to: a) Find suitable PIR and PCR streams and
demonstrate vertical integration of all the technological steps.; b) Test the efficiency of ML-
designed zeolites the extrusion-cracking process and further optimize zeolites for various
polyolefin streams, e.g., polyethylene vs. polypropylene.
In the medium and long term, we strongly suggest extending the procedure to multi-material
systems containing tie layers (which are the majority commercially), which will turn into
compatibilizers once molten. We have ideas on how to achieve such a goal and will be pursuing
