Acute intoxication and trauma severity in emergency department patients: a systematic review
Authors: Marie Emilie Hjorth Lutambi, Laura Andrea Juul Petersen, Ann Merete Møller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvery day, trauma patients under the influence of alcohol and/or psychotropic drugs are treated in Emergency Departments (EDs). Psychoactive substances alter cognition, judgment, and behavior and may influence injury severity, clinical management, and outcomes following trauma. Although numerous observational studies have examined intoxication among trauma patients, only a limited number of reviews have synthesized evidence on its association with injury severity and clinical outcomes.ObjectiveTo evaluate the association between acute alcohol and/or psychotropic drug intoxication and trauma severity and outcomes, and to determine the prevalence of intoxication among trauma patients presenting to EDs.MethodsA systematic literature search of MEDLINE and Embase was conducted in January 2024 with a top-up search in November 2025. Prospective cohort studies including patients aged ≥ 15 years presenting to hospital-based EDs were eligible. After screening 2,132 records, 88 articles underwent full-text review, and 18 studies met the inclusion criteria.ResultsAcross the included studies, the prevalence of alcohol intoxication ranged from 8.3% to 65.9%, drug intoxication from 14.5% to 22.1%, and combined alcohol and drug intoxication from 6.0% to 38.8%. The average prevalence was 24.7% (range 8.3–65.9%) for alcohol intoxication, 17.3% (range 14.5–22.1%) for drug intoxication and combined alcohol and drug intoxication had an average prevalence of 13.1% (range 6.0–38.8%). Nine studies reported higher injury severity scores (ISS) or a higher proportion of severely injured patients among intoxicated individuals compared with non-intoxicated patients, while other studies reported no difference or lower injury severity. Trauma outcomes, including hospital length of stay and mortality, varied substantially across studies. Male sex and younger age were consistently associated with intoxicated trauma presentations.ConclusionThis systematic review found inconsistent evidence regarding the association between intoxication and trauma severity and outcomes. However, a majority of the included studies in this review reported higher injury severity among intoxicated patients, particularly in those under the influence of alcohol and/or drugs. Intoxicated trauma patients were predominantly male and younger, highlighting key risk groups for targeted prevention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12873-026-01623-7.

Introduction

Worldwide, patients frequently present to the Emergency Department (ED) intoxicated with alcohol or psychotropic drugs [1]. Psychotropic drugs are defined as drugs that change the mind and behaviour of the person using the drug [2]. These include illicit drugs such as cocaine and heroin, as well as legally prescribed medications commonly used in psychiatric treatment, such as lithium and antidepressants. Alcohol or drug intoxication generally changes the patient’s personality, cognition, judgement and memory among other personal characteristics and these alterations pose additional challenges in the clinical assessment and management of injured patients.

Alcohol and psychotropic drugs are known to be risk factors for injuries and accidents [3], and acute intoxication may impair protective reflexes, reduce muscle tone, and alter reaction time, thereby increasing the transfer of kinetic energy to the body during trauma. This biomechanical effect may contribute to increased injury severity among intoxicated patients and is consistent with observed associations between intoxication and risk behaviour such as seatbelt non-use. Numerous observational studies have examined intoxication among trauma patients presenting to EDs. In 2015 a study estimated that 16.4% of all injuries in 37 different EDs were directly caused by alcohol intoxication [4]. Furthermore, it has been found, that alcohol intoxication increases the risk of trauma recidivism [5], meaning that patients, who repeatedly present to the ED, more frequently are under the influence of alcohol.

Although numerous observational studies have examined the prevalence of alcohol and drug intoxication among trauma patients [6] only a limited number of reviews have included the evidence regarding the association between intoxication, injury severity, and clinical outcomes [7]. Consequently, there remains uncertainty as to whether intoxication is associated with increased trauma severity and worse outcomes, or whether observed differences are attributable to confounding factors such as injury mechanism and patient characteristics.

This systematic review aims to address these gaps by synthesizing evidence exclusively from prospective cohort studies, focusing on acute alcohol and/or psychotropic drug intoxication, and comparing trauma severity and outcomes between intoxicated and non-intoxicated patients presenting to the ED.

Methods

Our review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [8] and was not preregistered in PROSPERO because the initial search and screening process had already begun before registration was considered.

Database search

The two databases MEDLINE and Embase were first searched in January 2024, followed by a top-up search in November 2025, for all literature regarding intoxicated injured or trauma patients. The top-up search was conducted to ensure inclusion of the most recent literature prior to submission. To minimize the risk of ascertainment bias the screening of records identified in the top-up search was conducted independently using the same predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria as the initial search. Screening decisions were based solely on eligibility criteria and were not influenced by prior study findings or results from the initial screening phase. The top-up search was added to the original search for a complete result. Figure 1 summarizes the search process.

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram visualising the search process

Each database was searched with a list of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free text keywords. Both the MeSH terms and the free keywords were carefully chosen in collaboration with a librarian specialized in systematic reviews. The search was aimed to cover acute alcohol or drug intoxication as opposed to chronic misuse of the substances, as well as it was aimed to find articles that researched bigger injuries or traumas instead of smaller injuries or wounds.

The search strategy was divided into 3 aspects of the subject; the intoxication, the trauma or injury and the publication type. The MeSH terms of the intoxication aspects were Drinking behaviour/Alcohol drinking OR Alcoholic/Alcohol Intoxication OR Drunken Driving/Driving under the Influence OR Psychotropic Agent/Drugs OR Illicit Drug/Drugs OR Cannabis/Marijuana Smoking. The MeSH terms of the trauma aspect were Hospital Emergency Service/Emergency Service Hospital OR Injury OR Wound OR Injury Scale OR Accident OR Wounds and Injuries OR Injury Severity Score. Lastly, the MeSH terms for the publications type were Prospective Studies. In all aspects, the free text keywords matched the MeSH terms.

The full search strategy for each database is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Screening of records by title and abstract

In November and December 2025, the records were screened in the program Rayyan by the two reviewers, first by title and then by abstract.

The title and abstract screening were done by the two reviewers independently. Disagreements between the two reviewers were resolved by consensus.

At the title screening stage, records were excluded if they specifically did not relate to trauma patients, intoxication (alcohol or psychotropic drugs), or emergency department or hospital-based settings or if the study was stated as non-prospective in the title.

During abstract screening, studies were excluded if they did not meet the predefined inclusion criteria, including non-prospective study design, non-trauma populations, or studies focusing exclusively on chronic substance use instead of acute intoxication.

Inclusion criteria

The following inclusion criteria made the full-text articles eligible for inclusion in the qualitative analysis. (1) Study type was prospective cohort study. (2) Studies examining acute alcohol and/or drug intoxication among trauma patients. (3) Publications that focused on injured trauma patients above 15 years of age that were either intoxicated with alcohol, psychotropic/illicit drugs or both. (4) Publications that studied the severity (ISS, NISS or ISS90) and compared it between the sober and the intoxicated patient. (5) Publications from studies in all regions, states and countries in the world where the patients were treated in an Emergency Department. (6) Studies in which intoxication was assessed using biochemical testing or clinical assessment.

Studies focusing exclusively on highly specific injury subpopulations were carefully evaluated for inclusion. While such studies were included when they met the predefined eligibility criteria, their potential to limit generalizability to broader trauma populations was considered in the interpretation of results.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria were: (1) All other methodological studies than prospective cohort studies. (2) Studies that investigated long-term or chronic drinking/intoxication or alcohol habits. (3) Publications from studies outside the ED (e.g. Nursing homes, music festivals etc.) (4) Publications studying law issues, interventions/prevention or test machines/screen tools for alcohol or drug intoxication. (5) Patient age under 15. (6) Overdoses/self-poisoning presentations. (Overdose was defined as presentations primarily related to toxicological or pharmacological effects requiring medical treatment (e.g., respiratory depression, cardiovascular instability), rather than trauma-related injury.) (7) Intoxications with herbal drugs or publications that studies polypharmacy in trauma patients. (8) There was no valid method of testing if the study participants were intoxicated (Eg. The patient’s own statement, with no clinical or biochemical testing).

Studies with insufficient information to assess eligibility were excluded.

Data extraction

Data was extracted by both reviewers from the included articles following the PRISMA 2020 check-list for systematic reviews and meta-analyses [9]. The PRISMA checklist is provided in Supplementary Material 2. A standard extraction sheet was made by one reviewer and the following information was extracted from each study: Title, author and year of publication, study area/country, study objective, sample size and sample age. In studies where applicable, the following characteristics were extracted additionally: Method used for alcohol and/or blood screening, alcohol positive patients, drug positive patients, factors significantly associated with trauma and severity score.

Furthermore, data on trauma severity and outcome in intoxicated patients as opposed to sober patients was collected in studies where possible in order to compare the effect of intoxication on the severity and outcome of the trauma. The data of the studies comparing these two groups of patients were collected in tables for simplicity of the data.

Where applicable, factors that were significantly associated with a greater risk of trauma due to intoxication were noted due to the relevance of clinical practice.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias for the included studies was evaluated by the two reviewers. Risk of bias was assessed using domains relevant to observational cohort studies, including confounding, selection bias, exposure classification, and outcome measurement.

Statistical analysis

Due to substantial heterogeneity in study populations, and outcome measures, a formal meta-analysis was not performed. Quantitative data are presented descriptively to illustrate the distribution of findings across studies. Forest plots were constructed to provide a visual overview of study-level estimates and the direction of associations. These plots are presented for descriptive purposes only and should not be interpreted as pooled effect estimates.

The studies that gave continuous data as the mean ISS and the Standard Deviation (SD) were used for comparison by Standard Mean Difference.

Proportions of male sex and the event of having a severe ISS was given as dichotomous data in some of the studies and they were compared by Risk Ratio. The statistical analyses were made using MetaAnalysisOnline. Formal test of heterogeneity was made with I2.

Results

Search results

Medline gave 1,200 results and Embase gave 1,388 results. Duplicate records were removed and 2,132 articles went on to the screening process.

After screening by title, 1,552 records were excluded, reducing the number of records from 2,132 to 580. The remaining 580 records were then screened by abstract. 492 studies were excluded by both reviewers, leaving eighty-eight articles left for Full-text reading.

Full-text review was conducted in December 2025 and eighteen articles were included for analysis.

Study characteristics

Out of 2,132 articles from the database searches, a total of eighteen studies were included for review and data extraction. The characteristics for each study are shown in Table 1. There was a total sample size of 29,817 patients in the included studies, samples ranging from 69 to 12,451 patients in each study. Eleven studies tested all included patients for alcohol and/or drugs [10, 13–16, 18–21, 24, 26], while seven studies only tested a subgroup of the patients [11, 12, 17, 22, 23, 25, 27] and thus all studies examined the prevalence of patients under the influence. The testing methods and prevalence of each study is shown in Table 2. The prevalence ranged from 8.3% to 65.9% of patients intoxicated with alcohol, and 14.5% to 22.1% of patients being intoxicated by drugs, and 6% to 38.8% of patients intoxicated by drugs and alcohol in combination. The average prevalence of patients intoxicated with alcohol was 24.7% (range 8.3–65.9%), the average prevalence of drug intoxication was 17.3% (range 14.5–22.1%) and the average prevalence of patients intoxicated with alcohol and drugs was 13.1% (range 6.0–38.8%). Reporting of specific substances varied considerably across studies, and substance-specific comparisons (e.g., alcohol versus cannabis or cocaine) were not consistently feasible.

Assessment of risk of bias

All included studies were prospective cohort studies and therefore inherently subject to risk of bias, particularly related to confounding and exposure classification. According to the ROBINS-I framework, most studies would be considered at least at moderate risk of bias, as none were comparable to a high-quality randomized trial.

Confounding was a major concern across studies. Important factors such as injury mechanism, speed, use of safety equipment, comorbidities, and risk-taking behavior were inconsistently measured or adjusted for, limiting causal interpretation of differences in injury severity or outcomes.

Exposure misclassification represented another important source of bias. While most studies used biochemical testing to assess alcohol and/or drug intoxication, some relied partly on clinical assessment and self-report. In two studies, not all patients underwent biochemical testing for alcohol and/or psychotropic drugs [21, 26]. In one study [26], intoxication status was based on physical examination and history-taking, whereas the other study [21] combined self-report with clinical signs. These studies were retained, as classification was not based solely on patient self-report. Such misclassification may have biased results toward the null.

Selection bias was also possible, as severely injured or unconscious patients may have been more likely to undergo biochemical screening. Outcome assessment was generally robust, as injury severity scores and mortality are objective measures, reducing the risk of outcome misclassification.

Trauma severity

All included studies assessed injury severity using Injury Severity Score–based measures (ISS, NISS, or ISS90). Fifteen studies reported mean ISS values for intoxicated and non-intoxicated patients [10, 11, 13–22, 24, 25, 27], while five studies categorized patients according to injury severity thresholds [12, 16, 22, 23, 26].

Reported mean ISS among intoxicated patients ranged from 7.2 to 24.6, compared with 3.3 to 31.7 among non-intoxicated patients. Nine studies reported higher mean ISS or a higher proportion of severely injured patients among intoxicated individuals [13–18, 22, 24, 27], whereas six studies reported lower mean ISS among intoxicated patients compared with non-intoxicated patients [10, 11, 19–21, 25]. In one study, injury severity differed by substance type, with higher ISS observed in patients intoxicated by drugs alone, but lower ISS in patients intoxicated with alcohol alone or in combination with drugs [22].

Among studies categorizing injury severity, the proportion of intoxicated patients in the “severe injury” group ranged from 5.5% to 46.6%, compared with 7.3% to 35.0% among non-intoxicated patients. The forest plot in Fig. 3 shows the results of the five studies dividing the patients after mild and severe ISS. Two studies reported a higher proportion of intoxicated patients in the severe injury group [16, 23] while two studies reported a slightly higher proportion among non-intoxicated patients [12, 26]. One study demonstrated higher severity among patients intoxicated by drugs alone, but lower severity among patients intoxicated with alcohol alone or combined alcohol and drugs compared with sober patients [22].

Trauma outcome

Seven studies found the length of hospital stay to be increased in intoxicated patients [10, 13, 17, 18, 20, 24, 25], whereas one study found no difference [12] and one reported shorter hospital stays among the intoxicated patients [11]. Two studies reported longer ICU stay in intoxicated patients [13, 18] while two studies reported shorter ICU stays [10, 20]. One study [21] found that intoxicated patients had fewer complaints at 2 weeks post-injury and that they had a better outcome 6 months after the trauma when compared to sober patients, both results were statistically significant.

Mortality outcomes were inconsistent. Five studies reported lower mortality among intoxicated patients [10, 11, 20, 25, 26], whereas four studies reported a higher mortality in intoxicated patients [15, 16, 18, 24]. Several studies [10, 15, 18, 25, 27] noted that patients intoxicated with alcohol often presented with lower GCS and/or reduced systolic blood pressure, alongside higher ISS, compared to sober patients.

Factors significantly associated with trauma due to intoxication

As shown in Table 3 and in the forest plot in Fig. 4, the main factor associated with trauma due to intoxication by alcohol and/or psychotropic drugs was male sex. Eight out of the eighteen studies found that male sex was significantly associated with admission due to intoxication [14–18, 21, 26, 27], and another six studies found a higher prevalence of men in the intoxication group even though it was not statistically significant [10, 13, 19, 22, 24, 25]. Another factor significantly associated with trauma due to intoxication seen in eight studies [11, 15, 16, 18, 19, 21, 26, 27] was younger age. Other significant factors for trauma due to intoxication included lower BMI, not wearing a seat belt or other safety equipment, being the one at fault for the motor vehicle collision (MVC) and patients with non-collision accidents.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesizes evidence from eighteen prospective cohort studies examining trauma severity and outcomes among patients intoxicated with alcohol and/or psychotropic drugs presenting to emergency departments. Intoxication was common, with substantial heterogeneity observed across studies that likely reflect differences in study populations, injury mechanisms, definitions of intoxication, and screening methods for alcohol and drugs. Because of this heterogeneity, the quantitative analyses in the forest plots in Figs. 2, 3 and 4 should be interpreted as descriptive rather than confirmatory.

The average prevalence across studies was 24.7% (range 8.3–65.9%) for alcohol-related cases, 17.3% (range 14.5–22.1%) for drug-related cases, and 13.1% (range 6.0–38.8%) for combined intoxications.

The association between intoxication and injury severity was inconsistent. Nine studies reported higher ISS or a greater proportion of severely injured patients among intoxicated [13–18, 24, 27] while other studies reported no difference or lower injury severity [10, 11, 19–21, 25]. Notably, studies reporting lower ISS among intoxicated patients generally demonstrated small and statistically insignificant differences, whereas studies reporting higher ISS often showed more pronounced disparities, suggesting potential clinical relevance.

Substance-specific effects may partly explain these conflicting findings. One study demonstrated higher injury severity among patients intoxicated with drugs alone, but lower severity among those intoxicated with alcohol alone or in combination with drugs [22]. This finding supports the hypothesis that psychotropic drugs and alcohol may be associated with distinct injury mechanisms and risk profiles, underscoring the importance of stratified analyses by substance type.

The included studies were conducted across a range of geographic regions, including Europe, North America, and Asia. Differences in trauma systems, alcohol legislation, substance use patterns, and prehospital care may contribute to variation in both the prevalence of intoxication and its association with injury severity. These regional differences may partly explain the heterogeneity observed across studies and limit direct comparability.

Another important source of variability relates to differences in how intoxication is assessed and documented across healthcare systems in the included reports. In some settings, biochemical confirmation is routinely performed, whereas in other included studies, intoxication is partly assessed clinically or based on self-report. These differences may be influenced by resource availability and medico-legal considerations and may affect both the reported prevalence of intoxication and its association with trauma severity.

Trauma outcomes also varied widely. Seven studies [10, 13, 17, 18, 20, 24, 25] reported longer hospital stays for intoxicated patients. However, one study [11] observed shorter hospital stays, and another study [12] found no significant difference between groups.

Male sex and younger age were consistently associated with intoxicated trauma presentations [11, 14–17, 19, 21, 26, 27], aligning with existing literature linking substance use and risk-taking behavior to injury risk. Additional factors such as lack of safety equipment and non-collision injury mechanisms further suggest that behavioral and situational factors play an important role.

Mortality outcomes were similarly inconsistent. Four studies [15, 16, 18, 24] identified higher mortality rates in intoxicated patients, with only one [18] demonstrating statistical significance. Five studies [10, 11, 20, 25, 26] reported lower mortality rates among intoxicated patients, though these findings were not statistically significant.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies, with I² values ranging from 91% to 99% in the forest plots (Figs. 2, 3 and 4), precluding meaningful pooling of results and limiting the interpretability of summary estimates. Several included studies focused on specific injury subgroups, particularly traumatic brain injury, which may differ mechanistically and clinically from general trauma populations. This may limit the generalizability of the findings and introduces potential bias when comparing injury severity across studies. Intoxication may influence injury patterns differently depending on the mechanism of injury, and pooling findings across heterogeneous populations may obscure these differences. In addition, injury severity scores such as ISS are not linear measures, which complicates direct comparison across studies. Some studies also reported non-clinical primary outcomes, further contributing to variability in findings. Given these factors, quantitative synthesis was not considered appropriate, and the results are therefore presented as a descriptive review. Visual summaries are included to illustrate the distribution and direction of findings across studies, but no pooled estimates are derived.

Second, incomplete reporting of injury severity scores in some studies restricted their inclusion in quantitative analyses, as ISS was not always a predefined primary outcome. Several included studies were conducted in earlier decades, prior to the widespread implementation of modern trauma systems and advanced imaging. This may limit the external validity of the findings to contemporary trauma care settings.

Despite their prospective design, the included studies remain vulnerable to bias. Most were judged to be at least at moderate risk of bias, primarily due to residual confounding and potential exposure misclassification. Key confounders—including injury mechanism, use of safety equipment, comorbidities, and risk-taking behavior—were inconsistently accounted for, complicating causal interpretation of the observed associations between intoxication and trauma severity.

Variation in exposure assessment, with some studies relying partly on clinical evaluation or self-report rather than systematic biochemical testing, may have introduced non-differential misclassification and bias toward the null. In some healthcare systems, formal documentation of intoxication requires biochemical confirmation, whereas clinical assessment alone may carry medico-legal implications. Variability in testing practices across trauma centers may therefore affect both study comparability and reported prevalence. Selection bias may also have occurred, as patients with more severe injuries were more likely to be tested.

Outcome assessment was generally robust, as injury severity scores, length of hospital stay, and mortality are objective measures. The risk of publication bias appears limited, as several included studies reported null or conflicting findings. Due to the limited number of studies included in quantitative analyses, formal assessment of publication bias (e.g., funnel plot) was not performed.

The inclusion of studies identified through an additional search performed at a later stage introduces a potential limitation, as the timing of this search may have influenced study selection. Although efforts were made to maintain consistency in the screening process, this factor should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Future studies should aim to disentangle the independent effect of intoxication from key confounders, including injury mechanism, use of safety equipment, and pre-existing comorbidities. Large, multicenter prospective cohorts with standardized biochemical assessment of alcohol and drug exposure, as well as uniform reporting of injury severity and outcomes, are needed. Stratified analyses by substance type may be particularly important, as the present review suggests differential effects between alcohol and drugs.

The findings of this review have several implications for clinical practice in emergency departments. Intoxication may influence triage decisions and diagnostic evaluation. Clinicians may be more likely to perform additional imaging or prolonged observation in intoxicated patients because clinical examination may be less reliable. This can increase resource utilization and length of stay in the emergency department. Another important implication is the risk of missed injuries. Symptoms of traumatic brain injury, internal bleeding, or other serious conditions may initially be attributed to intoxication, potentially delaying diagnosis. Emergency clinicians should therefore maintain a high index of suspicion when evaluating intoxicated trauma patients.

While previous reviews have reported inconsistent associations between intoxication and injury severity, this study adds to the literature by highlighting how heterogeneity in study populations, regional practices, and intoxication assessment methods may contribute to these inconsistencies. This emphasizes the need for more standardized approaches in future research.

Conclusion

Intoxicated patients under the influence of alcohol or drugs account for a substantial proportion of trauma cases in EDs, with an overall prevalence of 24.7% (range 8.3–65.9%). The prevalence of trauma patients intoxicated by a combination of alcohol and drugs was 13.1% (range 6–38.8%), emphasizing the widespread impact of substance use on emergency care.

This systematic review demonstrates inconsistent evidence regarding the association between intoxication and trauma severity. While several studies in this review reported higher injury severity among intoxicated patients, others found no difference or lower severity compared to non-intoxicated patients. Importantly, studies included in this review reporting higher severity among intoxicated patients showed more consistent and clinically relevant differences.

Lastly, this review found that male sex and younger age were consistently reported as significantly associated with intoxicated trauma presentations across several included studies.
