Metastatic gestational trophoblastic neoplasia presenting initially as intraparenchymal and subdural hematoma: a case report
Authors: Selemon Gebrezgabiher Asgedom, Dejen Tekiea Gebrewahd, Thomas Bogale Megerssa, Seare Halefom Kahsay, Dinksira Bekele Deneke, Kedir Dekebi Guduru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGestational trophoblastic neoplasia is a rare but highly chemosensitive pregnancy-related malignancy with a strong tendency for hematogenous spread and hemorrhagic metastasis. Brain involvement occurs in a minority of patients and may present as life-threatening intracranial hemorrhage.Case presentationA 40-year-old Ethiopian woman presented with a sudden-onset severe headache, focal seizures, vomiting, and progressive right-sided weakness. She had a history of spontaneous abortion five months prior. Neuroimaging revealed a left frontoparietal intraparenchymal hemorrhagic mass with surrounding edema and a concomitant holohemispheric subacute subdural hematoma causing mass effect. Serum β-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) levels were elevated to 1500 IU/L. Although lower than the levels typically observed in disseminated choriocarcinoma, modest β-hCG elevations may occur in partially regressed primary lesions or in isolated metastatic disease. The patient underwent urgent craniotomy with evacuation of the subdural hematoma and resection of a hemorrhagic intraparenchymal metastatic lesion. Histopathological examination confirmed a diagnosis of metastatic choriocarcinoma. Staging evaluation revealed pulmonary and uterine involvement, consistent with International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage IV disease, with a World Health Organization prognostic score of 11. She received multi-agent chemotherapy with progressive neurological recovery and normalization of β-hCG levels during follow-up.ConclusionSimultaneous intraparenchymal and subdural hemorrhages can represent a rare initial presentation of metastatic gestational trophoblastic neoplasia, even in the absence of gynecologic symptoms. Prompt measurement of serum β-hCG levels is essential for early diagnosis of unexplained intracranial hemorrhage in women of reproductive age. Early neurosurgical intervention combined with timely multi-agent chemotherapy can result in favorable outcomes, even in high-risk stage IV disease with brain metastasis.

Background

Gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (GTN) is a rare group of malignant disorders arising from the trophoblastic tissue of the placenta and includes invasive mole, choriocarcinoma, placental site trophoblastic tumors, and epithelioid trophoblastic tumors. Despite its aggressive biological behavior, GTN remains one of the most curable gynecological malignancies owing to its high sensitivity to chemotherapy and the availability of serum β-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) as a reliable diagnostic and surveillance marker[1, 2].

Survival outcomes depend on the disease stage and risk stratification. In low-risk disease, cure rates exceed 90–95%, whereas even in high-risk GTN, survival rates approach 80–90% with appropriate multi-agent chemotherapy [3, 4]. Central nervous system (CNS) involvement occurs in approximately 10–20% of patients with metastatic GTN. However, brain metastasis is a high-risk feature associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Nevertheless, advances in combined-modality treatments, including systemic chemotherapy, neurosurgical intervention, and radiotherapy, in selected cases, have significantly improved outcomes [2–5].

Choriocarcinoma, the most aggressive subtype of GTN, is characterized by rapid hematogenous dissemination, with the lungs, vagina, liver, and brain being the most common sites of metastasis [6, 7]. Brain metastases from GTN have a marked propensity for hemorrhage because of aggressive vascular invasion, endothelial destruction, and formation of fragile neoplastic vessels prone to rupture [8, 9]. Intracranial hemorrhage may be the initial clinical manifestation of metastatic GTN, particularly in women of reproductive age with a recent history of pregnancy, miscarriage, or molar gestation. Such presentations often mimic primary cerebrovascular events, leading to a delayed diagnosis [10–12]. Subdural hematoma in the context of metastatic choriocarcinoma is rare and is thought to result from tumor invasion of dural vessels, rupture of fragile neoplastic microvasculature within the subdural space, or bleeding from tumor-infiltrated cortical or bridging veins [5, 8]. The simultaneous involvement of multiple intracranial compartments, including both intraparenchymal and subdural spaces, is exceedingly uncommon and poses significant diagnostic challenges [8, 13, 14].

Herein, we report a rare case of metastatic gestational trophoblastic neoplasia presenting with simultaneous intraparenchymal and subdural hematomas in a previously healthy woman with a history of spontaneous abortion.

Case presentation and management

Patient history and physical findings

A 40-year-old Ethiopian woman, previously healthy with no known chronic medical conditions, presented to the emergency department with a sudden onset of severe global headache. The headache was described as abrupt onset and being intense without preceding warning symptoms. Shortly after the onset of headache, the patient developed abnormal involuntary movements involving the right upper limb, suggestive of focal seizure activity. This was accompanied by three episodes of projectile vomiting of ingested material without hematemesis.

Over the subsequent two days, the patient experienced progressive weakness of the right side of the body, prompting presentation to the hospital. There was no history of loss of consciousness at onset. According to family members, the patient was in her usual state of health before an acute neurological event.

Her obstetric history was notable for nulliparity and spontaneous abortion five months after her presentation, for which no β-hCG monitoring or gynecologic follow-up was performed after the spontaneous abortion, and the patient denied abnormal vaginal bleeding following the event. She denied a history of persistent vaginal bleeding, amenorrhea, abdominal pain, abdominal distension, or symptoms suggestive of metastatic disease. There was no history of chronic headache, cough, hemoptysis, or the use of anticoagulants or antiplatelet medications. She had no history of malignancy, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy. Her family history was noncontributory.

On physical examination, the patient was alert but uncomfortable. Vital signs were within the normal range, with no evidence of fever, hypotension, or respiratory compromise. Cardiovascular and respiratory examinations were unremarkable. Neurological examination revealed a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 14/15 (E4V4M6). The cranial nerve was intact, with no facial asymmetry or visual field deficits. Mild aphasia was observed. Motor examination demonstrated right-sided hemiparesis with Medical Research Council (MRC) grade 4/5 strength. Increased tone and hyperreflexia were present on the right side, with an extensor plantar response consistent with upper motor neuron involvement. The sensory examination was intact bilaterally, and no cerebellar signs were observed. Examination of other systems, including abdominal and pelvic examinations, did not reveal any palpable masses or organomegaly.

Investigations and staging

Complete blood counts, renal and liver function tests, serum electrolyte levels, and coagulation profiles were all within normal limits. There was no evidence of anemia, thrombocytopenia, coagulopathy, or metabolic derangement.

Noncontrast brain CT confirmed a hyperdense-to-isodense left holohemispheric subdural collection causing a significant mass effect with a midline shift of approximately 5.7 mm at the level of the septum pellucidum (Fig. 1a). In addition, the noncontrast CT revealed a left frontotemporal intraparenchymal hyperdense lesion measuring 4.3 × 5.1 × 4 cm, with perilesional hypodensity indicative of vasogenic edema, suggestive of hemorrhagic metastasis (Fig. 1b). Considering the hemorrhagic intraparenchymal lesion suspected to be metastatic, the patient’s reproductive age, and history of spontaneous abortion, serum β-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) was measured and found to be 1500 IU/L (reference range: < 20 IU/L), raising suspicion for gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD). Although the β-hCG level was lower than that typically reported in metastatic choriocarcinoma, it remained significantly elevated in nonpregnant women. Lower β-hCG levels have been reported in cases of isolated brain metastases or partially regressed primary lesions, which may explain the relatively modest elevation observed in this patient [11, 15].

Comprehensive systemic staging was done using contrast-enhanced chest CT, which demonstrated multiple well-defined bilateral pulmonary nodules consistent with metastatic deposits. Abdominopelvic CT revealed an enlarged uterus with a heterogeneously enhancing anterior myometrial mass containing central necrosis, and a second posterior myometrial mass with no evidence of liver or other intra-abdominal metastases.

Based on the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) staging, the patient was classified as having Stage IV gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (GTN) with brain metastasis and a WHO prognostic score of 11, indicating high-risk GTN. Management and outcome

Based on clinical presentation and neuroimaging findings consistent with a left frontoparietal intra-axial hemorrhagic mass lesion with an associated left holohemispheric late subacute subdural hematoma, the patient was admitted for urgent neurosurgical intervention. Differential diagnoses included hemorrhagic intracranial neoplasms and vascular pathologies.

Preoperatively, the patient was administered intravenous phenytoin for seizure prophylaxis and managed with appropriate analgesia and supportive care. After informed consent was obtained, the patient was transferred to the operating theater for surgical management.

Left frontoparietal craniotomy was performed. Upon dural opening, approximately 80 mL of dark hemolyzed fluid was evacuated under high pressure, which was consistent with a late subacute subdural hematoma. The subdural space was thoroughly irrigated with normal saline, resulting in a gradual re-expansion of the brain. Meticulous hemostasis was achieved and no organized mass lesions were identified within the subdural compartment.

Subsequently, a limited cortical incision (corticotomy) was made over the left frontoparietal region to access the intra-axial component. The intracerebral hematoma was carefully evacuated using saline irrigation and bipolar coagulation. Following hematoma evacuation, an underlying gray-to-whitish intraparenchymal mass measuring approximately 2 cm × 2.5 cm × 3 cm, with soft-to-firm consistency and moderate to high vascularity, was identified in the left frontoparietal region.

Gross total excision of the mass was achieved while carefully preserving the surrounding brain parenchyma. Hemostasis was secured. The dural layer was closed primarily, the bone flap was replaced and fixed, and the scalp was closed in anatomical layers. The estimated intraoperative blood loss was 750 ml. Postoperatively, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for neurological monitoring and managed with mannitol and bed elevation to decrease the ICP. She was successfully extubated on the day of the surgery without immediate complications.

The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful. She demonstrated marked improvement in mental status, with resolution of confusion and progressive recovery of motor strength on the right side during serial neurological assessments. No postoperative seizures or neurological deficits were observed in any of the patients. Postoperative computed tomography (CT) revealed complete resolution of the subdural hematoma and the associated intracerebral hemorrhage (Fig. 3). Histopathological examination of the excised intracranial mass (Fig. 4a–e) revealed biphasic trophoblastic proliferation composed of cytotrophoblasts and multinucleated syncytiotrophoblasts with extensive hemorrhage, necrosis, vascular invasion, and intravascular tumor emboli. No chorionic villi were identified, which supported the diagnosis of metastatic choriocarcinoma. Immunohistochemical staining was not performed because of resource limitations. Following neurosurgical recovery, the patient was initiated on multi-agent chemotherapy using the EMA-CO regimen administered in standard alternating cycles for high-risk GTN. Serial β-hCG levels showed a progressive decline and normalization after the initiation of chemotherapy. At the six-month follow-up, the patient remained neurologically stable, with independent ambulation and no recurrent seizures. Her modified Rankin Scale score improved to 1. Follow-up neuroimaging demonstrated no recurrent intracranial lesions or hemorrhages, and serial β-hCG levels normalized after the completion of chemotherapy. Discussion

Gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (GTN) is a rare pregnancy-related malignancy arising from the trophoblastic tissue, with choriocarcinoma representing its most aggressive form [1, 16]. Although the overall prognosis of GTN is excellent with modern chemotherapy, metastatic disease develops in approximately 15–20% of cases, most commonly involving the lungs, followed by the vagina, liver, and brain [3, 6–8]. Central nervous system (CNS) metastasis occurs in 10–20% of metastatic GTN cases and is defined as FIGO stage IV disease [5, 16]. Historically associated with high mortality, survival has improved substantially with multi-agent chemotherapy, with cure rates of 80–90% in high-risk GTN and 70–85% in patients with brain metastases when treated aggressively [4, 5, 17].

Choriocarcinoma is characterized by biphasic cytotrophoblast and syncytiotrophoblastic proliferation, marked vascular invasion, extensive hemorrhage, and necrosis [6, 8]. Its propensity for hematogenous dissemination and vascular destruction explains the high frequency of hemorrhagic brain metastases. The proposed mechanisms of intracranial bleeding include endothelial invasion, tumor embolization, and pseudoaneurysm formation, with subsequent rupture [9, 12]. Intraparenchymal hemorrhage is the most frequent neurological manifestation of metastatic choriocarcinoma [10–13]. In contrast, subdural hematoma is rare and is thought to result from invasion of dural vessels or rupture of fragile neoplastic microvasculature within the subdural space [5, 8]. Similar cases of metastatic GTN presenting with multiple intracranial hemorrhages have rarely been reported [13, 14, 18]. However, simultaneous intraparenchymal and subdural hemorrhage remains uncommon. Compared with previously reported cases, our patient demonstrated a favorable neurological recovery following prompt neurosurgical decompression and early initiation of multi-agent chemotherapy.

Intracranial hemorrhage, the initial manifestation of GTN, poses a significant diagnostic challenge because it often mimics the primary cerebrovascular events [10–12]. Patients typically present with acute headaches, seizures, focal deficits, or signs of increased intracranial pressure, frequently without gynecologic symptoms [8, 10]. In our case, the patient presented with a sudden, severe headache, focal seizures, vomiting, and progressive hemiparesis, with no persistent vaginal bleeding and no known molar pregnancy. The only antecedent event was a spontaneous abortion five months earlier. Neuroimaging demonstrated a hemorrhagic intra-axial lesion at the gray–white matter junction with surrounding edema, suggestive of metastasis, but the presence of a concurrent subdural hematoma broadened the differential diagnosis. The absence of vascular abnormalities on CT angiography further supports a nonvascular etiology.

Measurement of serum β-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) levels is critical for raising the suspicion of GTN. Although markedly elevated β-hCG levels are typical in metastatic choriocarcinoma, lower levels may occur in cases with necrotic or partially regressed primary lesions [11, 15], and rare β-hCG–negative cases have been reported [15]. In reproductive-aged women presenting with unexplained hemorrhagic brain lesions, early β-hCG testing is essential to avoid diagnostic delays [10, 16].

Management of brain metastases from GTN requires a multidisciplinary approach that combines neurosurgical and systemic therapies. Surgical intervention was indicated because of the significant mass effect, progressive neurological deterioration, diagnostic uncertainty, and need for hemorrhage control. In our patient, craniotomy allowed evacuation of both hematomas, relief of intracranial pressure, and gross total excision of the hemorrhagic metastasis, resulting in rapid neurological improvement and histopathological confirmation. Systemic chemotherapy is the cornerstone of the treatment. High-risk GTN (WHO score ≥ 7) is treated with a multi-agent regimen, EMA-CO, which has significantly improved survival outcomes [3, 4, 16]. A recent series reported a long-term survival rate exceeding 70% in patients with brain metastases managed with aggressive chemotherapy and supportive neurosurgical care [5].

Brain metastasis is an adverse prognostic factor that is defined as stage IV disease [16]. Additional poor prognostic indicators include a high WHO score, markedly elevated β-hCG levels, liver metastasis, and a prolonged interval since antecedent pregnancy. Despite these risk factors, the outcomes have dramatically improved in the modern era of chemotherapy [5, 13]. Compared with previously reported cases of multiple intracranial hematomas, [13, 14] our patient had a favorable early outcome, likely due to prompt decompression, absence of liver metastasis, and early initiation of multi-agent chemotherapy.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that metastatic gestational trophoblastic neoplasia may initially present with simultaneous intraparenchymal and subdural hematomas, even in the absence of gynecological symptoms. In women of reproductive age presenting with unexplained intracranial hemorrhage, early measurement of serum β-human chorionic gonadotropin levels is essential to avoid diagnostic delays. Prompt neurosurgical intervention in the presence of a mass effect or neurological deterioration can be life-saving and facilitate definitive diagnosis. Early initiation of multi-agent chemotherapy offers a high likelihood of remission, even in stage IV disease with brain metastasis. Timely recognition and coordinated multidisciplinary management are critical to achieve favorable outcomes in this curable but life-threatening condition.
