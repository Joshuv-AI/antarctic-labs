Circulating kidney injury molecule-1 as a prognostic biomarker for long-term renal outcomes in lupus nephritis: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Keisuke Sako, Yoshitada Toyota, Shiori Nakagawa, Daichi Yomogida, Misaki Yoshida, Akari Takeji, Takahiro Yuasa, Akihiko Koshino, Shunsuke Tsuge, Yuta Yamamura, Megumi Oshima, Satoshi Hara, Kiyoaki Ito, Norihiko Sakai, Miho Shimizu, Takashi Wada, Joseph V Bonventre, Yasunori Iwata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo determine whether circulating kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1) predicts long-term renal outcomes in lupus nephritis (LN), including relapse, progression to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) and mortality.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 63 Japanese patients with biopsy-confirmed LN who were treated at Kanazawa University Hospital between 1990 and 2022. After excluding 17 patients who did not meet the eligibility criteria, 46 patients were included in the final analysis. Circulating KIM-1 levels were measured in serum samples collected at the time of renal biopsy prior to treatment or additional treatment. Patients were stratified into low-KIM-1 and high-KIM-1 groups using the median value (153 pg/mL) as the cut-off. The primary endpoints were LN relapse, ESKD and all-cause mortality. Survival was analysed using Kaplan-Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 127 months, nine patients (20%) experienced relapse, four (9%) developed ESKD and six (13%) died. The high-KIM-1 group had lower baseline renal function, greater proteinuria, a higher frequency of Class IV histology and higher activity index scores. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated significantly lower LN relapse-free (p=0.027; log-rank) and ESKD-free survival (p=0.049) in the high-KIM-1 group. In the multivariable Cox regression analysis, circulating KIM-1 was independently associated with LN relapse (per-IQR HR: 1.92, 95% CI 1.07 to 3.45; p=0.029).ConclusionElevated circulating KIM-1 at the time of renal biopsy was associated with adverse long-term renal outcomes in LN and independently associated with relapse, although its incremental predictive value beyond conventional clinical markers was modest. KIM-1 may support early risk stratification and individualised treatment; however, prospective validation in larger cohorts is needed.Keywords: Lupus Nephritis, Mortality, Risk Factors

Introduction

SLE is a chronic autoimmune disease characterised by loss of self-tolerance, the production of diverse autoantibodies and immune complex-mediated inflammation affecting multiple organs.1 Lupus nephritis (LN) is one of its most severe manifestations, affecting 25–60% of patients with SLE.2 Over recent decades, advances in immunosuppressive therapy, including glucocorticoids combined with cyclophosphamide or mycophenolate mofetil, have significantly improved short-term renal outcomes.3 The addition of calcineurin inhibitors to standard regimens has further increased remission rates and accelerated proteinuria reduction.4 5 Nevertheless, long-term renal prognosis remains suboptimal, with nearly 20% of patients progressing to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) within 10 years of diagnosis.6

Even among patients who achieve clinical remission, sustained renal preservation is not guaranteed, largely because of recurrent flares and cumulative treatment-related toxicity.7–9 Patients who only achieve partial or no remission face an even higher risk of progressing to ESKD.10 These challenges underscore the need to identify reliable biomarkers capable of predicting LN outcomes early in the disease course and supporting individualised treatment strategies.11

Conventional biomarkers, including proteinuria, serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), anti-double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) antibodies and serum complement levels (C3 and C4), are routinely used to monitor LN activity and treatment response.12 Current international guidelines recommend regular assessment using these parameters.13–15 However, these biomarkers have important limitations. Proteinuria does not reliably distinguish active inflammation from chronic structural damage.16 Anti-dsDNA titres and complement levels correlate inconsistently with renal activity.17 Moreover, many conventional biomarkers become abnormal only after significant renal injury has occurred, limiting their sensitivity for detecting early or subclinical disease activity. Although renal biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosis and histological classification, its invasive nature precludes repeated use and limits its utility for longitudinal monitoring.11

Kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1) is a type 1 transmembrane glycoprotein expressed on proximal tubular epithelial cells.18 It is minimally expressed in normal kidney tissue, but is markedly upregulated following ischaemic or toxic injury.19 Urinary KIM-1 has been extensively studied as an early diagnostic marker of acute kidney injury (AKI)20 and has demonstrated predictive value for chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression.21 In LN, previous studies have primarily examined urinary KIM-1 as a marker of disease activity.22–24 However, urinary KIM-1 concentrations may be influenced by urine dilution and collection variability, resulting in measurement variability.25 In contrast, circulating KIM-1 reflects systemic concentrations, is not subject to urinary dilution or normalisation, and may reflect integrated kidney production over time.26 In CKD populations, circulating KIM-1 is associated with tubular dysfunction and predicts adverse renal outcomes.26 27 However, the prognostic significance of circulating KIM-1 in LN remains insufficiently explored, particularly in relation to long-term renal outcomes.

Several clinical and histopathological factors influence long-term renal outcomes in LN, including histological class (with Class IV associated with the poorest prognosis), chronicity index, impaired baseline renal function, hypertension, delayed treatment initiation and failure to achieve remission.28 29 However, many of these predictors require renal biopsy or prolonged follow-up, limiting their utility for early risk stratification. Easily accessible serum biomarkers could enhance prognostic assessment at diagnosis and, through minimally invasive blood sampling, facilitate earlier treatment decisions.

The aim of this study was to evaluate whether circulating KIM-1, measured at the time of renal biopsy, independently predicts long-term renal outcomes in LN. Specifically, we examined the association between circulating KIM-1 concentrations at biopsy and subsequent LN relapse, progression to ESKD requiring maintenance haemodialysis and all-cause mortality in a retrospective cohort design.

Methods

Study design and patients

This single-centre retrospective cohort study included 63 Japanese patients with SLE who underwent renal biopsy at Kanazawa University Hospital between 1990 and 2022. The date of renal biopsy was defined as the study entry point. Patients were followed up until 31 October 2025. Patients were included if they (1) fulfilled at least four of the 1997 American College of Rheumatology classification criteria for SLE,30 (2) had histologically confirmed LN on renal biopsy, (3) had available pretreatment stored serum samples and (4) had accessible medical records. Patients were excluded if they (1) had fewer than five scorable glomeruli in the biopsy specimen or (2) had coexisting glomerular pathologies not attributable to LN (figure 1). In total, 63 patients were assessed for eligibility, of whom 17 were excluded based on predefined criteria, resulting in 46 patients included in the final analysis. As this was an exploratory retrospective cohort study, no a priori sample size calculation was performed, and the sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients with available stored serum samples during the study period.

Clinical data collection

Clinical and laboratory data were retrospectively extracted from medical records. Baseline parameters recorded at the time of renal biopsy included age, sex and comorbidities (eg, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, diabetes mellitus and antiphospholipid syndrome). Treatment-related variables included the initial glucocorticoid dose and use of methylprednisolone pulse therapy. Laboratory parameters included serum creatinine, serum albumin, serum complement levels (C3, C4 and 50% haemolytic complement activity (CH50)), and anti-dsDNA titres. eGFR was calculated using the three-variable Japanese equation developed by Matsuo et al: eGFR (mL/min/1.73 m²) = 194 × serum creatinine (mg/dL)−¹·⁰⁹⁴ × age (years)−⁰·²⁸⁷ × 0.739 (if female).31 Urinary protein excretion was assessed by calculating the urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio (g/g Cr) from spot urine specimens. Disease activity was evaluated using the SLE Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K).32 The use of each immunosuppressive therapy was recorded as a binary indicator. The biopsy-year-based era (pre-2006 vs 2006–2022) was used for sensitivity analysis. No missing data were present for the primary exposure (circulating KIM-1) or primary outcome variables. For variables with missing data, affected cases were excluded from relevant sub-analyses.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoints were LN relapse, progression to ESKD and all-cause mortality. LN relapse was defined, after achievement of complete remission, as any of the following: (1) doubling of the urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio to >0.5 g/g Cr; (2) reappearance of active urinary sediment; or (3) ≥25% increase in serum creatinine. Complete remission was defined as a urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio <0.5 g/g Cr, serum creatinine within 125% of baseline or within the normal range, and normalisation of the urinary sediment.33 ESKD was defined as initiation of maintenance haemodialysis. All patients who required dialysis received maintenance haemodialysis rather than conservative management. Cumulative incidences of LN relapse and ESKD were assessed, with ESKD and death treated as competing risks for LN relapse, and death as the competing risk for ESKD. Remission and relapse were confirmed during routine outpatient follow-up visits.

Histological studies

Renal biopsy specimens were examined by light microscopy. Specimens were fixed in 10% phosphate-buffered formalin (pH 7.4), embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 4 µm thickness. H&E, periodic acid-Schiff, Azan and periodic acid–methenamine silver staining were performed. All specimens were classified according to the 2018 revision of the International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society (ISN/RPS) classification.34 Activity and chronicity indices were assessed using the modified National Institutes of Health (NIH) scoring system.35 Histological assessment followed previously reported institutional protocols.36

Circulating and urinary KIM-1 measurements

Serum and urine samples collected at the time of renal biopsy were centrifuged and stored at −30°C until analysis. Samples were limited to one freeze–thaw cycle before KIM-1 measurement. Circulating and urinary KIM-1 concentrations were measured using commercially available ELISA kits (Human Serum TIM-1/KIM-1/HAVCR Quantikine ELISA Kit, DSKM100; and Human Urinary TIM-1/KIM-1/HAVCR Quantikine ELISA Kit, DKM100; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.37 Urinary KIM-1 concentrations were normalised to urinary creatinine. Urinary creatinine was measured in the same samples at a 10-fold dilution using LabAssay Creatinine (291–93901, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan). Nine patients were excluded from analyses involving urinary KIM-1 because their urine samples were not stored for logistical reasons unrelated to clinical characteristics; therefore, complete-case analysis was performed for urinary biomarker comparisons.

Statistical analyses

To assess the association between circulating KIM-1 levels and clinical outcomes, participants were stratified into low-circulating and high-circulating KIM-1 groups using the median value as the cut-off. To confirm that the findings were not dependent on this dichotomisation, sensitivity analyses based on tertile/quartile (Kaplan-Meier) and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic analysis were also performed. Continuous variables are presented as mean±SD for normally distributed data or median (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables and the χ² or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Correlations of circulating KIM-1 levels with urinary KIM-1 levels and SLEDAI-2K scores were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient, and the concordance between circulating and urinary KIM-1 levels was additionally examined visually using scatterplots. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. The prognostic significance of circulating KIM-1 for LN relapse was analysed using univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. In the multivariable model, circulating KIM-1, serum creatinine and urinary protein were included as clinically relevant covariates.

Sensitivity analyses included a partially prespecified four-variable Cox model (data-informed covariate selection) incorporating circulating KIM-1, serum creatinine, urinary protein and hypertension, as well as penalised Cox regression. HRs for circulating KIM-1 are reported per SD, IQR, doubling and 100 pg/mL (per-IQR as primary); Harrell’s C-statistic and the continuous and three-category net reclassification improvement (NRI; for the three-category index, risk thresholds of 5% and 20%; both evaluated at 36 months), with 95% CIs obtained by bootstrapping. Discrimination of circulating versus urinary KIM-1 was additionally compared head-to-head on the subset with both markers available, again using Harrell’s C-statistic.

Competing-risk analysis was performed using the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards model, with ESKD and death treated as competing events for the LN-relapse endpoint and death as the competing event for the ESKD endpoint. Results are reported as HR with 95% CIs. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p<0.05.

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (V.11; GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA) and EZR (V.1.70; Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University).38 For variables with missing data, complete-case analysis was performed, and no imputation methods were applied. The number and proportion of missing values for each variable are summarised in online supplemental table S1.

Patient and public involvement

This retrospective study used stored samples and medical records. Patients and members of the public were not involved in the study design, conduct, reporting or dissemination.

Hypertension was present in 9 patients (20%), dyslipidaemia in 5 (11%), diabetes mellitus in 2 (4%) and antiphospholipid syndrome in 22 (48%). Prednisolone was administered to 42 patients (91%) at a median initial dose of 30 mg/day (IQR: 20–40 mg/day), and 22 patients (48%) received methylprednisolone pulse therapy.

The median follow-up duration was 127 months (IQR: 41–209 months). During follow-up, LN relapse occurred in nine patients (20%), ESKD developed in four patients (9%) and six patients (13%) died. Among the nine relapse events, the proteinuria criterion was met in seven, urinary sediment in five and ≥25% creatinine rise in five (overlapping).

Comparison of clinical characteristics according to circulating KIM-1 levels

As no established clinical cut-off value for circulating KIM-1 exists, patients were stratified according to the median baseline concentration (153 pg/mL). Circulating KIM-1 levels showed a right-skewed distribution (online supplemental figure S1). The low-KIM-1 group (<153 pg/mL, n=23) had a median concentration of 63 pg/mL (IQR: 46–121 pg/mL), whereas the high-KIM-1 group (≥153 pg/mL, n=23) had a median concentration of 453 pg/mL (IQR: 227–1003 pg/mL; p<0.001).

Compared with the low-KIM-1 group, patients in the high-KIM-1 group were older (median: 42 vs 27 years; p=0.045) and had a lower proportion of females (65% vs 96%; p=0.022). Renal function parameters differed significantly between the groups. The high-KIM-1 group had higher median serum creatinine (0.70 vs 0.55 mg/dL; p=0.003), lower median eGFR (83.6 vs 107.7 mL/min/1.73 m²; p=0.005), higher median urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio (1.95 vs 0.20 g/g Cr; p<0.001) and lower median serum albumin (2.90 vs 3.60 g/dL; p=0.003). Hypertension (35% vs 4%; p=0.022) and dyslipidaemia (22% vs 0%; p=0.049) occurred significantly more frequently in the high-KIM-1 group.

No significant between-group differences were observed in serum complement levels (C3, C4, CH50), anti-dsDNA titres or SLEDAI-2K scores. However, circulating KIM-1 showed a weak-to-moderate positive correlation with the SLEDAI-2K score (r=0.48; p=0.001) (online supplemental figure S2).

As expected, urinary KIM-1 levels, normalised to urinary creatinine, were significantly higher in the high-circulating KIM-1 group than in the low-KIM-1 group (median: 5.86 ng/mg Cr (IQR: 3.56–11.03) vs 1.64 ng/mg Cr (IQR: 1.07–2.65); p<0.001) (table 1). Circulating and urinary KIM-1 levels were strongly positively correlated (r=0.86; p<0.001) (online supplemental figure S3A). Individual-level plots revealed partial discordance between circulating and urinary concentrations, with a subset of patients showing disproportionately high or low urinary KIM-1 values relative to circulating KIM-1 levels (online supplemental figure S3B).

Renal biopsy findings according to the 2018 ISN/RPS classification are summarised in table 2. Class IV (diffuse LN) was more frequent in the high-KIM-1 group (48% vs 13%; p=0.023). The total activity index score was significantly higher in the high-KIM-1 group (median 4 vs 2; p=0.043), particularly for endocapillary hypercellularity (2 vs 1; p=0.022) and cellular or fibrocellular crescents (p=0.040). No significant between-group difference was observed in the total chronicity index score (2.5 vs 2; p=0.104).

Circulating KIM-1 as a predictor of LN relapse

Kaplan-Meier analysis showed significantly lower relapse-free survival in the high-circulating KIM-1 group than in the low-KIM-1 group (log-rank test; p=0.027) (figure 2A). When ESKD and death were treated as competing events, the cumulative incidence of LN relapse remained significantly higher in the high-KIM-1 group (p=0.033) (online supplemental figure S4A).

Baseline characteristics of the relapse (n=9) and non-relapse (n=37) groups are presented in online supplemental table S2. Compared with the non-relapse group, the relapse group showed higher circulating KIM-1 (median: 442 vs 135 pg/mL; p=0.099), lower serum albumin (2.68 vs 3.40 g/dL; p=0.079) and a higher prevalence of hypertension (50% vs 13%; p=0.071). Urinary KIM-1 levels were numerically higher in the relapse group (median 6.73 ng/mg Cr vs 1.85 ng/mg Cr), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p=0.104). Stratification by median urinary KIM-1 level did not demonstrate a significant difference in cumulative incidence of renal relapse (data not shown). In a head-to-head comparison restricted to the 37 of 46 patients with both markers available, urinary KIM-1 was a significant univariable predictor of relapse (per doubling HR 1.63, 95% CI 1.07 to 2.48; p=0.023), and the discrimination of circulating and urinary KIM-1 did not differ significantly (Harrell’s C 0.761 vs 0.800; ΔC = −0.039, 95% CI −0.135 to +0.063; p=0.506; Online supplemental table S3).

Univariable Cox proportional hazards analysis identified circulating KIM-1 as a significant predictor of LN relapse (HR: 1.001, 95% CI: 1.000 to 1.001; p=0.017) (table 3). The univariable per-SD HR, per-IQR and per-doubling were 1.86 (95% CI 1.12 to 3.10), 1.30 (95% CI 1.05 to 1.62) and 1.44 (95% CI 1.02 to 2.02), respectively. A complete univariable analysis of the 10 candidate predictors is provided in online supplemental table S4. Univariable HRs for serum albumin, urinary KIM-1, and total glomerulosclerosis are reported in table 3. In the multivariable analysis including circulating KIM-1, serum creatinine and urinary protein, circulating KIM-1 remained independently associated with relapse (HR: 1.002, 95% CI 1.000 to 1.003; per-SD HR 4.62, 95% CI 1.17 to 18.29; per-IQR HR 1.92, 95% CI 1.07 to 3.45; p=0.029) (table 3). Because the per-100 pg/mL, per-IQR and per-SD expressions are simple linear rescaling of the same covariate, they all yielded the same p value, whereas the per-doubling expression—fitted on the log scale and therefore not algebraically equivalent—was attenuated to borderline significance (HR 1.63, 95% CI 0.92 to 2.87; p=0.094). This scale dependence reflects the leverage of a few patients with markedly elevated circulating KIM-1 (>1000 pg/mL), whose influence is reduced by log-transformation; the association nonetheless remained directionally consistent across both scales.

This association showed a graded, dose–response pattern across increasing KIM-1 categories. In tertile-stratified and quartile-stratified Kaplan-Meier analyses, relapses accrued progressively across categories (tertiles: 2/16, 2/15 and 5/15, log-rank p=0.067; quartiles: 1/12, 1/11, 3/11 and 4/12, log-rank p=0.165; Online supplemental figure S5) and the time-dependent area under the curve for circulating KIM-1 was 0.87 at 36 months and 0.82 at 60 months (online supplemental figure S6). The association was also independent of treatment and calendar era: immunosuppressive treatment use did not differ between the low-KIM-1 and high-KIM-1 strata (all p≥0.35), and the prognostic effect of circulating KIM-1 persisted in era-stratified analysis (HR 1.43, per doubling, 95% CI 1.00 to 2.04; p=0.050; Online supplemental table S5).

After additional adjustment for hypertension (events-per-variable 2.25, below the conventional threshold of 10), the association of circulating KIM-1 with relapse was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (per-IQR HR 1.87, 95% CI 0.96 to 3.62; per-doubling 1.34, 95% CI 0.70 to 2.56; Online supplemental table S6). However, the point estimate was essentially unchanged (per-IQR 1.92 to 1.87), indicating that this loss of significance reflected standard-error inflation from overparameterisation rather than confounding. Accordingly, KIM-1 was retained as a positive predictor in all penalised models (online supplemental table S7).

When added to a model containing established clinical predictors, the incremental discriminative value of circulating KIM-1 was modest: incorporating circulating KIM-1 into the base model raised Harrell’s C-statistic only marginally, from 0.776 to 0.781 (ΔC = +0.005, 95% CI −0.08 to +0.26; p=0.48) and yielded a three-category NRI of +0.38 at 36 months (95% CI −0.49 to +0.93; p=0.245).

Taken together, these findings indicate that elevated circulating KIM-1 at the time of renal biopsy is independently associated with long-term renal relapse in LN, although its added discriminative value beyond conventional clinical markers is modest.

Circulating KIM-1 and progression to ESKD

Kaplan-Meier analysis showed significantly lower ESKD-free survival in the high-circulating KIM-1 group than in the low-KIM-1 group (log-rank test, p=0.049) (figure 2B). When death was treated as a competing event, a similar trend was observed, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (p=0.053; Online supplemental figure S4B).

Baseline characteristics stratified by ESKD status (n=4 with ESKD) are presented in online supplemental table S8. The ESKD group (n=4) had markedly higher circulating KIM-1 levels than the non-ESKD group (median 2411 vs 142 pg/mL; p=0.004), along with higher serum creatinine (3.53 vs 0.63 mg/dL; p=0.019), lower eGFR (20.0 vs 94.5 mL/min/1.73 m²; p=0.015) and lower serum albumin (2.49 vs 3.45 g/dL; p=0.008). Urinary KIM-1 levels were also significantly higher in the ESKD group (median 14.62 ng/mg Cr vs 2.13 ng/mg Cr; p=0.002) (online supplemental table S8). However, Kaplan-Meier analysis stratified by median urinary KIM-1 levels (low vs high groups) did not show a significant difference in cumulative incidence of ESKD (data not shown). Histopathological analysis showed significantly higher chronicity index scores in the ESKD group for total glomerulosclerosis (1.5 vs 0; p=0.023) and fibrous crescents (1 vs 0; p=0.022).

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis was not performed for ESKD because the limited number of events (n=4) precluded sufficient statistical power. Nevertheless, the substantial difference in circulating KIM-1 levels between the ESKD and non-ESKD groups, together with the significant Kaplan-Meier results, supports an association between elevated circulating KIM-1 levels and progression to ESKD.

Overall survival

No significant difference in overall survival was observed between the high-circulating and low-circulating KIM-1 groups (log-rank test, p=0.104) (figure 2C).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, elevated circulating KIM-1 levels at the time of renal biopsy were associated with adverse long-term renal outcomes in LN, including relapse and progression to ESKD. Patients with higher circulating KIM-1 levels exhibited more severe clinical and histopathological features, including greater proteinuria, lower serum albumin, higher prevalence of hypertension, more frequent Class IV lesions and higher activity index scores. Importantly, multivariable Cox regression analysis demonstrated that circulating KIM-1 remained associated with relapse after adjustment for conventional prognostic markers. These results support circulating KIM-1 as a potential prognostic biomarker for long-term renal outcomes in LN.

Previous research, including that by Nozaki et al, identified urinary KIM-1 as a biomarker for LN activity.22 23 In contrast, the prognostic significance of circulating KIM-1 has been less extensively investigated. In the present study, circulating KIM-1 was associated with histological activity, as reflected in higher activity index scores and a greater prevalence of Class IV LN. Circulating KIM-1 showed a weak-to-moderate correlation with the SLEDAI-2K score (r=0.48), consistent with the renal contribution to that composite and is not interpretable as a marker of extrarenal disease activity.

Circulating and urinary KIM-1 levels were strongly correlated (r=0.86), indicating that both likely reflect underlying tubular injury. However, urinary samples were unavailable for nine patients, resulting in more missing data than for serum measurements and limiting the direct comparison of prognostic performance. Urinary biomarker concentrations may be influenced by urine dilution and collection variability.25 In our cohort, individual-level discrepancies between circulating and urinary KIM-1 levels were observed (online supplemental figure S3B), suggesting that creatinine normalisation may not fully account for this variability. In a head-to-head analysis (online supplemental table S3), circulating and urinary KIM-1 did not show a statistically significant difference in discrimination (ΔC = −0.039; p=0.506). Given the strong collinearity (r=0.86), the focus on circulating KIM-1 reflects practical advantages of serum sampling (no urinary normalisation is required, the measurement is applicable even to patients with anuria and there are fewer preanalytical sources of variance) rather than any claim of superior discrimination. Beyond these practical considerations, circulating KIM-1 reflects systemic concentrations and may represent integrated kidney production over time, rather than the point-in-time signal captured by spot urinary measurements. Conversely, because urinary KIM-1 is measured closer to the site of tubular injury, it may more directly reflect local tubular damage.25 A definitive head-to-head comparison should be a focus of future, larger studies.

The observed prognostic significance of circulating KIM-1 is biologically plausible. KIM-1 is upregulated in proximal tubular epithelial cells in response to injury, and circulating levels reflect tubulointerstitial damage.26 Emerging evidence identifies tubulointerstitial injury as a key determinant of long-term renal outcomes in LN, independent of glomerular lesions.39 40 Consistent with this concept, patients who progressed to ESKD in our study had markedly higher circulating KIM-1 levels than those who did not (median 2411 pg/mL vs 142 pg/mL). KIM-1 has also been established as a biomarker of tubular injury across various kidney diseases, supporting its pathophysiological relevance.27 41 Our findings are consistent with this concept and suggest that circulating KIM-1 captures clinically meaningful renal injury that is not fully reflected by conventional biomarkers.

The prognostic role of circulating KIM-1 has previously been demonstrated in type 1 diabetic kidney disease,26 non-diabetic CKD27 and AKI/post-AKI.41 To our knowledge, this is the first long-term prognostic evaluation in LN. Because circulating KIM-1 reflects a distinct biological compartment related to tubular injury, it may complement the emerging LN biomarkers reviewed by Querin et al42 Notably, its histological correlates—class IV LN, endocapillary hypercellularity, cellular crescents and the NIH activity index—overlap with those of the five top-quality biomarkers in that review.

The incremental value of circulating KIM-1 beyond conventional markers was nonetheless modest and did not reach statistical significance. Adding circulating KIM-1 raised the C-statistic only marginally (0.776 to 0.781; ΔC = +0.005, 95% CI −0.08 to +0.26, p=0.48), and the three-category NRI at 36 months was +0.38 (95% CI −0.49 to +0.93; p=0.245).

Within these limitations, the findings carry potential clinical implications. If validated prospectively, measurement of circulating KIM-1 at the time of renal biopsy could help identify patients at higher risk of relapse or progression, enabling closer monitoring or consideration of intensified maintenance therapy. Longitudinal assessment might also detect subclinical tubular injury before deterioration of conventional biomarkers such as proteinuria or serum creatinine, supporting more personalised treatment strategies and, potentially, improved long-term renal outcomes.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a small, single-centre retrospective study with a limited number of ESKD and mortality events, restricting statistical power and generalisability and precluding robust multivariable analyses for these endpoints. The generalisability of these findings may be limited, as differences in ethnicity, healthcare systems and management strategies may influence the applicability of these results to other populations. With only nine events, the events-per-variable is low; data-informed and penalised analyses (online supplemental table S6 and table S7) mitigate this. Furthermore, this study is subject to potential biases inherent to retrospective designs, including selection bias due to inclusion of patients with available stored samples and information bias related to reliance on medical records. Although multivariable analyses were performed, residual confounding by unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. The long study period spanning more than three decades may have introduced heterogeneity in diagnostic approaches and treatment strategies, which could have influenced clinical outcomes. Complete-case analysis was used (online supplemental table S1). Second, serum samples were stored at −30°C before analysis; although KIM-1 is relatively stable in frozen samples, prolonged storage at suboptimal temperatures may have introduced measurement variability.43 Third, as an observational study, causality cannot be established. Fourth, circulating KIM-1 was measured only at baseline; longitudinal assessment would clarify its utility as a monitoring biomarker. Fifth, although circulating KIM-1 was statistically associated with relapse, the effect size per unit increase was small and its clinical significance should be interpreted in the context of larger changes in biomarker levels. Sixth, external validation in independent cohorts is required. Finally, the median value (153 pg/mL) was used as the stratification cut-off. The Youden threshold (~178 pg/mL) needs prospective validation. The establishment of clinically optimal cut-off values and standardisation across assay systems will be required before routine implementation.

Elevated circulating KIM-1 levels at the time of renal biopsy were associated with adverse long-term renal outcomes in LN and independently associated with relapse. Circulating KIM-1 may therefore support early identification of patients at high risk and inform more personalised treatment strategies. Larger prospective studies are warranted to validate these observations and establish clinically meaningful cut-off values.
