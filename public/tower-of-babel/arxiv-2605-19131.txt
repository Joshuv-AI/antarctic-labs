Limit Laws for Consensus Protocols on the Complete Graph
Julian Becker, Konstantinos Panagiotou

Abstract
We study a distributed consensus problem on a complete communication network of n n vertices, each holding one of two opinions. The vertices communicate in rounds, possibly in the presence of adversarial noise, and exchange information until they all agree on a single opinion. We consider a general class of protocols, where the vertices randomly sample neighbors and update their own opinion according to an update function f f depending on the sampled opinions. A prominent example is the k k -maj protocol, where every vertex adopts the majority opinion of k k randomly sampled neighbors. We consider the runtime R n R_{n} that is the number of rounds until all vertices agree on the same opinion, which we call the dominating opinion D n D_{n} . In our main result we describe the limiting distributions of these two key quantities for a large class of update functions f f , for arbitrary initial configurations and under the presence of an adversary who may alter the opinions of up to o ⁡ ( n ) o(\sqrt{n}) vertices in each round. We show that there are f f -specific constants γ , m > 0 \gamma,m>0 such that R n R_{n} centers around μ n = 1 2 ​ log γ ​ n + log m ⁡ ln ⁡ n \mu_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\log_{\gamma}n+\log_{m}\ln n , and we describe the asymptotic distribution of R n − μ n R_{n}-\mu_{n} . In particular, we show that it does not converge, and that it becomes asymptotically periodic both in the log ⁡ n \log n as well as the log ⁡ log ⁡ n \log\log n scale. Applied to k k -maj , our results show, among other things, that γ k -maj = ( k − 1 ⌊ k / 2 ⌋ ) ​ 2 1 − k ​ k ∼ ( 2 ​ k / π ) 1 / 2 \gamma_{\texttt{$k$-maj}}=\binom{k-1}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}2^{1-k}k\sim({2k}/{\pi})^{1/2} .

Introduction

In this paper we provide a tight analysis for the runtime of a large class of consensus protocols on fully connected networks that includes the well-studied
k
-maj
(
k
-majority) protocol. In particular, we describe the limiting distribution of the runtime and of the eventually dominating opinion in a general setting, where there is (not necessarily) a dominating opinion in the beginning, and where there might be adversarial changes to the opinion distribution.

The study of consensus protocols and related probabilistic dynamics – where vertices in a network exchange information, synchronously or asynchronously, possibly in the presence of noise, in order to reach consensus – has attracted extensive attention not only within distributed computing, but also among other disciplines, where opinion forming and synchronization are relevant primitives. The literature is vast, and we refer to the extensive overview by Becchetti et. al.
[
4
]
for a broad discussion and many further references, and to the subsequent sections.

Majority protocols have been studied extensively, a small sample is

We say that a sequence of random variables

Set

1.1
Model & Results

Let
f:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
be, for the moment, an arbitrary function. We associate to
f
a consensus protocol that is a stochastic process
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
with initial configuration

X_{0}=\frac{n}{2}+d\sqrt{n}+o(\sqrt{n}).

Thus, at
t=0
, there are
X_{0}
vertices holding opinion
X
, and there is an initial bias of
d\sqrt{n}
(vertices). As we will see, the most interesting and complex case is when
d
is bounded and independent of
n
, since then the process moves just a little or even not at all in expectation; if, on the other hand, the initial bias is larger, then our analysis applies as well but starts already in a ’simpler’ situation. Now
f
comes into play. Given
X_{t}
, the probability that a specific vertex has opinion
X
at time
t+1
is given by
f(X_{t}/n)
, and this is independent of all other vertices. In other words, the probability that a vertex maintains or changes its opinion depends only on the fraction of vertices that have adopted
X
through
f
; this resembles the fact that we consider protocols where vertices sample the opinions of their neighbors. Formally, the process evolves according to the recursion

X_{t+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathrm{Bin}\big(n,f(X_{t}/n)\big),\quad t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

We also write
Y_{t}=n-X_{t}
for the number of vertices that have adopted opinion
Y
at time
t
. We denote by
R_{n}(X_{0})
the runtime and by
D_{n}(X_{0})
the eventually dominating opinion, so that

R_{n}(X_{0}):=\inf\big\{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:X_{t}=n\text{ or }Y_{t}=n\big\}\quad\text{and}\quad D_{n}(X_{0}):=\begin{cases}X,\quad&\text{if }X_{t}=n\text{ for some }t,\\
Y,\quad&\text{if }Y_{t}=n\text{ for some }t.\end{cases}

Note that in all preceding definitions it is not required that
X_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
– any non-negative real number works. This will be sometimes helpful in later formulations. Throughout, we will consider only specific
f
.

Definition 1.1
.

A function
f:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
is a
majority-type update function
, if

•

f
is
m
-times continuously differentiable for some
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
;

•

f
is convex on
[0,1/2]
;

•

f
is symmetric around
1/2
, i.e.,
1-f(1-x)=f(x)
for
x\in[0,1]
;

•

there exist
\gamma>1
and
\beta>0
such that, as
x\to 0
,

f(x)=\beta x^{m}+O(x^{m+1})\quad\text{and}\quad f(1/2+x)=1/2+\gamma x+O(x^{2}).

For
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we denote the
i
-th iterate of a majority-type update function by

f^{(0)}(x)=x\quad\text{and}\quad f^{(i)}(x)=f\circ f^{(i-1)}(x),\quad\text{for }i\geq 1.

The technical assumptions on
f
reflect the nature of the processes that we want to study and that are
reasonable
to study. Indeed, since
f(0)=0
and
f(1)=1
the consensus states are absorbing, which emphasizes the term
runtime
. By symmetry of
f
, starting with
X_{0}=n/2
, it holds that
\mathbb{P}(X_{t}=0)=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}=n)
for every
t\geq 0
; in particular, the process has no inherent drift towards one of the two opinions (the biased case is much easier to study by basic methods). This assumption will later allow us to show that
X_{t}\approx n/2+N_{t}
, where
N_{t}
follows a normal distribution with increasing variance in
t
. We will see that after some point in time
t
, when the process has accumulated enough bias,
X_{t}
is concentrated around its mean, and so the long-term behavior will be described by iterates of
f
.
Therefore, the assumption that
f
is convex, behaves polynomially around
0
and linearly in
1/2
ensures that we understand iterates of
f
well enough.

Our first main result pins down the probability that eventually all vertices agree upon some specific opinion. Here and in the sequel we denote by
{\cal N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
a gaussian random variable with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{R}
,
f
be a majority-type update function and
X_{0}=n/2+d\sqrt{n}+o(\sqrt{n})
be the initial number of vertices with opinion
X
. Let
Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}\big(d,1/4(\gamma^{2}-1)\big)
. Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\mathbb{P}\big(D_{n}(X_{0})=X\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(Z\geq 0\big)+o(1)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}\big(D_{n}(X_{0})=Y\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(Z\leq 0\big)+o(1).

Thus, both opinions have a chance of becoming eventually dominating, even if one of the opinions has a (slight) dominance in the beginning. Our second main result describes the limit distribution of the runtime. As preparation, for a majority-type update function
f
we define the auxiliary function

g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\quad x\mapsto 2-\log_{m}2-x+\lim_{a\to\infty,a\in\mathbb{N}}\lim_{b\to\infty,b\in\mathbb{N}}b-a-\log_{m}|\ln f^{(b)}(1/2-\gamma^{-a-x})|.

In Section
1.3

we will see that
g
is well-defined and continuous. Moreover, we prove that
g
is
1
-periodic and
\sup g-\inf g<1
.
With this at hand we obtain the following result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{R}
,
f
be a majority-type update function and
X_{0}=n/2+d\sqrt{n}+o(\sqrt{n})
be the initial number of vertices with opinion
X
. Let
Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}\big(d,1/4(\gamma^{2}-1)\big)
. Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\sup_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(R_{n}(X_{0})\geq s\right)-\mathbb{P}\Big(Z_{n}+\log_{m}\ln n+g(Z_{n})\geq s\Big)\right|=o(1),\quad\text{where}\quad Z_{n}:=\text{\scalebox{.7}{$\frac{1}{2}$}}\log_{\gamma}(n/Z^{2}).

The theorem has various immediate consequences. Without digesting the full details, it readily
establishes that the runtime centers around
\frac{1}{2}\log_{\gamma}n+\log_{m}\ln n
and that it has stochastically bounded fluctuations (that we can describe precisely in terms of a (function of a) gaussian random variable).

Corollary 1.4
.

In the situation of Theorem
1.3
,

R_{n}(X_{0})=\text{\scalebox{.7}{$\frac{1}{2}$}}\log_{\gamma}n+\log_{m}\ln n+O_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

To get a very informal justification of why such a result is expected, note that
f
being a majority-type update function implies for small
x
that a) if the fraction of vertices with opinion
X
is
1/2+x
, then after one round it is in expectation roughly
1/2+\gamma x
and b) if it is
1-x
, then after one round it is roughly
1-\beta x^{m}
. So, if we start with
x=\Theta(1/\sqrt{n})
, then after about
\frac{1}{2}\log_{\gamma}n
rounds the fraction will be significantly larger than
1/2
, and after a constant number of rounds it will be close to 1. Then, due to b), a doubly exponential race to 1 will be initiated, resulting in the doubly logarithmic term. The fine details and the fluctuations of the process are then captured by Theorem
1.3
.

In a very reduced form, Corollary
1.4
implies that, whp,
R_{n}(X_{0})\sim\frac{1}{2}\log_{\gamma}n
.
In particular, this yields the coarser bound
R_{n}(X_{0})=\Theta(\ln n)
whp, which was the best known result so far (for
k-maj
).
Apart from that, and this is not immediately apparent, a further and direct consequence of our main result is that there is
no
limiting distribution, as the fractional parts of
\frac{1}{2}\log_{\gamma}n
and
\log_{m}\ln n
vary with
n
;
in order to obtain convergence, we have to restrict to specific subsequences of
n
.
We obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
x,y\in[0,1)
and
(n_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be an increasing sequence in
\mathbb{N}
such that, as
i\to\infty
,

\text{\scalebox{.7}{$\frac{1}{2}$}}\log_{\gamma}n_{i}-\Big\lfloor\text{\scalebox{.7}{$\frac{1}{2}$}}\log_{\gamma}n_{i}\Big\rfloor\to x\quad\text{and}\quad\log_{m}\ln n_{i}-\lfloor\log_{m}\ln n_{i}\rfloor\to y.

Then, in the setting of Theorem
1.3
and with
h(x)=g(x)+x
, in distribution,

R_{n_{i}}(X_{0})-\left(\Big\lfloor\text{\scalebox{.7}{$\frac{1}{2}$}}\log_{\gamma}n_{i}\Big\rfloor+\lfloor\log_{m}\ln n_{i}\rfloor\right)\to\lfloor h(x-\log_{\gamma}|Z|)+y\rfloor.

We can extract some information about the limiting distribution, see also Figure
1
. In particular, it has the following (asymmetric) tail properties.

Lemma 1.6
.

Let
x,y\in[0,1)
and
H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\lfloor h(x-\log_{\gamma}|Z|)+y\rfloor
. Then, as
t\to\infty
,

\mathbb{P}(H\geq t)=\Theta\left(\gamma^{-t}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}(H\leq-t)=\exp\left(\Theta\left(-\gamma^{2t}\right)\right).

Up to now we have only considered initial configurations where the bias is
O(\sqrt{n})
. However, our forthcoming analysis also applies to the case where we start with a bias of
\omega(\sqrt{n})
(and this is actually the less elaborate case). Here we obtain the very strong result that the runtime concentrates on (at most) three values.

Corollary 1.7
.

Let
f
be a majority-type update function and
X_{0}=n/2+d\sqrt{n}
be the initial number of vertices with opinion
X
, where
d=\omega(1)
and
0<d\leq(1/2-\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}
for some
0<\varepsilon<1/2
.
Let
s_{n,d}:=\left\lceil\frac{1}{2}\log_{\gamma}(n/d^{2})+\log_{m}\ln n+g(\frac{1}{2}\log_{\gamma}(n/d^{2})\right)\rceil
and
S_{n,d}:=\{s_{n,d}-1,s_{n,d}\}
. Then, whp,

D_{n}(X_{0})=X\quad\text{and}\quad R_{n}(X_{0})\in S.

Finally, our analysis is robust enough to deal with an adversary who may change up to
o(\sqrt{n})
opinions in every round; we elaborate on this more in the proof overview.

Open problems.

There are several natural research directions that can be pursued: allowing more than two opinions, allowing a growing number (in
n
) of neighbors that can be sampled, and investigating more powerful adversaries. Some of the techniques developed here will carry over to these settings, for example the study of the fine behavior and the fluctuations after a bounded number of rounds, or the fact that the process follows essentially a deterministic trajectory when one opinion is dominant. However, studying more opinions or larger neighborhoods will certainly introduce significant technical challenges, especially regarding the analysis of iterated multi-dimensional functions.
Another research question that is left open here concerns the convergence of higher moments of the runtime. While our results describe the asymptotic distribution, it would be interesting to study stronger notions of convergence. One possible direction is to investigate large deviation bounds for the runtime around its expectation, as in
[
17
]
for rumor spreading protocols.

1.2
Proof Overview

The analysis is divided into three main parts. First, we establish a central limit theorem (CLT), which states that after any bounded number of rounds, the process, appropriately centered and normalized, converges to a normal distribution. Second, we show that afterwards the process stays sufficiently close to iterates of the function
f
until it reaches almost unanimity.
Lastly, we show that we require
\log_{m}\ln n
more time steps before reaching a consensus state. In what follows we present a detailed outline of all steps within the proof of Theorem
1.3
, and we assume that we are in the situation of that theorem.

In our initial configuration the two opinions are (almost) evenly distributed between all vertices. Our first milestone is the following CLT that describes the distribution of
X_{t}
after a bounded number of rounds. The proof is given in Section
2.7
.

Lemma 1.8
.

For any
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, as
n\to\infty
,

\frac{X_{t}-n/2}{\sqrt{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{t},\sigma_{t}^{2}\right),\quad\text{where}\quad\mu_{t}=\gamma^{t}d\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{t}^{2}=\frac{\gamma^{2t}-1}{4\left(\gamma^{2}-1\right)}.

We observe that while the mean of
X_{t}
stays close to
n/2
– in particular, if
d=0
, then
\mu_{t}=0
always – the variance takes off. So, although the process may not have a significant
drift
, the variance becomes eventually so big, that a large deviation from
n/2
becomes likely. In order to formalize this observation,
let
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and define
M_{t}:=\max\left\{X_{t},Y_{t}\right\}
as the number of vertices with majority opinion at time
t
. Since
Y_{t}=n-X_{t}
, we have
M_{t}=n/2+|n/2-X_{t}|
. Thus, the previous lemma not only characterizes the distribution of
X_{t}
, but also of
Y_{t}
and
M_{t}
. In fact, by applying the continuous mapping theorem, we obtain

(M_{t}-n/2)/\sqrt{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}|\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{t},\sigma_{t}^{2}\right)|
. Let
C>1
and define

t_{1}:=\lfloor\log_{\gamma}C\rfloor\quad\text{and}\quad Z_{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t_{1}}/C,\sigma_{t_{1}}^{2}/C^{2}).

(1)

We will choose
C
(very) large later on, so that specific properties are satisfied.
By basic properties of the normal distribution, Lemma
1.8
guarantees that

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\mathbb{P}(M_{t_{1}}\geq x\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(n/2+|Z_{C}|C\sqrt{n}\geq x\big)\big|=o(1),

(2)

which says that at
t_{1}
, we expect a bias of order
C\sqrt{n}
towards the one or the other opinion.
Allowing an adversary who may change the opinion of up to
o(\sqrt{n})
vertices every round obviously does not affect this statement, as Lemma
1.8
is robust with respect to changing
o(\sqrt{n})
opinions.
Note that, as
C\to\infty
with
\log_{\gamma}C\in\mathbb{N}
, we get
Z_{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}Z
, where
Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}(d,1/4(\gamma^{2}-1))
.
From now on, assume without loss of generality that opinion
X
is the majority opinion at time
t_{1}
, i.e.

M_{t_{1}}=X_{t_{1}}\quad\text{and define}\quad\tilde{X}_{t_{1}}:=X_{t_{1}}-n/2.

As already described, the main motivation for introducing
t_{1}
and letting
C
get large is that at time
t_{1}
we will observe a non-negligible bias, so that from that point on, the process will follow essentially a deterministic trajectory. Our second milestone establishes this by showing that if we start at
t_{1}
, the number of vertices with opinion
X
in subsequent time steps can be described quite precisely by iterates of
f
.
The proof of the next lemma is in Section
2.8
.

Lemma 1.9
.

Let
c_{\gamma}=1+\gamma/(\gamma-1)^{2}
. Then, for any
C\geq 1
the event
{\cal E}_{C}
occurs with probability at least
1-e^{-C}
, where

{\cal E}_{C}=\bigcap_{t\geq 0}\left\{\left|X_{t_{1}+t}-f^{(t)}\left(X_{t_{1}}/n\right)n\right|\leq c_{\gamma}\gamma^{t}\sqrt{Cn}\right\}.

The statement of the lemma also holds in the presence of an adversary that can modify in each round the opinions of
o(\sqrt{n})
vertices, possibly by replacing
c_{\gamma}
with a slightly larger constant.
The analysis of distributed algorithms via iterates of appropriate functions appears also at other places, see
[
14
,
28
]
for similar settings. Although it is mostly not possible to determine the iterated function, it is usually sufficient for the further analysis to know the behavior near its fixed points.
Indeed, by the properties of
f
, we can deduce for small
x
and
t\in\mathbb{N}
that
f^{(t)}(1/2+x)\approx 1/2+\gamma^{t}x
. Hence, if we write
\{z\}=z-\lfloor z\rfloor
for the fractional part of
z
, informally

f^{(s)}(X_{t_{1}}/n)=f^{(s)}(1/2+\tilde{X}_{t_{1}}/n)\approx 1/2+\gamma^{-a-\{\log_{\gamma}(n/\tilde{X}_{t_{1}})\}},\;\text{where}\;s=\lfloor\log_{\gamma}(n/\tilde{X}_{t_{1}})\rfloor-a,\penalty\ a\in\mathbb{N}.

That is, at time
t_{1}+\lfloor\log_{\gamma}(n/\tilde{X}_{t_{1}})\rfloor-a
the bias should be
linear in
n
and of magnitude close to
\gamma^{-a}n
. The following lemma makes this precise, its proof is in Section
2.9
.

Lemma 1.10
.

There exist constants
c>0
and
a_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
a\geq a_{0}
the following holds. Consider the random time and fractional part

T_{2}:=t_{1}+\lfloor\log_{\gamma}(n/\tilde{X}_{t_{1}})\rfloor-a\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi:=\{\log_{\gamma}(n/\tilde{X}_{t_{1}})\}.

(3)

Then, whp,
t_{1}\leq T_{2}<\infty
and

\frac{1}{2}+\gamma^{-a-\Phi}(1-c\gamma^{-a})\leq f^{(T_{2}-t_{1})}(X_{t_{1}}/n)\leq\frac{1}{2}+\gamma^{-a-\Phi}.

(4)

After
T_{2}
we perform
b\in\mathbb{N}
more steps.
The function
f
is monotonically increasing, since it is of majority-type and therefore convex on
[0,1/2]
and concave on
[1/2,1]
.
Thus, by Lemma
1.10
, for sufficiently large
a\in\mathbb{N}
, whp,

f^{(b)}\big(1/2+\gamma^{-a-\Phi}(1-c\gamma^{-a})\big)\leq f^{(T_{2}-t_{1}+b)}\big(X_{t_{1}}/n\big)\leq f^{(b)}\big(1/2+\gamma^{-a-\Phi}\big).

Let

T_{3}:=T_{2}+b
.
By applying Lemma
1.9
and using that
f(1/2+x)\geq 1/2
for
x\in[0,1/2]
, we obtain that there exists a (possibly different)
c>0
such that for
a\in\mathbb{N}
large enough and
b\in\mathbb{N}
, with probability tending to one as
n,C\to\infty
,

\tilde{L}n\leq X_{T_{3}}\leq\tilde{U}n,

where

\displaystyle\tilde{L}

\displaystyle=f^{(b)}\big(1/2+\gamma^{-a-\Phi}(1-c\gamma^{-a})\big)\cdot(1-c\gamma^{-a+b}\sqrt{Cn}/\tilde{X}_{t_{1}}),

\displaystyle\tilde{U}

\displaystyle=f^{(b)}\big(1/2+\gamma^{-a-\Phi}\big)\cdot(1+c\gamma^{-a+b}\sqrt{Cn}/\tilde{X}_{t_{1}}).

This looks messy, but note that by the CLT property in (
2
) we know that
\sqrt{Cn}/\tilde{X}_{t_{1}}
tends in distribution to
1/\sqrt{C}|Z_{C}|
, which, in turn, by (
1
), tends in probability to
0
as
C\to\infty
.
We use all this to prove the following streamlined statement in Section
2.10
.
In what follows, and throughout the paper, we write
o^{\star}(1)
for a term tending to zero as
a\to\infty
, provided that
b,C,n
are chosen sufficiently large.
More precisely, for
F:\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
,

F(a,b,C,n)=o^{\star}(1)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\limsup_{a\to\infty}\limsup_{b\to\infty}\limsup_{C\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}|F(a,b,C,n)|=0.

In our setting, the parameters
a,b,C
describe some event
\mathcal{E}_{a,b,C,n}
depending also on
n
(where
C
is introduced at
T_{1}
,
a
at
T_{2}
and
b
at
T_{3}
).
Thus, saying that
\mathcal{E}_{a,b,C,n}
holds with probability
1-o^{\star}(1)
means that for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is an
a_{0}
such that, for every
a\geq a_{0}
, there is a
b_{0}=b_{0}(a)
such that, for every
b\geq b_{0}
, there is a
C_{0}=C_{0}(b)
such that, for every
C\geq C_{0}
, there is an
n_{0}=n_{0}(C)
such that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{a,b,C,n})\geq 1-\varepsilon
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. In essence, for any
\varepsilon>0
we
can choose

a,b\in\mathbb{N}
and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that for all sufficiently large
n
the desired event happens with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
.

Lemma 1.11
.

There exist a constant
c>0
such that with probability
1-o^{\star}(1)
,

Ln\leq X_{T_{3}}\leq Un,\quad\text{where}\quad T_{3}:=T_{2}+b,

T_{2}
is as in Lemma
1.10
and
U,L\in(1/2,1)
are given by

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle:=L(a,b,C,n)=f^{(b)}\big(1/2+\gamma^{-a-\Phi}(1-c\gamma^{-a})\big)\cdot(1-C^{-1/4}),

(5)

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle:=U(a,b,C,n)=f^{(b)}\big(1/2+\gamma^{-a-\Phi}\big)\cdot(1+C^{-1/4}).

Since
f
is of majority-type,
f^{(b)}(1/2+\varepsilon)\to 1
as
b\to\infty
and for any
\varepsilon>0
.
Thus, taking the limits
C
and
b
to infinity in the previous lemma yields
U,L\to 1
whenever
a
is large enough.
Therefore, at
T_{3}
, an appropriate choice of parameters ensures that almost all vertices agree on the majority opinion.
In the next step we translate the conclusion of Lemma
1.11

to the runtime via the notion of
stochastic dominance
–
for random variables
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
, we write
``Z_{1}\succsim Z_{2}"
, if
\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}\geq x)\geq\mathbb{P}(Z_{2}\geq x)
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Similarly,
Z_{1}\precsim Z_{2}
is defined with
``\leq"
instead of
``\geq"
.

Lemma 1.12
.

Let
x,x^{\prime}\in[1/2,1]
with
x\leq x^{\prime}
. Then

R_{n}(xn)\precsim R_{n}(x^{\prime}n).

The (easy) proof is in Section
2.11
.
As a third milestone, for
1/2<x<1
, the next lemma asserts that starting with
xn
vertices having the majority opinion, the process reaches consensus in approximately
\log_{m}\ln n-|\ln(1-x)|-\log_{m}2+1
rounds. The proof is in Section
2.12
.

Lemma 1.13
.

Let
1/2<x<1
.
There are functions
\varepsilon_{+},\varepsilon_{-}:[1/2,1]\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\varepsilon_{+}(x),\varepsilon_{-}(x)\to 0
as
x\to 1
and such that uniformly, whp,

\displaystyle R_{n}(xn)

\displaystyle\precsim\left\lceil\log_{m}\ln n-\log_{m}|\ln(1-x)|-\log_{m}2+1+\varepsilon_{+}(x)+o(1)\right\rceil,

\displaystyle R_{n}(xn)

\displaystyle\succsim\left\lceil\log_{m}\ln n-\log_{m}|\ln(1-x)|-\log_{m}2+1-\varepsilon_{-}(x)-o(1)\right\rceil.

Based on the two previous lemmas the main statement in Theorem
1.3
follows by proving that
R_{n}(Un)
and
R_{n}(Ln)
coincide with probability
1-o^{\star}(1)
.
This is established in Section
2.1
.
Note that if all these events occur, then the majority opinion remains unchanged after
t_{1}
and is therefore also the dominating opinion.

1.3
Technical preliminaries

We will need the following useful properties about the functions
g
and
h
in Theorem
1.3
and Corollary
1.5
. The proofs are in Sections
2.13
and
2.14
.

Lemma 1.14
.

The function
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, given by

g(x)=2-\log_{m}2-x+\lim_{a\to\infty,a\in\mathbb{N}}\lim_{b\to\infty,b\in\mathbb{N}}b-a-\log_{m}|\ln(f^{(b)}(1/2-\gamma^{-a-x}))|,

is well-defined, continuous,
1
-periodic, and
\sup g-\inf g<1
.

The function
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

is continuous and injective.

2.1
Proof of Theorem
1.3

By Lemma
1.11
, with probability
1-o^{\star}(1)
we have
Ln\leq X_{T_{3}}\leq Un
, where
L,U
are as in (
5
) and,

t_{1}=\lfloor\log_{\gamma}C\rfloor,\quad T_{2}=t_{1}+\lfloor\log_{\gamma}(n/\tilde{X}_{t_{1}})\rfloor-a\geq t_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad T_{3}=T_{2}+b,

where
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Since it will facilitate our computations, we assume from now on that
\log_{\gamma}C\in\mathbb{N}
, so that in particular
T_{3}=\log_{\gamma}C+\lfloor\log_{\gamma}(n/\tilde{X}_{t_{1}})\rfloor-a+b
. By Lemma
1.12
, with probability
1-o^{\star}(1)
,

R_{n}(Ln)\precsim R_{n}(X_{T_{3}})\precsim R_{n}(Un).

Thus, using Lemma
1.13
, there are functions
\varepsilon_{+},\varepsilon_{-}:[1/2,1]\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\varepsilon_{+}(x),\varepsilon_{-}(x)\to 0
as
x\to 1
and such that with probability
1-o^{\star}(1)
,

\displaystyle R_{n}(X_{T_{3}})

\displaystyle\precsim\left\lceil\log_{m}\ln n-\log_{m}|\ln(1-U)|-\log_{m}2+1+\varepsilon_{+}(U)+o(1)\right\rceil,

(6)

\displaystyle R_{n}(X_{T_{3}})

\displaystyle\succsim\left\lceil\log_{m}\ln n-\log_{m}|\ln(1-L)|-\log_{m}2+1-\varepsilon_{-}(L)-o(1)\right\rceil.

For this to be useful, we need to compare the logarithmic terms involving
U
and
L
. The next lemma does exactly that. The proof is in Section
2.15
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
U,L
be as in (
5
). Then, with probability
1-o^{\star}(1)
,

\Big|\log_{m}|\ln(1-U)|-\log_{m}|\ln(1-L)|\Bigr|=o^{\star}(1)\quad\text{and}\quad U,L=1-o^{\star}(1).

Since
f
is of majority-type, it is symmetric around
1/2
and increasing; indeed
f
is convex on
[0,1/2]
and concave on
[1/2,1]
.
Thus,
L\leq 1-f^{(b)}(1/2-\gamma^{-a-\Phi})\leq U
, where
\Phi=\{\log_{\gamma}(n/\tilde{X}_{t_{1}})\}
as in Lemma
1.10
.
Together with (
6
) this implies, with probability
1-o^{\star}(1)
,

\displaystyle R_{n}(X_{T_{3}})

\displaystyle\precsim\left\lceil\log_{m}\ln n-\log_{m}|\ln f^{(b)}(1/2-\gamma^{-a-\Phi})|-\log_{m}2+1+o^{\star}(1)\right\rceil,

\displaystyle R_{n}(X_{T_{3}})

\displaystyle\succsim\left\lceil\log_{m}\ln n-\log_{m}|\ln f^{(b)}(1/2-\gamma^{-a-\Phi})|-\log_{m}2+1-o^{\star}(1)\right\rceil.

Note that
R_{n}(X_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}R_{n}(X_{T_{3}})+T_{3}
, where
T_{3}=\log_{\gamma}C+\lfloor\log_{\gamma}(n/\tilde{X}_{t_{1}})\rfloor-a+b
on an event with probability
1-o^{\star}(1)
. Thus, recalling that

h(x)=2-\log_{m}2+\lim_{a\to\infty,a\in\mathbb{N}}\lim_{b\to\infty,b\in\mathbb{N}}b-a-\log_{m}|\ln(f^{(b)}(1/2-\gamma^{-a-x}))|,

we obtain with probability
1-o^{\star}(1)
,

\displaystyle R_{n}(X_{0})

\displaystyle\precsim\left\lceil\log_{m}\ln n+h(\Phi)+\lfloor\log_{\gamma}(n/\tilde{X}_{t_{1}})\rfloor+\log_{\gamma}C-1+o^{\star}(1)\right\rceil,

\displaystyle R_{n}(X_{0})

\displaystyle\succsim\left\lceil\log_{m}\ln n+h(\Phi)+\lfloor\log_{\gamma}(n/\tilde{X}_{t_{1}})\rfloor+\log_{\gamma}C-1-o^{\star}(1)\right\rceil.

Note that
h(x+s)=h(x)+s
for any
s\in\mathbb{Z}
, since
h(x)=g(x)-x
and
g
is
1
-periodic by Lemma
1.14
.
Therefore,

h(\Phi)+\lfloor\log_{\gamma}(n/\tilde{X}_{t_{1}})\rfloor+\log_{\gamma}C=h\left(\log_{\gamma}(Cn/\tilde{X}_{t_{1}})\right).

Recall that
\tilde{X}_{t_{1}}=M_{t_{1}}+n/2
, where
M_{t_{1}}
is the number of vertices with majority opinion at time
t_{1}
, and by (
2
),

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathbb{P}(M_{t_{1}}\geq x)-\mathbb{P}(n/2+|Z_{C}|C\sqrt{n}\geq x)|=o(1),\quad\text{where}\quad Z_{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t_{1}}/C,\sigma_{t_{1}}^{2}/C^{2}).

From this, we readily deduce that

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathbb{P}(Cn/\tilde{X}_{t_{1}}\geq x)-\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{n}/|Z_{C}|\geq x)|=o(1).

Moreover, recall that
Z_{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}Z
as
C\to\infty
. Thus, since
h
is continuous by Lemma
1.15
, with probability
1-o^{\star}(1)
, uniformly for any
z\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(R_{n}(X_{0})\geq z)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\left\lceil\log_{m}\ln n+h(\log_{\gamma}(\sqrt{n}/|Z|))-1+o^{\star}(1)\right\rceil\geq z)+o^{\star}(1),

(7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(R_{n}(X_{0})\geq z)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(\left\lceil\log_{m}\ln n+h(\log_{\gamma}(\sqrt{n}/|Z|))-1-o^{\star}(1)\right\rceil\geq z)-o^{\star}(1).

Furthermore,
h(\log_{\gamma}(\sqrt{n}/|Z|)
is a continuous random variable, since
h
is injective by Lemma
1.15
. Hence, the upper and lower bound in (
7
) converge, uniformly in
z\in\mathbb{Z}
, to

\mathbb{P}(\left\lceil\log_{m}\ln n+h(\log_{\gamma}(\sqrt{n}/|Z|))-1\right\rceil\geq z)

Finally, the fact that
\mathbb{P}(\lceil W-1\rceil\geq z)=\mathbb{P}(W\geq z)
holds for any continuous random variable
W
and
z\in\mathbb{Z}
together with
h(x)=g(x)+x
, proves Theorem
1.3
.

2.2
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Recall from the end of Section
1.2
that, with probability
1-o^{\star}(1)
, eventually all vertices agree on the majority opinion at
t_{1}=\log_{\gamma}C
, assuming
\log_{\gamma}C\in\mathbb{N}
. Opinion
X
(resp.
Y
) is the majority opinion at
t_{1}
exactly when
X_{t_{1}}-n/2\geq 0
(resp.
X_{t_{1}}-n/2\leq 0
). Thus,

\mathbb{P}(D_{n}(X_{0})=X)=\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{1}}-n/2\geq 0)+o^{\star}(1)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}(D_{n}(X_{0})=Y)=\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{1}}-n/2\leq 0)+o^{\star}(1).

Moreover Lemma
1.8
shows for
Z_{t_{1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}\left(\gamma^{t_{1}}d,\frac{\gamma^{2t_{1}}-1}{4(\gamma^{2}-1)}\right)
that

\mathbb{P}(D_{n}(X_{0})=X)=\mathbb{P}(Z_{t_{1}}\geq 0)+o^{\star}(1)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}(D_{n}(X_{0})=Y)=\mathbb{P}(Z_{t_{1}}\leq 0)+o^{\star}(1).

This proves the claim, since
Z_{t_{1}}/C\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}Z
with
Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}\left(d,\frac{1}{4(\gamma^{2}-1)}\right)
.

2.3
Proof of Corollary
1.4

Recall from Lemma
1.14
that
g
is
1
-periodic and continuous, so that
g
and in particular
g(Z_{n})
is bounded. The claim then follows by the simple observation that
Z_{n}=\log_{m}\ln n+O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
and Theorem
1.3
.

2.4
Proof of Corollary
1.5

Let
x,y\in[0,1)
and
(n_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a strictly increasing sequence in
\mathbb{N}
such that

\left\{\text{\scalebox{.7}{$\frac{1}{2}$}}\log_{\gamma}n_{i}\right\}\to x\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \penalty\ \left\{\log_{m}\ln n_{i}\right\}\to y\quad\text{as}\quad i\to\infty.

(8)

Let
s\in\mathbb{Z}
and
t=s+\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\log_{\gamma}n_{i}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\log_{m}\ln n_{i}\right\rfloor
. Then

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(R_{n_{i}}(X_{0})-\left(\left\lfloor\text{\scalebox{.7}{$\frac{1}{2}$}}\log_{\gamma}n_{i}\right\rfloor+\lfloor\log_{m}\ln n_{i}\rfloor\right)\geq s\Bigr)=\mathbb{P}\left(R_{n_{i}}(X_{0})\geq t\right).

Since
g
is
1
-periodic by Lemma
1.14
and
h(x)=g(x)+x
, it follows from Theorem
1.3
that

\mathbb{P}\left(R_{n_{i}}(X_{0})\geq t\right)=\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\left\{\log_{m}\ln n_{i}\right\}+h\left(\left\{\text{\scalebox{.7}{$\frac{1}{2}$}}\log_{\gamma}n_{i}\right\}-\log_{\gamma}|Z|\right)\geq s\Bigr)+o(1).

Using that
Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}\big(d,1/4(\gamma^{2}-1)\big)
is continuous, that
h
is injective by Lemma
1.15
, and (
8
), we obtain, as
i\to\infty
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\left\{\log_{m}\ln n_{i}\right\}+h\left(\left\{\text{\scalebox{.7}{$\frac{1}{2}$}}\log_{\gamma}n_{i}\right\}-\log_{\gamma}|Z|\right)\geq s\Bigr)\to\mathbb{P}\Bigl(h(x-\log_{\gamma}|Z|)+y\geq s\Bigr).

This proves the claim, since
\mathbb{P}(h(x-\log_{\gamma}|Z|)+y\geq s)=\mathbb{P}(\left\lfloor h(x-\log_{\gamma}|Z|)+y\right\rfloor\geq s)
for any
s\in\mathbb{Z}
.

2.5
Proof of Lemma
1.6

We recall the following (folklore) tail bounds for the normal distribution. Let
W\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
. Then,

\mathbb{P}(|W|\leq z)=\Theta(z),\ \text{as }z\to 0,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}(|W|\geq z)=\exp\left(-\Theta\left(z^{2}\right)\right),\ \text{as }z\to\infty.

(9)

Let
x,y\in[0,1)
and
H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\lfloor h(x-\log_{\gamma}|Z|)+y\rfloor
, where
Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}\big(d,1/4(\gamma^{2}-1)\big)
. Since the function
g
is periodic, it attains its maximum value
g_{\text{max}}
and minimum value
g_{\text{min}}
on
\mathbb{R}
. Thus,
z+g_{\text{min}}\leq z+g(z)=h(z)\leq z+g_{\text{max}}
. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}(H\geq t)=\mathbb{P}(\lfloor h(x-\log_{\gamma}|Z|)+y\rfloor\geq t)=\mathbb{P}\left(|Z|\leq\Theta\left(\gamma^{-t}\right)\right).

Applying (
9
) proves the upper tail. Similarly, the lower tail follows by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(H\leq-t)=\mathbb{P}\left(\lfloor h(x-\log_{\gamma}|Z|)+y\rfloor\leq-t\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(|Z|\geq\Theta\left(\gamma^{t}\right)\right)=\exp\left(-\Theta\left(\gamma^{2t}\right)\right).

2.6
Proof of Corollary
1.7

We are in the setting
X_{0}=n/2+d\sqrt{n}
with
d=\omega(1)
and
d\leq(1/2-\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}
for some
0<\varepsilon<1/2
. In Section
1.2
, we use the parameter
C
to describe a bias of
C\sqrt{n}
towards the majority opinion at time
t_{1}=\lfloor\log_{\gamma}C\rfloor
. Here, we already start with an initial bias of
d\sqrt{n}
, so that
C=d
and the process starts at
t_{1}=\lfloor\log_{\gamma}d\rfloor
.
We already established that, whp, the majority opinion at time
t_{1}
is the dominating opinion. Thus, whp,
D_{n}(X_{0})=X
. Moreover, Lemma

Proof of Lemma

is non-random, in the case

, where

\mathbb{P}\left(\overline{X}_{t+1}\leq x\right)=\sum_{|j|\leq A}\mathbb{P}\left(\overline{X}_{t+1}\leq x\mid E_{j}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(E_{j}\right)+O(R_{A}),

(10)

where

is an error term which can be bounded as follows. Note that the continuous mapping theorem asserts that

\displaystyle R_{A}\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\overline{X}_{t}\right|>A\varepsilon\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{N}\left(\gamma_{t},\sigma_{t}^{2}\right)\right|>A\varepsilon\right)+o(1).

This shows that, if we choose
\varepsilon>0

, up to the

10
), in particular
|j|\leq A
. Recall that conditional on

X_{t+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathrm{Bin}\left(n,f\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{X_{t}-n/2}{n}\right)\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathrm{Bin}\left(n,f\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\overline{X}_{t}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right).

Recall also that
f(1/2+x)=1/2+\gamma x+O(x^{2})
, since

is of majority-type. Thus, if we condition on

\displaystyle X_{t+1}\succeq\mathrm{Bin}\left(n,f\left(1/2+j\varepsilon/\sqrt{n}\right)\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathrm{Bin}\left(n,1/2+\gamma j\varepsilon/\sqrt{n}+O\left(j^{2}\varepsilon^{2}/n\right)\right)

\varepsilon
. Hence, conditioned on

and uniformly in

Thus,

denote the density function of
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t},\sigma_{t}^{2})

|j|\leq A

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(E_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\mathbb{P}\left(j\varepsilon<\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{t},\sigma_{t}^{2}\right)\leq(j+1)\varepsilon\right)=(1+o(1))\int_{(j\varepsilon,(j+1)\varepsilon]}f_{t}(x)\,dx.

\varepsilon f_{t}(\xi)=\int_{(j\varepsilon,(j+1)\varepsilon]}f_{t}(x)\,dx

Since

, the process needs less than

Next, we identify

as a critical threshold in the following sense. If the number of vertices holding opinion

Lemma 2.3
.

Let

such that

and
Y_{t^{\prime}}\leq\sqrt{n}/\ln n
. Then

\mathbb{P}(Y_{t+1}=0\mid Y_{t})=o(1)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}(Y_{t^{\prime}+1}>0\mid Y_{t^{\prime}})=o(1).

The proofs of Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3
are at the end of this section.
With those properties at hand we are now able to prove Lemma
1.13
whp. To that end, define

\displaystyle\overline{t}_{u}:=\min\left\{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:Y_{t^{*}+t}\leq\sqrt{n}/\ln n\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{t}_{\ell}:=\min\left\{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:Y_{t^{*}+t}\leq\sqrt{n}\ln n\right\}.

Thus, by Lemma

\overline{t}_{\ell}+1\precsim R_{n}(xn)\precsim\overline{t}_{u}+1.

Let

\displaystyle\implies Y_{t^{*}+t}\leq\sqrt{n}/\ln n

Setting

\displaystyle t_{\ell}+1\precsim R_{n}(xn)\precsim t_{u}+1.

(19)

In the remainder of the proof, we determine

,

Since

is of majority-type, we have

as

and

It follows by induction that

\displaystyle 0<x^{m^{t}}(\beta-rx)^{(m^{t}-1)/(m-1)}\leq f^{(t)}(x)\leq x^{m^{t}}(\beta+rx)^{(m^{t}-1)/(m-1)}<1,\quad\text{for }0<x\leq\delta^{*}.

(21)

Since the claim is stated for

Then by (

Applying the logarithm on both sides, we obtain

m^{t}\Big(|\ln y_{t^{*}}|-\ln(\beta\pm ry_{t^{*}})/(m-1)\Big)\geq\text{\scalebox{.7}{$\frac{1}{2}$}}\ln n-\ln c_{n}-\ln(\beta\pm ry_{t^{*}})/(m-1).

Finally, solving for
t
shows that there exists a function
\varepsilon_{*}:[0,1/2]\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\varepsilon_{*}(x)\to 0

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle\geq\log_{m}\ln n-\log_{m}|\ln(y_{t^{*}})|-\log_{m}2+\varepsilon_{*}(y_{t^{*}})+o(1).

Since

such that
\varepsilon_{+}(x),\varepsilon_{-}(x)\to 0

\displaystyle\leq\left\lceil\log_{m}\ln n-\log_{m}|\ln(1-x)|-\log_{m}2+\varepsilon_{+}(x)+o(1)\right\rceil,

\displaystyle\geq\left\lceil\log_{m}\ln n-\log_{m}|\ln(1-x)|-\log_{m}2-\varepsilon_{-}(x)+o(1)\right\rceil.

This proves the claim by (

\displaystyle R(xn)

\displaystyle\precsim\left\lceil\log_{m}\ln n-\log_{m}|\ln(1-x)|-\log_{m}2+1+\varepsilon_{+}(x)+o(1)\right\rceil,

\displaystyle R(xn)

\displaystyle\succsim\left\lceil\log_{m}\ln n-\log_{m}|\ln(1-x)|-\log_{m}2+1-\varepsilon_{-}(x)+o(1)\right\rceil.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
0<\delta<1/4
. Then, all events in
\{C_{t}:0\leq t\leq 2\log_{m}\ln n\}
hold simultaneously with high probability, where

C_{t}:=\left\{\left|Y_{t+1}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}]\right|\leq\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}]^{1/2+\delta}+n^{\delta}\right\}.

Proof.

Let
n^{\prime}:=n^{(m-1)/m+\delta(m-1)/m^{2}}
. We distinguish two cases according to the value of
Y_{t}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\overline{C_{t}})=\mathbb{P}(\overline{C_{t}}\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{t}<n^{\prime}\}})+\mathbb{P}(\overline{C_{t}}\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{t}\geq n^{\prime}\}}).

(22)

Since
f
is of majority-type, there exist
c_{\ell},c_{u}>0
such that

\displaystyle c_{\ell}x^{m}\leq f(x)\leq c_{u}x^{m}.

(23)

We first consider
\mathbb{P}(\overline{C_{t}}\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{t}<n^{\prime}\}})
. Recall that
Y_{t+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathrm{Bin}(n,f(Y_{t}/n))
. On the event
Y_{t}<n^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}]=nf(Y_{t}/n)\leq c_{u}Y_{t}^{m}n^{1-m}\leq c_{u}n^{\delta(m-1)/m}.

(24)

If we assume
\overline{C_{t}}
, then either

\displaystyle Y_{t+1}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}]>\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}]^{1/2+\delta}+n^{\delta}

(25)

or

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}]-Y_{t+1}>\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}]^{1/2+\delta}+n^{\delta}.

(26)

Note that
\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}]\geq 0
.
By (
24
), there exists
n_{0}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
case (
26
) is impossible. In the following, we assume that
n\geq n_{0}
. Thus, by (
25
) and (
24
), with room to spare,

Y_{t+1}\geq n^{\delta}\geq c_{u}^{-1}n^{\delta/m}\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}].

By applying Markov’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\overline{C_{t}}\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{t}<n^{\prime}\}})\leq\mathbb{P}(Y_{t+1}\geq c_{u}^{-1}n^{\delta/m}\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}])\leq c_{u}n^{-\delta/m}.

(27)

It remains to treat the case
Y_{t}\geq n^{\prime}
. Applying (
23
) and the definition of
n^{\prime}
imply

\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}]=nf(Y_{t}/n)\geq c_{\ell}Y_{t}^{m}n^{1-m}\geq c_{\ell}n^{\delta(m-1)/m}.

Since
Y_{t+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathrm{Bin}(n,f(Y_{t}/n))
, its conditional variance satisfies
\mathbb{V}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}]=nf(Y_{t}/n)(1-f(Y_{t}/n))
.
Furthermore, as
f(x)\in[0,1]
for any
x\in[0,1]
,

\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}]=nf(Y_{t}/n)\geq nf(Y_{t}/n)(1-f(Y_{t}/n))=\mathbb{V}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}].

Hence,
\overline{C_{t}}=\left\{\left|Y_{t+1}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}]\right|>\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}]^{1/2+\delta}+n^{\delta}\right\}
implies with room to spare

\left|Y_{t+1}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}]\right|>\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}]^{1/2+\delta}\geq c_{\ell}^{\delta}n^{\delta^{2}(m-1)/m}\mathbb{V}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}]^{1/2}.

Applying Chebyshev’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\overline{C_{t}}\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{t}\geq n^{\prime}\}})\leq\mathbb{P}(\left|Y_{t+1}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}]\right|\geq c_{\ell}^{\delta}n^{\delta^{2}(m-1)/m}\mathbb{V}[Y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}]^{1/2})\leq c_{\ell}^{-2\delta}n^{-2\delta^{2}(m-1)/m}.

(28)

Combining (
22
), (
27
) and (
28
), we obtain
\mathbb{P}(\overline{C_{t}})=n^{-O(1)}
, and the statement in the lemma follows with room to spare from a union bound.
∎

Proof of Lemma
1.14

The proof is inspired by the strategy in

for analyzing asymptotic problems on iterated functions.
By the properties of

such that

\displaystyle f(x)=x^{m}(\beta+p(x))\quad\text{and}\quad f^{(t)}(x)=x^{m^{t}}\prod_{0\leq s\leq t-1}\left(\beta+p(f^{(s)}(x))\right)^{m^{t-1-s}}.

(36)

To shorten the notation, set

In the following, we assume that
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
. Applying (

) with

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle=2-x+\lim_{a\to\infty}-a-\log_{m}\left|\ln z+\sum_{s\geq 0}m^{-s-1}q(f^{(s)}(z))\right|.

(37)

Define the following two functions on

Z(x):=-m\ln x\quad\text{and}\quad W(x):=\sum_{s\geq 0}m^{-s}q(f^{(s)}(x))-Z(x)

such that
g(x)=3-x+\lim_{a\to\infty}-a-\log_{m}\left|W(z)\right|
. Moreover,
Z(x)=q(x)+Z(f(x))/m

and consequently

\displaystyle W(x)=W(f(x))/m.

(38)

In the following, we will describe
all
solutions of the functional equation (
38
), which eventually enables us to establish every property of
W
that we need.
We proceed in three steps:

1.

Describe a solution
W_{0}
of (
38
);

2.

Show that any solution of (
38
) has the form
W_{0}(x)v(x)
, where
v
is a function fulfilling

;

Establish continuity of
W
.

1. Base solution
W_{0}
:
First, consider the functional equation for
M
,

\displaystyle M(f(x))=f^{\prime}(1/2)M(x).

(39)

Since
f
is bijective, its inverse

and also iterates of it are well-defined. Using this one can construct a

of (

\displaystyle M_{0}(x)=(1/2-x)\prod_{j\geq 0}\eta(f^{(-j)}(x)),\quad\text{with}\quad\eta(x):=f^{\prime}(1/2)\frac{1/2-f^{(-1)}(x)}{1/2-x},

(40)

since

tends exponentially fast to
0
as

.
As
f^{\prime}(1/2)=\gamma
, the function

W_{0}(x):=m^{\log_{\gamma}(M_{0}(x))}

38
).

2. Uniqueness:
It remains to show that the solution of (
38
) is unique up to multiplying by a function

. Let

be a solution of (

) and fix

. For any

, since

Consequently, the function

\displaystyle T(x)=m^{-u}T(f^{(u)}(x)),\quad\text{where}\quad f^{(u)}(x)\in(f(x_{0}),x_{0}].

We therefore express

\displaystyle T(x)

\displaystyle=m^{-u}T(f^{(u)}(x))=m^{-u}m^{\log_{\gamma}(M_{0}(f^{(u)}(x)))}w(\log_{\gamma}(M_{0}(f^{(u)}(x))))

\displaystyle=m^{\log_{\gamma}(M_{0}(x))}w(u+\log_{\gamma}(M_{0}(x))).

Thus, we can extend

In the uniqueness part, we saw that

\displaystyle=\log_{\gamma}(x)+\sum_{j\geq 0}O(1/2-f^{(-j)}(1/2-x))
