The PREVENT Risk Equations and Cardiovascular Prevention in East Asia
Authors: Mitsuaki Sawano, Kentaro Ejiri, Yuichiro Mori, Hokyou Lee, Aya Hirata, Kosuke Inoue, Takeshi Kitai, Shun Kohsaka, Hyeon Chang Kim, Kunihiro Matsushita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular disease remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in East Asia, although its epidemiological profile differs substantially from that of Western populations. As East Asia experiences rapid increases in chronic kidney disease, diabetes, multimorbidity, and heart failure driven by population aging, accurate cardiovascular risk prediction has become increasingly important for the risk-stratified prevention of these diseases. Traditional risk prediction models, including the Framingham Risk Score, Pooled Cohort Equations, and SCORE, were derived largely from Western populations and often overestimated the risk in East Asian cohorts without recalibration. In 2025, the American Heart Association introduced the PREVENT equations to estimate 10-year and 30-year risks of total cardiovascular disease, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, and heart failure within an integrated cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic framework. Given the growing globalization of cardiovascular prevention and international clinical research, understanding whether PREVENT performs well in East Asian populations has important implications for the clinical implementation and harmonization of risk assessment frameworks. This review summarizes the evolution of cardiovascular risk prediction, key innovations of PREVENT, and emerging validation evidence from East Asia. Early studies from Korea and China suggest the reasonable transportability of these approaches. However, evidence from Japan remains limited, and further validation and recalibration are warranted. We also discuss the challenges related to heart failure prediction, add-on variables, electronic health record data infrastructure, and AI-assisted risk assessment.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, Risk prediction, PREVENT equation, Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome, East Asia

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in East Asia, although its epidemiological profile differs from that observed in the United States (US) and European countries 1 , 2) . Historically, many East Asian countries have experienced lower rates of atherosclerotic CVD (ASCVD) alongside a disproportionately higher burden of stroke 3) . At the same time, population aging, rising rates of obesity, diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and multimorbidity have contributed to a rapidly expanding burden of heart failure and other cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) conditions 4) . These distinctions underscore the importance of region-specific approaches to cardiovascular risk assessment and prevention.

Comprehensive cardiovascular risk assessment has become central to cardiovascular prevention because it enables patient-level risk communication using estimates derived from large population cohorts to better quantify the effects of preventive interventions. The most widely used cardiovascular risk prediction models include the Framingham Risk Score (FRS) and the 2013 American College of Cardiology (ACC) /American Heart Association (AHA) Pooled Cohort Equations (PCE), both of which were derived from cohorts in the US 5 , 6) . In Europe, risk prediction scores, such as SCORE and the updated SCORE2, have been developed as counterparts to earlier US-based prediction models and endorsed in local prevention guidelines 7 - 9) . These risk prediction models have played a foundational role in preventive cardiology by shifting decision-making from isolated risk factors toward integrated absolute risk and are incorporated into guidelines for tackling individual cardiovascular risk factors 10 , 11) . However, lower baseline cardiovascular event rates in East Asian countries, historically higher stroke-to-coronary disease ratios, and population-specific differences in body composition, risk factor distributions, physical activity, and treatment patterns have repeatedly been recognized as important sources of risk misestimation when Western-derived prediction tools are applied without appropriate validation or recalibration.

The recently developed AHA Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease Events (PREVENT) equations introduced a broader CKM framework that extends beyond traditional atherosclerotic outcomes 12 , 13) . By estimating 10-year and 30-year risks of total CVD, ASCVD, and heart failure, PREVENT reflects the contemporary recognition that cardiovascular prevention cannot be reduced to coronary disease or ischemic stroke alone and that earlier risk factor control may help prevent future cardiovascular events before they clinically manifest, particularly as emerging cardiometabolic and renal therapies continue to expand opportunities for earlier intervention and disease modification. As cardiovascular prevention and clinical research become increasingly globalized, understanding whether PREVENT performs well across East Asian populations has important implications for harmonizing risk assessment frameworks and guiding preventive strategies in regions with distinct epidemiological profiles. This review summarizes the background and methodological foundations of PREVENT and discusses its potential applicability and relevance to cardiovascular prevention in East Asian populations.

Evolution of Cardiovascular Risk Prediction

In 1998, the Framingham Risk Score (FRS) was derived from the Framingham Heart Study in the US and was designed to predict the risk of incident coronary heart disease (CHD) 14 , 15) . The score provided the first quantitative tool to translate broad epidemiological findings into personalized patient care and has since become the foundation for individual cardiovascular prevention. The FRS is a sex-specific cardiovascular risk prediction algorithm used to estimate an individual’s 10-year risk of developing CHD. When applied to East Asian cohorts, the FRS demonstrated acceptable discrimination but poor calibration, generally overestimating cardiovascular risk without recalibration 16 , 17) . The 2013 Pooled Cohort Equations (PCE) further expanded cardiovascular risk prediction to include both CHD and stroke and subsequently became a major foundation for statin decision-making in primary prevention 6 , 18) . However, the 2013 PCE also continued to demonstrate acceptable discrimination but poor calibration, with a consistent pattern of risk overestimation in East Asian cohorts 16 , 19) .

In Europe, the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) jointly with the European Atherosclerosis Society (EAS) published the SCORE (Systematic COronary Risk Evaluation) in 2003 and later in 2021, the SCORE2 and SCORE2-OP were developed and recalibrated across European risk regions to estimate fatal and nonfatal CVD risk. The original SCORE was designed to predict 10-year risk of fatal CVD in apparently healthy European adults. The original SCORE model performance was evaluated in a Japanese cohort (NIPPON DATA80) showing poor calibration, again consistently overestimating cardiovascular risk 20) . The SCORE2 is an updated European cardiovascular risk prediction algorithm that estimates the 10-year risk of fatal and nonfatal CVD (myocardial infarction, stroke, or cardiovascular death) in individuals without prior CVD or diabetes aged 40-69 years 8) . The model was further expanded for older adults aged 70 years or above through SCORE2-OP 9) . This represented a major advancement from the original SCORE model, which only predicted cardiovascular mortality and thereby underestimated total CVD burden. The SCORE2 has been validated in East Asian cohorts with reasonable discrimination but variable calibration, that lead to the development of the SCORE2 Asia-Pacific model specifically recalibrated for the region. The SCORE2 Asia-Pacific model demonstrated moderate discrimination across 13 prospective studies from 12 countries involving more than 9.5 million individuals and 350,550 cardiovascular events. Although performance varied across the cohorts, with C-indices ranging from 0.605 to 0.840 and generally lower discrimination observed in lower-risk regions such as Japan, Korea, and Singapore, the model overall demonstrated acceptable predictive performance across the Asia-Pacific region.

To address the overestimation observed with Western-derived models, original risk prediction models have been developed, validated, and incorporated into clinical guidelines in several countries in East Asia 21) . In Japan, several locally derived risk prediction models, including the Hisayama score, Suita Score, JALS, and EPOCH-JAPAN, have been incorporated into guidelines from major academic societies to support cardiovascular risk stratification and preventive management of dyslipidemia, hypertension, and related risk factors 22 - 24) . In 2009, the Hisayama Study, a long-running prospective cohort study from Hisayama town in Fukuoka Prefecture initiated in 1961, developed a cardiovascular risk equation to estimate the 10-year risk of incident CVD among community-dwelling adults aged 40–84 years 25 , 26) . In 2014, the Suita Study, an urban population-based cohort conducted in Suita City, Osaka, and managed by the National Cerebral and Cardiovascular Center, published the Suita coronary heart disease score, followed by the broader Suita CVD score in 2020 27 , 28) . In 2019, the JALS pooled data from multiple Japanese cohort studies involving 67,969 adults aged 40–89 years and used multiple Poisson regression models to estimate both 5-year and 10-year risks of incident stroke, acute myocardial infarction, composite stroke plus myocardial infarction, and fatal CVD 29) . In 2021, the EPOCH-JAPAN risk equations were formally published using pooled individual participant data from eight prospective community-based cohorts across Japan (Community-based cohorts including the Suita Study (Osaka), Hisayama Study, JMS Cohort, and Osaki Cohort, and nationwide and occupational cohorts included NIPPON DATA90 and cohorts from YKK and Aichi), including 44,869 adults aged 40–79 years with a median follow-up of 12.7 years 30 , 31) . The EPOCH-JAPAN equation predicts the 10-year risk of fatal coronary heart disease, fatal stroke, and fatal cardiovascular disease.

In Korea, Western-derived risk equations, including the Framingham Risk Score and PCE, have also been shown to overestimate absolute ASCVD risk, prompting recalibration efforts and the development of Korean-specific prediction models 19 , 32) . These include the Korean Study stroke risk prediction model, the Korean Heart Study coronary heart disease prediction model , the Korean Risk Prediction Model for ASCVD, and an ASCVD risk prediction model incorporating repeated risk factor measurements in the National Health Insurance database 33 - 35) . More recent approaches have used large-scale national health screening data and machine learning methods, including deep learning algorithms and web-based ASCVD risk calculators 36 - 38) . A Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES)-based ASCVD prediction model has also been developed and externally validated, and the 5th Korean Guidelines for the Management of Dyslipidemia introduced its optional use as a risk enhancer in low- or moderate-risk individuals 39) . However, the ASCVD risk scores have not yet been adopted as the primary framework for treatment decisions in the Korean clinical guidelines.

Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic Syndrome framework

The AHA proposed the CKM syndrome framework in its 2023 Presidential Advisory to address several fundamental limitations in how CVD had traditionally been conceptualized and managed.

The framework recognizes that obesity, diabetes, CKD, and CVD are not isolated disorders but interconnected conditions driven by shared pathophysiological mechanisms, including adiposity-related inflammation, insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and multiorgan dysfunction 13) . Simultaneously, existing risk prediction models, such as the PCE, have been increasingly recognized to have important limitations, including the overestimation of cardiovascular risk in contemporary populations, limited racial and ethnic representation, omission of the kidney function, and failure to incorporate heart failure despite its growing public health burden. These developments paralleled the growing evidence of therapies such as sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, and finerenone, which simultaneously improve cardiovascular, kidney, and metabolic outcomes, reinforcing the need for a more integrated prevention strategy. The CKM construct therefore introduced a staged life-course model emphasizing earlier intervention, potential reversibility of disease progression, incorporation of social determinants of health, and interdisciplinary care across cardiology, nephrology, endocrinology, and primary care.

The CKM framework defines five stages of disease progression: Stage 0, absence of CKM risk factors; Stage 1, excess or dysfunctional adipose tissue; Stage 2, presence of metabolic risk factors or CKD; Stage 3, subclinical CVD; and Stage 4, overt clinical CVD. This staging system describes the progressive continuum of CKM syndrome and highlights opportunities for prevention and early intervention, shifting cardiovascular prevention from fragmented organ-specific care toward a more integrated cardiometabolic approach to care 40) . The CKM framework differs from other previously proposed cardiometabolic frameworks in two important ways: the explicit inclusion of CKD as a central component of the disease continuum and the introduction of a formal bidirectional staging system that emphasizes not only disease progression, but also the possibility of regression through effective intervention.

PREVENT Equations: Derivation, Validation, and Key Innovations

The PREVENT equations were developed by the CKD Prognosis Consortium and the AHA as a new CVD risk prediction tool for the US population, aligned with the CKM syndrome framework 12) . Several social, scientific, and therapeutic developments motivated the shift from PCE to PREVENT. First, concerns grew regarding the use of self-reported race (eg, White or African American) as a biological risk factor in clinical algorithms such as the PCE, as race largely reflects a social construct rather than a true biological background, particularly in the U.S. 41) . This movement was exemplified by the 2021 creatinine- and cystatin C-based equations to estimate glomerular filtration rate without race 42) . PREVENT similarly omits race as a risk factor. Instead, to account for factors associated with social disadvantage, the Social Deprivation Index (SDI) can be incorporated as an optional variable representing neighborhood socioeconomic disadvantage 43) . Second, the emergence of therapies that improve both the cardiovascular and renal outcomes has strengthened the rationale for including heart failure as an outcome and the kidney function as a predictor 44 , 45) . In PREVENT, the estimated glomerular filtration rate is incorporated as a key variable, whereas the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) is included as an optional variable. This approach reflects the CKM framework while maintaining practicality in settings where albuminuria is not routinely measured.

The PREVENT equation was derived from 25 U.S. epidemiologic cohorts and clinical databases, including several NHLBI-sponsored cohorts, such as ARIC, CARDIA, CHS, the Framingham Heart Study (Original and Offspring cohorts), MESA, and the Jackson Heart Study, comprising approximately 3.3 million adults aged 30–79 years without baseline ASCVD or heart failure. External validation was performed using 21 additional datasets, including another 3.3 million individuals ( Table 1 ) 12) . PREVENT estimates the 10-year and 30-year risks of total CVD, ASCVD, and heart failure while accounting for competing risks from non-cardiovascular death. Total CVD includes myocardial infarction, stroke, and incident heart failure, whereas ASCVD outcomes focus on myocardial infarction and stroke. Notably, the PREVENT outcome definitions do not differentiate between ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes, treating stroke as a single outcome category. The variables required for the calculation included age, sex, systolic blood pressure, antihypertensive treatment, smoking status, diabetes, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, non-HDL cholesterol, body mass index, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and statin use. Because statin use is included as a variable, the base PREVENT score can also be applied to individuals already receiving lipid-lowering therapy. The add-on or enhanced PREVENT models further incorporate hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), UACR, and the zip code–based SDI, which may improve risk prediction, particularly among individuals with CKM syndrome or health disparities.

External Validation in the US and European Cohorts

In prespecified external validation datasets, PREVENT demonstrated good calibration and discrimination for the total CVD risk, with performance that compared favorably with the PCE ( Fig.1 ) 12) . A validation study in the US Veterans Affairs population also reported better contemporary risk prediction with PREVENT than with the PCE in a multiethnic cohort 46 , 47) . Similarly, in the Kaiser Permanente Southern California healthcare electronic health record (EHR) cohort, PREVENT demonstrated better calibration for 10-year ASCVD risk compared with the PCE 48) . Furthermore, a racially and ethnically diverse primary care EHR cohort from Sutter Health suggested that PREVENT may provide reasonable risk prediction across disaggregated Asian and Hispanic subgroups within the United States. However, not all validation studies have shown consistent results. In the All of Us Research Program cohort, PREVENT overestimated ASCVD risk, a finding attributed in part to selection bias related to voluntary participation and the requirement for available biomarker data, which may have enriched the analytic sample with individuals already receiving medical care for comorbid conditions 49) . In contrast, a validation study across four large US academic health systems involving more than 270,000 adults without prior ASCVD found that PREVENT underestimated observed ASCVD incidence in several tertiary care settings 50) . Validation studies conducted in European cohorts have also yielded mixed results. In the UK Biobank, PREVENT generally demonstrated better calibration for ASCVD events than the PCE, although calibration differed by sex 51) . A Swiss population-based validation reported an underestimation of ASCVD risk compared with the observed events 52) . These findings highlight that PREVENT may be more transportable than older equations, but not immune to regional differences in baseline event rates, treatment practices, and outcome definitions.

External Validation in East Asian Cohorts

To date, the most comprehensive nationwide evaluation of PREVENT in East Asia comes from the National Health Information Database of the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS), a nationwide, single-payer database covering the entire South Korean population (approximately 50 million individuals) through a universal healthcare system and biennial health screening programs. In this validation study 53) , 7.7 million adults aged 30–79 years without either prior cardiovascular disease or end-stage kidney disease were followed for a median of 13.3 years. The PREVENT demonstrated good discrimination across outcomes, with C-statistics ranging from 0.766 to 0.805. The calibration was reasonable for total cardiovascular disease and it was particularly favorable for ASCVD prediction, although the heart failure risk tended to be over-predicted, especially among men.

A second validation was conducted in the Kailuan cohort, a large prospective regional community-based cohort study initiated in 2006 in Tangshan, China, involving employees and retirees of the Kailuan Group who underwent standardized biennial health examinations through an integrated health-care system 54 , 55) . For the PREVENT validation analysis, 79,497 adults aged 40 to 79 years without prior CVD were included after excluding individuals outside the target age range and those with baseline CVD. Over a median follow-up of 10 years, 4,425 ASCVD events were recorded. Discrimination was good in women and moderate in men, with C-statistics of approximately 0.74 and 0.69, respectively. A calibration analysis demonstrated that PREVENT modestly underestimated ASCVD risk, particularly among men, whereas PCE substantially overestimated the risk in both sexes.

These findings suggest the potential transportability of PREVENT to East Asian populations, especially when a universal risk tool is desired for global trials or observational studies. These results contrast with the earlier view that Western-derived ASCVD risk equations invariably overestimate the risk in East Asian populations. Differences in population-level ASCVD incidence and mortality remain important, but such differences may narrow substantially after adjusting for age, sex, and contemporary risk factor distributions. Residual miscalibration may warrant recalibration, but such efforts should be undertaken only after careful assessment of outcome ascertainment, competing risks, treatment patterns, and the possibility that apparent differences reflect data quality rather than biology. It is also important to note that the PREVENT equations do not distinguish between ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke, incorporating both within a single stroke outcome. This may be particularly relevant in East Asian populations, where hemorrhagic stroke accounts for a greater proportion of cerebrovascular events than in Western populations. Although studies from Korea have demonstrated the favorable performance of PREVENT, further validation in Japanese and other East Asian cohorts is important to determine whether differences in stroke subtype distribution influence model calibration and risk estimation.

Challenges in Predicting Incident Heart Failure

Heart failure prediction appears particularly sensitive to population characteristics and diagnostic practices, as shown in validation studies conducted in the US. In MESA, PREVENT overestimated the heart failure risk despite the cohort contributing to derivations, whereas underestimation has been reported among selected younger racial and ethnic groups 56) . These observations do not undermine the conceptual value of heart failure prediction, but highlight the need to evaluate heart failure models separately from ASCVD models and consider how heart failure is defined and captured across health systems. As the prevalence of heart failure continues to rise in East Asia, the prevention of this condition has become increasingly important because of its major impact on the quality of life and economic burden on healthcare systems 57 , 58) .

Earlier heart failure risk prediction tools provided an important foundation for risk stratification but also highlighted challenges related to heterogeneity, limited validation, and uncertain clinical utility. For example, the Health ABC heart failure score developed from 2,935 community-dwelling participants of older adults aged 70 to 79 years, used nine routinely available clinical variables to estimate 5-year heart failure risk and demonstrated good discrimination with C-statistics of approximately 0.73–0.74 across derivation and validation cohorts 59 , 60) . Similarly, the ARIC study developed a more parsimonious 10-year heart failure risk model in 13,555 participants with a C-statistic of 0.80, while incorporation of N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) further improved discrimination to approximately 0.81, supporting the role of biomarker-enhanced heart failure prediction in clinical and electronic health record settings 61) . Nonetheless, the broader clinical utility of these scores remains incompletely validated, as differences in heart failure definitions, healthcare systems, and event ascertainment may substantially influence model performance across populations. Whereas earlier studies such as ARIC relied on physician-adjudicated events, PREVENT incorporated large EHR-based datasets that often depended primarily on the International Classification of Disease (ICD) coding. Emerging natural language processing and artificial intelligence (AI) based approaches may help improve future validation by integrating diagnostic codes with clinical narratives and longitudinal records 62) .

Another challenge is that many predictors are measured only at a single baseline time point, whereas the development of heart failure is inherently trajectory-based. Clinically relevant risk markers, including natriuretic peptides, imaging findings, renal function, and glycemic status, evolve over time. Thus, single measurements may provide only a limited snapshot of a dynamic disease process and may fail to fully capture longitudinal trajectories in heart failure development, potentially identifying only those at the highest risk while missing individuals at intermediate risk who happen to demonstrate moderate values at a single time point. The STOP-HF study illustrates this point, where BNP-guided screening combined with serial monitoring and collaborative care reduced the incidence of newly diagnosed heart failure and left ventricular dysfunction, suggesting that risk prediction may be most effective when paired with structured preventive interventions rather than used solely for risk labeling 63 , 64) .

Add-On PREVENT Models and Social Determinants of Health

The incorporation of albuminuria as an add-on to the PREVENT model may be particularly relevant in East Asia, where CKD, diabetes, population aging, and cardiometabolic multimorbidity are increasingly contributing to cardiovascular risk and healthcare burden. In Japan and Korea, the serum creatinine levels are widely measured through annual health checkup programs, although albuminuria is not universally collected within standard screening systems 65 , 66) . Growing evidence suggests that reliance on serum creatinine alone may miss a substantial proportion of individuals at elevated cardiovascular and renal risk, who could otherwise be identified through albuminuria 67) . Furthermore, recent Japanese health-economic modeling has suggested the potential value of repeated UACR testing, even among non-diabetic adults, supporting the evaluation of albuminuria-based screening strategies 68) . This issue has important implications for countries such as Japan, where the prevention of progression to end-stage kidney disease and hemodialysis represents a major public health and healthcare economic priority in one of the world’s largest dialysis populations 69) .

In the add-on PREVENT model, the US SDI was incorporated as a surrogate for the social determinants of health, although direct application outside the United States remains challenging given the differences in socioeconomic structures and healthcare systems. The index may need to be replaced with locally validated measures in the future. For example, in Japan, census-based measures, such as the Area Deprivation Index, may provide a more appropriate local analog, as area-level deprivation has been associated with mortality and healthy life expectancy 70 , 71) . However, the association of geographic deprivation indices may be smaller in countries with narrower income inequality, as reflected by lower Gini coefficients. Disparities between urban and rural areas are increasingly recognized as important determinants of health, influenced not only by where individuals live but also by the broader community’s capacity to sustain accessible and functional healthcare systems.

Future Directions

Modernizing EHR and Data Infrastructure

PREVENT reflects the broader transition toward large-scale EHR–based epidemiology capable of capturing longitudinal clinical data from millions of individuals. In the US, large EHR platforms such as Epic Cosmos (~295 million patients, ＞250 health systems), TriNetX (＞150 million patients globally, 182 healthcare organizations), Optum EHR (＞80 million patients, ＞600 hospitals), Cerner Real-World Data (＞1.2 billion clinical encounters, 86–110 health systems), and Truveta (＞120 million individuals, 30–31 health systems) have created large multi-institutional data ecosystem for cardiovascular research 72 , 73) . Compared with these platforms, East Asian EHR databases remain smaller and less interoperable, highlighting the need for further investment in scalable and research-ready data infrastructure. Although EHR-based datasets remain imperfect, the field is moving toward greater granularity and cross-validation using not only administrative codes, but also clinical notes and other EHR documentation to improve adjudication. This evolution may become increasingly important with the emergence of generative AI and large language models capable of extracting clinically relevant information from complex unstructured data.

Shared Decision-Making and AI-Augmented Prevention

PREVENT reflects the direction in which modern epidemiological cohorts and preventive cardiology are evolving. The incorporation of CKM-related variables further encourages closer collaboration among cardiologists, nephrologists, endocrinologists, primary care physicians, and other healthcare professionals to address shared CKM risk factors using a more unified framework. Such interdisciplinary approaches may ultimately promote earlier screening and more aggressive preventive interventions across specialties. However, one of the greatest challenges remains communication with individuals at earlier stages of the disease, who may derive the greatest long-term benefit from prevention. Risk communication for asymptomatic conditions is often undervalued and difficult to fully appreciate for patients, frequently requiring time-consuming discussions to achieve adequate understanding and shared decision-making in already time-constrained clinical settings. As healthcare moves further into the AI era, such communication may become increasingly augmented through personalized educational tools adapted to patients’ health literacy, educational background, and learning preferences outside the clinical environment 74 , 75) . PREVENT equations and other risk calculators are increasingly being integrated into web-based tools, EHRs, and clinical workflows to support shared decision-making and the identification of individuals who may benefit from preventive therapies. EHR integration enables automated risk estimation and guideline-concordant recommendations using routinely collected clinical data, potentially shifting prevention away from a parameter-by-parameter approach in which elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, hypertension, or diabetes are treated primarily as isolated numerical abnormalities rather than components of an interconnected CKM continuum. Although AI may help patients better understand health information and long-term risks, human interaction, empathy, and physicians communicating risks clearly and honestly based on scientific evidence will remain integral to preventive care.

Conclusions

PREVENT represents an important shift from isolated ASCVD estimation toward integrated CKM risk assessment through the incorporation of the kidney function, race-free modeling, and longer-term risk horizons, aligning with the evolving CVD burden in East Asia. Early validation studies from Korea and China suggest reasonable transportability across East Asian populations and raise the possibility that PREVENT may also perform reasonably well in Japan, given the similar epidemiological characteristics. However, validation and recalibration remain essential before widespread implementation, and the 30-year and add-on PREVENT models, as well as models predicting incident heart failure events, remain largely unvalidated across diverse populations and healthcare settings.
