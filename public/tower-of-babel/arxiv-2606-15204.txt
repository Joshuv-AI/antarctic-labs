A generalization of U ​ Q UQ rings
Peter Danchev, Mina Doostalizadeh, Omid Hasanzadeh

Abstract
. We examine the newly defined class of n n - U ​ Q UQ rings described by the condition that u n − 1 ∈ Q ​ N ​ ( R ) u^{n}-1\in QN(R) for every unit u ∈ U ⁡ ( R ) u\in U(R) , where Q ​ N ​ ( R ) QN(R) denotes the set of quasi-nilpotent elements (see [ 22 ] ). This class naturally extends the recently defined class of rings in [ 11 ] and [ 12 ] , as well as expectedly generalizes previously explored concepts such as U ​ J UJ , U ​ U UU and U ​ Q UQ rings. We conduct here a comprehensive structural analysis of these n n - U ​ Q UQ rings and study their stability under various ring-theoretic constructions including matrix rings, group rings, trivial extensions and power series rings. As a result, several new characterizations are established, thus revealing relevant connections between n n - U ​ Q UQ rings and fundamental classes of rings such as reduced, clean, exchange, semi-regular and potent rings, respectively. Moreover, we prove that the classes of n n - U ​ J UJ and n n - U ​ U UU rings are properly contained in the class of n n - U ​ Q UQ rings. These achievements not only unify and expand existing theories in this branch, but also provide a robust framework for possible further investigations into the interplay between the unit behavior and quasi-nilpotency in noncommutative ring theory.

1.
Introduction and Background

Through the text, let
R
be an associative
not
necessarily commutative ring with identity. For such a ring, we denote by
U(R)
the group of units, by
Nil(R)
the set of nilpotent elements, by
QN(R)
the set of quasi-nilpotent elements, by
C(R)
the set of central elements, and by
Id(R)
the set of idempotent elements of
R
. The Jacobson radical of
R
is denoted by
J(R)
. Moreover, the rings of all
n\times n
matrices and all
n\times n
upper triangular matrices over
R
are, respectively, designed by
M_{n}(R)
and
T_{n}(R)
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Recall also that a ring
R
is said to be
abelian
provided that each idempotent in
R
lies in its center, that is,
Id(R)\subset C(R)
.

Some extra classical fundamentals that we need in the sequel are like these: A ring
R
is called
regular
(respectively,
unit-regular
) in the sense of von Neumann if, for each
a\in R
, there is
x\in R
(respectively,
x\in U(R)
) such that
axa=a
. In particular, the ring
R
is said to be
strongly regular
if
a\in a^{2}R
for all
a\in R
. Recall, besides, that a ring
R
is called an
exchange ring
if, for each
a\in R
, there is an idempotent
e=e^{2}\in aR
such that
1-e\in(1-a)R
. Moreover,
R
is termed
clean
if every element of
R
can be written as a sum of a unit and an idempotent (see
[
21
]
); in case these two elements commute the ring is named
strongly clean
. It is principally known that every clean ring is exchange, though the converse implication does
not
hold in general; however, it is valid in the abelian case (see
[
21
, Proposition 1.8]
). In addition, a ring
R
is said to be
semi-regular
if the quotient
R/J(R)
is regular and all idempotents of
R
lift modulo
J(R)
. It is well confirmed that all semi-regular rings are exchange, yet the converse fails in all generality (see, for instance,
[
21
]
).

Concerning the modern terminology, we can say the following: It is well known that the inclusion
1+J(R)\subseteq U(R)
holds always. Referring to
[
8
]
or
[
17
]
, we define a ring
R
to be a
JU-ring
whenever the equality
U(R)=1+J(R)
holds. More generally, a ring
R
is called an
n
-UJ ring
provided
u-u^{n}\in J(R)
for each
u\in U(R)
, where
n\geq 2
is a fixed integer, or equivalently,
u^{n-1}-1\in J(R)
for each
u\in U(R)
and
n\geq 2
. This concept was initially introduced by Danchev in
[
10
]
, and later independently by Koşan
et al.
in
[
18
]
. Note that for
n=1
these rings coincide exactly with the aforementioned
JU-rings
, often termed also as
UJ-rings
. It was incidentally shown there that the properties of being semi-regular, exchange and clean are all equivalent for
2n
-UJ rings.

In a related direction, mimicking
[
13
]
, we recall that
R
is called a
UU-ring
whenever
U(R)=1+Nil(R)
is true, noticing that the containment
1+Nil(R)\subseteq U(R)
is fulfilled always. Generally, for a fixed integer
n\geq 2
, a ring
R
is said to be
n
-UU
if
u^{n}-1\in Nil(R)
for every
u\in U(R)
. This notion was firstly introduced by Danchev in
[
9
]
and was further investigated in greater detail in
[
12
]
. In particular, it was shown in
[
12
]
that a ring
R
is simultaneously
(n-1)
-UU and strongly
\pi
-regular for any
n>1
if, and only if,
R
is strongly
n
-nil-clean. These rings serve as a natural generalization of the aforementioned UU-rings. This class of rings was originally introduced by Călugăreanu in
[
2
]
, and further studied by Danchev and Lam in
[
13
]
, where it was established that

is strongly nil-clean in the sense of
[
14
]
if, and only if,
R
is an exchange UU-ring. Moreover, in
[
15
]
, Zhou
et al.
proved that
R
is a UU ring if, and only if, every unit in
R
is uniquely nil-clean as defined in
[
14
]
.

An element
a
of a ring
R
is called
quasi-nilpotent
if
1-ax
is invertible for every
x\in R
satisfying
xa=ax
. It is immediate that both
Nil(R)
and
J(R)
are (possibly proper) subsets of
QN(R)
. Note that quasi-nilpotent elements play a significant role in exploring the intricate structure of Banach algebras. Additionally, utilizing quasi-nilpotent, several notable concepts have been developed which include (strongly) J-clean rings (
[
5
]
), nil-clean rings and its strong and unique versions (
[
14
]
), generalized Drazin inverses (
[
16
]
), quasi-polar rings (
[
23
]
), as well as some other ones.

Further on, in 2024, Danchev
et al.
introduced
[
11
]
the concepts of UQ rings and
strongly quasi-nil-clean
rings, which serve as non-trivial extensions of
UJ rings
and
strongly J-clean rings
, respectively. In fact, a ring
R
is said to be a
UQ ring
if
1+U(R)=QN(R)
. Furthermore,
R
is called
strongly quasi-nil-clean
if every element of
R
can be expressed as a sum of an idempotent and a quasi-nilpotent element that commute each another. It was shown there that a ring
R
is strongly quasi-nil-clean if, and only if, it is both strongly clean and UQ.

As a common extension of the aforementioned classes, the novel concept of
n
-UQ rings
was suggested in
[
22
]
for an arbitrary integer
n>1
as follows: A ring
R
is said to be
n
-UQ
if, for each unit
u\in U(R)
, its
n
-th power
u^{n}
can be expressed as a sum of an idempotent and a quasi-nilpotent that commute one another; equivalently, it can be written that
u^{n}=1+q
, where
q\in QN(R)
. This class logically expands all
UQ rings
(and, thereby, includes the class of unit uniquely clean rings as proposed in
[
3
]
), as well as rings possessing precisely two units. While every
n
-UJ
ring (and, hence, every
UJ
ring) belongs to the class of
n
-UQ
rings, the opposed claim does
not
hold in general, thus highlighting the genuine novelty of this newly defined class.

The principal aim motivating this work is to provide an in-depth characterization of these
n
-UQ rings by thoroughly examining their intrinsic algebraic structures and their interrelations with the well-examined classes of
n
-UU and
n
-UJ rings. Likewise, we seek to uncover new, previously unobserved properties of
n
-UQ
rings, thereby expanding the theoretical framework and enriching the current understanding of ring theory. Our findings are expected to bridge gaps between these various ring classes and to open avenues for future research, especially in the context of applications where the interplay between idempotent and quasi-nilpotent elements governs structural and functional behaviors.

.

Moreover, when

, that is the ring consisting of

over

.

Additionally, the set of units of the trivial extension

We standardly designate by

-bi-module and

an
R
-bi-module. Then, the following issues hold:

(i)

The trivial extension
T(R,M)
is
n
-
UQ
if, and only if,
R
is
n
-
UQ
.

(ii)

The formal triangular matrix ring
\begin{pmatrix}R&N\\
0&S\end{pmatrix}
is
n
-
UQ
if, and only if, both
R
and
S
are
n
-
UQ
.

(iii)

For any
n\geq 1
, the triangular matrix ring
T_{n}(R)
is
n
-
UQ
if, and only if,
R
is
n
-
UQ
.

(iv)

For any
n\geq 1
, the quotient ring
R[x]/\langle x^{n}\rangle
is
n
-
UQ
if, and only if,
R
is
n
-
UQ
.

(v)

The power series ring
R[[x]]
is
n
-
UQ
if, and only if,
R
is
n
-
UQ
.

Proof.

We need to establish only case (i), because the remaining cases follow from Lemma
2.11
using a similar line of reasoning.

To that goal, assume that
T(R,M)
is an
n
-
UQ
ring. Since
R\cong T(R,0)
, we may interpret
R
as a subring of
T(R,M)
. Hence, Proposition
2.4
(iv) works to find that
R
is an
n
-
UQ
ring.

Conversely, suppose that
R
is an
n
-
UQ
ring, and choose
(u,m)\in U(T(R,M))
. Then,
u\in U(R)
. Since
R
is
n
-UQ, we have
1-u^{2}\in QN(R)
. Employing Lemma
2.11
, we obtain

(1,0)-(u,m)^{n}=(1-u^{n},*)\in T(QN(R),M)\subseteq QN(T(R,M)).

That is why,
T(R,M)
is an
n
-
UQ
ring, as wanted.
∎

Let
Nil_{*}(R)
denote the
prime radical
(also known as the
lower nil-radical
) of a ring
R
that means the intersection of all
prime ideals
of
R
. It is globally known that
Nil_{*}(R)
is a nil-ideal of
R
.

On the same hand, a ring
R
is said to be
2-primal
if its prime radical coincides with the set of all nilpotent elements of
R
or, in other words, if
Nil_{*}(R)=Nil(R)
. It is well-established that both reduced rings and commutative rings are non-trivial examples of 2-primal rings.

As usual, we denote by
R[x]
the
ring of polynomials
over
R
.

In accordance with our preceding result, a ring
R
is
n
-UQ absolutely when the power series ring
R[[x]]
is
n
-UQ. This readily leads us to the question: Under what extra circumstances the polynomial ring
R[x]
also possesses the
n
-UQ property, provided that
R
does? In what follows, we aim to address this query remembering firstly a well-known affirmation.

Lemma 2.13
.

[
11
, Corollary 3.2.]

Let
R
be a 2-primal ring. Then, the following equality is true:

J(R[x])=QN(R[x])=\text{Nil}(R)[x]=\text{Nil}_{*}(R)[x]=\text{Nil}_{*}(R[x])

We now menage to prove the following slightly curious assertion.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
R
be a 2-primal ring. Then, the following four conditions are equivalent:

(i)

R
is an
n
-
UU
ring.

(ii)

R[x]
is an
n
-
UQ
ring.

(iii)

R[x]
is an
n
-
UJ
ring.

(iv)

R[x]
is an
n
-
UU
ring.

Proof.

Both implications (iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii) and (iv)
\Rightarrow
(ii) are pretty simple, since we always have that
\text{Nil}(R),J(R)\subseteq QN(R)
. The equivalence (ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii) follows immediately from Lemma
2.13
.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(iv): Write
u=\sum_{i=0}^{n}u_{i}x^{i}\in U(R[x])
. Since
R
is 2-primal,
[
6
, Theorem 2.5]
enables us that
u_{0}\in U(R)
and
u_{i}\in\text{Nil}_{*}(R)
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.
As
R
is
n
-
UU
, we derive
1-u_{0}^{n}\in\text{Nil}(R)
. So, in view of the ideal property of
\text{Nil}_{*}(R)
, it follows that

1-u^{n}\in(1-u_{0}^{n})+\text{Nil}_{*}(R)[x]x\subseteq\text{Nil}_{*}(R)[x]=\text{Nil}_{*}(R[x])\subseteq\text{Nil}(R[x]),

as required.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(i): Given
u\in U(R)\subseteq U(R[x])
, then
1-u^{n}\in\text{Nil}(R[x])\cap R\subseteq\text{Nil}(R)
. Hence,
R
is an
n
-
UU
ring.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): Chosen
u\in U(R)\subseteq U(R[x])
, then
1-u^{n}\in QN(R[x])=\text{Nil}_{*}(R)[x]
. Thus,
1-u^{n}\in\text{Nil}_{*}(R)=\text{Nil}(R)
.

The claim sustained after all.
∎

3.
Main Results on
n
-
UQ
Rings

This section explores how
n
-UQ rings are related to other important classes of rings such as regular rings, semi-potent rings and potent rings. For clarity, recall that a ring
R
is called
semi-potent
if every one-sided ideal outside
J(R)
contains a non-zero idempotent. Furthermore, a semi-potent ring is said to be
potent
when all idempotents can be lifted modulo
J(R)
.

We are now in a position to show validity of the following result.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
R
be a semi-potent ring. The following are equivalent:

(i)

The ring
R/J(R)
is a (2n-1)-UQ ring.

(ii)

The ring
R/J(R)
has the identity
x^{2n}=x
.

(iii)

The ring
R
is a (2n-1)-UJ ring.

(iv)

The ring
R/J(R)
is a (2n-1)-UU ring.

Proof.

The implications (iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv)
\Rightarrow
(i) are self-evident.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): First, in virtue of Lemma
2.7
, we know that
R/J(R)
is reduced. Now, suppose there is
a\in R/J(R)
such that
a-a^{2n}\neq 0
in
R/J(R)
. Since
R/J(R)
is semi-potent, there is an idempotent
e=e^{2}\in R/J(R)
such that
e\in(a-a^{2n})R/J(R)
. So,
e=(a-a^{2n})b
for some
b\in R/J(R)
.

As
R/J(R)
is abelian, we extract that

e=ea(1-a^{2n-1})b=e(1-a^{2n-1})ab.

Thus, Proposition
2.6
reaches us that
ea,\,e(1-a^{2n-1})\in U(eR/J(R)e)
.

In the other vein, we observe that

(ea)^{2n-1}+e(1-a^{2n-1})=e.\qquad(*)

Since
R/J(R)
is a
(2n-1)
-UQ ring, Proposition
2.4
(v) ensures that
eR/J(R)e
is also
(2n-1)
-UQ. Consequently, equation
(*)
contradicts Lemma
2.9
. Hence,
R/J(R)
satisfies the identity
x^{2n}=x
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii): Put
u\in U(R)
. Then, by (ii), we arrive at
u-u^{2n}\in J(R)
. Since
J(R)
is an ideal and
u
is a unit, it follows that
1-u^{2n-1}\in J(R)
. Thus,
R
is a
(2n-1)
-UJ ring, as desired.
∎

Some additional background is now helpful to be recollected: A ring
R
is called
\pi
-regular
if, for each
a\in R
,
a^{n}\in a^{n}Ra^{n}
for some integer
n
depending on
a
. So, regular rings are always
\pi
-regular. Likewise, a ring
R
is said to be
strongly
\pi
-regular
provided that, for any
a\in R
, there exists
n
depending on
a
such that
a^{n}\in a^{n+1}R
.

We are now ready to formulate and prove the following consequence.

Corollary 3.2
.

The following four statements are equivalent for a
(2n-1)
-
UQ
ring
R
:

(i)

R
is a regular ring.

(ii)

R
is a
\pi
-regular reduced ring.

(iii)

R
is a strongly regular ring.

(iv)

R
is a unit-regular ring.

(v)

R
satisfies the identity
x^{2n}=x
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): Since
R
is a regular ring, it must be that
J(R)=(0)
. Moreover,
R
is a semi-potent ring, because, for every
0\neq a\in R
, there is
b\in R
such that
a=aba
. Apparently,
e:=ab\in aR
is an idempotent. So, Lemma
2.7
assures that
R
is reduced.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): This follows from
[
1
, Theorem 3]
.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(iii): Looking at (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii), we may assume
R
is reduced (and hence abelian). Therefore,
R
is a strongly regular ring.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv): This is always true.

(iv), (v)
\Rightarrow
(i): This is quite elementary.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(v): Since every regular ring is semi-potent and
J(R)=(0)
, the result follows at once from Theorem
3.1
.
∎

Our next chief result sounds surprisingly like this.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
R
be a potent ring. Then, the following six conditions are equivalent:

(i)

R
is a
(2n-1)
-
UQ
ring.

(ii)

R/J(R)
is a
(2n-1)
-
UQ
ring.

(iii)

R/J(R)
satisfies the identity
x^{2n}=x
.

(iv)

R
is a
(2n-1)
-
UJ
ring.

(v)

R/J(R)
is a
(2n-1)
-
UJ
ring.

(vi)

R/J(R)
is a
(2n-1)
-
UU
ring.

Proof.

One observes that Theorem
3.1
establishes the equivalences (ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iv)
\Leftrightarrow
(vi), whereas
[
18
, Proposition 2.9]
proves (iv)
\Leftrightarrow
(v). The implication (iv)
\Rightarrow
(i) is immediate, leaving only (i)
\Rightarrow
(iii) to be verified below.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(iii): Since
R
is a semi-potent ring, we find that
\overline{R}=R/J(R)
is also semi-potent. We will show now that
\overline{R}
is a reduced ring.

To that purpose, suppose
x^{2}=0
but
0\neq x\in\overline{R}
. Then,
[
20
, Theorem 2.1]
teaches us that there is
\bar{e}\in\overline{R}
such that
\bar{e}\overline{R}\bar{e}\cong M_{2}(S)
, where
S
is a non-zero ring. This, however. contradicts Lemma
2.10
(ii). Hence,
\overline{R}
is reduced.

Now, suppose there is
a\in\overline{R}
with
a-a^{2n}\neq 0
in
\overline{R}
. Since
\overline{R}
is semi-potent, there is
0\neq e=e^{2}\in\overline{R}
such that
e\in(a-a^{2n})\overline{R}
. Thus,
e=(a-a^{2n})b
for some
b\in\overline{R}
. Since
e
is central, we have

e=ea\cdot e(1-a^{2n-1})\cdot ey,

and so both
ea,e(1-a^{2n-1})\in U(e\overline{R}e)
. So,
(ea)^{2n-1}+e(1-a^{2n-1})=e
, contradicting Lemma
2.10
(i). Finally,
\overline{R}
has the identity
x^{2n}=x
, as pursued.
∎

Three more consequences state as follows.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
R
be a
(2n-1)
-
UQ
ring. Then, the following three points are equivalent:

(i)

R
is an exchange ring.

(ii)

R
is a clean ring.

(iii)

R
is a semi-regular ring.

Proof.

(ii)\Rightarrow(i)
: It is clear.

(i)\Rightarrow(ii)
: If
R
is exchange
(2n-1)
-
UQ
, then
R
is reduced with Lemma
2.7
at hand, and hence it is abelian. Therefore,
R
is abelian exchange, so it is clean (see
[
21
]
).

(i)\Rightarrow(iii)
: Since
R
is exchange, all idempotents lift modulo
J(R)
(see
[
21
]
). Also, with the aid of Theorem
3.3
,
R/J(R)
possesses the identity
x^{2n}=x
. So,
R
is semi-regular.

(iii)\Rightarrow(i)
: It is plain, because semi-regular rings are always exchange.
∎

To simplify exposition, we henceforth will say that a potent ring whose elements modulo Jacobson radical satisfy the equation
x^{2n}=x
is just
semi-
(2n)
-potent
.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
R
be a ring. Then,
R
is a
(2n-1)
-
UQ
clean ring if, and only if,
R
is semi-
(2n)
-potent.

Proof.

Adapting Theorem
3.3
, every clean
(2n-1)
-UQ ring is semi-
(2n)
-potent.

Conversely, assume
R
is semi-
(2n)
-potent. Set
u\in U(R)
, and let
u=e+j
be a semi-
(2n)
-potent representation of
u
with
e^{2n-1}\in Id(R)
and
j\in J(R)
. Then, one sees that

e=u-j\in U(R)+J(R)\subseteq U(R)\Rightarrow e^{2n-1}\in U(R)\cap Id(R)=\{1\}

and, consequently,
u^{2n-1}=e^{2n-1}+j
for some
j\in J(R)
. Hence, one obtains that

u^{2n-1}\in 1+J(R)\subseteq 1+QN(R).

We now show that
R
is a clean ring. In fact, since
R/J(R)
has the identity
x^{2n}=x
, it follows that
R/J(R)
is strongly regular and so
[
7
, Theorem 1.1.2]
employs to get that it is clean. Moreover, by the definition of semi-
(2n)
-potent rings, idempotents lift modulo
J(R)
. Therefore,
R
is a clean ring, as asked.
∎

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
R
be an artinian (in particular, a finite) ring. Then, the following three conditions are equivalent:

(i)

R
is a
(2n-1)
-
UQ
ring.

(ii)

R
is a
(2n-1)
-
UJ
ring.

(iii)

R
is a
(2n-1)
-
UU
ring.

Proof.

As established in
[
4
, Corollary 6]
, every artinian ring is clean which, in conjunction with Theorem
3.3
, insures the equivalence between the
(2n-1)
-
UQ
and
(2n-1)
-
UJ
conditions in this context.

Moreover, the artinian assumption is a guarantor that the Jacobson radical
J(R)
is contained in the nil-radical
Nil(R)
. So,
[
1
, Lemma 2.1]
can be applied to get that the ring
R
satisfies the
(2n-1)
-
UU
property if, and only if, the factor-ring
R/J(R)
does. Furthermore, Theorem
3.3
tells us that the
(2n-1)
-
UJ
and
(2n-1)
-
UU
properties are equivalent for artinian rings, thereby completing the argument.
∎

We are now concerned with group rings. To that goal, let
R
be any ring and
G
any group. We traditionally denote by
RG
the
group ring
of
G
over
R
. Likewise, the
augmentation ideal

\Delta(RG)
is defined as the kernel of the standard augmentation homomorphism
\varepsilon:RG\to R
, where

\varepsilon\left(\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g\right)=\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}.

Remember also that a group
G
is called a
p
-group
if all its elements have orders that are powers of a prime
p
, and
locally finite
if every finitely generated subgroup is finite.

What we can perceive in this matter are the following two claims.

Proposition 3.7
.

(i)

If
RG
is an
n
-
UQ
ring, then
R
is also an
n
-
UQ
ring.

(ii)

If
R
is an
n
-
UQ
ring and
G
is a locally finite
p
-group, where
p
is a prime number such that
p\in J(R)
, then
RG
is an
n
-
UQ
ring.

Proof.

(i)

Since
R
is a rationally closed subring of
RG
, Proposition
2.4
(iv) informs us that
R
inherits the
n
-
UQ
property of
RG
.

(ii)

Regarding
[
24
, Lemma 2]
, we have
\Delta(RG)\subseteq J(RG)
. So, the isomorphism
RG/\Delta(RG)\cong R
along with Corollary
2.8
give us the conclusion.

∎

We terminate our work with the following observation.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
RG
be an n-
UQ
ring with
3\in J(RG)
, and let
G
be a
2
-group. Then,
G
is a group of exponent
2
.

Proof.

First of all, we prove for every
g\in G
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
that
1+g^{2^{k}}\in U(RG)
. Since
R\langle g\rangle
is a rationally closed subring of
RG
, invoking Proposition
2.4
(iv) we deduce that
R\langle g\rangle
is also an
n
-
UQ
ring. Therefore, without loss of generality, we may assume that the element
g
is central.

As
G
is a
2
-group, let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be the smallest integer with
g^{2^{k}}=1
. Consequently, one inspects that
1-g^{2^{k}}=0
. Since
3\in J(RG)
, we check that

1+g^{2^{k}}=1-g^{2^{k}}-g^{2^{k}}+3g^{2^{k}}=-g^{2^{k}}+3g^{2^{k}}\in U(RG)+J(RG)\subseteq U(RG).

Now, consider the equality
(1-g^{2^{k-1}})(1+g^{2^{k-1}})=0
. Since
1+g^{2^{k-1}}\in U(RG)
, we must have
1=g^{2^{k-1}}
, which leads to an impossibility. That is why,
G
must be a group of exponent
2
, as formulated.
∎

.
