On k k -th power Diophantine triples of the form { a k , b , c } \{a^{k},b,c\}
Clemens Fuchs and Miriam Schönauer

Abstract
. In this paper, we prove that there are no k k -th power Diophantine triples of the form { a k , b , c } \{a^{k},b,c\} for k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 and 1 < a k < b < c 1<a^{k}<b<c .

1.
Introduction

The mathematician Diophantus of Alexandria was the first to consider the problem of finding a set of four rational numbers such that the product of any two elements increased by one is a perfect square.
He discovered a set of numbers that satisfy this property:
\left\{\frac{1}{16},\frac{33}{16},\frac{17}{4},\frac{105}{16}\right\}
.
The first set of integers with this property was found by Fermat, namely
\left\{1,3,8,120\right\}
.
Now, we call a set of
m
positive integers
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\}
a Diophantine
m
-tuple if
a_{i}a_{j}+1
is a perfect square for all
1\leq i<j\leq m
.

The question of how large these sets can be has created a wide and active field of research. To name only a few, one of the first important results was by Baker and Davenport
[
2
]
, who showed that for a Diophantine quadruple of the form
\{1,3,8,d\}
the positive integer
d
must be
120
. Thus, showing that this quadruple could not be extended to a quintuple.

The first absolute bound for Diophantine
m
-tuples was established in 2001 by Dujella
[
8
]
, who showed that
m\leq 8
. In 2004 the same author further sharpened the upper bound and proved that there are no Diophantine sextuples and only finitely many quintuples, see
[
9
]
. Finally, He, Togbé and Ziegler
[
12
]
proved that there are no Diophantine quintuples. A detailed overview of the literature and results on Diophantine
m
-tuple can be found on the webpage
[
11
]
, see also
[
10
]
.

As a generalization of Diophantine
m
-tuples one may consider sets of
m
positive integers
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\}
such that
a_{i}a_{j}+1
is a perfect
k
-th power for all
1\leq i<j\leq m
and
k\geq 3
. Such sets are called
k
-th power Diophantine
m
-tuples. Examples of such sets for
k=3
and
k=4
are
\{2,171,25326\}
and
\{1352,9539880,9768370\}
, respectively. Bugeaud and Dujella
[
7
]
examined
k
-th power Diophantine
m
-tuples in 2003 and established absolute bounds for them, namely if

C_{k}:=\sup\{|\mathit{A}|:\mathit{A}\text{ is a }k\text{-th power Diophantine }m\text{-tuple}\}

for
k\geq 3
then it holds that
C_{3}\leq 7,C_{4}\leq 5
,
C_{5}\leq 5
,
C_{k}\leq 4
for
6\leq k\leq 176
and
C_{k}\leq 3
for
k\geq 177
. As pointed out by Bérczes, Dujella, Hajdu and Luca
[
5
]
a slight inaccuracy in the proof of
[
7
, Corollary 4]
resulted in the bound
C_{5}\leq 4
instead of
C_{5}\leq 5
.
Additionally, it follows from
[
12
]
that for even
k
, it holds that
C_{k}\leq 4
.
In 2004 Bugeaud
[
6
]
showed that for a
k
-th power Diophantine triple of the form
\{1,a,b\}
,
k
may not exceed
74
. In 2007 Bennett
[
4
]
sharpened this result employing several techniques such as the hypergeometric method of Thue and Siegel as well as a number of gap principles and proved that there are no
k
-th power Diophantine triple of the form
\{1,a,b\}
for
k\geq 3
. Following these techniques, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k\geq 3
and
1<a<b<c
be integers with
a^{k}<b<c
. Then there exists no
k
-th power Diophantine triple of the form
\{a^{k},b,c\}
.

Overview

The proof strategy follows closely the approach presented in
[
4
]
and begins with the assumption that there exists a
k
-th power Diophantine triple as defined in Theorem
1.1
. Next, we show that
c
must be much larger than
a
and
b
and derive a good rational approximation of

\sqrt[k]{1+\frac{1}{a^{k}b}}.

Then Lemma
2.1
yields a contradiction for
k\geq 5
. As outlined in Bennett
[
4
]
, for the cases
k=4
and
k=3
, we introduce gap principles and show that
a^{2}t/s
is a convergent of the infinite simple continued fraction expansion of
\sqrt[k]{a^{k}b}
. This yields a contradiction in all but a finite number of cases.
The remaining cases are then resolved using computer calculations of the continued fraction expansion of
\sqrt[k]{a^{k}b}
with Sagemath
[
13
]
.

Remark 1.2
.

We note that for
a\geq 2
setting
r:=a^{k}+1
yields

r^{k}=(a^{k}+1)^{k}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}a^{ik}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\binom{k}{i}a^{ik}+1=a^{k}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k-1}\binom{k}{i+1}a^{ik}+1.

Also it holds that

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k-1}\binom{k}{i+1}a^{ik}>\binom{k}{2}a^{k}\geq a^{k}.

Therefore, for all
a\geq 2
setting
b:=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\binom{k}{i+1}a^{ik}
yields a
k
-th power Diophantine pair
\{a^{k},b\}
with
1<a^{k}<b
, warranting our investigation into triples of the form
\{a^{k},b,c\}
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Let us suppose that there exists a
k

as described in Theorem

a^{k}b+1=r^{k},\qquad a^{k}c+1=s^{k},\qquad bc+1=t^{k}.

(3.3)

As for composite
k=pq
the above equations may be written in the form

(a^{p})^{q}b+1=(r^{p})^{q},\qquad(a^{p})^{q}c+1=(s^{p})^{q},\qquad bc+1=(t^{p})^{q},

it suffices to consider the cases
k=4
and
k\geq 3
a prime in order to prove Theorem
1.1
.
We begin by establishing a lower bound for
a^{k}c
.

Let now
k=4
or
k\geq 3
be a prime. From (
3.3
) it follows that

r^{k}s^{k}=(a^{k}b+1)(a^{k}c+1)>a^{2k}(bc+1)=a^{2k}t^{k}.

From this we obtain
rs>a^{2}t
, which implies that there exists a non-zero positive integer
z
such that

rs=a^{2}t+z.

(3.4)

This leads to the equality

\displaystyle r^{k}s^{k}

\displaystyle=(a^{2}t+z)^{k}=\left((a^{2k}(bc+1))^{\frac{1}{k}}+z\right)^{k}

(3.5)

\displaystyle=a^{2k}(bc+1)+k(a^{2k}(bc+1))^{\frac{k-1}{k}}z

\displaystyle\qquad+\binom{k}{2}(a^{2k}(bc+1))^{\frac{k-2}{k}}z^{2}+\cdots+z^{k}.

We have

\displaystyle r^{k}s^{k}

\displaystyle=(a^{k}b+1)(a^{k}c+1)=a^{2k}bc+a^{k}b+a^{k}c+1

\displaystyle\leq a^{2k}(bc+1)+a^{k}b+a^{k}c+1

and, thus, it follows from (
3.5
) that

\displaystyle a^{k}b+a^{k}c+1

\displaystyle\geq k(a^{2k}(bc+1))^{\frac{k-1}{k}}z+\binom{k}{2}(a^{2k}(bc+1))^{\frac{k-2}{k}}z^{2}+\cdots

(3.6)

\displaystyle\qquad\cdots+z^{k}.

Assume that
a^{k}b\geq(a^{2k}(bc+1))^{\frac{k-2}{k}}
. Then for
k\geq 4
we obtain

(a^{k}b)^{k}\geq(a^{2k}(bc+1))^{k-2}\geq a^{2k(k-2)}(bc)^{k-2},

which implies

contradicting

.
For the case
k=3

(a^{3}b)^{3}\geq(a^{6}(bc+1))>a^{6}bc,

which yields

a^{3}b^{2}>c.

From (
3.6
) it follows that

3(a^{6}bc)^{\frac{2}{3}}z<3(a^{6}(bc+1))^{\frac{2}{3}}z\leq a^{3}b+a^{3}c+1\leq 3a^{3}c,

which implies
a^{3}b^{2}\leq c
, a contradiction.
Thus, for
k\geq 3
we have

a^{k}b<(a^{2k}(bc+1))^{\frac{k-2}{k}}.

(3.7)

Now, assume that
a^{k}c\leq k(a^{2k}(bc+1))^{\frac{k-1}{k}}z
.
Then, from the inequality (
3.6
) we obtain

\displaystyle a^{k}b+a^{k}c+1

\displaystyle\geq k(a^{2k}(bc+1))^{\frac{k-1}{k}}z+\binom{k}{2}(a^{2k}(bc+1))^{\frac{k-2}{k}}z^{2}+\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad\cdots+k(a^{2k}(bc+1))^{\frac{1}{k}}z^{k-1}+z^{k}

\displaystyle\geq a^{k}c+(a^{2k}(bc+1))^{\frac{k-2}{k}}+1,

which leads with (
3.7
) to the contradiction

a^{k}b\geq(a^{2k}(bc+1))^{\frac{k-2}{k}}>a^{k}b.

Therefore, it holds that
a^{k}c>k(a^{2k}(bc+1))^{\frac{k-1}{k}}z
.
This implies

a^{k}c>k(a^{2k}(bc+1))^{\frac{k-1}{k}}z>k(a^{2k}bc)^{\frac{k-1}{k}}z.

Finally, we can conclude

a^{k}c>k^{k}z^{k}(a^{k}b)^{k-1}.

(3.8)

Next, we derive an upper bound for
a^{k}c
, which alongside (
3.8

From (
3.3
) it follows that

\displaystyle 0<\frac{r^{k}s^{k}}{(a^{2}t)^{k}}-\frac{a^{k}b+1}{a^{k}b}

\displaystyle=\frac{(a^{k}b+1)(a^{k}c+1)}{a^{2k}(bc+1)}-\frac{a^{k}b+1}{a^{k}b}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{k}b^{2}+b-a^{2k}b-a^{k}}{a^{2k}b(bc+1)}

\displaystyle<\frac{a^{k}b}{a^{2k}(bc+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{k}b}{(a^{2}t)^{k}}.

This, combined with (

\left|\sqrt[k]{1+\frac{1}{a^{k}b}}-\frac{rs}{a^{2}t}\right|<\frac{a^{k}b}{k(a^{2}t)^{k}}.

We want to employ Lemma

(3.24)

In our case, it holds that
r\geq 5
.
Furthermore, we have
r^{3}-1=a^{3}b
and quick computations confirm that there is no
r<9
of this form.
Next, for the remaining values of
r
in (
3.24
) one can directly compute
a_{9}\leq 10
.
Therefore, (
3.23
) again guarantees that

s\geq q_{10}>q_{8}>5r^{6}

for
r\geq 9
.
From this we can derive the inequality

a^{3}c>125(a^{3}b)^{6},

which, by applying (
3.8
), results in

125^{3-\lambda}(a^{3}b)^{18-6\lambda}<8^{3}3^{\frac{3}{2}}(a^{3}b)^{3+\lambda}.

Thus, we have

(a^{3}b)^{15-7\lambda}<8^{3}3^{\frac{3}{2}}125^{\lambda-3},

which is contradicted by
r\geq 7973
.
For
9\leq r\leq 7972
, from (
3.11
) it follows that

a^{2}t<\left(8\sqrt{3}(a^{3}b)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{3-\lambda}}

and for
a^{3}b=r^{3}-1
and
\lambda
as defined in Lemma
2.1
it holds that

p_{j}=a^{2}t<10^{32}.

(3.25)

Additionally, it holds that

(r^{3}-1)q_{j}^{3}-p_{j}^{3}=a^{3}bs^{3}-a^{6}t^{3}=r^{3}-1-a^{6},

which implies

(r^{3}-1)q_{j}^{3}-p_{j}^{3}\mid r^{3}-1-a^{6}.

(3.26)

Now, we can systematically eliminate all remaining candidates for
r
.
Simple calculations show that for
9\leq r\leq 7972
only
1892
are of the form
r^{3}-1=a^{3}b
.
For the remaining cases, we can verify with inequality (
3.25
) that none satisfies the condition (
3.26
).
Therefore, we have proven Theorem
1.1
for
k\geq 3
.
\square
