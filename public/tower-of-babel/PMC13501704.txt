Associations of insulin-like growth factor-1 with gait vigor, symmetry, and stability metrics measured by wearable sensors in older adults
Authors: Chi Zhang, Ye Liu, Yushan Zhang, Jie Zhang, Guoqing Fan, Zehong Huo, Dapeng Dai, Hong Shi, Ping Zeng, Ji Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) is a hormone associated with metabolic function and cellular growth. This study investigates the relationship between plasma IGF-1 levels and digital gait characteristics in older adults.MethodsSix hundred forty two older adults completed gait measurements and provided blood samples. Wearable sensors were used to measure periodic, kinetic, and spatiotemporal gait parameters. The asymmetry index (AI) and coefficient of variation (CV) were calculated for each parameter. Plasma IGF-1 and related biomarkers were measured. Multiple linear regressions were employed to examine the associations between IGF-1 and gait parameters.ResultsThe mean age was 70.46 ± 6.13 years. Background

Gait is a well-established marker of overall health status in older adults, with its decline indicating increased risk of falls, functional impairment, and mortality [1, 2]. Although previous studies have consistently shown that slower gait speed, a key indicator of declined skeletal muscle function, is closely associated with frailty and sarcopenia, more refined gait characteristics that capture early and subtle mobility impairment have not been widely integrated into routine geriatric health assessments [3, 4]. Digital health technologies enable the accurate, multidimensional assessment of gait characteristics, which is essential for capturing comprehensive biomechanical information beyond gait speed alone. Conventional stopwatch-based tests are limited to measuring speed and rely on subjective observation, thus failing to capture subtle features such as gait asymmetry or variability during dynamic walking [5, 6]. By contrast, wearable sensors enable continuous, objective monitoring of multiple gait parameters in real-world settings, offering a powerful tool for the early detection of functional decline and disease-related symptoms that cannot be identified by gait speed alone [4, 7–9].

A multidimensional approach to gait analysis is critical for unraveling the intricacies of mobility in older adults. Spatiotemporal metrics such as gait speed and stride length reflect overall walking capacity for routine geriatric assessment. Kinetic metrics mirror lower limb muscle function and dynamic stability, periodic metrics indicate subclinical motor control impairment, and asymmetry and variability metrics quantify gait coordination as established early warning markers for falls and frailty in older adults [10–12]. Collectively, these advanced parameters are sensitive indicators of functional status and fall risk across diverse clinical populations, including those with neurological and musculoskeletal disorders [13, 14]. The advent of wearable sensor technology enables continuous monitoring of these gait metrics, supporting the derivation of digital mobility outcomes and the advancement of precision health applications [15–17]. Integrating multidimensional gait parameters with biomarker data may elucidate the physiological mechanisms of gait change or impairment, and facilitate precise identification of high-risk older adults. However, the existing literature remains largely limited to descriptive gait analyses in specific populations, seldom exploring the clinical correlations with biological indicators or potential mechanisms, even for markers with established roles in energy metabolism and cellular growth.

Among potential biomarkers relevant to gait impairment, insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) stands out as a prime candidate due to its wide-ranging positive effects on metabolic processes and cellular development. IGF-1 is a key anabolic hormone essential for skeletal muscle health, promoting protein synthesis, inhibiting atrophy via the PI3K/Akt pathway, and supporting microvascular and mitochondrial function to preserve muscle perfusion and capacity [18, 19]. IGF-1 also exerts pleiotropic effects on the neuromuscular axis by interfacing with neurotrophic factors at the neuromuscular junction, thus integrating neural signals with muscular performance. It acts synergistically with the nervous system by regulating neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, and neuronal survival, thereby supporting the neural circuits responsible for balance and gait control [20, 21]. Previous studies have shown that IGF-1 is closely associated with physical function and gait performance in older adults [22–24]. For example, Yoshiko et al. demonstrated that IGF-1 signaling contributes to the relationship between skeletal muscle oxidative capacity and gait speed [25]. In a case-control study conducted by Daisy and colleagues, the combination of IGF-1 levels and single-task gait velocity effectively predicted outcomes following sport-related concussion [26]. Furthermore, a recent systematic review indicated that exercise interventions increase IGF-1 levels in older adults with sarcopenia or frailty and are associated with improved skeletal muscle function [23]. Despite these findings highlighting the value of IGF-1 for physical function, no studies have systematically examined the relationship between IGF-1 and multidimensional gait metrics covering vigor, symmetry, and stability domains measured objectively using wearable sensors.

In the current study, we aimed to investigate the associations between circulating IGF-1 levels and multidimensional gait parameters, encompassing periodic, kinetic, and spatiotemporal domains, as well as their asymmetry and variability metrics in older adults. We further performed exploratory analysis of related biomarkers to provide preliminary clues for potential biological processes.

Methods

Study population

From February to August 2023, we conducted a cross-sectional study involving 937 older adults from five communities in Beijing, China. All investigators received unified and standardized training on data collection, and passed the qualification assessment before conducting face-to-face interviews to ensure the consistency and accuracy of data acquisition. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 60 years; (2) clear consciousness and adequate communication ability; (3) absence of severe psychiatric disorders; and (4) voluntary participation to complete the questionnaire and physical examination. We sequentially excluded individuals who were unable to complete gait measurements due to limited mobility (n = 51), those who did not provide blood samples (n = 217), and those with aberrant gait data due to systematic errors (n = 27). Consequently, a total of 642 valid participants were included in the final analysis. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Hospital (Approval No. 2022BJYYEC-260-03), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Gait measurement

Data on gait characteristics were collected using a multi-sensor wearable gait analysis system (IDEEA, MiniSun LLC, USA). The IDEEA system comprises one main unit and seven miniature sensors, with accuracy and repeatability confirmed in various settings [27–30]. Seven acceleration sensors were attached to the sternum, both thighs, both ankles, and both feet of the participants to capture gait data. The sensors used manufacturer-standardized sampling and built-in filtering for reliable signal acquisition. Specifically, the hardware captures high-frequency raw kinematic signals up to 3200 Hz, which are then processed through built-in microprocessors to filter out high-frequency electrical noise and movement artifacts using Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) and wavelet analysis. The refined kinematic data are ultimately sampled and output at an effective frequency of 64 Hz. Sensor calibration was performed per the manufacturer’s protocol before each test to ensure accuracy. After assisting participants in donning the device, investigators instructed them to walk in a straight line on level ground for 12 meters at their usual walking speed, with no assistive devices used. All tests were conducted in a quiet, well-lit indoor environment with a flat, non-slip straight walkway, free of obstacles and distractions. To avoid the influence of initial acceleration and terminal deceleration phases on gait stability, raw gait data were processed via the IDEEA system’s standardized procedure, which automatically identified and excluded gait cycles from the initial acceleration and terminal deceleration phases, and only steady-state gait parameters from the middle walking segment and all valid steady-state strides per participant were extracted for the final analysis. Upon completion of the test, the main unit was connected to a computer, and relevant data were extracted and processed using ActView analysis software. A total of 15 gait parameters related to vigor performance were collected, representing the average performance of both lower limbs. These metrics included periodic gait parameters (double-limb support time, initial limb support time, terminal limb support time, swing time, step time, and stride time), kinetic gait parameters (thigh acceleration, thigh swing work, ground reaction force, foot landing control force, and toe-off angle), and spatiotemporal gait parameters (gait speed, stride frequency, step length, and stride length). Among these, kinetic parameters including thigh swing work, ground reaction force, and foot landing control force were estimated via the system’s validated standardized algorithm based on the collected acceleration signals. These software-derived kinetic variables are mathematically estimated based on Newtonian mechanics and multi-segment rigid body models. Specifically, the ground reaction force is estimated from the maximum vertical deceleration during the weight acceptance phase; thigh swing work is calculated based on the maximum deceleration during the mid-to-terminal swing phase combined with the estimated limb mass; and foot landing control force reflects the dynamic acceleration-deceleration profile of the foot upon heel strike. In addition, an asymmetry index (AI) was calculated for each parameter based on the inter-limb differences, with a higher AI indicating poorer balance. The AI was calculated as follows: AI = |Left parameter – Right parameter|/[0.5 × (Left parameter + Right parameter)]. To evaluate within-participant gait stability, the coefficient of variation (CV) was computed for each gait parameter across repeated strides during the walking trial, defined as CV = standard deviation/mean. A higher CV indicates poorer gait stability. To avoid the influence of initial acceleration and terminal deceleration phases, only steady-state gait cycles were included. A median of 20 valid steady-state strides (IQR: 18–22) per participant were used to calculate AIs and CVs.

Fasting venous blood samples (5 mL) were collected from all participants before breakfast and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4 °C. Plasma IGF-1 concentration was quantified using a RayPlex multiplex bead-based immunoassay (RayBiotech, USA: FAH-CUST), which showed 100% recovery, with inter-assay and intra-assay CVs of 2.6% and 3.6%, respectively. The performance of multiplex assays for quantifying plasma proteins has been well established in previous studies [31, 32]. Plasma IGF-1 levels followed an approximately normal distribution in our sample.

To explore potential underlying mechanisms, a panel of circulating biomarkers was also analyzed. These biomarkers encompassed glycolipid metabolism parameters including total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), and fasting blood glucose (Glu); nutritional indicators like albumin (ALB); renal function markers including creatinine (CREA) and uric acid (UA); energy metabolism regulators such as leptin and adiponectin; skeletal muscle function-related factors including myostatin, 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 [25(OH)D3], and irisin; neural modulation markers such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF); inflammatory mediators including high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β); as well as oxidative stress indicators including malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), and glutathione reductase (GR). Biochemical indicators were measured using a fully automated biochemical analyzer, while the other biomarkers were quantified either by RayPlex methods or enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The inter-assay and intra-assay CVs for these analytes ranged from 1.9% to 7.1% and 2.6% to 8.6%, respectively.

Covariates

A series of covariates were considered based on previous studies. Demographic characteristics (age, gender, ethnicity, education level, and marital status) and data on current smoking, current alcohol consumption, and exercise habits were collected during face-to-face interviews by trained investigators. Height and weight were measured to calculate body mass index (BMI). The Barthel Index was used to assess activities of daily living (ADL), with a total score <95 indicating impaired ADL. Cognitive impairment was assessed using the Mini-cog test, which consists of a 3-word recall task and the clock drawing test. Mental health status was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), with a total score >4 indicating depressive symptoms. The Charlson Comorbidity index was used to assess the presence of common chronic diseases.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or as median with interquartile range (P25–P75) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were described as counts and percentages (n [%]). All correlation analyses were performed using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Multivariable linear regression was used to analyze the associations of IGF-1 levels with each gait metric, including vigor parameters, AIs, and CVs. False discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was conducted to address concerns related to multiple testing. We performed sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the main findings: (1) Excluding 42 participants with ADL impairment; (2) Excluding 28 participants with stroke and 1 participant with Parkinson’s disease; (3) Standardizing each gait vigor parameter and incorporating the Z-scores into the multivariate linear regression model; (4) We further adjusted for gait speed when analyzing other gait parameters to examine the independent effect of IGF-1 on these metrics. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.1), and a two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

As shown in Fig. 3, IGF-1 was significantly correlated with multiple gait physical performance parameters. Specifically, IGF-1 exhibited mild negative correlations with double-limb support time (r = −0.12, P = 0.003), terminal limb support time (r = −0.08, P = 0.035), swing time (r = −0.13, P = 0.001), step time (r = −0.12, P = 0.002), and stride time (r = −0.15, P < 0.001). Multivariable analyses of the association between IGF-1 and gait parameters

Multivariable linear regression analyses of the association between IGF-1 and gait parameters across three domains are shown in Table 2. After adjusting for all covariates, IGF-1 (per 1 SD increase) remained positively associated with thigh acceleration (β = 0.055, P = 0.001), thigh swing work (β = 0.023, P = 0.004), ground reaction force (β = 0.048, P = 0.001), foot landing control force (β = 0.151, P < 0.001), gait speed (β = 1.105, P = 0.025), stride frequency (β = 1.223, P = 0.024), and stride length (β = 0.013, P = 0.012). Conversely, IGF-1 was negatively associated with the AI of thigh acceleration (β = −0.029, P = 0.023) and foot landing control force (β = −0.032, P = 0.017), as well as with the CV of swing time (β = −0.019, P = 0.007), thigh acceleration (β = −0.027, P = 0.006), foot landing control force (β = −0.011, P = 0.036), and toe-off angle (β = −0.045, P = 0.001) in model 3. After FDR correction for multiple testing, we also observed significant associations of IGF-1 with gait vigor parameters (thigh acceleration, thigh swing work, ground reaction force, foot landing control force, and stride length), the AIs of thigh acceleration and foot landing control force, and the CVs of swing time, thigh acceleration, foot landing control force, and toe-off angle. The associations with gait speed and stride frequency were no longer significant at the 0.05 level. The direction of effect estimates was consistent before and after FDR correction.

Exploratory analysis of related biomarkers

We performed bivariate correlation analyses to examine the associations of IGF‑1 and gait parameters with potentially relevant biomarkers. As shown in Fig. 4 (Fig. A), IGF-1 demonstrated significant positive correlations with TC (r = 0.14, P < 0.001), TG (r = 0.20, P < 0.001), albumin (r = 0.13, P < 0.001), leptin (r = 0.33, P < 0.001), BDNF (r = 0.36, P < 0.001), SOD (r = 0.14, P < 0.001), and GR (r = 0.12, P = 0.003). Conversely, significant negative correlations were observed with HDL (r = −0.12, P = 0.004), UA (r = −0.18, P < 0.001), adiponectin (r = −0.23, P < 0.001), myostatin (r = −0.20, P < 0.001), IL-6 (r = −0.21, P < 0.001), MDA (r = −0.09, P = 0.022), and GSH-Px (r = −0.11, P = 0.008).

Potential mechanisms of the association between IGF-1 and gait parameters (Fig. A: Correlations between IGF-1 and related biomarkers, where red/blue color indicates positive/negative correlation and chord thickness represents the magnitude of correlation strength; Fig. B: Correlations of biomarkers with gait vigor parameters; Fig. C: Correlations of biomarkers with gait symmetry parameters; Fig. D: Correlations of biomarkers with gait variability parameters)

TC, TG, albumin, leptin, BDNF, SOD, 25(OH)D, and GR exhibited negative correlations with periodic parameters but positive correlations with kinetic and spatiotemporal parameters. In contrast, UA, adiponectin, myostatin, hs-CRP, and IL-6 showed negative correlations with most kinetic and spatiotemporal parameters (Figure 4: Fig. B). Furthermore, among the IGF-1-associated biomarkers, TC, TG, albumin, leptin, and BDNF were simultaneously negatively correlated with the AIs and CVs of kinetic gait parameters, whereas UA and IL-6 were positively correlated with the asymmetry and variability of thigh acceleration, ground reaction force, and foot landing control force (Figure 4: Fig. C and D).

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted four sensitivity analyses to address potential biases from severe physical and cognitive impairments. After excluding 42 participants with impaired ADL, IGF-1 still showed positive associations with most kinetic gait parameters, while exhibiting negative correlations with the AIs of thigh acceleration and foot landing control force, as well as negative correlations with the CVs of swing time, thigh acceleration, and toe-off angle (Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, after excluding 28 individuals with a history of stroke and 1 with Parkinson’s disease, the associations between IGF-1 and gait vigor, symmetry, and stability metrics remained consistent with the main results (Supplementary Table 2). When gait vigor parameters were Z-transformed, the association of IGF-1 with gait kinetic and spatiotemporal parameters remained significant (Supplementary Table 3). After adjusting for gait speed as a covariate, the effect sizes of IGF-1 on other gait parameters were attenuated, but the statistical significance was maintained for swing time, thigh acceleration, thigh swing work, ground reaction force, and landing control force (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

Based on wearable sensor-based measurements, we demonstrated for the first time that higher IGF-1 levels were associated with better gait vigor, balance, and stability performance. We identified a panel of circulating biomarkers that were concurrently correlated with both IGF-1 levels and gait characteristics. Our findings offer preliminary insights into the value of IGF-1 as a novel biomarker for refined gait motor function.

In this study, we incorporated periodic, kinetic, and spatiotemporal gait measurements and further analyzed the asymmetry and variability of each gait characteristic. These high-dimensional gait features provided more systematic and comprehensive biomechanical information regarding neuromuscular coordination compared to gait speed alone. As shown in Figure 2, the asymmetry and variability of kinetic gait parameters were more pronounced in older adults, suggesting differential manifestations of gait characteristics across multiple dimensions during the aging process. We found that even after adjusting for demographic factors and various health statuses, circulating IGF-1 was positively associated with multiple gait vigor indicators, such as thigh acceleration, thigh swing work, ground reaction force, foot landing control force, gait speed, stride frequency, and stride length (all P < 0.05). Although the effect sizes of IGF‑1 on some gait parameters were modest per 1‑SD increase in fully adjusted models, even subtle improvements in gait performance carry clear clinical relevance in older adults. Small gains in gait speed, lower‑extremity kinetic function, and stability are linked to lower fall risk, better functional independence, and reduced mortality. Thus, these modest but consistent associations have meaningful implications for identifying at‑risk individuals and guiding interventions to preserve mobility. Kinetic measures reflect lower-limb muscle power, energy efficiency, and neuromuscular coordination, whereas spatiotemporal measures summarize overall walking rhythm and functional capacity. These findings are consistent with evidence linking low serum IGF-1 to reduced muscle mass and strength, contributing to slower gait speed, and suggest that IGF-1’s neuroprotective effects via pathways such as IGF-1R/Wnt/β-catenin may indirectly influence gait kinetics [33, 34]. IGF-1 combined with single-task gait measures has also been used to predict motor outcomes [26]. However, Cappola et al. reported that the positive association between IGF-1 and gait speed in older women was evident only within the lower IGF-1 range, weakened after adjustment for covariates, and attenuated beyond a certain threshold, suggesting non-linear or context-specific effects [35]. Such discrepancies may reflect differences in populations, comorbidities, outcome definitions, and study designs. Overall, our results show robust associations between circulating IGF-1 levels and multiple kinetic and spatiotemporal gait parameters in older adults. These observed associations are consistent with the biological effects of IGF-1 on muscle, vascular, and neural function reported in previous basic studies. Importantly, clarifying the connection between these refined gait parameters and clinically meaningful outcomes is of great value. From a clinical perspective, parameters such as kinetic forces and gait variability can detect subtle impairments in motor control and dynamic balance. Identifying older adults with these abnormal kinetic or variability profiles, even before visible declines in routine gait speed occur, could facilitate the early screening of sarcopenia and the timely implementation of interventions against falls and frailty. Furthermore, considering the known anabolic effects of IGF-1, it is plausible that physical fitness and skeletal muscle function act as crucial mediators in this relationship, which warrants targeted investigation in future studies. Further studies are needed to validate these associations across diverse populations and clinical contexts.

This study observed that multiple circulating biomarkers related to anabolic, neurotrophic, and metabolic processes were concurrently correlated with both IGF-1 levels and multidimensional gait parameters. These correlated biomarkers may form a biological network that links circulating IGF-1 to gait performance in older adults. Firstly, IGF-1 was inversely correlated with myostatin and positively associated with albumin, patterns that may indicate synergistic interactions contributing to enhanced muscle and gait function [36–39]. Albumin plays a role in maintaining osmotic pressure and transporting various molecules, thus supporting overall muscle health [40]. On the other hand, myostatin is a negative regulator of muscle growth, and its inhibition by IGF-1 may be related to improved muscle mass [41]. Secondly, IGF-1 demonstrates complex interactions with metabolic biomarkers, including lipid profiles, leptin, and adiponectin, underscoring its integral role in energy metabolism. Leptin regulates glucose and lipid metabolism, thereby ensuring a sufficient energy supply for physical activity [42]. Deficiency or resistance to leptin has been shown to lead to energy metabolism disorders, which can subsequently impair exercise endurance and performance [43, 44]. However, adiponectin exhibits an inverse relationship with IGF-1, potentially indicating a metabolic trade-off wherein energy resources are allocated according to physiological demands. In response to impaired gait and declining motor capacity, the body may counteract metabolic imbalance via the upregulation of adiponectin, which enhances glucose metabolism and fatty acid oxidation [45]. Thirdly, IL-6, a common pro-inflammatory factor, was negatively correlated with both IGF-1 and gait performance, while specific oxidative stress markers may also explain the relationship between IGF-1 and gait. These results suggest that IGF-1 plays a key role in inflammation regulation and antioxidant defense, two processes that are credible causes of musculoskeletal disorders such as sarcopenia and physical frailty [46, 47]. Finally, the positive correlation between IGF-1 and BDNF underscores their cooperative role in promoting neuronal survival, synaptic plasticity, and angiogenesis. Mechanistically, IGF-1 activates the PI3K/Akt/mTORC1 pathway to promote neural repair, while BDNF enhances plasticity and has been shown to act synergistically with IGF-1 in depression models to improve motor behavior [48, 49]. Consistently, evidence from spinal cord injury models demonstrates that exogenous IGF-1 facilitates recovery of motor function by repairing the neural microenvironment, further supporting its neurotrophic effects [48]. Moreover, IGF-1 contributes to neuromuscular protection by enhancing oxidative balance, reflected in higher superoxide dismutase and glutathione reductase activity together with lower malondialdehyde levels [50].

Another innovative finding of this study is the negative association observed between IGF-1 and the asymmetry or variability of swing time, thigh acceleration, foot landing control force, and toe-off angle. This result suggests a beneficial role for IGF-1 in maintaining the coordination and homeostasis of lower limb muscle groups during locomotion. Clues from our biomarker network analysis (Figure 4) may explain the underlying mechanisms. The collaboration between IGF-1 and BDNF appears to support both gait strength and coordination. BDNF enhances acetylcholine release and receptor sensitivity at the neuromuscular junction. This improved neuromuscular transmission can facilitate synchronized muscle activation and coordination, thereby reducing the variability and asymmetry of kinetic parameters during the gait cycle [51]. In Parkinson’s disease models, reductions in BDNF have been linked to motor dysfunctions, including tremor and bradykinesia [52]. Additionally, increased expression of IL-6 can exacerbate neuroinflammatory responses [53, 54]. These results show that IGF-1 levels are associated with better gait symmetry and stability, which may be related to neurotrophic and anti-inflammatory processes. Furthermore, elevated UA was associated with greater asymmetry, plausibly by impairing neuromuscular coordination and promoting maladaptive muscle activation under inflammatory stress [55, 56]. Kettlety et al. reported that elevated UA levels may affect motor function and gait, potentially reflecting muscle metabolic disturbances such as purine metabolism dysregulation [57]. Within the oxidative stress markers, we identified that SOD, an antioxidant factor, was positively correlated with both IGF-1 levels and gait balance/steadiness. Previous foundational studies indicate that IGF-1 can activate the Nrf2 signaling pathway, thereby increasing the expression levels of antioxidant enzymes like SOD and GR. This enhances the cell's capacity to clear reactive oxygen species (ROS) and subsequently mitigates oxidative damage to neural cells involved in skeletal motor function during movement [58, 59]. IGF-1 can also activate the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, promoting mitochondrial biogenesis and function, which helps prevent excessive ROS production resulting from mitochondrial dysfunction [60].

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal inference between IGF-1 and gait performance. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to establish the predictive value of IGF-1 for gait changes over time, and to better control for key confounding factors such as comprehensive physical fitness and precise skeletal muscle mass. Second, all participants were recruited from urban areas in China, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to populations with different demographic or environmental characteristics. Additionally, while our 12-meter straight walkway test was highly standardized, it may not perfectly capture the ecological validity of continuous, free-living gait in real-world environments. Third, although we included a series of biomarkers to further explore the potential links between IGF-1 and gait metrics, this biomarker analysis remains largely descriptive rather than mechanistic, and it acts mainly as a hypothesis-generating step. Future studies integrating multi-omics data and experimental validation are warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms more comprehensively. Additionally, although we have for the first time identified the correlations between IGF-1 and multiple gait parameters, potential false-positive associations cannot be fully ruled out. Our preliminary findings require cautious interpretation prior to independent replication in external larger samples.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates the positive association between IGF-1 and multidimensional gait performance in older adults. By integrating molecular profiles with wearable devices-based functional assessments, these findings offer clues for the physiological pathways underlying gait regulation in aging. Our study supports IGF-1 as a potential biomarker for gait impairments, and lays a foundation for future interventions to reduce mobility decline in older adults.
