Surgical Resection of a Giant Left Ventricular Fibroma Causing Palpitations in a Young Woman
Authors: Sameh M. Said, Mohamed A. Gabr, Hazem M. Fouda, Ibrahim Abdelbaser, Ahmed A. Elsayed, Hany A. Girgis, Ayman A. Gad, Jawed Polad, Mohammed Sanad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary cardiac tumors are rare, and fibromas—benign fibrous tumors of the myocardium—are exceedingly uncommon in adults. Although labeled benign, they may cause arrhythmia, obstructive symptoms, heart failure, or sudden death. Complete surgical resection remains the treatment of choice to alleviate symptoms and prevent complications.Case SummaryWe report the case of a 20-year-old woman presenting with progressive shortness of breath and palpitations. Cardiac imaging revealed a large intramyocardial mass consistent with left ventricular fibroma. Surgical resection was performed, and the mass was enucleated in its entirety. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 5. Histopathology confirmed fibroma with no malignant features.DiscussionThis case highlights successful surgical management of a rare left ventricular fibroma in an adult. Early recognition and complete resection are essential for symptomatic relief and to prevent complications.Take-Home MessagesLeft ventricular fibromas, although rare, can cause significant symptoms in young adults. Prompt diagnosis and proper surgical enucleation provide safe and effective treatment.

Primary cardiac tumors are rare, with an incidence of approximately 0.001% to 0.03% in autopsy series.1 Among benign cardiac tumors, fibromas represent a small subset, mostly reported in the pediatric population but seldom in adults.2 These tumors are composed of dense fibroblastic tissue, which can be a nidus for life-threatening arrhythmias, and if large enough, they can cause obstructive symptoms and become hemodynamically significant. They may also compromise cardiac function, resulting in heart failure or sudden cardiac death. There is also a subset of patients who may remain asymptomatic, in whom fibromas may be discovered incidentally.Take-Home Messages•Left ventricular fibromas can cause significant symptoms and life-threatening arrhythmias, even in young adults.•Surgical enucleation with myocardial preservation under cardiopulmonary bypass is the preferred treatment for symptomatic left ventricular fibroma.

Complete surgical resection is considered the definitive treatment for symptomatic fibromas. Given the rarity of these tumors, literature has been limited to case reports. In the current report, we present a case of a giant left ventricular fibroma in a young adult that was successfully resected under cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB).

Institutional review board approval was not required given the case report nature of the study, however informed consent was obtained from the patient to publish the current case.

History of Presentation

A 20-year-old woman presented with a 6-month history of progressive exertional shortness of breath and intermittent palpitations. She denied chest pain, syncope, or peripheral edema. Physical examination revealed regular heart rate with no murmurs, normal blood pressure, and clear lungs.

Past Medical History

The patient had no known cardiac history or significant medical comorbidities.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for an intramyocardial mass included benign cardiac tumors such as fibroma, rhabdomyoma, myxoma (rare in ventricles), and malignant tumors such as sarcomas. Other considerations included thrombus and intracardiac cystic lesions.

Management

Given the patient's young age and symptomatic presentation, surgical resection was recommended. The procedure was performed via median sternotomy, and normothermic CPB was established via aortic and bicaval cannulation. After cardioplegic arrest, an incision was created in the left ventricular anterolateral wall where the tumor was located (Figure 3). The tumor was dissected free from the surrounding myocardium (Figure 4A) and enucleated completely without capsule rupture (Figure 4B). Postbypass transesophageal echocardiography confirmed no residual mass or ventricular septal defect and good ventricular function.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was extubated within 4 hours postoperatively. Her postoperative course was uneventful, and continuous telemetry monitoring showed no significant arrhythmias. She was discharged on postoperative day 5 in stable condition. At the 6-month follow-up, she was asymptomatic and recovering well, with normalized echocardiograms showing preserved left ventricular function and no residual mass.

Discussion

Cardiac fibromas, although rare in adults, may cause symptoms ranging from arrhythmias to heart failure or sudden death depending on size and location.3 In the left ventricle, their proximity to the conduction tissues and intracavitary effect often precipitates palpitations and dyspnea. Early diagnosis employing multimodality imaging, particularly cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, is critical to differentiate tumors from thrombus or other masses.4

Pediatric cardiac fibromas and adult fibromas differ notably in clinical behavior and implications. Pediatric fibromas are more commonly diagnosed because of the early onset of severe arrhythmias or obstructive symptoms, reflecting more rapid growth during infancy and childhood.2,3 They often present in the setting of congenital heart disease evaluation and can cause early morbidity and mortality. Conversely, adult fibromas are exceedingly rare and tend to exhibit slower or negligible growth, with symptoms presenting later and often more subtly as palpitations, exertional dyspnea, or incidental findings on imaging.3,4 Histologically, adult fibromas may demonstrate greater fibrosis and heterogeneity than typical pediatric tumors.3,4 This distinction underscores the need for tailored clinical approaches based on patient age, tumor behavior, and symptomatology.

Surgical excision remains the treatment of choice for symptomatic fibromas. Complete resection is feasible, as these masses are well encapsulated; it is critical to identify the correct plane that separates the tumor from the surrounding myocardium, which will enable complete enucleation under CPB. Risks include ventricular dysfunction, arrhythmias, and bleeding, but advances in surgical technique and critical care have improved outcomes.

Regarding asymptomatic patients, surgical indications require nuanced consideration given the unpredictable natural history of fibromas.1,2 Surgery is generally recommended for those with symptoms such as arrhythmias, heart failure, and/or obstruction. However, even asymptomatic patients may warrant surgical resection when imaging demonstrates tumor growth, involvement of conduction tissue portending arrhythmogenic risk, or potential for hemodynamic compromise. Small, stable, and truly asymptomatic fibromas might be managed conservatively with serial imaging and multidisciplinary assessment, balancing operative risks against the possibility of sudden adverse events. This individualized decision-making is essential to optimize outcomes and patient safety.

In cases where complete surgical resection is not feasible because of extensive myocardial infiltration or tumor size, heart transplantation may be considered.5 Although transplantation is rarely indicated, it remains a life-saving option when resection poses unacceptable risk or residual tumor burden is likely. The procedure eliminates the tumor risk entirely but involves challenges related to donor availability, immunosuppression, and long-term complications. Hence, transplantation is reserved for select cases in a multidisciplinary context.

This case highlights the successful surgical management of a rare left ventricular fibroma in an adult. Early recognition and complete resection are essential for symptomatic relief and to prevent complications.5

Conclusions

In young adults presenting with cardiac masses, primary fibroma should be considered despite its rarity. Multimodality imaging aids diagnosis, and surgical enucleation under cardiopulmonary bypass is safe and effective, offering excellent symptomatic relief and prognosis.
