 ABSTRACT

This report presents an invention that has the potential to significantly impact the production efficiency of
Metal Inert Gas (MIG) welding operations. The device, which could be in a handheld pocket tool
configuration, integrated into the welding torch itself, or in a service station configuration for robotic
welding, enables the quantitative measurement of wirefeed system behavior for consistent setup prior to
welding and for periodic maintenance checks throughout. Problems associated with wire feeding, such as
arc instabilities, burn backs, wire breaks, and tangling, aka “bird nests”, are the greatest source of downtime
in MIG welding operations. The challenge of setting up the wire feed system for consistent operation can
also be complicated by variance in many factors, including drive roller age/condition, wire diameter, wire
surface finish/roughness, wire contamination/surface products, and upstream drag in the wirefeed system.
The device presented provides a solution to all these issues through measurement of actual feed behavior
and a repeatable method for making system adjustments.

Keywords: Metal Inert Gas, MIG, Gas Metal Arc Welding, GMAW, Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing,
WAAM, Directed Energy Deposition, DED, Wire, Drive Roller Tension, Setup, Commissioning,

 1. INTRODUCTION AND PROBLEM STATEMENT

There are tens of millions of Metal Inert Gas (MIG) welding systems in use worldwide. In the MIG process,
also known as Gas Metal Arc Welding (GMAW), the wire itself doubles as the feedstock and the electrode.
This type of welding is also leveraged by the Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing (WAAM) large-scale 3D
printing process. In these processes, reliable, consistent wire feeding is critical for process stability,
repeatability, and efficient production. Problems associated with wire feeding (arc instability, burn-backs,
wire breaks, and tangling, aka “bird nests”) are the primary equipment-related cause of system downtime
and lost production. Problems associated with the welding power supply itself, chiller, shield gas, or robot
are much less frequent. In MIG systems, whether manual or robot-based, wire is fed through a wire bundle
from the spool or drum to the welding torch by one or more sets of drive rollers. The drive rollers exert a
clamping force on the wire, which is user-adjustable and known as “drive roller tension”. This is a critical
setting for supporting the welding process: too loose, and the wire can slip frequently, leading to
inconsistent wirefeed and burn-backs; too tight, and the wire can be deformed, break, or bird nest if process
obstructions are encountered. Therefore, setting the tension is a delicate balancing act. Despite the
importance of drive roller tension, there are no tools currently available to quantify it, or the resulting wire
push force, which is the downstream, process-oriented outcome (drive roller tension allows the rollers to
exert a push force on the wire). Instead, operators rely on qualitative, or literally “feels like”, approaches,
such as pinching the wire between their fingers1 or feeding it against a block of wood2 to observe the
wirefeed behavior. These methods are not quantifiable or repeatable and highly susceptible to human
interpretation/preference, making consistent system setup or troubleshooting when problems do occur
extremely difficult or impossible. This especially impacts large operations or multi-agent systems where
there is a desire to setup many welders in the same way. Simply setting the drive roller tension knob to the
same “number” on every welder does not work either due to many factors in which there can be variance,
e.g., drive roller age/condition, wire diameter, wire surface finish/roughness, wire contamination/surface
products, and upstream drag in the wirefeed system stemming from variable liner conditions, spool weights,
and spool brake settings.

 2. DEVICE DESCRIPTION

The solution is a “welding wirefeed push force measurement device”, which could be in a handheld pocket
tool configuration, in a service-station configuration that a robot-based welder could move its torch to for
testing, or in a configuration where it is integrated directly into the welding torch itself. This device enables
a quantitative, rather than qualitative, approach for setting drive roller tension in a repeatable manner. The
device would be welder agnostic, i.e., it could be used with any make/model of MIG welder. The concept
is presented in Figure 1 using the handheld configuration as an example. In the handheld configuration,
the device could temporarily replace the welding contact tip, attaching to the end of the torch using the
same threads as the tip; adapters would be available for each brand of contact tip. Or, the device could
clamp onto the welding torch using spring-loaded jaws. The wire would feed into the device, and the device
would have a means of applying a sensor feed load, which would simulate a process load and replace the
commonplace “feels like” observations that involve pinching the wire with fingers or observing it feed
against a block of wood. This load could be applied in a number of different ways, e.g., direct braking of
the wire itself, braking of a contact roller, or use of a device like a wire straightener, which requires force
to move the wire through it. The sensor feed load would be adjustable; this would be a manual adjustment

a few simple steps to find incipient motion (the wire slip/stick condition) and the wire push force (in either
the static/sticking or kinetic/sliding condition) associated with it. Because this invention is at its inception
at the time of this report, the desired or “ground truth” wire push force values are unknown and could
depend on several factors, including weld mode, feedstock material, and diameter. Eventually, after some
amount of usage of this device, reference tables could be established for recommended settings for push
force.

 3. METHODS OF DEVICE USE

There are a few potentially effective methods of setting and checking wirefeed tension. Method 1, static to
sliding: 1) Tighten drive roller tension to high level and reduce sensor feed load to low level, 2) While
feeding wire, increase the sensor feed load until the desired wire push force (static/sticking) is measured,
and 3) Loosen the drive roller tension just until the wire slip condition begins to occur (wire stops feeding).
Method 2, sliding to static: 1) Loosen drive roller tension to low level and increase sensor feed load to high

 3

level, 2) While attempting to feed wire, increase the drive roller tension until the desired wire push force
(kinetic/slipping) is measured, and 3) Reduce sensor feed load just until the wire stick condition begins to
occur (wire starts feeding). The method for checking a pre-existing drive roller tension setting would be:
1) Loosen sensor feed load to low level and 2) While feeding wire, increase the sensor feed load just until
the wire slip condition begins to occur (wire stops feeding). These methods are not all inclusive, and there
may be additional approaches that are equally effective. Automated, iterative approaches of checking
wirefeed tension could be programmed in the torch-integrated or service station configurations for robotic
welding. In terms of the wire feed rate that is used in these approaches, the best practice will likely be to
feed wire at the wire feed rate that is intended to be used during welding, rather than at a manual feed rate
setting which may be different, in order to replicate the wirefeed behavior that will be experienced during
the process. For welding systems with multiple sets of drive rollers, the handheld version of this device
could be used to sequentially set the drive roller tension on each individual set of rollers one-by-one, moving
methodically from upstream to downstream in the wirefeed system.

 4. BENEFITS AND APPLICATION AREAS

This device allows for the quantitative setting of drive roller tension in MIG welders based on a sensor feed
load. This will allow for the consistent setup and troubleshooting of welding systems. The device allows
for the measurement of feed load while the wire is moving (in the static/sticking condition) and the
identification of incipient motion (the slip/stick condition), which would not be possible by simply feeding
the tip of the wire against a scale, for example. This device has the potential to significantly impact the
production efficiency of welding operations worldwide by reducing downtime and defects associated with
wirefeed issues. There are no competing technologies currently, and welders rely on qualitative or quite
literally “feels like” approaches. Potential application areas include MIG welding across the tens of
millions of MIG welders worldwide in nearly every industry and in WAAM 3D printing operations, as well
as other forms of wire-fed Directed Energy Deposition (DED), such as laser-wire and electron beam DED.

 5. RELATED ART

There appears to be at least one device available commercially that is marketed as a wire feeder tension
gauge3. There is a lack of information available as to the functionality of the device, but it is described as
a scale that could measure the pressure that the drive rollers exert on the wire. This would provide a
calibrated measurement of the drive roller tension, i.e., roller pressure, which for an individual welding
system would provide context for the unitless hash marks on the tensioner knob, but would not, functionally
speaking, be any different than the tensioner knob setting, which is based on spring tension. The device
may aid with similar setup from welder to welder, especially across different makes/models; however, it
may give users a false sense of security because the drive roller tension setting does not consistently
correlate to wire push force because of all the factors that were described previously in the problem
statement, and therefore this device would not reliably enable the replication of wirefeed behavior across
systems.

Some welding system integrations, particularly those that are robot based, will show a display or expose a
data feed of the drive motor current, i.e., amperage, when running, which gives an indication of the load in
the wirefeed system. This primarily is an indication of the drag in the wirefeed bundle upstream of the wire
feeder, which can be useful information to have, especially for setting the spool brakes, but does not

 6. CONCLUSIONS

The welding wirefeed push force measurement device presented here has the potential to significantly
improve the production efficiency of MIG welding operations across the manufacturing spectrum, from the
hobbyist welder to high-volume production operations, including welding in the automotive industry. The
device is an example of a “system agnostic analysis toolkit” which is a growing area of interest in
manufacturing across a range of processes, from machining to welding, including advanced processes like
WAAM and other forms of wire DED.
