SHP2 as a pivotal modulator of the tumor microenvironment in gastrointestinal cancers: from mechanisms to targeted therapies
Authors: Sushan Wang, Xiaoqing Zhao, Jianhua Dong, Jing Zhang, Yuxin Wang, Honglian Jin, Xinyu Jiang, Li Chen, Ming Zhang, Lei Ji, Yawei Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSrc homology 2 domain–containing protein tyrosine phosphatase 2 (SHP2) is a critical signaling molecule involved in tumor proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and immune evasion. In gastrointestinal (GI) cancers, however, its function is highly complex and context-dependent, varying across distinct cellular compartments within the tumor microenvironment (TME).Main bodyThis review focuses on five major GI malignancies and reframes SHP2 function within a GI-specific TME, emphasizing how its roles are shaped by key environmental determinants—including cell type, tissue microenvironment, and disease state. We further discuss how current therapeutic modalities, such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy, modulate SHP2 activity and contribute to treatment resistance. In addition, we evaluate the therapeutic potential of SHP2 inhibition, highlighting recent advances in allosteric inhibitors, emerging combination strategies, and ongoing clinical studies.ConclusionsTogether, this review provides a conceptual framework for understanding SHP2 as both a signaling integrator and a precision therapeutic target in GI cancers, offering insights that may inform future mechanistic studies and the development of context-aware therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) cancers comprise a heterogeneous group of malignancies arising from the digestive tract and its accessory organs, including gastric cancer (GC), colorectal cancer (CRC), liver cancer, mainly hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), esophageal cancer (EC), particularly esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), and pancreatic cancer, predominantly pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). According to GLOBOCAN 2022, these major GI malignancies collectively accounted for nearly 4.8 million new cases and more than 3.2 million deaths worldwide, underscoring their substantial global burden. Among them, CRC represented the largest incidence burden, with approximately 1.93 million new cases, whereas GC and liver cancer remained major contributors to cancer-related mortality. Notably, liver, esophageal, and pancreatic cancers showed particularly high mortality-to-incidence ratios, with estimated deaths approaching their annual new case numbers [1]. Despite advances in surgery and systemic therapies, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, molecularly targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, treatment outcomes for advanced GI cancers remain unsatisfactory, largely due to tumor heterogeneity, an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), and intrinsic or acquired therapeutic resistance [2]. Therefore, identifying and targeting key molecular regulators that drive tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance is of critical importance.

Src homology 2 domain–containing protein tyrosine phosphatase 2 (SHP2), encoded by PTPN11, is a non-receptor protein tyrosine phosphatase that serves as an essential signaling node in multiple oncogenic pathways. Structurally, SHP2 consists of two Src homology 2 (SH2) domains (N-SH2 and C-SH2) and a catalytic protein tyrosine phosphatase (PTP) domain [3]. SHP2 has been implicated in tumor proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and recurrence across a wide range of malignancies [4]. Beyond its well-established role as a downstream mediator of receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) signaling that promotes oncogenic pathways such as rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (RAS)–mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)–protein kinase B (AKT), SHP2 also critically interfaces with the immune landscape of the TME [5, 6]. Notably, accumulating evidence indicates that SHP2 exerts context-dependent biological functions within the TME, acting as either an oncogenic driver or a tumor suppressor, which underscores the complexity of targeting this phosphatase.

Scope and aims

This review focuses on five major malignancies of the digestive tract—EC, GC, HCC, PDAC, and CRC. These were selected because they collectively encompass the principal functional units of the digestive system. Furthermore, they are highly representative in terms of epidemiology and clinical burden, and provide well-established models to dissect the context-dependent functions of SHP2 in GI cancer biology.

In addition to delineating the biological roles of SHP2, this review extends existing SHP2-focused literature in several important aspects. First, we specifically frame SHP2 function within the GI-specific TME, integrating tumor-intrinsic signaling with immune, stromal, and vascular components rather than focusing solely on tumor cell–intrinsic signaling. Second, we reconcile the seemingly paradoxical tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressive effects of SHP2 by emphasizing contextual determinants such as cell type and disease state. Third, we summarize how current therapeutic modalities reshape SHP2 signaling and contribute to therapy resistance in GI cancers, and we discuss the emerging application of SHP2 inhibitors and their rational combination with existing treatments.

Guided by these considerations, this review is structured around several key unresolved questions: (i) How do tumor-intrinsic and microenvironmental SHP2 signaling pathways interact to shape GI cancer progression and immune regulation? (ii) Under what cellular and disease contexts does SHP2 exert tumor-promoting versus tumor-suppressive effects in GI cancers? (iii) How do tumor-intrinsic and immune cell–specific SHP2 functions interact to shape treatment response and resistance? (iv) Under what conditions might SHP2 modulation potentially reconcile its dual roles to achieve durable therapeutic responses in GI cancers? Addressing these questions may help refine the conceptual basis for SHP2-targeted strategies and inform their rational development in GI cancers.

Biological roles of SHP2 in GI cancers

The molecular structure and functional basis of SHP2

SHP2, encoded by PTPN11, is a non-receptor protein tyrosine phosphatase. Its structure comprises two SH2 domains (N-SH2 and C-SH2), a central PTP catalytic domain, and a regulatory C-terminal tail, which together constitute its functional basis [7]. The phosphopeptide-binding pockets of both SH2 domains are positioned adjacent to the PTP domain and fully exposed on the molecular surface, enabling efficient subcellular localization and signaling engagement [3]. In the absence of phosphotyrosine ligands, the N-SH2 domain maintains SHP2 in an auto-inhibited state by occluding the active site of the PTP domain, whereas the C-SH2 domain primarily enhances binding affinity and substrate specificity without directly contributing to activation [3]. These structural features collectively establish the autoinhibitory ground state of SHP2.

Activation of SHP2 is principally governed by conformational rearrangements that disrupt the inhibitory N-SH2–PTP interface. Binding of phosphotyrosine-containing ligands promotes a transition of N-SH2 from the β-state to the α-state, weakening its interaction with the PTP catalytic domain and thereby enabling enzyme activation [8]. Structural studies further suggest that opening of the central β-sheet within the N-SH2 domain plays a critical role in stabilizing the active conformation [9]. In parallel, the C-terminal tail contains regulatory tyrosine residues, particularly Tyr542 and Tyr580, which undergo phosphorylation in response to extracellular stimulation [7]. Consequently, these phosphorylation events exert pleiotropic effects. They not only release the autoinhibited state but also, via adaptor recruitment, help stabilize the active conformation. Together, these actions calibrate both the amplitude and duration of downstream SHP2 signaling [10].

Dysregulation of SHP2 in GI cancers

The protein tyrosine phosphatase SHP2 is widely expressed and acts as a pivotal signaling hub downstream of multiple RTKs and cytokine receptors, integrating key mitogenic and survival pathways such as RAS–MAPK, PI3K–AKT, and Janus kinase (JAK)–signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) [11–13]. By coordinating these pathways, SHP2 fine-tunes cell proliferation, differentiation, survival, and immune regulation. Consequently, dysregulation of SHP2 activity can profoundly reshape intracellular signaling and is implicated in malignant transformation.

In GI cancers, this dysregulation manifests through somatic gain-of-function mutations that lead to constitutive SHP2 activation [14]. A common SHP2 mutant in CRC is E76G. The E76G mutation disrupts the inhibitory intramolecular interaction within the PTP domain and leads to excessive activation of the PTP domain, resulting in increased SHP2 activity [15]. Similarly, the well-characterized E76K mutation, a recurrent activating variant in multiple solid tumors and leukemias, markedly increases SHP2 catalytic output and sustains downstream oncogenic signaling [16, 17]. Functionally, these hyperactive mutants can potentiate pro-tumorigenic pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin, thereby driving epithelial-mesenchymal transition, tumorigenesis, and metastatic progression [18]. Together, these findings establish mutant SHP2 as an important oncogenic driver in GI cancers and provide a molecular rationale for therapeutic targeting.

Apart from genetic alterations, dysregulated SHP2 expression and activation status are also implicated in GI cancers. Although evidence linking high SHP2 gene expression to poor prognosis in GI cancers remains limited, one study analyzed the effect of SHP2 phosphorylation on patient survival. An immunohistochemical analysis of phosphorylated SHP2 (p-SHP2) in CRC tissue microarrays demonstrated that elevated p-SHP2 levels exhibited significantly worse overall survival and disease-free survival in patients with concurrently high WW domain–containing E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 1 (WWP1) expression, suggesting that SHP2 activation may influence the prognosis of patients [19]. In addition, Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) mutations are frequently observed in PDAC and CRC and drive multiple downstream signaling cascades. Blocking a single downstream pathway often fails to achieve satisfactory therapeutic efficacy [20, 21]. SHP2 functions as a central node in sustaining KRAS-driven oncogenic signaling. Pharmacological SHP2 inhibition effectively suppresses its catalytic activity and phosphorylation, thereby blocking the compensatory reactivation of key pathways like RAS-mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase (MEK)-extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) and PI3K–AKT [22, 23]. Collectively, the activation of SHP2 (including conformational changes, phosphorylation, functional activation, etc.) constitutes an important factor in the carcinogenic signal transduction and therapeutic responsiveness in GI cancers.

In summary, accumulating genomic and functional evidence indicates that SHP2 dysregulation in GI cancers is driven not only by gain-of-function mutations but also by aberrant enhancement of its activation state. Both mechanisms converge on sustained amplification of oncogenic signaling pathways, thereby contributing to tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic resistance. These observations suggest the scientific validity of targeting SHP2 as a therapeutic target for GI malignancies.

The dual role of SHP2 in the TME of GI cancers

The TME is a complex ecosystem that is instrumental in GI cancer progression, therapy resistance, and immune evasion [24]. Owing to the distinct physiological and immunological characteristics of different GI organs, GI cancers exhibit unique TMEs. In a substantial subset of GCs, particularly those associated with Helicobacter pylori infection, the TME is strongly influenced by chronic bacterial colonization of the acidic stomach [25]. As an immune-tolerant organ, the liver harbors a complex TME enriched in Kupffer cells. These liver-resident macrophages exhibit phenotypic plasticity, capable of switching between a pro-inflammatory state (M1-like) and an anti-inflammatory/reparative state (M2-like)—a dynamic process actively involved in the development of HCC [26]. In addition, the TME of PDAC is characterized by high immune suppression and insufficient immune cell infiltration, which together constrain effective antitumor immunity [27]. Across these diverse TME contexts, SHP2 plays opposite roles in different cell types and even under different conditions in the same cell, rooted in its dual role as a scaffold protein and phosphatase, as well as cell-type-specific signaling networks (Fig. 1; Table 1).

Role of SHP2 in tumor cells

The role of SHP2 in GI tumor cells is highly multifaceted and context‑dependent. In tumor cells, SHP2 can regulate proliferative RTK–RAS–MAPK signaling, modulate PI3K–AKT activity, restrain excessive inflammatory STAT3 responses in selected settings, and display divergent roles under distinct oncogenic backgrounds. These context-dependent functions are summarized below.

SHP2 as an oncogenic hub of RTK–RAS–MAPK signaling

SHP2 is a well-established positive regulator of the RTK–RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK cascade. Following RTK activation, SHP2 facilitates RAS–MAPK signaling and thereby promotes proliferative and survival responses. Through its ability to transmit diverse upstream growth signals, SHP2 functions as a central signaling hub in multiple GI cancers, driving tumor proliferation and survival [28].

SHP2 is required to relay proliferative signals from activated RTKs to downstream MAPK pathways. In RTK-driven established GC—where amplification of fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 (FGFR2) or mesenchymal–epithelial transition factor (MET) is common—SHP2 serves as a key downstream node that relays proliferative signals to the RAS–ERK pathway [29]. Similarly, in c-Met- or EGFR-driven HCC, SHP2 is required to relay oncogenic signaling, and its deletion or pharmacologic inhibition therefore markedly suppresses tumorigenesis [30]. In ESCC, when tumor cell growth is dominated by the RTK–RAS–ERK pathways, SHP2 is a central amplifier of tumor-promoting signaling, and its inhibition blocks these pro-survival signals [31]. In addition, Helicobacter pylori CagA can aberrantly engage SHP2 in GC, resulting in ERK activation and tumorigenic phenotypes such as the “hummingbird phenotype” [32, 33]. Together, these observations establish SHP2 as a convergent oncogenic hub that channels heterogeneous upstream inputs into sustained MAPK signaling across GI cancers. Beyond sustaining oncogenic signaling, SHP2 may drive therapy-induced reactivation of RTK–RAS–MAPK signaling and subsequent resistance, as discussed in Section "SHP2 as a central mediator of therapy resistance in GI cancers".

SHP2 rewires PI3K–AKT signaling in a context-dependent manner

SHP2 regulates PI3K–AKT signaling in a heterogeneous manner across GI cancers, reflecting strong dependence on cellular state and environmental cues. SHP2 has traditionally been viewed as a positive regulator of PI3K–AKT signaling, with its N-terminal SH2 domain contributing to growth factor-induced pathway activation [11]. However, recent studies indicate that this regulation is highly context-dependent. According to upstream stimuli, cellular state, and metabolic conditions, SHP2 may either promote or constrain AKT signaling across GI cancers.

In many GI cancer contexts, SHP2 promotes PI3K–AKT signaling to support tumor cell survival and treatment tolerance. SHP2 promotes tumor cell proliferation and inhibits apoptosis in CRC and ESCC through the PI3K–AKT pathway [31, 34]. One study demonstrated that SHP2 suppresses ferritinophagy by regulating PI3K–BRD4–TFEB signaling, thereby inhibiting reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and ferroptotic cell death mechanisms, which promotes the progression of CRC [35]. Similarly, SHP2 overexpression promotes HCC growth and progression by activating the PI3K–AKT–mTOR pathway [36]. Collectively, these findings suggest that SHP2 commonly promotes PI3K–AKT-dependent tumor cell proliferation and survival across GI cancers.

Conversely, the functional relationship between SHP2 and PI3K–AKT signaling may be substantially reshaped under specific microenvironmental or metabolic conditions. In HCC under hypoxic conditions, SHP2 phosphorylation is reduced, which relieves its tonic inhibitory effect on the PI3K–AKT pathway, thereby paradoxically promoting tumor progression [37]. Metabolic context may further reshape the functional consequences of SHP2–PI3K signaling. In diabetes-associated CRC, SHP2 knockdown reduced PI3K–AKT phosphorylation but paradoxically enhanced tumor cell invasiveness, implying metabolic reprogramming rewires SHP2–PI3K crosstalk [38].

Overall, the influence of SHP2 on PI3K–AKT activity is highly plastic and context-dependent, with outputs shaped by tumor lineage, microenvironmental stress, and metabolic state. While PI3K–AKT suppression may contribute to the antitumor activity of SHP2 inhibitors in some GI cancers, this effect cannot be generalized. Rational application of SHP2-targeted therapies requires context-aware assessment of the dominant PI3K–AKT regulatory mode in each tumor.

Context-dependent regulation of tumor metabolism by SHP2

Beyond canonical proliferative signaling, SHP2 also participates in metabolic reprogramming in GI tumor cells, particularly in the regulation of glycolysis.

In GC, SHP2 has been shown to promote glycolytic metabolism and malignant progression through a pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2)-dependent mechanism. Mechanistically, SHP2 promotes GC glycolysis, proliferation, and migration by regulating PKM2 phosphorylation through the SHP2/PKM2/adenosine 5′-monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) axis [39]. A similar pro-glycolytic role has been reported in CRC under specific mutational backgrounds. Gain-of-function SHP2 mutations, such as SHP2D61Y and SHP2E76K, have been shown to enhance CRC cell glycolysis, proliferation, migration, and invasion through PKM2 nuclear translocation and PKM2/hnRNPK-related signaling [40]. Conversely, in HCC, SHP2 activation downstream of the ACE2/Ang-(1–7)/Mas receptor axis suppresses ROS–hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF1α)-driven glycolysis and limits tumor growth [41].

These findings indicate that SHP2-mediated metabolic regulation is not uniformly oncogenic but depends on tumor lineage, upstream regulatory context, and SHP2 genetic status.

SHP2 restrains excessive STAT3 signaling and inflammation-associated tumorigenesis

SHP2 may function as a tumor suppressor in selected GI contexts by limiting excessive STAT3 activity and preserving epithelial signaling homeostasis; accordingly, loss of SHP2 can remove this regulatory brake and promote persistent inflammatory and cytokine signaling.

This protective role is particularly evident during inflammation-associated tumor initiation, where loss or reduction of SHP2 activity leads to sustained STAT3 signaling and promotes inflammation-driven tumor development. In GC, within the specific context of IL-6/gp130 signaling, SHP2 activates the ERK–activator protein-1 axis to fine-tune and balance concurrent STAT3 activation. Consequently, genetic or functional loss of SHP2 in this setting leads to STAT3 hyperactivation, disrupts gastric mucosal homeostasis, and paradoxically promotes tumorigenesis [42]. Similarly, hepatocyte-specific loss of Ptpn11 amplifies STAT3-dominant inflammatory signaling, causing liver inflammation and subsequent HCC development [43]. Further evidence from the diethylnitrosamine-induced HCC model showed that SHP2 deficiency in hepatocytes induces genomic instability and enhances pro-inflammatory signaling, while tumor-derived IL-6 further activates the IL-6–JAK–STAT3 pathway and recruits tumor-promoting immune cells, thereby increasing tumor burden [44]. In colitis-associated CRC, epithelial SHP2 deletion relieves repression of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), STAT3, and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, resulting in severe inflammation, oxidative stress, and DNA damage that strongly promote malignant transformation [15]. In certain established tumors, SHP2 inhibition may relieve suppression of STAT3 signaling, thereby promoting pro-survival transcriptional programs. In ESCC cell models, inhibition of SHP2 increases STAT3 phosphorylation and enhances expression of its downstream pro-proliferative (Cyclin D1) and anti-apoptotic (Survivin) target genes [45].

Overall, these data suggest that SHP2 inhibition may not be universally beneficial, particularly in inflammatory or STAT3-high settings where SHP2 loss could exacerbate disease progression.

Functional divergence of SHP2 under specific genetic backgrounds

Beyond tissue lineage and signaling context, the functional output of SHP2 is strongly shaped by specific genetic backgrounds.

In KRAS-driven GI cancers, SHP2 frequently promotes tumor progression and mediates resistance to therapies. SHP2 serves as a critical positive regulator of the RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK pathway in sporadic CRC and often functions as an oncogenic driver, particularly in KRAS-mutant contexts [15]. In PDAC, SHP2 is essential for the survival and proliferation of KRAS-mutant tumor cells and functions as a central signaling node within the KRAS-driven oncogenic network. In an animal experiment, by gene ablation of Ptpn11, the occurrence of pancreatic cancer induced by KRAS was significantly inhibited [46].

A similar theme emerges in BRAF‑mutant contexts. In BRAF V600E CRC, SHP2 forms a complex with mucin-1 C-terminal subunit (MUC1-C) and mediates feedback reactivation of MAPK signaling following BRAF inhibitor treatment [47].

In PDAC with concurrent p53 loss, ROS accumulation can impair SHP2 catalytic activity and redirect signaling dominance toward the JAK2–STAT3 pathway, thereby promoting tumor growth and drug resistance [48]. This suggests that p53 loss may weaken tumor dependence on SHP2.

Overall, the role of SHP2 in GI cancers is highly genotype-dependent, with distinct genetic backgrounds determining sensitivity to SHP2-targeted strategies.

SHP2 as a negative regulator of antitumor immunity: T cells and natural killer cells

In the TME, SHP2 plays a central negative regulatory role in adaptive immune cells (T cells) and innate immune cells, particularly natural killer (NK) cells. By suppressing immune cell activation and cytotoxic functions, SHP2 contributes to tumor immune evasion. Accordingly, inhibition of SHP2 activity is generally associated with enhanced antitumor immune responses.

SHP2 is closely associated with functional impairment of CD8+ T cells. In MC38 CRC mouse models, genetic knockdown of SHP2 or pharmacological inhibition using the SHP2 inhibitor SHP099 significantly increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration, activation, and effector function, accompanied by suppressed tumor growth, suggesting an important role for SHP2 in limiting antitumor T-cell responses [49]. Similarly, studies in GC indicate that SHP2 participates in tumor cell–induced CD8⁺ T-cell dysfunction. Inhibition of SHP2 restores the ability of T cells to produce interferon (IFN) -γ, thereby enhancing immune recognition and killing [50]. In another GC study, SHP2 expression in GC cells has also been associated with reduced chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell cytotoxicity through ROS/JNK-related signaling [51]. Mechanistically, SHP2 acts as a critical downstream mediator of programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and CD28 signaling [52, 53]. Through its N-SH2 domain, SHP2 is recruited to phosphorylated tyrosine residues on PD-1 [54], thereby attenuating sustained T-cell receptor–associated signaling [55]. This regulatory pattern suggests that differences in the efficacy of anti-PD-1 antibodies observed in preclinical models may be related to their differential capacity to recruit SHP2 [56].

SHP2 is also involved in the integration of inhibitory signaling in NK cells. Through interactions with immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motifs on multiple inhibitory receptors, SHP2 raises the activation threshold of NK cells and reduces their cytotoxic and cytokine-producing capacity [57]. In HCC, ligands for Siglec-9 expressed on tumor cells engage the corresponding receptors on NK cells and recruit SHP1/SHP2, thereby strongly suppressing NK cell–mediated antitumor activity [58]. In CRC, inhibition of SHP2 elevates the antitumor potential of NK cells and enhances their ability to produce IFN-γ [59]. In addition, a newly identified immunosuppressive axis in GC, KIR3DL3–HHLA2, likewise suppresses the effector functions of NK cells and CD8+ T cells through recruitment of SHP1/SHP2 [60], further indicating the immunosuppressive role of SHP2 in NK cells and T cells.

Role of SHP2 in B cells: oncogenic signaling functions

SHP2 has also been implicated in the intrinsic oncogenic transformation of B cells under specific pathogenic contexts. In Helicobacter pylori-associated gastric diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, the bacterial virulence protein CagA is injected into B cells and directly binds and activates SHP2 after phosphorylation, which in turn activates downstream ERK signaling pathways and drives abnormal proliferation and lymphomagenesis of B cells [61]. Therefore, instead of modulating the antitumor function of effector B cells, SHP2 acts as a critical mediator of oncogenic signaling during malignant transformation of B cells themselves.

Role of SHP2 in macrophage compartments and other myeloid subsets: context-dependent immune regulation

In TAMs, SHP2 serves as a complex and context-dependent regulator. It predominantly drives macrophages toward an immunosuppressive, pro-tumorigenic M2-like phenotype, thereby suppressing antitumor immunity. Nevertheless, in specific organ microenvironments or cellular subtypes, SHP2 can paradoxically exert tumor-protective functions.

In most research contexts, SHP2 is a key molecule shaping and maintaining the immunosuppressive function of TAMs. SHP2 inhibits STAT3 signaling and disrupts the IL-10/STAT3/SOCS3 anti-inflammatory axis in colitis-associated cancer, thereby impairing macrophage-mediated anti-inflammatory responses and promoting intestinal inflammation and tumorigenesis [62]. In CRC TAMs, SHP2 negatively regulates stimulator of interferon genes (STING)–TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1)–interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3)-mediated type I IFN signaling, thereby limiting IFN-β production and M1-like macrophage activation [59]. SHP2 also acts downstream of the CD47–signal regulatory protein alpha (SIRPα) macrophage checkpoint in CRC, where its activation suppresses macrophage phagocytosis and contributes to an immunosuppressive, immunotherapy-resistant TME [63]. In addition, TAM-intrinsic SHP2 has been linked to STAT3/PI3K activation, exosome-associated programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) signaling, and PD-1-related CAR-T cell apoptosis in CRC, further supporting its role in shaping a suppressive myeloid microenvironment [64]. In GC, tumor cell-derived macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) upregulates SHP2 expression in macrophages, which activates the SHP2/STAT3/PI3K axis and promotes the release of soluble PD-1-containing exosomes from TAMs, thereby directly inhibiting T-cell function [65].

Although tumor promotion is mainstream, the function of SHP2 can be reversed in specific microenvironments or macrophage subsets. When SHP2 was specifically knocked down in macrophages, tyrosine kinase with immunoglobulin-like and EGF-like domains 2 (Tie2)–PI3K–AKT–mTOR signaling became dysregulated, resulting in massive secretion of pro-angiogenic factors (vascular endothelial growth factor, matrix metalloproteinases), which instead significantly promoted liver metastasis of CRC [66]. Specific knockdown of SHP2 in liver-resident macrophages (Kupffer cells) leads to their apoptosis, reduced numbers, and triggers compensatory recruitment of non-Kupffer macrophages with TAM function, ultimately creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment that inhibits CD8+ T cells and exacerbates HCC progression [67]. These findings indicate that the role of SHP2 varies across macrophage populations, exerting tumor-promoting functions in TAMs while displaying tumor-suppressive effects in tissue-resident macrophages. Accordingly, therapeutic targeting of SHP2 requires precise modulation rather than indiscriminate inhibition.

Beyond macrophages, limited preclinical evidence also suggests that SHP2-targeted interventions may affect other myeloid subsets in GI cancers, including myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and dendritic cells (DCs). In PDAC and CRC models, SHP2 inhibition has been associated with reduced granulocytic MDSCs and impaired MDSC immunosuppressive capacity, suggesting that SHP2 may help sustain suppressive myeloid programs within the TME [68, 69]. In HCC models, SHP2 may also participate in regulating hepatic myeloid homeostasis, as SHP2 inhibition altered DC proportions within the liver TME [70]. However, these findings remain largely descriptive and are not yet sufficient to define cell-intrinsic roles of SHP2 in MDSCs or DCs. Further studies are therefore needed to clarify whether SHP2 directly regulates the differentiation, recruitment, antigen-presenting function, or immunosuppressive activity of these myeloid subsets in GI cancers.

Role of SHP2 in stromal and vascular compartments: context-dependent remodeling of tumor ecology

The function of SHP2 in stromal components such as tumor-associated fibroblasts, adipocytes, and endothelial cells remains less well characterized, but emerging evidence suggests that SHP2 participates in stromal and vascular remodeling within the TME.

By forming a direct complex with pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 subunit alpha 1 in adipocytes, SHP2 drives a ROS-dependent axis that promotes adipocyte maturation and IL-6 secretion. This remodels the metabolic TME, thereby fostering the progression of PDAC in obesity [71]. In addition, SHP2-dependent tumor-cell signaling may support stromal fibroblast activation in PDAC, as SHP2 inhibition reduced alpha-smooth muscle actin-positive fibroblasts and tumor-associated fibrosis in a tumor cell–dependent manner [68]. Furthermore, SHP2 can play different roles in angiogenesis. In CRCs, endothelial SHP2 supports tumor vascular integrity by restraining pro-apoptotic STAT3 signaling and sustaining ERK1/2-dependent endothelial survival [72]. In PDAC models, SHP2 inhibition was reported to reduce tumor vascularity and angiogenic gene expression, further supporting a pro-angiogenic role of SHP2 in selected vascular contexts [68]. By contrast, SHP2 in myeloid cells, particularly Tie2⁺ macrophages, restrains pro-angiogenic remodeling in colorectal liver metastasis models [66].

Collectively, these findings indicate that SHP2 is involved in the remodeling of stromal and vascular compartments, with context-dependent effects on tumor progression.

SHP2 as a central mediator of therapy resistance in GI cancers

Current standard therapies for GI cancers, including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and molecularly targeted agents, are frequently limited by primary or acquired resistance. Accumulating evidence now positions SHP2 as a pivotal signaling node that is dynamically modulated by these therapies and, in turn, drives multiple adaptive resistance mechanisms (Fig. 2). The role of SHP2 in resistance to targeted therapies is often shaped by lineage-specific oncogenic drivers and therefore differs among GI malignancies. SHP2-mediated resistance to radiotherapy in GI cancers

Radiotherapy remains an important component of treatment for several GI malignancies. However, therapeutic efficacy is frequently limited by intrinsic or acquired radioresistance. Emerging evidence suggests that SHP2 may contribute to this process through both tumor-intrinsic survival signaling and modulation of the TME.

One representative mechanism involves immune evasion through the CD47/SIRPα axis. In CRC, following irradiation, increased CD47 signaling can activate SIRPα-associated SHP2 in macrophages, thereby suppressing macrophage-mediated phagocytosis, allowing tumor cells to evade immune clearance post-radiation and contributing to radioresistance [73, 74]. These findings suggest that SHP2 may promote radioresistance by reinforcing post-radiotherapy immune evasion, highlighting SHP2 as a potential radiosensitization target.

Tumor-intrinsic SHP2-associated signaling may contribute to radioresistance in CRC. Kelley et al. (2017) reported that radioresistant rectal cancer was associated with reduced miR-451a expression and increased CAB39/EMSY levels, which may enhance SHP2/RAF-associated survival signaling. Pharmacologic inhibition of SHP2 reduced tumor cell proliferation and helped restore radiosensitivity, supporting a role for SHP2 in radiotherapy resistance [75].

In other GI malignancies, direct studies defining the role of SHP2 in radiotherapy resistance are still lacking. Nevertheless, previous studies in GI cancers have shown that resistance to radiotherapy involves multiple pro-survival and pro-proliferative pathways, including Wnt/β-catenin, PI3K–AKT–mTOR, and JAK/STAT3 signaling [76]. Given the close functional association of SHP2 with these networks, SHP2 may participate in the regulation of radiotherapy resistance in these cancers.

SHP2-mediated resistance to chemotherapy in GI cancers

Chemotherapy remains a cornerstone of treatment for advanced or unresectable GI malignancies. However, the development of intrinsic or acquired chemoresistance severely limits long-term therapeutic efficacy. Accumulating evidence identifies SHP2 as an important signaling node implicated in chemotherapy response and resistance, which in turn promotes cell survival through multiple downstream mechanisms. Importantly, the mechanisms by which SHP2 influences chemotherapy resistance differ across GI cancer types.

Among GI malignancies, GC remains a representative setting in which platinum- and fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy is widely used, making resistance to cytotoxic therapy a persistent challenge. Studies have shown that cisplatin, as a therapeutic drug for GC, can activate SHP2, thereby increasing the phosphorylation level of γ-H2A.X in DNA double-strand break repair, ultimately reducing the sensitivity of GC cells to cisplatin. Mechanistically, SHP2 dephosphorylates and activates PKM2, establishing a positive feedback loop with AMPK that enhances aerobic glycolysis, which further fuels tumor proliferation and contributes to the Warburg effect. Activation of this SHP2/PKM2/AMPK axis has been validated as a key determinant of cisplatin resistance, and targeting SHP2 represents a promising strategy to resensitize GC cells to platinum-based chemotherapy [39]. Clinical evidence further supports the relevance of SHP2 signaling in GC chemotherapy response. The PTPN11 rs2301756 A allele was associated with improved response and prolonged overall survival in GC patients receiving cisplatin/5-fluorouracil-based chemotherapy, suggesting that PTPN11 genotyping may have predictive value for chemosensitivity [77].

A similar theme of SHP2‑driven survival signaling emerges in CRC. In oxaliplatin-resistant CRC cells, the expression of SHP2 is elevated, and it promotes resistance by activating the AKT and ERK signaling pathways [34]. In acquired oxaliplatin/5-fluorouracil-resistant CRC cells, SHP2 contributes to PI3K–AKT-driven oxidative metabolic adaptation. The SHP2 inhibitor SHP099 reverses resistance by suppressing PI3K–AKT signaling and disrupting this metabolic adaptation, suggesting that SHP2 is an important mediator of acquired chemoresistance in CRC [78]. In addition, SHP2 inhibits ferritinophagy through the PI3K–BRD4–TFEB axis, reduces ROS production and inhibits ferroptosis, thereby promoting the progression of CRC and chemotherapy resistance [35]. Collectively, these data suggest that SHP2 may enhance CRC chemoresistance by simultaneously sustaining survival signaling and suppressing therapy-induced cell death pathways.

In HCC, SHP2 may contribute to chemoresistance mainly by expanding cancer stem cell (CSC) populations. Clinically, elevated SHP2 expression correlates with aggressive disease behavior and poor prognosis. Mechanistically, SHP2 has been linked to treatment resistance through maintenance of CSC-like traits. By enhancing β-catenin signaling, SHP2 promotes self-renewal and dedifferentiation programs associated with reduced chemotherapy sensitivity [4]. This observation suggests that SHP2 may contribute to chemotherapy resistance in HCC by preserving stem-like tumor cell populations and reinforcing pro-survival signaling.

In PDAC, direct evidence linking SHP2 to intrinsic or acquired chemoresistance remains limited. Nevertheless, pharmacological SHP2 inhibition has been reported to enhance the efficacy of standard chemotherapy, suggesting a potential chemosensitizing role in this setting [79]. Further mechanistic and translational studies are needed to clarify whether SHP2 inhibition can be rationally integrated into chemotherapy-based regimens for PDAC.

In ESCC, SHP2 may exert a context-dependent role distinct from that in other GI malignancies. One study showed that SHP2 depletion reduced cisplatin sensitivity and increased the expression of the drug resistance-associated transporter ATP-binding cassette subfamily G member 2 and the stemness marker Nanog, suggesting that SHP2 may help preserve chemosensitivity by restraining STAT3-associated resistance programs [79]. Conversely, elevated SHP2 expression has been linked to enhanced chemotherapy-induced apoptosis in ESCC cells. These findings suggest that SHP2 does not exert uniform effects across GI cancers, and therapeutic targeting may need to be tailored to specific tumor contexts.

Collectively, these observations demonstrate that SHP2 serves as a convergent node for chemoresistance across multiple GI malignancies, yet through strikingly different mechanisms. This mechanistic heterogeneity underscores the necessity of cancer type–specific considerations when targeting SHP2 to overcome chemoresistance.

SHP2-mediated resistance to targeted therapies in GI cancers

Molecularly targeted therapies have expanded the therapeutic landscape of GI cancers, yet their clinical benefit is often constrained by adaptive resistance. SHP2 has emerged as an important mediator of this process by linking compensatory upstream signals to downstream pathway reactivation. Because the dominant oncogenic drivers and therapeutic targets differ among GI malignancies, SHP2-mediated resistance to targeted therapies is discussed below according to tumor type.

GC

Owing to its marked molecular heterogeneity, GC can develop acquired resistance to targeted therapies through multiple adaptive signaling routes. SHP2 has emerged as an important mediator in this process, particularly in resistance to RTK- and MEK-directed therapies.

In GC treated with RTK-targeted therapies, adaptive resistance often converges on SHP2-dependent restoration of MAPK signaling, as illustrated by resistance to MET and FGFR2 inhibitors. In MET-amplified diffuse-type GC, SHP2 is tyrosine-phosphorylated downstream of MET and sustains MAPK signaling. Importantly, SHP2 remains functionally engaged in MET inhibitor-resistant cells, as its knockdown or pharmacological inhibition selectively suppresses their growth. This indicates that SHP2 contributes to acquired MET inhibitor resistance by maintaining MAPK-dependent survival signaling [29]. Similarly, in FGFR2-driven GC, SHP2 mediates FGFR2–FRS2α–MAPK signaling, suggesting that SHP2 may contribute to adaptive escape from FGFR2-targeted therapy [80]. These findings indicate that, in RTK-addicted GC, SHP2 may function as a convergent node through which heterogeneous upstream receptors sustain downstream MAPK signaling under therapeutic pressure.

In KRAS‑dependent GC, MEK inhibitors are a rational therapeutic option, but adaptive resistance frequently limits efficacy. MEK inhibitors trigger adaptive resistance through SHP2-mediated activation of kinase suppressor of Ras 1 (KSR1), which reactivates ERK signaling and limits the durability of treatment [81]. In addition, in KRAS-amplified gastroesophageal cancer, MEK inhibition triggers adaptive resistance through SHP2-dependent reactivation of KRAS-GTP and AKT signaling [82]. These findings suggest that SHP2 supports MEK inhibitor resistance in GC through context-specific adaptive signaling mechanisms.

Together, these studies establish SHP2 as a key mediator of targeted therapy resistance in GC, particularly in tumors driven by RTK amplification or KRAS–MAPK pathway dependency, providing a strong mechanistic rationale for combining SHP2 inhibitors with these agents to prevent or overcome acquired resistance.

HCC

In HCC, SHP2-mediated resistance to targeted therapy is closely linked to adaptive feedback signaling and CSC-associated plasticity.

Sorafenib resistance represents a major setting in which SHP2 contributes to therapeutic escape. SHP2 blockade has been shown to mitigate adaptive resistance to sorafenib in HCC, suggesting that SHP2 is involved in compensatory survival signaling after multikinase inhibition [83]. SHP2 also promotes sorafenib resistance in HCC by expanding β-catenin-dependent CSC-like populations. Mechanistically, SHP2 dephosphorylates CDC73 to facilitate β-catenin nuclear translocation and inhibits glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta-mediated β-catenin degradation, thereby enhancing HCC cell dedifferentiation and CSC self-renewal. Clinically, the observation that HCC patients with low SHP2 expression benefit from sorafenib, whereas those with high SHP2 expression do not, further supports SHP2 as a mediator of sorafenib resistance [4]. Together, these findings indicate that SHP2 promotes sorafenib resistance in HCC through both adaptive survival signaling and β-catenin-driven CSC plasticity, making it a potential target for overcoming multikinase inhibitor resistance.

SHP2 also participates in resistance to mTOR-targeted therapy. Under therapeutic pressure from mTOR inhibitors, RTK feedback activation engages SHP2 to restore downstream signaling, including reactivation of the mTOR pathway itself, thereby contributing to adaptive resistance [84]. This finding further supports the role of SHP2 as an adaptive feedback node that enables HCC cells to restore survival signaling after targeted pathway inhibition.

Collectively, these studies suggest that SHP2 contributes to targeted therapy resistance in HCC through both feedback pathway reactivation and CSC-related cellular plasticity, providing a rationale for combining SHP2 inhibitors with sorafenib or mTOR-directed therapies in selected HCC contexts.

CRC

In CRC, SHP2-mediated resistance to targeted therapy is closely associated with adaptive restoration of EGFR–RAS–MAPK and PI3K–AKT signaling. This is particularly evident in resistance to EGFR-, BRAF-, and KRAS-directed therapies, where SHP2 often acts as a downstream relay that maintains proliferative and survival signaling despite pathway blockade.

Cetuximab, an EGFR-targeted monoclonal antibody, is approved for RAS wild-type metastatic CRC, but resistance remains frequent. Elevated PLCγ1 expression bypasses cetuximab-mediated EGFR inhibition through SHP2-dependent ERK and AKT activation, thereby driving cetuximab resistance. SHP2 inhibition restores cetuximab sensitivity in PLCγ1-resistant CRC cells, supporting the PLCγ1–SHP2 axis as an actionable mechanism of anti-EGFR resistance in CRC [85].

SHP2 is also involved in resistance to BRAF-targeted therapy. In BRAF V600E CRC, SHP2 participates in BRAF inhibitor resistance by mediating MUC1-C-dependent feedback activation of MAPK signaling. Mechanistically, MUC1-C associates with SHP2 and is required for SHP2 activation, which in turn drives feedback reactivation of the MAPK pathway following BRAF inhibition [47]. Thus, SHP2-mediated MAPK feedback represents an important mechanism limiting the durability of BRAF inhibition in BRAF V600E CRC.

In KRAS-mutant CRC, SHP2 may participate in targeted therapy resistance by maintaining residual RAS–MAPK signaling. SHP2 deletion has been shown to reduce RAF/MEK/ERK phosphorylation in KRAS-mutant CRC cells, indicating that mutant KRAS-driven tumors can retain partial dependence on upstream SHP2-mediated signaling. This residual signaling capacity may facilitate adaptive pathway reactivation after KRAS- or MAPK-directed inhibition [86].

Together, these findings support SHP2 as an adaptive signaling node in molecularly defined CRC subsets and provide a rationale for combining SHP2 inhibitors with EGFR-, BRAF-, or emerging KRAS-directed therapies.

PDAC

In PDAC, SHP2 plays an important role in adaptive resistance to KRAS- and MAPK-directed therapies by serving as an upstream regulator of RAS activation and a hub for RTK-driven feedback signaling.

SHP2 contributes to resistance to downstream MAPK pathway inhibition. In KRAS-mutant PDAC, MEK inhibition can relieve ERK-mediated negative feedback, allowing compensatory RTK–SHP2 signaling to reactivate downstream MAPK and survival pathways [46]. This feedback response may help explain the limited durability of MEK inhibitor activity in PDAC.

SHP2 is also involved in resistance to direct KRAS inhibition. In KRAS G12C-mutant PDAC, KRAS G12C inhibition can induce RTK/SOS-dependent RAS reactivation, and SHP2 acts upstream of this feedback process to restore downstream MAPK signaling and contribute to drug resistance [68].

Collectively, these findings suggest that SHP2 contributes to targeted therapy resistance in PDAC by enabling adaptive restoration of RAS effector signaling after either downstream MAPK blockade or direct KRAS inhibition.

ESCC

Compared with other GI malignancies, direct evidence linking SHP2 to targeted therapy resistance in ESCC remains limited. Most available studies in ESCC have focused on the role of SHP2 in tumor cell signaling and chemotherapy response rather than resistance to molecularly targeted agents. These findings suggest that the therapeutic impact of SHP2 inhibition in ESCC may differ from that observed in RTK- or KRAS-driven GI cancers. Further studies are needed to determine whether SHP2 participates in resistance to targeted agents in molecularly defined ESCC subsets.

Collectively, these studies suggest that SHP2 serves as an important mediator of adaptive resistance to radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy in GI cancers. Notably, SHP2-mediated resistance mechanisms vary across GI cancer types and treatment settings, underscoring the need for tumor type-specific therapeutic strategies. Targeting SHP2 may therefore provide a promising approach to overcome therapeutic resistance and enhance treatment efficacy in selected patient populations.

The application of SHP2 inhibitors in the treatment of GI cancers

Given its central role in oncogenic signaling and immune regulation, SHP2 has emerged as a compelling therapeutic target in GI cancers. A spectrum of SHP2-targeting strategies has been developed, including catalytic-site inhibitors, allosteric inhibitors, and proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) degraders [5, 87, 88]. Although substantial preclinical progress has been achieved, the clinical translation of these strategies remains challenging.

SHP2 inhibition as a monotherapy: efficacy and mechanisms

As monotherapy, SHP2 inhibitors exert antitumor effects through a dual mechanism: direct suppression of oncogenic signaling in tumor cells and systemic reprogramming of the immunosuppressive TME.

Direct antitumor effects via pathway inhibition

In KRAS-mutant CRC, allosteric inhibitors such as SHP099 and the novel compound NI-1 (PCC0208023) suppress tumor cell proliferation by blocking upstream RAS activation and downstream MAPK signaling [89].

Similarly, in EC, SHP2 allosteric inhibition suppresses tumor growth mainly by blocking SHP2-mediated AKT and ERK signaling and inducing apoptosis. TK-642 significantly inhibits proliferation and promotes apoptosis in KYSE-520 cells [31], while the highly potent inhibitors TK-684 and TK-685 exert similar effects in KYSE-150 cells. Notably, TK-685 also shows strong antitumor efficacy in vivo, markedly reducing tumor growth in KYSE-150 xenograft models following oral administration [90].

In PDAC, the allosteric inhibitor SHP099 suppresses IL-6–driven signaling linked to obesity-associated PDAC progression, thereby reducing tumor cell migration and invasion and modestly restraining cell-cycle progression [71]. Similarly, the potent SHP2 allosteric inhibitor Lead compound 23 effectively inhibits the proliferation, migration, and invasion of MIA PaCa-2 cells [91].

In GC, SHP099 can suppress GC cell proliferation and migration by targeting the SHP2/PKM2/AMPK pathway, thereby restraining tumor progression [39]. In MET-amplified diffuse-type GC, where SHP2 functions as a critical downstream effector sustaining malignant behavior, SHP099 effectively inhibits tumor growth and helps overcome resistance to MET inhibitors [29].

Thus, SHP2 serves as an actionable node for directly inhibiting proliferation and survival in diverse GI cancers.

Remodeling the immunosuppressive TME

A key strength of SHP2 inhibitors is their ability to reverse multiple facets of immunosuppression, which underlies their efficacy in immunocompetent models.

SHP2 inhibition has been shown to augment CD8+ T cell infiltration, activation, and effector function. In CRC models, SHP099 and the phenylurea inhibitor A8 increase intratumoral CD8+ T cell infiltration, activation, and effector function, shifting the TME toward an immune-active state [92]. In GC, the SHP2 inhibitor TNO155 enhances CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity and granzyme B/IFN-γ expression, significantly reducing the migration and invasion capabilities of HGC-27 cells [50]. Together, these findings suggest that SHP2 inhibition reinvigorates effective cytotoxic T-cell responses, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity in GI cancers.

In parallel, these inhibitors effectively modulate innate immune and myeloid cells. In HCC, SHP099 can reduce pro-inflammatory cytokines and inhibit the C-C chemokine receptor type 5 axis, thereby attenuating the protumorigenic functions of TAMs [70]. Furthermore, SHP099 can enhance type I interferon signaling in tumors and immune cells via the STING–TBK1–IRF3 pathway, fostering a more immunogenic milieu [59]. In the CT26 CRC model, the allosteric inhibitor RMC-4550 shifts TAMs from a protumorigenic M2-like state toward an antitumor M1-like phenotype. This reprogramming occurs through a mechanism independent of CD8+ T cells or IFN-γ, primarily by reducing the selective depletion of colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor–driven M2-like macrophages [69]. Notably, the immunomodulatory impact of SHP2 inhibition exhibits tissue-specific nuances. For instance, in HCC, SHP099 primarily corrects the inflammatory myeloid compartment with a more neutral effect on adaptive immunity, notably increasing but not fully activating T and NK cells. The modulation of hepatic antitumor innate immunity by SHP099 was manifested by an upregulated proportion of DCs, indicative of enhanced antigen presentation potential [70]. In CRC, the SHP2 inhibitor RMC-4550 paradoxically increased the intratumoral abundance of MDSCs while effectively attenuating their immunosuppressive capacity [69].

Overall, SHP2 inhibition achieves systemic immunomodulation in GI cancers, converting the immunosuppressive TME into an immune-permissive state that provides a strong rationale for combination immunotherapy.

Synergistic strategies: combining SHP2 inhibitors

The major therapeutic promise of SHP2 inhibition lies in its capacity to synergize with established treatment modalities by blocking adaptive resistance pathways and enhancing antitumor immunity. Below, we summarize the evidence for combining SHP2 inhibitors with targeted therapies, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy.

Combination with targeted therapies

Combining SHP2 inhibitors with targeted therapies has emerged as an important approach to limit adaptive resistance in GI cancers. By blocking feedback and bypass signaling after pathway inhibition, SHP2 inhibitors may improve the depth and durability of targeted therapy responses.

A major application is the combination of SHP2 inhibitors with MAPK pathway inhibitors. In PDAC, the SHP2 inhibitor RMC-4550 combined with the ERK inhibitor LY3214996 more effectively blocks MAPK signaling and enhances tumor cell apoptosis compared with monotherapy [93]. Similarly, in PDAC and GC models, combining trametinib with SHP2 inhibitors such as SHP099 or GS493 suppresses RTK–SHP2 feedback signaling and enhances MAPK pathway blockade [46, 82]. In KRAS-mutant GC, SHP099 combined with MEK inhibitors such as AZD6244 blocks SHP2/KSR1-dependent ERK reactivation, promotes apoptosis, and inhibits tumor growth in vivo [81]. These findings support combined SHP2 and MEK/ERK inhibition as a vertical blockade strategy to prevent MAPK pathway rebound. Consistently, TNO155 has been shown to synergize with BRAF plus MEK inhibition in BRAF^V600E CRC models by blocking RTK-mediated ERK feedback activation [94].

SHP2 inhibitors can also be combined with RTK-, mTOR-, and multikinase-targeted therapies. In CRC, SHP099 restores cetuximab sensitivity by suppressing PLCγ1-SHP2-mediated bypass activation of ERK and AKT signaling [95]. In FGFR2-driven GC, dual targeting of FGFR2 and SHP2 more effectively suppresses ERK activity and tumor growth [80]. Furthermore, combining the SHP2 inhibitors SHP099 or RMC-4550 with the mTOR inhibitor AZD8055 effectively blocks RAS–MAPK and mTOR–PI3K–AKT signal transduction, promotes MYC degradation and downregulation, and produces stronger antitumor effects in HCC models [84]. In addition, in HCC, SHP099 prevents the reactivation of MAPK and AKT pathways by blocking the RTK–SHP2 feedback, thereby reversing resistance to the multi-kinase inhibitor sorafenib [83]. Similarly, in CRC models, SHP099 enhanced the antitumor effect of the multikinase inhibitor regorafenib, leading to reduced tumor growth, vascular normalization, and increased CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T-cell activation [96]. Overall, these examples indicate that SHP2 inhibitors can reinforce RTK-, mTOR-, and multikinase-targeted therapies by blocking compensatory survival signaling after pathway inhibition.

Direct KRAS inhibition provides another important setting for SHP2-based combinations. In PDAC treated with KRAS G12C inhibitors, concurrent SHP2 blockade prevents RTK-mediated reactivation of wild-type RAS, promoting the remodeling of the TME [68]. Similarly, the combination of the SHP2 inhibitor TNO155 and the KRAS G12C inhibitor sotorasib suggests potent activity in suppressing KRAS-driven signaling [97]. Emerging evidence also supports this concept in KRAS G12D-mutant pancreatic cancer models. In KRAS G12D-mutant pancreatic cancer models, combining LPM5140276 with the SHP2 inhibitor RMC-4550 suppresses rebound ERK–AKT activation and enhances antitumor efficacy [98].

Furthermore, pairing SHP2 inhibitors with anti-angiogenic drugs enables a concerted attack on the tumor by co-targeting malignant cells, the vasculature, and the immune microenvironment. For example, combining SHP099 with the Ang1/Ang2-Tie2 inhibitor AMG386 can slow tumor progression by co-targeting the vasculature and tumor cell proliferation [72]. More comprehensively, in metastatic CRC models, the combination of SHP099 and the anti-angiogenic agent regorafenib not only induces tumor shrinkage and vascular normalization but also increases CD8+ T-cell infiltration and promotes favorable remodeling of the immune microenvironment [96]. These findings suggest that SHP2 inhibition may enhance anti-angiogenic therapy not only by suppressing tumor cell signaling, but also by improving vascular and immune microenvironmental conditions.

Overall, these studies indicate that combining SHP2 inhibitors with various targeted agents may help intercept adaptive resistance mechanisms and improve the durability of therapeutic responses.

Combination with chemotherapy

SHP2 inhibitors may resensitize selected chemoresistant GI cancers to conventional cytotoxic drugs by blocking SHP2-dependent survival signaling, metabolic adaptation, and other resistance-associated programs.

In CRC, SHP2 inhibition has shown potential to restore sensitivity to fluoropyrimidine- and platinum-based chemotherapy. SHP099 restores sensitivity in oxaliplatin-resistant CRC cell lines [34]. Furthermore, in cells with acquired resistance to oxaliplatin and 5-fluorouracil, SHP099 suppresses PI3K–AKT signaling, disrupts oxidative metabolic adaptation, and reduces proliferation, migration, and stem-like properties, thereby restoring chemosensitivity [78]. In addition, beyond standard cytotoxic chemotherapeutics, SHP099 has been reported to enhance celastrol-induced CRC growth suppression by blocking RTK–SHP2–AKT/ERK feedback activation and increasing CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration [99]. These findings suggest that SHP2 inhibition can counteract adaptive survival and metabolic programs that sustain chemotherapy resistance in CRC.

In GC, cisplatin treatment can activate SHP2 and promote chemoresistance, whereas SHP099 enhances cisplatin sensitivity and reverses resistance [39]. This supports the combination of SHP2 inhibitors with platinum-based chemotherapy in selected GC contexts. In PDAC, the allosteric SHP2 inhibitor RMC4630 synergizes with nab-paclitaxel plus gemcitabine in KRAS G12D-mutant models, producing stronger growth inhibition than chemotherapy alone [100]. Although direct evidence for SHP2-driven chemoresistance in PDAC remains limited, this finding suggests that SHP2 inhibition may enhance the efficacy of standard chemotherapy.

Overall, these studies indicate that SHP2 inhibitors may improve chemotherapy responses in selected GI cancers by suppressing compensatory survival and metabolic pathways. However, because SHP2 can exert context-dependent effects in some tumors, such as ESCC, the use of SHP2 inhibitors as chemosensitizers should be guided by tumor type and molecular background.

Combination with immunotherapy

The immunomodulatory effects of SHP2 inhibitors provide a mechanistic rationale for their combination with immune checkpoint blockade.

The combination of the SHP2 allosteric inhibitor TNO155 with an anti-PD-L1 antibody enhances antitumor immunity in a subcutaneous CT26 tumor model and significantly reduces CRC liver metastasis [73]. Similarly, combining the SHP2 inhibitor SHP099 with anti-PD-1 therapy synergistically enhances CD8⁺ T-cell activation and antitumor function, leading to delayed tumor growth in CRC mouse models [49]. These findings collectively validate that SHP2 inhibition can effectively overcome key mechanisms of immunosuppression, creating a favorable context for immune checkpoint blockade agents to achieve superior and more durable antitumor responses.

Collectively, these findings suggest that SHP2 inhibitors may function as sensitizing agents in combination regimens. By targeting key adaptive responses, SHP2 inhibitors can help overcome therapeutic resistance and enhance the efficacy of established treatment modalities across GI cancers. Emerging SHP2 degraders compared with allosteric inhibitors

Although allosteric SHP2 inhibitors have shown promising activity in preclinical models and early clinical studies, emerging SHP2 degraders provide a conceptually distinct therapeutic strategy.

Classical SHP2 allosteric inhibitors, represented by SHP099, TNO155, and RMC-4630, bind to a tunnel-like allosteric pocket and stabilize SHP2 in its autoinhibited conformation, thereby suppressing phosphatase activity [87, 102, 103]. However, these inhibitors do not eliminate the SHP2 protein itself, leaving open the possibility that phosphatase-independent scaffolding functions may persist. In contrast, emerging SHP2 degraders reduce SHP2 protein abundance, thereby potentially producing more complete and durable suppression of SHP2-dependent signaling than allosteric inhibitors. For example, in KYSE520 ESCC cells, the PROTAC-based SHP2 degrader SHP2-D26 reduced SHP2 protein levels by more than 95% and inhibited ERK phosphorylation and cell growth more potently than the allosteric inhibitor SHP099 [88]. Other emerging degradation strategies, such as autophagy-based approaches, may also offer alternative routes for reducing SHP2 protein abundance. The SHP2-AUTAC degrader SA-8 recruits microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3) and induces selective SHP2 degradation through the autophagy–lysosome pathway, demonstrating the feasibility of AUTAC-mediated SHP2 degradation [104]. In KRAS G12D-mutant PDAC models, the autophagy-based SHP2 degrader 11n promotes SHP2 degradation by enhancing SHP2–LC3 interactions and shows potent antitumor activity both in vitro and in vivo [105].

A potential advantage of SHP2 degraders is their ability to address resistance mechanisms associated with conformational changes or activating mutations of SHP2. Gain-of-function mutations in SHP2, such as E76K, destabilize the autoinhibited conformation and reduce the binding affinity of allosteric inhibitors like SHP099, thereby limiting their inhibitory efficacy [17]. Because degraders reduce SHP2 protein abundance through ubiquitin-proteasome or autophagic pathways rather than simply locking its conformation, they could theoretically circumvent resistance driven by activating mutations. Recent studies have provided proof-of-concept evidence: bioPROTACs have been shown to effectively degrade various oncogenic SHP2 mutants that are otherwise challenging to selectively target with small molecule constructs [106]. Another potential advantage of SHP2 degraders is that, by reducing SHP2 protein abundance, they may disrupt both catalytic and scaffold-associated components of SHP2-dependent signaling. In KRAS-mutant GC, SHP2 promotes adaptive resistance to MEK inhibition by interacting with the scaffold protein KSR1 and regulating KSR1 activation, whereas a KSR1 activating mutation (S269A) diminishes the synergistic anti-proliferative effect of combined SHP2 and MEK inhibition [81]. This might be because allosteric inhibitors only block phosphatase activity without eliminating the SHP2 protein, meaning that they may not disrupt such scaffolding functions. In contrast, by reducing SHP2 protein abundance, SHP2 degraders may provide a theoretical means to attenuate both catalytic and scaffold-associated pro-resistance mechanisms, although this possibility still requires direct validation in cancer models. Therefore, SHP2 degraders may provide a potential strategy to overcome resistance mechanisms that are not fully addressed by catalytic or allosteric inhibition alone.

Despite these potential advantages, SHP2 degraders remain at an early stage of development and face several important challenges. PROTAC-based degraders often have unfavorable physicochemical properties, including large molecular weight, poor aqueous solubility, and limited cell permeability, which may compromise oral bioavailability, pharmacokinetic performance, and therapeutic efficacy [107]. These pharmacological barriers may limit the translational potential of SHP2 degraders.

Although emerging SHP2 degraders have shown promising preclinical activity, systematic validation in GI cancer models remains limited. Future efforts should focus on developing next-generation SHP2 degraders with improved potency, selectivity, and drug-like properties, as well as optimized delivery strategies to enhance tumor accumulation, stability, and pharmacological performance.

Clinical translation

The clinical development of SHP2 inhibitors remains at an early and exploratory stage, with multiple small-molecule SHP2 inhibitors entering the clinical trial stage for the monotherapy or combination therapy of solid tumors or drug-resistant cancers (Table 3). No dedicated clinical cohorts have yet been established to evaluate SHP2 inhibitors in GI cancers specifically; available evidence is mainly derived from GI cancer patients enrolled within broader pan-solid-tumor trials, leaving GI cancer-specific efficacy insufficiently defined. Recent clinical progress of SHP2 allosteric inhibitors

Several recent trials have provided the first clinical readouts for SHP2 allosteric inhibitors. TNO155, a representative SHP2 allosteric inhibitor that entered clinical development early, was evaluated as monotherapy in a first-in-human phase I dose-escalation/expansion trial (CTNO155 × 2101, NCT03114319). This study enrolled 118 patients with advanced solid tumors, among whom CRC and GI stromal tumors accounted for 54% and 16%, respectively. TNO155 showed favorable pharmacokinetic properties, including rapid absorption, with a median Tmax of approximately 1.1 h, and a relatively long effective median half-life of approximately 34 h. Pharmacodynamic analyses further demonstrated evidence of target inhibition: among patients treated with TNO155 at doses ≥ 20 mg/day who had evaluable paired tumor samples, 90% (38/42) showed a ≥ 25% reduction in DUSP6 expression, suggesting effective suppression of SHP2–MAPK pathway signaling. However, the best observed response to TNO155 monotherapy was stable disease, reported in 24 patients (20%), with a median duration of 4.9 months; moreover, 80% of patients discontinued treatment because of disease progression [108]. These findings provide early clinical evidence that SHP2 inhibitor monotherapy has limited efficacy and further support the development of rational combination strategies.

JAB-3312 (sitneprotafib) is another clinically developed SHP2 allosteric inhibitor, and its combination with the KRAS G12C inhibitor glecirasib represents an important clinical exploration of dual SHP2/KRAS G12C blockade. In a phase I/IIa study (NCT05288205), glecirasib plus JAB-3312 showed preliminary antitumor activity in patients with KRAS G12C-mutant solid tumors. The study included 194 patients with advanced solid tumors, including 171 patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Among patients with NSCLC, the objective response rate was 71% in previously untreated patients, 49% in patients previously treated with systemic therapy but naïve to KRAS G12C inhibitors, and 10% in those previously treated with KRAS G12C inhibitors. Although the phase I/IIa trial included only a limited number of GI cancer patients (17 with CRC or PDAC), the manageable safety profile and high response rate in NSCLC provide a strong rationale for evaluating this combination in KRAS G12C-mutant GI malignancies [109]. These developments suggest the potential clinical relevance of JAB-3312-based combinations in molecularly selected GI cancer populations, although their GI cancer-specific efficacy remains to be established in dedicated clinical cohorts.

RMC-4630 is another clinically investigated SHP2 inhibitor that has been evaluated in combination with KRAS G12C inhibition. In the CodeBreaK101 phase Ib study, sotorasib was combined with RMC-4630 in patients with KRAS p.G12C-mutated NSCLC, CRC, or other solid tumors. Among 21 enrolled patients, six had CRC. The combination showed a manageable safety profile, with no grade ≥ 4 treatment-related adverse events; the most common treatment-related adverse events were peripheral or localized edema, diarrhea, and fatigue. Although the most pronounced antitumor activity was observed in NSCLC, five of six patients with CRC achieved disease control, including one patient with a 26% reduction in tumor burden [110]. These findings provide early clinical support for the feasibility of combining SHP2 inhibition with KRAS G12C blockade, while also indicating that the efficacy of this strategy in KRAS G12C-mutant CRC remains preliminary and requires further validation in GI cancer-specific cohorts.

A recently published first-in-human phase 1 study of BBP-398 (NCT04528836) provides clinically relevant evidence for the translational development of SHP2 allosteric inhibitors. BBP-398 is an orally active, selective allosteric SHP2 inhibitor designed to suppress MAPK pathway-driven tumors. The trial included colon, rectal, pancreatic, GI stromal, and cholangiocarcinoma cases. BBP-398 monotherapy mainly achieved disease stabilization, with stable disease observed in 26% of evaluable patients in the dose-escalation phase and 30% in the dose-expansion phase, whereas no objective responses were reported. Median progression-free survival was 1.8 months in the dose-escalation phase and 2.2 and 1.9 months in the 350-mg and 450-mg expansion cohorts, respectively. From a safety perspective, daily BBP-398 doses up to 450 mg showed an acceptable tolerability profile, whereas dose escalation was halted at 550 mg because of increased thrombocytopenia and edema. Common treatment-emergent adverse events included diarrhea, nausea, peripheral edema, decreased platelet count, vomiting, increased weight, rash, fatigue, and electrolyte abnormalities [111].

Collectively, these early clinical data suggest that SHP2 allosteric inhibitors have shown manageable safety and preliminary pharmacodynamic activity, but their single-agent efficacy remains limited. For GI cancers, current evidence is still mainly derived from small exploratory cohorts within pan-solid tumor trials. Future studies should therefore focus on biomarker-guided combination strategies and GI cancer-specific clinical cohorts to better define the therapeutic value of SHP2 inhibition.

Key factors for identifying potential beneficiary populations

The clinical benefits of SHP2-targeted therapy largely depend on the precise identification of potential beneficiary populations.

Genetic background is an important basis for identifying potential beneficiary patients. Gain-of-function mutations in the PTPN11 gene can affect the selection of SHP2 inhibitors. For example, the E76K mutation can significantly reduce the inhibitory efficacy of SHP099 [17], while the E69K mutation is quite sensitive to the allosteric inhibitor SHP099 of SHP2 [112], demonstrating the influence of oncogenic mutations of SHP2 on the sensitivity of SHP2 inhibitors.

In addition, the therapeutic effects of SHP2 inhibition vary across distinct oncogenic contexts and are particularly influenced by upstream RTK dependency and KRAS mutational status. SHP2 serves as a critical signaling node linking upstream RTKs to the downstream RAS–MAPK pathway. Consequently, GI cancers driven by RTK activation tend to be more sensitive to SHP2 inhibition. For instance, SHP099 has a significant inhibitory effect on RTK-dependent cancer cells, effectively inhibiting cell proliferation and the level of p-ERK [87]. Moreover, cancer cells harboring different KRAS mutations show variable sensitivity to SHP2 inhibitors. Cancer cells carrying mutations such as KRAS Q61H, G13D, and Q61X are generally resistant to SHP2 inhibitors [87, 113, 114], while cell lines with mutations at the G12 site (especially G12C) tend to show increased sensitivity to SHP2 inhibitors [115]. The value of SHP2 inhibitors in the treatment of KRAS mutant tumors is more reflected in combination therapy. Combination with MEK inhibitors or KRAS G12D inhibitors may help overcome adaptive resistance and enhance therapeutic efficacy [81, 116].

Tumor immunophenotypes may also inform the selection of patients for SHP2 inhibitor therapy and may serve as auxiliary markers for evaluating the potential of the combination of SHP2 inhibition and immunotherapy. For instance, high infiltration of SHP2-positive M2-like macrophages (CD68+SHP2+) in tumor tissues is associated with a poor prognosis, suggesting that such patients may benefit from reshaping the immunosuppressive microenvironment through SHP2 inhibition [117].

Context-dependent risks of SHP2 inhibition

Although SHP2 inhibition has shown therapeutic potential in multiple GI cancer models, the context-dependent functions of SHP2 raise an important translational dilemma. In selected disease contexts, broad SHP2 blockade could compromise protective signaling functions or promote compensatory tumor programs. Recognition of these risk contexts may improve patient selection and treatment design.

A key concern is that SHP2 inhibitors may confer limited benefit or even produce counterproductive effects in specific tumor contexts where SHP2 physiologically serves to restrain pro-survival or pro-inflammatory signaling. In inflammation-driven or STAT3-high GI cancers, SHP2 loss may fail to suppress tumor growth and may instead accelerate tumor initiation or progression by unleashing STAT3-associated survival and inflammatory programs. For example, in colitis-associated CRC, loss of SHP2 in the intestinal epithelium accelerates tumor formation [15]. In HCC, hepatocyte-specific Ptpn11 deletion promotes inflammation-associated tumor development, accompanied by enhanced STAT3-related inflammatory signaling and genomic instability [43]. Similarly, in GC, SHP2 depletion under IL-6/gp130-driven inflammatory signaling leads to excessive STAT3 activation and promotes tumorigenesis [42]. Beyond inflammation-driven contexts, other genetic backgrounds, such as p53-deficient PDAC, may shift the signaling balance toward a dominant JAK2–STAT3 axis, thereby weakening tumor dependency on SHP2 and limiting the efficacy of its targeted inhibition [48]. Collectively, these findings emphasize that inappropriate SHP2 inhibition may relieve its intrinsic pathway-restraining functions and lead to unfavorable tumor-biological consequences, underscoring the necessity of identifying the functional context of SHP2 before applying such therapies.

Another important translational risk lies in the cell type-specific effects of SHP2 inhibition within the TME. Systemic SHP2 inhibitors do not selectively act on malignant epithelial cells, but may also modulate T cells, macrophages, endothelial cells, and stromal compartments. However, the biological consequences of SHP2 inhibition may differ substantially across these cellular compartments, and some macrophage-lineage populations may show unfavorable consequences after SHP2 inhibition or loss. For example, SHP2 loss in macrophage subsets has been linked to pro-angiogenic remodeling and enhanced CRC liver metastasis [66], while SHP2 depletion in Kupffer cells can disrupt liver immune homeostasis and promote HCC progression [67]. These findings suggest that inappropriate systemic SHP2 inhibition may unintentionally remodel the TME in a tumor-promoting direction. Therefore, future studies should incorporate tumor type-specific and cell type-specific analyses to define when SHP2 inhibition is beneficial and when it may be ineffective or potentially harmful.

Overall, patient selection for SHP2-targeted therapy should integrate genetic background, pathway dependency, inflammatory and STAT3 signaling status, tumor immunophenotype, and cell type-specific SHP2 functions within the TME. However, clinically validated predictive biomarkers remain lacking. Future clinical trials should incorporate biomarker-driven stratification, paired tumor biopsies, immune profiling, and pharmacodynamic monitoring to identify patients most likely to benefit while avoiding contexts in which SHP2 inhibition may be ineffective or potentially harmful.

Limitations of current evidence

Despite growing interest in targeting SHP2 in GI cancers, several limitations of the current evidence should be acknowledged. First, much of the mechanistic insight into SHP2 function is derived from preclinical studies, and robust clinical validation in GI cancer patients remains limited. Second, substantial heterogeneity exists across different GI cancer types regarding SHP2 genetic alterations, activation status, and downstream signaling dependencies, which complicates the generalization of findings. Moreover, the compartment-specific and context-dependent functions of SHP2 introduce additional biological complexity, posing challenges for effective and safe therapeutic translation.

Conclusions

As a key intersection between tumor-intrinsic signaling and the immune microenvironment, SHP2 is emerging as an important therapeutic target in GI cancers. This review summarizes the multifaceted and context-dependent roles of SHP2 in GI cancers. It examines the molecular basis of SHP2 as a signaling hub, its dual roles in tumorigenesis and immune regulation, and its therapeutic potential for overcoming resistance to radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy.

SHP2 exerts context-dependent dual roles in cancer. By regulating RAS–MAPK and PI3K–AKT signaling and shaping T cells, NK cells, TAMs, and vascular/stromal compartments, SHP2 drives tumor progression and drug resistance yet can also support immune homeostasis under specific conditions. This duality is stage- and cell type–dependent: during early inflammation-driven tumor initiation (e.g., colitis-associated cancer and diethylnitrosamine-induced HCC), SHP2 is tumor suppressive by restraining pro-inflammatory STAT3 signaling, whereas in established tumors it becomes oncogenic by sustaining RTK–RAS–MAPK signaling. Similarly, SHP2 promotes tumorigenesis and immunosuppression in tumor cells and TAMs, but its loss in tissue-resident macrophages (e.g., Kupffer cells) or inflamed epithelial cells can paradoxically amplify pro-tumorigenic inflammation and angiogenesis. Overall, the effects of SHP2 modulation are determined by cellular context, inflammatory milieu, and tumor stage, underscoring the need for context-aware therapeutic strategies. This intrinsic paradox underlies both the therapeutic promise and the inherent challenges of SHP2 targeting. SHP2 inhibitors offer a new approach to overcoming resistance to current treatment modalities, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. We further emphasize that the clinical benefits of SHP2 inhibition are not guaranteed, but depend on precise patient selection, rational combination regimens, and a deeper understanding of compensatory pathway activation and immune adaptation. This review focuses on major GI cancers such as CRC, GC, HCC and PDAC, integrating basic research, animal models and early clinical evidence, providing a more mechanistically in-depth and clinically oriented understanding framework for SHP2 as a therapeutic target and immune regulatory lever.

Most current studies have overlooked the dynamic regulation of SHP2 itself by therapeutic approaches and focused on the downstream effects of SHP2. Systematically exploring how chemotherapy, radiotherapy or targeted therapy dynamically regulates the expression, subcellular localization and post-translational modification of SHP2 is crucial for clarifying mechanisms of therapeutic resistance and optimizing combination treatment strategies. At the same time, we emphasize that the future therapeutic strategies for SHP2 must be context-dependent and precisely targeted.

At present, SHP2 inhibitors have shown significant potential in combination immunotherapy, in combination with KRAS–MAPK pathway inhibition, and in reversing treatment resistance. However, the durability of efficacy, safety, and appropriate patient populations still require further clinical validation. Future research urgently needs to focus on patient stratification guided by biomarkers, dynamic immune profiling, and new-generation precise regulatory strategies to better define the true therapeutic window of SHP2-targeted therapy and address the complex environmental dependence of SHP2.

In conclusion, this review summarizes the core biological functions and therapeutic potential of SHP2 in GI cancers and further highlights its roles in combination therapy, overcoming therapeutic resistance, and regulating the immune microenvironment, with the aim of promoting more precise and effective SHP2-related research and treatment strategies.
