Amelioration of neurogenic erectile dysfunction by ST36 electroacupuncture
Authors: Weipeng Zhang, Wende Yang, Yuxuan Zhang, Kunjing Wu, Zhenkang Liang, Chaowei Zhang, Kangping Sun, Yixi Liu, Minxian Duan, Hongbo Wei, Zongheng Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCavernous nerve (CN) injury-induced neurogenic erectile dysfunction (ED) remains clinically intractable, mainly driven by post-injury cavernous autonomic imbalance and subsequent pathological remodeling. In a bilateral cavernous nerve injury (BCNI) rat model, we detected early sustained sympathetic hyperactivity and parasympathetic suppression in the corpus cavernosum, which significantly correlated with fibrotic progression. Electroacupuncture (EA) at Zusanli (ST36), a non-invasive neuromodulation, effectively reversed this autonomic imbalance, achieving significant erectile function recovery and attenuated penile pathological remodeling. Proteomics showed that EA-ST36 markedly upregulated cavernous L-arginine biosynthesis, with key enzyme argininosuccinate synthase 1 (ASS1) enriched in cavernous endothelial cells. α-Methyl-DL-aspartate (α-MDLA) inhibition of this pathway abolished EA’s protective effects, which were rescued by arginine supplementation, confirming that EA acts via the L-arginine/nitric oxide (NO) signaling. Collectively, non-invasive ST36 EA is a promising strategy for penile rehabilitation in neurogenic ED patients.Subject areas: Health sciences, Biological sciences, Neuroscience, Clinical neuroscience, Sensory neuroscience

Introduction

Pelvic floor organ surgeries carry a high risk of postoperative neurogenic erectile dysfunction (ED), with incidence rates ranging from 50% to 90%, primarily due to inevitable intraoperative injury to the cavernous nerve (CN)—a branch of the pelvic autonomic nerves.1 This complication not only inflicts profound psychological distress on patients and their partners but also strains family relationships due to the challenges in treatment. Furthermore, it exacerbates the burden on healthcare systems globally, making it a significant public health challenge worldwide.2

Clinical observations indicate that some patients with CN injury may regain erectile responses during later stages, yet fail to achieve functional erections sufficient for intercourse.3 Similarly, in a rat bilateral CN crush injury model, a subset of rats exhibited spontaneous erectile responses by day 28 post-injury, with ultrastructural examination revealing signs of CN regeneration at this time point.4 This phenomenon suggests the potential existence of endogenous recovery mechanisms that regulate post-injury nerve regeneration and functional compensation. Mounting evidence confirms that functional preservation of penile corporal tissue significantly improves neurogenic ED, while also aiding in the structural integrity of both distal corpora cavernosa and proximal pelvic ganglia segments of the CNs.5,6,7

The sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems mediate vasoconstriction and vasodilation; during penile erection, their dynamic interplay is crucial for achieving functional erections.8 Excessive sympathetic activation or impaired parasympathetic signaling disrupts this balance, leading to sustained contraction of cavernous smooth muscle and compromised local blood flow. This state activates inflammatory factors and fibroblasts, promoting collagen deposition and extracellular matrix remodeling, thereby severely impairing corporal tissue function.9 Research demonstrates that bilateral CN injury (BCNI) in rats induces hyperactivation of sympathetic activity, a key driver in the progression toward irreversible neurogenic ED.10 Persistent neuro-functional imbalance not only disrupts cavernosal microcirculation but also exacerbates fibrosis and vascular endothelial damage, ultimately triggering structural pathological remodeling of the penile corpus cavernosum. Consequently, sympathetic/parasympathetic imbalance is now recognized as a pivotal mechanism mediating pathological corporal remodeling. However, the precise characteristics and regulatory mechanisms governing post-CN injury changes in sympathetic and parasympathetic function within the penile corpora remain incompletely understood and warrant further investigation.

Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) demonstrates therapeutic potential for conditions including epilepsy, stroke, and peripheral nerve injury, primarily by activating the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway and promoting neural repair.11,12,13,14,15 However, the clinical utility of traditional invasive VNS is constrained by surgical trauma and inherent infection risks. In contrast, electroacupuncture (EA) emerges as a key non-invasive neuromodulatory approach. By delivering electrical stimulation to specific acupoints, EA activates relevant neural pathways and serves as a pivotal technique for non-invasive parasympathetic nervous system modulation.16,17,18,19

Zusanli (ST36), a core acupoint of the Stomach Meridian of Foot-Yangming, could enhance parasympathetic efferent activity through stimulation-induced activation of dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus (DMV) projections.20 Anatomically, the deep fascia at ST36 is innervated by specific sensory neurons expressing PROKR2. This group of neurons drives the vagus nerve-mediated anti-inflammatory response through the spinal cord-nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS)-DMV nerve pathway.21,22 In contrast, regions such as the abdomen lack such fiber distribution. Functional magnetic resonance imaging studies confirm that EA at ST36 specifically upregulates neuronal activity within the brainstem nucleus tractus solitarius and DMV. This activation subsequently suppresses systemic inflammatory responses and promotes tissue repair via cholinergic pathways.23 This non-invasive method of parasympathetic activation offers a potentially unique intervention strategy for autonomic rebalancing in neurogenic ED. Research demonstrates that ST36 EA specifically activates parasympathetic branches, eliciting physiological effects analogous to VNS and playing a significant role in organ protection.24 While prior studies indicate an association between acupuncture stimulation and ED modulation, whether ST36 EA can reverse the sympathetic-parasympathetic functional imbalance within the corpus cavernosum in neurogenic ED, and its specific role in erectile function rehabilitation, remains to be elucidated.

Therefore, this study aims to systematically investigate the role of sympathetic-parasympathetic imbalance following CN injury in the pathological remodeling of the corpus cavernosum and to elucidate the regulatory effects and underlying molecular mechanisms of non-invasive ST36 EA intervention on this imbalance. In this study, we investigated whether sympathetic-parasympathetic imbalance modulates the progression of neurogenic ED, particularly its role in mediating pathological remodeling of the corpus cavernosum. To achieve this, we established a rat model of BCNI. Our results show that CN injury in the early stages induces sympathetic-parasympathetic imbalance. This imbalance is closely associated with the progression of pathological remodeling in the corpus cavernosum. Furthermore, using the in vivo model, we found that EA at ST-36 effectively activates parasympathetic function within the corpus cavernosum and antagonizes its pathological remodeling. Further proteomic analysis revealed that ST-36 EA primarily exerts its therapeutic effects by enhancing the L-arginine biosynthesis pathway in corpus cavernosum endothelial cells. Importantly, we discovered that combining ST-36 EA with inhibition of the L-arginine biosynthesis pathway significantly aggravated ED in BCNI rats, and this aggravation could be reversed by an L-arginine-enriched diet. These findings may provide important insights for future treatments, which could prove beneficial for patients with neurogenic ED.

Results

Imbalance of autonomic nerve function activity in the corpus cavernosum of BCNI rats and its correlation with pathological remodeling of penis

To characterize changes in autonomic nerve function following BCNI in rats, we assessed parasympathetic activity using c-Fos, a marker of neuronal activity, and the core parasympathetic marker protein CHAT. We observed a significant decrease in cavernous parasympathetic activity (CHAT+/c-Fos+) in BCNI rats by postoperative day 3, followed by a progressive decline over time. Conversely, assessment of sympathetic activity using c-Fos and the core sympathetic marker protein tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) revealed a progressive increase starting from day 3 post-surgery (Figures 1A–1F). These findings indicate a rapid onset and persistent progression of autonomic imbalance during the pathophysiology of neurogenic ED in BCNI rats. Corpus cavernosum fibrosis (CCF) represents a core pathophysiological change in the pathological remodeling of penile tissue associated with neurogenic ED. To investigate the relationship between CCF and cavernous autonomic imbalance, we evaluated penile structural changes using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and assessed CCF using Masson’s trichrome staining. Results demonstrated that collagen fiber deposition was evident by postoperative day 7 and progressively worsened thereafter (Figures 1G–1I), confirming a clear time-dependent progression of the fibrotic cavernous response. To preliminarily investigate whether autonomic imbalance contributes to pathological remodeling of the corpus cavernosum after BCNI, we performed correlation analyses. These analyses revealed a positive correlation between the degree of sympathetic hyperactivity and CCF, while parasympathetic activity showed a negative correlation with CCF (Figures 1J and 1K). These findings suggest that the early-onset autonomic imbalance, characterized by sympathetic hyperactivity and parasympathetic suppression, likely constitutes the initiating factor inducing pathological remodeling of the corpus cavernosum in neurogenic ED.

EA at ST36 demonstrably reverses corpus cavernosum autonomic imbalance and promotes erectile function recovery

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of ST36 stimulation for autonomic imbalance in the corpus cavernosum and its protective effects against neurogenic ED in BCNI rats, EA stimulation was applied to the ST36 acupoint (EA-ST36) starting on postoperative day 3. The specific stimulation parameters and intervention protocol are detailed in Figure 2A. Results demonstrated that EA-ST36 treatment effectively ameliorated the autonomic imbalance in the corpus cavernosum of BCNI rats by 2 weeks post-intervention, accompanied by a significant increase in parasympathetic activity (Figures 2B–2D). Erectile function was assessed by measuring intracavernosal pressure (ICP) changes in response to electrical stimulation of the proximal CN, using mean arterial pressure (MAP) as the baseline blood pressure reference (Figure 2E). Results revealed that rats receiving EA at ST-36 exhibited significantly improved erectile function compared to those receiving stimulation 1 cm lateral to ST36 (Figures 2F–2H). These findings suggest that precise EA stimulation at the ST36 acupoint effectively reverses autonomic imbalance in the corpus cavernosum of BCNI rats and subsequently promotes recovery of erectile function in neurogenic ED.

EA at ST36 effectively reverses pathological remodeling in the corpus cavernosum of BCNI rats

Masson’s trichrome and H&E staining confirmed improved fibrosis deposition in the corpus cavernosum of EA-ST36-treated rats following BCNI. In contrast, consistent with the erectile function results, stimulation 1 cm lateral to ST-36 (BCNI) did not improve collagen deposition (Figures 3A, 3B, and 3D). TUNEL assay results demonstrated a significant reduction in positive staining within the corpus cavernosum of the EA-ST36 group compared to the lateral stimulation group, indicating decreased pathological apoptosis (Figures 3C and 3E). Fluorescence double-staining experiments further revealed significantly enhanced preservation of smooth muscle components in the EA-ST36 group. Treated rats exhibited more pronounced co-localization of α-SMA, a smooth muscle marker, and Bax, an anti-apoptotic marker within the corpus cavernosum (Figures 3F–3H). These findings suggest that the reversal of autonomic imbalance by EA-ST36 exerts its therapeutic effect on neurogenic ED, at least partially, by mitigating or even reversing pathological remodeling of the corpus cavernosum.

EA at ST36 induces upregulation of arginine biosynthesis in the corpus cavernosum

To further elucidate the underlying mechanisms of ST36 EA in treating neurogenic ED, we employed proteomic sequencing to identify alterations within the corpus cavernosum (Figure 4A). Analysis revealed significant differences in protein expression profiles between the corpus cavernosum of EA-ST36-treated rats and the control group (Figure 4B). Gene Ontology enrichment analysis demonstrated that differentially expressed genes were enriched in biological processes related to response to endogenous stimulus and regeneration, suggesting their potential involvement in adaptive responses to endogenous metabolites (Figure 4C). Pathway enrichment analysis indicated significant alterations in pathways closely associated with arginine biosynthesis in the EA-ST36 group compared to control (Figure 4D). These findings suggest that EA at ST-36 induces metabolic remodeling within the corpus cavernosum. Importantly, the upregulation of arginine biosynthesis may constitute a key alteration in this process.

EA at ST36 reverses autonomic imbalance and promotes erectile function recovery in neurogenic ED rats via upregulation of the arginine biosynthesis pathway

The arginine biosynthesis pathway is a critical metabolic route for nitric oxide (NO) production (Figure 5A), and NO is the central mediator in the penile erection pathway. To investigate whether EA at ST36 promotes erectile recovery in neurogenic ED by upregulating arginine biosynthesis, we employed α-methyl-DL-aspartate (α-MDLA), an inhibitor of citrulline arginine metabolism, and supplemental arginine in drinking water arginine-enriched diet for further validation, as outlined in Figure 5B. Results showed that ASS1, a key marker protein of the arginine biosynthesis pathway, was downregulated in the model group but significantly elevated in the EA-ST36 group, consistent with the proteomic findings. α-MDLA intervention markedly suppressed ASS1 expression, while this suppression was reversed by the arginine-enriched diet (Figures 5C–5E, 5G, and 5H), confirming effective modulation by the interventions. Tissue NO content measurements further corroborated the activation and inhibition states of the arginine biosynthesis pathway (Figure 5D). Immunofluorescence double staining revealed that ASS1 co-localized with cavernous endothelial cells, and its expression changes paralleled those of the endothelial marker, suggesting endothelial NO synthase activity as a key mediator of EA-ST36’s effect. Interestingly, neither α-MDLA nor the arginine-enriched diet affected the EA-ST36-induced activation of parasympathetic nerve activity. However, erectile functional recovery was inhibited in the α-MDLA group, and this inhibition was reversed by the arginine-enriched diet (Figures 5M and 5O). These results strongly suggest that the mechanism by which EA at ST-36 reverses sympathetic imbalance in the corpus cavernosum and promotes erectile recovery in neurogenic ED is at least partially mediated by the upregulation of the arginine-NO biosynthesis pathway.

EA at ST36 reverses autonomic imbalance and alleviates pathological remodeling in the corpus cavernosum via upregulation of arginine biosynthesis

Masson’s trichrome staining confirmed improved fibrosis deposition in the EA-ST36 group. This protective effect was abolished by α-MDLA intervention but reversed with arginine-enriched dietary supplementation (Figures 6A and 6B). Fluorescence double staining demonstrated significantly enhanced anti-apoptotic capacity in the smooth muscle components of the EA-ST36 group compared to controls. Similarly, the protective effect of EA-ST36 on cavernous smooth muscle was lost following α-MDLA administration but restored by arginine enrichment (Figures 6C and 6D). Structurally intact and functionally competent corpus cavernosum is essential for successful CN regeneration. Fluorescence staining for neuronal NO synthase nNOS, a CN marker protein, revealed effective preservation of nNOS expression in both the corpus cavernosum and proximal ganglia following EA-ST36. This protection was significantly diminished by α-MDLA and subsequently rescued by arginine supplementation (Figures 6E–6G). These findings collectively validate that EA at ST36 alleviates pathological remodeling in the corpus cavernosum by reversing sympathetic imbalance through upregulation of the arginine biosynthesis pathway.

Discussion

Preserving the functional integrity of the corpora cavernosa is paramount for ensuring the functional regeneration of the CNs.25 Given the suboptimal rate of peripheral nerve regeneration, preventing pathological remodeling of penile cavernosal tissue following denervation is critical for restoring erectile function in neurogenic ED. Physiological penile erection involves precise neuro-orchestration by sympathetic and parasympathetic pathways. After CN injury, compensatory hyperactivation of sympathetic tone may serve as the triggering factor for pathological cavernosal remodeling.26 This study aims to develop a non-invasive neuromodulation strategy targeting the reversal of autonomic imbalance within the corpora cavernosa, thereby preserving tissue functionality and promoting functional recovery of erection.

The role of autonomic nervous system dysfunction in disease progression is increasingly recognized. Studies indicate that environmentally induced benign stress promotes liver regeneration via the sympathetic nerve/ILC1/IL-22 axis.27 Alcohol abuse triggers hepatic steatosis and injury in mice by activating the sympathetic nerve-Adrb3-mediated lipolysis pathway in adipose tissue. Conversely, ablation of sympathetic nerves through systemic or local injection of 6-hydroxydopamine significantly attenuates acute alcohol-induced adipose tissue lipolysis.28 Research by Fnu et al. demonstrates that sympathetic nerve ablation or inhibition of ADRB2 β2-adrenergic receptor delays disease progression and prolongs survival in a small-cell lung cancer mouse model.29 Similarly, activation of the vagus nerve increases acetylcholine release from hepatic vagal terminals, activating the IL-6 signaling pathway in liver macrophages to ultimately promote hepatocyte proliferation and accelerate liver regeneration.30 This compellingly suggests that specific activation states of the autonomic nervous system exert divergent or opposing effects in different disease contexts.

Previous studies have confirmed that in rat CNI models, the expression of TH, a key marker of sympathetic nerve activity, is significantly upregulated.31 Further studies in rat BCNI models have clearly demonstrated that CN injury induces sustained TH upregulation and marked downregulation of parasympathetic nerve markers in cavernosal tissue, forming a stable autonomic imbalance phenotype that directly drives ED progression.32 This pathological mechanism has been validated in clinical settings: CN injury from radical prostatectomy leads to sympathetic hyperinnervation and autonomic dysfunction in the human corpus cavernosum, which is a key inducer of postoperative ED.33 Accumulating clinical evidence has confirmed that standardized postoperative rehabilitation interventions can significantly improve erectile function outcomes in patients undergoing pelvic surgery with CN injury, highlighting the clinical value of exploring the underlying mechanism of rehabilitative strategies to optimize treatment protocols.34 Increased TH-positive sympathetic nerve activity within the corpus cavernosum may lead to abnormal contraction of penile smooth muscle. This can trigger a cascade of pathological reactions, including subsequent hypoxia and oxidative stress within the cavernous tissue, ultimately resulting in abnormal sexual behavior. Notably, the application of a specific monoclonal antibody against the tyrosine kinase receptor A (TrkA-mAb) significantly suppressed sympathetic nerve activity. Consequently, this treatment effectively promoted CN regeneration and facilitated the recovery of erectile function,31 indicating that sympathetic inhibition indeed exerts a protective effect in neurogenic ED. However, the risk of multi-organ systemic effects associated with the systemic administration of TrkA-mAb remains a concern. Emerging clinical evidence has suggested the potential of acupuncture as an alternative therapy for ED, with some studies reporting improvements in IIEF scores.35 However, the underlying neural mechanisms, particularly how acupuncture modulates the autonomic balance within the target organ, remain largely unexplored. Several critical scientific questions still require in-depth investigation: the specific characteristics of autonomic nerve changes within the corpus cavernosum in neurogenic ED, the optimal intervention window for activating the parasympathetic nervous system, the potential for non-invasive approaches to activate parasympathetic activity in the corpus cavernosum, and the underlying mechanisms by which suppression of sympathetic nerve activity mediates protection of erectile function.

In this study, we observed autonomic dysfunction within the target organ, the corpus cavernosum, in a BCNI rat model. This dysfunction manifested as sustained sympathetic overactivity coupled with persistent suppression of parasympathetic activity, mirroring the loss of sustained regulatory control over target organs observed after spinal cord injury (SCI). However, the phenomenon of neural plasticity offers a potential avenue for modulating autonomic function, which may thereby establish a foundation for therapeutic intervention. Post-SCI, acute intermittent hypoxia-induced long-term facilitation has been shown to ameliorate injury-induced sympathetic hypoactivity.36 Similarly, magnetic stimulation of the vagus nerve, enabling precise vagal modulation, has demonstrated significant therapeutic potential in myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury.37 In our current investigation, applying specific electrical stimulation parameters to the classic ST36 Zusanli acupoint, based on traditional Chinese acupoint theory, we demonstrate effective reversal of BCNI-induced cavernous autonomic dysfunction. Crucially, the successful activation of parasympathetic function promoted the recovery of erectile function in model rats. Indeed, prior research has established that EA at specific acupoints can effectively activate parasympathetic pathways, potentially improving overall health status. For instance, EA at ST36 with a 0.5 mA current activates the vagal pathway, improving gastrointestinal motility in rats with diabetic gastroparesis.38 Recent studies further indicate that ST36 EA rapidly activates cholinergic neurons in the DMV, leading to increased acetylcholine release from hepatic vagal terminals.30 This subsequently activates the IL-6 signaling pathway in liver macrophages, ultimately promoting hepatocyte proliferation and accelerating liver regeneration. Our findings corroborate that ST36 EA effectively activates systemic parasympathetic function. Importantly, we demonstrate its feasibility for targeted modulation within penile cavernous tissue. More significantly, the reversal of autonomic imbalance effectively preserved cavernous tissue function. Our study revealed a significant amelioration of pathological cavernous remodeling, primarily characterized by cavernous smooth muscle cell (CSMC) apoptosis and diffuse fibrotic deposition. Multiple prior studies have established that early-onset cavernous fibrosis is a key factor contributing to the refractory nature of neurogenic ED, not only due to irreversible pathological changes but also through the inhibition of retrograde neurotrophic support within the corpus cavernosum.39 Classic studies have well established that the NO/cyclic guanosine monophosphate (NO/cGMP) signaling pathway is the core anti-fibrotic regulatory axis after CN injury, and activation of this pathway significantly inhibits cavernosal smooth muscle apoptosis, collagen deposition, and fibrotic remodeling, thereby preserving the functional integrity of the corpus cavernosum.40,41 Therefore, the demonstrated efficacy and safety of non-invasively reversing the underlying autonomic imbalance via acupoint electrostimulation, without pharmacological intervention, presents a promising non-pharmacological strategy for the future treatment of neurogenic ED.

The ST36 EA protocol from this study has a solid feasibility and safety profile for human clinical translation. Based on relevant clinical trial data, the optimized clinical adaptation is as follows: for stimulation parameters, following the rodent-to-human electrical stimulation conversion principle, we adopt a 2 Hz dense-dispersed wave, 1–3 mA intensity (adjusted to induce local soreness, numbness, and distension without pain), 30 min per session, consistent with the core parameters of this study.42 For the intervention schedule, treatment for patients with neurogenic ED is initiated in the acute phase after etiological management, at once daily, 6 days per week for a 4-week standard course,43 matching the intervention cycle of our study. This protocol has proven safe and effective in multiple human clinical trials, offering a clear roadmap for clinical implementation of our research findings.

Metabolic pathways within tissues play a crucial role in regulating tissue and organ function. The arginine biosynthetic pathway is pivotal in the urea cycle, polyamine synthesis, and NO generation by maintaining nitrogen balance and producing key bioactive molecules. Studies indicate that rhubarb extract alleviates neuronal damage in Alzheimer’s disease by inhibiting the glutamine-aspartate-arginine-NO metabolic axis,7 thereby suppressing neuroinflammation and neuronal injury. Dietary L-arginine supplementation has been shown to prevent bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis, with the mechanism potentially attributable to increased NO production.44 Penile erection relies on the formation of large sinusoidal spaces filled with blood, where endothelial cells are interwoven with CSMCs. During sexual arousal, NO released from the cavernous endothelium and nerves stimulates CSMC relaxation, leading to massive blood inflow into the sinusoidal spaces and subsequent erection. In this study, autonomic imbalance was already evident in the rat corpus cavernosum by postoperative day 3 following BCNI. Therefore, initiating ST-36 EA stimulation on day 3 appears physiologically justified. Our findings reveal that ST-36 EA upregulated the arginine-NO metabolic pathway in cavernous tissue. Notably, ASS1, a key enzyme in arginine synthesis, showed high co-localization with cavernous endothelial cells, suggesting that endothelial NO synthesis may be a critical mediator of ST-36’s effects. Crucially, pharmacological inhibition of the arginine synthesis pathway abolished the protective effect of ST-36 EA on erectile function, even when parasympathetic activity remained elevated. This aligns with prior research demonstrating that manual acupuncture at ST-36 activates NOS, promotes NO release, and induces peripheral vasodilation effects; however, NO synthase inhibitors block the hypotensive effect.45 These results collectively suggest that the enhanced parasympathetic activity induced by ST-36 EA is mediated, at least partially, through activation of the arginine-NO pathway. Indeed, earlier studies established that sympathetic overactivation causes nitrergic nerve dysfunction, leading to impaired neurogenic NO signaling and ED.46 Thus, our work further demonstrates that ST-36-induced autonomic remodeling protects erectile function largely through activating downstream NO signaling pathways. Future studies should investigate the precise mechanisms by which parasympathetic activity regulates the arginine biosynthetic pathway.

In summary, this study demonstrates that autonomic imbalance, characterized by reduced parasympathetic activity and upregulated sympathetic activity, is a key factor driving pathological remodeling of the corpora cavernosa following CN injury. ST-36 electrostimulation effectively reversed pathological cavernosal remodeling and improved ED. This therapeutic effect is partially mediated through activation of parasympathetic signaling and upregulation of the endothelial arginine-NO synthesis pathway within the corpora cavernosa, as illustrated in Figure 7. Consequently, targeting the restoration of autonomic balance in penile tissue represents a viable therapeutic strategy for neurogenic ED. ST-36 electrostimulation may thus serve as a promising approach for preventing and treating ED resulting from CN injury.

Electroacupuncture at ST36 exerts a rehabilitative effect on ED by regulating autonomic nerve function. Limitations of the study

A core limitation of this study is the use of young adult Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats aged 8 to 10 weeks. While 8- to 10-week-old male SD rats are the most widely used adult model in BCNI/CNI-related studies, with fully developed erectile function, stable endocrine status, and a stable ED phenotype after modeling, they avoid interference from pubertal or aging factors and provide a stable dynamic window for intervention effect observation.47,48 Another limitation is that the sham operation group was only used for validation of successful model establishment, without full-dimensional parallel detection of pathological indicators consistent with the EA group. Therefore, we cannot fully evaluate the complete protective effect of EA intervention on cavernous tissue, and relevant systematic verification will be conducted in our follow-up studies. While the present findings are based on current data and analytical methods, future validation is required to account for potential outliers and individual variations. This study primarily focused on assessing the early protective effects of ST-36 electrostimulation on cavernous tissue preservation and erectile function recovery. Although prior research has established the importance of early intervention for functional erectile rehabilitation, whether long-term functional preservation can be sustained warrants further investigation. Although ST-36 served as the primary acupoint in this work, previous studies have reported that CV4 Guanyuan and KI1 Yongquan also reduce sympathetic nerve activity.49,50 Future studies should therefore explore whether these acupoints confer protective benefits against neurogenic ED and whether coordinated stimulation yields superior therapeutic efficacy. Future work will explore the protective effects of these acupoints on neurogenic ED and the superior efficacy of coordinated stimulation. Additionally, as we did not measure eNOS phosphorylation and its downstream metabolite L-citrulline here, our findings only offer indirect evidence for NO-related pathway involvement, without direct molecular mechanism validation, which will be the core focus of our subsequent mechanistic studies.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal models

Male Sprague-Dawley SD rats aged 8–10 weeks were procured from Rigor Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Guangzhou, Guangdong, China. All animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, ambient temperature maintained at 24 ± 2°C, humidity controlled between 30% and 70%, and provided ad libitum access to food and water. The experimental protocol was approved by the Seyotin Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee Approval (No. SYT2023004), which strictly adheres to the National Institutes of Health guide for the care and use of Laboratory animals (NIH Publications No. 8023, revised 1978). Neurogenic Erectile Dysfunction Model Rats were established via bilateral cavernous nerve injury (BCNI), following established methodology.51 The procedure was performed as follows: Rats were anesthetized with 5% sodium pentobarbital. The bladder and prostate were carefully exposed. The major pelvic ganglion (MPG), appearing as a white multi-branched structure dorsal to the prostate, was identified. The cavernous nerve (CN), the largest nerve branch extending from the MPG to the corpora cavernosa was isolated. Using Stortz hemostatic forceps with serrated jaws, the CN was clamped 5 mm distal to the MPG for 2 minutes. Identical crush injuries were applied to both left and right CNs to complete BCNI modeling. The abdominal incision was sutured, disinfected, and rats were placed on thermal pads until full recovery, this study established 4 independent experimental cohorts with no cross-cohort reuse of animals, with the detailed allocation as follows: the time-course cohort (corresponding to Figure 1) included 36 rats, which were randomly divided into the Sham-operated group and bilateral cavernous nerve injury (BCNI) model group and further subdivided into subgroups at 3, 7, and 14 days post-operation with 6 rats per subgroup, and all rats were euthanized for tissue harvesting at the corresponding time points; the intervention efficacy validation cohort (corresponding to Figures 2 and 3) included 18 rats, which were randomly allocated to 3 groups (6 rats per group): the Sham group, BCNI model group, and ST36 electroacupuncture group, and all rats underwent erectile function assessment followed by euthanasia and tissue harvesting for subsequent histological and molecular biological assays; the proteomics validation cohort (corresponding to Figure 4) included 12 rats, which were randomly allocated to 2 groups (6 rats per group): the BCNI model group and ST36 electroacupuncture group, and tissues were harvested after euthanasia for proteomics detection; the mechanistic validation cohort (corresponding to Figures 5 and 6) included 24 rats, which were randomly allocated to 4 groups (6 rats per group): the BCNI model group, ST36 electroacupuncture group, ST36 electroacupuncture + α-MDLA group, and ST36 electroacupuncture + α-MDLA + L-arginine group, and all rats underwent erectile function assessment followed by euthanasia and tissue harvesting for subsequent relevant assays, After euthanasia, the glans penis and modeling-induced injured corpus cavernosum segment (25-gauge needle) were completely resected under a stereomicroscope, only the intact uninjured main corpus cavernosum segment was retained and longitudinally equally divided into two segments: the distal 1/2 for 4% paraformaldehyde fixation, paraffin embedding and Masson’s trichrome staining, the proximal 1/2 snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and further equally split into two sub-segments for frozen section histology, nitric oxide assay and tissue homogenate proteomic analysis respectively, with no reuse of the same sample for multiple assays, and this protocol was pre-experimentally validated to meet the sample requirements of all assays.with no repeated sampling or testing performed on any rats included in the study.

Method details

Electroacupuncture (EA) stimulation at ST-36

Three days after establishing the BCNI model, rats were randomly divided into two groups n=6 per group: EA-ST36 group: Received electroacupuncture stimulation at bilateral ST-36 Zusanli acupoints. BCNI group: Received electroacupuncture stimulation 1 cm lateral to ST-36 as a procedural control. An additional Sham group n=6, underwent surgical exposure of bilateral CN without injury, serving as the blank control. Intervention Protocol: Rats were anesthetized with 2.5–3% isoflurane. Bilateral hindlimbs were shaved, and rats were secured on a surgical platform. The ST36 acupoint was identified by palpating the fibular head landmark on the lateral calf and marked 2 mm distal to this point. Sterile acupuncture needles connected to a bipolar stimulator (Chengdu Taimeng Technology Co., China) were inserted at ST36. following established protocols,52,53 The EA stimulation parameters were set as: continuous wave, frequency 2 Hz,continuous wave., current intensity 1.5 mA, pulse width 0.5 ms, single stimulation duration 15 min per day, for 12 consecutive days from postoperative day 3 to day 14. Electrodes were spaced to prevent contact. All experimental endpoints were evaluated 24 hours after the final intervention i.e.,14 days post BCNI modeling, including in vivo erectile function testing and histological examination of harvested specimens.

Histological staining (Masson’s trichrome & H&E)

Penile tissue sections 5 μm thickness from paraffin-embedded samples were processed as follows: Mounted on glass slides, baked at 60°C for 1 hour; Immersed in xylene, graduated ethanol series: 99%, 95%, 70% each 5min. Staining Protocols: Masson’s Trichrome: Differentiated collagen blue, muscle red; H&E: Nuclear hematoxylin and cytoplasmic eosin staining.

Immunofluorescence staining

Penile tissue was harvested immediately after rat euthanasia, and isolation of the target tissue segment was completed within 10 minutes post-euthanasia. OCT embedding and snap-freezing in liquid nitrogen were finished within 20 minutes after tissue harvesting.Frozen tissue sections were processed as follows: Fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 minutes. Permeabilized with 0.1% TBST solution (Cat# G1204, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 5 minutes. Blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for 60 minutes. Incubated with respective primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Treated with corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies at room temperature for 60 minutes. Counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), 0.5μg/mL, for 10 minutes. Images were acquired using an LSM880 confocal microscope Zeiss, Germany and analyzed with ImageJ software. Antibody details used in this experiment are provided in key resources table.

Proteomic sequencing

Penile cavernous tissues from each group were obtained. The tissues were rapidly ground under liquid nitrogen to achieve thorough fragmentation. Lysis buffer was added to completely lyse and dissolve proteins. The mixture was centrifuged to remove insoluble impurities, and the protein-containing supernatant was collected. Protein quantification was performed using the BCA method. A reducing agent dithiothreitol was added to break disulfide bonds in proteins. An alkylating reagent iodoacetamide was added to block free sulfhydryl groups and prevent reformation of disulfide bonds. Protease was added to digest proteins into peptide mixtures. Solid-phase extraction columns were used to remove interfering substances from the digested mixtures. Purified peptide solutions were eluted and separated by liquid chromatography, followed by mass spectrometry analysis. Finally, bioinformatics analysis was conducted.The raw mass spectrometry proteomics data generated in this study have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the iProX partner repository under the accession number IPX0016677000.

Nitric oxide (NO) content detection

Penile tissues were stored at -80°C after collection. During retrieval, tissues were weighed on ice and homogenized in ice-cold phosphate buffer saline at a 1:10 w/v ratio. Homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C, and supernatants were collected for analysis. Total NO was measured using a commercial kit (Solarbio, BC1475) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were first reduced via the nitrate reductase method; nitrite levels were then detected using the Griess reaction. Absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader. A sodium nitrite standard curve was generated, and results were expressed as μmol/g wet tissue weight. All samples were analyzed with technical replicates n=2–3, and validated through at least three independent experiments to ensure reproducibility.

α-MDLA and arginine-enriched diet treatment

To evaluate the impact of ASS1 inhibition on neurogenic ED under ST36 electrostimulation, rats were orally administered the ASS1 inhibitor α-methyl-DL-aspartic acid (α-MDLA), (MCE, Cat# HY-W142119).According to the drug prescribing information and relevant literature,54,55 α-MDLA was dissolved in sterile saline and administered daily via oral gavage at 100 mg/kg from postoperative day 3 after BCNI modeling. Erectile function was assessed on day 14, followed by histological evaluation. To investigate the rescue effect of an arginine-enriched diet, rats receiving α-MDLA were simultaneously supplemented with 0.50% L-Arginine (MCE, Lot# HY-N0455) in drinking water. Control groups had ad libitum access to standard chow and supplemented tap water throughout the experiment. All interventions ceased 24 hours before terminal assessments i.e.,14 days post modeling, when erectile function tests and histological analyses were performed.

Erectile function assessment

After anesthetizing rats, the MPG and CN were re-exposed through the original incision. Bipolar stimulating electrodes probe size: 2mm, inter electrode distance:1mm were positioned on the CN. Stimulation parameters were set at: current 1.5 mA, frequency 20 Hz, pulse duration 0.2 ms, stimulation duration 50 s. A heparin-moistened 25G butterfly needle was inserted into the right corpus cavernosum or right penile artery. The needle was connected to a BL420N Bio Signal System (Taimeng Technology, Chengdu, China) to record intracavernosal pressure (ICP). A midline cervical incision exposed the right common carotid artery. A PE-50 catheter was inserted into the artery via a 25G butterfly needle and connected to a BL-420s Bio Signal System for mean arterial pressure (MAP) measurement. Erectile function was quantified by calculating: The ratio of maximum ICP (ICPmax) to mean MAP (MAPmean); The ratio of area under the ICP curve (AUC ICP) to AUC MAP during stimulation.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Experimental data were presented as mean±standard deviation (SD). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For all inter-group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple pairwise comparisons was used, and Levene’s test was performed prior to ANOVA to verify the homogeneity of variances. Linear regression and correlation between variables were assessed via two-tailed Pearson correlation analysis. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
