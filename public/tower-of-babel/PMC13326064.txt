Blood donor serosurveys and national dengue burden estimates in Argentina
Authors: Fernán González Bernaldo de Quirós, Alejandro Macchia
Published: 2026

Blood donor serosurveys can be valuable for haemovigilance. Flichman and colleagues report increasing anti-dengue virus (DENV) IgG seroprevalence in blood donors from three Argentine regions and detectable DENV RNA in donors during the 2022–23 outbreak.1 These findings support targeted assessment of transfusion risk. However, the use of these data to infer that approximately 10.2 million new infections occurred nationally raises important concerns about target population, sampling frame, and precision.

The study population consisted of eligible adult blood donors recruited from 12 urban and peri-urban donation centres.1 Such a frame may under- or overestimate population exposure, and the direction of bias cannot be assumed a priori. This uncertainty is precisely the problem. Without known selection probabilities, population calibration, or post-stratification to the national demographic and territorial structure, the estimate remains a donor-based sentinel estimate rather than a population estimate for Argentina.

The weighting strategy used by the authors does not resolve this problem. The authors reweighted 2025 donors to match the provincial composition of their 2023 donor sample. This approach improves comparability between survey periods, but it does not calibrate the sample to Argentina's population by age, sex, province, socioeconomic position, or probability of selection; calibration and post-stratification require external population margins or known selection structures aligned with the intended target population.2 Confidence intervals around national infection estimates may therefore convey a level of population precision that the design cannot support.

Buenos Aires illustrates the issue. Flichman and colleagues describe previous Argentine studies as local and fragmented. Yet one study in the City of Buenos Aires used a stratified, multistage, population-based household design, two sampling frames including informal settlements, Kish selection within households, and calibrated expansion weights.3 It estimated adult seroprevalence of 8.1% before the outbreak that began in December, 2023, and 18.6% after the 2023–24 outbreak, with an explicit denominator of 2.4 million adults.3 These estimates are local by jurisdiction, but population-based by design.

The conclusions should therefore be restricted to eligible adult blood donors from participating centres in regions with viral circulation, which is the population actually sampled. The data support increased DENV exposure among these donors and justify discussion of transfusion-safety measures during outbreaks. They do not, by themselves, support design-based national estimates of infection burden or precise national infection counts. Overstated national estimates are not a minor statistical matter. They can shape public perception of susceptibility, urgency, vaccine demand, procurement decisions, and the allocation of scarce public health resources. This concern is especially important when donor-based estimates are used to frame the need for national epidemic preparedness, including the assessment of vaccination strategies.

Argentina needs national dengue serosurveillance. That effort should integrate probability-based population surveys, sentinel haemovigilance, and transparent burden models while keeping their estimates distinct. This distinction affects estimates of susceptibility, force of infection, vaccine policy, and public risk communication.
