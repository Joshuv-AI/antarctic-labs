Ontology-driven dual-channel relational graph convolutional network (DCR-GCN) for lettuce leaf phenotype classification
Authors: Ting Li, Sheng Wu, Guangjie Qiu, Ying Zhang, Minggang Zhang, Congmin Wang, Decong Zheng, Xinyu Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLettuce (Lactuca sativa L.) is an important leafy vegetable with substantial diversity in leaf topology, geometry, color, and texture, which poses challenges for germplasm identification and new variety protection. However, current approaches to complex phenotypic analysis are often limited in their ability to explicitly represent and exploit semantic relationships among phenotypic traits. To address this limitation, a knowledge graph-enhanced graph learning framework for lettuce phenotypic traits was developed. Phenotypic traits were first extracted from leaf images of five lettuce types. Based on the trait description standards of the International Union for the Protection of New Varieties of Plants (UPOV), a Lettuce Leaf Phenotypic Trait Knowledge Graph (LLPT-KG) was constructed to represent semantic associations among traits. On this basis, a Dual-Channel Relational Graph Convolutional Network (DCR-GCN) was developed to jointly integrate node attribute features and graph structural information for lettuce type classification. To improve interpretability, node- and edge-level importance analyses were further performed to identify the phenotypic traits and semantic relations most relevant to type discrimination. The proposed framework achieved an accuracy of 0.94 and a Macro-F1 score of 0.94. Compared with the best-performing single-channel graph baseline, R-GCN (Relational Graph Convolutional Network), DCR-GCN improved accuracy by approximately 9% points and Macro-F1 by 10% points. These results demonstrate that combining knowledge graphs with graph neural networks can effectively capture complex phenotypic relationships in lettuce and improve classification performance. The proposed framework provides methodological support for precise lettuce germplasm identification, digital phenotypic evaluation for new variety protection, and digital-assisted pre-screening prior to field-based DUS (Distinctness, Uniformity, and Stability) testing.Keywords: Lettuce leaf, Graph neural networks, Phenotypic trait knowledge graph, Phenotypic identification, Digital DUS testing

Introduction

Lettuce (Lactuca sativa L.) is one of the most widely cultivated leafy vegetables worldwide and is of considerable economic and nutritional importance [1]. Over the past two decades, global production of lettuce (including chicory) has increased by nearly 60%, exceeding 29 million tons in 2019 and ranking third among leafy vegetable crops [2]. Through long-term domestication and breeding, lettuce has diversified into several cultivated types, including Butterhead, Romaine, Iceberg, Stem lettuce, and Loose-leaf lettuce [3]. Among these, lettuce leaf shows substantial morphological variation in traits such as color, shape, size, texture, leaf margin, and surface structure [4–6]. To ensure that newly developed varieties satisfy the requirements of distinctness, uniformity, and stability (DUS), breeders and certification authorities have established standardized DUS examination systems [7]. At present, DUS testing mainly relies on morphological descriptors. For instance, the UPOV and Japan’s Ministry of Agriculture have defined more than 60 appearance-related traits, including seed, seedling, plant diameter, leaf posture, number of leaf lobes, leaf shape, and leaf tip morphology [8, 9]. However, traditional manual measurements are labor-intensive and subjective, and they often fail to capture complex geometric and textural characteristics of leaves with sufficient precision. Recent advances in high-throughput phenotyping (HTP) [10, 11] and machine learning have greatly improved the quantitative analysis of lettuce traits. Du et al. developed an automated phenotyping workflow based on convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for semantic segmentation and extracted 266 features, including geometric, venation, and color traits, from single-leaf images [12]. 3D point cloud modeling techniques have also been used to quantify leaf blistering, textural irregularities, and local curvature, with measurements showing high agreement with manual assessment () [13]. In addition, deep learning models such as YOLO-VOLO-LS have been applied to identify lettuce varieties at the seedling stage, achieving a test accuracy of 93% [14]. Hierarchical clustering of morphological traits has likewise been shown to support cultivar discrimination; for example, Leschuk et al. analyzed 42 morphological traits and demonstrated that such data can effectively separate cabbage varieties and reduce redundancy in the identification of similar cultivars [15]. Zhao et al. further proposed the concept of a “lettuce phenotypic fingerprint,” showing that multidimensional phenotypic fusion can generate unique characteristic profiles for individual plants and support precise discrimination of variety types and superior genotypes [16]. Despite these advances, the large volume and heterogeneity of phenotypic data remain major obstacles to integrated analysis and higher-level biological interpretation.

As multi-omics and phenotypic datasets continue to expand, unified platforms for data organization and knowledge integration are becoming increasingly important. LettuceGDB, for example, integrates two reference genomes, resequencing data from 1048 accessions, and multi-omics and phenotypic information from more than 1300 germplasm resources worldwide, providing a valuable foundation for lettuce genetics and breeding research [2]. Meanwhile, knowledge graphs have emerged as an effective framework for integrating heterogeneous agricultural data through structured entity–relation representations [17]. In the food domain, they have been used for applications such as quality traceability [18] and recipe development [19], transforming fragmented multi-source data into organized and machine-readable knowledge. Similar approaches have also been adopted in agriculture. CropDP-KG, for example, integrates 13,840 entities and 21,961 relations extracted from image–text databases and supports unified management and visual query of crop pest and disease knowledge [20]. Pierre et al. developed AgroLD, an ontology-driven knowledge graph that enables semantic annotation, standardized mapping, and multi-source integration of plant molecular and phenotypic data [21]. At the same time, graph neural networks (GNNs) have become powerful tools for learning from graph-structured data and capturing higher-order relationships among biological entities [22]. In crop variety adaptability assessment, GNN-based models trained on graphs combining climate variables and trait data have outperformed conventional machine learning methods [23]. In maize yield prediction, bidirectional GNNs have been used to model associations among growing regions and trait features, enabling robust forecasting [24]. Chiranjeevi et al. applied graph-based modeling to classify plant leaf diseases [25]. Sajitha et al. further proposed a framework that converts image data into knowledge graphs and combines knowledge embeddings with graph convolutional networks, providing a promising strategy for structured fusion of multimodal plant phenotyping data [26].

Taken together, these studies show that lettuce phenotyping has progressed from qualitative description toward precise quantitative characterization. However, current approaches still lack an effective framework for unified representation of multidimensional phenotypic data and systematic modeling of relationships among traits. To address this gap, samples from five lettuce types were collected, and leaf-level phenotypic traits were extracted using two-dimensional image-based phenotyping. A Lettuce Leaf Phenotype Feature Knowledge Graph (LLPT-KG) was then constructed to integrate 13 continuous phenotypic indicators with discrete traits defined by UPOV guidelines. On this basis, a Dual-Channel Relational Graph Convolutional Network (DCR-GCN) was developed to predict lettuce sample types. Node- and edge-level importance analyses were further performed to quantify the contributions of individual phenotypic traits and their associations to classification performance, providing a new framework for DUS-oriented phenotypic evaluation.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

Lettuce materials with broad genetic diversity were used in this study. The experiment was conducted in a multi-span greenhouse at the Beijing Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences (39°46 N, 116°16 E) (Fig. 1(a)). The plant materials included five lettuce types—Butterhead, Iceberg, Loose-leaf, Oakleaf, and Romaine—covering a total of 103 varieties [27]. Each variety was planted three replicates, resulting in 309 samples in total. Seeds were sown in March 2023, and image data were collected over seven consecutive days at the maturity stage. To ensure consistent image quality, a dedicated imaging setup—including a backdrop, tripod, and digital camera (EOS 77D, Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan), was installed inside the greenhouse near the plants to provide a stable imaging environment under natural light (Fig. 1(b)). Images were acquired using a 22 mm fixed-focus lens at a working distance of approximately 1.2 m. Three representative leaves were collected from each plant, and images of both the adaxial and abaxial surfaces were captured immediately. After image acquisition for all 309 plants, manual phenotypic observations were conducted in accordance with UPOV guidelines for lettuce. All traits were independently assessed by three trained technicians under standardized conditions. Final scores were assigned by consensus, and samples with inconsistent assessments were re-evaluated to ensure scoring reliability. The manually evaluated DUS traits, including leaf blistering, size of blisters, number of incisions, and longitudinal section, were subsequently used to construct the lettuce leaf phenotypic trait knowledge graph.

Data preprocessing

To accurately extract lettuce leaf phenotypic features, we established a data-processing workflow that included annotation, data augmentation, semantic segmentation, feature extraction, and structured representation. First, major leaf tissues, including the lamina, midrib, and lateral veins, were manually annotated using LabelMe [28]. The annotated dataset was then augmented by rotation, flipping, and scaling to improve model robustness across diverse leaf morphologies and imaging conditions. Based on the processed dataset, a semantic segmentation model was developed using DeepLabV3+ [29]. MobileNetV2 was used as the backbone to reduce model size and computational cost, and the CBAM attention module was inserted after the ASPP module to improve the extraction of leaf edges, vein textures, and fine local structures. After training, the model produced pixel-level masks for the whole leaf, midrib region, and lateral vein region from new leaf images.

Phenotypic traits were subsequently quantified from the segmentation results using the lettuce leaf DUS trait quantification framework proposed by Qiu et al. [30], which provides computable measures for seven key DUS traits—leaf shape, tip shape, margin shape, vein shape, hue, lightness, and anthocyanin coloration—according to UPOV guidelines. Based on this framework, we extracted a set of quantitative phenotypic indicators, including leaf length (LL), leaf width (LW), leaf perimeter (LP), length-to-width ratio (LWr), leaf area (LA), Average_H_green (AH), Average_V_green (AV), anthocyanin coloration (AHc), anthocyanin area value (AAv), Main vein area (MVa), lateral vein area (LVa), Vein shape_mean_angle (LVsm), and Vein shape_std_angle (LVss). Traits related to leaf shape, tip shape, margin shape, and vein shape were primarily determined using geometric threshold-based rules, whereas chromaticity, brightness, and anthocyanin-related traits were quantified from statistical features in the HSV color space [30]. For DUS traits that could not be directly obtained from image analysis, manual assessment was performed in accordance with UPOV guidelines. All phenotypic indicators were then organized into a structured dataset for subsequent knowledge graph construction. Construction of the lettuce leaf phenotypic trait knowledge graph

To support unified modeling and semantic reasoning of lettuce leaf morphological traits, we constructed the Lettuce Leaf Phenotypic Trait Knowledge Graph (LLPT-KG) using a hybrid top-down and bottom-up strategy. The construction workflow consisted of four stages: ontology design, alignment of structured phenotypic data, ontology-based instance mapping, and graph database storage (Fig. 2).

Ontology-guided framework for constructing the lettuce leaf phenotypic trait knowledge graph (LLPT-KG)

Ontology design

The scope of the knowledge graph was determined by integrating domain literature, specialized books, the UPOV guidelines, and expert knowledge. Based on this scope, we developed the Lettuce Leaf Phenotypic Ontology (LLPO), which adopts a hierarchical semantic structure to formally represent domain concepts and their relationships. The main entity types and relationship types defined in LLPO are summarized in Tables 2 and 3, respectively. LLPO includes seven entity types: Lettuce_leaf (collected leaf samples), Variety (cultivated varieties), Lettuce_type (Butterhead, Iceberg, Loose-leaf, Romaine, and Oak-leaf), State (trait states defined according to UPOV standards), Method (trait acquisition methods), UPOV_characteristic (official UPOV categories for lettuce description), and Phenotype_trait (specific traits such as color, leaf shape, and incisions). In total, 17 data properties were defined to describe entity identifiers, names, and measured values, and 21 object properties were used to represent semantic relationships among entities. These relations included derives_from, belongs_to_types, method_of, is_category_of, and is_UPOV_characteristic_of. In addition, multiple sample-state-trait relations were defined for leaf morphology, color, and texture to represent trait states of each sample under the UPOV framework. The ontology was specified in OWL (Web Ontology Language)and developed in Protégé [31]. The ontology was logically validated to ensure structural consistency and completeness.

According to the LLPT-KG graph schema shown in Fig. 2, sample, variety, and type information were mapped to Lettuce_leaf, Variety, and Lettuce_type entities, respectively. In the complete knowledge graph, semantic relations such as derives_from and belongs_to_types were defined to represent hierarchical associations among samples, varieties, and lettuce types. Continuous phenotypic traits were stored as numerical attributes of Lettuce_leaf nodes and further organized into the input feature matrix X for the graph neural network. Discrete UPOV trait states were mapped to State nodes and linked to Lettuce_leaf or Variety through phenotype–state relations. Semantic associations among State, Phenotype_trait, UPOV_characteristic, and Method were represented using predefined ontology relations, including is_category_of, is_UPOV_characteristic_of, and method_of. In this way, LLPT-KG preserves both the quantitative information of continuous phenotypes and the semantic structure of discrete UPOV traits, thereby providing a unified data representation for subsequent dual-channel modeling with DCR-GCN.

All instances and relations generated through ontology-based mapping were finally represented as RDF (Resource Description Framework) triples, i.e., < subject, predicate, object > and imported into the Neo4j graph database for storage, querying, and visualization. The final graph scale and the statistics of the generated RDF triples are reported in Sect 3.5.

Construction of the DCR-GCN

In this study, a Dual-Channel Relational Graph Convolutional Network (DCR-GCN) was developed, and its overall architecture is shown in Fig. 3. The model consists of two parallel channels. In the feature channel, attributes extracted from sample nodes in the knowledge graph were fed into a multilayer perceptron (MLP) to learn node-specific semantic representations. In the structural channel, the graph topology and relation types were processed by a relational graph convolutional network (R-GCN) to learn structural embeddings based on graph connectivity. The outputs of the two channels were then concatenated and further integrated by an additional MLP layer to obtain a unified node representation. Finally, the fused representations were fed into a classifier for prediction. By combining semantic attributes with relational structure, the dual-channel architecture improved the model’s ability to capture complementary information and enhanced predictive performance.

Experimental setup

All experiments were conducted on a desktop computer equipped with an Intel i7-12700 CPU, 16 GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3070 Ti GPU. The computational environment was based on Python 3.8, and all graph neural network models were implemented using PyTorch Geometric.

In the default setting, the dataset was randomly split at the sample level into training, validation, and test sets at a ratio of 6:2:2. The proportions of the five lettuce types were preserved across all subsets to ensure balanced category representation. Because the constructed graph contains links between samples and their corresponding varieties, we further introduced a variety-level partitioning strategy as a supplementary evaluation to examine the potential influence of information propagation through shared variety nodes. Model initialization and other stochastic operations during training were controlled using a fixed random seed. To further assess model robustness, dataset partitioning was repeated using ten different random seeds, and all experiments were conducted independently for each split. During training, class-balanced weights computed from the training set were applied to the cross-entropy loss to alleviate class imbalance. All models were evaluated under the same data partitioning strategy and using the same evaluation metrics to ensure fair comparison. In addition, reverse edges were added during training to enhance the modeling of relational dependencies in the graph and improve model performance and generalization.

Results

Performance of the DCR-GCN model

To evaluate the node classification performance of DCR-GCN on the lettuce leaf phenotypic feature knowledge graph, we first examined the loss and accuracy curves for the training and validation sets (Fig. 4). The loss decreased steadily and gradually stabilized, indicating effective model convergence. Training and validation accuracy both increased rapidly during the early stages of training and remained consistently high thereafter, suggesting strong classification performance. The small gap between the training and validation loss curves, together with the stable accuracy trends, further indicates that the model achieved good generalization. The row-normalized confusion matrix for the test set (Fig. 5(a)) shows that Butterhead and Oak leaf samples were classified with high accuracy. Misclassifications mainly occurred for Loose leaf and Romaine samples: two Loose leaf samples were predicted as Romaine, and one Romaine sample was predicted as Butterhead. Overall, only a small number of samples were misclassified, indicating that DCR-GCN can effectively distinguish lettuce varieties with similar phenotypic characteristics.

To further assess the effectiveness of the proposed model, DCR-GCN was compared with five baseline methods: Random Forest (RF), multilayer perceptron (MLP), graph convolutional network (GCN), R-GCN and the image-based deep learning model ResNet-18. As shown in Table 4, DCR-GCN achieved the best overall performance among all compared methods, with mean Accuracy, Macro-F1, and Micro-F1 scores of 0.94, 0.94, and 0.94, respectively. Compared with the best-performing graph-based baseline, R-GCN, the proposed model improved Accuracy by approximately 9% points and Macro-F1 by 10% points. Traditional machine learning methods such as RF and MLP rely only on node attribute features, which limits their ability to capture subtle differences among lettuce types and leads to relatively lower performance. Although GCN and R-GCN incorporate graph structural information, their single-channel architectures may not fully capture both relational structure and phenotypic feature variation. In addition, uneven propagation across relation types may reduce discriminative power during message passing. To examine whether image-only deep learning could achieve comparable results, ResNet-18 was introduced as a visual baseline for end-to-end classification using cropped leaf images. ResNet-18 achieved an Accuracy of 0.80 and a Macro-F1 score of 0.82, which were both lower than those of DCR-GCN. The row-normalized confusion matrix of ResNet-18 (Fig. 5(b)) also shows greater confusion among several lettuce types, suggesting that image-only models have difficulty distinguishing samples with highly similar phenotypes.

Evaluation based on variety hierarchy

To further assess the generalization ability of the proposed model to previously unseen varieties, we conducted an additional evaluation based on variety-level data partitioning. In the default setting, the dataset was randomly split at the sample level. Under this setting, samples from the same variety could be distributed across the training, validation, and test sets. Because the knowledge graph contains links between samples and their corresponding variety nodes, structural information may propagate among samples of the same variety during message passing, which could lead to overly optimistic evaluation results. To address this issue, we introduced a variety-level partitioning strategy. Under this protocol, all samples belonging to the same variety were assigned exclusively to either the training, validation, or test set, so that no variety was shared across different subsets. This design prevents the model from indirectly accessing structural information associated with training samples through shared variety nodes during testing and therefore provides a more rigorous assessment of generalization.

All model architectures, parameter settings, and training procedures were kept the same as those used in the previous experiments. Dataset partitioning was repeated using ten different random seeds, and results are reported as mean ± standard deviation. Under this stricter evaluation protocol, the proposed model achieved an average Accuracy of 0.86 and a Macro-F1 score of 0.86 (Table 5). Although this performance was lower than that obtained under sample-level random partitioning, the model still maintained strong predictive ability. These results suggest that the proposed approach captures phenotype-related patterns that generalize across varieties, rather than relying primarily on variety-specific structural information.

Analysis of node feature importance

Plant phenotypes are composed of multiple trait dimensions, including morphology, structure, color, texture, and size, whose variation reflects the combined effects of genetics, environment, and management [31]. Based on the 13 continuous phenotypic features in the feature channel, we calculated gradient-based node feature importance, normalized the resulting scores, and averaged them within each lettuce type. The features were then ranked by mean contribution, and the top eight were selected for radar plot visualization. As shown in Fig. 6(a), each lettuce type exhibited a distinct feature fingerprint. Geometric traits (e.g., LL, LP, and LWr) and vein-related traits (e.g., MVa and LVss) contributed substantially across most lettuce types, whereas color- and anthocyanin-related indicators (e.g., AH and AAv) showed more type-specific contributions. These results suggest that morphological and vein-related traits provide stable discriminative dimensions across lettuce types, while pigment-related traits offer additional information for distinguishing specific types.

Visualization of node feature importance. a Radar plots showing the top eight node feature importance scores for the five lettuce types, based on the eight highest-ranked features according to . b Heatmap displaying node feature importance scores for five types of lettuce

The observed feature importance patterns were also consistent with the characteristic phenotypes of different lettuce types (Fig. 6(b)). Butterhead was associated with higher contributions from overall leaf size, main vein development, length-to-width ratio, brightness, and anthocyanin distribution, corresponding to its broad, soft leaves and visible color variation. Iceberg showed a predominantly vein-related pattern, with the model relying more on vein complexity and spatial configuration than on size or brightness, consistent with its compact leaf structure and relatively uniform morphology. Loose leaf lettuce showed no single dominant trait dimension, and several features had near-zero importance values. The model appeared to classify this type more by the absence of strong defining features than by a distinct trait pattern, suggesting greater phenotypic heterogeneity within this category. Oak-leaf lettuce was primarily distinguished by leaf outline and margin complexity, in agreement with its deeply lobed morphology. Romaine exhibited a characteristic pattern defined by leaf width, lateral vein prominence, and relatively stable coloration, reflecting its narrow, elongated leaves with upright and clearly defined veins [38]. Overall, the feature-importance analysis helps explain the high classification accuracy of the model and indicates that continuous phenotypic indicators derived from two-dimensional leaf images effectively capture key discriminative dimensions, including morphology, venation, leaf-edge structure, and pigmentation, across different lettuce types.

Relationship-level biological analysis

Unlike node feature importance, which measures the local sensitivity of continuous phenotypic variables to the classification output, relation importance reflects the contribution of semantic relations in the knowledge graph to information propagation during graph learning. In LLPT-KG, these relations correspond directly to formal phenotypic descriptors in the lettuce DUS/UPOV system, such as leaf color, anthocyanin coverage, venation, blistering, and margin structure. Their importance distribution therefore reveals how the model uses standardized phenotypic descriptors for classification. Overall, the mean importance values were relatively balanced across relation types (Fig. 7), indicating that the model relied on multiple sources of phenotypic semantic information rather than on any single relation. Among them, color_T3 (area covered by anthocyanin coloration), shape_T19 (leaf shape), shape_T27 (number of divisions), shape_T23 (leaf venation), and shape_T20 (shape of apex) showed the highest importance. This suggests that anthocyanin coverage, overall leaf shape, degree of division, venation pattern, and apex morphology were particularly informative for distinguishing lettuce types. In contrast, shape_T25 (width of lobes) showed relatively low global importance. This is biologically plausible because, according to the UPOV lettuce guidelines, this trait is mainly relevant to the Oakleaf type and therefore functions more as a type-specific auxiliary descriptor than as a generally informative trait across all lettuce types.

Category-level analysis further revealed distinct patterns of relation importance among lettuce types (Fig. 8). In Butterhead, the most important relations included shape_T20, shape_T21 (size of blisters), and shape_T17 (longitudinal section), indicating that classification depended mainly on overall morphological cues, including apex shape, blister size, and longitudinal profile. This is consistent with the characteristic Butterhead phenotype of soft leaves, a broad rounded form, and a pronounced head structure [39]. In Iceberg, shape_T23, shape_T22 (undulation of margin), and other structure-related relations contributed strongly, with venation-related information being especially prominent. This agrees with the UPOV description of Iceberg lettuce as having fan-shaped venation, thicker brittle leaves, and stronger leaf overlap. For Loose-leaf lettuce, color_T13 (leaf color), color_T14 (intensity of green color), texture_T7 (depth of margin incisions), and shape_T27 were among the most important relations, indicating that the model primarily distinguished this type through color variation, incision depth, and degree of lobing. This matches the broad color variation, irregular margins, and variable lobed morphology commonly observed in non-heading Loose-leaf accessions under UPOV descriptors. In Oakleaf, color_T3, texture_T7, shape_T23, texture_T6 (density of margin incisions), and shape_T22 showed high importance, highlighting the role of anthocyanin coverage, margin incision, and deep lobing in type discrimination. This closely matches the characteristic Oakleaf morphology of persistently lobed leaves with distinctly incised and undulating margins. For Romaine, texture_T12 (leaf blistering), texture_T6, shape_T21, and color_T10 (leaf anthocyanin coloration) contributed relatively strongly, suggesting that the model distinguished Romaine not only by its elongated leaves and prominent midrib, but also by additional cues related to blistering and margin structure. Overall, DCR-GCN identified phenotypic relations with strong discriminative value for lettuce classification. The importance patterns were consistent with key DUS/UPOV traits, such as anthocyanin coverage, leaf shape, degree of division, venation, blistering, and margin structure, supporting the biological interpretability and practical relevance of semantic relation modeling.

Lettuce leaf phenotypic trait knowledge graph

The Lettuce Leaf Phenotypic Trait Knowledge Graph (LLPT-KG) was constructed using leaf phenotype data collected at the mature stage from five lettuce types. LLPT-KG was designed to support automated type identification and phenotypic knowledge management by following the RDF specification [40], and incorporating the official UPOV trait structure for structured representation and semantic association of phenotypic data. LLPT-KG comprises 7 classes, 17 data properties, and 21 object properties, and contains 621 entity nodes and 6464 RDF triples. As summarized in Table 6, the main relationship types in LLPT-KG are presented together with their corresponding triple counts and representative example triples, thereby providing a clearer connection between the ontology-defined relations and the actual graph data. Several phenotypic relations, including color_T10, shape_T19, and texture_T6, each comprised 412 triples, indicating broad coverage of these key descriptors across leaf samples. The derives_from relation comprised 309 triples, linking individual leaf samples to their corresponding varieties and ensuring phenotype traceability. In addition, LLPT-KG encodes semantic relations for trait hierarchy and acquisition methods, such as is_category_of, is_UPOV_characteristic_of, and method_of. Together, these relations form a structured semantic network that supports downstream knowledge querying, semantic reasoning, and phenotype-based identification.

Ontology assessment results

LLPO, the ontology underlying LLPT-KG, was quantitatively evaluated within a semiotic framework across syntactic, semantic, and pragmatic dimensions. As shown in Table 7, LLPO exhibited strong syntactic quality, with full use of key OWL components and no detected syntax violations, indicating a well-defined and highly formalized structure. Semantic evaluation showed high interpretability and clarity, suggesting that term naming was generally consistent with conventions in the lettuce phenotype domain. Semantic consistency was comparatively lower, owing to the presence of duplicate or closely related terms across concepts, which indicates room for further refinement of naming and concept consolidation. From a pragmatic perspective, LLPO covered the main information required to describe lettuce leaf phenotypes, although its scope was slightly smaller than the average of the reference ontologies [37]. Expert validation nevertheless indicated high accuracy, supporting its practical reliability. Overall, these results suggest that LLPO provides a sound ontological basis for subsequent reasoning and application of LLPT-KG.

Discussion

Supporting lettuce DUS phenotypic identification with knowledge graphs and graph representation learning

In this study, knowledge graph technology was used to model phenotypic feature data extracted and quantified from lettuce leaf images, enabling unified representation of trait indicators and semantic relationships. This framework provides a basis for the structured organization and knowledge discovery of phenotypic information. The LLPT-KG explicitly connects leaf phenotypes through a “sample-state-trait” structure, forming a machine-readable descriptive system for commonly used the DUS traits. Such structuring enables more consistent comparisons across batches and locations and facilitates the transition from isolated datasets to interoperable phenotypic resources. As a result, LLPT-KG improves data sharing, reuse, and downstream analysis. For example, LLPT-KG supports complex trait-combination queries and similar variety or type searches, allowing retrieval of multidimensional phenotypic patterns and providing decision support for variety identification and utilization. Existing Plant Ontology and Trait Ontology resources [41] do not fully address the specific trait-state representation requirements of UPOV-based lettuce DUS testing or variety-type management [42]. In this context, LLPT-KG helps fill this gap and provides a foundation for subsequent DUS testing and germplasm resource management. Future interoperability with TO, PO, or Crop Ontology could further improve system integration.

Building on this framework, the DCR-GCN model performed node classification on LLPT-KG and demonstrated that the graph contains learnable structural and semantic information suitable for rapid lettuce types identification. This provides a quantitative and interpretable supplementary tool for DUS evaluation [43]. Moreover, the model remained effective under the more stringent variety-level partitioning strategy, suggesting that it learned transferable discriminative patterns from leaf phenotypic traits and their semantic relations rather than relying primarily on shared variety information. This further supports the robustness of LLPT-KG and the proposed dual-channel relational modeling strategy for cross-variety phenotypic recognition. Traditional DUS assessment relies heavily on multi-environment, multi-year field observations and expert judgment, making the process labor-intensive and sensitive to environmental variation [44]. In contrast, the knowledge graph-based approach integrates multi-scale leaf traits, including morphology, structure, color, texture, and size, into a standardized representation and enables high-accuracy classification through graph representation learning. Importantly, the node- and edge-level importance scores generated by the model provide interpretable indicators for quantitatively assessing similarities between candidate varieties and existing materials. These indicators may assist breeders in reducing redundant materials and improving pre-trial screening efficiency. In addition, edge-importance analysis transforms traditionally experience-based DUS indicators into quantifiable and rankable graph-structural evidence. On the one hand, this supports the relevance of UPOV morphological traits in lettuce type identification; on the other hand, it provides a basis for a future digital workflow that combines image phenotyping with knowledge graphs for DUS evaluation. Such a workflow could help identify a minimal subset of informative and stable traits, thereby reducing the workload associated with field surveys and manual scoring. Over the longer term, the accumulation of trait-level edge weights may also provide empirical evidence for refining DUS guidelines so that they better reflect actual phenotypic variation. More broadly, integrating phenotypic identification with knowledge graphs may support the iterative construction of large-scale repositories for lettuce phenomics. Such repositories could facilitate material organization, similarity comparison, and trait-based pre-screening in new variety rights applications, thereby contributing to the gradual automation and standardization of DUS examinations. Notably, the framework proposed here is not limited to lettuce and may also have broader potential in other crops, offering a useful reference for phenotypic research and variety evaluation.

Limitations and future directions

Although the DCR-GCN model achieved high accuracy in automatic lettuce type identification, and the node- and edge-level importance analyses were broadly consistent with the key morphological traits defined in the UPOV Lettuce DUS Guidelines, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the present study considered only five major lettuce types, whereas more than 100 varieties are cultivated in practice. As a result, the current analysis did not reach cultivar-level resolution and should be regarded as an initial phenotype-based assessment rather than fine-grained variety discrimination. Second, the modeling framework and subsequent analyses were conducted primarily at the leaf scale and did not comprehensively incorporate other traits commonly used in DUS testing into the knowledge graph. This may limit the ability of the current system to support full variety-level identification. In addition, the present study focused mainly on type-level discrimination and model interpretability, without systematically evaluating Uniformity and Stability, which are essential components of the DUS framework. Therefore, the proposed approach is better viewed as a rapid pre-screening and decision-support tool to complement, rather than replace, multi-location and multi-year DUS field trials [45]. Future work should extend the framework toward cultivar-level phenotypic characterization by collecting multi-environment and multi-season datasets that capture broader phenotypic variation. Incorporating uniformity-related indicators, such as off-types and within-variety variability metrics, into the knowledge graph, as well as assessing stability across environments, would further improve its applicability within the DUS framework. Such developments would contribute to the digitalization and standardization of lettuce DUS testing.

Conclusion

This study developed a Lettuce Leaf Phenotypic Trait Knowledge Graph (LLPT-KG) that integrates continuous and discrete multidimensional leaf phenotypic traits across five lettuce types, together with a dual-channel relational graph convolutional network (DCR-GCN) for lettuce type identification. DCR-GCN achieved an accuracy of 0.94 and a Macro-F1 score of 0.94, outperforming the best single-channel graph baseline, R-GCN, by approximately 9% points in accuracy and 10% points in Macro-F1. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of combining knowledge graphs with graph representation learning for leaf-scale lettuce phenotypic classification. Gradient-based importance analysis further revealed the contributions of key phenotypic traits and semantic relations to type discrimination. The results indicate that LLPT-KG can effectively organize multidimensional phenotypic information, including morphology, color, venation, and texture, while DCR-GCN jointly exploits node attributes and graph structural semantics to improve classification performance. Together, these findings highlight the potential of knowledge graph-guided graph neural networks for crop leaf phenotypic identification. This framework provides methodological support for digital DUS pre-screening and assisted evaluation in lettuce, as well as for germplasm resource management and phenotypic knowledge organization. Future work will extend the framework by integrating multi-environment and multi-season data to construct variety-level multimodal knowledge graphs and incorporate richer phenotypic and genetic information, thereby supporting deeper analysis of lettuce phenotypic variation, identification of highly similar varieties, and digital DUS testing.
