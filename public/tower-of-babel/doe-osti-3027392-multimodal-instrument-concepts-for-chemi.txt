 ABSTRACT
The development of advanced chemical and energy systems is inherently complex, relying heavily on their
unique pathways. Because no two catalysts or batteries follow exactly the same kinetic trajectories, con-
ventional sequential measurements often obscure crucial relationships between structure and dynamics
through statistical averaging. This report introduces two neutron scattering instrument concepts for the
Second Target Station (STS) specifically designed to overcome this barrier: the Extended Q-range Diffrac-
tometer (EQD) and the Multimodal Chemical Spectrometer (MCS).
By leveraging the unique high brilliance and broad bandwidth of the STS, these instruments provide con-
current, single-shot measurements on individual samples, ensuring that relevant length and energy scales
are captured simultaneously. The EQD enables a simultaneous view of structural correlations across four
orders of magnitude (0.1 to 1000 Å), while the MCS simultaneously measures structure via diffraction and
dynamics via inelastic scattering and neutron vibrational spectroscopy. These capabilities facilitate the
study of kinetic processes under in situ and operando conditions up to sub-second time resolution, directly
addressing key challenges in chemistry, materials science, and soft matter. This document presents their
conceptual designs, technical parameters, and technical readiness, highlighting a path toward a data-driven
ecosystem for time-resolved neutron science.

 1. INTRODUCTION

 No two catalysts deactivate at the same rate, and no two batteries fail via the same pathway.
 The evolution of complex chemical systems is a non-deterministic, path-dependent process
 where subtle, uncontrollable local variations dictate the kinetic trajectory. While traditional se-
 quential measurements using separate probes can attempt to reconstruct these processes through
 large sample ensembles, such an approach is highly inefficient and often results in statistical
 blurring that obscures the vital, transient correlations between different length and energy scales.
 The Extended Q-range Diffractometer and Multimodal Chemical Spectrometer overcome these
 limitations by providing concurrent, single-shot measurements on the exact same sample. By
 simultaneously capturing atomic-scale bonding and mesoscale structural evolution on sub-second
 timescales, these instruments directly reveal the hidden correlations governing a system’s life-
 cycle with unprecedented efficiency and precision. This approach is beyond the current reach
 of existing facilities and will generate high-fidelity, high-volume datasets (up to 1,000x larger
 than existing instruments) that are essential for a data-driven ecosystem to accelerate materials
 discovery. Furthermore, the Second Target Station (STS)’s high brilliance makes this multi-
 perspective view feasible even for mass-limited samples, such as novel nanomaterials or rare
 actinides, where large ensemble studies are impractical.

Neutron scattering serves as an essential technique for advancing our understanding of chemical sciences,
which play a pivotal role in supporting the economic and energy security of the United States. A compre-
hensive understanding of chemical systems, however, requires a suite of complementary neutron scattering
techniques. For example, small-angle neutron scattering (SANS) characterizes nanoscale structures such as
morphology and porosity, while neutron powder diffraction (NPD) probes the long-range atomic order in
crystalline materials. These techniques are often complemented by pair distribution function (PDF) anal-
ysis, which uses the total scattering signal to reveal local atomic arrangements in amorphous or nanocrys-
talline systems. In addition, neutron vibrational spectroscopy (NVS) measures the energy of molecular

vibrations, providing a direct fingerprint of chemical bonding and dynamics, and is particularly sensitive to
hydrogen.

The STS at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) is designed to address pressing scientific problems
that are beyond the reach of current neutron sources by integrating and advancing these capabilities. The
2024 STS Grand Challenges Workshop Report [1] highlights a common need across the chemical
sciences (see Tab. 1): to understand correlations across multiple length scales and between structure and
dynamics in complex, evolving systems under real-world conditions. However, conventional approaches
at existing neutron facilities, which rely on sequential studies with different techniques, are inadequate for
this task. For stochastic processes like catalyst deactivation, where individual particles follow different de-
activation pathways, sequential measurements on many samples are required to build up reliable statistics,
which can blur the very correlations of interest. To address these challenges, concurrent, multimodal ca-
pabilities are needed to simultaneously probe multiple length and time/energy scales. Such capabilities,
which are beyond those of existing facilities, would allow researchers to directly and precisely reveal these
correlations in individual samples, and are crucial for maintaining U.S. scientific and technological leader-
ship.
To fill this need, we propose two new STS instrument concepts: the Extended Q-range Diffractometer
(EQD) and the Multimodal Chemical Spectrometer (MCS). The baseline performance of the concepts is
estimated using existing technologies to ensure a reliable assessment of core capabilities, while novel ap-
proaches, such as angular-dependent choppers for divergence control and correlation techniques for energy
discrimination, are proposed as high-reward alternatives. These potential paths require further feasibility
studies to validate their integration and performance at the STS. This document outlines the scientific case
for each instrument (Sec. 2) and presents their conceptual designs and a global peer comparison. Further-
more (EQD in Sec. 3, MCS in Sec. 4), we discuss how these concepts leverage the STS’s unique source
characteristics (Sec. 5), the user communities they will serve (Sec. 6), and the technical maturity and in-
frastructure needs for their realization (Sec. 7).

 2. SCIENCE CASE

 Progress in the chemical sciences from catalysis and soft matter to materials engineering re-
 quires an understanding of complex, functional systems under realistic operating conditions.
 These systems are often heterogeneous, evolving in response to external stimuli and exhibit-
 ing stochastic behaviors across multiple length and time scales simultaneously. However, tra-
 ditional neutron scattering approaches, which often study materials at equilibrium or probe dif-
 ferent scales in separate experiments, obscure the critical correlations across length scales and
 between structure and dynamics. This creates a significant capability gap, one that the two con-
 current, multimodal instruments are proposed to address.

2.1 CAPABILITY GAP: THE NEED FOR CONCURRENT, MULTIMODAL MEASUREMENT
The STS workshop report details several instances of current challenges in chemical sciences. For ex-
ample, in catalysis, a primary challenge is to elucidate the mechanisms governing catalytic transforma-
tions under realistic conditions, valuable for improving energy-intensive processes like ammonia synthe-
sis [2]. In materials science and engineering, a fundamental gap exists in obtaining real-time, atomic-
level insights into material properties during additive manufacturing or under the extreme conditions found
in advanced reactors. For soft matter, the challenge lies in understanding how hierarchical structures,
from molecular to mesoscale, emerge and dictate function in systems from high-performance polymers
to biological tissues.
To address these challenges, research shall shift from traditional equilibrium studies to in situ and operando
investigations under realistic conditions. However, current neutron scattering facilities face a critical lim-
itation: they cannot simultaneously probe broad length and energy scales with sufficient time resolution.
This forces researchers to collect data sequentially. For example, measuring atomic structure with NPD
and then nanoscale structure with SANS in separate experiments, which obscures the vital correlations be-
tween time-dependent phenomena occurring at different length scales, as well as the connection between
structural evolution and dynamics.
To bridge this capability gap, we must move beyond highly specialized, single-function instruments. We
propose the development of multimodal instruments that offer concurrent, multi-perspective views of com-
plex, dynamic systems, a need that resonates with recent STS technical reports [3, 4]. While each mea-
surement mode will be individually competitive, the unique advantage of this approach is the ability to
correlate disparate phenomena, such as chemical bonding, atomic structure, and nanoscale morphology,
within a single, time-resolved experiment. Enabled by the high brightness and low-repetition rate of the
STS source, these instruments will be able to:
 • Capture kinetic processes on sub-second timescales through rapid, time-resolved measurements;
 • Probe structural correlations from the atomic (sub-Å) to the mesoscopic (sub-µm) scale in a single
 measurement;
 • Correlate structure and dynamics concurrently to generate the high-fidelity data needed for pre-
 dictive modeling;
 • Enable autonomous experimentation through a design that natively supports data-driven control
 and analysis.

2.2 OPPORTUNITIES FOR STS
The STS is uniquely positioned to enable this transformation. Its low-dimensional, under-moderated parahy-
drogen moderators generate ample neutrons across a wide energy range, from less than 1 meV (9 Å) to
more than 1000 meV (0.3 Å), as shown in Fig. 1. Source data are from ST-13 -CY46D-MID-March_2022.dat
and ST-17-TU90D-MID-March_2022.dat for the STS cylindrical and tube parahydrogen moderators, re-
spectively; and from a1G3AD-2-f5.dat, a1G3AD-8-f5.dat and a1G3AD-5-f5.dat for the First Target Station
(FTS) decoupled poisoned supercritical hydrogen moderator (DPSH), coupled hydrogen moderator (CoH),
and decoupled ambient water moderator (DcH2 O), respectively. Calculations are based on source powers
of 700 kW for STS and 2 MW for FTS.

 4

The STS will complement existing ORNL neutron sources, each optimized for distinct research needs.
FTS focuses on science requiring thermal and cold neutrons with sharp temporal resolution for the high-
est precision measurements of atomic-scale phenomena, while High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) is used
for isotope production and is optimized for measurements benefiting from the highest time-averaged flux
of monochromatic neutron beams. In contrast, STS will support research demanding high-brilliance cold
neutrons, and it is ideal for very small samples, non-equilibrium systems, broad ranges of length and time
scales, and high-resolution, data-intensive studies.
For cold neutrons, the estimated STS pulse brightness is 20 times that of ORNL’s FTS (CoH modera-
tor) and 5 times that of the European Spallation Source (ESS) [1]. Although optimized for cold neutrons,
the STS source also demonstrates significant brilliance in the thermal region. For instance, at 200 meV
(0.63 Å), the STS cylindrical moderator exhibits similar time-average brilliance to the FTS DPSH and is
only 25% less bright than the FTS DcH2 O. However, at 5 meV (4 Å), it has a 20 times higher time-average
brilliance than the FTS DPSH and 150 times higher than the FTS DcH2 O.
The higher brilliance of the cold neutrons generated from the STS moderators compared to the FTS mod-
erator designs comes from the cost of broader emission times, as shown in Fig. 2. Since the neutron wave-
lengnth and energy are determined from the time-of-flight (TOF), large pulse widths will lead to larger
uncertainties in the Q and E, worsening their resolutions. However, the STS runs at a four times slower
repetition rate. Therefore, longer beamlines can be used to restore the required resolution. Importantly,
even with these longer beamlines, STS instruments can still achieve a much broader wavelength bandwidth
in a single frame, enabling the simultaneous, multiscale measurements.
STS’s unique source characteristics make it possible to develop the next-generation instruments to rapidly
advance the chemical sciences. To fill the existing capability gap, we propose two new concepts designed
specifically to leverage the STS source, and their full world-leading performance cannot be realized at FTS
or HFIR.
 • An EQD to simultaneously probe a broad range of correlation lengths from 0.1 to 1000 Å.
 • A MCS to concurrently measure the structural evolution via elastic scattering and track the dynamics
 via inelastic scattering.
These two instruments will serve a broad user community across chemistry, materials science, and soft
matter. The following sections describe these two instrument concepts. For distributions, ∆ and σ denote
the Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) and standard deviation, respectively.

 3. EXTENDED Q-RANGE DIFFRACTOMETER

 The Extended Q-range Diffractometer is designed for multiscale, in situ characterization of
 chemical systems under application-relevant conditions, providing a broad view from 0.1 to
 1000 Å. This instrument will provide world-leading capabilities for simultaneous NPD and
 SANS measurements. It has a PDF capability with rapid data collection rate to monitor local
 structural changes and extends the Q range into the SANS region to probe large-scale correla-
 tions.

The key capability of the EQD is the time-resolved structural characterization of fast kinetic processes,
such as chemical reactions and phase transitions in materials. The instrument is designed to study a wide
range of complex chemical systems beyond highly crystalline materials, including nanocrystalline and
non-crystalline systems such as polymers and colloids. For materials where long-range order is absent,

 5

such as glasses, liquids, and amorphous solids, the analysis approach shifts from Bragg scattering to the
pair distribution function (PDF). Therefore, the EQD will be optimized for PDF studies by prioritizing a
high Qmax with medium Q-resolution. Concurrently, it will provide excellent performance in the SANS
regime, which requires sufficient resolution at low Q to probe large-scale structures.

High-level requirements for the EQD are summarized in Tab. 2. Typical samples of powder, liquid, or mix-

studies, a real-space resolution better than 0.1 Å necessitates a Qmax > 31 Å−1 (∆r ≈ π/Qmax , represent-

SANS, a Qmin < 0.067 Å−1 is required (Lmax ≈ Q2π min
 ).
A broad wavelength bandwidth is crucial for covering an extended Q range in a single frame, with band-
width inversely proportional to the total beamline length (moderator to detector). Simultaneously, a TOF

 6

diffractometer must be sufficiently long to provide the necessary wavelength resolution (∆λ/λ).

3.1.1 Primary Spectrometer
EQD will be positioned to face the cylindrical moderator, which offers sharper neutron pulses and a higher
thermal neutron component compared to tube moderators (see Fig. 1). This choice is crucial for simultane-
ously achieving the required bandwidth for SANS and the resolution for PDF analysis, as the latter benefits
from a strong thermal neutron flux at high Q.
The derived instrument parameters are summarized in Tab. 3. A primary flight path (L1 ) of 26.0 m was se-
lected to balance the requirements between a large wavelength band (favoring a shorter L1 ) and good reso-
lution for diffraction data (favoring a longer L1 ). The secondary flight path (L2 ) for the diffraction detector
banks is 1.2-4.0 m to accommodate large sample environments and collimators. A minimum of 4.0 m for
the SANS detectors is needed to reach the required Qmin . The beam divergences are derived from the re-
quired resolution, and a large solid-angle detector coverage is necessary for time-resolved experiments.

and sample sizes:

                                         (∆θ)2 = (∆θbeam )2 + (∆θdet )2 .

At the low Q limit, the Q resolution is predominantly governed by beam divergence, necessitating a highly
collimated beam. Thus, SANS experiments can accept a relatively broader ∆λ/λ of approximately 10% but
require a tight divergence of 0.1-0.2◦ , utilizing long wavelengths to reach lower Qmin . Conversely, at the
high Q limit, Q resolution becomes limited by the wavelength resolution, requiring a good wavelength res-
olution (∆λ/λ ≲ 0.5%). For NPD and PDF studies, a relatively larger beam divergence is often preferred
for higher flux, especially with the shortest useful neutron wavelengths to improve the signal near the high
Q region.
An ideal neutron transport system would simultaneously deliver long-wavelength neutrons with small
beam divergence for SANS data and short-wavelength neutrons with much larger beam divergence for
PDF data. However, conventional neutron transport systems, typically composed of supermirror-based
guides, deliver larger beam divergence for longer wavelength neutrons. Although modern designs incorpo-
rating ballistic guides can achieve a nearly constant beam divergence over a broad wavelength band [9], the
beam divergence preferred here has not been realized in any existing beamlines. Traditional approach us-
ing a single guide system will either sacrifice resolution at low Q or reduce flux for high Q data. To over-
come these shortcomings, one potential solution is to use a dual guide system, similar to what is proposed
for HEIMDAL at ESS [10]. This is schematically shown in Fig. 3(a) and will be discussed later in Sec. 7.
The recently proposed nested mirror optics (NMO) [11] could potentially replace long neutron guides for
transport; however, detailed evaluations are required to determine if their performance can meet the neces-
sary specifications.

3.2 SCIENTIFIC IMPACT AND FIRST EXPERIMENTS

 A compelling first experiment is the study of zeolite catalyst deactivation [12]. The EQD will
 make it possible to correlate the growth of coke particles (measured by SANS) with changes in
 the catalyst’s T-O-T bond angle (measured by PDF), all with second or even sub-second time
 resolution. The T-O-T angle is a fundamental parameter that connects the atomic-scale geometry
 of a zeolite to its macroscopic properties like stability and catalytic activity. This type of mea-
 surement, which is critical for designing more robust catalysts, is not currently feasible. This
 experiment directly addresses the Grand Challenge of "Elucidating the fundamental mechanisms
 governing catalysis".

By overcoming the limitations of sequential measurements, EQD will simultaneously track atomic- and
nano-scale structures, allowing researchers to follow sub-second structural changes of catalysts under real-
world conditions. This capability will affect multiple scientific areas. Examples include:
 • Chemistry and Catalysis: Elucidating molecular mechanisms of catalysis and energy storage in
 real time. This involves resolving transient intermediates, hydrogen dynamics, and reaction path-
 ways, and visualizing evolving processes in situ and operando. Specific applications include ad-
 vancing understanding of nitrogen fixation and ammonia synthesis. The EQD’s high flux will also
 enable studies of mass-limited heavy elements, such as actinides, often available only in microgram-
 to-milligram quantities.
 • Materials Science: Improving understanding of materials under operating conditions, from atomic-
 scale defect formation to large-scale mechanical behavior. This includes creating time-resolved
 datasets of material evolution during processes like melting, curing, solidifying, and cooling with
 atomic-level detail, supporting advances in additive manufacturing and ensuring component integrity
 for nuclear and aerospace industries. The EQD will also be important for developing science-based
 certification methods for new materials by providing data for accurate digital twins, tracking mate-
 rial kinetics over four orders of magnitude in length scale.
 • Soft Matter and Polymers: Visualizing how these materials evolve across multiple length scales,
 allowing real-time observation of phase transitions under non-equilibrium conditions, which is im-
 portant for advanced energy storage and adaptive manufacturing.
For example, in in situ studies of electrochemical processes, the instrument will provide a complete struc-
tural picture of transport and reaction phenomena on timescales from sub-seconds to hours. It will corre-
late atomic-scale structural changes with redox composition during transformations (via PDF) and measure
the evolution of mesoscopic, spatial inhomogeneities, such as the formation of rough deposits (via SANS).
This concurrent, multiscale view from the sub-Ångstrom to the 100-nanometer level is critical for under-
standing and improving a wide variety of electrochemical systems, including conducting polymers, redox
polymers, and metal oxides.

 4. MULTIMODAL CHEMICAL SPECTROMETER

 The Multimodal Chemical Spectrometer is designed to characterize a chemical system’s evolu-
 tion by simultaneously measuring its structure and dynamics. It will provide unique capabilities
 to track chemical changes via NVS while concurrently collecting structural data from the atomic
 to the mesoscale via NPD and SANS. This integrated approach allows for the direct correla-
 tion of phenomena in heterogeneous, non-equilibrium systems, providing the high-fidelity data
 needed to build and validate predictive models.

The scientific impact of the MCS comes from its ability to provide time-resolved measurements of a mate-
rial’s structure and dynamics in a single experiment. By eliminating the need for sequential measurements,
it removes the ambiguity inherent in comparing data taken under unavoidable, different pathways. This
is critical for understanding kinetic and non-equilibrium phenomena in advanced chemical systems that
show non-deterministic behaviors. The instrument’s combination of high flux and simultaneous cover-
age in broad Q and E ranges will enable the continuous tracking of phenomena across a vast range of time
and length scales. In a single measurement, it can characterize across a wide range of time scales: high-
frequency atomic vibrations (femtoseconds), slower diffusive and relaxational dynamics (picoseconds),
and atomic to mesoscale structural evolution (sub-seconds or slower).
NVS directly probes inter-atomic interactions to identify chemical bonds, functional groups, and their
transformations during reactions like catalysis or thermal decomposition. Its key advantage is an excep-
tional sensitivity to hydrogen, stemming from the proton’s large incoherent scattering cross-section. This
allows for the precise detection of proton dynamics and H-atom vibrations, even at very low concentra-
tions. Beyond chemical identification, the full vibrational spectrum provides rich, multi-faceted informa-
tion: the vibrational density of states is used to determine fundamental thermodynamic properties like en-
tropy and specific heat, while subtle spectral changes reveal shifts in molecular configuration. This versa-
tility makes NVS an valuable tool for chemistry, materials science, and soft matter, with direct relevance to
industrial applications.

4.1.2 Secondary Spectrometer
To resolve inelastic scattering, the instrument will feature an indirect-geometry design where a crystal an-
alyzer system fixes the final energy of the scattered neutrons. The primary design challenge is balancing
energy resolution with the large solid angle coverage (ideal > 6.0 sr) required for time-resolved studies.
While flat analyzers in a perfect time-focusing geometry, like the original TOSCA design, provide excel-
lent energy resolution, they would require oversized detectors to achieve such a large solid angle. There-
fore, the MCS will employ a curved, focusing pyrolytic graphite (PG) analyzer system, drawing inspiration
from VISION and the TOSCA upgrade [7, 15], to achieve a large solid angle. Resolution degradation typ-
ically associated with curved analyzers is minimized by carefully optimizing the analyzer’s curvature and
focusing properties, specifically by focusing neutrons at a distance behind the detector plane to create a
large, spread image [15]. To complement this, software masking of the position-sensitive detector (PSD)
array offers a flexible trade-off between intensity gain and resolution during data reduction.
Figure 6 shows the estimated energy resolution, following VISION’s calculation [7]. A resolution better
than 0.3% can be achieved in the fingerprint region under ideal conditions. In a realistic configuration, we
anticipate an overall resolution of 1.0-1.5% [16]. Further details are available in Appendix A.2.
Complementing the NVS spectrometer, SANS and NPD detector banks will provide quasi-continuous an-
gular coverage from 0.5◦ to 170◦ . The open sample geometry is designed for a wide range of sample envi-
ronments. A radial collimator will be placed before the diffraction detectors to reduce parasitic scattering
and ensure high-quality structural data can be collected concurrently with the NVS spectra.

 5. UNIQUENESS OF STS SOURCE

 The proposed instruments will utilize unique characteristics of the STS source:
 • High Brilliance: The high-brightness source allows for the illumination of smaller sample
 volumes with higher neutron flux than is possible at other facilities. This will enable
 measurements on mass-limited samples and the study of kinetic processes on sub-second
 timescales.
 • Broad Energy Spectrum: The compact, under-moderated parahydrogen moderator
 generates ample neutrons over a wide energy range from less than 1 meV (9 Å) to more
 than 1000 meV (0.3 Å) that can be used by broad band instruments.
 • Low Repetition Rate and Short Pulse: The 15 Hz source frequency provides a long TOF
 window (66.7 ms), which, combined with short pulses, allows a broad wavelength band of
 neutrons to be used in a single pulse with a good TOF resolution. This broad bandwidth
 is the key to enabling simultaneous measurements across wide ranges of Q and E.

The feasibility of these concepts relies on the STS’s unique source characteristics, particularly its high bril-
liance across a broad energy range (Fig. 1). The proposed instruments are expected to significantly outper-
form current world-leading instruments in the cold neutron region and, by taking advantage of advance-
ments in neutron optics and beamline design tools, they are projected to be competitive in the thermal re-
gion as well. This broad energy capability is crucial, as the proposed instruments require thermal neutrons
for high-Q PDF analysis and the vibrational "fingerprint region" (usually quoted as 60-220 meV [19] or
50-185 meV [15]) for NVS, while simultaneously needing cold neutrons for SANS experiments.

5.1 EXTENDED Q-RANGE DIFFRACTOMETER
The design of the EQD is tied to the broad bandwidth available at the STS. This bandwidth, a direct con-
sequence of the low source frequency, allows the instrument to access a Q-range spanning four orders of
magnitude (0.006 to 40 Å−1 ) in a single shot. A higher-repetition-rate source would have to reduce resolu-
tion or flux for such a broad coverage.
With the broad bandwidth, the EQD can concurrently resolve atomic-scale correlations (via PDF) and
nanoscale structures (via SANS). The high brilliance of the STS then makes it possible to perform these
measurements with sub-second time resolution, enabling the study of material transformations in real-time
under operando conditions.

5.2 MULTIMODAL CHEMICAL SPECTROMETER
The MCS concept requires both the broad bandwidth and the high brilliance of the STS. The broad band-
width is necessary for an indirect geometry spectrometer, as it allows for the measurement of the entire
vibrational spectrum (1-500 meV) in a single frame, with excellent energy resolution determined using a
long primary flight path. Simultaneously, the high source brilliance provides the high flux needed for time-

6.2 BEYOND CHEMICAL SCIENCES
6.2.1 Planetary Science
Although well-suited for planetary science, neutron scattering has been underutilized in this field due to
the complexity of geological materials and the challenges of recreating extreme conditions (such as high
temperatures and pressures). The proposed EQD and MCS instruments will address these challenges. The
high scattering contrast neutrons provide for elements with similar atomic numbers (e.g., Fe and Mn) and
their non-destructive nature make these instruments ideal for probing complex, multi-phase planetary ma-
terials. Crucially, they will enable rigorous in situ investigations under geological conditions, avoiding the
experimental artifacts of traditional studies using quenched samples. This capability will provide a direct
probe of mineral phase stability and evolution, resolving key questions in planetary science.
The proposed instruments will provide a real-time probe of these processes. The EQD will track mineral
transformations under geological conditions, providing kinetic data on processes like cation ordering in
olivines, which are inaccessible with traditional quench-and-measure techniques. The MCS will comple-
ment this by correlating these structural changes with atomic dynamics. Revealing the correlation is im-
portant to clarify the role of light elements (e.g., H, Li) in hydrous minerals and melts, which is a critical
factor in mantle dynamics and fluid-rock interactions.

6.2.2 Biochemistry
The study of condensates formed by Liquid-Liquid Phase Separation (LLPS) is a rapidly growing area in
biochemistry, but it is a challenge for conventional structural biology techniques due to the non-crystalline
and dynamic nature of these systems. Neutron scattering offers a powerful solution. The unique sensitivity
of neutrons to hydrogen and the use of deuterium labeling make it possible to decouple the structural and
dynamic properties of the different components (proteins, RNA, lipids) within these complex assemblies.
The proposed instruments are uniquely suited to address these challenges. The EQD will provide time-
resolved structural information from the molecular to the mesoscale, revealing the kinetics of LLPS assem-
bly and the internal organization of condensates. The MCS will then provide a direct link between these
structural changes and the underlying molecular dynamics, offering unprecedented detail on water-protein
interactions and the vibrational modes of specific, deuterated components within the larger assembly.

6.2.3 New Ecosystem: AI and Data Science
The large-volume, high-fidelity datasets produced by these instruments are ideal for machine learning ap-
plications and will attract a new community of researchers with expertise in AI and data science. These
users will develop advanced, AI-assisted analysis pipelines to extract critical insights into kinetics un-
der realistic, operando conditions. This integration of rich experimental data with AI will enable a new
paradigm for materials design, including the development of digital twins for real-time monitoring and the
accelerated certification of novel materials with targeted performance and reliability.

tomation into instrument operations. While the focus of this section is on these R&D opportunities, suffi-
cient infrastructure is also indispensable for optimizing scientific productivity.

7.1 NEUTRON TRANSPORT AND BEAM CONTROL SYSTEM

 A challenge for multimodal neutron instruments is the simultaneous optimization of distinct
 beam characteristics for different scattering modes. Long-wavelength neutrons with tight beam
 divergence are necessary for low-Q SANS, while thermal neutrons with larger beam divergence
 are preferred for NPD. For NVS, thermal neutrons are required, with beam divergence as large
 as reasonably achievable.

One promising solution is to employ a dual guide system, as schematically shown in Fig. 3. This system
features a dedicated curved guide branch for cold neutrons and a straight guide efficient for transporting
thermal neutrons. When high-resolution low-Q data are needed, the straight guide will transport large
beam divergence, thermal neutrons (the first half-band); while the curved guide simultaneously delivers
small beam divergence, colder neutrons (the second half-band). This system also provides some flexibil-
ity for experiments that do not require high-resolution low Q data. In these cases, the straight guide will
deliver a large beam divergence across the full band.
We have developed a conceptual design for a dual-guide system for the EQD, as shown in Fig. 7. This de-
sign requires a custom monolith insert with a wide horizontal opening to ensure sufficient downstream sep-
aration between the two guides for choppers and slits. The simulated beam divergences from this system
match the requirement, as shown in Fig. A.1.
An alternative, cost-saving solution is to use a single elliptical guide coupled with a pair of bandwidth
choppers that have angular-dependent openings. This setup can control the wavelength-dependent beam
divergence, simultaneously optimizing performance for diverse capabilities like SANS, NPD, and NVS.
However, this approach comes at the cost of reduced flexibility in adjusting beam divergences and requires

 19

a feasibility study.

7.2 ENERGY DISCRIMINATION SYSTEM
The current design for the MCS’s inelastic capability is an indirect-geometry spectrometer with a large
array of crystal analyzers. This approach faces two main challenges: the limited supply of the preferred
analyzer material, highly oriented pyrolytic graphite (HOPG), and the trade-off between the space re-
quired for the analyzers and the solid angle available for diffraction detectors. A potential alternative is a
direct-geometry design that uses advanced correlation techniques for energy discrimination. This option
would not require analyzers and could use the same detectors for both elastic and inelastic signals (see Ap-
pendix B), however, it requires a feasibility study.

7.3 AI INTEGRATION
A key opportunity lies in integrating modern AI and computational methods to accelerate scientific discov-
ery. These instruments shall be designed to be ready for an autonomous, closed-loop workflow where an
AI agent intelligently controls the experiment, including the sample environment, and analyzes data in real
time to dynamically modify the data collection strategy.
For example, a physics-based predictive model could provide an initial temperature ramp strategy for
studying a chemical reaction. The AI agent would then monitor the live data stream, detecting the emer-
gence of specific vibrational peaks (via NVS) or crystalline phases (via NPD) that signals a reaction inter-
mediate. In response, the agent could autonomously refine the experimental plan. For example, reducing
the temperature ramp rate allows for high-fidelity capture of fast transition kinetics, thereby maximizing
the scientific output of the beamtime.
This will create a powerful feedback loop, where experimental data continuously refines predictive ma-
terials models, which in turn guide more efficient, targeted measurements. This approach will empower
researchers to navigate complex parameter spaces and identify subtle correlations within high-volume
datasets that are intractable with traditional methods.
Moreover, AI integration is crucial for serving a broad user base, particularly the growing number of non-
neutron experts, by automating data reduction and simplifying analysis. These instruments will be de-
signed for AI integration, in contrast to existing facilities where AI is often an add-on. This approach will
fundamentally change the experimental workflow and accelerate the pace of discovery.

7.4 INFRASTRUCTURE

 To ensure maximum scientific productivity, the proposed instruments will be supported by a ro-
 bust experimental ecosystem designed for flexibility, efficiency, and ease of use. A relatively
 small investment in areas such as advanced sample environments, automation, and laboratory
 support can largely enhance beamline productivity.

7.4.1 Sample Environments and Support Laboratories
A comprehensive and modular suite of sample environments will be available to enable a wide variety of
in situ and operando measurements, including high-pressure cells, cryostats, furnaces, and magnetic fields.
The open-geometry design will accommodate various sample types (powders, liquids, solids) and provide
optical access for complementary characterization methods (e.g., UV-VIS, IR, Raman). To support these
experiments, specialized laboratories, such as an electrochemistry lab and gas handling systems, will be
available. The emphasis on modularity and off-line testing will facilitate rapid exchange of sample envi-
ronments, maximizing beamtime utilization.

7.4.2 Integrated Control and Data Analysis
A key aspect of the infrastructure will be an integrated software ecosystem for instrument control, data ac-
quisition, and analysis. This system will be designed from the ground up to support time-resolved, multi-
modal experiments by synchronizing all instrument components and sample environment parameters. Key
features will include:
 • Real-time data processing with advanced visualization tools for immediate feedback.
 • User-friendly interfaces for experimental setup, monitoring, and data analysis.
 • Support for parallel operation of multiple sample environments to maximize beamline efficiency.
The data analysis software will be modular and extensible, incorporating advanced algorithms for data
reduction and analysis. It will be designed to handle the large datasets generated by these instruments and
will be user-friendly for a broad range of expertise, from novice users to data science experts, facilitating
the integration of new analytical techniques, including AI/ML methods.

 8. SUMMARY
This report presents two new STS instrument concepts, the Extended Q-range Diffractometer and Multi-
modal Chemical Spectrometer, to address critical gaps in the chemical sciences, as identified in the 2024
STS Workshop Report. They will enable simultaneous, multiscale characterization of dynamic systems
under realistic in situ and operando conditions.
The EQD will combine SANS and NPD to provide a single-shot view of structural correlations from 0.1
to 1000 Å, capturing kinetic processes by concurrently observing atomic-scale ordering and nanoscale
assembly.
The MCS will uniquely integrate NVS with SANS and NPD, allowing direct correlation of chemical bond-
ing and molecular dynamics with structural evolution across atomic to mesoscopic scales. This capability
is of great value for understanding catalysis, energy materials, and soft matter.
These two concepts fully utilize the unique characteristics of the STS source. Its high brilliance, short
pulses, and low repetition rate will enable studies of tiny samples and rapid kinetic processes, while pro-
viding the broad bandwidth required for multiscale measurements in a single frame. The resulting instru-
ments will offer world-leading capabilities to a broad chemistry community, spanning academia, govern-
ment laboratories, and industry. They are also expected to attract user communities from biochemistry and
planetary science and to promote an AI-driven ecosystem to accelerate materials discovery. R&D in neu-
tron optics and autonomous experimentation can further boost these instruments’ performance.

 APPENDIX B. CORRELATION TECHNIQUES
Conventional direct geometry spectrometer (DGS) instruments typically collect both elastic and inelastic
scattering signals using identical detector banks, but they are limited by a very narrow incident wavelength
band, which significantly reduces their neutron powder diffraction (NPD) capability (utilizing only 1-2%
of the useful flux). While efficiency can be improved by a factor of 4 − 10 at Second Target Station (STS)
with a frame multiplication chopper, enabling simultaneous use of multiple incident beam energies, this
approach remains sub-optimal for NPD.

Figure B.1. Demonstration of the cross-correlation technique used to separate elastic and inelastic
scattering. (a) Raw TOF data are binned according to the time channels of a statistical chopper. (b)
Cross-correlation analysis of this data separates the elastic and inelastic contributions, allowing the
scattering function, S(E), to be reconstructed from the change in TOF without requiring crystal analyzers.

Correlation techniques enable the simultaneous measurement of both elastic and inelastic channels across
a broad wavelength band, offering several advantages over conventional spectrometer designs. This ap-
proach eliminates the need for dedicated energy analyzers, resolving the common space competition be-
tween NPD detector banks and neutron vibrational spectroscopy (NVS) components in indirect geometry
spectrometers. It also overcomes the narrow wavelength limitations of direct geometry instruments and
provides a substantial flux enhancement compared to frame multiplication techniques. While this method
has been successfully demonstrated for elastic diffuse scattering (e.g., at Corelli [22]), further investigation
is required to fully extend its capabilities to inelastic channels.
