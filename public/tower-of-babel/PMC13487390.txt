Integrative multi-omics reveals comprehensive gut-liver-adipose metabolic changes in peak laying hens with high or low egg production
Authors: Junjie Ma, Kailong Qin, Zhihao Qiao, Zhouzheng Ren, Xiaojun Yang, Yanli Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Eggs serve as an indispensable global nutritional resource, sustaining the economic foundation of the commercial poultry industry. To meet this continuous demand, egg formation involves an exceptionally energy-intensive biological process requiring continuous yolk precursor synthesis, which imposes a massive metabolic burden on laying hens. However, the comprehensive metabolic differences across the gut-liver-adipose axis between peak laying hens with high or low egg production remain incompletely characterized. A total of 180 healthy Hy-Line Brown laying hens (45-week-old) were continuously fed and monitored for production performance over a 6-week period. Following the exclusion of individuals with extremely low egg production (≤10 eggs during the 6-week monitoring period; n = 5) and candidate hens that repeatedly produced unqualified eggs over multiple weeks (n = 20), the remaining hens were ranked by 6-week average laying rate and allocated into FH (high-production hens at 50 weeks of age, n = 15) and FL (low-production hens at 50 weeks of age, n = 14) groups. Initial body weight did not differ significantly between groups (FH: 1936 ± 29.0 g; FL: 1894 ± 48.2 g; P-value = 0.446). We integrated transcriptomic, untargeted metabolomic, targeted bile acid metabolomics, and microbiome (16S rRNA and metagenomic) profiles to characterize comprehensive metabolic changes across the gut-liver-adipose axis associated with divergent egg-production phenotypes. The results showed that: (1) FH hens exhibited higher serum APOB and lower conjugated bile acids (TCDCA, TCA, and THDCA), with hepatic upregulation of FASN, PPARA, CPT1A, and VTG1 along with downregulation of CYP7A1, CYP7B1, CYP8B1, and CYP27A1; (2) intersecting module hub genes (MHGs) with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified 354 upregulated and 299 downregulated core genes, with EEF2 identified as the primary hepatic downregulated hub gene; (3) in abdominal fat, GSEA revealed significant enrichment in fatty acid transport (NES = 1.54), long-chain fatty acid metabolic process (NES = 1.39), and steroid hormone biosynthesis (NES = 1.85), accompanied by significant downregulation of ANGPTL4 and upregulation of HSD3B1, VTG1, VTG2, and VTG3; (4) ileal mucosal transcriptomics identified 619 DEGs (502 upregulated), with GSEA highlighting enrichment in cell junction organization (NES = 1.52) and tube morphogenesis (NES = 1.39), which were further categorized into functional modules including enteric synaptic signaling, epithelial adhesion, mucosal vascularization, and tissue renewal; (5) the functional profile of the ileal microbiota in FH hens showed enrichment of functions related to complex carbohydrate degradation and carbohydrate-binding modules, with keystone taxa including Blautia and Bifidobacterium associated with production and lipid markers. Collectively, these findings suggest that high egg production during the peak laying period is associated with coordinated metabolic differences across the liver, abdominal fat tissue, and intestine. The observed profiles included reduced hepatic translation-related and primary bile acid synthesis-related signatures, adipose endocrine-related changes and microbial functional potential related to carbohydrate utilization and antioxidant-related functional potential. These findings provide candidate multi-omics features for precision nutritional strategies and genetic improvement in commercial poultry.

Introduction

Eggs are widely recognized as a high-quality source of animal protein and essential nutrients, making them a cornerstone of the global poultry industry. As a profoundly energy-intensive biological process, egg formation imposes a massive metabolic burden on the host, necessitating a continuous and high rate of systemic lipid biosynthesis (van Eck et al., 2026). The yolk constitutes approximately 31% of the total egg weight but contains nearly all the lipids and the majority of the egg’s energy content (Nys and Guyot, 2011). Accordingly, the coordinated assembly and deposition of yolk components represent an important priority during the laying cycle. The continuous synthesis of yolk precursors, such as vitellogenin (VTG) and very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDL), is a cornerstone for maintaining stable and high laying performance (Walzem et al., 1999). Therefore, elucidating how yolk precursor synthesis is coordinated with systemic lipid metabolism in hens with high egg production is critical for developing nutritional interventions to extend the peak laying period.

To meet the host’s sustained reproductive demand, the liver functions as the central metabolic organ for de novo lipogenesis and vitellogenesis (Guo et al., 2024). While hepatic lipid dysregulation is known to impair egg production, the intrinsic metabolic differences between healthy high- and low-production hens remain unclear. Interestingly, abdominal fat tissue plays a complex yet vital role in this process. Although excessive fat deposition is generally associated with reduced reproductive efficiency and metabolic imbalance (Ma et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2018), reduced fat deposition in high-production hens may instead be associated with differences in adipose lipid turnover and substrate utilization. Enhanced lipolysis and adipose-derived endocrine factors have been shown to supply important substrates that support hepatic yolk synthesis and stimulate laying performance (Wang et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2022). Therefore, exploring the metabolic coordination between the liver and abdominal fat tissue is essential for understanding the biological characteristics associated with divergent egg-production phenotypes.

Crucially, this intensive multi-organ lipid metabolism is closely linked to the gut–liver axis through bile acid (BA) metabolism. BAs are synthesized in the liver from cholesterol; therefore, bile acid synthesis and yolk-directed lipid production may represent competing hepatic cholesterol-utilization pathways (Li et al., 2014). Beyond their classical role in lipid digestion, BAs function as signaling molecules that regulate lipid homeostasis (Zhao et al., 2024). Recent studies have demonstrated that specific microbiota–BA interactions significantly influence host fat deposition and lipid mobilization (Wang et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2023; Zha et al., 2024). Moreover, hepatic lipid imbalance is frequently accompanied by altered expression of BA synthesis enzymes and disruptions in systemic BA profiles (Zhang et al., 2025a). Together, these findings indicate that modulation of BA metabolism and gut microbial composition may be associated with hepatic lipid homeostasis under increased reproductive demand.

During the peak laying period, the number and weight of eggs produced account for over 60% of the total lifetime yield. The persistency of this peak phase directly determines the total egg production and economic benefits of the entire production cycle, while indirectly reflecting the subsequent laying performance and health status of the hens. However, the sustainability of the peak period varies considerably in commercial poultry farming (Francoeur et al., 2025). Therefore, understanding the biological basis of sustained high production at this stage is essential for extending peak laying persistency. To address this, we established a strictly screened cohort of healthy high- and low-production hens at 45 weeks of age, a critical window of peak metabolic demand (Li et al., 2025). This study integrates transcriptomics, untargeted metabolomics, targeted BA profiling, 16S rRNA sequencing, and metagenomics to characterize comprehensive metabolic differences across the liver, abdominal adipose tissue, ileal mucosa, and ileal microbiota. The primary objective was to identify multi-organ metabolic changes associated with divergent egg-production phenotypes under standardized rearing conditions.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics

All experimental protocols and animal handling procedures in the study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Northwest A&F University (Permit Number: DK2021005).

Experimental design and sample collection

The experimental flow was illustrated in Fig. S1A. A total of 180 healthy Hy-Line Brown hens (45-week-old; initial laying rate: 91.88% ± 1.244%; initial body weight: 1910 g ± 11.5 g) were selected for this study. All hens were housed individually in a standard environmentally controlled laying-hen facility, exposed to 16 h of artificial light per day, and provided free access to feed and water. The basal diet was formulated to meet the nutritional requirements recommended by the Hy-Line Brown Commercial Management Guide (2022). Its ingredient composition and nutrient levels are shown in Table 1. During the 6-week monitoring period (45 to 50 weeks of age), egg number, egg weight, and the occurrence of unqualified eggs were recorded daily. Unqualified eggs included broken eggs, white eggs, dirty eggs, spotted eggs, and malformed eggs. Feed consumption was recorded weekly to calculate the average daily feed intake (ADFI) and feed conversion ratio (FCR). Before final group assignment, a stepwise selection procedure was applied to select physiologically representative high- and low-production hens and to avoid including hens with extremely low production or evident egg-quality abnormalities (Fig. S1B). First, hens producing ≤ 10 eggs during the 6-week monitoring period were categorized as Excluded_Low (n = 5) because their production performance was markedly below the expected level during the peak laying period. Second, during the final selection of extreme production phenotypes, hens that repeatedly produced unqualified eggs over multiple weeks were excluded from the candidate selection pool and categorized as Excluded_Quality (n = 20). After these exclusions, the remaining hens were ranked according to their 6-week average laying rate, and the top 15 hens and bottom 14 hens were assigned to the FH (high-production hens at 50 weeks of age) and FL (low-production hens at 50 weeks of age) groups, respectively. Investigators were not blinded during group assignment because final selection was based on predefined production records and egg-quality screening rather than subjective allocation. The distributions of 6-week average laying rate and total egg number for all 180 hens are shown in Fig. S1C and Fig. S1D, respectively. The two-group design was used as a discovery-oriented comparison of divergent production phenotypes under standardized rearing conditions. Selection from the upper and lower ends of the laying-rate distribution increased phenotypic contrast and facilitated the identification of molecular, metabolic, and microbial differences associated with egg-production phenotype. Following a 12-h overnight fast (with free access to water), blood samples were collected from the wing vein of the selected hens. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 10 min, aliquoted into sterile microcentrifuge tubes, and immediately stored at −80°C for subsequent biochemical and bile acid profile analyses. Subsequently, all selected hens were euthanized via cervical dislocation for immediate tissue dissection. The liver, abdominal fat, ileal contents, and ileal mucosa were rapidly harvested. All collected tissues and intestinal contents were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until further analysis.

Egg quality detection

At the end of the 6-week trial, 3 intact eggs per selected hen were collected for quality assessment. For external traits, eggshell strength and thickness (averaged from the blunt end, pointed end, and equator) were determined using a texture analyzer (EFG-0503; Robotmation Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and a dial pipe gauge (ETG-1061; Robotmation Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), respectively. For internal traits, albumen height was measured with a multifunction egg quality analyzer (EMT-5200; Robotmation Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Subsequently, the yolk, albumen, and eggshell were manually separated and individually weighed using an electronic balance. The mean value of the 3 eggs was used as the observation for each hen.

Measurements of serum biochemical parameters

Frozen serum samples were gently thawed at 4°C prior to analysis. Serum biochemical indices were quantitatively determined using the BIOBASE BK-400 automatic biochemical analyzer (Shandong Biobase Biological Industry Co., Ltd., Jinan, Shandong, China). To comprehensively evaluate the systemic metabolic status, a diverse panel of parameters was measured. These included markers of liver function: alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST); comprehensive lipid and lipoprotein profiles: apolipoprotein A1 (APOA1), apolipoprotein B (APOB), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c); as well as the carbohydrate metabolism indicator, glucose (GLU). Serum oxidative-redox indices were determined using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, including malondialdehyde (MDA, Cat. No. A003-1-2) and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC, Cat. No. A015-2-1).

Serum bile acid metabolomics

Serum bile acid profiles were quantified via a targeted metabolomics approach utilizing an ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) platform (ACQUITY UPLC-Xevo TQ-S; Waters Corp., Milford, MA). The analysis was outsourced to Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). For metabolite extraction, serum aliquots were vigorously vortexed with ice-cold methanol spiked with isotopic internal standards. Subsequently, 50% pre-chilled aqueous methanol was added to the mixture. After centrifugation at 4,000 × g (4°C), the resulting supernatants were carefully transferred to autosampler vials and sealed for instrumental analysis. The UPLC-MS/MS operational parameters were set in accordance with a previous study (Deng et al., 2023). Quality control (QC) samples were incorporated throughout the analytical batch to monitor system stability. The acquired raw data were processed using QuanMET software v2.0 (Metabolo-Profile, Shanghai, China) for peak detection, integration, and absolute quantification. To ensure data accuracy, metabolite signal intensities were normalized based on the best-matched internal standard method, and statistical outliers were eliminated utilizing z-score filtering. All identified bile acids were confirmed against authentic reference standards, satisfying the criteria for the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) confidence level 1.

Transcriptome

Total RNA was extracted from the snap-frozen tissues of the liver, abdominal fat, and ileal mucosa using TRIZOL kit protocol (AG21101; Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd., Changsha, Hunan, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol, and the quality and concentration of the RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The qualified RNA was used to construct sequencing libraries with the Illumina TruSeq® Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA) and the sequencing was performed by Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. using an Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Raw data were initially processed using fastp software (Chen et al., 2018) with a minimum read length threshold of 150 bp. Comprehensive summaries of the sequencing data yield and quality control metrics for the transcriptomes of the liver, abdominal fat, and ileal mucosa are detailed in Table S1, Table S2, and Table S3, respectively. The resulting clean reads were then mapped to the chicken reference genome (GRCg7b, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genome/111) using HISAT2 (version 2.2.1) (Kim et al., 2019). Gene expression levels were quantified by counting the reads mapped to each gene utilizing HTSeq (version 2.0.5) (Putri et al., 2022). Differential gene expression analysis between the FH and FL groups across the three tissues was performed using the DESeq2 R package (version 1.50.2) (Love et al., 2014), which was used for both sample clustering and identification of significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (|log2 fold change| ≥ 1 and P-value < 0.05).

WGCNA

To systematically identify co-expressed gene modules associated with hepatic metabolic adaptation and laying performance, a weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed using the WGCNAshiny plugin embedded in TBtools-II software (version 2.119) (Chen et al., 2023). Briefly, following the determination of an optimal soft-thresholding power (β = 14) to ensure a scale-free network topology, the co-expression network was constructed based on a topological overlap matrix (TOM) (Fig. S2). Dynamic hierarchical tree cutting was employed for module detection with a minimum module size set to 30. Highly similar modules were subsequently merged using a clustering height cut-off of 0.10. To evaluate module-trait relationships, key host phenotypic and metabolic parameters were integrated as continuous trait variables, including laying rate (LR), egg number from 45 to 50 weeks (EN45.50), total egg weight (TEW), feed conversion ratio (FCR), serum apolipoprotein B (APOB), and hepatic conjugated bile acids (TCA, TCDCA, and THDCA). Pearson correlation coefficients between the module eigengenes and these target traits were calculated to identify the most biologically relevant modules for downstream hub gene extraction.

Liver untargeted metabolomics

Untargeted metabolomic profiling of the liver tissues was performed by Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Briefly, snap-frozen liver samples were homogenized in an ice-cold methanol extraction buffer spiked with specific internal standards. The homogenate was vortexed and centrifuged to precipitate proteins, and the resulting supernatant was carefully transferred to autosampler vials for subsequent instrumental analysis. The metabolic extracts were analyzed utilizing an Ultra-High Performance Liquid Chromatography (UHPLC) system coupled to a Q-Exactive high-resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Raw data processing, which included feature extraction, peak identification/alignment, and spectral library matching, was conducted in accordance with the established protocols (Li et al., 2017). Molecular mass validation was rigorously performed utilizing exact m/z values derived from redundant ion peaks. For multivariate statistical analysis, Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) was implemented using the Ropls R package to mitigate inter-group variability. Differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs) were identified from the list of primary substances in the sample and screened according to the preset P-value and variable importance projection (VIP) thresholds in the statistical test (P-value < 0.05, VIP > 1, |fold change| > 2). Finally, to characterize the biological functions of these DAMs, pathway enrichment analysis was conducted by mapping the annotated metabolites to canonical metabolic pathways within the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (http://www.kegg.jp/).

Functional enrichment analysis

The Gene Ontology (GO) term and KEGG pathway analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package (version 4.18.4) (Wu et al., 2021). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the Broad Institute's GSEA platform (http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/). Terms with P-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RT-qPCR

RT-qPCR analysis was performed on RNA samples derived from both liver and abdominal fat tissues. The extracted RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA utilizing the Evo M-MLV RT Master Mix kit (AG11728, Agbio, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Subsequently, quantitative PCR amplification was performed using an FQD-96A Real-Time PCR System (Hangzhou Bioer Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China) with the SYBR Green Pro Taq™ II kit (Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd., Changsha, Hunan, China). To guarantee the analytical specificity of the amplified products and rule out primer-dimer formation, a melting curve analysis was systematically conducted at the end of each PCR run. β-actin was used as the internal reference for subsequent normalization, with primer sequences detailed in Table S4. Following Livak's method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001), the relative fold change of target genes was calculated using the 2−△△Ct method.

16S rRNA analysis

Total genomic DNA from ileal contents was extracted using the MagBeads FastDNA Kit (MP Biomedicals, Irvine, CA) and assessed for quality via a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and agarose gel electrophoresis. The 16S rRNA V3–V4 hypervariable regions were amplified using specific primers (338F: 5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′ and 806R: 5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). Following purification with AMPure XP Beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA), paired-end sequencing was performed by Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China, on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Raw sequence data were processed using the QIIME2 framework (version 2024.5). High-resolution Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) were generated using the DADA2 plugin for quality filtering, denoising, and chimera removal. Taxonomic assignment of ASVs was performed using a pre-trained Naive Bayes classifier against the gg2_2022_10 database. To identify specific differential biomarkers between the FH and FL groups, Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis was conducted with the following threshold: linear discriminant analysis (LDA) > 2 and P-value < 0.05. Furthermore, the random forest machine-learning algorithm was utilized to corroborate and predict the core discriminatory taxa at the genus and species levels.

Metagenomic analysis

Raw metagenomic reads from the ileal contents were quality-filtered using fastp (version 0.23.2) and depleted of host DNA via Minimap2 (version 2.24-r1122) alignment against the chicken reference genome (GRCg7b). Comprehensive statistics regarding the sequencing data yield, quality control metrics, and host DNA depletion efficiencies are summarized in Table S5. Subsequently, the unmapped microbial reads were de novo assembled into contigs utilizing MEGAHIT (version 1.1.2). Only high-quality contigs with a minimum length of 300 bp were retained for downstream analysis. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted via MetaGeneMark (version 3.38) and subsequently clustered using MMseqs2 (version 13-45111; sequence identity ≥ 90%, alignment coverage ≥ 90%) to construct a non-redundant gene catalog. Gene abundances were quantified using Salmon software (version 1.9.0) and normalized as Transcripts Per Kilobase Million (TPM) to correct variations in sequencing depth and gene length.

Taxonomic classification of the non-redundant genes was performed using MMseqs2 against the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB, Release 207). To comprehensively decode functional capacities, representative sequences were systematically annotated against the KEGG, CAZy, and TCDB databases. To assess the ecological structure and metabolic complexity of the microbiome, alpha diversity and beta diversity indices were calculated based on both the taxonomic and functional profiles using the vegan package (version 2.7-3) in R. Furthermore, to pinpoint core discriminatory species associated with divergent laying performance, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was applied to the species-level abundance matrix. Specifically, candidate species were strictly filtered by a statistical significance of P-value < 0.05, and the top 20 most significant taxa were subsequently ranked and selected as key biomarkers based on their robust predictive power (Area Under the Curve, AUC > 0.8).

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as mean ± SEM (Standard error of the mean). Comparisons between the FH and FL groups were analyzed with the unpaired Student's t-test, and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Differences were considered significant at P-value < 0.05, whereas 0.05 ≤ P-value < 0.10 was considered indicative of a statistical trend. Statistical significance is indicated by *P-value < 0.05, **P-value < 0.01.

Consistent with the metabolomic profiles, principal component analysis (PCA) of the transcriptome data (Fig. 1C) exhibited clear separation between the two groups. Differential expression analysis identified 2,061 DEGs, including 1,380 upregulated and 681 downregulated genes in the FH group (Fig. 1D). To explore the relationship between these broad transcriptional shifts and specific phenotypes, WGCNA was conducted. Four modules exhibiting the most significant correlations with the phenotypic traits were selected. Specifically, the yellow (r = 0.81, P-value < 0.001), red (r = 0.74, P-value < 0.001), and tan (r = 0.70, P-value < 0.001) modules showed strong positive correlations, whereas the turquoise module (r = -0.83, P-value < 0.001) showed a strong negative correlation with laying performance (Fig. 1E).

Module hub genes (MHGs) were subsequently screened based on gene significance (GS) and module membership (MM), yielding 743 positively correlated and 681 negatively correlated genes. By intersecting these MHGs with the DEGs, 354 upregulated and 299 downregulated core genes were identified (Fig. 1F). Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis of the downregulated core genes revealed a highly connected network, with EEF2 identified as the principal hub gene (Fig. 1G). Conversely, the upregulated PPI network presented a more decentralized structure comprising multiple interacting nodes (Fig. 1H). RT-qPCR was performed to assess the expression patterns of selected core genes identified from the transcriptomic analysis. The relative mRNA expression levels of EEF2 (Fig. 1I) and H6PD (Fig. 1J) were significantly decreased in the liver of FH hens, consistent with the RNA-seq results. However, EIF5B (Fig. 1K) and EIF5A (Fig. 1L), which were identified within the upregulated transcriptomic network, showed significant downregulation by RT-qPCR, exhibiting trends opposite to those observed in the RNA-seq data. Therefore, EIF5B and EIF5A were not considered validated targets and were not used for downstream interpretation of the upregulated network or translation-related changes.

For the hepatic transcriptome, GO analysis (Fig. 2B) indicated that the DEGs were significantly enriched in biological processes associated with energy turnover and stress responses, such as ATP generation from ADP, lipid homeostasis, steroid metabolic processes, and superoxide metabolic processes. Guided by the functional enrichment results, the expression levels of key genes involved in hepatic lipid and energy metabolism were quantified via RT-qPCR. Compared to the FL group, FH hens exhibited a significant decrease in the relative mRNA expression of ChREBP (Fig. 2D). In contrast, the expression of FASN, a key gene involved in de novo lipogenesis, was significantly higher in FH hens (Fig. 2E). The relative mRNA expression levels of PPARA and CPT1A, which are associated with fatty acid oxidation, were significantly lower in the FH group (Fig. 2F and G). Furthermore, the expression of VTG1, encoding the major yolk precursor protein, was significantly upregulated (Fig. 2H). The relative mRNA expressions of four critical cytochrome P450 enzymes responsible for bile acid synthesis (CYP7A1, CYP7B1, CYP8B1, and CYP27A1) were all significantly downregulated in the FH group (Fig. 2I-L). Concurrently, several hepatic antioxidant-related genes were downregulated in FH hens, including the key antioxidant regulator Nrf2, and its downstream effector genes SOD2, SOD3, GPX3, GSTA3, and NOX1 (Fig. 2M-R).

Transcriptomic profiling of abdominal fat identifies changes related to lipid metabolism and steroidogenesis

To investigate transcriptomic alterations in the abdominal fat tissue, RNA sequencing was performed. Although PCA did not completely separate the FH and FL groups (Fig. 3A), differential expression analysis identified 978 DEGs, comprising 906 upregulated and 72 downregulated genes in the FH hens (Fig. 3B). At the KO level, functional alpha diversity was generally elevated in the FH group (Fig. 4F). Discussion

Sustaining egg production during the peak laying period is a major economic objective in the commercial poultry industry. The period between 40 and 50 weeks of age represents an important production stage during which cumulative reproductive and metabolic demands may contribute to phenotypic divergence among laying hens (Li et al., 2025). Integrated transcriptomic, metabolomic, targeted bile acid, 16S rRNA, and metagenomic analyses indicated that high and low egg production were associated with comprehensive metabolic changes across the liver, abdominal adipose tissue, ileal mucosa, and ileal microbiota.

Reliable phenotypic stratification was essential for identifying biological characteristics associated with sustained egg production (Reis et al., 2023). Comparisons of divergent production phenotypes have been used in poultry omics studies to identify candidate genes, pathways, and cellular features associated with egg production and other quantitative reproductive traits (Mishra et al., 2020; Mu et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025b). In the present study, continuous production monitoring and the exclusion of hens with extremely low laying rates or recurrent production of poor-quality eggs reduced potential interference from overt health abnormalities. The FH and FL groups represented relative production extremes within a screened healthy population rather than a comparison between healthy high-production hens and pathologically affected low-production hens. This design increased phenotypic contrast and facilitated the identification of candidate molecular, metabolic, and microbial differences associated with divergent egg-production phenotypes.

The relationships among feed intake, body weight, and egg production further characterized the FH phenotype. FH hens showed only a modest increase in feed intake, whereas body weight remained unchanged. In contrast, cumulative egg production and total egg mass were greater, while average egg weight and FCR were lower. This pattern indicates that the greater cumulative egg output primarily reflected a higher laying frequency rather than the production of larger eggs and was not accompanied by additional body-weight gain. Recent evidence suggests that laying persistency depends on the balance among nutrient intake, egg-mass output, and body composition rather than feed intake alone (Anene et al., 2021; Lyons et al., 2023; van Eck et al., 2024). Taken together, the combination of modestly higher feed intake, unchanged body weight, greater egg output, and lower FCR in FH hens was consistent with greater apparent productive utilization of dietary nutrients while body-weight homeostasis was maintained.

Continuous yolk deposition places substantial cumulative demands on hepatic lipid synthesis and transport (Li et al., 2014). Consistent with this requirement, serum APOB concentration was higher in FH hens, supporting a greater capacity for lipoprotein-mediated transport of yolk-directed lipids (Chen et al., 2025; Song et al., 2023). Concomitantly, the reduction in several circulating conjugated bile acids further suggested that hepatic lipid and cholesterol metabolism differed between FH and FL hens. Given the liver's dual role as the major site of primary bile acid synthesis and the central hub for APOB-mediated lipid distribution (Kiriyama and Nochi, 2021; Yang et al., 2026b), these coordinated phenotypic and metabolic differences indicate that hepatic metabolic changes may be an important feature associated with sustained high egg production. FH hens showed increased expression of FASN, PPARA, CPT1A, and VTG1, linking de novo lipogenesis, fatty acid utilization, and yolk precursor synthesis. These transcriptional changes were accompanied by altered hepatic concentrations of several free fatty acids, including palmitoleic acid and 2-trans-hexadecenoic acid, and a lower acetyl-CoA concentration. The concurrent lipogenic and fatty acid oxidation-related transcriptional changes are physiologically plausible because continuous yolk formation may require both the synthesis of new fatty acids and the rapid utilization or remodeling of existing lipid substrates.

Importantly, this hepatic state should be distinguished from pathological lipid accumulation. Excessive free fatty acid exposure can induce lipid deposition, oxidative injury, and ferroptosis-related responses through the ROS/GPX4 pathway in primary chicken hepatocytes (Cheng et al., 2024). In the present study, FH hens exhibited increased APOB concentration and altered expression of genes related to fatty acid utilization, lipid transport, and yolk precursor synthesis. Serum ALT, AST, and ALP did not differ between the groups, providing no biochemical indication of increased hepatocellular injury in FH hens. Therefore, the observed hepatic profile was more consistent with production-associated lipid processing and transport than with overt pathological steatosis.

Beyond lipid metabolism, translation-related transcriptional changes may represent another aspect of hepatic adaptation to sustained production. EEF2 was identified as a core downregulated hub gene in the protein-protein interaction network. Given that EEF2 mediates ribosomal translocation during translation elongation (Leprivier et al., 2013; Milicevic et al., 2024), its downregulation may reflect an altered transcriptional change related to translation elongation in FH hens. However, EIF5A and EIF5B showed opposite expression patterns between RNA-seq and RT-qPCR and were therefore not considered validated targets. Accordingly, the present results do not demonstrate suppression of global hepatic protein synthesis, and the translation-related interpretation was restricted to the consistently observed change in EEF2 expression.

The organ-specific nature of the metabolic response was also evident in the redox-related results. Serum T-AOC was higher in FH hens, whereas serum MDA concentration did not differ between groups, indicating greater circulating total antioxidant capacity without a detectable reduction in systemic lipid peroxidation. In the liver, several antioxidant-related genes were downregulated, whereas Nrf2, GPX3, and SOD3 were upregulated in abdominal fat tissue. These contrasting patterns indicate tissue-specific differences in redox-related transcriptional responses, rather than generalized enhancement or suppression of antioxidant defense.

Alongside these hepatic and redox-related changes, abdominal fat tissue displayed transcriptional changes related to lipid turnover and reproductive metabolism. Although previous studies have described abdominal fat as a passive energy reservoir associated with reduced reproductive efficiency (Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2018), the present results indicated that abdominal fat tissue may participate in metabolic processes associated with sustained egg production. Increased HSD3B1 expression suggested an altered steroidogenesis-related transcriptional capacity because HSD3B1 participates in steroid hormone biosynthesis (Wadood et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2022). Furthermore, although VTG is usually considered to be synthesized exclusively in the liver (Tata and Smith, 1979; Wallace, 1985), white adipose tissue in oviparous vertebrates possesses the endocrine capability for ectopic vitellogenesis (Tingaud-Sequeira et al., 2012). Collectively, these findings suggest that abdominal fat tissue may represent a metabolically engaged component of reproductive metabolism rather than merely a passive energy reservoir.

Another prominent feature of FH hens was the lower concentrations of TCDCA, TCA, and THDCA. Microbial choloylglycine hydrolase abundance and ileal SLC10A2 expression did not differ significantly between groups, providing little support for enhanced microbial deconjugation or altered ileal bile acid reabsorption as the principal explanation for these reductions. In contrast, hepatic expression of CYP7A1, CYP7B1, CYP8B1, and CYP27A1 was lower in FH hens, indicating a reduced bile acid synthesis-related transcriptional state. Cholesterol serves as a common precursor for bile acid synthesis, lipoprotein assembly, and steroidogenesis (Sun et al., 2023). Serum total cholesterol remained unchanged, whereas serum APOB concentration and hepatic VTG1 expression were higher in FH hens. This coordinated pattern was compatible with altered hepatic cholesterol utilization involving reduced bile acid synthesis-related activity and stronger yolk-directed lipoprotein transport. Excessive accumulation of conjugated bile acids and dysregulated CYP expression have been associated with hepatic lipotoxicity and fat deposition in poultry (Liu et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024b). Therefore, the lower conjugated bile acid concentrations in FH hens may be associated with reduced bile acid-related metabolic pressure. Beyond hepatic metabolism, bile acid composition is closely connected with intestinal microbial activity and host signaling. Functional evidence in chickens showed that Parabacteroides goldsteinii converted TCDCA to CDCA and activated FXR signaling, thereby increasing mucin production and improving mucosal barrier function (Yang et al., 2026a). This finding provides a relevant mechanistic context for the biological connection between microbial bile acid transformation and host intestinal signaling, although this pathway was not directly demonstrated in the present study.

The ileal analyses further identified distinct mucosal transcriptional and microbial features associated with the FH phenotype. Although PCA of the ileal mucosal transcriptome and KO-level microbial beta-diversity did not show clear separation between FH and FL hens, this does not indicate an absence of intestinal differences. Instead, the enrichment of cell junction organization and tube morphogenesis reflected transcriptional changes related to epithelial junction organization and mucosal structural remodeling, rather than direct evidence of enhanced intestinal barrier function. Likewise, the enrichment of microbial functions related to complex carbohydrate degradation, carbohydrate-binding modules, and glutamate metabolism indicated selected microbial functional potential, rather than directly demonstrated energy or antioxidant support to the host. The enrichment of Blautia and Bifidobacterium may indicate greater microbiota-associated potential for short-chain fatty acid production (He et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2021; Ye et al., 2023). These microbiome-derived SCFAs have been implicated in strengthening the intestinal mucosal barrier and maintaining gut health, supporting the evidence that gut microbiota are essential for protecting the host from physiological stress (Yang et al., 2024a). Taken together, the integrated analyses identified hepatic lipid synthesis, fatty acid utilization, APOB-associated transport, and yolk precursor production as prominent metabolic changes associated with the FH phenotype (Fig. 6). Several considerations should be noted when interpreting these findings. The cross-sectional comparison of two divergent production groups identifies phenotype-associated molecular and metabolic differences but does not establish causality or characterize intermediate responses across the full production distribution. In addition, transcript and steady-state metabolite abundances do not directly quantify metabolic flux. Future longitudinal and targeted intervention studies will be useful for testing the biological relationships identified here.

Conclusion

This study identified comprehensive metabolic changes across the gut-liver-adipose axis of peak laying hens with high or low egg production. The FH phenotype was associated with hepatic lipid synthesis, fatty acid utilization, APOB-associated lipid transport, and yolk precursor synthesis, together with downregulation of EEF2 and multiple primary bile acid synthesis-related genes. Abdominal adipose tissue exhibited transcriptional changes related to lipid metabolism and steroidogenesis, whereas the ileal mucosa and microbiota showed selected changes related to tissue organization, carbohydrate utilization, and glutamate metabolism. These findings provide a multi-organ framework and candidate molecular and microbial targets for future nutritional and genetic improvement of laying performance.
