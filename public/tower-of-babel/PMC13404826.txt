Exclusive breastfeeding and its determinants among infants attending a tertiary care hospital serving an underprivileged population in Rawalpindi, Pakistan: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mobeen Tabassum, Ammara Ayub, Fahim Ahmed Subhani, Faisal Ajaz Awan, Bilal Ahmad, Irfan Ullah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo determine the pattern and determinants of exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) among infants visiting a tertiary care hospital of underprivileged population.DesignCross-sectional study.StudyThe Department of Pediatrics, Pakistan Railway Hospital, Rawalpindi, Pakistan.MethodsA total of 385 infants, born with gestational age between 34–42 weeks, were analysed. Data collection was performed through a structured questionnaire designed to capture relevant demographic and clinical details. The pattern of breastfeeding (BF), including EBF, formula milk, animal milk (cow/buffalo/goat milk) or mixed methods was determined. The collected data were entered and analysed using IBM-SPSS V.26.ResultsIn a total of 385 infants, EBF was reported in 206 (53.5%) infants, while 36 (9.4%) were exclusively formula-fed (FF), 122 (31.7%) received a combination of BF and formula feeding (mixed FF and BF), 14 (3.6%) were given a mix of BF and animal milk, and 7 (1.8%) received BF along with water. Age (p=0.033), birth weight (p=0.050), mode of delivery, neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission (p=0.028), BF initiation timing (p<0.001), pre-lacteal feeding (p=0.021) and immunisation status (p=0.002) influenced EBF patterns. Maternal education (p=0.001) and antenatal counselling (p<0.001) had significant associations with BF patterns. Significant differences were observed in awareness of the appropriate duration of EBF (p=0.022).ConclusionsDespite the well-established benefits of EBF, only around half of the infants were exclusively breastfed, highlighting the need for targeted interventions. Introduction

Breastfeeding (BF) is a fundamental practice for ensuring optimal infant health and survival, playing a crucial role in reducing morbidity and mortality in early childhood. The WHO and the UNICEF strongly recommend exclusive BF (EBF) for the first 6 months of life, followed by continued BF along with appropriate complementary feeding up to 2 years of age or beyond.1 Around 1.4 million babies die due to suboptimal milk intake while BF is estimated to reduce neonatal mortality by 98%, infantile mortality 95%, and mortality under 5 years by 94%.2 3 Despite its well-established benefits, global adherence to EBF remains suboptimal, with fewer than half of infants under 6 months receiving EBF.4

In Pakistan, the Pakistan Demographic and Health Survey 2017–2018 reported that only 20% of children born in the last 2 years were breastfed within the first hour of birth, and 48% of infants under 6 months received EBF.5 The median duration of EBF was found to be only 1.6 months, significantly shorter than the recommended 6 months.5 Similar disparities exist in South Asian countries showing EBF rates between 43% and 75%.6 In contrast, high-income countries have much lower rates, with the USA reporting only 25.8% of infants exclusively breastfed at 6 months.7 In Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province of Pakistan, the prevalence of EBF among infants aged 0–6 months was found to be 61.2%, whereas in Sindh province, early initiation of BF was reported in 68% of infants, but 32% received pre-lacteal feeds such as honey, herbal teas or formula.8 9

Infants who have EBF are less likely to develop diarrhoea, respiratory infections and fever, which remain leading causes of infant mortality in low-income settings.10 BF is linked to enhanced cognitive development, with a meta-analysis showing that breastfed children have higher IQ scores compared with non-breastfed counterparts.11 For mothers, BF reduces the risk of postpartum haemorrhage, type 2 diabetes, breast cancer and ovarian cancer.12 13 A review estimated that increasing BF rates could prevent 823 000 child deaths and 20 000 maternal deaths annually.14 This study aims to determine the pattern and determinants of EBF among infants visiting Railway Hospital, Rawalpindi, Pakistan, a tertiary care hospital of an underprivileged population. It was hypothesised that there is significant variation in the patterns of EBF among infants of underprivileged populations. It is crucial to evaluate BF patterns in different healthcare settings to identify gaps and opportunities for intervention. The results of this study are thought to help in designing targeted interventions to address key barriers to EBF and improve maternal and child health outcomes in underprivileged areas of developing countries.

Materials and methods

Study design

Analytical, cross-sectional study.

Setting

The Department of Pediatrics, Pakistan Railway Hospital, Rawalpindi, Pakistan, during October 2024 to March 2025. As this hospital serves an underprivileged population, findings from this study may provide valuable insights into the existing BF trends and inform policy recommendations to enhance BF promotion programmes.

Participants

Eligibility assessment

Eligibility was assessed at enrolment through caregiver interview and review of available records, including birth records, immunisation cards, outpatient notes or neonatal discharge summaries where available. Infant age, gestational age and relevant medical history were verified from documented records whenever possible; otherwise, caregiver report was used.

Inclusion criteria

The study included infants aged 0–12 months who presented to Pakistan Railway Hospital, Rawalpindi, for routine well-baby visits, immunisation or minor self-limiting illnesses during the study period. Eligible infants were those born at a gestational age of 34–42 completed weeks. Only infants whose biological mothers or primary caregivers were available to provide detailed feeding history and maternal/perinatal information.

Exclusion criteria

Infants were excluded if they had major congenital anomalies or congenital disorders likely to interfere with feeding, sucking, swallowing, growth or maternal feeding choice. Infants with known metabolic disorders in which BF is contraindicated, such as galactosemia, were excluded. Infants with lactose intolerance, chronic systemic illnesses or medical conditions likely to alter normal feeding practices were also excluded, including congenital heart disease, chronic lung disease, chronic kidney disease, chronic neurological impairment or any other significant chronic illness requiring prolonged medical care or special feeding support. These exclusions were assessed by caregiver history and review of available medical records at the time of enrolment. Infants were also excluded if their caregivers were unable to provide reliable feeding information or if gestational age, birth history or relevant medical history could not be reasonably verified.

Sample size

The sample size was determined using the WHO sample size calculator, considering the proportion of exclusive EBF as 50%,15 with a 95% confidence level and a margin of error of 5%.

Data collection procedures

Infants were recruited using non-probability consecutive sampling. Confidentiality was maintained by assigning unique identification codes to each participant instead of using names, ensuring that personal details remain anonymous. Data were securely stored, accessible only to authorised research personnel. While there were no direct physical risks associated with participation in this study, discussing BF practices may cause emotional distress in some mothers, particularly those who have struggled with BF. To address this, participants were reassured about the confidentiality of their responses, and the study was conducted in a non-judgmental manner. Mothers facing difficulties with BF were provided with relevant information and, if needed, referred to lactation counselling.

Data collection was performed through a structured questionnaire designed to capture relevant demographic and clinical details. The questionnaire included information on the infant’s age, gender, gestational age, birth weight, immunisation status and feeding practices. Maternal data included age, parity, marital status, educational level, employment status, number of antenatal visits and whether antenatal counselling regarding BF was received or not. The questionnaire also documented details of delivery, including mode (spontaneous vaginal delivery, assisted vaginal delivery or caesarean section), place of birth (hospital, clinic or home) and postnatal care status. BF initiation timing, pre-lacteal feeding practices and BF continuity were recorded. The pattern of BF, including EBF, formula milk, animal milk or mixed methods, was determined. Data regarding the determinants of BF discontinuation were also collected, including inadequate milk supply, inadequate infant weight gain, maternal workload, postpartum pain or weakness, postpartum psychiatric illness, lack of family support or lack of knowledge. Mothers’ knowledge regarding colostrum, the recommended duration of EBF, total BF duration and the appropriate time for weaning initiation were also assessed. EBF was labelled as EBF for a minimum period of initial 6 months of life, except rehydration solution or drops/syrups of vitamins, minerals or medicines. Pre-lacteal feeding was defined as the administration of any food or liquid other than breast milk before BF was established after birth.

To minimise selection bias, all eligible infants presenting to the hospital during the study period were consecutively enrolled, regardless of the reason for visit, ensuring a diverse representation of the underprivileged population served by the hospital. To limit recall bias, mothers were encouraged to provide recent or ongoing feeding practices, and questions were clearly worded to facilitate accurate responses. Social desirability bias was reduced by assuring participants of the confidentiality and anonymity of their responses, conducting interviews in a non-judgemental, supportive environment and emphasising that there were no ‘right’ or ‘wrong’ answers. Participants struggling with BF were offered counselling or referral, further fostering openness and reducing the likelihood of biased responses due to perceived judgement. Data confidentiality was maintained by assigning unique identifiers and restricting data access to authorised research staff only, further encouraging honest disclosure.

Statistical analysis

The collected data was entered and analysed using IBM-SPSS V.26. Descriptive statistics were applied, with categorical variables such as sex, socioeconomic status, maternal education and BF status presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables such as age and weight were expressed as mean±SD, or median and IQR. Post-stratification analysis was conducted by cross-tabulating EBF status with effect modifiers to identify significant associations. The χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used to determine statistical significance, with a p value of ≤0.05 considered significant.

Participant and public involvement

Participants were informed about the study objective prior to enrolment. Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this study. Written informed consent was obtained from parents/guardians before enrolment, as applicable.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 385 infants were included in the study (figure 1). EBF was reported in 206 (53.5%) infants, while 36 (9.4%) were exclusively formula-fed (FF), 122 (31.7%) received a combination of BF and formula feeding (mixed FF and BF), 14 (3.6%) were given a mix of BF and animal milk (cow/buffalo/goat milk) and 7 (1.8%) received BF along with water. Association of infants’ characteristics and feeding practice

Age significantly influenced feeding practices (p=0.033) with a higher proportion of mixed feeding with formula milk in younger infants (31.1% in those ≤3 months). Gestational age distribution did not show significant variation among feeding groups (p=0.062). Birth weight showed a significant difference (p=0.050), with 8.3% of FF infants having a birth weight <1.5 kg, compared with 2.4% among those EBF. Caesarean section delivery was significantly associated with lower EBF rates (p=0.003), as only 37.9% of caesarean-born infants were EBF, compared with 62.1% of those born vaginally. Neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission at birth was higher among non-EBF infants (p=0.028), with 28.6% of those receiving a mix of BF and animal milk requiring NICU care. Breastfeeding initiation timing was significantly associated with feeding status (p<0.001), as 47.1% of EBF infants were initiated on BF within the first hour, compared with only 2.8% among FF infants. Pre-lacteal feeding was reported in 64.1% of EBF infants, 70.5% of mixed FF and BF infants, and 85.7% of BF and water-fed infants (p=0.021). Immunisation coverage was significantly lower in FF infants, with 22.2% either partially or non-immunised (p=0.002). Maternal characteristics and their association with feeding practices

Maternal age was not significantly associated with feeding status (p=0.572), but education level was strongly linked to EBF (p=0.001) as 51.0% EBF mothers had primary education or below, while 30.6% of FF mothers had intermediate-level education. Antenatal counselling about BF was strongly linked to EBF status (p<0.001), with 76.7% of EBF mothers having received professional counselling, compared with 66.7% among FF mothers (table 2).

Discussion

The EBF is recognised as the optimal form of infant nutrition, providing essential immunological, nutritional and developmental benefits. However, the practice of EBF remains suboptimal in many regions, particularly in low-resource settings.16 In this study, the EBF rate was 53.5%, while data from various parts of the world show variations reporting EBF rates between 21% and 83%.17–21 This study found that mode of delivery was significantly associated with EBF status with vaginally delivered infants having higher rates of EBF. These findings align with those of Prior et al, who conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrating that caesarean delivery negatively affects early BF initiation and EBF rates (OR 0.57; 95% CI 0.50 to 0.64, p<0.001).22 The underlying mechanisms may include maternal discomfort post-caesarean, delayed lactogenesis and reduced skin-to-skin contact, which is crucial for BF initiation.23 Vasilevski et al24 reported that caesarean delivery significantly reduced EBF rates at hospital discharge (p<0.001). These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions, such as lactation support and early BF initiation programmes, particularly for mothers undergoing caesarean deliveries.

Gestational age was not significantly associated with EBF, though NICU admission was a strong determinant of non-EBF feeding practices (p=0.028). Infants requiring NICU admission were significantly more likely to receive mixed feeding, particularly with formula or animal milk. These findings are consistent with those of Vasilevski et al,24 who found that NICU admission significantly reduced EBF rates at discharge (p<0.001). NICU stays often involve separation from the mother, tube feeding and delayed BF initiation, all of which may negatively impact EBF. This underscores the importance of promoting breast milk expression, kangaroo mother care and lactation counselling in NICUs to support BF continuation. This study also identified significant associations between birth weight and feeding practices. This aligns with findings from Mphasha et al,25 who reported that preterm and low-birth-weight infants are more likely to receive formula feeding due to concerns about weight gain and feeding difficulties. However, breast milk remains the preferred feeding option even for preterm infants, given its protective effects against necrotising enterocolitis and infections.26 27

BF initiation timing was highly linked to EBF. The WHO recommends BF initiation within the first hour, as early suckling enhances milk production and maternal–infant bonding.14 These findings mirror those of Asare et al,28 where 63.4% of mothers initiated BF within the first hour, significantly improving EBF rates. However, Saebela et al29 highlighted various barriers to early BF initiation, including maternal fatigue, lack of knowledge and cultural practices encouraging pre-lacteal feeding. This study found that 64.1% of EBF infants still received pre-lacteal feeds, indicating a persistent cultural challenge that needs to be addressed through targeted educational programmes.

Maternal education was significantly associated with EBF practices (p=0.001). While 51.0% of EBF mothers had primary education or below, 30.6% of FF mothers had intermediate education, suggesting that higher education levels were paradoxically associated with formula feeding. This contrasts with findings from Asare et al,28 where mothers with tertiary education were less likely to practice EBF (AOR 0.18; 95% CI 0.36 to 0.85). Mothers with higher education levels are more likely to be working, making EBF difficult due to workplace constraints and limited maternity leave policies. Educated mothers may have greater exposure to alternative feeding options and marketing influences. Conversely, less educated mothers may follow traditional BF practices and have financial constraints that discourage formula feeding. The finding that 71.4% of mothers who combined BF with animal milk had primary or below education suggests economic factors and accessibility influence feeding decisions. Receiving BF counselling during antenatal care was strongly associated with feeding practices (p<0.001). It was noted that 76.7% of EBF mothers received counselling from healthcare professionals, reinforcing the importance of structured education, while 30.6% of formula-feeding mothers did not receive BF counselling, suggesting a potential gap in awareness. Interestingly, 42.9% of mothers who practised BF with water received counselling from a female health visitor, raising concerns about misinformation or cultural beliefs influencing feeding choices. These findings are supported by another local study from Shahnawaz et al,30 who reported that structured antenatal BF education improved EBF adherence. Given that only 66.7% of FF mothers received any form of counselling, there is a substantial gap in antenatal education that needs to be addressed through expanded prenatal lactation programmes.

This findings of the present study underscore several critical areas for intervention. The high caesarean section rate among non-EBF infants suggests that postpartum lactation support should be prioritised for mothers undergoing caesarean deliveries. The significant role of antenatal counselling suggests that expanding BF education during prenatal care visits could improve EBF rates. Workplace policies supporting BF mothers could mitigate the observed trend of higher formula use among educated mothers.

This study has several limitations as well. Its cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causal relationships between determinants and EBF status. Single-centre, hospital-based design may limit generalisability to other settings or broader populations. Self-reported data on BF practices may be subject to recall or social desirability bias. The study was conducted in a single hospital serving a low-income population, which may limit the generalisability of findings to other socioeconomic groups. Future longitudinal studies exploring BF practices over time, as well as multicentre studies, would provide more comprehensive insights.

Conclusion

Despite the well-established benefits of EBF, only around half of the infants were exclusively breastfed, highlighting the need for targeted interventions. Caesarean delivery, NICU admission and pre-lacteal feeding practices were significant barriers to EBF, while early BF initiation, antenatal counselling and maternal education played pivotal roles in promoting EBF. Addressing these barriers through structured lactation support, policy reforms and public health education is essential for improving BF outcomes in Pakistan.
