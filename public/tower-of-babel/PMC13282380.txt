Targeting WFS1 overcomes KRASG12D dependency and adaptive resistance to KRAS inhibition in pancreatic cancer
Authors: Yixi Chen, Jingwen Zhang, Ying Miao, Jiayu Wang, Jingtong Na, Xiyu Liu, Zhengfang Yi, Huang Chen, Mingyao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKRASG12D-driven pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains a therapeutic challenge characterized by limited treatment options. While MRTX1133, a potent and selective KRASG12D inhibitor, is currently under clinical evaluation, the emergence of acquired resistance restricts its long-term therapeutic efficacy. In this study, we identified Wolfram syndrome 1 (WFS1) as a potential molecular vulnerability in KRASG12D-driven PDAC. Our findings suggest that WFS1 expression is upregulated following KRASG12D activation and may promote tumorigenesis and metastasis. Moreover, WFS1 expression was further elevated in tumors resistant to MRTX1133, independently of KRAS activation status. Inhibition of WFS1 partially restored sensitivity to MRTX1133 in resistant tumors by inducing excessive and sustained activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR), subsequently triggering apoptosis. Further exploration revealed that, while WFS1 is regulated by the MAPK and PI3K/AKT pathways in MRTX1133-sensitive cells, acquired resistance is associated with downregulation of the E3 ubiquitin ligase Smurf1, leading to increased WFS1 protein stability. Overall, these results highlight WFS1 as an adaptive factor and potential therapeutic target in KRASG12D-driven malignancies, offering a novel approach to enhance the efficacy of KRASG12D inhibitors and overcome acquired resistance.Subject terms: Cancer, Oncology

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly aggressive malignancy of the digestive tract characterized by an increasing mortality rate and a paucity of viable treatment options1,2. KRAS mutations are present in over 90% of PDAC cases, making them a compelling target for intervention3. Although covalent KRASG12C inhibitors, including sotorasib and adagrasib, have demonstrated efficacy in specific clinical contexts4, their therapeutic benefits are often circumvented by pathway reactivation and acquired resistance5,6. Furthermore, these inhibitors demonstrate limited efficacy against the KRASG12D variant, the most common PDAC mutation. This therapeutic gap underscores the urgent need for G12D-specific inhibitors7,8. MRTX1133, a highly selective KRASG12D inhibitor, has recently entered clinical trials and represents a promising therapeutic avenue9,10. However, the emergence of acquired resistance significantly limits and sustained clinical responses, underscores the necessity for in-depth mechanistic investigations and the development of novel combination strategies11.

In addition to aberrations in the KRAS signaling pathway, endoplasmic reticulum stress (ER stress) and the unfolded protein response (UPR) play pivotal roles in PDAC progression and therapy resistance12. The UPR plays a dual role in oncogenesis: moderate activation facilitates cellular adaptation to the stressful microenvironment13,14, while sustained overactivation triggers apoptosis15. Evidence suggests that protective UPR activation in KRAS-mutant tumors, promotes resistance to targeted therapies16. Therefore, identifying key nodes that simultaneously link KRAS signaling and UPR regulation is critical for overcoming resistance. Wolfram syndrome protein (WFS1) is an ER transmembrane protein that acts as a negative regulator of the UPR and is involved in maintaining ER calcium homeostasis and mitochondrial function17,18. Its loss leads to ER dysfunction, hyperactivation of the UPR, and apoptosis19,20. While its role in metabolic and neurological diseases is relatively well-established, its function in cancer, especially in the context of KRASG12D-mutant PDAC, remains poorly characterized21.

Motivated by resistance to the KRASG12D inhibitor MRTX1133, this study investigated WFS1’s role in tumor proliferation and resistance through its regulation of UPR activation. We demonstrated that WFS1 expression was upregulated in resistant models, independently of KRAS activity. Notably, inhibition of WFS1 enhanced UPR-mediated pro-apoptotic signaling and partially reversed resistance to MRTX1133. These findings suggest that WFS1 may have a dual regulatory function in KRASG12D-mutant PDAC and highlight the potential therapeutic value of targeting the WFS1-mediated UPR pathway to mitigate drug resistance.

Results

WFS1 exhibits elevated expression in PDAC, and this elevation appears more pronounced in the context of KRASG12D mutations

Previous studies have reported increased WFS1 expression in multiple malignancies. Analysis of RT–qPCR across various cancer cell lines and CCLE RNA-seq data confirmed relatively high WFS1 expression, particularly in PDAC (Fig. 1A, B and Supplementary Fig. 1A). TCGA data from the GEPIA2 database indicated significantly higher WFS1 expression in PDAC tumor samples (N = 179) compared to normal tissues (N = 171) (Fig. 1C), and Kaplan–Meier analysis showed a trend toward reduced overall survival among patients exhibiting higher WFS1 expression (Fig. 1D). Further analysis of CCLE data revealed that KRASG12D-mutant PDAC displayed higher WFS1 expression compared to other KRAS-mutant subtypes (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. 1B). Endogenous WFS1 levels were consistently higher in KRASG12D-mutant PDAC cell lines relative to other KRAS-mutant subtypes (Fig. 1F, G and Supplementary Fig. 1C, E). Additionally, elevated WFS1 expression was observed in certain other KRASG12D-mutant tumors (Supplementary Fig. 1D, F). Exogenous expression of KRASG12D in HEK 293 T and hTERT HPNE cells resulted in increased WFS1 expression, while KRASG12C or KRASG12V expression did not produce similar effects (Fig. 1H and Supplementary Fig. 1G). Collectively, these results indicate that KRASG12D mutations may contribute to elevated WFS1 expression in PDAC, although further studies are needed to confirm mutation-specific regulatory mechanisms definitively.

WFS1 facilitates tumor progression and metastasis in KRASG12D-mutant PDAC

Invasion and metastasis represent primary therapeutic challenges in PDAC and contribute significantly to patient mortality22. To elucidate the mechanistic role of WFS1 in KRASG12D-driven pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), we developed stable WFS1-knockdown models in the KRASG12D-mutant cell lines SU.86.86, PANC-1, HPAC, and AsPC-1 (Fig. 2A, B). Functional analyses revealed that WFS1 depletion significantly impaired proliferation and clonogenic potential across these models (Fig. 2C–E), underscoring its critical role in tumor growth. Moreover, WFS1 knockdown attenuated invasion and migration (Fig. 2F, G and Supplementary Fig. 2A), suggesting its involvement in the metastatic cascade. Additionally, we performed WFS1 knockdown in KRASG12C-mutant (MIA PaCa-2) and KRASG12V-mutant (CAPAN-2) control lines (Supplementary Fig. 2B); suppression of WFS1 expression in these variants did not affect oncogenic functions (Supplementary Fig. 2C–H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that WFS1 selectively promotes KRASG12D-mutant PDAC pathogenesis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target to inhibit both primary tumor progression and systemic dissemination.

WFS1 mediates MRTX1133 resistance

MRTX1133 is a promising therapeutic candidate that specifically targets KRASG12D-mutant PDAC; however, acquired resistance remains a significant clinical obstacle. To assess the role of WFS1 in therapeutic resistance, we developed acquired-resistant variants (HPACMR and AsPC-1MR) through stepwise dose escalation of MRTX1133 in parental KRASG12D-mutant PDAC cell lines (Fig. 3A, B and Supplementary Fig. 3A–C). Our observations indicate that extended exposure to MRTX1133 results in a reduced inhibition of both downstream signaling pathways and WFS1 expression. Additionally, in the resistant cell lines, KRAS downstream signaling was reactivated, accompanied by a significant upregulation of WFS1 expression (Fig. 3C, D). Consistent with our cellular observations, analysis of an established in vivo resistance model revealed a profound upregulation of WFS1 expression upon resistance development, as confirmed by Western blot and immunohistochemical analyses (Fig. 3E–G). To determine if WFS1 functionally mediates resistance to MRTX1133, we performed genetic ablation of WFS1 in MRTX1133-resistant cells (Fig. 3H). Notably, depletion of WFS1 restored cellular sensitivity to MRTX1133, effectively reversing the acquired-resistant phenotype (Fig. 3I, J and Supplementary Fig. 3D). In summary, the findings indicate that WFS1 is markedly upregulated in both in vitro and in vivo models of MRTX1133-resistant tumors. Notably, the targeted inhibition of WFS1 successfully reinstates sensitivity to MRTX1133 in resistant cells.

WFS1-dependent UPR drives MRTX1133 resistance

WFS1 functions as a key negative regulator of ER stress and UPR activation23. Loss-of-function studies indicate that WFS1 deficiency induces excessive ER stress and shifts UPR signaling from an adaptive to a pro-apoptotic state24,25. By silencing WFS1 using shRNA or siRNA in KRASG12D-mutant PDAC cells, we confirmed that WFS1 depletion augmented apoptosis (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. 4A–D). Simultaneously, expression of key UPR-associated proteins was suppressed in resistant cells (HPACMR and AsPC-1MR), mirroring observations following WFS1 overexpression in HEK 293 T cells; furthermore, treatment with the KRASG12D inhibitor MRTX1133 increased UPR activity in sensitive cells, whereas no significant changes were observed in resistant cells (Supplementary Fig. 5A–G). These findings align with evidence that WFS1 inhibits sustained and excessive UPR activation26, thereby reducing the expression of key markers such as GRP7827, XBP128, and the apoptotic executor CHOP, and ultimately promoting cell survival.

Building on these observations, we utilized stable WFS1-knockdown models in KRASG12D-mutant PDAC cells, and confirmed that WFS1 depletion significantly augmented apoptosis (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. 5H). Consistent with genetic WFS1 knockdown, pharmacological inhibition of WFS1 with WSF1-IN-1 similarly activated the UPR and induced apoptosis (Fig. 4C). However, the significant attenuation of apoptosis following the suppression of key UPR mediators confirms that WFS1 drives apoptosis primarily through UPR activation and that intact UPR signaling is essential for this pro-apoptotic effect (Fig. 4C). In alignment with prior studies, we further confirmed that inhibiting individual UPR pathways partially restored MRTX1133 sensitivity in resistant cells (Fig. 4D, E). Furthermore, we have demonstrated that inhibiting WFS1 results in a more robust therapeutic response than targeting individual UPR branches or simultaneously inhibiting all three UPR pathways. This approach significantly enhances the efficacy of MRTX1133 against resistant tumors. (Fig. 4D, E and Supplementary Fig. 5I). In summary, these findings provide robust evidence that WFS1 inhibition triggers pro-apoptotic UPR activation and induces cell death.

KRAS-independent WFS1 upregulation mediates resistance to MRTX1133

As a critical downstream effect of KRAS, WFS1 levels are markedly reduced by MRTX1133 treatment in a sensitive cell line. In contrast, WFS1 expression is only modestly affected or unchanged in cell lines with acquired resistance (Supplementary Fig. 6A, B). Given that MAPK and PI3K/AKT are two major effector pathways downstream of oncogenic KRAS29, we assessed their roles in regulating WFS1. Treatment with trametinib (a MEK inhibitor) or MK2206 (an AKT inhibitor) effectively suppressed WFS1 expression in parental HPAC and AsPC-1 cells but had minimal impact on their resistant counterparts (Fig. 5A–D). These results indicate the presence of a KRAS-independent mechanism for WFS1 regulation in cells resistant to MRTX1133. Comparative analysis revealed elevated WFS1 protein levels in resistant cells, without a concomitant increase in mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. 6C), suggesting post-translational regulatory mechanisms. Proteasome inhibition with MG132 prevented MRTX1133-mediated WFS1 suppression in sensitive cells (Fig. 5E), and cycloheximide chase assays demonstrated enhanced WFS1 stability in resistant variants (Fig. 5F), confirming impaired ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation. Considering the established roles of E3 ubiquitin ligases in resistance to KRAS inhibition and in modulating UPR activation30–32, we screened candidate regulators in resistant cells33,34 and observed significant downregulation of Smurf1, inversely correlating with WFS1 accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 6D, E and Fig. 5G). Knockdown of Smurf1 in sensitive cells increased WFS1 stability and decreased drug sensitivity, whereas Smurf1 overexpression in resistant cells reduced WFS1 levels and partially restored sensitivity to MRTX1133 (Fig. 5H, I). Collectively, these findings suggest that KRAS-independent activation of WFS1 is associated with resistance to MRTX1133, and Smurf1 suppression may contribute to WFS1-mediated cell survival. Further studies are necessary to elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying Smurf1-mediated WFS1 regulation (Fig. 6A, B).

Co-inhibition of WFS1 and KRAS suppresses proliferation in resistant PDAC

Following our discovery that WFS1 contributes to resistance against KRASG12D-targeted therapy and that its inhibition can restore sensitivity to MRTX1133 in resistant cells, we investigated the combined effects of the WFS1 inhibitor WSF1-IN-1 and the KRASG12D inhibitor MRTX1133 in PDAC. Parental (HPAC and AsPC-1) and resistant (HPACMR and AsPC-1MR) cell lines were treated with this combination. The combination treatment demonstrated significantly superior anti-proliferative activity compared to single-agent treatment, with a Bliss synergy score >10, indicating strong synergistic effects. The synergistic effect of WSF1-IN-1 and MRTX1133 was proved superior to combinations of MRTX1133 with trametinib or MK2206 in sensitive and resistant cells (Fig. 7A and Supplementary Fig. 7A, B). In contrast, the combination of WSF1-IN-1 and the KRASG12C inhibitor Sotorasib (AMG510) showed no synergistic effect and failed to suppress clonogenic survival (Supplementary Fig. 7C, D). Furthermore, the combination of WSF1-IN-1 and MRTX1133 effectively inhibited clonogenic survival in both sensitive and resistant cells (Fig. 7B), and resulted in markedly increased levels of Cleaved Caspase-3 and Cleaved PARP, indicative of enhanced apoptotic activation (Fig. 7C, D and Supplementary Fig. 7E). Collectively, these data establish that concomitant inhibition of KRASG12D and WFS1 effectively inhibits proliferation and promotes apoptosis in KRASG12D-mutant PDAC.

Targeting WFS1 enhances MRTX1133 therapy in vivo

Building on the potent in vitro finding that WFS1 inhibition resensitized resistant cells to MRTX1133, we further evaluated this combination strategy in vivo. We established bilateral xenografts using AsPC-1MR cells with or without WFS1 knockdown. WFS1 depletion significantly attenuated tumor proliferation. While MRTX1133 monotherapy showed limited efficacy in controlling tumor progression, its combination with WFS1 knockdown led to profound and sustained tumor regression, achieving complete responses in three of five subjects (Fig. 8A–C). Notably, no adverse effects related to the treatment were detected, as indicated by stable body weights and the absence of pathological abnormalities in major organs. (Supplementary Fig. 8A–C). Mechanistically, either inhibiting WFS1 or MRTX1133 treatment alone activated all three UPR branches (PERK, IRE1α, and ATF6), with maximal activation observed in the combination group. Combined inhibition of WFS1 and MRTX1133 treatment potently induced tumor cell apoptosis (Fig. 8D). Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis confirmed that the combined therapy most significantly reduced Ki-67 expression and increased apoptosis compared with monotherapies (Fig. 8E). These results established that WFS1 ablation enhanced MRTX1133 efficacy by concurrently activating the UPR and mitochondrial apoptosis in KRASG12D-mutant PDAC.

Discussion

Oncogenic mutations in KRAS are the primary genetic drivers of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). Coupled with recent breakthroughs in targeting the KRAS family proteins, specifically KRAS mutations, has become a prominent research focus for PDAC treatment. MRTX1133, a highly selective KRASG12D inhibitor, represents a transformative advance given that the G12D mutation accounts for ~50% of KRAS-driven PDAC. These developments have propelled KRASG12D-targeted therapy to the forefront of innovative treatment strategies. However, the complexity of KRAS signaling and tumor heterogeneity often leads to rapid resistance emergence through multiple mechanisms, including upstream RTK activation, reactivation of downstream KRAS effectors, parallel pathway crosstalk, or compensatory feedback loops35. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying resistance is critical for developing novel therapeutic strategies for patients harboring KRASG12D-mutant tumors.

In PDAC, the complex tumor microenvironment and exposure to cytotoxic therapies often induce chronic activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR)34, which facilitates tumor cell survival and mediates multidrug resistance36. Studies demonstrate varying degrees of protective UPR pathway activation in KRAS-mutant tumors12, including ER stress-independent reactivation of IRE1α16, PERK-mediated upregulation of P-gp37, and ATF6-driven protective autophagy38,39. These adaptive mechanisms collectively facilitate UPR activation, thereby promoting cancer cell survival. Paradoxically, while KRAS-mutant PDAC requires sustained protein synthesis for proliferation, persistent UPR activation can trigger apoptosis. Thus, precise modulation of ER stress pathways to control the switch between pro-survival and pro-apoptotic UPR signaling represents a promising therapeutic strategy for KRAS-mutant malignancies40,41.

WFS1 is predominantly associated with glycolysis and the unfolded protein response (UPR), functioning as a crucial negative regulator of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress signaling and UPR activation42. Dysfunction of WFS1 disrupts ER homeostasis, leading to sustained and terminal UPR activation. Additionally, WFS1 modulates ER calcium balance; its impairment induces abnormal calcium release, impairs mitochondrial function, and ultimately triggers apoptotic cascades43.

Our study establishes the ER-resident transmembrane protein WFS1 as a potential molecular vulnerability in KRASG12D-mutant pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). Within this genetic context, we observed significant upregulation of WFS1, a functionally essential gene for sustaining tumor cell proliferation and survival. Clinical correlation analyses further reveal that high WFS1 expression is strongly associated with advanced disease stage and poor patient prognosis, underscoring its utility as a promising therapeutic target in KRASG12D-driven PDAC.

Mechanistic investigations demonstrated that WFS1 depletion induces substantial apoptosis in both drug-sensitive and therapy-resistant KRASG12D-mutant PDAC cells. We determined that this apoptotic response is driven by the chronic disruption of ER proteostasis, leading to terminal activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR). Specifically, loss of WFS1 converts the transient, adaptive UPR into a sustained, pro-apoptotic state, characterized by the upregulation of UPR markers such as GRP78 and XBP1, followed by CHOP-mediated apoptotic signaling. This mechanistic shift was consistently validated across multiple cellular models, confirming WFS1’s pivotal role in determining terminal UPR outcomes.

A therapeutically significant finding is that WFS1 inhibition effectively resensitizes resistant cells to KRASG12D inhibitors, revealing its crucial role in maintaining the drug-resistant phenotype. To elucidate the mechanism sustaining WFS1 expression in resistance, we conducted an in-depth analysis of its regulation. In treatment-sensitive settings, WFS1 transcription is regulated by canonical KRAS downstream effector pathways, specifically MAPK and PI3K/AKT. In contrast, in acquired resistance models, WFS1 protein expression is enhanced through a post-translational mechanism, that appears largely independent of transcriptional regulation. These findings suggest that this stabilization might partly result from the downregulation of the E3 ubiquitin ligase Smurf1, indicating a potential KRAS-independent bypass pathway. This adaptive mechanism may allow cancer cells to maintain WFS1-mediated survival benefits under therapeutic stress, providing insight into the dynamic evolution of resistance in KRAS-driven cancers.

This study has several limitations. First, the precise mechanism of WFS1 reactivation in resistant cells remains incompletely understood. While the HECT-domain E3 ubiquitin ligase Smurf1 appears to regulate WFS1 stability, detailed aspects of this regulation have yet to be elucidated. In addition, how acquired resistance impairs Smurf1 activity remains unknown. Given Smurf1’s established roles in suppressing TGF-β/BMP signaling and modulating Wnt/β-catenin cascades, we speculate that resistance-associated pathways might disrupt Smurf1 function44, thereby contributing to WFS1 stabilization in resistant cells. Second, given WFS1’s essential physiological roles, developing strategies to selectively target its oncogenic functions while preserving its normal function is critical45. Our findings underscore the crucial need to investigate further the mechanistic roles of WFS1 and Smurf1 in KRASG12D-mediated drug resistance.

In conclusion, this study identifies WFS1 as a potential therapeutic target in KRASG12D-driven malignancies, highlighting its dual role in tumor adaptation and the acquisition of therapeutic resistance. Targeting the regulation of the unfolded protein response (UPR) may thus offer a promising strategy to overcome resistance in KRAS-mutant cancers.

Methods

Cells lines

Human PDAC cell lines (SU.86.86, HPAC, PANC-1, AsPC-1, HPAF II, CFPAC-1, CAPAN-2, MIA PaCa-2, BxPC-3, Panc04.03, PK59, PSN1, and SW1990), human embryonic kidney cells (HEK 293T), immortalized human pancreatic ductal epithelial cells (hTERT HPNE), mouse pro-B lymphocytes (BAF3), and other cell lines used in this study were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and/or the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC).Cells were cultured as follows: SU.86.86, AsPC-1, BxPC-3, hTERT HPNE, BAF3, PK59, PSN1, A549, A375, LS174T, LS513, LoVo, PC9, HCC827, NCI-H1975, MOLM-13, and AGS in RPMI-1640; HPAC in DMEM/F12; MIA PaCa-2, PANC-1, HEK 293T, SK-MEL-28, HAcAT, GP2D, and SW403 in DMEM; HPAF II in MEM; CFPAC-1 and CAPAN-1 in IMDM; CAPAN-2 in McCoy’s 5 A; and SW1990, MDA-MB-231, SW1463, SW620, and SW480 in Leibovitz’s L-15. Unless otherwise specified, media were supplemented with 10–20% fetal bovine serum (FBS). For specific formulations: Panc04.03 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% insulin; MIA PaCa-2 cells in medium supplemented with 2.5% equine serum; HPAF II cells in medium supplemented with 1% (each) NEAA, L-glutamine, and sodium pyruvate; and BAF3 cells in medium supplemented with IL-3 (10 ng/mL). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Gene silencing and overexpression

Gene knockdown was achieved by siRNA transfection (using Lipofectamine 3000) or lentiviral transduction. For stable knockdown, shRNA oligonucleotides were cloned into the pLKO.1 vector; for overexpression, coding sequences were inserted into the PCDH vector (primers are listed in Supplementary Table S1). Lentiviruses were produced in HEK 293 T cells, and viral supernatants were collected at 72 h post-transfection and used to transduce target cells for 24 h.

Subsequently, stable cell lines were selected with puromycin. All siRNA knockdown experiments were performed within three passages, while shRNA-mediated stable knockdown or overexpression experiments were conducted within seven passages. Throughout the experimental process, knockdown/overexpression efficiency was continuously monitored and confirmed by immunoblotting and/or RT–qPCR.

Immunofluorescence

Cells (2 × 104–3 × 104) were seeded on sterile coverslips and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Cells were fixed, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000) before mounting. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded at 5000 cells per well in 12-well plates or 10,000 cells per well in six-well plates. After 24 h, cells were treated with the indicated drug combinations or DMSO control for 7–14 days, with medium and compounds refreshed every 3–4 days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), stained with 2% crystal violet, imaged, and quantified using ImageJ.

Transwell migration/invasion assay

After serum starvation for 24 h, 5000–8000 cells were resuspended in serum-free medium and seeded into the upper chamber of Transwell inserts (with Matrigel coating for invasion assays, where indicated). Medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After 12–24 h, cells on the lower surface were fixed, stained, and counted in 5–10 randomly selected fields.

Wound-healing assay

Cells were grown in six-well plates to 80–90% confluence. A uniform scratch was generated using a sterile 200-μL pipette tip in serum-reduced medium. Images were captured at the indicated time points, and wound closure was quantified using ImageJ.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was measured using the CCK-8 assay. Cells (2 × 103 per well) were seeded in 96-well plates, treated with the indicated compounds for 72 h, and then incubated with 10 μL CCK-8 reagent at 37 °C for 1–2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a SpectraMax 190 microplate reader. Dose–response curves and IC50 values were calculated by nonlinear regression in GraphPad Prism 9.5.

RT–qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Takara), reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and quantified by real-time PCR using SYBR Green chemistry (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the reference gene. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Immunoblotting

Cells or tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 30 min at room temperature. Signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL).

Apoptosis analysis

Apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide apoptosis detection kit (Beyotime, C1062L). Cells were treated for 48 h in six-well plates, stained according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and analyzed using a BD FACS Calibur flow cytometer.

Subcutaneous xenograft animal model

Animals. Immunodeficient male mice (BALB/c nude and SCID strains, 6–8 weeks old) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of East China Normal University. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle, controlled temperature (22 ± 2 °C) and humidity (50 ± 10%), and provided with autoclaved food and water ad libitum. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Biomedical Research Institute at East China Normal University (Approval No. M + R 20250101) and performed in strict accordance with their guidelines.

In vivo MRTX1133 Resistance Model (HPAC cells). HPAC cells (5 × 10⁶ in 100 μL) were suspended in 50% Matrigel (0.1 mL) and subcutaneously injected into the posterior dorsum of BALB/c nude mice under brief isoflurane anesthesia (2–3% induction and 1–2% maintenance). Tumor growth was monitored until the average volume reached ~150 mm³. Mice were then randomly allocated into treatment groups (N = 5 per group) using a computer-generated random number table (GraphPad Prism). Baseline tumor volumes were measured prior to randomization, and the researcher performing tumor measurements was blinded to group allocation. Mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of control solvent or MRTX1133 (10 mg/kg). Tumor dimensions were measured every 7 days, and volume was calculated as V = (length × width²) × 0.52. Treatment continued until sustained tumor growth was observed in the MRTX1133-treated group.

Tumor Models Using MRTX1133-Resistant Cells (AsPC-1MR ± WFS1 knockdown). AsPC-1MR parental cells and AsPC-1MR shWFS1 stable transfectants (8 × 10⁶ in 100 μL) were subcutaneously injected into the left and right flanks of the same SCID mouse, respectively, allowing direct paired comparison. When the average tumor volume of all tumors reached ~150 mm³, mice were randomly allocated into treatment groups (N = 5 per group) using the same protocol as above. Baseline tumor volumes were confirmed to be not significantly different between untreated and treated parental groups, and similarly for shWFS1 groups (p > 0.05). Mice then received daily intraperitoneal injections of control solvent or MRTX1133 (10 mg/kg), and tumor volume and body weight were recorded every 5 days.

Humane Endpoints and Euthanasia. Humane endpoints included tumor burden >1500 mm³, ulceration, or body weight loss >20%. At the endpoint, all mice were euthanized by CO₂ asphyxiation. Death was confirmed by the absence of respiration, fixed/dilated pupils, and lack of response to toe pinch. Tumors were excised, weighed, and processed for Western blot and immunohistochemistry.

Immunoblotting

Cells or tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 30 min at room temperature. Signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL).

Hematoxylin and eosin staining

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol, followed by hematoxylin and eosin staining. After dehydration through ascending ethanol, sections were mounted with DPX and imaged using a Leica DM500 brightfield microscope.

Statistical analysis

Image analysis was performed using ImageJ. Statistical analyses were carried out with GraphPad Prism 9.5, and data were presented as mean ± SD. Comparisons between two groups were assessed by Student’s t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with appropriate post hoc tests. Synergistic drug interactions were evaluated using Bliss synergy scores calculated with SynergyFinder 3.0 software, with values >10 indicating statistically significant synergistic effects. Statistical significance in all other analyses is denoted as follows: ****P < 0.0001, ***P < 0.001, **P < 0.01, *P < 0.05; ns not significant.
