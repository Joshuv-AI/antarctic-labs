Effects of Antibiotic and Temperature Treatments on Phycosphere Bacterial Communities of Kelp Gametophytes

Yuewen Zhang, Shanshan Liang, Huiling Xia, Xiaoyu Zhong, Yi Zheng

Abstract

Microbiome dysbiosis represents a critical bottleneck in sustainable kelp aquaculture, yet the distinct regulatory mechanisms of antibiotics and temperature remain poorly characterized. This study systematically elucidates the divergent effects of penicillin–streptomycin exposure and temperature gradients on Saccharina japonica gametophytes and their epiphytic microbiota. Antibiotic treatment significantly reduced Proteobacteria from 93.02% to 49.70% and increased Bacteroidetes from 6.98% to 49.20%, replacing beneficial Sulfitobacter and Colwellia with opportunistic Kordia and Klebsiella, indicating severe dysbiosis driven by the creation of unoccupied ecological niches. In contrast, temperature variation did not alter dominant phyla: Proteobacteria remained predominant, but Vibrio peaked at 15°C (44.11%) alongside the maximal gametophyte specific growth rate of 5.21% per day, concurrently elevating pathogenic loads of Vibrio and Pseudomonas. These findings reveal that antibiotics disrupt core community architecture, whereas temperature modulates metabolic rates within stable taxonomic frameworks. Consequently, we propose a stratified preservation strategy: short‐term seedling cultivation at 15°C requires stringent antimicrobial measures to mitigate disease risk, while lower temperatures are preferable for long‐term germplasm storage to minimize contamination potential. This framework provides essential insights for precision microbial management, balancing growth promotion with disease control in kelp aquaculture systems.
We reveal antibiotics drive qualitative turnover, while temperature induces quantitative fluctuations in kelp gametophyte microbiomes. This 15°C growth‐pathogen trade‐off guides precision seedling management.

Introduction

Saccharina japonica represents an economically significant macroalgal species (Lee et al. ). This kelp undergoes a heteromorphic diplohaplontic life cycle characterized by alternating microscopic haploid gametophytic and macroscopic diploid sporophytic generations (Monteiro et al. ). The gametophytes, originating from germinated meiospores, lack tissue specialization and possess a simple body plan devoid of advanced defence or regulatory systems found in mature sporophytes (Wu et al. ; Zhou et al. ). This structural simplicity renders them highly susceptible to environmental fluctuations (Veenhof et al. ; Yu et al. ).

Successful germplasm preservation depends on maintaining a stable equilibrium between the host and its associated microbiota. However, bacterial contamination remains a persistent threat, with field‐derived materials often introducing opportunistic pathogens that compromise culture viability and germplasm integrity. Pathogenic infection interferes with the photosynthetic system of kelp, thereby indirectly leading to a reduction in the synthesis of carotenoids, such as fucoxanthin (Citti and Blanchard ), Vibrio infection may reduce fucoxanthin content by impairing photosynthetic metabolism. Meanwhile, Pseudomonas produce volatile sulphur compounds including dimethyl disulphide and dimethyl trisulfide at low temperatures, contributing to spoilage off‐odours in preserved samples (Parlapani et al. ). Antibiotic treatments are routinely employed to suppress pathogenic proliferation and stabilize gametophyte cultures (Sun et al. ). While effective in the short term, these interventions exert collateral effects by selectively eliminating susceptible bacterial taxa while permitting proliferation of resistant lineages, thereby restructuring the phycosphere community in ways that may undermine long‐term preservation stability.

The phycosphere microenvironment surrounding kelp gametophytes functions as a chemically defined niche shaped by algal exudation of dissolved organic matter and signalling metabolites (Bell and Mitchell ). The phycosphere microbiome associated with gametophytes exerts bidirectional regulatory effects on the host via metabolic exchange (Lian et al. ).

Temperature critically governs kelp gametophyte development by inducing sex‐biased transcriptional regulation (Monteiro et al. ), subsequently modulating phycosphere microbiota through thermal restructuring. Thermal shifts alter the competitive landscape within the phycosphere, favouring temperature‐adapted taxa while disrupting stable host‐microbe associations. Empirical observations indicate that gametophytes maintained at 15°C exhibit greater susceptibility to microbial contamination than those cultured at 10°C, suggesting that temperature regimes critically determine the stability of gametophyte‐microbiome interactions during preservation (Mars Brisbin et al. ).

Despite accumulating evidence that macroalgal microbiomes modulate host stress responses, bacterial dynamics in kelp gametophytes under preservation stress remain poorly resolved. Most studies focus on sporophytes, though Veenhof et al. () recently showed that gametophyte and sporophyte microbiomes differ qualitatively and that gametophyte‐associated bacteria are essential for survival and thermal tolerance. Ocean warming can restructure kelp microbiota and enrich putative pathogens (Castro et al. ), and Molnar et al. () demonstrated that temperature is a dominant driver of kelp microbiome composition. Yet the comparative effects of temperature fluctuations and antibiotic interventions—two routinely manipulated preservation variables—remain untested. High‐throughput sequencing has not been applied to resolve bacterial succession across preservation conditions in Saccharina japonica gametophyte systems (Vadillo Gonzalez et al. ). A central unresolved question is whether preservation stressors drive wholesale taxonomic turnover or merely adjust taxon abundances, a distinction critical for optimizing microbiome management. Clarifying these dynamics will advance both our understanding of early‐life‐stage holobiont interactions and evidence‐based germplasm preservation strategies.

Here, we applied this approach to track bacterial dynamics in Saccharina japonica gametophyte systems under paired temperature and antibiotic treatments during early gametophyte development. While prior work has separately documented each stressor's effects, no study has directly compared their regulatory outcomes or identified specific taxa linked to preservation risks. At 15°C, the gametophyte growth rate reached the maximum of 5.21% d−1, while the relative abundance of Vibrio also peaked at 44.11%, increasing the risk of pathogenic contamination. Concurrently, the relative abundance of Pseudomonas reached its maximum at this temperature. Pseudomonas can produce dimethyl disulphide and dimethyl trisulfide at low temperatures, leading to spoilage off‐odours. Antibiotic treatment reduced the proportion of Proteobacteria from 93.02% to 49.70%, while Bacteroidetes increased from 6.98% to 49.20%. This shift replaced beneficial genera such as Sulfitobacter and Colwellia with opportunistic pathogens such as Kordia and Klebsiella, thereby increasing pathogenicity. These findings provide taxon‐level evidence for microbial management in kelp germplasm preservation.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Materials

S. japonica reproductive stock was harvested from Lianjiang Guanwu Marine Science and Technology Development Co. Ltd., Fujian Province, China. Specimens were selected based on robust thallus architecture, uniform pigmentation, and absence of pathological signs. Microscopic inspection verified clean surfaces free of epibiotic colonization.

Experimental Procedures

Zoospore Collection From Laminaria Breeding Stock

Mature S. japonica sporophytes meeting established morphological criteria were triple‐rinsed with 0.22 μm‐filtered sterile seawater. 5 × 5 cm explants were aseptically prepared using flame‐sterilized scalpels and surface‐cleaned with autoclaved gauze. Explants were immersed in 7‰ potassium iodide solution at 10°C for 20 min, then incubated at 12°C and 85% relative humidity with gentle aeration for 4 h. Treated tissues were transferred to 8°C sterile seawater to induce synchronous zoospore release.

Establishment of Axenic Clonal Lines

Zoospore suspensions were diluted with seawater filtered through 0.22 μm polycarbonate membranes to 1–2 spores per microscopic field at 100× magnification using a hemacytometer. One millilitre aliquots were aseptically dispensed into 90 mm Petri dishes pre‐filled with 10 mL PES medium. Primary cultures were maintained at 10°C under continuous photosynthetically active radiation at 17–25 μmol photons·m−2 s−1 for 12 days in climate‐controlled growth chambers. Cultures were subsequently transferred to 1.7–2.5 μmol photons·m−2 s−1 and maintained for 15–20 days to induce gametogenesis.

Mature haploid gametophytes were sexually differentiated and individually isolated via micromanipulation at ×10 magnification using flame‐sterilized glass micropipettes. Isolated filaments were transferred to 50 mL autoclaved Erlenmeyer flasks containing enriched PES medium. Gametophytic aggregates were mechanically dissociated using a sterile tissue homogenizer at 2000 rpm with 30 s pulses to generate short filamentous segments. Resulting suspensions were washed three times with 0.22 μm filtered sterile seawater and passed through 200 μm nylon meshes to retain 2–5 celled fragments. Filtered fragments were re‐inoculated into fresh PES medium. Repeated subculture cycles produced axenic clonal lineages suitable for subsequent experimental scale‐up.

Impact of Antibiotics on Phycosphere Communities in Kelp Gametophytes

Zoospore suspensions were diluted with seawater filtered through 0.22 μm polycarbonate membranes to 1–2 spores per microscopic field at ×100 magnification. One millilitre aliquots were aseptically dispensed into 90 mm Petri dishes pre‐filled with 10 mL Provasoli's Enriched Seawater medium. Experimental cultures received both penicillin (0.2 g/L) and streptomycin (0.1 g/L) simultaneously. Unsupplemented controls were maintained in parallel. All treatments were established in triplicate.

Cultures were incubated at 10°C under continuous photosynthetically active radiation at 17–25 μmol photons·m−2 s−1 for 12 d, after which irradiance was reduced to 1.7–2.5 μmol photons·m−2 s−1 for the remainder of the experimental period. Final sampling occurred on day 20. Planktonic bacterial cells were recovered from culture supernatants by vacuum filtration through 0.22 μm polycarbonate membranes and stored at −80°C pending metagenomic processing.

Gametophytic clonal aggregates were mechanically dissociated for 90 s at 2000 rpm using a sterile tissue homogenizer. Homogenates were passed through 75 μm nylon mesh until 0.20 g of retained biomass was obtained. Exactly 0.20 g of this biomass was aseptically transferred to 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 150 mL sterile PES medium. All treatments were established in triplicate. Flasks were incubated in climate‐controlled growth chambers at 10°C ± 0.5°C, 15°C ± 0.5°C, and 20°C ± 0.5°C. Cultures were agitated orbitally at 180 rpm three times daily. Continuous photosynthetically active radiation was supplied at 17–25 μmol photons·m−2 s−1 for the first 12 d, after which irradiance was adjusted to 1.7–2.5 μmol photons·m−2 s−1 for the remainder of the 21 d experimental period.

Culture media underwent weekly replacement; depleted culture supernatants were vacuum‐filtered through 0.22 μm polycarbonate membranes for planktonic bacterial collection, with membranes cryopreserved at −80°C for subsequent metagenomic analysis. Epiphytic bacterial collection was conducted at the experimental endpoint (Day 21). Gametophyte biomass was aseptically harvested, rinsed briefly in sterile filtered seawater to remove loosely attached planktonic cells, and stored at −80°C pending DNA extraction. Bacterial cells were dislodged from algal surfaces via mechanical homogenization immediately prior to DNA extraction.

At terminal sampling (Day 21), 0.5 g fresh weight of female gametophytes from 10°C treatments was transferred to pre‐weighed centrifuge tubes and flash‐frozen in liquid N2 prior to −80°C storage. Post‐cultivation, all female gametophytes were harvested via 300‐mesh (48 μm) bolting silk filtration to gravimetric endpoint for biomass quantification. DNA Extraction and PCR Amplification

Genomic DNA was extracted from samples using the DNA Kit (Omega Bio‐tek, Norcross, GA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. The quality and quantity of extracted DNA were assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and spectrophotometric analysis. The V3‐V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rDNA gene were amplified for microbial diversity analysis using the Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform. The primer sequences were as follows:

338F(5′‐ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG‐3′).

806R(5′‐GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT‐3′).

PCR amplification was performed in a total volume of 25 μL, comprising 12.5 μL KAPA 2G Robust Hot Start Ready Mix, 1 μL of each primer, 5 μL template DNA, and 5.5 μL nuclease‐free water. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 min; followed by 28 cycles of 95°C for 45 s, 55°C for 50 s, and 72°C for 45 s; and a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. Amplified products were purified using the GeneJET Gel Extraction Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Data Quality Control and Biological Analysis

High‐throughput sequencing was conducted utilizing the Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform. Raw sequencing reads underwent initial quality control via adapter and primer excision employing Cutadapt. Chimeric sequences were subsequently subjected to bioinformatic filtration to generate refined effective datasets.

Bioinformatic analysis followed a standardized workflow. Raw sequencing reads were demultiplexed based on sample‐specific barcodes using Cutadapt. Sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units at a 97% pairwise nucleotide identity threshold. Representative sequences for each operational taxonomic unit were taxonomically classified against a curated reference database, and abundance matrices were generated for all identified taxa. Community structure characterization included α‐diversity assessments covering species richness and Shannon evenness, as well as OTU abundance distribution analyses.

Data are expressed as mean ± standard error (n = 3). Raw data were organized and pre‐processed using Microsoft Excel 2019 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). Inter‐group differences in α‐diversity indices and continuous variables were analysed using SPSS Statistics 27.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). For data meeting parametric assumptions, one‐way analysis of variance (one‐way ANOVA) was performed to test overall inter‐group differences, followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test for pairwise multiple comparisons at a significance level of α = 0.05. Inter‐group differences in β‐diversity were evaluated via permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) in R v4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All remaining figures were prepared using Origin 2024 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).

Ethical Statement

The kelp gametophyte germplasm used in this study is a common laboratory strain and is not an endangered or protected species. All experimental procedures were conducted on plant and microbial materials under laboratory conditions and did not involve any experimentation on animals. This research was conducted in accordance with standard academic ethical guidelines.

Effects of Antibiotic on Phycosphere Bacterial Communities in Kelp Gametophytes

Species Richness Analysis Under Antibiotic Treatment

Following sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform and stringent quality control filtration, high‐fidelity sequencing data were obtained for downstream analyses. Post‐normalization analysis of diversity revealed that (Figure ) identified 27 conserved operational taxonomic units present across all experimental cohorts, delineating a core microbiome. The control group KS1 contained 104 OTUs in total, with 77 unique to this group, whereas the antibiotic‐treated group KS2 contained 81 OTUs in total, with 54 unique. Comparative analysis revealed distinct restructuring between these communities, evidenced by significant divergences in sample‐specific unique OTU composition (p < 0.05). To further quantify community restructuring, alpha diversity metrics were analysed.

The Shannon index was significantly higher in KS2 than in KS1 (Figure ), with KS1 showing a constrained interquartile range and KS2 an expanded one, indicating greater inter‐sample heterogeneity. The Chao1 and ACE indices, which quantify species richness, were significantly greater in KS1 than in KS2 (Figure ), consistent with the reduction in total and unique OTU counts. The Simpson index was significantly higher in KS2 (Figure ), reflecting greater dominance by specific bacterial taxa in the antibiotic‐treated group.

These results correspond to antibiotic‐mediated community simplification: antimicrobial agents selectively eliminate susceptible taxa, reducing overall richness while allowing resistant populations to increase in relative abundance.

Effects of Temperature on Phycosphere Communities

Specific Growth Rate

Specific growth rate quantifies growth velocity within defined time intervals. As presented in Figure , kelp gametophytes exhibited maximal specific growth at 15°C (5.21% d−1), significantly exceeding rates observed at 10°C (3.20% d−1) and 20°C (3.95% d−1). This temperature‐dependent growth pattern identifies 15°C as the thermal optimum for gametophyte development, while the 34.7% reduction at 10°C relative to the 15°C optimum signifies pronounced cold‐temperature growth limitation.

Species Richness Analysis Under Different Temperature Treatments

Following sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform and stringent quality control filtration, high‐fidelity sequencing data were obtained. Subsequent to normalization, analysis of its diversity revealed that (Figure ) identified 8 conserved operational taxonomic units present across all experimental cohorts, delineating a distinct core microbiome. The epiphytic bacteria group PZ1 contained 74 OTUs in total, with 48 unique to this group, whereas the planktonic bacteria groups WK1, WK2, and WK3 contained 80, 70, and 51 OTUs in total, with 62, 43, and 29 unique OTUs, respectively. Comparative analysis revealed distinct restructuring between these communities, evidenced by divergences in sample‐specific unique OTU composition. To further quantify community restructuring, alpha diversity metrics were analysed.

The Shannon index showed no significant difference among PZ1, WK1, WK2, and WK3 (Figure ), although WK3 exhibited a diminished median index with expanded whisker ranges, suggesting reduced diversity metrics and pronounced inter‐sample heterogeneity. The Chao1 and ACE indices, which quantify species richness, showed lower values in WK3 compared to PZ1, WK1, and WK2 (Figure ), consistent with the reduction in total and unique OTU counts observed in the Venn diagram. The Simpson index (Figure ) was elevated in WK3, reflecting greater dominance by specific bacterial taxa under elevated thermal regimes.

These results correspond to temperature‐mediated community restructuring: cultures maintained at 20°C generated the lowest OTU richness indices, indicating that elevated thermal regimes suppress species diversity and reduce overall richness while allowing thermotolerant populations to increase in relative abundance. Conversely, suboptimal temperatures (10°C) sustained multispecies coexistence and maintained richness comparable to PZ1 controls.

Specifically, the 20°C regime induced a substantial restructuring of the planktonic microbiome, while PZ1 occupied a distinct ecological niche that diverged markedly from both the cold‐adapted consortia and the general 20°C planktonic background. While the PERMANOVA result did not reach statistical significance at α = 0.05, the relatively high R2 value and the clear ordination pattern suggest a biologically meaningful thermal niche differentiation among planktonic bacterial assemblages.

Phylum‐Level Analysis

Figure , by comparing the control group (KS1) with the antibiotic‐treated group (KS2), clearly demonstrates the strong selective pressure exerted by antibiotics on the bacterial community. In the control group KS1, Proteobacteria was the absolutely dominant phylum, exhibiting a very high relative abundance. In contrast, in the antibiotic‐treated group KS2, the abundance of this phylum decreased significantly, indicating a substantial inhibitory effect of the antibiotics on its growth. As the abundance of Proteobacteria declined, opportunities for other bacterial phyla increased. The most pronounced change was observed in Bacteroidetes, whose relative abundance increased sharply in KS2 compared to KS1, becoming a major phylum with abundance comparable to that of Proteobacteria.

As shown in Figure , in contrast to the drastic shifts induced by antibiotic treatment, the composition of dominant phyla remained relatively stable across samples subjected to different temperature conditions. Proteobacteria maintained a high relative abundance in all temperature samples, indicating its broad temperature adaptability and satisfactory acclimation to the experimental temperature gradient. Although the dominant phylum remained unchanged, the relative proportions of the various phyla exhibited regular variations in response to temperature. Bacteroidetes dominated the epiphytic PZ1 assemblage but declined progressively within the planktonic fraction as temperature increased, co‐dominating with Proteobacteria at 15°C and being supplanted by Proteobacteria at 20°C.

Conclusions and Discussion

Impact of Antibiotic Treatments on the Bacterial Community Structure in the Phycosphere of Kelp Gametophytes

Penicillin–streptomycin treatment reduced Proteobacteria from 93.02% to 49.70% and enriched Bacteroidetes from 6.98% to 49.20%, with a genus‐level shift from Sulfitobacter and Colwellia to Kordia and Klebsiella.

At the phylum level, the reduction of Proteobacteria and the concomitant enrichment of Bacteroidetes are consistent with the known targets of penicillin and streptomycin and with differences in growth strategy and envelope permeability. Penicillin inhibits peptidoglycan cross‐linking and streptomycin impairs translation, which together preferentially suppress fast‐growing surface‐associated Proteobacteria that rely on sustained biosynthesis to maintain epiphytic populations (Moazed and Noller ). In contrast, Bacteroidetes exhibit relative tolerance linked to outer‐membrane‐mediated permeability control and slower growth, and they can increase in relative abundance when competitors are suppressed (Hedberg and Nord ). Ecologically, Proteobacterial clades create vacant niches and reduce competitive exclusion for nutrient resources. Concurrently, Bacteroidetes can exploit carbon and nitrogen substrates released from lysed sensitive cells (Chabanol et al. ), providing an immediate post‐disturbance growth advantage.

At the genus level, the loss of Sulfitobacter and Colwellia and the enrichment of Kordia and Klebsiella indicate a functional turnover in the epiphytic consortium. Sulfitobacter and Colwellia have been reported to engage in mutualistic interactions with marine algae and can contribute to suppression of opportunistic colonizers through competitive exclusion and production of antimicrobial metabolites; this refers to protection of the host rather than degradation of applied antibiotics (Amin et al. ). Their reduction under antibiotic treatment suggests a weakening of the native microbial barrier on kelp gametophyte surfaces. Kordia includes Bacteroidetes with polysaccharide‐degrading capacities that enable growth on algal‐derived substrates, which may confer a post‐disturbance advantage (Lim et al. ). In the context of long‐term germplasm preservation, the proliferation of polysaccharide‐degrading Kordia may alter the physicochemical properties of the phycosphere mucus layer, potentially affecting gametophyte surface integrity during extended storage. Klebsiella is an opportunistic taxon with documented resistance traits in other systems. Its increase here should be interpreted cautiously as an indicator of community dysbiosis rather than evidence of pathogenicity. The enrichment of Klebsiella in antibiotic‐treated samples raises concerns regarding the proliferation of antimicrobial resistance genes within the preservation system, particularly if antibiotic residues persist.

In relation to kelp preservation, the observed phylum‐ and genus‐level shifts are consistent with a transition from a community that supports colonization resistance to one with reduced barrier function. Because prophylactic antibiotic use did not lower disease risk in our system and coincided with enrichment of opportunistic or disturbance‐favoured taxa, careful evaluation of antimicrobial interventions in germplasm culture is warranted.

Temperature Effects on Phycosphere Communities of Kelp Gametophytes

Temperature variation across the 10°C–20°C range induced quantitative restructuring of the gametophyte phycosphere without triggering wholesale taxonomic turnover. Within this tolerated thermal envelope, temperature acts as a metabolic modulator that adjusts the growth rates and carrying capacities of major bacterial phylogenetic groups, producing abundance shifts without displacing core taxa (Arandia‐Gorostidi et al. ). Consistent with observations from other kelp systems where gradual shifts occur within thermal tolerance limits (Molnar et al. ). the present gametophyte community remained stable across 10°C–20°C, a range far below the approximately 26°C threshold associated with wholesale collapse (Veenhof et al. ). Thus, temperature modulated competitive hierarchies while the ecological filters governing phycosphere colonization stayed intact.

At 15°C, the gametophyte specific growth rate peaked at 5.21% d−1, while pathogenic Vibrio and Pseudomonas concurrently reached their maximum relative abundances. Below 15°C, such as at 10°C, growth rate decreases but the risk of pathogenic invasion is reduced, resulting in a more stable epiphytic community. Above 15°C, such as at 20°C, gametophyte growth rate declines and thermal stress may compromise host health, even though the relative abundance of certain pathogens might decrease. For kelp preservation, this creates a trade‐off: 15°C maximizes biomass accumulation but coincides with the highest contamination risk, whereas lower temperatures like 10°C minimize microbial disturbance at the cost of slower growth. Therefore, the choice of temperature should align with the preservation objective.

These findings highlight a fundamental dichotomy in how antibiotics and temperature regulate epiphytic communities, with direct implications for the design of germplasm preservation protocols.

Implications for Germplasm Preservation

Based on the relationships between treatment, microbial community shifts, and host health, the following strategies are proposed to improve the quality of kelp germplasm preservation. For antibiotic use, the observed qualitative community turnover, characterized by the loss of beneficial mutualists (Sulfitobacter, Colwellia) and the enrichment of opportunistic taxa (Kordia, Klebsiella), indicates that broad‐spectrum antibiotics disrupt the native microbial barrier and create unoccupied niches that facilitate dysbiosis. Chronic application further risks selecting for antimicrobial‐resistant strains. Therefore, prophylactic antibiotic use should be avoided in germplasm preservation protocols. If contamination control is necessary, short‐term, low‐dose, or taxon‐selective interventions are recommended over broad‐spectrum suppression, and only during the initial culture phase rather than long‐term storage. For temperature management, the quantitative restructuring driven by temperature directly modulates pathogenic loads without displacing core taxa. At 15°C, the thermal optimum for gametophyte growth coincides with peak relative abundances of Vibrio (44.11%) and Pseudomonas (15.77%), meaning that conditions favouring biomass accumulation simultaneously elevate disease risk. For short‐term seedling production where rapid growth is prioritized, cultures can be maintained at 15°C, but this requires concurrent microbial monitoring or aseptic handling to suppress pathogen proliferation. Conversely, at 10°C, pathogen abundances are significantly lower and community structure and alpha diversity remain stable. For long‐term germplasm conservation, constant storage at 10°C without antibiotic supplementation is advised. This approach preserves the integrity of the native epiphytic microbiome, maintains colonization resistance against opportunistic invaders, and avoids antibiotic‐induced dysbiosis, thereby improving the viability and quality of preserved gametophytes.
