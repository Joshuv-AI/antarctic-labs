High exposure of pregnant women to SARS-CoV-2 during COVID-19 waves in Senegal
Authors: Abou Abdallah Malick Diouara, Fatou Thiam, Seynabou Coundoul, Noël Magloire Manga, Halimatou Diop Ndiaye, Assane Senghor, Sophie Deli Tene, Sarbanding Sané, Betty Kane, Habibou Sarr, Yakhya Dieye, Seynabou Lo, Fodé Danfakha, Marie Edouard Faye Dieme, Babacar Biaye, Cheikh Momar Nguer, Martine Peeters, Coumba Toure Kane, Ahidjo Ayouba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic raised concerns about SARS-CoV-2 infection in pregnant women, especially regarding pregnancy outcomes and maternal-child health. Despite the limited seroprevalence data in this population category, it is crucial for public health strategies in Africa. Therefore, this study aims to determine the seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 in pregnant women attending antenatal consultations (ANC) in Senegal.MethodsThis is a retrospective serological study using archived plasma samples collected prospectively, ensuring no redundancies, among pregnant women attending ANC, between March and July 2021 during the Delta wave of COVID-19. Plasma samples were stored at -80 °C until analysis for the presence of IgG and IgM antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 using the WANTAI SARS-CoV-2 Ab ELISA (Wantai Biological Pharmacy Enterprise, Beijing), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Bivariate analysis was conducted to evaluate characteristics associated with seropositivity using JMP® Pro Version 15.0.0 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, 1989–2021). For categorical variables with frequencies below 5%, either chi-squared or Fisher tests were applied as appropriate. Significance was defined as p < 0.05 for all tests.ResultA total of 1,227 pregnant women were recruited from five health facilities, including two in Dakar (n = 166) and one in each of the following regional capitals: Saint-Louis (n = 400), Ziguinchor (n = 264), and Kédougou (n = 397). The median age was 25 years [range:18–50]. The overall seroprevalence observed was 64.9% [95% CI: 62.24–67.57], with higher exposure rates in Dakar 71.6% [95% CI: 64.40–77.99], and Ziguinchor 68.1% [95% CI: 62.34–73.51]. Kédougou and Saint-Louis reported 62.9% [95% CI: 58.12–67.58] and 62% [95% CI: 57.15–66.62], respectively. According to age group, the seroprevalence was 62.6% [95% CI: 58.49–66.71], 66.6% [95% CI: 61.62–71.36], 66.3% [95% CI: 59.94–72.24], and 67.2% [95% CI: 58.37–75.01], respectively, for [18–23], [24–29], [30–35], and ≥ 36 years old. Despite these observed variations in the seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 across localities, differences are not statistically significant (p = 0.7024).ConclusionIn summary, this study highlights a high seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 among pregnant women, with higher exposure rates in Dakar and Ziguinchor. These findings demonstrated that SARS-CoV-2 has spread massively in Senegal, despite few reported cases, similar to trends observed in other African countries. Ultimately, this study reveals seroepidemiological data for SARS-CoV-2 in pregnant women, previously unknown at the national level.Keywords: SARS-CoV-2, Seroprevalence, Pregnancy and COVID-19, Senegal

Background

Since the declaration of COVID-19 as a pandemic in March 2020 [1], evidence has shown that SARS-CoV-2 infection, transmission, and mortality have often been substantially underestimated [2, 3]. At that time, SARS-CoV-2 exposure among pregnant women remained poorly documented, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa [2, 4].

Despite the need for prenatal consultations, most pregnant women have avoided health facilities for fear of SARS-CoV-2 infection. These factors, especially during the second and third COVID-19 waves in Senegal [5, 16], could increase the risk to pregnant women. The pandemic has raised concerns about the impact of SARS-CoV-2 infection in pregnant women [2], including pregnancy outcome and mother-child health during and after pregnancy [2, 6, 7]. Among the main concerns is the higher mortality risk for pregnant women with SARS-CoV-2 compared to age-matched nonpregnant individuals [2, 4, 8, 9]. Further risks such as preterm deliveries, complications needing intensive care, and invasive ventilation should also be considered [6, 10–12]. Despite concerns about COVID-19 among pregnant women in Africa, there is little published SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence data in this population [2, 4, 9, 13]. Therefore, it is necessary to investigate the extent of SARS-CoV-2 exposure in pregnant women.

In Senegal, the first case of COVID-19 was registered on March 2, 2020 [14]. During the first wave of COVID-19 (March – November 2020), all confirmed cases were isolated and monitored at the Treatment Center for Epidemics (TCE) until recovery. The second wave occurred from December 2020 to March 2021. The third wave lasted from July to September 2021 [5]. During these periods, only patients with the most severe or high-risk of the disease required hospitalization. Individuals who tested positive with minor or no symptoms – diagnosed via contact tracing or screening at the Blaise Diagne International Airport and other border crossings – were quarantined with regular follow-up at the out-of-hospital TCE [15]. Non-pharmaceutical interventions included population sensitization on prevention measures, bans on large gatherings, mandatory face masks, schools and universities closures, and lockdown restrictions [16].

To address the COVID-19 pandemic in Senegal, several strategies have been implemented. These include the Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) system [17, 18], the Community Event-Based Surveillance (CEBS) system [19], and the activation of the Health Emergency Operation Center [20]. In addition, as defined by WHO, there was also a formal identification of COVID-19 cases, including their active case-contact detection [21] in order to identify transmission clusters [22]. Despite this diversity of approaches, it seems important to highlight the absence of a strategy specifically targeting vulnerable populations such as pregnant women. Vaccination began on February 23, 2021. It first targeted health personnel, the elderly, and those with comorbidities. The campaign gradually extended to the general population. For pregnant women, vaccination was available only from August 9, 2021. At the time of the second national COVID-19 seroprevalence survey (November 2–14, 2021), vaccination coverage was already 18% lower [23]. In the laboratory component, diagnostic strategies have been pursued, focusing on molecular methods for detecting viral genetic material, antigen tests, and serological assays, as well as innovations to enhance diagnostic sensitivity and capabilities [24, 25]. Given the health emergency, in Senegal, several initiatives focused on managing SARS-CoV-2 infection emerged in the first months of the pandemic. Laboratory strategy involved first, testing all symptomatic suspected cases and systematically testing the contacts of confirmed cases. Due to the high cost and resource demands, this approach was changed and limited to test contacts cases with symptoms or at high risk of death, such as elderly people or those with chronic diseases [22]. Initially, only RT-PCR (reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction) or qRT-PCR (quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction), both molecular techniques that amplify viral RNA to detectable levels, were used as the gold standard [24, 25]. Due to the constraints of these tests, additional diagnostic strategies, including antigen-based and serological approaches, were increasingly considered.

Serological methods, which detect antibodies indicating past and present exposure to the virus, can also help monitor the epidemic dynamics in the target population and aid in controlling the rapid global spread of SARS-CoV-2 [25, 26]. In the absence of universal access to RT-PCR or qRT-PCR, and even if it is not recommended for establishing the diagnosis of COVID-19, serological detection of present and post-exposure constitutes a major alternative in terms of public health strategies. Thus, this study aimed to determine the seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 infection in pregnant women attending antenatal consultations (ANCs) in Senegal.

Materials and methods

Patients and study design

This ancillary study is part of the EPIVHE (Environment and Epidemiology of the Hepatitis E Virus) project, which was a seroepidemiological survey of hepatitis E among pregnant women attending antenatal consultations (ANC) in Senegal. This is a retrospective serological study using archived plasma samples collected prospectively, ensuring no redundancies, among pregnant women attending ANC, between March and July 2021 during the Delta wave of COVID-19. Details on sample size calculation, recruitment sites, data collection, criteria for participants inclusion and non-inclusion, and relevant socio-demographic information are fully described in parent study [27]. During the study period, no participant showed clinical signs of severe COVID-19, no hospitalizations were reported, and none had received a COVID-19 vaccine.

A written and signed informed consent form was obtained from each participant before the interview and sample collection. Ethical and administrative authorization was obtained from the National Health Research Ethics Committee (N°000130/MSA/CNRES/Sec) and the Ministry of Public Health and Social Action of Senegal (N°00000582/MSAS/DPRS/DR). In addition to the EPIVHE study, this coverage extended to all ancillary studies based on secondary exploitation of collected biological samples and their associated data, as specified in the research protocol submitted for approval.

Sample preparation and anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies detection

All leftover plasma samples stored at -80 °C with sufficient volume (> 200 µL) were subjected to a qualitative assay based on the immunoenzymatic method. The test used was the WANTAI SARS-CoV-2 Ab ELISA (Wantai Biological Pharmacy Enterprise, Beijing), and it was performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. It should be noted that this test enables the qualitative detection of total antibodies (including IgM and IgG), markers of the immune response to SARS-CoV-2 in patients suspected of having a previous infection, or for the detection of seroconversion in patients following a known recent infection. The WANTAI SARS-CoV-2 Ab ELISA is a two-step incubation antigen “sandwich” enzyme immunoassay kit that utilizes polystyrene microwell strips pre-coated with the recombinant SARS-CoV-2 spike protein receptor-binding domain (S1/RBD) antigen.

The reported sensitivity and specificity of the test are 94.36% and 100% respectively, according to the manufacturer [28]. Optical density was read using the MICRO READ 1000 ELISA Plate Analyser (Global Diagnostics B, Belgium). The results are calculated by relating each specimen absorbance (A) value to the plate’s cut-off value (C.O.). For the calculation of the cut-off value (C.O.), it was C.O. = Nc + 0.16. Nc = the mean of the absorbance value for three negative controls. A ratio of signal to Cut-off values was calculated for each sample, and a sample was declared to be negative if the index < 1, positive if the index was ≥ 1, and borderline if the index was in the range 0.9 to 1.1. All samples declared positive in the first tests were retested by WANTAI SARS-CoV-2 Ab in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

The SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence was calculated as the number of individuals with specific SARS-CoV-2 antibodies divided by the total number of individuals in the sample, with a 95% confidence level. The selected general factors examined were participants’ age, residential locality, recruitment period, level of education, marital status, and professional status. These were selected due to their potential association with SARS-CoV-2 infection as observed in other studies [4, 6, 8–12, 29–31]. To assess factors associated with seropositivity, bivariate analyses were performed with JMP® Pro Version 15.0.0 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, 1989–2021). For frequencies below 5, chi2 or Fisher tests were performed. In all cases, the confidence interval was set at 95%, and the p-values < 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Demographic and socio-economic characteristics

Geographic distribution of recruitment

During the EPIVHE seroepidemiological survey [27], we recruited 1227 pregnant women from five health facilities. Two facilities were located in Dakar: the gynecological clinic of CHUN Aristide le DANTEC (n = 50) and Gaspard Kamara Health Center (n = 116). The other study sites were the Saint-Louis Regional Hospital Center (RHC) (n = 400), the Kédougou Health District (n = 397), and the Néma district of the Ziguinchor Health Center (n = 264), all of which are indicated in Fig. 1.

Participant characteristics

Regarding age distribution, 43% of participants were in the [18–23] group, 29.1% were in the [24–29] group, and 18.2% were in the [30–35] group. Only 9.7% were aged 36 or older, including 3.6% (n = 45) over 40. The median age of participants was 25 years [18–50 years].

Regarding the level of education, only 9% of participants had reached a higher level of education. The overall illiteracy rate was 31.7%, with a higher proportion recorded in Kédougou (58.7%). In contrast to other localities, 25.9% of women in Dakar had completed higher education. Only 18.7% of participants had regular income (salaried or self-employed); among them, 45.8% resided in Dakar, and 11.8% in Saint-Louis. In this study, this last point was evaluated as an example of non-pharmaceutical prevention measures. The aim was to determine the level of adoption of this measure among pregnant women, regardless of their educational level, age, place of residence, or socioeconomic status.

SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence, variability, and potential associated factors

Overall SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence

Based on total anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies (Ab) measurement, the overall seroprevalence was 64.9% [95% CI: 62.24–67.57], with higher exposure rates in Dakar 71.6% [95% CI: 64.40–77.99] and Ziguinchor 68.1% [95% CI: 62.34–73.51]. Kédougou and Saint-Louis recorded 62.9% [95% CI: 58.12–67.58] and 62% [95% CI: 57.15–66.62], respectively (Fig. 1; Table 2).

SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence and potential associated factors

In summary, a high overall seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 was observed among pregnant women attending ANC. A numerically higher seroprevalence was also recorded in Dakar and Ziguinchor, while no statistically significant differences were observed by age, location, education, marital status, or occupation. These findings indicate that SARS-CoV-2 exposure was likely widespread among pregnant women attending ANC during the study period.

Discussion

Summary of key findings

This is the first large-scale study to document the seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies in pregnant women in Senegal. Retrospective analysis of sera collected during the second and third COVID-19 waves revealed a high SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence among pregnant women attending ANC (64.9% [95% CI: 62.24–67.57]) in Senegal during 2021, suggesting substantial prior exposure in this population despite limited routine case detection. Seroprevalence was numerically higher in Dakar and Ziguinchor, but no statistically significant differences were observed across the demographic and socioeconomic variables examined.

Comparison with previous studies

The overall seroprevalence found in this study was higher than those recorded during the first national survey (28.4% in November-December 2020) [32] and the second national survey (72.2% in November 2021) [23]. Participants from Dakar had a numerically higher seroprevalence than those from other localities (Fig. 1). Previous Senegalese studies also reported this variability [32–34]. Although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.7024), Dakar, the capital, remains the epicenter of the epidemic [22]. Dakar has strong international connections, the highest population density, and most of the country’s economic activities [35]. Otherwise, fewer participants were enrolled in Dakar (less than half of those in Saint-Louis and Kédougou, 1.5 times fewer than in Ziguinchor). During recruitment, the magnitude of the COVID-19 crisis in Dakar may have reduced antenatal care attendance, as reported elsewhere [2, 7].

Our findings showed that Ziguinchor was the second locality with the highest SARS-CoV-2 total Ab seroprevalence (68.1%) (Fig. 1). High seroprevalences were also found in the border region after the first 3 transmission waves. Among pregnant women in the Gambia, the rate was 90%. Among adults involved in healthcare and research, it was 18% [36]. In unvaccinated people living with HIV in Guinea-Bissau, it was 27.7% [37]. In the general population of Guinea Conakry, the prevalence was 42.4% [38, 39].

Interpretation and public health implications

Ultimately, the high seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 total Ab observed in this unvaccinated subpopulation could be consistent with sustained community transmission during later epidemic waves in Senegal, including periods when more transmissible variants circulated nationally [40, 41]. The lack of targeted interventions for pregnant women during the pandemic, along with routine surveillance systems that rely mostly on symptom screening, may explain the high seroprevalence. Together, these gaps suggest that many infections in pregnant women may have gone undetected.

The heterogeneity in SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalences observed aligns with findings elsewhere. In their review, Lewis et al. documented strong heterogeneity both within countries, such as between urban and rural areas, and across age groups – as well as across African countries and subregions, emphasizing the need for targeted serosurveys and interventions [13]. In Senegal, previous national population-based cross-sectional survey [32] and studies conducted in healthcare settings [33] and among hemodialysis patients [34] have all reported varying seroprevalence rates by locality, with consistently high rates in Dakar and Ziguinchor. Similar to the sentinel surveillance program for HIV and other sexually transmitted infections (STIs) established decades ago, ANC platform could be integrated into epidemic surveillance or maternal health preparedness strategies during future outbreaks.

Laboratory strategies, interventions and public health implications

Contrary to what is usually or even systematically done in northern countries, RT-PCR tests from nasopharyngeal samples are not offered to pregnant women admitted for labour and delivery in Senegal [25, 31]. Access to molecular diagnostics was still limited. During the sampling period, the use of rapid diagnostic tests, including ELISA tests, was not authorized by local authorities for the diagnosis of SARS-CoV-2 infection. Only RT-PCR tests were mandatory at that time. These detect the active infection of SARS-CoV-2 in both symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals [22]. Therefore, individuals with resolved infections or those with ongoing infections who are no longer testing positive by RT-PCR may be missed. This limits the ability to measure the extent of exposure to SARS-CoV-2 during a pandemic period. In such situation, and retrospectively, the use of serological tests based on the detection of antigen, IgM, and IgG or IgG antibodies specific to SARS-CoV-2 [25] would enable the examination of the extent of exposure. However, diagnostic performance would be influenced by the timing and duration of seroconversion, which varies depending on the patient and disease severity [25]. Some studies have also reported the rapid decay of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies over time [42, 43]. Others suggest that IgG stability persists for at least 5 months after infection [39].

In this study, we used the Wantai SARS-CoV-2 Total Ab ELISA. This test has shown satisfactory anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies detection performance in samples collected 14 days post-onset of symptoms in both hospitalized patients and those with mild or asymptomatic infections [26, 28, 44, 45]. This test has been widely used in other studies in Africa to measure seroprevalence [13, 26, 32, 44, 46]. Considering the variability in the persistence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies [25, 39, 42, 43] and the possibility of premature sample collection after a positive SARS-CoV-2 PCR [25, 26, 44], we cannot exclude the possibility of underestimating the seroprevalences observed in this study.

Strengths and limitations

The major limitations of this study were the absence of information on maternal and perinatal outcomes. Unlike several similar studies, our study does not provide data on gestational age, pre-pregnancy diabetes, pre-pregnancy hypertension, or pregnancy outcomes such as preterm birth, delivery with or without complications, maternal and neonatal mortality, or vertical or horizontal transmission [4, 6, 10–12, 29]. Furthermore, this study relied primarily on bivariate analysis without adjustment for confounding factors. We also do not include pregnant women exposed to SARS-CoV-2 who did not receive follow-up care in the ANC services of participating health facilities. It should be noted the small sample sizes in some subgroups of participants, such as the ‘divorced’ and ‘single’ groups compared to the others should be taken into account in the interpretation of these results. Therefore, the results cannot be generalized to this population. However, despite the heterogeneity in seroprevalences between localities, this study fills a gap in knowledge about the level of exposure to SARS-CoV-2 among pregnant women attending to ANC at the national level. This information demonstrates the relevance and added value of implementing SARS-CoV-2 serological-based diagnostics routinely, in addition to molecular tests.

Future directions

For future research, these findings encourage a broader exploration of the determinants of seroprevalence. Investigating social determinants of health, cultural factors, or even access to healthcare services could provide valuable insights.

Conclusions

This study highlighted a high seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 (64.9% [95% CI: 62.24–67.57]) in pregnant women attending an antenatal consultation (ANC). These findings also showed that SARS-CoV-2 has spread massively in Senegal, despite the few reported cases, as in other African countries. No statistically significant differences in seroprevalence by age and locality were found, as well as association with factors such as education level, marital status, and professional status. These results suggest that the spread of SARS-CoV-2 might not be influenced by traditional demographic or socioeconomic factors. The SARS-CoV-2 exposure rate could be linked to the participants’ enrollment period, which overlaps with the spread of the more transmissible variant during the second and third waves of COVID-19 in Senegal. For public health strategies, these findings have significant implications, including the need for interventions for decision-making regarding care for pregnant women in a pandemic context. This implies more research to understand the factors driving seroprevalence.
