 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

In the event of cladding rupture during a postulated LOCA in a pressurized water reactor, fuel particles,
along with fission gases, can be expelled into the reactor core from the fractured fuel rod, a phenomenon
referred to as fuel dispersal. The initial stage of fuel dispersal is strongly influenced by the high-pressure
ejection of fuel fragments, the size and geometry of the ruptured cladding, and the depressurization history
of the fuel rod during the postulated LOCA transient. Depending on the location of the burst orifice relative
to the quench front, the dispersal event represents an intricate three-phase flow and heat transfer
phenomenon, where high-temperature fuel particles carried by the fission gases interact with the coolant
within the narrow subchannels of the fuel assemblies, inducing localized phase change. Given the unique
multiphysics nature of this phenomena, the current study develops a dedicated computational framework
to predict the mass distribution and cooling of dispersing fuel particles, facilitating post-accident assessment
and management of the fuel assemblies.

Considering the scale of nuclear reactor applications, a continuum three-fluid model is proposed for
simulating the transport of solids within the reactor core. With high-temperature fuel fragments within the
liquid media, nucleation sites inducing phase changes are dispersed within the flow domain. Coupled with
the fact that the transient dispersal event occurs on different time scales than other three-phase flow
applications, this study derives a time-averaged three-fluid flow model without losing generality. The
assumptions regarding the continuum treatment of the solid phase and the modeling of fuel dispersal
behavior are incorporated to simplify the governing equations and derive applicable closure relations. The
computational validation of the model was conducted using adiabatic experimental results obtained from
ongoing research at Oregon State University, focusing on characterizing fuel dispersal behavior during
simulated LOCA conditions. Settlement characteristics of the solids, quantified by the probability
distribution of equivalent particles, closely matched the probability density functions reported in
experimental studies.

The transport of fuel particles within a scaled 5 × 5 lattice of a pressurized-water reactor rod bundle
geometry was modeled through a two-fluid Eulerian framework. The required boundary conditions were
evaluated from the fuel performance code BISON in a postulated large-break LOCA scenario. The
modeling framework considered solid fuel particles as granular matter, interacting with the gaseous dry
steam phase and fission gases through the governing interfacial momentum exchange between the
participating fluids. The simulation results provided the volume fraction of the solids obtained at the bottom
surface of the enclosing tank geometry.

 ii

Postulated LOCA leading to fuel dispersal phenomena involves the strong coupling between fuel
thermomechanics, cladding deformation, thermal-hydraulics, and fuel particle transport. Incorporation of
such a strong coupling in numerical simulation is performed by coupling the multiphysics solvers. In the
case of fuel dispersal, a strong coupled simulation can be performed by coupling the BISON code for fuel
performance, the TRACE code for system-level thermal hydraulics, and fuel particle transport in
Multiphysics Object-Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE). For such intricate infrastructure, the
MOOSE Framework eases the data transfer between codes. The recent version of MOOSE has incorporated
the Navier-Stokes module for the fluid flow. An exploratory exercise was done to gain familiarity with
finite volume capabilities in the MOOSE framework to incorporate the Spalart-Allmaras (SA) turbulence
model. New finite-volume and auxiliary kernels were introduced to assemble the SA transport equation,
compute turbulent viscosity, and evaluate wall distance and diagnostic turbulence terms, fully integrated
with existing Navier-Stokes modules. A turbulent lid-driven cavity at a Reynolds number of approximately
10,000 is used for verification. MOOSE shows the robust solver convergence and produces the turbulent
features. But it underpredicts the velocity profile and turbulent quantities, emphasizing the need to develop
improved SA near-wall treatments (e.g., low-Re corrections or wall functions) as a key direction for future
work.

1. INTRODUCTION TO NUMERICAL SIMULATION OF FUEL DISPERSAL STUDY

1.1 Fuel Dispersal Study Under LOCA Conditions

The key contributing phenomena observed during the FFRD evaluation are fuel rod bending, cladding
ballooning, cladding bursting, fuel rod depressurization, high-burnup fuel fragmentation, fuel relocation,
and fuel dispersal (Capps et al., 2021). While these phenomena are highly interrelated, the size distribution
of the fuel particles, rupture size and shape, depressurization rate, and amount of fuel discharged into the
coolant system are the governing boundary conditions required for modeling the high-burnup fuel dispersal
behavior. Of the studies performed to examine fuel rod performance during postulated LOCA transients,
experiments conducted at the integral test facility using the Halden reactor (Wiesenack, 2013), the semi-
integral tests at Studsvik (Flanagan et al., 2013), the Argonne National Laboratory in-cell LOCA integral
tests (Yan et al., 2002), and the Oak Ridge National Laboratory Severe Accident Test Station (SATS)
(Raftery et al., 2018) tests are most relevant to the study of high burnup fuels.

The in-pile Halden series of experiments (Wiesenack, 2013; Wiesenack, 2015) employed both nuclear and
electrical heating under simulated in-reactor conditions to investigate the postulated LOCA phenomena.
The gas fill pressure within the fuel rod in these experiments was between 6 and 7 MPa and the burnup
ranged from 44 to 92 GWd/MTU. Some of the important findings reported are:
 • The degree of fragmentation was influenced by the local strain within the fuel rod and is
 directly proportional to the fuel burnup
 • The degree of ballooning was affected by the local gas pressure, which in turn affects the
 fuel fragmentation and relocation
 • The fuel packing factor increased beyond a threshold burnup value, thus approaching the
 plug-like flow behavior

The out-of-pile NRC experiments performed at Studsvik used irradiated rods with burnup values of 55 - 69
GWd/MTU and gas fill pressures ranging from 8 to 11 MPa that were electrically heated by infrared
radiation (Flanagan et al., 2013). These investigations, along with the in-cell LOCA tests and SATS tests,
support the hypothesis of a threshold burnup value that induced fine fuel fragmentation, which was in line
with the Halden findings. The reported size distribution of the fuel fragments was 0.125–4 mm, with 100%
of fuel having fragment sizes under 1 mm for burnups exceeding 82 GWd/MTU (Capps et al., 2020).
Depending on the cladding material (with consistent cladding thickness and similar outer diameter across
experiments), internal pressure, and burst temperature condition, the cladding rupture shape is quantified

 1

by its length and width and varied between 7 and 24 mm in length and 0.2 and 17.5 mm in width,
respectively (Capps et al., 2020). In addition, Capps et al. (2020) conducted a study to estimate the location
of cladding burst. They used experimental data from previous LOCA tests where cladding rupture was
induced by varying heating rates and initial rod internal pressures in a steam environment. Based on the
data, burst criteria were developed, which correlated the cladding burst temperature with hoop stress and
cladding hoop strain. The results indicated that the rod has a potential to rupture at approximately 75% of
its axial length from the bottom.

1.2 Multiphase CFD

Three-phase flows are commonly occurring flow types and play a crucial role in various industrial
processes, such as biochemical, chemical, food processing, petrochemical, and pharmaceutical industries
(Pan et al., 2016). Several industrial applications have three-phase flows with phase changes that are lacking
in detailed modelling frameworks.

Currently, there are three primary approaches used in Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) to simulate
multiphase flow systems: the Eulerian method, the Lagrangian method, and Direct Numerical Simulation
(DNS).
  I. Eulerian Method: This approach treats phases as fully interpenetrating continuous phases, using
 the same form of conservation equations for all phases. It requires additional closure relationships,
 such as the stress and viscosity of the solid phase. The Eulerian method provides macroscopic
 average quantities valuable to engineers, enabling the simulation of device-scale systems or
 reactors.
 II. Lagrangian Method: This approach solves the Newtonian equations of motion for individual
 dispersed phase objects, such as particles or bubbles, accounting for particle collisions and
 interactions with other phases. It tracks the trajectories of individual particles, describing the
 discrete character of the solid phase at micro or meso scales. This method realistically accounts for
 particle-wall and particle-particle interactions.
III. Direct Numerical Simulation (DNS): In this method, the grid size is typically much smaller than
 the size of the dispersed phase objects. The moving interface is represented using implicit or explicit
 schemes, including the moving-grid, grid-free, and fixed-grid methods. The front tracking (FT) and
 front capturing (FC) techniques are commonly used to predict interface position and movement.
 FT defines and tracks the interface explicitly through Lagrangian markers on a fixed grid, while
 FC treats moving and deformable interfaces implicitly using a marker function on a fixed grid.

 2

Closure models are required to capture micro-scale information. For example, the Immersed Boundary
Method (IBM) resolves the flow field around every particle, providing detailed micro-scale data that can
help formulate improved correlations for higher scales.

Multiphase flows exhibit multi-scale phenomena (macro, meso, and micro scales) in reactors, presenting
significant modeling challenges, especially in three-phase fluidized bed reactors (FBRs). To model three-
phase flow at various spatial and temporal scales under limited computing capacity, researchers often
combine multi-scale models, selecting methods for individual phases from the three approaches mentioned
above.

With increasing reactor scale, the Lagrangian or DNS approaches require substantial computational
resources to simulate flow behaviors. Although these methods can theoretically be used for any length scale,
their application is mostly limited to experimental or micro scales due to high computational costs.
Nevertheless, DNS and Lagrangian approaches require fewer empirical closure relations and offer higher
accuracy than the Eulerian approach.

To achieve accurate multi-scale flow descriptions in various reactor sizes while managing computational
feasibility, a balanced multi-scale modeling approach is essential. The following are four of the common
multi-scale models used to simulate gas-liquid-solid FBRs:
  I. Pseudo Two-Fluid Model: Based on the Eulerian approach, this model treats the solid and liquid
 phases as a pseudo-homogeneous suspension with varying density and viscosity modified by
 particle loading. It simplifies a three-phase system into a two-fluid system, reducing numerical
 costs while providing sufficient information, especially for systems with small particles.
 II. Three-Fluid Model: This model treats all three phases as interpenetrating continua using a
 Eulerian approach. It requires closure relationships for interphase interactions and solid phase
 properties like pressure and viscosity. While it has low computational costs, it needs numerous
 empirical closures, limiting its accuracy.
III. Two-Fluid Model + Discrete Particle Method (DPM): This model uses the Eulerian approach
 for gas and liquid phases and the Lagrangian approach for the solid phase. It tracks the particle
 trajectories directly from forces acting on them, making it computationally intensive and suitable
 for applications incurring low particle loading.
IV. CFD + FT/FC + DPM: This model combines the front tracking or front capturing approach for
 dispersed gas bubbles, DPM for solid particles, and the Eulerian approach for the continuous liquid

 3

 phase. Although it provides detailed descriptions, it is impractical for industrial-scale reactors due
 to computational limitations.

Considering the scale of nuclear reactor applications during accidental conditions, the Eulerian three-fluid
modeling framework is deemed suitable, providing sufficient detail at reasonable computational costs. The
Eulerian treatment of the solid phase is credited to the works of Anderson & Jackson (1967) and Ishii
(1975), who presented a formal averaging procedure for the dispersed phase governing equations. Van
Wachem et al. (2001) reported that the results from either model were similar on an engineering scale with
differences in microscopic features associated with localized solids distribution, and the study further stated
that the similarity between the model predictions was mainly due to the relative dominance of the interfacial
drag and buoyancy forces acting on the solid particles. The difference in the treatment of the solid-phase
stress term played a minor role.

An important aspect of the current study is the continuum description of the fragmented fuel particles during
the dispersal event. This aspect of the study is covered in sufficient detail and is documented in this report.
The subsequent section summarizes the specific objectives of the research on modeling and simulation of
fuel dispersal behavior.

2. CONTINUUM THREE-FLUID MODEL TO STUDY FUEL DISPERSAL PHENOMENA

2.1 Introduction

Three-phase flows are prevalent in numerous industrial processes, spanning gas-liquid-solid fluidization
systems and slurry bubble column reactors, with applications in chemical, petrochemical, pharmaceutical,
biochemical, and food processing industries (Pan et al., 2016). Despite their practical importance, there is
a notable lack of comprehensive frameworks for modeling three-phase flows under non-isothermal
conditions. Furthermore, the challenge posed by the interaction of dispersed solid particles carried by the
gas media with the fluid phase raises concerns regarding the adequacy of existing modeling frameworks
(Majumdar et al., 1997; Matonis et al., 2002; Schallenberg et al., 2005; Panneerselvam et al., 2009;
Hamidipour et al., 2012) developed for fluidized systems and slurry bubble column reactors. Additionally,
within the nuclear engineering field, there is an essential need for a flow model dedicated to analyzing high-
temperature, high-pressure transient accident scenarios involving interactions among solid, liquid, and gas
phases. Although the Eulerian three-fluid flow model naturally extends from the two-fluid model (Ishii,
1975) for gas and liquid two-phase flows, reevaluating the closure relations and the forcing terms is
necessary to accommodate the third participating solid phase. This is particularly important in the context
of fuel dispersal studies, where the dispersed solid phase carries sufficient enthalpy to induce boiling in the
liquid phase. To develop a modeling framework for such a phase change flow application, this study
provides a formal derivation of the time-averaged governing equations and closure relations, ensuring
generality. Assumptions regarding the continuum treatment of the solid phase and the modeling of fuel
dispersal are then incorporated to simplify the governing equations and derive an applicable set of closure
relations. The remainder of this chapter is organized as follows. Section 2.2 provides an overview of fuel
dispersal phenomena near the burst orifice. It describes the governing equations for three-fluid continuum
modeling, the closure relations for solids pressures and solids stress, the closure relation for mass transfer,
the closure relation for interphase momentum exchange, and low-energy fuel-coolant interactions. Section
2.3 describes the experimental database and geometry setup. Section 2.4 describes the initial and boundary
conditions for fuel dispersal simulation. Section 2.5 presents the results and accompanying discussion of
and presents the results of adiabatic three-fluid model validation studies. Finally, the concluding remarks
are provided in Section 2.6.

2.2 Fuel Dispersal Phenomena in Presence of Liquid Coolant Near Burst Orifice

 7

The transport of ejected fuel fragments within reactor pressure vessel transients through various flow
regimes depending on the local flow conditions encountered downstream of the ruptured location. A
hypothetical accident scenario is considered, where the burst location is presumed to be below the
quenching front. This case is chosen as a representative scenario posing the greatest challenge in modeling
the fuel dispersal behavior. Depending on factors such as fuel particle mass flow rate, fission gas release
rate, and size distribution of the dispersed fuel fragments, the transport of the fuel fragments is governed
by (I) extent of gas blanketing due to fission gas jetting into the coolant, (II) interaction with fission gases,
(III) fuel-coolant interaction, and (IV) impingement on internal wall boundaries such as neighboring
cladding, internals, and the reactor pressure vessel wall. These mechanisms are postulated as primary
contributors influencing the trajectory and settlement of dispersed fuel particles. Figure 2.1 provides an
illustrative representation of the release of fuel fragments carried by fission gases upon rupture of fuel
cladding.

 Figure 2.1. Schematics of fission gas release and fuel dispersal from a ruptured fuel rod cladding.

 I. Extent of gas blanketing due to fission gas jetting into the coolant
Following an event resulting in cladding failure, the fission gases contained within the fuel rod escape into
the coolant fluid. The high-pressure fission gases carry sufficient energy to potentially cause dry out in the
immediate vicinity and result in elevated temperatures on neighboring fuel pins upon impingement
(Hoglund et al., 1971). However, the chance of neighboring pin failure due to gas impingement is discarded
regardless of the gas release rate based on studies reported by van Erp et al. (1974) in liquid metal-cooled
fast breeder reactors. The fission gas release takes the form of horizontal submerged gas jets into liquid
crossflows as it transitions from sonic discharge to sub-sonic flows. The high-momentum discharge of
fission gases assists in carrying the fragmented fuel particles into the coolant system. A recent study

 8

conducted by Epstein et al. (2023) on failed annular fuel pins in a lead-cooled fast reactor developed a
model for two-phase fuel-gas ejection mass flow rates and discussed the heating implications on
neighboring fuel pins due to gas blanketing. The study also proposed a heat transfer model for the target
fuel pin's impingement surface, based on a homogeneous model for the three-component fuel, fission gas,
and entrained coolant flow (Epstein et al., 2023).

Critical to the current study on modeling the fuel dispersal behavior in LWRs due to fission gas jetting are
transient gas release rates, coolant entrainment due to shearing of the gas-liquid interface, the vaporization
heat transfer rate at the gas-liquid interface, and wall heat transfer rates on the impinging surfaces within
the coolant channel. The gas release rate influences the zone of gas blanketing that subsequently affects the
trajectory of the ejected fuel particles. The high-pressure compressible gas jet expands at the near ejection
region where liquid droplets entrain due to shearing of the gas-liquid interface. Furthermore, the volume of
the gaseous phase further increases due to the evolution of the vapor phase facilitated by the evaporation
heat transfer between high-temperature fission gases interacting with the coolant liquid. The gas-to-wall
heat transfer rate serves as a heat flux boundary condition for the gas-phase energy transport. The inlet
conditions at the cladding rupture location are assumed to be known boundary conditions based on
predictions made from fuel performance codes, such as BISON (Williamson et al., 2021) under accident
conditions. For the current framework, the condensation of volatile fission gases and the chemical reaction
of fission gases with the coolant are neglected.

 II. Interaction of fuel fragments with fission gases
The fission gas phase acts as the carrier phase for the transport of the fragmented fuel particles out of the
ruptured fuel rod. The buoyancy and the interfacial forces acting on the gas-solid interface govern the
trajectory of the fuel particles. The fission gas flow field is also affected by the motion of the solid phase.
From a modeling point of view, a granular flow assumption for the solid phase enables treating the solid
phase using continuum equations, similar to the fluid flow, to describe the solid bulk flow behavior. The
particle concentration near the cladding rupture may correspond to a dense granular flow regime, where
particle-particle interactions play an important role in determining the flow properties. The fragmented fuel
phase, being almost 103 times denser than the gas phase (compared to xenon gas density), tends to be largely
governed by particle inertia. However, very fine fragments might stay entrained within the gaseous phase.
An assumption of thermal equilibrium between the ejecting gas and the fuel fragment phase helps simplify
the required thermal boundary conditions at the inlet burst location.

 III. Fuel coolant interaction

 9

The fuel coolant interaction, commonly referred to as FCI, involves excessive amounts of vapor production
when hot fuel particles rapidly release their energy into the surrounding coolant (Raynaud, 2012). A shorter
timescale of vapor generation compared to vapor expansion may lead to local pressurization causing high
pressure shock waves to propagate through the domain. This phenomenon is referred to as a steam
explosion, which is a potential hazard in LWRs when molten reactor core materials come in contact with
residual coolant water in the reactor vessel after a prolonged period of non-availability of core cooling (Park
et al., 1999). In the context of FFRD studies under design basis LOCA conditions, energetic FCI is unlikely
as the cladding rupture temperature is well below the fuel melt temperature (~2,200°C) (Park et al., 1999).
Therefore, from a modeling standpoint, the occurrence of a steam explosion during FFRD is precluded.
Unlike energetic FCI, the fuel fragments remain as solid particles, and the generation of the vapor phase
occurs at the solid-liquid interfaces. Depending on solid-liquid flow regimes and relative significance of
the evolved vapor phase, the trajectory of the fuel fragments is influenced by the interfacial forces acting
on the fuel particles. The solid-liquid flow regimes are classified into homogeneous or heterogeneous flows
based on the particle Reynolds number and local volume fraction of the particle phase.

 IV. Impingement of fuel fragments on internal wall boundaries
The coolant flow cross-section within the reactor core depicts a complex geometry, where the separation
between adjacent fuel rods is roughly equal to the diameter of fuel rods. Furthermore, because of the internal
support members, such as spacer grids, there is local variation in the flow cross-section along the length of
the reactor core. Following a rupture on the fuel cladding, the ejected fission gas along with the fuel
fragments impinge on the neighboring wall structures. Epstein et al. (2023) developed a heat transfer model
for the impinging molten fuel fragments carried by the fission gases in lead-cooled fast reactors. Following
the discussions made above sub-section, for LWR applications, the dispersed fuel fragments are considered
as solid particles that exhibit instantaneous collisions with the wall surface, as opposed to molten fragments
that have an affinity to attach to the impinging surface. Therefore, the heat transfer effects associated with
fuel fragments impinging on the wall surface can be neglected, while the trajectory of the fuel fragments is
chiefly be affected by to collisions with wall boundaries.

2.2.1.1 Closure Relations for Solids Pressure and Solids Stress

The characterization of the continuum solid phase is established on the granular flow assumption, wherein
the solid phase flow is treated analogously to a that of a granular medium consisting of a collection of
macroscopic particles. The flow dynamics of granular materials are categorized into two distinct regimes:
the frictional and the kinetic flow regimes. Within the frictional flow regime, stresses emerge due to the
shear capacity of densely packed granular material, while in the kinetic flow regime, stresses result from
instantaneous particle-particle collisions and their associated momentum transfer. Achieving a unified
model capable of describing solid stress across different regimes remains a challenge (Savage, 1998).
Researchers have addressed this issue by defining the solid phase’s stress tensor through a superposition of
frictional and kinetic contributions (Johnson and Jackson, 1987; Massoudi et. al, 1992). In the frictional
flow regime, characterized by high solid particle concentrations, flow dynamics are governed by sustained
contacts within the granular medium, which have a more significant effect than individual particle inertia.
Conversely, in the kinetic flow regime, the flow is rapid with intermittent particle-particle collisions,
commonly known as collision-dominated flow. In such flow conditions, Chapman & Cowling (1970)
proposed an analogy to gas kinetic theory to define the effective stresses within the solid phase. The
development of kinetic theory-based models assumes that the interstitial fluid phase does not contribute
significantly to the granular phase mechanics. Following the kinetic theory of granular flow, similar to the
thermodynamic temperature for gases, the granular temperature measures the particle velocity fluctuations,
and the solid-phase stress tensor is expressed as a function of this granular temperature. In applications
relating to gas-solid and liquid-solid slurry flows, this kinetic-theory-based modeling approach is well
established in the literature (Syamlal, 1987; Gidaspow, 1994). A widely adopted simplifying assumption is
that of steady-state balance of granular energy, wherein local granular energy arises solely from generation
and dissipation terms. This simplification neglects the convection and diffusion of granular energy, as well
as energy dissipation due to fluctuating forces exerted by the surrounding phase through fluctuating
velocities of the particles. Under this assumption, the balance equation for granular temperature simplifies

depends on the flow regime exhibited by the gas-liquid flow. These flow regimes include the viscous

In the viscous regime, the primary viscous forces impede the deformation of a gas bubble caused by
interfacial instabilities or turbulent forces. The spherical drag model proposed by Schiller and Naumann
(1935) finds its applicability for defining the single particle (bubble) drag coefficient. Ishii (1975) reported
that the same spherical drag formulation is still applicable when the gas volume fraction increases within
the same flow regime. However, for distorted bubbly flow regime, the drag experienced by the dispersed
phase increases with an increase in the void fraction. A drag model suitable for such a flow regime,
accounting for deformation of the bubble, was given by Ishii and Zuber model (Ishii, 1975). In transition
to churn-turbulent flow regime, there is a marked increase in the bubble diameter that allows the wake
region to overlap with the bubble boundary layer. For such a flow regime, the drag formulation is expressed

 17

only as a function of the void fraction. The slug flow regime is demarcated by an increased bubble volume
where the bubble diameter is comparable to the characteristic dimension of the flow channel. The drag
correlation for the slug regime corrects the churn-turbulent flow drag correlation by a factor proportional
to the ratio of the bubble radius to the length scale of the flow channel. For low superficial liquid and gas
velocities, the Tomiyama (Tomiyama, 1998) and Grace (Grace, 1973) models are predominantly used. The
drag predicted by the Tomiyama model is higher than that of the Grace model, hence assisting in increasing
the holdup values when applied to bubble column reactors. Other reported models are the Morsi and
Alexander model (Morsi & Alexander, 1972) and the Clift model (Clift et al., 2005), which relate to the
single bubble drag coefficient expressed over multiple ranges of particle Reynolds numbers. For the fuel
dispersal study, the flow of fission gases at the near injection region exhibits a large void fraction while in
the immediate downstream, may dissipate into the bubbly flow regime upon impingement with neighboring
wall boundaries. A comparison of the drag models through a validation study could provide further insights
into applicable drag correlations. A single bubble-based drag coefficient model can be chosen as the starting
point for such a comparison study.

Fuel-coolant interaction presents challenges when modeling the interaction between molten corium and
liquid coolant. These challenges branch from the significant differences in time scales of the contributing
phenomena, non-equilibrium heat transfer, multiple fragmentation of the molten jet, and hydrogen
generation (Ursic et. al, 2017). Even in scenarios involving low-energy fuel-coolant interaction, like that
encountered in fuel dispersal phenomena, modeling the heat transfer effects remains complex. For instance,
when the temperature difference between solid fuel particles and the liquid coolant exceeds 100 K, heat
transfer occurs primarily through film boiling, involving heat conduction and radiation across the vapor
film (Shiotsu & Hama, 2000). In such scenarios, a complete vapor film is formed at the solid boundary that
prevents direct contact of the liquid media with the hot solid surface. Studies in the literature have
experimentally investigated the configuration of the vapor film on spheres, depending on coolant flow
velocity and degree of subcooling (Walfort, 1969; Stevens & Witte, 1971; Zvirin et al., 1990). Forced
convection film boiling is typically categorized into saturated and subcooled film boiling. Saturated film
boiling is characterized by a thick vapor film at the leading edge, where most of the heat transferred through
the interface causes evaporation. While, in subcooled film boiling, most of the heat is convected away in
the liquid, with separated bubbles from the vapor film tending to condense immediately. A thorough review
of film boiling models to evaluate the heat transfer coefficient from a heated sphere under saturated and
subcooled conditions can be found in Liu & Theofanous (1994). With respect to numerical simulation of
film boiling, there is a vast amount of work reported in the literature that conducts two-phase flow
simulation through tracking of the vapor liquid interface with the solid surface acting as the wall boundary.
However, none treats all participating media through an interpenetrating continua field. Therefore, in the
context of modeling film boiling during fuel dispersal, proposed is an analysis framework that quantifies

The adiabatic single-rod experiments performed at OSU as a part of an ongoing investigation to characterize

The experiments simulated fuel dispersal behavior in a glass tank filled with mineral oil, using spherical

2.5 Adiabatic Three-Fluid Flow Model Validation Studies

The current framework considers both the gas and solid phases as dispersed phases interacting with the
primary liquid phase. The characteristic dimension of the solid phase is set to 2 mm, based on the particle
diameter used in the experiments. Observations from an instantaneous snapshot taken at 50 ms, 100 ms,
and 150 ms into the burst event reveal that the gas phase is carried as a streak of trailing channels, following
the solid particle transport into the bulk liquid (Latimer, 2021). Subsequently, these trailing channels
disengage from the particles and rise as a swarm of bubbles to the open free surface. Based on these
observations, the characteristic dimension, which affects the projected area of gas flow and influences solid
transport, is approximated to be equal to the particle diameter. The transient gas and solid mass flow rate
were prescribed as an inlet boundary condition at the orifice based on the procedure described in the
previous subsection.

The Eulerian three-fluid model momentum equations, along with the k-omega dispersed turbulent equations
(Menter, 1994), were solved using the finite-volume-based, phase-coupled, SIMPLE algorithm in the
commercial ANSYS Fluent CFD software. The dispersed turbulence model is typically applied for dilute
and/or granular flows. The dispersed phase turbulence quantities are not explicitly solved through transport
equations, but rather obtained by algebraic correlations as a function of fluctuating quantities of the primary
phase (Techen-theory correlations for the dispersed phases, Hinze (1975)). For discretization, a first-order
upwind scheme was applied to the momentum, volume fraction, turbulent kinetic energy, and specific
dissipation rate equations, while the PRESTO scheme (Peyret, 1996), was employed for the continuity
equations. The simulation employed the previously discussed granular flow properties, with the maximum

 27

packing fraction set to 0.63. To accurately capture the high-speed gas flow, a very fine mesh resolution is
essential on the orifice surface. In this study, a cell dimension of 1 × 1 × 2 mm in the near-orifice region
proved sufficient to achieve a continuity convergence of 10-4 with a simulation time step of 10-5 s. The
geometry of the tank, along with the computational mesh on a cut plane aligned with the burst orifice as
well as the prescribed initial volume distribution, is shown in Figure 2.4.

With submerged gas-solid injection into the liquid pool, the gas phase exhibits a jet-like behavior, achieving
a penetration length of approximately 0.25 m within 250 ms of injection. The high-speed gas core drives
the bulk of the solid phase to disperse into the liquid pool, resulting in a diffused solid volume fraction with
a peak of less than 2% in the liquid bulk. Notably, the solid phase precedes the gas core in penetration
length. By the end of the injection at t = 500 ms, the gas core remains intact, extending approximately 0.33
m into the domain, while the solid phase continues to penetrate, reaching nearly 0.4 m. Beyond 1.0 s,
buoyancy forces begin to dominate in the gas phase, causing the gas core to rise towards the free surface.
Concurrently, the gas phase disperses further, exhibiting a more diffused distribution with increasing height.
With a local void fraction of less than 40%, the gas phase can be classified as being within a bubbly flow
regime as it ascends to the free surface. In contrast, the dispersed solid phase shows a dominant downward
displacement beyond 1 s, with the majority of the solid phase having settled by 4 s.

2.6 Conclusion

The validation of the model was conducted using adiabatic experimental results of the high-pressure cases
of 4 MPa, 8 MPa, and 12 MPa actuation pressure, obtained from ongoing research at OSU, focusing on
characterizing fuel dispersal behavior during LOCA conditions. The simulation model employed a circular
flat orifice with a diameter identical to that of the burst orifice on a cylindrical tests rod used in the
experiments. The results pertaining to solid particle settlement characteristics, quantified by probability
distribution of the equivalent number of particles, exhibited a close match with the probability density
functions (PDFs) reported in experimental studies. Additionally, the simulation studies assumed the gas
phase to be incompressible and employed velocity inlet boundary conditions informed by particle velocities
recorded during experimental studies. It was observed that the majority of the solid phase settled within 5
s of the initial burst across all operating cases.

Furthermore, for the benchtop setup, designed to mimic the pressure history during a hypothetical LOCA
event, transient profiles of the volumetric phase distribution and velocities of the gas-solid-liquid flow were
analyzed. Notably, particle-particle interactions contributing to the granular pressure within the solid phase
were predominantly confined to the near orifice region during the injection period. The solid phase
exhibited diffuse penetration into the tank, with peak volume fraction recorded below 2% in the liquid bulk.
Liquid velocity contours were primarily influenced by the initial high-speed gas phase. As the gas-solid

 41

injection period concluded, gas-liquid velocities approached near equilibrium, with the initial gas core
length gradually diffusing into a bubbly flow as it rises to the free top surface of the liquid pool.
Subsequently, buoyancy forces take effect, resulting in the settling of solid phases at the tank bottom surface
and the escape of the gas phase from the liquid pool. This comprehensive analysis provides valuable insights
into the behavior of gas-solid-liquid flow during a simulated fuel dispersal event.

3.1 Introduction

Modeling and simulation of fuel dispersal in Chapter 2 was performed with three-fluid model. While the
three-fluid model offers a general modeling approach for three-phase flows, further work is needed on the
continuous treatment of the solid phase expelled from a burst orifice. Therefore, this chapter focuses on
employing an interpenetrating gas-solid two-fluid modeling framework to simulate the transport of
fragmented fuel particles within a 5 × 5 fuel bundle geometry. Additionally, this framework provides
closure relations for the granular treatment of the solid phase during rapid transients in confined geometries.
The required boundary conditions, such as representative particle size, burst pressure, and cladding rupture
dimensions, are obtained from the fuel performance code BISON analysis under a postulated large-break
LOCA scenario. Section 3.2 introduces the gas-solid modeling framework, closures relations, and provides
a brief overview of the BISON simulation. Section 3.3 elaborates on the boundary conditions, simulation
settings, and details of the fuel bundle geometry under consideration. In Section 3.4, the results of the
simulation of 5 × 5 fuel bundle geometry are presented. Finally, the concluding remarks are provided in
Section 3.5.

3.2.2 BISON Simulation

BISON is a nuclear fuel performance code (Williamson et al., 2021) developed primarily by Idaho National
Laboratory with contributions from other national laboratories, industry, and academia. The code has been
under development since about 2009, with accident capability development beginning in 2014. In support
of these capability additions, several simulations to separate effects and integral fuel rod experiments have
been performed. One such case, the Instrumented Fuel Assembly (IFA)-650.4 experiment conducted at
Halden (Kekkonen, 2007) was used to obtain initial and boundary condition inputs for the numerical study
used in this work. The base irradiation and experimental conditions of the IFA-650.4 experiment were
applied to a rodlet of the dimensions used in this study. The results of the simulation were provided as
boundary conditions for the rod bundle geometry simulations.

3.3 Numerical Simulation

The present study performs a three-dimensional adiabatic simulation using a 5 × 5 pin configuration
featuring a burst rod at the center. This investigation primarily focuses on the transport and subsequent
deposition of fragmented fuel particles within the rod bundle geometry, with the burst orifice directly
oriented toward an adjacent fuel pin.

3.3.2 Boundary Conditions

The necessary boundary conditions include the inlet conditions for gas-solid flow, the size and shape of the
orifice, and the distribution of particle sizes. These parameters are acquired through a BISON simulation
conducted under representative large-break LOCA conditions. As mentioned previously, the representative

average fuel particle size to be 1.55 mm (converting assumed particle shapes to spheres) and an oval rupture

3.5 Conclusions

Fuel dispersal behavior within a simulated pressurized water reactor fuel bundle geometry was investigated
using boundary conditions derived from the fuel performance code BISON under large break LOCA
conditions. In this study, a two-fluid granular flow model was utilized to simulate the transport of UO2
particles. The employed closures for the granular bulk properties assume the kinetic and collisional
contributions to particle-particle interaction as the primary factors governing the granular temperature
equation. We observed that most of the solids are located outside the 5 × 5 bundle area, with the highest

 54

concentration found at the corners of the bounding tank. Given the rapid transient nature of the dispersal
event within this confined geometry, it is evident that the trajectory of solids is primarily influenced by the
particle-wall interaction within the rod bundle geometry. In particular, the values of the specular coefficient
and the restitutive coefficient for the particle-bundle wall interaction could play a crucial role in reducing
the kinetic energy of the solids, consequently influencing the settlement distribution. Further investigation
into these coefficients will be considered as a future work.

4. SPALART-ALLMARAS TURBULENCE MODEL IN MOOSE

4.1 Introduction

Development of robust modeling approaches and mechanistic models for predicting fuel fragment behavior
and spatial distribution at high burnup during postulated LOCA transients is essential for informing the
nuclear safety case. While empirical and semi-empirical correlations exist for fuel fragmentation and
relocation, comprehensive modeling and reliable estimation of fuel dispersal following cladding rupture
remain in an early stage of development. In the preceding chapters of this study, computational studies of
fuel dispersal relied on initial and boundary conditions specified a priori. However, realistic accident
scenarios, such as a postulated LOCA leading to FFRD, are inherently multiphysics, involving strong
coupling between fuel thermomechanics, cladding deformation, coolant thermal-hydraulics, and multiphase
particle transport. These couplings directly influence the flow field and, consequently, particle relocation
within the reactor core. Therefore, predictive analysis ultimately requires an integrated approach capable
of exchanging information between fuel performance models, system thermal-hydraulic solvers, and
detailed particle transport frameworks. To enable such an integrated workflow, one can, for example,
perform fuel performance analysis using BISON, simulate the system-level thermal-hydraulics using
TRACE, and combine those results with the solid transport model (STM) for fuel dispersal. The
Multiphysics Object Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE) provides an attractive platform for
supporting the aforementioned infrastructure. Its modular design allows flexible development of new
physics components, facilitates data exchange between applications, and supports tight coupling strategies
as needed (Harbour et al., 2025).

Within the fluid mechanics context, the MOOSE has gradually expanded its turbulence modeling
capabilities. Earlier versions of the Navier-Stokes module did not include turbulence closures, but recent
developments now provide two-equation RANS models, including the 𝑘𝑘 − 𝜖𝜖 and 𝑘𝑘 − 𝜔𝜔 formulations.
Nevertheless, Eulerian multiphase turbulence models and granular/particle transport closures remain under
development, which are essential for fully resolving fuel fragment dispersal in two-phase steam-liquid
environments. The specific aim of this work is not to replace the existing turbulence modules but rather to
gain familiarity with MOOSE’s finite-volume kernel-based formulation by implementing a well-
established turbulence model, the Spalart-Allmaras (SA) one-equation model (Spalart & Allmaras, 1992).
The SA model is attractive for this purpose because it requires solving only a single transport equation for
the modified turbulent viscosity and generally exhibits good convergence characteristics for attached flows.
Implementing SA provides a focused and instructive pathway for understanding how MOOSE handles

 56

variable coupling, residual assembly, stabilization, and boundary condition enforcement. The remainder of
this chapter is organized as follows. Section 4.2 provides a brief overview of the SA model. Section 4.3
describes its implementation within the MOOSE framework. Section 4.4 outlines the simulation problems
used for verification, and Section 4.5 presents the results and accompanying discussion. Finally,
Section 4.6 offers concluding remarks.

4.2 Spalart-Allmaras Turbulence Model

The present study focuses on the preliminary implementation of the SA turbulence model for flows with
constant fluid properties using the finite volume capabilities of the MOOSE framework. The SA model is
the one-equation Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) turbulence model that solves a transport
equation for the modified turbulent kinematic viscosity, 𝜈𝜈�, from which the turbulent viscosity, 𝜇𝜇𝑡𝑡 , is
computed. Because it requires only a single additional scalar equation, the SA model is computationally
efficient and well suited for initial development and validation.

In the RANS approach, flow variables are decomposed into mean and fluctuating components (Wilcox,

4.3 MOOSE Implementation

The MOOSE framework provides a flexible environment for implementing fluid-flow simulations using
the finite volume method. Users construct governing equations by selecting appropriate finite volume
kernels (FVKernels), each of which contributes specific terms to the residual of the discretized equations.

4.3.1 Existing Navier-Stokes FVKernels

The Navier-Stokes module in MOOSE already includes several core FVKernels that assemble the mass and
momentum equations. A brief overview of the relevant FVKernels used in this work summarized below.

 INSFVMassAdvection kernel: This kernel constructs the mass advection term, ∇ ⋅ (𝜌𝜌𝐯𝐯), with the default
interpolation scheme set to upwind. Users may change the interpolation method through input parameters.

 59

 INSFVMomentumAdvection kernel: This kernel incorporates the momentum advection term, ∇ ⋅ (𝜌𝜌𝐯𝐯𝐯𝐯),
and also uses an upwind interpolation scheme by default. It requires specification of the
moment_component parameter (x, y, or z).

 INSFVMomentumDiffusion kernel: This kernel builds the diffusion term corresponding to the Laplacian
form of the viscous stress, −∇ ⋅ (𝜇𝜇∇𝐯𝐯). Users must specify the velocity component direction. Importantly,
this kernel can be instantiated twice, once for molecular viscosity and once for turbulent viscosity, allowing
clean separation of the two contributions.

 INSFVMomentumPressure kernel: This kernel introduces the pressure gradient term, ∇𝑝𝑝, into the
momentum equation.

 INSFVMomentumGravity kernel: This kernel adds body force contributions by incorporating the
gravitational term.

These core kernels are sufficient for the fundamental test problems considered in this work, such as the lid-
driven cavity flow, and allow construction of the incompressible mass and momentum equations for each
velocity component. MOOSE requires that velocity variables be defined as INSFVVelocityVariable, and
pressure as INSFVPressureVariable variable, ensuring compatibility with the finite volume Navier-Stokes
infrastructure.

4.3.2 Implementation of Spalart-Allmaras Transport Equation

To incorporate the transport equation for the SA modified turbulent viscosity, 𝜈𝜈�, additional FVKernels must
be implemented. The governing equation contains four classes of terms:
 (a) Unsteady term,

 (b) Advection term,

 (c) Diffusion term, and

 (d) Source-sink term (including production, destruction, and gradient squared contributions)

Because 𝜈𝜈� is a scalar transport variable, it is represented in MOOSE as an INSFVEnergyVariable, derived
from the base class of INSFVScalarVariable. In the present work, three new FVKernels were developed to
construct the SA transport equation:

 60

 INSFVSAViscosityAdvection kernel: This kernel produces the advection term, ∇ ⋅ (𝜌𝜌𝜈𝜈�𝐯𝐯). Users may
specify interpolation methods for both advection and velocity.

 INSFVSAViscosityDiffusion kernel: This kernel constructs the diffusion term, −∇ ⋅ 𝜇𝜇𝑒𝑒𝑒𝑒𝑒𝑒 ∇𝜈𝜈�, where 𝜇𝜇𝑒𝑒𝑒𝑒𝑒𝑒
includes either molecular viscosity or turbulent viscosity. Consequently, two instances of this kernel must
be provided in the input file, one for each viscosity contribution. This kernel will also require the constant

 INSFVSAViscositySourceSink kernel: This kernel evaluates all remaining terms – the production,
destruction, and gradient square terms. It requires velocity components (x and y for 2D problems),
molecular viscosity, density, and all SA constants (𝐶𝐶𝑣𝑣1 , 𝜅𝜅, 𝐶𝐶𝑏𝑏1 , 𝐶𝐶𝑏𝑏2 , 𝜎𝜎𝜈𝜈� , 𝐶𝐶𝑤𝑤3 , 𝐶𝐶𝑤𝑤2 ) A key required parameter
is the distance to the nearest wall, 𝑑𝑑, which plays a crucial role in evaluating source-sink terms.

4.3.3 Auxiliary Kernels for Supporting Quantities

Along with the finite volume kernels, MOOSE also provides auxiliary kernels (AuxKernel), which are
evaluated outside the non-linear residual assembly and are useful for updating derived quantities during the
simulation. Several AuxKernels were developed to support the SA model.

 SpalartAllmarasViscosity AuxKernel: This AuxKernel computes the turbulent viscosity 𝜇𝜇𝑡𝑡 according to
Eq. (4.6). Because 𝜇𝜇𝑡𝑡 must be updated for every cell at each iteration, this kernel is executed during both
NONLINEAR and LINEAR stages for steady simulations, and additionally at TIMESTEP_END for transient
cases. Alternatively, this operation could be implemented as a Material-Functor, treating 𝜇𝜇𝑡𝑡 as a material
property.

 WallDistanceAux AuxKernel: This kernel calculates the shortest distance from the cell centroid to the
nearest wall. Since this value depends only on the mesh, it is computed once at simulation initialization
(execute_on = INITIAL). The resulting distance is required by the source-sink
INSFVSAViscositySourceSink FVkernel.

 SAProduction AuxKernel: Used for visualization of the production term. It does not participate in the
residual evaluation and is executed at the end of the simulation (execute_on = FINAL).

 61

 SADestruction AuxKernel: Similar to SAProduction AuxKernel, this kernel computes the destruction
term for visualization purposes and is executed at FINAL.

4.6 Conclusions

The Spalart-Allmaras turbulence model was successfully implemented in the MOOSE framework using its
finite volume infrastructure. New FVKernels and AuxKernels were developed to assemble the SA transport
equation and compute the associated turbulent viscosity. A lid-driven cavity test case was used to verify
and demonstrate the implementation.

The PJFNK solver exhibited stable convergence, and MOOSE produced physically consistent flow fields.
Comparisons against ANSYS Fluent showed that MOOSE captures the overall velocity trends; however,
notable deviations occur in the central region and near wall boundaries, particularly for the y-velocity and
turbulence quantities. These discrepancies appear to stem from the lack of near-wall turbulence treatments
in the current MOOSE implementation of SA, whereas Fluent applies wall damping and low-Re corrections
by default. This preliminary study suggests that incorporating appropriate SA wall boundary treatments in
MOOSE, such as wall functions or low-Reynolds-number corrections, will be necessary for improved
agreement with established CFD solvers in future work.

5. COUPLED SIMULATION BETWEEN BISON AND TRACE

5.1 Introduction

BISON (Williamson et al., 2021) is a MOOSE-based fuel performance code primarily developed at INL.
The purpose of the software is to calculate the thermo-mechanical response of various nuclear fuel
designs under irradiation. Models exist to evaluate performance under normal operating and transient
conditions. Being built upon MOOSE enables analyses to be performed in a wide variety of dimensions,
including 1D Cartesian, 1D spherical, layered 1D, 2D axisymmetric, 2D planar, layered 2D, and 3D. The
dimension of choice is traditionally selected to be the lowest geometric fidelity necessary to capture the
phenomenon and physics of interest. This enables evaluation of the fuel performance while keeping
computational costs low.

The TRAC/RELAP Advanced Computational Engine (TRACE) is software developed by the United
States (US) Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC), designed to evaluate the thermal-hydraulics
behavior of both light-water and advanced reactors. (NRC, 2012). The thermal-hydraulic response
computed by TRACE can be used to inform the exterior cladding boundary condition in a fuel
performance analysis. Under accident conditions, such as a LOCA, this coupling becomes more important
as TRACE can capture details of the fluid flow that the internal coolant channel model within BISON
does not account for (e.g., two-phase flow, crossflows).

5.2 Coupling Strategy

The coupling of BISON and TRACE began in 2019 through a joint project between INL and the NRC
(Gardner et al., 2019). This coupling is implemented in the MOOSE-based application called BlueCRAB,
which brings together several of the MOOSE-based applications developed by the Department of
Energy’s Office of Nuclear Energy together with NRC codes including TRACE and more recently, FAST
(Porter et al., 2020). That prior work focused on standing up the initial coupling capability and navigating
unique challenges associated with the data transfer due to the dissimilar meshes in BISON and TRACE.
A major deliverable of that work was demonstrating coupled simulations of the LOFT L2-5 experiment.
More recently, the algorithm for coupling BISON to TRACE has been reformulated in another project to
improve efficiency and robustness while also enabling the MOOSE-based applications to be run in
parallel while the TRACE calculation is run serially.
The work completed as part of the current project leverages the latest version of the BISON/TRACE
coupling capabilities within BlueCRAB to demonstrate their functionality on a LOCA scenario where

 70

fuel dispersal is possible. Information passing uses a special transfer within MOOSE called a
MultiAppGeneralFieldNearestLocationTransfer, which allows data to be transferred
between potentially dissimilar meshes (i.e., one more axially refined than the other). Generally speaking,
when coupling BISON to TRACE, the TRACE mesh is finer than the BISON mesh in the axial direction.
TRACE is a 1D code, and the direction of its spatial dimension is along the cylindrical the axis of the fuel
rod. The geometry assumed in BISON for the studies completed here is 2D axisymmetric, with the x-axis
corresponding to the radial direction, and the y-axis corresponding to the axial direction. The Controls
system in MOOSE is commonly used in BISON to transition from using internally computed thermal-
hydraulics during a base irradiation simulation to using the TRACE informed boundary conditions during
the LOCA transient.

different TRACE components such as pipes, heat structure faces, and the whole heat structure. When
evaluating the thermal-hydraulic boundary conditions for a BISON simulation, the outer quantity on the
outer heat structure face is of most importance. The 1D MOOSE mesh corresponding to the TRACE

5.4 Demonstration Problem
Development of TRACE inputs for new or different case studies are a difficult and cumbersome task for
those without experience. Therefore, this project leverages past work that developed coupled simulation
for an upcoming TREAT experiment (Smith et al., 2025) using the transient water irradiation system for
TREAT (TWIST) (Jensen et al., 2022) device. The specimen selected for the present demonstration
corresponds to the LOC-C-4 experiment conducted on a length of 25 cm of fresh fuel with a 15 cm2 free
volume at an initial internal pressure of 4 MPa. The transient occurs over 1500 s. An external pressure of
3.4 MPa is applied until blowdown at 1059 s, quickly dropping to 0.646 MPa by 1062 s. A power pulse is
initiated shortly before blowdown to stabilize the fuel at temperature to simulate the LOCA from a full
power condition.

For comparison purposes, two simulations were completed, one using the BISON internal coolant channel
model and one coupling BISON to TRACE for the convective boundary conditions on the cladding
surface. A cladding failure criterion corresponding to a plastic instability scenario was assumed. This
criterion indicates failure has occurred when the inelastic (creep) strain exceeds 2.78 × 10-2 s-1 (Di
Marcello et al., 2014). Since the specimen is made of fresh fuel, no fragments form, so no fuel fragments
are available for dispersal. However, an investigation on the impacts of thermal hydraulic treatment on
the rupture opening area can be performed. Selected relevant quantities of interest from the simulation are
presented in Table 5.4. These include the peak cladding temperature, rupture time, and the rupture area
computed by the two models discussed previously. The case that uses BISON’s internal coolant channel
model predicts a lower peak cladding temperature at rupture, and rupture at a later time compared to the
coupled simulation. The differences in computed rupture opening area illustrate the impact of focusing on
peak cladding strain or peak cladding stress as the driving force for the correlation development. In the
case of the coupled simulation, the rupture opening area computed by the Capps model is zero because
there is a minimum hoop strain threshold (𝐶𝐶2 =8%, as indicated in Table 5.2) that must be met prior to an
opening length being computed.

5.5 Conclusions
Significant work has been completed through multiple prior projects to enable proper coupling between
BISON and TRACE for improved thermal-hydraulic calculations during LOCA scenarios. The work in
the current project builds upon that foundation to explore the impacts of the coupling approach employed
for representing interactions between the fuel performance and thermal-hydraulic models. The differences
between the rupture opening area predicted using the modeling assumptions tested here indicate the
importance of using a coupled approach. In the scenario considered here, it was found that the coupled
simulation achieved much higher cladding temperatures in a shorter period of time than did the internal
coolant channel model in BISON. Once the experimental data is obtained for the scenario modeled here,
it can be used to compare and improve upon the coupling strategies employed here. An experimentally
validated model that can accurately predict cladding opening area will be very useful for providing the
initial conditions for future fuel dispersal studies that build on the experiments modeled in earlier sections
of this report.

6. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION

This study presents a detailed modeling and simulation framework to predict the distribution of fragmented
fuel particles within a reactor core during a postulated LOCA event involving cladding rupture. Due to the
unique multiphase characteristics of fuel dispersal, the study reevaluates the three-fluid modeling
framework and introduces targeted simplifying assumptions for the continuum treatment of the solid phase
and the gas-solid-liquid interfacial exchange mechanisms within the reactor core. The model was validated
using adiabatic experimental results from fuel rod depressurization into an open tank under simulated
LOCA conditions. Key to the validation was the prescribed gas-solid injection boundary conditions, which
directly influence the dispersal characteristics. The accuracy of the model was found to be strongly
dependent on the estimation of interfacial area concentration between each phase pair, a quantity governed
by the evolving three-phase flow regime. Insights into this flow regime were provided by a Lagrangian
tracking model that simplified the simulated fuel particles as inert point masses moving under the influence
of interfacial and buoyancy-driven forces. Finally, the simulation of fuel dispersal phenomena was
conducted within a simulated pressurized water reactor core geometry, revealing that the distribution of
fuel particles was primarily governed by particle-wall interactions.

The following briefly summarizes the conclusions drawn from the continuum three-fluid flow simulation.
 1. Particle-particle interactions contributing to granular pressure within the solid phase were mainly
 confined to the near orifice region during injection.
 2. The solid phase exhibited diffuse penetration into the tank, and liquid velocity contours were
 chiefly influenced by the initial high-speed gas phase.
 3. The initial gas core length gradually diffused into a bubbly flow as it ascends to the free surface of
 the liquid pool.
 4. The solid particle settlement characteristics, quantified by particle probability distribution, closely
 matched the probability density functions from experimental studies.

As potential future work, the uncertainties associated with the closure models for the solid phase bulk
treatment and interfacial terms in gas-solid-liquid flow interactions can be addressed through the
development of a multi-scale modeling framework for fuel dispersal simulation. One approach is to model
the fuel particles using a Discrete Element Model (DEM) that accounts for particle polydispersity and the
associated contact mechanics of particle-particle and particle-wall interactions. This model could consider
the effects of different interstitial fluids. Additionally, by employing appropriate statistical methods to
analyze the distribution and correlation of forces and velocities, the microscale interaction data can be

 76

averaged to represent macroscopic quantities.

Additionally, in this study also includes preliminary work using finite volume capabilities in MOOSE to
implement the Spalart-Allmaras (SA) turbulence model. The newly developed finite volume kernels were
successfully integrated into the existing Navier-Stokes module, and the implementation was demonstrated
using a turbulent lid-driven cavity problem. While MOOSE reproduced the overall flow trends, deviation
in velocity fields and turbulent quantities were observed when compared with ANSYS Fluent results. These
discrepancies highlights the need for enhanced near-wall modeling or low-Reynolds number corrections to
improve agreement.

Lastly, a demonstration problem was provided to illustrate the coupling of BISON to the systems code
TRACE for thermal hydraulics to more accurately represent the coolant behavior during a postulated LOCA
transient. Relevant fuel performance metrics necessary for dispersal studies was also output including the
rupture opening area, burst pressure, and burst temperature. The results were compared to a standalone
BISON calculation using the internal coolant channel model. Slight differences in rupture time and rupture
area, and peak cladding temperature were observed.

The key takeaway from the above exercise was to show that given a limited gas plenum volume, the

A.2 Near Orifice Gas Volume
The ejected gas stream upon interaction with the fluid media transitions from a jet-like flow regime to a
plume-like flow regime. Considering the gas phase to be incompressible would underpredict the extent of
the jet-like regime within the liquid domain due to the underestimation of the inlet gas inertia. However,
the difference in the jet penetration length might not be appreciable based on the above analysis.
The particle trajectories are influenced by the residence time of the particle phase within the gas phase. The
higher the near orifice gas volume, higher is the residence time hence greater is the particle penetration
length. Under the incompressible assumption, the simulation framework currently employs identical
choked flow velocity as the gas inlet boundary condition while using a constant gas density to determine
the gas mass flow rate. As discussed above, for the experimental conditions, the effect of density variation
does not contribute to a large extent in affecting the near orifice gas volume. Hence, the assumption of
incompressible gas phase does not contribute significantly to the prediction of the particle trajectories. It
may also be noted that based on the current simulation results, the particle residence time within the gas
phase is approximately 20 ms.
