Shaping research integrity: Perceptions of mentorship and questionable research practices among undergraduate dental students and mentors in Karachi, Pakistan
Authors: Asma Siddiqui, Ashar Malik, Rashna Hoshang Sukhia, Abdul Aleem, Syed Shah Faisal, Hasnain Sakrani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundQuestionable research practices (QRPs) undermine research integrity and may become normalized early during academic training. In undergraduate dental research, students' ethical conduct is shaped not only by formal instruction but also by mentorship, supervision, hierarchy, and institutional culture. However, limited qualitative evidence is available from Pakistan on how undergraduate dental students and mentors perceive ethical challenges and the role of mentorship in navigating QRPs.MethodsAn exploratory descriptive qualitative study was conducted in two dental teaching institutions in Karachi, Pakistan: one public-sector and one private-sector institution. In-depth interviews were conducted with undergraduate dental students and faculty mentors involved in undergraduate research supervision. Participants were purposively selected to capture variation by participant role, institution type, gender, year of study, and supervisory experience. Separate semi-structured interview guides were developed for students and mentors, pilot-tested, and refined before data collection. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis, informed by Bandura’s Social Learning Theory. Reporting was guided by the COREQ and SRQR principles.ResultsFour themes were developed: ethical awareness with limited procedural confidence; QRPs as normalized research shortcuts; mentorship as ethical scaffolding and a source of silence; and institutional systems as conditions that enable or constrain research integrity. Participants described basic awareness of ethical principles such as informed consent, confidentiality, plagiarism avoidance, and ethics approval. However, they also described difficulty applying these principles to authorship decisions, field procedures, data interpretation, and institutional review processes. Improper authorship, selective reporting, interpretive distortion, and procedural shortcuts were commonly discussed as QRPs. Mentorship was perceived as supportive when mentors were accessible, explicit, and ethically engaged, but hierarchical relationships and inconsistent supervisory engagement sometimes discouraged students from questioning questionable practices. Institutional barriers included limited practical ethics training, weak procedural support, productivity pressure, and a lack of safe reporting pathways.ConclusionThis study does not establish a causal or statistical relationship between mentorship and QRPs. Rather, it shows how undergraduate dental students and mentors perceive research integrity as being shaped through everyday interactions among ethical awareness, mentor–mentee dynamics, hierarchy, and institutional culture. The findings suggest that structured ethics teaching, clearer authorship guidance, reflective mentorship, and safe reporting mechanisms may strengthen undergraduate dental research integrity, although these interventions require further evaluation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12910-026-01537-w.

Introduction

Undergraduate research is increasingly recognized as an integral component of dental education, as it cultivates critical thinking, scientific writing, appraisal skills, and an evidence-based approach to clinical practice [1–4]. However, research training may simultaneously expose students to questionable research practices (QRPs), including inappropriate authorship, plagiarism, selective reporting, poor data handling, and other ethically problematic behaviors that undermine research quality and credibility [1, 2, 5, 6]. While overt misconduct, such as fabrication and falsification, is widely condemned, QRPs are more common, more subtle, and more easily normalized within academic culture [1, 2, 7].

Growing evidence indicates that research behavior is shaped not only by individual knowledge or intention but also by the broader research environment, including institutional culture, supervision, and professional norms [2, 3, 7]. In undergraduate settings, where students are still learning how research is conducted, mentorship becomes particularly important. Mentors influence not only the technical aspects of research—such as study design, data interpretation, and authorship practices—but also the ethical standards that students adopt during training [8, 9]. High-quality mentorship, characterized by accessibility, clear communication, constructive feedback, and explicit ethical guidance, has been described as supporting responsible research conduct. Conversely, weak supervision, poor mentor–mentee communication, and problematic power dynamics may create conditions in which students feel less able to recognize, question, or respond to questionable practices [8–10].

This issue is especially relevant in dental education, where research is often conducted within hierarchical academic systems, and students may depend heavily on supervisors for project access, authorship, grades, and academic progression. In such contexts, students may hesitate to question decisions even when they perceive them as inappropriate. Recent literature in dental education highlights the importance of ethical training, research integrity, and supportive supervision in fostering responsible research conduct [5, 6, 11, 12]. A multicenter study from China found that an ethical academic environment, formal integrity teaching, and proper supervision are key factors associated with research integrity knowledge among dental undergraduates [11]. Similarly, recent evidence suggests that students' perceptions of faculty ethics are shaped by institutional and hierarchical conditions, reinforcing the importance of the hidden curriculum in professional formation [12].

In Pakistan, this question carries added significance because undergraduate medical and dental students continue to face barriers to research engagement, including inadequate guidance, limited training opportunities, and inconsistent institutional support [13, 14]. Local work also suggests that students perceive mentorship as important for understanding ethical concerns, although formal mechanisms to prevent QRPs remain limited [10, 15]. These findings indicate that mentorship may be a modifiable factor in strengthening research integrity within undergraduate dental education.

Despite growing attention to QRPs and research integrity in health professions education, limited qualitative work has explored how undergraduate dental students and mentors in Pakistan understand ethical challenges within student research. In particular, little is known about how mentorship, supervision, hierarchy, authorship norms, and institutional procedures are perceived to shape students' responses to questionable research practices. This gap is important because mentorship and institutional research culture are modifiable features of the undergraduate research environment.

Therefore, this study aimed to explore undergraduate dental students' and mentors' perceptions and experiences of QRPs, ethical guidance, and mentor–mentee dynamics during undergraduate dental research projects in Karachi, Pakistan. The study sought to generate context-specific qualitative insights that may inform future faculty development, structured supervision, authorship guidance, and research ethics training in dental education.

Methods

Study design

This exploratory descriptive qualitative study examined perceptions of questionable research practices (QRPs) and mentorship among undergraduate dental students and their mentors. A qualitative approach enabled in-depth exploration of participants' experiences within their real educational context [16, 17].

Study setting and duration

The study was conducted in two dental teaching institutions in Karachi, Pakistan: one public-sector institution and one private-sector institution. These institutions were selected purposively to capture variation in institutional context, including differences in resource availability, administrative structure, research governance, and supervisory culture. The public–private comparison was not intended to produce a statistical comparison or institutional ranking; rather, it was used to broaden contextual understanding of how research integrity and mentorship are experienced across different educational environments. Karachi was selected because it is a major urban academic center with established undergraduate dental research activity. Data collection occurred from May 2025 to October 2025.

Theoretical framework

Bandura’s Social Learning Theory informed the study, positing that learning occurs through observation, modelling, imitation, and reinforcement [18]. Mentors act as role models whose behaviour shapes students' understanding of responsible research conduct and responses to QRPs [18, 19]. The framework is illustrated in Fig. 1. This framework guided both data collection and analysis by focusing attention on observational learning processes within mentor–mentee interactions.

Operational definition of mentor and supervisor

In this study, the term “mentor” refers to a faculty member who provided academic, methodological, ethical, or professional guidance to undergraduate students during research activities. This definition is consistent with mentoring literature, which describes mentoring as a developmental relationship in which a more experienced individual supports the learning, professional growth, and ethical development of a less experienced individual. The term “supervisor” refers more specifically to a faculty member formally responsible for overseeing a student research project, including protocol development, ethics submission, data collection, analysis, or manuscript preparation. In research training, mentors and supervisors may contribute to research integrity by modelling responsible conduct, guiding ethical decision-making, and supporting appropriate research practices. In participating institutions, these roles often overlapped; therefore, faculty participants were eligible if they were involved in either formal supervision or mentorship of undergraduate dental research. Throughout the manuscript, “mentor” is used as an umbrella term for instances in which participants described both formal supervisory and broader developmental guidance roles [8, 20].

Participants and sampling

Participants were recruited using purposive sampling to include individuals with direct experience of undergraduate dental research [21]. Eligible student participants were third-year or final-year undergraduate dental students who were actively engaged in a research project at the time of data collection. Eligible faculty participants were mentors or supervisors involved in undergraduate dental research at either participating institution. Sampling sought variation by participant role, institution type, gender, year of study among students, and supervisory experience among mentors [21].

Potential participants were identified through departmental research coordinators and faculty networks. To reduce perceived pressure to participate, initial information about the study was shared through institutional intermediaries, and interested participants contacted the research team voluntarily. A total of 15 students and 15 mentors were approached or invited. Of these, 10 students and 10 mentors agreed to participate, while five individuals in each group declined or did not respond. Reasons for non-participation, when shared, included lack of time and scheduling constraints due to the semester break.

Sample adequacy was assessed iteratively during data collection and analysis. The research team reviewed emerging codes after successive interviews to determine whether new interviews were generating substantially new categories. Saturation was considered at two levels: within participant groups — students and mentors separately —and across the combined dataset. Student interviews began to show repetition after approximately seven interviews, while mentor interviews showed repetition after approximately six interviews [22]. Recruitment continued until both datasets provided sufficient depth to support the final themes, and no major new categories were emerging [23].

Data collection tool and procedure

Data were collected using separate semi-structured interview guides for undergraduate students and faculty mentors [24, 25]. The guides were developed from the study objectives, relevant literature on QRPs and mentorship, and Bandura’s Social Learning Theory. The student guide explored awareness of research ethics, experiences of QRPs, authorship, consent, data handling, communication with mentors, hierarchy, and reporting concerns. The mentor guide explored supervisory practices, ethical guidance, authorship discussions, institutional expectations, research governance, and strategies for preventing QRPs [8, 18]. The guides were pilot-tested with two students and two mentors who met eligibility criteria but were not included in the final analysis. Pilot testing led to the refinement of question wording, the improved sequencing of sensitive questions, and the addition of probes on authorship, safe reporting, and the practical application of informed consent. No major conceptual changes were made to the interview domains [25].

Interviews were conducted face-to-face in a private room at the participating institutions and lasted approximately 30–45 minutes. Interviews were conducted by AS, who is a dental faculty member at KMDC and a postgraduate student in bioethics at AKU. Participants were informed about the interviewer’s academic role, the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality, and their right to decline any question or withdraw. Where any prior academic or institutional relationship existed between the interviewer and participants, this was acknowledged and managed by emphasizing voluntariness, non-evaluation, and confidentiality. Interviews were audio-recorded with consent and supplemented by field notes. Recordings were transcribed verbatim, de-identified, and checked for accuracy before analysis [23–25]. The English-language version of the interview guides is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach: familiarization, initial coding, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report [26, 27]. Analysis combined deductive sensitization from Bandura’s Social Learning Theory [18, 26], with inductive openness to categories emerging from the data [26, 27]. This approach allowed the analysis to examine role modelling, observation, imitation, reinforcement, hierarchy, and institutional norms while remaining attentive to participants’ own accounts.

Two researchers independently read the transcripts and generated preliminary codes. Qualitative coding and theme development were conducted manually using Microsoft Word/Excel matrices to organize coded excerpts, analytic notes, codebook revisions, and theme development tables. No dedicated qualitative data analysis software, such as NVivo, ATLAS.ti, or MAXQDA, was used. Student and mentor transcripts were initially coded separately to preserve differences between participant groups and then compared to identify convergent and divergent patterns [26, 27]. A preliminary codebook was developed after coding the first few interview transcripts and was refined iteratively as new codes emerged. Coding disagreements were discussed in team meetings and resolved through consensus. Where interpretations differed, researchers returned to the transcript excerpts and field notes to assess which interpretation was better supported by the data.

Codes were then clustered into categories and candidate themes. Candidate themes were reviewed against the coded extracts and the full dataset to ensure internal coherence and distinctiveness. The final themes were developed through repeated discussion of patterns, contradictions, minority accounts, and contextual differences between public and private institutions. An audit trail was maintained through coding notes, analytic memos, codebook revisions, and theme development tables. A supplementary table showing the progression from selected codes to categories, subthemes, and final themes has been added as Supplementary Table S1.

Trustworthiness and rigor

Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement, verbatim transcripts, pilot testing, and team discussion of coding [26, 28]. Dependability was supported by an audit trail [28]. Confirmability was strengthened through reflexive discussion [23, 28]. Transferability was addressed through a thick description of context and methods [23, 28]. Reporting followed qualitative guidance [16, 23].

Qualitative reporting

Reporting was guided by the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) and the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) [16, 23]. Details on interviewer characteristics, participant recruitment, study setting, data collection, analysis, reflexivity, and trustworthiness have been reported in the relevant sections of the manuscript. A completed COREQ checklist is provided as Supplementary File 2.

Reflexivity

The research team included dental educators, clinicians, and researchers with experience in undergraduate teaching, research supervision, and bioethics. The primary interviewer, AS, is a dental faculty member and postgraduate student in bioethics, with prior academic interest in research integrity and mentorship. This background supported contextual understanding but also created the possibility that participants might provide socially desirable responses or that researchers might interpret accounts through an ethics-focused lens.

To address this, reflexivity was built into data collection and analysis. During interviews, neutral prompts were used, and participants were encouraged to describe both positive and negative experiences. The interviewer avoided presenting QRPs as individual moral failures and instead invited participants to discuss institutional and contextual factors. During analysis, the team maintained reflexive notes on assumptions about mentorship, hierarchy, and institutional responsibility. Alternative explanations were actively discussed, including whether reported QRPs reflected deliberate misconduct, lack of procedural knowledge, institutional pressure, or ambiguity in undergraduate research processes. Emerging interpretations were revised when contradictory or minority accounts did not fit the initial coding structure [23, 28].

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the relevant institutional review boards. All participants provided written informed consent, were informed of voluntary participation and withdrawal rights, and were assured of confidentiality. Data were de-identified and stored securely on password-protected devices [16].

Patient and public involvement statement

This study did not involve patient or public contributors in its design, conduct, analysis, or dissemination. The research focused on undergraduate dental students and faculty mentors as research participants rather than patients or members of the public. As such, patient and public involvement did not apply to this study. However, the findings have indirect relevance to patients and the public, as strengthening research integrity among future dental professionals ultimately contributes to the credibility and trustworthiness of dental research that informs clinical care.

Results

A total of 20 in-depth interviews were conducted: 10 with undergraduate dental students and 10 with mentors. Each participant group comprised five individuals from the public dental school and five from the private dental school. Students from the public institution were coded as UG_Stu_Pub and students from the private institution as UG_Stu_Pri. Mentors from the public institution were coded as M_Pub and mentors from the private institution as M_Pri. Quotations are presented with participant role and institution type to help readers interpret how experiences differed across student and mentor groups and between public and private institutional contexts. Student interviews were labeled IDI_Stu 01–10, and mentor interviews were labeled IDI_Men 01–10.

The term “mentor” is used in this section as an umbrella term for faculty members who provided either formal research supervision or broader academic, methodological, and ethical guidance during undergraduate research.

The themes should not be read as separate or linear categories. Together, they describe a research integrity ecosystem in which students' ethical awareness, mentor–mentee relationships, hierarchy, and institutional structures interacted in everyday research practice. The analysis suggests that QRPs were rarely described as isolated acts of deliberate wrongdoing. Rather, participants often framed them as the outcome of uncertainty, normalization of shortcuts, unclear authorship expectations, time pressure, and limited safe spaces for questioning decisions. Mentorship appeared in participants’ accounts as a double-edged process: it could translate abstract ethics into practical judgment, but it could also reinforce silence when embedded within hierarchical or inconsistent supervisory relationships.

Theme 1: Ethical awareness with limited procedural confidence

This theme indicates that participants generally possessed a basic awareness of research ethics, yet this awareness was often superficial and insufficient for managing real-world research situations. Ethical principles were commonly recognized at a conceptual level, but their practical application remained inconsistent. Rather than reflecting a complete absence of ethical knowledge, participants described an awareness–practice gap in which students knew the language of research ethics but were less confident in applying ethical principles during actual research tasks.

Subtheme 1.1 Foundational ethical awareness

Students from both sectors described familiarity with core ethical concepts, including plagiarism, confidentiality, informed consent, and the importance of ethics approval. This pattern was evident in accounts from UG_Stu_Pub, IDI_Stu 01; UG_Stu_Pub, IDI_Stu 02; and UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 06. Mentors similarly observed that students could generally identify major ethical principles, as noted by M_Pub, IDI_Men 01, and M_Pri, IDI_Men 06. However, participants often described this awareness as conceptual rather than procedural. One public-sector student explained: “We are aware that plagiarism and data manipulation are wrong, but sometimes we are not sure about the proper procedures of informed consent, especially when participants are minors or illiterate” (UG_Stu_Pub, IDI_Stu 02).

A private-sector mentor similarly highlighted the gap between knowing ethical terminology and applying it in real situations: “Students can repeat definitions of ethics, but when confronted with a practical dilemma, they lack the confidence to make the right decision” (M_Pri, IDI_Men 06).

These accounts suggest that participants were familiar with the basic vocabulary of research ethics, but this did not always translate into procedural confidence or independent ethical decision-making.

Subtheme 1.2 Difficulty translating ethics into practice

Although ethical principles were recognized, participants frequently reported difficulty applying them in research procedures such as ethics applications, participant recruitment, documentation, data interpretation, and authorship decisions. This difficulty was particularly evident in narratives from UG_Stu_Pub, IDI_Stu 03; UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 07; and UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 08. Mentors M_Pub, IDI_Men 02, and M_Pri, IDI_Men 07, also emphasized this gap.

Participants described difficulty translating ethical rules into research procedures, particularly when completing ethics forms, navigating institutional review processes, or applying consent requirements. One undergraduate student from the private sector described ethics documentation as a procedural burden rather than a learning process: “It is one thing to know the rule, but another to know what to do step by step when you are actually conducting the study” (UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 07). Another student from the private sector said: “The university ethics form is long and complicated, but we never get trained on how to fill it. So, we just copy what others did last year” (UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 09).

Faculty accounts also suggested that institutional review processes could appear intimidating to students and may discourage meaningful engagement with ethics. These responses indicate that ethics procedures were sometimes treated as administrative requirements rather than as safeguards for responsible research conduct.

Subtheme 1.3 Ambiguity in consent and field procedures

Uncertainty became more pronounced in field-based or participant-facing research, particularly when time constraints, literacy barriers, or vulnerable populations were involved. This concern was described by UG_Stu_Pub, IDI_Stu 04; UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 09; M_Pub, IDI_Men 03; and M_Pri, IDI_Men 08.

Uncertainty was especially visible in field-based or community-based research, where students had to explain research procedures to participants with different literacy levels or vulnerabilities. One private-sector student explained: “We are told to keep things brief, but then I worry if people really understand what they’re agreeing to” (UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 09).

This quotation illustrates a practical ethical tension: students recognized the importance of meaningful consent but also felt pressure to keep field procedures brief. The subtheme therefore reflects not only a lack of knowledge but also the difficulty of applying ethical principles under real-world field conditions.

Theme 2: QRPs as normalized research shortcuts

This theme describes the types of questionable research practices that participants reported encountering during undergraduate research. The most prominent concerns related to authorship, data presentation, interpretation of findings, and procedural compliance. Participants often described these practices as “shortcuts” or routine adjustments rather than as explicit misconduct. This normalization is important because it shows how QRPs may become embedded in everyday research practice when ethical expectations are unclear or weakly supervised.

The strongest code clusters in this theme were improper authorship, power imbalance in authorship decisions, selective reporting, misinterpretation of participant responses, procedural shortcuts, and weak procedural compliance. These practices were often described as routine or tolerated research shortcuts rather than as overt misconduct.

Subtheme 2.1 Improper authorship

Improper authorship was the most frequently discussed form of QRP. Participants described situations in which credit was unevenly distributed, substantial contributions were overlooked, or senior names were included by default. These experiences were reported by UG_Stu_Pub, IDI_Stu 05; UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 10; and UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 08. Mentors M_Pub, IDI_Men 04, and M_Pri, IDI_Men 09, also acknowledged that authorship decisions were often influenced by hierarchy rather than transparent criteria.

Improper authorship was one of the most frequently reported QRPs. The main codes within this subtheme were contributors not being credited appropriately, unqualified individuals being included, seniority shaping author order, and power imbalances affecting authorship decisions. These patterns suggest that authorship concerns were not only technical misunderstandings of authorship criteria; they also reflected academic hierarchy and students' limited ability to negotiate credit.

One private-sector student stated: “Sometimes the person who contributed the most is not the one recognized most, and senior names are added automatically” (UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 10).

Authorship-related concerns were therefore not only about a lack of knowledge of authorship rules. They also reflected hierarchy, dependence on faculty approval, and limited space for students to negotiate credit openly.

Subtheme 2.2 Selective reporting and interpretive distortion

Participants also described selective reporting of findings and reinterpretation of data in ways that favoured expected outcomes. This was discussed by UG_Stu_Pub, IDI_Stu 01; UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 06; and UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 07, as well as by mentors M_Pub, IDI_Men 05 and M_Pri, IDI_Men 10.

Selective reporting and interpretive distortion were reflected in codes such as omission of data points, exclusion of participant responses, discrepancy between raw responses and final reports, and interpretation of findings to fit expected outcomes. Participants’ accounts suggested that these practices may occur through selective emphasis or weak analytic supervision rather than always through explicit fabrication. This subtheme therefore captures the grey zone between poor methodological practice and ethically questionable reporting.

One private-sector student remarked: “If some responses do not support the expected point, they may be left out or given less importance in the final report” (UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 06).

Mentors interpreted such practices as resulting partly from poor methodological understanding and partly from performance pressure. This created a tension between producing a clean or expected result and representing data transparently. Participants did not always describe such practices as deliberate falsification; rather, they often appeared to occur through selective emphasis, weak analytic guidance, or pressure to produce presentable findings.

Subtheme 2.3 Procedural shortcuts

Procedural shortcuts included commencing tasks before all formalities were completed, giving weak attention to documentation, and treating consent as a routine step rather than a meaningful ethical process. This issue emerged in accounts from UG_Stu_Pub, IDI_Stu 02; UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 09; M_Pub, IDI_Men 01; and M_Pri, IDI_Men 06.

Procedural shortcuts were reflected in codes such as beginning research steps before formal approval, incomplete consent processes, weak documentation, and treating ethics procedures as routine paperwork. These patterns suggest that some QRPs became normalized when institutional processes were perceived as administrative hurdles rather than educational safeguards. The student quotation about copying previous ethics forms also supports this subtheme: “The university ethics form is long and complicated, but we never get trained on how to fill it. So, we just copy what others did last year” (UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 09).

One public-sector mentor explained: “What students may view as minor shortcuts can actually become significant ethical problems when approvals, documentation, or consent are treated casually” (M_Pub, IDI_Men 01).

These findings suggest that some QRPs were normalized because they were perceived as practical adjustments rather than ethical violations. Procedural shortcuts appeared more likely when students lacked step-by-step guidance or when institutional processes were viewed mainly as administrative hurdles, rather than resulting from deliberate intent alone.

Theme 3: Mentorship as ethical scaffolding and a source of silence

This theme indicates that mentorship was central to how students learned and enacted research ethics. When mentoring was accessible and ethically engaged, it supported integrity; when it was hierarchical or inconsistent, it created conditions in which silence and questionable practices could persist. The analysis therefore does not present mentorship as simply protective or harmful. Instead, mentorship functioned as a dual process: it could scaffold ethical learning, but it could also reproduce silence when students felt unable to question senior decisions.

The findings suggest that mentorship was experienced as both supportive and inconsistent. Mentorship was helpful when mentors discussed ethical issues, guided students during dilemmas, and were approachable. However, mentoring was less effective when it remained informal, inconsistent, or shaped by hierarchy. The main code clusters under this theme were mentor accessibility, mentor-led ethical discussion, ethical role modelling, inconsistent mentor involvement, power imbalance, and student hesitation in questioning seniors.

Subtheme 3.1 Mentorship as ethical support

Students who described strong mentoring relationships consistently linked them with a better understanding of research integrity and greater confidence in handling ethical dilemmas. This subtheme was illustrated by UG_Stu_Pub, IDI_Stu 03; UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 08; and UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 10, with supporting reflections from M_Pub, IDI_Men 02 and M_Pri, IDI_Men 07.

Mentorship was perceived as supportive when mentors helped students understand ethical concerns and guided them in applying ethical principles. Participants reported that mentorship was helpful when mentors were approachable and provided practical ethical guidance. This was reflected in one private-sector student’s account: “When the mentor actively explains things, ethical issues become much easier to understand and manage” (UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 08).

Students described mentorship as useful when it helped them interpret ethical principles, clarify research procedures, and respond to questionable situations. In these accounts, mentors functioned as practical guides rather than only academic supervisors. Mentorship, therefore, operated as ethical scaffolding when it translated abstract rules into concrete research practices.

Mentors who saw themselves as role models emphasized the importance of discussing authorship, consent, and reporting practices explicitly rather than assuming students would learn these informally. This indicates that mentorship was most useful when it translated ethical concepts into practical actions, such as how to obtain consent, document contributions, manage data, and report findings honestly.

Subtheme 3.2 Hierarchy and silence

At the same time, students described the mentor–mentee relationship as shaped by hierarchy, which sometimes discouraged open communication. Students from both public and private sectors reported reluctance to question decisions made by senior faculty. This was evident in the narratives of UG_Stu_Pub, IDI_Stu 04; UG_Stu_Pub, IDI_Stu 05; and UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 06, and was also recognized by mentors M_Pub, IDI_Men 03 and M_Pri, IDI_Men 08.

The findings identified power imbalances as a major concern in undergraduate research involvement and authorship decisions. These accounts support the interpretation that students may recognize ethical discomfort but still hesitate to question senior faculty. In this subtheme, the strongest codes were fear of questioning seniors, dependence on mentor approval, power imbalance in authorship decisions, and concern about academic or relational consequences.

One public-sector student stated: “Even when something feels uncomfortable, it is difficult to raise because mentors are senior and students do not want to appear disrespectful” (UG_Stu_Pub, IDI_Stu 05).

This subtheme highlights how hierarchy could reduce the protective value of mentorship. In such cases, students' ethical concern did not necessarily lead to action because questioning a mentor could be perceived as disrespectful or risky. Silence therefore emerged as a social response to hierarchy, not simply as a lack of ethical awareness.

Subtheme 3.3 Variability in supervisory engagement

Mentoring quality was not experienced uniformly. Some participants reported regular guidance and active oversight, whereas others described minimal interaction outside key administrative steps. This variation was reported by UG_Stu_Pub, IDI_Stu 01; UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 07; UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 09; M_Pub, IDI_Men 04; and M_Pri, IDI_Men 09.

Mentorship was not described as uniform across participants. Most students perceived mentors as accessible, while a smaller proportion described minimal mentor involvement. This contrast supports the interpretation that simply assigning a mentor is not sufficient. The ethical value of mentorship depended on whether mentors were available, engaged, explicit about ethical expectations, and willing to discuss dilemmas.

One private-sector student observed: “Some mentors stay involved throughout the project, but in other cases, students are mostly left to manage on their own” (UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 07).

This variation is important because it challenges the assumption that assigning a mentor is sufficient. Participants’ accounts suggest that the ethical value of mentorship depended on the quality, timing, consistency, and openness of supervisory engagement.

Minority and contrasting accounts

Not all accounts were negative. Several students perceived mentors as approachable and helpful in ethical discussions. This contrasting pattern suggests that hierarchy did not automatically prevent ethical guidance. Rather, the mentor–mentee relationship became restrictive when authority was combined with limited availability, unclear expectations, or a lack of safe questioning.

Theme 4: Institutional systems as conditions that enable or constrain research integrity

This theme indicates that ethical conduct was strongly influenced by the broader institutional environment. Participants described both structural deficiencies and cultural pressures that limited the consistent practice of research integrity. The findings suggest that undergraduate QRPs should not be interpreted only as individual student errors. They were also connected to institutional systems that shaped access to training, resources, reporting pathways, and expectations around research productivity.

Subtheme 4.1 Limited resources and formal training

Participants, particularly those from the public sector, emphasized limited access to plagiarism-detection tools, practical ethics teaching, and structured support for research processes. This issue was raised by UG_Stu_Pub, IDI_Stu 02; UG_Stu_Pub, IDI_Stu 04; UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 08; M_Pub, IDI_Men 05; and M_Pri, IDI_Men 10.

Resource limitations were especially visible in relation to plagiarism checking, practical ethics training, and research guidance. One participant described the lack of access to plagiarism-detection tools: “We don’t have proper plagiarism software, so even if we want to check our work, we can’t” (UG_Stu_Pub, IDI_Stu 02).

This quotation shows that students' ability to follow ethical research practices was partly dependent on institutional resources. Ethical conduct was expected, but the tools and support needed to enact it were not always available.

One public-sector student stated: “We are expected to conduct research properly, but the training is not always enough, and the practical support is limited” (UG_Stu_Pub, IDI_Stu 04).

These accounts suggest that ethical behaviour was expected without always being institutionally enabled. Participants did not describe formal ethics teaching as absent in every case, but they repeatedly emphasized that practical, stepwise guidance was insufficient.

Subtheme 4.2 Productivity pressure

Mentors, more than students, discussed publication pressure and performance expectations as contributors to compromised research conduct. This subtheme was reflected in accounts from M_Pub, IDI_Men 04; M_Pub, IDI_Men 05; M_Pri, IDI_Men 09; and M_Pri, IDI_Men 10, with supporting student perceptions from UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 10.

Publication pressure was described as an institutional factor that could shift attention away from quality and integrity. One mentor stated: “When your job security depends on how many papers you produce, quantity sometimes overshadows quality” (M_Pub, IDI_Men 05).

This quotation supports the interpretation that productivity pressure may indirectly normalize QRPs by making output appear more important than careful ethical reflection, transparent reporting, and methodological rigor.

One private-sector mentor stated: “When output becomes the main measure of success, there is less space for careful ethical reflection” (M_Pri, IDI_Men 09).

Participants interpreted productivity culture as a factor that could indirectly encourage QRPs. This did not mean that publication pressure automatically caused unethical conduct; rather, participants perceived that pressure to complete or publish work could make shortcuts appear more acceptable or less visible.

Subtheme 4.3 Absence of safe reporting pathways

A final institutional barrier was the lack of clear and confidential mechanisms through which students could report questionable practices. This concern was shared by UG_Stu_Pub, IDI_Stu 05; UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 07; UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 09; M_Pub, IDI_Men 03; and M_Pri, IDI_Men 08.

Participants also emphasized the absence of confidential reporting mechanisms. One private-sector student explained: “Even when students notice something questionable, there is no obvious safe way to raise it without risking relationships” (UG_Stu_Pri, IDI_Stu 07).

This account shows how the absence of safe reporting channels may reinforce silence. Students may recognize questionable practices but remain uncertain about how to raise concerns without risking academic or relational consequences.

The absence of reporting channels appeared to reinforce silence and dependence on informal resolution. Participants suggested that students may recognize questionable practices but remain uncertain about whom to approach, how concerns will be handled, and whether raising concerns could affect academic relationships.

Public–private comparison

Across both institutions, participants described similar core concerns: authorship ambiguity, limited procedural confidence, hierarchical silence, and inconsistent practical ethics guidance. However, the institutional context shaped how these concerns were experienced. Public-sector participants more often emphasized limited access to plagiarism-detection tools, procedural support, and practical research guidance. Private-sector participants more often described stronger administrative oversight, although they also reported hierarchy and performance pressure. These differences suggest that institutional resources and governance structures may influence how ethical challenges are managed, but they did not eliminate the underlying concerns related to mentorship, authorship, and silence. Therefore, the public–private comparison is best understood as contextual variation rather than evidence of one sector being ethically stronger than the other.

The public–private comparison was supported by codes related to resource availability, procedural support, administrative oversight, and institutional accountability. Public-sector accounts more strongly reflected resource limitations, particularly limited access to plagiarism-checking tools and procedural research support, while private-sector accounts more often reflected administrative oversight alongside continuing concerns about hierarchy and performance pressure. These patterns suggest contextual variation between institutions without implying that one sector was ethically superior to the other.

Summarized findings

Taken together, the findings indicate that ethical awareness was present across both participant groups, but practical application remained fragile. Participants described a pattern in which students knew core ethical concepts but struggled to apply them in real research situations, particularly in relation to informed consent, ethics forms, authorship, reporting, and field procedures. QRPs were most frequently reflected in codes related to authorship ambiguity, power imbalance, selective reporting, interpretive distortion, procedural shortcuts, and weak documentation. Mentorship emerged as an important perceived influence: when mentors were accessible and explicit, participants described mentorship as helping students interpret ethical principles; when mentorship was inconsistent or hierarchical, participants described uncertainty, silence, and reduced confidence to challenge questionable practices. Institutional limitations, including insufficient practical training, limited plagiarism-detection resources, productivity pressure, and lack of safe reporting pathways, further complicated the development of a robust culture of research integrity.

Overall, the results suggest that research integrity formation in undergraduate dental research was not only an individual knowledge issue. It was shaped by the interaction of student ethical awareness, mentor behaviour, supervisory hierarchy, authorship norms, institutional resources, productivity expectations, and reporting culture (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This exploratory qualitative study examined how undergraduate dental students and faculty mentors in two dental institutions in Karachi perceived questionable research practices (QRPs), mentorship, hierarchy, and institutional research culture. The study does not establish a statistical association or causal relationship between mentorship quality and QRPs. Rather, it provides qualitative insight into how participants understood the everyday conditions through which research integrity was learned, negotiated, supported, or compromised during undergraduate dental research.

A central finding was the gap between ethical awareness and procedural confidence. Students and mentors described awareness of core ethical principles, including informed consent, confidentiality, plagiarism avoidance, and ethics approval. However, participants also described uncertainty when these principles had to be applied to authorship decisions, field procedures, data handling, interpretation of findings, and ethics review processes. This distinction between ethical literacy and ethical competence is important because knowing ethical terminology does not necessarily mean that students can manage ethically complex research situations. Similar concerns have been reported among undergraduate medical and dental students in Pakistan, where limited guidance, inadequate research training, and weak institutional support were identified as barriers to student research engagement [13]. International evidence from dental education also suggests that research integrity knowledge among undergraduate students is shaped by formal ethics teaching, academic environment, and supervision quality [11].

Participants described QRPs not only as deliberate misconduct but also as normalized shortcuts occurring in ambiguous research situations. Improper authorship, selective reporting, interpretive distortion, and procedural shortcuts were repeatedly discussed. These practices were often framed as practical adjustments made under time pressure, unclear expectations, or weak supervision rather than as explicitly recognized ethical breaches. This interpretation is consistent with literature describing QRPs as “grey-zone” practices that may fall between accepted research conduct and clear misconduct, yet still undermine credibility, transparency, and reproducibility [29]. Broader research integrity literature also shows that QRPs are influenced by organizational culture, institutional norms, productivity expectations, and perceptions of research climate, rather than by individual behaviour alone [7].

Authorship emerged as one of the most visible ethical concerns. Students described uncertainty about contribution, credit, seniority, and author order. Some participants reported that senior names were added automatically or that students who contributed substantially were not always recognized appropriately. These findings are consistent with international literature showing that honorary and inappropriate authorship remain common in health sciences research [30]. They also align with evidence that health science students may have limited knowledge of formal authorship criteria and may therefore be vulnerable to unfair or hierarchical authorship decisions [31]. In the present study, authorship problems were not simply technical misunderstandings; they reflected broader power dynamics in undergraduate research. Transparent authorship discussions at project initiation, use of recognized authorship criteria, and documentation of contributor roles may therefore be important practical safeguards [32, 33].

Mentorship emerged as a central interpretive category, but not as a simple protective factor. Participants described mentorship as supportive when mentors were accessible, explicit, and willing to discuss ethical issues such as informed consent, data accuracy, authorship, and reporting. In these cases, mentorship helped students translate abstract ethical principles into practical research decisions. This supports evidence that mentors and research supervisors transmit responsible research conduct through role modelling, feedback, supervision, and everyday research practices [8]. Bandura’s Social Learning Theory is relevant here because students learn not only through formal instruction but also through observation, modelling, imitation, and reinforcement within educational relationships [18].

However, mentorship was also described as potentially permissive when supervision was inconsistent, hierarchical, or focused mainly on output. Students reported reluctance to question senior faculty, even when they felt uncomfortable with a research decision. This suggests that mentorship may support research integrity only when it includes psychological safety, explicit expectations, and space for ethical questioning. Where the mentor–mentee relationship is highly hierarchical, students may learn silence rather than ethical agency. This finding is consistent with the literature emphasizing that learners in health professions education are shaped by observed behaviours, informal norms, and role modelling in clinical and academic settings [19]. In this study, students appeared to learn not only from what mentors taught, but also from what mentors tolerated, ignored, or prioritized.

The hidden curriculum was therefore an important interpretive lens for understanding the findings. Formal research ethics teaching may communicate principles such as consent, confidentiality, and plagiarism avoidance, but students also learn from everyday institutional signals: whose name is added to a manuscript, whether protocol deviations are questioned, whether incomplete documentation is tolerated, and whether students can safely challenge decisions. When ethical conduct is treated mainly as paperwork or compliance, students may internalize a narrow view of ethics as form completion rather than responsible research practice. Conversely, when mentors openly discuss ethical dilemmas and model transparency, students may develop greater confidence to recognize and respond to QRPs.

Institutional culture further shaped participants’ experiences of QRPs. Participants described limited practical ethics training, variable access to plagiarism-detection tools, unclear procedures, and a lack of safe reporting pathways. These findings align with Pakistan-based evidence showing that medical and dental faculty report engagement with QRPs and that institutional systems require strengthening to promote responsible research conduct [34]. They also resonate with local evidence that undergraduate medical and dental students face barriers related to supervision, training, and institutional support [13]. Together, these findings suggest that undergraduate QRPs should not be understood only as student-level knowledge deficits. They are embedded within institutional environments that may either support or weaken ethical research practice.

The public–private comparison added contextual insight. Participants from both institutions described similar ethical concerns, particularly authorship ambiguity, inconsistent practical guidance, and hierarchical silence. However, public-sector participants more often emphasized limited resources and procedural support, while private-sector participants more often described stronger administrative oversight but still reported hierarchy and productivity pressure. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence that one sector is ethically stronger than the other. Rather, they suggest that institutional context shapes how ethical challenges are experienced and managed. Resource availability, governance structure, and research expectations may influence how students and mentors navigate QRPs, but they do not remove the need for structured ethics education and accountable mentorship.

Another important finding was the role of productivity pressure. Mentors particularly described publication expectations and performance-driven academic culture as conditions that could reduce space for ethical reflection. This supports wider evidence that research environments emphasizing output, publication count, and institutional performance may unintentionally encourage QRPs [7]. In undergraduate dental education, this pressure can be especially problematic because students are still developing research competence and may depend heavily on faculty guidance. When research output is prioritized over the research process, students may internalize a narrow view of research success that values completion and publication more than transparency, accountability, and methodological care.

The main analytical contribution of this study is that it shows research integrity formation as a relational and institutional process rather than only an individual knowledge issue. Students did not describe QRPs simply as isolated unethical acts; instead, they located them within supervision quality, hierarchy, institutional ambiguity, authorship norms, and productivity culture. This finding extends existing literature by showing how undergraduate dental students and mentors in a Pakistani context perceive the everyday mechanisms through which ethical research conduct is either supported or weakened. It also reinforces the importance of addressing research integrity at multiple levels: student training, mentor behaviour, authorship policy, institutional governance, and reporting culture.

The study has implications for undergraduate dental education, but these should be interpreted cautiously. The findings do not prove that any single intervention will reduce QRPs. Instead, participant narratives suggest areas for institutional attention. First, ethics education should move beyond isolated lectures and include case-based, longitudinal teaching focused on real undergraduate research dilemmas, including authorship, consent, data handling, selective reporting, and protocol deviations. Second, mentorship expectations should be made explicit, including mentor availability, ethical guidance, authorship discussions, and review of research procedures. Third, institutions should consider transparent authorship policies aligned with recognized standards such as ICMJE criteria and contributor role documentation [32, 33]. Fourth, confidential and non-punitive advisory or reporting pathways may help students seek guidance without fear of damaging academic relationships.

Despite its limitations, this study contributes context-specific evidence on research integrity formation in undergraduate dental education in Pakistan. By including both students and mentors, the study highlights how ethical conduct is shaped through the interaction of individual awareness, supervisory relationships, hierarchy, institutional resources, and academic culture. The findings suggest that promoting research integrity requires more than telling students what is right or wrong. It requires repeated ethical practice, credible role modelling, transparent authorship norms, supportive mentorship, and institutional systems that make responsible conduct easier to enact.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It included both undergraduate students and mentors, allowing the research environment to be examined from two complementary perspectives. It also drew participants from public and private dental institutions, which improved contextual breadth and enabled comparison across institutional settings. The use of in-depth interviews allowed participants to discuss sensitive issues—such as authorship, hierarchy, silence, and ethical uncertainty—in greater depth than would be possible through survey methods.

However, the study also has limitations. As a qualitative study conducted in two institutions within one city, the findings are context-specific and not statistically generalizable. The sample size, although appropriate for qualitative inquiry, does not permit prevalence estimation. The study relied on self-reported accounts, which may have been influenced by recall bias or social desirability, especially around sensitive issues such as QRPs. Finally, because the study captured perceptions at a single time point, it cannot determine how ethical awareness or mentoring experiences change over time.

Policy implications

The findings should be interpreted as qualitative evidence of perceived needs rather than direct evidence that specific interventions will reduce QRPs. Within this limitation, the participant narratives suggest several areas for institutional attention. First, undergraduate research ethics teaching may need to move beyond isolated lectures or form-based instruction toward case-based, longitudinal learning that helps students apply principles to authorship, consent, data handling, and reporting dilemmas. Second, institutions could consider structured mentorship expectations that encourage early discussion of authorship, contribution criteria, research responsibilities, and procedures for handling ethical uncertainty. Third, transparent authorship guidance aligned with recognized standards may help reduce ambiguity and perceived hierarchy-driven credit allocation. Fourth, confidential and non-punitive reporting or advisory pathways may help students seek guidance without fear of damaging academic relationships. These implications require future evaluation through larger multi-institutional and intervention-focused studies.

Way forward

Future research should include larger multi-institutional studies across dental schools in Pakistan to determine whether the patterns identified here are replicated in other regions and institutional types. Mixed-methods and longitudinal designs would be particularly valuable for examining whether formal ethics teaching, mentor training, and governance reforms reduce QRPs over time. At the practical level, pilot interventions should be designed and evaluated, such as mentor training in research integrity, case-based ethics workshops for students, authorship agreements at project initiation, and department-level reflective forums where ethical dilemmas can be discussed safely. A durable culture of integrity is unlikely to emerge from one-time workshops; it requires repeated exposure, institutional reinforcement, and credible role modelling.

Conclusion

This exploratory qualitative study suggests that undergraduate dental research integrity is shaped through everyday interactions among ethical awareness, mentorship, hierarchy, and institutional culture. Participants described awareness of core ethical principles but limited confidence in applying these principles to authorship, consent, data handling, interpretation, and reporting. QRPs were most visible in accounts of improper authorship, selective reporting, interpretive distortion, and procedural shortcuts. Mentorship was perceived as valuable when it provided accessible, explicit, and ethically grounded guidance; however, hierarchical or inconsistent supervision could discourage students from questioning questionable practices. These findings do not demonstrate a causal relationship between mentorship and QRPs, but they highlight perceived mechanisms through which undergraduate research environments may support or constrain research integrity. Strengthening ethics education, authorship transparency, reflective mentorship, and safe advisory pathways may be useful directions for institutional reform and future evaluation.
