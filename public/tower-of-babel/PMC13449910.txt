A case of retroperitoneal dedifferentiated liposarcoma
Authors: Yunyun Lyu, Yu Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report a case of retroperitoneal dedifferentiated liposarcoma (DDLPS) confirmed by histopathology and molecular testing. A 66-year-old woman presented with an incidentally discovered left abdominal mass. Contrast-enhanced CT showed multiple adjacent soft-tissue masses in the retroperitoneum; some lesions contained minimal fat density and demonstrated irregular ring-shaped and nodular calcifications. On postcontrast imaging, the tumors exhibited a ``slow-in, slow-out'' centripetal enhancement pattern. Postoperative immunohistochemistry revealed co-expression of MDM2 and CDK4, confirming the diagnosis. A follow-up contrast-enhanced CT scan obtained 9 months after surgery showed local recurrence, and the patient is currently receiving further treatment. This case underscores the importance of considering DDLPS in retroperitoneal masses that show minimal fat content, irregular calcifications, and characteristic enhancement dynamics; confirmatory MDM2/CDK4 molecular testing is essential for an accurate diagnosis.Keywords: Retroperitoneum, Dedifferentiated liposarcoma, Computed tomography

Introduction

Retroperitoneal dedifferentiated liposarcoma (DDLPS) is a highly aggressive mesenchymal neoplasm that predominantly affects middle-aged and older adults, with the retroperitoneum and pelvis being the most common sites of involvement [1]. Tumors arising in the retroperitoneum carry the poorest prognosis and are notably prone to local recurrence and distant metastasis [2]. Imaging features vary widely depending on the relative proportions of well-differentiated adipose tissue and dedifferentiated nonadipose sarcomatous components [3]; as a result, DDLPS often lacks the typical imaging hallmarks of liposarcoma, rendering preoperative diagnosis challenging. Herein, we report a case of retroperitoneal DDLPS confirmed by histopathology and molecular testing. The CT findings in this case displayed several distinctive features—particularly multiple lesions, minimal or absent intralesional fat, irregular calcifications, and a ``slow-in, slow-out'' enhancement pattern—that provided important clues for accurate preoperative diagnosis.

Case report

A 66-year-old woman was admitted with a left abdominal mass that she had first noticed 2 weeks earlier. At presentation, she reported no pain, nausea, vomiting, or urinary or gastrointestinal symptoms, including frequency, urgency, gross hematuria, or diarrhea. She was alert, had a good appetite, and reported no recent significant weight loss. Her medical history was unremarkable for hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, or cor pulmonale. She had no known history of hepatitis, tuberculosis, or bacillary dysentery, and reported no prior trauma, surgery, or blood transfusions. Her immunization history was consistent with standard public health guidelines. On physical examination, a nontender, ill-defined mass, approximately 80 × 60 mm, was palpable in the left abdomen. The abdomen was soft overall, with no tenderness, rebound tenderness, or guarding.

Laboratory studies revealed the following: CEA 1.12 ng/mL, CA199 5.6 U/mL, CA125 7.4 U/mL, CA153 6.7 U/mL, eosinophil percentage 13.40% (elevated), magnesium 1.06 mmol/L (elevated), hepatitis B surface antigen 131.28 mIU/mL (elevated), and hepatitis B core antibody 0.45 IU/mL (elevated). Contrast-enhanced CT was performed using a 128-slice CT scanner (Philips) after intravenous injection of 100 mL of nonionic iodinated contrast medium (Iohexol, 350 mgI/mL) at a rate of 3.0 mL/s, followed by a 30-mL saline flush. Arterial phase, venous phase, and equilibrium phase images were acquired at 25-30 seconds, 60-70 seconds, and 180 seconds after contrast injection, respectively. Slice thickness was 5 mm with 1.25-mm reformats. All images were reviewed on a standard PACS workstation. Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT showed multiple retroperitoneal masses of mixed density. One larger lesion, measuring approximately 69 × 59 mm, demonstrated irregular ring-like and nodular calcifications at its periphery, surrounded by multiple calcified nodules and fibrous strands; the adjacent fat plane was slightly blurred, with no obvious central enhancement (Fig. 1), along with multiple surrounding solid nodular lesions that demonstrate similar calcific features (Fig. 2). A second larger lesion, located near the psoas major muscle, measured approximately 72 × 61 mm and consisted predominantly of solid soft tissue, with only a minute fat-density component (CT value approximately -84 HU). Following contrast administration, this lesion showed mild heterogeneous enhancement in the arterial phase and persistent heterogeneous enhancement in the venous and equilibrium phases, with CT values of 45 HU, 70 HU, and 104 HU, respectively (Fig. 3). The lesion compressed the adjacent left psoas muscle and had indistinct margins with the calcified lesion described above. The adjacent peritoneum appeared unevenly thickened. The patient subsequently underwent retroperitoneal tumor resection. Intraoperatively, 6 tumors of varying sizes—ranging from approximately 15 × 15 × 20 mm to 60 × 65 × 75 mm—were identified in the left retroperitoneum, protruding into the abdominal cavity and densely adherent to the descending colon, its mesentery, and the left psoas muscle. The tumors were grayish-white, firm, and solid. Gross sectioning revealed yellowish-white, fish-flesh–like cut surfaces, with 1 tumor containing approximately 20 mL of brown necrotic material. Estimated intraoperative blood loss was approximately 50 mL. Postoperatively, the patient received cefotaxime sodium for infection prophylaxis, along with hemostatic therapy, analgesia, gastric mucosal protection, nutritional support, and symptomatic care. The drainage tube was removed on postoperative day 10, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 16 in good condition for further radiation and chemotherapy at a tertiary referral center.

Postoperative pathology confirmed a retroperitoneal soft tissue tumor. Immunohistochemical staining showed CK (–), vimentin (+), CD34 (+), S100 (–), MDM2 (+), P16 (+), CDK4 (+), pan-TRK (–), and SATB2 (–), with a Ki-67 proliferation index of approximately 5%. Taken together with the immunophenotypic profile and hematoxylin-eosin morphology, these findings were diagnostic of dedifferentiated liposarcoma. A follow-up contrast-enhanced CT scan obtained 9 months after surgery revealed a new enhancing soft-tissue nodule adjacent to the left retroperitoneal surgical bed, consistent with local recurrence (Fig. 4). The patient remains asymptomatic but has been referred for multidisciplinary evaluation to determine further management. She is currently being followed according to our institution's sarcoma surveillance protocol (Fig. 5).

Discussion

The imaging appearance of DDLPS is complex and variable, and is closely related to the proportion of dedifferentiated components within the tumor. Owing to its deep retroperitoneal location and indolent growth, the disease is typically asymptomatic in its early stages, and tumors are often already quite large by the time of detection [4]. CT is the primary imaging modality for diagnosing retroperitoneal liposarcoma; it clearly delineates tumor location, size, margins, internal composition, and anatomic relationships with adjacent structures, all of which are essential for preoperative evaluation and surgical planning [5].

The CT scan in this case shows multiple adjacent soft-tissue masses in the retroperitoneum. A larger lesion adjacent to the psoas major muscle contains only a scant amount of fatty tissue and demonstrates a ``sudden break sign''—an abrupt transition in density between fat and soft tissue—rather than a uniformly intermixed, mosaic pattern [6]. Another large paracolonic lesion shows no definite fatty components. Notably, irregular ring-shaped and nodular calcifications are seen at the lesion margins, with no central enhancement, giving them a thick-walled, goose-egg-like appearance. Such calcifications are thought to reflect retrograde differentiation of tumor cells toward primitive mesenchymal elements; they are considered an important radiographic indicator of dedifferentiation and are often associated with a poorer prognosis [7]. This finding was particularly conspicuous in our case and merits close clinical follow-up.

On enhanced imaging, the mass near the psoas major muscle in this case showed mild heterogeneous enhancement in the arterial phase and persistent enhancement in the venous and delayed phases, with CT values of 45 HU, 70 HU, and 104 HU, respectively. This pattern of typical “slow-in, slow-out” centripetal enhancement is presumed to be associated with intratumoral malignant fibrofibroblastic or fibrosarcomatous components. A study by Priya et al. noted that DDLPS should be strongly suspected when the postenhancement CT value of the soft tissue component of a retroperitoneal tumor exceeds 90 HU or when the degree of enhancement is greater than that of the psoas major muscle at the same level [8]. In this case, the delayed-phase CT value reached 104 HU, which is highly consistent with these findings.

Hong et al. proposed an imaging classification for DDLPS based on the distribution of fat and soft-tissue components within the tumor [9]. Type I lesions are predominantly fatty, with nonfatty components present internally. Type II masses are predominantly soft tissue, with or without interspersed fat. Type III consists of multiple soft-tissue masses adjacent to fat-containing components. Type IV comprises multiple adjacent soft-tissue masses, with fat being either minimal or radiologically undetectable. This schema provides useful preoperative information regarding the likely degree of dedifferentiation; Type IV in particular tends to be associated with more aggressive behavior and a poorer prognosis. In the present case, the patient had multiple adjacent soft-tissue masses in the retroperitoneum, with a very small amount of fat identified within the lesion near the psoas major muscle—findings that fit the Type IV pattern. Notably, the patient developed a local recurrence 9 months after surgery, which appears to lend clinical support to the prognostic implications of this imaging subtype.

When DDLPS lacks obvious fatty components or characteristic calcifications, the differential diagnosis encompasses several retroperitoneal entities. Leiomyosarcoma, the second most common primary retroperitoneal malignancy, occurs more frequently in women. These tumors are typically large at presentation, show heterogeneous density, and are prone to necrosis and cystic change; calcifications are uncommon, and contrast-enhanced CT usually reveals marked heterogeneous enhancement. Undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma ranks third in incidence among primary retroperitoneal tumors and predominantly affects middle-aged and older adults. It often appears as an irregular mass with heterogeneous density; internal necrosis, cystic degeneration, hemorrhage, and streaky or patchy irregular calcifications may be present. Following contrast administration, the solid components demonstrate pronounced heterogeneous enhancement, and septations may occasionally be observed. Solitary fibrous tumor, though rare, is another important consideration in the retroperitoneum. It typically shows well-defined margins and is prone to necrosis and cystic changes, while calcification is unusual. On enhanced imaging, it often exhibits heterogeneous, map-like enhancement, but usually follows a ``fast-in, slow-out'' hemodynamic pattern—distinct from the progressive delayed enhancement seen in our case. Chronic hematoma should also be excluded. In its chronic phase, it typically presents with ring- or arc-shaped marginal calcifications, lacks internal solid soft-tissue components, and does not show progressive enhancement, making it relatively straightforward to differentiate. In our patient, the combination of multiple adjacent soft-tissue masses, minimal intralesional fat, irregular calcifications, and persistent centripetal enhancement pointed toward DDLPS as the most likely diagnosis.

When imaging findings are inconclusive, MDM2 gene amplification serves as a highly specific molecular marker for DDLPS and can be detected via immunohistochemistry (IHC) or fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH). Concurrent evaluation of CDK4 expression further enhances diagnostic sensitivity and specificity [10]. In the present case, postoperative IHC demonstrated positive staining for both MDM2 and CDK4, and FISH confirmed MDM2 amplification, thereby establishing a definitive diagnosis. This case underscores the indispensable role of integrating imaging with molecular pathology in achieving an accurate diagnosis of DDLPS.

In summary, retroperitoneal dedifferentiated liposarcoma (DDLPS) exhibits complex and variable imaging features. However, the presence of multiple adjacent masses, minimal or absent intralesional fat, irregular calcifications, and a ``slow-in, slow-out'' enhancement pattern—particularly when conforming to Hong Type IV characteristics—should raise strong clinical suspicion. Integrating imaging findings with MDM2/CDK4 molecular testing is essential for achieving an accurate diagnosis and guiding personalized therapeutic strategies. Furthermore, this case underscores that even after complete surgical resection, the risk of local recurrence remains substantial; therefore, rigorous surveillance with contrast-enhanced CT, in accordance with established sarcoma follow-up guidelines, is imperative.
