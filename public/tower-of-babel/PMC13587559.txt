Associations of indoor bacterial, metabolomic, and other chemical exposures with allergic rhinitis in school-aged children
Authors: Shiqi Huang, Xuping Gao, Hao Liang, Yonghui Feng, Haokun Huang, Yuan Li, Peilin Cheng, Jiaxin Xie, Chengjun Lu, Guanhao He, Tao Liu, Wenjun Ma, Fangfang Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe indoor environment has been implicated as a critical factor in the development of allergic diseases. However, the interplay among indoor microbiota, metabolites, and other environmental chemicals in relation to childhood allergic rhinitis (AR) remains underexplored, particularly in subtropical regions. This study investigates the joint associations of multi-omics profiles in classroom dust with AR prevalence among school-aged children in southern China.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted in 2023 across 45 middle schools in Guangzhou, China, encompassing 3,944 students. Classroom dust was analyzed using 16S rRNA gene sequencing for bacterial community profiling and untargeted UHPLC-MS/MS for small-molecule compound profiling (including metabolites and environmental chemicals). Classrooms were stratified into high- and low-AR prevalence groups based on the median AR prevalence. Multivariate statistics, machine learning (random forest with SHAP), and interaction analysis were employed to compare bacterial communities and compound profiles between the two groups and to examine the joint effects of bacterial–compound pairs on AR prevalence.ResultsSignificant differences in bacterial β-diversity were observed between high- and low-AR prevalence classrooms (PERMANOVA, p < 0.05). At the genus level, low-AR prevalence classrooms were enriched with environmental bacteria including Pseudorhodoferax, Cupriavidus, Enterobacter, and Marmoricola, whereas high-AR prevalence classrooms showed higher relative abundances of Sutterella, Tannerella, Bifidobacterium, Fusobacterium, Bacteroides, and Lawsonella. A total of 395 small-molecule compounds were differentially abundant between groups (variable importance in projection [VIP] > 1, p < 0.05), of which 239 were elevated in high-prevalence classrooms and 156 in low-prevalence classrooms. Interaction analysis revealed a significant signal intensity-dependent interaction between Tannerella and the oxidized ceramide Cer(d17:1/6 keto-PGF1alpha) (FDR = 0.002): Tannerella was positively associated with AR prevalence at low ceramide levels but negatively associated at high levels.ConclusionsClassroom bacterial community composition and small-molecule compound profiles are associated with AR prevalence among school-aged children in subtropical South China. Specific environmental compounds can substantially modify the direction of the association between bacteria and AR, highlighting the need to move beyond traditional diversity-based approaches and integrate multi-omics data to identify modifiable environmental exposure risks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05339-3.

Introduction

Allergic rhinitis (AR) is a prevalent chronic inflammatory disease of the upper airways, with a steadily increasing global prevalence that poses a significant public health challenge [1–3]. In China, recent meta-analyses have reported a high prevalence of 22% in children and 19% in adults [4, 5]. Beyond nasal symptoms, AR substantially impairs sleep quality, cognitive function, and academic performance in children, while also increasing the risk of asthma and reducing work productivity, thereby contributing to a considerable socioeconomic burden [6, 7]. Therefore, implementing effective preventive strategies targeting prevalent environmental triggers is essential to mitigate the substantial impact of AR.

The etiology of AR involves immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated responses to triggering allergens, which are closely associated with exposure to environmental factors such as household and occupational dust [8]. As a major component of dust, indoor microbiome and associated metabolites may act as important triggers in the pathogenesis of AR [9]. Notably, the rising prevalence of AR, which parallels economic development, can be partly attributed to rapid urbanization and associated lifestyle changes that reduce the diversity and alter the composition of the environmental microbiome [10]. The disruption of the environmental microbiome is a recognized risk factor for chronic inflammatory conditions such as asthma and AR [11]. Mechanistically, microbial exposures are thought to drive AR by dysregulating T‑cell responses (e.g., inducing Th2 polarization), promoting IgE production, and triggering eosinophil infiltration and nasal mucosal inflammation [11, 12].

Several epidemiological studies have examined the relationship between indoor microbiome and metabolome and the risk of allergic rhinitis. Studies across diverse regions, including China (Taiyuan, Shanxi, and Shanghai), Malaysia and Finland, have linked indoor microbial exposures to rhinitis risk, with higher microbial diversity and certain bacteria (e.g., Prevotella) being protective, while others (e.g., Collinsella) are potential risk factors [13–19]. Metabolites, such as pipecolic acid, keto acids, indoles, pyridines, and flavonoids, were significantly enriched in healthy children compared with those with rhinitis [13, 19]. However, most existing studies are limited by small sample sizes and have primarily been conducted in relative cold regions. Considering environmental factors (e.g., temperature and humidity) are key determinants of microbial composition, the bacterial profiles in warmer southern regions of China (e.g., Guangzhou) are likely substantially different [20]. Thus, the generalizability of findings from colder climates to subtropical areas remains uncertain, highlighting the need for further region-specific investigations.

Given that classrooms represent a critical indoor environment for children, the primary objective of this study was to characterize the bacterial community composition and the small-molecule compounds (comprising metabolites from diverse biological origins and exogenous environmental chemicals) of classroom dust, and to investigate their joint associations with allergic rhinitis risk in school-aged children. By integrating bacterial, metabolomic, and other chemical exposure data from classroom dust, this work seeks to provide novel insights into the environmental determinants of childhood allergic rhinitis in a previously understudied subtropical region (Guangzhou, China).

Materials and methods

Study subjects

This cross-sectional study was conducted from September to November 2023 in Guangzhou, China. A stratified cluster sampling method was employed to select secondary schools from all 11 districts of the city (Liwan, Yuexiu, Haizhu, Tianhe, Baiyun, Huangpu, Panyu, Huadu, Nansha, Conghua, and Zengcheng). Five schools were chosen from each district, with allocation based on educational stage (junior or senior high school) and type (public or private). Specifically, three junior high schools (two public, one private) and two senior high schools (one public, one private) were selected per district, resulting in 55 participating schools. Data collection

A structured, self-administered questionnaire was distributed on-site to all students in the selected classrooms (Appendix A). Designed for school-aged children in Guangzhou, the questionnaire drew on previous epidemiological research on indoor environmental exposures and allergic diseases [19]. It collected information on demographic characteristics, residential and indoor environmental exposures, and physician-diagnosed respiratory and infectious diseases. The questionnaire was reviewed by public health experts and pre-tested among a small group of students to ensure clarity and comprehension. During the survey administration, school liaison staff and trained fieldworkers explained the questions and completion requirements to students. All returned questionnaires were reviewed by designated personnel to verify data completeness, and the responses were double-entered to minimize transcription errors. A total of 3,944 valid questionnaires were returned, representing a response rate of 68.4%.

In addition, a separate survey was administered to school administrators to collect information on school-level characteristics, including classroom building age, distance to the adjacent roadway, and daily ventilation duration. On-site measurements of key indoor environmental parameters, including indoor temperature, relative humidity, carbon dioxide (CO2) concentration, fine particulate matter (PM2.5) concentration, airflow velocity, and fresh air exchange rate, were also conducted.

AR was defined based on self-reported history of a prior physician diagnosis, indicated by a positive response to the question: "Have you ever been diagnosed by a doctor with allergic rhinitis?" This diagnosis was further confirmed through follow-up verification of medical records.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jinan University (Approval No.: JUNKY-2023–0125). Informed consent was obtained from all participating students and their parents or legal guardians.

Dust sampling and processing

Dust sampling was conducted in three classrooms per selected school, following the principle of selecting different floors or buildings. Dust samples were collected using a 400 W vacuum cleaner fitted with a special dust collector (ALK Abello, Copenhagen, Denmark) and a microporous filter (pore size: 6 µm). According to the manufacturer, the cellulose acetate filter retains 74% of particles sized 0.3–0.5 µm, 81% of 0.5–1.0 µm particles, 95% of 1–10 µm particles, and nearly 100% of larger particles (> 10 µm). Each classroom was divided into two zones (corridor side and window side). In each zone, sampling was performed for four minutes (two minutes on the floor and two minutes on other surfaces such as desks and chairs). Samples from both zones were then pooled to generate one composite sample per classroom for downstream analyses. No fixed sampling area was predefined, and floor materials were not restricted, reflecting real-world classroom variability. All filters were sealed in plastic bags for subsequent processing. The dust samples were sieved through a 0.3 mm metal mesh to obtain fine dust, which was then aliquoted into two 5 mL tubes for different analyses: 300 mg ± 30 mg for 16S rRNA gene sequencing and 200 mg ± 20 mg for untargeted metabolomic and chemical profiling. The processed samples were stored at –80 °C until further use. Among 159 targeted classrooms, dust samples were successfully collected from 144. Following quality control, 31 samples were excluded. The remaining samples underwent 16S rRNA gene sequencing and untargeted metabolomic profiling. After matching with environmental measurements and questionnaire data, 106 samples with complete information were included in the final analysis.

16S sequencing and untargeted compound profiling of indoor dust

Total DNA was extracted from dust samples using the MagAttract PowerSoil Pro DNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), which is designed for soil and other environmental samples and incorporates inhibitor removal technology. Cell lysis was achieved by mechanical beating (TissueLyser II, 6 m/s, 40 s) followed by chemical lysis, ensuring balanced extraction efficiency for both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Bacterial community composition was characterized by 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. The V3–V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified using primers 338 F (ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG) and 806R (GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT), and sequenced on the Illumina PE300 platform (Majorbio, Shanghai, China). Purified PCR products were used to construct libraries with the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit (Bioo Scientific, Austin, TX, USA). Raw reads were processed using fastp and FLASH (version 1.2.11) for quality control and merging, followed by OTU clustering at 97% similarity using UPARSE. Taxonomic classification was performed against the SILVA 16S rRNA database (version 138) using the RDP classifier with a 70% confidence threshold. Low-abundance OTUs (mean relative abundance < 0.01% or prevalence < 10%) were removed prior to analysis [21, 22].

Untargeted compound profiling was performed using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC–MS/MS; AB SCIEX Triple TOF System, Marlborough, MA, USA). Sieved dust was extracted with methanol–water (4:1, v/v) containing internal standards and analyzed under both positive and negative ion modes. Chromatographic separation was achieved using an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA). Small-molecule features were identified using Progenesis QI (v3.0) by matching MS and MS/MS spectra to public databases including the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), the Metabolite and Tandem MS Database (METLIN), and self-built libraries. The mass tolerance for MS1 was set at < 10 ppm, and compounds were further confirmed based on MS/MS spectral matching scores. A pooled quality control (QC) sample was prepared by mixing an aliquot of supernatant from each study sample. Features with relative standard deviation (RSD) > 30% in the QC sample were excluded from subsequent analysis [23]. A total of 2,732 small-molecule compounds were detected in the dust samples, mainly including organic acids, amino acid derivatives, lipids, carbohydrates, nucleosides, nucleotides, steroids, vitamins, and exogenous chemicals (e.g., pharmaceuticals and industrial intermediates).

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2) and Python (version 3.12.7). Two-sided tests were used, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages, and group differences were assessed using the chi-square test. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using the t-test. Classrooms were categorized into low- and high-prevalence groups using the median AR prevalence as the cutoff, as it is robust to skewed distributions and extreme values.

To quantify the contribution of environmental factors to bacterial community variation, a permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities was performed using the adonis2 function in the R package vegan. The model estimated the proportion of variance explained (R2) by indoor environmental and building-related variables, including temperature, relative humidity, CO2 concentration, PM2.5, airflow velocity, air change rate, floor level, daytime ventilation duration, distance to adjacent roadway, and building age.

To compare indoor bacterial profiles between classrooms with low and high AR prevalence, α-diversity indices (Richness, Pielou's evenness, Shannon, and Simpson) were calculated using the R packages phyloseq and vegan, with group differences tested by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. β-diversity was assessed by PERMANOVA (999 permutations) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities and visualized using principal coordinates analysis (PCoA). Genus-level differential taxa were identified using LEfSe (p < 0.05, linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score ≥ 2.0). To validate these genera, linear regression models were fitted without adjustment (Model 1) and with adjustment for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), family history of AR, indoor relative humidity, and CO2 concentration (Model 2).

For metabolomic and chemical profiling, partial least squares–discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was performed to assess group separation. Discriminant compounds were selected based on variable importance in projection (VIP) score > 1 and univariate t-test p < 0.05, with fold changes visualized in a volcano plot. These candidates were then entered into a random forest model (Python, scikit-learn), and their contribution to group classification was evaluated using Shapley Additive Explanation (SHAP) values.

Spearman correlation analysis was conducted between discriminant compounds and LEfSe-identified bacterial genera, with p-values adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method for multiple comparisons. The results were visualized as correlation heatmaps.

To further examine whether compound signal intensities modify the association between bacterial genera and AR prevalence, we performed interaction analyses. For each pair consisting of a LEfSe-identified genus and a SHAP-identified compound, a linear regression model was fitted with AR prevalence as the dependent variable, including the genus, the compound, their interaction term, and the aforementioned covariates. A significant interaction term was considered evidence of effect modification, with a Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05. Significant interactions were visualized by plotting marginal effects of the bacterial genus across compound signal intensities, with reference lines at the compound’s mean and mean ± 1 SD.

Result

Study population and environmental characteristics

Baseline characteristics of included students and classrooms are summarized in Tables S1 and S2. The mean age of the students was 14.3 years (SD = 1.51), and 53.5% were male. The overall self-reported prevalence of allergic rhinitis (AR) was 15.0%. Students with AR were slightly older and more likely to attend senior high schools (p < 0.05). A family history of allergic diseases was also significantly more common among students with AR (p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in gender distribution, BMI, or boarding status between non-AR and AR groups (all p > 0.05). Indoor environmental parameters, including temperature, humidity, CO₂, PM2.5, and ventilation indicators, were comparable between low- and high-AR prevalence classrooms (all p > 0.05).

Environmental drivers of bacterial community variation

To evaluate the contribution of environmental factors to bacterial community variation, PERMANOVA based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was performed. Indoor temperature explained the largest proportion of variation (R2 = 0.045, p = 0.001), followed by indoor relative humidity (R2 = 0.025, p = 0.002), indoor CO2 concentration (R2 = 0.022, p = 0.006), and building age (R2 = 0.018, p = 0.019). Distance to adjacent roadway also showed a statistically significant but smaller effect (R2 = 0.017, p = 0.036). Other variables, including indoor PM2.5, airflow velocity, air change rate, floor level, and daytime ventilation duration, were not statistically significant (all p > 0.05; Table S3).

SHAP analysis identified the top 20 metabolites and other environmental chemicals contributing most to group classification in the random forest model (Fig. 3c). Positive SHAP values indicated that Lansioside A and 2-O-p-Coumaroylhydroxycitric acid were characteristic of the high-prevalence group, whereas Cer(d17:1/6 keto-PGF1alpha) and DG(14:0/0:0/TXB2) (negative SHAP values) were enriched in the low-prevalence group.

To further characterize these key compounds, we classified them into five categories based on HMDB: endogenous metabolites, endogenous plant/animal metabolites, exogenous food-derived metabolites, exogenous synthetic chemicals, and uncertain origin. None of the top 20 compounds could be confidently assigned to bacterial metabolism. The compounds exhibited a wide dynamic range (median: 3.8–6.9; Table S4), with 2-O-p-Coumaroylhydroxycitric acid and (1R,2R,4S)-p-Menthane-1,2,8-triol 8-glucoside showing the widest dispersion (SD > 1.2), suggesting substantial variability in exposure levels across classrooms.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of 45 schools across Guangzhou, we characterized the bacterial community and small-molecule compound profiles in classroom dust and evaluated their joint associations with AR prevalence in school-aged children. We identified distinct environmental drivers of bacterial community variation, including indoor temperature, relative humidity, and CO₂ concentration. β-diversity analysis revealed a modest but significant separation between high- and low-prevalence classrooms. Several bacterial genera (e.g., Tannerella, Sutterella, Bifidobacterium) were differentially enriched between the two groups. A wide range of compounds—including endogenous metabolites, plant-derived metabolites, and synthetic chemicals—also distinguished high- from low-prevalence classrooms. Correlation analyses uncovered distinct bacterial–compound association patterns between the two groups. Interaction analysis showed that Tannerella broadly interacted with multiple compounds; however, only the Tannerella–Cer(d17:1/6 keto-PGF1alpha) pair survived FDR correction. This interaction was signal intensity-dependent: Tannerella was positively associated with AR at low ceramide levels but negatively at high levels. Collectively, these findings offer a new perspective on how indoor microbial exposure may relate to respiratory health in subtropical regions.

In the analysis of the overall indoor bacterial community, we found that while α-diversity indices (Richness, Pielou's evenness, Shannon, and Simpson) showed no significant differences between high- and low-AR prevalence classrooms, β-diversity analysis based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity revealed a modest but statistically significant compositional separation between the groups (PERMANOVA, p < 0.05). This aligns with previous reports indicating that community composition, rather than overall diversity, plays a more critical role in allergic disease development [11, 24]. This pattern suggests that the quality (specific composition) of bacterial exposure, rather than the quantity (overall diversity), is critical in shaping AR risk.

At the genus level, comparative evidence from indoor microbiome studies reveals both overlapping and setting-specific patterns. Studies on household dust have reported higher abundances of the family Bacteroidaceae in allergic subjects, consistent with our observation of Bacteroides enrichment in high-AR classrooms [25]. A survey of schools in Shanxi, China, found Brachybacterium to be inversely associated with rhinitis, whereas Pseudoalteromonas and uncharacterized Betaproteobacteria were positively associated [16]. By contrast, a university-based investigation reported negative associations of Fusobacterium and Prevotella with rhinitis [26], while we observed Fusobacterium enrichment in high-prevalence classrooms. This cross-study heterogeneity indicates that the links between indoor bacteria and rhinitis are strongly influenced by local context.

Most previous studies on indoor microbiomes and allergic diseases have been conducted in cold or temperate regions, where lower temperatures and reduced humidity may constrain bacterial growth and alter community composition. In contrast, the present study was conducted in a subtropical environment characterized by relatively high temperature and humidity, which may support distinct bacterial ecological structures. Previous studies have demonstrated that indoor bacterial communities are strongly influenced by meteorological factors and geographic location, with temperature and relative humidity playing key roles in shaping bacterial abundance and composition [27]. In particular, relative humidity has been shown to correlate with bacterial biomass and surface-associated DNA concentrations in indoor environments, and seasonal variations can significantly influence indoor bacterial exposure patterns [28]. In our analysis, indoor temperature explained the largest proportion of variation in bacterial community composition (PERMANOVA, R2 = 0.045), followed by relative humidity (R2 = 0.025), underscoring the role of climatic factors in shaping indoor microbiomes.

Beyond direct climatic influences, building-related and other geographic factors also contribute to cross-study heterogeneity. Our results suggested that building age and distance to the nearest roadway also contribute to bacterial community composition (PERMANOVA, R2 = 0.018 and 0.016, respectively). Geographic and climatic differences affect outdoor bacterial inputs and indoor conditions—such as ventilation, temperature, and humidity—shaping distinct community structures and exposure profiles [29, 30]. Building design and occupant behaviors further modify bacterial diversity and composition [31]. Methodological variations, including sample type, sequencing approach, and bioinformatic pipeline, also contribute to inconsistencies in taxonomic signals across studies [32–34]. Moreover, host-related factors such as immune status and medication use can alter responses to bacterial exposure, leading to population-specific associations [35, 36].

In our study, classrooms with high AR prevalence showed elevated abundances of Sutterella, Tannerella, Bifidobacterium, Fusobacterium, Bacteroides, and Lawsonella. Several of these genera, particularly Sutterella and Fusobacterium, have been associated with mucosal inflammation, epithelial barrier disruption, and Th2-dominant immune responses [37–39]. Although Bifidobacterium is generally considered beneficial, its enrichment in high-prevalence classrooms may reflect species- or strain-specific functional differences. Indeed, the immunomodulatory effects of Bifidobacterium are known to vary by species and strain: while certain strains, such as Bifidobacterium infantis 35,624, exhibit anti-inflammatory properties in humans, other species or strains (e.g., adult-type B. adolescentis or specific B. bifidum isolates) can induce pro-inflammatory cytokine production in vitro or correlate with allergic phenotypes in infants [40–42].

Compared with previous studies, our findings reveal both overlapping and distinct metabolic patterns associated with AR. Consistent with recent metabolomic investigations of household dust [19]—which reported enrichment of plant- and bacterial-derived metabolites such as flavonoids and indoles in healthy indoor environments—we also identified protective compounds, including taxiphyllin and Cer(d17:1/6-keto-PGF1α), that were enriched in classrooms with lower AR prevalence. In contrast to that study [19], however, we observed elevated abundances of certain metabolites, such as Lansioside A, 2-O-p-coumaroylhydroxycitric acid and nicotinuric acid, together with synthetic chemicals including 1-Acetylpiperidine, in high-prevalence environments. These differences may reflect regional variations in exposure sources, pollutant profiles, or chemical usage patterns. Moreover, unlike a Malaysian school investigation that identified only pipecolic acid as a protective metabolite [13], our data uncovered a more complex metabolic landscape shaped by multiple plant secondary metabolites and synthetic chemicals.

Our interaction analyses suggest that some metabolites and other environmental chemicals may modify the association between bacterial genera and AR prevalence, hinting that environmental compounds could play a role in shaping these bacterial–AR relationships. The interaction between Tannerella and Cer(d17:1/6-keto-PGF1α) appeared noteworthy, as it remained significant after FDR correction (coefficient = −0.939, p = 1.01 × 10–5, FDR = 0.002). This interaction showed a clear signal intensity-dependent pattern: Tannerella was positively associated with AR prevalence at low ceramide levels but negatively associated at high levels. Cer(d17:1/6 keto-PGF1alpha) is an oxidized ceramide; ceramides are sphingolipids involved in apoptosis, fibrosis, oxidative stress, and inflammation [43], and have been reported as inflammatory mediators and predictors of oral health outcomes [44]. Several lines of evidence link ceramides and Tannerella to allergic diseases: airway Tannerella was negatively correlated with mite-specific and fecal IgE levels, suggesting a protective role against allergic sensitization [45], and a metabotype with elevated serum ceramides had the lowest prevalence of allergic rhinitis in children with moderate to severe asthma [46]. Taken together, these findings suggest that ceramides may modulate the Tannerella-AR association through inflammatory pathways. Given that ceramides are present in indoor dust and can vary with humidity, temperature, and microbial activity, their interaction with bacteria like Tannerella could offer a new perspective on how indoor microbial exposure relates to childhood AR, although further functional studies are needed to explore this hypothesis.

This study emphasizes the potential role of indoor bacterial, metabolomic, and chemical exposures in relation to AR risk among school-aged children. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analysis was based on classroom dust rather than individual biological samples. Although dust sampling provides an integrated measure of long-term environmental exposure, it does not capture real-time host responses or dynamic metabolic interactions within the nasal or systemic compartments of children. Future studies incorporating paired nasal or serum metabolomic and microbiome profiling would help clarify how environmental exposures translate into host-level immunometabolic alterations. Second, fungal and viral communities—often abundant in indoor dust—were not assessed, limiting a more comprehensive characterization of the indoor microbiome and its potential associations with AR [16]. Future research should include fungal and viral communities to better understand the joint effects of the whole microbiome on allergic diseases. Third, although standard quality control procedures were applied, no dedicated negative controls were included in either the bacterial sequencing or metabolomic workflows. While pooled QC samples and stringent filtering were used in metabolomics, the absence of blank controls may limit the ability to fully exclude background contamination, particularly for low-abundance or anthropogenic compounds. Fourth, the cross-sectional design identifies associations but does not establish temporal or causal relationships. Fifth, the study was limited to classroom environments in a single geographic region (Guangzhou, China) and a single sampling period (autumn, September–November), which may limit generalizability and preclude assessment of seasonal variability.

Conclusion

In summary, this study links multidimensional profiles of indoor dust microbiomes and metabolomes in subtropical schools to childhood AR. Bacterial community composition, rather than overall diversity, distinguishes classrooms with differential AR prevalence; specifically, Tannerella, Lawsonella, and Fusobacterium were enriched in high-prevalence settings. Notably, the interaction between Tannerella and an oxidized ceramide, Cer(d17:1/6-keto-PGF1α), exhibits a signal intensity-dependent directional shift, suggesting that environmental compounds may critically modulate microbe–health relationships. These results highlight the importance of integrating multi-omics data beyond traditional diversity-based approaches, a necessary step toward understanding how specific bacterial–compound interactions influence allergic susceptibility and guiding targeted environmental interventions for respiratory health promotion in school-aged children.
