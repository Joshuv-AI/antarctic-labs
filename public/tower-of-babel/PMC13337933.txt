Impact of Repeated Antigen Exposure on Humoral Tolerance: Antidrug Antibodies After Single-Dose Versus Multi-dose Adalimumab
Authors: Anika M Valk, Jolinde van Strien, Ninotska Derksen, Dorien Kos, Júlia Castellon Teixell, Jerry Janssen, Floris C Loeff, Lisanne Dijk, Maureen Leeuw, Catherine Smith, Teresa Tsakok, Richard Warren, Nick J Reynolds, Christopher E M Griffiths, Jonathan Barker, BSTOP Study Group, PSORT Consortium, Robert Rissman, Wouter ten Voorde, Justin Jacobse, Daniel Alvarez, Marieke van Ham, Gertjan Wolbink, Anja ten Brinke, Theo Rispens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTherapeutic proteins such as adalimumab can elicit an antibody response. How dosing regimens impact immunogenicity remains ill-understood, especially with respect to the frequency of dosing.ObjectiveWe aimed to investigate the relationship between single versus multiple adalimumab doses and immunogenicity, in terms of anti-drug antibody production and skewing towards the non-inflammatory immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4) subclass.MethodsImmunoglobulin M, immunoglobulin G, and IgG4 anti-drug antibodies were analyzed in retrospective cohorts of healthy individuals and patients with rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, spondyloarthritis, or psoriasis, receiving one or multiple doses of adalimumab, using optimized drug-tolerant assays, newly developed in the case of IgG4.ResultsA single dose of adalimumab proved highly immunogenic, while repeated dosing, resulting in prolonged exposure to high antigen concentrations, led to attenuation of the anti-drug antibody response. Immunoglobulin G4 anti-drug antibodies developed earlier than previously reported with drug-sensitive assays, appearing as early as week 3, even after a single dose of adalimumab. Skewing towards IgG4 was nevertheless stronger with repeated dosing.ConclusionsRepeated high-dose adalimumab exposure can limit both the magnitude and inflammatory potential of the antibody response. These results highlight drug exposure as a factor modulating the immunogenicity of biologics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40259-026-00792-y.

Key Points

A single dose of adalimumab is more immunogenic than repeated dosing.Immunoglobulin G4 skewing of the anti-drug antibody response occurs even after a single dose; repeated dosing leads to further skewing.Repeated high-dose adalimumab exposure therefore limits both the magnitude and inflammatory potential of the antibody response.

Introduction

The introduction of biologics, particularly that of therapeutic monoclonal antibodies, has led to major successes in the treatment of several chronic inflammatory diseases. Adalimumab is a widely prescribed example, that consists of a fully human immunoglobulin G1, targeting proinflammatory cytokine tumor necrosis factor (TNF). It is prescribed to treat a variety of inflammatory diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and psoriasis, leading patients into clinical remission and alleviation of symptoms. However, immunogenicity of biologics remains an issue. Unwanted immune responses may be triggered by the drug, resulting in drug-specific antibody production. These anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) may limit the activity of the drug via neutralization and/or enhanced clearance, leading to loss of treatment efficacy if certain ADA titers are exceeded [1–3]. Because of the variety in assay formats being used, it remains challenging to compare percentages between studies. In the case of adalimumab, studies report a range of 0–87% of patients developing ADAs. [2, 4–8]

A lingering question with respect to treatment with biologics such as adalimumab is how immunogenicity relates to dosing regimens. In particular, a number of studies have suggested that repeated and/or prolonged exposure to high doses of antigen may limit antibody formation, a concept loosely referred to as ‘high-dose tolerance.’ The idea was first established in 1924, when Glenny and Hopkins found that repeated antigen doses reduced the immune response to horse antiserum in rabbits [9]. Animal studies in the 1950s and 1960s have further developed this concept, showing inducible tolerance to soluble protein and bacterial polysaccharide antigens when transferred in massive and repeated doses [10–15]. High dosing schemes were shown to inhibit antibody production, yet the phenomenon remained otherwise mechanistically poorly understood [16]. In the 1970s, high-dose tolerance was proven clinically effective, being used as a strategy to eradicate ADAs to clotting factor VIII (FVIII) in hemophilia A. Owing to a genetic deficiency in FVIII, these patients require life-saving protein replacement therapy in the form of recombinant or plasma-derived FVIII. Yet approximately 5–88% of patients develop neutralizing ADAs (inhibitors) that block the therapy [17, 18]. In line with ‘high-dose tolerance,’ repeated massive doses of FVIII rendered patients susceptible to treatment again, which soon became standard practice in the form of immune tolerance induction therapy [19, 20]. In the case of TNF inhibitors, low and intermittent dosage is considered most immunogenic [21, 22]. In the study of Maini et al., the highest continuous dose of infliximab (in combination with methotrexate) was most effective in treating RA [45]. Unfortunately, ADA assays used in that study were not drug tolerant. Whether repeated and/or prolonged treatment affects ADA development remains unclear, in part because of the lack of studies comparing single versus multiple doses, and the lack of assay comparability (see above).

In humans, such repeated and/or prolonged antigen exposure is associated with a different type of tolerance as well, namely the skewing towards immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4) antibodies. Interestingly, the ADA response to biologics is often described to be IgG4 skewed, as for instance for adalimumab, infliximab, and FVIII [23–25]. Immunoglobulin G4 is one of the four subclasses of IgG and is regarded as a blocking antibody with limited inflammatory potential, as it combines poor FcγR binding and complement activation with a high neutralization capacity [26]. Moreover, because of their ability to exchange Fab arms, most serum IgG4 antibodies are functionally monovalent and unable to crosslink into large immune complexes [27, 28]. Next to further reducing effector functions of IgG4, it complicates their detection in assays that rely on bivalency, such as the bridging enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) often used as ADA assay formats. Despite their low inflammatory potential, it is worthwhile to note that IgG4 ADAs can still be problematic owing to their neutralizing effects.

Prolonged antigenic stimulation has been described as one of the common determinants of IgG4 class switching. For instance, repeated allergen or venom exposure has the potential to skew an immunoglobulin E-mediated allergic response towards IgG4-mediated tolerance, as shown for beekeepers and allergic patients receiving allergen-specific immunotherapy [29–31]. In terms of ADA responses, IgG4 skewing has been linked to the often repeated dosing of biologics, but direct proof is lacking and the merely sporadic development of IgG4 ADAs to chronic interferon-β treatment could argue against this analogy [25]. Furthermore, it is unclear to what extent IgG4 switching and high-dose tolerance are related phenomena.

Here, we studied the impact of dosing regimens on the immunogenicity of adalimumab and IgG4 skewing of the ADA response, by comparing a single administration to repeated administration. With the use of optimized drug-tolerant IgG and (newly developed) IgG4 ADA assays, we could analyze the ADA responses over time in several longitudinally sampled cohorts. We compared a single dose of adalimumab (40 mg, ‘single dose’) to two different regimens of multiple doses: biweekly 40 mg (‘multi-dose’), or a loading dose of 80 mg followed by biweekly 40 mg (‘loading-multi-dose’).

Methods

Participant Characteristics

This is a multicenter study, combining multiple previously collected cohorts. We included healthy participants and patients of several cohorts treated with adalimumab (originator or biosimilar) as detailed in the paragraphs below. We defined three groups of participants depending on the adalimumab or biosimilar dose that they received: the ‘single-dose’ group that received a single injection of 40 mg of adalimumab, the ‘multi-dose’ group that received a biweekly dose of 40 mg of adalimumab, and the ‘loading-multi-dose’ group that received a ‘double’ dose at baseline of 80 mg of adalimumab and biweekly 40 mg of adalimumab starting from week 1 onwards (Table 1). Sex was not used as a variable for inclusion nor analysis.

The ‘single-dose’ group consists of 42 healthy participants combined from two studies. Of these, 12 participants were described previously in the clinical trial by Jacobse et al. 2021 (those receiving a subcutaneous injection) [32]. Participants were healthy and immunocompetent, with a median age of 23 years (range 20–42 years), and of whom 50% was female. Serum samples were collected at baseline and multiple timepoints up to 70 days after adalimumab injection. The other 30 healthy participants were part of a phase I biosimilar study [7]. All were healthy volunteers, with approximately 75% aged between 18 and 45 years, and of whom approximately 50% was female. Serum samples were drawn at baseline and several timepoints up to 42 days after adalimumab administration.

The ‘multi-dose’ group (n = 104) consisted of ten patients with RA, four patients with psoriatic arthritis, and 90 patients with spondyloarthritis receiving 40 mg of adalimumab subcutaneously every other week (without methotrexate co-medication). For RA, median (interquartile range [IQR]) baseline disease duration was 5.5 (1–22) years, C-reactive protein was 9.2 (0.9–16) mg/L, erythrocyte sedimentation rate was 29 (8.7–32) mm/h, and Disease Activity Score-28 score was 2.8 (2.3–3.2); for spondyloarthritis, disease duration was 6 (1–14) years, C-reactive protein was 5 (3–13) mg/L, erythrocyte sedimentation rate was 17 (7–31) mm/h, and Axial Spondyloarthritis Disease Activity score was 4.3 (3.2–5.5); for psoriatic arthritis, disease duration was 15.5 (7–21) years, C-reactive protein was 6.1 (4.3–16) mg/L, erythrocyte sedimentation rate was 15 (15–19) mm/h, Disease Activity Score-28 score was 2.5 (1.9–3.2), and Psoriasis Area and Severity Index was 5.4 (0.1–13). Serum samples were drawn at baseline, and multiple (varying) timepoints up to week 40 after initiation of adalimumab treatment. Patients were aged between 24 and 76 years (median: 42 years) and 58% was female. This cohort includes 74 patients diagnosed with axial spondyloarthritis included in a previous study [6]. All patients were treated at the Amsterdam Rheumatology and Immunology Center, Reade, Amsterdam, The Netherlands.

The ‘loading-multi-dose’ group consists of 77 patients with psoriasis who received a double dose (80 mg) at the start of treatment, followed by a biweekly dose of 40 mg starting at week 1. Median age was 43 (range 20–74) years, and 42% was female. Median (IQR) baseline Psoriasis Area Severity Index was 13.5 (9.0–15). Serum samples were drawn at baseline, 1, 4, and 12 weeks after the start of adalimumab treatment.

Measurement of Adalimumab Concentration

Functional adalimumab concentrations were measured by ELISA as described previously [34, 35]. Briefly, monoclonal anti-TNF antibodies were coated on a flat-bottom ELISA plate (Nunc MaxiSorp 439454; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Herlev, Denmark) and incubated with TNF. Subsequently serum samples were added and adalimumab concentrations were then measured by a biotinylated anti-adalimumab rabbit IgG (produced in-house). Concentrations were retrieved with the use of an adalimumab titration curve per plate. The lower limit of detection was 10 ng/mL [36].

Measurement of Total Immunoglobulin G (IgG) Anti-drug Antibodies (ADAs) to Adalimumab by an Acid-Dissociation Radioimmunoassay

In drug-sensitive assays, a complex formation between the ADA and the drug in serum hinders ADA detection, resulting in an underestimation of ADA levels. This phenomenon, typically referred to as drug interference, is especially problematic in the early phase of the response, when ADA titers are unlikely to exceed drug concentrations leaving no ADAs available for detection. In order to circumvent such drug interference, drug-ADA complexes should be dissociated prior to detection, for instance by a pH shift [37].

Therefore, IgG ADA levels were measured by an acid-dissociation radioimmunoassay (ARIA) as previously described [37] and illustrated in Fig. 1A of the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). In short, serum samples were incubated with 32 mM of acetic acid for 30–60 minutes after which the samples were neutralized in PBS-FAT buffer (100 mM of phosphate buffer [pH 7.6] supplemented with 50 mM of NaCl, 0.3% bovine serum albumin, 0.02% Tween, as well as 20 μg of IVIg F(ab′)2 fragments) containing biotinylated adalimumab F(ab’)2 fragments. After overnight incubation with protein A Sepharose, IgG ADAs were detected by radioactively (125I) labeled streptavidin.

Measurement of Immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4) ADAs to Adalimumab

To measure ADAs to adalimumab in a subclass-specific manner, a new drug-tolerant IgG4-specific assay was set up (Fig. 1B of the ESM). Serum samples were diluted tenfold in 300 mM of acetic acid at pH 2.6 for 1 hour at room temperature (final pH around 3.0). Samples were subsequently neutralized in a buffer containing PBS-Tris (PBS with 1.5M Tris at pH 10), 10% Tween-20, and 30% bovine serum albumin, supplemented with biotinylated adalimumab with a mouse immunoglobulin G2b backbone (ADL-mIgG2b-bt, clone as described in van Schouwenburg et al., engineered with a murine constant domain as described in Falkenburg et al.) [38, 39]. After a 1-h incubation at room temperature, samples were transferred to a streptavidin-coated 96-well plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated at room temperature for 1.5 hours. Afterwards IgG4 ADA levels could be detected using an anti-IgG4 HRP-labeled antibody (clone MH164-4; Sanquin Reagents, Amsterdam, the Netherlands) that was incubated for 1 h. Samples were developed using tetramethylbenzidine substrate solution and stopped with 0.2M H2SO4. Optical density was read at 450/540 nm using a microtiter plate reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). Samples were calibrated using recombinantly expressed monoclonal anti-adalimumab IgG4 (clone 1.2), as described in van Schouwenburg et al. (Fig. 2A of the ESM) [39], to which units were assigned based on its signals in the ARIA, allowing comparison between IgG and IgG4 results. Baseline reactivity was subtracted from samples of all later timepoints to eliminate nonspecific pre-existing assay signals (see Sect. 3 and Fig. 2D of the ESM) and results were expressed as ΔIgG4 in AU/mL. For a subset of the multi-dose group, no baseline sample was available and measurement of week 1 was used as surrogate. A cut-off of 20 AU/mL was determined as a pragmatic limit of detection based on the 99th percentile of week 1 samples. To test the drug tolerance of the assay, serum samples of healthy individuals were spiked with adalimumab (Humira) at 0, 2, and 10 μg/mL (Fig. 2B of the ESM).

Measurement of Immunoglobulin M (IgM) ADAs to Adalimumab

Immunoglobulin M (IgM) ADAs were measured in serum samples using an ELISA format (Fig. 1C of the ESM). To reduce the background, samples were first pre-incubated for 1 hour on a tocilizumab-coated (20 μg/mL; Hoffman-La Roche, Basel, Switzerland), 96-well flat-bottom plate (Nunc MaxiSorp 439454) to pre-absorb unspecific IgM. Subsequently, samples were transferred to an ADL-mIgG2b-coated, 96-well flat-bottom plate (Nunc MaxiSorp 439454) and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Immunoglobulin ADAs were detected using HRP-labeled anti-IgM (Clone MH15-1; Sanquin Reagents) and tetramethylbenzidine substrate solution. The reaction was stopped with 0.2 M H2SO4 and optical density was read at 450/540 nm using a BioTek microtiter plate reader. Two concentrations (12.5 and 125 ng/mL) of a recombinantly expressed monoclonal anti-adalimumab IgM: IgM-aADL2.4 (clone 2.4, as described in van Schouwenburg et al. 2014, engineered with an IgM constant domain as described in Oskam et al.) [39, 40] were used as reference of a low and high anti-adalimumab IgM concentration, respectively, on each plate and used to normalize optical densities (Fig. 2E of the ESM). Results

Strong ADA Production After a Single Adalimumab Dose Attenuated by Continuous Dosing

To study the effect of different strategies of adalimumab exposure on ADA development, we compared a single-dose regimen (‘single dose’; 40 mg) with two schemes of multiple doses: a biweekly dose (‘multi-dose’; 40 mg), or a double dose followed after 1 week by regular biweekly dosing (‘loading-multi-dose’; 80–40 mg), see Table 1. Longitudinally collected serum samples of several cohorts (see Sect. 2) were included in the present study. The single-dose group consisted of healthy participants, the multi-dose group consisted of patients diagnosed with psoriatic arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, or RA, and the loading-multi-dose group consisted of patients diagnosed with psoriasis.

All serum samples from the different cohorts were uniformly analyzed for ADAs with the use of a drug-tolerant ADA assay (ARIA) [Fig. 1A]. In the single-dose group, 52.4% (22/42) had measurable IgG ADAs at week 4 (median 40.6; IQR < 30–140.1 AU/mL) versus 33.3% and 22.7% for the multi-dose and loading-multi-dose groups, respectively, with median levels of < 30 (IQR < 30–52.0) and < 30 (IQR < 30–< 30) AU/mL. At week 10, almost all (11/12, 91.7%) participants in the single-dose group were ADA positive, with a median level 473 (IQR 206–644) AU/mL. By contrast, in the multi-dose and loading-multi dose groups, these percentages were 54.8% (17/31) and 64.4% (47/73), respectively, at week 12 (the earliest timepoint past week 10 that was sampled in these cohorts), with median levels of 55.1 (IQR < 30–116.5) and 57 (IQR < 30–245.6) AU/mL.

Because we used a drug-tolerant ADA assay, we did not expect these results to have been caused by differences in drug concentrations, which in fact, as expected, differed for the different groups (Fig. 3C of the ESM). Nevertheless, to fully exclude the interference of varying serum adalimumab concentrations, we reanalyzed samples after additionally spiking adalimumab in samples of the single-dose cohort to match those of the multi-dose samples, which yielded similar results (Fig. 2B of the ESM). Taken together, these results indicate a clear reduction in ADA formation to adalimumab upon repeated regular dosing versus single-dose administration.

IgM ADA Response Peaks at Weeks 3/4 and Declines Afterwards

To investigate the early antibody response to adalimumab in more detail, we developed an IgM-specific adalimumab ADA assay (see Sect. 2 and Fig. 1C of the ESM). To minimize interference by IgM rheumatoid factor within our assay, samples were first pre-absorbed on a plate that was coated with an irrelevant IgG monoclonal antibody (20 μg/mL of tocilizumab). In addition, baseline reactivity was subtracted from all samples to further minimize background signals. An overall weak and transient IgM response to adalimumab was observed with a peak around weeks 3–4 (Fig. 1C). No correlation or predictive value was found between IgM and IgG or IgG4 ADA titers (Fig. 4 of the ESM).

Early IgG4 Skewing of ADA Responses

As ADA responses are typical IgG4-skewed responses, we next developed a drug-tolerant IgG4-specific adalimumab ADA assay (see Sect. 2 and Fig. 1B of the ESM). Drug interference was overcome using an acid dissociation step, separating ADAs from adalimumab. Baseline reactivity was subtracted from all samples to eliminate nonspecific pre-existing assay signals, which were fairly high in some individuals (Fig. 2C/D of the ESM), but not recapitulated in the total IgG assay, and thus discarded as not being genuine pre-existing ADAs and probably nonspecific IgG4 adsorption [41]. In all cohorts, including the single-dose group, IgG4 ADAs were detectable around week 4. In the single-dose group, 45.2% (19/42) and 75% (9/12) of participants had IgG4 ADA at week 6 and week 10, respectively, with median levels of 24.3; IQR < 20–51.4 AU/mL at week 6 and 66.3; IQR < 20–120.4 AU/mL at week 10. For the multi-dose and loading-multi-dose group, IgG4 ADA were detected for the majority of patients at week 16 and week 12, respectively, with median levels of 105.5 (IQR < 20–502.8) AU/mL and 30.6 (IQR < 20–102.2) AU/mL, respectively (Fig. 2A). These trends parallel those of IgG ADA to adalimumab (Fig. 2B).

The estimated proportion of IgG4 ADAs (fraction of IgG) was larger in the multi-dose group compared with the single-dose group at week 4 (respectively, 56.0 and 17.4%). At week 10/12, the single-dose group had the lowest proportion of IgG4 over IgG (18.5%) as compared with both the multi-dose group (39.8%) as the loading-multi-dose group (54.3%). Furthermore, the proportion of IgG4 did only change over time between week 4 and week 10/12 within the loading-multi-dose group (Fig. 2D).

Discussion

Here, we investigated the relationship between dose and immunogenicity of the biologic adalimumab, in terms of ADA production and IgG4 skewing. A single dose of adalimumab proved highly immunogenic, while repeated dosing, resulting in prolonged exposure to high antigen concentrations (Fig. 3 of the ESM), led to attenuation of the ADA response. Immunoglobulin G4 ADAs developed earlier than previously reported with drug-sensitive assays, appearing as early as week 3 even after a single dose of adalimumab. Skewing of the ADA response towards IgG4 was nevertheless stronger with a high loading dose followed by repeated dosing.

Our results align with the idea of high-dose tolerance, and specifically indicate that prolonged exposure to high levels of antigen during extended periods of time, weeks or even months, is the condition that induces a slower and lower antibody response. High dose also implies amounts of antigen exceeding the capacity of an emerging initial antibody response for essentially complete antigen clearance. It remains to be elucidated how, mechanistically, prolonged and repeated dosing induces a slower and lower ADA response. In case of FVIII replacement therapy in hemophilia A, where high dosing is used to induce tolerance and reduce ADA levels, it has been shown that high FVIII serum levels reduce the development of FVIII-specific antibody-secreting cells [42]. Additionally, when it comes to endogenous proteins, low-abundant proteins tend to have a stronger potential to break tolerance compared with high-abundant proteins. Both antigen-specific T and B cells seem to be able to escape tolerance checkpoints if a self-antigen is only available in low amounts [43, 44]. Interestingly, in the multi-dose group, there appears to be a trend towards declining ADA titers at later timepoints, also in line with for example data on dupilumab [58] and natalizumab [59], also highlighting the dynamic nature of these phenomena.

Following an initial typical response of B and T cells recognizing the exogenous foreign antigen, the prolonged presence of prodigious amounts of the same antigen (in the absence of other signals such as DAMPS) may eventually instruct some T cells to acquire a regulatory phenotype, thereby skewing the ongoing antibody response and enforcing a degree of tolerance. A switch to IgG4 may be one of the consequences thereof (see below). Detailed phenotyping of the antigen-specific T and B cells over time may shed more light on this phenomenon.

From a clinical point of view, the picture that emerges from this and other studies is that prevention of clinically relevant ADA formation to adalimumab (i.e., reduction of ADAs to levels where sufficient drug remains in circulation), and likely also infliximab, is facilitated by continuous exposure to high concentrations of the drug during the first weeks/months of treatment. It could be beneficial to closely monitor drug concentrations during this period and adjust the dose to keep sufficient exposure, thereby minimizing ADA formation [45, 46]. Along the same lines, a loading dose prior to biweekly dosing may be considered advisable in patients with a predisposition to develop ADAs, such as genetic factors (human leukocyte antigen type), previous TNF inhibitor treatment, or activated immune systems. Furthermore, the fact that many of the therapeutic monoclonal antibodies approved in recent years are often administered in high doses may contribute to the fact that ADA formation is often low in the majority of recipients. Of note, to monitor the ADA impact on drug neutralization, isotyping will not be required as a rule.

Immunoglobulin G4 class switching is generally known to develop late into the response, usually months after the first antigen encounter [47–49]. However, within our current cohorts, IgG4 production to adalimumab was observed strikingly early in the response, and even after a single dose. Earlier studies reporting IgG4 ADAs against TNF inhibitors indicate appearance from 3 months after treatment initiation [50], for infliximab after 9 months [51], and specifically for adalimumab after 4 months [23]. Here, we demonstrate IgG4 class switching as early as week 3, facilitated by the use of a drug-tolerant IgG4 assay, eliminating underestimation of the true IgG4 ADA levels. Although we might still miss some ADAs owing to rapid clearance of adalimumab-ADA complexes, this assay will give a more accurate estimate than previously used drug-sensitive ADA assays measuring IgG4.

A known (although not sufficient [52]) driver of IgG4 class switching is repeated antigenic exposure, and IgG4 skewing of the ADA response has therefore also been linked to repeated and long-term dosing of biologicals [29, 30, 47]. Whereas our results indicate that continuous/prolonged antigen exposure leads to increased IgG4 class switching, antigen exposure at high concentrations may be the more effective route to an IgG4 class switch, rather than a repeated antigen challenge in itself. This in combination with the development of IgG4 after a single dose of adalimumab would argue against a recall response (possibly involving sequential class switching from human immunoglobulin G1 to IgG4) being required to arrive at IgG4 formation. Nevertheless, the groups that received repeated dosing show proportionally more IgG4 over IgG titers, implying a stronger IgG4 skewing of the response upon a repeated antigen challenge. Recently, we and others have shown IgG4 class switching after repeated messenger RNA severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 vaccination, with specific IgG4 levels rising steeply after a third vaccination [53–55]. Interestingly, the proportion of specific IgG4 levels started rising already after the second vaccination. Possibly, the messenger RNA vaccines result in the prolonged presence of antigen in germinal centers, thereby facilitating the switch towards IgG4 [56, 57]. To conclude, in vivo IgG4 switching is not necessarily a slow event and can happen in weeks rather than months.

A limitation of the current study is the heterogeneity of the cohorts. The single-dose group consists of healthy donors, whereas the ‘multi-dose’ and ‘loading-multi-dose’ groups consisted of patients with inflammatory diseases. An inflammatory environment could potentially lead to different cellular activation compared with a healthy system. Yet, inflammation would rather be expected to increase antibody production as opposed to the reduction in antibodies we observed. The disease groups were selected for being on adalimumab monotherapy, as it is known that for example methotrexate co-medication can suppress the antibody response to adalimumab.

Conclusions

We have shown that the immunogenicity of adalimumab relates to its dosing. A single dose induced a fast and strong ADA response including IgG4 class switching. Repeated dosing led to a slower and lower ADA response, with stronger IgG4 skewing. Immunoglobulin G4 skewing of the ADA response appeared much earlier than shown in previous reports and in contrast to the classical IgG4 dogma, IgG4 class switching occurred already after a single dose suggesting that repeated dosing was not required for its development. These findings shed new light on IgG4 class switching and the ADA response to TNF inhibitors.
