A growth-adjusted digital prosthetic protocol for a child with X-linked hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia: a 5-year longitudinal clinical report
Authors: Rui Xie, Tiankai Di, Luming Hai, Taoyun Xu, Lulu Wang, Yujiang Chen, Li-an Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA four-year-old boy with X-linked hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia (XLHED) presented with complete anodontia, severe esthetic concerns, and functional limitations. The treatment plan aimed to provide both functional restoration and esthetic improvement, utilizing a fully digital workflow. After a comprehensive clinical and radiographic evaluation, digital cast scanning and 3D analysis were used to create a precise virtual plan for the fabrication of a customized complete denture. The initial dentures were followed by regular follow-up visits at 6-month intervals, with the patient returning for adjustments to accommodate his rapid growth. Over a five-year period of follow-up, the child received updated dentures as his facial and oral structures developed. This approach not only restored the boy’s masticatory, speech, and esthetic functions but also allowed for continuous adaptation of the prostheses as the child grew. This report contributes to the existing literature by introducing a reproducible, quantitative workflow. Unlike purely descriptive digital denture reports, this protocol integrates a quantitative segmented morphometric analysis into a fully digital workflow to support objective, growth‑oriented clinical decision‑making, particularly in distinguishing between conservative relining and the need for complete prosthesis refabrication.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08699-4.Keywords: X-linked hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia, Digital complete denture, Pediatric dentistry, 3D printing

Introduction

Ectodermal dysplasia (ED) is a genetically diverse group of disorders that primarily affect tissues derived from the ectoderm, including the skin, hair, nails, and teeth [1, 2]. Among the various types of ED, hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia (HED) is the most prevalent, with an incidence rate of approximately 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 100,000 live births [3]. The majority of cases are caused by X-linked inheritance, leading to X-linked hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia (XLHED) [3–6]. The clinical features of XLHED include severe hypodontia, thin and sparse hair, and impaired sweat gland function, which significantly impact both the esthetic and functional aspects of patients’ lives [1, 2]. Oral manifestations, particularly the absence of both primary and permanent teeth, are prominent and often require early prosthetic intervention to improve masticatory, speech, and social functions [7].

Despite substantial advancements in prosthetic materials and techniques, restoring oral function in pediatric patients with congenital anodontia remains a formidable clinical challenge [8]. Conventional complete dentures, although considered the gold standard of care, frequently exhibit a fundamental discrepancy with the dynamic, multidimensional growth of the pediatric craniofacial complex [9]. As a child develops, the inherent rigidity of a static prosthesis can function as a physical barrier, potentially impeding the physiological expansion of the alveolar ridges and jeopardizing jaw bone development [10, 11].

Currently, clinical denture management for pediatric patients is largely reactive. Interventions such as relining or prosthesis replacement are typically initiated only upon the manifestation of clinical symptoms, such as significant discomfort or loss of stability [10, 11]. However, there is a pronounced lack of evidence-based guidelines defining the optimal timing and frequency for these interventions. To address these limitations, it is essential to transition toward a dynamic, growth-oriented treatment strategy that prioritizes the continuous adaptation of the prosthesis in response to craniofacial development [11]. In this context, digital tools including intraoral scanning and three-dimensional (3D) cast analysis, provide unique diagnostic advantages for longitudinal monitoring that extend beyond simple manufacturing [12].

This 5-year longitudinal follow-up of a child with XLHED integrates morphometric data with clinical observations to establish a standardized and reproducible growth-adjusted protocol. Rather than relying solely on the duration of follow-up, this work distinguishes itself by providing a quantifiable method for monitoring specific segmental arch growth, seeking to balance immediate functional restoration with the unhindered long-term development of the juvenile jaws. In contrast to existing pediatric digital denture reports that primarily describe fabrication techniques or clinical outcomes, the present study proposes a reproducible, measurement‑driven follow‑up strategy. By translating longitudinal segmented morphometric data into predefined clinical management decisions, this protocol advances digital denture workflows beyond retrospective description toward proactive, growth‑adaptive care.

Case description

Chief complaints

The patient, a 4-year-old boy, presented with missing teeth, difficulty chewing, and esthetic concerns due to congenital absence of teeth. His mother reported that he could only consume soft, easily chewable foods such as rice paste and porridge, and he felt embarrassed about talking and smiling, leading to social withdrawal and discomfort when attending kindergarten.

Medical history

The boy has a medical history of XLHED, diagnosed by a pediatrician (Fig. 1). He exhibits typical symptoms including sparse hair, reduced lower facial height, and severe hypohidrosis. The peripheral blood samples of the affected child and his parents were collected for whole exome sequencing and Sanger sequencing. The results indicated that both the affected child and the mother carried the EDA gene mutation (c.911 A > C [p.Tyr304Ser]), which is associated with X-linked recessive inheritance.

Extraoral examination

Extraoral examination revealed typical features of XLHED, including sparse hair, absence of eyebrows and eyelashes. The boy also exhibited reduced lower facial height, which is consistent with manifestations of congenital anodontia (Fig. 2).

Frontal and lateral views of the patient’s facial profile

Intraoral examination

Intraoral examination showed complete anodontia, with no presence of primary or permanent teeth. The alveolar ridges were thin and atrophic, with a knife-edge appearance, particularly in the mandibular arch. There was a notable loss of vestibular height, and the patient’s oral cavity exhibited minimal structural development of the dental arches. Radiographic examination confirmed the absence of both primary and permanent dentitions and a reduced height of the maxillary and mandibular ridges (Fig. 3).

Clinical and radiographic examination. A Intraoral photographs; B Panoramic radiograph confirming the absence of both primary and permanent dentitions and reduced alveolar ridge height

Diagnosis and prognosis

Based on the clinical manifestations, family history, and genetic sequencing, the child was definitively diagnosed with XLHED. The prognosis for the child’s dental rehabilitation is favorable, with the planned treatment focused on the fabrication and fitting of complete dentures to restore masticatory function, aesthetics, and phonetics. As the child is growing rapidly, the dentures will need periodic adjustments to accommodate changes in his oral and facial structures.

Treatment plan and details

Fabrication of custom trays and acquisition of detailed models (Fig. 4)

Fabrication of custom trays and acquisition of detailed models with occlusal relationships. A Fabrication of individual custom trays; B-C Precision impression of the maxilla and mandible

Custom trays were fabricated to ensure an accurate fit for obtaining the impression (Light-Curing Model Material, Huge, China). Following the acquisition of precision impressions and the determination of the centric relation, definitive impressions were made using a vinyl polysiloxane material (Virtual Heavy Body, Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein), after which definitive casts were poured. These cast models were then converted into digital models via a desktop scanner (Ceramill Map 400+, Amann Girrbach, Austria), effectively establishing a virtual maxillofacial environment that maintained the patient’s specific occlusal alignment for subsequent CAD design.

3D-printed diagnostic dentures and functional impressions (Figs. 5 and 6)

Digital complete denture design and comparison. A-B Considering the short dental arch characteristic of the patient, the digital arrangement was strategically limited to the first molars to ensure proper fit and functional stability of the prosthesis. B Finalized digital complete denture design

Acquisition of 3D-printed diagnostic dentures and functional impressions. A 3D-printed diagnostic denture (maxillary). B 3D-printed diagnostic denture (mandibular). C Intraoral try-in of the 3D-printed diagnostic denture. D Functional impression

Based on the digital model data acquired in centric relation, diagnostic dentures were designed within the CAD/CAM software environment (3Shape Dental System, 3Shape, Denmark). Subsequently, the diagnostic dentures for both arches were fabricated using a 3D printer.

The 3D-printed diagnostic dentures served as customized, close-fitting individual trays for acquiring highly accurate functional impressions. During the intraoral try-in, these diagnostic dentures were carefully evaluated for fit, extension, stability, retention, occlusal vertical dimension (OVD), and esthetics. Minor adjustments to the border extensions and occlusal contacts were performed directly in the oral cavity to achieve optimal patient comfort and preliminary functional acceptance.

Once adjusted, a light-bodied, highly elastic silicone impression material (Honigum Light, DMG Dental, Germany) was applied to the tissue surface of the diagnostic dentures. The patient was then guided through a series of controlled functional movements, including swallowing, speaking, and a light functional bite. This “closed-mouth functional impression” technique allowed for the recording of the dynamic tissue displacement and border molding within the neutral zone, capturing the precise functional anatomy of the alveolar ridges and surrounding soft tissues under load.

Definitive digital denture fabrication and delivery (Fig. 7)

Definitive dentures. A Maxillary and mandibular completed dentures. B Intraoral try-in results of the complete dentures

Digital scanning of the functional impressions, integrated with the diagnostic dentures, yielded a refined virtual model of the oral tissues. This dataset was subsequently superimposed onto the original digital casts within the CAD software, enabling precise modification of the definitive denture base intaglio surface.

A lingualized occlusal scheme was implemented to accommodate the anatomical characteristics of the developing oral cavity, with the aim of improving masticatory efficiency while reducing unfavorable lateral forces on the delicate alveolar ridges. The definitive denture bases were designed with careful control of thickness and border extension to optimize support and retention without interfering with dynamic muscle attachments.

Both the denture bases and the artificial teeth were additively manufactured using a digital light processing (DLP) 3D printing workflow (SprintRay, China). Prior to fabrication, routine equipment preparation and calibration were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions to maintain printing accuracy and consistency. The definitive prostheses were printed layer by layer from biocompatible photopolymer resins intended for denture fabrication. Separate materials were used for the denture bases and artificial teeth in order to better match their different clinical requirements for fit, strength, and wear resistance.

After printing, the components underwent standard post-processing procedures, including removal of excess uncured resin, alcohol-based cleaning, post-curing, and finishing/polishing in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. The artificial teeth were then bonded to the denture bases, and the completed prostheses were adjusted clinically to establish appropriate occlusal vertical dimension, centric relation, and occlusal harmony. The patient’s caregiver received detailed instructions regarding insertion and removal, hygiene maintenance, and dietary adaptation. Immediate improvements in deglutition and articulation after prosthetic rehabilitation were observed (Supplementary Files 1 and 2).

Follow-up

Our follow-up protocol scheduled visits at 6-month intervals to monitor skeletal growth and prosthetic fit. To ensure clinical reproducibility, standardized decision-making criteria were established to determine the necessity of relining versus refabrication:

Relining criteria

Retention

Mild loss of retention where the denture base borders still cover the functional vestibular depth.

Arch dimensions

Quantitative segmental growth of < 2 mm (based on digital comparison) where the mismatch can be compensated by the impression material thickness.

Occlusal vertical dimension

Minor loss of OVD (< 2 mm) due to wear or growth, correctable by adding resin to the occlusal surfaces.

Refabrication criteria

Retention

Persistent instability despite relining, or when the denture base becomes too narrow to provide adequate support.

Arch dimensions

Significant segmental growth (> 2 mm) resulting in a denture base that encroaches on the neutral zone or fails to cover the expanding tuberosity/retromolar pad areas.

Occlusal vertical dimension

Significant reduction in lower facial height (> 2 mm) requiring a complete re-establishment of the maxillo-mandibular relationship.

Over the 5-year follow-up period, the patient attended 8 scheduled visits (with absences at the 6-month and 48-month appointments due to personal reasons). At the 24-month and 60-month evaluations, inadequate denture retention accompanied by loss of posterior occlusal contact and anterior premature contact were observed. Therefore, new complete prostheses were fabricated at these two time points to accommodate the patient’s vertical and transverse arch growth, ensuring optimal fit and function(Fig. 8). During other follow-up visits, occlusal function was restored solely through the application of self-curing relining resin to the tissue and occlusal surfaces (hard or soft liner, depending on the clinical situation) (Table 1).

These quantitative thresholds, particularly the 2-mm cutoff, were derived empirically from the longitudinal observation of this individual patient and should be interpreted as exploratory, case-specific reference values rather than definitive clinical standards. In the present case, a dimensional mismatch exceeding approximately 2 mm tended to coincide with clinically apparent prosthetic instability, caregiver-reported discomfort, and masticatory difficulty that could no longer be adequately compensated by relining alone. Although this value is not intended to represent a universal biological threshold, it may roughly correspond to the cumulative arch dimensional change occurring over an active growth interval in early childhood and was therefore used here as a practical guide for individualized prosthetic decision-making [8].

Dental arch measurement and longitudinal assessment

Based on scanned data of the patient’s maxillary and mandibular casts, the diagnostic cast analysis was performed using specific software (MeshMixer, Autodesk, USA). Using a segmented dental arch measurement method, the width and length of the maxillary and mandibular arches were precisely measured. This segmentation method divides each dental arch into three segments based on four landmarks: the dental arch midline point (a), the canine eminence (b, b’), the distal end of the retromolar pad (for the mandible) or the fovea palatina (for the maxilla) as reference points (d, d’), and the midpoint between b and d (c, c’) (Fig. 9). These landmarks were selected due to their relative anatomical stability in edentulous jaws, as the canine eminence reflects the basal bone contour and the retromolar pad remains structurally stable despite alveolar resorption [9–11]. The segmentation into anterior, middle, and posterior regions allows for a differential analysis of transverse and sagittal growth vectors, which is critical in patients with anodontia. Arch widths were measured across the anterior segment (b-b’), the middle segment (c-c’), and the posterior segment (d-d’), while the maxillary and mandibular lengths were measured along the segmented curves(a-b, b-c, c-d, a-b’, b’-c’, c’-d’) (Table 2). The intra-examiner agreement was verified by the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC), which was 0.970 for maxillary width deviation, 0.915 for mandibular width deviation, 0.918 for maxillary length deviation, and 0.989 for mandibular length deviation, showing satisfactory results. All morphometric measurements were performed by a single calibrated examiner using a standardized digital measurement protocol. Because this was a single‑case longitudinal analysis, examiner blinding was not feasible; however, this limitation is unlikely to affect internal measurement consistency, as supported by the high intra‑examiner reliability.

While the identification of stable landmarks in severely atrophic ridges is often challenging, the landmarks selected in this patient appeared relatively identifiable throughout the 5-year follow-up. As shown in the clinical examination images and impression/cast records (Figs. 3 and 4, and 9), the canine eminence and posterior reference regions remained sufficiently discernible on the digital models to support repeated measurements in this specific case. Nevertheless, some degree of landmark positional variability cannot be excluded, particularly in a growing child with marked ridge deficiency, and this may have resulted in slight over- or underestimation of the longitudinal segmental measurements. Therefore, these morphometric findings should be interpreted cautiously.

The longitudinal assessment of jaw development was based on measurements of dental arch width and length. This evaluation was carried out at three time points: the initial visit, 24 months, and 60 months, corresponding to the ages of 4, 6, and 9 years, respectively (Fig. 10) [12].

Trends in arch width changes

The mandibular arch width remained consistently greater than the maxillary width, particularly in the middle (c-c’) and posterior (d-d’) segments. The most pronounced increase was observed in the posterior segment width (d-d’), with the maxilla increasing by 6.77 mm and the mandible by 7.20 mm. Regarding the anterior segment width (b-b’), the mandibular growth was slightly higher than that of the maxilla (+ 4.18 mm vs. +3.93 mm).

Trends in arch length changes

The total increase in maxillary arch length was greater than that of the mandible (+ 35.3% vs. +18.0%). Maxillary growth was primarily localized in the mid-posterior regions, specifically the b’-c’ segment (+ 7.47 mm) and the c’-d’ segment (+ 6.49 mm). In contrast, mandibular growth was relatively uniform across all segments, with the most significant increase occurring in the b’-c’ segment (+ 3.12 mm).

In this individual patient, longitudinal analysis suggested a relatively greater increase in maxillary arch length compared with the mandible over the 5‑year observation period. These developmental changes are specific to this case and are presented as preliminary observations rather than generalized growth patterns for XLHED.

Discussion

Digital technologies provide a significant advantage over traditional methods in the restoration of edentulous pediatric patients, especially those with complex cases like XLHED [13–16]. In the present clinical report, the use of a 3D-printed diagnostic denture with a full arch form and an accurate closed-mouth individual tray enabled the creation of highly precise functional impressions. This approach ensured a better fit and greater retention compared to traditional manual methods, which often struggle with the low, knife-edge alveolar ridges found in such cases [17–19]. The integration of digital technologies was instrumental in monitoring these subtle morphological changes. By converting the patient’s arch forms into 3D virtual models, we were able to perform precise morphometric analyses that would have been challenging with traditional stone models.

This digital workflow not only enhanced the accuracy of our measurements but also facilitated the fabrication of replacement prostheses at the 24-month and 60-month intervals. While previous reports have demonstrated the viability of digital dentures for pediatric patients, the current protocol advances clinical practice by incorporating a reproducible segmentation measurement method. Instead of relying on subjective assessments of fit, this protocol utilizes specific landmarks to quantify dimensional changes in the anterior, middle, and posterior segments. This data-driven approach allows clinicians to distinguish between simple retention loss and structural growth mismatch, providing a practical threshold for determining when a prosthesis must be refabricated rather than relined. The principal contribution of this protocol lies not solely in the application of digital denture fabrication, but in the systematic integration of quantitative segmented morphometric analysis to inform longitudinal prosthetic decision‑making during active craniofacial growth.

In terms of material performance, the 3D-printed prostheses showed clinically acceptable durability during the follow-up period in this individual patient. Mild occlusal wear of the artificial teeth was observed over time, particularly before relining or refabrication, and slight discoloration was noted after prolonged intraoral use. However, no major fracture of the denture bases occurred during the 5-year observation period. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, as they are based on clinical observation in a single child rather than formal laboratory testing of the printed materials. Nevertheless, documenting these practical outcomes may help clinicians better understand the short- to medium-term behavior of digitally fabricated removable prostheses in growing patients.

The 5-year longitudinal assessment of this pediatric XLHED patient allowed for the observation of distinct developmental patterns in the maxillary and mandibular arches within this specific case. The data suggested that the maxillary arch showed a greater increase in sagittal (length) dimension in this patient over the observation period. The underlying biological explanation remains uncertain and warrants investigation in larger cohorts. In contrast, the mandibular arch consistently maintained greater transverse dimensions (width) compared to the maxilla throughout the follow-up period. Although based on a single observation, this trend may simply reflect the individualized growth pattern of this patient rather than any syndrome-specific developmental characteristic [19]. Given the single‑case design, these observations should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as hypothesis‑generating rather than representative of generalizable craniofacial growth patterns in pediatric patients with XLHED.

A growth-adjusted follow-up protocol is critical to ensuring that prosthetic therapy facilitates, rather than constrains, the biological growth trajectory of the developing oral cavity. In the clinical management of this case, a 6-month follow-up protocol was deemed essential to monitor the rapid skeletal development characteristic of pediatric patients. During these intervals, the application of self-curing relining resin was employed to maintain a stable occlusal relationship and effectively compensate for the progressive increase in the OVD. It should be emphasized that the proposed dimensional cutoffs, including the 2‑mm threshold, are not intended as universally applicable rules but as preliminary guidance designed to support hypothesis generation and future validation in larger pediatric cohorts. This systematic monitoring ensured timely intervention, allowing for periodic relining as a primary conservative measure, while reserving complete prosthetic remakes for instances where morphological changes exceeded the compensatory capacity of relining.

In the initial assessment, the Patient’s Denture Assessment (PDA) scale was utilized; however, it was subsequently excluded from this report as the scale is not specifically validated for pediatric populations. Although the data were provided by the patient’s legal guardians, the authors recognize that a general adult scale may not fully capture the unique functional and psychological nuances of a developing child. Although these evaluations were primarily qualitative, they provided clinically meaningful insight in the absence of validated pediatric patient‑reported outcome measures for removable prostheses. Future studies should incorporate age-appropriate, pediatric-specific instruments to more accurately evaluate the prosthetic outcomes and quality of life in children with XLHED.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as a single-case clinical report, the findings—particularly the quantitative thresholds proposed for relining versus prosthesis refabrication—cannot be considered universally generalizable to all pediatric patients with XLHED. The dimensional changes documented in this study are specific to this individual patient. In the absence of a healthy control group or a larger XLHED cohort, it is difficult to distinguish with certainty between growth variations related to the syndrome itself and those potentially influenced by prosthetic intervention. Given that craniofacial growth is subject to substantial individual variability, depending on both genetic severity and environmental influences, the measurements reported here should be interpreted as case-specific reference observations rather than absolute standards.

A second limitation concerns the assessment of functional adaptation, satisfaction, and quality of life. Because the child was of pre-school age at the start of treatment, he was unable to provide nuanced feedback using conventional adult prosthodontic assessment tools. Although a simplified behavioral scale was used to longitudinally document the child’s adaptation to the dentures, this approach has not undergone formal psychometric validation. Similarly, the adult-based PDA scale was not included in the final analysis because it is not validated for young pediatric patients. Taken together, these issues highlight a significant gap in pediatric prosthodontics: the absence of validated, age-appropriate patient-reported outcome measures for children wearing removable prostheses. Therefore, findings related to patient “satisfaction” in this report should be interpreted as preliminary, descriptive, and context-specific.

The application of a quantitative threshold for prosthesis refabrication also warrants cautious interpretation. The 2-mm cutoff used in this protocol represents an exploratory reference value derived from the longitudinal observation of this patient. Although this magnitude of change may roughly correspond to the cumulative arch growth that can occur over an active developmental interval in early childhood, it should not be regarded as an absolute biological limit. In this case, the decision to refabricate the prosthesis was ultimately guided by functional clinical findings—such as instability, occlusal discrepancy, and discomfort—while the 2-mm value served as a retrospective quantitative correlate of these changes [8]. Accordingly, the proposed threshold should be viewed as a practical case-based guide rather than a definitive treatment criterion.

Another methodological limitation relates to the use of segmentation landmarks for longitudinal measurements. In patients with severe alveolar atrophy, as commonly seen in XLHED, the reduced anatomical definition of the ridges may lead to positional variability of landmarks and thereby influence measurement accuracy. In the present patient, the selected landmarks appeared sufficiently discernible on the clinical records and digital models to permit repeated measurements over time; however, this observation is limited to this case. Subtle changes in soft tissue contour and underlying skeletal growth may still have influenced the perceived location of landmarks such as the canine eminence or retromolar pad. For this reason, the morphometric findings should also be interpreted as exploratory.

Despite these limitations, the present report provides a standardized methodological framework for future investigation. The integration of reproducible segmented morphometric analysis with predefined clinical decision-making criteria may facilitate more systematic longitudinal monitoring in children with congenital anodontia. Future multicenter prospective studies involving larger cohorts, healthy comparison groups, and more robust imaging methods—such as CBCT-based superimposition where appropriate—will be important to validate the reliability of surface-based measurements, refine intervention thresholds, and develop evidence-based, growth-adapted prosthetic protocols for pediatric patients with XLHED.

Conclusion

The integration of digital technologies, including 3D-printed diagnostic dentures, virtual tooth arrangement, and longitudinal digital morphometric monitoring, provided a precise and practical approach for the prosthetic rehabilitation of this child with X-linked hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia. Over the 5-year follow-up period, continuous digital assessment documented measurable maxillomandibular changes and supported individualized decisions regarding relining versus prosthesis refabrication. The definitive prostheses improved masticatory function, esthetics, and phonetics, thereby contributing to the patient’s quality of life. However, as this report describes a single clinical case, any apparent growth tendencies observed should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory and hypothesis-generating only. Within this limitation, the proposed growth-adjusted digital protocol may offer a useful framework for longitudinal monitoring and individualized prosthetic management in growing children with congenital anodontia, pending further validation in larger cohorts.
