Infima and cardinal characteristics of critical ideals for countable compact spaces
Małgorzata Kowalczuk

Abstract
. For each countable ordinal α ≥ 2 \alpha\geq 2 , the ideals 𝖼𝗈𝗇𝗏 α \mathsf{conv}_{\alpha} were introduced in Critical ideals for countable compact spaces (to appear in Fund. Math., see also arXiv:2503.12571) to characterize compact countable spaces homeomorphic to ω α ⋅ n + 1 \omega^{\alpha}\cdot n+1 with the order topology. We study the structure of these ideals in the Katětov order, namely for limit ordinals α \alpha , we show that 𝖼𝗈𝗇𝗏 α \mathsf{conv}_{\alpha} do not serve as greatest lower bounds of the 𝖼𝗈𝗇𝗏 β \mathsf{conv}_{\beta} for β < α \beta<\alpha . We therefore define the ideals 𝖼𝗈𝗇𝗏 < α \mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha} with this property and show that together, the ideals 𝖼𝗈𝗇𝗏 α \mathsf{conv}_{\alpha} and 𝖼𝗈𝗇𝗏 < α \mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha} form intertwined decreasing hierarchies of Σ 4 0 \Sigma^{0}_{4} - and Π 5 0 \Pi^{0}_{5} -complete ideals. Furthermore, we examine several cardinal invariants of 𝖼𝗈𝗇𝗏 α \mathsf{conv}_{\alpha} , computing invariants that have recently appeared in the literature in various contexts.

1.
Introduction

See Preliminaries for notions and notations used in the introduction.

For each countable ordinal
\alpha\geq 2
, the ideal
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
was introduced in
[
9
]
and used to characterize the class of all compact countable spaces homeomorphic to
\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n+1
with the order topology. In that work, the characterization was formulated in terms of the existence of a convergent subsequence defined on a set not belonging to
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
.
These ideals extend
\mathsf{Fin}\otimes\mathsf{Fin}
and
\mathsf{BI}
in the sense that
\mathsf{conv}_{2}
is isomorphic to
\mathsf{Fin}\otimes\mathsf{Fin}
and
\mathsf{conv}_{3}
is isomorphic to
\mathsf{BI}
, and the resulting hierarchy proceeds downward in the Katětov order. The ideal
\mathsf{BI}
was introduced in
[
15
]
and is isomorphic to the ideal
\mathcal{WT}
introduced in
[
3
]
, where the authors showed its connection with weakly tight almost disjoint families.

In this paper, we investigate the ideals
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
, examining both their structure and some of their properties.

In the first section we focus on the structure.
The ideals
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
, for all countable ordinals
\alpha\geq 2
, form a decreasing hierarchy in the Katětov order. We show, however, that for a limit ordinal
\alpha
, the ideal
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
is not the greatest lower bound of the ideals
\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}
for
\beta<\alpha
.
We therefore define an ideal
\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}
which serves as the greatest lower bound of
\{\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}:\beta<\alpha\}
and show that it is strictly above
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
in the Katětov order. Moreover, we prove that the ideals
\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}
are
\Pi^{0}_{5}
-complete. This result gains additional significance when contrasted with the fact that the ideals
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
are
\Sigma^{0}_{4}
-complete. Consequently, we obtain two intertwined chains of ideals of length
\omega_{1}
with different Borel complexities.

In the second section of this paper, we focus on some cardinal invariants, which are fundamental tools for studying ideals on countable sets. They were originally introduced in
[
4
]
in the context of studying ultrafilters on
\omega
. More recently, they have been independently rediscovered by other authors and applied in a variety of different contexts. In
[
5
,
6
]
, these invariants were used to study ideals related to Laver and Miller forcings. In
[
10
]
, to study connections between different types of convergences of real-valued functions. We also compute the invariant
\covp(\mathcal{I})
introduced in
[
8
]
, in which the authors investigate its relationship to a form of destructibility of ideals in forcing extensions.
We calculate these new invariants for the ideals that are the main focus of this paper, namely the ideals
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
.

2.
Preliminaries

Recall that an ordinal number
\alpha
is equal to the set of all ordinal numbers less than
\alpha
.
In particular, the smallest infinite ordinal number
\omega=\{0,1,\dots\}
is equal to the set of all natural numbers
\mathbb{N}
, and each natural number
n=\{0,\dots,n-1\}
is equal to the set of all natural numbers less than
n
.
Using this identification, we can for instance write
n\in k
instead of
n<k
and
n<\omega
instead of
n\in\omega
.
Moreover, for ordinals
\alpha<\beta
, we write

[\alpha,\beta]
to denote the set of all ordinals
\xi
such that
\alpha\leq\xi\leq\beta
, and similarly for
(\alpha,\beta)
and other intervals.
We write
[A]^{\omega}
to denote the family of all infinite countable subsets of
A
.
By
A\subseteq^{*}B
we mean that
A\setminus B
is finite, and by
f\leq^{*}g
for functions
f,g:\omega\to\omega
we mean that there is some
k\in\omega
such that
f(n)\leq g(n)
for all
n\geq k
.

An
ideal
on a nonempty set
X
is a family
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)
that satisfies the following properties:

(1)

\emptyset\in\mathcal{I}
and
X\not\in\mathcal{I}
,

(2)

if
A,B\in\mathcal{I}
then
A\cup B\in\mathcal{I}
,

(3)

if
A\subseteq B
and
B\in\mathcal{I}
then
A\in\mathcal{I}
,

(4)

\mathcal{I}
contains all finite subsets of
X
.

We write
\mathcal{I}^{+}=\{A\subseteq X:A\notin\mathcal{I}\}
and call it the
coideal of
\mathcal{I}
.
An ideal
\mathcal{I}
is
tall
if for every infinite
A\subseteq X
there is an infinite
B\in\mathcal{I}
such that
B\subseteq A
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
be ideals on
X
and
Y
respectively.
We say that
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
are
isomorphic
(in short
\mathcal{I}\approx\mathcal{J}
)
if there exists a bijection
f:X\rightarrow Y
such that
A\in\mathcal{I}\iff f[A]\in\mathcal{J}
for every
A\subseteq X
.
We say that
\mathcal{J}
is below
\mathcal{I}
in the Katětov order
(in short
\mathcal{J}\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}
) if there is a function
f:X\rightarrow Y
such that
f^{-1}[A]\in\mathcal{I}
for every
A\in\mathcal{J}
.
We say that the ideals
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
are
\leq_{K}
-equivalent
if
\mathcal{I}\leq_{K}\mathcal{J}
and
\mathcal{J}\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}
.
We say that an ideal
\mathcal{J}
on
Y
is below an ideal
\mathcal{I}
on
X
in the
Katětov-Blass order
(in short
\mathcal{J}\leq_{KB}\mathcal{I}
) if there is a finite-to-one function
f:X\to Y
such that
f^{-1}[A]\in\mathcal{I}
for every
A\in\mathcal{I}
.
The
disjoint union
of
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
is an ideal on
(\{0\}\times X)\cup(\{1\}\times Y)
given by

\mathcal{I}\oplus\mathcal{J}=\{A\subseteq(X\times\{0\})\cup(Y\times\{1\}):\{x\in X:(x,0)\in A\}\in\mathcal{I}\text{ and }\{y\in Y:(y,1)\in A\}\in\mathcal{J}\}.

By identifying sets of natural numbers with their characteristic functions,
we equip
\mathcal{P}(\omega)
with the topology of the Cantor space
\{0,1\}^{\omega}
and therefore
we can assign topological complexity to ideals on
\omega
.
In particular, an ideal
\mathcal{I}
is Borel (
F_{\sigma}
, analytic, resp.) if
\mathcal{I}
is Borel (
F_{\sigma}
, analytic, resp.) as a subset of the Cantor space.
We will also be interested in
\Sigma^{0}_{\alpha}
-complete sets and
\Pi^{0}_{\alpha}
-complete sets, for some
\alpha\in\omega_{1}
. These notions are thoroughly explained in
[
13
]
.

Example 2.1
.

(1)

\mathsf{Fin}(X)
is the ideal of all finite subsets of
X
.
We write
\mathsf{Fin}
instead of
\mathsf{Fin}(\omega)
.

(2)

\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathsf{Fin}
is the ideal on
\omega\times\omega
defined by

A\in\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathsf{Fin}\iff\forall i\in\omega\,(|\{j:(i,j)\in A\}|<\omega).

(3)

\mathsf{Fin}^{2}=\mathsf{Fin}\otimes\mathsf{Fin}
is the ideal on
\omega\times\omega
defined by

A\in\mathsf{Fin}^{2}\iff\exists i_{0}\in\omega\,\forall i\geq i_{0}\,(|\{j\in\omega:(i,j)\in A\}|<\omega).

(4)

\mathsf{BI}
is the ideal on
\omega\times\omega\times\omega

introduced in
[
15
, Definition 4.1]

and defined by

\begin{split}A\in\mathsf{BI}\iff\exists i_{0}\in\omega\,\left[\right.&\forall i<i_{0}\,(\,\{(j,k)\in\omega\times\omega:(i,j,k)\in A\}\in\mathsf{Fin}^{2})\ \land\\
&\left.\hskip-3.0pt\forall i\geq i_{0}\,(|\{(j,k)\in\omega\times\omega:(i,j,k)\in A\}|<\omega)\right].\end{split}

For a topological space
X
,
we write
c(X)
to denote the set of all convergent sequences in
X
.
For a subset
D\subseteq X
of a topological space
X
, we write
\mathsf{conv}(D)
to denote
the ideal on
D
consisting of all subsets of
D
which can be covered by ranges of finitely many sequences in
D
which are convergent in
X
i.e.

A\in\mathsf{conv}(D)

if and only if
A\subseteq D
and there exist
k\in\omega
and sequences
(d^{(i)}_{n})_{n\in\omega}\in c(X)\cap D^{\omega}
for
i<k
such that

A\subseteq\bigcup_{i<k}\left\{d^{(i)}_{n}:n\in\omega\right\}.

A point
p\in X
is an
accumulation
(a.k.a.
limit
)
point
of a set
A\subseteq X
in a topological space
X
if
p\in\closure(A\setminus\{p\})
.
By
A^{d}
we denote the
derived set of
A
i.e. the set of all accumulation points of
A
.

Let
X
be a sequentially compact space.
Let
D\subseteq X
be a countable infinite subset of
X
.
For every
A\subseteq D
,

A\in\mathsf{conv}(D)\iff A^{d}\text{ is finite}.

For every infinite set
A\subseteq D
there exists an infinite set
B\subseteq A
such that
B\in\mathsf{conv}(D)
. In particular, the ideal
\mathsf{conv}(D)
is tall.

Definition 2.2
.

(1)

If
X=[0,1]
with the Euclidean topology, we define the ideal

\mathsf{conv}=\mathsf{conv}(\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]).

(2)

For a countable ordinal
\alpha\geq 2
and
the space
X=\omega^{\alpha}+1
with the order topology, we define the ideal

\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}=\mathsf{conv}(\omega^{\alpha}+1).

In the rest of the paper, we write “
\alpha
” to mean ”a countable ordinal
\alpha\geq 2
”.

The ideals
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha

are
\Sigma^{0}_{4}
-complete
(see
[
9
, Corollary 7.4]
)
and the ideal
\mathsf{conv}
is
\Sigma^{0}_{4}
-complete (see
[
15
, Proposition 4.9]
and
[
16
, p. 21]
).

It is worth noting that whenever
X\subseteq\omega^{\alpha}+1
and
Y\subseteq\omega^{\alpha}+1
are order isomorphic for some
\alpha
, then the ideal
\mathsf{conv}(X)
is isomorphic to the ideal
\mathsf{conv}(Y)
.
We will frequently use the above observation together with the fact that
\omega^{\alpha}
is order isomorphic to every interval of the form
[\omega^{\beta},\omega^{\alpha})
for some
\beta<\alpha
(see e.g.
[
14
, Exercise 5 in Chapter I]
).

Proposition 2.3

(
[
9
]
)
.

Let
A\subseteq\omega^{\alpha}+1
.
The following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

A\in\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
.

(2)

For every increasing sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n<\omega}
in
\omega^{\alpha}
, the set

A\cap[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1}]

is finite for all but finitely many
n
.

As a corollary, we obtain that the ideals
\mathsf{conv}_{2}
and
\mathsf{Fin}\otimes\mathsf{Fin}
(
\mathsf{conv}_{3}
and
\mathsf{BI}
, resp.) are isomorphic.

The following theorem establishes the structure of the aforementioned ideals in the Katětov order and additionally records one of their properties.

Proposition 2.4

(
[
9
]
)
.

(1)

If
\alpha<\beta
, then
\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}\leq_{K}\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
and
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}\not\leq_{K}\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}
.

(2)

\mathsf{conv}\leq_{K}\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
and
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}\not\leq_{K}\mathsf{conv}
for every
\alpha
.

(3)

\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}\leq_{K}\mathcal{I}\iff\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}\leq_{KB}\mathcal{I}
for every
\alpha
and every ideal
\mathcal{I}
.

3.
Infima of critical ideals for countable compact spaces

In this section, for each limit ordinal
\alpha
, we define an ideal
\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}
and prove that it is strictly above the ideal
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
in the Katětov order and that it is the greatest lower bound of the family
\{\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}:\beta<\alpha\}
with respect to the Katětov order. Then we investigate its Borel complexity.

Definition 3.1
.

For every limit ordinal
\alpha
define the ideal,

\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}=\{A\subseteq\omega^{\alpha}+1:\forall\beta<\alpha\ (A\cap\omega^{\beta}\in\mathsf{conv}_{\beta})\}.

Observe that the ideal
\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}
is Katětov equivalent to the disjoint union of
\{\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}:\beta<\alpha\}
, which serves as a natural greatest lower bound in the Katětov order. The crucial point, however, is that
\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}
is not Katětov equivalent to
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
. This is easier to prove when working directly with
\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}
, since both ideals are defined on the same space.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\alpha
be a limit ordinal.

(1)

The ideal
\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}
is the greatest lower bound of the family
\{\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}:\beta<\alpha\}
in the Katětov order i.e.

(a)

\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}\leq_{K}\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}
for every
\beta<\alpha
,

(b)

for all ideals
\mathcal{I}
, whenever
\mathcal{I}\leq_{K}\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}
for each
\beta<\alpha
, then
\mathcal{I}\leq_{K}\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}.

(2)

\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}\leq_{K}\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}

and

\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}\nleq_{K}\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}

Proof.

(1a) It is witnessed by the map
id:\omega^{\beta}+1\to\omega^{\alpha}+1
,
id(\xi)=\xi
for each
\xi\in\omega^{\beta}+1
.

(1b) Take a strictly increasing sequence
(\alpha_{n})
that is cofinal in
\alpha
with
\alpha_{0}\geq 2
and let
f_{n}:\omega^{\alpha_{n}}+1\to\omega
be the witness for
\mathcal{I}\leq_{K}\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha_{n}}
for each
n
. Define a function
f:\omega^{\alpha}+1\to\omega
by

f=f_{0}\cup\bigcup_{n>0}f_{n}\restriction(\omega^{\alpha_{n-1}},\omega^{\alpha_{n}}]\cup\{(\omega^{\alpha},0)\}.

Then
f
witnesses
\mathcal{I}\leq_{K}\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}
. To see this, take any
A\notin\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}
. Then there is some
\beta<\alpha
with
A\notin\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}
. Let
n
be the smallest natural number with
\alpha_{n}\geq\beta
. Then
A\notin\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha_{n}}
. Since
\omega^{\alpha_{n}}+1=[0,\omega^{\alpha_{0}}]\cup\bigcup_{0<i\leq n}(\omega^{\alpha_{i-1}},\omega^{\alpha_{i}}]
, there is some
k\in\omega
with
0<k\leq n
such that
A\cap(\omega^{\alpha_{k-1}},\omega^{\alpha_{k}}]\notin\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha_{k}}
. And since
f_{k}
is a witness for
\mathcal{I}\leq_{K}\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha_{k}}
, we get that
f[A]\supseteq f[A\cap(\omega^{\alpha_{k-1}},\omega^{\alpha_{k}}]]=f_{k}[A\cap(\omega^{\alpha_{k-1}},\omega^{\alpha_{k}}]]\notin\mathcal{I}
.

(2)
The inequality
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}\leq_{K}\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}
follows from item (1b) and Proposition
2.4
.
Below, we prove that the reversed inequality does not hold.
Suppose for the sake of contradiction that there is some
f:\omega^{\alpha}+1\to\omega^{\alpha}+1
such that
f^{-1}[B]\in\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
for every
B\in\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}
. Take a strictly increasing sequence
(\alpha_{n})
that is cofinal in
\alpha
with
\alpha_{0}=2
.
We will construct sequences
(k_{i})_{i\geq 1}
,
(n_{i})_{i\in\omega}
, and
(x^{i}_{n})_{n\in\omega}
,
(y^{i}_{n})_{n\in\omega}
for
i\in\omega
with the following properties:

•

the sequences
(k_{i})_{i\geq 1}
and
(n_{i})_{i\in\omega}
are strictly increasing and
n_{i}<k_{i+1}
for each
i\in\omega
,

•

\{x^{0}_{n}:n\in\omega\}\subseteq[0,\omega^{2}]
and
\{x^{i}_{n}:n\in\omega\}\subseteq[\omega^{\alpha_{k_{i}}},\omega^{\alpha_{k_{i}}}+\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i-1}}})
for each
i\geq 1
,

•

\{y^{0}_{n}:n\in\omega\}\subseteq[0,\omega^{\alpha_{n_{0}}})
and
\{y^{i}_{n}:n\in\omega\}\subseteq[\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i-1}}},\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}})
for each
i\geq 1
,

•

(x^{i}_{n})_{n}
and
(y^{i}_{n})_{n}
are convergent, and
f(x^{i}_{n})=y^{i}_{n}
for all
n
and
i
.

Suppose we have constructed the mentioned sequences. Let
A=\{x^{i}_{n}:i,n\in\omega\}
and
B=\{y^{i}_{n}:i,n\in\omega\}
. Then
A\notin\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
and
B\in\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}
. However,
A\subseteq f^{-1}[B]
and since
f
is the Katětov reduction
f^{-1}[B]\in\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
, a contradiction.

To finish the proof of item (2), we need to construct
k_{i}
,
n_{i}
, and
(x^{i}_{n})
,
(y^{i}_{n})
.
We proceed by induction on
i\in\omega
. For
i=0
, note that since
[0,\omega^{2}]\notin\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
and
f
is the witness for the Katětov inequality,
f[[0,\omega^{2}]]\notin\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}
, so there is some
n_{0}
such that
f[[0,\omega^{2}]]\cap\omega^{\alpha_{n_{0}}}\notin\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha_{n_{0}}}
. Hence there is an injective convergent sequence
(y^{0}_{n})_{n}
with values in the set
f[[0,\omega^{2}]]\cap\omega^{n_{0}}
.
We can choose an injective sequence
(x^{0}_{n})_{n}
with values in the interval
[0,\omega^{2}]
such that
f(x^{0}_{n})=y^{0}_{n}
for each
n
.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that the sequence
(x^{0}_{n})_{n}
is convergent and that
(y^{0}_{n})_{n}
remains convergent.

Suppose we have already constructed
k_{i},n_{i},(x^{i}_{n})_{n\in\omega}
and
(y^{i}_{n})_{n\in\omega}
for some
i
that satisfy the conditions above.

Claim
.

There is some
k_{i+1}>\max(n_{i},k_{i})
such that the set

(f[[\omega^{\alpha_{k_{i+1}}},\omega^{\alpha_{k_{i+1}}}+\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}]])^{d}\setminus(\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}+1)

is infinite.

Proof of Claim.

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that the set
(f[[\omega^{\alpha_{k}},\omega^{\alpha_{k}}+\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}]])^{d}\setminus(\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}+1)
is finite for every
k>\max(n_{i},k_{i})
.
For every such
k
, define the set

F_{k}=f[[\omega^{\alpha_{k}},\omega^{\alpha_{k}}+\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}]]\setminus(\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}+1)

and note that since

is finite,
F_{k}\in\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}
, and therefore
f^{-1}[F_{k}]\in\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
. Let
g:\bigcup_{k>\max(n_{i},k_{i})}[\omega^{\alpha_{k}},\omega^{\alpha_{k}}+\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}]\to\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}+1
be a function such that

(1)

g
coincides with
f
on the set

\bigcup_{k>\max(n_{i},k_{i})}[\omega^{\alpha_{k}},\omega^{\alpha_{k}}+\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}]\setminus f^{-1}[F_{k}]

(2)

g\restriction([\omega^{\alpha_{k}},\omega^{\alpha_{k}}+\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}]\cap f^{-1}[F_{k}])

,

(3)

g[[\omega^{\alpha_{k}},\omega^{\alpha_{k}}+\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}]\cap f^{-1}[F_{k}]]\subseteq[\omega\cdot k,\omega\cdot(k+1)).

We will show that

\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha_{n_{i}}}\leq_{K}\mathsf{conv}(\bigcup_{k>\max(n_{i},k_{i})}[\omega^{\alpha_{k}},\omega^{\alpha_{k}}+\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}])

\mathsf{conv}\left(\bigcup_{k>\max(n_{i},k_{i})}[\omega^{\alpha_{k}},\omega^{\alpha_{k}}+\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}]\right)\approx\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha_{n_{i}}+1}\not\geq_{K}\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha_{n_{i}}}.

To see that the function
g
witnesses the Katětov reduction, take any
A\notin\mathsf{conv}(\bigcup_{k>\max(n_{i},k_{i})}[\omega^{\alpha_{k}},\omega^{\alpha_{k}}+\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}])
. Since
A^{d}
is infinite we have two cases.

Case 1.
There is
k>\max(n_{i},k_{i})
such that
A\cap[\omega^{\alpha_{k}},\omega^{\alpha_{k}}+\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}]\notin\mathsf{conv}([\omega^{\alpha_{k}},\omega^{\alpha_{k}}+\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}])
. Then
B=A\cap[\omega^{\alpha_{k}},\omega^{\alpha_{k}}+\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}]\setminus f^{-1}[F_{k}]\notin\mathsf{conv}([\omega^{\alpha_{k}},\omega^{\alpha_{k}}+\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}])
, so
g[B]=f[B]\notin\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}
. However,
g[B]\subseteq[0,\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}]
and therefore
g[B]\notin\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha_{n_{i}}}.

Case 2.
The set
\{l>\max(n_{i},k_{i}):|A\cap[\omega^{\alpha_{l}},\omega^{\alpha_{l}}+\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}]|=\omega\}
is infinite.
Let
\{l_{j}:j\in\omega\}
be an
enumeration of the set above, and let
B_{l}=A\cap[\omega^{\alpha_{l}},\omega^{\alpha_{l}}+\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}]
for every

.
Then

g\left[\bigcup_{j\in\omega}B_{l_{j}}\right]=f\left[\bigcup_{j\in\omega}B_{l_{j}}\setminus f^{-1}[F_{l_{j}}]\right]\cup g\left[\bigcup_{j\in\omega}B_{l_{j}}\cap f^{-1}[F_{l_{j}}]\right].

Since
\bigcup_{j\in\omega}B_{l_{j}}\notin\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
, either
\bigcup_{j\in\omega}B_{l_{j}}\setminus f^{-1}[F_{l_{j}}]\notin\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
or
\bigcup_{j\in\omega}B_{l_{j}}\cap f^{-1}[F_{l_{j}}]\not\in\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
. In the former case, it follows that
f[\bigcup_{j\in\omega}B_{l_{j}}\setminus f^{-1}[F_{l_{j}}]]\notin\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}
. But
f[\bigcup_{j\in\omega}B_{l_{j}}\setminus f^{-1}[F_{l_{j}}]]\subseteq[0,\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}]
and therefore
g[A]\supseteq f[\bigcup_{j\in\omega}B_{l_{j}}\setminus f^{-1}[F_{l_{j}}]]\notin\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha_{n_{i}}}

f^{-1}[F_{l_{j}}]\in\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
for each

, the set
\{j\in\omega:|B_{l_{j}}\cap f^{-1}[F_{l_{j}}]|=\omega\}
is infinite. By the definition of

, we get that
g[\bigcup_{j\in\omega}B_{l_{j}}\cap f^{-1}[F_{l_{j}}]]\notin\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha_{n_{i}}}
.
∎

Let
k_{i+1}
be as stated in the claim. Then there is some
n_{i+1}
and an injective convergent sequence
(y^{i+1}_{n})_{n}
with

\{y^{i+1}_{n}:n\in\omega\}\subseteq f[[\omega^{\alpha_{k_{i+1}}},\omega^{\alpha_{k_{i+1}}}+\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}]\cap(\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}},\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i+1}}}).

Let
(x^{i+1}_{n})_{n}
be a sequence such that

\{x^{i+1}_{n}:n\in\omega\}\subseteq[\omega^{\alpha_{k_{i+1}}},\omega^{\alpha_{k_{i+1}}}+\omega^{\alpha_{n_{i}}}]\quad\text{and}\quad f(x^{i+1}_{n})=y^{i+1}_{n}

∎

Proposition 3.3
.

The ideal
\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}
is
\Pi^{0}_{5}
-complete
for every limit ordinal
\alpha
.

Proof.

To see that the ideal
\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}
is
\Pi^{0}_{5}
, note that

\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}=\bigcap_{\beta\in\alpha}\{A\subseteq\omega^{\alpha}+1:A\cap\omega^{\beta}\in\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}\},

where the set
C_{\beta}=\{A\subseteq\omega^{\alpha}+1:A\cap\omega^{\beta}\in\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}\}
is
\Sigma^{0}_{4}
for each
\beta
, since
\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}\in\Sigma^{0}_{4}
and the set
C_{\beta}
is homeomorphic to the set
\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}\times\mathcal{P}((\omega^{\beta},\omega^{\alpha}])
.

To show completeness, take any
A\in\Pi^{0}_{5}(X)
, where
X
is a zero-dimensional Polish space. We need to find a continuous function
f:X\to\mathcal{P}(\omega^{\alpha}+1)
with
f^{-1}[\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}]=A
. Take a strictly increasing sequence
(\alpha_{k})_{k}
cofinal in
\alpha
with
\alpha_{0}=0
and
\alpha_{1}\geq 2
. Since
A=\bigcap\{A_{n}:n\in\omega\}
for some
A_{n}\in\Sigma^{0}_{4}(X)
and
\mathsf{conv}([\omega^{\alpha_{n}},\omega^{\alpha_{n+1}}))
is
\Sigma^{0}_{4}
-complete (as it is isomorphic to
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha_{n+1}})
, there exists a continuous function
f_{n}:X\to\mathcal{P}([\omega^{\alpha_{n}},\omega^{\alpha_{n+1}}))
with
f_{n}^{-1}[\mathsf{conv}([\omega^{\alpha_{n}},\omega^{\alpha_{n+1}}))]=A_{n}

for each
n
. Define
f:X\to\mathcal{P}(\omega^{\alpha}+1)
by

f(x)=\bigcup_{n}f_{n}(x).

Then
f
is continuous and

\begin{split}f^{-1}[\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}]=&\bigcap_{n\in\omega}\left\{x\in X:f(x)\cap[\omega^{\alpha_{n}},\omega^{\alpha_{n+1}})\in\mathsf{conv}([\omega^{\alpha_{n}},\omega^{\alpha_{n+1}}))\right\}\\
=&\bigcap_{n\in\omega}\left\{x\in X:f_{n}(x)\in\mathsf{conv}([\omega^{\alpha_{n}},\omega^{\alpha_{n+1}}))\right\}=\bigcap_{n\in\omega}A_{n}=A.\qed\end{split}

Having found the greatest lower bound for the families
\{\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}:\beta<\alpha\}

\alpha

.
We have not yet been able to determine it, nor to establish whether it exists at all, and thus the following question remains open.

Question 3.4
.

Does the family

\{\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\omega_{1}\}

have the greatest lower bound in the Katětov order?

By Proposition
2.4

, the ideal
\mathsf{conv}
is a lower bound of the family
\{\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\omega_{1}\}

\mathsf{conv}_{<\alpha}
in this case, however the main obstacle in this approach
is that the family of ideals which must be managed has size
\omega_{1}
, whereas we need an ideal defined on a countable set. A possible solution is suggested by the predecessor of the critical ideals
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
, namely the ideal
\mathsf{conv}
. It is determined by an uncountable set
[0,1]
, but it is defined on its countable dense subset
\mathbb{Q}

[

2.3.15]
) from which it follows that the product of
\omega_{1}
separable topological spaces is separable.

Definition 3.5

Let
D

X=\prod_{2\leq\alpha<\omega_{1}}\omega^{\alpha}+1

\mathsf{conv}_{\omega_{1}}=\{A\subseteq D:\forall\alpha\,\{f(\alpha):f\in A\}\in\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}\}.

Proposition 3.6
.

\mathsf{conv}_{\omega_{1}}\leq\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
for each

Proof.

For each successor ordinal
\xi\in\omega^{\alpha}+1
pick
d_{\xi}\in D
with
d_{\xi}(\alpha)=\xi
. Define
f:\omega^{\alpha}+1\to D

f(\xi)=\begin{cases}d_{\xi+1}&\xi\in\omega^{\alpha},\\
d_{1}&\xi=\omega^{\alpha}.\end{cases}

Then

witnesses
\mathsf{conv}_{\omega_{1}}\leq\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
. To see this take any

and note that
f[A]\supseteq\{d_{\xi+1}:\xi\in A\setminus\{\omega^{\alpha}\}\}

4.
Cardinal characteristics of critical ideals for countable compact spaces

We now turn to the calculation of certain cardinal invariants of the ideals
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
.

Some properties of ideals can be described by cardinal characteristics associated with them. There are four well known cardinal characteristics called additivity, covering, uniformity, and cofinality defined in the following way
for an ideal
\mathcal{I}
on
X
(see e.g.
[
1
]
):

\add(\mathcal{I})=\min\left\{|\mathcal{A}|:\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\land\bigcup\mathcal{A}\notin\mathcal{I}\right\}
,

\cov(\mathcal{I})=\min\left\{|\mathcal{A}|:\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\land\bigcup\mathcal{A}=X\right\}
,

\non(\mathcal{I})=\min\{|A|:A\notin\mathcal{I}\}
,

\cof(\mathcal{I})=\min\{|\mathcal{A}|:\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\land\forall B\in\mathcal{I}\,\exists A\in\mathcal{A}\,(B\subseteq A)\}
.
These characteristics are useful in the case of ideals on an uncountable set
X
(for instance in the case of the
\sigma
-ideal of all meager sets and the
\sigma
-ideal of all Lebesgue null sets).
Apart from the cofinality, the other cardinal
characteristics are trivial, since they are all equal to
\aleph_{0}
.
Fortunately,
Hernández and Hrušák
introduced in
[
11
]
(see also
[
4
]
and
[
12
]
) certain versions of these characteristics more suitable for tall ideals on countable sets:

\begin{split}\adds(\mathcal{I})&=\min\{|\mathcal{A}|:\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\land\neg\exists B\in\mathcal{I}\,\forall A\in\mathcal{A}\,(A\subseteq^{*}B)\},\\
\covs(\mathcal{I})&=\min\{|\mathcal{A}|:\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\land\forall B\in[\omega]^{\aleph_{0}}\,\exists A\in\mathcal{A}\,(|A\cap B|=\aleph_{0})\},\\
\nons(\mathcal{I})&=\min\{|\mathcal{A}|:\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\omega]^{\omega}\land\forall B\in\mathcal{I}\,\exists A\in\mathcal{A}\,(|A\cap B|<\aleph_{0})\},\\
\cofs(\mathcal{I})&=\min\{|\mathcal{A}|:\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\land\forall B\in\mathcal{I}\,\exists A\in\mathcal{A}\,(B\subseteq^{*}A)\}.\end{split}

It is known (see e.g.
[
12
]
) that

\adds(\mathsf{conv})=\adds(\mathsf{Fin}\otimes\mathsf{Fin})=\aleph_{0}
,

\nons(\mathsf{conv})=\nons(\mathsf{Fin}\otimes\mathsf{Fin})=\aleph_{0}
,

\covs(\mathsf{conv})=\cofs(\mathsf{conv})=\mathfrak{c}
,

\covs(\mathsf{Fin}\otimes\mathsf{Fin})=\mathfrak{b}
and
\cofs(\mathsf{Fin}\otimes\mathsf{Fin})=\mathfrak{d}
, where

\mathfrak{b}
is the
bounding number
i.e. the smallest cardinality of any unbounded family in the poset
(\omega^{\omega},\leq^{*})
and

\mathfrak{d}
is the
dominating number
i.e. the smallest cardinality of
any dominating family in the poset
(\omega^{\omega},\leq^{*})
(for more on these cardinals see for instance
[
2
]
).

The following theorem provide the values of the above mentioned characteristics for critical ideals considered in the paper.

Proposition 4.1

(
[
9
]
)
.

For every
\alpha
,

\nons(\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha})=\adds(\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha})=\aleph_{0},\quad\covs(\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha})=\mathfrak{b}\quad\text{and}\quad\cofs(\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha})=\mathfrak{d}.

There are also the following
\omega
-versions of the aforementioned cardinal characteristics, defined and studied in
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
and
[

\begin{split}\addo(\mathcal{I})&=\min\{|\mathcal{A}|:\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\land\neg\exists\mathcal{B}\in[\mathcal{I}]^{\omega}\,\forall A\in\mathcal{A}\ \exists B\in\mathcal{B}\,(A\subseteq^{*}B)\},\\
\nono(\mathcal{I})&=\min\{|\mathcal{A}|:\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\omega]^{\omega}\cap\mathcal{I}\land\forall\mathcal{B}\in[\mathcal{I}]^{\omega}\,\exists A\in\mathcal{A}\,\forall B\in\mathcal{B}\,(|A\cap B|<\aleph_{0})\},\\
\cofo(\mathcal{I})&=\min\{|\mathcal{A}|:\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\mathcal{I}]^{\omega}\land\forall\mathcal{B}\in[\mathcal{I}]^{\omega}\,\exists C\in\mathcal{A}\,\forall B\in\mathcal{B}\,\exists A\in\mathcal{C}(B\subseteq^{*}A)\}\end{split}

with the convention that
\min\emptyset=\mathfrak{c}^{+}
.
Let us point out that whenever
\mathcal{I}
is tall, then

\nono(\mathcal{I})=\min\{|\mathcal{A}|:\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\omega]^{\omega}\land\forall\mathcal{B}\in[\mathcal{I}]^{\omega}\,\exists A\in\mathcal{A}\,\forall B\in\mathcal{B}\,(|A\cap B|<\aleph_{0})\}.

In
[

and

\cofo(\mathsf{conv})=\mathfrak{c}
,

\addo(\mathsf{Fin}\otimes\mathsf{Fin})=\mathfrak{b}
and
\nono(\mathsf{Fin}\otimes\mathsf{Fin})=\cofo(\mathsf{Fin}\otimes\mathsf{Fin})=\mathfrak{d}

Finally, we consider the cardinal invariant

\covp(\mathcal{I})=\min\{|\mathcal{A}|:\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\land\forall B\in\mathcal{I}^{+}\,\exists A\in\mathcal{A}\,(|A\cap B|=\aleph_{0})\},

introduced and studied in

Lemma 4.2
.

\addo(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathsf{Fin})=\mathfrak{b}\quad\text{and}\quad\cofo(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathsf{Fin})=\nono(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathsf{Fin})=\mathfrak{d}.

Proof.

Since the proofs of these equalities are rather similar, we present only the proof of the first. The others are obtained by the same method, with families defined analogously.

(\addo(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathsf{Fin})=\mathfrak{b})
Let
\mathcal{A}

\addo(\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathsf{Fin})
also witnesses
\addo(\mathsf{Fin}\otimes\mathsf{Fin})
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}\approx\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}\oplus\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

Define
f:\omega^{\alpha}\to\omega^{\alpha}\times 2
by

f(\xi)=\begin{cases}(\lambda+k/2,0)&k\text{ even},\\
(\lambda+(k-1)/2,1)&k\text{ odd},\end{cases}

where

\lambda
is
a unique limit ordinal and
k
is a unique natural number such that
\xi=\lambda+k
.
Then
f
is an isomorphism between
\mathsf{conv}(\omega^{\alpha})
and
\mathsf{conv}(\omega^{\alpha})\oplus\mathsf{conv}(\omega^{\alpha})
and since
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
is isomorphic to
\mathsf{conv}(\omega^{\alpha})
the proof is finished.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

\mathsf{conv}(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n)\approx\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
, for every
\alpha
and every
n\in\omega\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

By induction on
n\in\omega\setminus\{0\}
. Suppose the statement holds for
n
. Notice that
\mathsf{conv}(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n+1))\approx\mathsf{conv}(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n)\oplus\mathsf{conv}([\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n+1)))
, as witnessed by
f:\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n+1)\to(\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n+1))\times 2
,

f(\xi)=\begin{cases}(\xi,0)&\xi\in\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n,\\
(\xi,1)&\xi\in[\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n+1)).\end{cases}

Using the induction hypothesis together with the fact that
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}\approx\mathsf{conv}([\omega^{\alpha}\cdot n,\omega^{\alpha}\cdot(n+1)))
, it is enough to show that
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}\approx\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}\oplus\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
, which is true by Lemma
4.3
.
∎

Proposition 4.5
.

For every
\alpha

\addo(\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha})=\mathfrak{b}\quad\text{and}\quad\cofo(\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha})=\nono(\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha})=\mathfrak{d}.

(
\addo(\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha})\leq\mathfrak{b}

Since
\addo(\mathsf{conv}_{2})=\addo(\mathsf{Fin}\otimes\mathsf{Fin})=\mathfrak{b}

\alpha

\addo(\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha})

\mathfrak{d}=\cofs(\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha})\leq\cofo(\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha})

such that for each
i
there is some
k_{i}
such that
A_{i}\cap[0,\lambda_{n})\subseteq^{*}F^{n}_{(\gamma_{n},k_{i})}
. Then the family
\{F^{n}_{(\gamma_{n},k_{i})}\cup K_{(\delta_{0},j_{i})}:i,n\in\omega\}\in\mathcal{G}
is as required. Indeed, take any
A_{i}
and the corresponding
f(i)
. Then
A_{i}\cap[0,\lambda_{f(i)})\subseteq^{*}F^{f(i)}_{(\gamma_{f(i)},k_{i})}
and
A\setminus[0,\lambda_{f(i)})\subseteq^{*}K_{(\delta_{0},j_{i})}
, and therefore
A_{i}\subseteq^{*}F^{f(i)}_{(\gamma_{f(i)},k_{i})}\cup K_{(\delta_{0},j_{i})}
.

(
\nons_{\omega}(\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha})=\mathfrak{d}
)
Since
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}\leq_{K}\mathsf{conv}_{2}\approx\mathsf{Fin}\otimes\mathsf{Fin}
, we obtain
\nono(\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha})\leq\nono(\mathsf{Fin}\otimes\mathsf{Fin})=\mathfrak{d}
for each
\alpha
.
Now we prove that
\nono(\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha})\geq\mathfrak{d}
by induction on
\alpha
. Take any
\alpha
and assume that it holds for
\beta<\alpha
.
In the case that
\alpha=\beta+1
for some
\beta
, define
\lambda_{n}=\omega^{\beta}\cdot n
. In the case that
\alpha
is a limit ordinal, take a strictly increasing sequence
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\omega}
that is cofinal in
\alpha
with
\alpha_{0}=0
and define
\lambda_{n}=\omega^{\alpha_{n}}
.
Take any family
\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\omega^{\alpha}]^{\omega}
such that
|\mathcal{A}|<\mathfrak{d}
. Define the family

\mathcal{A}_{0}=\{A\in\mathcal{A}:\forall n\ (A\cap[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1})<\omega)\}.

Consider the ideal
\mathcal{J}=\{C\subseteq\omega^{\alpha}:\forall n\ (|C\cap[\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n+1}]<\omega)\}.
Note that
\mathcal{J}
is isomorphic to the ideal
\{\emptyset\}\otimes\mathsf{Fin}
and
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
. Then
\mathcal{A}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{J}\cap[\omega^{\alpha}]^{\omega}
, and since
|\mathcal{A}_{0}|<\mathfrak{d}
and
\nono(\mathcal{J})=\mathfrak{d}
, there exists
\mathcal{B}_{0}\in[\mathcal{J}]^{\omega}
such that for every
A\in\mathcal{A}_{0}
there is some
B\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
such that the set
B\cap A
is infinite.

For every
n\geq 1
, consider the family

\mathcal{A}_{n}=\{A\cap\lambda_{n}:A\in\mathcal{A}\text{ and }|A\cap\lambda_{n}|=\omega\}.

Then
\mathcal{A}_{n}\subseteq[\lambda_{n}]^{\omega}
and since
|\mathcal{A}_{n}|<\mathfrak{d}
, by the induction hypothesis and the Lemma
4.4
,
|\mathcal{A}_{n}|<\nono(\mathsf{conv}(\lambda_{n}))
so there exists
\mathcal{B}_{n}\in[\mathsf{conv}(\lambda_{n})]^{\omega}
such that for every
A\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
there is some
B\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
such that the set
A\cap B\cap\lambda_{n}
is infinite.
Let
\mathcal{B}=\bigcup_{n\in\omega}\mathcal{B}_{n}
. Then
\mathcal{B}\in[\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}]^{\omega}
and we claim that it witnesses
|\mathcal{A}|<\nono(\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha})
. Take any
A\in\mathcal{A}
. Since it is infinite, either there is some
n\geq 1
such that the set
A\cap\lambda_{n}
is infinite, then the set
A\cap B
is infinite for some
B\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
, or
A\in\mathcal{A}_{0}
in which case one can find a corresponding
B\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
with
B\cap A
being infinite.
∎

Up to this point, all cardinal invariants of
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
have taken the same values as for
\mathsf{Fin}\otimes\mathsf{Fin}
. We now turn to the first invariant where they differ, namely
\covp(\mathcal{I})
. This difference is unavoidable, since this invariant is closely related to the property
P^{-}(\omega)
, as shown in
[
15
, Theorem 6.5 and Proposition 2.5]
. From this it follows that its value for
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\geq 3
must be uncountable, whereas for
\mathsf{Fin}\otimes\mathsf{Fin}
it is countable, which we will not prove, since we determine the exact value below.

Proposition 4.6
.

For every
\alpha\geq 3
,

\covp(\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha})=\mathfrak{b}.

Proof.

First we show that
\covp(\mathsf{conv}_{3})=\mathfrak{b}.

One inequality follows from the fact that
\covp(\mathsf{conv}_{3})\leq\covs(\mathsf{conv}_{3})=\mathfrak{b}
. To see the reversed inequality, take any family
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathsf{conv}_{3}
such that
|\mathcal{A}|<\mathfrak{b}
. For every
j\in\omega
and
F\in\mathcal{A}
consider the function
f_{F,j}:\omega\to\omega
defined by

f_{F,j}(i)=\max\{k\in\omega:\omega^{2}\cdot j+\omega\cdot i+k\in F\}

with the convention that maximum of an infinite and empty set is
0
.
As
|\{f_{F,j}:F\in\mathcal{A},j\in\omega\}|\leq|\mathcal{A}|\cdot\omega<\mathfrak{b}
, there is some function
f:\omega\to\omega
such that
f_{F,j}\leq^{*}f
for each
F\in\mathcal{A}
and
j\in\omega
. Define

A=\{\omega^{2}\cdot j+\omega\cdot i+(f(i)+1):i,j\in\omega\}.

Since
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathsf{conv}_{3}
, for every
F\in\mathcal{A}
and every
j\in\omega
there is some
i(F,j)\in\omega
such that the set
F\cap[\omega^{2}\cdot j+\omega\cdot i,\omega^{2}\cdot j+\omega\cdot(j+1)]
is finite for each
i\geq i(F,j)
. Therefore the set
A\cap F\cap[\omega^{2}\cdot j,\omega^{2}\cdot(j+1)]
is finite for every
F\in\mathcal{A}
and
j\in\omega
.
For every
F\in\mathcal{A}
define
g_{F}:\omega\to\omega
by

g_{F}(j)=\min\{k:\forall i\geq k\ (A\cap F\cap[\omega^{2}\cdot j+\omega\cdot i,\omega^{2}\cdot j+\omega\cdot(i+1))=\emptyset)\}.

Again,
|\{g_{F}:F\in\mathcal{A}\}|\leq|\mathcal{A}|<\mathfrak{b}
, so there is some function
g:\omega\to\omega
such that
g_{F}\leq^{*}g
for each
F\in\mathcal{A}
. Define

B=A\setminus\bigcup_{j\in\omega}[\omega^{2}\cdot j,\omega^{2}\cdot j+\omega\cdot g(j)).

Then
B\not\in\mathsf{conv}_{3}
and we claim that
B
witnesses
|\mathcal{A}|<\covp(\mathsf{conv}_{3})
. To see this take any
F\in\mathcal{A}
and notice that since there is some
j_{F}
such that
g_{F}(j)\leq g(j)
for every
j\geq j_{F}
, we obtain that
B\cap F\cap[\omega^{2}\cdot j+\omega\cdot g(j),\omega^{2}\cdot(j+1)]=\emptyset
for all
j\geq j_{F}
. And since for every
j<j_{F}
, we know that
B\cap F\cap[\omega^{2}\cdot j,\omega^{2}\cdot(j+1))
is finite, the set
B\cap F
is finite as well.

We now address the general case. Assume
\alpha\geq 3
. Using the properties of these cardinal invariants, we know that
\covp(\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha})\leq\covs(\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha})=\mathfrak{b}
and
\covp(\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha})\geq\covp(\mathsf{conv}_{3})=\mathfrak{b}
since
\mathsf{conv}_{\alpha}\leq_{K}\mathsf{conv}_{3}
.
∎
