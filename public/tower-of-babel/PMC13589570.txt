DeepMASS v.2: An enhanced deep learning platform for large-scale discovery and structural annotation of unknown plant metabolites
Authors: Siyu Jiang, Qiong Yang, Ziyao Xiong, Kairong Li, Qinliang Dai, Meifeng Su, Yaqing Lyu, Yanchun Peng, Ran Du, Jianbin Yan, Hongchao Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDetermining the structures of unknown metabolites remains a fundamental bottleneck in plant metabolomics, as the vast chemical diversity of plant secondary metabolites far exceeds the coverage of existing spectral libraries. Here, we present DeepMASS v.2, a substantially enhanced platform for annotating unknown metabolites from liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry data, designed to address this challenge at scale. DeepMASS v.2 leverages a semantic spectral representation model trained on millions of spectra from GNPS, NIST, and in-house resources. By integrating Spec2Vec-based embeddings with HNSW (hierarchical navigable small world) graph retrieval and a unified chemical space defined by molecular fingerprints, DeepMASS v.2 identifies structurally related neighbors of unknown spectra and ranks candidate structures according to their proximity to the predicted structural neighborhoods within chemical space. Benchmarking against Critical Assessment of Small Molecule Identification datasets and a curated natural product collection demonstrated that DeepMASS v.2 outperforms state-of-the-art in silico annotation tools, including SIRIUS, CFM-ID, MetFrag, and MS-Finder. Importantly, DeepMASS v.2 maintains strong performance for metabolites absent from spectral libraries, highlighting its capacity to annotate genuinely unknown compounds. Application of DeepMASS v.2 to large-scale plant metabolomics datasets demonstrated its ability to expand accessible metabolome coverage. Implemented as an intuitive web platform, DeepMASS v.2 provides the community with a scalable, interpretable, and high-throughput solution for structural annotation, enabling more comprehensive characterization of plant chemical diversity and accelerating natural product discovery in molecular plant science. The DeepMASS v.2 web server is publicly available at http://deepmass.cn.Key words: LC–MS/MS, metabolomics, metabolite annotation, deep learning, plant secondary metabolites, natural products

Introduction

Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) has become an indispensable analytical technique for profiling the diverse small molecules present in complex biological systems. In particular, it plays a central role in metabolomics and phytochemistry, supporting discoveries across life sciences (Chen et al., 2023), biosynthesis (Shen et al., 2023), foodomics (Herrero et al., 2012), exposome research (Maitre et al., 2022), etc. LC–MS/MS-based metabolomics is particularly powerful for decoding the extraordinary chemical diversity of plant secondary metabolites, which underpin plant adaptations to environmental stresses, ecological interactions, and synthesis of bioactive natural products with agricultural and pharmaceutical relevance. Despite the power of LC–MS/MS, the structural annotation of unknown metabolites remains a major bottleneck in plant metabolomics. The most commonly used strategy for structural identification is the comparison of MS/MS spectra from the detected compounds against standard spectral libraries such as GNPS, MassBank, and NIST. This strategy is limited in two ways: first, different instrument types, conditions, and parameter settings can cause the same compound to have different MS/MS spectra, reducing the universal applicability of standard spectral libraries and thus hampering the identification of known molecules (Bazsó et al., 2016). Second, the coverage of existing libraries is exceedingly small compared with the chemical diversity of plant metabolites, making it nearly impossible to identify novel compounds through library matching. As a result, more than 90% of detected features in untargeted plant metabolomics data remain unannotated, forming “chemical dark matter” that conceals much of the biological and ecological significance of the plant metabolome (Da Silva et al., 2015).

Improvements in library-searching algorithms, such as MS-Entropy and MS2Query, have enhanced the robustness of spectral matching, partially overcoming the first challenge and improving the efficacy and efficiency of molecular annotation (Li et al., 2021; De Jonge et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023). Molecular networking, on the other hand, can reveal the chemical neighbors of unknown molecules and estimate their classes, but it cannot automatically predict their specific structures (van der Hooft et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2016; Morehouse et al., 2023). The expansion of standard spectral databases is also unsustainable, as many plant-specific metabolites are difficult to isolate or synthesize as pure standards.

The use of computational methods to predict chemical structures from spectra that are absent from spectral databases is therefore the most promising approach for improving annotation coverage (Cai et al., 2023; Hu and Qiu, 2023). Current strategies can be broadly divided into in silico annotation algorithms (Dührkop et al., 2015, 2019; Tsugawa et al., 2016; Lai et al., 2018; Ji et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021) and known-to-unknown extension through pathways (Wang et al., 2017; Shen et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2022). Of these two approaches, in silico annotation algorithms are the most general and flexible, as they can be applied to spectra from any source and at any scale. By contrast, pathway-based propagation methods depend on prior knowledge, i.e., annotations of precursor metabolites obtained through spectral matching. Therefore, these methods can only extend known regions of the metabolome.

In silico annotation typically follows a two-step workflow. First, candidates are identified from a structural database (Kim et al., 2015) on the basis of a chemical formula inferred from the precursor masses, ionization types, and isotopic pattern or through specialized toolkits such as BUDDY (Xing et al., 2023) or MIST-CF (Goldman et al., 2023). Next, potential candidates are assessed and prioritized on the basis of specific rules. For example, SIRIUS scores candidates by comparing the predicted fingerprints of an unknown compound with the calculated fingerprints of potential candidates (Dührkop et al., 2019). MS-Finder scores candidates by considering the frequency of hydrogen rearrangement inferred from the fragment ions (Tsugawa et al., 2016), and CFM-ID evaluates candidates on the basis of the accessibility of the inferred substructural motif from the fragment ions (Wang et al., 2021).

Recent advances in deep learning have shown that structural similarities between molecules are inherently reflected in their MS/MS spectra. Neural networks can capture these relationships directly from large spectral datasets, enabling data-driven inference of chemical relatedness beyond what is available in existing databases (Ji et al., 2019; Huber et al., 2021a, 2021b; Guo et al., 2023). Building on this principle, we initially developed DeepMASS v.1 (Ji et al., 2019), which uses deep learning to identify compounds by introducing a structural-similarity scoring model that links MS/MS spectra to molecular structures (Hong et al., 2025). This approach demonstrated that sets of predicted structurally related compounds could provide valuable guidance for inferring the structures of unknown metabolites. By evaluating spectral similarity in a data-driven manner, DeepMASS v.1 established a framework for systematically leveraging the structural features encoded in MS/MS spectra to suggest plausible candidates for previously uncharacterized compounds. However, the original framework relied on pairwise combinations and fully connected architectures, which severely constrained its scalability to hundreds of spectra and limited its coverage of chemical space.

Here, we present DeepMASS v.2, a comprehensive framework for large-scale structural annotation of LC–MS/MS data. By capturing relationships between spectra, DeepMASS v.2 facilitates the identification of shared substructures, structural motifs, and putative scaffolds across complex datasets, supporting the annotation of plant secondary metabolites. DeepMASS v.2 is implemented as a web-based platform with an intuitive graphical interface, enabling users to interactively explore spectral neighborhoods, prioritize candidate structures, and integrate annotation results into LC–MS/MS-based workflows. We benchmarked DeepMASS v.2 against state-of-the-art annotation tools and demonstrated its superior performance on reference datasets. We then used it to annotate secondary metabolites across a large number of plant species, highlighting its ability to expand our understanding of plant metabolomes and accelerate natural product discovery.

Results

DeepMASS v.2 within the plant metabolomics workflow

Within the LC–MS/MS data-processing workflow, DeepMASS v.2 is used after the preprocessing of raw files. Molecular features and their corresponding mass spectra are first exported from data preprocessing software such as XCMS (Forsberg et al., 2018), MZMine (Schmid et al., 2023), OpenMS (Pfeuffer et al., 2024), KIPC2 (Ji et al., 2017), Met4DX (Luo et al., 2023), and MS-DIAL (Tsugawa et al., 2015). DeepMASS v.2 then focuses on structural annotation of the detected features, including those that lack matches in existing spectral libraries.

DeepMASS v.2 begins by accepting a spectrum of features and then retrieves structural candidates for the unknown compounds from a comprehensive structural database (Supplemental Table 1) assembled from various biological sources (Supplemental Table 2). If a chemical formula has already been assigned, the retrieval process is based on this formula. Otherwise, DeepMASS v.2 retrieves candidates on the basis of the monoisotopic mass derived from precursor masses and ionization types. All candidates with a monoisotopic mass difference below a specific threshold (10 ppm as default) are considered. The core task then becomes ranking the candidates.

DeepMASS v.2 evaluates candidates on the basis of their relationships to structurally similar compounds, predicted by the Spec2Vec algorithm. By identifying neighboring spectra from reference collections, the platform places unknown features within a local structural context. The correct candidate is expected to be located close to multiple predicted neighbors. A similarity scoring function is then used to rank all candidates, enabling confident annotation of the unknown compound and expanding the scope of structural identification beyond traditional library-based methods (Figure 1).

Workflow of DeepMASS v.2.

Raw mass spectrometry data are preprocessed to generate feature tables and corresponding MS1/MS2 spectra. MS1 data are used to estimate molecular formulas and retrieve candidate structures from chemical databases, and MS2 spectra are embedded using the semantic model and compared with a reference library to find structurally related neighbors. Both candidates and reference structures are projected into a unified chemical space based on molecular fingerprints. The structure of the unknown compound is inferred by assessing its spatial position relative to known neighbors.

Web server, standalone GUI, and usage

DeepMASS v.2 provides a web server and a standalone graphical user interface (GUI) to enable end-to-end processing from data upload to structural annotation and results visualization in LC–MS/MS-based metabolomics. The model used in the web server is trained with publicly available datasets (public version). The standalone GUI is accompanied by a user guide to help researchers use the model with these full datasets or extend it with additional spectral libraries of their own.

Users can directly upload MS/MS datasets in common formats (e.g., MGF and MSP) through a drag-and-drop interface. The system automatically parses spectral metadata, including ionization mode and precursor information, to guide downstream annotation workflows. When molecular formula information is available, candidate retrieval is performed accordingly; otherwise, annotation is initiated on the basis of precursor mass. This design ensures robustness across diverse experimental conditions commonly encountered in plant metabolomics.

The results are organized into an interactive visualization interface that consists of three coordinated panels. The spectrum panel displays the MS/MS spectrum of the query feature together with structurally related reference spectra, enabling users to inspect the annotation evidence. The structure panel highlights substructures shared among the candidate molecules, facilitating the interpretation of putative structural relationships. The information panel summarizes the metadata associated with each feature (Figure 2). In comparisons of runtime per sample, DeepMASS v.2 showed good execution efficiency relative to other GUI-based tools (see supplemental information).

Performance evaluation on benchmarking datasets

To evaluate the performance of DeepMASS v.2, we performed benchmarking on three datasets: the merged Critical Assessment of Small Molecule Identification (CASMI) dataset, an in-house natural product dataset (NP dataset), and a specialized daphnane dataset. The CASMI dataset comprised 499 spectra corresponding to 478 unique compounds from the CASMI challenges in 2014, 2016, and 2022 (Supplemental Table 3), with 21 compounds measured on different instruments and under different acquisition conditions. The NP dataset comprised 250 natural products analyzed using a Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer in positive ion mode (Supplemental Table 4). For 156 of these, spectra were successfully extracted. The daphnane dataset included 58 daphnane diterpenoids isolated in a previous phytochemical investigation and annotated using a modular fragmentation-based structure assembly (MFSA) strategy (Zhang et al., 2025). We compared DeepMASS v.2 with four state-of-the-art in silico annotation tools: SIRIUS, CFM-ID, MetFrag, and MS-Finder. All tools were used with the same comprehensive database of biochemical compounds, as described in the methods section.

For the CASMI dataset, DeepMASS v.2 achieved a top-1 accuracy of 57.7%, outperforming SIRIUS (44.7%), CFM-ID (43.3%), MetFrag (43.1%), and MS-Finder (36.1%); the public version of DeepMASS v.2 achieved an accuracy of 52.1%. When the top 10 annotations were evaluated, DeepMASS v.2 reached 79.6% accuracy, higher than all the other methods (DeepMASS v.2 public: 77.8%, SIRIUS: 65.8%, CFM-ID: 70.5%, MetFrag: 72.9%, and MS-Finder: 68.1%). Specifically, for the 217 spectra in the benchmarking dataset that were associated with compounds present in the training sets, DeepMASS v.2 achieved 88.9% top-1 accuracy, whereas SIRIUS, CFM-ID, MetFrag, and MS-Finder achieved 71.9%, 58.1%, 57.1%, and 53.1%, respectively. This higher accuracy reflects the fact that these compounds are easily identifiable. Library matching methods failed to provide correct annotations for compounds that were absent from the reference dataset, whereas DeepMASS v.2 showed 33.7% top-1 accuracy, outperforming SIRIUS (28.7%), CFM-ID (31.9%), MetFrag (32.3%), and MS-Finder (24.8%) (Figure 3A).

For the NP dataset, DeepMASS v.2 achieved the highest overall accuracy, with top-1 and top-10 scores of 46.8% and 76.3%, respectively, whereas the public version performed similarly to SIRIUS (top 1: 35.9% and 38.4%; top 10: 73.7% and 73.0%). Other tools performed less well (top 1, CFM-ID: 17.3%, MetFrag: 12.8%, and MS-Finder: 7.8%; top 10, CFM-ID: 59.6%, MetFrag: 57.7%, and MS-Finder: 44.2%), reflecting the challenge of predicting fragmentation in structurally complex natural products. Among the 127 spectra with available spectral matches, DeepMASS v.2 achieved top-1 and top-10 accuracies of 53.5% and 81.8%, outperforming its competitors. For the remaining 29 spectra, for which corresponding compounds were absent from the spectral library, DeepMASS v.2 showed superior performance (top 1/top 10: 17.2%/51.7%) compared with the other tools (Figure 3B). We introduced a composite confidence-assessment strategy that integrated both ranking scores and structural consistency among the top candidates, thus providing a basis for evaluating the credibility of the annotations (see supplemental information).

We analyzed annotation outcomes to better characterize the error profiles of the different methods. Specifically, we evaluated whether incorrectly annotated results still preserved the correct molecular scaffold or category (Figure 3C). Among the tested methods, DeepMASS v.2 achieved 25.3% correct Murcko scaffolds and 60.1% correct superclasses, a relatively balanced and satisfactory result. Finally, we compared the runtime per sample and found that DeepMASS v.2 again displayed good execution efficiency compared with other GUI-based tools.

For the daphnane dataset, we used the same experimental settings and candidate pool as reported in the original MFSA study. We also included comparisons with dedicated natural-product annotation tools such as MFSA and ICEBERG. With the exception of MFSA, DeepMASS v.2 outperformed all other methods. However, it is important to note that MFSA requires class-specific model training tailored to a particular family of compounds. By contrast, DeepMASS v.2 provides a generalizable framework that can be directly applied across diverse metabolite classes, making it a better choice for metabolomics analysis of heterogeneous plant compounds (Figure 3D). Additional benchmarking results on the MassSpecGym dataset and comparisons with emerging machine-learning models are provided in the supplemental information (Bushuiev et al., 2025; Kalia et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025; Zhou Chen and Hassoun, 2026).

Interpretability and confidence assessment

The interpretability of DeepMASS v.2 results was illustrated using two example spectra. Dimensionality reduction of Morgan fingerprints using uniform manifold approximation and projection enabled the visualization of candidate and neighboring compounds in chemical space. Top neighboring structures are often located near the correct annotation, thus guiding DeepMASS v.2 decisions. For instance, in the positive-mode spectrum of 6-bromoquinolin-2(1H)-one, neighbors such as 6-chloroquinolin-2-ol share similar carbon skeletons and fragment patterns. In the negative-mode spectrum of N-acetyl-5-aminosalicylic acid, top neighbors include identical structures and additional references such as acetaminophen, highlighting substructure similarity and confirming the chemical-space position. These examples demonstrate that the decisions of DeepMASS v.2 are both interpretable and consistent with theoretical expectations (Figure 4A). We next analyzed annotation outcomes to better characterize the error profiles of the different methods. Specifically, in addition to exact structure matches, we evaluated whether incorrectly annotated results still preserved the correct molecular scaffold or category. To facilitate practical interpretation of the ranked outputs, we introduced a composite confidence assessment that integrates both the model ranking score and the structural consistency among top-ranked candidates. This information can be used as a reference to help users assess the reliability of the annotation results. The structure score given by DeepMASS v.2 is the direct reference, and higher scores indicate higher confidence levels. In addition, we suggest that the fingerprint-based similarity between leading candidates be taken into consideration, as we observed that high-confidence annotations are typically associated with strong structural agreement within the top-ranked set (Figure 4B). For practical use, we define high-confidence annotations as those with a structural score > 3.0 and a log10 nearest L2 distance < 5. To provide a more in-depth view of the factors that can influence annotation accuracy, we propose and test several hypotheses in the supplemental information.

Case study 1: Discovery of biomarkers for tomato ripening

To explore the practical utility of DeepMASS v.2, we performed a metabolomic analysis of the cherry tomato cultivar Qianxi during the ripening process (Figure 5A). Data preprocessing and MS/MS extraction were performed using MS-DIAL (Tsugawa et al., 2015). In total, 21,601 features were obtained by MS/MS in positive ion mode and 18 846 features in negative ion mode. DeepMASS v.2 annotated 20 140 of the features from positive-mode data and 17,776 from negative-mode data, accounting for 93.2% and 94.3% of the features, respectively (Figure 5B). In comparison, TraceFinder, a commercial spectrum-matching software used in the other tested pipeline, annotated 21.7% of the features from positive-mode data and 14.0% of those from negative-mode data by library matching.

We performed a statistical analysis to compare differences in the metabolomes from different ripening stages using the DeepMASS v.2 and TraceFinder annotations. Principal-component analysis was used to assess metabolite differences among the ripening stages. As shown in the score plots (Figure 5C), fruit samples from different ripening stages could be clearly distinguished using the DeepMASS v.2 results but were less clearly distinguished using those from TraceFinder. In addition, continuous shifts through the principal-component space were observed over the course of fruit ripening. These results demonstrate that the additional metabolites annotated by DeepMASS v.2 were valuable for characterizing the metabolic changes that occurred during the ripening of Qianxi cherry tomatoes.

We next performed partial least squares discriminant analysis to identify biomarkers within the DeepMASS v.2 annotations, focusing on those with variable importance scores greater than 2 (Supplemental Table 5). Many of the identified metabolites have previously been linked to traits such as resistance and fruit quality (Tieman et al., 2017; Klee and Tieman, 2018; Zhu et al., 2018). We then examined trends in the levels of these metabolites across ripening stages. Notably, fruit ripening was accompanied by a gradual increase in the abundance of metabolites related to flavor and nutrients, including glucose, norfuraneol, glutamine, citric acid, and aspartic acid (Figure 5D). These trends are consistent with known changes in flavor- and nutrient-related metabolites during ripening. We observed a decline in metabolites that are related to resistance but are undesirable for consumers, such as solasodine, tomatidine, cryptochlorogenic acid-like compounds, and hydroxycoumarin-like compounds, especially during the later ripening stages. This pattern is consistent with observed trends in the cherry tomato cultivar. To confirm these findings, we compared the detected MS/MS spectra with those of standard substances. In addition, we obtained the extracted ion chromatograms (XICs) of these metabolites and confirmed the trends in their chromatographic peaks. Two biomarkers (solasodine and tomatidine) were confirmed on the basis of their retention times, whereas DeepMASS v.2 cannot distinguish between positional isomers of cryptochlorogenic acid-like and hydroxycoumarin-like compounds, preventing confirmation based on retention time (Figure 5E). Overall, these findings underscore the utility of DeepMASS v.2 for obtaining comprehensive and accurate metabolomic data. Its enhanced capabilities significantly improve the potential for discovering metabolites of interest. Although several representative biomarkers were confirmed using authentic standards, comprehensive orthogonal validation for all annotated features was not feasible. The limited availability of standards for many plant specialized metabolites is a common constraint in large-scale plant metabolomics.

Case study 2: Anti-tumor metabolite profiling across 580 plant species

To evaluate the performance of DeepMASS v.2 for large-scale natural product discovery, we performed systematic profiling of anti-tumor metabolites across an extensive dataset that contained 1940 untargeted metabolomics samples from approximately 580 plant species. Data for 450 of these species were collected from publicly available repositories (Allard et al., 2023; Hart et al., 2025), whereas data for the remaining 130 species were generated from our own experimental work. All samples were analyzed using a high-resolution LC–MS platform. All MS features were subjected to compound-level annotation using DeepMASS v.2, which produced ranked candidate structures as output. From all annotation results, we curated a target list of known anti-tumor compounds reported in the literature as having experimentally validated or putative anticancer activity. These included members of representative classes such as terpenoids, flavonoids, peptides/amino acid derivatives, lipids, etc. For each compound, we screened all samples to determine its presence and frequency of occurrence. To confirm the annotations, we used authentic spectral matching with reference spectra.

From the DeepMASS v.2 annotation results, we identified 306 anti-tumor natural products in the plant samples. Using authentic reference spectra, we applied a spectral entropy score threshold of 0.7 (Li et al., 2021; Li and Fiehn, 2023) to ensure high-confidence identification. It is important to note that the sampling density of plant specimens was uneven across taxonomic families, and Figure 6A therefore reflects primarily the breadth rather than the relative abundance of anti-tumor natural products across lineages. To illustrate the chemical diversity of these compounds, we generated uniform manifold approximation and projection embeddings based on their computed molecular fingerprints (Figure 6B). The resulting map reveals a highly dispersed distribution without tight clustering of major classes, demonstrating that anti-tumor natural products occupy a broad and heterogeneous chemical space. Figure 6C compares the ranking assigned by DeepMASS v.2 to each predicted structure with the spectral entropy score obtained through spectral matching against reference MS/MS data. A clear monotonic trend is observed: compounds ranked higher tend to exhibit substantially higher spectral similarity to authentic standards. This relationship indicates that the probabilistic ranking is well calibrated with real fragmentation evidence. In other words, when the model assigns a candidate to the top of the ranked list, this ranking not only reflects statistical confidence but is also strongly supported by experimentally measured fragmentation agreement. The same pattern is reflected at the aggregate level in Figure 6D, which depicts the number of metabolites with a spectral entropy score above 0.7 at each ranking position. The steep decline in confirmed identifications as the ranking position increases demonstrates that the top-ranked predictions from DeepMASS v.2 are enriched for correct structures. This behavior is consistent with a well-performing structure prediction model: high-ranking candidates correspond to accurate identifications, whereas lower-ranking predictions have greater uncertainty. Figure 6E further illustrates the relationship between plant taxonomy and the chemical classes of anti-tumor natural products. Although our dataset does not represent a phylogenetically balanced sampling, this plot nonetheless reveals clear chemical trends. Figure 6F illustrates two representative examples of anti-tumor natural products, i.e., butylidenephthalide and alpinetin, which were confidently identified across multiple plant species using DeepMASS v.2. For both compounds, DeepMASS v.2 assigned top-ranking structural predictions that were subsequently confirmed through direct comparison with authentic MS/MS spectra. Their experimental fragmentation patterns showed striking agreement with those of the corresponding standards, exhibiting a high spectral entropy score and largely overlapping fragment ion series. All MS/MS spectral comparison plots between experimental spectra and reference standards are provided in the supplemental information.

Discussion

In this study, we introduce DeepMASS v.2 as a major iteration of our previous work (Ji et al., 2019), which used an unsupervised semantic similarity model of the mass spectral language, eliminating the need for manually curated metabolite pairs. Building on this progress, we increased the number of spectra by three orders of magnitude. With its capacity to handle large, diverse datasets, DeepMASS v.2 offers a wider range of potential applications across fields, especially high-throughput plant metabolomics.

DeepMASS v.2 is both a competitor and a complement to SIRIUS, CFM-ID, and MS-Finder for MS/MS-based compound identification, as all of these tools share the common goal of structurally annotating compounds, irrespective of the availability of corresponding spectra. Unlike CFM-ID and MS-Finder (which are based on fragmentation trees) or SIRIUS (based on fingerprint prediction), DeepMASS v.2 does not explicitly model fragmentation rules or predict predefined molecular descriptors from spectra. Instead, it learns a shared representation space in which MS/MS spectra and chemical structures are jointly embedded. This unified space captures implicit chemical context derived from large-scale spectrum–structure associations. Candidate rankings are therefore assigned on the basis of similarity within this learned embedding space rather than by reconstructing fragmentation pathways or matching predicted fingerprints. This strategic integration addresses the limitations in coverage observed in standard spectral databases, ensuring more comprehensive compound identification. This goal distinguishes DeepMASS v.2 from enhanced spectrum-matching software such as MatchMS or MS-Entropy, as well as from spectral embedding algorithms for numerical representation and spectral network establishment, such as MS2DeepScore or Spec2Vec.

When applied to the CASMI and in-house benchmark datasets, DeepMASS v.2 achieved higher annotation accuracy than the other tested software. Because some test spectra had corresponding reference spectra in the spectral database, we evaluated performance separately for spectra in the database and those that were absent. As expected, both DeepMASS v.2 and the other tested software exhibited significantly better performance when using spectra already present in the database. From an application perspective, DeepMASS v.2 provides a foundation for large-scale comparative metabolomics across plant lineages. Its ability to annotate unknowns at scale makes DeepMASS v.2 a powerful engine for natural product discovery, ecological metabolomics, and systems-level research on plant specialized metabolism.

Looking ahead, DeepMASS v.2 shows promise for the identification of undiscovered compounds, provided that their possible structures are predicted and added to the database. The limitation of the current version is its strong dependence on both the quantity and quality of spectra within the reference database. This is evident from the difference in performance observed when NIST spectra are included as reference data versus when they are not. This reliance is crucial for identifying plausible and accurate neighboring compounds that help to pinpoint the chemical space of the unknown metabolite. The expansion of spectral data enhances the precision of the connection between structural similarity and semantic similarity within the mass spectral language. As with that of other MS/MS-based annotation tools, the performance of DeepMASS v.2 depends on the quality of the input spectra obtained from the upstream software. In large-scale plant metabolomics, co-elution and low signal-to-noise ratios may generate chimeric or suboptimal spectra. DeepMASS v.2 incorporates basic noise filtering and intensity normalization but does not perform chromatographic deconvolution, which is expected to be handled during upstream preprocessing.

Overall, DeepMASS v.2 demonstrates strong annotation performance in both benchmark evaluations and real-world data analysis. It offers meaningful and valuable metabolite annotations, features user-friendly and extensible software, and shows the potential for even better performance as more reliable data are incorporated in the future.

Methods

Computational implementation of DeepMASS v.2

DeepMASS v.2 performs metabolite annotation by placing MS/MS spectra and candidate molecules into a shared, learned chemical space and evaluating their relative proximity. The core principle is that structurally similar compounds generate similar spectra and therefore cluster within localized regions of this space. DeepMASS v.2 infers the most probable chemical neighborhood for an unknown spectrum by identifying structurally related reference spectra, which collectively define the local structural context for candidate evaluation.

To predict the most likely region of this space for an unknown compound, the system identifies structurally related neighbors from a spectral reference library. Since the release of DeepMASS v.1, a number of unsupervised methods have emerged, including Spec2Vec (Huber et al., 2021a), DreaMS (Bushuiev et al., 2024), CMSSP (Chen et al., 2024), and MSBERT (Zhang et al., 2024). We compared the performance of DeepMASS v.2 based on each of these methods, and Spec2Vec stood out for its accuracy and ability to handle millions of spectra (see supplemental information). DeepMASS v.2 therefore uses the Spec2Vec package, which is based on a semantic similarity model inspired by natural language processing. The model was trained on GNPS, NIST, and our in-house spectral library, and the spectra in the reference library were converted into vectors for rapid searching during the next step. In this study, the chemical space constructed from molecular fingerprints represents the similarity between compounds in terms of their shared structural features and substructures rather than their physicochemical properties or biological functions.

Efficient similarity searching in the embedded space is achieved using a hierarchical navigable small world (HNSW) graph (Malkov and Yashunin, 2020), which enables rapid retrieval of nearest neighbors for each query spectrum. Candidate structures are retrieved from a comprehensive molecular database based on precursor mass or molecular formula. To refine the candidate ranking, DeepMASS v.2 evaluates structural consistency between each candidate and its predicted spectral neighbors. Molecular structures are encoded using Morgan fingerprints, which capture fragment-level structural features. The similarity between candidates and their neighboring compounds is quantified using Dice similarity, reflecting their proximity in chemical space.

Finally, candidates are ranked using a scoring function that integrates spectral similarity and structural agreement with neighboring compounds. Correct annotations are expected to show consistent proximity to multiple neighbors in the learned space, enabling DeepMASS v.2 to prioritize structurally coherent candidates. This framework enables robust annotation of unknown metabolites and extends structural inference beyond direct spectral matching.

Construction of a comprehensive molecular database

To provide a comprehensive database for retrieval of structural candidates across different research areas, DeepMASS v.2 collects, organizes, and integrates chemical structures from various databases (Supplemental Table 1). This extensive database is derived from 18 different sources: ChEBI (Hastings et al., 2016), BloodExp (Barupal and Fiehn, 2019), DrugBank (Knox et al., 2023), ECMDB (Sajed et al., 2016), FooDB (Naveja et al., 2018), HMDB (Wishart et al., 2022), KEGG (Kanehisa et al., 2017), NANPDB (Ntie-Kang et al., 2017), NPAtlas (van Santen et al., 2019), PMHub (Tian et al., 2023), PMN (Hawkins et al., 2021), SMPDB (Frolkis et al., 2010), STOFF (Williams et al., 2017), T3DB (Wishart et al., 2015), TCMSP (Ru et al., 2014), YMDB (Ramirez-Gaona et al., 2017), GNPS (Nothias et al., 2020), and NIST (Simón-Manso et al., 2013) (Supplemental Table 2). If a compound appears in multiple databases, the duplicate entries are identified and merged using InChIKeys, and all corresponding database IDs are retained. As a result, 559,589 unique compounds are included. Each entry includes the compound name, SMILES, InChIKey, chemical formula, exact molecular mass, database ID, and molecular class. Most of the information is inherited from the source database, and the rest is calculated using the RDKit package. The molecular classifications are predicted with NPClassifier (Kim et al., 2021). These sources cover a wide range of possible chemical structures derived from humans, plants, microbes, foods, herbs, drugs, toxicants, and the environment, ensuring broad applicability across various fields of chemobiological research.

Semantic model training

Three datasets were used to train the word2vec-based semantic model. First, the open-source GNPS dataset, acquired on June 1, 2022, comprised 495,810 MS/MS spectra. Second, commercial NIST 20 software contributed 1,026,506 MS/MS spectra. Finally, our in-house dataset added an additional 23,610 MS/MS spectra. A publicly available version of DeepMASS v.2, excluding the NIST 20 training set, is provided owing to commercial licensing constraints. To evaluate the effect of training data on model performance, we also compared DeepMASS v.2 trained on different open-source spectral libraries, such as MSnLib; these analyses are detailed in the supplemental information (Brungs et al., 2025).

The spectra were first cleaned and corrected using the MatchMS package (Huber et al., 2020): 1) structural information (SMILES, InChI, and InChIKey) was extracted from the metadata, and entries for which this information could not be derived were discarded; 2) information related to precursor type (adduct and charge) was extracted from the metadata, retaining only the spectra with adduct types of M+H, M+Na, M+K, and M+NH4 for positive mode and M-H and M+Cl for negative mode; 3) spectra with fewer than five fragment ions were excluded; 4) peak intensities were normalized to a range of 0–1; and 5) to avoid any overlap in data, all spectra with a cosine similarity greater than 0.95 compared to any spectrum in the CASMI datasets were eliminated. Finally, 600,289 positive spectra derived from 43,653 unique compounds and 182,323 negative spectra from 20,883 unique compounds were retained.

After processing, the spectra were transformed into documents. In this step, each peak was represented by a word containing its position up to a specified decimal precision (“peak@xxx.xx”). In all presented outcomes, a binning approach involving two decimal places was used, meaning that a peak at 200.445 m/z was represented as “peak@200.45.” Together with all peaks in a spectrum, neutral losses ranging from 5.0 to 200.0 Da were also included as “loss@xxx.xx.” The calculation of neutral losses involved the precursor m/z subtracted from the peak m/z. The compilation of all the generated peak and loss words constitutes what is referred to here as a document. Two semantic models were trained separately using the Spec2Vec package (Huber et al., 2021a) for spectra in positive and negative modes. The hyperparameters were configured as follows: 1) word vector dimension = 300, 2) negative sampling (negative = 5), 3) initial learning rate = 0.025, 4) learning rate decay per epoch = 0.00025, and 5) number of epochs = 30. During parameter optimization, 10% of the dataset was randomly separated from the training set to form the validation set. The validation set was used to optimize the above hyperparameters, using the loss function as a metric. However, the entire training set was used when training the final model. After training, the semantic information is expected to be captured through the Word2Vec model.

Neighbor compound searching

Using our trained semantic model, we embedded all spectra from the training set into vectors. This process resulted in two matrices: a 600,289 × 300 matrix for positive mode and a 182,323 × 300 matrix for negative mode. Each row in these matrices represents an embedded vector, and the similarity between vectors correlates with the structural similarity of the corresponding molecules. An unknown spectrum is then embedded into a vector with the same model. Previous studies on Spec2Vec have demonstrated that the distance between the semantic-model embedded vectors accurately reflects the structural similarity of the corresponding metabolites (Huber et al., 2021a). Here, our objective was to efficiently compare the embedded vector of the query spectrum with pre-built matrices.

To this end, we used HNSW graphs. HNSW is an approximate nearest-neighbor search (ANNS) method based on hierarchical graph structures and graph traversal techniques. HNSW indices do not require training with data. The construction parameters were M = 64 and ef_construction = 800, where M defines the maximum number of outgoing connections in the graph and ef_construction defines a construction time/accuracy trade-off. Essentially, the HNSW index serves as an efficient data structure, organizing hierarchically multi-layer graphs to store, organize, and calculate distances between vectors. This approach significantly improves the speed of ANNS. The ANNS method is used to rapidly retrieve the top 300 vectors with the smallest distances. The structures associated with the retrieved vectors are considered neighboring compounds, indicating that they are likely to share similar structures with the unknown compound.

Candidate retrieval and ranking

A typical metabolomic data-processing workflow using comprehensive software such as XCMS (Forsberg et al., 2018), MZMine (Schmid et al., 2023), OpenMS (Pfeuffer et al., 2024), KPIC2 (Ji et al., 2017), or MS-DIAL (Tsugawa et al., 2015) provides quantitative features together with corresponding information such as precursor m/z, ion type, charge, and isotopic pattern for unknowns. This information enables users to annotate the molecular formulas using external software tools such as BUDDY (Xing et al., 2023) or MIST-CF (Goldman et al., 2023). We therefore encourage users to input unknown spectra along with assigned formulas, enabling the software to retrieve candidates from the structural bio database on the basis of the provided formulas. In cases where the uploaded spectra lack this information, DeepMASS v.2 also accepts precursor m/z, ion type, charge, and isotopic pattern, inferring the monoisotopic mass on the basis of these parameters and retrieving and evaluating potential candidates with a monoisotopic mass difference below a set threshold (defaulting to 10 ppm).

DeepMASS v.2 evaluates and ranks candidates by considering the relative location between the encompassed region of neighboring compounds and the candidates within the chemical space. The positions of compounds in the chemical space are estimated using Morgan fingerprints, and the distance between each pair of compounds is determined using the Dice distance calculated from their fingerprint vectors.

Specifically, for each candidate, DeepMASS v.2 scans the neighboring compounds and identifies the nearest 20 compounds. The algorithm then calculates the cumulative distance between these selected neighboring compounds and the candidate, using this summed distance as the ranking score. The mathematical expression is

According to this equation, candidates that are closer to the predicted neighboring compounds will receive higher scores. In fact, it is not necessary for all predicted compounds to be highly related to the unknown substance. A subset of neighboring compounds with structural similarities to the unknown substance is adequate to map the chemical space, thereby enabling the correct structure to receive a significantly higher score than the others. This feature also means that the prediction of neighbors does not have to be exceptionally accurate. A few false positive predictions do not significantly affect the identification results.

CASMI benchmarking data

We used CASMI 2014, 2016, and 2022 as benchmarking data for our evaluation. MS/MS peak lists for CASMI 2014 and 2016 were readily available on the official website. In the case of CASMI 2022, raw files in mzML format are provided. We converted the raw files into MGF format with MSConvert (Chambers et al., 2012) and loaded them with the MatchMS package (Huber et al., 2020). We directly extracted MS/MS data from the loaded spectra using the provided retention time and precursor m/z information. The threshold for precursor m/z was set to 0.01 Da, and the threshold for retention time was set to 5 s. If more than two MS/MS spectra were obtained, the one with the highest precursor intensity was selected. CASMI 2017 was excluded because it uses MSE mode, whereas all spectra in the reference dataset are from DDA mode. We assumed that correct molecular formulas were annotated and could be used directly for candidate retrieval in the software utilized for comparison.

Acquisition of experimental spectra for standards

Standard reagents were purchased from MedChemExpress. A sample of each reagent (2 μl) was diluted with MeOH/MeCN/H2O (v:v:v = 2:2:1) to obtain a stock solution of 50 μg/ml, and the remaining reagent was stored at −80°C. For every group of 50 reagents, 5 μl was taken from each stock solution and mixed together. A table was created to indicate the types of compounds in each sample and to ensure that no compounds with the same molecular weight were included in the same sample.

Mixtures were separated on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (Waters, MA, USA) (100 × 2.1 mm, 2.6 μm) and analyzed using a Vanquish UHPLC system coupled with a Q Exactive HF-X orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA) equipped with an HESI probe. The injection volume was set to 1 μl, and the flow rate was set to 300 μl/min. Solvents for the mobile phase were 0.05% formic acid in acetonitrile (A) and 0.1% formic acid in water (B). The gradient elution program was 0% B for 0–12 min, then a linear increase from 30% to 100% B until 20 min, 100% B until 22.5 min, and 0% B until 25 min. Data in the mass range of 80–1200 m/z were acquired by data-dependent MS/MS acquisition. The full scan and fragment spectra were collected at resolutions of 70 000 and 17 500, respectively. The raw files exported from the platform were converted to mzML format using MSConvert (Chambers et al., 2012). MS/MS data were extracted from the mzML files with PyOpenMS (Röst et al., 2014). If more than two MS/MS data sets were obtained, the one with the highest precursor intensity was selected.

Tomato ripening study

The cherry tomato cultivar Qianxi was grown in the greenhouse at the AGIS experimental station in Shenzhen, China (114°30′3.63″ E, 22°36′4.66″ N) for 30–40 days at 28°C and then transplanted to the field. Three to five fruits per plant were picked at selected ripening stages and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. The frozen tissues were ground into a fine powder using an IKA A11 mill (IKA Works, NC, USA) and stored in a −80°C freezer for metabolomic analysis.

For nontargeted metabolomic analysis, 100 mg of tomato powder from each sample was extracted in 1 ml of extraction buffer (MeOH:H2O = 80:20, v/v, with 0.1% formic acid and 100 ng/ml of d5-coumarin, d5-L-phenylalanine, and d3-homovanillic acid as internal standards [Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, MA, USA]), followed by a 30-min ultrasonic treatment. The supernatants were pipetted and dried in a LABCONCO CentriVap vacuum centrifugal concentrator (We Brothers Scientific, MO, USA) and resuspended in 100 μl of reconstitution buffer (MeOH:H2O = 80:20, v/v). A 10-μl aliquot of the supernatant from each sample was pipetted and mixed to obtain a QC sample.

Extracts were analyzed using a U3000 UHPLC system coupled to a Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an HESI probe in both positive and negative modes. Extracts were separated on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (Waters) (150 × 2.1 mm, 2.6 μm). The injection volume was set to 1 μl, and the flow rate was set to 300 μl/min. Solvents for the mobile phase were 0.05% formic acid in acetonitrile (A) and 0.1% formic acid in water (B). The elution gradient program was as follows: 0–25 min, a linear gradient from 40% to 100% A, followed by column washing with 100% B and 100% A. Data in the mass ranges of 80–1,200 and 80–1,200 m/z were acquired separately in positive and negative ion modes using data-dependent MS/MS acquisition. The full scan and fragment spectra were collected with resolutions of 70,000 and 17,500, respectively. Data preprocessing and MS/MS extraction were performed with MS-DIAL (Tsugawa et al., 2015). The data-processing settings were as follows: peak height of 20,000, mass slice of 0.1 Da, and alignment retention-time tolerance of 0.05 min. The MS/MS files were exported and used as input for DeepMASS v.2 software.

Parameters of other tested software

The performance of DeepMASS v.2 was assessed relative to that of other state-of-the-art in silico metabolite annotation tools: MS-Finder (v.3.52), CFM-ID (v.4.0), MetFrag (v.2.6.1), and SIRIUS (v.5.8.1). The parameter settings were as follows: SIRIUS, MS2 mass accuracy of 10 ppm, database “custom,” and a heuristic above 300 m/z; MF-Finder, MS2 mass tolerance of 0.01 Da, tree depth of 2, and a user-defined database; CFM-ID, candidate_file of local_csv, prob_thresh of 0.001, ppm_mass_tol of 10 ppm, and score_type of Dice; and MetFrag, database_type of local_csv, mass_deviation of 0.001 Da, and score_types of FragmenterScore. In general, with the exception of the candidate list, all the methods were run with default parameters. Both DeepMASS v.2 and the other software tools used spectra in MSP format; the chemical formulas were considered known. SIRIUS used the CSI:FingerID algorithm, and the other methods used an in silico fragmentation algorithm to prioritize candidate rankings. The compound databases used by all methods were identical and were loaded as custom uploads using their parameter-setting functions. Both SIRIUS and MS-Finder have already integrated the newest version of GNPS data into their training sets. Consequently, a portion of the CASMI data could be considered exposed to their models.
