Volumetric analysis of cervical intervertebral discs: Reference values for interventional cervical disc procedures
Authors: Waleed Sultan, Jay Napolitano, William Lee, Fred DeFrancesch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMRI does not reliably identify symptomatic cervical discs, and normative cervical disc volumetrics remain poorly defined.ObjectiveTo establish level-specific cervical disc volume norms after excluding non-intradiscal contrast flow and to compare volumes between pain-positive and pain-negative discs.MethodsThis retrospective 10-year study analyzed cervical discograms with contained intradiscal filling; discs with epidural, vascular, or prevertebral extravasation were excluded. Injected volumes were recorded, and interlevel and pain-group comparisons were performed using ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis, t-tests, and Mann–Whitney U testing.ResultsA total of 135 discs met the inclusion criteria. The overall mean injected cervical disc volume was 0.24 ± 0.10 mL (range, 0.10 – 0.60 mL), with no significant differences across cervical levels. Pain-positive discs demonstrated higher injected volumes at C3/4, C4/5, C5/6, and C6/7 compared with pain-negative discs (p < 0.05).ConclusionCervical disc volumetrics are consistent across levels, with higher volumes noted in pain-positive discs at mid-cervical levels. These baseline values should be referenced for intradiscal injections to guide administered volumes and to help prevent disc overpressurization.

Background

Cervical discography is a targeted diagnostic procedure used to evaluate axial neck pain of suspected disc origin by identifying provoked pain responses in the anatomic levels tested. Although MRI is commonly used to assess cervical disc degeneration, it does not reliably distinguish symptomatic from asymptomatic discs, limiting its ability to identify true pain generators [1]. Consequently, discography remains a selective but valuable tool when surgical or interventional decision-making requires precise localization of a symptomatic motion segment [1,2]. Early cervical disc stimulation studies demonstrated reproducible referred pain patterns and highlighted the diagnostic relevance of controlled provocation testing for internal disc disruption [2], while long-term clinical series underscored substantial variability in disc morphology and filling behavior [3].

A central principle of discography is confirming true intradiscal contrast containment. Bogduk emphasized that epidural, vascular, or prevertebral contrast spread invalidates diagnostic interpretation, as these patterns reflect annular incompetence or technical deviation [4]. Additionally, it has been shown that the posterior elements should be excluded from consideration as a pain generator prior to the consideration and performance of cervical discography to avoid an unnecessarily high flase positive rate which may occur if this important step is excluded [4]. Beyond pain provocation, discography also provides volumetric information that reflects disc integrity and containment capacity. Pressure-controlled discography research in the lumbar spine has shown that abnormal discs demonstrate altered pressure-volume dynamics, underscoring the importance of standardized injection parameters [5]. Similarly, MRI discography correlation studies reveal that structural changes visible on imaging do not always correspond to symptomatic discs, reinforcing the value of physiologic intradiscal testing [6].

Recent International Pain & Spine Intervention Society (IPSIS) guidelines have further standardized disc access technique, fluoroscopic verification, and criteria for acceptable intradiscal contrast patterns, emphasizing the need to exclude all extradiscal flow for diagnostic accuracy [7]. Despite these advances, no studies have systematically characterized cervical disc volumetrics across levels after strict exclusion of extravasation.

The objective of this study was to quantify level-specific injected cervical disc volumes using rigorous IPSIS-aligned criteria. Establishing normative volumetric ranges may improve diagnostic accuracy, enhance procedural safety, and provide reference values applicable to cervical discography and intradiscal biologics/therapeutic interventions. Data set reviewed was a subset of data from a previous study and was collected in accordance with approval as waived by the Western IRB.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study evaluated cervical discography procedures performed between 2014 and 2023 at a spine and pain management center. All procedures were performed by master International Pain & Spine Intervention Society (IPSIS) bioskills instructors using standardized fluoroscopic guidance as described in the IPSIS Technical Manual [7]. Cervical discography was performed to identify axial pain generators when investigation of the posterior elements/zygapophyseal joints had been excluded as a potential pain generator. Procedural reports and archived fluoroscopic images were reviewed to extract injected contrast volume (mL), cervical level tested, contrast fill pattern, and patient-reported concordant pain response.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria: Only discs with complete documentation of injected volume and filling patterns were eligible for analysis. All cervical levels from C2–3 through C7–T1 were screened. To ensure accurate assessment of true intradiscal volumetric behavior, only discs demonstrating contained intradiscal filling, defined as core filling, bilateral recess filling, or a combination of these patterns, were included. Discs exhibiting epidural contrast spread, vascular opacification, or prevertebral tracking were excluded. In total, 28 discs met exclusion criteria, leaving 135 discs for final quantitative analysis.

Injected contrast volume served as the primary outcome measure. Descriptive statistics—including minimum, maximum, mean, median, standard deviation, and frequency—were calculated for each cervical level. Differences in injected volume across cervical levels were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with Kruskal–Wallis testing performed as a nonparametric alternative. Comparisons between pain-positive and pain-negative discs were conducted using an independent samples t-test and confirmed with Mann–Whitney U testing. All statistical tests were two-sided.

All analyses were performed in Python. Pandas was used for data cleaning, filtering, and descriptive statistics; SciPy was used for inferential statistical testing (t-test, Mann–Whitney U, ANOVA, and Kruskal–Wallis); and Matplotlib was used to generate histograms, dot plots, scatter plots, and comparative volume figures. Cleaned datasets, statistical tables, and all graphical outputs were exported for manuscript integration.

Interlevel volume analysis

Analysis of injected contrast volumes demonstrated relative uniformity across cervical levels. Mean and median injected volumes did not differ significantly from C2/3 through C7/T1, with no statistically significant interlevel differences observed on one-way ANOVA (p = 0.106) or Kruskal–Wallis testing (p = 0.071). Across all included discs, the mean injected cervical disc volume was 0.24 ± 0.10 mL, with a median of 0.20 mL and a range of 0.10–0.60 mL, providing a global reference for expected intradiscal filling under contained conditions (Table 1). Analysis of injected contrast volumes revealed two principal findings. First, injected volumes were relatively consistent across cervical levels, with no statistically significant interlevel differences observed on one-way ANOVA (p = 0.106) or Kruskal–Wallis testing (p = 0.071). This indicates that, after exclusion of extravasation, cervical discs from C2/3 through C7/T1 demonstrate similar volumetric capacity and filling behavior. Across all included cervical discs (n = 135), the mean injected volume was 0.24 ± 0.10 mL, with a median of 0.20 mL and a range of 0.10–0.60 mL, providing a global reference for expected intradiscal filling under contained conditions.

Second, comparison of pain-positive and pain-negative discs identified statistically significant volumetric differences at several mid-cervical levels. Specifically, discs at C3/4, C4/5, C5/6, and C6/7 demonstrated higher mean injected volumes in pain-positive cases, with significance confirmed by Mann–Whitney U testing and, at most levels, independent t-testing. These findings suggest that symptomatic discs may exhibit reduced annular containment or greater internal structural disruption, allowing higher injected volumes before pressurization elicits pain.

Levels C2/3 and C7/T1 lacked sufficient pain-positive cases to permit meaningful statistical comparison. When all cervical levels were pooled, global analysis demonstrated a significant difference between pain-positive and pain-negative discs (t = 5.79, p < 0.001; Kruskal–Wallis p < 0.001). Collectively, these results indicate that although overall cervical disc volumetric capacity is uniform across levels, discs associated with concordant pain responses tend to accommodate greater injected volumes, reflecting impaired containment rather than normal anatomic variation.

Discussion

This study offers a detailed evaluation of cervical disc volumetrics, providing level-specific values derived exclusively from discs demonstrating true intradiscal contrast containment. Consistent with prior systematic reviews on cervical discography [1], these findings expand the understanding of physiologic disc filling behavior and establish reference ranges that may enhance diagnostic confidence and procedural safety. The diagnostic utility of provoked pain responses and disc stimulation patterns has been demonstrated in earlier work [2,3]; however, many prior studies did not consistently exclude extradiscal contrast flow or systematically evaluate injected volume across cervical levels, limiting the availability of reliable normative volumetric data.

Bogduk emphasized that epidural, vascular, and prevertebral contrast extravasation invalidate diagnostic interpretation because they indicate annular incompetence or technical deviation [4]. By rigorously excluding all such non-intradiscal patterns, this study aligns directly with IPSIS safety recommendations, which define proper disc access, confirmatory fluoroscopic criteria, and acceptable contrast distribution patterns for diagnostic accuracy [7]. The strict inclusion criteria applied here, therefore, allow for an accurate assessment of physiologic cervical disc volumetrics on disc access.

A key finding of this analysis is the overall uniformity of injected disc volume across cervical levels from C2–3 through C7–T1. Both ANOVA and Kruskal–Wallis testing demonstrated no significant interlevel differences, supporting the concept that cervical discs share relatively consistent volumetric capacity when evaluated under controlled intradiscal conditions. This observation parallels pressure-controlled lumbar discography research, which suggests that symptomatic discs are more often distinguished by altered pressure–volume relationships rather than by absolute volumetric capacity alone [5]. Similarly, MRI–discography correlation studies have shown that internal disc disruption and discogenic pain may occur independently of disc size or radiographic degeneration severity [6]. The overall mean injected cervical disc volume of approximately 0.24 mL provides a practical benchmark for intradiscal injection under contained conditions, supporting interpretation of volumetric behavior as a reflection of disc containment rather than pain generation alone.

Importantly, this study identified statistically significant differences in injected volume between pain-positive and pain-negative discs at several mid-cervical levels. Discs at C3/4, C4/5, C5/6, and C6/7 demonstrated higher mean injected volumes in pain-positive cases, confirmed by Mann–Whitney U testing and, at most levels, independent t-testing. These findings suggest that symptomatic discs may accommodate greater injected volume due to reduced annular containment or increased internal disc disruption. Rather than reflecting increased intrinsic disc capacity, higher injected volumes in pain-positive discs likely represent uncontrolled fissuring or compromised annular integrity, allowing contrast to disperse within the disc before sufficient pressurization occurs to provoke pain. This interpretation aligns with histologic, biomechanical, and imaging-based evidence linking discogenic pain to annular fissuring, neoinnervation, and inflammatory endplate changes rather than to gross volumetric anatomy [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. Levels C2/3 and C7/T1 lacked sufficient pain-positive cases for comparison, limiting conclusions at these extremes.

Volumes across all levels did not exceed 0.4 cc in negative discs and 0.6 cc overall, with a mean volume of 0.24 cc ± 0.1 cc. No evaluation of volume regarding the presence or absence of a startle response was considered, as there has been no correlation reported to its association with a painful response and no suggestion of any impacts of this phenomenon on volume [12,13]. From a clinical perspective, these findings reinforce the importance of interpreting injected volume as a marker of disc containment and internal architecture rather than as an isolated determinant of pain response. An understanding expected disc volumetrics and normative volumetric ranges may inform safer and more consistent intradiscal injection strategies, facilitate early recognition of aberrant filling patterns, and enhance future interventions(biologic/intradiscal therapies).

Several limitations should be acknowledged. As a retrospective analysis, this study relies on accurate procedural documentation. As no standardized or practical solution for cervica disc manometry is currently accepted due to the low volumes present, the data does not include pressure–volume curve measurements, which may provide additional diagnostic resolution in pressure-controlled discography in the lumbar spine [5]. Pain provocation responses are inherently subjective and may vary across individuals and stages of degeneration. Furthermore, injected volumes were not correlated with imaging-based degeneration severity or endplate morphology, which may further clarify structural contributors to altered volumetric behavior [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]].

Despite these limitations, this study provides robust normative cervical disc volumetric data under rigorous exclusion criteria and IPSIS-aligned standards. The findings demonstrate consistent volumetric capacity across cervical levels while identifying meaningful volumetric differences between symptomatic and asymptomatic discs at key mid-cervical levels. These insights advance procedural understanding of cervical discography and disc access as well as support improved diagnostic precision and interventional planning in cervical spine care.

Conclusion

This study defines normative cervical disc volumetrics using strict intradiscal containment criteria and IPSIS safety standards. Disc volumes were consistent across cervical levels; however, pain-positive discs at C3/4, C4/5, C5/6, and C6/7 demonstrated significantly higher injected volumes, suggesting reduced containment or greater internal disruption. These findings underscore the diagnostic value of volumetric behavior during discography and establish baseline reference ranges for cervical intradiscal volume administration. Notably, the overall mean cervical disc volume of approximately 0.24 mL provides a practical benchmark that may guide interventionalists in establishing safe injection thresholds, helping to minimize the risk of overpressurization during contrast or biologic intradiscal injections. Collectively, these data support safer, more accurate cervical disc interventions and improved procedural standardization.
