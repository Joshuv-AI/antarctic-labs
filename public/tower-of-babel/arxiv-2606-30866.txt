A data-dependent DKW inequality for regenerative Markov chains
Daniel C. Jerison

Abstract
. We prove a version of the Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz inequality for Markov chains with a regenerative structure. Suppose we have a regenerative Markov chain with stationary distribution π \pi . Given a functional θ \theta on the state space and a confidence level 1 − δ 1-\delta , our result provides a uniform 1 − δ 1-\delta confidence band for the CDF of θ \theta under π \pi based on the empirical CDF. By inversion, we get a 1 − δ 1-\delta confidence band for the quantile function of θ \theta under π \pi . Our bounds are fully explicit and nearly optimal. In addition, they are data-dependent in the following sense: in the formula for the width of the confidence band, the leading term can be computed directly from the sample path without any a priori information about the convergence rate of the chain. A convergence bound is required, but it contributes to the width of the confidence band only through a lower-order term. For this reason, our result is attractive for Markov chains whose convergence rate is much quicker in practice than what can be proved in theory. Data-dependent bounds of this type are called empirical concentration inequalities in the literature. Thus, our result is an empirical concentration inequality for the empirical CDF of θ \theta given the sample path.



1.
Introduction

This paper is concerned with explicit, nonasymptotic bounds for Markov chain Monte Carlo estimation. Suppose that
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a discrete time Markov chain on a state space
\mathcal{X}
with stationary distribution
\pi
. Let
\theta:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbf{R}
be an observable quantity of interest. The empirical distribution of
\theta
given the sample path
(X_{0},\ldots,X_{n-1})
is

\hat{\pi}_{n}(\theta\in S)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathbf{1}\{\theta(X_{t})\in S\},\qquad\text{for measurable $S\subseteq\mathbf{R}$}.

We say that the Glivenko–Cantelli property is satisfied if the empirical cumulative distribution function of
\theta
converges uniformly to the cumulative distribution function of
\theta
under
\pi
:

(1.1)

\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}}\left|\hat{\pi}_{n}(\theta\leq x)-\pi(\theta\leq x)\right|\to 0\qquad\text{almost surely as }n\to\infty.

A DKW inequality for the Markov chain
(X_{t})
is a quantitative upper bound on the supremum in (
1.1
) that holds with high probability. It is named after the classical inequality of Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz
[
7
]
, which provides the same type of bound in the case of iid data.

Various authors
[
6
,
10
,
13
,
21
,
2
]
have proved DKW-type inequalities for Markov chains (or, more generally, for dependent sequences satisfying strong mixing conditions) under different assumptions. In any such result, it is necessary to have some control over the mixing properties of the chain. This is a major issue:

•

Many chains used in the practice of MCMC do not have theoretical convergence guarantees.

•

Some chains do have theoretical convergence guarantees, but only of a qualitative nature (e.g., the chain is known to be geometrically ergodic but without any explicit convergence rate).

•

Some chains have theoretical convergence guarantees with explicit rates, but the numerical convergence bounds are very conservative compared with the observed behavior of the chain.

When dealing with a Markov chain in the third category, we might hope to get tighter estimation error bounds by feeding in information from the sample path. The main result of this paper can be stated informally as follows. Suppose that the chain
(X_{t})
satisfies a certain exponential convergence bound, which is associated with constants
B
and
\gamma
(see (
1.3
) below). Then, for every
\delta>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
we have

(1.2)

\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}}\left|\hat{\pi}_{n}(\theta\leq x)-\pi(\theta\leq x)\right|\leq\frac{\log(n)}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot C_{1}(X,\delta)+\frac{\log(n)}{n}\cdot C_{2}(B,\gamma,\delta)

where
C_{1}(X,\delta)
and
C_{2}(B,\gamma,\delta)
have explicit formulas and do not grow with
n
. The notation
C_{1}(X,\delta)
means that
C_{1}
depends on
\delta
and on the sample path
(X_{0},\ldots,X_{n-1})
, but not on the exponential convergence bound. The notation
C_{2}(B,\gamma,\delta)
means that
C_{2}
depends on
\delta
and on the exponential convergence bound (but not on the sample path). See Theorem
2.1
for the fully precise statement.

In (
1.2
), the leading term

\frac{\log(n)}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot C_{1}(X,\delta)

has nearly optimal dependence on
n
, except for the logarithmic factor.

1

To the author’s knowledge, all similar results either also have a log factor or impose an extra condition, such as uniform ergodicity, that allows the log factor to be removed. The bound in

has a factor of
\sqrt{\log(n)}
, which is slightly better than (

In addition, since
C_{1}(X,\delta)
is fully data-dependent, its size is determined by the actual mixing properties of the Markov chain and not by the convergence bound, which may be quite conservative. The second term

\frac{\log(n)}{n}\cdot C_{2}(B,\gamma,\delta)

has optimal dependence on
n
and
B,\gamma
, including the logarithmic factor, as we discuss in Section
1.4
.

1.1.
Regenerative chains

Our results hold for Markov chains that have a regenerative structure. Let
\nu
be a probability measure on the state space
\mathcal{X}
. A
\nu
-regeneration time
for the chain
(X_{t})
is a random time
T
with the property that
X_{T}\sim\nu
, and moreover, the law of
X_{T}
is independent of the value of
T
and of the history
(X_{t})_{t<T}
. In other words, at time
T
the chain regenerates in a state drawn independently from
\nu
.

A standard way to construct regeneration times is to identify a so-called
small set
. This is a subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
together with a constant
\varepsilon>0
and a probability measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
such that

\mathbf{P}(X_{t+1}\in\cdot\mid X_{t}=x)\geq\varepsilon\cdot\nu(\cdot)\qquad\text{for all }x\in A.

The regeneration time
T
and the chain
(X_{t})
can be defined simultaneously on the same probability space as follows. First, run the chain until it reaches
A
at some time
\tau_{A}
. Suppose that
X_{\tau_{A}}=a
. Flip an
(\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon)
coin. If the result is
\varepsilon
, draw
X_{\tau_{A}+1}
from
\nu
and set
T=\tau_{A}+1
. Otherwise, draw
X_{\tau_{A}+1}
from the remainder measure

\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}[\mathbf{P}(X_{t+1}\in\cdot\mid X_{t}=a)-\varepsilon\nu(\cdot)]

and keep going. Every time that the chain reaches
A
, flip an independent
(\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon)
coin; if the result is
\varepsilon
, jump to
\nu
and set the value of
T
accordingly. As long as the chain almost surely visits
A
infinitely often, eventually the coin will show
\varepsilon
and the random time
T
will be defined.

It is immediate from this description that
T
is a
\nu
-regeneration time. In fact, if we restart the algorithm after every regeneration, we obtain a sequence
T_{1},T_{2},\ldots
of
\nu
-regeneration times. It will be convenient to assume that the chain is started from
X_{0}\sim\nu
, so that we can define
T_{0}=0
. Then, the sample path
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is partitioned into iid tours
(X_{t})_{T_{k-1}\leq t<T_{k}}
with the property that each tour starts at a regeneration and ends just before the next regeneration.

To apply the results of this paper for a particular Markov chain
(X_{t})
, it is necessary to simulate
(X_{t})
and identify the regeneration times. Finding an appropriate set
A
and measure
\nu
may be quite difficult. But, once
A
and
\nu
have been chosen, identifying the regeneration times is often straightforward
[
20
,
1
]
. (In particular, it is not necessary to compute the normalizing constant for
\nu
.) The requirement that
X_{0}\sim\nu
is easily satisfied: simply run the chain until the first regeneration and discard the previous samples.

Our results require a sequence
0=T_{0},T_{1},T_{2},\ldots
of regeneration times such that the tours
(X_{t})_{T_{k-1}\leq t<T_{k}}
are iid. The small set procedure described above is one way to construct such a sequence. If the regeneration times are constructed in some other way, that is fine, as long as the tours are iid and the regenerations can be identified in simulation.

There is a vast literature on regenerative Markov chains. Much of it focuses on using regenerations to prove convergence bounds. Briefly, three ingredients are needed: (1) the regenerative structure, which may be provided by a small set; (2) some control over the frequency of regenerations, which may be provided by a so-called
drift function
or
Lyapunov function
; and (3) an aperiodicity condition (such as reversibility with nonnegative eigenvalues). See
[
18
,
12
]
for more information.

In this paper, we rely on ingredients (1) and (2). We will specifically assume that the tail of the tour length distribution decays exponentially: the first positive regeneration time
T=T_{1}
satisfies

(1.3)

\mathbf{P}(T>t)\leq Be^{-\gamma t}\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0,

where
B
and
\gamma
are positive constants. (The notation
\mathbf{P}(T>t)
incorporates our standing assumption that
X_{0}\sim\nu
.) The inequality (
1.3
) is satisfied for some
B,\gamma
whenever the chain is geometrically ergodic, but we need numerical values for
B
and
\gamma
. Indeed, the values of
B
and
\gamma
are the only way in which our
a priori
knowledge about the chain affects the upper bound in (
1.2
). Everything else comes from the sample path.

We note that (
1.3
) is not enough by itself to bound the convergence rate of the chain. In fact, the chain could satisfy (
1.3
) even if it is periodic. This does not pose any problem for a bound of the form (
1.2
), because the periodicity is washed out by the time average. Accordingly, we will need no extra hypotheses such as reversibility or aperiodicity to establish (
1.2
).

1.2.
Empirical concentration inequalities

An empirical concentration inequality (or data-dependent concentration inequality) is one that obtains sharper bounds by using properties of the random sample. To illustrate the general form, we describe a result of Maurer and Pontil
[
16
]
.

Let
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
be iid random variables supported on
[0,1]
with mean
\mathbf{E}(Z)
and variance
\Var(Z)
. Then, the sample mean
\overline{Z}_{n}=(Z_{1}+\cdots+Z_{n})/n
concentrates around
\mathbf{E}(Z)
. According to
[
16
]
, the classical Bennett’s inequality yields the following bound. For all
\delta>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

(1.4)

\overline{Z}_{n}-\mathbf{E}(Z)\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\Var(Z)\log(1/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{3n}.

(A symmetric bound on
\mathbf{E}(Z)-\overline{Z}_{n}
follows from replacing
Z_{i}
with
1-Z_{i}
.)

The bound (
1.4
) is attractive when
\Var(Z)
is small. Maurer and Pontil
[
16
]
envision a scenario in which we have no
a priori
bound on
\Var(Z)
(beyond
\Var(Z)\leq 1/4
), but the sample variance
\hat{V}_{n}(Z)
of the data
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
is small. In that case, they prove a version of (
1.4
) in which
\Var(Z)
is replaced with
\hat{V}_{n}(Z)
: For all
\delta>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

(1.5)

\overline{Z}_{n}-\mathbf{E}(Z)\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\hat{V}_{n}(Z)\log(2/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{7\log(2/\delta)}{3(n-1)}.

It is instructive to compare the form of (
1.5
) with our result (
1.2
). In both cases, the leading
1/\sqrt{n}
term is purely data-dependent, which makes the inequality tighter when the
a priori
bound is conservative compared with the sample data.

Many authors have developed empirical concentration inequalities in the vein of (
1.5
) in various contexts. The recent work
[
14
]
provides a substantial quantitative improvement on the techniques of
[
16
]
. We highlight two papers that extend the theory of empirical concentration inequalities in the direction of Markov chains and dependent sample data. Wintenberger
[
24
]
considers a geometrically ergodic Markov chain with a small set that provides regenerations and a drift function
V
that controls their frequency. He proves an empirical Bernstein inequality that can be used, for example, to bound the convergence of
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}V(X_{t})
to
\mathbf{E}_{\pi}(V)
. Mirzaei et al
[
19
]
extend the inequality (
1.5
) of
[
16
]
to the setting of dependent sample data that satisfies a decay condition of the
\beta
-mixing coefficients. Since the
\beta
-mixing coefficients of a geometrically ergodic Markov chain decay exponentially
[
23
]
, this leads to an empirical concentration inequality for the convergence of
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}f(X_{t})
to
\mathbf{E}_{\pi}(f)
whenever
(X_{t})
is geometrically ergodic and
f
is bounded.

For our purposes, the word “empirical” has two different senses: as a synonym for “data-dependent” in empirical concentration inequalities, and as a descriptor for the empirical CDF in the context of DKW-type bounds. One could characterize our main result (
1.2
) as an empirical concentration inequality for the empirical CDF associated with a functional of a regenerative Markov chain. Aside from the linguistic curiosity, this perspective shows what is new about our work. DKW bounds and empirical concentration inequalities have already been established for Markov chains. Our result is the first to combine both of these in a data-dependent DKW inequality.

1.3.
General approach

We now discuss our approach to proving (
1.2
). We are given a Markov chain
(X_{t})
with a sequence
0=T_{0},T_{1},T_{2},\ldots
of
\nu
-regeneration times such that the tours
(X_{t})_{T_{k-1}\leq t<T_{k}}
are iid. For each
j\geq 0
, let
\pi_{j}
be the conditional law of
X_{t}
given that the chain has taken
j
steps since the most recent regeneration. That is,

\pi_{j}(\cdot)=\mathbf{P}(X_{j}\in\cdot\mid T>j),

recalling our assumption that
X_{0}\sim\nu
and our convention that
T=T_{1}
. The stationary distribution
\pi
can be written as a mixture of the
\pi_{j}
using the well-known formula

\pi=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}q_{j}\pi_{j},

where

q_{j}=\frac{\mathbf{P}(T>j)}{\mathbf{E}(T)}

is the long-term fraction of the time that the chain spends “at
\pi_{j}
.”

Consider the problem of approximating the CDF of
\theta
under
\pi
,

F(x)=\pi(\theta\leq x),

with the empirical CDF

\hat{F}_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathbf{1}\{\theta(X_{t})\leq x\}.

As the Markov chain runs, the sample path learns the distribution of tour lengths, and therefore the weights
q_{j}
, to increasing accuracy. In addition, every time that the chain reaches
j
steps after the most recent regeneration is a new independent sample from
\pi_{j}
. Thus, the empirical CDF of
\theta
on these samples will converge to
\pi_{j}(\theta\leq x)
at a rate which is controlled by the classical DKW inequality for iid samples. The upper bound on

\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}}|\hat{F}_{n}(x)-F(x)|

follows from combining the convergence of the empirical tour length distribution with the separate DKW bounds for each
j
.

In order for the argument to work, we will need control over the 1-Wasserstein distance between the empirical distribution of tour lengths and the law of
T
. The 1-Wasserstein metric arises quite naturally in the proof; see Lemma
2.5
.

1.4.
Sharpness

We would like to show that the second term in (
1.2
),

\frac{\log(n)}{n}\cdot C_{2}(B,\gamma,\delta),

has optimal dependence on
n
and on
B,\gamma
. This will come out from a further analysis of the proof strategy described in Section
1.3
.

The fundamental reason that MCMC convergence diagnostics can be misleading is that the target distribution
\pi
could be bimodal or multimodal. If the Markov chain spends all its time exploring one mode, then the sample path will appear to have converged. Suppose that the state space
\mathcal{X}
is partitioned into two regions
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{1}\cup\mathcal{X}_{2}
, with
\pi(\mathcal{X}_{1})\approx\pi(\mathcal{X}_{2})\approx 1/2
, and such that the chain is very unlikely to move between
\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{2}
. If we define a small set
A
and regeneration measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{X}_{1}
, then the chain started from
\nu
will enjoy frequent regenerations with short tour lengths as long as it stays within
\mathcal{X}_{1}
. Eventually, the chain will jump to
\mathcal{X}_{2}
and stay there for a long time. This will show up in the sample path as an extremely long tour length that seemingly came out of nowhere.

Suppose that our sample path has gone through
K
regenerations, so that we have
K
independent samples from the tour length distribution. Also suppose that all of the observed tour lengths are reasonably short. From this information, we can say that long tours are unusual: with high probability, they occur with frequency at most
c/K
for some constant
c
. But this says nothing about the length of a long tour once it occurs. In the bimodal example, the length of a long tour is determined by the probability of jumping from
\mathcal{X}_{2}
to
\mathcal{X}_{1}
, which could be arbitrarily small.

This is why we need the
a priori
bound (
1.3
) on tour length, which we repeat here:

(1.6)

\mathbf{P}(T>t)\leq Be^{-\gamma t}.

Suppose that we have observed
K
regenerations for a total of
n
samples from
(X_{t})
. It could be that long tours occur with frequency
1/K
. Solving

Be^{-\gamma t}=\frac{1}{K}

for
t
yields

t=\frac{\log(BK)}{\gamma}.

Thus, the bound (
1.6
) is consistent with the long tours having approximate length
\log(BK)/\gamma
when they occur. If we append a long tour of this length after a sequence of
K
short tours of total length
n
, the empirical CDF
\hat{F}_{n}(x)
could increase or decrease by up to

\frac{\log(BK)/\gamma}{n+\log(BK)/\gamma}.

Note that as
K
and
n
increase, the ratio
n/K
converges to the average tour length
\mathbf{E}(T)
. Once
K
and
n
are large enough, we have
\log(BK)/\gamma\leq n
, and it follows that

\frac{1}{2n}\cdot\frac{\log(BK)}{\gamma}\leq\frac{\log(BK)/\gamma}{n+\log(BK)/\gamma}\leq\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{\log(BK)}{\gamma}.

The second term in (
1.2
), which we have written as

\frac{\log(n)}{n}\cdot C_{2}(B,\gamma,\delta),

is in fact equal to

\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{\log(BK)}{\gamma}

up to a multiplicative constant depending on
\delta
, at least in the regime where

\frac{\log(BK)}{\gamma}\geq 1.

See Section
2.1
. Thus, it has optimal dependence on
n
and on
B,\gamma
.

1.5.
Discussion

Our main result, Theorem
2.1
, is a data-dependent DKW inequality for regenerative Markov chains. Since it provides a
1-\delta
confidence band for the CDF of the functional
\theta
under
\pi
, by inverting we obtain a
1-\delta
confidence band for the quantile function of
\theta
. In other words, Theorem
2.1
allows us to estimate all quantiles of
\theta
simultaneously.

Various improvements to Theorem
2.1
are possible. First, the dependence on
\delta
could be improved by replacing the use of Markov’s inequality in Lemma
2.8
with an exponential concentration inequality. In addition, the result could be extended. Suppose that instead of estimating the CDF of a single functional
\theta
, we care about a family of functionals
\{\theta_{s}\}_{s\in\mathcal{S}}
. It is likely that empirical process theory could be used to bound

\sup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}}\left|\hat{\pi}_{n}(\theta_{s}\leq x)-\pi(\theta_{s}\leq x)\right|

under appropriate conditions on
\mathcal{S}
. In a different direction, we could hope for a bound that is uniform over all possible choices of
n
, as has been achieved in
[
11
]
for the classical DKW inequality.

A fundamental limitation of the techniques in this paper is that they apply only to regenerative chains. What can be said about Markov chains for which the
a priori
convergence bound comes in some other form, such as a bound on the spectral gap? Theorem
2.1
leverages the sample path
(X_{t})
into structural information about the Markov chain by counting regenerations. In what other ways does the sample path carry information about the chain?

The ultimate goal of results like Theorem
2.1
is to provide numerical bounds that are tight enough to be practically useful. The example discussed in Section
1.4
shows the limitations of the regenerative approach: in the absence of additional conditions, the bounds given by Theorem
2.1
cannot be substantially improved. What extra conditions could we impose that would lead to tighter bounds, and could those conditions be verified for chains of practical interest?

2.
Main result

In this section, we state our main result, Theorem
2.1
, in precise form. We then split the proof into a series of lemmas. Section
3
proves the lemmas to complete the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Instead of running the Markov chain
(X_{t})
for a fixed number of steps
n
and letting
K
be the number of regenerations during that time, we run the chain for a fixed number of regenerations
K
and let
n
be the total number of steps. In other words, in Theorem
2.1
,
K
is fixed and
n
is random. This makes very little difference from the point of view of practical implementation and simplifies the proof.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a discrete time Markov chain on the state space
\mathcal{X}
with stationary distribution
\pi
, and let
\theta:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbf{R}
be any observable quantity associated with the chain. For some fixed probability measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
, assume that
(X_{t})
is equipped with a sequence
0=T_{0},T_{1},T_{2},\ldots
of
\nu
-regeneration times such that the tours
(X_{t})_{T_{k-1}\leq t<T_{k}}
are iid. (In particular, this means that
X_{0}\sim\nu
.) Write
T=T_{1}
, and assume that

\mathbf{P}(T>t)\leq Be^{-\gamma t}\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0

for some fixed positive constants
B,\gamma
.

Fix an integer
K\geq 1
, and let
n=T_{K}
be the
K
-th regeneration time. The first
K
tour lengths are
W_{k}=T_{k}-T_{k-1}
for
1\leq k\leq K
. Let

M=\max(W_{1},\ldots,W_{K})

be the maximum of these tour lengths. Also, for each integer
j\geq 0
, let

m_{j}=\#\{1\leq k\leq K:W_{k}>j\}

be the number of tour lengths that exceed
j
.

Let

\hat{\pi}_{n}(\theta\leq x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathbf{1}\{\theta(X_{t})\leq x\}

be the empirical cumulative distribution function of
\theta
given the sample path
(X_{0},\ldots,X_{n-1})
. Then, for every
\delta>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
we have

\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}}\left|\hat{\pi}_{n}(\theta\leq x)-\pi(\theta\leq x)\right|\\
\begin{aligned} &\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\sqrt{\frac{m_{j}}{n}\log\left(\frac{6n}{\delta m_{j}}\right)\wedge\frac{2m_{j}^{2}}{n}}+\frac{\sqrt{K}}{n}\cdot\frac{M}{\sqrt{\delta/3}}\\
&\qquad+\frac{2}{n\sqrt{\delta/3}}\left(\frac{\log(BK)}{\gamma}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)}+\frac{1}{1-e^{-\gamma/2}}\right).\end{aligned}

2.1.
Size of the upper bound

Before proving Theorem
2.1
, we explain why the upper bound has the form promised in (
1.2
). First, the following bound is a direct consequence of Jensen’s inequality.

Lemma 2.2
.

In the context of Theorem
2.1
,

\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\sqrt{\frac{m_{j}}{n}\log\left(\frac{6n}{\delta m_{j}}\right)\wedge\frac{2m_{j}^{2}}{n}}\leq\sqrt{M\log\left(\frac{6M}{\delta}\right)}.

From Lemma
2.2
it follows that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\sqrt{\frac{m_{j}}{n}\log\left(\frac{6n}{\delta m_{j}}\right)\wedge\frac{2m_{j}^{2}}{n}}+\frac{\sqrt{K}}{n}\cdot\frac{M}{\sqrt{\delta/3}}\\
\leq\frac{M}{\sqrt{n}}\left[\sqrt{\frac{1}{2M}\log\left(\frac{6M}{\delta}\right)}+\frac{\sqrt{K}}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta/3}}\right].

We have
M=\max(W_{1},\ldots,W_{K})
, where the tour lengths
W_{k}
are iid with an exponentially decaying tail

\mathbf{P}(W_{k}>t)\leq Be^{-\gamma t}.

Thus,
M
has size
\log(K)
, which is the same order of magnitude as
\log(n)
since
n/K
approaches
\mathbf{E}(T)
as
n,K\to\infty
. The bound

\frac{M}{\sqrt{n}}\left[\sqrt{\frac{1}{2M}\log\left(\frac{6M}{\delta}\right)}+\frac{\sqrt{K}}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta/3}}\right]

therefore has the desired form

\frac{\log(n)}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot C_{1}(X,\delta)

from (
1.2
).

The lower-order term in Theorem
2.1
,

(2.1)

\frac{2}{n\sqrt{\delta/3}}\left(\frac{\log(BK)}{\gamma}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)}+\frac{1}{1-e^{-\gamma/2}}\right),

has the form

\frac{\log(n)}{n}\cdot C_{2}(B,\gamma,\delta)

from (
1.2
), since again
\log(K)
and
\log(n)
have the same order of magnitude.

\log(n)
from the term in parentheses and then take a supremum over
K

.
Thus, we have verified the form (
1.2
) of Theorem
2.1
.

It is also claimed in Section
1.4
that the lower-order term (
2.1
) equals

\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{\log(BK)}{\gamma}

up to a multiplicative constant depending on
\delta
, in the regime where

(2.2)

\frac{\log(BK)}{\gamma}\geq 1.

This comes down to the statement that

(2.3)

\frac{1}{1-e^{-\gamma/2}}\leq c\cdot\frac{\log(BK)}{\gamma}

for some universal constant
c
. Indeed, if
\gamma\geq 2
then the left side is at most
1/(1-e^{-1})
, so we can take
c=1/(1-e^{-1})
and invoke (
2.2
). If
\gamma\leq 2
then we use the inequality

1-e^{-\gamma/2}\geq\frac{1-e^{-1}}{2}\cdot\gamma\qquad(0\leq\gamma\leq 2),

which holds because the left side is concave and the endpoints match, to choose
c=2/(1-e^{-1})
. Thus, we have verified (
2.3
). We conclude that in the regime where (
2.2
) holds, the term (
2.1
) equals

\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{\log(BK)}{\gamma}

up to a multiplicative constant depending on
\delta
.

2.2.
Proof of Theorem
2.1

We follow the outline laid out in Section
1.3
. For each
i>0
, set
p_{i}=\mathbf{P}(T=i)
. Also, for each
j\geq 0
, set

P_{j}=\mathbf{P}(T>j)=\sum_{i>j}p_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad q_{j}=\frac{P_{j}}{\mathbf{E}(T)}.

We have

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}q_{j}=1.

For each
j\geq 0
, let

\pi_{j}(\cdot)=\mathbf{P}(X_{j}\in\cdot\mid T>j)

be the conditional law of
X_{t}
given that the chain has taken
j
steps since the most recent regeneration. We have the decomposition of measures

\pi=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}q_{j}\pi_{j},

which gives a decomposition of CDFs

(2.4)

\pi(\theta\leq x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}q_{j}\pi_{j}(\theta\leq x).

We now find an analogue of (
2.4
) for the empirical distribution of
\theta
. For each
j\geq 0
, let

S_{j}=\{T_{k-1}+j:1\leq k\leq K,\,T_{k-1}+j<T_{k}\}

be the set of times that occur exactly
j
steps after a regeneration and before the next regeneration. We observe that
S_{j}
contains a time in the
k
-th tour if and only if the tour length satisfies
W_{k}>j
. Thus, each
|S_{j}|=m_{j}
.

Since the maximum tour length is
M
, we have
m_{M-1}>0
and
m_{M}=0
. The sets
S_{j}
form a partition of
\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
, so

m_{0}+m_{1}+\cdots+m_{M-1}=n.

Write the elements of
S_{j}
in increasing order as

S_{j}=\{t_{1}^{(j)},t_{2}^{(j)},\ldots,t_{m_{j}}^{(j)}\}\qquad(t_{1}^{(j)}<t_{2}^{(j)}<\cdots<t_{m_{j}}^{(j)}).

The samples
X_{t_{1}^{(j)}},\ldots,X_{t_{m_{j}}^{(j)}}
are iid with distribution
\pi_{j}
, due to independence of the tours.

Denote the empirical CDF of
\theta
by

\hat{F}(x)=\hat{\pi}_{n}(\theta\leq x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathbf{1}\{\theta(X_{t})\leq x\}

and, for each
0\leq j\leq M-1
,

\hat{F}_{j}(x)=\frac{1}{m_{j}}\sum_{t\in S_{j}}\mathbf{1}\{\theta(X_{t})\leq x\}.

Then, we have the decomposition

(2.5)

\hat{F}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\frac{m_{j}}{n}\hat{F}_{j}(x).

Notice that the decompositions (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) have different weights.

We use (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) to obtain the following bound.

Lemma 2.3
.

\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}}\left|\hat{F}(x)-\pi(\theta\leq x)\right|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\frac{m_{j}}{n}\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}}\left|\hat{F}_{j}(x)-\pi_{j}(\theta\leq x)\right|+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|\frac{m_{j}}{n}-q_{j}\right|.

The first sum on the right side of Lemma
2.3
represents the errors in learning the distribution of
\theta
under each
\pi_{j}
. Since each
\hat{F}_{j}(x)
is computed using iid samples from
\pi_{j}
, we can use the classical DKW inequality to prove the bound below:

Lemma 2.4
.

With probability at least
1-\delta/3
,

\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\frac{m_{j}}{n}\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}}\left|\hat{F}_{j}(x)-\pi_{j}(\theta\leq x)\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\sqrt{\frac{m_{j}}{n}\log\left(\frac{6n}{\delta m_{j}}\right)\wedge\frac{2m_{j}^{2}}{n}}.

The second sum on the right side of Lemma
2.3
represents the error in learning the tour length distribution. For
i>0
and
j\geq 0
, we have already defined
p_{i}=\mathbf{P}(T=i)
and

P_{j}=\mathbf{P}(T>j)=\sum_{i>j}p_{i}.

Now, define the empirical frequencies

\hat{p}_{i}=\frac{\#\{1\leq k\leq K:W_{k}=i\}}{K},\qquad\hat{P}_{j}=\sum_{i>j}\hat{p}_{i}=\frac{m_{j}}{K}.

Also, define the CDF and empirical CDF by

G(x)=\sum_{i\leq x}p_{i},\qquad\hat{G}(x)=\sum_{i\leq x}\hat{p}_{i}.

The 1-Wasserstein distance between the distributions
\hat{p}=(\hat{p}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
and
p=(p_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is given by

W_{1}(\hat{p},p)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\hat{G}(x)-G(x)|\,dx=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|\hat{G}(j)-G(j)|=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|\hat{P}_{j}-P_{j}|.

It turns out that the second sum on the right side of Lemma
2.3
is bounded above in terms of this distance.

Lemma 2.5
.

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|\frac{m_{j}}{n}-q_{j}\right|\leq\frac{2K}{n}W_{1}(\hat{p},p).

To control
W_{1}(\hat{p},p)
, we first use a standard argument
[
3
, Theorem 3.2]
to show that:

Lemma 2.6
.

\sqrt{\mathbf{E}(W_{1}^{2}(\hat{p},p))}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{P_{j}(1-P_{j})}.

Next, we split the sum on the right side of Lemma
2.6
into two pieces:

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{P_{j}(1-P_{j})}=\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\sqrt{P_{j}(1-P_{j})}+\sum_{j=M}^{\infty}\sqrt{P_{j}(1-P_{j})}.

As each
P_{j}(1-P_{j})\leq 1/4
, we have

(2.6)

\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\sqrt{P_{j}(1-P_{j})}\leq\frac{M}{2}.

Since we observed
K
independent tour lengths with a maximum length of
M
, we can say with high probability that tours of length greater than
M
are rare. In addition, we have the exponential bound

P_{j}=\mathbf{P}(T>j)\leq Be^{-\gamma j}.

Combining these two pieces of information yields the following bound.

Lemma 2.7
.

With probability at least
1-\delta/3
,

\sum_{j=M}^{\infty}\sqrt{P_{j}(1-P_{j})}\leq\frac{\log(BK)}{\gamma\sqrt{K}}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-e^{-\gamma/2}}.

When we plug (
2.6
) and Lemma
2.7
into the right side of Lemma
2.6
, we get the following statement. With probability at least
1-\delta/3
,

(2.7)

\sqrt{\mathbf{E}(W_{1}^{2}(\hat{p},p))}\leq\frac{M}{2\sqrt{K}}+\frac{1}{K}\left(\frac{\log(BK)}{\gamma}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)}+\frac{1}{1-e^{-\gamma/2}}\right).

At this point, the next step should be to apply an exponential concentration bound for
W_{1}(\hat{p},p)
around its mean. There are various results in this direction such as
[
3
, Theorem 7.1]
and
[
4
,
8
,
9
]
, but they are not completely satisfactory. A natural idea is to use an extension of McDiarmid’s inequality that allows for some unbounded differences. Here, too, there is an established literature
[
5
,
17
]
, but it is difficult to apply in our situation. It might be possible to use the extension of McDiarmid’s inequality in
[
24
]
.

Since we could not manage to invoke an exponential concentration inequality for
W_{1}(\hat{p},p)
, we instead use Markov’s inequality. This results in a bound with suboptimal dependence on
\delta
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
Z
be any nonnegative random variable. With probability at least
1-\delta/3
,

Z\leq\frac{\sqrt{\mathbf{E}(Z^{2})}}{\sqrt{\delta/3}}.

We apply Lemma
2.8
with
Z=W_{1}(\hat{p},p)
. Using (
2.7
) and a union bound, we obtain the following statement. With probability at least
1-2\delta/3
,

W_{1}(\hat{p},p)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta/3}}\cdot\frac{M}{2\sqrt{K}}+\frac{1}{K\sqrt{\delta/3}}\left(\frac{\log(BK)}{\gamma}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)}+\frac{1}{1-e^{-\gamma/2}}\right).

Now, we plug this bound into Lemma
2.5
. With probability at least
1-2\delta/3
,

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|\frac{m_{j}}{n}-q_{j}\right|\\
\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta/3}}\cdot\frac{M\sqrt{K}}{n}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta/3}}\cdot\frac{2}{n}\left(\frac{\log(BK)}{\gamma}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)}+\frac{1}{1-e^{-\gamma/2}}\right).

We combine with Lemma
2.4
and use another union bound. With probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\frac{m_{j}}{n}\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}}\left|\hat{F}_{j}(x)-\pi_{j}(\theta\leq x)\right|+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|\frac{m_{j}}{n}-q_{j}\right|\\
\begin{aligned} &\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\sqrt{\frac{m_{j}}{n}\log\left(\frac{6n}{\delta m_{j}}\right)\wedge\frac{2m_{j}^{2}}{n}}+\frac{\sqrt{K}}{n}\cdot\frac{M}{\sqrt{\delta/3}}\\
&\qquad+\frac{2}{n\sqrt{\delta/3}}\left(\frac{\log(BK)}{\gamma}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)}+\frac{1}{1-e^{-\gamma/2}}\right).\end{aligned}

The proof of Theorem
2.1
is finished by invoking Lemma
2.3
. ∎

3.
Proofs of lemmas

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

Let

f(x)=\sqrt{x\log\left(\frac{6}{\delta x}\right)}.

A direct computation shows that this function is concave on
(0,1)
. We have

\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\sqrt{\frac{m_{j}}{n}\log\left(\frac{6n}{\delta m_{j}}\right)\wedge\frac{2m_{j}^{2}}{n}}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\sqrt{\frac{m_{j}}{n}\log\left(\frac{6n}{\delta m_{j}}\right)}=\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}f\left(\frac{m_{j}}{n}\right).

Since
f
is concave on
(0,1)
, for
x_{0},\ldots,x_{M-1}\in(0,1)
we have by Jensen’s inequality

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}f(x_{j})\leq f\left(\frac{x_{0}+\cdots+x_{M-1}}{M}\right).

Plugging in
x_{j}=m_{j}/n
, we observe that
x_{0}+\cdots+x_{M-1}=1
. Thus,

\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}f\left(\frac{m_{j}}{n}\right)\leq Mf\left(\frac{1}{M}\right)=\sqrt{M\log\left(\frac{6M}{\delta}\right)}.\qed

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

We compute using (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) that

\begin{split}\hat{F}(x)-\pi(\theta\leq x)&=\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\frac{m_{j}}{n}\hat{F}_{j}(x)-\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}q_{j}\pi_{j}(\theta\leq x)\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\frac{m_{j}}{n}[\hat{F}_{j}(x)-\pi_{j}(\theta\leq x)]+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{m_{j}}{n}-q_{j}\right]\pi_{j}(\theta\leq x),\end{split}

recalling that
m_{j}=0
for
j\geq M
. It follows that

\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}}|\hat{F}(x)-\pi(\theta\leq x)|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\frac{m_{j}}{n}\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}}|\hat{F}_{j}(x)-\pi_{j}(\theta\leq x)|+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|\frac{m_{j}}{n}-q_{j}\right|.

∎

Proof of Lemma
2.4
.

To bound the sum

\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\frac{m_{j}}{n}\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}}\left|\hat{F}_{j}(x)-\pi_{j}(\theta\leq x)\right|,

we use the classical DKW inequality for iid data
[
7
,
15
,
22
]
separately for each
j
. Given any constants
\varepsilon_{j}>0
, the DKW inequality says that for each
j
,

\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}}\left|\hat{F}_{j}(x)-\pi_{j}(\theta\leq x)\right|\leq\varepsilon_{j}

with probability at least
1-2\exp(-2m_{j}\varepsilon_{j}^{2})
. Of course, if we happen to choose
\varepsilon_{j}\geq 1
, it also holds that

\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}}\left|\hat{F}_{j}(x)-\pi_{j}(\theta\leq x)\right|\leq 1.

So, we can say that

(3.1)

\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}}\left|\hat{F}_{j}(x)-\pi_{j}(\theta\leq x)\right|\leq\varepsilon_{j}\wedge 1

with probability at least
1-2\exp(-2m_{j}\varepsilon_{j}^{2})
.

We will set

\varepsilon_{j}^{2}=\frac{1}{2m_{j}}\log\left(\frac{6n}{\delta m_{j}}\right),

so that

2\exp(-2m_{j}\varepsilon_{j}^{2})=\frac{\delta m_{j}}{3n}.

By a union bound, (
3.1
) holds for all
0\leq j\leq M-1
with probability at least

1-\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\frac{\delta m_{j}}{3n}=1-\frac{\delta}{3}.

This gives us

\begin{split}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\frac{m_{j}}{n}\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}}\left|\hat{F}_{j}(x)-\pi_{j}(\theta\leq x)\right|&\leq\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\frac{m_{j}}{n}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2m_{j}}}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{6n}{\delta m_{j}}\right)}\wedge 1\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\sqrt{\frac{m_{j}}{n}\log\left(\frac{6n}{\delta m_{j}}\right)\wedge\frac{2m_{j}^{2}}{n}}.\end{split}

∎

Proof of Lemma
2.5
.

We know that

q_{j}=\frac{P_{j}}{\mathbf{E}(T)},\qquad\frac{m_{j}}{n}=\frac{m_{j}/K}{n/K}=\frac{\hat{P}_{j}}{n/K}.

Thus,

\begin{split}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|\frac{m_{j}}{n}-q_{j}\right|&=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|\frac{\hat{P}_{j}}{n/K}-\frac{P_{j}}{\mathbf{E}(T)}\right|\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{n/K}\left(\hat{P}_{j}-P_{j}\right)+P_{j}\left(\frac{1}{n/K}-\frac{1}{\mathbf{E}(T)}\right)\right|\\
&\leq\frac{K}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|\hat{P}_{j}-P_{j}\right|+\left|\frac{1}{n/K}-\frac{1}{\mathbf{E}(T)}\right|\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}P_{j}.\end{split}

Since

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}P_{j}=\mathbf{E}(T)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\hat{P}_{j}=\frac{n}{K},

we have

\begin{split}\left|\frac{1}{n/K}-\frac{1}{\mathbf{E}(T)}\right|\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}P_{j}&=\left|\frac{1}{n/K}-\frac{1}{\mathbf{E}(T)}\right|\mathbf{E}(T)=\frac{1}{n/K}\left|\mathbf{E}(T)-\frac{n}{K}\right|\\
&=\frac{K}{n}\left|\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}P_{j}-\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\hat{P}_{j}\right|\leq\frac{K}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|P_{j}-\hat{P}_{j}\right|.\end{split}

It follows that

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|\frac{m_{j}}{n}-q_{j}\right|\leq\frac{2K}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|\hat{P}_{j}-P_{j}\right|=\frac{2K}{n}W_{1}(\hat{p},p).\qed

Proof of Lemma
2.6
.

Following the argument in
[
3
, Theorem 3.2]
, we write

W_{1}(\hat{p},p)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|\hat{P}_{j}-P_{j}|.

By the triangle inequality in
L^{2}
,

\sqrt{\mathbf{E}(W_{1}^{2}(\hat{p},p))}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\mathbf{E}(|\hat{P}_{j}-P_{j}|^{2})}.

Each
\hat{P}_{j}
is a random variable whose expected value is
P_{j}
. Thus,

\mathbf{E}(|\hat{P}_{j}-P_{j}|^{2})=\Var(\hat{P}_{j}).

We know that
\hat{P}_{j}=m_{j}/K
, where

m_{j}\sim\textrm{Binomial}(K,P_{j}),

since each tour length has an independent
P_{j}
chance to be longer than
j
. Therefore,

\Var(\hat{P}_{j})=\frac{P_{j}(1-P_{j})}{K}.

We conclude that

\sqrt{\mathbf{E}(W_{1}^{2}(\hat{p},p))}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\Var(\hat{P}_{j})}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{P_{j}(1-P_{j})}.\qed

Proof of Lemma
2.7
.

We first observe that
P_{M}
should be small with high probability, because
M
was the maximum tour length among
K
independent samples. Let us imagine a selection process for
M
based on auxiliary independent
\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)
random variables
U_{1},\ldots,U_{K}
. Namely, after sampling each
U_{k}
, the length
W_{k}
of the
k
-th tour is chosen by taking the
U_{k}
-th quantile of the tour length distribution. For each
0<\beta<1
,

\mathbf{P}(\max(U_{1},\ldots,U_{K})\leq 1-\beta)=(1-\beta)^{K}.

We choose

\beta=\frac{1}{K}\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)

so that

(1-\beta)^{K}\leq e^{-\beta K}=\frac{\delta}{3}.

With probability at least
1-\delta/3
,
\max(U_{1},\ldots,U_{K})
is at least
1-\beta
. This means that an independently chosen sample from the tour length distribution has probability at most
\beta
to exceed
M
. In other words,

(3.2)

P_{M}\leq\beta=\frac{1}{K}\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right),

and the same upper bound applies to all
P_{j}
with
j\geq M
.

In addition, we know that for all
j
,

P_{j}=\mathbf{P}(T>j)\leq Be^{-\gamma j}.

For large enough
j
, this bound eventually is tighter than (
3.2
). Define

J=\frac{1}{\gamma}\log(BK),

so that

Be^{-\gamma J}=\frac{1}{K}\leq\frac{1}{K}\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right).

We will use the bounds

P_{j}\leq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{K}\log(3/\delta)&\text{if }M\leq j<J,\\
Be^{-\gamma j}&\text{if }j\geq J.\end{cases}

It follows that

\begin{split}\sum_{j=M}^{\infty}\sqrt{P_{j}(1-P_{j})}&\leq\sum_{M\leq j<J}\sqrt{P_{j}}+\sum_{j\geq J}\sqrt{P_{j}}\\
&\leq\sum_{M\leq j<J}\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)}+\sum_{j\geq J}\sqrt{B}e^{-\gamma j/2}\\
&\leq\frac{J}{\sqrt{K}}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)}+\sqrt{B}e^{-\gamma J/2}\sum_{j\geq J}e^{-\gamma(j-J)/2}\\
&\leq\frac{\log(BK)}{\gamma}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)}+\sqrt{Be^{-\gamma J}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-e^{-\gamma/2}}\\
&=\frac{\log(BK)}{\gamma\sqrt{K}}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-e^{-\gamma/2}},\end{split}

where we used in the last line that
Be^{-\gamma J}=1/K
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.8
.

Apply Markov’s inequality to
Z^{2}
,

\mathbf{P}(Z^{2}\geq a)\leq\frac{\mathbf{E}(Z^{2})}{a},

with

a=\frac{\mathbf{E}(Z^{2})}{\delta/3}

to obtain

\mathbf{P}\left(Z\geq\frac{\sqrt{\mathbf{E}(Z^{2})}}{\sqrt{\delta/3}}\right)=\mathbf{P}\left(Z^{2}\geq\frac{\mathbf{E}(Z^{2})}{\delta/3}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbf{E}(Z^{2})}{\mathbf{E}(Z^{2})/(\frac{\delta}{3})}=\frac{\delta}{3}.

It follows that

Z\leq\frac{\sqrt{\mathbf{E}(Z^{2})}}{\sqrt{\delta/3}}

with probability at least
1-\delta/3
.
∎
