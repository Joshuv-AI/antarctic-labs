Interaction between CD82 and integrin αVβ3 selectively regulates collective movement of tumor cells via endolysosomal trafficking
Authors: Xue Jun Wang, Mekel M. Richardson, Yingjun Ding, Chao Jiang, Songlan Liu, Bin Zhou, Shuping Li, Junxiong Chen, Eric Rubinstein, Franklin A. Hays, Kamiya Mehla, Xin A. Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tetraspanin CD82/KAI1 inhibits cell movement and metastasis of malignant tumors, and reduced and lost expressions of CD82 predict worse outcomes of patients with malignant tumors. Here we found that CD82 inhibits both solitary and collective movement of tumor cells. The CD82 YVAA mutation, which affects CD82 trafficking, selectively abrogates CD82-mediated inhibition of collective migration. Cilengitide, at the concentration that specifically inhibits integrin αVβ3, also selectively blocks collective movement, underscoring a promotive role of integrin αVβ3 in this mode of cell motility. In contrast, integrin αVβ5 appears non-essential for collective migration, and both αVβ3 and αVβ5 are dispensable for solitary movement on fibronectin, highlighting distinct functions of different integrins in different modes of tumor cell movement. CD82 interacts with αVβ3 and αVβ5 integrins and downregulates their protein levels, while CD82 YVAA mutation relinquishes this downregulation without disrupting CD82 interactions with these integrins. Mechanistically, CD82, but not the YVAA mutant, considerably reduces digitation junction—the structure where integrin αVβ3 localizes—and likely directs integrin αVβ3 for lysosomal degradation, thereby lowering its level and suppressing collective migration. Thus, our study reveals that i) integrin αVβ3 promotes collective movement of tumor cells, ii) CD82 counteracts this by diminishing integrin αVβ3 and its presence in microextrusions, and iii) digitation junction likely participates in collective cell movement. Our study further demonstrates that endolysosomal trafficking of CD82 and integrin αVβ3 is needed for their collective movement-regulatory activities and that coupling of metastasis suppressor CD82/KAI1 with different partners regulates different modes of cell movement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06303-0.

Introduction

Collective cell movement refers to the coordinated locomotion of cells as cohort, while solitary cell movement describes the migration of individual cells in an isolated manner [1, 2]. Although it is well-known that collective cell movement is involved in development, wound healing, and tumor invasion and metastasis, the molecular mechanisms underlying coordinated cellular locomotion remain largely uncharacterized [3, 4]. In recent years, the significance of collective movement in tumor progression has become increasingly appreciated, but how to perturb collective movement of tumor cells for clinical benefit also remains unclear [5, 6].

The membrane-spanning protein CD82, a member of the tetraspanin superfamily, functions as a metastasis suppressor in a wide spectrum of malignant tumors [7–9]. Inhibition of tumor cell movement is a key, if not the, mechanism for CD82 to suppress metastasis [9]. CD82 inhibits multiple locomotive modes of tumor cells including solitary cell migration (e.g. Transwell cell migration), collective cell migration (e.g. wound healing), and cell invasion (e.g. infiltration through three-dimensional matrices) [10]. Previous studies have demonstrated that CD82 inhibits cell movement by downregulating the activities of associated membrane molecules such as growth factor receptors and integrins [11]. These effects result from CD82-mediated disengagement of the plasma membrane (PM) from sub-membranous actin cytoskeleton, compartmentalization of cell membrane, and/or modulation of vesicular trafficking and secretion [11].

Notably, CD82 is a highly glycosylated membrane protein, and this post-translational modification is important for CD82-mediated inhibition of cellular motility [12–15]. Palmitoylation is another form of CD82 post-translational modification and also required for the migration- and invasion-inhibitory activities of CD82 [15, 16]. Phosphorylation of CD82 has not been reported, despite the presence of residues eligible for phosphorylation in cytoplasmic side of CD82. CD82 contains a tyrosine-based motif (YXXΦ, where X is any amino acid residues and Φ represents amino acid residues with a bulky hydrophobic side chain), in its C-terminal cytoplasmic domain. A well-known function of this motif is to regulate endolysosomal trafficking. By generating and utilizing the mutant of this motif in CD82 or CD82 YVAA mutant, we found herein that this motif is needed for proper trafficking of CD82 to endosomes and lysosomes and for cell movement-inhibitory activity of CD82. Hence, this YVAA mutant served as a valuable tool for evaluating the role of CD82 trafficking in its mediated movement-inhibitory and metastasis-suppressive functions.

Microextrusions between tumor cells, also referred to as digitation junctions (DJs), mediate dynamic interactions between cells [17]. We predict that DJ participates in collective cell movement, although their precise role in this movement mode remains to be fully characterized. Our observations in this study, including that i) the YVAA mutant abrogates the inhibitory effect of CD82 on DJs and ii) integrin αVβ3 is present in DJ and promotes collective movement, support this prediction. In addition, this study also reveals several novel elements that regulate collective movement of tumor cells: i) an inhibitory effect of CD82, ii) the importance of YXXΦ trafficking motif for this inhibitory effect, iii) a supportive role of integrin αVβ3, iv)the contribution of dynamic interactions between moving cells, and v) the involvement of endolysosomal trafficking and turnover.

Results

Establishment of the wild type and mutant CD82 stable transfectants

A YXXΦ-sorting motif is located at the C-terminal cytoplasmic domain of CD82 and conserved in CD82 proteins in vertebrates (Fig. 1A). This motif is absent in CD82 homologues from invertebrates such as fruit fly (Homologous Gene Database, https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/hgd). To determine the role of this YXXΦ motif in CD82 function, we mutated the Y261SKV264 sequence in human CD82 to A261SKA264sequence by replacing crucial Y and Φ residues of this motif with A residues (Fig. 1B), referred to as YVAA mutation hereafter, and established the stable transfectants of Mock, CD82 WT, and CD82 YVAA mutant in PC3 and Du145 human metastatic prostate cancer cells. PC3 and Du145 cells do not or barely express endogenous CD82 [16, 18, 19]. After antibiotic selection, clonal pooling, and flow cytometric sorting, PC3-CD82 WT and PC3-CD82 YVAA transfectants express similar levels of total cellular CD82 proteins with a trend of more CD82 proteins in PC3-CD82 YVAA transfectants, based on Western blot (Fig. 1C), while more CD82 YVAA mutant proteins are located at the cell surface, based on flow cytometry (Figs. 1D and S1). For Du145 transfectants, Western blot and flow cytometry showed that Du145-CD82 WT and Du145-CD82 YVAA mutant transfectant cells displayed equivalent levels of both total cellular and cell surface CD82 proteins (Figs. 1C, D, and S1). CD82 can be located at the cell surface and intracellular vesicles [20, 21]. Because our previous results have demonstrated that the CD82 palmitoylation-deficient mutant displays altered subcellular distribution [16], we carried out the confocal microscopy to address whether the YXXΦ motif plays a role in the subcellular distribution of CD82. Our results indicated that the YVAA mutation did not significantly affect the steady-state subcellular distribution of CD82 proteins (Figure S2A). But we found that forced expression of CD82 decreased the number of microextrusions or microspikes, the elongated, thin, and outward tubular structure of the plasma membrane [22], in both PC3 and Du145 cells, while the YVAA mutant did not exhibit such an effect (Figure S2B). The diminished microextrusions in the CD82 WT transfectant cells appear to distribute more frequently at each end of spindle-like cells. Because microextrusions between adjacent cells or DJs are involved in dynamic cell–cell interactions [17], reduction in microextrusions likely affects the biological events that require dynamic cell–cell interaction, such as collective cell movement. As shown in earlier studies, forced expression of CD82 altered cellular morphology [16, 18, 23], while the YVAA mutant lost the impact on cellular morphology (Figure S2A). We applied 1% Brij98 detergent to the cells for permeabilization. With detergent permeabilization, the antibody would access the intracellular pool of the antigen and thereby stained the antigen throughout the entire cell, while without detergent permeabilization, the antibody largely stained the antigen at the cell surface. Although, due to certain degree of membrane permeabilization during the fixation process, intracellular CD82 could also be observed in some cells from the group of “without 0.1% Brij98 permeabilization” (Figure S2A). Protein phosphorylation is one of the most important and well-studied post-translational modifications of proteins. Phosphorylation can alter the structural conformation of proteins and result in changes in their activities and functions. This modification principally occurs at serine, threonine, and tyrosine amino acid residues. To assess whether the tyrosine (Y) residue in the YXXΦ motif is a phosphorylation site of CD82, whole cell lysates of PC3 and Du145 transfectants were firstly immunoprecipitated with CD82 monoclonal antibody (mAb) TS82b and then blotted with phosphotyrosine mAb 4G10. There are no apparent differences in the TS82b immunoprecipitate profiles between Mock, CD82 WT, and CD82 YVAA cells of both PC3 and Du145 stable transfectants (Figure S3A and S3B), suggesting that i) CD82 is not tyrosine-phosphorylated and ii) the tyrosine residue in the YSKV motif is not a phosphorylation site, at least under basal conditions. We also examined MDA-MB-231 human breast cancer cell line which expresses endogenous CD82 [21]. MDA-MB-231 cell lysates were immunoprecipitated with phospho-p38 MAPK (Thr180&Tyr182) or CD82 antibody, followed by immunoblotting with CD82 mAb TS82b, phosphotyrosine mAb P-Tyr-100 and 4G10 (Figure S3C). CD82 proteins in MDA-MB-231 cell ranged between 35 ~ 50 kDa and can be detected by TS82b, but no signal was detected within this molecular weight range with the phosphotyrosine mAbs; while phospho-p38 MAPK was found to be tyrosine-phosphorylated and served as a positive control (Figure S3C). Hence, CD82 is not tyrosine-phosphorylated in MDA-MB-231 cells either.

The role of CD82 YXXΦ motif in CD82 trafficking

Because a function of YXXΦ motif is to regulate endolysosomal trafficking, we examined the role of this motif in CD82 endocytosis and recycling. Mutation of CD82 YXXΦ motif, i.e., CD82 YVAA mutation, didn’t markedly alter CD82 endocytosis in PC3 cells, assayed for 1-h internalization of CD82 mAb by immunofluorescence (Fig. 2A). If it is quantified by the number of intracellular vesicles containing the internalized CD82, CD82 endocytosis was elevated statistically in PC3-CD82 YVAA mutant cells, compared to PC3-CD82 WT cells; but if it is quantified by fluorescence intensity of the internalized CD82, CD82 endocytosis remained equivalent statistically between the two groups although CD82 YVAA mutant cells displayed a trend of elevation. Given a higher level of CD82 YVAA mutant expression at the PC3 cell surface (Fig. 1D), its endocytosis was unlikely increased and probably marginally decreased in PC3 cells (Fig. 2A). Indeed, after normalization by the cell surface levels of CD82 proteins on PC3 transfectants (Fig. 1D), the ratios of CD82 WT to CD82 YVAA mutant in terms of CD82 endocytosis were changed from 1.00:1.40 to 1.00:0.82, if endocytosis was quantified as the number of CD82-containing vesicles per cell, and from 1.00:1.46 to 1.00:0.86, by the quantification as the fluorescence intensity of CD82-positive vesicles per cell. To substantiate these findings, we also analyzed CD82 endocytosis in Du145 transfectants using the reversable biotinylation method [24], in which the reduction treatment removes the biotin from the uninternalized but biotinylated proteins at the cell surface, as illustrated in Fig. 2B. More internalized CD82 proteins were found in Du145-CD82 WT cells than in Du145-CD82 YVAA mutant cells for 60-min and 120-min endocytosis, and the internalization of CD82 WT proteins but not CD82 YVAA mutant proteins reached plateau after 60-min endocytosis (Fig. 2B), suggesting that YVAA mutation diminishes and slows down CD82 endocytosis in Du145 transfectant cells.

We then examined the role of this YXXΦ motif in recycling of CD82 back to the PM. For PC3 transfectants, significantly more CD82 proteins were retained intracellularly in CD82 YVAA mutant cells than in CD82 WT cells after 3-h recycling (Fig. 2C), denoting a slower pace of CD82 recycling in the mutant cells and suggesting that CD82 needs the YXXΦ motif for its efficient recycling in PC3 cells. An alternative explanation is that the YXXΦ motif supports CD82 trafficking to lysosome for degradation, while the YVAA mutation results in less degradation of CD82 proteins and then more cellular accumulation of CD82. We also performed the same recycling experiments with Du145 transfectants, which recapitulated PC3 transfectants in CD82 recycling (Fig. 2C), and reached the same conclusion.

The YXXΦ motif is indispensable for CD82-mediated inhibitions of solitary and collective cell migrations

Second, we used Transwell migration assay to evaluate solitary cell migration. CD82 WT expression in PC3 and Du145 cells markedly reduced migration onto fibronectin and laminin-111, compared to Mock groups, indicating that CD82 inhibits solitary cell migration (Figs. 3B and S4). These results are consistent with earlier studies using the same or other tumor cells [16, 29, 30]. Du145-CD82 YVAA mutant cells showed even greater solitary migration than Du145-Mock cells, while PC3-CD82 YVAA mutant cells displayed the reduced solitary migration compared to PC3-Mock cells but approximately 4.1-fold increase in solitary migration onto fibronectin and 2.7-fold increase onto laminin-111, compared to PC3-CD82 WT cells (Figs. 3B and S4). These results indicate that, for the inhibitory activity of CD82 on solitary migration, the YXXΦ motif is essential in Du145 cells and important in PC3 cells.

In summary, our results demonstrated that CD82 inhibits both solitary and collective cell movement in PC3 and Du145 cells, while CD82 YVAA mutant completely or partially lost these inhibitory abilities, underlining the importance of proper endolysosomal trafficking of CD82 in its migration-inhibitory activity.

CD82 down-regulates the expression levels of αV integrins and needs its YXXΦ motif for this downregulation

It is reported that integrin αV is involved in prostate cancer cell migration and invasion, and our previous study demonstrated that the ablation of Cd82 in mice up-regulates the surface level of integrin αV on endothelial cells [31–34]. So, we examined the total cellular and cell surface levels of αV integrins by Western blot and flow cytometry, respectively. Overexpression of CD82 WT in PC3 and Du145 cells decreased the cell surface distribution of integrins αV proteins (Figs. 4A and S5), consistent with earlier observations from endothelial cells. CD82 YVAA mutation abolished or largely diminished these downregulation effects of CD82 WT (Figs. 4A and S5). Integrin αV subunit can pair with five integrin β subunits (β1, β3, β5, β6, or β8) to form heterodimers and play important roles in cell adhesion and migration. Flow cytometry analysis with mAbs of integrins αVβ3 (mAb LM609), αVβ5 (mAb P1F6), and αVβ6 (mAb 10D5) revealed their expression patterns similar to the one of integrin αV, i.e., ectopic expression of CD82 reduced the cell surface levels of these integrins while CD82 YVAA mutant didn’t (Fig. 4A). CD82 down-regulates the level of integrin αVβ3 protein to inhibit collective cell migration

To gain more mechanistic insight into the collective cell migration-inhibitory function of CD82 and to demonstrate whether integrins αVβ3 and/or αVβ5 participate in this process, we employed a potent and specific inhibitor of integrins αVβ3 and αVβ5, cilengitide, to address these issues. Cilengitide is a cyclized pentapeptide containing RGD motif and can block the binding of integrin αVβ3 and integrin αVβ5 to their ligands selectively with IC50 of 4.1 nM and 79 nM, respectively [35]. As shown in Fig. 5A, cilengitide at 4 nM only inhibited the collective cell migration of Du145-Mock cells by 19.79%, but cilengitide at 20 nM, which inhibits integrin αVβ3 largely or mainly, dramatically inhibited the collective cell migration of both Du145- and PC3-Mock cells. The inhibition rates of wound closure of the Mock, CD82 WT, and CD82 YVAA mutant cells for cilengitide at 20 nM were 44.19% (p = 2.30*10–6), 18.99% (p = 0.0434), and 38.89% (p = 3.43*10–5), respectively, in PC3 cells, while 45.50% (p = 3.96*10–5), 21.5% (p = 0.0158), and 20.7% (p = 5.40*10–4), respectively, in Du145 cells (Fig. 5A). Because CD82 undergoes endolysosomal trafficking [20], physical proximities of CD82 with these integrins suggest that they undergo endolysosomal trafficking together and CD82 modulates the trafficking of these integrins. Indeed, CD82 expression elevated the steady state colocalizations of integrins αVβ3 and αVβ5 with LysoTracker, a marker of lysosome or low pH vesicular compartment, reflected by greater Pearson colocalization coefficients (Fig. 7B), suggesting that CD82 promotes lysosomal trafficking of these integrins. In the YVAA mutant cells, the integrin-Lysotracker colocalizations at steady state became equivalent to the one in the Mock cells (Fig. 7B), suggesting that CD82 YVAA mutant cells lost the ability to enhance lysosomal trafficking of integrins αVβ3 and αVβ5. Interestingly, in Du145 cells, the subcellular distribution of integrin αVβ3 displayed a puncta and/or vesicle pattern while some αVβ5 are more frequently present in nascent adhesions/focal complexes and focal adhesions at the cell peripheries (Figure S7C). Such a subcellularly differential distribution between integrins αVβ3 and αVβ5 suggests that integrin αVβ3 undergoes relatively more vesicular trafficking compared to integrin αVβ5, while integrin αVβ5 more actively engages in cell–matrix adhesion.

We further investigated endocytosis of integrins αVβ3 and αVβ5 into lysosome. CD82 expression elevated the endocytosis of these integrins, while YVAA mutation abolished such elevation (Fig. 7C). Colocalizations of the internalized integrins αVβ3 and αVβ5 with CD63, a marker of lysosome, were also elevated by CD82 expression, and likewise the elevations were lost upon the YVAA mutation (Fig. 7C). Such changes are reflected not only by Pearson’s coefficients which report global colocalization but also by Manders 1 coefficients which report lysosomal internalized integrins normalized by or out of total internalized integrins (Fig. 7C), the latter suggesting the tendency or effectiveness of internalized αV integrin trafficking toward lysosome. Differential trafficking of integrins αVβ3 and αVβ5 to lysosome among Mock, CD82 WT, and CD82 YVAA mutant cells inversely correlates well with differential levels of these integrins at the cell surface of these three transfectants (Fig. 4A), underlining a link between lysosomal trafficking and cell surface presence of these integrins. The differential trafficking of αV integrins to lysosome also largely inversely correlates with the migration abilities of these transfectant cells (Fig. 3).

To substantiate the role of CD82 in turnover of αV integrins, we treated the transfectant cells with cycloheximide and pulse-chased the changes in integrin αV proteins. Only CD82 WT, not its YVAA mutant, accelerated the protein turnover of αV integrins (Fig. 7D), reflecting well their difference in lysosomal targeting (Fig. 7B and C). To confirm that lysosome is functionally important for CD82-meidated inhibition of cell movement, we assess the effect of lysosome inhibitor Bafilomycin on wound healing and found Bafilomycin markedly reduced the abilities for collective migration of all 3 transfectants, abolished the difference between Mock and CD82 WT transfectants, but could not abrogate the difference between CD82 YVAA and other transfectants (Fig. 7E), supporting the importance of lysosome function in i) collective migration and ii) CD82-mediated inhibition of collective migration.

Discussion

By analyzing tumor metastasis suppressor CD82 and its YVAA mutant, our study revealed novel elements that regulate collective movement of tumor cells: i) an inhibitory effect of CD82, ii) the importance of YXXΦ trafficking motif for CD82-mediated inhibitory effect, iii) differential roles of αV integrins, iv) contribution of DJs, v) involvement of endolysosomal trafficking, and vi) degradative function of lysosome.

Collective cell movement requires the moving cells, at least some of them, dynamically interact with each other to move as a group [41, 42]. So far, our understanding on the cell–cell interactions involved in collective cell movement is limited to traditional cell–cell junctions such as tight, adherens, and gap junctions and non-junctional mechanisms such as IgSFs and Ephrin/Eph receptor mediated cell–cell adhesion [41, 42]. Compared to traditional cell–cell junctions, DJ or the microextrusions between cells can naturally be a subcellular structure that makes two adjacent moving cells associate and interact with each other in a dynamic manner [17]. Although integrins are important for collective cell movement, contribution of integrins to the cell–cell interactions in collective cell movement is uncertain because integrins typically are matrix receptors. Also, the cell structure by which integrins engage cell–cell interaction is unknown. In DJs, integrins can conveniently engage both cell–matrix and cell–cell interactions, and the presence of integrin αVβ3 in DJs supports this notion. In addition, the inhibition of DJ by CD82 WT, loss of such inhibition by CD82 YVAA mutant, and no inhibition of DJ by Mock correlate well with the roles of these transfectants in collective movement, and link DJ to collective movement. Moreover, by taking advantage of differential sensitivity of αV integrins to cilengitide, we further elucidated that integrin αVβ3, rather than integrin αVβ5, promotes collective migration of tumor cells; while both of these integrins are not required for solitary cell migration onto fibronectin.

CD82 expression increases the endocytosis of membrane proteins like integrin α6, to reduce its presence at the cell surface, while Cd82 knockout decreases the endocytosis of membrane proteins like CD44, to enhance its presence at the cell surface [31, 43]. Thus, it’s not surprising to observe that αV integrins are downregulated at the cell surface upon CD82 WT expression. Indeed, CD82 expression also increases the endocytosis of αV integrins (Fig. 7C). In addition, the findings from this study support another layer of mechanism, i.e., more αV integrins traffic to lysosome for turnover in CD82 WT-expressing cells. The upregulated αV integrins at the cell surface of CD82 YVAA mutant, compared to the ones on CD82 WT cells, likely result from compromised lysosomal trafficking of this CD82 mutant (Fig. 2C and our unpublished data) and then less lysosomal targeting of its associated αV integrins (Fig. 7B and C). However, the caveat of this explanation is that it’s difficult to explain no marked increase, despite an upward trend, in total cellular level of αV integrin proteins in CD82 YVAA mutant cells, compared to that in CD82 WT cells (Fig. 4B). CD82 YVAA mutant is also possibly deficient in recycling (Fig. 2C), but less recycling of CD82 YVAA mutant cannot explain more presence of its associated αV integrins at the surface of CD82 YVAA mutant cells. The observation that bafilomycin cannot abolish the ability of CD82 YVAA mutant cells to migrate collectively (Fig. 7E) suggests that aberrant endolysosomal trafficking of CD82 in these mutant cells confers their high potency in wound healing. Comparable surface levels and endocytosis of αV integrins between Mock and CD82 YVAA mutant cells (Figs. 4A and 7C) supports the importance of maintaining the cell surface αV integrins by proper endolysosomal trafficking for cell movement.

Tetraspanins CD9 and CD151, together with integrin α3β1, were reported to promote collective cell migration of epithelial cells by interacting with cell–cell adhesion protein JAM-A through integrin α3β1 [44]. Hence, regulating collective cell movement could be a shared property of the tetraspanins able to express at the cell surface, especially given that the primordial function of tetraspanins is likely to be the modulation of cell–cell interaction as they emerge in evolution when multi-cellular eukaryotes occur [45, 46]. As dynamic interactions between cells determine the cell movement as cohort, it is predictable for the contribution of DJs, i.e., the microextrusions between cells, to CD82 inhibition of collective movement, given that microextrusions and DJs were diminished upon CD82 expression but enhanced upon Cd82 removal [17, 29, 31]. With CD82 YVAA mutant, this study further unfolds that proper endolysosomal trafficking of CD82 is crucial for CD82 inhibition of collective movement. CD82 YVAA mutant still possesses the ability to inhibit solitary cell migration, underlining the endolysosomal trafficking step(s) altered by this mutant is not crucial for solitary movement.

In conclusion, tetraspanin CD82 inhibits but integrin αVβ3 promotes collective movement of tumor cells. These contrast effects on collective movement are linked to i) the microextrusions between cells or DJs, ii) the presence of integrin αVβ3 on DJs, and iii) the endolysosomal trafficking and turnover of these membrane-spanning proteins as physically associated functional partners.

Methods

Antibodies and reagents

Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) used in this study were tetraspanin mAbs CD82 mAb TS82b [20] and FITC-conjugated CD63 mAb H5C6 (BioLegend); integrins mAbs including αV mAb P3G8 [47], αVβ3 mAbs LM609 (Millipore), 23C6 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), and BV3 (Abcam), αVβ5 mAbs P1F6 (Millipore), D24A5 (CST) and P5G2 [48], and αVβ6 mAb 10D5 (Millipore); and other mAbs including neuropilin-1 mAb M01324 (BOSTER), phospho-tyrosine mAb 4G10 (Sigma) and P-Tyr-100 (CST), phospho-p38 MAPK (Thr180/Tyr182) mAb D3F9 (CST), and β-actin mAb 2A3 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Polyclonal antibodies (pAbs) used in this study were integrin αV pAb 4711 (CST), integrin β3 pAb 18309–1 (Proteintech), rabbit IgG (Sigma), and murine IgG2b (Sigma). Other reagents are mitomycin c, cycloheximide, NP-40, Brij97, and Brij98 from Sigma, fibronectin, laminin 111, fluorochrome-conjugated secondary Abs from Caliper, and LysoTracker Red-DND99 from Invitrogen. Integrins αVβ3/αVβ5 inhibitor cilengitide was purchased from Selleckchem.

Cells and transfectants

PC3 (RRID:CVCL_0035) and Du145 (RRID:CVCL_0105) cells were obtained from ATCC, and the cells were cultured in DMEM, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 units/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and L-glutamine, at 37° in 5% CO2. CD82 YVAA (YSKV > > ASKA) mutant was generated by simultaneously replacing the tyrosine and valine residues in the YSKV sequence of CD82 C-terminal cytoplasmic domain with alanine residues through PCR. The full-length cDNAs encoding CD82 wild type and YVAA mutant were constructed into pcDNA3.1 vector. The cells were transfected with the constructs using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) and then selected with geneticin. The geneticin-resistant, CD82-positive cells were collected by flow cytometric sorting and maintained as stable transfectants, as described in our earlier studies [16, 19].

Flow cytometry

Cells were detached with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Invitrogen) or EDTA-PBS, blocked with 15% goat serum on ice for 0.5 h, incubated sequentially with primary Ab on ice for 1 h and fluorochrome-conjugated secondary Ab on ice for 1 h, and then analyzed by flow cytometer. The cells were washed with PBS thrice after each step described above.

Western blot, immunoprecipitation and co-immunoprecipitation

Cells were lysed with either 1% Brij 97 or RIPA buffer at 4 °C for 1 h. Insoluble material was removed by 14,000 g centrifugation, Laemmli sample buffer was added to the lysates, and then the lysates were heated at 95 °C for 5 min. The proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and electrically transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad). The membranes were sequentially blotted with primary mAb and horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary Ab, followed by chemiluminescence assay (Perkin-Elmer, Waltham, MA).

For immunoprecipitation and co-immunoprecipitation, cells were lysed with lysis buffer containing 1% Brij 98, 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl and 2 mM CaCl2 supplemented with protease inhibitors at 4 °C for 1 h. After centrifugation, the lysates were precleared by incubation at 4 °C for 6 h with protein A/G-conjugated Sepharose beads. Then the mAb-preabsorbed protein A/G-conjugated Sepharose beads were incubated with cell lysates overnight at 4 °C. The immunoprecipitates were then washed with ice-cold lysis buffer and processed for immunoblot.

Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy

For the steady-state analysis, cells on coverslips were fixed, permeabilized, blocked, and incubated sequentially with primary and fluorochrome-conjugated secondary Abs, followed by mounting of coverslips with FluroSave reagent (Calbiochem). In the case of double staining, cells were further incubated with fluorochrome-labeled primary Ab. For LysoTracker labeling, cells were incubated with LysoTracker Red DND99 (0.25 µg/mL) at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 10 min prior to aforementioned steps.

For epifluorescence analysis, cells were examined with an Axiophot fluorescent microscope (Carl Zeiss), and images were captured using an Optronics digital camera. For confocal fluorescence analysis, cells were examined and photographed with a LSM510 laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss). Confocal image represents a 1.0-µm “Z” optical section of the cell. Colocalizations were quantified with Ziess LSM software and/or visual counting of double-positive vesicles.

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

PLA was performed by following manufacturer’s instruction. Briefly, cells cultured on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at RT for 10 min, washed with PBS, permeabilized with 1% Brij98 at RT for 90 s, blocked at RT for 1 h, and then incubated at 4℃ overnight with primary Abs, followed by addition of Duolink-In Situ-Detection-Reagents Red (DUO92008-100RXN, Sigma) and wash with buffer A. The slides were placed in humidity chamber at 37℃, incubated sequentially with the PLA probe solution for 1 h, ligation solution for 30 min, and amplification solution for 100 min, washed with buffer B, stained with DAPI, and then mounted. The slides were examined and images were acquired with a Leica SP8 confocal microscope.

Internalization and recycling assays

Endocytosis was examined by either Ab uptake or biotinylation assay. For Ab uptake assay [20, 31], attached cells were incubated with mAb on ice for 1 h, followed by three washes to remove unbound mAb, and then incubated with serum-free media at 37 °C for 1 h, unless otherwise stated. After the cell surface mAb were removed by acidic washes (0.1 M glycine/PBS, pH 2.7), the cells were fixed, permeabilized, blocked with goat serum, incubated with 2nd Ab, washed, and examined by fluorescent microscopy. For biotinylation assay [24, 49], attached cells were washed with cold PBS twice, labeled with Sulfo-NHS-SS-Biotin/PBS (0.5 mg/mL) on ice for 60 min, washed with cold PBS twice, and incubated at 37 °C in serum-free media for the indicated time for endocytosis. Then on ice the cells were washed with PBS, treated with reduction solution (50 mM GSH, 75 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% bovine serum albumin, and 75 mM NaOH) twice for a total of 20 min, washed with PBS, and lyzed with RIPA buffer. The biotinylated proteins were pulled down by avidin-conjugated beads, for immunoblot of CD82.

For recycling [24], after mAb was internalized and the cell surface-bound mAb was removed as described above, the cells were further incubated at 37 °C for 1–3 h to allow the recycling of internalized mAb and its-probed CD82, followed by acidic wash to remove the recycled mAb from cell surface. Then the cells were fixed, permeabilized, incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated 2nd Ab, and examined as described above. Change in the internalized mAb, relative to the starting point of recycling analysis, denotes the level of recycling. Larger in the change indicates more of the recycling.

Cell migration assays

For solitary migration, Transwell cell migration assay was performed [30]. Briefly, cells were detached with 10 mM EDTA and reseeded into Transwell inserts with 8-µm pore size, the underside of which were coated with either fibronectin (10 µg/mL) or laminin 111 (10 µg/mL). For Du145 transfectants, 5 × 103 cells in 150 µL of 0.1% heat-inactivated BSA were plated into each insert. For PC3 transfectants migration toward fibronectin or laminin 111, 1 × 104 or 5 × 104 cells in 150 µL of 0.1% heat-inactivated BSA were plated into each insert, respectively. For all of the above cases, 500 µL of 10% FBS-DMEM were added to each lower well. After incubation at 37° for 3 or 6 h, cells that had not migrated through the inserts were removed and cells that had migrated to the undersides of the inserts were fixed and stained with Diff-Quick (Baxter).

For collective cell migration, wound healing assay was performed. Briefly, cells were cultured in 6-well plates. After monolayers were formed, wounds were created by scratching the monolayer with a sterile p-200 pipette tip. The cells were extensively washed with PBS to remove detached cells, and wounded monolayers were replenished with complete DMEM (with or without different concentrations of cilengitide). Wound closure was analyzed using an inverted light microscope at indicated time points. Wound closure/healing was quantitated by measuring the area of wound closure at different time points.

Bioinformatics analysis

Correlation analysis on gene expression between CD82 and αV integrins in prostate cancer and normal prostate tissues was performed through three web servers: GEPIA2 [50], CANCERTOOL [51], and Correlation AnalyzeR [52].Their correlations are presented as the Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients (r) and p-value.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least three times. Data are presented as mean ± SD or mean ± SEM. Data were analyzed statistically using either a two-tailed Student’s t-test or a one-way ANOVA with a Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
