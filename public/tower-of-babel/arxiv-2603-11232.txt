Long-Time Asymptotics for the Heat Kernel and for Heat Equation Solutions on Homogeneous Trees Thanks: Acknowledgments. This work is funded by the Deutsche Forschungsgemeinschaft (DFG, German Research Foundation)–SFB-Geschäftszeichen –Projektnummer SFB-TRR 358/1 2023 –491392403.
Effie Papageorgiou

Abstract
. We study the large-time behavior of the continuous-time heat kernel and of solutions to the heat equation on homogeneous trees. First, we derive sharp asymptotic formulas for the heat kernel as t → ∞ t\to\infty . Second, using them, we show that solutions with initial data in weighted ℓ 1 \ell^{1} classes, asymptotically factorize in ℓ p \ell^{p} norms, p ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ] p\in[1,\infty] , as the product of the heat kernel, times a p p -mass function, dependent on the initial condition and p p . The p p -mass function is described in terms of boundary averages associated with Busemann functions for p < 2 p<2 , while for p ≥ 2 p\geq 2 , it is expressed through convolution with the ground spherical function. For comparison, the case of the integers shows that a single constant mass determines the asymptotics of solutions to the heat equation for all p p , emphasizing the influence of the graph geometry on heat diffusion.

1.
Introduction

The study of the heat equation has given rise to two fundamental tools in modern mathematics: the Fourier transform and the heat kernel. Harmonic analysis, understood as the study of the Fourier transform, is one of the main tools used in this paper, while the heat kernel is its central object of study. In the setting of Riemannian geometry, the heat kernel can be constructed intrinsically (see, e.g.,
[
8
]
), revealing a deep connection between its analytic behavior and the geometry of the underlying manifold.

On the other hand, random walks on graphs form a vast subject with strong connections to probability, analysis, geometry, and algebra, and they have long been studied as discrete analogs of diffusions on manifolds. A central theme is the relationship between the geometry of an infinite graph and the asymptotic behavior of the associated random walk. In close connection to discrete time heat kernel are
local limit theorems
; however, these deal with fixed vertices, while many applications require uniform asymptotics in large space–time regimes.
Continuous-time random walks provide a more regular framework than discrete-time walks and may be defined via subordination by a Poisson process; a standard reference is
[
4
, Section 5]
.

In this paper, we consider the heat semigroup
(e^{-t\mathcal{L}})_{t>0}
associated with the natural (nearest-neighbor) Laplacian
\mathcal{L}
on a homogeneous tree, a basic example of a graph with exponential volume growth.
The first main result of this paper is the derivation of large-time asymptotics for the continuous-time heat kernel on homogeneous trees, in large space-time regimes. To state this result, let us recall that a homogeneous tree
\mathcal{T}
of degree
q+1
,
q\geq 2
, is a connected graph with no loops, in which every vertex is adjacent to
q+1
other vertices. We denote by
d
the natural distance on
\mathcal{T}
. Let
o
be a fixed reference point on
\mathcal{T}
; write
|x|
for
d(x;o)
. Denote by
\gamma(0)=2\sqrt{q}/(q+1)
.
The following heat kernel asymptotics are the first main result of this work.

Theorem A
.

Let
h_{t}
denote the heat kernel on a homogeneous tree and
h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}
that of the integers. Then, for
x\in\mathcal{T}
such that
|x|\to\infty
and
t\to\infty
, we have the following asymptotics:

h_{t}(x)=c\,\frac{2}{\gamma(0)}\,t^{-1}e^{-(1-\gamma(0))t}\,(1+|x|)\,q^{-|x|/2}\,h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(|x|+1)+o(1),

where
c
is an explicit constant depending on the rate of
|x|/t
.

Instead, if
x\in\mathcal{T}
is such that
|x|\leq\rho(t)
, with
\frac{\rho(t)}{\sqrt{t}}\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, then

h_{t}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{q(q+1)}{(q-1)^{2}}\gamma(0)^{-3/2}\,t^{-3/2}\,e^{-(1-\gamma(0))t}\,\varphi_{0}(x)\left(1+O\left(\frac{\rho(t)}{\sqrt{t}}\right)\right),

where
\varphi_{0}(x)=\left(1+\frac{q-1}{q+1}|x|\right)q^{-|x|/2}
.

To the best of our knowledge, so far only global bounds (albeit sharp) were available in the literature, see
[
6
, Proposition 2.5]
. Instead, global asymptotics for the heat kernel on hyperbolic space as time and space grow to infinity were established in
[
1
]
.

The next main task of the present work is to discuss the large-time behavior of
solutions
to the heat equation on
\mathcal{T}
; in fact, the previous asymptotics on the heat kernel are instrumental to this end, while bounds seem not to suffice. More precisely, the question is motivated by a classical result on Euclidean space, which we now describe. Let
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, denote by
M=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u_{0}(x)\,dx
its mass, and by
G_{t}(x)=(4\pi t)^{-n/2}e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4t}}
the Euclidean heat kernel. Then for all
1\leq p\leq\infty
,

(1.1)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{\|G_{t}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}\|u_{0}\ast G_{t}(x)-M\,G_{t}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=0.

Notice that Young’s inequality only gives
\|u_{0}\ast G_{t}\|_{p}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{1}\|G_{t}\|_{p}
, so the mass
M
times
G_{t}
“cancels enough” with
u_{0}\ast G_{t}
so that (
1.1
) holds.

Beyond the Euclidean framework, the geometry of the underlying space plays a decisive role. On manifolds with non-negative Ricci curvature, analogous asymptotic results hold, see
[
9
]
. In contrast, negatively curved spaces exhibit markedly different behavior. In real hyperbolic spaces
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, Vázquez showed in
[
15
]
that the classical
L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
-asymptotic convergence in (
1.1
) holds for
radial
initial data; instead, he showed that the
L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{3})
result may fail if the initial condition is non-radial, and that the
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{3})
result in (
1.1
) may fail even with radial symmetry of the solution. These results were later extended in
[
2
]
to Riemannian symmetric spaces of non-compact type
G/K
, which include all hyperbolic spaces; in the same setting, a correction to the constant (showing it should vary with
p
), so that (
1.1
) holds for
p>1
-albeit just for symmetric initial data- was obtained in
[
11
]
.

The appropriate approach toward understanding this phenomenon turns out to be the introduction of suitable
mass corrections
, which turn out to be
functions
instead of constants. For symmetric spaces
G/K
, and for any compactly supported initial datum, it was shown in
[
12
]
that the asymptotic behavior of caloric functions, that is, solutions to the heat equation, depends crucially on the value of
p\in[1,\infty]
: for
1\leq p<2
and
2\leq p\leq\infty
, distinct mass terms arise naturally. In the bi-
K
-invariant setting, these masses reduce to constants, related to the spherical transform of the initial condition. Analogous results were later obtained in
[
13
]
in the non-Archimedean setting of affine buildings (even exotic ones) for discrete-time random walks, where mass functions were defined using boundary integrals and Macdonald’s ground spherical functions, and where the dichotomy at the critical exponent
p=2
again plays a fundamental role.

The purpose of the present work is to establish the continuous-time analog of these results on
homogeneous trees
. Homogeneous trees occupy a natural position between symmetric spaces and affine buildings: they constitute the simplest example of the latter, but also a discrete analog to real hyperbolic spaces. Even more, heat propagation on homogeneous trees exhibits striking non-Euclidean features, as already observed in
[
6
,
10
]
(see also
[
14
]
for random walks on affine buildings), as a result of the “negatively curved” geometry.

To state our second result, let us introduce some further notation. The geometric boundary
\Omega
of a homogeneous tree
\mathcal{T}
can be viewed as the set of all rays tipped at
o
. For
any
x\in\mathcal{T}
, we set
\Omega(o,x)=\{\omega\in\Omega:\,\text{the geodesic }[o,\omega)\,\text{contains }x\}
. The boundary
carries the harmonic measure
\nu
centered at
o
, such that
\nu(\Omega(o,x))
depends only on the distance

d(o,x)
. Recall that we say that a function
f
is radial if
f(x)
depends only on
d(o,x)
. Let
h_{\omega}(x)
be the Busemann (or horocycle or height) function for
x\in\mathcal{T}
and
\omega\in\Omega
.

We next introduce mass functions; to this end, we need some weighted
\ell^{1}
spaces. We say that
f
belongs to
\ell^{1}(w_{p})
,

p\in[1,\infty]
, if

\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}|f(y)|w_{p}(y)<\infty,

where the weight
w_{p}
is given by

w_{p}(y)=\begin{cases}q^{\frac{1}{p}|y|}&\text{if }p\in[1,2),\\
q^{\frac{1}{2}|y|},&\text{if }p\in[2,\infty].\end{cases}

Now, for
f\in\ell^{1}(w_{p})
, the mass function
M_{p}(f)
is defined by the formula

\displaystyle M_{p}(f)(x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}f(y)\fint_{\Omega(o,x)}q^{\frac{1}{p}h_{\omega}(x)}\,d\nu(\omega),\qquad

\displaystyle\text{if }p\in[1,2),

or

\displaystyle M_{p}(f)(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\varphi_{0}(x)}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}f\ast\varphi_{0}(x),

\displaystyle\text{if }p\in[2,\infty),

and it is in fact a bounded function on
\mathcal{T}
. The mass function
M_{p}(f)
is well-defined also when
f
is
radial
and belongs to
\ell^{1}_{\delta_{p}}(\mathcal{T})
; this space is defined by

\ell^{1}_{\delta_{p}}(\mathcal{T})=\Big\{f:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}:\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}|f(y)|\varphi_{i\delta_{p}}(y)<\infty\Big\},

where
\varphi_{\lambda}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, are the radial eigenfunctions of the Laplace operator
\mathcal{L}
satisfying
the normalisation condition
\varphi_{\lambda}(o)=1
, while

\delta_{p}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2},&\quad 1\leq p<2,\\
0,&\quad 2\leq p\leq\infty.\end{cases}

In this case then, that is, when the initial condition is in
\ell^{1}_{\delta_{p}}(\mathcal{T})
and radial, mass functions simplify to
constants
:

M_{p}(f)\equiv\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}f(y)\varphi_{i\delta_{p}}(y).

Then the second main result is the following.

Theorem B
.

Let
h_{t}
be the continuous time heat kernel on a homogeneous tree, associated with the combinatorial Laplacian
\mathcal{L}
.
If
u=e^{-t\mathcal{L}}f
, where
f\in\ell^{1}(w_{p})
,
p\in[1,\infty]
, or
f
is radial and
belongs to
\ell^{1}_{\delta_{p}}(\mathcal{T})
, then

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}}\|u(t;\cdot)-M_{p}(f)(\cdot)\,h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}=0.

The main class of functions in
\ell^{1}(w_{p})
are those that are finitely supported. Indeed, the result for the other classes follows by density arguments; notice that

\text{finitely supported}\subseteq\ell^{1}_{w_{p}}\subseteq\ell^{1},

but

\text{finitely supported radial }\subseteq\ell^{1}_{\text{rad}}\subseteq\ell^{1}_{i\delta_{p}}\text{ radial}.

It is worth mentioning that for radial functions in
\ell^{1}(\mathcal{T})

we have

M_{1}(f)\equiv\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}f(y),

which is reminiscent of (
1.1
), and it is the discrete-space, continuous-time analog of results on hyperbolic space or Riemannian symmetric spaces of the non-compact type (continuous time-space) or random walks on affine buildings (discrete time-space). The crucial ingredient in all these non-positively curved settings seems to be a careful analysis of the heat kernel, see e.g.
[
1
,
2
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
, especially in the so-called
heat concentration regions
, where the interplay of the geometry versus the fast decay in space of the heat kernel becomes apparent. It is now that Theorem
A
becomes essential. Remarkably, we obtain here the same mass functions as for (isotropic, aperiodic, irreducible, finite-range) random walks on homogeneous trees in
[
13
]
: the transition densities for discrete time, as well as their concentration regions, seem to memorize information for the walk, but the mass functions do not, see e.g.
[
13
,
14
]
.

The case of
\mathbb{Z}
is also discussed here for comparison: for the integers, the constant
M=M(f)=\sum_{\mathbb{Z}}f
works for all
p\in[1,\infty)
and all
f\in\ell^{1}
. This complies with the result (
1.1
) on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
; see also
[
10
]
for a comparison between the heat kernel on
\mathbb{Z}
and the heat kernel on a homogeneous tree with
q\geq 2
, in terms of heat concentration.

This paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
, we present some preliminaries on homogeneous trees and the
corresponding harmonic analysis. In Section
3
we discuss the heat kernel on homogeneous trees, its connection with the heat kernel on
\mathbb{Z}
, as
well as
\ell^{p}
heat concentration on critical regions. Section
4
is devoted to obtaining asymptotics for the heat kernel, hence containing the proof of Theorem
A
. It also contains asymptotics for ratios of heat kernels in certain regions, as an indispensable technical tool for the proof of Theorem
B
, which deals with the asymptotic behavior of caloric functions, that is, solutions to the heat equation. To this end, in Section
5
, we first juxtapose with the long-time convergence result for the heat equation on the integers, where the result is essentially Euclidean. Finally, Section
6
deals with the
asymptotic behavior of caloric functions on homogeneous trees of exponential volume growth. We introduce mass functions for
p\in[1,\infty]
, and we discuss
their properties. We next show that for finitely supported initial data, the corresponding solutions to the heat equation, converge in the
\ell^{p}

critical region to the mass function times the heat kernel as time grows to infinity. On the other hand, we
show that both the solution and the heat kernel vanish asymptotically outside the critical regions. In the last
subsection, we discuss these problems for more general initial data in the
\ell^{p}
,
p\in[1,\infty]
setting, thus completing the proof of Theorem
B
.

2.
Homogeneous trees

A homogeneous tree
\mathcal{T}
of degree
q+1
is an infinite connected graph with no loops, in which every vertex is adjacent to
q+1
other vertices. When
q=1
, a homogeneous tree can be identified with the set of integers. From now on, we assume that
q\geq 2
unless specified, and we identify
\mathcal{T}
with its set of vertices. For more details, see
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
.

A homogeneous tree carries a natural distance
d
and a natural measure
\mu
. Specifically,
d(x,y)
is the number of edges of the shortest path joining
x
to
y
and
\mu
is the counting measure. For the counting measure, the volume of any sphere
S(x,n)
in
\mathcal{T}
is given by

|S(x,n)|=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }n=0\\
(q+1)q^{n-1},&\text{if }n\in\mathbb{N}.\end{cases}

Let us fix a base point
o
and set
|x|=d(x,o)
. Functions depending only on
|x|
are called radial. By a slight abuse of notation, we shall often write
f(x)=f(|x|)
for a radial function.
We may define the convolution of two functions
f_{1},f_{2}
on
\mathcal{T}
, provided
f_{2}
is radial, by

(2.1)

(f_{1}\ast f_{2})(x)=\sum\limits_{n\geq 0}f_{2}(n)\sum\limits_{d(x,y)=n}f_{1}(y).

Finally, we denote by
\ell^{p}(\mathcal{T})
the Lebesgue spaces associated to the measure
\mu
, with norms given by

\|f\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathcal{T})}=\begin{cases}\left(\intop\nolimits\limits_{\mathcal{T}}|f(x)|^{p}dx\right)^{p}=\left(\sum\limits_{x\in\mathcal{T}}|f(x)|^{p}dx\right)^{p},&\text{if }1\leq p<\infty\\
\sup\limits_{x\in\mathcal{T}}|f(x)|,&\text{if }p=\infty.\end{cases}

2.1.
Boundary, Poisson Kernel and Harmonic Analysis

A useful reference for this section in
[
5
]
. An infinite geodesic ray
\omega
on
\mathcal{T}
is a one-sided sequence
\{\omega_{n},\;n=0,\,1,\,2,...\}
where the
\omega_{n}
are in
\mathcal{T}
. Two infinite geodesic rays
\omega=\{\omega_{n}:\;n=0,\,1,\,2,...\}
and
\omega^{\prime}=\{\omega_{n}^{\prime}:\;n=0,\,1,\,2,...\}

are said to be equivalent if there exist natural numbers
n
and
m
such that
\omega_{i}=\omega_{i+m}^{\prime}
for all
i
greater than
n
. This identification is an equivalence relation and partitions the set of all infinite geodesic rays into disjoint classes. In every such
equivalence class there exists a unique geodesic ray which starts from
o
.

The boundary of
\mathcal{T}
is the set of all infinite geodesic rays starting at
o
and will be denoted by
\Omega
. For any

x\in\mathcal{T}
and
\omega\in\Omega
there is a unique geodesic ray, denoted by
[x,\omega]
, which has base vertex
x
and represents

\omega
. For
x\in\mathcal{T}
, we set

\Omega(o,x)=\big\{\omega\in\Omega:x\in[o,\omega]\big\}.

The boundary carries
the harmonic measure
\nu
centered at
o
, such that
\nu(\Omega(o,x))
depends only on the distance
d(x,o)
.

Given
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega
, we define
c(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
to be the confluence point of
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
, that is,
the last common point between the infinite geodesics
\omega=\{\omega_{n}:\;n=0,\,1,\,2,...\}
and
\omega^{\prime}=\{\omega_{n}^{\prime}:\;n=0,\,1,\,2,...\}
. Similarly for
x\in\mathcal{T}
and
\omega\in\Omega
we define
c(x,\omega)=x_{l}
to be the last point lying on
\omega
in the geodesic path
\{o,x_{1},,...,x\}
joining
o
to
x
.

The Poisson kernel
p(x,\omega)
(which can be viewed as the Radon-Nikodym derivative of a probability measure defined on the boundary) is given by

(2.2)

p(x,\omega)=q^{h_{\omega}(x)},\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{T},\,\forall\omega\in\Omega,

where the height
h_{\omega}(x)
is defined by

h_{\omega}(x)=2|c(x,\omega)|-|x|.

In other words, we fix a geodesic ray
\omega=\{x_{m}:m\in\mathbb{N}\}
in
\mathcal{T}
, and consider the associated height function
h_{\omega}
, which is the discrete analogue of the Busemann function in Riemannian geometry, defined by

h_{\omega}(x)=\lim_{y\to\omega}(d(o,y)-d(x,y))=\lim_{m\to\infty}\bigl(m-d(x,x_{m})\bigr).

From the explicit formula above, it turns out that the Poisson kernel is a non-negative function on
\mathcal{T}\times\Omega
which, for any fixed
x
, takes only finitely many values as a function of
\omega
. More precisely, for every fixed
x
, the quantity
c(x,\omega)
can range from
0
to
|x|
and consequently

(2.3)

-|x|\leq h_{\omega}(x)\leq|x|\quad\forall\omega\in\Omega.

More generally, see
[
7
, pp.34–35]
, one can introduce the function
h(x,y;\omega)
which is defined
as the distance of the
\omega
-horocycle
H(x)
containing
x
and the
\omega
-horocycle
H(y)
containing
y
, taken with the positive sign if

H(y)
is closer to
\omega
than
H(x)
, and with the negative sign
otherwise. With this notation,

(2.4)

\frac{d\nu_{y}}{d\nu_{x}}(\omega)=q^{h(x,y;\omega)}.

Then for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{T}
and
\omega\in\Omega
, we have the cocycle relation

(2.5)

h(x,y;\omega)=h(x,z;\omega)+h(z,y;\omega).

2.2.
The spherical Fourier transform on homogeneous trees

Our main aim in this section is to define the notion of a spherical Fourier transform and its inverse on a homogeneous tree
\mathcal{T}
. For more details, see
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
16
]
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the mean operator

(\mathcal{M}f)(x)=\frac{1}{q+1}\sum\limits_{y\in\mathcal{T},\;d(x,y)=1}f(y).

Then, the Laplacian
\mathcal{L}
on
\mathcal{T}
is defined by

\ \mathcal{L}=I-\mathcal{M};

it is easily seen to be bounded on
L^{p}(\mathcal{T})
for every
p
in
[1,\infty]
, and self-adjoint on
L^{2}(\mathcal{T})
. The real segment
[1-\gamma(0),1+\gamma(0)]
,
\gamma(0)=2\sqrt{q}/(q+1)
, coincides with the
\ell^{2}
spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
.

The
spherical function

\varphi_{\lambda}
of index
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is the unique radial eigenfunction of the operator
\mathcal{L}
, which is associated to the eigenvalue

(2.6)

\gamma(\lambda)=\frac{q^{i\lambda}+q^{-i\lambda}}{q^{\frac{1}{2}}+q^{-\frac{1}{2}}}=\gamma(0)\cos(\lambda\log q),

and which is normalized by
\varphi_{\lambda}(o)=1
. Set

\tau=\frac{2\pi}{\log q}.

The spherical functions have the following explicit expressions:

(2.7)

\varphi_{\lambda}(x)=\begin{cases}\left(1+\frac{q-1}{q+1}|x|\right)q^{-|x|/2}&\text{if }\lambda\in\tau\mathbb{Z},\\
\left(1+\frac{q-1}{q+1}|x|\right)q^{-|x|/2}(-1)^{|x|}&\text{if }\lambda\in\frac{\tau}{2}+\tau\mathbb{Z},\\
\textbf{c}(\lambda)q^{(-\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda)|x|}+\textbf{c}(-\lambda)q^{(-\frac{1}{2}-i\lambda)|x|}&\text{if }\lambda\notin(\frac{\tau}{2})\mathbb{Z},\\
\end{cases}

where
c
is the meromorphic function

(2.8)

\textbf{c}(z)=\frac{1}{q^{1/2}+q^{-1/2}}\frac{q^{1/2+iz}-q^{-1/2-iz}}{q^{iz}-q^{-iz}},\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C}\backslash(\frac{\tau}{2})\mathbb{Z}.

Note that
\varphi_{\lambda}
is periodic with period
\tau
,
\varphi_{\lambda}=\varphi_{-\lambda}
and that
\varphi_{i/2}\equiv 1
. An integral representation of these elementary spherical functions, see
[
5
, p.18]
or
[
7
]
, is given by

(2.9)

\varphi_{\lambda}(x)=\intop\nolimits_{\Omega}p^{\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda}(x,\omega)\,d\nu(\omega),\quad x\in\mathcal{T}.

The
spherical Fourier transform
of a radial function
f\in\ell^{1}(\mathcal{T})
is defined by

\ \mathcal{H}f(\lambda)=\sum\limits_{x\in\mathcal{T}}f(x)\varphi_{\lambda}(x)=f(0)+\sum\limits_{n\geq 1}(1+q)q^{n-1}f(n)\varphi_{\lambda}(n),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}.

Since
\varphi_{\lambda}=\varphi_{-\lambda}
and
\varphi_{\lambda+\tau}=\varphi_{\lambda}
,
\mathcal{H}f
is even and
\tau
-periodic. Then, the following inversion formula holds true:

f(x)=\frac{q\log q}{4\pi(q+1)}\intop\nolimits_{-\tau/2}^{\tau/2}\mathcal{H}f(\lambda)\varphi_{\lambda}(x)\frac{d\lambda}{|\textbf{c}(\lambda)|^{2}},\quad x\in\mathcal{T},

where

(2.10)

|\mathbf{c}(\lambda)|^{-2}=\frac{4(q+1)^{2}\sin^{2}(\lambda\log q)}{(q+1)^{2}\sin^{2}(\lambda\log q)+(q-1)^{2}\cos^{2}(\lambda\log q)},\quad\ \lambda\in[-\tau/2,\tau/2).

2.3.
The Helgason-Fourier transform

The Helgason-Fourier transform
\widehat{f}
of a finitely supported function
f
on
\mathcal{T}
is a function on
\mathbb{C}\times\Omega

defined by the formula

(2.11)

\widehat{f}(\lambda,\omega)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}}f(x)\,p^{\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda}(x,\omega).

It is clear that
\widehat{f}(\lambda,\omega)=\widehat{f}(\lambda+\tau,\omega)
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. If
f
is radial, then its Helgason-Fourier transform becomes independent of the variable
\omega

and

\widehat{f}(\lambda,\omega)=\mathcal{H}f(\lambda)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}}f(x)\varphi_{\lambda}(x),

that is, the Helgason-Fourier transform reduces to the spherical Fourier transform (see also
[
5
, p.18]
).

3.
The heat kernel on homogeneous trees

We now discuss the notion of the continuous-time heat kernel on homogeneous trees. For more details, see
[
6
,
10
]
.

The heat semigroup generated by the Laplacian
\mathcal{L}
is denoted by
(e^{-t\mathcal{L}})_{t>0}
. Since
\mathcal{L}

is bounded on
\ell^{p}(\mathcal{T})
whenever
p\geq 1
and
t>0
,
e^{-t\mathcal{L}}
is given by the formula

e^{-t\mathcal{L}}=\sum_{n=0}\frac{(-t\mathcal{L})^{n}}{n!};

the series converges in the uniform operator topology, and
(e^{-t\mathcal{L}})_{t>0}
is a uniformly
continuous semigroup on
\ell^{p}(\mathcal{L})
. At the kernel level, the heat kernel is
h_{t}(x,y)=e^{-t\mathcal{L}}\delta_{y}(x)
, while, for any
f\in\ell^{p}(\mathcal{T})
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we have

e^{-t\mathcal{L}}f(x)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}h_{t}(x,y)f(y)=f\ast h_{t}(x),

namely the norm analytic solution in
\ell^{p}(\mathcal{T})
of the heat equation

(\partial_{t}+L)u=0,\,\forall(x,t)\in\mathcal{T}\times(0,+\infty),\quad u(x,0)=f(x).

The self-adjointness of
e^{-t\mathcal{L}}
yields that the heat kernel is symmetric in
x,y
; the identity
e^{-t\mathcal{L}}1=1
gives then

1=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}h_{t}(x,y)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}}h_{t}(x,y)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}}h_{t}(y,x)\quad\forall t\geq 0,\,y\in\mathcal{T},

showing that heat is conserved.

The heat kernel
h_{t}
is given as the inverse spherical Fourier transform (
3.1
) of the function

m_{t}(\lambda)=e^{-t(1-\gamma(\lambda))},

where
\gamma(\lambda)
is defined on (
2.6
). By evenness, it holds

(3.1)

h_{t}(x)=\frac{q\log q}{2\pi(q+1)}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\tau/2}e^{-t(1-\gamma(\lambda))}\varphi_{\lambda}(x)\frac{d\lambda}{|\textbf{c}(\lambda)|^{2}},\quad x\in\mathcal{T}.

From this expression, it is clear that the heat kernel is a radial function, so we shall slightly abuse notation and write
h_{t}(|x|)
or
h_{t}(d(x,o))
as well.

Formulas for the heat kernel on
\mathcal{T}
are closely related to those for the heat kernel on the set of integers
\mathbb{Z}
(which can be identified with a homogeneous tree with
q=1
). More precisely, the Laplacian on the integers
\mathbb{Z}
is given by

\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Z}}f(j)=f(j)-\frac{f(j+1)+f(j-1)}{2},

and the corresponding heat kernel
h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is given by

\displaystyle h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}(j)=\frac{e^{-t}}{2\pi}\intop\nolimits_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}e^{t\cos s}\cos(sj)ds=e^{-t}I_{|j|}(t),

for every
j\in\mathbb{Z}
and
t>0
; here we have the modified Bessel function of imaginary
argument. It is clear that
h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}
has sum equal to
1
as well (i.e., it is a probability measure on
\mathbb{Z}
). The following lemma by Cowling, Meda and Setti, describes the behavior of
h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

(
[
6
, Theorem 2.3]
)
Let
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
F:\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
denote the functions defined by the rules

\xi(z)=(1+z^{2})^{1/2}+\log\left(\frac{z}{1+(1+z^{2})^{1/2}}\right)

and

F(j,t)=\begin{cases}(2\pi)^{-1/2}\frac{\exp\{-t+|j|\,\xi(t/|j|)\}}{(1+j^{2}+t^{2})^{1/4}},&\text{if }j\neq 0\\
(2\pi)^{-1/2}(1+t^{2})^{-1/4},&\text{if }j=0.\end{cases}

Then

h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}(j)\asymp F(j,t)\text{ for all }j\in\mathbb{Z},\text{ for all }t>0,

and

h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}(j)\sim F(j,t)\text{ as }j^{2}+t^{2}\text{ tends to }\infty.

We now return to the heat kernel on trees. The result in
[
6
]
, given below, connects
h_{t}
with
h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

(
[
6
, Proposition 2.5]
)
The following hold for every
t>0
and
x\in\mathcal{T}
:

(i)
h_{t}(x)=e^{-(1-\gamma(0))t}q^{-|x|/2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^{-k}\left[h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(|x|+2k)-h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(|x|+2k+2)\right],

(ii)
h_{t}(x)=\frac{2e^{-(1-\gamma(0))t}}{\gamma(0)t}q^{-|x|/2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^{-k}\,(|x|+2k+1)\,h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(|x|+2k+1),

(iii)
h_{t}(x)\asymp\frac{e^{-(1-\gamma(0))t}}{t}(1+|x|)\,q^{-|x|/2}\,h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(|x|+1).

The
\ell^{p}
norms of the heat kernel can be derived either by the pointwise estimates of the heat kernel,
[
10
, p.1740]
, or by using harmonic analysis,
[
6
, Lemma 2.1]
:

(3.2)

\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}\asymp\begin{cases}\min\{1,t^{-\frac{1}{2p^{\prime}}}\}\,e^{-b_{p}t},\quad&\text{if}\quad 1\leq p<2;\\
\min\{1,t^{-\frac{3}{4}}\}\,e^{-b_{2}t},\quad&\text{if}\quad p=2;\\
\min\{1,t^{-\frac{3}{2}}\}\,e^{-b_{2}t},\quad&\text{if}\quad 2\leq p\leq\infty.\end{cases}

Here, for all
1\leq p\leq 2
, we set

b_{p}=1-\gamma\left(i\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right),

which actually coincides with the infimum of the real part of the bottom of the
\ell^{p}
spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
,
[
6
, p.4275]

We now introduce a notion that is essential for the
\ell^{p}
concentration of heat on homogeneous trees. Let
1\leq p<\infty
. We say that
\textbf{B}_{p}(t)\subseteq\mathcal{T}
is an
\ell^{p}

critical region
for the heat kernel on
\mathcal{T}
, if

\frac{1}{\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}}\left(\sum_{x\in\textbf{B}_{p}(t)}h_{t}(x)^{p}\right)^{1/p}\rightarrow 1,\quad\text{as}\quad t\rightarrow\infty,

or equivalently, if

\frac{1}{\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}\setminus\textbf{B}_{p}(t)}h_{t}(x)^{p}\right)^{1/p}\rightarrow 0,\quad\text{as}\quad t\rightarrow\infty.

Using the above-mentioned heat kernel estimates, Medolla and Setti determined in
[
10
]
the
\ell^{1}
critical region of
h_{t}
:

Theorem 3.3
.

(
[
10
, p.1740]
) Assume that
q\geq 2
. Let
R_{1}=(q-1)/(q+1)
and
r(t)
be a positive function such that

\frac{r(t)}{\sqrt{t}}\rightarrow\infty,\quad\frac{r(t)}{t}\rightarrow 0,\quad\text{ as }t\rightarrow\infty.

Then, for all
y\in\mathcal{T}
,

\sum_{x:\;R_{1}t-r(t)\leq d(x,y)\leq R_{1}t+r(t)}h_{t}(x,y)\rightarrow 1,\quad\text{ as }t\rightarrow\infty.

Woess gave an interesting probabilistic interpretation -and a strengthening- of this phenomenon in
[
17
]
, in terms of a central limit theorem: for the heat diffusion process
(X_{t})_{t>0}
,
d(X_{t},X_{0})
is asymptotically normal with mean
R_{1}t
and variance
t
(see also
[
3
]
for a probabilistic interpretation for a similar phenomenon on
L^{1}
on symmetric spaces).
Analogous statements can be made for any
1\leq p\leq\infty
; however, since the probabilistic interpretation is not clear for
p>1
, we stick to the analytic statement. More precisely, the following
\ell^{p}
concentration results are proven in
[
10
]
:

Proposition 3.4
.

(
[
10
, p.1470]
) The following space-time regions describe where the heat kernel on
\mathcal{T}
concentrates in the
\ell^{p}
sense:

(i)

Let
1\leq p<2
. Set
R_{p}=(q^{1/p}-q^{1/p^{\prime}})/(q+1)
. If
r(t)
is any function such that
r(t)/t^{1/2}\rightarrow+\infty
,
r(t)/t\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
, then

\left\|h_{t}\right\|_{p}^{-p}\sum_{R_{p}t-r(t)\leq|x|\leq R_{p}t+r(t)}h_{t}(x)^{p}\rightarrow 1\quad\text{ as }t\rightarrow+\infty.

(ii)

Let
p=2
. If
r_{1}(t)/t^{1/2}\rightarrow 0
and
r_{2}(t)/t^{1/2}\rightarrow+\infty
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
, then

\left\|h_{t}\right\|_{2}^{-2}\sum_{r_{1}(t)\leq|x|\leq r_{2}(t)}h_{t}(x)^{2}\rightarrow 1\quad\text{ as }t\rightarrow+\infty.

(iii)

Let
2<p<+\infty
. If
r_{3}(t)\rightarrow+\infty
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
, then

\left\|h_{t}\right\|_{p}^{-p}\sum_{|x|\leq r_{3}(t)}h_{t}(x)^{p}\rightarrow 1\quad\text{ as }t\rightarrow+\infty;

if
p=+\infty
, then the maximum of
h_{t}(x)
is attained at
o
and

\left\|h_{t}\right\|_{\infty}^{-1}\sup_{|x|\geq r_{3}(t)}h_{t}(x)\rightarrow 0\quad\text{ as }t\rightarrow+\infty.

4.
Heat kernel asymptotics

The purpose of this section is to give results concerning large-time heat kernel asymptotics, that is, to prove Theorem
A
. So far, to the best of our knowledge, only (sharp) bounds were available in the literature
[
6
]
, so these results are new; in addition, to study the large-time asymptotic behavior of solutions to the heat equation, see Theorem
B
, bounds would not be sufficient. On hyperbolic space (and in fact on all rank one non-compact symmetric spaces), complete asymptotics for the heat kernel were obtained in
[
1
, p.1080]
, as time and space go to infinity; moreover, these asymptotics were dictated by the ratio of space to time. The main results of this section on homogeneous trees are analogous.

Let us start by defining here for future reference

(4.1)

\zeta(z):=\frac{\xi(z)}{z},\quad z>0,

where
\xi
is the function given in Theorem
3.1
. Then,

(4.2)

\zeta^{\prime}(z)=-\frac{1}{z^{2}}\log\left(\frac{z}{1+\sqrt{1+z^{2}}}\right),

and

(4.3)

\displaystyle\zeta^{\prime\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{z^{3}}\left(\sqrt{1+z^{2}}-2\,\operatorname{arcsinh}\!\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)\right),

which we notice that is bounded away from zero.

We start with an auxiliary lemma about heat kernels on
\mathbb{Z}
, which is essential to determining asymptotics for the heat kernel on
\mathcal{T}
. As already highlighted by Cowling, Meda and Setti in
[
6
, p.4280]
, the connection between these two heat kernels is reminiscent of the relationship between the heat kernel of a symmetric space and that of a maximal flat submanifold.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}
denote the continuous time heat kernel on
\mathbb{Z}
. Then, we have

(4.4)

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q^{-k}\frac{h^{\mathbb{Z}}_{t\gamma(0)}(|x|+2k+1)}{h^{\mathbb{Z}}_{t\gamma(0)}(|x|+1)}\to{C},\quad\text{as }|x|\to\infty,\,t\to\infty.

Here, the quantity
C
depends on the limit of
|x|/t
. More precisely,

C=\begin{cases}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q^{-k}\left(\frac{s_{0}}{1+\sqrt{1+s_{0}^{2}}}\right)^{2k},\quad&\text{if}\quad|x|/t\to C_{0}>0,\,\text{where }s_{0}:=\frac{\gamma(0)}{C_{0}};\\
\frac{1}{q-1},\quad&\text{if}\quad|x|/t\to 0,\\
0,\quad&\text{if}\quad|x|/t\to\infty.\end{cases}

Proof.

First, by
[
6
, p.4282]
, we know that
h^{\mathbb{Z}}_{t\gamma(0)}(|x|+2k+1)\leq h^{\mathbb{Z}}_{t\gamma(0)}(|x|+1)
for all
t,|x|,k
. Hence, owing to
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q^{-k}<\infty
, we may apply dominated convergence to the sum in (
4.4
) under consideration.

According to Theorem
3.1
, the quotient of heat kernels on
\mathbb{Z}
is asymptotically equal to

\displaystyle\frac{h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(|x|+2k+1)}{h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(|x|+1)}

\displaystyle\sim\left(\frac{1+\gamma(0)^{2}t^{2}+(|x|+1)^{2}}{1+\gamma(0)^{2}t^{2}+(|x|+2k+1)^{2}}\right)^{1/4}\times

\displaystyle\exp\left\{t\gamma(0)\left[\zeta\left(\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+2k+1}\right)-\zeta\left(\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+1}\right)\right]\right\},

where
\zeta
is defined in (
4.1
).

First, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1+\gamma(0)^{2}t^{2}+(|x|+1)^{2}}{1+\gamma(0)^{2}t^{2}+(|x|+2k+1)^{2}}=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{4k(|x|+1+k)}{1+\gamma(0)^{2}t^{2}+(|x|+2k+1)^{2}}.

The right-hand side is uniformly bounded for all
|x|,t\geq 0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, so using that for
(1-u)^{1/4}=1+O(u)
,
u\in[0,1]
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1+\gamma(0)^{2}t^{2}+(|x|+1)^{2}}{1+\gamma(0)^{2}t^{2}+(|x|+2k+1)^{2}}\right)^{1/4}

\displaystyle=1+k\,O\left(\frac{1}{1+\gamma(0)t+(|x|+1+2k)}\right)

\displaystyle=1+k\,O(t^{-1}).

Clearly, as
kq^{-k}
is summable, the error term will not affect the computation of
C
as
t\rightarrow\infty
; on the other hand, as the main term above is
1
, multiplication with it will not affect the value of
C
either. We can therefore ignore the contribution of this quantity in (
4.4
) and only focus on the exponential term.

By the mean value theorem, we have for the exponent

t\gamma(0)\left[\zeta\left(\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+2k+1}\right)-\zeta\left(\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+1}\right)\right]=-2k\,\zeta^{\prime}(w)\,\frac{t\gamma(0)}{(|x|+1)}\,\frac{t\gamma(0)}{(|x|+2k+1)},

for some
\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+2k+1}<w<\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+1}
: we write

(4.5)

w=\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+\xi\cdot 2k+1}\quad\text{for some }\xi\in(0,1).

Notice that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{w^{2}}\,\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+1}\,\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+2k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{|x|+\xi\cdot 2k+1}{|x|+1+2k}\,\frac{|x|+\xi\cdot 2k+1}{|x|+1}

\displaystyle=\left(1+2k\,\frac{\xi-1}{|x|+1+2k}\right)\left(1+2k\,\frac{\xi}{|x|+1}\right)

(4.6)

\displaystyle=1+k\,O(|x|^{-1}).

Since by (
4.2
),
\zeta^{\prime}(w)=-\frac{1}{w^{2}}\log\left(\frac{z}{1+\sqrt{1+w^{2}}}\right)
, we get

(4.7)

\displaystyle t\gamma(0)\left[\zeta\left(\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+2k+1}\right)-\zeta\left(\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=2k\left(1+k\,O(|x|^{-1})\right)\psi(w),

where

(4.8)

\psi(z):=\log\left(\frac{z}{1+\sqrt{1+z^{2}}}\right),\,z>0,\quad\psi^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{z}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+z^{2}}}.

We next distinguish cases for
|x|,t\to\infty
.

Case I:
Assume that
\frac{t}{|x|}\to C_{0}>0
. Then

w\to s_{0}:=\frac{\gamma(0)}{C_{0}},

and

\psi(w)\to\psi(s_{0})=\log\left(\frac{s_{0}}{1+\sqrt{1+s_{0}^{2}}}\right).

Hence, using (
4.7
), we get, as
t,|x|\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\exp\left\{t\gamma(0)\left[\zeta\left(\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+2k+1}\right)-\zeta\left(\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+1}\right)\right]\right\}\to\left(\frac{s_{0}}{1+\sqrt{1+s_{0}^{2}}}\right)^{2k}.

It follows that as
t,|x|\to\infty
, the sum in (
4.4
) converges to

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\sqrt{q}\,\frac{1+\sqrt{1+s_{0}^{2}}}{s_{0}}\right)^{-2k}.

It is straightforward to verify that for any
C_{0}>0
, the quantity
\sqrt{q}\,\frac{1+\sqrt{1+s_{0}^{2}}}{s_{0}}
is greater than
1
. Hence that the geometric series above is summable, and this gives the value of
C
in (
4.4
) in the first case.

Case II:
Assume that
\frac{t}{|x|}\to\infty
. Then by (
4.5
), we have
w\to\infty
. It follows that

\lim_{w\to\infty}\psi(w)=0,

so, by (
4.7
),

\displaystyle\exp\left\{t\gamma(0)\left[\zeta\left(\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+2k+1}\right)-\zeta\left(\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+1}\right)\right]\right\}\to 1.

Thus, the quotient
h_{t\gamma(0)}(|x|+2k+1)/h_{t\gamma(0)}(|x|+1)
converges to
1
, in turn
proving the claim for
C=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q^{-k}
in (
4.4
) in the second case.

Case III:
Assume that
\frac{t}{|x|}\to 0
. Then by (
4.5
), we have
w\to 0
. It follows that

\lim_{w\to\infty}\psi(w)=-\infty,

so by (
4.7
),

\displaystyle\exp\left\{t\gamma(0)\left[\zeta\left(\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+2k+1}\right)-\zeta\left(\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+1}\right)\right]\right\}\to 0,

which proves the claim
C=0
in the final case.
∎

The following result is one of the main ones in the present work.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
h_{t}
denote the heat kernel on a homogeneous tree. Then, for
x\in\mathcal{T}
such that
|x|\to\infty
and
t\to\infty
, we have the asymptotics

h_{t}(x)\sim\frac{2}{\gamma(0)}\,(C+1)\,\frac{e^{-(1-\gamma(0))t}}{t}\,(1+|x|)\,q^{-|x|/2}\,h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(|x|+1),

where
C
is the constant of Lemma
4.1
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.2
,

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma(0)t}{2e^{-(1-\gamma(0))t}}q^{|x|/2}h_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^{-k}\,(|x|+2k+1)\,h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(|x|+2k+1)

\displaystyle=(|x|+1)\,h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(|x|+1)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q^{-k}\,(|x|+2k+1)\,h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(|x|+2k+1)

(4.9)

\displaystyle=(|x|+1)\,h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(|x|+1)\times

\displaystyle\times\left\{1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q^{-k}\,\frac{|x|+2k+1}{|x|+1}\,\frac{h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(|x|+2k+1)}{h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(|x|+1)}\right\}.

On the one hand, we have

\frac{|x|+2k+1}{|x|+1}=1+k\,O(|x|^{-1}).

On the other hand, as already mentioned (see for instance
[
6
, p.4282]
), the quotient of the heat kernels on
\mathbb{Z}
in (
4
) is bounded by
1
for all
|x|,t,k
. Since
kq^{-k}
is summable, one can split the sum (
4
) in a main term and an error term. Taking the limit for
t,|x|\to\infty
, and invoking Lemma
4.1
, we get the desired result.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Let us compare this asymptotic with known global, in time and space, heat kernel bounds. According to
[
6
, Proposition 2.5]
, for all
x\in\mathcal{T}
and
t>0
, it holds

(4.10)

\frac{q+1}{\sqrt{q}}\leq\frac{h_{t}(x)}{\frac{e^{-(1-\gamma(0))t}}{t}\,\varphi_{0}(x)\,h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(|x|+1)}\leq\frac{\sqrt{q}(q+1)^{3}}{(q-1)^{3}}.

Observe first that by (
2.7
), we have

(4.11)

\displaystyle\frac{(1+|x|)q^{-|x|/2}}{\varphi_{0}(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{q+1}{q-1}+O(|x|^{-1}),

so, since
\gamma(0)=2\sqrt{q}/(q+1)
, by Theorem
4.2
, we can also write

\frac{h_{t}(x)}{\frac{e^{-(1-\gamma(0))t}}{t}\,\varphi_{0}(x)\,h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(|x|+1)}\sim(C+1)\,\frac{(q+1)^{2}}{\sqrt{q}(q-1)}.

Therefore, this means that for any
C\geq 0
, the lower bound in (
4.10
) is immediately satisfied, while above we should have

C+1\leq\frac{q(q+1)}{(q-1)^{2}}.

For the constants
C=0
or
C=(q-1)^{-1}
of Lemma
4.1
and Theorem
4.2
, this is clear. Consider now the constant of the first case in Lemma
4.1
; for any
s_{0}>0
, write

s^{*}:=q^{-1}\left(\frac{s_{0}}{1+\sqrt{1+s_{0}^{2}}}\right)^{2}\in(0,q^{-1}).

Then

C+1=\frac{1}{1-s^{*}}\in\left(1,\frac{q}{q-1}\right),

since the quantity
\frac{1}{1-s^{*}}
is increasing in
s^{*}
. Thus, we are in the regime of constants determined in
[
6
]
.

Finally, the next result concerns the long-time behavior of the heat kernel when it is spatially concentrated around the origin, and more precisely, when
|x|=o(\sqrt{t})
. Notice that
x
need not travel to infinity, so this case is not covered by Theorem
4.2
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{T}
be such that
|x|\leq\rho(t)
, with
\frac{\rho(t)}{\sqrt{t}}\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty.

Then

h_{t}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{q(q+1)}{(q-1)^{2}}\gamma(0)^{-3/2}\,e^{-(1-\gamma(0))t}\,t^{-3/2}\,\varphi_{0}(x)\left(1+O\left(\frac{\rho(t)}{\sqrt{t}}\right)\right).

We shall first need an auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 4.5
.

For any
\delta>0
, we have

\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\tau/2}e^{-\delta t\sin^{2}(\lambda\frac{\log q}{2})}\,\sin^{2}(\lambda\log q)\,d\lambda=\frac{2\sqrt{\pi}}{\log q}\,\delta^{-3/2}\,t^{-3/2}+O(t^{-5/2}),\quad\text{as}\quad t\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

Observe first that for any
c>0
and any
m\geq 0
, we have

(4.12)

\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\tau/2}e^{-ct\lambda^{2}}\,\lambda^{m}\,d\lambda\asymp_{c,m,\tau}t^{-\frac{m+1}{2}},\quad t>1.

Next, using that
\sin\alpha=2\sin\frac{\alpha}{2}\cos\frac{\alpha}{2}
and a change of variables (
\mu=\sin(\lambda\frac{\log q}{2})
),

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\tau/2}e^{-\delta t\sin^{2}(\lambda\frac{\log q}{2})}\,\sin^{2}(\lambda\log q)\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{8}{\log q}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}e^{-\delta t\mu^{2}}\,\mu^{2}\,\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\,d\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{8}{\log q}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}e^{-\delta t\mu^{2}}\,\mu^{2}\,(1+O(\mu^{2}))\,d\mu.

The error term is
O(t^{-5/2})
, owing to (
4.12
), so let us check the first integral; after a change of variables (
\sqrt{\delta}\sqrt{t}\mu=u
) we get

4.1.
Asymptotics for ratios of heat kernels

This subsection contains some technical results for ratios of heat kernels, which are to be used later. In fact, this subsection will be dealing with
|x|=|x_{t}|\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
, with
|x_{t}|=O(t)
. To this end, let us start with a preparatory lemma. Already from the result that follows, it becomes clear that in this range, the asymptotic behavior of the quotient of the heat kernel depends on the (bounded) ratio
|x|/t
; moreover, the ratio limit is related to the
Busemann
function.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
x,y\in\mathcal{T}
such that
|x|=|x_{t}|\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
, with
|x_{t}|=O(t)
, and
d(y,o)<\rho
for some
\rho>0
. Then

\frac{h_{t}(d(x,y))}{h_{t}(d(x,o))}=q^{\frac{1}{2}(d(x,o)-d(x,y))}\,\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{1+w^{2}}}{w}\right)^{d(x,o)-d(x,y)}(1+O(|x|^{-1})),

where
w=\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+1}(1+O(|x|^{-1}))
.
The implied constant in the error terms depends only on the geometry and the size of
y
.

Remark 4.8
.

Notice that
w
stays bounded below away from zero. Since
w\mapsto(1+\sqrt{1+w^{2}})/w
is bounded in
(1,\infty)
and
d(x,o)-d(x,y)=O(|y|)=O(1)
, the expression above is justified.

Proof.

We begin by
noticing that the triangle inequality and the fact that
d(x_{t},o)\to\infty
, yield
d(x,y)\asymp d(x,o)
for
t
large enough.
Using Theorem
4.2
, we can write

(4.14)

\frac{h_{t}(d(x,y))}{h_{t}(d(x,o))}\sim q^{\frac{1}{2}(d(x,o)-d(x,y))}\,\frac{1+d(x,y)}{1+d(x,o)}\,\frac{h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(d(x,y)+1)}{h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(d(x,o)+1)},\quad t\to\infty.

The triangle inequality clearly implies that

\frac{1+d(x,y)}{1+d(x,o)}=1+O(|x|^{-1}),

so it remains to study the asymptotic behavior of the quotient of the heat kernels on
\mathbb{Z}
. To this end, we work as in the proof of Theorem
4.2
; recall that we had set in (
4.1
)

\zeta(z)=\frac{\xi(z)}{z},\quad z>0,

and that by Theorem
3.1
, one gets

(4.15)

\displaystyle\frac{h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(d(x,y)+1)}{h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(d(x,o)+1)}

\displaystyle\sim\left(\frac{1+\gamma(0)^{2}t^{2}+(d(x,y)+1)^{2}}{1+\gamma(0)^{2}t^{2}+(d(x,o)+1)^{2}}\right)^{-1/4}\times

\displaystyle\exp\left\{t\gamma(0)\left[\zeta\left(\frac{t\gamma(0)}{d(x,y)+1}\right)-\zeta\left(\frac{t\gamma(0)}{d(x,o)+1}\right)\right]\right\}.

One can check that by the boundedness of
y
and the fact that
d(x,o)
and
d(x,y)
are
O(t)
, it holds

\frac{1+\gamma(0)^{2}t^{2}+(d(x,y)+1)^{2}}{1+\gamma(0)^{2}t^{2}+(d(x,o)+1)^{2}}=1+O(t^{-1}).

Hence, the term in (
4.15
) raised to the
-1/4
is

1+O(t^{-1})=1+O(|x|^{-1}),

as
t\rightarrow\infty
, given that
|x|=O(t)
. Therefore, it remains to treat the exponential term appearing in (
4.15
). We follow certain arguments already presented in the proof of Theorem
4.2
.

By the mean value theorem, we have

\zeta\left(\frac{t\gamma(0)}{d(x,y)+1}\right)-\zeta\left(\frac{t\gamma(0)}{d(x,o)+1}\right)=\zeta^{\prime}(w)\,t\gamma(0)\,\frac{d(x,o)-d(x,y)}{(d(x,o)+1)(d(x,y)+1)},

for some
w
between
\frac{t\gamma(0)}{d(x,o)+1}
and
\frac{t\gamma(0)}{d(x,y)+1}
; thus for some
\xi\in(0,1)
, we may write

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=t\gamma(0)\left(\xi\frac{1}{d(x,y)+1}+(1-\xi)\frac{1}{d(x,o)+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{t\gamma(0)}{d(x,o)+1}\left(1+\xi\,\frac{d(x,o)-d(x,y)}{d(x,y)+1}\right)

(4.16)

\displaystyle=\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+1}(1+O(|x|^{-1})).

We remark that in case the ratio
\frac{t}{|x|}\to\infty
goes to infinity, we understand the right-hand side as comparable to
\frac{t}{|x|}
.
Therefore, since
d(x,y)=d(x,o)+O(1)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{w^{2}}\,\frac{t\gamma(0)}{d(x,o)+1}\,\frac{t\gamma(0)}{d(x,y)+1}

\displaystyle=(|x|+1)^{2}\frac{1}{|x|+1}\frac{1}{|x|+O(1)}(1+O(|x|^{-1}))

\displaystyle=1+O(|x|^{-1}).

Combining this with the fact that by (
4.2
),
\zeta^{\prime}(w)=-\frac{1}{w^{2}}\psi(w)
, where, as defined in (
4.8
),

\psi(w)=\log\left(\frac{w}{1+\sqrt{1+w^{2}}}\right).

In fact, since
|x|=O(t)
, we have
w\gtrsim 1
, so
\psi
is bounded.
It follows from (
4.15
) that

\displaystyle t\gamma(0)\left[\zeta\left(\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+2k+1}\right)-\zeta\left(\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\psi(w)(d(x,o)-d(x,y))\times

\displaystyle\times(1+O(|x|^{-1})).

Since
y\in\mathcal{T}
satisfies
|y|<\rho
, we may write

(4.17)

\displaystyle e^{-\psi(w)(d(x,o)-d(x,y))(1+O(|x|^{-1})}

\displaystyle=e^{-\psi(w)(d(x,o)-d(x,y))}(1+O(|x|^{-1})).

Combining (
4.15
) and (
4.17
), we get

(4.18)

\displaystyle\frac{h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(d(x,y)+1)}{h_{t\gamma(0)}^{\mathbb{Z}}(d(x,o)+1)}=e^{-\psi(w)(d(x,o)-d(x,y))}(1+O(|x|^{-1}),

which, substituted into (
4.14
), completes the proof.
∎

The next two results focus on the regions that will be mostly relevant for the study of caloric functions. The size of
|x|
compared to that of
t
now becomes crucial.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
C_{0}>0
and
r(t)
be any function such that
r(t)/t^{1/2}\rightarrow+\infty
,
r(t)/t\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
. Then for
x\in\mathcal{T}
such that
C_{0}t-r(t)\leq|x|\leq C_{0}t+r(t)
, and
y\in\mathcal{T}

\frac{h_{t}(d(x,y))}{h_{t}(d(x,o))}=\left(\sqrt{q}\,\frac{1+\sqrt{1+s_{0}^{2}}}{s_{0}}\right)^{d(x,o)-d(y,o)}\left(1+O\left(\frac{r(t)}{t}\right)\right),

where
s_{0}=\frac{\gamma(0)}{C_{0}}
. The implied constant in the error term depends only on the geometry and the size of
y
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.7
and the fact that
|x|/t\to C_{0}
, it suffices to determine

w=\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+1}(1+O(|x|^{-1}))=\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+1}+O(t^{-1}).

Since
||x|-C_{0}t|\leq r(t)
, it is immediate that

\frac{t\gamma(0)}{|x|+1}=\frac{\gamma(0)}{C_{0}}+O\left(\frac{r(t)}{t}\right),

therefore

w=s_{0}+O\left(\frac{r(t)}{t}\right),\quad s_{0}=\frac{\gamma(0)}{C_{0}}

By the mean value theorem for
z\mapsto\frac{1+\sqrt{1+z^{2}}}{z}
,
z>0
, and the fact that the derivative of this function is bounded away from
0
, we then get

\frac{1+\sqrt{1+w^{2}}}{w}=\frac{1+\sqrt{1+s_{0}^{2}}}{s_{0}}+O\left(\frac{r(t)}{t}\right).

Substituting into Lemma
4.7
, and using that
|d(x,o)-d(x,y)|\leq d(y,o)
is bounded, proves the claim.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

Let
C_{0}=R_{p}=\frac{q^{1/p}-q^{1/p^{\prime}}}{q+1}
,
1\leq p<2
, be the constants of Proposition
3.4
(i). Set

\delta_{p}=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}.

Since

q^{1/p}-q^{1/p^{\prime}}=q^{1/2}\left(q^{\delta_{p}}-q^{-\delta_{p}}\right)=2\sqrt{q}\sinh(\delta_{p}\ln q),

it follows that

s_{0}=s_{0}(p)=\frac{\gamma(0)}{R_{p}}=\frac{1}{\sinh(\delta_{p}\ln q)}.

Thus, we get

\displaystyle\sqrt{q}\,\frac{1+\sqrt{1+s_{0}(p)^{2}}}{s_{0}(p)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{q}(\sinh(\delta_{p}\ln q)+\cosh(\delta_{p}\ln q))

\displaystyle=q^{1/2}\,e^{\delta_{p}\ln q}

\displaystyle=q^{1/p}.

Proposition 4.11
.

Assume
r_{1}(t),r_{2}(t)
are positive functions such that

\begin{cases}\frac{r_{1}(t)}{\log t}\rightarrow+\infty\quad\text{and}\,&\frac{r_{1}(t)}{t^{1/2}}\rightarrow 0,\\
\frac{r_{2}(t)}{t^{1/2}}\rightarrow+\infty\quad\text{and}\,&\frac{r_{2}(t)}{t}\rightarrow 0,\end{cases}

as
t\rightarrow+\infty
. Let
\varepsilon(t)=\min\{r_{1}(t)^{-1},r_{2}(t)/t\}\rightarrow 0.

Then for
x\in\mathcal{T}
such that
r_{1}(t)\leq|x|\leq r_{2}(t)
, and
y
bounded,

\displaystyle\frac{h_{t}(d(x,y))}{h_{t}(d(x,o))}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varphi_{0}(d(x,y))}{\varphi_{0}(d(x,o))}\,\left(1+O\left(\frac{r_{2}(t)}{t}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=q^{\frac{1}{2}(d(x,o)-d(x,y))}\,+O(\varepsilon(t)).

The implied constant in the error term depends only on the geometry and the size of
y
.

Proof.

Notice first that the two expressions are equal, as

\frac{1+\frac{q-1}{q+1}d(x,y)}{1+\frac{q-1}{q+1}d(x,o)}=1+O(r_{1}(t)^{-1}),

so by (
2.7
),

\frac{\varphi_{0}(d(x,y))}{\varphi_{0}(d(x,o))}=q^{\frac{1}{2}(d(x,o)-d(x,y))}(1+O(r_{1}(t)^{-1})),

since
d(x,o)-d(x,y)
is bounded.

To conclude, we revisit Lemma
4.7
, whence it is clear that in the present time-space region, we have
w\to+\infty
(in fact,
w\asymp t/|x|\gtrsim t/r_{2}(t)
). We now use that the function
u\mapsto u+\sqrt{1+u^{2}}
,
u\geq 0
is
1+O(u)
for
u\to 0
; then for
u=\frac{1}{w}=O(\frac{r_{2}(t)}{t})
, this implies that

(4.19)

\frac{1+\sqrt{1+w^{2}}}{w}=w^{-1}+\sqrt{1+w^{-2}}=1+O\left(\frac{r_{2}(t)}{t}\right).

Substituting into Lemma
4.7
and using that
d(x,o)-d(x,y)
is bounded, proves the claim.
∎

5.
Long time behavior of solutions to the heat equation on the integers

The aim of this section is to show how geometry affects the behavior of solutions to the heat equation on
\mathbb{Z}
, where the volume growth is polynomial; for a related discussion on heat diffusion, juxtaposing the geometries of
\mathbb{Z}
and of homogeneous trees with
q\geq 2
, see also
[
10
]
.
As far as we know, the results in this section are new.

Recall that
h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is even, and that
\|h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}\|_{\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z})}=1
. We shall show in this section that if
f\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z})
, then setting

M:=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}f(j),\quad u(t;j):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}(j-k)\,f(k),

we have

(5.1)

\|h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}^{-1}\,\|u(t;\cdot)-M\,h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as}\quad t\rightarrow+\infty,\quad\forall 1\leq p<\infty.

This result is clearly reminiscent of the result (
1.1
) in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
; namely, that the total
constant
mass
M=\sum_{\mathbb{Z}}f
of the initial condition
f
, ensures convergence of
u(t;\cdot)
to (a multiple of) the heat kernel for
all

p
.
To this end, it suffices to consider
f
to be finitely supported, say in
|j|<\rho
; then a standard density argument allows us to conclude for the whole
\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z})
class (see, for instance, the density arguments in the last section; namely, the proof of Theorem
6.2
).

An inspection of
[
10
, p.1741]
(proven there for
p=1
, but the arguments hold for
1\leq p<\infty
) shows that for
1\leq p<\infty
, we have

(5.2)

\|h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}^{-p}\sum_{r_{1}(t)\leq|j|\leq r_{2}(t)}h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}(j)^{p}\rightarrow 1,

where
r_{1}(t)
and
r_{2}(t)
are as in Proposition
3.4
(ii). By the Minkowski inequality, we have, outside this region,

\displaystyle\|u(t;\cdot)\|_{\ell^{p}(|j|<r_{1}(t)\text{ or }|j|>r_{2}(t))}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{|j|<r_{1}(t)\text{ or }|j|>r_{2}(t)}\big|\sum_{|k|<\rho}h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}(j-k)\,f(k)\big|^{p}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{|k|<\rho}\bigg(\sum_{|j|<r_{1}(t)\text{ or }|j|>r_{2}(t)}h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}(j-k)^{p}\,|f(k)|^{p}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{|k|<\rho}|f(k)|\bigg(\sum_{|j|<r_{1}(t)\text{ or }|j|>r_{2}(t)}h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}(j-k)^{p}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}

(5.3)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{|k|<\rho}|f(k)|\bigg(\sum_{|j|<2r_{1}(t)\text{ or }|j|>\frac{1}{2}r_{2}(t)}h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}(j)^{p}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}},

where the last step is justified by the evenness of
h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}
and the triangle inequality

|j-k|\leq|j|+\rho<r_{1}(t)+\rho<2r_{1}(t),\quad|j-k|\geq|j|-\rho>r_{2}(t)-\rho>\frac{1}{2}r_{2}(t),

which holds true for all
t
large enough. Therefore, the quantity in (
5
) is
o(\|h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}\|_{p})
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
, by (
5.2
). This shows that by a crude triangle inequality, (
5.1
) is valid as long as we sum over
\mathbb{Z}\cap(0,r_{1}(t))
or
\mathbb{Z}\setminus(0,r_{2}(t))
.

On the other hand, for
r_{1}(t)\leq|j|\leq r_{2}(t)
, we have, pointwise,

\displaystyle u(t;j)-M\,h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}(j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|k|<\rho}h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}(j-k)f(k)-\sum_{|k|<\rho}f(k)\,h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}(j)

\displaystyle=h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}(|j|)\,\sum_{|k|<\rho}\left(\frac{h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}(j-k)}{h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}(j)}-1\right)f(k).

Clearly,

\frac{1}{2}r_{1}(t)\leq|j-k|\leq 2r(t)

for all
t
large enough, since
k
is bounded. Hence, slightly adjusting (
4.18
), by taking
w=t/|j|\to\infty
, and using (
4.19
), one can see that

\frac{h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}(j-k)}{h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}(j)}=1+O(\varepsilon(t)),\quad r_{1}(t)\leq|j|\leq r_{2}(t),\quad k\text{ bounded},

where
\varepsilon(t)=\min\{r_{1}(t)^{-1},r_{2}(t)/t\}\rightarrow 0.

This means that

|u(t;j)-M\,h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}(j)|=h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}(j)\,\|f\|_{\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z})}\,O(\varepsilon(t)),

so

\|u(t;j)-M\,h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}\|_{\ell^{p}(r_{1}(t)\leq|j|\leq r_{2}(t))}\lesssim\|h_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\,\|f\|_{\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z})}\,\varepsilon(t),

which proves the claim (
5.1
).

6.
Long time behavior of solutions to the heat equation on a homogeneous tree

6.1.
Finitely supported initial data.

This section is devoted to the large-time behavior of caloric functions
e^{-t\mathcal{L}}f
on a homogeneous tree, provided
f
is finitely supported. We will show that, unlike the case of
\mathbb{Z}
, there is no
constant

M
that works for
all

p
.

Instead, for
each

p\in[1,\infty]
, we shall introduce a notion
of a
p
-mass
function
and prove that caloric functions with finitely supported initial data, asymptotically decouple as the product of this mass function and the heat kernel in the
\ell^{p}
sense. This function boils down to a constant, still depending on
p
, if the initial datum is radial. Interestingly, the same object was the correct mass function for isotropic finite-range aperiodic random walks on homogeneous trees, see
[
13
, Subsection 4.2]
.

6.1.1.
The case
1\leq p<2
.

Let
f
be finitely supported, and consider the function

\displaystyle M_{p}(f)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}f(y)\,\frac{1}{\nu(\Omega(o,x))}\intop\nolimits_{\Omega(o,x)}q^{\frac{1}{p}h_{\omega}(y)}\,d\nu(\omega)

(6.1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}f(y)\fint_{\Omega(o,x)}q^{\frac{1}{p}h_{\omega}(y)}\,d\nu(\omega).

Clearly, this function is bounded, since for any
\omega\in\Omega
and
y\in\mathcal{T}
, we have
|h_{\omega}(y)|\leq|y|
by (
2.3
).

Moreover, if
f
is radial, then this mass function becomes a constant, namely

M_{p}(f)(x)=M_{p}(f)=\mathcal{H}f(\pm i\delta_{p})=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}f(y)\varphi_{\pm i\delta_{p}}(y),\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{T},

where

\delta_{p}=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}.

Indeed, recalling the definition of the Helgason-Fourier transform (
2.11
), and that it boils down to the spherical transform for radial functions, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}f(y)\fint_{\Omega(o,x)}q^{\frac{1}{p}h_{\omega}(y)}\,d\nu(\omega)

\displaystyle=\fint_{\Omega(o,x)}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}f(y)q^{\frac{1}{p}h_{\omega}(y)}\,d\nu(\omega)

\displaystyle=\fint_{\Omega(o,x)}\widehat{f}(-i\delta_{p},\omega)\,d\nu(\omega)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}f(\pm i\delta_{p})\fint_{\Omega(o,x)}d\nu(\omega)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}f(\pm i\delta_{p}).

Notice that for
p=1
and
f
radial, we get by (
2.9
) that
\varphi_{i/2}\equiv 1
, hence

M_{1}(f)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}}f(x),

which is reminiscent of the mass (constant) in the case of
\mathbb{Z}
, see Section
5
.

Let
\mathrm{supp}f\subseteq B(o,\rho)
for some
\rho>0
. We discuss the behavior of caloric functions separately

u(t;x)=e^{-t\mathcal{L}}f(x)=f\ast h_{t}(x)=\sum_{y}f(y)\,h_{t}(d(x,y)),

inside and outside the critical
\ell^{p}
region
\textbf{B}_{p}(t)
,
1\leq p<2
, whose description is given in Proposition
3.4
.

6.1.2.
Outside the critical
\ell^{p}
region

The next result shows that the solution itself vanishes asymptotically in time in the
\ell^{p}
sense, provided we sum outside the critical region
\textbf{B}_{p}(t)
,
1\leq p<2
, which was given in Proposition
3.4
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Assume that
f
is finitely supported. Then the corresponding caloric function satisfies

\|u(t;\cdot)\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathcal{T}\setminus\textbf{B}_{p}(t))}=o(\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}),\quad\text{as}\quad t\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

By the triangle inequality, if
d(x,o)>R_{p}t+r(t)
,
R_{p}>0
, then

d(x,y)\geq d(x,o)-d(y,o)>R_{p}t+r(t)-\rho>R_{p}t+\frac{1}{2}r(t),

while if
d(x,o)<R_{p}t-r(t)
, then

d(x,y)\leq d(x,o)+d(y,o)<R_{p}t-r(t)+\rho<R_{p}t-\frac{1}{2}r(t),

for
t
large enough. Therefore, if
x\notin\textbf{B}_{p}(t)
, then
x\notin\widetilde{\textbf{B}}_{p}^{y}(t)
, where

\widetilde{\textbf{B}}_{p}^{y}(t):=\left\{x\in\mathcal{T}:\,|d(x,y)-R_{p}t|\leq\frac{1}{2}r(t)\right\}.

Therefore, arguing as in (
5
), one can show that

\displaystyle\|u(t;\cdot)\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathcal{T}\setminus\textbf{B}_{p}(t))}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{|y|<\rho}|f(y)|\bigg(\sum_{x\notin\widetilde{\textbf{B}}_{p}^{y}(t)}h_{t}(d(x,y))^{p}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Using Proposition
3.4
(i) for
h_{t}(d(\cdot,y))
over the complement of
\widetilde{\textbf{B}}_{p}^{y}(t)
, we can conclude.
∎

As a result, since the mass function is bounded, we have

\displaystyle\|u(t;\cdot)-M_{p}(f)(\cdot)\,h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathcal{T}\setminus\textbf{B}_{p}(t))}

\displaystyle\leq\|u(t;\cdot)\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathcal{T}\setminus\textbf{B}_{p}(t))}

(6.2)

\displaystyle+\|M_{p}(f)\|_{\infty}\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathcal{T}\setminus\textbf{B}_{p}(t))}\rightarrow 0,

owing to Proposition
6.1
and Proposition
3.4
.

6.1.3.
Inside the critical region.

For
x\in\textbf{B}_{p}(t)
, we write pointwise for
u(t;\cdot)-M_{p}(f)(\cdot)\,h_{t}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}h_{t}(x,y)\,f(y)-M_{p}(f)(x)\,h_{t}(x)

(6.3)

\displaystyle\qquad=h_{t}(x)\sum_{y:\,d(y,o)<\rho}f(y)\left(\frac{h_{t}(d(x,y))}{h_{t}(d(x,o))}-\fint_{\Omega(o,x)}q^{\frac{1}{p}h_{\omega}(y)}\,d\nu(\omega)\right).

Using the quotient asymptotics from Proposition
4.9
, and Remark
4.10
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{h_{t}(d(x,y))}{h_{t}(d(x,o))}=q^{\frac{1}{p}(d(x,o)-d(x,y))}+O\left(\frac{r(t)}{t}\right).

On the other hand, for each
\omega\in\Omega(o,x)
, where
x\in\textbf{B}_{p}(t)
, we have

x\in[y,\omega]
if
t
is sufficiently large, since
d(x,o)\rightarrow\infty
; in other words,

h_{\omega}(y)=d(o,x)-d(y,x),\quad\omega\in\Omega(o,x),\quad|x|\rightarrow\infty,\quad|y|<\rho.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\fint_{\Omega(o,x)}q^{\frac{1}{p}h_{\omega}(y)}\,d\nu(\omega)

\displaystyle=\fint_{\Omega(o,x)}q^{\frac{1}{p}(d(o,x)-d(y,x))}\,d\nu(\omega)

\displaystyle=q^{\frac{1}{p}(d(o,x)-d(y,x))}.

Consequently, by (
6.3
), for
x\in\textbf{B}_{p}(t)
and
y
bounded,

u(t;x)-M_{p}(f)(x)\,h_{t}(x)=h_{t}(x)\|f\|_{\ell^{1}}\,O\left(\frac{r(t)}{t}\right),

Summing over the critical region
\textbf{B}_{p}(t)
,
1\leq p<2
, we get

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{x\in\textbf{B}_{p}(t)}\left|u(t;x)-M_{p}(f)(x)\,h_{t}(x)\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigg(\sum_{\textbf{B}_{p}(t)}h_{t}^{p}\bigg)^{1/p}\|f\|_{\ell^{1}}\,\frac{r(t)}{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}\|f\|_{\ell^{1}}\,\frac{r(t)}{t},

which completes the proof in the
\ell^{p}
case,
1\leq p<2
. Indeed, combining this estimate with (
6.2
), we have shown that

\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}^{-1}\|u(t;\cdot)-M_{p}(f)(\cdot)\,h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}\rightarrow\infty,\quad\text{as}\quad t\rightarrow\infty.

6.1.4.
The case
2<p\leq\infty

Let us start with the following observation: fix
y\in\mathcal{T}
; then for all
x\in\mathcal{T}
, we have
by the integral representation (
2.9
) of spherical functions, the cocycle relation (
2.5
) and the Radon-Nikodym derivative (
2.4
),

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}(d(x,y))

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{\Omega}q^{\frac{1}{2}h(x,y;\omega)}d\nu_{x}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{\Omega}q^{-\frac{1}{2}h(o,x;\omega)}\,q^{\frac{1}{2}h(o,y;\omega)}\,d\nu_{x}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{\Omega}q^{\frac{1}{2}h(o,x;\omega)}\,q^{\frac{1}{2}h(o,y;\omega)}\,d\nu(\omega)

(6.4)

\displaystyle\leq q^{\frac{1}{2}|y|}\,\varphi_{0}(x),

where in the last inequality we have also used the Busemann function bounds (
2.3
).

Recall now the
\ell^{p}
critical region,
2<p\leq\infty
, given in Proposition
3.4
:

\textbf{B}_{p}(t)=\{x\in\mathcal{T}:\,d(x,o)\leq r_{3}(t)\},\quad\frac{r_{3}(t)}{\log{t}}\rightarrow\infty,\quad\frac{r_{3}(t)}{\sqrt{t}}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as}\quad t\rightarrow\infty.

We work for
u(t;x)=e^{-t\mathcal{L}}f
, with
\mathrm{supp}f\subseteq B(o,\rho)
, by distinguishing cases whether
x
belongs to
\textbf{B}_{p}(t)
or not.

Outside the critical region, by the Minkowski inequality we have

\displaystyle\|u(t\cdot)\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathcal{T}\setminus\textbf{B}_{p}(t))}

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}\setminus\textbf{B}_{p}(t)}\big|\sum_{y:\,d(o,y)<\rho}h_{t}(d(x,y))\,f(y)\big|^{p}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{y:\,d(o,y)<\rho}\bigg(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}\setminus\textbf{B}_{p}(t)}h_{t}(d(x,y))^{p}\,|f(y)|^{p}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{y:\,d(o,y)<\rho}|f(y)|\bigg(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}\setminus\textbf{B}_{p}(t)}h_{t}(d(x,y))^{p}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

For
x\notin\textbf{B}_{p}(t)
and
y
bounded, we have

d(x,y)\geq r_{3}(t)-d(y,o)\geq\frac{1}{2}r_{3}(t),

for
t
large enough; therefore, the inner sum above is dominated by the sum in
\mathcal{T}\setminus B(y,\frac{1}{2}r_{3}(t))
. In turn, this is
o(\|h_{t}\|_{p})
by a standard modification of the arguments of Proposition
6.1
or using Proposition
3.4
. It follows that

\|h_{t}\|_{p}^{-1}\,\|u(t;\cdot)\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathcal{T}\setminus\textbf{B}_{p}(t))}\rightarrow 0.

Instead, when
x
is in the critical region
\textbf{B}_{p}(t)
, and
y
is bounded, the result of Proposition
4.4
yields

\displaystyle\frac{h_{t}(d(x,y))}{h_{t}(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varphi_{0}(d(x,y))}{\varphi_{0}(x)}\left(1+O\left(\frac{r_{3}(t)}{t}\right)\right)

(6.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varphi_{0}(d(x,y))}{\varphi_{0}(x)}+O\left(\frac{r_{3}(t)}{t}\right).

where the last equality is justified by (
6.4
).

For
2<p\leq\infty
, define now the mass function

(6.6)

M_{p}(f)(x)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{\varphi_{0}(d(x,y))}{\varphi_{0}(x)}f(y)=\frac{1}{\varphi_{0}(x)}(f\ast\varphi_{0})(x),\quad x\in\mathcal{T}.

Hence, owing to (
6.4
), when
f
is finitely supported, the mass function is bounded. Moreover, when
f
is radial, the mass function boils down to the constant
\mathcal{H}f(0)
, since
(f\ast\varphi_{0})(x)=\mathcal{H}f(0)\,\varphi_{0}(x)
.

Thus for
x\in\textbf{B}_{p}(t)
,
2<p\leq\infty
, we have, pointwise, for
u(t;\cdot)-M_{p}(f)(\cdot)\,h_{t}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}h_{t}(d(x,y))\,f(y)-M_{p}(f)(x)\,h_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=h_{t}(x)\sum_{y:\,d(o,y)<\rho}f(y)\left(\frac{h_{t}(d(x,y))}{h_{t}(x)}-\frac{\varphi_{0}(d(x,y))}{\varphi_{0}(x)}\right),

so by (
6.1.4
), if
x\in\textbf{B}_{p}(t)
and
y
bounded,

u(t;x)-M_{p}(f)(x)\,h_{t}(x)=h_{t}(x)\|f\|_{\ell^{1}}\,O\left(\frac{r_{3}(t)}{t}\right).

Hence, if
p\in(2,\infty)
, by summing over the critical region
\textbf{B}_{p}(t)
, we get

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{x\in\textbf{B}_{p}(t)}\left|u(t;x)-M_{p}(f)(x)\,h_{t}(x)\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigg(\sum_{x\in\textbf{B}_{p}(t)}h_{t}(x)^{p}\bigg)^{1/p}\|f\|_{\ell^{1}}\frac{r_{3}(t)}{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}\|f\|_{\ell^{1}}\frac{r_{3}(t)}{t},

which shows that

\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}^{-1}\|u(t;\cdot)-M_{p}(f)(\cdot)\,h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}(\textbf{B}_{p}(t))}\rightarrow 0,\quad\text{as}\quad t\rightarrow\infty.

We have thus shown that for
2<p<\infty
, if the initial datum
f
is finitely supported and
M_{p}(f)
is defined as in (
6.6
), then for
u=e^{-t\mathcal{L}}f
we have

\|h_{t}\|_{p}^{-1}\|u(t;\cdot)-M_{p}(f)(\cdot)\,h_{t}\|_{p}\rightarrow 0,\quad\text{as}\quad t\rightarrow\infty.

Lastly, for
p=\infty
the reasoning is similar. We omit the details.

6.1.5.
The case
p=2

Using the arguments of the previous two sections, it should be clear that when
f
is finitely supported, we can use both expressions

(6.7)

\varphi_{0}^{-1}(x)(f\ast\varphi_{o})(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}f(y)\fint_{\Omega(o,x)}q^{\frac{1}{2}h_{\omega}(y)}\,d\nu(\omega),

to serve as a mass function
M_{2}(f)
; notice that both expressions in (
6.7
) are bounded functions.

The reason for that is the heat kernel quotient asymptotics of Proposition
4.11
, in the
\ell^{2}
critical region
\textbf{B}_{2}(t)
. Starting from the asymptotics
h_{t}(d(x,y))/h_{t}(x)\sim\varphi(d(x,y))/\varphi(x)
, using the expression
M_{2}(f)=\varphi_{0}^{-1}(f\ast\varphi_{o})
and working as in the case
2<p\leq\infty
, one can show that

(6.8)

\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{-1}\|u(t;\cdot)-M_{2}(f)(\cdot)h_{t}\|_{\ell^{2}}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as}\quad t\rightarrow\infty.

Alternatively, notice that
x\in\textbf{B}_{2}(t)
implies
d(x,o)\rightarrow\infty
. Thus, using in Proposition
4.11
the asymptotics
h_{t}(d(x,y))/h_{t}(x)\sim q^{\frac{1}{2}(d(x,o)-d(x,y))}
, one can use as
M_{2}(f)
the second expression in (
6.7
), and argue as in the case
1\leq p<2
to get (
6.8
).

It needs to be stressed that the quantities in (
6.7
) are not necessarily equal everywhere, as one can see by taking
f=\delta_{y}
,
y\neq o
: the first expression simply becomes
\varphi_{0}(d(x,y))/\varphi_{0}(x)
and the second
\fint_{\Omega(o,x)}q^{\frac{1}{2}h_{\omega}(y)}\,d\nu(\omega)
. However, they are
asymptotically
equal in the
\ell^{2}
critical region, given that in Proposition
4.11
, we have shown that

\displaystyle\frac{\varphi_{0}(d(x,y))}{\varphi_{0}(d(x,o))}

\displaystyle=q^{\frac{1}{2}(d(x,o)-d(x,y))}(1+O(r_{1}(t)^{-1}))

\displaystyle=q^{\frac{1}{2}(d(x,o)-d(x,y))}+O(r_{1}(t)^{-1}),\quad x\in\textbf{B}_{2}(t),\quad y\,\text{bounded}.

In the case that
f
is radial, though, both expressions boil down (globally) to the same constant
\mathcal{H}f(0)
.

Hence, by this slight abuse of notation, that is, calling them both
M_{2}(f)
, we wish to emphasize that
p=2
is a critical value for the change of the behavior of mass functions. It is exactly for this value of
p
that the exponential volume growth of the graph is canceled by the exponential decay of
\varphi_{0}^{2}
in
h_{t}^{2}
(recall the heat kernel estimates in Proposition
3.2
). Thus, the case
p=2
is a threshold for the behavior of mass functions and thus the asymptotic behavior of solutions to the heat equation.

6.2.
Beyond finitely supported data in
\mathcal{T}

6.2.1.
Radial initial data.

The aim of this section is to go beyond finitely supported initial conditions. Let us first start with the spaces
\ell^{1}_{\delta_{p}}(\mathcal{T})
,
p\in[1,\infty]
, which are defined

(6.9)

\ell^{1}_{\delta_{p}}(\mathcal{T}):=\Big\{f:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}:\,\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}|f(y)|\varphi_{i\delta_{p}}(y)<\infty\Big\},

where

\delta_{p}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2},&\quad 1\leq p<2\\
0,&\quad 2\leq p\leq\infty.\end{cases}

Let us observe that
\ell^{1}_{1/2}(\mathcal{T})=\ell^{1}(\mathcal{T})
, since by (
2.9
),
\varphi_{i/2}\equiv 1
. Moreover, if
f
belongs to
\ell^{1}(\mathcal{T})
then it belongs
to all
\ell^{1}_{\delta_{p}}(\mathcal{T})
for
p\in(1,\infty]
. This follows by (
2.7
), which implies that
\varphi_{0}\lesssim 1
while
0<\varphi_{i\delta_{p}}\lesssim_{p,q}q^{-{|\cdot|}/{p^{\prime}}}
for all
p\in(1,2)
.

Furthermore, we can extend the definition of the mass function to radial functions belonging to
\ell^{1}_{\delta_{p}}(\mathcal{T})
.
In fact, in this case, the mass is a constant, the reasoning being the same as in the finitely supported case for radial initial conditions, and the definition of the spherical transform, see Section
2.2
. More precisely, for all
x\in\mathcal{T}

M_{p}(f)(x)=M_{p}(f)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{H}f(i\delta_{p}),\quad&p\in[1,2);\\
\mathcal{H}f(0),\quad&p\in[2,\infty].\end{cases}

Our aim in this subsection is to extend the previous result to
radial
functions in
\ell^{1}_{\delta_{p}}(\mathcal{T})
.
The proof draws on both the Kunze–Stein phenomenon and Herz’s
principe de majoration
for radial convolutors, tools that are available on homogeneous trees. We recall them briefly.

We denote by
\ell^{p}_{\text{rad}}(\mathcal{T})
the subset of
\ell^{p}(\mathcal{T})
constisting of radial functions.
The Kunze-Stein phenomenon states that
for all
1\leq p<2
, we have

\ell^{2}(\mathcal{T})\ast\ell^{p}_{\text{rad}}(\mathcal{T})\subseteq\ell^{2}(\mathcal{T}).

This, in turn, implies, by interpolation with its dual version and the trivial
inclusion
\ell^{1}(\mathcal{T})\ast\ell^{1}_{\text{rad}}(\mathcal{T})\subseteq\ell^{1}(\mathcal{T})
, that for all
2<q,r<\infty
, such that
q/2<r<q
, one has

\ell^{q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{T})\ast\ell^{r}_{\text{rad}}(\mathcal{T})\subseteq\ell^{q}(\mathcal{T}).

Herz’s
principe de majoration
ensures that a positive radial function

k
convolves
\ell^{p}(\mathcal{T})
,
p\in[1,2]
, into itself if and only if
\mathcal{H}k(i\delta_{p})
is finite, see e.g.
[
16
]
, and in fact

\|\ast k\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathcal{T})\rightarrow\ell^{p}(\mathcal{T})}=\mathcal{H}k(i\delta_{p}).

The following result allows us to consider the whole class of radial functions in
\ell^{1}_{\delta_{p}}(\mathcal{T})
as initial conditions for the
\ell^{p}
asymptotic convergence of caloric functions. It follows some ideas on symmetric spaces presented in
[
11
]
, see also
[
12
]
or
[
13
]
. The arguments are relatively standard once the convergence result for finitely supported data has been established, but we include them for the sake of completeness.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty]
. Then for each
u=e^{-t\mathcal{L}}f
with
f
being a radial function
in
\ell^{1}_{\delta_{p}}(\mathcal{T})
, we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}}\big\|u(t;\cdot)-M_{p}(f)\,h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}=0.

Proof.

First, let us consider
p\in[1,2]
. We employ a density argument to use the convergence result we already had if the initial condition was finitely supported.

Assume
f\in\ell^{1}_{\delta_{p}}(\mathcal{T})
is a radial function. Let
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\tilde{f}
be a radial and finitely supported
function such that

\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}|f(y)-\tilde{f}(y)|\varphi_{i\delta_{p}}(y)\leqslant\tfrac{1}{3}\varepsilon.

Since

\displaystyle\big|M_{p}(f)-M_{p}(\tilde{f})\big|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}\big|f(y)-\tilde{f}(y)\big|\varphi_{i\delta_{p}}(y)=\mathcal{H}(|f-\tilde{f}|)(i\delta_{p}),

we have

\big\|M_{p}(f)h_{t}-M_{p}(\tilde{f})h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}\leqslant\tfrac{1}{3}\varepsilon\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}.

Let
\tilde{u}:=e^{-t\mathcal{L}}\tilde{f}
.
Then by the results of Section
6.1
for finitely supported initial conditions, there is
t_{1}\geqslant 1
such that for all
t\geqslant t_{1}
,

\frac{1}{\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}}\,\|\tilde{u}(t;\cdot)-M_{p}(\tilde{f})\,h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}\leqslant\tfrac{1}{3}\varepsilon.

Since
h_{t}
is radial, and so are
f
and
\tilde{f}
, their convolution is commutative. By the radial version of Herz’s
principe
, we get

\big\|u(t;\cdot)-\tilde{u}(t;\cdot)\big\|_{\ell^{p}}\leqslant\mathcal{H}(|f-\tilde{f}|)(i\delta_{p})\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}\leqslant\tfrac{1}{3}\varepsilon\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}.

Therefore, for
t\geqslant t_{1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\big\|u(t;\cdot)-M_{p}(f)h_{t}\big\|_{\ell^{p}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\big\|u(t;\cdot)-\tilde{u}(t;\cdot)\big\|_{\ell^{p}}+\big\|M_{p}(f)\,h_{t}-M_{p}(\tilde{f})h_{t}\big\|_{\ell^{p}}

\displaystyle\phantom{\leqslant\big\|u(t;\cdot)-\tilde{u}(t;\cdot)\big\|_{\ell^{p}}\ }+\big\|\tilde{u}(t;\cdot)-M_{p}(\tilde{f})h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\varepsilon\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}},

which finishes the proof.

Assume that
p\in(2,\infty]
and let
t>0
. Since by the semigroup property
h_{t}=e^{-t/2\mathcal{L}}h_{t/2}
, that is,

h_{t}(d(x,y))=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{T}}h_{t/2}(d(x,z))h_{t/2}(d(y,z)),

we have

\displaystyle u(t;x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}f(y)h_{t}(d(x,y))

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}f(y)\sum_{z\in\mathcal{T}}h_{t/2}d((y,z))h_{t/2}(d(z,x))

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{T}}u(t/2;z)h_{t/2}(d(z,x)).

Hence,

\displaystyle u(t;x)-M_{p}(f)h_{t}(x)=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{T}}\big(u(t/2;z)-M_{p}(f)h_{t/2}(z)\big)h_{t/2}(d(z,x)).

Therefore, by the Kunze–Stein phenomenon, we get

\displaystyle\left\|u(t;\cdot)-M_{p}(f)h_{t}\right\|_{\ell^{p}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{z\in\mathcal{T}}\big(u(t/2;z)-M_{p}(f)h_{t/2}(z)\big)h_{t/2}(d(z,x))\right\|_{\ell^{p}(x)}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{p}\left\|u(t/2;\cdot)-M_{p}(f)h_{t/2}(\cdot)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\|h_{t/2}\|_{\ell^{2}}.

Then, in view of (
3.2
), we have for all
p\in(2,\infty]
and
t>1
,

\|h_{t/2}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}\asymp t^{-\frac{3}{2}}e^{-(1-\gamma(0))t}\asymp\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}.

Hence, by the first part of the proof for
p=2
(recall that
M_{p}(f)=\mathcal{H}f(0)
for all
p\geq 2
, provided
f
radial in
\ell^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{T})
), we conclude that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}}\left\|u(t/2;\cdot)-M_{p}(f)h_{t/2}(\cdot)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\|h_{t/2}\|_{\ell^{2}}=0.

The proof is now complete.
∎

6.2.2.
Data in weighted
\ell^{1}
spaces.

Lastly, we study the
\ell^{p}
-convergence of the caloric functions with boundary datum from a class of functions which are not
necessarily radial nor finitely supported. To this end, for each
p\in[1,\infty]
we introduce the following weights,

w_{p}(y)=\begin{cases}e^{\frac{1}{p}\,{|y|}}&\text{if }p\in[1,2),\\
e^{\frac{1}{2}\,{|y|}}&\text{if }p\in[2,\infty],\end{cases}

where
y\in\mathcal{T}
. Since
w_{p}(y)\geqslant 1
, we have

\ell^{1}(w_{p})=\Big\{f:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}:\quad\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}|f(y)|\,w_{p}(y)<+\infty\Big\}\subseteq\ell^{1}.

Moreover, if
f\in\ell^{1}(w_{p})
then the mass function
M_{p}(f)
is well-defined and bounded. Indeed, if
p\in[1,2)
, then by the definition of
M_{p}(f)
in (
6.1.1
), and using (
2.3
), we get

\displaystyle|M_{p}(f)(x)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}|f(y)|\fint_{\Omega(o,x)}e^{\frac{1}{p}\,h_{\omega}(y)}\,d\nu(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}|f(y)|\,e^{\frac{1}{p}\,{|y|}}<\infty.

On the other hand, if
p\in[2,\infty]
, then by the definition of the mass function in (
6.6
) and (
6.4
), we have

\displaystyle|M_{p}(f)(x)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}|f(y)|\frac{\varphi(d(y,x))}{\varphi(d(o,x))}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}|f(y)|\,e^{\frac{1}{2}\,{|y|}}.

For initial data in
\ell^{1}(w_{p})
we have the following result.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty]
. Then for each
u=e^{-t\mathcal{L}}f
with
f\in\ell^{1}(w_{p})
, we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}}\big\|u(t;\cdot)-M_{p}(f)(\cdot)\,h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}=0.

Proof.

Let
p\in[1,2)
and
\varepsilon>0
. Since
fw_{p}\in\ell^{1}(\mathcal{T})
, there is a finitely supported function
g
on

\mathcal{T}
such that

\displaystyle\|f-g\|_{\ell^{1}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}}|f(x)-g(x)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}}|f(x)-g(x)|w_{p}(x)\leqslant\tfrac{1}{3}\varepsilon.

Moreover, by (
6.1.1
) and (
2.3
), for each
x\in\mathcal{T}
, we have

\displaystyle\big|M_{p}(f)(x)-M_{p}(g)(x)\big|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}|f(x)-g(x)|\fint_{\Omega(o,x)}e^{\frac{1}{p}\,{h_{\omega}(y)}}\,d\nu(\omega)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}|f(y)-g(y)|\,e^{\frac{1}{p}\,|y|}<\tfrac{1}{3}\varepsilon,

thus

\big\|M_{p}(f)h_{t}-M_{p}(g)h_{t}\big\|_{\ell^{p}}\leqslant\tfrac{1}{3}\varepsilon\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}.

Let
\tilde{u}:=e^{-t\mathcal{L}}g
. Then by the results of Section
6.1
for finitely supported initial conditions, there is
t_{1}\geqslant 1
,
such that for all
t\geqslant t_{1}
,

\frac{1}{\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}}\big\|\tilde{u}(t;\cdot)-M_{p}(g)h_{t}\big\|_{\ell^{p}}\leqslant\tfrac{1}{3}\varepsilon.

Using the Minkowski inequality for integrals, we get

\displaystyle\big\|u(t;\cdot)-\tilde{u}(t;\cdot)\big\|_{\ell^{p}}

\displaystyle=\bigg\|\sum_{y\in\mathcal{T}}(f(y)-g(y))h_{t}(d(y,\cdot))\bigg\|_{\ell^{p}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|f-g\|_{\ell^{1}}\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}\leqslant\tfrac{1}{3}\varepsilon\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}.

Hence, for
t\geqslant t_{1}
,

\displaystyle\big\|u(t;\cdot)-M_{p}(f)h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\big\|u(t;\cdot)-\tilde{u}(t;\cdot)\big\|_{\ell^{p}}+\big\|\tilde{u}(t;\cdot)-M_{p}(g)h_{t}\big\|_{\ell^{p}}

\displaystyle+\big\|M_{p}(f)h_{t}-M_{p}(g)h_{t}\big\|_{\ell^{p}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\varepsilon\|h_{t}\|_{\ell^{p}}.

The proof for
p\in[2,\infty]
follows by the same line of reasoning provided one uses (
6.4
). We omit the details.
∎
