Background

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death globally and is rising in Korea with population aging and lifestyle westernization, intensifying the need for primary prevention. In 2022, the American Heart Association introduced Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) [1], an integrated cardiovascular health (CVH) construct comprising four health behaviors (diet, physical activity, smoking, sleep) and four health factors (body mass index, non-HDL cholesterol, blood glucose, blood pressure). Inverse associations between LE8 and cardiovascular outcomes have been demonstrated mainly in Western [2] and Chinese [3] cohorts; however, East Asian populations differ in dietary patterns (higher carbohydrate and sodium intake), a lower prevalence of obesity at equivalent metabolic risk, and population-wide universal healthcare access—factors that may modify the applicability of LE8 and the relative weight of its components.

In Korea, prior work has linked LE8 to all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [4], but its association with incident major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) has not been examined in a nationally representative cohort with claims-based outcome ascertainment. We therefore investigated the association between LE8 and incident 3-point MACE using nationwide Korean data.

Methods

In this retrospective cohort study, we linked the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) 2014–2018 to National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) claims for longitudinal outcome ascertainment. After excluding individuals under 20 years (n = 7605), pregnant or breastfeeding women (n = 332), participants with missing LE8 components (n = 6655; predominantly the Korean Healthy Eating Index [KHEI, 12.9%], fasting glucose [8.4%], and non-HDL cholesterol [8.1%]), missing covariates (n = 697), prevalent myocardial infarction or stroke (n = 175), and events within a 1-year lag (n = 129), 21,082 participants were analyzed. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of St. Vincent’s Hospital (VC24OISI0271); informed consent was waived.

LE8 scores were calculated using AHA algorithms [1] with two culturally adapted instruments: the validated KHEI for dietary quality [5] and the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire. Participants were categorized as low (0–49), moderate (50–79), or high (80–100) CVH per the original AHA framework [1]. Household income (Quartile 1 versus Q2–4) and alcohol consumption (non, mild [<30 g/day men, <20 g/day women], or heavy) were assessed via standardized KNHANES instruments. The primary outcome was 3-point MACE (cardiovascular death, non-fatal myocardial infarction [ICD-10 I21/I22], non-fatal ischemic stroke [I63/I64 with concomitant brain imaging]), ascertained from validated NHIS coding [6]. Multivariable Cox models were adjusted for age, sex, household income, alcohol consumption, and chronic kidney disease; hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia—being LE8 constituents—were not entered as covariates. Component effects were modeled per 10-point increment. Subgroup analyses examined effect modification by age (<65 vs. ≥65 years), sex, household income, and education. Analyses used SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Among 21,082 adults (42.2% male; 24.9% aged ≥65 years; mean LE8 score 62.9), participants with higher CVH were more frequently female, college-educated, and in higher income quartiles, with markedly lower prevalence of current smoking, hypertension, and diabetes than the low-CVH group (Panel A).

Over a median follow-up of 4.29 years, 371 MACE events occurred. Incidence per 1000 person-years declined across LE8 categories: low (n = 3769) 7.70, moderate (n = 14,823) 3.81, and high (n = 2490) 0.71. In fully adjusted models, the hazard ratio (HR) was 0.65 (95% CI, 0.52–0.81) for moderate and 0.31 (0.15–0.64) for high versus low CVH (P for trend <0.001); both the health behaviors (HR for high vs. low, 0.50; 0.31–0.81) and health factors (0.64; 0.46–0.90) subdomains were independently protective (Panel B). Kaplan–Meier curves showed graded, dose-dependent cumulative incidence by LE8 category for the composite outcome and for each component—myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, and cardiovascular mortality (all log-rank P < 0.001; Panel C).

Modeled continuously, each 10-point increment in the Total LE8 score corresponded to a 24% lower composite risk (HR, 0.76; 0.70–0.83; P < 0.001). The signal was strongest for ischemic stroke (0.71; 0.63–0.80), intermediate for myocardial infarction (0.82; 0.70–0.95), and null for cardiovascular mortality (0.91; 0.76–1.09) within this follow-up window. Component-level associations varied substantially: blood glucose (0.92; 0.88–0.96), smoking (0.94; 0.91–0.97), blood pressure (0.95; 0.92–0.98), sleep duration (0.95; 0.92–0.99), and KHEI diet (0.96; 0.93–0.99) were significant for the composite, whereas physical activity, BMI, and non-HDL cholesterol were not—although non-HDL cholesterol was paradoxically associated with higher cardiovascular mortality (1.11; 1.02–1.20) and blood glucose was the strongest predictor of myocardial infarction (0.87; 0.81–0.94) (Panel D).

The Total CVH score was protective across age, sex, income, and education strata (all P-interaction >0.05). By subdomain, the health factors score was significantly modified by age (P-interaction = 0.004) and household income (0.04): strongly protective in adults <65 years (HR for high vs. low, 0.32; 0.18–0.56) and higher-income strata (0.43; 0.26–0.69) but attenuated in older (0.97; 0.63–1.48) and lowest-income (1.01; 0.63–1.64) adults. The health behaviors score remained directionally protective across all strata (Panel E).

Discussion

In this nationwide Korean cohort, higher LE8 was independently and dose-dependently associated with lower incident MACE, with a 24% relative risk reduction per 10-point increment. This is the first Korean nationally representative analysis linking LE8 to incident MACE ascertained from nationwide insurance claims, extending prior Korean mortality evidence [4] and confirming cross-population concordance with Western [2] and Chinese [3] cohorts and a recent global meta-analysis [7].

The graded effect across outcomes—largest for ischemic stroke, intermediate for myocardial infarction, and null for short-term cardiovascular mortality—suggests that LE8 most strongly captures modifiable atherothrombotic risk and is less informative for the competing-risk-driven cardiovascular deaths observed within a median 4.3-year window. The component hierarchy—glycemic control, smoking, and blood pressure as the strongest predictors—identifies high-yield, actionable prevention targets aligned with the dominant cardiometabolic burden in East Asia, while culturally adapted dietary quality (KHEI) and sleep also contributed independently. The non-significance of BMI and non-HDL cholesterol for the composite, and the paradoxical non-HDL signal for cardiovascular mortality, likely reflect mediation through the already-adjusted glucose and blood-pressure pathways, competing risks, and a follow-up too short to capture lipid- and obesity-mediated atherogenesis; notably, 84.9% of MACE cases were physically inactive versus 71.2% of event-free participants, suggesting a ceiling effect for the physical activity metric in Korea.

From a prevention standpoint, the dominant contributions of glycemic control, smoking, and blood pressure suggest that opportunistic LE8 assessment during routine primary-care visits could help prioritize the modifiable risk factors with the greatest preventive impact in Korean adults. The subgroup attenuation of the health factors subdomain in older and lower-income adults may reflect cumulative legacy exposure and residual social determinants persisting even under universal healthcare, whereas the consistent protection from the overall CVH score and from health behaviors across strata supports lifestyle-centered prevention as broadly equitable.

Limitations include the observational design, single-time-point self-reported behaviors, 18% LE8 missingness driven primarily by complex composite variables rather than systematic differential exclusion, the short median follow-up, and the inability to adjust for other comorbidities (chronic lung disease or liver disease). Nevertheless, our study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first to examine the association between LE8 and incident MACE in a nationally representative Korean cohort, and it further benefits from claims-linked outcome ascertainment, Korean-adapted dietary scoring, and comprehensive component-, outcome-, and subgroup-level analyses.
