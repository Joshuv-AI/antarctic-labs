Sharp Sobolev Sandwich and Approximation Rates of Radon-Domain L p L^{p} Ridge Integral Spaces for ReLU k Networks
Juncai He, Zitong Tian

Abstract
We develop the L p L^{p} space and approximation theory for shallow neural networks with ReLU k \mathrm{ReLU}^{k} activations. The central object is the Radon-domain L p L^{p} space ℛ ​ L k p ​ ( Ω ) \mathcal{R}L^{p}_{k}(\Omega) containing all functions on a bounded domain Ω \Omega that admit a ridge integral representation whose coefficient density belongs to L p L^{p} in the Radon domain. In the Hilbert case p = 2 p=2 , we prove by elementary Fourier analysis that this space recovers the critical Sobolev space H k + ( d + 1 ) / 2 ​ ( Ω ) H^{k+(d+1)/2}(\Omega) . For general 1 < p < ∞ 1<p<\infty , the identity becomes a Sobolev sandwich. The sharp gap of each side is exactly the Seeger–Sogge–Stein loss for the Radon transform as a Fourier integral operator. This also clarifies how the activation regularity and Radon back-projection jointly produce the regularity. As an application, we discretize the integral representation using a deterministic interpolation skeleton plus uniform sampling. This yields high-probability L p L^{p} approximation rates and the optimal Hilbert rate O ⁡ ( n − 1 2 − 2 ​ k + 1 2 ​ d ) O\!\big(n^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2k+1}{2d}}\big) at p = 2 p=2 for linearized neural networks.

Introduction

Although a great variety of specialized architectures now drive state-of-the-art results across machine learning, networks with a single hidden layer remain the basic building block from which the theory of deep learning is assembled. We write such a shallow network as an element of

\displaystyle\Sigma_{n}^{\sigma}:=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\,\sigma(w_{i}\cdot x+b_{i})\;:\;a_{i},b_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\ w_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right\},

The critical Sobolev space and integral representations.

Among Sobolev targets, the space
H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)
at the critical smoothness

\displaystyle s_{k}:=k+\frac{d+1}{2}=\frac{d+2k+1}{2}

is distinguished for the
\mathrm{ReLU}^{k}
activations: it is the reproducing
kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) generated by the bounded-bias ridge kernel

\displaystyle K(x,y)=\int_{\mathcal{M}_{R}}\sigma_{k}(w\cdot x-b)\,\sigma_{k}(w\cdot y-b)\,d\lambda(w,b),

with
R>R_{0}:=\sup_{x\in\Omega}\left|x\right|
, and
\mathcal{M}_{R}:=\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times[-R,R],d\lambda:=d\sigma\,db
.
The spectral computation underlying this
identification goes back to
[
3
]

who diagonalized the
\mathrm{ReLU}^{k}
kernel for the uniform feature
distribution, and was turned into a precise integral representation by
[

\tilde{x}=\binom{x}{1}
and normalizing the parameters to the sphere

\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}

\displaystyle H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)=\Big\{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\tilde{x})\,\psi(\theta)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta:\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})\Big\},\quad\left\|f\right\|_{H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)}\simeq\inf_{\psi}\left\|\psi\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})},

(1.2)

identify
H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)
as the
\mathrm{ReLU}^{k}
RKHS, and show that
linearized networks with fixed, well-distributed inner parameters

\{\theta_{j}^{*}\}\subset\mathbb{S}^{d}
already attain the optimal rate

O(n^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2k+1}{2d}})
in
H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)
.

[
46

also attains the same rate for

\sigma_{k}
.

The representation (

\mathbb{S}^{d}

spherical harmonics but at odds with the asymmetric roles the two parameters play

space theory and its approximations.

Random feature methods.

Fixing the inner parameters

into a convex least-squares problem. This is the random feature

52

which realizes a kernel as a Monte-Carlo average of feature products. Drawing the
features i.i.d. from a fixed (e.g. uniform) distribution reproduces the kernel

Monte-Carlo rate
n^{-1/2}

[
52
,
53
]

information-theoretic limit set by the kernel eigenvalues generally requires

[
4

and
by
[
53

[
7

studies random feature models beyond the Hilbertian regime by a duality framework.
Recent work
[

provides theoretical
backing for random feature methods in the critical Sobolev space

by linearized networks, with rate
\mathcal{O}\left((n/\log n)^{-s_{k}}\right)
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{*}\Lambda^{d-1}\mathcal{R}f(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\omega\right|^{d-1}\widehat{f}(\omega w)\,e^{i\omega(w\cdot x)}\,d\omega\,\,d\sigma(w)

(2.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{f}(\xi)\,e^{i\xi\cdot x}\,d\xi=c_{d}^{-1}f(x).

This gives the inversion formula

f=c_{d}\,\mathcal{R}^{*}\Lambda^{d-1}\mathcal{R}f
.

Radon–Plancherel isometry.

By the one-dimensional Plancherel identity in
b
, the slice theorem
(
2.4
), and (
2.3
) with

h(w,\omega)=\left|\widehat{f}(\omega w)\right|^{2}
,

\displaystyle\left\|\Lambda^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\mathcal{R}f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathcal{F}_{b}\big(\Lambda^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\mathcal{R}f\big)(w,\omega)\right|^{2}\,d\omega\,\,d\sigma(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}}\left|\omega\right|^{d-1}\left|\widehat{f}(\omega w)\right|^{2}\,d\omega\,\,d\sigma(w)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\widehat{f}(\xi)\right|^{2}\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi)^{d}}{\pi}\,\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=c_{d}^{-1}\,\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}.

(2.7)

From a sparsifying operator to the canonical density.

The exponent
k+1
is already visible in one dimension, where
\sigma_{k}=t_{+}^{k}/k!

is the Green’s function of
\partial_{t}^{k+1}
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{\,k+1}\sigma_{k}=\delta_{0}.

In several variables, the analogous role is played
by the Radon-domain operator
[
41
]

\mathrm{R}_{k}:=c_{d}\,\partial_{b}^{\,k+1}\,\Lambda^{d-1}\,\mathcal{R}
,
which
sparsifies
a single neuron: for a ridge atom

x\mapsto\sigma_{k}(w_{0}\cdot x-b_{0})
with
(w_{0},b_{0})\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}_{k}\big[\sigma_{k}(w_{0}\,\cdot\,-b_{0})\big]=\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(\delta_{(w_{0},b_{0})}+(-1)^{k+1}\delta_{(-w_{0},-b_{0})}\Big)\qquad\text{on }\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R},

(2.8)

a pair of antipodal point masses on the Radon cylinder

[
41
,
42
]
. The
antipodal sign
(-1)^{k+1}
records the evenness

\mathcal{R}f(w,b)=\mathcal{R}f(-w,-b)
of the Radon transform together with the

k+1
slice derivatives.

Following the terminology of frame and wavelet analysis, where an analysis
operator maps a signal to its coefficient representation and a synthesis
operator reconstructs a signal from coefficients
[
9
]
,
we call

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}^{k}:=\mathrm{R}_{k}=c_{d}\,\partial_{b}^{\,k+1}\,\Lambda^{d-1}\,\mathcal{R},

(2.9)

the
analysis operator
and
\mathrm{S}^{k},\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}
(
2.2
)
the
synthesis operators
.

Identity (
2.8
) shows that
\mathrm{A}^{k}
recovers the parameters of a
single neuron. Applied to a general target
u
, the slice theorem turns (
2.9
) into a
Fourier multiplier. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 2.1

(Canonical density)
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\widehat{u}\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The

canonical density
of
u
is
g_{u}:=\mathrm{A}^{k}u
, i.e. the distribution on

\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}
determined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{b}g_{u}(w,\omega):=c_{d}\,(i\omega)^{k+1}\left|\omega\right|^{d-1}\,\widehat{u}(\omega w),\qquad(w,\omega)\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}.

Here
(i\omega)^{k+1}
comes from
\partial_{b}^{k+1}
and
\left|\omega\right|^{d-1}
from

\Lambda^{d-1}
, so
g_{u}
inverts the integral
\mathrm{S}^{k}

(Proposition
2.1
). The two operators realize one
principle: on the Radon side an
L^{2}
density corresponds to an
H^{s_{k}}

function, with the gain

\displaystyle s_{k}=(k+1)+\tfrac{d-1}{2}

splitting into the
k+1
orders supplied by the ridge profile
\sigma_{k}
and the

\tfrac{d-1}{2}
orders supplied by the regularity of the dual Radon transform
\mathcal{R}^{*}
.
Theorem
2.1
makes this exact as a global isometry on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Proposition
2.1
synthesizes
u
from
g_{u}
via
\mathrm{S}^{k}
,
and Proposition
2.2
localizes the gain to the
bounded window
\mathcal{M}_{R}
, yielding the decomposition of
Theorem
2.2
and the norm equivalence of
Corollary
2.1
.

Warm up:
d=1
case of sandwich embedding.

The one-dimensional case already exhibits the whole mechanism. The loss

\delta_{p}=(d-1)\left|1/p-1/2\right|
vanishes for every
p
, the two exponents coincide,

s_{k,p}=r_{k,p}=k+1=s_{k}
, and the sandwich becomes a single norm equivalence

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}L^{p}_{k}(\Omega)=W^{k+1,p}(\Omega).

To see this, note that
\mathbb{S}^{0}=\{\pm 1\}
is two points, so

\mathcal{R}^{*}\Phi(x)=\Phi(1,x)+\Phi(-1,-x)
has order
(d-1)/2=0
and contributes no
smoothing of its own. Writing
g=(g_{+},g_{-})
for the two sheets (each extended by zero
off
[-R,R]
), the ridge integral is a pair of convolutions

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g(x)=(\sigma_{k}\ast g_{+})(x)+(\sigma_{k}\ast g_{-})(-x),

hence a
(k+1)
-fold antiderivative of
g
. As
\sigma_{k}=t_{+}^{k}/k!
solves

\partial^{\,k+1}\sigma_{k}=\delta_{0}
, differentiating
k+1
times returns the densities,
which is (
2.8
) for
d=1
, while the intermediate kernels

\partial^{\,\ell}\sigma_{k}=\sigma_{k-\ell}
with
0\leq\ell\leq k
stay bounded on the
compact window. Both bounds follow at once, and since no singular integral intervenes
the equivalence in fact holds for every
1\leq p\leq\infty
.

This also explains the canonical density. When
d=1
the analysis map

\mathrm{A}^{k}=c_{d}\,\partial_{b}^{\,k+1}\Lambda^{d-1}\mathcal{R}
collapses to

\partial^{\,k+1}
, so it is literally
(k+1)
-fold differentiation, of which

\mathrm{A}^{k}
is the Radon-domain generalization in higher dimension. Like

\partial^{\,k+1}
, whose null space is
\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the operator
\mathrm{A}^{k}

annihilates polynomials of degree
\leq k

[
41
, Lemma 19]
, so a density recovered from a given

u
is determined only up to such a polynomial, which is the polynomial term of
Proposition
2.1
. In arbitrary dimension the theory is this one-dimensional identity applied one Radon slice at a time. Along each line normal to

w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
the activation still inverts
\partial_{b}^{\,k+1}
, and the only

d
-dependent step is the reassembly by
\mathcal{R}^{*}
, which supplies the remaining

(d-1)/2
orders of
s_{k}=(k+1)+\tfrac{d-1}{2}
and, away from
p=2
, the loss

\delta_{p}\propto d-1
. Introducing the Radon transform is thus exactly the device that
turns the
d
-dimensional ridge problem into the elementary fact above followed by a
controlled back-projection.

2.1
An
L^{2}
isometry on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

Using the isometry (
2
) of
L^{2}
functions, we can show

Theorem 2.1

(Sobolev–Radon isometry)
.

Let
u\in H^{s_{k}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and let
g_{u}=\mathrm{A}^{k}u
be the canonical
density of
u
.
Then
g_{u}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})
, with the homogeneous isometry

\displaystyle\|g_{u}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})}=\sqrt{c_{d}}\,|u|_{\dot{H}^{s_{k}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

(2.10)

Proof.

For
u\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, Definition
2.1
together with

k+d=\frac{d-1}{2}+s_{k}
gives

\displaystyle\|g_{u}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})}^{2}=c_{d}^{2}\,\left\|\Lambda^{k+d}\mathcal{R}u\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})}^{2}=c_{d}^{2}\,\left\|\Lambda^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\mathcal{R}\,(-\Delta)^{s_{k}/2}u\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})}^{2},

where the second equality is the commutation (
2.5
).
Applying the Radon–Plancherel isometry (
2
) with

f=(-\Delta)^{s_{k}/2}u
,

\displaystyle\|g_{u}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})}^{2}=c_{d}^{2}\,c_{d}^{-1}\,\left\|(-\Delta)^{s_{k}/2}u\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=c_{d}\,|u|_{\dot{H}^{s_{k}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2},

which proves (
2.10
) by density.
∎

2.2
Ridge integral representation for compactly supported functions

The following technical lemma allows us to integrate
\sigma_{k}
against the
density
g_{u}
in the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We postpone the proof to the
appendix
A.1
.

Lemma 2.1

(Weighted integrability of the canonical density)
.

Let
u\in H^{s_{k}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be compactly supported, and let
g_{u}=\mathrm{A}^{k}u
be
the canonical density of
u
. Then
(1+|b|)^{k}g_{u}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})
.

Integrating the density
g_{u}
recovers
u
up to a polynomial of degree
k
.

Proposition 2.1

(Pointwise function representation from density)
.

Let
u\in H^{s_{k}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be compactly supported, with canonical density
g_{u}=\mathrm{A}^{k}u
.
Then there exists
p\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\displaystyle u(x)=\mathrm{S}^{k}g_{u}(x)+p(x)

holds pointwise for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. More compactly, one may have

\mathrm{S}^{k}\mathrm{A}^{k}=\mathrm{Id}_{H^{s_{k}}\cap C_{c}^{\infty}}\mod{\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
, for a.e.
w
the function

\displaystyle F(w,t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sigma_{k}(t-b)\,g_{u}(w,b)\,db

is well defined for every
t
, has at most polynomial growth of degree
k
in

t
, and satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}^{k}g_{u}=\mathcal{R}^{*}F,\qquad\partial_{t}^{k+1}F=g_{u}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}).

(2.11)

Let
F_{0}:=c_{d}\,\Lambda^{d-1}\mathcal{R}u
, that is,

\mathcal{F}_{b}F_{0}(w,\omega):=c_{d}\left|\omega\right|^{d-1}\widehat{u}(\omega w)
. Then

\partial_{t}^{k+1}F_{0}=g_{u}
as well, since

(i\omega)^{k+1}\mathcal{F}_{b}F_{0}=\mathcal{F}_{b}g_{u}
by
Definition
2.1
. The filtered back-projection (
2.6
) gives

\mathcal{R}^{*}F_{0}=c_{d}\,\mathcal{R}^{*}\Lambda^{d-1}\mathcal{R}u=u,\text{ in }\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

The difference
Q:=F-F_{0}
satisfies
\partial_{t}^{k+1}Q=0
, hence is a
polynomial of degree
\leq k
in
t
,

Q(w,t)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{j}(w)\,t^{j},a_{j}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}),

so that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{*}Q(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\langle a_{j}(w),(w\cdot x)^{j}\rangle\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Combining
\mathcal{R}^{*}F_{0}=u
with (
2.11
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}^{k}g_{u}=\mathcal{R}^{*}F=\mathcal{R}^{*}F_{0}+\mathcal{R}^{*}Q=u+\mathcal{R}^{*}Q\quad\text{in }\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

hence
u=\mathrm{S}^{k}g_{u}+p
with
p:=-\mathcal{R}^{*}Q\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Finally
s_{k}=k+(d+1)/2>d/2
, so
u
has a continuous representative. By the
weighted
L^{1}
bound of Lemma
2.1
,
\mathrm{S}^{k}g_{u}

is given by a locally absolutely convergent integral and is continuous by dominated
convergence, so the identity holds pointwise for every
x
.
∎

2.3
Bounded-bias regularization and

L^{2}
integral representation of

H^{k+(d+1)/2}(\Omega)

The dual Radon transform gives a derivative gain of order
(d-1)/2
when
p=2
.
We prove it by elementary Fourier analysis.

Lemma 2.2

(Localized dual Radon regularity)
.

Let
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})
, and

r\geq 0
. Then for every
\Phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1};H^{r}(\mathbb{R}))
,

\displaystyle\left\|\eta\,\mathcal{R}^{*}(\psi\Phi)\right\|_{H^{r+(d-1)/2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C_{\eta,\psi,r}\,\left\|\Phi\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1};H^{r}(\mathbb{R}))}.

(2.12)

Proof.

By density it suffices to treat
\Phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})
. For each
fixed
w
, multiplication by
\psi(w,\cdot)\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is bounded on

H^{r}(\mathbb{R})
, with operator norm controlled by finitely many derivatives of
\psi

uniformly in
w
; hence multiplication by
\psi
is bounded on

L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1};H^{r}(\mathbb{R}))
. We may therefore prove
(
2.12
) with right-hand side

\left\|\psi\Phi\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1};H^{r}(\mathbb{R}))}
and absorb the multiplier constant at
the end. Write
\Psi:=\psi\Phi
and
G:=\mathcal{F}_{b}\Psi
. Fourier inversion in the
second variable gives

\displaystyle\widehat{\eta\,\mathcal{R}^{*}\Psi}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{\eta}(\xi-\omega w)\,G(w,\omega)\,d\omega\,d\sigma(w).

Fix
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and set
\langle\omega\rangle:=(1+|\omega|^{2})^{1/2}
. Factoring the
integrand as

|\widehat{\eta}(\xi-\omega w)|^{1/2}\langle\omega\rangle^{-r}\cdot|\widehat{\eta}(\xi-\omega w)|^{1/2}\langle\omega\rangle^{r}|G(w,\omega)|

and applying Cauchy–Schwarz in
(w,\omega)
,

\displaystyle|\widehat{\eta\,\mathcal{R}^{*}\Psi}(\xi)|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,K(\xi)\,J(\xi),

(2.13)

where

\displaystyle K(\xi):

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\widehat{\eta}(\xi-\omega w)|\langle\omega\rangle^{-2r}\,d\omega\,d\sigma(w),

\displaystyle J(\xi):

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\widehat{\eta}(\xi-\omega w)|\langle\omega\rangle^{2r}|G(w,\omega)|^{2}\,d\omega\,d\sigma(w).

Denote
h(\zeta):=\langle\zeta\rangle^{-2r}|\zeta|^{-(d-1)}
, so that

K=2\,|\widehat{\eta}|*h
by polar coordinates (
2.3
). Since

h\notin L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
when
r\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
, we read off the decay of
K
from the
convolution itself rather than from
\left\|h\right\|_{L^{1}}
. By Peetre’s inequality

\langle\xi\rangle^{2r+d-1}\leq C\langle\xi-\zeta\rangle^{2r+d-1}\langle\zeta\rangle^{2r+d-1}
together with

\langle\zeta\rangle^{2r+d-1}h(\zeta)=\langle\zeta\rangle^{d-1}|\zeta|^{-(d-1)}\leq C\bigl(1+|\zeta|^{-(d-1)}\bigr)
, the factor

\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle^{2r+d-1}|\widehat{\eta}|\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

convolved against
1+|\zeta|^{-(d-1)}\in L^{\infty}+L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
is bounded
uniformly in
\xi
. Hence
K(\xi)\leq C_{0}\langle\xi\rangle^{-(2r+d-1)}
for all

r\geq 0
, with
C_{0}=C_{0}(\eta,r,d)
. Inserting this into
(
2.13
) gives

\langle\xi\rangle^{2r+d-1}|\widehat{\eta\,\mathcal{R}^{*}\Psi}(\xi)|^{2}\leq\tfrac{C_{0}}{(2\pi)^{2}}J(\xi)
. Integrating in
\xi
, and applying Tonelli’s
theorem together with the translation invariance

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{\eta}(\xi-\omega w)|\,d\xi=\left\|\widehat{\eta}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

(independent of
(w,\omega)
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\xi\rangle^{2r+d-1}|\widehat{\eta\,\mathcal{R}^{*}\Psi}(\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi\leq\frac{C_{0}\left\|\widehat{\eta}\right\|_{L^{1}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle\omega\rangle^{2r}|G(w,\omega)|^{2}\,d\omega\,d\sigma(w).

(2.14)

Since
2\bigl(r+\tfrac{d-1}{2}\bigr)=2r+d-1
, the left-hand side of
(
2.14
) is a constant multiple of

\left\|\eta\,\mathcal{R}^{*}\Psi\right\|_{H^{r+(d-1)/2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}
, while by Plancherel in
b

the right-hand side is a constant multiple of

\left\|\Psi\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1};H^{r}(\mathbb{R}))}^{2}
. Recalling
\Psi=\psi\Phi
and the
multiplier bound
\left\|\psi\Phi\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1};H^{r}(\mathbb{R}))}\leq C_{\psi}\left\|\Phi\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1};H^{r}(\mathbb{R}))}
proves
(
2.12
).
∎

Applying this lemma, we can show

Proposition 2.2

(Bounded-bias ridge integral regularity)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be bounded and let
R>R_{0}
. For
g\in L^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{R})
, define

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g(x):=\int_{\mathcal{M}_{R}}\sigma_{k}(w\cdot x-b)g(w,b)\,d\lambda(w,b).

Then
\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g\in H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)
and

\displaystyle\left\|\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g\right\|_{H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{R})},

(2.15)

where
C
depends only on
\Omega
,
R
,
d
and
k
.

Proof.

Choose
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}((-R,R))
with
\chi=1
on
[-R_{0},R_{0}]
. For a.e.

w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, set

\displaystyle F(w,t):=\int_{-R}^{R}\sigma_{k}(t-b)g(w,b)\,db.

Then, in the sense of distributions on
(-R,R)
,

\partial_{t}^{k+1}F(w,\cdot)=g(w,\cdot)
. Moreover, for
0\leq\ell\leq k
, the kernels

\partial_{t}^{\ell}\sigma_{k}(t-b)
are uniformly bounded on

\supp\chi\times[-R,R]
. Hence

\displaystyle\left\|\chi F(w,\cdot)\right\|_{H^{k+1}(\mathbb{R})}\leq C\left\|g(w,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}([-R,R])},

and integrating in
w
gives

\displaystyle\left\|\chi F\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1};H^{k+1}(\mathbb{R}))}\leq C\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{R})}.

For
x\in\Omega
one has
|w\cdot x|\leq R_{0}
, hence
\chi(w\cdot x)=1
, so

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g(x)=\mathcal{R}^{*}(\chi F)(x),\qquad x\in\Omega.

Fix
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\eta\equiv 1
on
\Omega
and

\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})
with
\psi\equiv 1
on

\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\supp\chi
, so that
\psi\,(\chi F)=\chi F
. By
Lemma
2.2
with
r=k+1
and

s_{k}=r+\tfrac{d-1}{2}
,

\displaystyle\left\|\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g\right\|_{H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\eta\,\mathcal{R}^{*}(\psi\,\chi F)\right\|_{H^{s_{k}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\left\|\chi F\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1};H^{k+1}(\mathbb{R}))}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{R})},

which proves (
2.15
).
∎

Remark 1
.

It is natural to ask whether a reverse inequality holds for
Proposition
2.2
. In general this is false, due to
the nontrivial null space of the localized ridge integral
operator
[
66
,
33
]
, even if

\Omega
is replaced by the full ball
B_{R}(0)
. One can construct
a large class of counterexamples by spherical harmonics in general dimensions.

For example, take
d=2
and
k=1
, and write

w=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)
. Set

\displaystyle g(\varphi,b):=\cos(3\varphi)\mathbf{1}_{[-R,R]}(b).

For
x\in B_{R}(0)
, let

t=w\cdot x=x_{1}\cos\varphi+x_{2}\sin\varphi
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{-R}^{R}(t-b)_{+}\,db=\int_{-R}^{t}(t-b)\,db=\frac{1}{2}(t+R)^{2}.

As a function of
\varphi
,
(t+R)^{2}
is a trigonometric polynomial with
angular frequencies at most
2
. It is therefore orthogonal to

\cos(3\varphi)
in
L^{2}([0,2\pi])
, and hence

\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g(x)=0
for every
x\in B_{R}(0)
, although

g\not\equiv 0
.

The polynomial part in Proposition
2.1
can be
realized by
\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}
. This lemma is elementary so we postpone the proof
to the appendix
A.2
.

Lemma 2.3

(
L^{p}
ridge realization of polynomials on the window)
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be bounded.
For every
q\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
there
exists
g_{q}\in L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})
, supported in
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times[-R,-R_{0}]
, such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g_{q}=q\quad\text{on }\Omega,\qquad\left\|g_{q}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})}\leq C\left\|q\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)},

with
C
depending only on
\Omega
,
R
,
d
,
k
, and
p
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be bounded and admit a bounded extension operator in

H^{s_{k}}
, and choose
R>R_{0}
. Now we can synthesize
the entire Sobolev space
H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)
as the image of
L^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{R})
under
\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

The ridge integral
\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}:L^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{R})\to H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)
is a surjective
bounded linear operator.

Proof.

Boundedness is Proposition
2.2
:

\left\|\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g\right\|_{H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{R})}
, and linearity
is immediate.

For surjectivity, let
u\in H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)
. Since
\Omega
admits a bounded
extension operator, choose a compactly supported
u_{e}\in H^{s_{k}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with

u_{e}|_{\Omega}=u
and
\left\|u_{e}\right\|_{H^{s_{k}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\left\|u\right\|_{H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)}
.
By Proposition
2.1
,

u_{e}=q_{u}+\mathrm{S}^{k}\widetilde{g}_{u}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
q_{u}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\widetilde{g}_{u}
is the canonical density of
u_{e}
. Split

\widetilde{g}_{u}=g_{u}+h_{u}
with
g_{u}:=\widetilde{g}_{u}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{M}_{R}}
and

h_{u}:=\widetilde{g}_{u}-g_{u}
; by Theorem
2.1
,

g_{u}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{R})
. For
x\in\Omega
the tail parameter obeys
\left|b\right|>R>\left|x\right|
,
so
\sigma_{k}(w\cdot x-b)
is a polynomial of degree
\leq k
in
x
and

r_{u}:=(\mathrm{S}^{k}h_{u})|_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Restricting the synthesis to

\Omega
,

\displaystyle u=(q_{u}+r_{u})+\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g_{u}\quad\text{on }\Omega,\qquad q_{u}+r_{u}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

By Lemma
2.3
there is
g_{\star}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{R})
with

\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g_{\star}=q_{u}+r_{u}
on
\Omega
. Hence

u=\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}(g_{u}+g_{\star})
with
g_{u}+g_{\star}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{R})
, proving
surjectivity.
∎

Corollary 2.1
.

\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}
induces a topological isomorphism between
L^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{R})/\ker\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}

and
H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)
; equivalently

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)}\simeq\inf\big\{\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{R})}:g\in L^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{R}),\ \mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g=u\text{ on }\Omega\big\}.

(2.16)

Proof.

By Theorem
2.2
,
\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}
is a bounded
linear surjection between Banach spaces with closed kernel, so it factors as a
continuous bijection
L^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{R})/\ker\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}\to H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)
, whose
inverse is continuous by the open mapping theorem. The right-hand side of
(
2.16
) is exactly the quotient norm.
∎

3
L^{p}
theory and Sobolev sandwich embedding

The exact isometry of Theorem
2.1
is special to the Hilbert
case: it rests on Plancherel, which has no
L^{p}
analogue. The mechanism behind
it is the regularity of the Radon transform
and its dual which survives for
p\neq 2
once we measure it
correctly. The right tool is the local
L^{p}
Sobolev regularity of
\mathcal{R}

and
\mathcal{R}^{*}
as Fourier integral operators, available precisely on the
open range
1<p<\infty
and carrying the sharp Seeger–Sogge–Stein loss

\delta_{p}
. The exact equality of
p=2
then degrades by
2\delta_{p}
into
the two-sided Sobolev sandwich of
Theorem
3.3
. Throughout this section

1<p<\infty
, and
W^{s,p}
denotes the Bessel-potential Sobolev space.
The sharp
L^{p}
regularity of the Radon transform
(Theorem
3.1
) carries the Seeger–Sogge–Stein loss

\displaystyle\delta_{p}:=(d-1)\left|\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}\right|,\qquad\rho_{p}:=\frac{d-1}{2}-\delta_{p}=(d-1)\min\!\left(\frac{1}{p},\,1-\frac{1}{p}\right)>0,

(3.1)

so that
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{*}
gain exactly
\rho_{p}
derivatives in
L^{p}

(Lemma
3.2
). Accordingly, set

\displaystyle s_{k,p}:=s_{k}+\delta_{p}=k+\frac{d+1}{2}+\delta_{p},\qquad r_{k,p}:=s_{k}-\delta_{p}=k+1+\rho_{p}.

Thus
s_{k,2}=r_{k,2}=s_{k}
, while
s_{k,p}-r_{k,p}=2\delta_{p}\geq 0
and

r_{k,p}\geq k+1>0
for every
p
. For
p\geq 2
these reduce to

s_{k,p}=k+d-\tfrac{d-1}{p}
and
r_{k,p}=k+1+\tfrac{d-1}{p}
, whereas for

1<p\leq 2
one has
\rho_{p}=\tfrac{d-1}{p^{\prime}}
,
p^{\prime}=\tfrac{p}{p-1}
. The exponent

s_{k,p}
controls the analysis map
u\mapsto\mathrm{A}^{k}u

(Theorem
3.2
); the exponent
r_{k,p}
controls the
synthesis map
g\mapsto\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g

(Proposition
3.1
).

Definition 3.1

(Radon-domain
L^{p}
space of order
k
)
.

Fix a bounded open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

R>R_{0}
. For
1<p<\infty
, the
Radon-domain
L^{p}

space of order
k
on
\Omega
for the
\sigma_{k}
activation is

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}L^{p}_{k}(\Omega):=\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}\bigl(L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})\bigr)\subset L^{p}(\Omega),

equipped with the quotient norm

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{R}L^{p}_{k}(\Omega)}:=\inf\left\{\left\|g\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})}:g\in L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R}),\ \mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g=u\text{ on }\Omega\right\}.

(3.2)

The map
\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}:L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})\to L^{p}(\Omega)
is bounded, by Hölder’s
inequality on the finite-measure window
\mathcal{M}_{R}
and the boundedness of the kernel

\sigma_{k}(w\cdot x-b)
on the compact set
\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{M}_{R}

(see (
3.13
) below). Hence
\ker\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}
is a

closed
subspace of
L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})
, and the quotient norm
(
3.2
) endows
\mathcal{R}L^{p}_{k}(\Omega)
with the
natural Banach-space structure: it is isometrically isomorphic to the quotient

L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})/\ker\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}
. Since
L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})
is separable and, for

1<p<\infty
, reflexive, the space
\mathcal{R}L^{p}_{k}(\Omega)
is separable and
reflexive as well
[
26
]
.

The definition is independent of the choice of
R>R_{0}
: enlarging
R
adds only
densities supported at bias
|b|>R_{0}
, which contribute polynomials of degree

\leq k
on
\Omega
, and these already lie in
\mathcal{R}L^{p}_{k}(\Omega)
by
Lemma
2.3
. When
p=2
, we have proved in
Theorem
2.2
and
Corollary
2.1
that

\mathcal{R}L^{2}_{k}(\Omega)=H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)
with equivalent norms.

3.1
L^{p}
regularity for the Radon transform

The Hilbertian computation behind Theorem
2.1
no longer
applies when
p\neq 2
, since it rests on Plancherel. What survives is the

regularity
of the Radon transform and its dual, now read off the

L^{p}
-Sobolev scale. The precise tool is the local
L^{p}
-Sobolev theory of Fourier
integral operators
(FIOs)
[
22
,
63
,
65
]
, whose sharp form
is the Seeger–Sogge–Stein theorem and whose loss is
\delta_{p}
.

Theorem 3.1

(Sharp
L^{p}
–Sobolev bounds for FIOs)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be smooth
d
-dimensional manifolds, and let

T\in I^{\mu}(Y,X;\mathcal{C})
be a properly supported Fourier integral operator whose
canonical relation is locally the graph of a homogeneous canonical transformation.
Then, for every
1<p<\infty
, every
s\in\mathbb{R}
, and every pair of compactly
supported cutoffs
\chi_{X},\chi_{Y}
,

\displaystyle\left\|\chi_{Y}T\chi_{X}f\right\|_{W^{s-\mu-\delta_{p},p}(Y)}\leq C\,\left\|f\right\|_{W^{s,p}(X)},\qquad\delta_{p}:=(d-1)\left|\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}\right|.

Let
\Pi_{Y\times X}:\mathcal{C}\longrightarrow Y\times X,\,(y,\eta;x,\xi)\longmapsto(y,x),

be the natural projection of the canonical relation to base variables. If
T
is
elliptic and
d\Pi_{Y\times X}
has maximal rank
2d-1
somewhere, then
the loss
\delta_{p}
cannot be improved.

Proof.

The estimate is the local
L^{p}
–Sobolev regularity theorem of
Seeger–Sogge–Stein
[
55
, Corollary 2.4]
; see also

[
65
, Ch. IX, Sec. 4]
and

[
63
, Ch. 6]
. The sharpness statement is
the corresponding elliptic maximal-rank sharpness result in

[
55
]
.
∎

A single microlocal fact lets us apply Theorem
3.1
here:
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{*}
are FIOs of order
-(d-1)/2
whose canonical relations, after
a conic cutoff separating the two antipodal branches, are local canonical graphs.
We record this and defer the verification together with a self-contained review of
the FIO vocabulary to Appendix
B
.

Lemma 3.1

(Radon transform as a Fourier integral operator)
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
Y:=\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}
, with
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
Y
both of dimension
d
.
The Radon transform
\mathcal{R}:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(Y)
and its dual

\mathcal{R}^{*}:C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are elliptic Fourier integral
operators of Hörmander order
-(d-1)/2
, with canonical relations
C_{\mathcal{R}}

and
C_{\mathcal{R}}^{t}
. Each relation has two antipodal branches, and a conic cutoff
removing one branch turns it into a local canonical graph. Consequently, for all
cutoffs
\chi_{X}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\chi_{Y}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Y)
, the localized
operators
\chi_{Y}\mathcal{R}\chi_{X}
and
\chi_{X}\mathcal{R}^{*}\chi_{Y}
satisfy hypotheses of Theorem
3.1
with
\mu=-(d-1)/2
, so the
loss there specializes to
\delta_{p}=(d-1)\left|1/p-1/2\right|
.

Proof.

See Section
B.5
.
∎

With Theorem
3.1
and Lemma
3.1
in hand, we record
the mapping properties of
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{*}
used below.

Lemma 3.2

(Local
L^{p}
–Sobolev mapping of the Radon
transform)
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
1<p<\infty
, and let
\delta_{p},\rho_{p}
be as in
(
3.1
). The order
-(d-1)/2
of
\mathcal{R}
and

\mathcal{R}^{*}
produces the Sobolev regularity gain

\displaystyle\frac{d-1}{2}-\delta_{p}=\rho_{p}\quad\bigl(=\tfrac{d-1}{p}\text{ when }p\geq 2\bigr).

(3.3)

That is, for all
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})
,
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\left\|\psi\,\mathcal{R}(\chi f)\right\|_{W^{s+\rho_{p},\,p}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\leq C\,\left\|f\right\|_{W^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

(3.4)

\displaystyle\left\|\chi\,\mathcal{R}^{*}(\psi\Phi)\right\|_{W^{s+\rho_{p},p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq C\,\left\|\Phi\right\|_{W^{s,p}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})}.

(3.5)

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
, after inserting the cutoffs and passing to a finite
atlas on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, both
\psi\mathcal{R}\chi
and
\chi\mathcal{R}^{*}\psi
are,
modulo smoothing operators, finite sums of properly supported FIOs of order

-(d-1)/2
whose canonical relations are local canonical graphs. The smoothing remainders map
W^{s,p}
continuously
into
C^{\infty}
and are therefore bounded. Theorem
3.1
with

\mu=-(d-1)/2
then yields the gain

s-\mu-\delta_{p}=s+\tfrac{d-1}{2}-\delta_{p}=s+\rho_{p}
, which is exactly
(
3.4
) and (
3.5
).
∎

Theorem 3.2

(
L^{p}
estimate for the canonical density)
.

Let
1<p<\infty
and fix any support radius
A\geq R_{0}
. If
u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

with
\supp u\subset B_{A}(0)
, then the canonical density
g_{u}=\mathrm{A}^{k}u
of
Definition
2.1
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\|g_{u}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})}\leq C_{A,d,k,p}\,\left\|u\right\|_{W^{s_{k,p},p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

(3.6)

Consequently
u\mapsto g_{u}
extends continuously to all
u\in W^{s_{k,p},p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

supported in
B_{A}(0)
, with (
3.6
) still valid.

Proof.

Set
N:=k+d
, and write
\mathcal{H}
for the Hilbert transform in the bias
variable
b
, normalized by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{b}(\mathcal{H}\Phi)(w,\omega)=-\,i\,\sgn(\omega)\,\mathcal{F}_{b}\Phi(w,\omega).

(3.7)

The multiplier defining
\mathrm{A}^{k}
in Definition
2.1

factors as

\displaystyle(i\omega)^{k+1}\left|\omega\right|^{d-1}=i^{\,1-d}\,(\sgn\omega)^{d-1}\,(i\omega)^{N},

(3.8)

an exact identity, since
(\sgn\omega)^{d-1}\,\omega^{d-1}=\left|\omega\right|^{d-1}
and

i^{1-d}i^{d-1}=1
. By (
3.7
), the factor
(\sgn\omega)^{d-1}

is the symbol of
(i\mathcal{H})^{d-1}
, which reduces to the identity when
d
is
odd and to
i\mathcal{H}
when
d
is even. Writing
M_{d}:=(i\mathcal{H})^{d-1}
and
using the slice identity

\mathcal{F}_{b}(\mathcal{R}u)(w,\omega)=\widehat{u}(\omega w)
, equation
(
3.8
) becomes

\displaystyle g_{u}=c_{d}\,i^{\,1-d}\,M_{d}\,\partial_{b}^{N}\mathcal{R}u\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}),

consistent with the multiplier definition of
\mathrm{A}^{k}
used in
Section
2
. The Hilbert
transform is bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for every
1<p<\infty
; see, e.g.,

[
14
,
65
]
.
Applying this in the variable
b
for
each fixed
w
and integrating in
w
shows that
M_{d}=(i\mathcal{H})^{d-1}
is
bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})
, hence

\displaystyle\left\|g_{u}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})}\leq C\,\left\|\partial_{b}^{N}\mathcal{R}u\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})}.

(3.9)

Since
\supp u\subset B_{A}(0)
, the Radon transform
\mathcal{R}u
is supported in

\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times[-A,A]
. Choose
\zeta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\zeta\equiv 1

on
B_{A}(0)
, and
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})
with
\psi\equiv 1
on

\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times[-A,A]
, so that
\mathcal{R}u=\psi\,\mathcal{R}(\zeta u)
. As

\partial_{b}
is a coordinate vector field on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{b}^{N}\mathcal{R}u\right\|_{L^{p}}=\left\|\partial_{b}^{N}\bigl(\psi\,\mathcal{R}(\zeta u)\bigr)\right\|_{L^{p}}\leq\left\|\psi\,\mathcal{R}(\zeta u)\right\|_{W^{N,p}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})}.

Because
s_{k,p}+\rho_{p}=N
(indeed

s_{k,p}+\rho_{p}=(s_{k}+\delta_{p})+(\tfrac{d-1}{2}-\delta_{p})=s_{k}+\tfrac{d-1}{2}=k+d
),
Lemma
3.2
(with
\chi=\zeta
,
f=u
,
s=s_{k,p}
) gives

\displaystyle\left\|\psi\,\mathcal{R}(\zeta u)\right\|_{W^{N,p}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})}\leq C\,\left\|u\right\|_{W^{s_{k,p},p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

(3.10)

Chaining (
3.9
)–(
3.10
) proves
(
3.6
) for
u\in C_{c}^{\infty}
. Both sides are
continuous in the
W^{s_{k,p},p}
topology, so the bound extends by density to all

u\in W^{s_{k,p},p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
supported in
B_{A}(0)
, and the limiting
g_{u}
agrees
with
\mathrm{A}^{k}u
distributionally.
∎

Lemma 3.3

(Localized dual Radon
L^{p}
regularity)
.

Let
1<p<\infty
and
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then

\displaystyle\left\|\eta\,\mathcal{R}^{*}\Phi\right\|_{W^{\rho_{p},\,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C_{\eta,p}\,\left\|\Phi\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})}

(3.11)

for all
\Phi\in L^{p}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})
, with
\rho_{p}
as in
(
3.1
).

Proof.

Pick
A>0
with
\supp\eta\subset B_{A}(0)
and
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})
with
\psi\equiv 1
on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times[-A,A]
. Since
\eta\,\mathcal{R}^{*}\Phi
only evaluates
\Phi(w,b)
at
b=w\cdot x
with
\left|w\cdot x\right|\leq A
on

\supp\eta
, we have
\eta\,\mathcal{R}^{*}\Phi=\eta\,\mathcal{R}^{*}(\psi\Phi)
.
Estimate (
3.5
) of Lemma
3.2
with

s=0
and
\chi=\eta
, together with (
3.3
),
gives (
3.11
).
∎

3.2
L^{p}
sandwich embedding between Sobolev spaces

With the regularity results in hand, we can now prove

Proposition 3.1

(
L^{p}
bounded-bias ridge regularity)
.

Let
1<p<\infty
, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be bounded and Lipschitz, and let

R>\sup_{x\in\Omega}|x|
. For
g\in L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})
, define

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g(x)=\int_{\mathcal{M}_{R}}\sigma_{k}(w\cdot x-b)g(w,b)\,d\lambda(w,b).

Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g\in W^{r_{k,p},p}(\Omega),\qquad\left\|\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g\right\|_{W^{r_{k,p},p}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|g\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})},

(3.12)

where
r_{k,p}=k+1+\rho_{p}
and
C
depends only on
\Omega,R,d,k,p
.

Proof.

The elementary estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|g\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})}

(3.13)

follows from Hölder’s inequality on the finite measure set
\mathcal{M}_{R}
and boundedness
of the kernel on
\Omega\times\mathcal{M}_{R}
.

Let
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{R}(0))
satisfy
\eta=1
on
\Omega
. For

|\alpha|=k+1
, differentiation in
x
gives, first for smooth
g
and then by

L^{p}
density,

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g=\mathcal{R}^{*}(w^{\alpha}g)\qquad\text{on }\Omega.

The choice of
\eta
ensures that
|w\cdot x|<R
on
\supp\eta
, so the delta
mass produced by
\partial_{t}^{k+1}\sigma_{k}=\delta_{0}
is evaluated inside the bias
window. Lemma
3.3
gives

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g\right\|_{W^{\rho_{p},p}(\Omega)}\leq\left\|\eta\mathcal{R}^{*}(w^{\alpha}g)\right\|_{W^{\rho_{p},p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\left\|g\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})}.

Finally, the standard Bessel-potential norm equivalence on Lipschitz
domains
[
1
]
gives

\displaystyle\left\|f\right\|_{W^{k+1+\rho_{p},p}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+\sum_{|\alpha|=k+1}\left\|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}f\right\|_{W^{\rho_{p},p}(\Omega)}\right).

Together with (
3.13
), this proves
(
3.12
).
∎

The two embeddings below differ by
s_{k,p}-r_{k,p}=2\delta_{p}
, the round trip of
the Seeger–Sogge–Stein loss through analysis and synthesis. At
p=2
this gap
closes and the sandwich becomes the norm equivalence

\mathcal{R}L^{2}_{k}(\Omega)=H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)
of
Corollary
2.1
.

Theorem 3.3

(
L^{p}
sandwich embedding between Sobolev spaces)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be bounded and Lipschitz, let
1<p<\infty
and let

R>R_{0}:=\sup_{x\in\Omega}|x|
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}L^{p}_{k}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow W^{r_{k,p},p}(\Omega),\qquad\left\|u\right\|_{W^{r_{k,p},p}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{R}L^{p}_{k}(\Omega)},

(3.14)

and

\displaystyle W^{s_{k,p},p}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{R}L^{p}_{k}(\Omega),\qquad\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{R}L^{p}_{k}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|u\right\|_{W^{s_{k,p},p}(\Omega)}.

(3.15)

Proof.

The first embedding is immediate from
Proposition
3.1
: if
u=\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g
on

\Omega
, then
\left\|u\right\|_{W^{r_{k,p},p}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|g\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})}
, and
taking the infimum over all such
g
gives (
3.14
).

For (
3.15
) we argue first on
C_{c}^{\infty}
and then pass to the
limit. Fix
A>0
with
\Omega\subset B_{A}(0)
and let

u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\supp u\subset B_{A}(0)
. Since
u\in H^{s_{k}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
Proposition
2.1
yields
q\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and the
canonical density
\widetilde{g}=\mathrm{A}^{k}u
with
u=q+\mathrm{S}^{k}\widetilde{g}
on

\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Set
g_{0}:=\widetilde{g}\,\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{M}_{R}}
. By
Theorem
3.2
(valid for
1<p<\infty
),

\displaystyle\left\|g_{0}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})}\leq\left\|\widetilde{g}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R})}\leq C\left\|u\right\|_{W^{s_{k,p},p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

(3.16)

Exactly as in the proof of Theorem
2.2
, for

x\in\Omega
the tail parameter obeys
|b|>R>|x|
, so

\bigl(\mathrm{S}^{k}(\widetilde{g}-g_{0})\bigr)|_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, whence

\displaystyle u=q_{0}+\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g_{0}\quad\text{on }\Omega,\qquad q_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

By (
3.13
) and (
3.16
),

\displaystyle\left\|q_{0}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\left\|u\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+\left\|\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g_{0}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|u\right\|_{W^{s_{k,p},p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

By Lemma

with exponent

on

on

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{R}L^{p}_{k}(\Omega)}\leq\left\|g_{0}+g_{q_{0}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})}\leq C\left\|u\right\|_{W^{s_{k,p},p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},\qquad u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{A}(0)).

Now let

be arbitrary and set

, a compactly

(enlarging

if necessary)

in

3.3
A microlocal proof of the
L^{p}
sandwich and its sharpness

The proof of Theorem
3.3
above separates the
Radon smoothing from the
k+1
derivatives carried by the activation. For readers
comfortable with Fourier integral operators, the same result has a shorter
microlocal interpretation. The relevant analysis and synthesis maps are the
compositions

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}^{k}

\displaystyle=c_{d}\,\partial_{b}^{k+1}\Lambda^{d-1}\mathcal{R},

(3.19)

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}^{k}g

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}^{*}(\sigma_{k}*_{b}g),\qquad\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g=\left.\mathcal{R}^{*}(\sigma_{k}*_{b}\widetilde{g})\right|_{\Omega},\quad\widetilde{g}:=g\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{M}_{R}}.

(3.20)

Here
\mathrm{S}^{k}
is the full-parameter synthesis operator appearing in the inversion
formula, whereas
\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}
is the bounded-bias synthesis map used in

\mathcal{R}L^{p}_{k}(\Omega)
. The FIO orders of this pair are:

\displaystyle\underbrace{(k+1)}_{\partial_{b}^{k+1}}+\underbrace{(d-1)}_{\Lambda^{d-1}}\underbrace{-\tfrac{d-1}{2}}_{\mathcal{R}}=s_{k},\qquad\underbrace{-(k+1)}_{\sigma_{k}\ast_{b}}\underbrace{-\tfrac{d-1}{2}}_{\mathcal{R}^{*}}=-s_{k}.

Thus the two maps have opposite FIO orders on each localized Radon branch.
The sandwich is then one application of the Seeger–Sogge–Stein theorem
(Theorem
3.1
) to each, and the two copies of the loss in

s_{k,p}-r_{k,p}=2\delta_{p}
are visibly
the same loss, traversed in opposite
directions
.

Lemma 3.4

(Analysis and synthesis as opposite-order Radon FIOs)
.

Let

and

with canonical relation

\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{R}}

Consequently, for all

\displaystyle\left\|\chi\,\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g\right\|_{W^{r_{k,p},p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

Proof.

For the analysis map, the

3.1
, applied with

For synthesis, the identity

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}*_{b}g=J_{b}^{-(k+1)}g+K_{\mathrm{low}}g+\sum_{j=0}^{k}b^{j}c_{j}(g),

; it is not a statement about the

\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}

Proposition 3.2

(Sharpness of the sandwich)
.

Under the same assumptions of Theorem
3.3
,
for every
\varepsilon>0
there is no continuous embedding

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}L^{p}_{k}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow W^{r_{k,p}+\varepsilon,p}(\Omega),

(3.24)

and no continuous embedding

\displaystyle W^{s_{k,p}-\varepsilon,p}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{R}L^{p}_{k}(\Omega).

(3.25)

For

, the gap

is therefore optimal in the
Bessel-potential scale. For
p=2
, this reduces to the standard sharpness of the
Sobolev scale around
H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)
.

Proof sketch.

For

the claim is the usual non-improvability of Sobolev embeddings. Assume

p\neq 2
. By Appendix
B.5
, the Radon canonical
relation has natural projection of maximal rank
2d-1
on each antipodal branch.
The elliptic pseudodifferential factors in (

) and in the

) do not change the canonical

and the FIO part of

satisfy precisely

.

Applying that sharpness to

rules out any estimate

on a

rules out any estimate

for the localized synthesis map. The

) are smoother

), after composing with the order-zero

The localization, proper-support reductions, and quotient-norm transfer are spelled
out in Appendix

.
∎

4
High-probability approximation for

1<p<\infty

Using the above space theory, we now discretize the integral representation
and prove a high-probability approximation result.
We retain the notation of Sections
2

and
3
:
\sigma_{k}(t)=t_{+}^{k}/k!
,

s_{k}=\tfrac{d+2k+1}{2}
,
\mathcal{M}_{R}=\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times[-R,R]
,

d\lambda(w,b)=d\sigma(w)\,db
, and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bounded Lipschitz
domain with
R>\sup_{x\in\Omega}\left|x\right|
fixed. Write

\displaystyle\phi^{k}_{\theta}(x):=\sigma_{k}(w\cdot x-b),\qquad\theta=(w,b)\in\mathcal{M}_{R},

for the bounded-bias ridge dictionary, and let

\displaystyle\mu_{R}:=\Lambda_{R}^{-1}\lambda,\qquad\Lambda_{R}:=\lambda(\mathcal{M}_{R})=2R\,\left|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\right|,

be the uniform probability measure on
\mathcal{M}_{R}
. Recall that

\displaystyle Q_{d,k}:=\dim\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\binom{d+k}{k},\qquad\gamma_{k,p}:=\frac{k+1/p}{d},

\displaystyle\alpha_{p}:=\min\left\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}\right\}=1-\frac{1}{\min(p,2)},\qquad p^{\prime}:=\frac{p}{p-1}.

Thus
\alpha_{p}=\tfrac{1}{2}
for
2\leq p<\infty
, while

\alpha_{p}=\tfrac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1-\tfrac{1}{p}
for
1<p<2
. In particular

\gamma_{k,2}=\tfrac{2k+1}{2d}=\gamma_{k}
and

\alpha_{2}+\gamma_{k,2}=\tfrac{1}{2}+\gamma_{k}=s_{k}/d
.

The space theory of Section
3
is valid on the full range

1<p<\infty
. The sampling step, however, depends on the Rademacher type of

L^{p}(\Omega)
, which equals
\min(p,2)
with type constant depending only on
p

[
27
]
. Accordingly, the theorem
below has the sampling exponent
\alpha_{p}=1-1/\min(p,2)
. For
p\geq 2
this is the
usual Hilbertian Monte-Carlo exponent
\tfrac{1}{2}
; for
1<p<2
it becomes the
type-
p
exponent
\tfrac{1}{p^{\prime}}
. We write

\mathcal{R}L^{p}_{k}(\Omega)
for the Radon-domain

space of order

of
Definition
3.1
; recall that for

one has

\mathcal{R}L^{2}_{k}(\Omega)=H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)
with equivalent norms
(Corollary
2.1
). The result of this section
discretizes the bounded-bias decomposition of Sections
2

and
3
into a mixed feature model:
\mathcal{O}(n)

neurons together with
n
i.i.d. uniform neurons achieve the rate

with high probability. For

this is the optimal

. The comparison with the Sobolev exponent for

is recorded in

.

Theorem 4.1

(High-probability approximation)
.

There exist

, depending only on

, such that for every

there are deterministic points

\displaystyle\Big\|u-\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\,\phi^{k}_{\zeta_{i}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}\,\phi^{k}_{\Theta_{j}}\Big\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C\bigl(\log(2/\delta)\bigr)^{\alpha_{p}}\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{R}L^{p}_{k}(\Omega)}\ n^{-\alpha_{p}-\gamma_{k,p}}

.

Corollary 4.1

(Sobolev form and the optimal Hilbert rate)

Assume that

holds with probability at least

.
In the Hilbert case

, and

\displaystyle\Big\|u-\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\,\phi^{k}_{\zeta_{i}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}\,\phi^{k}_{\Theta_{j}}\Big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)}\,\left\|u\right\|_{H^{s_{k}}(\Omega)}\,n^{-s_{k}/d}

The proof of Theorem

combines two ingredients.
First, a
deterministic skeleton

(Proposition

): piecewise Lagrange interpolation
in the parameter

approximates every dictionary element by

in the type

4.1
A deterministic skeleton on the parameter cylinder

For a Banach space
H
, a cube
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\alpha\in(0,1]
, let

C^{k,\alpha}(Q;H)
denote the maps
F:Q\to H
whose

-valued Fréchet
derivatives up to order

exist and are bounded on
Q
, equipped with the
seminorm

\displaystyle\left|F\right|_{C^{k,\alpha}(Q;H)}:=\max_{\left|\beta\right|=k}\,\sup_{\eta\neq\eta^{\prime}\in Q}\frac{\left\|D^{\beta}F(\eta)-D^{\beta}F(\eta^{\prime})\right\|_{H}}{\left|\eta-\eta^{\prime}\right|^{\alpha}}.

Lemma 4.1

(Parameter smoothness of the dictionary)
.

Let
1<p<\infty
,
\mathcal{C}:=[-1,1]^{d}
, and let
\varphi:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{M}_{R}
be a smooth
chart. Then
P(\eta):=\phi^{k}_{\varphi(\eta)}
belongs to

C^{k,1/p}(\mathcal{C};L^{p}(\Omega))
, with
\left|P\right|_{C^{k,1/p}}
and

\max_{\left|\beta\right|\leq k}\sup_{\mathcal{C}}\left\|D^{\beta}P\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}
bounded by a
constant depending only on
\Omega
,
R
,
d
,
k
,
p
, and the chart.

Proof.

Write
\varphi(\eta)=(w(\eta),b(\eta))
and
t(x,\eta):=w(\eta)\cdot x-b(\eta)
.
Since
\sigma_{k}^{(j)}=\sigma_{k-j}
for
0\leq j\leq k
, the chain rule gives, for

1\leq\left|\beta\right|\leq k
,

\displaystyle D^{\beta}_{\eta}\,\sigma_{k}(t(x,\eta))=\sum_{j=1}^{\left|\beta\right|}q_{\beta,j}(x,\eta)\,\sigma_{k-j}(t(x,\eta)),

(4.4)

where each
q_{\beta,j}
is a polynomial in
x
with coefficients smooth in

\eta
; all these factors, and the truncated powers
\sigma_{k-j}
with

0\leq k-j\leq k
, are bounded on
\Omega\times\mathcal{C}
. Difference quotients of the
right-hand side converge in
L^{p}(\Omega)
by dominated convergence (the
exceptional set
\{x\in\Omega:t(x,\eta)=0\}
is a hyperplane slice, of measure
zero), so
D^{\beta}P
exists as an
L^{p}(\Omega)
-valued derivative, is given by
(

), and is bounded.

For the Hölder bound, fix
\left|\beta\right|=k
. When
k=0
, this means estimating

P(\eta)=\mathbf{1}_{\{t(\cdot,\eta)>0\}}
itself, and the slab estimate below
directly gives the required

-Hölder continuity. Assume now
k\geq 1
. In
(
4.4
) every term with

involves

\sigma_{k-j}
with
k-j\geq 1
and is therefore Lipschitz in
\eta
, uniformly over

x\in\Omega
. The only term requiring care is

, which contains the indicator

\sigma_{0}(t)=\mathbf{1}_{\{t>0\}}
. On the symmetric difference

\{t(\cdot,\eta)>0\}\,\triangle\,\{t(\cdot,\eta^{\prime})>0\}
one has

\left|t(x,\eta)\right|\leq\left|t(x,\eta)-t(x,\eta^{\prime})\right|\leq C\left|\eta-\eta^{\prime}\right|
for

x\in\Omega
; since a slab of width
\varepsilon
meets the bounded set
\Omega

in measure
O(\varepsilon)
,

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{1}_{\{t(\cdot,\eta)>0\}}-\mathbf{1}_{\{t(\cdot,\eta^{\prime})>0\}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big|\{x\in\Omega:\left|w(\eta)\cdot x-b(\eta)\right|\leq C\left|\eta-\eta^{\prime}\right|\}\big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C^{\prime}\left|\eta-\eta^{\prime}\right|.

Thus the indicator term is
\tfrac{1}{p}
-Hölder in
L^{p}(\Omega)
. Combining the
Lipschitz factors with this
\tfrac{1}{p}
-Hölder factor yields

\left|P\right|_{C^{k,1/p}(\mathcal{C};L^{p}(\Omega))}<\infty
.
∎

Lemma 4.2

(Function-valued Bramble–Hilbert estimate)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the reference cube, and let

for

\displaystyle\sup_{\eta\in Q}\Big\|F(\eta)-\sum_{i=1}^{Q_{d,k}}\ell_{i}^{Q}(\eta)\,F(y_{i}^{Q})\Big\|_{H}\leq C\,h^{k+\alpha}\,\left|F\right|_{C^{k,\alpha}(Q;H)},

where

Proof.

The pullback

and let

4.2

\min(p,2)

We recall the relevant Banach-space geometry. A Banach space

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{q}W_{j}\right\|_{B}^{r}\leq C_{r}\,T_{r}(B)^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{q}\mathbb{E}\left\|W_{j}\right\|_{B}^{r}.

We shall only use this consequence below. For

and this type is optimal in infinite-dimensional

spaces. See

[

; for the application of

[

.

\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g_{q}=q
on
\Omega
. Finally, since

\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times[-R,-R_{0}]
has finite measure and
g_{q}
is built from
a_{i}
by a
fixed bounded linear map,

\displaystyle\left\|g_{q}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})}\leq C\sum_{m=0}^{k}\left\|\nu_{m}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\leq C\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left\|a_{i}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\leq C\left\|q\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)},

where the last step uses equivalence of all norms on the finite-dimensional space

\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(here
\Omega
has nonempty interior, so
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

is a norm on
\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
).
∎

B.1
Oscillatory integrals and symbol classes

Symbols.

For
m\in\mathbb{R}
, the symbol class
S^{m}(X\times\mathbb{R}^{N})
consists of

a\in C^{\infty}(X\times\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that, for every compact
K\subset X
and all
multi-indices
\alpha,\gamma
,

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{x}^{\gamma}\partial_{\theta}^{\alpha}a(x,\theta)\right|\leq C_{K,\alpha,\gamma}\,(1+\left|\theta\right|)^{\,m-\left|\alpha\right|},\qquad x\in K,\ \theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

A symbol is
classical
(polyhomogeneous) if it
admits an asymptotic expansion
a\sim\sum_{j\geq 0}a_{m-j}
with
a_{m-j}
positively
homogeneous of degree
m-j
in
\theta
for
\left|\theta\right|\geq 1
; its
principal
symbol
is
a_{m}
, and
a
is
elliptic
if
a_{m}(x,\theta)\neq 0
for

\theta\neq 0
.

Phase functions.

A
phase function
on
X
with
N
phase variables is a real

\varphi\in C^{\infty}\!\bigl(X\times(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus 0)\bigr)
, positively homogeneous
of degree
1
in
\theta
, with
d\varphi\neq 0
. It is
nondegenerate
if the

N
differentials
d_{x,\theta}\bigl(\partial_{\theta_{1}}\varphi\bigr),\dots,d_{x,\theta}\bigl(\partial_{\theta_{N}}\varphi\bigr)
are linearly independent on the
critical set

\displaystyle C_{\varphi}:=\bigl\{(x,\theta)\in X\times(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus 0):\ d_{\theta}\varphi=0\bigr\}.

(B.1)

Oscillatory integrals.

Given a nondegenerate phase
\varphi
and
a\in S^{m}
, the oscillatory integral

\displaystyle I_{\varphi}(a)(x):=(2\pi)^{-N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}e^{\,i\varphi(x,\theta)}\,a(x,\theta)\,d\theta

converges absolutely when
m<-N
. For general
m
it is defined as a distribution
by regularization: with
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
\chi\equiv 1
near
0
, one
inserts
\chi(\theta/\Lambda)
and integrates by parts using the first-order
operator

\displaystyle L=\left|d_{\theta}\varphi\right|^{-2}\sum_{j}(\partial_{\theta_{j}}\varphi)\,\tfrac{1}{i}\partial_{\theta_{j}},

which satisfies
L\,e^{i\varphi}=e^{i\varphi}
away from
C_{\varphi}
and lowers the
symbol order by one at each application. The limit
\Lambda\to\infty
exists in

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(X)
and is independent of
\chi
and of the chosen
L
.

B.2
Fourier integral operators and their order

A
Fourier integral operator

T:C_{c}^{\infty}(X)\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(Y)
is an
operator whose Schwartz kernel is locally an oscillatory integral

\displaystyle K_{T}(y,x)=(2\pi)^{-N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}e^{\,i\varphi(y,x,\theta)}\,a(y,x,\theta)\,d\theta,\qquad a\in S^{m},

(B.2)

with
\varphi
a nondegenerate phase on
Y\times X
in the sense
of (
B.1
). The operator is
properly supported
if the two
coordinate projections

\operatorname{supp}K_{T}\to Y,\,\operatorname{supp}K_{T}\to X

are proper maps, which means preimages of compact sets remain compact.
Since all estimates used in this paper are
local, one may insert compact cutoffs or use the standard proper-support
decomposition and work microlocally with properly supported pieces. See

[
63
,
22
]
.

The
Hörmander order
of (
B.2
) is

[
22
]

\displaystyle\mu\,=\,m\,+\,\frac{N}{2}\,-\,\frac{n_{X}+n_{Y}}{4},

(B.3)

and one writes
T\in I^{\mu}(Y,X;\mathcal{C})
, where
\mathcal{C}
is the canonical relation of
Section
B.4
. The normalization (
B.3
) is fixed by the
requirement that a pseudodifferential operator of order
m
have FIO order
m
:
its kernel
(2\pi)^{-n}\!\int e^{i(x-y)\cdot\xi}a(x,\xi)\,d\xi
on
X=Y=\mathbb{R}^{n}
has

N=n
phase variables and amplitude order
m
, and (
B.3
) returns

m+\tfrac{n}{2}-\tfrac{2n}{4}=m
.

Pseudodifferential operators.

The pseudodifferential operators are exactly the FIOs carrying the phase

(x-y)\cdot\xi
. We write
\Psi^{m}(X)
for those of order
m
:
P\in\Psi^{m}(X)
acts
locally by

\displaystyle Pf(x)=(2\pi)^{-n_{X}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{X}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{X}}}e^{\,i(x-y)\cdot\xi}\,p(x,\xi)\,f(y)\,dy\,d\xi,\qquad p\in S^{m}.

Their canonical relation is the diagonal of

(T^{*}X\setminus 0)\times(T^{*}X\setminus 0)
, the graph of the identity, and unlike a
generic FIO they map
W^{s,p}(X)\to W^{s-m,p}(X)
for every
1<p<\infty
with

no
extra loss (the Calderón–Zygmund theory). The loss
\delta_{p}
of
Theorem
3.1
is the price of a canonical relation that genuinely
moves the base point, which the diagonal does not.

The Radon transform.

The Radon transform
\mathcal{R}:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(Y)
,

Y=\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}
, has Schwartz kernel
\delta(b-w\cdot x)
, i.e. the
oscillatory representation

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}f(w,b)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\,i\tau(b-w\cdot x)}f(x)\,dx\,d\tau,

(B.4)

with a single phase variable
\tau\in\mathbb{R}\setminus 0
(so
N=1
), phase

\varphi=\tau(b-w\cdot x)
, and amplitude
\equiv 1
(so
m=0
). The phase is
nondegenerate:
\partial_{\tau}\varphi=b-w\cdot x
, whose differential has

db
-component
1
and is therefore nonzero on
C_{\varphi}=\{b=w\cdot x\}
. By
(
B.3
) with
n_{X}=n_{Y}=d
,

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathcal{R}}=0+\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{d+d}{4}=-\tfrac{d-1}{2}.

The amplitude
\equiv 1
never vanishes, so
\mathcal{R}
is elliptic. Its canonical
relation is identified in Section
B.4
, and the order, ellipticity, and
local-graph property are assembled into a proof of Lemma
3.1
in
Section
B.5
.

B.3
The wave front set

Heuristic.

The Fourier transform turns smoothness into decay: a compactly supported
distribution is smooth exactly when its Fourier transform is rapidly decreasing.
Localizing by a cutoff
\phi
supported near a point
x_{0}
, the piece
\phi u
is
smooth near
x_{0}
if and only if
\widehat{\phi u}(\xi)
decays rapidly in
every direction. A singularity at
x_{0}
persists only along those frequency
directions
\xi
in which
\widehat{\phi u}
fails to rapidly decay. The wave front
set records precisely this directional refinement of the singular support: not
merely where
u
is singular, but in which directions the obstruction
to smoothness lies. These directions are intrinsically covectors, not vectors: the
frequency
\xi
enters only through the phase
x\cdot\xi
, where it is paired with
the displacement
x
, so under a change of coordinates
x=\kappa(\tilde{x})
it
transforms by the inverse transpose
{}^{t}(d\kappa)^{-1}
of the Jacobian, which is the
transformation law of a cotangent vector, the one that keeps
\xi\,dx
invariant.
The set of non-decay directions at
x_{0}
is thus a conic subset of the fibre

T^{*}_{x_{0}}X
, and
WF(u)
lives in the cotangent bundle
T^{*}X\setminus 0
rather than
in
TX
.

Definition.

For
u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(X)
, a point
(x_{0},\xi_{0})\in T^{*}X\setminus 0
is

not
in the wave front set
WF(u)
if there exist
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X)

with
\phi(x_{0})\neq 0
and an open cone
\Gamma\ni\xi_{0}
such that, in a coordinate
chart,

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\phi u}(\xi)\right|\leq C_{M}\,(1+\left|\xi\right|)^{-M}\qquad\text{for all }\xi\in\Gamma\text{ and all }M\in\mathbb{N}.

Thus
WF(u)\subset T^{*}X\setminus 0
is closed, conic, and coordinate-free. Let

\pi_{X}:T^{*}X\to X
,
(x,\xi)\mapsto x
, denote the base (bundle) projection; then

\displaystyle\pi_{X}\bigl(WF(u)\bigr)=\operatorname{sing\,supp}u,

so the wave front set refines the singular support. Pseudodifferential operators
are
microlocal
:
WF(Pu)\subset WF(u)
for every
P\in\Psi^{m}(X)
.

Examples.

The point mass has full cotangent fibre over its support,

WF(\delta_{x_{0}})=\{x_{0}\}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n_{X}}\setminus 0)
. More generally, if

S\subset X
is a smooth submanifold and
u
is
conormal
to
S
(e.g. the
surface delta
\delta_{S}
), then

\displaystyle WF(u)\subset N^{*}S\setminus 0,\qquad N^{*}S:=\bigl\{(x,\xi)\in T^{*}X:\ x\in S,\ \xi|_{T_{x}S}=0\bigr\},

(B.5)

the conormal bundle of
S
. In particular the Radon kernel
\delta(b-w\cdot x)

is conormal to the incidence manifold
Z=\{(w,b,x):b=w\cdot x\}\subset Y\times X
,
so
WF\bigl(K_{\mathcal{R}}\bigr)\subset N^{*}Z\setminus 0
; after the sign twist of
Section
B.4
this conormal bundle is exactly the canonical relation

\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{R}}
of
\mathcal{R}
computed in (
B.8
)
below.

Singularities under the Radon transform.

The geometric meaning of
\mathcal{R}
being an FIO with canonical relation

\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{R}}
is that it transports wave front sets along
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{R}}
:

\displaystyle WF(\mathcal{R}f)\subset\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{R}}\circ WF(f).

(B.6)

Concretely, a singularity of
f
at
(x_{0},\xi_{0})
produces a singularity of

\mathcal{R}f
over precisely the hyperplane through
x_{0}
whose unit normal is

w=\pm\,\xi_{0}/\left|\xi_{0}\right|
, at offset
b=w\cdot x_{0}
. The intuition is transversal
averaging: integrating
f
over a moving family of hyperplanes detects a jump of

f
across a hypersurface only when the integration plane is
tangent
to that
hypersurface (its normal aligned with
\xi_{0}
). Planes meeting the singularity
transversally average it away and leave
\mathcal{R}f
smooth there. Estimate
(
B.6
) is the qualitative shadow of the quantitative

L^{p}
–Sobolev bound the SSS theorem provides.

B.4
Lagrangian submanifolds and canonical relations

Lagrangians.

Equip
T^{*}X
with the tautological one-form
\alpha_{X}=\xi\,dx=\sum_{j}\xi_{j}\,dx_{j}

and the canonical symplectic form
\omega_{X}=d\alpha_{X}=\sum_{j}d\xi_{j}\wedge dx_{j}
. A
submanifold
\Lambda\subset T^{*}X
is
Lagrangian
if
\dim\Lambda=n_{X}
and

\omega_{X}|_{\Lambda}=0
. The Lagrangians relevant to FIOs are conic. Two model
families: the conormal bundles
N^{*}S
of (
B.5
), and the
phase-generated Lagrangians

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\varphi}=\bigl\{(x,d_{x}\varphi(x,\theta)):\ (x,\theta)\in C_{\varphi}\bigr\},

which is a conic Lagrangian whenever
\varphi
is nondegenerate; conversely, every
conic Lagrangian is locally of this form

[
22
, §3.1]
. A distribution

u\in I^{m}(X,\Lambda)
associated with
\Lambda
satisfies
WF(u)\subset\Lambda
,
and FIO kernels are exactly the Lagrangian distributions whose Lagrangian is the
twist of a canonical relation.

Canonical relations, transformations, and the twist.

On the product
T^{*}Y\times T^{*}X
, let
\operatorname{pr}_{Y}
and
\operatorname{pr}_{X}

denote the two factor projections, and form the
twisted
symplectic form

\displaystyle\omega_{Y}\ominus\omega_{X}:=\operatorname{pr}_{Y}^{*}\omega_{Y}-\operatorname{pr}_{X}^{*}\omega_{X}.

(B.7)

A
canonical relation
is a conic Lagrangian

\mathcal{C}\subset(T^{*}Y\setminus 0)\times(T^{*}X\setminus 0)
for
\omega_{Y}\ominus\omega_{X}
. A

homogeneous canonical transformation
is a homogeneous diffeomorphism

\chi:T^{*}X\setminus 0\to T^{*}Y\setminus 0
with
\chi^{*}\omega_{Y}=\omega_{X}
. The two
notions stand to each other as
map
to
graph
: the graph

\mathcal{C}_{\chi}=\{(\chi(x,\xi);\,x,\xi):(x,\xi)\in T^{*}X\setminus 0\}
of a canonical
transformation is a canonical relation, and the identity
\chi^{*}\omega_{Y}=\omega_{X}

is exactly what makes
\mathcal{C}_{\chi}
Lagrangian for the
minus
sign
in (
B.7
). Canonical relations are strictly more general:
a relation need not be a graph, since its projections to the two factors
may degenerate. It is locally a graph and then the graph of a canonical
transformation precisely under the condition isolated below.

The twist.
The minus sign in (
B.7
) is what couples the
two factors symplectically, but it prevents
\mathcal{C}
from being a Lagrangian submanifold
in the usual (untwisted) sense. The fibrewise antipode

\iota:(x,\xi)\mapsto(x,-\xi)
on the
X
-factor repairs this: since

\iota^{*}\omega_{X}=-\omega_{X}
, applying
\mathrm{id}_{T^{*}Y}\times\iota
converts a
Lagrangian for
\omega_{Y}\ominus\omega_{X}
into an honest Lagrangian for the

sum

\omega_{Y\times X}=\omega_{Y}\oplus\omega_{X}
, the canonical symplectic
form of
T^{*}(Y\times X)
. Accordingly the kernel of
T\in I^{\mu}(Y,X;\mathcal{C})
is a
Lagrangian distribution on
Y\times X
associated with the untwisted Lagrangian

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\bigl\{(y,\eta;\,x,-\xi):(y,\eta;\,x,\xi)\in\mathcal{C}\bigr\}\subset T^{*}(Y\times X),

and the order
\mu
of
T
is, by definition, the order of this Lagrangian
distribution.

The local-graph condition.

The two projections

\displaystyle\pi_{L}:\mathcal{C}\to T^{*}Y\setminus 0,\qquad\pi_{R}:\mathcal{C}\to T^{*}X\setminus 0

need not be diffeomorphisms in general. One says
\mathcal{C}
is a
local canonical
graph
when both
\pi_{L}
and
\pi_{R}
are local diffeomorphisms, equivalently when

\mathcal{C}
is locally the graph
\mathcal{C}_{\chi}
of a canonical transformation
\chi
. This
nondegeneracy is precisely the geometric hypothesis under which the
Seeger–Sogge–Stein
L^{p}
–Sobolev regularity theorem
(Theorem
3.1
) applies, with the sharp loss

\delta_{p}=(d-1)\left|1/p-1/2\right|
.

B.5
Proof of the Radon FIO lemma

We can now collect the verification deferred from the main text and prove
Lemma
3.1
.

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

We argue for
\mathcal{R}
; the dual
\mathcal{R}^{*}
follows by transposition.
Throughout we use the standard FIO calculus for smooth densities: passing from
half-densities to the product measures
d\sigma(w)\,db
on
Y
and
dx
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

conjugates the operators by smooth elliptic factors and alters neither the order
nor the canonical relation

[
18
, §6]
,

[
50
, App. A]
.

FIO structure, order, ellipticity.
The oscillatory representation
(
B.4
) exhibits
\mathcal{R}
as a Fourier integral operator
with a single phase variable (
N=1
), phase
\varphi=\tau(b-w\cdot x)
homogeneous
of degree one, and amplitude
\equiv 1
(so
m=0
). The phase is nondegenerate,
since
\partial_{\tau}\varphi=b-w\cdot x
has
d_{w,b,x}(\partial_{\tau}\varphi)
with

db
-component
1
, hence nonzero, on
C_{\varphi}=\{b=w\cdot x\}
. The order formula
(
B.3
) with
n_{X}=n_{Y}=d
gives
\mu_{\mathcal{R}}=-(d-1)/2
, and the
nonvanishing amplitude makes
\mathcal{R}
elliptic.

Canonical relation.
Identify
T_{w}^{*}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\cong T_{w}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
by the
round metric and write
x_{w}^{\top}:=x-(x\cdot w)w
. Differentiating
\varphi
gives

d_{b}\varphi=\tau
,
d_{w}\varphi=-\tau x_{w}^{\top}
, and
-d_{x}\varphi=\tau w
, so

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{R}}=\bigl\{(w,b;-\tau x_{w}^{\top},\tau;\ x,\tau w):\ b=w\cdot x,\ \tau\neq 0\bigr\}\subset(T^{*}Y\setminus 0)\times(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus 0).

(B.8)

Local-graph property.
The input covector is
\xi=\tau w
, so for fixed

(x,\xi)
there are exactly the two antipodal solutions

(w,\tau)=(\xi/\left|\xi\right|,\left|\xi\right|)
and
(-\xi/\left|\xi\right|,-\left|\xi\right|)
; a conic cutoff
selecting one sign of
\tau
removes the second branch. On
\tau>0
the relation
(
B.8
) is the graph of the homogeneous canonical
transformation

\displaystyle(x,\xi)\ \longmapsto\ \Bigl(\tfrac{\xi}{\left|\xi\right|},\ x\cdot\tfrac{\xi}{\left|\xi\right|}\,;\ -\left|\xi\right|\,x_{w}^{\top},\ \left|\xi\right|\Bigr),\qquad w=\tfrac{\xi}{\left|\xi\right|},

with smooth inverse
x=bw-\eta_{w}/\eta_{b}
,
\xi=\eta_{b}w
(here
\eta_{w},\eta_{b}
are
the
T^{*}Y
components
-\tau x_{w}^{\top},\tau
); the branch
\tau<0
is identical with

\eta_{b}<0
. Each branch is therefore a local canonical graph, so every compactly
cut-off piece
\chi_{Y}\mathcal{R}\chi_{X}
meets the geometric hypothesis of
Theorem
3.1
.

Natural projection rank.

The sharpness part of Theorem
3.1
uses the natural projection of

\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{R}}
to the base variables
(w,b,x)\in Y\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. On the branch

\tau>0
, parametrize the relation by
(x,w,\tau)
with
w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and

\tau>0
:

\displaystyle(x,w,\tau)\longmapsto(w,b,x)=(w,w\cdot x,x).

Its differential sends
(\delta x,\delta w,\delta\tau)
to

\displaystyle\bigl(\delta w,\,w\cdot\delta x+x\cdot\delta w,\,\delta x\bigr).

If this vanishes, then
\delta w=0
and
\delta x=0
, while
\delta\tau
is free.
Thus the kernel is exactly the radial direction in the conic fibre and the rank is

2d-1
, the maximal possible rank for a conic canonical relation. The branch

\tau<0
is identical, and transposition gives the same rank statement for

\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{R}}^{t}
. Composing with elliptic pseudodifferential operators in the

b
-variable changes the symbol and order but not the canonical relation, so

\mathrm{A}^{k}
and the high-frequency FIO part of the synthesis map inherit the same
maximal-rank condition.

The dual transform.

\mathcal{R}^{*}
has the transposed Schwartz kernel

\delta(b-w\cdot x)
, whence
\mathcal{R}^{*}\in I^{-(d-1)/2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},Y;\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{R}}^{t})

with the transposed relation
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{R}}^{t}
, the graph of
\chi^{-1}
. Since
the transpose of a local canonical graph is again one, the cut-off pieces

\chi_{X}\mathcal{R}^{*}\chi_{Y}
satisfy the same hypothesis.

Both
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{*}
are thus elliptic Fourier integral operators
of order
-(d-1)/2
whose canonical relations, after the conic branch cutoff, are
local canonical graphs, which is the assertion of Lemma
3.1
.
∎

B.6
Composition calculus and microlocal cutoffs

The microlocal proof in Section
3.3
treats

\mathrm{A}^{k}

The bounded-window map
\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}

by extending the
density by zero outside

Two standard pieces of calculus underwrite this: the behaviour of the

Composition with pseudodifferential operators.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subset(T^{*}Y\setminus\underline{0})\times(T^{*}X\setminus\underline{0})

and

same
canonical relation
\mathcal{C}
. The principal symbol of
PT
(resp.
TQ

multiplied by

(resp.

\Psi

\pi_{L},\pi_{R}
of
Section
B.4

(resp.

(resp.

[
63

and

[
22
]
. The mechanism is the one already
noted in Section

\mathcal{C}
nor its

exactly why

and

and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{R}}^{t}
verified for

in

.

If
\mathcal{C}_{1}=\mathrm{graph}(\chi_{1})

\displaystyle I^{\mu_{1}}(Z,Y;\mathcal{C}_{1})\circ I^{\mu_{2}}(Y,X;\mathcal{C}_{2})\subset I^{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}}\!\bigl(Z,X;\mathcal{C}_{1}\circ\mathcal{C}_{2}\bigr),\qquad\mathcal{C}_{1}\circ\mathcal{C}_{2}=\mathrm{graph}(\chi_{1}\circ\chi_{2})

[

,

[
22
]
. In particular,
composing a graph with the transpose of the
same
graph gives the diagonal,

\displaystyle\mathrm{graph}(\chi)\circ\mathrm{graph}(\chi)^{t}=\mathrm{graph}(\chi\circ\chi^{-1})=\Delta_{T^{*}Y},

so
I^{\mu}(Y,X;\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{R}})\circ I^{-\mu}(X,Y;\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{R}}^{t})\subset\Psi^{0}(Y)

on the cut-off branch. The second antipodal branch contributes an order-zero FIO
associated with the smooth diffeomorphism
(w,b)\mapsto(-w,-b)
of
Y
, which is
bounded on every
L^{p}(Y)
,
1<p<\infty
. This is the operator
B
of
Section
B.7
.

Conic cutoffs and microlocal splitting.

A
conic cutoff
is an operator
\Xi\in\Psi^{0}(Y)
whose symbol is homogeneous
of degree
0
at infinity and supported in a closed cone

\Gamma\subset T^{*}Y\setminus\underline{0}
; it is microlocal,

WF(\Xi u)\subset WF(u)\cap\Gamma
. Choosing
\Xi
adapted to the branch
\tau>0
of
(
B.8
) and
1-\Xi
to
\tau<0
splits a localized Radon
operator into the two single-graph pieces used throughout, the cross terms being
smoothing.

We also split a
b
-multiplier whose symbol is classical for
\left|\omega\right|\geq 1
but
singular at
\omega=0
. Fix
\chi_{0}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with
\chi_{0}\equiv 1
near
0
.
For the analysis amplitude, of order
a=k+d\geq 0
,

\displaystyle(i\omega)^{k+1}\left|\omega\right|^{d-1}=\bigl(1-\chi_{0}(\omega)\bigr)(i\omega)^{k+1}\left|\omega\right|^{d-1}+\chi_{0}(\omega)(i\omega)^{k+1}\left|\omega\right|^{d-1}.

The first term is a classical elliptic symbol in
S^{k+d}(\mathbb{R})
(on

\{\omega\neq 0\}
, hence on
\{\eta_{b}\neq 0\}\supset\pi_{L}(C_{\mathcal{R}})
); the
second is bounded with compact support, so its operator has a smooth band-limited
convolution kernel and maps
L^{p}(Y)\to W^{N,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(Y)
for every
N
,
harmless on the bias window. Thus
\partial_{b}^{k+1}\Lambda^{d-1}
is, modulo
smoothing, the classical elliptic operator of order
k+d
named in
Lemma
3.4
.

For the synthesis amplitude, of order
a=-(k+1)<0
, the singularity at
\omega=0
is
genuine:
(i\omega)^{-(k+1)}
is not locally integrable, mirroring the polynomial
growth of
\sigma_{k}=t_{+}^{k}/k!
. The Fourier multiplier
m_{\sigma_{k}}
of

\sigma_{k}\ast_{b}
is a fixed homogeneous extension of
(i\omega)^{-(k+1)}
across

\omega=0
, so it equals
(i\omega)^{-(k+1)}
off the origin and differs from any
other such extension only by a finite combination
\sum_{j=0}^{k}\gamma_{j}\delta^{(j)}(\omega)
. With
\chi_{0}
as above, write

\displaystyle m_{\sigma_{k}}=\underbrace{(1-\chi_{0})(i\omega)^{-(k+1)}}_{\text{symbol of }J_{b}^{-(k+1)}}\;+\;\underbrace{\chi_{0}\,m_{\sigma_{k}}^{\mathrm{hom}}}_{\text{symbol of }K_{\mathrm{low}}}\;+\;\sum_{j=0}^{k}\gamma_{j}\delta^{(j)},

where
m_{\sigma_{k}}^{\mathrm{hom}}
is the homogeneous part of
m_{\sigma_{k}}
. The
first term defines the classical elliptic operator
J_{b}^{-(k+1)}\in\Psi^{-(k+1)}(Y)
;
the second,
K_{\mathrm{low}}
, has a band-limited (hence smooth) convolution kernel
of at most polynomial growth
\left|b\right|^{k}
, so on the bounded window it maps

L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})
boundedly into
C^{\infty}
; the third is a polynomial of degree
\leq k
in

b
. Therefore, for each fixed
w
and
g\in L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}\ast_{b}g=J_{b}^{-(k+1)}g+K_{\mathrm{low}}\,g+\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}b^{j}\,c_{j}(g)\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}).

(B.11)

Crucially
J_{b}^{-(k+1)}
, not the cut-off-free homogeneous multiplier, is the
genuine pseudodifferential factor; (
B.11
)
includes
the
low-frequency remainder
K_{\mathrm{low}}
, without which the equality would fail.
The polynomial coefficients are moments over the finite window,

c_{j}(g)=\langle\,\cdot\,,g(w,\cdot)\rangle
against fixed test functions, so by
Hölder

\displaystyle\left|c_{j}(g)\right|\leq C\left\|g(w,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{p}([-R,R])},\qquad 0\leq j\leq k,

which closes the norm bound on the polynomial remainder. Applying
\mathcal{R}^{*}
:

\mathcal{R}^{*}J_{b}^{-(k+1)}
is an FIO of order
-s_{k}
;
\mathcal{R}^{*}K_{\mathrm{low}}
,
after localization by a cutoff
\chi
, is bounded
L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})\to C^{\infty}(\supp\chi)

(only
b=w\cdot x
with
\left|x\right|
bounded is sampled); and the polynomial part maps
to
\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, exactly the
q
of Proposition
2.1
.

Assembling Lemma
3.4
.

Combining (
B.9
) with the order
-(d-1)/2
of

\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{*}
from Lemma
3.1
, and with the orders

k+d
and
-(k+1)
of the two
b
-operators above, gives the FIO orders
+s_{k}
and

-s_{k}
. The canonical relations remain
C_{\mathcal{R}}
and
C_{\mathcal{R}}^{t}
,
and ellipticity is preserved on the conic set
\eta_{b}\neq 0
.

For the full-parameter synthesis, this says that

\mathrm{S}^{k}=\mathcal{R}^{*}(\sigma_{k}*_{b}\cdot)
is, modulo the low-frequency smoothing term and

\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the elliptic FIO
\mathcal{R}^{*}J_{b}^{-(k+1)}
of order
-s_{k}
. The
polynomial term is exactly the ambiguity appearing in
Proposition
2.1
. For the bounded-window map,

\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}g=\left.\mathcal{R}^{*}(\sigma_{k}*_{b}(g\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{M}_{R}}))\right|_{\Omega}

its localized high-frequency part has the
same FIO order and canonical relation as the corresponding part of
\mathrm{S}^{k}
.

By (
B.10
), on a selected branch

C_{\mathcal{R}}^{t}\circ C_{\mathcal{R}}=\Delta_{T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}}
, which is the microlocal
content of the reconstruction
\mathrm{S}^{k}\mathrm{A}^{k}=\mathrm{Id}\bmod\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. On
the parameter side, the localized composition
\mathrm{A}^{k}\eta\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}
is an
order-zero operator: the same branch gives a pseudodifferential operator on
Y
,
and the opposite branch gives an order-zero FIO associated with the antipodal
map
(w,b)\mapsto(-w,-b)
.

B.7
Sharpness of the sandwich

This appendix supplies the details behind
Proposition
3.2
. The proof uses the sharpness part
of Theorem
3.1
and the maximal-rank verification in
Section
B.5
.

Sharp FIO input.

Let
T\in I^{\mu}(Y,X;\mathcal{C}_{1})
be elliptic, properly supported, and let
\mathcal{C}_{1}
be a local
canonical graph. If the natural projection
\mathcal{C}_{1}\to Y\times X
has rank
2d-1
at a
point, then Theorem
3.1
is locally sharp there: for
p\neq 2
and
any
\varepsilon>0
, no estimate with output smoothness

s-\mu-\delta_{p}+\varepsilon
can hold microlocally near that point.
If the projection has lower rank, the
sharp order can change
[
54
]
.

We sketch an example showing that the gain
\rho_{p}
cannot be improved.
See also
[
55
,
63
]
. In local
coordinates adapted to a single Radon branch, test
R
on the plate-type packet

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}(x)=e^{i\lambda x_{d}}\,\phi(\lambda^{\beta}x^{\prime})\,\zeta(x_{d}),\qquad x=(x^{\prime},x_{d}),\quad\lambda\gg 1,

with
\beta=0
for
p\geq 2
and
\beta=1
for
1<p\leq 2
. By the Fourier slice
theorem
\widehat{Rf_{\lambda}}(w,\tau)=\widehat{f_{\lambda}}(\tau w)
, the image

Rf_{\lambda}
concentrates on the plate
\{|w-e_{d}|\lesssim\lambda^{\beta-1}\}
with

b
-frequency
\sim\lambda
, and a direct computation gives

\|Rf_{\lambda}\|_{W^{\rho,p}}/\|f_{\lambda}\|_{L^{p}}\sim\lambda^{\rho-\rho_{p}}
with
\rho_{p}=(d-1)\min(1/p,1-1/p)
, so the gain
\rho_{p}
is
attained and no
\rho>\rho_{p}
is admissible. This is the Radon-transform
instance of the Seeger–Sogge–Stein sharpness, realized for the half-wave
propagator by
[
45
]
.

Right side:
r_{k,p}
cannot be raised.

Choose a compact parameter patch
V\Subset\mathcal{M}_{R}
and a conic cutoff contained in one
Radon branch with
\eta_{b}\neq 0
. On this patch,

\displaystyle\chi\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}h=\chi\mathcal{R}^{*}J_{b}^{-(k+1)}h+Eh,

where
E:L^{p}(V)\to W^{N,p}(\Omega)
is bounded for every fixed
N
, after shrinking

V
if necessary and absorbing the finite-dimensional polynomial term. Suppose
that (
3.24
) held. Since

\left\|\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}h\right\|_{\mathcal{R}L^{p}_{k}(\Omega)}\leq\left\|h\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})}
, it would
imply

\displaystyle\left\|\chi\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}h\right\|_{W^{r_{k,p}+\varepsilon,p}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|h\right\|_{L^{p}(V)}.

Together with the boundedness of
E
, this would give the forbidden estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\chi\mathcal{R}^{*}J_{b}^{-(k+1)}h\right\|_{W^{r_{k,p}+\varepsilon,p}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|h\right\|_{L^{p}(V)}.

But
\mathcal{R}^{*}J_{b}^{-(k+1)}
is an elliptic FIO of order
-s_{k}
with maximal-rank
canonical relation, and Theorem
3.1
says that its
L^{p}
output
smoothness cannot exceed
s_{k}-\delta_{p}=r_{k,p}
. This contradiction proves
(
3.24
).

Left side:
s_{k,p}
cannot be lowered.

Suppose instead that (
3.25
) held. Then every

v\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U)
, with
U\Subset\Omega
, would admit a bounded-window
representation
v=\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}h
on
\Omega
with

\displaystyle\left\|h\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{M}_{R})}\leq C\left\|v\right\|_{W^{s_{k,p}-\varepsilon,p}(\Omega)}.

(B.12)

Choose
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
with
\eta\equiv 1
on
U
and a parameter cutoff

\psi
supported in one branch. Since
v=\eta\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}h
on
U
,

\displaystyle\psi\mathrm{A}^{k}v=\psi\mathrm{A}^{k}\eta\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}h.

By Lemma
3.4
and the composition calculus,

B:=\psi\mathrm{A}^{k}\eta\mathrm{S}^{k}_{R}
is an order-zero operator on
Y
, up to a
smoothing term and the antipodal order-zero branch. Hence
B
is bounded on

L^{p}(Y)
, and (
B.12
) would imply

\displaystyle\left\|\psi\mathrm{A}^{k}v\right\|_{L^{p}(Y)}\leq C\left\|v\right\|_{W^{s_{k,p}-\varepsilon,p}(\Omega)}.

This contradicts the sharpness of the elliptic FIO

\mathrm{A}^{k}\in I^{s_{k}}(Y,\mathbb{R}^{d};C_{\mathcal{R}})
: by
Theorem
3.1
, the input smoothness needed to make

\mathrm{A}^{k}v
locally
L^{p}
is exactly
s_{k}+\delta_{p}=s_{k,p}
and cannot be lowered.
This proves (
3.25
) and completes the proof of
Proposition
3.2
.
