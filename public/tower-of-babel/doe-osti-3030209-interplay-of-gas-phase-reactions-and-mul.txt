1. Executive Summary:

The formation, growth and evolution of secondary organic aerosols (SOA) are complex
multiphase chemical processes, which represent one of the most challenging and demanding
problems in research on atmospheric aerosol processes. The research goal of this project was to
advance the fundamental understanding of the interplay between gas-phase chemistry and
multiphase processes on phase state and growth dynamics of SOA for better predictivity of
aerosol effects on climate and air quality. We have achieved two research objectives: to develop
a novel aerosol process model by combining the explicit gas-phase chemistry model GECKO-A
with the kinetic multi-layer model KM-GAP; and to apply the developed model to chamber
experiments for evaluating the interplay of gas-phase reactions and multiphase processes on the
SOA lifecycle including formation, growth, and chemical transformation. We have developed
machine-learning models for prediction of glass transition temperature and vapor pressure of
organic compounds. By combining multiphase kinetic modeling and experimental measurements,
we have discovered the formation of surface crusts upon SOA evaporation, demonstrating the
critical role of particle phase state in SOA processes. With chemical transport modeling, we
showed that SOA particles adopt semisolid and glassy solid in regions with low relative humidity
and in the free troposphere with prolonged equilibration timescale and reduced mass
accommodation for SOA partitioning, suggesting that kinetic partitioning would need to be
considered for in future large-scale model studies. This project led to 12 peer-reviewed
publications and trained two postdoctoral fellows and two graduate students.

2. Research objectives:

The overall research goal is to advance the fundamental and micro-physicochemical
understanding of gas- and multi-phase processes of SOA for better predictivity of aerosol effects
on climate and air quality. The specific objectives with associated tasks are:

1: Development of a novel aerosol process model by combining the kinetic multi-layer model KM-
GAP with the gas-phase chemistry model GECKO-A, which can explicitly simulate gas-phase
reactions and multiphase processes including the effects of particle phase state on SOA
partitioning kinetics and particle size distribution dynamics.

2: Application of the developed model to chamber experiments for evaluating impacts and
interplay of gas-phase reactions and multiphase processes on SOA lifecycle (formation, growth,
and chemical transformations).

We have achieved these objectives by publishing the following 12 peer-reviewed publications.

4. Main results:

We developed a method to predict particle phase state and viscosity of SOA, which was applied
to analyze laboratory experiments and field observations. This method was also applied to

 3

chemical transport modeling, predicting spatial distributions of SOA phase state in the atmosphere.
We have applied machine learning techniques to estimate glass transition temperature
(tgBoost; Galeazzo & Shiraiwa, 2022) and vapor pressure (GC2NN; Kruger et al., 2025) of organic
compounds. We have trained multiple state-of-the-art machine learning models on databases of
experimental Tg of organic compounds and their corresponding molecular embeddings. The best
prediction model is the tgBoost model built with an Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)
regressor trained via a nested cross-validation method, reproducing experimental data very well.
We combined KM-GAP with tgBoost and an explicit gas chemistry model GECKO-A, achieving a
detailed SOA chemistry model that resolves particle phase state (Geleazzo et al., 2022). We
demonstrated that, counterintuitively, the viscosity of n-alkane SOA becomes smaller with an
increase of carbon number in n-alkane precursors with C8-13 SOA as an amorphous semisolid
state and C14-17 SOA as a liquid state.

We have developed a series of parameterizations for glass transition temperature (Tg) of organic
compounds based on molar mass, atomic O:C ratio, volatility, as well as molecular composition.
We have then developed a method to predict viscosity from the Tg-scaled Arrhenius plot of fragility
by considering Gordon-Taylor mixing rule and hygroscopic growth of SOA particles. In tight
collaborations with experimental groups, our Tg and viscosity prediction method has been applied
to high resolution mass spectrometry data of toluene SOA and biomass burning aerosols, SOA
generated by diesel fuels, β-Caryophyllene, and surrogate VOC mixtures by healthy and stressed
plants, agreeing well with viscosity measurements (Maclean et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2024).

We implemented our Tg and viscosity prediction method into regional and global chemical
transport models, GEOS-Chem (Luu et al., 2025). The simulated spatial distributions show that
SOA at the surface exist as liquid over equatorial regions and oceans, semisolid in the midlatitude
continental regions, and glassy solid over lands with low relative humidity (Fig. 1a). In the free
troposphere, SOA particles are mostly predicted to be semisolid at 850 hPa and glassy solid at
500 hPa, except over tropical regions including Amazonia, where SOA are predicted to be low
viscous. Phase state also exhibits seasonal variation with a higher frequency of semisolid and
solid particles in winter compared to warmer seasons. Figure 1b shows viscosity calculated from
measured volatility distributions at global sites compared to the viscosities predicted using GEOS-
Chem, showing a good agreement. We calculated equilibration time scales of SOA partitioning
(τeq) and effective mass accommodation coefficient (αeff), indicating that τeq is shorter than the
chemical time step of GEOS-Chem of 20 min and αeff is close to unity for most locations at the
surface level, supporting the application of equilibrium SOA partitioning. However, τeq is prolonged
and αeff is lowered over drylands and most regions in the upper troposphere, suggesting that
kinetically limited growth would need to be considered for these regions in future large-scale
model studies.

Gas-particle partitioning is critical for the evolution of SOA in the atmosphere. SOA particles
evaporate more slowly than expected at nearly size-independent rates, but the underlying
mechanism remains controversial. We applied kinetic multilayer modeling to simulate evaporation
of α-pinene SOA, demonstrating that surface crust formation, emerging from accumulation of low-
volatility compounds at the particle surface, leads to slow evaporation and reduced size
dependence of the evaporation rate (Schervish et al., 2026). While evaporation induced by

 4

decomposition of oligomers would naturally lead to size-independent evaporation rates, we
observe and simulate nearly size-independent slow evaporation of polyethylene glycol mixture
particles containing polymeric species that do not decompose, confirming the relevance of
composition-dependent diffusivity for size-independent, slow evaporation. Slow evaporation of
limonene SOA was also observed in environmental chamber experiments, and model simulations
demonstrate strong surface crust formation with bulk diffusivity being depressed by up to 5 orders
of magnitude compared to the inner bulk. We present experimental evidence using a surface-
based mass spectrometry technique that shows that the particle surface becomes enriched in
high molecular weight compounds upon evaporation of monomers. Our findings imply that viscous
surface crusts may also limit the growth and chemical transformation of SOA particles, influencing
their impacts on air quality and climate.

5. Training and professional development

This project provided a great opportunity for training and professional development of the
participating postdoctoral researchers. Two postdoctoral researchers and two graduate students
have been trained in this project as below.

Dr. Tommaso Galeazzo worked on the application of machine learning to predict glass transition
temperature and vapor pressure of organic compounds. He also applied GECKO-A model to
simulate n-alkane oxidation and SOA formation.

Dr. Meredith Schervish conducted kinetic multilayer modeling to simulate timescales of SOA

Ms. Regina Luu implemented the method to predict SOA phase state in the global chemical
transport model GEOS-Chem, demonstrating the spatial and vertical distributions of SOA phase
state in agreement with available measurements.

Ms. Kate. Nguyen implemented coagulation in the KM-GAP model, quantifying the impact of
particle phase state on the competition between condensation and coagulation upon nanoparticle
