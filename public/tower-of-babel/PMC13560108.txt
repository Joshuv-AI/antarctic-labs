Outcomes, eligibility, and waiting time for cadaveric kidney transplantation in Qatar: a national study
Authors: Ahmed Hamdi, Mohamad M Alkadi, Essa Abuhelaiqa, Muhammad Asim, Omar Fituri, Adel Ashour, Awais Nauman, Yousuf K Al-Maslamani, Riadh Fadhil, Omar Ali, Salahaldin A Latif, Baleegh Alsharam, Jonas Wadström, Hassan Al-Malki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKidney transplantation is the optimal treatment for end-stage kidney disease (ESKD); however, access to transplantation remains limited by donor availability. Data on transplant evaluation outcomes and waiting times in the Middle East are scarce. This study aimed to assess eligibility for kidney transplantation, outcomes of referred patients, waiting time to cadaveric transplantation, and factors associated with receiving a cadaveric kidney transplant in Qatar.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of adult patients with ESKD referred for kidney transplant evaluation at the national transplant center in Qatar between 2003 and 2017. Patients were assessed for transplant suitability through a multidisciplinary process. Among those listed for cadaveric transplantation, outcomes included transplantation, death, or remaining on the waiting list. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with undergoing cadaveric transplantation.ResultsA total of 1201 patients were referred, of whom 835 (69.5%) completed transplant evaluation. Among these, 797 (95.4%) were deemed suitable for transplantation, while 38 (4.6%) were rejected, primarily due to advanced age or cardiovascular disease. Of eligible patients, 361 (45.3%) underwent living donor transplantation, and 436 were listed for cadaveric transplantation. During the study period, 84 patients (19.3%) received a cadaveric transplant after a mean waiting time of 1.79 ± 1.71 years, 59 (13.5%) died, and 293 (67.2%) remained on the waiting list. In multivariable analysis, peritoneal dialysis was associated with a higher likelihood of transplantation (p = 0.003).ConclusionsMost patients referred for kidney transplantation in Qatar were deemed suitable; however, access to cadaveric transplantation remained limited. While waiting time among transplanted patients appeared relatively shorter compared to some international settings, a substantial proportion of patients remained on the waiting list or died before transplantation. The association between peritoneal dialysis and transplantation likely reflects underlying differences in patient characteristics rather than a causal effect.Keywords: Kidney transplantation, End-stage kidney disease, Waiting list, Cadaveric donor

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a global concern, affecting 9.1% of the population [1]. One potential outcome of CKD is end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), which requires renal replacement therapy (RRT) in the form of dialysis or kidney transplantation. Over the past two decades, the annual growth of dialysis patients have averaged between 6 and 12% and is continuing to increase, particularly in developing countries posing a significant financial burden on healthcare systems [2].

Currently, over 3.2 million people worldwide require RRT to survive, but this figure likely represents less than 10% of those who actually need RRT, resulting in the deaths of more than one million people annually from untreated kidney failure [3].

Kidney transplantation offers the best chance to restore a healthy, productive life, reduce long-term dialysis-related morbidities, and improve survival rates; however, there has always been shortage in donated kidneys. Despite the steady increase in the number of cadaveric transplantations performed each year, the number of patients receiving dialysis is rising more. This trend has led to larger waiting lists and longer waiting times for kidney transplantation. For Instance, there are currently more than 90,000 patients on the kidney transplant waiting list in the United States of America [4]. The waiting time for a deceased donor can be as long as five years, and in some states, it approaches ten years [4]. Longer waiting time increases the risk of patients being inactive on the list or dying while waiting-one report indicated a mortality rate of five deaths for every 100 candidates awaiting a kidney [5]. Additionally, not all dialysis patients are added to the waiting list. One study reported that only 10% of dialysis patients were eligible for listing, with reasons of ineligibility including age, primary kidney disease, comorbidities, and patient weight [6]. Published date on ESKD on transplant waiting lists in Middle Eastern countries remains limited and largely restricted to single-center reports and national transplant activities summaries, with a lack of comprehensive registry-based studies evaluating waitlist outcomes, mortality, and access to transplantation across the region.

Hamad General Hospital (HGH) is the sole transplant center in Qatar, with the first kidney transplantation performed in 1987. However, little is known about the status of patients referred for kidney transplant evaluation in our program, the average waiting time from listing to cadaveric kidney transplantation, and the mortality rates of patients while on the transplant waiting list. We aim in this study to assess the outcomes of patients referred for kidney transplant evaluation in Qatar, determine the causes and factors associated with patients’ rejection from the waiting list, determine the average waiting time from listing to cadaveric kidney transplantation, and identify factors associated with undergoing cadaveric kidney transplantation.

Methods

Study cohort

This retrospective study included all adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) referred to HGH from ambulatory dialysis units and nephrology clinics across Qatar for kidney transplant evaluation between January 1, 2003, and December 31st, 2017.

Suitability for kidney transplantation was determined during transplant multidisciplinary team (MDT) meetings following the completion of transplant workup. The MDT consisted of nephrologists, transplant surgeons, donor and recipient coordinators, and the histocompatibility laboratory director. Patients who were deemed fit and did not have a living donor were added to the national cadaveric kidney transplant waiting list. Allocation of deceased donor kidneys in Qatar is based on blood group compatibility, waiting time, immunological matching, time on dialysis, and clinical suitability, as determined by the transplant committee. This research was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of Hamad Medical Corporation, and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards.

Data collection

We collected data regarding patients’ age, gender, primary kidney disease, comorbidities, blood group, BMI, previous transplant, other organ transplant, dialysis modality, cardiovascular risk, smoking status, transplantation or death. Cardiovascular risk was defined based on a documented history of cardiovascular disease, including ischemic heart disease, heart failure, or cerebrovascular disease, in addition to pre-transplant cardiac evaluation including stress testing and cardiology risk assessment. All data were collected from the national electronic medical records (Cerner). The study was approved by the Hamad Medical Corporation Institutional Review Board. Human Ethics and Consent to Participate declarations were not applicable. At the patient level, informed consent was not required, as the data were de-identified national registry data.

Statistical analysis

The data were tabulated using a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations (SD), while categorical data were reported as frequencies and percentages. Differences in continuous data were analyzed using unpaired t tests or Mann‒Whitney U tests while differences in categorical data were analyzed using Chi Square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All presented p values were two-tailed, and p values < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. Time to transplant was calculated using the mean time spent on the waiting list. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to compare waiting time till cadaveric transplant between groups. The log-rank test was used to assess statistical significance between the groups. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with undergoing cadaveric kidney transplantation. All the statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

Between January 1, 2003, and December 31, 2017, 1201 patients were referred to our transplant center for kidney transplant evaluation. The mean age at the time of referral was 47.5 ± 14, with males constituting approximately two-thirds of the patients. Diabetes was the leading cause of ESKD (43.7%) followed by hypertension (n = 22.1%). Most referred patients had Blood group O (45.9%), while only 4.7% of patients had blood group AB. Sixty-eight patients (5.7%) had previous kidney transplant. The baseline characteristics of all referred patients are summarized in Table 1.

Outcomes of patients referred for kidney transplant evaluation

Of the 1201 referred patients for kidney transplant evaluation, 835 (69.5%) patients completed their pre-transplant workup and were discussed during transplant MDT meetings, while 366 patients (30.5%) were still under workup at the time of data collection; these patients may represent delayed evaluation, loss to follow-up, or other barriers. Out of the 835 patients, 38 patients (3.2%) were deemed unfit for kidney transplant, primarily due to severe cardiovascular disease or age limit (more than 75 year old), while 797 patients (66.4%) were deemed fit for kidney transplant. Forty-five percent of patients deemed fit for kidney transplant (n = 361) had a living donor, while the remaining 55% (n = 436) did not have a living donor and were added to the national cadaveric transplant waiting list. During the study period, 84 out of 436 patients (19%) on the waiting list underwent cadaveric kidney transplantation, while 59 patients died (13.5%) (Fig. 1). Among pre-transplant deaths, the leading cause was cardiovascular disease (n = 62.9%), followed by infection (18.6%), malignancy (n = 3%), and other causes, including vascular, pulmonary, and gastrointestinal conditions (combined n = 15.6%).

Causes and factors associated with patients’ rejection from the waiting list

The primary causes for patients being rejected from the waiting list were severe cardiovascular diseases (n = 18) and advanced age (n = 14). Other causes included malignancy (n = 1), severe vascular calcifications (n = 1), Down syndrome (n = 1), paralysis (n = 1), neurologic disease (n = 1), and poor compliance (n = 1).

Compared to patients deemed suitable for kidney transplantation, rejected patients were significantly older (59.4±16.4 vs. 44.6 ± 13.6 years; p < 0.0001), had a higher prevalence of diabetic primary kidney disease (81.6% vs. 36.9%; p < 0.0001), and had higher rates of diabetes (81.6% vs. 43%; p < 0.0001), cardiovascular disease (60.5% vs. 14.3%; p < 0.0001) and peripheral vascular disease (57.9% vs. 8.3%; p < 0.0001). They were also more likely to be on hemodialysis (HD) (94.8% vs. 55.2%; p < 0.0001) (Table 2).

Time from listing to cadaveric kidney transplant

The average waiting time from listing to cadaveric kidney transplantation was 1.79 ± 1.71 years. Patients with blood group O had longer waiting times on the waiting list than did patients with blood group AB (2.1 versus 0.4 years; p = 0.006) (Fig. 2). Waiting time was longer among those with a BMI of 30 kg/m2 or above than those with a BMI lower than 30 kg/m2 (2.3 vs. 1.6 years); however, the difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 3).

Factors associated with undergoing a cadaveric kidney transplantation

Patients who had cadaveric kidney transplantation were younger (43±10.9 vs. 46.6 ± 13.1 years; p = 0.011), were more likely to be on peritoneal dialysis (PD) (58.3% vs. 36.1%; p = 0.001), had normal cardiac stress test results (100% vs. 92%; p = 0.005), were less likely to have cardiovascular disease (7.1% vs. 17%; p = 0.023), peripheral vascular disease (3.6% vs. 13.6%p = 0.010), had less estimated perioperative cardiac risk (3.6% vs. 18.2%; p = 0.0003) and were more likely to have negative or <10% panel reactive antibody (PRA) class 1 (88.1% vs. 75.6%; p = 0.013) and PRA class 2 (90.5% vs. 80%; p = 0.023) compared to patients who stayed on the waiting list (Table 3). Multivariate analysis for factors associated with cadaveric transplant versus those remaining on the waiting list revealed that being on PD as the only independent factor associated with undergoing cadaveric kidney transplantation (p = 0.003) as shown in Table 4. Further characterization of patients on different modes of RRT, revealed that those maintained on peritoneal dialysis were significantly younger and had less incidence of diabetes, hypertension and peripheral vascular disease (Table 5).

Discussion

Deceased donor kidney transplantation (DDKT) is common in high-income western countries. However, the utilization of deceased organs in Asia is suboptimal due to multiple factors, including cultural and religious influences as well as poor public knowledge and attitudes. In Qatar, deceased organ transplantation was legalized in 1997 through a decree on the regulation of human organ transfer and transplantation [7].

This national cohort study provides important insights into kidney transplant evaluation and waitlist outcomes in Qatar. The majority of patients who completed transplant workup were deemed suitable for transplantation, reflecting effective referral and selection processes. However, despite high eligibility rates, access to cadaveric transplantation remained limited, with only one-fifth of listed patients undergoing transplantation during the study period.

The study period for this research spanned from January 2003 to December 2017, and we obtained data on a substantial volume of referrals for kidney transplant evaluation, averaging 1201 patients. Of these, 797 were accepted by the MDT for inclusion on the waiting list, while 38 were rejected, primarily due to age limits or advanced cardiovascular disorders. These rejection criteria underscore the careful consideration and assessment undertaken by the MDT to ensure optimal patient selection for transplantation.

Demographic analysis revealed pertinent characteristics of the referred patient population, with a mean age of approximately 47 years and a greater proportion of males than females. Diabetes and hypertension have emerged as the leading causes of ESKD, collectively prevalent in approximately two-thirds of cases. Blood group O was the most common blood group, with a notably greater prevalence of diabetes among rejected patients than among those admitted to the waiting list. These findings are consistent with international data, where comorbidity burden remains a major determinant of transplant eligibility. The high prevalence of diabetes and cardiovascular disease in rejected patients further underscores the importance of early referral and optimization of comorbid conditions.

In United States, although much effort has been made to overcome organ shortages, there are still problems to be solved in management for transplant wait-listed patients [8]. According to a report from Irish investigators [9], older age, immunological factors such as high PRA, and blood type O status were suggested as risk factors for long waiting times.

A key finding of this study is that a substantial proportion of patients (67.2%) remained on the waiting list, and 13.5% died before receiving a transplant. These findings highlight the ongoing impact of donor organ shortage, even in a system with relatively short waiting times among transplanted patients. Importantly, the reported average waiting time of 1.79 years applies only to those who eventually received a transplant and does not reflect the full experience of all listed patients. This distinction is critical to avoid overestimation of access to transplantation.

Further analysis revealed significant differences between patients who received cadaveric transplants and those who remained on the waiting list, with cadaveric transplant recipients being younger, more likely to be on peritoneal dialysis (PD), and exhibiting fewer cardiovascular comorbidities. Multivariate analysis identified the mode of dialysis as the sole independent variable associated with cadaveric transplantation status, underscoring its impact on transplant outcomes. Notably, patients on peritoneal dialysis were found to be younger and experienced lower incidences of diabetes, hypertension, and peripheral vascular disease compared to those on other modalities of renal replacement therapy (RRT). This observation emphasizes the potential benefits of peritoneal dialysis in preserving patient health while awaiting transplantation [10]. And also reflecting our local practice of preferring peritoneal dialysis as the starting mode of RRT in our ESKD patients unless proved otherwise. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously as that dialysis modality likely reflects underlying patient selection rather than a direct causal effect. Residual confounding cannot be excluded despite multivariable adjustment. Therefore, while peritoneal dialysis may be associated with favorable outcomes, it should not be interpreted as an independent determinant of transplant access.

No difference in time on the waiting list was found between males and females, which is encouraging given that previous studies have shown longer waiting times for females [11].

Analysis of waiting times revealed potential predictors, with higher body mass index (BMI) and blood group O status associated with longer wait times. Obesity has been associated with longer waiting times for transplantation; reportedly, the likelihood of receiving a kidney transplant, for those already in the transplant pool, decreases with increasing degrees of obesity, as measured by BMI [12].

Additionally, patients with higher panel reactive antibodies (PRA) class 2 levels experienced prolonged wait times, indicating the influence of immunological factors on transplant candidacy and allocation. A recent local desensitization protocol has been implemented to help solve the high PRA barrier.

The results of our study confirms those of previous work showing longer waiting times for blood group O patients [13]. According to a study from German investigators, the export of blood group O donor kidneys to other blood groups leads to longer waiting times, a higher death rate and the accumulation of blood group O patients on the waiting list, which will further aggravate the problem in the future [14]. Per our local practice, every effort is made to avoid the export of blood group O donors to non-O blood group recipients.

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. The study was conducted in a single geographic location with relatively small sample size which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, due to time constraints, the study reflects data up to 2017; therefore, findings may not fully represent current practices following recent developments in transplantation. The study also identified a considerable proportion of patients (30.5%) who did not complete transplant evaluation at the time of data collection, this represents a potential source of selection bias and may reflect delays in workup, loss to follow-up, or evolving clinical status. Further evaluation of barriers to completion of transplant assessment is warranted.

In conclusion, despite these limitations, this study provides valuable real-world data from a region where such information is limited. The findings emphasize the need to expand donor availability, optimize waitlist management, and address barriers to transplantation. Future studies comparing our findings with the most recent data after the Doha donation accord will provide a better understanding of the current status of waiting list times in Qatar.
