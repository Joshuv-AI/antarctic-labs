Detection–Control Games under Hidden Modes: Resilience-Induced Blindness Phenomenon
Anh Tung Nguyen, Quanyan Zhu

Abstract
This paper studies resilient control for cyber-physical systems operating under hidden degraded or compromised modes. We formulate hidden-mode detection and belief-dependent control as a game between two decision makers with different objectives: the detector seeks informative belief updates, while the controller seeks regulation performance. This objective mismatch shows why the usual separation intuition between detector design and controller design may fail, leading to a performance-reversal phenomenon induced by the resilience of the controller. For a two-mode linear Gaussian system, we theoretically characterize this phenomenon by linking the resilience margin to the log-likelihood evidence. The analysis shows that a well-performing controller with a large resilience margin can suppress mode-dependent information and slow belief adaptation, which in turn degrades the control performance. The resilience-induced blindness phenomenon and its mitigation are illustrated in numerical simulations.



I
Introduction

Resilient control architectures for cyber-physical systems are often designed according to a two-stage paradigm. A detector is first designed to identify anomalies, attacks, faults, or hidden operational modes from system
measurements
[
9
,
13
,
4
,
8
,
2
]
.
To achieve a desired performance objective, the controller is then designed
based on the detector outcome through switching, reconfiguration, or adaptive feedback
[
11
,
12
,
3
]
. Examples include fault-tolerant control
[
5
]
and attack-resilient state estimation
[
6
,
7
]
.

In most existing approaches, the detector and the controller are designed
independently under an implicit
separation principle
. The detector is optimized
to achieve accurate inference or anomaly detection, while the controller is
optimized to achieve stabilization or regulation performance based on the
detector output. However, such a separation neglects the intrinsic closed-loop coupling between detection and control.
Indeed, the detector operates on observations generated by the controlled
system, implying that the controller directly shapes the statistical structure
of the measurements used for detection. Conversely, the controller reacts to
the detector output through the estimated mode or posterior belief. Thus, the
detector and controller should not be viewed as isolated modules, but rather as
two dynamically coupled decision-making processes.

Based on this argument, from a game-theoretic viewpoint
[
1
]
, the detector and the controller naturally form a dynamic noncooperative game. The detector seeks to minimize a detection cost using measurements generated under the controlled dynamics, while the controller seeks to minimize a control cost using the detector outcome. When
these two components are designed independently, the resulting architecture is generally not globally optimal for the overall resilient system. Rather, the
outcome corresponds naturally to a Nash equilibrium in which the detector is optimal given the controller policy and the controller is optimal given the
detector policy.
This viewpoint is important since it reveals intrinsic closed-loop phenomena
that are not captured when detection and control are designed independently.

The coupling creates an information–regulation tradeoff. A controller, designed with a large resilience margin to strongly suppress state deviations, may reduce the mode-dependent variability present in the measurements, making the induced observation laws statistically similar across hidden modes. This reduces the rate at which information about the true mode is generated and can slow the convergence of the detector belief process. Conversely, controllers that generate stronger mode-dependent excitation may accelerate mode discrimination, but potentially at the expense of larger regulation cost or control effort.

Based on the above observation,
this paper studies this phenomenon as a detection–control game with three contributions. We first formulate detection and control as coupled decision problems and use the Nash-equilibrium viewpoint to clarify why separated designs can be individually consistent but jointly inefficient. Second, to study the phenomenon in more detail, we then specialize to a two-mode linear Gaussian system. This case enables us to derive a quantitative bound linking a belief-dependent resilience margin to the log-likelihood evidence. This bound formalizes the resilience-induced blindness phenomenon, where resilient regulation can suppress the information needed for detection, causing persistent belief mismatch and degraded switched-mode performance. Third, we provide simulations showing the effect of the phenomenon and motivate a mitigation of the phenomenon by tuning the detector’s objective. The simulation result strongly suggests
a co-design objective for the detector and the controller.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section II formulates
hidden-mode detection and belief-dependent control as a game between two
decision makers with different objectives. Section III specializes the
framework to a two-mode linear Gaussian system and derives the
resilience-to-detection bound that characterizes the resilience-induced blindness phenomenon. Section IV presents numerical evidence showing the reversal between fixed-mode and switching-mode performance, as well as a mitigation by
increasing detector sensitivity, while Section V concludes the paper.

II
Detection–Control Game

Consider the hidden-mode system

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=f_{\theta_{k}}(x_{k},u_{k},w_{k}),

\displaystyle y_{k}

\displaystyle=h_{\theta_{k}}(x_{k},v_{k}),

(1)

where
\theta_{k}\in\Theta
is an unobserved operational mode,
x_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is the state,
u_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is the control input, and
y_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
is the measured output. Here,
w_{k}
and
v_{k}
are process disturbance and measurement noise with appropriate dimensions.
The system dynamics and measurement functions
f_{\theta_{k}}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
and
h_{\theta_{k}}(\cdot,\cdot)
are given for each mode
\theta_{k}
.
The mode
\theta_{k}
represents nominal operation, compromised operation, faults, or attack configurations.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{k}^{D}\triangleq\sigma(y_{0:k},u_{0:k-1})
denote the detector information and
\mathcal{I}_{k}^{C}\triangleq\sigma(y_{0:k},u_{0:k-1},\pi_{k})
denote the controller information. The detector constructs a posterior mode belief
\pi_{k}\in\Delta(\Theta)
, where
\Delta(\Theta)
is the probability simplex over
\Theta
, while the controller chooses
u_{k}
using the measurement history and the current belief provided by the detector. A detector policy is a sequence
\delta\triangleq\{\delta_{k}\}
with
\pi_{k}\triangleq\delta_{k}(\mathcal{I}_{k}^{D})
, and a controller policy is a sequence
\mu\triangleq\{\mu_{k}\}
with
u_{k}\triangleq\mu_{k}(\mathcal{I}_{k}^{C})
.

For a fixed controller, the detector minimizes

J_{D}(\delta;\mu)=\mathbb{E}^{\delta,\mu}\textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\ell_{D}(\pi_{k},\pi_{k-1}),

(2)

where a representative stage cost is defined as:

\displaystyle\ell_{D}

\displaystyle\triangleq D_{\rm KL}(\pi_{k}\|\pi_{k-1})-\beta\textstyle\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\pi_{k}^{(\theta)}\log L_{k}(\theta),

(3)

and the likelihood
L_{k}(\theta)={\mathbb{P}}(y_{k}\mid\theta_{k}=\theta,\mathcal{I}_{k-1}^{D})
. Here,
D_{\rm KL}(\pi_{k}\|\pi_{k-1})
stands for the Kullback-Leibler divergence of
\pi_{k}
from
\pi_{k-1}
. In (
3
), the first term penalizes abrupt belief changes, while the second term penalizes assigning probability to modes that poorly explain the current measurement. For fixed prior beliefs and likelihoods, the stagewise detector problem becomes

\min_{\pi_{k}\in\Delta(\Theta)}\left\{D_{\rm KL}(\pi_{k}\|\pi_{k-1})-\beta\textstyle\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\pi_{k}^{(\theta)}\log L_{k}(\theta)\right\},

(4)

which is convex on the simplex. Using the standard Lagrangian method, (
4
) gives us the following optimal solution:

\pi_{k}^{(\theta)}=\frac{\pi_{k-1}^{(\theta)}L_{k}(\theta)^{\beta}}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}\pi_{k-1}^{(\theta^{\prime})}L_{k}(\theta^{\prime})^{\beta}},\qquad\theta\in\Theta.

(5)

Here,
\beta>0
controls how strongly the detector reacts to the evidence given by the recent measurement. Interestingly,
\beta=1
recovers the standard Bayesian update, while larger
\beta
approaches a maximum-likelihood selector
[
10
, Ch. 2]
. This general form (
5
) provides us with a more flexible belief update
since alternating
\beta
changes the detector objective itself, not only the numerical value of the posterior.

For a fixed detector, the controller minimizes the cost:

J_{C}(\mu;\delta)=\mathbb{E}^{\delta,\mu}\textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\ell_{C}(x_{k},u_{k}),

(6)

with a typical quadratic cost
\ell_{C}(x_{k},u_{k})\triangleq x_{k}^{\top}Qx_{k}+u_{k}^{\top}Ru_{k}~(Q\succ 0,R\succeq 0)
. In general, this is a stochastic output-feedback problem since the controller only observes the state through the measurement history and the detector belief. Hence, the relevant controller information can be summarized by a state estimate, denoted as
\hat{x}
, and a belief
\pi_{k}
i.e.,
\xi_{k}=(\hat{x}_{k},\pi_{k})
, which motivates the belief-dependent formulation in the next section.

A pair
(\delta^{\star},\mu^{\star})
is a Nash equilibrium if neither player can improve its own objective by unilateral deviation:

\displaystyle J_{D}(\delta^{\star};\mu^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq J_{D}(\delta;\mu^{\star}),\quad\forall\delta,

\displaystyle J_{C}(\mu^{\star};\delta^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq J_{C}(\mu;\delta^{\star}),\quad\forall\mu.

(7)

The Nash equilibrium is individually consistent but need not be information-efficient: the controller may reduce the statistical separation needed by the detector, while the detector belief determines the controller gain. Thus, the equilibrium can represent a locally rational interaction whose objectives are not aligned with the final resilient closed-loop objective.

To see the induced coupling, suppose the controller applies a belief-dependent feedback law

\displaystyle u_{k}=-K(\pi_{k})\hat{x}_{k}.

(8)

The resulting closed-loop trajectory determines the next measurements and likelihoods, which then determine the next belief. Therefore, the detector and controller are coupled through the following recursion

\displaystyle\pi_{k}\longrightarrow K(\pi_{k})\longrightarrow x_{k+1},y_{k+1}\longrightarrow\pi_{k+1}.

(9)

Within such a restricted architecture (
9
), the tempered Bayesian detector (
5
) and the belief-dependent controller (
8
) are best responses to each other in their respective objectives. However, the resulting operating point can still be inefficient for the overall resilient task, where the controller can be optimal for regulation under its current belief while simultaneously suppressing the information needed for the detector to correct that belief. To demonstrate this inefficient phenomenon, we focus on a standard linear Gaussian case in the next section.

III
Linear Hidden-Mode Systems and Resilience-Induced Blindness Phenomenon

In this section, we specialize the detection-control game into a standard linear Gaussian system with two hidden modes. For the standard linear-quadratic regulation (LQR) problem, we analyze its resilience margin to show the resilience-induced blindness phenomenon.

III-A

Two-Mode Linear Gaussian Model

We specialize to
\Theta=\{0,1\}
, where
\theta=0
is nominal and
\theta=1
is compromised or degraded. For the analysis, we first consider the local behavior under a fixed but unknown mode
\theta
. In the simulations, the mode is allowed to switch,
and the same local analysis is applied after each switching time. The linear Gaussian version of (
1
) is given as follows:

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=A_{\theta}x_{k}+B_{\theta}u_{k}+w_{k},

(10)

\displaystyle y_{k}

\displaystyle=C_{\theta}x_{k}+v_{k},

(11)

where
w_{k}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,\Sigma_{w})
and
v_{k}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,\Sigma_{v})
.
The compromised mode may represent reduced actuation, distorted sensing, or degraded dynamics. Thus, compromised behavior is captured through the mode-dependent matrices
(A_{\theta},B_{\theta},C_{\theta})
.

For each mode
\theta
, we assume that the system
(A_{\theta},C_{\theta})
is observable. Hence, a mode-conditioned state observer is given as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{k|k-1}^{(\theta)}

\displaystyle=A_{\theta}\hat{x}_{k-1}^{(\theta)}+B_{\theta}u_{k-1},~\hat{y}_{k|k-1}^{(\theta)}=C_{\theta}\hat{x}_{k|k-1}^{(\theta)},

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{k}^{(\theta)}

\displaystyle=\hat{x}_{k|k-1}^{(\theta)}+L_{\theta}(y_{k}-\hat{y}_{k|k-1}^{(\theta)}),

(12)

where
A_{\theta}-L_{\theta}C_{\theta}
is Schur stable. The innovation is defined as follows:

\displaystyle r_{k}^{(\theta)}\triangleq y_{k}-\hat{y}_{k|k-1}^{(\theta)}.

(13)

Under hypothesis
i\in\{0,1\}
, the Gaussian property gives us

y_{k}\mid i\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{y}_{k|k-1}^{(i)},\Sigma_{i}),

(14)

which leads to the likelihood

L_{k}(i)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{p}\det(\Sigma_{i})}}\exp\!\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}r_{k}^{(i)\top}\Sigma_{i}^{-1}r_{k}^{(i)}\bigg).

(15)

With abuse of notation, let
\pi_{k}
denote the belief of the nominal mode (
\theta=0
) afterward. Unless otherwise stated, the detector uses the tempered Bayesian belief update (
5
) with
\beta=1
, as the standard Bayesian update in
[
10
, Ch. 2]
:

\pi_{k}=\frac{\pi_{k-1}L_{k}(0)}{\pi_{k-1}L_{k}(0)+(1-\pi_{k-1})L_{k}(1)}.

(16)

The detector-sensitivity parameter
\beta
is revisited in Section IV as a phenomenon mitigation policy, while this section sticks to
\beta=1
to study the interaction between the detector and the controller.
For this binary-mode case, the following proposition presents how the belief update (
5
) estimates the true mode of the system.

Proposition 1

Consider the binary-mode case

\Theta=\{0,1\}
.
Suppose that

\pi_{0}\in(0,1),

L_{k}(0)>0
, and
L_{k}(1)>0

for all
k\geq 1
. Let the detector update be given by
(
5
) with
\beta>0
. Define the accumulated
log-likelihood ratio

\displaystyle\Lambda_{k}\triangleq\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\log\frac{L_{t}(0)}{L_{t}(1)}.

(17)

If
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\Lambda_{k}=+\infty
,
then one has

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\pi_{k}=1.

If

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\Lambda_{k}=-\infty
,
then one has

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\pi_{k}=0.

Proof:

Since
L_{k}(0),L_{k}(1)>0
and the initial belief has full support,

\pi_{k}
remains strictly positive for all
k
.
Taking the ratio of the two updates yields

\frac{\pi_{k}}{1-\pi_{k}}=\frac{\pi_{k-1}}{1-\pi_{k-1}}\left(\frac{L_{k}(0)}{L_{k}(1)}\right)^{\beta}.

Define the log-belief ratio

z_{k}\triangleq\log\frac{\pi_{k}}{1-\pi_{k}},

which gives us

z_{k}=z_{k-1}+\beta\log\frac{L_{k}(0)}{L_{k}(1)}.

Iterating from
0
to
k
gives us:

z_{k}=z_{0}+\beta\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{k}\log\frac{L_{t}(0)}{L_{t}(1)}=z_{0}+\beta\Lambda_{k}.

Note that
\pi_{0}\in(0,1)
leads to
0<z_{0}<+\infty
. If
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\Lambda_{k}=+\infty
, then
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}z_{k}=+\infty
for

\beta>0
, resulting in

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\pi_{k}}{1-\pi_{k}}=+\infty.

This implies

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\pi_{k}=1
.
We obtain the analogous result if
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\Lambda_{k}=-\infty
.
∎

Proposition
1
shows that the belief update (
16
) can identify the true hidden mode when sufficient likelihood evidence (
17
) accumulates. In particular, if the
accumulated log-likelihood ratio diverges in favor of one mode, then the posterior belief concentrates on that mode. In the next part, we present how the Bayesian belief update (
16
) can be used to design a control law.

III-B

Belief-dependent LQR

With the aim of showing the resilience-induced blindness phenomenon in even standard cases, we adopt the standard LQR setting for designing the controller.
For a fixed
\pi_{k}
at time
k
, the objective function (
6
) of the controller is converted into the following subjective LQR problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\{u_{t}\}_{t\geq k}}\sum_{t=k}^{\infty}\bar{x}_{t}^{\top}Q\bar{x}_{t}+u_{t}^{\top}Ru_{t},

(18)

where

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\bar{A}_{k}\bar{x}_{t}+\bar{B}_{k}u_{t},

\displaystyle\bar{A}_{k}

\displaystyle=\pi_{k}A_{0}+(1-\pi_{k})A_{1},

\displaystyle\bar{B}_{k}

\displaystyle=\pi_{k}B_{0}+(1-\pi_{k})B_{1}.

Here, we assume that the system
(\bar{A}_{k},\bar{B}_{k})
is controllable for any
\pi_{k}\in(0,1)
.
Furthermore, the controller also considers the belief-weighted state estimate as:

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=\pi_{k}\hat{x}_{k}^{(0)}+(1-\pi_{k})\hat{x}_{k}^{(1)}.

(19)

Solving (
18
) is standard using the associated Riccati equation with
P_{k}\succ 0
:

\displaystyle P_{k}=

\displaystyle~Q+\bar{A}_{k}^{\top}P_{k}\bar{A}_{k}

\displaystyle-\bar{A}_{k}^{\top}P_{k}\bar{B}_{k}(R+\bar{B}_{k}^{\top}P_{k}\bar{B}_{k})^{-1}\bar{B}_{k}^{\top}P_{k}\bar{A}_{k}.

(20)

The belief-dependent gain and control input are

\displaystyle K(\pi_{k})

\displaystyle=(R+\bar{B}_{k}^{\top}P_{k}\bar{B}_{k})^{-1}\bar{B}_{k}^{\top}P_{k}\bar{A}_{k},

\displaystyle u_{k}

\displaystyle=-K(\pi_{k})\bar{x}_{k}.

(21)

Leveraging the separation principle between the state estimation and the control problems for the considered standard linear Gaussian system, we can use a state-estimate feedback controller for (
21
) as:

\displaystyle u_{k}

\displaystyle=-K(\pi_{k})\hat{x}_{k}.

(22)

In the following, we analyze how the belief-dependent LQR controller interacts with the two hidden modes.

III-C

Belief-dependent Resilience

Within the scope of this section, we consider the mode
\theta=1
as compromised dynamics. Therefore, a controller, which can stabilize the system under the two modes, is referred to as resilient to the compromised mode.
The following lemma quantifies the resilience margin of the belief-dependent controller (
21
) for both modes, since the system operates at only one mode at a time.

Lemma 1
(Belief-dependent LQR resilience)

Consider the system (
10
)-(
11
) under the control law (
21
) in the noise-free case. Let us employ several auxiliary notations:

\displaystyle\bar{A}_{\rm cl}

\displaystyle\triangleq\bar{A}_{k}-\bar{B}_{k}K(\pi_{k}),

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\triangleq Q+K(\pi_{k})^{\top}RK(\pi_{k}),

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\triangleq\lambda_{\min}(S),

\displaystyle\eta_{\theta}

\displaystyle\triangleq\begin{cases}1-\pi_{k},&\theta=0,\\
\pi_{k},&\theta=1,\end{cases}

(23)

\epsilon_{\rm av}^{\theta}\triangleq\eta_{\theta}\left\|A_{1}-A_{0}\right\|+\eta_{\theta}\left\|K(\pi_{k})\right\|\left\|B_{1}-B_{0}\right\|.

(24)

If the following inequality holds true

\alpha>2\left\|P_{k}\right\|\left\|\bar{A}_{\rm cl}\right\|\epsilon_{\rm av}^{\theta}+\left\|P_{k}\right\|(\epsilon_{\rm av}^{\theta})^{2},

(25)

then there exists
m_{\theta}>0
such that

A_{\rm cl}^{\theta\top}P_{k}A_{\rm cl}^{\theta}-P_{k}\preceq-m_{\theta}I,

(26)

where
A_{\rm cl}^{\theta}\triangleq A_{\theta}-B_{\theta}K(\pi_{k})
and

m_{\theta}\triangleq\alpha-2\left\|P_{k}\right\|\left\|\bar{A}_{\rm cl}\right\|\epsilon_{\rm av}^{\theta}-\left\|P_{k}\right\|(\epsilon_{\rm av}^{\theta})^{2}.

(27)

Consequently,
A_{\rm cl}^{\theta}
is Schur stable.

Proof:

The Riccati equation (
20
) gives
\bar{A}_{\rm cl}^{\top}P_{k}\bar{A}_{\rm cl}-P_{k}=-S\preceq-\alpha I
. For the true mode, write
A_{\rm cl}^{\theta}=\bar{A}_{\rm cl}+E_{\theta}
, where
E_{\theta}=(A_{\theta}-\bar{A}_{k})-(B_{\theta}-\bar{B}_{k})K(\pi_{k})
and
\left\|E_{\theta}\right\|\leq\epsilon_{\rm av}^{\theta}
. Given
P_{k}
and a Lyapunov function
V(x_{k})=x_{k}^{\top}P_{k}x_{k}
, we expand its difference as follows:

\displaystyle A_{\rm cl}^{\theta\top}P_{k}A_{\rm cl}^{\theta}-P_{k}=(\bar{A}_{\rm cl}+E_{\theta})^{\top}P_{k}(\bar{A}_{\rm cl}+E_{\theta})-P_{k}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\bar{A}_{\rm cl}^{\top}P_{k}\bar{A}_{\rm cl}-P_{k}+\bar{A}_{\rm cl}^{\top}P_{k}E_{\theta}+E_{\theta}^{\top}P_{k}\bar{A}_{\rm cl}+E_{\theta}^{\top}P_{k}E_{\theta}.

The first two terms are bounded by
-\alpha I
. For any vector
x
, the last three terms give us:

x^{\top}\left(\bar{A}_{\rm cl}^{\top}P_{k}E_{\theta}+E_{\theta}^{\top}P_{k}\bar{A}_{\rm cl}\right)x\leq 2\|P_{k}\|\|\bar{A}_{\rm cl}\|\epsilon_{\rm av}^{\theta}\|x\|^{2},

and

x^{\top}E_{\theta}^{\top}P_{k}E_{\theta}x\leq\|P_{k}\|\left(\epsilon_{\rm av}^{\theta}\right)^{2}\|x\|^{2}.

Consequently, by the definition (
27
), one obtains (
26
), resulting in Schur stable
A_{\rm cl}^{\theta}
.
∎

III-D

Resilience-to-Detection Bound

In this part, we leverage the resilience margin
m_{\theta}
, assumed to be positive, in Lemma
1
to analyze the speed of the detector. Let us define the log-odds and log-likelihood ratio

z_{k}\triangleq\log\frac{\pi_{k}}{1-\pi_{k}},\qquad\ell_{k}\triangleq\log\frac{L_{k}(0)}{L_{k}(1)}.

(28)

From (
16
), one has
z_{k}=z_{k-1}+\ell_{k}
. Hence,
|\ell_{k}|
is the instantaneous speed of the belief update. We aim to quantify the upper bound for
|\ell_{k}|
using
m_{\theta}
in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
(Resilience-to-detection bound)

Consider the local noise-free trajectories with gain
K(\pi_{k})
and a fixed belief
\pi_{k}
. Recall the resilience margin
m_{\theta}>0
in (
27
) and define

\rho_{\theta}=\sqrt{1-\frac{m_{\theta}}{\lambda_{\max}(P_{k})}},\qquad c_{P}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\max}(P_{k})}{\lambda_{\min}(P_{k})}}.

(29)

Let us assume:

(A1)
\Sigma_{0}=\Sigma_{1}=\Sigma\succ 0
;

(A2)
\left\|x_{k}^{(\theta)}-\hat{x}_{k}^{(\theta)}\right\|\leq\bar{e}_{k}
for
\theta\in\{0,1\}

(A3)
\left\|K(\pi_{k})\right\|\leq\bar{K}

Then, the following inequality holds true

\displaystyle|z_{k+1}-z_{k}|=|\ell_{k+1}|\leq\bar{r}_{k+1}\kappa_{\Sigma}\omega_{k+1},

(30)

where
\kappa_{\Sigma}=\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\|

\displaystyle\Gamma_{e}=2\left\|C_{1}A_{1}\right\|+\left\|C_{1}A_{1}-C_{0}A_{0}\right\|+\bar{K}\left\|C_{1}B_{1}-C_{0}B_{0}\right\|,

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt+\bar{K}c_{P}\max\{\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\}\left\|C_{1}B_{1}-C_{0}B_{0}\right\|

V
Conclusion

This paper formulated hidden-mode detection and belief-dependent control as a game between two decision makers with different objectives: the detector seeks informative belief updates, while the controller seeks regulation performance. This objective mismatch shows why the usual separation intuition between detector design and controller design may fail. For two-mode linear Gaussian systems, we theoretically characterized the resilience-induced blindness phenomenon by linking the LQR resilience margin to the log-likelihood evidence. The analysis showed that a controller that is more resilient in a fixed-mode regulation task can suppress mode-dependent output information, slow belief adaptation after mode switching, and degrade switched-mode performance. Numerical results showed that the fixed-mode performance trend can reverse under mode switching, while increasing detector sensitivity can partially restore the expected trend. These results motivate co-design methods that align regulation performance, belief adaptation, and measurement informativeness for future work.
