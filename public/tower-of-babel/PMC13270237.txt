From adoption to implementation: paediatricians’ experiences with C-reactive protein point-of-care testing in the community setting
Authors: Erinn D’hulster, Isabelle Lauwers, Mary Jaunsen Meijdam, Tine De Burghgraeve, Sibyl Anthierens, Jan Yvan Verbakel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe ARON trial demonstrated the (cost-)effectiveness of a multifaceted antimicrobial stewardship intervention including C-reactive protein (CRP) point-of-care testing (POCT) for children with acute illness in Belgian general practice and community-based paediatrics. However, little is known about how CRP POCT is experienced in paediatric practice in the community, particularly in the absence of structural support and a formal legal framework. The objective of the study was to explore how paediatricians use, interpret, and adapt CRP POCT in real-world community-based practice and to identify factors influencing its sustained implementation.MethodsWe conducted semi-structured interviews with Belgian paediatricians experienced in using CRP POCT, either through participation in the ARON trial or as part of routine care, allowing us to capture both protocol-guided and routine practice implementation experiences. Participants were recruited using a mixed sampling approach, combining convenience and snowball sampling. Data were analysed thematically, guided by two complementary implementation science frameworks: the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research and Normalization Process Theory.ResultsFifteen paediatricians were interviewed. Participants generally viewed CRP POCT positively, but reported navigating substantial implementation challenges, including the absence of reimbursement and unclear logistical routes. Use frequently extended beyond its intended diagnostic purpose, with CRP POCT employed to support communication with parents, provide reassurance or legitimise treatment decisions. Interpretation and confidence evolved through repeated use and informal peer exchange. In the absence of formal feedback or training, clinicians relied primarily on experiential learning to refine their practice.ConclusionsThese findings illustrate both the flexibility and fragility of self-directed implementation in the absence of system-level support. While paediatricians valued CRP POCT, translating early adoption into sustained and equitable routine use in community paediatric care will require formal guidance, financing structures, and shared interpretative frameworks.

Introduction

Acute infections are the most common reason for children to present in ambulatory care.1 The vast majority are self-limiting without the need for antibiotics, with serious infections accounting for approximately 4%–5% of cases.2 Nonetheless, antibiotic prescribing remains high, with one third to one half of prescriptions estimated to be inappropriate.3 This contributes to antimicrobial resistance, recognized by the World Health Organization as a major global health threat,4 while also exposing children to avoidable adverse effects and reinforcing unnecessary medicalization of mild illness.5 Despite sustained stewardship efforts, inappropriate prescribing persists—driven by diagnostic uncertainty, perceived parental expectations, risk-averse decision-making in young children and time pressure, among other factors.6,7

C-reactive protein point-of-care testing (CRP POCT) provides rapid information on the inflammatory response, assisting clinicians in distinguishing patients at low risk of serious infection from those requiring further evaluation and treatment.8–10 CRP POCT is performed on a finger-prick capillary sample with a quantitative result available within minutes during the consultation. By complementing clinical judgement with real-time diagnostic information, it can strengthen decisions to withhold antibiotics when serious infection is unlikely.8–10 In adult populations, CRP POCT is well established and recommended in several primary care national guidelines, including those of the UK, the Netherlands and some Scandinavian countries.11–13 In children, however, the evidence base is still emerging. Earlier evidence in paediatric ambulatory care has suggested that CRP POCT, used alongside clinical assessment, can support the identification of children at low risk of serious infection and reduce immediate antibiotic prescribing.14,15 Recently, the ARON trial demonstrated that a multifaceted intervention including CRP POCT safely reduced antibiotic prescribing at the index consultation (16% versus 22%; odds ratio 0.72, 95% confidence interval 0.55–0.94) in Belgian general practice and paediatric care.16 In Belgian primary care, however, its use is currently not regulated by a formal legal or reimbursement framework.17

Moving from evidence to routine practice requires understanding not only whether an intervention is effective, but also how it is adopted, adapted and sustained in real-world clinical settings.18 Implementation science frameworks such as the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) and Normalization Process Theory (NPT) highlight the importance of examining how clinical reasoning, organizational routines and contextual factors interact in everyday practice.19,20 This perspective aligns with the Medical Research Council’s guidance on evaluating complex interventions, which advocates for comprehensive process evaluation alongside outcome assessment to inform implementation and scale-up.18

Prior process evaluation research within the ARON trial focused on general practitioners (GPs) and parents,21 while the perspectives of paediatricians remain largely unexplored. Yet paediatricians operate in distinct clinical contexts, managing more complex or referred cases and working within different organizational and diagnostic frameworks. These differences may shape how CRP POCT is interpreted, integrated into workflows and sustained over time.

To address this gap, we conducted a qualitative study exploring how Belgian paediatricians perceive and use CRP POCT in the management of acutely ill children in the community. Through in-depth interviews, we examined factors influencing adoption, clinical use and integration into routine practice, with the aim of informing implementation strategies tailored to community-based paediatric care.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a qualitative study using semi-structured interviews with paediatricians, held either online (Microsoft Teams) or in person. This format allowed in-depth exploration of paediatricians’ experiences with CRP POCT in routine practice, while leaving flexibility to follow-up on emerging topics. Reporting followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research and the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research to ensure methodological rigour and transparency.22,23

Context and study setting

This study forms part of the nested process evaluation within the ARON trial, a pragmatic cluster-randomized controlled trial conducted in Belgian general practice and community-based paediatric care to support appropriate antibiotic prescribing in acutely ill children aged 6 months to 12 years. The ARON intervention combines a validated clinical decision tree, CRP POCT and structured safety-netting advice. Although the trial assessed the intervention as a whole, the present study narrowed its focus to CRP POCT, examining how this diagnostic tool is used and interpreted in clinical practice. A full description of the trial and the intervention is available elsewhere.24

This study centres on community-based paediatricians. In Belgium, many paediatricians work across both hospital and community settings. Families have direct access to community paediatricians without referral from a GP, and paediatricians are often the first point of contact for acute childhood illness. Community practices are typically solo or small group practices that operate largely independently from hospital infrastructure, with limited or no on-site access to laboratory diagnostics or imaging; they rely primarily on clinical assessment, with referral to hospitals for further investigation, which may shape how rapid tests are integrated into clinical decision-making. In this paper, we focused exclusively on CRP POCT use in paediatric care in the community in line with the ARON trial setting.

Recruitment and sampling

Participants were recruited using a combination of convenience and snowball sampling to maximize diversity and reach. Initially, study information was disseminated through established professional networks, more specifically structured peer review meetings conducted regularly across Belgium's paediatricians’ community and via study flyers containing a digital registration link. Snowball sampling was subsequently applied by asking participants to identify colleagues with experience using CRP POCT. All referred clinicians were approached using standardized study information and invitation procedures.

Recruitment initially focused on paediatricians working in community settings who had participated in the ARON trial, as their experience would provide insight into protocol-guided implementation. However, only a small number of ARON-trial paediatricians proved available and willing to participate within the recruitment window. Therefore, the inclusion criteria were broadened to include paediatricians using CRP POCT in routine care outside the trial context. This pragmatic adaptation allowed us to capture implementation experiences across different adoption pathways: structured trial-based implementation versus organic integration into existing practice. Paediatricians who participated in the ARON trial used CRP POCT within a standardized protocol with structured support and guidance, reflecting planned implementation. In contrast, paediatricians outside the trial applied the test based on their own clinical judgement and routines, typically with longer experience, reflecting self-directed adoption. This variation enabled exploration of how differing implementation contexts and stages of experience shaped the uptake and integration of CRP POCT into routine care. Participants were otherwise comparable in terms of practice setting, and by the time of the interview, ARON participants had used the test for at least one month, ensuring sufficient exposure to allow meaningful comparison between the two groups.

Interview procedure

Interviews were conducted by two female general practice trainees (M.J.M. and I.L.). Although they had no prior clinical relationships with participants, their clinical background facilitated rapport building and nuanced exploration of clinical decision-making processes.

Interviews were conducted either online or face to face, depending on participant preference. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was approached, with a target sample of 14–18 paediatricians using CRP POCT. After each interview, the team discussed emerging insights, and no further interviews were scheduled once no new themes emerged.

Interview design and content

A semi-structured topic guide was developed iteratively, grounded in implementation science frameworks. Following pilot testing with a physician outside the main sample, the wording of several questions was refined to improve clarity.

The topic guide was structured to map onto the CFIR and NPT, enabling systematic exploration of determinants of CRP POCT adoption and sustained use across multiple levels, while maintaining conversational flow (see Appendix, available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online). In practice, the guide functioned as a flexible framework rather than a fixed list of questions. Probes were used selectively to deepen exploration of topics that appeared particularly relevant.

At the individual level, interviews explored clinicians’ experiences with CRP POCT devices, including indications for testing, interpretation of CRP values and integration of results into clinical decision-making. Questions addressed confidence in interpretation and perceived influence on diagnostic and treatment decisions. Interpersonal aspects focused on communication with parents or caregivers, particularly how CRP results were used to explain illness severity, negotiate treatment decisions and manage expectations during consultations. Additionally, organizational and contextual factors were systematically addressed, including workflow integration, collaboration with laboratory services, institutional policies governing POC testing and economic considerations including device costs and reimbursement. This multi-level approach enabled a comprehensive assessment of factors influencing CRP POCT uptake and integration into routine practice.

Data analysis

All interviews were audio recorded with participant consent and transcribed verbatim by the interviewers (M.J.M. and I.L.) to preserve contextual understanding. To protect participant confidentiality, identifying information was removed during transcription. Recordings were deleted after quality checks, and transcripts were stored on password-protected, encrypted systems in accordance with data protection protocols.

Thematic analysis was conducted using NVivo 12 software (QSR International Pty Ltd, Melbourne, Australia).25 Data collection and analysis proceeded iteratively, with insights from early interviews informing subsequent data collection and refinement of analytical focus.

An initial codebook was developed by E.D. through close reading of all transcripts. This codebook was refined through iterative discussions with M.J.M. and I.L., drawing on their familiarity with interview contexts. The final codebook was applied across the complete dataset, with regular researcher triangulation meetings to ensure consistency and resolve interpretive differences through consensus.

Codes were subsequently synthesized into broader categories and themes through iterative analytical meetings involving the full research team (E.D., S.A., J.Y.V., T.D.B., M.J.M. and I.L.). Team members brought diverse perspectives—clinical practice experience, implementation science expertise and qualitative methodology knowledge—which strengthened analytical depth and reduced individual bias.

Thematic analysis was structured using the same two implementation science frameworks used to develop the interview topic guide. The CFIR supported identification of structural and contextual factors influencing CRP POCT implementation across multiple levels.19 Concurrently, the NPT sensitized the analysis to processes through which CRP POCT was understood, enacted and sustained within routine practice.20 This dual-framework approach enabled a multi-level interpretation of implementation dynamics and supported translation of findings into theoretically informed implementation.

Ethics

The study protocol and consent procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee Research of University Hospitals Leuven (S62005) as an amendment to the ARON trial protocol.

Prior to each interview, participants received written study information and provided digital informed consent via a secure online LimeSurvey platform,26 which was reviewed again at the start of each interview.

Results

Fifteen paediatricians were interviewed between April and September 2024. Fourteen interviews were conducted online and one in person, with a mean duration of 41 min. Five participants had previously participated in the ARON trial and still had access to the CRP POCT device and cartridges at the time of the interview.

Themes and subthemes

Four main themes and eleven subthemes were identified, describing the implementation landscape of CRP POCT in community-based paediatric care, revealing both modifiable barriers amenable to intervention and contextual factors that shape sustainable adoption. Theme 1 relates primarily to CFIR constructs, focusing on test characteristics and contextual constraints. Themes 2–4 reflect core NPT processes, including evolving understanding of the test (‘coherence’), learning through use (‘collective action’) and adaptation over time (‘reflexive monitoring’). However, the analysis revealed considerable interplay between the two frameworks. The interrelations between (sub)themes are shown in Figure 1.

Theme 1: Context-specific implementation is shaped by absence of structured guidance

Subtheme 1a: Lack of legal framework creates fragmented and inequitable CRP POCT implementation

Paediatricians described widely varying, improvised financial and logistical arrangements for CRP POCT implementation due to the absence of a formal legal framework in the community setting. Practices ranged from direct patient charging to informal collaboration with hospital laboratories for testing and invoicing, alongside diverse procurement strategies. These ad hoc solutions, often shaped by personal networks and institutional affiliations, were identified as core barriers to adoption and equitable access.

The cost is the main issue, of course. I take tests from the hospital; they tolerate that I use them in private practice. We bought the device as a group and calculated that if we charge €7 per test, we can recoup the cost.—P15, 27 years of experience, not in ARON

Some participants highlighted a disconnect between antimicrobial stewardship priorities and the lack of structural support for implementation.

The government wants to tackle resistance? Then start by allowing good diagnostics. CRP is essential for that.—P9, 46 years of experience, not in ARON

Subtheme 1b: Perceived ease of use and seamless integration into routine practice

Participants described CRP POCT as easy to use, minimally invasive compared with venous blood draws and well-integrated into existing workflow patterns. Rapid availability of results supported timely decision-making without disrupting consultation flow. Minor drawbacks, such as occasional device errors or environmental waste, were noted but not seen as key barriers to continued use.

By the time the child is dressed again and I’ve finished explaining everything to the parents, a few minutes have usually passed anyway, so I don’t really consider the waiting time a problem.—P6, 15 years of experience, ARON participant

Subtheme 1c: High acceptance and uptake of CRP POCT shaped by previous experience

Many paediatricians adopted CRP POCT in their community practice drawing on familiarity with rapid diagnostics in hospital settings. Prior experience acted as a key facilitator of uptake.

I’m used to testing in the hospital. It’s great that I can do this at home now too. I really think the future is having a mini-lab in every paediatric practice.—P12, 36 years of experience, not in ARON

Parents were perceived to be similarly receptive, valuing the presence of an objective marker alongside clinical judgement, which enhanced trust, and in some cases, this may even create demand-side pull for adoption.

Some parents tell me they came to see me because other paediatricians don’t perform the test. I believe they want a doctor’s opinion, yes, but they also value having an objective parameter.—P6, 15 years of experience, ARON participant

Theme 2: Redefining the role of CRP POCT in daily practice

Subtheme 2a: From diagnostic test to relational and communication tool

Some paediatricians described using CRP POCT not only to reduce diagnostic uncertainty, but also to reassure parents and build trust. Even when clinical judgement was clear, the test was sometimes used to demonstrate that antibiotics were unnecessary, aligning perceived parental expectations with clinical reasoning. Several clinicians also highlighted how the test offered a tangible action during consultations where intervention was expected.

If you can reassure parents with a CRP, they trust the treatment plan more. It makes the doctor-parent relationship easier.—P15, 27 years of experience, not in ARON

Subtheme 2b: Evidence-based exclusion versus post hoc confirmation and justification

Despite evolving and extended applications, paediatricians consistently affirmed that CRP POCT’s primary role remains the exclusion of serious infection, particularly in cases where clinical presentation is largely reassuring yet certain features raise uncertainty (e.g. persistent fever without clear signs of severity).

I mainly use it in children who look clinically reassuring, but where the illness persists or the story has worrying elements, to check whether my clinical impression is correct.—P11, 16 years of experience, ARON participant

If I am very reassured, I won’t use it, and if I am certain antibiotics are needed I won’t either. For me it is really a tool for in the grey zone.—P5, 17 years of experience, not in ARON

Contrarily, some clinicians reported using CRP POCT to substantiate antibiotic prescribing, indicating a shift from its intended role as an exclusionary tool to post hoc confirmation of treatment decisions.

I prescribe antibiotics very rarely, but when I do, I want it backed up by a CRP value.—P6, 15 years of experience, ARON participant

Subtheme 2c: Extended use as a monitoring tool in evolving illness and treatment

Several paediatricians reported using CRP POCT repeatedly to monitor illness progression or treatment response, extending beyond ARON’s one-time testing protocol. Clinicians stressed the importance of timing, warning against testing too early.

Sometimes I ask them to return. If the CRP hasn’t gone up much, I know I can leave it. But you shouldn’t do it too early, because then you can’t interpret it.—P1, 11 years of experience, not in ARON

Theme 3: Clinical interpretation evolves through experience and reflection

Subtheme 3a: Heuristics develop through repeated use and peer learning

Paediatricians described a learning curve primarily related to interpreting CRP POCT results. With experience, many developed informal heuristics or mental decision rules, often shaped through peer discussion rather than formal guidance.

For me, below 40 is probably viral. Above 70, probably bacterial. Over 150, I think of streptococcus. But between 40 and 70, I call it neither fish nor fowl.—P15, 27 years of experience, not in ARON

Reported interpretive thresholds varied strongly across clinicians, reflecting personal experience and peer learning.

Subtheme 3b: Grey zone values become manageable with experience

Most paediatricians reported that intermediate values were initially confusing, but repeated exposure enabled them to contextualize results and integrate them into diagnostic reasoning.

At first, fairly high values caused panic. Now we look at the child, and if the CRP is elevated but the child is well, we might wait and see. We didn’t have this kind of insight at the beginning, but within a few months we learned.—P12, 36 years of experience, not in ARON

Still, some continued to find mid-range values challenging, which increased perceived complexity and reduced confidence in the test’s added value.

Subtheme 3c: Integrating CRP into broader clinical reasoning

Paediatricians emphasized that CRP POCT is only one element of the diagnostic puzzle and should be interpreted alongside clinical presentation, illness history, age and timing. Several noted that certain viral infections can elicit high CRP values, reinforcing the need for contextual interpretation.

It’s like a GPS in your car. It can be helpful, but if you follow it blindly, you might end up in a dead end.—P12, 36 years of experience, not in ARON

When CRP results conflicted with strong clinical concern, clinical judgement took precedence.

If the CRP is very low but the child looks very sick, I won’t let the test decide for me. Conversely, a surprisingly high CRP will prompt further investigation.—P10, 24 years of experience, not in ARON

Theme 4: Practice shaped by the absence of formal guidance

Subtheme 4a: Use sustained by experiential satisfaction in the absence of feedback

Clinicians generally believed CRP POCT reduced their antibiotic prescribing but acknowledged uncertainty in the absence of formal feedback. Substituting for systematic outcome monitoring or feedback on prescribing behaviour, memorable clinical experiences—such as unexpectedly high CRP values—were key drivers of continued use.

I think it has an important influence on my prescribing, I think I prescribe much less… but actually, I don’t know.—P9, 46 years of experience, not in ARON

Subtheme 4b: Divergent views on standardization and training

Paediatricians reported that training focused mainly on technical device use, with limited guidance on clinical interpretation. Without shared protocols, practice varied and views on standardization diverged: some favoured clearer guidance, while others cautioned that rigid protocols risk oversimplifying complex clinical judgement.

To be useful, a protocol would need to include age, duration of fever, and so much more. Honestly, I think it’s almost impossible to capture all relevant factors. I think it would require a massive flowchart.—P10, 24 years of experience, not in ARON

Discussion

This qualitative study examined how paediatricians implement and perceive CRP POCT in the management of acutely ill children in community-based care, with the aim of identifying modifiable barriers and facilitators to inform future implementation efforts. Embedded within the ARON process evaluation, these findings complement earlier interviews with GPs and parents,21 while addressing the distinct clinical context in which paediatricians operate—often managing younger children with more severe or ambiguous presentations. More broadly, our findings contribute to discussions on diagnostic stewardship in ambulatory care,27 highlighting how POCT requires clear frameworks for indications, interpretation and follow-up to achieve stewardship potential. In the absence of such guidance, the paediatricians in this study described substantial variation in CRP POCT use, evolving interpretive heuristics and extension of the test beyond its evidence-based role as a rule-out tool for serious infection.

Participants largely represented early adopters who implemented CRP POCT without formal reimbursement or standardized guidance, offering a naturalistic view of implementation challenges and adaptive strategies in the absence of structured support.

Guided by two complementary implementation science frameworks, the CFIR and the NPT, our analysis identified four interconnected themes highlighting facilitators and barriers and pointing to leverage points for implementation at policy, organizational and individual levels.

Implementation challenges and adaptive responses

Paediatricians expressed broadly positive attitudes towards CRP POCT, echoing earlier ARON interviews with GPs who viewed the test as a supportive decision aid.21 Their early-adopter status likely contributed to this favourable view.

However, the absence of formal financial and logistical frameworks emerged as a dominant implementation barrier, representing a critical policy-level challenge beyond the reach of provider-focused interventions. This limits scalability and equitable access and underscores the need for policy advocacy to establish formal reimbursement as a prerequisite for broader uptake. This finding reinforces Van Hoof et al.'s emphasis on the critical importance of formal legal frameworks and structured implementation support,17 while aligning with international evidence demonstrating that CRP POCT adoption requires robust structural support.28

Evolution of clinical application and interpretation

Paediatricians described an evolving use of CRP POCT shaped by clinical experience, with the test increasingly applied beyond its evidence-based indication for ruling out serious infections to manage perceived parental expectations or justify treatment decisions. Similar patterns were observed in earlier ARON interviews with GPs.21 From an implementation science perspective, this adaptive expansion represents both a facilitator and a potential threat: it not only demonstrates flexible integration into routine practice but also raises concerns about intervention fidelity, overuse and deviation from intended indications, with possible cost and effectiveness implications. This tension aligns with quantitative evidence suggesting that non-targeted use may reduce both the clinical utility and cost-effectiveness of CRP POCT.29,30

These patterns exemplify what NPT describes as ‘coherence work’, whereby clinicians actively reinterpret and redefine a technology’s purpose through repeated use rather than adhering to fixed protocol definitions. For implementation planning, this highlights the need for strategies that acknowledge CRP POCT’s communicative value while reinforcing appropriate clinical use, such as decision support, audit and feedback or training focused on communication as an alternative to test-based reassurance.

Interpretive confidence and clinical experience

Paediatricians reported increasing interpretive confidence with repeated CRP POCT use and informal peer exchange. This growing confidence likely reflects both improved interpretation skills and more selective test use. From an implementation perspective, this experiential learning not only signals engagement but also poses risks: without formal guidance, variable heuristics may emerge, leading to inconsistent practice, particularly during early-adoption phases. Most paediatricians in this study, many with several years of CRP POCT experience, felt quite comfortable navigating intermediate (‘grey-zone’) values and integrating results into holistic clinical assessment. In contrast, earlier ARON interviews with GPs, who generally had less experience with CRP POCT, showed that grey-zone results often increased diagnostic uncertainty.21 This divergence suggests that interpretive competency develops gradually and requires targeted support during early implementation.

This pattern exemplifies NPT’s concept of ‘collective action’: the way interventions become embedded through repeated enactment and shared learning. However, reliance on experiential learning alone may prolong learning curves and amplify practice variation. From an implementation planning perspective, this highlights the need for structured early-phase support, for example, through mentorship programmes pairing experienced users with new adopters, case-based learning modules addressing common interpretation challenges, or decision support tools that provide real-time interpretive guidance for intermediate values.

Self-directed learning: the need for systematic feedback

In the absence of formal feedback, retraining or shared protocols, paediatricians relied on individual experience to refine CRP POCT use. While this self-directed learning reflects clinical flexibility, it limited insight into actual prescribing impacts, representing a significant missed opportunity for optimization and quality improvement and contributed to practice variation.

This finding resonates with broader implementation science literature linking inadequate formal evaluation to reduced protocol fidelity.19 While informal peer exchange supports learning, it cannot replace systematic feedback and outcome monitoring. This self-directed adaptation represents NPT’s ‘reflexive monitoring’ construct, but in the absence of formal feedback, appraisal remained individual rather than collective, potentially constraining shared learning essential for sustained implementation.

From an implementation planning perspective, these findings highlight the value of routine audit and feedback systems to support data-driven reflection on testing and prescribing patterns. Formalizing peer learning through quality improvement collaboratives could further strengthen shared learning while reinforcing evidence-based practice. At the same time, tension between clinical autonomy and standardization remains a key implementation challenge when introducing protocols.

Towards a comprehensive implementation strategy for CRP POCT in paediatric practice in the community

These findings highlight that sustainable CRP POCT implementation in community-based paediatric care requires more than test availability alone. Strengths and limitations

This study is, to our knowledge, the first to focus exclusively on paediatricians’ use and perceptions of CRP POCT in community-based care. A key strength is the inclusion of experienced users who had integrated the test into routine practice, offering a naturalistic perspective on implementation beyond a trial setting and providing insight into sustainability and adaptive use. The use of open-ended interviews, analysis guided by two complementary frameworks (CFIR and NPT) and a multidisciplinary research team further strengthened analytical depth and reflexivity.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Only a small number of participants had used CRP POCT within the ARON protocol, limiting direct comparison between structured and self-directed use. Interviews and analysis were conducted by different team members, which may have reduced continuity in interpretation. Data may also have been shaped by interviewer–participant dynamics and social desirability. To mitigate this, both interviewers received training in qualitative interviewing, used a piloted topic guide with open-ended prompts, and held regular debrief meetings with the wider research team to reflect on their own positioning and emerging interpretations. Additionally, participants were recruited through convenience and snowball sampling, including personal networks, and consisted mainly of experienced users with a favourable attitude towards the test, which may have introduced a positive bias. Furthermore, no information was available on antibiotic prescribing rates or quality among participating paediatricians, and the specific Belgian context may limit transferability to other healthcare systems or to less experienced users, though the core implementation challenges identified are likely relevant across settings.

Conclusion

The intersection of structural barriers, adaptive use and evolving interpretive confidence underscores the complexity of implementing diagnostic innovations in routine care. Sustainable uptake requires supportive frameworks that combine clear guidance with ongoing training, feedback and shared learning. As highlighted in previous work on implementation needs and budget impact for CRP POCT in Belgium,31 future efforts must address both infrastructure and the dynamic processes through which clinicians embed new diagnostics in practice. Our findings identify modifiable factors that can inform comprehensive, theory-informed implementation strategies.
