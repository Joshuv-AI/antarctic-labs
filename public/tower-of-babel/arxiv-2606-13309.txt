A complete solution to the biased Alon-Krivelevich-Spencer-Szabó criterion problem for the discrepancy game Supported by the National Science Foundation of China (Nos. 12471329, 12401461 and 12061059).
Yaping Mao, Meiqin Wei, Gang Yang

Abstract
Let H = ( V , ℰ ) H=(V,\mathcal{E}) be a finite hypergraph. For positive integers p p and q q , the ( p : q ) (p:q) -biased discrepancy game on H H is played in complete rounds. In each round, Balancer first claims p p previously unclaimed vertices, and then Unbalancer claims q q previously unclaimed vertices. Let B B and U U be the final sets of vertices claimed by Balancer and Unbalancer, respectively. For an edge e ∈ ℰ e\in\mathcal{E} , define D e = q | B ∩ e ​ | − p | ​ U ∩ e | = ( p + q ) ​ | B ∩ e | − p ​ | e | D_{e}=q|B\cap e|-p|U\cap e|=(p+q)|B\cap e|-p|e| . Thus D e D_{e} measures the deviation of Balancer’s share of e e from the density p / ( p + q ) p/(p+q) . In 2005, Alon, Krivelevich, Spencer and Szabó proved a Chernoff-type potential criterion for the unbiased alternating discrepancy game, corresponding to the case p = q = 1 p=q=1 , and asked for a biased analogue for general p , q p,q . In this paper, we give an affirmative answer of their problem and prove a complete biased analogue in the complete-round formulation. More precisely, for every finite hypergraph H = ( V , ℰ ) H=(V,\mathcal{E}) and every fixed bias ( p : q ) (p:q) , we give an explicit exponential condition under which Balancer has a strategy forcing − L e − ≤ D e ≤ L e + -L_{e}^{-}\leq D_{e}\leq L_{e}^{+} for every e ∈ ℰ e\in\mathcal{E} , where L e + L_{e}^{+} and L e − L_{e}^{-} are prescribed edge-dependent target values. Introduction

Discrepancy theory has its roots in the classical study of irregularities of
distribution, initiated by van der Corput and developed substantially by Roth

[
7
,
15
]
. In the combinatorial setting, one studies a
finite set system, or equivalently a hypergraph
H=(V,\mathcal{E})
, and asks
whether the vertex set can be divided into two parts so that every hyperedge is
nearly balanced.
This hypergraph viewpoint was explicitly
introduced and developed by Beck in his work on discrepancies of integer
sequences and hypergraphs
[
2
]
; an early foundational result in
this direction is the Beck–Fiala theorem
[
5
]
. Hypergraph
discrepancy has since become a central topic in combinatorics, with deep
connections to probabilistic methods, set systems, geometric discrepancy and
positional games; see, for example,

[
4
,
6
,
14
,
16
]
.

A
positional game
is usually described by a pair

(X,\mathcal{F})
, where
X
is a finite board and

\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{X}
is a family of distinguished subsets,
often called
target sets
or
winning sets
. Equivalently, one may view

(X,\mathcal{F})
as a hypergraph whose vertices are the elements of

X
and whose hyperedges are the members of
\mathcal{F}
.
The systematic study of positional games goes back to the work of
Hales and Jewett
[
10
]
and Erdős and Selfridge

[
8
]
; see also the monograph of Hefetz,
Krivelevich, Stojaković and Szabó
[
11
]
.

In the classical weak and strong versions of positional games, the
players alternately claim previously unclaimed elements of the board,
and the objective is to occupy all elements of some winning set.
Discrepancy games follow the same hypergraph and claiming framework,
but the objective is quantitative rather than existential: instead of
trying to complete a winning set, one evaluates the final partition of
the board by the discrepancies it induces on all hyperedges.

In this quantitative direction, Alon, Krivelevich, Spencer and
Szabó
[
1
]
introduced a game-theoretic form of hypergraph
discrepancy. The game is played on the vertex set of a finite hypergraph

H=(V,\mathcal{E})
. Here and throughout, to
claim
a vertex means
to choose a previously unclaimed vertex and assign it permanently to that
player. In the fair alternating game, Balancer and Unbalancer claim
previously unclaimed vertices one at a time. At the end of the game,
Balancer’s vertices are labelled
+1
and Unbalancer’s vertices are
labelled
-1
. Thus each edge
e\in\mathcal{E}
receives an edge-sum,
and Balancer’s aim is to keep all these edge-sums small in absolute
value.

Alon, Krivelevich, Spencer and Szabó
[
1
]
proved a Chernoff-type sufficient condition under which Balancer can achieve this aim. Their argument is based on a potential function, in the spirit of the Erdős–Selfridge criterion for Maker–Breaker games
[
8
]
and Beck’s fake probabilistic method
[
3
,
4
]
. Related potential arguments also appear in biased games and pseudorandom graph games; see, for example, Lu’s matching-game criterion
[
13
]
, the game of JumbleG of Frieze, Krivelevich, Pikhurko and Szabó
[
9
]
, and the Bart–Moe games studied by Hefetz, Krivelevich and Szabó
[
12
]
.

In the notation of the present paper, the main result of Alon,
Krivelevich, Spencer and Szabó may be stated as follows

[
1
, Theorem 1]
.
The final sets of vertices claimed by Balancer and Unbalancer are denoted by
B
and
U
, respectively.

Theorem 1.1

(Alon,
Krivelevich, Spencer and Szabó
[
1
]
)
.

Let
H=(V,\mathcal{E})
be a finite hypergraph with

\mathcal{E}=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\}
.
Suppose that
|V|
is even, and consider the fair alternating discrepancy
game on
V
in which Balancer moves first. Let

b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})

be a vector of nonnegative real numbers. If

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\exp\!\left\{-\frac{b_{i}^{2}}{2|e_{i}|}\right\}\leq\frac{1}{2},

then Balancer has a strategy guaranteeing

\bigl||B\cap e_{i}|-|U\cap e_{i}|\bigr|\leq b_{i}

for every
i=1,\ldots,m
.
In particular, Balancer has such a strategy whenever

b_{i}\geq\sqrt{2|e_{i}|\log(2m)}

for every
i=1,\ldots,m
.

In the notation of the present paper, Beck’s uniform biased discrepancy
theorem may be stated as follows
[
4
, Theorem 17.5]
.

Theorem 1.2

(Beck
[
4
]
)
.

Let
H=(V,\mathcal{E})
be a finite
n
-uniform hypergraph, and put

m=|\mathcal{E}|
. In the
(p:q)
-game, Balancer claims
p
previously
unclaimed vertices and then Unbalancer claims
q
previously unclaimed
vertices in each complete round. Suppose that Balancer moves first.
Then Balancer has a strategy such that, at the end of the game, for every
edge
e\in\mathcal{E}
,

\frac{p-\alpha}{p+q}\,n<|B\cap e|<\frac{p+\alpha}{p+q}\,n,

where

\alpha=\left(1+O\!\left(pq\sqrt{\frac{\log m}{(p+q)n}}\right)\right)2pq\sqrt{\frac{\log m}{(p+q)n}}.

Equivalently, Balancer can force

\left||B\cap e|-\frac{p}{p+q}n\right|<\frac{\alpha}{p+q}n\qquad\text{for every }e\in\mathcal{E}.

We shall use the following biased version of the discrepancy game. Let
p,q
be positive integers. In the
(p:q)
-game
, a complete round consists of two parts: first Balancer claims
p
previously unclaimed vertices, and then Unbalancer claims
q
previously unclaimed vertices. In the complete-round formulation considered below, the number of vertices of the board is divisible by
p+q
, so that all vertices are claimed after an integral number of complete rounds.

At the end of their paper, Alon, Krivelevich, Spencer and Szabó
[
1
]
posed the corresponding biased problem for general hypergraphs and general
p,q
. We record it in the following form.

Problem 1.3

(Alon,
Krivelevich, Spencer and Szabó
[
1
]
)
.

Let
p,q
be positive integers. Establish a discrepancy criterion for the
(p:q)
-game on a finite hypergraph
H=(V,\mathcal{E})
. In particular, give conditions on
H
which guarantee that Balancer can force his final share of every edge
e\in\mathcal{E}
to be close to
p|e|/(p+q)
.

For an edge
e\in\mathcal{E}
, the natural target density for Balancer is
p/(p+q)
. We measure the signed deviation from this target by

D_{e}=q|B\cap e|-p|U\cap e|=(p+q)|B\cap e|-p|e|.

(1.1)

Thus controlling
D_{e}
is exactly equivalent to controlling the deviation of
|B\cap e|
from its target value
p|e|/(p+q)
.

Problem
1.3
is genuinely different from the fair alternating game. In the fair game, after a move of Balancer, Unbalancer claims only one vertex before Balancer moves again. In the
(p:q)
-game, a complete round may affect the same edge several times.
We shall therefore regard each complete round as a single step. Balancer first chooses a set of
p
unclaimed vertices, and then Unbalancer chooses a set of
q
vertices from those which remain unclaimed. Balancer’s choice will be made as one
p
-set, rather than as
p
independent single choices.

We now give the notation used in the criterion. Empty edges have
D_{e}=0
and will be omitted. For each edge
e\in\mathcal{E}
, we prescribe two nonnegative numbers
L_{e}^{+}
and
L_{e}^{-}
. The number
L_{e}^{+}
bounds the allowed positive deviation of
D_{e}
, while
L_{e}^{-}
bounds the allowed negative deviation of
D_{e}
. Thus the desired final estimate for
e
is

-L_{e}^{-}\leq D_{e}\leq L_{e}^{+}
.
We also associate two positive parameters
t_{e}
and
s_{e}
with
e
. The parameter
t_{e}
is used for the upper inequality
D_{e}\leq L_{e}^{+}
, while
s_{e}
is used for the lower inequality
D_{e}\geq-L_{e}^{-}
. During the game,
c_{e}
denotes the number of vertices of
e
which have already been claimed.

We next introduce two auxiliary functions. For the upper inequality, the relevant exponential expression is
e^{tD_{e}}
. If Balancer claims one vertex of
e
, then
D_{e}
increases by
q
, and this expression is multiplied by
e^{qt}
. If Unbalancer claims one vertex of
e
, then
D_{e}
decreases by
p
, and this expression is multiplied by
e^{-pt}
. We divide both one-vertex factors by the same positive number
R
. The upper factor
R^{+}_{p,q}(t)
is defined to be the positive solution of

p\frac{e^{qt}}{R}+\left(\frac{e^{-pt}}{R}\right)^{q}=p+1.

(1.2)

For the lower inequality, the relevant exponential expression is
e^{-sD_{e}}
. If Balancer claims one vertex of
e
, this expression is multiplied by
e^{-qs}
; if Unbalancer claims one vertex of
e
, it is multiplied by
e^{ps}
. The lower factor
R^{-}_{p,q}(s)
is defined to be the positive solution of

p\frac{e^{-qs}}{R}+\left(\frac{e^{ps}}{R}\right)^{q}=p+1.

(1.3)

The left hand sides of (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) are continuous and strictly decreasing functions of
R>0
, tend to infinity as
R\to 0^{+}
, and tend to zero as
R\to\infty
. Hence the positive solutions are well defined and unique.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
H=(V,\mathcal{E})
be a finite hypergraph with no empty edges. Suppose that
|V|
is divisible by
p+q
, and that the
(p:q)
-game on
V
is played in complete rounds with Balancer moving first in each round. For every edge
e\in\mathcal{E}
, let
L_{e}^{+},L_{e}^{-}\geq 0
. Assume that there exist parameters
t_{e},s_{e}>0
such that

\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}e^{-t_{e}L_{e}^{+}}\bigl(R^{+}_{p,q}(t_{e})\bigr)^{|e|}+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}e^{-s_{e}L_{e}^{-}}\bigl(R^{-}_{p,q}(s_{e})\bigr)^{|e|}\leq 1.

(1.4)

Then Balancer has a strategy guaranteeing, at the end of the game,

-L_{e}^{-}\leq q|B\cap e|-p|U\cap e|\leq L_{e}^{+}\qquad\text{for all }e\in\mathcal{E}.

Equivalently,

-\frac{L_{e}^{-}}{p+q}\leq|B\cap e|-\frac{p}{p+q}|e|\leq\frac{L_{e}^{+}}{p+q}\qquad\text{for all }e\in\mathcal{E}.

Theorem
1.4
should be compared with Theorems
1.1

and
1.2
as follows. First, when
p=q=1
, equations
(
1.2
) and (
1.3
) give
R^{+}_{1,1}(t)=R^{-}_{1,1}(t)=\cosh t
.
Thus, by taking

L_{e}^{+}=L_{e}^{-}=b_{e}
,
and

t_{e}=s_{e}=\frac{b_{e}}{|e|}
,
and using the elementary inequality
\cosh x\leq\exp(x^{2}/2)
, the condition
in Theorem
1.4
reduces to

2\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\exp\!\left\{-\frac{b_{e}^{2}}{2|e|}\right\}\leq 1.

This is precisely the condition in Theorem
1.1
. Hence
Theorem
1.4
recovers the fair alternating discrepancy criterion
of Alon, Krivelevich, Spencer and Szabó.

Second, Beck’s Theorem
1.2
is a biased result, but it is
uniform: all edges have the same size
n
, and a single common error term is
used for every edge. Theorem
1.4
gives an edge-dependent
criterion. The hypergraph need not be uniform, the upper and lower target
values
L_{e}^{+}
and
L_{e}^{-}
may vary with
e
, and the exponential parameters

t_{e}
and
s_{e}
may also vary with
e
. In the special case of an

n
-uniform hypergraph with common targets and common parameters,
Theorem
1.4
gives a Beck-type Chernoff-scale estimate around the
density
p/(p+q)
.
The formal specializations of Theorem
1.4
yielding
Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
are recorded after the
proof of Theorem
1.4
; see Section
2
.

We finally state the pseudo-random graph application. This is the

Problem
1.3
. For disjoint sets
S,T\subseteq[n]
, let

e_{G}(S,T)
denote the number of edges of
G
with one endpoint in
S
and the

T
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Fix positive integers
p,q
, and put

\rho=\frac{p}{p+q}.

There exist constants

and
\eta_{0}=\eta_{0}(p,q)>0

In the

satisfying

\left|d_{G_{B}}(v)-\rho(n-1)\right|\leq C\sqrt{n\log n}+O_{p,q}(1)\qquad\text{for every }v\in[n],

and, for every pair of disjoint sets

\left|\frac{e_{G_{B}}(S,T)}{|S||T|}-\rho\right|\leq\eta.

Proof of Theorem
1.4

We now prove two elementary lemmas which will be used to choose Balancer’s
p
vertices in one round. The first lemma is a one-edge estimate.

Let
C>0
.
For integers
0\leq i\leq p
and
0\leq j\leq q
, define

H_{C}(i,j)=(C-1)\Bigl((p-i)jC^{i}-i(q-j)C^{i-1}\Bigr).

(2.1)

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
C,M>0
.
For all integers
0\leq i\leq p
and
0\leq j\leq q
, if
pC+M^{q}\leq p+1
, then

C^{i}M^{j}-1+\frac{1}{q}H_{C}(i,j)\leq 0.

Proof.

Put
\delta=C-1
and
\rho=j/q
. Then
0\leq\rho\leq 1
, and the assumption gives
M^{q}\leq p+1-pC=1-p\delta
. Since
M^{q}>0
, we have
1-p\delta>0
. We use the elementary fact that, for
0\leq\rho\leq 1
, the function
x\mapsto x^{\rho}
is concave on
(0,\infty)
; hence
x^{\rho}\leq 1+\rho(x-1)
for every
x>0
. Applying this with
x=1-p\delta
, we get
(1-p\delta)^{\rho}\leq 1-\rho p\delta
. Therefore
M^{j}=(M^{q})^{\rho}\leq(1-p\delta)^{\rho}\leq 1-\rho p\delta
, and so

C^{i}M^{j}-1\leq C^{i}-1-\rho p\delta C^{i}.

(2.2)

Since
j=q\rho
, it follows from (
2.1
) that

\frac{1}{q}H_{C}(i,j)=\delta\bigl((p-i)\rho C^{i}-i(1-\rho)C^{i-1}\bigr).

(2.3)

Combining (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) gives

\displaystyle C^{i}M^{j}-1+\frac{1}{q}H_{C}(i,j)

\displaystyle\leq C^{i}-1-\rho p\delta C^{i}+\delta\bigl((p-i)\rho C^{i}-i(1-\rho)C^{i-1}\bigr)

\displaystyle=C^{i}-1-i\rho\delta C^{i}-i(1-\rho)\delta C^{i-1}

\displaystyle=C^{i}-1-i\delta C^{i-1}(\rho C+1-\rho)

\displaystyle=C^{i}-1-i(C-1)C^{i-1}\bigl(1+\rho(C-1)\bigr).

It remains to show that the last expression is nonpositive. If
i=0
, this is immediate. Assume
i\geq 1
.
First suppose that
C\geq 1
. Then
C^{i}-1=(C-1)(1+C+\cdots+C^{i-1})\leq i(C-1)C^{i-1}
. Since
1+\rho(C-1)\geq 1
, it follows that
C^{i}-1\leq i(C-1)C^{i-1}\bigl(1+\rho(C-1)\bigr)
, as required.

It remains to consider
0<C\leq 1
. Write
\eta=1-C
. Then
0\leq\eta<1
and
1+\rho(C-1)=1-\rho\eta\in[0,1]
. The desired inequality is equivalent to
1-C^{i}\geq i(1-C)C^{i-1}\bigl(1-\rho(1-C)\bigr)
. But
1-C^{i}=(1-C)(1+C+\cdots+C^{i-1})\geq i(1-C)C^{i-1}
, because
0<C\leq 1
implies
1,C,\ldots,C^{i-1}\geq C^{i-1}
. Since
1-\rho(1-C)\leq 1
, the desired inequality follows. This proves
C^{i}M^{j}-1+\frac{1}{q}H_{C}(i,j)\leq 0
in all cases.
∎

p
-set at once.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
X
be a finite set with
|X|\geq p+q
. Let
\mathcal{I}
be a finite index set. For each
r\in\mathcal{I}
, let
E_{r}\subseteq X
, let
w_{r}\geq 0
, and let
C_{r},M_{r}>0
satisfy

pC_{r}+M_{r}^{q}\leq p+1
.
Let
S
be a
p
-subset of
X
such that

\sum_{r\in\mathcal{I}}w_{r}C_{r}^{|E_{r}\cap S|}=\min\left\{\sum_{r\in\mathcal{I}}w_{r}C_{r}^{|E_{r}\cap A|}\,|\,A\subseteq X,|A|=p\right\}

(2.4)

over all
p
-subsets of
X
. Then for every
q
-subset

T\subseteq X\setminus S
,

\sum_{r\in\mathcal{I}}w_{r}\left(C_{r}^{|E_{r}\cap S|}M_{r}^{|E_{r}\cap T|}-1\right)\leq 0.

Proof.

Let
F(S)=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{I}}w_{r}C_{r}^{|E_{r}\cap S|}
.
Fix a
q
-subset
T\subseteq X\setminus S
. For
x\in S
and
y\in T
, write

S-x+y=(S\setminus\{x\})\cup\{y\}
, which is again a
p
-subset of
X
.
Let

\Delta=\sum_{x\in S}\sum_{y\in T}\bigl(F(S-x+y)-F(S)\bigr).

(2.5)

Since
F(S)=\min\left\{F(V)\,|\,V\subseteq X,|V|=p\right\}
, we have

F(S-x+y)-F(S)\geq 0
for
x\in S
and
y\in T
.
Therefore,
\Delta\geq 0
.
We now compute the same quantity by expanding the definition of
F
. Since

F(A)=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{I}}w_{r}C_{r}^{|E_{r}\cap A|}\qquad(A\subseteq X,\ |A|=p),

it follows from (
2.5
) that

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in S}\sum_{y\in T}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{I}}w_{r}\left(C_{r}^{|E_{r}\cap(S-x+y)|}-C_{r}^{|E_{r}\cap S|}\right)

(2.6)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{I}}w_{r}\sum_{x\in S}\sum_{y\in T}\left(C_{r}^{|E_{r}\cap(S-x+y)|}-C_{r}^{|E_{r}\cap S|}\right).

Fix
r\in\mathcal{I}
, and let

i=|E_{r}\cap S|,j=|E_{r}\cap T|
.
We compute the inner double sum for this fixed
r
. If

x\notin E_{r}
and
y\in E_{r}
, then the exponent

increases from
i
to
i+1
, and the change is

C_{r}^{i+1}-C_{r}^{i}=(C_{r}-1)C_{r}^{i}
.
There are
(p-i)j

(x,y)\in S\times T
of this type.
If
x\in E_{r}
and
y\notin E_{r}
, then the exponent decreases from
i
to

i-1
, and the change is

C_{r}^{i-1}-C_{r}^{i}=-(C_{r}-1)C_{r}^{i-1}
.
There are
i(q-j)
ordered pairs of this type.
In the two remaining cases, namely when both

lie in
E_{r}
or when
neither of them lies in
E_{r}
, the number

does not change,

is

(p-i)j(C_{r}-1)C_{r}^{i}-i(q-j)(C_{r}-1)C_{r}^{i-1}
.
Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in S}\sum_{y\in T}\left(C_{r}^{|E_{r}\cap(S-x+y)|}-C_{r}^{|E_{r}\cap S|}\right)

(2.7)

\displaystyle=(C_{r}-1)\Bigl((p-i)jC_{r}^{i}-i(q-j)C_{r}^{i-1}\Bigr)=H_{C_{r}}(i,j).

Since

and

, it follows from (

\Delta=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{I}}w_{r}H_{C_{r}}(|E_{r}\cap S|,|E_{r}\cap T|).

Remark 2.1

; the proof for

R^{-}_{p,q}
is the same.
Since
R^{+}_{p,q}(t)>0
, we write

R^{+}_{p,q}(t)=e^{r(t)}
,
that is,
r(t)=\log R^{+}_{p,q}(t)
.
Substituting this into (
1.2
), we obtain

pe^{qt-r(t)}+e^{-pqt-qr(t)}=p+1.

(3.1)

To justify the expansion of
r(t)
near
t=0
, define

F(t,r)=pe^{qt-r}+e^{-pqt-qr}-(p+1).

Then,

F(0,0)=0

and

\frac{\partial F}{\partial r}(0,0)=-p-q\neq 0.

Hence the equation
F(t,r)=0
determines, for
t
sufficiently close to
0
, a unique analytic function
r=r(t)
with
r(0)=0
. Therefore the Taylor expansion of
r(t)
has the form

r(t)=\alpha t+\beta t^{2}+O_{p,q}(t^{3})
,
where
\alpha
and
\beta
are constants depending only on
p,q
.
Substituting this expansion into (
3.1
)
gives

pe^{(q-\alpha)t-\beta t^{2}+O_{p,q}(t^{3})}+e^{(-pq-q\alpha)t-q\beta t^{2}+O_{p,q}(t^{3})}=p+1.

(3.2)

Using
e^{at+O(t^{2})}=1+at+O(t^{2})
, the coefficient of
t
in the left hand side is

p(q-\alpha)+(-pq-q\alpha)=-(p+q)\alpha
.
The right hand side
p+1
has no term of order
t
, and hence this coefficient must be zero. Therefore
\alpha=0
, and hence (
3.2
) becomes

pe^{qt-\beta t^{2}+O_{p,q}(t^{3})}+e^{-pqt-q\beta t^{2}+O_{p,q}(t^{3})}=p+1.

Using

e^{at+bt^{2}+O(t^{3})}=1+at+\left(b+\frac{a^{2}}{2}\right)t^{2}+O(t^{3}),

we have

pe^{qt-\beta t^{2}+O_{p,q}(t^{3})}=p\left(1+qt+\left(\frac{q^{2}}{2}-\beta\right)t^{2}+O_{p,q}(t^{3})\right),

(3.3)

and

e^{-pqt-q\beta t^{2}+O_{p,q}(t^{3})}=1-pq\,t+\left(\frac{p^{2}q^{2}}{2}-q\beta\right)t^{2}+O_{p,q}(t^{3}).

(3.4)

By (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), we give

\displaystyle pe^{qt-\beta t^{2}+O_{p,q}(t^{3})}

\displaystyle+e^{-pqt-q\beta t^{2}+O_{p,q}(t^{3})}

\displaystyle=p+1+\left[p\left(\frac{q^{2}}{2}-\beta\right)+\left(\frac{p^{2}q^{2}}{2}-q\beta\right)\right]t^{2}+O_{p,q}(t^{3}).

Since the right hand side of (
3.1
) is the constant
p+1
,
the coefficient of
t^{2}
must be zero. Hence

p\left(\frac{q^{2}}{2}-\beta\right)+\left(\frac{p^{2}q^{2}}{2}-q\beta\right)=0.

Equivalently,

\frac{pq^{2}(p+1)}{2}-(p+q)\beta=0,

and therefore

\beta=\frac{pq^{2}(p+1)}{2(p+q)}.

Consequently,

\log R^{+}_{p,q}(t)=r(t)=\frac{pq^{2}(p+1)}{2(p+q)}t^{2}+O_{p,q}(t^{3}).

The same computation applied to (
1.3
) gives

\log R^{-}_{p,q}(s)=\frac{pq^{2}(p+1)}{2(p+q)}s^{2}+O_{p,q}(s^{3}).

Choosing
A=A(p,q)
sufficiently large and then choosing

\tau=\tau(p,q)>0
sufficiently small proves the lemma.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
A
and
\tau
be the constants from Lemma
3.1
.
We shall choose the constants in the statement in the following order. First choose

K=K(p,q)
so large that

\frac{(p+q)^{2}K^{2}}{4A}\geq 3.

Next choose
\eta_{0}=\eta_{0}(p,q)>0
so small that

0<\eta_{0}\leq\frac{1}{4}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{(p+q)\eta_{0}}{4A}\leq\tau.

Finally choose
C=C(p,q)
sufficiently large. During the proof we shall increase

C
several times, always depending only on
p,q
.

Let
0<\eta\leq\eta_{0}
, and put

k=\lceil\eta n\rceil
.
By increasing
C
if necessary, the assumption

\eta^{3}n\geq C\log(e/\eta)

implies

\eta n\geq 1

and hence

k\leq 2\eta n
.
It also implies that
n
is sufficiently large for all estimates below.

We first prove the assertion in the complete-round formulation. The board of the
auxiliary discrepancy game is the edge set of
K_{n}
, that is,
W=E(K_{n})
.
A set claimed by Balancer in this auxiliary game will be regarded as the edge set
of the graph
G_{B}
.

We define an auxiliary hypergraph

\mathcal{H}=(W,\mathcal{F})

as follows. The first family of hyperedges consists of the stars

F_{v}=\{vx:x\in[n]\setminus\{v\}\}(v\in[n])
.
Thus

|F_{v}|=n-1
.
The second family of hyperedges consists of bipartite edge sets

F_{S,T}=\{xy:x\in S,\ y\in T\}
,
where
S,T\subseteq[n]
are disjoint
k
-sets. Thus

|F_{S,T}|=k^{2}
.
We shall apply Theorem
1.4
to this auxiliary hypergraph.

For every star
F_{v}
, set

L_{v}^{+}=L_{v}^{-}=(p+q)K\sqrt{(n-1)\log(2n)}.

(3.5)

We choose the same exponential parameter for the two tails:

t_{v}=s_{v}=\frac{L_{v}^{+}}{2A(n-1)}=\frac{(p+q)K}{2A}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2n)}{n-1}}.

(3.6)

After increasing
C
, the hypothesis on
\eta^{3}n
guarantees that

n
is large enough so that

t_{v}=s_{v}\leq\tau

for
v\in[n]
.

By Lemma
3.1
,

\log R^{+}_{p,q}(t_{v})\leq At_{v}^{2}
and
\log R^{-}_{p,q}(s_{v})\leq As_{v}^{2}
.
Therefore, by (
3.5
) and (
3.6
), each upper star summand in (
1.4
) is at most

\displaystyle e^{-t_{v}L_{v}^{+}}\bigl(R^{+}_{p,q}(t_{v})\bigr)^{n-1}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\{-t_{v}L_{v}^{+}+A(n-1)t_{v}^{2}\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\frac{(L_{v}^{+})^{2}}{4A(n-1)}\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\frac{(p+q)^{2}K^{2}}{4A}\log(2n)\right\}.

The same estimate holds for the lower star summand. Since

\frac{(p+q)^{2}K^{2}}{4A}\geq 3,

the total contribution of the upper and lower tails of all stars is at most

2n(2n)^{-3}<\frac{1}{4}

for
n\geq 3
.

We now consider the bipartite hyperedges
F_{S,T}
. For every ordered pair
of disjoint
k
-sets
S,T
, set

L_{S,T}^{+}=L_{S,T}^{-}=(p+q)\frac{\eta}{2}k^{2}.

(3.7)

Again choose the same exponential parameter for the two tails:

t_{S,T}=s_{S,T}=\frac{L_{S,T}^{+}}{2Ak^{2}}=\frac{(p+q)\eta}{4A}.

(3.8)

The choice of
\eta_{0}
gives

t_{S,T}=s_{S,T}\leq\tau
.
Thus Lemma
3.1
gives

\log R^{+}_{p,q}(t_{S,T})\leq At_{S,T}^{2}

and
\log R^{-}_{p,q}(s_{S,T})\leq As_{S,T}^{2}
.
Hence, by (
3.7
) and (
3.8
), each upper bipartite summand is at most

\displaystyle e^{-t_{S,T}L_{S,T}^{+}}\bigl(R^{+}_{p,q}(t_{S,T})\bigr)^{k^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\{-t_{S,T}L_{S,T}^{+}+Ak^{2}t_{S,T}^{2}\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\frac{(L_{S,T}^{+})^{2}}{4Ak^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\frac{(p+q)^{2}}{16A}\eta^{2}k^{2}\right\}.

The lower bipartite summand satisfies the same bound. Put

\gamma=\frac{(p+q)^{2}}{16A}.

The number of ordered pairs of disjoint
k
-subsets is at most

\binom{n}{k}^{2}
.
Using

\binom{n}{k}\leq\left(\frac{en}{k}\right)^{k}

and
k\geq\eta n
,
we get

\binom{n}{k}^{2}\leq\left(\frac{en}{k}\right)^{2k}\leq\left(\frac{e}{\eta}\right)^{2k}.

Therefore the total contribution of all upper and lower bipartite tails is at most

2\exp\left\{2k\log(e/\eta)-\gamma\eta^{2}k^{2}\right\}
.
Since
k\geq\eta n
, we have

\eta^{2}k^{2}\geq\eta^{3}nk
.
Thus the exponent is at most

2k\log(e/\eta)-\gamma\eta^{3}nk
.
By increasing
C
so that

\gamma C\geq 5
,
the hypothesis

\eta^{3}n\geq C\log(e/\eta)

implies

2k\log(e/\eta)-\gamma\eta^{3}nk\leq-3k\log(e/\eta)
.
As
k\geq 1
and
\log(e/\eta)\geq 1
, the total contribution of all bipartite
hyperedges is less than

2e^{-3}<\frac{1}{4}
.

Combining the star and bipartite estimates, the sum in (
1.4
)
for the auxiliary hypergraph is strictly smaller than
1
. Theorem
1.4

therefore applies. Hence Balancer has a strategy such that, for every vertex

v\in[n]
,

\left||B\cap F_{v}|-\rho|F_{v}|\right|\leq\frac{L_{v}^{+}}{p+q}=K\sqrt{(n-1)\log(2n)}.

Since
B
is the edge set of
G_{B}
, we have

|B\cap F_{v}|=d_{G_{B}}(v)

and

|F_{v}|=n-1
.
Thus

\left|d_{G_{B}}(v)-\rho(n-1)\right|\leq K\sqrt{(n-1)\log(2n)}\qquad(v\in[n]).

After increasing the constant
C=C(p,q)
in the statement, this gives

\left|d_{G_{B}}(v)-\rho(n-1)\right|\leq C\sqrt{n\log n}\qquad(v\in[n])

in the complete-round setting.

Similarly, for every ordered pair of disjoint
k
-sets
S,T
, Theorem
1.4

gives

\left||B\cap F_{S,T}|-\rho|F_{S,T}|\right|\leq\frac{L_{S,T}^{+}}{p+q}=\frac{\eta}{2}k^{2}.

Since

|B\cap F_{S,T}|=e_{G_{B}}(S,T)

and
|F_{S,T}|=k^{2}
,
we obtain

\left|e_{G_{B}}(S,T)-\rho k^{2}\right|\leq\frac{\eta}{2}k^{2}

for all disjoint
k
-sets
S,T
.

It remains to discuss the possible last incomplete round. If the board size is not
divisible by
p+q
, or if the rules force a final incomplete round, then at most

p+q-1
final claims are outside the complete-round analysis. Such claims can change
the degree of any vertex by at most
p+q-1
, and can change the value of

e_{G_{B}}(S,T)
for any fixed pair
S,T
by at most
p+q-1
. Hence the degree estimate
gets an additional
O_{p,q}(1)
term. For the bipartite estimates, by increasing

C
once more we may assume

p+q-1\leq\frac{\eta}{2}k^{2}
.
Indeed, since
k\geq\eta n
, we have

\eta k^{2}\geq\eta^{3}n^{2}\geq\eta^{3}n\geq C\log(e/\eta)
,
and the right hand side can be made larger than any fixed constant depending only on

p,q
. Therefore, in the original game, for all disjoint
k
-sets
S,T
,

\left|e_{G_{B}}(S,T)-\rho k^{2}\right|\leq\eta k^{2}
.

Finally we pass from
k
-sets to arbitrary larger sets. Let
S,T\subseteq[n]
be
disjoint sets with

|S|,|T|\geq\eta n
.
Since
k=\lceil\eta n\rceil
, we have
k\leq|S|
and
k\leq|T|
. Choose uniformly
random
k
-subsets

S^{\prime}\subseteq S
and

T^{\prime}\subseteq T
.
For every choice of
S^{\prime}
and
T^{\prime}
, the sets are disjoint, and hence the estimate just
proved gives

\left|e_{G_{B}}(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})-\rho k^{2}\right|\leq\eta k^{2}.

(3.9)

Now each edge between
S
and
T
is
chosen in
e_{G_{B}}(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})
with probability

\frac{k}{|S|}\cdot\frac{k}{|T|}=\frac{k^{2}}{|S||T|}.

Therefore

\mathbb{E}[\,e_{G_{B}}(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})]=\frac{k^{2}}{|S||T|}e_{G_{B}}(S,T).

Since (
3.9
)
holds for every choice of the
k
-subsets
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
and

T^{\prime}\subseteq T
, it also holds after averaging over all such choices. Hence

\left|\mathbb{E}[e_{G_{B}}(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})]-\rho k^{2}\right|\leq\eta k^{2}.

Using

\mathbb{E}[e_{G_{B}}(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})]=\frac{k^{2}}{|S||T|}e_{G_{B}}(S,T),

we obtain

\left|\frac{k^{2}}{|S||T|}e_{G_{B}}(S,T)-\rho k^{2}\right|\leq\eta k^{2}.

Dividing by
k^{2}
gives

\left|\frac{e_{G_{B}}(S,T)}{|S||T|}-\rho\right|\leq\eta.

Together with the degree estimate, this proves Theorem
1.5
.
∎
