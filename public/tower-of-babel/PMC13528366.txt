An imperative protection framework for China’s karst landscape ecosystems
Authors: Mingrong Liang, Shaokun Li, Ling Tang, Meng Zhu, Hong Yang
Published: 2026

China’s karst caves, concentrated mainly in the southwest, form part of one of the world’s largest karst systems that cover roughly one-third of the country’s land area [1,2]. They serve as archives of geological and paleoclimatic change and support groundwater storage, recharge, surface–subsurface water exchange, and biodiversity conservation [2,3].

Yet these fragile systems are increasingly threatened by solid waste dumping, sewage discharge, livestock farming, mining, unauthorized construction, illegal stalactite harvesting, and poorly regulated tourism [3,4]. Recent cases in Guizhou, Hunan, and Guangxi—including waste accumulation, manure discharge, mining-related fluoride and heavy-metal contamination, and severe local degradation—show that cave protection remains largely reactive, fragmented, and inconsistently enforced [[3], [4], [5]].

Climate extremes further heighten these risks by altering groundwater dynamics and increasing the likelihood of karst collapse and other geological hazards [6]. For example, in Laibin, Guangxi, following a year-long drought, an extreme rainstorm (maximum daily precipitation reaching 442 mm) rapidly recharged the karst conduit system, causing groundwater level fluctuations and trapped air pressure (“air blasting”), which led to 17 clustered sinkhole collapses over 1500 m2 [7].

China therefore needs a targeted karst cave protection framework that shifts from emergency clean-up to prevention, monitoring, accountability, and restoration. The first step should be a regional inventory and risk classification of caves in major karst provincial-level regions, including Guizhou, Guangxi, Yunnan, Hunan, and Chongqing. Each cave should be classified by hydrological connectivity, biodiversity value, pollution exposure, tourism pressure, collapse risk, and proximity to villages, farms, livestock facilities, mines, or waste-disposal sites.

Governance should be strengthened through karst-specific regional legislation and a legally mandated “Cave Chief” accountability system. Designated township- or village-level officials should oversee routine inspections, pollution-source control, prevention of illegal construction, stalactite protection, and emergency response. Entrance information boards should disclose responsible officers, protection categories, prohibited activities, inspection frequency, and public reporting channels. Performance should be assessed using measurable indicators, including inspection completion, waste removal, water-quality compliance, and response time.

Monitoring must also be practical. Priority sites should include underground river channels, runoff zones, recharge areas, tourist caves, and caves near farms, livestock facilities, mines, or settlements. In collapse-prone areas, rainfall, groundwater level, soil moisture, fissure expansion, and ground deformation should be fed into early-warning systems.

Finally, long-term protection requires social and financial mechanisms. Groundwater regulations should be integrated with village covenants and local ecological norms. Public-interest litigation should be simplified for severe pollution cases, representative cave clusters should be incorporated into the National Park System or equivalent protected-area frameworks, and a “geological cost” compensation mechanism should direct greater support to areas with higher fragility, stronger hydrological connectivity, and greater restoration difficulty [3].
