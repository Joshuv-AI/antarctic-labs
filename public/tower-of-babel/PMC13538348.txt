Evaluation and analysis of the impact of factors affecting urban flooding using SCS-TR20 and SBUH hydrological models
Authors: Nahid Dehdehi, Mahdi Soleimani‑Motlagh, Ali Haghizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Land use change and intense rainfall, particularly in urban areas, are among the key factors contributing to flood occurrences. This study investigates the impact of land use and rainfall changes on flood events using the Soil Conservation Service - Technical Release 20 (SCS-TR20) and the Santa Barbara Urban Hydrograph method (SBUH) models within the HydroCAD software. A key novelty of this study is the comparative application of the SBUH model-never before used in Iran for rainfall-runoff simulation-alongside SCS-TR20, combined with a detailed multi-temporal land use change analysis over a near-decade period (2010–2019) in the Doab-Veysian watershed. For this analysis, characteristics of five sub-basins (Bahramjo, Karganeh, Chenar-Khoshkeh, Cham-Anjir, and Doab-Veysian) including drainage area, time of concentration, curve number (CN), and cross-sectional geometry of river reaches were defined in HydroCAD. A 24-hour Type II rainfall pattern was selected as the optimal regional rainfall model. The models were then calibrated based on two rainfall events (24/04/2010 and 21/10/2014) and validated using a third rainfall event (01/04/2019). The results demonstrated that both the SCS-TR20 and SBUH models are capable of simulating floods with R² and Nash-Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE) values exceeding 90%, though these metrics were slightly lower for the SBUH model compared to SCS-TR20. An analysis of land use changes between 2010 and 2019 revealed that the expansion of dry farming (from 53,000 to 66,000 hectares), the decline of poor and moderate rangelands (from 50,000 to 43,000 hectares), and the reduction of forests (from 116,000 to 108,000 hectares) were additional factors increasing flood risks. Moreover, residential, commercial, and infrastructural developments in urban and rural areas expanded significantly, with such lands growing from 2,744 hectares in 2010 to 6,897 hectares in 2019. These changes led to an increase in the curve number (CN) from 81.72 to 83.47 in the Doab-Veysian watershed and from 91 to 92 in the urban area of Khorramabad. Combined with an increase in rainfall (from 21 mm to 116 mm), these changes resulted in a substantial rise in peak flood discharge (from 106 to 2,227 m³/s) and flood volume (from 3.83 to 98.33 million m³) according to the SCS-TR20 model. The SBUH model also indicated an increase in discharge (from 77.79 to 1,687 m³/s). These findings highlight the significant influence of land use changes and heavy rainfall on flood occurrences. In urban areas, flood discharge estimated by the SCS-TR20 model increased from 11.97 m³/s in 2010 to 135.87 m³/s in 2019, while flood volume rose from 0.146 to 2.42 million m³ over the same period. Correspondingly, the average flood depth across the urban watershed increased from 6 mm to 66 mm. These figures underscore the substantial impact of land use changes and increased rainfall in exacerbating floods in urban areas. In conclusion, the findings indicate that although both models perform effectively, SCS-TR20 simulates peak discharge more accurately than SBUH. Furthermore, rainfall was introduced as the major and dominant factor in flood generation in the region, although the role of antecedent soil moisture (AMC) land use changes and the CN should not be overlooked.

Introduction

Urban and industrial development alters land use, reducing soil permeability and increasing impervious surfaces. This leads to greater runoff and higher peak flood discharge, exacerbating flood risk especially in urban areas when combined with heavy rainfall1–6. Hydrological modeling, which incorporates changes in both rainfall and land use, can enhance the accuracy of flood predictions. Numerous studies have investigated the combined effects of land use change and extreme rainfall on watershed hydrology. For example, Hernandez et al.,7 developed a method to evaluate the effects of land cover change and rainfall volume variability on watershed response by applying two hydrological models, KINEROS and SWAT, to a small catchment. Danquah,8 investigated climate change and its impact on flooding in Ghana using ArcGIS and HydroCAD software. The study concluded that developing flood prediction plans and assessing their effectiveness depends on land use characteristics and other climatic parameters. Toman and Hubacikova9 studied land use changes and their effects on runoff in the Pankava watershed for the years 1954, 1971, 1996, and 2009 using the HydroCAD model. Their analysis demonstrated that vegetation cover selection can significantly influence runoff.

Suribabu and Bhaskar,10 assessed the impacts of urban growth on surface runoff using the SCS-CN method and the Green-Ampt infiltration model to estimate runoff from continuous time-series rainfall data. They proposed a land use coefficient based on curve number (CN) to adjust infiltration rates according to the dominant land cover/use. Using remote sensing data from Indian satellites and GIS, they prepared spatial layers for different land covers. A 50-year return period rainfall event was used as input to the hydrological model to evaluate increased runoff due to land cover changes. Their study revealed that land cover/use change has a more pronounced effect on long-duration storms than short-duration storms of the same magnitude. Furthermore, runoff depth and coefficient were higher for short-duration rainfall compared to long-duration rainfall of equal intensity. Ahmad et al.,11 employed an integrated hydrological-hydraulic modeling approach combining the SWAT rainfall-runoff model with HydroCAD. Peak flood hydrographs were generated in HydroCAD by optimizing watershed parameters including CN, runoff volume, and peak flows across three catchments. Their results indicated that extensive deforestation scenarios reduced groundwater recharge by approximately 6.3%, while urbanization scenarios increased surface runoff by about 7.1% in recent decades. Anuthaman et al.,12 examined urban flood prediction modeling under climate change and land use/land cover (LULC) change scenarios in Chennai, India, using HEC-HMS and HEC-RAS. The results projected a 6% increase in built-up areas and a 74% rise in rainfall intensity by 2050 relative to 2015, accompanied by a 60.4% increase in flood depth.

Previous studies have primarily investigated the effects of land use changes and rainfall intensity on flooding and runoff using complex, parameter-heavy models such as HEC-HMS and SWAT. In contrast, the present study employs newer, simplified models with fewer parameters and an easier implementation process within the HydroCAD software environment, with a specific focus on urban development. Although a limited number of prior studies have utilized HydroCAD’s built-in models, most were applied to small, ungauged catchments. For instance, Soleimani-Motlagh and Davoodi13 estimated and compared peak discharge and flood volume in two small, ungauged watersheds using this approach.

HydroCAD is a well-established tool in water resources management and hydraulic engineering, offering precise modeling of hydrological processes14. Given the direct relationship between land use changes and hydrological alterations, studying these effects can contribute to improved water resource management, sustainable planning, and flood control. The software facilitates simulations and predictions by providing access to various rainfall distribution patterns and eliminates the need for hourly rainfall data. Notably, the SBUH model integrated into HydroCAD - a specialized urban flood hydrograph method - has not yet been applied in Iran for rainfall-runoff simulations.

Due to its user-friendly interface and faster results compared to similar hydrological modeling software, HydroCAD was selected to simulate peak flood discharge resulting from combined rainfall and land use changes in the Doab-Veysian watershed. The overarching objective of this study are to:

Identify the most effective model among HydroCAD’s suite for rainfall-runoff simulation,Quantify the percentage change in peak flood discharge and runoff volume attributable to land use changes under varying rainfall conditions.

Materials and methods

The study area is the Doab-Veysian watershed, which consists of five sub-watersheds located in Lorestan Province, with Khorramabad as the urban center (see Fig. 1). The region experiences an average rainfall of 546 mm. Investigations have shown that land use change, particularly urban development, causes alterations in peak discharge and flood volume. This factor, along with other parameters such as rainfall intensity and amount, as well as high topographic slopes, has led to the exacerbation of flood events. In recent years, land use in the Doab Veysian study area, especially in the urban region of Khorramabad, has changed under the influence of human activities, resulting in more severe flood events. The flood event of April 1, 2019, is an example of the effects of these changes, which caused extensive damage to the public. Furthermore, the geographical location of the Doab Veysian watershed, with its complex topography and steep slopes, has provided a suitable context for the intensification of this phenomenon. Therefore, this area has been selected for study due to the aforementioned reasons.

The data required for this research consists of daily rainfall data and maximum annual rainfall data from the following stations: Khorramabad, Kakareza, Sarab Seyed Ali, Sorkhab Kashwar, Tireh Dorood, and Afrineh. Additionally, flood data will be obtained from hydrometric stations, including Karganeh, Bahramjoo, Chenar Khoshkeh, Cham-Anjir, and Doab-Veysian. Furthermore, a set of base layers, including a Digital Elevation Model (DEM) map, geological map, soil texture map, and slope map, was employed. Also main waterway length, Landsat satellite images, land use maps, a Curve Number (CN) map, and geometric information of river sections at the hydrometric stations were utilized. Methodology

According to Fig. 2, the methodology consists of several sequential steps. First, land use maps for 2010, 2014, and 2019 were prepared using the SVM algorithm within Google Earth Engine. Next, the Curve Number (CN) was calculated for each sub-basin based on hydrological soil groups, land use types, Hydrologic Condition and Antecedent Moisture Condition. Subsequently, runoff and peak discharge for three rainfall events (2010, 2014, and 2019) were simulated using both the SCS-TR20 and SBUH hydrological models within HydroCAD software. Finally, the models were calibrated and validated using observed discharge data and statistical criteria, including R² and Nash-Sutcliffe efficiency.”

Rainfall zoning

To simulate the flood, three rainfall events were analyzed: on April 24, 2010; October 21, 2014; and April 1, 2019. The watershed rainfall values were zoned using the interpolation method that yielded the least error. Based on the mean square error evaluation, the ordinary Kriging method (error: 12.59 mm) and the IDW method (errors: 18.49 mm and 17.57 mm) were selected as the best interpolation methods for the respective dates mentioned above.

CN preparation

The Curve Number (CN) is a crucial parameter for estimating runoff. Higher CN values indicate more runoff, while lower values suggest greater infiltration. In this study, CN layers were created by combining data on land use, soil types (groups A-D), hydrological conditions, and antecedent moisture condition. The following is a detailed explanation of the parameters needed to prepare CN.

Soil hydrological group

The soil hydrological group is categorized into four groups: A, B, C, and D, based on soil texture, ranging from low runoff potential (Group A) to high runoff potential (Group D). In the studied watershed, clay loam texture predominates, covering more than 64% of the surface area. The next most prevalent texture is silty clay loam, which makes up about 25% of the watershed. Other soil textures present include silty loam, sandy clay, loam, sandy, sandy loam, sandy clay loam, and silty clay, although these represent smaller percentages of the area. Consequently, the studied watershed is primarily classified as being in hydrological group D.

Land use mapping using google earth engine

To generate land use maps, we analyzed Landsat 8 OLI (30 m resolution) atmospherically corrected images (LC08/C01/T1_SR) from 2013 to 2014 and 2017–2018, focusing on the Doab Visian watershed with < 10% cloud cover. Bands 2–7 were used, along with NDVI layers for seasonal periods. Forest and urban areas were classified using Global Land Cover Facility (GLCF) and Global Human Settlement Layer (GHSL) codes, respectively, while DEM/DSM data enhanced accuracy.

Layer stacking and training data (control/selected points) were processed using the LibSVM algorithm, which outperformed other methods. Post-classification adjustments yielded the final land use layer. For 2010, Landsat 7 data followed the same workflow. Accuracy was evaluated via overall accuracy and the kappa coefficient (Fig. 4).

Rainfall-runoff simulation

For the rainfall-runoff simulation, two methods were utilized within HydroCAD software: the SCS TR-20 method and the SBUH method. The scientific reason for using the method employed in this study is the application of simple models such as the Santa Barbara Urban Hydrograph (SBUH) and SCS-CN within the HydroCAD software environment. These models enable the simulation of land use change and rainfall effects on flooding and runoff. Unlike complex models such as HEC-HMS and HEC-RAS, they require less data, are fast to execute, user-friendly, and suitable for various watersheds, especially those lacking statistical data. Furthermore, the SBUH model has not yet been used in Iran for rainfall-runoff simulation. Below are explanations for each model:

SCS TR-20 Model

After defining the sub-watersheds and river segments as Nodes and Reaches in HydroCAD, the SCS TR-20 runoff generation method was selected in the Calculation Settings window. The runoff simulation using this method was executed following these steps: (a) a daily Type 2 rainfall distribution, which illustrates how the rainfall depth is distributed over time, was chosen. (b) The design rainfall depth was determined from rainfall maps across different events and incorporated into the model settings. SBUH Model

The hydrograph computation involves two key steps: (1) calculating the instantaneous hydrograph by applying the SCS Runoff Equation to determine rainfall excess at fixed time intervals, and (2) generating the runoff hydrograph by routing the instantaneous values through a conceptual reservoir with a delay equal to the watershed’s time of concentration. This method transforms rainfall excess into a time-distributed runoff response. To generate the runoff hydrograph using the SBU method, the same inputs and settings as the SCS-TR20 method are applied, with only the method selection changed to SBUH in the model interface. Flow routing

The Storage-Indication method routes flow through reaches by combining stage-discharge (Manning’s equation) and stage-storage relationships into a storage-indication curve. Routing proceeds stepwise: inflows update storage and outflow values via the curve, iterated across the hydrograph, with an optional refinement for long-reach travel time. Details of this method are available in Studies McCuen15 and Soleimain-Motlagh and Davoodi (2024).

Sensitivity analysis and calibration and validation

The study evaluated SCS-TR20 and SBUH models sensitivity by varying three key parameters: roughness coefficient, slope, and CN and AMC. By comparing resulting discharge changes against baseline values, the analysis identified which parameter exerted the greatest influence on simulated streamflow. The parameter producing the largest percentage change in discharge was determined to be the most sensitive.

The HydroCAD hydrological model was manually calibrated using a trial-and-error approach and verified with extreme rainfall events and corresponding flood data from watershed hydrometric stations. Model performance was then evaluated using statistical criteria, including the coefficient of determination (R²) and the Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE). This evaluation confirmed the model’s accuracy in simulating flood responses.

Results

This study investigates the effects of rainfall and urban development on urban flooding by modeling past flood events. It utilizes the SCS-TR20 and SBUH methods to estimate flood discharge and volume while identifying the optimal rainfall-runoff model using HydroCAD. The results first analyze the impact of land use and then provide flood estimates.

Land use changes analysis

Land use layers were created using Support Vector Machine (SVM) classification in Google Earth Engine. The accuracy of these layers was evaluated using a kappa coefficient greater than 71% and an overall accuracy exceeding 76%. Twelve distinct land use types were identified, including: built-up areas, dry farming, irrigated farming, gardens, poor rangeland, moderate rangeland, low forest, moderate forest, dense forest, woodland, water bodies, and roads. (Figures 5 and 6).

According to Table 7, the percentage of land use varies across different years. Build-up areas have been increased from 2,744 hectares in 2010, to 3,777 hectares in 2014, and finally to 6,897 hectares in 2018. Dry farming expanded from 53,000 in 2010 to 66,000 hectares in 2018. Orchard acreage has consistently grown throughout the study period, rising from 2,138 hectares in 2010 to 2,858 hectares in 2014 and then to 3,347 hectares in 2018. The area of pasture with low canopy cover changed significantly, going from 11.33% in 2010 to 7.96% in 2014, before dropping to 7.32% in 2018. On the other hand, the area categorized as pasture with medium canopy cover has remained relatively stable, starting at 8.92% of the total area at the beginning of the study, increasing in 2014 to about 10.77%, and then decreasing again in 2018 to 9.85%. Throughout the study period, the percentage low, moderate, and dense forests has shown a decreasing trend. The most significant change occurred in medium forests, which decreased from 50,790 hectares in 2010, to 48,425 hectares in 2018. Flood modelling and simulating

The study focused on configuring a hydraulic-hydrological model using the average rainfall for the watershed and a 24-hour Type 2 rainfall pattern. In addition, sub-watershed characteristics such as area, concentration time, and Curve Number (CN) were defined. The geometric data of the river sections was also included in the model. Flood hydrographs were simulated for three events using the SCS-TR20 and SBUH models. Two of these events (April 24, 2010, and October 21, 2014) were used for calibration, while the third event (April 04, 2019) was used for validation.

Sensitivity analysis results

The results of the sensitivity analysis were initially presented for three parameters: roughness coefficient, concentration time, and curve number (CN). It was found that changing the CN parameter had the most significant impact on the simulated discharge, indicating that it is the most sensitive parameter. While variations in all three parameters had a minimal effect on discharge in the upstream sub-watersheds, they significantly influenced the discharge in downstream sub-watersheds, such as Cham-anjir and Doab-Veysian (Fig. 8).

Furthermore, the results of the sensitivity analysis in Fig. 8(d) indicate that the Antecedent Moisture Condition (AMC) of the soil has a direct and significant effect on the peak flow discharge. As the AMC changes from dry (1) to wet (2 and 3) conditions, the peak flow discharge markedly increases.

Simulation of flood hydrograph

Table 10 presents the results of the statistical criteria during the calibration and validation periods for both the SCS-TR20 and SBUH models. Figures 9 and 10 illustrate the flood hydrographs at the outlets of various sub-watersheds and river reaches. The results indicate that the simulated discharge estimates, compared to the observed discharge (excluding base flow), achieve an R² value and Nash-Sutcliffe coefficient of over 99% during the calibration period. This demonstrates the high accuracy of the models employed. Additionally, in the validation period, the accuracy of the models in estimating simulated discharge (including base flow) also exceeds 99%. However, it is worth noting that the Nash-Sutcliffe coefficient for the SBUH model is slightly lower than that of the SCS-TR20 model.

The purpose of separating base flow from direct runoff was to more accurately evaluate model performance in simulating surface runoff, as the models used do not account for base flow. During the calibration phase, using hourly data and the WHAT-Baseflow software, base flow was separated from direct runoff. The results showed excellent agreement and high accuracy for both models after base flow removal. During the validation phase, due to the unavailability of hourly data, the total observed discharge (including base flow) was used. In this phase, both models still demonstrated high accuracy; however, the SBUH model exhibited a slightly lower Nash–Sutcliffe coefficient compared to the SCS-TR20 model.

It is worth noting that the simulated peak discharge at the sub-basin outlets was compared with the observed values. Probably, if the total width of the simulated hydrographs for each flood event were compared with the observed ones, the aforementioned statistical criteria (R2 and Nash-Sutcliffe coefficient) might have yielded a lower value than the high one reported. However, it should be noted that the optimal fitting of these models depends on changes in the AMC and CN parameters, which are sensitive, and even slight variations in these parameters especially CN produce the best fit. As Mishra and Singh (2004) demonstrated, the CN parameter in the SCS method is highly sensitive, and minor changes in it lead to major changes in the model output.

Another point worth noting is that using only a single flood event for validation, although it has limitations in terms of generalizability, is an accepted approach in hydrological studies when data from multiple independent events are not available. For example, the study by Han et al.,16, focusing on the extreme July 2021 flood in western Germany, specifically addressed the challenge of managing a single extreme flood event in hydrological model evaluation and demonstrated that including that same single very large flood event in the calibration stage is essential for the model to learn the correct behavior in simulating peak discharges; without it, the model fails to reproduce large floods. Furthermore, Dessers et al.,17 used data from a single flood event (the July 2021 floods in the Vesdre and Amblève catchments of Belgium) to calibrate and validate their hydrological models, emphasizing the importance of accurate rainfall data and confirming the local nature of the phenomenon. Accordingly, the selection of the 2019 flood event for the validation stage in this study was made because of its unprecedented peak discharge recorded across various sub-basins of the region; the main objective is to assess the model’s capability in simulating the most critical conditions (maximum discharges), and such an extreme event provides a suitable benchmark for evaluating model performance under extreme conditions.

Discussion and conclusions

To achieve the research objectives, we conducted modeling and simulation of historical flood events by testing various hypotheses. The study began with preparing land use maps using the Support Vector Machine (SVM) classification method in Google Earth Engine, incorporating training samples from different land cover categories and NDVI datasets through custom coding. Subsequently, Curve Number (CN) maps were generated for each sub-watershed, followed by calculations of concentration time, area, roughness coefficient, and hydraulic cross-section characteristics. These inputs were then used to simulate historical flood events through the SCS-TR20 and SBUH hydrological models. Finally, the impacts of land use changes and rainfall variability on flood occurrence were systematically evaluated. This chapter presents a comprehensive analysis and assessment of the research findings.

Evaluation and analysis of land use change results

Based on the findings, land use maps were generated using Landsat 7 and 8 imagery through Support Vector Machine (SVM) classification algorithm, achieving a Kappa coefficient above 71% and an overall accuracy exceeding 76% for the years 2010, 2014, and 2018. The mapping accuracy was deemed acceptable, showing reasonable consistency with the accuracy levels reported by other researchers such as Ghasemi Amin et al., (2018), who achieved a Kappa coefficient of 0.88 and an overall accuracy of 0.86. Furthermore, the high accuracy of land use mapping using the SVM algorithm compared to other classification methods has been confirmed by Mokhtari and Najafi18 and Abdoli Laktasaraei and Haghighi19.

Land use changes in the watershed have significantly altered land cover distribution, with built-up areas expanding from 1.1% (2010) to 2.67% (2018) due to urban and rural development. This 143% increase transformed 2,744 hectares into 6,897 hectares of developed land, elevating the curve number (CN) and reducing infiltration while increasing runoff and flood risk—especially in urban zones. Rainfed agriculture also expanded by 25% (53,000 to 66,000 ha), often replacing rangelands (which declined by 14% to 43,000 ha) and forests (which decreased by 7% to 108,000 ha). These shifts in land cover have exacerbated flooding and ecosystem degradation across the watershed.

Evaluation of CN maps

An analysis of the CN (Curve Number) maps for the years 2010, 2014, and 2018 revealed that, depending on land use and considering the soil hydrological group, land hydrological conditions, and antecedent moisture, the CN exhibited spatial variations ranging from 30 to 98. Since the soil in the watershed predominantly falls under hydrological group D, most land uses have a CN of around 80 or higher. Consequently, this factor has led to an average CN of 82 to 83 in the upstream sub-watersheds, such as Bahramjo and Korganeh, during the studied years. Similarly, in the Chenar- Khoshkeh and Doab-Veysian sub-watersheds, the average CN ranged between 83 and 84. In the Cham- Anjir sub-watershed, due to the presence of urban areas, this parameter varied between 85 and 85.5 during the examined years.

Notably, a question arises as to why, despite the increase in impervious surfaces due to urban development, the change in CN between 2018 and 2010 was minimal and insignificant. The reason is that, alongside urban construction, green spaces, trees, and planted forests have also expanded within the urban watershed, collectively influencing the average CN.

Efficiency of rainfall-runoff models in flood simulation

After calibrating the model, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to identify the most sensitive parameter with the greatest impact on simulated flood events. The results revealed that the CN (curve number) parameter had the most significant influence on peak flood discharge, particularly at the Cham-Anjir and Doab-Veysian stations. Incremental changes in CN at low percentages (up to 5%) did not substantially alter discharge values; however, when CN increased by more than 5%, the discharge rose exponentially. For instance, a 10% increase in CN resulted in a discharge difference of 386 m³/s at the Doab-Veysian station compared to a 2% increase. Previous studies, such as those by Soomro et al., (2019) and Wang & Bi (2020), also identified the curve number as the most effective and sensitive parameter in direct runoff simulation, aligning with the findings of this research. In contrast, other parameters, such as the time of concentration, showed negligible impact—even with variations ranging from 5% to 30%, the discharge at Doab-Veysian decreased by only about 11 m³/s. For the roughness coefficient, increasing its value from 0.016 to 0.07 reduced the discharge by approximately 17 m³/s, indicating higher sensitivity compared to the time of concentration but still lower than CN. Earlier research by Soleimani-Motlagh and Davoodi13 also demonstrated that the roughness coefficient is more sensitive than channel bed slope, further supporting these observations.

Calibration and validation results demonstrated that both the SCS-TR20 and SBUH models performed well in flood simulation, achieving R² and Nash-Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE) values above 90%. However, the SBUH model exhibited slightly lower performance across different simulation stages compared to the SCS-TR20 model. This finding aligns with previous studies, such as those by Sondarva et al.,20 and Sangin et al.,21, which highlight the high accuracy and reliability of the SCS-TR20 (or NRCS-CN) model in rainfall-runoff simulations.

While the current study applies established SCS-TR20 and SBUH models, the combined implementation within HydroCAD offers methodological innovations beyond routine software application. Unlike standalone applications, HydroCAD enables simultaneous hydrograph routing and multiple-event analysis with automatic propagation through the drainage network, as well CN calculations rarely available in traditional TR-20 implementations22. Comparative studies have shown that SCS-TR20 and HEC-HMS produce comparable peak flow estimates under identical inputs, but SCS-TR20 and SBUH requires less data and computational effort, making it more suitable for data-sparse urban watersheds23. Furthermore, recent research demonstrates that low-complexity models can achieve peak minimal flow errors while running significantly faster than fully 2D hydrodynamic models such as HEC-RAS, with the trade-off between complexity and speed being critical for operational flood forecasting24,25. Therefore, the SCS-TR20 and SBUH combination in HydroCAD is not claimed to be superior in all contexts, but rather represents an optimal balance of accuracy, transparency, and computational efficiency for urban flood assessment under limited data conditions-a contribution that advances hydrological science by rigorously evaluating where simpler models outperform complex ones.

Impact of land use change and rainfall on the watershed flooding

Analysis of land use changes from 2010 to 2019 revealed significant transformations in the watershed. Urban and rural construction areas expanded by over 150%, while rainfed agricultural lands increased by 13,000 hectares. Concurrently, the watershed lost approximately 7,000 hectares of poor-to-medium rangeland and 8,000 hectares of forested areas (including various density classes). These land use modifications substantially impacted the curve number (CN) parameter, a critical factor in flood control. Given the watershed’s predominantly Group D soils, most land uses exhibited CN values exceeding 80.

Pre-modeling assessments showed baseline CN values ranging from 82 to 83 in the Bahramjoo and Karganeh sub-watersheds to 83–85 in the Chenar-koshkhe, Doab-veysian, and Cham-Anjir sub-watersheds. Calibration modeling demonstrated a rise in weighted average CN from 81.72 in 2010 to 83.5 in 2019. While statistical comparison revealed no significant difference in average CN between different years, the paired t-test result (p = 0.07) approached significance, indicating CN’s notable influence on peak discharge and flood volume dynamics.

Rainfall emerged as the dominant flood driver, with statistical analysis confirming significant differences between 2010 and 2019 rainfall patterns. Comparative studies, including Mahmoodi et al.,26, support these findings, demonstrating how combined land use changes (2% CN increase) and extreme rainfall events (5.6 times higher in 2019) dramatically amplify flooding. The SCS-TR20 model simulations showed discharge increasing from 106.05 to 2,227 m³/s (with corresponding volumes rising from 3.83 to 98.33 million m³), while SBUH model results indicated an increase from 77.79 to 1,687 m³/s, clearly demonstrating the compound effects of these hydrological factors.

Overall, the analysis of the results indicated that the effect of rainfall on peak discharge is dominant compared to CN, especially at the Doab-Veysian hydrometric station located at the outlet of the basin. However, the role of land use changes and CN, albeit small in magnitude, should not be overlooked in the generation and intensification of runoff. Additionally, the results of the sensitivity analysis revealed that the antecedent moisture condition across its different states (wet, normal, and dry) has a significant effect on peak discharge. Furthermore, the influence of rainfall amount across different events (from 2010 to 2014 and 2019) is undeniable, such that the combination of amount of high rainfall with wet antecedent moisture leads to a very substantial increase in discharge. This is precisely where researchers have emphasized the role of antecedent soil moisture conditions in flood prediction27.

Evaluation of the effect of land use change and rainfall on urban flooding

The evaluation of the impact of urban land-use changes on urban flooding revealed that the urban area of Khorramabad increased from 2,355 hectares in 2010 to 3,666 hectares in 2019. This expansion in urban land use affected the curve number (CN), raising the average CN from 91 to 92. Additionally, the change in rainfall intensity—from 21 mm during the event on 24/04/2010 to 116 mm during the event on 01/04/2019—contributed to significant urban flooding. The modeling results using the SCS-TR20 model showed that, based on input data, the peak discharge increased from 11.97 m³/s in the 2010 event to 135.87 m³/s in the 2019 event. Meanwhile, the SBUH model predicted lower values compared to the SCS-TR20 model. These findings align with the results of Banjara et al.,28, whose study also discussed the impact of land-use changes in urban watersheds and their effect on flooding. Their research indicated that the studied watershed is rapidly urbanizing, with agricultural and forested areas being replaced by urban zones. Accordingly, it is projected that urban areas will expand from 11.6% in 2021 to 34.1% by 2050 and 44.2% by 2080. These changes are expected to result in a 5% and 6.8% increase in peak discharge and an 8% and 13.3% rise in runoff volume for 100-year storm events in 2050 and 2080, respectively.

In summary, this study successfully modeled historical flood events by analyzing land use changes and rainfall variability using SVM classification and hydrological models (SCS-TR20 and SBUH). Urban expansion and rainfall intensity were identified as key drivers of increased flooding, with CN being the most sensitive parameter. The SCS-TR20 model outperformed SBUH in accuracy, demonstrating the compounding effects of land use shifts and extreme weather on flood risk. These findings highlight the urgent need for sustainable watershed management to mitigate future flood impacts.
