Dialectical behavioral therapy-based skills training program for family members of suicide attempters: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Jose H. Marco-Salvador, Sandra Perez-Rodriguez, Laura Diaz-Sanahuja, Antonio Grau, Macarena Paredes-Mealla, Verónica Guillen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFamily members of individuals who have attempted suicide may experience feelings of guilt, depression, hopelessness, and burnout. Previous studies suggest that Psychoeducational Interventions (PI) for caregivers of people with suicide attempts improve their feelings of caregiver burden and that Dialectical Behavioral Therapy (DBT) helps to reduce caregivers’ burden, depression, and validating behaviors. However, no studies have analyzed whether DBT is more effective than PI for caregivers of people who have made suicide attempts.MethodsThe aim of the study was to analyze the differential efficacy of the DBT vs. PI. The study was a superiority trial with a two-arm randomized controlled design. The intention-to-treat principle was employed using mixed-effects models. In all, 108 caregivers of patients diagnosed with Suicide Behavior Disorder were randomized.ResultsDBT was equally effective as PI in alleviating caregivers’ burden. DBT was more effective than PI in increasing caregivers´ family empowerment and validation behaviors and reducing stress.ConclusionsThis study suggests that DBT could be an effective intervention for caregivers of people with suicide attempts.Trial registrationThe study was registered on December 15, 2021 in https://www.clinicaltrials.gov/ ID: NCT05157607.

Introduction

Death by suicide is a major mental health problem that directly affects more than 720,000 people each year [1]. Suicide rates have increased by 60% in the past 45 years. In fact, suicide was the third leading cause of death among 15 to 29-year-olds worldwide in 2021 [1]. These statistics indicate that suicide prevention is a complex challenge that should be approached from different angles. Various studies have suggested that previous suicide attempts are the main risk factor for suicide [2]. Therefore, it is important to develop prevention and/or intervention programs for people who have carried out previous suicide attempts.

Given the magnitude and severity of the problem and its consequences, the American Psychiatric Association included Suicidal Behavior Disorder (SBD) in Section III of the DSM-5 as a condition warranting further study. SBD is defined as the presence of a suicide attempt within the past 24 months, and it is intended to capture suicidal behavior as a clinically significant condition independent from other psychiatric diagnoses. In contrast, in other diagnoses with a high prevalence of suicide attempts, such as Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD), suicidal behavior is conceptualized as one component of a broader clinical syndrome. BPD is a personality disorder characterized by pervasive patterns of emotional dysregulation, interpersonal instability, and impulsivity, with suicidal behavior representing only one of its possible features.

Beyond these definitional differences, SBD and BPD also differ in their diagnostic scope, temporal stability, and clinical conceptualization. SBD is a condition that specifically involves discrete episodes of suicidal behavior, but without requiring a pervasive pattern of personality traits. It may be diagnosed in individuals with a wide range of psychiatric conditions, or even in the absence of another formal mental disorder. In contrast, BPD is a chronic and pervasive disorder in personality functioning, and it typically emerges in adolescence or early adulthood. It is characterized by long-standing patterns of cognition, affectivity, interpersonal functioning, and impulse control that persist across contexts. Furthermore, whereas SBD is primarily defined by behavioral criteria (i.e., a recent suicide attempt), BPD is defined by a constellation of symptoms, of which suicidality is only one possible manifestation and not a necessary criterion for diagnosis [3]. Additionally, the clinical course of BPD tends to involve enduring interpersonal and affective instability, whereas SBD may reflect acute risk states that can occur episodically and independently of a stable personality structure.

Some psychotherapies have been effective in reducing the frequency of suicide attempts. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) was found to reduce suicidal ideation and suicide attempts [4], and Dialectical Behavioral Therapy (DBT) [5] was able to decrease the frequency and intensity of suicide attempts and NSSI in patients with a high risk of suicide, as in cases of borderline personality disorder [6].

Suicide attempts impact both the individual who makes the attempt and their caregiver. In fact, repeated suicide attempts of a close relative represent one of the most challenging situations for caregivers. They create persistent stress that often overwhelms caregivers with feelings of guilt, irritability, tension, and burnout [7]. Over time, this situation can lead to depression and hopelessness [8]. Furthermore, caregivers do not always perceive suicide as a likely outcome, which may diminish their level of concern [9]. Caregivers of people with a mental diagnosis with a high risk of suicide, such as BPD, showed greater depressive and anxious symptomatology and worse quality of life compared to a sample of people without a relative with a personality disorder [10]. When these families are assessed, they indicate that their needs are not being met by the professionals who care for their close relatives. They stipulate that they are not adequately attended to by mental health services, and they would like to better understand the problem and the treatments received by patients who have attempted suicide [7]. More importantly, they feel the need to acquire the appropriate skills to effectively engage with and support their close relative who tried to commit suicide.

Fortunately, psychological interventions are available for caregivers of individuals with various mental disorders, such as schizophrenia [11], eating disorders [12], and borderline personality disorder [13]. These interventions provide caregivers with the necessary knowledge and skills to better support their relatives, which helps to reduce their stress, emotional burden, and depressive symptoms, in addition to improving family relationships.

Interventions aimed exclusively at caregivers of participants who have tried to take their own lives have mainly adopted two models, the DBT model [14] and the psychoeducational model [15]. Based on the psychoeducational model, to date, two controlled studies have been carried out with different samples. Pineda and Dadds [15] studied the effectiveness of the Resourceful Adolescent Parent Program, which consisted of four two-hour sessions (held once a week or once every two weeks) with caregivers of adolescents who had engaged in at least one episode of suicidal behavior (including suicidal ideation, intent, and deliberate self-inflicted injurious behavior). They found that the program improved family functioning and reduced psychiatric symptoms in adolescents with a high risk of suicide. Moreover, Sun et al. [16], with a sample of caregivers, compared the effectiveness of a psychoeducational intervention (PI) led by nurses versus a control group with no intervention. The authors found that a PI composed of four two-hour sessions plus free telephone consultations developed participants’ ability to take care of their relatives with suicidal attempts. However, the study had the following limitations: (a) The participants were caregivers of people who had engaged in suicidal ideation or attempts, but it was not clear that they had been diagnosed with SBD; (b) This treatment was not compared to active treatments (e.g. DBT or CBT); and (c) There was only one outcome measure, the Suicide Caring Ability Scale (SCAS), which was designed ad hoc for that study. Therefore, the authors recommend using reliable instruments to analyze changes in the intervention.

Based on the DBT model, Hoffman [17] developed an intervention program focused on caregivers of people with BPD. The aim of this psychological intervention is to reduce burden and stress, improve family relationships, increase validation strategies, and reduce invalidating behaviors. The program, called Family Connections (FC), is structured in six therapeutic components adapted from the original DBT skills training program [5]: psychoeducation, emotional awareness skills, interpersonal effectiveness, validation strategies, radical acceptance, and problem-solving skills. FC has been shown to reduce feelings of burden and discomfort, improve depression and grief, and increase coping skills in caregivers of patients with borderline personality disorder in four previous studies [17–20]. However, these studies were not conducted with relatives of people who had attempted suicide, and they were not randomized controlled trials (RCT) [21].

Despite the clinical differences between people diagnosed with SBD vs. BPD, there is a meaningful overlap between these two disorders, particularly regarding the presence of recurrent suicidal behavior, emotional dysregulation, and crisis-prone interpersonal dynamics [2, 3]. FC was originally developed for caregivers of individuals with BPD and chronic suicidality, and there is empirical support for its capacity to reduce burden and improve emotion regulation [4, 5]. Therefore, FC may be appropriate for caregivers of individuals with SBD, even in the absence of a BPD diagnosis.

At the same time, not all individuals with SBD meet the criteria for BPD, and their clinical presentations may differ in important ways (e.g., absence of pervasive personality pathology). This situation supports the need for DBT adaptations specifically tailored to the broader and more heterogeneous population captured by SBD. In the present study, the intervention was designed to address the needs of caregivers of individuals with suicidal behavior, regardless of the underlying diagnosis, focusing on core mechanisms such as emotional dysregulation, crisis management, and interpersonal effectiveness.

In one previous pilot study, FC was adapted for caregivers of individuals who had tried to commit suicide [14]. The intervention was delivered to 13 caregivers of patients with suicide attempts. FC was shortened to nine weekly group sessions. The results indicated that, after the intervention, caregivers reported less distress, anxiety, perceived criticism, critical comments, and emotional over-involvement. Although no significant improvements were observed in caregivers’ depression or quality of life, it is worth noting that these outcomes did not worsen either. Given the considerable psychological burden associated with supporting a suicidal relative, maintenance in these domains may also reflect a clinically meaningful outcome. Thus, the results were encouraging, but the main limitation was that there was no control group or randomization of the participants [14].

In sum, previous studies indicate that PI for caregivers of people with suicidal ideation or suicide attempts is effective in improving caregiver skills [15]. Other studies have shown that interventions based on DBT are efficacious in reducing burden, depression, anxiety, and validating behaviors in caregivers of people with BPD and/or suicide attempters. However, to date, no studies have compared these two types of interventions in samples of caregivers of patients diagnosed with SBD. Thus, in order to evaluate the effectiveness of interventions for caregivers of patients diagnosed with SBD, it is necessary to compare these two intervention programs. Specifically, the aim of the present RCT was to analyze the differential efficacy of the FC intervention and a PI in caregivers of patients diagnosed with SBD.

Method

Participants

Participants were caregivers of patients who met the criteria for SBD (DSM5-TR) [3]. The sample was recruited from several mental health services that specialized in patients diagnosed with this problem. To be included in the study, the caregivers had to: (1) take care of a patient with a diagnosis of SBD; and (2) sign the informed consent. Caregivers were excluded if they: (1) did not complete the pre-treatment evaluation; and/or (2) had received a diagnosis of psychosis, schizophrenia, substance dependence, or dementia.

Study design

This study was a superiority trial with a two-arm RCT design. There were two conditions, FC-SBD and PI, and family members were randomized to one of the two groups. If a patient had more than one family member attending the group, they were randomized together to be included in the same condition. The treatment outcomes were measured before starting the intervention and after it ended. The study followed the CONSORT statement [22] and the SPIRIT guidelines [23]. It was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Valencia, with reference number UV-INV_ETICA-1,623,849, and subsequently registered on December 15, 2021 in https://www.clinicaltrials.gov/ ID: NCT05157607.

Procedure

The researchers explained the study to clinical psychologists from several mental health services, and these clinical psychologists selected the relatives of their patients who met the inclusion criteria. Participants were recruited across 11 mental health services located in different cities throughout Spain. The SBD diagnostic status was established by a clinical psychologist specialized in clinical psychology at the referring specialized treatment center. The participating caregivers were primarily living with patients diagnosed with SBD, and all of them were family members who were non-professional caregivers. The researchers then explained the study’s aims and gave the relatives the opportunity to participate and complete their informed consent forms. For randomization purposes, an experimenter who was not a member of the research team assigned each family member to one of the two study conditions (FC-SBD or PI) using a random number software program. The assessment of the primary and secondary measures was carried out through an online survey before and after the intervention. The post-intervention assessment of the PI condition was collected four months after the pre-treatment assessment; therefore, the assessments in the FC condition and the PI condition were performed at the same time. All the patients related to the caregivers selected for the study were treated in mental health services specialized in suicidal behavior throughout the course of the study.

Both interventions (FC and PI) were performed via online videoconference by general health psychologists with a master’s degree. The psychotherapist’s adherence to the treatment protocol (FC) was controlled during the study. In the FC condition, there was a manual for the psychotherapist and another one for the patient. Each session offered theoretical contents and practical exercises that had to be covered. To control adherence to the original treatment manual, we held weekly clinical sessions with the team of psychotherapists to confirm that they had taught all the contents and performed all the exercises in the manual without adding any other psychotherapeutic components to the FC condition. If we detected that a specific topic or exercise had not been addressed, the psychotherapists were asked to teach it in the next session. We used a subjective scale created ad hoc to evaluate adherence to the contents, exercises, and homework tasks of the FC intervention. The therapist rated adherence to the psychotherapist’s treatment manual from 0 (bad) to 10 (very good). The adherence scores for the FC groups ranged between 8 (good) and 10 (very good). Adherence was controlled by the project directors.

Sample size

The sample size for this study was calculated based on effect sizes found in other studies [13] that showed medium to large effect sizes for family empowerment (d = 1.4) and burden (d = 0.45). Based on these results, we expected an effect size of 0.60. To calculate the sample size, we used statistical software, considering an α = 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.80 in a two-tailed t-test. We initially proposed a sample size of 90 participants (45 relatives per condition) to reach an effect size of 0.60 for burden.

Measures

Data on demographic variables: age, gender, educational level, income, marital status, and diagnosis of mental disorder.

Measures of primary outcomes

Burden assessment scale (BAS, [24]). This 19-item scale assesses two dimensions of caregiver burden due to a loved one’s illness (objective and subjective) in the past six months [25]. The items were rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1–4), where higher scores indicate higher levels of illness burden. In our sample, the reliability was adequate for the objective burden scale (α = 0.89) and the subjective burden scale (α = 0.85). Objective burden refers to the observable behavioral effects of care, including financial problems, disrupted activities (limitations placed on personal activities), household disturbances (alterations in family functioning), and social interactions (significant changes in work and social and family life). Subjective burden includes the feelings, attitudes, and emotions expressed about care experiences. We used the Spanish adaptation [26].

Registry of critical incidents with the patients diagnosed with SBD. This record was developed ad hoc for this study. The caregivers indicated the number of days on which the patient with SBD had engaged in suicide attempts and NSSI in the past three months. During the assessment interview, participants received an explanation of the difference between suicide attempts and NSSI, so that they could respond to this registry. A suicide attempt was defined as a self-destructive act deliberately carried out with a clear expectation of death. NSSI was defined as the deliberate, self-inflicted destruction of body tissue without suicidal intent and for purposes that are not socially sanctioned. It includes behaviors such as cutting, burning, biting, and scratching the skin. Although the study was conducted with caregivers of people who attempted suicide, it is well known that NSSI is common in these patients and a reliable predictor of suicide risk, and so we also recorded its frequency.

Measures of secondary outcomes

Family empowerment scale (FES, [27]). This scale has a total of 34 items that measure family empowerment in families with children who have emotional disabilities. It is composed of three subscales that refer to attitudes, knowledge, and behaviors related to effective family relationships, adaptation to the disorder, use of the social services system, and community participation. The items are rated on a Likert scale (1–5), where higher scores show greater feelings of empowerment. In our sample, the reliability of the scale was α = 0.92.

Depression, anxiety, and stress scale (DASS-21 [28]). We used the short, validated Spanish version [29], which is composed of 21 items that assess the frequency of depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms in the past week. The items were rated on a Likert scale (0–3); the higher the score, the higher the frequency of symptoms of depression, anxiety, and/or stress. In our sample, the reliability was measured for the Depression subscale (α = 0.91), the Anxiety subscale (α = 0.87), and the Stress subscale (α = 0.88).

Difficulties in emotion regulation scale - Spanish version (DERS [30]). This questionnaire is divided into five subscales. However, for our research we only utilized the following three subscales: Emotional loss of control, Emotional interference, and Emotional rejection. The items were rated on a Likert scale (1–5), with higher scores indicating greater difficulty in regulating emotions. Psychometric properties were excellent for all the subscales: Emotional loss of control (α = 0.93), Emotional interference (α = 0.92), and Emotional rejection (α = 0.91).

Quality of life index-Spanish version (QoLI, [31]). This scale consists of a 10-item index of perceived quality of life. It refers to physical and emotional wellbeing, functioning at work, satisfaction with personal relationships and self-independence, and perceived overall quality of life. The items were rated on a Likert scale (0–10), with higher scores indicating higher perceived quality of life. The psychometric properties were good in our sample (α = 0.92).

Validating and Invalidating Responses Scale (VIRS, [32]). This 16-item scale measures the validation and invalidation of family members’ responses to their loved ones. It is divided into two subscales (validation and invalidation), and the items are rated on a Likert scale (0–4), with higher scores indicating higher perceived validation or higher perceived invalidation (depending on the subscale). Both subscales showed adequate psychometric properties: Validation (α = 0.76) and Invalidation (α = 0.84).

Interventions

● Family Connections for caregivers of Patients with SBD.

The intervention lasted three months and included 12 weekly two-hour group sessions. The FC program [17] contained six therapeutic modules:

Module 1: Up-to-date information and research on suicide (epidemiology, frequency, risk factors, protective factors).

Module 2: Psychoeducation on the development of suicide, based on Joiner´s Interpersonal Theory of Suicidal Behavior [33], and information about evidence-based treatments and comorbidity. Joiner’s theory is the theoretical framework used to understand the factors that lead to and maintain suicide attempts. The three main components of Joiner’s theory are: (a) the feeling of being a burden to others (perceived burden); (b) a lack of connection to a social or family group (threatened belonging); and (c) the identification of suicidal behaviors (acquired capacity for suicide). Within this theoretical framework, participants are taught the importance of not assigning blame and learning to create an environment of mutual support and respect in order to reduce perceived burden and frustrated belonging.

Module 3: Emotion regulation skills, acceptance, validation, approach and awareness skills, and strategies to decrease emotional reactivity.

Module 4: Skills to improve the quality of relationships in family interactions (letting go of guilt and anger, acceptance skills in relationships).

Module 5: Communication skills and effective self-expression.

Module 6: Problem management and making safe plans for crisis management.

● Psychoeducational Intervention.

As Table 1 shows, this intervention consisted of one three-hour group session with the following aims: (1) presentation of individual caregivers, with a depiction of their relationship with the patients diagnosed with SBD and a description of the last suicide attempt; (2) emotional expression amongst the caregivers; and (3) a psychoeducation intervention based on updated information and research on suicide: epidemiology, risk factors, protective factors, comorbidity, explanatory theories about suicide, and evidence-based treatments for suicide; and (4) psychoeducation on the development of suicide based on the Interpersonal Theory of Suicidal Behavior [33].

Data analysis

First, participants’ pre-intervention scores in the two conditions were compared using ANOVAs for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. For the outcome measures at post-treatment, the intention-to-treat principle was employed when analyzing pre- and post-treatment data, using mixed-effects models with full information and maximum likelihood estimation. This method has been recommended due to its flexibility in handling missing data [34]. The proportion of missing data was low and occurred primarily in the post-treatment assessment. Given the use of mixed-effects modeling with full information maximum likelihood estimation, all the available observations contributed to parameter estimation, and no additional handling of missing data (e.g., imputation) was considered necessary. For all the outcomes, Time was treated as a within-group factor and Treatment Condition as a between-group factor. The fixed-effects factor was the Treatment Condition (FC vs. PI), and the random-effects factor was Family Unity. Significant effects were followed up with pairwise comparisons (adjusted by Bonferroni correction). Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated [35, 36]. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0 for Windows.

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants

The ages of the caregivers ranged between 20 and 68 years, with a mean age of 50.16 (SD = 8.94). In terms of gender, 78% (n = 71) were women, and 22% (n = 20) were men. Regarding their marital status, 64.8% (n = 59) were married or had a partner, 22% (n = 20) were separated or divorced, and 13.2% (n = 12) were single. In terms of their educational level, 49.5% (n = 45) had higher studies, 37.4% (n = 34) had secondary studies, and 13.3% (n = 12) had primary studies. Regarding their annual income: 35.2% (n = 32) earned less than 20,000 euros, 22% (n = 20) earned between 20,000 and 29,999 euros, 14.3% (n = 12) earned between 30,000 and 39,999 euros, 11% (n = 10) earned between 40,000 and 49,999 euros, and 17.6% (n = 16) had incomes of more than 50,000 euros. Additionally, 62.6% (n = 57) were currently working, whereas 47.3% (n = 34) were not. The relationship with the patient was: mother, 58.2% (n = 53), father, 13.2% (n = 12), brother or sister, 7.7% (n = 7), child, 13.2% (n = 12), partner, 3.3% (n = 3), and other, 4.4% (n = 4). Thus, the participating caregivers were primarily living with patients diagnosed with SBD, and all of them were non-professional (i.e., family) caregivers.

Regarding the clinical characteristics of the caregiver sample, 30.8% (n = 28) met the criteria for a mental disorder according to the DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). Specifically, 15 participants were diagnosed with an anxiety disorder, 10 with a depressive disorder, and three with an eating disorder. The remaining 69.2% (n = 63) did not meet the diagnostic criteria for any mental disorder.

Participant flow and attrition

To recruit the sample, 229 caregivers of patients diagnosed with SBD were initially contacted, and only those who met the inclusion criteria were selected. Thus, n = 108 caregivers (from 89 families) signed informed consent forms and were then assessed and subsequently randomized. However, n = 91 caregivers (from 79 families) began the treatment. Thus, 17 participants did not begin the intervention for several different reasons: n = 6 due to schedule incompatibility, n = 3 because they did not wish to participate in group psychotherapy, n = 5 because they did not complete the baseline assessment, and n = 3 due to difficulties with videoconferencing. Finally, 52 caregivers participated in the FC condition, and 39 participated in the PI condition. At posttreatment, n = 75 (82.41%) participants who began the intervention remained in the study, n = 42 (88.13%) in the FC condition and n = 33 (79.59%) in the PI condition. Primary outcomes

On the subjective burden scale (BAS), in the post hoc analyses, all the participants improved at posttreatment (F = 26.75, p = .001); however, there was no interaction effect (time x experimental condition) (F = 2.51, p = .12). Table 3 shows that both the participants in the FC condition (F = 26.04, p = .001) and those in the PI condition (F = 5.75, p = .02) obtained statistically significant improvements in subjective burden (BAS). However, the effect size was higher in the FC condition (Cohen’s d = 0.58) than in the PI condition (Cohen’s d = 0.27). Likewise, on the objective burden scale (BAS), although all the participants improved at posttreatment (F = 25.66, p = .001), there was no interaction effect (time x experimental condition) (F = 0.71, p = .79). Moreover, Table 3 shows that both the participants in the FC condition (F = 13.20, p = .001) and those in the PI condition obtained statistically significant improvements in objective burden (BAS) (F = 12.5; p = .001), with similar effect sizes: FC condition (Cohen’s d = 0.41), PI condition (Cohen’s d = 0.40).

Regarding the frequency of NSSI, the participants in the FC condition obtained statistically significant improvements at posttreatment (F = 4.21, p = .04), whereas the participants in the PI condition did not obtain a statistically significant reduction in NSSI (F = 0.78, p = .37); however, there was no interaction effect (time x experimental condition) (F = 0.38, p = .53). The effect size was higher in the FC condition (Cohen´s d = 0.26) than in the PI condition (Cohen´s d = 0.10). Finally, on the frequency of suicide attempts at posttreatment, all the participants improved (F = 5.24, p = .02), but there was no interaction effect (time x experimental condition) (F = 0.10, p = .92). Table 3 shows that neither the participants in the FC condition (F = 3.48, p = .06) nor those in the PI condition obtained statistically significant reductions in the frequency of suicide attempts (F = 2.03; p = .16). The effect size was greater in the FC condition (Cohen’s d = 0.23) than in the PI condition (Cohen’s d = 0.16).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to carry out an RCT to analyze the differential efficacy of the FC intervention, compared to a PI, in caregivers of patients diagnosed with SBD. On the primary outcomes, our results suggest that FC was as effective as the PI in reducing both objective and subjective caregiver burden in caregivers of individuals with SBD. The findings suggest that both interventions were effective in reducing objective and subjective caregiver burden. These results indicate that caregivers’ objective and subjective burden can be reduced by giving them information about the characteristics of patients diagnosed with SBD, the risk factors for suicide, how suicide attempts affect caregivers, and evidence-based treatments for suicide. It is important to highlight this encouraging finding, which shows that a three-hour session can significantly reduce caregivers’ burden. This result supports previous studies that found PI to be effective in improving family functioning and reducing psychiatric symptoms [15] and strengthening caregivers’ ability to take care of their relatives [16].

We wish to clarify that, although the study was conducted with caregivers of people who had attempted suicide, it is widely accepted in the specialized literature that the presence of NSSI is common in patients who attempt suicide and, therefore, a reliable predictor of suicidal risk [33, 37]. For this reason, we also recorded the frequency of NSSI, in addition to the frequency of suicide attempts. Another relevant finding was that, although our findings suggest that FC was not more effective than PI in reducing the frequency of NSSI, when caregivers of individuals diagnosed with SBD participated in the FC intervention, the patients under their care exhibited a reduction in the frequency of NSSI. These results suggest that patients’ NSSI may be reduced when caregivers develop validation, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness skills [17]. A growing body of research supports the association between family relational processes and NSSI. The Interpersonal Theory of Suicide [38] proposes that perceived burdensomeness and thwarted belongingness—constructs often shaped by family interactions—play a central role in the emergence of suicidal ideation and behavior [39]. Empirical studies further indicate that family environments characterized by high levels of conflict, criticism, and emotional invalidation are associated with an increased risk of NSSI [40]. Conversely, supportive and validating family relationships appear to function as protective factors [41]. Changes in caregiver behaviors—particularly improvements in emotion regulation, validation, and communication—may reduce interpersonal stress and maladaptive reinforcement patterns that contribute to the maintenance of NSSI. Accordingly, interventions targeting family dynamics are both theoretically and empirically justified as mechanisms for achieving reductions in NSSI. Our findings are quite encouraging and open the door to a future line of NSSI prevention through secondary prevention aimed at caregivers.

Regarding the secondary outcomes, our results showed that, at posttreatment, the FC intervention for caregivers of patients diagnosed with SBD was more effective than PI in increasing caregivers’ feelings of empowerment. This result is similar to findings from previous studies that applied FC to caregivers [17]. Changes in family members’ empowerment, caregiving skills, and use of community services may be due to a greater understanding of the SBD patient’s problem, as well as an increase in interpersonal effectiveness and problem solving, all of which are therapeutic goals that caregivers are taught during the FC intervention.

Moreover, FC was more effective than PI in increasing validation comments, attitudes, and behaviors when dealing with patients diagnosed with SBD. These findings were expected because producing increases in validating attitudes and behaviors is a fundamental objective of FC. Validation skills are introduced in Module 3 and trained in the following modules (4–6). Likewise, the FC intervention was more effective than PI in reducing caregivers’ stress symptoms. Similar results were found in previous studies on the efficacy of FC in caregivers of borderline personality disorder patients [21].

Although our findings suggest that FC was not more effective than PI in reducing depression, anxiety, emotional loss of control, emotional interference, emotional rejection, and invalidation, while increasing quality of life, a pattern of post-intervention reductions was nevertheless observed in the participants in the FC condition. In a previous study [19], FC was able to reduce discomfort and anxiety, but not depression. Given that our adaptation of FC consists of 12 sessions, three more than the adaptation by Rajalin [19], it may produce a greater change in caregivers’ depressive symptomatology. However, future studies should analyze whether increasing the number of sessions can specifically increase FC’s ability to reduce depression in caregivers.

FC reduced the emotional dysregulation of the caregivers. Specifically, it improved emotional loss of control and emotional interference in their lives, and they stopped rejecting and avoiding negative emotions. These results confirm those obtained by Rajalin [19], who showed a decrease in caregivers’ emotional over-involvement. Furthermore, the FC intervention increased the caregivers’ quality of life. We can highlight this result because caregivers of patients diagnosed with SBD show impaired wellbeing, lack of social and emotional support, economic hardship, and negative experiences that lead to low quality of life. In fact, in previous studies, interventions with caregivers did not produce an increase in quality of life [17, 19]. Therefore, finding an intervention that improves the quality of life of caregivers allows us to address this important need in families of patients diagnosed with SBD.

As mentioned above, the participants in the PI condition only improved their levels of burden. This result suggests that, in order to produce significant changes in stress, depression, and anxiety and improve emotion regulation, quality of life, and mastery and empowerment in caregivers of people with SBD, it is necessary to apply a structured multi-component intervention program based on evidence-based therapies such as DBT.

Our results suggest that it is important to implement this type of intervention program with family members to improve their quality of life. As family relationships improve, the sense of belonging increases, and frustrated belongingness, family violence, and NSSI decline, all of which are risk factors for suicidal behavior. Thus, the main practical application of our study addresses the need to implement this program for caregivers in mental health services (hospitals, emergency services, outpatient mental health services) where people with previous suicide attempts are treated, parallel to the treatment of patients.

This study, to date, is the first RCT to compare a DBT program for family members of patients diagnosed with SBD versus a PI. The inclusion criteria were broad so that more caregivers could be represented, and the sample was obtained in a natural setting. However, the study also has some limitations. The main shortcoming is that we were unable to carry out a follow-up, which keeps us from measuring the maintenance of the results in the follow-up. Another limitation is that the assessment of the frequency of NSSI or suicide attempts was conducted through the Registry of Critical Incidents, which was completed by caregivers. Therefore, some caregivers may not have been aware of these incidents, and the caregivers’ perceptions of severity may have differed greatly. Furthermore, the assessment of the frequency of NSSI and suicide attempts in the three months prior to the intervention cannot be considered a baseline, and so the results obtained through the Registry of Critical Incidents should be viewed with some caution. Moreover, the imbalance in the treatment doses—24 h in the FC intervention compared to a single three-hour session in the PI—limits the extent to which observed between-group differences can be attributed to DBT-specific components rather than nonspecific factors such as greater therapist contact, group support, and intervention intensity. An additional limitation of the study is that when more than one family member from the same household participated, they were randomized together into the same condition. The inclusion of multiple caregivers from the same family could introduce non-independence in the data and potentially influence the outcomes. To address this issue, the Family Unit was modeled as a random effect in the mixed-effects analyses, thereby accounting for the clustering of participants within families. Moreover, although 108 caregivers were randomized, the final sample size (n = 75) reduced the statistical power of the study and may have limited the ability to detect smaller but clinically meaningful effects. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Another limitation of our study is that we used the Family Empowerment Scale [27], which was designed to assess family members of children with disabilities in English-speaking countries. We used it with Spanish-speaking family members of people with SBD. We decided to use this scale because none of the items mention the concept of disability, age, or diagnosis. Thus, this scale is very general and assesses attitudes, knowledge, and behaviors related to effective family relationships, adaptation to the disorder, use of the social services system, and community participation. It also showed very good reliability. A future study will have to confirm the psychometric characteristics of the scale in a sample of Spanish-speaking family members of people with SBD. The study did not include a pre- and post-intervention assessment of caregivers’ knowledge, which limits the ability to evaluate the specific impact of the psychoeducational component on participants’ understanding of suicide-related issues. Additionally, the absence of systematic data about whether patients were experiencing a first suicide attempt or chronic suicidality is a limitation because the chronicity and recurrence of suicidal behavior can significantly influence caregiver burden and psychological adjustment. A further limitation is the lack of assessment of family dynamics or the quality of family relationships at the time of the suicidal crisis. Given that family functioning can play a significant role in the onset, course, and resolution of suicidal behavior, future research would benefit from including standardized measures of family functioning, both prior to and immediately following a suicidal crisis. Moreover, given that the primary objective of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of an intervention for caregivers, assessing patients’ diagnoses was beyond its scope; therefore, no data on patients’ psychiatric conditions were collected. This constitutes a limitation, given that patients’ clinical characteristics (e.g., type and severity of the disorder) may influence caregiver outcomes and act as moderators of treatment effects. The absence of these data limits the interpretability and generalizability of the findings. Future research should include detailed patient clinical information in order to examine these potential moderating effects and better tailor interventions. Another limitation of the study is that the SBD diagnostic status was not independently verified in the study setting; instead, diagnoses established at the referring clinical center were used. Although these diagnoses were made by qualified clinical professionals, the lack of independent diagnostic confirmation may introduce variability and limit the ability to fully verify diagnostic consistency. The results of this study are generalizable to family caregivers of individuals who have attempted suicide. This distinction is important because there may be differences between the needs of formal (workplace) caregivers and those of family caregivers of patients with serious mental disorders, as well as differences in their levels of anxiety, depression, and burnout. Moreover, the sample was heterogeneous in terms of employment status, with a large proportion of participants working full-time, whereas others were unemployed. This variability probably reflects differences in the time available for caregiving, which may have influenced the caregiving experience and should be taken into account when interpreting and generalizing the findings. Future studies should carry out an RCT that compares FC connections to an active control group (i.e., a structured and manualized intervention, such as CBT), with follow-ups at 12 months and a larger sample.

Conclusions

In conclusion, despite these limitations, this study advances the research on the efficacy of DBT, compared to a PI, in reducing stress and increasing mastery and empowerment and validating behaviors in caregivers of people diagnosed with SBD.
