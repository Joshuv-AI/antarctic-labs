Acupuncture for early Wallerian degeneration of bilateral brachium pontis: a case report

Background

Wallerian degeneration (WD) is a well-characterized axonal degeneration process triggered by nerve fiber injury, commonly occurring after cerebral infarction, trauma, or neurodegenerative diseases [1]. It typically progresses subacutely (3–4 weeks post-injury) and often involves the corticospinal tract, but involvement of the bilateral brachium pontis is exceptionally rare [2, 3]. Clinically, WD mimics recurrent cerebral infarction on imaging and exacerbates neurological deficits, leading to misdiagnosis and delayed intervention [4, 5]. Despite extensive research, no definitive pharmacological or surgical treatments exist for WD, highlighting the need for alternative therapeutic strategies.

Acupuncture, a core component of traditional Chinese medicine, has been widely used for post-stroke rehabilitation, with evidence supporting its efficacy in improving motor, speech, and swallowing functions [6–8]. Animal studies have shown that acupuncture upregulates neurotrophic factors and enhances cerebral blood flow, mechanisms potentially relevant to WD-related neural dysfunction [10]. However, no case reports or clinical trials have documented acupuncture for WD of the brachium pontis. Here, we report a case of early WD of the bilateral brachium pontis treated with acupuncture, with detailed diagnostic criteria, objective outcome measures, and long-term follow-up.

Case presentation

A 60-year-old Chinese female patient got acute pontine infarction (Fig. 1A–D), primarily characterized by left limb dysfunction for 19 days, treated with aspirin (100 mg/d) for antiplatelet therapy. She reported history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, without atrial fibrillation. She had no smoking or alcohol consumption history and was not taking any long-term medications. She was referred to our clinic because of suddenly developed dysarthria, dysphagia, and bilateral lower limb weakness at day 19. On examination, the patient was fully conscious, presenting with dysarthria, symmetrical frontal lines, normal pupils, symmetrical nasolabial sulcus, grade IV muscle strength in both lower limbs, a positive left Babinski sign, and ataxia of the lower limbs, with Barthel Index (BI) of 75, Modified Rankin Scale (MRS) of 2, and Water Swallowing Test (WST) of 4. The head MRI showed high signal in DWI, slightly high signal in T2WI, slightly low signal in T1WI, and low signal in ADC of bilateral brachium pontis (Fig. 2A–D). These findings were consistent with early WD of bilateral brachium pontis (axonal degeneration with cytotoxic edema). No new infarcts or contrast enhancement were observed, ruling out recurrent infarction. Due to the lack of specific conventional treatments for WD, acupuncture was performed based on traditional Chinese medicine theory by regulating meridians to restore qi and blood circulation in the brain [8] and preclinical evidence of acupuncture’s neuroprotective effect [10]. The patient was fully informed and prefer to complementary therapy. Acupoints of Fengchi (GB20) (bilateral), Shenting (GV24), Baihui (GV20), Lianquan (CV23), Quchi (LI11) (left), Hegu (LI4) (left), Xuehai (SP10) (bilateral), Liangqiu (ST34) (bilateral), Zusanli (ST36) (bilateral), Yanglingquan (GB34) (bilateral), Fenglong (ST40) (bilateral), Taichong (LR3) (bilateral) were eslected with disposable stainless steel filiform needles (0.30 mm × 40 mm, Huatuo). After skin disinfection with 75% ethanol, needles were inserted horizontally at the head points to a depth of 1–1.5 inches, and vertically at the remaining points to a depth of 1–1.5 inches. The needles were retained for 30 min after the arrival of Qi (twisting method, 180° rotation, 60 times/min until patient’s subjective sensation of soreness, numbness, or distension). The whole procedure was performed by a licensed acupuncturist with 7 years of clinical experience. Aspirin (100 mg/d) continued throughout the treatment period; no other rehabilitation therapy, medications, or complementary interventions were used. After 5-consecutive-day acupuncture treatment once daily, the patient's symptoms of dysarthria, dysphagia, and ataxia of the lower limbs disappeared, with bilateral lower limb strength improved to IV +, with BI of 95, MRS of 1, and WST of 1. Sustained recovery was confirmed at 6-month follow-up with no symptom recurrence (Table 1).

Discussion and conclusion

Wallerian degeneration, a secondary neuropathological change following cerebral infarction [1], can result in diminished muscle strength and heightened muscle tension in paralyzed limbs, subsequently exacerbating motor disorders and dysphasia. This condition closely resembles acute cerebral infarction on imaging [2, 3], which can easily lead to misdiagnosis as a recurrence of cerebral infarction. The MRI findings reflect the pathological process of axonal degeneration and cytotoxic edema, distinguishing it from acute infarction and demyelinating diseases. Wallerian degeneration within the central nervous system frequently affects the corticospinal tract, whereas involvement of the brachium pontis is exceedingly rare.

The rarity of Wallerian degeneration in the brachium pontis is highlighted in several studies that focus on similar degenerative processes in related structures. For instance, Wallerian degeneration of the brachium pontis following pontine infarction has been documented, emphasizing the challenges in diagnosis and the importance of recognizing specific imaging patterns to avoid misdiagnosis with other neurological conditions such as demyelinating diseases [4, 5]. These studies underscore the need for heightened awareness among clinicians regarding the neuroimaging characteristics and clinical manifestations of Wallerian degeneration in these regions.

MRI findings reveal diverse signal alterations across various sequences during different stages of Wallerian degeneration [9], often manifesting as high signals on T1, T2, FLAIR, and DWI sequences in bilateral brachium pontis. These must be meticulously distinguished from cerebral infarction by correlating with the disease history, clinical symptoms, and physical signs. The earlier Wallerian degeneration occurs, the poorer the patient’s prognosis [10]. The classic time course of WD 3–4 weeks post-infarction, but our patient developed symptoms 19 days after infarction—consistent with “early WD” as reported in a recent study [11].

This case involved a patient presenting with bilateral brachium pontis Wallerian degeneration, which emerged 19 days post-primary cerebral infarction onset. To date, no definitive treatment for Wallerian degeneration exists. As for acupuncture, there is currently no documented evidence or studies specifically addressing its use in treating Wallerian degeneration of the brachium pontis. Acupuncture is a traditional Chinese medicine technique that has been explored for various neurological conditions, but its application in the context of Wallerian degeneration, particularly in such a rare location as the brachium pontis, remains unexplored. Traditional Chinese medicine posits that WD-related neurological deficits result from “qi stagnation and blood stasis” in the brain meridians, and acupuncture at Fengchi (GB20), Baihui (GV20), and Zusanli (ST36) regulates qi and blood circulation to promote neural recovery. Preclinical studies have shown that acupuncture upregulates BDNF and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [12], which enhance axonal regeneration and cerebral blood flow. Clinically, acupuncture has been shown to improve motor and swallowing functions in post-stroke patients [6, 13], providing indirect evidence for its potential in WD-related deficits. In this case,’ the patient’s rapid symptom improvement and sustained recovery at 6-month follow-up suggest that acupuncture may be an effective approach to ameliorate WD-related neurological deficits. While the natural course of WD typically involves gradual axonal degeneration over months, our patient’s recovery was faster than expected, supporting a potential therapeutic effect of acupuncture. We hypothesize that acupuncture may act through two mechanisms: (1) modulating neurotrophic factors to inhibit axonal degeneration and promote functional recovery; and (2) improving cerebral blood flow to the injured brachium pontis, reducing edema and enhancing nutrient deliver. However, these hypotheses require validation in preclinical and clinical studies.

In conclusion, this case suggests that acupuncture may be a promising complementary therapy for alleviating neurological symptoms associated with early WD of the brachium pontis. However, this case report has several limitations. First, single-case design cannot establish causality—spontaneous recovery or placebo effect may have contributed to the outcome, although the rapid improvement and sustained recovery are inconsistent with the natural course of WD. Second, no control group to compare acupuncture with other interventions. Third, lack of mechanistic evidence to confirm the proposed mechanisms. Last, short-term follow-up cannot rule out long-term progression of WD.
