A feature-enhanced transformer for point-like micro-defect detection on semiconductor wafer surfaces
Authors: Zhen Wu, Hu Zhang, Tianren Ming, Xiaoan Hu, Fengkai Luan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Semiconductor wafers constitute a critical substrate material for modern information-technology products, and surface defects can directly compromise chip yield and device reliability. In practical industrial settings, wafer defects are typically minute in scale and are characterized by low-contrast detail attenuation, severe background noise, and dense spatial distributions, which collectively hinder the stable extraction of discriminative features by existing visual inspection methods. To this end, a deep feature–enhanced Transformer-based detector, termed Wafer-DETR, is proposed. Specifically, a frequency–spatial collaborative enhancement backbone, FresNet, is constructed, where FFT-based spectral modeling is integrated with edge enhancement to reinforce defect details, thereby alleviating the progressive attenuation of low-contrast cues as network depth increases. In addition, a cross-scale adaptive fusion module, CSAF, is designed to improve defect focusing under heavy-noise conditions via dynamic gating. During feature reconstruction, a content-aware upsampling operator, CARAFE, is incorporated to strengthen boundary representations and mitigate adhesion among densely distributed defects. Experiments on a self-built wafer defect dataset collected from a real industrial production line, under a typical point-like micro-defect scenario, show that the proposed method achieves 79.7% average precision at an IoU threshold of 0.50 and 25.2% average precision averaged over IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95, yielding absolute gains of 3.3 and 1.9 percentage points over RT-DETR, respectively. Overall, Wafer-DETR exhibits more stable detection performance in low-contrast, noisy, and densely defective scenarios, thereby providing effective technical support for automated visual quality inspection in wafer manufacturing.

Introduction

Semiconductor wafers serve as a key substrate material in integrated-circuit manufacturing1, and their surface quality directly affects the consistency of subsequent processes, including lithography, deposition, and etching, thereby determining chip yield and device reliability2,3. In practical industrial production, point-like micro-defects are prone to occur on wafer surfaces4. Such defects are typically associated with localized planarity anomalies or non-uniform material deposition and may induce fluctuations in electrical characteristics, leading to failure risks such as memory-cell short circuits and signal crosstalk5. With the continued refinement of fabrication processes and the sustained tightening of quality-inspection standards6, achieving high-precision and repeatable automated defect detection under practical production conditions has become a critical step toward strengthening quality assurance in wafer manufacturing.

In recent years, driven by continuous advances in machine vision, automated visual defect inspection has gradually emerged as a pivotal approach for industrial surface-defect detection, partially mitigating the inefficiency and subjectivity inherent to manual inspection7–10. Nevertheless, in real-world industrial inspection, wafer-surface defects are typically minute in scale and are often accompanied by extremely low contrast, severe background-noise interference, and dense spatial distributions11. These characteristics impede the accurate extraction of defect-discriminative information, thereby substantially increasing detection difficulty. In particular, the associated challenges can be summarized in the following three aspects.

On the one hand, wafer defects are often characterized by low contrast and weak texture responses, causing their fine-grained structures to attenuate readily during deep feature extraction12. As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), the defect appears in the original field of view merely as a tiny protrusion with only subtle grayscale variation, which is almost overwhelmed by the large-area, homogeneous wafer background. Even within the locally magnified region, the defect boundary remains blurred and lacks clear texture support, making it difficult to induce a salient gradient response. For conventional CNN-based generic backbones, such weak defect cues may still trigger limited local activations in shallow layers; however, as the network deepens, successive convolutions and downsampling operations tend to further smooth fine details and suppress high-frequency information. Consequently, defect signals become progressively assimilated into background textures, ultimately resulting in missed detections or reduced confidence scores13,14. Therefore, how to continuously preserve and reinforce the subtle defect details and edge information throughout deep feature learning remains a key factor that constrains further performance gains in wafer defect detection.

On the other hand, complex background textures and noise interference can substantially obscure defect responses, making it difficult for the model to establish a stable capability for defect-focused representation15,16. As shown in Fig. 1(b), the wafer surface exhibits pronounced texture undulations and illumination non-uniformity, where the background region contains large-area intensity variations and fine-grained textural patterns. These background structures can produce grayscale and gradient responses comparable to those of true defects, thereby generating abundant defect-like distractors. Under such severe interference, even end-to-end trained deep models must perform intricate discrimination between genuine defect cues and background pseudo-responses15,16. This issue becomes more prominent during cross-scale feature fusion, where information competition and semantic misalignment are exacerbated17. Shallow features retain richer local details but simultaneously introduce noisy activations, whereas deep features provide higher-level semantics yet may be driven by background textures, leading to attention drift. As a result, defect responses become unstable and are manifested as false positives or missed detections. Therefore, enabling adaptive focusing on defect regions while suppressing interference introduced by background textures during inter-layer feature interaction remains a key challenge for improving detection reliability in noisy industrial scenarios.

In addition, when defects are densely distributed, the inter-object spacing becomes extremely small, which can readily induce boundary ambiguity and target adhesion during feature reconstruction18,19. As illustrated in Fig. 1(c), multiple defects occur consecutively within a local region, with short inter-defect distances and overlapping edge textures, rendering the contour boundaries of individual defects difficult to separate clearly. Under such conditions, the fixed interpolation-based upsampling widely adopted in existing detection frameworks lacks content adaptivity19. As a result, reconstruction strategies cannot be adjusted according to local structural variations, and fine details are prone to being over-smoothed, causing multiple targets to be effectively merged at the feature level. This effect further leads to localization deviations and reduced detection rates. Therefore, maintaining boundary separability while restoring feature resolution, and simultaneously mitigating mutual interference among densely packed defects, remains essential for improving localization stability in dense-defect scenarios.

To address the above challenges, this study proposes Wafer-DETR, a deep feature-enhanced Transformer network for semiconductor wafer surface defect detection. The framework is designed to alleviate the difficulty of stably extracting discriminative cues of subtle defects under low-contrast, heavy-noise, and densely distributed conditions, and to improve detection robustness and localization accuracy in complex industrial scenarios. To tackle three representative issues, namely low-contrast detail attenuation, severe noise interference, and adhesion among densely distributed defects, a frequency–spatial collaborative enhancement backbone (FresNet), a cross-scale adaptive fusion module (CSAF), and a content-aware feature reassembly operator (CARAFE)19 are developed to strengthen defect representation and enhance model robustness.

Related work

Semiconductor wafer surface defect detection

With the increasing complexity of semiconductor manufacturing processes, deep learning–based visual inspection has gradually supplanted traditional hand-crafted feature extraction owing to its superior representational capacity, thereby becoming the dominant paradigm for wafer defect recognition6,20–22. Early studies primarily focused on image-level classification. For instance, Kyeong et al. employed convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to categorize wafer surface defect patterns and further enabled effective recognition of previously unseen defect categories through dedicated mechanisms23. Similarly, other studies constructed CNN-based models to cluster and analyze spatial defect patterns in wafer maps4.

To meet industrial demands for precise defect localization, object detection frameworks were subsequently introduced into this domain11,24. Prior work has demonstrated that transferring generic detectors such as Faster R-CNN, SSD, and YOLO, to surface defect inspection is effective; compared with traditional approaches reliant on hand-crafted features, these detectors have shown superior performance in complex backgrounds relative to conventional image-processing techniques25. Further related studies indicate that detection models for specific industrial defect scenarios usually require task-oriented designs that account for target scale, background interference, and defect morphology26,27. Nevertheless, when confronted with substantial scale variations and the prevalence of tiny defects on wafers, generic detectors often exhibit limited capability, which has motivated a series of task-specific enhancements. Ma et al. proposed CC-De-YOLO, which augments YOLOv7 by introducing Coordinate Attention into the backbone and employs CAR-EVC as a content-adaptive reconstruction operator during upsampling to strengthen multi-scale feature representation, thereby improving wafer surface defect detection performance28. In addition, Shu et al. developed an improved wafer defect detection algorithm, termed YOLO-BA, based on YOLOv10n, where Bottleneck Transformers (BoT) and Adaptive Kernel Convolution (AKConv) are incorporated into the backbone to suppress complex background interference and enhance feature extraction efficiency, leading to improved detection performance29.

Although these CNN-based enhancements have advanced wafer defect detection to some extent, their overall performance remains constrained by the inherently local receptive fields of convolutional operations and the progressive loss of fine details induced by stage-wise downsampling. On the one hand, in scenarios involving low contrast and weak textures, critical high-frequency details are prone to be over-smoothed during deep feature extraction, making defect-discriminative cues difficult to preserve stably. On the other hand, complex background textures and noise can induce abundant defect-like pseudo-responses, rendering cross-scale fusion vulnerable to feature competition and response instability. Moreover, for densely distributed defects, insufficient boundary modeling during feature reconstruction can still lead to target adhesion and localization deviations. Therefore, how to continuously retain subtle defect details throughout deep representation learning while improving detection stability under heavy interference and dense distributions remains an important direction for further research in wafer surface defect inspection.

Transformer-based detectors for wafer defect detection

Traditional object detectors, including the R-CNN and YOLO families, rely heavily on post-processing steps such as non-maximum suppression (NMS), which constrains both inference efficiency and the effective utilization of global features30,31. The introduction of DETR30 broke this impasse by reformulating detection as a set-prediction problem through self-attention, thereby enabling end-to-end detection. Nevertheless, the original DETR suffers from slow convergence and limited accuracy on small objects. To address these issues, Deformable DETR32 substantially reduces computational complexity via sparse attention; DINO33 and DN-DETR34 incorporate denoising training strategies to markedly improve training stability and convergence speed; and RT-DETR35 further balances speed and accuracy, making it more suitable for real-time deployment. In wafer inspection, MDCA-DETR represents an early attempt to introduce Transformer-based architectures to mini-LED wafer surface defect detection, where multi-channel deformable convolution is integrated to achieve notable performance gains36.

Despite the strong global modeling capability of Transformers, directly applying them to wafer micro-defect detection remains accompanied by notable adaptation challenges. Wafer defects typically exhibit extremely small scales and very low contrast, while background regions may contain structured textures and stochastic noise perturbations, causing defect responses to be easily overwhelmed by interference. In addition, during multi-level feature interaction, standard self-attention mechanisms have limited capability to preserve local high-frequency details, making it difficult for the boundary and texture features of tiny defects to be accumulated stably, thereby affecting detection robustness and localization accuracy. For wafer defect detection tasks under low-contrast, tiny-object, and locally dense-distribution conditions, the foundational framework must not only possess strong global modeling and cross-scale representation capabilities, but should also reduce, as much as possible, the influence of post-processing on the stability of detection results in dense-target scenarios. Based on the above task characteristics, this study chooses RT-DETR as the foundational framework and further enhances its capability to represent subtle defect details on this basis.

Methodology

To address the central bottleneck in wafer surface defect detection, namely the difficulty of accurately extracting defect-discriminative cues, this study proposes an end-to-end deep feature–enhanced Transformer framework, referred to as Wafer-DETR. During feature learning, the texture and boundary representations of low-contrast defects are jointly reinforced, while both the focusing capability in cross-scale feature interaction and the boundary separability in the reconstruction stage are further improved. As a result, the detection challenges arising from progressive detail attenuation, background-noise interference, and adhesion among densely distributed defects are alleviated. FresNet feature extraction network

Fully preserving and strengthening the discriminative features of subtle defects provides an effective means to alleviate the progressive attenuation of defect details under low-contrast conditions on wafer surfaces. To this end, a frequency–spatial collaborative feature-enhancement backbone, FresNet, is constructed to continuously reinforce defect texture and boundary responses throughout deep representation learning, as illustrated in Fig. 3. FresNet adopts FreEEBlock as the core building unit, within which two complementary branches are devised, namely frequency-domain modeling and spatial edge enhancement.

FresNet is built on a ResNet backbone, where the standard convolutional blocks in Stages 2–5 are replaced with the proposed FreEEBlock (Frequency–Spatial Edge Enhancement Block).

The feature sequence is then processed through four stages, where the last three stages output multi-scale features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{ C_{1} ,C_{2} ,C{}_{3}\}$$\end{document}, whose spatial resolutions are progressively reduced from H/8, W/8 to H/32, W/32.

FreEEBlock adopts an efficient Cross Stage Partial (CSP)37 architecture design, improving feature representation while maintaining computational efficiency. Specifically, the input feature is first channel-adjusted by a 1 × 1 convolution and then split into two parts: the shortcut branch directly preserves part of the original features to maintain basic representations, while the other part is fed into the FreEEneck unit for deep feature extraction. As the core computation unit, FreEEneck employs a dual-branch structure to simultaneously enhance global structural representation and local geometric details, enabling weak defect-related information to continuously accumulate and be reinforced in deep features.

The SFM Branch (Spectral Feature Modulation) in FreEEneck performs global structural modeling in the frequency domain (as visualized by SFM in Fig. 4). Cross-scale adaptive fusion

During the construction of the feature pyramid, deep features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{d}$$\end{document} usually possess stronger semantic discriminability, but suffer from low spatial resolution and insufficient geometric details. In contrast, shallow features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{s}$$\end{document} preserve richer textures and edge cues, yet are more vulnerable to background textures and noise perturbations. In wafer defect detection, defects often exhibit low contrast and weak textures, together with severe background interference, which makes cross-scale feature fusion prone to unstable responses and feature competition. Moreover, due to successive downsampling and receptive-field discrepancies, there exists pronounced semantic misalignment between shallow and deep features. If simple linear fusion is directly adopted, background-induced spurious responses may be amplified, or weak defect information may be further diluted during fusion.

To address these issues, we design CSAF, which introduces an Augmented Mixup Mechanism to achieve adaptive alignment and fusion of cross-scale features via pixel-wise dynamic gating. In doing so, the fused representation maintains semantic discriminability while preserving defect boundary and texture details, as illustrated in Fig. 5.

The outputs of the two paths are aggregated to form the context-guided feature:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{c} = F_{g} + F_{l}$$\end{document}

After obtaining the context feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{c}$$\end{document}, to handle feature competition and response instability under strong noise interference, CSAF introduces a Pixel-wise Gated Attention (PGA) module to dynamically reweight cross-scale features. As shown in Fig. 7, the base feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{0}$$\end{document} and the context feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{c}$$\end{document} are concatenated along the channel dimension, and a grouped convolution (Grouped Convolution) is employed to capture per-channel differences, generating a weight map:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{p} = Sigmoid\left( {{\mathrm{GroupConv}}\left( {{\mathrm{Concat}}\left( {F_{0} {\mathrm{,}}F_{c} } \right)} \right)} \right) \in \left[ {0{\mathrm{,}}1} \right]^{{C \times H \times W}}$$\end{document}

Fig. 7Schematic illustration of PGA.

Schematic illustration of PGA.

The weight map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{p}$$\end{document} is dynamically generated by concatenating the base feature and the contextual feature, followed by grouped convolution and the Sigmoid activation function, and is used to adaptively regulate the fusion ratio of shallow and deep features at different spatial locations. In defect-boundary regions or texture-change areas, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{p}$$\end{document} tends to assign higher weights to enhance shallow-detail representation; in flat-background regions or noise-dominated areas, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{p}$$\end{document} tends to reduce the shallow contribution, allowing the fused feature to rely more on deep semantics to suppress spurious responses. Finally, the fusion output of CSAF follows an Augmented Mixup form:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{{{\mathrm{out}}}} = F_{0} + A_{p} \odot X_{s} + \left( {1 - A_{p} } \right) \odot X_{d}$$\end{document}

With the above pixel-wise dynamic gating mechanism, CSAF enables more stable focusing on defect regions during cross-scale fusion and effectively reduces false detections caused by background textures and noise interference, such that the fused features simultaneously possess strong semantic discriminability and fine-grained geometric expressiveness.

CARAFE

In the feature reconstruction stage, defect features need to be gradually restored from a low-resolution semantic space to a high-resolution geometric space. However, conventional upsampling operators, such as nearest-neighbor and bilinear interpolation, typically reconstruct features using isotropic, fixed kernels and therefore lack the ability to adapt to local structural variations. In wafer defect detection, such content-agnostic reconstruction may cause two adverse effects: (i) the edges and weak texture details that low-contrast defects rely on can be further smoothed and weakened during upsampling; (ii) when defects are densely distributed, boundary responses between multiple targets tend to diffuse and overlap, exacerbating target adhesion and localization bias.

To better preserve subtle boundaries during reconstruction, CARAFE is introduced in the neck to replace fixed interpolation-based upsampling. As illustrated in Fig. 8, CARAFE follows a two-stage mechanism that consists of dynamic kernel prediction and content-aware reassembly, enabling the upsampling process to adapt to local semantics and structural variations. This design is more favorable for recovering the boundaries and geometric contours of densely distributed tiny defects.

Fig. 8Schematic illustration of the CARAFE mechanism.

Schematic illustration of the CARAFE mechanism.

Specifically, given a low-resolution input feature map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi \in \mathbb{R}^{{{\mathrm{C}} \times H \times W}}$$\end{document} and an upsampling factor σ = 2, CARAFE is mainly used for upsampling reconstruction between adjacent feature levels in the neck network, where adjacent feature levels usually satisfy the standard twofold spatial-resolution relationship. This setting is also consistent with the common multi-scale feature configurations in object detection tasks19,48. CARAFE first employs a kernel prediction branch to generate an exclusive reassembly kernel for each target pixel on the high-resolution feature grid. To reduce computational complexity and improve inference efficiency, this branch begins with a channel compressor. A 1 × 1 convolution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _{{cmpr}}$$\end{document} is used to compress the input channels to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{m}$$\end{document} (set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{m} = 64$$\end{document} in this work), producing the compressed feature:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _{{cmpr}} = \phi _{{cmpr}} (\chi ) \in \mathbb{R}^{{C_{m} \times H \times {\mathrm{W}}}}$$\end{document}

Next, a context encoder \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _{{enc}}$$\end{document} is applied to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi _{{cmpr}}$$\end{document} to extract spatial content information. A Pixel Shuffle operation PS(⋅) is then used to expand channels into spatial dimensions, yielding the unnormalized upsampling-kernel tensor:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_{{up}} = PS(\phi _{{enc}} (\chi _{{cmpr}} )) \in \mathbb{R}^{{\sigma H \times \sigma W \times k_{{up}}^{2} }}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{{up}}$$\end{document} denotes the reassembly kernel size, which is set to 5 in this work.

To ensure that the reassembly kernels satisfy an energy-conservation constraint (i.e., the local mean distribution is not altered), a kernel normalizer is applied. Specifically, Spatial Softmax is performed over each local kernel window in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_{{up}}$$\end{document} to obtain the normalized weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_{{l^{\prime}}}$$\end{document}, such that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sum\limits_{{i = 1}}^{n} {W_{{l^{\prime}}} (n) = 1}$$\end{document}.

On this basis, the content-aware reassembly branch uses the predicted dynamic kernels to precisely map semantic information onto the high-resolution grid. For the output feature map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^{\prime}_{{}} \in \mathbb{R}^{{{\mathrm{C}} \times \sigma H \times \sigma W}}$$\end{document} , consider an arbitrary target position \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l^{\prime} = (i^{\prime},j^{\prime})$$\end{document}. The module first maps it back to the input coordinate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l = ([i^{\prime}/\sigma ],[j^{\prime}/\sigma ])$$\end{document}, and extracts the local neighborhood centered at l, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N(l,k_{{up}} )$$\end{document}. As shown in the lower part of Fig. 7, the reassembly operation is performed by a weighted summation of the neighborhood features using the corresponding predicted kernel \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_{{l^{\prime}}}$$\end{document} .16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^{\prime}_{{l^{\prime}}} = \sum\limits_{{n \in N(l,k_{{up}} )}} {W_{{l^{\prime}}} (n)\cdot} \chi _{{(l + n)}}$$\end{document}

With this mechanism, CARAFE enables the upsampling process to adapt to local structures: in flat background regions, it tends to produce smoother reassembly weights to mitigate noise perturbations; while around defect boundaries and texture-change regions, it generates more discriminative reassembly responses, thereby improving boundary separability and localization stability in dense-defect scenarios. Combined with the detail-preserving capability of FresNet and the cross-scale focusing of CSAF, CARAFE further reduces the risk of boundary degradation during reconstruction, allowing the final output features to maintain more stable defect representations under complex industrial imaging conditions.

Results

Dataset and experiments settings

Datasets

The experiments were conducted on a wafer surface defect dataset collected from a real industrial production line. The acquisition target was unpatterned bare wafers prior to the patterning process, with monocrystalline silicon as the substrate material. The wafer defect data were acquired by an automated optical acquisition system on the production line, and the original image resolution was 1440 × 1024. The dataset covers multiple production batches and contains a total of 1,000 images and 4,584 annotated defect instances. The detection target is a single category of point-like micro-defects, whose spatial distribution includes both isolated defects and locally clustered defects. The dataset was divided into the training, validation, and test sets at a ratio of 8:1:1, and a cross-production-batch split strategy was adopted to reduce the potential risk of data leakage and to more objectively evaluate the model’s generalization capability under distribution shifts in real industrial production lines. During training, the input images were uniformly resized to 640 × 640. All annotations were completed by researchers who had received unified training and were further reviewed by professionals with experience in industrial yield control.

Experiments settings

As summarized in Table 1, all experiments are performed on a server equipped with an AMD Ryzen 7 9700X CPU and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU, running Ubuntu 22.04. The deep learning framework is built with Python 3.9.0, PyTorch 2.1, and CUDA 12.1. During training, three online data-augmentation strategies were adopted, namely Mosaic augmentation, random horizontal flipping, and color jitter; no rotation augmentation, noise simulation, or other additional augmentation strategies were used. During testing, test-time augmentation was not applied. The proposed method is built on an anchor-free detection framework, and no predefined anchor boxes were used during either training or inference.

This metric suite provides a thorough assessment from multiple perspectives such as classification accuracy, localization precision, and scale generalization, and is particularly suitable for fine-grained detection in small-object and densely distributed wafer-defect scenarios.

In addition, to quantify the practical contribution of each component to performance improvement, the ablation study adopts the mAP metrics commonly used in YOLO-style evaluation, namely mAP@50 and mAP@50:95, and further reports Precision and Recall as complementary measures to characterize the impact of module-level enhancements on classification discriminability and defect coverage. Since industrial inspection is highly sensitive to both missed detections and false alarms, the trade-off between Precision and Recall is particularly critical. Performance comparison

All methods are trained and tested with identical hardware conditions, training configurations, and data-processing pipelines to ensure fairness and reproducibility.

Fig 9 presents qualitative detection results of different methods on typical wafer samples. The selected images include real industrial imaging characteristics such as low-contrast weak defects, complex texture noise, and locally uneven illumination. Moreover, some regions contain densely distributed tiny defects, which places higher demands on a model’s ability to preserve fine details and resist interference. Overall, YOLO-series detectors exhibit varying degrees of missed detections in these scenarios. In particular, for low-contrast defects with weak edges, the predicted boxes are often missing or have low confidence, making it difficult to form stable and reliable discriminative responses. Although RT-DETR benefits from global modeling to some extent, its confidence distribution remains relatively conservative under strong background noise and periodic texture interference; its responses to fine-grained defect structures are insufficiently concentrated, and missed detections or low-confidence outputs can still be observed in some cases. In contrast, Wafer-DETR consistently detects tiny defect targets across all examples, with generally higher and more concentrated confidence scores and almost no missed detections. Meanwhile, in dense-defect regions, it maintains clearer boundary localization and target separability, demonstrating stronger stability and robustness. This behavior is closely related to the synergistic gains of FresNet, CSAF, and CARAFE: FresNet enhances the detail representation of low-contrast defects, CSAF improves defect-focused stability during cross-scale fusion, and CARAFE further strengthens boundary structures in the upsampling reconstruction stage, enabling reliable detection under complex noise and dense distributions.

Industrial Reliability Analysis. In addition to the COCO-style metrics, in order to further evaluate the practical industrial reliability of the models under strict decision conditions, this study additionally compares the Defect Recall (DR), Missed Detection Rate (MDR), and False Detection Rate (FDR) of different models. The results are presented in Table 4.

As shown in Table 4, Wafer-DETR achieves a DR of 82.58% and an MDR of 17.42%, corresponding to the highest recall and the lowest missed-detection rate among all compared models, respectively. This indicates that the proposed method is able to cover more true defects under the fixed decision criterion. Meanwhile, the FDR of Wafer-DETR is 19.1%. Although this value is not the lowest among all models, it remains acceptable to a certain extent in high-recall industrial preliminary screening scenarios while maintaining strong recall capability. For wafer surface quality inspection tasks, missed detection of true defects may allow abnormal samples to continue entering subsequent manufacturing processes or device application stages, thereby increasing rework costs, yield loss, and potential reliability risks. In contrast, false detection results can usually be further handled through subsequent review mechanisms, secondary screening rules, or threshold calibration. Therefore, overall, the proposed method achieves a more reasonable trade-off between defect coverage capability and false-detection control from the perspective of industrial reliability.

Statistical Stability and Noise Robustness Analysis. To verify the reliability of the results obtained by the proposed method, this study further supplemented a statistical stability analysis and a noise-robustness analysis. In the statistical stability analysis, the training and validation procedures were kept unchanged, and 30 repeated resampling runs on the fixed test set were conducted. Ablation experiments

To further investigate the underlying mechanism and individual contribution of each core component in Wafer-DETR, this section combines qualitative visualization with quantitative evaluation. Specifically, the physical roles of FresNet, CSAF, and CARAFE are analyzed from three perspectives: frequency-energy distribution in the spectrum, semantic attention regions in heatmaps, and spatial-detail reconstruction in feature maps. The necessity of integrating these modules is then validated through progressively enhanced ablation results. All ablation experiments in this section are conducted using the original RT-DETR as the baseline model, with the specific configuration including the standard ResNet-18 backbone, the original hybrid encoder, and the detection head. On this basis, the effectiveness of each core component is systematically validated by introducing them progressively.

Original image with defect ROI (left), spectrum of features from the baseline backbone (before, middle), and spectrum of features from FresNet (after, right). FresNet shows stronger high-frequency responses, helping preserve defect textures and boundaries.

As shown in Fig. 11, the backbone’s capability to preserve subtle defect details in deep representations is examined by taking features from defect regions as input and comparing the spectral distributions produced by the baseline backbone and FresNet. For the baseline backbone (middle column), energy is primarily concentrated in the low-frequency bands, whereas peripheral high-frequency components that encode edges, fine textures, and local structural variations exhibit substantial attenuation, manifested as dark-blue regions. This observation implies that conventional convolutional backbones introduce a pronounced smoothing effect through successive convolutions and downsampling, causing the discriminative high-frequency cues of subtle defects to progressively diminish during deep feature propagation. By contrast, the spectra of FresNet outputs (right column) display richer energy responses in peripheral regions, characterized by diffuse yellow and green patterns, indicating that high-frequency details are retained and reinforced more effectively through the combination of frequency-domain modeling and spatial detail compensation. As a result, the decay of low-contrast defect details is mitigated, boundary and texture responses become more separable, and stronger fine-grained evidence is provided for subsequent detection heads, thereby improving detection stability.

As shown in Fig. 12, we adopt Grad-CAM to visualize class-activation responses during feature fusion, in order to assess the model’s defect-focusing capability under complex background interference. For the baseline fusion results (middle column), high-response regions are relatively scattered and some activations fall onto non-defect background areas, indicating that under strong texture noise and spurious responses, conventional cross-scale fusion is prone to attention drift, which disrupts defect-related information during cross-layer interaction. This observation is consistent with the challenge of strong background noise and unstable defect responses described in the introduction, and also reflects potential Semantic Misalignment during multi-level fusion. After incorporating CSAF (right column), the heatmap responses become noticeably more concentrated: highly activated regions more consistently focus on the defect body. This suggests that the PGA gating mechanism inside CSAF can dynamically adjust the contribution weights of multi-scale features at the pixel level, thereby strengthening defect-related responses while suppressing background spurious activations, and improving alignment and focusing stability during cross-scale fusion.

As shown in Fig. 13, we compare feature responses after bilinear interpolation and CARAFE upsampling to analyze how the reconstruction stage affects boundary representation for dense defects. With bilinear interpolation (middle column), the reconstruction lacks content adaptivity, leading to smoothing and blurring at fine-detail levels and thus indistinct defect boundaries. In particular, for the dense-defect case (second row, Dense), adjacent tiny defects exhibit clear response coupling and Feature Adhesion, where local responses of multiple targets tend to merge, reducing separability and increasing the risk of localization bias. In contrast, with CARAFE (right column), responses are more concentrated and boundary structures are clearer; in dense regions, the response locations of neighboring defects are better separated, mitigating adhesion. For the Normal example (third row), CARAFE also yields weaker irrelevant responses in background areas, suggesting a certain robustness to noise perturbations. Overall, CARAFE dynamically predicts content-aware reassembly kernels, enabling adaptive feature reassembly according to local structural differences, thereby enhancing boundary representation while restoring resolution and improving localization stability in dense-defect scenarios.

Building upon the above qualitative visual analysis, Table 7 further substantiates the specific contribution of each module to detection performance through a progressively structured set of quantitative results. The baseline model establishes the fundamental detection capability of the framework, achieving a mAP@50 of 77.2% and a Precision of 76.1%, indicating that basic defect localization can be attained using a generic backbone coupled with a Transformer detection head. However, the mAP@50:95 remains at 23.7%, revealing limited localization accuracy when dealing with the boundaries of tiny targets and reflecting the inherent constraint of the original architecture. Upon this foundation, the introduction of FresNet yields the most pronounced performance gain. The mAP@50 increases to 80.7% (an absolute improvement of 3.5 percentage points), with Precision rising concurrently to 80.7%. This substantial improvement corroborates the effectiveness of FresNet in strengthening fine-grained representations of subtle defects. Through frequency–spatial collaborative enhancement, defect-related high-frequency textures and edge cues are preserved more sufficiently in deep features, thereby improving defect discernibility and reducing the risk of missed detections. Subsequently, integrating CSAF further elevates mAP@50 to 81.0% and improves mAP@50:95 to 25.2%. This trend suggests that, during cross-scale feature interaction, the introduced dynamic gating mechanism enables more stable focusing on defect regions while suppressing spurious responses induced by complex background textures and noise, thereby alleviating feature dilution caused by semantic misalignment between shallow and deep representations. Finally, with the incorporation of CARAFE, the complete model (Wafer-DETR) achieves the best performance across all metrics. Notably, Precision reaches 82.2%, corresponding to an absolute gain of 6.1 percentage points over the baseline. This improvement indicates that, after FresNet and CSAF enhance detail retention and cross-scale fusion stability, respectively, CARAFE further strengthens boundary representation and target separability during the upsampling-based reconstruction stage via content-aware feature reassembly. As a result, false positives and adhesion phenomena in dense-defect scenarios are reduced, leading to improved overall detection reliability.

As shown in Fig. 15, the remaining limitations of the current model are mainly manifested as low-confidence responses to some subtle defects, which may further translate into the risk of missed detections after threshold filtering. Specifically, these phenomena are mainly concentrated in two situations. First, in locally dense-distribution scenarios, when weak defects appear together with neighboring defects of higher response, the model tends to prioritize the more salient targets while assigning relatively low confidence to adjacent weak targets, as shown in Fig. 15(a) and Fig. 15(d). Second, under imaging conditions with locally low or high brightness, the detection confidence of isolated defects declines markedly, as shown in Fig. 15(b) and Fig. 15(c). These observations indicate that locally dense distributions and abnormal local brightness variations still affect the response stability of the model to subtle defects.

Conclusion

To address the challenges encountered in subtle defect detection on semiconductor wafer surfaces under complex industrial imaging conditions, including low-contrast detail attenuation, severe background-noise interference, and adhesion among densely distributed defects, this study proposes a feature-enhanced Transformer framework for wafer surface defect detection, termed Wafer-DETR. To alleviate the weakening of subtle defect textures and boundary information during deep feature propagation, a frequency–spatial collaborative enhancement structure, FresNet, is constructed in the backbone to strengthen defect-related high-frequency textures and boundary representations. Meanwhile, a CSAF module is introduced during cross-scale feature interaction to improve the focusing capability on defect regions and the feature-alignment capability under complex backgrounds. In the feature reconstruction stage, the content-aware upsampling operator CARAFE is employed to enhance boundary separability and localization stability in dense-defect scenarios. Experimental results on a self-built industrial-grade wafer defect dataset demonstrate that Wafer-DETR achieves 79.7% \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{AP}}_{{50}}$$\end{document}, representing an absolute improvement of 3.3percentage points over the baseline model RT-DETR, and outperforms multiple mainstream detectors in overall performance. These results indicate that the proposed method can achieve more stable defect detection and localization under low-contrast, high-noise, and densely distributed defect scenarios, thereby providing effective technical support for automated visual inspection, yield control, and quality assurance of wafer surfaces.

Although the proposed method achieves favorable detection performance in the current task, it still has certain limitations. It should be noted that the experiments in this study are mainly based on typical point-like micro-defect scenarios from a real industrial production line; therefore, the relevant conclusions are currently more applicable to this type of task. For more diverse defect types, complex imaging conditions, and cross-fab scenarios, the generalization performance of the proposed method still requires further validation. In addition, the failure cases indicate that locally dense defects and abnormal brightness conditions may still lead to decreased detection confidence for some weak defects. Future work will further focus on multi-class defect detection, cross-fab generalization evaluation, illumination-aware enhancement, adaptive confidence calibration, and lightweight deployment optimization, so as to improve the applicability of the model in real industrial environments.
