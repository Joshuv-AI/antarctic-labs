The synergy and regional differences between the development of new urbanization and zero-carbon strategy: Evidence from China
Authors: Xin Wen, Jiaxin Wen, Zhibo Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrbanization serves as an engine for economic development, yet the risk of “carbon lock-in” caused by infrastructure and consumption patterns threatens long-term sustainability. Studying the synergistic effects and regional differences between China’s new urbanization (NU) and zero-carbon strategies (ZCS) is a crucial economic and social issue for China to participate in global climate governance and achieve urban modernization. This study systematically analyzed the evolution law and regional differences of the coupling coordination degree (CCD) between NU and TFCEE by employing the entropy-weighted TOPSIS method, coupling coordination model, Dagum-Gini coefficient, and convergence model. The findings are as follows: (1) The coupling coordination level (CCL) between NU and Total-factor carbon emission efficiency (TFCEE) has been increasing year by year, with the eastern region taking the lead in forming intermediate coordination and maintaining a leading position; (2) In terms of intra-regional differences, the CCL in the eastern, central, and northeastern regions exhibit a fluctuating decline, whereas those in the western region demonstrate a fluctuating increase. In terms of inter-regional differences, the disparity between the eastern and western regions (east-west) stands out as the most prominent. Here, inter-regional variations play a primary role in accounting for the overall inequalities; (3) In the σ-convergence and β-convergence analyses, the spatial imbalance in the western region is more pronounced, indicating that although the western region has shown a positive catch-up effect, the absolute gap with the eastern region has not yet narrowed due to differences in initial conditions. In general, the CCD between NU and TFCEE is gradually optimizing, but the spatial imbalance remains prominent, especially in the western region, which urgently needs to be improved through targeted policy guidance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13021-026-00478-6.Keywords: Total-factor carbon emission efficiency, New urbanization, Coupling coordination, Regional differences

Introduction

The spatial imbalance of carbon emissions during the urbanization process is a common problem faced by all countries at a specific stage of development [1, 2]. Developed countries also encountered similar challenges during the mature stage of urbanization [3]. The United Kingdom long experienced spatial imbalances in carbon emissions between London and its surrounding areas, a problem that was later effectively mitigated through the establishment of regional low-carbon funds, narrowing the emission gap between core cities and peripheral regions. In Germany, against the backdrop of uneven carbon emission levels between the Ruhr industrial zone and the southern regions, the government systematically promoted the construction of zero-carbon industrial zones under the Regional Structural Transformation Act, thereby progressively eliminating spatial disparities in carbon emissions during urbanization.

In China, cities serve as the primary carriers of energy consumption, approximately 75% of energy consumption is concentrated in urban areas, generating substantial carbon emissions. For a long time, China’s urbanization has been mainly driven by scale expansion, with a focus on promoting rapid economic growth and encouraging population concentration in cities. At a certain stage, this development model led to a reliance on a “high pollution, high energy consumption, low output” approach, resulting in phenomena such as a relatively concentrated energy demand and high single-factor energy consumption levels [4]. When the scale expands to a certain extent, concomitant challenges emerge in the domains of coordinated regional development and sustainable development. Thus, narrowing regional carbon emission gaps through institutional design and policy intervention is practically feasible. The “14th Five-Year Plan Implementation Plan for New Urbanization” issued by The State Council clearly states that “carbon peaking and carbon neutrality should be incorporated into the overall layout of urbanization development”. However, there is uncertainty as to whether the implementation of China’s new urbanization (NU) strategy can be in line with the zero-carbon strategies (ZCS). On the one hand, China’s NU may improve energy utilization efficiency, promote economic development, and reduce carbon emission intensity through technological progress and improve spatial utilization efficiency [5, 6]. On the other hand, it may exacerbate carbon emissions due to industrialization, urbanization [7], and alterations in consumption patterns [8], potentially resulting in heightened carbon emissions and intensifying the risk of “carbon lock-in,” thereby threatening long-term sustainability. Nationally, significant regional disparities exist in urbanization and carbon emission control among China’s provinces and cities, evident in resource endowment, economic scale, and industrial structure [9, 10]. China is resolutely committed to its “dual carbon” objectives under the global zero-carbon plan, and enhancing total-factor carbon emission efficiency (TFCEE) is a crucial method for balancing economic growth with carbon reduction. Carbon neutrality is a strategic cornerstone of high-quality development and of global climate governance. As China’s NU transitions to a phase of scaling up for quality enhancement, fostering the synchronized advancement of NU and TFCEE has emerged as a significant concern in China’s economic and social modernization.

In recent years, substantial multidisciplinary research has emerged regarding the relationship between NU and zero-carbon policies. The initial section examines the unidirectional influence of NU on carbon emission efficiency, with research revealing varying impacts, including facilitation or non-linear associations. For instance, studies have pointed out that urban morphological attributes exert different impacts on carbon emissions. Specifically, the expansion of urban spatial structure leads to increased carbon emissions, while the compactness of urban structure curbs carbon emissions [11]. Other scholars have found that urbanization has a negligible impact on carbon dioxide emissions [12], however, NU exerts significant spatial spillover effects and nonlinear influence effects on TFCEE [13]. Theoretically, the connotation and logical framework of NU establish the conceptual underpinnings of green and low-carbon urbanization [14]. In empirical research, certain academics have utilized spatial econometric models to elucidate the geographical spillover effects of NU in western regions, highlighting the pivotal significance of TFCEE in the high-quality advancement of NU [15, 16]. The second research cluster focuses on the bidirectional impacts between NU and green development, with studies unraveling the underlying mechanisms [17, 18]. These studies predominantly examine the coupling and coordination between NU and macro-level green development, ecological environment [19], analyzing the relationship from multiple dimensions such as population agglomeration, economic growth, and industrial structure adjustment [20, 21]. The results indicate that the eastern region has a predominant tendency [22]. The third area investigates the coupling coordination degree (CCD) between NU and TFCEE in specific industries, typically focusing on sectors like land use [23], agricultural development [24] and construction industry [25]. For example, studies reveal that NU-agricultural development cooperation has moved from “moderate disharmony” to “primary coordination,” with governmental support driving system synergy [26]. Additionally, the coordination between NU and intensive land use exhibits a “Center-edge” mode spatial distribution with significant regional disparities [27].

In summary, there are still several research gaps. Firstly, most existing studies adopt a one-way causal framework and fail to reveal the essential laws of the coordinated evolution of multiple subsystems under the ZCS. Secondly, the majority of studies remain at the level of measuring static coupling states, without decomposing the structural sources of regional inequality. Thirdly, there is a notable sectoral focus bias, coupled with a lack of systematic investigation into convergence dynamics and driving factors at the regional level, particularly in environmentally sensitive areas such as western China. Therefore, in-depth exploration of the coupling coordination, and synergistic effects as well as regional differences between TFCEE and NU is crucial for formulating scientific urbanization policies and environmental policies.

Therefore, China provides an appropriate context for this research, and the marginal contributions of this study are as follows: Firstly, it redefines NU and TFCEE as a co-evolutionary system, shifts the research focus from one-way influence to the complex correlation and dynamic equilibrium relationship between the two, and establishes a progressive dynamic analysis framework of “co-evolution, regional disparity, dynamic convergence”, which opens up a brand-new research perspective for exploring the internal connection between urban development and climate governance under the framework of Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Secondly, this paper innovatively adapts the Dagum-Gini coefficient model and convergence model to the complex system scenario of NU-TFCEE, breaking through the limitation that traditional coupling models cannot decompose internal differences. It can accurately identify the sources and structural characteristics of regional differences. Lastly, this paper clarifies the practical characteristics and in-depth influencing factors of low CCL and solidified regional absolute gaps in the western regions, abandons the one-sided prediction of convergent development, and provides a basis for formulating spatially differentiated and forward-looking policy regulation strategies. These contributions not only enrich the literature on China’s NU and carbon emission reduction but also provide practical enlightenment with Chinese characteristics for the international community to explore the synergy between urbanization and the ZCS.

Interaction mechanism

The NU and TFCEE are two open and complex subsystems. NU is an extensive process driven by modernity, involving multifaceted development across demographics, economy, ecology, industry, and urban-rural integration. It represents the unity of residents’ well-being, ecological sustainability, economic development, and social equity. Improving the TFCEE is the core pathway to achieving regional carbon neutrality goals. Specific measures include enhancing resource utilization efficiency, promoting green technological innovation, fostering the clustering of clean energy industries, and accelerating industrial structure upgrades. The essence of their coupling and coordination is a non-linear process in which the two systems evolve from disordered non-coordination to ordered coordination through factor exchange, information transmission, and feedback regulation. This theory breaks through the traditional linear causal assumption, emphasizes the interactive matching of multiple elements within the system, and provides a core basis for measuring the collaborative relationship between the two systems and their non-linear evolution characteristics.

On the one hand, energy activities, particularly the combustion of fossil fuels, constitute the principal source of global carbon emissions [28]. Improving TFCEE can reduce carbon emissions per unit of output through intensive resource utilization and technological innovation, thereby providing higher economic value and social welfare for NU [29]. First, under the dual-carbon goal, the economic and social demand for clean, efficient, energy-saving, and emission-reducing solutions is growing, providing opportunities for the diffusion of new energy technologies and the agglomeration of emerging industries. This trend has not only created new opportunities for energy financial investment but has also generated numerous industrial employment prospects [30, 31]. Consequently, it spurs the adjustment and upgrading of the industrial structure while simultaneously enhancing the development quality and competitive edge of urban areas [32]. Second, reducing carbon emissions will help reduce environmental problems, such as air and water pollution, improve the ecological environment quality of cities and towns [33], provide people with a better living environment and a healthier lifestyle, and enhance the attractiveness, subjective well-being, and livability of these areas [34]. Third, improving TFCEE, the key lies in deepening green innovation [35], which requires a focus on promoting research and development activities and practical applications in the green sector [36, 37]. This not only fosters novel avenues of economic growth for NU, yet elevates the smart and green development standards of both urban and rural areas [19]. Fourth, China’s NU is, in essence, a two-way process of rural and urban agglomeration and penetration. Enhancing TFCEE can facilitate the merging of both urban and rural areas through the implementation of green industries and renewable energy infrastructure [38, 39]. For instance, by building distributed photovoltaic, wind power and biomass energy projects in rural areas and establishing an urban-rural energy community, these steps will further dismantle the urban-rural dichotomy and advance the development for NU.

On the contrary, under the guidance of ZCS, NU is also playing a positive role in enhancing TFCEE through targeted measures such as industrial layout, green planning, and sustainable consumption guidance. First, ZCS in the context of NU actively promotes industrial structure upgrading by guiding investment toward low-carbon industries and phasing out inefficient high-carbon production capacity [21]. Secondly, China’s NU highlights the comprehensive planning of new infrastructure, facilitating the establishment of a networked, low-carbon, environmentally sustainable transportation and building system [40]. The growing adoption of electric vehicles, photovoltaic streetlights, energy-efficient insulation materials, smart lighting, and ventilation systems is in line with the rising trend of eco-friendly consumption during urbanization. The transition has facilitated the advancement and enhancement of clean energy consumption practices, thereby enhancing the TFCEE [41]. Thirdly, the relocation of rural inhabitants to urban regions promotes the transition from traditional biomass to cleaner and more efficient energy sources such as electricity and natural gas [42, 43], and the increase in income changes the consumption capacity. Fourthly, the accessibility of high-caliber educational resources in urban and suburban localities has improved the quality of human capital, and enabled individuals to possess the knowledge for engaging in technological research and development as well as the ability to apply advanced energy technologies, thus promoting the research, development, and application of energy technologies [44]. Simultaneously, with the popularization of education and the deepening of environmental protection publicity, the concept of zero-carbon living has been integrated into daily life. Urban and rural residents are more inclined to choose low-carbon and environmentally friendly consumption modes in their daily lives, which helps reduce carbon emissions [45].

Therefore, there is mutual promotion and interdependence between the NU and TFCEE. Amidst the progression of promoting China’s emerging urbanization, it is essential to thoroughly evaluate the interconnection and harmonious advancement of the NU and TFCEE to attain the synchronized development of economic, social, and environmental systems. In assessing China’s NU development, we draw upon the calculation process of some scholars [19]. In conclusion, the model employed in this study constitutes a complete analytical chain of “indicator measurement, relationship analysis, variance assessment, dynamic testing”. The entropy weight TOPSIS method and the super-efficiency SBM model are the data sources for the entire analysis. They independently calculate TFCEE and NU respectively, providing core variables for subsequent analysis. The outputs from the first two models are immediately fed into the coupled coordination model. The connection between two separate systems can be shown in the form of a measurable coordination index by figuring out their coupling degree and coordination degree. The Dagum-Gini coefficient utilizes the coupling coordination index as its analytical focus to precisely discern the origins of regional discrepancies. The convergent model further examines the long-term evolution trend based on these gap data, forming a progressive analysis of “static disparity description, dynamic trend prediction”.

Construction of evaluation index system and data source

Comprehensive evaluation system for the level of NU

Traditional urbanization has typically centered around population concentration and economic expansion. However, this is often at the cost of wasting resources and damaging the environment [50, 51]. Consequently, a series of issues has surfaced, including the overexploitation of resources, environmental degradation, unbalanced economic development, and exacerbation of social conflicts [52, 53]. NU, however, is “people-centered” emphasizing the harmonious development of the economic, social, and environmental spheres. It is one of the core policy tools for China to break the urban-rural dual structure, promote coordinated regional development, and achieve common prosperity. Referencing relevant literature [6, 54, 55], a comprehensive index system including 17 indicators from six dimensions, including population, economic, social, ecological, industrial, and urban-rural integration, was constructed. Therefore, the entropy weight TOPSIS method was used to measure NU levels in various regions.

Zero-carbon strategy: total-factor carbon emission rate

Compared with a single-factor index, the positive impact of the TFCEE on economic development has been fully demonstrated [56]. According to total factor productivity theory, TFCEE considers both CO₂ emissions and the impact of diverse input components and their substitutive impacts on carbon emission efficiency [57]. Its core is to measure the level of economic output (GDP) supported by unit carbon emissions when factors such as capital, labor, and energy inputs are fixed, or the ability to minimize carbon emissions when output is fixed. In pursuit of carbon peaking and carbon neutrality, it is imperative to diminish carbon emissions across the whole manufacturing process and improve the TFCEE. The input variables are labor input, capital input (with capital stock evaluated using the perpetual inventory approach), and energy input. These are correspondingly denoted by the employment count in urban areas, fixed capital stock, and energy usage. The desirable output is shown as real GDP, adjusted for inflation using 2010 as the base year, and the undesirable output is CO₂ emissions.

In the eastern area, the CCD increased from 0.553 to 0.675, reflecting a growth rate of 22.06%.

Coupling coordination level in China and the four major regions

Research indicates that, from several perspectives, economic and social urbanization trails behind demographic and ecological urbanization [58]. This difference also led to significant differences in CCD values between the different provinces (Fig. 2). Specifically, eastern regions such as Guangdong (0.801), Jiangsu (0.753), Shanghai (0.751), and Zhejiang (0.733) showed a high degree of coordination, while central regions such as Hunan (0.658) and Anhui (0.658) showed moderate levels of coordination. Their elevated CCD values further indicated that the level of development between TFCEE and NU was relatively balanced, with no serious imbalance. On the other hand, Qinghai (0.435), Guizhou (0.497) and Ningxia (0.502), which ranked at the bottom, have relatively backward economic and social development, and are also affected by objective factors such as rugged terrain and climate, which have hindered coordination and are not easy to improve. Simultaneously, its industrial framework is primarily characterized by heavy industry, leading to elevated power and energy consumption per unit of GDP, with its carbon intensity ranking among the highest, therefore contributing to environmental pollution and degradation [59]. Therefore, both natural conditions and artificial development models have jointly led to environmental deterioration, resulting in the CCD lagging behind for many years.

The CCD value across 30 provinces and cities from 2010 to 2022

From the heat map, all provinces and municipalities within the central and northeastern parts of China have reached the “barely coordinated level” or above (CCD > 0.500). In contrast, a few western provinces remain at the “near dysfunctional”. In the eastern area, the majority of developed provinces and municipalities exhibit substantial resource-agglomeration advantages across several dimensions, including the economy, science and technology, and human resources. For example, advanced technology sectors have concentrated and evolved in the eastern area [60]. During the course of the NU, these areas can effectively leverage their ample financial resources. They can channel these funds into energy transformation initiatives, the construction of green infrastructure, and the research, development, and application of advanced carbon-emission management technologies, these targeted investments and resource advantages correspond to a trend of improved alignment between NU advancement and TFCEE.

Spatio-temporal evolution of the CCD

To analyze regional patterns, we selected the spatiotemporal evolution results of provincial CCL for four representative years: 2010, 2014, 2018, and 2022 (Fig. 3). In 2010, the distribution of CCD in China was not uniform, and the CCD was on the verge of imbalance and barely coordinated in most provinces; only Guangxi Province exhibited a severe dysfunctional decline or worse (Fig. 3 (a)). By 2014, the value of CCD had increased in eastern coastal areas and inland central areas(Fig. 3 (b)). By 2018, a few areas were darker blue, indicating that CCD had significantly improved, spreading from west to east, with only Qinghai, Gansu, Ningxia, and Guizhou in the mildly dysfunctional light blue area (Fig. 3 (c)). This is because these four provinces and cities are affected by geographical factors, and the rugged terrain limits the construction of the NU. By 2022, there has been a notable rise in inland China (Fig. 3 (d)). The central, southern, and eastern coastal regions exhibit high CCD values, while Qinghai and Guizhou show significantly lower CCD values. A notable trend in CCD was observed from the “11th Five Year Plan” to the “13th Five Year Plan.” With respect to regional disparities, from 2010 to 2022, the coastal regions experienced relatively rapid development, whereas the growth rate in non-coastal areas was comparatively sluggish. However, regional disparities still exist. This pattern not only reflects the overall progress but also the imbalance in development among regions.

Regional differences and source decomposition of the CCD

Analysis of overall differences

This study utilizes the Dagum-Gini coefficient calculation formula to evaluate the overall disparities in the coupling and coordination levels between NU and TFCEE from 2010 to 2022, along with the variations across the eastern, central, and western regions. The results are illustrated in Fig. 4. The sample investigation revealed that the Dagum-Gini coefficient of the CCD for the two systems in China ranged from 0.087 to 0.074, with an average value of 0.078. The Dagum-Gini coefficient of CCD shows a trend of first rising and then falling, reaching a peak of 0.087 in 2016, and then steadily declining. At the 18th National Congress of the Communist Party of China in 2012, the strategic goal of “advancing the path of NU with Chinese characteristics” was put forward. The economy, society, culture, and other fields are developing rapidly. However, due to the weak foundation in the early stage of NU construction, the regional differences are still expanding. In 2016, after the “13th Five-Year Plan,” governments at all levels, from the central to the local, made comprehensive arrangements for climate change and low-carbon development, identified key tasks such as the low-carbon energy revolution and the construction of a low-carbon industrial system, and achieved positive results in the coordinated development of NU construction and TFCEE, with regional differences narrowing.

Analysis of intra-regional differences

As shown in Fig. 4, regarding the extent of difference, the intra-regional differences between the eastern and western regions are relatively large, with mean values of 0.079 and 0.057 respectively. Next, the average values of the central and northeastern regions are 0.027 and 0.010 respectively, which indicates that there are significant differences in CCD between the eastern and western regions. In the eastern region, these distinctions may stem from variations in developmental foundations, economic status, energy distribution factors, and other facets among provinces and cities. Beijing, Tianjin, Shanghai, and other urban centers boast of elevated urbanization levels and comprehensive TFCEE, endowing them with inherent advantages in fostering coupling and coordinated development. However, in provinces such as Shandong and Fujian, the coupled coordination between NU and TFCEE remains in an exploratory adaptation phase. Compared with provinces and cities at higher development levels in this domain, notable disparities still exist in their coordination mechanisms. In the western region, Chongqing, the sole municipality directly governed by the central government, collaborates with Sichuan to establish the “Chengdu-Chongqing Economic Circle,” which significantly surpasses other western provinces in terms of resource allocation, economic development, and infrastructure capacity. The disparities in CCL between the central and northeastern regions were comparatively small. The provinces in the central region are vigorously engaged in undertaking the layout and transfer of energy transformation, actively fulfilling the function of “linking the east and stimulating the west,” and the “two horizontal and three vertical” urbanization layout runs through the whole central region, undertaking economic and cultural exchanges between the east and the west. Owing to the influence of natural environments, such as geographical location and climate factors, policy support and coverage of provinces in NU construction and TFCEE showed a relatively balanced trend, and regional differences were relatively small.

In the dynamic evolution of intra-regional disparities, the Dagum-Gini coefficient in the northeastern regions decreased by 64.29%, with the largest drop. The decrease in the central region was relatively small, at 48.57%. The eastern region had the smallest decrease, at 24.42%, indicating that the spatial disequilibrium of the CCD in the central and northeastern regions improved, and the eastern region relied on the first-mover advantage to support NU construction and low-carbon transformation. However, during the sample observation period, intra-regional disparities in the western area exhibited an upward trajectory, with the Dagum-Gini coefficient rising by 35.56%, from 0.045 in 2010 to 0.061 in 2022. This trend represents the sole instance of growth across the four regions, highlighting notable regional differentiation in CCD within the western region. Therefore, western provinces and cities may adopt development paths and management measures tailored to their own characteristics and advantages, with a view to mitigating the structural contradictions between NU construction and carbon emission reduction in the region, as well as addressing intra-regional imbalances.

Analysis of inter-regional differences

Figure 5 shows the inter-regional differences in the CCL of NU and TFCEE in the four major regions from 2010 to 2022. From the perspective of the degree of fluctuation, the difference in the coupling and coordination level between NU and TFCEE among most regions first increased, then decreased, and tended to be stable. However, the difference between the eastern and central regions gradually decreased. Before 2016, the trend of “the strong are stronger, and the weak are difficult to catch up” was highlighted among various regions, and the Gini coefficient increased significantly. After the “13th Five-Year Plan” in 2016, China has significantly narrowed regional development disparities through coordinated strategies. Key approaches include: strengthening cross-regional infrastructure connectivity, fostering economic corridor collaboration, and expanding international cooperation channels. These measures have effectively enhanced spatial synergy across eastern, western, central, and northeastern regions.

The largest regional discrepancy was between eastern and western areas, with a mean difference coefficient of 0.115. The east-northeast disparity, measured by a mean coefficient of 0.096, was slightly narrower than the east-west gap. The results underscore a significant developmental disparity: the eastern area demonstrates considerable differences in coupling coordination development relative to the western and northeastern regions. The differences between most regions changed over time and then started to go down after 2016. This suggests that the developmental gap between the eastern, central, western, and northeastern regions is slowly closing thanks to smart planning of relevant regional policies. Changes in the coupling-coordination level also show a fairly balanced developmental trajectory.

Convergence analysis

Sigma convergence

σ-convergence focuses on the changes over time in the degree of dispersion of economic indicators among different regions. Specifically, within a certain period, if the coefficient of variation of the CCD between NU and TFCEE in various regions gradually decreases, it indicates that the coordination gaps among regions are continuously narrowing and tending towards uniformity. Figure 6 shows the results of the σ-convergence test of the variation coefficient measure of the CCD of NU and TFCEE from 2010 to 2022. In terms of fluctuation patterns, the national coefficient of variation index remained relatively stable between 2010 and 2022. Index values in the central and northeastern regions exhibited a fluctuating downward trend, whereas those in the eastern and western regions remained relatively stable. From the perspective of evolution, during the sample study period, the variation coefficient of each region decreased from large to small in the following order: Northeast > Central > Eastern> Western. Among them, the fluctuations in the Northeast region show a “decline-rise-decline-rise” trend, corresponding to the states of convergence from 2010 to 2012, divergence from 2013 to 2016, convergence in 2017, and divergence from 2018 to 2022. It is worth noting, however, that the coefficient of variation in this region decreased by 60.52%, from 0.154 in 2010 to 0.061 in 2022. At the national level, the overall coefficient of variation showed stability. From 2010 to 2022, it showed a converging trend, with the coefficient of variation decreasing by 5.89%, from 0.327 in 2010 to 0.308 in 2022. The Western region showed an “rise-decline-rise” trend, with divergence from 2010 to 2014, convergence from 2015 to 2018, and divergence again from 2019 to 2022. The coefficient of variation in this region increased from 0.196 in 2010 to 0.310 in 2022, an increase of 58.16%.

Change trend of coupling coordination level σ-convergence

Overall, except for the western region, which is the only region that does not show obvious σ-convergence, the CCD of other regions exhibits significant σ-convergence. This indicates that there are inequality issues in the western region in the process of promoting NU and improving TFCEE. First, due to excessive reliance on the traditional resource-based economy, the industrial development in the western regions has fallen into a high-carbon path, and the disconnection between the industrial chain and the innovation chain has restricted regional technological innovation [61]. Second, its immature green financial system causes insufficient green capital investment [62], hindering the large-scale application of low-carbon technologies. Third, severe brain drain and a shortage of refined carbon management talents block the effective transformation of advanced low-carbon technologies into efficiency [63]. Accordingly, the western region has failed to rapidly improve the CCD, cannot narrow the development gap, and finds it difficult to achieve the coordinated development of the CCD within the region.

β-convergence merely indicates that the disparities in growth rates are narrowing. However, it will take time for the actual economic gaps between regions to shrink. Specifically, regions with relatively lower initial levels of coupled coordination between NU and TFCEE tend to exhibit catch-up growth, while those with higher initial levels experience growth deceleration, leading all regions to converge toward a common steady-state equilibrium over time.

It is necessary to significantly increase support for western regions in areas such as infrastructure construction, innovation support, R&D, and recruitment, thereby improving the economic development conditions of western regions, enhancing the CCL between NU and TFCEE, and promoting balanced regional economic development.

Conclusions and policy implications

Conclusion

Exploring the collaborative promotion of NU and TFCEE, and narrowing regional gaps, is crucial for reconciling the contradiction between regional development and emission reduction, and achieving the “dual carbon” goals. Although developed countries have balanced regional differences in carbon emissions, they only started emission reduction after urbanization was mature, resulting in a decoupling feature between emission reduction and urbanization. Other developing countries, however, face asynchrony between urbanization and emission reduction, which hinders the implementation of the ZCS. Taking China as an example, this study constructs a new framework for the coupling of NU and TFCEE, and uses entropy-weighted TOPSIS, super-efficiency SBM, coupling coordination, and convergence models to analyze their regional differences and spatiotemporal evolution. The results show that the national NU-TFCEE coupling coordination level in China has risen from “barely coordinated” to “basically coordinated”, showing a decreasing gradient from east to central to west. Intra-group collaboration fluctuates, with significant gaps between the east and west, and expanding internal differences in the west; most regions, except the western region, show significant σ-convergence, and there is obvious absolute β-convergence. This framework provides a new empirical perspective and key decision support for research on unbalanced regional development in China, and also offers practical references for other developing countries to balance urbanization and regional emission reduction.

Policy implications

Drawing on the foregoing research conclusions, this study has the following policy implications.

First, the synergistic development of NU and TFCEE fundamentally necessitates embedding low-carbon transformation throughout the entire process of urban spatial restructuring and factor allocation. In the NU process, by optimizing the energy structure, a linkage mechanism is established between the energy utilization system and TFCEE improvement, simultaneously advancing the reduction and substitution of fossil energy consumption and the large-scale application of clean energy, while expanding the scale of urban energy use. Focusing on the demands of NU for green buildings, smart transportation, and circular industries, a series of applied research and development programs are deployed to promote the large-scale validation and commercial diffusion of energy-saving and carbon-reducing technologies in urban renewal and new district construction. Meanwhile, a cross-regional technology diffusion network is established, leveraging collaborative innovation platforms of urban agglomerations to accelerate the gradient transfer of mature low-carbon technologies from the eastern region to the urbanizing areas in the central and western regions, thereby narrowing the technology acquisition gap and enhancing TFCEE from the supply side. Furthermore, green finance is deeply integrated with urban renewal projects. Through instruments such as carbon reduction-linked loans and local government special bonds, targeted support is provided for infrastructure construction that delivers both carbon mitigation and public welfare benefits, such as urban rail transit and clean heating, thereby creating a positive feedback loop between capital investment and improvements in carbon emission efficiency.

Second, regional disparities should be respected and precision governance implemented. The one-size-fits-all low-carbon assessment in NU development should be abolished, and a differentiated policy toolbox based on main functional zones should be established. Cross-regional carbon offset mechanisms and technical assistance mechanisms should also be set up to promote nationwide overall synergy through orderly local convergence. The eastern region should continue to strengthen refined government regulation and technology-driven innovation, while optimizing the implementation pace of environmental regulations to avoid the suppression of dual-system synergy by short-term transition costs, thereby breaking through the bottleneck of high-level coordination. The northeastern region should further activate its existing innovation capacity, focusing on key areas such as energy conservation, environmental protection, and equipment remanufacturing, to facilitate the transition of the CCD from rapid catch-up to high-quality synergy. The central region should optimize its opening-up structure and industrial relocation pattern, with emphasis on introducing low-carbon and high-value-added industries. Meanwhile, it should improve intra-regional resource allocation mechanisms to mitigate the loss of factors caused by the siphon effect and enhance endogenous drivers of coordinated development. The western region should intensify efforts in labor skill training and low-carbon technology introduction. Leveraging its late-developer advantage, it can directly adopt a low-carbon urbanization orientation. By undertaking the transfer of low-carbon industries from the eastern region and deploying clean energy bases, the region can achieve simultaneous qualitative improvements in NU and TFCEE, thereby avoiding the path-dependence costs associated with the “develop first, treat pollution later” model experienced by the eastern region.

Third, interregional cooperation and exchanges should be promoted, while discrepancies in the coupling and coordination of the two systems should be reduced. When combined with areas with high coordination levels, the inter-regional flow of resources encourages regional technological collaboration and exchange, maximizes their own radiation capability, and supports synchronous improvement of TFCEE in the NU process. Regions with a low level of coupling coordination should attach importance to their own advantages and key factors, select transformation paths according to local conditions, continuously promote urban industrial upgrading and the construction of low-carbon industrial systems, and strive to narrow the gaps with regions with high-level development. For regions highly reliant on high-carbon resources, the approach of using resource premiums to fund green transitions can be adopted. The imposition of reasonable premiums on oil, gas, and other resource products has been identified as a means of establishing local green transition funds. These funds should be allocated with a specific focus on addressing the early-stage financing challenges associated with low-carbon urbanization transitions. This support should encompass the implementation of clean energy projects and technological upgrades within traditional high-carbon industries.

Limitations

This study validates the core conclusions of existing literature and expands the scope of research within an analytical framework that integrates NU with zero-carbon strategy. Previous studies have revealed that the western regions face challenges such as an overly heavy industrial structure, low energy efficiency, fragile ecological foundations and insufficient green technological innovation [64, 65]. These regions face significant challenges in balancing ecological protection and green transformation in economic development, further confirming that they are a weak link in the coordinated development of NU and ZCS. This article studies the deep dynamic evolution trend of the coordinated development of the two in each region, but it has certain limitations. Firstly, due to the limitation of data availability, this study adopted data at the provincial level. To draw more detailed and reliable conclusions, future research can expand its scope to the county and municipal levels, which will significantly enhance the accuracy of policy impacts. Secondly, the spatial effects that may be exhibited in the coordinated development of NU and TFCEE have not been explored in depth. The insignificant σ-convergence in the western region may be exacerbated by the weak flow of factors between adjacent provinces, and may also directly affect the differences in regional coordination levels due to spatial interaction mechanisms such as pollution spillover and technological innovation diffusion. Subsequent studies will make necessary responses to these real impact mechanisms. Thirdly, Whilst the present study has delineated the characteristics of the level of coordinated development, a more profound analysis of the core factors influencing the synergy effect is required. The mechanisms through which key variables exert their effects, such as the intensity of policy synergy, the distribution of green innovation resources, and the closeness of industrial correlation, require further exploration.
