Cardiometabolic and inflammatory profiles associated with intermediate post-DST cortisol levels (1.0–1.8 µg/dL) in patients with adrenal incidentalomas
Authors: Azad Duman, Murat Alay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe clinical relevance of post-dexamethasone cortisol levels below the conventional 1.8 µg/dL threshold remains uncertain in patients with adrenal incidentalomas. We aimed to evaluate whether cortisol levels within the 1.0–1.8 µg/dL range are associated with adverse cardiometabolic and inflammatory profiles.MethodsIn this retrospective, single-center cohort study, 199 patients with adrenal incidentalomas evaluated between 2023 and 2025 were included. Participants were stratified according to 1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test (DST) results into two groups: <1.0 µg/dL (n = 112) and 1.0–1.8 µg/dL (n = 87). Baseline hormonal (ACTH, DHEAS), metabolic (fasting glucose, HbA1c), and hematological parameters were obtained prior to DST to minimize the acute effects of dexamethasone. Systemic inflammation was assessed using the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII). Group comparisons were performed using appropriate parametric or non-parametric tests. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent predictors of hypertension and diabetes mellitus.ResultsThe prevalences of hypertension (71.3% vs. 42.9%, p < 0.001) and diabetes mellitus (37.9% vs. 18.8%, p = 0.003) were significantly higher in the 1.0–1.8 µg/dL group. These patients exhibited lower basal ACTH (p = 0.012) and DHEAS (p = 0.001) levels, along with higher fasting plasma glucose (p < 0.001) and HbA1c (p = 0.003). Inflammatory markers, including NLR (p = 0.024) and SII (p = 0.040), were also significantly elevated. After adjustment for age, sex, tumor size, and LDL cholesterol, cortisol levels within the 1.0–1.8 µg/dL range remained independently associated with both hypertension (OR = 2.74, 95% CI: 1.35–5.54, p = 0.005) and diabetes mellitus (OR = 2.39, 95% CI: 1.15–4.97, p = 0.020).ConclusionCortisol levels between 1.0 and 1.8 µg/dL were associated with increased systemic inflammation and an unfavorable cardiometabolic profile in patients with adrenal incidentalomas. These findings support the concept that cardiometabolic risk may exist along a continuum of post-DST cortisol values, even below the conventional 1.8 µg/dL threshold. Patients within this intermediate cortisol range may benefit from closer clinical follow-up and cardiometabolic risk assessment, although prospective studies are needed to clarify its long-term clinical significance.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Adrenal incidentaloma, Cardiometabolic risk, Dexamethasone suppression test, Diabetes mellitus, Hypertension, Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), Systemic immune-inflammation index (SII)

Introduction

With the widespread use of cross-sectional imaging techniques today, the detection rate of adrenal incidentalomas has increased significantly. The majority of these lesions are traditionally classified as masses that do not exhibit hormonal excess and are clinically considered “non-functional” [1]. However, in recent years, increasing evidence suggests that these patients are not completely biologically inert and that even low levels of cortisol secretion may have significant effects on cardiometabolic risk [2]. This suggests that the functional assessment approach in patients with adrenal incidentalomas may not be limited to significant hormonal excess and that a broader clinical spectrum should be considered [3].

Current clinical guidelines generally recommend a cut-off value of 1.8 µg/dL for the 1 mg dexamethasone suppression test (DST) to exclude autonomous cortisol secretion [4]. However, there is growing evidence that patients in the range of 1.0–1.8 µg/dL, which falls below this threshold and is traditionally considered non-functional, may not be clinically completely silent. As a matter of fact, the lower ACTH and DHEAS levels observed in patients within this “gray zone” are consistent with previous reports suggesting subtle suppression of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, even at cortisol levels below the conventional DST threshold [5, 6]. The increase in metabolic comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes that accompany these hormonal changes supports the cardiometabolic effects of low-level cortisol exposure [7].

An important yet insufficiently elucidated aspect of the relationship between cortisol autonomy in incidental adrenal tumors and cardiometabolic disorders is the potential mediating role of chronic low-grade inflammation in this process. Cortisol exposure is known to alter the distribution of immune cells by inducing neutrophilia and relative lymphopenia, and to promote a pro-inflammatory environment through endothelial dysfunction and metabolic imbalance [8, 9]. Indeed, it has been reported that inflammation-based scores in adrenal cortical tumors are associated with the degree of cortisol secretion [10]. In this context, while the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) is evaluated as an indicator of systemic inflammation and cardiovascular risk; the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) stands out as a more comprehensive biomarker that integrates inflammatory and thrombotic activity by reflecting the combined components of neutrophils, platelets, and lymphocytes [11, 12]. However, it is observed that the current literature largely focuses on patients with marked cortisol excess, and the clinical significance of these inflammatory markers at different cortisol levels has not been sufficiently elucidated. Therefore, the clinical and prognostic value of these indices remains uncertain.

Despite accumulating evidence, the relationship between post-dexamethasone cortisol levels in the 1.0–1.8 µg/dL range and systemic inflammatory response has not been specifically evaluated in patients with adrenal incidentalomas traditionally considered non-functioning. In particular, the clinical and prognostic significance of emerging inflammatory indices, such as the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), within this specific ‘grey zone’ has not been clearly established. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies specifically evaluating systemic inflammatory burden within the 1.0–1.8 µg/dL DST range. The primary objective of this study was to compare the hormonal, metabolic, and inflammatory profiles of patients with cortisol suppression < 1.0 µg/dL and those in the 1.0–1.8 µg/dL range. We hypothesized that this subtle impairment in cortisol suppression serves as an independent risk factor for cardiometabolic comorbidities—specifically hypertension and diabetes mellitus—and is associated with increased systemic inflammatory markers.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective, single-center study included patients diagnosed with adrenal incidentaloma who were evaluated at the Department of Endocrinology between January 2023 and December 2025. Only patients with radiologically benign adrenal incidentalomas and post-dexamethasone suppression test (DST) cortisol levels below 1.8 µg/dL were eligible for inclusion. Patients who underwent a 1 mg overnight DST and had complete clinical, biochemical, and hematological data were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded in the presence of overt Cushing’s syndrome, exogenous glucocorticoid use, active infection, chronic inflammatory or autoimmune diseases, known malignancy, hematological disorders, previous adrenal surgery, or missing baseline laboratory data (Fig. 1). In addition, patients with biochemically confirmed functional adrenal tumors were excluded from the study. Pheochromocytoma was evaluated using plasma free metanephrines and/or 24-hour urinary fractionated metanephrines, whereas patients with suspected primary aldosteronism underwent guideline-recommended screening with aldosterone-to-renin ratio assessment and confirmatory testing when appropriate. In order to minimize the potential acute pharmacological effects of dexamethasone on glucose metabolism and inflammatory parameters, laboratory measurements obtained on the day following dexamethasone administration were excluded from the analysis, with the exception of post-DST serum cortisol levels. All metabolic, hormonal, lipid, and hematological parameters included in the study were obtained from baseline blood samples collected prior to dexamethasone administration as part of routine clinical evaluation.

Flowchart of patient selection and study design

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration and was approved by the Local Ethics Committee of Van Yüzüncü Yıl University (Approval No.: 2026/03–21, Date: 13 March 2026). Informed consent was waived by the Local Ethics Committee of Van Yüzüncü Yıl University due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Biochemical and hormonal assessment

All biochemical, hormonal, and hematological data were retrospectively retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical records. To minimize potential confounding effects of circadian variation and immediate dietary intake on metabolic and hematological parameters, all baseline venous blood samples were strictly collected between 08:00 AM and 10:00 AM following an overnight fast of at least 8 h. To avoid the acute pharmacological confounding effects of exogenous dexamethasone on glucose metabolism and leukocyte kinetics, only post-DST serum cortisol levels were measured on the morning following dexamethasone administration. Basal adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS), fasting plasma glucose, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and lipid profile parameters (LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, and triglycerides) were obtained during this standardized baseline assessment period prior to the DST. Complete blood count parameters, including absolute neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet counts, were also derived from the same fasting baseline venous samples to allow for the precise evaluation of underlying cellular shifts and derived inflammatory indices.

Inflammatory indices

Systemic inflammatory status was assessed using the following hematological indices, calculated from baseline complete blood count parameters and defined in the literature [13–15]:

Neutrophil-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR): Neutrophil count divided by lymphocyte count.

Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII): (Neutrophils × Platelets) / Lymphocytes.

Platelet-Lymphocyte Ratio (PLR): Platelet count divided by lymphocyte count.

Lymphocyte-to-Monocyte Ratio (LMR): Lymphocyte count divided by monocyte count.

Definitions and grouping

Patients were divided into two groups based on their post-DST cortisol levels:

Control group: Cortisol < 1.0 µg/dL (n = 112).

Study group: Patients with adrenal incidentalomas classified as traditionally non-functioning and with cortisol levels in the 1.0–1.8 µg/dL range (n = 87).

Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure of ≥ 140 mmHg and/or a diastolic blood pressure of ≥ 90 mmHg, or the use of active antihypertensive medication. Diabetes mellitus was defined according to American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria or the use of antidiabetic treatment.

Clinical and radiological evaluation: Demographic characteristics (age, sex), clinical data (presence of hypertension and diabetes), and radiological characteristics of adrenal lesions (maximum tumor diameter and location) were recorded.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 31.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) where appropriate. For between-group comparisons, the independent samples t-test was used for variables with a normal distribution, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for those without a normal distribution. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test.

Multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of hypertension and diabetes mellitus. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariate analyses were included in the multivariate models. Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). Model calibration was examined using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. To further evaluate the potential impact of age imbalance between groups, additional age-adjusted analyses were performed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) and linear regression models with age entered as a covariate. To account for multiple comparisons, p-values obtained from these analyses were additionally adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) method. Variables that remained significant after age adjustment and FDR correction were considered robust findings. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

When the demographic and clinical characteristics of the 199 patients included in the study were compared according to their dexamethasone suppression test (DST) groups, no statistically significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of gender distribution (p = 0.812) and lesion localization (p = 0.252); however, significant differences were observed in terms of metabolic comorbidities. In particular, in the group with DST results in the 1.0–1.8 µg/dL range, the prevalence of diabetes mellitus (37.9% vs. 18.8%, p = 0.003) and the prevalence of hypertension (71.3% vs. 42.9%, p < 0.001) were significantly higher compared to the group achieving complete suppression (Fig. 2). Patients in the higher DST group (1.0–1.8 µg/dL) exhibited larger adenoma sizes with a median of 24.0 mm (IQR 17.5–32.0), whereas the median size was 17.0 mm (IQR 11.0–23.25) in the group with DST < 1.0 µg/dL (p = 0.001) (Table 1).

The multivariable logistic regression model for hypertension demonstrated acceptable calibration according to the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p = 0.343) and modest explanatory power (Nagelkerke R² = 0.218). After adjustment for age, sex, tumor size, and LDL cholesterol, the DST group (1.0–1.8 µg/dL) remained an independent predictor of hypertension (OR = 2.74, 95% CI: 1.35–5.54, p = 0.005). Age was also independently associated with hypertension, whereas sex, tumor size, and LDL cholesterol were not significant predictors.

Similarly, the multivariable logistic regression model for diabetes mellitus showed acceptable calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.993) and modest explanatory power (Nagelkerke R² = 0.10). The overall model was statistically significant (Omnibus test, p = 0.003). In contrast, age, sex, tumor size, and LDL cholesterol were not identified as independent predictors. Overall, both models demonstrated adequate calibration and supported the robustness of the observed associations between intermediate post-DST cortisol levels and adverse cardiometabolic outcomes (Table 4).

Discussion

This study showed that patients with adrenal incidentalomas and post-DST cortisol levels between 1.0 and 1.8 µg/dL had a less favorable cardiometabolic and inflammatory profile compared with those who achieved complete cortisol suppression (< 1.0 µg/dL). In particular, hypertension and diabetes mellitus were significantly more common in this subgroup. Moreover, after adjustment for age, sex, tumor size, and LDL cholesterol, belonging to the 1.0–1.8 µg/dL DST category remained independently associated with both hypertension (OR 2.74) and diabetes mellitus (OR 2.39). In addition to these metabolic differences, patients in the intermediate cortisol range had lower ACTH and DHEAS levels, together with higher NLR and SII values. These findings suggest that even cortisol levels below the conventional 1.8 µg/dL threshold may be associated with measurable hormonal, metabolic, and inflammatory alterations. Importantly, the associations observed for ACTH, NLR, and SII remained significant after age-adjusted analyses, indicating that they could not be explained solely by the older age of patients in the 1.0–1.8 µg/dL group. Taken together, our findings support the concept that cardiometabolic risk may exist along a continuum of post-DST cortisol values rather than a strictly binary classification. However, given the retrospective design of the study, these observations should be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in prospective studies.

Current clinical guidelines recommend a cortisol threshold of 1.8 µg/dL following a dexamethasone suppression test (DST) to rule out mild autonomous cortisol secretion (MACS) [16]. However, accumulating evidence suggests that cardiometabolic risk may exist along a continuum of cortisol secretion rather than according to a strict dichotomous classification, with adverse outcomes reported even at cortisol levels below 1.8 µg/dL [17, 18]. In particular, Favero et al. demonstrated a graded increase in the prevalence of hypertension and diabetes mellitus with rising post-DST cortisol levels [19]. Consistent with these observations, patients in our cohort with post-DST cortisol levels between 1.0 and 1.8 µg/dL exhibited significantly higher prevalences of hypertension and diabetes mellitus compared with those achieving complete cortisol suppression (< 1.0 µg/dL). Importantly, these associations persisted after adjustment for age, sex, tumor size, and LDL cholesterol, suggesting that the observed increase in cardiometabolic burden cannot be explained solely by differences in demographic characteristics or tumor-related factors. The higher prevalence of hypertension observed in this subgroup is biologically plausible, as even subtle increases in cortisol activity may influence blood pressure regulation through enhanced vascular responsiveness, endothelial dysfunction, and alterations in sodium handling [20]. Similarly, low-level cortisol activity may contribute to impaired glucose metabolism through increased hepatic gluconeogenesis, reduced insulin sensitivity, and alterations in pancreatic β-cell function [20, 21]. Although causality cannot be inferred from the present study, the persistence of these associations after multivariable adjustment supports the potential clinical relevance of this intermediate cortisol range and raises the possibility that low-level cortisol activity may be associated with early cardiometabolic alterations in patients traditionally classified as having non-functioning adrenal incidentalomas.

Importantly, patients within the 1.0–1.8 µg/dL cortisol range exhibit both an unfavorable metabolic profile and a heightened systemic inflammatory burden, underscoring the clinical relevance of this intermediate cortisol range. In particular, the significantly higher systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) observed in this group suggest that low-level cortisol activity may be associated with subtle alterations in immune cell distribution and a low-grade inflammatory profile. In contrast, CRP levels did not differ significantly between groups, suggesting that the observed inflammatory signal may be more closely reflected by leukocyte-derived inflammatory indices than by acute-phase reactants. Although previous studies have demonstrated that inflammation-based indices are associated with overt cortisol excess and adrenal incidentalomas [22, 23], the clinical significance of this relationship within this specific “gray zone” remains insufficiently characterized. Our findings suggest a potential biological link between low-level hormonal activity and systemic inflammation. In addition, inflammation-based markers such as SII and NLR are widely associated not only with adrenal pathologies but also with various cardiometabolic conditions, including cardiovascular disease, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes [24–26]. This relationship may be mediated by the genomic and non-genomic effects of cortisol on glucocorticoid receptors in bone marrow and peripheral immune cells. Even within the 1.0–1.8 µg/dL range, low-level cortisol activity may contribute to alterations in leukocyte distribution, including relative neutrophilia and lymphocyte redistribution. These cellular shifts provide a plausible pathophysiological basis for the elevated SII and NLR levels observed in our study. The concurrent increase in multiple leukocyte-based inflammatory indices may further support the presence of an altered inflammatory profile associated with intermediate post-DST cortisol levels. However, given the cross-sectional nature of the present study, the directionality of this association cannot be determined. Elevated inflammatory markers may reflect subtle hormonal activity, underlying cardiometabolic comorbidity, or a combination of both mechanisms. Therefore, the observed association between intermediate post-DST cortisol levels and inflammatory indices should be interpreted cautiously and warrants further investigation in prospective studies.

Tumor size was significantly higher in patients with cortisol levels in the 1.0–1.8 µg/dL range compared to those with complete suppression. This finding supports the hypothesis that increasing tumor burden may be associated with a higher prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidities, even in patients with clinically non-secreting adrenal lesions [27]. Although tumor size was not identified as an independent predictor in multivariable analysis, its association with higher cortisol levels suggests a potential role in the early stages of hormonal dysregulation. These results are consistent with previous studies indicating that larger adrenal adenomas may exhibit greater biological activity despite the absence of overt hormonal excess.

Our study has several methodological strengths. One of the key strengths of our study is that the metabolic and inflammatory parameters evaluated have been carefully isolated from the potential pharmacological effects of dexamethasone. It is known in the literature that the use of exogenous glucocorticoids can acutely affect glucose metabolism and cause temporary changes in leukocyte distribution (particularly neutrophilia and lymphopenia). To minimize this potential confounding effect, all biochemical and hematological data used in our analyses—with the exception of cortisol levels measured after DST—were obtained as baseline measurements prior to the test as part of routine clinical evaluation. This approach suggests that the observed elevated SII and NLR values may be related to the patients’ endogenous cortisol activity rather than the acute effects of dexamethasone. Furthermore, the fact that the cohort of 199 patients was evaluated at a single center and within the framework of standardized protocols supports the internal validity and consistency of our data. The combined analysis of hormonal, metabolic, and inflammatory parameters has enabled us to assess the systemic effects of low-level cortisol release in a more comprehensive manner.

Our study has certain limitations. First, its retrospective and single-center design limits causal inference and generalizability. Second, although the sample size was adequate, larger multicenter studies are needed to confirm these findings. Importantly, the lack of data on body mass index (BMI), smoking status, and detailed medication use represents a potential source of residual confounding. Obesity and commonly used medications such as antihypertensives, statins, and antidiabetic agents may influence cardiometabolic risk and inflammatory indices (NLR, SII), and could partially affect the observed associations. To mitigate this limitation, we applied strict exclusion criteria, including active infections, hematological disorders, autoimmune diseases, known malignancies, and exogenous glucocorticoid use, in an effort to reduce potential confounding from inflammatory conditions and pharmacological factors (Fig. 1). Furthermore, multivariable analyses were adjusted for age, sex, tumor size, and LDL cholesterol, and additional age-adjusted analyses using ANCOVA and False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction were performed for hormonal and inflammatory parameters to evaluate whether the observed associations for ACTH, NLR, and SII remained independent of the age imbalance between groups (Table 5). Nevertheless, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. In addition, the relatively modest Nagelkerke R² values observed in the multivariable models indicate that factors not captured in the present dataset may also contribute to the development of hypertension and diabetes mellitus in this population. Finally, the absence of long-term follow-up data precludes evaluation of the prognostic impact of low-level cortisol secretion. Nevertheless, the use of standardized protocols and baseline measurements strengthens the internal consistency of our findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, patients with adrenal incidentalomas and post-DST cortisol levels between 1.0 and 1.8 µg/dL exhibited a less favorable cardiometabolic and inflammatory profile compared with those who achieved complete cortisol suppression (< 1.0 µg/dL). Hypertension and diabetes mellitus were more prevalent in this subgroup, and these associations remained significant after adjustment for age, sex, tumor size, and LDL cholesterol. In addition, lower ACTH and DHEAS levels together with higher NLR and SII values suggest that this intermediate cortisol range may be associated with measurable hormonal and inflammatory alterations. Taken together, these findings support the concept that cardiometabolic risk may exist along a continuum of post-DST cortisol values, even below the conventional 1.8 µg/dL threshold. These observations highlight the potential clinical relevance of this intermediate cortisol range and suggest that patients within this subgroup may benefit from closer cardiometabolic assessment during routine follow-up. However, given the retrospective design of the study, prospective investigations are required to clarify the long-term clinical significance of these findings and their implications for patient management.
