Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a orchestrates bidirectional Treg-CAF crosstalk to sustain immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments
Authors: Haicheng Ma, Yingying Zheng, Mengmeng Pei, Yanyan Zeng, Zhenyu Zhang, Yanyan Xu, Xueting Wang, Weihang Zhang, Niannian Li, Shaoqiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Resistance to immunotherapy is often reinforced by the resilient immunosuppressive architecture of the tumor microenvironment (TME). Although the crosstalk between regulatory T cells (Tregs) and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) is increasingly recognized as an important contributor to this resistance, the specific molecular links that sustain this interaction remain incompletely understood. This review proposes a bidirectional Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a–CAF signaling model to explain how immunosuppressive remodeling may be maintained. Current evidence suggests that activated Tregs can release exosomes enriched in miR-146a and may thereby influence the response state of recipient CAFs through signaling nodes such as TRAF6/IRAK1, NF-κB, and JAK2-STAT3. Meanwhile, CAF-derived signals may support Treg recruitment and functional maintenance. Through the secretion of chemokines and cytokines such as CCL2, CXCL12, TGF-β, and IL-6, CAFs contribute to Treg recruitment, local persistence, and suppressive function. Because these processes are currently supported largely by indirect evidence derived from different experimental models, this review integrates them into a bidirectional Treg–CAF communication framework that requires further validation, rather than presenting them as a fully established causal feedback loop. To therapeutically interrogate and potentially disrupt this putative circuit, we discuss strategies focused on blocking exosome biogenesis, neutralizing extracellular miR-146a, or disrupting downstream metabolic and signaling dependencies. These strategies are better viewed as candidate approaches for validating and intervening in this model, rather than as clinically mature targeted therapies. By distinguishing validated mechanisms from indirect evidence and testable inferences, this review aims to provide a clearer experimental framework and potential translational directions for future studies.

Introduction

The immunosuppressive landscape within the tumor microenvironment represents a major barrier to achieving durable responses to cancer immunotherapy [1]. Within this complex ecosystem, Tregs and CAFs may cooperatively shape an immune-excluded niche through complementary mechanisms. Tregs can suppress both the priming and effector phases of antitumor immunity [2], whereas CAFs establish physical and metabolic barriers by depositing extracellular matrix, altering tissue stiffness, and remodeling the nutrient and metabolic milieu [3, 4]. Together, these processes may limit effector T-cell infiltration into tumor nests and promote immune exhaustion and therapeutic resistance [5]. Although previous studies have suggested spatial and functional associations between Tregs and CAFs in multiple tumor types, how these two cell populations mutually sustain an immunosuppressive niche remains to be understood within a broader communication network [1, 2]. This view is also consistent with earlier work describing CAFs as active stromal regulators rather than passive bystanders in tumor progression [77].

Among the known modes of Treg–CAF communication, soluble factors represent the most frequently discussed layer. Cytokines and chemokines derived from CAFs or enriched within the TME, such as TGF-β, IL-6, CCL2, and CXCL12, may participate in Treg recruitment, local maintenance, and regulation of suppressive function [1, 51]. Beyond paracrine signaling, metabolic cues may also contribute to this interaction: CAF glycolytic reprogramming, lactate efflux, hypoxic adaptation, and nutrient competition can collectively reshape the adaptive fitness of Tregs and effector T cells within the local microenvironment [33, 34]. In addition, spatial proximity and direct cell contact between Tregs and CAFs may enhance communication efficiency; for example, ICAM-1/LFA-1-associated adhesive interactions may facilitate the formation of local immune–stromal synapses [5]. Meanwhile, CAF-mediated ECM deposition and stromal barriers may further restrict effector T-cell infiltration, thereby reinforcing the local impact of soluble, metabolic, and spatial modes of Treg–CAF communication [3, 4].

Therefore, Treg–CAF communication should not be understood as a single linear pathway, but rather as a multilayered network composed of multiple interacting levels. Soluble factors and metabolic cues mainly explain Treg recruitment, local maintenance, functional regulation, and the formation of a CAF-activated microenvironment, whereas cell contact and spatial proximity may enhance the efficiency of local signal transmission. In contrast, exosome-mediated communication places greater emphasis on the intercellular delivery of specific cargoes, whereby microRNAs (miRNAs), proteins, or lipids enter recipient cells and influence intracellular signaling nodes. The focus of this review on Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a does not imply that it represents the sole pathway replacing all soluble-factor- or direct-contact-mediated mechanisms; rather, miR-146a is considered a candidate cargo mediator that may link Treg exosomal signaling to altered CAF response states.

However, the mechanisms described above mainly explain the multilayered context of Treg–CAF interactions, but do not fully clarify whether Tregs can exert more directional effects on CAF response states through specific molecular cargoes. Indeed, Tregs do not rely solely on CTLA-4-mediated suppression or IL-10 signaling to exert their functions [9]. As intercellular communication vehicles, exosomes can deliver protein and nucleic acid cargoes within the TME, thereby participating in long-range regulation between immune cells and stromal cells [6–8]. Therefore, in the context of Treg–CAF interactions, the key question is not whether exosomes can transmit signals, but whether specific Treg-derived exosomal cargoes can influence CAF response states, and in which tumor types and CAF-subset contexts this may occur, thereby providing reciprocal support for Treg recruitment, maintenance, and functional regulation.

Within the repertoire of Treg-derived exosomes, miR-146a appears to be prominently enriched [11, 50]. Although its role in maintaining Treg function has been documented [12, 13], an important mechanistic gap remains: when this miRNA is delivered into CAFs, which signaling cascades and metabolic programs are altered? Clarifying this process may help fill a missing link in the construction of an immune–stromal crosstalk model [14, 15]. It should be noted that the immunological effects of miR-146a are highly context-dependent. Within Tregs or other immune cells, miR-146a generally functions as a negative feedback regulator of inflammation and contributes to immune tolerance and Treg functional maintenance by limiting signaling pathways involving TRAF6/IRAK1, STAT1, and related nodes [12, 13]. In contrast, when miR-146a exists as extracellular RNA or as an exosomal cargo, its effects may be shaped by the recipient cell type, accompanying cargoes, and pattern-recognition receptor pathways. Some studies have suggested that extracellular miR-146a may also induce innate immune responses through TLR7/MyD88-related mechanisms [99]. Therefore, the Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a–CAF axis discussed in this review should be understood as a candidate mode of action of miR-146a within a specific immune–stromal niche, rather than as a fixed function of miR-146a across all immunological contexts.

This review integrates current evidence on Treg-derived exosomes, miR-146a signaling, and CAF remodeling, and proposes a working model of “Treg → exosomal miR-146a → CAF → Treg” communication. It should be emphasized that this model has not been fully demonstrated within a single experimental system, but instead consists of multiple interconnected steps supported by evidence of varying strength. Relatively direct evidence mainly supports the ability of Tregs to release miR-146a-containing exosomes, as well as the involvement of miR-146a in Treg functional regulation and inflammatory signaling control. Indirect evidence suggests that exosomes carrying miR-146a from different cellular sources or tumor models can influence fibroblast states and involve signaling nodes such as TRAF6/IRAK1, SOCS1, NF-κB, and JAK2-STAT3. On this basis, CAF-mediated stromal remodeling, chemokine secretion, lactate metabolism, and reciprocal effects on Treg recruitment and stability should be understood as functional outputs and feedback components that require further validation. Following this logic, miR-146a is better viewed as a candidate molecular mediator linking immune cells and stromal cells, rather than as a single determinant of all downstream phenotypes. The aim of this review is not to claim that this feedback loop has been fully established, but to summarize the strength of evidence for each step, identify current evidence gaps, and propose a testable mechanistic framework for future studies (Fig. 1).

Fundamental basis of exosomal miR-146a communication

Exosomal loading of miR-146a

To further understand the communication machinery between Tregs and CAFs, increasing attention has been directed toward miR-146a, a molecule closely associated with Treg stability and immunosuppressive function. Within the landscape of Foxp3+ Treg biology, miR-146a has been recognized as an important regulator of Treg functional maintenance [11, 16]. Physiologically, high intracellular levels of miR-146a can constrain STAT1-mediated Th1 responses, a mechanism that may help prevent Tregs from acquiring effector-like traits or undergoing lineage drift under inflammatory stimulation [11]. This intrinsic stability is considered an important component of systemic immune tolerance.

Evidence from high-throughput sequencing and exosomal profiling suggests that miR-146a does not function solely as an intracellular regulator within the Treg cytoplasm, but can also be enriched in exosomes secreted by Tregs [11, 17]. This enrichment should not be simply interpreted as random diffusion, but is more likely to involve regulated RNA sorting, inward budding of endosomal membranes, and vesicular trafficking. First, the entry of miR-146a into exosomes may depend on RNA-binding protein-mediated cargo selection. RNA-binding proteins such as hnRNPA2B1 can recognize specific miRNA motifs and facilitate the association of corresponding miRNAs with exosomal loading complexes, thereby increasing their probability of incorporation into intraluminal vesicles [17, 18]. Therefore, the “specificity” of miR-146a loading is more likely to arise initially from the recognition between RNA-binding proteins and miRNA sequences or modification states, rather than from direct recognition of miR-146a by endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT) or Rab proteins themselves.

Second, ESCRT-related sorting mechanisms may provide the structural basis for the entry of these RNA–protein complexes into exosomes. ESCRT-0, ESCRT-I, ESCRT-II, and ESCRT-III can sequentially participate in endosomal cargo clustering, membrane invagination, neck constriction, and intraluminal vesicle formation, allowing some RNA–protein complexes to be packaged into intraluminal vesicles within multivesicular bodies (MVBs) that may subsequently be released as exosomes [18]. Therefore, ESCRT is better understood as an endosomal sorting platform that influences exosome biogenesis and cargo-packaging efficiency, rather than as a molecular recognizer that independently determines miR-146a sequence selectivity. In parallel, Rab GTPases, especially Rab27a/Rab27b, Rab11, and Rab35, mainly regulate the transport of MVBs toward the plasma membrane, vesicle docking, and fusion with the plasma membrane, thereby influencing the amount of Treg-derived exosome release and the final output efficiency of miR-146a cargo [18, 56, 72].

Therefore, in the model proposed in this review, the generation of Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a can be understood as the result of multiple coordinated steps: RNA-binding proteins participate in the selective enrichment of miR-146a, ESCRT-related mechanisms promote intraluminal vesicle formation and cargo packaging, and Rab proteins control vesicle transport, docking, and release. It should be emphasized that these mechanisms currently mainly support the molecular basis by which miR-146a can be selectively enriched and released with Treg-derived exosomes. However, whether ESCRT components or Rab proteins directly and specifically determine miR-146a loading in Tregs remains insufficiently supported by cell-type-specific evidence. Furthermore, whether these exosomes are preferentially taken up by CAFs in the in situ tumor environment and produce functional consequences still needs to be validated by subsequent experiments involving Treg-specific Rab/ESCRT intervention, miR-146a cargo quantification, and CAF uptake-tracing assays.

Directional delivery dependent on immune-stromal synapses

Although molecular loading provides the mechanistic basis for exosomal signaling, the efficiency of signal transmission within the complex TME is also shaped by spatial architecture. Compared with soluble endocrine-like signals that can diffuse over longer distances or depend on systemic circulation, exosome-mediated communication is generally more dependent on local cell proximity, with delivery efficiency likely increasing as the distance between donor and recipient cells decreases. This spatial constraint suggests that the efficient operation of the proposed “Treg → exosomal miR-146a → CAF → Treg” communication model may require close apposition between these two cell populations. At the invasive fronts of malignancies such as hepatocellular carcinoma and breast cancer, Foxp3+ Tregs do not appear to be randomly distributed; instead, they preferentially localize within microdomains less than 20 μm from α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA)+ fibroblasts [5]. This spatial association is more pronounced than the distances observed between Tregs and other immune cell populations, such as CD8+ T cells or natural killer (NK) cells [19], suggesting a potential physical basis for local vesicle transfer.

This co-localization may represent more than a simple geographic association and may contribute to the formation of a functional immune–stromal synapse [5]. High ICAM-1 expression on activated CAFs, together with its interaction with LFA-1 on the Treg surface, may facilitate the assembly of a relatively stable and spatially confined synaptic interface [5]. Within this local microenvironment, Treg-derived exosomes may be partially protected from interstitial fluid dilution or enzymatic degradation, allowing them to interact with the CAF membrane at higher local concentrations [21]. After contact, CAFs can take up Treg-derived exosomes through endocytosis or membrane fusion, allowing their cargoes to enter the cytoplasm of recipient cells [14]. Therefore, the main significance of the immune–stromal synapse lies in enhancing local delivery efficiency, rather than independently determining the final phenotype of CAFs. The final CAF phenotype still depends on the baseline state and origin of recipient fibroblasts, tumor type, and coexisting cytokine milieu [22].

Signaling regulation of CAF response states

Proximal targeting of TRAF6/IRAK1

In examining how Treg-derived exosomes influence stromal cells within the tumor microenvironment, available studies suggest that miR-146a transferred to recipient CAFs may not exert broad nonspecific effects. Rather, this miRNA may function as a sequence-guided post-transcriptional regulator by recognizing complementary sites within the 3′ untranslated regions (3′ UTRs) of IRAK1 and TRAF6 mRNAs [23]. Such sequence-specific interactions may facilitate the recruitment of Argonaute proteins and the assembly of the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC). This process can promote deadenylation and subsequent degradation of target transcripts, thereby reducing the post-transcriptional expression of these adaptor proteins in a context- and dose-dependent manner [24].

TRAF6 and IRAK1 are proximal signaling nodes in Toll-like receptor and IL-1 receptor pathways, where they participate in the relay of extracellular inflammatory cues to MyD88-dependent K63-linked ubiquitination events [25]. miR-146a-mediated suppression of TRAF6/IRAK1 could therefore dampen this cascade at an upstream signaling level. This alteration may reduce the responsiveness of CAFs to inflammatory stimuli prevalent in the tumor milieu, such as IL-1β, thereby attenuating responses to typical pro-inflammatory cues. At the same time, reduced TRAF6/IRAK1 signaling may weaken the sustained NF-κB activity required to maintain an inflammatory CAF state (iCAF), although whether this shift further promotes immunosuppressive or matrix-remodeling CAF features depends on tumor context and additional signaling inputs [15, 44, 45, 54]. Because much of the supporting evidence derives from cell-line models, this signaling attenuation should be interpreted as a potential upstream regulatory event rather than as direct proof of a complete CAF phenotypic transition [28].

These findings suggest that the regulation of TRAF6/IRAK1 by miR-146a should first be understood at the level of proximal targeting, rather than being directly equated with terminal phenotypic changes in CAFs [25]. At this level, downregulation of TRAF6/IRAK1 may initially alter the response threshold of CAFs to inflammatory stimuli such as IL-1β; subsequently, reduced NF-κB activity may weaken the sustained pro-inflammatory signaling required to maintain an inflammatory CAF state [15, 44, 45]. On this basis, whether CAFs further exhibit phenotypic or secretory changes involving α-SMA, FAP, IL-6, CXCL12, or TGF-β remains subject to the combined regulation of STAT3 activity, the local cytokine milieu, and stromal states across different tumor types [54]. Therefore, a more reasonable causal sequence should be “miR-146a targeting of TRAF6/IRAK1 → altered inflammatory signaling threshold → rebalancing of NF-κB/STAT3 pathways → changes in CAF functional outputs”, rather than viewing all pathway and phenotypic changes as synchronous and inevitable consequences of miR-146a activity [1, 26, 28].

Rebalancing of the NF-κB–STAT3 pathway

After attenuation of proximal signaling nodes, the transcriptional landscape in CAFs does not simply shut down. Instead, available data suggest a reconfiguration of pathway activity, characterized by reduced NF-κB responsiveness and relatively enhanced JAK2/STAT3 signaling under certain contexts. Suppression of TRAF6 may reduce IκBα phosphorylation and degradation, thereby limiting nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity of the p65 subunit. This change may help explain the reduced expression of genes associated with inflammatory CAF subsets, including Cxcl1 and Il6 [29]. Although much of this evidence derives from manipulated cell-line models, diminished NF-κB activity may impair the maintenance of an inflammatory fibroblast state [46], raising the possibility that miR-146a-exposed fibroblasts may become less responsive to pro-inflammatory cues.

This signaling rebalance can be understood at several interconnected biochemical levels. First, after exosomes are taken up by CAFs, the miR-146a cargo must enter the cytoplasm of recipient cells and mediate post-transcriptional repression of target mRNAs through the Argonaute/RISC complex. In addition to the proximal inflammatory signaling nodes TRAF6/IRAK1, existing studies suggest that miR-146a-related signaling may also affect SOCS1 levels [12, 14]. Under physiological conditions, SOCS1 can restrict JAK kinase activity; therefore, reduced SOCS1 may weaken the negative regulation of JAK2, making JAK2 phosphorylation and subsequent STAT3 phosphorylation more likely to occur [14]. In some fibroblast models, treatment with miR-146a-related exosomes or miRNA mimics has been associated with increased JAK2 phosphorylation, an elevated p-STAT3/STAT3 ratio, and changes in NF-κB/p65 activity. These effects can be attenuated by corresponding pathway inhibitors, suggesting that JAK2–STAT3 and NF-κB-related signaling are at least involved in fibroblast activation [31].

From the perspective of functional outputs, enhanced STAT3 activity should not be equated with the immediate formation of a stable myCAF phenotype, but is more likely to represent an intermediate step in the alteration of CAF response states. STAT3-related transcriptional outputs may be associated with stromal remodeling and secretory programs involving α-SMA, FAP, type I collagen, CXCL12, or IL-6, thereby supporting myCAF-like remodeling, chemokine release, and local immune regulation [3, 26, 52]. However, this process remains highly context-dependent. Current supportive evidence linking JAK2–STAT3 activation to myCAF-like remodeling is mainly derived from miRNA mimics, overexpression systems, cell lines, or non-Treg-derived exosome models, rather than from direct tracing validation of Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a acting on primary CAFs within the same in vivo system. Therefore, a more cautious interpretation is that Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a may influence CAF response states through nodes such as SOCS1/JAK2–STAT3, but whether it is sufficient to induce stable myCAF polarization still depends on the initial state of recipient CAFs, the local cytokine milieu, and tumor type.

Therefore, proximal inhibition of TRAF6/IRAK1 should not be understood as a simple shutdown of CAF function, nor should NF-κB and JAK2/STAT3 be viewed as two unrelated parallel pathways. A more reasonable hierarchical relationship is that miR-146a-mediated inhibition of TRAF6/IRAK1 first affects the threshold of NF-κB-related inflammatory responses. Under specific cytokine and receptor contexts, changes in regulatory nodes such as SOCS1/ZBTB2 may further relieve constraints on the JAK2/STAT3 axis [14, 47]. Subsequently, enhanced STAT3 activity is more likely to manifest as changes in the CAF secretory profile, stromal remodeling capacity, and immunomodulatory function.

It should be noted that this pathway rebalancing characterized by reduced NF-κB responsiveness and relatively enhanced STAT3 activity does not necessarily occur uniformly across all CAF subsets. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), for example, myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs) generally exhibit stronger α-SMA expression, ECM remodeling, and contractility-related features, whereas inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs) are more biased toward inflammatory and chemokine-secreting states characterized by IL-6 and CXCL12; these subsets differ in spatial localization, cytokine dependence, and functional outputs [45]. Further single-cell studies have also defined antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs) expressing major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC II) and CD74, suggesting that some CAF subsets may possess immunoregulatory or antigen-presentation-related features rather than functioning merely as matrix-depositing cells [81]. In addition, metabolically active CAFs (meCAFs) are associated with distinct stromal densities, immune states, and therapeutic responses in some PDAC samples, indicating that the metabolic background of CAFs may also influence their responses to miR-146a-, lactate-, and STAT3-related signals [92]. Therefore, whether miR-146a-related signaling ultimately induces myCAF-like stromal remodeling, iCAF-like inflammatory or chemokine outputs, apCAF-associated immunoregulation, or meCAF-like metabolic alterations may depend on the initial state of recipient CAFs, their spatial niche, and the local cytokine milieu within tumors. The final outcome may also be jointly regulated by ZBTB2, TXNIP, local cytokine availability, and tumor subtype [47].

Therefore, the NF-κB enhancement observed in some tumor-cell-derived exosomes discussed below should be understood as the result of combined effects of specific donor cells, co-loaded miRNA combinations, and recipient fibroblast states, rather than being directly equated with the direction of action of Treg-derived miR-146a on the TRAF6/IRAK1–NF-κB axis in CAFs.

Stromal barriers and chemokine outputs

It should be noted that the effects of exosomal miR-146a in fibroblasts are not consistent across all models. Some studies have shown that exosomal miR-146a derived from colorectal cancer or breast cancer cells can promote CAF-like activation of fibroblasts, accompanied by changes in JAK2–STAT3- or NF-κB-related signaling [31, 55]. However, these findings cannot be simply extrapolated to suggest that Treg-derived exosomes exert the same effects. Exosomes released by different donor cells may differ not only in miR-146a abundance, but also in their co-loaded miRNAs, proteins, and lipid components, thereby altering the direction and magnitude of responses in recipient fibroblasts [15].

Tumor type itself may also define the boundary conditions of this model. Pan-cancer single-cell analyses have shown that CAFs across different solid tumors share certain common features, while also displaying pronounced tumor type dependence, state plasticity, and differences in intercellular interactions [93]. Single-cell transcriptomic and spatial proteomic studies have further proposed multiple frameworks for classifying CAF phenotypes and have shown that these CAF states differ in their intratumoral spatial distribution and interactions with neighboring cells [94]. In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), functional CAF subtypes can also exhibit different degrees of tumor-protective effects, immune cell recruitment capacity, and associations with therapeutic responses, suggesting that CAF function cannot be summarized using a single “pro-tumor” or “immunosuppressive” label [95]. Therefore, miR-146a-related exosomal effects observed in colorectal cancer, breast cancer, lung cancer, HCC, or PDAC should be interpreted as indirect support related to specific tumor niches, rather than as direct evidence that Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a exerts universally consistent effects on CAFs. Primary CAFs, normal fibroblasts, and cell-line models may also differ in their sensitivity to the same exosomal cargo. Accordingly, findings derived from tumor-cell-derived or other non-Treg-derived exosomes are better regarded as indirect support for the involvement of miR-146a in stromal remodeling, rather than being directly equated with the specific effects of Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a on CAFs [14, 31, 55].

Despite these findings in reductionist systems, the clinical relevance of such mechanisms across diverse tumor types remains less clear. Persistent STAT3 activity in affected fibroblasts may be associated with transcriptional programs involving α-SMA and type I collagen, potentially favoring myofibroblastic CAF features related to extracellular matrix deposition and remodeling [3, 52]. The resulting matrix remodeling and increased stromal stiffness may provide structural support for tumor cell invasion while physically limiting CD8 + T-cell access to the tumor bed [49, 53]. This physical exclusion is consistent with patterns observed in many immune-excluded human cancers, although direct causation from STAT3-driven remodeling in patient samples has not yet been firmly established.

The secretory changes associated with this pathway raise an issue that requires further validation: activated JAK2–STAT3 signaling in fibroblasts may promote the transcription of chemokines and cytokines such as CCL2, CXCL12, and IL-6 [1, 32]. These molecules may form gradients that attract regulatory T cells into stromal regions and support their local expansion. However, this process should not be equated with a fully demonstrated “Treg → CAF → Treg” feedback loop. More cautiously, current preclinical data support a testable hypothesis: exogenous miR-146a-related signals may alter fibroblast states, and these altered fibroblasts may influence Treg distribution through STAT3-related secretory outputs. Whether this hypothesis is consistently present in human tumors, whether it depends on Treg-derived exosomes rather than exosomes from other cellular sources, and whether it can be replaced by compensatory pathways under therapeutic pressure or different stromal compositions remain to be further clarified. The proposed “TRAF6/SOCS1–STAT3” axis provides a plausible explanation for how exogenous miRNAs may alter stromal behavior, but whether it dominates over other regulatory inputs in clinical settings still requires confirmation through primary tumor analyses and functional studies in immunocompetent models.

This model should also be understood in the context of different cancer types and immune infiltration states. In general, immune-hot tumors already contain substantial T-cell infiltration. If the Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a–CAF axis exists in this context, it may be more involved in limiting local inflammatory responses, maintaining the Treg niche, or weakening effector T-cell function. In immune-excluded tumors, T cells are restricted to stromal regions or tumor margins, and CAF-mediated ECM deposition, TGF-β, CXCL12, and lactate output may make this axis more likely to cooperate with stromal barriers and Treg retention. In immune-cold or immune-desert tumors, however, because overall T-cell and Treg infiltration is low, Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a–CAF communication alone may be insufficient to explain immune escape; instead, insufficient antigen presentation, failed T-cell priming, and myeloid-mediated immunosuppression should also be considered.

At the cancer-type level, gastrointestinal tumors show considerable internal heterogeneity. Microsatellite instability-high (MSI-high) colorectal cancer or some inflammation-associated gastric cancers may display strong immune infiltration, whereas microsatellite-stable (MSS) colorectal cancer, PDAC, or stroma-rich gastrointestinal tumors more commonly exhibit immune-excluded or immune-cold features. In the latter contexts, CAF-related CXCL12, TGF-β, stromal deposition, and lactate metabolism may be more closely associated with Treg retention and effector T-cell exclusion, although these observations still cannot be directly equated with a complete feedback loop driven by Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a [31, 55, 98]. Melanoma is generally regarded as one of the tumor types more prone to immune-hot features, but its CAF subsets and spatial architecture can still influence immune-cell entry and activation. Single-cell and spatial analyses have shown that mCAF and iCAF subsets in melanoma and other skin tumors can regulate the tumor immune microenvironment through matrix encasement or cytokine/chemokine outputs, respectively [91]. Ovarian cancer, especially high-grade serous ovarian cancer, often exhibits marked stromal heterogeneity. CAF-S1 and CXCL12β-related signals are associated with immunosuppressive Treg niches, suggesting that CAF-to-Treg support may be more prominent in immune-excluded or stroma-rich contexts [100]. Lung cancer also exhibits pronounced immune heterogeneity, ranging from immune-hot states with high T-cell infiltration and strong PD-L1 expression to immune-excluded or cold states involving CAF-, TGF-β-, and ECM-related signals. Therefore, the Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a–CAF axis is more likely to represent a candidate mechanism within specific spatial niches in lung cancer, rather than a shared feature of all NSCLC samples [95].

CAF-to-Treg reciprocal support network

When discussing the reciprocal support of Tregs by CAFs, it should be noted that the existing evidence does not all derive from a single complete in vivo model of “Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a → CAF → Treg” feedback. More precisely, related studies suggest the possibility of this reciprocal framework from different perspectives. On the one hand, CAF-derived chemokines and cytokines may be associated with Treg recruitment, local maintenance, and functional regulation [1, 19, 32]. On the other hand, glycolytic reprogramming and lactate efflux in CAFs may also alter the metabolic adaptability of Tregs under hypoxic conditions and nutrient competition [33, 34]. Together, these clues support a testable possibility that, within specific tumor niches, certain CAF subsets may provide reciprocal support for the Treg niche through secretory and metabolic outputs. However, direct evidence remains lacking in the same immunocompetent in vivo system to demonstrate that Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a first alters CAFs, after which these CAFs reciprocally stabilize Tregs and further promote miR-146a-containing exosome release. Therefore, this section discusses this process as a reciprocal support framework that requires further validation, rather than as a fully established closed feedback mechanism.

Lactate-mediated metabolic support

miR-146a-associated CAF remodeling may not be a completely unidirectional process. Existing studies suggest that the secretory state of CAFs is associated with local Treg accumulation, survival, and functional maintenance, in which chemokines and cytokines may participate in Treg recruitment and local expansion [1, 19, 32]. On the other hand, the metabolic state of CAFs may also influence the adaptability of Tregs under hypoxic and nutrient-competitive conditions through lactate production and efflux [33, 34]. However, this reciprocal support should not be understood as a shared property of all CAF subsets. Only CAF subsets with specific secretory profiles, glycolytic states, or spatial locations may provide stable support for the Treg niche in particular tumor contexts. Based on these findings, a reciprocal supportive relationship may exist between Tregs and CAFs, but it should not yet be described as a fully demonstrated stable feedback loop. A more cautious interpretation is that Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a may alter the response states of certain CAFs, whereas CAF-derived lactate, chemokines, and cytokines may reciprocally support the Treg niche in specific microenvironments.

In CAFs exposed to miR-146a, suppression of NF-κB signaling may be accompanied by changes in cellular energy metabolism. These cells may reduce their dependence on oxidative phosphorylation and enhance aerobic glycolysis, thereby releasing lactate through MCT4 [34, 35]. For Tregs, lactate is not merely a metabolic waste product. Functional studies have shown that tumor-infiltrating Tregs can take up and utilize lactate through MCT1 to support their metabolic adaptation, proliferation, and suppressive function in glucose-restricted, hypoxic, and acidic TMEs. Treg-specific interference with MCT1 impairs intratumoral Treg adaptation and can enhance antitumor immunotherapeutic responses [96]. Therefore, the relationship between lactate and Treg stability is not merely based on spatial coexistence or expression correlation, but is supported by functional evidence to some extent.

A potentially deeper consequence involves epigenetic regulation, but this layer should be distinguished from the metabolic effects that have already received more direct functional support. Beyond its role as a metabolic substrate, lactate-related signaling may influence Treg-associated transcriptional programs and immunosuppressive function through histone lactylation or non-histone lactylation [41, 83]. These findings provide important support for the possibility that lactate may participate in Treg stability, but they do not directly demonstrate that myCAF-derived lactate induces histone lactylation in Tregs in vivo.

Therefore, clear evidence boundaries should be maintained for the lactate–histone lactylation axis. First, the effects of lactate on Tregs may be jointly shaped by the degree of hypoxia, glucose competition, fatty acid oxidation status, and the local cytokine milieu. Second, direct evidence remains lacking to demonstrate that “myCAF-derived lactate specifically enters Tregs and stabilizes their immunosuppressive identity through Foxp3-associated histone lactylation.” More importantly, there is currently no direct evidence showing that histone lactylation within Tregs itself can drive exosome biogenesis, miR-146a loading, or increased release of Treg-derived exosomes. Exosome production and cargo sorting are more likely to be regulated by multilayered mechanisms involving ESCRT components, Rab GTPases, nSMase2, RNA-binding proteins, and cellular activation states [10, 17, 18, 27]. Therefore, the chain of “myCAF-derived lactate → Treg lactylation → exosomal miR-146a secretion” should currently be regarded as a hypothesis requiring validation, rather than as a directly demonstrated mechanistic fact.

Chemokine and cytokine support

In addition to this metabolic exchange, miR-146a-associated CAF remodeling may also influence the immune landscape through changes in CAF secretory profiles [48]. Suppression of NF-κB signaling in these cells does not necessarily abolish secretion; rather, it may shift the balance of secretory outputs under specific tumor contexts [14, 38]. In preclinical systems, certain CAF subsets have been associated with the release of chemokines such as CCL2, CCL5, and CXCL12, which may contribute to chemotactic gradients that recruit naive CD4 + T cells or preexisting Tregs into stromal regions of the TME [1, 15, 39].

Once recruited into this niche, T cells may encounter CAF-derived cytokines and immunomodulatory factors such as TGF-β, IL-6, and VEGF-A. This local milieu may support the conversion of naive CD4 + T cells into Foxp3 + Tregs and enhance the suppressive capacity or local persistence of established Tregs [1, 40]. Direct cell contact may further strengthen this interaction: ICAM-1 on activated CAFs can engage LFA-1 on Tregs, thereby facilitating stable immune–stromal interactions that may reinforce local regulatory signaling [5].

Overall, existing studies support the view that CAFs can participate in shaping a local environment favorable for Treg recruitment and functional maintenance by secreting TGF-β, IL-6, VEGF-A, and various chemokines [1, 11, 39]. Compared with simple spatial correlation, some studies have provided evidence closer to functional causality. For example, in breast cancer, the CAF-S1 subset is closely associated with an immunosuppressive microenvironment and can influence the attraction, retention, and Treg differentiation of CD4 + CD25+ T cells through molecules such as CXCL12, CD73, B7-H3, and DPP4 [97]. Another study in pancreatic cancer showed that mesothelial cell-derived apCAFs can directly induce the conversion of naive CD4 + T cells into Tregs, whereas blockade of mesothelin reduces apCAF formation and the associated induction of Tregs [98]. These studies indicate that specific CAF subsets can functionally shape the Treg niche through chemotactic, contact-dependent, or antigen-presentation-like mechanisms. In addition, an IL-6–adenosine positive feedback loop between CD73 + γδTregs and CAFs has been reported in human breast cancer, suggesting that regulatory T-cell populations and CAFs can form context-dependent reciprocal circuits through cytokine and metabolic pathways [36].

However, these findings still need to be interpreted within appropriate boundaries. First, the above studies involve specific CAF subsets such as CAF-S1 or apCAFs, and therefore should not be equated with the idea that all myCAFs possess the same capacity for Treg recruitment or stabilization. Second, these mechanisms mainly involve soluble factors, metabolic outputs, cell contact, or antigen-presentation-like interactions, rather than the delivery of exosomal miR-146a cargo itself. Third, they support the biological plausibility of CAF-to-Treg reciprocal support, but do not by themselves prove that Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a has established a complete closed feedback loop through CAFs.

Therefore, a more cautious interpretation is that Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a may influence the response states of certain CAFs, whereas CAF-derived cytokines, chemokines, lactate, and contact-dependent signals may participate in Treg recruitment, local maintenance, and functional regulation within specific microenvironments. These two modes of communication may cooperate spatially, but they differ in their evidence sources and mechanistic levels. In particular, whether CAF-supported Tregs further enhance exosome release or maintain miR-146a-related communication remains largely inferential at present [10, 27]. Future studies should integrate cell tracing, conditional CAF/Treg blockade, intervention of chemokine or lactate pathways, and immunocompetent models to determine whether CAF-derived signals truly mediate Treg recruitment and stabilization, or merely represent one component of multiple immunosuppressive mechanisms [1, 14, 39, 40].

The Treg–CAF–Tumor triadic network

This Treg-CAF interaction rarely confines itself to a simple two-cell dialogue. Data from several tumor models indicate that metabolites and signals released in this exchange frequently reach neighboring malignant cells, integrating them into a larger circuit that reinforces progression [30]. Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a, while clearly implicated in CAF reprogramming, may also reach tumor cells, though direct transfer from Tregs remains poorly documented in primary tissues.

The targets of miR-146a differ markedly between cell types. In tumor cells, available evidence—mostly from exosomes of tumor or stromal origin—points to TXNIP as a primary target. Suppression of TXNIP disinhibits Wnt/β-catenin signaling, promoting β-catenin translocation to the nucleus and activation of an epithelial-mesenchymal transition program that reduces E-cadherin while increasing vimentin expression, thereby enhancing motility and invasiveness [37]. Reports associate miR-146a enrichment with reduced NUMB expression, which stabilizes Notch signaling and may preserve cancer stem cell traits linked to chemoresistance and relapse [20]. The degree to which Treg exosomes specifically supply the miR-146a responsible for these shifts in patient tumors, as opposed to contributions from malignant cells themselves, is uncertain.

Metabolic output from activated CAFs likely extends the reach of this axis. High glycolytic activity in miR-146a-exposed myCAFs generates substantial lactate efflux into the surrounding milieu [34]. Tumor cells that import this lactate can use it not only for energy but also as a substrate for histone lactylation [41]. Recent work suggests that such modifications, particularly at H3K18, accumulate at promoters of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and certain angiogenesis genes, driving PD-L1 upregulation and potentially strengthening checkpoint-mediated escape [42]. Lactate has further been tied to vasculogenic mimicry, supporting metastatic dissemination in hypoxic or nutrient-stressed regions [43].

Whether this sequence—from Treg exosome delivery to CAF metabolic shift and subsequent tumor cell adaptation—represents a primary driver of resistance in human cancers remains an open question. The clinical failure of isolated checkpoint blockade or tumor-directed therapy often correlates with dense stromal barriers and metabolic reprogramming, yet multiple redundant circuits typically operate in parallel [40]. Establishing causality will require tools that trace miR-146a flux across cell types in situ and test whether selective interruption of Treg-CAF exchange alters tumor phenotype without simply revealing compensatory mechanisms.

Layered validation and potential intervention directions

Broad depletion of Tregs or CAFs often shows limited efficacy in clinical settings. Although systemic removal of Tregs can enhance effector immune responses in some models, it may also disrupt immune tolerance and increase the risk of autoimmunity [2]. Similarly, indiscriminate depletion of CAFs has clear limitations, because certain inflammatory or state-specific fibroblast subsets may exert tumor-restraining or immune-supportive functions [71]. Therefore, this section does not present these interventions as mature therapeutic strategies, but instead considers them as validation approaches for dissecting key steps in the Treg–CAF communication model [1, 3, 40].

In relation to the model proposed in this review, different strategies are positioned at different distances from the core mechanism. GW4869 and Rab/SNARE-related interventions are mainly useful for determining whether exosome release participates in this process. Anti-miR-146a approaches or miR-146a-deficient models can be used to test whether the miR-146a cargo itself is functionally required. STAT3 inhibitors and MCT1/4 inhibitors are more suitable for evaluating whether CAF secretory profiles, stromal states, and lactate metabolism constitute downstream functional outputs. It should be emphasized that most of these strategies are not specific interventions targeting Treg-derived miR-146a or CAFs. Their main value lies in helping to establish a validation sequence and potential combination directions, rather than being directly equated with clinically mature targeted therapies.

We reframe these intervention nodes as proof-of-concept validation strategies rather than as a mature therapeutic workflow. Source-specific controls, miR-146a knockout, and GW4869-mediated blockade can be used to examine differences in exosome origin, the functional necessity of miR-146a cargo, and the dependence on exosome release, respectively (Fig. 3). Correspondingly, we summarize pharmacological and bioengineering tools that can be used to test key steps of this model, following the logic of “upstream blockade of exosome release → intermediate neutralization of miR-146a cargo → downstream evaluation of STAT3/MCT-related functional outputs” (Table 2).

Blockade of exosome release

The main value of upstream blockade is to test whether Treg–CAF communication depends on exosome release, rather than to directly demonstrate its clinical therapeutic value. Exosome formation and release involve processes such as ESCRT-related sorting, ceramide-dependent changes in membrane curvature, and Rab GTPase-mediated vesicle trafficking [56, 72]. Among these processes, ceramide can promote intraluminal vesicle formation within MVBs, and nSMase2 activity is closely associated with exosome release capacity [57, 73]. Beyond GW4869, Cambinol has also been described as an nSMase2-related inhibitor in preclinical settings, but its relevance to the Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a–CAF axis should be interpreted as indirect support for testing exosome-release dependence rather than as evidence for axis-specific therapeutic blockade [59].

Another parallel direction is to target Rab GTPase-mediated vesicle trafficking. Rab27a/Rab27b can influence the final output of exosomes by regulating MVB transport toward the plasma membrane, membrane docking, and SNARE-related release processes [61]. Existing studies suggest that Rab27a expression is increased in tumor-infiltrating activated Tregs and may be associated with enhanced exosome secretion capacity and immunosuppressive function [62]. Interfering with Rab27a in Tregs can reduce exosome output and, to some extent, restore effector T-cell proliferation or cytotoxic function [11]. In addition, the IL-2/STAT5 axis may influence the secretory state of Tregs by regulating Rab27a transcription, providing another potential route for upstream regulation of exosome release [63].

However, systemic inhibition of exosome biogenesis or release itself carries clear risks. Exosomes are not communication tools exclusive to tumor cells or Tregs, and nSMase2-, ESCRT-, Rab GTPase-, and SNARE-related pathways also participate in vesicle exchange among normal immune cells, endothelial cells, stromal cells, and tissue-repair processes [56, 72]. Therefore, broad blockade of exosome release may simultaneously affect antigen presentation, immune synapse maintenance, regulation of cytokine responses, stromal homeostasis, and metabolic communication, thereby producing unpredictable immune or tissue toxicities. Accordingly, the upstream blockade strategies discussed in this section are more suitable as experimental tools for validating “exosome dependence”, rather than being directly equated with clinically applicable Treg–miR-146a–CAF-specific targeted therapies [57, 58, 73–75]. Future studies need to further define the minimum effective dose, reversibility, dosing schedule, tissue distribution, changes in the exosome profile of non-tumor tissues, and effects on immune homeostasis. Compared with long-term systemic inhibition, local administration, short-term pulsed blockade, Treg- or CAF-targeted delivery, and layered validation combined with exosome source tracing may be more suitable for assessing the safety boundaries of this axis. Other broad exosome-release inhibitors identified through high-throughput screening, such as Climbazole and Neticonazole, may also be useful for testing exosome-release dependence, although their effects should not be interpreted as specific inhibition of Treg-derived miR-146a delivery to CAFs [76].

Neutralization of miR-146a cargo

Inhibition of exosome biogenesis can reduce signal input at the upstream source, but exosomes also mediate essential intercellular exchange under physiological conditions; therefore, complete suppression of secretion may disrupt immune and metabolic homeostasis. In contrast, intermediate interception centered on miR-146a is more suitable for addressing a more specific question: whether the effects of Treg-derived exosomes on CAFs truly depend on miR-146a as the cargo. Antagomirs, LNA-modified antisense oligonucleotides, or engineered decoy exosomes can be used to neutralize miR-146a and to examine whether changes in TRAF6/IRAK1, NF-κB/STAT3-related signaling, and CAF phenotypic outputs are reversed [13, 78]. If miR-146a neutralization attenuates CAF remodeling without markedly altering exosome abundance, this would support the functional necessity of miR-146a cargo in this model. If the effect is limited, this would suggest that other co-loaded miRNAs, protein cargoes, or soluble factors may also participate.

It should be noted that global blockade of miR-146a is not equivalent to simply releasing immunosuppression. miR-146a is also involved in Treg stability, immune tolerance, negative feedback regulation of inflammation, and extracellular RNA-mediated immune stimulatory responses under certain conditions [12, 13, 99]. Therefore, systemic anti-miR-146a intervention may weaken inflammatory braking mechanisms in normal immune cells and increase the risk of autoimmune-like responses, chronic inflammation, or tissue damage. Delivery also remains challenging: antagomirs or LNA-modified antisense oligonucleotides may be limited by plasma nucleases, renal clearance, hepatic and splenic enrichment, insufficient endosomal escape, and blockade by dense ECM. Engineered circRNA sponges, viral vectors, or decoy exosomes also face issues such as vector immunogenicity, controllability of long-term expression, uptake by non-target cells, and off-target binding [66, 78–80]. Therefore, anti-miR-146a approaches are more suitable as tools for validating the functional necessity of miR-146a cargo, whereas clinical translation still requires solutions for cell-type selectivity, local tumor delivery, reversibility, and systemic immune safety.

Progress in antisense oligonucleotide design provides several experimental approaches for targeting intracellular or extracellular miRNAs. Antagomirs and LNA-modified antisense oligonucleotides can bind complementary miRNAs with high affinity and thereby reduce their availability for interaction with target mRNAs or incorporation into functional silencing complexes. Chemical modifications, such as 2′-O-methylation, phosphorothioate backbones, and LNA substitutions, can improve nuclease resistance and binding stability, which may be advantageous in the protease- and nuclease-rich tumor microenvironment [13]. In experimental models of Treg–CAF interaction, systemic or intratumoral administration of anti-miR-146a oligonucleotides could be used to test whether neutralizing extracellularly delivered miR-146a attenuates CAF responses. After Treg-derived exosomes are taken up by CAFs, cytoplasmic antagomirs may sequester released miR-146a before or during RISC engagement [14]. If this intervention preserves TRAF6 and IRAK1 expression, maintains NF-κB responsiveness, or weakens CAF-like remodeling, it would support the functional involvement of miR-146a cargo in this model. However, such findings would still need to be interpreted as evidence for cargo dependence rather than as proof of a clinically actionable therapeutic strategy. Evidence from non-oncological inflammatory models suggests that exosome-mediated anti-miRNA delivery can interfere with intercellular miRNA transfer and restore target-gene expression in recipient cells [65]. Nevertheless, whether this approach can be efficiently translated to solid tumors remains uncertain, given CAF heterogeneity, dense extracellular matrix, rapid nuclease activity, variable endosomal escape, and heterogeneous uptake by stromal and immune cells. Although clinical experience with LNA-based anti-miRNA agents in other disease contexts provides useful technical precedent, it does not directly establish the safety or efficacy of anti-miR-146a strategies in the Treg–CAF axis of solid tumors [13].

Beyond synthetic oligonucleotides, bioengineered approaches based on molecular sponge principles may also be useful for model validation. Circular RNAs are intrinsically resistant to exonuclease degradation because of their covalently closed structure and can function as miRNA decoys when engineered to contain multiple binding sites. Engineered circRNAs carrying repeated high-affinity binding sequences for miR-146a could theoretically serve as durable intracellular sinks. Delivery of circRNA-encoding constructs to CAFs through viral vectors or lipid nanoparticles may help test whether persistent miR-146a neutralization alters CAF responses under continuous Treg-derived exosomal input [66, 79]. However, vector immunogenicity, incomplete delivery in fibrotic stroma, long-term expression control, and off-target binding remain important concerns. A complementary extracellular strategy involves engineered decoy exosomes. Donor cells such as dendritic cells or mesenchymal stromal cells could be engineered to release exosomes enriched with anti-miR-146a payloads or RNA-binding components, allowing competitive interception of miR-146a-containing vesicles in the interstitial space [67, 80]. These decoys may help distinguish extracellular cargo neutralization from global blockade of exosome biogenesis, but their specificity, biodistribution, and uptake by non-target cells require careful evaluation. Upstream cargo-loading regulation may provide another validation layer: transcriptional or RNA-sorting regulators implicated in miR-146a packaging could be manipulated to test whether reduced miR-146a loading alters downstream CAF responses [68]. A key unresolved question is whether combining cargo-specific neutralization with upstream modulation of cargo loading is superior to single-layer blockade for validating this axis without compromising systemic immune tolerance. Overall, selective miR-146a targeting, whether intracellular or extracellular, may provide a more mechanism-focused validation strategy than broad exosome inhibition, but clinical validation in solid tumors remains limited and will require careful assessment of delivery efficiency, tumor selectivity, compatibility with checkpoint blockade, and long-term effects on tumor-associated inflammation.

Functional validation of STAT3/MCT-related outputs

Even if some miR-146a escapes upstream blockade and intermediate interception and enters CAFs, downstream functional outputs can still be targeted to test and potentially weaken the Treg–CAF interaction network. Based on the model discussed above, two categories of downstream nodes deserve particular attention. The first is JAK2/STAT3-related transcriptional output, which can be used to examine whether CAF secretory profiles and stromal states depend on this transcriptional layer. The second is MCT1/4-mediated lactate exchange and metabolic coupling [14, 33–35], which can be used to test whether lactate exchange between CAFs and Tregs participates in the metabolic feedback layer [1, 32, 42, 82]. The former corresponds to “CAF functional outputs”, whereas the latter corresponds to the feedback component of “CAF reciprocal support for Tregs”. It should be emphasized that whether miR-146a entry into CAFs inevitably leads to NF-κB suppression, STAT3 activation, and enhanced glycolysis may still depend on donor-cell source, recipient CAF state, and the local cytokine milieu. Therefore, downstream decoupling strategies are more suitable as candidate tools for validating different mechanistic layers, rather than as definitive therapies that have already been proven to completely block the Treg–CAF feedback loop [14, 33–35].

Strategies aimed at correcting signaling imbalance mainly focus on inhibiting aberrantly activated STAT3 signaling. In the mechanistic model proposed in this review, miR-146a may alter the balance between NF-κB and JAK2–STAT3 signaling in CAFs through TRAF6/IRAK1- or SOCS1-related regulation [14]. Activated p-STAT3 can act as a transcription factor to promote the expression of chemokines and cytokines such as CCL2, CXCL12, and IL-6, thereby supporting Treg recruitment and local maintenance. Small-molecule STAT3 inhibitors such as Stattic or Napabucasin can therefore be used to test the causality of this step. Stattic can bind to the SH2 domain of STAT3 and inhibit its phosphorylation, dimerization, and nuclear transcriptional function. If Stattic treatment reduces markers such as α-SMA, IL-6, TGF-β, or CXCL12 in CAFs and weakens the ability of CAFs to recruit or maintain Tregs, this would support the role of STAT3 as a downstream functional output node in this model [69, 81]. However, the antitumor activity of STAT3 inhibitors such as Napabucasin in colorectal cancer and pancreatic cancer more broadly reflects the druggability of STAT3 as a widespread tumor and stromal signaling node, rather than directly proving that these agents specifically block the Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a–CAF axis. Therefore, overly strong terms such as “specific blockade” or “breaking the malignant cycle” should be avoided here; instead, the validation value and potential significance for combination therapy should be emphasized [14, 69, 81].

The main value of downstream intervention lies in testing whether CAF functional outputs and reciprocal Treg support depend on specific signaling or metabolic components. At the signaling level, STAT3 inhibitors can be used to evaluate whether CAF secretory profiles, stromal states, and chemokine outputs depend on JAK2/STAT3-related transcriptional programs [69, 81]. At the metabolic level, lactate-related coupling may exist between CAFs and Tregs: remodeled CAFs can export lactate through MCT4, whereas Tregs can take up lactate through MCT1 and use it to maintain metabolic adaptation and suppressive function under hypoxic or glucose-competitive conditions [33, 34, 42]. MCT1 inhibitors such as AZD3965 can be used to test the dependence of Tregs on lactate uptake, whereas MCT4-related interventions can be used to assess whether lactate efflux from CAFs participates in Treg niche maintenance [82, 84]. However, existing evidence does not prove that “myCAF-derived lactate inevitably induces histone lactylation in Tregs and further drives secretion of Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a”; the link between lactylation in the Foxp3 promoter region and Treg stability still requires further validation within this specific axis [41, 83]. Therefore, STAT3- and MCT1/4-related interventions should be positioned as functional validation tools and potential combination-therapy candidates for dissecting key steps of the model, rather than being described as strategies capable of “complete decoupling” or “full reversal” of the Treg–CAF network.

Combination strategies and validation boundaries

Although interference with the Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a–CAF axis may provide theoretical support for enhancing immune checkpoint blockade or chemotherapy sensitivity, this interpretation should remain cautious. Current studies more strongly support associations among exosomal signaling, CAF-mediated stromal barriers, PD-L1-related immune escape, and therapeutic resistance, rather than directly proving that blockade of Treg-derived miR-146a is sufficient to convert immune-excluded tumors into inflamed tumors [64]. Similarly, in models such as small-cell lung cancer and colorectal cancer, combined treatment with GW4869 and platinum-based drugs can enhance chemosensitivity. However, these findings mainly reflect the relationship between general exosome blockade and chemoresistance, and cannot be directly extrapolated as specific therapeutic evidence for the Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a–CAF axis [58].

Therefore, combination with immunotherapy or chemotherapy sensitization is better positioned as a subsequent evaluation direction after completion of mechanistic validation, rather than as a direct clinical extension of the current model. Future studies need to simultaneously assess exosome release, miR-146a cargo, CAF phenotypic outputs, changes in the Treg niche, and effector T-cell infiltration in immunocompetent models, while also incorporating more refined delivery platforms and candidate biomarkers such as circulating exosomal miR-146a to explore suitable patient populations [70, 85]. Only after the causality, specificity, and safety boundaries of this axis have been confirmed should related interventions proceed to research stages closer to clinical translation. Conclusions and future perspectives

Immunosuppressive imbalance within the TME is an important factor limiting the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy, and the interaction between Tregs and CAFs may represent an important component in maintaining immune exclusion, stromal remodeling, and therapeutic resistance. Centered on Treg-derived exosomal miR-146a, this review proposes a bidirectional “Treg–exosomal miR-146a–CAF” communication working model to integrate Treg exosome release, miR-146a-mediated immune regulation, changes in CAF response states, and the potential reciprocal support of the Treg niche by CAFs. It should be emphasized that this model is currently better understood as a layered hypothetical framework, rather than as a fully demonstrated causal feedback loop in vivo.

Existing evidence provides relatively strong support for the ability of Tregs to release miR-146a-enriched exosomes, for the involvement of miR-146a in Treg functional maintenance and inflammatory signaling regulation, and for its capacity to influence related nodes such as TRAF6/IRAK1, SOCS1/JAK2–STAT3, and NF-κB in different models. Meanwhile, CAFs can affect the local immune state through stromal deposition, chemokines, cytokines, and metabolic products such as lactate. However, direct and real-time in vivo evidence remains lacking for the complete process in which miR-146a is directionally transferred from Tregs to CAFs, induces remodeling of specific CAF subsets, and further reciprocally promotes Treg stability and exosome release. Therefore, the main value of this axis lies in providing a research framework that can be experimentally dissected and tested in future studies, rather than in offering a definitive mechanistic basis for current clinical intervention.

Future studies need to address several key questions. Do Treg-derived exosomes have distinguishable recipient preferences compared with tumor-cell- or CAF-derived exosomes? Do different CAF subsets respond consistently to miR-146a-related signals? Are CAF-derived lactate, chemokines, and cytokines sufficient to maintain the Treg niche? Can blockade of this axis improve antitumor immunity without disrupting normal immune homeostasis? The applicability of this model may also be jointly limited by CAF subsets, tumor type, and immune-hot or immune-cold context. myCAF-dominated environments with high stromal deposition, iCAF-dominated inflammatory and chemokine-rich environments, apCAF-associated immunoregulatory environments, and meCAF-related metabolically active environments may provide distinct recipient backgrounds and functional outputs for miR-146a-related signaling. At the cancer-type level, tumors such as PDAC, breast cancer, NSCLC, HCC, and colorectal cancer may differ in stromal density, immune state, degree of hypoxia, and composition of exosome sources. Therefore, this model should not be assumed to operate in the same manner across all solid tumors.

At the translational level, upstream blockade of exosome biogenesis, intermediate interception of miR-146a, and downstream intervention of STAT3/MCT-related outputs are better positioned as candidate strategies for model validation and exploration of combination therapies, rather than as definitive therapeutic frameworks with sufficient clinical evidence. Given that miR-146a is also involved in immune tolerance, inflammatory regulation, and Treg stability, systemic inhibition of exosome release or global blockade of miR-146a may lead to unintended immune consequences. Therefore, future studies should systematically evaluate the toxicity, tissue specificity, delivery efficiency, and immune-homeostasis risks of exosome biogenesis inhibitors, miR-146a antagomirs, or other nucleic acid delivery strategies. In addition, the value of plasma exosomal miR-146a as a companion diagnostic biomarker remains worth exploring, but whether it can distinguish signals derived from Tregs, CAFs, or tumor cells still requires further validation. Only after these issues of causality, specificity, and safety have been resolved can the Treg–exosomal miR-146a–CAF axis potentially develop from an attractive mechanistic model into a translational direction for patient stratification, mechanistic validation, and combination-therapy design.
