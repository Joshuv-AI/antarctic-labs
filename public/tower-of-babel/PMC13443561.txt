Stable readout of visual representations mediates flexible generalization
Authors: Ramanujan Srinath, Martyna M Czarnik, Marlene R Cohen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFlexible visually guided behavior requires visual generalization, mapping diverse inputs to a common inference, and behavioral generalization, mapping one inference to multiple actions. Here, we tested whether visual neurons support both by maintaining a relatively fixed mapping between visual information amid many variations in stimuli and actions. We recorded neuronal populations in visual cortex in two male monkeys while they estimated the curvature of random 3D objects and flexibly mapped those judgments to different eye movements. Although visual responses varied substantially across objects, perceptual judgments were best explained by a common, shape-general readout of activity in V1 and V4, rather than by shape-specific strategies. V4 population activity additionally encoded impending saccades while maintaining a stable representation of stimulus curvature, allowing a single perceptual estimate to be linked to multiple behavioral outputs. Together, our findings suggest that visual cortex populations contain the ingredients necessary to implement multiple forms of flexible generalization.Subject terms: Object vision, Perception, Neural decoding, Decision

Introduction

Generalization is at the heart of visually guided behavior. We perform visual generalization every time we ignore the vast amount of irrelevant information bombarding our visual system to identify an object, feature, or location of interest. We perform behavioral generalization every time we use different ways to communicate what we see, whether by reaching for an object with one hand or another, looking at a location of interest, or writing or telling a friend what we saw.

The neurophysiological basis of visual and behavioral generalization has arguably been best studied in the context of visual attention. The two theories guiding most studies (including our own) were that attention performed visual generalization by enhancing the way that task-relevant stimuli and features are encoded in visual cortex1–4 and behavioral generalization by flexibly changing the mapping from visual neurons to those involved in decision-making and motor planning5–7. When we recorded simultaneously from a visual and a decision area while monkeys performed a task that varied spatial attention8,9, we found that neither of those hypotheses accounted for attention-related changes in behavior. Instead, we found that the well-known effects of attention on visual cortical neurons modulated the representation so that a relatively fixed mapping between visual neurons and behavior more efficiently guides the activity of decision neurons and ultimately behavior.

However, spatial attention may be a special case. Neurons that encode the same location are near each other both in the visual cortex and in pre-oculomotor neurons that have been hypothesized to be the source of spatial attention10–12. This topographic organization may allow simple mechanisms, such as feedback from oculomotor neurons to a spatially localized group of visual neurons, to transform visual representations in the desired ways. It is less obvious how the brain accomplishes visual and behavioral generalization when the visual information of interest is not spatially organized. For example, performing visual generalization when estimating a visual feature, like the curvature of stimuli that vary in other irrelevant features, involves neurons throughout a visual cortical area with different receptive fields and tuning for many relevant and irrelevant visual features. And performing behavioral generalization when mapping one inference to many movements involves modulation by premotor or similar processes that might not be organized in the visual cortex. Although the existence of these premotor signals in sensory areas (and vice versa) is now widely appreciated13–17, understanding how they are mixed with sensory signals18,19 and the format of the resultant activity of populations of neurons holds the key to constraining mechanistic hypotheses about how sensory activity in a single area guides behavior20. Specifically for behavioral generalization, the ability to flexibly map a single perceptual estimate onto different actions depending on the behavioral context will involve analyzing the differences between visual representations resulting from different task- or context-dependent inputs to visual cortices.

We reasoned that the flexible roles of a single population of neurons might be easier to identify using a task that incorporates many aspects of flexible behavior that are absent from typical laboratory tasks. We recorded groups of neurons in V1 and V4 while monkeys performed a perceptual estimation task that strongly links neural responses to choices on a continuous scale (Fig. 1A, B). We added perceptual and behavioral complexity in two ways to encourage the monkeys to generalize across visual features and across behavioral plans: (1) the monkeys estimated a visual feature (the medial axis curvature of a 3D shape) while ignoring changes in other features like color, 3D shape, orientation, etc., and (2) the monkeys reported their estimations by making a saccade to a target arc in the upper hemifield which could be displayed in one of several variations.

We tested the hypothesis that the mechanism we identified for spatial attention could account for visual and behavioral generalization in this task, which requires monkeys to flexibly map a rich set of visual stimuli to a continuous behavior where the mapping from a perceptual judgment to eye movement is not fixed. Specifically, we tested whether population activity in the visual cortex contains signals that are organized in a format that could support both visual and behavioral generalization. Importantly, these signals may reflect mixtures of sensory, cognitive, and premotor processes. Our goal was therefore not to isolate purely sensory representations, but to determine whether population activity could support stable readout of perceptual judgments across variations in both visual inputs and behavioral mappings. To test whether the neural representations in the visual cortex could enable visual generalization, we measured the mapping between visual neurons and perceptual judgments amid variation in a task-relevant and many irrelevant visual features. To test whether these representations could support behavioral generalization21,22, we measured decoder performance amid variation in the action used to communicate the perceptual judgment. In both cases, support for generalization would be a stable representation of the relevant visual feature and of the upcoming behavior used to communicate a judgment23–26. Because perceptual judgments are accompanied by changes in attention, working memory, and motor preparation, activity in the visual cortex likely reflects mixtures of these processes. Our goal was therefore to determine whether these mixed signals are organized in a way that allows perceptual judgments and action plans to be read out simultaneously from the population.

Our results demonstrate that both V1 and V4 neurons enable visual generalization: they robustly encode task-relevant and irrelevant visual features, and a stable linear combination of those responses predicts the monkeys’ perceptual judgments across variations in task-irrelevant features. We also show that V4, but not V1, can enable behavioral generalization: representations in V4 simultaneously reflect the perceptual judgment and the direction of the upcoming eye movement. These insights were made possible by three aspects of our experiment design—(1) the multi-parameter random 3D stimuli provided control of task-relevant and irrelevant variance in visual responses which is not possible with simple stimuli like gratings or unparameterized stimuli like natural images, (2) the continuous estimation task (as opposed to a two-alternative forced choice task) provides a rich behavioral output which proved vital for fitting better neural models of behavior, and (3) multi-area population recordings which were necessary to discover alignment of stimulus representations with behavior and saccades. These innovations made it possible to observe that the substrates of visual and behavioral generalization can be found in the format of population representations of neurons in the visual cortex.

Results

Several challenges have limited progress in understanding the neural mechanisms underlying flexible visual and behavioral generalization. First, most research has focused on either stimulus or behavioral variability, but not both. Second, the dominant feedforward models led to a focus on association areas like parietal or premotor regions as the site of flexibility, overlooking the flexibility in the visual cortex. Finally, binary task designs limit the statistical power to differentiate opposing hypotheses.

To address these issues, we designed an experimental paradigm to test the hypothesis that a single population of visual neurons could support both visual and behavioral generalization. Two rhesus monkeys performed a continuous curvature estimation task (Fig. 1A, B) while we recorded neural activity from areas V1 and V4 using chronically implanted multi-electrode arrays (Fig. 2A). After fixating on a central spot, an abstract, randomly generated shape stimulus was presented in the joint receptive fields of the recorded neurons (Fig. 2B). The monkeys indicated their curvature judgment by looking at a corresponding position on an arc. A saccade to the leftmost portion of the arc indicated a straight stimulus, a saccade to the rightmost end corresponded to maximum curvature, and the monkey could indicate any intermediate value in between. The monkeys were rewarded according to their accuracy (see “Methods”).

Stimuli varied across multiple irrelevant features, including cross-sectional shape, aspect ratio, color, 3D rotation, twist along the axis, gloss, and thickness profile (Figs. 1A and S1). In each session, stimuli were drawn from three to seven base shapes, each with 10-20 curvatures. The monkeys generalized across irrelevant feature changes (Figs. 1B and S2), producing similar patterns of errors for familiar and novel shapes (Fig. S2C, D). Their average behavioral errors across shapes were 0.14 and 0.16 for the two monkeys. The monkey’s choices were not driven primarily by the visual similarity to an exemplar curvature value within any shape (Fig. S2E), and the differences in the monkeys’ average behavior across shapes were not driven by the visual similarity across those shapes (Fig. S2F). In some sessions, we randomized the angular position and length of the target arc across trials, requiring monkeys to plan eye movements after the arc was presented flexibly. These manipulations probed the ability of visual neurons to support behavioral generalization. Our experimental design does not fully orthogonalize curvature and saccade direction, and therefore, the signals we observe likely reflect mixtures of sensory and motor-related activity. However, this experimental design enables us to test whether curvature-related and saccade-related information coexist in separable components of the population response.

The responses of V1 and V4 neurons were sensitive to task-relevant and irrelevant features, including curvature, color, and orientation (Fig. 2C, D). Here, we use the term “curvature” as a shorthand for the feature the monkey was rewarded for reporting without trying to quantify or characterize the encoding of the actual medial axis curvature27–29 from the underlying object and image features30,31 that are correlated with curvature in these shapes. In our recordings, curvature selectivity was shape-dependent but did not change with arc onset, size, or location (Fig. S3A, B). Additionally, the representation of curvature quantified using decoding analyses remained stable throughout the trial (Fig. S3C).

A stable weighted combination of V1 and V4 neurons explains perceptual judgments amid task-irrelevant changes

Our visual generalization hypothesis predicts that although task-irrelevant visual features influence visual neuronal responses, the representations must enable the animal to use a stable decision strategy. For example, choices might be based on a stable linear combination of neuronal responses for any stimulus. We tested this prediction by analyzing how V1 and V4 representations of the curvature of different shapes relate to the monkeys’ judgments. In this section, we analyzed simultaneously collected neural data from V1 (69 and 90 multi-units on average from the two monkeys) and V4 (79 and 83 multi-units on average from the two monkeys) from 83 recording sessions (41 for monkey 1 and 42 for monkey 2), during which we tested between 3 and 5 shapes (mean 3.5).

Both V1 and V4 represent curvature in ways that differ strikingly across shapes. In both areas, population responses to shapes that vary from straight to curvy trace smooth, uninterrupted paths in neural population space where each dimension is the firing rate of one neuron (as depicted in representative sessions in Figs. 2E and S4). We estimated curvature using a linear combination of neurons fit for each shape and quantified this “shape-specific” decoder performance as the correlation between the predicted and actual curvature on held-out trials (Fig. S4A). Strikingly, we were able to decode the veridical curvature of the visual stimulus with very high accuracy using the responses of the limited population in V1 or V4 (Fig. S4D), suggesting that there is more information about curvature in the visual cortex than the monkey is seemingly using to guide choices. As exemplified by the representative sessions, changing the shape (Fig. 2E, F), orientation (Fig. S4B), or color (Fig. S4C) of the stimulus changed the stimulus representations such that different weights best decoded the curvature of shapes with different features. This suggests that these shape-specific decoders are not reliable decoders of curvature itself, but a reflection of the visual variation caused by changes in curvature specific to those shapes.

We further tested the hypothesis that curvature readout cannot rely on projecting responses to novel shapes onto a learned exemplar. We compared the misalignment between neural representations (schematized in Fig. 3A, B) to the monkeys’ highly stereotyped estimation behavior across shapes (Fig. 3C). The misalignment was severe enough that cross-shape decoding (estimating the curvature of one shape using the weighted linear combination of neurons that best-predicted the curvature of a different shape) consistently under-performed within-shape decoding in both V1 and V4 (compare Fig. 3D, E), regardless of whether the shapes varied in overall appearance, orientation only, or color only (Fig. 3F). As a counterpoint, pairs of shapes that varied in color only had a smaller mean difference between within- and cross-shape decoding (MSE = 0.027 vs 0.06) compared to pairs that varied in overall shape (MSE = 0.025 vs 0.166) or orientation only (MSE = 0.025 vs 0.175) which suggests that variation in stimulus color produced representational differences that were largely orthogonal to the curvature representation (see also32,33). Overall, these misalignments suggest that if the monkeys’ shape-agnostic behavior was guided by a shape-specific strategy to readout curvature from V1 and/or V4, they would at the very least need to employ a unique downstream neuron for each shape, each of which computed a differently weighted sum of responses.

We hypothesized that the brain instead employed a shape-general strategy, using a common linear combination of neural responses to estimate the curvature of any stimulus. We first tested whether the accuracy of linear decoders trained on the visual responses to a subset of shapes and tested on held-out shapes scales with the number of unique shapes in the training set. As expected from the cross-decoding analysis above, decoders trained on one shape and tested on the rest performed poorly, but as more shapes were added to the training set, performance improved (Fig. S4F). This indicates that the learned decoder approaches a shape-general curvature decoder with every added shape. To test whether the accuracy of such a shape-general decoder relates to curvature estimation behavior, we trained a decoder by pooling data across all shapes tested in each session to create a shape-general curvature decoder (Fig. S5A; see “Methods”). We compared this to the shape-specific decoder that is trained simply to decode the curvature of one shape. Comparing the curvature decoding performance of the two decoders is tricky. Naturally, the best shape-general curvature decoder trained across all shapes outperforms the shape-specific decoders because it was trained using three to five times as many trials. This is a problem both for comparing curvature decoding performance and for comparing the relationship between each decoding strategy against the monkeys’ choices. When we matched the number of trials used to train the two decoders, the shape-specific decoder unsurprisingly outperformed the general one (Fig. S5G). This difference is expected but problematic for comparing to behavior: if one decoder performs badly, it would trivially not correlate strongly with behavior. To fairly compare the two decoders to behavior, we therefore subsampled trials across many cross-validation folds such that the curvature decoding performance of the shape-general decoder matched that of the shape-specific decoders (Fig. S5G–I). After finding the predicted curvatures using shape-specific and shape-general decoders in V1 and V4, we tested three predictions that related different quantifications of the decoded curvatures to the monkeys’ choices.

First, the monkeys’ choices should be reflected in the performance of the shape-general curvature decoder (Fig. 4A). This prediction explicitly tests whether the trial-by-trial prediction of curvature by a fixed, weighted combination of responses in V1 and V4 is correlated with the monkey’s behavior. Supporting this, the monkey’s choices were better correlated with the prediction of the shape-general decoder than the shape-specific decoder (Fig. 4B) for both V1 and V4, and when the stimuli varied in overall shape, only orientation, or only color.

Second, if choices are based on projections of visual responses onto a general decoder, then monkeys should make larger errors on shapes whose curvature values are in portions of the representation that are misaligned with the general decoding axis (Fig. 4C). This prediction asserts that the geometry of the neural representation of curvature for any given shape is a strong constraint on how well the relevant feature of the shape can be read out. In other words, this is a stronger test because it doesn’t assume that the population representations are perfectly linear (see examples in Figs. 2E and S4) and that different parts of the curved geometry of the neural representations would produce different projections onto the linear shape-specific and shape-general axes (see refs. 24,34). These anisotropies will produce different patterns of errors. Consistent with this idea, we observed that the two monkeys had largely the same over- or under-estimation errors on particular curvature ranges of different shapes (Fig. S5D), and these errors were more strongly correlated with the decoding errors of the shape-general than the shape-specific decoder (Figs. 4D and S5E, F).

Third, we compared the overall performance of the shape-general decoder with the average performance of the monkey on those shapes in two ways (Fig. S6). (1) Shapes with representations that are better aligned with the shape-general axis should yield lower estimation errors overall. Since some visual feature variations cause larger relative misalignments than others (Fig. 3), it follows that some shapes should be similarly more or less misaligned with the shape-general decoder. We found that monkeys made smaller errors on average on shapes whose curvatures were better estimated by the shape-general decoder in V4 (Fig. S6A). (2) Relatedly, between any two shapes, the one better aligned with the shape-general axis should yield lower behavioral estimation errors (Fig. S6B). In other words, instead of comparing the absolute value of the performance of the shape-general decoder to the performance on each shape, we compared the decoder’s performance on predicting the curvature values of each pair of shapes that was tested on randomly interleaved trials in a given session. We found that performance was typically better for the one that was better decoded by the shape-general decoder in V4. In V1, while the trial-by-trial measures were significant (the first two predictions above), we did not find a significant neural correlate of the average behavioral performance.

Population representations in V4, but not V1, contain the ingredients necessary for behavioral generalization

In our task, monkeys can make a perceptual judgment upon stimulus presentation but must wait to map it to an eye movement until the arc is displayed. To dissociate the curvature judgment from the eye movement, we varied the arc’s position and length across trials. If changes in V1 or V4 responses associated with the arc onset (which might be attributable to any combination of surround modulation, drawing attention to the arc, action planning, or other modulatory processes) support behavioral generalization, the population responses would reflect the appropriate eye movement while maintaining a stable curvature representation. We tested this by comparing neural representations before and after arc onset, hypothesizing that a stable linear combination of responses would inform the saccade after arc onset (Fig. 5A). This is somewhat counterintuitive for the visual cortex, which might be expected to largely encode the curvature judgment without premotor signals (although see refs. 10,35 about premotor signals in V4). Without premotor signals, the decoded saccade will be arc-independent, or behavior-general (Fig. 5B, middle).

We found that V4, but not V1, encodes the direction of the upcoming eye movement (Fig. 5C). While V1 represented the visual properties of the shape, the neural changes associated with the arc onset (if any) were not related to the monkey’s action plans. We quantified the curvature and saccade decoding accuracies in V1 and V4 across unique shapes tested in randomly interleaved trials during each session. As in the sessions with a stable target arc across trials (Fig. S3C), we found that the curvature decoding performance remained stable and high in both V1 and V4 (Fig. S7A, top). When we removed the response variance caused by the variation in curvature across trials by subtracting the mean curvature response for each neuron, the curvature decoding performance fell to 0 (by definition), but in V1, the saccade decoding performance also fell to 0 (Fig. S7, bottom left). By comparison, the saccades could be decoded from curvature-removed V4 responses.

We split the decoded saccades by the various arc conditions (as illustrated for the example session in Fig. 5) and quantified the intercept and the slope of the relationship between the actual and the decoded saccades for each condition. We tested the prediction of the behavioral generalization hypothesis that a single linear weighted combination of responses should predict the upcoming saccade irrespective of the arc condition, i.e., the intercept of the relationship between the actual and decoded saccade should be 0 after the onset of the arc (Fig. 6A). Across sessions, before the onset of the target arc, the responses in both areas were arc-dependent in a way that is consistent with the representation of curvature, not saccades (Fig. 6B left and 6C left). After the onset of the arc, the responses in V1 continued to represent the stimulus curvature while responses in V4 were able to additionally predict the upcoming saccade in an arc-independent way (Fig. 6B right and 6C right). Therefore, saccade direction information in V4 is enmeshed in curvature representation in a dissociable format to allow for a stable readout of both saccade and curvature information across different action mappings.

V4 is one of many areas in the monkey brain that encode the direction of an upcoming eye movement. To test the hypothesis that the stable representations of curvature and saccade directions we observed in V4 could be sufficient for behavioral generalization, we created a model that has only one brain area. We trained a recurrent neural network (RNN) to map stimulus curvature to a saccade under varying arc conditions (Fig. S8). Like V4 neurons, the RNN hidden units initially represented curvature and, after arc onset, represent the direction of the upcoming saccade (Fig. S8E). We then extended the model by adding a pre-trained convolutional neural network (CNN) front-end to give the RNN its “visual inputs” in the form of hidden layer activations (Fig. S9A). Like V1 and V4 neural populations, this RNN learned stable representations of shape-general curvature and saccade direction (Fig. S9B).

Our results demonstrate that the visual cortex contains the ingredients to enable both visual and behavioral generalization. Both V1 and V4 are sufficient for visual generalization because their representations can be used to map many visual features to one judgment (Fig. 4). V4 could support behavioral generalization by mapping one judgment onto multiple actions (Figs. 5, 6).

Discussion

Together, our results show that population representations in the visual cortex contain the necessary ingredients to enable visual and behavioral generalization, two important aspects of flexible behavior. We demonstrated three ways that V4 populations are well-suited to this function:

The representations of the relevant feature (curvature), while strongly modulated by task-irrelevant features, were smooth and continuous (Figs. 2 and S4) and, therefore, well-positioned to mediate continuous judgments.

These results suggest the tantalizing possibility that the format of neural population representations subserves visual generalization and that amendments to this format via feedback, efferent, recurrent, or neuromodulatory action could underlie behavioral generalization.

Flexibility that depends on the format of population representations is consistent with observations about the stability of the tuning of single neurons and with known biological processes

Perceptual, cognitive, and motor processes have long been known to modulate the responses of sensory neurons. For example, contrast, adaptation, surround modulation, attention, learning, task switching, arousal, and action planning are all associated with modulations of neural responses in the visual cortex13,36–41. These modulations multiplicatively scale (change the gain of) relatively stable tuning curves42. More recently, modulation related to a broad set of perceptual, cognitive, and motor processes has been observed in essentially every brain area in many species13–15.

Despite these widespread modulations across different tasks, cognitive states, stimuli, and motor plans, neurons in the visual cortex largely retain their tuning to visual features and undergo modest task-specific modulations36. Because of this tuning stability, visual and behavioral generalization are typically attributed to flexible interactions between neural populations9,43. The implied neural mechanism passes off the computational burden of inferring the task-relevant stimulus features and mapping to behavioral choices to the modulation or gating of synapses between different neurons that communicate with each other via dedicated channels or communication subspaces. These signals could originate in other areas and be reflected in V1 or V4 through feedback or recurrent interactions. Our results, therefore, do not imply that these computations occur locally within the visual cortex. Rather, they demonstrate that the population activity present in these areas contains the information necessary for downstream areas to implement flexible readout.

Here, we tested the alternative, but not mutually exclusive, hypothesis that flexibility can be enabled by the formatting or geometry of neural representations, such that a fixed subset of readout neurons could selectively reflect the combination of perceptual, cognitive, and motor information necessary to guide behavior. Only a subset of neurons in any brain area project to a given downstream target44, which is consistent with the idea that the readout strategy (e.g., by downstream decision neurons) is largely stable. Attempts to identify the subset of information that is functionally shared between any two neural populations (i.e., the “communication subspace”43) have shown that those interactions are largely stable45, even though the information shared via that stable subspace can vary9. While our results suggest that relevant information is identified in the visual cortex and communicated to downstream areas to guide decisions, they do not preclude channels for additionally communicating task-irrelevant information to downstream decision areas46. Together, these results and those presented here are consistent with the idea that, at least for forms of behavioral flexibility that can change on the timescale of a single trial, the output channels (defined loosely as projection neurons, communication subspaces, and/or readout strategies) are largely fixed. An interesting question for future work is whether flexible processes that evolve on longer timescales, such as perceptual or motor learning, might involve synaptic changes that modify the output channels.

Role of distinct brain areas in flexible behavior

If modulations to visual neurons contain all the ingredients for flexible, visually guided behavior, why does the monkey (and the human) have so many other brain areas? An emerging body of evidence, including that presented here, is that the formatting of different information sources (e.g., visual features, cognitive signals, and other task-relevant or irrelevant information; sometimes called the representational geometry34,47) places important constraints on the role a neural population plays in a particular computation20. Area V4 is particularly well-suited to the kinds of decisions and flexibility we studied here because it contains representations of the visual features we varied29,48, it is modulated by processes like feature attention, task switching, motor planning, attention, and surround modulation10,22,39, and our core tasks required judgments about stationary visual stimuli. We showed here that area V4 has the combination of stability of representations and task-related flexibility to mediate multiple forms of generalization. This study opens up a range of avenues for future study of how the brain balances the need for stability of representations of the world with flexibility in rules for generalization and sensorimotor mappings. For example, tasks that require subjects to integrate across time or space to perform sequences of decisions or actions might rely more on areas with more complex dynamics49–51 and fundamentally different formatting of task-relevant information. An exciting possibility is that the stable linear relationships between neurons and behavior that we demonstrated here could persist across a vast array of stimuli and task demands. Such a finding could inspire future neuroscientific studies in many species and systems, translational efforts to enhance and repair cognitive flexibility in human patients, and efforts to create biologically inspired artificial systems that mimic the remarkable flexibility of the human brain.

Methods

Experimental model details

Two adult male rhesus monkeys (Macaca mulatta, 8 and 9 kg, aged ~6 years, sourced from Alpha Genesis, Inc.) were implanted with a titanium head post before behavioral training. After training, we chronically implanted an 8 × 12 microelectrode array in each of cortical areas V1 and V4 of the right hemisphere (Blackrock Neurotech, Salt Lake City, UT). The arrays were connected to a percutaneous pedestal, which allowed for recording. Each electrode within the array was 1 mm long, and the distance between adjacent electrodes was 400 μm. Areas V1 and V4 were identified by visualizing the sulci during array implantation and using stereotactic coordinates. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of the University of Pittsburgh and Carnegie Mellon University, where the electrophysiological and psychophysical data were collected. Additionally, this study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE animal use and reporting guidelines.

Method details

Experimentation apparatus

We presented 2D projections of the 3D visual stimuli on a 24″ ViewPixx monitor (1920 × 1080 pixels; 120 Hz refresh rate; calibrated to linearize luminance using X-Rite calibrator) placed 56–60 cm from the monkey. The onset of stimuli was coincident with the onset of a marker on the screen, which was captured by a photodiode and recorded (at 30 kHz) to synchronize the display with data acquisition. We monitored eye position using an infrared eye tracker (EyeLink 1000 Plus; SR Research) and recorded eye position and pupil diameter (at 2 kHz). Neuronal responses were acquired (at 30 kHz) using a CerePlex E headstage and CerePlex amplifier (Blackrock Neurotech, Salt Lake City, UT). Reward was delivered using a gravity-based solenoid system (Crist Instruments, Hagerstown, MD)/ Behavioral monitoring, reward delivery, and stimulus rendering were controlled by custom MATLAB software and the Psychophysics Toolbox (v.3; ref. 52).

Shape stimulus generation

We used a custom generative algorithm to build and render shape stimuli. Details of the generation parameters are schematized in Fig. S1. Briefly, random closed-loop B-splines (with parameterized complexity) were used to create the 2D cross-sectional shape. These shapes were stacked in the 3rd dimension along the curved medial axis while being scaled and rotated (the length, curvature, thickness profile, and helical twist profile were all randomly generated per shape). This generated the vertices of the 3D shape, which were connected with edges and smoothed. The global shape parameters (x, y position on the screen, in-plane and out-of-plane rotation, color, and gloss) were then randomly selected and applied. The shape was rendered in perspective view with the rendering camera directly in front and the light source directly above the camera to create the appropriate shading and specularity cues for the 3D shape. The image was saved and displayed on the screen during the experiment.

Behavioral task and training

Monkeys were trained to perform the continuous curvature estimation task schematized in Fig. 1. During training sessions, monkeys were exposed to 10–15 randomly generated shapes, which were repeated during recording sessions with additional changes in color and orientation. In a subset of sessions, we also presented random shapes that the monkeys had never seen before. The lengths of each epoch during each trial were sampled from uniform distributions with the following ranges. Monkeys fixated a central fixation point (within a 0.5° fixation window) for 150–200 ms before the shape stimulus was displayed for 500–700 ms. A thin, white arc that usually subtended 140° at the center and a radius of about 3.7° of visual angle was then displayed in the upper hemifield between −70° and 70°. Since the medial axis of the shape was a circular arc, the curvature value the monkey had to guess was the inverse of the radius of the circle. This curvature was linearly mapped to the target arc. In subsets of sessions, the arc was rotated around the center subtending either between −90° and 50°, −70° and 70°, −50° and 90° (arc length 140°), or −70° and 30°, −50° and 50°, −30° and 70° (arc length 100°). The sessions with these arc manipulations (29 in monkey 1 and 28 in monkey 2) were only analyzed to test the behavioral generalization predictions in Figs. 5, 6 and were not included in the 124 sessions that are analyzed in Figs. 1–4, in which the 140° arc was central and stable across trials. The radius of the arc was also varied between 3.67 and 7.3° of visual angle in some sessions, but since they did not have an effect on the direction of saccades, these sessions were omitted from analysis. After 150–200 ms, the fixation point was turned off, which served as the go cue for the monkeys to saccade to the correct portion of the target arc to make a continuous report of the curvature of the stimulus. After the monkeys maintained fixation on the selected arc location for 200 ms, a juice reward was delivered, the magnitude of which depended on the angular error in the curvature report. The reward amount varied from 0.2 to 0.1 cc up to an error of 10% of the arc length. After the reward was or was not delivered, the monkeys were given feedback: the contrast of the target arc was lowered to reveal a 2° bright spot over the correct target location. Monkeys were rewarded with a small amount of juice to saccade to and fixate the correct location. This second saccade served only as an error correction signal and was not analyzed as an additional or corrected choice. A full trial took between 1.4 and 2 s and all trials in which the monkeys successfully completed the trial (rewarded or unrewarded) were analyzed. Trials with breaks in fixation during any task epoch were not analyzed. The reward strategy was consistent across training, only varying in amount across sessions. Details of the session and neuron counts are specified in each figure and analysis. Comparisons of behavioral performance across shapes, orientations, colors, and across trained vs novel shapes are highlighted in Fig. S2C, D.

Electrophysiological recordings and response epochs

We filtered the recorded activity (bandpass 250–5000 Hz) and detected timestamps where the activity crossed a channel-specific threshold (set to 2-3x RMS value). The raw electrical signal, waveforms at each crossing, and local field potential activity filtered with 2–200 Hz were also saved. The timestamps were chunked by trial start and stop times and aligned to stimulus onset, determined based on the photodiode signals. To allow for the latency of responses in the visual cortex, stimulus-evoked firing rate of each channel was calculated between 50 and 550 ms. The baseline firing rates were calculated based on the spike counts in the 200 ms time period prior to the onset of the stimulus. Across 124 sessions, stimulus-evoked firing rates ranged from 0 to 403 (mean 107) spikes/s for V1 units and 0 to 358 (mean 109) spikes/s for V4 units, calculated between 50 and 550 ms after stimulus onset. During the arc presentation duration (0–200 ms after arc onset), firing rates ranged from 0 to 349 (mean 88) spikes/s for V1 units and 0 to 344 (mean 91) spikes/s for V4 units. Mean baseline firing rates were 59 (V1) and 68 (V4) spikes/s. Analyses comparing single and multi-units in previous work2,53, did not find systematic differences between single neurons and multi-units for the types of population analyses presented here. For that reason, we did not sort spikes, and the term “unit” refers to the multiunit activity at each recording site.

Unit, trial, and session exclusion

We opted for a generous criterion for units and trials. We included units for analysis if the average stimulus-evoked firing rate across all trials in an experimental session was 1.1x the average baseline firing rate (calculated before the stimulus onset while the monkey was maintaining fixation). Only trials in which the monkeys maintained fixation throughout the stimulus period and made a successful selection were included. For analyses related to the decoding of curvature or selectivities, we analyzed data from 124 sessions (61 and 63 from the two monkeys). In a subset of these sessions (22 and 19 sessions for the two monkeys), the monkeys were provided a cue for the correct answer for a small subset of trials as a training signal. To avoid contamination of these cues in our behavioral metrics and correlations of behavior with neural responses, we excluded that entire set of sessions even though the cue was provided only on some trials. In a mutually exclusive set of experiments, we varied the position and the length of the target arc (29 sessions for monkey 1 and 28 sessions for monkey 2). These data were analyzed in Figs. 5, 6 and S7.

Receptive field (RF) mapping

The receptive fields of the populations of V1 and V4 neurons were mapped using the same procedure. The monkey fixated on a central spot while 2D and 3D objects, and texture images were flashed in a grid of locations in the relevant visual quadrant for 200–250 ms with a 200–250 ms inter-stimulus interval. Monkeys were rewarded to maintain fixation for 6–8 flashes. The images were sampled from a diverse set of random 2D closed-loop B-spline objects, 3D objects generated using the same procedure as for the curvature task (but not used in the curvature task), 2D silhouettes of those objects, and natural texture patches. The images were flashed in at least two sizes to ensure sufficient overlap between locations. For each unit, we calculated a stimulus-triggered average image and an ellipse that fit the RF envelopes. The centers and average sizes of the V1 and V4 receptive fields are shown in Fig. 2. We did not exclude units on the basis of RF location or size.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Behavioral performance and psychometric curves

The monkey’s choice was taken as the angular location on the arc where the monkey entered the arc-shaped window and maintained fixation. The angular location was linearly mapped onto curvature. Psychometric curves in Figs. 1 and S2 were plotted by binning actual curvatures and calculating the mean and standard error of the mean (SEM) of monkey choices. The similarity between images across curvature variations and across shapes used in Fig. S2E, F was calculated as one minus the normalized Euclidean distance between the population activations of units in layer 4 in a deep convolutional neural network (VGG-16) to pairs of stimulus images. The choice of layer did not affect the comparison with the monkey’s choice differences.

Curvature selectivity

We calculated the shape-specific curvature selectivity of each unit by first calculating the presentation-averaged curvature response function (Fig. 2D) and then calculating the selectivity as (rMax − rMin)/(rMax + rMin), where rMax and rMin were the maximum and minimum response (Fig. S3). We calculated this using the stimulus-evoked response (50–500 ms after stimulus onset) or using the response during the arc presentation period (while the monkey was still fixating; 0–200 ms after arc onset). For each shape, the curvature values that corresponded to the maximum and minimum responses were maintained while calculating the selectivity during the arc presentation period. So, the selectivity during the stimulus period has a range of 0–1, but not during the arc period.

Principal components analysis, curvature representation, and shape-specific decoding

We used principal components analysis primarily for visualizing the population activity and demonstrating a smooth representation of the relevant continuous feature, curvature. We also typically reduced the dimensionality of the population responses to 20–25 before performing decoding or cross-decoding analyses (details below) to prevent rank deficiency. In the population response visualizations (Figs. 2E and S4), we plotted each stimulus presentation, where feasible, and the presentation-average responses and color-coded them based on the stimulus curvature. We also fit a 3D polynomial or linear space curve to highlight the relevant variation axis for visualization.

For shape-specific curvature decoding (Figs. 3, 4), we linearly decoded (using Ridge regression) the curvature values of the presented stimulus from V1 and V4 responses. We found the regression parameters using cross-validation for each shape and added a constant term to account for the intercept. This ensured that a simple shift in the underlying representation across stimulus conditions could be captured by the decoder. For display, we binned curvature values and plotted the mean and SEM of decoded curvature values. Unless otherwise mentioned, all decoding analyses were performed with leave-one-out cross-validation (10- and 20-fold cross-validation were also performed with qualitatively similar results). We typically used the correlation (r) between the decoded and actual feature values as an indicator of decoding performance. We obtained qualitatively similar results using r2, mean squared error (Fig. 3), and other measures of similarity.

Cross-shape curvature decoding

To evaluate whether two shape-specific curvature axes were typically aligned, we split each shape response into equal numbers of trials. We trained a linear decoder using the shape responses for one shape and predicted either the held-out trials of the same shape (Fig. 3D) or the other shape (Fig. 3E), controlling for trial numbers. We did this in both directions for all pairs of shapes, yielding 844 shape pairs across 83 recording sessions. We calculated the mean squared error of self- and cross-decoding and plotted them for shape pairs that differed in overall shape, orientation only, or color only (Fig. 3F).

Shape-general curvature decoding and behavior correlations

To decode curvature using a shape-general strategy, we trained a linear decoder using Ridge regression (as above) on responses across different shape stimuli within the same recording session. Since the curvature axes are demonstrably non-aligned, we selected a number of trials to train the shape-general decoder such that the shape-specific and shape-general decoders both have equal curvature decoding performance. To do this, we gradually increased the number of training trials by randomly sampling trials across shapes (across 100 folds) and then comparing curvature decoding performance with the specific decoder of that shape. It took about 40% more trials to train a shape-general decoder to match the curvature decoding performance of the shape-specific decoder. We then compared the decoded curvature values from both decoders to the monkey’s curvature estimation behavior. This yielded two correlation values, which are plotted in Fig. 4B. We also compared the curvature decoding performance of the shape-general decoder with the monkey’s average curvature estimation error (Fig. S6A), and the curvature estimation errors of pairs of shapes within a recording session (Fig. S6B). All comparisons were tested for significance using the Wilcoxon Signed Rank test.

Saccade decoding and intercept analysis

We calculated the spike rate of each unit in V1 and V4 during the arc presentation duration (0–200 ms after arc onset) and linearly decoded using Ridge regression (same as the curvature decoders above) the actual saccade locations on the arc in arc degrees. We then compared the actual saccade and the decoded saccade, split between arc conditions. (Note that we did not fit separate decoders for each arc condition; we instead used an arc-general saccade decoding strategy and projected the responses for each unique arc condition onto it.) We tested the hypothesis that V1 and V4 responses would encode the curvature and the saccade simultaneously. The null hypothesis would be that V1 and V4 responses would continue to only represent the stimulus in their RFs. If the null hypothesis were true, the relationship between the actual and decoded saccades would mimic the relationship between the actual saccade and the stimulus curvature and therefore be arc-dependent. We tested this by calculating the intercept of this relationship across different arc conditions per stimulus shape, both before and after arc onset. In many cases, we tested several arc conditions (variations in both arc length and position) for the same shape and several shapes in the same experiment, therefore, yielding curvatures × shapes × arc location × arc position trial conditions. We repeated the saccade decoding analysis after subtracting the mean response for each curvature value per shape from each trial’s response during the arc period. This eliminated curvature information from the population response (validated by confirming that the curvature decoding performance fell to 0). The saccade decoding performance also fell to 0 in V1, but not in V4.

Recurrent neural network (RNN) modeling of saccade outcomes

We trained an RNN using PsychRNN54 to understand the possible computations underlying stimulus and saccade representations. We input the fixation as a one-hot variable, the curvature as a continuous variable between 0 and 1, and the arc condition as two variables that represented the arc location and length (Fig. S8A). Three arc angular positions and two arc lengths were tested. The RNN was trained to calculate the correct saccade for the input variable combination. The timing of the different inputs was randomized and sequenced to replicate the monkey task (schematized in Fig. S8B). The RNN had 50 hidden units and was trained on noisy, random inputs. The network learned to do the task (compare Fig. S8D, F) by first representing the curvature during the stimulus epoch and, after arc onset, rotating the representation to align with a saccade axis such that both the curvature and the saccade can be read out.

Decoding curvature from convolutional network (CNN) activations

Since the veridical curvature was supplied as input to the RNN above, we next tested whether a pre-trained convolutional neural network (VGG-16) could be used as a front-end to extract the 3D curvature from image inputs. To do that, we rescaled the input images to the appropriate size and the shape of the stimulus in the image to the size of the RF of the pooling layer neurons in layer 4 of VGG-16. We generated either 1000 fully random shapes with random curvatures (image set 1) or 50 unique shapes with 20 curvatures each (image set 2). We trained a linear decoder using the activations of the central units (since the stimulus image was scaled and placed in the middle of the image) and tested it on a testing set of 5 shapes with 20 curvatures each. We reduced the dimensionality of the response space to 256 dimensions to prevent rank deficiency. The curvatures of the test set were successfully decoded by shape-general decoders trained on either of the image sets (Fig. S9A).

RNN saccade modeling using CNN inputs

We then supplied an RNN with the dimensionality-reduced CNN inputs and trained it using the same method described above. We used a larger network (512 hidden units) to compensate for the high-dimensional input, but otherwise kept the training regimen and trial timing the same as above. We trained separate RNNs of the two image sets, and both were able to generate the correct saccades (Fig. S9B; network behavior) by first representing the curvature on the stimulus axis and then rotating the representation in an arc-dependent manner to the saccade axis (Fig. S9B; network state).
