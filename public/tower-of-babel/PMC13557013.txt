Boron-substituted aluminum clusters B3Aln−3+/0/− (n = 3–21): stability, halogen properties and ring current characters
Authors: Bao-Ngan Nguyen-Ha, Quang Huy Ho, Nguyen Minh Tam, My Phuong Pham-Ho, Minh Tho Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Boron-substituted aluminum clusters B3Aln−3+/0/− with n = 3–21 in three charge states are systematically investigated using density functional theory (DFT) computations using the TPSSh functional and 6-311+G(d) basis set. Low-energy structures of B3Aln−3+/0/− clusters are constructed around a stable boron trimer, while Al atoms are progressively built surrounding the framework. An exohedral-to-endohedral transition occurs when the framework reaches thirteen Al atoms, and a triangle-to-line rearrangement of the B3 unit is observed at eighteen Al atoms for the neutral and cationic series. Such a transformation is suppressed for the anions due to the exceptional stabilization of the cyclic B3− anion. Thermodynamic analysis shows that B3 incorporation tends to enhance the stability of the Al frameworks in all charge states, with the B3Aln−3− anions being the most stable overall. Pronounced electron affinity EAa maxima are observed for B3Al10, B3@Al13 and B3@Al16 (∼3.0–3.1 eV), indicating that a quasi-halogen behaviour is retained even with slightly smaller EAa. These EAa reflect a strong tendency to accept additional electron(s) to reach closed-shell configurations in some magic clusters, namely [(1S)2(1P)6(2S)2(1D)10(2P)6(1F)14] for B3Al10−, [(1S)2(1P)6(1D)10(1F)14(2S)2(2P)6(2D)10(3S)2(3P)6] for B3@Al16−, and [(1S)2(1P)6(1D)10(1F)14(2S)2(2P)6(2D)10] for B3@Al132−. Polarized Alδ+–Bδ− interactions are identified with electron transfer from Al to the B3 dopant, and multicenter delocalized bonding supports an aromatic stabilization across the framework due to the isovalent character of both B and Al elements. Coherent electron dynamics calculations predict a pronounced circulating ring current for the linear-B3 unit, which is strongly enhanced when the full p-orbital set (px, py and pz) is included.

Introduction

The boron trimer is widely recognized as an exceptionally stable species that favors a highly symmetric triangular D3h geometry, which induces a pronounced aromatic stabilization across the charge states B3+/0/−. Notably, the B3− anion displays a double aromaticity arising from a simultaneous delocalization of two π electrons and two σ electrons.1 By comparison, The B3 neutral radical exhibits only π aromaticity, whereas its unpaired electron is distributed over a (3c-1e) σ bond.1 The B3+ cation likewise retains π aromaticity but a σ aromatic character no longer exists.2 Owing to their high thermodynamic stability, these trimers serve as fundamental motifs in the construction of larger boron-based architectures.3–7 In addition to serving as structural building blocks, B3+/0/− units can also be incorporated as dopants in diverse atomic cluster cages.8–10

Although the boron trimer has been combined with several elemental clusters from both the main group (both metals and non-metals),11–26 as well as transition metal elements,27–32 most previous studies focused on small sizes and predominantly considered exohedral motifs. In such exohedral complexes, the three boron atoms often behave as a B3 unit which generally retains its triangular framework or undergoes slight angular distortion, except for when they are bound to highly electronegative partners such as O24 and S26 or in a highly charged anionic system like Si2B3 (ref. 3–5) where the B3 fragment becomes unconnected or rearranges into a linear-like motif. In contrast, endohedral arrangements have been examined in only a few cases, notably for Si-, Ge- and Sc cluster systems.8–10 Specifically, when confined within Si or Ge host frameworks, the encapsulated B3 trimer essentially preserves its triangular shape; this geometric retention promotes an electron delocalization and endows the resulting B3Si12+ and B3Ge12+ hexagonal tubes with a strong aromatic character. These tubular units can further align to form extended nanotubular structures.8,9 By comparison, encapsulation of boron trimer within a scandium cage leads to a distinct and remarkable structural response. The B3 fragment in B3@Sc10+/0 undergoes a triangular-to-linear transformation, adopting a linear arrangement inside the gyroelongated square-bipyramidal framework of the Sc10+/0 system.

In view of the iso-valence of both B and Al elements, introduction of a B3 unit into aluminum clusters has been explored as a practical route to generate diverse bonding motifs having potentially useful properties. Previous investigations of B3Aln+/0/− clusters with n = 2–6 indicate that the three boron atoms typically retain its inherent trimeric framework, which is stabilized by a combination of B <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="13.200000pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 13.200000 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.017500,-0.017500)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> B double bond character and (3c-2e) delocalized bonding, or an aromatic character mentioned above.16 For the B3Al3 cluster, five (3c-2e) σ bonds were identified in which two are mainly associated with the B–B–B fragment, whereas three others are extended over mixed B–B–Al units, suggesting that Al incorporation partially reshapes the σ-bonding network.33 In addition, electron density is generally shifted from B to Al, driven by the electronegativity difference between both elements.33 Moreover, for small AlxBy clusters such as Al2B, Al3B2, Al4B3… an increasing boron content tends to raise the thermal equilibrium temperature, reaching a maximum of ∼1800 K for Al4B3.34

Meanwhile, aluminum clusters themselves have long been investigated for their distinctive structural and electronic behaviour, especially for their high thermodynamic stability and superhalogen character, with Al13 cluster being a representative example.35–37 Notably, the singly doped BAl12− and doubly doped B2Al11− anions were found to be highly stable and behave similarly to the Al13− anion, as both adopt an Al13−-like icosahedral framework and exhibit a closed electron shell of 40 electrons, a magic cluster characteristic.38 In this context, it is of interest to determine how incorporation of a B3 unit influences the geometry and stability and in particular the superhalogen nature of aluminum clusters which becomes an intriguing question.

Our objectives are to establish the geometric growth trends and to identify the cluster size(s) at which an exohedral-to-endohedral transition occurs, and to determine as to whether the B3 fragment undergoes a triangular-to-linear rearrangement. In addition, the electronic and chemical factors governing their stability and bonding are figured out, with a particular emphasis on the superhalogen behaviour which is an inherent property of aluminum clusters. Previous studies on B3Aln+/0/− clusters were limited to small sizes of n = 2–6. The present work therefore extends the global structural search to a substantially larger size domain, systematically exploring B3-doped aluminum clusters B3Aln−3+/0/− with n going from 3 to 21 across the cationic, neutral and anionic charge states. The upper limit of n = 21 is selected because this size range is sufficient to capture these key structural transformations.

Computational methods

Electronic structure calculations are performed using the Gaussian 16 program package.39

The 6-311+G(d) basis set40,41 is employed in this study because it provides a favorable balance between computational cost and accuracy for B3-related and Al–B systems.5,8,9,13,42,43 Previous studies have shown that several density functionals, including M06,44 ωB97XD45 and TPSSh density functional46,47 can reasonably describe group-13-element clusters, including Ga, Al, and B systems.33,48 Therefore, these functionals, together with the 6-311+G(d) basis set, are selected for a benchmark assessment of relative energies against higher-level CCSD(T)/cc-pVTZ results for representative B3Aln−3+/0/− clusters with n = 4–11 in all three charge states.

The benchmark results, summarized in Tables S1–S4 of the SI, indicate that TPSSh is among the functionals showing good agreement with CCSD(T). In particular, the predicted electronic structures and relative stabilities of representative anionic, neutral, and cationic B3Aln−3+/0/− clusters with n = 4–11, calculated at the TPSSh/6-311+G(d) level, remain either nearly degenerate in energy or essentially unchanged in energetic ordering compared with the CCSD(T) results. More specifically, for the smaller B3Al+/0/− and B3Al2+/0/− clusters, the M06 functional gives results closer to CCSD(T), whereas with increasing numbers of Al atoms, TPSSh provides more consistent agreement with CCSD(T) than the other tested functionals. Accordingly, the TPSSh/6-311+G(d) method is adopted as the primary level of theory for the extensive structural exploration in the present work.

To locate low-energy configurations, both manual and automated approaches are employed. First, initial geometries of B3Aln−3+/0/− are manually constructed by placing B atoms at various positions within previously reported global-minimum frameworks of neutral and charged pure Al clusters and related B-doped Al clusters16,33,38,48–51 For larger sizes, additional starting structures are produced by stepwise addition of Al or B atoms onto different regions of smaller clusters, including vertices, edges, and faces. Structural motifs reported for Ga–B, Sc–B, and Al–Sc systems10,33,48,52–54 are also taken into account as structural references, given their comparable valence-electron counts to the clusters examined here. All initial geometries are first optimized using the TPSSh functional with the small LANL2DZ basis set.

To further broaden the configurational sampling for B3Aln−3+/0/− with n = 3–21, an automatic stochastic algorithm55 based on an improved random-kick procedure56,57 is applied. The low-lying isomers within an energy range of 1 eV obtained from the initial optimizations are used as input structures for further exploration. New isomers generated from the genetic-algorithm step are subsequently used as inputs for the random-kick search, while new structures obtained from the random-kick search are included again as inputs for the genetic-algorithm step. This iterative procedure is continued until no new lower-energy structures are obtained from either approach. For example, for the Al10B3 cluster, up to approximately 500 candidate structures are evaluated. Duplicate or nearly duplicate optimized structures are identified by visual inspection and comparison of their geometrical features, relative energies, and connectivity patterns, and redundant structures are removed manually. Although this procedure is less automated, it is practical for the present cluster sizes and allows distorted but chemically distinct low-energy isomers to be retained for further examination.

The structures generated from this procedure are optimized at the TPSSh/LANL2DZ level. For each converged geometry, additional optimizations are carried out for different spin multiplicities. Specifically, for systems with an even number of electrons, spin multiplicities from singlet to nonet are examined, while for systems with an odd number of electrons, spin multiplicities from doublet to decet are considered. If optimization at a different spin state leads to a distinct geometry, that structure is retained and further optimized at other relevant spin multiplicities. This procedure allows us to identify the preferred spin state for each low-energy isomer and to avoid missing important structures arising from spin-dependent distortions. When near-degenerate isomers are encountered, symmetry-constrained re-optimizations are performed to validate their geometries and symmetry assignments.

All local minima lying within ∼3 eV (∼69 kcal mol−1) of the global minimum for each cluster size are subsequently re-optimized and characterized by harmonic vibrational frequency calculations at the TPSSh/6-311+G(d) level. Zero-point energy (ZPE) corrections obtained at the latter level are included in the energetics reported in the next sections.

Electronic structure analyses including natural electron configuration (NEC) and spin-density distributions, are performed with the aid of the NBO 5.0 program.58 Delocalized bonds are further examined with adaptive natural density partitioning (AdNDP)59 as implemented in the Multiwfn program.60

There are two effective ways of probing ring currents in molecular aromatic systems. The first approach involves an application of a magnetic field perpendicular to the molecular plane to control the delocalized π electrons.61 The second approach uses a circularly polarized light (CPL) to excite a coherent rotation of π electrons in highly symmetric aromatic molecules.62 This coherent rotation originates from a pair of degenerate electronic excited states. Their two types of superposition, in-phase and out-of-phase, are eigenstates of the angular-momentum operator (with the component perpendicular to the molecular plane), possessing positive or negative eigenvalues. As a result, CPL selectively excites one of the two superpositions depending on its handedness that are left- or right-circular polarizations.

Results and discussion

Structural evolution of B3Aln−3+/0/− clusters with n going from 3 to 21

As for a labeling convention, the B3Aln−3+/0/− isomers are assigned to by a compact label of the form x.y.z where x specifies the charge state, namely c for cationic, n for neutral, and a for anionic; y is the total number of atoms n in the cluster considered, and z indicates the relative-energy ranking, z = 1, 2, 3,… in order of increasing relative energy. For example, c.5.1 corresponds to the lowest-energy isomer of the B3Al2+ cation.

As for a reference, the global energy minimum structures of pure Aln+/0/− with n = 3–21 clusters are provided in Fig. S1 of the SI file. Fig. 1 shows the lowest-lying isomers of B3Aln−3+/0/− clusters with n = 4–11, calculated at the (U)CCSD(T)/cc-pVTZ level. The lowest-lying isomers of the larger of B3Aln−3+/0/− clusters with n = 12–21, calculated at the TPSSh/6-311+G(d) level, are presented in Fig. 2. Additional low-energy structures of B3Aln−3+/0/− in all three charge states are given in Fig. S2–S13 (SI). For the sizes n = 5–9, the lowest-energy B3Aln−3+/0/− structures identified in this work are consistent with those previously reported by Wen et al.16

Overall, structures of B3Aln−3+/0/− clusters cannot be interpreted simply as arising from either substitution of three B atoms into, or attachment of a B3 unit onto a pure Aln−3+/0/− host framework. Instead, owing to the high intrinsic stability of the B3 ring, these clusters are effectively assembled around a B3 core in which the three boron atoms remain locally connected to one another, while the aluminum atoms are progressively incorporated around each B3 building block.

By comparison, in B3-doped Sc clusters, the B3 trimer becomes linear as early as at the size of eight Sc atoms (B3Sc8+/0), and the exohedral-to-endohedral transition occurs at the size of ten Sc atoms B3@Sc10+/0.10 In aluminum hosts, such an encapsulation is shifted to larger sizes. Because the Al atoms has a smaller atomic radius, Al frameworks cannot fully enclose the B3 unit until the size of thirteen Al atoms is reached, where an exohedral-to-endohedral transition takes place. Notably, in B3@Al13+/0/−, the encapsulated B3 unit remains triangular. The B3 fragment continues to adopt either a triangular or slightly distorted triangular geometry up to the size of seventeen Al atoms B3@Al17+/0. At the size of eighteen Al atoms, a triangular-to-linear rearrangement is observed for both cationic and neutral clusters (B3@Al18+/0/), but not for the anion B3@Al18− where the cyclic form persists. Such a difference is attributed to the strong thermodynamic stabilization of the B3 ring in the anionic state (to be discussed more in following sections). Consequently, for the B3@Al18− anion, the lowest-energy structure still contains an obtuse triangular B3 motif, whereas the isomer containing a linear B3 form lies ∼5 kcal mol−1 higher in energy (cf. Fig. S13). Both the exohedral-to-endohedral transition and the triangular-to-linear rearrangement of B3 are further examined at the M06/6-311+G(d) level. The results are consistent with those obtained at the TPSSh/6-311+G(d) level, further supporting the reliability of the proposed structural evolution.

The structural evolution of anionic B3Aln−3− clusters (n = 3–21) can be rationalized in terms of four fundamental building units, namely B3−, B3Al10−, B3@Al13− and B3@Al16− (cf. Fig. S14). At small sizes, additional Al atoms progressively surround the triangular B3 unit until a particularly stable, near-icosahedral framework is reached for of B3Al10−. Subsequent growth to B3Al11− and B3Al12− mainly proceeds by adding Al atoms to multiple sites of this near-icosahedral framework. At B3@Al13−, encapsulation of the B3 unit begins, and the resulting endohedral structure serves as a building block for larger B3@Al14− and B3@Al15− clusters. The B3 ring then starts to open into an obtuse shape at B3@Al16−, and the following structures of B3@Al17− and B3@Al18− can be regarded as minor growth variants derived from this building unit.

For both the cationic and neutral series, structural evolution may likewise be summarized by four representative structural units including B3+/0, B3Al10+/0, B3@Al13+/0 and B3@Al18+/0 (cf.Fig. 3 and S14). The overall growth pattern is similar to that of the anion up to the onset of encapsulation at B3@Al13+/0. Ring opening tendencies emerge at B3@Al15 in the neutral series and at B3@Al16+ in the cationic series. However, these intermediates are relatively less stabilized, and multiple isomers containing either B3 triangles or obtuse B3 arrangements often appear close in energy. The gradual development of ring opening and obtuse-angle B3 motifs at sizes B3@Al15+/0, B3@Al16+/0 and B3@Al17+/0 can thus be viewed as structural preparation for a final linearization of B3 as observed at the size B3@Al18+/0.

Thermodynamic stability and electron affinity

Thermodynamic stability of the B3Aln−3+/0/− (n = 3–21) clusters is evaluated using the average binding energy (Eb) of the lowest-energy isomer at each size and in each charge state. In addition, to examine size- and charge-dependent stability trends and to quantify the tendency of these clusters to lose or gain an electron, the adiabatic ionization energies (IEa), the adiabatic electron affinities (EAa) and the vertical detachment energies (VDE) are also considered. The B3-doped clusters exhibit higher binding energies than the corresponding pure Aln+/0/− clusters in all three charge states, and the anionic species turn out to be the most stable forms overall (cf.Fig. 4). Although incorporation of B3 help stabilize the Al frameworks, the mixed-B3Aln−3− anions end up being less stabilized than the isolated B3− trimer, in terms of binding energy. In particular, the anionic B3− trimer has the largest Eb value (∼3.5 eV), whereas the Eb of the mixed B3Aln−3− anions are <3.3 eV. By contrast, incorporation of Al around the B3 unit strengthens the neutral and cationic series, leading to increasing Eb values relative to both the corresponding pure Aln+/0 clusters and the isolated B3+/0 species.

The exceptionally high thermodynamic stability of the triangular B3− anion makes the ring opening energetically unfavorable in the anionic state. This provides a rationale for the reason why, at small and medium sizes, B3-doped anionic clusters retain a triangular B3 unit and only begin to open into an obtuse configuration at larger sizes, starting from clusters containing sixteen Al atoms. It also accounts for the reason for why the complete linearization of B3 observed in the neutral and cationic series is not favoured in the anions.

The adiabatic ionization energies IEa illustrated in Fig. 5A decrease gradually with increasing cluster size, indicating that electron detachment from the neutrals becomes progressively easier for larger clusters. However, at sizes with an even total number of atoms (e.g., 10, 16, 18, and 20), the IEa values are slightly higher than those of the adjacent odd sizes, suggesting relatively more difficult ionization. This behavior is no doubt due to the unfavourable spin conversion required upon ionization at these sizes, namely the transition from a singlet neutral ground state to a doublet cation. In addition, the cluster with a total size of thirteen atoms also shows a comparatively higher IEa, consistent with the tendency of the icosahedral framework to prefer electron uptake to reach a closed-shell anionic configuration rather than electron loss to form the cation.

The adiabatic electron affinity EAa generally increases with increasing cluster size, indicating that electron attachment becomes progressively more stabilized for the resulting anions as the cluster grows. Notably, the B3Al10, B3@Al13 and B3@Al16 exhibit pronounced EAa maxima, with values of approximately 3.0–3.1 eV (cf.Fig. 5 and Table S7). Although these peaks are lower than that of Al13 (EAa ∼3.4 eV), they remain substantial and keep them to be qualified as quasi-halogens. The vertical detachment energies (VDEs) further support the enhanced stability of the corresponding anions B3Al10−, B3@Al13− and B3@Al16−, with values of approximately 3.0–3.2 eV, which are higher than those of the neighboring sizes (cf. Table S7).

While both B3Al10 and B3@Al16 are radicals like Al13, B3@Al13 is a closed-shell singlet species whose large EA is a quite remarkable feature. Let us note that the EA = 3.1 eV of B3@Al13 is larger than the EA = 2.6 eV of the pure isovalent Al16 counterpart (cf.Fig. 5B). These size-specific clusters also serve as key structural building units in the anionic series, underscoring the importance of a favourable match between geometric structure and electronic configuration, as discussed in the next section.

Electronic structure's evolution in high electron affinity clusters B3Al10, B3@Al13 and B3@Al16

The B3Al10, B3@Al13 and B3@Al16 are determined to possess high electron affinities, reflecting a strong tendency to accept additional electron(s) and thereby attain closed-shell electronic configurations in the anionic or di-anionic charge states.

For the neutral B3Al10, the lowest-energy structure is close to an icosahedral shape, and can be regarded as an Al13-type icosahedral framework that is slightly truncated at one pentagonal-pyramidal cap, due to the smaller radius of boron at that site. Although this geometry is not a perfect icosahedron like Al13, its electronic structure still follows an Al13-like shell-filling pattern and shows a clear preference for reaching a closed-shell configuration in the anionic state. Specifically, the neutral B3Al10 cluster adopts a doublet ground state with a 39-electron open-shell configuration of [(1S)2(1P)6 (1D)10(2S)2(2P)6(1F)13] (cf.Fig. 6), corresponding to one electron missing from the 1F molecular orbital (the 1Fy(3x2−y2)). Upon electron attachment, this single vacancy becomes filled, yielding a full closed-shell anion having a 40-valence-electron configuration of [(1S)2(1P)6(2S)2(1D)10(2P)6(1F)14], thereby enhancing the stability of a magic cluster.

β-Spin electron configuration of the doublet n.13.1 isomer at the TPSSh/6-311+G(d) level. The corresponding α-spin electron configuration is provided in Fig. S15 (SI).

For the neutral B3@Al16, the two lowest-lying isomers n.19.1 and n.19.2 are nearly degenerate, differing from each other in energy by only 2.5 kcal mol−1. The n.19.1 corresponds to a more distorted configuration, whereas the n.19.2 exhibits a C2v symmetry and is structurally consistent with the anionic global minimum (the a.19.1). Upon electron attachment, the C2v isomer becomes further stabilized, and the resulting anion is more stable than the distorted alternative by 7.1 kcal mol−1. In particular, the n.19.2 adopts a doublet ground state with a 57-electron open-shell electronic configuration of [(1S)2(1P)6(1D)10(1F)14(2S)2(2P)6(2D)10(3S)2(3P)5] (cf.Fig. 7), corresponding to one electron deficient in the 3P-MO (the 3Px). Upon electron attachment, the 3P-MO becomes fully occupied, yielding a closed-shell anion having 58-valence-electron configuration of [(1S)2(1P)6(1D)10(1F)14(2S)2(2P)6(2D)10(3S)2(3P)6] for the a.9.1.

β-Spin electron configuration of the doublet n.19.2 isomer at the TPSSh/6-311+G(d) level. The corresponding α-spin electron configuration is provided in Fig. S16 (SI).

As mentioned above, unlike the neutral B3Al10 and B3@Al16 which have doublet ground states, the most stable neutral B3@Al13 cluster (n.16.1 isomer) has a closed-shell singlet ground state. Structurally, it features a B3 ring sandwiched between a 6-atom triangular face and a 7-atom hexagonal face, and possesses an inherent closed-shell of 48-valence-electron configuration of [(1S)2(1P)6(1D)10(1F)14(2S)2(2P)6(2D)8] (cf.Fig. 8), with the HOMO and LUMO belonging to the 2D-MOs (HOMO: 2Dyz and LUMO: 2Dxy). The corresponding-LUMO gap amounts to ∼0.6 eV, facilitating electron attachment to give the anion with a 49-electron configuration of [(1S)2(1P)6(1D)10(1F)14(2S)2(2P)6(2D)9] for the global-minimum anion (the a.16.1 isomer). This anionic a.16.1 isomer is markedly stabilized, lying 12.4 kcal mol−1 below the next-lowest anionic isomer. Further electron attachment leads to the di-anion with a fully filled-2D shell of 50-electron configuration of [(1S)2(1P)6(1D)10(1F)14(2S)2(2P)6(2D)10]. The di-anionic structure is exceptionally stabilized, exceeding the second lowest di-anionic isomer by more than 18 kcal mol−1. Consistent with such an electron-count stabilization, B3@Al13 also shows the largest electron affinity of ∼3.1 eV among the three clusters discussed here. Chemical bonding

In the neutral B3Al10, B3@Al13 and B3@Al16 clusters, natural electron population analysis reveals a common charge trend for Al atoms, but a clear difference in how charge is distributed over the B3 unit between the exohedral and endohedral structures. Overall, Al atoms carry positive charges (typically +0.2 to +0.7 electron), whereas B atoms are negatively charged, indicating polarized Alδ+–Bδ− bonds.

For the exohedral B3Al10 cluster having an unpaired electron, the two outer B atoms bear charges of about −1.2, while the central B atom is more negative of ∼−1.7. For the endohedral B3@Al13 and B3@Al16 clusters, the central B atoms carry approximately −1.3 and −1.7, respectively, and the outer B atoms become even more negative, reaching a larger charge of −2.0. This shift toward a stronger negative charge localization on the B atoms is a key factor allowing a distinction between both exohedral and endohedral arrangements.

The corresponding natural electron populations further indicate pronounced orbital hybridization. On Al atoms, the electron population on 3s atomic orbital (AO) decreases by ∼0.7–0.9 electron, while the AO-3p electron population increases by ∼0.2–0.4 electron, denoting a 3s–3p hybridization. On B atoms, a clear 2s → 2p electron redistribution is observed. In exohedral B3Al10 cluster, the central B atom shows an ∼1 e decrease in AO-2s and an ∼2 electron increase in AO-2p, whereas in endohedral B3@Al13 and B3@Al16 clusters, the central B atom exhibits an ∼1.5 electron decrease in AO-2s accompanied by an ∼3 electron increase in AO-2p. Taken together with the negative charges on B atoms, these population shifts are consistent with a tendency of electron transfer from Al atoms toward the B3 fragment.

Consistently, the Wiberg bond indices indicate that Al atoms have values of ∼3, while B atoms show larger values near 4, suggesting that the boron atoms participate more effectively in the bonding network and act as key bonding centers within the clusters. In addition, Fig. S20–S22 (SI file) show that the electrons are delocalized over the cluster framework rather than being confined to localized two-center bonds; the bonding is dominated by extended multicenter interactions throughout the cluster skeletons.

Coherent ring current in the linear-B3@Al18 cluster

The coherent π-electron rotation treatment is applied to generate the magnetic ring current maps in the linear B3c.21.1 isomer. This structure comprises six mutually parallel planes, each containing three Al atoms arranged as an (approximately) equilateral triangle. Three representative planes, labeled as Plane 1, Plane 2, and Plane 3, in the positive-z region (z > 0) (cf.Fig. 11A). In conventional aromatic molecules, π-MOs are typically dominated by a single p-type atomic orbital, most commonly the pz orbital when the z-axis is perpendicular to the ring plane.62,63 In cluster systems, however, the relevant MOs may involve all three p-type orbitals, px, py, and pz. Accordingly, eqn (4) (cf. Computational methods) is used to compute the bond current <J(t)ij> along each edge of the triangular unit under two orbital-selection schemes: (i) including only pz contributions and (ii) including the full set of p-type orbitals (px, py and pz).

The pair of degenerate excited states for the c.21.1 isomer are determined from time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) calculations performed at the TPSSh/6-311+G(d) level. The excitation energies, configuration weights and dominant MO coefficients are summarized in Table S10 (SI file). Ten pairs of degenerate excited states are obtained (Table S11), and all correspond to optically allowed transitions from the ground state, as indicated by non-zero transition dipole moments (TDM).

First, the cluster is excited by a left-handed circularly polarized laser pulse propagating along the positive the z-axis. The electronic wave function evolves from the ground state to the target pair of excited states. The bond currents are evaluated at the time of maximum coherence between the two excited states, where Im[ρ12] = 0.5.

The bond currents are then evaluated for each degenerate pair Eh (h = 1–10). For each bond between two neighboring aluminum atoms, the bond current < Jij> are obtained by intergrating over a surface (Sij) perpendicular to the line connecting atoms i and j. Within the same plane, the normal vector n⃑ij associated with the three surfaces (Sij) are arranged in a clockwise manner around the z-axis. The resulting values for each plane are listed in Table S10 (SI file) for both orbital-selection schemes. A positive value of <Jij> indicates that the current density flows through the surface (Sij) in the direction of the corresponding normal vector.

For a given pair, the bond-current magnitudes are identical for the three edges within a plane. Moreover, planes that are symmetric with respect to the middle plane (z = 0) display the same current direction and magnitude.

Within the classical Hückel model, a π-MO is described as a linear combination of pz-atomic orbitals. For a pair of degenerate excited states, πx and πy, whose TDM vectors are oriented along the positive of x- and y-axes, respectively. The superposition πx + iπy (πx − iπy) corresponds to eigenstates of the z-component of the angular momentum, with eigenvalues of angular momentum lz = +ℏ (ℏ). In the present case, a state with positive angular momentum of is selectively excited by left-handed circularly polarized laser. Consequently, the electron undergoes counter-clockwise rotation of electron is triggered around z-axis, because negative charge of electron, the bond currents of almost excited states are positive.

For the full p-orbital (px, py and pz orbital), the red lines indicate the redistribution of bond currents relative to the pz-only description (cf.Fig. 10). For nearly all degenerate pairs, inclusion of the px and py orbitals increases the bond-current magnitude relative to the pz only estimate, showing that the current cannot be described by a purely pz-derived contribution. More specifically, on Plane 1 and Plane 2, the bond currents obtained from the full p-orbital differ significantly from those calculated using only pz-orbital, whereas both cases yield approximate results for the all of pair of excited states on Plane 3.

Regarding the current direction, reversals of bond currents are observed for several pairs of excited state: on Plane 1 and Plane 2 for E4 and E5, and on Plane 1 for E8. Nevertheless, in the plane exhibiting the largest bond current magnitude, the current direction remains consistent with that predicted by the only pz orbital case. This result indicates that the dominant rotational motion of the electron is still preserved, corresponding to a counterclockwise around the z-axis.

The most pronounced enhancement is observed for the E8 pair, particularly on Planes 1 and 2, whereas the increase on Plane 3 is comparatively modest. Specifically, the bond current rises from 14.84 µA to 101.23 µA on Plane 1 (but direction is reversed) and from 37.0 µA to 100.52 µA on Plane 2 (direction is un-reversed), while it increases from 7.0 µA to 13.0 µA on Plane 3. The magnitude of the bond current can also be understood from the dominant transitions MO associated with the relevant excited states. According to eqn (4), the bond current depends on the contributions from pairs of occupied MOs coupled to the same virtual MO, or from pairs of virtual MOs from the same occupied MO. The enhanced bond current magnitude observed for E8 can be attributed to the contribution arising the overlap between the occupied MOs ϕ123 and ϕ124 when px and py orbitals are included in the bond current expression. However, in the pz-only description, the LCAO coefficients of the pz AO in ϕ124 vanish for all aluminum atoms in the cluster system, and therefore this contribution is absent. However, the reversal of bond current direction on Plane 1 cannot be explained within the present framework.

The direction of the current-density vectors is determined by the coherent rotation of electrons arising from the superposition of two degenerate excited states, Φx and Φy, which are associated with transition dipole moments oriented along the x- and y-axes, respectively.62

In the present system, the coherent ring current associated with the E8 excitation originates from the pair of degenerate states S40 and S41 (Table S9), which correspond to ϕx and (−1ϕy), respectively. The negative sign arises from the opposite orientation of the transition dipole moment of S41 along the y-axis (Table S10 in the SI). Consequently, the superposition S40–iS41 is equivalent to ϕx + iϕy. As a result, the induced current density vectors shown in Fig. 11C and D are arranged clockwise around the z-axis in both cases, namely the only pz orbital case and full p-orbital case. Owing to the negative charge of electron, the current density pattern corresponds to the counter-clockwise rotation of the electron around z-axis, which is associated with a positive angular momentum lz = +1ℏ, transferred from the spin angular momentum of the left-handed circularly polarized laser.

Concluding remarks

In the theoretical present study, the B3-substituted aluminum B3Aln−3+/0/− clusters with n = 3–21 are systematically explored using the TPSSh/6-311+G(d) methods, and several important conclusions emerge as follows:

• The resulting structures of B3Aln−3+/0/− clusters are not well described as simple B3 substitution into, or attachment onto, an Aln+/0/− host. Instead, they are best viewed as being assembled around a connected and stable B3 core while Al atoms progressively build up the surrounding framework. An exohedral-to-endohedral transition occurs at the size of thirteen Al atoms, and a triangular-to-linear rearrangement of B3 appears at the size of eighteen Al atoms for the neutral and cationic series, but such a rearrangement is suppressed for the anions due to the exceptional stabilization of the B3− anion.

• Thermodynamically, B3 incorporation enhances the stability of the Al framework in all charge states, with B3Aln−3− being the most stable overall. Pronounced EAa maxima occur for B3Al10, B3@Al13 and B3@Al16 with values of ∼3.0–3.1 eV. Because the EA of the icosahedral Al13 radical amounts up to ∼3.6 eV, the halogen character of the substituted clusters can be regarded as being kept, even with smaller EAa. These maxima reflect a strong tendency to accept additional electron(s) to reach closed-shell configurations, namely [(1S)2(1P)6(2S)2(1D)10(2P)6(1F)14] for B3Al10−, [(1S)2(1P)6(1D)10(1F)14(2S)2(2P)6(2D)10(3S)2(3P)6] for B3@Al16−, and [(1S)2(1P)6(1D)10(1F)14(2S)2(2P)6(2D)10] for B3@Al132−, making them magic clusters.

• Natural electron population analysis reveals polarized Alδ+–Bδ− bonds interactions and a tendency for electron transfer from Al toward the B3 fragment.

• The coherent-rotation analysis reveals a clear circulating magnetic bond current in the linear-B3c.21.1 isomer. Including the full p-orbital set (px, py and pz) markedly strengthens the predicted current as compared to the pz-only treatment. The most pronounced effect occurs for the E8 degenerate pair, where the bond current increases from 15 to 62 µA on the Plane 1, from 37 to 145 µA on the Plane 2, and from 7 to 17 µA on the Plane 3 when px, py and pz contributions are included. Consistently, the current density maps show a distinct circulation on the middle plane (z = 0), indicating that the magnetic ring current response in this series of cluster requires a combined px, py and pz participation rather than a purely pz-derived π-current description. The circulation of magnetic ring current is related to the aromatic character of the clusters involved.
