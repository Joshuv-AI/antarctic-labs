Practice patterns of pulmonologists regarding extended anticoagulation therapy after pulmonary embolism
Authors: Ezgi Erdem Türe, Hasret Gizem Kurt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/ObjectivesDetermining the optimal duration of anticoagulation after pulmonary embolism remains a clinical challenge. Although guidelines recommend individualized decisions based on recurrence and bleeding risk, no universally accepted algorithm exists. This study aimed to evaluate pulmonologists’ practice patterns regarding extended anticoagulation in Türkiye.MethodsA 14 item questionnaire based on current international guidelines on pulmonary embolism management was distributed to pulmonology specialists. The survey collected information on physicians’ demographics, preferred duration of extended anticoagulation therapy, anticoagulant choice, use of imaging or laboratory tests when determining treatment duration, and reliance on clinical guidelines. Associations between professional characteristics and clinical decision-making patterns were analyzed using chi-square tests and Fisher’s exact tests.ResultsA total of 159 pulmonologists participated in the study. Most physicians (76.1%) preferred extending anticoagulation therapy for an additional three months, and 88.1% reported using bleeding risk assessment tools when determining treatment duration. The most common reasons for extended therapy were high thrombus burden (51.6%), transient or reversible venous thromboembolism risk factors (50.3%), prior thrombolytic therapy (47.2%), and persistent deep vein thrombosis (45.3%). Direct oral anticoagulants were the most frequently preferred agents, with rivaroxaban selected by 74.8% of respondents. More than half of respondents routinely requested imaging or laboratory tests at the third month, most commonly lower extremity venous doppler ultrasonography (61.6%), echocardiography (56.0%), and computed tomography pulmonary angiography (52.2%). Additionally, 67.9% of participants reported that current clinical guidelines were partially sufficient for guiding treatment decisions.ConclusionsConsiderable variability exists in self-reported practice patterns regarding extended anticoagulation after pulmonary embolism among the participating pulmonologists. Introduction

Pulmonary embolism (PE), the most severe clinical manifestation of venous thromboembolism (VTE), represents an important global health problem. The 2023 American Heart Association report estimated an annual incidence of approximately 393,000 cases [1]. Globally, the annual incidence of PE has been reported to range between 39 and 115 cases per 100,000 individuals [2].

PE is associated with substantial mortality and morbidity both during the acute phase and in long-term follow-up [2, 3]. The management of VTE consists of several phases, including an initiation phase, an initial treatment phase, and an extended treatment phase. Current clinical guidelines generally recommend anticoagulation therapy for 3–6 months in patients with acute PE. At the end of this period, clinicians must decide whether anticoagulation should be discontinued or continued as extended therapy beyond the initial treatment phase [2–5].

The decision to continue anticoagulation largely depends on the underlying risk factors. Classification of VTE events as major transient, minor transient, persistent, or no identifiable risk factors plays a central role in determining the need for extended therapy [2]. During follow-up after pulmonary embolism, recurrent VTE has been reported in approximately 14.1% of patients, corresponding to an annual recurrence risk of 6.4% [6]. In clinical practice, treatment decisions should balance the risks of VTE recurrence and bleeding [2–4]. Although several risk assessment tools have been proposed [7, 8], no single validated model adequately guides decisions regarding the optimal duration of anticoagulation.

Consequently, determining which patients should discontinue anticoagulation after three months and which should receive extended therapy remains one of the most challenging aspects of clinical practice. As a result, considerable variability in clinical practice may occur when physicians determine the optimal duration of anticoagulation therapy. While international guidelines provide general recommendations, a universally accepted clinical algorithm for treatment discontinuation or extension is still lacking.

This study aimed to evaluate the practice patterns of pulmonologists who participated in this survey regarding extended anticoagulation therapy in patients with pulmonary embolism, including preferred treatment duration, choice of anticoagulant agents, and factors influencing decisions to continue or discontinue therapy.

Materials and methods

A 14-item questionnaire was developed for the purposes of this study based on current international guidelines on pulmonary embolism and was not adapted from a previously published survey instrument. After drafting the survey, its clarity and comprehensibility were pilot-tested among five physicians to assess readability and clinical comprehensibility. The final version was distributed via the Turkish Respiratory Research Association mailing list, through which approximately 1032 physicians were reached (of whom 363 were residents and the remainder were specialists), and via professional WhatsApp groups with 570 members. While the overlap between individuals reached through these two channels cannot be precisely determined, the survey platform was configured to accept only one submission per email account, thereby preventing duplicate responses. Consequently, the exact number of eligible physicians who received the survey, as well as the overall response rate, could not be calculated. The questionnaire is provided in Supplementary File 1.

Only pulmonary specialists were eligible to participate in the study. Pulmonology residents were excluded. Participants who provided consent were directed to complete the survey, and responses to all questions were required for submission. The questionnaire included both single choice and multiple choice items. No personal identifying information was collected.

Clinical management strategies for patients with pulmonary embolism were assessed, focusing specifically on non-cancer patients and excluding special populations such as pregnancy. The survey collected data on demographic and professional characteristics (age, years of practice, type of institution), as well as clinical approaches to pulmonary thromboembolism management. Specifically, it assessed decisions regarding extended anticoagulation therapy, duration of extended treatment, preferred anticoagulant agents, laboratory or imaging investigations requested at the third month when considering treatment discontinuation or continuation, timing of such investigations, assessment of bleeding risk scores, follow-up duration during extended therapy, use of clinical guidelines, and physicians’ perceptions regarding the adequacy of existing guidelines. Transient or reversible VTE risk factors were defined as temporary clinical conditions associated with venous thromboembolism, including surgery, trauma, temporary immobilization or hormone therapy.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee (protocol code 1470 and date of approval 25.06.2025). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as absolute frequencies and percentages. Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using chi-square (χ²) tests. When expected cell counts were insufficient for standard chi-square assumptions, Fisher’s exact tests were applied as appropriate. Effect sizes for categorical associations were assessed using Cramer’s V. Associations between physicians’ years of professional experience and clinical decision making patterns were analyzed. Multiple exploratory comparisons were performed, statistically significant p values were considered descriptive and hypothesis-generating only. Linear-by-linear association tests were performed to evaluate trends across ordered categories such as years of professional experience. All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) version 26.0 software package.

Results

A total of 159 physicians participated in the study. Among the participants, 52.8% (n = 84) were aged 30–39 years, 28.9% (n = 46) were aged 40–49 years, 13.8% (n = 22) were aged 50–59 years, and 4.4% (n = 7) were aged over 59 years. Regarding years of professional experience, 16.4% (n = 26) had 1–5 years of experience, 31.4% (n = 50) had 6–10 years, 28.3% (n = 45) had 11–20 years, and 23.9% (n = 38) had more than 20 years of professional practice. With respect to institutional affiliation, 26.4% (n = 42) were working in state hospitals, 18.9% (n = 30) in university hospitals, 37.1% (n = 59) in training and research hospitals, 6.3% (n = 10) in city hospitals, and 11.3% (n = 18) in private hospitals (Table 1).

A minority of respondents (n = 7, 4.4%) indicated that they would terminate therapy at three months without extension. The majority of participants (n = 121, 76.1%) favored extending treatment for an additional three months. The majority of participants (88.1%) reported assessing bleeding risk using validated risk scores when determining extended treatment duration. The most frequently reported reason for extending therapy was a high thrombus burden (n = 82, 51.6%). The presence of transient or reversible risk factors for VTE was reported by 80 physicians (50.3%), the presence of persistent deep vein thrombosis (DVT) on imaging by 72 respondents (45.3%), and prior thrombolytic therapy for massive thrombus at initial presentation by 75 participants (47.2%). Elevated troponin levels at the time of diagnosis were less commonly cited (n = 36, 22.6%). Notably, 63 participants (39.6%) reported that no identifiable risk factor for VTE had been detected in the patient (Table 2).

Preferences regarding extended anticoagulation therapy

When the data were analyzed according to years of professional experience, a statistically significant association was observed between professional experience and the decision to extend anticoagulation therapy in the presence of persistent DVT at three months (χ² = 8.588, p = 0.035, Cramer’s V = 0.232). Respondents with more than 20 years of professional experience reported a higher tendency to favor extended anticoagulation therapy compared with less experienced groups. A significant linear trend was also identified (linear-by-linear association p = 0.020), indicating an increasing tendency toward extended anticoagulation with greater professional experience (Fig. 1). No statistically significant associations were observed between years of professional experience and the other factors influencing decisions regarding extended anticoagulation therapy. Regarding follow-up frequency during extended therapy, 48.4% of physicians reported scheduling follow-up visits every three months, whereas 37.7% reassessed patients prior to making a decision on discontinuation of extended anticoagulation therapy (Table 3).

When making decisions about treatment duration beyond three months, 69.8% of specialists reported consulting a second specialist in selected complex cases, whereas 48 (30.2%) participants reported making decisions independently (Table 4). Years of professional experience was strongly associated with consultation behavior regarding another specialist when making decisions regarding extended therapy (χ² = 20.417, p < 0.001, Cramer’s V = 0.358). Physicians with fewer years of experience were significantly more likely to seek consultation, whereas more experienced physicians tended to make decisions independently. A significant linear trend was observed (linear-by-linear association p < 0.001), indicating that physicians with greater professional experience were generally less likely to seek consultation. Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the practice patterns of participating pulmonologists regarding extended anticoagulation therapy in patients with PE. Considerable variability was observed in both the clinical parameters influencing decisions on extended therapy and the diagnostic investigations requested before treatment discontinuation. Rivaroxaban was the most commonly preferred anticoagulant agent, while lower extremity venous Doppler ultrasonography, echocardiography, and CT pulmonary angiography were the most frequently requested investigations during follow up. Most participants reported extending treatment to six months regardless of patients’ VTE risk factor status. In addition, high thrombus burden emerged as a major determinant influencing decisions regarding extended anticoagulation therapy. Notably, most participants considered current clinical guidelines to be only partially sufficient for guiding decisions regarding extended anticoagulation therapy. This discrepancy between guideline recommendations and physicians’ self reported management practices may partly reflect the absence of a clear and universally accepted algorithm for determining treatment duration.

Although earlier studies frequently used the “provoked” and “unprovoked” VTE classification, in which provoked VTE referred to events associated with temporary or reversible risk factors such as surgery, trauma, immobilization, pregnancy, or hormone therapy, and unprovoked VTE referred to events occurring in the absence of such identifiable triggering factors, the 2019 ESC guidelines emphasized a more individualized risk assessment approach based on transient, persistent, or absent identifiable risk factors when considering extended anticoagulation therapy. The duration of anticoagulant therapy should be individualized according to the risks of recurrence and bleeding [2–4]. Several clinical prediction models have been proposed to estimate bleeding and recurrence risk [7–11]; however, current guidelines still do not provide a definitive algorithm for determining the optimal duration of anticoagulation therapy. Recurrence rates are higher after unprovoked VTE compared with provoked events [6]. According to the current guidelines, anticoagulation therapy may be discontinued after three months in patients with a first VTE event associated with major transient or reversible risk factors. In patients without major transient risk factors, a treatment duration of 3–6 months is also generally recommended, and extending therapy beyond 6–12 months is not routinely recommended for all patients [2, 3, 5]. Evidence from clinical studies suggests that extended anticoagulation reduces the risk of recurrence during the treatment period; however, this protective effect does not persist after anticoagulation is discontinued [12, 13].

In our study, most physicians preferred to extend anticoagulation therapy, and approximately one-fifth of participants believed that three months of treatment may be insufficient to prevent recurrence. The majority of physicians reported making treatment decisions based on bleeding risk assessment in accordance with guideline recommendations, and the most commonly preferred duration of extended anticoagulation therapy was six months. Previous physician surveys conducted in other countries have reported similar variability in clinical practice. In a survey conducted in the Netherlands, 73% of physicians reported prescribing extended anticoagulation therapy in patients with unprovoked VTE. The majority of physicians rarely used formal bleeding risk scores (62%) or recurrence risk models (77%), and many participants reported that existing risk scores were difficult to implement in routine clinical practice. Most physicians expressed the need for a clinical tool that integrates both bleeding and recurrence risk to guide treatment decisions [14]. An Australian physician survey reported that 41% of clinicians preferred fixed treatment durations of 6 or 12 months for provoked pulmonary embolism, whereas 83% recommended similar treatment durations for unprovoked pulmonary embolism [15]. In another physician survey, 95% of participants recommended discontinuation of anticoagulation after 3–6 months for provoked events, whereas 97% supported long-term anticoagulation for unprovoked VTE [16]. In a multicenter cohort study conducted in France, extended anticoagulation therapy was prescribed in 71.5% of patients. Although many patients with identifiable risk factors did not receive extended therapy, most patients with unprovoked PE or DVT were treated with prolonged anticoagulation. Notably, a high bleeding risk defined by the VTE-BLEED score was not significantly associated with withholding extended anticoagulation [17]. Taken together, these findings suggest considerable variability in physicians’ approaches to extended anticoagulation therapy. Similar to the findings of our study, most clinicians tend to prefer approximately six months of treatment, although individual decisions often differ. This variability may partly reflect the absence of a definitive algorithm in current guidelines and the continued reliance on individualized clinical judgment when determining anticoagulation duration. Although the recently published 2026 AHA/ACC pulmonary embolism guideline [3] updated the level of evidence for several aspects of venous thromboembolism management, we believe that heterogeneity in physicians’ treatment approaches will likely continue in routine clinical practice.

Current guidelines primarily support extended anticoagulation in patients with unprovoked VTE, persistent risk factors, or increased recurrence risk [2–4]. In contrast, severity related markers such as thrombus burden, thrombolytic therapy, and imaging findings are frequently considered in clinical practice despite more limited and inconsistent evidence regarding their role in determining treatment duration. Notably, high thrombus burden was reported more frequently than no identifiable VTE risk factor as a determinant of extended therapy, despite the stronger guideline support for recurrence-based risk stratification. A multicenter cohort study has reported that patients with intermediate-high or high-risk pulmonary embolism are more likely to receive extended anticoagulation therapy [17]. Although indices such as the modified Miller score can quantify thrombus burden, current evidence does not uniformly support their routine use in guiding treatment duration or decisions regarding reperfusion strategies [3]. Nevertheless, some studies have suggested that higher pulmonary vascular obstruction indices may be associated with an increased risk of recurrent VTE [2], and thrombolytic therapy may reduce recurrence in selected patients [18]. Despite these findings, physicians in our study tended to favor longer durations of anticoagulation therapy in patients who had received thrombolytic therapy, possibly reflecting a perception of greater disease severity.

Meta-analyses have also demonstrated that patients with concomitant DVT and pulmonary embolism have higher recurrence rates than those with isolated pulmonary embolism [19]. Consistently, multicenter cohort studies have reported that patients without transient risk factors or with concomitant DVT are more likely to receive extended anticoagulation therapy [17]. However, physician surveys indicate substantial variability regarding whether thrombus location or disease severity should influence treatment duration, with no clear consensus among clinicians [16]. Similarly, in our study, physicians showed considerable variation in their decisions regarding treatment extension. Although evidence suggests that the presence of DVT may increase recurrence risk, only approximately half of the participants reported that they would prescribe extended therapy in such cases. Interestingly, the likelihood of extending anticoagulation in the presence of persistent DVT increased with years of professional experience, suggesting that clinicians may rely more heavily on accumulated clinical experience when making treatment decisions in the absence of clear guideline recommendations. Collectively, these findings suggest that physicians may place greater emphasis on perceived disease severity markers than on strictly guideline-supported recurrence stratification criteria when determining anticoagulation duration in clinical practice.

DOACs were the most frequently preferred anticoagulant agents in our study, with rivaroxaban being the most commonly selected drug. Current clinical guidelines recommend the use of DOACs over vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) in patients with venous thromboembolism without cancer, both during the initial treatment phase and in the extended treatment phase. Compared with VKAs, DOACs have demonstrated similar efficacy with a lower risk of major bleeding and greater ease of use, which has contributed to their widespread adoption in the long-term management of pulmonary embolism. However, current guidelines do not provide a clear preference among individual DOAC agents [2, 3, 5]. Similar findings have been reported in physician surveys. In a survey conducted in the Netherlands, 45% of clinicians reported that they always prescribe DOACs for extended anticoagulation therapy, most commonly rivaroxaban followed by apixaban, whereas none reported always prescribing VKAs [14]. Likewise, an Australian physician survey found that 76% of clinicians preferred DOACs over vitamin K antagonists for the treatment of pulmonary embolism in patients without contraindications [15]. Although DOACs were also the preferred agents in our study, the relatively high rate of warfarin use observed in our cohort may reflect reimbursement limitations for DOACs within the Turkish healthcare system.

In our study, the majority of physicians reported performing follow-up tests at the end of the third month of treatment. More than half of the participants requested lower extremity venous Doppler ultrasonography and echocardiography, while approximately half preferred CT pulmonary angiography and one-third requested V/Q scintigraphy. In addition, nearly one-third of physicians indicated that they preferred to use the same imaging modality that had been used for the initial diagnosis when deciding whether to discontinue or extend anticoagulation therapy. However, current guidelines do not recommend the routine use of CT angiography, D-dimer testing, or ultrasonography for determining treatment duration) [2, 3, 5]. Although persistently elevated D-dimer levels after discontinuation of anticoagulation have been associated with an increased risk of recurrent VTE [20, 21], anticoagulant treatment duration should be individualized based on an assessment of recurrence and bleeding risks [4]. Similar patterns have been observed in physician surveys from other countries. In an Australian survey, 35% of clinicians reported routinely requesting V/Q scans before discontinuing anticoagulation in patients with unprovoked pulmonary embolism, whereas only 5% requested CT scans and 17% requested both imaging modalities. In the same study, echocardiography was used routinely by 16% of physicians during follow-up, while others preferred to perform echocardiography in patients with persistent symptoms or with evidence of right ventricular dysfunction at diagnosis. Interestingly, only 18% of physicians reported frequently using D-dimer testing when determining anticoagulation duration [15].

Guidelines recommend periodic reassessment of the need for anticoagulation in patients receiving extended therapy [2, 4]. In addition, V/Q scintigraphy remains the most effective screening tool for chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH). Current evidence suggests that the optimal time to evaluate patients for CTEPH is during routine follow-up approximately three months after acute pulmonary embolism [2]. In our study, most physicians scheduled follow-up visits every three months, and the relatively high rates of echocardiography and V/Q scintigraphy may partly reflect attempts to evaluate patients for possible pulmonary hypertension. Although ESC guidelines recommend further investigations primarily in symptomatic patients, routine screening of asymptomatic patients is not supported by current evidence [2]. These findings may reflect the uncertainty clinicians face when determining anticoagulation duration in daily practice. As a result, physicians may rely on additional diagnostic investigations despite limited guideline support. Follow-up strategies should therefore be individualized rather than routinely applied.

In our study, most physicians reported that clinical guidelines played an important role in their decision-making process. Notably, national guidelines, which are largely derived from international recommendations, were more frequently used by physicians in Türkiye. This preference may be explained by the fact that these national guidelines summarize and adapt recommendations from multiple international guidelines into a single practical framework for local clinical practice. However, the majority of participants indicated that guidelines were only partially sufficient and that clinical judgment remained necessary when making treatment decisions. We also observed that the likelihood of seeking a second specialist opinion decreased with increasing years of professional experience. This finding may reflect the influence of accumulated clinical experience, which may lead more experienced physicians to rely more on their own clinical judgment when making management decisions. Similar findings have been reported in physician surveys from other countries. For example, an Australian physician survey demonstrated that 77% of clinicians reported basing their pulmonary embolism management decisions on one or more clinical guidelines [15].

This study has several limitations. The main limitation of this study is the sampling strategy. Because the number of eligible pulmonologists who actually received the survey, overlap between distribution channels, and non-responder characteristics could not be determined, response rate and representativeness could not be assessed. Therefore, substantial selection bias is possible, and the findings should not be interpreted as estimates of national pulmonology practice in Türkiye. In addition, the questionnaire was pilot-tested only for readability and clinical comprehensibility, and no formal validation process assessing content validity, construct validity, or reliability was performed. Participation was also limited to physicians who could be reached through the relevant communication channels, which may have affected the sample size and representativeness of the study population. Finally, the majority of respondents were relatively young physicians, which may have resulted in limited representation of more experienced clinicians.

Conclusions

The findings of this study suggest that clinical practice regarding extended anticoagulation after pulmonary embolism varies considerably among physicians, largely reflecting the absence of a universally accepted algorithm to guide these decisions. This observation highlights the weight of individual clinical experience in decision-making as well as a perceived need for more practical risk assessment approaches and clearer clinical guidance in this field. Further studies with larger numbers of participants are warranted to enable a broader perspective on these findings.
