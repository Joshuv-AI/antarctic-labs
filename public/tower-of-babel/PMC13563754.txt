Knowledge, attitudes, and practices on injection site complications among small ruminant animal health providers in Kumasi, Ghana
Authors: Augustine Teye, Bismark Gyasi, Albright Stephen Effah Ameyaw, Tony Opoku-Agyemang, Raphael Deladem Folitse, Prince Nana Takyi, Alisa Elise Wepere Luguterah, Benjamin Obukowho Emikpe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInjection-site complications are a poorly recognised problem in small ruminant production in Ghana. The improper injection can lead to tissue damage, abscesses, fibrosis, drug residues, lower meat quality and carcass condemnation, which can cause economic and public health issues. Veterinarians are legally qualified to give injections, but in practice, para-veterinarians and animal herders often do so, as they are in close contact with livestock and are often responsible for immediate animal care. The aim of this study was to evaluate the knowledge, attitudes and practices of animal health providers on injection-site complications in Kumasi, Ghana.MethodsA comparative cross-sectional study was carried out among 230 respondents including veterinarians (59), para-veterinarians (18), animal herders (143) and meat handlers (10) with a structured questionnaire. Descriptive statistics and Chi-square tests were used to collect and analyse data on knowledge, attitudes, anatomical injection-site preferences and injection practices. Retrospective abattoir records from January to August 2025 were also reviewed to determine the prevalence and distribution of injection-site lesions in slaughtered small ruminants.ResultsOccupation, age, gender, years of experience, and formal training were significantly associated with the level of knowledge (p < 0.05). Veterinarians had the highest awareness of injection-site complications and correct anatomical injection sites, whereas animal herders had lower awareness, especially about tissue necrosis, contamination-related infections, and improper drug absorption. The neck area was the most common site for intramuscular injection by veterinarians, while the thigh and gluteal muscles were the most common sites for para-veterinarians and animal herders. A total of 100 injection-site lesions were found in 43,483 slaughtered animals during abattoir inspection, with lesions mainly located in the thigh area. There was a significant association between formal training and positive attitudes with good injection practices (p < 0.05).ConclusionThere were significant differences in knowledge and practices about injection-site complications among animal health providers in Kumasi. Injection administration by para-veterinarians and herders, and inappropriate injection-site selection, may be factors in the lesions seen during meat inspection. Injection safety, meat quality and animal welfare can be enhanced through targeted training and inclusive veterinary extension programs that engage all livestock handlers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05647-w.

Background

Small ruminants play a crucial role in Ghana’s livestock economy, serving as a source of income, nutrition, and resilience for traders and households [1, 2]. Their management requires the frequent administration of injectable medications for treatment, and prevention of diseases [3, 4]. While injections are a common practice in veterinary medicine, improper techniques result in tissue damage, abscesses, necrosis, fibrosis, and injuries from needles [5]. These complications can reduce meat quality, lower market value, weaken animal welfare, and result in carcass trimming or condemnation [6, 7].

Globally, ISCs have been associated with a lack of anatomical understanding, poor hygiene practices, inappropriate techniques, and a lack of training, particularly among non-formal animal health practitioners [8–10]. In Ghana, veterinarians, para-veterinarians, and herders all administer injections, but their training, competence, and adherence to best practices vary widely [11]. Despite field observations and abattoir reports showing injection-related lesions in small ruminants, there is no systematic documentation of the extent of the problem or its human-related drivers, which may include the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) of veterinarians, para-veterinarians, and herders regarding injection-site complications [4, 12].

Though veterinarians are the only ones with legal licensure to undertake injections within Ghana, para-veterinarians and herders often undertake such injections because of a lack of veterinary service reach and accessibility, as well as livestock owners’ dependence on alternative animal health practitioners [13]. Nevertheless, no study has addressed this gap within Ghana, and such a gap limits training and policy formulation for improvement [12, 14]. Farm owners and butchers could suffer economic losses as a result of such negative outcomes and also pose a welfare threat to animals, as well as reducing productivity and increasing treatment expenses with a possible condemnation of slaughtered animals at meat inspection [15]. The practice by para-professionals and livestock owners, often with no proper training regarding safe injection practice, contributes to this problem. Despite injectable medications being common for small ruminant treatment within Ghana, no study has addressed the levels of competencies within animal health practitioners within Ghana. To create focused interventions that increase injection safety, boost animal welfare, and protect meat quality for customers, it is crucial to understand the gaps in KAP of veterinarians and other animal handlers, such as para-veterinarians and herders who often perform these procedures due to limited veterinary coverage [4, 12].

Therefore, this study includes all veterinarians, para-veterinarians, and herders who administer injections in practice, regardless of legal authorisation, to better reflect the full range of behaviours influencing injection-site lesions.

Methodology

Study design

A cross-sectional survey was conducted to assess the knowledge, attitudes and practices of veterinarians, para-veterinarians, animal herders and meat handlers regarding injection-site complications in small ruminants from January to August 2025.

Study area and sampling

The survey was conducted in the capital city of Ashanti Region, Kumasi (Fig. 1). The city is ideal for researching injection-related issues in small ruminants due to its blend of urban and peri-urban livestock systems, cattle trade, and the wide range of animal health specialists. Participants were purposively selected to ensure inclusion of all relevant professional groups involved in administering injections: veterinarians, para-veterinarians, and animal herders. Veterinarians were identified from the Veterinary Services Directorate registry in the Kumasi Metropolitan Area, para-veterinarians and agro-vet shop employees from agro-vet directories and lists of members of the professional association, and animal herders from farm registries, extension officer records and farmer association membership lists.

This purposive sampling method enabled the inclusion of all possible behaviours and practices that could affect injection-site complications. The participants’ list initially comprised 250 individuals from available records, but after excluding incomplete entries, 230 respondents were included in the study. Of these, 59 were veterinarians (26%), 18 were para-veterinarians (8%), 143 were animal herders (62%) and 10 were meat handlers (4%), with the same proportions across professional categories.

The inclusion of para-veterinarians, herders, and meat handlers did not indicate that they were authorised to perform injections. These two groups (para-veterinarians and herders) were included because they frequently perform injections without being licensed, while meat handlers at the abattoir are responsible for packaging.

Data collection instrument and data

A structured survey, adapted from Cresswell et al. [7] and modified, was used to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of veterinarians, para-veterinarians, and animal herders in Kumasi regarding injection-site lesions in small ruminants. The questionnaire was divided into four sections: Demographics (age, sex, education level, years of experience, location); Knowledge (awareness of injection techniques, anatomical Knowledge of drug administration, knowledge of complications); Attitudes (attitudes towards injection practices and meat quality); and Practices (commonly used anatomical injection sites and lesion prevalence and Injection-site Selection).

Pre-launch testing was conducted with 12 participants not part of the study sites to assess understanding and practical application. The alpha value obtained was 0.76.

Cumulative data from the Kumasi Abattoir were examined retrospectively from January through August 2025, with a view to estimating prevalence and attributes of injection-site lesions found among slaughtered sheep and goats. All data included cumulative numbers of sheep and goats slaughtered monthly, cumulative numbers of injection-site lesions observed on monthly post-mortems, and regions where injection-site lesions accumulated. Data on injection-site lesions were obtained from abattoir records, in which lesions were routinely identified and documented by qualified slaughterhouse inspectors during standard meat inspection procedures.

Data analysis

The data from the questionnaire were entered into Microsoft Excel 2019, cleaned, coded and then exported to IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were used and results were presented as frequencies and percentages. The answers to the knowledge, attitude and practice (KAP) items were scored. Nine items of knowledge were evaluated for injection techniques and injection sites. Correct or positive responses were given a score of 1, and incorrect, uncertain, “No”, “Maybe”, “Not aware” or blank responses were given a score of 0. The total knowledge score was 9, and scores ≥ 5 were considered as good knowledge and scores < 5 as poor knowledge. Five items related to attitude, proper injection administration, aseptic techniques, withdrawal periods, and injection site lesions were scored. Responses of “yes” were given a score of 1 and responses of “no” or “don’t know” were given a score of 0. The total attitude score was 5, and scores ≥ 3 were considered as good attitude, while scores < 3 were considered as poor attitude. Five core practice items and four injection site preference items were evaluated for practice. Responses of “Always” or “Often” were given a score of 1, and “Sometimes”, “Rarely”, “Never”, or blank responses were given a score of 0. The total practice score was 9, and scores of ≥ 5 were considered good practice, while scores of < 5 were considered poor practice. Chi-square (χ²) analysis was used to assess associations between demographic characteristics and KAP levels with regards to injection sites in small ruminants. The correlation between knowledge, attitude and practice was also tested. The level of statistical significance was p < 0.05 or 95% confidence interval.

Results

Demographic characteristics of respondents

The demographic characteristics of the respondents are shown in Table 1. The respondents were predominantly in the 30–39 age group (38.3%), followed by those aged 20–29 (32.2%). Majority of the respondents were males (78.3%). Animal herders constituted the highest occupational group (62.2%), while veterinarians and para-veterinarians accounted for 25.7% and 7.8%, respectively. More than half of the respondents had 1–5 years of experience in animal handling and injection administration (55.2%). Less than half of the respondents (44.3%) reported having received formal training on injection administration in small ruminants, whereas 48.7% had no formal training.

Respondents’ knowledge of injection site

The knowledge assessment revealed a clear competency gradient across the three professional groups. Veterinarians demonstrated universal awareness (100%) of all ten ISC types. Para-veterinarians showed high awareness (> 80%) for most complications, with the notable exception of improper drug absorption (86.8%) and hardened/thick tissue (81.1%).

Animal herders demonstrated the most variable knowledge profile. While nearly all could identify obvious clinical signs such as localised pain (98.3%), swelling (97.4%), and lameness (94%), fewer recognised deeper pathological processes such as improper drug absorption (38.5%), tissue necrosis (62.5%), and contamination-related infection (66.7%). Only 57.3% could identify damaged skin/muscle, and 76.0% were aware of abscess formation (Table 6).

Common anatomical injection sites

The neck region was the most common site for injection (88.33%) while only 11.67% used the shoulder muscles. If lesions did occur, 75% were in the neck region and 25% in the gluteal muscles of the veterinarian treated animals. The pattern of site selection was, however, alarming for para-veterinarians, as 84.91% of them selected the thigh muscles as an inappropriate high value site, and only 5.66% selected the recommended neck region. Similarly, 94.34% of lesions in Para-veterinarian treated animals were in the thigh muscles. The animal herders showed similar patterns to the para-veterinarians, with 73.49% using the thigh muscles and 21.37% using the gluteal area. The shoulder region was used by only 3.42% and the neck was not used by any. These practices were demonstrated in the distribution of lesions, with 73.49% of lesions in the thigh muscles, 20.51% in the gluteal region and 6.00% in the shoulder (Table 8).

Discussion

The study evaluated the knowledge, attitudes and practices of veterinarians, para-veterinarians and animal herders in small ruminant production in Kumasi, Ghana, on injection-site complications. While veterinarians are the only legally authorized professionals to administer injections, the frequent presence of para-veterinarians and herders in the delivery of injections is indicative of the realities of livestock production systems in Ghana [16], where animal owners and herders are often directly involved in livestock management on a day-to-day basis and work closely with veterinary personnel. Herders are often the first point of contact for animals, as they are in constant contact with the animals, especially in extensive and semi-intensive production systems [17, 18]. In many cases, veterinarians may give instructions or medications, and herders may do so because they are accessible, inexpensive, convenient, or necessary [19]. This underscores an important disconnect between veterinary regulations and field practices and the need to involve all actors in livestock health management in intervention strategies.

The results revealed significant differences in knowledge between the professional groups. Veterinarians demonstrated the greatest knowledge of injection site complications, anatomical injection sites, and drug administration techniques. This is to be expected due to their formal education in anatomy, pathology, pharmacology and clinical practice. Likewise, para-veterinarians had relatively high awareness of most injection-related complications. Animal herders, however, demonstrated relatively limited knowledge, especially regarding more complex pathological complications such as improper drug absorption, tissue necrosis and contamination-related infections. Herders were more aware of clinical signs of swelling, pain and lameness than of the underlying pathological mechanisms. The same has been observed in other African countries where non-professional livestock handlers are aware of the visible disease outcomes but have limited knowledge of the underlying pathological processes and pharmacological implications [1, 20].

The strong relationship between occupation and knowledge level found in this study also reinforces the need for professional training to promote safe injection practices. Formal training was also strongly linked to knowledge and practice levels, suggesting that training interventions could have a significant impact on the injection safety of livestock handlers. The results are consistent with earlier studies, which reported that insufficient practical training of livestock health workers is associated with poor drug administration practices and higher injection site complications [12, 14].

The differences in anatomical knowledge and preferred injection sites among the professional groups are particularly important. The neck area was the most common site for intramuscular injection, consistent with the internationally recommended meat quality assurance practices, as the neck area has lower value muscle cuts and minimizes carcass losses due to lesion trimming. Para-veterinarians and animal herders, on the other hand, preferred the thigh and gluteal muscles for injection. These areas have economic value in terms of muscle tissue and are more prone to causing carcass trimming and economic losses if lesions are present. In Nigeria and Northern Ghana, non-professional injectors have been reported to inject into easily accessible muscle masses without taking into account the implications for meat quality [12, 13].

The abattoir results also support the association between injection practices and lesion occurrence. Abscesses, fibrosis, granulomas and tissue damage were consistently observed in slaughtered animals between January and August 2025. The overall prevalence seemed low, but routine meat inspection procedures may underestimate the true prevalence of lesions as some lesions are deep within the muscle and may not be easily detected during routine inspection [6, 7]. The high percentage of lesions in the thigh area is similar to the reported preference of para-veterinarians and herders for hindlimb injections. This finding is of economic importance, as lesions in high-value muscles may increase carcass quality and trimming losses, which may reduce profitability in already resource-limited livestock systems.

It should be noted that not all lesions identified at slaughter can be unequivocally attributed to injection practices; differential diagnoses such as caseous, parasitic cysts, and traumatic injuries must be considered [6, 7]. Lesion attribution in this study was based on anatomical location, gross morphological characteristics, and concordance with documented injection site preferences recorded by qualified meat inspectors during routine post-mortem examination. The overlap between lesion distribution and the injection-site practices of para-veterinarians and herders provides a reasonable, though not definitive, basis for inferring an injection-related aetiology.

The overall attitudes of the respondents towards aseptic injection practices were positive, although there were differences in knowledge among the groups. All groups strongly supported the use of aseptic techniques during injections. There were, however, some significant differences in awareness of withdrawal periods, antimicrobial-associated lesions, and meat quality concerns. Veterinarians and para-veterinarians were more aware of these issues than animal herders, who were less concerned, especially about meat quality and adherence to withdrawal periods. Only three-quarters of herders were aware of the need for withdrawal periods, and almost half had little concern about the effects on meat quality. The same has been noted in other studies, where the livestock producers tend to focus on short-term treatment results rather than long-term public health or carcass quality effects [4, 21]. This lack of awareness can lead to inappropriate use of antimicrobials, residues in meat and higher risk of antimicrobial resistance.

Interestingly, the study also demonstrated that good knowledge alone did not necessarily translate into optimal practice. Formal training and attitude were more strongly related to practice level than was knowledge. This discovery indicates that even among knowledgeable personnel, there may be a relationship between injection practices and behavioural factors, routine work conditions, and access to resources. Veterinary antimicrobial stewardship studies have also identified knowledge-to-practice gaps, including time pressure, workload, client expectations, and resource limitations, that impact adherence to recommended practices [22, 23]. Veterinarians showed high knowledge scores, but some reported that they did not always rotate injection sites and that they did not always monitor for post-injection effects. The risks of infection transmission and zoonotic exposure may be further elevated among herders, where injection hygiene, monitoring, and safe disposal practices are not as well adhered to.

These findings are consistent with evidence on abattoir-detected injection-site lesions in small ruminants. Regassa et al. [24] reported that abscess and fibrosis formation were among the principal causes of carcass condemnation in sheep and goats at Luna Export Abattoir in Ethiopia, with non-professional handlers identified as a key contributing factor in lesion distribution across hindlimb musculature. In Ethiopia, Jibat et al. [25] similarly documented that abscess-related condemnations were concentrated in high-value muscle regions, consistent with the thigh and gluteal injection preferences observed among para-veterinarians and herders in the present study. In Ghana, Ntiamoah et al. [26] conducted an abattoir-based study of lesion prevalence in sheep and goats slaughtered at the Kumasi abattoir and documented abscess, granuloma, and tissue degeneration as recurrent findings, underscoring the relevance of local abattoir surveillance for detecting subclinical pathology in small ruminants. At the broader African level, Caudell et al. [27] conducted a multi-country KAP survey across five African livestock systems including Ghana and found that individuals living or working at the farm level routinely make drug administration decisions independently, with very limited input from veterinary professionals, a pattern that closely mirrors the herder-led injection practices and knowledge deficits identified in this study.

In Ghana, while no prior peer-reviewed abattoir study on injection-site lesions in small ruminants exists, the present study fills this gap and aligns with regional evidence. The relatively low overall prevalence (0.23%) observed may reflect underdetection, consistent with Cresswell et al. [7], who noted that routine meat inspection commonly misses deep intramuscular lesions.

The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationship between KAP deficiencies and lesion occurrence. Self-reported practice data are subject to social desirability bias, which may overestimate actual compliance. Abattoir data were retrospective and reliant on routine inspection records, likely underestimating the true prevalence of lesions, particularly those deep within muscle tissue. Histopathological examination of the lesion that could have given insight into the aetiology was not performed, limiting the ability to definitively distinguish injection-related pathology from other conditions such as caseous lymphadenitis or parasitic lesions. Finally, purposive sampling from a single metropolitan area limits the generalisability of findings to other regions of Ghana.

Conclusion

The results of this study show significant differences in knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding injection-site complications among veterinarians, para-veterinarians and animal herders in Kumasi, Ghana. Veterinarians were found to be the most competent, and animal herders were found to have important gaps in anatomical knowledge, injection-site selection, and knowledge of deeper pathological complications of injections. The distribution of lesions observed during abattoir inspection was consistent with the frequent use of the thigh and gluteal muscles by para-veterinarians and herders. While positive attitudes towards aseptic practices were generally noted in all groups, there were significant gaps in the areas of withdrawal periods, meat quality concerns, and post-injection monitoring practices. The findings provide an empirical baseline on injection safety in small ruminant production in Ghana and offer a foundation for evidence-based policy and training interventions.

The results of this study have significant implications for livestock production, meat safety and veterinary public health in Ghana. The frequent contact with herders and para-veterinarians in administering injections implies that interventions aimed at veterinarians alone may not be sufficient to address injection-site complications. Practical, field-based training programs specifically designed for herders and para-veterinarians could significantly enhance injection practices and decrease lesion rates, as herders are in close contact with animals on a daily basis and are often directly involved in animal treatment. Enhancing veterinary extension services, access to trained personnel and awareness of proper injection sites, withdrawal times and meat quality could help reduce economic losses and public health risks due to injection-site complications.

Recommendation

The results reflect the reality that herders are still actively involved in administering injections, as they are in close daily contact with livestock and are often the first to care for them, especially in areas where veterinary services are scarce. Therefore, interventions to enhance injection safety in Ghana should be inclusive, engaging herders and not just licensed professionals.

Further research is needed to explore the economic consequences of injection-site lesions in Ghanaian livestock systems, such as carcass trimming losses and meat quality. Longitudinal studies are also required to determine specific drugs, administration methods, and management factors that are linked to the development and severity of lesions. Furthermore, intervention-based research to assess the effectiveness of targeted field training, veterinary extension and abattoir feedback systems could offer practical and cost-effective solutions to minimise injection-site complications and enhance meat safety in the small ruminant production sector in Ghana.
